Engineered probiotic-propelled micro-robot as biological truck for deep tumor penetration and treatment
Authors: Kaida Huang, Yubiao Lin, Wenhui Zheng, Xin Fan, Yufei Ji, Shuitu Feng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractEffectively penetrating complex tumor physiological barriers and delivering therapeutic drugs precisely to each cancer cell remains a key challenge in modern oncology. For this purpose, we have developed an engineered probiotic-driven, macrophage membrane-camouflaged microrobot (EcN-MP/DOX). This system can not only serve as a “biological truck" for precise delivery of anti-cancer drugs, but also a natural immunomodulatory agent. By coating the surface of EcN with hybrid vesicles composed of macrophage membranes and pH-responsive liposomes, this system ingeniously integrates the autonomous motility and hypoxia chemotaxis of probiotics, the active targeting capability of macrophage membranes, and the pH-triggered drug release property. This design enables precise infiltration into and drug enrichment within deep tumor tissues. Furthermore, EcN-MP/DOX can express tumor-specific antigens and function as an “in situ vaccine" by promoting the secretion of IFN-γ and TNF-α, thereby stimulating a potent anti-tumor T cell immune response. Consequently, this microrobot achieves synergistic chemotherapy and immunotherapy. Experimental results demonstrate that EcN-MP/DOX exhibits outstanding tissue penetration and antitumor efficacy in human-derived tumor organoids constructed in vitro, as well as promising therapeutic effects in vivo. Moreover, this drug delivery system shows excellent biocompatibility and safety, offering a promising preclinical strategy that merits further translational investigation.Keywords: Micro-robot, Engineered probiotics, Macrophage membrane camouflage, pH-responsive drug release, Tumor penetration therapy

Introduction

Cancer remains one of the most challenging global health burdens and a leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide [1,2]. Chemotherapy continues to serve as a cornerstone of conventional cancer treatment regimens [3,4]. However, its clinical efficacy is severely hampered by the complex and multifactorial physiological barriers inherent to solid tumors [5,6]. These barriers including markedly elevated interstitial fluid pressure, a densely packed extracellular matrix, and pronounced hypoxic heterogeneity collectively impede the deep penetration and homogeneous distribution of therapeutic agents throughout the tumor mass [7,8]. As a consequence, subtherapeutic drug concentrations in the central core regions of tumors contribute to inadequate cytoreduction, ultimately fostering tumor recurrence, treatment failure, and the emergence of multidrug resistance [[9], [10], [11]]– (see Scheme 1)

Schematic design and treatment mechanism of M1 macrophage-membrane-coated enzyme driven pH responsive micro-robot EcN-MP/DOX for the treatment of gastric cancer. The figures created with BioGDP.com [12].

To overcome these barriers, various nanomedicine delivery systems have been explored [[13], [14], [15]]. While strategies such as leveraging the enhanced permeability and retention (EPR) effect or using active targeting ligands have shown progress, these systems generally lack the autonomous propulsion needed to actively navigate the dense tumor stroma and effectively reach hypoxic regions [16,17]. Recently, bacterial-based therapies have emerged as a promising alternative, offering unique advantages such as innate tumor tropism [[18], [19], [20]]. Certain anaerobic strains, like Escherichia coli Nissle 1917 (EcN), exhibit natural hypoxia-seeking behavior and an ability to colonize tumor tissues [21,22]. Importantly, beyond serving as drug carriers, certain engineered probiotics like EcN have shown intrinsic immunomodulatory potential, capable of activating antitumor immunity by modulating the tumor microenvironment or expressing therapeutic antigens, thereby bridging targeted drug delivery with immunotherapy [23,24]. However, standalone bacterial therapies face limitations, including restricted drug-loading capacity [25], potential immunogenicity, and a lack of spatially/temporally controlled drug release [[26], [27], [28]]. Integrating the tumor-targeting and immunostimulatory properties of probiotics with advanced drug delivery technologies thus represents a compelling yet underexplored strategy.

Herein, we report a biomimetic drug delivery platform designed to synergistically integrate the tumor-homing and potential immunomodulatory functions of engineered probiotics with cell-membrane camouflage and stimuli-responsive drug release. We constructed EcN-MP/DOX: a probiotic-driven microrobot coated with a hybrid nanovesicle, formed by fusing M1 macrophage membranes with pH-responsive liposomes loaded with doxorubicin (DOX). This integrated design enables EcN-MP/DOX to function not only as a targeted drug delivery vehicle for deep tumor penetration and controlled drug release but also as a multifunctional immunomodulator. The EcN core provides autonomous, hypoxia-guided propulsion into tumors while inherently possessing the capacity to stimulate antitumor immune responses. The M1 macrophage membrane coating further enhances active tumor targeting and confers immune-evasive properties, and the pH-responsive liposome component ensures precise drug release within the acidic tumor microenvironment. This multifaceted synergy is engineered to significantly enhance drug accumulation and penetration while concurrently potentiating antitumor immunity. We validated the deep-penetration capability, potent antitumor efficacy, and immune activation role of EcN-MP/DOX using both in vitro human-derived tumor organoid models and in vivo mouse models. The platform favorable biocompatibility and an acceptable safety profile in the evaluated mouse model, warranting further long-term toxicological assessment. Thus, our work presents a promising strategy to overcome key tumor physiological barriers while enabling synergistic chemo-immunotherapy, providing a valuable reference for advancing precision medicine in aggressive cancers.

Results and discussion

Preparation and characterization of hybrid nanovesicles MP/DOX

In this study, we designed and prepared MP/DOX hybrid nanovesicles capable of actively targeting tumor tissues and releasing drugs in a pH-responsive manner. The preparation process is illustrated in Fig. 1a. First, primary macrophages were isolated and purified from mouse bone marrow derived macrophage (BMDM). These BMDM cells were then stimulated with LPS to induce polarization into the M1 phenotype. Finally, the M1 macrophage membranes were extracted and purified. As shown in Fig. 1b, monocytes exhibited a rounded morphology, while differentiated BMDM cells adopted a characteristic spindle shape. Flow cytometry analysis confirmed high purity of the obtained cells: BMDMs specifically labeled with F4/80 and CD11b reached 99.9% purity (Fig. 1c), and M1 macrophages labeled with F4/80 and iNOS showed 99.1% purity (Fig. 1d). Successful M1 polarization was further verified by qPCR, which demonstrated significantly elevated iNOS mRNA expression levels in M1 macrophages (Fig. 1e). Lyso-Tracker Green staining revealed that the differentiated M1 macrophages maintained intact morphology and contained abundant lysosomes (Fig. 1f). The M1 macrophage membranes were subsequently isolated using a commercial membrane extraction kit. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images (Fig. 1g and h) confirmed the successful separation of intact membrane structures.

Next, pH-responsive drug-loaded liposomes (pHLP/DOX) were prepared. The successful synthesis of the pH-sensitive material DSPE-HYD-mPEG2000 was confirmed by 1H NMR spectroscopy (Fig. S1), which demonstrated the incorporation of an acyl hydrazone (HYD) bond a linkage known for its pH-responsive cleavage capability. Following DOX loading, pHLP/DOX nanoparticles were obtained. As illustrated in Fig. 2a, M1 macrophage membranes and pHLP/DOX liposomes were hybridized to form MP/DOX hybrid nanovesicles, which are designed to release their encapsulated drug payload under acidic conditions. TEM images revealed that both pHLP/DOX and MP/DOX nanoparticles exhibited uniform particle sizes (Fig. 2b and c). Notably, the surface of MP/DOX nanoparticles displayed distinct protein-like folds compared to pHLP/DOX, indicating successful membrane-liposome hybridization. Dynamic light scattering analysis showed that MP/DOX had an average hydrodynamic diameter of approximately 122 nm (Fig. 2d). Consistent with this, SEM images further confirmed the uniform size and morphology of the MP/DOX nanoparticles (Fig. 2e). The pH-responsive disintegration behavior of MP/DOX was assessed using TEM and dynamic light scattering (DLS) following 1 h of incubation at different pH values (Fig. 2f–S2). Pronounced morphological changes were observed, particularly at pH 5.0, where the nanoparticles exhibited extensive fragmentation and loss of their intact vesicular architecture. In a time-course study at pH 5.0, the nanoparticles progressively ruptured and released their contents, accompanied by notable shifts in particle size distribution (Fig. 2g–S2). Together, these findings confirm the pH-triggered drug release capability of the MP/DOX platform. Finally, the drug loading performance of MP/DOX was quantified, showing an encapsulation efficiency of 85.3% and a drug loading capacity of 36.5%.

Preparation and characterization of EcN-MP/DOX micro-robot

As shown in Fig. 3a, MP/DOX nanovesicles were conjugated to the surface of Escherichia coli Nissle 1917 (EcN) using a biotin–streptavidin binding strategy. First, a large quantity of highly viable EcN was prepared by cultivation in LB medium. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images of the cultured probiotics revealed a well-defined rod-shaped morphology, with a length of approximately 2-3 μm (Fig. 3b). The bacterial viability and morphology were further confirmed by staining with the live-cell fluorescent probe SYTO 9 and imaging under confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM), which showed characteristic rod-shaped structures with high metabolic activity (Fig. 3c). To quantitatively assess bacterial viability, EcN was stained with a combination of live/dead fluorescent probes and analyzed by flow cytometry. As shown in Fig. 3d, both unmodified EcN and the modified EcN-MP/DOX construct exhibited high survival rates, indicating that surface functionalization with MP/DOX nanovesicles did not significantly compromise bacterial viability. Finally, TEM and SEM images of the resulting EcN-MP/DOX microrobots (Fig. 3e) revealed numerous spherical, protein-rich nanostructures attached to the bacterial surface, confirming the successful decoration of EcN with the hybrid nanovesicles.

To further confirm the successful surface modification of EcN with MP/DOX nanovesicles, we performed fluorescent labeling and protein analysis. First, MP/DOX nanovesicles were labeled with an Alexa Fluor® 647-conjugated anti-CD86 antibody (abcam, recombinant clone EPR21962), a marker specific for M1 macrophage membranes. These labeled vesicles were then conjugated to EcN to construct EcN-MP/DOX. The bacteria were concurrently stained with the live-cell probe SYTO 9 and visualized by confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM). As shown in Fig. 3f, distinct red fluorescence (CD86) was observed on the green fluorescent bacterial surface, with strong overlap between the red signal and the blue fluorescence of DOX, confirming the attachment of MP/DOX to EcN. Additionally, Coomassie Brilliant Blue staining was used to separate and detect the proteins in different components (pHLP/DOX, MP/DOX, EcN, EcN-MP/DOX, Marker)(Fig. S3). The resulting band patterns further verified the presence of macrophage membrane proteins in the EcN-MP/DOX construct, supporting successful surface functionalization. Together, these results comprehensively demonstrate the successful preparation of EcN-MP/DOX and validate the synthetic approach employed.

To assess the viability of the probiotic within the prepared microrobotic system EcN-MP/DOX, a live/dead bacterial double-staining kit (Shanghai Maokang Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China, MX 4234) was employed, followed by visualization using confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM). As shown in Fig. 4a and b, the red fluorescence intensity associated with dead bacteria increased gradually over time, indicating a progressive decline in probiotic viability. This trend was further supported by optical density measurements at 600 nm (OD600), which revealed that the concentration of EcN-MP/DOX decreased significantly compared to unmodified EcN over the same period (Fig. 4c). These results suggest that surface modification with MP/DOX nanovesicles not only increases the mortality rate of EcN but also markedly inhibits its reproductive capacity. This inhibitory effect may be attributed to the dense coating of nanovesicles on the bacterial surface, which could physically interfere with normal cell division and proliferation. Additionally, the encapsulation efficiency of MP/DOX on EcN was determined to be 61.2%, with a corresponding drug loading rate of 23.4%.

To examine whether surface modification with MP/DOX affects the motility of EcN, the motion behaviors of unmodified EcN and the EcN-MP/DOX microrobot were observed and recorded using CLSM under a 100× objective. Three-dimensional fluorescence imaging via confocal z-axis layer scanning (Fig. 4d and Movie S1) showed that within the same focal plane, the movement ranges of EcN and EcN-MP/DOX were comparable. Trajectory analysis of EcN and EcN-MP/DOX from Movie S2 was performed using Fiji software (Fig. 4e), revealing similar path lengths over the same observation period. Quantitative motility parameters including mean-squared displacement (MSD), effective diffusion coefficient (Deff), and average velocity were further calculated with MATLAB [29,30]. As shown in Fig. 4f–h, EcN-MP/DOX exhibited lower MSD, velocity, and Deff values than unmodified EcN. This reduction may result from partial interference of MP/DOX decoration with surface flagellar structures. Nevertheless, EcN-MP/DOX retained substantial motility, and its microrod architecture remained conducive to tissue penetration. The pH-responsive drug release of EcN-MP/DOX relies on the acid-labile acyl hydrazone (HYD) bond within the pHLP/DOX liposomes, which cleaves under acidic conditions, triggering drug release from the hybrid nanovesicles. As shown in Fig. 4i and S4 drug release from EcN-MP/DOX was fastest and most extensive at pH 5.0, with significant differences compared to release at pH 6.5 and pH 7.4. These results confirm the pH-triggered drug release capability of the EcN-MP/DOX platform.

Cell uptake and cell penetration of EcN-MP/DOX micro-robot

The cellular uptake behavior of the EcN-MP/DOX microrobot was investigated using confocal microscopy. MKN45 cells were incubated with EcN-MP/DOX and imaged at different time points. As shown in Fig. 5a and S5, cellular uptake of the microrobot increased progressively over time, with numerous distinct rod-shaped structures clearly visible inside the MKN45 cells. This design leverages the inherent flagella-driven motility and tissue penetrating capability of the rod-shaped probiotic carrier to promote active tumor targeting.

It is important to dissect the respective contributions of the two components of EcN-MP/DOX in promoting deep penetration. The macrophage membrane coating primarily serves to provide active recognition and adherence to tumor cells via integrin and receptor-ligand interactions, which enhances the initial cellular uptake and prevents rapid clearance [31,32]. However, passive targeting or enhanced cellular adhesion alone is insufficient to traverse the densely packed solid tumors. In this regard, the EcN core acts as the primary ‘engine'. Its flagella-driven motility, directed by hypoxia chemotaxis, generates the physical force necessary to entire targeted drug delivery vehicle through the interstitial spaces [33,34]. To further evaluate the tumor penetration and accumulation efficacy of EcN-MP/DOX, a patient-derived gastric cancer organoid model (GC-009) was established. The organoids were treated with free DOX or EcN-MP/DOX for 6 h and analyzed by confocal z-stack imaging (Fig. 5b). Uptake in the free DOX group was markedly lower than in the EcN-MP/DOX group (Fig. S6). Representative layer images (Fig. 5c and S7) revealed abundant microrobots distributed deep within the organoids treated with EcN-MP/DOX. We use image J to measure the fluorescence intensity attenuation from the periphery to the core in the confocal Z-stacked image data of organoids. As shown in Fig. S8, the ratio of fluorescence intensity at the core to that at the periphery indicates that EcN-MP/DOX has a stronger penetration and enrichment ability than DOX. demonstrating its ability to penetrate and enrich throughout the tumor tissue. These results demonstrate EcN-MP/DOX has ability to penetrate and enrich throughout the tumor tissue, confirm that the self-propelled EcN core facilitates deep penetration into the organoid architecture.

Anti-tumor evaluation of EcN-MP/DOX micro-robot in vitro

Cell viability of MKN45 cells was assessed after treatment with different formulations (DMEM, DOX, EcN, MP/DOX, and EcN-MP/DOX) at varying concentrations. As shown in Fig. 6a, the EcN-MP/DOX group exhibited the lowest cell viability compared to the EcN, DOX, and MP/DOX groups, demonstrating the potent antitumor efficacy of the EcN-MP/DOX microrobot. To rigorously distinguish the cytotoxicity contributed by the carrier from that of the released drug, we will add an CCK-8 assay of EcN-MP (without DOX) group. We will prepare EcN-MP microrobots following the same conjugation protocol but using empty pH-responsive liposomes (without DOX loading). The result is shown in Fig. S9, EcN-MP shows minimal cytotoxicity (<18% reduction in cell viability at the highest tested concentration), confirming that the potent antitumor effect observed with EcN-MP/DOX is primarily attributed to the pH-triggered release of DOX rather than intrinsic carrier toxicity. To visually evaluate the antitumor effect, three types of gastric cancer organoids were cultured and treated with the different formulations for 24 h. As illustrated in Fig. 6b–d, organoids treated with free DOX maintained relatively intact spherical morphology. In contrast, those treated with MP/DOX showed obvious structural rupture, loss of integrity, and release of interior cells. Notably, organoids incubated with EcN-MP/DOX were nearly completely disrupted, with mostly fragmented cellular debris and no recognizable three-dimensional structure. These results indicate that the EcN-MP/DOX microrobot exerts a significant antitumor effect, likely attributable to its enhanced tumor tissue penetration and efficient accumulation within the tumor microenvironment.

To dynamically monitor the dissolution process, gastric cancer organoids (GC-009) treated with EcN-MP/DOX were imaged over time using confocal microscopy. As shown in Fig. 6e, the organoids progressively exhibited basement membrane rupture, structural disintegration, shedding of internal cells, and eventual fragmentation with increasing incubation time. These observations indicate that the EcN-MP/DOX microrobot rapidly penetrates the organoid matrix and releases its drug payload to effectively kill tumor cells. Furthermore, apoptosis in gastric cancer organoids was quantified via flow cytometry after treatment with DOX, MP/DOX, or EcN-MP/DOX for varying durations. The results (Fig. 6f–S10, S11) demonstrated that EcN-MP/DOX induced significantly stronger tumor cell killing compared to the other groups, with efficacy increasing over time. Those results proved that EcN-MP/DOX has significant anti-tumor effect compared to other groups, indicating that the design of this study is reasonable.

Tumor targeting of EcN-MP/DOX micro-robot

Firstly, we examined the tumor cell targeting ability of EcN-MP/DOX micro-robot in vitro. Specifically, three types of gastric cancer organoids (GC-XMMC-Y001, GC-SDZL-SO15, GC-009) were cultured and incubated with different groups (DOX, P/DOX, MP/DOX, EcN-MP/DOX) for 3 h. The fluorescence intensity and distribution of the organoids were observed using laser confocal microscopy. As shown in Fig. 7a–d the fluorescence intensity of the three types of organs incubated in the P/DOX group was the lowest. The fluorescence intensity of DOX is higher than that of P/DOX, possibly due to its small molecule morphology, which facilitates passive diffusion into the organoids. Notably, the MP/DOX group showed a significant increase in fluorescence intensity compared to DOX alone, which may be attributed to the tumor-targeting ability of MP/DOX, promoting its uptake and penetration into the organoids. Furthermore, the EcN-MP/DOX group demonstrated stronger fluorescence than the MP/DOX group. This enhancement may result from the combined effects of tumor tissue targeting and probiotic-driven tissue penetration, along with the subsequent release of DOX, which together improve organoid uptake and deep penetration. Collectively, these results indicate that the EcN-MP/DOX micro-robot possesses favorable tumor-targeting and penetration properties.

To evaluate the tumor-targeting efficacy of the EcN-MP/DOX micro-robot in vivo, a tumor model was established by inoculating MKN45 cells into BALB/c mice. The EcN component was labeled with the Cy5.5-NHS fluorescent probe and tracked via Cy5.5 fluorescence imaging, while the other three treatment groups were monitored using Cy5-DOX fluorescence. After intravenous administration of the different formulations (DOX, MP/DOX, EcN, and EcN-MP/DOX, with EcN administered at 1 × 105 CFU/mL and DOX at a dose of 10 mg/kg), fluorescence intensity in the mice was measured at various time points (0, 1, 3, 6, 9, and 12 h) using a small animal fluorescence imaging system. As shown in Fig. 7e and f, the MP/DOX nanoparticles, which carry M1 macrophage membranes with active tumor-targeting functionality, exhibited significantly enhanced tumor accumulation compared with free DOX. The EcN group also demonstrated notable tumor targeting, likely attributable to its anaerobic nature, which promotes homing to the hypoxic tumor microenvironment. Remarkably, the EcN-MP/DOX micro-robot reached tumors more rapidly than either MP/DOX or EcN alone. This accelerated targeting can be attributed to the combined effects of active tumor targeting mediated by the macrophage membrane, together with the anaerobic motility and tissue-penetrating capacity of the rod-shaped probiotic EcN, which collectively promote deep tumor penetration and efficient accumulation.

After 12 h of administration, the mice were sacrificed, and major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lungs, and kidneys) as well as tumor tissues were collected from each treatment group. As shown in Fig. 7g and S12, the DOX and EcN groups exhibited higher fluorescence intensity in the liver than in the tumor, indicating poor tumor targeting and significant hepatic accumulation. In contrast, the MP/DOX and EcN-MP/DOX groups showed stronger fluorescence in tumor tissues than in the liver, demonstrating effective tumor targeting and enrichment with reduced liver uptake. Moreover, owing to its active targeting capability and hypoxia driven tropism, the EcN-MP/DOX group displayed markedly higher fluorescence intensity in tumor tissue compared to the EcN and MP/DOX groups alone. Although the MP/DOX coating reduced EcN motility parameters, no significant difference was achieved(Fig. 4b–f). Despite the decrease in speed, EcN-MP/DOX still outperforms MP/DOX in terms of organoid penetration and in vivo tumor accumulation, confirming the remaining mobility, combined with chemotaxis and membrane targeting, providing sufficient tumor targeting. These results confirm that the EcN-MP/DOX micro-robot enables rapid and effective tumor-targeted accumulation, while exhibiting a favorable biodistribution profile with minimal off-target localization.

Anti-tumor evaluation of the EcN-MP/DOX micro-robot in vivo

The anti-tumor effect scheme of EcN-MP/DOX micro-robot in vivo is shown in Fig. 8a. Briefly, MKN45 cells are implanted subcutaneously in mice. Once tumor volume reached approximately 500 mm3, the mice received intravenous injections of different formulations (saline, DOX, EcN, MP/DOX, and EcN-MP/DOX; EcN dose: 1 × 105 CFU/mL, DOX dose: 10 mg/kg) every two days. After eight treatment cycles, the mice were euthanized, and tumors were excised and weighed. As shown in Fig. 8b, tumor volume increased markedly in the saline, DOX, EcN, and MP/DOX groups. In contrast, the EcN-MP/DOX group exhibited a significant inhibitory effect on tumor growth compared with the other treatments. Throughout the study, mice in the EcN-MP/DOX group showed no notable decrease in body weight, unlike those in the other groups (Fig. 8c). Representative images of excised tumors (Fig. 8d–S13) confirmed that the EcN-MP/DOX group had the smallest tumor size. Consistently, tumor weight was significantly lower in all treatment groups (DOX, EcN, MP/DOX and EcN-MP/DOX) than in the saline control, with the EcN-MP/DOX group showing the lowest value (Fig. 8e). Survival analysis (Fig. 8f) revealed that mice in the MP/DOX and EcN-MP/DOX groups survived longer than those in the DOX, EcN, and saline groups, with similar survival curves observed between the two former groups.

To quantitatively assess the direct tumor-killing effects of the different treatment regimens, we performed apoptosis detection on paraffin-embedded mouse tumor sections using the TUNEL assay. The quantitative results (Fig. 8g and h) demonstrate that treatment with EcN-MP/DOX induced the most pronounced level of tumor cell apoptosis, which was significantly higher than that observed in each monotherapy group (EcN or DOX alone) as well as in the non-targeted composite control group (MP/DOX). This direct evidence reveals that the induction of tumor cell apoptosis constitutes a central mechanism through which the EcN-MP/DOX micro-robot exerts its anti-tumor activity. Furthermore, the in vivo experimental outcomes confirm that this therapeutic approach can effectively ameliorate the condition of tumor bearing mice and markedly extend their overall survival period.

To clarify whether EcN-MP/DOX micro-robot can activate tumor local immunity and drive T cell dependent anti-tumor effects, we analyzed tumor tissues. Specifically, tumor tissues from different treatment groups were prepared into single-cell suspensions and the phenotype of tumor infiltrating lymphocytes was detected by flow cytometry. We analyzed the ratio and absolute number of CD4+ T cells and CD8+ T cells in cells. As shown in Fig. 8i, compared with the control group, the number of CD8+ T cells in groups EcN and EcN-MP/DOX significantly increased, indicating that group EcN-MP/DOX has a regulatory effect on key effector immune cells in the tumor microenvironment. The potential significance of increased CD8+ T cell infiltration in converting “cold" tumors into “hot" tumors. To evaluate the systemic immune response, we quantitatively detected the levels of IFN-γ and TNF-α in mouse plasma using ELISA kit. Plasma cytokine test (Fig. 8j) showed that the levels of IFN-γ and TNF-α in EcN and EcN-MP/DOX treatment group were significantly higher than those in the control group, indicating successful activation of systemic Th1 immune response. IFN-γ and TNF-α not only directly suppresses tumor proliferation but also enhances tumor immunogenicity by upregulating MHC-I expression [35,36], thereby accounting for the significant tumor regression observed in vivo. This result directly confirms that EcN-MP/DOX can effectively stimulate systemic anti-tumor T cell immunity.

We detect these cytokines IL-2, IL-12, and TGF-β in tumor lysate to demonstrate that the EcN-MP/DOX system induces Th1 type immune response through in situ vaccine. The results (Fig. S14) showed that in the EcN MP/DOX treatment group, the levels of IL-2 and IL-12 in tumor lysate were significantly upregulated. This is consistent with the reported increase in IFN-γ, and the upregulation of IL-12 further confirms that this treatment promotes the maturation of dendritic cells (DCs) and Th1 cell polarization [37,38]; The elevation of IL-2 provides persistent survival and proliferation signals for infiltrating CD8+ T cells. It is worth noting that in the local tumor microenvironment, the TGF-β levels in the EcN-MP/DOX group of mice were significantly lower than those in the control group (Fig. S14). The synergistic therapeutic effect of EcN-MP/DOX not only relies on the adjuvant effects of DOX induced ICD and EcN, but also manifests in the overall remodeling of the tumor immune microenvironment. The above results demonstrate that EcN-MP/DOX micro-robot has significant anti-tumor immunotherapy effects.

To evaluate the biosafety of the EcN-MP/DOX micro-robot, BALB/c mice were divided into three treatment groups (saline, DOX, and EcN-MP/DOX, with EcN administered at 1 × 105 CFU/mL and DOX at 10 mg/kg). The mice received intravenous injections every other day over a 15 day period. After the treatment regimen, blood samples and major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lungs, and kidneys) were collected from each group. Blood biochemical analysis (Fig. S15) revealed that DOX treatment induced significant hepatotoxicity and nephrotoxicity, whereas the EcN-MP/DOX group exhibited markedly reduced damage in these organs. H&E staining results (Fig. S16) further demonstrated severe pathological alterations in the liver, spleen, and kidneys of DOX treated mice compared with the saline control. Specifically, the DOX group presented diffuse ballooning degeneration in the liver, inflammatory cell infiltration in the kidneys, and disrupted tissue architecture with vacuolation in both the spleen and kidneys.

To further evaluate the in vivo biosafety of the EcN-MP/DOX micro-robot, the plate counting method to quantitatively monitor the bacterial load in blood, liver, spleen, and tumors within 7 days after administration of EcN-MP/DOX. The results (Fig. S17) showed that EcN-MP/DOX was cleared from the systemic circulation within 7 days, but still existed in large quantities in tumor tissues, demonstrating the excellent tumor targeting and colonization ability and its high biological safety. The quantitative data of viable EcN counts in mouse blood at different time points (1 h, 6 h, and 24 h post injection) during the treatment course. The results (Fig. S18) showed that EcN-MP/DOX is rapidly cleared from the bloodstream, with detectable bacteria only at 1 h (∼103 CFU/mL) and 6 h (∼102 CFU/mL), and no viable bacteria detected at 24 h. This indicates that systemic bacteremia risk is minimal with our dosing regimen. Collectively, these findings indicate that the EcN-MP/DOX micro-robot effectively mitigates the systemic toxicity associated with free DOX, confirming its favorable biocompatibility profile.

Conclusions and discussion

In this study, we have developed a biomimetic probiotic-driven microrobot, EcN-MP/DOX, that synergistically integrates the hypoxia-directed motility and tumor-colonizing capability of engineered probiotics, the homologous targeting and immune-evasive properties of M1 macrophage membrane camouflage, and the spatiotemporally controlled release behavior of pH-responsive liposomes. This tripartite design enables the system to overcome the multifactorial physiological barriers of solid tumors including elevated interstitial fluid pressure, dense extracellular matrix, and hypoxic heterogeneity and achieve deep tumor penetration with localized drug enrichment. Both in vitro patient-derived tumor organoid models and in vivo murine xenograft experiments consistently validated the superior tissue penetration, potent antitumor efficacy, and favorable safety profile of EcN-MP/DOX.

To contextualize our findings within the rapidly expanding field of bacteria-based cancer therapy, it is instructive to compare our system with previously reported bacterial drug delivery platforms. First, regarding the choice of bacterial chassis, EcN distinguishes itself from other tumor-homing bacteria such as Salmonella typhimurium, Listeria monocytogenes, and Clostridium species through its established safety record as a probiotic with safe status. While Salmonella-based vectors have demonstrated potent antitumor effects, their inherent pathogenicity and potential for septic complications remain significant translational hurdles [39]. EcN offers a compelling balance between tumor-targeting efficacy and biosafety a characteristic well-established over a decade of research [40]. Our study extends this body of work by demonstrating that EcN can serve not merely as a passive carrier but as an active propulsive force that enhances deep tissue penetration, addressing a limitation common to many static nanoparticle systems.

Second, with respect to surface functionalization strategies, our use of M1 macrophage membrane camouflage represents a significant advancement over conventional PEGylation or ligand-mediated targeting approaches. Macrophage membrane-coated nanoparticles have emerged as promising tools offering immune evasion, extended circulation, and cell-specific targeting [41]. However, most reported macrophage membrane-camouflaged systems rely on synthetic or inert cores that lack autonomous motility. By conjugating macrophage membrane-derived vesicles to live EcN, we have created a hybrid architecture that combines the passive targeting advantages of biomimetic membranes with the active, self-propelled tumor-homing capability of bacteria. This integration addresses a key limitation of membrane-coated nanoparticles their dependence on passive diffusion or blood circulation for tumor accumulation and represents, to our knowledge, one of the first demonstrations of a motile, membrane-camouflaged bacterial microrobot for deep tumor penetration.

Third, in the broader context of biohybrid microrobots for cancer therapy, our work contributes to a rapidly growing field that includes magnetotactic bacteria, algae-based microrobots, and other bacteria-driven systems. While magnetotactic bacteria offer external magnetic guidance, they require specialized equipment and may face limitations in tissue penetration depth. By contrast, our EcN-based system harnesses the bacterium's intrinsic hypoxia chemotaxis, eliminating the need for external actuation while achieving autonomous navigation. Furthermore, the dual functionality of our platform serving simultaneously as a drug delivery vehicle and an immunomodulatory agent distinguishes it from microrobots designed solely for physical drug transport.

The immunotherapeutic dimension of our system warrants particular attention in light of recent advances in probiotic-based cancer immunotherapy. Emerging evidence demonstrates that probiotics can enhance tumor immunotherapy through gut microbiome modulation, systemic immune activation, and direct stimulation of antitumor T-cell immunity [42]. Our findings extend this paradigm by showing that EcN-MP/DOX not only delivers DOX to the tumor core but also functions through an in situ vaccination-like mechanism. Specifically, DOX-induced immunogenic cell death (ICD) releases tumor-associated antigens, while EcN's pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) serve as a natural adjuvant to promote dendritic cell maturation and antigen cross-presentation. The observed elevation of IFN-γ and TNF-α, along with increased CD8+ T-cell infiltration and enhanced Granzyme B expression in the EcN-MP/DOX treatment group, provides direct evidence for the successful priming of antigen-specific T-cell immunity a mechanistic depth that, to our knowledge, has not been fully characterized in previous EcN-based drug delivery systems.

The biosafety considerations inherent to live bacterial therapies deserve careful discussion in the context of clinical translation. Unlike conventional chemotherapeutics or nanoparticle formulations, live bacteria carry inherent risks including bacteremia and septic shock [43]. Our safety framework incorporates multiple layers of protection: (i) the use of EcN, a probiotic strain with low inherent toxicity and rapid systemic clearance; (ii) intravenous administration that enables controlled systemic exposure; (iii) the potential for real-time monitoring of blood-borne bacteria with prompt antibiotic intervention; and (iv) the future integration of inducible suicide circuits (e.g., thymidine kinase or toxin-antitoxin systems) as an additional biocontainment layer. This multi-layered approach addresses the primary concerns raised in recent reviews regarding the translational barriers of bacterial therapeutics. While we have not yet incorporated genetic suicide switches in the current formulation, the extensive safety data from our in vivo studies including serum biochemistry and histopathological examination—demonstrate that EcN-MP/DOX exhibits significantly reduced hepatotoxicity and nephrotoxicity compared with free DOX, confirming its favorable biocompatibility profile.

In conclusion, this study demonstrates that EcN-MP/DOX offers a promising platform to overcome persistent tumor physiological barriers and achieve precise drug delivery in cancer therapy. By synergistically integrating the hypoxia-driven motility and tumor-colonizing ability of probiotics, the homologous targeting and immune-evasion properties conferred by macrophage membranes, and the spatiotemporally controlled release behavior of pH-responsive liposomes, this bio-hybrid system achieves efficient and deep tumor penetration, ensuring targeted drug accumulation and localized payload release. Both in vitro patient-derived tumor organoid models and in vivo murine experiments consistently validated the superior tissue penetration, potent antitumor efficacy, and excellent biocompatibility and safety profile of EcN-MP/DOX. These results not only provide a novel biomimetic strategy that circumvents the limitations of conventional drug delivery in solid tumors but also establish a solid foundation for future development of microorganism-mediated intelligent therapeutic systems in precision medicine. Future work will focus on translational aspects such as scalable manufacturing, dose-regimen optimization, comprehensive long-term toxicological assessments, and the incorporation of additional biocontainment features to facilitate the clinical translation of this platform.

Experimental section

M1 macrophage membrane extraction and characterization

First, BMDM were isolated and purified as follows. BALB/c mice were euthanized by cervical dislocation, surface-sterilized with ethanol, and dissected to isolate the limb bones. After removing attached muscle tissue, the bones were immersed in PBS containing 1% penicillin-streptomycin. The marrow was repeatedly flushed from the bones using a syringe until the cavities appeared white. The flushed marrow was suspended in 1640 medium, gently pipetted to dissociate aggregates, and pas MSD(Δt)sed through a 70 μm cell strainer. The filtrate was centrifuged at 2000 rpm for 5 min. A discontinuous density gradient was prepared by carefully layering 3 mL of Histopaque-1119 and 3 mL of Histopaque-1077 in a 15 mL centrifuge tube. An equal volume of the cell suspension was slowly overlaid onto the gradient. Centrifugation was performed at 800 × g for 40 min at room temperature, with acceleration and deceleration set to 1 and 0, respectively. After centrifugation, the monocyte layer was carefully collected and resuspended in RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with 30% FBS. The cells were seeded into 6-well plates and incubated for 12 h. Non-adherent cells were removed by gentle shaking and aspiration, followed by 2-3 washes with D-Hanks buffer. The adherent cells were further cultured for 6 days in medium contain 40 ng/mL M-CSF, with a medium change on day 3. The resulting BMDM were characterized by flow cytometry using antibodies against F4/80 and CD11b, and by microscopic examination. To examine temporal changes in M1 macrophage polarization, BMDM were stimulated with LPS (100 ng/mL) for 48 h. M1 macrophages were analyzed by flow cytometry using antibodies against F4/80 and iNOS, and iNOS expression was also evaluated by qPCR. Additionally, M1 macrophages were stained with Lyso-Tracker Green probe and imaged by laser scanning confocal microscopy.

Subsequently, M1 macrophage subtypes were isolated by sorting F4/80+/iNOS+ cells using flow cytometry. Membranes of the sorted M1 macrophages were extracted with a commercial cell membrane extraction kit. Briefly, harvested cells were resuspended in membrane protein extraction buffer and incubated on ice for 15 min. The suspension was then homogenized with a glass homogenizer using 20 strokes. The homogenate was centrifuged at 1500 × g for 10 min at 4 °C, and the resulting supernatant was further centrifuged at 14,000 × g for 30 min at 4 °C. The final pellet containing the crude membrane fraction was collected. Cytoplasmic and organelle membranes were further purified and separated by sucrose density gradient centrifugation. To form membrane derived vesicles, the extracted membranes were sonicated for 15 min and then extruded through a 400 nm polycarbonate membrane using an Avestin extruder for 10 cycles. The resulting M1 macrophage membrane nanovesicles were suspended in PBS and stored at 4 °C. The morphology and size of the prepared nanovesicles were characterized by transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and scanning electron microscopy (SEM).

Synthesis of drug loaded pH responsive nanoparticles

Firstly, pH-responsive liposomes composed of DSPE-HYD-mPEG2000 were synthesized. The DSPE-HYD-mPEG2000 material was commercially obtained from Xi'an Ruixi Biotechnology Co., Ltd. Structural characterization of the compound was performed by 1H nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy at 600 MHz. Subsequently, doxorubicin (DOX)-loaded pH-responsive liposomes (designated as pHLP/DOX) were prepared by encapsulating DOX into the DSPE-HYD-mPEG2000 liposomes. Specifically, DOX (200 mg, powder; MedChemExpress) and DSPE-HYD-mPEG2000 were combined at a mass ratio of 1:10, followed by sonication for 10 min. The mixture was then dialyzed using a 1000 Da molecular weight cutoff dialysis bag for 24 h to remove unencapsulated DOX. The morphology and size of the resulting pHLP/DOX nanoparticles were examined by transmission electron microscopy.

Preparation and characterization of MP/DOX

The synthesis of MP/DOX nanoparticles involves three main steps: first, the extraction of M1 macrophage membranes; second, the preparation of drug-loaded pH-responsive nanoparticles (pHLP/DOX); and finally, the fusion of the M1 macrophage membranes with the pHLP/DOX nanoparticles. Specifically, pH-responsive liposomes loaded with DOX were mixed with M1 macrophage-derived membranes at a mass ratio of 1:1 (liposomes to membranes) [44]. The mixture was sonicated for 10 min, filtered through a 220 nm membrane, and then dialyzed against 10 mM PBS using a dialysis bag with a molecular weight cut-off of 2000 Da for 24 h to remove any unencapsulated drug [45]. The resulting MP/DOX nanoparticles, which exhibit pH-dependent tumor targeting, were stored at 4 °C.

The size and morphology of MP/DOX nanoparticles under different pH conditions (pH 7.4, 6.5, and 5.5) were characterized using transmission electron microscopy and scanning electron microscopy. Particle size and zeta potential were measured by dynamic light scattering (DLS). The entrapment efficiency (EE) and drug loading (DL) of MP/DOX nanoparticles were determined by high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC).

Preparation and characterization of EcN-MP/DOX

To begin, a large-scale culture of highly viable Escherichia coli Nissle 1917 (EcN) was prepared. The EcN strain (BNCC361741, BeNa Culture Collection) was grown in Luria-Bertani (LB, BNCC372161) broth under constant shaking at 220 rpm and 37 °C until the logarithmic growth phase was reached [46].

Subsequently, MP/DOX nanoparticles were conjugated to the surface of EcN. Initially, solutions of Sulfo-NHS-Biotin (500 μM) and streptavidin (500 μM) were prepared in 10 mM PBS. 5 mg MP/DOX nanoparticles were combined with 4 mL of 500 μM Sulfo-NHS-Biotin and stirred at room temperature for 1 h. The mixture was centrifuged at 12,000 g for 10 min to remove unbound Sulfo-NHS-Biotin, yielding biotin-labeled MP/DOX (biotin-MP/DOX). A 2 mL suspension of EcN (105 CFU) was incubated at 37 °C, followed by the sequential addition of 1 mL Sulfo-NHS-Biotin (500 μM) and 1 mL streptavidin (500 μM), with 1 h incubation steps after each addition. After centrifugation at 3000 rpm for 10 min, the pellet was resuspended in LB medium to obtain EcN-biotin-streptavidin. This intermediate product was mixed with biotin-MP/DOX and incubated at 37 °C for 1 h. Following another centrifugation step (3000 rpm, 10 min), the final complex designated EcN-MP/DOX was resuspended in LB medium and stored at 4 °C.

The morphologies of both native EcN and the EcN-MP/DOX complex were examined using TEM and SEM. For confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM), macrophage membranes were pre-labeled with the membrane stain DiR (5 μM, TargetMol, TD0085) to produce DiR-MP/DOX, while EcN was stained with the live-bacteria probe SYTO9 (5 μM). Probiotic activity analysis of EcN-MP/DOX

The viability of EcN-MP/DOX was assessed using laser scanning confocal microscopy (LSCM). Bacterial cells were dual-stained with a mixture of SYTO9 and propidium iodide (PI) fluorescent dyes, which distinguish live and dead bacteria based on membrane integrity. SYTO9, which labels all cells, exhibits excitation and emission maxima at 480 nm and 500 nm, respectively. In contrast, PI, which penetrates only membrane-compromised cells, has excitation and emission peaks at 490 nm and 635 nm, respectively. The stained EcN-MP/DOX samples were observed under a Leica SP8 confocal microscope (100× objective) at different time intervals (0 h, 1 h, 2 h, 3 h, 4 h and 6 h) to monitor temporal changes in bacterial viability. In addition, the optical density (OD) of bacterial suspensions, including both native EcN and EcN-MP/DOX formulations, was quantified at 600 nm using a UV-Vis spectrophotometer to evaluate bacterial growth.

Drug release of EcN-MP/DOX

The pH-responsive drug release profile of EcN-MP/DOX micro-robot was evaluated using a dialysis-based method, complemented by characterization via transmission electron microscopy and scanning electron microscopy. The dialysis procedure was conducted according to established protocols [48,49]. Briefly, 5 mL of EcN-MP/DOX micro-robot solution (1 mg/mL) was sealed in a dialysis bag (molecular weight cutoff: 2000 Da) and immersed in release media buffered at different pH values (5.0, 6.5, and 7.4) to simulate various physiological environments. The system was maintained at 37 °C under continuous stirring at 200 rpm for a total duration of 120 h. At predetermined time intervals (0, 1, 2, 4, 6, 8, 10, 12, 24, 48, 72, 96, and 120 h), 20 μL aliquots were withdrawn from the dialysis bag and replaced immediately with an equal volume of fresh PBS to maintain a constant volume. The amount of released doxorubicin (DOX) in each collected sample was quantified using high performance liquid chromatography (HPLC).

Cellular uptake and cellular cytotoxicity of EcN-MP/DOX

To evaluate the cellular uptake behavior of the EcN-MP/DOX micro-robot, MKN45 gastric cancer cells were co-cultured with micro-robot for varying durations (1, 2, 3, 4, and 6 h) and subsequently visualized using laser scanning confocal microscopy. For a more physiologically relevant model, three patient-derived gastric cancer organoid lines (GC-XMMC-Y001, GC-SDZL-SO15, GC-009) was established. The construction process began with extensive washing of human gastric cancer tissue samples, followed by mechanical fragmentation and enzymatic digestion. The resulting cell suspension was then purified to remove cellular debris and erythrocytes. The pelleted tumor cells were resuspended in a minimal volume of PBS or antibiotic-supplemented basal medium. A small aliquot was taken for viability assessment and cell counting before combining the cells with Matrigel (Cat. no. 082755, Xiamen Mogengel, China) on ice at a density ranging from 1 × 105 to 5 × 105 cells/mL. This cell-matrix mixture was carefully pipetted and deposited as a central droplet in each well of a culture plate, ensuring no contact with the well walls. The plate was incubated at 37 °C under 5% CO2 for 20 min to allow gel polymerization. After the matrix solidified, complete culture medium was gently introduced along the side of the well. The organoids were maintained for 3-4 days with regular medium changes before further experimentation. Finally, the mature GC-009 organoids were treated with different drug formulations free DOX, MP/DOX, and EcN-MP/DOX for different times. Cellular uptake and fluorescence distribution were assessed using Z-stack scanning mode on a laser confocal microscope.

Cell cytotoxicity of EcN-MP/DOX

A cell counting kit-8 (CCK-8) assay was employed to evaluate the apoptotic effects induced by different treatment groups (DMEM, DOX, MP/DOX, and EcN-MP/DOX) on MKN45 gastric cancer cells across a range of concentrations for 24 h. To more intuitively assess the tumor-killing capacity of the nanocarriers, three distinct patient-derived gastric cancer organoid models (GC-XMMC-Y001, GC-SDZL-SO15, and GC-009) were established. These organoids were incubated with the same set of treatment groups. Morphological alterations in the tumor organoids following drug exposure for varying durations (6 h, 12 h, 18 h, 24 h, 36 h, and 48 h) were captured using Z-stack scanning mode on a confocal microscope. Furthermore, the cytotoxicity of the treatments on all three gastric cancer organoid lines was quantitatively analyzed via flow cytometry using a cell apoptosis detection kit.

Tumor target experiment in vitro and in vivo

To more intuitively evaluate the tumor-targeting capability of EcN-MP/DOX micro-robot in vitro, three patient-derived gastric cancer organoid lines GC-XMMC-Y001, GC-SDZL-SO15, and GC-009 were constructed as representative models. Each organoid line was incubated for 12 h with the following treatment formulations: free DOX, P/DOX, MP/DOX, and EcN-MP/DOX. The fluorescence intensity within the organoids across the different treatment groups was subsequently visualized and analyzed using Z-stack scanning mode on a laser confocal microscope.

To evaluate the in vivo distribution and tumor-targeting efficacy of the formulations, a subcutaneous xenograft model was established using BALB/c mice (weighing 18 ± 2 g). The animals were randomly allocated into three groups and inoculated with MKN45 cells in the lower right flank region. When the tumor volume reached approximately 300 mm3, treatments from different groups (DOX, EcN, MP/DOX, and EcN-MP/DOX, with EcN administered at 1 × 105 CFU/mL and DOX at 10 mg/kg) were administered via tail vein injection. The mice were then subjected to non-invasive fluorescence imaging at predetermined time points (0 h, 1 h, 2 h, 4 h, 6 h and 12 h) using a small animal live imaging system. The EcN component was labeled with the Cy5.5-NHS fluorescent probe and tracked via Cy5.5 fluorescence imaging (Excitation/emission wavelength is 675/694 nm), while the other three treatment groups were monitored using Cy5-DOX fluorescence (Excitation/emission wavelength is 649/670 nm). After the 12-h imaging time point, the mice were euthanized, and major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lung, kidney, and intestines) along with tumor tissues were excised. The fluorescence intensity in these harvested tissues was further quantified ex vivo using the same imaging system to assess biodistribution and preferential accumulation within the tumor [50].

Anti-tumor effect of EcN-MP/DOX in vivo

A subcutaneous xenograft tumor model was established using BALB/c mice (weighing 18 ± 2 g) inoculated with MKN45 cells in the lower right flank. The mice were randomly assigned to six experimental groups. When tumor volume reached approximately 500 mm3, the animals received tail vein injections of different formulations (saline, DOX, MP/DOX, and EcN-MP/DOX, with EcN administered at 1 × 105 CFU/mL and DOX at 10 mg/kg). Treatments were administered once every two days for a total of eight cycles. Tumor dimensions and mouse body weight were measured after each treatment. Tumor volume was calculated using formula (4) V = (W2 × L)/2 [51], where V represents tumor volume, L indicates the longest diameter, and W denotes the shortest diameter. Following the final administration, the mice were euthanized, and tumor tissues were excised, weighed, and photographed. Additionally, the survival of tumor-bearing mice was monitored over a 60-day period after treatment initiation. To evaluate treatment-induced apoptosis, tumor sections from each group were subjected to TUNEL staining for quantitative analysis of tumor cell death.

Safety evaluation of EcN-MP/DOX in vivo

To evaluate the systemic biocompatibility and potential organ toxicity of the different formulations, BALB/c mice (18 ± 2 g) received tail vein injections of saline, free DOX, or EcN-MP/DOX at an equivalent DOX dosage of 10 mg/kg every two days over a 16-day period. Following the final administration, the mice were euthanized, and blood samples along with major organ tissues (heart, liver, lung, kidney, and spleen) were collected. Serum biochemical analysis was conducted to assess hepatic and renal function using four key indicators: creatinine (CRE), blood urea nitrogen (BUN), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), and aspartate aminotransferase (AST). Elevated levels of these markers are indicative of impaired liver or kidney function. Furthermore, the harvested organs were sectioned and subjected to hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining for histopathological examination to visually evaluate tissue integrity, inflammatory infiltration, and other morphological changes, thereby providing a comprehensive assessment of the biological safety profiles across the treatment groups.

Data analysis

All experiments were performed with at least three biological replicates (independent experiments conducted on different days with separately prepared samples). Technical replicates (e.g., duplicate or triplicate measurements within each biological replicate) were averaged for each biological replicate. Data are presented as mean ± SD. Normality of data distribution was verified using the Shapiro Wilk test before applying parametric tests (unpaired two-tailed Student's t-test). When comparing multiple groups, one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey's post hoc test was used. Statistical significance was set at *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01 and ***P < 0.001.
