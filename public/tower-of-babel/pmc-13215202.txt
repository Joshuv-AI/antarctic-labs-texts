A spin-orbit torque–based key generation system with key concealment and attack detection through irreversible physical changes
Authors: Yan Xu, Wei Duan, Hao Wu, Yaoyuan Wang, Tian Qin, Shuai Zhang, Zhe Guo, Meilin Wan, Min Song, Long You
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The confidentiality of key storage devices determines the trustworthiness of cryptographic systems. Existing attack detection methods typically depend on monitoring external variations of devices, which can be circumvented by attackers. This work presents a unified key generation and protection scheme using spin-orbit torque (SOT) devices. By unifying static and dynamic entropy sources within the same devices, the key generation system functions both as physically unclonable functions and as true random number generators. In idle states, devices retain only random numbers and conceal any extractable key. Illegal key access irreversibly destroys stored random numbers, offering a robust attack detection mechanism. A comparator circuit flags illegal key extraction activity, ensuring that the user can promptly detect and revoke compromised keys. This approach merges key generation, concealment, and detection into a single physical platform, providing a hardware-rooted, unforgeable method for key protection.

INTRODUCTION

Classical cryptography uses cryptographic algorithms and keys to authenticate electronic devices and encrypt or decrypt information (1). In general, all the communication participants have the same secret keys to encryption or decryption for secure authentication and data transmission. Therefore, cryptographic keys are the foundation of modern information security architectures, safeguarding data confidentiality, integrity, and availability. The security of keys directly determines the overall trustworthiness of the information system. Consequently, any illegal access and modification of keys poses a critical threat to system security. Among all security threats, the most concerning is not merely that the attacker has accessed keys, but the user’s unawareness of such access (2–4). The user may continue to use the compromised keys for data encryption, leading to silent eavesdropping, acceptance of forged data as trustworthy, and the inability to trace the source and timing of the breach. To counter such threats, various attack detection mechanisms have been developed to monitor and prevent potential illegal access (5–8). In the event of illegal access to keys, these mechanisms are designed to issue an alert to users, indicating a potential compromise. Users can revoke or disable further use of keys to stop data breaches or malicious activities.

Attack detection mechanisms can generally be classified into software-based and hardware-based methods. Compared to the former method, hardware-based detection enables accurate identification of physical attacks by monitoring environmental parameters (9–12). One hardware-based technique is to use on-chip sensor circuits, which can track physical conditions such as voltage, temperature, and light intensity to detect anomalies indicative of malicious activity (13–16). These approaches are relatively lightweight and have been widely adopted in integrated circuit chips. However, the monitored physical indicators are externally observable and can be forged or manipulated. Another technique involves embedding a top-layer metal mesh into the chip to detect physical tampering attempts (17, 18). Once disrupted, these meshes can trigger intrusion alarms or key erasures. While effective under certain conditions, this approach consumes valuable layout area, incurs high implementation costs, and lacks sufficient spatial resolution. Meanwhile, it also does not protect against attacks from the backside of chips, which are becoming increasingly prevalent due to advances in laser and microscopy techniques (19–21). All these issues highlight a fundamental challenge in present hardware-based attack detection: The reliance on external indicators and circuit-level thresholds makes such approaches vulnerable to evasion or desensitization. To address this challenge, attack detection must rely on inherent physical changes within the key generation and storage devices that are unforgeable and inevitably triggered, rather than on external detection circuits.

In contrast, emerging nonvolatile memory (NVM) technologies such as memristors and spintronic devices exhibit more diverse and tunable changes in their internal physical properties under different excitations (22–24). For example, under the application of set and reset voltages, memristors can form or rupture conductive filaments, resulting in resistive switching between high-resistance and low-resistance states (25–30). Therefore, physically unclonable function (PUF) keys represented by the conductance states of different memristor devices can be dynamically concealed and recovered through set and reset operations. However, because the concealment is based on reversible conductance switching, attackers may retrieve keys and reset devices to the concealed state, leaving no detectable evidence of the attack. Recently, spin-orbit torque (SOT) devices have been proposed and demonstrated to exploit irreversible physical change, including random switching or domain wall motion under excitation currents, for hardware security devices such as true random number generators (TRNGs) and reconfigurable PUFs (31–36).

In this work, we propose a key generation system (KGS) based on the SOT effect, which integrates the functions of TRNGs and PUFs. The dynamic and static dual-entropy system enables dynamic random numbers as well as unique key generation within a unified physical architecture. It introduces a fundamentally different approach to attack detection. Instead of storing keys persistently, the system retains only randomized magnetization states during idle periods, which are unrelated to any valid key. To reconstruct keys, a specific excitation must be applied, which irreversibly alters the initial physical states of SOT devices. Any attempt to access keys leaves a permanent and detectable trace, enabling intrinsic detection of illegal access. By binding key accessibility to unforgeable physical transitions, the system effectively eliminates the risk of covert key extraction.

RESULTS

Intrinsic key concealment and attack detection

In modern hardware security, PUFs and TRNGs are two fundamental security primitives (37–40). PUFs exploit inherent physical variations introduced during manufacturing as the static entropy source to generate unique and unclonable keys. While TRNGs rely on the randomness of physical processes as the dynamic entropy source to produce unpredictable random numbers. In this work, both dynamic entropy for TRNGs and static entropy for PUFs are derived from SOT-induced magnetization switching behaviors. These two types of entropy can be selectively extracted by applying different excitation signals E1 and E2, enabling the on-demand generation of random bits or unique keys, as depicted in Fig. 1A. The dynamic entropy source originates from stochastic magnetization switching. When an in-plane current flows through a heavy metal layer, a spin current is generated and exerts a torque on the adjacent ferromagnetic (FM) layer (41, 42). If in-plane torque is sufficiently strong, then it can rotate the magnetization of the perpendicular magnetic anisotropy (PMA) FM into the in-plane direction. Upon removal of the current, the ferromagnet relaxes from this unstable in-plane state into either the “up” or “down” perpendicular magnetization state, determined by thermal noise. As illustrated in Fig. 1B, applying a current exceeding the switching saturation threshold (i.e., excitation signal E1) enables such stochastic magnetization switching for TRNGs. To convert such stochastic switching into deterministic switching, an external in-plane magnetic field is typically required. This field can be realized via the Oersted field generated by the current Jtop from the top electrode (Fig. 1A) (43, 44). Under a constant in-plane magnetic field, the magnetization reversal depends on the relative magnitude of the applied current Jbottom and the critical switching current Jc, as shown in Fig. 1C. However, due to inevitable process variations, such as interface roughness, material thickness variation, and inhomogeneous magnetization distribution, the critical switching current varies across different devices. Under a fixed in-plane magnetic field and current (excitation signal E2), all devices exhibit stable and unique magnetization states, which can serve as the static entropy source for PUF key generation. The unified physical platform, combined with flexible entropy control, enables the sharing of peripheral circuitry. This not only offers substantial advantages in resource consumption by integrating PUFs and TRNGs but also provides a previously unexplored approach for attack detection.

We leverage the inherent uncontrollability of SOT-induced stochastic magnetization switching to protect PUF keys. As shown in Fig. 1D, if an excitation E1 is applied to an array of SOT devices, then each device undergoes stochastic magnetization switching and retains the random state. To extract static entropy from this array for unique key generation, the excitation E2 must be applied to deterministically produce a device-unique PUF response. However, this process inevitably alters the magnetization states of the devices, thereby destroying the initial random configuration. Because these random states are inherently uncontrollable and physically irreproducible, they cannot be recovered once destroyed. This irreversible nature of state transition enables built-in key concealment and attack detection. As illustrated in Fig. 1E, applying excitation E1 after the PUF key extraction randomizes the magnetization states of the devices. In these randomized states, the devices retain only a set of random bits #1, and no usable PUF key information. If an attacker attempts to extract the key by applying E2, then the initial randomized states will be irreversibly destroyed, erasing the stored random bits #1. Even if the attacker reapplies excitation E1, only new random bits #2 will be generated, and the initial random bits #1 cannot be recovered. Therefore, the user can easily detect whether the key has been accessed by an attacker through checking whether the random bits have changed. This detection mechanism is enabled by the unified entropy architecture, where both the random bits and the unique key originate from the magnetization states. The nonvolatility of these magnetization states ensures that the generated random bits are physically retained in the devices. Any unauthorized access disrupts the retained information, leaving persistent and detectable traces. This is not achievable with conventional PUFs or key storage devices.

Extraction of static and dynamic entropy

To achieve the functions described above, we fabricated SOT-based Hall bar devices with a Ta (5 nm)/CoFeB (1.5 nm)/MgO (1 nm)/Ta (2 nm) layer structure. The central area of the Hall bar is 6 μm by 6 μm, as shown in Fig. 2A. The output is the anomalous Hall resistance (AHR) Rxy, which is proportional to the perpendicular magnetization component of the CoFeB layer with PMA (section S1). The Rxy is obtained by dividing the anomalous Hall voltage measured in the y direction by the current applied in the x direction. In micron-scale devices, multidomain structures cause magnetization to vary continuously instead of switching between two distinct states as in single-domain structures. Consequently, the measured Rxy values represent the sum of multiple domains and are analog rather than digital. We first exploit the stochastic magnetization switching mechanism to realize dynamic responses. In our experiment, we applied a saturation pulse current Js = +15.7 MA/cm2 as the excitation signal E1 to induce stochastic magnetization switching. Unless otherwise specified, the pulse width of currents used in this study is 1 ms. The results shown in Fig. 2B demonstrate that the measured Rxy for one device after each excitation follows a random distribution, suggesting successful stochastic magnetization switching in the CoFeB layer. By performing analog to digital conversion, the sign of Rxy (positive or negative) is mapped to binary “1” and “0” to generate random bits. Statistical results show that 47.3% of the bits are 0 and 52.7% are 1, which is close to an ideal 50/50 distribution, indicating that it is a highly promising dynamic entropy source (Fig. 2C). To evaluate the random number generation performance, we conducted NIST SP800-22 randomness tests (section S2). These results demonstrate that the SOT-based device has excellent statistical randomness.

Next, we demonstrate the implementation of a static response. By applying an in-plane magnetic field Hx along the x direction, the collinear current can deterministically switch the magnetization to up or down direction, and, hence, Rxy would correspondingly be either a positive or negative value. This magnetic field can usually be generated using a top electrode. Here, to simplify device fabrication, we use an external electromagnet instead. Due to inevitable fabrication deviation, the critical switching current Jc under the same magnetic field differs from device to device. As shown in Fig. 2D, the Rxy as a function of current is plotted for two adjacent devices fabricated in the same batch under a +100 Oe in-plane magnetic field. Device #1 exhibits a polarity change in Hall resistance from negative to positive at a current of +6 MA/cm2, whereas device #2 undergoes the same transition at +7 MA/cm2. The two adjacent devices fabricated simultaneously are first reset to a down state (negative Rxy) and then subjected to the same excitation E2 (JM = +6.7 MA/cm2 with Hx = +100 Oe). After that, one device retains down (negative Rxy), whereas the other switches to up state (positive Rxy), as shown in Fig. 2E. This is because one device has a higher Jc1, and the applied current is insufficient to induce switching. In contrast, the applied current exceeds the critical switching current Jc2 of the other device, providing sufficient torque to reverse its magnetization. The critical switching current variation among devices generates stable and repeatable responses, which can serve as static entropy sources for PUFs (section S3). It should be noted that, although no unstable switching was observed during our measurements, a certain degree of switching uncertainty exists within a narrow region near the critical switching current (45, 46). Nevertheless, this region is much smaller than the overall D2D variation. Therefore, by selecting an excitation current density well outside the switching regions of each device, the validity of the PUF entropy source can be reliably ensured in our case. To further eliminate uncertainty, a robust enrollment process and error-correction code strategies are typically used (47). To quantitatively verify the D2D variation, 12 arrays are fabricated under identical process conditions, with each array consisting of 16 Hall bar devices. After an initial reset of all devices, excitation E2 is applied and Rxy values are measured. Figure 2F shows the bitmap obtained from the Rxy after analog-to-digital conversion. We calculate the interchip Hamming distance (HD), which is evaluated by computing the HDs for all pairwise combinations in the 12 arrays and is typically used to evaluate the PUF uniqueness. As shown in Fig. 2G, the normalized interchip HD is 0.51, close to the ideal value of 0.5. Detailed evaluation results of the PUFs and TRNGs are provided in section S2. It is worth noting that our approach remains feasible when using current pulses on the microsecond and even nanoscale timescale (section S4), as well as for nanoscale devices (section S5). These results demonstrate that the SOT-based devices exhibit both high randomness for TRNGs and strong uniqueness for PUFs under different excitation.

Hardware realization of secure key generation

For conventional PUF devices, if an attacker extracts the key during the idle period, then they can restore the device to its initial idle state without leaving any evidence of the attack. If the compromised key continues to be used for encryption, then the attacker can easily decrypt the ciphertext, leading to serious security consequences. Moreover, when nonvolatile devices are used to implement PUFs, the responses remain persistently stored in the devices due to their nonvolatility, unless the devices are reset. This persistent storage may allow attackers to directly read responses, posing a threat to key confidentiality (48, 49). The PUF scheme based on NVM inevitably faces this problem. However, we exploit this nonvolatility and the unified entropy properties to enhance key security. Because both TRNG and PUF responses are derived from magnetization states and can be mutually converted, the PUF response can be revealed only when needed while remaining securely concealed under random magnetization states at all other times. As shown in Fig. 3A, an excitation E3 consisting of one E2 and one E1 is applied to device #1 (Fig. 2D) every 100 s. A reset operation is performed before each E3 to ensure that the device starts from the same initial down state. The AHR of the device is continuously measured throughout the entire process. Each time the excitation is applied, Rxy temporarily transitions to the down state (−3.6 Ω) and then stabilizes at a constant value until the next excitation is applied 100 s later. Although the resistance exhibits the same transient value immediately upon excitation, the final stabilized value differs in each cycle. This behavior arises because the applied E2 pulse induces a stable and repeatable response, whereas the subsequent E1 pulse results in random responses. Therefore, to correctly extract the static entropy, the resistance must be read within a short time interval after the application of E2 and before the application of E1. We apply the excitation E3 to the 16 × 12 device array. As shown in Fig. 3B, the unique key is only available during the active period, and only random bits are present in devices during idle states. Because both the random bits and the unique key are determined by the magnetization states, each key extraction inherently destroys the bits stored during the previous idle period. The applied E1 pulse only generates new random bits and cannot restore the previously stored bits. If an attacker attempts to extract the key from devices during the idle state, then the application of E2 is required, which irreversibly alters the magnetization state and destroys the stored random bits. Compared to resetting or powering off PUFs during idle periods, this method ensures that PUFs enter a new idle state after each key extraction, preventing attackers from erasing evidence of attack by restoring PUFs to the previous state. A detailed analysis of the attack models is provided in section S6.

We design a corresponding KGS. The system integrates an array of dual-entropy SOT-based devices, a custom motherboard, and a microcontroller (section S7). The device array consists of 32 Hall bars, and each read operation produces a 32-bit output. To better satisfy the requirements of practical encryption systems, we performed additional experiments to demonstrate the validity of the 128-bit output (section S8). In our scheme, the static and dynamic entropy sources arise from the interaction between spin current and magnetization with/without an in-plane magnetic field. The interaction has been validated in high-density memory and stochastic computing based on SOT devices (50, 51). Therefore, the proposed concept is readily scalable and holds great promise for large-scale integration. In our SOT device array, the region outside the Hall-bar area is etched down to the insulating silicon substrate. This physical isolation eliminates direct electrical leakage paths, thus preventing the operating currents from spreading laterally to adjacent devices. Moreover, the magnetic coupling in our actual devices, even high-density nanodevices, is too small to affect magnetization switching (section S9). In addition, the device, which exhibits high thermal stability, maintains stable high- and low-resistance states after 106 write cycles, demonstrating qualified endurance and retention performance (section S10). All 32 devices exhibit stability for 4 days at 85°C, which corresponds to an equivalent operating time exceeding 10 years at room temperature (section S10). These results indicate that the devices meet the long-term storage-reliability requirements for practical deployment. However, the critical switching current Jc depends on temperature and exhibits a linear relationship (fig. S12). This phenomenon can be quantitatively explained using a thermal activation model, consistent with previous finding (52). The adaptive bias technique can effectively eliminate the temperature drift, enabling the device to operate stably within the switching window over a wider temperature range (section S10).

The hardware architecture is shown in Fig. 4A. The microcontroller hosts flashed routines for device array write/read operations and controls an electromagnet to generate target in-plane magnetic fields. Although the field is applied globally, it is insufficient to switch the magnetization of unselected devices without excitation current, owing to the strong PMA of the CoFeB layer. Excitation current for the array is sourced from a digital-to-analog converter and voltage-controlled current source circuit. Anomalous Hall voltages are digitized by an analog-to-digital converter, with acquired data transmitted via the microcontroller’s universal asynchronous receiver and transmitter (UART) interface. Figure 4B is a physical photograph of the KGS. By adjusting the current and magnetic field, excitations E1 and E2 can be applied to the devices, enabling the system to generate variable random numbers or reproducible keys. The read current is set to 400 μA (0.8 MA/cm2), which is insufficient to trigger magnetization reversal or disturb the stored state, yet sufficiently to generate millivolt-level Hall voltage signals, thereby ensuring a clear and reliable readout. The system noise is analyzed in detail in section S11. Attackers often crack keys by eavesdropping on outputs or exploiting side-channel attacks when keys are read. However, random numbers and unique keys are read through the same circuitry in the proposed KGS. It is difficult for attackers to determine whether the current readout involves key extraction. The shared readout path also mitigates circuit-level variations caused by functional switching, reducing the observability of side-channel signatures. It is also worth noting that the nanoscale SOT–based devices used in the actual integrated chip exhibit read/write operations on the order of tens of nanoseconds (53, 54). The time window available to attackers is extremely limited, increasing the difficulty of such attacks. This design substantially enhances resistance against attacks during key extraction operations. Consequently, attacks are more likely to occur during long idle periods.

We applied the excitation E3 as the key extraction trigger and allowed the system to continuously perform readout. A D flip-flop stores the KGS output from the previous cycle, which is then compared with the current output (Fig. 4C). Under normal operating status, the unique key can only be retrieved during the active period when the excitation is applied, as shown in Fig. 4D. In the idle period, the output consists of random bits, refreshed after key access. The comparator output is logic 1 only during the active cycle and the following cycle, due to the output changes caused by key extraction. As shown in Fig. 4E, if attackers extract the key during idle period, then the comparator output will change from 0 to 1, alerting the user to illegal access. Attackers may attempt to bypass the comparator or force its output to logic 0 to avoid detection. However, this would also prevent the comparator output from switching to logic 1 during the legitimate active period, alerting the user that the comparator circuit has been tampered with and an attack has occurred (Fig. 4F). Last, even if attackers extract the key using prolonged and sophisticated methods, they still cannot recover the initially stored random bits. This is because, as the key generation module, the system does not include memory-like circuits that allow independent read and write access to each device. The attacker cannot restore the original information through direct writing and must instead rely on excitation E1 to regenerate it. However, even for a short 32-bit value, there are 232 possible random numbers, making such recovery practically infeasible. As shown in Fig. 4G, illegal key extraction can be detected by securely backing up the stored random number and comparing it before chip usage. Therefore, our approach ensures that the attempt to extract the key inevitably leaves physical traces that can be detected. Once the user becomes aware that the key has been compromised, it can be immediately retired from use to prevent further data leakage or potential damage.

Last, we evaluated the power consumption and area of our PUF scheme based on 45-nm complementary metal-oxide semiconductor technology, and the results show that its performance is comparable to, or even superior to, other PUF technologies (section S12). The key advantage of our approach lies in the unique physical mechanism of SOT switching. SOT switching is essentially an electronic process driven by spin angular momentum transfer, rather than atomic migration or phase transitions. This mechanism gives it inherent high endurance and largely avoids resistance drift issues. In many conventional PUF platforms, the underlying state switching is, in principle, reversible. If an attacker perturbs the device state during a readout attempt, then it is possible to subsequently electrically rewrite or restore the state, potentially erasing evidence of the intrusion. By contrast, our approach explicitly exploits the inherent thermodynamic randomness of SOT-driven magnetization dynamics. When a specific excitation is applied to extract the key, the operation necessarily drives the magnetization from a thermally induced stochastic state into a deterministic state, thereby irreversibly destroying the initial entropy associated with the preattack configuration. Because the preattack state is completely random, an attacker cannot deterministically reconstruct or rewind the device to the same random bit pattern after the access attempt. As a result, any unauthorized key access leaves a permanent and detectable physical footprint, enabling an attack-detection mechanism at the device level. This characteristic is typically unattainable in other PUF paradigms, stemming from the unique combination of inherent randomness and state evolution of SOT-based devices. Our scheme is particularly advantageous in operating environments with long idle times, occasional legitimate key access, and the risk of actual physical attacks, such as edge nodes, field-deployed sensors, hardware tokens, and supply chain scenarios.

DISCUSSION

We have reported a unified KGS based on SOT devices that integrate both PUF and TRNG functionalities. Dynamic entropy is derived from a random switching mechanism under zero-field conditions. Static entropy based on deterministic switching originates from significant variations in switching current due to manufacturing process deviation. By applying different excitations, the system extracts either static or dynamic entropy from the same physical platform, enabling the generation of either variable random numbers or unique keys. An intrinsic attack detection mechanism is implemented in the system. Keys are concealed under random magnetization states during idle periods, and any access attempt irreversibly alters these states. This ensures that illegal key extraction leaves detectable physical traces, thereby preventing covert attacks and undetected key compromises. Real-time monitoring designed on the basis of this feature provides an effective method for detecting illegal key access. This approach offers a physical-layer security paradigm by integrating key generation, concealment, and attack detection in a unified and efficient platform.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Sample preparation and electrical measurements

Magnetron sputtering was used to deposit a film structure of Ta (5 nm)/CoFeB (1.5 nm)/MgO (1 nm)/Ta (2 nm) on a thermally oxidized Si substrate at room temperature. The film was then processed into the Hall-bar structure by photolithography and argon-ion milling. The Hall bars contained the entire thin film stack, with the region outside the Hall bars etched down to the insulating Si substrate. The channel width of the Hall devices was 6 μm. For the measurement of AHR, currents were applied by a dc source (Keithley model 6221, Tektronix Inc.). The Hall voltage was measured by a nanovoltmeter (Keithley model 2182A, Tektronix Inc.). A constant low current of 100 μA (0.2 MA/cm2) was applied to read out the Hall resistance. In the system prototype, we used a higher read current of 400 μA (0.8 MA/cm2) for Hall voltage readout. The external magnetic field was generated by a Helmholtz coil driven by a power supply (Model EM5 and Model P7050, East Changing Technologies Inc., Beijing).
