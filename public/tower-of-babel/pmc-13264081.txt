Time-dependent effects of beavers on terrestrial invertebrate biomass and diversity
Authors: Marcus Max Gintberg, Simon Bahrndorff, Line Holm Andersen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryBeaver (Castor spp.) activity can alter habitat structure and hydrology, yet their influence on terrestrial invertebrates remains understudied. We examined the effects of beaver activity on ground-dwelling terrestrial invertebrate biomass and diversity across mid-summer to early autumn and assessed habitat differences between sites with and without beaver activity by analyzing environmental variables related to habitat heterogeneity. We found significant interactions between beaver activity and research dates, suggesting that beaver effects are dynamic and seasonally dependent. Specific invertebrate orders and Coleoptera families were significantly associated with beaver-active sites, indicating that beaver-modified habitats support distinct invertebrate assemblages. Habitat heterogeneity, measured as the normalized difference vegetation index (NDVI), also varied temporally, with a significant interaction between beaver activity and date. These results demonstrate that beavers can enhance invertebrate biodiversity, partly through increasing habitat heterogeneity, and highlight the importance of sampling timing when evaluating how rewilding and keystone species reintroductions affect invertebrate communities.

Introduction

Freshwater ecosystems are important habitats that provide many essential ecosystem services and serve as biodiversity hotspots supporting approximately 9.5% of the earth’s described animal species.1,2,3 These ecosystems encompass a highly diverse assemblage of both aquatic and terrestrial invertebrates, and the riparian zone, which is the interface between land and water, is particularly rich in terrestrial invertebrate communities.1,3,4,5,6 Freshwater ecosystems are subject to increasing anthropogenic pressures including land-use change, pollution, and habitat deterioration, resulting in loss of ecosystem integrity and an accelerated loss of freshwater biodiversity at local and global scale.1,2,5,6 This includes arthropods and it has been estimated that the biomass of arthropods alone has declined by approximately 75% and that nearly one million species are at risk of extinction as a consequence of human activities.7,8

To mitigate biodiversity loss and ecological degradation, conservation strategies such as rewilding and reintroduction of keystone species are increasingly advocated.9,10 The beaver (Castor spp.) is a keystone species whose activities restore natural processes, enhance habitat complexity, and increase ecological resilience within freshwater ecosystems.11,12,13 Beaver activities include dam construction, herbivory, tree-cutting, burrowing in riverbanks, lodge building, and the creation of ponds and channels, which significantly modify freshwater ecosystems.13,14 These modifications lead to increased habitat heterogeneity across time and space and enhance biodiversity of many taxa by providing diverse ecological niches and abundant resources.12,15,16,17,18,19,20 For example, flooding and tree-cutting by beavers generate extensive deadwood resources, which provide habitats for cavity-nesting birds, insects, and fungi that thrive in the decaying wood.21,22,23

Most existing research on invertebrates in beaver wetlands has focused on aquatic or semi-aquatic taxa.11,24,25,26 In contrast, the effects of beaver activity on strictly terrestrial invertebrates remain underexplored.24,26,27 Existing studies have reported mixed responses with regards to the way terrestrial invertebrates respond to beaver activities, something that could depend on the sampling time.27,28,29,30,31

The aim of this study was to widen our knowledge on ground-dwelling terrestrial invertebrates in beaver wetlands. We compare ground-dwelling invertebrate biomass and diversity between areas with and without beaver with continuous sampling across July, August, and September to capture temporal variations. In order to investigate the underlying environmental parameters shaping the invertebrate community in areas with and without beaver activity, we further sampled parameters linked to habitat heterogeneity (including vegetation diversity, litter depth, bare soil cover, and normalized difference vegetation index [NDVI]). As invertebrate biomass and diversity respond to habitat disturbance, habitat heterogeneity, and structural complexity, and since beaver engineering is known to enhance habitat heterogeneity,7,16,17,32,33,34,35 we hypothesized that invertebrate biomass and diversity would be higher in beaver wetlands, but that these effects would vary temporally.32

Diversity of terrestrial invertebrates at the order level

A total of 40,675 invertebrates were identified to the order level, representing 34 orders, along with two suborders within Diptera and three suborders within Hemiptera. Rarefaction curves indicated that sampling effort was sufficient and that most orders present in the environment were captured (Figure S4). Five orders were found exclusively at beaver sites and two at non-beaver sites; however, all in low abundance (Table S1 and Figure 2). The most abundant invertebrates were soil- and litter-dwelling taxa, primarily Acari and Collembola, which made up over 65% of the total catch. Among insects, the most abundant orders were Diptera and Coleoptera. Diptera were more numerous at beaver sites (n = 3,275) than at non-beaver sites (n = 2,815). Coleoptera were also more common at beaver sites (n = 1,449) compared to non-beaver sites (n = 717) (Table S1 and Figure 2), a difference further supported by the indicator species analysis, which identified Coleoptera as significantly associated with beaver sites (indicator species analysis, p < 0.05). Diversity of terrestrial Coleoptera families

A total of 2,162 Coleoptera individuals were identified to the family level, representing 19 families, with 15 found at beaver sites and 16 at non-beaver sites. Rarefaction curves indicated that sampling was sufficient (Figure S7). There was no significant difference between beaver and non-beaver sites in bare soil coverage (non-parametric Kruskal-Wallis test, p > 0.05), plant species richness (parametric t test, p > 0.05), diversity indices (parametric t test, p > 0.05), or Ellenberg-type indicator values (non-parametric Kruskal-Wallis test, p > 0.05) (Figure S8).

A total of 98 coarse woody debris (CWD) pieces were recorded, 51 at beaver sites and 47 at non-beaver sites. No CWD was found at two of the non-beaver sites. The most common species of CWD were Betula spp. (n = 26), Picea spp. (n = 25), and Pinus spp. (n = 24). At beaver sites, 47.1% of CWD was deciduous and 52.9% coniferous, with most CWD downed (84.3%). In contrast, at non-beaver sites, 38.3% was deciduous and 61.7% coniferous, with 46.8% standing and 53.2% downed. Statistical analysis revealed a significant difference in median CWD between beaver and non-beaver sites (non-parametric Kruskal-Wallis test, p < 0.01), and CWD decay class (non-parametric Kruskal-Wallis test, p < 0.05), with beaver sites showing a more even distribution across decay classes, whereas non-beaver sites predominantly contained highly degraded wood (Figure 6). No significant differences were detected between beaver and non-beaver sites in CWD coverage, type, or position (non-parametric Kruskal-Wallis test, p > 0.05).

Normalized difference vegetation index

There was a significant effect of date on normalized difference vegetation index (NDVI) (GLMM, p < 0.001) and a significant interaction between beaver activity and date (GLMM, p < 0.05) (Table S3 and Figure S9). We found no overall difference in NDVI between beaver and non-beaver sites (GLMM, p > 0.05) but found significant temporal variation (Figure S10).

Discussion

In this study, we found significant interactions between beaver activity and date on the biomass and diversity of ground-dwelling terrestrial invertebrates, suggesting that beaver effects are dynamic and vary temporally and highlighting the importance of short-term temporal dynamics in shaping the invertebrate community.

Specific invertebrate orders and Coleoptera families were significantly associated with beaver sites, indicating that beaver-modified habitats support distinct invertebrate assemblages. In particular, soil-dwelling decomposers sensitive to moisture, as well as above-ground taxa linked to plant diversity, responded positively to beaver activity. Notably, NDVI also varied over time, with a significant interaction between beaver activity and date, suggesting that changes in habitat heterogeneity may help explain temporal variation in invertebrate biomass and diversity. Below, we explore the results and interactions between invertebrate biomass, diversity, and environmental factors in greater detail. Overall, our findings suggest that beavers can influence community composition of terrestrial ground-dwelling species over time. This provides new insight into beavers’ ecological role beyond aquatic systems. Given the increasing use of beavers in rewilding and habitat restoration, understanding these patterns is critical for effective ecosystem management.

Although our sampling was limited to July–September, this period still encompasses an ecologically meaningful seasonal transition in temperate Denmark. Insect activity generally peak in mid-summer, when temperatures and vegetative productivity are highest, and begin to decline as day length shortens and resources diminish toward early autumn.36,37,38,39,40,41 Thus, even within this relatively narrow window, clear phenological shifts occur that can influence invertebrate responses to habitat conditions as shown by the interaction between beaver activity and sampling date.

Beavers’ effect on terrestrial invertebrate biomass

Contrary to our expectation, no overall significant difference in invertebrate biomass was observed between beaver and non-beaver sites. However, we found a significant effect of date and a significant interaction between beaver activity and date where biomass fluctuated significantly at beaver sites across mid-summer and early autumn, while remaining stable at non-beaver sites (Figures 1 and S3). These results are consistent with findings by Andersen et al.,28 who also reported temporal variation but no invertebrate biomass differences between sites with and without beaver activity.

Temporal fluctuations could be explained by seasonal changes in beaver behavior. In early autumn, beavers increase logging activity to prepare winter food caches,14 which enhances habitat complexity through greater litter accumulation and input of woody debris. In our study, litter depth increased significantly at beaver sites in autumn, likely improving conditions for detritivores, shelter-seeking invertebrates, and their predators, and thereby contributing to the higher biomass in early September. This interpretation aligns with other studies linking litter accumulation to elevated invertebrate abundance and biomass in temperate ecosystems.42,43

In temperate regions, invertebrate biomass typically peaks during the summer due to warmer temperatures and increased productivity.36,44 We saw no such pattern. During our year of study, summer temperatures were lower and precipitation higher than average while September was warmer and drier.45 Increased rainfall during summer may have led to flooding and elevated soil saturation, particularly at beaver sites where water is more effectively retained due to damming.46 Such conditions can reduce oxygen levels and physically disturb the soil and litter layer, negatively impacting invertebrate activity and survival.42,47

Previous research has documented indirect effects of beaver activity on terrestrial invertebrates, primarily via increased aquatic insect emergence or elevated activity of insectivores. For instance, beaver ponds have been linked to increased bat activity,48 greater abundance of insectivorous waterbirds,49 and enhanced aquatic insect emergence.11,50,51 However, the strength of these effects seems to vary depending on local hydrology, vegetation structure, and beaver density.52,53,54 Our findings support the idea that beaver activity can influence terrestrial invertebrate biomass but highlight that such effects are modulated by local environmental conditions and temporal and seasonal variation.

Beavers’ effects on the diversity of terrestrial invertebrates

The effects of beaver activity on terrestrial invertebrates were order- and family-specific, with several taxa significantly associated with beaver sites. This suggests that beaver-modified habitats can enhance invertebrate diversity at the landscape scale.32,55 Additionally, we observed a significant temporal effect, as well as a significant interaction between beaver activity and date on order-level diversity. For Coleoptera, a significant temporal effect was also detected, though limited to species richness. Together, these results indicate that the influence of beaver activity on invertebrate communities depends on variation across time.

Klosterheden Plantage is a large Natura 2000 area characterized by diverse habitat types and high baseline biodiversity.56 In such settings, the additional ecological influence of a keystone species may be less pronounced.53 This could potentially explain the lack of overall differences between beaver and non-beaver sites with regards to invertebrate biomass and diversity. Habitat characteristics in our study were also largely similar between beaver and non-beaver sites, except for litter depth, CWD quantity, and decay class. This baseline similarity may further explain the limited differences in invertebrate richness and diversity.

Beaver activity increases soil saturation and the input of organic matter (e.g., leaf litter and woody debris), thereby enhancing conditions favorable to invertebrates that thrive in moist, nutrient-rich soil.14,57 We found that Poduromorpha was significantly associated with beaver sites, suggesting a preference for the wetter, organic-rich conditions found at these sites. Conversely, Entomobryomorpha and Mesostigmata were more abundant at non-beaver sites, possibly reflecting lower tolerance for saturated or hypoxic soils.26,42,58

Sternorrhyncha and Heteroptera were significantly more abundant at beaver sites, likely due to the beavers’ influence on plant communities. Beaver activity has been shown to increase plant species richness and diversity,24,25,59 which may in turn support greater hemipteran abundance.60,61 Our data also showed slightly higher median plant richness and Shannon diversity at beaver sites (Figure S8). In contrast, the increase in vegetation heterogeneity may have negatively affected cicadas and hoppers (Auchenorrhyncha), which were more abundant at non-beaver sites (Figure 2) and are known to prefer more homogeneous plant structures.61

Orazi et al.27 identified distinct Coleoptera assemblages in beaver wetlands but, similar to our findings, reported no significant differences in overall richness or diversity. Where other studies have found more deadwood and more saproxylic Coleoptera species in beaver wetlands compared to non-beaver wetlands,21,23,31,58,62 we saw no such patterns.

Geotrupidae, which favor moist, nutrient-rich soils and decomposing organic matter,63,64 likely benefited from the litter input and increased soil moisture induced by beaver activities. Beaver activity has also been found to increase the presence of large herbivores,22,65,66 thereby enhancing dung availability, which is an important resource for many Geotrupidae species.63,64 Carabidae are sensitivity to habitat structure and abiotic conditions67,68 and probably benefited from the greater microhabitat complexity associated with beaver activity. Hydrophilidae, though primarily aquatic, were also more abundant at beaver sites, consistent with findings that variable water depths and vegetation structure in beaver wetlands enhance aquatic Coleoptera richness.69

Using higher-taxon surrogates for species richness is practical and can be used to detect broad ecological patterns.70,71,72 However, this coarse taxonomic resolution may obscure fine-scale ecological variation and reduce sensitivity to detecting environmental change. Although Coleoptera families have been shown to reflect diversity patterns in some contexts,32,55 this level of taxonomic resolution may still limit the ability to detect the full ecological effects of beaver activity.

Limitations of the study

This study was conducted over a three-month period from mid-summer to early autumn of 2023. The absence of spring and late-autumn sampling limits the ability to capture full seasonal dynamics, and longer sampling periods would help assess whether the observed patterns persist across seasons. Because the summer of 2023 was wetter than average, the results reflect invertebrate responses during wet climatic conditions. Invertebrates were identified to order or family level rather than species level, which may obscure finer taxonomic responses to beaver activity. Finally, because sampling was restricted to a single region in Denmark, broader geographic replication will be needed to assess the generality of these findings across Europe.

Method details

Study area

This study was conducted in Klosterheden Plantage, Denmark (56°29′ N, 8°21′ E), from July 7 to September 27, 2023. Klosterheden (6400 ha) consists of a mosaic of temperate woodlands, lakes, valleys, and riparian zones. A 759-hectare section of the area is designated as a Natura 2000 protected site, aimed at preserving priority habitats and species of conservation concern.56,73 In 1999, 18 Eurasian beavers (Castor fiber) were reintroduced to six sites within Klosterheden to support species recovery and restore associated key ecological functions. These beavers were sourced from a population in Elben, Germany,73 and have since expanded beyond Klosterheden, with the population now exceeding 270 individuals.56,74

Sampling design and data collection

To assess the impact of beavers on terrestrial invertebrates and habitat heterogeneity, ten sites were sampled: five sites with beaver activity (Beaver site) and five control sites without beaver activity (Non-beaver site) (Figure S1). Beaver and non-beaver sites were identified on orthophotos, through field work, and dialog with the local Danish Nature Agency. Beaver activity was defined by the presence of beaver dams, lodges, burrows, gnawed stumps, deadwood, and scrub vegetation cut by beavers. It was unknown how long each site had been occupied by beavers.

Invertebrates were sampled using pitfall traps. At each of the 10 sampling sites, five pitfall stations were randomly positioned on dry ground using QGIS (v. 3.36. 0-Maidenhead).75 Each pitfall station contained three pitfall traps arranged in a triangular formation with 5 m spacing (Figure S2),76 totaling 15 traps per sampling site. Pitfall stations were randomly placed across the full extent of each site and spaced at least 25 m apart, providing a non-overlapping catchment area of roughly 625 m2 and ensuring coverage of all habitat types while reducing bias toward areas of high or low beaver activity. This area exceeds the typical home range of many ground-dwelling invertebrates77,78 and is larger than the zone directly influenced by beaver foraging, which generally extends 20–50 m from the stream but may reach up to 200 m when beavers create extended channel systems.13,14,79

Traps consisted of two stacked plastic jars (Ø 85 mm): a larger jar filled with concrete to prevent flotation in waterlogged areas and a smaller jar containing a saltwater-dish soap solution to reduce surface tension and preserve specimens.76 Traps were emptied every 10 days during the sampling period. In the laboratory, samples were sieved using a 200 μm mesh to remove fine debris, while larger bycatch was removed manually. The contents of the three traps at each pitfall station were then pooled into a single container (hereafter referred to as a pitfall station) and preserved in 70% ethanol for further analysis.

Biomass of terrestrial invertebrates

The biomass of the 400 pitfall stations were estimated following the methodology of Hallmann et al.7 with modifications by Andersen et al.32 The ethanol in each sample was replaced in order to ensure a standardized concentration of 70%, which was verified after 24 h. Before weighing, samples were poured through a 200 μm laboratory sieve positioned at a 30° angle above a collection container and allowed to drain until the drip interval reached 10 s. Biomass was then measured on a RADWAG PS 4500/C/2 scale (±0.01 g), and measurement precision was assessed by reweighing two randomly selected pitfall stations ten times each, yielding a mean deviation <0.2 g.80

Diversity of terrestrial invertebrates

A subset consisting of 90 pitfall stations were sorted to order- and family level for Coleoptera. The 90 samples consisted of 30 randomly selected pitfall stations from each of three dates (July 19, August 28, and September 27). The diversity of terrestrial invertebrates was assessed at the order level, and at the family level for beetles (Coleoptera). Order-level data were used as a proxy for broader biodiversity patterns,72,81,82,83 and Coleoptera was used as bioindicators due to their sensitivity to environmental change.84,85

Invertebrates were identified to the order level using Sømme86 with additional keys for springtails (Collembola),87 mites (Acari),88 and terrestrial snails and slugs (Gastropoda).89 Furthermore, true flies (Diptera) and true bugs (Hemiptera) were sorted into suborders, while beetles (Coleoptera) were identified to the family level using Marshall.63 Only intact adult specimens were identified, while nymphs and larvae were excluded.

Plant community and site characteristics

Vegetation coverage was recorded in July using the Braun-Blanquet scale90 within the triangular area encompassed by the pitfall stations. Percent cover for each species was estimated following Heard and Channon,91 using a modified scoring system from Gehrig et al.92 in which the Braun–Blanquet classes were assigned the following numeric values for analysis: 0.1 for N (1–10 individuals), 0.5 for T (very sparse cover, <5%), 1 for class 1 (<5% cover), 2 for class 2 (5–25%), 3 for class 3 (25–50%), 4 for class 4 (50–75%), and 5 for class 5 (>75%).92

Plants were identified to the species level using Frederiksen et al.,93 with the exception of sedges that were classified at the genus level. Ellenberg-type indicator values, which are used as bioindicators of the environment, were noted for light, temperature, moisture, soil reaction and nutrients, and site means were used to provide insights into the environmental conditions at each site.94

The coverage of bare soil was measured in July. Patches were delineated into rectangular sections and measured with a measuring tape for precise area calculation, and total coverage was expressed as a percentage of the entire site. This method was adapted from Fredshavn et al.,95 but applied across the entire site rather than within a fixed radius.

Litter depth was measured at each pitfall trap by inserting a ruler vertically through the litter layer and recording the distance from the surface to the mineral soil.96 To capture seasonal variation, measurements were conducted in July and at the end of September.

Deadwood was recorded as coarse woody debris (CWD) following the protocol of Fredshavn et al.,97 with the inclusion of logs ≥10 cm in diameter to account for smaller microhabitats.98 For each piece of CWD, length and diameter at breast height (DBH; approximately 1.3 m) were measured and classified by position (standing or downed), tree genus, and decay class. CWD volume was calculated using the truncated cone formula.97

Normalized difference vegetation index (NDVI)

Vegetation productivity was assessed using the Normalized Difference Vegetation Index (NDVI). NDVI was calculated in QGIS using Sentinel-2 satellite imagery obtained via the Copernicus Open Access Hub.99 Images from four cloud-free dates between July and September of 2023, were downloaded and analyzed at a spatial resolution of 10 m. NDVI was calculated using the raster calculator in QGIS with the following formula:NDVI=NIR[Band8]−RED[Band4]NIR[Band8]+RED[Band4]where NIR [Band 8] and RED [Band 4] represent the near-infrared and red reflectance, respectively. Raster cells were converted to points and intersected with site polygons to extract NDVI values. Analyses followed general guidelines from Tempa et al.100

Quantification and statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted in RStudio (v. 2025.09.2 + 418).101 Prior to analysis, the data were tested for normality using the Shapiro-Wilk test, and based on the results, either parametric or non-parametric methods were applied.

Invertebrate biomass model

Generalized linear mixed modeling (GLMM) was used to assess the effect of beaver activity on invertebrate biomass.102 The GLMMs were computed using the r package lme4.103 Beaver activity and date were treated as fixed factors, while sampling site and pitfall station numbers were included as random factors to account for site-level variation. Pitfall station numbers were treated as nested random effects, as each station was uniquely associated with a specific site. A log-link was incorporated into the model as this distribution provided the best fit to the biomass data.104 Differences in invertebrate biomass between beaver and non-beaver sites were assessed using estimated marginal means from the emmeans package.105

Diversity calculations and diversity models

Diversity was calculated using Hill numbers for rarefied species richness (q = 0), the exponential Shannon entropy (hereafter called the Shannon diversity) (q = 1), and the inverse Simpson concentration (hereafter called Simpson’s diversity) (q = 2), using the iNEXT package.106,107 Given that sample sizes differed across sites, rarefication was applied to standardize the data, ensuring appropriate comparisons between sites.106 The rarefication endpoint was set to twice the minimum abundance, so that samples below this threshold were extrapolated, while samples exceeding this value were interpolated. Linear mixed models (LMMs) were used to assess the effect of beaver activity on species richness and diversity indices. For species richness and Simpson’s diversity at the Coleoptera family level, where assumptions of normality were not met, GLMMs with a lognormal distribution were applied instead. In the models, beaver activity and date were treated as fixed factors, while sampling site was included as a random factor to account for site-level variation. Differences in diversity between beaver and non-beaver sites were assessed using estimated marginal means.

The R package indicspecies108 was used to identify invertebrate orders and Coleoptera families associated with beaver and non-beaver sites.

Vegetation and site differences

Plant community and site characteristics were analyzed using non-parametric Kruskal-Wallis tests. Differences between beaver and non-beaver sites were tested for vegetation cover, Ellenberg-type indicator values, bare soil, litter depth, and CWD. Additionally, ratios were calculated to compare CWD position (standing/downed) and CWD type (deciduous/coniferous) across sites. Vegetation diversity was assessed using Hill numbers, where vegetation cover was used as a measure of relative abundance.109 Differences in vegetation diversity between Beaver and Non-beaver sites were analyzed using t-tests.

NDVI model

To examine the effect of beaver activity on NDVI, GLMM was applied using the glmmTMB package.110 Beaver activity and date were included as fixed factors, with sampling site as a random factor to account for site-specific variation. A beta distribution was chosen, as it provided the best fit to the data and is well-suited for modeling variables bounded between 0 and 1, such as NDVI.111
