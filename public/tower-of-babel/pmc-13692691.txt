Completion, publication, and outcome reporting of registered randomized trials in abdominal surgery: registry-based cohort study
Authors: Klaudia Narbekovas, Christoph Kuemmerli, Sandro Huegli, Jasper P Sijberden, Lukas W Widmer, Oliver Strobel, Max F Stauffer, Ethan B Ludmir, Carl-Stephan Leonhardt
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAbdominal surgery poses unique challenges to the conduct of randomized clinical trials (RCTs). However, trial discontinuation, non-publication, and selective reporting may result in an incomplete picture of the available evidence base. This study assessed the trajectories of RCTs in abdominal surgery from trial registration to the publication and reporting of results.MethodsRCTs in adult abdominal surgery were identified through a systematic search of ClinicalTrials.gov, the EU Clinical Trials Register, and the International Clinical Trials Registry Platform from database inception until 31 January 2018. Corresponding trial publications were identified by searching PubMed, Google Scholar, and Google. Selective reporting was evaluated by examining consistency of primary endpoints and reported P values in published trials.ResultsAfter screening 2654 registry records, 295 trials were deemed eligible for inclusion. Among these, 160 (54%) were reported as completed in trial registries. An elective non-cancer trial population was associated with lower odds of non-completion (odds ratio (OR) 0.46; 95% c.i. 0.27 to 0.78), whereas trials with no reported funding had higher odds of non-completion compared with funded trials (OR 2.05; 95% c.i. 1.16 to 3.66). Among completed trials, a corresponding publication was identified for 119 trials (74%). Completed superiority trials with added or modified primary endpoints were more likely to report P values < 0.050 than primary endpoint-unmodified trials (OR 4.28; 95% c.i. 2.23 to 8.22).ConclusionNearly half of registered trials are not reported as completed, and a significant proportion of completed trials are not published. Among published trials, discrepancies between registered and published primary outcomes are common. This is consistent with possible selective outcome reporting and evidentiary bias in abdominal surgery, with implications for both guidelines and meta-analytical work.Keywords: trial registration, trial completion, non-publication, dissemination bias

Introduction

Randomized clinical trials (RCTs) present the highest level of evidence in clinical research and, by extension, systematic reviews and meta-analyses of RCTs1. However, abdominal surgery presents unique challenges in conducting RCTs, including low interest from commercial sponsors, difficulties in selecting an appropriate control group, and the frequent lack of clinical equipoise2. In addition, surgeons’ experience and learning-curve effects may compromise equipoise and distort trial comparison3. Similarly, suboptimal control arms because of less experienced surgeons or learning-curve effects may lead to poorer outcomes in the control group and thus overestimates of the apparent benefit of the experimental intervention4. It has been estimated that less than one-quarter of surgical interventions are based upon evidence from RCTs, compared with more than half in internal medicine5. Furthermore, logistical and standardization challenges further limit equipoise, particularly because surgical techniques are often adopted during an earlier IDEAL (Idea, Development, Exploration, Assessment and Long-term study) stage than RCTs6–8.

In addition to the intrinsic difficulties of conducting RCTs in surgery, poor accrual is a leading reason for early termination9. Notably, data indicate that many reasons for recruitment failure are preventable10. Importantly, when conducting systematic reviews and meta-analyses, unpublished studies remain ‘hidden’ from investigators and lead to an incomplete picture of the evidence11. Studies indicate that published trials represent a biased subset of all conducted studies, particularly if statistically significant or positive results are more likely to be published, a phenomenon known as dissemination bias12,13. However, dissemination bias extends beyond the selective publication of statistically significant or positive results and includes selective reporting by study authors, non-publication, and selective inclusion of studies in evidence syntheses, each of which may alter the available evidence base and lead to biased effect estimates in meta-analyses, overestimating the true effect size11,12.

To better understand this phenomenon, the complete trajectories of RCTs from registration to publication and the reporting of results need to be considered, because this represents a more comprehensive picture. Previous work has focused on select components of these pathways14,15. The aim of the present study was to examine the trajectories of registered RCTs in abdominal surgery from registration to publication, including trial completion, non-publication, and discrepancies between registered and published primary outcomes

Methods

This study was conducted in accordance with the PRISMA 2020 guidelines16 and was registered with PROSPERO (CRD42024589095), with initial registration on 10 September 2024 and last updated on 21 September 2024. The study was conducted following a predefined protocol. Deviations from the protocol are indicated as post hoc analyses.

Data sources and search strategy

The search strategy was developed using the SDMO (Studies, Data, Methods, Outcomes) framework. A systematic search using the search strings presented in Appendix S1 was conducted in ClinicalTrials.gov, the EU Clinical Trials Register (EUCTR), and the International Clinical Trials Registry Platform (ICTRP) on 15 November 2024. In addition, PROSPERO was searched for similar systematic review protocols that were ongoing or recently completed. The search was limited to English language publications and studies in human adult subjects.

Data management and screening procedures

All citations identified were exported to EndNote version 21.5 (Clarivate, Philadelphia, PA, USA) with the automatic removal of duplicates. Subsequently, references were exported in RIS format and uploaded to Covidence (Veritas Health Innovation, Melbourne, Victoria, Australia) for screening, where deduplication was performed. The search was last updated in December 2025.

Screening and data extraction

All screening procedures were performed by at least two independent reviewers (C.-S.L., K.N., M.F.S., C.K., S.H., J.P.S., and L.W.W.) based on the defined inclusion and exclusion criteria. In the event of differing decisions, a third reviewer was consulted to reach a consensus. Data were extracted into a dedicated case report form. All excluded citations were given a reason for exclusion. Full-text analysis was conducted by all reviewers, with each text being assessed by at least two reviewers. All the extracted information was checked for accuracy by C.-S.L., C.K., and K.N. Any conflicts were resolved by consensus.

Identification of publications

Registry entries were reviewed to identify linked publications. If none were listed, independent literature searches were conducted in PubMed using the National Clinical Trial identifier, study titles, investigator names, and relevant keywords, with additional searches in Google Scholar and Google when necessary. Publications were matched to trials by comparing registry and manuscript details. When no publication was identified, study authors were contacted for clarification, with a maximum of two contact attempts via email. Trials were classified as published if results were reported in a peer-reviewed manuscript and unpublished otherwise.

Data definitions and characterization

Data elements were defined according to the ClinicalTrials.gov glossary of common site terms and the protocol data element definitions17. The study population was categorized as cancer patients, non-cancer patients, emergency surgery patients, and mixed populations based on eligibility criteria and trial descriptions. Study design was classified as superiority and non-inferiority/equivalence according to the primary hypothesis specified in the registry.

Funding source was determined from listed sponsors and collaborators and categorized as government, industry, academic, multiple, or none.

Trial status was recorded as reported in ClinicalTrials.gov and categorized as completed, recruiting, active, not yet recruiting, terminated, withdrawn, and unknown. For the regression analysis, trial status was summarized as completed versus non-completed, with the latter encompassing the categories terminated and unknown. The categories active, not recruiting, and withdrawn were excluded for this analysis considering the study question. As a post hoc sensitivity analysis based on reviewers’ comments, the category unknown was excluded from the non-completed trials, which therefore only encompassed trials listed as terminated.

Publications were considered sufficiently detailed if they reported a clearly defined primary outcome or provided an effect estimate with corresponding confidence intervals. In the event of missing confidence intervals, the confidence intervals were calculated from P values and reported effect size18. If no reported primary endpoint could be identified in the published study, the study was excluded.

Reasons for trial discontinuation were extracted from ClinicalTrials.gov records and supplemented by email correspondence with study investigators when information was missing or unclear. Discontinuation reasons were grouped into prespecified categories, including lack of funding, difficulty with patient recruitment, clinical factors and unspecified reasons.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis followed a statistical analysis plan as outlined in the study protocol. To evaluate associations between trial characteristics and trial non-completion in initiated registered trials, binary logistic regression models were fitted, with trial non-completion as the dependent variable. Modelling followed a descriptive purpose19. Based on previous studies and domain knowledge, the following variables were considered in the multivariable logistic regression model: planned sample size, feasibility status (yes/no), the number of trial sites (multicentre versus single centre), population type (elective non-cancer, emergency, or mixed), and funding source (academic, government, industry, multiple, or none). Multivariable model building was performed using backward stepwise selection based on the Akaike information criterion. Odds ratios (ORs) with their corresponding 95% confidence interval (c.i.) were reported for univariable and multivariable analysis. For the post hoc sensitivity analysis excluding trials with status unknown, Firth logistic regression was used because of almost complete separation due to the small sample size and the unbalanced number of events20. Post hoc sensitivity analyses were performed to assess temporal changes over time of the main analysis results. Again, Firth logistic regression was used because of the small sample size.

In addition, P values from primary endpoints were extracted from published studies in a post hoc deviation from the study protocol. Primary endpoints were categorized as added/modified versus registry consistent. Added/modified endpoints were considered endpoints that were not reported in the registry or deviated from the registered endpoint as judged by two reviewers independently (M.F.S., C.-S.L.), with disagreements resolved by discussion. Cohen’s kappa was used to indicate agreement with a value of 0.77. Only the last endpoint documented in the registry was considered. P values that were not reported with a precise estimate were excluded. Generalized estimating equations with exchangeable working correlation were used to test the association between P values of primary endpoints (added/modified versus registry consistent) and statistical significance (significant versus non-significant) to account for clustering21. Some studies included more than one primary endpoint and therefore more than one P value; therefore, P values cannot be assumed to be independent. Thus, these results, and the sensitivity analysis, need to be considered exploratory.

All analyses were performed using R (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria; https://www.R-project.org/) using the RStudio interface Version 2025.09.2+418 (Posit Software, PBC, Boston, MA, USA). Two-sided P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Trial characteristics

The initial registry search identified 7248 RCTs in surgery, of which 2654 focused on abdominal surgery (Fig. 1). Subsequently, 311 trials met the inclusion criteria. Of these, 16 trials were excluded because they were still recruiting (12), active but not recruiting (2), or withdrawn (2). Finally, 295 trial records were included in the analysis (Table S1). Most trials were conducted in colorectal surgery (104, 35.3%), followed by hepatopancreatobiliary (55, 18.6%) and upper gastrointestinal (51, 17.3%) surgery (Table S2). The most common trial design was superiority (249, 84.4%), followed by non-inferiority (35, 11.9%) and equivalence (n = 11, 3.7%) trials.

Trial completion and predictors

In all, 160 trials (54.2%) were indicated as completed, whereas 115 (39.0%) had an unknown status and 20 (6.8%) were prematurely terminated (Table 1). Overall, reasons for discontinuation could be identified for 23 of 135 non-completed trials (17.0%). Among these trials, reported recruitment failure was the most frequently reported reason (12, 52%; Table S3). In univariable analysis, trials involving an elective non-cancer population were less likely to be discontinued (OR 0.38; 95% c.i. 0.23 to 0.63), whereas trials without reported funding were more likely to be discontinued (OR 2.14; 95% c.i. 1.23 to 3.77; Table S4). After adjustment for covariates, elective non-cancer trials had lower odds of non-completion (OR 0.46; 95% c.i. 0.27 to 0.78), whereas trials without reported funding had higher odds of non-completion (OR 2.05; 95% c.i. 1.16 to 3.66; Fig. 2). A post hoc sensitivity analysis excluding trial status unknown from non-completed trials is presented in Table S5. Similarly, sensitivity analysis differentiating between studies registered before the year 2010 versus studies registered in 2010 and afterwards is presented in Tables S6 and S7.

Trial publication and reporting of results

Among the 160 completed trials, a corresponding publication could be identified for 119 (74.4%), with a median time from registration to publication of 51 (interquartile range (i.q.r.) 26–80) months (Fig. 3 and Fig. S1). This varied across surgical subspecialties, ranging from a median of 42 (i.q.r. 24–79) months in colorectal surgery to 74 (i.q.r. 46–97) months in hernia surgery. Time to publication also differed according to funding source, with shorter median times observed among academically funded trials than among industry-funded and government-funded trials (41 (i.q.r. 24–74) versus 65 (i.q.r. 44–76) and 80 (i.q.r. 55–92) months, respectively; Tables S8 and S9). Among 295 registered trials, 175 (59.3%) were retrospectively registered, defined as registration occurring after enrolment of the first participant, whereas 120 (40.7%) were prospectively registered.

As an exploratory analysis, P values were extracted from all primary endpoints for completed trials and corresponding publications. In all, 148 (57.6%) of 257 P values were reported as below a nominal significance threshold of 0.050 (Fig. S2). Published primary endpoints that were added or modified relative to the trial registry were more frequently reported with P values below 0.050 than registry-consistent primary endpoints (OR 4.28; 95% c.i. 2.23 to 8.22; Table S10). Results were similar when all trials with an identifiable publication were included, independent of trial registry status (Fig. S3).

Discussion

This study demonstrated that approximately half of registered RCTs in abdominal surgery are completed; among these, a corresponding publication could be identified for only 74.4%. Furthermore, among the published RCTs with a superiority design, P values showed a peak below a nominal significance threshold of 0.050. This study examined the entire pathway from registration through publication to the reporting of results, thereby providing a more comprehensive picture of dissemination bias in the field of abdominal surgery.

Dissemination bias not only distorts the results of systematic reviews and meta-analyses but also raises serious ethical concerns because informed consent is provided under the assumption that trial participation will result in findings being made available and possibly benefiting others.

In this study, both reported funding and type of trial population were associated with trial non-completion. These findings are consistent with previous studies22,23 in adult and paediatric populations across a range of different clinical disciplines. Lower discontinuation rates among industry-sponsored trials may reflect greater access to financial and human resources, including dedicated research coordination and established trial infrastructure. In contrast, investigator-initiated academic trials may be more vulnerable to non-completion if feasibility assessments and resource planning are insufficient. This is particularly critical because trials in surgery are commonly less attractive for industry to fund5. Participation in cancer clinical trials is generally low, due, in part, to restrictive eligibility criteria, limited trial availability, complex protocols, and structural barriers to enrolment, all of which can prolong recruitment and increase the risk of non-completion24.

Trial registration is intended to prevent dissemination bias and selective reporting of outcomes25–27. Accordingly, the World Health Organization and the International Committee of Medical Journal Editors have called for prospective public registration of all clinical trials28–30. This policy has been implemented by most scientific journals. Nevertheless, compliance with regulatory requirements for timely reporting of trial results in registries such as ClinicalTrials.gov and the EUCTR remains low31. For example, fewer than half of Canadian trials completed between 2009 and 2019 had reported results more than 10 years after completion9. Consistent with these findings, in the analysis and as noted above, almost half the initiated RCTs in abdominal surgery were not reported as completed and a publication could be identified for only 74.4% of reportedly completed trials, highlighting persistent shortcomings in trial registration and reporting. Furthermore, 59.3% of registered trials were retrospectively registered, similar to previous work assessing retrospective registration in RCTs32,33. Importantly, a central purpose of prospective trial registration is to establish a publicly accessible record of study objectives, endpoints, and planned analyses before participant enrolment, thereby reducing the risk of protocol modifications and selective reporting32.

Previous studies have excluded trials with status unknown from their analyses8,14. In contrast, these were included in the present analysis because these trials represent the bulk of registered trials and most are not reported as completed, fundamentally going against the idea of trial registries8. A systematic review11 suggests that approximately 40% of RCTs registered in trial registries remain unpublished as full journal articles more than 2 years after study completion, suggesting a substantial impact of publication bias on the medical literature. Mandatory requirements have not ensured complete and timely updating of trial registries9. Sensitivity analysis to challenge the assumption was conducted and no statistically significant factors were identified. However, this was expected considering the low sample size for this analysis and conclusions are therefore limited.

The findings of this study are consistent with previous evidence8,34 indicating that selective reporting is common in RCTs. A systematic review35 comparing registered and published primary outcomes reported a median discrepancy rate of 31%. Similarly, Robinson et al.36 identified reporting bias in approximately half of surgical RCTs, with the highest prevalence observed in general surgery trials. Importantly, changes in primary outcomes have been associated with larger reported treatment effects and, when combined with inadequate trial registration, this raises concerns about selective outcome reporting37.

In a post hoc exploratory analysis, the distribution of primary-outcome P values in published superiority trials showed a peak immediately below 0.050. No statistical inference can be drawn from this observation. Given uncertainty regarding the statistical properties of P curve analysis for detecting selective reporting38, neither this nor similar approaches were used. Only the latest registry entry was assessed; consequently, the timing and nature of any earlier endpoint modifications could not be determined34.

Unlike studies restricted to trials published in select high-impact journals34,36, this study began with trial registries and therefore captured a broader view of the clinical trial pathway. Although the observed patterns are consistent with dissemination bias, other explanations cannot be excluded. Registry-based meta-research relies on surrogate information without access to individual trial data or the full history of protocol amendments39. The findings of this study should therefore be interpreted as evidence of deficiencies in trial registration and reporting rather than as a direct estimate of the prevalence of dissemination bias.

Not all RCTs may have been registered, potentially limiting identification through trial registries40. This is particularly relevant for RCTs conducted in regions where registration in ClinicalTrials.gov or EUCTR is less common, such as parts of Asia. In addition, the search was restricted to articles published in English only and the search for published results may not have identified all studies. However, the inability to identify a study using its trial identification number makes the presence of dissemination bias presumably more likely, thus rather underestimating the extent of dissemination bias41.

In addition, trials with status unknown were grouped with non-completed trials, which was considered a sensible assumption. However, although formal sensitivity analysis was conducted, the results were not interpretable because the low number of events in several of the prespecified variables resulted in complete separation and therefore unstable effect estimates. Future studies with larger sample sizes will be necessary to investigate these assumptions.

Some trials may have been published without having the registry status updated. However, when considering trial records with the status unknown, only 38 additional publications could be identified, and previous work22 indicates that discontinued trials are more likely to remain unpublished. Furthermore, the aim of the present study was to outline the trajectories from registration to publication because registration aims to reduce selective reporting, including selected P values42.

Registry data were incomplete for several variables, including reasons for trial discontinuation, and could not be fully completed despite efforts to contact investigators. In addition, sponsors may have applied heterogeneous definitions of registry data elements, potentially leading to misclassification. Similarly, there was no access to trial protocols, in contrast with previous studies43 investigating dissemination bias using submitted protocols to ethics committees. Although ClinicalTrials.gov has been shown to have better technical performance than other trial registries, registry-based analyses remain dependent on the accuracy and completeness of submitted information44.

The timing of registry updates was not assessed relative to trial conduct or data analysis. Consequently, discrepancies between registered and published primary outcomes could reflect either legitimate protocol amendments documented during the study or selective outcome reporting. Furthermore, trial registry entries often provide limited detail regarding endpoint definitions and statistical analysis plans, which may complicate the identification and interpretation of outcome discrepancies. In some cases, apparent discrepancies may reflect clarification of incompletely specified outcomes rather than true modifications of prespecified endpoints. Therefore, the analysis cannot distinguish between prespecified endpoint modifications and changes made after trial initiation, and endpoint discrepancies should not be interpreted as direct evidence of reporting bias. In addition, confirmatory and exploratory endpoints were not compared in the trial registry, because this was beyond the scope of the present study. This needs to be considered as a limitation when interpreting the exploratory analysis regarding outcome reporting.

Finally, the study focused on abdominal surgery. RCTs represent only a small part of the evidence base in abdominal surgery; thus, dissemination bias is likely even more pronounced among non-randomized trials.

The findings of this study suggest important deficiencies in the reporting of randomized trials in abdominal surgery. A combined effort by researchers, academic institutions, journal editors, and regulators is required to enforce the available evidence being reported fully, and meta-analyses and guidelines can take the available evidence into account.
