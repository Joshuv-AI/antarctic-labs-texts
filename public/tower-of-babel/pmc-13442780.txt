Green synthesis water-soluble ZnO nanoparticles from sweet and chili pepper (Capsicum annuum L.) extracts and their photocatalytic activity
Authors: Azeez Abdullah Barzinjy, Banaz Shahab Haji, Isam Abdullah
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study presents the green synthesis of zinc oxide nanoparticles (ZnO NPs) using aqueous extracts of sweet and chili varieties of Capsicum annuum L. as natural reducing, capping, and stabilizing agents. The biosynthesized ZnO NPs were thoroughly characterized using UV–Vis spectroscopy, Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR), scanning electron microscopy (SEM), X-ray diffraction (XRD), and energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDX). SEM analysis revealed average particle sizes of 21.5 ± 2.3 nm for sweet-extract ZnO NPs and 28.7 ± 3.1 nm for chili-extract ZnO NPs, with predominantly semi-spherical morphology. UV–Vis spectra indicated bandgap energies of 3.25 eV and 3.07 eV, respectively, reflecting the influence of particle size and phytochemical composition. Photocatalytic activity was evaluated via methylene blue (MB) degradation under UV–Vis irradiation, showing 95% degradation for sweet-extract ZnO NPs and 82% for chili-extract ZnO NPs within 25–35 min. The higher efficiency of the sweet-extract nanoparticles is attributed to their smaller size, enhanced surface area, and improved reactive oxygen species generation. These findings demonstrate the potential of Capsicum annuum-derived ZnO NPs as effective and sustainable photocatalysts for environmental remediation, highlighting the critical role of phytochemical-mediated green synthesis in controlling nanoparticle properties and catalytic performance.Keywords: Green synthesis nanoparticles, ZnO NPs, Plant extract, Capsicum annuum, Methylene blue, Water treatment

Introduction

In recent years, there has been a significant increase in research attention on zinc oxide nanoparticles (ZnO NPs) due to their essential physicochemical properties [1]. ZnO NPs are among the most extensively utilized nanomaterials across a wide range of applications, including advanced UV sensors [2], targeted drug delivery systems [3], antioxidant [4]and anticancer activities [5], biosensing platforms [6], environmental remediation [7], water purification technologies [8], as well as enhancing drought tolerance and serving as nutrient sources in agriculture [9]. The synthesis of nanoparticles is generally achieved through three primary methodologies: physical, chemical, and green/biological approaches [10].

Physical and chemical methods are generally more effective in producing robust nanostructures with uniform size; nevertheless, they often fall short of achieving long-term environmental sustainability [11]. A crucial aspect of nanoparticle synthesis is the selection of an eco-friendly solvent that can serve as an effective reducing, capping, and stabilizing agent. Conventional chemical and physical synthesis techniques frequently involve hazardous reagents and complex processes that pose risks to ecological systems. This challenge has led to the emergence of “green nanotechnology” as an innovative and sustainable discipline. The green synthesis approach, which utilizes biological entities such as bacteria, fungi, yeast, and plant extracts, offers a cost-effective, reliable, and environmentally benign alternative for producing various nanomaterials. Consequently, there is an increasing demand for developing environmentally friendly nanoparticle synthesis methods that avoid the use of toxic substance [12].

The green synthesis approach is widely regarded as the most eco-friendly, cost-effective, safe, and straightforward method available [13]. Among these biological sources, plants are especially favored due to their capacity to facilitate large-scale production of stable nanoparticles with diverse shapes and sizes through the use of readily accessible, inexpensive, and environmentally sustainable plant extracts and related biomolecules [14]. The efficacy of plant-based synthesis is primarily attributed to the presence of phytochemicals, which function as reducing, capping, and stabilizing agents. Various plant parts—including leaves, fruits, roots, stems, and seeds—have been utilized to produce nanoparticles, with the inherent phytochemical composition enabling precise control over nanoparticle size and morphology to meet specific application requirements [15].

Phytochemicals, also known as phytonutrients, are typically secondary metabolites produced by plants, encompassing a diverse array of chemical structures such as alkaloids, saponins, indoles, phytosterols, phenolic acids, isothiocyanates, phytoprostanes/furanes, proteins, and enzymes [16]. Although these metabolites are not essential for the growth and development of plants, they have evolved to confer benefits for human health and disease anagement [17, 18]. In the context of nanoparticle synthesis, these phytochemicals serve as effective reducing, capping, and stabilizing agents. However, the precise mechanisms underlying green synthesis processes remain a subject of ongoing debate and investigation.

Accelerating urbanization and industrialization have resulted in large volumes of pollutants being discharged into natural water systems. Among these, synthetic dyes, particularly azo dyes, are among the most persistent and toxic contaminants due to their complex aromatic structures and resistance to biodegradation [19, 20]. These dyes are extensively employed in textiles, printing, cosmetics, food processing, and pharmaceuticals, accounting for over 50% of global dye production. Moreover, their derivatives have been utilized in advanced technological fields including photonic materials, solar cells, laser dyes, and drug design [21].

Methylene Blue (MB), although not an azo dye by chemical classification, is widely used as a model organic pollutant in photocatalytic studies due to its well-defined molecular structure, intense color, and measurable absorption characteristics [22]. Its stability and slow natural degradation make it a reliable representative for assessing the efficacy of novel decontamination methods. Therefore, the effective removal of MB from wastewater not only addresses dye pollution but also serves as a benchmark for evaluating advanced photocatalytic systems [23].

The common release of azo dyes, particularly from the textile industry, poses a serious environmental and public health concern. These synthetic dyes are chemically stable and resistant to biodegradation, making their removal from wastewater highly challenging [24]. Their persistence in aquatic systems significantly reduces light penetration, disrupts photosynthesis in aquatic flora, and alters dissolved oxygen levels, leading to ecological imbalance. Additionally, several studies have reported that certain azo dyes and their degradation products exhibit toxic, carcinogenic, and mutagenic effects on living organisms. Therefore, transforming these hazardous compounds into non-toxic byproducts remains a major focus of current environmental research, with increasing attention on green and sustainable technologies for dye remediation [25, 26].

Many methods have been used to remove dyes from wastewater, such as physical, chemical, and biological methods. Nevertheless, some of these methods are expensive in operation and maintenance. Moreover, some of them produce secondary contaminants that also need to be removed, which makes the techniques inappropriate and not cost-effective for the treatment of large volumes of water.

Nanoparticles have gained considerable attention in recent years for their effectiveness in the remediation of polluted water containing organic and inorganic contaminants. Among the various techniques, dye degradation using metal oxide nanoparticles has appeared as a cost-effective and eco-friendly method for removing toxic dyes from wastewater. Metal oxides such as ZnO, CuO, and TiO₂ have shown promising results in this regard [27, 28]. With a broad bandgap energy (~ 3.2–3.37 eV), zinc oxide (ZnO) nanoparticles are n-type semiconductors that provide effective UV light absorption and robust photocatalytic activity [29]. ZnO NPs are highly attractive for diverse applications, including wastewater treatment, electronics, optics, and biomedical sciences, owing to their excellent photostability, less toxicity, and cost-effectiveness. ZnO NPs have demonstrated significant efficacy in the remediation of persistent organic pollutants in environmental contexts through photocatalytic processes driven by the generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) upon light exposure. These attributes underscore their potential as effective and sustainable nanomaterials for addressing water pollution challenges [30].

In the present study, green synthesis method was employed, as an environmentally sustainable, straightforward, and safe approach for synthesizing ZnO NPs, utilizing Capsicum annuum extract. Plant-mediated synthesis methods have garnered widespread recognition due to their eco-friendly nature, cost-effectiveness, and capacity to produce nanoparticles with enhanced stability and controlled morphology. The bioactive compounds present in Capsicum annuum extract act as natural reducing, capping, and stabilizing agents, facilitating the synthesis process while minimizing the need for hazardous chemicals. This approach aligns with the principles of green chemistry, offering a viable alternative to conventional physical and chemical synthesis techniques by reducing environmental impact and improving biocompatibility of the resulting nanomaterials [31, 32].

This study investigates the green synthesis of ZnO nanoparticles using Capsicum annuum extracts and their photocatalytic degradation of Methylene Blue (MB). UV–Vis analysis demonstrated the efficient photocatalytic performance of the biosynthesized ZnO NPs, highlighting their potential as sustainable nanomaterials for environmental remediation and wastewater treatment applications.

Materials and methods

Plant extract preparation

In this investigation, sweet and chili varieties of Capsicum annuum were selected owing to their recognition as valuable dietary sources attributed to their abundant phytochemical composition [33]. Fresh, green-colored fruits of Capsicum annuum L., specifically banana-shaped cultivars encompassing both sweet and chili varieties, were procured from a local market in Erbil city, located in the northern region of Iraq (36.1877° N, 44.0107° E), during March 2025. These cultivars were selected based on their typical morphological characteristics and commercial availability to ensure consistency and representativeness for the subsequent analyses. Although both sweet and chili peppers belong to the same species (Capsicum annuum L.), they were differentiated based on their morphological characteristics, commercial classification, and pungency level. Sweet peppers contain negligible amounts of capsaicinoids and exhibit a mild taste, whereas chili peppers are characterized by high capsaicinoid content and pungency [34].

Moreover, both sweet and chili varieties classified as Capsicum annuum are members of the same species within the Solanaceae family. Sweet Capsicum annuum has small amounts of capsaicin, while chili ones contain high levels of capsaicin, which contributes to their heat. Capsicum annuum contains a diverse array of bioactive compounds, including capsaicinoids such as capsaicin and dihydrocapsaicin; carotenoids including lutein, zeaxanthin, capsorubin, and β-carotene; flavonoids such as quercetin, kaempferol, catechin, epicatechin, rutin, and luteolin; as well as steroidal saponins including capsicidine and capsicosides E, F, and G. The primary constituents of C. annuum essential oil are capsiamide and acetic acid. Extensive studies have documented a broad spectrum of biological activities attributed to C. annuum, including antioxidant, antibacterial, antiviral, antiproliferative, anti-adipogenic, antimutagenic, enzyme inhibitory, anti-inflammatory, hepatoprotective, antidiabetic, renoprotective, hypocholesterolemic, antitumor, anti-obesity, analgesic, appetite-modulating, and anti-reflux effects [35]. These phytochemicals collectively function as reducing, capping, and stabilizing agents during nanoparticle synthesis. For extract preparation, ten grams of both sweet and chili Capsicum annuum samples were thoroughly washed with distilled water, finely chopped, and immersed in 200 mL of deionized water. The mixture was subsequently heated at 50 °C for 30 min, allowed to cool to room temperature, and then filtered through filter paper to remove unwanted organic residues.

ZnO NPs synthesis

An amount of 2 g of zinc acetate dihydrate was dissolved in 50 mL of deionized water and stirred at 60 °C for 20 min. Subsequently, 150 mL of the Capsicum annuum extract solutions (both sweet and chili varieties) were added dropwise to the zinc acetate solution individually. The resulting mixtures were then heated and continuously stirred at 60 °C for 30 min until a noticeable color change occurred, transitioning to a brownish hue or forming a brownish paste (Fig. 1c). The obtained paste was subsequently subjected to calcination at 500 °C for approximately two hours in a furnace. The resulting powder was thoroughly washed multiple times with a mixture of ethanol and deionized water to remove any residual impurities and organic compounds associated with the synthesized ZnO nanoparticles.

The proposed mechanism underlying the biosynthesis of ZnO nanoparticles using chili and sweet pepper (Capsicum annuum) extracts

The underlying mechanism of ZnO NPs formation and activity can be elucidated as follows: First, the most essential step is the reduction phase in which plant extracts, metabolites can connect with metal ions. This connection helps the metal ions become metal atoms. These interactions facilitate the reduction of metal ions to their corresponding metal atoms, followed by the nucleation of the reduced metal atoms [36]. Second, growth stage: during the coarsening process, individual metal atoms unite to provide big particles of a stable size and shape. As the growth phase lengthens, NPs constantly aggregate and form on various forms like nanorods, nanotubes, nano hexahedrons, nano prisms, and others [37]. The stabilization phase is the last step in the biosynthesis of nanoparticles. When nanoparticles are covered with plant metabolites, they gradually obtain their most strongly favorable and stable form [38]. Figure 1. shows the effective mechanism of ZnO NPs using plant based green synthesis method.

As previously noted, both sweet and chili varieties of Capsicum annuum extracts are rich in phytochemicals, including carotenoids, flavonoids, steroidal saponins, essential oils, and acetic acid. These bioactive compounds play crucial roles in the green synthesis of ZnO NPs by acting as natural reducing and capping agents. Additionally, the presence of long-chain organic molecules within the plant extract helps prevent the agglomeration of ZnO NPs, thereby promoting the formation of stable and well-dispersed nanostructures [39]. Typically, a metallic salt, such as zinc salts, is added to a solution of plant extract; however, in our case, the mixture was heated for an appropriate duration and at the ideal temperature to create a foundation of metallic ions. It has been confirmed that several factors, such as pH, temperature, duration, extract concentration, and type of salts, must be optimized to achieve the highest yields of NPs [40, 41]. In these processes, many hydroxyl OH groups extracted from sweet and chili Capsicum annuum extract have shown promise for reducing zinc ions Zn2+ to zero-valent zinc nanoparticles Zn0 [42]. Furthermore, zinc ions in the solution may interact with phytochemicals, such as polyphenols, found in the sweet and chili Capsicum annuum extract. This interaction facilitates the formation of zinc hydroxide (Zn (OH)₂) through a hydrolysis reaction. Upon heating, the resulting complex undergoes thermal decomposition, leading to the formation of stable ZnO nanoparticles. Subsequently, the precipitation of Zn (OH)₂, controlled heating, and stirring were used to ensure uniform mixing and promote the dehydration of Zn (OH)₂. These situations helped the quick nucleation of ZnO and contributed to the formation of uniform crystal structures. The process also prevented agglomeration by preserving a stable and homogeneous reactive environment, minimizing the formation of clusters or large zinc oxide particles [43].

Results and discussions

UV-Vis spectra of chili and sweet Capsicum annuum extracts

The optical properties of the synthesized ZnO nanoparticles and plant extracts were analyzed using UV–Visible spectroscopy within the wavelength range of 200–800 nm at room temperature. The absorption spectra were recorded using quartz cuvettes with deionized water as the reference solvent. The band gap energy of the synthesized ZnO nanoparticles was estimated using Tauc plot analysis based on the obtained UV–Vis absorption spectra.

Recent research has focused on harnessing specific phytochemicals from plant extracts to synthesize nanoparticles with controlled size and morphology. These naturally occurring compounds facilitate the reduction of metal ions to metal nanoparticles, allowing the plant extract to function dually as both a reducing and stabilizing agent. The progress of the synthesis process is commonly monitored through UV–Vis spectroscopy [44]. Typically, the absorption range of 200–400 nm is suitable for identifying phytochemicals present in plant extracts. Figure 2 presents the UV–Vis spectra of sweet and chili Capsicum annuum extracts. The analysis revealed that the sweet Capsicum annuum extract exhibited a prominent absorption peak at 281 nm, which is most likely attributable to the presence of capsidiol, a key phytochemical component. The small absorption band appearing before 291 nm can therefore be attributed to overlapping electronic transitions of these phytochemicals, particularly π→π* transitions of aromatic and phenolic compounds. Furthermore, plant extracts are chemically complex mixtures, and the presence of multiple absorption bands in the UV region is commonly observed due to the coexistence of different organic molecules with distinct chromophoric groups. Therefore, the observed shoulder/secondary peak does not indicate impurity, but rather reflects the natural phytochemical composition of the chili extract.

UV-Vis spectra of a Sweet and b Chili Capsicum annuum extract

Capsidiol is a phytoalexin, a kind of antimicrobial compound that is present in plants. It belongs to the sesquiterpenoid class of compounds. Capsidiol is found mostly in plants like Capsicum (peppers) and Nicotiana (tobacco) [45]. As illustrated in Fig. 2, the maximum UV absorption peak for the chili Capsicum annuum extract exhibits a shift from 281 nm to 291 nm. This spectral shift is likely attributed to the presence of p-coumaric acid in place of capsidiol. The substitution of capsidiol with p-coumaric acid may significantly influence the nanoparticle formation process, as these phytochemicals differ in their chemical structure and reactivity. Such variations can alter the reduction kinetics, nucleation behavior, and stabilization efficiency during nanoparticle synthesis, thereby impacting the overall morphology and stability of the resulting ZnO nanoparticles in a complex and multifaceted manner.

FTIR analysis of chili and sweet Capsicum annuum extracts

Fourier-transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy was employed to identify the functional groups present in both the Capsicum annuum extracts and the synthesized ZnO nanoparticles, with a particular focus on identifying the functional groups associated with electron-donating biomolecules. The FTIR spectra were recorded within the wavenumber range of 4000–500 cm⁻¹ using the KBr pellet method at room temperature. The analysis was conducted to determine the biomolecules responsible for the reduction, capping, and stabilization of the ZnO nanoparticles during the green synthesis process. Fourier-transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy was employed to analyze both the Capsicum annuum plant extracts and the synthesized ZnO nanoparticles. This analysis aimed to determine the specific functional groups involved in the reduction of zinc ions and the stabilization of ZnO nanoparticles during the green synthesis process. The sharpness of the UV–Vis absorption peak alone is insufficient to directly confirm the monodispersity of the green-synthesized ZnO nanoparticles. However, the actual assessment of nanoparticle dispersity was primarily supported by SEM analysis, which provided direct morphological evidence regarding particle size distribution and aggregation behavior.

Figure 3 presents the FTIR spectra of both sweet and chili Capsicum annuum extracts. The spectra for both varieties exhibit characteristic absorption bands within the range of 4000–500 cm⁻¹. Notably, broad and intense peaks appear at 3239 cm⁻¹ and 3248 cm⁻¹ for the sweet and chili extracts, separately, equivalent to O–H stretching vibrations, indicative of hydroxyl groups typically found in alcohols and phenolic compounds. Additionally, the peaks observed at 2880 cm⁻¹ (sweet) and 2866 cm⁻¹ (chili) are attributed to C–H stretching vibrations, which suggest the presence of organic capping agents within the plant extracts.

FTIR spectroscopy for a Sweet and b Chili Capsicum annuum extract

These capping agents play a vital role in controlling nanoparticle growth by preventing agglomeration and enhancing solubility in various solvents. It is evident from Fig. 3 that the O–H peak is broader in the sweet Capsicum annuum extract, implying a higher concentration or diversity of capping molecules, which may contribute to improved nanoparticle stability over time by influencing size, morphology, and aggregation behavior [46].

The absorption bands at 1604 cm⁻¹ and 1633 cm⁻¹ in both extracts are assigned to C = O stretching vibrations, indicating the presence of stabilizing carbonyl-containing compounds. Furthermore, peaks located at 1022 cm⁻¹ and 1009 cm⁻¹ in the sweet and chili extracts, respectively, correspond to C–O stretching, which is characteristic of alcohols, ethers, and esters—key functional groups associated with reducing agents in green synthesis.

These reducing agents facilitate the conversion of metal ions to their zero-valent atomic states by donating electrons. The resulting atoms then nucleate and aggregate to form nanoparticles. Additional peaks observed at 639 and 468 cm⁻¹ in the sweet Capsicum annuum extract, and at 640 cm⁻¹ in the chili extract, are attributed to C–H bending vibrations. These signals are typical of hydrocarbon chains, alkyl groups, lipids, or long-chain organic molecules.

The presence of two distinct C–H peaks in the sweet Capsicum annuum extract suggests a higher abundance of organic compounds, potentially enriched with functional groups such as –OH and –CHO. These groups are likely involved in the reduction and stabilization of metal ions, further supporting the plant’s efficacy in nanoparticle synthesis [47].

Characterization of zinc oxide nanoparticles

UV-Vis analysis

UV-Vis spectroscopy is a widely employed characterization technique for investigating the optical properties of NPs. It enables the confirmation of NPs formation by monitoring the surface plasmon resonance (SPR) phenomenon associated with their optical behavior [48]. This technique provides valuable information regarding the particle size, band gap energy, colloidal stability, and degree of agglomeration of the synthesized NPS [49]. In the case of ZnO nanoparticles, their characteristic optical absorption typically falls within the ultraviolet (UV) region, specifically in the wavelength range of 300 to 380 nm.

Figure 4 presents the UV–Vis absorption spectra of ZnO nanoparticles synthesized via green methods using sweet and chili Capsicum annuum extracts. It is evident from the Figure that both extracts were utilized to produce ZnO NPs. However, with Sweet Capsicum annuum extract, the highest absorption peak showed up at 340 nm, while with Chili Capsicum annuum extract, it showed up at 351 nm. An alternative notable point that can be assumed from the Figure is that in the cases of Sweet Capsicum annuum, the UV-Vis peak is sharper, indicating the synthesis of monodisperse ZnO NPs. However, the Chili Capsicum annuum extract consequences in polydisperse ZnO nanoparticles, which is more clearly shown in the SEM section. It is generally recognized that the green synthesis of nanoparticles using plant extracts tends to yield polydisperse nanoparticles, due to the complex and variable nature of the organic constituents involved, which are often challenging to regulate precisely [50].

UV-Vis spectra and bandgap energy of ZnO NPs synthesized using a Sweet and b Chili Capsicum annuum extract

To the best of our knowledge, several parameters, including the type and concentration of the metal salt, the nature and concentration of the plant extract, pH, reaction time, and reaction temperature, significantly influence the formation of macromolecules during the nucleation phase. These factors collectively affect the mono- or polydispersity of the resulting nanoparticles and may ultimately impact the synthesis rate [40, 51]. In the present study, all synthesis conditions were kept constant, with the sole variable being the type of plant extract used (sweet or chili Capsicum annuum). As a result, the observed polydispersity in the ZnO nanoparticles was comparable between the two conditions [52].

The energy bandgap of the green synthesized ZnO NPs was calculated through Tauc’s plot by assuming the lined ratio of the UV-Vis curve. The electrical and optical characteristics of nanomaterials depend heavily on their band gap. The number of atoms or molecules reduces with decreasing particle size, which leads to a smaller valence and conduction band and less orbital overlap. The band gap between these bands widens as their widths shrink, limiting electron mobility and exposing quantum confinement. Some of the partially free conduction electrons in metallic nanoparticles (NPs) are retained by different atoms, while others are free to circulate among them and create metallic connections that fortify them. These electrons undergo intra-band quantum excitations in response to UV radiation, creating discrete conduction bands.

The energy band gap of ZnO nanoparticles synthesized using sweet and chili Capsicum annuum extracts was determined through Tauc plot analysis, based on the direct transition model derived from the UV–Vis absorption spectra (Fig. 4). The results indicate that ZnO nanoparticles synthesized with sweet Capsicum annuum extract exhibit a larger band gap of 3.25 eV, whereas those synthesized with chili Capsicum annuum extract display a slightly lower band gap of 3.07 eV. This variation is most likely attributed to differences in nanoparticle size. ZnO nanoparticles produced using sweet extract are smaller in size compared to those synthesized with chili extract.

It is well established that nanoparticle size significantly influences the band gap energy. Smaller nanoparticles contain fewer atoms, which reduces the attractive interactions between conduction band electrons and metal ions, thereby resulting in an increased band gap [53]. Conversely, larger nanoparticles possess a greater number of atoms, enhancing the interaction between conduction electrons and metal ions, which leads to a reduction in the band gap energy [54]. These observations underscore the strong size-dependent optical behavior of ZnO nanoparticles synthesized through green methods.

Upon UV irradiation, electrons absorb sufficient energy to transition from the valence band to the conduction band, generating electron–hole pairs that play a key role in photocatalytic reactions [55]. The photogenerated electrons and holes subsequently participate in redox reactions, leading to the formation of reactive oxygen species (ROS), such as hydroxyl radicals (•OH) and superoxide radicals (•O₂⁻), which are responsible for the degradation of methylene blue molecules. The smaller ZnO nanoparticles synthesized using sweet extract exhibited a slightly larger bandgap (3.25 eV), which can be attributed to quantum confinement effects and reduced electron delocalization. In contrast, the relatively larger nanoparticles synthesized using chili extract displayed a lower bandgap value (3.07 eV), reflecting enhanced orbital overlap and narrower energy separation between the valence and conduction bands.

FTIR spectra of green synthesized ZnO NPs

The Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectra were used to analyze the plant extract and ZnO nanoparticles, focusing on detecting functional groups related to electron-donating biomolecules and identifying those that peak at various wavenumbers corresponding to different functional groups (Fig. 5) while exhibiting numerous bonds. The strong broadband in Fig. 5, at 3435 cm⁻¹ using chili (Capsicum annuum) extract and 3454 cm⁻¹ with sweet (Capsicum annuum) extract, is ascribed to the O–H stretching vibration mode. The figure indicates that this peak is sharper with chili (Capsicum annuum) extract compared to the sweet variety, due to a higher number of hydroxyl groups in the former. The double bands at 2832 cm⁻¹ and 1399 cm⁻¹ observed in ZnO nanoparticles synthesized with chili (Capsicum annuum) extract are likely due to the C-H stretching vibrations of aldehydes. In contrast, the sweet (Capsicum annuum) extract displayed a single peak at 1413 cm⁻¹, suggesting a variation in organic composition. This difference could imply a higher concentration of capsaicinoids (capsaicin and dihydrocapsaicin) in the chili extract, as these compounds possess unique functional groups that influence surface functionalization [56]. The absorption peaks observed at 1643 cm⁻¹ in ZnO nanoparticles synthesized using chili (Capsicum annuum) extract and at 1662 cm⁻¹ using sweet (Capsicum annuum) extract correspond to the N–H bending vibrations of primary amines. These functional groups play a significant role in the synthesis process by acting as effective capping agents, contributing to the stabilization of the nanoparticles. Additionally, the peaks identified at 1064 cm⁻¹ for the chili extract-derived ZnO NPs and at 1017 cm⁻¹ for those synthesized using the sweet extract are attributed to C–O stretching vibrations, which are indicative of alcohols, ethers, or phenolic compounds involved in the nanoparticle formation and stabilization. The C-O stretching vibrations are commonly seen in this range (1000–1100 cm⁻¹), which is typically attributed to phenols, alcohols, or flavonoids in plant-mediated synthesis [57]. The broad bands at 654 cm⁻¹ and 473 cm⁻¹ in ZnO nanoparticles synthesized using chili (Capsicum annuum) extract were slightly shifted to 649 cm⁻¹ and 482 cm⁻¹ in those synthesized using sweet (Capsicum annuum) extract. These peaks are attributed to Zn-O bonding vibrations in ZnO nanoparticles [58].

XRD analysis of the green synthesized ZnO NPs

The crystalline structure and phase purity of the biosynthesized ZnO nanoparticles were investigated using X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis with Cu Kα radiation (λ = 0.15406 nm). The diffraction patterns were recorded over a 2θ range of 20°–80° at room temperature. The average crystallite size of the ZnO nanoparticles was estimated using the Debye–Scherrer equation based on the full width at half maximum (FWHM) of the major diffraction peaks.

In this study, X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis was employed to investigate the crystalline structure of zinc oxide nanoparticles synthesized via green methods. The corresponding XRD patterns for ZnO nanoparticles synthesized using sweet and chili Capsicum annuum extracts are presented in the figure, revealing insights into their phase purity and crystallographic characteristics.

As illustrated in Fig. 6, the XRD patterns of ZnO nanoparticles synthesized using sweet and chili Capsicum annuum extracts exhibit prominent diffraction peaks at 2θ values of 31.4976°, 34.1410°, 35.9301°, 47.2762°, 56.2957°, 62.5506°, 67.6801°, and 68.7801°, corresponding to the (100), (002), (101), (102), (110), (103), (200), and (112) crystallographic planes, respectively. These diffraction peaks align well with the standard Joint Committee on Powder Diffraction Standards (JCPDS) Card No. 89–0510, confirming that the synthesized ZnO nanoparticles possess a hexagonal wurtzite crystal structure.

XRD patterns of the biosynthesized ZnO nanoparticles using a sweet and b chili Capsicum annuum extracts

Notably, the (101) plane exhibits the highest intensity in both samples, indicating that crystal growth is predominantly oriented along this plane. Therefore, the statement regarding preferential crystal growth refers specifically to the dominant orientation along the (101) crystallographic plane. In hexagonal wurtzite ZnO structures, the plane exhibiting the highest diffraction intensity is generally considered the preferred growth orientation or dominant crystalline plane. Since the (101) reflection showed the maximum intensity in the obtained XRD patterns, it indicates that the synthesized ZnO nanoparticles predominantly grew along the (101) plane. The sharp and well-defined diffraction peaks further suggest the formation of highly crystalline and phase-pure ZnO nanoparticles [59].

The crystallite size of the synthesized ZnO nanoparticles was estimated using the Debye–Scherrer equation [60]: D= kλ/β cosθ where D denotes the average crystallite size, λ is the wavelength of the Cu Kα X-ray radiation (λ = 0.15406 nm), k is the shape factor (typically taken as 0.89), β represents the full width at half maximum (FWHM) of the diffraction peak, and θ is the Bragg angle.

Based on this formula, the average crystallite size of ZnO nanoparticles synthesized using chili and sweet Capsicum annuum extracts was calculated to be approximately 45 nm and 30 nm, respectively. These findings are consistent with previous reports on green-synthesized ZnO nanoparticles using Eclipta prostrata leaf extract [61], Ocimum lamifolium leaf extract [62], and Salvia hispanica seed extract [63].

SEM and EDX analysis of the green synthesized ZnO NPs

The surface morphology and particle size of the synthesized ZnO nanoparticles were examined using scanning electron microscopy (SEM) at appropriate accelerating voltage under high-vacuum conditions. The elemental composition and purity of the nanoparticles were analyzed using energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDX) attached to the SEM instrument.

The morphological characteristics of the green-synthesized ZnO NPs were examined using scanning electron microscopy (SEM). Figure 7 presents the SEM images of the ZnO NPs using chili and sweet (Capsicum annuum) extracts. In both cases, the ZnO nanoparticles predominantly exhibited nanoscale dimensions, well-defined morphologies, and distinct semi-spherical shapes, with average particle diameters of approximately 55 nm and 35 nm, respectively. The average particle size was estimated by measuring more than 10 randomly selected nanoparticles from different regions of the SEM images, and the reported values represent the average particle diameter. Furthermore, the grain size of the biosynthesized ZnO nanoparticles using sweet (Capsicum annuum) extracts is smaller than that from chili (Capsicum annuum) extracts. This is expected since the sweet (Capsicum annuum) extract contains a higher concentration of C-H groups that are present in capsidiol. C-H has a vital role that acts as copping agent. This leads to an increase in the nucleation rate, forming more ZnO nuclei in a short time, and subsequently, smaller NPs can be produced [64]. On the other hand, the FTIR analysis of chili (Capsicum annuum) extracts indicates that the peaks are less pronounced than those of the sweet variety (Capsicum annuum). Stronger peaks indicate a higher concentration of functional groups in the sweet extract, suggesting that it contains more organic molecules. Although these molecules can facilitate nucleation, they do not provide sufficient steric hindrance or electrostatic repulsion to prevent nanoparticles from agglomerating. Consequently, the ZnO nanoparticles synthesized using the sweet extract exhibited a higher tendency toward aggregation, as illustrated in Fig. 7. The SEM images revealed that ZnO nanoparticles synthesized using sweet Capsicum annuum extract exhibited relatively smaller and more uniformly distributed particles, whereas nanoparticles synthesized using chili extract showed comparatively larger particles with greater aggregation tendencies. Therefore, the conclusions regarding nanoparticle dispersity were revised to rely mainly on SEM observations rather than solely on UV–Vis peak characteristics.

SEM images of the ZnO NPs synthesized from both a sweet and b chili Capsicum annuum extracts

The semi-spherical morphology of the green-synthesized ZnO nanoparticles is mainly attributed to the influence of phytochemicals present in the Capsicum annuum L. extracts during the nucleation and growth processes. Plant-derived biomolecules, such as phenolic compounds, flavonoids, capsaicinoids, proteins, and other organic constituents, act as natural reducing and capping agents, regulating crystal growth and surface stabilization during nanoparticle formation. During the synthesis process, these biomolecules adsorb onto the surface of the growing ZnO nuclei, reducing surface energy and limiting anisotropic crystal growth. As a result, particle growth occurs more uniformly in multiple directions, favoring the formation of quasi-spherical or semi-spherical morphologies rather than highly anisotropic structures such as rods or plates. In addition, synthesis parameters such as precursor concentration, reaction temperature, pH, and calcination conditions also influence the final morphology of the nanoparticles. Under the present synthesis conditions, the controlled nucleation and isotropic growth environment promoted the formation of predominantly semi-spherical ZnO nanoparticles.

Energy Dispersive X-ray (EDX) analysis was employed to investigate the elemental composition and surface characteristics of the green-synthesized ZnO nanoparticles, offering a comprehensive understanding of their topographical and elemental profiles. As shown in Fig. 8, both samples primarily consist of zinc, oxygen, and silver elements. The peak observed in the EDX spectra corresponds to silver (Ag) originates from the thin conductive Ag coating applied to the samples prior to SEM/EDX analysis. This coating is commonly used to improve surface conductivity and minimize charging effects during electron beam exposure, particularly for nanoscale materials and non-conductive samples. Therefore, the detected Ag signal is not associated with the chemical composition of the synthesized ZnO nanoparticles.

Degradation of methylene blue

The photocatalytic activity of the biosynthesized ZnO nanoparticles was evaluated through the degradation of methylene blue (MB) aqueous solution under UV–Vis irradiation. For each experiment, a known concentration of MB solution was mixed with an appropriate amount of ZnO nanoparticles and continuously stirred to ensure uniform dispersion. The photocatalytic reaction was carried out under continuous UV–Vis light exposure at room temperature. At regular time intervals, aliquots of the reaction mixture were withdrawn and analyzed using UV–Visible spectroscopy to monitor the degradation process. The removal of synthetic dyes, such as methylene blue (MB), from wastewater represents a significant environmental concern due to their toxicity, stability, and persistence in aquatic ecosystems [65]. Photocatalysis employing metal oxide nanoparticles presents a promising and sustainable remediation strategy, especially when utilizing green synthesis methods that eliminate the use of hazardous chemicals [66]. In the present study, the photocatalytic performance of green-synthesized ZnO nanoparticles was assessed by monitoring the degradation of MB under UV-Vis irradiation [67].

This enhanced activity is attributed to the smaller crystallite size and higher surface area of ZnO NPs produced from sweet pepper extract, which increases the number of active sites available for photocatalytic reactions. In contrast, the larger-sized ZnO NPs from chili extract exhibit reduced surface-to-volume ratio, limiting their ability to generate and transfer photogenerated charge carriers efficiently. Consequently, the smaller ZnO NPs provide more effective light absorption and reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation, accelerating dye degradation and demonstrating the crucial role of nanoparticle size in photocatalytic efficiency. In the present study, ZnO nanoparticles synthesized using sweet Capsicum annuum L. extract exhibited a smaller particle size and a slightly higher bandgap energy (3.25 eV) compared to those synthesized using chili extract (3.07 eV), yet demonstrated superior photocatalytic degradation of methylene blue (MB). This enhanced photocatalytic activity can primarily be attributed to the smaller particle size and larger surface-area-to-volume ratio of the sweet-extract-derived ZnO nanoparticles. Smaller nanoparticles provide a greater number of active surface sites for light absorption, dye adsorption, and reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation, thereby accelerating photocatalytic reactions [70]. In addition, the reduced particle size shortens the migration distance of photogenerated electron–hole pairs from the bulk to the nanoparticle surface, decreasing the probability of charge carrier recombination and improving photocatalytic efficiency. Although a slightly larger bandgap generally requires higher excitation energy, it may also enhance charge separation efficiency and reduce electron–hole recombination in smaller nanoparticles due to quantum confinement effects. Consequently, the improved surface properties and more efficient charge transfer processes of the smaller ZnO nanoparticles outweigh the minor increase in bandgap energy, leading to faster MB degradation. Therefore, the superior photocatalytic performance of ZnO nanoparticles synthesized using sweet Capsicum annuum extract is mainly governed by their smaller particle size, enhanced surface reactivity, and improved charge carrier dynamics rather than by bandgap energy alone [71].

The bar graph (Fig. 10c) compares the photocatalytic degradation efficiency of methylene blue using ZnO nanoparticles synthesized from sweet and chili Capsicum annuum L. extracts. The results clearly demonstrate that the ZnO nanoparticles derived from the sweet extract achieved a higher degradation efficiency (~ 95%) compared to those from the chili extract (~ 82%). This difference reflects the influence of nanoparticle size, surface properties, and phytochemical composition of the extracts on photocatalytic performance, highlighting the superior activity of the sweet-extract-mediated ZnO nanoparticles under the same experimental conditions.

Conclusion

This study successfully demonstrates the green synthesis of zinc oxide nanoparticles (ZnO NPs) using aqueous extracts of sweet and chili varieties of Capsicum annuum L. as natural reducing and stabilizing agents. The biosynthesized ZnO NPs exhibited well-defined crystalline structures, semi-spherical morphology, and controlled particle sizes of 21.5 ± 2.3 nm (sweet extract) and 28.7 ± 3.1 nm (chili extract). UV–Vis and FTIR analyses confirmed the role of phytochemicals in capping and stabilizing the nanoparticles, while XRD and SEM/EDX characterization verified their structural and elemental integrity. Photocatalytic evaluation against methylene blue demonstrated superior degradation efficiency (95%) for sweet-extract ZnO NPs compared to 82% for chili-extract ZnO NPs, highlighting the influence of nanoparticle size, surface properties, and reactive oxygen species generation on catalytic performance.

This work advances the understanding of plant-mediated green synthesis by linking phytochemical composition to nanoparticle properties and photocatalytic efficiency. The findings underscore the potential of Capsicum annuum-derived ZnO NPs as eco-friendly, cost-effective photocatalysts for wastewater treatment. Future studies could focus on scaling up the synthesis process, optimizing visible-light-driven photocatalysis, exploring degradation of other organic pollutants, and evaluating long-term stability and reusability, thereby broadening the practical applications of these sustainable nanomaterials.
