Key words: Bhargava cube, frieze, Farey graph, hyperdeterminant, integer hypertiling, integer tiling, SL 2 \text{SL}_{2} -tiling. There is no data associated with this article.
Oleg Karpenkov, Ian Short, Matty van Son, and Andrei Zabolotskii

Abstract
There are two objectives to this work: to classify all tame integer tilings and to classify all tame integer hypertilings. Motivation for the first objective comes from Conway and Coxeter’s modelling of positive integer friezes using triangulated polygons, which has received significant attention since the discovery of cluster algebras by Fomin and Zelevinsky in 2002. Assem, Reutenauer, and Smith introduced SL 2 \text{SL}_{2} -tilings as generalisations of friezes, and Bessenrodt, Holm, and Jørgensen classified positive integer SL 2 \text{SL}_{2} -tilings using infinite triangulated polygons. Here we consider N N -tilings, of which SL 2 \text{SL}_{2} -tilings are the case N = 1 N=1 . We provide a geometric model for all tame integer N N -tilings using a generalisation of the Farey graph in the hyperbolic plane. Highlights of this model include classifications of all positive integer N N -tilings and of all quiddity sequences of positive rational friezes using triangulated polygons. The second objective is motivated by Bhargava’s celebrated study of binary quadratic forms using integer cubes and by an observation of Demonet et al. that there is essentially only one three-dimensional positive integer tiling with SL 2 \text{SL}_{2} cross sections. We consider a richer class of three-dimensional tilings, which we call hypertilings, using the Cayley hyperdeterminant. We classify all tame integer hypertilings using generalised Farey graphs; remarkably, those with Cayley hyperdeterminant 1 prove to have a simple description in terms of triple Hadamard products of integer pairs.

Introduction

This paper has twofold objectives: to classify all tame integer tilings and to classify all tame integer hypertilings. The provenance for such classifications is Conway and Coxeter’s modelling of positive integer friezes using triangulated polygons
[
6
]
. Models of this type have blossomed since the discovery of cluster algebras by Fomin and Zelevinsky in the 2000s
[
11
]
and the work of Caldero and Chapoton
[
5
]
, who showed that Conway and Coxeter’s friezes arise from cluster algebras of Dynkin type
A
. Later, Assem, Reutenauer, and Smith introduced
\text{SL}_{2}
-tilings
[
1
]
, generalising friezes.

The positivity conjecture of Fomin and Zelevinksy, which was verified for all skew-symmetric cluster algebras by Lee and Schiffler
[
14
]
, led to interest in positive integer
\text{SL}_{2}
-tilings and infinite friezes. Bessenrodt, Holm, and Jørgensen offered a combinatorial classification of the former
[
3
]
and Baur, Parsons, and Tschabold provided the same sort of classification for the latter
[
2
]
. The second author reinterpreted these classifications using the geometry of the Farey graph in
[
18
]
, building on the vision of Morier-Genoud, Ovsienko, and Tabachnikov
[
15
]
. Then, in
[
10
]
, Felikson et al. took the same approach for a three-dimensional Farey graph, using Eisenstein integers, and the latter three authors of this paper extended this to more general rings in
[
17
]
.

Inspiration for this work comes from the elegant result of Demonet et al.
[
9
]
that there is (essentially) a
unique
three-dimensional tiling of positive integers with the property that every cross section is an
\text{SL}_{2}
-tiling. Here we consider broader classes of three-dimensional integer tilings in which every 2-by-2-by-2 block is required to have Cayley hyperdeterminant 1 or, more generally,
N
. Such cubes of integers are known as
Bhargava cubes
following the pioneering work of Bhargava
[
4
]
who interpreted Gauss composition of binary quadratic forms using these cubes. We combine Bhargava’s approach with a more general concept of Farey graphs to model three-dimensional integer tilings, which we call
hypertilings
. The cross sections of hypertilings are
not
required to be
\text{SL}_{2}
-tilings – they can be
N
-tilings, where each 2-by-2 square has determinant
N
(and
N
depends on the cross section). For this reason, we begin with a comprehensive study of
N
-tilings, which advances all previous classifications of integer
\text{SL}_{2}
-tilings. An appealing consequence of this is a complete description of all quiddity cycles of positive
rational
friezes using triangulated polygons, taking us back to the inspired idea of Conway and Coxeter that started this all.

We will now introduce our main results, first for integer tilings and then for integer hypertilings. Throughout,
\mathcal{I}
,
\mathcal{J}
, and
\mathcal{K}
are index sets of consecutive integers of length at least 3, possibly finite, possibly infinite, each of which contains
0
and
1
. We write
\mathcal{I}^{\prime}
for the set obtained from
\mathcal{I}
by removing the least element from
\mathcal{I}
, if it has one, and we write
\mathcal{I}^{*}
for the set obtained from
\mathcal{I}^{\prime}
by removing the greatest element from
\mathcal{I}^{\prime}
, if it has one.

1.1
Integer tilings

In this work, integer tilings are considered to be types of (possibly infinite) integer matrices. They are unrelated to tilings from the theory of tessellations. We define a
subblock
of a matrix to be a block submatrix; that is, a submatrix with contiguous entries.

Definition
.

For any integer
N
, an
N
-tiling
is a function
\mathbf{M}\colon\mathcal{I}\times\mathcal{J}\longrightarrow\mathbb{Z}
, for some index sets
\mathcal{I}
and
\mathcal{J}
, with the property that every 2-by-2 subblock has determinant
N
. An
integer tiling
is an
N
-tiling, for some value of
N
. We write
m_{ij}
for
\mathbf{M}(i,j)
.

In Section
7
we will consider 0-tilings. For now, we assume that
N
is a nonzero integer. A 1-tiling is what is usually called an
\text{SL}_{2}
-tiling. Clearly, we can create an
N
-tiling from a 1-tiling by multiplying every other row, or every other column, by
N
. There are plenty of
N
-tilings that do not arise in this fashion.

Definition
.

An
N
-tiling is
tame
if the determinant of each 3-by-3 subblock is 0.

This definition resembles that for tame
\text{SL}_{2}
-tilings. To get to grips with
N
-tilings, we need further data (which is trivial for
\text{SL}_{2}
-tilings) as follows.

Consider a tame
N
-tiling
\mathbf{M}
. The
greatest common divisor

K
of
\mathbf{M}
is the greatest common divisor of all the entries from
\mathbf{M}
. Clearly
K=1
for an
\text{SL}_{2}
-tiling. Now, suppose for the moment that
N
is positive. Let
t
be the greatest common divisor of all the 2-by-2 minors of
\mathbf{M}
, the Plücker coordinates of
\mathbf{M}
. Let
r
be the greatest common divisor of the 2-by-2 minors from any two consecutive columns of
\mathbf{M}
, and let
R=N/r
. Likewise, let
s
be the greatest common divisor of the 2-by-2 minors from any two consecutive rows of
\mathbf{M}
, and let
S=N/s
. We will see later that
R
and
S
are independent of which columns and rows are chosen. We will also see that
RS=N/t
. Notice that
t
is divisible by
K^{2}
. Hence
N=K^{2}LRS
, where
L=t/K^{2}
.

When
N
is negative, we replace
r
,
s
, and
t
with their negatives and then define
R
,
S
, and
L
as before. Consequently,
R
and
S
are (still) positive but now
L
is negative.

Definition
.

For a tame
N
-tiling
\mathbf{M}
, the integers
K
,
L
,
R
, and
S
are called the
tameness parameters
of
\mathbf{M}
. We will usually write these as a 4-tuple
(K,L,R,S)
. We call
R
the
vertical tameness parameter
of
\mathbf{M}
and
S
the
horizontal tameness parameter
of
\mathbf{M}
.

We illustrate this definition with the tame 9-tiling
\mathbf{M}
with greatest common divisor 1 shown in Figure
1
(a). The greatest common divisor of the minors from the two shaded vertical rows is 9, so the vertical tameness parameter is
R=9/9=1
. Similarly, the horizontal tameness parameter is
S=9/3=3
, so
L=3
. In summary, the tameness parameters of
\mathbf{M}
are
(1,3,1,3)
.

More generally, we define a class of Farey graphs indexed by the positive integers as follows.

Definition
.

For any positive integer

•

•

We will often denote the vertex
(a,b)
by the formal fraction

because this fractional notation is suggestive for how we manipulate vertices of Farey graphs. All graphs

are connected. The notation

The directed graph
\mathscr{F}_{1}
(which we also denote by just

\mathscr{F}_{R}
it helps to work with the quotient of

obtained by identifying pairs of vertices

and

\text{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})

\mathscr{F}_{R}

Definition

A

is a sequence of vertices

To ease notation, we sometimes write

be a nonzero integer. We define

This group is sometimes called the

We can define a left action of

\begin{pmatrix}a&bL\\

y_{2}\end{pmatrix}\right)=\left(\begin{pmatrix}a&bL\\
c&d\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}x_{1}\\
x_{2}\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
cL&d\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}y_{1}\\
y_{2}\end{pmatrix}\right).

This restricts to an action on
\mathscr{F}_{R}\times\mathscr{F}_{S}

\mathscr{F}_{R}
and
\mathscr{F}_{S}

Any positive rational or real frieze is determined by the periodic part of its third row; this sequence, up to cyclic permutations, is known as the
quiddity cycle
of the frieze. The quiddity cycle is highlighted in Figure
5
. We will explain how to classify positive rational quiddity cycles in the manner of Conway and Coxeter’s classification of positive integral quiddity cycles. To achieve this, we use weighted triangular polygons, which are defined as follows.

Consider a selection
v_{0},v_{1},\dots,v_{m-1}
of the vertices of a triangulated polygon, listed in clockwise order. Let
d_{i}
be the Farey distance between
v_{i-1}
and
v_{i}
in the triangulated polygon (with
v_{m}=v_{0}
). To each vertex
v_{i}
we assign a positive integer weight
w_{i}
such that the products
d_{i}w_{i-1}w_{i}
are all equal to the same positive integer
N
, for
i=1,2,\dots,m
. A triangulated polygon with a collection of vertices of this type is called a
weighted triangular polygon
.

For example, consider the triangulated decagon of Figure
7
(a) – ignore the integer labels for now. Seven of the ten vertices are coloured black or white; these are the vertices
v_{0},v_{1},\dots,v_{6}
. We define the black ones to have weight 1 and the white ones to have weight 2. This triangulated decagon with these weighted vertices is a weighted triangular polygon with
N=2
. It is a Euclidean version of the combinatorially equivalent hyperbolic ideal triangulated polygon shaded in Figure
6
(a). The black and white vertices correspond to the vertices of the path shown in Figure
6
(a), with black for vertices
a/b
with
\gcd(a,b)=1
and white for vertices
a/b
with
\gcd(a,b)=2
.

At each of the distinguished vertices of the weighted triangular polygon we multiply the weight of that vertex by the number of triangles incident to that vertex; these are the integer labels. Reading them clockwise in order we obtain the quiddity cycle of a positive integer 4-frieze, as shown in Figure

(b). That is, we obtain a type of frieze where the diamonds have determinant 4 rather than 1 (this is an example of a frieze pattern with coefficients

]

). On dividing each entry of this frieze by 2 we obtain the positive rational frieze of Figure

Integer hypertilings

We now turn our attention to integer hypertilings. We will demonstrate that each tame integer hypertiling can be constructed from three paths in Farey graphs and a
Bhargava cube
, which is an element of
\mathbb{Z}^{2}\otimes\mathbb{Z}^{2}\otimes\mathbb{Z}^{2}
, named in honour of the pioneering work of Bhargava
[
4
]
. We can regard a Bhargava cube
M
as a function
M\colon\{0,1\}^{3}\longrightarrow\mathbb{Z}
. Let us write
m_{ijk}
for
M(i,j,k)
, and then we can represent the cube in the form

\left(\begin{matrix}m_{000}&m_{010}\\
m_{100}&m_{110}\end{matrix}\ \middle|\ \begin{matrix}m_{001}&m_{011}\\
m_{101}&m_{111}\end{matrix}\right).

(1.1)

Here we can think of the left-hand square as lying above the right-hand square to form a cube of eight integers (and we use similar notation for larger cubes). There is a left action of the direct product
\text{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})^{3}=\text{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})\times\text{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})\times\text{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
on Bhargava cubes under which the triple
(A,B,C)\in\text{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})^{3}
sends the Bhargava cube
M
to
M^{\prime}
, where

m^{\prime}_{ijk}=\sum_{p,q,r=0}^{1}A_{ip}B_{jq}C_{kr}m_{pqr}.

Indeed, given any triple of 2-by-2 integer matrices
(A,B,C)
(not necessarily in
\text{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})^{3}
), this formula defines another Bhargava cube
M^{\prime}
from
M
.

The
Cayley hyperdeterminant
(or just
hyperdeterminant
) of a Bhargava cube
M=(M_{1}\mid M_{2})
is

\displaystyle\Det M

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{4}\left(\det(M_{1}+M_{2})-\det(M_{1}-M_{2})\right)^{2}-4\det M_{1}\det M_{2}

\displaystyle=((m_{000}m_{111}-m_{011}m_{100})+(m_{001}m_{110}-m_{010}m_{101}))^{2}

\displaystyle\hskip 14.22636pt-4(m_{000}m_{110}-m_{010}m_{100})(m_{001}m_{111}-m_{011}m_{101}).

We write
\Det
for hyperdeterminants and
\det
for normal determinants (of square matrices). The
\text{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})^{3}
action preserves hyperdeterminants
[
13
]
. More generally, if
A
,
B
, and
C
, are 2-by-2 integer matrices and
M^{\prime}=(A,B,C)M
, then
\Det M^{\prime}=(\det A\det B\det C)^{2}\Det M
.

We are now in a position to define integer hypertilings, which are tensors
\mathbf{M}\colon\mathcal{I}\times\mathcal{J}\times\mathcal{K}\longrightarrow\mathbb{Z}
subject to certain conditions. A subblock of a tensor is defined in much the same way as a subblock of a matrix.

Definition
.

For any nonzero integer
N
, an
N
-hypertiling
is a function
\mathbf{M}\colon\mathcal{I}\times\mathcal{J}\times\mathcal{K}\longrightarrow\mathbb{Z}
, for some index sets
\mathcal{I}
,
\mathcal{J}
, and
\mathcal{K}
, with the property that every 2-by-2-by-2 subblock has hyperdeterminant
N
. An
integer hypertiling
is an
N
-hypertiling, for some value of
N
.

Next we will define tame integer hypertilings. For this, we could mimic the definition of tame integer tilings by considering hyperdeterminants of 3-by-3-by-3 subblocks; however, for our purposes, a more fruitful approach is to use cross sections of hypertilings. Formally, a
cross section
of an integer hypertiling
\mathbf{M}\colon\mathcal{I}\times\mathcal{J}\times\mathcal{K}\longrightarrow\mathbb{Z}
is the restriction of
\mathbf{M}
to any one of
\{i_{0}\}\times\mathcal{J}\times\mathcal{K}
(for some
i_{0}\in\mathcal{I}
),
\mathcal{I}\times\{j_{0}\}\times\mathcal{K}
(for some
j_{0}\in\mathcal{J}
), or
\mathcal{I}\times\mathcal{J}\times\{k_{0}\}
(for some
k_{0}\in\mathcal{K}
). Part of the definition of a tame integer hypertiling (to follow) is that each cross section must be a tame integer tiling. This includes the possibility that the cross section may be a tame 0-tiling; these are discussed in Section
7
.

In addition to cross sections, we need one further concept to handle some awkward hypertilings. Consider any 2-by-2-by-3 or 2-by-3-by-2 or 3-by-2-by-2 subblock
M
of
\mathbf{M}
; let us assume the first case – the others are similar. There are four 1-by-1-by-3 subblocks of
M
. Choose any two of these subblocks and stack them alongside each other to form a 3-by-2 matrix

\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\\
e&f\end{pmatrix}.

We say that the integer hypertiling
\mathbf{M}
is
synchronised
if
ad-bc=cf-de
for any 3-by-2 matrix formed in this way. If the two 1-by-1-by-3 subblocks belong to the same tame cross section, then this identity is automatically satisfied; it is only when the two subblocks are from different cross sections that the synchronised property offers an additional restriction.

Definition
.

An integer hypertiling
\mathbf{M}
is
tame
if it is synchronised and if each cross section of
\mathbf{M}
is a tame integer tiling.

It is a straightforward exercise (which we omit) to show that every positive integer hypertiling with all cross sections integer tilings is necessarily tame.

The synchronised condition removes some pathological integer hypertilings. For example, consider the 65-hypertiling

\left(\begin{matrix}[r]0&\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$-$}1&0\\
8&0&\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$-$}8\\
0&1&0\end{matrix}\ \middle|\ \begin{matrix}[r]0&\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$-$}1&0\\
1&0&\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$-$}1\\
0&1&0\end{matrix}\ \middle|\ \begin{matrix}[r]0&\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$-$}7&0\\
4&0&\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$-$}4\\
0&7&0\end{matrix}\right).

Each cross section is a tame integer tiling, however, by considering the 3-by-2 matrix

\begin{pmatrix}[r]8&\!\!-1\\
1&\!\!-1\\
4&\!\!-7\end{pmatrix}

we can see that this integer hypertiling is not synchronised.

Our first main result on integer hypertilings is a complete classification of all tame 1-hypertilings using triple Hadamard products of paths in
\mathscr{F}
. We use the subgroup

\{(I,I,I),(I,-I,-I),(-I,I,-I),(-I,-I,I)\}

of
\text{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
, where
I
is the identity matrix. This subgroup is isomorphic to the Klein four-group
\mathbb{Z}_{2}\times\mathbb{Z}_{2}
, and it acts on triples of paths in
\mathscr{F}
.

Theorem C
.

The map

(\mathbb{Z}_{2}\times\mathbb{Z}_{2})\backslash\left\{\parbox{96.73918pt}{\centering{triples of paths in $\mathscr{F}$}\@add@centering}\right\}\quad\longrightarrow\quad\left\{\parbox{91.04872pt}{\centering{tame
1-hypertilings}\@add@centering}\right\}

determined by

m_{ijk}=a_{i}c_{j}e_{k}+b_{i}d_{j}f_{k},\qquad\text{$(i,j,k)\in\mathcal{I}\times\mathcal{J}\times\mathcal{K}$,}

for paths
a_{i}/b_{i}
,
c_{j}/d_{j}
, and
e_{k}/f_{k}
in
\mathscr{F}
, is a one-to-one correspondence.

Theorem
C
will be proved in Section
9
. In that section we will also see that every Bhargava cube of hyperdeterminant 1 is
\textnormal{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})^{3}
-equivalent to

\left(\begin{matrix}1&0\\
0&0\end{matrix}\ \middle|\ \begin{matrix}0&0\\
0&1\end{matrix}\right).

This result can be achieved using the methods of Bhargava
[
4
]
; instead, however, we describe explicit triples in
\textnormal{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})^{3}
that achieve the equivalence.

Our fourth and final main result is about classifying all tame integer hypertilings. In this theorem we use the notation
u_{0i}/u_{1i}
,
v_{0j}/v_{1j}
, and
w_{0k}/w_{1k}
(for
i\in\mathcal{I},j\in\mathcal{J},k\in\mathcal{K}
) to describe paths in
\mathscr{F}_{R}
,
\mathscr{F}_{S}
, and
\mathscr{F}_{T}
. We say that a Bhargava cube
A
is
nonsingular
if
\Det A\neq 0
.

Theorem D
.

Let
A
be a nonsingular Bhargava cube. For any positive integers
R
,
S
, and
T
, there is a map

\left\{\parbox{105.2751pt}{\centering{triples of minimal paths in $\mathscr{F}_{R}$, $\mathscr{F}_{S}$, and $\mathscr{F}_{T}$ }\@add@centering}\right\}\quad\longrightarrow\quad\left\{\parbox{110.96556pt}{\centering{tame integer hypertilings}\@add@centering}\right\}

determined by

m_{ijk}=\sum_{p,q,r=0}^{1}A_{pqr}u_{ip}v_{jq}w_{kr},\qquad\text{$(i,j,k)\in\mathcal{I}\times\mathcal{J}\times\mathcal{K}$,}

for minimal paths
u_{0i}/u_{1i}
,
v_{0j}/v_{1j}
, and
w_{0k}/w_{1k}
in
\mathscr{F}_{R}
,
\mathscr{F}_{S}
, and
\mathscr{F}_{T}
. Furthermore, each tame integer hypertiling arises in this way.

For a given nonsingular Bhargava cube
A
, and positive integers
R
,
S
, and
T
, the formula of Theorem
D
determines a tame
N
-hypertiling, where
N=(RST)^{2}\Det A
. Every tame
N
-hypertiling arises in this way, although typically not all from the same cube
A
(the case
N=1
of Theorem
C
is exceptional). Any Bhargava cube
A^{\prime}
that is
\text{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})^{3}
-equivalent to
A
will determine exactly the same collection of tame
N
-hypertilings in Theorem
D
as that of
A
.

There may be many triples of paths that determine the same hypertiling for a given Bhargava cube
A
. Precisely which triples depends on the stabilizer of
A
in
\text{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})^{3}
. We will see in Section
9
that the stabilizer of a Bhargava cube with hyperdeterminant 1 is the group
\mathbb{Z}_{2}\times\mathbb{Z}_{2}
discussed earlier; however, stabilizers in general need not be so simple. Theorem
D
will be proved in Section
8
.

2
Paths and itineraries

We recall that, given an index set
\mathcal{I}
, the set
\mathcal{I}^{*}
is obtained from
\mathcal{I}
by removing the greatest element from
\mathcal{I}
(if it has one) and removing the least element from
\mathcal{I}
(if it has one).

Definition
.

The
itinerary
of a path
(a_{i}/b_{i})_{i\in\mathcal{I}}
in
\mathscr{F}_{R}
is the rational sequence

\lambda_{i}=\frac{1}{R}(a_{i-1}b_{i+1}-b_{i-1}a_{i+1}),

for
i\in\mathcal{I}^{*}
.

This definition of itinerary coincides with that of
[
17
]
in the special case
R=1
. The next lemma is similar to
[
17
, Lemma 3.2]
, so we abridge the proof.

Lemma 2.1
.

The itinerary of a path
\gamma
in
\mathscr{F}_{R}
with vertices
(a_{i}/b_{i})_{i\in\mathcal{I}}
is the unique sequence of rationals
(\mu_{i})_{i\in\mathcal{I}^{*}}
that satisfies

a_{i-1}+a_{i+1}=\mu_{i}a_{i}\quad\text{and}\quad b_{i-1}+b_{i+1}=\mu_{i}b_{i},

for
i\in\mathcal{I}^{*}
.

Proof.

Let
(\lambda_{i})_{i\in\mathcal{I}^{*}}
be the itinerary of
\gamma
. Then

a_{i-1}+a_{i+1}=\frac{1}{R}(a_{i-1}(a_{i}b_{i+1}-b_{i}a_{i+1})+a_{i+1}(a_{i-1}b_{i}-b_{i-1}a_{i}))=\lambda_{i}a_{i},

and similarly
b_{i-1}+b_{i+1}=\lambda_{i}b_{i}
. The uniqueness assertion of the lemma is straightforward.
∎

In the classical Farey graph
\mathscr{F}_{1}
, a path is determined by its itinerary, up to
\text{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
equivalence. This is
not
true in
\mathscr{F}_{R}
, for
R>1
. For example, the paths
\langle\tfrac{1}{0},\tfrac{0}{2},\tfrac{-1}{2}\rangle
and
\langle\tfrac{2}{0},\tfrac{0}{1},\tfrac{-2}{1}\rangle
in
\mathscr{F}_{2}
have the same itinerary – namely the sequence with a single element 1 – but they are not equivalent under
\text{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
because
\tfrac{1}{0}
and
\tfrac{2}{0}
lie in distinct orbits. The next theorem states that
minimal
paths in
\mathscr{F}_{R}
with the same itinerary
are
equivalent under
\text{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
, which explains the importance of the concept of minimal paths.

Theorem 2.2
.

Let
\gamma
and
\delta
be two minimal paths in
\mathscr{F}_{R}
with the same itinerary. Then
\gamma
and
\delta
are equivalent under the action of
\textnormal{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
.

Proof.

Let
\gamma
have vertices
a_{i}/b_{i}
and
\delta
have vertices
c_{i}/d_{i}
, for
i\in\mathcal{I}
. We define

A=\begin{pmatrix}a_{0}&a_{1}\\
b_{0}&b_{1}\end{pmatrix},\quad B=\begin{pmatrix}c_{0}&c_{1}\\
d_{0}&d_{1}\end{pmatrix},\quad\text{and}\quad U=\begin{pmatrix}c_{0}&c_{1}\\
d_{0}&d_{1}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}b_{1}&-a_{1}\\
-b_{0}&a_{0}\end{pmatrix}.

Then
BA^{-1}=\frac{1}{R}U
and
\det U=R^{2}
. Let us choose
X\in\text{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
such that

UX^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}r&0\\
s&t\end{pmatrix},

where
r,t>0
and
0\leqslant s<t
. Let
\gamma^{\prime}
be the path
X\gamma
with vertices
a_{i}^{\prime}/b_{i}^{\prime}
, which has the same itinerary as
\gamma
and is also minimal, and let

A^{\prime}=\begin{pmatrix}a_{0}^{\prime}&a_{1}^{\prime}\\
b_{0}^{\prime}&b_{1}^{\prime}\end{pmatrix}.

Then we have

B=(BA^{-1})A=\frac{1}{R}UA=\frac{1}{R}\begin{pmatrix}r&0\\
s&t\end{pmatrix}A^{\prime}.

Now, let
(\lambda_{i})_{i\in\mathcal{I}^{*}}
be the itinerary of
\gamma
and
\delta
. Let
\Lambda_{0}=I
and

\Lambda_{i}=\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\
1&\lambda_{1}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\
1&\lambda_{2}\end{pmatrix}\dotsb\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\
1&\lambda_{i}\end{pmatrix},

for
i>0
(with a similar formula for
i<0
). Then

\begin{pmatrix}a_{i}^{\prime}&a_{i+1}^{\prime}\\
b_{i}^{\prime}&b_{i+1}^{\prime}\end{pmatrix}=A^{\prime}\Lambda_{i}\quad\text{and}\quad\begin{pmatrix}c_{i}&c_{i+1}\\
d_{i}&d_{i+1}\end{pmatrix}=B\Lambda_{i}.

So

\begin{pmatrix}c_{i}\\
d_{i}\end{pmatrix}=\frac{1}{R}\begin{pmatrix}r&0\\
s&t\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}a_{i}^{\prime}\\
b_{i}^{\prime}\end{pmatrix}.

Therefore
c_{i}=a_{i}^{\prime}r/R
, for
i\in\mathcal{I}
. Since
\gamma^{\prime}
is minimal, the greatest common divisor of the integers
a_{i}^{\prime}
must be 1. Hence
r
is divisible by
R
; say,
r=kR
, where
k\geqslant 1
. Then
kt=R
. We have

d_{i}=\frac{1}{k}\left(\frac{sa_{i}^{\prime}}{t}+b_{i}^{\prime}\right),\quad\text{for $i\in\mathcal{I}$.}

Hence
sa_{i}^{\prime}/t+b_{i}^{\prime}
is an integer, for each
i\in\mathcal{I}
. However,
0\leqslant s/t<1
, and since the greatest common divisor of the integers
a_{i}^{\prime}
is 1, it must be that
s=0
. Then
d_{i}=b_{i}^{\prime}/k
, and since the greatest common divisor of the integers
b_{i}^{\prime}
is 1, we must have
k=1
. We conclude that
\gamma^{\prime}
and
\delta
are equal, so
\gamma
and
\delta
are equivalent under the action of
\text{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
, as required.
∎

3
Tame integer tilings

We begin this section by adapting a well-known result from
\text{SL}_{2}
-tilings to
N
-tilings before establishing properties of tameness parameters of
N
-tilings that were promised in the introduction. Throughout,
N
is a nonzero integer.

The following lemma is elementary; it can be obtained quickly from
[
17
, Lemma 6.3]
.

Lemma 3.1
.

Consider any 3-by-3 integer matrix

A=\begin{pmatrix}a&b&c\\
d&e&f\\
g&h&i\end{pmatrix}

with determinant 0 for which

\det\!\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
d&e\end{pmatrix}=\det\!\begin{pmatrix}b&c\\
e&f\end{pmatrix}=\det\!\begin{pmatrix}d&e\\
g&h\end{pmatrix}=\det\!\begin{pmatrix}e&f\\
h&i\end{pmatrix}=N.

Then

d+f=(\Delta/N)e,

where
\Delta=af-cd=di-fg
.

The next theorem is similar to known results for
\text{SL}_{2}
-tilings (rather than
N
-tilings), notably
[
17
, Theorem 6.1]
. We give a sketch proof.

Theorem 3.2
.

An
N
-tiling
\mathbf{M}
is tame if and only if there are sequences
(r_{i})_{i\in\mathcal{I}^{*}}
and
(s_{j})_{j\in\mathcal{J}^{*}}
in
\frac{1}{N}\mathbb{Z}
such that

\displaystyle m_{i-1,j}+m_{i+1,j}

\displaystyle=r_{i}m_{i,j},\quad\text{for $i\in\mathcal{I}^{*},j\in\mathcal{J}$,}

\displaystyle m_{i,j-1}+m_{i,j+1}

\displaystyle=s_{j}m_{i,j},\quad\text{for $i\in\mathcal{I},j\in\mathcal{J}^{*}$.}

Furthermore, if
\mathbf{M}
is tame, then
(r_{i})_{i\in\mathcal{I}^{*}}
and
(s_{j})_{j\in\mathcal{J}^{*}}
are the unique sequences of rationals satisfying these recurrence relations.

Proof.

Suppose that
\mathbf{M}
is tame. Then, by Lemma
3.1
, we have

m_{i,j-1}+m_{i,j+1}=\frac{\Delta_{i,j}}{N}m_{i,j},\quad\text{for $i\in\mathcal{I}^{*},j\in\mathcal{J}^{*}$,}

where
\Delta_{i,j}=m_{i-1,j-1}m_{i,j+1}-m_{i-1,j+1}m_{i,j-1}=m_{i,j-1}m_{i+1,j+1}-m_{i,j+1}m_{i+1,j-1}
. This last pair of equations show that, for any
j\in\mathcal{J}^{*}
, we have
\Delta_{i,j}=\Delta_{i^{\prime},j}
, for all

i,i^{\prime}\in\mathcal{I}^{*}
. Consequently, there is
s_{j}\in\frac{1}{N}\mathbb{Z}
with

m_{i,j-1}+m_{i,j+1}=s_{j}m_{i,j},\quad\text{for $i\in\mathcal{I}^{*}$,}

as required. A similar argument gives the existence of the sequence
(r_{i})_{i\in\mathcal{I}^{*}}
.

For the converse, the recurrence relations imply that any three rows (or columns) of a 3-by-3 subblock
A
of
\mathbf{M}
are linearly dependent, so
\det A=0
. Hence
\mathbf{M}
is tame. The uniqueness assertion of the theorem is immediate.
∎

The next lemma shows that the vertical and horizontal tameness parameters are well defined (they do not depend on the pair of consecutive rows or columns chosen).

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
\mathbf{M}
be a tame
N
-tiling. For any indices
j,j^{\prime}\in\mathcal{J}
, the integer

m_{i,j}m_{i+1,j^{\prime}}-m_{i,j^{\prime}}m_{i+1,j}

is independent of
i
. Similarly, for
i,i^{\prime}\in\mathcal{I}
,
m_{i,j}m_{i^{\prime},j+1}-m_{i^{\prime},j}m_{i,j+1}
is independent of
j
.

The minor
m_{i,j}m_{i+1,j^{\prime}}-m_{i,j^{\prime}}m_{i+1,j}
is illustrated below.

\begin{matrix}m_{i,j}&\cdot&\cdot&\cdot&\cdot&\cdot&m_{i,j^{\prime}}\\
m_{i+1,j}&\cdot&\cdot&\cdot&\cdot&\cdot&m_{i+1,j^{\prime}}\end{matrix}

Proof.

Let us assume that
j<j^{\prime}
. By Theorem
3.2
, we have

\begin{pmatrix}m_{i,j^{\prime}}&m_{i+1,j^{\prime}}\\
m_{i,j^{\prime}+1}&m_{i+1,j^{\prime}+1}\end{pmatrix}=U\begin{pmatrix}m_{i,j}&m_{i+1,j}\\
m_{i,j+1}&m_{i+1,j+1}\end{pmatrix},

where

U=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
-1&s_{j^{\prime}}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
-1&s_{j^{\prime}-1}\end{pmatrix}\dotsb\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
-1&s_{j+1}\end{pmatrix}.

Let

J=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
-1&0\end{pmatrix}.

Observe that if
A
is a 2-by-2 matrix of determinant
N
, then
AJA^{T}=NJ
. Now, the integer
m_{i,j}m_{i+1,j^{\prime}}-m_{i,j^{\prime}}m_{i+1,j}
is the negative of the top-left entry of the matrix

\begin{pmatrix}m_{i,j^{\prime}}&m_{i+1,j^{\prime}}\\
m_{i,j^{\prime}+1}&m_{i+1,j^{\prime}+1}\end{pmatrix}J\begin{pmatrix}m_{i,j}&m_{i+1,j}\\
m_{i,j+1}&m_{i+1,j+1}\end{pmatrix}^{\!T}.

However, this matrix is equal to

U\begin{pmatrix}m_{i,j}&m_{i+1,j}\\
m_{i,j+1}&m_{i+1,j+1}\end{pmatrix}J\begin{pmatrix}m_{i,j}&m_{i+1,j}\\
m_{i,j+1}&m_{i+1,j+1}\end{pmatrix}^{\!T}=NUJ.

Since
NUJ
is independent of
i
, we see that
m_{i,j}m_{i+1,j^{\prime}}-m_{i,j^{\prime}}m_{i+1,j}
is independent of
i
, as required.
∎

For the remainder of this section we write

\Delta(i,i^{\prime};j,j^{\prime})=m_{i,j}m_{i^{\prime},j^{\prime}}-m_{i,j^{\prime}}m_{i^{\prime},j}.

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
\mathbf{M}
be a tame
N
-tiling. Let
i<i^{\prime}
and
j<j^{\prime}
. Then

N\Delta(i,i^{\prime};j,j^{\prime})=\Delta(i,i+1;j,j^{\prime})\Delta(i,i^{\prime};j,j+1).

The minors
\Delta(i,i^{\prime};j,j^{\prime})
,
\Delta(i,i+1;j,j^{\prime})
, and
\Delta(i,i^{\prime};j,j+1)
are illustrated below.

\begin{matrix}m_{i,j}&m_{i,j+1}&\cdot&\cdot&\cdot&\cdot&\cdot&m_{i,j^{\prime}}\\
m_{i+1,j}&\cdot&\cdot&\cdot&\cdot&\cdot&\cdot&m_{i+1,j^{\prime}}\\
\cdot&\cdot&\cdot&\cdot&\cdot&\cdot&\cdot&\cdot\\
\cdot&\cdot&\cdot&\cdot&\cdot&\cdot&\cdot&\cdot\\
\cdot&\cdot&\cdot&\cdot&\cdot&\cdot&\cdot&\cdot\\
\cdot&\cdot&\cdot&\cdot&\cdot&\cdot&\cdot&\cdot\\
m_{i^{\prime},j}&m_{i^{\prime},j+1}&\cdot&\cdot&\cdot&\cdot&\cdot&m_{i^{\prime},j^{\prime}}\end{matrix}

Proof.

We prove the result by induction on
n=(i^{\prime}-i)+(j^{\prime}-j)
, for
n=2,3,\dotsc
. When
n=2
we must have
i^{\prime}=i+1
and
j^{\prime}=j+1
, in which case the result follows immediately.

Suppose that
N\Delta(i,i^{\prime};j,t)=\Delta(i,i+1;j,t)\Delta(i,i^{\prime};j,j+1)
for some values
i<i^{\prime}
and
t=j+1,j+2,\dots,j^{\prime}
. We will prove that
N\Delta(i,i^{\prime};j,j^{\prime}+1)=\Delta(i,i+1;j,j^{\prime}+1)\Delta(i,i^{\prime};j,j+1)
. A
similar argument (which we omit) shows that
N\Delta(i,i^{\prime}+1;j,j^{\prime})=\Delta(i,i+1;j,j^{\prime})\Delta(i,i^{\prime}+1;j,j+1)
. Together these provide the inductive step that establishes the result.

Observe that
m_{t,j^{\prime}+1}=\lambda m_{t,j^{\prime}}-m_{t,j^{\prime}-1}
, for
t=i,i+1,\dots,i^{\prime}
, where
\lambda=s_{j}^{\prime}
from Theorem
3.2
.
Then

\displaystyle\Delta(i,i^{\prime};j,j^{\prime}+1)

\displaystyle=m_{i,j}m_{i^{\prime},j^{\prime}+1}-m_{i,j^{\prime}+1}m_{i^{\prime},j}

\displaystyle=m_{i,j}(\lambda m_{i^{\prime},j^{\prime}}-m_{i^{\prime},j^{\prime}-1})-(\lambda m_{i,j^{\prime}}-m_{i,j^{\prime}-1})m_{i^{\prime},j}

\displaystyle=\lambda\Delta(i,i^{\prime};j,j^{\prime})-\Delta(i,i^{\prime};j,j^{\prime}-1).

Hence

\displaystyle\Delta(i,i+1;j,j^{\prime}+1)\Delta(i,i^{\prime};j,j+1)

\displaystyle\qquad\hskip 44.0pt=(\lambda\Delta(i,i+1;j,j^{\prime})-\Delta(i,i+1;j,j^{\prime}-1))\Delta(i,i^{\prime};j,j+1)

\displaystyle\qquad\hskip 44.0pt=\lambda N\Delta(i,i^{\prime};j,j^{\prime})-N\Delta(i,i^{\prime};j,j^{\prime}-1)

\displaystyle\qquad\hskip 44.0pt=N\Delta(i,i^{\prime};j,j^{\prime}+1),

as required.
∎

We can now prove a result about tameness parameters promised in the introduction. To state this theorem, it is helpful to define the
total tameness parameter

T
of a tame
N
-tiling
\mathbf{M}
to be the
quotient of
N
by the greatest common divisor of all the minors of
\mathbf{M}
.

Theorem 3.5
.

Let
\mathbf{M}
be a tame
N
-tiling with vertical tameness parameter
R
, horizontal tameness parameter
S
, and total tameness parameter
T
. Then
RS=T
.

Proof.

Let
r
be the greatest common divisor of the minors from two consecutive rows of
\mathbf{M}
. By Lemma
3.3
, this definition does not depend on which pair of rows we choose. Let
s
be the greatest
common divisor of the minors from two consecutive columns of
\mathbf{M}
. Let
t
be the greatest common divisor of all minors of
\mathbf{M}
. We will prove that
rs=Nt
.

Observe that
\Delta(i,i+1;j,j^{\prime})
is divisible by
r
and
\Delta(i,i^{\prime};j,j+1)
is divisible by
s
, for
i<i^{\prime}
and
j<j^{\prime}
. By Lemma
3.4
,
N\Delta(i,i^{\prime};j,j^{\prime})
is divisible by
rs
. Hence
Nt
is
divisible by
rs
.

Conversely, applying Lemma
3.4
again, we see that
\Delta(i,i+1;j,j^{\prime})\Delta(i,i^{\prime};j,j+1)
is divisible by
Nt
, for all
i<i^{\prime}
and
j<j^{\prime}
. Remember from Lemma
3.3
that
\Delta(i,i+1;j,j^{\prime})
is
independent of
i
. Thus, by keeping
j
and
j^{\prime}
fixed and varying
i
and
i^{\prime}
, we see that
s\Delta(i,i+1;j,j^{\prime})
is divisible by
Nt
. Then by varying
j
and
j^{\prime}
we see that
rs
is divisible
by
Nt
, as required.
∎

4
Proof of Theorem
A

This section comprises a proof of Theorem
A
. We choose nonzero integers

,
L
,

,

, and

with

Consider two minimal paths
\gamma=(a_{i}/b_{i})_{i\in\mathcal{I}}
and
\delta=(c_{j}/d_{j})_{j\in\mathcal{J}}
in
\mathscr{F}_{R}
and
\mathscr{F}_{S}
. Let
\mathbf{M}
be the array with entries

m_{i,j}=K(a_{i}d_{j}-Lb_{i}c_{j})=K\begin{pmatrix}a_{i}&b_{i}\end{pmatrix}J_{L}\begin{pmatrix}c_{j}\\
d_{j}\end{pmatrix},\quad\text{where }J_{L}=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
-L&0\end{pmatrix}.

Let us check that
\mathbf{M}
is an
N
-tiling. We have

\begin{pmatrix}m_{i,j}&m_{i,j+1}\\
m_{i+1,j}&m_{i+1,j+1}\end{pmatrix}=K\begin{pmatrix}a_{i}&b_{i}\\
a_{i+1}&b_{i+1}\end{pmatrix}J_{L}\begin{pmatrix}c_{j}&c_{j+1}\\
d_{j}&d_{j+1}\end{pmatrix}.

The determinant of the matrix product on the right is

, which equals

\mathbf{M}
is indeed an
N
-tiling. Let us now prove that it is tame. By Lemma
2.1
, the itinerary

and

are linearly dependent, so the determinant of any 3-by-3 subblock of

is 0.

Next, we need to show that the map is constant on
\Gamma_{0}(L)
orbits of pairs of minimal paths. To see this, recall that with

A=\begin{pmatrix}a&bL\\
c&d\end{pmatrix}\in\Gamma_{0}(L)\quad\text{and}\quad B=\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
cL&d\end{pmatrix},

the action of

x
,
y

x^{\prime}
, and

A^{T}J_{L}B=J_{L}

(\gamma,\delta)
and
(\gamma^{\prime},\delta^{\prime})

are

equivalent, then they
give rise to the same tame
N
-tiling
m_{i,j}=K(a_{i}d_{j}-Lb_{i}c_{j})
.

We now need to check that
\mathbf{M}
has tameness parameters
(K,L,R,S)
. Observe that, for

and

\mathcal{J}

\begin{pmatrix}m_{i,j}&m_{i,j^{\prime}}\\

Taking determinants of both sides gives

Since
\delta
is minimal, the greatest common divisor of all the integers
c_{j}d_{j^{\prime}}-d_{j}c_{j^{\prime}}
is 1. Hence, for
N>0
, the greatest common divisor of all the integers
m_{i,j}m_{i+1,j^{\prime}}-m_{i,j^{\prime}}m_{i+1,j}
is

\mathbf{M}
is

\mathbf{M}
is

.

Next, suppose that all integers

. It cannot be that

is divisible by
p

would be divisible by
p
or all

would be divisible by

\frac{1}{K}\begin{pmatrix}m_{i,j}&m_{i,j^{\prime}}\\

a_{i^{\prime}}&b_{i^{\prime}}\end{pmatrix}J_{L}\begin{pmatrix}c_{j}&c_{j^{\prime}}\\

, for all

and

. It follows that all the minors of one of
\gamma
or
\delta
are divisible by

a_{i}d_{j}-Lb_{i}c_{j}

\mathbf{M}
is

. We have now proven that the map
m_{i,j}=K(a_{i}d_{j}-Lb_{i}c_{j})

Our next task is to prove that the map is injective. Suppose that pairs of minimal paths

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}a_{i+1}&b_{i+1}\end{pmatrix}J\begin{pmatrix}a_{i-1}\\

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{LS}\begin{pmatrix}a_{i+1}&b_{i+1}\end{pmatrix}J_{L}U^{T}JUJ_{L}^{T}\begin{pmatrix}a_{i-1}\\

Next, by Theorem

.

Now, for
n\geqslant n_{0}
, we have

m_{i,j}=K(a^{(n)}_{i}d^{(n)}_{j}-Lb^{(n)}_{i}c^{(n)}_{j})=K(a^{(n+1)}_{i}d^{(n+1)}_{j}-Lb^{(n+1)}_{i}c^{(n+1)}_{j}),

for
i\in\mathcal{I}_{n},j\in\mathcal{J}_{n}
. By the earlier injectivity argument we can see that the two pairs of paths
(a^{(n)}_{i}/b^{(n)}_{i},c^{(n)}_{j}/d^{(n)}_{j})
and
(a^{(n+1)}_{i}/b^{(n+1)}_{i},c^{(n+1)}_{j}/d^{(n+1)}_{j})
are
equivalent under
\Gamma_{0}(L)
. By applying an element of
\Gamma_{0}(L)
we can ensure that in fact the latter sequence is equal to the former, for
i\in\mathcal{I}_{n},j\in\mathcal{J}_{n}
. We then define
a_{i}/b_{i}=a^{(n)}_{i}/b^{(n)}_{i}
, for
n\geqslant n_{0}
, where
n
is chosen to be sufficiently large that
i\in\mathcal{I}_{n}
. We define
c_{j}/d_{j}
similarly. These two paths
a_{i}/b_{i}
and
c_{j}/d_{j}
are the required minimal paths in
\mathscr{F}_{R}
and
\mathscr{F}_{S}
to complete the proof.

Positive integer tilings

In this section we use Theorem
A
to classify positive integer tilings in terms of pairs of paths. The approach is motivated by
[
18
, Theorem 1.2]
for
\text{SL}_{2}
-tilings; indeed, that theorem is the special case
N=1
of Theorem
5.1
, to follow. For convenience, we assume that
N
is positive, which requires only a little loss of generality – after all, reflecting an
N
-tiling in any row or column gives an
(-N)
-tiling.

We endow the extended real line
\mathbb{R}_{\infty}=\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\}
with the cyclic order inherited from the unit circle by stereographic projection. With this cyclic order, a decreasing sequence of real numbers is a clockwise sequence, as is, for example, the sequence
-1
,
\infty
,
1
. Let us say that a path
\gamma
in
\mathscr{F}_{R}
is a
clockwise path
if the sequence of vertices forms a clockwise sequence in
\mathbb{R}_{\infty}
that does not complete a full cycle of
\mathbb{R}_{\infty}
. Here we have adopted the sleight of hand that a vertex
a/b
in
\mathscr{F}_{R}
, which was defined to be a pair
(a,b)
, is treated as the real number obtained from the quotient of
a
by
b
. It is helpful to consider
a/b
as both a formal pair and a real number, as one often does with fractions.

Any clockwise path
\gamma
has forward and backward limit points
\gamma_{\infty}
and
\gamma_{-\infty}
. If
\mathcal{I}
is bounded above, then
\gamma_{\infty}
is the final vertex of
\gamma
, and if
\mathcal{I}
is unbounded above, then
\gamma_{\infty}
is the forward limit in
\mathbb{R}_{\infty}
of the sequence of vertices of
\gamma
. We say that two clockwise paths
\gamma
in
\mathscr{F}_{R}
and
\delta
in
\mathscr{F}_{S}
are
compatible
if
(\gamma_{-\infty},\gamma_{\infty},\delta_{-\infty},\delta_{\infty})
is a clockwise sequence in
\mathbb{R}_{\infty}
, where possibly
\gamma_{\infty}=\delta_{-\infty}
and possibly
\delta_{\infty}=\gamma_{-\infty}
. Now, given a positive integer
L
, and
\delta\in\mathscr{F}_{S}
with vertices
c_{j}/d_{j}
, we define
L\delta
to be the path in
\mathscr{F}_{LS}
with vertices
(Lc_{j})/d_{j}
. To apply Theorem
A
, we are interested in the property that the pair
(\gamma,L\delta)
in
\mathscr{F}_{R}\times\mathscr{F}_{LS}
is compatible (
not
the pair
(\gamma,\delta)
). We need to check that this property is invariant under the action of
\Gamma_{0}(L)
on
\mathscr{F}_{R}\times\mathscr{F}_{S}
. This is a straightforward calculation which relies on the fact that elements of
\Gamma_{0}(L)
act on
\mathbb{R}_{\infty}
as orientation-preserving homeomorphism. We omit the details.

We say that an
N
-tiling is
positive
if all its entries are positive and
negative
if all its entries are negative. By Theorem
A
, we can write the entries of a tame
N
-tiling
\mathbf{M}
as
m_{i,j}=K(a_{i}d_{j}-Lb_{i}c_{j})
. Under this correspondence, exchanging the path
a_{i}/b_{i}
for the path
(-a_{i})/(-b_{i})
corresponds to replacing
\mathbf{M}
with
-\mathbf{M}
. For this reason it is convenient to consider positive and negative
N
-tilings together when describing them using paths.

Theorem 5.1
.

For

, let
\mathbf{M}
be a tame
N
-tiling with tameness parameters
(K,L,R,S)
determined by the pair of minimal paths
\gamma
in
\mathscr{F}_{R}
and

in

. Then
\mathbf{M}
is positive or negative if and only if
\gamma
and

are compatible clockwise paths.

Here the paths
\gamma
and

‘determine’
\mathbf{M}
according to the correspondence of Theorem

.

To illustrate Theorem

, consider the minimal paths

Proof of Theorem
5.1
.

Suppose first that
\gamma
and
L\delta
(with vertices
a_{i}/b_{i}
and
Lc_{j}/d_{j}
) are compatible clockwise paths. The orbit of
\infty
under
\Gamma_{0}(L)
is dense in
\mathbb{R}_{\infty}
, so there is an element of this orbit that lies clockwise of
Lc_{0}/d_{0}
and anticlockwise of
Lc_{1}/d_{1}
. By mapping this element to
\infty
, and adjusting
\gamma
and
L\delta
accordingly, we can assume that
Lc_{0}/d_{0}<Lc_{1}/d_{1}
. Additionally, by applying the negative identity matrix
-I
if need be (which belongs to
\Gamma_{0}(L)
), we can assume that
b_{0}>0
. We then have

\frac{Lc_{0}}{d_{0}}<\frac{Lc_{-1}}{d_{-1}}<\dotsb<\frac{a_{2}}{b_{2}}<\frac{a_{1}}{b_{1}}<\frac{a_{0}}{b_{0}}<\frac{a_{-1}}{b_{-1}}<\frac{a_{-2}}{b_{-2}}<\dotsb<\frac{Lc_{2}}{d_{2}}<\frac{Lc_{1}}{d_{1}}.

(5.1)

Observe that
b_{i-1}b_{i}
is positive for all
i\in\mathcal{I}^{\prime}
, because

\frac{R}{b_{i-1}b_{i}}=\frac{a_{i-1}}{b_{i-1}}-\frac{a_{i}}{b_{i}}.

(5.2)

Hence
b_{i}>0
, for
i\in\mathcal{I}
. By replacing
c_{j}/d_{j}
with
(-c_{j})/(-d_{j})
if need be (which changes
\delta
and reverses the sign of every entry of
\mathbf{M}
) we can also assume that
d_{0}>0
. Similar arguments to those used already then show that
d_{j}>0
for
j\leqslant 0
and
d_{j}<0
for
j>0
. Lastly, because
a_{i}/b_{i}>Lc_{j}/d_{j}
, for
i\in\mathcal{I}
and
j\leqslant 0
, we see from the equation

m_{i,j}=K(a_{i}d_{j}-Lb_{i}c_{j})=Kb_{i}d_{j}\left(\frac{a_{i}}{b_{i}}-\frac{Lc_{j}}{d_{j}}\right)

(5.3)

that
m_{i,j}>0
. Similarly,
m_{i,j}>0
if
j>0
. So
\mathbf{M}
is positive.

Conversely, suppose that
\mathbf{M}
is positive or negative. By replacing
c_{j}/d_{j}
with
(-c_{j})/(-d_{j})
if need be we can assume that
\mathbf{M}
is positive. Also, by applying a suitable element of
\Gamma_{0}(L)
, we can assume that

and

. A short calculation then shows that
d_{1}<0
.

Consider (
5.3
) with

. Since

and
m_{i,1}>0
, we have that
b_{i}>0
and
Lc_{0}/d_{0}<a_{i}/b_{i}<Lc_{1}/d_{1}
, for

. We can then see from (
5.2
) that the sequence
a_{i}/b_{i}
is decreasing.

Now, choose any

. From (
5.3
) we see that
Lc_{j}/d_{j}>a_{i}/b_{i}
if and only if

. Let us prove by induction that
d_{j}<0
for

. We know that
d_{1}<0
. Suppose that

, for some

. If

, then
-d_{j-1}c_{j}=S
, so

, in which case

– a contradiction. If

, then

\frac{Lc_{j}}{d_{j}}=\frac{Lc_{j-1}}{d_{j-1}}-\frac{LS}{d_{j-1}d_{j}}>\frac{Lc_{j-1}}{d_{j-1}}>\frac{a_{i}}{b_{i}},

which implies that

– another contradiction. So we must have

, as required. Hence
Lc_{j-1}/d_{j-1}-Lc_{j}/d_{j}=LS/(d_{j-1}d_{j})>0
, for

.

A similar argument shows that

,
a_{i}/b_{i}>Lc_{j}/d_{j}
, and
Lc_{j-1}/d_{j-1}>Lc_{j}/d_{j}
, for
j\leqslant 0
. Consequently, the chain of inequalities (
5.1
) is satisfied, so
\gamma
and
\delta
are compatible clockwise paths.
∎

6
Positive rational friezes

In this section we prove Theorem
B
. Our approach for positive rational friezes is in the same spirit as that of
[
15
]
for positive integer friezes. The definition of a frieze that we use is the standard one (termed a ‘regular frieze’ in
[
17
]
).

Definition
.

A
rational frieze
of width
n
is a function

\mathbf{F}\colon\{(i,j)\in\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{Z}:0\leqslant i-j\leqslant n\}\longrightarrow\mathbb{Q}

with entries
m_{i,j}=\mathbf{F}(i,j)
such that

•

m_{i,i}=m_{i+n,i}=0
, for
i\in\mathbb{Z}
(top and bottom rows of zeros),

•

m_{i+1,i}=m_{i+n,i+1}=1
, for
i\in\mathbb{Z}
(second and second-last rows of ones),

•

m_{i,j}m_{i+1,j+1}-m_{i,j+1}m_{i+1,j}=1
, for
0<i-j<n
(diamond rule).

A
positive rational frieze
is a rational frieze for which all nonzero entries are positive.

We encountered an example of a positive rational frieze already, in Figure
5
, which has width 7. Coxeter proved that positive rational friezes (or indeed positive real friezes) are periodic, so the entries
m_{i,j}
lie in
\tfrac{1}{N}\mathbb{Z}
, for some positive integer
N
. In this case we describe the positive rational frieze as a positive frieze over
\tfrac{1}{N}\mathbb{Z}
.

It is well known and straightforward to prove that there is a unique way to extend a positive frieze
\mathbf{F}
to a tame bi-infinite
\text{SL}_{2}
-tiling
\mathbf{M}
(indexed by
\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{Z}
). The
\text{SL}_{2}
-tiling
\mathbf{M}
has entries
m_{i,j}
that satisfy
m_{i+n,j}=m_{i,j+n}=-m_{i,j}
, for
i,j\in\mathbb{Z}
. This allows us the liberty of regarding friezes as special types of
\text{SL}_{2}
-tilings.

Given any positive frieze over
\tfrac{1}{N}\mathbb{Z}
of width
n
– thought of an
\text{SL}_{2}
-tiling over
\tfrac{1}{N}\mathbb{Z}
– we can multiply each entry of the
\text{SL}_{2}
-tiling by
N
to give an
N^{2}
-tiling with
integral
entries. We define the
greatest common divisor
of a positive frieze over
\tfrac{1}{N}\mathbb{Z}
to be the greatest common divisor of this
N^{2}
-tiling. Notice that a positive frieze over
\tfrac{1}{N}\mathbb{Z}
is also a positive frieze over
\tfrac{1}{M}\mathbb{Z}
for any multiple
M
of
N
– and the greatest common divisor depends on the choice of denominator. Typically it makes sense to work with the
smallest
choice of
N
; however, we do not require that choice in our calculations.

Lemma 6.1
.

The tameness parameters
(K,L,R,S)
of this
N^{2}
-tiling satisfy
L=1
and
R=S
.

Proof.

Let us denote the entries of this bi-infinite integral
N^{2}
-tiling
\mathbf{M}
by
m_{i,j}
, for
i,j\in\mathbb{Z}
. Coxeter
[
7
]
observed that positive friezes are not only periodic but in fact they have a glide reflection symmetry. This symmetry, inherited by the
N^{2}
-tiling, says that
m_{i,j}=m_{j,i-n}
, for
i,j\in\mathbb{Z}
.

Let
t
be the greatest common divisor of all the minors of
\mathbf{M}
, and let
K
be the greatest common divisor of all entries of
\mathbf{M}
. Clearly,
K^{2}\mid t
. Conversely, we observe that
t
is a factor of the minor

m_{i,j}m_{j,i+n}-m_{i,i+n}m_{j,j}=m_{i,j}^{2},

for any
i,j\in\mathbb{Z}
. Hence
t\mid K^{2}
. It follows that
t=K^{2}
. Recall that
N^{2}=K^{2}LRS
and
RS=N^{2}/t
, by Theorem
3.5
. Therefore
L=1
.

Equality of the vertical and horizontal tameness parameters follows immediately from the glide reflection property.
∎

A
closed clockwise path
in
\mathscr{F}_{R}
of length
n
is a path
\langle a_{0}/b_{0},a_{1}/b_{1},\dots,a_{n}/b_{n}\rangle
in
\mathscr{F}_{R}
, where
a_{n}=-a_{0}
and
b_{n}=-b_{0}
, and where the values of
a_{0}/b_{0},a_{1}/b_{1},\dots,a_{n-1}/b_{n-1}
on
\mathbb{R}_{\infty}
are in strict clockwise order and complete less than one cycle of
\mathbb{R}_{\infty}
. An example of a closed clockwise path is shown in Figure
6
(a). Any closed clockwise path can be extended to a bi-infinite path by defining
a_{i+n}=-a_{i}
and
b_{i+n}=-b_{i}
, for
i\in\mathbb{Z}
. We will move freely between a finite closed clockwise path and its bi-infinite counterpart.

Let us now prove Theorem
B
. We remark that the formula
m_{i,j}=\frac{1}{R}(a_{j}b_{i}-b_{j}a_{i})
in that theorem specifies a positive frieze over
\tfrac{1}{N}\mathbb{Z}
of width
n
even when
a_{i}/b_{i}
is a closed clockwise path that is
not
minimal. The requirement that
a_{i}/b_{i}
is minimal is present in Theorem
B
to give a clean one-to-one correspondence. For an indication of why this is necessary, observe that two non-minimal paths of the form
(2u_{i})/v_{i}
and
u_{i}/(2v_{i})
, which typically are not
\text{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
-equivalent, have the same image under the map
m_{i,j}=\frac{1}{R}(a_{j}b_{i}-b_{j}a_{i})
.

Proof of Theorem
B
.

Observe that
1/R=K/N
. The map from paths to tilings determined by the formula
m_{i,j}=\tfrac{K}{N}(a_{j}b_{i}-b_{j}a_{i})
is, up to scaling by a factor
1/N
, the restriction of the one-to-one correspondence of Theorem
A
(with
L=1
and
S=R
) to the collection of those pairs of paths
(\gamma,-\gamma)
, where
\gamma
is a minimal clockwise path in
\mathscr{F}_{R}
of length
n
. It follows, then, that all we need to check is that the image of a pair
(\gamma,-\gamma)
is a positive frieze over
\tfrac{1}{N}\mathbb{Z}
of width
n
and greatest common divisor
K
, and that the preimage of any positive frieze over
\tfrac{1}{N}\mathbb{Z}
of width
n
and greatest common divisor
K
is such a pair
(\gamma,-\gamma)
.

For the first of these two tasks, let
\gamma
be a minimal closed clockwise path in
\mathscr{F}_{R}
of length
n
with vertices
a_{i}/b_{i}
, for
i\in\mathbb{Z}
(where
a_{i+n}=-a_{i}
and
b_{i+n}=-b_{i}
). Then, by Theorem
A
with
\delta=-\gamma
, the bi-infinite array
\mathbf{M}
with entries
m_{i,j}=\tfrac{K}{N}(a_{j}b_{i}-b_{j}a_{i})
is a tame
\text{SL}_{2}
-tiling over
\tfrac{1}{N}\mathbb{Z}
. Let us check that
\mathbf{M}
is in fact a rational frieze of width
n
. To do this, notice, for example, that

m_{i+n,i+1}=\frac{1}{R}(a_{i+1}b_{i+n}-b_{i+1}a_{i+n})=\frac{1}{R}(-a_{i+1}b_{i}+b_{i+1}a_{i})=1.

The other properties of a rational frieze can be proved in a similar way.

The entries of the frieze belong to
\tfrac{1}{N}\mathbb{Z}
. Since
Nm_{i,j}=K(a_{j}b_{i}-b_{j}a_{i})
, and
\gamma
is minimal, we see that the greatest common divisor of the integers
Nm_{i,j}
is
K
.

We must show that
m_{i,j}>0
, for
0<i-j<n
. For this, notice that we can assume after applying an element of
\text{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
that
a_{0}/b_{0}>a_{1}/b_{1}>\dotsb>a_{n-1}/b_{n-1}
and
b_{0}>0
. Then
b_{i}>0
, for
i=1,2,\dots,n-1
. Observe that

m_{i,j}=\frac{1}{R}(a_{j}b_{i}-b_{j}a_{i})=\frac{b_{i}b_{j}}{R}\left(\frac{a_{j}}{b_{j}}-\frac{a_{i}}{b_{i}}\right).

(6.1)

Hence
m_{i,j}>0
, for
i,j\in\{0,1,\dots,n-1\}
with
i>j
. The glide reflection symmetry ensures that
m_{i,j}>0
for all
0<i-j<n
. Hence
\mathbf{M}
is indeed a positive frieze over
\tfrac{1}{N}\mathbb{Z}
of width
n
and greatest common divisor
K
.

For the second of the two tasks, let
\mathbf{M}
be a positive frieze over
\tfrac{1}{N}\mathbb{Z}
of width
n
and greatest common divisor
K
. After multiplying each entry of this frieze by
N
we obtain a tame
N^{2}
-tiling
\mathbf{M}^{\prime}
with tameness parameters
(K,1,R,R)
, by Lemma
6.1
. Let us write
m_{i,j}^{\prime}
for the
(i,j)
th entry of this
N^{2}
-tiling. From Theorem
A
we can find minimal paths
a_{i}/b_{i}
and
c_{j}/d_{j}
in
\mathscr{F}_{R}
with
m^{\prime}_{i,j}=K(a_{i}d_{j}-b_{i}c_{j})
, for
i,j\in\mathbb{Z}
. Furthermore, after applying an element of
\text{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
, we can assume that
b_{0}=0
and
a_{0}>0
.

Since
m^{\prime}_{i,i}=0
we see that
a_{i}d_{i}=b_{i}c_{i}
, so there exists
\lambda_{i}\in\mathbb{Q}
with
c_{i}=\lambda_{i}a_{i}
and
d_{i}=\lambda_{i}b_{i}
, for
i\in\mathbb{Z}
. We have
m^{\prime}_{i+1,i}=N
and
N=KR
, so

N=K(a_{i+1}d_{i}-b_{i+1}c_{i})=K\lambda_{i}(a_{i+1}b_{i}-b_{i+1}a_{i})=-KR\lambda_{i}.

Therefore
\lambda_{i}=-1
, so
\delta=-\gamma
. From the equations
m^{\prime}_{i+n,0}=-m^{\prime}_{i,0}
and
m^{\prime}_{i+n,1}=-m^{\prime}_{i,1}
we see that
a_{i+n}=-a_{i}
and
b_{i+n}=-b_{i}
. Also, since
m^{\prime}_{i,0}=a_{0}b_{i}
, we have
b_{i}>0
, for
i=1,2,\dots,n-1
. Finally, from (
6.1
), we obtain
a_{1}/b_{1}>a_{2}/b_{2}>\dotsb>a_{n-1}/b_{n-1}
, so
\gamma
is a closed clockwise path, as required.
∎

7
Zero tilings

It is necessary for us to consider 0-tilings because they arise as cross sections of hypertilings. We choose the simplest definition of a tame 0-tiling to suit our purposes, which corresponds to setting
L=0
in Theorem
A
.

Definition
.

A
0
-tiling
is a function
\mathbf{M}\colon\mathcal{I}\times\mathcal{J}\longrightarrow\mathbb{Z}
with the property that every 2-by-2 subblock has determinant
0
. A 0-tiling is
tame
if there are positive integers
K
,
R
, and
S
and minimal paths
a_{i}/b_{i}
in
\mathscr{F}_{R}
and
c_{j}/d_{j}
in
\mathscr{F}_{S}
with
m_{i,j}=Ka_{i}d_{j}
, for
i\in\mathcal{I},j\in\mathcal{J}
.

Note that
R
and
S
are not necessarily unique.

To illustrate this definition, consider the 0-tilings

\begin{pmatrix}1&0&2\\
0&0&0\\
1&0&2\end{pmatrix}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\begin{pmatrix}[r]2&0&\!\!\!-2\\
0&0&0\\
\!-2&0&2\end{pmatrix}.

The first matrix is the tensor product of the vectors
(1,0,1)
and
(1,0,2)
; however, it is not tame because the sum of the top and bottom rows is not a scalar multiple of the middle row (see Lemma
7.1
, to follow). The second matrix is a tame 0-tiling with tameness parameters
(2,0,1,1)
. It is given by the formula
m_{i,j}=2a_{i}d_{j}
, where
a_{i}/b_{i}
and
c_{j}/d_{j}
are the vertices of the paths
\langle\tfrac{1}{0},\tfrac{0}{1},\tfrac{-1}{0}\rangle
and
\langle\tfrac{0}{1},\tfrac{-1}{0},\tfrac{0}{-1}\rangle
.

The next lemma is a version of Theorem
3.2
for 0-tilings.

Lemma 7.1
.

Let
\mathbf{M}
be a tame 0-tiling. Then there are sequences of rationals
(r_{i})_{i\in\mathcal{I}^{*}}
and
(s_{j})_{j\in\mathcal{J}^{*}}
such that

m_{i-1,j}+m_{i+1,j}=r_{i}m_{i,j}\quad\text{and}\quad m_{i,j-1}+m_{i,j+1}=s_{j}m_{i,j},

for
i\in\mathcal{I}^{*},j\in\mathcal{J}^{*}
.

Proof.

We can write
m_{i,j}=Ka_{i}d_{j}
for paths
a_{i}/b_{i}
in
\mathscr{F}_{R}
and
c_{j}/d_{j}
in
\mathscr{F}_{S}
. By Lemma
2.1
there are sequences of rationals
r_{i}
and
s_{j}
with
a_{i-1}+a_{i+1}=r_{i}a_{i}
and
d_{j-1}+d_{j+1}=s_{j}d_{j}
, for
i\in\mathcal{I}^{*}
and
j\in\mathcal{J}^{*}
. The required result follows immediately.
∎

8
Tame integer hypertilings

We now turn our attention to integer hypertilings – and prove Theorem
D
(we prove Theorem
C
in the next section). The more straightforward part of Theorem
D
is that three paths determine an integer hypertiling, so let us begin with that.

Theorem 8.1
.

Let
A
be a nonsingular Bhargava cube, and let
u_{0i}/u_{1i}
,
v_{0j}/v_{1j}
, and
w_{0k}/w_{1k}
be three minimal paths in
\mathscr{F}_{R}
,
\mathscr{F}_{S}
, and
\mathscr{F}_{T}
. Then the function

m_{ijk}=\sum_{p,q,r=0}^{1}A_{pqr}u_{ip}v_{jq}w_{kr},\qquad\text{$(i,j,k)\in\mathcal{I}\times\mathcal{J}\times\mathcal{K}$,}

is a tame
N
-hypertiling, where
N=R^{2}S^{2}T^{2}\Det A
.

Proof.

Let
\mathbf{M}\colon\mathcal{I}\times\mathcal{J}\times\mathcal{K}\longrightarrow\mathbb{Z}
be the array with entries
m_{i,j,k}
. Observe that the 2-by-2-by-2 subblock

\left(\begin{matrix}m_{i,j,k}&m_{i,j+1,k}\\
m_{i+1,j,k}&m_{i+1,j+1,k}\end{matrix}\quad\middle|\quad\begin{matrix}m_{i,j,k+1}&m_{i,j+1,k+1}\\
m_{i+1,j,k+1}&m_{i+1,j+1,k+1}\end{matrix}\right)

is the image of
A
under the triple of 2-by-2 integer matrices

\left(\begin{pmatrix}u_{i,0}&u_{i,1}\\
u_{i+1,0}&u_{i+1,1}\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}v_{j,0}&v_{j,1}\\
v_{j+1,0}&v_{j+1,1}\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}w_{k,0}&w_{k,1}\\
w_{k+1,0}&w_{k+1,1}\end{pmatrix}\right).

Therefore it has hyperdeterminant
N=R^{2}S^{2}T^{2}\Det A
, so
\mathbf{M}
is an
N
-hypertiling.

Next we show that each cross section of
\mathbf{M}
is a tame integer tiling. Consider the restriction of
\mathbf{M}
to
\mathcal{I}\times\mathcal{J}\times\{k_{0}\}
, for some integer
k_{0}\in\mathcal{K}
. Let
m_{i,j}=m_{i,j,k_{0}}
, for
i\in\mathcal{I}
and

j\in\mathcal{J}
. Let

B=\begin{pmatrix}A_{0,0,0}w_{k_{0},0}+A_{0,0,1}w_{k_{0},1}&A_{0,1,0}w_{k_{0},0}+A_{0,1,1}w_{k_{0},1}\\
A_{1,0,0}w_{k_{0},0}+A_{1,0,1}w_{k_{0},1}&A_{1,1,0}w_{k_{0},0}+A_{1,1,1}w_{k_{0},1}\end{pmatrix}.

Observe that
B
is not the zero matrix because
A
is nonsingular. We have

m_{i,j}=\begin{pmatrix}u_{i,0}&u_{i,1}\end{pmatrix}B\begin{pmatrix}v_{j,0}\\
v_{j,1}\end{pmatrix}.

Let
K
be the greatest common divisor of
B
. Using the Smith normal form we can find
X,Y\in\text{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
with

B=KX^{T}\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
-L&0\end{pmatrix}Y,

for some integer
L
. We define

\begin{pmatrix}u_{i,0}^{\prime}\\
u_{i,1}^{\prime}\end{pmatrix}=X\begin{pmatrix}u_{i,0}\\
u_{i,1}\end{pmatrix}\quad\text{and}\quad\begin{pmatrix}v_{j,0}^{\prime}\\
v_{j,1}^{\prime}\end{pmatrix}=Y\begin{pmatrix}v_{j,0}\\
v_{j,1}\end{pmatrix}.

Then
u_{i,0}^{\prime}/u_{i,1}^{\prime}
and
v_{j,0}^{\prime}/v_{j,1}^{\prime}
are minimal paths in
\mathscr{F}_{R}
and
\mathscr{F}_{S}
. Observe that

m_{i,j}=K\begin{pmatrix}u_{i,0}&u_{i,1}\end{pmatrix}X^{T}\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
-L&0\end{pmatrix}Y\begin{pmatrix}v_{j,0}\\
v_{j,1}\end{pmatrix}=K(u_{i,0}^{\prime}v_{j,1}^{\prime}-Lu_{i,1}^{\prime}v_{j,0}^{\prime}).

Hence the restriction of
\mathbf{M}
to
\mathcal{I}\times\mathcal{J}\times\{k_{0}\}
is a tame
(K^{2}LRS)
-tiling, by Theorem
A
(or by definition if
L=0
).

It remains to prove that
\mathbf{M}
is synchronised. To do this, we will consider the 3-by-2 matrix

\begin{pmatrix}m_{i-1,j-1,k}&m_{i-1,j,k-1}\\
m_{i,j-1,k}&m_{i,j,k-1}\\
m_{i+1,j-1,k}&m_{i+1,j,k-1}\end{pmatrix}

and prove that
m_{i-1,j-1,k}m_{i,j,k-1}-m_{i-1,j,k-1}m_{i,j-1,k}=m_{i,j-1,k}m_{i+1,j,k-1}-m_{i,j,k-1}m_{i+1,j-1,k}
. Other cases can be handled similarly. Now, by Lemma
2.1
, there exists a rational
\mu_{i}
with
(u_{i-1,0},u_{i-1,1})+(u_{i+1,0},u_{i+1,1})=\mu_{i}(u_{i,0},u_{i,1})
. Hence
m_{i-1,q,r}+m_{i+1,q,r}=\mu_{i}m_{i,q,r}
, for
q\in\mathcal{J},r\in\mathcal{K}
. It follows that

\displaystyle(m_{i-1,j-1,k}+m_{i+1,j-1,k})m_{i,j,k-1}

\displaystyle=\mu_{i}m_{i,j,k-1}m_{i,j-1,k}

\displaystyle=(m_{i-1,j,k-1}+m_{i+1,j,k-1})m_{i,j-1,k}.

Rearranging this gives the required identity.
∎

Our next task is to prove that any tame integer hypertiling can be realised by three paths in Farey graphs. Some preliminary steps are required to reach this goal.

Lemma 8.2
.

Consider a 3-by-3-by-3 subblock

M=\left(\begin{matrix}a_{1}&a_{2}&a_{3}\\
a_{4}&a_{5}&a_{6}\\
a_{7}&a_{8}&a_{9}\end{matrix}\ \middle|\ \begin{matrix}b_{1}&b_{2}&b_{3}\\
b_{4}&b_{5}&b_{6}\\
b_{7}&b_{8}&b_{9}\end{matrix}\ \middle|\ \begin{matrix}c_{1}&c_{2}&c_{3}\\
c_{4}&c_{5}&c_{6}\\
c_{7}&c_{8}&c_{9}\end{matrix}\right)

of a tame integer hypertiling
\mathbf{M}
. There exists a rational number
\lambda
with
a_{i}+c_{i}=\lambda b_{i}
, for
i=1,2,\dots,9
.

Proof.

There are six vertical cross sections through
M
with index sets
\{1,2,3\}
,
\{4,5,6\}
,
\{7,8,9\}
,
\{1,4,7\}
,
\{2,5,8\}
, and
\{3,6,9\}
. Let us call these
vertical index sets
. We have three observations.

(a)

Let
\mathcal{I}
be a vertical index set. By Theorem
3.2
and Lemma
7.1
, there is a rational number
\lambda_{\mathcal{I}}
with
a_{i}+c_{i}=\lambda_{\mathcal{I}}b_{i}
, for
i\in\mathcal{I}
.

(b)

The rational
\lambda_{\mathcal{I}}
from observation (a) is unique unless
b_{i}=0
, for
i\in\mathcal{I}
. In the exceptional case when
b_{i}=0
, for
i\in\mathcal{I}
, the rational
\lambda_{\mathcal{I}}
can be chosen arbitrarily.

(c)

Each index
i\in\{1,2,\dots,9\}
belongs to precisely two vertical index sets,
\mathcal{I}
and
\mathcal{J}
. If
b_{i}\neq 0
, then
\lambda_{\mathcal{I}}=(a_{i}+c_{i})/b_{i}=\lambda_{\mathcal{J}}
.

Suppose that
b_{5}\neq 0
. If any one of the four neighbouring entries
b_{2}
,
b_{4}
,
b_{6}
, or
b_{8}
is nonzero, then one can show that all the rationals
\lambda_{\mathcal{I}}
can be chosen to be equal, so there exists a rational
\lambda
as required. We sketch a proof of this elementary assertion. Suppose without loss of generality that
b_{2}\neq 0
. If
b_{1}=b_{4}=b_{7}=0
,
b_{3}=b_{6}=b_{9}=0
, or
b_{7}=b_{8}=b_{9}=0
, then the existence of
\lambda
is easily established. Otherwise, one of
b_{1}
or
b_{4}
is nonzero, one of
b_{3}
or
b_{6}
is nonzero, and one of
b_{7}
,
b_{8}
, or
b_{9}
is nonzero. We can then apply observations (a)–(c) to deduce the existence of
\lambda
.

Suppose now that
b_{5}=0
. If any of the four corner entries
b_{1}
,
b_{3}
,
b_{7}
, or
b_{9}
is nonzero, then, with similar reasoning to the previous paragraph, we can establish the existence of the required rational
\lambda
.

In summary, the lemma has been established for all cases apart from those in which the middle cross section of entries
b_{i}
has one of the forms

\begin{pmatrix}\ast&0&\ast\\
0&\ast&0\\
\ast&0&\ast\end{pmatrix}\quad\text{or}\quad\begin{pmatrix}0&\ast&0\\
\ast&0&\ast\\
0&\ast&0\end{pmatrix}.

We will consider the second case; the first case can be handled similarly. In this second case, if any of the entries labelled
\ast
are 0, then it is straightforward to establish the existence of the rational
\lambda
, so let us assume they are all nonzero. From observations (a)–(c) we can see that
\lambda_{1,2,3}=\lambda_{2,5,8}=\lambda_{7,8,9}
and
\lambda_{1,4,7}=\lambda_{4,5,6}=\lambda_{3,6,9}
. We denote the first of these values by
\lambda
and the second by
\mu
; we must show that
\lambda=\mu
.

From the fact that each cross section of
M
is a tame 3-by-3 integer tiling, we can see that

M=\left(\begin{matrix}x&q&-x\\
p&y&-p\\
-x&-q&x\end{matrix}\quad\middle|\quad\begin{matrix}0&b&0\\
a&0&-a\\
0&-b&0\end{matrix}\quad\middle|\quad\begin{matrix}-x&s&x\\
r&-y&-r\\
x&-s&-x\end{matrix}\right),

for some integers
a
,
b
,
x
,
y
,
p
,
q
,
r
, and
s
. Consider the 3-by-2 matrix

A=\begin{pmatrix}p&q\\
a&b\\
r&s\end{pmatrix}.

By the synchronised property we have
pb-qa=as-br
, so

\mu=\frac{p+r}{a}=\frac{q+s}{b}=\lambda,

as required.
∎

Lemma
8.2
established the existence of a rational number
\lambda
subject to certain properties; in fact, there is only one such rational number that satisfies these properties. The next lemma is a stronger uniqueness assertion, for smaller subblocks.

Lemma 8.3
.

Consider a 2-by-2-by-3 subblock

M=\left(\begin{matrix}a_{1}&a_{2}\\
a_{3}&a_{4}\end{matrix}\ \middle|\ \begin{matrix}b_{1}&b_{2}\\
b_{3}&b_{4}\end{matrix}\ \middle|\ \begin{matrix}c_{1}&c_{2}\\
c_{3}&c_{4}\end{matrix}\right)

of a tame integer hypertiling
\mathbf{M}
. There exists a unique rational number
\lambda
with
a_{i}+c_{i}=\lambda b_{i}
, for
i=1,2,3,4
.

Proof.

The existence of
\lambda
was established in Lemma
8.2
; we have only to prove uniqueness. This is immediate, for we must have
b_{i}\neq 0
, for some
i\in\{1,2,3,4\}
, in which case
\lambda=(a_{i}+c_{i})/b_{i}
.
∎

The following theorem is a version of Theorem
3.2
for integer hypertilings rather than tilings. There is a converse to the theorem (with some modifications); we omit this because we do not need it.

Theorem 8.4
.

Let
\mathbf{M}
be a tame integer hypertiling. Then there are sequences of rationals
(r_{i})_{i\in\mathcal{I}^{*}}
,
(s_{j})_{j\in\mathcal{J}^{*}}
, and
(t_{k})_{k\in\mathcal{K}^{*}}
such that

\displaystyle m_{i-1,j,k}+m_{i+1,j,k}=r_{i}m_{i,j,k},\quad\text{for $i\in\mathcal{I}^{*},j\in\mathcal{J},k\in\mathcal{K}$,}

\displaystyle m_{i,j-1,k}+m_{i,j+1,k}=s_{j}m_{i,j,k},\quad\text{for $i\in\mathcal{I},j\in\mathcal{J}^{*},k\in\mathcal{K}$,}

\displaystyle m_{i,j,k-1}+m_{i,j,k+1}=t_{k}m_{i,j,k},\quad\text{for $i\in\mathcal{I},j\in\mathcal{J},k\in\mathcal{K}^{*}$.}

Furthermore, the sequences
r_{i}
,
s_{j}
, and
t_{k}
are uniquely determined by
\mathbf{M}
.

Proof.

Let us prove the existence and uniqueness of the sequence
t_{k}
; the other two cases are similar. Consider some integer
k\in\mathcal{K}^{*}
. Let
M_{i,j}=(A_{k-1}\mid A_{k}\mid A_{k+1})
, where

A_{\ell}=\begin{pmatrix}m_{i-1,j-1,\ell}&m_{i-1,j,\ell}&m_{i-1,j+1,\ell}\\
m_{i,j-1,\ell}&m_{i,j,\ell}&m_{i,j+1,\ell}\\
m_{i+1,j-1,\ell}&m_{i+1,j,\ell}&m_{i+1,j+1,\ell}\end{pmatrix}.

By Lemma
8.2
, there exists
\lambda_{i,j}\in\mathbb{Q}
with
m_{p,q,k-1}+m_{p,q,k+1}=\lambda_{i,j}m_{p,q,k}
, for
p\in\{i-1,i,i+1\}
and
q\in\{j-1,j,j+1\}
. From considering the overlap of the subblocks
M_{i,j}
and
M_{i,j+1}
and applying Lemma
8.3
, we deduce that
\lambda_{i,j}=\lambda_{i,j+1}
. Arguing in the same way we can see that there exists a rational
t_{k}
such that
\lambda_{i,j}=t_{k}
, for all
i\in\mathcal{I}
,
j\in\mathcal{J}
, which is unique, by Lemma
8.3
.
∎

We remarked in the introduction that there is an action of
\text{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})^{3}
on Bhargava cubes
\mathbb{Z}^{2}\otimes\mathbb{Z}^{2}\otimes\mathbb{Z}^{2}
. Here we need this same action in higher dimensions. Specifically, let us assume that
k
,
\ell
, and
m
are positive integers, all at least 2. Then
\text{SL}_{k}(\mathbb{Z})\times\text{SL}_{\ell}(\mathbb{Z})\times\text{SL}_{m}(\mathbb{Z})
acts on
\mathbb{Z}^{k}\otimes\mathbb{Z}^{\ell}\otimes\mathbb{Z}^{m}
by the formula
\mathbf{M}^{\prime}=(U,V,W)\mathbf{M}
, where
U\in\text{SL}_{k}(\mathbb{Z})
,
V\in\text{SL}_{\ell}(\mathbb{Z})
,
W\in\text{SL}_{m}(\mathbb{Z})
, and
\mathbf{M},\mathbf{M}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{Z}^{k}\otimes\mathbb{Z}^{\ell}\otimes\mathbb{Z}^{m}
, and the entries of
\mathbf{M}^{\prime}
are

m_{ijk}^{\prime}=\sum_{(p,q,r)\in\mathcal{I}\times\mathcal{J}\times\mathcal{K}}U_{ip}V_{jq}W_{kr}m_{pqr}.

For convenience, we assume in the next lemma that the index sets of
\mathbf{M}
and
\mathbf{M}^{\prime}
are
\mathcal{I}=\{0,1,\dots,k-1\}
,
\mathcal{J}=\{0,1,\dots,\ell-1\}
, and
\mathcal{K}=\{0,1,\dots,m-1\}
.

Lemma 8.5
.

Let
\mathbf{M}
be a
k
-by-
\ell
-by-
m
tame integer hypertiling. There exists
U\in\textnormal{SL}_{k}(\mathbb{Z})
,
V\in\textnormal{SL}_{\ell}(\mathbb{Z})
, and
W\in\textnormal{SL}_{m}(\mathbb{Z})
such that the tensor
\mathbf{M}^{\prime}=(U,V,W)\mathbf{M}
satisfies
m_{ijk}^{\prime}=0
whenever
i
,
j
, or
k
is greater than 1.

Proof.

Let
v_{i}
denote the
\ell
-by-
m
integer matrix with entries
m_{iqr}
, for
q=0,1,\dots,\ell-1
and
r=0,1,\dots,m-1
. By Theorem
8.4
, there are integers
\eta_{i}
,
\lambda_{i}
, and
\mu_{i}
(where
\eta_{i}\neq 0
) with
\eta_{i}v_{i}=\lambda_{i}v_{0}+\mu_{i}v_{1}
and
\gcd(\eta_{i},\lambda_{i},\mu_{i})=1
, for
i=2,3,\dots,k-1
. Let
A
be the
(k-2)
-by-
k
matrix

A=\begin{pmatrix}-\lambda_{2}&-\mu_{2}&\eta_{2}&&&\\
-\lambda_{3}&-\mu_{3}&&\eta_{3}&&\\
\vdots&\vdots&&&\ddots&\\
-\lambda_{k-1}&-\mu_{k-1}&&&&\eta_{k-1}\end{pmatrix}.

Then
\sum_{p=0}^{k-1}A_{ip}v_{p}=0
, for
i=0,1,\dots,k-2
. Using the Smith normal form we can find
X\in\text{SL}_{k-2}(\mathbb{Z})
and
Y\in\text{SL}_{k}(\mathbb{Z})
with

A=X\begin{pmatrix}0&0&d_{2}&&&\\
0&0&&d_{3}&&\\
\vdots&\vdots&&&\ddots&\\
0&0&&&&d_{k-1}\end{pmatrix}Y,

for nonzero integers
d_{2},d_{3},\dots,d_{k-1}
. Hence, for
i\geqslant 2
, we have

\sum_{p=0}^{k-1}Y_{ip}v_{p}=\frac{1}{d_{i}}\sum_{p=0}^{k-1}(X^{-1}A)_{i-2,p}v_{p}=0.

We choose
U=Y
. Similarly we can find
V\in\textnormal{SL}_{\ell}(\mathbb{Z})
and
W\in\textnormal{SL}_{m}(\mathbb{Z})
with
\sum_{q}V_{jq}m_{iqk}=0
(for
j\geqslant 2
) and
\sum_{r}W_{kr}m_{ijr}=0
(for
k\geqslant 2
) . Then
(U,V,W)
is the required triple.
∎

We need one final lemma before we can prove Theorem
D
.

Lemma 8.6
.

Let
A
be a nonsingular Bhargava cube and let
M
be a 3-by-2-by-2 subblock of a tame integer hypertiling with entries
m_{ijk}=\sum_{p=0}^{1}u_{ip}A_{pjk}
, for
i\in\{0,1,2\}
and
j,k\in\{0,1\}
. Then

u_{00}u_{11}-u_{01}u_{10}=u_{10}u_{21}-u_{11}u_{20}
.

Proof.

Given
j,k\in\{0,1\}
, let
j^{\prime}=1-j
and
k^{\prime}=1-k
. Since
M
is a subblock of a tame integer hypertiling we have that, for any two values
j
and
k
,

m_{0jk}m_{1j^{\prime}k^{\prime}}-m_{1jk}m_{0j^{\prime}k^{\prime}}=m_{1jk}m_{2j^{\prime}k^{\prime}}-m_{2jk}m_{1j^{\prime}k^{\prime}}.

Now,
m_{0jk}m_{1j^{\prime}k^{\prime}}-m_{1jk}m_{0j^{\prime}k^{\prime}}
equals

\hskip-42.67912pt(u_{00}A_{0jk}+u_{01}A_{1jk})(u_{10}A_{0j^{\prime}k^{\prime}}+u_{11}A_{1j^{\prime}k^{\prime}})-(u_{10}A_{0jk}+u_{11}A_{1jk})(u_{00}A_{0j^{\prime}k^{\prime}}+u_{01}A_{1j^{\prime}k^{\prime}}),

and this simplifies to

(u_{00}u_{11}-u_{01}u_{10})(A_{0jk}A_{1j^{\prime}k^{\prime}}-A_{1jk}A_{0j^{\prime}k^{\prime}}).

Similarly
m_{1jk}m_{2j^{\prime}k^{\prime}}-m_{2jk}m_{1j^{\prime}k^{\prime}}=(u_{10}u_{21}-u_{11}u_{20})(A_{0jk}A_{1j^{\prime}k^{\prime}}-A_{1jk}A_{0j^{\prime}k^{\prime}})
. Since
\Det A\neq 0
, we see from the formula for
\Det A
stated in the introduction that there must be a pair
j,k\in\{0,1\}
for which
A_{0jk}A_{1j^{\prime}k^{\prime}}-A_{1jk}A_{0j^{\prime}k^{\prime}}\neq 0
. Consequently,
u_{00}u_{11}-u_{01}u_{10}=u_{10}u_{21}-u_{11}u_{20}
, as required.
∎

We can now show that any tame integer hypertiling can be described by three paths in Farey graphs, the reverse statement of Theorem
8.1
.

Theorem 8.7
.

Let
\mathbf{M}
be a tame N-hypertiling. Then there are positive integers
R
,
S
, and
T
, minimal paths
u_{i0}/u_{i1}
,
v_{j0}/v_{j1}
, and
w_{k0}/w_{k1}
in
\mathscr{F}_{R}
,
\mathscr{F}_{S}
, and
\mathscr{F}_{T}
, and a Bhargava cube
A
with
N=R^{2}S^{2}T^{2}\Det A
such that

m_{ijk}=\sum_{p,q,r=0}^{1}A_{pqr}u_{ip}v_{jq}w_{kr},

for
i\in\mathcal{I},j\in\mathcal{J},k\in\mathcal{K}
.

Proof.

Let us assume for the moment that
\mathbf{M}
is of finite size
k
-by-
\ell
-by-
m
indexed by
\mathcal{I}=\{0,1,\dots,k-1\}
,
\mathcal{J}=\{0,1,\dots,\ell-1\}
, and
\mathcal{K}=\{0,1,\dots,m-1\}
.

By Lemma
8.5
, we can find
U\in\textnormal{SL}_{k}(\mathbb{Z})
,
V\in\textnormal{SL}_{\ell}(\mathbb{Z})
, and
W\in\textnormal{SL}_{m}(\mathbb{Z})
such that
\mathbf{M}^{\prime}=(U^{-1},V^{-1},W^{-1})\mathbf{M}
satisfies
m_{ijk}^{\prime}=0
whenever
i
,
j
, or
k
is greater than 1. Let
(u_{i0},u_{i1})=(U_{i0},U_{i1})
(the first two columns of
U
),
(v_{j0},v_{j1})=(V_{j0},V_{j1})
(the first two columns of
V
), and
(w_{k0},w_{k1})=(W_{k0},W_{k1})
(the first two columns of
W
). Let
A
be the Bhargava cube with
A_{pqr}=m_{pqr}^{\prime}
, for
p,q,r\in\{0,1\}
. We have
\mathbf{M}=(U,V,W)\mathbf{M}^{\prime}
. So

m_{ijk}=\sum_{p,q,r=0}^{1}A_{pqr}u_{ip}v_{jq}w_{kr}.

We must show that
u_{i0}/u_{i1}
,
v_{j0}/v_{j1}
, and
w_{k0}/w_{k1}
are minimal paths in
\mathscr{F}_{R}
,
\mathscr{F}_{S}
, and
\mathscr{F}_{T}
, for some positive integers
R
,
S
, and
T
. Let us do this for
u_{i0}/u_{i1}
. Let
B
be the Bhargava cube with entries
B_{ijk}=\sum_{q,r=0}^{1}A_{iqr}v_{jq}w_{kr}
, for
i,j,k\in\{0,1\}
. Then
m_{ijk}=\sum_{p=0}^{1}B_{pjk}u_{ip}
, so
B
is nonsingular, because
\mathbf{M}
is an
N
-hypertiling. By Lemma
8.6
, we have
u_{i-1,0}u_{i,1}-u_{i-1,1}u_{i,0}=u_{i,0}u_{i+1,1}-u_{i,1}u_{i+1,0}
, for all
i\in\mathcal{I}^{*}
. By replacing
u_{i0}
by
-u_{i0}
for all indices
i
, if need be (and adjusting
A
accordingly), we can assume that in fact
u_{i-1,0}u_{i,1}-u_{i-1,1}u_{i,0}=R
, for some positive integer
R
and all
i\in\mathcal{I}^{\prime}
(
R\neq 0
because
\mathbf{M}
is an
N
-hypertiling). Hence
u_{i0}/u_{i1}
is a path in
\mathscr{F}_{R}
. It is a minimal path because it is formed from the first two columns of an element of
\text{SL}_{k}(\mathbb{Z})
.

Similar arguments show that
v_{j0}/v_{j1}
and
w_{k0}/w_{k1}
are minimal paths in
\mathscr{F}_{S}
and
\mathscr{F}_{T}
, for some positive integers
S
and
T
. As a consequence, we have
N=R^{2}S^{2}T^{2}\Det A
, and this concludes the proof in the finite case.

The argument to go from the finite case to the infinite case is similar to that used in proving Theorem
A
. Let
\mathbf{M}\colon\mathcal{I}\times\mathcal{J}\times\mathcal{K}\longrightarrow\mathbb{Z}
be an infinite tame
N
-hypertiling. Let
\mathcal{I}_{1}\subset\mathcal{I}_{2}\subset\dotsb
,
\mathcal{J}_{1}\subset\mathcal{J}_{2}\subset\dotsb
, and
\mathcal{K}_{1}\subset\mathcal{K}_{2}\subset\dotsb
be sequences of finite intervals with limits
\mathcal{I}
,
\mathcal{J}
, and
\mathcal{K}
, respectively, and let
\mathbf{M}_{n}
be the finite tame
N
-hypertiling that is the restriction of
\mathbf{M}
to
\mathcal{I}_{n}\times\mathcal{J}_{n}\times\mathcal{K}_{n}
. We define
R_{n}
,
S_{n}
, and
T_{n}
to be the dimensions of the graphs for the corresponding minimal paths of
\mathbf{M}_{n}
. By passing to subsequences we can assume that in fact all threse sequences are constant, with values
R
,
S
, and
T
.

It follows that we can find minimal paths
u_{i0}^{(n)}/u_{i1}^{(n)}
,
v_{j0}^{(n)}/v_{j1}^{(n)}
, and
w_{k0}^{(n)}/w_{k1}^{(n)}
in
\mathscr{F}_{R}
,
\mathscr{F}_{S}
, and
\mathscr{F}_{T}
and a Bhargava cube
A^{(n)}
with

m_{ijk}=\sum_{p,q,r=0}^{1}A^{(n)}_{pqr}u_{ip}^{(n)}v_{jq}^{(n)}w_{kr}^{(n)},\quad\text{for $(i,j,k)\in\mathcal{I}_{n}\times\mathcal{J}_{n}\times\mathcal{K}_{n}$.}

By Theorem
2.2
we can assume that in fact
u_{i0}^{(n+1)}/u_{i1}^{(n+1)}
is equal to
u_{i0}^{(n)}/u_{i1}^{(n)}
on
\mathcal{I}_{n}
, after applying an element of
\text{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
(which changes
A^{(n+1)}
). We define
u_{i0}/u_{i1}
to be the termwise limiting value of this process, and we define the other paths similarly. Then, from considering the restriction of
\mathbf{M}
to
\{0,1\}^{3}
, we can see that the sequence of (adjusted) Bhargava cubes
A^{(n)}
is constant, giving us the required decomposition.
∎

Together Theorems
8.1
and
8.7
imply Theorem
D
.

9
Proof of Theorem
C

Theorem
C
is a stronger version of Theorem
D
for tame 1-hypertilings. Key to this is the fact that all Bhargava cubes of hyperdeterminant 1 are
\textnormal{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})^{3}
-equivalent. To prove this, we define

\mathbf{I}=\left(\begin{matrix}1&0\\
0&0\end{matrix}\ \middle|\ \begin{matrix}0&0\\
0&1\end{matrix}\right),

which has hyperdeterminant 1.

Theorem 9.1
.

Every Bhargava cube of hyperdeterminant 1 is
\textnormal{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})^{3}
-equivalent to
\mathbf{I}
.

Proof.

Let
A
be a Bhargava cube of hyperdeterminant 1. We will first prove that
A
is
\textnormal{GL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})^{3}
-equivalent to
\mathbf{I}
. The greatest common divisor of the four entries in the top layer of
A
is 1 because
\Det A=1
. From the Smith normal form for 2-by-2 integer matrices, we can apply an element
\textnormal{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})\times\textnormal{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})\times\{I\}
(where
I
is the identity matrix) to reduce the top layer of
A
to the form

\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&\ast\end{pmatrix}.

Then, after applying an element of
\{I\}\times\{I\}\times\textnormal{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
, we can reduce
A
to the form

\left(\begin{matrix}1&0\\
0&\alpha\end{matrix}\quad\middle|\quad\begin{matrix}0&\beta\\
\gamma&\delta\end{matrix}\right),

with hyperdeterminant
\delta^{2}+4\alpha\beta\gamma=1
. Observe that

\alpha\beta\gamma=\left(\frac{1-\delta}{2}\right)\left(\frac{1+\delta}{2}\right).

The result is easily established if one of
\alpha
,
\beta
, or
\gamma
is 0, so let us assume that all three are nonzero, in which case
\delta\neq\pm 1
. Let

Q=\begin{pmatrix}[r]1&0\\
0&\!\!-1\end{pmatrix}.

After applying one of the triples
(Q,I,I)
,
(I,Q,I)
, or
(I,I,Q)
, if need be, we can assume that
\alpha
,
\beta
,
\gamma
, and
\delta
are all negative. We define

\displaystyle g_{\alpha}=\gcd(\alpha,(1-\delta)/2),

\displaystyle\qquad h_{\alpha}=-\gcd(\alpha,(1+\delta)/2),

\displaystyle g_{\beta}=\gcd(\beta,(1-\delta)/2),

\displaystyle\qquad h_{\beta}=-\gcd(\beta,(1+\delta)/2),

\displaystyle g_{\gamma}=\gcd(\gamma,(1-\delta)/2),

\displaystyle\qquad h_{\gamma}=-\gcd(\gamma,(1+\delta)/2).

Then
g_{\alpha},g_{\beta},g_{\gamma}>0
and
h_{\alpha},h_{\beta},h_{\gamma}<0
. Notice that
\alpha/g_{\alpha}
and
(1-\delta)/(2g_{\alpha})
are coprime, and

\left(\frac{1-\delta}{2g_{\alpha}}\right)\left(\frac{1+\delta}{2}\right)

is divisible by
\alpha/g_{\alpha}
. Hence
(1+\delta)/2
is divisible by
\alpha/g_{\alpha}
, and
\alpha
is divisible by
\alpha/g_{\alpha}
also, so
\alpha/g_{\alpha}\mid h_{\alpha}
. Hence
\alpha\mid g_{\alpha}h_{\alpha}
. Now,
(1-\delta)/2
and
(1+\delta)/2
are coprime, so
g_{\alpha}h_{\alpha}\mid\alpha
. Hence
g_{\alpha}h_{\alpha}=\alpha
. Similarly,
g_{\beta}h_{\beta}=\beta
and
g_{\gamma}h_{\gamma}=\gamma
.

Next, we have
g_{\alpha}g_{\beta}g_{\gamma}h_{\alpha}h_{\beta}h_{\gamma}=(1-\delta)/2\times(1+\delta)/2
. Each of
g_{\alpha}
,
g_{\beta}
, and
g_{\gamma}
is coprime with
(1+\delta)/2
, so we must have

g_{\alpha}g_{\beta}g_{\gamma}=\frac{1-\delta}{2}\quad\text{and}\quad h_{\alpha}h_{\beta}h_{\gamma}=\frac{1+\delta}{2}.

We now define

U_{\alpha}=\begin{pmatrix}h_{\alpha}&g_{\alpha}\\
\frac{\delta-1}{2g_{\alpha}}&\frac{\delta+1}{2h_{\alpha}}\end{pmatrix},\quad U_{\beta}=\begin{pmatrix}h_{\beta}&g_{\beta}\\
\frac{\delta-1}{2g_{\beta}}&\frac{\delta+1}{2h_{\beta}}\end{pmatrix},\quad U_{\gamma}=\begin{pmatrix}h_{\gamma}&g_{\gamma}\\
\frac{\delta-1}{2g_{\gamma}}&\frac{\delta+1}{2h_{\gamma}}\end{pmatrix}.

Each of these matrices belongs to
\text{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
, and one can check that
(U_{\alpha},U_{\beta},U_{\gamma})
maps

\left(\begin{matrix}1&0\\
0&0\end{matrix}\quad\middle|\quad\begin{matrix}0&0\\
0&1\end{matrix}\right)\quad\text{to}\quad\left(\begin{matrix}1&0\\
0&\alpha\end{matrix}\quad\middle|\quad\begin{matrix}0&\beta\\
\gamma&\delta\end{matrix}\right).

To summarise, we have found a triple
(V_{\alpha},V_{\beta},V_{\gamma})\in\text{GL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})^{3}
with
(V_{\alpha},V_{\beta},V_{\gamma})A=\mathbf{I}
. By post-composing with some of the triples
(Q,I,I)
,
(I,Q,I)
, or
(I,I,Q)
we can find a triple
(W_{\alpha},W_{\beta},W_{\gamma})\in\text{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})^{3}
for which
(W_{\alpha},W_{\beta},W_{\gamma})A
is equal to either
\mathbf{I}
or

\mathbf{I}^{\dagger}=\left(\begin{matrix}1&0\\
0&0\end{matrix}\ \middle|\ \begin{matrix}[r]0&0\\
0&\!\!\!-1\end{matrix}\right).

However,
(J,J,J)\mathbf{I}=\mathbf{I}^{\dagger}
, where, as usual

J=\begin{pmatrix}[r]0&1\\
\!-1&0\end{pmatrix},

so all Bhagarva cubes of hyperdeterminant 1 are indeed
\text{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})^{3}
-equivalent, as required.
∎

We can calculate the stabilizer of
\mathbf{I}
in
\textnormal{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})^{3}
explicitly.

Theorem 9.2
.

The stabilizer of
\mathbf{I}
in
\textnormal{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})^{3}
is the group

G=\{(I,I,I),(I,-I,-I),(-I,I,-I),(-I,-I,I)\}.

Proof.

Clearly
G
is contained in the stabilizer of
\mathbf{I}
. Conversely, let us consider the equation
(A,B,C)\mathbf{I}=\mathbf{I}
, where
A,B,C\in\text{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
. Then

(A,B,I)\mathbf{I}=(I,I,C^{-1})\mathbf{I}
, which gives

\left(\begin{matrix}A_{00}B_{00}&A_{00}B_{10}\\
A_{10}B_{00}&A_{10}B_{10}\end{matrix}\quad\middle|\quad\begin{matrix}A_{01}B_{01}&A_{01}B_{11}\\
A_{11}B_{01}&A_{11}B_{11}\end{matrix}\right)\quad=\quad\left(\begin{matrix}C_{11}&0\\
0&-C_{01}\end{matrix}\quad\middle|\quad\begin{matrix}-C_{10}&0\\
0&C_{00}\end{matrix}\right).

From the zero entries we see that either
A=\pm I
and
B=\pm I
or
A=\pm J
and
B=\pm J
. In the first case we see from the nonzero entries that
(A,B,C)\in G
. In the second case we obtain

C=\pm\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix},

which is not in
\text{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
, so in fact this case cannot arise. Hence the stabilizer of
\mathbf{I}
is
G
.
∎

Evidently
G
is a subgroup of the stabilizer of any Bhargava cube. Some nonsingular cubes have larger stabilizers though. For example, given
A\in\text{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
, the stabilizer of the Bhargava cube
(A\mid I)
contains the triple
(A,(A^{-1})^{T},I)
– and plenty of other triples besides. A complete understanding of stabilizers in
\text{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})^{3}
can be derived from Bhargava’s original work on the Gauss composition law for quadratic forms
[
4
]
.

Proof of Theorem
C
.

From Theorem
D
with
\mathbf{I}
as our choice of Bhargava cube
A
, we see that, given paths
a_{i}/b_{i}
,
c_{j}/d_{j}
, and
e_{k}/f_{k}
in
\mathscr{F}
, the formula
m_{ijk}=a_{i}c_{j}e_{k}+b_{i}d_{j}f_{k}
defines a tame 1-hypertiling. Here
R=S=T=1
and
\Det A=1
. Clearly this formula is invariant under the group
G\cong\mathbb{Z}_{2}\times\mathbb{Z}_{2}
of Theorem
9.2
. Let us now prove that the map is injective. Suppose then that
m_{ijk}=a_{i}^{\prime}c_{j}^{\prime}e_{k}^{\prime}+b_{i}^{\prime}d_{j}^{\prime}f_{k}^{\prime}
, where
a_{i}^{\prime}/b_{i}^{\prime}
,
c_{j}^{\prime}/d_{j}^{\prime}
, and
e_{k}^{\prime}/f_{k}^{\prime}
are paths in
\mathscr{F}
. Then

\displaystyle\left(\begin{pmatrix}a_{i-1}&b_{i-1}\\
a_{i}&b_{i}\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}c_{j-1}&d_{j-1}\\
c_{j}&d_{j}\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}e_{k-1}&f_{k-1}\\
e_{k}&f_{k}\end{pmatrix}\right)\mathbf{I}

\displaystyle=\left(\begin{pmatrix}a_{i-1}^{\prime}&b_{i-1}^{\prime}\\
a_{i}^{\prime}&b_{i}^{\prime}\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}c_{j-1}^{\prime}&d_{j-1}^{\prime}\\
c_{j}^{\prime}&d_{j}^{\prime}\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}e_{k-1}^{\prime}&f_{k-1}^{\prime}\\
e_{k}^{\prime}&f_{k}^{\prime}\end{pmatrix}\right)\mathbf{I}.

From Theorem
9.2
, after applying an element of
G
to one of the paths, we can ensure that
a_{i}/b_{i}=a_{i}^{\prime}/b_{i}^{\prime}
,
c_{j}/d_{j}=c_{j}^{\prime}/d_{j}^{\prime}
, and
e_{k}/f_{k}=e_{k}^{\prime}/f_{k}^{\prime}
, for
i\in\mathcal{I},j\in\mathcal{J},k\in\mathcal{K}
.

It remains to prove that every tame 1-hypertiling
\mathbf{M}
arises in this way. Theorem
D
tells us that we can express
\mathbf{M}
in terms of three paths in
\mathscr{F}
and a Bhargava cube
A
. Then Theorem
9.1
allows us to choose
A
to be
\mathbf{I}
, as required.
∎

10
Integer hypertilings with
\text{SL}_{2}
cross sections

We finish with a discussion of the positive integer hypertiling
m_{ijk}=F_{2(i+j+k)-1}
shown in Figure
10
(and conceived in
[
9
]
), which offers an attractive illustration of topics covered so far. We also consider integer hypertilings with
\text{SL}_{2}
cross sections in more generality, without the restriction that all entries are positive.

We recall that
(F_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}
is the sequence of Fibonacci numbers, where
F_{0}=0
,
F_{1}=1
, and
F_{n+1}=F_{n}+F_{n-1}
, for
n\in\mathbb{Z}
. Observe that, for
n
odd,
F_{-n}=F_{n}
and
F_{n+2}F_{n-2}-F_{n}^{2}=1
; the latter follows from a special case of Catalan’s identity.

Lemma 10.1
.

Let
X=\{(a,b)\in\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{N}:ab\mid a^{2}+b^{2}+1\}
. Then

X=\{(F_{2n-1},F_{2n+1}):n\in\mathbb{Z}\}.

This lemma is well-known in mathematical olympiad communities. It can be proved by considering the invertible map
\phi\colon X\longrightarrow X
defined by
\phi(x,y)=((x^{2}+1)/y,x)
and proving that if
(a,b)\in X
, then
\phi^{n}(a,b)=(1,1)
, for some integer
n
. We omit the details.

Theorem 10.2
.

All six 2-by-2 cross sections of a positive integer Bhargava cube
A
have determinant 1 if and only if
A
is equal to

A_{n}=\left(\begin{matrix}F_{2n-3}&F_{2n-1}\\
F_{2n-1}&F_{2n+1}\end{matrix}\quad\middle|\quad\begin{matrix}F_{2n-1}&F_{2n+1}\\
F_{2n+1}&F_{2n+3}\end{matrix}\right),

for some integer
n
.

Proof.

It is straightforward to check that every cross section of
A_{n}
has determinant 1. For the converse, suppose that the positive integer Bhargava cube

A=\left(\begin{matrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{matrix}\ \middle|\ \begin{matrix}e&f\\
g&h\end{matrix}\right)

has the property that every cross section has determinant 1. Observe that

\hskip-39.83368pt(a+d)(cf-bg)=(af-be)c-(ag-ce)b-(bh-fd)c+(ch-dg)b=c-b-c+b=0.

Hence
cf=bg
. Therefore
c=c(af-be)=b(ag-ce)=b
and
f=g
. Similarly

\hskip-39.83368pt(a+f)(bg-de)=(ag-ce)b-(ad-bc)e-(eh-fg)b+(bh-fd)e=b-e-b+e=0.

Hence
bg=de
. Therefore
e=e(bh-fd)=b(eh-fg)=b
and
d=g
. We then have
ad-b^{2}=1
and
bh-d^{2}=1
. Consequently,
d\mid b^{2}+1
and
b\mid d^{2}+1
, so we can apply Lemma
10.1
to see that
(b,d)=(F_{2n-1},F_{2n+1})
, for some integer
n
. Then
a=(1+b^{2})/d=F_{2n-3}
and
h=(1+d^{2})/b=F_{2n+3}
, so
A
is of the required form.
∎

It follows immediately from Theorem
10.2
that all positive integer hypertilings with
\text{SL}_{2}
cross sections have the form
m_{ijk}=F_{2(e+i+j+k)}-1
, for some integer
e
. These are 5-hypertilings, because
\Det A_{n}=5
.

All the cubes
A_{n}
are
\text{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})^{3}
equivalent. Specifically, let

P=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
-1&3\end{pmatrix};

then one can check that
(P^{p},P^{q},P^{r})A_{n}=A_{n+p+q+r}
. Of course, the property of having
\text{SL}_{2}
cross sections is
not
preserved by the action of
\text{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})^{3}
, so there are plenty of Bhargava cubes in the
\text{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})^{3}
orbit of
A_{n}
without this property.

The 5-hypertiling
m_{ijk}=F_{2(i+j+k)-1}
can be obtained from three copies of the same path
F_{2n-1}/F_{2n+1}
in
\mathscr{F}
(with itinerary
\dotsc,3,3,3,\dotsc
) and the Bhargava cube

\left(\begin{matrix}[r]3&\!\!\!-1\\
\!-1&0\end{matrix}\ \middle|\ \begin{matrix}[r]\!-1&0\\
0&1\end{matrix}\right).

One can verify this from the Fibonacci identity

\displaystyle F_{2(i+j+k)-1}

\displaystyle=3F_{2i-1}F_{2j-1}F_{2k-1}-F_{2i-1}F_{2j-1}F_{2k+1}-F_{2i-1}F_{2j+1}F_{2k-1}

\displaystyle\hskip 14.22636pt-F_{2i+1}F_{2j-1}F_{2k-1}+F_{2i+1}F_{2j+1}F_{2k+1},

which can be checked using Binet’s formula.

Let us now turn to more general (not necessarily positive) integer hypertilings with
\text{SL}_{2}
cross sections. We define
G
to be the subgroup of
\text{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})^{3}
generated by
(J,I,I)
,
(I,J,I)
, and
(I,I,J)
, which has order 64. The kernel
K
of the action of this group on Bhargava cubes is the subgroup
\{(I,I,I),(I,-I,-I),(-I,I,-I),(-I,-I,I)\}
of order 4, so
G/K
has order 16. One can check that this action preserves the property of
\text{SL}_{2}
cross sections. Hence each of the 16 images of
A_{n}
under
G
is a Bhargava cube with
\text{SL}_{2}
cross sections; for example,

(J,J,I)A_{n}=\left(\begin{matrix}F_{2n+1}&-F_{2n-1}\\
-F_{2n-1}&F_{2n-3}\end{matrix}\quad\middle|\quad\begin{matrix}F_{2n+3}&-F_{2n+1}\\
-F_{2n+1}&F_{2n-1}\end{matrix}\right).

To grasp this orbit of 16 cubes, it helps to observe that, within the orbit, the entry
\pm F_{2n-3}
can appear in each of the 8 corners of the cube exactly twice, once in positive form and once in negative form.

These are not quite all Bhargava cubes with
\text{SL}_{2}
cross sections because one can also carry out the operation
m_{ijk}\longmapsto(-1)^{i+j+k}m_{ijk}
, which preserves the
\text{SL}_{2}
cross section property. This corresponds to acting on cubes by the triple
(Q,Q,Q)\in\text{GL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})^{3}
, where

Q=\begin{pmatrix}[r]1&0\\
0&\!\!\!-1\end{pmatrix}.

Let us define
H
to be the subgroup of
\text{GL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})^{3}
generated by
(Q,Q,Q)
and
G
.

Theorem 10.3
.

All six 2-by-2 cross sections of a Bhargava cube
A
have determinant 1 if and only if
A\in H(A_{n})
, for some integer
n
.

Proof.

We have noted already that all cross sections of elements of
H(A_{n})
have determinant 1. Conversely, suppose that
A
is a Bhargava cube with the property that every cross section has determinant 1. It is straightforward to verify that no entry of
A
can be 0. One can check (details omitted) that there is an element
g
of
H
for which the image cube
A^{\prime}=g(A)
has the property that the top layer of
A^{\prime}
comprises positive entries and the bottom layer does not comprise all negative entries. A quick algebraic check shows that this is only possible if in fact the bottom layer comprises positive entries also, so
A^{\prime}=A_{n}
for some integer
n
, by Theorem
10.2
.
∎

We see from Theorem
10.3
that the full collection of Bhargava cubes with
\text{SL}_{2}
cross sections belong to exactly two
\text{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})^{3}
orbits (and just one
\text{GL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})^{3}
orbit). Any integer hypertiling with
\text{SL}_{2}
cross sections is formed by piecing together Bhargava cubes of the form
H(A_{n})
.
