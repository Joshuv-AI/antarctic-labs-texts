Management of ketogenic diet therapy in patients with ileostomy: A report of 2 cases
Authors: Braden J Baker, Abigail E Olbina, Marissa A Godfrey, Annie E Graber, Taylor N Parrish, Sarah Isis R Delima
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis case series explores the feasibility and clinical considerations of implementing the ketogenic diet therapy (KDT) for epilepsy management in patients with ileostomies. Two pediatric patients with medication‐resistant epilepsy and ileostomies continued KDT under clinical supervision. The first patient had an ileostomy placed while on KDT. Fluids and electrolytes were increased initially, although sodium (Na) levels remained within a normal range. The second patient had an ileostomy before starting KDT. Hyponatremia and acidosis occurred initially. With routine laboratory monitoring, and electrolyte and bicarbonate supplementation, normal laboratory values were achieved. Overall fluid needs did not change. Both patients maintained adequate hydration and electrolytes. Seizure control was not compromised in either case while having an ostomy. This study supports the feasibility of KDT in patients with ileostomies, while also highlighting the need for individualized monitoring of hydration status and electrolytes, specifically sodium levels.Keywords: fluids–electrolytes/acid–base, nutrition assessment, pediatric medicine, pediatrics, rehabilitation

INTRODUCTION

Ketogenic diet therapy (KDT) is a high‐fat, adequate‐protein, and low‐carbohydrate diet that uses ketones as the primary fuel source to produce ketone bodies. KDT is indicated in patients with epilepsy who have failed two or more anti‐seizure medications (ASMs) and for specific metabolic conditions such as glucose transporter‐1 deficiency syndrome and pyruvate dehydrogenase deficiency. 1 KDT is believed to work by altering neurotransmitter levels such as adenosine, norepinephrine, glutamate, and γ‐aminobutyric acid. KDT has proven successful by mechanisms such as influencing mitochondrial function and reactive oxygen species, decreasing inflammation, and increasing immune function. 2

The most recent KDT consensus report from 2018 outlined suggested screening laboratory studies prior to implementation of KDT, including complete blood count, electrolytes, liver and kidney function tests, lipid profile, and metabolic profile such as total and free carnitine levels and acylcarnitine profile. Initiation of KDT is commonly done while admitted for observation; however, it can be started on an outpatient basis. Side effects of the diet can include constipation, delayed gastric emptying, increase in gas and bloating, emesis, hyperlipidemia, metabolic acidosis, and nutrient deficiencies. There are few studies discussing long‐term complications, but those noted include bone fractures, dyslipidemia, and kidney stones. 1

Patients who cannot maintain adequate oral intake or nutrition to meet their metabolic demands are considered for feeding tube placement, guided by a nutrition protocol constructed with interdisciplinary members to address the patient's individualized dietary needs. 3

Some further challenges can arise in patients with colonic growths, malignancies, inflammatory bowel diseases, or perforation peritonitis resulting in the need for a stoma to divert feces away from distal portions of the bowel. Patients can receive an ileostomy, which is created from their small bowel, or a colostomy, which stems from their colon. 4 Ileostomies can either be temporary or permanent, depending on the clinical situation. Their purpose is to evacuate stool from the body via the ileum rather than through the usual route of the anus, allowing waste to bypass damaged areas of the intestine, reducing pain, inflammation, and discomfort. 5 The prevalence of ileostomies for children varies depending on their medical conditions and specific requirements with one study showing that, out of 336 children who needed a stoma, 57% or 192/336 required ileostomy. 6 The use of KDT and ileostomies together in children is not documented in medical literature.

The ileum serves to absorb most nutrients, including carbohydrates, fats, and water. Ileostomies can greatly impact the surface area of nutrient absorption. Patients with stomas require specific considerations regarding disturbances in water and electrolyte balance, and this can be influenced by the amount of output in the stoma bag. Since nutrient absorption can be impaired in patients with stomas, dietary modifications need to occur in order to replenish what the patient is losing. 7 Because KDT relies heavily on fats, nutritional deficiencies can be further impacted in patients who require KDT and have an ileostomy. Ileostomy output can also further complicate a patient's fluid and water balance which can be exacerbated by KDT.

KDT is individualized and can be adjusted based on specific needs. Patients can receive different ketogenic ratios, which refers to the amount of fat compared to the amount of combined protein and carbohydrates. This ratio is often used for dosing purposes because KDT is used to decrease seizure burden. Depending on a patient's specific diet needs, homemade electrolyte water can be used. For example, instead of using store‐bought electrolyte rehydration drinks, individuals can create their own at home using solutions such as Morton Salt and baking soda to fulfill sodium, chloride, and potassium needs.

Despite the established use of KDT in epilepsy and the common use of ostomies in gastrointestinal care, there is no published literature of the two used in conjunction. Therefore, the management for electrolyte balances, water composition, and nutrition is not widely known. Here we discuss two cases, detailing the nutritional course and changes, and seizure and disease progression. Both of these patients had an ostomy as well as intractable epilepsy for which KDT was used as treatment.

Case presentations

Patient 1

Patient 1 is a 16‐year‐old female with history of congenital cytomegalovirus, microcephaly, spastic quadriplegic cerebral palsy, generalized intractable epilepsy, and chronic dysmotility requiring nutrition via gastrojejunostomy (G/J) tube who was started on KDT for seizure control. Before KDT, patient 1 had several daily tonic seizures. After KDT, tonic seizures resolved, and she was weaned off of her ASMs. In November 2023, an ileostomy was performed due to chronic dysmotility and KDT was continued. DISCUSSION

As epilepsy is a debilitating neurological disorder, significant efforts have been dedicated to improving its management while minimizing treatment‐related side effects. The primary goals of treatment include identifying effective ASMs or therapies for individual patients, minimizing treatment‐related side effects, and ensuring sustained long‐term seizure control. 1 , 2 Despite advances in ASMs, approximately 30%–40% of patients continue to have medication‐resistant epilepsy, which has led to increased use of alternative therapies such as KDT. 2

KDT is a well‐established treatment option for patients with medication‐resistant epilepsy, particularly those who have failed two or more appropriately chosen ASMs or who have epilepsy syndromes known to respond well to dietary therapy. 1 , 2 In these select patients, KDT commonly results in meaningful seizure reduction, with approximately 50%–60% achieving at least a 50% reduction in seizures and 10%–15% achieving seizure freedom. 2 , 8 While higher response rates have been reported in select cohorts, these outcomes are not representative of most patients. Nonetheless, for many individuals, KDT can be a life‐changing intervention.

Although complications such as hypoglycemia, metabolic acidosis, dehydration, and electrolyte abnormalities can occur, these risks are reduced when KDT is initiated gradually. Gradual initiation typically involves slowly advancing the ketogenic ratio over several days, avoiding prolonged fasting, and closely monitoring serum glucose, electrolytes, and acid–base status during initiation. 9 Compared with ASMs, KDT has a different adverse‐effect profile which includes die metabolic complications requiring close monitoring. 1 , 2

Dehydration is a particularly important consideration with KDT, as low‐carbohydrate diets promote increased urinary sodium and water losses through a diuretic and natriuretic effect, especially during early phases of ketosis. 2 , 8 Electrolyte abnormalities, including hyponatremia, are therefore more likely to occur during diet initiation and require proactive management.

In patients with ileostomies, the risk of dehydration and electrolyte imbalance is further increased. These patients lack colonic reabsorption of water and sodium, resulting in higher baseline fluid and sodium losses. 3 , 10 While fluid losses occur in patients with ileostomies regardless of diet, the addition of KDT may exacerbate these losses due to its diuretic effects. In the two patients discussed, hydration status and sodium balance required close monitoring, and both patients required sodium supplementation. This suggests a shared physiologic response rather than isolated events. Although the small sample size limits broader conclusions, these cases highlight the importance of careful fluid and electrolyte management when implementing KDT in patients with altered gastrointestinal anatomy.

CONCLUSION

This case series describes the implementation of KDT in two patients with ileostomies and highlights the importance of close monitoring during diet initiation and maintenance. In both patients, careful attention to hydration status, serum electrolytes, and metabolic parameters was required. Laboratory monitoring after each ratio or recipe change, followed by regular outpatient follow‐up, allowed for early identification and management of dehydration and electrolyte abnormalities.

While these cases demonstrate that KDT can be successfully implemented in select patients with ileostomies, there are several study limitations that need to be addressed. These findings are limited to the two patients described and should not be generalized to all individuals with epilepsy and ostomies. Patients with altered gastrointestinal anatomy may be at increased risk for complications during KDT and require individualized monitoring and multidisciplinary support. Further studies are needed to better define optimal monitoring strategies and long‐term outcomes in this higher‐risk population.

For this case report, there is no comparison group and a cause‐and‐effect relationship cannot be determined. The two cases differ substantially in both underlying conditions, complications, and timeframe of starting the ketogenic diet in relation to ostomy placement. This introduces heterogeneity and prevents standardization of treatment. Lastly, this study relies on retrospective data, introducing the potential for information bias including incomplete or inaccurate reporting, variable laboratory timing, and subjective caregiver reporting of seizure activity.
