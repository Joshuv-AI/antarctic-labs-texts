Interpreting Glycopeptide Escalation and Acute Kidney Injury with Caution
Authors: Shahzad Ali Jiskani, Muhammad Mustafa Asmat, Mariam Khan
Published: 2026

To the Editor,

We read with great interest the recent article in this journal by Tahta et al. [1], who introduced an innovative unsupervised clustering framework to identify clinically meaningful phenotypes in pediatric febrile neutropenia. Their work constitutes an important step beyond the effects of average treatment toward phenotype-informed antimicrobial decision-making. Nevertheless, three methodological considerations temper the causal interpretation of the reported association between early glycopeptide escalation and acute kidney injury (AKI) and should be addressed before these findings are translated into clinical practice.

First, nephrotoxic co-exposures were modeled as a binary variable rather than as a cumulative exposure burden. Pediatric oncology patients are frequently exposed to multiple nephrotoxic agents, including aminoglycosides, amphotericin B, acyclovir, iodinated contrast, and nephrotoxic chemotherapy, often administered concurrently or sequentially. These agents exert synergistic renal toxicity that cannot be adequately represented by a simple present-or-absent covariate [2, 3]. Without quantifying the cumulative nephrotoxic burden through a weighted exposure score or similar approach, the independent contribution of glycopeptide remains difficult to isolate. The observed concentration of AKI within escalation-prone phenotypes may therefore reflect greater supporting intensity and overall therapeutic complexity rather than a specific effect of glycopeptide therapy alone.

Second, the phenotyping strategy relied exclusively on variables available during the first 6 hours of presentation, whereas glycopeptide escalation occurred over the subsequent 48 hours. In routine practice, escalation decisions are rarely based on admission characteristics alone. Clinicians respond to evolving fever patterns, hemodynamic instability, inflammatory trends, microbiological data, and early organ dysfunction [4]. Anchoring phenotypes to static baseline variables risks conflating deterioration during hospitalization, which independently predicts AKI, with the escalation event itself. Incorporating time-varying covariates or target trial emulation methods would better distinguish severity at presentation from severity of evolution and reduce residual indication bias [5, 6].

Third, important antimicrobial stewardship metrics were unavailable. Glycopeptide duration, the time of de-escalation, therapeutic drug monitoring, and adherence to discontinuation criteria directly influence nephrotoxicity risk. A brief empiric course discontinued after negative clusters is biologically distinct from clinician risk tolerance and institutional prescribing culture. Without multilevel modeling that accounts for provider-level clustering, apparent phenotype-driven escalation may instead represent physician-specific prescribing heuristics rather than patient-specific disease biology.

Tahta et al. [1] have provided a valuable hypothesis-generating framework that deserves further investigation. Prospective validation incorporating cumulative nephrotoxicity scoring, dynamic trajectory modeling, stewardship measures including treatment duration and de-escalation, and hierarchical analytical approaches will strengthen causal inference [7]. Until these sources of confounding are more fully addressed, the association between early glycopeptide escalation and AKI should be interpreted as an important observation signal rather than evidence of a direct causal relationship.

We thank Jiskani et al. for their thoughtful comments on our article titled “Clinical Phenotypes and Glycopeptide Escalation Patterns in Pediatric Febrile Neutropenia: Associations with Short-Term Outcomes and Acute Kidney Injury Risk” [1]. We appreciate their recognition of our phenotype-based approach and welcome the opportunity to clarify the rationale and interpretation of our study.

Their commentary focuses primarily on the association between early glycopeptide escalation and acute kidney injury (AKI). We agree that observational studies remain susceptible to residual confounding, particularly confounding by indication, despite analytical approaches such as propensity score weighting [2, 3]. For this reason, we intentionally avoided causal language throughout our study. As discussed in our original article, the temporal relationship between glycopeptide escalation and AKI could not be established because of the retrospective study design; therefore, the observed association was presented as hypothesis-generating rather than evidence of a direct nephrotoxic effect.

The apparent difference between our interpretation and that of Jiskani et al. reflects the different objectives of two analyses. Their comments appropriately focus on causal treatment-effect estimation, whereas our study was designed to identify baseline clinical phenotypes and examine how these phenotypes relate to escalation behavior and short-term clinical outcomes in routine practice.

We also acknowledge that cumulative nephrotoxic exposure allows a more comprehensive assessment of renal risk than a binary indicator of concomitant nephrotoxic agents. However, our objective was not to develop a nephrotoxicity prediction model but rather to account for major baseline confounding while preserving a parsimonious analytical framework appropriate for the available sample size. Although residual confounding cannot be excluded, we do not believe that it alters the principal observation of our study: escalation behavior and renal outcomes clustered preferentially within specific baseline clinical phenotypes.

Jiskani et al. further suggest that incorporating time-varying covariates or target trial emulation would strengthen causal inference. We agree that these approaches are appropriate when the objective is to estimate treatment effects [3]. However, that was not the objective of our study. Our study was designed to identify baseline clinical phenotypes present at the onset of febrile neutropenia rather than to emulate a therapeutic trial. Restricting phenotype derivation to baseline variables was therefore intentional, as incorporating post-baseline information would fundamentally redefine the construct under investigation, yielding dynamic prognostic classifications rather than baseline clinical phenotypes. This strategy is consistent with phenotype discovery studies on sepsis and acute respiratory distress syndrome, where latent classes are derived from baseline clinical characteristics to preserve temporal validity and biological interpretability [4, 5]. Target trial emulation and longitudinal modeling are valuable approaches, but they address different research questions and should be regarded as complementary rather than alternative methodologies.

Jiskani et al. also note that physician-specific prescribing preferences and institutional stewardship practices may have influenced escalation decisions. We agree that these factors contribute to antimicrobial prescription in routine care. This is consistent with the observational design of our study, in which glycopeptide escalation was analyzed not simply as antimicrobial exposure but as a clinical decision made under diagnostic uncertainty. Accordingly, prescribing variability was considered part of the phenomenon under investigation rather than merely a source of residual bias. This distinction is central to the interpretation of our findings and explains why our study was designed as a phenotype discovery investigation rather than a treatment-effect analysis.

In summary, we appreciate the thoughtful comments of Jiskani et al. and agree that future studies integrating cumulative nephrotoxic burden, longitudinal clinical trajectories, and modern causal inference methods will further refine antimicrobial stewardship in pediatric febrile neutropenia. Nevertheless, we believe that these approaches complement rather than diminish the principal contribution of our study, which was to identify clinically meaningful baseline phenotypes and examine how they relate to real-world escalation practices. We hope that this phenotype-based framework will provide a foundation for future studies evaluating phenotype-specific treatment strategies and outcomes in pediatric febrile neutropenia.

We thank Jiskani et al. for their constructive comments and the Editor-in-Chief of the journal for the opportunity to further clarify our work.
