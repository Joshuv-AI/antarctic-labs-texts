Transforming Healthcare: A Scoping Review of the Significance of Value-Based Healthcare
Authors: Hind Almodaimegh, Laila Carolina Abu Esba, Numan Alabdan, Yousif S. Alakeel, Mohammed Alharbi, Saleh Aldekhael, Khalil Almajed
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
IntroductionValue-based healthcare (VBHC) is supplanting the traditional volume-based healthcare paradigm in healthcare systems. In the healthcare industry, value creation focuses on the health outcomes that individuals and institutions achieve while maintaining an optimal cost-benefit ratio. This scoping review aimed to determine the essential components and results of VBHC implementation.MethodsA scoping review was conducted using the patient, intervention, and outcome model. PubMed (Medline), Web of Science, and Embase databases were searched using a combination of keywords. The search results were then subjected to a step-by-step screening protocol to identify the related studies and extract data.ResultsThe initial database search identified 3055 studies. After removing duplicates, 1920 studies were eligible for title/abstract screening. Abstract/title screening identified 268 studies, of which 115 studies were selected for full-text screening. Fifteen studies were selected for the scoping review after full-text screening. Analysis revealed that 73% of studies reported improved interdisciplinary collaboration, 67% demonstrated enhanced patient-centered care approaches, and 60% showed measurable cost reductions. Key implementation factors included strong leadership (identified in 87% of studies), patient involvement (reported in 80% of studies), and integrated care units (implemented in 73% of studies). All studies agreed on the importance of VBHC in improving patient care quality and reducing costs.ConclusionVBHC can help improve the overall healthcare experience. The VBHC model is a promising strategy to enhance healthcare effectiveness and long-term viability; however, successful implementation requires careful attention to organizational factors and stakeholder engagement.

INTRODUCTION

The call for value-based healthcare (VBHC) to replace volume-driven healthcare emerged during the 1990s.[1] This shift involves healthcare systems prioritizing the quality of care provided over the quantity of care delivered. VBHC, a novel approach to delivering and evaluating healthcare, was initially introduced by Porter and Teisberg,[2] which led to increased interest in transitioning to a value-driven healthcare system. Value is defined as the health outcomes achieved per dollar expended; VBHC is committed to providing patients with value by reducing healthcare expenditures, enhancing outcomes, or both.[1]

VBHC has gained growing attention since its inception. Consequently, healthcare institutions in various countries are adapting their strategies toward VBHC. VBHC consists of six interconnected components: (1) structuring care into integrated practice units; (2) implementing a supportive information technology platform; (3) adopting bundled payments for care cycles; (4) assessing costs and outcomes for each patient; integrating care delivery across different facilities; (5) expanding high-quality services across geographic areas; and (6) organizing care into integrated practice units.[3,4] These elements have been presented in Figure 2. During the initial phases, however, sufficient information was scarce regarding the identification and subsequent use of specific tactics for implementing VBHC was scarce. The circumstances in which such strategies would be most successful were also not well understood during the early era of VBHC.[5]

The Health Sector Transformation Strategy was launched in 2021 by the Ministry of Health in the Kingdom of Saudi Arabia (KSA) as part of the National Transformation Program. This effort aims to streamline the healthcare system by implementing a comprehensive, efficient, and synchronized approach based on VBHC concepts. Significant differences in healthcare service quality partly contributed to this shift, due to inadequate measurement of patient procedures and results, as well as the absence of standardized treatment pathways and protocols.[6] The healthcare system in the KSA is influenced by a range of socioeconomic issues, including an aging and growing population, increasing healthcare costs, and a growing need to adopt technological innovations that improve the quality, availability, effectiveness, and efficiency of healthcare services.[7,8]

The VBHC model represents a paradigm shift from traditional volume-based care delivery.[9,10] While conventional healthcare systems often prioritize consultation capacity and throughput over individual patient outcomes, the VBHC approach emphasizes comprehensive, patient-centered care coordination.[11] This transition addresses the clinical trend of declining patient-centeredness by proposing VBHC as a means to revitalize patient participation and ensure healthcare service sustainability. The model implements both quantitative strategies (through outcome measurement and cost analysis) and qualitative approaches (through patient experience enhancement and care process improvement) to manage and deliver healthcare to populations.[12]

Despite the implementation and analysis of various VBHC initiatives over the past few years, the concept remains unaccepted, and a knowledge gap exists regarding the existence of a consensus on the definition of the VBHC concept. This can be attributed to several factors, including discrepancies in the global health system and the scarcity of data demonstrating the efficacy of interventions implemented following the VBHC model.[13] Diverse conceptions of value and essential components for effective execution exist alongside numerous endeavors promoting distinct favorable results. The COVID-19 pandemic highlighted significant flaws in fee-based healthcare models in terms of task prioritization and service delivery, underscoring that VBHC is the way forward.

This review examines and consolidates the current understanding of VBHC by comprehensively assessing available literature, focusing on VBHC conceptualization, essential components, and implementation results. We aim to discover, compare, and summarize findings related to (1) the importance of implementing VBHC, (2) challenges in implementation, (3) success stories for VBHC implementation, and (4) strategies for implementation in a tertiary center.

METHODS

Study Design

The current study was designed as a scoping review, in which pertinent studies on a particular topic are meticulously identified and selected according to predetermined criteria; this strategy is best suited for topics that are broad and diverse in terms of available literature.[14] The Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines and PRISMA Extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) protocol were followed.[15–17]

Search Strategy

A literature search strategy was formulated to enhance the efficiency of evidence retrieval from databases, using the patient, intervention, comparison, and outcome (PICO) framework as its foundation. The process entailed constructing the research inquiry by considering the patient or issue of interest (the healthcare system), the intervention (VBHC), and the outcomes (the impact on healthcare efficiency and the challenges faced during implementation).[17] Hence, the format used was the patient, intervention, and outcome (PIO) model.[3]

Multiple electronic databases were queried, including PubMed (Medline), Embase, and Web of Science, encompassing the period from January 2006 to January 2024, when Porter and Teisberg coined the term VBHC.[2]

Although no specific Medical Subject Heading (MeSH) term in PubMed pertains to VBHC, the search was expanded to include the MeSH term “value-based health insurance,” as it represents a related concept within healthcare payment models that some VBHC studies discuss. Without a VBHC MeSH term, an array of search terms was implemented. The search terminology was set up as follows: “VBHC” OR “Value-Based Health Insurance” OR “value based care” “OR value-based healthcare” OR “valuebased care” OR “value-based health care” OR “valuebased healthcare” “value based health care” OR “value-based care” OR “value-based healthcare” OR “value-based health care.”

Study Eligibility

All English-language, full-text articles that described the adoption of VBHC in a healthcare system or hospital and were published between January 2006 and January 2024 were eligible for inclusion. Porter and Teisberg[2] coined the term “value-based healthcare” in a 2006 study, but the phrases “value-based care” and “value-driven care” had already been in use. To carefully adhere to the notions presented by the aforementioned study, the article selection process was refined to studies that specifically used the terms “VBHC” or “value-based care” with a clear citation to the original study. The selection procedure excluded studies that examined broader conceptualizations or similar ideas, such as population health or integrated payments, but did not use the aforementioned terms.

Study Screening and Selection

EndNote (Clarivate Analytics) was used to import the studies obtained from electronic databases and eliminate duplicates. The eligibility determination procedure was executed using the web-based application, Rayyan. Two independent reviewers assessed the titles and abstracts. In the absence of agreement, the disputed titles were subject to deliberation by a third reviewer. The same two reviewers independently carried out the full-text screening procedure. Data Extraction

The data from all included studies were extracted and evaluated. The results of the studies were organized and summarized according to the extraction variables: year, country, author, VBHC component, VBHC outcome, and study design. Then the data were restructured to correspond with the research inquiries regarding VBHC conceptualization. To determine what is presently operational or planned for operation, we analyzed data obtained for VBHC component and implementation. The research specified the implementation strategies and corresponding effects in the healthcare sector alongside current challenges.

RESULTS

Study Selection and Characteristics

The initial database search identified 3055 studies. After removing duplicates from different databases, 1920 studies were eligible for title/abstract screening. The abstract/title screening identified 268 studies. Of these, 115 studies were selected for full-text screening. Following this, 15 studies were selected for the scoping review after meeting our strict inclusion criteria, which required the specific use of VBHC terminology with clear reference to Porter and Teisberg’s framework (Table 1).[2]

The 15 included studies represented diverse geographical regions, including seven studies (47%) from European countries (the Netherlands, Sweden, Denmark, Germany, Spain, United Kingdom), four studies (27%) from North America (United States), three studies (20%) from multicountry Latin American collaborations, and one study (6%) from Asia. Regarding study design, 12 studies (80%) employed qualitative methodologies, two studies (13%) used quantitative approaches, and one study (7%) used mixed methods. The timeframe of studies ranged from 2006 to 2023, with most studies (73%) published after 2020, indicating growing research interest in VBHC implementation.

Value-Based Healthcare (VBHC) Conceptualization and Definition

The central focus of each definition of VBHC identified in the literature review was the patient. VBHC is a methodology based on three guiding principles. First, assessing medical costs and outcomes. Second, the coordination of healthcare services in alignment with patients’ comprehensive care cycles and medical conditions. Third, the maximization of patient value.[18–20] Following these basic tenets, Steinman et al[21] defined value as “what patients consider to be the most significant.” The patient value can be defined as “the ratio of the costs associated with achieving those outcomes to the patient experience and optimal patient-relevant health outcomes.”[22,23]

Enhancing value within the framework of VBHC requires optimizing outcomes concerning cost. This highlights the importance of quantifying health costs and outcomes, supporting the third principle of VBHC as argued by Nilsson et al.[18–20] The value equation is defined as the ratio of health outcomes to costs.[24] VBHC is “a global phenomenon that requires significant changes throughout different levels of healthcare organizations, including managerial viewpoints and the doctor-patient dynamic.”[25]

VBHC must prioritize patient outcomes throughout healthcare delivery.[13] The medical conditions of patients should dictate the structure of healthcare. Adopting a value agenda that delineates the healthcare framework as integrated practice units further supports this concept. Healthcare systems should adopt integrated payment models for care cycles, and implementing information technology platforms is crucial for achieving VBHC.[24]

Analysis of the 15 studies revealed consistent usage of the term “patient” across all definitions. In 11 studies (72.7%), a correlation is observed between costs and outcomes. However, there were inconsistencies among definitions regarding the delineation of the cost-outcome relationship. This correlation was established in some cases in direct relation to the VBHC concept, whereas in others, it was exclusively linked to the definition of “value” as a component of the concept.

Critical Success Factors for VBHC Implementation

Leadership and governance

Strong leadership was identified as essential in 13 studies (87%) to prevent potential setbacks in the implementation process or termination of value-based initiatives.[18–20] Effective leadership teams are characterized by their ability to motivate and advance groups, endure challenging circumstances, and consistently contribute innovative ideas and approaches.[13,22,23] In the realm of VBHC, outstanding leaders advocate for implementing changes and restructuring to ensure the organization’s efficiency and establish patient-centric processes.[26]

Several studies concentrated on the criticality of leadership in orchestrating collaboration during the preparatory stages of VBHC implementation.[19,27] Studies indicate that the leadership of the hospital director is critical for the effective implementation of the VBHC approach, as it lends validation to team decisions and facilitates preorganizational redesign, both of which are essential for VBHC to function as a management tool.[19,27]

Patient involvement and engagement

Patient involvement was emphasized in 12 studies (80%) as crucial for understanding the patient’s viewpoint and ensuring that VBHC implementation by teams aligns with patient experiences. Several studies have emphasized that the patient is the central focus of VBHC.[19,26,28] Patient participation is vital for understanding the patient’s viewpoint and ensuring consistency between VBHC implementation and patient experiences. Teams can effectively evaluate care delivery by considering patient value when patients or their representatives actively engage in the process.

Patient involvement in shared decision-making processes is considered a critical element of VBHC.[19] In the pre-implementation phase, patients are regarded as essential constituents of the value chain; their participation is critical for ensuring personalized care that considers their preferences and needs, and for determining outcomes that will be significant to assess in the future.[23]

Organizational structure and integration

Before implementing VBHC, 11 studies (73%) emphasized the criticality of transforming healthcare organizations, which are frequently divided into separate departments, into integrated care units. Specialized department-based healthcare systems present difficulties in quantifying costs throughout the process, analyzing outcomes, and monitoring patients’ movement between departments. Owing to these rationales, care provision should be organized into multidisciplinary care pathways or integrated care facilities dedicated to a specific patient population afflicted with a particular medical condition.[29–31]

Outcomes and Challenges of VBHC Implementation

Improved collaboration and communication

Positive outcomes from implementing VBHC included enhanced collaboration among departments and professionals, reported in 11 studies (73%). This enhanced coordination facilitated outcome measurement and improved data quality.[31] The creation of integrated units centered on medical conditions enabled hospitals to better address interdependencies of various activities required for patient care and fostered closer collaboration among all parties involved in treating patients with specific medical conditions.[19]

Enhanced patient-centered care

Ten studies (67%) reported that implementing VBHC facilitated enhanced patient follow-up and streamlined access to appropriate patient care. The implementation of VBHC increased the importance of considering patients’ perspectives and priorities. Patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs) and patient experience measures (PREMs) were implemented systematically, improving data quality through methodical approaches to data collection, thereby mitigating the risk of overlooking information and diminishing analytic bias.

Cost reduction and efficiency gains

Nine studies (60%) demonstrated measurable cost reductions through VBHC implementation. Of these, six showed direct cost reductions with maintained or improved outcomes, two demonstrated improved outcomes with cost-neutral approaches, and one showed initial increased costs with long-term savings through reduced complications and readmissions. Value-based measurements encouraged team members to collaborate by fostering a sense of shared accountability for particular objectives.[21,27]

System-level improvements

VBHC implementation increased organizational sustainability, particularly when knowledgeable professionals and effective leadership were present. The implementation process yielded notable organizational benefits, including enhanced collaboration between inpatient and outpatient treatment, thereby facilitating patients’ access to appropriate care at the proper time.

Implementation challenges and barriers

Despite positive outcomes, the studies identified several challenges in VBHC implementation. The lack of a standardized framework governing the identification and execution of essential components resulted in observed discrepancies in examination results. Additionally, five studies (33.3%) reported difficulties in measuring outcomes consistently across different departments and care settings.

Resource allocation challenges were mentioned in seven studies (47%), particularly regarding the allocation of sufficient time and personnel to guarantee that implementation teams possess requisite resources for planning, implementing necessary reforms, monitoring and adapting to changes, and identifying and standardizing pertinent patient outcome measures.

DISCUSSION

This review aimed to analyze VBHC, its necessary components for successful implementation, and the advantages that result from adopting VBHC in healthcare systems. The definitions established in this literature review of the VBHC conceptualization referred to both the concept of value within the model and the term VBHC as a whole. Value is commonly described as the overall health outcomes achieved for the patients, including the costs of the overall treatment process, and the majority of research supports this definition.[13]

Studies vary in their approach to essential elements or factors in implementing VBHC despite widespread consensus on the concept of value. Because the term “VBHC” is not well-defined, multiple ways to apply it rely on how health systems are managed and the geographical context.[32] This work has the potential to clarify the enigma surrounding the fundamental elements of VBHC implementation documented in scholarly literature.

The most commonly scrutinized critical elements of VBHC implementation include: (1) capable leadership to direct and inspire teams throughout pre-implementation and implementation stages; (2) incorporating patient viewpoints to guarantee individualized care and ensure VBHC implementation is responsive to patient experience; (3) establishing integrated care centers tailored to particular medical conditions or patient populations to enable continuous patient monitoring; and (4) allocating sufficient time and personnel to guarantee implementation teams possess requisite resources.

Several benefits of implementing VBHC have been identified, providing valuable insights for future implementation endeavors.[33] Multiple studies have examined ways to enhance collaboration among healthcare professionals, encompassing both the promotion of understanding regarding the importance of cooperation and the promotion of collaboration between departments and professionals involved in providing patient care.[34,35] The creation of integrated units was beneficial in promoting better communication among healthcare providers who treat patients with a particular disease, as well as between different levels of care.[36,37]

Furthermore, implementing VBHC not only enhanced patient accessibility and facilitated their ability to receive appropriate treatment, but also increased follow-up care quality. Implementing this paradigm made it easier to monitor outcomes and improve data quality. According to the International Consortium for Health Outcomes Measurement (ICHOM), monitoring outcomes is crucial for optimal patient care.[38]

The design and implementation strategies for VBHC must begin with assembling a multidisciplinary team that incorporates experts from various fields to develop comprehensive, tailor-made “value” plans. The plan should prioritize the clinical conditions of utmost significance, followed by appropriate training for support team members. Real-time monitoring of clinical and patient-reported outcomes, as well as analysis of cost benefits, must follow the implementation of the “value” plan. Data must be incorporated into performance tools to provide feedback on the plan and identify improvements that can be made to enhance patient experience and cost-effectiveness. This scoping review enhances the existing literature by addressing the lack of a clear and comprehensive definition and understanding of the model. It does so by examining variations in the interpretation and conceptualization of VBHC and analyzing included studies, both individually and comparatively. Additionally, this study aimed to fill a gap in previous studies by providing a comprehensive analysis of the positive outcomes reported and evaluating the methodologies used for implementation.

Limitations

Despite considerable contributions provided by the literature review, several limitations exist. According to the PRISMA 2020 guidelines, our study protocol was not created and registered prospectively. Furthermore, owing to the limitations of our search, which only included publications in English from 2006 to 2024, we may have missed key studies in other languages that could have provided valuable insights into understanding VBHC.

The significant reduction from 3055 to 15 studies reflects our strict inclusion criteria, which required studies to specifically use “VBHC” or “value-based care” with clear citation to Porter and Teisberg’s original framework.[2] This exclusion includes broader population health or integrated payment studies that do not align with the specific VBHC model. While this stringent approach ensured conceptual consistency, it resulted in a focused but limited sample size.

The predominance of qualitative studies (80% of included studies) limits quantitative assessment of VBHC effectiveness. While qualitative research provides valuable insights into implementation processes, barriers, and facilitators crucial for understanding VBHC adoption, it constrains our ability to provide definitive quantitative evidence of effectiveness.

An additional significant limitation is that a considerable number of examined studies merely provide accounts of individual experiences without evaluating efficacy of broader implementation of interventions. The lack of empirical evidence supporting effectiveness of these interventions limits generalizability of findings. Notwithstanding constraints that underscore the need for additional research to rectify knowledge gaps pertaining to this pivotal healthcare paradigm and the capacity to sustain and expand the VBHC model, we maintain that conclusions analyzed in this comprehensive literature review are dependable and may serve as a guide for subsequent investigations.

For successful VBHC implementation, healthcare organizations should focus on: (1) establishing strong leadership committed to value-based transformation; (2) actively involving patients in all phases of implementation; (3) creating integrated care units organized around patient conditions rather than departmental structures; (4) investing in robust information technology platforms for outcome measurement; and (5) developing comprehensive training programs for all stakeholders.

Future research should prioritize quantitative studies with robust outcome measures, multisite implementation studies to test generalizability, economic evaluations to demonstrate return on investment, and longitudinal studies to assess the sustainability of VBHC initiatives. Academics must collaborate across disciplines to conduct future research on the effectiveness of implementing VBHC and advance this critical healthcare transformation.

CONCLUSION

The results of this scoping literature study suggest that VBHC implementation may improve the sustainability and efficiency of healthcare. These results highlight the urgent need for a shared understanding of VBHC, focusing on key elements to reduce misunderstandings and provide a unified approach to its application. It is essential to have a shared sense of VBHC to differentiate it from other, more general ideas and guarantee that everyone knows how it should be applied. Furthermore, there is a considerable variance in interpretation, resulting in various methods for implementing VBHC initiatives, as well as differing levels of success in terms of efficacy and the long-term sustainability of healthcare systems. Another key finding from this investigation was the dearth of studies on assessing implementation techniques. Our research suggests that the administrative aspects of implementing VBHC in practice have not received enough attention.
