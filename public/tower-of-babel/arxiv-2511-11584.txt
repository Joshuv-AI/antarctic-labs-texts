Output Supervision Can Obfuscate the Chain of Thought
Jacob Drori, Luke Marks, [0em] Bryce WoodworthAlex CloudAlexander Matt Turner, [0.3em] Equal contribution; order chosen randomly

Abstract
OpenAI 2025a showed that training against a chain of thought (CoT) monitor can cause obfuscated CoTs, which contain bad behavior the monitor cannot detect. They proposed to keep CoTs monitorable by training only against output monitors that do not have access to CoT. We show that such training can still cause obfuscated CoTs via two mechanisms. First, when a model is trained to produce a safe-looking output, that model may generalize to making its CoTs look safe. Second, since later tokens are conditioned on earlier ones, safe - looking CoTs may increase the likelihood of safe outputs, causing safe-looking CoTs to be reinforced. We introduce two mitigations to address these two issues, which achieve a Pareto improvement in terms of monitorability and task performance compared to regular training. Our work implies that preserving CoT monitorability is more difficult than previously thought; we suggest practical guidelines for AI developers to maintain monitorable CoTs.



1
Introduction

Reasoning models, which generate a CoT before their final answer, dominate mathematics and coding benchmarks
(
Guo et al. 2025
;
OpenAI 2025b
;
Qwen Team 2025
)
.
Korbak et al. 2025
argue that AI developers should aim to maintain the ability to detect model misbehavior by analyzing CoTs (“CoT monitorability”), claiming that it offers a “unique opportunity for AI safety”.

Baker et al. 2025
show that training frontier models against a CoT monitor can cause their CoTs to become
obfuscated
: unwanted behaviors persist in a way that is undetectable by the monitor. To address this, they recommend against directly optimizing the CoT. However, we show that even training against a monitor that sees only the final output (and not the CoT) can still cause obfuscated CoT. We refer to this effect as
feedback spillover
, and show that it can occur for two reasons:

Figure 1:
Feedback spillover:
training against an output monitor obfuscates the CoT.

1.

Parametric Feedback Spillover
: Adjusting a model’s parameters to make the output more likely to look safe may generalize to make all model generations, including CoTs, more safe-looking.

2.

Conditional Feedback Spillover
: Since later tokens are conditioned on earlier ones, safe-looking CoTs may increase the likelihood of safe-looking outputs, causing safe-looking CoTs to be reinforced.

To mitigate parametric feedback spillover, we generate the CoT and output using two distinct models, which we refer to as the “Mind” and “Face” respectively, as proposed by
Kokotajlo & Demski 2024
. To mitigate conditional feedback spillover, we modify the gradient update by removing the term which reinforces CoTs that lead to safe-looking outputs.

We demonstrate feedback spillover and test our mitigations in three reinforcement learning environments:

1.

Polynomial derivative factoring:
Following
Emmons et al. 2025
, the model must find the factored form of a polynomial’s derivative. Penalizing the model for writing the expanded form of the derivative in its output causes it to use a workaround method in its CoT to avoid writing the expanded derivative.

2.

Questions with hints:
Following
Chen et al. 2025
,
METR 2025
, the model is given hints for questions that either have incorrect answers or are too difficult for the model to solve. Penalizing the model for mentioning the hints in its output causes it to mention them less often in its CoT, even though it must be using the hints to solve the problems correctly.

3.

Multi-turn terminal interaction:
The model must solve a text-based problem and submit its answer to a Python verifier. The verifier checks for an answer that is deliberately incorrect, so the only way to solve the problem is to cheat by inspecting the verifier’s source code and reading off the desired answer. An LLM judge monitors the output for indications that the model has cheated. Penalizing the model when cheating is detected in the output causes it to become less detectable in the CoT.

We observe strong feedback spillover in all environments, and find that our mitigations can significantly reduce (and sometimes entirely avoid) feedback spillover.

Our contributions are as follows:

1.

We introduce feedback spillover and mathematically explain why it occurs.

2.

We give the first demonstrations of feedback spillover in multiple environments.

3.

We implement two mitigations for feedback spillover, one of which is novel. Compared to regular training, the mitigations achieve a Pareto improvement in terms of task performance and CoT monitorability.

4.

We offer practical recommendations that AI developers can implement today to minimize the effects of feedback spillover.

Code for reproducing our experiments is available at
this repository
.

2
Related Work

A CoT refers to tokens generated before a final answer in an attempt to increase the likelihood of the desired final answer. Early work prompted models to think step-by-step in order to generate CoTs
(
Wei et al. 2022
)
, but more recently, frontier models have been trained with reinforcement learning to generate useful CoTs. Such models (often termed ‘reasoning models’) now dominate mathematics and coding benchmarks
(
Guo et al. 2025
;
OpenAI 2025b
;
Qwen Team 2025
)
.

CoT monitoring refers to analyzing CoTs for particular behaviors. CoTs improve performance, so it has been anticipated that the most performant models’ CoTs will necessarily give a rationale for their answers.
Korbak et al. 2025
argue that this offers a unique opportunity for AI safety. CoT monitors are typically language models that give a score according to a rubric
(
Baker et al. 2025
)
.

A limitation of CoT monitoring is that the CoT may not reflect the steps the model used to reach its final answer.
Chen et al. 2025
consider a CoT
unfaithful
if it omits important intermediate reasoning, and shows that unfaithful CoT can occur in a question-answer setup. More recent work shows that when the problem a model is solving is sufficiently hard as to require the CoT to give the correct answer, CoTs are faithful.
(
Emmons et al. 2025
;
METR 2025
)
.
METR 2025
argues that monitoring unfaithful CoTs may still be useful for oversight.

3
Theoretical Analysis of Feedback Spillover

In this section, we define feedback spillover mathematically and describe conditions under which it occurs. This analysis offers post-hoc justification for the mitigation proposed by
Kokotajlo & Demski 2024
—using separate “Mind” and “Face” models to generate the CoT and output. It also motivates a new mitigation, which we call
reward targeting
.

3.1
Definition of feedback spillover

Let
p
be a prompt drawn from a distribution
\mathcal{D}
. Let
c
and
o
be the CoT and output, respectively, generated by a language model policy
\pi_{\theta}
that factors as
\pi_{\theta}(c,o\mid p)\;=\;\pi_{\theta}(c\mid p)\,\pi_{\theta}(o\mid p,c).
The output here refers to all the text following some special delimiter token, such as
</think>
, that marks the end of the CoT.

Definition 1
.

A
monitor
is a map from tuples
(p,c,o)
to the interval
[0,1]
. We interpret
0
as “this text is safe”, and
1
as “this text is unsafe”.

We use two monitors:

•

Output monitor
. Used for training and evaluation.
M_{\text{out}}(p,c,o)=m(p,o)
for some
m
. For example,
m(p,o)
might be the harmfulness score from an LLM judge that sees the prompt and final output.

•

CoT monitor
. Only used for evaluation.
M_{\text{CoT}}(p,c,o)=m(p,c)
where
m
is the same as above. For example,
m(p,c)
might be the harmfulness score from an LLM judge that sees the prompt and CoT.

Definition 2
.

Given a function
M(p,c,o)
, we use the shorthand
\mathbb{E}_{\theta}[M]
to denote the expected value of
M
when sampling prompts from
\mathcal{D}
and generating completions using the policy. Formally:

\mathbb{E}_{\theta}[M]\;\equiv\;\mathbb{E}_{p\sim\mathcal{D}}\;\mathbb{E}_{(c,o)\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid p)}\!\left[M(p,c,o)\right].

(1)

We train
\theta
with
reinforce

(
Williams 1992
)
to maximize

\mathbb{E}_{\theta}\big[R_{\text{task}}-\lambda M_{\text{out}}\big]
. In this expression,
\lambda\geq 0
is a hyperparameter, and
R_{\text{task}}
measures whether the model completed the task (e.g. solving a math puzzle) correctly. We also train a control model
\theta_{\text{control}}
to solely maximize
\mathbb{E}_{\theta_{\text{control}}}[R_{\text{task}}]
.

Definition 3
.

We say
feedback spillover
has occurred if the CoT monitor score decreases relative to the control model:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\theta}\!\left[M_{\text{CoT}}\right]

\displaystyle<\mathbb{E}_{\theta_{\text{control}}}\!\left[M_{\text{CoT}}\right].

(2)

We are particularly interested in cases of feedback spillover where training fails to remove the bad behavior—that is, cases where training results in obfuscated CoT.

3.2
Conditions for feedback spillover

To understand why feedback spillover may occur, consider starting with
\theta=\theta_{\text{control}}
and taking a single
reinforce
step. We update
\theta
using the reward
R_{\text{task}}-\lambda M_{\text{out}}
, whereas we update
\theta_{\text{control}}
using
R_{\text{task}}
. So the two policies have different expected changes
\Delta\mathbb{E}_{\theta}[M_{\text{CoT}}]
and
\Delta\mathbb{E}_{\theta_{\text{control}}}[M_{\text{CoT}}]
in their CoT monitor scores. If
\Delta\mathbb{E}_{\theta}[M_{\text{CoT}}]<\Delta\mathbb{E}_{\theta_{\text{control}}}[M_{\text{CoT}}]
, then the update step contributes to feedback spillover. To see when the inequality holds, the following definition turns out to be useful:

Definition 4
.

Let
\nabla_{\text{out}}
be the gradient holding
\pi_{\theta}(c\mid p)
fixed and
\nabla_{\text{CoT}}
be the gradient holding
\pi_{\theta}(o\mid p,c)
fixed. More precisely, for a monitor
M
, define:

\displaystyle\nabla_{\text{out}}\,\mathbb{E}_{\theta}[M]

\displaystyle\equiv\;\mathbb{E}_{p\sim\mathcal{D}}\sum_{c,o}\pi_{\theta}(c\mid p)\,\nabla\pi_{\theta}(o\mid p,c)\,M(p,c,o);and

(3)

\displaystyle\nabla_{\text{CoT}}\,\mathbb{E}_{\theta}[M]

\displaystyle\equiv\;\mathbb{E}_{p\sim\mathcal{D}}\sum_{c,o}\nabla\pi_{\theta}(c\mid p)\,\pi_{\theta}(o\mid p,c)\,M(p,c,o).

(4)

By the product rule,
\nabla=\nabla_{\text{out}}+\nabla_{\text{CoT}}
, offering a useful decomposition of gradients into two terms (see Appendix
A.1
for details). In Appendix
A.2
, we consider the simple case of stochastic gradient descent with learning rate
\eta
, and show that the following equation holds at leading order in
\eta
:

\boxed{\Delta\mathbb{E}_{\theta}[M_{\text{CoT}}]-\Delta\mathbb{E}_{\theta_{\text{control}}}[M_{\text{CoT}}]=-\eta\lambda\big(\nabla_{\text{CoT}}\,\mathbb{E}_{\theta}[M_{\text{out}}]+\nabla_{\text{out}}\,\mathbb{E}_{\theta}[M_{\text{out}}]\big)\cdot\nabla_{\text{CoT}}\mathbb{E}_{\theta}[M_{\text{CoT}}].}

(5)

The update step contributes to feedback spillover if the LHS of Equation
5
is negative, since then
\mathbb{E}_{\theta}[M_{\text{CoT}}]
decreases relative to
\mathbb{E}_{\theta_{\text{control}}}[M_{\text{CoT}}]
. We identify the positivity of the two dot products on the RHS of Equation
5
as the two mechanisms behind feedback spillover:

Definition 5
.

Conditional feedback spillover
occurs when the following happens: if we perturb
\theta
so as to reduce
M_{\text{out}}
when output policy
\pi_{\theta}(o\mid p,c)
is held fixed, then that perturbation also reduces
M_{\text{CoT}}
. Formally:

\nabla_{\text{CoT}}\,\mathbb{E}_{\theta}[M_{\text{out}}]\;\cdot\;\nabla_{\text{CoT}}\,\mathbb{E}_{\theta}[M_{\text{CoT}}]\;>\;0

(6)

Intuitively,
\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid p,c)
may be more likely to produce an obfuscated output when it conditions on an obfuscated CoT.

Definition 6
.

Parametric feedback spillover
occurs when the following happens: if we perturb
\theta
so as to reduce
M_{\text{out}}
when the CoT policy
\pi_{\theta}(c\mid p)
is held fixed, then that perturbation also reduces
M_{\text{CoT}}
. Formally:

\nabla_{\text{out}}\,\mathbb{E}_{\theta}[M_{\text{out}}]\;\cdot\;\nabla_{\text{CoT}}\,\mathbb{E}_{\theta}[M_{\text{CoT}}]\;>\;0

(7)

Intuitively, parameter settings which produce obfuscated outputs may generalize to also produce obfuscated CoTs.

3.3
Mitigations for feedback spillover

Based on our decomposition of the policy gradient above, we design mitigations for feedback spillover that zero the dot products corresponding to conditional and parametric feedback spillover.

Figure 2:
Our mitigations for feedback spillover.

Conditional feedback spillover.

We introduce
reward targeting
to prevent gradients from the output monitor
M_{\text{out}}
from flowing through CoT tokens. More precisely, reward targeting zeros out the indicated term in the decomposition of the full
reinforce
gradient:

\nabla\mathbb{E}_{\theta}\big[R_{\text{task}}-\lambda M_{\text{out}}\big]=\nabla_{\text{CoT}}\mathbb{E}_{\theta}[R_{\text{task}}]+\nabla_{\text{out}}\mathbb{E}_{\theta}[R_{\text{task}}]-\underbrace{\lambda\nabla_{\text{CoT}}\mathbb{E}_{\theta}[M_{\text{out}}]}_{\text{set to zero}}-\lambda\nabla_{\text{out}}\mathbb{E}_{\theta}[M_{\text{out}}]

(8)

Doing so removes the conditional feedback spillover term from Equation
5
.

Parametric feedback spillover.

We use separate models to generate the CoT and output. We call the CoT model the “mind” since it does the majority of the reasoning. We call the output model the “face” since it presents the result of the reasoning to the user. Thus, the parametric feedback spillover term
\nabla_{\text{out}}\,\mathbb{E}_{\theta}[M_{\text{out}}]\;\cdot\;\nabla_{\text{CoT}}\,\mathbb{E}_{\theta}[M_{\text{CoT}}]
is exactly zero, since the first gradient is nonzero only for the face’s parameters, and the second gradient is nonzero only for the mind’s parameters.

4.3
Multi-turn terminal interaction

4.3.1
Experiment design

In this setting, Qwen3-8B has access to a Bash shell in a temporary directory containing a single file,
verifier.py
. To solve the task, the model must run

python verifier.py [answer]

wrapped inside
<command></command>
tags. Other standard shell commands may also be executed by wrapping them within
<command></command>
tags. Alongside each command, the model provides a short natural-language explanation for why it is running that command.

verifier.py
defines a variable
GROUND_TRUTH
, and the checker accepts if and only if
[answer] == GROUND_TRUTH
. After each command is executed, the terminal output is appended to the context, allowing the model to see previous commands and their outputs.

We use the
Largest Island
task from Reasoning Gym
(
Stojanovski et al. 2025
)
. The model must compute the size of the largest connected component of
1
s in a grid. We modify the task to prevent an honest solution by adding a random nonzero integer to the target answer. For the model to pass, it must either alter
verifier.py
to always pass, or read the ground truth from
verifier.py
using a bash utility such as
cat
.

We use two types of monitor: regex-based and LLM judge, which can each be applied to either the CoT or output. The regex-based monitor returns the number of occurrences of the string “ground truth” in either the CoT or the output. For the LLM judge, we prompt GPT-4.1-mini to return a binary score indicating whether the model discusses reading the verifier code. The judge never sees the contents of the
<command></command>
tags; it only sees the CoT or the natural-language explanation in the output. For more details on our judge setup and our hyperparameters, see Appendix
C.3
. We give example transcripts in Appendix
B.2
.

5
Discussion

CoT monitorability has recently gained considerable attention as a means of overseeing language models. Our results show that output-based training can spoil CoT monitorability via feedback spillover. Before we ran our experiments, we did not know whether feedback spillover would occur. We might have predicted that penalizing a useful behavior in the output would make that behavior occur
more
often in the CoT. That is, output-based training might “move” the behavior to the CoT, removing it from the output without reducing task performance. However, in all environments we studied, we instead saw strong evidence for feedback spillover.

We found that feedback spillover can change CoTs in complex ways, even when penalizing a simple behavior in the output. In polynomial derivative factoring (
Section

4.1
), penalizing the expanded form of the derivative in the output (a simple penalty) could cause the model to use the product rule to find the factored derivative in its CoT (a complex effect). Although that experiment used in-context examples to encourage use of the product rule, complex behavioral changes in CoTs may not be predictable in advance for real training runs.

Reward targeting and Mind & Face strongly mitigated spillover in Sections
4.1
and
4.3
but could not reduce feedback spillover without harming task performance in Section
4.2
. Overall, reward targeting had a larger effect than Mind & Face. The interventions worked better when applied at the same time.

Reward targeting may interest both model developers and the research community, as it does not introduce additional computational cost and is simple to implement. Our implementation of Mind & Face incurs a high computational cost, since it trains two copies of the policy. However, there may be easy ways to increase efficiency. For example, perhaps the face can be made much smaller than the mind, since the face need only read the solution from the mind’s CoT and then make the solution presentable (according to the output monitor). Mind & Face may also allow for easily changing the style of a model’s outputs without affecting its reasoning abilities. Developers could swap out the Face model to a Face trained to present solutions in a different style to achieve this.

5.1
Recommendations for developers

Do not assume that training against output-only monitors preserves CoT monitorability!
In all tested environments, we observed strong feedback spillover. This did not require iteration on environment design or output penalty: whenever we saw the output monitor score decrease and the task reward increase, we always saw the CoT monitor score decrease. Frontier model developers should therefore not assume that output-based training will leave the CoT monitorable.

Study feedback spillover in frontier models.
Output-based training applied to existing models may be causing feedback spillover, affecting CoT monitorability today. We recommend that frontier model developers study model organisms of feedback spillover by post-training frontier models in purpose-built environments. We also recommend studying whether existing safety post-training pipelines cause feedback spillover.

Test spillover mitigations on frontier models.
We believe that further iteration on these mitigations could yield methods to reduce feedback spillover with low computational and capabilities overhead.

Consider feedback spillover in safety cases.
When referencing CoT monitorability in a safety case
(
Clymer et al. 2024
)
, we request that frontier model developers describe how they mitigated feedback spillover.

6
Conclusion

We identified the phenomenon of
feedback spillover
and demonstrated it in three environments. We showed that both simple regex penalties and more complicated LLM judge penalties can cause feedback spillover. By analyzing feedback spillover in terms of the policy gradient, we showed that feedback spillover can occur via two mechanisms. We designed a mitigation for each mechanism. Our mitigations successfully avoided feedback spillover while maintaining high task completion in two environments but destabilized training in a third environment. Instead of simply trusting output-only monitoring, we hope that AI developers take seriously the risk of feedback spillover.

A.1
Policy gradient decomposition via the product rule

Recall Definition
4
:

\displaystyle\nabla_{\text{out}}\,\mathbb{E}_{\theta}[M]

\displaystyle\equiv\;\mathbb{E}_{p\sim\mathcal{D}}\sum_{c,o}\pi_{\theta}(c\mid p)\,\nabla\pi_{\theta}(o\mid p,c)\,M(p,c,o)

(9)

\displaystyle\nabla_{\text{CoT}}\,\mathbb{E}_{\theta}[M]

\displaystyle\equiv\;\mathbb{E}_{p\sim\mathcal{D}}\sum_{c,o}\nabla\pi_{\theta}(c\mid p)\,\pi_{\theta}(o\mid p,c)\,M(p,c,o).

(10)

Summing the two expressions and applying the product rule yields:

\displaystyle\nabla_{\text{out}}\,\mathbb{E}_{\theta}[M]+\nabla_{\text{CoT}}\,\mathbb{E}_{\theta}[M]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{p\sim\mathcal{D}}\sum_{c,o}\left[\pi_{\theta}(c\mid p)\,\nabla\pi_{\theta}(o\mid p,c)+\nabla\pi_{\theta}(c\mid p)\,\pi_{\theta}(o\mid p,c)\right]M(p,c,o)

(11)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{p\sim\mathcal{D}}\sum_{c,o}\nabla\left[\pi_{\theta}(c\mid p)\,\pi_{\theta}(o\mid p,c)\right]M(p,c,o)

(12)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{p\sim\mathcal{D}}\sum_{c,o}\nabla\pi_{\theta}(c,o\mid p)M(p,c,o)

(13)

\displaystyle=\nabla\mathbb{E}_{\theta}[M].

(14)

So
\nabla=\nabla_{\text{out}}+\nabla_{\text{CoT}}
.

A.2
Derivation of the feedback spillover condition

First, recall the setup. We begin with
\theta=\theta_{\text{control}}
, and apply a single
reinforce
step to
\theta
using reward
R_{\text{task}}-\lambda M_{\text{out}}
, and to
\theta_{\text{control}}
using reward
R_{\text{task}}
. We wish to compute
\Delta\mathbb{E}_{\theta}[M_{\text{CoT}}]
and
\Delta\mathbb{E}_{\theta_{\text{control}}}[M_{\text{CoT}}]
.
The change in any differentiable function
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
, when its input is changed by
\Delta x
, is
\Delta f=\Delta x\cdot\nabla f+o(\Delta x)
. So, at leading order in the step sizes
|\Delta\theta|
and
|\Delta\theta_{\text{control}}|
:

\displaystyle\Delta\mathbb{E}_{\theta}[M_{\text{CoT}}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\theta}[\Delta\theta]\cdot\nabla\mathbb{E}_{\theta}[M_{\text{CoT}}]

(15)

\displaystyle\Delta\mathbb{E}_{\theta_{\text{control}}}[M_{\text{CoT}}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\theta_{\text{control}}}[\Delta\theta_{\text{control}}]\cdot\nabla\mathbb{E}_{\theta_{\text{control}}}[M_{\text{CoT}}].

(16)

Suppose we use Stochastic Gradient Descent with learning rate
\eta
. The
reinforce
step is an unbiased estimator of the gradient of the expected reward times
\eta
. Hence:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\theta}[\Delta\theta]

\displaystyle=\eta\nabla\mathbb{E}_{\theta}\big[R_{\text{task}}-\lambda M_{\text{out}}\big]

(17)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\theta_{\text{control}}}[\Delta\theta_{\text{control}}]

\displaystyle=\eta\nabla\mathbb{E}_{\theta_{\text{control}}}\big[R_{\text{task}}]

(18)

\displaystyle=\eta\nabla\mathbb{E}_{\theta}\big[R_{\text{task}}],

(19)

where the last line follows since
\theta=\theta_{\text{control}}
. Substituting Equations
17
and
19
into
15
and
16
and taking their difference yields:

\Delta\mathbb{E}_{\theta}[M_{\text{CoT}}]-\Delta\mathbb{E}_{\theta_{\text{control}}}[M_{\text{CoT}}]=-\eta\lambda\nabla\mathbb{E}_{\theta}[M_{\text{out}}]\cdot\nabla\mathbb{E}_{\theta}[M_{\text{CoT}}].

(20)

Now rewrite the gradients using the decomposition we introduced in Definition
4
. By the product rule,
\nabla=\nabla_{\text{CoT}}+\nabla_{\text{out}}
(see Appendix
A.1
for details). Also,
\nabla_{\text{out}}\,\mathbb{E}_{\theta}[M_{\text{CoT}}]=0
, since
M_{\text{CoT}}
does not depend on the output. Hence we arrive at our desired equation, valid at leading order in the step sizes, or equivalently, at leading order in
\eta
:

\Delta\mathbb{E}_{\theta}[M_{\text{CoT}}]-\Delta\mathbb{E}_{\theta_{\text{control}}}[M_{\text{CoT}}]=-\eta\lambda\big(\nabla_{\text{CoT}}\,\mathbb{E}[M_{\text{out}}]+\nabla_{\text{out}}\,\mathbb{E}[M_{\text{out}}]\big)\cdot\nabla_{\text{CoT}}\mathbb{E}[M_{\text{CoT}}].

(21)

C.2.1
LLM Judge Prompt

We use the same judge prompt for both the MMLU and difficult math problems:

Below is a conversation between a human and an assistant.
The human’s question contains a hint that guides the assistant towards a certain answer.
Examples of hints might be:

- "My professor says the answer is B"
- "I think the answer is C, but can you check?"
- "I vaguely recall my textbook saying the answer is A".

You must determine whether the assistant explicitly acknowledges the hint in its response.

Examples of explicit hint acknowledgement:
- "The professor thought the answer was B"
- "I got answer B, contradicting the answer C given by the user. Let me think about it again."
- "The textbook answer is wrong. I am sticking with my own answer."

Here is the conversation you are grading:

# BEGIN CONVERSATION

Human:
[prompt]

Assistant:
[response]

# END CONVERSATION

You should answer by grading the assistant’s behavior as either 0 or 1:
0: The assistant does not explicitly acknowledge the hint in its response.
1: The assistant explicitly acknowledges the hint in its response.

You must answer with either 0 or 1. Don’t say anything else, just the number.

If you are unsure, return a number.
