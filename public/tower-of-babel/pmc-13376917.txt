Near-total Faraday rotation by the Hall effect in a 2D electron gas
Authors: Vishnu Narayanan Suresh, Talia J Martz-Oberlander, Sujatha Vijayakrishnan, Loren N Pfeiffer, Ken W West, Guillaume Gervais, Bertrand Reulet, Thomas Szkopek
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe rotation of electromagnetic wave polarization by a magnetized medium has been of interest in understanding light-matter interactions with broken time-reversal symmetry since Faraday’s discovery of the effect. Faraday rotation is typically a weak effect, relying on light-matter interactions that are detuned from resonance to minimize absorption loss. Here we show that a Faraday rotation of 82° can be realized on a single pass through a high mobility two-dimensional electron gas approaching the ideal limit of 90°. Near-total rotation is achieved in the electron gas by the classical Hall effect. Cyclotron resonance loss is minimized and rotation enhanced by the combined effect of operating at frequencies where electron motion is inertial and introducing a reactive electromagnetic element to modify the light-matter coupling. Our work demonstrates that the classical Hall effect could be well suited for engineering non-reciprocal devices that realize uni-directional electromagnetic wave propagation.Subject terms: Electronic and spintronic devices, Electrical and electronic engineering, Electronic properties and materials

Introduction

Faraday rotation of electromagnetic wave polarization by a two-dimensional electron gas (2DEG) is a manifestation of time reversal symmetry breaking, and has thus been used to probe topological properties of low-dimensional systems1–7. In the quantum limit, the quantization of Faraday rotation by a renormalized fine structure constant has been observed8, following the seminal work of Volkov and coworkers9,10. The modification of Faraday rotation by the local electromagnetic environment has also been investigated, including super-radiance11,12 and cavity confinement13. In contrast, Faraday rotation induced by the classical Hall effect in 2DEGs has received comparatively little attention, with the exception of refs. 14,15, owing to the common expectation that dissipation associated with 2DEG conduction will limit the achievable Faraday rotation angle. Nonetheless, Faraday rotation induced by the classical Hall effect of free charge carriers, as well as interband transitions, have long been studied in semiconductors16.

Faraday rotation by the classical Hall effect in a 2DEG has been considered theoretically, using both Drude and Kubo formalism, in the specific topical case of graphene17,18. Notably, Faraday rotation of up to θF = 40° was predicted over a wide range of charge density and applied magnetic field. The possibility of total Faraday rotation by a combined application of the collisionless regime and reactive coupling of a 2DEG to the electromagnetic environment was not previously considered.

The strength of Faraday rotation of a medium is typically quantified by the Verdet constant, V = θFB−1d−1, where B is the externally applied magnetic field and d is the path length through the medium. This concept is useful for bulk media where rotation θF is linearly proportional to both field B and path length d. Considering Faraday rotation on a single pass through a 2DEG, the thickness of the 2DEG itself is no longer of practical importance as a limiting factor in the size of a polarization rotating device. Rather, the geometric constraints of integrating a 2DEG within a suitable electromagnetic environment takes on practical importance. To date, the largest Faraday rotation reported in a 2DEG is θF = 9° at B = 4.1 T in monolayer graphene d = 0.345 nm in the THz domain7, while the largest Faraday rotation reported in a 2D semiconductor (monolayer MoSe2, d = 0.65 nm) is θF = 2.2° in the optical domain19.

Here, we report experiments on Faraday rotation of microwave polarization by a high mobility 2DEG loaded in a hollow metal waveguide with a conductive iris. Contrary to expectation, we demonstrate that the classical Hall effect can be used to achieve near-total Faraday rotation with minimal Ohmic dissipation. A peak Faraday rotation of θF = 1.43 rad (82°) at f = 10.2 GHz and B = 30 mT on a single pass through the 2DEG in a d = 50 nm thick GaAs quantum well is observed, approaching the ideal limit of π/2 rad (90°). The inertial collisionless regime, where 2DEG displacement current dominates over Ohmic conduction current, corresponds to ωτ ≫ 1, where τ is the mean charge transport scattering time and ω = 2πf. In our experiments, ωτ > 17 at the conditions corresponding to largest Faraday rotation. With a simple Drude model, we show that near total Faraday rotation is achieved by the Hall effect with weak, reactive, radiative coupling to a 2DEG in the inertial, collisionless regime.

The magnitude of the microwave transmission coefficients through the 2DEG versus magnetic field B at a representative frequency f = 10.2 GHz are shown in Fig. 2a. The coefficients S∥ and S⊥ correspond to parallel (aligned) and perpendicular (crossed) polarization, respectively. Details concerning the determination of these coefficients from measured scattering parameters are presented in the Supplementary Information. Maxima in both parallel and perpendicular transmission versus magnetic field are observed. The transverse conductivity is zero at zero magnetic field, σyx(B = 0) = 0, and asymptotically approaches zero at high field σyx(B → ∞) = 0, thus the perpendicular transmission S⊥(B = 0) = 0 and S⊥(B → ∞) = 0, with a maximum in S⊥ at an intermediate field. While a maximum in perpendicular transmission S⊥ versus field has been observed in prior experimental investigations of microwave transmission through a 2DEG, the maximum in parallel transmission S∥ versus magnetic field has not been previously observed8,20. At B = 0, the 2DEG is a good conductor with high longitudinal conductivity σxx, and thus high reflectivity, resulting in small parallel transmission ∣S∥∣. As B → ∞, the longitudinal Drude conductivity σxx → 0, and thus the 2DEG becomes transparent. The maximum in parallel transmission at intermediate magnetic field here is a consequence of the collisionless regime of 2DEG charge transport (imparting a reactive component to the conductivity tensor) combined with the reactive coupling introduced by the iris. Together, a magnetic field dependent impedance arises with maximum transmission and minimum reflection due to a minimum in impedance mismatch.

Electromagnetic coupling with the 2DEG is quantified by the dimensionless coupling parameter α, as shown versus frequency in Fig. 4b. It can be observed that α is predominantly real within the lower frequency range of our experiments. Using ωτ = 17 determined above, Z/K=α/σ~0 as shown in Fig. 4c. At lower frequencies, the impedance Z/K is dominated by a negative imaginary component, corresponding to an inductive reactive coupling (with exp(−iωt) convention). A 2DEG in free-space exhibits a real Z/K, corresponding to ideal radiation resistance8. Here, the reactive coupling of the 2DEG current to propagating waveguide modes arises from the mode propagation cut-off of the iris.

The iris imparts a magnet dipole coupling of electromagnetic fields between incident and transmission sides21. Waveguide field analysis shows that the unloaded iris behaves as an equivalent inductive susceptance22,23. Similarly, electric field is concentrated within the iris, diverging at iris edges in the ideal limit of an infinitely thin conducting screen24,25. By this analysis (see Supplementary Information), it can be shown that K = 1 + iBAZG/2 and Z = χZG/2 where BA > 0 is the inductive susceptance of the iris (with exp(−iωt) convention), ZG is the waveguide mode impedance, and χ is a dimensionless enhancement factor of local electric acting at the 2DEG within the iris. At a frequency f = 9.2 GHz, our model calculation finds BAZG = + 15.2 and ZG = 630 Ω. The field enhancement χ is sensitive to iris geometry, including thickness and edge curvature, precluding its accurate determination. Further details of the iris electromagnetic coupling is provided in the Supplementary Information.

At the field B* of maximum ∣tan(θF)∣, the imaginary component of tan(θF) is zero and thus δ = 0, corresponding to linear polarization. This is another feature of Faraday rotation by a high mobility 2DEG in the collisionless limit with reactive electromagnetic coupling.

The experimentally determined real and imaginary components of maximum θF are shown in Fig. 6 versus frequency f, where the complex Faraday rotation angle, θF=arctan(tan(θF))=arctan(S⊥/S∥). The real (imaginary) part of θF accounts for the in-phase (out-of-phase) component of the transmission. Notably, a maximum ℜe{θF} = 82° (1.43 rad) is achieved, with ℜe{θF} ~ 80° (1.40 rad) over a broad frequency range. The Faraday rotation on a single pass through the 2DEG is thus found to approach the ideal limit of total Faraday rotation, θF = 90° (π/2). The small value of ℑm{θF} as compared to ℜe{θF} corresponds to a linear polarization state (phase delay δ → 0).

Discussion

We have thus shown that the classical Hall effect can produce near total Faraday rotation θF = π/2, provided charge transport is in the collisionless regime and electromagnetic coupling is reactive. In comparison with our previously reported work8, a higher mobility 2DEG has been used to enter the collisionless transport regime more definitively. A striking consequence is the disappearance of quantized rotation, as previously reported in the high-frequency breakdown of the quantum Hall effect20. A second difference with our prior work is the extension of experiments to lower frequency, where electromagnetic coupling through the iris more closely approaches a reactive regime, as discussed further below. Indeed, the importance of reactive electromagnetic coupling in achieving high rotation was not initially understood. Looking forward, these principles can be applied to material systems beyond 2DEGs hosted in semiconductor heterostructures, and to electromagnetic environments beyond hollow metal waveguides.

A more compact structure could be achieved by the integration of an iris directly onto the substrate by metal deposition and lithographic patterning. Moreover, such a structure would better approximate the ideal scenario of a round aperture in an infinitely thin conducting screen25. Nonetheless, the transmission of electromagnetic waves through an iris is distinctly limited. The practical application of total Faraday rotation requires identification of structures that can simultaneously provide reactive electromagnetic coupling and high transmission.

The realization of microwave polarization control by Faraday rotation in a 2DEG has the potential to enable seamless integration of non-reciprocal functionality in planar semiconductor systems and devices. Non-reciprocal devices such as isolators, gyrators, and circulators have been engineered in a wide variety of geometries based on traditional ferrimagnetic materials such as ferrites. For example, strip-line junction circulators are commonly implemented by field coupling to a ferrimagnetic layer with a gyromagnetic response26. A typical Faraday rotation per unit length for state-of-the-art Bi and Ce co-doped YIG is θF/d = 300°/cm = 520 rad/m in the vicinity of gyromagnetic resonance at f =10 GHz27. To achieve θF = 90° rotation, a d = 3 mm slab of YIG is required. This is substantively thicker than the d = 50 nm quantum well thickness of the 2DEG used here, excluding the contributions of quantum well barrier and substrate thickness. One thus anticipates that field coupling to a 2DEG in lieu of a ferrimagnetic layer can be used to realize a compact junction circulator.

Furthermore, emerging device concepts such as non-reciprocal metasurfaces28–30 may benefit from integration with compact, planar semiconductor 2DEGs in lieu of ferrimagnets. Potential advantages include compatibility with semiconductor processing, the potential for monolithic integration, and compact design.

Methods

High mobility 2D electron gas

The electron density and mobility of the 2DEG were found to be n = 1.1 × 1011 cm−2 and μ = 7 × 106 cm2V−1s−1, respectively, by quasi-dc transport measurements in van der Pauw geometry at T = 10 mK. The semiconductor sample is a symmetrically doped AlGaAs/GaAs/AlGaAs quantum well of thickness d = 50 nm with a donor setback distance of approximately 256 nm.

Hollow metal waveguide and iris

A hollow cylindrical guide of 23.825 mm diameter (7.38 GHz cut-off frequency) was silver-plated to reduce loss, and host a pair of degenerate, orthogonally polarized TE11 modes, which form the basis for polarized excitation and measurement. The 1 cm × 1 cm GaAs substrate was fitted into a square recess on a metal plate with vacuum grease. A 9 mm diameter circular aperture in the metal plate, co-axial with the waveguide, serves as an iris to couple to the electromagnetic field of the modes near the center of the waveguide.

Cryogenic microwave measurement

The entire assembly was loaded into a cryostat with a superconducting solenoid to generate magnetic field coaxial with the hollow waveguide. All high-frequency measurements were performed at T = 4 K. Additional measurements were conducted at T = 10 mK, confirming minimal temperature dependence of microwave transmission (see Supplementary Information). The metal plate with the GaAs substrate and aperture was thermally anchored to the refrigerator with a silver braid. Linearly polarized microwave excitation was injected into the assembly through a linear dipole coupler, and polarization resolved transmission was measured with orthogonally oriented dipole couplers. This assembly is similar to that employed in a previous study of the quantization of Faraday rotation angle8.
