Categorical representation of DRC-semigroups


Abstract
DRC-semigroups model associative systems with domain and range operations, and contain many important classes, such as inverse, restriction, Ehresmann, regular ∗ * -, and ∗ * -regular semigroups. In this paper we show that the category of DRC-semigroups is isomorphic to a category of certain biordered categories whose object sets are projection algebras in the sense of Jones. This extends the recent groupoid approach to regular ∗ * -semigroups of the first and third authors. We also establish the existence of free DRC-semigroups by constructing a left adjoint to the forgetful functor into the category of projection algebras. Keywords : DRC-semigroup, biordered category, projection algebra. MSC: 20M50, 20M10, 18B40, 20M05.

Preliminaries on biordered categories

Throughout the paper, we identify a small category
\mathcal{C}
with its morphism set, and identify the objects of
\mathcal{C}
with the identities, the set of which is denoted
v\mathcal{C}
. Thus, we have domain and range maps
{\bf d},{\bf r}:\mathcal{C}\to v\mathcal{C}
, for which the following hold, for all
a,b,c\in\mathcal{C}
:

•

a\circ b
exists if and only if
{\bf r}(a)={\bf d}(b)
, in which case
{\bf d}(a\circ b)={\bf d}(a)
and
{\bf r}(a\circ b)={\bf r}(b)
. (So we are composing morphisms ‘left to right’.)

•

{\bf d}(a)\circ a=a=a\circ{\bf r}(a)
.

•

If
a\circ b
and
b\circ c
exist, then
(a\circ b)\circ c=a\circ(b\circ c)
.

•

{\bf d}(p)={\bf r}(p)=p
for all
p\in v\mathcal{C}
.

A
left-ordered category
is a pair
(\mathcal{C},\leq)
, where
\mathcal{C}
is a small category and
\leq
a partial order on
\mathcal{C}
satisfying the following, for all
a,b,c,d\in\mathcal{C}
:

•

If
a\leq b
, then
{\bf d}(a)\leq{\bf d}(b)
and
{\bf r}(a)\leq{\bf r}(b)
.

•

If
a\leq b
and
c\leq d
, and if
{\bf r}(a)={\bf d}(c)
and
{\bf r}(b)={\bf d}(d)
, then
a\circ c\leq b\circ d
.

•

For any
p\in v\mathcal{C}
with
p\leq{\bf d}(a)
, there exists a unique
u\leq a
with
{\bf d}(u)=p
. This element is denoted
u={}_{p}{\downharpoonleft}a
, and called the
left restriction
of
a
to
p
.

(These are the
\Omega
-structured categories with restrictions
, in the terminology of
[
33
]
.)

Right-ordered categories
are defined analogously, where the third item above is replaced by:

•

For any
q\in v\mathcal{C}
with
q\leq{\bf r}(a)
, there exists a unique
v\leq a
with
{\bf d}(v)=q
. This element is denoted
v=a{\downharpoonright}_{q}
, and called the
right restriction
of
a
to
q
.

A
biordered category
is a triple
(\mathcal{C},\leq_{\ell},\leq_{r})
, where:

•

(\mathcal{C},\leq_{\ell})
is a left-ordered category, and
(\mathcal{C},\leq_{r})
a right-ordered category, and

•

\leq_{\ell}
and
\leq_{r}
restrict to the same order on
v\mathcal{C}
, meaning that
p\leq_{\ell}q\ \Leftrightarrow\ p\leq_{r}q
for all
p,q\in v\mathcal{C}
.

(The Ehresmann categories considered in
[
33
]
are certainly biordered categories in the above sense.)
Unless there is a chance of confusion, we typically speak of ‘a biordered category
\mathcal{C}
’, and assume the orders are named
\leq_{\ell}
and
\leq_{r}
. It is important to note that left and right restrictions in a biordered category are quite independent of each other. For example, if
{\bf r}({}_{p}{\downharpoonleft}a)=q
, then we need not have
{}_{p}{\downharpoonleft}a=a{\downharpoonright}_{q}
; in fact,
a{\downharpoonright}_{q}
need not even have domain
p
. (Some concrete examples are discussed in Section
9
.)

We write
{\bf BC}
for the (large) category of biordered categories. Morphisms in
{\bf BC}
are the
biordered morphisms
. Such a morphism
(\mathcal{C},\leq_{\ell},\leq_{r})\to(\mathcal{C}^{\prime},\leq_{\ell}^{\prime},\leq_{r}^{\prime})
is a functor
\phi:\mathcal{C}\to\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
preserving both orders, in the sense that

a\leq_{\ell}b\ \Rightarrow\ a\phi\leq_{\ell}^{\prime}b\phi\and a\leq_{r}b\ \Rightarrow\ a\phi\leq_{r}^{\prime}b\phi\hskip 20.00003pt\text{for all $a,b\in\mathcal{C}$.}

(2.1)

Although a biordered category need not be particularly symmetric in its own right, there is a certain symmetry/duality in the category
{\bf BC}
itself. Specifically, we have an isomorphism
{\bf O}:{\bf BC}\to{\bf BC}
, given at the object level by
{\bf O}(\mathcal{C},\leq_{l},\leq_{r})=(\mathcal{C}^{\operatorname{op}},\leq_{r},\leq_{l})
. Here
\mathcal{C}^{\operatorname{op}}
denotes the
opposite category
to
\mathcal{C}
, in which domains and codomains are swapped, and composition is reversed. (At the morphism level we simply have
{\bf O}(\phi)=\phi
.)

Throughout the paper, we will typically construct biordered categories by first defining an ordering on objects, and then defining suitable restrictions, as formalised in the next result. We omit the proof, as it is directly analogous to that of
[
13
, Lemma 2.3]
.

Lemma 2.2
.

Suppose
\mathcal{C}
is a small category for which the following two conditions hold:

(i)

There is a partial order
\leq
on the object set
v\mathcal{C}
.

(ii)

For all

\begin{array}[]{ccccc}a\leq_{\ell}b&\qquad\Leftrightarrow&\qquad a={}_{p}{\downharpoonleft}b\ \ \text{for some $p\leq{\bf d}(b)$}&\qquad\Leftrightarrow&\qquad a={}_{{\bf d}(a)}{\downharpoonleft}b,\\[5.69054pt]

(2.3)

Moreover, any biordered category has the above form. ∎

For biordered categories
\mathcal{C}
and

, it is easy to check that a functor

is a biordered morphism, i.e. satisfies (

), if and only if it satisfies

({}_{p}{\downharpoonleft}a)\phi={}_{p\phi}{\downharpoonleft}a\phi\and(a{\downharpoonright}_{q})\phi=a\phi{\downharpoonright}_{q\phi}\hskip 20.00003pt\text{for all $a\in\mathcal{C}$, and all $p\leq{\bf d}(a)$ and $q\leq{\bf r}(a)$.}

(2.4)

Here, and henceforth, we denote by
\leq
the common restriction of
\leq_{\ell}
and
\leq_{r}
to

.

Consider a biordered category
\mathcal{C}
, and let

. We then have two maps

\vartheta_{a}:{\bf d}(a)^{\downarrow}\to{\bf r}(a)^{\downarrow}\and\partial_{a}:{\bf r}(a)^{\downarrow}\to{\bf d}(a)^{\downarrow},

(2.5)

Here for a partially ordered set
(P,\leq)
, we write
t^{\downarrow}=\{s\in P:s\leq t\}
for the

of

. It follows quickly from

(2.6)

A
v
-congruence

\mathcal{C}

on
\mathcal{C}
satisfying the following, for all

3
DRC-semigroups

We now come to the main object of our study, the class of DRC-semigroups. We begin in Section
3.1
by recalling the definitions, and listing some basic properties; while many of these preliminary lemmas exist in the literature (see for example
[
30
,
57
,
50
]
), we give brief proofs to keep the paper self-contained. In Section
3.2
we show how to construct a biordered category from a DRC-semigroup, leading to a functor
{\bf DRC}\to{\bf BC}
.

3.1
Definitions and basic properties

Definition 3.1
.

A
DRC-semigroup
is an algebra
(S,\cdot,D,R)
, where
(S,\cdot)
is a semigroup, and where
D
and
R
are unary operations
S\to S
satisfying the following laws, for all
a,b\in S
:

(DRC1)

D(a)a=a
,

(DRC2)

D(ab)=D(aD(b))
,

(DRC3)

D(ab)=D(a)D(ab)D(a)
,

(DRC4)

R(D(a))=D(a)
,

aR(a)=a
,

R(ab)=R(R(a)b)
,

R(ab)=R(b)R(ab)R(b)
,

D(R(a))=R(a)
.

Throughout the article, we will almost always identify an algebra with its underlying set, including for a DRC-semigroup
S\equiv(S,\cdot,D,R)
. Thus, we typically speak of ‘a DRC-semigroup
S
’, write multiplication as juxtaposition, and assume the unary operations are denoted
D
and
R
.

We denote by
{\bf DRC}
the category of all DRC-semigroups, with DRC-morphisms. Such a morphism
(S,\cdot,D,R)\to(S^{\prime},\cdot^{\prime},D^{\prime},R^{\prime})
in
{\bf DRC}
is a function
\phi:S\to S^{\prime}
preserving all the operations, meaning that

(a\cdot b)\phi=(a\phi)\cdot^{\prime}(b\phi),\hskip 20.00003ptD(a)\phi=D^{\prime}(a\phi)\and R(a)\phi=R^{\prime}(a\phi)\hskip 20.00003pt\text{for all $a,b\in S$.}

As noted in the introduction, Lawson first introduced DRC-semigroups in
[
33
]
, where he referred to them as
reduced
U
-semiabundant semigroups satisfying the congruence conditions
. (The laws in axiom
(DRC2)
are typically called the
congruence conditions
as, for example, the identity
D(ab)=D(aD(b))
is equivalent, in the presence of the other laws, to the relation
\{(a,b)\in S\times S:D(a)=D(b)\}
being a left congruence on
S
.) Lawson was mainly interested in Ehresmann semigroups, which additionally satisfy the law
{D(a)D(b)=D(b)D(a)}
, and hence also
R(a)R(b)=R(b)R(a)
, which makes
(DRC3)
redundant in the presence of the other laws.

As with
{\bf BC}
, the category
{\bf DRC}
posesses a natural symmetry/duality. Specifically, if
(S,\cdot,D,R)
is a DRC-semigroup, and if
(S,\star)
is the
opposite semigroup
of
S
, where
a\star b=b\cdot a
for
a,b\in S
, then
(S,\star,R,D)
is a DRC-semigroup. Thus, any equality that holds in
(S,\cdot,D,R)
can be converted to a dual equality by reversing the order of all products, and interchanging
D
and
R
.

Remark 3.2
.

Some authors use superscripts for the unary operations on a DRC-semigroup. For example, Jones
[
30
]
and Wang
[
57
]
write
a^{+}=D(a)
and
a^{*}=R(a)
. Here we prefer the functional notation, as it emphasises the connection with domains and ranges in the corresponding categories.

Note that
(DRC4)
says
RD=D
and
DR=R
. It follows from these that

D=RD=(DR)D=D(RD)=DD,\hskip 20.00003pt\text{and similarly}\hskip 20.00003ptR=RR.

That is, we have the following consequence of
(DRC1)
–
(DRC4)
:

(DRC5)

D(D(a))=D(a)
,

R(R(a))=R(a)
.

In calculations to follow, we use
=_{1}
to indicate equality in a DRC-semigroup by one or more applications of
(DRC1)
, and similarly for
(DRC2)
–
(DRC5)
.

It follows from
(DRC4)
that
\operatorname{im}(D)=\operatorname{im}(R)
. We denote this common image by
{\bf P}(S)
, so

{\bf P}(S)=\{D(a):a\in S\}=\{R(a):a\in S\}.

The elements of
{\bf P}(S)
are called
projections
. We will typically use the next result without explicit reference.

Lemma 3.3

(cf.
[
50
, Proposition 1.2]
)
.

We have
{\bf P}(S)=\{p\in S:p^{2}=p=D(p)=R(p)\}
.

Proof.

Certainly every element of the stated set belongs to
\operatorname{im}(D)={\bf P}(S)
. Conversely, let
p\in{\bf P}(S)
, so that
p=D(a)
for some
a\in S
. Then
p=D(a)=_{5}D(D(a))=D(p)
, and similarly
p=R(p)
. From
p=D(p)
, it also follows that
p^{2}=D(p)p=_{1}p
.
∎

Since every projection is an idempotent, it follows that
(DRC3)
is equivalent (in the presence of the other axioms) to:

(DRC3)
′

D(ab)=D(a)D(ab)=D(ab)D(a)
,

R(ab)=R(b)R(ab)=R(ab)R(b)
.

Lemma 3.4
.

For any
a\in S
and
p\in{\bf P}(S)
we have

D(pa)=pD(pa)=D(pa)p\and R(ap)=pR(ap)=R(ap)p.

Proof.

The first follows from
(DRC3)′
and
p=D(p)
; the second is dual.
∎

Since projections are idempotents, the natural partial order on idempotents restricts to a partial order on
{\bf P}(S)
:

p\leq q\hskip 10.00002pt\Leftrightarrow\hskip 10.00002ptp=qpq\hskip 10.00002pt\Leftrightarrow\hskip 10.00002ptp=pq=qp\hskip 20.00003pt\text{for $p,q\in{\bf P}(S)$.}

(3.5)

The next result shows that this is equivalent to either of
p=pq
or
p=qp
. This is referred to as the
reduced
property in the literature
[
50
]
.

Corollary 3.6
.

For
p,q\in{\bf P}(S)
, we have
p\leq q\ \Leftrightarrow\ p=pq\ \Leftrightarrow\ p=qp
.

Proof.

In light of (
3.5
), and by symmetry, it suffices to check that
p=pq\ \Rightarrow\ p=qp
. But if
p=pq
, then using Lemma
3.4
we have
p=R(p)=R(pq)=qR(pq)=qR(p)=qp
.
∎

Remark 3.7
.

An equivalent definition of DRC-semigroups stems from this order-theoretic perspective. Thus, a DR-semigroup is defined in
[
50
]
to be a semigroup
S
in which a (sub)set of idempotents
P
is distinguished such that for each
a\in S
there exist smallest
p,q\in P
under the natural partial order for which
a=pa=aq
, called
p=D(a)
and
q=R(a)
. This defines unary operations
D
and
R
on such
S
(both with image
P
), and it follows that DRC-semigroups are precisely the DR-semigroups satisfying the congruence conditions
(DRC2)
.

The set
{\bf P}(S)
can be given the structure of a
projection algebra
, as defined by Jones in
[
30
]
. We will recall the formal definition in Section
4
, but the next result will be used to verify the axioms.
For
p\in{\bf P}(S)
we define the functions
\theta_{p},\delta_{p}:{\bf P}(S)\to{\bf P}(S)
by

q\theta_{p}=R(qp)\and q\delta_{p}=D(pq)\hskip 20.00003pt\text{for $q\in{\bf P}(S)$.}

Lemma 3.8

(cf.
[
30
, Proposition 7.2]
)
.

For any
p,q,\in{\bf P}(S)
, we have

(i)

p\theta_{p}=p
,
p\theta_{q\theta_{p}}=q\theta_{p}
,
\theta_{q}\theta_{q\theta_{p}}=\theta_{q}\theta_{p}
,
\theta_{p}\delta_{p}=\theta_{p}
,
\theta_{p\delta_{q}}\theta_{p}=\theta_{q}\theta_{p}
,

(ii)

p\delta_{p}=p
,
p\delta_{q\delta_{p}}=q\delta_{p}
,
\delta_{q}\delta_{q\delta_{p}}=\delta_{q}\delta_{p}
,
\delta_{p}\theta_{p}=\delta_{p}
,
\delta_{p\theta_{q}}\delta_{p}=\delta_{q}\delta_{p}
.

Proof.

We just prove
(i)
, as
(ii)
is dual. In what follows, we write
=_{*}
to indicate an application of Lemma
3.4
(and continue to use
=_{1}
,
=_{2}
, etc.). For the first item we have
p\theta_{p}=R(pp)=R(p)=p
, and for the second,

p\theta_{q\theta_{p}}=R(pR(qp))=_{*}R(R(qp))=_{5}R(qp)=q\theta_{p}.

For the remaining three items, fix
t\in P
, and first note that
t\theta_{q}\theta_{p}=R(R(tq)p)=_{2}R(tqp)
. The third and fifth items follow by combining this with

t\theta_{q}\theta_{q\theta_{p}}=R(R(tq)R(qp))=_{2}R(tqR(qp))=_{*}R(tqpR(qp))=_{1}R(tqp)

and

t\theta_{p\delta_{q}}\theta_{p}=R(R(tD(qp))p)=_{2}R(tD(qp)p)=_{*}R(tD(qp)qp)=_{1}R(tqp).

For the fourth we have
t\theta_{p}\delta_{p}=D(pR(tp))=_{*}D(R(tp))=_{4}R(tp)=t\theta_{p}
.
∎

3.2
From DRC-semigroups to biordered categories

We now wish to show that a DRC-semigroup
S
naturally induces a biordered category
\mathcal{C}(S)
with morphism set
S
, and object/identity set
{\bf P}(S)
. For this we need the following.

Lemma 3.9
.

If
S
is a DRC-semigroup, and if
a,b\in S
are such that
R(a)=D(b)
, then
D(ab)=D(a)
and
R(ab)=R(b)
.

Proof.

We have
D(ab)=_{2}D(aD(b))=D(aR(a))=_{1}D(a)
; the other is dual.
∎

As a consequence, we can make the following definition.

Definition 3.10
.

Given a DRC-semigroup
S
, we define the (small) category
\mathcal{C}=\mathcal{C}(S)
as follows:

•

The underlying (morphism) set of
\mathcal{C}
is
S
.

•

The object/identity set is
v\mathcal{C}=P={\bf P}(S)
.

•

For
a\in\mathcal{C}
we have
{\bf d}(a)=D(a)
and
{\bf r}(a)=R(a)
.

•

For
a,b\in\mathcal{C}
with
{\bf r}(a)={\bf d}(b)
we have
a\circ b=ab
.

For
a\in\mathcal{C}
, and for
p\leq{\bf d}(a)
and
q\leq{\bf r}(a)
we define
{}_{p}{\downharpoonleft}a=pa
and
a{\downharpoonright}_{q}=aq
. (Here
\leq
is the partial order on
P
in (
3.5
).)

Proposition 3.11
.

For any DRC-semigroup
S
, the category
\mathcal{C}=\mathcal{C}(S)
is biordered, with
\leq_{\ell}
and
\leq_{r}
as in (
2.3
).

Proof.

We just need to check conditions
(ii)(O1)
–
(ii)(O4)
from Lemma
2.2
. By symmetry, we just need to check the properties of left restrictions.

(ii)(O1)
. For
p\leq{\bf d}(a)=D(a)
we have
p=pD(a)
, and then

{\bf d}({}_{p}{\downharpoonleft}a)=D(pa)=_{2}D(pD(a))=D(p)=p.

We also have
R(pa)=_{3}R(a)R(pa)R(a)
, which says that
{\bf r}({}_{p}{\downharpoonleft}a)=R(pa)\leq R(a)={\bf r}(a)
.

(ii)(O2)
. We have
{}_{{\bf d}(a)}{\downharpoonleft}a=D(a)a=_{1}a
.

(ii)(O3)
. From
p\leq q
we have
p=pq
, and so
{}_{p}{\downharpoonleft}{}_{q}{\downharpoonleft}a=p(qa)=pa={}_{p}{\downharpoonleft}a
.

(ii)(O4)
. Here we have
q={\bf r}({}_{p}{\downharpoonleft}a)=R(pa)
, and also
q={\bf d}({}_{q}{\downharpoonleft}b)={\bf d}(qb)
, so

{}_{p}{\downharpoonleft}(a\circ b)=pab=_{1}paR(pa)b=paqb=pa\circ qb={}_{p}{\downharpoonleft}a\circ{}_{q}{\downharpoonleft}b.\qed

Remark 3.12
.

Note that our orders
\leq_{\ell}
and
\leq_{r}
were denoted
\leq_{r}
and
\leq_{\ell}
(the other way round) by Lawson in the context of Ehresmann semigroups
[
33
]
. We have chosen the current terminology, as our
\leq_{\ell}
is contained in Green’s
\leq_{\mathrel{\mathscr{L}}}
preorder, which is defined by

. An analogous statement applies to

and
\leq_{\mathrel{\mathscr{R}}}
. These containments are strict in general. For example, if
M
is a DRC-monoid with identity

, then every element is
\leq_{\mathrel{\mathscr{L}}}
and
\leq_{\mathrel{\mathscr{R}}}
below

, but only the projections are
\leq_{\ell}
or
\leq_{r}
below
1
.

The construction of
\mathcal{C}(S)
from
S
is an object map from
{\bf DRC}
to
{\bf BC}
. For a DRC-morphism
\phi
, we define
\mathcal{C}(\phi)=\phi
. (Since the underlying set of
\mathcal{C}(S)
is just
S
, this is well defined.)

Proposition 3.13
.

\mathcal{C}
is a functor
{\bf DRC}\to{\bf BC}
.

Proof.

It remains to check that any DRC-morphism
\phi:S\to S^{\prime}
is a biordered morphism
\mathcal{C}\to\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
, where
\mathcal{C}=\mathcal{C}(S)
and
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}=\mathcal{C}(S^{\prime})
. This amounts to checking (cf. (
2.4
)) that

(i)

(a\circ b)\phi=(a\phi)\circ^{\prime}(b\phi)
for all
a,b\in\mathcal{C}
with
{\bf r}(a)={\bf d}(b)
,

(ii)

({}_{p}{\downharpoonleft}a)\phi={}_{p\phi}{\downharpoonleft}a\phi
for all
a\in\mathcal{C}
and
p\leq{\bf d}(a)
, and

(iii)

(a{\downharpoonright}_{q})\phi=a\phi{\downharpoonright}_{q\phi}
for all
a\in\mathcal{C}
and
q\leq{\bf r}(a)
.

Here, we use dashes to distinguish the parameters and operations associated to
\mathcal{C}
and
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
. We will, however, denote multiplication in both
S
and
S^{\prime}
by juxtaposition. Next we note that for any
a\in\mathcal{C}
,

{\bf d}^{\prime}(a\phi)=D^{\prime}(a\phi)=D(a)\phi={\bf d}(a)\phi,\hskip 20.00003pt\text{and similarly}\hskip 20.00003pt{\bf r}^{\prime}(a\phi)={\bf r}(a)\phi.

(i)
. If
{\bf r}(a)={\bf d}(b)
, then
{\bf r}^{\prime}(a\phi)={\bf r}(a)\phi={\bf d}(b)\phi={\bf d}^{\prime}(b\phi)
, and so

(a\circ b)\phi=(ab)\phi=(a\phi)(b\phi)=(a\phi)\circ^{\prime}(b\phi).

(ii)
. If
p\leq q
, where for simplicity we write

, then
p=pq
, and so
p\phi=(p\phi)(q\phi)
. This gives
p\phi\leq q\phi={\bf d}^{\prime}(a\phi)
, and it follows that
({}_{p}{\downharpoonleft}a)\phi=(pa)\phi=(p\phi)(a\phi)={}_{p\phi}{\upharpoonleft}a\phi
.

(iii)
. This is dual to
(ii)
.
∎

4
Projection algebras

The set
{\bf P}(S)
of projections of a DRC-semigroup
S
need not be a subsemigroup of
S
. Nevertheless, it can be given the structure of a
projection algebra
, as axiomatised by Jones
[
30
]
. These algebras will play a crucial role in all that follows, and here we gather the information we need. In Section
4.1
we recall Jones’ definition, albeit in an alternative format, and prove some preliminary results (some of which can again be found in the existing literature). In Section
4.2
we consider the category
{\bf PA}
of projection algebras, and show how the construction of
{\bf P}(S)
from a DRC-semigroup
S
leads to a functor
{\bf DRC}\to{\bf PA}
. In Section
4.3
we show how a projection algebra
P
induces two biordered categories, the
path category

\mathscr{P}(P)
and the
chain category

\mathscr{C}(P)
. These will be crucial ingredients in constructions of subsequent sections.

4.1
Definitions and basic properties

Definition 4.1
.

A
projection algebra
is an algebra
(P,\theta,\delta)
, where
\theta=\{\theta_{p}:p\in P\}
and
\delta=\{\delta_{p}:p\in P\}
are families of unary operations satisfying the following laws, for all
p,q\in P
:

(P1)

p\theta_{p}=p
,

(P2)

p\theta_{q\theta_{p}}=q\theta_{p}
,

(P3)

\theta_{q}\theta_{q\theta_{p}}=\theta_{q}\theta_{p}
,

(P4)

\theta_{p}\delta_{p}=\theta_{p}
,

(P5)

\theta_{p\delta_{q}}\theta_{p}=\theta_{q}\theta_{p}
,

p\delta_{p}=p
,

p\delta_{q\delta_{p}}=q\delta_{p}
,

\delta_{q}\delta_{q\delta_{p}}=\delta_{q}\delta_{p}
,

\delta_{p}\theta_{p}=\delta_{p}
,

\delta_{p\theta_{q}}\delta_{p}=\delta_{q}\delta_{p}
.

The elements of a projection algebra are called
projections
. We typically speak of ‘a projection algebra
P
’, and assume that its operations are denoted
\theta
and
\delta
.

Again there is a natural symmetry/duality, as whenever
(P,\theta,\delta)
is a projection algebra, so too is
(P,\delta,\theta)
. As usual, this allows us to shorten proofs, by omitting the proof of any statement whose dual has already been proved. This will often be done without explicit comment.

Remark 4.2
.

Jones’ original formulation of projection algebras in
[
30
]
utilised two
binary
operations, denoted
\times
and
\star
, which are related to our unary operations by:

p\times q=q\delta_{p}\and p\star q=p\theta_{q}\hskip 20.00003pt\text{for $p,q\in P$.}

Jones’ axioms (with his labelling) are as follows:

(LP1)

e\times e=e
,

(LP2)

(e\times f)\times e=e\times f
,

(LP3)

g\times(f\times e)=(g\times f)\times(f\times e)
,

(LP4)

(g\times f)\times e=(g\times f)\times(g\times e)
,

(RP1)

e\star e=e
,

(RP2)

e\star(f\star e)=f\star e
,

(RP3)

(e\star f)\star g=(e\star f)\star(f\star g)
,

(RP4)

e\star(f\star g)=(e\star g)\star(f\star g)
,

(PA1)

(f\times e)\star f=f\times e
and
f\times(e\star f)=e\star f
,

(PA2)

(e\star(f\times g))\star g=(e\star f)\star g
and
g\times((g\star f)\times e)=g\times(f\times e)
.

Our axioms
(P1)
–
(P3)
correspond to Jones’
(LP1)
–
(LP3)
and
(RP1)
–
(RP3)
, and our
(P4)
–
(P5)
are Jones’
(PA1)
–
(PA2)
.
For example, directly translating
(RP3)
to the unary setting yields
e\theta_{f}\theta_{g}=e\theta_{f}\theta_{f\theta_{g}}
, which is equivalent to the equality of maps,
\theta_{f}\theta_{g}=\theta_{f}\theta_{f\theta_{g}}
(requiring one less parameter), the first part of our
(P3)
.

Jones’
(LP4)
and
(RP4)
are absent from our list, as they are consequences of the other axioms; see Lemma
4.5
and Remark
4.6
. The reason Jones included those axioms is that
(LP1)
–
(LP4)
are axioms for ‘left projection algebras’, which are used to model projections of ‘DC-semigroups’, and
(LP4)
cannot be deduced from
(LP1)
–
(LP3)
, as can be shown for example with Mace4
[
40
]
. Similar comments apply to
(RP1)
–
(RP4)
.

One reason we have opted for the unary approach is due to associativity of function composition, meaning that bracketing becomes unnecessary for complex projection algebra terms. Another is to emphasise the connection with certain natural mappings
\vartheta_{a}
,
\Theta_{a}
,
\partial_{a}
and
\Delta_{a}
on associated categories, which will be introduced below. For a more detailed comparison of the binary and unary approaches, we refer to
[
13
, Remark 4.2]
.

Here then is the connection between DRC-semigroups and projection algebras. The following is well defined because of Lemma
3.8
.

Definition 4.3

(cf.
[
30
, Proposition 7.2]
)
.

The projection algebra of a DRC-semigroup
S
has

•

underlying set
{\bf P}(S)=\operatorname{im}(D)=\operatorname{im}(R)=\{p\in S:p^{2}=p=D(p)=R(p)\}
, and

•

\theta
and
\delta
operations defined by

q\theta_{p}=R(qp)\and q\delta_{p}=D(pq)\hskip 20.00003pt\text{for $p,q\in{\bf P}(S)$.}

For the rest of this section, we fix a projection algebra
P
. In calculations to follow, we use
=_{1}
to indicate equality by one or more applications of
(P1)
, and similarly for the other axioms.

Lemma 4.4
.

For any
p,q\in P
we have

p=p\theta_{q}\ \Rightarrow\ p=q\theta_{p}\and p=p\delta_{q}\ \Rightarrow\ p=q\delta_{p}.

Proof.

For the first implication (the second is dual), suppose
p=p\theta_{q}
. Then

q\theta_{p}=_{2}p\theta_{q\theta_{p}}=p\theta_{q}\theta_{q\theta_{p}}=_{3}p\theta_{q}\theta_{p}=p\theta_{p}=_{1}p.\qed

The next lemma gathers some fundamental consequences of
(P1)
–
(P5)
, which will be used so frequently as to warrant naming them
(P6)
–
(P10)
.

Lemma 4.5
.

For any
p,q\in P
we have

(P6)

\theta_{p}\theta_{p}=\theta_{p}
,

(P7)

\theta_{q\theta_{p}}=\theta_{p}\theta_{q\theta_{p}}=\theta_{q\theta_{p}}\theta_{p}
,

(P8)

q\theta_{q\theta_{p}}=q\theta_{p}
,

(P9)

p\delta_{q}\theta_{p}=q\theta_{p}
,

(P10)

\delta_{q}\theta_{q\theta_{p}}=\delta_{q}\theta_{p}
,

\delta_{p}\delta_{p}=\delta_{p}
,

\delta_{q\delta_{p}}=\delta_{p}\delta_{q\delta_{p}}=\delta_{q\delta_{p}}\delta_{p}
,

q\delta_{q\delta_{p}}=q\delta_{p}
,

p\theta_{q}\delta_{p}=q\delta_{p}
,

\theta_{q}\delta_{q\delta_{p}}=\theta_{q}\delta_{p}
.

Proof.

(P6)
. We have
\theta_{p}=_{4}\theta_{p}\delta_{p}=_{4}\theta_{p}\delta_{p}\theta_{p}=_{4}\theta_{p}\theta_{p}
.

(P7)
. Using the now-established
(P6)
, we have

\theta_{p}\theta_{q\theta_{p}}=_{5}\theta_{q\theta_{p}\delta_{p}}\theta_{q\theta_{p}}=_{4}\theta_{q\theta_{p}}\theta_{q\theta_{p}}=_{6}\theta_{q\theta_{p}}\and\theta_{q\theta_{p}}\theta_{p}=_{3}\theta_{q\theta_{p}}\theta_{q\theta_{p}\theta_{p}}=_{6}\theta_{q\theta_{p}}\theta_{q\theta_{p}}=_{6}\theta_{q\theta_{p}}.

(P8)
. We have
q\theta_{q\theta_{p}}=_{1}q\theta_{q}\theta_{q\theta_{p}}=_{3}q\theta_{q}\theta_{p}=_{1}q\theta_{p}
.

(P9)
. Since
p\delta_{q}=_{4}(p\delta_{q})\theta_{q}
, it follows by Lemma
4.4
that
p\delta_{q}=q\theta_{p\delta_{q}}
, and then

p\delta_{q}\theta_{p}=q\theta_{p\delta_{q}}\theta_{p}=_{5}q\theta_{q}\theta_{p}=_{1}q\theta_{p}.

(P10)
. We have
\delta_{q}\theta_{q\theta_{p}}=_{4}\delta_{q}\theta_{q}\theta_{q\theta_{p}}=_{3}\delta_{q}\theta_{q}\theta_{p}=_{4}\delta_{q}\theta_{p}
.
∎

Remark 4.6
.

The
\theta_{q\theta_{p}}=\theta_{p}\theta_{q\theta_{p}}
and
\delta_{q\delta_{p}}=\delta_{p}\delta_{q\delta_{p}}
parts of

are Jones’ axioms
(RP4)
and
(LP4)
; cf. Remark
4.2
.

Lemma 4.7
.

For
p,q\in P
, the following are equivalent:

(i)

p=p\theta_{q}
,

(ii)

p=r\theta_{q}
for some
r\in P
, i.e.
p\in\operatorname{im}(\theta_{q})
,

(iii)

p=p\delta_{q}
,

(iv)

p=r\delta_{q}
for some
r\in P
, i.e.
p\in\operatorname{im}(\delta_{q})
,

(v)

p=p\theta_{q}=p\delta_{q}=q\theta_{p}=q\delta_{p}
.

Proof.

The following implications are clear:

\ref{leqP5}\ \Rightarrow\ \ref{leqP1}\ \Rightarrow\ \ref{leqP2}\and\ref{leqP5}\ \Rightarrow\ \ref{leqP3}\ \Rightarrow\ \ref{leqP4}.

We will show that
\ref{leqP2}\ \Rightarrow\ \ref{leqP1}\ \Rightarrow\ \ref{leqP5}
, and then
\ref{leqP4}\ \Rightarrow\ \ref{leqP3}\ \Rightarrow\ \ref{leqP5}
will follow by duality.

\ref{leqP2}\ \Rightarrow\ \ref{leqP1}
. If
p=r\theta_{q}
for some
r\in P
, then
p=r\theta_{q}=_{6}r\theta_{q}\theta_{q}=p\theta_{q}
.

\ref{leqP1}\ \Rightarrow\ \ref{leqP5}
. If
p=p\theta_{q}
, then
p=p\theta_{q}=_{4}p\theta_{q}\delta_{q}=p\delta_{q}
. Lemma
4.4
then gives
p=q\theta_{p}=q\delta_{p}
.
∎

Definition 4.8
.

For
p,q\in P
we say that

if any (and hence all) of conditions

–
(v)
of Lemma

hold. That is,

p\leq q\hskip 10.00002pt\Leftrightarrow\hskip 10.00002ptp\in\operatorname{im}(\theta_{q})\hskip 10.00002pt\Leftrightarrow\hskip 10.00002ptp\in\operatorname{im}(\delta_{q})\hskip 10.00002pt\Leftrightarrow\hskip 10.00002ptp=p\theta_{q}=p\delta_{q}=q\theta_{p}=q\delta_{p}.

(4.9)

Proposition 4.10

(cf.
[
30
, Lemma 5.2]
)
.

\leq
is a partial order.

Proof.

Reflexivity follows from
(P1)
. For anti-symmetry, suppose
p\leq q
and
q\leq p
, so that
p=p\theta_{q}
and
q=q\theta_{p}
. Then
p=p\theta_{q}=_{2}q\theta_{p\theta_{q}}=q\theta_{p}=q
. For transitivity, suppose
p\leq q
and
q\leq r
, so that
p=p\theta_{q}
and
q=q\theta_{r}
. Then
p=p\theta_{q}=_{6}p\theta_{q}\theta_{q}=p\theta_{q}\theta_{q\theta_{r}}=_{3}p\theta_{q}\theta_{r}=p\theta_{r}
, which gives
p\leq r
.
∎

It follows from (
4.9
) that

\operatorname{im}(\theta_{p})=\operatorname{im}(\delta_{p})=p^{\downarrow}=\{q\in P:q\leq p\}\hskip 20.00003pt\text{for all $p\in P$.}

The next basic result will be used several times.

Lemma 4.11
.

If
p\leq q
, then
\theta_{p}=\theta_{p}\theta_{q}=\theta_{q}\theta_{p}
and
\delta_{p}=\delta_{p}\delta_{q}=\delta_{q}\delta_{p}

If

∎

In addition to the partial order
\leq

Definition 4.12
.

We define the relation
\mathrel{\mathscr{F}}
on a projection algebra

Note that

In the next sequence of results we gather some basic facts about the relation
\mathrel{\mathscr{F}}

Lemma 4.13
.

Let
p,q\in P
, and put
p^{\prime}=q\delta_{p}
and

. Then:

(i)

,

(ii)

\delta_{q}\delta_{p}=\delta_{q^{\prime}}\delta_{p^{\prime}}

p^{\prime}=p
,

(iv)

Proof.

(i)
. We obtain

and

by definition. We also have

We obtain
p^{\prime}\theta_{q^{\prime}}=q^{\prime}
by symmetry, and hence
p^{\prime}\mathrel{\mathscr{F}}q^{\prime}
.

(ii)
. We have
\theta_{p}\theta_{q}=_{3}\theta_{p}\theta_{p\theta_{q}}=_{5}\theta_{p\theta_{q}\delta_{p}}\theta_{p\theta_{q}}=_{9}\theta_{q\delta_{p}}\theta_{p\theta_{q}}=\theta_{p^{\prime}}\theta_{q^{\prime}}

(iii)

Corollary 4.14
.

If

, then for any

and

For the first (the second is dual), part

Lemma 4.15
.

If

For the first (the second is dual), we have

Corollary 4.16

For

. ∎

4.2
The category of projection algebras

Definition 4.18
.

We write
{\bf PA}
for the category of projection algebras. A
projection algebra morphism

{(P,\theta,\delta)\to(P^{\prime},\theta^{\prime},\delta^{\prime})}
in
{\bf PA}
is a map
\phi:P\to P^{\prime}
such that

(q\theta_{p})\phi=(q\phi)\theta^{\prime}_{p\phi}\and(q\delta_{p})\phi=(q\phi)\delta^{\prime}_{p\phi}\hskip 20.00003pt\text{for all $p,q\in P$.}

In Definition
4.3
(cf. Lemma
3.8
) we saw that a DRC-semigroup

gives rise to a projection algebra

. In this way,
{\bf P}
can be thought of as an object map
{\bf DRC}\to{\bf PA}
. Since a DRC-morphism
\phi:S\to S^{\prime}
maps projections to projections (by the law
D(a)\phi=D^{\prime}(a\phi)
), it follows that we can define

{\bf P}(\phi)=\phi|_{{\bf P}(S)}:{\bf P}(S)\to{\bf P}(S^{\prime})

to be the (set-theoretic) restriction of
\phi
to
{\bf P}(S)
.

Proposition 4.19
.

{\bf P}
is a functor
{\bf DRC}\to{\bf PA}
.

Proof.

It only remains to show that

is a projection algebra morphism for any DRC-morphism

. But for any

we have

(q\theta_{p})\phi=R(qp)\phi=R^{\prime}((qp)\phi)=R^{\prime}((q\phi)(p\phi))=(q\phi)\theta^{\prime}_{p\phi},\hskip 20.00003pt\text{and similarly}\hskip 20.00003pt(q\delta_{p})\phi=(q\phi)\delta^{\prime}_{p\phi}.\qed

.

Let

\displaystyle=(q_{1},\ldots,q_{k}),

Lemma 4.23
.

If

5
Chained projection categories

In Proposition
3.11
we saw that a DRC-semigroup
S
gives rise to a biordered category
\mathcal{C}(S)
whose object set is the projection algebra
{\bf P}(S)
. It turns out that the category
\mathcal{C}(S)
carries rather a lot more information, and has the structure of what we will call a
chained projection category
. In this section we introduce this class of categories, and the category
{\bf CPC}
formed by them. We do so sequentially, by first defining weak projection categories (Section
5.1
), projection categories (Section
5.2
), weak chained projection categories (Section
5.3
), and finally chained projection categories (Section
5.4
). At each step of the way we will show that a DRC-semigroup gives rise to a structure of the relevant kind, ultimately leading to a functor
{\bf C}:{\bf DRC}\to{\bf CPC}
; see Theorem
5.20
. We will eventually see in Section
7
that
{\bf C}
is an isomorphism.

5.1

Definition 5.1
.

A

{}_{p}{\downharpoonleft}q=pq=p

In what follows, we will need to understand the

\displaystyle p\vartheta_{a}

\displaystyle=R(pa)\text{ \ for $p\leq{\bf d}(a)$,}

\displaystyle p\Theta_{a}=R(pa)\text{ \ for $p\in P$,}

\displaystyle p\partial_{a}

\displaystyle=D(ap)\text{ \ for $p\leq{\bf r}(a)$,}

\displaystyle p\Delta_{a}=D(ap)\text{ \ for $p\in P$.}

5.2
Projection categories

We saw in Lemma
5.3
(iii)
that the
\Theta
and
\Delta
maps in a weak projection category
(P,\mathcal{C})
behave well with regard to left and right restrictions, respectively, in the sense that
\Theta_{{}_{p}{\downharpoonleft}a}=\theta_{p}\Theta_{a}
and
\Delta_{a{\downharpoonright}_{q}}=\delta_{q}\Delta_{a}
for any
p\leq{\bf d}(a)
and
q\leq{\bf r}(a)
. We are particularly interested in the situation in which the analogous statements hold for the other restrictions.

Definition 5.6
.

A
projection category
is a weak projection category
(P,\mathcal{C})
satisfying:

(C1)

For every
a\in\mathcal{C}
, and every
p\leq{\bf d}(a)
and
q\leq{\bf r}(a)
, we have

\Theta_{a{\downharpoonright}_{q}}=\Theta_{a}\theta_{q}\and\Delta_{{}_{p}{\downharpoonleft}a}=\Delta_{a}\delta_{p}.

We write
{\bf PC}
for the category of projection categories. A morphism
(P,\mathcal{C})\to(P^{\prime},\mathcal{C}^{\prime})
in
{\bf PC}
is a biordered morphism
\phi:\mathcal{C}\to\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
whose object map
v\phi:P\to P^{\prime}
is a projection algebra morphism.

Proposition 5.7
.

The assignment
S\mapsto({\bf P}(S),\mathcal{C}(S))
is the object part of a functor
{\bf DRC}\to{\bf PC}
.

Proof.

We first check that
(P,\mathcal{C})=({\bf P}(S),\mathcal{C}(S))
is a projection category for any DRC-semigroup
S
. By Lemma
5.4
, it remains to check that
(C1)
holds.
By symmetry, it suffices to show that
\Theta_{a{\downharpoonright}_{q}}=\Theta_{a}\theta_{q}
for all
a\in\mathcal{C}
, and all
q\leq{\bf r}(a)
. For this we fix
t\in P
, and use (
5.5
) to calculate

t\Theta_{a{\downharpoonright}_{q}}=t\Theta_{aq}=R(taq)=_{2}R(R(ta)q)=R(ta)\theta_{q}=t\Theta_{a}\theta_{q}.

Now we know that
{\bf C}
maps objects of
{\bf DRC}
to objects of
{\bf PC}
.
It remains to check that any DRC-morphism
\phi:S\to S^{\prime}
is a projection category morphism from
(P,\mathcal{C})=({\bf P}(S),\mathcal{C}(S))
to
(P^{\prime},\mathcal{C}^{\prime})=({\bf P}(S^{\prime}),\mathcal{C}(S^{\prime}))
, i.e. that:

•

\phi
is a biordered category morphism
\mathcal{C}\to\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
, and

•

v\phi
is a projection algebra morphism
P\to P^{\prime}
.

But these follow from Propositions
3.13
and
4.19
, respectively.
∎

5.3

We now bring in an extra layer of structure.

Definition 5.8
.

Given a projection category

Within the image of

.

Let

be a projection category, and
\varepsilon:\mathscr{C}\to\mathcal{C}

For all

(ii)

For all

.

A

(\varepsilon[p_{1},\ldots,p_{k}])\phi=\varepsilon^{\prime}[p_{1}\phi,\ldots,p_{k}\phi]\hskip 20.00003pt\text{for $p_{1},\ldots,p_{k}\in P$ with $p_{1}\mathrel{\mathscr{F}}\cdots\mathrel{\mathscr{F}}p_{k}$.}

S
, and write

Lemma 5.11
.

\varepsilon=\varepsilon(S)
is an evaluation map.

Proof.

We first claim that for
\mathfrak{c}=[p_{1},\ldots,p_{k}]\in\mathscr{P}

By symmetry it suffices to prove the first. The

. We then use

to calculate

Next we check that

is a
v
-functor. Certainly

for all

. Now consider composable chains

\varepsilon({}_{q}{\downharpoonleft}\mathfrak{c})={}_{q}{\downharpoonleft}\varepsilon(\mathfrak{c})\hskip 10.00002pt\text{and}\hskip 10.00002pt\varepsilon(\mathfrak{c}{\downharpoonright}_{r})=\varepsilon(\mathfrak{c}){\downharpoonright}_{r}\hskip 20.00003pt\text{for all $\mathfrak{c}\in\mathscr{C}$, and for all $q\leq{\bf d}(\mathfrak{c})$ and $r\leq{\bf r}(\mathfrak{c})$.}

6.2
A functor

We can think of the construction of the DRC-semigroup
{\bf S}(P,\mathcal{C},\varepsilon)
from the chained projection category
(P,\mathcal{C},\varepsilon)
as an object map
{\bf CPC}\to{\bf DRC}
. At the level of morphisms, any chained projection functor
\phi:(P,\mathcal{C},\varepsilon)\to(P^{\prime},\mathcal{C}^{\prime},\varepsilon^{\prime})
is a map
\mathcal{C}\to\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
. Since the underlying sets of the semigroups
S={\bf S}(P,\mathcal{C},\varepsilon)
and
S^{\prime}={\bf S}(P^{\prime},\mathcal{C}^{\prime},\varepsilon^{\prime})
are
\mathcal{C}
and
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
, respectively, we can think of
\phi
as a map
\phi={\bf S}(\phi):S\to S^{\prime}
. We now show that
{\bf S}:{\bf CPC}\to{\bf DRC}
, interpreted in this way, is a functor.

Theorem 6.7
.

{\bf S}
is a functor
{\bf CPC}\to{\bf DRC}
.

Proof.

It remains only to check that a morphism
\phi:(P,\mathcal{C},\varepsilon)\to(P^{\prime},\mathcal{C}^{\prime},\varepsilon^{\prime})
in
{\bf CPC}
is also a morphism
S\to S^{\prime}
in
{\bf DRC}
, where
S={\bf S}(P,\mathcal{C},\varepsilon)
and
S^{\prime}={\bf S}(P^{\prime},\mathcal{C}^{\prime},\varepsilon^{\prime})
. For simplicity, we will write
\overline{a}=a\phi
for
a\in S\ (=\mathcal{C})
. We also use dashes to distinguish between the various parameters and operations on
\mathcal{C}
and
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
, and on
S
and
S^{\prime}
.

If
a\in S
, then writing
p=D(a)={\bf d}(a)
, we have

D^{\prime}(\overline{a})={\bf d}^{\prime}(\overline{a})={\bf d}^{\prime}(\overline{p\circ a})={\bf d}^{\prime}(\overline{p}\circ^{\prime}\overline{a})={\bf d}^{\prime}(\overline{p})=\overline{p}=\overline{D(a)},\hskip 20.00003pt\text{and similarly}\hskip 20.00003ptR^{\prime}(\overline{a})=\overline{R(a)}.

It therefore remains to show that
\overline{a\bullet b}=\overline{a}\bullet^{\prime}\overline{b}
for all
a,b\in S
. So fix some such
a,b
, and let

p={\bf r}(a),\hskip 20.00003ptq={\bf d}(b),\hskip 20.00003ptp^{\prime}=q\delta_{p}\and q^{\prime}=p\theta_{q}.

As shown above, we have
{\bf r}^{\prime}(\overline{a})=\overline{{\bf r}(a)}=\overline{p}
, and similarly
{\bf d}^{\prime}(\overline{b})=\overline{q}
. It follows that

a\bullet b=a{\downharpoonright}_{p^{\prime}}\circ\varepsilon[p^{\prime},q^{\prime}]\circ{}_{q^{\prime}}{\downharpoonleft}b\and\overline{a}\bullet^{\prime}\overline{b}=\overline{a}{\downharpoonright}_{\overline{p}^{\prime}}\circ^{\prime}\varepsilon^{\prime}[\overline{p}^{\prime},\overline{q}^{\prime}]\circ^{\prime}{}_{\overline{q}^{\prime}}{\downharpoonleft}\overline{b},

where
\overline{p}^{\prime}=\overline{q}\delta^{\prime}_{\overline{p}}
and
\overline{q}^{\prime}=\overline{p}\theta^{\prime}_{\overline{q}}
. Since

is a projection algebra morphism, we have

\overline{p^{\prime}}=\overline{q\delta_{p}}=\overline{q}\delta^{\prime}_{\overline{p}}=\overline{p}^{\prime},\hskip 20.00003pt\text{and similarly}\hskip 20.00003pt\overline{q^{\prime}}=\overline{q}^{\prime}.

Since

Since

\overline{a{\downharpoonright}_{p^{\prime}}}=\overline{a}{\downharpoonright}_{\overline{p^{\prime}}}=\overline{a}{\downharpoonright}_{\overline{p}^{\prime}},\hskip 20.00003pt\text{and similarly}\hskip 20.00003pt\overline{{}_{q^{\prime}}{\downharpoonleft}b}={}_{\overline{q}^{\prime}}{\downharpoonleft}\overline{b}.

Putting everything together, we have

\overline{a\bullet b}=\overline{a{\downharpoonright}_{p^{\prime}}\circ\varepsilon[p^{\prime},q^{\prime}]\circ{}_{q^{\prime}}{\downharpoonleft}b}=\overline{a{\downharpoonright}_{p^{\prime}}}\circ^{\prime}\overline{\varepsilon[p^{\prime},q^{\prime}]}\circ^{\prime}\overline{{}_{q^{\prime}}{\downharpoonleft}b}=\overline{a}{\downharpoonright}_{\overline{p}^{\prime}}\circ^{\prime}\varepsilon^{\prime}[\overline{p}^{\prime},\overline{q}^{\prime}]\circ^{\prime}{}_{\overline{q}^{\prime}}{\downharpoonleft}\overline{b}=\overline{a}\bullet^{\prime}\overline{b}.\qed

7
The category isomorphism

In this section we will show that the functors

from Theorems

and

Theorem 7.1
.

The category

Proof.

. We do this in Propositions

8
Free and fundamental projection-generated DRC-semigroups

Theorem
7.1
shows that the categories
{\bf DRC}
and
{\bf CPC}
are isomorphic. It also follows from the definition of the functors
{\bf C}:{\bf DRC}\to{\bf CPC}
and
{\bf S}:{\bf CPC}\to{\bf DRC}
that the algebras of projections of DRC-semigroups are precisely the same as the (structured) object sets of chained projection categories. But the theorem does not answer the following:

Question 8.1
.

Given an abstract projection algebra
P
, as in Definition
4.1
, does there exist a DRC-semigroup
S
with
{\bf P}(S)=P
? Or equivalently, does there exist a chained projection category
(P,\mathcal{C},\varepsilon)
?

The semigroup version of this question was answered in the affirmative by Jones in his study of
fundamental
DRC-semigroups
[
30
]
, i.e. the DRC-semigroups with no non-trivial projection-separating congruences. In
[
30
, Section 9]
, Jones constructed a DRC-semigroup, denoted
C(P)
, from a projection algebra
P
, and showed it to be the
maximum
fundamental DRC-semigroup with projection algebra
P
, meaning that:

•

C(P)
is fundamental, and has projection algebra
P={\bf P}(C(P))
, and

•

any fundamental DRC-semigroup with projection algebra
P
embeds canonically into
C(P)
.

In a sense, one of the purposes of the current section is to provide another way to answer Question
8.1
, in that we construct two different DRC-semigroups, denoted
F_{P}
and
M_{P}
, both with projection algebra
P
. However, we have a deeper purpose than this (as did Jones), and our main objective here is to demonstrate the existence of
free (projection-generated) DRC-semigroups
. Specifically, we show that the assignment
P\mapsto F_{P}
is the object part of a functor
{\bf F}:{\bf PA}\to{\bf DRC}
, and that this is in fact a left adjoint to the forgetful functor
{\bf P}:{\bf DRC}\to{\bf PA}
from Proposition
4.19
, which maps a DRC-semigroup
S
to its underlying projection algebra
{\bf P}(S)
. It follows that
F_{P}
is the free (projection-generated) DRC-semigroup with projection algebra
P
.

The semigroup
F_{P}
is defined abstractly in terms of a presentation by generators and relations. By contrast, the semigroup
M_{P}
is a concrete semigroup consisting of pairs of self-maps of
P
, built from the underlying
\theta_{p}
and
\delta_{p}
operations of
P
. The main initial purpose of
M_{P}
is to provide a concrete homomorphic image of
F_{P}
, which will be useful in certain proofs concerning the latter. But we will also see that
M_{P}
is very special in its own right, in that it is the unique projection-generated fundamental DRC-semigroup with projection algebra
P
.

The definitions of
F_{P}
and
M_{P}
are given in Section
8.1
, and their key properties are established in Theorem
8.11
. Categorical freeness of
F_{P}
is established in Section
8.2
; see Theorem
8.17
. The unique fundamentality properties of
M_{P}
are established in Section
8.3
; see Theorem
8.25
.

Before we begin, we briefly revise our notation for semigroup presentations. Let
X
be an alphabet, and write
X^{+}
for the free semigroup over
X
, which is the semigroup of all non-empty words over
X
, under concatenation. Also let
R\subseteq X^{+}\times X^{+}
be a set of pairs of words over
X
, and write
R^{\sharp}
for the congruence on
X^{+}
generated by
R
. We then write

\langle X:R\rangle=X^{+}/R^{\sharp},

and call this the semigroup defined by the
presentation

\langle X:R\rangle
. Elements of
X
are called
letters
or
generators
, and elements of
R
are called
relations
. A relation
(u,v)\in R
is often written as an equality,
u=v
.

8.1
Definition of
F_{P}
and
M_{P}

Fix a projection algebra
P
for the duration of this section. Also fix an alphabet

X_{P}=\{x_{p}:p\in P\}

in one-one correspondence with
P
, and let
R_{P}
be the set of relations, quantified over all
p,q\in P
:

(R1)

x_{p}^{2}=x_{p}
,

(R2)

x_{p}x_{q}=x_{p}x_{p\theta_{q}}
,

(R3)

x_{p}x_{q}=x_{q\delta_{p}}x_{q}
.

Write
{\sim}=R_{P}^{\sharp}
for the congruence on
X_{P}^{+}
generated by the relations
(R1)
–
(R3)
, and denote by
\overline{w}
the
\sim
-class of a word
w\in X_{P}^{+}
. We then take
F_{P}
to be the semigroup defined by the presentation

F_{P}=\langle X_{P}:R_{P}\rangle=X_{P}^{+}/{\sim}=\{\overline{w}:w\in X_{P}^{+}\}.

We will soon see that
F_{P}
is a DRC-semigroup with projection algebra (isomorphic to)
P
.

We also define a second semigroup associated to
P
. First, we denote by
\mathcal{T}_{P}
the
full transformation semigroup
over
P
, i.e. the semigroup of all maps
P\to P
under composition. As before, we also write
\mathcal{T}_{P}^{\operatorname{op}}
for the opposite semigroup. Our second semigroup will be a subsemigroup of the direct product
\mathcal{T}_{P}\times\mathcal{T}_{P}^{\operatorname{op}}
, in which the operation is given by
(\alpha,\alpha^{\prime})(\beta,\beta^{\prime})=(\alpha\beta,\beta^{\prime}\alpha^{\prime})
. For each
p\in P
, we define the pair

\widehat{p}=(\theta_{p},\delta_{p})\in\mathcal{T}_{P}\times\mathcal{T}_{P}^{\operatorname{op}},

and the semigroup

M_{P}=\langle\widehat{p}:p\in P\rangle\leq\mathcal{T}_{P}\times\mathcal{T}_{P}^{\operatorname{op}}.

A typical element of
M_{P}
has the form

\widehat{p}_{1}\cdots\widehat{p}_{k}=(\theta_{p_{1}}\cdots\theta_{p_{k}},\delta_{p_{k}}\cdots\delta_{p_{1}})\hskip 20.00003pt\text{for $p_{1},\ldots,p_{k}\in P$.}

Define the (surjective) semigroup homomorphism

\psi:X_{P}^{+}\to M_{P}:x_{p}\mapsto\widehat{p}=(\theta_{p},\delta_{p}).

Lemma 8.2
.

We have
R_{P}\subseteq\ker(\psi)
.

Proof.

We need to check that each relation from
R_{P}
is preserved by
\psi
, in the sense that
u\psi=v\psi
for each such relation
(u,v)\in R_{P}
. To do so, let
p,q\in P
. For
(R1)
we have

x_{p}^{2}\psi=\widehat{p}\cdot\widehat{p}=(\theta_{p}\theta_{p},\delta_{p}\delta_{p})=(\theta_{p},\delta_{p})=\widehat{p}=x_{p}\psi,

by
(P6)
. For
(R2)
we use
(P3)
and
(P5)
to calculate:

(x_{p}x_{q})\psi=\widehat{p}\cdot\widehat{q}=(\theta_{p}\theta_{q},\delta_{q}\delta_{p})=(\theta_{p}\theta_{p\theta_{q}},\delta_{p\theta_{q}}\delta_{p})=\widehat{p}\cdot\widehat{p\theta_{q}}=(x_{p}x_{p\theta_{q}})\psi.

We deal with
(R3)
in similar fashion.
∎

It follows that
\psi
induces a well-defined (surjective) semigroup homomorphism

\Psi:F_{P}\to M_{P}\hskip 20.00003pt\text{given by}\hskip 20.00003pt\overline{w}\Psi=w\psi\hskip 20.00003pt\text{for $w\in X_{P}^{+}$.}

Note in particular that
\overline{x}_{p}\Psi=\widehat{p}=(\theta_{p},\delta_{p})
for
p\in P
.

In what follows, we write
\sim_{1}
to denote
\sim
-equivalence of words from
X_{P}^{+}
by one or more applications of relations from
(R1)
, with similar meanings for
\sim_{2}
and
\sim_{3}
.

Lemma 8.3
.

If
p,q\in P
, then
x_{p}x_{q}\sim x_{p^{\prime}}x_{q^{\prime}}
, where
p^{\prime}=q\delta_{p}
and
q^{\prime}=p\theta_{q}
.

Proof.

We have
x_{p}x_{q}\sim_{2}x_{p}x_{p\theta_{q}}\sim_{3}x_{(p\theta_{q})\delta_{p}}x_{p\theta_{q}}=x_{q\delta_{p}}x_{p\theta_{q}}
, using
(P9)
in the last step.
∎

For a path
\mathfrak{p}=(p_{1},\ldots,p_{k})
from the path category
\mathscr{P}=\mathscr{P}(P)
, we define the word

w_{\mathfrak{p}}=x_{p_{1}}\cdots x_{p_{k}}\in X_{P}^{+}.

Lemma 8.4
.

Any word over
X_{P}
is
\sim
-equivalent to
w_{\mathfrak{p}}
for some
\mathfrak{p}\in\mathscr{P}
.

Proof.

Consider an arbitrary word
w=x_{p_{1}}\cdots x_{p_{k}}\in X_{P}^{+}
. We will show by induction on
k
that

w\sim x_{p_{1}^{\prime}}\cdots x_{p_{k}^{\prime}}\hskip 20.00003pt\text{for some $p_{i}^{\prime}\leq p_{i}$ with $p_{1}^{\prime}\mathrel{\mathscr{F}}\cdots\mathrel{\mathscr{F}}p_{k}^{\prime}$.}

The
k=1
case is trivial, so we now assume that
k\geq 2
. First, Lemma
8.3
gives

x_{p_{k-1}}x_{p_{k}}\sim x_{p_{k-1}^{\prime\prime}}x_{p_{k}^{\prime\prime}},\hskip 20.00003pt\text{where}\hskip 20.00003ptp_{k-1}^{\prime\prime}=p_{k}\delta_{p_{k-1}}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{and}\hskip 10.00002ptp_{k}^{\prime\prime}=p_{k-1}\theta_{p_{k}},

and by Lemma
4.13
(i)
we have
p_{k-1}^{\prime\prime}\leq p_{k-1}
,
p_{k}^{\prime\prime}\leq p_{k}
and
p_{k-1}^{\prime\prime}\mathrel{\mathscr{F}}p_{k}^{\prime\prime}
. By induction, we have

x_{p_{1}}\cdots x_{p_{k-2}}x_{p_{k-1}^{\prime\prime}}\sim x_{p_{1}^{\prime}}\cdots x_{p_{k-2}^{\prime}}x_{p_{k-1}^{\prime}},

for some
p_{i}^{\prime}\leq p_{i}
(
i=1,\ldots,k-2
) and
p_{k-1}^{\prime}\leq p_{k-1}^{\prime\prime}
, with
p_{1}^{\prime}\mathrel{\mathscr{F}}\cdots\mathrel{\mathscr{F}}p_{k-1}^{\prime}
. We also have

x_{p_{k-1}^{\prime}}x_{p_{k}^{\prime\prime}}\sim_{2}x_{p_{k-1}^{\prime}}x_{p_{k}^{\prime}},\hskip 20.00003pt\text{where}\hskip 20.00003ptp_{k}^{\prime}=p_{k-1}^{\prime}\theta_{p_{k}^{\prime\prime}}.

Since
p_{k-1}^{\prime}\leq p_{k-1}^{\prime\prime}\mathrel{\mathscr{F}}p_{k}^{\prime\prime}
, it follows from Corollary
4.14
that
p_{k-1}^{\prime}\mathrel{\mathscr{F}}p_{k}^{\prime}
. Putting everything together, we have

w=x_{p_{1}}\cdots x_{p_{k}}\sim x_{p_{1}}\cdots x_{p_{k-2}}x_{p_{k-1}^{\prime\prime}}x_{p_{k}^{\prime\prime}}\sim x_{p_{1}^{\prime}}\cdots x_{p_{k-2}^{\prime}}x_{p_{k-1}^{\prime}}x_{p_{k}^{\prime\prime}}\sim x_{p_{1}^{\prime}}\cdots x_{p_{k-2}^{\prime}}x_{p_{k-1}^{\prime}}x_{p_{k}^{\prime}},

with all conditions met. (Note that
p_{i}^{\prime}\leq p_{i}^{\prime\prime}\leq p_{i}
for
i=k-1,k
.)
∎

For a path
\mathfrak{p}=(p_{1},\ldots,p_{k})\in\mathscr{P}
, we define

\widehat{\mathfrak{p}}=w_{\mathfrak{p}}\psi=\widehat{p}_{1}\cdots\widehat{p}_{k}=(\theta_{p_{1}}\cdots\theta_{p_{k}},\delta_{p_{k}}\cdots\delta_{p_{1}})\in M_{P}.

It follows from Lemma
8.4
, and surjectivity of
\Psi
, that

F_{P}=\{\overline{w}_{\mathfrak{p}}:\mathfrak{p}\in\mathscr{P}\}\and M_{P}=\{\widehat{\mathfrak{p}}:\mathfrak{p}\in\mathscr{P}\}.

Lemma 8.5
.

If
\mathfrak{p},\mathfrak{q}\in\mathscr{P}
, then

(i)

\widehat{\mathfrak{p}}=\widehat{\mathfrak{q}}\ \Rightarrow\ {\bf d}(\mathfrak{p})={\bf d}(\mathfrak{q})
and
{\bf r}(\mathfrak{p})={\bf r}(\mathfrak{q})
,

(ii)

\overline{w}_{\mathfrak{p}}=\overline{w}_{\mathfrak{q}}\ \Rightarrow\ {\bf d}(\mathfrak{p})={\bf d}(\mathfrak{q})
and
{\bf r}(\mathfrak{p})={\bf r}(\mathfrak{q})
.

Proof.

Since
\overline{w}_{\mathfrak{p}}=\overline{w}_{\mathfrak{q}}\ \Rightarrow\ w_{\mathfrak{p}}\sim w_{\mathfrak{q}}\ \Rightarrow\ \widehat{\mathfrak{p}}=w_{\mathfrak{p}}\psi=w_{\mathfrak{q}}\psi=\widehat{\mathfrak{q}}
, it suffices to prove
(i)
. For this, write
{\mathfrak{p}=(p_{1},\ldots,p_{k})}
and
\mathfrak{q}=(q_{1},\ldots,q_{l})
, so that

\widehat{\mathfrak{p}}=(\theta_{p_{1}}\cdots\theta_{p_{k}},\delta_{p_{k}}\cdots\delta_{p_{1}})\and\widehat{\mathfrak{q}}=(\theta_{q_{1}}\cdots\theta_{q_{l}},\delta_{q_{l}}\cdots\delta_{q_{1}}).

It then follows from Lemma
4.15
that
\widehat{\mathfrak{p}}=\widehat{\mathfrak{q}}
implies
p_{1}=q_{1}
and
p_{k}=q_{l}
.
∎

It follows that we have well-defined maps
D,R:F_{P}\to F_{P}
and
D,R:M_{P}\to M_{P}
given by

\displaystyle D(\overline{w}_{\mathfrak{p}})

\displaystyle=\overline{x}_{{\bf d}(\mathfrak{p})}=\overline{x}_{p_{1}},

\displaystyle D(\widehat{\mathfrak{p}})

\displaystyle=\widehat{{\bf d}(\mathfrak{p})}=\widehat{p}_{1}=(\theta_{p_{1}},\delta_{p_{1}}),

\displaystyle R(\overline{w}_{\mathfrak{p}})

\displaystyle=\overline{x}_{{\bf r}(\mathfrak{p})}=\overline{x}_{p_{k}},

\displaystyle R(\widehat{\mathfrak{p}})

\displaystyle=\widehat{{\bf r}(\mathfrak{p})}=\widehat{p}_{k}=(\theta_{p_{k}},\delta_{p_{k}}),

for
\mathfrak{p}=(p_{1},\ldots,p_{k})\in\mathscr{P}
.

(8.6)

There should be no confusion in using
D
and
R
to denote these operations on both semigroups
F_{P}
and
M_{P}
, as they take on different kinds of arguments in the two semigroups.
Note also that

D(\overline{w}_{\mathfrak{p}})\Psi=(\overline{x}_{{\bf d}(\mathfrak{p})})\Psi=\widehat{{\bf d}(\mathfrak{p})}=D(\widehat{\mathfrak{p}})=D(\overline{w}_{\mathfrak{p}}\Psi),\hskip 20.00003pt\text{and similarly}\hskip 20.00003ptR(\overline{w}_{\mathfrak{p}})\Psi=R(\overline{w}_{\mathfrak{p}}\Psi).

(8.7)

Lemma 8.8
.

If
\mathfrak{p}\in\mathscr{P}
, and if
s\leq{\bf d}(\mathfrak{p})
and
t\leq{\bf r}(\mathfrak{p})
, then

x_{s}w_{\mathfrak{p}}\sim w_{{}_{s}{\downharpoonleft}\mathfrak{p}}\and w_{\mathfrak{p}}x_{t}\sim w_{\mathfrak{p}{\downharpoonright}_{t}}.

Proof.

For the first (the second is dual), write
\mathfrak{p}=(p_{1},\ldots,p_{k})
, and fix
s\leq p_{1}
. We then have

{}_{s}{\downharpoonleft}\mathfrak{p}=(s_{1},\ldots,s_{k}),\hskip 20.00003pt\text{where}\hskip 20.00003pts_{i}=s\theta_{p_{1}}\cdots\theta_{p_{i}}\hskip 20.00003pt\text{for each $i$,}

and we must show that

x_{s}x_{p_{1}}\cdots x_{p_{k}}\sim x_{s_{1}}\cdots x_{s_{k}}.

If
k=1
, then
x_{s}x_{p_{1}}\sim_{2}x_{s}x_{s\theta_{p_{1}}}=x_{s}x_{s}\sim_{1}x_{s}=x_{s_{1}}
(using
s\leq p_{1}
in the second step). For
k\geq 2
, we apply induction to calculate

x_{s}x_{p_{1}}\cdots x_{p_{k-1}}x_{p_{k}}\sim x_{s_{1}}\cdots x_{s_{k-1}}x_{p_{k}}\sim_{2}x_{s_{1}}\cdots x_{s_{k-1}}x_{s_{k-1}\theta_{p_{k}}},

and we are done, since
s_{k-1}\theta_{p_{k}}=s_{k}
.
∎

For paths
\mathfrak{p}=(p_{1},\ldots,p_{k})
and
\mathfrak{q}=(q_{1},\ldots,q_{l})
with
p_{k}\mathrel{\mathscr{F}}q_{1}
, the concatenation of
\mathfrak{p}
with
\mathfrak{q}
is a path, which we denote by

\mathfrak{p}\oplus\mathfrak{q}=(p_{1},\ldots,p_{k},q_{1},\ldots,q_{l}).

Lemma 8.9
.

For paths
\mathfrak{p}=(p_{1},\ldots,p_{k})
and
\mathfrak{q}=(q_{1},\ldots,q_{l})
, we have

w_{\mathfrak{p}}w_{\mathfrak{q}}\sim w_{\mathfrak{p}{\downharpoonright}_{s}\oplus{}_{t}{\downharpoonleft}\mathfrak{q}},\hskip 20.00003pt\text{where}\hskip 20.00003pt\text{$s=q_{1}\delta_{p_{k}}$ and $t=p_{k}\theta_{q_{1}}$.}

Proof.

Using
(R1)
, and Lemmas
8.3
and
8.8
, we have

w_{\mathfrak{p}}w_{\mathfrak{q}}\sim w_{\mathfrak{p}}\cdot x_{p_{k}}x_{q_{1}}\cdot w_{\mathfrak{q}}\sim w_{\mathfrak{p}}\cdot x_{s}x_{t}\cdot w_{\mathfrak{q}}\sim w_{\mathfrak{p}{\downharpoonright}_{s}}\cdot w_{{}_{t}{\downharpoonleft}\mathfrak{q}}=w_{\mathfrak{p}{\downharpoonright}_{s}\oplus{}_{t}{\downharpoonleft}\mathfrak{q}}.\qed

Proposition 8.10
.

F_{P}
is a DRC-semigroup.

Proof.

We need to check the axioms
(DRC1)
–
(DRC4)
, and as usual we only have to prove one part of each. To do so, fix paths
\mathfrak{p}=(p_{1},\ldots,p_{k})
and
\mathfrak{q}=(q_{1},\ldots,q_{l})
.

(DRC1)
. We have
D(\overline{w}_{\mathfrak{p}})\overline{w}_{\mathfrak{p}}=\overline{x}_{p_{1}}\cdot\overline{x}_{p_{1}}\cdots\overline{x}_{p_{k}}=_{1}\overline{x}_{p_{1}}\cdots\overline{x}_{p_{k}}=\overline{w}_{\mathfrak{p}}
.

(DRC2)
. Let
s=q_{1}\delta_{p_{k}}
and
t=p_{k}\theta_{q_{1}}
, and write
\mathfrak{p}{\downharpoonright}_{s}=(s_{1},\ldots,s_{k})
and
{}_{t}{\downharpoonleft}\mathfrak{q}=(t_{1},\ldots,t_{l})
. Lemma
8.9
then gives

\overline{w}_{\mathfrak{p}}\overline{w}_{\mathfrak{q}}=\overline{w}_{\mathfrak{p}{\downharpoonright}_{s}\oplus{}_{t}{\downharpoonleft}\mathfrak{q}}\and\overline{w}_{\mathfrak{p}}D(\overline{w}_{\mathfrak{q}})=\overline{w}_{\mathfrak{p}}\cdot\overline{x}_{q_{1}}=\overline{w}_{\mathfrak{p}}\cdot\overline{w}_{(q_{1})}=\overline{w}_{\mathfrak{p}{\downharpoonright}_{s}\oplus{}_{t}{\downharpoonleft}(q_{1})}.

Since
\mathfrak{p}{\downharpoonright}_{s}\oplus{}_{t}{\downharpoonleft}\mathfrak{q}=(s_{1},\ldots,s_{k},t_{1},\ldots,t_{l})
and
\mathfrak{p}{\downharpoonright}_{s}\oplus{}_{t}{\downharpoonleft}(q_{1})=(s_{1},\ldots,s_{k},t)
, it then follows that

D(\overline{w}_{\mathfrak{p}}\overline{w}_{\mathfrak{q}})=\overline{x}_{s_{1}}=D(\overline{w}_{\mathfrak{p}}D(\overline{w}_{\mathfrak{q}})).

(DRC3)
. Keeping the notation of the previous part, and keeping in mind
s_{1}\leq p_{1}
, we have

D(\overline{w}_{\mathfrak{p}}\overline{w}_{\mathfrak{q}})D(\overline{w}_{\mathfrak{p}})=\overline{x}_{s_{1}}\overline{x}_{p_{1}}=_{2}\overline{x}_{s_{1}}\overline{x}_{s_{1}\theta_{p_{1}}}=\overline{x}_{s_{1}}\overline{x}_{s_{1}}=_{1}\overline{x}_{s_{1}}=D(\overline{w}_{\mathfrak{p}}\overline{w}_{\mathfrak{q}}).

An analogous calculation gives
D(\overline{w}_{\mathfrak{p}})D(\overline{w}_{\mathfrak{p}}\overline{w}_{\mathfrak{q}})=D(\overline{w}_{\mathfrak{p}}\overline{w}_{\mathfrak{q}})
.

(DRC4)
. We have
R(D(\overline{w}_{\mathfrak{p}}))=R(\overline{x}_{p_{1}})=\overline{x}_{p_{1}}=D(\overline{w}_{\mathfrak{p}})
.
∎

Theorem 8.11
.

If
P
is a projection algebra, then
F_{P}
and
M_{P}
are projection-generated DRC-semigroups, with

{\bf P}(F_{P})\cong{\bf P}(M_{P})\cong P.

Proof.

We saw in Proposition
8.10
that
F_{P}
is a DRC-semigroup. It follows that

{\bf P}(F_{P})=\operatorname{im}(D)=\{\overline{x}_{p}:p\in P\},

so we have a surjective map

\xi:P\to{\bf P}(F_{P}):p\mapsto\overline{x}_{p}.

Combining the definitions with Corollary
4.16
shows that
\xi
is also injective, as for
p,q\in P
we have

p\xi=q\xi\ \Rightarrow\ \overline{x}_{p}=\overline{x}_{q}\ \Rightarrow\ \widehat{p}=\overline{x}_{p}\Psi=\overline{x}_{q}\Psi=\widehat{q}\ \Rightarrow\ (\theta_{p},\delta_{p})=(\theta_{q},\delta_{q})\ \Rightarrow\ p=q.

(8.12)

So
\xi:P\to{\bf P}(F_{P})
is a bijection, and we now wish to show that it is a projection algebra morphism, meaning that

(p\theta_{q})\xi=(p\xi)\theta^{\prime}_{q\xi}\and(p\delta_{q})\xi=(p\xi)\delta^{\prime}_{q\xi}\hskip 20.00003pt\text{for all $p,q\in P$,}

where here we write
\theta^{\prime}
and
\delta^{\prime}
for the projection algebra operations in
{\bf P}(F_{P})
. For the first (the second is dual), we write
p^{\prime}=q\delta_{p}
and
q^{\prime}=p\theta_{q}
, and use Lemma
8.3
to calculate

(p\xi)\theta^{\prime}_{q\xi}=(\overline{x}_{p})\theta^{\prime}_{\overline{x}_{q}}=R(\overline{x}_{p}\overline{x}_{q})=R(\overline{x}_{p^{\prime}}\overline{x}_{q^{\prime}})=R(\overline{w}_{(p^{\prime},q^{\prime})})=\overline{x}_{q^{\prime}}=q^{\prime}\xi=(p\theta_{q})\xi.

By definition,
F_{P}=\langle X_{P}:R_{P}\rangle
is generated by the projections
\overline{x}_{p}
(
p\in P
).

This completes the proof of the assertions regarding
F_{P}
. Those for
M_{P}
follow quickly, in light of the fact that the surjective homomorphism
\Psi:F_{P}\to M_{P}
preserves the
D
and
R
operations on the two semigroups (cf. (
8.7
)), and maps
{\bf P}(F_{P})
bijectively (as
\overline{x}_{p}\Psi=\overline{x}_{q}\Psi\ \Rightarrow\ p=q
, as in (
8.12
)).
∎

Remark 8.13
.

By identifying each projection
p\in P
with the
\sim
-class
\overline{x}_{p}\in F_{P}
, we can identify
P
itself with the projection algebra
{\bf P}(F_{P})
. It will be convenient to do so in the next section.

8.2
Free projection-generated DRC-semigroups

Recall that we have a functor
{\bf P}:{\bf DRC}\to{\bf PA}
, which at the object level maps a DRC-semigroup
S
to its projection algebra

{\bf P}(S)=\operatorname{im}(D)=\operatorname{im}(R)=\{p\in S:p^{2}=p=D(p)=R(p)\},

with operations given by

q\theta_{p}=R(qp)\and q\delta_{p}=D(pq)\hskip 20.00003pt\text{for $p,q\in{\bf P}(S)$.}

We can think of
{\bf P}
as a
forgetful functor
, as when we construct
{\bf P}(S)
from
S
, we remember only a subset of the elements of
S
, and retain only partial information about their products.

It turns out that
{\bf P}
has a left adjoint,
{\bf F}:{\bf PA}\to{\bf DRC}
, which involves the semigroups constructed in Section
8.1
. At the object level, we define

{\bf F}(P)=F_{P}\hskip 20.00003pt\text{for a projection algebra $P$.}

To see how
{\bf F}
acts on a projection algebra morphism
\phi:P\to P^{\prime}
, we begin by defining a semigroup morphism

\varphi:X_{P}^{+}\to F_{P^{\prime}}=X_{P^{\prime}}^{+}/R_{P^{\prime}}^{\sharp}\hskip 20.00003pt\text{by}\hskip 20.00003ptx_{p}\varphi=\overline{x}_{p\phi}\hskip 20.00003pt\text{for $p\in P$.}

It is easy to see that
R_{P}\subseteq\ker(\varphi)
. For example, if
p,q\in P
then

\displaystyle(x_{p}x_{q})\varphi=\overline{x}_{p\phi}\overline{x}_{q\phi}

\displaystyle=\overline{x}_{p\phi}\overline{x}_{(p\phi)\theta^{\prime}_{q\phi}}

as
R_{P^{\prime}}
contains the relation
x_{p\phi}x_{q\phi}=x_{p\phi}x_{(p\phi)\theta^{\prime}_{q\phi}}

\displaystyle=\overline{x}_{p\phi}\overline{x}_{(p\theta_{q})\phi}

as
\phi
is a projection algebra morphism

\displaystyle=(x_{p}x_{p\theta_{q}})\varphi,

which shows that
\varphi
preserves
(R2)
. It follows that
\varphi
induces a well-defined semigroup homomorphism

\Phi:F_{P}=X_{P}^{+}/R_{P}^{\sharp}\to F_{P^{\prime}}\hskip 20.00003pt\text{given by}\hskip 20.00003pt\overline{w}\Phi=w\varphi\hskip 20.00003pt\text{for $w\in X_{P}^{+}$.}

(8.14)

We then define
{\bf F}(\phi)=\Phi
. It is essentially trivial to check that
\Phi
preserves the
D
and
R
operations on
F_{P}
and
F_{P^{\prime}}
, in light of the definitions of these operations in (
8.6
), meaning that
{\bf F}(\phi)=\Phi
is indeed a well-defined DRC-morphism
{\bf F}(P)\to{\bf F}(P^{\prime})
.

Proposition 8.15
.

{\bf F}
is a functor
{\bf PA}\to{\bf DRC}
, and we have
{\bf P}{\bf F}=\operatorname{id}_{{\bf PA}}
.

Proof.

The first claim follows quickly from the above discussion. For the second, we must show that:

•

{\bf P}({\bf F}(P))=P
for all projection algebras
P
, and

•

{\bf P}({\bf F}(\phi))=\phi
for all projection algebra morphisms
\phi
.

This is routine, in light of the identification of
P
with
{\bf P}(F_{P})
, as in Remark
8.13
; cf. (
8.14
).
∎

So

{F_{P}=\langle P\rangle}
.
∎

8.3
Fundamental projection-generated DRC-semigroups

Following
[
30
]
, a
(DRC-)congruence
on a DRC-semigroup
S
is an equivalence relation
\sigma
on
S
respecting all of the operations, in the sense that

[a\mathrel{\sigma}b\text{ and }a^{\prime}\mathrel{\sigma}b^{\prime}]\ \Rightarrow\ aa^{\prime}\mathrel{\sigma}bb^{\prime}\and a\mathrel{\sigma}b\ \Rightarrow\ [D(a)\mathrel{\sigma}D(b)\text{ and }R(a)\mathrel{\sigma}R(b)].

The congruence
\sigma
is
projection-separating
if
p\mathrel{\sigma}q\ \Rightarrow\ p=q
for all projections
p,q\in{\bf P}(S)
. Among other things,
[
30
, Proposition 2.3]
shows that
S
has a
maximum projection-separating congruence
, denoted
\mu_{S}
; that is,
\mu_{S}
is projection-separating, and any other projection-separating congruence is contained in
\mu_{S}
. We say
S
is
(DRC-)fundamental
if
\mu_{S}
is the trivial congruence
\iota_{S}=\{(a,a):a\in S\}
. In general,
S/\mu_{S}
is fundamental, the maximum fundamental image of
S
.

Remark 8.20
.

Here we follow Jones’ convention in dropping the ‘DRC-’ prefix, and speak simply of ‘fundamental DRC-semigroups’. But the reader should be aware that a ‘fundamental’ (ordinary) semigroup is usually defined as a semigroup with no non-trivial congruence contained in Green’s
\mathrel{\mathscr{H}}
-relation. For regular semigroups, this is equivalent to having no non-trivial idempotent-separating congruence
[
32
]
.

Proposition 8.21
.

If
S
is a DRC-semigroup, then

\displaystyle\mu_{S}

\displaystyle=\{(a,b)\in S\times S:\Theta_{a}=\Theta_{b}\text{ and }\Delta_{a}=\Delta_{b}\}

\displaystyle=\{(a,b)\in S\times S:\vartheta_{a}=\vartheta_{b}\text{ and }\partial_{a}=\partial_{b}\}

Proof.

Writing
P={\bf P}(S)
, Proposition 2.3 of
[
30
]
says that for
a,b\in S
we have
(a,b)\in\mu_{S}
if and only if

D(ap)=D(bp)\hskip 10.00002pt\text{and}\hskip 10.00002ptR(pa)=R(pb)\hskip 20.00003pt\text{for all $p\in P^{1}$,}

where
P^{1}
is
P
with an adjoined identity element. Keeping (
5.5
) in mind, this is equivalent to all of the following holding:

\Theta_{a}=\Theta_{b},\hskip 20.00003pt\Delta_{a}=\Delta_{b},\hskip 20.00003ptD(a)=D(b)\and R(a)=R(b).

Remembering also that
{\bf d}=D
and
{\bf r}=R
, we can therefore prove the first equality in the current proposition by showing that

[\Theta_{a}=\Theta_{b}\text{ and }\Delta_{a}=\Delta_{b}]\hskip 20.00003pt\Rightarrow\hskip 20.00003pt[{\bf d}(a)={\bf d}(b)\text{ and }{\bf r}(a)={\bf r}(b)].

(8.22)

To do so, suppose
\Theta_{a}=\Theta_{b}
and
\Delta_{a}=\Delta_{b}
. Writing
q={\bf r}(a)
, we have

{\bf d}(a)={\bf d}(a{\downharpoonright}_{q})=q\partial_{a}=q\delta_{q}\partial_{a}=q\Delta_{a}=q\Delta_{b}\leq{\bf d}(b),\hskip 20.00003pt\text{and similarly}\hskip 20.00003pt{\bf d}(b)\leq{\bf d}(a).

The proof that
{\bf r}(a)={\bf r}(b)
is dual.

For the second equality in the proposition, we will show that

[\Theta_{a}=\Theta_{b}\text{ and }\Delta_{a}=\Delta_{b}]\hskip 20.00003pt\Leftrightarrow\hskip 20.00003pt[\vartheta_{a}=\vartheta_{b}\text{ and }\partial_{a}=\partial_{b}]\hskip 20.00003pt\text{for $a,b\in S$.}

(8.23)

In light of the identities
\Theta_{a}=\theta_{{\bf d}(a)}\vartheta_{a}
and
\vartheta_{a}=\Theta_{a}|_{{\bf d}(a)^{\downarrow}}
(the second of which is Lemma
5.3
(iv)
), and the analogous identities for
\Delta_{a}
and
\partial_{a}
, we can prove (
8.23
) by showing that

\displaystyle[\Theta_{a}=\Theta_{b}\text{ and }\Delta_{a}=\Delta_{b}]

\displaystyle\hskip 20.00003pt\Rightarrow\hskip 20.00003pt[{\bf d}(a)={\bf d}(b)\text{ and }{\bf r}(a)={\bf r}(b)],

\displaystyle\text{and}\hskip 20.00003pt[\vartheta_{a}=\vartheta_{b}\text{ and }\partial_{a}=\partial_{b}]\hskip 6.544pt

\displaystyle\hskip 20.00003pt\Rightarrow\hskip 20.00003pt[{\bf d}(a)={\bf d}(b)\text{ and }{\bf r}(a)={\bf r}(b)].

The first implication is (
8.22
), which was proved above.
The second follows from the fact that
\operatorname{dom}(\vartheta_{a})={\bf d}(a)^{\downarrow}
and
\operatorname{dom}(\partial_{a})={\bf r}(a)^{\downarrow}
.
∎

Fundamental DRC-semigroups were classified in
[
30
]
by means of a transformation representation. We will not attempt to reprove this here, but instead we prove a result concerning
projection-generated
fundamental DRC-semigroups (see Theorem
8.25
below), which was not given in
[
30
]
. For its proof, we will make use of the following result, which shows that the maps
\Theta_{a}
and
\Delta_{a}
have very natural forms when
a
is a product of projections.

Lemma 8.24
.

If
S
is a DRC-semigroup, and if
p_{1},\ldots,p_{k}\in{\bf P}(S)
, then

\Theta_{p_{1}\cdots p_{k}}=\theta_{p_{1}}\cdots\theta_{p_{k}}\and\Delta_{p_{1}\cdots p_{k}}=\delta_{p_{k}}\cdots\delta_{p_{1}}.

Proof.

To prove the first statement (the second is dual), let
t\in{\bf P}(S)
. Then using (
5.5
) and Lemma
4.17
(i)
, we have
t\Theta_{p_{1}\cdots p_{k}}=R(tp_{1}\cdots p_{k})=t\theta_{p_{1}}\cdots\theta_{p_{k}}
.
∎

We are now ready to prove our final result of this section:

Theorem 8.25
.

For any projection algebra
P
there is a unique (up to isomorphism) projection-generated fundamental DRC-semigroup with projection algebra
P
, namely
M_{P}\cong F_{P}/\mu_{F_{P}}
.

Proof.

By Theorem
8.11
, we can identify the projection algebras
{\bf P}(F_{P})
and
{\bf P}(M_{P})
with
P
itself, and in this way the semigroups
F_{P}
and
M_{P}
are both generated by
P
. To avoid confusion, we will denote the products in these semigroups by
\bullet
and
\star
, respectively, so that typical elements have the form

p_{1}\bullet\cdots\bullet p_{k}\equiv\overline{x_{p_{1}}\cdots x_{p_{k}}}\in F_{P}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{and}\hskip 10.00002ptp_{1}\star\cdots\star p_{k}\equiv\widehat{p}_{1}\cdots\widehat{p}_{k}=(\theta_{p_{1}}\cdots\theta_{p_{k}},\delta_{p_{k}}\cdots\delta_{p_{1}})\in M_{P},

for some
p_{1},\ldots,p_{k}\in P
. In what follows, we use Proposition
8.21
freely.

We have already noted that
M_{P}
is a projection-generated DRC-semigroup with projection algebra
P
. To see that it is fundamental, we need to show that
\mu_{M_{P}}
is trivial. To do so, suppose
(a,b)\in\mu_{M_{P}}
; we must show that
a=b
. As above, we have

a=p_{1}\star\cdots\star p_{k}=(\theta_{p_{1}}\cdots\theta_{p_{k}},\delta_{p_{k}}\cdots\delta_{p_{1}})\and b=q_{1}\star\cdots\star q_{l}=(\theta_{q_{1}}\cdots\theta_{q_{l}},\delta_{q_{l}}\cdots\delta_{q_{1}}),

for some
p_{1},\ldots,p_{k},q_{1},\ldots,q_{l}\in P
. Since
(a,b)\in\mu_{M_{P}}
, we have
\Theta_{a}=\Theta_{b}
and
\Delta_{a}=\Delta_{b}
. It then follows from Lemma
8.24
that

\theta_{p_{1}}\cdots\theta_{p_{k}}=\Theta_{a}=\Theta_{b}=\theta_{q_{1}}\cdots\theta_{q_{l}},\hskip 20.00003pt\text{and similarly}\hskip 20.00003pt\delta_{p_{k}}\cdots\delta_{p_{1}}=\delta_{q_{l}}\cdots\delta_{q_{1}}.

But then
a=(\theta_{p_{1}}\cdots\theta_{p_{k}},\delta_{p_{k}}\cdots\delta_{p_{1}})=(\theta_{q_{1}}\cdots\theta_{q_{l}},\delta_{q_{l}}\cdots\delta_{q_{1}})=b
.

Now let
S
be an arbitrary projection-generated fundamental DRC-semigroup with projection algebra
{\bf P}(S)=P
; we denote the product in
S
simply by juxtaposition. The proof will be complete if we can show that
S\cong F_{P}/\mu_{F_{P}}
. Applying Proposition
8.16
to the identity morphism
\phi:P\to P={\bf P}(S)
, we see that there is a DRC-morphism

\Phi:F_{P}\to S:p\mapsto p.

Since
S
is projection-generated,
\Phi
is surjective, and so
S\cong F_{P}/\ker(\Phi)
by the fundamental homomorphism theorem. It therefore remains to show that
\ker(\Phi)=\mu_{F_{P}}
. Since
\Phi
maps
P={\bf P}(F_{P})
identically,
\ker(\Phi)
is projection-separating, and so
\ker(\Phi)\subseteq\mu_{F_{P}}
. Conversely, let
(a,b)\in\mu_{F_{P}}
, so that
\Theta_{a}=\Theta_{b}
and
\Delta_{a}=\Delta_{b}
. Since
F_{P}
is projection-generated, we have

a=p_{1}\bullet\cdots\bullet p_{k}\and b=q_{1}\bullet\cdots\bullet q_{l}\hskip 20.00003pt\text{for some $p_{1},\ldots,p_{k},q_{1},\ldots,q_{l}\in P$.}

But then applying Lemma
8.24
in both
F_{P}
and
S
, we have

\Theta_{a}=\Theta_{p_{1}\bullet\cdots\bullet p_{k}}=\theta_{p_{1}}\cdots\theta_{p_{k}}=\Theta_{p_{1}\cdots p_{k}}=\Theta_{a\Phi},\hskip 20.00003pt\text{and similarly}\hskip 20.00003pt\Theta_{b}=\Theta_{b\Phi}.

It follows that
\Theta_{a\Phi}=\Theta_{a}=\Theta_{b}=\Theta_{b\Phi}
, and simlarly
\Delta_{a\Phi}=\Delta_{b\Phi}
, so that
(a\Phi,b\Phi)\in\mu_{S}
. Since
S
is fundamental, it follows that
a\Phi=b\Phi
, i.e. that
(a,b)\in\ker(\Phi)
.
∎

9
Regular
*
- and
*
-regular semigroups

As mentioned in Section
1
, the current paper extends to DRC-semigroups the groupoid-based approach to
regular
*
-semigroups
from
[
13
]
. In the case of regular
*
-semigroups (whose definition will be recalled below), many of the current categorical constructions simplify, sometimes drammatically. In this section we outline many of these simplifications, and indicate why the more elaborate approach of the current paper is necessary to encompass the full generality of DRC-semigroups. In doing so, we will contrast the situation with Drazin’s broader class of
*
-regular semigroups

[
12
]
, which induce natural DRC-structures. These are already complex enough to require the more complicated setup, and a ready source of (counter)examples will be provided by the real matrix monoids
M_{n}(\mathbb{R})
, which are known to be
*
-regular
[
46
]
. (Due to quirks of terminology,
*
-regular and regular
*
-semigroups are distinct classes, though the former contains the latter.) We will also observe in Section
10
that many of the simplifications hold in the
DRC-restriction semigroups
considered by Die and Wang in
[
8
]
.

Before we begin, we recall some basic semigroup theoretical background; for more information see for example
[
27
,
4
]
. Let
S
be a semigroup, and
S^{1}
its monoid completion. So
S^{1}=S
if
S
is a monoid, or else
S^{1}=S\cup\{1\}
, where
1
is a symbol not belonging to
S
, acting as an adjoined identity. Green’s
\mathrel{\mathscr{L}}
,
\mathrel{\mathscr{R}}
and
\mathrel{\mathscr{J}}
equivalences are defined, for
a,b\in S
, by

a\mathrel{\mathscr{L}}b\ \Leftrightarrow\ S^{1}a=S^{1}b,\hskip 20.00003pta\mathrel{\mathscr{R}}b\ \Leftrightarrow\ aS^{1}=bS^{1}\and a\mathrel{\mathscr{J}}b\ \Leftrightarrow\ S^{1}aS^{1}=S^{1}bS^{1}.

From these are defined the further equivalences
{\mathrel{\mathscr{H}}}={\mathrel{\mathscr{L}}}\cap{\mathrel{\mathscr{R}}}
and
{\mathrel{\mathscr{D}}}={\mathrel{\mathscr{L}}}\vee{\mathrel{\mathscr{R}}}
, where the latter is the join in the lattice of all equivalences on
S
. We have
{\mathrel{\mathscr{D}}}={\mathrel{\mathscr{L}}}\circ{\mathrel{\mathscr{R}}}={\mathrel{\mathscr{R}}}\circ{\mathrel{\mathscr{L}}}
, and if
S
is finite then
{\mathrel{\mathscr{D}}}={\mathrel{\mathscr{J}}}
.

An element
a
of a semigroup
S
is
regular
if
a=axa
for some
x\in S
. This is equivalent to having
a=aya
and
y=yay
for some
y\in S
(if
a=axa
, then take
y=xax
), and such an element
y
is called a
(semigroup) inverse
of
a
. Note then that
a\mathrel{\mathscr{R}}ay
and
a\mathrel{\mathscr{L}}ya
, with
ay
and
ya
idempotents. In fact, an element of a semigroup is regular if and only if it is
\mathrel{\mathscr{D}}
-related to an idempotent.

9.1
Matrix monoids

Fix an integer
n\geq 1
, and let
M_{n}(\mathbb{R})
be the monoid of
n\times n
real matrices, under ordinary matrix multiplication. (We consider real matrices merely for convenience, though what we say can be adapted to the complex field.) Denoting the row and column spaces of
a\in M_{n}(\mathbb{R})
by
\operatorname{Row}(a)
and
\operatorname{Col}(a)
, and writing
\operatorname{rank}(a)=\dim(\operatorname{Row}(a))=\dim(\operatorname{Col}(a))
for the rank of
a
, Green’s relations on
M_{n}(\mathbb{R})
are given by

\displaystyle a\mathrel{\mathscr{L}}b

\displaystyle\ \Leftrightarrow\ \operatorname{Row}(a)=\operatorname{Row}(b),

\displaystyle a\mathrel{\mathscr{R}}b

\displaystyle\ \Leftrightarrow\ \operatorname{Col}(a)=\operatorname{Col}(b)\and a\mathrel{\mathscr{J}}b\ \Leftrightarrow\ a\mathrel{\mathscr{D}}b\ \Leftrightarrow\ \operatorname{rank}(a)=\operatorname{rank}(b).

(9.1)

(See for example
[
44
, Lemma 2.1]
.) Since idempotents of arbitrary rank
0\leq r\leq n
clearly exist,
M_{n}(\mathbb{R})
is regular.

In fact, any matrix
a\in M_{n}(\mathbb{R})
can be assigned a
special
inverse
[
46
]
. Using
{}^{\operatorname{T}}
to denote transpose, there exists a unique matrix
x\in M_{n}(\mathbb{R})
satisfying

a=axa,\hskip 20.00003ptx=xax,\hskip 20.00003pt(ax)^{\operatorname{T}}=ax\and(xa)^{\operatorname{T}}=xa.

(9.2)

This
x
is known as the
Moore–Penrose inverse
, and is denoted
x=a^{\dagger}
. There is no simple formula for
a^{\dagger}
; its existence is established in
[
46
]
by using the linear dependence of the sequence
(a^{\operatorname{T}}a)^{k}
,
k=1,2,3,\ldots
. Nevertheless, many computational packages exist for working with the Moore–Penrose inverse, e.g. Matlab
[
54
]
. For example, with
a=\left[\begin{smallmatrix}1&1&1\\
1&0&0\\
0&0&0\end{smallmatrix}\right]\in M_{3}(\mathbb{R})
, Matlab tells us that
a^{\dagger}=\left[\begin{smallmatrix}0&1&0\\
\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}&-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}&0\\
\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}&-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}&0\end{smallmatrix}\right]
. One can readily check the identities (
9.2
), and we note that
aa^{\dagger}=\left[\begin{smallmatrix}1&0&0\\
0&1&0\\
0&0&0\end{smallmatrix}\right]
and
a^{\dagger}a=\left[\begin{smallmatrix}1&0&0\\
0&\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}&\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}\\
0&\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}&\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}\end{smallmatrix}\right]
are symmetric idempotents, with
aa^{\dagger}\mathrel{\mathscr{R}}a\mathrel{\mathscr{L}}a^{\dagger}a
; cf. (
9.1
).

9.2
Regular
*
- and
*
-regular semigroups as DRC-semigroups

The defining properties of the Moore–Penrose inverse in
M_{n}(\mathbb{R})
led to the introduction in
[
12
]
of the class of
*
-regular semigroups
, to which we now turn.

First, a
*
-semigroup
is an algebra
(S,\cdot,{}^{*})
, where
(S,\cdot)
is a semigroup, and where
∗
is an involution, i.e. a unary operation
S\to S:a\mapsto a^{*}
satisfying the laws

a^{**}=a\and(ab)^{*}=b^{*}a^{*}\hskip 20.00003pt\text{for all $a,b\in S$.}

For example,
M_{n}(\mathbb{R})
becomes a
*
-semigroup with involution given by transpose:
a^{*}=a^{\operatorname{T}}
.
The category of
*
-semigroups with
*
-morphisms (i.e. the semigroup morphisms preserving
∗
) contains two important (full) subcategories, whose definitions we will shortly recall:

•

{\bf RSS}
, the category of
regular
*
-semigroups

[
43
]
, and

•

{\bf SRS}
, the category of
*
-regular semigroups

[
12
]
.

Beginning with the latter, a
regular
*
-semigroup
is a
*
-semigroup
(S,\cdot,{}^{*})
additionally satisfying

a=aa^{*}a\hskip 20.00003pt\text{for all $a\in S$.}

It follows from the axioms that also
a^{*}aa^{*}=a^{*}
in a regular
*
-semigroup, meaning that
a^{*}
is a (semigroup) inverse of
a
.

A
*
-regular semigroup
is a
*
-semigroup
(S,\cdot,{}^{*})
for which every element
a\in S
has an inverse that is
\mathrel{\mathscr{H}}
-related to
a^{*}
. It follows from basic semigroup-theoretic facts that this inverse is unique; it is denoted by
a^{\dagger}
, and called the
Moore–Penrose inverse
of
a

[
46
]
. The terminology stems from the fact (see
[
42
, Corollary 1.2]
) that
a^{\dagger}
can also be characterised as the unique element
x
satisfying

a=axa,\hskip 20.00003ptx=xax,\hskip 20.00003pt(ax)^{*}=ax\and(xa)^{*}=xa.

In this way, a
*
-regular semigroup can be equivalently defined as an algebra
(S,\cdot,{}^{*},{}^{\dagger})
, where
(S,\cdot)
is a semigroup, and
∗
and
†
are unary operations satisfying:

a^{**}=a,\hskip 10.00002pt(ab)^{*}=b^{*}a^{*},\hskip 10.00002ptaa^{\dagger}a=a,\hskip 10.00002pta^{\dagger}aa^{\dagger}=a^{\dagger},\hskip 10.00002pt(aa^{\dagger})^{*}=aa^{\dagger}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{and}\hskip 10.00002pt(a^{\dagger}a)^{*}=a^{\dagger}a.

(9.3)

Various other laws can be deduced as consequences (see for example
[
42
, Theorem 1.3]
), including:

a^{\dagger\dagger}=a\and a^{*\dagger}=a^{\dagger*}.

(9.4)

Regular
*
-semigroups are the
*
-regular semigroups in which
{a^{\dagger}=a^{*}}
for all
a
. The matrix monoid
M_{n}(\mathbb{R})
is
*
-regular (cf. (
9.2
)), but it is not a regular
*
-semigroup, as
a^{\operatorname{T}}
need not be an inverse of
a
, even when
n=1
.

It is well known (and easy to see) that any
*
-morphism
\phi:S\to T
between
*
-regular semigroups preserves Moore–Penrose inverses, meaning that
(a^{\dagger})\phi=(a\phi)^{\dagger}
for all
a\in S
.
Less obvious is the following:

Theorem 9.5

(Jones
[
30
, Proposition 4.2]
)
.

Any
*
-regular semigroup
S\equiv(S,\cdot,{}^{*})
gives rise to a DRC-semigroup
{\bf I}(S)=(S,\cdot,D,R)
, under the unary operations

D(a)=aa^{\dagger}\and R(a)=a^{\dagger}a\hskip 20.00003pt\text{for $a\in S$.}

Remark 9.6
.

The proof of Theorem
9.5
is completely routine in the special case that
S
is a regular
*
-semigroup. We also note that the elements
D(a)=aa^{\dagger}
and
R(a)=a^{\dagger}a
appearing in the theorem are the unique projections of
S
that are
\mathrel{\mathscr{R}}
- and
\mathrel{\mathscr{L}}
-related to
a
, respectively. This was observed, for example, in
[
5
]
.

We can think of the assignment
S\mapsto{\bf I}(S)
as an object map from
{\bf SRS}
to
{\bf DRC}
, and this can be easily extended to a functor
{\bf I}:{\bf SRS}\to{\bf DRC}
.
Given a
*
-morphism
\phi:S\to T
in
{\bf SRS}
, we simply define
{\bf I}(\phi)=\phi
. Since
\phi
preserves
†
, it also preserves
D
and
R
.

The functor
{\bf I}:{\bf SRS}\to{\bf DRC}
is not full (surjective on morphisms), however. Indeed, consider an abelian group
G
with identity
1
, in which at least one element is not its own inverse (e.g. the cyclic group of order
3
). Then
S_{1}=(G,\cdot,{}^{-1})
and
S_{2}=(G,\cdot,{}^{\star})
are both
*
-regular semigroups, where
a^{\star}=a
for all
a\in G
. Moreover, we have
{\bf I}(S_{1})={\bf I}(S_{2})
, since
D(a)=R(a)=1
in both semigroups for all
a\in G
. It follows that the identity map
\iota:G\to G
is a DRC-morphism
{\bf I}(S_{1})\to{\bf I}(S_{2})
, but it is not a
*
-morphism, as
(a^{-1})\iota\not=(a\iota)^{\star}
for any non-self-inverse
a\in G
.

On the other hand, the restriction of
{\bf I}
to the subcategory
{\bf RSS}
of regular
*
-semigroups
is
full, as follows from the next result (recall that
†
coincides with
∗
in a regular
*
-semigroup).

Proposition 9.7
.

If
\phi:{\bf I}(S)\to{\bf I}(T)
is a DRC-morphism, for
*
-regular semigroups
S
and
T
, then
(a^{\dagger})\phi=(a\phi)^{\dagger}
for all
a\in S
.

Proof.

Since
\phi
is a DRC-morphism, we have

a\phi\cdot a^{\dagger}\phi=(aa^{\dagger})\phi=D(a)\phi=D(a\phi)=a\phi\cdot(a\phi)^{\dagger},\hskip 20.00003pt\text{and similarly}\hskip 20.00003pta^{\dagger}\phi\cdot a\phi=(a\phi)^{\dagger}\cdot a\phi.

Combining these gives

a^{\dagger}\phi=(a^{\dagger}\cdot a\cdot a^{\dagger})\phi=a^{\dagger}\phi\cdot a\phi\cdot a^{\dagger}\phi=a^{\dagger}\phi\cdot a\phi\cdot(a\phi)^{\dagger}=(a\phi)^{\dagger}\cdot a\phi\cdot(a\phi)^{\dagger}=(a\phi)^{\dagger}.\qed

It follows that
{\bf RSS}
is (isomorphic to) a full subcategory of
{\bf DRC}
, and is hence isomorphic to its image under the isomorphism
{\bf C}:{\bf DRC}\to{\bf CPC}
from Theorem
5.20
. One could then go on to show that this image is isomorphic to the category
{\bf CPG}
of
chained projection groupoids
, as defined in
[
13
]
, thereby proving the main result of that paper. We will not give the full details here, as a similar deduction was considered in
[
13
]
itself, where the isomorphism
{\bf RSS}\cong{\bf CPG}
was used to reprove the Ehresmann–Nambooripad–Schein Theorem on inverse semigroups and inductive groupoids.

The situation for the more general
*
-regular semigroups, however, is more complicated. Since the functor
{\bf I}:{\bf SRS}\to{\bf DRC}
is not an isomorphism onto a
full
subcategory, we are left with what seems to be a very interesting and important open problem:

Problem 9.8
.

Obtain an ‘ESN-type’ theorem for the category of
*
-regular semigroups.

9.3
Projection algebras of regular
*
- and
*
-regular semigroups

For the rest of this section we fix a
*
-regular semigroup
S\equiv(S,\cdot,{}^{*},{}^{\dagger})
, and denote the corresponding DRC-semigroup from Theorem
9.5
by
S^{\prime}={\bf I}(S)=(S,\cdot,D,R)
. Using (
9.3
) and (
9.4
) we see that

R(a^{*})=(a^{*})^{\dagger}a^{*}=(a^{\dagger})^{*}a^{*}=(aa^{\dagger})^{*}=aa^{\dagger}=D(a),\hskip 20.00003pt\text{and similarly}\hskip 20.00003ptD(a^{*})=R(a),

(9.9)

meaning that
S^{\prime}
is an
involutory DRC-semigroup
in the terminology of Jones
[
30
, Section 4]
.
Among other things, this leads to a simplification in the structure of the projection algebra
P={\bf P}(S^{\prime})
. First, we note that

P=\{p\in S:p^{2}=p=p^{*}\}=\{p\in S:p^{2}=p=p^{\dagger}\}.

Indeed, the first equality was shown in
[
30
, Proposition 4.2]
. For the second, suppose first that
p^{2}=p=p^{*}
. It follows that
p
is an inverse of
p
that is
\mathrel{\mathscr{H}}
-related (indeed,
equal
) to
p^{*}
, so that
{p=p^{\dagger}}
. Conversely, if
p^{2}=p=p^{\dagger}
, then
p=pp=pp^{\dagger}=(pp^{\dagger})^{*}=p^{*}
. Using (
9.9
), it then follows that for any
p,q\in P
we have

q\theta_{p}=R(qp)=R(q^{*}p^{*})=R((pq)^{*})=D(pq)=q\delta_{p}.

In other words, we have
\theta_{p}=\delta_{p}
for all
p
, meaning that the two families of operations of
P
reduces to one, and
P
is
symmetric
in the terminology of Jones
[
30
, Section 11]
.

In fact, not only do the
\theta_{p}
and
\delta_{p}
operations coincide in a
*
-regular semigroup, but they can be defined equationally in terms of the
†
operation. To describe this, and for later use, we need the following basic result:

Lemma 9.10
.

If
S
is a
*
-regular semigroup, then for any
a\in S
and
p\in{\bf P}(S)
we have

(pa)^{\dagger}=(pa)^{\dagger}p\and(ap)^{\dagger}=p(ap)^{\dagger}.

Proof.

Since
(pa)^{\dagger}\mathrel{\mathscr{H}}(pa)^{*}=a^{*}p
, it follows that
p
is a right identity for
(pa)^{\dagger}
, which gives the first identity. The second is dual.
∎

Proposition 9.11
.

If
S
is a
*
-regular semigroup, then for any
p,q\in{\bf P}(S)
we have

q\theta_{p}=q\delta_{p}=p(pq)^{\dagger}=(qp)^{\dagger}p.

Proof.

Using Lemma
9.10
we see that
q\theta_{p}=R(qp)=(qp)^{\dagger}qp=(qp)^{\dagger}p
. A symmetrical calculation gives
{q\delta_{p}=p(pq)^{\dagger}}
, and we have already seen that
q\theta_{p}=q\delta_{p}
.
∎

Remark 9.12
.

In the case that
S
is a regular
*
-semigroup, we obtain the simpler expression

q\theta_{p}=p(pq)^{\dagger}=p(pq)^{*}=pqp.

(9.13)

This does not hold in
*
-regular semigroups in general, however. For example, with the projections
p=\left[\begin{smallmatrix}\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}&\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}\\

(P2)

\theta_{p}\theta_{p}=\theta_{p}

(P3)

(P4)

\theta_{p}\theta_{q}\theta_{p}=\theta_{q\theta_{p}}

(P5)

\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}&0&\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}\end{smallmatrix}\right]\and q=\left[\begin{smallmatrix}1&0&0\\

We continue to fix the

Proposition 9.15
.

If
S
is a
*
-regular semigroup, then
\mathcal{C}=\mathcal{C}(S^{\prime})
is a groupoid, in which
a^{-1}=a^{\dagger}
for all

.

Proof.

Fix
a\in S
, and first note that (
9.4
) gives

{\bf d}(a^{\dagger})=a^{\dagger}a^{\dagger\dagger}=a^{\dagger}a={\bf r}(a),\hskip 20.00003pt\text{and similarly}\hskip 20.00003pt{\bf r}(a^{\dagger})={\bf d}(a).

It follows that the compositions
a\circ a^{\dagger}
and
a^{\dagger}\circ a
exist in
\mathcal{C}
. Specifically, we have

{\bf d}(a)=aa^{\dagger}=a\circ a^{\dagger},\hskip 20.00003pt\text{and similarly}\hskip 20.00003pt{\bf r}(a)=a^{\dagger}\circ a,

so that
a^{\dagger}
is indeed the inverse of

in
\mathcal{C}
.

Using (

) and (

), it is also worth noting that for

{\bf d}(a^{*})=a^{*}a^{*\dagger}=a^{*}a^{\dagger*}=(a^{\dagger}a)^{*}=a^{\dagger}a={\bf r}(a),\hskip 20.00003pt\text{and similarly}\hskip 20.00003pt{\bf r}(a^{*})={\bf d}(a).

In what follows, we will typically use the laws
{\bf d}(a^{\dagger})={\bf d}(a^{*})={\bf r}(a)
and
{\bf r}(a^{\dagger})={\bf r}(a^{*})={\bf d}(a)
without explicit reference.

A further simplification occurs in the special case that

is a regular
*
-semigroup (where we recall that
∗
and

Proposition 9.16
.

If
S
is a regular
*
-semigroup, then the orders
\leq_{\ell}
and
\leq_{r}
on

coincide.

Proof.

By symmetry it suffices to show that
a\leq_{\ell}b\ \Rightarrow\ a\leq_{r}b
for
a,b\in\mathcal{C}
, so suppose
a\leq_{\ell}b
. This means that
a={}_{p}{\downharpoonleft}b=pb
, where
p={\bf d}(a)\leq{\bf d}(b)=bb^{*}
. It follows from the latter that
p=bb^{*}p
. Write
q={\bf r}(a)=a^{*}a
, and note that the biordered category axioms give
q\leq{\bf r}(b)
. It then follows that

b{\downharpoonright}_{q}=bq=ba^{*}a=b(pb)^{*}a=bb^{*}pa=pa=a,

so that
a\leq_{r}b
.
∎

\nicefrac{{1}}{{3}}&\nicefrac{{1}}{{3}}&\nicefrac{{1}}{{3}}\end{smallmatrix}\right]\not=f_{1}(p,b,s).

(9.29)

Not only can axiom
(C2)
be quantified over a smaller set of projections when
S
is a regular
*
-semigroup, but the projections
e_{i}
and
f_{i}
in (
9.24
) take on much simpler forms. Specifically, when
(e,f)
is
b
-linked (and
S
is a regular
*
-semigroup), we have

e_{1}(e,b,f)=e\theta_{q},\hskip 20.00003pte_{2}(e,b,f)=f\Delta_{b},\hskip 20.00003ptf_{1}(e,b,f)=e\Theta_{b}\and f_{2}(e,b,f)=f\delta_{r},

(9.30)

where again
q={\bf d}(b)
and
r={\bf r}(b)
.
For example, using
\theta_{t}=\delta_{t}
,
(P4)′
, Lemma
5.3
(ii)
and (
9.25
), we have

e_{1}(e,b,f)=f\Delta_{b}\delta_{e\theta_{q}}=f\Delta_{b}\theta_{e\theta_{q}}=f\Delta_{b}\theta_{q}\theta_{e}\theta_{q}=f\Delta_{b}\delta_{{\bf d}(b)}\theta_{e}\theta_{q}=f\Delta_{b}\theta_{e}\theta_{q}=e\theta_{q}.

The simplifications in (
9.30
) do not hold in general DRC-semigroups. Indeed, small counterexamples can be readily constructed using Mace4
[
40
]
, although we do not currently know of any
*
-regular
counterexamples.

As well as the simplification in the projections (
9.24
) and (
9.30
), the ‘double restrictions’ in (
9.23
) are just single restrictions for regular
*
-semigroups, as equality of the
\leq_{\ell}
and
\leq_{r}
orders leads to the identities
({}_{p}{\downharpoonleft}a){\downharpoonright}_{q}=a{\downharpoonright}_{q}
and
{}_{r}{\downharpoonleft}(a{\downharpoonright}_{s})={}_{r}{\downharpoonleft}a
(for appropriate
p,q,r,s
). As usual, these do not hold in general
*
-regular semigroups.

10
Other special cases

In this final section we consider another two important subclasses of DRC-semigroups, specifically the Ehresmann and DRC-restriction semigroups considered by Lawson
[
33
]
and Die and Wang
[
8
]
. Again, although we could in principle deduce the main results of
[
33
,
8
]
from ours, we will not give all of the details. Rather, we just outline the ways in which our constructions simplify in these cases.

10.1
Ehresmann semigroups

We noted in Section
3.1
that the
Ehresmann semigroups
of Lawson
[
33
]
are precisely the DRC-semigroups
S
satisfying the additional law

D(a)D(b)=D(b)D(a)\and R(a)R(b)=R(b)R(a)\hskip 20.00003pt\text{for all $a,b\in S$.}

Given
(DRC4)
, either of the above identities implies the other, and of course they say that projections of
S
commute. This is a very strong property, and to unpack it we first prove the following:

Proposition 10.1
.

If
p,q\in{\bf P}(S)
for a DRC-semigroup
S
, then the following are equivalent:

(i)

pq=qp
,

(ii)

p\theta_{q}=q\theta_{p}
and
p\delta_{q}=q\delta_{p}
,

(iii)

p\theta_{q}=q\theta_{p}=p\delta_{q}=q\delta_{p}
,

(iv)

p\theta_{q}=q\theta_{p}=p\delta_{q}=q\delta_{p}=pq=qp
.

Proof.

(i)
\ \Rightarrow\
(iv)
. Suppose first that
pq=qp
. By Lemma
3.4
we have
D(pq)=D(pq)p
, and since
{D(pq)=D(qp)}
, the same lemma gives
D(pq)=D(pq)q
. Together with
(DRC1)
these imply
{D(pq)=D(pq)pq=pq}
, which says
q\delta_{p}=pq
. The remaining equalities are analogous.

(iv)
\ \Rightarrow\
(iii)
\ \Rightarrow\
(ii)
. These implications are clear.

(ii)
\ \Rightarrow\
(i)
. Now suppose
p\theta_{q}=q\theta_{p}
and
p\delta_{q}=q\delta_{p}
, which means that
R(pq)=R(qp)
and
D(pq)=D(qp)
. As in (
7.4
), we have
pq=D(pq)R(pq)
, so also

qp=D(qp)R(qp)=D(pq)R(pq)=pq.\qed

Corollary 10.2
.

If
p,q\in{\bf P}(S)
for a DRC-semigroup
S
, and if
pq=qp
, then

(i)

the meet
p\wedge q
exists in the poset
(P,\leq)
, and
p\wedge q=pq=qp
,

(ii)

p\mathrel{\mathscr{F}}q\ \Leftrightarrow\ p=q
.

Proof.

(i)
. By Proposition
10.1
we have
pq=p\theta_{q}\in P
, and moreover
pq=p\theta_{q}\leq q
and similarly
pq\leq p
. So
pq
is a lower bound for
p
and
q
. If we had another lower bound
r\leq p,q
, then
r=prp=qrq
(cf. (
3.5
)); combined with
pq=qp
this leads to
r=(pq)r(pq)
, i.e.
r\leq pq
.

(ii)
. If
p\mathrel{\mathscr{F}}q
, then
p=q\delta_{p}
and
q=p\theta_{q}
, and since
q\delta_{p}=p\theta_{q}
(by Proposition
10.1
) it follows that
p=q
.
∎

Corollary
10.2
has some important simplifying consequences for an Ehresmann semigroup
S
, since
all
projections commute. First,
P={\bf P}(S)
is a meet semilattice, and hence a subsemigroup of
S
. Second,
{\mathrel{\mathscr{F}}}=\iota_{P}
is the equality relation. Thus, the chain category
\mathscr{C}(P)
is simply a copy of
P
, in which the only compositions are
p=p\circ p
(
p\in P
), and in which
{\leq_{\ell}}={\leq_{r}}={\leq}
is the order from (
4.9
). It follows that the chained projection category
(P,\mathcal{C},\varepsilon)={\bf C}(S)
of the Ehresmann semigroup
S
carries no more information than the projection category
(P,\mathcal{C})
itself.
Moreover, since
{\mathrel{\mathscr{F}}}=\iota_{P}
is trivial, axiom
(C2)
simplifies as well. Indeed, we first note that since
e\mathrel{\mathscr{F}}e_{1},e_{2}
and
f_{1},f_{2}\mathrel{\mathscr{F}}f
by Lemma
5.16
, it follows that
e=e_{1}=e_{2}
and
f=f_{1}=f_{2}
. Thus,
(C2)
asserts the equality of the double restrictions:

({}_{p\theta_{q}}{\downharpoonleft}b){\downharpoonright}_{f}={}_{e}{\downharpoonleft}(b{\downharpoonright}_{s\delta_{r}})\hskip 20.00003pt\text{for all $b\in\mathcal{C}(q,r)$ and $p,s\in P$, \hskip 10.00002ptwhere $e=s\Delta_{b}\delta_{p}$ and $f=p\Theta_{b}\theta_{s}$.}

(10.3)

This can be used to prove the following, which is one of the Ehresmann category axioms from
[
33
]
.

Proposition 10.4
.

In an Ehresmann semigroup
S
, we have
{\leq_{\ell}}\circ{\leq_{r}}={\leq_{r}}\circ{\leq_{\ell}}
.

Proof.

By symmetry, it suffices to prove the inclusion
{\leq_{\ell}}\circ{\leq_{r}}\subseteq{\leq_{r}}\circ{\leq_{\ell}}
. To do so, suppose
a,b\in\mathcal{C}=\mathcal{C}(S)
are such that
a\leq_{\ell}u\leq_{r}b
for some
u\in\mathcal{C}
. This means that

a={}_{p}{\downharpoonleft}(b{\downharpoonright}_{s})\hskip 20.00003pt\text{for some $s\leq{\bf r}(b)$ and $p\leq{\bf d}(b{\downharpoonright}_{s})=s\partial_{b}$.}

(So here
u=b{\downharpoonright}_{s}
.) We can complete the proof by showing that

a=({}_{p}{\downharpoonleft}b){\downharpoonright}_{t},\hskip 20.00003pt\text{where}\hskip 20.00003ptt={\bf r}(a).

(10.5)

To do so, we now write
q={\bf d}(b)
and
r={\bf r}(b)
, and we work towards interpreting (
10.3
) in the current context. First note that

s\leq{\bf r}(b)=r\and p\leq{\bf d}(b{\downharpoonright}_{s})\leq{\bf d}(b)=q,

which imply that
p\theta_{q}=p
and
s\delta_{r}=s
. We also have

e=s\Delta_{b}\delta_{p}=s\delta_{r}\partial_{b}\delta_{p}=s\partial_{b}\delta_{p}=p,

because
p\leq s\partial_{b}
; cf. (
4.9
). Thus, (
10.3
) gives

({}_{p}{\downharpoonleft}b){\downharpoonright}_{f}={}_{p}{\downharpoonleft}(b{\downharpoonright}_{s}),

where
f=p\Theta_{b}\theta_{s}
. Since
a={}_{p}{\downharpoonleft}(b{\downharpoonright}_{s})=({}_{p}{\downharpoonleft}b){\downharpoonright}_{f}
, it follows that
f={\bf r}(a)
, completing the proof of (
10.5
), and hence of the proposition.
∎

Lawson in
[
33
]
denotes by
\leq_{e}
the composition
{\leq_{\ell}}\circ{\leq_{r}}={\leq_{r}}\circ{\leq_{\ell}}
, and we note that the above proof shows that if
a\leq_{e}b
, then we have

a=({}_{p}{\downharpoonleft}b){\downharpoonright}_{t}={}_{p}{\downharpoonleft}(b{\downharpoonright}_{t}),\hskip 20.00003pt\text{where}\hskip 20.00003ptp={\bf d}(a)\hskip 10.00002pt\text{and}\hskip 10.00002ptt={\bf r}(a).

This is
[
33
, Lemma 4.6]
.

Finally, we note that
{\mathrel{\mathscr{F}}}=\iota_{P}
has the consequence that the
\bullet
product on the category
\mathcal{C}
from Definition
6.1
takes on the simpler form

a\bullet b=a{\downharpoonright}_{e}\circ{}_{e}{\downharpoonleft}b,\hskip 20.00003pt\text{where}\hskip 20.00003pte={\bf r}(a)\wedge{\bf d}(b).

10.2
DRC-restriction semigroups

We conclude the paper by briefly commenting on the
DRC-restriction semigroups
considered by Die and Wang in
[
8
]
. These are the DRC-semigroups
S\equiv(S,\cdot,D,R)
satisfying the additional laws

D(ab)a=aD(R(a)b)\and aR(ba)=R(bD(a))a\hskip 20.00003pt\text{for all $a,b\in S$}.

It was observed in
[
8
, Section 1]
that these laws need only be quantified over
a\in S
and
b\in{\bf P}(S)
, given
(DRC2)
. The DRC-restriction laws might seem unwieldy, but it turns out that they are equivalent to the orders
\leq_{\ell}
and
\leq_{r}
coinciding, as shown in
[
8
, Lemma 5.8]
.

The full subcategory
{\bf DRCR}
of
{\bf DRC}
consisting of all DRC-restriction semigroups (and DRC-morphisms) is isomorphic to its image under the isomorphism
{\bf C}:{\bf DRC}\to{\bf CPC}
from Theorem
6.7
(cf. Theorem
7.1
). One could then use this to deduce the results from
[
8
]
. Again we omit the details, but we do note that the constructions and results of
[
8
]
involve some (but not all) of the simplifications that we discussed in Section
9
for regular
*
-semigroups. As we have already mentioned, the
\leq_{\ell}
and
\leq_{r}
orders coincide, so one works with ordered rather than biordered categories. However, the
\theta_{p}
and
\delta_{p}
operations need not be equal, i.e.
{\bf P}(S)
need not be symmetric. Similarly, the
\vartheta/\Theta
and
\partial/\Delta
maps need not be related to each other; for example,
\vartheta_{a}
(
a\in S
) need not be equal to any
\partial_{b}
map (for
b\in S
). On the other hand,
\vartheta_{a}
is always a projection algebra morphism
{\bf d}(a)^{\downarrow}\to{\bf r}(a)^{\downarrow}
, with a similar statement for
\partial_{a}
; see
[
8
, Lemmas 4.7 and 7.7]
. This leads to the various equivalent conditions in the analogue of
(C1)
in
[
8
]
, including (
9.22
); cf. Remark
9.21
. Likewise, the simplifications in
(C2)
discussed at the end of Section
9.4
all hold in DRC-restriction semigroups; specifically, the axiom can be quantified over
b
-linked pairs (cf. (
9.25
)), and the projections
e_{i},f_{i}
take on the simpler forms in (
9.30
).
