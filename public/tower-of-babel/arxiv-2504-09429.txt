Galois groups of reductions modulo p p of D D -finite series
Xavier Caruso, Florian Fürnsinn, Daniel Vargas-Montoya

Abstract
The aim of this paper is to investigate the algebraicity behavior of reductions of D D -finite power series modulo prime numbers. For many classes of D D -finite functions, such as diagonals of multivariate algebraic series or hypergeometric functions, it is known that their reductions modulo prime numbers, when defined, are algebraic. We formulate a conjecture that uniformizes the Galois groups of these reductions across different prime numbers. We then focus on hypergeometric functions, which serves as a test case for our conjecture. Refining the construction of an annihilating polynomial for the reduction of a hypergeometric function modulo a prime number p p , we extract information on the respective Galois groups and show that they behave nicely as p p varies.

Introduction

The starting point of this paper is the observation that there exist many interesting series in
\mathbb{Q}[\![x]\!]
which are transcendental over
\mathbb{Q}(x)

but whose reductions modulo
p
(when they exist) are algebraic over
\mathbb{F}_{p}(x)
for a large set of primes
p
.
A typical example is provided by “diagonals”.
By definition, the
diagonal
of a multivariate series

f(t_{1},\ldots,t_{k})=\sum_{\mathclap{n_{1},\ldots,n_{k}\in\mathbb{N}}}a_{n_{1},\ldots,n_{k}}t_{1}^{n_{1}}\cdots t_{k}^{n_{k}}

is the univariate series

\Delta f(x)=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}}a_{n,\ldots,n}x^{n}.

In characteristic zero, the diagonal construction usually does not preserve algebraicity.
However this astonishing property does hold in positive characteristic: the diagonal of an algebraic function over
\mathbb{F}_{p}(t_{1},\ldots,t_{k})
is algebraic over
\mathbb{F}_{p}(x)
!
This result was originally observed and proved by Furstenberg
[
Fur67
]
when
f
is a rational function;
it was then extended by Deligne
[
Del84
]
to algebraic functions and reproved many times by different authors
[
Sal86
,
Sal87
,
DL87
,
Har88
,
SW88
]
throughout the years.
Deligne’s theorem shows that diagonals exhibit the phenomenon that interests us in this article:
the diagonal
\Delta f(x)
of an algebraic function
f(t_{1},\ldots,t_{k})\in\mathbb{Q}[\![t_{1},\ldots,t_{k}]\!]
is usually transcendental
but its reductions modulo the primes, when they are defined, are algebraic (since they agree with the diagonals of
f\bmod p
).

The community then got interested in finding upper bounds on the degree of algebraicity of
\Delta f(x)\bmod p
.
When
f
is a rational function, Furstenberg’s original proof leads to a bound of the form
p^{n}
where
n
depends only on
f
.
It turns out that Deligne’s proof leads to a similar bound when
f(t_{1},t_{2})
is a bivariate algebraic function.
The general case was solved by Adamczewski and Bell
[
AB13
]
who obtained again a general bound of the form
p^{n}
where
n
only depends on
f
.
In
[
ABC23
]
, after providing sharper bounds on the exponent
n
, the authors observed that, not only
\Delta f(x)\bmod p
is annihilated by a polynomial
Z_{p}(Y)
of degree at most
p^{n}
,
but that there always exists such an annihilating polynomial of the form

Z_{p}(Y)=c_{0}(x)Y+c_{1}(x)Y^{p}+\cdots+c_{n}(x)Y^{p^{n}}\qquad(c_{i}(x)\in\mathbb{F}_{p}[x]).

(1.1)

They noticed moreover (see Remark 5.3 of
loc. cit.
) that this property implies that all the Galois conjugates of
\Delta f(x)\bmod p
lie in a
\mathbb{F}_{p}(x)
-vector space of dimension
n
,
which further shows that the Galois group of
\Delta f(x)\bmod p
canonically embeds, up to conjugacy, into
\GL_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{p})
.
Going even further, they suggested that all those Galois groups could have a common origin living in characteristic zero.

In the present article, we explore this question in slightly different contexts.
To start with, we consider the class of
D
-finite functions.
By definition the series
f(x)\in\mathbb{Q}[\![x]\!]
is called
D
-finite
if it is a solution of a linear differential equation of the form

f^{(r)}(x)+a_{r-1}(x)f^{(r-1)}(x)+\cdots+a_{1}(x)f^{\prime}(x)+a_{0}(x)f(x)=0

(1.2)

where the coefficients
a_{i}(x)
are rational functions.
It is a standard fact that diagonals are
D
-finite but it turns out that the class of
D
-finite series is much larger;
for instance, it includes the function
f(x)=\exp(\arctan(x))
whose reduction modulo
p
only makes sense for primes
p
which are congruent to
1
modulo
4

(whereas diagonals can always be reduced modulo
p
for almost all primes
p
).

In Section
2
, we formulate several hypotheses predicting the behavior of the Galois groups of
f(x)\bmod p
when
f(x)
is
D
-finite.
Roughly speaking, if
n
denotes the minimal order of a differential equation satisfied by
f(x)
,
what we expect is the existence of a number field
K
and a finite family
G_{1},\ldots,G_{t}
of subgroups of
\GL_{n}(K)
with the following property:
for any prime
\mathfrak{p}
of
K
for which
f(x)\bmod\mathfrak{p}
is well defined and algebraic over
\mathbb{F}_{p}(x)
, there exists an integer
i\in\{1,\ldots,t\}
such that

\Gal\big(f(x)\bmod p\mid\bar{\mathbb{F}}_{p}(x)\big)\simeq\image\big(G_{i}\cap\GL_{n}(\mathcal{O}_{(\mathfrak{p})})\rightarrow\GL_{n}(k_{\mathfrak{p}})\big).

Here
\mathcal{O}_{(\mathfrak{p})}
denotes the localization of the ring of integers of
K
at
\mathfrak{p}
, that is the ring consisting of fractions of the form
\frac{a}{b}
with
b\not\in\mathfrak{p}
,
and
k_{\mathfrak{p}}
is the residue field. We refer to Conjecture
2.2.6
in the body of the text for a more precise statement.

We then elaborate further on this conjecture, trying to predict what could be the field
K
, the groups
G_{i}
, and the dependence of the index
i
with respect to
p
.
This leads us to formulate stronger forms (see Conjectures
2.2.8
and
2.2.11
) which, despite being technical, capture all what we have glimpsed at so far. Finally, in Subsection
2.3.5
, we investigate a possible relationship between the groups
G_{i}
and the differential Galois group of the differential equation (
1.2
).

In Section
3
, we provide evidence towards our conjectures by studying in more details the special case of hypergeometric functions.
We recall that, given two tuples
\boldsymbol{\alpha}=(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n})
and
\boldsymbol{\beta}=(\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{m})
of rational numbers which are not nonpositive integers, the corresponding
hypergeometric function
is

{}_{n}F_{m}\left(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta};x\right)\coloneqq\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(\alpha_{1})_{k}(\alpha_{2})_{k}\cdots(\alpha_{n})_{k}}{(\beta_{1})_{k}(\beta_{2})_{k}\cdots(\beta_{m})_{k}}\cdot\frac{x^{k}}{k!}

where
(\alpha)_{k}\coloneqq\alpha\cdot(\alpha+1)\cdots(\alpha+k-1)
is the
Pochhammer symbol
.
It is a standard fact that hypergeometric functions are
D
-finite (but not necessarily diagonals).
In Subsection
3.1
, refining a former result of Christol, we classify the primes
p
for which the series
{}_{n}F_{n-1}\left(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta};x\right)
reduces properly modulo
p
.
In Subsection
3.2
, we move to the main interest of this article and, following the strategy introduced in
[
Var24
]
, we exhibit an annihilating polynomial for the function
{}_{n}F_{n-1}\left(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta};x\right)\bmod p
(when it is defined) and study the corresponding Galois group.
More precisely, we prove the following theorem.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
n
be a positive integer. Let
\boldsymbol{\alpha}=(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n})
and
\boldsymbol{\beta}=(\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{n-1})
be tuples of rational numbers with
\alpha_{i},\beta_{j}\not\in-\mathbb{N}
for all
i,j
.
Let
d
be the smallest common denominator of the
\alpha_{i}
and
\beta_{i}
and let
\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{d})
be the
d
-th cyclotomic extension of
\mathbb{Q}
.
Let
K
be the subextension of
\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{d})
corresponding under Galois correspondence to the group

D\coloneqq\big\{\,\lambda\in(\mathbb{Z}/d\mathbb{Z})^{\times}\,:\,\lambda{\cdot}\boldsymbol{\alpha}\equiv\boldsymbol{\alpha}\!\!\!\!\pmod{\mathbb{Z}}\text{ and }\lambda{\cdot}\boldsymbol{\beta}\equiv\boldsymbol{\beta}\!\!\!\!\pmod{\mathbb{Z}}\text{ as sets}\,\big\}\\
\subseteq(\mathbb{Z}/d\mathbb{Z})^{\times}\simeq\Gal\big(\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{d})/\mathbb{Q}\big).

Let
p>2d{\cdot}\max\{|\alpha_{i}|+1,|\beta_{j}|+1\}
be a prime number such that
{}_{n}F_{n-1}\left(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta};x\right)\in\mathbb{Z}_{(p)}[\![x]\!]
.
Then, letting
k_{\mathfrak{p}}
be the residue field of
K
at a place
\mathfrak{p}
above
p
and writing
q\coloneqq\mathrm{Card}(k_{\mathfrak{p}})
, the function
{}_{n}F_{n-1}\left(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta};x\right)\bmod p
is annihilated by a nonzero polynomial of the form

Z_{p}(Y)=c_{0}(x)Y+c_{1}(x)Y^{q}+\cdots+c_{n}(x)Y^{q^{n}}\quad\text{with}\quad c_{i}(x)\in k_{\mathfrak{p}}(x).

In particular, the Galois group of
{}_{n}F_{n-1}\left(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta};x\right)\bmod p

over
k_{\mathfrak{p}}(x)
embeds into
\GL_{n}(k_{\mathfrak{p}})
.

We notice in particular the emergence of a number field
K
, which is perfectly in line with our formulation of Conjecture
2.2.6
.
Moreover, we underline that the integer
n
appearing in
\GL_{n}(k_{\mathfrak{p}})
, say the
embedding dimension
, is truly the same as the integer
n
we started with,
which is also the order of the minimal-order differential equation satisfied by
{}_{n}F_{n-1}\left(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta};x\right)
. Again, this is in perfect accordance with Conjecture
2.2.6
.
We mention nevertheless that, in this special case, the embedding dimension can even be lowered and looks more closely related to the dimension of the space of solutions of the hypergeometric equation in characteristic
p
.

Finally, in Subsection
3.3
, we consider the special case of Gaussian hypergeometric functions
{}_{2}F_{1}\left(\boldsymbol{\alpha},(1);x\right)
with
\boldsymbol{\alpha}=(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2})\in\big(\mathbb{Q}\setminus(-\mathbb{N})\big)^{2}
.
In this situation, two important facts occur.
Firstly, keeping the notation and assumptions of Theorem
1.1
, the hypergeometric series
{}_{2}F_{1}\left(\boldsymbol{\alpha},(1);x\right)
can be reduced modulo
p
for all primes
p
not dividing
d
.
Secondly, the Galois group of
{}_{2}F_{1}\left(\boldsymbol{\alpha},(1);x\right)\bmod p
over
k_{\mathfrak{p}}(x)
always embeds into
\GL_{1}(k_{\mathfrak{p}})=k_{\mathfrak{p}}^{\times}
; in particular, it is commutative.
The following theorem exhibits explicit subgroups of
\GL_{1}(K)=K^{\times}
, which are serious candidates for uniformizing the above Galois groups when
p
varies.

Theorem 1.2
.

We keep the notation and assumptions of Theorem
1.1
and assume moreover that
n=2
,
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}\not\in\mathbb{Z}
and
\beta_{1}=1
.
We set
K^{+}\coloneqq K\cap\mathbb{R}
, we let
m
be the denominator of an irreducible fraction representing
\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}
, and we define the subgroup
G
of
\GL_{1}(K)
by

G\coloneqq\begin{cases}\GL_{1}(K)&\text{if $K=K^{+}$}\\
\GL_{1}(K^{+})\cdot\langle\xi-\bar{\xi}\rangle&\text{if $K\neq K^{+}$ and $m=2$}\\
\GL_{1}(K^{+})\cdot\langle 1+\zeta_{m}\rangle&\text{if $K\neq K^{+}$ and $m\neq 2$}\end{cases}

where
\xi
is an element of
K\setminus K^{+}
,
\bar{\xi}
is its complex conjugate,

\zeta_{m}=\exp(2\mathbf{i}\pi/m)
is a primitive
m
-th root of unity and the notation
\langle\,\cdot\,\rangle
stands for the generated subgroup.

Then, for any prime number
p
which does not divide
d
and any prime
\mathfrak{p}
of
K
above
p
, we have

\Gal\big({}_{2}F_{1}\left(\boldsymbol{\alpha},(1);x\right)\bmod p\mid k_{\mathfrak{p}}(x)\big)\subseteq\image\big(G\cap\GL_{1}(\mathcal{O}_{(p)})\rightarrow\GL_{1}(k_{\mathfrak{p}})\big),

where
\mathcal{O}_{(\mathfrak{p})}
is the localization of the ring of integers of
K
at
\mathfrak{p}
.

Moreover, the above inclusion is an equality when
d\in\{2,3,4,6,8,12,24\}
.

We actually expect that the inclusion of Theorem
1.2
is (almost) always an equality but, unfortunately, we were not able to prove it in full generality.
If our expectation is correct, this would say that the group
G
of Theorem
1.2
uniformizes the Galois groups of
{}_{2}F_{1}\left(\boldsymbol{\alpha},(1);x\right)\bmod p
.
For this reason, it appears as a very important invariant attached to the hypergeometric function
{}_{2}F_{1}\left(\boldsymbol{\alpha},(1);x\right)
.
Nonetheless, it remains quite mysterious to us: it looks like it is built from several pieces of different nature but, so far, we do not have a clear understanding of where these parts come from.

A general picture of what we expect

In this section, we consider the general class of
D
-finite series
f(x)
and
aim at drawing a general picture of the behavior of the Galois groups of
f(x)\bmod p
(when it is defined) when
p
varies.
Let us first recall from the introduction that a series
f(x)\in\mathbb{Q}[\![x]\!]
is
D
-finite
(or
differentiably finite
) if it is solution of a linear differential equation of the form

f^{(r)}(x)+a_{r-1}(x)f^{(r-1)}(x)+\cdots+a_{1}(x)f^{\prime}(x)+a_{0}(x)f(x)=0

(2.0.1)

with
a_{i}(x)\in\mathbb{Q}(x)
for all
i
.
It is a standard fact that
D
-finiteness translates to a recurrence relation on the coefficients of
f(x)
.
It is also well known that the class of
D
-finite series is closed under sums and products; in other words, it forms a subring of
\mathbb{Q}[\![x]\!]

[
Sta80
]
.

On the Galois side, we introduce the following notation.

Definition 2.0.1
.

Let
K
be a field and let
K^{\text{\rm sep}}
be a separable closure of
K
.
For an element
a\in K^{\text{\rm sep}}
, we set
\Gal(a\mid K)\coloneqq\Gal(L/K)
where
L
denotes the extension of
K
generated by
a
and its Galois conjugates.

From now on, we fix a separable closure
\mathbb{F}_{p}(x)^{\text{\rm sep}}
of
\mathbb{F}_{p}(x)
.
Since the extension
\mathbb{F}_{p}(\!(x)\!)/\mathbb{F}_{p}(x)
is itself separable, any algebraic series
f(x)\in\mathbb{F}_{p}[\![x]\!]
is automatically separable over
\mathbb{F}_{p}(x)

and thus can be considered (noncanonically) as an element of
\mathbb{F}_{p}(x)^{\text{\rm sep}}
.
It then makes sense to talk about
\Gal\big(f(x)\mid\mathbb{F}_{p}(x)\big)
and we check that this Galois group does not depend, up to isomorphism, on the choice of the representative of
f(x)
in
\mathbb{F}_{p}(x)^{\text{\rm sep}}
.

In what follows, if
f(x)
is a series with coefficients in
\mathbb{Z}_{(p)}
and
f(x)\bmod p
is algebraic, we shall often slightly abuse notation and write
\Gal\big(f(x)\mid\mathbb{F}_{p}(x)\big)
for
\Gal\big(f(x)\bmod p\mid\mathbb{F}_{p}(x)\big)
.

2.1
Getting intuition by examples

In this introductory subsection, we present and discuss various examples, illustrating the most important phenomena we have observed.
For each example
f(x)
, we will determine the primes
p
for which
f(x)\bmod p
is well-defined and algebraic and compute (or at least estimate) the corresponding Galois groups.

In the next subsections, we will rely on these results to elaborate our conjectures and justify their statements.

2.1.1
f(x)=(1-x)^{a/d}

We begin with a simple example, which is already algebraic over
\mathbb{Q}(x)
.
Before proceeding, we underline that the expansion

f(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{k}\cdot\frac{a}{d}\cdot\left(\frac{a}{d}-1\right)\cdots\left(\frac{a}{d}-k+1\right)\cdot\frac{x^{k}}{k!}

shows that
f(x)
is indeed in
\mathbb{Q}[\![x]\!]
.
We also note that it is also
D
-finite
1
1

1

In fact, it is a standard result that algebraic functions are always
D
-finite.
since it is solution of the differential equation

d\cdot(1-x)f^{\prime}(x)-af(x)=0
.

As
f(x)
is itself algebraic over
\mathbb{Q}(x)
, we start by computing its Galois group over
\mathbb{Q}(x)
.
For this, assuming that
a
and
d
are coprime, we observe that the conjugates of
f(x)
are the functions
\zeta_{d}^{v}f(x)
(
0\leq v<d
) where
\zeta_{d}
is a primitive
d
-th root of unity, namely
\zeta_{d}=\exp(\frac{2\mathbf{i}\pi}{d})\in\mathbb{C}
.
The corresponding splitting field is
\mathbb{Q}(x)\big(\zeta_{d},f(x)\big)
, which can be viewed as a Kummer extension on top of a cyclotomic extension:

\mathbb{Q}(x)

\mathbb{Q}(x)\big(\zeta_{d}\big)

\mathbb{Q}(x)\big(\zeta_{d},f(x)\big)

cyclotomic extension
Kummer extension

The Galois group of the cyclotomic extension is
(\mathbb{Z}/d\mathbb{Z})^{\times}
, while that of the Kummer extension is cyclic of order
d
, and hence isomorphic to
\mathbb{Z}/d\mathbb{Z}
.
We deduce from this that

\Gal\big(f(x)\mid\mathbb{Q}(x)\big)\simeq\mathbb{Z}/d\mathbb{Z}\rtimes(\mathbb{Z}/d\mathbb{Z})^{\times}

where
(\mathbb{Z}/d\mathbb{Z})^{\times}
acts on
\mathbb{Z}/d\mathbb{Z}
by multiplication.
Concretely, the action of the Galois group on
\mathbb{Q}(x)\big(\zeta_{d},f(x)\big)
is given by
\zeta_{d}\mapsto\zeta_{d}^{u}
,
f(x)\mapsto\zeta_{d}^{v}f(x)
for
u\in(\mathbb{Z}/d\mathbb{Z})^{\times}
and
v\in\mathbb{Z}/d\mathbb{Z}
.

The series
f(x)
can be properly reduced modulo
p
for any prime number
p
which does not divide
d
.
Assuming this, we can compute
\Gal\big(f(x)\mid\mathbb{F}_{p}(x)\big)
by applying the same strategy as in characteristic zero.
The only difference is that the Galois group of the cyclotomic part can now be a
strict
subgroup of
(\mathbb{Z}/d\mathbb{Z})^{\times}
.
More precisely, it is the same as the Galois group of
\mathbb{F}_{p}(\xi_{d})/\mathbb{F}_{p}
where
\xi_{d}
is a primitive
d
-th root of unity in an algebraic closure of
\mathbb{F}_{p}
.
This Galois group being generated by the Frobenius
z\mapsto z^{p}
, we conclude that
\Gal\big(\mathbb{F}_{p}(x)(\xi_{d})/\mathbb{F}_{p}(x)\big)
is the subgroup of
(\mathbb{Z}/d\mathbb{Z})^{\times}
generated by
p
.
Therefore, we finally find

\Gal\big(f(x)\mid\mathbb{F}_{p}(x)\big)\simeq\mathbb{Z}/d\mathbb{Z}\rtimes\langle p\bmod d\rangle.

Altogether this shows that there are only finitely many possibilities for
\Gal\big(f(x)\mid\mathbb{F}_{p}(x)\big)
when
p
varies.
More precisely, this Galois group is entirely determined by the class of
p
modulo
d
.

2.1.2
f(x)=\exp(\arctan x)

In this example, the function
f(x)
is transcendental over
\mathbb{Q}(x)
but still it is the solution of a linear differential equation of order
1
, given that

\frac{f^{\prime}(x)}{f(x)}=\arctan^{\prime}(x)=\frac{1}{x^{2}+1}.

(2.1.1)

Using Dwork’s criterion
[
Rob00
, p.409]
, one proves that
f(x)\in\mathbb{Z}_{(p)}[\![x]\!]
if and only if
p\equiv 1\pmod{4}
(see also
[
BCR24
, Example 3.4]
).
For those primes, it turns out that the differential equation (
2.1.1
) can be solved over the
p
-adics.

Let
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
be the ring of
p
-adic integers and let
i\in\mathbb{Z}_{p}
be a square root of
-1
(which exists because
p
is congruent to
1
modulo
4
).
Over
\mathbb{Z}_{p}(x)
, we have the following partial fraction decomposition:

\frac{1}{x^{2}+1}=\frac{1}{2}\cdot\left(\frac{1}{1+ix}+\frac{1}{1-ix}\right).

Solving Equation (
2.1.1
) and taking care of the initial condition
f(0)=1
, we obtain the following closed formula for
f(x)
:

f(x)=\left(1+ix\right)^{-i/2}\cdot\left(1-ix\right)^{i/2}\in\mathbb{Z}_{p}[\![x]\!].

(2.1.2)

Here the
p
-adic exponentiation is defined through its
p
-adic expansion; namely, for
a\in\mathbb{Z}_{p}
and a formal expression
u
, we set

(1+u)^{a}\coloneqq\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{a\cdot(a-1)\cdots(a-k+1)}{k!}\cdot u^{k}.

We now write the
p
-adic expansions of the exponents
-i/2
and
i/2
as follows:

\textstyle-\frac{i}{2}=a_{0}+pa_{1}+p^{2}a_{2}+\cdots\quad\text{and}\quad\frac{i}{2}=b_{0}+pb_{1}+p^{2}b_{2}+\cdots

with
a_{k},b_{k}\in\{0,1,\ldots,p{-}1\}
.
Reducing Equation (
2.1.2
) modulo

then gives

f(x)\bmod p=\prod_{k=0}^{\infty}\big(1+ix^{p^{k}}\big)^{a_{k}}\cdot\big(1-ix^{p^{k}}\big)^{b_{k}}\in\mathbb{F}_{p}[\![x]\!],

where
i\equiv 2b_{0}\pmod{p}
.

Proposition 2.1.1
.

For all primes
p
congruent to
1
modulo
4
, the function
f(x)\bmod p
is transcendental over
\mathbb{F}_{p}(x)
.

Proof.

For an integer
u\in\{0,\ldots,p{-}1\}
, we define
g_{u}(x)\coloneqq(1+ix)^{u}\cdot(1-ix)^{p-1-u}
and,
for a sequence
\underline{u}=(u_{k})_{k\geq 0}
, we set

g_{\underline{u}}(x)\coloneqq\prod_{k=0}^{\infty}g_{u_{k}}\big(x^{p^{k}}\big)=\prod_{k=0}^{\infty}g_{u_{k}}(x)^{p^{k}}.

Noticing that
a_{0}+b_{0}=p
and
a_{k}+b_{k}=p{-}1
as soon as
k\geq 1
,
we find
f(x)=(1-ix)\cdot g_{\underline{a}}(x)
in
\mathbb{F}_{p}[\![x]\!]
where
\underline{a}=(a_{k})_{k\geq 0}
.
Hence it is enough to prove that
g_{\underline{a}}(x)
is transcendental.

For
r\in\{0,\ldots,p{-}1\}
, we introduce the section operator

\sigma_{r}:\mathbb{F}_{p}[\![x]\!]\to\mathbb{F}_{p}[\![x]\!],\quad\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a_{n}x^{n}\mapsto\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a_{pn+r}x^{n}.

The action of
\sigma_{r}
on the series
g_{\underline{u}}(x)
is easily described.
Indeed, remarking that
\sigma_{r}(g(x)^{p}h(x))=g(x)\sigma_{r}(h(x))
for all series
g(x)
and
h(x)
, we obtain the relation

\sigma_{r}\big(g_{\underline{u}}(x)\big)=\sigma_{r}\big(g_{u_{0}}(x)\big)\cdot g_{S\underline{u}}(x)

where
S\underline{u}
is the shifted sequence defined by
(S\underline{u})_{k}=u_{k+1}
for
k\in\mathbb{N}
.
We notice in addition that the prefactor
\sigma_{r}(g_{u_{0}}(x))
is a scalar in
\mathbb{F}_{p}
given that
g_{u_{0}}(x)
is a polynomial of degree
p{-}1
.
Moreover, when
r=0
, this scalar is
1
and we just have
\sigma_{0}(g_{\underline{u}}(x))=g_{S\underline{u}}(x)
.

We now use the following characterization of algebraicity, known as Christol’s theorem
[
Chr79
,
Chr+80
]
:
a series
g(x)\in\mathbb{F}_{p}[\![x]\!]
is algebraic over
\mathbb{F}_{p}(x)
if and only if there exists a finite set which contains
g(x)
and is stable by the
\sigma_{r}
.
In our case of interest, it is then enough to prove that the series
\sigma_{0}^{k}(g_{\underline{a}}(x))=g_{S^{k}\underline{a}}(x)
are pairwise distinct.

Let
\ell>k
be two nonnegative integers.
We first notice that the sequences
S^{k}\underline{a}
and
S^{\ell}\underline{a}
cannot be equal.
Indeed, from their coincidence, we would deduce that
\underline{a}
is ultimately periodic (with period
\ell{-}k
), which is not the case because
i\not\in\mathbb{Q}
.
We now fix an integer
n
such that restricting to the
n
first terms is enough to distinguish between
S^{k}\underline{a}
and
S^{\ell}\underline{a}
.
An easy computation shows that

\displaystyle g_{S^{k}\underline{a}}(x)

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{1}{1-ix}\cdot\left(\frac{1+ix}{1-ix}\right)^{a_{k}+pa_{k+1}+\cdots+p^{n-1}a_{k+n-1}}\pmod{x^{p^{n}}}

\displaystyle g_{S^{\ell}\underline{a}}(x)

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{1}{1-ix}\cdot\left(\frac{1+ix}{1-ix}\right)^{a_{\ell}+pa_{\ell+1}+\cdots+p^{n-1}a_{\ell+n-1}}\pmod{x^{p^{n}}}

Thanks to our choice of
n
, the exponents appearing in the above expressions are distinct.
Observing moreover that the change of variables
y=\frac{1+ix}{1-ix}
is invertible in
\mathbb{F}_{p}[\![x]\!]
(with inverse
x=i{\cdot}\frac{y-1}{y+1}
),
we conclude that
g_{S^{k}\underline{a}}(x)
and
g_{S^{\ell}\underline{a}}(x)
are not congruent modulo
x^{p^{n}}
, and so that the are not equal.
∎

Examples

(Case of Example

•

2.1.3
\displaystyle f(x)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\binom{2n}{n}^{\!2}\cdot x^{n}

Before computing Galois groups, we briefly mention that the series
f(x)
is indeed
D
-finite as the sequence of its coefficients obviously satisfies a recurrence relation with polynomial coefficients.
Concretely,
f(x)
is a solution of the differential equation

x(16x-1)f^{\prime\prime}(x)+(32x-1)f^{\prime}(x)+4f(x)=0.

(2.1.3)

Apart from this, the function
f(x)
is also one of the simplest examples of a series exhibiting the
p
-Lucas property, which will play a very important role throughout this paper.
To explain what it means, we pick a nonnegative integer
n
and write the Euclidean division of
n
by
p
as follows:
n=u+pv
.
We then have the congruence

(1+x)^{2n}\equiv(1+x)^{2u}\cdot(1+x^{p})^{2v}\pmod{p},

which, after extracting the coefficient of
x^{n}
, leads to

\binom{2n}{n}\equiv\binom{2u}{u}\cdot\binom{2v}{v}\pmod{p}.

Raising this congruence to the square, we deduce that the coefficients
a_{n}
of
f(x)
satisfy the congruence

a_{n}\equiv a_{u}a_{v}\pmod{p}
as well; we say that the series
f(x)
is
p
-Lucas
.

The
p
-Lucas property has strong consequences on the Galois groups we are interested in.
Indeed, defining the truncation

A_{p}(x)=\sum_{n=0}^{p-1}a_{n}x^{n}\in\mathbb{F}_{p}[x],

being
p
-Lucas translates to the algebraic relation

f(x)\equiv A_{p}(x)\cdot f(x)^{p}\pmod{p}.

In particular,
f(x)\bmod p
is algebraic and, even better, it appears as a
(p{-}1)
-th root of the polynomial
A_{p}(x)
.
Since
\mathbb{F}_{p}
contains all the
(p{-}1)
-th roots of unity, Kummer’s theory ensures that

\Gal\big(f(x)\mid\mathbb{F}_{p}(x)\big)
embeds into
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{p-1}(\mathbb{F}_{p})=\mathbb{F}_{p}^{\times}
and that equality holds as soon as
A_{p}(x)
is a not a power (with exponent at least
2
) of a rational function.

The following lemma will be used many times throughout the rest of the paper. It helps to determine the multiplicity of certain polynomials tied to solutions of differential equations.

Lemma 2.1.4
.

Let
g(x)\in\mathbb{F}_{p}[\![x]\!]
be a power series, which satisfies a linear differential equation of order
r<p
with polynomials coefficients.
Let
\bar{\mathbb{F}}_{p}
denote a fixed algebraic closure of
\mathbb{F}_{p}
and let
\xi\in\bar{\mathbb{F}}_{p}
be an ordinary point of that differential equation.
If
g(x)=A(x)h(x^{p})
for some polynomial
A(x)
of degree less than
p
and some power series
h(x)\in\mathbb{F}_{p}[\![x]\!]
, then
\val_{\xi}(A(x))<r
.

In particular, if
g(x)
is
p
-Lucas, then
\val_{\xi}(g(x)\bmod x^{p})<r
.

Proof.

From
g(x)=A(x)h(x^{p})
, it is clear that
A(x)
is a solution of the same differential equation as
g(x)
. The local exponents of this equation at
\xi
are
0,1,\ldots,r-1
. Thus,
\val_{\xi}(A(x))\in\{0,1,\ldots,r-1\}\bmod p
. Because
\deg(A(x))<p
, we conclude
\val_{\xi}(A(x))\in\{0,1,\ldots,r-1\}
.
∎

Back to our example of the generating function of the squares of the central binomial coefficients, we can use Lemma
2.1.4
to obtain the following.

Corollary 2.1.5
.

If
p>2
,
the polynomial
A_{p}(x)
cannot be written as
B(x)^{e}
with
B(x)\in\mathbb{F}_{p}(x)
and
e>1
.

Proof.

To start with, we observe that
A_{p}(x)
is a polynomial of degree exactly
\frac{p-1}{2}
, because, when
\frac{p}{2}\leq n<p
, the central binomial coefficient
\binom{2n}{n}
is divisible by
p
.
Moreover, any element
\xi\in\bar{\mathbb{F}}_{p}\setminus\big\{0,\frac{1}{16}\big\}
is an ordinary point of the differential equation (
2.1.3
). By Lemma
2.1.4
, we find that
\xi
cannot be a double root of
A_{p}(x)
.
Clearly
A_{p}(0)=1
, so
0
is also not a (double) root of
A_{p}(x)
.
To conclude, it then remains to exclude the case
A_{p}(x)=(1-16x)^{\frac{p-1}{2}}
.
This can be done easily by looking at the coefficient of
x
: in
A_{p}(x)
, it is equal to
4
, while in the right-hand side, it is equal to
-8(p-1)
, which is
8
modulo
p
.
∎

It follows from all what precedes that
\Gal\big(f(x)\mid\mathbb{F}_{p}(x)\big)\simeq\mathbb{F}_{p}^{\times}
for
p>2
.
The isomorphism is actually also correct when
p=2
because
f(x)\bmod p=1
in this case.

Remark 2.1.6
.

Our computations of the Galois group also give a proof that
f(x)
is transcendental over
\mathbb{Q}(x)
. Indeed, assume it were algebraic of given algebraicity degree

. Then also

would have algebraicity degree at most

, hence the size of its Galois group would be bounded independently of

. However, we have just shown that this is not the case. The same argument will also apply to show the transcendence of the next examples we are going to consider.

Another proof of the transcendence of

can be easily deduced by rewriting it as a hypergeometric function and applying the classification of algebraic hypergeometric functions which we will recall in Theorem
3.1.7
.

2.1.4
\displaystyle f(x)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\binom{2n}{n}^{\!3}\cdot x^{n}

This example looks similar to the previous one (the only difference is that the exponent

has been replaced by

)
but we will see that the behavior of the Galois groups exhibits a new interesting phenomenon.

The function

is still
D
-finite and is now a solution of a differential equation of order

x^{2}(64x-1)f^{\prime\prime\prime}(x)+x(288x-3)f^{\prime\prime}(x)+(208x-1)f^{\prime}(x)+8f(x)=0.

Of course,

is again

-Lucas, from which we get the algebraic relation

f(x)\equiv A_{p}(x)\cdot f(x)^{p}\pmod{p},

where
A_{p}(x)
is the truncation of
f(x)
defined by

A_{p}(x)=\sum_{n=0}^{p-1}\binom{2n}{n}^{\!3}\cdot x^{n}\in\mathbb{F}_{p}[x].

Again, this implies that
\Gal\big(f(x)\mid\mathbb{F}_{p}(x)\big)
embeds into

but it might happen now that this inclusion is strict.

Lemma 2.1.7
.

Let
p
be prime number with
p\equiv 1\pmod{4}
.
Then
A_{p}(x)
is a square in
\mathbb{F}_{p}[x]
.

Proof.

We reinterpret the function
f(x)
as an hypergeometric series as follows:

\textstyle f(x)={}_{3}F_{2}\left(\big(\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}\big),(1,1);64x\right).

It then follows from Clausen’s formula
[
Cla28
]
that
f(x)=g(x)^{2}
with

\textstyle g(x)={}_{2}F_{1}\left(\big(\frac{1}{4},\frac{1}{4}\big),(1);64x\right)
.
We now let
B_{p}(x)
denote the truncation at
x^{p}
of
g(x)\bmod p
, so that

A_{p}(x)\equiv B_{p}(x)^{2}\pmod{x^{p}}.

(2.1.4)

As in the proof of Corollary
2.1.5
, we find that
A_{p}(x)
has degree
\frac{p-1}{2}
.
Similarly, using the congruence
p\equiv 1\pmod{4}
, we prove that
\deg B_{p}(x)=\frac{p-1}{4}
.
Hence the congruence (
2.1.4
) has to be an equality in
\mathbb{F}_{p}[x]
, which proves the lemma.
∎

By Lemma
2.1.4
, we have that
A_{p}(x)
is not an
e
-th power for any

.
Moreover, when
p\equiv 3\pmod{4}
, it is not a square either because it has odd degree.
We conclude that

\Gal\big(f(x)\mid\mathbb{F}_{p}(x)\big)\simeq\begin{cases}(\mathbb{F}_{p}^{\times})^{\square}&\text{if }p\equiv 1\pmod{4}\\
\mathbb{F}_{p}^{\times}&\text{if }p\equiv 3\pmod{4}\end{cases},

(2.1.5)

where, when
G
is a commutative group, the notation

refers to its subgroup of squares.

2.1.5
\displaystyle f(x)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{n+k}{k}^{\!2}\binom{n}{k}^{\!2}\cdot x^{n}
and related functions

The series
f(x)
is the so-called

because the sequence of its coefficients appears as a key ingredient in Apéry’s proof of the irrationality of
\zeta(3)

[

,

]
.
It is solution of the differential equation

x^{2}(x^{2}-34x+1)f^{\prime\prime\prime}(x)+3x(2x^{2}-51x+1)f^{\prime\prime}(x)+(7x^{2}-112x+1)f^{\prime}(x)+(x-5)f(x)=0

and it turns out that the reductions of

modulo the primes behave quite similarly to what we described in the previous example.
First of all, it is known that
f(x)
is
p
-Lucas
[

]

satisfy the algebraic relation

f(x)\equiv A_{p}(x)\cdot f(x)^{p}\pmod{p}

where
A_{p}(x)
is the truncation of
f(x)
:

A_{p}(x)=\sum_{n=0}^{p-1}\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{n+k}{k}^{\!2}\binom{n}{k}^{\!2}\cdot x^{n}\in\mathbb{F}_{p}[x].

As a consequence the Galois group
\Gal\big(f(x)\mid\mathbb{F}_{p}(x)\big)
appears as a subgroup of
\mathbb{F}_{p}^{\times}
.
Moreover since the order of the differential equation (

) is

, Lemma

shows that

cannot be an

(except if it is a constant polynomial).
However it still can be a square and, as we will see in the sequel, this actually happens from time to time.

Computational experiments suggest that whether
A_{p}(x)
is a square or not is governed by the congruence class of

modulo
24
.
More precisely, we observed the following.

Heuristic observation 2.1.8
.

There exists a polynomial
B_{p}(x)\in\mathbb{F}_{p}[x]
such that

•

A_{p}(x)=B_{p}(x)^{2}
if

,

•

A_{p}(x)=(x^{2}-34x+1)\cdot B_{p}(x)^{2}
if
p\equiv 13,17,19,23\pmod{24}
.

We leave the above heuristic as an open question for the time being and explore its consequences.
First of all, at the level of Galois groups, it implies

\Gal\big(f(x)\mid\mathbb{F}_{p}(x)\big)\simeq\begin{cases}(\mathbb{F}_{p}^{\times})^{\square}&\text{if }p\equiv 1,5,7,11\pmod{24}\\
\mathbb{F}_{p}^{\times}&\text{if }p\equiv 13,17,19,23\pmod{24}\end{cases}

given that the polynomial

is not a square in
\mathbb{F}_{p}[x]
as soon as

.

Going further, Heuristic

suggests to introduce the following functions:

Those can be expanded as series in the variable

, and so they are elements of

.
Moreover, it turns out that they are both

-finite, being solutions of the differential equations

\displaystyle 4x(x^{2}-34x+1)g^{\prime\prime}(x)+4(2x^{2}-51x+1)g^{\prime}(x)+(x-10)g(x)

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle 4x(x^{2}-34x+1)h^{\prime\prime}(x)+4(4x^{2}-85x+1)h^{\prime}(x)+(9x-78)h(x)

\displaystyle=0.

Of course,

and

are also algebraic and one can be interested in their Galois groups.
When

, we have

g(x)=\frac{g(x)^{p}}{\sqrt{f(x)^{p-1}}}\equiv B_{p}(x)\cdot g(x)^{p}\pmod{p}

and similarly

is a not an

\Gal\big(g(x)\mid\mathbb{F}_{p}(x)\big)\simeq\Gal\big(h(x)\mid\mathbb{F}_{p}(x)\big)\simeq\mathbb{F}_{p}^{\times}.

On the contrary, when
A_{p}(x)=(x^{2}-34x+1)\cdot B_{p}(x)^{2}
, we get the relations

\displaystyle g(x)

\displaystyle\equiv(x^{2}-34x+1)^{\makebox{${}^{\frac{p{+}1}{2}}$}}B_{p}(x)\cdot h(x)^{p}\pmod{p}

(2.1.7)

\displaystyle h(x)

\displaystyle\equiv B_{p}(x)\cdot g(x)^{p}\pmod{p}

(2.1.8)

which now intertwine the functions
g(x)
and

.
Combining these equations, we deduce the untangled algebraic relations

\displaystyle g(x)

\displaystyle\equiv(x^{2}-34x+1)^{\makebox{${}^{\frac{p{+}1}{2}}$}}B_{p}(x)^{p+1}\cdot g(x)^{p^{2}}\pmod{p}

(2.1.9)

\displaystyle h(x)

\displaystyle\equiv(x^{2}-34x+1)^{\makebox{${}^{\frac{p(p{+}1)}{2}}$}}B_{p}(x)^{p+1}\cdot h(x)^{p^{2}}\pmod{p}.

(2.1.10)

We observe that the coefficients of

and of

on the right-hand sides of these equations both are

-th powers, but not higher ones. So, at the level of Galois groups, we obtain

\Gal\big(g(x)\mid\mathbb{F}_{p}(x)\big)\simeq\Gal\big(h(x)\mid\mathbb{F}_{p}(x)\big)\simeq\sqrt{\mathbb{F}_{p}^{\times}}\rtimes\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z},

where by definition

\sqrt{\mathbb{F}_{p}^{\times}}\coloneqq\Big\{\,x\in\mathbb{F}_{p^{2}}^{\times}\,\,\text{such that}\,\,x^{2}\in\mathbb{F}_{p}^{\times}\,\Big\}

and where

acts on it through the Frobenius.

Remark 2.1.9
.

In these examples, we observe that both of the Galois groups
\Gal\big(g(x)\mid\mathbb{F}_{p}(x)\big)
and

might be commutative for infinitely many primes

and noncommutative for another infinity of primes
p
at the same time (at least if we believe in Heuristic
2.1.8
).

We actually expect that all these inclusions are equalities but were not able to prove it in full generality.

This example is novel in the sense that, for the first time, it goes beyond cyclic groups (or simple semi-direct products of them) and involves groups of matrices of higher rank.
We see moreover that the latter does not show up in each case: for some classes of congruences, the expected Galois group remains resoluble while it suddenly becomes more complicated for other congruences classes.

2.2
The conjectures: weak and strong forms

The aim of this subsection is to formulate plausible conjectures describing the behavior of
\Gal\big(f(x)\mid\mathbb{F}_{p}(x)\big)
when
p
varies.

2.2.1
Discarding too naive expectations

Since
\Gal\big(f(x)\mid\mathbb{F}_{p}(x)\big)
always appears as a subgroup of
\GL_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{p})
for some integer
n
(depending possibly on
p
),
the first naive question we may wonder about is the following: does there exist a subgroup of
\GL_{n}(\mathbb{Z})
whose reduction modulo
p
agrees with
\Gal\big(f(x)\mid\mathbb{F}_{p}(x)\big)
for (almost) all primes
p
?
Unfortunately, the answer is negative, even if we replace
\mathbb{Z}
by
\mathbb{Z}[\frac{1}{N}]
for a positive integer
N
.
Indeed, Subsection
2.1.5
exhibits a situation where
\Gal(g(x)\mid\mathbb{F}_{p}(x))
is commutative for half of the primes and noncommutative for the other half;
the next lemma shows that this cannot occur for the reductions modulo
p
of a single subgroup of
\GL_{n}\big(\mathbb{Z}[\frac{1}{N}]\big)
.

Lemma 2.2.1
.

Let
G
be a subgroup of
\GL_{n}\big(\mathbb{Z}[\frac{1}{N}]\big)
for some positive integers
n
and
N
.
We assume that, for infinitely many primes
p
, the image of
G
in
\GL_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{p})
is commutative.
Then
G
is commutative.

Proof.

We pick
A,B\in G
and form the commutator
C=ABA^{-1}B^{-1}
.
For any prime
p
not dividing
N
, the matrix
C\bmod p
is the commutator of
A\bmod p
and
B\bmod p
.
Thanks to our assumption, these commutators are trivial for infinitely many such primes
p
.
Hence, denoting by
I_{n}
the identity matrix, we get
C\equiv I_{n}\pmod{p}
for infinitely many primes
p
.
Therefore
C=I_{n}
and we conclude that
A
and
B
commute in
G
.
∎

Remark 2.2.2
.

One may argue that the issue we just raised with the function
g(x)
, defined as the square root of the Apéry series in Example
2.1.5
, can be easily fixed by considering Galois groups over
\mathbb{F}_{p^{2}}(x)
instead of
\mathbb{F}_{p}(x)
:
doing this, the factors
\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}
disappear and all the Galois groups
\Gal(g(x)\mid\mathbb{F}_{p^{2}}(x))
are commutative. This is indeed correct.
However, Example
2.1.6
shows an even worse situation where half of the groups are resoluble, while the other half of them are not; in this case, the issue cannot be solved by changing the base field.

If one unique subgroup of
\GL_{n}\big(\mathbb{Z}[\frac{1}{N}]\big)
is not enough to uniformize, one may now wonder if a finite family of them could do the job.
Unfortunately, this is again not the case: a counterexample is given by Example
2.1.3
, as shown by the next proposition.

Proposition 2.2.3
.

Let
t,n
, and
N
be positive integers and let
G_{1},\ldots,G_{t}
be subgroups of
\GL_{n}\!\big(\mathbb{Z}[\frac{1}{N}]\big)
.
Then, there exist infinitely many prime numbers
p
, not dividing
N
, for which none of the groups
G_{1}\bmod p,\ldots,G_{t}\bmod p
are cyclic of order
p{-}1
.

Proof.

For
i\in\{1,\ldots,t\}
, we let
\mathcal{P}_{i}
denote the set of primes
p
, not dividing
N
, for which
G_{i}\mod p
is cyclic of order
p{-}1
.
We also set
\mathcal{P}\coloneqq\mathcal{P}_{1}\cup\cdots\cup\mathcal{P}_{t}
and denote by
\mathcal{P}^{\prime}
the complement of
\mathcal{P}
in the set of all primes.
We have to show that
\mathcal{P}^{\prime}
is infinite.

Without loss of generality, we may assume that each
\mathcal{P}_{i}
is infinite.
Indeed, when
\mathcal{P}_{i}
is finite, the lemma for the family
(G_{1},\ldots,G_{i-1},G_{i+1},\ldots,G_{t})
implies the lemma for
(G_{1},\ldots,G_{t})
.

We fix an index
i
. We claim that all matrices in
G_{i}
are semi-simple,
i.e.
, diagonalizable over an algebraic closure
\bar{\mathbb{Q}}
.
Indeed, pick
M\in G_{i}
and a change-of-basis matrix
A\in\GL_{n}(\bar{\mathbb{Q}})
such that
T\coloneqq A^{-1}MA
is in Jordan form.
We notice that
A
has all its entries in a certain number field
K
. Besides,
A
and
M
can be reduced modulo
p
for almost all prime numbers
p
.
We assume by contradiction that
T
has a nontrivial Jordan block of the form

T_{0}\coloneqq\left(\begin{matrix}\lambda&1\\
&\ddots&\ddots\\
&&\lambda&1\\
&&&\lambda\end{matrix}\right).

For almost all primes
p\in\mathcal{P}_{i}
, we have

T_{0}^{p-1}\equiv\left(\begin{matrix}\lambda^{p-1}&-\lambda^{p-2}&\star&\star\\
&\ddots&\ddots&\star\\
&&\lambda^{p-1}&-\lambda^{p-2}\\
&&&\lambda^{p-1}\end{matrix}\right)\pmod{p}.

From the fact that
G_{i}\bmod p
is cyclic of order
p{-}1
, we deduce that
\lambda
must vanish modulo
p
. Since this holds for infinitely many primes
p
, we finally obtain
\lambda=0
, which is a contradiction.

On the other hand, we deduce from Lemma
2.2.1
that
G_{i}
is commutative.
Consequently, all matrices in
G_{i}
are simultaneously diagonalizable,

i.e.
, there exists a matrix
A\in\GL_{n}(K_{i})
(for a certain number field
K_{i}
) such that
A^{-1}MA
is diagonal for all
M\in G_{i}
.
Up to enlarging
N
, we may further assume that
A\in\GL_{n}\!\big(\mathcal{O}_{K_{i}}[\frac{1}{N}]\big)
where
\mathcal{O}_{K_{i}}
denotes the ring of integers of
K_{i}
.
Let
H_{i}
be the subgroup of
\mathcal{O}_{K_{i}}[\frac{1}{N}]^{\times}
generated by all the eigenvalues of all the matrices
M\in G_{i}
.
By Dirichlet’s theorem, we know that
\mathcal{O}_{K_{i}}[\frac{1}{N}]^{\times}
is of finite type. Hence
H_{i}
is also and we can choose a finite set of generators
S_{i}
of
H_{i}
.

We now uniformize this construction over
i
: we let
K
be the number field generated by all the
K_{i}
and set
S\coloneqq S_{1}\cup\cdots\cup S_{t}
.
By Chebotarev’s density theorem applied to the number field
L=K(\sqrt{s},s\in S)
,
there exist infinitely many primes
p
such that
p
splits in
K
and
s\bmod\mathfrak{p}
is a square in
\mathbb{F}_{p}
for all
s\in S
and all primes
\mathfrak{p}
of
K
above
p
.
For those primes, all groups
G_{i}
reduce modulo
p
to a subgroup of
(\mathbb{F}_{p}^{\times})^{\square}
and hence cannot have order
p{-}1
.
∎

2.2.2
First formulation of the conjecture

The previous discussion leaves open the possibility that the Galois groups
\Gal\big(f(x)\mid\mathbb{F}_{p}(x)\big)
are uniformized by a finite family
(G_{1},\ldots,G_{t})
of subgroups of
\GL_{n}\!(\mathbb{Q})
in the following sense:
for each prime
p
, there exists an index
i
such that

\Gal\big(f(x)\mid\mathbb{F}_{p}(x)\big)\simeq\image\big(G_{i}\cap\GL_{n}(\mathbb{Z}_{(p)})\to\GL_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{p})\big),

where we recall that
\mathbb{Z}_{(p)}
denotes the localization of
\mathbb{Z}
at
p
.
At least, this formulation clearly allows for catching
\mathbb{F}_{p}^{\times}=\GL_{1}(\mathbb{F}_{p})
for all
p
, as we can simply take
G=\GL_{1}(\mathbb{Q})
.

Although this expectation looks rather reasonable, many examples encountered in Subsection
2.1
involve the multiplicative group of some finite extension of
\mathbb{F}_{p}
.
It then sounds natural to look for the groups
G_{i}
, not inside
\GL_{n}(\mathbb{Q})
, but rather inside
\GL_{n}(K)
where
K
is a number field.

In a similar fashion, we observe that most of the Galois groups computed in Subsection
2.1
are not exactly subgroups of some
\GL_{n}
but rather
semi-direct products of groups of this type with cyclic groups, the latter corresponding to an unramified initial extension of the form
\mathbb{F}_{q}(x)/\mathbb{F}_{p}(x)
.
This phenomenon is actually a general fact about splitting fields of series in
\mathbb{F}_{p}[\![x]\!]
and can be isolated.

Definition 2.2.4
.

The
residual splitting field
of an algebraic series
f(x)\in\mathbb{F}_{p}[\![x]\!]
is
L\cap\bar{\mathbb{F}}_{p},
where
L\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{p}(x)^{\text{\rm sep}}
is the splitting field of
f(x)
.

Lemma 2.2.5
.

Let
f(x)\in\mathbb{F}_{p}[\![x]\!]
be an algebraic series and let
\ell
be its residual splitting field.
For any subfield
k
of
\bar{\mathbb{F}}_{p}
, we have an exact sequence

1\longrightarrow\Gal\big(f(x)\mid k(x)\big)\longrightarrow\Gal\big(f(x)\mid\mathbb{F}_{p}(x)\big)\longrightarrow\Gal\big(k\cap\ell/\mathbb{F}_{p}\big)\longrightarrow 1.

(2.2.1)

Proof.

For a subfield
k
of
\bar{\mathbb{F}}_{p}
, we let
S_{k}\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{p}(x)^{\text{\rm sep}}
be the splitting field of
f(x)
over
k(x)
.
By definition,
\Gal(f(x)\mid k(x))
is then the Galois group of
S_{k}
over
k(x)
.
For convenience, we also set
S\coloneqq S_{\mathbb{F}_{p}}
.

We consider the field
\mathbb{F}_{p}(x)[f]
. It is a subfield of
\mathbb{F}_{p}(\!(x)\!)
and so it is linearly disjoint from
k
.
As a consequence, the minimal polynomial of
f(x)
over
\mathbb{F}_{p}(x)
remains irreducible over
k(x)
.
Therefore, all the Galois conjugates of
f(x)
over
\mathbb{F}_{p}(x)
are also Galois conjugates over
k(x)
.
It follows that
S_{k}
is the compositum of
S
and
k(x)
,
i.e.
,
S_{k}=S\cdot k(x)
as subfields of
\mathbb{F}_{p}(x)^{\text{\rm sep}}
.
Noticing moreover that
S\cap k(x)=(k\cap\ell)(x)
, the exact sequence (
2.2.1
) follows from classical Galois theory.
∎

It follows from Lemma
2.2.5
that
\Gal(f(x)\mid k(x))
is independent of
k
as soon as
k
contains the residual splitting field of
f(x)
.
This is the part of the Galois group we will be mostly interested in and which is depicted by our conjecture.

Conjecture 2.2.6
.

Let
f(x)\in\mathbb{Q}[\![x]\!]
be a
D
-finite series and let
n
be the minimal order of a differential operator that annihilates
f(x)
.

Then, there exist a number field
K
and a family
(G_{1},\ldots,G_{t})
of subgroups of
\GL_{n}(K)
such that the following holds:
for almost all rational primes
p
for which
f(x)\bmod p
is well defined and algebraic over
\mathbb{F}_{p}(x),
and for any prime
\mathfrak{p}
of
K
above
p
,
there exists an index
i\in\{1,\ldots,t\}
such that

(a)

the residual splitting field of
f(x)\bmod p
is contained in
k_{\mathfrak{p}}
, and

(b)

there exists an (abstract) isomorphism of groups

\Gal\big(f(x)\mid k_{\mathfrak{p}}(x)\big)\simeq\image\big(G_{i}\cap\GL_{n}(\mathcal{O}_{(\mathfrak{p})})\to\GL_{n}(k_{\mathfrak{p}})\big),

where, in what precedes,
\mathcal{O}_{(\mathfrak{p})}
denotes the localization of the ring of integers of
K
at
\mathfrak{p}
and
k_{\mathfrak{p}}
is the residue field of
K
at
\mathfrak{p}
.

We briefly comment Conjecture
2.2.6
on several important points.

First, we remark that the assertion
(a)
above indicates that the Galois group
\Gal(f(x)\mid k_{\mathfrak{p}}(x))
has attained the limit,
in the sense that it is equal to
\Gal(f(x)\mid k(x))
for any extension
k
of
k_{\mathfrak{p}}
sitting inside
\bar{\mathbb{F}}_{p}
.
We deduce from this observation that, if Conjecture
2.2.6
holds with the number field
K
, then it holds as well for any finite extension
K^{\prime}
of
K
with the same family of groups
(G_{1},\ldots,G_{t})
.

Second, we underline that the
n
in
\GL_{n}(K)
,
i.e.
, the size of matrices, is expected to be controlled by the order of a differential operator
\mathcal{L}
with
\mathcal{L}(f(x))=0
.
In fact, more is expected: for a given prime
p
, we actually believe that
\Gal\big(f(x)\mid k_{\mathfrak{p}}(x)\big)
embeds into
\GL_{n_{p}}(k_{\mathfrak{p}})
where
n_{p}
is the
\mathbb{F}_{p}(x^{p})
-dimension of the space of solutions of
\mathcal{L}\bmod p

(observe that
n_{p}\leq n
).
We will come back to these dimension bounds and give more evidences and arguments supporting them in Subsection
2.3
.

Finally, we mention that it is still unclear to us what could be the (minimal) number field
K
in general, although we expect it to be somehow related to the differential Galois group (or maybe the monodromy group) of the minimal differential equation satisfied by
f(x)
. Again, we will give more evidences supporting this hope in Subsection
2.3
.

Back to the examples.

We now come back to the examples of Subsection
2.1
and show that Conjecture
2.2.6
perfectly fits with all of them.

In Example
2.1.1
, the part of the Galois group that the conjecture is concerned with is the factor
\mathbb{Z}/d\mathbb{Z}
, which is always present regardless of the choice of the prime
p
.
Since this factor corresponds to the Galois group over
\mathbb{F}_{p}(x)(\zeta_{d})
, the natural choice for the number field
K
is the cyclotomic extension
K=\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{d})
.
For any prime
\mathfrak{p}
of
K
, we have
\Gal(f(x)\mid k_{\mathfrak{p}}(x))\simeq\mathbb{Z}/d\mathbb{Z}
and we can simply take
t=1
and
G_{1}=\boldsymbol{\mu}_{d}(K)\subseteq\GL_{1}(K)
.

In Example
2.1.2
, there is nothing to comment on, since
f(x)\bmod p
is never algebraic.

In Example
2.1.3
, we can simply take
t=1
and
G=\mathbb{Q}^{\times}=\GL_{1}(\mathbb{Q})
.

In Example
2.1.4
, one option is to take
t=2,
as well as
G_{1}=\GL_{1}(\mathbb{Q})^{\square}
, and
G_{2}=\GL_{1}(\mathbb{Q})
:
when
p\equiv 1\pmod{4}
, we choose the index
i=1
whereas, when
p\equiv 3\pmod{4}
, we choose
i=2
(and we choose either
i=1
or
i=2
when
p=2
).
It is however a nice observation that the Galois groups can actually be uniformized by a unique subgroup
G\subseteq\GL_{1}(\mathbb{Q})
, namely

G\coloneqq\GL_{1}(\mathbb{Q})^{\square}\cdot\{\pm 1\}\subseteq\GL_{1}(\mathbb{Q}).

Indeed, one checks that
G
reduces modulo
p
to
\GL_{1}(\mathbb{F}_{p})^{\square}\cdot\{\pm 1\}
and, now, we remember that
-1
is a square modulo
p
if and only if
p=2
or
p\equiv 1\pmod{4}
.

The Apéry series
f(x)
of Example
2.1.5
is quite similar to what precedes:
either, we can take
G_{1}=\GL_{1}(\mathbb{Q})^{\square}
and
G_{2}=\GL_{1}(\mathbb{Q})
or we can pick just one group which is

G\coloneqq\GL_{1}(\mathbb{Q})^{\square}\cdot\langle-6\rangle\subseteq\GL_{1}(\mathbb{Q}).

Indeed, it follows from the law of quadratic reciprocity that
-6
is a square modulo a prime number
p>3
if and only if
p\equiv 1,5,7,11\pmod{24}
.

For the series
g(x)
and
h(x)
, we observe that the relevant part of
\Gal(g(x)\mid\mathbb{F}_{p}(x))\simeq\Gal(h(x)\mid\mathbb{F}_{p}(x))
is, in some sense, the “square root” of
\Gal(f(x)\mid\mathbb{F}_{p}(x))
.
This observation leads us to consider the number field
K=\mathbb{Q}(\mathbf{i}\sqrt{6})
together with the group

G\coloneqq\GL_{1}(\mathbb{Q})\cdot\big\langle\mathbf{i}\sqrt{6}\big\rangle\subseteq\GL_{1}(K).

When
p
is
1
,
5
,
7
or
11
modulo
24
, there are two places
\mathfrak{p}_{1}
and
\mathfrak{p}_{2}
above
p
with residue field
\mathbb{F}_{p}
.
Moreover, in this case, the reduction of
\mathbf{i}\sqrt{6}
in the residue field lies obviously in
\mathbb{F}_{p}
, and so

\image\big(G\cap\GL_{1}(\mathcal{O}_{(\mathfrak{p}_{i})})\to\GL_{1}(\mathbb{F}_{p})\big)=\GL_{1}(\mathbb{F}_{p})=\mathbb{F}_{p}^{\times}.

On the contrary, when
p\equiv 13,17,19,23\pmod{24}
, there is only one place
\mathfrak{p}
above
p
with residue field
\mathbb{F}_{p^{2}}
and we find

\image\big(G\cap\GL_{1}(\mathcal{O}_{(\mathfrak{p})})\to\GL_{1}(\mathbb{F}_{p^{2}})\big)=\sqrt{\mathbb{F}_{p}^{\times}},

which is also the Galois group of
g(x)
(resp.
h(x)
) over
\mathbb{F}_{p^{2}}(x)
.

In Example
2.1.6
finally, getting inspired by what precedes, we are looking for a number field for which the splitting properties of a rational prime
p
depends on its
congruence class modulo
9
. A natural candidate is the cyclotomic extension
K=\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{9})
. Regarding the groups, one checks that we can take

\begin{array}[]{r@{\hspace{0.5ex}}ll}G_{1}\hskip 2.15277pt&\coloneqq\GL_{1}(K)&\text{(works for $p\equiv 2\!\!\!\pmod{3}$),}\\
G_{2}\hskip 2.15277pt&\coloneqq\GL_{2}(K)&\text{(works for $p\equiv 1\!\!\!\pmod{3}$).}\end{array}

Remark 2.2.7
.

In the Conjecture
2.2.6
we consider prime numbers for which
f(x)\bmod p
is well-defined and algebraic, without considering which prime numbers satisfy this condition. In a refined version, Conjecture
2.2.11
we ask for some structure of this set of prime numbers. Here, we mention one important case related to an old conjecture of Christol. A power series
f(x)\in\mathbb{Q}[\![x]\!]
is said to be
globally bounded
, if there is
a\in\mathbb{Z}
such that
f(ax)-f(0)\in\mathbb{Z}[\![x]\!]
. In particular, in that case
f(x)
can be reduced modulo almost all primes
p
, possibly with the exceptions of the primes dividing
a
and the denominator of
f(0)
. Christol’s conjecture
[
Chr86a
]
states that every D-finite power series that is globally bounded, is the diagonal of a multivariate rational function. By Fursteberg’s Theorem
[
Fur67
]
,
f(x)\bmod p
then is algebraic for almost all
p
. Christol’s conjecture is still wide open, for example, for the globally bounded function
{}_{3}F_{2}\left(\left[\frac{1}{9},\frac{4}{9},\frac{5}{9}\right],\left[\frac{1}{3},1\right];x\right)
, discussed in Subsection
2.1.6
, it is not known, whether it is a diagonal
[
BY22
, p.1085, footnote]
.

2.2.3
Two refinements

Although Conjecture
2.2.6
might look already rather strong and difficult, there are several points on which it remains quite vague and unprecise.
The first one is that it does not say too much about the groups
G_{i}
, except that they exist and are contained in some
\GL_{n}
.
However, while this fragility is certainly the most significant one, discussing it will require some categorical language and machinery.
For this reason, we prefer postponing this to Subsection
2.3
and start by presenting, in this subsection, two other simpler improvements.

A Galois equivariant version.

A first evident weakness of Conjecture
2.2.6
is that it does not concern the complete Galois group
\Gal(f(x)\mid\mathbb{F}_{p}(x))
but only a part of it, even if it is quite large and arguably the most interesting one.
This issue can be solved by adding descent data in the formulation of the conjecture as follows.
We require that
K/\mathbb{Q}
is Galois and that each group
G_{i}\subseteq\GL_{n}(K)
is stable by the Galois action. For a prime
\mathfrak{p}
of
K
, the group

G_{i,\mathfrak{p}}\coloneqq\image\big(G_{i}\cap\GL_{n}(\mathcal{O}_{(\mathfrak{p})})\to\GL_{n}(k_{\mathfrak{p}})\big)

(2.2.2)

which appears in Conjecture
2.2.6
is then stable by the natural action of
\Gal(k_{\mathfrak{p}}/\mathbb{F}_{p})
;
indeed this action corresponds to that of the decomposition group at
\mathfrak{p}
which, by assumption, stabilizes
G_{i}
.
We can then form the semi-direct product
G_{i,\mathfrak{p}}\rtimes\Gal(k_{\mathfrak{p}}/\mathbb{F}_{p})
(where
\Gal(k_{\mathfrak{p}}/\mathbb{F}_{p})
acts on
G_{i,\mathfrak{p}}
through its natural action on
\GL_{n}(k_{\mathfrak{p}})
), which looks like a very good candidate to be the complete Galois group
\Gal(f(x)\mid\mathbb{F}_{p}(x))
.
This expectation is actually a bit too naive because the factor
\Gal(k_{\mathfrak{p}}/\mathbb{F}_{p})
may vary a lot with the choice of
K
. Instead, we propose the following formulation.

Conjecture 2.2.8
.

Let
f(x)\in\mathbb{Q}[\![x]\!]
be a
D
-finite series and let
n
be the minimal order of a differential operator that annihilates
f(x)
.

Then, there exist a Galois extension
K/\mathbb{Q}
and a family
(G_{1},\ldots,G_{t})
of subgroups of
\GL_{n}(K)
stable by the action of
\Gal(K/\mathbb{Q})
such that the following holds:
for almost all rational primes
p
for which
f(x)\bmod p
is well defined and algebraic over
\mathbb{F}_{p}(x),
and for any prime
\mathfrak{p}
of
K
above
p
,
there exists an index
i\in\{1,\ldots,t\}
such that

(a)

the residual splitting field of
f(x)\bmod p
is the field
\ell_{\mathfrak{p}}

defined as the smallest subfield of
k_{\mathfrak{p}}
for which
G_{i,\mathfrak{p}}\subseteq\GL_{n}(\ell_{\mathfrak{p}})
.

(b)

there is an isomorphism of exact sequences:

\textstyle{1\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{\Gal\big(f(x)\mid k_{\mathfrak{p}}(x)\big)\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\sim}
\textstyle{\Gal\big(f(x)\mid\mathbb{F}_{p}(x)\big)\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\sim}
\textstyle{\Gal\big(\ell_{\mathfrak{p}}/\mathbb{F}_{p}\big)\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{1}
\textstyle{1\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{G_{i,\mathfrak{p}}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{G_{i,\mathfrak{p}}\rtimes\Gal\big(\ell_{\mathfrak{p}}/\mathbb{F}_{p}\big)\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{\Gal\big(\ell_{\mathfrak{p}}/\mathbb{F}_{p}\big)\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{1}

where
G_{i,\mathfrak{p}}
is defined by Equation (
2.2.2
) and the exact sequence on the top is that of Lemma
2.2.5
.

Remark 2.2.9
.

The point
(b)
above is equivalent to requiring, in addition of the assertion
(b)
of Conjecture
2.2.6
, that the exact sequence of Lemma
2.2.5
splits and that the induced action of
\Gal(\ell_{\mathfrak{p}}/\mathbb{F}_{p})
on
G_{i,\mathfrak{p}}
agrees with its natural action on
\GL_{n}(\ell_{\mathfrak{p}})
.

Remark 2.2.10
.

Another interesting feature of Conjecture
2.2.8
is that it clearly highlights that all the places
\mathfrak{p}
above a fixed rational prime
p
play the same role (since they are permuted by
\Gal(K/\mathbb{Q})
).

Finally, we leave it as an exercise to the reader to check that all the examples of Subsection
2.1
satisfy Conjecture
2.2.8
.

Controlling the partition of primes.

The second point on which we would like to add precisions to Conjecture
2.2.6
concerns the dependence of the index
i
,
i.e.
, the subgroup
G_{i}
we choose, with respect to
p
.
On all the examples of Subsection
2.1
, we observe that this dependence is very easy to settle: the choice of
i
always only depends on the congruence class of
p
modulo a certain fixed number.
We would not expect however such a simple pattern to be repeated in full generality.
Our doubts come already from the case of linear differential equation of order
1
, namely

f^{\prime}(x)+a(x)\cdot f(x)=0.

More precisely, we have explained in Remark
2.1.2
that for a solution
f(x)
of the above equation, the property to be reducible modulo
p
is governed by the factorization properties of
a(x)\bmod p
which, in turn, can be read off from the decomposition of
p
in the splitting field
L
of
a(x)
over
\mathbb{Q}
.
This behavior is indeed controlled by congruence conditions on
p
when
L
is an abelian extension of
\mathbb{Q}
; however it might be much more complicated in general.

For this reason, we prefer formulating our refined conjecture as follows.

Conjecture 2.2.11
.

Let
f(x)\in\mathbb{Q}[\![x]\!]
be a
D
-finite series.
Let
n
be the minimal degree of a differential operator that annihilates
f(x)
.
Then, there exist

•

a number field
K
,

•

a finite Galois extension
L/\mathbb{Q}
,

•

two subsets
\mathcal{C}_{\text{\rm alg}}\subseteq\mathcal{C}_{\text{\rm red}}
of the set of conjugacy classes of
\Gal(L/\mathbb{Q})
and

•

for each
C\in\mathcal{C}_{\text{\rm alg}}
, a subgroup
G_{C}\subseteq\GL_{n}(K)

such that, for almost all primes
p
, if
C
denotes the conjugacy class containing the Frobenius morphism at
p
in
\Gal(L/\mathbb{Q})
, we have:

(1)

C\in\mathcal{C}_{\text{\rm red}}
if and only if
f(x)\in\mathbb{Z}_{(p)}[\![x]\!]
,

(2)

C\in\mathcal{C}_{\text{\rm alg}}
if and only if
f(x)\in\mathbb{Z}_{(p)}[\![x]\!]
and
f(x)\bmod p
is algebraic over
\mathbb{F}_{p}(x)
,

(3)

if
C\in\mathcal{C}_{\text{\rm alg}}
, for any prime
\mathfrak{p}
in
K
above
p
, the residue field
k_{\mathfrak{p}}
contains the residual splitting field of
f(x)\bmod p
and
there is an isomorphism

\Gal\big(f(x)\mid k_{\mathfrak{p}}(x)\big)\simeq\image\big(G_{C}\cap\GL_{n}(\mathcal{O}_{(\mathfrak{p})})\to\GL_{n}(k_{\mathfrak{p}})\big).

We underline that Conjecture
2.2.11
also includes a criterion for recognizing whether
f(x)
can be reduced modulo
p
and, when this is the case, whether the reduction is algebraic or not.
This part was not covered in the previous formulations.

On a different note, it is obvious that if Conjecture
2.2.11
holds with a number field
L
and it holds for any extension
L^{\prime}
of
L
(with
L^{\prime}/Q
Galois) as well.
Since a similar property also holds for the number field
K
, one can safely assume that
K=L
in Conjecture
2.2.11
.
However, it looks important to us to make the distinction between
K
and
L
as they seem to play different roles.
For instance, in many examples considered in Subsection
2.1
, taking simply
L=\mathbb{Q}
(which corresponding to having just one group
G_{i}
) is possible while it is often necessary to work with a nontrivial number field
K
.

Finally, we notice that the two refinements provided by Conjectures
2.2.8
and
2.2.11
are somehow independent and can be combined.

2.3
The language of differential modules and Frobenius modules

In this subsection, following standard ideas which go back to Dwork and Christol (see in particular
[
Chr86
]
),
we reinterpret the main protagonists we have encountered so far using the more powerful language of differential modules and Frobenius modules and use it to gain height on Conjecture
2.2.6
.
This will help us to better understand what could be the mysterious groups
G_{i}
by connecting them to the differential Galois group of the underlying differential equation.

2.3.1
Different types of modules

Differential modules.

We begin with linear differential equations.
It is a standard fact that the categorical shadow of these equations is the notion of differential modules, whose definition is recalled below.

Definition 2.3.1
.

Let
K
be a field equipped with a derivation
\partial:K\to K
.
A
differential module
(or a
module with connection
) over
(K,\partial)
is
a finite dimensional
K
-vector space
M
, together with an additive map
\nabla
satisfying

\forall\lambda\in K,\,\forall m\in M,\quad\nabla(\lambda m)=\partial(\lambda)m+\lambda\nabla(m).

We write
\mathrm{Mod}^{\nabla}_{K}
for the category of differential modules over
(K,\partial)
.

To any differential equation of the form

\partial^{r}(f)+a_{r-1}\partial^{(r-1)}(f)+\cdots+a_{1}\partial(f)+a_{0}f=0\qquad(a_{i}\in K)

(2.3.1)

we attach the differential module
M=Ke_{0}\oplus\cdots\oplus Ke_{r-1}
with connection
\nabla
defined by

\displaystyle\nabla(e_{i})

\displaystyle=e_{i+1}\qquad\text{if }0\leq i\leq r{-}2,

\displaystyle\nabla(e_{r-1})

\displaystyle=-(a_{0}e_{0}+\cdots+a_{r-1}e_{r-1}).

The map
f\mapsto\sum_{i=0}^{r-1}\partial^{i}(f)e_{i}
induces a bijection between the space of solutions in
K
of the differential equation (
2.3.1
)
with the space
M^{\nabla=0}
of
horizontal vectors
, that are, by definition, vectors
m
such that
\nabla(m)=0
.

This framework also allows for looking at solutions in extensions.
More precisely, let
L
be an extension of
K
over which the derivation
\partial
extends.
Given a differential module
M
over
K
, one can form the tensor product
L\otimes_{K}M
and endow it with a connection defined by

\nabla(\lambda\otimes m)=\partial(\lambda)\otimes m+\lambda\otimes\nabla(m)\qquad(\lambda\in L,m\in M).

The solutions of
M
over
L
are then defined as the space of horizontal vectors of
L\otimes_{K}M
, that is
(L\otimes_{K}M)^{\nabla=0}
.

Frobenius modules.

For the general purpose of this article, we recall from the introduction (see Equation (
1.1
)) that we are mostly interested in algebraic relations of the form

Y^{p^{n}}+c_{n-1}Y^{p^{n-1}}+\cdots+c_{1}Y^{p}+c_{0}Y=0

(2.3.2)

where
Y
is the unknown and the coefficients
c_{i}
lie in some field of characteristic
p
which, in our case of interest, is
\mathbb{F}_{p}(x)
or an extension of the form
\ell(x)
where
\ell/\mathbb{F}_{p}
is a field extension.
Fortunately, this type of equations can be handled very similarly to what we have presented above for linear differential equations.

In the algebraic situation, the relevant objects are the so-called Frobenius modules that we define now.

Definition 2.3.2
.

Let
R
be a ring equipped with an endomorphism
\varphi:R\to R
.
A
Frobenius module
over
(R,\varphi)
is a finitely generated projective
R
-module
M
equipped with an additive map
\varphi_{M}:M\to M
such that

\forall\lambda\in K,\,\forall m\in M,\quad\varphi_{M}(\lambda m)=\varphi(\lambda)\cdot\varphi_{M}(m).

We let
\mathrm{Mod}^{\varphi}_{R}
denote the category of Frobenius modules over
(R,\varphi)
.

Notation 2.3.3
.

When the ground ring
R
has characteristic
p
and
\varphi:R\to R
is the map
x\mapsto x^{q}
(where
q
is a power of
p
), we write
\mathrm{Mod}^{q}_{R}
for
\mathrm{Mod}^{\varphi}_{R}
.

The relationship between Frobenius equations and Frobenius modules is realized as follows.
Starting from Equation (
2.3.2
) with
c_{i}\in R
, we define the Frobenius module
M=Re_{0}\oplus\cdots\oplus Re_{r-1}
(where
R
is equipped with its natural Frobenius) with

\displaystyle\varphi_{M}(e_{i})

\displaystyle=e_{i+1}\qquad\text{if }0\leq i\leq r{-}2,

\displaystyle\varphi_{M}(e_{r-1})

\displaystyle=-(c_{0}e_{0}+\cdots+c_{r-1}e_{r-1}).

The solutions of Equation (
2.3.2
) then correspond to vectors
m\in M
such
\varphi_{M}(m)=m

via
the map
y\mapsto\sum_{i=0}^{r-1}y^{p^{i}}e_{i}
.

Galois representations.

The last notion we would like to encapsulate in our new language is, of course, that of Galois groups.
For this, given a field
K
, we write
G_{K}
for its absolute Galois group,
i.e.
,
G_{K}\coloneqq\Gal(K^{\text{\rm sep}}/K)
.
Given an auxiliary field
E
, we introduce the category
\mathrm{Rep}_{E}(G_{K})
of all
E
-linear finite dimensional representations of
G_{K}
.

The connection with the Galois groups we used to consider earlier is as follows.
Assume that
K
contains
E
and let then
x
be an element in the separable closure
K^{\text{\rm sep}}
of
K
.
We consider its Galois conjugate
x_{1}=x,x_{2},\ldots,x_{N}\in K^{\text{\rm sep}}
and the
E
-vector space
V
they generate.
Clearly
V
is stable by the action of
G_{K}
. Hence, it is a
E
-linear representation of
G_{K}
,
i.e.
, an object in the category
\mathrm{Rep}_{E}(G_{K})
.
Besides, if
\rho:G_{K}\to\GL(V)
is the morphism defining the representation, one checks that
\Gal(x\mid K)\simeq\im\rho
.

2.3.2
Katz’ functor

Katz’ functor connects Frobenius modules with Galois representations;
in some sense, it is the categorical realization of the operation that takes an algebraic equation of the form (
2.3.2
) and produces the Galois group of one of its solutions.

Concretely, it is defined as follows. We pick a field
K
of characteristic
p
, together with a finite extension
\mathbb{F}_{q}
of
\mathbb{F}_{p}
. We then define

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\mathbf{V}:\quad\mathrm{Mod}^{q}_{K}&\longrightarrow&\mathrm{Rep}_{\mathbb{F}_{q}}(G_{K})\\
M&\longmapsto&\big(K^{\text{\rm sep}}\otimes_{K}M\big)^{\varphi=1}\end{array}

(2.3.3)

where we let
\varphi
act on
K^{\text{\rm sep}}\otimes_{K}M
by
\lambda\otimes m\mapsto\lambda^{q}\otimes\varphi_{M}(m)
and
where the notation “
\varphi=1
” means that we consider the subset of elements that are fixed by
\varphi
.
When
M
comes from an equation of the form (
2.3.2
) (and thus
q=p
), it follows directly from the definition that
the representation
\mathbf{V}(M)
is the
\mathbb{F}_{p}
-vector space of the solutions in
K^{\text{\rm sep}}
equipped with the standard Galois action.

To study Katz’ functor, it is convenient to introduce the following definition.

Definition 2.3.4
.

Let
R
be a ring equipped with an endomorphism
\varphi:R\to R
.
We say that a Frobenius module
M\in\mathrm{Mod}^{\varphi}_{R}
is
étale
when the image of
\varphi_{M}
spans
M
.

We write
\mathrm{Mod}^{\varphi,\text{\rm ét}}_{K}
for the subcategory of
\mathrm{Mod}^{\varphi}_{K}
consisting of étale Frobenius modules.
Moreover, when
\varphi
is the map
x\mapsto x^{q}
, we write
\mathrm{Mod}^{q,\text{\rm ét}}_{K}
for
\mathrm{Mod}^{\varphi,\text{\rm ét}}_{K}
(see also Notation
2.3.3
).

Theorem 2.3.5

(Katz)
.

Let
K
be a field of characteristic
p
and let
q
be a power of
p
.
The functor
\mathbf{V}
induces an equivalence of categories
\mathrm{Mod}^{q,\text{\rm ét}}_{K}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\sim}}{{\longrightarrow}}\mathrm{Rep}_{\mathbb{F}_{q}}(G_{K})
.
Moreover, it preserves the dimension in the sense that, for all
M\in\mathrm{Mod}^{q,\text{\rm ét}}_{K}
, we have

\dim_{K}M=\dim_{\mathbb{F}_{q}}\mathbf{V}(M).

Proof.

See
[
Kat73
, Proposition 4.1.1]
.
∎

Remark 2.3.6
.

There exists an explicit formula for the inverse functor
\mathbf{M}
of
\mathbf{V}
:
if
V
is a
\mathbb{F}_{q}
-linear representation of
G_{K}
, its corresponding étale Frobenius module is

\mathbf{M}(V)\coloneqq(K^{\text{\rm sep}}\otimes_{K}V)^{G_{K}}

where
G_{K}
acts diagonally on the tensor product
K^{\text{\rm sep}}\otimes_{K}V
.

Beyond Katz’ theorem, it will be also important for our purpose to study the action of Katz’ functor on nonétale Frobenius modules.
In this perspective, we set the following definition.

Definition 2.3.7
.

Let
K
be a field equipped with a ring endomorphism
\varphi:K\to K
and let
M
be a Frobenius module over
(K,\varphi)
.
The
étale part
of
M
is defined as

M^{\text{\rm ét}}\coloneqq\bigcap_{k\geq 0}\langle\varphi_{M}^{k}(M)\rangle_{K}

where the notation
\langle\,\cdot\,\rangle_{K}
stands for the
K
-span.

It is straightforward to check that
M^{\text{\rm ét}}
is stable by
\varphi_{M}
,
i.e.
, it is again a Frobenius module, and that it is étale.
In other words, the construction
M\mapsto M^{\text{\rm ét}}
defines a functor
\mathrm{Mod}^{\varphi}_{K}\to\mathrm{Mod}^{\varphi,\text{\rm ét}}_{K}
.

Lemma 2.3.8
.

We assume that
K
has characteristic
p
and that
q
is a power of
p
.
Then, for all
M\in\mathrm{Mod}^{q}_{K}
, we have
\mathbf{V}(M)=\mathbf{V}(M^{\text{\rm ét}})
.

Proof.

We form the quotient
M/M^{\text{\rm ét}}
which inherits an action of
\varphi_{M}
and thus is an object in the category
\mathrm{Mod}^{\varphi}_{K}
.
Besides, it follows readily from the definition that
\varphi_{M}
acts nilpotently on
M/M^{\text{\rm ét}}
.
Therefore, it also acts nilpotently on
K^{\text{\rm sep}}\otimes_{K}M/M^{\text{\rm ét}}
, from which follows that
\mathbf{V}(M/M^{\text{\rm ét}})=0
.
We now consider the exact sequence

0\longrightarrow M^{\text{\rm ét}}\longrightarrow M\longrightarrow M/M^{\text{\rm ét}}\longrightarrow 0

which remains exact after tensoring by
K^{\text{\rm sep}}
. Taking then the fixed points under
\varphi
(which is a left exact functor), we end up with

0\longrightarrow\mathbf{V}(M^{\text{\rm ét}})\longrightarrow\mathbf{V}(M)\longrightarrow\mathbf{V}(M/M^{\text{\rm ét}})=0.

The lemma follows.
∎

Remark 2.3.9
.

Lemma
2.3.8
tells us that
M^{\text{\rm ét}}
is the étale Frobenius module which corresponds to the Galois representation
\mathbf{V}(M)
.
With the notation of Remark
2.3.6
, we then have
M^{\text{\rm ét}}=\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{V}(M))
.

2.3.3
Frobenius structures on differential modules

The relationship between differential modules and Frobenius modules is far less concrete, but it can nevertheless be approached through the notion of Frobenius structures.
Basically, the idea is to equip a differential module
M
with an extra structure which will eventually define a Frobenius module after reduction modulo
p
.

In order to define it properly, we cannot continue working over
\mathbb{Q}(x)
but need to move over the
p
-adics.
Precisely, we define the field
E_{p}
as the completion of
\mathbb{Q}(x)
for the
p
-adic Gauss norm (defined as the sup norm on the
p
-adic unit disc);
it is the so-called field of
p
-adic analytic elements
. It is equipped with the derivation
\frac{d}{dx}
and a Frobenius map
\varphi:E_{p}\to E_{p}
which comes from the change of variables
x\mapsto x^{p}
.

Definition 2.3.10
.

Let
(M_{1},\nabla_{1})
and
(M_{2},\nabla_{2})
be two differential modules over
E_{p}
.
A
Frobenius morphism
is a
\varphi
-semilinear map
\Phi:M_{1}\to M_{2}
such that

\nabla_{2}\circ\Phi=px^{p-1}\cdot\Phi\circ\nabla_{1}
.

It is instructive to understand what the effect of a Frobenius morphism at the level of solutions is.
The commutation relation of Definition
2.3.10
ensures that a Frobenius morphism
\Phi:M_{1}\to M_{2}
induces a
\mathbb{Q}_{p}
-linear mapping on horizontal vectors

\Phi:M_{1}^{\nabla=0}\longrightarrow M_{2}^{\nabla=0}
.
If we equip
M_{1}
and
M_{2}
with distinguished bases and if
\mathrm{Mat}(\Phi)
denotes the matrix of
\Phi
in those bases, we deduce that
any solution
(f_{1}(x),\ldots,f_{n}(x))
of
M_{1}
produces a solution of
M_{2}

via
the formula

\big(\varphi(f_{1}(x)),\ldots,\varphi(f_{n}(x))\big)\cdot\mathrm{Mat}(\Phi)=\big(f_{1}(x^{p}),\ldots,f_{n}(x^{p})\big)\cdot\mathrm{Mat}(\Phi).

If we assume in addition that
\Phi:M_{1}\to M_{2}
is an isomorphism, then any solution of
M_{2}
can be (uniquely) written in this way.
Frobenius morphisms then appear as a general machinery that permits to interpret the images under
\varphi
of a solution of a given differential equation as linear combinations of the solutions of another differential equation
and
vice versa
when
\Phi
is an isomorphism.
Phrased in this way, we understand that they could provide a powerful tool to find the algebraic relations we are looking for (remember that
f(x^{p})=f(x)^{p}
after reduction modulo
p
).

In order to fully take advantage of this observation, it is convenient to use the concept of
preimages under the Frobenius
.
To avoid unnecessary complications, we assume from now on that we are given a
D
-finite series
f(x)\in\mathbb{Z}_{(p)}[\![x]\!]
and that

M
is the differential module associated to the linear differential equation over
E_{p}
of minimal order satisfied by
f(x)
.
A theorem of Christol (see
[
Ked10
, Theorem 10.4.2]
) then implies that
M
admits a preimage under the Frobenius,
which means that there exists a Frobenius
isomorphism

M^{(-1)}\to M
where
M^{(-1)}
is another differential module.
Even better, one can apply Christol’s theorem repeatedly and get an infinite sequence of Frobenius isomorphisms

\cdots\longrightarrow M^{(-3)}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\sim}}{{\longrightarrow}}M^{(-2)}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\sim}}{{\longrightarrow}}M^{(-1)}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\sim}}{{\longrightarrow}}M.

We now come to an alternative: either the sequence of
M^{(-i)}
is periodic or it is not.
In the first case,
i.e.
, when there exists a positive integer
h
such that
M^{(-h)}
is isomorphic to
M
as a differential module, we say that
M
admits a
strong Frobenius structure
(of period
h
).
When this occurs, the composite

M\simeq M^{(-h)}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\sim}}{{\longrightarrow}}\cdots\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\sim}}{{\longrightarrow}}M^{(-1)}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\sim}}{{\longrightarrow}}M.

defines a structure of Frobenius modules on
M
for the twisting morphism
\varphi^{h}
.
This makes the connection we were looking for.

On the contrary, when
M
does not admit a strong Frobenius structure, the above construction fails;
however, in this case, we suspect that
f(x)\bmod p
is not algebraic.

Some examples.

It is quite interesting to revisit some examples of Subsection
2.1
in light of what precedes.
Let us start with Example
2.1.2
.
Here
f(x)=\exp(\arctan(x))
and we recall that, whenever
p\equiv 1\pmod{4}
, we had obtained the following alternative formula for
f(x)
as a
p
-adic series:

f(x)=\left(1+ix\right)^{-i/2}\cdot\left(1-ix\right)^{i/2}\in\mathbb{Z}_{p}[\![x]\!]

where
i\in\mathbb{Z}_{p}
is a square root of
-1
.
We recall that we also decomposed the exponents
\pm i/2
in base
p
as follows:

\textstyle-\frac{i}{2}=a_{0}+pa_{1}+p^{2}a_{2}+\cdots\quad\text{and}\quad\frac{i}{2}=b_{0}+pb_{1}+p^{2}b_{2}+\cdots

It turns out that this decomposition actually reflects the successive preimages by Frobenius of the underlying differential module.
Indeed, setting
u_{1}\coloneqq\frac{-i/2-a_{0}}{p}=a_{1}+pa_{2}+\cdots
,
we can rewrite

\left(1+ix\right)^{-i/2}=\left(1+ix\right)^{a_{0}}\left(\frac{(1+ix)^{p}}{1+ix^{p}}\right)^{u_{1}}\left(1+ix^{p}\right)^{u_{1}}.

Now we observe that the two first factors of the right-hand side lie in
E_{p}
:
it is obvious for the first one, which is a polynomial, and
it holds true for the second one as well because
\frac{(1+ix)^{p}}{1+ix^{p}}
is a rational function, which is congruent to
1
modulo
p
.
Proceeding similarly for the factor
\left(1-ix\right)^{i/2}
, we end up with a factorization of the form
f(x)=g_{0}(x)f_{1}(x^{p})
where
g_{0}(x)\in E_{p}
and

f_{1}(x)=\left(1+ix\right)^{u_{1}}\cdot\left(1-ix\right)^{v_{1}}

with
v_{1}\coloneqq\frac{i/2-b_{0}}{p}=b_{1}+pb_{2}+\cdots
.
Moreover, a simple computation shows that the function
f_{1}(x)
is a solution of the linear differential equation

y^{\prime}(x)=\frac{i(u_{1}{-}v_{1})+(u_{1}{+}v_{1})x}{1+x^{2}}\cdot y(x).

It is the preimage under the Frobenius of our initial differential equation for
f(x)
.
Repeating this argument, we find that the successive preimages under the Frobenius are the differential equations

y^{\prime}(x)=\frac{i(u_{k}{-}v_{k})+(u_{k}{+}v_{k})x}{1+x^{2}}\cdot y(x)

with
u_{k}=a_{k}+pa_{k+1}+p^{2}a_{k+2}+\cdots
and
v_{k}=b_{k}+pb_{k+1}+p^{2}b_{k+2}+\cdots
.
The nonperiodicity of the sequences
(a_{k})_{k\geq 0}
and
(b_{k})_{k\geq 0}
, or equivalently that of the sequences
(u_{k})_{k\geq 0}
and
(v_{k})_{k\geq 0}
, now implies that the sequence of preimages under the Frobenius is not periodic either.
This conclusion is perfectly in line with the fact that
f(x)\bmod p
is not algebraic.

Another interesting example to consider is Example
2.1.5
with the Apéry series.
Here, we recall that we had defined two functions
g(x)
and
h(x)
whose reductions modulo
p
satisfy interlaced algebraic relations (see Equations (
2.1.7
)–(
2.1.10
)).
Those equations suggest that the differential equations that
g(x)
and
h(x)
satisfy are fixed by the Frobenius when
p\equiv 1,5,7,11\pmod{24}
and exchanged by the Frobenius when
p\equiv 13,17,19,23\pmod{24}
.

Similar patterns will often occur in Section
3
, when we will study hypergeometric series.

2.3.4
The long road from
\mathrm{Mod}^{\nabla}_{\mathbb{Q}(x)}
to
\mathrm{Rep}_{\mathbb{F}_{q}(x)}(G_{\mathbb{F}_{q}(x)})

\mathrm{Mod}^{\nabla}_{\mathbb{Q}(x)}
M

\mathrm{Mod}^{\nabla}_{\mathbb{Q}(x)}
\mathrm{Mod}^{\nabla}_{\mathbb{Q}(x)}

\mathrm{Mod}^{\nabla}_{\mathbb{Q}(x)}
\in

\mathrm{Mod}^{\nabla}_{\mathbb{Q}(x)}
M_{p}

\mathrm{Mod}^{\nabla}_{\mathbb{Q}(x)}
\mathrm{Mod}^{\nabla}_{E_{p}}

\mathrm{Mod}^{\nabla}_{\mathbb{Q}(x)}
\in

\mathrm{Mod}^{\nabla}_{\mathbb{Q}(x)}
M_{p}

From differential equations in characteristic

All the previous constructions provide a path for going from modules with connections over
\mathbb{Q}(x)
(which encode the differential equation satisfied by
f(x)
) to Galois representations of the absolute Galois group
G_{\mathbb{F}_{q}(x)}
of
\mathbb{F}_{q}(x)
(which encodes the Galois group of
f(x)\bmod p
).
It consists in the following steps.

(Factorization over
E_{p}
)
We “
p
-minimize” the differential equation we have for
f(x)
, meaning that we replace it by the differential equation
over
E_{p}
of minimal order which is satisfied by
f(x)
;
we emphasize that this operation may result in a drop of order even if our initial differential equation was already minimal over
\mathbb{Q}(x)
.
At the level of modules, this consists in replacing
M
by a submodule
M_{p}\subseteq E_{p}\otimes_{\mathbb{Q}(x)}M
.

(Frobenius structure)
Following the discussion of Subsection
2.3.3
, we equip
M_{p}
with a Frobenius structure
\Phi_{h}:M_{p}\to M_{p}
, obtaining this way a Frobenius module over
E_{p}
for the twisting morphism
\varphi^{h}
(where
h
is the period);
we underline that this operation may fail if the sequence of the Frobenius preimages of
M_{p}
is not periodic.

(Finding a lattice)
In order to reduce modulo
p
afterwards, we need to find an integral structure inside
M_{p}
.
Let
E_{p}^{\circ}
denote the valuation ring of
E_{p}
, that is the subring of
E_{p}
consisting of elements of nonnegative valuation.
We select a finitely generated
E_{p}^{\circ}
-submodule
M_{p}^{\circ}
of
M_{p}
having the following properties:
it generates
M_{p}
over
E_{p}
, it is stable by
\Phi_{h}
and it defines an étale Frobenius module over
E_{p}^{\circ}
.

Such a module may actually not exist; if it does not, we allow ourselves to change the Frobenius structure,
i.e.
, to replace
\Phi_{h}
by
\Phi_{h}\circ\iota
where
\iota
is a
E_{p}
-linear automorphism of
M_{p}
commuting with the connection.
If even after this relaxation, we are still not able to find a suitable module
M_{p}^{\circ}
, we agree that this step has failed.

(Reduction modulo
p
)
We reduce
M_{p}^{\circ}
modulo
p
,
i.e.
, we set
\overline{M}_{p}\coloneqq M_{p}^{\circ}/pM_{p}^{\circ}
.
It is an étale Frobenius module over
\mathbb{F}_{p}(x)
for the twisting morphism
f\mapsto f^{q}
with
q=p^{h}
.

(Katz’ functor)
We finally apply Katz’ functor to get a representation of
G_{\mathbb{F}_{q}(x)}\coloneqq\Gal(\mathbb{F}_{q}(x)^{\text{\rm sep}}/\mathbb{F}_{q}(x))
.
Some precaution should be taken here because Katz’ theorem requires that the base field of the Frobenius module contains
\mathbb{F}_{q}
, which is not the case in our setting.
We then first extend scalar to
\mathbb{F}_{q}(x)
,
i.e.
, we define

V_{p}\coloneqq\mathbf{V}\big(\mathbb{F}_{q}(x)\otimes_{\mathbb{F}_{p}(x)}\overline{M}_{p}\big)=\big(\mathbb{F}_{q}(x)^{\text{\rm sep}}\otimes_{\mathbb{F}_{p}(x)}\overline{M}_{p}\big)^{\varphi=1}.

All this process is summarized in Figure
1
.
Dashed arrows correspond to steps that may fail. However, if a failure is encountered at some point, our feeling is that
f(x)\bmod p
cannot be algebraic.
On the contrary, when all operations succeed, it is known that
f(x)\bmod p
is algebraic
[
Var21
, Theorem 2.6]
.
What we expect actually is that
f(x)\mod p
is somehow “contained”, together with its conjugates, in the Galois representation
V_{p}
which is produced at the end of the process.
More precisely, if we let
\rho_{p}:G_{\mathbb{F}_{q}(x)}\to\GL(V_{p})
be the corresponding group morphism, we expect the Galois group
\Gal(f(x)\mid\mathbb{F}_{q}(x))
to be isomorphic to a quotient of the image of
\rho_{p}
.

Remark 2.3.11
.

It worths noticing that Theorem 2.6 of
[
Var21
]
only requires the existence of a strong Frobenius structure, and not that of a lattice. This suggests that Step
2.3.4
in our construction could never fail.

2.3.5
Connecting Galois groups: insights towards our conjectures

The Tannakian language provides efficient tools to define Galois groups at each step of our constructions and, to some extent, to follow them throughout the whole process.
We refer to
[
DM82
]
for an exposition of the Tannakian theory.
Here, we just recall that the Tannakian formalism attaches a “Galois group” to any object in a Tannakian category. Its definition goes through the notion of constructions.
Given
T
in a Tannakian category
\mathcal{T}
, a
construction
from
T
is by definition a subquotient of an object of the form

C\coloneqq T^{\otimes{n_{1}}}\oplus T^{\otimes{n_{2}}}\oplus\cdots\oplus T^{\otimes{n_{t}}}\qquad(n_{i}\in\mathbb{Z})

where, when
n
is negative,
T^{\otimes n}
is understood as the dual of
T^{\otimes(-n)}
.
The Galois group
\Gal_{\mathcal{T}}(T)
is then the group of linear automorphisms
\sigma:T\to T
satisfying the following condition:
for any
C
as above and any subobject
S\subseteq C
, the natural extension of
\sigma
to
C
stabilizes
S
.
One proves that
\Gal_{\mathcal{T}}(T)
is an
algebraic
subgroup of
\GL(T)
.

We now analyze the effect on Galois groups of each step of Figure
1
in light of what precedes.
Step
2.3.4
has already quite a nontrivial impact.
To describe it properly, it is convenient to split it into two parts: firstly, we extend scalars to
E_{p}
and secondly, we minimize the differential equation.
Regarding scalar extension, it follows from the definition that we have an inclusion

\Gal_{\mathrm{Mod}^{\nabla}_{E_{p}}}(E_{p}\otimes_{\mathbb{Q}(x)}M)\subseteq\Gal_{\mathrm{Mod}^{\nabla}_{\mathbb{Q}(x)}}(M).

Besides, if
E_{p}\otimes_{\mathbb{Q}(x)}M
admits a submodule
M^{\prime}
, any element of the Galois group of
E_{p}\otimes_{\mathbb{Q}(x)}M
necessarily stabilizes
M^{\prime}
.
Applying this remark with
M^{\prime}=M_{p}
, we obtain a group morphism

\varpi:\Gal_{\mathrm{Mod}^{\nabla}_{E_{p}}}(E_{p}\otimes_{\mathbb{Q}(x)}M)\longrightarrow\GL(M_{p})

and it follows from the Tannakian theory that the Galois group of
M_{p}
is the image of
\varpi
.
As a summary, one can retain that
\Gal_{\mathrm{Mod}^{\nabla}_{E_{p}}}(M_{p})
appears as a subquotient of
\Gal_{\mathrm{Mod}^{\nabla}_{\mathbb{Q}(x)}}(M)
.

Although the steps
2.3.4
and
2.3.4
seem to be the most delicate ones, their effect on the Galois groups is quite harmless.
Indeed, a theorem of Matzat
[
Mat09
,
Mat09a
]
shows that

\Gal_{\mathrm{Mod}^{\nabla}_{E_{p}}}(M_{p})=\Gal_{\mathrm{Mod}^{q,\text{\rm ét}}_{E_{p}^{\circ}}}(M_{p}^{\circ})

as soon as
M_{p}^{\circ}
is defined.
More precisely, the datum of the lattice
M_{p}^{\circ}
defines a prolongation to
\text{Spec }\mathbb{Z}_{p}
of the algebraic group
\Gal_{\mathrm{Mod}^{\nabla}_{E_{p}}}(M_{p})
, which was
a priori
only defined over
\text{Spec }\mathbb{Q}_{p}
.

Step
2.3.4
is a scalar extension and so, it has a similar effect on Galois groups as in Step
2.3.4
:
the Galois group of
\overline{M}_{p}
is a subgroup of the reduction modulo
p
of the Galois group of
M_{p}^{\circ}
.
Concerning Step
2.3.4
, the same phenomenon occurs when extending scalars to
\mathbb{F}_{q}(x)
, and finally, applying Katz’ functor is transparent at the level of Galois groups because it is an equivalence of categories.

All in all, the conclusion is that the Galois group of
f(x)\bmod p
appears as a subquotient of a certain scalar extension of the reduction modulo
p
of the differential Galois group of
M
.
This remark suggests that the groups
G_{i}
of Conjecture
2.2.6
could show up as subquotients of the
K
-valued points of
\Gal_{\mathrm{Mod}^{\nabla}_{\mathbb{Q}(x)}}(M)
.
The fact that we are loosing dimensions when passing from
M
to
M_{p}
also explains that
G_{i}
sometimes (naturally) embeds in
\GL_{m}(K)
with
m<\dim M
.

Uniformity in
p
.

So far, we have fixed a prime number
p
from the beginning and carried out all our constructions and reasonings with this particular
p
.
In fact, the content of Conjecture
2.2.6
(and its refinements) is somehow that all of this can be achieved uniformly with respect to
p
, with precise control encoded by certain number fields!

Recycling the same set of ideas, one could also expect that some numerical invariants that we have encountered along the way,
e.g.
, the dimension of
M_{p}
, behave quite nicely when
p
varies.
This leads to the following two independent questions, which sound quite interesting to us, and that we ask to the community.

Question 2.3.12
.

Let
f(x)\in\mathbb{Q}[\![x]\!]
be a
D
-finite series and,
for all prime number
p
, let
r_{p}
be the minimal order of a differential equation over
E_{p}
satisfied by
f(x)
.
How does
r_{p}
vary with respect to
p
?

Question 2.3.13
.

Let
M
be a differential module over
\mathbb{Q}(x)
and,
for almost all prime number
p
, let
d_{p}
be the dimension over
\mathbb{F}_{p}(x^{p})
of the space of the solutions of
M
over
\mathbb{F}_{p}(x)
.
By Cartier’s Lemma (see
[
BCR24
, Theorem 3.19]
),
d_{p}
is also the dimension of the kernel of the
p
-curvature of
M
.
How does
d_{p}
vary with respect to
p
?

If the exponents at
0
of the minimal differential equation of a
D
-finite series
f(x)\in\mathbb{Q}[\![x]\!]
are all
0
, the function
f(x)
is said to have
maximal unipotent monodromy (MUM)
and one proves that
r_{p}=d_{p}=1
for almost all

. This answers Questions

and
2.3.13
in this case.
In Section
3
(see Theorem
3.1.17
), we will study the case where
M
is the differential module corresponding to a hypergeometric operator
\mathcal{H}
and show, in this situation, that

depends only on the congruence class of

modulo a common denominator of the parameters of
\mathcal{H}
.

3
Hypergeometric series and their reductions modulo
p

The hypergeometric differential operator is defined as

\mathcal{H}(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})=-x\prod_{i=1}^{n}\big(\vartheta+\alpha_{i}\big)+\vartheta\prod_{j=1}^{m}(\vartheta+\beta_{j}-1)\qquad\Big(\vartheta\coloneqq x\cdot\frac{d}{dx}\Big)

(3.0.1)

where
n
and
m
are positive integers,
\boldsymbol{\alpha}=(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n})\in(\mathbb{Q}\setminus(-\mathbb{N}))^{n}
and
\boldsymbol{\beta}=(\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{m})\in(\mathbb{Q}\setminus(-\mathbb{N}))^{m}
. The solutions of the equation
\mathcal{H}(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})y=0
over the rational numbers can be expressed in terms of hypergeometric series

{}_{n}F_{m}\left(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta};x\right)\coloneqq\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}h(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta};k)x^{k}\in\mathbb{Q}[\![x]\!],

(3.0.2)

with

h(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta};k)\coloneqq\frac{(\alpha_{1})_{k}\cdots(\alpha_{n})_{k}}{(\beta_{1})_{k}\cdots(\beta_{m})_{k}}\cdot\frac{1}{k!}\in\mathbb{Q},

where
(\gamma)_{k}\coloneqq\gamma\cdot(\gamma+1)\cdots(\gamma+k-1)
denotes the rising factorial.

In the following we will restrict to the case
m=n-1
. Moreover, if we consider a set of parameters
\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta}\in\mathbb{Q}^{n+(n-1)}
, we set
\beta_{n}=1
and write
\boldsymbol{\beta}
both for the
(n{-}1)
-tuple of rational parameters and for the
n
-tuple also including
\beta_{n}=1
. For given parameters
\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta}
we denote by
d(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})
the least common multiple of their denominators in reduced form. If the parameters are clear, we will omit them from the notation and will simply write
d
.

Let us recall some facts about the hypergeometric differential equation
\mathcal{H}(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})y=0
. It is
Fuchsian
, meaning that all of its
singularities
are
regular
. The singular points are
0,1,\infty
and the sets of
local exponents
at those singular points are

\begin{array}[]{rl}\text{at 0:}&\big\{1-\beta_{1},\ldots,1-\beta_{n-1},0\big\},\\
\text{at 1:}&\textstyle\big\{0,1,2,\ldots,n-2,-1+(\sum_{j=1}^{n}\beta_{j}-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{i})\big\},\\
\text{at $\infty$:}&\big\{\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n}\big\}.\end{array}

Finally, assuming that
\alpha_{i}-\beta_{j}\not\in\mathbb{Z}
for all
i,j\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
, a
\mathbb{Q}
-basis of solutions of
\mathcal{H}(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})y=0
at the singularity
0
is given by

z^{1-\beta_{j}}{}_{n}F_{n-1}\left(1+\boldsymbol{\alpha}-\beta_{j},1+\boldsymbol{\beta}-\beta_{j};x\right),

(3.0.3)

where
1+\beta_{j}-\beta_{j}
is omitted in the bottom parameter.

Hypergeometric functions and equations serve as test cases for many conjectures and investigations concerning
D
-finite power series. For example, Christol conjectured that any globally bounded
D
-finite power series is a diagonal of an algebraic multivariate series, and investigated this for hypergeometric series
[
Chr86a
]
. Adamczewski and Delaygue, as attributed by Vargas-Montoya
[
Var21
, Conjecture 1.1]
, conjectured that the reduction modulo
p
of any
G-function
, which can be reduced modulo an infinite number of primes, is algebraic over
\mathbb{F}_{p}(x)
for almost all such primes
p
. For hypergeometric series
{}_{n}F_{n-1}
, this statement follows from Christol’s work
[
Chr86
]
showing that solutions of equations with strong Frobenius structure are
p
-automatic, and thus they are algebraic, following earlier work by Christol
[
Chr79
,
Chr+80
]
. This result was made explicit by Vargas-Montoya
[
Var21
,
Var24
]
, bounding the algebraicity degree and giving an explicit shape of an annihilating polynomial in the form of Equation (
2.3.2
). As test cases to Conjecture
2.2.6
and its refinements we will study hypergeometric series for the remaining part of this article.

3.1
Reductions of hypergeometric series modulo
p

In this section, we investigate how hypergeometric functions and equations behave when passing to positive characteristic.
Our first result classifies those prime numbers
p
, modulo which a given hypergeometric function can be reduced, in terms of the repartition of the values
\exp(2\mathbf{i}\pi\lambda\alpha_{i})
and
\exp(2\mathbf{i}\pi\lambda\beta_{j})
(for
\lambda
varying in
\mathbb{Z}
) on the complex unit circle.
This refines a theorem of Christol
[
Chr86a
, Proposition 1]
, classifying all globally bounded hypergeometric functions, that is, those where the reduction is possible modulo almost all primes. We refer to
[
DRR17
, Section 5]
for a more recent treatment in English.

We then study the solution space of a hypergeometric differential equation modulo
p
and describe an explicit basis of it.
We connect in particular its dimension to numerical invariants attached to the parameters
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
and
\boldsymbol{\beta}
defining the underlying hypergeometric operator and show that it only depends on the congruence class of
p
modulo
d
, a common denominator for
\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta}
.
Our results provide in particular a satisfactory answer to Question
2.3.13
, raised at the end of Section
2
in the case of hypergeometric functions.

3.1.1
Interlacing criteria

We denote the
p

v_{p}(\cdot)
. Further, we write
\{\cdot\}_{\!{}_{0}}
(resp.
\{\cdot\}_{\!{}_{1}}

1
instead of
0
).
Christol defines a total ordering
\preceq

\mathbb{Q}
as follows:

a\preceq b\ratio\Longleftrightarrow\{a\}_{\!{}_{1}}<\{b\}_{\!{}_{1}}\text{ or }(\{a\}_{\!{}_{1}}=\{b\}_{\!{}_{1}}\text{ and }a\geq b).

Let

\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta}\in\big(\mathbb{Q}\setminus(-\mathbb{N})\big)^{2n-1}
and set
\beta_{n}=1
. We denote by
d

\mathcal{M}:\mathbb{Q}\times(\mathbb{Z}/d\mathbb{Z})^{\times}\to\mathbb{Z}
by

\mathcal{M}(x,\lambda)\coloneqq\big|\{1\leq i\leq n:\lambda\alpha_{i}\preceq x\}\big|-\big|\{1\leq j\leq n:\lambda\beta_{j}\preceq x\}\big|.

(3.1.1)

We note that the function
\mathcal{M}(\cdot,\lambda)
is piecewise constant and continuous from the left, and that its jump points are among the values
\{\alpha_{i}\lambda\}_{\!{}_{0}}+\mathbb{Z}
and
\{\beta_{j}\lambda\}_{\!{}_{0}}+\mathbb{Z}

We say that

for all

is always positive, it suffices to check that

for all

and
\lambda\beta_{j}
according to

simultaneously and check if among the first

of the joint ordered list there are always at least as many entries of the form

as of the form

.

3.1.3
Solutions of the hypergeometric differential equation modulo
p

We now discuss the question of determining the dimension of the space of solutions of a hypergeometric differential equation modulo
p
in
\mathbb{F}_{p}[\![x]\!]
when
p
does not divide
d
.
It turns out that this dimension is also encoded by the relative position of the exponentials of
\alpha_{i}
and
\beta_{j}
on the unit complex circle;
the slight difference between what precedes is that we will not work with the reductions of
\alpha_{i}
and
\beta_{j}
modulo
\mathbb{Z}
, but modulo
p
.
More precisely, for each index
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
, we consider the complex number

\alpha_{i,p}\coloneqq\zeta_{2p}\cdot\zeta_{p}^{-\alpha_{i}\bmod p}=\exp\left(2\mathbf{i}\pi\cdot\left(\frac{1}{2p}-\frac{\alpha_{i}\bmod p}{p}\right)\right)

and color it in green. We underline that the previous definition is not ambiguous because the fraction
(\alpha_{i}\bmod p)/p
is well-defined modulo
\mathbb{Z}
.
In concrete terms,
\zeta_{p}^{-\alpha_{i}\bmod p}=\zeta_{p}^{-\alpha_{i,p}}
where
\alpha_{i,p}
is an
integer
which is congruent to
\alpha_{i}
modulo
p
.
Similarly, we color in yellow the points

\beta_{j,p}\coloneqq\zeta_{p}\cdot\zeta_{p}^{-\beta_{j}\bmod p}=\exp\left(2\mathbf{i}\pi\cdot\left(\frac{1}{p}-\frac{\beta_{j}\bmod p}{p}\right)\right)

for
j
varying in
\{1,\ldots,n\}
as well. Since
\beta_{n}=1
, the point
1\in\mathbb{C}
is always colored in yellow.

Definition 3.1.16
.

The
p
-interlacing number
of
(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})
is the number of times we observe a green point followed by a yellow one (or, equivalently, a yellow one followed by a green) when running clockwise through the unit circle.

The
p
-interlacing number will allow us to answer Question
2.3.13
for hypergeometric equations.
The proof of the following result can be easily deduced from Katz’ results on the
p
-curvature of a hypergeometric operator
\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{H}(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})

[
Kat90
, Proof of Sublemma 5.5.2.1, p. 174 f.]
.
Below, we reformulate it in a more down-to-earth fashion, avoiding the use of the
p
-curvature.

Theorem 3.1.17
.

Let
\mathcal{T}_{p}(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})
denote the set of exponents
t
corresponding to a yellow point for which there is a green point before we reach the next yellow point when running clockwise on the unit complex circle.
For
t\in\mathcal{T}_{p}(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})
, we denote by
k_{t}
the exponent corresponding to the next green point. We set

f_{t}(x)\coloneqq\sum_{k=t}^{k_{t}-1}h(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta};k)\cdot p^{-v_{p}(h(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta};t))}x^{k}.

(3.1.3)

The family
(f_{t}(x))_{t\in\mathcal{T}_{p}(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})}
is a
\mathbb{F}_{p}(x^{p})
-basis of the space of solutions of
\mathcal{H}(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})
.

In particular, the dimension of this vector space is given by the
p
-interlacing number of
(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})
.

Proof (after Katz).

We view
\mathbb{F}_{p}(x)
as a
p
-dimensional vector space over
\mathbb{F}_{p}(x^{p})
. The operator
\mathcal{H}(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})
is a linear endomorphism of this vector space. It is determined by
\mathcal{H}(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})(x^{k})=-A(k)x^{k+1}+B(k)x^{k}
, where
A(k)=\prod_{i=1}^{n}(k+\alpha_{i})
and
B(k)=\prod_{j=1}^{n}(k+\beta_{j}-1)
.
We chose
m\in\mathbb{F}_{p}
, such that
m\equiv 1-\alpha_{i}\pmod{p}
for some
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
. The matrix of this linear map in the basis
(x^{m},x^{m+1},\ldots,x^{m+p-1})
is of the form

\left(\begin{matrix}B(m)&0&0&\cdots&0&-A(m+p-1)\\
-A(m)&B(m+1)&0&\cdots&0&0\\
0&-A(m+1)&B(m+2)&&0&0\\
\vdots&&\ddots&\ddots&&\vdots\\
0&0&0&\ddots&B(m+p-2)&0\\
0&0&0&&-A(m+p-2)&B(m+p-1)\end{matrix}\right),

where the top right entry is zero, as
A(m+p-1)=0
by definition. The matrix decomposes into a block diagonal form, each zero of
A
separating two blocks. Each of the blocks is a lower diagonal matrix
with consecutive values of
B
on the main diagonal, and consecutive values of
A
, all nonzero, on the diagonal below. The co-rank of this matrix is at most one, as the bottom left minor of order one less is non-zero. Moreover as the blocks are lower triangular, the co-rank is zero is and only if all entries on the main diagonal are different from zero.

Consider such a block of co-rank one and let
b\coloneqq 1-\beta_{j}\pmod{p}
be the last zero of
B
in this block, and let
a\coloneqq-\alpha_{i^{\prime}}\pmod{p}
the following zero of
A
. It is then easy to convince oneself that

v_{b}\coloneqq\left(\begin{matrix}0&\cdots&0&1&\frac{A(b)}{B(b+1)}&\frac{A(b)A(b+1)}{B(b+1)B(b+2)}&\cdots&\frac{A(b)\cdots A(a-1)}{B(b+1)\cdots B(a)}\end{matrix}\right)^{\top}

is a well-defined vector in the kernel of this block. It corresponds to the function
h(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta};b)^{-1}f_{b}(x)
. The proposition follows from these observations.
∎

3.2
Algebraicity modulo
p
and Galois groups

In
[
Var24
]
, polynomials annihilating reductions of hypergeometric functions modulo
p
were constructed.
In this subsection, we recall the methods of
loc. cit.
and develop them further in order to get annihilating polynomials of smaller sizes.
This will be important for the computations of the Galois groups later on.

3.2.1
The Dwork map

A main tool in the construction is the Dwork map
\mathfrak{D}_{p}:\mathbb{Z}_{(p)}\to\mathbb{Z}_{(p)},
defined by Euclidean division:
as before, we write
\gamma\in\mathbb{Z}_{(p)}
uniquely as
\gamma=pQ(\gamma,p)-R(\gamma,p)
with
Q(\gamma,p)\in\mathbb{Z}_{(p)}
and
0\leq R(\gamma,p)<p
.
We set
\mathfrak{D}_{p}(\gamma)\coloneqq Q(\gamma,p)
. In other words,
\mathfrak{D}_{p}
is uniquely defined by the relation

p\cdot\mathfrak{D}_{p}(x)-x\in\{0,1,\ldots,p{-}1\}.

We collect a few facts about
\mathfrak{D}_{p}
.

Lemma 3.2.1
.

(1)

For
\gamma\in\mathbb{Z}_{(p)}
, let

-\gamma=\gamma^{(0)}+p\gamma^{(1)}+p^{2}\gamma^{(2)}+\cdots

be the
p
-adic expansion of
-\gamma
. Then, for any integer
k\geq 1
,

-\mathfrak{D}_{p}^{k}(\gamma)=\gamma^{(k)}+p\gamma^{(k+1)}+p^{2}\gamma^{(k+2)}+\cdots.

(2)

The Dwork operator
\mathfrak{D}_{p}
acts on
\mathbb{Z}_{(p)}/\mathbb{Z}
; more precisely, for any fixed
d\in\mathbb{N}
not divisible by
p
, it acts on
(\frac{1}{d}\mathbb{Z})/\mathbb{Z}
by multiplication with
p^{-1}
.

(3)

The Dwork operator
\mathfrak{D}_{p}
maps
(0,1]\cap\frac{1}{d}\mathbb{Z}
to itself.

(4)

For
\gamma\in(0,1]\cap\mathbb{Z}^{\times}_{(p)}
, the smallest positive integer
\ell
such that
\mathfrak{D}_{p}^{\ell}(\gamma)=\gamma
is equal to the smallest positive integer
\ell
such that
p^{\ell}\gamma\equiv\gamma\pmod{\mathbb{Z}}
, that is, if
\gamma=\frac{c}{d}
with
\gcd(c,d)=1
,
\ell
is given as the order of
p
modulo
d
.

Proof.

The first assertion follows directly from the definition when
k=1
. For general
k
, it follows by induction.
For (2), we write
\gamma=\frac{c}{d}
with
\gcd(c,d)=1
.
We then have
\frac{c}{d}=p\mathfrak{D}_{p}(\gamma)-R(\gamma,p)
with
R(\gamma,p)\in\{0,1,\ldots,p-1\}
, so clearly
\mathfrak{D}_{p}(\gamma)\in\frac{1}{d}\mathbb{Z}
, say
\mathfrak{D}_{p}(\gamma)=\frac{e}{d}
. Then
c\equiv pe\pmod{d}
, so
e
only depends on
c\bmod d
and
\mathfrak{D}_{p}
indeed acts by multiplication with
p^{-1}
.
For (3), keeping the previous notation, we note that
e>p
implies
c=pe-R(\gamma,p)d
and then
c>d
, contradicting
0<c\leq d
. Likewise, if
e\leq 0
, then
c\leq 0
, also a contradiction.
The last point is obvious from what precedes.
∎

For
\gamma\in\mathbb{Z}_{(p)}
, let
\ell_{p}(\gamma)
denote the smallest positive integer
\ell
such that
p^{\ell}\gamma\equiv\gamma\pmod{\mathbb{Z}}
as in Lemma
3.2.1
.(4).
More generally, given an arbitrary sets of parameters
\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta}\in\mathbb{Z}_{(p)}^{n+(n-1)}
, we define
\ell_{p}(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})
as the smallest positive integer such that
\mathfrak{D}_{p}^{\ell_{p}(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})\equiv\boldsymbol{\alpha}\pmod{\mathbb{Z}}
and
\mathfrak{D}_{p}(\boldsymbol{\beta})^{\ell_{p}(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})}\equiv\boldsymbol{\beta}\pmod{\mathbb{Z}}
, where these equations are to be read as equalities of sets. In particular,
\ell_{p}(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})
always divides the order of
p
in
(\mathbb{Z}/d\mathbb{Z})^{\times}
, where
d
denotes the common denominator of the parameters. However, in general there is no equality, as demonstrated by the following example.

Example 3.2.2
.

We have
\ell_{13}((\frac{1}{7},\frac{6}{7}),(\frac{1}{2}))=1
, as
\mathfrak{D}_{13}(\frac{1}{7})=\frac{6}{7}
,
\mathfrak{D}_{13}(\frac{6}{7})=\frac{1}{7}
and
\mathfrak{D}_{13}(\frac{1}{2})=\frac{1}{2}
. At the same time we have
\operatorname{ord}_{14}(13)=2
.

When the parameters
\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta}
are clear from the context, we will suppress them in the notation and write simply
\ell_{p}
for
\ell_{p}(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})
.

3.2.2
The hypergeometric relation graph

We are going to construct a graph, whose vertices correspond to parameters of hypergeometric functions, and whose edges will encode algebraic relations between those functions.
We denote by

\mathcal{V}_{p}\coloneq\big(\mathbb{Z}_{(p)}\setminus(-\mathbb{N})\big)^{n+(n-1)}

the set of vertices corresponding to all possible parameters.
Moreover, we let

\mathcal{V}_{p}(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})\coloneqq\big\{(\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\prime})\in\mathcal{V}_{p}\,\,:\,\,\alpha_{i}\equiv\alpha_{i}^{\prime}\!\!\!\!\pmod{\mathbb{Z}},\,\,\beta_{j}\equiv\beta_{j}^{\prime}\!\!\!\!\pmod{\mathbb{Z}}\big\}.

These sets form a partition of
\mathcal{V}_{p}
.

We define the
hypergeometric relation graph

\mathcal{G}_{p}
as the following directed graph with labeled edges. The vertices are given by
\mathcal{V}_{p}
. For the edges we carry out the following construction. For
\boldsymbol{\gamma}\in\mathbb{Z}_{(p)}^{m}
and let

, we put

\mathfrak{D}_{p,r}(\gamma)\coloneqq\begin{cases}\mathfrak{D}_{p}(\gamma)+1&\text{if $(\gamma)_{r}\in p\mathbb{Z}_{(p)}$ }\\
\mathfrak{D}_{p}(\gamma)&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

For every

, as defined in Equation (
3.1.4

with

with a directed edge

labeled by the polynomial
Q(e)\coloneqq f_{s}(x)
as defined by Equation (
3.1.3
).

We define
\mathcal{G}_{p}(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})
as the sub-graph, of
\mathcal{G}_{p}
containing
(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})
, and all vertices reachable by an oriented path, starting at
(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})
.

Lemma 3.2.3
.

(1)

Let

For (1), we first assume by contradiction that

is in
\mathcal{S}_{p}(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})
only depends on the congruence class of
p\bmod d
.
Indeed, choose
\Delta
with
\Delta p\equiv 1\pmod{d}
. We have
v_{p}(h(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta};r))=\mathcal{M}(\beta_{j(r)}\Delta,\Delta)
by a combination of Lemmata
3.1.10
and
3.1.12
. Here the right-hand side only depends on
p\bmod d
. Also
\mathfrak{D}_{p}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})
,
\mathfrak{D}_{p}(\boldsymbol{\beta})
and the value of
\ell_{p}
only depend on the congruence class of
p\bmod d
. Thus, the number of vertices on each level and the number of levels is fixed for each congruence class, according to part (1). Also, by (1), all edges between vertices of two consecutive levels, exist in
\mathcal{G}_{p}(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})
. Thus the claim follows.

We finally prove (4). We have seen in the proof of part (1) that the width at level
k
is equal to the cardinality of
\mathcal{S}_{p}(\mathfrak{D}_{p}^{k-1}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}),\mathfrak{D}_{p}^{k-1}(\boldsymbol{\beta}))
.
From the definition of
\mathcal{M}
(see Equation (
3.1.1
)), we find that this quantity equals the number of
\beta_{j}
such that
\mathcal{M}(\Delta^{k}\beta_{j},\Delta^{k})=0
. This proves the first statement.

By Theorem
3.1.3
a hypergeometric function can be reduced modulo
p
, if and only if it satisfies Christol’s interlacing condition for all
\lambda\in\langle p\bmod d\rangle=\langle\Delta\bmod d\rangle
. According to Equation (
3.0.3
), a basis of solutions at
0
of
\mathcal{H}(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})y=0
is given monomial multiples of
F_{(j)}\coloneqq{}_{n}F_{n-1}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{(j)},\boldsymbol{\beta}_{(j)};x)
, where
\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{(j)}=\boldsymbol{\alpha}+1-\beta_{j}
and
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{(j)}=\boldsymbol{\beta}+1-\beta_{j}
. Let
\mathcal{M}_{(j)}
denote the function
\mathcal{M}
for the sets of parameters
\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{(j)},\boldsymbol{\beta}_{(j)}
. We have
\mathcal{M}_{(j)}(\cdot,\lambda)=\mathcal{M}(\cdot,\lambda)-\mathcal{M}(\lambda\beta_{j},\lambda)
. The solution
F_{(j)}
can be reduced modulo
p
if
\mathcal{M}_{(j)}(\cdot,\lambda)
is always non-negative for all
\lambda\in\langle p\bmod d\rangle
. As
\mathcal{M}(\cdot,\lambda)
is always non-negative for all
\lambda
, but assumes the value
0
, this can only be the case if
\mathcal{M}(\lambda\beta_{j},\lambda)=0
. So the number of solutions that can be reduced modulo
p
is less than the number of
b_{j}
such that
\mathcal{M}(\lambda\beta_{j},\lambda^{k})=0
for all
\lambda\in\langle p\bmod p\rangle
, and the claim follows.
∎

3.2.3
Finding algebraic relations

We now want to find relations between several reductions of hypergeometric functions modulo a fixed prime number
p
and combine them to obtain annihilating polynomials for the reduction of a single hypergeometric function. We recall that we have determined in Theorem
3.1.3
the set of prime numbers for which a hypergeometric function can be reduced.

In the language of the hypergeometric relation graph
\mathcal{G}_{p}(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})
introduced above, a modified version of Lemma 8.1 of
[
Var24
]
, which we will need, reads as follows.

Proposition 3.2.6
.

Let
v=(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})\in\mathcal{V}_{p}
. If
p
does not divide any of the numerators of the elements of
\mathfrak{D}_{p}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})\cup\mathfrak{D}_{p}(\boldsymbol{\beta})
and if
{}_{n}F_{n-1}\left(v;x\right)
can be reduced modulo
p
, then

{}_{n}F_{n-1}\left(v;x\right)\equiv\sum_{\mathclap{v\stackrel{{\scriptstyle e}}{{\longrightarrow}}v^{\prime}}}Q(e)\cdot{}_{n}F_{n-1}\left(v^{\prime};x\right)^{p}\pmod{p}.

Proof.

According to Theorem
3.1.17
, the polynomials

f_{t}(x)=\sum_{k=t}^{k_{t}-1}h(v;k)p^{-v_{p}(h(v;t))}x^{k}

with
t\in\mathcal{T}_{p}(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})
form a basis of solutions of
\mathcal{H}(v)
over
\mathbb{F}_{p}(\!(x^{p})\!)
.
Since
{}_{n}F_{n-1}\left(v;x\right)
is a solution that can be reduced modulo
p
, there exist
g_{t}\in\mathbb{F}_{p}(\!(x)\!)
such that

{}_{n}F_{n-1}\left(v;x\right)\equiv\sum_{\mathclap{t\in\mathcal{T}_{p}(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})}}f_{t}(x)\cdot g_{t}(x^{p})\pmod{p}.

We recall that, for
r\in\{0,\ldots,p{-}1\}
, we have introduce the section operator

\sigma_{r}:\mathbb{F}_{p}[\![x]\!]\to\mathbb{F}_{p}[\![x]\!],\quad\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a_{n}x^{n}\mapsto\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a_{pn+r}x^{n}.

Applying
\sigma_{t}
to the previous equality, we obtain

\sigma_{t}\big({}_{n}F_{n-1}\left(v;x\right)\big)\equiv\tilde{h}(v;t)\cdot g_{t}(x)\pmod{p},

where, by definition,
\tilde{h}(v;t)=h(v;t)\>p^{-v_{p}(h(v;t))}\in\mathbb{Z}_{(p)}^{\times}
.
Besides, it follows from
[
Var24
, Lemma 8.7.(5)]
that, for all
t\in\mathcal{T}_{p}(v)
,

\sigma_{t}\big({}_{n}F_{n-1}\left(v;x\right)\big)\equiv h(v;t)\cdot{}_{n}F_{n-1}\left(\mathfrak{D}_{p,t}(v);x\right)\pmod{p}.

Comparing these congruences, we find that
g_{t}(x)=0
whenever
h(v;t)\equiv 0\pmod{p}
,
i.e.
,
t\in\mathcal{T}_{p}(v)\setminus\mathcal{S}_{p}(v)
and
g_{t}(x)={}_{n}F_{n-1}\left(\mathfrak{D}_{p,t}(v);x\right)
otherwise. For
t\in\mathcal{S}_{p}(v),
we have
Q(v\to\mathfrak{D}_{p,t}(v))=f_{t}
and the proposition follows.
∎

Remark 3.2.7
.

Proposition
3.2.6
is connected to the notion of Frobenius preimages we discussed in Subsection
2.3.3
.
Precisely, a theorem of Kedlaya
[
Ked22
, Theorem 4.1.2]
(which is in fact attributed to Dwork in
loc. cit.
) indicates that the hypergeometric equation
\mathcal{H}(\mathfrak{D}_{p}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}),\mathfrak{D}_{p}(\boldsymbol{\beta}))
is the preimage by Frobenius of
\mathcal{H}(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})
.
The relation of Proposition
3.2.6
can be seen as an incarnation modulo
p
of this property.

Proposition
3.2.6
provides a large set of algebraic relations between hypergeometric series in positive characteristic.
We will now combine them in order to find an annihilating polynomial for
{}_{n}F_{n-1}\left(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta};x\right)
.

Theorem 3.2.8
.

Let
(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})\in\mathcal{V}_{p}
. If
p>2d\cdot\max\{|\alpha_{i}|+1,|\beta_{j}|+1\}

and
{}_{n}F_{n-1}\left(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta};x\right)
can be reduced modulo
p
then there exists a relation of the form

A_{0}(x){}_{n}F_{n-1}\left(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta};x\right)+\cdots+A_{w}(x){}_{n}F_{n-1}\left(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta};x\right)^{q^{w}}\equiv 0\pmod{p},

where
q=p^{\ell_{p}(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})}
,
w
is the width of the graph
\mathcal{G}_{p}(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})

and the
A_{i}(x)
are in
\mathbb{F}_{q}[x]
and do not all vanish.

Proof.

We use the theory of Frobenius modules (see Definition
2.3.2
).
We define

M\coloneqq\bigoplus_{\mathclap{v\in\mathcal{G}_{p}(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})}}\,\mathbb{F}_{p}(x)\cdot m_{v},

where the notation means that the sum runs over the set of
vertices

v
of
\mathcal{G}_{p}(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})
.
We then turn
M
into an object of the category
\mathrm{Mod}^{p}_{\mathbb{F}_{p}(x)}
by setting

\varphi_{M}(m_{v})=\sum_{v^{\prime}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle e}}{{\longrightarrow}}v}Q(e)\cdot m_{v^{\prime}}.

To it, is attached the
\mathbb{F}_{p}
-linear representation
\mathbf{V}(M)
of
\Gal(\mathbb{F}_{p}(x)^{\text{\rm sep}}/\mathbb{F}_{p}(x))
defined by Formula (
2.3.3
).
A straightforward computation shows that
\mathbf{V}(M)
consists of families
(f_{v})_{v\in\mathcal{G}_{p}(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})}
of elements of
\mathbb{F}_{p}(x)^{\text{\rm sep}}
satisfying the equations

f_{v}=\sum_{\mathclap{v\stackrel{{\scriptstyle e}}{{\longrightarrow}}v^{\prime}}}Q(e)\cdot f_{v^{\prime}}^{p}.

In particular, Proposition
3.2.6
shows that it contains the family
\big({}_{n}F_{n-1}(v;x)\bmod p\big)_{v\in\mathcal{G}_{p}(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})}
.

On the other hand,
M
admits a canonical decomposition corresponding to the level partition of the graph
\mathcal{G}_{p}(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})
, namely we have

M=M_{0}\oplus M_{1}\oplus\cdots\oplus M_{\ell_{p}(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})-1},

where the summand
M_{k}
corresponds to the vertices of level
k
.
The fact that an edge in
\mathcal{G}_{p}(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})
connects a vertex of level
k
to a vertex of level
k{+}1
implies that
\varphi_{M}
maps
M_{k+1}
to
M_{k}
.
It follows that each
M_{k}
is stable by the composite
\varphi_{M}^{\ell_{p}(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})}
, which thus defines a structure of
q
-Frobenius module on this space for
q=p^{\ell_{p}(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})}
.

If
\mathcal{G}_{p}(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})_{0}
denote the set of vertices of
\mathcal{G}_{p}(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})
of level
0
,

\mathbf{V}(M_{0})
is a finite dimensional
\mathbb{F}_{q}
-representation of
\Gal(\mathbb{F}_{q}(x)^{\text{\rm sep}}/\mathbb{F}_{q}(x))
and we have an inclusion

\mathbf{V}(M_{0})\subseteq\big(\mathbb{Q}(x)^{\text{\rm sep}}\big)^{\mathcal{G}_{p}(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})_{0}}

commuting with the Galois action. Moreover
\mathbf{V}(M_{0})
contains a vector whose
(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})
-coordinate is
{}_{n}F_{n-1}\left(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta};x\right)\bmod p
.
Let then

\varpi:\big(\mathbb{Q}(x)^{\text{\rm sep}}\big)^{\mathcal{G}_{p}(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})_{0}}\to\mathbb{Q}(x)^{\text{\rm sep}}

denote the projection on the
(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})
-coordinate and
V
be the image of
\mathbf{V}\big(M_{0}\big)
under
\varpi
.
Clearly
V
is finite dimensional over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
, it is stable by the action of
\Gal(\mathbb{F}_{q}(x)^{\text{\rm sep}}/\mathbb{F}_{q}(x))
and it contains
{}_{n}F_{n-1}\left(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta};x\right)\bmod p
.
These properties permits to build an annihilating polynomial for
{}_{n}F_{n-1}\left(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta};x\right)\bmod p
, namely

Z(Y)\coloneqq\prod_{y\in V}\,(Y-y).

The stability by the Galois action ensures that
Z(Y)
has coefficients in
\mathbb{F}_{q}(x)
. Moreover,
the fact that
V
is a
\mathbb{F}_{q}
-linear vector space implies that
Z(Y)
is a
q
-linearized polynomial,
i.e.
, it has the shape

Z(Y)=c_{0}Y+c_{1}Y^{q}+\cdots+c_{s}Y^{q^{s}}\qquad(c_{i}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}(x)).

By comparing degrees, we find that
s
is the dimension of
V
over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
.

It then only remains to show that
s\leq w
.
For this, we recall that we have introduced in Definition
2.3.7
the
étale part
of a Frobenius module.
Applying Theorem
2.3.5
and Lemma
2.3.8
, we get

s=\dim_{\mathbb{F}_{q}}V\leq\dim_{\mathbb{F}_{q}}\mathbf{V}(M_{0})=\dim_{\mathbb{F}_{q}}\mathbf{V}\big(M_{0}^{\text{\rm ét}}\big)=\dim_{\mathbb{Q}(x)}M_{0}^{\text{\rm ét}}.

On the other hand, we observe that
\varphi_{M}
induces a map
M_{k+1}^{\text{\rm ét}}\to M_{k}^{\text{\rm ét}}
.
We need to be careful that it is not linear but we can actually make it linear by twisting the domain,
i.e.
, we consider

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\psi_{k}:\mathbb{F}_{q}(x)\otimes_{\varphi,\mathbb{F}_{q}(x)}M_{k+1}^{\text{\rm ét}}&\longrightarrow&M_{k}^{\text{\rm ét}}\\
\lambda\otimes m&\mapsto&\lambda\varphi_{M}(m)\end{array}

which is now
\mathbb{F}_{q}(x)
-linear.
Furthermore, the composite of the
\psi_{k}
(correctly twisted) is the linearization of
\varphi_{M}^{\ell_{p}(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})}
acting on
M_{0}^{\text{\rm ét}}
and hence is an isomorphism by étaleness.
We conclude that each
\psi_{k}
is itself an isomorphism, which finally leaves up with the inequality

\textstyle\dim_{\mathbb{F}_{q}(x)}M_{0}^{\text{\rm ét}}=\min_{k}\dim_{\mathbb{F}_{q}(x)}M_{k}^{\text{\rm ét}}.

Let
w_{k}
be the width of level
k
of
\mathcal{G}_{p}(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})
.
Under our assumptions on
p
, it follows from Proposition
3.2.5
that

\dim_{\mathbb{F}_{q}(x)}{M^{\text{\rm ét}}_{k}}\leq\dim_{\mathbb{F}_{q}(x)}{M_{k}}=w_{k}

whenever
k>0
. This estimation also holds for
k=0
if the vertex
(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})
survives in
\mathcal{G}_{p}(\bar{\boldsymbol{\alpha}},\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}})
.
On the contrary, if it does not, Proposition
3.2.5
again ensures that there is no edge in
\mathcal{G}_{p}(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})
ending at
(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})
.
Consequently, the corresponding vector
m_{(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})}
cannot be in
M_{0}^{\text{\rm ét}}
and we deduce

\dim_{\mathbb{F}_{q}(x)}{M^{\text{\rm ét}}_{0}}\leq\dim_{\mathbb{F}_{q}(x)}{M_{0}}-1=w_{0}.

In all cases, we thus have
\dim_{\mathbb{F}_{q}(x)}{M^{\text{\rm ét}}_{k}}\leq w_{k}
, which finally implies that
s\leq\min_{k}w_{k}=w
as desired.
∎

3.2.4
Approaching Galois groups

Our objective is now to reinterpret the previous results along the lines of Conjecture
2.2.6
.
For this, we need to exhibit a number field whose residue fields are somehow related to the values
\ell_{p}(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})
.

In all what follows, our number fields will all be defined as subfields of the field of complex numbers
\mathbb{C}
.
For a positive integer
d
, we set
\zeta_{d}=\exp(2\mathbf{i}\pi/d)\in\mathbb{C}
; it is a primitive
d
-th root of unity and it generates the cyclotomic field
\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{d})
.
We recall that
\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{d})
is a Galois extension of
\mathbb{Q}
and that its Galois group is canonically isomorphic to
(\mathbb{Z}/d\mathbb{Z})^{\times}
:
an element
\lambda\in(\mathbb{Z}/d\mathbb{Z})^{\times}
acts on
\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{d})
by
\zeta_{d}\mapsto\zeta_{d}^{\lambda}
.
In particular, we notice that the element
-1\in(\mathbb{Z}/d\mathbb{Z})^{\times}
acts as complex conjugation.

As before, we let
d
be the smallest common denominator of all the parameters
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
and
\boldsymbol{\beta}
. Further we define the group

D(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})\coloneqq\big\{\,\lambda\in(\mathbb{Z}/d\mathbb{Z})^{\times}\,:\,\lambda{\cdot}\boldsymbol{\alpha}\equiv\boldsymbol{\alpha}\!\!\!\!\pmod{\mathbb{Z}}\text{ and }\lambda{\cdot}\boldsymbol{\beta}\equiv\boldsymbol{\beta}\!\!\!\!\pmod{\mathbb{Z}}\text{ as sets}\,\big\}.

We can map
D(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})
to a product of symmetric groups.
Precisely, let
\mathfrak{S}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})
(resp.
\mathfrak{S}(\boldsymbol{\beta})
) be the group of bijections of
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
(resp.
\boldsymbol{\beta}
) viewed as a multiset.
We then have a group morphism
\sigma:D(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})\to\mathfrak{S}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})\times\mathfrak{S}(\boldsymbol{\beta})
that takes
\lambda
to the multiplication by
\lambda
.

Lemma 3.2.9
.

The morphism
\sigma
is injective.

Proof.

We write
\alpha_{i}=\frac{a_{i}}{d}
,
\beta_{i}=\frac{b_{i}}{d}
.
By definition of
d
, we have
\gcd(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n},b_{1},\ldots,b_{n-1},d)=1
as otherwise the fractions
\frac{a_{i}}{d}
and
\frac{b_{i}}{d}
could all be simplified by a common factor, which is excluded.
By Bézout’s theorem, there exist integers
u_{1},\ldots,u_{n},v_{1},\ldots,v_{n-1},w
such that

u_{1}a_{1}+\cdots+u_{n}a_{n}+v_{1}b_{1}+\cdots+v_{n-1}b_{n-1}+wd=1.

We now assume that we are given
\lambda,\mu\in D(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})
such that
\sigma(\lambda)=\sigma(\mu)
.
Then, following the definition, we find that
\lambda a_{i}\equiv\mu a_{i}\pmod{d}
and
\lambda b_{i}\equiv\mu b_{i}\pmod{d}
for all
i
.
Weighting these congruences with the coefficients
u_{i}
and
v_{i}
and summing them, we get

\lambda\cdot(u_{1}a_{1}+\cdots+u_{n}a_{n}+v_{1}b_{1}+\cdots+v_{n-1}b_{n-1})\\
\equiv\mu\cdot(u_{1}a_{1}+\cdots+u_{n}a_{n}+v_{1}b_{1}+\cdots+v_{n-1}b_{n-1})\pmod{d}

which finally reduces to
\lambda\equiv\mu\pmod{d}
.
∎

Definition 3.2.10
.

We define
K(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})
as the subextension of
\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{d})
corresponding to
D(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})

via
Galois correspondence.

In concrete terms,
K(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})\coloneqq\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{d})^{D(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})}
.
The field
K(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})
is undoubtedly the number field we are looking for, as we shall demonstrate in the sequel.
First of all, we observe that it is closely related to the Galois group of the hypergeometric differential equation in characteristic zero.
Indeed, it is the subfield generated by the entries of the monodromy matrices exhibited in Theorem
3.1.9
and we know that those matrices generate the differential Galois group as an algebraic group.

Now, we make the connection between the field
K(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})
and the numbers
\ell_{p}(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})
,
which will eventually relate
K(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})
to the Galois groups of the reduction of the hypergeometric series
{}_{n}F_{n-1}\left(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta};x\right)
modulo the primes.

Proposition 3.2.11
.

Let
p
be a prime number which does not divide
d
, and let
\mathfrak{p}
be a prime in
K(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})
above
p
.
Then the residue field of
K(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})
at
\mathfrak{p}
has degree
\ell_{p}(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})
over
\mathbb{F}_{p}
.

Proof.

Throughout the proof, we omit the parameter
(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})
in the notation and simply write
K
and
\ell_{p}
for
K(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})
and
\ell_{p}(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})
respectively.
The assumption on
p
implies that
p
is not ramified in the extension
\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{d})/\mathbb{Q}
.
Let
\mathfrak{q}
be a prime of
\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{d})
above
\mathfrak{p}
and write
k_{\mathfrak{p}}
(resp.
k_{\mathfrak{q}}
) for the residue field of
K
at
\mathfrak{p}
(resp. of
\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{d})
at
\mathfrak{q}
).
The Galois group of the extension
k_{\mathfrak{q}}/\mathbb{F}_{p}
is the subgroup of
(\mathbb{Z}/d\mathbb{Z})^{\times}
generated by
p
; we call it
P
.
Since
K
is defined as the fixed subfield by
D
, we deduce that
k_{\mathfrak{p}}
is the subfield of
k_{\mathfrak{q}}
fixed by
P\cap D
. Thus

\Gal(k_{\mathfrak{p}}/\mathbb{F}_{p})=P/(D\cap P).

Besides the cardinality of
P/(D\cap P)
is the smallest positive integer
e
such that
p^{-e}\in D
.
The latter condition is equivalent to the statement “
\mathfrak{D}^{e}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})=\boldsymbol{\alpha}
and
\mathfrak{D}^{e}(\boldsymbol{\beta})=\boldsymbol{\beta}
”
given that, according to Lemma
3.2.1
.(2), the Dwork map acts by division by
p
on
\mathbb{Z}_{(p)}/\mathbb{Z}
.
Comparing with the definition of
\ell_{p}
, we find that
P/(D\cap P)
has cardinality
\ell_{p}
, which concludes the proof.
∎

Theorem 3.2.12
.

Let
p
be a prime number with
p>2d\cdot\max\{|\alpha_{i}|+1,|\beta_{j}|+1\}
and let
\mathfrak{p}
be a prime in
K(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})
above
p
.
Then

\Gal\big({}_{n}F_{n-1}\left(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta};x\right)\,\big|\,k_{\mathfrak{p}}(x)\big)\,\subseteq\,\GL_{w}(k_{\mathfrak{p}})

where
w
denotes the width of the graph
\mathcal{G}_{p}(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})
.

Proof.

By Proposition
3.2.11
, we have
k_{\mathfrak{p}}\simeq\mathbb{F}_{p^{\ell_{p}(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})}}
. With this, the theorem follows from Theorem
3.2.8
.
∎

We conclude this section by looking at some examples we already considered in Section
2
.

Example 3.2.13
.

We recall the example from Subsection
2.1.4
:

f(x)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\binom{2n}{n}^{3}\cdot x^{n}={\textstyle{}_{3}F_{2}\left(\big(\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}\big),(1,1);64x\right)}.

Clearly
\ell_{p}(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})=1
for all prime numbers
p
, as
(\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z})^{\times}
is trivial. Moreover, as all bottom parameters are equal to
1
, it follows from Example
3.2.4
that the width
w
of the graph is
1
, and consequently, the Galois group
\Gal\big(f(x)\,\big|\,\mathbb{F}_{p}(x)\big)
embeds into
\mathbb{F}_{p}^{\times}
. This agrees with the
ad-hoc
arguments deployed in Subsection
2.1.4
. However, we cannot provide a systematic argument explaining the dichotomy of the Galois groups exhibited in Equation (
2.1.5
) here.

Example 3.2.14
.

We now consider the example
f(x)={}_{3}F_{2}\left(\big(\frac{1}{9},\frac{4}{9},\frac{5}{9}\big),\big(\frac{1}{3},1\big);x\right)
from Subsection
2.1.6
.
We have seen in Example
3.1.8
that
f(x)
is globally bounded.
An easy computation shows that
\ell_{p}(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})=\ell_{p}=\operatorname{ord}_{9}(p)
and these values are given by

\ell_{p}=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }p\equiv 1\pmod{9}\\
2&\text{if }p\equiv 8\pmod{9}\\
3&\text{if }p\equiv 4,7\pmod{9}\\
6&\text{if }p\equiv 2,5\pmod{9}\end{cases}

We next discuss the width of the graphs
\mathcal{G}_{p}(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})
.
In Figure
3
, we see that, for
\lambda=2,5,8
, we have
\mathcal{M}(\lambda^{k}\beta_{j},\lambda^{k})=0
only for
\beta_{j}=1
and, for
\lambda=1,4,7
, the equation also holds for
\beta_{j}=\frac{1}{3}
. We have
\langle p\bmod 9\rangle\subseteq\{1,4,7\}
if and only if
p\equiv 1,4,7
. This shows that the width
w
of the graph is
2
in the first case and
1
in the second case. By Theorem
3.2.12
, we conclude that
\Gal(f(x)\mid\mathbb{F}_{q}(x))
embeds into
\GL_{w}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
, where
q=p^{\ell_{p}(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})}
. If we pass to
\Gal(f(x)\mid\mathbb{F}_{p}(x))
we have additionally the Frobenius morphism acting.
Altogether, this shows that the Galois groups of
f(x)
over
\mathbb{F}_{p}(x)
embed into the semidirect products given in Equation (
2.1.11
). We cannot make any statement about the index or precise nature of the subgroup with the techniques developed here, although we conjecture equality for all prime numbers based on computer experiments.

3.3
The case of Gaussian hypergeometric functions

We now consider the case of Gaussian hypergeometric functions with bottom parameter
1
, that are functions of the form
{}_{2}F_{1}\left(\boldsymbol{\alpha},(1);x\right)
with
\boldsymbol{\alpha}=(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2})\in\mathbb{Q}^{2}
and
\boldsymbol{\beta}=(1)
.
While this is an extremely particular case of globally bounded
D
-finite functions, already for such function an astonishing amount of nontrivial phenomena occur.
We present strong evidence towards an affirmative answer to Conjecture
2.2.6
(and its refinements) in this case.

Throughout this subsection, the letter
p
refers to a prime number which is coprime with
d
.
For simplicity, we will often omit the parameter
(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})
in the notation when no confusion can occur;
for example, we shall simply write
\ell_{p}
for
\ell_{p}(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})
and similarly, we set
D\coloneqq D(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})
and
K\coloneqq K(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})
.
By Lemma
3.2.9
, the group
D
has cardinality at most
2
,
i.e.
,
D=\{1,\lambda\}
with
\lambda\in(\mathbb{Z}/d\mathbb{Z})^{\times}
,
\lambda^{2}=1
.
We also define
e_{p}\coloneqq\ell_{p}/2
.

3.3.1
Estimating the Galois group

We know from Example
3.2.4
that, in our case of interest, the graph
\mathcal{G}_{p}(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})
has the
\rho
-shape drawn in Figure
6
.
Let
(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{k},(1))
denote the parameter corresponding to the vertex of level
k
in the circle part,
i.e.
,
\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{k}=\mathfrak{D}_{p}^{k^{\prime}}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})
where
k^{\prime}
is large enough and congruent to
k
modulo
\ell_{p}
.
Let also
H_{k}(x)
denote the reduction modulo
p
of the hypergeometric series attached to the parameters
(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{k},(1))
.
Similarly we define
B_{k}(x)
as the truncation at
x^{p}
of
H_{k}(x)\bmod p
; thus
B_{k}(x)
is a polynomial of degree less than
p
with coefficients in
\mathbb{F}_{p}
.
Proposition
3.2.6
ensures that

H_{k}(x)\equiv B_{k}(x)\cdot H_{k+1}^{p}(x).

(3.3.1)

Combining these equations, we get the relation
H_{0}(x)=A(x)\cdot H_{0}(x)^{q}
where
q=p^{\ell_{p}}
and

A(x)=B_{0}(x)\cdot B_{1}(x)^{p}\cdots B_{\ell_{p}-1}(x)^{p^{\ell_{p}-1}}.

(3.3.2)

Similarly, we find that there exist a polynomial
\tilde{A}(x)\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[x]
and a nonnegative integer
m
such that
{}_{2}F_{1}\left(\boldsymbol{\alpha},(1);x\right)=\tilde{A}(x)H_{0}(x)^{q^{m}}
.
We observe moreover that the relation

{}_{2}F_{1}\left(\boldsymbol{\alpha},(1);x\right)\equiv\tilde{A}(x)H_{0}(x)^{q^{m}}=\tilde{A}(x)\cdot A(x)^{1+q+\cdots+q^{m-1}}H_{0}(x)\pmod{p}

indicates that the functions
{}_{2}F_{1}\left(\boldsymbol{\alpha},(1);x\right)
and
H_{0}(x)
generate the same extension over
k_{\mathfrak{p}}(x)
.
In other words, we can assume without loss of generality that the parameters
\alpha_{1}
and
\alpha_{2}
are both in the interval
(0,1]
,
i.e.
,
H_{0}(x)={}_{2}F_{1}\left(\boldsymbol{\alpha},(1);x\right)\bmod p
.

Besides, if
\alpha_{1}
or
\alpha_{2}
is equal to
1
, the hypergeometric series
{}_{2}F_{1}\left(\boldsymbol{\alpha},(1);x\right)
is equal to
(1-x)^{-\alpha}
(where
\alpha
is the value of the other parameter),
a case we already covered in Subsection
2.1.1
.
Therefore, from now on, we suppose
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}\in(0,1)
.

The Galois groups we are interested in can be computed using Kummer theory.

Proposition 3.3.1
.

Let
\mathfrak{p}
be a prime of
K
above
p
.
The Galois group
\Gal\big({}_{2}F_{1}\left(\boldsymbol{\alpha},(1);x\right)\mid k_{\mathfrak{p}}(x)\big)
naturally embeds in
k_{\mathfrak{p}}^{\times}
and hence is a cyclic group.
Moreover, its order is

\frac{q{-}1}{\gcd(r,q{-}1)}

where
r
denotes the largest integer such that
A(x)
is
r
-th power in
k_{\mathfrak{p}}(x)
.

Proof.

By what precedes, we know that
H_{0}(x)
is a
(q{-}1)
-th root of the polynomial
A(x)\in\mathbb{F}_{p}[x]\subseteq k_{\mathfrak{p}}(x)
.
Since
k_{\mathfrak{p}}
contains all the
(q{-}1)
-th roots of unity, Kummer’s theory tells us that
\Gal(H_{0}(x)\mid k_{\mathfrak{p}}(x))
embeds into
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{q-1}(k_{\mathfrak{p}}(x))=k_{\mathfrak{p}}^{\times}

and that its order is
\frac{q{-}1}{\gcd(r,q{-}1)}
.
∎

After Proposition
3.3.1
, we are led to determine when
A(x)
is a perfect power. In general, we do not expect this to occur, although it could happen “by accident.”
There is, however, one important case where this happens systematically, namely in the case when
\ell_{p}
is even and
-p^{e_{p}}\in D
.

In all what follows, if
\gamma\in\mathbb{Z}_{(p)}
, we let
\gamma^{(0)},\gamma^{(1)},\ldots
denote the “digits” of the
p
-adic integer
(-\gamma)
,
i.e.
,
\gamma^{(k)}\in\{0,\ldots,p{-}1\}
for all
k
and the following expansion

-\gamma=\gamma^{(0)}+p\gamma^{(1)}+p^{2}\gamma^{(2)}+\cdots

holds in the ring of
p
-adic integers.
When
\boldsymbol{\gamma}=(\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2})
, we also let
B(\boldsymbol{\gamma};x)\in\mathbb{F}_{p}[x]
be the truncation at
x^{p}
of
{}_{2}F_{1}\left(\boldsymbol{\gamma},(1);x\right)\bmod p
.
By definition, we thus have
B_{k}(x)=B(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{k};x)
.

Lemma 3.3.2
.

Let
\boldsymbol{\gamma}=(\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2})\in(\mathbb{Z}_{(p)}\cap(0,1))^{2}
. Then

\displaystyle\deg B(\boldsymbol{\gamma};x)

\displaystyle=\min(\gamma_{1}^{(0)},\gamma_{2}^{(0)}),

\displaystyle\deg B(1-\boldsymbol{\gamma};x)

\displaystyle=p-1-\max(\gamma_{1}^{(0)},\gamma_{2}^{(0)}).

Moreover, we have the relation

B(\boldsymbol{\gamma};x)=(1-x)^{\gamma_{1}^{(0)}+\gamma_{2}^{(0)}-(p-1)}\cdot B(\boldsymbol{1}-\boldsymbol{\gamma};x).

(3.3.3)

Proof.

Set
\delta=\min(\gamma_{1}^{(0)},\gamma_{2}^{(0)})
.
By definition, the coefficient of
x^{r}
for
r<p
in
B(\boldsymbol{\gamma};x)
is
\frac{(\gamma_{1})_{r}(\gamma_{2})_{r}}{(r!)^{2}}
. Hence it vanishes modulo
p
if and only if the numerator does. When
r>\delta
, the numerator contains the factor
(\gamma_{1}+\delta)\cdot(\gamma_{2}+\delta)
, and so it vanishes modulo
p
. On the contrary, when
r=\delta
, we have

(\gamma_{1})_{r}(\gamma_{2})_{r}=\gamma_{1}(\gamma_{1}+1)\cdots(\gamma_{1}+\delta-1)\cdot\gamma_{2}(\gamma_{2}+1)\cdots(\gamma_{2}+\delta-1)\cdot,

and, again by definition of
\delta
, none of the above factors vanish. We conclude that
B(\boldsymbol{\gamma};x)
has degree
\delta
, as claimed. The second statement on the degree of
B(1-\boldsymbol{\gamma};x)
is proved similarly.

Thanks to Euler’s transformation, we know that

{}_{2}F_{1}\left(\boldsymbol{\gamma},(1);x\right)=(1-x)^{1-\gamma_{1}-\gamma_{2}}\cdot{}_{2}F_{1}\left(1-\boldsymbol{\gamma},(1);x\right).

We now note that the exponent
1-\gamma_{1}-\gamma_{2}
is congruent to
\gamma_{1}^{(0)}+\gamma_{2}^{(0)}-(p-1)
modulo
p
. Therefore,

B(\boldsymbol{\gamma};x)\equiv(1-x)^{\gamma_{1}^{(0)}+\gamma_{2}^{(0)}-(p-1)}\cdot B(1-\boldsymbol{\gamma};x)\pmod{x^{p}}.

If
\gamma_{1}^{(0)}+\gamma_{2}^{(0)}\geq p-1
, both sides are polynomials of degree at most
p
, and we can conclude that they are indeed equal. On the contrary, when
\gamma_{1}^{(0)}-\gamma_{2}^{(0)}\leq p-1
, we rewrite the above congruence as

B(1-\boldsymbol{\gamma};x)\equiv(1-x)^{(p-1)-\gamma_{1}^{(0)}-\gamma_{2}^{(0)}}\cdot B(\boldsymbol{\gamma};x)\pmod{x^{p}}

and we conclude similarly.
∎

We introduce one more numerical invariant attached to the situation; it is the integer
m
defined by

m\coloneqq\frac{d}{\gcd(d,\,a_{1}{+}a_{2})}

where we have written
\alpha_{i}=a_{i}/d
for
i=1,2
.
We note that
m
is also the denominator of the irreducible fraction representing
\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}
.

Proposition 3.3.3
.

We assume that
\ell_{p}
is even and that
-p^{e_{p}}\in D
. Then

A(x)=(1-x)^{(1+p^{e_{p}})(1-\alpha_{1}-\alpha_{2})}\left(B_{e_{p}}B_{e_{p}+1}^{p}\cdots B_{\ell_{p}-1}^{p^{e_{p}-1}}\right)^{1+p^{e_{p}}}.

It is a perfect power with exponent
\displaystyle\frac{1+p^{e_{p}}}{m}
.

Proof.

We recall from Lemma
3.2.1
.(2) that the Dwork operator
\mathfrak{D}_{p}
acts by division by
p
on
\mathbb{Z}_{(p)}/\mathbb{Z}
.
The assumption of the proposition then tells us that
\mathfrak{D}_{p}^{e_{p}}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})\equiv-\boldsymbol{\alpha}\pmod{\mathbb{Z}}
, which implies more generally
\mathfrak{D}_{p}^{e_{p}+k}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})\equiv-\mathfrak{D}_{p}^{k}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})\pmod{\mathbb{Z}}
for all
k
.
Since our parameters are all in
(0,1)
, we deduce that
\mathfrak{D}_{p}^{e_{p}+k}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})=1-\mathfrak{D}_{p}^{k}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})
.
On the other hand, we recall from Lemma
3.2.1
that
\mathfrak{D}_{p}^{k}(\gamma)^{(0)}=\gamma^{(k)}
for all
k
.
Hence, Lemma
3.3.2
gives

B_{k}(x)=(1-x)^{\alpha_{1}^{(k)}+\alpha_{2}^{(k)}-(p-1)}B_{k+e_{p}}(x).

Coming back to the definition of
A(x)
, we find

\displaystyle A(x)=\prod_{k=0}^{\ell_{p}-1}B_{k}(x)^{p^{k}}

\displaystyle=\prod_{k=0}^{e_{p}-1}(1-x)^{p^{k}(\alpha_{1}^{(k)}+\alpha_{2}^{(k)}-(p-1))}B_{k+e_{p}}(x)^{p^{k}+p^{k+e_{p}}}

\displaystyle=(1-x)^{v}\cdot\left(\prod_{k=0}^{e_{p}-1}B_{k+e_{p}}(x)^{p^{k}}\right)^{1+p^{e_{p}}},

where the exponent
v
is given by
v=\sum_{k=0}^{e_{p}-1}p^{k}u_{k}
with

u_{k}=\alpha_{1}^{(k)}+\alpha_{2}^{(k)}-(p-1)
.
Now, we remark that, for
\gamma\in\mathbb{Z}_{(p)}
, we have

-(1-\gamma)=-1-\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}p^{m}\gamma^{(m)}=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}p^{m}(p-1-\gamma^{(m)}),

implying that
(1-\gamma)^{(m)}=p-1-\gamma^{(m)}
for all
m\in\mathbb{N}
.
Applying this result with
\gamma=\mathfrak{D}_{p}^{k}(\alpha_{i})
(
i\in\{1,2\}
) and remembering that
\mathfrak{D}_{p}^{e_{p}+k}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})=1-\mathfrak{D}_{p}^{k}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})
, we conclude that

u_{e_{p}+k}=-u_{k}
. Weighting by
p^{k}
and summing over
k
(and doing the computation in the ring of
p
-adic integers), we find

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}p^{k}u_{k}=\left(\sum_{k^{\prime}=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{k^{\prime}}p^{e_{p}k^{\prime}}\right)\cdot\left(\sum_{k=0}^{e_{p}-1}p^{k}u_{k}\right)=\frac{1}{1+p^{e_{p}}}\sum_{k=0}^{e_{p}-1}p^{k}u_{k}.

On the other hand, coming back to the definition, we have

\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}p^{k}u_{k}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}p^{k}\big(\alpha_{1}^{(k)}+\alpha_{2}^{(k)}-(p-1)\big)=1-\alpha_{1}-\alpha_{2}.

Hence, we conclude that
v=(1+p^{e_{p}})\cdot(1-\alpha_{1}-\alpha_{2})
and the first part of the proposition is proved.

We write
\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}=a/m
with
a
coprime with
m
. We thus have

v=\frac{(1+p^{e_{p}})(m-a)}{m}.

Since
m-a
is coprime with
m
, we deduce that
m
divides
1+p^{e_{p}}
and then that
v
is a multiple of
r\coloneqq\frac{1+p^{e_{p}}}{m}
.
Given that
(1+p^{e_{p}})
is also obviously a multiple of
r
, we conclude that
A(x)
is a
r
-th power.
∎

As a conclusion, we have proved the following.

Corollary 3.3.4
.

Let
\mathfrak{p}
be a prime of
K
above
p
.
The Galois group
\Gal({}_{2}F_{1}\left(\boldsymbol{\alpha},(1);x\right)\mid k_{\mathfrak{p}}(x))
embeds in
k_{\mathfrak{p}}^{\times}
and hence is a cyclic group.
Moreover, if
\ell_{p}
is even and
-p^{e_{p}}\in D
, its cardinality divides
(p^{e_{p}}-1)\cdot m

3.3.2
Uniformizing the Galois groups

We now aim at finding a common origin of the Galois groups given by Corollary
3.3.4
in the terms of Conjecture
2.2.6
.
For this, we shall consider several subgroups of
\GL_{1}(K)=K^{\times}
.
The first one is
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{m}(K)
, the group of
m
-th roots of unity in
K
. The following lemma ensures that
K
contains all
m
-th roots of unity, meaning that
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{m}(K)
is a cyclic group of cardinality
m
.

Lemma 3.3.5
.

We have
\zeta_{m}\in K
.

Proof.

It follows immediately from the definition that
m
divides
d
.
Hence
\zeta_{m}\in\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{d})
and it is enough to prove that
\zeta_{m}=\zeta_{m}^{\lambda}
for any
\lambda\in D
.
Let
\lambda\in D
. By definition, the multiplication by
\lambda
induces a permutation of
\{\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}\}
modulo
\mathbb{Z}
.
Thus

\lambda\cdot(a_{1}+a_{2})\equiv a_{1}+a_{2}\pmod{d}

and we conclude that
\lambda\equiv 1\pmod{m}
. The lemma follows.
∎

The second subgroup of
\GL_{1}(K)
that will play a major role in what follows is the group
\GL_{1}(K^{+})
where
K^{+}\coloneqq K\cap\mathbb{R}
.
Since the complex conjugacy is represented by the element
-1\in(\mathbb{Z}/d\mathbb{Z})^{\times}
, we deduce that
K^{+}
is the subfield of
\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{d})
cutted out by the subgroup

D^{+}\coloneqq D\cdot\{\pm 1\}\subseteq(\mathbb{Z}/d\mathbb{Z})^{\times}.

Definition 3.3.6
.

We define the subgroup
G
of
\GL_{1}(K)
by

G\coloneqq\begin{cases}\GL_{1}(K)&\text{if $K=K^{+}$}\\
\GL_{1}(K^{+})\cdot\langle\xi-\bar{\xi}\rangle&\text{if $K\neq K^{+}$ and $m=2$}\\
\GL_{1}(K^{+})\cdot\langle 1+\zeta_{m}\rangle&\text{if $K\neq K^{+}$ and $m\neq 2$}\end{cases}

where
\xi
is an element of
K\setminus K^{+}
and
\bar{\xi}
is its complex conjugate.

Remark 3.3.7
.

The reason why the definition is slightly different when
m=2
comes simply from the fact that
1+\zeta_{m}
vanishes in this case.
We notice moreover that, as soon as
\xi\in K\setminus K^{+}
, the element
\xi-\bar{\xi}
is a nonzero real multiple of
\mathbf{i}\in\mathbb{C}
.
The group
\GL_{1}(K^{+})\cdot\langle\xi-\bar{\xi}\rangle
is then the subgroup

(K^{\times}\cap\mathbb{R})\sqcup(K^{\times}\cap\mathbb{R}\mathbf{i})\subseteq K^{\times}.

In particular, it does not depend on the choice of
\xi
, and so Definition
3.3.6
is not ambiguous.

We are going to prove that the group
G
fulfills the requirements of Conjecture
2.2.6
.
We start by a lemma that reformulates the condition of Corollary
3.3.4
in more abstract terms.

Lemma 3.3.8
.

Let
\mathfrak{p}
be a prime in
K
above
p
and let
k_{\mathfrak{p}}
(resp.
k^{+}_{\mathfrak{p}}
) denote the residue field of
K
(resp.
K^{+}
) at
\mathfrak{p}
.
The extension
k_{\mathfrak{p}}/k^{+}_{\mathfrak{p}}
is nontrivial if and only if
\ell_{p}
is even and
-p^{e_{p}}\in D
.
Moreover, when this occurs, it has degree
2
.

Proof.

Let
P
denote the subgroup of
(\mathbb{Z}/d\mathbb{Z})^{\times}
generated by
p
.
We know from the proof of Proposition
3.2.11
that
\Gal(k_{\mathfrak{p}}/\mathbb{F}_{p})=P/(D\cap P)
,
and similarly one proves that
\Gal(k^{+}_{\mathfrak{p}}/\mathbb{F}_{p})=P/(D^{+}\cap P)
.
Hence the extension
k_{\mathfrak{p}}/k^{+}_{\mathfrak{p}}
is trivial if and only if

D\cap P=D^{+}\cap P.

(3.3.4)

If
-1\in D
, we have
D=D^{+}
and the condition (
3.3.4
) is obviously satisfied.
On the other hand, still assuming that
-1\in D
, the condition
-p^{e_{p}}\in D
is equivalent to
p^{e_{p}}\in D
, which cannot be true by minimality of
\ell_{p}
.
The equivalence stated in the lemma is then proved in this case.

We now assume that
-1\not\in D
. Then
D^{+}
is the disjoint union of
D
and
(-D)
, from what we deduce that the condition (
3.3.4
) is equivalent to the fact that
P
does not meet
(-D)
.
Clearly, if
-p^{e_{p}}\in D
, then
P
meets
(-D)
. Conversely, let us assume that
P\cap(-D)=\emptyset
,
i.e.
, that there exists an exponent
e\in\mathbb{N}
such that
-p^{e}\in D
.
Then
p^{2e}\in D
and it follows that
2e
needs to be a multiple of
\ell_{p}
: there exists an integer
n
such that
2e=n\ell_{p}
.
Necessarily
n
has to be odd because otherwise, we would deduce that
\ell_{p}
divides
e
and, consequently, that
p^{e}\in D
; this cannot be true given that
-p^{e}\in D
and
-1\not\in D
by assumption.
It follows that
\ell_{p}
is even. Now, writing
n=2n^{\prime}+1
, we get
p^{e}=p^{n^{\prime}\ell_{p}+e_{p}}\equiv p^{e_{p}}\pmod{d}
and so
-p^{e_{p}}\in D
.

Finally, the fact that
k_{\mathfrak{p}}/k^{+}_{\mathfrak{p}}
has degree at most
2
follows from the fact that
D\cap P
has index at most
2
in
D^{+}\cap P
.
∎

Following Conjecture
2.2.6
, for any place
\mathfrak{p}
of
K
, we set

G_{\mathfrak{p}}\coloneqq\text{image}\big(G\cap\GL_{1}(\mathcal{O}_{(\mathfrak{p})})\longrightarrow\GL_{1}(k_{\mathfrak{p}})\big)

(3.3.5)

where
\mathcal{O}_{(\mathfrak{p})}
denotes the localization of the ring of integers of
K
at
\mathfrak{p}
and
k_{\mathfrak{p}}=\mathcal{O}_{(\mathfrak{p})}/\mathfrak{p}\mathcal{O}_{(\mathfrak{p})}
is the residue field.

Proposition 3.3.9
.

Let
\mathfrak{p}
be a prime ideal above
p
.
Then
G_{\mathfrak{p}}
is equal to
\GL_{1}(k_{\mathfrak{p}})
if
\ell_{p}
is odd or
-p^{e_{p}}\not\in D
; otherwise, it is its cyclic subgroup of order
(p^{e_{p}}-1)\cdot m
.

Proof.

We only write the proof when
m\neq 2
, the case
m=2
being treated in a similar fashion.

To start with, we observe that
G
contains
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{m}(K)
, which follows from writing

\zeta_{m}=(\zeta_{m}+1)^{2}\cdot(\zeta_{m}+\zeta_{m}^{-1}+2)^{-1}

and noticing that the second factor lies in
\GL_{1}(K^{+})
since it is invariant under the transformation
\zeta_{m}\mapsto\zeta_{m}^{-1}
.
Therefore, we equally have
G=\GL_{1}(K^{+})\cdot H
with
H\coloneqq\boldsymbol{\mu}_{m}(K)\cdot\langle 1+\zeta_{m}\rangle
.
We note that the former equality also holds when
K=K^{+}
since
G=\GL_{1}(K)
in this case.

We now compute the intersection
G\cap\GL_{1}(\mathcal{O}_{(\mathfrak{p})})
.
Given that
p
is coprime with
d
, and hence with
m
, Hensel’s lemma ensures that the morphism
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{m}(K)\to\boldsymbol{\mu}_{m}(k_{\mathfrak{p}})
is an isomorphism.
Thus
\zeta_{m}
, which is a generator of
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{m}(K)
, reduces modulo
\mathfrak{p}
to a generator of
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{m}(k_{\mathfrak{p}})
.
Therefore
1+\zeta_{m}
does not vanish in
k_{\mathfrak{p}}
.
This shows that the group
H
sits inside
\GL_{1}(\mathcal{O}_{(\mathfrak{p})})
and, consequently, that

G\cap\GL_{1}(\mathcal{O}_{(\mathfrak{p})})=\GL_{1}(\mathcal{O}_{(\mathfrak{p})}^{+})\cdot H

where
\mathcal{O}_{(\mathfrak{p})}^{+}
is the localization at
\mathfrak{p}
of the ring of integers of
K^{+}
.
Reducing modulo
\mathfrak{p}
, we finally find

G_{\mathfrak{p}}=\GL_{1}(k_{\mathfrak{p}}^{+})\cdot H_{\mathfrak{p}}

where
H_{\mathfrak{p}}
is the reduction of
H
modulo
\mathfrak{p}
.

When
\ell_{p}
is odd or
-p^{e_{p}}\not\in D
, Lemma
3.3.8
shows that
k_{\mathfrak{p}}=k_{\mathfrak{p}}^{+}
and so
G_{\mathfrak{p}}=\GL_{1}(k_{\mathfrak{p}})
as claimed.

We now consider the case where
\ell_{p}
is even and
-p^{e_{p}}\in D
.
Applying Lemma
3.3.8
again, we find that the cardinality of
\GL_{1}(k_{\mathfrak{p}}^{+})
is
p^{e_{p}}-1
and so the cardinality of the quotient
\GL_{1}(k_{\mathfrak{p}})/\GL_{1}(k_{\mathfrak{p}}^{+})
is
p^{e_{p}}+1
.
Moreover, both of these groups are cyclic.
We then reduced to prove that the image of
H_{\mathfrak{p}}
in
\GL_{1}(k_{\mathfrak{p}})/\GL_{1}(k_{\mathfrak{p}}^{+})
has cardinality
m
. For this, we observe that the kernel of
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{m}(k_{\mathfrak{p}})\to\GL_{1}(k_{\mathfrak{p}})/\GL_{1}(k_{\mathfrak{p}}^{+})
is
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{m}(k_{\mathfrak{p}}^{+})
,
which is a cyclic group of order
\gcd(m,p^{e_{p}}-1)=\gcd(m,2)
because
m
divides
p^{e_{p}}+1
.

By what precedes, it is enough to show that the order of
1+\zeta_{m}
in the quotient group
\GL_{1}(k_{\mathfrak{p}})/\GL_{1}(k_{\mathfrak{p}}^{+})\boldsymbol{\mu}_{m}(k_{\mathfrak{p}})
is
1
when
m
is odd and
2
when
m
is even.
For this, we first notice that

(\zeta_{m}+1)^{2}=\zeta_{m}\cdot(\zeta_{m}+\zeta_{m}^{-1}+2)\in\GL_{1}(k_{\mathfrak{p}}^{+})\boldsymbol{\mu}_{m}(k_{\mathfrak{p}})

since the first factor is in
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{m}(k_{\mathfrak{p}})
and the second factor lies is
k_{\mathfrak{p}}^{+}
.
Hence the order of
1+\zeta_{m}
is
1
or
2
.
Now, we observe that
1+\zeta_{m}
lies in
\GL_{1}(k_{\mathfrak{p}}^{+})\boldsymbol{\mu}_{m}(k_{\mathfrak{p}})
if and only if there exists an integer
a
such that
\zeta_{m}^{a}(1+\zeta_{m})
is invariant by the transformation
\zeta_{m}\mapsto\zeta_{m}^{-1}
.
After simplification, this further reduces to
\zeta_{m}^{2a+1}=1
.
When
m
is odd, such an element
a
obviously exists (we can take
a=\frac{m-1}{2}
).
On the contrary, when
m
is even, it does not exist because
\zeta_{m}
has order
m
in
\GL_{1}(k_{\mathfrak{p}}^{+})
and
m
cannot divide the odd number
2a+1
.
∎

Combining Proposition
3.3.9
with Corollary
3.3.4
, we get the first part of Theorem
1.2
of the introduction.

3.3.3
Approaching the Galois group from below

Up until now, we have only bounded the Galois groups of reductions of hypergeometric series from above.
We now develop also bounds from below, that will eventually give a complete proof of Theorem
1.2
.

We recall from Proposition
3.3.1
that the order of the Galois group of
\Gal({}_{2}F_{1}\left(\boldsymbol{\alpha},1;x\right)\mid k_{\mathfrak{p}}(x))
with
\boldsymbol{\alpha}=(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2})
is given by

\frac{q{-}1}{\gcd(r,q{-}1)},

where
q=p^{\ell_{p}}
and
r
is the largest integer such that the polynomials
A(x)
introduced in Equation (
3.3.2
), is a perfect
r
-th power.
To estimate
r
, we rely on the fact that
r
must divide the valuation of
A(x)
at any point, and also its degree (
i.e.
, its valuation at infinity).

Valuation at singular points.

The degree, which we denote by
\nu_{\infty}
, has been already essentially computed in Subsection
3.3.1
.
Precisely, we recall from there that
B_{k}(x)
is defined as the truncation at
x^{p}
of
{}_{2}F_{1}\left(\mathfrak{D}_{p}^{k}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}),1;x\right)\bmod p
.
Then, Lemma
3.3.2
shows that
B_{k}(x)
has degree
\min(\alpha_{1}^{(k)},\alpha_{2}^{(k)})
, where
\alpha_{i}^{(k)}
denotes the
k
-th digit in the
p
-adic expansion of
-\alpha_{i}
for
i\in\{1,2\}
.
Therefore

\nu_{\infty}=\sum_{k=0}^{\ell_{p}-1}\min(\alpha_{1}^{(k)},\alpha_{2}^{(k)})\cdot p^{k}.

(3.3.6)

The valuation at
1
of
A(x)
can be computed similarly.
As in Subsection
3.3.1
, we write
B(\boldsymbol{\gamma};x)
for the truncation of
{}_{2}F_{1}\left(\boldsymbol{\gamma},1;x\right)\bmod p
at
x^{p}
.

Lemma 3.3.10
.

Let
\boldsymbol{\gamma}=(\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2})\in(\mathbb{Z}_{(p)}\cap(0,1))^{2}
. Then

\val_{1}\big(B(\boldsymbol{\gamma};x)\big)=\max\big(0,\,\gamma_{1}^{(0)}+\gamma_{2}^{(0)}-p+1\big)

where
\val_{1}
denotes the valuation at
1
.

Proof.

We know from Lemma
3.3.2
that

B(\boldsymbol{\gamma};x)=(1-x)^{\gamma_{1}^{(0)}+\gamma_{2}^{(0)}-p+1}\cdot B(1-\boldsymbol{\gamma};x).

Without loss of generality, we assume that
\gamma_{1}^{(0)}+\gamma_{2}^{(0)}\geq p-1
; otherwise we exchange the roles of
\boldsymbol{\gamma}
and
1-\boldsymbol{\gamma}
.
The local exponents at
1
of
\mathcal{H}(\boldsymbol{\gamma},(1))\bmod p
are
0
and
\gamma_{1}^{(0)}+\gamma_{2}^{(0)}-p-1
, while that of
\mathcal{H}(1-\boldsymbol{\gamma},1)\bmod p
are
0
and
-1-(\gamma_{1}^{(0)}+\gamma_{2}^{(0)})
; besides
B(\boldsymbol{\gamma};x)
are
B(1-\boldsymbol{\gamma};x)
are solutions of the associated differential equations respectively.
The only option to conceal these conditions is to have

\displaystyle\val_{1}\big(B(1-\boldsymbol{\gamma};x)\big)

\displaystyle\equiv-1-\big(\gamma_{1}^{(0)}+\gamma_{2}^{(0)}\big)\pmod{p}

\displaystyle\val_{1}\big(B(\boldsymbol{\gamma};x)\big)

\displaystyle\equiv\gamma_{1}^{(0)}+\gamma_{2}^{(0)}-p+1+\val_{1}\big(B(1-\boldsymbol{\gamma};x)\big)\equiv 0\pmod{p}.

Since, moreover,
\val_{1}(B(\boldsymbol{\gamma};x))\leq\deg B(\boldsymbol{\gamma};x)<p
, we conclude that
\val_{1}(B(\boldsymbol{\gamma};x))=0
.
∎

Applying the previous lemma with
\boldsymbol{\gamma}=\mathfrak{D}_{p}^{k}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})
for
k
varying between
0
and
\ell_{p}{-}1
, we find

\nu_{1}\coloneqq\val_{1}\big(A(x)\big)=\sum_{k=0}^{\ell_{p}-1}\max\big(0,\,\alpha_{1}^{(k)}+\alpha_{2}^{(k)}-p+1\big)\cdot p^{k}.

(3.3.7)

Apart from the singular points
1
and
\infty
, the last singular point of the hypergeometric differential equation is
0
. However, the valuation at
0
of
A(x)
is clearly
0
, by definition so this does not provide any further information in our quest to determine
r
.

Valuation at ordinary points.

Equation (
3.3.1
) ensures that we can apply Lemma
2.1.4
to
B(\boldsymbol{\gamma};x)
and conclude that each
\xi\in\bar{\mathbb{F}}_{p}
different from
1
is at most a simple root of each of the polynomials
B_{k}(x)
.
Then, each root of
A(x)
different from
1
has multiplicity

\varepsilon_{0}+\varepsilon_{1}\cdot p+\cdots+\varepsilon_{\ell_{p}-1}p^{\ell_{p}-1}

(3.3.8)

for some
\varepsilon_{i}\in\{0,1\}
.

Assuming now, that
A(x)
is a perfect
r
-th power, we know that for each of its roots, the expression (
3.3.8
) must be divisible by
r
.
We use this information is a very special case. For each
k
,
we let
b_{k}
be the number of distinct roots of
B_{k}(x)
different from
1
, and we order them by size:

b_{j_{1}}<\cdots<b_{j_{\ell}}.

We underline that the values of the
b_{k}
can obviously be deduced from the degrees of
B_{k}(x)
and the multiplicity of
1
as a root of each of them, two quantities we have alreadly computed previously.
We take
c
maximal such that
b_{j_{1}}+\cdots+b_{j_{c}}<b_{j_{\ell}}
.
Then there exists a root
\xi_{0}
of
B_{j_{\ell}}(x)
, that is not a root of any of the polynomials
B_{j_{1}}(x),\ldots,B_{j_{c}}(x)
.
Set
J\coloneqq\{j_{k+1},\ldots,j_{\ell-1}\}
, so that the multiplicity of
\xi_{0}
as a root of
A(x)
is given by

\nu_{J^{\prime}}\coloneqq p^{j_{\ell}}+\sum_{j\in J^{\prime}}p^{j}

for some subset
J^{\prime}\subseteq J
.

Putting all the constraints together, we conclude that
r
has to be a divisor of

g_{J^{\prime}}\coloneqq\gcd\big(\nu_{\infty},\,\nu_{1},\,\nu_{J^{\prime}},\,p^{\ell_{p}}-1\big)

for some
J^{\prime}\subseteq J
.
Consequently, the index of
\Gal({}_{2}F_{1}\left(\boldsymbol{\alpha},(1);x\right)\mid k_{\mathfrak{p}}(x))
in the conjectured group
G_{\mathfrak{p}}
of Proposition
3.3.9
is bounded from above by

g_{J^{\prime}}/h
where

h\coloneqq\begin{cases}p^{e_{p}}+1&\text{if $\ell_{p}$ is even and $p^{-e_{p}}\in D$}\\
1&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

Of course, the set
J^{\prime}
is
a priori
not known, but we can even bound the index by taking the maximum (or the least common multiple) of all the previous quantities when
J^{\prime}
varies.

Uniformity in
p
.

To make statements about all primes simultaneously, we need to study the behavior of
g
with respect to
p
.
The key observation is that
\alpha_{1}^{(k)}
and
\alpha_{2}^{(k)}
depend only on
s\coloneqq\lfloor p/d\rfloor
and
t\coloneqq p\bmod d
, as shown by the following lemma.

Lemma 3.3.11
.

Let
\gamma\in\mathbb{Z}_{(p)}\cap(0,1)
with denominator
d
, and let
t\in\{1,\ldots,d{-}1\}
with
\gcd(t,d)=1
.
Then there exists a polynomial
\Gamma_{t}(x)\in\mathbb{Z}[x]
of degree at most
1
such that, for all positive integer
s
such that
p=ds+t
is a prime number,

\Gamma_{t}(s)
is the unique representative of
(-\gamma)\bmod p
in the interval
[0,p)
.

Proof.

We write
p=ds+t
.
We consider a Bézout relation
ut+vd=1
with
u,v\in\mathbb{Z}
and notice that
us-v\equiv-\frac{1}{d}\pmod{p}
for all
s
since

-d(us-v)\equiv ut+vd=1\pmod{p}.

If
\gamma=\frac{a}{d}
, we now define

\Gamma_{t}(x)\coloneqq aux-av-\left\lfloor\frac{au}{d}\right\rfloor(dx+t).

Clearly,
\Gamma_{t}(x)
is a polynomial with integral coefficients of degree at most
1
,
and
\Gamma_{t}(s)\equiv-\gamma\pmod{p}
for all
s
.
Besides the constant coefficient of
\Gamma_{t}(x)
is in the range
[0,d)
, while its linear coefficient is in
[0,t)
given that

-1<-\gamma=-av-\frac{au}{d}t\leq-av-\left\lfloor\frac{au}{d}\right\rfloor t<-av-\left(\frac{au}{d}-1\right)t=t-\gamma.

Therefore
0\leq\Gamma_{t}(s)<ds+t=p
for all
s
.
∎

Applying Lemma
3.3.11
with
\gamma=\mathfrak{D}_{p}^{k}(\alpha_{i})
(with
i\in\{1,2\})
, we find that each
\alpha_{i}^{(k)}
is a polynomial of degree at most
1
in
s
(for each fixed congruence class
t
modulo
d
).
Given that
\ell_{p}
also only depends on the congruence of
p
modulo
d
, we find that all the quantities
\nu_{\infty}
,
\nu_{1}
,
p^{\ell_{p}}{-}1
and all
\nu_{J^{\prime}}
are polynomials in
s
as well.
If the greatest common divisor of these polynomials computed in
\mathbb{Q}[s]
is equal to
h
for all
J^{\prime}
, we derive that the index of
\Gal({}_{2}F_{1}\left(\boldsymbol{\alpha},(1);x\right)\mid k_{\mathfrak{p}}(x))
in
G_{\mathfrak{p}}
is bounded independently of
p
, and we can even explicitly compute a bound by writing down Bézout relations between these polynomials.
In some cases, this bound is equal to
1
, so that we can conclude that
\Gal({}_{2}F_{1}\left(\boldsymbol{\alpha},(1);x\right)\mid k_{\mathfrak{p}}(x))=G_{\mathfrak{p}}
for all prime
p
which is congruent to
t
modulo
d
.

Example 3.3.12
.

For any parameter
\boldsymbol{\alpha}

p

\ell_{p}=1

2.1.4
