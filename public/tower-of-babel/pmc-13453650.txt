Konno aortic valve replacement in adults: Enabling larger prosthetic valve implantation and adequate aortic root enlargement for coronary clearance
Authors: Daisuke Yoshioka, Ai Kawamura, Kazuo Shimamura, Takuji Kawamura, Shin Yajima, Yusuke Misumi, Shunsuke Saito, Shigeru Miyagawa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveSurgical aortic valve replacement in patients with severe aortic stenosis and small annuli remains challenging owing to prosthesis-patient mismatch and the limited feasibility of subsequent valve-in-valve transcatheter aortic valve replacement (ViV-TAVR). The Konno procedure enables annular and left ventricular outflow tract (LVOT) enlargement; however, outcomes in adults remain insufficiently defined.MethodsBetween January 2023 and October 2025, 193 patients underwent surgical aortic valve replacement at our institution, of whom 19 (10%) received the Konno procedure for annulus diameter ≤19 mm, high likelihood of prosthesis-patient mismatch, or anticipated ViV-TAVR with estimated high risk of coronary obstruction. Operative results, echocardiographic and electrocardiographic parameters, and computed tomography measurements of the aortic root were assessed before and after surgery.ResultsPatients were aged 59 ± 19 years and 84% were women. Bioprostheses were implanted in 12 patients (63%) and mechanical valves in seven (37%), with enlargement permitting prosthesis implantation 2 to 3 sizes larger. Septal myectomy was performed in 6 patients. No in-hospital mortality occurred. Computed tomography demonstrated enlargement of the sinus of Valsalva (+7.0 mm) and sinotubular junction (+6.9 mm), consistent with ViV-TAVR feasibility. Echocardiography confirmed relief of LVOT obstruction; however, right ventricular diameter tended to increase, tricuspid annular plane systolic excursion declined, and QRS duration increased from 90 to 114 milliseconds, indicating intraventricular conduction delay.ConclusionsKonno aortic valve replacement facilitates implantation of larger prostheses, relieves LVOT obstruction, and optimizes root anatomy for future ViV-TAVR. Conduction disturbances and right ventricular functional decline warrant long-term surveillance.Key Words: Konno-AVR, aortic annular enlargement, TAV-in-SAV

Konno procedure enlarges the aortic root and enables larger valve implantation.

Central Message

Konno-AVR allows 2 to 3 larger valve sizes, preventing PPM. CT showed +7 mm sinus enlargement with adequate VTC enabling ViV-TAVR in all cases. QRS prolongation and RV decline warrant surveillance.

Perspective

Konno-AVR allowed valve upsizing by 2 to 4 sizes, preventing prosthesis-patient mismatch. CT confirmed +7 mm sinus of Valsalva enlargement and adequate valve-to-coronary distances, ensuring ViV-TAVR feasibility in all bioprosthetic cases and supporting lifetime management of small-annulus aortic stenosis. Postoperative QRS prolongation and right ventricular decline warrant close surveillance.

Securing an adequately indexed effective orifice area and preventing prosthesis-patient mismatch (PPM) are critical for long-term outcomes following surgical aortic valve replacement (SAVR) for severe aortic stenosis (AS). PPM occurs in up to 25% of SAVR cases and is associated with postoperative heart failure and reduced survival.1 To reduce this risk, aortic root enlargement should be considered—particularly in patients with a small annulus. Among the available techniques, Y-incision aortic annular enlargement has demonstrated superior hemodynamic performance and midterm survival compared with conventional Nicks or Manougian procedures and can be applied even in patients with annular sizes of 17 to 25 mm.2, 3, 4, 5 Unlike the Nicks, Manouguian, and Y-incision procedures, which enlarge the aortic annulus posteriorly, the Konno procedure achieves anterior annular enlargement through incision of the interventricular septum and right ventricular (RV) free wall.6 Although traditionally performed in pediatric patients, its application has been increasingly extended to adults with small aortic annuli in recent years. Furthermore, the Konno procedure is particularly valuable in situations where posterior enlargement techniques are not feasible, such as in patients with a heavily calcified aortomitral curtain, those requiring concomitant mitral valve replacement, or those with left ventricular outflow tract obstruction (LVOTO) extending into the subvalvular region.

Meanwhile, the expansion of transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR) in younger and lower-risk populations has shifted treatment selection toward individualized, lifetime-oriented planning. For bioprosthetic valve failure after SAVR, valve-in-valve TAVR (ViV-TAVR) offers an effective reintervention strategy; however, its success is strongly influenced by the initial surgical valve size, type, and implantation height, underscoring the importance of adequate annular enlargement during index surgery.2,3,5,7,8 Conversely, post-SAVR changes, including restricted annular dimensions, calcification, and prosthetic structures, can complicate subsequent TAVR, particularly in patients with a small annulus who remain prone to PPM and high residual gradients.9 Thus, annular enlargement procedures, including Konno-AVR, are pivotal not only for preventing PPM but also for ensuring the feasibility of future transcatheter interventions. This study aimed to evaluate the outcomes and anatomical impact of annular enlargement with Konno-AVR, focusing on its role in the lifetime management of AS.10, 11, 12

Methods

Patients and Indications

The indications for the Konno procedure in patients with AS were annulus diameter ≤19 mm, annular diameter measurement measured by preoperative cardiac computed tomography (CT) indicating a high likelihood of moderate or severe PPM, and patient’s preference for a bioprosthetic valve with anticipated future ViV-TAVR and a small sinus of Valsalva posing a high risk of coronary artery obstruction.

Between January 2023 and October 2025, among 193 patients who underwent SAVR for severe AS or structural valve dysfunction of the bioprosthetic/mechanical valve at our institution, 19 (10%) underwent the Konno procedure. During this period, other aortic root enlargement techniques were not performed. We evaluated the operative outcomes in these 19 patients, anatomical changes in the aortic root and annulus, and physiological changes before and after surgery. Written informed consent for publication was achieved from all patients, and this study was approved by the institutional board (No. 08218-12).

Operative Method

The Konno procedure was performed according to previously described principles.13 An aortic incision was made toward the commissure between the right and left coronary cusps, taking care not to damage the coronary artery orifices (Video 1). The calcified aortic valve leaflets or prosthetic valve were excised and the annular diameter and LVOT were measured using a cylindrical sizer. Next, an incision was made in the RV free wall to avoid damaging the pulmonary valve, and the interior of the RV was examined. Then, the ventricular septum between the pulmonary valve and the papillary muscles of the tricuspid valve was incised, starting from the aortic annulus. During this step, attention was paid to preserve the first branch of the left anterior descending artery, which runs within the interventricular septum between the LV and RV, and the incision was extended immediately before this branch. However, if the enlargement was insufficient for an adequate valve, the first septal branch was divided. For patients with preoperative LVOTO due to a thickened septal myocardium, myectomy was performed along with an incision. After the incision, a sizer was used to confirm that the LVOT and annular diameter were adequately enlarged. Approximately 3 to 5 pairs of 4-0 Prolene (Ethicon) mattress sutures with pledgets were placed from the LV side to the RV side. A Hemashield patch, trimmed to a width of approximately 4 to 5 cm, was applied and tied in place. For the second layer, continuous suturing was performed between the RV septum and Hemashield patch, and the suture was extended to both sides up to the base of the aorta for anastomosis. For the prosthetic valve, suturing was performed in the usual manner at the native annulus site, whereas on the patch portion, sutures were placed from the outside together with the second Hemashield patch. After securing the prosthetic valve, the first Hemashield patch was used to reconstruct and close the aortic contour, and the second patch was used to close the RV free wall.

Echocardiographic and CT Data

Echocardiography was performed preoperatively and 1 week postoperatively. All postoperative echocardiographic assessments were performed after extubation in fully awake patients. The RV end-diastolic diameter was measured at end-diastole in the apical 4-chamber view immediately below the tricuspid valve. For accelerated flow within the LV, measurements were performed separately from the aortic valve. Electrocardiography (ECG) was performed before surgery and immediately before discharge. Patients who underwent pacemaker implantation were excluded from the analysis.

Cardiac CT was performed in all patients during the initial postoperative hospitalization to compare anatomical changes with the preoperative state. CT measurements included LVOT, annulus, sinus of Valsalva, and sinotubular junction (STJ) diameters during systole. The sinus of Valsalva was measured in 3 directions, and the height of the coronary orifice was measured as previously described.14 Three-dimensional postoperative CT confirmed enlargement of the sinus of Valsalva (Figure 1, A), and the distance between the neoaortic wall and the tip of the prosthetic valve stent post (valve-to-aorta [VTA]) was also confirmed (Figure 1, B). The valve-to-coronary (VTC) distance was evaluated on a horizontal CT slice depicting both the coronary ostia and prosthetic valve, and the distance from the prosthetic valve to each coronary orifice was measured (Figure 1, C).

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using JMP Pro version 16.0.0 (SAS Institute). Continuous variables are expressed as mean ± SD or median with interquartile range (IQR), as appropriate. Categorical variables are expressed as numbers and percentages. Normality was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Differences between means were analyzed using paired or unpaired t tests. Nonnormal data were analyzed using the Mann-Whitney U test. Categorical variables were compared using the χ2 test.

Results

Patient characteristics are shown in Table 1. The mean age was 59 ± 19 years and 16 patients (84%) were women. The mean body surface area was 1.46 ± 0.37 m2. Bicuspid aortic valve was seen in 6 (32%) of patients. The preoperative Society of Thoracic Surgeons risk score was 2.46% ± 1.64%. Redo surgery was performed in 8 (42%) patients; all had undergone SAVR, and 1 had also undergone coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG). The preoperative echocardiography results are also shown. The mean ejection fraction of the LV was 66% ± 16%, and the peak velocity of the aortic valve was 4.1 ± 1.0 m/second. LVOTO was observed in 6 (32%) patients due to hypertrophic LV myocardium. Among the 6 patients with preoperative LVOTO, the mean peak velocity of accelerated intraventricular flow was 2.56 ± 0.97 m/second, corresponding to a mean pressure gradient of 27.7 ± 19.2 mm Hg. Preoperative CT measurement showed diameter of the aortic annulus was 19.7 ± 2.0 mm, and STJ was 23.5 ± 4.0 mm.

The operative results are summarized in Table 2. The mean real aortic annular diameter measured during cardiac arrest was 18.4 ± 2.6 mm. Mechanical valves were implanted in 7 patients (37%) and bioprosthetic valves in 12 patients (63%). Among the mechanical valve sizes were 19 mm (n = 1), 20 mm (n = 2), 21 mm (n = 3), and 23 mm (n = 1). Bioprosthetic valve sizes were 21 (n = 4), 23 mm (n = 3), and 25 mm (n = 5). Upsizing by 1 size was achieved in 2 patients (11%), by 2 sizes in 10 patients (53%), by 3 sizes in 5 patients (26%), and by 4 sizes in 2 patients (11%), with a median upsizing of 2 valve sizes (IQR, 2-3 sizes). The relationship between the diameter of the intraoperative measurement and the size of the implanted valve in each patient is shown in Figure 2. Concomitant procedures included mitral valve replacement with the maze procedure and tricuspid annuloplasty (TAP) in 1 case, unplanned isolated TAP due to severe tricuspid valve regurgitation (TR) after cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB) weaning in 1 case, CABG in 2 cases, ascending aortic replacement in 1 case, ascending aortic replacement combined with CABG in 1 case, and septal myectomy in 6 cases. The mean operative time was 342 ± 69 minutes, mean CPB time was 186 ± 52 minutes, and mean aortic crossclamp time was 131 ± 40 minutes. In 9 patients without concomitant surgery or redo surgery, the mean operative time was 295 ± 35 minutes, mean CPB time was 162 ± 28 minutes, and mean aortic crossclamp time was 121 ± 28 minutes. The peak postoperative creatine kinase-MB isoenzyme level was 70 ± 42 IU/L. Postoperative complications included re-exploration for bleeding in 1 patient (5%), permanent pacemaker implantation in 1 patient (5%), and 1 (5%) patient who required unplanned TAP experienced transient postoperative right heart failure with temporal continuous venovenous hemodiafiltration. Postoperative echocardiography revealed the peak velocity of 2.13 ± 0.35 m/second, mean pressure gradient of 10.2 ± 3.7 mm Hg, and effective orifice area of 1.89 ± 0.28 cm2 at the implanted prosthetic valve, and trivial perivalvular leakage at the portion of the Hemashield patch in 1 patient. The median intensive care unit stay was 5 days (IQR, 2-7 days), and the median hospital stay was 22 days (IQR, 14-28 days). No in-hospital mortalities were observed.

A comparative evaluation of cardiac function and the aortic root anatomy was conducted using physiological studies and CT imaging before and after surgery (Table 3). Echocardiography revealed that although the LV end-diastolic diameter decreased postoperatively, LVOTO was nearly completely resolved in 5 of the 6 patients postoperatively. One patient demonstrated residual accelerated flow in the midventricular portion (peak velocity 3.0 m/second) at 1 week after surgery; however, at the 1-year follow-up examination, this had improved markedly to 0.8 m/second. The RV end-diastolic diameter showed a mild enlargement. Excluding 2 patients who underwent planned or unplanned TAP, the TAP systolic excursion score decreased from 19.5 to 13.9 mm after surgery. Excluding 1 patient with planned TAP, the TR grade in 18 patients and pulmonary valve regurgitation (PR) grade in 19 patients were analyzed. As mentioned previously, 1 case required unplanned TAP for severe TR after CPB weaning; none of the other patients had more than moderate TR or PR. On ECG, the PR interval representing atrioventricular conduction remained unchanged. However, the QRS duration, which indicates intraventricular conduction, increased from 90 to 114 milliseconds. On CT, the height from the annulus to the coronary ostia decreased postoperatively by 4.6 mm for the right coronary artery (from 14.4 to 9.8 mm) and by 3.7 mm for the left coronary artery (from 13.3 to 9.6 mm) after surgery. In contrast, the diameter of the sinus of Valsalva, including the right and left coronary sinuses, increased by approximately 7 mm, and the diameter, including the noncoronary cusp increased by 4.5 mm. The STJ diameter also increased from 23.5 to 30.4 mm, reflecting an expansion of 6.9 mm. In 12 patients with bioprosthetic valves, VTC was 7.6 ± 2.1 mm to the right coronary artery and 6.5 ± 0.9 mm to the left coronary artery. In simulation by postoperative cardiac CT, future ViV-TAVR was feasible for all 12 patients with bioprosthetic valves.

Discussion

Our results show that Konno-AVR enabled the implantation of a prosthetic valve that was 2 to 3 size larger while simultaneously relieving LVOTO. Postoperative CT demonstrated adequate enlargement of the sinus of Valsalva, suggesting high feasibility for future ViV procedures. However, attention should be paid to the potential development of intraventricular conduction disturbances that may adversely affect biventricular function.

The Nicks, Manougian, Y-incision, and Konno procedures have been described as surgical enlargement techniques for small aortic annuli. The Nicks technique is simple but generally limited to 1 valve size of enlargement, whereas the Manougian procedure allows 1 to 2 valve sizes but may increase the risk of mitral regurgitation.15, 16, 17 More recently, the Y-incision method has been reported to achieve 3 to 4 sizes of enlargement, facilitating the implantation of larger prostheses and suggesting improved survival and hemodynamics compared with the Nicks or Manougian methods.18,19 However, it does not effectively relieve LVOTO. In contrast, the Konno procedure enlarges both the annulus and the LVOT, making it particularly valuable in patients with combined annular hypoplasia and LVOTO. Although traditionally used in pediatric and young adult cases, the outcomes in adults have also been favorable. Current guidelines note its superior LVOT enlargement but emphasize its technical complexity, limiting its use in adults to mainly redo cases.20 Overall, for patients with a small annulus and LVOTO, the Konno procedure may provide the most definitive enlargement and symptomatic relief, whereas further comparison with the Y-incision and Manougian techniques is warranted.

In our study, the sinus of Valsalva was enlarged by >7 mm, mainly in the left and right coronary cusp directions, and the average VTA at both the left coronary artery and right coronary artery was 6 to 7 mm, ensuring sufficient clearance of the ViV procedures in all bioprosthetic cases. Lifetime management is essential in AS, and implanting a larger prosthesis at the initial surgery helps secure an effective orifice area. Adequate VTA and VTC distances must also be preserved because oversizing increases the risk of coronary obstruction.9,21 Conventional SAVR may further reduce these distances due to changes in aortic root anatomy from suturing and closure of the aortotomy.14 Therefore, both a large prosthesis and adequate sinus enlargement are required to maintain the feasibility of a future ViV procedure. Both the Y-incision and Konno procedures enlarge the sinus of Valsalva; however, the direction of enlargement differs between the 2 techniques. The Y-incision primarily expands toward the noncoronary sinus, whereas the Konno procedure preferentially enlarges the right and left coronary sinuses. Truesdell and colleagues22 demonstrated that when the Y-incision is performed appropriately, adequate coronary clearance can be achieved, with postoperative VTC distances of 6.6 ± 2.3 mm to the left main coronary artery and 4.9 ± 2.0 mm to the right coronary artery. Nevertheless, Lee and colleagues23 reported that compared with the Y-incision group, the Konno group achieved longer postoperative VTCs and VTAs, and all Konno cases met the recommended thresholds of VTC ≥4.0 mm and VTA ≥2.0 mm, whereas a subset of Y-incision cases did not. These findings suggest that the Konno procedure may offer a particular advantage in coronary clearance over the Y-incision technique, especially in patients with small annuli where the direction of sinus enlargement is most critical for future ViV-TAVR feasibility, although direct comparative studies are warranted.

A potential concern with the Konno procedure is postoperative RV dysfunction, which may result directly from incisions into the interventricular septum and RV free wall, or indirectly through injury to right-sided valves.24, 25, 26 In the present study, echocardiographic assessment demonstrated RV end-diastolic diameter enlargement and TAP systolic excursion reduction, together with significant QRS prolongation, indicating intraventricular conduction disturbances. The long-term consequences of QRS prolongation and cardiac dyssynchrony following the Konno procedure remain incompletely understood. Schafer and colleagues25 reported that Ross-Konno procedures were associated with greater RV-LV and electromechanical dyssynchrony compared with the Ross procedure alone, potentially contributing to progressive right-sided heart failure over time. These findings, combined with the significant QRS prolongation observed in our series, underscore the importance of long-term surveillance with serial echocardiography and ECG. Accordingly, the Konno procedure should be approached with caution in patients with preoperative RV dysfunction or those at high risk of complete atrioventricular block. To mitigate the risk of RV dysfunction, we have also introduced the Mini-Konno procedure, which avoids an RV incision; however, comparative studies are warranted to evaluate its influence on postoperative RV function.27

This study had several important limitations. First, this was a single-center retrospective analysis with a limited sample size and limited follow-up duration, which may restrict the generalizability of our findings. Second, a direct comparison with other annular enlargement or sinus of Valsalva enlargement techniques was not feasible; therefore, the relative advantages and disadvantages of each procedure could not be fully clarified. Third, the physiological assessment was based solely on ECG and transthoracic echocardiography. A more comprehensive evaluation, including advanced imaging modalities and region-specific assessment of RV function, is required in future studies to better characterize the hemodynamic and functional consequences of these procedures.

Conclusions

The Konno-AVR procedure effectively enlarged the aortic annulus and LVOT in adult patients, allowing the implantation of larger prosthetic valves and creating a favorable aortic root anatomy for future ViV-TAVR. This approach may help prevent PPM and support long-term management strategies for patients with small annuli. However, a postoperative conduction delay and mild RV functional decline were observed, indicating the need for careful follow-up.
