Identification of source terms in the Schrödinger equation with dynamic boundary conditions from final data
Salah-Eddine Chorfi, Alemdar Hasanov, Roberto Morales

Abstract
In this paper, we study an inverse problem of identifying two spatial-temporal source terms in the Schrödinger equation with dynamic boundary conditions from the final time overdetermination. We adopt a weak solution approach to solve the inverse source problem. By analyzing the associated Tikhonov functional, we prove a gradient formula of the functional in terms of the solution to a suitable adjoint system, allowing us to obtain the Lipschitz continuity of the gradient. Next, the existence and uniqueness of a quasi-solution are also investigated. Finally, our theoretical results are validated by numerical experiments in one dimension using the Landweber iteration method.



Keywords:
Schrödinger equation, inverse problem, dynamic boundary condition, Fréchet gradient, Landweber iteration.

35J10; 35R25; 35R30; 49N45; 47A05.

1
Introduction

The Schrödinger equation is a fundamental model in quantum mechanics which governs how quantum systems evolve over time. The equation describes the dynamical behavior of particles at the quantum level, such as electrons or atoms, by determining their wave functions; see e.g.
[
10
]
. Linear and nonlinear Schrödinger equations have also been intensely studied due to their applications to plasma physics and laser optics; see e.g.
[
6
]
and
[
18
]
. In the recent literature, an increasing interest has been devoted to inverse problems associated with the Schrödinger equation. However, the literature on source identification problems from the final time-measured data is scarce.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
be a bounded domain
(N\geq 1)
with boundary
\Gamma:=\partial\Omega
of class
C^{2}
. For
T>0
, we adopt the notation
Q:=\Omega\times(0,T)
and
\Sigma:=\Gamma\times(0,T)
. In the sequel, function spaces refer to spaces of complex-valued functions. Consider the following initial-boundary value problem for the Schrödinger equation with dynamic boundary conditions

\displaystyle\begin{cases}i\partial_{t}y+d\Delta y-\vec{p}\cdot\nabla y+a(x)y=f(x,t)&\text{ in }Q,\\
i\partial_{t}y_{\Gamma}-d\partial_{\nu}y+\gamma\Delta_{\Gamma}y_{\Gamma}-\vec{p}_{\Gamma}\cdot\nabla_{\Gamma}y_{\Gamma}+a_{\Gamma}(x)y_{\Gamma}=f_{\Gamma}(x,t)&\text{ on }\Sigma,\\
y\big|_{\Gamma}=y_{\Gamma}&\text{ on }\Sigma,\\
(y(\cdot,0),y_{\Gamma}(\cdot,0))=(0,0)&\text{ in }\Omega\times\Gamma.\end{cases}

(1.1)

Here,
i=\sqrt{-1}
is the imaginary unit,
d,\gamma>0
are constant,
(a,a_{\Gamma})\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)\times L^{\infty}(\Gamma)
are potentials,
(\vec{p},\vec{p}_{\Gamma})
are drift vector fields, and
(f,f_{\Gamma})\in L^{2}(Q)\times L^{2}(\Sigma)
is the unknown source term. In addition,
y\big|_{\Gamma}
denotes the trace of
y
at the boundary
\Gamma
,
\partial_{\nu}y=(\nabla y\cdot\nu)\big|_{\Gamma}
is the normal derivative with respect to the outer unit normal field
\nu
on
\Gamma
, and
\nabla_{\Gamma}y
is the tangential gradient defined as
\nabla_{\Gamma}y=\nabla y-(\partial_{\nu}y)\nu
for a smooth function
y
. Due to the linearity of the problem, the pair of initial data
(y_{0},y_{\Gamma,0})
is assumed to be zero. Finally,
\Delta_{\Gamma}
denotes the Laplace-Beltrami operator acting on
\Gamma
(considered a compact Riemannian manifold).

In this paper, we study the
Inverse Source Problem

(ISP)
of simultaneous identifying the pair of unknown spatial-temporal sources
f(x,t)
and
f_{\Gamma}(x,t)
in (
1.1
) from the following final time measured outputs

\displaystyle\begin{cases}u_{T}(x):=y(x,T),&x\in\Omega,\\
u_{T,\Gamma}(x):=y_{\Gamma}(x,T),&x\in\Gamma,\end{cases}

(1.2)

where
y(x,T)\equiv y(x,t)|_{t=T}
and
y_{\Gamma}(x,T)\equiv y_{\Gamma}(x,t)|_{t=T}
are appropriately defined traces,
(y(x,t),y_{\Gamma}(x,t))
is the weak solution of the initial-boundary value problem (
1.1
) corresponding to
(f(x,t),f_{\Gamma}(x,t))
, and
u_{T}(x)
,
u_{T,\Gamma}(x)
represent the measured outputs containing a random noise.

Dynamic boundary condition models (such as (
1.1
)) have attracted increasing interest in recent years. They arise in many models of applied sciences and engineering such as heat transfer, fluid dynamics, the biology of cells, etc; see, for instance,
[
16
,
19
,
27
]
. The reader may refer to
[
20
]
and
[
37
]
for physical interpretations of dynamical boundary conditions. In particular, the null controllability of parabolic equations has been considered in
[
32
]
; the cubic Ginzburg-Landau equation in
[
11
]
and the Schrödinger equation in
[
33
]
.

1.1
Literature on inverse problems

Here, we briefly review some relevant works related to inverse source problems of simultaneous determination of the source terms for evolution PDEs, in particular for the Schrödinger equation under consideration. The inverse source problems of simultaneous determination of source terms in linear parabolic and hyperbolic problems from the final overdetermination have been studied in
[
22
]
and
[
23
]
. A weak solution approach combined with the quasi-solution and adjoint methods was proposed, and explicit gradient formulas for both problems were derived. This approach also provides a monotone iteration scheme to reconstruct unknown parameters, leading to rapid numerical reconstruction. One could also refer to
[
13
]
. It has been successfully applied to reconstruct forcing terms in wave equations
[
15
]
, Euler-Bernoulli beam equation
[
21
,
24
]
, source terms and initial temperatures in heat equations with dynamic boundary conditions
[
2
,
14
]
. For a comprehensive study on this method, the reader may refer to the book
[
25
]
. It should be emphasized that this method has not been applied to the Schrödinger equation up to our knowledge.

In addition to the aforementioned, problems related to uniqueness and stability have also been extensively studied. The problem of determining a spatial potential from boundary measurement using global Carleman estimates, leading to Lipschitz stability, was investigated in
[
8
,
9
]
. This technique has then been extended to a similar problem with a discontinuous principal coefficient in
[
7
]
. In
[
34
]
, the authors showed stability results under less boundary or internal measurements using degenerate Carleman weights. Some one-dimensional inverse source and potential problems based on boundary measurements have been studied using the boundary control method
[
4
,
5
]
. In
[
38
]
, the authors determined the wave function from boundary measurements using an improved kernel method. Regularization and optimal error bounds are given with numerical examples. The authors of
[
26
]
have proven the Lipschitz stability for real-valued source terms and potentials from data taken at the terminal time
T
with a boundary measurement.

For inverse parabolic problems with dynamic boundary conditions, we refer to
[
1
,
3
]
, where the Lipschitz stability was proved for source terms and potentials, while the logarithmic stability was established for initial data. Finally, we refer to
[
12
]
for the well-posedness and uniform stability of nonlinear Schrödinger equations.

1.2
Notation and well-posedness of the system

Throughout the paper,
\operatorname{Re}z
,
\operatorname{Im}z
,
\overline{z}
, and
|z|
will denote the real part, the imaginary part, the conjugate, and the modulus of the complex number
z
, respectively.

We introduce the Banach space

\displaystyle\mathbb{L}^{p}:=L^{p}(\Omega,dx)\times L^{p}(\Gamma,d\sigma),\qquad 1\leq p\leq\infty,

where we denoted by
dx
and
d\sigma
the Lebesgue and surface measures on
\Omega
and
\Gamma
, respectively. In the Hilbert space
\mathbb{L}^{2}
, we consider the inner product

\langle(y,y_{\Gamma}),(z,z_{\Gamma})\rangle_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}=\operatorname{Re}\int_{\Omega}y\overline{z}\,dx+\operatorname{Re}\int_{\Gamma}y_{\Gamma}\overline{z_{\Gamma}}\,d\sigma.

For
k\in\mathbb{N}
and
1\leq p\leq\infty
, we define the Banach space

\displaystyle\mathbb{W}^{k,p}:=\left\{(y,y_{\Gamma})\in W^{k,p}(\Omega)\times W^{k,p}(\Gamma)\,:\,y\big|_{\Gamma}=y_{\Gamma}\right\},

equipped by the natural norm. In particular, for
k\in\mathbb{N}
, we write

\displaystyle\mathbb{H}^{k}:=\mathbb{W}^{k,2}.

We emphasize that, for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
,
\mathbb{H}^{k}
is a Hilbert space with the inner product

\displaystyle\langle(u,u_{\Gamma}),(v,v_{\Gamma})\rangle_{\mathbb{H}^{k}}=\langle u,v\rangle_{H^{k}(\Omega)}+\langle u_{\Gamma},v_{\Gamma}\rangle_{H^{k}(\Gamma)}.

To simplify the computation in the well-posedness of (
1.1
) and the Fréchet gradient formula, we henceforth assume that the coefficients
\vec{p}
and
\vec{p}_{\Gamma}
in (
1.1
) have the following form:

\displaystyle\vec{p}(x)=-i\vec{r}(x)\;\text{ in }\Omega\qquad\text{and}\qquad\vec{p}_{\Gamma}(x)=-i\vec{r}_{\Gamma}(x)\;\text{ on }\Gamma,

(1.3)

where
(\vec{r},\vec{r}_{\Gamma})\in[\mathbb{W}^{1,\infty}]^{N}
is real-valued. Otherwise, a general class of drift coefficients can be recast to (
1.3
); see
[
29
]
for more details.
We have the following result:

Proposition 1.1
.

Consider
d,\gamma>0
.

(a)

Suppose that
(a,a_{\Gamma})\in\mathbb{L}^{\infty}
and
(f,f_{\Gamma})\in L^{1}(0,T;\mathbb{L}^{2})
. Then, there exists a unique weak solution
(y,y_{\Gamma})\in C^{0}([0,T];\mathbb{L}^{2})
of (
1.1
). Moreover, there exists a constant
C>0
independent of
(f,f_{\Gamma})
such that

\displaystyle\|(y,y_{\Gamma})\|_{C^{0}([0,T];\mathbb{L}^{2})}\leq C\|(f,f_{\Gamma})\|_{L^{1}(0,T;\mathbb{L}^{2})}.

(b)

Suppose that
(a,a_{\Gamma})\in\mathbb{W}^{1,\infty}
and
(f,f_{\Gamma})\in L^{2}(0,T;\mathbb{H}^{1})
. Then (
1.1
) has a unique solution
(y,y_{\Gamma})\in C^{0}([0,T];\mathbb{H}^{1})
with extra regularity
\partial_{\nu}y\in L^{2}(\Sigma)
. In addition, there exists a constant
C>0
independent of
(f,f_{\Gamma})
such that

\displaystyle\|(y,y_{\Gamma})\|_{C^{0}([0,T];\mathbb{H}^{1})}+\|\partial_{\nu}y\|_{L^{2}(\Sigma)}\leq C\|(f,f_{\Gamma})\|_{L^{2}(0,T;\mathbb{H}^{1})}.

The proof of Proposition
1.1
relies on some elements of the semigroup theory. We sketch a proof in the Appendix
A
for completeness.

1.3
Input-output operator

We introduce the
input-output operators

\displaystyle\begin{cases}(\Psi_{1}F)(x):=y(x,T;F),&x\in\Omega,\\
(\Psi_{2}F)(x):=y_{\Gamma}(x,T;F),&x\in\Gamma,\end{cases}

(1.4)

associated with the inverse source problem (
1.1
)-(
1.2
), where

F(x,t):=(f(x,t),f_{\Gamma}(x,t))\in\mathcal{F},

(1.5)

and
\mathcal{F}\subset L^{2}(0,T;\mathbb{L}^{2})
is an appropriately defined
set of admissible spatial-temporal sources
that is bounded, closed and convex.

In view of these operators, the inverse source problem can be reformulated as the following system of linear operator equations:

\displaystyle\begin{cases}(\Psi_{1}F)(x)=u_{T}(x),&x\in\Omega,\\
(\Psi_{2}F)(x)=u_{T,\Gamma}(x),&x\in\Gamma,\quad F\in\mathcal{F},\end{cases}

(1.6)

where
u_{T}\in L^{2}(\Omega)
and
u_{T,\Gamma}\in L^{2}(\Gamma)
are the measured outputs defined in (
1.2
). We will also write
U_{T}:=(u_{T},u_{T,\Gamma})
.

However, due to measurement errors in the outputs
u_{T}
and
u_{T,\Gamma}
, exact equality in the equations (
1.6
) can not be satisfied. Hence one needs to introduce the Tikhonov functional

J(F)=\frac{1}{2}\,\|\Psi_{1}F-u_{T}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\frac{1}{2}\,\|\Psi_{2}F-u_{T,\Gamma}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Gamma)},

(1.7)

and reformulate the inverse source problem (
1.1
)-(
1.2
) as the following minimization problem

J(F^{*})=\inf_{F\in\mathcal{F}}\,J(F).

(1.8)

This is called the quasi-solution method; see Ivanov
[
28
]
.

Proposition 1.2
.

The input-output operator
\Psi:L^{2}(0,T;\mathbb{H}^{1})\to\mathbb{L}^{2}
is compact.

Proof.

Let
(F_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
be a bounded sequence in
L^{2}(0,T;\mathbb{H}^{1})
. Then, by Proposition
1.1
, the sequence of associated weak solutions
Y(\cdot,\cdot,F_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
is bounded in
C^{0}([0,T];\mathbb{H}^{1})
. In particular, the sequence
Y_{k}:=Y(\cdot,T,F_{k})
is bounded in
\mathbb{H}^{1}
. Thus, by the compact embedding
\mathbb{H}^{1}\hookrightarrow\mathbb{L}^{2}
, there exists a subsequence of
(Y_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
which converges in
\mathbb{L}^{2}
. This implies that the input-output operator
\Psi
is compact.
∎

From Proposition
1.2
, it is clear that the inverse problem
(ISP)
is ill-posed in the sense of Hadamard. Due to the ill-posedness of (
1.6
), one usually considers a regularized version of (
1.7
) (e.g. a Tikhonov regularization). More precisely, for a regularizing parameter
\epsilon>0
, we introduce the regularized functional

\displaystyle J_{\epsilon}(F)=\frac{1}{2}\,\|\Psi_{1}F-u_{T}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\frac{1}{2}\,\|\Psi_{2}F-u_{T,\Gamma}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Gamma)}+\frac{\epsilon}{2}\|F\|^{2}_{L^{2}(0,T;\mathbb{L}^{2})},\quad F\in\mathcal{F}.

(1.9)

In the sequel, we omit the Tikhonov regularization, leveraging the regularization property of the adjoint Landweber iteration (see
[
36
]
). The reader may refer to
[
17
]
for regularization of ill-posed problems.

The rest of the article is organized as follows. In Section

(ISP)
. In Section

1.1
).

2
Fréchet differentiability and gradient formula

Now, we derive a gradient formula for the Tikhonov functional
J
which is the key result for the sequel.

Proposition 2.1
.

The Tikhonov functional
J\colon\mathcal{F}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is Fréchet differentiable and its gradient at each
F\in\mathcal{F}
is given by

\displaystyle J^{\prime}(F)=\Phi,

(2.1)

where
\Phi(\cdot,\cdot;F)=(\phi,\phi_{\Gamma})
is the unique weak solution of the following adjoint system

\displaystyle\begin{cases}i\partial_{t}\phi+d\Delta\phi+i\vec{r}\cdot\nabla\phi+b\phi=0&\text{ in }Q,\\
i\partial_{t}\phi_{\Gamma}-d\partial_{\nu}\phi+\gamma\Delta_{\Gamma}\phi_{\Gamma}+i\vec{r}_{\Gamma}\cdot\nabla_{\Gamma}\phi_{\Gamma}+b_{\Gamma}\phi_{\Gamma}=0&\text{ on }\Sigma,\\
\phi\big|_{\Gamma}=\phi_{\Gamma}&\text{ on }\Sigma,\\
(\phi(\cdot,T),\phi_{\Gamma}(\cdot,T))=i(Y(\cdot,T;F)-U_{T}),&\text{ in }\Omega\times\Gamma,\end{cases}

(2.2)

where the coefficients
b
and
b_{\Gamma}
are given by

\displaystyle b:=i\mathrm{div}(\vec{r})+\overline{a},\qquad b_{\Gamma}:=i\mathrm{div}(\vec{r}_{\Gamma})-i\vec{r}\cdot\nu+\overline{a}_{\Gamma}.

(2.3)

Proof.

Consider
F,\delta F\in\mathcal{F}
such that
F+\delta F\in\mathcal{F}
. Let us compute the difference

\displaystyle\delta J(F):=J(F+\delta F)-J(F).

Then, straightforward computations show that

\displaystyle\delta J(F)

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{2}\|Y(\cdot,T;F+\delta F)-U_{T}\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}-\dfrac{1}{2}\|Y(\cdot,T;F)-U_{T}\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}(|y(\cdot,T;F+\delta F)-u_{T}|^{2}-|y(\cdot,T;F)-u_{T}|^{2})\,dx

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Gamma}(|y_{\Gamma}(\cdot,T;F+\delta F)-u_{T,\Gamma}|^{2}-|y_{\Gamma}(\cdot,T;F)-u_{T,\Gamma}|^{2})\,d\sigma.

Now, using the complex identity

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}(|x-z|^{2}-|y-z|^{2})=\operatorname{Re}[(x-y)\overline{(y-z)}]+\dfrac{1}{2}|x-y|^{2}\qquad\forall x,y,z\in\mathbb{C},

we have

\displaystyle\delta J(F)=

\displaystyle\operatorname{Re}\int_{\Omega}(y(\cdot,T;F+\delta F)-y(\cdot,T;F))\overline{(y(\cdot,T;F)-u_{T}})\,dx

\displaystyle+\dfrac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}|y(\cdot,T;F+\delta F)-y(\cdot,T;F)|^{2}\,dx

\displaystyle+\operatorname{Re}\int_{\Gamma}(y_{\Gamma}(\cdot,T;F+\delta F)-y_{\Gamma}(\cdot,T;F))\overline{(y_{\Gamma}(\cdot,T;F)-u_{T,\Gamma})}\,d\sigma

\displaystyle+\dfrac{1}{2}\int_{\Gamma}|y_{\Gamma}(\cdot,T;F+\delta F)-y_{\Gamma}(\cdot,T;F)|^{2}\,d\sigma,

\displaystyle\begin{split}=&-\operatorname{Im}\int_{\Omega}\delta y(\cdot,T;F)\overline{\phi(\cdot,T;F)}\,dx+\dfrac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}|\delta y(\cdot,T;F)|^{2}\,dx\\
&-\operatorname{Im}\int_{\Gamma}\delta y_{\Gamma}(\cdot,T;F)\overline{\phi_{\Gamma}(\cdot,T;F)}\,d\sigma+\dfrac{1}{2}\int_{\Gamma}|\delta y_{\Gamma}(\cdot,T;F)|^{2}\,d\sigma,\end{split}

(2.4)

where
\delta Y=(\delta y,\delta y_{\Gamma})
is the solution of the following sensitivity system

\displaystyle\begin{cases}i\partial_{t}(\delta y)+d\Delta(\delta y)+i\vec{r}\cdot\nabla(\delta y)+a(x)(\delta y)=\delta f&\text{ in }Q,\\
i\partial_{t}(\delta y_{\Gamma})-d\partial_{\nu}(\delta y)+\gamma\Delta_{\Gamma}(\delta y_{\Gamma})+i\vec{r}_{\Gamma}\cdot\nabla_{\Gamma}(\delta y_{\Gamma})+a_{\Gamma}(x)(\delta y_{\Gamma})=\delta f_{\Gamma}&\text{ on }\Sigma,\\
\delta y\big|_{\Gamma}=\delta y_{\Gamma}&\text{ on }\Sigma,\\
(\delta y(\cdot,0),\delta y(\cdot,0))=(0,0)&\text{ in }\Omega\times\Gamma.\end{cases}

(2.5)

Then by integration by parts, it is clear that (
2.4
) can be written as

\displaystyle\begin{split}\delta J(F)=&\operatorname{Re}\iint_{Q}\delta f\overline{\phi}\,dx\,dt+\operatorname{Re}\iint_{\Sigma}\delta f_{\Gamma}\overline{\phi_{\Gamma}}\,d\sigma\,dt+\dfrac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}|\delta y(\cdot,T;F)|^{2}\,dx\\
&+\dfrac{1}{2}\int_{\Gamma}|\delta y_{\Gamma}(\cdot,T;F)|^{2}\,d\sigma.\end{split}

(2.6)

Therefore, the equality (
2.6
) implies the identity (
2.1
).
∎

Next, we prove a crucial lemma when dealing with gradient iterative methods.

Lemma 2.2
.

The Fréchet gradient
J^{\prime}
is Lipschitz continuous, i.e., there exists a constant
L>0
such that for all
F,\delta F\in\mathcal{F}
such that
F+\delta F\in\mathcal{F}
,

\displaystyle\|J^{\prime}(F+\delta F)-J^{\prime}(F)\|_{L^{2}(0,T;\mathbb{L}^{2})}\leq L\|\delta F\|_{L^{2}(0,T;\mathbb{L}^{2})}.

(2.7)

Proof.

Fix
F,\delta F\in\mathcal{F}
such that
F+\delta F\in\mathcal{F}
. Then,
\delta\Phi(\cdot,T;F)=(\delta\phi,\delta\phi_{\Gamma})
is the solution of the adjoint system

\displaystyle\begin{cases}i\partial_{t}(\delta\phi)+d\Delta(\delta\phi)+i\vec{r}\cdot\nabla(\delta\phi)+b(x)(\delta\phi)=0,&\text{ in }Q,\\
i\partial_{t}(\delta\phi_{\Gamma})-d\partial_{\nu}(\delta\phi)+\gamma\Delta_{\Gamma}(\delta\phi_{\Gamma})+i\vec{r}_{\Gamma}\cdot\nabla_{\Gamma}(\delta\phi_{\Gamma})+b_{\Gamma}(x)(\delta\phi_{\Gamma})=0&\text{ on }\Sigma,\\
\delta\phi\big|_{\Gamma}=\delta\phi_{\Gamma}&\text{ on }\Sigma,\\
(\delta\phi,\delta\phi_{\Gamma})(\cdot,T)=i(\delta y(\cdot,T;F),\delta y(\cdot,T;F))&\text{ in }\Omega\times\Gamma,\end{cases}

(2.8)

where
\delta Y:=(\delta y(\cdot,\cdot;F),\delta y_{\Gamma}(\cdot,\cdot;F))
is the solution of (
2.5
) associated to
\delta F
and
(b,b_{\Gamma})
are the potentials defined in (
2.3
). By Proposition
2.1
, we have for some generic constant
C>0
,

\displaystyle\|J^{\prime}(F+\delta F)-J^{\prime}(F)\|_{L^{2}(0,T;\mathbb{L}^{2})}=\|\delta\Phi\|_{L^{2}(0,T;\mathbb{L}^{2})}\leq C\|\delta Y(\cdot,T;F)\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}\leq C\|\delta F\|_{L^{2}(0,T;\mathbb{L}^{2})},

where we have used Proposition
1.1
(part (a)) applied to
\delta Y=(\delta y,\delta y_{\Gamma})
. This ends the proof of Lemma
2.2
.
∎

3
Existence and uniqueness of the solution to (ISP)

Using the gradient formula (
2.1
) and the sensitivity system (
2.5
), one can easily prove the monotonicity of the derivative
J^{\prime}:\mathcal{F}\to\mathbb{R}
. This implies the convexity of the functional
J
. Consequently, we have the following existence result.

Corollary 3.1
.

The Tikhonov functional
J
is continuous and convex on
\mathcal{F}
. Then, there exists a minimizer
F^{*}\in\mathcal{F}
such that

\displaystyle J(F^{*})=\min_{F\in\mathcal{F}}J(F).

Since the strict convexity of
J
is characterized by the strict monotonicity of
J^{\prime}
, straightforward computation yields the following sufficient condition for uniqueness.

Lemma 3.2
.

If the positivity condition

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}|\delta y(\cdot,T;F)|^{2}\,dx+\int_{\Gamma}|\delta y_{\Gamma}(\cdot,T;F)|^{2}\,d\sigma>0\qquad\forall F\in\mathcal{V},

holds on a closed convex subset
\mathcal{V}\subset\mathcal{F}
, then the problem
(ISP)
admits at most one solution in
\mathcal{V}
.

4
Numerical experiments for the 1D case

In this section, we examine the numerical reconstruction of a spatial forcing term. Let
\ell>0
and consider reconstructing a complex-valued function
f(x)
in the 1-D Schrödinger equation with dynamic boundary conditions

\displaystyle\begin{cases}iy_{t}+y_{xx}=f(x)g(x,t)&\qquad(x,t)\in(0,\ell)\times(0,T),\\
iy_{t}(0,t)+y_{x}(0,t)=0&\qquad t\in(0,T),\\
iy_{t}(\ell,t)-y_{x}(\ell,t)=0&\qquad t\in(0,T),\\
(y,y(0,\cdot),y(\ell,\cdot))\rvert_{t=0}=(0,0,0)&\qquad x\in(0,\ell),\end{cases}

(4.1)

where the function
g\in L^{2}\left(0,T;L^{\infty}(0,\ell)\right)
is known. This special form of forcing terms is usually required to ensure the uniqueness of the inverse problem (see, e.g.,
[
4
]
).

Remark 4.1
.

Here we consider the reconstruction of one internal source term
f(x)
, because boundary source terms
f_{\Gamma}(x)
are constant in this 1D case. For simplicity, we omit the reconstruction of constant functions.

Let
Y(x,t;f):=\left(y(x,t),y(0,t),y(\ell,t)\right)
be the solution to (
4.1
). Recall that the input-output operator
\Psi\colon L^{2}(0,\ell)\longrightarrow L^{2}(0,\ell)\times\mathbb{C}^{2}
is defined by

(\Psi f)(x):=Y(x,T;f)=\left(y(x,T),y(0,T),y(\ell,T)\right),\qquad x\in(0,\ell),

and the Tikhonov functional is given by

\displaystyle J(f)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left\|Y(\cdot,T;f)-U_{T}\right\|_{L^{2}(0,\ell)\times\mathbb{C}^{2}}^{2},\qquad f\in L^{2}(0,\ell),

\displaystyle\hskip-8.5359pt=\frac{1}{2}\left(\left\|y(\cdot,T)-u_{T}\right\|_{L^{2}(0,\ell)}^{2}+\left|y(0,T)-u_{T}^{0}\right|^{2}+\left|y(\ell,T)-u_{T}^{\ell}\right|^{2}\right),

where
U_{T}:=\left(u_{T},u_{T}^{0},u_{T}^{\ell}\right)\in L^{2}(0,\ell)\times\mathbb{C}^{2}
.
The adjoint system associated with (
4.1
) reads as follows

\displaystyle\begin{cases}i\phi_{t}+\phi_{xx}=0&\qquad(x,t)\in(0,\ell)\times(0,T),\\
i\phi_{t}(0,t)+\phi_{x}(0,t)=0&\qquad t\in(0,T),\\
i\phi_{t}(\ell,t)-\phi_{x}(\ell,t)=0&\qquad t\in(0,T),\\
\phi(x,T)=i(y(x,T)-u_{T}(x))&\qquad x\in(0,\ell),\\
(\phi(0,T),\phi(\ell,T))=i\left(y(0,T)-u_{T}^{0},y(\ell,T)-u_{T}^{\ell}\right)&\qquad x\in(0,\ell).\end{cases}

(4.2)

In a similar manner to the computations in Section
2
, the gradient of
J
can be expressed as

J^{\prime}(f)(x)=\int_{0}^{T}\phi(x,t;f)g(x,t)\,dt,\quad f\in L^{2}(0,\ell),\;x\in(0,\ell).

(4.3)

This gradient formula enables the implementation of gradient-type iterative methods. We shall use the Landweber iteration (Algorithm
1
). We refer to
[
25
, §3.4.3]
for more details.

Algorithm 1

Landweber iteration

Set
k=0
and choose an initial guess
f_{0}
;

1

Solve the forward problem (
4.1
) to obtain
Y(x,t;f_{k})
;

2

Knowing the computed
Y(x,T;f_{k})
and the measured
U_{T}
, solve the adjoint system (
4.2
) to obtain
\phi(x,t;f_{k})
;

3

Compute the descent direction
p_{k}=J^{\prime}(f_{k})
using (
4.3
);

4

Solve the forward problem (
4.1
) with source
f=p_{k}
to get the solution
\Psi p_{k}
;

5

Compute the step parameter
\displaystyle\alpha_{k}=\frac{\|p_{k}\|_{L^{2}(0,\ell)}^{2}}{\|\Psi p_{k}\|_{L^{2}(0,\ell)\times\mathbb{C}^{2}}^{2}}
;

6

Compute the next iteration
f_{k+1}=f_{k}-\alpha_{k}p_{k}
;

7

Stop the algorithm if the stopping criterion
J(f_{k+1})<e_{J}
holds for a given tolerance
e_{J}
. Otherwise, set
k:=k+1
and run Step 2;

The algorithm will be implemented using the
Wolfram
language to solve the forward and backward PDEs using the method of lines. We discretize the spatial interval
[0,\ell]
into a uniform grid
(x_{j})_{j=0}^{N_{x}}
with step size
h=\frac{\ell}{N_{x}}
using the finite difference method. Noting
y_{j}(t):=y(x_{j},t)
, the second derivative is approximated by

y_{xx}(x_{j},t)\approx\frac{y_{j-1}(t)-2y_{j}(t)+y_{j+1}(t)}{h^{2}},\qquad j=\overline{1,N_{x}-1}.

The first derivatives on the boundary are approximated by

\displaystyle y_{x}(0,t)

\displaystyle\approx\frac{y_{1}(t)-y_{0}(t)}{h},

\displaystyle y_{x}(\ell,t)

\displaystyle\approx\frac{y_{N_{x}}(t)-y_{N_{x}-1}(t)}{h}.

Then, we solve the resulting system of ordinary differential equations numerically.

The noisy terminal data will be generated from the exact output data as follows

U_{T}(x)=Y_{T}(x)+p\times\|Y_{T}\|_{L^{2}(0,\ell)\times\mathbb{C}^{2}}\times\text{(random number)},

where
p
represents the percentage of noise level.

In subsequent numerical experiments, we choose the following values

T=1,\quad\ell=1,\quad N_{x}=25,\quad g=1,\quad f_{0}=0.

5
Further comments and perspectives

We have investigated an inverse problem involving the determination of internal and boundary source terms in the Schrödinger equation with dynamic boundary conditions, using measured final time output data. We have analyzed a minimization problem for the associated Tikhonov functional and derived a gradient formula based on the solution of an appropriate adjoint system. Furthermore, we have established the Lipschitz continuity of the Fréchet gradient, which is crucial in gradient iterative methods.

The Landweber scheme we have considered provides stable and accurate results for reconstructing unknown source terms in the Schrödinger equation with dynamic boundary conditions. One could use other sophisticated gradient schemes such as conjugate gradient algorithms. Nevertheless, we chose the Landweber iteration as a basic scheme. Our approach can be extended to recover source terms in a multidimensional setting (
N=2
or
3
). However, dynamic boundary conditions are more delicate to handle numerically in this case due to the geometry of the domain
\Omega
. This will be addressed in a forthcoming paper.

Appendix. Proof of Proposition

In this appendix, we prove the well-posedness and regularity of solutions to problem (

A.1
Wentzell Laplacian

We introduce the Wentzell-Laplacian operator
A_{W}:D(A_{W})\subset\mathbb{L}^{2}\to\mathbb{L}^{2}
given by

\displaystyle A_{W}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}u\\
u_{\Gamma}\end{array}\right)=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}-d\Delta u\\
d\partial_{\nu}u-\gamma\Delta_{\Gamma}u_{\Gamma}\end{array}\right),

\displaystyle D(A_{W})=\{(u,u_{\Gamma})\in\mathbb{H}^{1}\,:\,(\Delta u,\gamma\Delta_{\Gamma}u_{\Gamma}-d\partial_{\nu}u)\in\mathbb{L}^{2}\}=\mathbb{H}^{2},

where the last equality is obtained by the elliptic regularity.

Next, we summarize some properties of
A_{W}
in the following proposition.

Proposition A.1

(See
[
32
]
)
.

The operator
-A_{W}
is densely defined, self-adjoint, non-positive and generates an analytic
C_{0}
-semigroup
(e^{-tA_{W}})_{t\geq 0}
on
\mathbb{L}^{2}
. Moreover,
D(A_{W}^{1/2})\equiv\mathbb{H}^{1}
.

Now, for
(\vec{r},\vec{r}_{\Gamma})\in[\mathbb{W}^{1,\infty}]^{N}
real-valued, let us consider the operator
A:D(A)\subset\mathbb{L}^{2}\to\mathbb{L}^{2}
defined by

\displaystyle A\left(\begin{array}[]{c}u\\
u_{\Gamma}\end{array}\right)=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}di\Delta u-\vec{r}\cdot\nabla u\\
-di\partial_{\nu}u+\gamma i\Delta_{\Gamma}u_{\Gamma}-\vec{r}_{\Gamma}\cdot\nabla_{\Gamma}u_{\Gamma}\end{array}\right),\quad D(A)=D(A_{W})=\mathbb{H}^{2}.

Proposition A.2
.

Suppose that
\vec{r}\cdot\nu\leq 0
on
\Gamma
. There exists a constant
\kappa\in\mathbb{R}
which depends on
d,\gamma>0
,
\vec{r}
and
\vec{r}_{\Gamma}
such that the scaled operator
A-\kappa I_{\mathbb{H}^{1}}
is maximal dissipative on
D(A_{W}^{1/2})
. Thus,
A
generates a
C_{0}
-semigroup
(e^{tA})_{t\geq 0}
on
\mathbb{H}^{1}
satisfying

\displaystyle\|e^{tA}\|_{\mathbb{H}^{1}}\leq e^{\kappa t},\quad t\geq 0.

Proof.

For each
U:=(u,u_{\Gamma})\in D(A_{W}^{1/2})\equiv\mathbb{H}^{1}
, we have

\langle AU,U\rangle_{D(A_{W}^{1/2})}=\langle AU,A_{W}U\rangle_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}+\langle AU,U\rangle_{\mathbb{L}^{2}},

with

\displaystyle\langle AU,A_{W}U\rangle_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}=

\displaystyle d\operatorname{Re}\int_{\Omega}(\vec{r}\cdot\nabla u)\Delta\overline{u}\,dx-d\operatorname{Re}\int_{\Gamma}(\vec{r}_{\Gamma}\cdot\nabla_{\Gamma}u_{\Gamma})\partial_{\nu}\overline{u}\,d\sigma

\displaystyle+\gamma\operatorname{Re}\int_{\Gamma}(\vec{r}_{\Gamma}\cdot\nabla_{\Gamma}u_{\Gamma})\Delta_{\Gamma}\overline{u}_{\Gamma}d\sigma

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-d\operatorname{Re}\int_{\Omega}\left(\vec{\nabla}\vec{r}(\nabla u,\nabla\overline{u})+\dfrac{1}{2}\vec{r}\cdot\nabla(|\nabla u|^{2})\right)\,dx

\displaystyle\hskip-19.91684pt-\gamma\operatorname{Re}\int_{\Gamma}\left(\vec{\nabla}_{\Gamma}\vec{r}_{\Gamma}(\nabla_{\Gamma}u_{\Gamma},\nabla_{\Gamma}u_{\Gamma})+\frac{1}{2}\vec{r}_{\Gamma}\cdot\nabla_{\Gamma}(|\nabla_{\Gamma}u_{\Gamma}|^{2})\right)\,d\sigma+d\int_{\Gamma}(\vec{r}\cdot\nu)|\partial_{\nu}u|^{2}\,d\sigma

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-d\operatorname{Re}\int_{\Omega}\vec{\nabla}\vec{r}(\nabla u,\nabla\overline{u})\,dx+\frac{d}{2}\int_{\Omega}\mathrm{div}(\vec{r})|\nabla u|^{2}\,dx+\dfrac{d}{2}\int_{\Gamma}(\vec{r}\cdot\nu)|\partial_{\nu}u|^{2}\,d\sigma

\displaystyle\hskip-36.98866pt-\frac{d}{2}\int_{\Gamma}(\vec{r}\cdot\nu)|\nabla_{\Gamma}u_{\Gamma}|^{2}\,d\sigma-\gamma\operatorname{Re}\int_{\Gamma}\vec{\nabla}_{\Gamma}\vec{r}_{\Gamma}(\nabla_{\Gamma}u_{\Gamma},\nabla_{\Gamma}\overline{u}_{\Gamma})\,d\sigma+\frac{\gamma}{2}\int_{\Gamma}\mathrm{div}_{\Gamma}(\vec{r}_{\Gamma})|\nabla_{\Gamma}u_{\Gamma}|^{2}\,d\sigma,

where we denote by
\vec{\nabla}\vec{r}
and
\vec{\nabla}_{\Gamma}\vec{r}_{\Gamma}
the Jacobian matrices in
\Omega
and on
\Gamma
, respectively. We have used integration by parts in
\Omega
and the surface divergence formula on
\Gamma
. The second term
\langle AU,U\rangle_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}
can be computed similarly. Since
(\vec{r},\vec{r}_{\Gamma})\in\mathbb{W}^{1,\infty}
, we can find
\kappa\in\mathbb{R}
such that

\displaystyle\langle AU-\kappa U,U\rangle_{D(A_{W}^{1/2})}\leq 0\qquad\forall\,U=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}u\\
u_{\Gamma}\end{array}\right)\in D(A_{W}^{1/2}).

On the other hand, to prove the maximal dissipativity of
A-\kappa I_{\mathbb{H}^{1}}
, we consider
\lambda>0
and
G=(g,g_{\Gamma})\in D(A_{W}^{1/2})
. Then, we need to prove the existence of a unique
F=(f,f_{\Gamma})\in D(A)
such that

\displaystyle[\lambda I-(A-\kappa I)]\left(\begin{array}[]{c}f\\
f_{\Gamma}\end{array}\right)=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}g\\
g_{\Gamma}\end{array}\right).

The equation (
A.1
) reads as follows:

\displaystyle\begin{cases}-di\Delta f+\vec{r}\cdot\nabla f+(\lambda+\kappa)f=g&\text{ in }\Omega,\\
di\partial_{\nu}f-\gamma i\Delta_{\Gamma}f_{\Gamma}+\vec{r}_{\Gamma}\cdot\nabla f_{\Gamma}+(\lambda+\kappa)f_{\Gamma}=g_{\Gamma}&\text{ on }\Gamma,\\
f\big|_{\Gamma}=f_{\Gamma}&\text{ on }\Gamma.\end{cases}

(A.5)

By the elliptic regularity results for
\Delta
and
\Delta_{\Gamma}
(see
[
35
]
), we can ensure the existence of a unique solution
(f,f_{\Gamma})
with the desired properties. This provides the maximal dissipativity of
A-\kappa I_{\mathbb{H}^{1}}
.
∎

A.2
Well-posedness

Now, we focus on the non-homogeneous Cauchy problem

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\partial_{t}u-di\Delta u+\vec{\rho}\cdot\nabla u+\alpha u=g&\text{ in }Q,\\
\partial_{t}u_{\Gamma}+di\partial_{\nu}u-\gamma i\Delta_{\Gamma}u_{\Gamma}+\vec{\rho}_{\Gamma}\cdot\nabla_{\Gamma}u_{\Gamma}+\alpha_{\Gamma}u_{\Gamma}=g_{\Gamma}&\text{ on }\Sigma,\\
u\big|_{\Gamma}=u_{\Gamma}&\text{ on }\Sigma,\\
(u(\cdot,0),u_{\Gamma}(\cdot,0))=(u_{0},u_{\Gamma,0})&\text{ in }\Omega\times\Gamma.\end{cases}

(A.6)

Proposition A.3
.

Suppose that
(u_{0},u_{\Gamma,0})\in\mathbb{L}^{2}
,
(\vec{\rho},\vec{\rho}_{\Gamma})\in\mathbb{W}^{1,\infty}
is real-valued,
(\alpha,\alpha_{\Gamma})\in\mathbb{L}^{\infty}
and
(g,g_{\Gamma})\in L^{1}(0,T;\mathbb{L}^{2})
. Then, there exists a constant
C>0
such that the weak solution
(u,u_{\Gamma})\in C^{0}([0,T];\mathbb{L}^{2})
of (
A.6
) satisfies

\displaystyle\|(u,u_{\Gamma})\|_{C^{0}([0,T];\mathbb{L}^{2})}\leq C\left(\|(u_{0},u_{\Gamma,0})\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}+\|(g,g_{\Gamma})\|_{L^{1}(0,T;\mathbb{L}^{2})}\right).

(A.7)

Proof.

We argue by density. Firstly, we multiply the first equation of (
A.6
) by
\overline{u}
and integrate on
\Omega
. Secondly, we multiply the second equation of (
A.6
) by
\overline{u}_{\Gamma}
and integrate on
\Gamma
. Next, we add up these identities and take the real part of the obtained equation. Then, the following identity holds for a.e.
t\in(0,T)

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\operatorname{Re}\int_{\Omega}\overline{u}\partial_{t}u\,dx+d\operatorname{Im}\int_{\Omega}\overline{u}\Delta u\,dx+\operatorname{Re}\int_{\Omega}\overline{u}(\vec{\rho}\cdot\nabla u)\,dx+\operatorname{Re}\int_{\Omega}\alpha|u|^{2}\,dx\\
&+\operatorname{Re}\int_{\Gamma}\overline{u}_{\Gamma}\partial_{t}u_{\Gamma}\,d\sigma-d\operatorname{Im}\int_{\Gamma}\overline{u}_{\Gamma}\partial_{\nu}u\,d\sigma+\gamma\operatorname{Im}\int_{\Gamma}\overline{u}_{\Gamma}\Delta_{\Gamma}u_{\Gamma}\,d\sigma\\
&+\operatorname{Re}\int_{\Gamma}\overline{u}_{\Gamma}(\vec{\rho}_{\Gamma}\cdot\nabla_{\Gamma}u_{\Gamma})\,d\sigma+\operatorname{Re}\int_{\Gamma}\alpha_{\Gamma}|u_{\Gamma}|^{2}\,d\sigma\\
=&\operatorname{Re}\int_{\Omega}\overline{u}g\,dx+\operatorname{Re}\int_{\Gamma}\overline{u}_{\Gamma}g_{\Gamma}\,d\sigma.\end{split}

(A.8)

Notice that

\displaystyle\operatorname{Re}\int_{\Omega}\overline{u}\partial_{t}u\,dx+\operatorname{Re}\int_{\Gamma}\overline{u}_{\Gamma}\partial_{t}u_{\Gamma}\,d\sigma=\dfrac{d}{dt}\left(\dfrac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}|u|^{2}\,dx+\dfrac{1}{2}\int_{\Gamma}|u_{\Gamma}|^{2}\,d\sigma\right).

(A.9)

Moreover, integration by parts in
\Omega
and the surface divergence theorem on
\Gamma
imply that

\displaystyle d\operatorname{Im}\int_{\Omega}\overline{u}\Delta u\,dx-d\operatorname{Im}\int_{\Gamma}\overline{u}_{\Gamma}\partial_{\nu}u\,d\sigma+\gamma\operatorname{Im}\int_{\Gamma}\overline{u}_{\Gamma}\Delta_{\Gamma}u_{\Gamma}\,d\sigma=0,

(A.10)

where we have used the side condition
u=u_{\Gamma}
on
\Sigma
. On the other hand,

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\operatorname{Re}\int_{\Omega}\overline{u}(\vec{\rho}\cdot\nabla u)\,dx+\operatorname{Re}\int_{\Gamma}\overline{u}_{\Gamma}(\vec{\rho}_{\Gamma}\cdot\nabla_{\Gamma}u_{\Gamma})\,d\sigma\\
=&-\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}\mathrm{div}(\vec{\rho})|u|^{2}\,dx-\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Gamma}\mathrm{div}_{\Gamma}(\vec{\rho}_{\Gamma})|u_{\Gamma}|^{2}\,d\sigma+\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Gamma}(\vec{\rho}\cdot\nu)|u|^{2}d\sigma.\end{split}

(A.11)

Substituting (
A.9
)-(
A.11
) into (
A.8
) and applying the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality we deduce

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\|(u(\cdot,t),u_{\Gamma}(\cdot,t))\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}\leq C\|(u(\cdot,t),u_{\Gamma}(\cdot,t))\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}+C\|(u(\cdot,t),u_{\Gamma}(\cdot,t))\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}\|(g(\cdot,t),g_{\Gamma}(\cdot,t))\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}.

Then, applying Gronwall’s and Hölder’s inequalities, we finally deduce the inequality (
A.7
).
∎

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{Im}\iint_{Q}\overline{u}\nabla u\cdot\partial_{t}\vec{q}\,dx\,dt-\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{Im}\int_{\Omega}\overline{u}\nabla u\cdot\vec{q}\,\bigg|_{0}^{T}\,dx

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{Im}\iint_{\Sigma}(\nabla_{\Gamma}u_{\Gamma}\cdot\partial_{t}\vec{q})\overline{u}_{\Gamma}\,d\sigma\,dt-\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{Im}\iint_{\Sigma}(\vec{q}\cdot\nabla_{\Gamma}u_{\Gamma})\overline{u}_{\Gamma}\,\bigg|_{0}^{T}d\sigma

\displaystyle+\frac{d}{2}\operatorname{Re}\iint_{Q}\overline{u}\nabla(\mathrm{div}(\vec{q}))\cdot\nabla u\,dx\,dt+\dfrac{1}{2}\operatorname{Re}\iint_{\Sigma}\partial_{\nu}u\vec{q}\cdot\nabla_{\Gamma}u_{\Gamma}\,d\sigma\,dt

\displaystyle+\frac{\gamma}{2}\operatorname{Re}\iint_{\Sigma}\overline{u}_{\Gamma}\nabla_{\Gamma}(\mathrm{div}_{\Gamma}(\vec{q}))\cdot\nabla_{\Gamma}u_{\Gamma}\,d\sigma\,dt+\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{Im}\iint_{Q}\mathrm{div}(\vec{q})(\vec{\rho}\cdot\nabla u)\overline{u}\,dx\,dt

\displaystyle+\operatorname{Im}\iint_{\Sigma}\alpha_{\Gamma}(\vec{q}\cdot\nabla_{\Gamma}\overline{u}_{\Gamma})u_{\Gamma}d\sigma dt

and

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}:=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{Im}\iint_{\Sigma}(\vec{q}\cdot\nu)\overline{u}_{\Gamma}g_{\Gamma}d\sigma dt-\operatorname{Im}\iint_{Q}g\left(\vec{q}\cdot\nabla\overline{u}+\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{div}(\vec{q})\overline{u}\right)\,dx\,dt

\displaystyle-\operatorname{Im}\iint_{\Sigma}g_{\Gamma}\left(\vec{q}\cdot\nabla_{\Gamma}u_{\Gamma}+\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{div}_{\Gamma}(\vec{q})\overline{u}_{\Gamma}\right)\,d\sigma\,dt.

Proof.

The proof follows from multiplier techniques for the Schrödinger equation; see for instance
[
31
]
for Dirichlet boundary conditions (see also
[
39
]
for a concise review). However, as we deal with dynamic boundary conditions, we should handle several new boundary terms. For the sake of completeness, we sketch the main steps of the proof.

We argue by density. We multiply the first equation of (
A.6
) by
-(\vec{q}\cdot\nabla\overline{u}+\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{div}(\vec{q})\overline{u})
and integrate in
Q
. Similarly, we multiply the second equation by
-(\vec{q}\cdot\nabla_{\Gamma}\overline{u}_{\Gamma}+\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{div}_{\Gamma}(\vec{q})\overline{u}_{\Gamma})
and integrate the results on
\Sigma
. Next, we add up both identities and take the imaginary part. After that, we use integration by parts and the surface divergence theorem to deal with the terms involving
\Delta u
,
\Delta_{\Gamma}u_{\Gamma}
,
\partial_{t}u
and
\partial_{t}u_{\Gamma}
. The rest of the proof runs as in
[

]
.

Lemma A.6
.

Assume the same conditions of Proposition
A.4
. Then, the normal derivative
\partial_{\nu}u
associated with the solution of (
A.6
) belongs to
L^{2}(\Sigma)
. Moreover, there exists
C>0
which depends on
d,\gamma,\rho,\rho_{\Gamma},\alpha,\alpha_{\Gamma}
such that

\displaystyle\|\partial_{\nu}u\|_{L^{2}(\Sigma)}\leq C\left(\|(u_{0},u_{\Gamma,0})\|_{\mathbb{H}^{1}}+\|(g,g_{\Gamma})\|_{L^{1}(0,T;\mathbb{H}^{1})}\right).

Proof.

Let us choose
\vec{q}\in C^{2}(\overline{\Omega};\mathbb{R}^{N})
such that
q=\nu
on
\Gamma
. The existence of this vector field is guaranteed (see
[
30
]
). Then, by Proposition
A.5
, we see that

\displaystyle\frac{d}{2}\|\partial_{\nu}u\|_{L^{2}(\Sigma)}^{2}=\mathcal{X}+\mathcal{Y}+\mathcal{G}.

(A.19)

Now, since
(\rho,\rho_{\Gamma})
,
\vec{q}
and their derivatives are bounded, we easily deduce that

\displaystyle|\mathcal{X}|\leq

\displaystyle C\|(u,u_{\Gamma})\|^{2}_{C^{0}([0,T];\mathbb{H}^{1})}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C\left(\|(u_{0},u_{\Gamma,0})\|_{\mathbb{H}^{1}}^{2}+\|(g,g_{\Gamma})\|_{L^{1}(0,T;\mathbb{H}^{1})}^{2}\right),

(A.20)

where we have used Proposition
A.4
. On the other hand, by Young’s inequality, it is easy to see that
\mathcal{Y}
can be estimated in the following way:

\displaystyle|\mathcal{Y}|\leq C\|(u_{0},u_{\Gamma,0})\|_{\mathbb{H}^{1}}^{2}+C\|(g,g_{\Gamma})\|_{L^{1}(0,T;\mathbb{H}^{1})}^{2}.

(A.21)

In a similar way, by Cauchy-Scharwz inequality we can estimate
\mathcal{G}
as follows:

\displaystyle|\mathcal{G}|\leq C\|(u_{0},u_{\Gamma,0})\|_{\mathbb{H}^{1}}^{2}+C\|(g,g_{\Gamma})\|_{L^{1}(0,T;\mathbb{H}^{1})}^{2}.

(A.22)

Finally, substituting (
A.20
)-(
A.22
) into (
A.19
), we get our desired conclusion.
∎
