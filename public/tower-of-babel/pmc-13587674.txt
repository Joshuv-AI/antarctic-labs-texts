Chemistry of Evaporating Charged Alcohol Microdroplets in Humid Environments
Authors: Wen Zhang, Kedong Gong, Vicki H. Grassian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
There has been recent interest in the physics of evaporating alcohol droplets under humid conditions. These studies focus on delineating time scales for different physical processes, but what about the chemistry? Given the novel reactivity of aqueous microdroplets, we surmise that there will also be interesting chemistry. Here we focus on the oxidation chemistry of single evaporating charged alcohol microdroplets, e.g., 1-nonanol, and find the formation of the corresponding aldehyde, carboxylic acid, and ester. Oxidation is observed only under humidified conditions, suggesting that water is the oxidant. This hypothesis is supported by isotope experiments using H2 18O. Furthermore, oxidation is charge-dependent with no oxidation products observed for alcohol microdroplets with minimal charge. We therefore propose a mechanism involving charge accumulation at the interface during evaporation followed by water adsorption and activation to yield hydroxyl radicals that initiate oxidation. Our findings reveal a critical role for water and interfacial charge in driving oxidation at the alcohol microdroplet interface, with broader implications for microdroplet reactivity and interfacial chemistry of nonaqueous microdroplets.

Microdroplets are increasingly recognized as unique chemical reactors in which interfacial electric fields and charge may play an important role in redox reactions. − However, there has been debate as it relates to the formation of reactive oxygen species. ,− Recent studies have critically examined the production of ·OH, H2O2, and H3O+·, − including the origins of the m/z 36 peak, − possible electrical-discharge artifacts, the presence of dissolved oxygen, , and other gas-phase constituents. ,, These discussions highlight the need for complementary approaches while independently controlling different variables.

Our approach involves (i) utilizing spectroscopy as a probe of single microdroplet chemistry; (ii) integrating environmental cells to control gas-phase composition; − (iii) measuring and modeling the temporal evolution of size and composition; − and (iv) determining the dependence of microdroplet size on reaction kinetics for different reactions. − , Here we investigate the chemistry of single evaporating charged alcohol microdroplets under controlled gas-phase composition. Previous studies have shown interesting physics for alcohol microdroplets in humid environments, , and here we show interesting chemistry, i.e. alcohol oxidation during evaporation in humid environments but only for charged microdroplets of sufficient charge . Experiments with H2 18O provide evidence that water is the primary oxidant. In these experiments, the size and composition of individual microdroplets are monitored using an optical photothermal infrared (O-PTIR) spectrometer coupled to an environmental cell, both from Photothermal Spectroscopy Corp. (Scheme ). , We propose a mechanism whereby charged alcohol microdroplets undergo evaporative cooling, followed by water adsorption onto the microdroplet surface and then, as the microdroplet shrinks, there is an increase in interfacial charge density that creates favorable conditions for water activation leading to ·OH formation and subsequent alcohol oxidation.

This deviation is attributed to the condensation of water , and subsequent chemistry occurring within the microdroplet.

O-PTIR spectral analysis in the carbonyl stretching region (1650 to 1800 cm–1) of the residual microdroplet shows a broad peak following evaporation under humid conditions (Figure B). Reference spectra of several standards, 1-nonanol, 1-nonanal, 1-nonanoic acid and methyl nonanoate, a representative ester are shown in Figure C (Figure SI-2 shows larger spectral range). Using these standard spectra, the carbonyl region is curve fit (Figure B), to three products, nonanal, nonanoic acid and nonyl nonanoate, consistent with the products identified in mass spectral analysis (Figure SI-3). Experiments using H2 18O to humidify clearly show incorporation of O-18, ν­(C18O), into the product (Figure D and Figure SI-4). A summary of products is shown in Figure E. Similar product formation is observed for microdroplets prepared from 1-nonanol bulk liquids that have not been dried. Oxidation chemistry is also observed for other primary alcohols, C4 to C10, as demonstrated by d 2 versus t plots and O-PTIR spectra shown in Figures SI-5 and SI-6.

To further explore the oxidation of single 1-nonanol microdroplets in humid environments, the temporal evolution of the O-PTIR spectra is shown in Figure A. The spectra show very little intensity in the carbonyl region until 165 min, when microdroplets have decreased to d of ∼25 μm. After 165 min, there is the appearance of a broad peak in the CO stretching region. The time-dependence is shown in more detail in Figure B where the percentage of each product is determined from calibration and curve fitting of the spectra (see Section 2 and Figure SI-7 in SI). Although each product peak is distinct, the product distribution is subject to some uncertainty due to peak fitting. However, the overall trend remains unchanged, with nonanal initially being the most abundant product and nonyl nonanoate the largest at later times. Most interesting is the induction time where no detectable products are observed, a time period that depends on initial microdroplet size and charge (Figures SI-8 and SI-9).

To investigate the importance of microdroplet charge state in the observed oxidation, the charge of 1-nonanol microdroplets produced from the Collison-type atomizer (CA) was measured and compared to two other methods, a stainless-steel pump spray (SPS) and a plastic pump spray (PPS). An electrical low-pressure impactor, ELPI+ (Dekati), was used to measure the net charge state for microdroplets produced from these three different generation methods (see Section 1 in SI) with the largest microdroplets measured in the size range of ca. 6 to 10 μm.

The results of these measurements are shown in Figure A. All three generation methods produce microdroplets that carry a positive net charge; however, the magnitude is quite different. The CA produces microdroplets with a net positive charge approximately 1 order of magnitude greater than that produced by the SPS and nearly 2 orders of magnitude greater than the PPS.

The corresponding O-PTIR spectra for evaporating 1-nonanol droplets from these different microdroplet generation methods are shown in Figure B. The highest intensity observed in the CO region following evaporation is for microdroplets with the greatest charge. No products are seen in the O-PTIR spectra for microdroplets produced using a PPS, the generation method that produces microdroplets with minimal net charge. The SPS method produces microdroplets with a lower net charge compared to the CA method, resulting in the lower intensity observed in the spectra, indicative of fewer products and little ester formation. The impact on oxidation with an independent measurement of charge supports charge as a factor driving alcohol oxidation.

Microdroplets of 100 μm in diameter are larger in size than microdroplets produced from the output of the CA; therefore, these are produced through coalescence of multiple microdroplets. To calculate the charge, we assume that primary microdroplets on the order of 10 μm in diameter with a charge of ca. +45 e per microdroplet can coalesce during deposition on the substrate to form larger hemispherical droplets (see Section 3 in SI). We then calculate a cumulative charge, Q, corresponding to a 100 μm diameter droplet. The surface potential associated with the net charge was estimated using the classical spherical-droplet expression: 2 V=Q4πε0εr,air(d/2) where Q is the droplet net charge, d is the diameter of the microdroplet, and ε 0 is the permittivity of free space and ε r,air ≈ 1. This approach assumes a uniform distribution of net charge over the droplet surface and no interactions between droplets. Here, Q is estimated to be +2 × 104 e for the initial 100 μm droplets formed from the coalescence of 500 smaller microdroplets. Calculated surface potential curves as a function of microdroplet diameter at fixed charge Q are shown in Figure C. As the microdroplet evaporates and shrinks, the charge density and surface potential increase with decreasing diameter. The flat line is the calculated potential needed to activate adsorbed water through water autoionization followed by electron transfer, as discussed by Colussi. , Values used for water activation are for bulk water and may differ for water at the microdroplet interface. The ELPI+ measures the average net charge of a microdroplet population. This calculation then provides an estimate and is most useful for comparing the relative orders of magnitude difference in charged microdroplets produced by these methods.

Taken together, these experimental data suggest a mechanism for the evaporation of charged 1-nonanol microdroplets in humid environments that involves (i) evaporative cooling to form smaller microdroplets with increased charge density; (ii) adsorption of water on the charged microdroplet surface; and (iii) activation of water to yield reactive oxygen species that go on to oxidize 1-nonanol. Water activation can possibly occur through the electron transfer between OH– and H+, forming ·OH and ·H, as discussed by Colussi and Verlet. − However, water activation only occurs when a large enough surface potential is reached, as the microdroplet undergoes evaporation and shrinks to smaller size. This results in the delayed formation of ·OH and therefore alcohol oxidation. Although reduction products were not detected, H· and subsequent H2 formation is most likely as this has been recently observed in methanol oxidation in aqueous microdroplets. Schematic of the chemistry of evaporating charged alcohol microdroplets in humid environments. Upon evaporation, water adsorbs on the microdroplet surface, and, as the size decreases, the surface potential increases to activate water through a mechanism involving autoionization of water followed by electron transfer between OH– and H+ to form ·OH and ·H. , Hydroxyl radicals then react with the alcohol to initiate oxidation.

In summary, by controlling key parameters, including microdroplet charge and gas-phase composition, we can control the different knobs that are important in alcohol microdroplet chemistry. This approach provides insights into mechanisms that drive the chemistry of evaporating charged alcohol microdroplets in humid environments and should provide a path forward for future studies of other organic compounds that can be explored for interesting chemistry and the parameters that control reactivity. In the context of studies on aqueous microdroplets, we agree with the statement by LaCour et al., paraphrased here: “One possible area for overcoming disagreements is to provide exact conditions ...This can include...the exact size of the droplet, the gas-phase composition that they are brought into, the charge...of the droplets being measured as without this information, “unique” and “enhanced” reactivity may be difficult to reproduce in different laboratories.”
