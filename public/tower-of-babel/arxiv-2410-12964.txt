Abstract
. A covering of a digraph D D by Hamilton cycles is a collection of directed Hamilton cycles (not necessarily edge-disjoint) that together cover all the edges of D D . We prove that for 1 / 2 ≥ p ≥ log 20 ⁡ n n 1/2\geq p\geq\frac{\log^{20}n}{n} , the random digraph D n , p D_{n,p} typically admits an optimal Hamilton cycle covering. Specifically, the edges of D n , p D_{n,p} can be covered by a family of t t Hamilton cycles, where t t is the maximum of the the in-degree and out-degree of the vertices in D n , p D_{n,p} . Notably, t t is the best possible bound, and our assumption on p p is optimal up to a polylogarithmic factor.



1.
Introduction

A
Hamilton cycle
in a graph is a cycle that passes through all the vertices of the graph exactly once. A graph is
Hamiltonian
if it contains a Hamilton cycle. Hamiltonicity is one of the most central notions in graph theory, and has been extensively studied in the last decades (see the surveys
[
15
,
21
]
). In particular, the seemingly easy problem of deciding if a graph is Hamiltonian is known to be
\NP
-hard and is one of Karp’s list of 21
\NP
-hard problems
[
18
]
. Thus, there is a lot of interest in finding general sufficient conditions for a graph to be Hamiltonian. For instance, a classical result of Dirac
[
6
]
states that every graph on
n
vertices with minimum degree
n/2
contains a Hamilton cycle.

Once Hamiltonicity has been established, there are many natural further questions, like the problems of covering and packing a graph by Hamilton cycles. Given graphs
H
and
G
, an
H
-
packing
of
G
is a set of edge disjoint copies of
H
in
G
and an
H
-
covering
of
G
is a set of (not necessarily edge-disjoint) copies of
H
covering all the edges of
G
. An
H
-packing is
optimal
if it is of largest size, while an
H
-covering is called
optimal
if it is of smallest possible size.

The size of an optimal packing of Hamilton cycles in a graph
G
is clearly at most
\lfloor\delta(G)/2\rfloor
, where
\delta(G)
denotes the minimum degree of
G
. The problem of determining for which graphs this bound is actually tight is of great interest. The first result in this direction was by Walecki in 1890, who showed that the complete graph
K_{n}
can be decomposed into Hamilton cycles when
n
is odd, and into Hamilton cyles plus a matching when
n
is even (see
[
1
]
). The result was later generalized by Csaba, Kühn, Lo, Osthus, and Treglown
[
5
]
, who showed that one can replace
K_{n}
by any regular graph with degree at least
n/2
, settling a longstanding conjecture of Nash and Williams
[
25
]
. This is clearly optimal, since there exist
(n/2-1)
-regular graphs with no Hamilton cycles. Therefore, if we wish to consider sparser graphs we need a different condition than a minimum degree.

A natural family of graphs to consider is the family of binomial
random graphs
. Let
G_{n,p}
be the binomial random graph on
n
vertices with each edge chosen independently with probability
p
. We say that a graph
G:=G_{n,p}
satisfies a property
with high probability
(whp) if the probability that
G
satisfies the property tends to
1
as
n\rightarrow\infty
. The study of packing of Hamilton cycles in random graphs was initiated by Bollobás and Frieze
[
4
]
who proved that for every fixed integer
k
, whp
G_{n,p}
contains
\lfloor k/2\rfloor
edge-disjoint Hamilton cycles plus a disjoint perfect matching if
k
is odd. Recently, the problem of finding optimal Hamilton packings and covering of random graphs has received a great amount of attention, resulting in its complete solution in a series of papers by several authors
[
4
,
19
,
20
,
22
]
. More precisely, they showed that for any
p
, in
G:=G_{n,p}
whp there exists a packing of
G
with
\lfloor\delta(G)/2\rfloor
Hamilton cycles, therefore confirming a conjecture of Frieze and Krivelevich
[
12
]
.

The problem of covering the edges of a random graph with Hamilton cycles was first investigated by Glebov, Krivelevich and Szabó
[
14
]
. Note that an optimal Hamilton covering has size at least
\lceil\Delta(G)/2\rceil
. The authors showed that for
p\geq n^{-1+\varepsilon}
this bound is approximately tight. More precisely, they showed that in this range the edges of
G:=G_{n,p}
whp can be covered by
(1+o(1))\Delta(G)/2
Hamilton cycles and conjectured that
\lceil\Delta(G)/2\rceil
should be the correct size.
In 2014, Hefetz, Kühn, Laplinskas and Osthus confirmed this conjecture for
1-n^{1/8}\geq p\geq\log^{117}n/n

[
16
]
, and recently in an impressive work by Draganić, Glock, Correia, and Sudakov, the problem was almost entirely solved. They confirmed the conjecture for
1-n^{1/8}\geq p\geq C\log n/n
for sufficiently large
C
. Up to the constant
C
, this is optimal, since for lower
p
,
G_{n,p}
is disconnected.

Our main result concerns an extension of the previous problem to the directed setting. Recall that a
directed graph
(or a
digraph
for short) is a pair
D=(V,E)
with a set of vertices
V
and a set of ordered pairs
E\subseteq V^{(2)}
called
directed edges
of
D
. As a matter of convenience, we shall denote an edge
(x,y)\in E
by
\overrightarrow{xy}\in E
. A
Hamilton cycle
in a digraph is a cycle passing through all the vertices exactly once, where all the edges are oriented in the same direction in a cyclic order. Given a digraph
D
, we denote by
\delta^{+}(D)
and
\delta^{-}(D)
the minimum out-degree and in-degree, respectively. Similarly, one can define
\Delta^{+}(D)
and
\Delta^{-}(D)
the maximum out-degree and in-degree, respectively. Lastly, it would be convenient to define
\delta(D):=\min\{\delta^{+}(D),\delta^{-}(D)\}
and
\Delta(D):=\max\{\Delta^{+}(D),\Delta^{-}(D)\}.

Since the above mentioned results regarding the covering problem heavily rely on the so-called Pósa rotation-extension technique, and since this technique is not applicable in the directed settings, the study of Hamiltonicity problems for directed graphs is considered more difficult. Let
D_{n,p}
be the binomial random digraph on
n
vertices, where each directed edge is chosen independently with probability
p
. It is known that a typical
D:=D_{n,p}
is Hamiltonian assuming
p\geq\frac{\log n+\omega(1)}{n}
(see
[
13
,
24
]
). Therefore, it is reasonable to ask about the size of optimal packing and coverings of the random directed graph by Hamilton cycles. A simple argument shows that every Hamilton packing of a digraph
D
has size at most
\delta(D)
. Moreover, every Hamilton covering of a digraph
D
has size at least
\Delta(D)
. It was shown in
[
8
]
that for
p\geq\log^{C}n/n
, the digraph
D:=D_{n,p}
whp has a packing of size
(1-o(1))pn
of edge-disjoint Hamilton cycles. Moreover, it was also shown that for
p\geq\log^{C}n/n
, the digraph
D:=D_{n,p}
whp has a covering of size
(1+o(1))pn
by Hamilton cycles. Both results are asymptotic optimal, since in a random digraph the in-degree and out-degree concentrates around
p(n-1)
.

In our main result, we improve upon these bounds and show that for
p\geq\log^{C}n/n
, the trivial bound is indeed optimal for the covering problem in random digraphs.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
1/2\geq p\geq\frac{\log^{20}n}{n}
. Then a digraph
D:=D_{n,p}
whp can be covered by
\Delta(D)
directed Hamilton cycles.

The paper is organized as follows: In Section
2
, we give a brief overview of the problem and introduce the standard probabilistic and graph theoretic tools used throughout the paper. Section
3
is devoted to study the problem of finding coverings of the random bipartite graph by perfect matchings. We will use those coverings in Section
4
to show that the random digraph can be almost covered by linear forests. In Section
5
, we discuss how to cover linear forests with Hamilton cycles. Finally, a proof of Theorem
1.1
is given in Section
6
.

2.
Overview and preliminaries

2.1.
Proof overview

In this subsection, we will describe our proof strategy to obtain a covering of
D:=D_{n,p}
with
\Delta(D)
Hamilton cycles. The proof is split into three parts. Given
D
, let
x_{\star}
be the vertex of maximum degree in
D
and suppose without loss of generality that the maximum degree is an out-degree. In the first part of our proof, we are going to reserve a set of
deg^{+}(x_{\star})-t
out-edges from
x_{\star}
, where
t
is chosen such that
deg^{+}(x_{\star})-t
is roughly
\sqrt{pn}
. We call this set
E
. We will then proceed to prove that the edges of
D\setminus E
can be covered by a family of linear forests
\mathcal{F}=\{F_{1},\ldots,F_{t}\}
and by a sparse graph
R_{1}
with
\Delta(R_{1})=o\left(|E|\right)
. In the second part of the proof, we will show that we can cover the edges of the family
\mathcal{F}
by
t
Hamilton cycles
\mathcal{C}=\{C_{1},\ldots,C_{t}\}
and another sparse graph
R_{2}
with
\Delta(R_{2})=o\left(|E|\right)
. Finally, in the last part we will show that one can cover
R:=R_{1}\cup R_{2}
and
E
by using
|E|
Hamilton cycles, each of which contains exactly one edge from
E
. This will give us a covering of
D
by
(\deg^{+}(x_{\star})-t)+t=\Delta(D)
Hamilton cycles, as desired.

The main idea for the first part is to consider an alternative random model for
D_{n,p}
using the random bipartite graph model, referred to as
B_{n,p}
, and a random permutation (see
Definition

4.2
). This reduces the problem of finding linear forests in
D_{n,p}
to finding almost perfect matchings in
B_{n,p}
. In Section
3
, we discuss how to obtain a covering of
B_{n,p}
by perfect matchings, while in Section
4
we construct our linear forests from such matchings. The main tool for the second and third part of the proof is a result from
[
9
]
which states that certain pseudorandom digraphs are Hamiltonian. In Section
5
, we will show how each of the linear forests we obtained can be extended into a Hamilton cycle by constructing an auxiliary pseudorandom digraph where we “contract” the paths of our linear forest into single vertices (see
Definition

5.3
).

This paper investigates asymptotic results, so every calculation we show regarding graphs with
n
vertices will be under the assumption that
n
is sufficiently large.
We now introduce some preliminary results for our proof.

2.2.
Chernoff’s bounds

We extensively use the following well-known Chernoff bounds for the upper and lower tails of binomial random variables (see
[
17
]
).

Theorem 2.1

(Chernoff Bound)
.

Suppose
X_{1},\ldots X_{n}
are independent
0/1
-random variables. Let
X=\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}
and let
\mathbb{E}(X)=\mu
. Then, for
\delta\in(0,1)
, we have

\displaystyle\mathds{P}(|X-\mu|>\delta\mu)\leq 2e^{-\delta^{2}\mu/3}

For larger deviations, we state another useful version that can be also found in
[
17
]
.

Theorem 2.2

(
[
17
]
, Corollary 2.4)
.

Suppose
X_{1},\ldots X_{n}
are independent
0/1
-random variables. Let
X=\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}
and let
a\geq 7\mathbb{E}(X)
be a real number. Then,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(X>a)\leq e^{-a}.

2.3.
Graph theoretical results

Here, we compile a list of graph theoretical results related to matchings. Given a multigraph
G
, a
proper edge coloring
is a coloring of the edges of
G
such that no adjacent edge has the same color. In other words, a proper coloring is a partition of
G
such that each color class is a matching. A multigraph
G
is said to have multiplicity
\mu:=\mu(G)
if
\mu
is the largest integer such that there exists two vertices of
G
with
\mu
edges connecting them. The next result is a generalization of the celebrated Vizing’s theorem.

Theorem 2.3

(
[
2
]
)
.

Let
G
be an undirected multigraph without self-loops. Then
G
can be properly edge colored with at most
\Delta(G)+\mu(G)
colors.

The next series of results are about finding matching factors in bipartite graphs. We start with Hall’s famous marriage theorem (see e.g. Theorem 3.1.11 in
[
27
]
).

Theorem 2.4

(Hall’s Theorem)
.

Let
B
be a bipartite graph with parts
X
and
Y
of the same size. There is a perfect matching in
B
if and only if for every subset
W\subseteq X
, we have that
|N(W)|\geq|W|
.

It follows from
Theorem

2.4
that any regular bipartite graph contains a perfect matching , and when we remove a perfect matching from a regular graph, the resulting graph is also regular. Thus, by repeatedly applying
Theorem

2.4
to find perfect matchings, we obtain:

Corollary 2.5
.

The edges of a
d
-regular bipartite graph can be decomposed into
d
edge-disjoint perfect matchings.

We will also need the following lemma due to Gale and Ryser (for instance, see
[
23
]
).

Lemma 2.6
.

Let
B
be a bipartite graphs with parts
X
and
Y
of size
n
. Then
B
contains an
r
-regular subgraph if and only if for all
S\subseteq X
,

,

e(S,T)>r(|S|+|T|-n)

Let

be any graph and let

. We say that

contains an

factor
if and only if there exists a spanning subgraph

such that for every

we have
\deg_{H}(v)=f(v)
. The following lemma due to Ore extends

and gives a characterization of

Lemma 2.7

(

)
.

Let

be a bipartite graph. Let

be such that

(1)

\displaystyle e(S,T)+\sum_{y\in Y\setminus T}f(y)\geq\sum_{x\in S}f(x)

2.4.
Properties of Random Graphs

We now introduce properties of typical random graphs that will be used in the proof of our main result. From now on, when we say that
B
is a balanced bipartite graph, we name the parts
X
and
Y
, two disjoint copies of
[n]
. The edge
xy
refers to the edge between the
x
th vertex in part
X
, and
y
th vertex in part
Y
. Even though the graph is undirected, in our convention,
xy\neq yx
. For convenience, we make the following technical definition:

Definition 2.8
.

Let
\varepsilon>0
and
p\in(0,1)
. We say that a balanced bipartite graph
B
is
\left(\varepsilon,p\right)
-good if

e(S,T)\in\left(1\pm\varepsilon\right)stp

for all

and
T\subseteq Y
with

and
|T|=t
such that

(a)

s,t\geq\frac{n}{\log^{2/3}n}
, or

(b)

s\leq\frac{n}{\log^{2/3}n}
and

s\sqrt{\log n}+t\geq n
, or

(c)

t\leq\frac{n}{\log^{2/3}n}
and

t\sqrt{\log n}+s\geq n
.

For a graph
B
, let
\Delta_{2}(B)
be the second largest degree of a vertex in

Lemma 2.9
.

For
1/2\geq p\geq\frac{\log^{10}n}{n}
, a random bipartite graph

whp satisfies the following properties:

(B1)

B
is
\left(\frac{1}{\log^{2}n},p\right)
-good.

(B2)

\Delta(B)-\delta(B)<4\sqrt{np\log n}
, and
\Delta(B)=(1+o(1))np

(B3)

For all vertices

\Delta_{2}(B)-\deg(v)\geq\frac{\sqrt{np}}{\log n}.

Proof.

(B2) is the bipartite version of Theorem 3.5 in
[
11
]
, and follows from the same proof.

(B3) is a weaker bipartite version of Theorem 3.15 in
[
3
]
proved by Bollobás (it can be seen by taking

to be a slowly increasing function). The same proof works for the bipartite case.

It is thus remains to prove (B1). We prove each case in Definition
2.8
separately:

(a)

s,t\geq n/\log^{2/3}n
. It follows from Chernoff’s bounds and the union bound that the probability that there exist
S
and
T
of sizes
s
and
t
, respectively, with
e(S,T)\notin\left(1\pm\delta\right)stp
with
\delta=\frac{1}{\log^{2}n}

is at most

\displaystyle\sum_{s,t\geq n/\log^{2/3}n}\binom{n}{s}\binom{n}{t}2\exp\left(-\delta^{2}stp/3\right)

\displaystyle\leq 2\cdot 4^{n}\exp\left(-\Theta(\delta^{2}n^{2}p/\log^{4/3}n)\right)

\displaystyle\leq 2\cdot 4^{n}\exp\left(-\Theta(\delta^{2}n\log^{5}n)\right)

\displaystyle=o(1).

where the second from the last inequality holds since
np/\log^{4/3}n\geq\log^{5}n
.

(b)

s\leq\frac{n}{\log^{2/3}n}
and

s\sqrt{\log n}+t+2\geq n
. It follows from Chernoff’s bounds (
Theorem

2.1
) and the union bound that the probability there exist
S
and
T
with sizes
s
and
t
, respectively, such that the number of edges between them satisfies
e(S,T)\notin\left(1\pm\delta\right)stp
, where
\delta=\frac{1}{\log^{2}n}
,
is at most

\displaystyle\sum_{s=1}^{n/\log^{2/3}n}\sum_{t=n-2-s\sqrt{\log n}}^{n}\binom{n}{s}\binom{n}{t}2\exp\left(-\delta^{2}stp/3\right)

\displaystyle\leq 2n\sum_{s=1}^{n/\log^{2/3}n}{n\choose s}\binom{n}{s\sqrt{\log n}+2}\exp\left(-\delta^{2}s(n-s\sqrt{\log n}-2)/3\right)

\displaystyle\leq 2n\sum_{s=1}^{n/\log^{2/3}n}n^{s+s\sqrt{\log n}+2}\exp\left(-\delta^{2}sn/4\right)

\displaystyle=o(1)

(c)

Similar to the proof of (b).

This completes the proof.

We will also need the following properties of
D_{n,p}

Lemma 2.10
.

For
p\geq\frac{\log^{10}n}{n}
, the digraph
D:=D_{n,p}
whp satisfies the following properties:

(D1)

Every vertex

\displaystyle(1-o(1))np\leq deg_{D}^{+}(v),\deg_{D}^{-}(v)\leq(1+o(1))np.

(D2)

Every set

(D3)

(D1)
is the directed version of Theorem 3.5 in
[
11
]
, and follows from the same proof.
First, we prove
(D2)
. Observe that for any set

e_{D}(X)\sim\mathrm{Bin}(|X|^{2},p)
, the probability that
e_{D}(X)\geq 12|X|\log^{2}n
is upper bounded by

\displaystyle{|X|^{2}\choose 12|X|\log^{2}n}p^{12|X|\log^{2}n}

\displaystyle\leq\left(\frac{e|X|p}{12\log^{2}n}\right)^{12|X|\log^{2}n}

\displaystyle<2^{-12|X|\log^{2}n}.

Now, since there are at most

{n\choose|X|}\leq n^{|X|}=2^{|X|\log n}

Now we prove

3.
Covering
B_{n,p}
with perfect matchings

In this section, we prove the following key lemma:

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
B:=B_{n,p}
with
1/2\geq p\geq\frac{\log^{10}n}{n}
. Then, whp, for all subgraphs
B^{\prime}
of
B
obtained by deleting
\Delta(B)-\Delta_{2}(B)
edges from the vertex of largest degree, there exist two families of perfect matchings of
B^{\prime}
,
\mathcal{M}_{1}
and
\mathcal{M}_{2}
, for which the following holds:

(1)

For
i\in\{1,2\}
and for every
M\neq M^{\prime}\in\mathcal{M}_{i}
, we have
M\cap M^{\prime}=\emptyset
, and

(2)

\bigcup_{M\in\mathcal{M}_{1}\cup\mathcal{M}_{2}}M=E(B^{\prime})
, and

(3)

.

Before proving Lemma
3.1

2.7
when the bipartite graph

\left(\frac{1}{\log n},p\right)

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
1/2\geq p\geq\frac{\log^{10}n}{n}

B

\left(\frac{1}{\log n},p\right)
-good bipartite graph. Let
M\leq\frac{np}{2\log^{4/3}n}
be an integer and

(i)

f(v)\leq M
holds for all

.

(ii)

f(v)\geq M/\sqrt{\log n}

Then, the graph
B
contains an

-factor.

Proof.

By
Lemma

2.7
, the graph
B
contains an

-factor if and only if equation (
1
) is satisfied. Let
S\subseteq X
and
T\subseteq Y
.
Lets first assume that
|S|,|T|\geq\frac{n}{\log^{2/3}n}
. Observe that because

\left(\frac{1}{\log^{2}n},p\right)

is bounded above by

\displaystyle e(S,T)+\sum_{y\in Y\setminus T}f(y)\geq e(S,T)\geq(1+o(1))\frac{n^{2}p}{\log^{4/3}n}\geq nM\geq\sum_{x\in S}f(x).

Therefore, we may assume that either

Without loss of generality we assume that
|S|<\frac{n}{\log^{2/3}n}

(otherwise equation (

\displaystyle\frac{M}{\sqrt{\log n}}|Y\setminus T|\leq\sum_{y\in Y\setminus T}f(y)<\sum_{x\in S}f(x)\leq M|S|.

Thus, we obtain that
|Y\setminus T|<|S|\sqrt{\log n}

|S|\sqrt{\log n}+|T|\geq n
. Hence, by part (b) of

2.8
we have that

\displaystyle e(S,T)+\sum_{y\in Y\setminus T}f(y)\geq e(S,T)\geq(1+o(1))p|S||T|>\frac{2np}{3}|S|\geq M|S|\geq\sum_{x\in S}f(x)

Thus, equation (

∎

Also before proving

Lemma 3.3
.

Let

and

be a random bipartite graph. The following holds with high probability: For all subsets of edges

Proof.

Let

with parts

(2)

\displaystyle\max\{e(S,T)-|E|,0\}>r(|S|+|T|-n).

If

4.
Finding an almost covering of
D_{n,p}
by linear forests

In this section our goal is to prove that the random digraph
D_{n,p}
can be typically decomposed into a family of
linear forests
and a “sparse” graph. A digraph
F
is said to be a
linear forest
if
F
can be partitioned into disjoint directed paths
F=\bigcup_{i=1}^{\ell}P_{i}
, where we also consider isolated vertices as paths. We say that
F
has
\ell
connected components if
F
is the union of
\ell
disjoint directed paths. For a digraph
D
on
n
vertices, let
\Delta_{1}(D)\geq\Delta_{2}(D)\geq\ldots\geq\Delta_{2n}(D)
be its ordered degree sequence of in-degrees and out-degrees.
For simplicity, from now on, we will assume that the maximum degree vertex of the directed graph
D_{n,p}
is an out-degree. The main theorem will still hold if the maximum degree vertex is an in-degree, since by flipping the orientation of each edge, we obtain a graph whose maximum degree vertex is an out-degree, and any Hamilton cycle on the “flipped” graph is a Hamilton cycle on the original graph. The main result of this section can be precisely stated as follows.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
D:=D_{n,p}
with
1/2\geq p\geq\frac{\log^{10}n}{n}
. Then, with high probability the following holds: Let
x_{\star}\in V(D)
be a vertex of largest degree (so in particular we have
\Delta_{1}(D)=\deg_{D}^{+}(x_{\star})
).Then
D
can be covered by a family
\mathcal{F}=\{F_{1},\ldots,F_{t}\}
of linear forests for
\Delta_{2}(D)-3\leq t\leq\Delta_{2}(D)
, a digraph
R
and a set of edges
E=D\setminus\left(R\cup\left(\bigcup_{i=1}^{t}F_{t}\right)\right)
satisfying

(i)

For every
1\leq i\leq t
, there exists vertex
y_{i}\in V(D)
such that
\overrightarrow{x_{\star}y_{i}}\in F_{i}

(ii)

For every
1\leq i\leq t
, the forest
F_{i}
has at most
4\log n
components.

(iii)

The edge set
E
consists of
\Delta_{1}(D)-t
out-edges from
x_{\star}

(iv)

The digraph
R
satisfies
\Delta(R)=O\left((np\log^{4}n)^{1/3}\right)
.

To prove
Lemma

4.1
we are going to consider a different random model that has the same distribution as
D_{n,p}
. In this model we construct a random digraph using a random permutation and a random bipartite graph as described below. Recall that in a bipartite graph, the edge
ij
refers to
i
in part
X
and
j
in part
Y
, so
ij\neq ji
.

Definition 4.2
.

Given a balanced bipartite graph
B=(X\cup Y,E)
with
|X|=n
, and a permutation
\pi\in S_{n}
, we define the digraph
D_{\pi}(B)
as the digraph with vertex set
[n]
and edge set given by

\displaystyle E(D_{\pi}(B))=\{\overrightarrow{ij}\in[n]^{2}:\>i\pi^{-1}(j)\in B\text{ and }i\neq j\}.

In other words, the digraph
D_{\pi}(B)
is the loopless digraph obtained by shuffling the vertices of the part
Y
by the permutation
\pi
and associating every edge of the new relabeled bipartite graph with a directed edge. The first observation is that the random model for directed graph
D_{\pi}(B_{n,p})
obtained by choosing a random bipartite graph in
B_{n,p}
and then independently choosing permutation uniformly at random in
S_{n}
has same distribution as
D_{n,p}
.

Fact 4.3
.

Let
\pi\in S_{n}
be a permutation chosen uniformly at random. Then
D_{\pi}(B_{n,p})
has the same probability distribution as
D_{n,p}
.

The main observation is that for a matching
M
, the digraph
D_{\pi}(M)
is a
1
-factor, i.e. a disjoint union of directed cycles and isolated vertices. By removing one edge from each directed cycle, one can obtain a linear forest. This makes a natural connection between families of matchings in
B
and families of linear forests in
D_{\pi}(B)
. Before we prove
Lemma

4.1
we will state some facts about cycles in random permutations. Given a permutation
\pi\in S_{n}
, let
C(\pi)
be the number of cycles in
\pi
. The next result allows us to show that typically a random permutation does not have too many cycles.

Theorem 4.4

(
[
10
]
, Theorem 1.24)
.

Let
\pi\in S_{n}
be a permutation chosen uniformly at random, then
\mathbb{E}(2^{C(\pi)})=n+1
.

Since each directed cycle in
D_{\pi}(M)
corresponds to a cycle in the permutation
\pi
, we can use the last theorem to bound the number of connected components in
D_{\pi}(M)
.

Corollary 4.5
.

Let
M
be a perfect matching and
\pi\in S_{n}
be a permutation chosen uniformly at random. Then with probabiliy
1-1/n^{3}
, the
1
-factor
D_{\pi}(M)
has at most
4\log n
components.

Proof.

Let
m\in S_{n}
be the permutation where
m(i)=j
when
ij\in M
. The number of components in
D_{\pi}(M)
is equal to the number of cycles in the permutation
\pi(m)
. Since
\pi
is chosen uniformly at random, regardless of
m
,
\pi(m)
is distributed uniformly. We may now apply
Theorem

4.4
to obtain
\mathbb{E}(2^{C(\pi(m))})=n+1
. By Markov’s inequality,

\mathds{P}\left(2^{C\left(\pi(m)\right)}\geq(n+1)^{4}\right)\leq\frac{1}{(n+1)^{3}},

implying:

\mathds{P}\left(C\left(\pi(m)\right)\geq 4\log(n+1)\right)\leq\frac{1}{(n+1)^{3}}.

∎

As mentioned before, our strategy to obtain linear forests will be to delete some edges of the
1
-factors obtained in
D_{\pi}(B)
. The edges are going to be deleted at random satisfying certain properties. Using the following result from
[
7
]
we can bound the degrees of the graph obtained by removing these edges.

Lemma 4.6
.

Let
\mathcal{M}=\{M_{1},\ldots,M_{r}\}
be a family of
r
disjoint matchings. For each
w\in[n]
and
1\leq i\leq r
, let
c_{i,w}
be the length of the cycle containing
w
in
D_{\pi}(M_{i})
. If
w
is isolated in
D_{\pi(M_{i})}
, define
c_{w,i}=1
. Then whp, for every vertex
w\in[n]
it holds that

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{r}\frac{1}{c_{i,w}}=O\left((r\log^{4}n)^{1/3}\right)

The proof of
Lemma

4.6
is based on the following technical claim that we postpone to the appendix (the proof is taken from
[
7
]
but since this paper has not been submitted yet, we include its proof for the convenience of the reader).

Proposition 4.7
.

For every
w\in[n]
, it holds that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left(\left(\sum_{i=1}^{r}\frac{1}{c_{i,w}}\right)^{3}\right)=O\left(\frac{r}{n}\log^{3}n\right).

The proof of the lemma now follows from a third moment argument.

Proof of
Lemma

4.6
.

Given any
w\in[n]
, by Markov’s inequality and
Proposition

4.7
, we have:

\mathds{P}\left(\left(\sum_{i=1}^{r}\frac{1}{c_{i,w}}\right)^{3}\geq O\left(r\log^{4}n\right)\right)\leq\frac{1}{n\log n}.

Therefore,

\mathds{P}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{r}\frac{1}{c_{i,w}}\geq O\left((r\log^{4}n)^{1/3}\right)\right)\leq\frac{1}{n\log n}.

The result now follows by a union bound.
∎

Before proving Lemma
4.1
, we need the following observation that in a typical
D:=D_{n,p}
there is a gap between
\Delta_{1}(D)
and
\Delta_{2}(D)
.

Proposition 4.8
.

Let
D:=D_{n,p}
with
1/2\geq p\geq\frac{\log^{10}n}{n}
. Then whp it holds that

\displaystyle\Delta_{1}(D)-\Delta_{2}(D)\geq\frac{\sqrt{np}}{2\log n}.

Proof.

By Fact
4.3
it suffices to show the proposition holds whp for
D_{\pi}(B_{n,p})
. We prove this by first exposing
B:=B_{n,p}
and then later exposing
\pi\in S_{n}
. Let
B:=B_{n,p}
, so with high probability, it has the properties of
Lemma

2.9
. Let
x_{1}
and
x_{2}
be the vertices of largest and second largest degree in
B
, respectively. Assume without loss of generality that both
x_{1}
,
x_{2}\in X
, i.e., both vertices are in the first partition of
B
. By property (B3) of
Lemma

2.9
we have that

\displaystyle\deg_{B}(x_{1})-\deg_{B}(x_{2})\geq\frac{\sqrt{np}}{\log n}.

Now reveal
\pi\in S_{n}
uniformly at random and consider
D:=D_{\pi}(B)
. By the construction of Definition
4.2
, we have that
\deg_{B}(x_{1})-1\leq\deg^{+}_{D}(x_{1})\leq\deg_{B}(x_{1})
and
\deg_{B}(x_{2})-1\leq\deg^{+}_{D}(x_{2})\leq\deg_{B}(x_{2})
. Thus,

\displaystyle\Delta_{1}(D)-\Delta_{2}(D)=\deg_{D}^{+}(x_{1})-\deg_{D}^{+}(x_{2})\geq\frac{\sqrt{np}}{\log n}-1\geq\frac{\sqrt{np}}{2\log n}.

Since the last bound holds for any
\pi\in S_{n}
, the result now follows.
∎

We are now ready to prove
Lemma

4.1
.

Proof of
Lemma

4.1
.

By Fact
4.3
it suffices to show that the result holds whp for
D_{\pi}(B_{n,p})
. As before, we will first expose
B:=B_{n,p}
and then later
\pi\in S_{n}
. Let
B:=B_{n,p}
, and observe that
B
simultaneously satisfies
Lemma

2.9
and
Lemma

3.1
with high probability. Let
x_{\star}
be the vertex of largest degree in
B
and assume without loss of generality that
x_{\star}\in X
. By property (B3) of
Lemma

2.9
, we have that

\displaystyle d:=\Delta(B)-\Delta_{2}(B)\geq\frac{\sqrt{np}}{\log n}.

Let
E^{\prime}\subseteq B
be an arbitrary subset of
d
edges in
B
containing
x_{\star}
. Set
B^{\prime}=B\setminus E^{\prime}
. Then by
Lemma

3.1
, there exists two families of perfect matchings
\mathcal{M}_{1}
and
\mathcal{M}_{2}
satisfying

(a)

For
j\in\{1,2\}
and for every
M\neq M^{\prime}\in\mathcal{M}_{j}
, we have
M\cap M^{\prime}=\emptyset
,

(b)

B^{\prime}=\bigcup_{M\in\mathcal{M}_{1}\cup\mathcal{M}_{2}}M
,

(c)

\Delta(B^{\prime})=\Delta_{2}(B)=|\mathcal{M}_{1}|+|\mathcal{M}_{2}|
.

We now reveal
\pi\in S_{n}
. Since
|\mathcal{M}_{1}|+|\mathcal{M}_{2}|=\Delta(B^{\prime})\leq n
, by a simple union bound we obtain that whp
\pi\in S_{n}
simultaneously satisfies
Corollary

4.5
for every
M\in\mathcal{M}_{1}\cup\mathcal{M}_{2}
and
Lemma

4.6
for both
\mathcal{M}_{1}
and
\mathcal{M}_{2}
. Take such an instance of
\pi\in S_{n}
. Let
D:=D_{\pi}(B)
, and observe by
Fact

4.3
that
D
is distributed identically to
D_{n,p}
.

For each
j\in\{1,2\}
, let
r_{j}=|\mathcal{M}_{j}|
and
\mathcal{M}_{j}=\{M_{1},\ldots,M_{r_{j}}\}
be the family of perfect matchings. Consider the
1
-factors
D_{\pi}(M_{1}),\ldots,D_{\pi}(M_{r_{j}})
. For each
j
, since
\mathcal{M}_{j}
is a disjoint family of matchings and
\deg_{D_{\pi}(B)^{\prime}}^{+}(x_{\star})\in\{\delta(B^{\prime}),\delta(B^{\prime})-1\}
, there exists at most one
D_{\pi}(M_{j})
such that
x_{\star}
is an isolated vertex. If such a matching exists, remove it from the family. After reordering we obtain a family
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}_{j}=\{M_{1},\ldots,M_{t_{j}}\}
where
r_{j}-1\leq t_{j}\leq r_{j}
and for
1\leq i\leq t_{j}
, there exists
y_{i}\in V(D)
with
\overrightarrow{x_{\star}y_{i}}\in D_{\pi}(M_{i})
.

We will now construct a graph
R_{j}
and a family of linear forest
\mathcal{F}_{j}=\{F_{1},\ldots,F_{t_{j}}\}
satisfying the following properties:

(F1)

For
1\leq i\leq t_{j}
, the edge
\overrightarrow{x_{\star}y_{i}}\in F_{i}
.

(F2)

For
1\leq i\leq t_{j}
, the forest
F_{i}
has at most
4\log n
components.

(F3)

D_{\pi}(\mathcal{M}_{j})=R_{j}\cup\left(\bigcup_{i=1}^{t_{j}}F_{i}\right)

(F4)

\Delta(R_{j})\leq O\left((t_{j}\log^{4}n)^{1/3}\right)
.

For each
1\leq i\leq t_{j}
and cycle

, select an edge from

independently and uniformly at random and add it to

. Set

. Clearly,

is a linear forest and
\overrightarrow{x_{\star}y_{j}}\in F_{i}
for

. This satisfies property (F1). Property (F2) follows from
Corollary

4.5
, since the number of components in

and

is the same. Property (F3) is immediate from the construction.

Finally, we will show that property (F4) holds. Let
w\in V(D)
and let
c_{i,w}
be the length of the cycle containing
w\in D_{\pi}(M_{i})
(If
w
is an isolated vertex, then set
c_{i,w}=1
). Then the degree of
w
in
R_{j}
can be written as
\deg^{+}_{R_{j}}(w)=\sum_{i=1}^{t_{j}}X_{i}
, where
X_{i}
are independent
0/1
-random variables given by
X_{i}=\deg^{+}_{R_{j}\cap D_{\pi}(M_{i})}(w)
. That is,
X_{i}
counts if the out-edge from
w
in the
1
-factor
D_{\pi}(M_{i})
was selected to
R_{j}
. One can observe that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(X_{i})=\begin{cases}0,&\quad\text{if $w=x_{\star}$ or $w$ is an isolated vertex in $D_{\pi}(M_{i})$}\\
\frac{1}{c_{i,w}-1},&\quad\text{if $w$ and $x_{\star}$ belongs to the same cycle in $D_{\pi}(M_{i})$}\\
\frac{1}{c_{i,w}},&\quad\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

and consequently
\mathbb{E}(X_{i})\leq 2/c_{i,w}
for
1\leq i\leq t_{j}
and
w\in V(D)
. By
Lemma

4.6
, the expected value of
\deg^{+}_{R_{j}}(w)
can be bounded by

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(\deg^{+}_{R_{j}}(w))\leq\sum_{i=1}^{t_{j}}\frac{2}{c_{i,w}}\leq O\left((t_{j}\log^{4}n)^{1/3}\right).

Thus, by
Theorem

2.2
, we have that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\deg^{+}_{R_{j}}(w)>O\left((t_{j}\log^{4}n)^{1/3}\right)\right)\leq\exp\left(-O\left((t_{j}\log^{4}n)^{1/3}\right)\right)=o\left(\frac{1}{n}\right).

Hence, by a union bound we obtain that whp
\Delta^{+}(R_{j})\leq O\left((t_{j}\log^{4}n)^{1/3}\right)
. Similarly, whp we have that
\Delta^{-}(R_{j})\leq O\left((t_{j}\log^{4}n)^{1/3}\right)
. Therefore, there exists a choice of

such that (F4) holds.

To finish the proof, take
R:=R_{1}\cup R_{2}\cup D_{\pi}\left(\left(\mathcal{M}_{1}\setminus\mathcal{M}^{\prime}_{1}\right)\cup\left(\mathcal{M}_{2}\setminus\mathcal{M}^{\prime}_{2}\right)\right)
,

and

. Note that

, since there might be an edge in

between
x_{\star}
and
\pi(x_{\star})
, and

has no self loops.
Therefore, since

and
r_{j}-1\leq t_{j}\leq r_{j}
for

, we have that

\displaystyle\Delta_{2}(D)-3\leq\Delta_{2}(B)-2\leq t=|\mathcal{F}|=t_{1}+t_{2}=\Delta_{2}(B)\leq\Delta_{2}(D).

Property (i) and (ii) follows immediately from (F1) and (F2), respectively. By construction,

consists of out-edges from

and (F3) gives us

\displaystyle E=D_{\pi}(E^{\prime})=D_{\pi}(B\setminus B^{\prime})=D_{\pi}(B)\setminus D_{\pi}(B^{\prime})=D\setminus\left(R\cup\left(\bigcup_{i=1}^{t}F_{t}\right)\right).

Hence, by the fact that
\deg^{+}_{R}(x_{\star})=0
, we have property (iii), i.e.,
|E|=\Delta_{1}(D)-t
. Property (iv) follows from (F4), since

\displaystyle\Delta(R)\leq O\left((t_{1}\log^{4}n)^{1/3}+(t_{2}\log^{4}n)^{1/3}\right)+2\leq O\left((t\log^{4}n)^{1/3}\right).

This concludes the proof.
∎

5.
Covering linear forests by Hamilton cycles

In this section we prove some lemmas that will enable us to cover linear forests by Hamilton cycles. Our main technical tool in this section is the main result of
[
9
]
that asserts that appropriate pseudorandom digraphs are Hamiltonian. We start with a definition.

Definition 5.1
.

A directed graph
H
on
n
vertices is
(n,\alpha,p)
-pseudorandom
if the following properties hold:

(P1)

For every
v\in V(H)
, we have

\displaystyle\min\{\deg^{+}_{H}(v),\deg^{-}_{H}(v)\}\geq(1/2+2\alpha)np

(P2)

For every
X\subseteq V(H)
of size
|X|\leq\frac{\log^{2}n}{p}
, we have

\displaystyle e_{H}(X)\leq|X|\log^{2.1}n

(P3)

For every two disjoint
X,Y\subseteq V(H)
with
|X|,|Y|\geq\frac{\log^{1.1}n}{p}
, we have

\displaystyle e_{H}(X,Y)\leq(1+\alpha/2)p|X||Y|

In other words, an
(n,\alpha,p)
-pseudorandom digraph is a graph with a certain minimum degree and “well-distributed” edges. In
[
9
]
, the authors proved that these properties are sufficient for containing a directed Hamilton cycle.

Theorem 5.2

(
[
9
]
, Theorem 3.2)
.

Let
\alpha>0
and
1/2\geq p\geq\frac{\log^{9}{n}}{n}
. Then for sufficiently large
n
, every
(n,\alpha,p)
-pseudorandom directed graph is Hamiltonian.

Now, in order to cover a directed linear forest by a Hamilton cycle, we construct an auxiliary digraph such that each Hamilton cycle in this digraph corresponds to a cover of the linear forest by a Hamilton cycle. Then, we show that this auxiliary digraph satisfies the notion of pseudorandomness in
Definition

5.1
and therefore by Theorem
5.2
it contains a Hamilton cycle. Before the formal description we need to introduce some notation.

A directed linear forest
F
can be written as an union of disjoint directed paths
F=\bigcup_{i=1}^{\ell}P_{i}
. Given a path
P=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{m})
, we define the
source

s(P)
of the path
P
as the first vertex in the path, i.e.,
s(P)=x_{1}
. Similarly, the
sink

t(P)
of
P
is defined as the last vertex of the path, i.e.,
t(P)=x_{m}
. Recall that we consider isolated vertices to be paths of length
0
. We now are able to define our auxiliary “contracting” digraph.

Definition 5.3
.

Give a directed graph
D
and a linear forest
F=\bigcup_{i=1}^{\ell}P_{i}
, we define the auxiliary directed graph
H_{F,D}
as the graph with vertex set
V(H_{F,D})=\{P_{j}\}_{j\in[\ell]}
and edge set given by

\displaystyle H_{F,D}=\left\{\overrightarrow{P_{i}P_{j}}:\>\overrightarrow{t(P_{i})s(P_{j})}\in D\right\}

That is,
H_{F,D}
is the graph where the vertices are the paths and we add an edge from
P_{i}
to
P_{j}
if there is an edge in
D
from the sink of
P_{i}
to the source of
P_{j}
.

Note by the last definition that the number of vertices of
H_{D,F}
is exactly the number of components of
F
. The next result shows that if our host digraph
D
is typical and
F
has a “relatively large” number of connected components, then to check that
F
can be covered by a Hamilton cycle is the same as checking that
H_{F,D}
satisfies property (P1) of
Definition

5.1
.

Proposition 5.4
.

Let
D:=D_{n,p}
with
1/2\geq p\geq\frac{\log^{10}n}{n}
. Then, whp the following holds: Suppose that
F
is a directed linear forest with
\ell\geq\frac{n^{1/3}}{2p^{2/3}}

components and that its auxiliary digraph
H_{F,D}
satisfies
\delta(H_{F,D})\geq 3p\ell/4
. Then there exists a Hamilton cycle in
D\cup F
covering all the edges of
F
.

Proof.

First, note that it suffices to show that
H_{F,D}
is Hamiltonian. Indeed, suppose that
H_{F,D}
has a Hamilton cycle
C
. Let
C^{\prime}
be the cycle in
D\cup F
obtained by replacing the vertex
P_{i}
in
H_{F,D}
by the path
P_{i}
in
F
. Clearly
C^{\prime}
is a directed cycle containing all the vertices of
V(D)
(since
V(F)=\bigcup_{i=1}^{\ell}P_{i}
) and
F\subseteq C^{\prime}
. Therefore
C^{\prime}
is a Hamilton cycle covering
F
.

We claim that
H_{F,D}
is
(\ell,1/8,p)
-pseudorandom and consequently by
Theorem

5.2
that it is Hamiltonian. First, note that

\displaystyle\frac{\log^{9}\ell}{\ell}\leq\frac{\log^{9}\left(\frac{n^{1/3}}{2p^{2/3}}\right)}{\frac{n^{1/3}}{2p^{2/3}}}\leq\frac{\log^{9}n-2^{9}\log^{9}\left(2^{3/2}p\right)}{3^{9}n^{1/3}}2p^{2/3}<p.

The first inequality holds because
\frac{\log^{9}x}{x}
is decreasing, and the last inequality holds because
p>\frac{\log^{10}n}{n}
.

Now that we’ve verified
p>\frac{\log^{9}\ell}{\ell}
, we just need to check properties (P1), (P2) and (P3) of
Definition

5.1
. Property (P1) follows immediately from the hypothesis of the proposition applied to
\alpha=1/8
. We now prove property (P2). Let
X\subseteq V(H_{D,F})
of size
|X|\leq\frac{\log^{2}\ell}{p}
. Consider the set
X^{\prime}\subseteq V(D)
defined by

\displaystyle X^{\prime}:=\left\{x\in V(D):\>x=s(P)\text{ or }x=t(P)\text{ for }P\in X\right\}

That is,
X^{\prime}
is the set of all sources and sinks of paths in
X
. Note by definition, that
|X^{\prime}|\leq 2|X|\leq\frac{2\log^{2}\ell}{p}\leq\frac{2\log^{2}n}{p}
, since
\ell\leq n
. Therefore, by property (D2) of
Lemma

2.10
we have that
e_{D}(X^{\prime})\leq 12|X^{\prime}|\log^{2}n
. Since any edge of
H_{F,D}[X]
corresponds to an edge in
D[X^{\prime}]
we obtain that
e_{H_{F,D}}(X)\leq e_{D}(X^{\prime})
. Therefore, for
\ell\geq\frac{n^{1/3}}{2p^{2/3}}
, we have that

\displaystyle e_{H_{F,D}}(X)\leq 12|X^{\prime}|\log^{2}n\leq 24|X|\log^{2}n\leq 216|X|\log^{2}\ell\leq|X|\log^{2.1}\ell,

and property (P2) holds.

To prove property (P3), let
X,Y
be disjoint subsets of
V(H_{F,D})
with
|X|,|Y|\geq\frac{\log^{1.1}{\ell}}{p}
. Let
X^{\prime}
and
Y^{\prime}
be subsets of
V(D)
defined by

\displaystyle X^{\prime}=\left\{t(P)\in V(D):\>P\in X\right\}\quad\text{and}\quad Y^{\prime}=\left\{s(P)\in V(D):\>P\in Y\right\}

That is,
X^{\prime}
is the set of sinks of
X
and
Y^{\prime}
is the set of sources of
Y
. Note that
|X^{\prime}|=|X|
and
|Y^{\prime}|=|Y|
. Thus, we have
|X^{\prime}|,|Y^{\prime}|\geq\frac{\log^{1.1}\ell}{p}\geq\frac{\log^{1.05}n}{p}
. Hence, by property (D3) of
Lemma

2.10

\displaystyle e_{H_{F,D}}(X,Y)=e_{D}(X^{\prime},Y^{\prime})\leq\left(1+\frac{1}{16}\right)p|X||Y|,

concluding the proof.
∎

The following result is the main technical lemma of the section. Roughly speaking, it states that given a large family of linear forests with small number of components, one can almost cover it with a family of Hamilton cycles in a way that guarantees the remaining edges form a sparse graph with small maximum degree. Moreover, for technical reasons, the choice of Hamilton cycles is done to contain specific edges of the linear forests.

Lemma 5.5
.

Let
D:=D_{n,p}
with
1/2\geq p\geq\frac{\log^{10}n}{n}
, and let
np/2\leq t\leq 3np/2
be an integer. The following holds whp: Suppose that
\mathcal{F}=\{F_{1},\ldots,F_{t}\}
is a family of directed linear forests and
x\in V(D)
is a vertex satisfying

(i)

For each
1\leq i\leq t
, the linear forest
F_{i}
has at most
4\log n
components.

(ii)

For each
1\leq i\leq t
, there exists a vertex
y_{i}\in V
such that
\overrightarrow{xy_{i}}\in F_{i}
.

Then there exists a family of Hamilton cycles
\mathcal{C}=\{C_{1},\ldots,C_{t}\}
such that
\overrightarrow{xy_{i}}\in C_{i}
and
C_{i}
is a cycle in
D\cup F_{i}
. Moreover, if
R=\bigcup_{i=1}^{t}\left(F_{i}\setminus C_{i}\right)
is the digraph of non-covered edges of
\mathcal{F}
by
\mathcal{C}
, then
\Delta(R)\leq 7t^{1/3}
.

Proof.

Let
V:=V(D)
. Note that whp
D:=D_{n,p}
simultaneously satisfies
Lemma

2.10
and
Proposition

5.4
. We first remove a small amount of edges of
\mathcal{F}
at random. Let
R
be the graph obtained by selecting edges from
\bigcup_{i=1}^{t}F_{i}\setminus\{\overrightarrow{xy_{i}}\}
independently with probability
q=t^{-2/3}
. Set
U_{i}=F_{i}\setminus R
. We now prove three properties (Claims
5.6
,
5.7
, and
5.8
) that are satisfied by
R
and the family of linear forests
\{U_{i}\}_{i=1}^{t}
. Let
m_{i}
and
\ell_{i}
and be number of connected components of
F_{i}
and
U_{i}
, respectively.

Claim 5.6
.

For
1\leq i\leq t
and
\varepsilon>0
, whp it holds that
(1-\varepsilon)nq\leq\ell_{i}\leq(1+\varepsilon)nq
.

Proof.

Note that every edge removed from
F_{i}
creates a new component. Therefore, the number of components in
U_{i}
is exactly
m_{i}+|F_{i}\cap R|
. We claim that whp
(1-\varepsilon/2)nq\leq|F_{i}\cap R|\leq(1+\varepsilon/2)nq
for
1\leq i\leq t
. Indeed, since
F_{i}
has at most
4\log n
components, we have
|F_{i}|=n-4\log n=(1-o(1))n
. Chernoff’s bound (
Theorem

2.1
) and the fact that
t\geq np/2
gives us that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\big||F_{i}\cap R|-q|F_{i}|\big|>\frac{\varepsilon nq}{3}\right)\leq\exp\left(-\frac{\varepsilon^{2}nq}{27}\right)=\exp\left(-\frac{\varepsilon^{2}n}{27t^{2/3}}\right)=o\left(\frac{1}{n}\right).

Hence, by a union bound, we have that
(1-\varepsilon/2)nq\leq|F_{i}\cap R|\leq(1+\varepsilon/2)nq
holds with probability
1-o(1)
. This implies the bound

\displaystyle(1-\varepsilon)nq\leq|F_{i}\cap R|\leq\ell_{i}=m_{i}+|F_{i}\cap R|\leq(1+\varepsilon/2)nq+4\log n\leq(1+\varepsilon)nq

by our choice of
q
.∎

Next, we check condition (P1) of
Definition

5.1
for the auxiliary graph
H_{U_{i},D}
. For each
1\leq i\leq t
, we write
F_{i}=\bigcup_{j=1}^{m_{i}}P_{ij}
and
U_{i}=\bigcup_{j=1}^{\ell_{i}}Q_{ij}
, where
\{P_{ij}\}_{j=1}^{m_{i}}
and
\{Q_{ij}\}_{j=1}^{\ell_{i}}
are the disjoint paths of
F_{i}
and
U_{i}
, respectively (recall that some of those paths might be isolated vertices).

Claim 5.7
.

For
1\leq i\leq t
, whp we have that
\delta(H_{U_{i},D})\geq 3p\ell_{i}/4
.

Proof.

We will prove that whp
\delta^{+}(H_{U_{i},D})\geq 3p\ell_{i}/4
for
1\leq i\leq t
. Fix an index
1\leq i\leq t
. Let
S(F_{i})=\{s(P_{ij}):\>1\leq j\leq\ell_{i}\}
be the set of sources of the paths in
F_{i}
and
S(Q_{i})=\{s(Q_{ij}):\>1\leq j\leq\ell_{i}\}
. For a vertex
v\in V
, we define

\displaystyle g(v):=\left|\left(N_{D}^{+}(v)\setminus\left(S(F_{i})\cup\{y_{i}\}\right)\right)\cap S(U_{i})\right|,

where
N_{D}^{+}(v)
is the out-neighborhood of
v
in the digraph
D
. Note that

\displaystyle\delta^{+}(H_{U_{i},D})=\min_{t(Q_{ij}):\>1\leq j\leq\ell_{i}}|N_{D}^{+}(t(Q_{ij}))\cap S(U_{i})|\geq\min_{v\in V}g(v),

and therefore we just need to estimate
g(v)
.

We claim that
g(v)\geq 3p\ell_{i}/4
holds for every
v\in V
with probability at least
1-o(1/n)
. Note that for every vertex
w\in V\setminus\left(S(F_{i})\cup\{y_{i}\}\right)
, there exists a unique vertex
z_{w}
such that
\overrightarrow{z_{w}w}\in F_{i}\setminus\{\overrightarrow{xy_{i}}\}
. Hence, the set
\left(V\setminus\left(S(F_{i})\cup\{y_{i}\}\right)\right)\cap S(U_{i})
can be characterized as

\displaystyle\left(V\setminus\left(S(F_{i})\cup\{y_{i}\}\right)\right)\cap S(U_{i})=\{w\in V(D)\setminus\left(S(F_{i})\cup\{y_{i}\}\right):\>\overrightarrow{z_{w}w\in R}\}

That is, the new sources created in
U_{i}
are the vertices from
V\setminus\left(S(F_{i})\cup\{y_{i}\}\right)
such that the edge preceding them in
F_{i}
was removed. Since the elements of
R
are chosen independently with probability
q
, the variable
g(v)
follows a binomial distribution
\Bin\left(|N_{D}^{+}(v)\setminus\left(S(F_{i})\cup\{y_{i}\}\right)|,q\right)
with average

\displaystyle q|N_{D}^{+}(v)\setminus\left(S(F_{i})\cup\{y_{i}\}\right)|

\displaystyle\geq q\left(|N_{D}^{+}(v)|-|S(F_{i})\cup\{y_{i}\}|\right)

\displaystyle\geq q\left((1-o(1))np-4\log n-1\right)\geq(1-o(1))npq,

where we use that
N_{D}^{+}(v)=(1-o(1))np
(property (D1) of
Lemma

2.10
),
|S(F_{i})|=m_{i}\leq 3\log n
. By Chernoff’s bound (Theorem
2.1
), we have that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(g(v)<\frac{4npq}{5}\right)\leq\exp\left(-\frac{npq}{100}\right)=\exp\left(-\frac{(np)^{1/3}}{200}\right)=o\left(\frac{1}{n^{2}}\right)

for
q=t^{-2/3}
and
t\leq 3np/2
. Therefore, by an union bound and Claim
5.6
, we obtain that

\displaystyle g(v)\geq\frac{4npq}{5}\geq\frac{3p\ell_{i}}{4}

for every vertex
v\in V
with probability
1-o(1/n)
. Hence, we obtain that whp
\delta^{+}(H_{U_{i},D})\geq 3p\ell_{i}/4
for
1\leq i\leq t
. The proof that the event
\delta^{-}(H_{U_{i},D})\geq 3p\ell_{i}/4
holds whp is analogous.
∎

Finally, we show that the graph
R
has small maximum degree.

Claim 5.8
.

The event
\Delta(R)\leq 7t^{1/3}
holds whp.

Proof.

We claim that whp
\deg_{R}^{+}(v)\leq 7t^{1/3}
for
v\in V
. If
v=x
, then
\deg_{R}^{+}(x)=0\leq 7t^{1/3}
by our construction. Otherwise, the degree
\deg_{R}^{+}(v)
follows a binomial distribution
\Bin(\deg_{\mathcal{F}}^{+}(v),q)
with average
q\deg_{\mathcal{F}}^{+}(v)\leq qt=t^{1/3}
and we obtain that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\deg_{R}^{+}(v)>7qt\right)\leq e^{-7qt}=e^{-7t^{1/3}}=o\left(\frac{1}{n}\right)

by
2.2
and
t\geq np/2
. The result now follows from an union bound. The proof that whp
\deg_{R}^{-}(v)\leq 7t^{1/3}
for
v\in V
is similar. If
v=x
, then
\deg_{R}^{-}(x)
follows a binomial distribution
\Bin(\deg_{\mathcal{F}}^{-}(x),q)
. Otherwise, the degree
\deg_{R}^{-}(v)
follows
\Bin(\deg_{\mathcal{F}}^{-}(v)-1,q)
. The rest of the argument is identical to the argument for
\deg_{R}^{+}(v)
. Therefore, whp
\Delta(R)\leq 7t^{1/3}
.
∎

We finish the proof as follows. Let
R
and
\{U_{i}\}_{i=1}^{t}
satisfy Claims
5.6
,
5.7
and
5.8
. Such a choice exists because all the claims hold with high probability. Note that Claim
5.6
gives us that
\ell_{i}\geq(1-o(1))nq=(1-o(1))n/t^{2/3}\geq\frac{n^{1/3}}{2p^{2/3}}
for
3np/2\geq t\geq np/2
and
1\leq i\leq t
. This implies that the linear forest
U_{i}
satisfies the hypothesis of
Proposition

5.4
. Therefore, there exists a family of Hamilton cycles
\mathcal{C}=\{C_{1},\ldots,C_{t}\}
such that
C_{i}\in D\cup F_{i}
and
\overrightarrow{xy_{i}}\in U_{i}\subseteq C_{i}
. The condition on the degree of
R
is immediately satisfied by Claim
5.8
.
∎

We finish the section by showing a way to fully cover matchings by Hamilton cycles. This will be useful for covering the graph
R
of edges not covered by
Lemma

5.5
. A digraph
M
is called a matching if the undirected underlying graph is a matching. First, we prove that we can cover a small matching with a Hamilton cycle in
D_{n,p}

Proposition 5.9
.

Let
D:=D_{n,p}
with
1/2\geq p\geq\frac{\log^{10}n}{n}
and let
j\geq 1
be an integer. Then whp every matching
M
with at most
j
edges can be covered by a Hamilton cycle in
D\cup M
.

Proof.

We will show that
M
satisfies the hypothesis of
Proposition

5.4
. First, note that when we consider
M
as a directed linear forest, it has at least
n-2j\geq\frac{n^{1/3}}{2p^{2/3}}
isolated vertices, which are components. Second, by property (D1) of
Lemma

2.10
, with high probability, the minimum degree in
D
is
(1+o(1))np
. In the auxilliary digraph
H_{M,D}
, only
j
pairs of vertices are contracted. Thus, the minimum degree in
H_{M,D}
is at least
(1+o(1))np-j>3(n-j)p/4
. Now, we may apply
Proposition

5.4
to obtain a Hamilton cycle in
D\cup M
covering
M
.
∎

The next result shows that any matching in
D_{n,p}
can be covered with constantly many Hamilton cycles, regardless of size.

Again, by technical reasons to be explained later, the covering is done in a way to include a specific set of extra edges.

Lemma 5.10
.

Let
D:=D_{n,p}
with
1/2\geq p\geq\frac{\log^{10}n}{n}
and
k\geq 10
be an integer. The following holds whp. Let
M
be a matching and
x\in V(D)
a vertex that is not matched in
M
,
Let
S=\{\overrightarrow{xy_{1}},\ldots,\overrightarrow{xy_{k}}\}
be a set of
k
edges going out from
x
. Then there exists a family of
k
Hamilton cycles
\mathcal{C}=\{C_{1},\ldots,C_{k}\}
in
D\cup M\cup S
such that
\mathcal{C}
covers the edges of
M
and
\overrightarrow{xy_{i}}\in C_{i}
for every
1\leq i\leq k
.

Proof.

We will start by partitioning
M
into
k-1
smaller matchings
M_{1},\ldots M_{\lfloor k/2\rfloor}
as follows: First, assign each vertex
v
a number
\phi(v)\in\left\{1,2,\ldots\lfloor k/2\rfloor\right\}
uniformly at random. Then, for each
i\in\left\{1,2,\ldots\lfloor k/2\rfloor\right\}
, let
M_{i}
be the matching containing all edges
\overrightarrow{ab}\in M
such that
\phi(b)=i
. Now, define

M_{i}^{\prime}=\{\overrightarrow{ab}\mid\overrightarrow{ab}\in M_{j},\text{ }b\neq y_{i}\}\cup\{\overrightarrow{xy_{i}}\}.

For each
i
, we will use
Proposition

5.4
to find a Hamilton cycle covering
M_{i}
. First, since each
M_{i}
is a matching, and we consider isolated vertices to be components, the number of components
\ell_{i}
in each
M_{i}
is least
n/2\geq\frac{n^{1/3}}{2p^{2/3}}
. Thus, it is sufficient to prove:

Claim 5.11
.

For
1\leq i\leq\lfloor k/2\rfloor
, whp we have that
\delta(H_{M_{i},D})\geq 3p\ell_{i}/4
.

Proof.

Let
t=t(P)
be the sink of a path in
M_{i}^{\prime}
, and let
S(M_{i}^{\prime})
be the set of sources of paths in
M_{i}^{\prime}
.
By property (D1) in
Lemma

2.10
,
\deg^{+}_{D}(t)=(1+o(1))np
. Now, note that
\deg^{+}_{H_{M_{i}^{\prime},D}}(P)=|N^{+}_{D}(t)\cap S(M_{i}^{\prime})|
. Each vertex
v\neq x
of
N^{+}_{D}(t)
is isolated in
M_{i}^{\prime}
if
\phi(v)\neq i
, which occurs with probability at least
4/5
. Let
I(N^{+}_{D}(t),M_{i}^{\prime})
be the number of vertices in
N^{+}_{D}(t)
isolated in
M_{i}^{\prime}
. The expectation of
I(N^{+}_{D}(t),M_{i}^{\prime})
is
np(\lfloor k/2\rfloor-1)/\lfloor k/2\rfloor\geq 4np/5
. Thus, by Chernoff’s bound (
Theorem

2.1
):

\mathds{P}\left(I(N^{+}_{D}(t),M_{i}^{\prime})<3np/4\right)\leq\exp\left(\frac{np}{100}\right)=o\left(\frac{1}{n^{2}}\right).

Since each isolated vertex is a source in
M_{i}^{\prime}
,

\mathds{P}\left(\deg^{+}_{H_{M_{i}^{\prime},D}}(P)<3np/4\right)=o\left(\frac{1}{n^{2}}\right).

We can use the same argument to show
\mathds{P}\left(\deg^{-}_{H_{M_{i}^{\prime},D}}(P)<3np/4\right)=o\left(\frac{1}{n^{2}}\right).

By a simple union bound, with probability
o\left(\frac{1}{n}\right)
, it holds that
\delta(H_{M_{i},D})\geq 3p\ell_{i}/4
.
∎

Now, by applying
Proposition

5.4
we obtain Hamilton cycles
C_{1}\ldots C_{\lfloor k/2\rfloor}
covering the matchings
M_{1}^{\prime}\ldots M_{\lfloor k/2\rfloor}^{\prime}
.
We still need to cover the edges
xy_{\lfloor k/2\rfloor+1}\ldots xy_{k}
and the set
Q
of edges that were contained in some
M_{i}
but not contained in
M_{i}^{\prime}
.
For each
1\leq i\leq\lfloor k/2\rfloor
, if
M_{i}
contains an edge
\overrightarrow{z_{i}y_{i}}
with
y_{i}
as a sink, then
M_{i}^{\prime}
does not contain that edge. In that case, let
F_{i+\lfloor k/2\rfloor}
be the linear forest containing
\overrightarrow{z_{i}y_{i}}
and
xy_{i+\lfloor k/2\rfloor}
. If
M_{i}
does not contain an edge with
y_{i}
as a sink, let
F_{i+\lfloor k/2\rfloor}
be the singleton linear forest containing
xy_{i+\lfloor k/2\rfloor}
. If
k
is odd, define
F_{k}
as the singleton linear forest containing
xy_{k}
. Each of these linear forest has at most two elements, so by
Proposition

5.9
, there exist Hamilton cycles
C_{\lfloor k/2\rfloor}\ldots C_{k}
in
D\cup M\cup S
covering each
F_{i}
. This concludes the proof of
Lemma

5.10
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
1.1

Finally, we put our work together and prove Theorem
1.1

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

First, note that whp
D:=D_{n,p}
simultaneously satisfies
Lemma

2.10
and all the statements of Sections
4

5

\Delta(D)
Hamilton cycles. Let
\Delta_{1}\geq\Delta_{2}\geq\ldots\geq\Delta_{2n}

, we have that

(3)

\displaystyle(1-o(1))np\leq\Delta_{2n}\leq\ldots\leq\Delta_{1}\leq(1+o(1))np,

(iii)

Lemma A.1

Let

Claim A.2

\mathds{P}(|T|=O(1))=O(n^{-3}).

Proof.

Conditioning on the size of

Case 1.

v\pi^{-1}(v)\notin E(G)

It will be helpful to describe the following exposure procedure for revealing the values of the (random) permutation

(2)

(ii)

i\in[r]
,

\mathbb{E}\left(\frac{1}{c^{3}_{i}}\right)=O\left(\frac{1}{n}\right)
.

(2)

For every distinct
i,j\in[r]
,

\mathbb{E}\left(\frac{1}{c^{2}_{i}c_{j}}\right)=O\left(\frac{\log^{2}n}{n^{2}}\right).

(3)

For every distinct
i,j,k\in[r]
,

\mathbb{E}\left(\frac{1}{c_{i}c_{j}c_{k}}\right)=O\left(\frac{\log^{3}n}{n^{3}}\right)
.

Proof.

We start by showing statement (1). Note that for a single cycle, the probability
\mathbb{P}(c_{i}=t)=1/n
. Hence,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left(\frac{1}{c_{i}^{3}}\right)=\sum_{a_{i}\in[n]}\frac{1}{a_{i}^{3}}\mathbb{P}(c_{i}=a_{i})=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{a_{i}\in[n]}\frac{1}{a_{i}^{3}}=O\left(\frac{1}{n}\right).

To prove statement (2) and (3) we use Lemma
A.1
. Indeed, for statement (2) we have that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left(\frac{1}{c_{i}^{2}c_{j}}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{a_{i},a_{j}\in[n]}\frac{1}{a_{i}^{2}a_{j}}\mathbb{P}\left((c_{i}=a_{i})\cap(c_{j}=a_{j})\right)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{a_{i},a_{j}\in[n]}\frac{1}{a_{i}a_{j}}\mathbb{P}\left((c_{i}=a_{i})\cap(c_{j}=a_{j})\right)\leq\sum_{a_{i}\leq a_{j}}\frac{2}{a_{i}a_{j}}\mathbb{P}\left((c_{i}=a_{i})\cap(c_{j}=a_{j})\right)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{a_{i}\leq a_{j}\leq n/4}\frac{2}{a_{i}a_{j}}\mathbb{P}\left((c_{i}=a_{i})\cap(c_{j}=a_{j})\right)+\sum_{a_{i}\in[n]}\frac{8}{na_{i}}\mathbb{P}\left(c_{i}=a_{i}\right)

\displaystyle=O(n^{-2})\left(\sum_{a_{i}\leq a_{j}\leq n/4}\frac{1}{a_{i}a_{j}}+\sum_{a_{i}\in[n]}\frac{1}{a_{i}}\right)=O\left(\frac{\log^{2}n}{n^{2}}\right),

where we use Lemma
A.1
for two cycles.

A similar computation holds for statement (3):

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left(\frac{1}{c_{i}c_{j}c_{k}}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{a_{i},a_{j},a_{k}\in[n]}\frac{1}{a_{i}a_{j}a_{k}}\mathbb{P}\left((c_{i}=a_{i})\cap(c_{j}=a_{j})\cap(c_{k}=a_{k})\right)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{a_{i}\leq a_{j}\leq a_{k}}\frac{6}{a_{i}a_{j}a_{k}}\mathbb{P}\left((c_{i}=a_{i})\cap(c_{j}=a_{j})\cap(c_{k}=a_{k})\right)=\Sigma_{1}+\Sigma_{2}+\Sigma_{3},

where

\displaystyle\Sigma_{1}:=\sum_{a_{i}\leq a_{j}\leq a_{k}\leq n/6}\frac{6}{a_{i}a_{j}a_{k}}\mathbb{P}\left((c_{i}=a_{i})\cap(c_{j}=a_{j})\cap(c_{k}=a_{k})\right)

\displaystyle\Sigma_{2}:=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a_{i}\leq a_{j}\leq n/6\\
a_{k}>n/6\end{subarray}}\frac{6}{a_{i}a_{j}a_{k}}\mathbb{P}\left((c_{i}=a_{i})\cap(c_{j}=a_{j})\cap(c_{k}=a_{k})\right),\textrm{ and }

\displaystyle\Sigma_{3}:=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a_{i}\in[n]\\
n/6<a_{j}\leq a_{k}\end{subarray}}\frac{6}{a_{i}a_{j}a_{k}}\mathbb{P}\left((c_{i}=a_{i})\cap(c_{j}=a_{j})\cap(c_{k}=a_{k})\right)

We bound each of the
\Sigma_{i}
s separately as follows:

By Lemma
A.1
applied for three cycles, we know that for
a_{1}\leq a_{2}\leq a_{3}\leq n/6
, the probability
\mathbb{P}((c_{i}=a_{i})\cap(c_{j}=a_{j})\cap(c_{k}=a_{k}))=O(n^{-3})
. Therefore,

\displaystyle\Sigma_{1}=O\left(n^{-3}\sum_{a_{1}\leq a_{2}\leq a_{3}\leq n/6}\frac{1}{a_{1}a_{2}a_{3}}\right)=O\left(\frac{\log^{3}n}{n^{3}}\right)

Similarly, Lemma
A.1
applied for two cycles gives that if
a_{1}\leq a_{2}\leq n/6
, then
\mathbb{P}((c_{i}=a_{i})\cap(c_{j}=a_{j}))=O(n^{-2})
. Hence

\displaystyle\Sigma_{2}\leq\sum_{a_{i}\leq a_{j}\leq n/6}\frac{36}{na_{i}a_{j}}\mathbb{P}\left((c_{i}=a_{i})\cap(c_{j}=a_{j})\right)=O\left(n^{-3}\sum_{a_{i}\leq a_{j}\leq n/6}\frac{1}{a_{i}a_{j}}\right)=O\left(\frac{\log^{2}n}{n^{3}}\right)

Finally, to bound
\Sigma_{3}
, we just need to use that
\mathbb{P}(c_{i}=a_{i})=1/n
. Therefore,

\displaystyle\Sigma_{3}\leq\sum_{a_{i}\in[n]}\frac{216}{n^{2}a_{i}}\mathbb{P}\left(c_{i}=a_{i}\right)=\frac{216}{n^{3}}\sum_{a_{i}\in[n]}\frac{1}{a_{i}}=O\left(\frac{\log n}{n^{3}}\right)

By summing everything together, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left(\frac{1}{c_{i}c_{j}c_{k}}\right)=O\left(\frac{\log^{3}n}{n^{3}}\right).

This concludes the proof of the claim.
∎

We finish the proof of the proposition by applying Claim
A.8
after expanding the third moment

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left(\left(\sum_{i\in[r]}\frac{1}{c_{i}}\right)^{3}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in[r]}\mathbb{E}\left(\frac{1}{c_{i}^{3}}\right)+3\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i,j\in[r]\\
i\neq j\end{subarray}}\mathbb{E}\left(\frac{1}{c_{i}^{2}c_{j}}\right)+6\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i,j,k\in[r]\\
i<j<k\end{subarray}}\mathbb{E}\left(\frac{1}{c_{i}c_{j}c_{k}}\right)

\displaystyle=r\cdot O\left(\frac{1}{n}\right)+r^{2}\cdot O\left(\frac{\log^{2}n}{n^{2}}\right)+r^{3}\cdot O\left(\frac{\log^{3}n}{n^{3}}\right)=O\left(\frac{r}{n}\log^{3}n\right),

as desired.
∎
