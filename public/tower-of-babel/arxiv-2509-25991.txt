Towards Unified Multimodal Misinformation Detection in Social Media: A Benchmark Dataset and Baseline
Haiyang Li, Yaxiong Wang, Lianwei Wu, Lechao Cheng, Zhun Zhong

Abstract
In recent years, detecting fake multimodal content on social media has drawn increasing attention. Two major forms of deception dominate: human-crafted misinformation (e.g., rumors and misleading posts) and AI-generated content produced by image synthesis models or vision–language models (VLMs). Although both share deceptive intent, they are typically studied in isolation. NLP research focuses on human-written misinformation, while the CV community targets AI-generated artifacts. As a result, existing models are often specialized for only one type of fake content. In real-world scenarios, however, the type of a multimodal post is usually unknown, limiting the effectiveness of such specialized systems. To bridge this gap, we construct the Omnibus Dataset for Multimodal News Deception (OmniFake), a comprehensive benchmark of 127K samples that integrates human-curated misinformation from existing resources with newly synthesized AI-generated examples. Based on this dataset, we propose Unified Multimodal Fake Content Detection (UMFDet), a framework designed to handle both forms of deception. UMFDet leverages a VLM backbone augmented with a Category-aware Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) Adapter to capture category-specific cues, and an attribution chain-of-thought mechanism that provides implicit reasoning guidance for locating salient deceptive signals. Extensive experiments demonstrate that UMFDet achieves robust and consistent performance across both misinformation types, outperforming specialized baselines and offering a practical solution for real-world multimodal deception detection.

Introduction

The proliferation of multimodal misinformation on social media platforms has emerged as a significant public security concern
Liu et al. 2024c
;
Tahmasebi et al. 2024
;
Guo et al. 2025b
. In response, the detection of deceptive content like rumors, misleading claims, and fabricated information has attracted considerable research interest in recent years
Hu et al. 2024
;
Zhang et al. 2024
;
Wu et al. 2024
. Mainstream misinformation can be broadly categorized into two types: human-crafted misinformation and AI-generated synthetic content. Human-crafted misinformation is intentionally created by individuals, often with motives such as gaining followers on social platforms. In contrast, AI-generated content is produced using generative AI tools, including image synthesis models, Large Language Models (LLMs), and Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs). The rise of such AI-synthesized misinformation represents a growing challenge in the era of advanced deep learning systems
Guo et al. 2024
;
Chen et al. 2025
.

Although both human-crafted misinformation and AI-generated content belong to misinformation, they are often studied in isolation. As shown in Figure 1, Researchers in natural language processing (NLP) have extensively studied human-authored deceptive content, such as human-written rumors and misleading information, with a long-standing tradition of fake news detection in social media
Liu et al. 2024a
;
Cui & Jia 2024
;
Jiang et al. 2025
. On the other hand, the computer vision community has focused primarily on detecting synthetic multimodal media generated by AI models, including deepfakes and other artificially produced visual content
Yan et al. 2024
;
Cheng et al. 2024
;
Zhang et al. 2025
. However, in real-world social platforms, both types of misinformation frequently coexist. A straightforward solution is to employ two separate expert models, each specialized in one type of misinformation, and combine their outputs for a final decision. Nevertheless, such an approach is suboptimal, lacking elegance and computational efficiency.

In response to this challenge, we aim to develop a unified framework capable of simultaneously detecting both human-crafted misinformation and AI-generated fake content. To support this research, we construct a large-scale Omnibus Dataset for Multimodal News Deception (OmniFake). Specifically, we collect samples from the Fakeddit dataset
Nakamura et al. 2019
and select misleading examples to form the category of human-crafted misinformation. For the AI-generated category, we first choose a subset of real samples from Fakeddit and apply a diverse set of manipulation techniques including face swapping, background replacement, and textual rewriting
et al.
, to simulate various forms of synthetic media. Through this process, we finally construct a dataset comprising 127,283 samples spanning three distinct categories: real news, human-crafted misinformation, and AI-generated multimodal deception.

To address the new challenges presented by the OmniFake dataset, we propose a Unified Multimodal Fake Content Detection (UMFDet) framework, designed to simultaneously combat both types of misinformation. UMFDet leverages a Multimodal Large Language Model (MLLM) as its backbone and introduces a Category-aware Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) Adapter to adapt the MLLM for misinformation detection. This adapter allocates specialized expert modules to identify specific types of deceptive content. To further enhance the model’s ability to accurately discern subtle cues of misinformation, we incorporate an Attribution Chain-of-Thought (CoT) strategy into the framework. Attribution CoT imposes an implicit reasoning process during the generation of detection results, guiding the model to trace and leverage attributions indicative of falsity for each misinformation type, thereby advancing detection accuracy.

In summary, we highlight the contributions of this paper as follows:

•

We conduct an early investigation into the simultaneous detection of both human-crafted and AI-generated misinformation on social media, a significant yet underexplored research problem.

•

We construct the Omnibus Dataset for Multimodal News Deception (OmniFake), comprising 127,283 samples of two prevalent types of misinformation. This dataset supports the unified detection of both human-written and AI-generated fake contents.

•

We propose a Unified Multimodal Fake Content Detection (UMFDet) framework to address this challenging task. The framework incorporates a Category-aware Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) Adapter and Attribution Chain-of-Thought (CoT) mechanism for accurate detection of both misinformation types.

Related Work

2.1
Fake News Detection

With the rapid growth of generative models and large language models, synthetic content on social
media has surged, raising concerns about credibility and governance. Existing approaches to fake
news detection fall into two lines: unimodal and multimodal. Unimodal methods process either
images or text in isolation
Ghai et al. 2024
;
Xiao et al. 2024b
, which makes it hard to capture cross-modal deceptive cues.
Recently, multimodal detection has received increasing attention
Peng et al. 2024
;
Luvembe et al. 2024
, and most methods rely on
cross-modal contrastive learning or feature fusion to improve vision–language representations and
alignment. Representative works include COOLANT
Wang et al. 2023
, which adopts an auxiliary-task–enhanced
contrastive framework with softened negatives and attention-based aggregation, and FKA-OWL
Liu et al. 2024b
,
which leverages world knowledge from large vision–language models to improve cross-domain generalization.
Despite these advances, most systems remain limited to binary classification and provide insufficient
support for multi-class and multi-source synthetic settings common in the wild, constraining real-world applicability.

2.2
Multi-Modal Large Language Models

Research has progressed along a clear trajectory. CLIP
Radford et al. 2021
and ALIGN
Li et al. 2021

established robust cross-modal alignment and zero-shot recognition via contrastive learning,
substantially improving image–text matching and retrieval. SimVLM
Wang et al. 2021

then advanced language-centric, caption-driven pretraining to strengthen generation and transfer.
To reduce the cost of end-to-end scaling, a decoupled paradigm emerged:
BLIP-2
Li et al. 2023a
and CoCa
Yu et al. 2022
connect a vision encoder to a language model
through cross-attention or lightweight bridges. Building on this, multimodal instruction alignment
became central, with InstructBLIP
Dai et al. 2023
and Qwen2-VL
Wang et al. 2024

demonstrating strong improvements in open-ended QA and reasoning via instruction tuning.
More recently, Florence-2
Xiao et al. 2024a
further highlights the trend of using language as a
unifying interface to aggregate large-scale priors and transfer them to downstream tasks.

We build our multimodal fake news dataset on top of r/Fakeddit
Nakamura et al. 2019
, a large-scale collection crawled from Reddit, a widely used social news and discussion platform. The source corpus contains over one million image–text pairs spanning 22 subreddits and a broad topical spectrum—from political news to everyday posts by Reddit users.
To construct an omnibus benchmark for multimodal misinformation, our OmniFake dataset covers three categories: (i) Real posts, (ii) Human-crafted misinformation, and (iii) AI-synthesized content generated with modern image and language tools. Particularly, we select 49,034 posts from r/Fakeddit
Nakamura et al. 2019
to form the
Real posts
category in OmniFake. We select 24,726 misleading posts from r/Fakeddit
Nakamura et al. 2019
to serve as the
Human-crafted misinformation
in OmniFake. In the following, we elaborate how we generate samples covering diverse manipulation patterns using AI models.

3.1
Synthetic Multimodal News

The proliferation of generative models including LLMs and MLLMs enables heterogeneous pipelines for fabricating persuasive multimodal content, posing new risks to social media
Achiam et al. 2023
;
Zhang et al. 2023
.
To better mimic in-the-wild fakes, we build a synthetic subset guided by two principles:

•

Broad manipulation coverage.
We include both fully synthesized images and localized edits (attribute changes, object insertion/replacement/removal, and scene recomposition), covering wide range of forgeries.

•

Controlled cross-modal semantics.
For each image–text pair, we explicitly set the modality relation, producing (i) semantically aligned pairs and (ii) subtly contradictory yet plausible pairs. This increases deceptiveness and stresses models’ multimodal reasoning.

Image Synthesis.

We consider two cases of images to construct the synthetic subset: for person-containing images (PCI), we use state-of-the-art face swapping and face attribute editing models to generate realistic forgeries; for non-person images (NPI), we synthesize data via full-image generation, style transfer, and image inpainting, yielding diverse, in-the-wild–like samples.

\vartriangleright

Face Swap Manipulation (PCI).
Face swapping has had substantial negative impacts on social media, particularly undermining the credibility of images depicting public figures and politicians.
We adopt SimSwap
Chen et al. 2020
and E4S
Li et al. 2023b
for face swap.
Given a source–target pair
\{I_{s},I_{t}\}
, the source image
I_{s}
is sampled from our dataset, while the target image
I_{t}
is randomly selected from CelebA–HQ
Zhu et al. 2022
.
We detect faces in both images using the DSFD detector
Li et al. 2019
, select the highest confidence face pair, and perform landmark based alignment and standardized cropping to confine the swap to the facial region.
Conditioned on an identity embedding extracted from
I_{s}
, the swapping model renders the source identity onto the target face while preserving the target’s pose, expression, illumination, and non facial context; the generated face is seamlessly blended back into
I_{t}
to produce the final swapped image.

\vartriangleright

Face Attribute Manipulation (PCI).
Face attribute editing is another common form of facial manipulation on social media, often used to disseminate misleading emotional content.
We employ the HFGI model
Wang et al. 2022
to edit multiple attributes, including expression changes (e.g.,
smile
and
sad
), age, lips, and eyes.
Given an input image
I
, we first detect the most prominent face
F
using the DSFD detector
Li et al. 2019
, then perform rotation-based alignment and standardized cropping to obtain the face patch
F_{c}
and facial landmarks
B
.
The patch
F_{c}
is fed into HFGI
Wang et al. 2022
with the selected attribute to produce the edited face
F_{e}
, which is pasted back to the original image guided by landmarks
B
with seamless blending.
For
expression edits
, we use an emotion classifier
Padgett & Cottrell 1996
to determine whether the original affect is positive or negative and apply reverse emotion editing accordingly .
For other facial attributes (e.g., age, eyes), we feed the aligned face into HFGI
Wang et al. 2022
and edit along the corresponding latent attribute direction. The edit magnitude is controlled by an editing-strength factor
\alpha
; larger
\alpha
yields stronger effects.
This produces high-quality swap samples representative of in-the-wild attacks.

Full image generation (NPI).
Rapid advances in text-to-image generation have flooded social media with photorealistic AI images, complicating authenticity checks.
To address this, we design a text-driven tampering pipeline: given an original news image, we first use the large vision–language model Qwen-2.5-VL
Bai et al. 2025
to produce a detailed textual description
T
.
We then filter low-quality descriptions to ensure that
T
captures key elements (content, background, color, composition, perspective, lighting).We compute the CLIP cosine similarity between the prompt
T
and the generated image, retaining only pairs with similarity
\geq 0.7
.
Finally, the validated description
T
is fed into SD-XL
Podell et al. 2023
to synthesize a new news image
\hat{I}_{n}
as the tampered sample.

\vartriangleright
Object/Background Replacement (NPI).

Local inpainting replacement is pervasive on social media, where many forged news images alter only a small region to mislead viewers.
Given an input image
I
, we first use Qwen-2.5-VL
Bai et al. 2025
to produce prompt phrases for the
r
most salient objects, forming a set
\{n_{j}\}_{j=1}^{r}
(with
r=3
by default).
We then randomly select one description
n_{x}
as the target and feed
(n_{x},I)
into SAM2
Ravi et al. 2024
to obtain the corresponding object mask
M
.
For local tampering, we adopt an inpainting pipeline based on SDXL Inpainting
Podell et al. 2023
: a language model converts the source object phrase
p_{\text{src}}=n_{x}
into a modified phrase
p_{\text{mod}}
by altering its type, color, or attribute while keeping unrelated context unchanged; the inpainting model takes
(I,M,p_{\text{mod}})
and synthesizes the edited image.
For subsequent identification and evaluation of manipulated regions, we store the mask
M
and the prompt pair
(p_{\text{src}},p_{\text{mod}})
as annotations.

Text Fabrication.

On social media, text manipulation can rapidly and easily produce misleading content that distorts facts and fabricates nonexistent news. We consider two manipulation strategies to modify the textual modality.
For both strategies, if the text contains
named entities
(e.g., personal or place names), we preserve these key tokens to retain contextual coherence and realism.

\vartriangleright

Pure fake text generation.

We take the original news headline
T
and apply SpaCy NER
Honnibal 2017
to detect named entities; if present,
they are stored as
\mathcal{E}=\{e_{j}\}_{j=1}^{r}
and kept unchanged.
We then feed
T_{i}
together with
\mathcal{E}
into Qwen3
Yang et al. 2025
, prompting the model to produce a

misleading
fake-news headline that preserves all entity tokens while rewriting the remaining
content; if no entities are detected, the
entire
headline is rewritten.
Then, we obtain a rewritten headline. This strategy enables comprehensive semantic-level falsification of the text while retaining critical information.

\vartriangleright

Key words/phrase Distortion.

We generate misleading content by replacing key adjectives in the headline.
Specifically, we apply SpaCy
Honnibal 2017
to the original title
T
to detect and preserve named entities,
yielding the set
\mathcal{E}=\{e_{j}\}_{j=1}^{r}
, and extract candidate keywords
to form
\mathcal{K}=\{k_{j}\}_{j=1}^{m}
.
We then feed
T_{i}
,
\mathcal{E}
, and
\mathcal{K}
into Qwen3
Yang et al. 2025
with a prompt that keeps all
entities unchanged while either randomly selecting 2–3 items from
\mathcal{K}
or choosing short
phrases in the text for controlled replacements—using antonyms for adjectives –thus producing the partly rewritten headline
T
.
Throughout this process, we log each replaced keyword
k_{x}
or phrase
p_{x}
and its position
P

to facilitate subsequent detection and analysis.

3.2
Dataset Statistics

With the above pipeline, we curate
OmniFake
, a multimodal fake-news corpus of
127,283 image–text pairs. Unlike prior sets that separate human-crafted misinformation
from AI-synthesized media and mostly adopt binary labels,
OmniFake
natively
supports
ternary
detection (Real / Human-crafted / AI-synthesized).
Beyond image-side edits, we generate text-side variants with LLMs to produce fluent yet
image-misaligned headlines—an underexplored but realistic challenge.
The corpus spans multiple domains (politics, health, people, society,
etc
.)
and multiple platforms, promoting broad coverage and cross-domain generalization.
It also covers a wide manipulation spectrum, including full-image generation, local
inpainting/region edits, style transfer, face swap and attribute edits, and text rewriting
(keyword/phrase substitutions). To our knowledge,
OmniFake
is the first
large-scale multimodal benchmark to jointly offer multi-class labels and such breadth
of manipulations for unified detection.

4
UMFDet Model

4.1
Architecture

We fine-tune Florence-2 (0.7B)
Xiao et al. 2024a
as the backbone to balance efficiency and effectiveness. We adapt the MLLM to multimodal fake news detection with a Category-aware Mixture of Experts(CMOE) based on Switch Transformer
Fedus et al. 2022
and augment it with an Attribution Chain-of-Thought (Att-COT). Following the MLLM paradigm, misinformation detection is formulated as question answering: the news image and title are formatted into a task prompt, and the model directly outputs the misinformation category. To capture category-specific cues, CMOE learns a prompt-conditioned routing function that dispatches features to the most relevant experts, while Att-COT provides implicit guidance for category reasoning and serves as an auxiliary output for category verification. Concretely, the image and text are first embedded and fused in a multimodal encoder, then routed through CMOE for expert encoding. The resulting feature is passed to the language decoder for answer generation and Att-COT reasoning. Finally, the network is optimized with both Att-COT language modeling and ground-truth answer supervision.

Image Embeddings.
Given input image
I
, we use DaViT
Ding et al. 2022
as our visual encoder, generating flattened visual token embeddings
E_{v}
, where
N_{v}
represents the number of tokens and
H_{v}
is the dimension of each token.

4.2
Instruction Design and Embedding

We build the instruction template as following:

T=[P_{\text{task}};\,P_{\text{opt}}(C);\,P_{\text{que}};\,P_{\text{resp}}],

(1)

where
P_{\text{task}}
specifies the goal,
P_{\text{opt}}(C)
lists the category set
and brief definitions,
P_{\text{title}}
define the question, and

P_{\text{resp}}
declares the expected answer format. The textual instruction is designed in the following format:

P_{\text{task}}
: Given the the news image and news title
(I,T)
to analyze which category the news belongs to.

P_{\text{opt}}(C)
: Define the three categories and guide the model.

P_{\text{que}}
: Define a question and have the model think and answer.

P_{\text{resp}}
: Finally, encapsulate the correct category.

After tokenization and embedding, we obtain
E_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{t}\times H}
.

4.3
Category-aware Mixture-of-Expert

Our Category-aware MoE (C-MoE) uses three experts, each aligned with one
information category (real, human-crafted, AI-synthesized). Given textual
embeddings
E_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{t}\times H}
and visual embeddings

E_{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{v}\times H}
(visual tokens are projected to width
H
),
we form the multimodal input by concatenation, which passes through language encoder for multimodal feature fusion:

X=L_{\text{enc}}([E_{v};E_{t}])\in\mathbb{R}^{(N_{v}+N_{t})\times H}

(2)

Subsequently, to capture category-specific features, we introduce a Category-aware Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) module composed of a router network and 3 experts. In particular, we define three experts as: Reality Expert
\mathcal{E}_{R}
, Deception Expert
\mathcal{E}_{D}
, and Synthesis Expert
\mathcal{E}_{S}
, each specialized for a distinct information category. Given a multimodal news representation
X
, the experts yield encodings
[\mathcal{E}_{R}(X),\mathcal{E}_{D}(X),\mathcal{E}_{S}(X)]
. Formally, each expert is a two-layer feed-forward network. W denotes the learnable weight matrices of the expert network:

\qquad\mathcal{E}(X)=\mathrm{Dropout}(y)\,W_{\text{out}}+b_{\text{out}},\quad y=u\odot v.\qquad

(3)

u=\mathrm{SiLU}(X\,W_{a}+b_{a}),\quad v=\mathrm{sigmoid}(X\,W_{b}+b_{b}).\quad

(4)

The router network, implemented as a linear layer with softmax activation, then selects the optimal expert index
e
as follows:

e=\underset{\{R,D,S\}}{\arg\max}\{\mathcal{W}_{R},\mathcal{W}_{D},\mathcal{W}_{S}\},\quad[\mathcal{W_{R},W_{D},W_{S}}]=\text{Softmax}(W\times X+b)

(5)

Finally, we pick the
\mathcal{E}_{e}(X)
and feed it into the language decoder for answer genneration. Language modeling loss is employed for training:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{Det}}=-\sum_{t=1}^{|Y|}\log p_{\theta}\!\big(y_{t}\,\big|\,y_{<t},\,X\big).

(6)

4.4
Attribution Chain-of-Though Guidance

CoT Generation.
Given a news sample with image–text–label triplet
(I,T,y)
, we produce an
attribution
chain of thought (CoT) in two stages before forming the final prompt.

Stage 1: Meta information extraction from text.

We first feed the news text
T
to an LLM
Qwen-3

Yang et al. 2025
to extract a set of meta entities

M=\{m_{1},\dots,m_{k}\}
covering persons, locations, organizations, and events or time.
For each
m_{i}
, the LLM generates a
entity description

K(m_{i})
that clarifies identity, role, time, and place with concise cues.
This yields a lightweight knowledge set
K(T)=\{K(m_{1}),\dots,K(m_{k})\}
summarizing the key entities in
T
.

Stage 2: Attribution CoT prompting.

To ensure the reasonings text aligning with the corresponding category, we explicitly integrate the annotation label into the prompt to guarantee the CoT-answer consistency. In specific,
our instruction asks the model to reason step by step why the news belongs to a category, grounding each step in visual and textual evidence and, in the entity descriptions:

P_{\text{Task}}
: Given
(I,T)
, use observable cues to justify the predicted category; reference entities from
M
and their descriptions
K(T)
.

P_{\text{Define}}
: Provide brief decision criteria for each category, including image–text consistency, visual manipulation traces, and text rewriting or generation cues; require the rationale to cite the criteria it uses.

P_{\text{Resp}}
: Output exactly <think>…</think><answer>…</answer>.

Detailed prompt is given in appendix.

Lightweight quality control.

We enforce a schema–constrained output.
A generation is kept only if: (i) the rationale contains two grounded spans,
one citing the image and one citing the text; (ii) the
<answer>
field
is a valid category and matches the ground–truth label of the sample; and
(iii) the
<think>
span is informative yet concise (token length in a
preset range) and every entity it mentions can be linked to
M
or appears in

T
. We regenerate up to
K
times on failure and discard otherwise.
This yields per–sample, traceable CoTs that attribute decisions to concrete
visual and textual evidence while keeping annotation overhead minimal.

Attribution CoT Guided Learning.

Subsequently, we utilize the generated CoT as the supervision and learn to reason the category prediction, thus validating the category prediction and reducing the uncertainty.
The raw news text is concatenated with text prompt via an instruction template and encoded to obtain the text embedding
E_{T}
.
This is fused with the image embedding
E_{V}
to form the joint representation
X
,the CoT rationale is used as supervision during training.

Think (reasoning):
an autoregressive cross-entropy over the CoT sequence that encourages structured, interpretable reasoning;

Answer (prediction):
a detection/classification objective
\mathcal{L}_{\text{Det}}
that ensures discriminative accuracy.

The CoT supervision loss is defined as

\mathcal{L}_{\text{cot}}=-\sum_{t=1}^{|Y|}\log p_{\theta}\!\big(y_{t}\,\big|\,y_{<t},\,X\big).

(7)

Minimizing
\mathcal{L}_{\text{cot}}
jointly optimizes reasoning generation and category prediction under a fixed CoT template. Finally, the full training objective reads:

\mathcal{L}\;=\;\mathcal{L}_{\text{Det}}\;+\;\lambda\,\mathcal{L}_{\text{cot}},

(8)

5.2
Ablation Study

Category-aware MoE.
We examine the effect of introducing category-aware mixture-of-experts, as shown in Tab.
6
(a).
Enabling the module consistently improves performance: accuracy increases from 84.30 to 86.67, precision from 85.01 to 86.80, recall from 84.33 to 86.67, and F1 from 84.42 to 86.73.
These correspond to gains of +2.37 in accuracy, +1.79 in precision, +2.34 in recall, and +2.31 in F1.
The results suggest that routing with category-aware experts mitigates cross-category interference and enhances representation capacity.

Depth of MoE.
We examine the effect of increasing the number of MoE layers, as shown in Tab.
6
(b).
Moving from one to two layers consistently improves performance:
accuracy rises from 86.60 to 86.67, precision from 86.64 to 86.80, recall from 86.60 to 86.67, and F1 from 86.61 to 86.73.
These correspond to gains of +0.07 in accuracy, +0.16 in precision, +0.07 in recall, and +0.12 in F1.
The results suggest that a deeper expert hierarchy enhances representation capacity while maintaining stability.

CoT Guidance.
We further evaluate the impact of chain-of-thought guidance, as shown in Tab.
6
(c).
Activating the guidance yields steady gains: accuracy rises from 84.30 to 85.52, precision from 85.01 to 87.52, recall from 84.33 to 85.52, and F1 from 84.42 to 85.21,
which are improvements of +1.22 in accuracy, +2.51 in precision, +1.19 in recall, and +0.79 in F1.
These results indicate that explicit reasoning guidance improves decision-making consistency and calibration during inference.

6
Conclusion

In this work, we tackle the challenge of detecting both human-crafted and AI-generated multimodal misinformation. We introduce OmniFake, a large-scale benchmark of 127K samples, and propose UMFDet, a unified detection framework that combines a VLM backbone, a Category-aware MoE adapter, and an attribution chain-of-thought mechanism. Through extensive experiments, we demonstrate that UMFDet achieves robust and generalized performance, surpassing specialized baselines and providing a practical solution for real-world multimodal deception detection.
