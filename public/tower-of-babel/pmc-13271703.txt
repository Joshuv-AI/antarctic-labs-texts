To Assess the Effect of Intraperitoneal Cut Suture Ends in Pediatric Minimally Invasive Surgery – A Study in Wistar Rats
Authors: Sandesh Parelkar, Sonal Jitendra Malviya, Beeejal Sanghvi, Rahul Kumar Gupta, Kedar Mudkhedkar, Rujuta Shah, Deepa Makhija
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground:Advanced minimal access surgery involves extensive intracorporeal suturing and results in a lot of cut suture ends in the operative field. The time and energy spent in retrieving these can be saved in case these cut ends can be left behind safely.Aim:The aim of this study was to assess the effect of intraperitoneal cut suture ends in pediatric minimally invasive surgery, a study in wistar rats.Materials and Methods:This is an experimental model in which Wistar rats were subjected to laparotomy, and cut suture ends were placed intraperitoneally. Eighteen adult Wistar rats were divided in three groups with 6 rats in each group. Suture used in Group 1 braided absorbable suture material (polyglycolic acid), Group 2 braided nonabsorbable suture material (polyester), and in Group 3 nonbraided nonabsorbable suture material (monofilament polyamide). Depending upon their group, suture material was introduced and left intraperitoneally under anesthesia. The abdominal wall of rats in all groups was closed in a single layer with nonbraided absorbable suture material (poliglecaprone 25). All animals were sacrificed after 4 weeks. The degree of inflammatory reaction in the peritoneal cavity was assessed.Results:There were no morbidity or mortality. The rate of adhesions found in the Group II (4 out of 6) was significantly higher (P < 0.0001) than the rate observed in the Group I (2 out of 6) and Group III (none out of 6).Conclusion:This pilot study concludes that depending on the type of suture materials used, braided suture materials preferably be removed. We can safely leave behind the cut ends of suture materials intraperitoneally. This will help save time and effort during advanced and prolonged laparoscopic surgeries. Further studies, including study of histology to quantify, are needed.

INTRODUCTION

Retained surgical items (RSIs) or objects are any instruments, tools, or surgical gauze unintentionally left in the body cavity at the completion of surgery. Advanced minimal access surgery involves multiple intracorporeal suturing and results in lot of cut suture ends in the operative field. A lot of time and energy is spent in retrieving these ends. The purpose of this study is to assess the effect of the formation of peritoneal adhesions by the cut suture ends due to their potential to induce foreign body reaction or alter the intensity of inflammatory response caused by surgical trauma. It is an experimental study in Wistar rats with three different types of commonly used suture materials in pediatric minimally invasive surgery (MAS).

MATERIALS AND METHODS

We are sharing the results of the pilot study being conducted in our institute regarding the study to assess the effects of intra-peritoneal cut suture ends the RSIs in wistar rats taking permission from the animal ethics committee and considering the medical, medicolegal and ethical aspects with due respect to all the animals and with the co-operation of dedicated center for animal studies and department of pharmacology and therapeutics. Permission of the Institutional Animal Ethics Committee and Human Ethics Committee of Seth G.S. Medical College and K.E.M Hospital, Mumbai was taken before commencement of the study. Animals were bred in the Center for Animal Studies of the Institute for the study. The study was conducted in accordance with the Committee for the Purpose of Control and Supervision of Experiments on Animals guidelines. We have used Wistar rats as an experimental model as they are most commonly used animal in research because of its easy availability, ease and feasibility of procedure in wistar rats, the results can be extrapolated to humans as they share the physiology, they readily survive in some of the most adverse conditions, they have a low cost of breeding with high survival rates. Four weeks of rats is equivalent to 2.5 human years, and because of the previously established scientific data this is an experimental model in which adult Wistar rats underwent laparotomy and 3 different types of cut suture ends of approximately 1 cm length were placed intraperitoneally in right iliac fossa to study the intraperitoneal reaction. We also consulted statistician to decide the number of rats to be used in the study to be statistically significant, 5 rats were used in each group, but we used 1 rat extra in each group because we were not sure of the survival of the rats for 4 weeks in study. Hence 18 rats were divided in three groups with 6 in each group.

The animals were segregated in separate cages with labels of suture material inserted on their cages for 4 weeks. General anaesthesia was administered to all the rats with an intraperitoneal injection of Ketamine and Xylazine. Under aseptic precautions, exploratory laparotomy was performed on them through a long median xiphoid-umbilical abdominal incision. 1 cm length of respective suture material was introduced in the right iliac fossa. The abdominal wall was closed in a single layer. They were given postoperative analgesics (meloxicam) and antibiotics (enrofloxacin) for 5 days. They were given food from the day of surgery itself, as there was no bowel handling in the surgery. Postoperatively taken care by dedicated animal laboratory team in the center for animal studies (postoperative analgesics and antibiotics for 5 days). Isolated-separate cages in enrichment medium in rice husk bedding with proper labels. All animals were sacrificed at 4 weeks, and the presence or absence of adhesions secondary to inflammatory reaction in the peritoneal cavity was noted. The Fischer’s exact test for 2 × N tables was used to statistically compare the three groups with regards to the presence of adhesions.

DISCUSSION

RSIs refer to any items left in a patient after any surgery or procedure that were intended to be removed. It has vast impact on patient’s quality of life, safety, and health care cost. It can lead to pain, patients suffering and re-surgeries. MAS is not devoid of similar complications of RSI. This study was conducted to find the consequences of cut suture ends left behind which is a norm in complex reconstructive MAS.

Complex and reconstructive advanced Paediatric MAS involves multiple intracorporeal suturing. It results in a lot of cut suture ends in the operative field. These cut suture ends are by definition classified as RSI. Other RSIs can be broken needles, blades of broken hand instruments, especially when the size of hand instruments in paediatric MAS is decreasing in size as per requirement of smaller children. Even if detected on the table, retrieving these RSIs can be difficult, time-consuming exercise without knowing the consequences if left behind. Hence, the index study was undertaken with the most commonly used suture materials in paediatric MAS, which were polyglycolic acid, polyester and monofilament polyamide. Wistar rats were used in our study. None of the cut suture ends of the used suture materials had any significant intra-peritoneal adhesions; only flimsy omental adhesions were seen that too with polyglycolic acid and polyester cut suture ends. Thus, cut suture ends of monofilament polyamide can safely be left behind without any fear of any consequences. Polyglycolic acid cut suture ends should be removed as much as possible as they induce some adhesions. Polyester cut suture ends should definitely be removed meticulously. These findings may guide future selection of suture materials. Larger studies with all types of suture material will be required to consolidate these findings, along with histological confirmation.

Furthermore, during MAS, surgeons insert and use devices that can have multiple parts, some of which are not radiopaque. Manufacturers cannot always place a radiopaque marker on plastic or rubber items, so when these multipart devices are removed, it is the job of the surgical team to recognize that a part may be missing and speak up so a search can be undertaken. It is important for the surgical team to have a transparent system in place to help track items since memory alone is a weak forcing function.

On an extensive literature search, incidences of needle loss and instrument malfunctions or breakage in adults during MAS are a known complication, but there are very few documented reports on needle loss and other incidences of RSI during pediatric MAS, including both laparoscopy and robotics. Our department had past experience of a broken needle and broken blade of a 2 mm hand instrument during laparoscopy, which were removed successfully but with great difficulty.[1] Lot of time was spent to retrieve both the RSIs details of which are given.[2] In a survey of 305 adult and pediatric surgeons, 64% reported managing a lost needle during MAS at some point in their career.[3] A second study found that device malfunction resulting in pieces of broken equipment falling into the patient occurred in 14.7% of reported adverse events.[4] Other studies have proposed algorithms for lost needles; however, these protocols are designed for MAS in nonurologic, adult cases.[35] Managing a lost object during paediatric laparoscopy and robotic surgery presents additional challenges due to the use of smaller instruments and needles, a decreased area for instrument manipulation, and lower insufflation pressures. With increasing popularity of advanced Paediatric MAS including use of Robotics, these RSIs will be a common occurrence. More and more centres should share their experience regarding avoidable RSIs.

Medina et al., proposed a strategical approach through a series of systematic steps for the surgical team to efficiently locate and retrieve the lost needle or any radiopaque RSI in MAS.[5] They recommended examination of the surgical field, trocar visualization, trocar disassembly, revision of the suction device in case needles of 4-0 or smaller are used in laparoscopic reconstructive surgeries with intraoperative or postoperative imaging of the surgical field of the patient if needed.[5]

There are multiple studies and randomised clinical trials conducted to assess the effects of different sutures on tissue healing and intraperitoneal adhesions. Gupta et al.[6] in a randomised clinical trial compared the efficacy of two suture materials, i.e., monofilament (poliglecaprone) suture and multifilament (polyglactin) suture as subcuticular skin stitches in post-cesarean women. They found that the number of patients having wound discharge, induration and wound dehiscence was significantly more in a group where multifilament (polyglactin) suture was used as compared to monofilament (poliglecaprone) suture. When wound healing was reviewed 2 months after surgery, excellent healing was seen in a group where monofilament (poliglecaprone) suture was used. Similarly, in our study, we found no intra-peritoneal adhesions in a group III where monofilament (polyamide) suture was used suggesting less inflammatory foreign-body reaction to nonabsorbable monofilament polyamide and hence less risk of intraperitoneal adhesions if these small cut suture ends are left behind in reconstructive Paediatric MAS.

A similar study was conducted by Urvashi Vats et al.[7] in a randomized clinical trial to compare the efficacy of three suture materials: monofilament absorbable poliglecaprone 25, multifilament absorbable polyglactin 910, and monofilament nonabsorbable polyamide as subcuticular skin stitches in postcesarean women. They found swelling, indurations, wound discharge and dehiscence maximum in patients with the use of absorbable multifilament polyglactin group. With only patient discomfort seen in 30% of the patients with the use of monofilament polyamide on day-10, wound healing was excellent in 93.3% of the patients in poliglecaprone group and 86.6% in polyamide group both being monofilament sutures. This suggest that monofilament sutures whether absorbable poliglecaprone or nonabsorbable polyamide develops less foreign body reaction and hence less risk of postoperative adhesions. This also aligns with our study that monofilament polyamide cut suture ends does not develop any intra peritoneal adhesions as seen in our study because of inbuilt less tissue reactivity properties, if left behind, but histological confirmation is needed to further validate our findings.

García-González et al.[8] conducted a single-arm, prospective, observational study in Germany and Spain involving patients treated under daily clinical practice using nylon sutures for oral mucosal closure after various dental surgical interventions. Intraoperative handling of the suture material appearance was excellent. The application of the nylon suture material led to a low postoperative complication rate combined with high patient satisfaction, an outstanding wound healing assessment by the oral surgeon and a low retention of microbiota. Our findings indicate that a nylon/polyamide-based suture material is safe and optimal for different dental surgeries to close the mucosa.

Cut suture ends being RSI and a foreign body can also act as a source of infection. Multiple clinical trials and both prospective and retrospective studies are conducted on different suture materials and their colonization by different micro-organisms. Dragovic et al. found significantly lower quantity of microorganisms on monofilament sutures compared to multifilament ones, with the least bacterial amount observed on polypropylene suture material. They analyzed a total of four different suture materials (nonabsorbable multifilament, nonabsorbable monofilament, absorbable multifilament, and absorbable monofilament) applied in oral surgery regarding biocompatibility, bacterial colonization, inflammatory reaction, and influence on wound healing. Their findings agreed with studies showing a lower microbial amount on nylon and polytetrafluoroethylene sutures compared to silk. The authors conclude that, whenever possible, monofilament synthetic sutures should be used to obtain the best soft tissue healing and to reduce the risk of postoperative infection.[9]

As far as the histological analysis of the effects of different suture material is concerned, Firat Selvi et al.[10] investigated in male rats the healing differences in between four different widely used suture materials in the oral maxillofacial surgery practice, including silk, polypropylene, coated polyglactin 910 and polyglecaprone 25 and reported less foreign body reaction with the use of polypropylene as being a nonabsorbable monofilament suture. The pitfall of this study was that only skin wounds were evaluated with no oral mucosa wounds tested. Similarly in our study we reported no evidence of any intraperitoneal adhesions with the use of monofilament polyamide which suggests less foreign body reaction to monofilament polyamide, but histological confirmation to document minimal foreign-body reaction with the use of monofilament polyamide is needed.

CONCLUSION

This pilot study concludes that cut suture ends of braided nonabsorbable multifilament materials as seen in different studies and polyester in our study cause more foreign-body reaction, more colonisation by micro-organisms, more infection rates and hence cause intra-peritoneal adhesions in our study should preferably be removed. Cut ends of monofilament suture materials as validated in different studies has less foreign-body reaction, less colonization by micro-organisms and less infection rate had caused no intraperitoneal adhesions in our study and could be left behind intraperitoneally and should not be included in RSI. This will help save time and effort during advanced and prolonged laparoscopic surgeries. Further studies, including study of histology to quantify, are needed. This study adds to the existing literature, especially in the pediatric age group as no other study conducted has assessed different types of suture materials and their effects on intraperitoneal adhesions in the pediatric population, hence it can be extrapolated.

Limitations

The limitations of our study are a small sample size, histological analysis is needed and long-term multi-institutional meta-analysis studies are also needed to further validate our findings. A negative control (laparotomy without suture insertion) would have helped establish baseline adhesion formation from surgical trauma alone.
