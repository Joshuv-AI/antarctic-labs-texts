Splicing skew shaped positroids
Eugene Gorsky, Soyeon Kim, Tonie Scroggin, José Simental

Abstract
. Skew shaped positroids (or skew shaped positroid varieties) are certain Richardson varieties in the flag variety that admit a realization as explicit subvarieties of the Grassmannian Gr ⁡ ( k , n ) \mathrm{Gr}(k,n) . They are parametrized by a pair of Young diagrams μ ⊆ λ \mu\subseteq\lambda fitting inside a k × ( n − k ) k\times(n-k) -rectangle. For every a = 1 , … , n − k a=1,\dots,n-k , we define an explicit open set U a U_{a} inside the skew shaped positroid S λ / μ ∘ S^{\circ}_{\lambda/\mu} , and show that U a U_{a} is isomorphic to the product of two smaller skew shaped positroids. Moreover, U a U_{a} admits a natural cluster structure and the aforementioned isomorphism is quasi-cluster in the sense of Fraser. Our methods depend on realizing the skew shaped positroid as an explicit braid variety, and generalize the work of the first and third authors for open positroid cells in the Grassmannian.



1.
Introduction

In this paper, we study cutting and gluing operations for skew shaped positroid varieties, extending the constructions in
[
17
]
for the maximal positroid cell.

1.1.
Main results

Let
\mu\subseteq\lambda
be partitions fitting inside a
k\times(n-k)
rectangle. The skew diagram
\lambda/\mu
defines the
skew shaped positroid
(or skew shaped positroid variety)
S_{\lambda/\mu}^{\circ}
which can be thought of as the intersection of a Schubert cell in the complete flag variety
\mathcal{F}\ell(n)
labeled by
\mu
and an opposite Schubert cell labeled by
\lambda
, see Definition
3.2.5
. Skew shaped positroids can be thought of as special cases of open Richardson
[
18
]
or positroid varieties
[
19
]
. Crucially, even though the skew shaped positroid is a Richardson variety in the full flag variety, it can also be defined inside the Grassmannian
\mathrm{Gr}(k,n)
. Thus we can, and for the most part will, think of
S_{\lambda/\mu}^{\circ}
as being a subvariety of the Grassmannian
\mathrm{Gr}(k,n)
. When
\mu=\varnothing
then skew shaped positroid is an open Schubert variety, considered in
[
28
]
.

Remark 1.1.1
.

While the skew shaped positroid
S^{\circ}_{\lambda/\mu}
is defined in a similar way to the skew Schubert varieties of
[
28
]
, when
\mu\neq\varnothing
the variety
S^{\circ}_{\lambda/\mu}
is generally not a skew Schubert variety. The variety
S^{\circ}_{\lambda/\mu}
is closely related to Grassmannian Richardson varieties, see Remark
3.2.7
.

Skew shaped positroids admit a cluster structure coming from source labels of plabic graphs, established in work of Galashin–Lam
[
12
]
following earlier work of Leclerc
[
21
]
and Serhiyenko–Sherman-Bennett–Williams
[
28
]
; see Section
4
for more details.

Using the lattice path labeling of a skew Young diagram described in Section
3.3
, we explicitly define the Grassmann necklace of
S_{\lambda/\mu}^{\circ}
based on the boundary ribbon of the skew diagram
\lambda/\mu
in Section
3.4
.

The main construction of the paper concerns cutting the skew diagram
\lambda/\mu
into two. Choose an integer
a
such that
1\leq a\leq n-k
.
Let
\lambda^{a,R}
be the partition obtained by considering only the first
a-1
columns (from the right) of
\lambda
, and define
\mu^{a,R}
similarly. Note that
\mu^{a,R}\subseteq\lambda^{a,R}
are partitions fitting inside a
k\times(a-1)
-rectangle, so we can consider the skew shaped positroid
S_{\lambda^{a,R}/\mu^{a,R}}^{\circ}\subseteq\mathrm{Gr}(k,k+a-1)
.

Now let
\lambda^{a,L}
be the partition obtained by considering the
a,\ldots,n-k
-columns (from the right) of
\lambda
, and similarly for
\mu^{a,L}
. Note that
\mu^{a,L}\subseteq\lambda^{a,L}
fit inside a
k\times(n-k-a+1)
-rectangle, so we can consider the skew shaped positroid
S_{\lambda^{a,L}/\mu^{a,L}}^{\circ}\subseteq\mathrm{Gr}(k,n-a+1)
. We are ready to state the main result of the paper.

Theorem 1.1.2
.

Let
n>0
and
0<k<n
. Let
\mu\subseteq\lambda
be partitions fitting inside a
k\times(n-k)
-rectangle. Choose
1\leq a\leq n-k
, and let
U_{a}
be the principal open set in
S_{\lambda/\mu}^{\circ}
defined by the non-vanishing of the cluster variables in the
a
-th column of
\lambda/\mu
(from the right), see Definition
5.1.1
. Then, we have an isomorphism

\Phi_{a}:U_{a}\xrightarrow{\sim}S_{\lambda^{a,L}/\mu^{a,L}}^{\circ}\times S_{\lambda^{a,R}/\mu^{a,R}}^{\circ}.

Furthermore,
\Phi_{a}
is a quasi-cluster equivalence.

In the theorem above,
quasi-cluster equivalence
means that the map
\Phi_{a}^{\ast}:\mathbb{C}[S^{\circ}_{\lambda^{a,L}/\mu^{a,L}}\times S^{\circ}_{\lambda^{a,R}/\mu^{a,r}}]\to\mathbb{C}[U_{a}]
sends cluster variables to cluster variables, up to nicely behaved monomials in frozen variables, see Section
4.1
. In particular,
\Phi_{a}^{\ast}
preserves cluster monomials, and the map
\Phi_{a}
sends cluster tori to cluster tori.

In
[
22
]
, Muller defines a
cluster chart
of a cluster variety to be the open set obtained by localizing some cluster variables in a single cluster, provided some technical conditions are satisfied, see e.g.
[
22
, Lemma 3.2]
. Theorem
1.1.2
, then, provides examples of cluster charts in skew shaped positroids that are themselves isomorphic to products of skew shaped positroids. We expect that this will allow for inductive methods to, for example, compute the cohomology
[
27
]
or deep locus
[
7
]
of skew shaped positroids. We remark, however, that outside of some special cases, the open sets
U_{a}
do not cover the skew shaped positroid
S^{\circ}_{\lambda/\mu}
.

We describe the construction of the map
\Phi_{a}
and prove Theorem
1.1.2
in Section
5
. Instead of going into details in the introduction, we chose to illustrate the complexity of
\Phi_{a}
in an example.

Sometimes, the cluster variables in the

-th column of the skew diagram

Corollary 1.1.4
.

Assume that all boxes in the

-th column from the right in

R(\lambda/\mu)
(see Definition

). Then
U_{a}
coincides with the whole skew shaped positroid
S_{\lambda/\mu}^{\circ}
, and we have the isomorphism

S_{\lambda/\mu}^{\circ}\simeq S_{\lambda^{a,R}/\mu^{a,R}}^{\circ}\times S_{\lambda^{a,L}/\mu^{a,L}}^{\circ}.

Remark 1.1.5
.

One can define and study the compositions of the splicing maps
\Phi_{a}
as follows. Suppose that

\lambda/\mu
, then we can draw two vertical cuts, one between
a
-th and
(a-1)
-th columns, and another between
b
-th and
(b-1)
-th columns. After performing these cuts we obtain three pieces: left

\lambda^{a,M}/\mu^{a,M}
, and right

Now, we can make an initial cut at

S_{\lambda/\mu}^{\circ}\xleftarrow{\Phi_{a}^{-1}}S_{\lambda^{a,L}/\mu^{a,L}\cup\lambda^{a,M}/\mu^{a,M}}^{\circ}\times S_{\lambda^{a,R}/\mu^{a,R}}^{\circ}\xleftarrow{\Phi_{b-a+1}^{-1}\times\mathrm{Id}}S_{\lambda^{a,L}/\mu^{a,L}}^{\circ}\times S_{\lambda^{a,M}/\mu^{a,M}}^{\circ}\times S_{\lambda^{a,R}/\mu^{a,R}}^{\circ}.

On the other hand, if we make the initial cut at

, then follow with a cut at

-th and

-th columns of
\lambda/\mu
. However, we expect that the maps do not coincide but differ by some explicit monomials in frozen cluster variables on

.

1.2.
Braid varieties

The key insight for the construction of the map
\Phi_{a}
comes from the construction of
braid varieties

[
3
,
4
]
. A braid variety
X(\beta)
is an affine algebraic variety which can be associated to a positive braid
\beta
on
k
strands. It parametrizes sequences of complete flags in
\mathbb{C}^{k}
with relative positions governed by the crossings in
\beta
, see Definition
2.4.1
for more details. Braid varieties have a cluster structure constructed in
[
2
,
14
,
13
]
.

Given a skew diagram
\lambda/\mu
, we define a
k
-strand braid
\beta_{\lambda/\mu}
, see Section
3.5
. The crossings in
\beta_{\lambda/\mu}
are in bijection with the boxes in
\lambda/\mu
, except for the top boxes in each column.

Example 1.2.1
.

For
\lambda=(7,7,5,3,1),\mu=(3,1)
we get the braid

\beta_{\lambda/\mu}=(\sigma_{4}\sigma_{3})(\sigma_{3}\sigma_{2})(\sigma_{3}\sigma_{2})(\sigma_{2}\sigma_{1})(\sigma_{2}\sigma_{1})(\sigma_{1})(\sigma_{1}).

The braid is shown in
blue
and the crossed out boxes are not part of it.

Remark 1.2.2
.

Note that all boxes in the
k
-th (topmost) row of
\lambda/\mu
are crossed out and not used in the braid
\beta_{\lambda/\mu}
. In fact, the topmost strand of
\beta_{\lambda/\mu}
goes right below the topmost row of
\lambda/\mu
.

We construct an explicit isomorphism between the skew shaped positroid and a certain braid variety. This is inspired by the constructions of
[
3
,
5
,
30
]
but we do not check if our isomorphism coincides with either of these.

Theorem 1.2.3
.

We have an isomorphism of algebraic varieties

(1.1)

S_{\lambda/\mu}^{\circ}\cong X\left(\underline{w_{0}}\beta_{\lambda/\mu}\right)\times(\mathbb{C}^{\times})^{s}.

Here
\underline{w_{0}}
is the half-twist braid on
k
strands and
s
is the number of crossed out boxes. Moreover, (
1.1
) is a quasi-cluster equivalence.

We can visualize Theorem
1.2.3
by the diagram

(1.2)

\Omega(V)=\left[\mathcal{F}^{W}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\underline{w_{0}}}}{{\dashrightarrow}}\mathcal{F}^{W^{\mathrm{op}}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\beta_{\lambda/\mu}}}{{\dashrightarrow}}\mathcal{F}^{0}\right],

where
\mathcal{F}^{W}
and
\mathcal{F}^{W^{\mathrm{op}}}
are a specific pair of opposite flags, and dotted arrows encode sequences of flags in
\mathbb{C}^{k}
. All the subspaces in all these flags can be defined as spans of some explicit subsets of columns of
V
, see Figure
5
for an example. Our construction of both the isomorphism
\Omega
and its inverse uses only elementary linear algebra, avoiding the use of microlocal sheaves
[
30
]
or the Chekanov–Eliashberg DGA
[
3
]
, or marking points in the braid closure of
\underline{w_{0}}\beta_{\lambda/\mu}

[
5
]
.

Next, we can describe the analogue of the map
\Phi_{a}
for braid varieties. We factor the braid
\beta_{\lambda/\mu}
as
\beta_{\lambda/\mu}=\beta_{L}\beta_{R}
where
\beta_{L}
and
\beta_{R}
are two braids corresponding to
\lambda^{a,L}/\mu^{a,L}
and
\lambda^{a,R}/\mu^{a,R}
, respectively. This allows us to expand (
1.2
) to the diagram

(1.3)

\Omega(V)=\left[\mathcal{F}^{W}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\underline{w_{0}}}}{{\dashrightarrow}}\mathcal{F}^{W^{\mathrm{op}}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\beta_{L}}}{{\dashrightarrow}}\mathcal{F}^{a}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\beta_{R}}}{{\dashrightarrow}}\mathcal{F}^{0}\right],

where
\mathcal{F}^{a}
is a specific flag on the boundary between
\beta_{L}
and
\beta_{R}
, refer to (
5.2
) for further details.

The key observation (Lemma
5.1.4
) is that the open subset
U_{a}\subset S_{\lambda/\mu}^{\circ}
corresponds under the isomorphism (
1.1
) to the set of sequences of complete flags (
1.3
) such that the flag
\mathcal{F}^{a}
is transversal to
\mathcal{F}^{W}
.
This allows us to define a map

(\Omega^{L},\Omega^{R}):U_{a}\rightarrow X\left(\underline{w_{0}}\beta_{R}\right)\times X\left(\underline{w_{0}}\beta_{L}\right)

given by

(1.4)

\Omega^{L}(V)=\left[\mathcal{F}^{W}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\underline{w_{0}}}}{{\dashrightarrow}}\mathcal{F}^{W^{\mathrm{op}}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\beta_{L}}}{{\dashrightarrow}}\mathcal{F}^{a}\right],\quad\Omega^{R}(V)=\left[\mathcal{F}^{W}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\underline{w_{0}}}}{{\dashrightarrow}}\mathcal{F}^{a}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\beta_{R}}}{{\dashrightarrow}}\mathcal{F}^{0}\right].

By using (
1.1
) again and taking into account the
\mathbb{C}^{\times}
factors, we arrive at our construction of the map
\Phi_{a}
. This argument shows that
\Phi_{a}
is indeed well defined, and is an isomorphism of algebraic varieties. In Section
5
, we prove that this construction of
\Phi_{a}
is consistent with the linear algebra construction of
V^{L}=(w_{1},\ldots,w_{n-a+1})
and
V^{R}=(u_{1},\ldots,u_{a+k-1})
, alluded to in Example
1.1.3
, and provide a precise definition of the latter.

To prove that
\Phi_{a}
is a quasi-isomorphism of cluster structures, we use the triangularity of
u_{i}
with respect to
v_{i}
and compare the corresponding minors in Lemma
5.4.6
. The computation of exchange ratios is then fairly straightforward (yet tedious), and is done in Lemmas
5.4.7
and
5.4.8
. This step does not require braid varieties.

To summarize, the most technically challenging part of the paper is to construct the element
V^{R}=(u_{1},\dots,u_{k+a-1})
and verify that it indeed represents an element in the skew shaped positroid
S^{\circ}_{\lambda^{a,R}/\mu^{a,R}}
.

In the forthcoming paper
[
16
]
, we use the cluster structure on braid varieties
[
2
,
14
,
29
]
to study more general splicing maps for braid varieties and to prove that these are cluster quasi-equivalences. This would give an alternative proof that
\Phi_{a}
is a quasi-equivalence.

2.
Background

2.1.
Flags and framed flags

We will work with the variety
\mathcal{F}\ell(k)
of complete flags in the
k
-dimensional space
\mathbb{C}^{k}
. Given a matrix
M=(m_{1},m_{2},\cdots,m_{k})\in\mathrm{GL}(k)
with columns
m_{1},\dots,m_{k}
, its associated flag is

\mathcal{F}(M):=\left(\{0\}\subseteq\langle m_{1}\rangle\subseteq\langle m_{1},m_{2}\rangle\subseteq\cdots\subseteq\langle m_{1},\dots,m_{k}\rangle=\mathbb{C}^{k}\right).

Note that
\mathcal{F}(M)=\mathcal{F}(MU)
for any upper triangular matrix
U
, so that we have an identification

\mathcal{F}\ell(k)=\mathrm{GL}(k)/\mathsf{B}(k),

where
\mathsf{B}(k)\subseteq\mathrm{GL}(k)
is the Borel subgroup of upper triangular matrices.

Let
\mathsf{U}(k)\subseteq\mathsf{B}(k)
be the subgroup of upper triangular matrices with
1
’s on the diagonal. The variety of
framed flags
is the space

\mathcal{F}\ell^{\mathrm{fr}}(k):=\mathrm{GL}(k)/\mathsf{U}(k).

An element of
\mathcal{F}\ell^{\mathrm{fr}}(k)
is known as a framed flag – it is the data of a flag
\mathcal{F}\in\mathcal{F}\ell(k)
, together with a choice of a nonzero element in
\mathcal{F}_{i}/\mathcal{F}_{i-1}
for all
i=1,\dots,k
(referred to as a framing). Forgetting the framing gives rise to a natural map
\pi:\mathcal{F}\ell^{\mathrm{fr}}(k)\to\mathcal{F}\ell(k)
. As above, every element
M\in\mathrm{GL}(k)
gives rise to a framed flag, that we will denote by
\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{fr}}(M)
.

2.2.
Relative position

Let
w\in S_{k}
. Abusing the notation, we denote by
w\in\mathrm{GL}(k)
the corresponding permutation matrix. In particular, we have the flag (resp. framed flag)
\mathcal{F}(w)
(resp.
\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{fr}}(w)
). The
standard flag

\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{std}}
is the flag
\mathcal{F}(e)
, where
e\in S_{k}
is the identity, and the
antistandard flag

\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{ant}}
is the flag
\mathcal{F}(w_{0})
, where
w_{0}\in S_{k}
is the longest element. The standard and antistandard framed flags are defined analogously.

Note that we have an action of the group
\mathrm{GL}(k)
both on
\mathcal{F}\ell(k)
and
\mathcal{F}\ell^{\mathrm{fr}}(k)
. We say that two flags
\mathcal{F}^{1},\mathcal{F}^{2}\in\mathcal{F}\ell(k)
are in relative position
w\in S_{k}
if there exists an element
g\in\mathrm{GL}(k)
such that

g\mathcal{F}^{1}=\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{std}},\qquad g\mathcal{F}^{2}=\mathcal{F}(w).

We will write
\mathcal{F}^{1}\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\longrightarrow}\limits^{w}}\mathcal{F}^{2}
to express that
\mathcal{F}^{1}
and
\mathcal{F}^{2}
are in relative position
w
. It is easy to verify that
\mathcal{F}^{1}\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\longrightarrow}\limits^{w}}\mathcal{F}^{2}
if and only if for every
i,j=1,\dots,k
we have

\dim(\mathcal{F}^{1}_{i}\cap\mathcal{F}^{2}_{j})=\big|\{1,\dots,i\}\cap\{w(1),\dots,w(j)\}\big|.

In particular, for a simple reflection
s_{j}\in S_{k}
, we have that
\mathcal{F}^{1}\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\longrightarrow}\limits^{s_{j}}}\mathcal{F}^{2}
if and only if
\mathcal{F}^{1}_{j}\neq\mathcal{F}^{2}_{j}
, but
\mathcal{F}^{1}_{i}=\mathcal{F}^{2}_{i}
for every
i\neq j
.

The notion of relative position for framed flags is slightly more complicated. We say that two framed flags
(\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{fr}})^{1}
and
(\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{fr}})^{2}
are in
strong relative position

w
if there exists
g\in\mathrm{GL}(k)
such that

g(\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{fr}})^{1}=(\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{fr}})^{\mathrm{std}},\qquad g(\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{fr}})^{2}=\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{fr}}(w).

As before, we write
(\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{fr}})^{1}\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\longrightarrow}\limits^{w}}(\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{fr}})^{2}
if the framed flags
(\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{fr}})^{1}
and
(\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{fr}})^{2}
are in strong relative position
w
.

In particular, consider two framed flags
\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{fr}},(\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{fr}})^{\prime}
with underlying flags
\mathcal{F}=\pi(\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{fr}}),\mathcal{F}^{\prime}=\pi((\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{fr}})^{\prime})
. Then
\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{fr}}\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\longrightarrow}\limits^{s_{i}}}(\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{fr}})^{\prime}
if and only if
\mathcal{F}\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\longrightarrow}\limits^{s_{i}}}\mathcal{F}^{\prime}
and the framing on
(\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{fr}})^{\prime}
is determined by the framing on
\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{fr}}
as follows.
Let

\mathcal{L}_{j}=\mathcal{F}_{j}/\mathcal{F}_{j-1}
and
\mathcal{L}^{\prime}_{j}=\mathcal{F}^{\prime}_{j}/\mathcal{F}^{\prime}_{j-1}
for
j=1,\ldots,n
. Then we have canonical isomorphisms

(2.1)

\mathcal{L}_{i}\simeq\mathcal{L}^{\prime}_{i+1},\ \mathcal{L}_{i+1}\simeq\mathcal{L}^{\prime}_{i},\ \mathcal{L}_{j}\simeq\mathcal{L}^{\prime}_{j}\ (j\neq i,i+1).

Indeed, the following composition is an isomorphism whenever
\mathcal{F}\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\longrightarrow}\limits^{s_{i}}}\mathcal{F}^{\prime}
:

\mathcal{L}_{i}=\mathcal{F}_{i}/\mathcal{F}_{i-1}\hookrightarrow\mathcal{F}_{i+1}/\mathcal{F}_{i-1}=\mathcal{F}^{\prime}_{i+1}/\mathcal{F}^{\prime}_{i-1}\rightarrow\mathcal{F}^{\prime}_{i+1}/\mathcal{F}^{\prime}_{i}=\mathcal{L}^{\prime}_{i+1}.

The other isomorphisms are constructed similarly. The framing on
(\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{fr}})^{\prime}
is then determined by the framing on
\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{fr}}
using these isomorphisms.

We also say that the framed flags
(\mathcal{F}^{r})^{1}
and
(\mathcal{F}^{r})^{2}
are in
weak relative position
w
if there exists
g\in\mathrm{GL}(k)
and a diagonal matrix
t
such that

g(\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{fr}})^{1}=(\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{fr}})^{\mathrm{std}},\qquad g(\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{fr}})^{2}=t\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{fr}}(w).

We write
(\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{fr}})^{1}\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\Longrightarrow}\limits^{w}}(\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{fr}})^{2}
to express this relationship. Note that
(\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{fr}})^{1}\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\longrightarrow}\limits^{w}}(\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{fr}})^{2}
implies
(\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{fr}})^{1}\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\Longrightarrow}\limits^{w}}(\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{fr}})^{2}
but the converse is not true. Also, note that
(\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{fr}})^{1}\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\Longrightarrow}\limits^{w}}(\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{fr}})^{2}
if and only if the corresponding flags
\pi((\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{fr}})^{1})
and
\pi((\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{fr}})^{2})
are in relative position
w
.

2.3.
Transversality

We say that two flags
\mathcal{F}^{1}
and
\mathcal{F}^{2}
are transversal, and write
\mathcal{F}^{1}\pitchfork\mathcal{F}^{2}
, if
\mathcal{F}^{1}\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\longrightarrow}\limits^{w_{0}}}\mathcal{F}^{2}
. Note that this is the case if and only if

\mathcal{F}^{1}_{i}\cap\mathcal{F}^{2}_{k-i}=\{0\},\qquad\text{equivalently}\qquad\mathcal{F}^{1}_{i}+\mathcal{F}^{2}_{k-i}=\mathbb{C}^{k}

for every
i=1,\dots,k
. By definition, if
\mathcal{F}^{1}\pitchfork\mathcal{F}^{2}
then there exists a matrix
g\in\mathrm{GL}(k)
such that
g\mathcal{F}^{1}=\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{std}}
and
g\mathcal{F}^{2}=\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{ant}}
, and this matrix is unique up to left multiplication by an element of
\mathsf{B}(k)\cap w_{0}\mathsf{B}(k)w_{0}
, i.e., a diagonal matrix. Equivalently, one can say that there exists a basis
u_{1},\dots,u_{k}
of
\mathbb{C}^{k}
such that

\mathcal{F}^{1}=\left(\langle u_{1}\rangle\subseteq\langle u_{1},u_{2}\rangle\subseteq\cdots\subseteq\mathbb{C}^{k}\right)\qquad\text{and}\qquad\mathcal{F}^{2}=\left(\langle u_{k}\rangle\subseteq\langle u_{k},u_{k-1}\rangle\subseteq\cdots\subseteq\mathbb{C}^{k}\right),

such a basis is unique up to scalar multiplication.

For framed flags, we say that
(\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{fr}})^{1}
and
(\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{fr}})^{2}
are transversal, and again write
(\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{fr}})^{1}\pitchfork(\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{fr}})^{2}
, if
(\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{fr}})^{1}\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\Longrightarrow}\limits^{w_{0}}}(\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{fr}})^{2}
(note that we are using
weak relative position
here). In this case, there exists a matrix
g\in\mathrm{GL}(k)
and a diagonal matrix
t
such that
g(\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{fr}})^{1}=(\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{fr}})^{\mathrm{std}}
and
g(\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{fr}})^{2}=t(\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{fr}})^{\mathrm{ant}}
. Since
\mathsf{U}(k)\cap w_{0}\mathsf{B}(k)w_{0}=\{1\}
, such a matrix
g
is unique. If we have framed flags
(\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{fr}})^{1}
and
(\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{fr}})^{2}
that are in strong relative position
w_{0}
, we say that
(\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{fr}})^{1}
and
(\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{fr}})^{2}
are strongly transversal.

2.4.
Braid varieties

Now we are ready to define braid varieties.

Definition 2.4.1
.

Let
\beta=\sigma_{i_{1}}\cdots\sigma_{i_{r}}\in\mathrm{Br}_{k}^{+}
be a positive braid word.
The braid variety
X(\beta)
is defined as the space of configurations of flags

(2.2)

\mathcal{F}^{0}\xrightarrow{s_{i_{1}}}\mathcal{F}^{1}\xrightarrow{s_{i_{2}}}\ldots\xrightarrow{s_{i_{r}}}\mathcal{F}^{r}.

such that
\mathcal{F}^{0}
is standard and
\mathcal{F}^{r}
is antistandard.
We will often abbreviate (
2.2
) as
\mathcal{F}^{0}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\beta}}{{\dashrightarrow}}\mathcal{F}^{r}
.

Following
[

,
6
]
we will often use the following equivalent version of Definition

. Given the braid diagram of
\beta
drawn horizontally, we encode a point in the braid variety

by labeling all regions by subspaces of
\mathbb{C}^{k}
as follows:

(a)

The unbounded region below
\beta
is labeled by
0
, and the unbounded region above
\beta
is labeled by
\mathbb{C}^{k}
.

(b)

If a subspace

labels a region directly above one labeled by a subspace

, then

and
\dim W=\dim V+1
.

(c)

At a crossing in

,

and

(d)

On the left boundary of

and on the right boundary of

we get the flag
\mathcal{F}^{r}=\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{ant}}

See Figure

Furthermore, we can think of elements of

as configurations of framed flags

(2.3)

(\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{fr}})^{0}\xrightarrow{s_{i_{1}}}(\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{fr}})^{1}\xrightarrow{s_{i_{2}}}\ldots\xrightarrow{s_{i_{r}}}(\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{fr}})^{r}.

such that

is transversal to

Lemma 2.4.2
.

Let
\underline{w_{0}}
be the positive braid lift of

.
The braid variety

can be equivalently described as the space of following data, up to simultaneous left action of

Proof.

Part (a) follows from the definition. Part (b) follows from (a) since

is equivalent to strong transversality of

and
(\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{fr}})^{1}

where we denote by

and

, respectively. Finally, if we are given the data as in (c) then the basis

3.
Skew shaped positroid varieties

3.1.
Positroid varieties

First, we recall equivalent ways of describing positroid varieties in the Grassmannian: Grassmann necklaces and bounded affine permutations.

Definition 3.1.1
.

A
(k,n)
-source Grassmann necklace

\mathcal{I}=(I_{1},I_{2},\dots,I_{n})
is an
n
-tuple of
k
-element subsets
I_{i}\subseteq[n]
where

•

if
i\in I_{i}
then there exists
j\in[n]
such that
I_{i-1}=(I_{i}\setminus\{i\})\cup\{j\}
, and

•

if
i\not\in I_{i}
then
I_{i}=I_{i-1}
.

We denote by
\mathsf{GN}(k,n)
the set of
(k,n)
-Grassmann necklaces.

Remark 3.1.2
.

Note that if
i\in I_{i}
then it may be that
I_{i-1}=I_{i}=(I_{i}\setminus\{i\})\cup\{i\}
. Finally, note that
I_{i-1}\setminus I_{i}
is either empty or a singleton.

Remark 3.1.3
.

Grassmann necklaces were first defined in
[
24
]
. Our definition is slightly different, as we use
I_{i-1}
instead of
I_{i+1}
, this difference is indicated by the word
source
in the name.

Definition 3.1.4

(
[
19
]
)
.

A bijection
f:\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{Z}
is called a
(k,n)
-bounded affine permutation
if it satisfies the following conditions:

(1)

f(i+n)=f(i)+n
for every
i\in\mathbb{Z}
.

(2)

For every
i\in\mathbb{Z}
,
i\leq f(i)\leq f(i)+n
.

(3)

We have:

\sum_{i=1}^{n}(f(i)-i)=nk.

Note that, by (1),
f
is uniquely determined by the values
f(1),\dots,f(n)
, which are pairwise distinct modulo
n
. We often simply denote
f=[f(1),\dots,f(n)]
. Note also that, upon the presence of (1) and (2), (3) is equivalent to
|\{i\in[n]:f(i)>n\}|=k
. We denote the set of
(k,n)
-bounded affine permutations by
\mathsf{BA}(k,n)
. We say that
f
is a
bounded
n
-affine permutation
if there exists
k
such that
f
is a
(k,n)
-bounded affine permutation.

Lemma 3.1.5
.

The sets
\mathsf{GN}(k,n)
and
\mathsf{BA}(k,n)
are in natural bijection.

In the proof, we will need the following definition.

Definition 3.1.6
.

We define the cyclic order
\leq_{i}
on the set
[n]
:
i<_{i}i+1<_{i}i+2<_{i}\cdots<_{i}n<_{i}1<_{i}\cdots<_{i}i-1
.

Proof of Lemma
3.1.5
.

This is essentially a combination of
[
19
, Corollary 3.13]
and
[
23
, Remark 2.4]
. For the reader’s convenience and to fix notation, we provide the bijection
\varphi:\mathsf{GN}(k,n)\to\mathsf{BA}(k,n)
. Let us first describe
\bar{f}_{\mathcal{I}}:=\varphi(\mathcal{I})\pmod{n}
through its inverse
\bar{f}_{\mathcal{I}}^{-1}
:
we have
\bar{f}^{-1}_{\mathcal{I}}(i)=i
if
I_{i-1}=I_{i}
, and
\bar{f}^{-1}_{\mathcal{I}}(i)=j
if
I_{i-1}\setminus I_{i}=\{j\}
. To lift this to a bounded affine permutation, we set
f_{\mathcal{I}}(i)=i+n
if
I_{i-1}=I_{i}
and
i\in I_{i}
, and
f_{\mathcal{I}}(i)=i
if
i\not\in I_{i}
.
On the other hand, if
f:\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{Z}
is a
k
-bounded affine permutation, let
I=\{i\in[n]\mid f(i)=i+n\}
, and
\bar{f}=f\pmod{n}
. Then
I_{i}
consists of
I
together with the elements
\{a\in[n]\mid a<_{i}\bar{f}(a)\}
. The collection
\mathcal{I}_{f}=(I_{1},\dots,I_{n})
is a Grassmann necklace, and
f\mapsto\mathcal{I}_{f}
,
\mathcal{I}\mapsto f_{\mathcal{I}}
are inverse bijections. ∎

Now, we associate a Grassmann necklace and a bounded affine permutation to an element

in the Grassmannian
\mathrm{Gr}(k,n)
following
[
19
,
24
]
. We represent

by a

matrix of rank

v_{1},\ldots,v_{n}\in\mathbb{C}^{k}
the columns of

. Furthermore, we define
v_{i}
for all

by
setting
v_{i+n}:=(-1)^{k-1}v_{i}
.
Given an (ordered)

-element subset

\Delta_{J}=\Delta_{J}(V)=v_{j_{1}}\wedge\cdots\wedge v_{j_{k}}
the maximal minor of

Definition 3.1.7
.

(
[
23
]
)

Note that the order

naturally extends to a partial order on the set of

\{j_{1}<_{i}j_{2}<_{i}\cdots<_{i}j_{k}\}\leq_{i}\{\ell_{1}<_{i}\ell_{2}<_{i}\cdots<_{i}\ell_{k}\}\qquad\text{if}\qquad j_{s}\leq_{i}\ell_{s}\;\text{for every}\;s=1,\dots,k.

Given this ordering, the

In other words,

is the maximal element, with respect to the

.

Remark 3.1.8
.

Note that the order

is the cyclic ordering where

Remark 3.1.9
.

Here we have used the source Grassmann necklace. Other works in the literature use

3.2.

We fix

Definition 3.2.5
.

Recall that
\mathsf{B}(n)
is the Borel subgroup of upper triangular matrices in

,
and
\mathsf{B}_{-}(n)
the opposite Borel subgroup of lower triangular matrices.
Given two partitions

as the intersection of
\mathsf{B}(n)w_{\mu}\mathsf{B}(n)/\mathsf{B}(n)
and
\mathsf{B}_{-}(n)w_{\lambda}\mathsf{B}(n)/\mathsf{B}(n)

Since

and

is a

-bounded affine permutation, where

\Pi^{\circ}_{w_{\lambda},w_{\mu}}
.

Remark 3.2.6
.

The skew shaped positroid

is, by definition, the open Richardson variety
R_{w_{{\lambda}},w_{\mu}}^{\circ}\subset\mathcal{F}\ell(n)
. It is known
[

that under the projection map
\mathcal{F}\ell(n)\to\mathrm{Gr}(k,n)
, the open Richardson variety
R_{w_{\lambda},w_{\mu}}^{\circ}
is isomorphic to the open positroid variety
\Pi_{w_{\lambda},w_{\mu}}^{\circ}
.

Remark 3.2.7
.

The skew shaped positroid
S^{\circ}_{\lambda/\mu}
differs from the skew Schubert varieties studied in
[
28
]
. The variety
S^{\circ}_{\lambda/\mu}
is closely related to Grassmannian Richardson varieties
[
8
]
as follows. Let
\mathsf{P}=\mathsf{P}(k,n)\subseteq\mathrm{GL}(n)
be the parabolic subgroup of block-upper triangular matrices, with diagonal blocks of sizes
k
and
n-k
. It is known and easy to show that

\mathrm{Gr}(k,n)=\mathrm{GL}(n)/\mathsf{P}.

For an element
w\in S_{n}
, the Grassmannian Schubert cell (resp. opposite Grassmannian Schubert cell) is the subvariety

\mathsf{B}(n)w\mathsf{P}/\mathsf{P}\subseteq\mathrm{Gr}(k,n)\qquad\text{(resp.}\qquad\mathsf{B}_{-}(n)w\mathsf{P}/\mathsf{P}\text{).}

These depend only on the coset

and not on
w
itself. In particular, we can take minimal-length coset representatives, which are precisely the
k
-Grassmannian permutations. If
w_{\lambda},w_{\mu}
are

-Grassmannian permutations, the Grassmannian Richardson variety is

\mathrm{G}R^{\circ}_{w_{\lambda},w_{\mu}}:=\left(\mathsf{B}_{-}(n)w_{\lambda}\mathsf{P}/\mathsf{P}\right)\cap\left(\mathsf{B}(n)w_{\mu}\mathsf{P}/\mathsf{P}\right)\subseteq\mathrm{Gr}(k,n).

The variety
\mathrm{G}R^{\circ}_{w_{\lambda},w_{\mu}}
is nonempty if and only if
w_{\lambda}\leq w_{\mu}
in Bruhat order, in which case it is an affine, smooth algebraic variety of dimension
\ell(w_{\mu})-\ell(w_{\lambda})
. In fact,
w_{\lambda}\leq w_{\mu}
if and only if
\mu\subseteq\lambda
, and in this case
\ell(w_{\mu})-\ell(w_{\lambda})=|\lambda/\mu|
. We will denote
\mathrm{G}R^{\circ}_{\lambda/\mu}:=\mathrm{G}R^{\circ}_{w_{\lambda},w_{\mu}}
.

The skew shaped positroid
S^{\circ}_{\lambda/\mu}
is a dense subvariety of the Grassmannian Richardson variety
\mathrm{G}R^{\circ}_{\lambda/\mu}
. Moreover, the Grassmannian Richardson variety
\mathrm{G}R^{\circ}_{\lambda/\mu}
may be expressed as the union of positroids

\mathrm{G}R^{\circ}_{\lambda/\mu}=\bigcup_{\begin{subarray}{c}w\leq w_{\mu}\\
w(S_{k}\times S_{n-k})=w_{\lambda}(S_{k}\times S_{n-k})\end{subarray}}\Pi^{\circ}_{w,w_{\mu}}

and
\dim(\Pi^{\circ}_{w,w_{\mu}})\leq\dim(\mathrm{G}R^{\circ}_{\lambda/\mu})
, with equality if and only if
w=w_{\lambda}
. Indeed, if
w(S_{k}\times S_{n-k})=w_{\lambda}(S_{k}\times S_{n-k})
, then the opposite Schubert cell in the flag variety
\mathsf{B}_{-}(n)w\mathsf{B}(n)/\mathsf{B}(n)
projects to the opposite Schubert cell
\mathsf{B}_{-}(n)w_{\lambda}\mathsf{P}/\mathsf{P}
in the Grassmannian, while the Schubert cell
\mathsf{B}(n)w_{\mu}\mathsf{B}(n)/\mathsf{B}(n)
projects to
\mathsf{B}(n)w_{\mu}\mathsf{P}/\mathsf{P}
so
\Pi^{\circ}_{w,w_{\mu}}\subseteq\mathrm{G}R^{\circ}_{\lambda/\mu}
. We also have
\ell(w_{\lambda})\leq\ell(w)
, with equality if and only if
w=w_{\lambda}
. We thank Melissa Sherman-Bennett for discussions pertaining to this remark.

3.4.
Grassmann necklace for a skew shaped positroid

We have seen in Remark
3.2.6
that the skew shaped positroid is in fact a positroid variety. In this subsection, we use the combinatorics from the previous subsection to explicitly describe the corresponding Grassmann necklace.
We define a Grassmann necklace associated to a skew diagram
\lambda/\mu
using the boundary ribbon

R(\lambda)=R(\lambda/\mu)\cup\overline{R}(\lambda/\mu)
from Definition
3.3.1
. Recall the definition of labels
I^{\prime}(a,i)
and
I_{\mu}
from Definition
3.3.6
.

Definition 3.4.1
.

Given a skew diagram
\lambda/\mu
, we define the corresponding Grassmann necklace
\mathcal{I}_{\lambda/\mu}
as follows:

•

If
\lambda_{1}<n-k
, we define
\mathcal{I}_{\lambda/\mu,1}=\cdots=\mathcal{I}_{\lambda/\mu,n-k-\lambda_{1}}=I_{\mu}
.

•

For a box
\square_{a,i}\in R(\lambda/\mu)
, we define
\mathcal{I}_{\lambda/\mu,a+i-1}=I^{\prime}(a,i)
.

•

For a box
\square_{a,i}\in\overline{R}(\lambda/\mu)
we define
\mathcal{I}_{\lambda/\mu,a+i-1}=I_{\mu}
.

•

If
\overline{\lambda}_{1}<k
, we define

\mathcal{I}_{\lambda/\mu,n-k+\overline{\lambda}_{1}}=\cdots=\mathcal{I}_{\lambda/\mu,n-1}=I_{\mu}
.

•

Finally,
\mathcal{I}_{\lambda/\mu,n}=I_{\mu}
.

Lemma 3.4.2
.

The collection of subsets
\mathcal{I}_{\lambda/\mu}
is in fact a Grassmann necklace.

Proof.

We first notice that
I^{\prime}(a,i)
and
I_{\mu}
all have the same cardinality
k
. For the steps between
I_{\mu}
and
I_{\mu}
, no additional verification is required. We now consider several cases:

Case 1a:
Horizontal step where both
\square_{a,i},\square_{a+1,i}
are in
R(\lambda/\mu)
. Here
i=\overline{\lambda}_{a}
, and by Lemma
3.3.12
we have
I^{\prime}(a+1,\overline{\lambda}_{a})=I^{\prime}(a,\overline{\lambda}_{a})\setminus\{a+\overline{\mu}_{a}\}\cup\{a+\overline{\lambda}_{a}\}
. Therefore

(3.6)

\mathcal{I}_{\lambda/\mu,a+\overline{\lambda}_{a}}=\mathcal{I}_{\lambda/\mu,a+\overline{\lambda}_{a}-1}\setminus\{a+\overline{\mu}_{a}\}\cup\{a+\overline{\lambda}_{a}\}.

Case 1b:
Horizontal step where
\square_{a,i}\in R(\lambda/\mu)
but
\square_{a+1,i}\in\overline{R}(\lambda/\mu)
. In this case, we similarly get

I_{\mu}=I^{\prime}(a,\overline{\lambda}_{a})\setminus\{a+\overline{\mu}_{a}\}\cup\{a+\overline{\lambda}_{a}\}
and (
3.6
) holds.

Case 1c:
From

to
I_{\mu}
.
If
\overline{\mu}_{n-k}<\overline{\lambda}_{n-k}
then
\mathcal{I}_{\lambda/\mu,n-k+\overline{\lambda}_{n-k}-1}=I^{\prime}(n-k,k)
and (similarly to Case 1) we get

(3.7)

\mathcal{I}_{\lambda/\mu,n-k+\overline{\lambda}_{n-k}}=I_{\mu}=\mathcal{I}_{\lambda/\mu,n-k+\overline{\lambda}_{n-k}-1}\setminus\{n-k+\overline{\mu}_{n-k}\}\cup\{n-k+\overline{\lambda}_{n-k}\}.

to

f=w_{\lambda}t_{k}w_{\mu}^{-1}
, while
w_{\lambda/\mu}=w_{\mu}w_{\lambda}^{-1}
and
t_{k}\pmod{n}
is the identity.
∎

]
.

Theorem 3.4.8
.

The skew shaped positroid
S_{\lambda/\mu}^{\circ}
is isomorphic to the open positroid variety
\Pi^{\circ}_{\mathcal{I}_{\lambda/\mu}}
.

Proof.

By definition, the skew shaped positroid
S_{\lambda/\mu}^{\circ}
is the open Richardson variety
R_{w_{\lambda},w_{\mu}}^{\circ}
. Since the permutation
w_{\mu}
is
k
-Grassmannian, the natural projection from the flag variety
\mathcal{F}\ell(n)
to
\mathrm{Gr}(k,n)
identifies
R_{w_{\lambda},w_{\mu}}^{\circ}
with its image, cf.
[
19
]
. By
[
19
]
, this projection identifies
R_{w_{\lambda},w_{\mu}}^{\circ}
with the positroid
\Pi_{f}^{\circ}
where

f=w_{\lambda}t_{k}w_{\mu}^{-1}
which agrees with

by Lemma
3.4.6

∎

Remark 3.4.9
.

Let
V
be a
(k\times n)
-matrix representing a point in the positroid
\Pi^{\circ}_{\mathcal{I}_{\lambda/\mu}}
. Since

is an element of the corresponding Grassmann necklace, we have that

for every element

3.5.
Skew shaped positroid as a braid variety

Following
[
3
, Lemma 2.5]
we define a
k
-strand braid
\beta_{\lambda/\mu}
associated to a skew diagram
\lambda/\mu
via the following procedure.

(1)

Cross out the top box of every column of
\lambda
.

(2)

For each column of
(n-k)^{k}
, put strands starting on the lower left corner of each box in this column, so that there are precisely
k
strands. We join the strands to corners in the right border of this column, as follows:

(a)

Join the strand starting in the lower left corner of the crossed box belonging to
\lambda/\mu
(from Step 1) in this column to the lower right corner of the lowest box in the same column belonging to
\lambda/\mu
. If the crossed box also belongs to
\mu
, then simply join the strand, in a straight line, to the lower right corner of the same box.

(b)

If a strand starts in the lower left corner of a non-crossed box in
\lambda/\mu
, join it to the upper right corner of the same box.

(c)

If a strand starts on the lower left corner of any box which is not of the above type, join it (in a straight line) to the lower right corner of the same box.

(3)

The braid
\beta_{\lambda/\mu}
is defined to be the concatenation of the
(n-k)
-braids from Step 2. In formulas,

(3.14)

\beta_{\lambda/\mu}=C_{1}C_{2}\cdots C_{n-k},\qquad C_{j}=\sigma_{\lambda_{j}^{t}-1}\sigma_{\lambda_{j}^{t}-2}\cdots\sigma_{\mu_{j}^{t}+1},

where
C_{j}
is the empty braid if
\mu_{j}^{t}+1>\lambda_{j}^{t}-1
.

See Example
1.2.1
for an example of
\beta_{\lambda/\mu}
.
We say that a box
\square\in\lambda/\mu
is a
braid box
if it is not the top row of its column.
By definition,
the braid boxes of
\lambda/\mu
are in bijection with the crossings of
\beta_{\lambda/\mu}
.

Next, we define a family of subspaces of
\mathbb{C}^{k}
labeled by the braid boxes of
\lambda/\mu
or, equivalently, by the regions of
\beta_{\lambda/\mu}
.

Definition 3.5.1
.

We define the subspace
V(a,i)=\spann\{v_{j}:j\in J(a,i)\}\subset\mathbb{C}^{k}
.

Lemma 3.5.2
.

We have
\dim V(a,i)=i
.

Proof.

We have
J(a,i)\subset J(a,\overline{\lambda}_{a})\subset I^{\prime}(a,\overline{\lambda}_{a})
by Corollary
3.3.13
, and
\Delta_{I^{\prime}(a,\overline{\lambda}_{a})}(V)\neq 0
by definition of the Grassmann necklace
\mathcal{I}_{\lambda/\mu}
. Therefore the vectors
\{v_{j}:j\in I^{\prime}(a,\overline{\lambda}_{a})\}
are linearly independent, and the vectors
\{v_{j}:j\in J(a,i)\}
are linearly independent as well.
∎

Lemma 3.5.3
.

We have containment

V(a,i)\subset V(a,i+1),\ V(a+1,i)\subset V(a,i+1).

Proof.

This follows from Lemma
3.3.12
.
∎

Lemma 3.5.4
.

Suppose that
\square_{a,i}
and
\square_{a+1,i}
are any two adjacent squares chosen from the
i
-th row of
R(\lambda/\mu)
. Then
V(a,i)=V(a+1,i)
.

Proof.

Assume that
\square_{a,i}
and
\square_{a+1,i}
are both in
R(\lambda/\mu)
. Lemma
3.3.12
implies that
J(a+1,i)
is obtained from
J(a,i)
by swapping
a+\overline{\mu}_{a}
by
a+\overline{\lambda}_{a}
. Combining Lemma
3.4.3
with Definition
3.1.7
, we have a containment

V(a+1,i)\subset V(a,i)
. Since
V(a,i)
and
V(a+1,i)
have the same dimension
i
, we can conclude that
V(a,i)=V(a+1,i)
.
∎

Example 3.5.5
.

In Example
3.3.11
we have
J(5,4)=\{5,6,7,8\}
and
J(6,4)=\{5,6,8,9\}
. Since
f(7)=9
, we have
v_{7}\in\langle v_{8},v_{9}\rangle
, so
V(5,4)=\langle v_{5},v_{6},v_{7},v_{8}\rangle\subset\langle v_{5},v_{6},v_{8},v_{9}\rangle=V(6,4)
. On the other hand,
\dim V(5,4)=\dim V(6,4)=4
, so
V(5,4)=V(6,4)
.

Lemma 3.5.6
.

Suppose that
\square_{a,i}
is not in
R(\lambda/\mu)
and
\square_{a+1,i}\in\lambda/\mu
. Then

V(a,i)\neq V(a+1,i).

Proof.

By Lemma
3.3.12
we get
J(a,i)\cup J(a+1,i)=J(a,i+1)
, so
V(a,i)+V(a+1,i)=V(a,i+1)
. If
V(a,i)=V(a+1,i)
then
V(a,i+1)=V(a,i)
which contradicts Lemma
3.5.2
.
∎

Definition 3.5.7
.

Let
I_{\mu}=\{b_{1},\ldots,b_{k}\}
as above, we define

W_{i}=\left\langle v_{b_{k-i+1}},\ldots,v_{b_{k}}\right\rangle,\quad W^{\mathrm{op}}_{i}=\left\langle v_{b_{1}},\ldots,v_{b_{i}}\right\rangle.

Lemma 3.5.8
.

The subspaces
W^{\mathrm{op}}_{i}
have the following properties:

a)

\dim W^{\mathrm{op}}_{i}=i
.

b)

Suppose that
\square_{a,i}\in\mu
but
\square_{a-1,i}\in\lambda/\mu
. Then
W^{\mathrm{op}}_{i}\subset V(a-1,i+1)
and
W^{\mathrm{op}}_{i}\neq V(a-1,i)
.

Proof.

Part (a) follows from
\Delta_{I_{\mu}}\neq 0
. For part (b), we first observe that

W^{\mathrm{op}}_{i}=\spann\left\{v_{j}\ :\ j\in\widetilde{J}(a,i)\right\},

so by Lemma
3.3.15
we get

W^{\mathrm{op}}_{i}+V(a-1,i)=V(a-1,i+1)
, in particular
W^{\mathrm{op}}_{i}\subset V(a-1,i+1)
. If
W^{\mathrm{op}}_{i}=V(a-1,i)
then
\dim V(a-1,i+1)=i
, contradiction.
∎

It is easy to see that the vector
v_{a+\overline{\mu}_{a}}
vanishes if and only if
\overline{\mu}_{a}=\overline{\lambda}_{a}
, that is, the
a
-th column of the skew diagram
\lambda/\mu
is empty (this includes the case
\overline{\mu}_{a}=\overline{\lambda}_{a}=0
). Indeed, by Lemma
3.4.3

f(a+\overline{\mu}_{a})=a+\overline{\lambda}_{a}
and by definition of the bounded affine permutation
f
the vector
v_{j}
vanishes if and only if
f(j)=j
. Below we will mostly focus on the case
\overline{\mu}_{a}<\overline{\lambda}_{a}
where
v_{a+\overline{\mu}_{a}}\neq 0
.

Lemma 3.5.9
.

For all
a
, if
\mu_{a}<\lambda_{a}
then the space

V(a,\overline{\mu}_{a}+1)\cap\left\langle v_{a+\overline{\mu}_{a}+1},\ldots,v_{a+\overline{\lambda}_{a}}\right\rangle

is one-dimensional and spanned by
v_{a+\overline{\mu}_{a}}
.

Proof.

We have
a+\overline{\mu}_{a}\in J(a,\overline{\mu}_{a}+1)
, so

v_{a+\overline{\mu}_{a}}\in V(a,\overline{\mu}_{a}+1)
. Furthermore, by Lemma
3.4.3
we have
f(a+\overline{\mu}_{a})=a+\overline{\lambda}_{a}
, so

v_{a+\overline{\mu}_{a}}\in\left\langle v_{a+\overline{\mu}_{a}+1},\ldots,v_{a+\overline{\lambda}_{a}}\right\rangle.

Next, observe that

J(a,\overline{\lambda}_{a})=J(a,\overline{\mu}_{a})\cup\left\{a+\overline{\mu}_{a}+1,\ldots,a+\overline{\lambda}_{a}-1\right\}

so

\displaystyle\dim\left[V(a,\overline{\mu}_{a}+1)+\left\langle v_{a+\overline{\mu}_{a}+1},\ldots,v_{a+\overline{\lambda}_{a}}\right\rangle\right]

\displaystyle\geq\dim\left[V(a,\overline{\mu}_{a}+1)+\left\langle v_{a+\overline{\mu}_{a}+1},\ldots,v_{a+\overline{\lambda}_{a}-1}\right\rangle\right]

\displaystyle=\dim V(a,\overline{\lambda}_{a})=\overline{\lambda}_{a}

Also,
\dim\left\langle v_{a+\overline{\mu}_{a}+1},\ldots,v_{a+\overline{\lambda}_{a}}\right\rangle\leq\overline{\lambda}_{a}-\overline{\mu}_{a}
.
Therefore

\dim\left[V(a,\overline{\mu}_{a}+1)\cap\left\langle v_{a+\overline{\mu}_{a}+1},\ldots,v_{a+\overline{\lambda}_{a}}\right\rangle\right]=\dim V(a,\overline{\mu}_{a}+1)+\dim\left\langle v_{a+\overline{\mu}_{a}+1},\ldots,v_{a+\overline{\lambda}_{a}}\right\rangle-

\dim\left[V(a,\overline{\mu}_{a}+1)+\left\langle v_{a+\overline{\mu}_{a}+1},\ldots,v_{a+\overline{\lambda}_{a}}\right\rangle\right]\leq\left(\overline{\mu}_{a}+1\right)+\left(\overline{\lambda}_{a}-\overline{\mu}_{a}\right)-\overline{\lambda}_{a}=1.

∎

Lemma 3.5.10
.

For all
a
, we have
v_{a+\overline{\mu}_{a}}\in W_{k-\overline{\mu}_{a}}
.

Proof.

We prove it by decreasing induction on
a
where
1\leq a\leq n-k
. Note that

W_{k-\overline{\mu}_{a}}=\left\langle v_{b_{\overline{\mu}_{a}+1}},\ldots,v_{b_{k}}\right\rangle=\spann\left\{v_{b_{i}}\mid b_{i}>a+\overline{\mu}_{a}\right\}.

By Lemma
3.4.3
we have
f(a+\overline{\mu}_{a})=a+\overline{\lambda}_{a}
, so

v_{a+\overline{\mu}_{a}}\in\left\langle v_{a+\overline{\mu}_{a}+1},\ldots,v_{a+\overline{\lambda}_{a}}\right\rangle.

For all
t
such that
a+\overline{\mu}_{a}+1\leq t\leq a+\overline{\lambda}_{a}
, we either have
t\in I_{\mu}
and then
v_{t}\in W_{k-\overline{\mu}_{a}}
, or
t=a^{\prime}+\overline{\mu}_{a^{\prime}}
for
a^{\prime}>a
and by the assumption of induction
v_{t}\in W_{k-\overline{\mu}_{a^{\prime}}}\subset W_{k-\overline{\mu}_{a}}
. Therefore
\left\langle v_{a+\overline{\mu}_{a}+1},\ldots,v_{a+\overline{\lambda}_{a}}\right\rangle\subset W_{k-\overline{\mu}_{a}}
and we are done.
∎

Lemma 3.5.11
.

For all
a
, if
\mu_{a}<\lambda_{a}
then the intersection

V(a,\overline{\mu}_{a}+1)\cap W_{k-\overline{\mu}_{a}}

is one-dimensional and spanned by
v_{a+\overline{\mu}_{a}}
.

Proof.

Let
L=V(a,\overline{\mu}_{a}+1)\cap W_{k-\overline{\mu}_{a}}
.
We have
J(a,\overline{\mu}_{a}+1)=\{b_{1},\ldots,b_{\overline{\mu}_{a}},a+\overline{\mu}_{a}\}
, so

V(a,\overline{\mu}_{a}+1)=W^{\mathrm{op}}_{\overline{\mu}_{a}}+\left\langle v_{a+\overline{\mu}_{a}}\right\rangle.

By Lemma
3.5.10
we have
v_{a+\overline{\mu}_{a}}\in V(a,\overline{\mu}_{a}+1)\cap W_{k-\overline{\mu}_{a}}
and
\dim L\geq 1
. On the other hand,
W^{\mathrm{op}}_{\overline{\mu}_{a}}\cap W_{k-\overline{\mu}_{a}}=0
, so
L\cap W^{\mathrm{op}}_{\overline{\mu}_{a}}=0
. At the same time,
L+W^{\mathrm{op}}_{\overline{\mu}_{a}}\subset V(a,\overline{\mu}_{a}+1)
, therefore
\dim L+\dim W^{\mathrm{op}}_{\overline{\mu}_{a}}\leq\dim V(a,\overline{\mu}_{a}+1)
and
\dim L\leq 1.

∎

Definition 3.5.12
.

We define
R^{1}(\lambda/\mu)\subset R(\lambda/\mu)

4.1.
Background on cluster algebras

Let us now recall the definition of a cluster algebra
[
9
]
. We restrict ourselves to the skew-symmetric case, as this is all that will be needed.

An
ice quiver
is a quiver (i.e., a directed graph, where we allow multiple arrows between vertices) with finite vertex set
Q_{0}
that has no loops nor directed
2
-cycles, and where a special subset
Q_{0}^{f}
of the vertices of
Q
is declared to be
frozen
. An element in
Q_{0}\setminus Q_{0}^{f}
is said to be a
mutable vertex
of
Q
.

Now let
Q
be an ice quiver, with
|Q_{0}|=n+m
, in such a way that there are
n
mutable vertices and
m
frozen ones. We consider a field
\mathcal{F}
of transcendence degree
n+m
over
\mathbb{C}
. A
seed

\Sigma=(Q,\mathbf{x})
consists of:

(1)

The ice quiver
Q
, and

(2)

A set
\mathbf{x}=\{x_{i}\mid i\in Q_{0}\}
that is a transcendental basis for
\mathcal{F}
, i.e.
\mathcal{F}=\mathbb{C}(x_{i}\mid i\in Q_{0})
. The set
\mathbf{x}
is known as the set of
cluster variables
of
\Sigma
.

Given a seed
\Sigma=(Q,\mathbf{x})
and a mutable vertex
k\in Q_{0}
, the
mutation
of
\Sigma
in the direction
k
is the seed
\mu_{k}(\Sigma)=(\mu_{k}(Q),\mu_{k}(\mathbf{x}))
where:

(1)

\mu_{k}(\mathbf{x})=(\mathbf{x}\setminus\{x_{k}\})\cup\{x^{\prime}_{k}\}
, where
x^{\prime}_{k}\in\mathcal{F}
is defined via the equation

x_{k}x_{k}^{\prime}=\prod_{i\to k}x_{i}+\prod_{k\to j}x_{j}.

(2)

\mu_{k}(Q)
has the same vertex set as
Q
, but the arrows change via the following three-step procedure:

(a)

Reverse all arrows incident with
k
.

(b)

For any pair of arrows
i\to k\to j
in
Q
insert a new arrow
i\to j
, unless both
i
and
j
are frozen.

(c)

The previous two steps may have created
2
-cycles. Remove all the arrows in a maximal collection of disjoint
2
-cycles.

Two seeds
\Sigma
and
\Sigma^{\prime}
are said to be
mutation equivalent
if there is a finite sequence of mutations taking one seed to the other. Since mutation at a fixed vertex
k
is an involutive operation, this is well-defined.

Definition 4.1.1
.

Let
\Sigma
be a seed. The
cluster algebra

\mathcal{A}(\Sigma)
is the subalgebra of the field
\mathcal{F}
generated by the sets of cluster variables in all seeds mutation equivalent to
\Sigma
, as well as by
x_{k}^{-1}
for
k\in Q_{0}^{f}
.
We say that a commutative algebra
\mathcal{A}

admits a cluster structure
if there exists a seed
\Sigma
such that
\mathcal{A}\cong\mathcal{A}(\Sigma)
. Similarly, we say that an affine algebraic variety
X
admits a cluster structure if the coordinate algebra
\mathbb{C}[X]
admits a cluster structure.

Crucially for this paper, a commutative algebra
\mathcal{A}
may admit more than one cluster structure, that is, there may exist two seeds
\Sigma,\Sigma^{\prime}
which are not mutation equivalent but such that
\mathcal{A}(\Sigma)\cong\mathcal{A}\cong\mathcal{A}(\Sigma^{\prime})
.

Let
\mathcal{A}(\Sigma)
and
\mathcal{A}(\Sigma^{\prime})
be the cluster algebras associated to the seeds
\Sigma
and
\Sigma^{\prime}
, respectively. Following Fraser
[
10
]
, see also
[
20
, Section 5.2]
, we define a class of particularly well-behaved morphisms between cluster algebras associated to seeds that are not necessarily mutation-equivalent.

For a seed
\Sigma
and a mutable vertex
i
, we define the exchange ratio
\widehat{y}_{i}
as the ratio

\widehat{y}_{i}=\dfrac{\prod_{j\rightarrow i}x_{j}^{\#\{j\rightarrow i\}}}{\prod_{i\rightarrow j}x_{j}^{\#\{i\rightarrow j\}}}.

Definition 4.1.2
.

[
10
,
11
]

Let
\mathcal{A}(\Sigma)
and
\mathcal{A}(\Sigma^{\prime})
be cluster algebras of rank
n+m
, both with
m
frozen variables. Let
\mathbf{x}=\{x_{1},\dots,x_{n+m}\}
be the cluster variables of
\Sigma
, and
\mathbf{x}=\{x^{\prime}_{1},\dots x^{\prime}_{n+m}\}
be the cluster variables of
\Sigma^{\prime}
. An algebra isomorphism
f:\mathcal{A}(\Sigma)\to\mathcal{A}(\Sigma^{\prime})
is said to be a
quasi-cluster isomorphism
if the following conditions are satisfied:

(1)

For each frozen variable
x_{j}\in\mathbf{x}
,
f(x_{j})
is a Laurent monomial in the frozen variables of
\mathbf{x}^{\prime}
.

(2)

For each mutable variable
x_{i}\in\mathbf{x}
,
f(x_{i})
coincides with
x^{\prime}_{i}
, up to multiplication by a Laurent monomial in the frozen variables of
\mathbf{x}^{\prime}
.

(3)

The exchange ratios are preserved, i.e., for each mutable variable
x_{i}
of
\Sigma
,
f(\widehat{y}_{i})=\widehat{y}^{\prime}_{i}
.

We remark that properties (1)–(3) are stable under mutations, i.e., if (1)–(3) holds for two seeds
\Sigma,\Sigma^{\prime}
, then it also holds for
\mu_{i}(\Sigma),\mu_{i}(\Sigma^{\prime})
for every mutable vertex
i
, see
[
20
, Section 5.2]
.

moves southwest until it reaches the boundary of
\mu
at which point the direction of the path reverses to either the north or east.

(a)

Initial turning rule:

•

If the edge
e_{i}
is horizontally oriented, then you make a left turn with respect to the orientation in the southern direction.

•

If the edge
e_{i}
is vertically oriented, then you make a right turn with respect to the orientation in the western direction.

•

Continue until you reach the boundary of
\mu
.

(b)

Behavior at the boundary of
\mu
:

•

Upon reaching an edge that is contained in the boundary of
\mu
, continue to follow the turning rule from step (a), until the next required step would be an edge contained in the interior of
\mu
.

•

At this point, reflect the direction of the new edge which would be contained in the interior of
\mu
.

(i)

If the rule would require you to take the horizontal edge in the western direction, then instead choose the horizontal edge in the eastern direction.

(ii)

Similarly, if the rule would require you to take the vertical edge in the southern direction, then instead choose the vertical edge in the northern direction.

(c)

Continuation of the path:

•

Continue straight until you reach some edge
e_{j}
for
j\in[n]\setminus{i}
.

(3)

Box labeling:

•

If the path
T_{i}
has a counterclockwise orientation, then label the boxes on the exterior of the path with the integer
i
, denote this set of boxes
X_{i}
.

•

If the path
T_{i}
has a clockwise orientation, then label the boxes on the interior of the path with the integer
i
, denote this set of boxes
N_{i}
.

Remark 4.3.3

If the path

satisfies step (b)(i) in Definition

4.4.
Cluster variable comparison

From Theorem
3.5.13
, the skew shaped positroid
S^{\circ}_{\lambda/\mu}
is very closely related to the braid variety
X(\underline{w_{0}}\beta_{\lambda/\mu})
, that also admits a cluster structure, cf.
[
2
,
29
]
. In this section, we briefly compare the cluster variables in both constructions.

An initial seed for the cluster structure on
X\left(\underline{w_{0}}\beta_{\lambda/\mu}\right)
is explicitly constructed in
[
29
]
, see
[
2
, Proposition 5.20]
. The cluster variables in
X\left(\underline{w_{0}}\beta_{\lambda/\mu}\right)
are in bijection with the crossings of the braid
\beta_{\lambda/\mu}
. Let
\beta_{\lambda/\mu}=\sigma_{i_{1}}\cdots\sigma_{i_{r}}
. If the flag right after the
j
-th crossing of
\beta_{\lambda/\mu}
is given by
(w_{1}|\cdots|w_{k})
then, up to a sign, the corresponding cluster variable is the
i_{j}
-th principal minor of the matrix
(w_{1}|\cdots|w_{k})
. Note that this coincides with the determinant of
(w_{1}|\cdots|w_{i_{j}}|e_{i_{j}+1}|\dots|e_{k})
, where
e_{1},\dots,e_{k}
is the standard basis of
\mathbb{C}^{k}
.

If
(v_{1}|\dots|v_{n})
is a matrix representing an element of
S^{\circ}_{\lambda/\mu}
, we may normalize so that
v_{b_{i}}=e_{i}
, where
I_{\mu}=\{b_{1}<\cdots<b_{k}\}
. Recall that, for a box
\square_{a,i}\in\lambda/\mu
, the corresponding cluster variable is
\Delta_{I^{\prime}(a,i)}
. From Proposition
3.3.10
,
I^{\prime}(a,i)=J(a,i)\cup\{b_{i+1},\dots,b_{k}\}
. Thus,

\Delta_{I^{\prime}(a,i)}(V)=\det(v_{j_{1}}|\cdots|v_{j_{i}}|v_{b_{i+1}}|\cdots|v_{b_{k}})=\det(v_{j_{1}}|\cdots|v_{j_{i}}|e_{i+1}|\cdots|e_{k}).

where
J(a,i)=\{j_{1},\dots,j_{i}\}
. Now, the space
V(a,i)
is spanned by the vectors
j_{1},\dots,j_{i}
, see Definition
3.5.1
. Since a braid box
\square_{a,i}\in\lambda/\mu
corresponds to a crossing between the
i
-th and
(i+1)
-st strands of
\beta_{\lambda/\mu}
we see that, upon setting the cluster variables corresponding to non-braid boxes equal to
1
, the cluster variables in the seed of
S^{\circ}_{\lambda/\mu}
given by Theorem
4.2.1
coincide, up to signs, with the cluster variables in the seed of
X\left(\underline{w_{0}}\beta_{\lambda/\mu}\right)
obtained in
[
29
]
.

Moreover, it follows by construction that, upon deleting the frozen vertices corresponding to non-braid boxes of
\lambda/\mu
, the quiver
Q_{\lambda/\mu}
coincides with the
opposite
of the quiver
Q_{X\left(\underline{w_{0}}\beta_{\lambda/\mu}\right)}
. Summarizing:

Theorem 4.4.1
.

Consider the isomorphism
\widetilde{\Omega}^{1}:S^{\circ,1}_{\lambda/\mu}\to X\left(\underline{w_{0}}\beta_{\lambda/\mu}\right)
. The dual map
\widetilde{\Omega}^{1,\ast}:\mathbb{C}\left[X\left(\underline{w_{0}}\beta_{\lambda/\mu}\right)\right]\to\mathbb{C}\left[S^{\circ,1}_{\lambda/\mu}\right]
sends, up to signs, the cluster variables in the initial seed of
X\left(\underline{w_{0}}\beta_{\lambda/\mu}\right)
from
[
29
]
to the cluster variables in the initial seed of
S^{\circ,1}_{\lambda/\mu}
given by Corollary
4.2.3
.

5.1.
Freezing a column

Let
S_{\lambda/\mu}^{\circ}
be the skew shaped positroid in the Grassmannian
\mathrm{Gr}(k,n)
.

Definition 5.1.1
.

Given
1\leq a\leq n-k
, we define the open subset
U_{a}\subset S_{\lambda/\mu}^{\circ}
by the inequalities

(5.1)

U_{a}:=\left\{V\in S_{\lambda/\mu}^{\circ}:\Delta_{I^{\prime}(a,i)}\neq 0,\text{ for }\overline{\mu}_{a}+1\leq i\leq\overline{\lambda}_{a}\right\}.

Given a skew diagram
\lambda/\mu
, we cut it along the boundary of the
(a-1)
-st and
a
-th column (from the right) where the
(a-1)
-st column is contained in the right part of the diagram and the
a
-th column is contained in the left part of the diagram.
We get two skew diagrams,
\lambda^{a,L}/\mu^{a,L}
on the left and
\lambda^{a,R}/\mu^{a,R}
on the right.

Remark 5.1.2
.

We can explicitly determine the resulting
\lambda
and
\mu
for the new diagrams using

\displaystyle\lambda^{a,L}_{i}=\begin{cases}n-k-a+1,&\text{if }\lambda_{i}>n-k-a,\\
\lambda_{i},&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

\displaystyle\mu^{a,L}_{i}=\begin{cases}n+k+a+1,&\text{if }\mu_{i}>n-k-a\\
\mu_{i},&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

\displaystyle\lambda^{a,R}_{i}=\begin{cases}\lambda_{i}-n+k+a-1,&\text{if }\lambda_{i}>n-k-a\\
0,&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

\displaystyle\mu^{a,R}_{i}=\begin{cases}\mu_{i}-n+k+a-1,&\text{if }\mu_{i}>n-k-a\\
0,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

It is clear from definitions that the corresponding braids satisfy

\beta_{\lambda/\mu}=\beta_{L}\beta_{R},\ \mathrm{where}\ \beta_{L}=\beta_{\lambda^{a,L}/\mu^{a,L}},\ \beta_{R}=\beta_{\lambda^{a,R}/\mu^{a,R}}.

Definition 5.1.3
.

Given
1\leq a\leq n-k
, we define the
flag at the cut

\mathcal{F}^{a}
as the following sequence of subspaces:

(5.2)

W^{\mathrm{op}}_{1}\subset\cdots\subset W^{\mathrm{op}}_{\overline{\mu}_{a}}\subset V\left(a,\overline{\mu}_{a}+1\right)\subset\cdots\subset V\left(a,\overline{\lambda}_{a}\right)\subset V\left(d_{\overline{\lambda}_{a}+1},\overline{\lambda}_{a}+1\right)\subset\cdots V(d_{k},k)

where
\left(d_{\overline{\lambda}_{a}+1},\overline{\lambda}_{a}+1\right),\ldots(d_{k},k)
are the rightmost boxes in rows
\overline{\lambda}_{a}+1,\ldots,k
as in (
3.3
).

One can check (see Example
5.1.5
below) that
\mathcal{F}^{a}
is the flag corresponding to the boundary between
\beta_{L}
and
\beta_{R}
under the map in Theorem
3.5.13
. In particular, (
5.2
) indeed defines a complete flag in
\mathbb{C}^{k}
.

Lemma 5.1.4
.

We have
V\in U_{a}
if and only if the flag
\mathcal{F}^{a}
is transversal to the flag
\mathcal{F}^{W}
.

Proof.

The two flags are transversal if, for all
i
, we have

\mathcal{F}^{a}_{i}+W_{k-i}=\mathcal{F}^{a}_{i}+\left\langle v_{b_{j+1}},\ldots,v_{b_{k}}\right\rangle=\mathbb{C}^{k}.

We have the following cases:

Case 1:
For
1\leq i\leq\overline{\mu}_{a}
, we have
\langle v_{b_{1}},\ldots,v_{b_{i}}\rangle+\langle v_{b_{i+1}},\ldots,v_{b_{k}}\rangle=\mathbb{C}^{k}
, this is automatic.

Case 2:
For
\overline{\mu}_{a}+1\leq i\leq\overline{\lambda}_{a}
, we have
I^{\prime}(a,i)=J(a,i)\cup\{b_{i+1},\ldots,b_{k}\}
and
V(a,i)=\spann J(a,i)
. Therefore
V(a,i)+W_{k-i}=\mathbb{C}^{k}
if and only if
\Delta_{I^{\prime}(a,i)}\neq 0
, these are precisely the conditions defining
U_{a}
.

Case 3:
For
\overline{\lambda}_{a}+1\leq i\leq k
, the box
\square_{d_{i},i}
belongs to the boundary ribbon
R(\lambda/\mu)
, so
\Delta_{I^{\prime}(d_{i},i)}\neq 0
and similarly to Case 2 we have

V(d_{i},i)+W_{k-i}=\mathbb{C}^{k}
.
∎

Example 5.1.5
.

In our running example, choose
a=6
. The subset
U_{a}
is given by one condition
\Delta_{5,6,8,9,12}\neq 0
. The flag
\mathcal{F}^{a}
is given by

\langle v_{5}\rangle\subset\langle v_{5},v_{6}\rangle\subset\langle v_{5},v_{6},v_{8}\rangle\subset\langle v_{5},v_{6},v_{8},v_{9}\rangle\subset\langle v_{5},v_{6},v_{8},v_{10},v_{11}\rangle,

while the flag

is given by

Note that
