Optimization of speech emotion recognition using hybrid dataset integration and deep learning-based feature fusion with a novel balanced focal entropy loss
Authors: Shimaa Nagro
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Speech Emotion Recognition is essential for human-computer interaction, as it enables the automated system to recognize the emotion of the individual from the speech. In order to improve the quantity, diversity, and quality of the speech dataset that deep learning algorithms use to identify the person’s emotions, this paper proposes a novel loss function, ‘Balanced_Focal_Entropy’, that is used to improve the performance of the unbalanced datasets by combining three separate open-source datasets. Two distinct datasets are used in the suggested method: the individual dataset and the hybrid dataset, which combines three distinct open-source datasets. The dataset is enhanced by the addition of noise, pitch shifting, and a combination of these techniques. Then, the four distinct handcrafted feature extraction techniques are employed to extract the features from the speech data, which are then combined to create a single set. These fused features are utilized to train the two deep learning models on the RAVDESS and hybrid datasets with the proposed loss function known as ‘Balanced_Focal_Entropy loss’. The RAVDESS dataset achieves 95.23% test accuracy, while the hybrid dataset reaches a maximum of 97.80%. Real-time application of the suggested method can be useful in various domains to accurately identify an individual’s emotion.

Introduction

Research on affective data mining and emotion identification aims to empower computers to identify, recognize, and articulate emotions1. Consequently, efforts to improve emotion recognition algorithms have significantly increased2. Speech Emotion Recognition (SER) is the method that uses audio features like pitch, energy, and tempo of the speaker from the speech data to determine the emotional State of the speaker3. Individuals go through multiple emotional states in different scenarios. Many other feelings an individual can face are from happiness to sadness, anger to shock, disapproval to anxiety, and mixed emotions45. These can be acknowledged from the speech signal’s vocal cues and auditory qualities6. In contrast to a sad voice, which typically has a lower pitch, less energy, and a slower speech tempo, a happy voice typically has a higher pitch, more energy, and a faster speech rate7. To accurately detect emotions in speech, these sound cues must be identified and examined.

Research in SER is essential since it enhances the capacity to understand and react to human emotions automatically using the applications developed in machine learning (ML), which results in narrowing the gap between machine and human interactions. SER, which allows computers to interact with human emotions through speech data, can be applied in various domains, including medical, customer service, and education. SER has a broad spectrum of uses in many different fields because of its ability to detect and react to human emotions immediately. During the customer’s feedback, SER’s job is to listen to their thoughts and feelings, understand whether the customer is happy or sad, and take action based on it to enhance the customer service. Like whether the caller is furious, pleased, or puzzled, real-time mood tracking allows call center operators and automated systems to alter their responses based on the caller’s mood8. SER is utilized in healthcare to identify philological cues that may indicate mental health concerns such as depression, anxiety, or stress; it could assist in recognizing these mental health issues9. SER systems in smart cars10 SER monitors the driver’s emotions to enhance safety and comfort in the vehicle. It is capable of picking up on signs of fatigue, anger, or stress, allowing it to send reminders for awareness or recommend taking breaks to help prevent accidents.

Furthermore, emotionally intelligent assistants can provide a smoother, user-focused experience by providing better work prioritizing and changing interaction techniques11. SER makes interactive games and virtual reality more immersive by letting characters or surroundings react to how players feel. When a player shows confidence or changes the story in response to emotions like joy or fear, the game could get more challenging. This emotional investment increases player engagement, resulting in a more personalized and dynamic gaming experience12.

Through the customization of content in accordance with the emotional states of students, SER enhances the effectiveness of e-learning platforms in educational settings. The system may make changes to the learning rate or provide further explanations if a student vocally displays confusion or discontent. This adaptive strategy personalizes instruction while increasing student interest and participation13. The variety of applications in the field highlights the importance of SER research for higher performance and accuracy. Along with speech, there are multiple ways for detecting the emotion, like using the EEG data14, which employs the graph neural networks. Moreover, instead of using a single modality, researchers are using multiple modalities like speech, videos, and text together for detecting emotions15.

Thus, this paper proposes a novel feature fusion DL-based approach with a new loss function named ‘Balanced_focal_entropy’ loss to detect emotions from speech data. The major contribution of the paper is that the proposed system uses a novel loss function and unifies many freely available speech emotion recognition datasets, including RAVDESS, TESS, and SAVEE. This multi-dataset approach improves the model’s generalization across a variety of speakers, accents, and affective expressions, thereby rendering it more applicable and robust in real-world situations. But these data sets are not balanced, hence Balanced_Focal_Entropy Loss improves each speech emotion class on imbalanced datasets by integrating the reliability of cross-entropy with the flexibility of focal loss without external reweighting, i.e., each class is treated equally.

Another important contribution is the pre-processing pipeline, which is both automated and structured. After extracting file locations and emotion labels from the raw .wav files, pitch shifting and noise addition are used. This enhancement enhances the dataset’s heterogeneity, which reduces overfitting and improves the DL model’s capacity for generalization. The integration of various acoustic characteristics to provide a complete representation of speech data is another important advancement. Short-time energy, Root Mean Square Energy (RMSE), Zero-Crossing Rate (ZCR), and Mel-Frequency Cepstral Coefficients (MFCCs) are all extracted using the framework. These properties encompass various aspects of speech: frequency, clarity, intensity, and temporal energy fluctuations, thereby enhancing the discriminative capability of input representation. The features extracted using the four extraction techniques are merged to form a structured dataset, which is then used to train the DL models.

Literature review

The research by Kang16 used the genetic algorithm to fine-tune the support vector machine (SVM) model for SER. The Ant Colony Optimization algorithm is implemented during the feature selection step to investigate the best possible combination of emotion features. Together, the ACO-SVM achieves a better performance of 91.5% and 88.7% for the Berlin Emotional Database (EMODB) and CASIA datasets. However, the limited number of emotion categories covered in this paper was insufficient to adequately address the wide range and complexity of human emotional expression.

Same as above, Qi et al.17 present an innovative multimodal fusion graph convolutional network that effectively facilitates information interactions both inside and across the two modalities. For the purpose of initializing the acoustic word vertex, a WavLM Large embedding that has been pre-trained and takes on 1024 dimensions is retrieved from the original waveform. Next, a 300-dimensional textual embedding is extracted using the pre-trained GloVe model, and the textual word vertex is initialized. A multi-perspective fusion method is developed for the inter-modal fusion in order to incorporate the complementary information from the two modalities, acoustic and textual. Then, the Gated Recurrent Units (GRU) are used for classification. Extensive trials conducted on the IEMOCAP and the Ryerson Audio–Visual Database of Emotional Speech and Song (RAVDESS) datasets show promising results, with accuracy of 77.3% and 85.7%, respectively. Consequently, Chowdhury et al.18 use popular handcrafted features to examine DL models such as CNN and CNN_Bi-LSTM. The validation of the suggested ensemble model proves to be effective, attaining an accuracy of 97.57% on RAVDESS, 98.43% on SAVEE, 100% on TESS, 98.66% on CREMA-D, and 98.60% on EmoDB.

Similarly, Parvez et al.19 developed a model that is proficient in identifying emotions by utilizing audio features extracted from speech datasets. To make sure that the model is generalized, four datasets like TESS, RAVDESS, CREMA-D, and Surrey Audio–Visual Expressed Emotion (SAVEE) are merged to work well with a wide range of speakers, recording settings, and emotional levels. Following the unification of emotion labels, the standardization of formats, and the extraction of prosodic features such as MFCCs is performed. The DL algorithm, like CNN, is used to extract temporal and spatial properties from the voice signals. An accuracy rate of 95% is attained throughout the whole range of emotions.

Similar to the above methods, Qi et al.20 suggested a multi-task framework named Acoustic feature excitation-and-aggregation mechanism network (AFEA-Net). The speech emotion learning is performed from WavLM and Fbank features. The acoustic features are combined so that they can be calibrated. Furthermore, the authors presented a continuity learning technique for investigating the uniqueness of intra- and inter-speech. The weighted accuracy of 75.1% and 80.3% is attained on the IEMOCAP and RAVDESS datasets, respectively.

Ravi and Taran21 also introduce an approach that utilizes energy information and adaptive mode selection, as well as Variational Mode Decomposition (VMD). Instead of directly studying speech signals, this work is mostly about getting raw speech signals ready for analysis. The voice signal is first decomposed using VMD, followed by the calculation of the energy for each mode. Dominant modes are selected for signal reconstruction based on energy calculation. The reconstructed speech signal becomes more predictable when VMD is used in conjunction with the energy estimate. Measures of root mean square and spectral entropy are utilized to demonstrate the enhancement in predictability. The reconstructed signal is separated into frames, and prosodic and spectral properties are calculated. The ReliefF method is used for feature selection after feature extraction. The fine K-nearest neighbor classifier is trained for emotion identification using the resultant feature set and attained accuracies of 93.8%, 95.8%, and 93.4% on the language-based RAVDESS-speech (English), Emo-DB (German), and EMOVO (Italian) datasets, respectively.

Alike, Yang et al.22 use hybrid feature extraction methods for a multi-feature approach to SER. To avoid the overfitting issues, the suggested method lowers the dimension of the hybrid features extracted through the three feature extraction methods, such as short-time energy (STE), zero-crossing rate, and average amplitude difference. The experiment attained significant accuracy across many datasets by utilizing a Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) network. The suggested method achieves accuracy of 98.47%, 100%, and 98.87% for the CASIA dataset, Emo-DB dataset, and EMOVO datasets, respectively. Likewise, Parlak et al.23 use unique filter banks in terms of shape and frequency regions, which are distinct from Mel and Bark scales and created to generalize the feature space. The feature space dimension is reduced from 6984 to 128, which helps enhance the performance of models like SVM, RBM, and sVGG (small-VGG) classifiers. RBMs achieve a remarkable 90.65% accuracy rate on EMODB.

Alternatively, Dabbabi and Mars24 use the pre-trained model, Distil HuBERT 2.0, to learn the integrated features from speech. In terms of accuracy, it performs much better than Wav2vec 2.0 on the RAVDESS and BAVED datasets. Distil HuBERT excels with similar performance at a fraction of the model size, although it marginally underperforms BERT’s accuracy. This makes it a great option for resource-constrained contexts, such as mobile devices. Distil HuBERT scores 96.33% on the BAVED database and 87.01% on the RAVDESS database.

Akinpelu et al.25 also use a Transformer (ViT) model to identify spatial dependencies and high-level features in the speech dataset that are good indicators of emotional states based on mel spectrogram input. The datasets used for experiments are EMODB and the Toronto English Speech Set (TESS), and attain the accuracies of 98% and 91%, respectively. The results are only based on the single handcrafted features, i.e., the mel spectrogram. The integration of multiple feature extraction techniques like MFCC, Chromagram, and Tonnetz can enhance the performance of the model.

Instead, Chen et al.26 present a pretrained model, Vesper, that uses an emotion-guided masking approach to find the areas that need to be hidden so that it can be more sensitive to emotional information. To further enhance its capacity to gather semantic and acoustic representations, both of which are fundamental for emotion identification, Vesper uses hierarchical and cross-layer self-supervision. Experiments on the Interactive Emotional Dyadic Motion Capture (IEMOCAP), MELD, and CREMA-D datasets are performed, and the highest accuracy of 80.6% is attained on CREMA-D. ,

On the other hand, Alkhamali et al.27 recommend the method that combines transformers, CNN, and LSTM. The manual feature extraction methods are used. The audio data is augmented by adding noise, performing time stretching, and shifting it. The multi-head attention is used to enhance the performance of the model. The accuracy rates of EMODB, the SAVEE, and the IEMOCAP datasets were 99.86%, 96.5%, and 85.3%, respectively, after the pre-processing and data augmentation methods were used. Additionally, the Saudi private educational dataset, which was also developed for this research, attained an accuracy rate of 83%.

Moreover, Khan et al.28 used CNN and Bi-LSTM and achieved the accuracy of 95% on the RAVDESS dataset. In contrast to the previous literature, Zaman et al.29,30 used NasNet-Large CNN and Faster R-CNN for detecting emotion using facial expressions.

In the literature analysis, it is observed that recent research in speech emotion recognition (SER) has looked at a number of machine learning and deep learning methods, with big differences in how the features were chosen, how the models were built, and how the datasets were used. The dependence on handcrafted features, MFCCs, Zero-Crossing Rate (ZCR), Chroma STFT, and Root Mean Square Energy (RMSE), is a prevalent tendency that can be found in a significant portion of the published research. These features are extracted before the model is trained, and they are still used as the basis for how many systems describe emotions. Most of these approaches continue to rely on engineered features instead of directly learning from unprocessed audio signals. While some research has attempted to process raw speech data directly, the vast majority has relied on pre-trained models such as WavLM, HuBERT, and the proposed Vesper encoder. On the other hand, the number of studies that make use of such pretrained models is still rather low. The majority of deep learning-based studies use CNN, LSTM, GRU, or Transformer architectures, but generally for classification problems rather than deep feature extraction. Better performance is being achieved by ensemble models that combine CNNs, Transformers, and LSTMs.

Moreover, the individual datasets are small in size; hence, combining the datasets instead of augmentation helps to enhance the accuracy in real time. However, these models also usually depend on features that have already been retrieved or added. Moreover, multi-feature fusion is increasingly being adopted to enhance robustness and generalization. Also, none of the research has focused on overfitting caused by the imbalanced data. Hence, this research proposes a novel loss function, ‘Balanced_Focal_Entropy’, which balances the classes and improves the performance of the models. Along with it, multiple datasets are integrated to increase the sample size in the dataset for better performance. The four feature extraction techniques are used and integrated to form a hybrid feature set, which is trained on the Customized CNN and DNN, with Balanced_Focal_Entropy loss (BFE).

Methodology

The paper proposes a new loss function named BFE, which combines focal and cross-entropy loss and provides the benefit for detecting emotions from speech. The four different feature extraction methods are used, and feature fusion is performed to form a common set of extracted features. The features are trained using the CNN and DNN models with hold-out and 5-fold cross-validation methods. The Fig. 1 shows the proposed architecture consisting of three different modules. Dataset description and pre-processing

The two sets of experiments are performed using the three different datasets. The first set uses the RAVDESS dataset, while the second set integrates the three different datasets, like RAVDESS, TESS, and SAVEE.

RAVDESS

The Ryerson Audio-Visual Database of Emotional Speech and Song (RAVDESS)31 is a dataset that is frequently utilized in research pertaining to the recognition of emotions. The dataset includes a wide range of expressive speech and vocal samples, which are accessible in both audio-only and audio-visual formats. The collection comprises recordings from 24 persons, evenly split by gender, who articulate prepared lines and musical snippets spanning eight emotional categories: neutral, calm, happy, sad, furious, afraid, disgusted, and startled. Every emotion is recorded at either a standard or heightened intensity level, with the exception of the neutral emotion, which is recorded at a single intensity. Due to the fact that the dataset contains high-fidelity audio files as well as extra video content, it is excellent for research that involves facial, verbal, and gestural cues. Hybrid dataset: integration of three datasets

Toronto Emotional Speech Set (TESS)32 provides a rigorously selected collection of emotional speech samples meant to investigate the impact of emotion on spoken language. Two female actors are featured in the recordings, articulating 200 target words covering seven distinct emotions: surprise, grief, rage, contempt, fear, happiness, and a neutral tone. An extensive resource for studying the auditory and grammatical aspects of emotional speech, the collection contains 2,800 audio recordings with 400 instances of each emotion. Audio that is constant and of good quality is provided by the recordings, which are in the .wav format. These recordings are useful for machine learning models that are focused on emotion classification.

Emotional speech samples from four male actors make up the Surrey Audio-Visual Expressed Emotion (SAVEE)33,34 dataset, which is a well-organized resource for emotion recognition research. There are seven different emotional states in the dataset: calm, anger, disgust, fear, happiness, sadness, and surprise. The dataset comprises 480 utterances, with each actor providing an equal quantity of samples for each emotional category. The dataset is ideal for multimodal emotion detection research that examines both facial and vocal emotional expressions because it includes time-aligned video clips and high-quality .wav recordings.

Now, the TESS and SAVEE datasets are combined with RAVDESS for the experimentations. In TESS and SAVEE, there are 7 categories in the target variable, i.e., neutral, happy, sad, angry, afraid, disgusted, and surprised. Thus, to combine all the datasets, neutral and calm are combined into one for the RAVDESS dataset. Data augmentation

Data augmentation is an important method in speech processing, especially to increase the performance of deep learning models when training data is scarce or unbalanced. Among the several techniques available, the addition of noise, pitch modulation, and their combination are frequently employed to replicate varied acoustic environments and vocal distinctions. The three augmentation techniques, i.e., noise addition, pitch sifting, and a combination of both, are used to generate the three different speech signals from one35. Thus, after data augmentation, there are 5760 samples in the RAVDESS dataset and 18880 samples in the integrated dataset.

Feature extraction and fusion

The four different feature extraction techniques, i.e., MFCC, are used to extract the features and combine them to form a single feature set. While MFCCs are good at capturing spectral properties, they are not very good at capturing other important elements like temporal dynamics, energy fluctuations, or pitch variations.

A variety of features encapsulates various dimensions of emotional discourse. MFCCs, for example, provide information about the spectrum, ZCR can distinguish between voiced and unvoiced sounds, RMSE can record changes in intensity, and STE can provide information about the total amount of energy that is present in speech. All of these traits provide the model a more complete representation of speech signals, allowing it to distinguish emotions more accurately.

Balanced focal entropy (BFE) loss

To address the challenges of class imbalance and hard-to-classify samples in speech emotion recognition, we propose a novel loss function termed Balanced Focal Entropy (BFE) Loss. The BFE loss combines the strengths of Weighted Cross-Entropy (WCE) and Weighted Focal Loss (WFL), ensuring both stability in optimization and emphasis on difficult samples. Unlike standard cross-entropy, the BFE loss incorporates a focusing mechanism and maintains balanced weights across all emotion classes.

(a) Weighted Cross-Entropy (WCE): The WCE penalizes misclassified samples by scaling the log-likelihood with class-specific weights \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$w_c$$\end{document}. For a given true label \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$y_c$$\end{document} and predicted probability \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hat{y}_c$$\end{document}, it is defined as:13\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \mathcal {L}_{\text {WCE}} = - \sum _{c=1}^{C} w_c \, y_c \log (\hat{y}_c), \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where C denotes the number of classes, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$y_c \in \{0,1\}$$\end{document}, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hat{y}_c \in [0,1]$$\end{document}. In this work, we set \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$w_c = 1 \ \forall c$$\end{document} to achieve balanced training across all emotion categories.

(b) Weighted Focal Loss (WFL): To handle class imbalance and focus on hard samples, the WFL modifies the WCE by adding a modulating factor \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$(1-\hat{y}_c)^{\gamma }$$\end{document}, where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\gamma > 0$$\end{document} is a focusing parameter:14\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \mathcal {L}_{\text {WFL}} = - \sum _{c=1}^{C} w_c \, (1-\hat{y}_c)^{\gamma } \, y_c \log (\hat{y}_c). \end{aligned}$$\end{document}This formulation down-weights the contribution of well-classified samples while assigning higher importance to misclassified or hard-to-learn examples.

(c) Balanced Focal Entropy (BFE) Loss: The proposed BFE loss integrates WCE and WFL into a single objective using a balancing factor \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\alpha \in [0,1]$$\end{document}:15\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \mathcal {L}_{\text {BFE}} = \alpha \cdot \mathcal {L}_{\text {WCE}} + (1-\alpha ) \cdot \mathcal {L}_{\text {WFL}}. \end{aligned}$$\end{document}Here, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\alpha$$\end{document} controls the trade-off between stability (WCE) and adaptivity (WFL). By setting \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$w_c = 1$$\end{document} for all classes, the loss remains balanced across the dataset while maintaining robustness to noisy and imbalanced data.

The BFE loss is a highly effective method for speech emotion detection tasks. It utilizes the stability of cross-entropy and the adaptability of focal loss, which enables it to recognize specific emotions (for example, fear and disgust) with a lower frequency compared to dominating emotions (for example, neutral and happy). This improves the ability to generalize and classify emotions more accurately across majority and minority groups. The Balanced Focal Entropy (BFE) loss adds a focusing mechanism and class-balancing element to the cross-entropy formulation. The BFE gradient shows that correctly classified samples yield smaller gradients, whereas samples with lower prediction confidence produce bigger gradients. As a result, challenging or incorrectly classified samples are given additional attention during the optimization process. Furthermore, the class balancing factor modifies the contribution of each class during backpropagation to prevent the majority classes from monopolizing the gradient updates. When compared to standard cross-entropy, this method leads to more balanced parameter updates and better convergence behavior. From a conceptual standpoint, BFE modifies the loss surface by enhancing gradients for hard samples and minority populations, leading the optimization procedure toward decision boundaries that are more discriminative. This characteristic helps with speech emotion detection, where class imbalance and small acoustic differences make some emotions difficult to learn.

The hybrid dataset, which integrates RAVDESS, TESS, and SAVEE, with the proposed loss function BFE, obtained a higher testing accuracy of 97.80% than the RAVDESS dataset alone, which only achieved 95.23%. This difference can be attributed to a variety of significant factors. Increased variety in the hybrid dataset is one of the main explanations. The model is exposed to a greater range of speech samples as a result of the combination of three distinct datasets. These samples include variances in speaker gender, age, accent, and emotional expression. This variability enhances the model’s capacity to identify emotions in a variety of voices and situations by assisting it in learning more robust and generalized features.

Furthermore, the hybrid dataset has a bigger volume of training data than RAVDESS alone. Larger datasets greatly aid deep learning models, particularly CNNs, since they enable them to avoid overfitting and learn more intricate and subtle patterns. The model is able to more effectively capture emotion-related features that may not be adequately represented in RAVDESS alone as a result of the additional data from TESS and SAVEE, which enrich the training process.

Moreover, due to its focal component, Balanced Focal Entropy (BFE) improves accuracy with class weights of 1. Even without explicit class balancing, the focused term minimizes the loss contribution from easily identified samples while focusing learning on more difficult or borderline cases. This improves the model’s capacity to revise decision bounds and keeps it from getting overconfident. Therefore, even with homogeneous weights, BFE outperforms ordinary Cross-Entropy in terms of accuracy and adaptability. The observed improvement in accuracy is the result of the model trained on the hybrid dataset with the proposed BFE loss performing more accurately and consistently when tested on test data.

Conclusion

This paper proposes a novel loss function named ‘Balanced_Focal_Entropy’, which presents an effective speech emotion recognition approach for imbalanced datasets by improving dataset quality and diversity by combining three open-source datasets. It also employs data augmentation techniques, such as noise addition and pitch shifting. The model’s enhanced generalization and reliability are demonstrated by fusing handcrafted acoustic features like MFCC, ZCR, STE, RMSE, and using them to train a CNN and a DNN model with the novel proposed loss function ‘Balanced_Focal_Entropy’. This approach demonstrates a significant potential for real-time applications in a variety of domains, including human-computer interaction, mental health monitoring, and virtual assistants. Despite the promising results, this research uses only English-language datasets for experimentation. Thus, adding the multilingual speech datasets will have a different impact on the performance of the proposed model. Hence, to improve emotional representation, future work will concentrate on assessing the system in noisy situations by integrating multilingual real-time speech.
