Mapping evolving care needs in older adults with disabilities: a qualitative study
Authors: Ya-Xin Jin, Xiao-Pan Wang, Li-Li Gong, Hong Zhang, Yong-Xiang Xie, Xing-Hou Ling, Qing Chen, Da-Hang Yang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
To construct a patient journey map visualizing the dynamic care needs of older adults with moderate-to-severe disability, and to develop a dynamic assessment tool for clinical nursing practice. The population of older adults with moderate-to-severe disabilities is growing, presenting complex physical and psychological challenges. Unmet care needs in this group are associated with increased adverse events and place a significant strain on healthcare resources. Current research lacks assessment tools that capture the dynamic nature of these needs across the care continuum, hindering the implementation of precise interventions. A qualitative descriptive study design was employed. Semi-structured interviews were conducted with 17 purposively selected participants (patients, family members, and healthcare staff). Interview transcripts were analyzed using Colaizzi’s phenomenological method. The patient journey was conceptualized into three phases: screening and diagnosis, inpatient treatment, and post-discharge rehabilitation and follow-up. The resulting journey map highlights the dynamic evolution of patients’ physiological and psychological needs across the three phases. A total of 28 themes were synthesized and categorized into three dimensions: tasks, emotions, and pain points. The care needs of older adults with moderate-to-severe disabilities are multidimensional and phase-specific. Addressing these evolving needs effectively requires a comprehensive support system integrating medical, familial, and social resources. The patient journey map serves as a valuable tool for dynamically identifying service gaps and facilitating personalized, timely interventions to improve patients’ quality of life and uphold their dignity.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-32338-7.

Introduction

The global population is aging, presenting substantial societal challenges.Chronic diseases, characterized by high prevalence and long duration, are a leading cause of disability among older adults1,2. In China, over 40 million older adults live with disabilities, comprising 18.3% of the aging population; this number is projected to exceed 77 million by 20303. The World Health Organization defines disability as an impairment in body function or structure, a limitation in activity, or a restriction in participation4. Commonly assessed by the Activities of Daily Living (ADL) scale5, moderate disability is typically indicated by difficulty in independently completing three to four of six core activities (e.g., eating, dressing).

Older adults with moderate-to-severe disabilities often face concurrent physical and psychological challenges. Substantial evidence confirms that unmet care needs correlate with a higher incidence of adverse events (e.g., falls, deep vein thrombosis)6,7, psychological distress (e.g., anxiety, depression)8, and considerable burden on caregivers and healthcare systems.This gap in care not only diminishes patients’ quality of life but also increases economic pressure and caregiver burnout, exacerbating the shortage of medical resources9.

While previous studies have explored the care needs of disabled older adults, many have focused on static needs within home or community settings8,10–12. There remains a lack of in-depth investigation into how these needs evolve throughout the disease trajectory and at critical transition points. Furthermore, a scarcity of assessment tools adapted to this dynamic process persists9.

The patient journey map, a patient-centered visual tool, illustrates the entire disease experience. It has gained traction in chronic disease management13–15. This map features a horizontal axis (timeline) and a vertical axis (key dimensions such as tasks and experiences), presenting changes in emotions, behaviors, and needs longitudinally16. It helps healthcare institutions identify gaps and optimize service delivery.

Therefore, this study aimed to construct a care needs journey map for older adults with moderate-to-severe disabilities. It seeks to explore the touchpoints, pain points, and satisfaction points from disease screening to home-based rehabilitation, providing a foundation for developing personalized care strategies and enabling dynamic, whole-process management of care needs.

Methods

Study design and framework development

A qualitative descriptive design was used. Initially, a preliminary framework for the patient journey map was developed based on a review of existing clinical protocols and expert consensus literature17. This framework defined the horizontal axis (time) as three consecutive phases: (1) the screening and diagnosis phase, (2) the inpatient treatment phase, and (3) the post-discharge rehabilitation and follow-up phase. The vertical axis encompassed key dimensions: patient tasks, emotions, pain points, and involved stakeholders. Guided by the standards for reporting qualitative research18, we then collected data through in-depth interviews to populate this framework with detailed qualitative insights.

Population and sampling

A purposive sampling strategy was employed to recruit participants from the geriatric department of a tertiary hospital. The recruitment period spanned from August 1 to September 30, 2025. Eligible participants were identified based on the following criteria:(1) age ≥ 60 years; (2)moderate-to-severe disability as per ADL criteria19—moderate disability defined as difficulty with 3–4 ADL items, severe disability as difficulty with 5–6 items; (3)diagnosis of at least one chronic disease; and(4) intact cognitive function. Exclusion criteria included withdrawal from the study, diagnosed psychiatric disorders, or end-stage disease. Recruitment ceased when data saturation was achieved, meaning no new themes emerged from subsequent interviews.

Data collection

After obtaining written informed consent, a researcher conducted face-to-face interviews with participants in a quiet, private room. The interview guide (Table S1) began with open-ended questions, such as inviting patients to describe their illness experience, followed by more specific questions about their feelings, difficulties, and physical and psychological needs during the disease and recovery periods. Each interview lasted 25–40 min and was audio-recorded.

Ethical considerations

This study was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Hubei Provincial Hospital of Integrated Chinese and Western Medicine (Approval No.:2025.051). It was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. For patient participants, additional written consent was secured from their legal guardians or next of kin.

Data analysis

The analysis proceeded in two stages. In the first stage, interview recordings were transcribed verbatim and analyzed using Colaizzi’s seven-step phenomenological method20. Two researchers independently read the transcripts repeatedly to extract significant statements, formulate meanings, and group these into themes. They then compared and discussed their independent analyses to reach a consensus on a set of preliminary themes.

In the second stage, the themes identified in the first stage were systematically organized into the pre-defined patient journey map framework. The researchers mapped the specific tasks, emotions, and pain points described by participants onto the corresponding phases and dimensions of the journey. This process resulted in a visual representation of the dynamic evolution of patient needs, which was subsequently refined for clarity.

The patient journey map

Based on the analytical framework, the patient journey was mapped across three phases. The main findings for each phase, organized by the dimensions of themes and actions, emotions, and pain points, are described below. The visual journey map is presented in Fig. 1.

Phase 1: screening and diagnostic period

Themes and actions

The primary themes were symptom identification and seeking medical consultation. Participants reported that symptoms like pain, facial deviation, or dizziness prompted hospital visits (N1: “It was my wife who noticed first… she found that my face was crooked…”). Following the consultation, patients underwent examinations to confirm a diagnosis (N2: “the doctor arranged for me to undergo relevant tests and informed me that I had been diagnosed with heart failure”).

Emotions

Common emotions included shock and denial upon receiving the diagnosis (N2: “I’m not that old… How could I get this disease?”). This was often followed by anxiety about the future and remorse (N3: “Does this disease require surgery? Will it cost a lot of money?“; N8: “I regretted not coming to the hospital… earlier.”).

Pain points

Key challenges were:

(1) Low health literacy, leading to a lack of scientific disease management knowledge (N5: “I often forget to take my medicine…”).

(2) Reluctance to seek care, often due to underestimating the condition’s severity or worrying about being a burden (N3: “I don’t want to be hospitalized…”; N6: “What about my little grandson?… I have to take care of him.”).

Phase 2: inpatient treatment period

This phase begins with the decision for hospitalization and covers the entire period of inpatient care, focusing on acute symptom management and initial adaptation to the disability.

Themes and actions

Key Themes and actions during this phase included:

(1) Managing the transition to inpatient care: This involved overcoming initial resistance and preparing for hospitalization, often facilitated by family persuasion and detailed explanations from healthcare providers (N6: “At first, the doctor told me that I needed to be hospitalized. I was very resistant. Later, the doctor explained a lot of knowledge… My son said I had to be hospitalized, and that’s why I came in.”).

(2) Alleviating acute symptoms: Patients prioritized seeking relief from distressing symptoms such as pain and breathing difficulties (N2: “After lying down, I always feel difficulty in breathing… My sleep quality is very poor.”; N4: “I am in extreme pain, so much that I can’t sleep. How can I relieve the pain?”).

(3) Participating in treatment decisions: Patients and families collaborated with healthcare teams to formulate and agree upon treatment plans (N1: “The doctor said a minor surgery was needed… I followed the doctor’s advice.”; N7: “The doctor developed a rehabilitation plan… My daughter also checked the information online. We all think this plan is feasible.”).

(4) Cooperating with treatment regimens: Patients adhered to medication schedules, nursing procedures, and diagnostic tests (N1: “The doctor told me to take the medicine on time… I don’t dare to be careless when measuring blood pressure and checking blood sugar.”; N4: “The nurse will perform some traditional Chinese medicine procedures… I also cooperate fully.”).

(5) Establishing a support system: Due to limited mobility, patients relied heavily on family caregivers and medical staff for activities of daily living and emotional support (N1: “Thanks to my wife’s care, I have been able to recover somewhat.”; N7: “When my daughter comes home from work, she helps me clean up and massage my legs.”; N17: “Some patients do not have caregivers… we need to proactively provide care ourselves.”).

Emotions

Patients experienced a range of intense emotions:

(1) Fear and helplessness in the face of a sudden, serious illness (N6: “My condition continued to deteriorate… I was extremely scared.”; N7: “I couldn’t move at all and felt as if the sky was about to collapse.”).

(2) A profound sense of dignity loss due to dependence on others for basic needs (N7: “I need others to help me with eating, drinking, defecation and urination. I have no dignity at all.”).

(3) Guilt and being a burden towards their family members (N1: “I think he is very hardworking and I have been the one holding him back.”; N5: “Because of my illness, everyone has to revolve around me. I’m really sorry.”).

(4) Anxiety about future life and functioning (N7: “Will I just lie in bed like this in the future?”; N8: “What can I do? I don’t want to rely on others.”).

Pain points

Major challenges identified were:

(1) Severely declined quality of life resulting from limited mobility and loss of independence (N8: “Even something as simple as cutting my nails can’t be done independently… This makes me feel inconvenient.”).

(2) Significant economic burden associated with the cost of treatment and care (N3: “It cost the child a lot of money.”; N5: “Our conditions were already poor to begin with. Now that I’m like this, it’s even more difficult for the family.”).

(3) Difficulty in active condition reporting and treatment adherence due to progressive functional decline and passive coping (N4: “I often forget to take my medicine now. Sometimes I feel unwell and don’t want to bother others.”; N8: “I feel that the treatment effect is average. Whether I receive treatment or not makes no difference.”).

Phase 3: Long-term home care period

This phase encompasses the continuous care and rehabilitation process after discharge, focusing on long-term adaptation, complication prevention, and psychosocial adjustment. The patient’s role shifts from active participant in clinical treatment to a recipient of long-term care, while family caregivers assume the primary responsibility for daily tasks.

Themes and actions

The core activities in this phase are primarily undertaken by family caregivers, with patients actively participating in care with support from caregivers:

(1) Patient Participation in Health Monitoring: Patients are involved in the routine monitoring of their health status, often with assistance from caregivers (N3: “The doctor said I need to continuously monitor this condition and reminded me to have regular check-ups.”; N6: “Test my blood sugar every day…”).

(2) Caregiver Provision of Daily Home Care: Family caregivers perform essential nursing tasks to maintain health and prevent complications, such as turning, patting, medication administration, and hygiene care (N14: “The doctor said that he needs to be turned over and back patted frequently, stating that this is to prevent pressure sores and pulmonary infections.”).

(3) Management of Diet and Lifestyle: Caregivers are responsible for implementing dietary and lifestyle changes as advised by healthcare professionals (N16: “For chronic diseases such as diabetes, stroke, and hypertension, special attention needs to be paid to the diet. Choose foods that are low in salt, fat, sugar, and easy to digest…”).

(4) Provision of Psychological Support by Caregivers: Caregivers actively attend to the psychological well-being of the patients, offering companionship and emotional counseling (N9: “He often doesn’t speak and just sits quietly. Chatting with him will make him feel much better.”).

Emotions

The emotional landscape was characterized by the patient’s experience:

(1) Persistent anxiety about disease recurrence (N5: “Measure blood sugar several times a day, afraid that it will suddenly become high, and dare not eat anything.”; N15: “Most patients are very anxious about their illness and will ask me online… whether certain symptoms indicate a recurrence.”).

(2) Disappointment and feelings of neglect when emotional and care demands were not fully met (N8: “Sometimes I want to talk to my child, but he is too busy… I feel like I’m being ignored, which makes me very sad.”).

(3) Social avoidance and interpersonal marginalization driven by stigma and physical limitations (N3: “Since getting sick, I haven’t gone out much and don’t want to interact with people.”; N5: “My hands tremble and I’m afraid that others will look at me with strange eyes.”).

(4) Suppression of personal needs to avoid being a perceived burden on the family (N12: “He was afraid that I would dislike him, so he wouldn’t tell when he needed to use the toilet. By the time I noticed, he was completely dirty.”).

Pain point

Long-term challenges included:

(1) Caregiver Burden and Family Tension: Long-term care responsibilities may lead to strain and conflict among family members due to uneven care responsibilities (N14: “My brother thinks that my parents should be taken care of by me, but he never offers any effort or financial support. Because of this, we often have arguments.”).

(2) Poor Patient Compliance: Patients may not adhere to treatment plans, often due to a lack of understanding of the disease’s seriousness (N13: “She often forgets to take her medicine. We remind her, but she actually thinks we are making a fuss over nothing.”).

(3) Inconvenient Access to Medical Services: The physical challenge of traveling to healthcare facilities poses a significant barrier (N10: “Every time I take her to the hospital, it takes a whole day of hassle. She also feels quite uncomfortable on the way.”).

(4) High Risk of Patient Dropout: Patients may disengage from follow-up systems, hindering continuous care (N17: “Many patients tend to ignore calls and avoid discussing issues after discharge, which is extremely detrimental to our comprehensive understanding…”).

Discussion

This study utilized a patient journey map to visually represent the experiences of 17 participants, identifying 28 themes related to tasks, emotions, and pain points across three critical phases of care for older adults with moderate-to-severe disabilities. The map dynamically illustrates how patients’ needs evolve from diagnosis to long-term home care, providing a framework for pinpointing service gaps and tailoring interventions. It also clarifies the respective roles of healthcare providers, families, and community services, supporting the establishment of a collaborative “hospital-community-family” care network to optimize resource allocation.

Our findings demonstrate that the needs of this patient population are multidimensional and phase-specific, necessitating differentiated intervention strategies.

During the Screening and Diagnostic Phase, the core pain points are low health literacy and reluctance to seek care. This aligns with existing literature identifying chronic diseases as a primary cause of disability21 delayed medical decisions often stem from a lack of disease awareness or limitations in primary healthcare. Therefore, strengthening preventive healthcare through community health campaigns using innovative methods (e.g., educational videos, VR simulations) is crucial for early identification. Furthermore, enhancing primary care facilities and establishing referral green channels can reduce diagnostic delays. Telehealth services can also improve access and convenience.

The core challenge during the Inpatient Treatment Phase is managing distressing symptoms and the accompanying psychological distress. As negative emotions like anxiety and depression are linked to reduced quality of life and increased risks22, healthcare workers must prioritize symptom management and patient education. Interventions such as cognitive-behavioral therapy23 can help address emotional stress. Establishing robust support systems, including family involvement and peer support groups, is equally important to meet both clinical and psychosocial needs.

The Long-term Home Care Phase is characterized by a critical need for psychological support and accessible medical follow-up. Caregiver burden, social isolation, and inconvenient healthcare access can lead to patient distress and disengagement. Communities should offer psychological counseling and respite care services to alleviate family strain. To address follow-up issues, a “hospital-community-family” linkage network24 leveraging digital health tools (WeChat groups, telemedicine apps) is essential for providing continuous, remote support, ensuring patients can manage their conditions effectively at home.

Conclusion

This study utilized patient journey maps, combined with participatory observation, in-depth interviews, and multi-dimensional analysis, to systematically present the dynamic needs and experiences of middle-to-severe older adults with disabilities patients during the disease screening period, hospitalization treatment period, and long-term home care period. From the three core dimensions of tasks, emotions, and pain points, key themes such as insufficient disease cognition, conflicts in family role transitions, economic burden pressure, care dependency dilemmas, and psychological-social demand gaps were extracted. This reveals that the care needs of older adults with disabilities patients are characterized by multi-dimensional interweaving and strong stage-specificity. The study holds that the physiological and psychological needs of middle-to-severe older adults with disabilities patients cannot be adequately met by relying on a single aspect of strength. Instead, a comprehensive and multi-level support system needs to be constructed, covering all aspects of society, including policy support, social resource allocation, medical services, community care, and the emotional companionship and daily care provided by family members. This will truly and effectively address the diverse physiological and psychological needs of middle-to-severe older adults with disabilities patients, thereby improving their quality of life and safeguarding their dignity in old age.

Relevance to clinical practice

The journey map is a dynamic assessment tool generated based on the patient’s experience. Its application in clinical settings can improve the patient’s experience and has practical significance. At the clinical treatment and care level, it can help healthcare workers formulate targeted intervention measures at key nodes, such as anticipating the patients’ possible concerns during diagnosis and discharge planning, optimizing communication and health education to alleviate the anxiety and uncertainty of patients and their families. At the organizational level, it may lead to a reduction in readmission rates, precise identification of service gaps, and optimization of resource allocation. It should be noted that this research was conducted in China. Due to differences in the medical system, policy support system, and cultural customs, its impact on other countries and regions may also vary.

Limitations

This study has limitations such as small sample size, significant differences in diseases, and subjectivity in interviews. Future research should increase the sample size, delve deeper into the needs of disabled patients with a certain type of disease, conduct multi-dimensional assessment and quantification, and improve the content of the patient journey map to obtain more precise, comprehensive and effective demand intervention strategies.
