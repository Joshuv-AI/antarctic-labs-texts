Rickard’s derived Morita theory: Review and Outlook
Gustavo Jasso, Henning Krause, Sibylle Schroll

Abstract
. We survey the main results in Jeremy Rickard’s seminal papers ‘Morita theory for derived categories’ and ‘Derived equivalences and derived functors’. These papers catalysed the later development of the Morita theory of (enhanced) compactly generated triangulated categories by Keller in the algebraic setting and by Schwede and Shipley in the topological setting. We also discuss the role of Rickard’s notion of splendid equivalence in the context of Broué’s abelian defect group conjecture, and indicate an alternative proof of parts of Rickard’s Derived Morita Theorem that leverages the notion of completion of a triangulated category.

1.
Introduction

In a series of seminal papers
[
Ric89
,
Ric89a
,
Ric91
,
Ric96
]

Rickard developed a Morita theory for derived categories. Given a
pair of associative algebras, classical Morita theory provides
criteria for their module categories to be equivalent.
The relevance of derived categories became apparent in
the 1980s, and it was Jeremy Rickard who provided an analogue of
Morita’s theory in that context. This marks the beginning of an exciting
development that continues to give new insights, because derived
equivalences are often used as bridges between neighbouring fields in
mathematics. Such derived equivalences arise from tilting
objects, and tilting theory, as introduced by Brenner and Butler into the
representation theory of finite dimensional algebras, can be seen as
an inspiration for Rickard’s theory.

This paper offers a survey and tries to put Rickard’s work into a
broader context. We proceed in historical order from classical Morita theory via tilting to Rickard’s derived Morita theory.
The discussion emphasises the categorical aspects and moves on to Broué’s abelian defect
group conjecture, including some of the main ideas in Rickard’s early work in this context.
Then we focus on more recent aspects which arise from the triangulated structure of a derived category. For instance, there is a notion
of completion which provides a new interpretation of some of Rickard’s results. Also, there are further classes of triangulated categories
beyond the derived categories of associative algebras, where a Morita theory has been developed. So we see that Rickard’s work continues to inspire new developments far beyond its origins in representation theory.

2.1.
Classical Morita theory

The following is one of the most basic questions in representation theory: Given two algebras
A

\operatorname{Mod}(A)
and
\operatorname{Mod}(B)

2.1

Let

(1)

There exists a

\operatorname{Mod}(A)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\sim}}{{\longrightarrow}}\operatorname{Mod}(B).

(2)

There exists an

(3)

There exists a finitely generated projective

(a)

There is an isomorphism of algebras

(b)

The finitely generated projective

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces-\otimes_{A}P\colon\operatorname{Mod}(A)}

By definition, two algebras are

At the risk of stating the obvious,

The most basic example of Morita equivalent algebras is given by the ground commutative ring

(indeed, the latter algebra is the endomorphism algebra of the free
k

). It is easy to show that Morita equivalence is preserved by the passage to the opposite algebras, hence this notion is left-right symmetric.

Morita equivalence can be detected on certain subcategories of the module category. Recall that an
A

is finitely presented if and only if the
k

\operatorname{Hom}_{A}(M,-)\colon\operatorname{Mod}(A)\longrightarrow\operatorname{Mod}(k)

It follows that every
k
-linear equivalence

\operatorname{Mod}(A)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\sim}}{{\longrightarrow}}\operatorname{Mod}(B)

\operatorname{mod}(A)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\sim}}{{\longrightarrow}}\operatorname{mod}(B).

Recall also that
\operatorname{mod}(A)
is obtained from the category
\operatorname{proj}(A)
by freely adjoining cokernels
[

\operatorname{Mod}(A)
is obtained from
\operatorname{mod}(A)
by freely adjoining filtered colimits, see for example
[
KS06

2.3
.

Let
A
and
B
be a pair of algebras. The following statements are equivalent.

(1)

There exists a
k

\operatorname{Mod}(A)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\sim}}{{\longrightarrow}}\operatorname{Mod}(B).

(2)

There exists a
k
-linear equivalence of categories

\operatorname{mod}(A)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\sim}}{{\longrightarrow}}\operatorname{mod}(B).

(3)

There exists a
k
-linear equivalence of categories

\operatorname{proj}(A)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\sim}}{{\longrightarrow}}\operatorname{proj}(B).

Morita equivalent algebras share many important invariants. For example, Morita equivalent algebras have isomorphic centres, since the centre of an algebra
A
is isomorphic to the endomorphism algebra of the identity functor of the category
\operatorname{Mod}(A)
, see for example
[
Gab62
, Section III.5.d]
. Consequently, two commutative algebras are Morita equivalent if and only if they are isomorphic. Also, Morita equivalent algebras have isomorphic Grothendieck groups and, more generally, isomorphic higher algebraic
K

[
Wei13
, Sections II.2 and IV.6]

Morita equivalences ‘propagate’ under certain constructions. For example, Morita equivalence is preserved by the passage to categories of bimodules; this is a consequence of the following more precise version of the Eilenberg–Watts Theorem (see
[
NS16
, Theorem 3.1]
for a proof of a more general statement): The functor

\operatorname{Mod}(A^{\mathrm{op}}\otimes_{k}B)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\sim}}{{\longrightarrow}}\operatorname{LFun}_{k}(\operatorname{Mod}(A),\operatorname{Mod}(B)),\qquad M\longmapsto(-\otimes_{A}M),

is an equivalence of
k
-linear categories, where the left-hand side is (equivalent to) the category of
A
-
B
-bimodules and the right-hand side denotes the category of cocontinuous
k
-linear functors
{\operatorname{Mod}(A)\to\operatorname{Mod}(B)}
. This equivalence can be used to produce many interesting examples of Morita equivalent algebras. Recall that, given an algebra
A
and an
A
-bimodule
M
, one may form the split square-zero extension
A\ltimes M
, which is the algebra with underlying
k
-module
A\oplus M
and the multiplication

(x,m)\cdot(x^{\prime},m^{\prime})\coloneqq(xx^{\prime},xm^{\prime}+x^{\prime}m),\qquad x,x^{\prime}\in A,\ m,m^{\prime}\in M.

It is not difficult to show that the category of
(A\ltimes M)
-modules can be reconstructed from the category
\operatorname{Mod}(A)
and the endofunctor
-\otimes_{A}M\colon\operatorname{Mod}(A)\to\operatorname{Mod}(A)
, compare with
[

, Lemma 10.11]

\Phi\colon\operatorname{Mod}(A)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\sim}}{{\longrightarrow}}\operatorname{Mod}(B),

induces a Morita equivalence between the algebras

and

is the essentially unique

Similarly, working now over a field, the category of modules of the preprojective algebra

of a hereditary finite-dimensional algebra

BGL87
]

whose restriction to the category of finitely presented

Working again over an arbitrary commutative ring, suppose now that

\operatorname{HH}^{\bullet}(A)\coloneqq\operatorname{Ext}_{A^{e}}^{\bullet}(A,A),\qquad A^{e}\coloneqq A\otimes_{k}A^{\mathrm{op}},

and the diagonal

under the Eilenberg–Watts equivalence. Although much more subtle, the shifted graded Lie algebra structure on Hochschild cohomology, given by the Gerstenhaber bracket
[

[

2.2.
Tilting theory: towards derived Morita Theory

In this subsection we let
k
be a field. Tilting theory has its origin in two results that we recall below; for more on the history of tilting theory and related developments we refer the reader to
[
AHHK07
]
.

In representation theory, the work of Bernstein, Gelfand and Ponomarev on reflection functors for quivers
[
BGP73
]
and its extension by Dlab and Ringel
[
DR76
]
to species in the sense of Gabriel
[
Gab73
]
lead to the introduction of tilting modules by Brenner and Butler in
[
BB80
]
. Given a finite-dimensional algebra
B
, a
tilting
B
-module
is a finite-dimensional
B
-module
T
with the following properties:

•

The projective dimension of
T
is at most
1
and

\operatorname{Ext}_{B}^{1}(T,T)=0.

In particular,
\operatorname{Ext}_{B}^{n}(T,-)=0
for all
n>1
.

•

There is an exact sequence of
A

0\to B\to T^{0}\to T^{1}\to 0

where
T^{0}
and
T^{1}
are direct summands of finite direct sums of copies of
T
.

The representation theory of the endomorphism algebra of
T
exhibits the following relation to that of the original algebra
A
, which at the time was rather surprising:

2.4
Brenner–Butler
[
BB80
]
.

Let
B
be a finite-dimensional algebra,
T
a tilting
B

. The following statements hold.

(1)

The following pair
(\mathcal{T}_{B},\mathcal{F}_{B})
of subcategories of
\operatorname{mod}(B)
is a torsion pair in the sense of Dickson
[
Dic66
]

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{B}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\left\{M\in\operatorname{mod}(B)\,\middle|\,\operatorname{Ext}_{B}^{1}(T,M)=0\right\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{B}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\left\{M\in\operatorname{mod}(B)\,\middle|\,\operatorname{Hom}_{B}(T,M)=0\right\}.

Similarly, the following pair
(\mathcal{T}_{A},\mathcal{F}_{A})
of subcategories of

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{A}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\left\{N\in\operatorname{mod}(A)\,\middle|\,N\otimes_{A}T=0\right\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{A}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\left\{N\in\operatorname{mod}(A)\,\middle|\,\operatorname{Tor}_{1}^{A}(N,T)=0\right\}.

(2)

There are equivalences of categories

\operatorname{Hom}_{B}(T,-)\colon\mathcal{T}_{B}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\sim}}{{\longrightarrow}}\mathcal{F}_{A}

and

\operatorname{Ext}_{B}^{1}(T,-)\colon\mathcal{F}_{B}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\sim}}{{\longrightarrow}}\mathcal{T}_{A}.

On the other hand, in algebraic geometry, a famous theorem of Beilinson
[

]
establishes an equivalence of derived categories

\operatorname{D}^{b}_{\mathrm{}}(\operatorname{coh}(\mathbb{P}^{n}))\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\sim}}{{\longrightarrow}}\operatorname{D}^{b}_{\mathrm{}}(\operatorname{mod}(A)),

between the bounded derived category of coherent sheaves on

\textstyle T\coloneqq\bigoplus_{i=0}^{n}\mathcal{O}(i)

For example, if

that identifies

.

2.3.
Derived Morita theory

Recall that the
derived category
of
A
is, by definition, the localisation

\operatorname{D}_{\mathrm{}}(\operatorname{Mod}(A))\coloneqq\operatorname{Ch}(\operatorname{Mod}(A))\![\mathrm{qis}^{-1}]

of the category
\operatorname{Ch}(\operatorname{Mod}(A))
of unbounded complexes

3

of right
A

\operatorname{D}_{\mathrm{}}(\operatorname{Mod}(A))

\operatorname{Hom}_{\operatorname{D}_{\mathrm{}}(\operatorname{Mod}(A))}(M,N[n])\cong\operatorname{Ext}_{A}^{n}(M,N),\qquad M,N\in\operatorname{Mod}(A),\ n\in\mathbb{Z}

However, unlike

We also remind the reader that the exact triangles in a triangulated category are not characterised by universal properties—a source of substantial technical difficulties in the theory as this often prevents the use of certain categorical arguments in the study of derived categories, see for example

Motivated by the analogous question for module categories as well as previous results in tilting theory, the following fundamental question, answered by Rickard’s work, emerges: Given two algebras
A
and
B
, when are the derived categories
\operatorname{D}_{\mathrm{}}(\operatorname{Mod}(A))
and
\operatorname{D}_{\mathrm{}}(\operatorname{Mod}(B))

We need some preparation in order to state Rickard’s main results.

2.6
.

Given an additive category
\mathcal{C}
, we write
\operatorname{K}_{\mathrm{}}(\mathcal{C})
for the
homotopy category
of
\mathcal{C}
, that is the ideal quotient of the category of chain complexes in
\mathcal{C}
by its ideal of null-homotopic morphisms. We also write
\operatorname{K}^{+}_{\mathrm{}}(\mathcal{C})
,
\operatorname{K}^{-}_{\mathrm{}}(\mathcal{C})
and
\operatorname{K}^{b}_{\mathrm{}}(\mathcal{C})
for the homotopy categories of complexes in
\mathcal{C}
that are bounded on the left, bounded on the right, and bounded on both sides, respectively. If
\mathcal{C}
is moreover abelian, we write
\operatorname{D}_{\mathrm{}}(\mathcal{C})
,
\operatorname{D}^{+}_{\mathrm{}}(\mathcal{C})
,
\operatorname{D}^{-}_{\mathrm{}}(\mathcal{C})
and
\operatorname{D}^{b}_{\mathrm{}}(\mathcal{C})
for the derived categories of complexes in
\mathcal{C}
that are unbounded, bounded on the left, bounded on the right, and bounded on both sides, respectively.

We first need to introduce analogues of the finitely generated projective modules.

2.7
.

Let
A
be an algebra. The canonical (exact) functor

\operatorname{K}^{b}_{\mathrm{}}(\operatorname{proj}(A))\longrightarrow\operatorname{D}_{\mathrm{}}(\operatorname{Mod}(A))

is fully faithful. By definition, its essential image consists of the
perfect complexes
of
A
-modules.

2.8
.

A complex of
A
-modules

is
compact
if the

k
-linear functor

\operatorname{Hom}_{\operatorname{D}_{\mathrm{}}(\operatorname{Mod}(A))}(P,-)\colon\operatorname{D}_{\mathrm{}}(\operatorname{Mod}(A))\longrightarrow\operatorname{Mod}(k)

preserves small coproducts. It is easy to see that perfect complexes are

Theorem

3.8
below
and compare also with the proof of
[

, Proposition 6.3]

k
-linear equivalence of derived categories

\operatorname{D}_{\mathrm{}}(\operatorname{Mod}(A))\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\sim}}{{\longrightarrow}}\operatorname{D}_{\mathrm{}}(\operatorname{Mod}(B))

restricts to an (exact) equivalence between
\operatorname{K}^{b}_{\mathrm{}}(\operatorname{proj}(A))
and
\operatorname{K}^{b}_{\mathrm{}}(\operatorname{proj}(B))
.

2.9
.

More generally, given a regular cardinal
\alpha

\operatorname{D}_{\mathrm{}}(\operatorname{Mod}(A))^{\alpha}
for the full triangulated subcategory of
\operatorname{D}_{\mathrm{}}(\operatorname{Mod}(A))
of

\operatorname{D}_{\mathrm{}}(\operatorname{Mod}(A))^{\aleph_{0}}=\operatorname{K}^{b}_{\mathrm{}}(\operatorname{proj}(A))
. Every (exact)

-linear equivalence of derived categories

\operatorname{D}_{\mathrm{}}(\operatorname{Mod}(A))\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\sim}}{{\longrightarrow}}\operatorname{D}_{\mathrm{}}(\operatorname{Mod}(B))

restricts to an (exact) equivalence between
\operatorname{D}_{\mathrm{}}(\operatorname{Mod}(A))^{\alpha}
and
\operatorname{D}_{\mathrm{}}(\operatorname{Mod}(B))^{\alpha}
. Recall also that an
A

is
\alpha

if the functor

\operatorname{Hom}_{A}(M,-)\colon\operatorname{Mod}(A)\longrightarrow\operatorname{Mod}(k)

preserves
\alpha

\alpha

\operatorname{mod}_{\alpha}(A)

\operatorname{mod}_{\aleph_{0}}(A)=\operatorname{mod}(A)

, there exists a regular cardinal

\operatorname{mod}_{\alpha}(A)

\operatorname{D}_{\mathrm{}}(\operatorname{mod}_{\alpha}(A))\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\sim}}{{\longrightarrow}}\operatorname{D}_{\mathrm{}}(\operatorname{Mod}(A))^{\alpha},

see

Let
A
be an algebra. The regular representation of
A
is clearly an example of a perfect complex (concentrated in degree

•

There is an isomorphism of algebras

A\cong\operatorname{Hom}_{\operatorname{D}_{\mathrm{}}(\operatorname{Mod}(A))}(A,A).

•

For each non-zero integer
n
, we have

\operatorname{Hom}_{\operatorname{D}_{\mathrm{}}(\operatorname{Mod}(A))}(A,A[n])\cong\operatorname{Ext}_{A}^{n}(A,A)=0.

•

The regular representation of
A

\operatorname{K}^{b}_{\mathrm{}}(\operatorname{proj}(A))
as an idempotent-complete triangulated category.

Thus, the existence of a
k
-linear equivalence of triangulated categories

\operatorname{D}_{\mathrm{}}(\operatorname{Mod}(A))\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\sim}}{{\longrightarrow}}\operatorname{D}_{\mathrm{}}(\operatorname{Mod}(B))

implies the existence of a complex
T\in\operatorname{D}_{\mathrm{}}(\operatorname{Mod}(B))
with analogous properties to those listed above. The following theorem of Rickard shows that the existence of such an object
T
is not only necessary but also sufficient.

2.10

[

Let
A

(1)

\operatorname{D}_{\mathrm{}}(\operatorname{Mod}(A))\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\sim}}{{\longrightarrow}}\operatorname{D}_{\mathrm{}}(\operatorname{Mod}(B)).

(2)

There exists a complex of

(3)

There exists a perfect complex of
B

•

There is an isomorphism of algebras

•

–

\operatorname{Hom}_{\operatorname{D}_{\mathrm{}}(\operatorname{Mod}(B))}(T,T[n])=0.

2.4.
Modular representation theory and splendid equivalences

The aim of this section is to relate and place into context the main ideas of Rickard’s work in
[
Ric96
]
which, building on
[
Ric89
]
and
[
Ric91
]
, introduces a special type of derived equivalence for blocks of group algebras in the context of Broué’s abelian defect group conjecture.
Namely, Rickard introduced the notion of a
splendid equivalence
to structurally explain the phenomenon of Broué’s notion of isotypy of characters of finite groups, which are compatible families of perfect isometries. Indeed, the main contributions of Rickard’s paper are the definition of splendid equivalences and the proof that a splendid equivalence between principal blocks of group algebras induces an isotypy at the level of their character groups.

The representation theory of finite groups falls into two categories: either the characteristic of the underlying field
k
divides the order of the group or not. In the latter case, by Maschke’s Theorem, every representation of the group decomposes into a direct sum of irreducible representations or equivalently the group algebra
kG
is semisimple.
Otherwise, this is no longer true and still relatively little is understood about the representation theory in this case, which is commonly referred to as the
modular representation theory of finite groups
.
For example, while for symmetric groups over the complex numbers everything is well understood, over a field of characteristic
p
a classification of the irreducible (=simple) representations or even their dimensions are generally not known.

Two important tools to tackle these open problems are on the one hand to work over a so-called
p-modular system
consisting of a discrete valuation ring
{\mathcal{O}}
with maximal ideal
\mathfrak{m}
such that the field of fractions
K
of
{\mathcal{O}}
is a field of characteristic zero and the residue field
k
of
{\mathcal{O}}
has characteristic
p>0
. The structure of the group algebra
\mathcal{O}G
is then closely related to both the structure of the group algebra
kG
over the residue field and also to the structure of the semi-simple group algebra
KG
over the field of characteristic zero. Similarly, there are deep connections between the categories of modules over
KG,\mathcal{O}G
and
kG
, respectively.
On the other hand there is a fundamental program of research on global-to-(
p
-)local statements relating the representations or characters of a group to that of its normalisers or centralisers of
p
-subgroups. Many of the most important conjectures such as the McKay conjecture
[
McK71
]
(the proof of which was recently completed in
[
CS25
]
), Alperin’s weight conjecture
[
Alp87
]
, and Broué’s
[
Bro90a
]
abelian defect group conjecture are in this global-to-local context and aim to provide reduction steps to smaller groups with an easier structure.

The group algebra
kG
of a finite group
G
is decomposed into blocks by the unique decomposition of its multiplicative identity into a sum
e_{1}+e_{2}+\cdots+e_{n}
of primitive orthogonal central idempotents. Each block
kG.e_{i}
is an algebra with identity
e_{i}
and is an indecomposable two-sided ideal of
kG
. This decomposition lifts uniquely to
{\mathcal{O}}G
inducing a natural bijection between the blocks of
kG
and those of
{\mathcal{O}}G
. Amongst the blocks, the
principal block
is the one which does not annihilate the trivial module
k
on which
G
acts as the identity. Each block has an associated
p
-subgroup, called the
defect group
of the block which measures the complexity of the block. In the case of the principal block the defect groups are the Sylow
p
-subgroups and so their structure can be seen as at least as complicated as that of any other block. On the other hand, the following result of Brauer
[
Bra56
]
can be seen as one of the foundational steps of the global-to-local programme. Namely, he showed that for a finite group
G
, the blocks of
kG
with
defect group
D
are in bijection with the blocks of
kN_{G}(D)
with defect group
D
.

The study of the representation theory of finite groups has its origin in their character theory over
K
. Observations stemming from properties or correspondences of characters and their deeper structural significance, for example in terms of equivalences of the associated module or derived categories, give rise to much of the contemporary work in modular representation theory.

As an example, given finite groups
G
and
H
, any derived equivalence between blocks

KG.e
and
KH.f
is given by a correspondence (with signs) of irreducible characters, that is in fact, an isometry with respect to the natural inner product of characters. However, passing to derived equivalences over
{\mathcal{O}}
, the corresponding isometries have extra properties, giving rise to so-called
perfect isometries
. Namely, the following theorem by Broué can be taken as a definition of a perfect isometry.

2.24
[
Bro90a
]
.

For finite groups
G
and
H
, a derived equivalence between blocks
{\mathcal{O}}G.e
and
{\mathcal{O}}H.f
induces an isometry between the (virtual) characters of
KG.e
and
KH.f
which preserves the subgroups of characters spanned by characters of projective modules for
{\mathcal{O}}G.e
and
{\mathcal{O}}H.f
.

Note that a perfect isometry can also be characterised by purely arithmetic properties of characters which can be ‘read off’ the character table of the groups.

Since a derived equivalence over
{\mathcal{O}}
induces a derived equivalence over
k
, we obtain as a consequence that the corresponding blocks
kG.e
and
kH.f
have the same number of simple modules.

Broué put forward the idea that a perfect isometry should be a shadow of a structural correspondence at the level of the corresponding derived categories, giving rise to Broué’s abelian defect group conjecture
[

]
:

2.25

Let

\operatorname{D}^{b}(\operatorname{mod}({\mathcal{O}}G.e))\simeq\operatorname{D}^{b}(\operatorname{mod}({\mathcal{O}}N_{G}(D).f)),

where
{\mathcal{O}}N_{G}(D).f

Namely, let

-(Q):\operatorname{mod}(kG)\longrightarrow\operatorname{mod}(kN_{G}(Q))

\textstyle M(Q)\coloneqq M^{Q}/(\sum_{R<Q}{\rm Tr}^{Q}_{R}(Q^{R}),

where for a finite group

are the relative traces of the

In order to relate Broué’s abelian defect group conjecture to Rickard’s splendid equivalences, we need the notion of an

Broué then further conjectured that, for

3.
New perspectives on derived Morita theory

In this section we discuss some further categorical aspects of derived Morita theory which arise in particular from the triangulated structure of the derived category of an algebra. One aspect of Rickard’s theory is that there are several versions of the derived category for a given algebra, and it turns out that one can construct one from another via a notion of completion. Also, we look at further classes of triangulated categories beyond the derived
categories of associative algebras, where a Morita theory has been developed.

3.1.
Completions of triangulated categories

Let us return to Rickard’s Derived Morita Theorem. In this section we
indicate how completions of triangulated categories arise when proving
some parts of this. We assume that
k
is a commutative ring and observe that no flatness assumptions on the algebras are needed.

3.1
.

Let
A
and
B
be a pair of
k
-algebras. The following statements are equivalent:

(1)

There exists a
k
-linear equivalence of triangulated categories

\operatorname{D}_{\mathrm{}}(\operatorname{Mod}(A))\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\sim}}{{\longrightarrow}}\operatorname{D}_{\mathrm{}}(\operatorname{Mod}(B)).

(2)

There exists a
k
-linear equivalence of triangulated categories

\operatorname{D}^{b}_{\mathrm{}}(\operatorname{pcoh}(A))\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\sim}}{{\longrightarrow}}\operatorname{D}^{b}_{\mathrm{}}(\operatorname{pcoh}(B)).

(3)

There exists a
k
-linear equivalence of triangulated categories

\operatorname{D}^{b}_{\mathrm{}}(\operatorname{proj}(A))\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\sim}}{{\longrightarrow}}\operatorname{D}^{b}_{\mathrm{}}(\operatorname{proj}(B)).

3.2
.

We view
\operatorname{pcoh}(A)
and
\operatorname{proj}(A)
as exact categories. When the ring
A
is right coherent, then the category
\operatorname{pcoh}(A)
equals
\operatorname{mod}(A)
and is an
abelian category. The canonical functor
\operatorname{K}^{b}_{\mathrm{}}(\operatorname{proj}(A))\to\operatorname{D}^{b}_{\mathrm{}}(\operatorname{proj}(A))
is an equivalence.

3.3
.

The chain of exact inclusions

\operatorname{proj}(A)\hookrightarrow\operatorname{pcoh}(A)\hookrightarrow\operatorname{Mod}(A)

induces fully faithful exact functors

\operatorname{D}^{b}_{\mathrm{}}(\operatorname{proj}(A))\rightarrow\operatorname{D}^{b}_{\mathrm{}}(\operatorname{pcoh}(A))\rightarrow\operatorname{D}_{\mathrm{}}(\operatorname{Mod}(A)).

The
proof of Theorem
3.1
shows that any of its
equivalences yields an equivalence when
restricted
to one
of the subcategories. On the other hand, any of the above
equivalences
extends
to an equivalence when one passes to
one of the bigger subcategories.

Let us sketch the proof of the theorem, and further details will be provided afterwards. We need the following definition from
[
Kra20
]
. A
Cauchy sequence
in a triangulated category
\mathcal{C}
is a sequence of morphisms

X=(X_{0}\to X_{1}\to X_{2}\to\cdots)

3.2.
Morita theory for compactly generated triangulated categories

Further study of the properties of derived categories of algebras and similar triangulated categories brings the following class of triangulated categories to the fore.

3.7
.

Let
\mathcal{T}
be a triangulated category and suppose that
\mathcal{T}
admits small coproducts. We say that
\mathcal{T}
is
compactly generated
if there exists a set
\mathcal{G}\subseteq\mathcal{T}
consisting of compact objects and which generate
\mathcal{T}
as a triangulated category with small coproducts.

The derived category of an algebra
A
is the prototypical example of a compactly generated triangulated category, with the regular representation of
A
serving as a compact generator. Recall also that we claimed in
Remark

2.8
that
\operatorname{K}^{b}_{\mathrm{}}(\operatorname{proj}(A))
consists precisely of the compact objects in
\operatorname{D}_{\mathrm{}}(A)
. This is a special case of the following general theorem.

3.8
Neeman
[
Nee92
]
.

Let
\mathcal{T}
be a compactly generated triangulated category and
\mathcal{G}\subseteq\mathcal{T}
a set of compact generators. Then, the full subcategory of
\mathcal{T}
spanned by the compact objects coincides with its smallest idempotent complete triangulated subcategory which contains
\mathcal{G}
.

The following question is therefore natural: When are two compactly-generated triangulated categories
\mathcal{S}
and
\mathcal{T}
equivalent? Clearly, an equivalence

\mathcal{S}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\sim}}{{\longrightarrow}}\mathcal{T}

induces an equivalence of triangulated categories between the corresponding subcategories of compact objects. However, additional structure seems to be necessary to prove a more general version of
Theorem

2.10
: to an object
X\in\mathcal{T}
one may associate its graded endomorphism algebra

\textstyle\mathcal{T}(X,X)^{\bullet}\coloneqq\bigoplus_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}\mathcal{T}(X,\Sigma^{i}(X)),\qquad g*f\coloneqq\Sigma^{j}(g)\circ f,\ |f|=j,

but the graded endomorphism algebra of a compact generator, say, need not determine
\mathcal{T}
as a triangulated category.

Recall that a differential graded (dg, for short) category is a category enriched in complexes of
k
-modules. For example, dg algebras identify with dg categories with a single object. If
\mathcal{A}
is a small dg category, then its derived category
\operatorname{D}_{\mathrm{}}(\mathcal{A})
is a compactly generated triangulated category
[
Kel94
]
. The derived category of
\mathcal{A}
has a salient property: it is equivalent to the stable category of a
k
-linear Frobenius exact category.
6
6

6

A Frobenius exact category is an exact category in the sense of Quillen that has enough projectives, enough injectives, and such that the classes of projectives and of injective objects coincide. Stable categories of Frobenius exact categories are triangulated by a well-known theorem of Happel
[
Hap88
]
, see also
[
Hel68
]
.
A triangulated category with the latter property is said to be
algebraic
. The following theorem shows that all algebraic compactly generated triangulated categories are of this form; for missing definitions and more information on the theory of dg categories we refer the reader to
[
Kel06
]
.

3.9
Keller’s Recognition Theorem
[
Kel94
]
.

Let
\mathcal{T}
be an algebraic compactly generated triangulated category. Then, there exists a small differential graded category and a
k
-linear equivalence of triangulated categories

\mathcal{T}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\sim}}{{\longrightarrow}}\operatorname{D}_{\mathrm{}}(\mathcal{A}).

3.10
.

In the context of
Theorem

3.9
, the differential graded category
\mathcal{A}
in general is not determined up to quasi-equivalence by
\mathcal{T}
even after fixing a preferred set of compact generators of the latter. When
\mathcal{A}
has a single object, this problem is closely related to the classification of differential graded algebras with a given cohomology graded algebra up to quasi-isomorphism: A
k
-linear equivalence of triangulated categories

\mathcal{T}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\sim}}{{\longrightarrow}}\operatorname{D}_{\mathrm{}}(A),

which identifies
G
and
A
and
induces an isomorphism of graded algebras

\textstyle\bigoplus_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}\mathcal{T}(G,\Sigma^{i}(G))\cong H^{\bullet}(A).

Nonetheless, the derived Morita theory of small dg categories is completely understood thanks to the results in
[
Kel94
]
, see
[
Kel06

for a precise formulation of the analogue of
Theorem

2.10

Theorem

2.14

K

[
Kel06

3.11

[

Let
\mathcal{A}
