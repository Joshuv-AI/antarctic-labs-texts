Type E endothelial cells orchestrate embryonic skeletogenesis via chondrogenic priming of skeletal progenitors
Authors: Yang-Yang Li, Sha Huang, Ou Hu, Qin Qin, Pulin Yan, Peng Lin, Ying-Bo Wang, Huai-Jian Jin, Jian Wu, Yu-Tong Wu, Na Luo, Qing-Hua Ma, Rui-Li Cai, Yang-Li Xie, Lin Chen, Yi-Bo Gan, Peng Liu, Jian He
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSkeletal stem/progenitor cells (SSPCs) are pivotal orchestrators of embryonic skeletogenesis, underlying chondrogenesis and osteogenesis, however, the molecular determinants governing SSPC functionality remain elusive.MethodsWe performed single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) to delineate cellular heterogeneity within the developing limb bud and to identify candidate cellular markers. The spatiotemporal distribution of target cell populations was further examined via immunofluorescence staining. Fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS) was used to isolate endothelial cells and SSPCs based on surface markers. Functional properties of these cells were assessed using in vitro assays, and direct co-culture experiments were conducted to evaluate intercellular communication and underlying molecular pathways.ResultsWe delineate a CD140a + PDPN + SSPC population characterized by robust self-renewal and multipotency, peaking in abundance at embryonic day 14.5. Concomitantly, we resolved the heterogeneity within endothelial cell (EC) populations during embryonic angiogenesis, identifying a distinct subpopulation exhibiting high Pecam1 and low Emcn expression (recapitulating type E). Critically, SSPCs manifested enhanced chondrogenic differentiation potential and type II collagen synthesis when co-cultured specifically with type E ECs, highlighting an indispensable role in endochondral ossification during long bone formation. Mechanistic intercellular crosstalk analyses demonstrated that BMP signaling plays an essential role in modulating type E endothelial cell-mediated regulation of SSPC potency through the coordinated actions of transcription factors ID1, MSX2, and SOX9. Notably, pharmacological inhibition of BMP signaling abolished the pro-chondrogenic and pro-osteogenic enhancement conferred by type E ECs upon SSPCs.ConclusionThese findings uncover a fundamental mechanism of long bone development mediated by CD140a + PDPN + SSPCs and modulated by type E ECs. This reciprocal, bipotent coupling between skeletogenesis and angiogenesis provides a conceptual framework for understanding skeletal development and homeostasis, proffering novel therapeutic avenues for associated pathologies.The translational potential of this articleBy elucidating the regulatory function of type E endothelial cells in orchestrating chondrogenic priming during embryonic skeletogenesis, this study unveils potential therapeutic strategies for bone regeneration through targeting the vascular network.

Introduction

Skeletal development is a complex, multistep process that begins with mesenchymal condensation, followed by the emergence of skeletal stem/progenitor cells (SSPCs), and culminates in lineage commitment toward chondrocytes and osteoblasts [1]. In humans, endochondral ossification of the long bones initiates at approximately five weeks post–conception, resulting in the formation of diverse chondrocyte, osteoblast, and stromal lineages that give rise to the primary and secondary ossification centers [2,3]. In mice, skeletogenesis originates within the limb bud mesenchyme, which is delineated by the expression of genes such as Prrx1, Hox genes family, and Twist at embryonic day 10.5 (E10.5) [[4], [5], [6]]. These molecular signatures are evolutionarily conserved and under strict regulation by multiple paracrine signals emanating from concurrent developmental processes, including myogenesis, angiogenesis, and hematopoiesis [7,8]. Despite their crucial roles in skeletal morphogenesis, the underlying mechanisms, particularly in the earliest embryonic stages, remain relatively unexplored.

Single-cell transcriptomic profiling has begun to unravel the trajectory of limb bud cell differentiation into long bone[[9], [10], [11]]. Previous findings indicate that SSPCs arise from Pdgfra-expressing mesenchymal progenitors and are critical for commitment to the skeletal lineage [12,13]. In a prior investigation, we delineated a distinct subset of human SSPCs defined by the co-expression of PDGFRAlow/−PDPN+CADM1+, which gives rise specifically to chondrocytes and osteoblasts, but not to stromal lineages [2]. In both adult human and murine models, SSPCs exhibit multipotency, including chondrogenic, osteogenic, and adipogenic potentials, which play pivotal roles in bone formation and sustaining the hematopoietic niche[12,[14], [15], [16]]. Variations in SSPC properties across developmental stages reflect their interaction with the complex and dynamic cellular environment of the skeletal niche. For instance, type H blood vessels are essential for maintaining skeletal integrity and facilitating regenerative capacity[[17], [18], [19]]. Angiogenesis following bone injury constitutes a fundamental component of the regenerative process, promoting osteoblast differentiation and the recruitment of inflammatory mediators that accelerate skeletal healing [17,20,21]. While the coupling of osteogenesis and angiogenesis has been extensively studied, the precise molecular mechanisms by which vascular cells modulate SSPC behavior during the early stages remain poorly characterized.

In this investigation, we delineate a comprehensive spatiotemporal roadmap of murine skeletogenesis, encompassing the critical morphogenetic window from E10.5 to E14.5 when skeletal elements initiate from limb bud mesenchyme [22,23]. We identified an increasing multipotent subset of SSPCs selectively enrichable via concomitant expression of PDGFRA and PDPN. Furthermore, by dissecting the heterogeneity among endothelial cells, we identified a distinct EC population that could be discriminated by CD31 (encoded by Pecam1) + EMCNlow/-, corresponding to type E ECs described at early developmental stages [24,25]. Functional assays, both in vitro and in vivo, demonstrated that SSPCs exhibit significantly increased chondrogenic capacity in the presence of type E ECs relative to their type H counterparts. Mechanistically, we established that the preservation of SSPC multipotency is principally mediated by BMP signaling originating from type E ECs. This trophic regulation is intricately governed through dynamic bidirectional crosstalk between SSPCs and type E ECs, thereby elucidating fundamental aspects of the molecular governance underlying skeletogenesis and regenerative processes.

Methods and materials

Mice

C57BL/6 Mice were fed at Animal Laboratory of Daping Hospital with regulated specific pathogen-free (SPF) facility. Mouse embryos were staged by somite pair (sp) counting: E10.0, 31-35 sp; E10.5, 36-40 sp; and E11.0, 41-45 sp. C57BL/6J mice were utilized as recipients for renal capsule transplantation of limb bud-derived SSPCs. All experimental procedures and animal handling were conducted in strict compliance with the regulatory requirements of the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee at the Army Medical University (AMUWEC20237442).

To obtain fetuses with precisely staged gestational ages, nulliparous female mice were co-housed with male mice at a 2:1 ratio (two females per male) daily at 6:00 pm Vaginal plugs were examined in the female genital tract at 8:00 am the following morning. The presence of a vaginal plug was recorded as embryonic day 0.5 (E 0.5) at 12:00 am on the day of detection.

Isolation of mouse embryonic limb bud tissues and preparation of single-cell suspension

Mouse embryonic limb buds were dissected and immediately transferred into ice-cold MEMα medium (Procell, PM150422A) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, VivaCell, C04002). The tissue was then rinsed thoroughly with Hanks' Balanced Salt Solution (Procell, PB180321) and carefully dissected to remove muscle and skin layers, retaining only long bones or core limb bud tissues. Using sterile instruments, we minced the tissue into small fragments and digested it in pre-warmed (37 °C) digestion solution containing 3 mg/ml Collagenase Type I (Worthington, LS004197), 4 mg/ml Dispase II (Roche, 04942078001), and 10 U/ml DNase I (Solarbio, D8071-100 mg).

Digestion consisted of two sequential 15-min rounds. Every 5 min during incubation, we vigorously agitated the mixture to ensure complete dissociation. After the first round, the suspension was briefly settled, and the supernatant was carefully transferred into HBSS supplemented with 2% FBS to stop enzymatic activity. Upon completing the second digestion, all dissociated material was pooled and passed through a 40-μm cell strainer. The resulting suspension was treated with red blood cell (RBC) lysis buffer for 3 min to remove erythrocytes, followed by centrifugation at 300 g for 5 min.

After discarding the supernatant, the cell pellet was resuspended in FACS collection buffer (D-Hank's with 1% FBS). All procedures were conducted on ice under strict aseptic conditions to maintain sterility.

Flow cytometry

The following antibodies were employed for endothelial cell screening: Fixable Viability Dye eFluor™ 780 (APC-Cy7 channel, eBioscience, 65-0865-14, diluted 1:250) to label dead cells; CD45-FITC (BioLegend, 11-0451-82, 1:50) and TER-119-FITC(eBioscience, 11-5921-82, 1:50) to label hematopoietic lineage cells; CD31-PE-Cy7 (BioLegend, 102524, 1:50) and EMCN-PE (Invitrogen, 12-5851-82, 1:50) to identify endothelial cells; CD140a-APC (PDGFRα, Invitrogen, 17-1401-81, 1:50) and PDPN-PE (BioLegend, 127407, 1:50) to mark SSPCs. Limb bud cells were resuspended in flow cytometry buffer and stained with the aforementioned antibodies for 30 min at 4 °C. Flow sorting was performed on a Beckman Flow Cytometry Instruments (Becman Moflo XDP), and data analysis was conducted using FlowJo™ v10.8 Software (BD Life Sciences).

Immunofluorescent staining

Embryonic limb bud and recipient kidney tissues were embedded in paraffin. Sections of 5 μm thickness were cut using an Epredia HM325 rotary microtome. Endogenous peroxidase activity was blocked with 3% H2O2 (Beyotime, P0100A) for 8 min. After antigen retrieval (methods detailed below), sections were incubated in blocking buffer containing 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) (Sigma, V900933-100G) and 1% Triton X-100 (Solarbio, T8200) in PBS for 30 min at room temperature. For cell slides, fix with 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 min, incubated with blocking buffer (5% BSA + 0.3% Triton + PBS) for 1 h. Primary antibodies COLII (Santa Cruz, sc-52658, 1:200), BMP2 (Abcam, ab214821, 1:400) and Ki67 (CST, 9129s, 1:400) were incubated overnight at 4 °C. Secondary antibodies were incubated for 1 h at room temperature. Sections were mounted with DAPI-containing antifade mounting medium (Beyotime, P0131).

For labeling vascular endothelial cells and Periostin+ SSPCs and recipient kidney tissues, Antigen retrieval: 10 mM sodium citrate buffer (pH 6.0), 120 °C for 10 min; Primary antibodies: Anti-CD31 (AiFang, SAF005, 1:100), Anti-EMCN (Santa Cruz, SC-65495, 1:100), Anti-Periostin (R&D, AF2955, 1:100), and Anti-COLI (Abclonal, A24112, 1:200). For labeling CTSK+ SSPCs, Antigen retrieval: 0.4% pepsin (ZSGB-BIO, ZLI-9013) for 20 min at 37 °C, Primary antibody: Anti-CTSK (Proteintech, 1239-1-AP, 1:100). Secondary antibodies: Donkey Anti-Rabbit IgG H&L (Alexa Fluor® 555) pre-adsorbed (Abcam, ab150062, 1:500) and Donkey Anti-Rat IgG H&L (Alexa Fluor® 555) pre-adsorbed (Abcam, ab150154, 1:500), Donkey Anti-Goat IgG H&L (Alexa Fluor® 555) (Abcam, ab150130, 1:500), Goat anti-Mouse IgG (H + L) Highly Cross-Adsorbed Secondary Antibody, Alexa Fluor™ Plus 555 (Invitrogen, A32727, 1:500), Donkey anti-Rabbit IgG (H + L) Highly Cross-Adsorbed Secondary Antibody, Alexa Fluor™ 647 (Invitrogen, A32795, 1:500).

Cell culture and CFU assay

Sorted cells were seeded in 24-well plates (2 × 104 cells/well) containing culture medium with MEMα (Procell, PM150422A) supplemented with 10% FBS (VivaCell, C04002), and incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO2 and 2% O2. Half of the medium was changed every 2 days. For coculture assay, SSPCs were mixed with ECs in a ratio of 10:1. To evaluate cell proliferative capacity, isolated cells were stained with Ki67 (Abcam, Ab279653) every 12 h. Total nuclei and Ki67+ nuclei were counted.

For CFU-F assay, sorted cells were seeded in 6-well plates (1 × 103 cells/well) containing MEMα medium (Procell, PM150422A) supplemented with 10% FBS (VivaCell, C04002) and incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO2 and 2% O2. Half of the medium was changed every 3-4 days. At day 7, cells were fixed and stained with crystal violet staining solution. Adherent colonies with more than 50 cells were quantified. Serial CFU-F colony formation was performed by seeding sorted cells in culture medium at clonal density, and serially passaged to generate the secondary and tertiary colonies.

Trilineage differentiation assay

The sorted cells were expanded and seeded into 24-well plates at a density of 2.0 × 104 cells/cm2. Cells were then treated with adipogenic differentiation medium (Stemcell, 05507) for adipogenic induction, osteogenic differentiation medium (Stemcell, 05455) for osteogenic induction, or chondrogenic differentiation medium (Stemcell, 05504) for chondrogenic induction. For BMPER2 inhibition, CDD-1653 (MCE, HY-164145) diluted in DMSO was supplemented into the medium with at a concentration of 2.8 nM.

After 5-10 days of induction, adipogenic differentiation efficacy was evaluated by Oil Red O staining (Cyagen Biosciences, OILR-10001). Osteogenic differentiation efficacy was assessed by Alizarin Red S staining (Cyagen Biosciences, ALIR-10001). Chondrogenic differentiation capacity was determined through Alcian blue staining (Cyagen Biosciences, ALCB-10001) combined with immunofluorescence staining for COL2 (Santa Cruz, sc-52658).

Renal subcapsular transplantation

CD140a+PDPN+ SSPCs were sorted by flow cytometry and cultured for 7-10 days as previously described. Briefly, 5 x 105 cells were resuspended in 5 μL of Matrigel (Biocoat, 354320) on ice and then aspirated into a micropipette (Drummond Scientific, 5-000-2010). A small incision was made near the kidney pole to separate the capsule from the renal parenchyma. Matrigel with cells was injected into the kidney pocket. Four weeks after transplantation, recipient kidney tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) for 24 h at room temperature, followed by decalcification in 15% EDTA solution for 5 days. Grafts were then cryosectioned at 10 μm and stained by Movat Pentachrome Staining Kit (ScyTek, MPS-1) to demonstrate bone and cartilage differentiation.

RNA extraction and qPCR

Total RNA was extracted from cells using Rneasy Micro Kit (Qiagen, 74004) according to the manufacturer's instructions. cDNA was prepared using PrimeScrip™ RT reagent Kit with gDNA Eraser (Takara, RR047A). qPCR reactions were prepared using TB Green® Premix Ex Taq™ II (Takara, RR820A) and run on a CFX96 Touch Real-Time PCR Detection System (Bio-Rad Laboratories). A list of the primers used was provided (Table S3). Gapdh was used as loading control and the relative mRNA abundance was calculated using a comparative CT method.

Western Blot

Whole-cell protein lysates were prepared by homogenization in RIPA lysis buffer (Strong; Beyotime, P0013B) supplemented with PMSF protease inhibitor (Beyotime, ST507). Total protein concentration was quantified using a bicinchoninic acid (BCA) assay. Equivalent protein aliquots were supplemented with 5X SDS-PAGE loading buffer (Beyotime, P0286), denatured by heating, resolved using 10% sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE), and electrotransferred onto polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membranes. A pre-stained protein molecular weight marker (10-180 kDa; Beyotime, P0068) was resolved concurrently. Membranes underwent in situ blocking followed by sequential incubation with primary and horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies, diluted in buffers provided within a dedicated Western immunoblotting solution kit (Beyotime, P0023). Primary antibodies employed were: rabbit anti-MSX1 (1:500, Proteintech, 17678-1-AP), rabbit anti-MSX2 (1:500, Abcam, ab227720), rabbit anti-ID1 (1:500, Proteintech, 18475-1-AP), rabbit anti-SOX9 (1:500, Abcam, ab185230), and mouse anti-GAPDH (1:1000, Proteintech, 81640-5-RR) as the loading control. Following secondary antibody incubation, immunoreactive bands were visualized via enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) substrate and imaged utilizing a dedicated chemiluminescence detection system. Band intensities were quantified using ImageJ software and normalized to corresponding GAPDH signal intensities.

Single-cell RNA-sequencing data collection and pre-processing

Single-cell RNA-sequencing datasets of murine embryonic limb bud, long bone, and bone marrow were collected from public repositories. A total of 43 datasets from scRNA-seq dataset of were downloaded from seven published studies, including limb buds ranging from E9.5 to E18.5, bone marrow ranging from E16.5 to P21, and whole bone at W8 [[9], [10], [11],18,[26], [27], [28]]. To ensure comparison among datasets, matrices generated by 10x Genomics platform were retained for subsequent analysis. The cell number in each enrolled sample rigorously adheres to the established standards for scRNA-seq under the 10x Genomics framework. To mitigate technical variability, a minimum of two samples per developmental timepoint was ensured, thereby safeguarding analytical robustness.

Original matrices were processed by quality control with standardized criterial. Firstly, DoubletFinder function was utilized to rule out potential doublets or multiplets. Then, cells with number of expressed genes more than 200, percentage of mitochondrial RNA counts less than 20% were retained for integrated analysis following the Scanpy and Seurat pipeline [29,30]. The matrix was normalized up to the top 5000 highly variable genes were calculated and used to generate principal components (PCs). To reduce the effects of cell cycle heterogeneity that contribute to clustering, the S.Score and G2M.Score were calculated and regressed out before reporting PCs. To remove the batch effects across datasets, Harmony integration was applied on the top 50 PCs and a total of 100 components were calculated for subsequent analysis.

Dimensionality reduction and clustering

For visualization and clustering of all cells, the top 50 harmony dimensions were further reduced using Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) with min_dist set as 0.5 and spread set as 1. For unsupervised clustering, the neighborhood graph was first computed on the top 50 harmony dimensions with size of neighborhood set as 30. Then Louvain clustering was performed with resolution set as 0.6, resulting in a total of 20 cell clusters. To determine the optimal clustering, SCCAF method was applied to calculate the mean CV accuracy, and the final clustering was optimized using SCCAF_optimize_all function with min_acc set as 0.85. Non-parametric Wilcoxon rank sum test was performed to find differentially-expressed genes (DEGs) among individual cell clusters with min_pct greater than 0.2 and logfc_threshold greater than 0.5. The annotation of each cell clusters was based on the expression of classic cell-type specific marker genes in DEGs from Cellmarker 2.0 database and literature. For visualization of skeletogenic cell clusters, the force-directed graph was constructed and Force Atlas dimensions were computed for each cell. Then partition-based graph abstraction (PAGA) was generated to illustrate the connectivity among cell clusters. For visualization and clustering of endothelial cells (ECs), the harmony integration was re-performed, and the newly generated harmony dimensions were used for UMAP visualization and constructed neighborhood graph. The second-round Louvain clustering of ECs was performed with resolution set as 0.5, resulting in a total of 5 cell clusters. DEGs among EC clusters were calculated using Wilcoxon rank sum test as described above.

Arterial-venous, EC type and cell cycle evaluation

Arterial and venous signature scores were calculated according to public gene lists, including 10 arterial genes (Dll4, Igfbp3, Unc5b, Gja4, Hey1, Mecom, Efnb2, Epas1, Vegfc and Cxcr4) and 3 venous genes (Nr2f2, Nrp2, and Aplnr) in a previous study [31]. For cell cycle analysis, cell cycle-related genes consisting of a previously defined core set of 43 G1/S genes and 54 G2/M genes implemented in Seurat package were used. The average expression of each gene set was calculated as corresponding scores, and manually assigned cells to approximate cell cycle phases based on the scores. Signature scores of type E, type H and type L were calculated using Gene set variation analysis (GSVA). Gene sets were downloaded from a previous study [24].

Cross-species comparative analysis

For comparative analysis between human and mouse datasets, the expression data matrices of human embryonic limb bud and long bone were collected [2]. The human samples are spanning Carnegie Stage 13 to 22 (corresponding to 5-8 post–conception weeks), a pivotal developmental interval characterized by the initiation of primary ossification and the emergence of definitive endochondral ossification patterns [32]. Homologous genes conversion was performed using gprofiler2 package. Correlation analysis among all cell subsets was done using cor function with Pearson method. Integrated murine and human matrices underwent PAGA connectivity analysis, employing principal components derived from intersecting highly variable genes processed via the Scanpy framework (https://scanpy.readthedocs.io/en/stable/). The connectivity delineated transcriptional relationships spanning all cross-species clusters.

NicheNet analysis

Cellular crosstalk between SSPCs and ECs was performed according to the pipeline from nichenetr package. The expression matrices of ECs and SSPCs were extracted. ECs were set as Senders while SSPCs were set as Receiver. Notably, DEGs between SSPCs earlier than E12.5 and SSPCs later than E14.5 were set as gene set of interest, and all expressed genes in SSPCs were set as background genes. The ligand activity was predicted and top 20 ligands were retained for ligand-receptor and ligand-target visualization.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using R (v4.1.3) and software packages compiled in R language. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Significance was determined using Student's t-test for the data with homogeneity of variance and normal distribution, and Wilcox-test for the other data. Comparisons among multiple groups were conducted using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). Additional statistical analyses are detailed in the corresponding sections of the manuscript.

Results

Integrated single-cell transcriptomic atlas of murine skeletal development

To comprehensively capture the dynamic cellular ecosystems that orchestrate skeletal development across multiple stages, we compiled single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) datasets from limb buds spanning E10.5 to E18.5, and bone marrow or whole long bones from E16.5 to postnatal week 8 (W8) (Fig. 1A) [[9], [10], [11],18,[26], [27], [28]]. A total of 43 high-quality samples, encompassing 296,531 cells that passed quality control, were retained for further integrative analysis. Although variability in sequencing methods and developmental stages led to heterogeneity across datasets, their overall gene expression profiles showed broad consistency (Fig. S1A–S1C). To mitigate technical batch effects, we applied the mutual nearest neighbors (MNN) correction algorithm. Subsequent clustering analysis identified 24 distinct cell subsets, comprising six mesenchymal populations: Naïve_Mes (highly expressing Irx3/5), Proximal_Mes1/2 (highly expressing Lix1), and Distal_Mes1/2/3 (highly expressing Msx1 and Hox genes); three myogenic lineages: Myoprogenitor_1/2 (highly expressing Myf5), and Myocyte (highly expressing Myog); seven skeletal cell subsets, including Skeprogenitor_1/2/3 (highly expressing Postn and Gas2), Chondroprogenitor (highly expressing Sox6/9), Chondrocyte (highly expressing Col2a1 and Acan), bone marrow mesenchyme (Marrow_Mes, highly expressing Adipoq and Lepr), and Tendon (highly expressing Tppp3); two ectodermal populations: Apical ectodermal ridge (AER, highly expressing Epcam) and Neural_Crest (highly expressing Sox10); two vascular subsets: Pericyte (highly expressing Rgs5) and Endothelium (highly expressing Pecam1), and four hematopoietic lineages, including Leukocyte_1/2/3 (highly expressing Ptprc) and Erythrocyte (highly expressing Gypa) (Fig. 1B and C, Fig. S1D, and Table S1). To dissect the spatiotemporal dynamics of cellular composition during skeletal development, we analyzed the transitional shifts in cell population frequencies. A progressive decline in mesenchymal precursor subsets was observed beginning at E9.5, while skeletal lineage subsets emerged and expanded significantly from E11.5 onwards in the limb bud (Fig. 1D and Fig. S1F). In the late fetal and adult stages, chondrocytes and mesenchymal stromal cells became predominant within the bone marrow, alongside an increase in hematopoietic cells, indicative of the maturation of hematopoietic niches (Fig. 1D and Fig. S1F). These observations are further supported by enrichment analysis, which demonstrated a marked overrepresentation of blood cell populations in bone marrow compartments (Fig. 1E). Notably, mesenchymal subsets including Proximal_Mes1/2 and Distal_Mes1/2 were enriched in the forelimb buds, whereas skeletal progenitor populations such as Skeprogenitor_1 and Chondroprogenitor were more prevalent in the hindlimb buds, implying divergent temporal dynamics in limb skeletogenesis (Fig. 1E and Fig. S1G) [[33], [34], [35]].

In summary, the integrated analysis of scRNA-seq datasets from limb buds to postnatal bone tissues has revealed a complex and evolving cellular ecosystem governing skeletal development. These findings provide a valuable resource for exploring developmental skeletal biology and are accessible through an online platform (http://dataset.ivdatlas.cn/mSkeletal_atlas).

Single-cell analysis reveals a skeletal progenitor subset enriched for Pdgfra and Pdpn expression at E14.5

To identify the crucial cell populations underlying skeletal development, we performed an in-depth analysis of skeletal cell subsets over the critical embryonic time window from E11.5 to E16.5. Utilizing partition-based graph abstraction (PAGA) combined with force-directed layout, we visualized the relationships among distinct cell subsets. This analysis demonstrated that Skeprogenitor_1 and Skeprogenitor_3 exhibited close developmental affinities with both mesenchymal and chondrogenic compartments (Fig. 2A). We observed that these skeletal progenitor subsets reached maximal enrichment between E12.5 and E15.5, an intermediate developmental phase between the mesenchymal and chondrogenic lineages (Fig. 2B and Fig. S2A). This temporal pattern suggests that these subsets play a pivotal, instructive role during the onset of skeletogenesis. To further dissect their developmental potential, we evaluated both single-cell entropy and CytoTRACE scores across the skeletal cell subsets (Fig. 2C). Skeprogenitor_1 emerged with the highest values for both metrics, highlighting its pronounced stemness and differentiation potential (Fig. 2C). This is corroborated by the finding that Skeprogenitor_1/2/3 subsets are more abundant in the hindlimb than in the forelimb at E11.5, result notably consistent with prior documentation of delayed hindlimb skeletogenesis initiation, followed by subsequent developmental acceleration to achieve equivalence with its forelimb counterpart (Fig. S2B) [33,34,36]. To explore limb-specific differences among skeletal subsets, we compared differentially expressed genes in forelimb and hindlimb compartments. As previously reported, Pitx1, Tbx4, and genes from the Hoxc cluster, known markers of hindlimb identity, were upregulated in all hindlimb progenitor subsets, whereas Tbx5 and Hoxb genes, associated with forelimb development, were enriched in their forelimb counterparts (Fig. S2C) [33,34,36]. Strikingly, despite limb-specific genetic programs, key signaling pathways such as BMP, FGF, Hedgehog, and retinoic acid (RA) showed similar levels in both limb buds at the outset of skeletal differentiation [37,38]. However, these pathways exhibited divergent dynamics over time, with signaling intensifying in the forelimb and diminishing in the hindlimb as differentiation progressed (Fig. 2D). Together, these findings reveal not only the distinct developmental potential of skeletal progenitor subsets during embryogenesis but also the molecular mechanisms underlying the asynchronous patterns of limb outgrowth.

To validate the stemness of these putative skeletal progenitors, we assessed the expression of well-recognized skeletal stem cell (SSC) markers (Fig. 2E). Skeprogenitor_1 through Skeprogenitor_3 robustly expressed multiple genes including Foxp1/2, Postn, Pdgfra, Pdpn, Cadm1, Ncam1, Ebf1, and Ctsk (Fig. 2E) [2,15,16,18,[39], [40], [41]]. Immunostaining confirmed the spatiotemporal expansion of these SSPCs marked by CTSK and POSTN during the formation of the primary ossification center (POC) from E10.5 to E14.5 (Fig. 2F). Analysis of dynamic gene expression across this developmental trajectory unveiled a temporal cascade of transcriptional events involving the sequential activation of pathways such as the cell cycle, cell adhesion, Wnt signaling, gap junction formation, extracellular matrix organization, and the PI3K–Akt signaling pathway (Fig. S2D). These observations underscore the tightly coordinated molecular processes governing the activation and differentiation of SSPCs during early skeletogenesis.

Identification of CD140a + PDPN + SSPCs

To investigate the evolutionary conservation of SSPCs, we performed cross-species comparative analyses between murine skeletal subsets and their human counterparts [2]. Murine skeletogenic progenitor subsets exhibited transcriptional homology with human BMSCs, LBDMCs, and eSSPCs, whereas murine chondrogenic subsets demonstrated similarities with human chondrogenic populations (Fig. S3A). Integrated PAGA trajectory mapping across all murine and human skeletal subsets further delineated conserved topological relationships. Notably, murine Skeprogenitor 1/2/3 subsets co-localized with human skeletal progenitor populations (BMSC, eSSPC, LBDMC) within the combinatorial graph (Fig. S3B). These findings support the existence of conserved transcriptomic programs orchestrating skeletogenesis during embryonic development across species.

We sought to prospectively isolate SSPCs by identifying discriminatory cell-surface markers. Notably, Pdgfra [15] and Pdpn [2,16], previously validated for enriching skeletal stem cells, were abundantly co-expressed in SSPCs (Fig. 2E). To assess their utility for isolating SSPCs in developing long bones, co-expression analysis was performed on denoised transcriptomic matrices (MAGIC-imputed), revealing robust synergistic expression of Pdgfra/Pdpn with established SSC markers (Foxp1/2, Postn, Cadm1, and Hmmr; Fig. S3C) [2,18,40,42]. This concordance substantiates their suitability for prospective enrichment.

Evaluation of developmental potency dynamics via proliferation and differentiation assays revealed progressively enhanced self-renewal capacity. SSPCs at E14.5 exhibited substantially greater accrual of Ki67+ cells compared to E12.5 counterparts (Fig. 3E). This augmented clonogenicity was corroborated by fibroblastic colony-forming unit (CFU-F) assays, wherein E14.5 SSPCs generated 6-fold more colonies than E12.5 cells (Fig. 3F). Augmented type II collagen synthesis in E14.5 SSPCs further underscores their commitment to chondrogenesis, a prerequisite for endochondral ossification (Fig. 3H) [43,44].

Endothelial heterogeneity analysis revealed a subset of skeletogenesis-coupling EC subset enriched by CD31+EMCN+

To investigate the potential role of vasculogenesis in skeletal morphogenesis, we conducted a correlation analysis of cell cluster abundance between endothelial cells and other cell subsets. Notably, a negative correlation was observed between endothelial subsets and distal mesenchymal populations, whereas Skeprogenitor_1, Skeprogenitor_2, and Skeprogenitor_3 exhibited positive correlations with endothelial cells, implying a coordinated developmental relationship between skeletogenesis and angiogenesis (Fig. S4A). Subsequent sub-clustering of endothelial cells identified five distinct subsets: two early EC populations marked by Eef1g (EC1) and Nme2 (EC2), an arterial-related subset enriched in Gja4 (EC3), and two late-stage subsets defined by Fdx1l (EC4) and Itm2a (EC5) (Fig. 4A–C and Table S2). Intriguingly, the Fdx1l+ EC4 subset was exclusively localized to the forelimb bud, hinting at a specialized function in forelimb developmental patterning (Fig. S4B). Further cell cycle analysis demonstrated that EC3 maintained a quiescent state, in contrast to the more proliferative behavior of the remaining EC subsets (Fig. 4D). Analysis of arterial and venous signature scores across these subsets revealed that EC3 exhibited significantly elevated arterial scores, supporting its identity as an arterial endothelial population (Fig. 4E). Recent studies have identified three EC subtypes in long bones: type E (CD31+ EMCNlow/-), type H (CD31+ EMCN+), and type L (CD31low EMCNlow), with type H being particularly important for osteogenesis and bone repair following injury [17,24,45]. To evaluate whether this classification applies to developing embryonic skeletal tissues, we performed gene set variation analysis (GSVA) using signature gene sets corresponding to the three EC subtypes. The results revealed that EC3 exhibited the highest type E signature score, whereas EC2 displayed the highest type H signature score, with both subsets showing minimal type L signature enrichment. This suggests a potential correspondence between the embryonic EC subpopulations and the previously described adult EC subclasses (Fig. 4F). The expression of Pecam1 and Emcn, two markers commonly used to delineate type E and type H ECs, showed strong positive correlations and were capable of enriching EC3 and EC2 populations (Fig. 4G). To further validate these molecular signatures, we conducted flow cytometry analysis using CD31 and EMCN as surface markers. The data revealed a transition from the type E (CD31+ EMCNlow/-) to the type H (CD31+ EMCN+) phenotype during endothelial maturation (Fig. 4H and S4C). Immunostaining studies further demonstrated that both type E and type H ECs accumulated specifically in the periosteum and bone marrow at embryonic day 14.5, suggesting that these two EC subsets participant in vascular ingression during skeletal development (Fig. 4I and J).

Assessment of trilineage differentiation potential demonstrated that type E EC co-culture augmented chondrogenesis in SSPCs, evidenced by elevated chondroitin sulfate deposition via Alcian blue staining (Fig. 5D). Conversely, type H EC co-culture promoted osteogenic commitment, indicated by enhanced mineralized matrix formation by Alizarin red S staining (Fig. 5D). Robust type II collagen synthesis further established the critically supportive role of type E ECs in SSPC chondrogenic programming (Fig. 5E). Conspicuously absent were differences in adipogenic potential, indicating negligible EC involvement in stromal lineage specification (Fig. 5D).

To validate these findings in vivo, we co-engrafted SSPCs with each EC subset via renal subcapsular transplantation (Fig. 5B). Histological analysis by Movat pentachrome staining of grafts harvested at 4 weeks revealed profound divergence. SSPCs co-transplanted with type E ECs underwent robust endochondral ossification, characterized by cartilaginous anlagen transitioning to trabecular bone (Fig. 5F). By contrast, type H EC co-transplants yielded scant mineralized tissue but abundant fibrous matrix (Fig. 5F). Corroborating this, pronounced type II collagen deposition was observed exclusively in type E EC-derived grafts (Fig. 5G). Collectively, these data establish type E ECs as essential regulators of SSPC chondrogenic priming, a pivotal determinant governing endochondral ossification during skeletogenesis.

To investigate the function of type E ECs in the development of axial bones characterized by intramembranous ossification, two types of ECs and SSPCs were isolated from E14.5 murine calvarial bones. Subsequent co-culture systems and trilineage differentiation assays (Fig. 5H) revealed that SSPCs cocultured with type E ECs exhibited significantly enhanced chondrogenic potential relative to SSPCs cultured alone or with type H ECs, correlating with augmented type II collagen synthesis (Fig. 5I and J). Conversely, coculture with type H ECs yielded superior osteogenic differentiation compared to other two groups (Fig. 5I). Adipogenic potential in SSPCs remained unaffected by either EC subtype (Fig. 5I). These data collectively suggest that type E ECs stimulate the chondrogenic potential of skeletal progenitors engaging in endochondral and intramembranous ossification pathways.

To dissect the mechanistic basis of type E EC-driven regulation, we performed ligand-receptor interaction profiling via NicheNet [46]. Significant enrichment of pathways involving TGFB1-TGFR1/2, FGF1-FGFR1, and BMP2/4-BMPR1A/1B/2, implicates their role in transducing EC-derived signals to SSPCs (Fig. S5A). We observed a significant upregulation of Bmp2 rather than Bmp4 gene expression in type E EC compared to type H EC (Fig. S5B and S5C). Immunostaining analysis demonstrated that type E ECs synthesize significantly higher levels of BMP2 compared to their type H ECs counterparts (Fig. S5D), indicating that they serve as the primary source of this protein. Furthermore, SSPCs co-cultured with type E EC demonstrated marked activation of predicted BMP signaling pathway targets including Id1, Msx1/2, and Sox9, along with enhanced Col2 expression (Fig. S5E and S5F), which aligns with established roles for these pathways in regulating chondrogenic differentiation [[47], [48], [49], [50]]. Notably, Pharmacological inhibition of BMP signaling (via BMPR antagonist CDD-1653) in type E EC-SSPC co-cultures abrogated chondrogenic/osteogenic potential and suppressed collagen II deposition (Fig. S5G). Additionally, we observed significant downregulation of ID1, MSX2, and SOX9 expression following BMP signaling inhibition (Fig. S5H). These data demonstrated that ID1, MSX2, and SOX9 function as critical mediators of type E EC-secreted BMP2 signaling in driving chondrogenic commitment of SSPCs.

In summary, our work unravels a specialized angiocrine function for type E ECs in orchestrating SSPC chondrogenic differentiation through BMP signaling, thereby coupling vascular morphogenesis to skeletal patterning during embryogenesis. This crosstalk mechanism illuminates the spatiotemporal coordination underpinning long bone development.

Discussion

Our study provides fundamental insights into the cellular and molecular transcriptomic profiles of embryonic skeletogenesis, with particular emphasis on the pivotal role of SSPCs and their regulatory crosstalk with specialized endothelial cell subtypes. Our findings reveal evolutionarily conserved principles governing mammalian limb development while resolving previously uncharacterized mechanisms of vascular-osteogenic coupling during early morphogenesis.

Following the initial characterization of skeletal stem cells residing within the postnatal growth plate, multiple distinct SSPC subtypes have been identified across diverse skeletal compartments, including long bones, calvariae, articulating joints, vertebrae, and the periodontium, both in homeostasis and pathophysiological contexts [[51], [52], [53], [54], [55], [56], [57], [58], [59]]. In this study, we center our investigation on early embryonic skeletogenesis (spanning E10.5 to E14.5), during which the long bone template differentiates from condensed limb bud mesenchyme exclusively through endochondral ossification. Our identification of a murine CD140a+PDPN+ SSPC population with chondro-osteogenic potential, peaking at E14.5 in developing long bones, demonstrates remarkable conservation between human and murine skeletogenesis. Cross-species transcriptomic alignment showed murine Skeprogenitor_1/2/3 subsets share core molecular signatures with human embryonic SSPCs and bone marrow stromal cells [2]. The conserved spatiotemporal emergence of these SSPCs, originating from mesenchymal progenitors, expanding through proliferative bursts, and executing chondrogenic and osteogenic lineage commitment, which mirrors established hallmarks of human skeletal development [1]. This phylogenetic preservation underscores SSPCs as fundamental architectural units orchestrating endochondral ossification across mammals. The dynamic enrichment of these progenitors specifically within nascent primary ossification centers further highlights their non-redundant role in skeletal formation.

Importantly, we elucidate a regulatory function for embryonic vasculature in skeletal patterning. While prior research established the critical role of type H ECs (CD31+EMCN+) in adult bone homeostasis and repair, promoting osteogenesis, their involvement in embryonic skeletogenesis remained ambiguous [24,60]. Through EC sub-clustering and functional validation, we demonstrate that type E ECs (CD31+EMCNlow/-) constitute the dominant angiocrine regulators during early limb development. Spatial mapping revealed type E ECs specifically co-localize with SSPCs in periosteal and bone marrow during primary ossification center formation, which contrasts starkly with the delayed emergence of type H ECs. Thus, our work demarcates a developmental shift in EC subtype functions, in which type E ECs drive initial skeletogenesis, while type H ECs maintain skeletal integrity later in life.

We establish that distinct EC subtypes differentially regulate SSPC lineage commitment. Through in vitro co-culture and in vivo renal capsule transplantation models, we demonstrate type E ECs are indispensable for SSPC chondrogenic priming, a prerequisite for endochondral ossification. SSPCs co-cultured with type E ECs exhibited enhanced chondrogenesis, whereas those with type H ECs favored osteogenic mineralization [18,24]. Intriguingly, renal grafts with SSPC-type E EC co-transplants recapitulated endochondral ossification with cartilage-capped bone structures, while SSPC-type H co-transplants yielded fibrous tissue. This functional divergence reveals a spatiotemporally segregated “relay regulation” paradigm. Type E ECs preserve chondrogenic potency via BMP signaling during embryogenesis, enabling cartilage template formation. Premature osteogenesis at this stage, driven by type H-like stimuli, would disrupt skeletal patterning. Type H ECs drive osteogenesis postnatally to mineralize the preformed cartilage scaffold and support bone remodeling.

Intriguingly, our investigations identified a distinct pericyte subpopulation exhibiting high expression of Pdgfrb, Rgs5, and Myh11 during skeletal development. Given the critical role of pericytes in vascular structure and their pro-osteogenic properties [[61], [62], [63]], the interactive regulation between pericytes and endothelial cells, particularly their molecular cross-talk in modulating type E endothelial cell specification and function, warrants further investigation. Elucidating these vascular networks throughout embryonic skeletal morphogenesis could yield critical mechanistic insights for developing targeted therapeutic interventions in orthopedic disorders.

Conclusion

Our study uncovers an intricate spatiotemporal coordination between angiogenesis and osteogenesis orchestrated by specialized EC subtypes and their target SSPCs. We establish vascular regulation of skeletogenesis is not a monolithic process but a stage-dependent dialogue where type E and type H ECs relay control over SSPC fate. This mechanism reveals potential therapeutic leverage points for regenerative medicine, where modulating specific angiocrine signals could redirect skeletal healing outcomes.
