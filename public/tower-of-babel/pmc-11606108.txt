Tips from an expert panel on the development of a clinical research protocol
Authors: Abdelrahman M Makram, Randa Elsheikh, Omar M Makram, Nguyen Thanh Van, Nguyen Hai Nam, Nguyen Khoi Quan, Nguyen Tran Minh Duc, Ngoc Quynh Tram Nguyen, Gibson Omwansa Javes, Sara S Elsheikh, Atsuko Imoto, Peter Lee, Norio Ohmagari, Hirotsugu Aiga, Yasuhiko Kamiya, Patricia Takako Endo, Nguyen Tien Huy
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractA research protocol is a document that outlines the proposed research idea and is submitted to funding agencies, institutions, or journals for approval. Writing a research protocol represents a challenge, particularly for early-career researchers. In this guide, we aim to provide detailed guidance with the key components and offer practical tips for crafting a research protocol in line with the various study designs. Specifically, the structure of a research protocol should contain the following items: (1) a title that is specific, catchy, and impressive within the word limitation; (2) an abstract that briefs the critical points of the study; (3) an introduction highlighting the study context from broad to narrow and defining the knowledge gap; (4) a justification underlining the significance of the proposed study; (5) Specific, Measurable, Attainable, Relevant, and Time-bound (SMART) objective(s) and aim(s); (6) a methodology covering seven sub-items, including [i] study design and settings, [ii] study subjects, [iii] sample size calculation and sampling, [iv] participants recruitment and follow-up, [v] questionnaire development, [vi] potential variables and outcomes, and [vii] data analysis plan; (7) dissemination of the results; (8) ethics and conflict of interests; (9) budgets analysis/ funding disclosure; and (10) references. This guide will give an overview of these steps and provide clear and concise tips on how to successfully draft a scientific protocol. With careful planning and appropriate guidance, it is possible to develop a well-structured and compelling protocol to obtain approval for the conduction of the study or funding from agencies, institutions, or organizations.Keywords: Research protocol, Clinical research, Grant proposal, Tips, Research question, Methods planning, Analysis planning

Introduction

A research protocol is a document that outlines the proposed research idea and is submitted to funding agencies, institutions, or journals for approval [1]. The ideal protocol should provide a clear and concise overview of the proposed research, including the research question, study design, data collection, analysis methods, preliminary timeline, and required funding [2].

Writing a research protocol can be a challenging task, particularly for researchers who are new to the process. However, with careful planning, it is possible to develop a well-structured and compelling protocol that will increase the likelihood of approval [1]. First, it is important to identify a research question that is feasible, relevant, and worthy of investigation [3]. Feasibility includes access to resources, ethical considerations, and the availability of participants. This question should be clearly defined, focused, of interest to the research community, and has the potential to contribute to new knowledge in the field [1]. The research question should be informed by a thorough review of the existing literature, which will provide a context for the proposed research and highlight gaps in the current knowledge that the study aims to address [2]. This can be done by using the evidence-based research (EBR) approach, where researchers should ensure that new studies are conducted only when a thorough evidence synthesis—whether existing or newly conducted—demonstrates a genuine need for further investigation. This approach not only optimizes the use of resources but also enhances the overall quality and relevance of scientific contributions [4–6].

Second, once the research question has been identified, the next step is proposing a study design that will enable the research question to be answered in a rigorous and systematic manner [3]. The study design should be carefully selected to ensure that the question can be accurately answered. In the same context, this design should be appropriate for the research context and the type of data that will be collected or created [1]. It is also important to consider the feasibility of the proposed design, including the availability of resources, the potential challenges and limitations, and the expected outcomes [2].

Third, the research protocol should include a detailed plan for data collection and analysis in agreement with the research question and study design [3]. The data collection and analysis methods should be clearly described, including the instruments, procedures, sample size, selection criteria, and data analysis techniques [1]. It is also important to consider the ethical implications of the research and to include a plan for obtaining informed consent and protecting the privacy and confidentiality of participants [2]. Furthermore, researchers must obtain ethical approval from an Institutional Review Board (IRB) or equivalent ethics committee, ensuring that the study meets all ethical standards. Importantly, the opinions and welfare of participants should be prioritized throughout the research process to maintain ethical integrity and participant trust.

Lastly, a discussion of the potential implications expected from the study should be included. This section should discuss the potential impact of the study on the clinical field, further research efforts, policy implications, as well as any limitations present in the proposed research. This provides a starting point for future research in the field and allows the reader to evaluate the importance and relevance of the proposed study.

Previous literature has focused on the general outline of research protocols and the benefit of having a research protocol in advance [7]. However, in this guide, we will provide an overview of the key components of publishable clinical research protocol for health sciences research and offer practical tips for crafting a successful protocol for the various study designs.

Title

Specificity is the key

When choosing a title for a clinical research protocol, it is crucial to consider both the audience and the primary purpose of the research. Avoid choosing a broad title, it should be concise and descriptive, often indicating functional relationships and research designs [8]. A title could use keywords that describe the focus of the research and provide readers with a clear understanding of the topic. It is also helpful to include specific information about the type of data collected or the methodology used [9]. An effective format to follow is the PICO criteria (target population, intervention, control/exposure, and outcome of interest) or any other appropriate tool like the SPIDER criteria (sample, phenomenon of interest, design, evaluation, and research type) and to include the study design [10].

First impressions matter

Your title will provide the first impression of your proposed research and various journals require a precise yet informative title [8]. For instance, the New England Journal of Medicine requires the titles to be concise and descriptive and not declarative [11]. Avoid using abbreviations or technical words unknown to the public.

Make it catchy

Review the titles of other related articles to make sure that yours is unique and stands out. This could be achieved by proposing several titles and searching datasets like Google Scholar and PubMed to find the highest cited keywords.

Keep an eye on the length

Lastly, you need to ensure compliance with journals’ guidelines especially when it comes to the length of the title and adding a short title with limited characters. The optimal length recommended by most journals is between 8 and 15 words [12, 13].

Abstract

The abstract of a research protocol should include a brief introduction of the chosen topic, the rationale (e.g., gap statement) of the study, the objective(s)/aim(s), and the methodology (e.g., study design, population, intervention, and possible comparators, collected variables, and the main outcomes. Many authors such as Tullu have developed a checklist for abstract writing [8]. Adhering to one of those checklists can help draft a concise and comprehensive abstract.

Introduction/Background

The introduction aims to explain why the study is needed and briefly explain why you intend to conduct the study. As mentioned earlier, the introduction should correspond to the research need identified using the EBR principles [14].

Broad to narrow

Start by putting your work in a broader context, then focus on your specific research question(s) so that the reader may clearly understand the significance of your research. End your introduction by clearly stating the research problem or gap you intend to work on followed by the rationale of your research. If possible, briefly mention the research objectives and formulate the tested hypothesis [14].

Think of the introduction as an inverted pyramid

The introduction should mentally prepare the reader for the structure of the document to follow and should be structured to cover three main sections [15]:

Context: The first part should state the context/scenario of the study. A literature review can be included in or after this section. This helps the reader to (1) understand what has been done and what is “known” to put your work into context, (2) identify the gaps in your chosen topic and highlight the relevance of your work, (3) weigh the evidence, and (4) understand the research question.

A literature review

When reviewing the literature, you should do it critically as if you were writing a review article. Start with a general overview of the topic you want to research and gradually narrow down your scope. A good way to introduce the reader to the topic is by following a chronological order starting with the older research to the most recent. When introducing the research done on your chosen topic, use recent and reliable references and rephrase briefly, concisely, and precisely the previous work ensuring you highlight similarities, differences, and limitations. Finally, address the current consensus on the topic and possible unknowns [5, 15].

Justification

Start with why

The research justification or the “problem” statement is a problem or issue the research aims to address. This part needs to convince the reviewers when assessing your protocol so that they can make an informed judgment. This step should include one or two sentences identifying the critical gaps, significance, and novelty of the research topic and indicating why the current research is pivotal. The justification should be linked directly and logically with the introductory section of the research protocol [16].

The legitimacy of the research can be justified after ensuring the adherence to the EBR principles, which may go along the following criteria [5]:

Provide references such as a few studies in different levels (i.e., global, country, local area, specific population) that have explored the research question proposed.

Brief contradicted results based on evidenced-based studies such as systematic review, RCT, or other reputable information sources.

Objectives

Separate the aim from the objectives

The aims and objectives of a study aim to express precisely and concisely what you intend to achieve through the conduction of this project. The aim represents a broader statement of your set goals, highlighting their long-term impact. Contrastingly, the objectives state in detail the intended time-bound achievements. In other words, the objectives break down the aim into smaller, more manageable tasks [17]. An example is available in Table 1.

Be SMART

The general objective (or aim) must be SMART, i.e., Specific, Measurable, Attainable, Relevant, and Time-bound. Briefly, the SMART objectives could be written using the OITT framework, i.e., stating the intended outcome(s), their indicator, target level, and time frame. To qualify as an outcome, the objective needs to be a feasible short-term goal that can be achieved through the intended project. To be an indicator, the goal needs to be measurable quantitatively. To qualify as a target, the intended change needs to correlate quantitatively to the indicator. Finally, time frames need to be specified in terms of specific dates, periods, or frequencies [18]. An example of a SMART objective could be to use drug X to achieve a reduction of disease X prevalence in community X from 40 to 20% by the end of the year. In this example, the specific outcome is the use of drug X to reduce disease X prevalence, the indicator is disease X, the attainable target is the reduction in prevalence from 40 to 20%, and the time frame is the end of the year.

Methodology [19, 20]

Study design

Always mention the study design

You should provide a clear declaration and description of the proposed design in this section, along with an explanation of why this specific study design was chosen. When writing a research protocol, the goal is to convince the audience that the choice of the study design will effectively address the problem. To captivate the readers, the protocol needs to express that the methodological steps are appropriate for the study and should be closely tied to the aims and objectives. It is necessary to mention the factors that led to the research design being chosen above other viable options. You should also disclose whether any guidelines or checklists were used in the study design and explain to the readers how you intend to register your protocol.

Be clear and descriptive

Make sure to describe the details of your intended study design. The steps for the implementation of the study design should be detailed in a manner that can be replicated. The study design provides a roadmap for the researcher to follow, and the future tense should be applied in its writing. The study could be observational or experimental; descriptive or analytical; qualitative, quantitative, or both; cross-sectional or longitudinal; prospective or retrospective; single-arm or multi-arm; single-center or multi-center; laboratory, community-based, school-based, healthcare setting-based, or others.

Setting

It always starts with “where”

Clearly describe the type of setting and the location where the investigation will be conducted, the reason for selecting this setting, as well as the period of data collection. The type of setting can be a laboratory, a research center, a hospital, a health department, etc. The location should include the country where the investigation will take place and the region if the investigation is targeting a specific population. If the study will take place in more than one setting, each one should be described in detail.

Map it

It is recommended to include a map of the site, if applicable.

Unit of analysis

Then comes the “who”, “whom”, or “what”

Ensure to include a clear description with explicit selection (recruitment) criteria (inclusion and exclusion criteria). The included unit of analysis may be a person, animal, or even a previous study. Describe how these units will be screened for eligibility; the criteria that define who/what will be included or excluded in your final study sample; and indicate specifically whether you will include or exclude any special populations like minors, pregnant women, patients with emergency conditions, cognitively impaired adults, economically or educationally disadvantaged participants, …etc. Describe if a control group will be included.

Sample size

Size matters

While this may not be necessary for some studies, following the identification of study subjects and settings, you must define the sample size and sampling methods. The key question with sampling lies in the sample size to ensure both internal and external validity of the study results [21], which depends on the study approach and the outcomes of interest (mainly the primary outcome) that investigators have selected. In qualitative studies, where the aim is the depth of data rather than representativeness from a sample to a population, sample size is not a significant issue. Hence, the sample size in qualitative approaches is relatively small to serve the volume of data needed to be analyzed [22]. On the other hand, sample size plays a crucial role in quantitative studies since a large sample size is required to be representative of the population. In either case, investigators need to determine two essential factors that account for an adequately sized sample: numbers needed to fulfill the requirements of a study design and the sample size needed to detect meaningful effects [23].

Beware of the traps

A larger sample size than needed is costly and lengthens the study duration It also poses a greater ethical concern in studies dealing with human subjects as there is the possibility to take risks without additional benefit. In contrast, a smaller sample size than needed exposes enrolled subjects to the risk of not benefitting from the study due to the lack of generalizability, hence is also deemed unethical. Therefore, the ideal protocol should provide information about the sample size and rationale for the targeted numbers [24].

Let’s talk real tips

The sample size section should include three subheadings:

Sample size calculation: a detailed description of how the sample size will be estimated. This should include a reference of the methodology used, which can be performed manually or using statistical software. The sample size is generally increased by 10–15%, or by a proportion the investigators can justify, to compensate for expected dropout, incomplete records, and other study-related problems [25]. The expected sample size, as well as the dropout rate, is calculated based on published studies.

Recruitment method

According to Thoma et al., over 80% of clinical trials fail secondary to the under-enrolment of qualified participants [26]. Therefore, you need to have a detailed participant recruitment and retention plan over the study course to ensure that the study can be successfully completed.

In this section, you must fully describe the processes and methods of participant recruitment. This includes the following aspects:

The period in which each step of recruitment occurs.

Don’t forget the barriers, inclusion, and diversity

Describe any differences in the recruitment procedures that involve, for example, a language other than the default study language. If translation is needed at any stage of the study, you should indicate the steps to ensure appropriate translation and the personnel responsible for translating the materials.

Hear their concerns

It is recommended to do the piloting of the questionnaire face-to-face to increase the engagement of the tested subjects and thoroughly discuss the whole experience of going through the questionnaire and if they felt uncomfortable answering some questions. An audio recording or note-taking (after consent) of the discussions would help as well [32].

Variables and outcomes, data collection, and follow-up

Search for the “True North”

One of the most critical aspects of any research project is the study's eventual findings. This is the study's "True North," a reference point for all subsequent investigations. The outcome measures are subjected to statistical analysis, and inferences are drawn therefrom. Negligently focusing on the wrong outcome variables is a common cause of erroneous findings in scientific studies. To make a wise decision, you must consider not only the overall situation but also the specifics of the inquiry being conducted. Unfortunately, there is no universal set of guidelines that can be applied. But you can always refer to previous studies.

Choose the methods wisely

During data collection, it is critical to check that all necessary fields are filled in and that the information was obtained lawfully and ethically. Quantitative, qualitative, and mixed approaches can be used to gather information. When compared to qualitative approaches, quantitative methods are faster and more cost-effective. Moreover, comparing results is simple because quantitative methods are so uniformly standardized. Interviews, open-ended questions, focus groups, case studies, etc. are all examples of qualitative data collection methods used in the pursuit of a more in-depth understanding. The topic of your study and your research questions will determine the approach you take to gathering information.

Statistical analysis

It’s an art and skill

Performing data analysis requires a few steps, including collecting, cleaning, and organizing the data. These steps, which typically involve software packages, are essential in getting the data ready for use. Please note that data analysis may make use of either quantitative methods, qualitative methods, or both (mixed methods). Since quantitative data is inherently numerical, its analysis necessitates dealing with numerical variables such as percentages, calculations, and measurements. To control data and reveal the insights beneath, quantitative data analysis typically involves the use of algorithms, mathematical analysis tools, and software. Information that cannot be reduced to a number is called qualitative. In qualitative data analysis, you deal with things like labels and properties, as well as categorical variables like percentages and counts. A data analyst may also use first-hand or participant observation methods; conduct interviews or focus groups; or review documents and artifacts. It is imperative that you state how you intend to perform your analysis and why a particular analytical test will be selected over the other.

Results dissemination

You finished your investigation

This part is dedicated to describing what will happen to the investigation after being concluded. For example, you may only want to present the data as a thesis to complete your degree or you may want to publish it in a peer-reviewed journal. If the participants will be informed about the results of the study, you need to state how and when this will happen. If public dissemination will be done, describe how you intend to conduct the process (e.g., through media).

Ethical considerations [33, 34]

Ethical approval

Don’t miss a beat

Various ethical issues should be considered in every study. For starters, a protocol must always report if an ethical or Institutional Review Board approval will be or was obtained. If it was obtained, you would be encouraged to share the approval number, date of approval, and the approving committee.

Potential risks and benefits

Risks are inevitable

Mostly no study is devoid of potential risks. Stating any minimal risks (e.g., anxiety) that can affect the participants is a must. Even in a survey study, the risks may include the time consumed and economic burdens (e.g., out-of-pocket payment) to answer the survey and the consequences that can happen in case the security or confidentiality are broken. Discuss how you plan to minimize these risks and what will you do if they occur.

Exchange benefits

While direct benefits (e.g., offering a shopping gift card) may not be available, other benefits that can be reflected in one’s health should be also discussed. This may include a deepening of participants’ understanding of a certain disease or phenomenon or the use of a new drug to treat a specific condition. Lastly, in case of paying the participants for their participation, you should describe the amount, timing, and tax information (differs according to the country of investigation) of any incentive.

Informed consent and/or assent form

Let them know

This is an integral part of clinical research. You must describe where the consent process will take place, any waiting period between informing the prospective subject and obtaining the consent, and the whole process of the consent obtainment. Any consent should include the type of consent (written, verbal, electronic), the enrollment option (opt-in or opt-out), and whether the subject will be contacted for any follow-up surveys. The protocol should also include a quick description of who would be available to answer any questions before, during, or after signing the consent form. For vulnerable populations, (e.g., underage, mentally infirm, … etc.), the process and the on-behalf signee should be further detailed [35]. When subjects are directly involved in a study, a copy of the informed consent form must accompany the protocol. An informed consent form may not be required for secondary analysis studies. If a consent form is not legally required, detailed reasons should be provided [36].

Confidentiality

Secure it

If anonymity or confidentiality is guaranteed, briefly mention what data collection, data management, and data storage procedures will be used to ensure their protection [37]. You should also mention the timeframe for the data storage (e.g., for five years) as well as the fate of disenrolled participants’ data. If personal information is collected, the process of anonymization should be described in detail. It is also important to consider participant identification through the combination of certain inputs. For example, in small populations, asking for a specific age can make it easy to identify the identity of the person if coupled with the sex or country of origin. You should make sure that proper anonymization is practiced (consider replacing names and addresses with codes to ensure that no one can identify the participants in published reports of the research). These ethical issues are regulated by the Ethics Committee (EC), whose central principle is to ensure respect for human dignity. The committee reviews the protocol before starting the research and monitors every step to ensure compliance with the protocol. Therefore, it is worth meeting with a representative of the local ethics committee before developing your protocol, and, if possible, involving them in the reflection process. You will benefit from their advice and be able to resolve minor issues that might otherwise delay ethics committee approval.

Conflict of interest and other ethical matters

Don’t ever miss this

This part includes any conflict of interest that should be disclosed. A clear statement of any conflict (actual conflict or possible conflict, direct or indirect, financial or non-financial, conflict of duty, or conflict of roles).

Budget and financial disclosure

Money can influence the results

If applicable, you should provide all the details about any financial support received by the team for the honorarium of interviewees, paper copying, transport fee, and others.

Data availability statement

A critical aspect of publishing a study protocol is the inclusion of a comprehensive data availability statement. This statement enhances the transparency and reproducibility of research as well as the ethical sharing of data. Hence, you are urged to plan for open data sharing as part of the study design. It is not sufficient to simply state that "data is available on request." This approach can often result in data remaining inaccessible, even when requests are made. Instead, you should strive to make your data openly available through reputable data repositories or platforms. A robust data availability statement should include the data repository, access conditions, data description, timeline for availability, and contact information.

Cite it

A reference list should be provided. It is preferable to use reference management software to ensure that the list is coherent.

Authorship

Proper authorship planning is crucial to avoid conflicts at the later stages of the project. Having conflicts at a late stage may lead to further hindrance of submission for publication. Therefore, clear and transparent authorship agreements made in advance can prevent conflicts and ensure that each contributor's role is appropriately recognized. We urge you to define everyone’s roles early, follow the International Committee of Medical Journal Editors (ICMJE) guidelines, use authorship agreements, acknowledge contributors who do not meet the criteria for authorship, and maintain open and transparent communication with all contributors [38].

Conclusion

Writing a publishable research protocol is a challenging endeavor. With careful preparation and smart strategy, researchers can develop a well-structured, well-articulated, and persuasive protocol to increase the likelihood of funding approval and gain international recognition long before the journal article is published. In this guide, an expert panel has gathered all the crucial steps that the researcher needs to cover in the protocol, with detailed and concise guidance on the ten critical components aligned with the various study designs. We also provided practical tips beginning with the title and ending with the references to help the reader publish their “killer” protocol.

Data Availability Statement

A critical aspect of publishing a study protocol is the inclusion of a comprehensive data availability statement. This statement enhances the transparency and reproducibility of research as well as the ethical sharing of data. Hence, you are urged to plan for open data sharing as part of the study design. It is not sufficient to simply state that "data is available on request." This approach can often result in data remaining inaccessible, even when requests are made. Instead, you should strive to make your data openly available through reputable data repositories or platforms. A robust data availability statement should include the data repository, access conditions, data description, timeline for availability, and contact information.

Authorship

Proper authorship planning is crucial to avoid conflicts at the later stages of the project. Having conflicts at a late stage may lead to further hindrance of submission for publication. Therefore, clear and transparent authorship agreements made in advance can prevent conflicts and ensure that each contributor's role is appropriately recognized. We urge you to define everyone’s roles early, follow the International Committee of Medical Journal Editors (ICMJE) guidelines, use authorship agreements, acknowledge contributors who do not meet the criteria for authorship, and maintain open and transparent communication with all contributors [38].
