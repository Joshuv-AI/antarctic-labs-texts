Virtual Reality Reduces Office Blood Pressure and Anxiety in Patients Undergoing Ambulatory Blood Pressure Monitoring: Enhanced Response among Those With a Pronounced White Coat Response
Authors: Lyle Wesley Baker, Rohith Karthik, Vinay R. Munlapudi, Jonathan Balog, Michael A. Mao, Christopher L. Trautman, Yaohua Ma, Zhihui Judy Fang, Ivan E. Porter
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTOffice blood pressure (OBP) may be transiently elevated during clinical encounters because of situational anxiety and stress‐related physiologic arousal, contributing to diagnostic uncertainty and potential overtreatment. Virtual reality (VR)‐based relaxation can reduce stress‐related physiologic responses, but its effects on OBP and potential role in mitigating the white coat response among patients undergoing ambulatory blood pressure monitoring (ABPM) have not been evaluated. We conducted a prospective, single‐arm, pre‐post study among adults undergoing ABPM in a tertiary hypertension clinic. Participants completed paired OBP measurements and validated anxiety assessments before and after a brief immersive VR relaxation experience. ABPM results were used to classify predefined blood pressure subgroups. Seventy‐eight participants completed the study (mean age 62 ± 15 years; 47% female). Across the cohort, systolic OBP decreased by 6.1 mmHg (95% CI, −7.6 to −4.6; p < 0.001) and diastolic OBP by 1.4 mmHg (p = 0.002) following VR exposure. Participants with a pronounced white coat response, prespecified as an office‐to‐daytime ambulatory systolic BP difference ≥15 mmHg, experienced the greatest systolic BP reduction (−12.2 mmHg; p < 0.001), compared with smaller or nonsignificant changes in other subgroups (between‐group p = 0.005). Following VR exposure, 32.1% of participants were reclassified to a lower OBP category. Anxiety scores decreased significantly, and no adverse effects were reported. A brief VR relaxation intervention was associated with significant reductions in OBP and anxiety, particularly among participants with a pronounced white coat response, supporting its potential as an adjunct for improving OBP assessment in hypertension clinics.Clinical Trial RegistrationClinical Trials. gov Identifier: NCT06960057

Introduction

White coat hypertension (WCH) describes elevated office blood pressure (OBP) with normal out‐of‐office blood pressure (BP) in individuals not receiving antihypertensive therapy, whereas white coat effect (WCE) describes elevated OBP relative to out‐of‐office BP among individuals receiving antihypertensive therapy [1, 2]. Both reflect office‐associated BP elevation that may contribute to diagnostic uncertainty, overtreatment, or inappropriate treatment intensification. Accurate differentiation between sustained hypertension, WCH, and WCE is therefore essential for optimal management. Ambulatory blood pressure monitoring (ABPM) remains the gold standard for confirming these diagnoses and quantifying the office‐to‐ambulatory BP difference but is not universally available and may increase patient burden or cost [1, 2, 3, 4]. Current guidelines indicate that randomized controlled trial evidence supporting cardiovascular (CV) benefit from pharmacologic treatment of WCH is lacking [2]. Although observational data suggest WCH may confer modest CV risk, treatment in the setting of normal out‐of‐office BP may expose patients to medication‐related risks without proven clinical benefit [2, 5].

The physiological basis of the white coat response involves activation of the sympathetic nervous system through a stress‐induced “alerting reaction.” [6, 7] Anxiety activates central autonomic pathways, increasing catecholamine release, vascular resistance, and BP during clinical encounters [8, 9]. Emotional stress evokes similar autonomic patterns, producing transient increases in systolic and diastolic pressures [8]. The prevalence of WCH among individuals with elevated OBP is estimated to range from 15% to 30%, with higher rates among older adults [1, 10, 11, 12]. Despite guideline emphasis on confirming hypertension through out‐of‐office monitoring [2], the white coat response remains a persistent clinical challenge, reflecting the powerful role of psychophysiological arousal in BP measurement.

Although current hypertension guidelines recommend repeated OBP measurements and out‐of‐office confirmation with ABPM or home blood pressure monitoring to reduce diagnostic misclassification, situational anxiety and autonomic arousal during clinic encounters continue to contribute to elevated OBP readings in susceptible individuals. As a result, there remains interest in identifying pragmatic, clinic‐based interventions capable of reducing the acute physiologic stress response during OBP assessment.

Virtual Reality as a Tool for Relaxation and Physiologic Regulation

Virtual reality (VR) represents a novel approach for modulating emotional and physiological responses in healthcare settings. VR systems create immersive, computer‐generated environments that engage multiple senses to promote relaxation and a sense of presence [13]. These immersive experiences have demonstrated efficacy in reducing stress, anxiety, and physiological arousal across diverse clinical applications, including procedural pain management, perioperative care, and rehabilitation [14, 15, 16, 17, 18, 19]. Mindfulness‐based and nature‐themed VR interventions, in particular, have been associated with reductions in perceived stress and autonomic arousal, including BP [20]. Immersive VR may reduce engagement with external clinical stimuli through multisensory immersion and attentional redirection, potentially attenuating anticipatory stress responses during OBP measurement [13, 20].

Evidence to Date

Preliminary pilot studies suggest VR may reduce BP in clinical settings, though existing evidence is limited by methodological constraints. In a 2024 pilot study, Milon et al. reported a modest reduction in systolic BP following a 5‐min VR session among patients with presumed WCH; however, ABPM confirmation was not performed [21]. Similarly, Ma et al. demonstrated modest BP reductions during VR exposure in simulated clinical environments without evaluation in patients with ABPM‐characterized office‐ambulatory BP differences [22]. To date, VR interventions have not been systematically evaluated among patients undergoing ABPM with characterization of office‐ambulatory BP differences or examined for their potential impact on OBP classification.

Study Objective

The present study was designed to address these gaps by evaluating the acute effects of a standardized, brief VR relaxation intervention on OBP and self‐reported anxiety among adults undergoing ABPM in a tertiary hypertension center. Using a prospective, pre‐post design, participants completed paired OBP measurements and validated anxiety assessments immediately before and after a 5‐min immersive VR experience. ABPM results were subsequently used to classify BP phenotypes and characterize office‐ambulatory BP differences, enabling subgroup analyses of VR responsiveness. In addition to evaluating changes in mean OBP, we examined shifts in OBP classification following VR exposure. We hypothesized that VR exposure would produce significant reductions in OBP and anxiety, with the most pronounced effects among individuals exhibiting a pronounced white coat response.

Methods

Study Design and Setting

This was a prospective, single‐arm, pre‐post interventional study conducted at the Mayo Clinic Comprehensive Hypertension Center in Jacksonville, Florida. The study evaluated the acute effects of a brief VR relaxation experience on OBP and anxiety among adults undergoing ABPM. Each participant served as their own control, allowing within‐subject comparisons of pre‐ and post‐VR measurements. The study was reviewed and approved by the Mayo Clinic Institutional Review Board (IRB #2025‐318474‐H01) and registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT06960057) prior to enrollment. Participant enrollment occurred between June and August 2025.

Participants

Adults aged ≥18 years who were scheduled to undergo ABPM at Mayo Clinic Florida for any clinical indication were screened for eligibility. Exclusion criteria included a history of epilepsy or seizure disorder, cognitive impairment interfering with VR engagement, significant visual or vestibular disorders (including Ménière's disease or chronic vertigo), unstable CV disease (including myocardial infarction within the past year), chronic hypoxemic respiratory failure requiring oxygen therapy, cervical spine instability, pregnancy, or inability to provide informed consent.

Study Procedures

Ambulatory Blood Pressure Monitoring (Standard Care)

Participants underwent standard clinical ABPM fitting using Spacelabs OnTrak monitors without modification to routine clinic workflow. OBP was measured using an automated oscillometric device following standardized accuracy protocols. Following a 5‐min seated rest period, BP was measured in both upper arms, and the arm with the higher reading was used for all subsequent OBP measurements and ABPM placement. Three BP readings were obtained at 1‐min intervals, and the average was recorded as the OBP. The ABPM device recorded BP every 10 min during daytime hours and every 20 min overnight for either a 24‐h or 6‐h protocol, as determined by the ordering provider. The 6‐h protocol consisted exclusively of daytime BP measurements obtained every 10 min and did not include overnight monitoring. The ordering provider selected either the 24‐h or 6‐h protocol as part of routine clinical care, and no modifications were made for study purposes.

The averaged OBP obtained at the ABPM placement visit served as the clinic‐based comparator for average daytime ABPM values and was used for exploratory subgroup analyses. Participants were classified as exhibiting a pronounced white coat response if systolic OBP exceeded average daytime ABPM systolic BP by ≥15 mmHg; this prespecified threshold was selected to identify participants with a pronounced office‐associated BP response, as current hypertension guidelines do not define a specific office‐ABPM difference [2]. Masked hypertension was defined as office SBP <130 mmHg with average daytime ABPM systolic BP ≥130 mmHg. Participants not meeting criteria for either a pronounced white coat response or masked hypertension were grouped as normotensive or sustained hypertension.

Virtual Reality Intervention (Study Visit)

At the ABPM return visit (typically 24 h after placement), participants completed all study procedures in a quiet clinic room under standardized conditions. After returning the ABPM device, participants completed a brief REDCap survey capturing clinical characteristics, including history of hypertension or mental health conditions, psychiatric medication use, recent caffeine intake, and prior VR exposure. Baseline anxiety was assessed using the Visual Analog Scale (VAS) for anxiety and the State‐Trait Anxiety Inventory‐6 (STAI‐6).

Following a 5‐min seated rest period, pre‐VR OBP was obtained using the same automated protocol described above (three readings at 1‐min intervals, averaged). Participants were then fitted with a Meta Quest 3 VR headset displaying a standardized 5‐min immersive beach‐based relaxation environment with ocean scenery and ambient sounds (Gratitui: Real Beaches & Meditation application; Video S1). A 5‐min VR exposure was selected pragmatically to balance physiologic relaxation with feasibility in a busy outpatient hypertension clinic workflow.

Immediately following VR exposure, participants remained seated for a brief 20‐s reorientation period, after which a post‐VR OBP was obtained using the identical automated protocol. Participants then repeated the VAS and STAI‐6 assessments and completed a post‐VR survey assessing comfort, enjoyment, perceived relaxation, and adverse effects. All study activities were completed during a single visit lasting approximately 30 min.

Outcome Measures

The primary outcome was the change in OBP (systolic and diastolic) from pre‐ to post‐VR exposure. Secondary outcomes included changes in self‐reported anxiety measured by the VAS and STAI‐6, as well as feasibility and tolerability assessed through participant‐reported comfort, enjoyment, and adverse effects. Exploratory outcomes included identification of clinical and demographic predictors of BP response and associations between changes in anxiety and changes in OBP. ABPM results were used to classify participants into predefined blood pressure subgroups for exploratory analyses.

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables were summarized as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median (interquartile range), and categorical variables as counts (percentages). Paired t‐tests were used to evaluate within‐subject differences before and after VR exposure. Analysis of variance (ANOVA) or Fisher's exact tests were used for between‐group comparisons, as appropriate. Exploratory descriptive analyses were also performed to evaluate changes in OBP classification before and after VR exposure using blood pressure categories defined according to the 2025 American Heart Association/American College of Cardiology (AHA/ACC) hypertension guidelines [2].

Given the exploratory pilot nature of this study and lack of prior ABPM‐validated VR data in this population, a formal a priori power calculation was not performed. The sample size was determined pragmatically based on anticipated recruitment feasibility during the study period.

To assess the robustness of the findings, post hoc sensitivity analyses were performed after excluding kidney transplant recipients and, separately, after excluding participants who underwent the abbreviated 6‐h daytime‐only ABPM protocol.

All tests were two‐sided, and p values <0.05 were considered statistically significant. Analyses were performed using SAS version 9.4 and R version 4.4.1.

Ethical Considerations and Safety

The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Mayo Clinic Institutional Review Board (IRB #2025‐318474‐H01) and conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. All participants provided written informed consent before any study procedures were performed. The VR intervention was classified as minimal risk. Participants were informed of possible transient symptoms such as dizziness or visual strain, and a trained study staff member remained present throughout VR exposure.

Results

Participant Flow

A total of 368 patients were screened for participation in the study (Figure 1). Of these, 79 participants provided consent and completed both the ABPM and VR study visit. One participant was subsequently excluded due to inadequate ABPM data, yielding a final analytic cohort of 78 participants. Fifteen participants consented but did not complete the VR intervention study visit, 27 expressed interest but did not proceed with enrollment, 116 declined participation, and 113 did not respond to the recruitment invitation. Eighteen individuals were found to be ineligible based on predefined exclusion criteria. No participants discontinued the study due to adverse effects related to VR exposure.

Primary Outcome: Change in Office Blood Pressure and Heart Rate Pre‐ and Post‐VR

Among all participants, OBP decreased significantly following the VR intervention (Table 2). Mean SBP declined from 131.3 ± 23.6 mmHg pre‐VR to 125.2 ± 21.2 mmHg post‐VR (mean change, Δ = −6.1 mmHg; 95% CI, −7.6 to −4.6; p < 0.001). Diastolic blood pressure (DBP) decreased from 74.9 ± 11.0 mmHg to 73.5 ± 10.6 mmHg (Δ = −1.4 mmHg; 95% CI, −2.3 to −0.5; p = 0.002). Heart rate showed a nonsignificant trend toward reduction, decreasing from 72.3 ± 11.1 BPM to 71.5 ± 10.9 BPM (Δ = −0.8 BPM; 95% CI, −1.6 to 0.1; p = 0.085).

Secondary Outcome: Anxiety, Feasibility, and Tolerability

Both self‐reported anxiety measures decreased significantly following VR exposure (Table 2). Mean VAS anxiety scores decreased from 2.6 ± 2.3 pre‐VR to 1.4 ± 1.9 post‐VR (mean change, Δ = −1.1; 95% CI, −1.5 to −0.8; p < 0.001). Similarly, STAI‐6 scores declined from 10.0 ± 3.1 to 7.9 ± 2.0 (Δ = −2.1; 95% CI, −2.8 to −1.4; p < 0.001). Despite relatively low baseline anxiety scores overall, significant reductions were observed following VR exposure. Effect sizes (Cohen's d) indicated moderate to large improvements across outcomes (d = 0.89 for SBP, 0.36 for DBP, and 0.72–0.73 for STAI‐6 and VAS), consistent with moderate‐to‐large within‐subject effect sizes across BP and anxiety outcomes (Table 2).

Feasibility and tolerability were excellent (Table 5). Over half of participants (54%) described the VR session as “very immersive,” and 59% rated it as “very relaxing.” Most participants (62%) reported feeling less anxious after the session (23% “much less,” 39% “somewhat less”). The headset was well tolerated, with 71% reporting it as comfortable, 23% neutral, and only 6% describing discomfort. No adverse events, including dizziness, nausea, or visual strain, were reported.

Blood Pressure and Anxiety Changes by ABPM‐Characterized Study Subgroup

When stratified by ABPM‐characterized study subgroup, the magnitude of VR‐induced reductions in OBP and anxiety differed among groups (Table 6). Participants exhibiting a pronounced white coat response experienced the greatest SBP reduction, decreasing by 12.2 mmHg (95% CI, −16.2 to −8.2; p < 0.001), compared with smaller reductions that did not reach statistical significance in the masked hypertension group (−4.5 mmHg; p = 0.085) and significant but more modest decreases among those with normotension or sustained hypertension (−5.2 mmHg; p < 0.001). The between‐group difference in systolic change was significant (overall p = 0.005). The distribution of SBP changes across ABPM‐characterized study subgroups is shown in Figure 2.

DBP fell significantly among participants with a pronounced white coat response (−3.5 mmHg; p = 0.004) but not among the masked hypertension or normotension/sustained hypertension subgroups (overall p = 0.055). Mean heart rate did not change significantly within any study subgroup.

Self‐reported anxiety scores also improved across all study subgroups, with the largest decrease observed among participants with a pronounced white coat response. STAI‐6 scores declined by 3.1 points (p = 0.04) in participants with a pronounced white coat response, compared with reductions of 1.4 points (p = 0.013) in the masked hypertension subgroup, and 2.1 points (p < 0.001) in sustained/normotensive participants (overall p = 0.37). VAS anxiety scores showed a similar pattern, decreasing by 1.4 points (p = 0.016) in the white coat response subgroup compared with smaller changes in the other study subgroups.

Sensitivity Analyses

The principal SBP findings remained consistent in sensitivity analyses. After exclusion of the 30 kidney transplant recipients (remaining n = 48), office SBP decreased by 6.8 mmHg overall, and the greater reduction among participants with a pronounced white coat response persisted (−12.2 vs. −4.5 mmHg in masked hypertension and −5.3 mmHg in normotension/sustained hypertension; between‐group p = 0.011) (Table S1). Similarly, after exclusion of the five participants who underwent the abbreviated 6‐h ABPM protocol (remaining n = 73), office SBP decreased by 5.9 mmHg overall, with corresponding subgroup reductions of −11.1, −4.5, and −5.2 mmHg, respectively (between‐group p = 0.021) (Table S2).

Discussion

In this prospective, single‐arm study conducted within a comprehensive hypertension clinic, a brief, standardized VR relaxation intervention was associated with significant reductions in OBP and self‐reported anxiety among adults undergoing ABPM. A single 5‐min VR exposure resulted in a mean SBP reduction of approximately 6 mmHg across the cohort, with substantially larger effects observed among individuals exhibiting a pronounced white coat response, defined by a prespecified office‐to‐daytime ambulatory systolic BP difference.

Importantly, VR exposure influenced not only mean OBP values but also hypertension classification. Nearly one‐third of participants were reclassified to a lower OBP category following the intervention, with the largest reduction observed among participants initially classified as Stage 2 hypertension. These findings suggest that acute reduction in stress‐related physiologic arousal during clinic encounters may influence OBP categorization and potentially reduce false‐positive hypertension classification in susceptible individuals.

The magnitude and subgroup specificity of the BP response support the hypothesis that VR may attenuate the autonomic mechanisms underlying the white coat response. Participants exhibiting a pronounced white coat response experienced a mean SBP reduction of 12 mmHg, more than double that observed in the overall cohort, whereas those with masked hypertension demonstrated smaller, nonsignificant changes. This pattern is biologically plausible given the established role of anxiety‐driven sympathetic activation in transient OBP elevations. By inducing a relaxation response and attenuating sympathetic arousal, VR appears particularly effective in individuals whose elevated OBP is most closely linked to psychophysiologic stress. Because participants with a pronounced white coat response were identified by a prespecified office‐to‐daytime ambulatory systolic BP difference, larger reductions in OBP within this subgroup were partly anticipated. Accordingly, these findings should be interpreted as supporting the hypothesis that individuals with a greater office‐associated BP response may derive greater benefit from VR‐mediated relaxation, rather than as definitive evidence of a uniquely greater treatment effect. Importantly, this pattern remained consistent in sensitivity analyses excluding kidney transplant recipients and, separately, participants who underwent the abbreviated 6‐h ABPM protocol. The absence of increased within‐visit BP variability and the subgroup‐specific response argue against passive acclimatization or regression to the mean as the primary explanation for the observed BP reductions.

The absence of a significant change in heart rate suggests that the BP‐lowering effect of VR was not simply due to generalized autonomic slowing. Instead, VR may lower BP by diminishing engagement with the clinical environment and blunting anticipatory stress, thereby influencing vascular tone without necessarily altering heart rate. Although changes in BP and self‐reported anxiety were directionally similar, correlations between these measures were modest and did not reach statistical significance, suggesting that VR‐related BP reductions may not be fully explained by subjective anxiety improvement alone.

Previous studies examining VR‐based interventions for BP reduction have reported modest SBP reductions following immersive relaxation experiences but were limited by small sample sizes, restricted populations, and lack of ABPM confirmation. In contrast, the present study integrates VR into a real‐world hypertension clinic workflow and uses ABPM to rigorously characterize participants according to recognized BP phenotypes and the magnitude of their office‐associated BP response. This approach allows for more precise evaluation of VR responsiveness among participants exhibiting a pronounced white coat response and supports the potential diagnostic relevance of VR beyond general stress reduction.

Notably, the magnitude of SBP reduction observed among participants exhibiting a pronounced white coat response approaches thresholds that are clinically meaningful for BP classification. VR is not intended to replace guideline‐recommended confirmation with ABPM or home blood pressure monitoring, but rather to serve as a pragmatic strategy to improve OBP assessment in susceptible individuals. These findings raise the possibility that brief VR exposure during OBP measurement could reduce diagnostic misclassification and unnecessary antihypertensive treatment escalation among patients with anxiety‐mediated OBP elevation.

A key strength of this study is the assessment of BP measurement stability. Analysis of within‐visit SBP variability across three consecutive measurements obtained before and after VR exposure demonstrated no change despite significant reductions in mean SBP. This finding argues against increased measurement noise as the primary explanation for the observed reductions. Additionally, all pre‐ and post‐VR measurements were obtained during a single clinic encounter under standardized conditions, minimizing time‐dependent and environmental confounding. Together, these features strengthen the internal validity of the observed associations.

Beyond physiologic effects, VR was feasible, well tolerated, and acceptable within a busy hypertension clinic setting. The intervention required minimal staff training and was completed within a single visit, with the VR exposure itself lasting 5 min. Participants reported high levels of comfort and relaxation, and no adverse effects were observed. The use of commercially available hardware and widely accessible software further supports scalability and adaptability across clinical settings. Unlike conventional audio or video relaxation interventions, immersive VR simultaneously minimizes visual and auditory exposure to the clinical environment while promoting a greater sense of presence within a calming virtual environment. This multisensory immersion may more effectively attenuate anticipatory stress responses than less immersive relaxation strategies, although future comparative studies are needed to determine whether VR provides incremental benefit over simpler relaxation approaches.

From an implementation standpoint, all study procedures were completed during a visit lasting approximately 30 min; however, this duration included research‐specific activities such as demographic data collection and pre‐ and post‐VR survey assessments. In routine clinical implementation, omission of these research components would be expected to reduce the incremental time burden, while the VR exposure itself lasted only 5 min, supporting the feasibility of integrating VR into outpatient clinic workflows without substantially increasing visit duration. During this research protocol, a trained study staff member remained present throughout VR exposure to ensure participant safety and protocol adherence. This level of supervision may represent a workflow consideration in routine clinical practice, even for a brief 5‐min intervention. Future implementation studies should determine whether VR can be delivered safely with intermittent observation, lower‐intensity supervision, or other workflow models.

Several limitations warrant consideration. The single‐arm, pre‐post design limits causal inference, and the absence of a control condition prevents definitive attribution of effects to VR‐specific mechanisms. The study assessed only short‐term responses to a single VR exposure, and the durability of BP reductions with repeated use remains unknown. Subgroup sample sizes, particularly within the pronounced white coat response and masked hypertension subgroups, were relatively small, which may limit statistical precision and increase the potential for type II error. Additionally, several subgroup and exploratory analyses were performed without formal adjustment for multiple comparisons. Accordingly, these findings should be interpreted as hypothesis‐generating.

A small proportion of participants completed an abbreviated 6‐h daytime‐only ABPM protocol as prescribed by the ordering provider, and kidney transplant recipients represented a substantial proportion of the cohort. Although the principal SBP findings remained materially similar in sensitivity analyses excluding kidney transplant recipients and, separately, participants who underwent the abbreviated 6‐h protocol, these cohort characteristics should be considered when interpreting the findings. In addition, the study was conducted at a single tertiary referral center with a high prevalence of complex comorbidities, which may limit generalizability. The study population was predominantly White and non‐Hispanic, which may further limit generalizability to broader and more diverse patient populations. Specific reasons for declining participation were not systematically collected, limiting interpretation regarding barriers to VR adoption or acceptability among nonparticipants.

Future randomized studies incorporating time‐matched seated control conditions or alternative relaxation comparators could further clarify VR‐specific effects. Future studies should also identify the clinical characteristics associated with greater responsiveness to VR and better define the patients most likely to benefit from this intervention. These findings provide preliminary pilot data supporting further investigation of VR as a pragmatic adjunct to improve OBP assessment. Additional work is also needed to determine whether repeated VR exposure improves long‐term concordance between office and ambulatory BP measurements, reduces unnecessary antihypertensive treatment escalation, and improves diagnostic accuracy during routine hypertension assessment.

Conclusion

In summary, a brief, immersive VR relaxation intervention was associated with significant reductions in OBP and anxiety, particularly among participants exhibiting a pronounced white coat response. Nearly one‐third of participants were reclassified to a lower OBP category following VR exposure, with substantial reductions observed in Stage 2 hypertension classification. These findings support the feasibility of VR as a patient‐centered adjunct to OBP assessment and provide preliminary data to inform future randomized studies evaluating diagnostic accuracy, workflow integration, and long‐term clinical utility in hypertension care.
