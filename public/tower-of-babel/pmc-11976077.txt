Evaluation of oral lesions and dental health in HIV-positive Saudi patients
Authors: Khalid Al Zoman, Khawlah Alshunaifi, Muteb Al-Mutairi, Hesham Altamimi, Atheer Binzoman, Sadeq Ali Al-Maweri, Abdulrahman Alrajhi, Yusra Tashkandy, Maha Al-Mozaini, Abdulaziz S Al Suwyed, Sultan Abdulaziz Al Mubarak
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesOral lesions are a serious complication of HIV infection and serve as clinical indicators of disease status. We conducted the current study to assess the prevalence of oral lesions and teeth condition in individuals with HIV in the Kingdom of Saudi Arabia (KSA).MethodsThis cross-sectional study included 264 patients recruited from the HIV dental clinic between January 2019 and March 2020. Participants were adults (aged ≥ 18 years) of Saudi nationality. We included 115 patients who were HIV-1 seropositive and149 matched healthy patients. Data were collected through patient interviews using a comprehensive questionnaire. Oral examinations were performed using artificial light, mouth mirrors, tweezers, gauzes, and wooden tongue depressors.ResultsThe average age of HIV-infected individuals was 43.22 ± 13.68 years, with 53.8 % being male. Dry mouth was more prevalent in HIV patients than in the control group (49.6 % vs. 33.1 %, p = 0.007). HIV patients demonstrated poorer oral hygiene, with fewer brushing their teeth at least twice daily (32.2 % vs. 50.3 %, p = 0.002) and a higher percentage not brushing regularly (23.5 % vs. 11.4 %, p = 0.011). Within the HIV cohort, the occurrence of dry mouth significantly correlated with dentition status. Neutrophil counts were significantly correlated with the type of screening test performed (r = 0.235; p = 0.011), antiretroviral medication (r = -0.205; p = 0.028), erythematous candidiasis (r = -0.192; p = 0.039), and the presence of hairy leukoplakia (r = -0.187; p = 0.046).ConclusionsHIV-positive individuals in KSA have poorer oral health and dentition status compared to healthy controls. Effective policies are needed to ensure adequate dental health in this high-risk group.Keywords: HIV, AIDs, Oral lesions, Dental, Saudi Arabia

Introduction

Acquired immunodeficiency syndrome (AIDS) poses a significant threat to human health. The primary cause of AIDS is human immunodeficiency virus (HIV), which belongs to the virus family Retroviridae and is classified under the genus Lentivirus (Lourenco et al., 2011). HIV can be transmitted through infected bodily fluids during sexual activity, blood transfusion, shared hypodermic needles, breastfeeding, and placental transfer from mother to fetus (Cann and Karn 1989). Since its discovery in 1983, HIV has caused over 35 million deaths. As of 2022, approximately 36.7 million individuals aged 15 and older are living with HIV (Koyio et al., 2014, Kumar et al., 2014, Mary et al., 2018).

Antiretroviral therapy (ART) is the only currently available treatment for individuals infected with HIV (Moodley and Wood, 2012, Mthethwa et al., 2013). Although ART does not cure HIV infection, it limits viral replication, reduces viral loads, and slows the progression of AIDS (Tami-Maury et al., 2011, Shu et al., 2022), effectively turning HIV into a chronic condition. However, long-term use of ART can lead to the development of multi-drug-resistant viruses and other adverse side effects. Additionally, ART is costly (Moodley and Wood, 2012, Mthethwa et al., 2013). Despite a global reduction in HIV infections due to effective ART, infection rates continue to rise in the Middle East and North Africa (Alsaywid et al., 2021, Alshammari et al., 2022).

HIV infects and destroys CD4 + T cells, leading to impaired immune defenses and an increased risk of opportunistic infections and tumor development. Therefore, the CD4 + cell count serves as a marker for disease progression (Oladokun et al., 2013, Ravi and Rao, 2015, Shu et al., 2020). HIV also disrupts cytokine production and cellular receptor engagement, thereby impairing CD8 + T-cell signaling. In response to HIV infection, the circulating CD8 + cell numbers typically increase, resulting in a reduced CD4/CD8 ratio (Ravi and Rao, 2015, Song et al., 2018, Shu et al., 2020).

Complications associated with HIV infection include cardiopulmonary disease, gastrointestinal disorders, neurological issues, and oral lesions. The most frequently observed oral lesions include oral thrush, herpetic infections, necrotizing ulcerative periodontitis, and Kaposi sarcoma (Batavia et al., 2016, Braz-Silva et al., 2017). These lesions are markers of immune suppression and can aid in primary recognition and diagnosis of HIV/AIDS (Bhayat et al., 2010). Moreover, oral lesions contribute to patient morbidity, affecting the mental and physiological well-being of infected individuals (Petruzzi et al., 2013). Maintaining oral health is critical to prevent microbial infections, which can be fatal in patients with HIV (Muller et al., 2015, Troya and Matarranz, 2020). HIV/AIDS-associated oral lesions are often ulcerative and painful, compromising the ability to eat and swallow, leading to malnutrition and further weakening of the already immunocompromised state. Besides their diagnostic importance, oral lesions are also clinically associated with CD4 + and CD8 + cell counts (Ravi and Rao, 2015, Song et al., 2018).

This study aimed to assess the condition of oral lesions and dental health in HIV-positive individuals in the Kingdom of Saudi Arabia (KSA) and to determine the relationship of dental caries with CD4± counts, neutrophil counts, and medication use among this population.

Material and Methods

Study Setting and Ethics

This cross-sectional comparative study was conducted at the HIV dental clinic of the King Faisal Specialist Hospital and Research Center (KFSHRC) in Riyadh, KSA. The study involved patients with HIV-1 and non-infected individuals as controls. The study adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki and was approval by the Research Ethics Committee and Research Advisory Council (RAC# 2181 123). All the participants provided written informed consent following institutional review board approval (KFSHRC).

Study population

In total, 264 patients were recruited from January 2019 to March 2020. The participants were adults (aged ≥ 18 years) of Saudi nationality. Among them, 115 were HIV-1 seropositive, but one patient was excluded because of missing data. The mean age of the HIV-1 group was 43.22 ± 13.68 years. The control group consisted of 149 matched individuals.

Data Collection

Data were gathered via interviews with patients using a comprehensive questionnaire with three sections. Section one involved the social and demographic information. Section two focused on oral hygiene and medical and dental histories, including details of dental complaints, oral lesions, and xerostomia. Section three included a clinical examination.

Oral examinations

The oral examinations were performed using artificial lights, mouth mirrors, tweezers, gauze, and wooden tongue depressors. The diagnostic criteria followed the World Health Organization (WHO) classification and diagnostic criteria for oral lesions in HIV infection (1993). Dentition status was assessed using the decayed, missing, and filled teeth (DMFT) index according to the WHO guidelines (Broadbent and Thomson 2005).

Statistical analysis

Frequency tables and percentages were computed to summarize the data. Pearson correlation analysis was used to assess relationships between the variables. Group comparisons were performed using one-way ANOVA. The association between risk factors and knowledge prevalence was evaluated using the odds ratio (OR) with 95 % confidence intervals (CIs). Multivariate logistic regression analysis was used to detect potential confounders. A p-value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. SPSS and R statistical software were employed for all analyses.

Results

Clinical and demographic Features of the study cohort

The HIV-1 infected group included 114 patients, while the control group consisted of 147 patients. The average age of individuals with HIV was 43.22 ± 13.68 years. Among them, 53.8 % were male and 46.2 % were female. A significantly higher percentage of HIV positive patients (77.4 %) resided in the city compared with 90.6 % in the control group (p = 0.042). Significant differences were also observed in education levels: 29.6 % of HIV-positive patients had a college/university education versus 57.7 % in the control group (p < 0.001); 16.5 % had only primary education compared to 8.1 % in the control group (p = 0.040); and 16.5 % had no education compared to 4.7 % in the control group (p = 0.002). There were significant differences in smoking status (28.7 % vs. 13.4 %, p = 0.002) and alcohol consumption (10.4 % vs. 0.7 %, p < 0.001) between the HIV-positive and control groups. Oral hygiene, systemic disease and medication status

The prevalence of dry mouth was higher in HIV-positive patients than in controls (49.6 % vs. 33.1 %, p = 0.007). Additionally, a higher number of HIV-positive patients did not brush their teeth regularly (13 % vs. 4 %, p = 0.011), fewer brushed their teeth at least twice daily (32.2 % vs. 50.3 %, p = 0.002), and many did not their brush regularly (23.5 % vs. 11.4 %, p = 0.011). No significant difference was found among denture wearers in the HIV-positive and control groups (6.1 % vs. 6.7 %, p = 0.84).

A significant difference was found in the use of anticoagulants, with 100 % of HIV patients using them compared to 88.6 % in the control group (p < 0.001). The use of other medications, including antihypertensive drugs, antidiabetic drugs, and aspirin, also differed significantly (31.5 %, p < 0.001). HIV-positive patients had notably lower CD4 and neutrophil counts. More patients in the HIV cohort were receiving antiretroviral medication compared to the control group (p < 0.001). Spearman correlation analysis

Within the HIV cohort, CD4 counts significantly correlated with aspirin medication (r = 0.252, p = 0.007) and neutrophil counts (r = 0.366, p < 0.001). CD4 counts also showed a significant correlation with erythematous candidiasis (r = -0.22, p = 0.018) and dentition status (r = 0.19, p = 0.042). Dry mouth significantly correlated with dentition status (r = 0.199, p = 0.033). Neutrophil counts significantly correlated with antiretroviral medication (r = -0.205, p = 0.028), and the occurrence of erythematous candidiasis (r = -0.192, p = 0.039), hairy leukoplakia (r = -0.187, p = 0.046), and high HIV plasma loads.

Discussion

Despite the global decrease in HIV prevalence, the number of HIV infections in the Middle East continues to increase. In KSA, the first Global AIDS Response Progress Report reported an approximately 10 % annual increase in new HIV-1 infections (Badreddine et al., 2007, Alzahrani, 2008). This increase has been associated with higher rates of oral manifestations and comorbidities (Aquino-Garcia et al., 2008, Anaya-Saavedra et al., 2013, Batavia et al., 2016, Braz-Silva et al., 2017, Rosa and Sufiawati, 2023). In this study, we investigated the oral and dental health of the growing HIV/AIDS population in KSA to highlight the need for effective policies to ensure adequate oral health.

Our cohort included 114 HIV-1 seropositive patients and 149 matched controls. We found that HIV-1 infected individuals in the region had some form of oral mucosal condition that likely affected their quality of life (Naidu et al., 2013, Oladokun et al., 2013, Petruzzi et al., 2013). These oral lesions likely resulted from the loss of immune function owing to the virus-mediated destruction of CD4 + lymphocytes, as a strong correlation between HIV infection status, HIV viral loads, low CD4 + cell counts, reduced neutrophil counts, and the occurrence of oral lesions was observed. Consistent with previous studies, oral candidiasis was the most common clinical finding in the HIV population (Naidu et al., 2013, Oladokun et al., 2013, Petruzzi et al., 2013) and was accompanied by a high frequency of dental caries (Meless et al., 2014). In HIV-positive individuals, general poor oral health and periodontal status were also observed compared to uninfected controls (Kumar et al., 2014). Collectively, these findings underscore the need to include oral health in the comprehensive care of HIV patients in KSA.

Highly active antiretroviral therapy (HAART) has successfully reduced the global morbidity and mortality due to HIV infection. Adherence to HAART also decreases the occurrence of oral lesions in HIV-positive individuals (Mthethwa et al., 2013). A recent meta-analysis highlighted a lower incidence of angular stomatitis, oral fungal infection, cold sores, and Kaposi tumors in patients adhering to HAART therapy. These positive effects are consistent with the strong correlation between HAART and CD4/CD8 ratio and an increase in CD4 counts (de Almeida et al., 2017, Shu et al., 2022).

This study found no significant association between age or sex and the occurrence of oral lesions in HIV/AIDS patients. However, younger individuals and those residing in urban areas showed higher HIV prevalence. Similarly, a previous study showed a notably higher risk of HIV infection among the urban poor, even though, in rural areas, poverty tends to show a weaker link with HIV infection. Measures of community-level correlations suggest that urban areas may have lower social cohesion compared to rural areas, and combined with pronounced socio-economic disparities; this makes the urban poor particularly vulnerable. In addition, another study reported epidemiological shifts and increased transmission rates within younger demographics and urban regions, possibly due to behavioral factors such as increased engagement in high-risk activities or gaps in preventive education and resources targeting this age group (Singh and Patra 2015). The development of oral lesions appeared to be HIV-mediated and not related to infection by opportunistic pathogens. This information can offer dental professionals, policymakers, and public health officials in KSA key insights into the target populations for dental care, helping to better meet the needs of people living with HIV/AIDS in the region. For dental professionals, understanding that oral lesions are not restricted to certain age or sex groups can guide them to screen for these issues more broadly among HIV/AIDS patients. For policymakers, the insight that urban areas have higher HIV prevalence suggests the need for increased resources and infrastructure for HIV/AIDS services in these locations. Policies can be developed to address gaps in dental care access for HIV/AIDS patients, particularly by focusing on urban centers with higher infection rates. Public health officials can use this information to target awareness campaigns and outreach programs for younger individuals in urban areas.

Our study has limitations. Firstly, we did not investigate whether the incidence of oral lesions was connected to the mode of transmission. Secondary, the assessment of dry mouth prevalence relied on patient self-reports rather than objective clinical measurements, which limits the accuracy of evaluating oral health in individuals with HIV. Thirdly, our cohort was also infected with HIV-1; thus, differences lesions occurrence among HIV-2 positive individuals cannot be ruled out. This highlights the need to assess the cultural characteristics of specific regions in studies on the prevalence of HIV/AIDS oral lesions.

Conclusions

HIV-positive individuals in KSA have poor oral health. There was a strong correlation between HIV infection status, high viral loads, low CD4 + cell counts, reduced neutrophil counts, and the occurrence of oral lesions. HIV-positive patients must receive HAART and effective dental treatment to ensure adequate oral health.
