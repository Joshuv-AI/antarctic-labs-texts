NatD promotes glioblastoma progression through epigenetic activation of FGFR4 signalling
Authors: Chan-Woo Kim, Taek-Yeol Jung, Chanil Kim, EungHan Kim, Heui Og Kim, Jiyeon Chae, Hyelee Kim, Ga-Yeon Ko, You-Jung Yeo, Ji-Young Park, Jaehyuk Pyo, Jeong Taeg Seo, Dokyeong Kim, Junseong Park, Seo Jin Kim, Minji An, Seok-Gu Kang, Jong Bae Park, Chul Hoon Kim, Seung-Hyun Jung, Sug Hyung Lee, Ho-Shik Kim, Hyun-Seok Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
N-terminal acetyltransferase D (NatD) acetylates histones H4 and H2A at serine 1 and has been implicated in oncogenesis, yet its role in glioblastoma (GBM) remains unexplored. Here, we show that NatD expression is significantly elevated in GBM at both transcript and protein levels. NatD knockdown markedly suppressed proliferation, colony formation, migration, invasion and tumoursphere formation in GBM cell lines and patient-derived models, indicating a critical role in maintaining cancer stem-like properties. These inhibitory effects were rescued by reintroduction of wild-type NatD but not by a catalytically inactive mutant, confirming dependence on its enzymatic activity. Transcriptomic profiling identified fibroblast growth factor receptor (FGFR) 4 as a key downstream effector. Mechanistically, NatD maintained N-terminal acetylation of histones H4 and H2A at the FGFR4 promoter, thereby antagonizing casein kinase 2α-mediated phosphorylation and sustaining an open chromatin state permissive for transcription. NatD depletion reduced FGFR4 expression and downstream ERK1/2 and AKT phosphorylation, whereas FGFR4 overexpression restored malignant phenotypes suppressed by NatD knockdown both in vitro and in xenograft models. Orthotopic xenograft models further demonstrated that NatD depletion suppresses intracranial tumour growth and prolongs survival, whereas FGFR4 re-expression partially restores tumour progression. Analysis of TCGA GBM datasets revealed that the positive correlation between NatD and FGFR4 expression is maintained across multiple molecular subtypes. Collectively, these findings identify NatD as an oncogenic regulator of GBM and underscore NatD–FGFR4 signalling as a promising therapeutic target.

Introduction

Glioblastoma (GBM), an IDH-wildtype diffuse astrocytic tumour classified as WHO grade IV, represents the most aggressive and prevalent primary brain tumour in adults1. GBM is characterized by rapid proliferation, diffuse infiltration into surrounding brain tissue and a high rate of recurrence2. Despite advances in multimodal therapy including surgery, radiotherapy and chemotherapy, the prognosis remains poor, with a median survival of only 12–15 months and recurrence almost inevitable3,4. At the molecular level, GBM harbours recurrent genetic alterations such as EGFR amplification, PTEN loss, TERT promoter mutation and combined chromosome 7 gain with chromosome 10 loss, which collectively drive uncontrolled proliferation and therapeutic resistance1,5,6. In addition, the tumour microenvironment (TME) is profoundly immunosuppressive and highly heterogeneous, further contributing to treatment failure7,8. Despite extensive efforts to delineate its molecular underpinnings, particularly at the level of transcriptional regulation, the fundamental drivers of GBM progression and recurrence remain incompletely understood, highlighting the urgent need to identify novel therapeutic targets.

Increasing evidence indicates that epigenetic dysregulation plays a pivotal role in GBM pathogenesis, with histone modifications emerging as key determinants of tumour progression and therapeutic resistance9–12. Diverse histone modifications, including acetylation, methylation and phosphorylation, dynamically regulate chromatin accessibility and transcriptional programs that govern tumour cell survival, maintenance of stem-like properties and therapeutic resistance in GBM. Beyond the effects of individual marks, recent studies have revealed extensive crosstalk among distinct histone modifications, whereby coordinated activities of histone-modifying enzymes reinforce malignant transcriptional states and cellular plasticity in GBM11,13. Consistent with this concept, epigenetic regulators such as histone acetyltransferases, deacetylases, methyltransferases and demethylases have gained increasing attention as potential therapeutic targets14–18. Although histone deacetylase (HDAC) inhibitors have demonstrated anti-tumour activity in preclinical GBM models, their limited clinical efficacy underscores the need to identify additional, functionally critical epigenetic vulnerabilities that drive GBM aggressiveness and treatment resistance19. In this context, elucidating novel epigenetic regulators that integrate oncogenic signalling with chromatin remodelling is essential for understanding GBM progression and for developing more effective therapeutic strategies.

Among histone-modifying enzymes, N-terminal acetyltransferase D (NatD, also known as N-α-acetyltransferase (NAA) 40) specifically acetylates the α-amino group of the N-terminal serine residue (Ser1) of histones H4 and H2A within the conserved SGRG motif20,21. Elevated NatD expression has been reported across multiple malignancies, where it promotes tumour growth and invasion through diverse mechanisms22–24. For instance, in lung cancer, NatD-mediated H4S1 acetylation antagonizes casein kinase (CK) 2α-dependent phosphorylation and promotes epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT) via Slug activation25; in breast cancer, NatD enhances invasion through the NatD–FOXA2–MMP14 axis26; and in colorectal cancer, NatD supports proliferation and suppresses apoptosis independently of p53 status27. NatD was also proposed as a prognostic marker in aggressive liver cancers28.

However, the molecular role of NatD in GBM remains poorly defined. In this study, we sought to elucidate the oncogenic functions of NatD in GBM and to identify its downstream effectors that mediate malignant phenotypes. Our findings demonstrate that NatD promotes tumour progression through epigenetic activation of the fibroblast growth factor receptor (FGFR) 4 signalling pathway, highlighting the NatD–FGFR4 axis as a potential therapeutic vulnerability in GBM.

Materials and methods

Analysis of The Cancer Genome Atlas datasets

NatD mRNA expression in tumour versus normal tissues was compared using The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) datasets (see Supplementary Methods for details).

Tissue microarray and immunohistochemistry

Commercial human GBM and normal brain tissue microarrays (TissueArray.Com LLC) were used to evaluate NatD and FGFR4 protein expression. Antibody information is provided in Supplementary Table 1, and the staining protocol is described in Supplementary Methods.

Cell culture

LN229, U-87 MG and U-373 MG cell lines, as well as patient-derived GBM stem cells, were used in this study (see Supplementary Methods for details). The use of patient-derived GBM tissue was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the National Cancer Center, Korea (NCC2019-0241).

Lentiviral plasmid construction and stable cell-line generation

Gene knockdown and reconstitution were performed using short hairpin RNAs (shRNAs) and lentiviral vectors (see Supplementary Methods for details).

Immunoblot analysis

Protein expression was analysed by immunoblot analysis as described in Supplementary Methods.

Functional assays

Cell proliferation, colony formation, migration and invasion assays were performed as described in Supplementary Methods.

Extreme limiting dilution assay

GBM stemness was evaluated by extreme limiting dilution assay (ELDA) as described in Supplementary Methods.

RNA sequencing and bioinformatics

RNA sequencing was performed on LN229 cells stably expressing control shRNA, shRNA targeting NatD (shNatD#4), and shNatD#4 rescued with lentivirus expressing wild-type NatD (shNatD/NatD-WT). RNA sequencing data have been deposited in the Gene Expression Omnibus under accession number GSE312750. Analytical details are provided in Supplementary Methods.

Chromatin immunoprecipitation assay

Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) assays were conducted using antibodies against histone modifications, pan-histone H3/H4 for normalization or IgG as a negative control. Precipitated DNA was quantified by quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR) with primers targeting the FGFR4 promoter (see Supplementary Methods for details). Antibody and primer information are listed in Supplementary Tables 1 and 2, respectively.

Mouse xenograft model

All animal procedures were approved by the Ewha Womans University IACUC (ESM 23-046). BALB/c nude mice (6-week-old males) were injected subcutaneously with 1 × 106 cells. Tumour volume was measured every 3 days and calculated as V = 1/2 × (width)2 × length. Mice were sacrificed on day 36, and tumours were harvested for analysis.

Orthotopic xenograft models

All animal procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Yonsei University College of Medicine (2023-2027). Orthotopic xenograft models were established by stereotactic intracranial implantation of LN229 cells into the striatum of 6-week-old male BALB/c nude mice. Tumour growth was assessed by MRI, and survival was analysed using the Kaplan–Meier method. Detailed procedures are described in the Supplementary Methods.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 9. Data are presented as mean ± SEM from at least three independent experiments unless otherwise indicated. Comparisons between two groups were made using Welch’s t test, and multiple comparisons were analysed by one-way analysis of variance with Bonferroni correction. A P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

NatD is required for proliferation and self-renewal of glioblastoma stem-like cells

Tumoursphere formation assays showed that NatD knockdown significantly reduced both the number and diameter of spheres derived from U-87 MG glioblastoma stem-like cells (GSCs) and U-373 MG GSCs (Supplementary Fig. 2). Correspondingly, proliferation of these GSCs was markedly impaired following NatD silencing (Fig. 3). ELDAs confirmed a decrease in sphere-forming frequency upon NatD knockdown (Fig. 3b). Specifically, stem-cell frequency was reduced by two independent shRNAs, decreasing from 1/13 to 1/194 (shNatD#2) and 1/278 (shNatD#4) in U-87 MG GSCs, and from 1/51 to 1/236 (shNatD#2) and 1/297 (shNatD#4) in U-373 MG GSCs, respectively (Supplementary Table 3). In patient-derived GBM stem cells (PDC124 and PDC131), NatD depletion similarly suppressed proliferation (Fig. 3c). ELDA analysis revealed a marked reduction in stem-cell frequency, with an approximately two-fold and 13-fold decrease following NatD knockdown in PDC124 and PDC131, respectively (Fig. 3d and Supplementary Table 3). Consistent with the ELDA results, NatD depletion reduced the expression of stemness-associated markers, including Nestin, OCT4 and SOX2 (Fig. 3e). The oncogenic function of NatD depends on its N-terminal acetyltransferase activity

Given that NatD silencing impaired proliferation and self-renewal, we next examined whether its enzymatic activity is required for these effects. The enzymatic deficiency of the catalytically inactive NatD mutant (NatD-E139Q29, hereafter NatD-EQ) was independently verified by an in vitro DTNB-based acetylation assay (Supplementary Fig. 3; see Supplementary Methods). Re-expression of wild-type NatD (NatD-WT) but not NatD-EQ, restored proliferation (Fig. 4a), colony formation (Fig. 4b and Supplementary Fig. 4a), migration (Fig. 4c and Supplementary Fig. 4b), and invasion (Fig. 4d and Supplementary Fig. 4c). GSC self-renewal was also restored by re-expression of NatD-WT with stem-cell frequency increased from 1/263 to 1/77 in U-87 MG GSCs and from 1/247 to 1/89 in U-373 MG GSCs (Fig. 4e and Supplementary Table 3). FGFR4 is transcriptionally regulated by NatD enzymatic activity

To identify downstream effectors mediating NatD function, RNA sequencing analysis was performed in LN229 cells. NatD knockdown resulted in 757 upregulated and 234 downregulated genes, of which 391 genes were restored by NatD reconstitution (Fig. 5a and Supplementary Tables 4, 5). Pathway enrichment of these 391 genes highlighted epithelial–mesenchymal transition (P = 8.0 × 10−14), TGFβ signalling (P = 6.4 × 10−5), MAPK cascade (P = 6.5 × 10−4) and potassium ion transport (P = 4.2 × 10−4) (Fig. 5b and Supplementary Table 6). Given that both MAPK and TGFβ signalling pathways were attenuated upon NatD knockdown, FGFR4, which encodes a receptor tyrosine kinase linked to MAPK, PI3K/AKT and TGFβ pathways implicated in GBM progression30–33, emerged as a prime candidate. Quantitative real-time RT-PCR (qRT-PCR) confirmed that NatD depletion reduced FGFR4 mRNA expression, along with several additional candidates including inhibitor of DNA binding (ID) 1, ID3 and TNF receptor superfamily (TNFRSF) 19 (Fig. 5c). Importantly, re-expression of NatD-WT, but not the catalytic mutant NatD-EQ, restored FGFR4 mRNA levels (Fig. 5d and Supplementary Fig. 5a), supporting a transcriptional mechanism dependent on NatD enzymatic activity. Consistently, immunoblotting demonstrated decreased FGFR4 protein levels and reduced phosphorylation of its downstream effectors fibroblast growth factor receptor substrate 2 (FRS2), ERK1/2 and AKT upon NatD depletion; these effects were rescued by re-expression of NatD-WT but not the catalytic mutant NatD-EQ (Fig. 5d and Supplementary Fig. 5b). This reduction in FGFR4 expression and downstream signalling was further recapitulated in patient-derived GBM stem cells (PDC124 and PDC131 cells, Fig. 5e and Supplementary Fig. 5c). Moreover, immunohistochemical analysis of clinical GBM specimens revealed a moderate positive correlation between NatD and FGFR4 expression (R2 = 0.4469, Fig. 5f), supporting the potential clinical relevance of the NatD–FGFR4 axis. NatD regulates FGFR4 transcription via histone modifications

ChIP analysis revealed that NatD depletion reduced N-terminal acetylation of histone H4 (Ac-N-H4), while concomitantly increasing phosphorylation of histones H2A and H4 at serine 1 (p-H2A/H4-S1), a modification previously attributed to CK2α activity25,34 (Fig. 6b) (the specificity of the custom anti-Ac-N-H4 antibody was validated using peptide-based assays; Supplementary Methods and Supplementary Fig. 6). These changes were accompanied by decreased enrichment of the active chromatin mark H3K4me3 (trimethylated H3K4) and increased enrichment of the repressive mark H3K27me3 (trimethylated H3K27) at the FGFR4 promoter (Fig. 6c). Notably, these chromatin alterations were reversed by re-expression of wild type NatD (NatD-WT) but not by the catalytically inactive mutant NatD-EQ. Furthermore, genetic knockdown (shCK2α) or pharmacological inhibition (CX-4945) of CK2α abolished the NatD depletion-induced increase in H2A/H4 serine 1 phosphorylation at the FGFR4 promoter and concomitantly restored FGFR4 expression at both the mRNA and protein levels (Fig. 6d–f). Discussion

This study identifies NatD as a previously unrecognized oncogenic regulator in GBM and elucidates a key mechanistic axis underlying NatD-mediated tumour progression. We demonstrate that NatD is significantly upregulated in GBM and that its enzymatic activity is indispensable for sustaining proliferation, migration, invasion and self-renewal of GSCs. The observation that NatD expression is elevated not only in GBM but also in LGG suggests that NatD activation may represent an early event during gliomagenesis. Although NatD expression is not significantly higher in GBM than in LGG, our findings indicate that NatD enzymatic activity plays a central role in sustaining malignant phenotypes in GBM. Through transcriptomic and functional analyses, we establish FGFR4 as a major downstream effector of NatD, thereby linking NatD-mediated histone modifications to activation of the MAPK and PI3K/AKT signalling pathways. Mechanistically, NatD catalyses the N-terminal acetylation of histones H4 and H2A, thus reshaping local chromatin architecture and promoting FGFR4 gene transcription. Upregulation of FGFR4, in turn, activates downstream MAPK and PI3K/AKT signalling pathways, supporting oncogenic and stem-like phenotypes in GSCs. Notably, NatD knockdown attenuated the anti-proliferative effect of FGFR4 inhibitor, BLU-554 (Supplementary Fig. 8), consistent with reduced FGFR4 expression in NatD-deficient cells. This finding further supports a functional link between NatD activity and FGFR4 signalling in GBM.

Previous studies have implicated N-terminal acetyltransferases in diverse aspects of tumour biology, including regulation of protein stability, chromatin dynamics and cell signalling20,21,35. However, the role of NatD in GBM has remained largely unexplored. Our findings advance this field by demonstrating that NatD directly modulates transcriptional programs through histone acetylation, thereby controlling FGFR4 expression in GBM. FGFR4 activation engages the MAPK and PI3K/AKT cascades, which critically regulate GBM growth, mesenchymal transition and maintenance of stem-like phenotypes2,36–40. Consistent with these reports, enforced FGFR4 expression restored ERK1/2 and AKT activation as well as GSC phenotypes suppressed by NatD depletion. In parallel, NatD depletion reduced the expression of stemness-associated transcription factors, including Nestin, OCT4 and SOX2, and impaired tumoursphere formation. Collectively, these findings show a unique epigenetic mechanism by which NatD regulates GBM stemness through the NatD–FGFR4 axis and support a central role for the NatD–FGFR4–MAPK/AKT signalling axis in maintaining GBM oncogenicity.

Transcriptomic analyses further revealed downregulation of TGFβ-related pathway signatures upon NatD depletion. Given that TGFβ signalling is a critical regulator of GSC stemness and that FGFR4 has been reported to modulate TGFβ/SMAD signalling in a context-dependent manner, particularly in fibrotic settings41–44, a potential link between the NatD–FGFR4 axis and TGFβ signalling was considered. However, canonical TGFβ target genes, including ID1 and ID3, were not restored by FGFR4 re-expression (Supplementary Fig. 9), suggesting that attenuation of TGFβ-associated transcriptional programs following NatD depletion may occur independently of FGFR4 downregulation. This raises the possibility that NatD could modulate TGFβ signalling through FGFR4-independent mechanisms. For example, NatD-mediated N-terminal acetylation of histones could directly influence chromatin accessibility at TGFβ target gene loci, altering their transcriptional responses. Alternatively, NatD may regulate other chromatin-associated factors or transcriptional co-regulators that impact TGFβ signalling.

Given the well-established role of TGFβ in immune suppression and TME remodelling in GBM, NatD-dependent modulation of TGFβ signalling may have broader implications beyond tumour cell-intrinsic phenotypes, potentially influencing immune surveillance and therapeutic responsiveness45–47. In particular, as TGFβ signalling can dampen antitumour immune responses and limit the effectiveness of immunotherapies48,49, NatD inhibition could potentially enhance the efficacy of immune checkpoint blockade or other immunotherapeutic strategies by reducing TGFβ-mediated immunosuppression within the TME. Additionally, targeting NatD may disrupt pro-tumorigenic aspects of TGFβ signalling that contribute to resistance against conventional therapies, further broadening the scope of clinical benefit. Further studies will be required to delineate the FGFR4-independent mechanisms by which NatD impacts TGFβ-related pathways in GBM and TME.

Although FGFR4 has been associated with poor prognosis in GBM32,33, our study identifies NatD as a critical upstream regulator of FGFR4, introducing a new layer of therapeutic relevance. Unlike broad-spectrum histone acetyltransferases such as CBP/p300 or GCN5, NatD displays unique substrate specificity towards histones H2A/H4 serine 1, linking this modification to direct transcriptional control of FGFR4 and its oncogenic signalling. From a clinical perspective, NatD represents a promising therapeutic target. Inhibition of its enzymatic activity may suppress GBM growth and stem-cell maintenance, particularly in tumours with elevated FGFR4 signalling. The positive correlation between NatD and FGFR4 expression in patient samples supports this translational potential. Moreover, as several FGFR4 inhibitors are already in clinical use or development50,51, our findings raise the possibility that NatD expression level may serve as a potential biomarker for stratifying GBM patients who are more likely to respond to FGFR4-targeted therapies.

In this study, we extended our in vivo analyses by employing an orthotopic xenograft model, which more closely recapitulates the brain microenvironment compared with subcutaneous models. Our results demonstrate that NatD depletion significantly suppresses intracranial tumour growth and prolongs survival, whereas FGFR4 re-expression partially restores tumour progression (Fig. 8). These findings provide strong evidence that the NatD–FGFR4 axis plays a critical role in GBM progression within a physiologically relevant brain context.

Given the molecular heterogeneity of GBM, the NatD–FGFR4 axis may exhibit subtype-specific relevance. Notably, FGFR4-driven MAPK/AKT signalling is consistent with features of the classical type, whereas the enrichment of TGFβ and EMT-related pathways suggests a potential role in the mesenchymal subtype, which is characterized by enhanced invasiveness and inflammatory signalling46,52. However, subtype-stratified analysis of TCGA GBM datasets revealed that NatD expression correlates positively with FGFR4 expression across all major molecular subtypes (Supplementary Fig. 10), suggesting that this regulatory axis is not restricted to a specific subtype but represents a broadly relevant mechanism in GBM.

Despite these advances, our study has certain limitations. Although NatD-dependent acetylation of histones H4 and H2A antagonizes CK2α-mediated serine 1 phosphorylation, the precise molecular mechanisms underlying this crosstalk remain to be fully elucidated. Notably, inhibition of CK2α alone was sufficient to restore FGFR4 expression following NatD depletion, suggesting that CK2α-dependent H4/H2A serine 1 phosphorylation represents a dominant repressive mechanism at the FGFR4 promoter that can override the loss of NatD-mediated N-terminal acetylation. Accordingly, CK2α inhibition may bypass the requirement for NatD-mediated N-terminal acetylation by relieving a dominant inhibitory constraint on FGFR4 transcription (Fig. 6d, f). In addition, the rescue experiments in this study were performed using ectopic expression systems, which may result in expression levels exceeding those of endogenous NatD or FGFR4. Although the differential effects observed between NatD-WT and the catalytically inactive NatD-EQ mutant strongly support an activity-dependent mechanism, future studies employing approaches that more closely recapitulate endogenous gene regulation, such as CRISPR/dCas9-based gene activation systems, may provide additional physiological insight into the role of NatD in GBM. Furthermore, our in vivo analyses were limited to subcutaneous and orthotopic xenograft models. Although orthotopic xenograft models capture key aspects of tumour growth in the brain, they do not fully reflect the genetic and phenotypic heterogeneity of human GBM. In this regard, patient-derived xenograft (PDX) models may provide a more clinically relevant platform for evaluating tumour behaviour and therapeutic response. Future studies using PDX systems will be important to further validate the role of the NatD–FGFR4 axis and assess its translational potential in heterogeneous GBM settings.

From a therapeutic perspective, effective targeting of the NatD–FGFR4 axis in GBM will require the development of selective NatD inhibitors with central nervous system-penetrant pharmacokinetic properties. The blood–brain barrier represents a major obstacle for the delivery of many small-molecule inhibitors, potentially limiting their efficacy in intracranial tumours53. Although potent bisubstrate inhibitors of NatD have been developed, including CoA-peptide-based compounds with high enzymatic potency and selectivity, these molecules remain primarily biochemical or probe-level inhibitors and may have limited drug-like properties for brain delivery54. More recently, cell-potent NatD bisubstrate inhibitors have been reported, suggesting progress towards cellular target engagement; however, further optimization will be required to improve blood–brain barrier permeability, adequate brain exposure, metabolic stability and overall pharmacokinetic profiles suitable for intracranial tumours55. Thus, future studies should focus on developing central nervous system-penetrant NatD inhibitors or optimized delivery strategies to translate NatD targeting into GBM therapy.

In conclusion, NatD functions as a key epigenetic driver of GBM progression by promoting FGFR4 transcription and subsequent MAPK/AKT activation, thereby sustaining malignant and stem-like phenotypes. Collectively, these results position the NatD–FGFR4 axis as a promising therapeutic target in GBM.
