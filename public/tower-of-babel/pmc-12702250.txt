Charting a healthier tomorrow for Europe's children and adolescents
Authors: Hans Henri P Kluge, Regina De Dominicis
Published: 2025

Europe's high standards of living and improvements in child and adolescent health in recent years risk masking serious challenges that threaten the health and well-being of children and adolescents across the region.

Deaths among children under the age of five in the region have more than halved since 1990—surpassing the global targets for child mortality. But in recent years, progress has stagnated, and deaths are increasingly concentrated in the first few weeks and months of life—often linked to poor quality care for babies and mothers.1

Inequities in access to quality healthcare services,2 combined with a rise in misinformation, have left millions of children at risk of preventable diseases, as vaccination rates among children continue to fall. Mental health challenges, particularly among adolescents, have taken hold3; and obesity has become a persistent problem.4

These trends are red flags signaling a level of complacency and a health care system that is not fit for every child and adolescent, putting our collective future at risk.

To address this, during the 75th Regional Committee, Member States across Europe and Central Asia took decisive action and adopted A Healthy Start for a Healthy Life: a Strategy for Child and Adolescent Health and Well-being, 2026–2030. The strategy—developed by WHO/Europe and UNICEF—has been shaped by adolescents through an extensive consultation process. Young people co-designed the priorities, co-drafted the actions and committed to annual reviews alongside policymakers.

The new strategy is a game changer: it shifts from talks to concrete action, and places mutual accountability at its heart.

It is a truly multisectoral strategy. Health ministries will be supported to lead concerted efforts across education, urban planning, social protection and beyond.

With our new joint strategy, we are calling on governments and partners to commit to young people's better health by focusing on five bold actions to accelerate change.

First, let's build financial transparency and redirect resources to essential child and adolescent health services. Focus on specialized training and recruitment and retention strategies for a quality child health workforce in those areas where they are most needed.

Second, make affordable quality care a reality for all by rolling out child-centered quality standards across prevention, primary and specialty services. Strengthen mental health support and ensure seamless handovers as young people move into adult care.

Third, protect children and young people from harmful and aggressive marketing. This includes the regulation of promotion of high-fat, salt and sugar products aimed at children and enforce digital safeguards to block unhealthy advertisements. Strengthen measures to prevent use of tobacco including novel products.

Fourth, set up cross-ministerial task forces, transform every school into a health-promoting space, invest in safe, green urban environments, and launch parenting and life-skills programmes to boost resilience and make early childhood a positive experience for children and parents alike.

And finally, digitize child health records, close gaps in mental health and NCD metrics, and publish an interactive regional dashboard so everyone can track progress—and drive real change.

Working together for the health of our children and young people

Countries in Europe and Central Asia now have a historic opportunity to lead the world with the betterment of child and adolescent health. Decisive actions by national health authorities can drive real, long-term change.

But governments are only one part of the solution. We believe that all sectors—education, social, services, environment, technology/AI and beyond—have a key role to play in nurturing environments where children can thrive.

Adolescents, together with their families and communities, also have a role to play. They can hold governments accountable, ensuring that promises translate into real and concrete outcomes.

This is more than a plan—it's a commitment. Children and adolescents will no longer be passive recipients of policy; they are partners, co-owners of their health journey. And WHO and UNICEF stand ready to support all partners and stakeholders throughout the process.

With this collective roadmap we can reverse the failures of the past and build a healthier, more equitable future for all.
