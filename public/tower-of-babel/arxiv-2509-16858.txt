Benchmarking Offline Reinforcement Learning for Emotion-Adaptive Social Robotics
Soon Jynn Chu, Raju Gottumukkala, Alan Barhorst

Abstract
The ability of social robots to respond to human emotions is crucial for building trust and acceptance in human-robot collaborative environments. However, developing such capabilities through online reinforcement learning is sometimes impractical due to the prohibitive cost of data collection and the risk of generating unsafe behaviors. In this paper, we study the use of offline reinforcement learning as a practical and efficient alternative. This technique uses pre-collected data to enable emotion-adaptive social robots. We present a system architecture that integrates multimodal sensing and recognition, decision-making, and adaptive responses. Using a limited dataset from a human-robot game-playing scenario, we establish a benchmark for comparing offline reinforcement learning algorithms that do not require an online environment. Our results show that BCQ and CQL are more robust to data sparsity, achieving higher state-action values compared to NFQ, DQN, and DDQN. This work establishes a foundation for benchmarking offline RL in emotion-adaptive robotics and informs future deployment in real-world HRI. Our findings provide empirical insight into the performance of offline reinforcement learning algorithms in data-constrained HRI. This work establishes a foundation for benchmarking offline RL in emotion-adaptive robotics and informs its future deployment in real-world HRI, such as in conversational agents, educational partners, and personal assistants, require reliable emotional responsiveness.



I
Introduction

Social robotics is a subset of human-robot interaction (HRI) that focuses on designing robots capable of engaging with humans through social behaviors, communication, and interaction patterns. They are increasingly being developed as conversational agents, educational partners, and personal assistants. For example, the PARO robot boosts happiness and reduces pain perception in older adults
[
1
]
, while the Pepper robot encourages activity in healthcare settings
[
2
]
. The ability to perceive and respond to human emotions presents opportunities for deeper engagement, but the relationship between emotional responsiveness and user trust remains complex and context-dependent. Achieving this requires both accurate emotion recognition and adaptive behavior selection. However, this relationship is complex and context-dependent, as naive mappings from detected emotions to robot actions often produce responses that feel unnatural or inappropriate.

While Wizard-of-Oz methods
[
3
]
were widely used to prototype social behaviors in earlier works, this approach is not scalable for real-world deployment. The need for a human operator to constantly control the robot highlights the challenge of achieving effective, autonomous HRI. Central to achieving truly autonomous HRI is the ability of the robot to perceive and interpret human emotional states in real-time. The ability to recognize emotions is crucial for robots to identify when humans are anxious or uncomfortable, enabling them to adjust their behavior to ensure safety and build long-term trust. Emotion recognition, which was extensively studied (through facial expression classification
[
4
]
and physiological signals
[
5
]
), is only the first step; robots must also respond appropriately to human affective states to be truly adaptive and engaging.

Reinforcement learning (RL) offers a data-driven approach to learning nuanced emotional response strategies that adapt to diverse human behaviors. Robots learn optimal emotion response policies through trial-and-error interaction with human users
[
6
]
. Online RL learns through direct interaction with humans, requiring thousands of trial-and-error episodes to converge to optimal policies. In HRI contexts, this approach becomes prohibitively expensive due to the need for human participants, lengthy interaction sessions, and potential risk of unsafe and socially inappropriate behaviors that might be harmful for participants.

Offline RL has gained attention as a practical alternative to online RL. It has been used in various applications such as controlling navigation for omni-wheeled robots
[
7

and developing effective and safer insulin dosing policies for blood glucose control
[
8
]
. Unlike online RL, which depends on extensive real-time exploration, offline RL enables policy learning entirely from pre-collected datasets. This makes it particularly well-suited for HRI, where arbitrary exploration can be unsafe, costly, or risk undermining user trust. By leveraging logged interaction data, offline RL allows robots to develop adaptive response policies while avoiding the inefficiencies and trial-and-error learning risks with human participants. Recent studies (e.g.,

In this work, we present a novel application of offline RL to overcome the limitations of online RL in training social robots, enabling emotion-adaptive behavior from limited data. We propose a framework for benchmarking offline RL algorithms under data-scarce conditions, using a game-playing scenario as a structured yet socially rich testbed that elicits diverse emotions while posing minimal risk. The framework processes multimodal user signals to generate adaptive responses. Moreover, we introduce and analyze a multimodal HRI dataset to assess whether learned policies can produce high state–action values within conservative bounds when deployed from the initial state distribution. Our contributions include a comprehensive analysis of algorithm performance on a multimodal HRI and a discussion of the lessons learned for developing robust and adaptive policies. This work establishes a baseline for a challenging new domain, demonstrating how offline RL can bridge the gap between theoretical research and practical HRI applications.

II
Related Works

Our work is informed by two key areas of research: affective computing for recognizing human emotional states and RL for generating adaptive robotic behaviors. We will first discuss the evolution of multimodal emotion detection and then review recent applications of offline RL that are most relevant to our proposed framework.

II-A

Multimodal Emotion Recognition

Recent advancements in deep learning have enhanced multimodal emotion recognition, a crucial component for recognizing human affective states. For instance,
[
13
]
proposed a hybrid fusion system that integrates facial expressions, galvanic skin response, and electroencephalogram signals, achieving up to 81.2% accuracy on their LUMED-2 dataset for three emotion classes. Other works include
[
14
]
, which introduced a multimodal deep learning framework that integrates facial, speech, and EEG modalities for emotion recognition. By combining improved GhostNet, a lightweight CNN, and a tree-like LSTM with decision-level fusion, their method achieved more robust and accurate recognition performance across benchmark datasets. These methods have shown potential by delivering strong and accurate results on benchmark datasets. However, existing literature primarily addresses the perception of human emotions, focusing on accurately identifying them. What remains unaddressed is the crucial next step: how to use this rich, multimodal information to create and deploy an autonomous agent that can generate and adapt its behavior.

II-B

Adaptive Response Generation using RL

Earlier works on emotion-adaptive responses primarily relied on rule-based approaches. For example,
[
15
]
developed an EEG-based system that recognized users’ emotional states in terms of valence, arousal, and dominance, and adapted advertisements accordingly through predefined rules based on the detected emotions. While effective in constrained settings, such rule-based systems lack flexibility and struggle to generalize to the variability and unpredictability of real-world human behavior.

RL has become a prominent approach for automated mapping between recognized states and actions, and it has been extensively researched for response generation in HRI. For example,
[
16
]
developed a chess companion robot that used visual cues and game context to model children’s states and applied multi-armed bandits to generate empathetic responses tailored to each child. Similarly,
[
17
]
designed a learning companion robot that leveraged verbal and non-verbal cues from children and trained a Q-learning model to adapt stories and interactions based on each learner’s engagement and progress. While these works demonstrate the potential of RL in adaptive HRI, they rely on online training, which is time- and resource-intensive, making algorithmic convergence investigations and cross-algorithm comparisons difficult.

Interactive RL has been proposed as a method to reduce exploration by incorporating feedback from human trainers during the learning process. For instance,
[
18
]
applied interactive RL to teachable social robots in classroom environments, demonstrating that robots trained in this way not only learned faster but also improved human learning outcomes compared to self-practice. However, despite its advantages, interactive RL relies on continuous human involvement, which limits scalability and makes it unsuitable for long-term or large-scale deployment.

II-C

Offline RL in HRI

Social robotics has yet to fully leverage offline RL methodologies, despite their clear advantages for safe, efficient, and scalable HRI training. By learning policies entirely from pre-collected datasets, offline RL eliminates the need for continuous human supervision and unsafe online exploration, making it a promising pathway for developing autonomous and scalable systems. For instance,
[
19
]
employed a Sequential Random Deep Q-Network, an offline RL algorithm, to train a robot head for generating backchanneling behaviors in a storytelling game. Similarly,
[
20
]
proposed a multimodal adaptive RL framework in a rock–paper–scissors interaction, combining Q-learning and Sarsa with ensemble classifiers to improve robustness against noisy inputs. While these works demonstrate the feasibility of offline approaches, their reliance on narrow input modalities, such as audio cues
[
19
]
or hand-motion signals
[
20
]
, limits the scope of affective understanding. Without incorporating internal affective states, robots may fail to fully capture user emotions and thus struggle to generate truly adaptive and socially meaningful responses.

II-D

Limitations and Research Gaps

One of the main gaps in existing literature is a lack of a systematic framework for developing and evaluating adaptive policies using limited, pre-collected multimodal data. Online RL is often impractical due to its time-consuming nature and safety risks. This is especially problematic for real-world HRI, where robots must be reliable without the luxury of extended, interactive training periods.

III
System Architecture

III-A

General Overview

Figure
1
illustrates our four-layer architecture, which processes multimodal human data to generate adaptive robot responses. The layers are: sensing, interpretation, decision-making, and response. The sensing layer captures both user and game states through three components: a wearable device, a head-mounted camera, and an arm-mounted camera. The wearable records biometric signals, while the cameras provide visual input of the user’s facial expressions and the game board.

Fig. 1
:
System Architecture

The interpretation layer is responsible for converting raw sensor data into meaningful information for the decision-making of the robot. This is achieved through two primary modules: the multimodal emotion recognition module and the game status analyzer. The emotion recognition module processes biometric signals from the sensing layer to detect arousal and uses head camera images to classify the facial expressions of the user. Concurrently, the game status analyzer extracts the current board state and computes the game score from images captured by the arm camera. Together, these modules provide the robot with a comprehensive understanding of both the emotional state and ongoing game context of the user.

Within the decision-making layer, two modules handle distinct functions: emotion-adaptive robot control and game control. The emotion-adaptive module receives emotion states and game scores from the interpretation layer, using these inputs to generate adaptive responses. A facial message is transmitted to the response layer for simulation on the screen of the robot, while a difficulty adjustment message is sent to the game control module. This second module adjusts game difficulty, analyzes the board state, and produces appropriate move patterns, which are then routed to the arms control module to manipulate the physical game pieces.

III-B

Interaction Cycle and Data Flow

After completing its move, the robot initiates a new interaction cycle by capturing data from the wearable device and head camera while waiting for the user’s turn. Once the user has placed their piece, the robot hovers its arm camera above the board to record the game state. The collected data then flows sequentially through the interpretation, decision-making, and response layers. Specifically, based on the processed information from the interpretation layer, the robot generates facial expressions on its display and adjusts the game difficulty as needed. The arms control module manipulates the physical game pieces based on the move provided by the game control module. Once the manipulation has completed, the cycle repeats with the user’s next turn.

IV-B

Implementation Details

The system architecture was implemented on the Baxter robot using Robot Operating System (ROS) 1. For arousal detection, we adopted a classical approach that identifies arousal from the presence of skin conductance response peaks
[
21
]
. Facial emotion recognition was performed using a mini-Xception network trained on the FER2013 dataset
[
22
,
4
]
. The model was trained using the dataset filtered into three emotion classes: angry, happy, and neutral.

For the game status analyzer, we collected and annotated a custom dataset, augmented it using RoboFlow, and trained a YOLOv8 model
[
23
]
. Piece positions were estimated by computing the relative distance between each piece and the nearest checkerboard square. The game score was calculated as the difference in the number of checker pieces between the human player and the robot.

Game control takes the checkerboard matrix generated by the analyzer and computes the best move using the minimax algorithm, where the search depth defines the difficulty level. The training details for the emotion-adaptive robot control module are described later in this section.

IV-C

Data Collection

We collected our dataset in the form of
D=\{\{s_{t},a_{t},r_{t},s_{t+1}\}_{t=0}^{T-1}\}_{i=0}^{N-1}
by logging the states and decisions of the robot, where
T
is the termination timestep, and
N
is the number of episodes. We have chosen American checkers as the game to be played, as it has a small learning curve. To ensure fairness, the robot takes an action sampled from a random uniform distribution for each episode. Moreover, we have set the game difficulty to start at level four, a balance we determined to be optimal, and it is confined within the range of one to eight to mitigate execution time. The participants choose the first player. No personally identifiable features are collected in the study. Moreover, consent to use the data for this work was obtained from each subject before and after the data collection.

The dataset was gathered from five participants, not all of whom have experience playing checkers. The average age and standard deviation are 21.2 and 2.64 years, respectively. A total of 232 steps is collected, with a mean and standard deviation of 46.4 and 11.6 steps. The experiment duration of each participant has a mean and standard deviation of 36.08 minutes and 7.04 minutes, respectively. The number of visits of each state-action pair is shown in Figure
2
, where it can range from zero to eight. The data indicate that the dataset has not been thoroughly explored, reflecting an exploration rate of only 63%.

IV-F

Evaluation Method

Unlike online RL, agents in offline RL have no access to the environment and therefore cannot interact to obtain cumulative rewards. For discrete state-action spaces, we evaluate policies by estimating the expected value from the initial state distribution, defined as
\hat{V}(s_{0})=\mathbb{E}{s_{0}\sim D}[Q(s_{0},\pi(s_{0}))]

[
30
]
. To the best of our knowledge, this provides the most appropriate evaluation method in such settings. A high
\hat{V}(s_{0})
indicates that the policy is expected to achieve a large cumulative future reward; however, excessively high values suggest Q-value overestimation, which can result in suboptimal policies. To mitigate this, we define an ideal upper bound based on the desired episode length and the maximum expected return per timestep. For an episode length of 60, with
\gamma=0.99
and maximum reward
r=1.0
, the upper bound is 45.28 (calculated using
\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\gamma^{t}r
). Policies with
\hat{V}(s_{0})
exceeding this threshold are considered to be overestimated and are therefore filtered.

Discussion of Findings

The results suggest that algorithms designed to mitigate the risks of off-policy evaluation are better suited for underexplored, small datasets. BCQ’s success is a direct result of its ability to constrain the learned policy to actions present in the dataset, effectively avoiding errors from evaluating out-of-distribution (ODD) actions. On the other hand, NFQ, DQN, and DDQN, which lack this explicit constraint, performed poorly as they cannot correct for overestimation in the absence of real-time environmental feedback. NFQ in particular diverged, likely because it uses the same network to both generate target values and estimate current Q-values. This coupling causes target values to shift whenever the network updates, creating a moving target that can destabilize the learning process. Moreover, proper tuning of the target network update frequency is also crucial, as evidenced by our results, where the first synchronization resulted in an immediate drop in loss. In contrast, subsequent synchronizations led to sharp spikes in loss. Our findings highlight the critical importance of a stable and conservative policy during offline training, particularly in social robotics applications where data collection is challenging and model safety is paramount.

VI
Conclusion, Limitations, and Recommendations for Future Work

In this paper, we explored the application of offline RL to HRI in a board game-playing scenario, where a robot adapts its behavior to multiple modalities of human emotion. We presented a system architecture that integrates multimodal sensing and recognition, decision-making, and adaptive response, detailing the data flow and interaction cycle. Using a limited dataset, we established a benchmark for comparing offline RL algorithms, demonstrating how to identify the most robust policy under realistic HRI constraints.
Our findings demonstrate the effectiveness of offline RL algorithms for emotion-adaptive robotics in data-limited scenarios, offering a practical framework for developing emotionally responsive robotic behaviors without requiring expensive or unfeasible online learning. This research establishes benchmarking standards for offline RL in emotion-adaptive systems and guides future implementation in real-world applications—including conversational agents, educational robots, and personal assistants—where consistent emotional intelligence is critical

This work opens new avenues of research. The dataset is small and homogeneous, which limits the generalizability of our findings. Future work should focus on gathering a larger and more diverse dataset to improve the reliability of the model across different age groups and backgrounds. Our current reward design is hardcoded, which can introduce bias; therefore, a key avenue for future work is exploring methods to automate reward assignments based on human preferences. Furthermore, the state-action space is underexplored, which could be addressed by incorporating human-guided actions to increase dataset coverage and quality.

Future research should explore offline-to-online deployment strategies, allowing for continuous learning and adaptation in real-world settings. Lastly, emerging methods, such as utilizing large language models to generate adaptive responses or critique strategies, offer a promising alternative for supporting long-term adaptation. Ultimately, our findings provide a practical foundation for building scalable, adaptive social robots that respond meaningfully to human affect.
