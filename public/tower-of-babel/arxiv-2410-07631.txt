Elementary action of classical groups on unimodular rows over monoid rings
Rabeya Basu, Maria A. Mathew

Abstract




Abstract: The elementary action of symplectic and orthogonal groups
on unimodular rows of length
2n
is transitive for
2n\geq\max(4,d+2)
in the symplectic case, and
2n\geq\max(6,2d+4)
in the orthogonal case,
over monoid rings
R[M]
, where
R
is a commutative noetherian ring of dimension
d
, and

M
is commutative cancellative torsion free monoid.
As a consequence, one gets the surjective stabilization bound
for the
{\rm K_{1}}
for classical groups. This is an extension of J. Gubeladze’s results for linear groups.

1
Introduction

The theory of unimodular rows became a popular topic in the study of classical algebraic
\rm K
-theory after A. Suslin produced the matrix theoretic proof of the celebrated Quillen–Suslin theorem; the main ingredient of Serre’s problem for projective modules. We see lots of results involving the theory of action of elementary group on unimodular rows in the works of Murthy–Swan–Tower–Mohan Kumar–Roitman–Vaserstien–Suslin–Kopeiko–Vavilov–Stepanov–Bhatwadekar–Rao
et al.
Later generalizations came for the traditional classical groups and for modules over Laurent polynomial rings. Since monoid rings are a natural extension of Laurent polynomial rings, D.F. Anderson
MR526663-Anderson
conjectured the freeness of finitely generated projective modules over normal monoid rings over a PID. The proof of Anderson’s conjecture by J. Gubeladze
Gubeladze2-maximalMR937805
led to the discussion of the nature of the action of elementary groups on unimodular rows over monoid rings. In two consecutive papers
Gubeladze-umodrow1-MR1161570

Gubeladze3-el2MR1206629
, published in 1989 and 1991, Gubeladze proved the transitive action of elementary group on unimodular rows over monoid rings under certain assumptions on the monoid. Finally, in
Gubeladze-unimodularMR3853049
, he gave a proof for for a much general case. In this paper, we deduce the analogue of Gubeladze’s result for the traditional classical groups;
viz.
for the symplectic and orthogonal groups.

Let us consider a ring
R
of Krull dimension
d
. We fix the notation
\mbox{$\mathbb{D}$}(R)
as

\mbox{$\mathbb{D}$}(R):=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\max\{4,d+2\},&\text{ in the symplectic case, }\\
\max\{6,2d+4\},&\text{ in the orthogonal case. }\\
\end{array}\right.

The aim of this article is to prove:

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
R
be a ring and
M
a monoid. Assume
2n\geq\mbox{$\mathbb{D}$}(R)
. Then
{\rm E}(2n,R[M])
acts transitively on
{\rm Um}(2n,R[M]),
in other words,

\frac{{\rm Um}(2n,R[M])}{{\rm E}({2n},R[M])}=\{*\}~~({\rm trivial}).

Next we obtain the above result in the relative case and establish surjective stabilization bounds for the relative Whitehead group
\widetilde{K}_{1}
(where
\widetilde{K}_{1}
is either
{\rm K_{1}Sp}
or
{\rm K_{1}O}
).

Theorem 1.2
.

Let R be a ring,
I\subseteq R
an ideal and M a monoid. Then the canonical map

\varphi_{k}:\frac{{\rm G}(k,R[M],IR[M])}{{\rm E}(k,R[M],IR[M])}\rightarrow\widetilde{K}_{1}(R[M],IR[M])

is surjective for
k\geq\mbox{$\mathbb{D}$}(R)-2
.

The reader may observe that using techniques in
Gubeladze-unimodularMR3853049
,
Maria-MKK1
,
DhorajiaSymplectic-MR2971853
,
Keshari-SymplecticMR2295082
and
Bhatwadekar-SymplecticMR1858341
these results can be extended to non torsion-free monoids and for monoid extensions of certain overrings. Further, these results open up a whole host of questions on stabilization in the setup of other classical groups and possibly even for higher
K
-groups.

Layout of the paper:

As has been the tradition with results in
K
-theory of monoid rings, owing to the connection monoid rings share with convex geometry, the paper will be a good mix of commutative algebra and convex geometry. We divide and discuss the preliminaries in two sections: The first summarizes the theory in rings that the reader needs to be familiar with and the second summarizes the theory of monoids and their corresponding rings. In Section 4, we will simplify the hypothesis by showing that it is enough to consider affine positive normal monoids. Section 5 is dedicated to proving Theorem
1.1
and as its application Section 6 establishes surjective bounds for
\widetilde{K}_{1}
.

3
Monoids

Now we shift our focus to the theory of monoids. Let
M
be a commutative multiplicative monoid with identity 1.
Let
\mathbb{R}_{+}
be the set of non-negative real numbers, and
\mathbb{Z}_{+}
of non-negative integers. By
\intt(M)
we denote the elements of
M
in the interior of the cone generated by
M,

i.e.
,

\intt(M)=M\cap\intt(\mathbb{R}_{+}{M}).

Let us denote by
M_{*}=\intt(M)\cup\{1\}
. Let
{\rm gp}(M)
denote the group completion of
M
and
{\rm U}(M)
the group of units of
M
. The monoid
M
is called positive , if
{\rm U}(M)
is trivial. Further,
M
is defined to be cancellative if for
x,y,z\in M,

xz=yz
implies
x=y
and torsion free, if
x,y\in M,

n\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}
and
x^{n}=y^{n},
then
x=y
.

One can restate the characteristic property of cancellation in terms of a property held by the corresponding inclusion map
i:M\rightarrow{\rm gp}(M)
. Note that
M
is cancellative if and only if the map
i
is injective. In this case, the torsion-freeness of
M
is equivalent to the torsion-freeness of
{\rm gp}(M)
. The rank of
M,
denoted by rank
(M)
, is the dimension of the
\mathbb{Q}
-vector space
{\rm gp}(M)\otimes\mathbb{Q}
. We say,
M
is affine if it is a isomorphic to a submonoid of
{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
for some
d\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{+},
and is finitely generated over
\mathbb{Z}_{+}
,
i.e.
, there exists
m_{1},\ldots,m_{k}\in M
such that
M=\mathbb{Z}_{+}\langle m_{1},\ldots,m_{k}\rangle
. For the sake of convenience, when dealing with affine monoids we often favour additive operation on the monoid. For an affine monoid
M,
the entity
\mathbb{R}_{+}(M)
, called the cone of
M
, is given by:

C:=\mathbb{R}_{+}(M)=\{a_{1}m_{1}+\cdots+a_{k}m_{k}\mid k\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{+},a_{i}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}\text{ and }m_{i}\in M\}.

The assumption on our monoids to be commutative, cancellative and torsion-free is significant since it allows to conveniently embed the monoid ring
R[M]
in the Laurent polynomial ring
R[X_{1}^{\pm{1}},\ldots,X_{r}^{\pm{1}}],
where
r
is the rank of
M
. In the interest of clarity, one may note that in such a setup
{\rm gp}(M)
will be torsion-free and thus isomorphic to
{\mathbb{Z}}^{r}
. With this understanding we proceed to our next set of definitions.

Let
M\subseteq N
be an extension of monoids. Then
N
is said to be integral over
M
, if for
x\in N,
there exists
c\in\mathbb{N}
such that
x^{c}\in M
. Much like in the theory of rings, we may define integral closure of
M
in
N
and say
M
is normal if it is integrally closed in
{\rm gp}(M)
. The monoid
M
is said to be seminormal, if for
x\in{\rm gp}(M)
, whenever
x^{2},x^{3}\in M,
then
x\in M
. It follows right away that a normal monoid is seminormal.
The monoid
M
is defined as

\phi
-simplicial, if there exists an embedding
i_{M}
of
M
in
{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}^{r}
such that
{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}^{r}
is integral over
i_{M}(M)
(
cf.
Gubeladze1-bookMR2508056
).
We are now in a position to discuss important (convex) geometrical aspects of monoids relevant to this article.

(

The cone

is a monic with respect to the associated pyramidal degree.

Since

4.3

are embeddings and

. We may assume

by Corollary

. For
k\gg 0,
define a simplex

generated as a convex combination of

\phi(t_{1}^{k}t_{i})

Observe that

and hence by Proposition
2.3
,

v\underset{{\rm E}(2n,R[{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}^{r}(\Delta)])}{\sim}e_{1}.

Choose

. For any

one may define

where

As

and

we have

. Therefore for large values of

\beta=\sigma^{-1}\tau(\sigma)\in{\rm E}(2n,A_{2})
.

By Proposition
2.3
and (

, Lemma 3.6) there exists
\sigma^{\prime}\in{\rm E}(2n,{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}^{r}(\Delta))
and

such that

\beta=\sigma^{\prime}\begin{pmatrix}\beta^{\prime}&0&0\\
0&1&0\\
0&0&1\end{pmatrix}

Define

\displaystyle w=(w_{1},\ldots,w_{2n-1},\tau{(v_{2n})}):=\tau(v)

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\beta^{\prime-1}&0&0\\
0&1&0\\
0&0&1\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=v\beta

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\beta^{\prime-1}&0&0\\
0&1&0\\
0&0&1\end{pmatrix}=v\sigma^{\prime}\underset{{\rm E}(2n,A_{2})}{\sim}v.

Since
\pi_{2}(t_{1})=m,
we choose
c\gg 0,
so that
\pi_{2}(\tau(v_{2n}))
is a monic in

and thus we may conclude the proof by observing

∎

Given a monoid

with complexity

henceforth we appoint as

the

\phi(M)=\conv(m_{1},\ldots,m_{\rank(M)-k(M)},Q).

For an polytope

define:

\widetilde{Q^{\prime}}:=\conv(m_{1},\ldots,m_{\rank(M)-k(M)},Q^{\prime})

Lemma 5.6
.

Let
(R,\mu)
be a local ring and Theorem
1.1
be true for

for

with

. For

is a polytope with the decomposition

. Then for

there exists

such that
u\underset{{\rm E}(2n,R[M(\widetilde{P})_{*}])}{\sim}v

Proof.

Let
z\in\intt(\widetilde{\gamma})
and

denote the homothetic transformation about

By Proposition 8.4 of

u_{\mathfrak{m}}\underset{{\rm E}(2n,R[M(P^{\prime})]_{\mathfrak{m}})}{\sim}e_{1}
for

(though implicit here, this is the step where the complexity restriction of the hypothesis is used). Let

Gubeladze-unimodularMR3853049
, we have the Karoubi square

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces R[M(\gamma)_{*}]}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces R[M(P)_{*}]}

\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces i_{1}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\pi_{i}}

From Corollary
4.3
owing to the inclusion

there exists

.
∎

Now our main result follows seamlessly using induction on

.

Proof of Theorem
1.1
:

As observed in Proposition

, the burden of transitive action shifts to the interior monoid algebra

Let

.

6
Surjective Stabilization of
\rm K_{1}
-group

The surjective stabilization of the
\rm K_{1}
functor of classical groups is a natural consequence of the transitive action of their elementary subgroups on unimodular rows. In this section, using a trick for the excision ring, we deduce a relative version of the main result. Then, as a consequence, we find the surjective stabilization bound for the respective
\rm K_{1}
-groups.

Definition 6.1
.

Given an ideal
I\subseteq R,
one defines the excision ring
R\oplus I
with the following operations

\displaystyle(r_{1},i_{1})+(r_{2},i_{2})

\displaystyle=(r_{1}+r_{2},i_{1}+i_{2}),

\displaystyle(r_{1},i_{1})\cdot(r_{2},i_{2})

\displaystyle=(r_{1}r_{2},r_{1}i_{2}+r_{2}i_{1}+i_{1}i_{2}),

where
r_{j}\in R
and
i_{j}\in I
for all
i
.

There exists a natural homomorphism
\phi:R\oplus I\rightarrow R,
given by
\phi(r,i)=r+i
. Also
\dim(R\oplus I)=\dim(R)
and,
R\oplus I
is noetherian, if
R
is noetherian.
We state some results that are required.

Lemma 6.2
.

(
Basu-Pillar
, Lemma 2.13, 2.14) Let R be a commutative ring and
I\subset R
an ideal. If
\alpha\in{\rm E}(k,R,I),
then there exists
\widetilde{\alpha}\in{\rm E}(k,R\oplus I)
such that
\phi(\widetilde{\alpha})=\alpha
. Further, the converse holds too.

Lemma 6.3
.

Let
I^{\prime}\subseteq R
be an ideal and
I=I^{\prime}R[N],
where
N
is a monoid. Then

R[N]\oplus I\simeq(R\oplus I^{\prime})[N].

Proof.

Define
\phi:R[N]\oplus I^{\prime}R[N]\rightarrow(R\oplus I^{\prime})[N]
as
\phi(\sum r_{j}n_{j},\sum i^{\prime}_{j}n_{j})=\sum(r_{j},i^{\prime}_{j})n_{j}
. (We may choose the same index as we are in a finite setup!)
∎

Theorem 6.4
.

Let
R
be a ring,
I\subseteq R
an ideal and
M
a monoid. Then the following orbit space is trivial for
2n\geq\mbox{$\mathbb{D}$}(R):

\frac{{\rm Um}(2n,R[M],I)}{{\rm E}({2n},R[M],I)}=\{*\}.

Proof.

Let
A=R[M]
and
v=(v_{1},\ldots,v_{2n})\in{\rm Um}(2n,R[M],I)
. Define
v^{\prime}=(v_{1}+0,\ldots,v_{n}+0)\in{\rm Um}(2n,R[M]\oplus I)
. By Lemma
6.3
,
A\oplus I=(R\oplus I)[M],
where
R\oplus I
is a
d
-dimensional noetherian ring.

Let
\phi
be the natural homomorphism
\phi:A\oplus I\rightarrow A
. On application of Theorem
1.1
, we may choose
\sigma^{\prime}\in{\rm E}(2n,A\oplus I)
such that
\sigma^{\prime}v^{\prime}=(1+0,0+0,\ldots,0+0)\in{\rm Um}(2n,A\oplus I)
. Then
\phi(\sigma^{\prime})=\sigma\in{\rm E}(2n,A,I),
by the converse of Lemma
6.2
. Then
e_{1}=\phi(\sigma^{\prime}v^{\prime})=\phi(\sigma^{\prime})(\phi(v^{\prime}))=\sigma(v)
.
∎

As a consequence of this, we have Theorem
1.2
:

Theorem 6.5
.

Let R be a ring,
I\subseteq R
an ideal and M a monoid. Then the canonical map

\varphi_{k}:\frac{{\rm G}(k,R[M],IR[M])}{{\rm E}(k,R[M],IR[M])}\rightarrow\widetilde{K}_{1}(R[M],IR[M])

is surjective for
k\geq\mbox{$\mathbb{D}$}(R)-2
.

Proof.

For
G\in{\rm G}(r,R[M]),
consider the following composition of maps

\displaystyle{\rm G}(r,R[M],IR[M])

\displaystyle\rightarrow\frac{{\rm G}(r,R[M],IR[M])}{{\rm E}(r,R[M],IR[M])}\rightarrow\widetilde{K}_{1}(R[M],IR[M])

\displaystyle G

\displaystyle\longmapsto\bar{G}\longmapsto[G]

Let
k=\mathbb{D}(R)-2
and
[S]\in\widetilde{K}_{1}{(R[M])}
. We require
T\in{\rm G}(k,R[M],IR[M])
such that
[S]=[T]
in
\widetilde{K}_{1}(R[M],IR[M])
. Without loss of generality we may assume
S\in{\rm G}(k+2,R[M],IR[M])
. One may note that the last column of
S
belongs to
{\rm Um}(k+2,R[M],IR[M])
. By Theorem
6.4
, there exists
\theta\in{\rm E}(k+2,R[M],IR[M])
such that

S\theta e_{k+2}=e_{k+2}

By (
Basu-Rao-MR2578583
, Lemma 3.6) there exists
\sigma^{\prime}\in{\rm E}(k+2,R[M])
and
T\in{\rm G}(k,R[M])
such that

S\theta=\sigma^{\prime}\begin{pmatrix}T&0&0\\
0&1&0\\
0&0&1\end{pmatrix}

Since
S\theta
belongs to
{\rm G}(k,R[M],IR[M]),
this in turn compels the decomposed components to also belong to the relative groups, i.e,
\sigma^{\prime}\in{\rm E}(k+2,R[M],IR[M])
and
T\in{\rm G}(k,R[M],IR[M])
.
The proof can be concluded by observing
[T]=[S]
in
\widetilde{K}_{1}(R[M])
.
∎
