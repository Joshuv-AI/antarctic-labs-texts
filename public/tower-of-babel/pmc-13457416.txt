Is a Video Worth a Thousand EEGs?
Authors: Danielle A Becker
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract Diagnostic value of stand-alone videos after inconclusive inpatient EEG–video monitoring Azevedo MO, Amin U, Rivera-Cruz A, Zemina K, Armanious M, Omura A, Tenney N, Huang Y, Benbadis SR. Epilepsia. 2026;00:1–6.Inpatient electroencephalographic (EEG) video monitoring occasionally fails to capture the events in question, leaving patients without a definitive diagnosis. Management after an inconclusive admission is generally limited to repeating monitoring or empirical adjustment of antiseizure medications. Although stand-alone videos are increasingly used in clinical practice, their diagnostic value in this setting has not been well established. We aimed to assess the diagnostic contribution, submission rate, and interrater reliability of stand-alone videos obtained after inconclusive inpatient EEG video monitoring. Over a 26-month period, we prospectively identified patients with inconclusive epilepsy monitoring unit (EMU) admissions who were instructed to submit videos of typical events not captured during their stay. Submitted videos were independently reviewed by two epileptologists, with adjudication by a third reviewer when consensus was not achieved. Among 619 EMU admissions, 165 (27%) were inconclusive. Twenty-seven (16%) patients submitted a stand- alone video. A definitive conclusion was reached in 23 (85%) patients, and in 20 (74%) patients, videos either established, confirmed, or revealed an additional diagnosis. Substantial interrater reliability was observed (κ = .690, 95% confidence interval = .456–.893), with perfect agreement for nonepileptic and physiologic events. This study provides preliminary evidence that stand- alone videos have the potential to be a valuable adjunct in the evaluation of seizures and seizure like events after inconclusive EMU admissions. Early and systematic reinforcement of video collection may improve diagnostic yield and reduce health care utilization when inpatient studies are unrevealing.

Commentary

Diagnostic evaluations of seizure-like activity can often be challenging. While inpatient electroencephalography (EEG) is the gold standard, these inpatient admissions may not represent a patient's real-life circumstances or everyday stressors, and typical events may go uncaptured. One study found that 1 in 4 inpatient video-EEG monitoring admissions failed to capture events. 1 Once a patient has an inconclusive inpatient admission, there are a few other diagnostic options we often try, including an ambulatory at home video-EEG study or repeating the inpatient study. The ambulatory at home EEG can often be ordered upon inpatient discharge to try to capture someone's events in their natural environment but that can still feel unnatural and remain inconclusive. If studies continue to remain inconclusive, some providers may ask a patient or caregiver to provide a video of the typical event(s), but this will not be accompanied by EEG evaluation. While this technique has proven useful anecdotally in the clinical setting and supported in the literature, its diagnostic benefit continues to be undervalued.2,3

The article highlighted in this commentary explored the diagnostic value, submission rate, and interrater reliability of stand-alone videos obtained after inconclusive inpatient video-EEG monitoring. 4 Patients who had an inconclusive epilepsy monitoring unit (EMU) evaluation between September 2023 and November 2025 were evaluated. As part of routine care, patients were asked to provide a video of any typical event not captured during their admission. Submitted videos were de-identified and independently reviewed by two fellowship-trained epileptologists who were not involved in the patient's care and were blinded to the patient's history. If consensus was not achieved between the two reviewers, a third reviewer was used to determine the final classification. The following categories/diagnoses were assigned to each patient event after full video review: epileptic seizure, psychogenic nonepileptic seizure (PNES), nonepileptic physiologic, epileptic, and PNES, cannot be determined. Six hundred and nineteen patients were admitted to the EMU during the study period. Video-EEG monitoring was inconclusive in 165 patients (27%), and completely inconclusive, where no interictal epileptiform discharges AND no events were captured, in 83 patients (13.5%). Following an inconclusive admission, 27 patients (16% of inconclusive admissions) submitted at least one video. On average, patients submitted 1–2 videos, with a median time of 25 days (range = 1-634) from EMU discharge. Stand-alone videos yielded a definitive conclusion in 23 patients (85%), defined as a finding that prompted change in patient management. Of the 27 videos reviewed, 8 (30%) established a new diagnosis; 10 (37%) confirmed a suspected diagnosis; 3 (11%) clarified an event as physiologic; 2 (7%) identified a concomitant diagnosis; and 4 (15%) were inconclusive. Cohen kappa demonstrated substantial interrater agreement (κ = .690, 95% CI = .456–.893). When examining agreement by event type and based on the final diagnoses, events classified as nonepileptic psychogenic or nonepileptic physiologic showed perfect interrater agreement, whereas interpretations resulting in epileptic seizures or “cannot be determined” accounted for all initial disagreements. This study demonstrated that stand-alone videos of typical events led to a diagnosis and change in management in 85% of the cases studies and provided valuable assessment in the evaluation of seizures and seizure-like events.

Major study limitations included small sample size (only 27/165 patients with inconclusive studies submitted recordings) and possible difficulties obtaining videos of typical events. One barrier to capturing acute events was the difficulty for witnesses to record an event, which would also be difficult if a patient lived alone. The technical quality of the videos was also variable. While interrater reliability in video analysis also presented challenges, most discrepancies arose from a small subset of videos with subtle or brief clinical signs. In contrast, clear PNES and clear epileptic seizures (ie, bilateral tonic–clonic seizures) demonstrated strong interrater agreement. The gold standard for this study was expert review of observable semiology, which is compelling. Although this technique is not as comprehensive as a gold standard EEG-captured event diagnosis, this approach can be helpful and necessary in a case of failed prior investigation and/or in a population with infrequent events.

This publication highlights and reinforces previous literature illustrating the incidence and problematic nature of inconclusive inpatient video-EEG EMU admissions. It further emphasizes the need for a complementary diagnostic approach when video-EEG monitoring remains inconclusive, especially given rising health care utilization and costs. In the study reviewed, 74% of patients who submitted a stand-alone video had already undergone one or more additional inconclusive EEG studies. The utilization and clinical value of stand-alone videos have previously been underestimated, as there is skepticism about the value of videos compared to conventional diagnostic tools with accompanying EEG. However, these investigative tools are not mutually exclusive. This article emphasizes and supports the adjunctive clinical value of stand-alone videos and the incorporation of review into our standard assessment, as the stand-alone videos offer an inexpensive tool and possible accessible alternative. Furthermore, when stand-alone video is the only data available, it offers substantially more than no data.

With the lack of formalized guidelines for repeated inconclusive video-EEG studies, there is a need for practice guidelines for assessment and management as part of routine care when studies are inconclusive. Videos can be reviewed at any time during evaluation, but a more formalized approach may help incorporate these inexpensive tools sooner and more consistently. For example, a process or system that supports multiple experts reviewing stand-alone videos, and/or compensates for this assessment with a specified CPT code, 5 may increase diagnostic utilization, accuracy and confidence. This publication identified a clear unmet need and reinforces the importance of improving access and providing alternative inexpensive assessment for patients with inclusive EMU studies. The use of these stand-alone videos would ideally increase the yield of diagnosis and ultimately provide more answers, education, prescription for possible mental health resources, and improve overall quality of life for our patients with epileptic and nonepileptic events. These guidelines could also help shorten diagnostic delay, reduce unnecessary repeat monitoring, and aid in consideration of empiric medication modifications, thereby decreasing both patient burden and health care costs. Lastly, as digital tools become increasingly incorporated into clinical care, ongoing efforts are needed to further emphasize the role of accessible, affordable, realistic time-sensitive options in epilepsy clinical evaluation.
