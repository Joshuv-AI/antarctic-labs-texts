Memory-Dominated Quantum Criticality as a Universal Route to High-Temperature Superconductivity
Byung Gyu Chae

Abstract
Understanding the dynamical origin of high–temperature superconductivity remains a central challenge in strongly correlated quantum matter. Conventional treatments of quantum criticality assume overdamped Markovian dissipation governed by Ohmic Landau damping of a small number of collective modes. Here we show that infrared dynamics is instead controlled more generally by the relaxation-rate spectrum of the underlying dissipative many-body evolution. Introducing the time–scale density of states (TDOS) of collective decay modes, we derive within the Martin–Siggia–Rose–Janssen–De Dominicis framework an exact spectral representation of the collective susceptibility in terms of this relaxation spectrum. We demonstrate that the low– λ \lambda scaling of the relaxation–rate spectrum directly classifies dynamical universality classes. In particular, a finite TDOS at vanishing relaxation rate defines a memory–dominated critical regime characterized by long–time kernels K ⁡ ( t ) ∼ 1 / t K(t)\sim 1/t and nonanalytic dynamical response. In this regime, intrinsic electronic pairing tendencies are dynamically amplified: the marginal logarithmic growth of BCS and Eliashberg theories is replaced by algebraic enhancement governed by the infrared spectral weight of slow modes. As a consequence, the superconducting transition temperature scales proportionally to the low–energy TDOS weight, naturally generating superconducting domes and Uemura scaling without invoking material-specific bosonic glue or fine tuning. The same relaxation spectrum controls anomalous normal-state dynamics, including long–time correlations and strange-metal behavior, providing a unified spectral framework for both thermodynamic and dynamical phenomena in correlated superconductors. These results identify dynamical spectral organization of relaxation modes as a fundamental organizing principle of quantum critical matter and establish memory–dominated criticality as a generic mechanism for enhanced superconductivity.

II
II. Emergence of a Slow-Mode Reservoir from Correlated Dynamical Spectra

Strongly correlated electronic matter is intrinsically a quantum many-body system.
At long times and coarse-grained scales,
its dynamics is governed not by isolated quasiparticles,
but by collective relaxation channels generated by the
full quantum dynamical evolution of the density matrix
12
;
13
;
14
,

\frac{d\hat{\varrho}}{dt}=\mathcal{L}\hat{\varrho},

(1)

where
\mathcal{L}
denotes the Liouvillian generator of the effective
(open or coarse-grained) dynamics.

The eigenmodes of
\mathcal{L}
,

\mathcal{L}R_{n}=\lambda_{n}R_{n},

(2)

define the fundamental decay channels of the interacting system.
For stable dynamics,
\mathrm{Re}\,\lambda_{n}\leq 0
,
and we define the collective relaxation rates as

\lambda\equiv-\mathrm{Re}\,\lambda_{n}\geq 0.

(3)

Infrared dynamics is therefore organized by the distribution
of these decay scales rather than by Hamiltonian excitation energies.

Observable collective responses—charge, spin, pairing,
current, and phase-coherence fluctuations—arise from projections of the full relaxation spectrum
onto experimentally accessible operators.
The resulting low-energy theory is thus controlled
by the statistical structure of relaxation rates.
We define the TDOS as the infrared
distribution of Liouvillian relaxation eigenvalues,

\rho(\lambda)=\sum_{n}\delta\!\left(\lambda+\mathrm{Re}\,\lambda_{n}\right),

(4)

or its continuum limit.

The spectral structure introduced here should therefore be understood
at the level of the full dynamical generator rather than solely in terms
of Hamiltonian energy eigenmodes.
Energy spectra characterize the conservative limit in which all
Liouvillian eigenvalues lie on the imaginary axis,
whereas generic interacting or coarse-grained systems develop
complex spectra with nonzero decay rates.
A detailed discussion of this relaxation-rate (Liouvillian)
formulation and its connection to memory kernels
is provided in Appendix A.

II.1
A. Dynamical stability spectrum and time-scale density of states

The infrared structure of correlated matter is encoded in the
spectrum of collective relaxation rates.
Rather than softening a single order parameter,
correlated critical regimes reorganize the entire decay spectrum,
producing a dense accumulation of near-marginal modes.

We characterize this structure by introducing the TDOS,
which measures the density of collective decay channels
at relaxation rate
\lambda
.
This object plays a role analogous to the energy density of states,
but in relaxation-rate space.
It determines how many collective modes decay on a given time scale.

In weakly interacting systems,
the number of slow modes remains finite,
and the TDOS vanishes as
\lambda\to 0
,
reflecting the presence of well-defined quasiparticles
and isolated collective excitations.

By contrast, in strongly correlated critical regimes,
the relaxation spectrum generically develops
an extensive accumulation of near-marginal decay channels,

\rho(\lambda\to 0)=\rho_{0}\neq 0,

(5)

forming a slow-mode reservoir that dominates
infrared collective dynamics.

This accumulation signifies a dynamical approach to marginal stability
along a macroscopic manifold of collective directions,
rather than along a single order-parameter coordinate.

II.2
B. Functional representation of the relaxation spectrum

To compute collective response functions,
we employ a functional representation of the dissipative dynamics.
The dissipative quantum dynamics of interacting electrons may be
formulated in the real–time Keldysh functional integral framework
23
;
24
.
Upon projection onto slow collective operators and in the regime
where decoherence suppresses quantum interference between forward
and backward contours, the theory reduces to a classical MSRJD
functional integral describing the infrared stochastic dynamics.

Let
\phi(\mathbf{r},t)
denote a coarse–grained collective bosonic field
representing charge, spin, pairing, or phase fluctuations.
Projecting the full quantum dynamics onto this operator sector
yields an exact decomposition in terms of relaxation eigenmodes.

Because the dissipative spectrum consists of decay rates
\lambda\geq 0
,
the projected dynamics may be represented as a continuum of relaxational modes
labeled by their decay scales,

\displaystyle\dot{\phi}

\displaystyle=-(r+c\nabla^{2})\phi+\int_{0}^{\Lambda}d\lambda\,g(\lambda)X_{\lambda}+\eta,

(6)

\displaystyle\dot{X}_{\lambda}

\displaystyle=-\lambda X_{\lambda}+g(\lambda)\phi+\zeta_{\lambda},

(7)

where
X_{\lambda}
represents a decay mode with relaxation rate
\lambda
,
and
g(\lambda)
encodes its overlap with
\phi
.

Introducing response fields
\tilde{\phi}
and
\tilde{X}_{\lambda}
,
the MSRJD action reads

\displaystyle S=

\displaystyle\int dt\,d^{d}r\,\tilde{\phi}\Big[\dot{\phi}+(r+c\nabla^{2})\phi-\int_{0}^{\Lambda}d\lambda\,g(\lambda)X_{\lambda}\Big]-D_{\phi}\tilde{\phi}^{2}

\displaystyle+\int dt\,d^{d}r\int_{0}^{\Lambda}d\lambda\,\tilde{X}_{\lambda}\big[\dot{X}_{\lambda}+\lambda X_{\lambda}-g(\lambda)\phi\big]

\displaystyle-\int dt\,d^{d}r\int_{0}^{\Lambda}d\lambda\,D_{\lambda}\tilde{X}_{\lambda}^{2}.

(8)

Because the reservoir fields
X_{\lambda}
enter quadratically,
they can be integrated out exactly.
Performing the Gaussian functional integration generates
a nonlocal-in-time memory kernel in the effective equation of motion,

\partial_{t}\phi(t)=-(r+c\nabla^{2})\phi(t)-\int_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime}\,K(t-t^{\prime})\,\phi(t^{\prime})+\eta_{\rm eff}(t),

(9)

where the memory kernel is given exactly by

K(t)=\int_{0}^{\Lambda}d\lambda\,\rho(\lambda)\,e^{-\lambda t},

(10)

with the time-scale density of states

\rho(\lambda)\equiv g(\lambda)^{2}.

(11)

Equation (
10
) makes explicit that the slow-mode reservoir
is entirely controlled by the distribution of relaxation rates.
The long-time structure of
K(t)
therefore encodes the infrared
organization of decay modes in the interacting system.

Fourier transforming the kernel yields the retarded self-energy,

\Sigma^{R}(\omega)=\int_{0}^{\Lambda}d\lambda\frac{\rho(\lambda)}{\lambda-i\omega},

(12)

so that the effective action becomes

S_{\rm eff}=\int_{\omega,\mathbf{q}}\tilde{\phi}(-\omega,-\mathbf{q})\Big[-i\omega+r+cq^{2}+\Sigma^{R}(\omega)\Big]\phi(\omega,\mathbf{q})+\cdots.

(13)

Separating real and imaginary parts,

\displaystyle\mathrm{Re}\,\Sigma^{R}(\omega)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\Lambda}d\lambda\,\rho(\lambda)\frac{\lambda}{\lambda^{2}+\omega^{2}},

(14)

\displaystyle\mathrm{Im}\,\Sigma^{R}(\omega)

\displaystyle=\omega\int_{0}^{\Lambda}d\lambda\,\rho(\lambda)\frac{1}{\lambda^{2}+\omega^{2}}.

(15)

The collective inverse susceptibility therefore assumes the exact
spectral representation

\displaystyle\chi_{R}^{-1}(\omega,\mathbf{q})

\displaystyle=r+cq^{2}+\int_{0}^{\Lambda}d\lambda\,\rho(\lambda)\frac{\lambda}{\lambda^{2}+\omega^{2}}

(16)

\displaystyle+i\omega\int_{0}^{\Lambda}d\lambda\,\rho(\lambda)\frac{1}{\lambda^{2}+\omega^{2}}.

Equation (
16
) provides an exact infrared response
fully controlled by the relaxation-rate density.
The slow-mode reservoir thus appears as a memory-dominated fixed point,
whose long-time kernel structure governs the dynamical amplification
mechanism discussed below.

The explicit Gaussian integration and derivation of the memory kernel
are presented in Appendix B.

II.3
C. Infrared universality classes

The infrared structure of collective dynamics is entirely determined by the
low–
\lambda
scaling of the relaxation spectrum encoded in the
time–scale density of states.
For a generic infrared form

\rho(\lambda)\sim\lambda^{\alpha},\qquad\lambda\to 0,

(17)

the dissipative part of the collective kernel follows directly from the
spectral representation in Eq. (
16
),

\mathrm{Im}\,\chi_{R}^{-1}(\omega)=\omega\int_{0}^{\Lambda}d\lambda\,\frac{\rho(\lambda)}{\lambda^{2}+\omega^{2}}\;\sim\;\mathrm{sgn}(\omega)\,|\omega|^{\alpha}.

(18)

Here
\omega
denotes the collective relaxation frequency
arising from the projected Liouvillian dynamics,
rather than a microscopic quasiparticle excitation energy.

It is therefore natural to write the long–wavelength dynamical response in
the scaling form

\chi^{-1}(\omega,\mathbf{q})=r+cq^{2}+\gamma|\omega|^{s},

(19)

in direct analogy with the conventional Hertz–Millis representation
3
;
4
.
The TDOS scaling immediately implies the universal identification
s=\alpha
.

Under the renormalization–group rescaling

\mathbf{q}\to b\,\mathbf{q}
and

\omega\to b^{z}\omega
,
the spatial gradient term scales as
q^{2}\to b^{2}q^{2}
,
while the dissipative term scales as

|\omega|^{s}\to b^{zs}|\omega|^{s}
.
Requiring both contributions to remain of comparable order at criticality
(
r=0
) fixes the dynamical exponent to

z=\frac{2}{s}.

(20)

In this framework the dissipation is generically non–Ohmic,

|\omega|^{s}
with
s\neq 1
,
reflecting the organization of the underlying relaxation spectrum.
Sub–Ohmic memory (
s<1
) implies
z>2
,
corresponding to critical slowing down stronger than ordinary diffusive
dynamics, whereas super–Ohmic behavior (
s>1
) yields
z<2
.
Infrared universality classes are thus classified directly by the
low–energy structure of the relaxation-rate density.

In particular, when the TDOS remains finite at the origin,

\rho(\lambda)\xrightarrow{\lambda\to 0}\rho_{0},

(21)

the dissipative kernel simplifies to

\displaystyle\mathrm{Im}\,\chi_{R}^{-1}(\omega)

\displaystyle=\omega\int_{0}^{\Lambda}d\lambda\,\frac{\rho_{0}}{\lambda^{2}+\omega^{2}}

\displaystyle\xrightarrow{|\omega|\ll\Lambda}\frac{\pi}{2}\rho_{0}\,\mathrm{sgn}(\omega),

(22)

while the reactive part acquires a logarithmic infrared singularity,

\mathrm{Re}\,\chi_{R}^{-1}(\omega)=\int_{0}^{\Lambda}d\lambda\,\rho_{0}\frac{\lambda}{\lambda^{2}+\omega^{2}}\simeq\rho_{0}\ln\frac{\Lambda}{|\omega|}.

(23)

Collective infrared dynamics is therefore governed by the marginal
non-Markovian kernel

\chi_{R}^{-1}(\omega,\mathbf{q})\simeq r+cq^{2}+\rho_{0}\ln\frac{\Lambda}{|\omega|}+i\frac{\pi}{2}\rho_{0}\,\mathrm{sgn}(\omega)+\cdots,

(24)

corresponding in the time domain to a universal long-memory response

K(t)\sim 1/t
, derived explicitly in Appendix C.

This flat-TDOS fixed point represents a breakdown of the conventional
overdamped critical dynamics underlying Hertz–Millis theory.
Rather than arising from a fast electronic bath coupled to a single soft
order-parameter mode, collective relaxation is controlled by the
self-organized accumulation of an extensive continuum of
slow decay modes.

III
III. Memory–Enhanced Pairing Beyond Eliashberg Theory

The emergence of a slow–mode reservoir reorganizes the infrared
structure of the Cooper pairing channel.
In conventional quantum critical superconductivity,
collective fluctuations act as overdamped bosonic mediators
whose Ohmic dynamics renders the pairing interaction marginal.
Here we show that when collective dynamics is instead governed
by a flat relaxation–rate density of states,
long–time memory effects transform
the Cooper channel into a relevant infrared instability.

III.1
A. Marginal pairing from Ohmic collective dynamics

Near metallic quantum critical points,
fermions couple to overdamped collective modes with retarded propagator

D_{R}^{-1}(\omega,\mathbf{q})=r+cq^{2}+\gamma|\omega|.

(25)

The linear frequency dependence reflects Landau damping
by the electronic continuum and produces a pairing kernel
scaling as

D(\omega)\sim\frac{1}{|\omega|}.

(26)

The infrared shell correction to the dimensionless Cooper–channel coupling

g
therefore reads

dg\propto g^{2}\int_{\Lambda/b}^{\Lambda}\frac{d\omega^{\prime}}{\omega^{\prime}}=g^{2}\ln b.

(27)

Introducing the RG scale
l=\ln b
yields

\frac{dg}{dl}=Cg^{2},

(28)

with
C>0
.

The pairing interaction is thus marginal and grows only logarithmically under
infrared coarse graining.
Physically, overdamped collective modes dissipate into the electronic
continuum, continuously erasing temporal coherence of pairing fluctuations
and preventing strong accumulation of superconducting correlations.

III.2
B. Infrared reorganization by slow collective modes

In the memory–dominated regime derived in Sec. II,
collective dynamics is governed not by a single overdamped soft mode
but by a continuum of slow relaxation modes
characterized by the time–scale density of states

\rho(\lambda)
.
After integrating out the relaxation reservoir,
the retarded collective susceptibility takes the exact
spectral form

\chi_{R}^{-1}(\omega,\mathbf{q})=r+cq^{2}+\Sigma(\omega),\qquad\Sigma(\omega)=\int_{0}^{\Lambda}d\lambda\,\frac{\rho(\lambda)}{\lambda-i\omega}.

(29)

For a flat TDOS in the infrared,
\rho(\lambda\to 0)=\rho_{0}
,
the effective polarization kernel in the Cooper channel inherits the
infrared scaling

\Pi(\omega)\propto\int_{0}^{\Lambda}d\lambda\,\rho_{0}\frac{1}{\lambda^{2}+\omega^{2}}\sim\frac{1}{|\omega|},\qquad|\omega|\to 0.

(30)

Unlike the conventional Ohmic case, this singular behavior does not originate
from Landau damping by fast electronic continua.
Instead, it emerges from the accumulation of an extensive continuum of
near–marginal relaxation modes encoded in a flat TDOS.
The resulting kernel corresponds in the time domain to a universal
long–memory response
K(t)\sim 1/t
.

Although both Eliashberg theory and the present memory–dominated regime
generate infrared kernels scaling as
1/|\omega|
,
their physical origins are fundamentally distinct.
In Eliashberg theory the singularity reflects overdamped
dissipation arising from coupling to a fast electronic bath
25
;
26
.
Here, by contrast, the same infrared scaling results from
spectral accumulation of slow collective modes,
producing intrinsically non–Markovian dynamics.

This dynamical reorganization qualitatively alters the Cooper channel:
instead of marginal logarithmic enhancement characteristic of overdamped
criticality, pairing fluctuations accumulate coherently across widely
separated time scales.
The slow–mode reservoir therefore converts the Cooper channel into
a relevant infrared interaction.

III.3
C. Relevant infrared amplification of the Cooper channel

The infrared shell correction in the memory–dominated regime becomes

dg\propto g^{2}\int_{\Lambda/b}^{\Lambda}\frac{d\omega^{\prime}}{\omega^{\prime}\ln(\Lambda/\omega^{\prime})}.

(31)

Introducing
u=\ln(\Lambda/\omega^{\prime})
yields

dg\simeq\alpha g\,dl,

(32)

with
\alpha>0
proportional to the infrared TDOS weight.

The resulting RG flow reads

\frac{dg}{dl}=\alpha g,

(33)

with solution
g(l)=g_{0}e^{\alpha l}
.

The Cooper–channel interaction therefore grows algebraically under infrared
coarse graining, signaling a relevant superconducting instability rather than
a marginal one.
This qualitative contrast with Eliashberg theory is illustrated in Fig. 2.
A detailed shell RG derivation demonstrating the crossover from logarithmic to
power-law pairing growth is presented in Appendix D.

Physically, the slow–mode reservoir retains dynamical correlations across
widely separated time scales, allowing collective fluctuations to act
repeatedly on electronic degrees of freedom and amplify intrinsic
electronic pairing tendencies.

Superconductivity in strongly correlated systems thus emerges from
infrared spectral organization of collective relaxation dynamics,
rather than from finely tuned exchange of specific bosonic mediators.

IV
IV. Pairing from Memory–Dominated Critical Dynamics

The dynamical framework developed in the preceding sections establishes that
strongly correlated matter generically self–organizes into a regime dominated
by an extensive reservoir of slow collective modes.
Rather than producing conventional overdamped fluctuations described by
Ohmic Eliashberg dynamics, the accumulation of near–marginal relaxation rates
generates long–time memory kernels and nonanalytic infrared response.

Superconductivity in this setting emerges as a dynamical instability of the
memory–dominated collective spectrum.
The Cooper channel is reorganized by the slow–mode reservoir into a relevant
infrared interaction, fundamentally distinct from marginal pairing mediated by
fast bosonic modes.
Consequently, even moderate intrinsic electronic pairing scales are dynamically
amplified into an algebraic superconducting instability.

In this section we demonstrate explicitly how microscopic electronic
correlations seed pairing and how the slow–mode TDOS reorganizes the Cooper
channel into a memory–enhanced critical regime, ultimately yielding algebraic
scaling of the transition temperature and robust phase coherence.

IV.1
A. Intrinsic electronic pairing and dynamical formulation

In strongly correlated materials proximate to Mott insulating phases,
short–range Coulomb interactions naturally generate local singlet
correlations through exchange processes.
Starting from the Hubbard Hamiltonian,

H=-t\sum_{\langle ij\rangle\sigma}c_{i\sigma}^{\dagger}c_{j\sigma}+U\sum_{i}n_{i\uparrow}n_{i\downarrow},

(34)

projection to the strong–coupling sector yields the superexchange interaction

H_{J}=J\sum_{\langle ij\rangle}\Big(\mathbf{S}_{i}\cdot\mathbf{S}_{j}-\frac{1}{4}n_{i}n_{j}\Big),\qquad J=\frac{4t^{2}}{U},

(35)

favoring local singlet formation.

More generally, correlated electronic systems possess an intrinsic
short–range pairing scale
E_{\rm pair}
generated purely by electronic
interactions, independent of long–wavelength collective mediators.
This intrinsic scale provides the microscopic seed for superconductivity.

Within the dynamical framework developed in Sec. II, the pairing
susceptibility assumes the universal
t
–matrix form

\chi_{\Delta}^{-1}(\omega)=\frac{1}{g_{\rm pair}}-\Pi(\omega),

(36)

with
g_{\rm pair}\sim E_{\rm pair}^{-1}
.
Critical dynamics does not create attraction; it amplifies a
preexisting electronic pairing tendency through infrared spectral
reorganization.

IV.2
B. Memory–dominated Cooper kernel from flat TDOS

Because single–particle propagators admit a relaxational spectral
representation governed by the slow–mode TDOS, the Cooper bubble reduces to a
continuum integral over relaxation rates,

\Pi(\omega)\sim\int_{0}^{\Lambda}d\lambda\,\rho_{\rm eff}(\lambda)\frac{1}{\lambda^{2}+\omega^{2}}.

(37)

When the slow–mode reservoir develops,

\rho_{\rm eff}(\lambda\to 0)=\rho_{0}\neq 0,

(38)

the infrared behavior becomes

\Pi(\omega)\xrightarrow{|\omega|\ll\Lambda}\frac{\pi\rho_{0}}{2}\,\frac{1}{|\omega|}.

(39)

This nonanalytic enhancement reflects the accumulation of collective
memory across widely separated time scales.
Unlike conventional Eliashberg kernels, which are marginal in the infrared,
the present Cooper channel becomes relevant due to the flat TDOS.
The slow–mode reservoir therefore reorganizes the pairing problem into a
memory–dominated critical regime.

IV.3
C. Algebraic Instability and Transition Scale

The superconducting transition follows from the Thouless criterion

\chi_{\Delta}^{-1}(\omega\sim T_{c})=0,

(40)

which yields

T_{c}\sim\rho_{0}\,E_{\rm pair},

(41)

up to numerical factors of order unity.
The finite-temperature regularization of the infrared divergence,
leading to
\Pi(T)\sim 1/T
, is derived explicitly in Appendix E.

The exponential suppression characteristic of BCS theory is thus replaced by
algebraic scaling governed by the infrared spectral weight of the slow–mode
reservoir.
High transition temperatures arise generically from dynamical amplification of
moderate intrinsic pairing scales.

It is important to emphasize that superconductivity requires not only pair
formation but also global phase coherence.
The transition temperature is therefore ultimately bounded by the smaller of
the pairing amplitude and the superfluid phase stiffness,

k_{B}T_{c}\sim\min\left(\Delta,\,\rho_{s}\right),

(42)

with
\rho_{s}\sim n_{s}/m^{*}
.
The detailed scaling of the phase stiffness within the slow–mode framework
is discussed in Sec. V.

The key result of the present section is that the pairing scale itself is
controlled by a single infrared parameter, the zero–frequency TDOS

\rho_{0}
.
The slow–mode reservoir therefore acts as a universal dynamical amplifier:
it converts intrinsic electronic correlation energy into an algebraically
enhanced instability of the Cooper channel, independent of any specific
bosonic mediator.

In this sense, the TDOS–controlled fixed point defines a dynamical
stability class distinct from both weak–coupling BCS superconductors and
conventional Eliashberg scenarios.
The emergence of superconductivity is governed not by the exchange of a
particular glue, but by infrared spectral organization of collective
relaxation modes.

V. Experimental consequences of memory–dominated critical dynamics

The results of Secs. II–IV imply that unconventional superconductivity in
strongly correlated systems is governed by a unifying organizing principle:
the infrared spectral reorganization of collective relaxation modes.
When the time–scale density of states develops a flat low–
\lambda

sector, the system enters a memory–dominated critical regime characterized
by long–time kernels
K(t)\sim 1/t
and universal infrared enhancement of the
particle–particle channel.
In this section we show that several central experimental signatures—the
superconducting dome, Uemura scaling, and anomalous long–time dynamics—follow
directly and generically from the same infrared spectral weight
\rho_{0}
of the
slow–mode reservoir.

naturally produces both the superconducting dome and Uemura scaling.

(43)

Conventional quantum critical theories assume overdamped local dynamics

Criticality is reinterpreted not as single-mode softening but as
reorganization of the entire decay spectrum.
The TDOS thus replaces phenomenological damping coefficients with
a microscopic spectral quantity governing infrared universality.

A direct physical consequence of this spectral reorganization is
qualitative enhancement of superconducting instabilities.
Superconductivity need not rely on finely tuned bosonic mediators.
Instead, intrinsic short-range electronic pairing tendencies are
dynamically amplified by the slow-mode reservoir.
The exponential suppression characteristic of weak-coupling BCS theory

Strongly correlated quantum matter near dynamical critical points may

More broadly, the TDOS framework elevates dynamical spectral organization

Memory-dominated criticality represents a universality class distinct

Appendix A
Appendix A: Relaxation-rate spectra as the fundamental dynamical basis of collective dynamics

In conventional quantum many-body theory, collective behavior is often
organized in terms of energy eigenmodes of an underlying Hamiltonian,

H|n\rangle=E_{n}|n\rangle,

(S1)

with dynamical response expressed through oscillatory Fourier components at
frequencies set by energy differences.
This viewpoint is natural for closed systems whose dynamics is unitary and
time-reversal invariant, and it emphasizes stationary states and conservative
motion.

However, energy eigenmodes provide only a special organizing basis.
In generic interacting settings—especially in open, driven, or internally
coarse-grained systems—the physically relevant long-time organization is not
set by Hamiltonian energies but by the
stability spectrum
of the
effective dynamical generator.
In this work, the central spectral object is therefore the distribution of

relaxation rates
governing irreversible decay and memory.

(I) Dynamical linearization and relaxation modes:

For a general high-dimensional (possibly coarse-grained) dynamical system,

\dot{x}(t)=F(x(t)),

(S2)

linearization about a metastable configuration
x^{*}
yields

\delta\dot{x}(t)=-A\,\delta x(t),\qquad A=-\left.\frac{\partial F}{\partial x}\right|_{x^{*}}.

(S3)

The eigenvalue problem

Av_{i}=\lambda_{i}v_{i}

(S4)

defines a spectrum of collective relaxation modes with temporal evolution

\delta x_{i}(t)\sim e^{-\lambda_{i}t}.

(S5)

The real parts of
\lambda_{i}
set decay time scales, while imaginary parts
encode oscillatory components.
Purely oscillatory energy-mode dynamics corresponds to the singular limit

\mathrm{Re}\,\lambda_{i}=0
for all modes, which is realized only in strictly
Hamiltonian, non-dissipative evolution.

Thus the relaxation-rate spectrum
\{\lambda_{i}\}
constitutes the natural
dynamical basis for generic collective phenomena, including metastability,
critical slowing down, and non-Markovian memory.

(II) Liouvillian formulation (closed, open, and non-Markovian dynamics):

A conceptually complete formulation of nonequilibrium quantum dynamics is given
in terms of the density matrix
\hat{\varrho}(t)
and its dynamical generator.
For a closed system,
\hat{\varrho}(t)
evolves by the von Neumann equation,

\frac{d\hat{\varrho}}{dt}=-i[H,\hat{\varrho}]\equiv\mathcal{L}_{H}\hat{\varrho},

(S6)

where
\mathcal{L}_{H}
is the Hamiltonian Liouvillian superoperator.
If
H|n\rangle=E_{n}|n\rangle
, then the operator basis
|n\rangle\langle m|

diagonalizes
\mathcal{L}_{H}
:

\mathcal{L}_{H}\big(|n\rangle\langle m|\big)=-i(E_{n}-E_{m})\,|n\rangle\langle m|,

(S7)

so the Liouvillian eigenvalues are

\lambda_{nm}=-i(E_{n}-E_{m}),

(S8)

lying purely on the imaginary axis and reproducing undamped oscillations.

For open quantum systems, the reduced density matrix typically obeys a
master equation with a dissipative contribution.
In the Markovian limit, the evolution takes Lindblad form,

\frac{d\hat{\varrho}}{dt}=\mathcal{L}\hat{\varrho}=-i[H,\hat{\varrho}]+\sum_{\mu}\left(L_{\mu}\hat{\varrho}L_{\mu}^{\dagger}-\frac{1}{2}\{L_{\mu}^{\dagger}L_{\mu},\hat{\varrho}\}\right),

(S9)

where the jump operators
L_{\mu}
encode decoherence and dissipation.
The resulting Liouvillian
\mathcal{L}
is generically non-Hermitian as a
superoperator and therefore possesses complex eigenvalues

\mathcal{L}R_{\alpha}=\lambda_{\alpha}R_{\alpha},\qquad\mathrm{Re}\,\lambda_{\alpha}\leq 0,

(S10)

with decay rates set by
-\mathrm{Re}\,\lambda_{\alpha}
.
The density matrix admits the modal expansion

\hat{\varrho}(t)=\hat{\varrho}_{\rm ss}+\sum_{\alpha\neq 0}c_{\alpha}\,e^{\lambda_{\alpha}t}\,R_{\alpha},

(S11)

where
\hat{\varrho}_{\rm ss}
is the stationary state (
\lambda_{0}=0
) and the
long-time approach is controlled by the Liouvillian spectral gap and any
near-marginal continuum.

More generally, when the environment or eliminated internal coordinates retain
memory, the reduced dynamics becomes non-Markovian and takes a memory-kernel
form (Nakajima–Zwanzig structure),

\frac{d\hat{\varrho}}{dt}=\int_{0}^{t}ds\;\mathcal{K}(t-s)\,\hat{\varrho}(s),

(S12)

where
\mathcal{K}(t)
is a causal superoperator kernel.
In Laplace space,

\tilde{\hat{\varrho}}(s)=\big[s-\tilde{\mathcal{K}}(s)\big]^{-1}\hat{\varrho}(0),

(S13)

so the poles and branch cuts of
s-\tilde{\mathcal{K}}(s)
define the intrinsic
relaxation spectrum.
In this sense, even for quantum systems the relevant organizing structure for
long-time dynamics is generically a
relaxation-rate spectrum
(possibly
continuous), not an energy spectrum.

(III) Memory kernels and continuous relaxation spectra:

The same spectral logic appears already at the level of linear causal dynamics
for a collective coordinate
\phi(t)
:

\dot{\phi}(t)+\int_{0}^{t}ds\,K(t-s)\phi(s)=\eta(t).

(S14)

Taking the Laplace transform gives

\tilde{\phi}(s)=\frac{\tilde{\eta}(s)}{s+\tilde{K}(s)}.

(S15)

The analytic structure of
s+\tilde{K}(s)
determines the intrinsic relaxation
content: isolated poles correspond to discrete decay modes, while branch cuts
encode continua of decay rates.
Whenever long-time memory is present, the causal kernel admits a spectral
representation of the form

K(t)=\int_{0}^{\infty}d\lambda\,\rho(\lambda)e^{-\lambda t},

(S16)

which defines the time-scale spectrum.
A flat infrared TDOS produces the universal tail
K(t)\sim 1/t
.

Thus continuous relaxation spectra arise generically from causality and memory,
independent of microscopic Hamiltonian structure.

(IV) Energy modes as a special limit:

The familiar Fourier-mode picture of conservative dynamics corresponds to the
singular limit in which the relevant spectrum collapses onto the imaginary axis.
In the closed-system Liouvillian case this appears as

\lambda_{nm}=-i(E_{n}-E_{m})
, while in the memory-kernel representation it
corresponds to eliminating all decay rates (no weight at
\mathrm{Re}\,\lambda>0
),
recovering purely oscillatory behavior.
Away from this limit, generic interacting and/or open systems naturally develop
broad spectra of relaxation rates controlling infrared dynamics.

(V) Physical implication:

The TDOS—the infrared distribution of collective relaxation rates—
is therefore the fundamental organizing variable of collective dynamics in
correlated systems.
Energy eigenmodes describe an integrable, conservative limit, whereas
relaxation-rate spectra capture the universal structure of irreversible,
critical, and strongly interacting matter.

Memory-dominated criticality corresponds precisely to the formation of an
extensive continuum of near-marginal relaxation modes.
The resulting TDOS controls dynamical response, pairing enhancement, and
infrared universality, providing a deeper and more generally applicable
foundation than energy-based descriptions.

Appendix B: Gaussian MSRJD integration of a relaxation-mode reservoir and emergence of the self-energy

In this appendix we derive how a continuum
of relaxation (Ornstein–Uhlenbeck) modes generates a nonlocal-in-time memory

\Sigma(\omega)
upon Gaussian functional
integration in the Martin–Siggia–Rose–Janssen–De Dominicis (MSRJD) formalism.
The derivation is standard in structure (Gaussian path integration), but we

(I) Coupled stochastic dynamics:

We consider a collective degree of freedom
\phi(t)
coupled linearly to a
continuum of reservoir modes

\lambda>0

\displaystyle\dot{\phi}(t)

\displaystyle=-r\,\phi(t)+\int d\lambda\;g(\lambda)\,X_{\lambda}(t)+\eta(t),

(S17)

\displaystyle\dot{X}_{\lambda}(t)

\displaystyle=-\lambda\,X_{\lambda}(t)+g(\lambda)\,\phi(t)+\xi_{\lambda}(t).

(S18)

\displaystyle\langle\eta(t)\eta(t^{\prime})\rangle

\displaystyle=2D_{\phi}\,\delta(t-t^{\prime}),

(S19)

\displaystyle\langle\xi_{\lambda}(t)\,\xi_{\lambda^{\prime}}(t^{\prime})\rangle

\displaystyle=2D_{\lambda}\,\delta(\lambda-\lambda^{\prime})\,\delta(t-t^{\prime}).

(S20)

No equilibrium fluctuation–dissipation relation is assumed;
D_{\lambda}
may be
arbitrary. Our goal is to integrate out
\{X_{\lambda}\}
and obtain an exact
effective MSRJD action for
(\phi,\tilde{\phi})
containing a memory kernel.

(II) MSRJD action for the coupled system:

For a generic additive-noise Langevin equation
\dot{y}=f(y)+\zeta

\langle\zeta(t)\zeta(t^{\prime})\rangle=2D\,\delta(t-t^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\int\mathcal{D}y\,\mathcal{D}\tilde{y}\;

(S21)

\displaystyle\exp\!\left[-\int dt\;\tilde{y}(t)\big(\dot{y}(t)-f(y(t))\big)+\int dt\;D\,\tilde{y}(t)^{2}\right],

y

Applying (
S21
) to (
S17
)–(
S18
)

Z=\int\mathcal{D}\phi\,\mathcal{D}\tilde{\phi}\;\prod_{\lambda}\mathcal{D}X_{\lambda}\,\mathcal{D}\tilde{X}_{\lambda}\;e^{-S[\phi,\tilde{\phi},\{X_{\lambda},\tilde{X}_{\lambda}\}]},

(S22)

\displaystyle S_{\phi}

\lambda

Fix

or

(S26)

Combining (

\displaystyle S^{(\lambda)}[X_{\lambda},\tilde{X}_{\lambda};\phi,\tilde{\phi}]=\int dt\;\tilde{X}_{\lambda}(t)\big(\dot{X}_{\lambda}(t)+\lambda X_{\lambda}(t)\big)

\displaystyle-\int dt\;g(\lambda)\,\tilde{X}_{\lambda}(t)\,\phi(t)-\int dt\;g(\lambda)\,\tilde{\phi}(t)\,X_{\lambda}(t)

(IV) Functional integration over
X_{\lambda}

(S28)

Then the first term in (

(VI) Effective action: memory kernel (self-energy) and induced colored noise:

We now evaluate the two remaining terms in the exponent of

S37

1. The
\tilde{X}_{\lambda}\phi
term (deterministic memory kernel).

Start from

\int dt\;g(\lambda)\,\tilde{X}_{\lambda}(t)\,\phi(t).

(S42)

Insert (
S41
):

\displaystyle\int dt\;g(\lambda)\,\tilde{X}_{\lambda}(t)\,\phi(t)=\int dt\,dt^{\prime}\;g(\lambda)^{2}\,G_{\lambda}^{A}(t-t^{\prime})\,\tilde{\phi}(t^{\prime})\,\phi(t).

(S43)

Relabel integration variables
(t,t^{\prime})\to(t^{\prime},t)

\int dt\,dt^{\prime}\;g(\lambda)^{2}\,G_{\lambda}^{A}(t^{\prime}-t)\,\tilde{\phi}(t)\,\phi(t^{\prime}).

(S44)

Using the identity
G_{\lambda}^{A}(t^{\prime}-t)=G_{\lambda}^{R}(t-t^{\prime})

G_{\lambda}^{R}(t)\equiv\Theta(t)\,e^{-\lambda t},\qquad(\partial_{t}+\lambda)G_{\lambda}^{R}(t)=\delta(t),

we obtain

\displaystyle\int dt\;g(\lambda)\,\tilde{X}_{\lambda}(t)\,\phi(t)

\displaystyle=\int dt\,dt^{\prime}\;\tilde{\phi}(t)\,\underbrace{g(\lambda)^{2}\,G_{\lambda}^{R}(t-t^{\prime})}_{\equiv K_{\lambda}(t-t^{\prime})}\,\phi(t^{\prime}).

(S46)

Thus a single OU mode contributes the causal memory kernel

K_{\lambda}(t-t^{\prime})=g(\lambda)^{2}\,\Theta(t-t^{\prime})\,e^{-\lambda(t-t^{\prime})}.

(S47)

2. The
\tilde{X}_{\lambda}^{2}

Next consider

\int dt\;D_{\lambda}\,\tilde{X}_{\lambda}(t)^{2}.

(S48)

Insert (
S41

\displaystyle\int dt\;D_{\lambda}\,\tilde{X}_{\lambda}(t)^{2}

\displaystyle=\int dt\,dt^{\prime}\,dt^{\prime\prime}\;D_{\lambda}\,g(\lambda)^{2}\,G_{\lambda}^{A}(t-t^{\prime})\,G_{\lambda}^{A}(t-t^{\prime\prime})\,\tilde{\phi}(t^{\prime})\,\tilde{\phi}(t^{\prime\prime}).

(S49)

Define the induced (generally nonlocal) noise kernel

\mathcal{N}_{\lambda}(t^{\prime},t^{\prime\prime})\equiv D_{\lambda}\,g(\lambda)^{2}\int dt\;G_{\lambda}^{A}(t-t^{\prime})\,G_{\lambda}^{A}(t-t^{\prime\prime}),

(S50)

\int dt\;D_{\lambda}\,\tilde{X}_{\lambda}(t)^{2}=\int dt^{\prime}\,dt^{\prime\prime}\;\tilde{\phi}(t^{\prime})\,\mathcal{N}_{\lambda}(t^{\prime},t^{\prime\prime})\,\tilde{\phi}(t^{\prime\prime}).

(S51)

In equilibrium one may impose a fluctuation–dissipation relation linking

\mathcal{N}_{\lambda}
and
K_{\lambda}
, but this is not required for the
deterministic self-energy structure derived below.

3. Summing the reservoir and assembling

Combining (

\displaystyle S_{\mathrm{eff}}[\phi,\tilde{\phi}]=\int dt\;\tilde{\phi}(t)\big(\dot{\phi}(t)+r\phi(t)\big)-\int dt\;D_{\phi}\,\tilde{\phi}(t)^{2}

\displaystyle-\int dt\,dt^{\prime}\;\tilde{\phi}(t)\,K(t-t^{\prime})\,\phi(t^{\prime})-\int dt\,dt^{\prime}\;\tilde{\phi}(t)\,\mathcal{N}(t,t^{\prime})\,\tilde{\phi}(t^{\prime}),

where the total memory kernel is the reservoir sum

(S53)

(VI) Frequency-domain self-energy

To connect (

(VII) Connection to TDOS (relaxation-rate density of states):

(X) Relation to the Keldysh functional integral:

We briefly clarify the formal relation between the
MSRJD formulation employed in the present work and the real–time
Keldysh functional integral for driven–dissipative quantum systems.

The MSRJD action for a stochastic dynamical variable
\phi(t)
with
memory kernel and Gaussian noise may be written as

\displaystyle S_{\mathrm{MSRJD}}

\displaystyle=\int dt\,dt^{\prime}\,\tilde{\phi}(t)\left[\delta(t-t^{\prime})(\partial_{t}+r)-K_{R}(t-t^{\prime})\right]

(S69)

\displaystyle\phi(t^{\prime})-\int dt\,dt^{\prime}\,\tilde{\phi}(t)\,\mathcal{N}(t,t^{\prime})\,\tilde{\phi}(t^{\prime}),

where
\tilde{\phi}
denotes the response field,

K_{R}
is the retarded dynamical kernel (memory kernel),
and
\mathcal{N}
is the noise correlator introduced above.

The real–time Keldysh functional integral for an open quantum system
is naturally formulated in terms of classical and quantum fields,

\phi_{\mathrm{cl}}
and
\phi_{q}
, defined by the Keldysh rotation

\phi_{\mathrm{cl}}=\frac{\phi_{+}+\phi_{-}}{2},\qquad\phi_{q}=\phi_{+}-\phi_{-},

(S70)

where
\phi_{\pm}
denote fields on the forward and backward branches of
the time contour.

To quadratic order in the quantum field, the Keldysh action assumes
the general structure

S_{\mathrm{K}}=\int_{\omega}\begin{pmatrix}\phi_{\mathrm{cl}}&\phi_{q}\end{pmatrix}_{-\omega}\begin{pmatrix}0&K_{A}(\omega)\\
K_{R}(\omega)&iK_{K}(\omega)\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\phi_{\mathrm{cl}}\\
\phi_{q}\end{pmatrix}_{\omega},

(S71)

where
K_{R,A}
are retarded/advanced kernels and

K_{K}
is the Keldysh (noise) component.
The retarded kernel encodes dissipative response,
while the Keldysh component determines fluctuation amplitudes.

In the regime of strong decoherence or large occupation,
where quantum interference effects are suppressed,
the quantum field
\phi_{q}
acts as a response field.
Identifying

\phi_{q}\;\leftrightarrow\;i\tilde{\phi},

(S72)

and integrating out
\phi_{q}
via a Hubbard–Stratonovich transformation,
one recovers a stochastic Langevin equation with noise correlator
set by
K_{K}
.
The corresponding MSRJD action then takes the form
(
S69
), with the correspondence

K_{R}(\omega)\;\leftrightarrow\;K_{R}(\omega),\qquad\mathcal{N}(\omega)\;\leftrightarrow\;\tfrac{1}{2}K_{K}(\omega).

(S73)

Thus the MSRJD formulation used in the present work can be viewed
as the classical (or strongly dissipative) limit of the Keldysh
functional integral, in which the relevant dynamical information
is encoded in the retarded kernel and its associated spectral
properties.

Importantly, our theory does not rely on coherent quantum interference
or operator-valued field structure.
Instead, the central object is the spectrum of the dynamical generator
(equivalently, the retarded kernel),
whose low-frequency density of states controls the infrared
behavior and pairing amplification mechanism discussed in the main text.

Appendix C
Appendix C: Exact emergence of a memory kernel from integrating out an OU reservoir

In this Appendix we provide a self-contained derivation showing that integrating out a continuum of relaxational
Ornstein–Uhlenbeck (OU) modes generates a nonlocal-in-time (memory) kernel for
the remaining collective coordinate.
The result makes explicit that long-memory response is the generic outcome of
eliminating hidden internal degrees of freedom, and that a flat time-scale
density of states yields the universal tail
K(t)\sim 1/t
.
This time-domain elimination is equivalent in content to the Gaussian MSRJD integration,
but is presented here directly at the level of the coupled equations of motion.

(I) Coupled Markovian dynamics (collective field plus relaxational reservoir):

For clarity we first suppress spatial dependence and focus on temporal
structure; the extension to
\mathbf{r}
(including
cq^{2}
terms) is immediate by
Fourier transforming in space.
We consider a slow collective variable
\phi(t)
linearly coupled to a
continuum of relaxational OU modes
X_{\lambda}(t)
labeled by decay rate

\lambda\geq 0
:

\displaystyle\dot{\phi}(t)

\displaystyle=-r\,\phi(t)+\int_{0}^{\Lambda}d\lambda\,g(\lambda)\,X_{\lambda}(t)+\eta(t),

(S74)

\displaystyle\dot{X}_{\lambda}(t)

\displaystyle=-\lambda\,X_{\lambda}(t)+g(\lambda)\,\phi(t)+\zeta_{\lambda}(t).

(S75)

Here
r>0
is the bare restoring (mass) parameter,
\Lambda
is an ultraviolet
cutoff on relaxation rates, and
\eta
and
\zeta_{\lambda}
are (possibly
independent) noise sources.
For
\lambda>0
each reservoir coordinate is exponentially stable; the sector

\lambda\to 0^{+}
should be understood as the infrared limit of a continuous
spectrum of increasingly slow relaxational modes rather than an exactly
conserved degree of freedom.
Equations
(
S74
)–(
S75
) define a
Markovian

(first-order) dynamical system in the enlarged state space

\{\phi,X_{\lambda}\}
.

(II) Exact solution for the OU reservoir modes
:

Equation (
S75
) is linear and can be solved exactly by the
integrating-factor method.
Multiply (
S75
) by
e^{\lambda t}
:

e^{\lambda t}\dot{X}_{\lambda}(t)+\lambda e^{\lambda t}X_{\lambda}(t)=e^{\lambda t}\big[g(\lambda)\phi(t)+\zeta_{\lambda}(t)\big].

(S76)

The left-hand side is the total derivative

\frac{d}{dt}\Big(e^{\lambda t}X_{\lambda}(t)\Big)=e^{\lambda t}\big[g(\lambda)\phi(t)+\zeta_{\lambda}(t)\big].

(S77)

Integrate from an initial time
t_{0}
to
t
:

e^{\lambda t}X_{\lambda}(t)-e^{\lambda t_{0}}X_{\lambda}(t_{0})=\int_{t_{0}}^{t}ds\,e^{\lambda s}\big[g(\lambda)\phi(s)+\zeta_{\lambda}(s)\big].

(S78)

Solving for
X_{\lambda}(t)
yields the exact causal representation

\displaystyle X_{\lambda}(t)

\displaystyle=e^{-\lambda(t-t_{0})}X_{\lambda}(t_{0})

(S79)

\displaystyle+\int_{t_{0}}^{t}ds\,e^{-\lambda(t-s)}\Big[g(\lambda)\phi(s)+\zeta_{\lambda}(s)\Big].

This expression already shows that the reservoir coordinate at time
t
depends
on the entire history
\{\phi(s)\}_{s<t}
.

(III) Substitution into the collective equation and emergence of a memory kernel:

Substituting (
S79
) into the collective equation
(
S74
) gives

\displaystyle\dot{\phi}(t)

\displaystyle=-r\,\phi(t)+\int_{0}^{\Lambda}d\lambda\,g(\lambda)\,

\displaystyle\Bigg[e^{-\lambda(t-t_{0})}X_{\lambda}(t_{0})+\int_{t_{0}}^{t}ds\,e^{-\lambda(t-s)}\Big(g(\lambda)\phi(s)+\zeta_{\lambda}(s)\Big)\Bigg]

\displaystyle+\eta(t)

This motivates the definition of the causal memory kernel

K(t-s)\equiv\Theta(t-s)\int_{0}^{\Lambda}d\lambda\,g(\lambda)^{2}\,e^{-\lambda(t-s)},

(S81)

so that (
) becomes

\int_{0}^{\Lambda}d\lambda\,g(\lambda)\int_{t_{0}}^{t}ds\,e^{-\lambda(t-s)}g(\lambda)\phi(s)=\int_{t_{0}}^{t}ds\,K(t-s)\,\phi(s).

(S82)

With this definition, Eq. (
) can be written as

\dot{\phi}(t)=-r\,\phi(t)+\int_{t_{0}}^{t}ds\,K(t-s)\phi(s)+\eta(t)+\xi_{\rm eff}(t)+J_{\rm ic}(t),

(S83)

where we have collected the remaining contributions into an effective noise
term (generated by the reservoir noise) and an initial-condition transient:

\displaystyle\xi_{\rm eff}(t)

\displaystyle\equiv\int_{0}^{\Lambda}d\lambda\,g(\lambda)\int_{t_{0}}^{t}ds\,e^{-\lambda(t-s)}\zeta_{\lambda}(s),

(S84)

\displaystyle J_{\rm ic}(t)

\displaystyle\equiv\int_{0}^{\Lambda}d\lambda\,g(\lambda)\,e^{-\lambda(t-t_{0})}X_{\lambda}(t_{0}).

(S85)

Equation (
S83
) is an exact
generalized Langevin

equation for
\phi(t)
.
Although the enlarged system (
S74
)–(
S75
)
is Markovian, eliminating the hidden reservoir coordinates produces a
non-Markovian (memory) term and, generally, colored effective noise.

Long-time limit.

For
t-t_{0}\gg\Lambda^{-1}
, the transient
J_{\rm ic}(t)
decays.
More precisely, decay holds provided the effective spectral weight

g(\lambda)X_{\lambda}(t_{0})
is integrable near
\lambda=0
so that the

\lambda\to 0^{+}
sector does not produce a non-decaying contribution.

(IV) TDOS form and Laplace-transform structure:

The memory kernel identified above depends only on the
combination
g(\lambda)^{2}
that weights the contribution
of each relaxational mode to the collective dynamics.
It is therefore natural to introduce the TDOS as the effective
coupling-weighted spectral density of relaxation rates:

\rho(\lambda)\equiv g(\lambda)^{2}.

(S86)

More generally, in a microscopic description with discrete
relaxational modes of density
\nu(\lambda)
and coupling amplitudes

g_{\lambda}
, the kernel involves the combination

\nu(\lambda)|g_{\lambda}|^{2}
.
For notational simplicity, we absorb any slowly varying factors
into the definition of
\rho(\lambda)
.
Thus
\rho(\lambda)
should be understood as the
effective spectral weight governing how strongly relaxation modes
at scale
\lambda
influence the collective coordinate
\phi
.

With this definition, the memory kernel becomes

K(t)=\int_{0}^{\Lambda}d\lambda\,\rho(\lambda)\,e^{-\lambda t},\qquad t\geq 0.

(S87)

Equation (
S87
) shows that the memory kernel is
precisely the Laplace transform of the TDOS (with ultraviolet cutoff

\Lambda
).
In this sense the TDOS is the fundamental spectral object of the
theory, while the time-domain memory kernel is its direct transform.
The infrared dynamical behavior of the collective coordinate is
therefore completely determined by the low-
\lambda
structure of

\rho(\lambda)
.

(V) Flat TDOS implies universal long-memory tail
K(t)\sim 1/t
:

If the TDOS is finite at the origin (“flat” TDOS),

\rho(\lambda)\xrightarrow{\lambda\to 0}\rho_{0},

(S88)

then for times
t\gg\Lambda^{-1}
the infrared part of
(
S87
) dominates and we may approximate
\rho(\lambda)\simeq\rho_{0}
over the relevant range.
In that case the kernel can be evaluated exactly:

\displaystyle K(t)=\int_{0}^{\Lambda}d\lambda\,\rho_{0}\,e^{-\lambda t}.

(S89)

Perform the change of variables
u=\lambda t
(so
d\lambda=du/t
):

\displaystyle K(t)

\displaystyle=\rho_{0}\int_{0}^{\Lambda t}\frac{du}{t}\,e^{-u}=\frac{\rho_{0}}{t}\Big(1-e^{-\Lambda t}\Big).

(S90)

Therefore, at long times
t\gg\Lambda^{-1}
,

K(t)\simeq\frac{\rho_{0}}{t},\qquad(t\gg\Lambda^{-1}),

(S91)

which is the universal long-memory tail discussed in the main text.

(VI) Frequency-space form and connection to the retarded self-energy:

For completeness we relate the time-domain memory kernel to the standard
frequency-space self-energy.
Taking the Fourier transform of the reservoir solution
(
S79
) in the stationary (long-time) limit yields

X_{\lambda}(\omega)=\frac{g(\lambda)}{\lambda-i\omega}\,\phi(\omega)+\frac{1}{\lambda-i\omega}\,\zeta_{\lambda}(\omega),

(S92)

and substituting into (
S74
) gives

\Big[-i\omega+r+\Sigma^{R}(\omega)\Big]\phi(\omega)=\eta(\omega)+\int_{0}^{\Lambda}d\lambda\,\frac{g(\lambda)}{\lambda-i\omega}\zeta_{\lambda}(\omega),

(S93)

with the retarded self-energy

\Sigma^{R}(\omega)=\int_{0}^{\Lambda}d\lambda\,\frac{g(\lambda)^{2}}{\lambda-i\omega}=\int_{0}^{\Lambda}d\lambda\,\frac{\rho(\lambda)}{\lambda-i\omega}.

(S94)

We use the convention
G_{R}^{-1}(\omega)=-i\omega+r+\Sigma^{R}(\omega)
; other
sign conventions for the self-energy appear in the literature.

Separating real and imaginary parts using

\frac{1}{\lambda-i\omega}=\frac{\lambda}{\lambda^{2}+\omega^{2}}+i\,\frac{\omega}{\lambda^{2}+\omega^{2}},

(S95)

one obtains

\displaystyle\mathrm{Re}\,\Sigma^{R}(\omega)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\Lambda}d\lambda\,\rho(\lambda)\frac{\lambda}{\lambda^{2}+\omega^{2}},

(S96)

\displaystyle\mathrm{Im}\,\Sigma^{R}(\omega)

\displaystyle=\omega\int_{0}^{\Lambda}d\lambda\,\rho(\lambda)\frac{1}{\lambda^{2}+\omega^{2}}.

(S97)

For a flat TDOS
\rho(\lambda)\simeq\rho_{0}
and
|\omega|\ll\Lambda
,

\displaystyle\mathrm{Im}\,\Sigma^{R}(\omega)

\displaystyle\simeq\omega\rho_{0}\int_{0}^{\Lambda}\frac{d\lambda}{\lambda^{2}+\omega^{2}}=\omega\rho_{0}\left[\frac{1}{|\omega|}\arctan\!\Big(\frac{\Lambda}{|\omega|}\Big)\right]

\displaystyle\xrightarrow{|\omega|\ll\Lambda}\frac{\pi}{2}\rho_{0}\,\mathrm{sgn}(\omega),

(S98)

and

\displaystyle\mathrm{Re}\,\Sigma^{R}(\omega)

\displaystyle\simeq\rho_{0}\int_{0}^{\Lambda}d\lambda\,\frac{\lambda}{\lambda^{2}+\omega^{2}}=\frac{\rho_{0}}{2}\int_{0}^{\Lambda}d\lambda\,\frac{2\lambda}{\lambda^{2}+\omega^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\rho_{0}}{2}\left[\ln(\lambda^{2}+\omega^{2})\right]_{\lambda=0}^{\lambda=\Lambda}

\displaystyle=\frac{\rho_{0}}{2}\ln\!\left(\frac{\Lambda^{2}+\omega^{2}}{\omega^{2}}\right)\simeq\rho_{0}\ln\!\left(\frac{\Lambda}{|\omega|}\right),

\displaystyle\qquad(|\omega|\ll\Lambda),

(S99)

which reproduces the logarithmic reactive singularity discussed in the main
text.

Summary.

Equations (
S83
)–(
S87
) show that
integrating out a continuum of OU relaxation modes yields an
exact

generalized Langevin equation for the collective coordinate with a causal
memory kernel
K(t)
.
The kernel is the Laplace transform of the TDOS.
A finite TDOS at the origin produces the universal long-memory tail

K(t)\sim 1/t
, and the corresponding retarded self-energy exhibits the

\mathrm{Im}\,\Sigma^{R}(\omega)\propto\mathrm{sgn}(\omega)
together with a

This makes explicit that memory-dominated infrared dynamics arises from the
self-organized accumulation of slow internal relaxation modes, rather than from
coupling to a fast Markovian bath.

(VII) Conceptual structure (from hidden modes to infrared memory):

The derivation above establishes a general structural result.
A Markovian dynamical system defined in an extended state space

\{\phi,X_{\lambda}\}
becomes non-Markovian when the internal degrees of
freedom
X_{\lambda}
are eliminated.
The resulting equation for
\phi
necessarily acquires a causal memory
kernel given exactly by the Laplace transform of the time-scale density
of states,

K(t)=\int_{0}^{\Lambda}d\lambda\,\rho(\lambda)e^{-\lambda t}.

(S100)

This relation is purely kinematic and does not rely on perturbation theory,
weak coupling, or specific microscopic assumptions.
The infrared dynamical structure of the collective field is therefore
completely determined by the low-
\lambda
scaling of
\rho(\lambda)
.

If the TDOS vanishes at the origin,
\rho(\lambda)\to 0
as

\lambda\to 0
, the kernel decays exponentially and the dynamics
reduces to effectively Markovian overdamping.
By contrast, when the TDOS remains finite at zero relaxation rate,

\rho(\lambda\to 0)=\rho_{0}\neq 0,

(S101)

the Laplace transform produces the universal long-time tail

K(t)\simeq\frac{\rho_{0}}{t}.

(S102)

Thus long-memory infrared dynamics is not an additional assumption
but an unavoidable consequence of a flat slow-mode spectrum.
The TDOS provides the fundamental organizing principle linking
microscopic stability structure to macroscopic temporal response.

In this sense, the emergence of memory-dominated criticality reflects
a dynamical phase characterized by an extensive accumulation of
near-marginal relaxation modes rather than by coupling to a fast bath.

Appendix D
Appendix D: Renormalization-group flow of the Cooper channel in conventional and memory-dominated regimes

In this Appendix we derive explicitly the infrared renormalization-group (RG)
flow equations for the effective pairing interaction in the Cooper channel.
We contrast the conventional Eliashberg regime with the memory-dominated
regime generated by a flat relaxation-rate density of states.
The derivation follows from shell integration of the linearized gap equation
and clarifies the origin of marginal versus relevant superconducting
instabilities.

Fermionic Matsubara frequencies are denoted by
\omega

and
\omega^{\prime}
, and
\Omega\equiv\omega-\omega^{\prime}
is the bosonic transfer
frequency entering the pairing kernel
g(\Omega)
.

(I) Linearized gap equation and RG shell construction:

At low energies the linearized gap equation takes the schematic form

\Delta(\omega)=\int_{T}^{\Lambda}\frac{d\omega^{\prime}}{|\omega^{\prime}|}\,g(\omega-\omega^{\prime})\,\Delta(\omega^{\prime}),

(S103)

where
\Lambda
is the ultraviolet cutoff and
T
provides the infrared
cutoff. The factor
1/|\omega^{\prime}|
originates from the fermionic Cooper bubble.
The effective pairing kernel
g(\Omega)
is generated by collective
fluctuations.

The RG procedure consists of integrating out a thin frequency shell

\omega^{\prime}\in[\Lambda/b,\Lambda]
with
b>1
and absorbing its contribution
into a renormalized coupling
g\rightarrow g+dg
.
Defining the RG scale
l=\ln b
, the flow equation follows from the shell
contribution.

(II) Conventional Eliashberg regime:

Near metallic quantum criticality with Ohmic Landau damping, the bosonic
propagator obeys

D(\Omega)\sim\frac{1}{|\Omega|},

(S104)

so that the pairing kernel scales as

g(\Omega)\sim g\,D(\Omega)\sim\frac{g}{|\Omega|},

(S105)

where
g
denotes the dimensionless pairing interaction.

The leading shell correction arises from the ladder diagram and is
proportional to

dg\propto g^{2}\int_{\Lambda/b}^{\Lambda}\frac{d\omega^{\prime}}{|\omega^{\prime}|}.

(S106)

The shell integral is elementary:

\int_{\Lambda/b}^{\Lambda}\frac{d\omega^{\prime}}{\omega^{\prime}}=\ln\Lambda-\ln(\Lambda/b)=\ln b\equiv dl.

(S107)

Hence the RG equation takes the marginal form

\frac{dg}{dl}=C\,g^{2},

(S108)

with
C
a positive numerical constant.

The solution is

g(l)=\frac{g_{0}}{1-Cg_{0}l}\simeq g_{0}+Cg_{0}^{2}l,

(S109)

demonstrating logarithmic enhancement characteristic of a marginal
superconducting instability.

(III) Memory-dominated regime:

In the memory-dominated regime derived in Sec. II, the collective
susceptibility behaves as

\chi(\Omega)\sim\frac{1}{\rho_{0}\ln(\Lambda/|\Omega|)},

(S110)

reflecting the flat TDOS of slow relaxation modes.

The pairing kernel therefore scales as

g(\Omega)\sim\frac{g}{\ln(\Lambda/|\Omega|)}.

(S111)

The shell correction becomes

dg\propto g^{2}\int_{\Lambda/b}^{\Lambda}\frac{d\omega^{\prime}}{|\omega^{\prime}|}\frac{1}{\ln(\Lambda/\omega^{\prime})}.

(S112)

Introducing the variable

u=\ln\frac{\Lambda}{\omega^{\prime}},\qquad du=-\frac{d\omega^{\prime}}{\omega^{\prime}},

(S113)

the integration limits transform as

\omega^{\prime}=\Lambda\Rightarrow u=0,\qquad\omega^{\prime}=\Lambda/b\Rightarrow u=\ln b\equiv dl.

(S114)

Equation (
S112
) becomes

dg\propto g^{2}\int_{0}^{dl}\frac{du}{u}.

(S115)

The apparent divergence at
u\to 0
is cut off at a microscopic scale

u_{\min}=O(1)
marking the onset of the logarithmic TDOS regime.
The shell contribution reduces to

dg=\alpha\,g\,dl,

(S116)

where
\alpha>0
depends on the spectral weight
\rho_{0}
and the onset
scale of the memory-dominated regime.

The RG equation is therefore

\frac{dg}{dl}=\alpha\,g,

(S117)

with solution

g(l)=g_{0}e^{\alpha l}=g_{0}b^{\alpha}.

(S118)

The pairing interaction thus grows as a power law under infrared coarse
graining, signaling a relevant superconducting instability.

(IV) Physical origin of marginal versus relevant behavior:

The qualitative difference between Eqs. (
S108
)
and (
S117
) originates from the infrared structure
of the pairing kernel.

In the Eliashberg regime, each logarithmic shell contributes
independently, leading to cumulative logarithmic growth.
In contrast, the memory-dominated kernel generated by a flat TDOS
correlates fluctuations across all time scales, producing linear
amplification of the effective interaction under RG.

Consequently, even moderate intrinsic electronic pairing scales are
dynamically amplified into a runaway superconducting instability once
a slow-mode reservoir is formed.

Summary.

Ohmic Landau damping yields a marginal Cooper-channel flow

dg/dl\propto g^{2}
, producing only logarithmic enhancement.
A flat relaxation-rate TDOS instead generates a memory-dominated kernel
that renders the pairing interaction relevant, with
dg/dl\propto g

and power-law growth.
This dynamical reorganization of infrared spectral weight underlies
the algebraic superconducting instability central to the present theory.

Appendix E
Appendix E: Finite-temperature regulation of the infrared divergence

In this Appendix we explicitly demonstrate how the zero–temperature infrared
divergence of the polarization kernel,

\Pi(\omega)\sim 1/|\omega|
, is regulated at finite temperature, yielding the
universal scaling
\Pi(T)\sim 1/T
.
This establishes rigorously that the thermal scale acts as the infrared cutoff
in the memory–dominated critical regime.

(I) Spectral integral representation:

In the slow–mode reservoir regime the Cooper-channel polarization admits the
spectral form

\Pi(\omega)=\int_{0}^{\Lambda}d\lambda\;\frac{\rho_{\rm eff}(\lambda)}{\lambda^{2}+\omega^{2}}.

(S119)

When the effective time–scale density of states is finite at the origin,

\rho_{\rm eff}(\lambda\to 0)=\rho_{0}
, the infrared behavior is dominated by the
small–
\lambda
sector and Eq. (
S119
) reduces to

\Pi(\omega)\simeq\rho_{0}\int_{0}^{\Lambda}d\lambda\;\frac{1}{\lambda^{2}+\omega^{2}}.

(S120)

The elementary integral yields

\int_{0}^{\Lambda}\frac{d\lambda}{\lambda^{2}+\omega^{2}}=\frac{1}{|\omega|}\tan^{-1}\!\left(\frac{\Lambda}{|\omega|}\right).

(S121)

In the low–frequency limit
|\omega|\ll\Lambda
, one obtains

\Pi(\omega)\simeq\frac{\pi\rho_{0}}{2}\,\frac{1}{|\omega|},

(S122)

which expresses the nonanalytic infrared enhancement at zero temperature.

(II) Thermal infrared cutoff:

At finite temperature the imaginary–time direction is compactified to the
interval
\tau\in[0,\beta]
with
\beta=1/T
, and dynamical correlations are
expressed in terms of discrete Matsubara frequencies.
As a consequence, continuous access to arbitrarily small frequencies is lost,
and the thermal scale provides an effective infrared regulator.

Operationally, the zero–temperature denominator
\omega^{2}
in
Eq. (
S120
) is replaced by the minimal dynamical scale of order

T^{2}
, yielding

\Pi(T)\simeq\rho_{0}\int_{0}^{\Lambda}d\lambda\;\frac{1}{\lambda^{2}+(cT)^{2}},

(S123)

where
c
is a numerical constant of order unity determined by the Matsubara
spectrum.

Evaluating the integral gives

\Pi(T)=\rho_{0}\,\frac{1}{cT}\tan^{-1}\!\left(\frac{\Lambda}{cT}\right)\simeq\frac{\pi\rho_{0}}{2c}\,\frac{1}{T},\qquad T\ll\Lambda.

(S124)

Thus the infrared divergence is universally cut off by the thermal scale,

\Pi(T)\propto\frac{1}{T}.

(S125)

(III) Equivalent Matsubara summation:

The same result follows directly from the finite–temperature Matsubara
representation of the polarization bubble,

\Pi(0,T)\sim T\sum_{\omega_{m}}\int_{0}^{\Lambda}d\lambda\;\frac{\rho_{0}}{\lambda^{2}+\omega_{m}^{2}},\qquad\omega_{m}=(2m+1)\pi T.

(S126)

Using the standard identity

T\sum_{\omega_{m}}\frac{1}{\lambda^{2}+\omega_{m}^{2}}=\frac{1}{2\lambda}\tanh\!\left(\frac{\lambda}{2T}\right),

(S127)

and expanding for
\lambda\ll T
,

\tanh\!\left(\frac{\lambda}{2T}\right)\simeq\frac{\lambda}{2T},

(S128)

one obtains

T\sum_{\omega_{m}}\frac{1}{\lambda^{2}+\omega_{m}^{2}}\simeq\frac{1}{2T}.

(S129)

Substitution into Eq. (
S126
) yields

\Pi(0,T)\sim\int_{0}^{\Lambda}d\lambda\;\rho_{0}\frac{1}{2T}\propto\frac{\rho_{0}}{T},

(S130)

confirming that finite temperature universally regulates the infrared
divergence.

(IV) Implication for the superconducting transition:

The Thouless criterion,

\chi_{\Delta}^{-1}(0,T_{c})=0\qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad\frac{1}{g_{\rm pair}}=\Pi(T_{c}),

(S131)

therefore gives

T_{c}\sim\rho_{0}\,g_{\rm pair},

(S132)

or equivalently
T_{c}\sim\rho_{0}E_{\rm pair}
in terms of the bare electronic
pairing scale.
The exponential suppression characteristic of BCS theory is thus replaced by
algebraic scaling controlled by the infrared spectral weight of the slow–mode
reservoir.

Appendix F
Appendix F: Spectral function generated by memory–dominated self–energy

In this Appendix we derive the real–frequency spectral function associated
with the collective retarded susceptibility in the memory–dominated critical
regime. We show that a flat slow–mode TDOS produces a broad non–Lorentzian
continuum with logarithmic infrared scaling and a universal collapse form.

(I) Retarded susceptibility in the memory–dominated regime:

In the infrared regime controlled by a flat TDOS,
the retarded inverse susceptibility takes the universal form

\chi_{R}^{-1}(\omega,\mathbf{q})=\Delta_{\mathbf{q}}+\rho_{0}\ln\!\left(\frac{\Lambda}{|\omega|}\right)+i\Gamma\,\mathrm{sgn}(\omega),

(S133)

where

\Delta_{\mathbf{q}}\equiv r+cq^{2},\qquad\Gamma=\frac{\pi}{2}\rho_{0},

(S134)

and
\Lambda
is the ultraviolet cutoff of the slow–mode reservoir.
Accordingly,

\chi_{R}(\omega,\mathbf{q})=\frac{1}{\Delta_{\mathbf{q}}+\rho_{0}\ln(\Lambda/|\omega|)+i\Gamma\,\mathrm{sgn}(\omega)}.

(S135)

(Throughout we use
\omega
for real frequency; Matsubara frequencies are
denoted separately in Appendix D.)

(II) Definition of the spectral function:

The spectral function of collective fluctuations is defined by

A(\omega,\mathbf{q})\equiv-2\,\mathrm{Im}\,\chi_{R}(\omega,\mathbf{q}).

(S136)

Writing
\chi_{R}^{-1}=X(\omega,\mathbf{q})+iY(\omega)
with

\displaystyle X(\omega,\mathbf{q})

\displaystyle=\Delta_{\mathbf{q}}+\rho_{0}\ln\!\left(\frac{\Lambda}{|\omega|}\right),

(S137)

\displaystyle Y(\omega)

\displaystyle=\Gamma\,\mathrm{sgn}(\omega),

(S138)

one has
\chi_{R}=(X-iY)/(X^{2}+Y^{2})
, hence

A(\omega,\mathbf{q})=\frac{2\Gamma}{\left[\Delta_{\mathbf{q}}+\rho_{0}\ln(\Lambda/|\omega|)\right]^{2}+\Gamma^{2}}.

(S139)

(III) Infrared scaling at criticality:

Along the critical manifold
\Delta_{\mathbf{q}}=0
,

A(\omega,\mathbf{q}=0)=\frac{2\Gamma}{\rho_{0}^{2}\ln^{2}(\Lambda/|\omega|)+\Gamma^{2}}.

(S140)

For
|\omega|\ll\Lambda
the logarithm dominates and the universal infrared tail is

A(\omega)\xrightarrow{|\omega|\to 0}\frac{2\Gamma}{\rho_{0}^{2}}\,\frac{1}{\ln^{2}(\Lambda/|\omega|)}.

(S141)

Thus the spectral weight is suppressed only logarithmically toward zero
frequency, reflecting the accumulation of memory across many time scales.

(IV) Peak / hump scale away from criticality:

For finite
\Delta_{\mathbf{q}}
, Eq. (
S139
) is maximized
when

\Delta_{\mathbf{q}}+\rho_{0}\ln\!\left(\frac{\Lambda}{|\omega_{*}|}\right)=0,

(S142)

which yields

|\omega_{*}|=\Lambda\exp\!\left(\frac{\Delta_{\mathbf{q}}}{\rho_{0}}\right).

(S143)

For
\Delta_{\mathbf{q}}>0
this scale lies above the infrared window
(
|\omega_{*}|\gtrsim\Lambda
) and no low–energy peak develops, whereas for

\Delta_{\mathbf{q}}<0
a pronounced low–frequency hump appears at

|\omega_{*}|\ll\Lambda
.

(V) Universal scaling collapse:

Define the scaling variable

u(\omega,\mathbf{q})\equiv\frac{\Delta_{\mathbf{q}}}{\rho_{0}}+\ln\!\left(\frac{\Lambda}{|\omega|}\right).

(S144)

Using
\Gamma=(\pi/2)\rho_{0}
, Eq. (
S139
) reduces to the
universal form

A(\omega,\mathbf{q})=\frac{\pi}{\rho_{0}}\,\frac{1}{u(\omega,\mathbf{q})^{2}+(\pi/2)^{2}},

(S145)

so that all momentum and tuning dependence enters only through
u
, implying a
collapse of line shapes onto a single curve.

(VI) Finite–temperature infrared cutoff:

At finite temperature the logarithmic growth is regulated by the thermal scale.
A minimal phenomenological implementation consistent with Appendix D is

|\omega|\;\rightarrow\;\max\{|\omega|,cT\},\qquad c=O(1),

(S146)

yielding

A_{T}(\omega,\mathbf{q})=\frac{2\Gamma}{\left[\Delta_{\mathbf{q}}+\rho_{0}\ln(\Lambda/\max\{|\omega|,cT\})\right]^{2}+\Gamma^{2}}.

(S147)

For
|\omega|\ll T
the logarithm saturates, producing a finite infrared spectral
weight rather than the zero–temperature logarithmic suppression.

(VII) Physical interpretation:

Unlike conventional overdamped criticality, where
\mathrm{Im}\,\Sigma^{R}(\omega)

vanishes linearly with
|\omega|
, the memory–dominated regime exhibits
frequency–independent damping (
\propto\mathrm{sgn}(\omega)
) accompanied by a
logarithmically singular reactive part. The resulting spectral function is
therefore a broad continuum with slowly varying infrared tails rather than a
sharp Lorentzian quasiparticle peak.

Equation (
S139
) (or equivalently the collapse form
(
S145
)) provides a directly testable dynamical
signature of the slow–mode reservoir and the associated TDOS universality
class.
