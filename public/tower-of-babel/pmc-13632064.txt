Vitamin D Supplementation Associated With Alterations in Lipid Metabolism in Children With Glomerular Disease
Authors: Denise C. Hasson, Aditya Surapaneni, Michal L. Melamed, Christine B. Sethna, Meredith A. Atkinson, Joshua J. Zaritsky, Mary B. Leonard, Marcus D. Goncalves, Morgan E. Grams, Michelle R. Denburg
Published: 2026

To the Editor:

Low levels of vitamin D are predictive of many adverse health outcomes. However, trials of vitamin D supplementation in adults, although successfully increasing vitamin D levels, have failed to show any benefit on cardiovascular, stroke, or bone health outcomes.1,2 Vitamin D supplementation in children and adolescents has similarly shown no improvement in cardiometabolic or body composition/growth outcomes.3 Whether there are metabolic effects associated with vitamin D supplementation that may attenuate other potential health benefits is uncertain.

The cohort was originally recruited from 4 centers as part of a prospective interventional clinical trial (NCT01835639). Institutional review boards approved this study at all sites, and informed consent and assent (when able) were obtained from each participant. The study was performed in accordance with the ethical principles as described in the 1964 Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. We evaluated the serum metabolome of 18 children with active primary glomerular disease who received cholecalciferol supplementation for 12 weeks.4 Eligible patients were deficient (serum 25-hydroxyvitamin D [25(OH)D] <20 ng/mL) or insufficient [25(OH)D 20-30 ng/mL] and received 4000 IU/d or 2000 IU/d cholecalciferol, respectively. Metabolites were measured via untargeted metabolomic profiling by Metabolon, Inc via protocols described previously5 from samples taken at baseline, 6 weeks, and 12 weeks of supplementation. Metabolites (N = 922) were log2-transformed; nondrug metabolites and those missing >80% were excluded (n = 149). Mixed linear models with random intercepts tested change after supplementation. An unadjusted model was used because changes were analyzed per person. A Bonferroni corrected P value of <6.5 × 10−5 (0.05 divided by 773 metabolites) was considered statistically significant.

The cohort had a mean age of 12.1 ± 4.7 (standard deviation) years, 50% were female, and estimated glomerular filtration rate (by the Chronic Kidney Disease in Children Under 25 equation) was 95 ± 36 mL/min/m2 and remained relatively stable throughout the study time period (88 ± 30 and 89 ± 32 mL/min/m2 at subsequent visits). Our results suggest that vitamin D supplementation, even over the short-term, alters lipid metabolism. This is similar to a recent analysis of the Chronic Kidney Disease in Children cohort that evaluated the association between 13 micronutrients and the plasma metabolome in children with chronic kidney disease. Vitamin D intake had the greatest association with the metabolome (27 metabolites) of all micronutrients, with the majority of associated metabolites (19 of the 27, 70%) related to lipid metabolites belonging to the plasmalogen, phosphatidylcholine, and lysophospholipid subpathways.6 In a study of the effect of vitamin D receptor activation by vitamin D supplementation, 20% of activated genes were related to lipid metabolism (on transcriptomic analysis), many of which were associated with glycerolipid and phospholipid metabolism, while metabolomic analysis found alterations (both increases and decreases) in phospholipids, lysophospholipids, and plasmalogens.7

It is plausible that these pleiotropic effects negate the potential benefits of vitamin D related to enhanced cellular immunity and endothelial function. Our study demonstrated an increase in diacylglycerols, which are structurally related to, and can serve as a precursor to, triglycerides. Prior studies have shown that administration of vitamin D resulted in intracellular accumulation of triglycerides, and in meta-analyses of both adults and children, increased low-density lipoprotein cholesterol.3,8 In mice, vitamin D receptor deficiency protected against hepatosteatosis.7 Interestingly, we also observed decreased androgenic steroids, similar to previous studies demonstrating decreased total testosterone with vitamin D supplementation in women with polycystic ovarian syndrome and who were postmenopausal.9,10 However, whether this decrease was due to decreased hepatic expression of sulfotransferases, which conjugate steroid metabolites, or due to reduced adrenal steroidogenesis, is unknown. Given the known association between decreased sex steroids and worse bone and cardiometabolic outcomes,11 the clinical effect of this off-target outcome of vitamin D supplementation should be studied, especially given the unexpected null results observed in multiple clinical trials.

Limitations of this study include its small sample size and lack of a control group. These limitations make difficult the separation of the effects of vitamin D supplementation from disease activity or other possible confounders such as concurrent therapies, medications, and diet. Additionally, these findings must be contextualized and weighed with the adverse associations with vitamin D deficiency in observational studies. As children with glomerular disease have higher risk of fracture and cardiometabolic disease, further investigation into the metabolic effects of vitamin D supplementation are merited.
