Germline pathogenic variant spectrum and prevalence among colorectal cancer patients undergoing multigene panel testing in Kazakhstan
Authors: Nurlan Baltayev, Saltanat Abdikerim, Georgiy Afonin, Arsen Rasulov, Aigul Zhunussova, Dilyara Kaidarova, Gulnur Zhunussova
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractColorectal cancer (CRC) is a type of malignancy with a hereditary component. In Kazakhstan, the spectrum of germline pathogenic variants (PV) among individuals with CRC remains limited. In this study, multigene panel testing was performed on a Kazakhstani cohort of CRC patients and their relatives to better understand genetic risk factors. The study included 155 CRC patients and 92 healthy relatives. Whole coding regions (> 1700 exons) and flanking noncoding sequences of 94 cancer-associated genes were analyzed using the Illumina TruSight Cancer NGS panel on blood-derived DNA. Results showed that 30 patients (19.4%) carried 31 PVs. Overall, 34.2% of patients had a family history of cancer, including 9.7% who had a family history of CRC. The most frequent germline PVs were in CHEK2 (22.58%) and APC (12.91%), followed by MLH1 (6.46%), MSH2 (6.46%), MSH6 (6.46%), MUTYH (6.46%), and BRCA1 (6.46%). Missense (35.5%) and frameshift (32.3%) variants predominated. A high number of PVs was found in individuals aged 18–44 years. Among overall identified PVs, six were novel: APC c.3405T > G, APC c.419_422delAGAG, PMS1 c.1258delC, MLH1 c.1291_1292delAT, NBN c.877delA, and EPCAM c.184 + 1G > A. Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) is the second most common malignancy in the Republic of Kazakhstan, accounting for 10.6% of all cancer cases, after breast cancer (14.9%), lung cancer (10.5%), and stomach cancer (7.8%). Despite the implementation of population-based screening using fecal occult blood testing (FOBT) since 2011, CRC incidence continues to rise, indicating the persistent impact of underlying etiological factors. The expected CRC incidence for 2026 is 19.0 per 100,000 population. Notably, there is an increasing incidence of CRC among younger individuals, with an average annual increase of approximately 2.3%1. Most studies characterizing germline pathogenic variants (PVs) in CRC have been conducted in European, East Asian, and North American populations, with large-scale genomic and multigene panel analysis2. Data on the prevalence and spectrum of germline PVs in Central Asian populations, including Kazakhstan, remain limited, with only a few small genetic studies reported to date3. The Kazakhstan population exhibits unique ancestral admixture and ethnic heterogeneity, which may influence the distribution and frequency of PVs. Population-specific data are therefore essential for accurate genetic risk assessment, effective genetic counselling, and the development of evidence-based screening guidelines tailored to this region.

Advances in genomics and transcriptomics over the past decade have significantly enhanced the diagnosis and prevention of CRC in individuals with a strong hereditary predisposition. Strategies for early detection in these high-risk individuals rely on understanding genetic heterogeneity and implementing risk stratification across different disease variants. Although hereditary tumor syndromes (HTS) are associated with an elevated risk of early-onset CRC, the majority of such cases (~ 85%) are not linked to germline PVs in known susceptibility genes. Furthermore, the molecular profile of sporadic early-onset tumors differs substantially from that of late-onset CRC4. The molecular and phenotypic heterogeneity of sporadic and hereditary CRC complicates differential diagnosis and the selection of candidate genes for DNA testing during initial evaluation. Known HTS with Mendelian inheritance account for only 4–5% of familial and hereditary CRC cases5. PVs in highly penetrant genes such as TP53 and CDH1, which contribute to familial clustering of other cancers, show variable associations with CRC risk6. PVs in genes such as ATM, CHEK2, MLH3, and EXO1, which are involved in various DNA repair pathways, are recognized as modifiers of CRC susceptibility2,7. Germline pathogenic variants in mismatch repair genes such as MLH1, MSH2, MSH6, PMS2, and EPCAM are strongly associated with Lynch syndrome and hereditary non-polyposis colorectal cancer, while APC and MUTYH are associated with hereditary polyposis syndromes8.

A key strategy to address this complexity is the implementation of multigene testing (MGT), enabling simultaneous analysis of multiple HTS-associated genes, assessment of the prognostic significance of germline variants within syndromic contexts, and optimization of the diagnostic workflow for patients and their relatives9. Families with aggregation of CRC cases exhibit substantial heterogeneity in phenotype, inheritance patterns, and lifetime cancer risk, which presents significant challenges for genetic counseling and targeted screening10. Accurate genetic diagnosis and the identification of PVs markedly enhance the effectiveness of early detection, personalized treatment, and ongoing surveillance of patients and their at-risk family members11. Recognition of HTS directly informs clinical management, including decisions regarding (sub)total colectomy versus segmental resection, selection of systemic therapy regimens, and tailored surveillance strategies, such as colonoscopy schedules and assessment for extraintestinal manifestations12.

The aim of the present study was to characterize the spectrum and prevalence of germline PVs in CRC patients and their apparently healthy relatives, and to assess the diagnostic utility of multigene panel testing.

Results

Clinical Characteristics of the study cohort

The clinical and demographic characteristics of the 155 CRC patients included in the study are summarized in Table 1. Among the patients, 74 (47.7%) were male and 81 (52.3%) were female. The ethnic composition of the cohort included 99 Kazakhs (63.9%), 45 Russians (29.0%), and 11 individuals of other ethnic backgrounds (7.1%). The mean age at diagnosis was 46.1 ± 3.5 years, with 42.6% of patients diagnosed between 18 and 44 years of age. Comparison of clinicopathological features between carriers and non-carriers of PVs revealed a significantly higher proportion of women among PV carriers (80.0% vs. 45.6%, p = 0.001). No statistically significant differences were observed with respect to age at diagnosis, ethnicity, tumor stage, tumor localization, or histological subtype (Table 1).

Among study cohort, 34.2% reported a family history of cancer. Specifically, 9.7% had a family history of CRC, while 24.5% had a family history of other cancers, including esophageal, gastric, duodenal, thyroid, prostate, endometrial, and breast cancers (Fig. 1). Solitary benign polyps were identified in various sites of the colon in 13.6% of patients, with tubular adenoma being the predominant histological type. In the absence of molecular tumor testing, it was not possible to definitively classify these cases as Lynch syndrome or other hereditary CRC subtypes.

Spectrum of rare genetic variants identified by multigene panel testing

Across 155 unrelated CRC patients, a total of 16,945 variants were identified in 89 genes. These included 5,625 missense, 9,380 synonymous, 522 3′-UTR, 892 5′-UTR, 10 frameshift, 5 nonsense, 14 in-frame, 71 splice-site, and 426 intronic variants. Of these, 850 variants were rare, occurring at a frequency of ≤ 1%. The spectrum of rare variants identified in the cohort is illustrated in Fig. 2, which outlines the variant filtering strategy applied in this study. To identify rare variants (850 variants; Fig. 2A), variants with a population frequency > 1% in public population databases (1000 Genomes, gnomAD, ExAC, and TOPMed) were excluded. Because Central Asian populations are underrepresented in these databases, this filtering step was applied conservatively to remove common variants while retaining potentially population-specific variants. Variants absent from global databases were retained and further evaluated according to clinical annotation and ACMG classification criteria.

Prevalence and distribution of germline pathogenic variants

Among the filtered variants, 31 pathogenic or likely PVs were identified in 17 genes across 30 patients, corresponding to a prevalence of 19.4% (30/155) (Supplementary Table S1). All PVs were detected as heterozygous. The most frequent PVs were observed in CHEK2 (22.58%, 7/31) and APC (12.91%, 4/31), followed by MLH1, MSH2, MSH6, MUTYH, and BRCA1 (each 6.46%, 2/31). PVs in the remaining genes were observed in single cases (Fig. 2B). The predominant types of PVs were missense (11, 35.5%) and frameshift (10, 32.3%) variants (Fig. 2C). Three PVs were observed recurrently: a missense variant c.470T > C (n = 5) and a frameshift variant c.1100delC (n = 2) in the CHEK2 gene, as well as a frameshift variant c.4068_4071dupGATT (n = 2) in the MSH6 gene. Carriers of rare PVs were predominantly represented by patients aged 18–44 years (16 patients; 53.3%). Among carriers with a positive family history (n = 8; 7 females and 1 male), a subgroup at high risk for hereditary cancer was identified. First-degree relatives of these patients had a history of CRC or Lynch syndrome–associated malignancies, including breast, endometrial, and gastric cancers. The mean age at diagnosis of this high-risk subgroup was 46.3 years. PVs were detected in the genes APC (c.3405T > G, c.3183_3187delACAAA), MLH1 (c.1291_1292delAT, c.1559-1G > T), MSH2 (c.1147 C > T), MSH6 (c.4068_4071dupGATT), CHEK2 (c.470T > C), and NSD1 (c.5622 + 2 T > G).

To better illustrate the clinical importance of identified PVs, detected variants were interpreted based on known hereditary colorectal cancer syndromes. Variants found in mismatch repair genes (MLH1, MSH2, and MSH6) and EPCAM were considered consistent with Lynch syndrome (LS). Variants in APC and MUTYH were interpreted within the context of polyposis syndromes, including familial adenomatous polyposis (FAP) and MUTYH-associated polyposis (MAP). Variants found in other genes (e.g., CHEK2, BRCA1, TP53, NBN, WRN) were classified as variants in cancer susceptibility genes not traditionally associated with CRC risk.

However, accurately classifying all patients into LS, hereditary non-LS CRC, or sporadic CRC was not possible because tumor-based molecular testing (such as microsatellite instability testing or mismatch repair protein immunohistochemistry) was not available for all cases. As a result, variant interpretation in this study relied mainly on germline genetic findings and family history data.

Distribution of pathogenic variants across protein domains

To visualize the positional distribution of PVs and their relation to functional protein domains, lollipop plots were generated for genes with the highest variant burden (CHEK2, BRCA1, MLH1, MSH2, MSH6, MUTYH, and APC). Overall, PVs clustered within key functional domains, highlighting potential hotspots for functional disruption (Fig. 3). In CHEK2, p.Ile157Thr clustered in the Forkhead-associated (FHA) domain, while p.Thr367Metfs*15 affected the kinase domain. BRCA1 PV (c.5467 + 1G > A) localized to the BRCT domain, and MLH1 c.1559-1G > T disrupted the C-terminal mismatch repair domain. MSH2 variants p.Arg383Ter and p.Leu330Pro occurred in the MutS domain III. MUTYH PVs (p.Gly368Asp, p.Tyr151Cys) mapped to the NUDIX and HhH-GPD domains. APC nonsense and frameshift variants (p.Tyr1135Ter, p.Lys1061_Gln1062insTer) affected the armadillo repeat and β-catenin–binding domains.

Interestingly, all CHEK2 variants identified in this cohort were detected in patients of Russian ancestry, while none were observed among Kazakh individuals. This observation may reflect previously described founder effects of certain CHEK2 variants in Eastern and Northern European populations. However, given the relatively small sample size of the present study, this finding should be interpreted cautiously and requires confirmation in larger population-based cohorts.

Identification of novel pathogenic variants

Six novel PVs (APC c.3405T > G, APC c.419_422delAGAG, PMS1 c.1258delC, MLH1 c.1291_1292delAT, NBN c.877delA, and EPCAM c.184 + 1G > A) were identified in this study (Supplementary Table S1). These novel variants were predominant in young patients with early-onset CRC and included alterations affecting tumor suppressor genes and mismatch repair pathways. One female patient (58 years old) carried the nonsense variant APC c.3405T > G. She had a history of rectosigmoid colon cancer and a family history of CRC in her mother and breast cancer in her older sister (Supplementary Table S2). Colonoscopy of her son, who carried the same variant, revealed attenuated polyposis (Supplementary Table S3). Another APC variant, c.419_422delAGAG (frameshift), was identified in a 32-year-old woman with sigmoid colon cancer and no family history. A novel MLH1 variant (c.1291_1292delAT) was found in a 46-year-old Kazakh patient with familial transverse colon cancer; her father had rectal cancer, her older brother had duodenal cancer, and her younger brother had transverse colon cancer. Additionally, three novel PVs including PMS1 c.1258delC, NBN c.877delA, and EPCAM c.184 + 1G > A (splice donor), were detected in young Kazakh patients with sporadic rectal cancer.

Genetic characteristics of patients’ relatives

Targeted DNA sequencing was performed on 92 apparently healthy first-, second-, or third-degree relatives of 47 CRC patients, using the same protocol applied to patient samples. The cohort included 47 women and 45 men. PVs were identified in 21 of 92 relatives (22.8%), with a mean age of 31.7 years among carriers. The mutational profiles in these relatives were concordant with the variants detected in the corresponding CRC patients, both in terms of affected genes and specific DNA sequences (Supplementary Table S2). In contrast, PVs identified in CRC patients were absent in 26 of their apparently healthy relatives. Notably, in one case, a 39-year-old Kazakh male with rectal cancer and a strong family history of cancer carried no PVs. However, his 65-year-old mother was found to harbor a CHEK2 c.1229delC PV.

Discussion

In this study, we focus on the 94 cancer-associated genes (Supplementary Table S4). A total of 31 PVs were identified in 17 genes among 30 patients. Notably, highly frequent PVs were observed in the CHEK2 and APC genes, followed by MLH1, MSH2, MSH6, MUTYH, and BRCA1. PVs c.470T > C (p.Ile157Thr) and c.1100delC (p.Thr367MetfsTer15) in the CHEK2 gene were only found in people of Russian ancestry. CHEK2 pathogenic variants were not observed among Kazakh patients in this cohort (Supplementary Table S1). Previous studies showed that these PVs not only poses a moderate risk of CRC but also poses a risk for developing breast, prostate, and thyroid cancers13. After the CHEK2 gene, the second highest PVs were observed in APC, included the variants c.3405T > G, c.3183_3187delACAAA, c.419_422delAGAG and c.147_150delACAA. Result of current work showed that carriers of rare PVs were predominantly represented by patients with age of 18–44 years. The 34% of CRC patients have a disease family history. High prevalence of pathogenic germline variants, particularly among early-onset CRC patients, underscoring both population-specific genetic architecture and the limitations of phenotype-based testing strategies.

The high frequency of PVs (19.4%) in our cohort underscores the need for genetic screening for HTS in CRC patients and their relatives. This approach involves testing a core set of genes reliably associated with CRC, supplemented by additional genes whose variants are clinically significant and relatively common14. Our prospective cohort study demonstrated that multigene panel testing, including genes associated with CRC, provides significant clinical benefits for both patients and their relatives15.

The pathogenic variants identified in this study fall into several categories with different levels of established association with colorectal cancer. First, variants were identified in well-established colorectal cancer predisposition genes, particularly mismatch repair genes (MLH1, MSH2, MSH6) associated with Lynch syndrome. Second, variants were detected in genes associated with polyposis syndromes, including APC and MUTYH. Finally, variants were identified in several cancer susceptibility genes involved in DNA damage response or tumor suppression pathways (such as CHEK2, BRCA1, TP53, WRN, and NBN). Although variants in these genes have been reported in patients with CRC, their direct contribution to CRC risk remains uncertain and requires further investigation. Therefore, their presence in this cohort should be interpreted primarily as part of a broad multigene screening approach rather than definitive CRC susceptibility genes. In comparison, Pearlman et al.4 and DeRycke et al.16 reported lower prevalence of PVs in these genes, at 3% and 6%, respectively. Most PVs in this subgroup were found in genes that involved in DDR pathways (7.75% of the entire group). Liao et al. reported a 5.7% frequency of germline PVs in DDR genes among Chinese CRC patients17, while Mauri et al., studying predominantly early-onset CRC patients of Italian ethnicity, found a prevalence of 3.9%18.

The absence of CHEK2 variants in Kazakh patients and their presence among individuals of Russian ancestry in our cohort may reflect historical population structure and possible founder effects rather than differences in CRC risk between ethnic groups. The most frequently observed variants in our cohort were detected in the CHEK2 gene, including the recurrent missense variant c.470T > C (p.Ile157Thr) and the frameshift variant c.1100delC. These variants have been widely reported in European populations and have been associated with increased susceptibility to several cancers, particularly breast cancer19–25. However, recent large cohort studies and updated clinical guidelines have questioned the strength of the association between CHEK2 variants and colorectal cancer risk. For example, recent analyses and updated NCCN recommendations no longer consider CHEK2 to be a colorectal cancer susceptibility gene. In our cohort, CHEK2 variants were detected both in CRC patients and in apparently healthy relatives, suggesting that their presence alone does not establish a causal relationship with CRC. Therefore, the variants identified in CHEK2 should be interpreted cautiously and may represent moderate-penetrance or incidental findings rather than direct drivers of colorectal cancer susceptibility. Since most PVs were detected in CHEK2 and all occurred in women, this contributed to the observed sex distribution among pathogenic variant carriers in the study cohort.

The prevalence of germline PVs in MMR genes among CRC patients has been reported to range from 5.1% to 10.5% in European cohorts8,26, which is aligned with our 5.8% prevalence of MMR gene mutations results. In our cohort, a novel MLH1 frameshift variant (c.1291_1292delAT) was detected in a 46-year-old patient with transverse colon cancer and a family history consistent with LS (per Bethesda criteria). Another patient, 54 years old, with ascending colon cancer and a family history of CRC (her sister), harbored MLH1 c.1559-1G > T, which disrupts splicing. This variant has been previously reported in HNPCC cohorts from Germany27, Romania28, and South Korea29.

When screening relatives of CRC patients, identical PVs were found, which have a high-risk factor for LS and CRC30–33(Supplementary Table S2). Among the relatives of a patient carrying the MLH1 c.1291_1292delAT variant, 2 of 6 underwent colonoscopy with no pathological findings; the procedure was postponed for the remaining 4 relatives due to their unwillingness (Supplementary Table S3). A 39-year-old female patient with rectal cancer carried a nonsense PV in MSH2 (c.1147 C > T) and a CHEK2 frameshift PV (c.1100delC), both implicated in DNA repair and cell cycle regulation. She had a positive family history (her mother with endometrial cancer). Her son was found to carry the same PVs in MSH2 and CHEK2 and he was under observation for further clinical findings. In contrast, a 36-year-old patient with sigmoid colon cancer carrying MSH2 c.989T > C represented a sporadic case (Supplementary Table S2). Identical PVs in MSH6 (c.4068_4071dupGATT) were found in two patients with rectal cancer. One patient (62-year-old) had synchronous rectal and ovarian cancer, with no PVs detected in her first- or second-degree relatives. The second patient (55-year-old) had a family history consistent with LS (father with stomach cancer, brother with thyroid cancer), still, no PVs were identified in her first- or third-degree relatives (Supplementary Table S2). In our study, a novel splice donor PV in EPCAM (c.184 + 1G > A) was detected in a 39-year-old patient with rectal cancer and no relevant family history. This PV was absent in the patient’s children, although the son carried an MSH6 PV (c.4068_4071dupGATT) causing a frameshift. These findings further support the clinical utility of cascade genetic testing in identifying asymptomatic carriers and enabling risk-adapted surveillance strategies in families with LS.

In this study we found three genes related to polyposis syndrome APC (2.58%), MUTYH (1.29%), and SMAD4 (0.65%). Germline PVs in the APC gene are causative for familial adenomatous polyposis (FAP). The APC protein functions as a tumor suppressor and Wnt signaling antagonist, regulating transcriptional activation, cell migration, and apoptosis34. The prevalence of APC germline variants varies considerably across patient populations, with higher frequencies observed in individuals with polyposis-related CRC, including FAP, and in patients with early-onset CRC. The frequency of APC variants in our study (2.58%) aligns previously published data26. In our cohort, four PVs in APC were identified in four patients. Two of these variants, c.3183_3187delACAAA and c.147_150delACAA, have been previously reported35,36. The c.3183_3187delACAAA variant has been described as a founder mutation in the Spanish population35 and is also prevalent in Chinese FAP patients36. The other two variants, c.3405T > G and c.419_422delAGAG, are novel and absent from public databases, including 1000 Genomes, dbSNP, and ExAC. The identification of novel APC variants further emphasizes the importance of population-specific sequencing efforts, as such variants may remain undetected in global reference databases. In our study, a missense variant c.1082G > A (p.Arg361His) in SMAD4 was identified in a 48-year-old female patient with sporadic descending colon cancer. This variant, located in exon 8 of the gene, results from a G-to-A substitution at nucleotide position 1082. It has previously been reported by Aretz et al.37 and Cao et al.38 in patients with juvenile polyposis syndrome (JPS), hereditary hemorrhagic telangiectasia (HHT), and early-onset CRC. Similar findings were described by Vautier et al. in large cohorts of JPS and HHT patients39. PVs in the MUTYH gene cause autosomal recessive MUTYH-associated polyposis. In our study, two pathogenic missense variants, c.1103G > A and c.452 A > G, were identified in two Russian patients with rectal cancer. These variants, previously described by D’Agostino et al.40 and Komine et al.41, have been shown to impair the DNA-binding and glycosylase activity of the MUTYH protein. No PVs were detected in the 34-year-old daughter of a patient carrying the MUTYH c.452 A > G variant. According to published data, these variants are relatively common in European populations but rare in Southeast Asian cohorts, as reported by Park et al.42 and Aretz et al.43. Moreover, these PVs exhibit a founder effect in certain Northern European populations44,45, and Reznick et al. have identified them in patients from Israel46.

Although this study provides the first comprehensive overview of germline PVs in CRC patients from Kazakhstan, several limitations should be acknowledged. The small sample size and ethnic diversity may influence the detection of rare variants. The absence of matched tumor or polyp sequencing limited the assessment of somatic alterations, loss of heterozygosity, and genotype–phenotype correlations, while incomplete cascade testing restricted penetrance evaluation. Future research with larger, population-based cohorts will be crucial to improve risk assessment and develop population-specific screening strategies. Tumor molecular testing, such as microsatellite instability analysis or mismatch repair immunohistochemistry, was not performed in all patients, limiting definitive clinical classification of hereditary syndromes. The relatively high prevalence of germline PVs observed in this cohort raises the question of whether certain variants may be more common in the Kazakhstan population. Recent population-scale genomic analyses of Kazakh individuals have started to define the genetic structure of this group. For example, whole-genome sequencing studies have identified unique patterns of genetic admixture and variant distribution in Kazakh populations compared to those in European and East Asian populations47. However, available datasets are still limited in size and do not provide enough information to accurately estimate carrier frequencies for rare pathogenic variants associated with hereditary cancer syndromes. Larger population-based genomic studies are necessary to determine whether the PV frequencies seen in this study reflect population-specific enrichment or if they are due to sampling effects.

Conclusions

This study provides one of the first population-specific assessments of germline pathogenic variants among CRC patients in Kazakhstan using multigene panel testing. A total of 31 germline PVs were identified in 19.4% of cases. While family history alone is not a reliable predictor of PVs, it remains critical for identifying candidates for genetic screening. We identified six novel PVs and found that young patients (18–44 years) accounted for 53.3% of all detected PVs, highlighting the importance of early screening in this population. Rare PVs were also detected in 21.7% of patients’ relatives, underscoring the value of familial testing. The most common variants involved genes in tumor suppression (APC), cell cycle control and DNA repair (CHEK2, BRCA1), and mismatch repair (MLH1, MSH2, MSH6, and MUTYH). Based on the genes identified in this cohort and current literature, multigene panel testing that includes established colorectal cancer predisposition genes, particularly mismatch repair genes, APC, and MUTYH, may be beneficial for genetic evaluation of CRC patients in Kazakhstan. However, broader panels that include additional cancer susceptibility genes should be interpreted cautiously and in accordance with international clinical guidelines. Finally, establishing a national registry of families with hereditary CRC syndromes would enable long-term follow-up, facilitate systematic evaluation of NGS-based diagnostics, and improve personalized clinical care.

Methods

Study cohort

The study included 247 participants, comprising 155 unrelated patients with CRC and 92 of their apparently healthy relatives. Patient ages ranged from 21 to 78 years. Patients were mainly recruited based on early age at diagnosis (< 50 years) and/or a family history of colorectal or related cancers. All eligible patients treated at the Almaty Oncology Center (AOC) during the study period (2021–2023) who met these criteria were invited to participate. Healthy relatives without a diagnosis of CRC, other colorectal conditions, or malignancy at study enrollment were recruited for biological sample collection at the AOC. There were no cases that met the diagnostic criteria for Lynch syndrome (Amsterdam II and Bethesda) or familial adenomatous polyposis (FAP). Samples from minors were obtained with their legal guardians’ consent. The study cohort is shown in Fig. 4. Inclusion criteria for the study were primary tumor localization in the colon or rectum and availability of complete clinical and histopathological data.

Isolation of genomic DNA

Genomic DNA was extracted from peripheral blood using the ReliaPrep Blood gDNA Miniprep System (Promega, USA) following the manufacturer’s protocol. The quantity and quality of the isolated DNA were assessed using a NanoDrop One spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific) and a Qubit fluorimeter (Invitrogen). Only DNA samples with a purity ratio (A260/A280) of 1.7–1.8 were used for subsequent analyses.

Preparation of DNA libraries for massively parallel sequencing (NGS)

DNA libraries were prepared using the TruSight Rapid Capture Kit (Illumina, USA). Library concentrations were measured with a Qubit 2.0 Fluorometer (Invitrogen, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA), and quality was assessed using an Agilent Bioanalyzer 2100 (Agilent Technologies, USA). Targeted sequencing was performed on the MiSeq platform (Illumina) covering over 1,700 exons and flanking intronic regions (50 bp) of 94 cancer-associated genes, representing approximately 255 kb of the human genome. Libraries with a molarity of 4 nM were cluster-generated on flow cells, and 600 paired-end sequencing cycles were carried out using the MiSeq Reagent Kit v3.

Bioinformatics data processing

Next-generation sequencing data were analyzed using MiSeq Reporter v.3.0 (Illumina). Variants covered by ≤ 50 reads were excluded. Reads were aligned to the hg19 reference genome (GRCh37.p13) using the Burrows–Wheeler Aligner (BWA) algorithm. Variant calling for targeted regions was performed with the Genome Analysis Toolkit (GATK, Broad Institute, USA). Resulting sequences in VCF format were further analyzed using Variant Studio, SnpEff, SnpSift, and OpenCravat. A Q-score threshold of 30 (error rate 1:1000) was applied to ensure sequencing quality. Additional filtering criteria included: total read depth > 50×, alternative allele read depth > 20×, and a quality score > 100. Genetic variants were annotated in accordance with Human Genome Variation Society (HGVS) nomenclature. Identified variants were interpreted using NCBI ClinVar, the Single Nucleotide Polymorphism Database (dbSNP), and the Leiden Open Variation Database (LOVD), as well as findings reported in published studies. Population frequencies of the variants were assessed using the 1000 Genomes (1000G), gnomAD, ExAC, and TOPMed databases. The potential functional impact of missense variants was evaluated using in silico prediction tools (SIFT and PolyPhen-2). These tools served as supporting evidence within the ACMG/AMP variant classification framework and were not used independently to determine pathogenicity. Final variant interpretation was conducted according to ACMG guidelines, incorporating multiple evidence types, including variant type, predicted functional effect, population frequency, and available clinical database annotations48. In this study, variants were considered “novel” if they were absent from major population databases and the literature at the time of analysis. However, some of these variants may subsequently appear in clinical databases such as ClinVar as additional sequencing data become available.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using the chi-square test for categorical variables. These tests were applied to evaluate phenotypic and clinicopathological characteristics. Results were considered statistically significant at p < 0.05, and all tests were two-sided. Data analysis was conducted using R software version 4.4.1 (RStudio, PBC, Boston, USA).
