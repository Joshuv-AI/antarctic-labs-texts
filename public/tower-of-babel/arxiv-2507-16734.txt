Gaussian sequence model: sample complexities of testing, estimation and LFHT
Zeyu Jia, Yury Polyanskiy

Abstract
We study the Gaussian sequence model, i.e. X ∼ 𝒩 ⁡ ( 𝜽 , I ∞ ) X\sim\mathcal{N}({\boldsymbol{\theta}},I_{\infty}) , where 𝜽 ∈ Γ ⊂ ℓ 2 {\boldsymbol{\theta}}\in\Gamma\subset\ell_{2} is assumed to be convex and compact. We show that goodness-of-fit testing sample complexity is lower bounded by the square-root of the estimation complexity, whenever Γ \Gamma is orthosymmetric. We show that the lower bound is tight when Γ \Gamma is also quadratically convex, thus significantly extending validity of the testing-estimation relationship from [ GP24 ] . Using similar methods, we also completely characterize likelihood-free hypothesis testing (LFHT) complexity for ℓ p \ell_{p} -bodies, discovering new types of tradeoff between the numbers of simulation and observation samples.



1
Introduction

Two flagship problems in non-parametric statistics are establishing minimax rates (or, equivalently, minimax sample complexities) of density estimation and testing. In the case of density estimation, statistician is given an apriori fixed large class
\Gamma
of probability distributions and
n
iid samples
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
from
P\in\Gamma
and is tasked with producing an estimate
\hat{P}
of the distribution such that
\mathbb{E}[d(P,\hat{P})]\leq\varepsilon
, where
d
is a distance and
\varepsilon
is the target accuracy. The minimal number
n
of samples required for the worst-case choice of
P
is the sample complexity
n_{\mathrm{est}}(\Gamma,\varepsilon)
. For example, if
\Gamma
consists of all
\beta
-smooth densities on a compact set in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and if accuracy metric is
d=\|\cdot\|_{2}
(the
\ell_{2}
distance),
then the classical work of Ibragimov and Khasminskii
[
IKm83
]
showed that
n_{\mathrm{est}}(\Gamma,\varepsilon)\asymp\varepsilon^{-(2\beta+d)/\beta}
upto universal constants, cf.
[
Tsy08
]
.

In the case of testing (or goodness-of-fit), statistician is given an apriori fixed large class
\Gamma
of probability distributions, a member
P_{0}\in\Gamma
and
n
iid samples
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
from some
P\in\Gamma
, and is tasked with testing hypothesis
P=P_{0}
against
d(P,P_{0})>\varepsilon
with a fixed small probability of error under either of the alternatives. The minimal number of samples needed for accomplishing this task (worst case over
P
and
P_{0}
) is the complexity of testing
n_{\mathrm{gof}}(\Gamma,\varepsilon)
. This way of looking at non-parametric testing, as well as many foundational results, was proposed by Ingster
[
Ing82
,
Ing86
,
Ing87
]
. In particular, for the Lipschitz class and
\mathrm{TV}
metric his result shows that
n_{\mathrm{gof}}(\Gamma,\varepsilon)\asymp\varepsilon^{-(d/2+2)}
. The fact that testing can be done with significantly fewer samples was both surprising and impactful for the development of theoretical statistics in 20th century.

Despite the similarities of two problems, our level of understanding of
n_{\mathrm{est}}
and
n_{\mathrm{gof}}
is dramatically different. Specifically, the work of LeCam
[
LC12
]
, Birge
[
Bir83
,
Bir86
]
, Yatracos
[
Yat85
]
, Yang and Barron
[
YB99
]
established a direct characterization of
n_{\mathrm{est}}
in terms of metric entropy of the class
\Gamma
, thus reducing the problem to that of approximation theory. At the same time, while several powerful tools
[
IS03
]
were developed for bounding
n_{\mathrm{gof}}
, its evaluation for each class
\Gamma
is largely still ad hoc. Thus, our longer term goal is to obtain metric entropy type characterization of
n_{\mathrm{gof}}
. This work is a step in this direction: by establishing general inequalities relating
n_{\mathrm{gof}}
and
n_{\mathrm{est}}
, implicitly we also obtain entropic bounds on
n_{\mathrm{gof}}
.

The origin of these kind of relations is the work
[
GP24
]
, which studied yet another statistical problem of
likelihood-free hypothesis testing
(LFHT), see (
10
) below, which “interpolates” between estimation and testing. By inspecting the minimax region for LFHT at two endpoints
[
GP24
]
observed that for rather different non-parametric types of models (smooth densities, discrete distributions, Gaussian sequence model over ellipsoids) one has a general relationship

\frac{n_{\mathrm{est}}(\Gamma,\varepsilon)}{\varepsilon^{2}}\asymp n_{\mathrm{gof}}^{2}(\Gamma,\varepsilon)\,.

(1)

This relation can informally be “derived” as follows. Up to precision
\varepsilon
the non-parametric model
\Gamma
can be thought of as a model of finite dimension
D(\Gamma,\varepsilon)
, rigorously defined below as a Kolmogorov dimension. Then, classical
parametric
estimation and testing rates allow us to guess

n_{\mathrm{est}}(\Gamma,\varepsilon)\asymp\frac{D(\Gamma,\varepsilon)}{\varepsilon^{2}},\qquad n_{\mathrm{gof}}(\Gamma,\varepsilon)\asymp\frac{\sqrt{D(\Gamma,\varepsilon)}}{\varepsilon^{2}}\,,

(2)

which clearly imply (
1
). While this intuition supports the general validity of (
1
), unfortunately neither (
2
) nor (
1
) hold generally. Indeed, one can easily find counter-example in
[
Bar02
]
, see (
4
) below. Nevertheless, in this work we show that both the
\lesssim
and the
\gtrsim
of (
1
) can hold under rather general assumptions. In particular, both of them hold for all
\ell_{p}
-bodies with
p\geq 2
.

This paper is written solely in the context of
Gaussian sequence model
, for which
\Gamma
consists of infinite-dimensional Gaussian densities
\mathcal{N}(\theta,I_{\infty})
with mean
\theta
constrained to belong to a compact convex set in
\ell_{2}
. We will abuse notation and simply write
\theta\in\Gamma
identifying densities with their means. Such models are both simplest to study and are universal: any non-parametric class
\Gamma
of densities can be shown to be Le Cam equivalent (in the limit of
n\to\infty
) to a certain Gaussian sequence model, cf.
[
Nus96
,
BL96
]
.

1.1
Our Contribution

Our contributions are two-fold. First, we derive a collection of results bounding
n_{\mathrm{gof}}
in terms of
n_{\mathrm{est}}
. Second, we also completely characterize LFHT region of general
\ell_{p}
-bodies.

More specifically, for any convex, compact and orthosymmetric set
\Gamma
(see definitions below), we show in
Corollary
2
that the minimax sample complexity
n_{\mathrm{gof}}(\Gamma,\varepsilon)
of goodness-of-fit testing and the minimax sample complexity
n_{\mathrm{est}}(\Gamma,\varepsilon)
of density estimation satisfy

n_{\mathrm{est}}(\Gamma,\varepsilon)\lesssim\frac{n_{\mathrm{gof}}^{2}(\Gamma,\varepsilon)}{\varepsilon^{2}}\cdot\mathrm{polylog}(D(\Gamma,\varepsilon))\,,

(3)

where
D(\Gamma,\varepsilon)
is the Kolmogorov dimension of
\Gamma
(see also Prop.
6
).
Hence, under rather general conditions half of the relationship (
1
) holds up to polylog factors.

Are there any counter-examples to (
1
)? The answer is positive as in fact already implicitly shown in
[
Bar02
]
. Specifically, let us define the following

\Gamma=\left\{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}=(\theta_{1:\infty}):\sum_{i\geq 1}i\cdot|\theta_{i}|\leq 1\right\},

(4)

which is an example of a more general class of
\ell_{p}
-bodies
[
Bar02
]
with
p=1
.
For this specific class, we find out in
Proposition
2
and
Proposition
3
that

n_{\mathrm{gof}}(\Gamma,\varepsilon)=\tilde{\Theta}\left(\varepsilon^{-\frac{12}{5}}\right)\quad\text{and}\quad n_{\mathrm{est}}(\Gamma,\varepsilon)=\tilde{\Theta}\left(\varepsilon^{-\frac{8}{3}}\right),

thus clearly violating (
1
) and showing that one can indeed have
n_{\mathrm{est}}\ll n_{\mathrm{gof}}^{2}/\varepsilon^{2}
.

Given this counter-example, can we find a general class of convex sets for which validity of (
1
) can be guaranteed? It turns out that the answer is positive (upto polylogarithmic factors, at least). This is the content of Theorem
3.2
, which shows that if in addition to orthosymmetry one requires quadratic convexity
[
DLM90
]
, we obtain

\frac{n_{\mathrm{est}}(\Gamma,\varepsilon)}{\varepsilon^{2}}\gtrsim n_{\mathrm{gof}}^{2}(\Gamma,\varepsilon)\,.

Therefore, together with (
3
) for such sets we indeed establish (
1
). In particular, this latter holds for all
\ell_{p}
-bodies with
p\geq 2
.

Our final set of results is in establishing minimax rates (regions) for the LFHT problem. Recall that in
[
GP24
]
, it was shown that the optimal testing region of LFHT for Gaussian sequence model over an ellipsoid
\Gamma
satisfies

\left\{m\geq\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}},\quad n\gtrsim n_{\mathrm{gof}}(\Gamma,\varepsilon),\quad\text{and}\quad mn\gtrsim n_{\mathrm{gof}}^{2}(\Gamma,\varepsilon)\right\}.

(5)

It is natural to ask whether this relation is in some sense universal. In this work, we show that for
\Gamma
, which are orthosymmetric, convex and quadratically convex (e.g.
\ell_{p}
-bodies with
p\geq 2
), the LFHT region remains the same. In particular, this is true for
\ell_{p}
bodies with
p\geq 2
. For
p<2
, the general form of the LFHT region is given in terms of an “effective dimension”
d(\Gamma,n,\varepsilon)
depending on
n
. Specifically, we show that the testing region is given by

\left\{m\geq\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}},\quad n\gtrsim\frac{\sqrt{d(\Gamma,n,\varepsilon)}}{\varepsilon^{2}},\quad\text{and}\quad mn\gtrsim\frac{d(\Gamma,n,\varepsilon)}{\varepsilon^{4}}\right\}.

For example, for the set
Eq. (
4
)
, we have
d(\Gamma,n,\varepsilon)\asymp\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}\cdot\varepsilon^{2}}
and the LFHT region is given by

\left\{m\geq\varepsilon^{-2},n\gtrsim\varepsilon^{-\frac{12}{5}},\quad\text{and}\quad m\cdot n^{\frac{3}{2}}\gtrsim\varepsilon^{-6}\right\}.

In particular, this shows that the tradeoff (
5
) does not extend to
\ell_{p}
-bodies with
p<2
.

1.2
Related Works

We review some related literatures in this section.

Non-parametric Density Estimation:
As we already discussed in the introduction, there has been a long line of work studying the density estimation. For fairly general non-parametric classes and distances between distributions Le Cam
[
LC12
]
(also in
[
vdV02
]
) and Birge
[
Bir83
,
Bir86
]
characterized the minimax rate in terms of the (local) Hellinger metric entropy. Other general estimators were proposed by Yang-Barron
[
YB99
]
, Yatracos
[
Yat85
]
and others. In the context of estimating smooth densities, the study of kernel density estimators is a rich subject
[
Tsy08
, Chapter 1]
, as is the method of wavelet-based techniques
[
DJKP96
]
. In the context of Gaussian sequence models, density estimation corresponds to parameter (mean) estimation, with state of the art beautifully summarized in
[
Joh19
]
.

Gaussian sequence model:
In the context of Gaussian sequence model density estimation is equivalent to parameter (
\theta
) estimation. This question received significant attention, see
[
JN20
, Chapter 4]
. We specifically mention pioneering work of Pinsker
[
Pin80
]
, who demonstrated optimality of linear estimators for the case of certain ellipsoids, and
[
DLM90
]
, who significantly extended this idea by showing optimality (upto a universal factor) of projection estimators for all quadratically convex sets – a notion which also found application in stochastic optimization
[
CLD19
]
.

Goodness-of-Fit Testing:
The sample complexity of goodness-of-fit testing has been pioneered by the already mentioned works of Ingster, whose book
[
IS12
]
surveys many of the classical developments. Subsequently,
[
LS99
]
obtained tight goodness-of-fit testing rate for Besov bodies
B_{s,p,q}(R)
where
p\in(0,2)
.

For Gaussian sequence model, we mention results of
[
Erm91
]
and, very relevant for our work, a comprehensive paper of Baraud
[
Bar02
]
. Specifically, for
\ell_{p}
bodies
\{\theta_{1:D}:\sum_{i=1}^{D}|\theta_{i}|^{p}/a_{i}^{p}\leq 1\}
where
a_{1}\geq a_{2}\geq\cdots\geq a_{D}
, they proposed the following dimension

\rho(n)=\sup_{d\in[D]}[(\sqrt{d}/n^{2}\wedge a_{d}^{2}\lceil\sqrt{d}\rceil^{1-2/p})],

and they show that the minimax sample complexity of goodness-of-fit testing is the smallest
n
such that
\rho(n)\leq\varepsilon
. More recently,
[
WW20
]
refined sample complexity to make it depend on a specific (rather than worst-case) choice of the mean in the null-hypothesis.

A large amount of work has also been done on the topic in computer science literature under different names of
identity testing
or
uniformity testing
, see
[
GGR98
,
BFR+00
,
Pan08
,
VV17
,
VV20
,
CW21
,
CHL+23
]
. A recent lower bound for robust testing was proposed in
[
DKS17
]
.
[
CCK+21
,
DKP23
]
proposed minimax optimal testing scheme for cases with unknown variances. An excellent monograph
[
Can22
]
surveys this line of work.

Likelihood Free Hypothesis Testing:
The form of likelihood free hypothesis testing was firstly introduced in
[
Gut89
,
Ziv88
]
. They studied the problem in fixed finite alphabet.
[
ZTM19
]
showed that the testing scheme introduced in
[
Gut89
]
is second-order optimal. This problem is extended into sequential and distributional setting in
[
HW20
,
HZT20
,
HTK21
,
BGiF22
]
. In this work, we will focus on the setting introduced in
[
GP24
]
.

2
Preliminaries and notation

We review some basic concepts of the Gaussian sequence model
[
IS12
]
, goodness-of-fit testing
[
IS12
]
, density estimation
[
Joh19
]
and likelihood-free hypothesis testing
[
GP24
]
in this section.

Gaussian Sequence Model.
We focus on unit-variance multivariate Gaussian location model, which is specified by an integer
D\in[1,\infty]
and a subset
\Gamma\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{D}
, so that the model consists of all distributions
\mathcal{P}(\Gamma)\triangleq\{\mathcal{N}({\boldsymbol{\theta}},I_{D}):{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\in\Gamma\}
. When
D=\infty
we also refer to this model as Gaussian sequence model. In the following, we often use subset
\Gamma
to denote the model
\mathcal{P}(\Gamma)
itself.

Goodness of Fit Testing.
Given an integer
D\in[1,\infty]
, we conduct the goodness of fit testing in
\mathbb{R}^{D}
. In this task, the statistician is given an class of parameters
\Gamma\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{D}
, and has the knowledge that the true parameter of the model
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\in\Gamma
. However,
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
is unknown and the statistician can only obtain information of
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
through
n
samples
\mathbf{X}=(\mathbf{X}_{1:n})\sim\mathbb{P}_{\mathtt{X}}^{\otimes n}
where
\mathbb{P}_{\mathtt{X}}=\mathcal{N}({\boldsymbol{\theta}},I_{D})
. The statistician conducts the following hypothesis testing problem

H_{0}:{\boldsymbol{\theta}}=\mathbf{0}\quad\text{versus}\quad H_{1}:\|{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\|_{2}\geq\varepsilon,

(6)

i.e., based on the samples in
\mathbf{X}
, the statistician makes choice
\psi(\mathbf{X})\in\{0,1\}
, where
\psi(\mathbf{X})=0
denotes the statistician accepts hypothesis
H_{0}
and
\psi(\mathbf{X})=1
denotes the statistician rejects
H_{0}
. We focus on the smallest number of
n
such that

\max_{i\in\{0,1\}}\sup_{P\in H_{i}}\mathbf{P}(\psi(\mathbf{X})\neq i)\leq\frac{1}{4},

(7)

where
\sup_{P\in H_{0}}
denotes the case
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}=\mathbf{0}
, and
\sup_{P\in H_{1}}
denotes the supreme over
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
with
\|{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\|_{2}\geq\varepsilon
. We let
n_{\mathrm{gof}}(\Gamma,\varepsilon)
to denote the smallest number of samples such that
Eq. (
7
)
holds.

Density Estimation.
Given an integer
D\in[1,\infty]
, we conduct the density estimation task in
\mathbb{R}^{D}
. In this task, the statistician is given an class of parameters
\Gamma\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{D}
, and has the knowledge that the parameter of the groundtruth distribution
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\in\Gamma
. However,
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
is unknown and the statistician can only obtain information of
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
through
n
samples
\mathbf{X}=(\mathbf{X}_{1:n})\sim\mathbb{P}_{\mathtt{X}}^{\otimes n}
where
\mathbb{P}_{\mathtt{X}}=\mathcal{N}({\boldsymbol{\theta}},I_{D})
. Based on the samples in
\mathbf{X}
, the statistician proposes an estimator
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{X})\in\mathbb{R}^{D}
. We focus on the smallest number of samples
n
such that there exists an estimator which achieves expected
\ell_{2}
estimation error no more than
\varepsilon^{2}
for any distribution in the distribution class, i.e.

\inf_{\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}\sup_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\in\Gamma}\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{X})-{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\right\|_{2}^{2}\right]\leq\varepsilon^{2}.

(8)

We use
n_{\mathrm{est}}(\Gamma,\varepsilon)
to denote the smallest number of samples such that
Eq. (
8
)
holds.

Likelihood Free Hypothesis Testing.
The task of likelihood free hypothesis testing was first introduced in
[
GP24
]
. The statistician conducts the testing in
\mathbb{R}^{D}
, and the statistician is given the class
\Gamma
of the Gaussian sequence model. Suppose there are two artificial densities
\mathbb{P}_{\mathtt{X}}=\mathcal{N}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{1},I_{D})
,
\mathbb{P}_{\mathtt{Y}}=({\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{2},I_{D})
and a true density
\mathbb{P}_{\mathtt{Z}}=\mathcal{N}({\boldsymbol{\theta}},I_{D})
where
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{1},{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{2},{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\in\Gamma
are unknown to the statistician. After collecting
n
samples from
\mathbb{P}_{\mathtt{X}}
and
\mathbb{P}_{\mathtt{Y}}
each to form artificial datasets
\mathbf{X}\sim\mathbb{P}_{\mathtt{X}}^{\otimes n}
and
\mathbf{Y}\sim\mathbb{P}_{\mathtt{Y}}^{\otimes n}
, and collecting
m
samples from
\mathbb{P}_{\mathtt{Z}}
to form the ground truth dataset
\mathbf{Z}\sim\mathbb{P}_{\mathtt{Z}}^{\otimes m}
, the statistician conducts the following hypothesis testing problem:

H_{0}:{\boldsymbol{\theta}}={\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{1}\quad\text{versus}\quad H_{1}:{\boldsymbol{\theta}}={\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{2}.

(9)

To characterize the minimax sample complexity of the above hypothesis testing problem, we suppose
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{1}
and
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{2}
satisfies
\|{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{1}-{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{2}\|_{2}\geq\varepsilon
for some
\varepsilon>0
, and this piece of information is given to the statistician. After receiving data
(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Y},\mathbf{Z})\in(\mathbb{R}^{D})^{n}\times(\mathbb{R}^{D})^{n}\times(\mathbb{R}^{D})^{m}
, the statistician makes choice
\psi(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Y},\mathbf{Z})\in\{0,1\}
, where
\psi(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Y},\mathbf{Z})=0
denotes the statistician accepts hypothesis
H_{0}
and
\psi(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Y},\mathbf{Z})=1
denotes the statistician rejects
H_{0}
. We focus on the conditions of
(m,n)
such that

\max_{i\in\{0,1\}}\sup_{P\in H_{i}}\mathbb{P}(\psi(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Y},\mathbf{Z})\neq i)\leq\frac{1}{4},

(10)

where
\sup_{P\in H_{0}}
denotes the supreme over
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{1}
and
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{2}
with
\|{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{1}-{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{2}\|_{2}\geq\varepsilon
and
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}={\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{1}
, and
\sup_{P\in H_{1}}
denotes the supreme over
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{1}
and
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{2}
with
\|{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{1}-{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{2}\|_{2}\geq\varepsilon
and
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}={\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{2}
.

For any pair
(m,n)
, we say that
(m,n)
lies in the feasible region of the likelihood-free hypothesis test if and only if there exists some test scheme
\psi
such that
Eq. (
10
)
holds.

\ell_{p}
Bodies.
Given an integer
D\in[1,\infty]
, the
\ell_{p}
bodies in
\mathbb{R}^{D}
is characterized by a non-increasing nonnegative sequence
a_{1}\geq a_{2}\geq\cdots\geq a_{D}\geq 0
. The
\ell_{p}
body (also appears in
[
Bar02
]
) characterized by
a_{1:D}
is given by

\Gamma=\left\{\theta=(\theta_{1},\theta_{2},\cdots,\theta_{D})\ \Big|\ \sum_{t=1}^{D}\frac{|\theta_{t}|^{p}}{a_{t}^{p}}\leq 1\right\}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{D}.

(11)

When
D=\infty
, in order to guarantee the compactness of this infinite-dimensional set, we only consider the
\ell_{p}
bodies characterized by sequence
a_{t}
goes to zero, i.e.
\lim_{t\to\infty}a_{t}=0
.

Orthosymmetric Sets.
We recall the definition of orthosymmetric sets, first introduced in
[
DLM90
]
: Given an integer
D\in[1,\infty]
, we say
\Gamma\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{D}
is an orthosymmetric set if for any element
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}=(\theta_{1},\theta_{2},\cdots,\theta_{D})\in\Gamma
and
{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}=(\varepsilon_{1},\varepsilon_{2},\cdots,\varepsilon_{D})\in\{-1,1\}^{D}
, we have

{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}}=(\varepsilon_{1}\theta_{1},\varepsilon_{2}\theta_{2},\cdots,\varepsilon_{D}\theta_{D})\in\Gamma.

We notice that many sets satisfy the orthosymmetric property. It is easy to verify that the
\ell_{p}
bodies introduced in
Eq. (
11
)
are orthosymmetric. Additionally, if there exists a function
f:(\mathbb{R}_{+}\cup\{0\})^{D}\to\mathbb{R}
such that
\Gamma
can be represented as

\Gamma=\left\{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}=(\theta_{1},\theta_{2},\cdots,\theta_{D}):f(|\theta_{1}|,|\theta_{2}|,\cdots,|\theta_{D}|)\geq 0\right\},

(12)

then
\Gamma
is an orthosymmetric set. It is easy to see that all
\ell_{p}
bodies can be written in the above form for some function
f
.

Kolmogorov Dimension and Coordinate-wise Kolmogorov Dimension.
Given an integer
D\in[1,\infty]
, the Kolmogorov dimension of a set
\Gamma\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{D}
is a notion to measure the minimal dimension of an affine space so that the distance between any point in
\Gamma
and the affine space is bounded by some tolerance. This notion is given formally as in the following definition.

Definition 1

(Kolmogorov Dimension)
.

Suppose
\Gamma\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{D}
. For any
\varepsilon>0
, we define the Kolmogorov dimension
D(\Gamma,\varepsilon)
of
\Gamma
at scale
\varepsilon
to be the largest integer
d\leq D
such that

\inf_{\Pi_{d}}\sup_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\in\Gamma}\|{\boldsymbol{\theta}}-\Pi_{d}{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\|_{2}\leq\varepsilon,

where
\Pi_{d}
denotes a
d
-dimensional linear projection, and the infimum is over all possible
d
-dimensional linear projections.

Notice that in the above definition, the projection operator can be chosen to be any linear projections. However, in some cases, we have to restrict ourselves to project only along the coordinates. In this way, we define the following coordinate-wise Kolmogorov dimension.

Definition 2

(Coordinate-wise Kolmogorov Dimension)
.

Suppose
\Gamma\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{D}
. For any
\varepsilon>0
, we define the coordinate-wise Kolmogorov dimension
D_{\sf{c}}(\Gamma,\varepsilon)
of
\Gamma
at scale
\varepsilon
to be the largest integer
d\leq D
such that

\inf_{A:|A|=d}\sup_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\in\Gamma}\sum_{i\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}\backslash A}(\theta_{i})^{2}\leq\varepsilon^{2},\qquad\text{where }{\boldsymbol{\theta}}=(\theta_{1},\theta_{2},\cdots,\theta_{D}),

where the infimum is over all size-
d
subsets
A
of
\{1,2,\cdots,D\}
.

It is clear that for any set
\Gamma
and
\varepsilon>0
,
D_{\sf{c}}(\Gamma,\varepsilon)\geq D(\Gamma,\varepsilon)
. However, it is possible to have
D_{\sf{c}}\gg D
. Fortunately, our bounds depend on
D_{\sf{c}}
only logarithmically, and hence the following pair of results can be used to roughly relate
D_{\sf{c}}
to
D
and
\varepsilon
.

Proposition 1
.

For any orthosymmetric, convex, compact set
\Gamma
, we have

D\left(\Gamma,\frac{\varepsilon}{D_{\sf{c}}(\Gamma,\varepsilon)}\right)\geq\frac{D_{\sf{c}}(\Gamma,\varepsilon)}{2}-2.

Corollary 1
.

Suppose orthosymmetric, convex, compact set
\Gamma
satisfies
D(\Gamma,\varepsilon)\lesssim\varepsilon^{-p}
for some
0<p<1
. Then the coordinate-wise Kolmogorov dimension satisfies

D_{\sf{c}}(\Gamma,\varepsilon)\lesssim\varepsilon^{-p/(1-p)}.

3
Goodness-of-Fit and Density Estimation

We return to the testing-estimation relation (

) that was shown in

]
to hold for a variety of models, including Gaussian sequence models with

is deferred to

Suppose the coordinate-wise Kolmogorov dimension of

Remark 1
.

It is easy to see that all

3.3
Testing-Estimation Equivalence for Quadratically Convex Set

In the above, we already verify that the lower bound side of

)
holds, if we assume orthosymmetric property, compactness and also convexity of the set
\Gamma
. We wonder in what circumstances can the opposite side of inequality

hold. In the following, we show that if we additionally assume that quadratically convexity of set

, then the opposite side of

also holds. This result, together with

3.1
(or

Definition 3

(Quadratically Convex Set)
.

We say a set
\Gamma\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{D}
is quadratically convex, if the following set is convex:

\left\{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{2}:{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\in\Gamma\right\},

where
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{2}=(\theta_{1}^{2},\cdots,\theta_{D}^{2})
for
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}=(\theta_{1},\cdots,\theta_{D})\in\Gamma
.

Remark 3
.

The main result in
[
DLM90
]
was the proof of optimality (upto a universal constant factor

) of linear estimators (also known as projection estimators) for the minimax estimation rate in the Gaussian location model over an orthosymmetric, compact, convex and quadratically convex class.

We have the following theorem for quadratically convex sets.

Theorem 3.2
.

Suppose
\Gamma
is an orthosymmetric, compact, convex and quadratically convex set, then we have

n_{\mathrm{gof}}(\Gamma,6\varepsilon)^{2}\leq\frac{n_{\mathrm{est}}(\Gamma,\varepsilon)}{\varepsilon^{2}}.

The proof of
Theorem
3.2
is deferred to
Section
A.4
.

Remark 4
.

As shown in
[
DLM90
]
,
\ell_{p}
-bodies with
p\geq 2
are quadratically convex sets.

4.1
LFHT and Density Estimation

In this section we generalize both of these bounds to the setting of LFHT.

Theorem 4.1

Suppose

The proof of
Theorem
4.1

B.1
. The above theorem provide a lower bound to the feasible region of LFHT. Next, we provide an upper bound to the feasible region of LFHT, in terms of the Kolmogorov dimension. We introduce the following testing scheme: For
\varepsilon>0
, let
\Pi_{d}

-dimensional linear projection satisfying

\sup_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\in\Gamma}\|{\boldsymbol{\theta}}-\Pi_{d}{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\|_{2}\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{3},

4.2

\ell_{p}

From the last section, we have both sufficient and necessary conditions of the feasible region of LFHT, in terms of the density estimation rate. These characterization are tight for

Firstly, we recall the form of the testing region in

\left\{(m,n):\qquad m\gtrsim\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}},\quad n\gtrsim n_{\mathrm{gof}}(\varepsilon)\quad\text{and}\quad mn\gtrsim n_{\mathrm{gof}}(\varepsilon)^{2}\right\},

\ell_{p}

bodies is not in the above form. This can be seen from the following theorem.

Theorem 4.3

(Informal)
.

For given positive integer

and set of parameters
\Gamma
given in
Eq.
(
11
)
, we define function

d(\Gamma,n,\varepsilon)=\max\left\{d:(a_{d})^{p}n^{\frac{p-2}{2}}\gtrsim\varepsilon^{2}\right\}.

Then the LFHT region for

\left\{(m,n):\qquad m\gtrsim\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}},\quad n\gtrsim\frac{\sqrt{d(\Gamma,n,\varepsilon)}}{\varepsilon^{2}}\quad\text{and}\quad mn\gtrsim\frac{d(\Gamma,n,\varepsilon)}{\varepsilon^{4}}\right\}.

(16)

In the following, we will first present the results for finite dimensions (in

4.2.1
Testing Scheme and Upper Bounds

We first let
\delta=1/32
and define

d_{u}(\Gamma,n,\varepsilon)=\max\left\{d:(a_{d})^{p}n^{\frac{p-2}{2}}\gtrsim\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{576\log(4D/\delta)}\right\}.

(17)

Suppose
(m,n)
satisfies

\left\{(m,n):\qquad m\geq\frac{32}{\varepsilon^{2}},\quad n\geq\frac{32\sqrt{d_{u}(\Gamma,n,\varepsilon)}}{\varepsilon^{2}}\quad\text{and}\quad mn\geq\frac{512d_{u}(\Gamma,n,\varepsilon)}{\varepsilon^{4}}\right\}.

(18)

In this section we will come up with a testing scheme
\Psi
such that
Eq. (
10
)
is achieved. First of all, for cases where
m>n
, if we let
m_{0}=n
, then
(m_{0},n)
is also in the above region, and also
m_{0}\leq m
. Hence without loss of generality, we only need to construct a testing scheme for

.

Next, when
mn\geq 1024d_{u}(\Gamma,n,\varepsilon)/\varepsilon^{2}
, if
m\geq 32\sqrt{d_{u}(\Gamma,n,\varepsilon)}/\varepsilon^{2}
, we let
m_{0}=n_{0}=32\sqrt{d_{u}(\Gamma,n,\varepsilon)}/{\varepsilon^{2}}
, then
m_{0}\leq m
and
n_{0}\leq n
, and
(m_{0},n_{0})
is in the region defined in
Eq. (
18
)
, hence we only need to construct testing for
(m_{0},n_{0})
. If
m\leq 32\sqrt{d_{u}(\Gamma,n,\varepsilon)}/\varepsilon^{2}
, by letting
n_{0}=\lceil 512d_{u}(\Gamma,n,\varepsilon)/(\varepsilon^{2}m)\rceil

m\leq n_{0}\leq n
, and
(m,n_{0})
satisfies
mn_{0}\leq 1024d_{u}(\Gamma,n,\varepsilon)/(\varepsilon^{2}m)

(m,n_{0})
. Therefore, without loss of generality, we assume

mn\leq\frac{1024d_{u}(\Gamma,n,\varepsilon)}{\varepsilon^{4}}.

(19)

We introduce a testing scheme for
\ell_{p}
bodies in this section. Inspired by the soft-thresholding algorithms for density estimation, which are known to be minimax optimal for
\ell_{p}
balls where
p\in[1,2]

Joh19
]
and also the goodness of fit testing algorithms for
\ell_{p}
bodies
[
Bar02

, we design an algorithm for the setting of likelihood-free hypothesis testing. To describe the testing regime, we use
\mathbf{X}=(\mathbf{X}_{1},\cdots,\mathbf{X}_{n})
to denote the
n
i.i.d. samples collected from
\mathbb{P}_{\mathtt{X}}
,
\mathbf{Y}=(\mathbf{Y}_{1},\cdots,\mathbf{Y}_{n})
to denote the
n
i.i.d. samples collected from
\mathbb{P}_{\mathtt{Y}}
and
\mathbf{Z}=(\mathbf{X}_{1},\cdots,\mathbf{Z}_{m})

\mathbb{P}_{\mathtt{Z}}

To begin with, we divide those
n

(\mathbf{X}_{1},\cdots,\mathbf{X}_{n})
from
\mathbb{P}_{\mathtt{X}}
and samples
\mathbf{Y}_{1},\cdots,\mathbf{Y}_{n}
from
\mathbb{P}_{\mathtt{Y}}
each into two parts: let
n_{0}=\lfloor n/2\rfloor

\displaystyle\mathbf{X}^{1}=(\mathbf{X}_{1},\cdots,\mathbf{X}_{n_{0}}),\quad

\displaystyle\mathbf{X}^{2}=(\mathbf{X}_{n_{0}+1},\cdots,\mathbf{X}_{n}),

\displaystyle\text{and}\quad\mathbf{Y}^{1}=(\mathbf{Y}_{1},\cdots,\mathbf{Y}_{n_{0}}),\quad

\displaystyle\mathbf{Y}^{2}=(\mathbf{Y}_{n_{0}+1},\cdots,\mathbf{Y}_{n}).

We use
\hat{\theta}_{\mathtt{X}}^{1},\hat{\theta}_{\mathtt{X}}^{2},\hat{\theta}_{\mathtt{Y}}^{1},\hat{\theta}_{\mathtt{Y}}^{2}

\hat{\theta}_{\mathtt{Z}}

\displaystyle\hat{\theta}_{\mathtt{X}}^{1}

\displaystyle\hat{\theta}_{\mathtt{Y}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n_{0}}\sum_{i=n_{0}+1}^{n}\mathbf{Y}_{i},\quad\text{and}\quad\hat{\theta}_{\mathtt{Z}}=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\mathbf{Z}_{j}.

(i)

Calculating a subspace of small dimension such that
\mathbb{P}_{\mathtt{X}}
and
\mathbb{P}_{\mathtt{Y}}
are different within the subspace;

(ii)

Adopting the likelihood free hypothesis testing scheme in

(a)

Calculating the Subspace:

which satisfies

and

T_{1}=\{1,2,\ldots,d_{u}(\Gamma,n,\varepsilon)\}\quad\text{and}\quad T_{2}=\{d_{u}(\Gamma,n,\varepsilon)+1,d_{u}(\Gamma,n,\varepsilon)+2,\ldots,D\},

(20)

where

)
. For

-th coordinate of

(21)

for some parameter

to be defined later. And we construct

(b)

After obtaining the set

of coordinates the last step, we use the samples within

and

And we adopt the testing scheme

This is summarized in the following theorem.

Proof of

Our proof is divided into two parts: the lower bound to the density estimation sample complexity and the upper bound to the density estimation sample complexity.

Lower Bound to Density Estimation:

Without loss of generality we assume
(2\varepsilon)^{-2/3}
is an integer (otherwise we replace
\varepsilon
by
\lfloor(2\varepsilon)^{-2/3}\rfloor^{-3/2}/2
and the argument follows only up to constant). We first construct a infinite-dimensional rectangle which is a subset of
\Gamma
:

M=\left\{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}=(\theta_{1},\theta_{2},\cdots):|\theta_{i}|\leq(2\varepsilon)^{4/3},\ \forall 1\leq i\leq(2\varepsilon)^{-2/3},\quad\text{and}\quad\theta_{i}=0,\ \forall i\geq d\right\}.

We can verify that for any
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}=(\theta_{1},\theta_{2},\cdots)\in M
,

\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}i\cdot|\theta_{i}|\leq\sum_{i=1}^{(2\varepsilon)^{-2/3}}i\cdot(2\varepsilon)^{4/3}\leq 1.

Hence
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\in\Gamma
. Therefore,
M\subseteq\Gamma
. We next lower bound the density estimation error of estimation using
n
samples: according to
[
Joh19
, Proposition 4.16]
, we have

\inf_{\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{n}}\sup_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\in M}\mathbb{E}\left[\|\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{n}-{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\|^{2}\right]=(2\varepsilon)^{-2/3}\cdot\inf_{\widehat{\theta}_{n}}\sup_{|\theta|\leq(2\varepsilon)^{4/3}}\mathbb{E}\left[(\widehat{\theta}_{n}-\theta)^{2}\right],

where
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{n}
denotes an estimator with
n
i.i.d. samples coming from
\mathcal{N}({\boldsymbol{\theta}},I)
, and
\widehat{\theta}_{n}
denotes an estimator with
n
i.i.d. samples coming from
\mathcal{N}(\theta,1)
. Next, according to Van trees inequality
[
VT04
]
(also in
[
Joh19
, (4.9)]
), we have

\inf_{\widehat{\theta}_{n}}\sup_{|\theta|\leq(2\varepsilon)^{4/3}}\mathbb{E}\left[(\widehat{\theta}_{n}-\theta)^{2}\right]\geq\frac{1}{2n}\wedge\frac{1}{2}\cdot(2\varepsilon)^{8/3}.

Hence we obtain that

\inf_{\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{n}}\sup_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\in M}\mathbb{E}\left[\|\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{n}-{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\|^{2}\right]\geq(2\varepsilon)^{-2/3}\cdot\left(\frac{1}{2n}\wedge(2\varepsilon)^{8/3}\right)=2\varepsilon^{2}\wedge\frac{1}{2n\cdot(2\varepsilon)^{2/3}}.

Hence in order to achieve no more than
\varepsilon^{2}
density estimation error (i.e.
Eq. (
8
)
holds), we require

n_{\mathrm{est}}(\Gamma,\varepsilon)\geq\frac{\varepsilon^{-8/3}}{4}.

(27)

Upper Bound to Density Estimation:

Next, we construct an estimator which will achieve
\varepsilon^{2}
density estimation error (i.e.
Eq. (
8
)
holds) with no more than
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(\varepsilon^{-8/3})
samples. For
n
samples
\mathbf{X}=(\mathbf{X}^{1:n})
where each
\mathbf{X}^{i}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{iid}}}{{\sim}}\mathcal{N}({\boldsymbol{\theta}},I)
, we consider the following estimator: let
\widehat{\theta}(\mathbf{X})=(\widehat{\theta}_{1},\widehat{\theta}_{2},\cdots)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}
, and further construct estimator

\widetilde{\theta}(\mathbf{X})=(\widetilde{\theta}_{1}(\mathbf{X}),\widetilde{\theta}_{2}(\mathbf{X}),\cdots)
, where

\widetilde{\theta}_{i}=\begin{cases}\delta_{\lambda}(\hat{\theta}_{i})&\quad\forall 1\leq i\leq D,\\
0&\quad i>D,\end{cases}

where
\lambda>0
and
D\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
are parameters to be specified later. Here
\delta_{\lambda}(\cdot):\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is the soft-thresholding function defined as:

\delta_{\lambda}(x)=\begin{cases}x-\lambda&\quad x\geq\lambda,\\
0&\quad-\lambda\leq x<\lambda,\\
x+\lambda&\quad x<-\lambda.\end{cases}

We will show that with proper choices of
\lambda
and
D
, the density estimation error can be upper bounded by
\varepsilon^{2}
with
n=\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(1/\varepsilon^{8/3})
number of samples. We write
\mathbf{X}^{i}=(X_{1}^{i},X_{2}^{i},\cdots)
. Then we have
X_{j}^{1},X_{j}^{2},\cdots X_{j}^{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{iid}}}{{\sim}}\mathcal{N}(\theta_{j},1)
for any
j\geq 0
. And we only need to verify for some choice of
\lambda
and
D
,

\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{E}\left[(\widetilde{\theta}_{j}(\mathbf{X})-\theta_{j})^{2}\right]\leq\varepsilon^{2}.

When
j>D
, we have
\widetilde{\theta}_{j}(\mathbf{X})=0
, hence

\mathbb{E}\left[(\widetilde{\theta}_{j}(\mathbf{X})-\theta_{j})^{2}\right]=\theta_{j}^{2}.

And for
j\leq D
, according to
[
Joh19
, (8.12)]
, we have

\mathbb{E}\left[(\widetilde{\theta}_{j}(\mathbf{X})-\theta_{j})^{2}\right]\leq\frac{1}{n}\exp\left(-\frac{n\lambda^{2}}{2}\right)+\min\left\{\theta_{j}^{2},\frac{1}{n}+\lambda^{2}\right\}.

Thus, we obtain

\displaystyle\quad\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{E}\left[(\widetilde{\theta}_{j}(\mathbf{X})-\theta_{j})^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{D}{n}\exp\left(-\frac{n\lambda^{2}}{2}\right)+\sum_{j=1}^{D}\min\left\{\theta_{j}^{2},\frac{1}{n}+\lambda^{2}\right\}+\sum_{j=D+1}^{\infty}\theta_{j}^{2}.

Since
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\in\Theta
, we have

\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}j\cdot|\theta_{j}|\leq 1.

If we choose
D=\lceil 2/\varepsilon\rceil
, for
\theta=(\theta_{1},\theta_{2},\cdots)

\sum_{j=D+1}^{\infty}\theta_{j}^{2}\leq\frac{1}{D^{2}}\left(\sum_{j=D+1}^{\infty}j\cdot|\theta_{j}|\right)^{2}\leq\frac{1}{D^{2}}\leq\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{4}.

(28)

In the next, we will choose the value of
\lambda
and further upper bound

\sum_{j=1}^{D}\min\left\{\theta_{j}^{2},\frac{1}{n}+\lambda^{2}\right\}.

(29)

Since set

\theta_{j_{1}}
and
\theta_{j_{2}}
we can make (
29

(\theta_{1},\cdots,\theta_{D})

N
to be the smallest integer such that

\frac{N(N-1)}{2}\cdot\sqrt{\frac{1}{n}+\lambda^{2}}\geq 1.

Then the maximizer has at most
N
nonzero items, which implies

\sup_{\sum_{j=1}^{D}j\cdot|\theta_{j}|\leq 1}\sum_{j=1}^{D}\min\left\{\theta_{j}^{2},\frac{1}{n}+\lambda^{2}\right\}\leq N\cdot\left(\frac{1}{n}+\lambda^{2}\right).

Finally, we choose
\lambda=(\varepsilon/8)^{4/3}
. When
n\geq(\varepsilon/8)^{-8/3}\cdot 4\log(1/\varepsilon)
we have

N\leq 2\cdot(\varepsilon/8)^{-2/3}\quad\text{and}\quad\frac{1}{n}\leq\lambda^{2},

which implies

\sum_{j=1}^{D}\min\left\{\theta_{j}^{2},\frac{1}{n}+\lambda^{2}\right\}\leq 2\cdot(\varepsilon/8)^{-2/3}\cdot 2\lambda^{2}\leq\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{2}.

(30)

Further according to our choice of
n
we have

\frac{D}{n}\exp\left(-\frac{n\lambda^{2}}{2}\right)\leq\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{2},

(31)

Combining

)

)
and
Eq. (
31
)
, we obtain that

\frac{D}{n}\exp\left(-\frac{n\lambda^{2}}{2}\right)+\sum_{j=1}^{D}\min\left\{\theta_{j}^{2},\frac{1}{n}+\lambda^{2}\right\}+\sum_{j=D+1}^{\infty}\theta_{j}^{2}\leq\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{4}+\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{2}+\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{4}=\varepsilon^{2}.

Therefore, we obtain

n_{\mathrm{est}}(\Gamma,\varepsilon)\leq\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{8}\right)^{-8/3}\cdot 4\log\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\right).

(32)

Above all, according to
Eq. (

)
and

32

n_{\mathrm{est}}(\Gamma,\varepsilon)=\tilde{\Theta}\left(\varepsilon^{-8/3}\right).

)
, we have

\mathbb{P}\left(\max_{d+1\leq i\leq D}|\widehat{\theta}_{i}|\leq\varepsilon^{6/5}\right)=\left(1-\varphi_{1}(\varepsilon^{6/5}\sqrt{n})\right)^{D-d}\geq 1-D\cdot\varphi_{1}(\varepsilon^{6/5}\sqrt{n})\geq 1-D\cdot\exp\left(-n\varepsilon^{12/5}\right)\geq\frac{7}{8}.

where
\varphi_{1}(\cdot)
is the probability density function of one-dimensional standard normal distribution. Hence both inequalities in
Eq. (
38
)
are verified.

Next, we verify
Eq. (
7
)
for the case
i=1
, i.e.

\sup_{P\in H_{1}}\mathbb{P}(\psi(\mathbf{X})=0)\leq\frac{1}{4}.

(39)

We only need to show that for any
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\in\Gamma
with
\|{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\|_{2}\geq\varepsilon
, we always have

\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{X}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{iid}}}{{\sim}}\mathcal{N}({\boldsymbol{\theta}},I)}\left[\psi(\mathbf{X})=1\right]\leq\frac{1}{4}.

Notice that when
\mathbf{X}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{iid}}}{{\sim}}\mathcal{N}({\boldsymbol{\theta}},I)
, we have
\widehat{\theta}_{i}\sim\mathcal{N}(\theta_{i},1/n)
independently. For
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\in\Gamma
which satisfies
\|{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\|_{2}\geq\varepsilon
, we have

\sum_{i=D+1}^{\infty}(\theta_{i})^{2}\leq\left(\sum_{i=D+1}^{\infty}|\theta_{i}|\right)\leq\frac{1}{D^{2}}\left(\sum_{i=D+1}^{\infty}i\cdot|\theta_{i}|\right)\leq\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{4},

which implies that

\sum_{i=1}^{d}(\theta_{i})^{2}+\sum_{i=d+1}^{D}(\theta_{i})^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{D}(\theta_{i})^{2}\geq\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{2}.

Therefore, we have either
\sum_{i=1}^{d}(\theta_{i})^{2}\geq\varepsilon^{2}/4
, or
\sum_{i=d+1}^{D}(\theta_{i})^{2}\geq\varepsilon^{2}/4
. If we have
\sum_{i=1}^{d}(\theta_{i})^{2}\geq\varepsilon^{2}/4
, since
\widehat{\theta}_{i}\sim\mathcal{N}(\theta_{i},1/n)
independently, we can calculate

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{d}(\widehat{\theta}_{i})^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{d}(\theta_{i})^{2}+\frac{d}{n}

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{d}(\widehat{\theta}_{i})^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{4}{n}\cdot\sum_{i=1}^{d}(\theta_{i})^{2}+\frac{2d}{n^{2}}.

Therefore, according to Chebyshev inequality we obtain that when
n
satisfies
Eq. (
36
)

\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{d}(\widehat{\theta}_{i})^{2}\leq\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{8}\right)\leq\frac{\frac{4}{n}\cdot\sum_{i=1}^{d}(\theta_{i})^{2}+\frac{2d}{n^{2}}}{|\sum_{i=1}^{d}(\theta_{i})^{2}+\frac{d}{n}-\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{8}|^{2}}\leq\frac{1}{4},\quad\text{hence}\quad\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{d}(\widehat{\theta}_{i})^{2}\geq\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{8}\right)\geq\frac{3}{4}.

Next, we consider the case where
\sum_{i=d+1}^{D}(\theta_{i})^{2}\geq\varepsilon^{2}/4
. We notice that

\sum_{i=d+1}^{D}(\theta_{i})^{2}\leq\left(\sum_{i=d+1}^{D}|\theta_{i}|\right)\cdot\max_{d+1\leq i\leq D}|\theta_{i}|\leq\frac{1}{d}\cdot\left(\sum_{i=d+1}^{D}i\cdot|\theta_{i}|\right)\cdot\max_{d+1\leq i\leq D}|\theta_{i}|\leq\frac{1}{d}\cdot\max_{d+1\leq i\leq D}|\theta_{i}|,

which implies that

\max_{d+1\leq i\leq D}|\theta_{i}|\geq d\cdot\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{4}\geq\varepsilon^{6/5}.

Since
\widehat{\theta}_{i}\sim\mathcal{N}(\theta_{i},1/n)
independently, when
n
satisfies
Eq. (
36
)
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\max_{d+1\leq i\leq D}|\widehat{\theta}_{i}-\theta_{i}|\leq\varepsilon^{6/5}\right)

\displaystyle=(1-\varphi_{1}(\varepsilon^{6/5}\sqrt{n}))^{D-d}

\displaystyle\geq 1-D\cdot\varphi(\varepsilon^{6/5}\sqrt{n})\geq 1-D\cdot\exp(-n\varepsilon^{12/5})\geq\frac{3}{4},

which implies that

\mathbb{P}\left(\max_{d+1\leq i\leq D}|\widehat{\theta}_{i}|\geq\varepsilon^{6/5}\right)\geq\frac{3}{4}.

According to the form of the testing scheme
\psi
, we verified
Eq. (
7
)
for the case
i=1
.
∎

A.2
Proofs of
Theorem
3.1
and
Corollary
2

In order to prove
Theorem
3.1
, we first bring up the following property of orthosymmetric convex sets.

Proposition 5
.

Suppose
\Gamma\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
is an orthosymmetric set. If
\Gamma
is also convex, then for any
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}=(\theta_{1},\cdots,\theta_{D})\in\Gamma
and
\alpha=(\alpha_{1},\cdots,{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{D})\in[-1,1]^{D}
, we have
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\cdot{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}=(\alpha_{1}\theta_{1},\alpha_{2}\theta_{2},\cdots,\alpha_{D}\theta_{D})\in\Gamma
.

Now we are ready for the proof of
Theorem
3.1
.

Proof of
Theorem
3.1
.

Without loss of generality, we assume that
n_{\mathrm{est}}(\Gamma,\varepsilon)\geq 4
. Our proof proceeds as follows. First, we consider parameter estimation via soft-thresholding. We show that there must exist
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{*}
in
\Gamma
such that
\|{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{*}\|^{2}\asymp\varepsilon^{2}
and each entry
|{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{*}_{i}|\leq n_{\mathrm{est}}(\Gamma,\varepsilon)^{-1/2}\mathrm{polylog}(n,\varepsilon)
(for otherwise, soft-thresholding estimator would beat the optimal sample complexity of estimation). Second, we use Ingster’s method of simple (
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}=0
) vs composite (
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}=(\pm{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{*}_{1},\ldots,\pm{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{*}_{D})
) hypothesis testing to lower bound the goodness of fit sample complexity
n_{\mathrm{gof}}(\Gamma,\varepsilon)
.

First Part: Density Estimation Rate:
We define the soft-thresholding function
\mathrm{sth}:\mathbb{R}\times(\mathbb{R}_{+}\cup\{0\})\to\mathbb{R}
:

\mathrm{sth}(x,\lambda)=\begin{cases}x-\lambda&\quad\text{if }x\geq\lambda,\\
0&\quad\text{if }-\lambda\leq x<\lambda,\\
x+\lambda&\quad\text{if }x<-\lambda.\end{cases}

(40)

For some
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\in\Gamma
, given
n
samples
X=X^{1:n}\sim\mathcal{N}({\boldsymbol{\theta}},I_{D})^{\otimes n}
, we construct the soft-thresholding estimator
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{\mathrm{sth}}^{n}=(\widehat{\theta}_{1}^{n},\cdots,\widehat{\theta}_{D}^{n})
as follows: for any
j\in[D]
, we choose

\widehat{\theta}_{j}^{n}=\mathrm{sth}\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{j}^{i},\lambda(n)\right),

where we denote
X^{i}=(X_{1}^{i},X_{2}^{i},\cdots,X_{D}^{i})
, and we let

\lambda(n)\triangleq\sqrt{\frac{2\log\left(\nicefrac{{2D}}{{(n\varepsilon^{2})}}\right)\vee 0}{n}}.

(41)

We let
n_{\mathrm{sth}}(\varepsilon)
to denote the smallest
n
such that
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{\mathrm{sth}}^{n}
induces expected estimation error
\varepsilon^{2}
for all
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\in\Gamma
, i.e.

n_{\mathrm{sth}}(\varepsilon)=\min\left\{n:\sup_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\in\Gamma}\mathbb{E}\left[\|\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{\mathrm{sth}}^{n}-{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\|^{2}\right]\leq\varepsilon^{2}\right\}.

(42)

Since
n_{\mathrm{est}}(\Gamma,\varepsilon)
is the minimal number of samples in order to reach expected estimation error
\varepsilon^{2}
, we have

n_{\mathrm{sth}}(\varepsilon)\geq n_{\mathrm{est}}(\Gamma,\varepsilon).

According to
Eq. (
42
)
, we have

\sup_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\in\Gamma}\mathbb{E}_{X_{1:n_{\mathrm{sth}}(\varepsilon)}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{iid}}}{{\sim}}\mathcal{N}({\boldsymbol{\theta}},I_{D})}\left[\left\|\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{\mathrm{sth}}^{n_{\mathrm{sth}}(\varepsilon)}-{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\right\|_{2}^{2}\right]\leq\varepsilon^{2}

(43)

and for any
n<n_{\mathrm{sth}}(\varepsilon)
,

\sup_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\in\Gamma}\mathbb{E}_{X_{1:n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{iid}}}{{\sim}}\mathcal{N}({\boldsymbol{\theta}},I_{D})}\left[\left\|\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{\mathrm{sth}}^{n}-{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\right\|_{2}^{2}\right]\geq\varepsilon^{2}.

(44)

Next, according to
[
Joh19
, (8.7), (8.12)]
, we have for any positive integer
n
and
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}=(\theta_{1},\cdots,\theta_{D})\in\Gamma
that

\mathbb{E}_{X_{1:n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{iid}}}{{\sim}}\mathcal{N}({\boldsymbol{\theta}},I_{D})}\left[\left\|\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{\mathrm{sth}}^{n}-{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\right\|_{2}^{2}\right]\leq\sum_{i=1}^{D}\frac{1}{n}\exp\left(-\frac{n\lambda(n)^{2}}{2}\right)+\min\left\{\theta_{i}^{2},\frac{1}{n}+\lambda(n)^{2}\right\},

which implies

\displaystyle\sup_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\in\Gamma}\mathbb{E}_{X_{1:n}\sim\mathcal{N}({\boldsymbol{\theta}},I_{D})}\left[\left\|\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{\mathrm{sth}}^{n}-{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\right\|_{2}^{2}\right]\leq\sup_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\in\Gamma}\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{D}\frac{1}{n}\exp\left(-\frac{n\lambda(n)^{2}}{2}\right)+\min\left\{\theta_{i}^{2},\frac{1}{n}+\lambda(n)^{2}\right\}\right\}.

With our choice of
\lambda(n)
in (
41
), we have for any
1\leq i\leq D
,

\sum_{i=1}^{D}\frac{1}{n}\exp\left(-\frac{n\lambda(n)^{2}}{2}\right)\leq\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{2}\quad\text{and}\quad\frac{1}{n}+\lambda(n)^{2}\leq\frac{1+(2\log\left(\nicefrac{{2D}}{{(n\varepsilon^{2})}}\right)\vee 0)}{n},

which implies

\sup_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\in\Gamma}\mathbb{E}_{X_{1:n}\sim\mathcal{N}({\boldsymbol{\theta}},I_{D})}\left[\left\|\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{\mathrm{sth}}-{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\right\|_{2}^{2}\right]\leq\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{2}+\sup_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\in\Gamma}\sum_{i=1}^{D}\min\left\{\theta_{i}^{2},\frac{1+\log\left(\nicefrac{{2D}}{{(n\varepsilon^{2})}}\right)}{n}\right\}.

We define set
L\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{D}
as

L=\left\{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}=(\theta_{1},\cdots,\theta_{D})\ \Big|\ |\theta_{i}|\leq\sqrt{\frac{2\log(\nicefrac{{2D}}{{(n\varepsilon^{2})}})\vee 0+1}{n}}\right\}

Note that according to
Proposition
5
we can replace
\sup_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\in\Gamma}
with
\sup_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\in\Gamma\cap L}
obtaining

\displaystyle\hskip-14.22636pt\sup_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\in\Gamma}\sum_{i=1}^{D}\min\left\{\theta_{i}^{2},\frac{2\log\left(\nicefrac{{2D}}{{(n\varepsilon^{2})}}\right)\vee 0+1}{n}\right\}

\displaystyle=\sup_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\in\Gamma\cap L}\sum_{i=1}^{D}\min\left\{\theta_{i}^{2},\frac{2\log\left(\nicefrac{{2D}}{{(n\varepsilon^{2})}}\right)\vee 0+1}{n}\right\}=\sup_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\in\Gamma\cap L}\|{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\|^{2}\,.

We choose
n=n_{\mathrm{sth}}(\varepsilon)-1
. Since
\Gamma\cap L
is compact, the above supreme is achieved at some
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{*}=(\theta_{1}^{*},\cdots,\theta_{D}^{*})\in\Gamma\cap L
, which has two properties. On one hand it satisfies

|\theta_{i}^{*}|\leq\sqrt{\frac{2\log\left(\nicefrac{{2D}}{{((n_{\mathrm{est}}(\varepsilon)-1)\varepsilon^{2})}}\right)\vee 0+1}{n_{\mathrm{sth}}(\varepsilon)-1}},

(45)

On the other hand, according to (
44
), we obtain that

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{D}(\theta_{i}^{*})^{2}

\displaystyle\geq\sup_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\in\Gamma}\mathbb{E}_{X_{1:n_{\mathrm{sth}}(\varepsilon)-1}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{iid}}}{{\sim}}\mathcal{N}({\boldsymbol{\theta}},I_{D})}\left[\left\|\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{\mathrm{sth}}^{n_{\mathrm{sth}}(\varepsilon)-1}-{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\right\|_{2}^{2}\right]-\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{2}\geq\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{2}.

Additionally, according to
[
Joh19
, Lemma 8.3]
, we have for any positive integer
n
and
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}=(\theta_{1},\cdots,\theta_{D})\in\Gamma
,

\mathbb{E}_{X_{1:n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{iid}}}{{\sim}}\mathcal{N}({\boldsymbol{\theta}},I_{D})}\left[\left\|\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{\mathrm{sth}}^{n}-{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\right\|_{2}^{2}\right]\geq\frac{1}{2}\cdot\sum_{i=1}^{D}\min\left\{\theta_{i}^{2},\frac{1}{n}+\lambda(n)^{2}\right\}

We choose
n=n_{\mathrm{sth}}(\varepsilon)
and
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}={\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{*}=(\theta_{1}^{*},\cdots,\theta_{D}^{*})
defined above. (
43
) implies

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{D}\min\left\{(\theta_{i}^{*})^{2},\frac{1}{n_{\mathrm{sth}}(\varepsilon)}+\lambda(n_{\mathrm{sth}}(\varepsilon))^{2}\right\}\leq 2\sup_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\in\Gamma}\mathbb{E}_{X_{1:n_{\mathrm{sth}}(\varepsilon)}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{iid}}}{{\sim}}\mathcal{N}({\boldsymbol{\theta}},I_{D})}\left[\left\|\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{\mathrm{sth}}^{n_{\mathrm{sth}}(\varepsilon)}-{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\right\|_{2}^{2}\right]\leq 2\varepsilon^{2}.

Further when
n_{\mathrm{sth}}(\varepsilon)\geq 4
,
Eq. (
45
)
gives that for any
i\in[D]
,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n_{\mathrm{sth}}(\varepsilon)}+\lambda(n_{\mathrm{sth}}(\varepsilon))^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n_{\mathrm{sth}}(\varepsilon)}+\frac{2\log\left(\nicefrac{{2D}}{{(n_{\mathrm{sth}}(\varepsilon)\varepsilon^{2})}}\right)\vee 0+1}{n_{\mathrm{sth}}(\varepsilon)}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2}\cdot\frac{2\log\left(\nicefrac{{2D}}{{((n_{\mathrm{sth}}(\varepsilon)-1))\varepsilon^{2}}})\right)\vee 0+1}{n_{\mathrm{sth}}(\varepsilon)-1}\geq\frac{1}{4}(\theta_{i}^{*})^{2},

Hence we obtain that

\sum_{i=1}^{D}(\theta_{i}^{*})^{2}\leq 4\sum_{i=1}^{D}\min\left\{(\theta_{i}^{*})^{2},\frac{1}{n_{\mathrm{sth}}(\varepsilon)}+\lambda(n_{\mathrm{sth}}(\varepsilon))^{2}\right\}\leq 8\varepsilon^{2}.

Overall, we constructed
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{*}=(\theta_{1}^{*},\cdots,\theta_{D}^{*})\in\Gamma
such that

(a)

For any
1\leq i\leq D
,
|\theta_{i}^{*}|\leq\sqrt{\frac{2\log\left(\nicefrac{{2D}}{{((n_{\mathrm{est}}(\varepsilon)-1)\varepsilon^{2})}}\right)\vee 0+1}{n_{\mathrm{sth}}(\varepsilon)-1}}
.

(b)

\sum_{i=1}^{D}(\theta_{i}^{*})^{2}\geq\nicefrac{{\varepsilon^{2}}}{{2}}
.

(c)

\sum_{i=1}^{D}(\theta_{i}^{*})^{2}\leq 8\varepsilon^{2}
.

Second Part: Goodness of Fit Rate:
Suppose we already identified
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{*}
which satisfies
(a)
,
(b)
and
(c)
. We consider distribution
\nu=\nu_{1}\otimes\nu_{2}\otimes\cdots\otimes\nu_{D}
where
\nu_{i}\in\Delta(\mathbb{R})
is given by

\nu_{i}(\cdot)=\frac{\delta_{\theta_{i}^{*}}(\cdot)}{2}+\frac{\delta_{-\theta_{i}^{*}}(\cdot)}{2}.

Since
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{*}\in\Gamma
and
\Gamma
is orthosymmetric, we have
\nu\in\Delta(\Gamma)
. And
(b)
gives for any
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\in\mathrm{supp}(\nu)
, we always have
\|{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\|\geq\nicefrac{{\varepsilon}}{{\sqrt{2}}}
. Thus, according to
[
IS12
, Proposition 2.11]
, for any testing scheme
\psi
with
n
samples for the following testing problem:

H_{0}:{\boldsymbol{\theta}}=\mathbf{0}\quad\text{versus}\quad H_{1}:\|{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\|_{2}\geq\frac{\varepsilon}{\sqrt{2}},\ {\boldsymbol{\theta}}\in\Gamma

as long as
n\leq\sqrt{\frac{n_{\mathrm{est}}(\Gamma,\varepsilon)}{64\varepsilon^{2}\log(2D)}}
, we have

\sup_{P\in H_{0}}\mathbb{P}(\psi(X)\neq i)+\sup_{P\in H_{1}}\mathbb{P}(\psi(X)\neq i)\geq 1-\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{TV}(\mathbb{E}_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\sim\nu}[P_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}^{\otimes n}],P_{0}^{\otimes n})\,,

where we denoted
P_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}=\mathcal{N}({\boldsymbol{\theta}},I_{D})
.
Hence, if we can show that the
\mathrm{TV}\geq\frac{1}{2}
for a certain value of
n
it must imply that
n_{\mathrm{gof}}(\Gamma,\nicefrac{{\varepsilon}}{{\sqrt{2}}})\geq n
. We will indeed show that
n=\sqrt{\frac{n_{\mathrm{est}}(\Gamma,\varepsilon)}{64\varepsilon^{2}\log(2D)}}
works.

To that end, we first use a standard bound
[
PW25
, Proposition 7.15]

\mathrm{TV}(\mathbb{E}_{P\sim\mu(\nu)}[P^{\otimes n}],P_{0}^{\otimes n})\leq\sqrt{\frac{1}{4}\chi^{2}\left(\mathbb{E}_{P\sim\mu(\nu)}[P^{\otimes n}]\|P_{0}^{\otimes n}\right)}\,.

Next, we have the standard bound of Ingster

\displaystyle\hskip-28.45274pt\chi^{2}\left(\mathbb{E}_{P\sim\mu(\nu)}[P^{\otimes n}]\|P_{0}^{\otimes n}\right)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(i)}}{{\leq}}\prod_{i=1}^{D}\exp\left(\frac{1}{2}n^{2}(\theta_{i}^{*})^{4}\right)-1=\exp\left(\frac{n^{2}}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{D}(\theta_{i}^{*})^{4}\right)-1,

where
(i)
uses
[
Ing87
, (3.68)]

and the inequality
\frac{1}{2}\exp(x)+\frac{1}{2}\exp(-x)\leq\exp\left(\frac{1}{2}x^{2}\right)
. Next according to
(a)
and
(c)
, we obtain that when
n_{\mathrm{est}}(\Gamma,\varepsilon)\geq 4
,

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{D}(\theta_{i}^{*})^{4}

\displaystyle\leq\left(\max_{i\in[D]}|\theta_{i}^{*}|\right)^{2}\cdot\left(\sum_{i=1}^{D}(\theta_{i}^{*})^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\leq 8\varepsilon^{2}\cdot\frac{2\log\left(\nicefrac{{2D}}{{((n_{\mathrm{est}}(\varepsilon)-1)\varepsilon^{2})}}\right)\vee 0+1}{n_{\mathrm{sth}}(\varepsilon)-1}\leq 64\varepsilon^{2}\cdot\frac{\log\left(\nicefrac{{2D}}{{(n_{\mathrm{est}}(\Gamma,\varepsilon)\varepsilon^{2})}}\right)\vee 0+1}{n_{\mathrm{est}}(\Gamma,\varepsilon)}.

Next, we notice that when the diameter
\mathrm{diam}(\Gamma)
of
\Gamma
is less than
\varepsilon
, then the density estimation rate
n_{\mathrm{est}}(\Gamma,\varepsilon)
is zero, hence the inequality in
Theorem
3.1
obviously holds. When
\mathrm{diam}(\Gamma)\geq\varepsilon
, the density estimation complexity
n_{\mathrm{est}}(\Gamma,\varepsilon)
satisfies
n_{\mathrm{est}}(\Gamma,\varepsilon)\geq\varepsilon^{-2}
. Hence we obtain that

\log\left(\nicefrac{{2D}}{{(n_{\mathrm{est}}(\Gamma,\varepsilon)\varepsilon^{2})}}\right)\vee 0+1\leq\log(2D)

Hence when
n
satisfies

n^{2}\leq\frac{n_{\mathrm{est}}(\Gamma,\varepsilon)}{64\varepsilon^{2}\cdot\log(2D)},

we have

\chi^{2}\left(\mathbb{E}_{P\sim\mu(\nu)}[P^{\otimes n}]\|P_{0}^{\otimes n}\right)\leq\exp(1/2)-1\leq\frac{2}{3},

completing the proof.
∎

Proof of
Corollary
2
.

We fix
\varepsilon>0
and let
D=D_{\sf{c}}(\Gamma,\varepsilon)
. According to the definition of coordinate-wise Kolmogorov dimension
Definition
2
, there exists a subset
A
of
\mathbb{Z}_{+}
such that

\sup_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\in\Gamma}\sum_{i\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}\backslash A}(\theta_{i})^{2}\leq 2\varepsilon^{2}.

Without loss of generality that we assume
A=\{1,2,\cdots,D\}
, and we use
\Pi_{D}
to denote the projection operator onto the first
D
coordinates.

We construct
\Gamma_{D}=\{\Pi_{D}{\boldsymbol{\theta}}:{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\in\Gamma\}
. Then for any density estimator
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{D}
for
\Gamma_{D}
, we can construct a density estimator
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
: when taking
X
, we define estimator
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
as

\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(X)=\big(\underbrace{\widehat{\theta}_{1}(X),\widehat{\theta}_{2}(X),\cdots,\widehat{\theta}_{k}(X)}_{\text{first }D\text{ coordinates match }\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{D}(\Pi_{D}X)},0,0,\cdots\big),

where
\Pi_{D}X
denotes the data after taking projection
\Pi_{D}
for all elements in
X
. Then we have for any
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\in\Gamma
,

\mathbb{E}\left[\|{\boldsymbol{\theta}}-\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(X)\|_{2}^{2}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\|\Pi_{D}{\boldsymbol{\theta}}-\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{D}(\Pi_{D}X)\|_{2}^{2}\right]+\|\Pi_{D}{\boldsymbol{\theta}}-{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\|^{2}\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\|\Pi_{D}{\boldsymbol{\theta}}-\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{D}(\Pi_{D}X)\|_{2}^{2}\right]+2\varepsilon^{2}.

Notice that
\Pi_{D}X
can be viewed as i.i.d. samples collected through
\mathcal{N}(\Pi_{D}{\boldsymbol{\theta}},I_{D})
, we obtain that

n_{\mathrm{est}}(\Gamma,\sqrt{3}\varepsilon)\leq n_{\mathrm{est}}(\Gamma_{D},\varepsilon).

(46)

Additionally, since
\Gamma
is orthosymmetric and convex, we have
\Gamma_{D}\subseteq\Gamma
. When carrying the goodness of fit test for
\Gamma_{D}
, the data after
D
-th coordinate are generated according to
\mathcal{N}(0,1)
and independent to the parameter
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\in\Gamma_{D}
. Therefore, carrying goodness of fit testing to
\Gamma
is no easier than carrying goodness of fit testing to
\Gamma_{D}
, which implies

n_{\mathrm{gof}}\left(\Gamma,\frac{\varepsilon}{\sqrt{2}}\right)\geq n_{\mathrm{gof}}\left(\Gamma_{D},\frac{\varepsilon}{\sqrt{2}}\right).

(47)

Since
\Gamma
is compact, orthosymmetric and convex,
\Gamma_{D}
is a
D
-dimensional compact, orthosymmetric and convex set as well. Hence
Theorem
3.1
implies that

n_{\mathrm{gof}}\left(\Gamma_{D},\frac{\varepsilon}{\sqrt{2}}\right)^{2}\geq\frac{1}{64\log(2D)}\cdot\frac{n_{\mathrm{est}}(\Gamma_{D},\varepsilon)}{\varepsilon^{2}}.

Bringing in
Eq. (
46
)
and
Eq. (
47
)
, we obtain that

\displaystyle n_{\mathrm{gof}}\left(\Gamma,\frac{\varepsilon}{\sqrt{2}}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{64\log(2D)}\cdot\frac{n_{\mathrm{est}}(\Gamma,\sqrt{3}\varepsilon)}{\varepsilon^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{64\log(2D)}\cdot\frac{n_{\mathrm{est}}(\Gamma,\sqrt{3}\varepsilon)}{\varepsilon^{2}}.

∎

A.3
Discussion between Kolmogorov Dimension and Coordinate-wise Kolmogorov Dimension

For any orthosymmetric set
\Gamma
, we have the following relationship, which provides an upper bound of the coordinate-wise Kolmogorov dimension
D_{\sf{c}}(\Gamma,\varepsilon)
in terms of the traditional Kolmogorov dimension
D(\Gamma,\varepsilon)
.

Proposition 6
.

For any orthosymmetric, convex, compact set
\Gamma
, we have

D\left(\Gamma,\frac{\varepsilon}{D_{\sf{c}}(\Gamma,\varepsilon)}\right)\geq\frac{D_{\sf{c}}(\Gamma,\varepsilon)}{2}-2.

The proof of
Proposition
6
requires the following lemmas.

Lemma A.1
.

Suppose set
\Lambda=\{\mathbf{e}_{1},\cdots,\mathbf{e}_{d}\}
consists of orthogonal unit vectors. Then for any
(d-1)
-dimensional projection
\Pi
, we have

\max_{i\in[d]}\left\|\mathbf{e}_{i}-\Pi[\mathbf{e}_{i}]\right\|\geq\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}.

(48)

Proof of
Lemma
A.1
.

Suppose
\mathbf{v}_{1},\cdots,\mathbf{v}_{d-1}
to be a set of orthogonal basis of the image of projection
\Pi
. Then for any
i\in[d]
,

\left\|\mathbf{e}_{i}-\Pi[\mathbf{e}_{i}]\right\|^{2}=\|\mathbf{e}_{i}\|^{2}-\|\Pi[\mathbf{e}_{i}]\|^{2}=1-\sum_{j=1}^{d-1}\langle\mathbf{e}_{i},\mathbf{v}_{j}\rangle^{2},

which implies that

\sum_{i=1}^{d}\left\|\mathbf{e}_{i}-\Pi[\mathbf{e}_{i}]\right\|^{2}=d-\sum_{i=1}^{d}\sum_{j=1}^{d-1}\langle\mathbf{e}_{i},\mathbf{v}_{j}\rangle^{2}=d-\sum_{j=1}^{d-1}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{d}\langle\mathbf{e}_{i},\mathbf{v}_{j}\rangle^{2}\right).

Since
\mathbf{e}_{1},\cdots,\mathbf{e}_{d}
are orthogonal unit vectors,

\sum_{i=1}^{d}\langle\mathbf{e}_{i},\mathbf{v}_{j}\rangle^{2}=\left\|\Pi_{\mathrm{span}(\mathbf{e}_{1},\cdots,\mathbf{e}_{d})}[\mathbf{v}_{j}]\right\|\leq 1,\qquad\forall j\in[d-1],

where the above
\Pi
denotes the projection onto the space spanned by
\mathbf{e}_{1},\cdots,\mathbf{e}_{d}
. Therefore,

\sum_{i=1}^{d}\left\|\mathbf{e}_{i}-\Pi[\mathbf{e}_{i}]\right\|\geq d-(d-1)=1,

which implies
Eq. (
48
)
.
∎

Lemma A.2
.

For orthosymmetric, convex, compact set
\Gamma
, suppose
d=D_{\sf{c}}(\Gamma,\varepsilon)-1
, then there exists
d
orthogonal vectors
\mathbf{u}_{1},\cdots,\mathbf{u}_{\lfloor d/2\rfloor}\in\Gamma
such that for any
1\leq i\leq\lfloor d/2\rfloor
,
\|\mathbf{u}_{i}\|\geq\varepsilon/\sqrt{d}
.

Proof of
Lemma
A.2
.

Suppose
\mathbf{e}_{1},\mathbf{e}_{2},\cdots
are unit vectors of each coordinates, and we let
v_{i}=\sup\{v\geq 0:v\mathbf{e}_{i}\in\Gamma\}
. Then since
\Gamma
is closed, we have
\mathbf{v}_{i}=v_{i}\mathbf{e}_{i}\in\Gamma
. Without loss of generality we assume
v_{1}\geq v_{2}\geq\cdots
. Then according to the definition of coordinate-wise Kolmogorov dimension in
Definition
2
, we have

\sum_{i=d+1}^{D}(v_{i})^{2}>\varepsilon^{2}.

If
v_{d}\geq\varepsilon/\sqrt{d}
, then by choosing
\mathbf{u}_{i}=\mathbf{v}_{i}
, these vectors satisfy the conditions. Next we assume
v_{d}<\varepsilon/\sqrt{d}
. To construct
\mathbf{u}_{1},\cdots,\mathbf{u}_{\lfloor d/2\rfloor}
, we initiate the following process: letting
\mathbf{u}_{1}=\sum_{i=d+1}^{k_{1}}(v_{i})^{2}
, where
k_{1}
is the smallest number such that
\|\mathbf{u}_{1}\|\geq\varepsilon/\sqrt{d}
. Then let
\mathbf{u}_{2}=\sum_{i=k_{1}+1}^{k_{2}}(v_{i})^{2}
where
k_{2}
is the smallest number such that
\|\mathbf{u}_{2}\|\geq\varepsilon/\sqrt{d}
, and so on. Since
v_{i}\leq v_{d}<\varepsilon/\sqrt{d}
holds for any
i\geq d+1
, the above construction gives that

\frac{\varepsilon}{\sqrt{d}}\leq\|\mathbf{u}_{i}\|\leq\frac{\sqrt{2}\varepsilon}{\sqrt{d}}.

Therefore, since
\sum_{i=d+1}^{D}(v_{i})^{2}>\varepsilon^{2}
, the above process can proceed at least
\lfloor d/2\rfloor
times. Hence it is eligible to construct
\mathbf{u}_{1},\cdots,\mathbf{u}_{\lfloor d/2\rfloor}
so that
\|\mathbf{u}_{i}\|\geq\varepsilon/\sqrt{d}
holds. It is easy to verify that
\mathbf{u}_{1},\cdots,\mathbf{u}_{\lfloor d/2\rfloor}
are orthogonal. And since
\Gamma
is orthosymmetric and convex, we have
\mathbf{u}_{i}\in\Gamma
for any
i
.
∎

Now we are ready to prove
Proposition
6
.

Proof of
Proposition
6
.

Let
d=D_{\sf{c}}(\Gamma,\varepsilon)-1
. According to
Lemma
A.2
, there exists orthogonal vectors
\mathbf{u}_{1},\cdots,\mathbf{u}_{\lfloor d/2\rfloor}\in\Gamma
such that
\|\mathbf{u}_{i}\|\geq\varepsilon/\sqrt{d}
. By injecting orthogonal unit vectors

\inf_{\Pi}\max_{i\in\lfloor d/2\rfloor}\left\|\mathbf{u}_{i}-\Pi[\mathbf{u}_{i}]\right\|\geq\frac{\varepsilon}{\sqrt{d}\cdot\sqrt{\lfloor d/2\rfloor}}>\frac{\varepsilon}{D_{\sf{c}}(\Gamma,\varepsilon)},

where the infimum is over all possible
\lfloor d/2\rfloor-1
projections. Since
\mathbf{u}_{i}\in\Gamma
, we obtain that

\inf_{\Pi}\max_{\mathbf{u}\in\Gamma}\left\|\mathbf{u}-\Pi[\mathbf{u}]\right\|>\frac{\varepsilon}{D_{\sf{c}}(\Gamma,\varepsilon)}.

Hence we have the following lower bound to the traditional Kolmogorov dimension:

D\left(\Gamma,\frac{\varepsilon}{D_{\sf{c}}(\Gamma,\varepsilon)}\right)\geq\lfloor d/2\rfloor-1\geq\frac{D_{\sf{c}}(\Gamma,\varepsilon)}{2}-2.

∎

\displaystyle=\frac{h/2\cdot\prod_{i=1}^{n}\exp\left(-(X_{j}^{i}-r)^{2}/2\right)}{(1-h)\cdot\prod_{i=1}^{n}\exp\left(-(X_{j}^{i})^{2}/2\right)+h/2\cdot\prod_{i=1}^{n}\exp\left(-(X_{j}^{i}-r)^{2}/2\right)+h/2\cdot\prod_{i=1}^{n}\exp\left(-(X_{j}^{i}+r)^{2}/2\right)}

\displaystyle=\frac{h/2\cdot\exp\left(r\cdot\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{j}^{i}\right)}{(1-h)\cdot\exp\left(r^{2}/2\right)+h/2\cdot\exp\left(r\cdot\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{j}^{i}\right)+h/2\cdot\exp\left(-r\cdot\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{j}^{i}\right)}.

(69)

Similarly, we get

\mathbb{P}(\theta_{j}=-1\mid\mathbf{X})=\frac{h/2\cdot\exp\left(-r\cdot\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{j}^{i}\right)}{(1-h)\cdot\exp\left(r^{2}/2\right)+h/2\cdot\exp\left(r\cdot\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{j}^{i}\right)+h/2\cdot\exp\left(-r\cdot\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{j}^{i}\right)}.

(70)

In the following, we use
[0,1]
-valued random variables
p_{j}(\mathbf{X})
and
q_{j}(\mathbf{X})
to denote

p_{j}(\mathbf{X})=\mathbb{P}(\theta_{j}=1\mid\mathbf{X})\quad\text{and}\quad q_{j}(\mathbf{X})=\mathbb{P}(\theta_{j}=-1\mid\mathbf{X}),\qquad\forall j\in[D].

We further notice that
\theta_{j}^{\prime}
and
\theta_{j}
are i.i.d. conditioned on
\mathbf{X}
. Hence we obtain

\displaystyle\hskip-14.22636pt\mathbb{P}(\theta_{j}=\theta_{j}^{\prime}\neq 0\mid X)\left(\exp(mr^{2})-1\right)+\mathbb{P}(\theta_{j}=-\theta_{j}^{\prime}\neq 0\mid X)\left(\exp(-mr^{2})-1\right)+1

\displaystyle=1+(p_{j}(\mathbf{X})^{2}+q_{j}(\mathbf{X})^{2})\left(\exp(mr^{2})-1\right)+2p_{j}(\mathbf{X})q_{j}(\mathbf{X})\left(\exp(-mr^{2})-1\right)

\displaystyle=1+\frac{(p_{j}(\mathbf{X})+q_{j}(\mathbf{X}))^{2}}{2}\cdot\left(\exp(mr^{2})+\exp(-mr^{2})-2\right)+\frac{(p_{j}(\mathbf{X})-q_{j}(\mathbf{X}))^{2}}{2}\cdot\left(\exp(mr^{2})-\exp(-mr^{2})\right).

(71)

Next using the AM-GM inequality we obtain that

(1-h)\cdot\exp\left(\frac{r^{2}}{2}\right)+\frac{h}{2}\cdot\exp\left(r\cdot\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{j}^{i}\right)+\frac{h}{2}\cdot\exp\left(-r\cdot\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{j}^{i}\right)\geq 1.

Bringing this back to
Eq. (
69
)
and
Eq. (
70
)
, we obtain that

\displaystyle\hskip-14.22636pt\left(p_{j}(\mathbf{X})+q_{j}(\mathbf{X})\right)^{2}\leq\frac{h^{2}}{4}\cdot\left(\exp\left(r\cdot\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{j}^{i}\right)+\exp\left(-r\cdot\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{j}^{i}\right)\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\text{and}\quad\left(p_{j}(\mathbf{X})-q_{j}(\mathbf{X})\right)^{2}\leq\frac{h^{2}}{4}\cdot\left(\exp\left(r\cdot\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{j}^{i}\right)-\exp\left(-r\cdot\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{j}^{i}\right)\right)^{2}.

(72)

We notice that according to the method of collecting samples,

X_{j}^{i}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{i.i.d.}}}{{\sim}}\mathcal{N}(\theta_{j},1)\quad\text{and}\quad\theta_{j}\sim(1-h)\cdot\delta_{0}+\frac{\eta}{2}\cdot\delta_{r}+\frac{\eta}{2}\cdot\delta_{-r},

which implies

\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{j}^{i}\sim(1-h)\cdot\mathcal{N}(0,n)+\frac{h}{2}\cdot\mathcal{N}(nr,n)+\frac{h}{2}\cdot\mathcal{N}(-nr,n).

Next, we notice that for Gaussian random variable
X\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu,\sigma)
, we have

\mathbb{E}[\exp(\alpha X)]=\exp\left(\alpha\mu+\frac{\alpha^{2}\sigma^{2}}{2}\right).

Bringing this back to
Eq. (
72
)
we obtain that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left(p_{j}(\mathbf{X})+q_{j}(\mathbf{X})\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{h^{2}}{4}\cdot\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(2r\cdot\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{j}^{i}\right)+2+\exp\left(-2r\cdot\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{j}^{i}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{h^{2}}{2}\exp\left(2r^{2}n\right)\cdot\left(1-h+\frac{h}{2}\exp\left(2r^{2}n\right)+\frac{h}{2}\exp\left(-2r^{2}n\right)\right)+\frac{h^{2}}{2}

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left(p_{j}(\mathbf{X})-q_{j}(\mathbf{X})\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{h^{2}}{4}\cdot\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(2r\cdot\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{j}^{i}\right)-2+\exp\left(-2r\cdot\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{j}^{i}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{h^{2}}{2}\exp\left(2r^{2}n\right)\cdot\left(1-h+\frac{h}{2}\exp\left(2r^{2}n\right)+\frac{h}{2}\exp\left(-2r^{2}n\right)\right)-\frac{h^{2}}{2}.

Hence if conditions

mr^{2}\leq 1\quad\text{and}\quad nr^{2}\leq 1

(73)

both hold, we have the following inequalities:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left(p_{j}(\mathbf{X})+q_{j}(\mathbf{X})\right)^{2}\right]\leq 30h^{2},

\displaystyle\qquad\mathbb{E}\left[\left(p_{j}(\mathbf{X})-q_{j}(\mathbf{X})\right)^{2}\right]\leq 30h^{2}r^{2}n,

\displaystyle\text{and}\qquad\exp(mr^{2})+\exp(-mr^{2})-2\leq 2m^{2}r^{4},

\displaystyle\qquad\exp(mr^{2})-\exp(-mr^{2})\leq 3mr^{2}.

Bringing them back to

71
)
, we obtain that

\displaystyle\hskip-14.22636pt\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{P}(\theta_{j}=\theta_{j}^{\prime}\neq 0\mid X)\left(\exp(mr^{2})-1\right)+\mathbb{P}(\theta_{j}=-\theta_{j}^{\prime}\neq 0\mid X)\left(\exp(-mr^{2})-1\right)+1\right]

\displaystyle\leq 1+30h^{2}m^{2}r^{4}+45h^{2}mnr^{4}\leq 1+75h^{2}mnr^{4},

where the last inequality uses the assumption

. Bring back to

\displaystyle\hskip-14.22636pt\chi^{2}(\mathbb{P}_{0,Z\mid X}\ \|\ \mathbb{P}_{1,Z\mid X}\mid\mathbb{P}_{0,X})]

\displaystyle\leq\prod_{j=1}^{d}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{P}(\theta_{j}=\theta_{j}^{\prime}\neq 0\mid X)\left(\exp(mr^{2})-1\right)+\mathbb{P}(\theta_{j}=-\theta_{j}^{\prime}\neq 0\mid X)\left(\exp(-mr^{2})-1\right)+1\right]-1

\displaystyle\leq(1+75h^{2}mnr^{4})^{d}-1.

Hence according to
Eq. (
67
)
, this implies that

\mathrm{TV}(\mathbb{P}_{0,XYZ},\mathbb{P}_{1,XYZ})^{2}\leq\sqrt{(1+75h^{2}mnr^{4})^{d}-1}.

(74)

Finally, we calculate the probability
\mu(\Gamma^{c})
and
\mu(B_{2}(\varepsilon))
