Service learning in dental education: enhancing student competence through an oral health training programme for children with autism spectrum disorder
Authors: Yina Cao, Liangyue Pang, Wenhua Meng, Ye Tao, Lixia Yu, Jingheng Liang, Yang Zhou, Huancai Lin, Yan Zhou, Qinghui Zhi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundService learning integrates community service with academic instruction, fostering reflective practice and enhancing professional competencies. In dental education, traditional curricula often emphasize technical skills over humanistic care, particularly for vulnerable populations such as children with autism spectrum disorder (ASD). This study designed and evaluated a structured service learning programme to explore its association with changes in dental students’ oral health knowledge, self-efficacy, and professional attitudes toward serving children, especially those with ASD.MethodsIn 2025, 102 fourth-year dental students participated in a single-group pretest-posttest study of a service learning programme focused on oral health for children with ASD. The programme included six modules: theoretical training, field investigation, data analysis, report writing, the development of educational materials, and health education delivery. Pre- and post-programme assessments measured changes in knowledge, self-efficacy, and professional attitudes via a 9-item knowledge test, a 6-item 5-point Likert scale, and a 6-item self-rated scale (0–100), respectively. Course feedback was collected via four dichotomous items. Statistical analyses included generalized estimating equations for knowledge test scores, Wilcoxon signed-rank tests for Attitudes and willingness, and paired t-tests for self-efficacy scores; a two-tailed p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.ResultsSignificant improvements were observed in students’ knowledge (correct understanding of Community periodontal index (CPI) probing increased from 65.7% to 92.9% (rate difference = 27.3%, 95%CI: 16.5 to 38.1, p < 0.001), self-efficacy (understanding of ASD oral hygiene rose from 32.27 ± 8.37 to 43.89 ± 2.73 (mean difference = 43.06, 95%CI: 37.01 to 49.12, p < 0.001), and professional empathy (93.5% of students reported enhanced empathy and responsibility). More than 87% of the students endorsed the expansion of this educational model.ConclusionFindings from this study suggest that service learning can be associated with the integration of knowledge, skills, and humanistic values in dental education. This model may help prepare students to address the oral health needs of underserved populations and may support the role transformation from learner to health advocate. Pending further validation with more rigorous designs, it offers a potential framework for training in special care dentistry.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-026-09647-0.

Introduction

Preventive dentistry is a core component of dental education, focusing on strategies to manage oral health at the individual and community levels [1]. However, conventional training often lacks an emphasis on the needs of special populations, such as individuals with autism spectrum disorder (ASD) [2].

ASD is an neurodevelopmental disorder characterized by persistent deficits in social communication and restricted, repetitive behaviors. Historically, ASD was classified into subtypes distinguished by learning ability and intellectual functioning [3], but the current Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (5th edition, DSM-5) adopts a dimensional severity classification but retains recognition of ASD’s profound heterogeneity in cognitive, learning and language abilities [4]. This has important implications for oral health care delivery. The latest epidemiological data indicate a continuous upward trend in ASD prevalence worldwide: the U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (2025) reported that approximately 1 in 31 8-year-old children were identified with ASD, a notable increase in recent years [5]. In China, estimates suggest an ASD prevalence of 0.7%, corresponding to ~ 1 in 143 children [6]. A recent systematic review and meta-analysis of 21 nationwide multicenter studies in China (2017–2023) revealed a weighted ASD prevalence of 1.47% among children and adolescents aged 0–18 years, with the highest prevalence (1.89%) observed in preschool children aged 3–6 years [7].

These epidemiological trends underscore the growing need for dental professionals to serve this population. Children with ASD often experience poor oral health because of sensory sensitivities, impaired motor skills, behavioural challenges, and difficulties in accessing professional care [8]. Studies report higher rates of dental caries and gingivitis among children with ASD than among their neurotypical peers [9, 10]. Moreover, 60%–80% of dentists express reluctance to treat patients with developmental disabilities [11]. Collectively, these factors highlight a critical gap in dental training and student preparedness for caring for children with ASD.

Service learning is an evidence-based pedagogical approach that integrally combines structured academic learning with meaningful community service and guided reflective practice, forming a reciprocal tripartite model that benefits learners, educational institutions, and community stakeholders [12]. Distinct from traditional volunteer work or clinical internships, service learning centers on intentional learning objectives, linking hands-on community service to curricular content to foster the application of theoretical knowledge in real-world contexts [13]. For health professions education, this approach has been widely validated to enhance not only professional knowledge and practical clinical competencies but also core non-technical attributes including empathy, cultural humility, social responsibility, and professional identity formation [14].

However, limited empirical evidence exists on the application of service learning in dental education specifically targeting children with ASD. To address this gap, the present study designed and evaluated a structured service learning programme implemented in a special school for children with ASD, which aimed to simultaneously improving their ASD-related oral health knowledge, clinical self-efficacy, and professional attitudes toward serving vulnerable groups. We hypothesized that this integrated model of skill training and on-site service would would be associated with positive changes in students’ competencies and attitudes, and may help address the gap between theoretical dental education and clinical practice for special populations. Pending further validation, this approach could serve as a potential model for special care dentistry training in dental education curricula.

Methods

Study design and participants

This study employed a single-group pretest-posttest design to valuate a service learning programme designed to enhance dental students’ competencies in addressing the oral health needs of children with ASD. A total of 102 fourth-year undergraduate dental students participated in the “Service Learning in Dental Education: Enhancing Students’ Holistic Competencies through Autism-Specific Oral Health Training” project. The study protocol was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of the Hospital of Stomatology, Sun Yat-sen University (Approval No. KOEC-2025-028-01). Informed consent was obtained from all guardians of the participants.

A sample size of 102 fourth-year dental students was determined via a power analysis using GPower 3.1. Based on a previous similar study [1], we set a large effect size of d = 0.5, a two-tailed significance level of alpha = 0.05, and power = 0.9. This analysis indicated a minimum required effective sample size of 45 participants. To account for a potential 10% attrition rate in the study implementation, the target sample size was adjusted to 50 participants. Our actual sample size of 102 was substantially higher than the adjusted target, which confirmed that the sample size was statistically adequate to detect meaningful pre-post differences in the primary outcomes (knowledge, self-efficacy, and professional attitudes).

The absence of a parallel control group substantially limits causal inference; therefore, findings are presented as associational evidence. This design was chosen because all students in the fixed curriculum received the same training, and withholding a potentially beneficial educational experience was not ethical. To clarify, the six-module service learning programme constitutes the educational intervention, while the pre-post assessments and analyses constitute the research methodology.

Theoretical teaching

The theoretical teaching component was delivered by faculty members from the Department of Preventive Dentistry, including two associate professors and one lecturer. Through didactic lectures, they covered fundamental concepts in oral epidemiology, standard techniques for oral health examination and questionnaire surveys, and the principles and methods of oral health education.

Field investigation

This component of the programme was conducted at Guangzhou Kanna School, a partner institution specializing in education and rehabilitation for children with ASD. A total of 102 fourth-year dental students, accompanied by two supervising lecturers, performed oral health examinations and administered questionnaires on-site. The participants included students from grades 3 to 8, with approximately 8 students assessed per grade, spanning an age range of 8 to 14 years.

ASD child selection criteria: A total of 64 children with ASD (8–14 years old, Grades 3–8) were recruited from Guangzhou Kangna School (a specialized ASD rehabilitation and education institution). The inclusion criteria for ASD children were: (1) a formal ASD diagnosis by a pediatric neurologist according to DSM-5 criteria; (2) aged 8–14 years (able to cooperate with basic oral examination and questionnaire survey); (3) no severe physical or mental comorbidities that prevent oral health assessment. Exclusion criteria: (1) incomplete ASD diagnosis; (2) inability to cooperate with any oral examination procedures.

Before the field activity, all students received targeted theoretical training on the oral health characteristics of children with ASD and standardized examination protocols. The oral health examination was performed via the checklist from the Fourth National Oral Health Survey and adhered to WHO standards. The examination involved screening for dental caries. During the process, one student acted as the examiner, while another acted as the recorder. Instructors provided immediate on-site guidance and confirmed any uncertain diagnoses.

The questionnaire, originally designed in Chinese by the instructors, assessed the children’s oral health knowledge and behaviours. It consisted of 9 items, including topics such as brushing habits, knowledge of cariogenic foods, the understanding of caries aetiology, and the efficacy of preventive measures such as pit and fissure sealants. One point was awarded for each correct answer. To accommodate the communication needs of children with ASD, the investigators explained the questions clearly without leading the responses, and a second student documented the answers.

This field investigation provided students with direct clinical and communicative experience in a special needs setting, fulfilling the dual objectives of service learning and reinforcing the cooperative agreement.

Data analysis and reporting

The dental students conducted oral screenings and administered questionnaires. They were then responsible for data entry and subsequent analysis, which included calculating caries rates and evaluating questionnaire scores.

Educational material development

Based on their data analysis, each student group submitted a survey report. Subsequently, the groups developed and delivered oral health education sessions tailored to children with ASD. The educational materials were specifically designed to be structured and accessible, featuring visual aids such as structured sequencing charts for oral examination and step-by-step guides for the Fones technique. The groups presented their work, including a final PowerPoint presentation, to instructors in the Department of Preventive Dentistry. These instructors then provided scoring and formative feedback on the materials and presentations through an in-person evaluation session.

Health education practice

A subset of dental students, accompanied by two instructors, conducted an on-site oral health education session at Guangzhou Kanna School, a specialized institution for children with autism. The session employed ASD-friendly teaching methods, including a structured PowerPoint presentation, storytelling, and interactive games. To facilitate skill acquisition, the students provided individualized instruction using tooth models for hands-on practice. To promote the generalization of learned skills, children were encouraged to take home and display the structured brushing sequence charts in their bathrooms so that they could serve as a visual prompt for daily routine establishment.

Structured reflection and evaluation

A multi-method assessment strategy was implemented to comprehensively evaluate the impact of the programme, with all data collected via online surveys (Questionnaire Star) administered anonymously immediately before (pre-) and after (post-) the intervention. The assessment instruments, including a 9-item knowledge test, a 6-item attitudes and willingness scale, a 6-item self-efficacy scale and and a course feedback questionnaire consisting of four dichotomous items, were developed specifically for this study (see supplementary file). We validated the content through review by 1 ASD rehabilitation specialist and 3 dental education experts (content validity index [CVI] = 0.92, ≥ 0.80 indicating excellent content validity). Internal consistency (reliability) was calculated using the current study data and is reported below for multi-item scales.

Operational definitions of key constructs are as follows: “knowledge” was measured via a 9-item multiple‑choice test; “attitudes and willingness” were measured via a 6-item Likert scale (with lower scores indicating more positive attitudes); “self‑efficacy” was measured via a 0–100 visual analogue scale (higher scores = greater confidence); “empathy and professional responsibility” were measured via two specific dichotomous items on the course feedback questionnaire (e.g., “This service learning teaching method enhanced my empathy and sense of responsibility”).

Knowledge test [15]

A 9-item multiple-choice test was developed to assess students’ grasp of core professional knowledge. The test covered key areas, including the application of the community periodontal index (CPI), particularly probing force; the criteria for caries diagnosis and recording (e.g., D threshold and tooth numbering); the calculation of oral health indices such as the caries fillable ratio; and recommended oral hygiene practices, including brushing duration and techniques of the Fones technique. The score was reported as the percentage of correct answers. Reliability: Cronbach’s α = 0.80.

Attitudes and willingness scale [16]

Students’ perceptions and professional inclinations were measured via a 6-item questionnaire with a 5-point Likert scale (1 = very knowledgeable, 5 = not knowledgeable at all). The items evaluated were self-assessed understanding of the aetiology and prevention of dental caries and periodontal disease, confidence in identifying risks for oral diseases, the willingness to perform oral examinations, willingness to provide basic oral health information to patients, and the understanding of community-based oral health screening procedures. A lower post-test score indicated a positive shift in attitudes and increased willingness. Reliability: Cronbach’s α = 0.85.

Self-efficacy assessment [17]

Students’ confidence in performing project-specific tasks was measured via six items, each self-rated on a visual analogue scale from 0 (not confident at all) to 100 (extremely confident). The following competencies were assessed: the understanding of the oral health status of children with ASD; the perceived value of and confidence in providing oral health education to children with ASD; the ability to perform a standardized oral examination; the ability to write a survey report; and the ability to produce oral health education materials. Reliability: Cronbach’s α = 0.88.

Course feedback questionnaire [1]

Upon project completion, students provided feedback on their overall learning experience via four dichotomous items (yes/no) (1 = yes). The questionnaire assessed the following: the preference for the service learning model over traditional teaching methods; the perceived depth of learning regarding course content; the increase in empathy and sense of professional responsibility; and the willingness to recommend this teaching method for future students. The results are reported as the percentage of students who strongly agreed with each statement.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) and R software (version 4.4.1). Knowledge test scores (correct response rates) were compared using generalized estimating equations (GEE) to account for potential correlations in repeated measures (pre-post) and ensure robust estimates. GEE was chosen because it is appropriate for repeated binary outcomes, accounts for within-subject correlation, and provides effect size estimates (odds ratios with 95% confidence intervals), which are more informative than p values alone. Attitudes and willingness Likert scale data, which were non-normally distributed, were analyzed using Wilcoxon signed-rank tests to compare pre-post differences. Self-efficacy scores (0–100 scale), which were normally distributed, were analyzed using paired t-tests to compare pre-post differences. Course feedback data were reported as percentages of students selecting “yes.“A two-tailed p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Understanding of proper probing force for the community periodontal index (CPI), which rose from 65.7% to 92.9% (rate difference = 27.3%, 95%CI: 16.5 to 38.1, p < 0.001), and the knowledge of recommended brushing duration, which dramatically increased from 38.4% to 96.0% (rate difference = 57.6%, 95%CI: 47.8 to 67.3, p < 0.001). Furthermore, students’ comprehension of key calculation metrics, such as the numerator for the “caries fillable ratio”, improved from 72.7% to 96.0% (rate difference = 23.2%, 95%CI: 14.0 to 32.4, p < 0.001). The correct technique for brushing the lingual surfaces of posterior teeth also saw a significant gain, rising from 44.4% to 99.0% (rate difference = 54.5%, 95%CI: 44.3 to 64.7, p < 0.001).

Several items, including the specific brushing techniques for buccal and labial surfaces, which initially had high baseline scores (> 65.7%), were further increased to above 96.0%. while two items with pre-training correct response rates above 94% (caries recording criteria) showed slight gains or maintained near-perfect rates without statistical significance.

Objective changes in self-efficacy

Students’ self-efficacy, i.e., the confidence in their abilities, was measured via a 0–100 point self-rating scale across six competencies. Mean values, standard deviations (SD), mean differences, and 95% CIs are presented in Table 4.

Self-rated understanding of the oral health status of children with autism, which rose dramatically from 32.28 ± 27.86 to 75.34 ± 17.77 (mean difference = 43.06, 95%CI: 37.01 to 49.12, p < 0.001). Confidence in producing oral health education materials increased from 58.64 ± 23.56 to 81.33 ± 15.17 (mean difference = 22.70, 95%CI: 18.32 to 27.07, p < 0.001), and the ability to conduct a standardized oral examination improved from 63.47 ± 24.23 to 83.56 ± 14.67 (mean difference = 20.09, 95%CI: 15.55 to 24.63, p < 0.001).

Discussion

The findings of this single-group pretest-posttest study indicate that participation in a structured service learning programme centred on the oral health needs of children with autism spectrum disorder (ASD) was associated with improvements in dental students’ professional knowledge, practical competencies, self-efficacy, and empathic attitudes. Given the study design limitations, these observed changes cannot be definitively attributed to the intervention alone.

Significant pre-post differences in students’ performance on the 9-item knowledge test were observed, which may reflect enhanced transfer from theoretical knowledge to practical application. Key clinical concepts, such as the appropriate probing force for the community periodontal index (CPI), saw correct response rates jump from 65.7% to 92.9% (mean difference = 27.3%, 95%CI: 16.5 to 38.1, p < 0.001). Similarly, the understanding of fundamental public health metrics, such as the calculation of the ‘caries fillable ratio,’ increased from 72.7% to 96.0% (mean difference = 23.2,%, 95%CI:14.0 to 32.4, p < 0.001). These changes are consistent with the idea that a “learning-through-practice” approach-enacted through real-world oral health screenings and data analysis at a specialized ASD school-may help students to solidify abstract concepts into applicable clinical reasoning skills [18, 19], a core advantage of this approach that is not limited to ASD care settings. Conducting these screenings in an authentic special care setting further added contextual value, allowing students to gain ASD-specific clinical experience. However, ceiling effects were observed in knowledge items, where post-intervention correct response rates exceeded 95%. Ceiling effects occur when a test is insufficiently challenging to distinguish differences in participants’ true abilities, which may have underestimated the full magnitude of knowledge gains in these specific domains.

Beyond knowledge acquisition, participation was associated with changes in students’ professional identity formation and empathic development. The largest observed change was observed in students’ self-rated understanding of the oral health status of children with ASD, which rose markedly from 32.28 ± 27.86 to 75.34 ± 17.77 (mean difference = 43.06, 95%CI: 37.01 to 49.12, p < 0.001). This finding suggests a potential reduction in initial knowledge barriers and misconceptions. Furthermore, 93.5% of students self-reported that the experience enhanced their empathy and sense of professional responsibility. It is important to acknowledge that these self-reported outcomes may be subject to social desirability bias and the Hawthorne effect, as students may have been inclined to report positive changes in response to their participation in the programme.

This direct, meaningful engagement with a vulnerable population aligns with global calls for health care providers who are equipped to deliver truly person-centred care, moving beyond technical proficiency to genuine human connection. A systematic review demonstrated that behavioural strategies to improve oral health in individuals with ASD are most effective when they actively engage both clinical professionals and family members. This collaborative model not only achieves the primary health objective but also concurrently fosters a greater sense of responsibility and empathy among trainees [20]—an outcome that aligns with the established understanding of the profound value of practice-based learning. A significant challenge in caring for children with autism is their often-limited cooperation during medical procedures, which contributes to the complexity and stress of health care delivery [21]. When trainees encounter these specific challenges, direct interactive experiences emerge as the most effective method for cultivating empathy and a sense of responsibility [22], consistent with the self-reported findings in this study.

Participation was also associated with increased self-reported social responsibility and a role transition from student to health advocate. This is supported by the significant positive shift in students’ willingness to provide basic oral health information to patients (median difference = -0.50, p < 0.05) and increased confidence in producing ASD-friendly oral health education materials (mean difference = 22.70, 95%CI: 18.32 to 27.07, p < 0.001). Additionally, students demonstrated marked gains in their ability to perform standardized oral examinations for this special population (mean difference = 20.09, 95%CI: 15.55 to 24.63, p < 0.001), reflecting perceived growth as practice-ready oral health advocates. Studies underscore the efficacy of practice-based learning that involves vulnerable groups may foster greater commitment to social justice. A relevant study on medical education demonstrated that deliberate interaction with specific populations, including families affected by autism, led to a marked increase in students’ awareness of the socio-cultural determinants of health [23]. Creating and delivering ASD-friendly health education appears to have transformed students from passive learners into active agents of community health, potentially instilling a commitment to addressing health disparities that is likely to extend into their future careers [21, 24].

Notably, the field investigation presented distinctive challenges that enriched the learning process. When conducting oral examinations, students encountered difficulties with some children’s responsiveness to verbal instructions. For instance, simple commands such as “open your mouth” were not always understood, requiring students to demonstrate the action visually—an approach that aligns with the recognized strength of visual processing over abstract verbal cues in many individuals with ASD. Similarly, during questionnaire administration, students needed to provide individualized guidance and rephrase questions to accommodate varying levels of comprehension. These experiences not only highlighted the communication barriers faced by this population but also underscored the need for adapted methodologies, subsequently informing the development of structured visual guides [20]. Successfully overcoming these challenges through adaptive communication and behavioural guidance techniques appeared to provide students with profound professional fulfilment, reinforcing their clinical competence and problem-solving abilities [25].

These observed improvements can be best interpreted through Kolb’s Experiential Learning Theory [26], with core Service-Learning pedagogy [27–29] providing the overarching structural framework that enables the complete learning cycle. This programme was intentionally designed to align with this cycle: students first gained hands-on clinical and communicative experience through on-site oral health screenings (concrete experience); then processed challenges and emotional responses in structured group reflections (reflective observation); integrated these experiences with academic knowledge of ASD characteristics and evidence-based practices in post-session lectures (abstract conceptualization); and finally applied their learning by developing and delivering ASD-tailored oral health education materials (active experimentation). Service-learning anchored this entire process by integrating academic learning and community service, explaining the observed improvements across knowledge, skills, self-efficacy, and empathic attitudes.

This study has several important limitations that must be considered when interpreting the findings: the single-group pretest-posttest design without a control group limits causal inference, as observed improvements may be partially attributed to concurrent educational experiences, maturation effects, or regression to the mean rather than the intervention alone; all outcome measures were self-reported, which introduces potential social desirability bias and the Hawthorne effect, where participants may alter their responses to align with perceived expectations; the study was conducted at one dental school in China, which may limit the generalizability of findings to other educational contexts or populations; several knowledge test items showed very high post-intervention correct rates (> 95%), which may have underestimated the true magnitude of knowledge gains due to ceiling effects; and the study only assessed immediate post-intervention outcomes, so the long-term retention of acquired knowledge, skills, and attitudinal changes remains unknown. Future studies should incorporate randomized controlled trial designs to establish causal relationships, include objective performance measures in addition to self-reports, conduct multi-centre investigations to enhance generalizability, and implement longitudinal follow-up to assess the durability of intervention effects.

Conclusion

Findings from this single-group pretest-posttest study indicate that participation in an ASD-focused service learning programme was associated with improvements in students’ knowledge scores (e.g., correct CPI probing force increased from 65.7% to 92.9%, p < 0.001), self-efficacy (e.g., understanding of ASD oral health status increased by 43.06 points, p < 0.001), and professional empathy (93.5% of students reported enhanced empathy). These preliminary findings suggest the programme may enhance students’ ability to serve children with ASD and potentially provide a scalable model for special care dentistry training.
