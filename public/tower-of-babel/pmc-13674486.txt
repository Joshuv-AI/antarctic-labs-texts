Methyltransferase RsmB chaperones E. coli ribosome assembly by orchestrating the recruitment of ribosomal proteins
Authors: Qilan Wei, Sarah A. Woodson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Ribosomal RNA modification enzymes increase cellular fitness, but it’s little known whether this benefit arises from rRNA modification or a chaperone function of the modification enzyme. Here, we show that E. coli methyltransferase RsmB chaperones the initial recruitment of ribosomal proteins to the 16S 3′ domain rRNA, a key step in assembly of the 30S subunit. Real-time single-molecule assembly experiments show that the RsmB product, 16S m5C967, has little effect on the rRNA affinity for ribosomal proteins. Instead, RsmB facilitates stable recruitment of primary protein uS7 during transcription while blocking the addition of secondary protein uS19. Completion of the methyltransferase cycle promotes RsmB release, allowing assembly to continue. Dimethyl sulfate footprinting of trapped RsmB-rRNA complexes revealed that RsmB restructures a critical rRNA helix junction. Our results illustrate how an RNA modification enzyme favors the proper sequence of protein addition by temporarily reshaping the energy landscape of co-transcriptional rRNA folding.

INTRODUCTION

Natural chemical modifications are prevalent across all classes of RNA molecules, regulating their functions and lifetimes (1). In ribosomal RNA (rRNA), modifications are concentrated in functional regions of the ribosome, such as the decoding center or peptidyl transferase center, underscoring their importance in translation (2). These modifications are reported to stabilize rRNA structure (3, 4) and modulate the binding of tRNA, mRNA, or antibiotics (5–8). Although modification enzymes are widely assumed to benefit cells by installing these chemical modifications, recent findings revealed that rRNA modification enzymes also chaperone ribosome assembly (9) by locally altering the rRNA conformation (10, 11). For example, the methyltransferase RsmA (also known as KsgA) facilitates proper maturation of 30S subunits (30S hereafter) by partially disassembling interactions within the decoding site of the inactive subunits at a late stage of 30S assembly (12). RsmC methyltransferase was reported to accelerate refolding of a 16S rRNA helix in 30S assembly intermediates (11). These findings raise the possibility that some RNA modification enzymes primarily act as assembly factors. However, there is little information on how modification enzymes work on the nascent rRNA and how they cooperate with ribosomal proteins (r-proteins) that must also bind the rRNA during transcription.

Here, we investigate how a small subunit RNA methyltransferase, RsmB, acts on early 30S assembly intermediates to facilitate the recruitment of r-proteins to the 16S rRNA. E. coli RsmB belongs to the superfamily of S-adenosyl-L-methionine (SAM)-dependent 5-methylcytosine RNA methyltransferases (13) and specifically modifies C967 in helix (h) 31 of 16S rRNA (16S h31 hereafter) (14) (Fig. 1A). The structure of RsmB shows that it contains two RNA-binding domains and a methyltransferase (MTase) domain (15). Two conserved cysteine residues within the active site of the MTase domain, 325Cys and 375Cys, participate in the methyl transfer reaction (16) (Fig. 1B).

Previous studies showed that deletion of E. coli rsmB leads to the accumulation of immature 17S rRNA (17), implicating it in rRNA processing and ribosome assembly. RsmB was able to methylate the 16S rRNA before the binding of r-proteins uS7 and uS19 (hereafter referred to as S7 and S19), but not later complexes (18), indicating that it acts only on early assembly intermediates. This likely occurs during transcription when the rRNA begins to fold and recruit the r-proteins (19, 20). By contrast, methylation of the neighboring G966 by RsmD requires binding of S19, suggesting that each modification enzyme acts at a specific stage of assembly (18). In addition, the site of RsmB modification, m5C967, lies at the tip of 16S h31 that forms part of the 30S P-site. This region of the 16S/18S rRNA was shown to be inherently prone to misfolding and dependent on chaperones (21, 22).

To dissect whether RsmB aids 30S assembly through the installation of m5C967 or as a chaperone (or in both ways), we examined the binding of r-proteins and RsmB during and after transcription. Surprisingly, we found that m5C967 itself has little effect on r-protein affinity. Instead, we found that RsmB binding facilitates the recruitment of S7 while inhibiting the binding of S19. These findings suggested that RsmB stabilizes a metastable conformation of the rRNA, which was further supported by dimethyl sulfate (DMS) footprinting of 16S 3′ domain rRNA in complex with inactive RsmB and SAM. Overall, our findings support a model in which RsmB coordinates the sequential addition of r-proteins to the 16S 3′ domain rRNA, thereby enhancing the efficiency of ribosome assembly.

RESULTS

Inactive RsmB dominantly inhibits cell growth

Since rsmB is non-essential (23) and rsmB deletion causes little change in growth on solid rich media, we tested the functional importance of the RsmB methyltransferase through a competitive growth assay between ∆rsmB strain JW3250 and the parental wild-type strain BW25113 (24). Over multiple cycles of culture expansion and dilution, the ∆rsmB strain was gradually outcompeted by the wild-type strain, resulting in a 25–50 times greater number of viable wild-type vs. ∆rsmB cells by the fourth cycle (Fig. 1C). This result indicated that deletion of rsmB slowed cell growth in culture, consistent with a perturbation to ribosome synthesis (25).

To determine whether reduced fitness was due to the loss of m5C967 or the loss of RsmB enzyme, we tested whether overexpression of an inactive mutant, RsmBC375A, could rescue the slow growth of the ∆rsmB strain. Alanine substitution of C375 in the methyltransferase site (Fig. 1B) retains RNA binding affinity but impairs catalytic activity (16). Therefore, rescue by RsmBC375A would suggest that RsmB has a function in ribosome assembly that is independent of its methylation activity.

To test our hypothesis, the wild-type and ∆rsmB strains were transformed with pBAD plasmids overexpressing either RsmBWT or RsmBC375A, or with empty vector. Comparable levels of overexpression were confirmed by Western blotting (fig. S1A). The lack of methylation activity of the mutant enzyme was confirmed by methylation assays (fig. S1B). Unexpectedly, RsmBC375A overexpression resulted in fewer viable colonies compared to cells harboring pBAD-RsmBWT or empty vector (Fig. 1D). Since RsmBC375A overexpression inhibited growth of both ∆rsmB and wild-type strains (Fig. 1D), we inferred that RsmB has a function beyond the synthesis of m5C967 that is still linked to its methyltransferase site. One explanation for this dominant negative effect is that inactive RsmBC375A becomes trapped on 16S h31, disrupting subsequent steps of ribosome assembly. We confirmed this explanation in binding experiments described below.

Modified C967 does not affect ribosomal protein affinity

To examine whether the m5C967 modification installed by RsmB directly affects protein recruitment during 30S assembly, we compared the binding of r-proteins to methylated and unmethylated in vitro transcripts corresponding to the 3′ domain of E. coli 16S rRNA (U921-A1396). Assembly of the 16S 3′ domain rRNA involves the binding of 8 r-proteins in a hierarchy that arises from progressive organization of the rRNA structure (26). As RsmB is only active on the early assembly intermediates (18), we focused on binding of the primary assembly protein, S7, which is the first r-protein to associate with the 3′ domain rRNA in the assembly map (27), and two secondary assembly proteins: S9, which directly interacts with m5C967 (28), and S19, whose binding inhibits methylation by RsmB (18) (Fig. 2A).

The dissociation constants (Kd) of r-proteins from unmethylated and methylated RNA were measured by single-molecule fluorescence correlation spectroscopy (smFCS) using a confocal microscope. This single-molecule method side-stepped complications of non-specific binding and aggregation when the rRNA was titrated with excess r-proteins. Complete methylation of RNA substrates by purified RsmB was confirmed via bisulfite conversion and primer extension (fig. S1, C and D).

As the molecular weight of the 3′ domain rRNA is 10 times larger than that of the r-proteins, titration of Cy5-labeled r-proteins with 3′ domain rRNA substantially decreased their diffusion coefficients (Fig. 2B), allowing the fraction of bound protein to be obtained from the autocorrelation functions (29) (Fig. 2C and fig. S2). To measure the Kd values of secondary assembly r-proteins S9 and S19, we titrated them with 3′ domain rRNA in the presence of S7 and S19 or S9, respectively (see table S1).

Unexpectedly, the results showed that S7, S9, and S19 had similar affinities for unmethylated and methylated RNA, as illustrated for S7 in Fig. 2C (Kd = 1.14 ± 0.10 nM for unmethylated RNA versus 1.37 ± 0.15 nM for methylated RNA). S9 also showed comparable affinity for unmethylated and methylated rRNA•S7•S19 (Fig. 2D and fig. S2A) in 330 mM KCl and 20 mM MgCl2 that minimized its non-specific binding (table S1). Methylation of C967 slightly increased the affinity of S19 for rRNA•S7•S9 with Kd = 41.3 ± 0.7 nM, versus 56.3 ± 1.5 nM for unmethylated RNA (Fig. 2D and fig. S2B). Overall, we found that m5C967 minimally influenced the stability of r-protein interactions with 3′ domain rRNA, likely because C967 lies on the surface of the 30S and m5C does not affect Watson–Crick base pairing or the RNA backbone.

Incomplete methylation traps RsmB on the rRNA

Since m5C967 does not enhance r-protein binding, RsmB itself may play an important role in ribosome assembly. We first measured the affinity of 3′ domain rRNA for RsmBWT-Cy5 without SAM in low-salt restart buffer (40 mM NH4Cl and 5 mM MgCl2) using smFCS. We used a low-salt buffer for these reactions because apo RsmB binding in high-salt buffer is too weak to quantify by smFCS. The activity of the labeled enzyme was confirmed by methylation assays (fig. S1B). We found that the binding of RsmBWT alone was unaffected by methylation of C967, with Kd = 1.20 ± 0.14 nM for unmethylated RNA compared to 1.18 ± 0.11 nM for methylated RNA in low salt (fig. S2C).

We next asked whether RsmB binding to RNA depends on the cofactor. Since RsmBWT modifies the unmethylated RNA in the presence of SAM, we could not measure binding to the RNA substrate directly. Therefore, to assess whether RsmB forms a stable substrate complex with SAM and unmethylated 3′ domain rRNA, we estimated the affinity of the inactive mutant RsmBC375A for the rRNA plus 100 μM SAM. Because 375Cys serves as the catalytic nucleophile (15), RsmBC375A is expected to bind substrates but not form the covalent enzyme-RNA intermediate (16). Without SAM, the affinity of RsmBC375A for the 3′ domain rRNA was like that of RsmBWT and was unaffected by C967 methylation (fig. S2D). In the presence of SAM, however, the affinity of RsmBC375A for unmethylated RNA greatly increased, with an apparent Kd = 15.4 ± 1.6 nM in high salt (200 mM KCl, 10 mM MgCl2), compared to Kd ∼ 249.0 ± 16.3 nM for methylated RNA in the same high-salt buffer (Fig. 3A and fig. S2E). We used high-salt buffer for these reactions because it is compatible with 30S assembly.

To assess the stability of the RsmB-product complex, we measured the affinity of RsmB for the 3′ domain rRNA in the presence of S-adenosyl-L-homocysteine (SAH). SAH also stabilized complexes with methylated RNA (Kd = 48.7 ± 3.5 nM for RsmBC375A and 69.1 ± 4.8 nM for RsmBWT; Fig. 3A and fig. S2F). However, both WT and inactive product complexes were less stable than the inactive mimic of the substrate complex formed by RsmBC375A with unmethylated RNA and SAM (Fig. 3A and fig. S2G). In E. coli, the concentration of SAM is >100-fold higher than the concentration of SAH (30), so SAH exchange for SAM after methyl transfer should further destabilize interactions between RsmB and its methylated RNA product (Kd ∼ 250–300 nM; fig. S2, E and G). Taken together, we infer that RsmBWT forms a stable substrate complex with unmethylated rRNA and SAM that becomes unstable once methylation is complete, explaining its poor affinity for unmethylated and methylated rRNA in the presence of SAM (fig. S2E).

Single-molecule fluorescence microscopy reveals RsmB binding kinetics

To investigate whether rRNA methylation affects the RsmB binding kinetics, we visualized the real-time binding of RsmB to the 3′ domain rRNA by single-molecule total internal reflection fluorescence (smTIRF) microscopy (Fig. 3B). Cy3-labeled 3′ domain rRNA was thermally refolded and immobilized on passivated quartz slides via a biotinylated DNA oligomer hybridized to the 5′ end of the RNA. RsmB-Cy5 (2.5 nM) was injected into the slide chamber, and binding events were detected by co-localization of the Cy5 and Cy3 signals (Fig. 3B).

RsmBWT interacted with the immobilized 3′ domain rRNA frequently and reversibly under the conditions of our experiments, with shorter lifetimes in lower Mg2+ concentrations and fewer binding events in higher KCl concentrations (fig. S3). In the absence of SAM, RsmBC375A and RsmBWT had similar binding residence times, consistent with the similar affinities of the apo-enzyme for the RNA (Fig. 3C). The distributions of their residence times (WT and C375A) yielded dissociation lifetimes, τ1 = 9.4 ± 1.4 s and τ2 = 1.6 ± 0.1 s, corresponding to medium-stable binding and unstable RsmB binding that is presumably non-specific. The observed residence times of RsmBWT were unchanged by the addition of SAM. However, the binding of RsmBC375A became much more stable with SAM (Fig. 3C and table S2), owing to a new population of long-lived (τ ∼ 90 s) complexes that replaced some of the medium-stable complexes (Fig. 3D). Therefore, the smTIRF results supported the conclusion that inactive RsmBC375A plus SAM becomes trapped on the 3′ domain rRNA, whereas RsmBWT dissociates rapidly.

To further test whether RsmBC375A becomes trapped on the rRNA in the presence of SAM, we performed a competition assay in which immobilized 3′ domain rRNA plus 100 μM SAM was pre-incubated with RsmBC375A or RsmBWT and then challenged with RsmBWT-Cy5 in the presence of unlabeled RsmB (C375A or WT). From the co-localization of Cy3 and Cy5 intensity, we calculated the average occupancy of RsmBWT-Cy5 over the population of immobilized RNA molecules over the total recording time. The occupancy of RsmBWT-Cy5 was significantly lower when the RNA was pre-incubated with RsmBC375A than with RsmBWT (0.07 vs 0.23; Fig. 3E), indicating that RsmBC375A inhibits binding (and modification) of the RNA by RsmBWT. These results help explain the dominant growth inhibition from overexpression of RsmBC375A and support the idea that completion of the methyl transfer reaction accelerates RsmB dissociation from the 3′ domain rRNA.

RsmB accelerates recruitment of primary assembly protein S7

As methylation of C967 made little difference to the stability of r-protein binding, we hypothesized that RsmB may help 30S assembly by acting as a chaperone to improve the likelihood of productive r-protein binding. r-protein S7 nucleates 3′ domain assembly (27) by stabilizing a complex junction between 16S helices h28, h29, h30 and h43 (31, 32). Because S7 recognizes a different helix junction (h28-h29-h43) than RsmB (h30-h31-h32), any interaction between the two proteins would presumably occur through their effects on the rRNA conformation. First, we verified by electrophoretic mobility shift assay (EMSA) that RsmB and S7 can bind the 3′ domain rRNA at the same time (fig. S4A). Next, we visualized the dynamics of S7 binding to the 3′ domain rRNA by smTIRF co-localization (Fig. 4A), as described for RsmB above. We observed both transient and long-lived co-localization of S7 with the 3′ domain rRNA, indicating that S7 forms both stable and unstable complexes with the rRNA, as previously reported (20) (Fig. 4B). The distribution of residence times yielded S7 dissociation lifetimes of τ1 = 41.8 ± 10.0 s and τ2 = 0.9 ± 0.1 s for stable and unstable complexes, respectively (Fig. 4C). Because r-proteins form more stable complexes with the properly folded rRNA (33), we assumed that long-lived binding events represent more native-like complexes. We defined a threshold for long-lived complexes of 5 s, which was five times the 1-s residence time of the unstable complexes.

As RsmBWT binds RNA weakly in the high-salt buffers suitable for ribosome assembly (fig. S3C), we used a two-stage reaction to study the effect of RsmB binding on S7 recruitment (Fig. 4A). In this assay, immobilized Cy3-labeled refolded 3′ domain rRNA in low-salt methylation buffer was pre-incubated with 5 nM unlabeled RsmBWT without SAM. In this way, we were able to examine whether RsmB-rRNA interactions, rather than methylation, facilitate S7 binding. After RsmBWT had an opportunity to interact with the rRNA, 0.5 nM S7-Cy5 in high-salt assembly buffer (150 mM KCl and 10 mM MgCl2) was added, and S7-Cy5 co-localization events were recorded. Without RsmB pre-treatment, 35% of the 3′ domain rRNA transcripts were stably (≥ 5 s) bound by S7, and the average recorded occupancy for all molecules was 0.13. When pre-incubated with RsmBWT, 58% of rRNAs formed a stable complex with S7, and the occupancy increased to 0.27 (Fig. 4, D and E). The true S7 occupancies are somewhat higher (∼0.23 and ∼0.48) after accounting for S7 labeling efficiency (see Methods). Pre-incubation with RsmBC375A produced similarly improved S7 binding effects (Fig. 4, D and E). Treatment with either enzyme had no effect on S7 binding lifetimes (table S3), indicating that RsmB does not directly stabilize S7-rRNA interactions. Instead, treatment with RsmB increased the population of complexes with the longest lifetimes (a1 in table S3). Thus, RsmB appears to shift the rRNA molecules into structures that are competent to stably add S7.

RsmB favors a metastable rRNA structure that binds S7

We next examined whether RsmB must be present on the RNA to chaperone the binding of S7. We found that RsmBWT-Cy5 dissociated quickly (∼2 s) upon switching to high-salt assembly buffer (fig. S4, B and C). Since most S7 binding events (τon, slow = 79 s at 0.5 nM) in the two-stage reaction occur after RsmB dissociation (τoff = 2.1 ± 0.2 s; fig. S4C), RsmB need not remain on the 3′ domain rRNA to facilitate S7 recruitment. We also confirmed by smFCS that S7 and RsmB do not bind each other in the absence of RNA (fig. S4D), making it unlikely that RsmB recruits S7 through a direct interaction between the two proteins. This is consistent with the idea that RsmB indirectly promotes stable S7 binding by acting on the RNA.

To test how long the effect of RsmB on the RNA persists after RsmB dissociates, 3′ domain rRNA pre-treated with RsmB was allowed to stand for 10 min in high-salt assembly buffer before S7 was added to the reaction. In this case, only 37% of rRNA molecules formed a stable complex with S7, similar to the no-RsmB control (fig. S4E). This result indicated that RsmB favors a metastable RNA conformation that must be captured by S7 before or shortly after RsmB dissociates from the RNA. By contrast, the fraction of transcripts stably bound by S7 at 10 min remained high if S7 was added to the RNA immediately after pre-incubation with RsmB (fig. S4F). This outcome suggests that frequent S7 interactions can maintain the rRNA in a structure that is competent to bind S7, even after RsmB can no longer bind. Altogether, these results imply that the likelihood of forming native-like assembly intermediates is greatest when RsmB and S7 both associate with the rRNA within a limited time interval (∼1 min in our conditions).

RsmB facilitates S7 binding during rRNA transcription

We next asked whether RsmB can chaperone the recruitment of S7 to the 16S 3′ domain rRNA during transcription, thereby preventing the rRNA from becoming trapped in misfolded structures. In an established single-molecule co-transcriptional assembly assay (19), stalled transcription elongation complexes (TECs) were immobilized on the slides through a biotinylated DNA oligomer hybridized to the 5′ end of the nascent RNA, and transcription was restarted with the addition of all four NTPs (Fig. 5A). When T7 RNA polymerase encountered the Cy3 fluorophore attached to the 3′ end of the DNA, the Cy3 intensity increased sharply due to protein-induced fluorescent enhancement (PIFE) (34), indicating the end of transcription (Fig. 5B). Binding of RsmB-Cy5 or S7-Cy5 to the nascent RNA was monitored through co-localization of the fluorescence signals as before.

Many RsmBWT-Cy5 binding events were observed before the end of transcription (Fig. 5B and fig. S5A), indicating that complete transcription of the 3′ domain rRNA is not required for RsmB binding. As complexes formed during transcription had even shorter residence times than complexes with refolded RNA (fig. S5B and table S2), some structure may be needed for specific recognition of 16S h31. RsmBC375A-Cy5 also associated with the rRNA during transcription and formed much more stable complexes with the nascent rRNA in the presence of SAM (Fig. 5C and table S2), in agreement with the equilibrium binding results (Fig. 3C). The occupancy of RsmB with non-restarted TECs was ≤ 4% of the occupancy of restarted TECs, indicating that RsmB does not appreciably bind the DNA template (fig. S5, C and D).

To study the effect of RsmB on S7 binding during transcription, excess RsmBC375A and SAM were added together with 2.5 nM S7-Cy5 and NTPs to ensure sufficient RsmB interactions with transcripts in the high-salt buffer needed for 30S assembly. Since S7 interacts with residues close to the end of the 3′ domain rRNA, most S7 binding events occurred after transcription was complete, as expected (Fig. 5D) (20). Little S7 co-localization was observed when transcription was not restarted (fig. S5E), indicating that co-localization reflects binding to the transcript. In the absence of RsmB, only 12% nascent transcripts formed a stable complex (≥ 5 s) with S7 during the 5-min video. This proportion increased with RsmBC375A concentration, until 29% rRNAs were stably complexed with S7 in 500 nM RsmBC375A. Excess RsmBWT (500 nM) plus SAH, which also form stable complexes with unmethylated RNA (fig. S2F), achieved a comparable improvement in the recruitment of S7-Cy5 (Fig. 5E). Thus, RsmB not only stabilizes a metastable folding intermediate that can recruit S7 but may also reduce the likelihood of rRNA misfolding during transcription.

To test whether RsmB can resolve misfolded nascent rRNA structures after transcription, we added S7-Cy5 and RsmB to the RNA 5 min after transcription restart (fig. S6A). We used RsmBC375A + SAM for these experiments because it better mimics the RsmB substrate complex than RsmBWT + SAH (fig. S2G). Different from the co-transcriptional S7 binding, the fraction of RNA in complex with S7 (≥ 5 s) reached 29% without RsmB, presumably because refolding of the 3′ domain rRNA during the 5-min incubation increased the number of transcripts competent to bind S7. RsmBC375A + SAM did not significantly change this population (fig. S6B). That RsmB did not improve this fraction further suggests it can capture and restructure near-native conformations during transcription, rendering these transcripts more likely to bind S7, but it cannot resolve stably misfolded transcripts. By contrast, we previously found that an ATP-dependent DEAD-box chaperone can improve assembly both during and after transcription (35). Extensive misfolding during transcription may also prevent RsmB from recognizing 16S h31. Indeed, RsmB improved S7 binding more when the rRNA was thermally refolded before the proteins were added (Fig. 4E), suggesting RsmB requires some local structure to act.

RsmB disfavors S19 addition by distorting its binding site

In the 30S assembly map (26, 27), S7 must bind the 16S 3′ domain rRNA before r-proteins S9 and S19 can join the complex. To investigate whether RsmB affects the next steps of assembly, we used smFCS to measure the Kd of S19 for the 3′ domain rRNA complexes with or without RsmB. The binding reactions included excess S7 and S9 to ensure specific S19 association. In contrast to S7, RsmBC375A plus SAM decreased the binding of S19 (Fig. 6A), with an estimated Kd = 151 ± 13 nM with RsmBC375A compared to 56.3 ± 1.5 nM without RsmB, indicating the importance of RsmB release for continued assembly of the 3′ domain rRNA.

To understand how RsmB destabilizes the binding of S19, we used DMS footprinting to probe the secondary structure of the 16S 3′ domain rRNA alone or bound to RsmBC375 plus SAM. Although the DMS modification pattern was mostly the same (fig. S7), in rRNA•RsmB complexes, we observed enhanced DMS modification of A974 and A977, indicating this region is less structured, and protection of A983 and A949, indicating this region is less solvent accessible (Fig. 6, B and C). These bases participate in base stacking and hydrogen bonding interactions that orient 16S h31 relative to h30 and h32 (36). Thus, RsmB alters the conformation of this helix junction, presumably as its active site engages C967 in the loop of h31. Because h30 and h32 form a continuous surface recognized by S19 (28), their misalignment by RsmB would likely prevent S19 recruitment (Fig. 6D). It is also possible that RsmB sterically prevents S19 binding.

DISCUSSION

Although none of the rRNA modifications in E. coli is essential for viability (17), installing them requires a substantial energy investment by the cell (2). This raises the question of why these chemical modifications and their dedicated enzymes are maintained in microbial genomes. A common answer is that rRNA modifications fine-tune the structure and function of the mature ribosome (37). For example, 16S m2G966 and m4Cm1402 directly interact with the P-site tRNA anticodon and mRNA codon, respectively. Loss of any of these modifications reduces translation fidelity by increasing initiation at non-AUG start codons and read-through at UGA stop codons (5, 6, 38).

Yet another answer is that some modification enzymes are retained in cells because they chaperone ribosome assembly, as we report here for RsmB methyltransferase. The product of RsmB, m5C967, does not affect the affinity of r-proteins for the rRNA. Instead, binding and release of RsmB protein stabilize metastable rRNA structures that facilitate r-protein recruitment to the 16S rRNA in the proper order. Dual roles in modification and assembly have been reported for other rRNA modification enzymes, such as RsmC, which promotes the native folding of 16S h34 (11), and RlmB and RlmKL, which function in 50S ribosome assembly independently of their modification activities (9).

If some modification enzymes are primarily assembly factors, it remains unclear why their modification activity is needed. Our results suggest that recognition by the enzyme active site acts to recruit a chaperone domain to specific RNA residues, temporarily distorting the RNA structure. For example, the related NSUN6 and NSUN2 tRNA methyltransferases unwind and remodel the tRNA to position the cytosine substrate in the active site (39, 40). The modification reaction catalyzed by the enzyme acts as a natural timing device, promoting release of the enzyme/chaperone from the modified RNA.

Evidence that RsmB temporarily binds and remodels its rRNA substrate comes in part from the stability of trapped complexes with the RsmBC375A mutant that cannot complete the methyltransferase cycle. Although we cannot rule out conformational differences between RsmBC375A and RsmBWT complexes, several lines of evidence suggest that RsmBC375A•SAM binding mimics the RsmBWT substrate complex. First, the C375A mutation prevents the formation of covalent reaction intermediates but does not impair RNA binding in the absence of SAM (16). Second, RsmBWT has high affinity for the unmethylated rRNA in the presence of SAH (fig. S2F), and RsmBWT•SAH also aids the recruitment of r-protein S7. Interestingly, the rank order of RsmB Kd values (fig. S2G) suggests that the stability of the RsmB-rRNA complexes depends on the crowdedness of the active site, with methylated rRNA complexes always less stable than unmethylated rRNA complexes. From this ranking, we expect the actual Kd for RsmBWT•SAM on unmethylated RNA to lie between that of RsmBC375A•SAM on unmethylated RNA and RsmBC375A•SAH on methylated RNA.

Using single-molecule co-localization co-transcriptional assembly (19), we visualized how a modification enzyme works on the rRNA during transcription, which is especially important for an enzyme like RsmB that modifies early assembly intermediates. Our results show that RsmB also facilitates association of S7 with the nascent rRNA. Because S7 recognizes residues at the 5′ and 3′ ends of the 16S 3′ domain (h28 and h43) (Fig. 2A), its binding site is especially likely to misfold during transcription (20), necessitating assistance by one or more chaperones. Indeed, footprinting experiments showed that h30, h31, h34 and the adjacent junction (j) between h34-h35-h38 fold slowly in vitro (41) and in E. coli cells lacking 30S assembly factors RimM or RbfA (21). To assess the likelihood of misfolding during transcription, we predicted the secondary structure of the 16S 3′ domain rRNA as its sequence is elongated using RNAfold (42). We found that the upstream half of the S7 binding site is predicted to form a stable non-native hairpin that persists until the 3′ domain rRNA is fully transcribed (blue, Fig. 7A). As the RNA sequence is extended, nucleotides flanking this hairpin are predicted to base pair with the upstream side of h34 (orange, Fig. 7A), trapping the basal half of the 16S 3′ domain rRNA in an incorrect secondary structure that would undermine assembly of the entire 30S subunit head.

RsmB chaperones co-transcriptional folding and assembly of 16S rRNA.

(A) Predicted misfolding of the 16S 3′ domain rRNA during transcription. Left, mature 16S secondary structure colored by folding segment and numbered by helix; dot, C967. Right, RNA secondary structures formed during transcription computed by RNAfold (ViennaRNA) (42). 16S h31 (green), h33 (purple) and h35–40 (pink) were predicted to fold correctly upon synthesis, whereas h28 (nt. 921–936) and h29 (nt. 939–943) (blue) form a non-native hairpin that is not resolved when the 3′ domain rRNA is fully transcribed. Improper base pairing between nt. 944–955 (blue) and nt. 1047–1064 (orange) disrupts h34 (orange). (B) Model for RsmB-facilitated assembly of the 30S 3′ domain rRNA. During transcription, RsmB binds and modifies the nascent 3′ domain rRNA, acting as a chaperone to prevent or refold non-native structure and maintain j30-31-32 in an open conformation. This restructuring is proposed to indirectly lower the energy barrier for S7 binding to h28 and h29 (rRNA refolding step 1) while inhibiting S19 binding to h30 and h32. After rRNA methylation, RsmB dissociates and j30-31-32 refolds (rRNA refolding step 2), permitting the addition of S19 and further steps of 30S assembly. Black dot, unmethylated C967; Red dot, m5C967.

Based on this predicted folding pathway and our DMS footprinting results, we propose a model in which both the binding and release of RsmB reshape the energy landscape of the 3′ domain assembly (Fig. 7B). Co-transcriptional binding of RsmB to h31 maintains j30–31-32 in an open conformation, preventing or resolving misfolding of h28, h29 and h30 and increasing the probability of stable S7 binding. That RsmB changes the rRNA structure agrees with a recent study showing that RsmB and RsmD reduce the accessibility of h30 (43). After methyl transfer, RsmB dissociates, restoring the alignment of h30 and h32 for recruitment of S19. This model agrees with previous studies that S7 binding alone cannot kinetically accelerate the S19 binding that requires additional folding steps (44). Release of RsmB would also allow h31 to pack against j34–35-38 and h43, consolidating rRNA structure needed for the addition of late assembly proteins, including uS3.

Given the evidence that RsmB binds to the 16S 3′ domain rRNA during transcription and inhibits the recruitment of S19, we can narrow the window for RsmB’s action to the time after transcription of h31 and before the binding of S19. Because S7 can only form a native complex after most of the 3′ domain rRNA has been transcribed, RsmB can interact with the nascent rRNA before S7 does.

Interestingly, the loop of h31 and j34–35-38 in yeast 18S rRNA are also prone to misfolding (22). This region of the yeast and human 18S rRNA is targeted by assembly factors, several modification enzymes, and conserved snoRNPs (45), such as snR35 in yeast (46), that not only modify their target sites during transcription but also hold the corresponding helices open for a time to ensure proper folding and successful pre-rRNA processing (47). This correspondence raises the possibility that bacterial modification enzymes and eukaryotic snoRNPs play analogous roles during early stages of small subunit assembly.

A limitation of this study is that our minimal reaction may not resemble the real environment in the cell, which is crowded with other r-proteins, modifying enzymes, and assembly factors. To compensate for weak RsmB binding in dilute solution, we used RsmBC375A + SAM or RsmBWT + SAH to prolong its interaction with the rRNA during slow 30S assembly in vitro. These strategies have been successfully used to capture complexes of related methyltransferases for structural studies (39, 40). The transcription speed in our study is also slower than rrn transcription in E. coli cells (Fig. 5C), which is reported to increase misfolding of the 3′ domain rRNA (20). Nevertheless, our in vitro studies provide a framework for understanding how a modification enzyme dynamically interacts with rRNA and coordinates the addition of r-proteins by modulating the rRNA structure. Our findings also provide a new example of how the entire RNA modification reaction cycle may function in RNP assembly.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Experimental design

We designed our study to directly visualize co-transcriptional binding of an rRNA modification enzyme, RsmB, with the goal of elucidating how rRNA modification enzymes act during ribosome assembly. We designed single-molecule fluorescence microscopy assays to directly observe RNA-protein interactions in a defined system of recombinant proteins and transcription complexes. These assays were supported by genetic complementation tests, chemical RNA structure probing, EMSAs, and methylation assays. This combined approach enables us to dissect the contributions of rRNA modification and the rRNA modification enzyme to assembly.

Bacterial strains and plasmids used in this study

E. coli strains BW25141 and JW3250 (kanR, ∆rsmB) were obtained from the Keio collection (24). BL21(DE3) cells for protein overexpression and purification were obtained from New England Biolabs. Plasmids for protein overexpression are listed in table S4. The rsmB coding sequence was amplified from genomic DNA (E. coli K-12 MG1655) with primer RsmB_Fwd and RsmB_Rev (table S5), and cloned using NEBuilder HiFi DNA Assembly (New England Biolabs). The C375A substitution was introduced using Q5 Site-Directed Mutagenesis (New England Biolabs) with primer RsmB_C375A_Fwd and RsmB_C375A_Rev (table S5). Sequences of RsmB plasmids (WT or C375A) are given in data S1.

Bacterial growth assay

For competitive growth experiments, E. coli cells of parental strain BW25141 and the kanamycin-resistant rsmB deletion strain, JW3250 (Keio collection) (24), were mixed in equal numbers in LB medium, diluted to OD600 = 0.001 in LB, and grown at 37°C until OD600 = 1. The mixed culture (50 μL) was used to inoculate 50 mL of fresh LB media, and the process was repeated for a total of five cycles. At each cycle, serial dilutions of bacterial culture (up to 1:10−6) were plated on LB agar and LB agar supplemented with kanamycin (50 μg/mL). The plates were incubated overnight at 37°C, and the colonies were counted.

For plasmid complementation assays, competent parental BW25141 or JW3250 (∆rsmB) were prepared by Mix & Go! E. coli Transformation Kit (Zymo Research). Both strains transformed with pBAD-empty, pBAD-RsmBWT, or pBAD-RsmBC375A were grown in LB with 100 μg/ml ampicillin at 37°C until OD600 = 0.8. 5 μL from four serial 100-fold dilutions were spotted onto LB agar with 100 μg/mL ampicillin and 0.02% arabinose, and incubated at 37°C for 14 h. Overexpression of RsmBWT or RsmBC375A from pBAD was verified by standard western blotting with 6x His tag antibody (Invitrogen).

Protein purification and fluorescent labeling

Ribosomal proteins

Ribosomal proteins S7, S9 and S19 were overexpressed from pRSF-1b-S7-S83C/Δ157–178, pRSF-1b-S19-T48C (20), and pET-S9-NoHis in BL21(DE3) cells (New England Biolabs). Transformed cells were cultured in 1 L LB medium at 37°C until OD600 = 0.4–0.6, then induced with 1 mM IPTG at 18°C for 16 h. Proteins were purified by FPLC cation exchange chromatography as described (48, 49) with some modifications. Harvested cells were resuspended in 20 mL r-protein lysis buffer [80 mM K-HEPES (pH 7.6), 200 mM KCl, 6 mM 2-mercaptoethanol (BME)], lysed (EmulsiFlex), and the lysate was cleared by centrifugation at 9,780g for 50 min at 4°C (Beckman JA-20 rotor at 9,000 rpm). Supernatant was dialyzed overnight at 4°C against r-protein binding buffer (20 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.0 at 4°C), 125 mM KCl, 6 M urea, 6 mM BME), and then filtered (0.45 μm) and loaded onto a 5-mL HiTrap SP HP column (GE Healthcare). The column was washed with ∼30 mL r-protein binding buffer and eluted with a gradient of 0.125–1 M KCl in r-protein binding buffer. For S9, the eluate was directly dialyzed against r-protein storage buffer (80 mM K-HEPES (pH 7.6), 1 M KCl, 20 mM MgCl2, 6 mM 2-mercaptoethanol (BME), 5% glycerol). S7 and S19 were further purified with a Heparin HP column (20) by dialyzing the eluate overnight at 4°C against 20 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.6), 500 mM KCl, 0.5 mM EDTA, 0.5 mM DTT, 10% glycerol. After dilution to 100 mM KCl (final), the sample was filtered (0.45 μm) and loaded onto a 5-mL HiTrap Heparin HP column (GE Healthcare). The column was washed with ∼30 mL r-protein heparin buffer (20 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.6), 100 mM KCl, 0.5 mM EDTA, 0.5 mM DTT, 10% glycerol) and eluted with a 1 M KCl gradient in r-protein heparin buffer. The eluate was dialyzed against r-protein storage buffer.

For fluorophore labeling, the purified proteins were incubated with 8-fold excess Cy5-maleimide (GE Healthcare) in 80 mM K-HEPES (pH 7.6), 1 M KCl, 1 mM TCEP, 6 M urea at 21°C. Unreacted dye was removed by repurification on HiTrap SP HP column. The reaction was diluted 25 times with 20 mM Tris-HCl (pH 6.3 at 21°C), 6 M urea, 0.01% (v/v) Nikkol, 6 mM BME, and loaded onto the column. The column was washed with 20 mM Tris-HCl (pH 6.3 at 21°C), 100 mM KCl, 6 M urea, 0.01% (v/v) Nikkol, 6 mM BME, and then eluted with 20 mM Tris-HCl (pH 6.3 at 21°C), 600 mM KCl, 6 M urea, 0.01% (v/v) Nikkol, 6 mM BME. The elution was dialyzed against r-protein storage buffer. The labeling efficiency was calculated from the ratio of dye content (measured by absorbance at 650 nm) and total protein concentration (measured by Bradford assay): S7, 56%; S9, 60%; S19, 64%.

RsmB methyltransferase

Unlabeled RsmBWT and RsmBC375A were overexpressed from pET28a-RsmBWT and pET28a-RsmBC375A. Transformed BL21(DE3) cells were cultured in 1 L LB medium at 37°C until OD600 = 0.6–0.8, then induced with 400 μM IPTG at 18°C for 16 h. Harvested cells were resuspended in 20 mL RsmB binding buffer (50 mM Na-HEPES (pH 7.6), 500 mM NaCl, 20 mM imidazole, 5% glycerol), lysed (EmulsiFlex), and the lysate was cleared by centrifugation at 9,780g for 50 min at 4°C (Beckman JA-20 rotor at 9,000 rpm). The column was washed with ∼30 mL RsmB binding buffer and eluted with a gradient up to 500 mM imidazole in RsmB binding buffer. The eluate was dialyzed against RsmB storage buffer (20 mM Na-HEPES (pH7.6), 70 mM NH4Cl, 150 mM NaCl, 0.1 mM EDTA, 1 mM DTT, 10% glycerol).

RsmB was fluorescently labeled by appending an LPETG-His6 tag to its open reading frame for modification by sortase. The flow-through was collected and directly applied to a Heparin HP column. Labeled proteins were eluted with sortase reaction buffer with 600 mM NaCl and 10% glycerol, followed by dialysis against RsmB storage buffer. The protein concentration was measured by absorbance at 280 nm. The labeling efficiencies: RsmBWT, 75%; RsmBC375A, 83%.

RNA preparation and labeling

In vitro transcription

E. coli 16S 3′ domain rRNA (U921-A1396) only or with a C-less cassette and anti-SA5 sequence tags was transcribed in vitro using T7 RNA polymerase from PCR-amplified template (table S6), and purified by denaturing 6% polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (PAGE).

Preparation of in vitro methylated RNA

500 nM 3′ domain rRNA was incubated together with 1.5 μM RsmB and 1 mM SAM in 40 mM K-HEPES pH 7.6, 40 mM NH4Cl, 2 mM Mg(OAc)2, 6 mM BME for 40 min at 37°C. The modified RNA was cleaned up before use (RNA Clean & Concentrator Kits; Zymo Research). To check the efficiency of methylation, bisulfite conversion and poisoned primer extension were performed. Non-methylated and methylated RNAs were treated with bisulfite using EZ RNA Methylation Kit (Zymo Research). Methylation was analyzed by extension of 5’-32P-d(TCAAAACCAAATAAAATTCTTCAC) with SuperScript III (Thermo Fisher Scientific) according to the manufacturer’s standard protocol, in the presence of dATP, dTTP, ddGTP and ddCTP. cDNA products were fractionated on a denaturing 20% sequencing PAGE. Gels were quantified using FIJI (51).

Fluorescent labeling of DNA oligomers

To label the oligonucleotides (Terminator_rev and SA5) (table S5), 1 mg sulfo-Cy3 NHS ester (Lumiprobe) was dissolved in 60 μL dimethyl sulfoxide. 5 nmol oligonucleotides containing an internal amino-alkyl modified nucleotide (Integrated DNA technologies, Inc) were dissolved in 70 μL of 100 mM NaHCO3 (pH 8.5) and then combined with 30 μL sulfo-Cy3 NHS ester in dimethyl sulfoxide. The reaction was incubated at 4°C overnight and cleaned up using a TE10 spin column (Takara) followed by ethanol precipitation.

RNA methylation assays

250 nM RNA and 250 nM RsmB were incubated together with 0.01 mCi/mL SAM-[3H] (PerkinElmer, NET155V250UC) in 40 mM K-HEPES pH 7.6, 40 mM NH4Cl, 2 mM Mg(OAc)2, 6 mM BME at 37°C. The reaction was stopped by adding 5 μL reaction mixture to 100 μL glycine-HCl pH 2.3, then applied to Amersham Hybond-N+ membrane (Cytiva) using Bio-Dot Microfiltration System. Each well was washed three times with 200 μL glycine-HCl pH 2.3. The membrane was air-dried, cut by sample wells, soaked in ScintiVerse BD Cocktail (Fisher Scientific), and 3H activity was measured by liquid scintillation counter (Beckman).

Fluorescence correlation spectroscopy

Unlabeled rRNA without sequence tags (see table S6) was refolded before use by heating at 70°C for 5 min and slowly cooling from 70°C to 25°C for 30 min in 80 mM K-HEPES pH 7.6, 50 mM KCl, 5 mM MgCl2. To measure the dissociation constant (Kd) of S7, 0.5 nM S7-Cy5 and 0.5 nM – 10 nM 3′ domain rRNA were incubated together in 40 mM K-HEPES pH 7.6, 200 mM KCl, 10 mM MgCl2, 0.02% (v/v) Triton X-100 at room temperature for 20 min. Details of binding reactions for other proteins are listed in table S1. smFCS measurements were carried out at room temperature using an EI-FLEX confocal microscope (Exciting Instruments). Coverslips were passivated with DDS-Tween20 as described previously (52). A 50-μL aliquot from each binding reaction was spotted on the coverslip, and 3-minute movies were recorded for each condition with excitation at 638 nm and emission detected with a 680/40 nm filter. FCS data were analyzed as described in the section on statistical analysis to obtain protein-RNA dissociation constants.

Electrophoretic mobility shift assays (EMSA)

100 nM 3′ domain rRNA was renatured with 1 μM SA5-Cy3 as described for FCS experiments. 15 nM renatured RNA was incubated with S7-Cy5 (0/45 nM) or RsmB (0/75 nM) or S7 and RsmB together in 40 mM K-HEPES pH 7.6, 150 mM KCl, 10 mM MgCl2, 0.02% (v/v) Triton X-100 at room temperature for 30 min. Co-transcriptional assembly

DMS footprinting

DMS modification was performed as previously described (53). 1 μM in vitro transcribed 16S 3′ domain RNA was refolded by heating at 70°C for 5 min and slowly cooling from 70°C to 25°C for 30 min in 80 mM K-HEPES (pH 7.6), 150 mM KCl. 10 mM MgCl2 was added after refolding and incubated at room temperature for 5 min. To probe the RNA structure when RsmB is bound, 100 μM SAM and 1.1 μM or 4 μM RsmBC375A were added at room temperature for 10 min before treatment with DMS (1% (v/v) final) at room temperature for 2 min. Statistical analysis

FCS data analysis and Kd determination

Autocorrelation analysis of fluorophore-labeled protein diffusion was performed using PAM (pulsed interleaved excitation analysis with MATLAB) (54). Free Cy5 NHS ester was used as the reference dye to calculate the confocal volume. Autocorrelation functions in the absence of RNA or in saturating RNA were first fitted using a 1-component 3D diffusion model to obtain the diffusion coefficients of the free protein (D1) and the protein-RNA complex (D2), respectively. Then, the autocorrelation functions at different RNA concentrations were fitted using a 2-component 3D diffusion model with D1 and D2 fixed. The concentrations of free protein and bound protein were calculated from their relative contributions to the amplitude of the autocorrelation function. The fraction of bound protein, θ, was calculated from these concentrations. For S7, S9 and RsmB, the Kd was obtained by fitting the data with a quadratic binding isothermθ=12 [(1+[X][P]+Kd[P])−(1+[X][P]+Kd[P])2−4[X][P]](1)in which [X] is the concentration of RNA and [P] is the concentration of protein. To calculate the Kd of S19 binding to 3′ domain•S7•S9 complexes, the fraction of bound S19 was fitted to the Hill equation, with Hill coefficient nθ=[X]nKdn+[X]n(2)

This model accounts for apparent cooperativity that may arise from a conformational change coupled to S19 binding. The Kd values were extracted from global non-linear least-squares fitting of three independent replicates using Python. The standard deviation of Kd was derived from the covariance matrix of the fitting parameters.

Single-molecule data analysis

Single-molecule TIRF movies were analyzed using Imscroll (55). Immobilized refolded RNAs or TECs with an active Cy3 signal were picked as areas of interest (AOIs) that were then translated to the Cy5 channel using the mapping function in Imscroll. The Cy3 and Cy5 intensities in each AOI were calculated at each frame and plotted as a single-molecule time trace. The background is subtracted using the rolling ball algorithm as previously described (35). For co-transcriptional experiments, successful transcription was indicated by a protein-induced fluorescence enhancement (PIFE) signal. Only traces with a PIFE signal were selected for further analysis. For post-transcriptional experiments (fig. S6), two movies were merged for co-localization. In these experiments, non-restarted TECs in the field were used as references for drift correction which sometimes occurred between acquisition of the two movies. Binding events were defined as periods of Cy5 intensity within each AOI above a threshold equal to 5 times the standard deviation of the background Cy5 intensity.

The residence times of protein binding were fitted by single, double, or triple exponential for different conditions to calculate koff and τ = 1/koff, using the non-linear least-squares method in Python. For S7, the threshold of stable binding was chosen to be 5 s, which is five times the transient lifetime. In all cases, we report parameters for the simplest model that described the data, as judged by the distribution of the residuals of the fit.

Protein occupancy was defined as the time that immobilized 3′ domain rRNAs were bound by S7-Cy5 or RsmB-Cy5 (for events >0.2 s) over total recording time, averaged across all analyzed molecules in one movie. The occupancy and fraction of rRNA transcripts that are stably bound were calculated separately for each replicate. Student’s t-test was performed to check the significance of our hypothesis. Replicates and numbers of molecules for each replicate are indicated in the figure legends.
