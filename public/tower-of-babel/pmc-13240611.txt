Clinical Presentation and Outcomes of Insulinomas in Pediatric Patients
Authors: Lauren M. Mitteer, Yesenia Sanchez-Kleinberg, Katherine Lord, Winifred Sigal, Lisa J. States, Tricia R. Bhatti, Robert J. Gallop, Andrea Kelly, N.Scott Adzick, Diva D. De León
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionInsulinomas are the leading cause of hyperinsulinemic hypoglycemia in adults, but they can also present during childhood, and, when they do, diagnosis and localization of the lesion can be difficult. Moreover, differentiating between congenital hyperinsulinism (HI) and pediatric insulinomas is challenging because both diseases have similar presentations. Our objective was to review the clinical presentation, diagnostic characteristics, and success of methods for localization, treatment, and outcomes in pediatric patients with insulinomas.MethodsRetrospective study of patients with confirmed insulinoma treated at a single academic pediatric hospital between 2006 and 2024. Descriptive statistics and logistic regression models were used to quantify the data and discriminate between groups, respectively. The optimal operating point (OOP) for diagnostic laboratory data was used to maximize discrimination between insulinomas and HI, with prediction accuracy determined by the sensitivity and specificity at the OOP.ResultsWe reviewed the records of 23 patients with insulinoma with a median age at presentation of 13 years. A positive family history of multiple endocrine neoplasia type 1 was recorded in 22%. The most common presenting symptom was altered mental status. Median plasma insulin and beta-hydroxybutyrate (BOHB) concentrations during hypoglycemia were 8.8 uIU/mL (61.1 pmol/L) and 0.3 mmol/L, respectively. Most patients had four imaging studies completed to localize their insulinoma. Twenty of the 23 patients reviewed had a single lesion successfully resected and were cured. Of these 20 patients, 12 had 18F-Fluoro-dihydroxyphenylalanine (18F-DOPA) positron emission tomography/computed tomography (PET/CT) completed, of which nine were positive (75%). Three patients had initial lesions localized and successfully resected but continued to have hypoglycemia requiring medical management. Median duration from presentation to surgery was 9 weeks. When comparing the critical samples of patients with insulinomas and HI, the combination of BOHB and insulin-like growth factor binding protein 1 at the time of hypoglycemia yielded a sensitivity and specificity of 0.89 and 0.84, respectively, for insulinoma versus HI when using a multivariate function on our cohorts.ConclusionIn this series, successful treatment of insulinomas took, on average, 9 weeks from diagnosis. Multiple imaging modalities were employed for preoperative lesion localization, with 18F-DOPA PET/CT and magnetic resonance imaging being the most successful. Improved imaging modalities are needed to localize insulinomas in pediatric patients and reduce the time between diagnosis and resection. We propose an algorithm to differentiate between pediatric insulinomas and congenital hyperinsulinism using laboratory data to quicken diagnosis and lessen time to treatment.

Introduction

Insulinomas are the most common cause of hyperinsulinemic hypoglycemia in adults [1]. Conversely, congenital disorders of insulin regulation – specifically, congenital hyperinsulinism (HI) – are the most common cause of persistent hypoglycemia in the pediatric population [2]. Overall, insulinomas are rare, with an estimated incidence of 1–3 cases per million persons per year in the adult population [3]. The incidence of insulinomas in the pediatric population is not well-defined, with few reported cases in the literature [4–8].

The biochemical confirmation of hyperinsulinemic hypoglycemia in patients with insulinomas is best captured by a “critical sample” collected after a diagnostic fast or during a spontaneous episode of hypoglycemia (plasma glucose < 50 mg/dL [2.8 mmol/L]) with detectable insulin and suppressed beta-hydroxybutyrate (BOHB; i.e., ketones) and free fatty acids. Unfortunately, for the pediatric population in particular, the differential diagnosis of an insulinoma can be complicated by an age of onset and diagnostic bloodwork that mimics congenital hyperinsulinism.

In turn, this leads to a reliance on imaging to localize the insulinoma lesions in young patients. Surgical resection is the treatment of choice for insulinomas, and the success of the procedure depends on accurate localization of the lesion prior to surgery [5]. The small size of insulinomas – most often under 1 cm3 – challenges detectability by conventional imaging techniques such as ultrasound, computed tomography (CT), and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). The use of invasive localization methods, such as endoscopic ultrasound (EUS) and arterial calcium stimulation with hepatic venous sampling (ASVS), is limited in younger patients. New imaging modalities have been implemented, including Gallium 68 (68Ga) 1,4,7,10-tetra-azacyclododecane-1,4,7,10-tetra-acetic acid (DOTA)-octreotate (68Ga-DOTATATE) positron emission tomography/computed tomography (PET/CT) and 18F-Fluoro-dihydroxyphenylalanine (18F-DOPA) PET/CT with various success rates, even in the adult population [9]. More recently, targeted imaging using 68Ga-Exendin-4 PET/CT has demonstrated significantly higher accuracy and sensitivity for localization of insulinomas in adults [10]; however, this modality is not available in the USA.

Once the insulinoma is localized, minimally invasive enucleation of the tumor is preferred, when possible, given its ability to achieve long-term cure and the low likelihood of post-surgical exocrine pancreatic insufficiency. Alternatively, an open surgical excision of the tumor may be required for pancreatic head and deeply embedded lesions [11]. Understandably, accurate localization of the tumor is crucial prior to surgical intervention. For patients in whom surgical cure is not possible, medical therapy along with dietary modifications is trialed. Diazoxide (the only FDA-approved therapy for HI) suppresses insulin secretion from pancreatic beta cells and has been shown to be an effective monotherapy for patients with insulinomas. Somatostatin analogs, such as octreotide, may also be employed for patients unresponsive to diazoxide [12]. We aimed to leverage our center’s data, which includes diagnostic and clinical data of individuals with hyperinsulinemic hypoglycemia, to better characterize clinical and diagnostic features and treatment of insulinomas in children and young adults.

Methods

The Children’s Hospital of Philadelphia Congenital Hyperinsulinism Center database was queried to identify patients with insulinoma. Inclusion criteria included biochemical evidence of: (1) hyperinsulinemic hypoglycemia defined as detectable plasma insulin, low plasma beta-hydroxybutyrate concentration (< 1.8 mmol/L), low plasma free fatty acid concentration (FFA; <1.7 mmol/L), and a glycemic response to glucagon > 30 mg/dL (1.7 mmol/L) during a spontaneous or provoked episode of hypoglycemia with plasma glucose < 50 mg/dL (2.8 mmol/L) [13]; (2) radiographic imaging and/or invasive testing that raised suspicion for insulinoma; and (3) histopathologic confirmation of an insulinoma following surgical resection. Patients without histopathologic confirmation of insulinoma were included in this series if they had a prior diagnosis of insulinoma (in cases of recurrent insulinomas) or very high suspicion for the diagnosis based on the patient’s age and biochemical testing.

We performed a retrospective medical record review of identified subjects with insulinomas treated by our center between 2006 and 2024. Data extracted from the records included: patient’s age and symptoms at presentation of hypoglycemia; critical sample laboratory measurements at the time of confirmed plasma glucose ≤ 50 mg/dL (2.8 mmol/L) including plasma glucose, insulin, BOHB, FFA, C-peptide, and insulin-like growth factor binding protein 1 (IGFBP-1); testing results for multiple endocrine neoplasia type 1 (MEN1); imaging and other localization studies; surgical management; and postoperative course. Cure following surgical resection was defined as demonstration of normal fasting mechanisms, including elevation of BOHB during a 24- to 48-h fasting test [14]. Ongoing management for patients who did not demonstrate a cure was also included in our review.

Critical sample data from a previously published cohort of subjects with HI treated at our center between August 2002 and June 2013 [13] were used as a comparator group to examine the diagnostic laboratory characteristics for insulinoma. This study was reviewed and approved by the Children’s Hospital of Philadelphia’s Institutional Review Board.

Statistical Analysis

Before analyses, inspection of many clinical measures revealed substantial skew; therefore, descriptive statistics for continuous measures were presented as medians and interquartile ranges, with categorical measures presented with percentages per level. Two logistic regression models were implemented to assess whether the magnitude of each clinical measure could predict the presence of insulinoma, with statistical significance assessed using a Wald Chi-square statistic and odds ratios, along with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for effect size. Odds ratio CIs not containing “1” correspond to our statistically significant effects at a significance level of 0.05.

A standard approach to summarizing the performance of logistic regression models is to display the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve [15]. The area under the curve (AUC) ranges from 0.50 (random discrimination) to 1.00 (perfect discrimination) [15]. Pairwise comparison of the discrimination of two logistic regression models can be based on their ROC curves [16].

Attention was also given to determining the optimal threshold from a diagnostic model [17]. This optimal threshold is called the optimal operating point (OOP) and provides clinical guidelines for decision-making regarding an individual patient’s likely presence of insulinoma. There are different approaches for deriving the OOP [18]. We chose the method that simultaneously maximized the probability of predicting a true positive (insulinoma) or a true negative (HI). Prediction of outcome status was at the sensitivity and specificity levels of the OOP. For a single predictor, the OOP represented a single threshold value to determine predictive insulinoma or HI. A single predictor was deemed sufficient to discriminate insulinoma if the AUC was 0.90 or higher. If a single predictor was not enough, a multivariate model was considered. For inclusion in the multivariate model, we followed the guidance in Chowdhury and Turin [19], in which variables significant at p < 0.25 were included in a backward selection process. The OOP generated the multivariate prediction model function F. If the function was positive (F(xi) ≥ 0), where xi represents the collection of multivariate predictors observed for the ith participant, then the patient was predicted as having insulinoma; otherwise, the patient was predicted as having HI.

Analyses were performed using SAS/STAT 15.1 and R 4.2.2. With the limited sample size per group, statistical significance was set at an alpha of 0.05 for all analyses.

Results

Twenty-three patients with insulinoma or highly suspicious for insulinoma who were evaluated at our center between 2006 and 2024 were identified in the database. Twenty-two patients had an insulinoma resected and confirmed by histopathology at our hospital; 1 patient had a presentation highly suspicious for insulinoma and later underwent successful insulinoma resection at another facility.

Diagnostic Evaluation

The diagnosis of hyperinsulinemic hypoglycemia was established based on the critical sample when the plasma glucose was confirmed <50 mg/dL [13, 14]. Of note, 1 patient had a confirmed plasma glucose of 50 mg/dL and was included in this report given the patient’s glucose trajectory during the diagnostic fast. Median plasma glucose at the time of the critical sample was 42 mg/dL (2.3 mmol/L). Median plasma insulin and BOHB were 8.8 uIU/mL (61.1 pmol/L) and 3.1 mg/dL (0.3 mmol/L), respectively (Table 2). A positive family history of a confirmed or presumed MEN1 diagnosis was recorded in five (22%) patients. Twenty-two patients had testing completed; of them, 13 (59%) were MEN1-negative, seven (32%) were MEN1 positive, and results were unavailable for two. The mean age of presentation of those with a positive test (13.62 years) and those with a negative test (14.58 years) was similar. Three patients in our cohort also underwent genetic testing for monogenic forms of HI, and the results were negative.

Once the diagnosis of insulinoma was suspected, patients underwent imaging to localize the lesion. Most patients had at least four imaging studies completed to localize the insulinoma. Imaging results were analyzed for patients with an insulinoma localized at their first presentation/encounter at our institution. Imaging modalities included abdominal MRI, CT abdomen, abdominal ultrasound, endoscopic ultrasound, 18F-DOPA PET/CT, 68Ga-DOTATATE PET/CT, octreotide scan, transhepatic portal venous sampling (THVS), and ASVS.

The most performed imaging study on this cohort was MRI (n = 19), which successfully localized the lesion in 63% of cases. 18F-DOPA PET/CT was the most successful noninvasive imaging modality, accurately localizing 75% of the lesions when used. 68Ga-DOTATATE PET/CT also localized 75% of the lesions but was only performed four times in our cohort. Octreotide scan was not successful in identifying the lesion in the two cases performed. Among invasive imaging modalities, THVS successfully localized the insulinoma in 1 patient; ASVS was performed by outside institutions in 2 other patients, successfully localizing the lesion in both (Table 3).

Treatment and Outcomes

Reports of attempted treatments before surgery included diazoxide (in 79% of patients), octreotide, cornstarch, continuous feeds, growth hormone, hydrocortisone, and nifedipine. Of the 23 patients identified, 22 underwent surgery at our institution (Fig. 1). The median duration from presentation of hypoglycemia to surgery was 9 weeks, ranging from 2 to 68 weeks. Nineteen patients underwent a single surgery, while the remaining three underwent two surgeries. Of the 19 single-surgery patients, 18 were cured after initial surgery as demonstrated by a fasting test. Two patients have had recurrence of hyperinsulinemic hypoglycemia after surgery, which raised suspicion for a recurrent tumor, but attempts for localization to date have not been successful. One of these patients was MEN1 positive, went 4 years before recurrence of symptoms, and had a lesion removed during a second surgery, which was confirmed by histopathologic examination to be a glucagonoma. The second patient did not have MEN1 and went 22 months without symptoms before recurrence. For a third patient, the initial surgery revealed a glucagonoma, followed by localization of insulinoma during a second surgery; however, this patient was not cured and was also MEN1 positive. A lesion has not been localized for an additional patient who was MEN1 negative and has not undergone exploratory surgery but continues with annual non-invasive surveillance imaging. In total, these 4 patients require ongoing medical management with diazoxide, lanreotide, or everolimus.

Distinguishing Features of Patients with Insulinomas

We examined features that distinguish insulinoma from HI in the pediatric population. Patients with insulinomas were older, with a median age at presentation of 153 months (12.8 years), whereas patients with HI presented at a median age of 3.5 months (0.3 years) (p < 0.0001).

We compared the critical samples from patients with insulinoma with those previously published for a cohort of patients with HI [13] (see Table 4). Given the differences in age at presentation, we report in Table 4 unadjusted and age-adjusted odds ratios for each univariate measure. Plasma BOHB at the time of hypoglycemia was significantly different between the two groups. BOHB concentration during hypoglycemia was inappropriately low in both groups; however, patients with insulinoma had higher BOHB concentrations than those with HI. Discrimination for BOHB was quantified as AUC = 0.75 (95% CI [0.60–0.89]), corresponding to fair discrimination. The OOP indicated a BOHB ≥ 0.40 mmol/L at the time of hypoglycemia yielded sensitivity and specificity of 0.68 and 0.76, respectively, for distinguishing insulinoma from congenital HI. Even with the observed difference in age of presentation reported above, the BOHB effect remains significant as indicated by the age-adjusted odds ratio confidence bound not including 1. Although not statistically significant, insulin concentrations were higher in the insulinoma group. The sensitivity and specificity of a detectable plasma insulin concentration at the time of hypoglycemia for the insulinoma and HI groups were 55% and 60%, respectively (area under the ROC, 0.61; 95% CI [0.44–0.77]). These were also not statistically significant.

Discussion

The diagnosis and successful treatment of insulinomas in children and adolescents are frequently delayed. Our cohort demonstrated a median time from presentation to surgery of 9 weeks; this delay is in part due to the low sensitivity of currently available imaging techniques for localizing the tumor. For this cohort, multiple imaging modalities were required for preoperative lesion localization, with 18F-DOPA PET/CT and MRI being the most successful. This differs from the work of Wiese et al. [9], who described a large cohort of 61 adults with insulinomas, in which EUS was the most effective imaging modality, correctly localizing 93% of cases, including 13 lesions that were identified only by EUS. However, their comparisons do not include 18F-DOPA imaging, and some of the imaging studies they report on are not available to pediatric patients [9]. Though surgery is curative for most patients, insulinomas may recur, as was the case for 2 of our patients. When surgical resection is not successful, medical management with diazoxide or octreotide may be necessary.

The international hyperinsulinism guidelines recommend evaluation for insulinoma in patients presenting with hyperinsulinemic hypoglycemia after 2 years of age [14]. The youngest patient in our cohort presented at 2 years old with an initial diagnosis of congenital hyperinsulinism. It was only after an extensive clinical and imaging workup that she was found to have an insulinoma [6]. This young age demonstrates the importance of considering the possibility of an insulinoma in children whose symptoms present after infancy [6]. Indeed, seven of the 23 insulinoma patients were diagnosed under the age of 10 years. Our data suggest that analysis of the critical sample may help distinguish patients with insulinomas from those with HI (patients with insulinomas have higher BOHB during hypoglycemia). Furthermore, when we combined biomarkers and used a function using BOHB and IGFBP-1, the sensitivity and specificity for predicting insulinoma significantly improved. These results need to be validated in other patient cohorts and need to be interpreted within the context of age of the patient and history suggestive of an acquired process vs. a congenital condition. While the BOHB effect remains significant in age-adjusted univariate analysis, the loss of significance of BOHB and IGFBP-1 in our age-adjusted multivariate model may indicate a potential confounding effect of age. Further, the difference observed in BOHB between the insulinoma, and HI cohorts are small and may not be of clinical significance but may reflect the differences in the pathophysiology of the two conditions. Our study described the clinical course of a relatively large cohort of pediatric patients with insulinoma and compared biomarkers in critical samples obtained during hypoglycemia with those in a cohort of patients with HI. Our study had limitations, including a retrospective design and the unavailability of data for all patients. Our hospital is also a referral center, meaning our data may be conflated by particularly complex cases who were unable to be treated at their local hospitals. Additionally, because insulinoma is so rare in the pediatric population, our sample size was small, which affects the power of our statistical analysis.

Our study highlights the importance of adequate imaging when diagnosing insulinoma and distinguishing patients presenting with hyperinsulinemic hypoglycemia due to insulinoma from those with congenital HI. We also propose a novel algorithm to assist in this differentiation. Further studies are needed to validate our prediction model and enable clinicians to more effectively diagnose insulinoma in the pediatric population.
