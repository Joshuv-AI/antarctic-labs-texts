Lithium-induced blepharospasm in a middle-aged woman with recurrent depressive disorder: a case report
Authors: Maria del Rosario Arevalo Bazalar, Jayamal De Silva
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis case describes the presence of blepharospasm as a side effect after a short period of lithium treatment in a middle-aged woman with recurrent depressive disorder. Almost immediately after cessation of lithium carbonate, side effects subsided, indicating that the medication was the culprit for her symptoms. Switch to a different psychotropic as a mood-stabilising agent commenced. Four months later, side effects experienced remained absent and mental state appears to have improved, obtaining a remission of illness. There is a limited number of case reports which have reported blepharospasm as a side effect of lithium carbonate. Lithium is used worldwide for the management of mood disorders, although its use has declined over the years in certain parts of the world. In Australia, it is still considered the most effective mood stabilising agent and is recommended by the Royal Australian and New Zealand College of Psychiatry clinical practice guidelines for mood disorders.Keywords: blepharospasm, case report, dyskinesia, extrapyramidal side effects, lithium side effects

Background

Blepharospasm is a type of neurological disorder known as focal dystonia which is characterised by involuntary and sustained muscle spasms of the eyelids. This results in impaired opening and closing function of the eyes and increased spontaneous blinking. 1 Non- primary blepharospasm is believed to be caused by space-occupying lesions in the diencephalon and basal ganglia or to be drug-induced. 2

Drug-induced blepharospasm is usually caused primarily by antipsychotic medications due to their known extrapyramidal side effects resulting in dyskinesia. Other agents known to cause dyskinesia include dopaminergic agents, antihistamines, calcium channel blockers, noradrenaline and serotonin reuptake inhibitors, 3 all of which are used to treat an array of psychiatric presentations.

Currently, there is a limited amount of data providing evidence for lithium-induced blepharospasm.

Methods

This case report was prepared following the CARE Checklist (Supplemental Material). 4

Case presentation

A woman in her mid 40’s, who lives at home with her husband, was referred to the local mental health services of a rural Victorian town by her General Practitioner (GP) for ongoing support and review. At the time of referral, there were ongoing depressive symptoms despite being on a combination of medications as per guidelines. 5 She had a history of coexisting diagnoses of generalised anxiety disorder and post-traumatic stress disorder.

Initially she was treated with venlafaxine 150 mg, Lurasidone 120 mg and Clonidine 50 µg (Table 1 and Figure 1). However, as symptoms remained to persist, she was started on lithium carbonate 500 mg nocte.

She was given a pathology slip to have some baseline investigations and advised to book an appointment with her GP to ensure physical wellness.

A week after commencement of lithium, she was reviewed as per protocol. The dose appeared to be well tolerated with no apparent impact on her daily functioning and no perceived side effects such as nausea, vomiting, or photophobia. Physical examination revealed a mild hand tremor and was otherwise unremarkable.

The patient’s mood improved over the following months. However, 3 months after commencement of lithium, she developed increased light sensitivity leading to blepharospasm impacting on her ability to drive.

No other medication changes nor physical health issues had emerged since the commencement of lithium that could indicate an alternative aetiology for her symptom. Thus, Lithium was weaned off as a trial.

Investigations

Pre-lithium commencement blood workup was completed. Full blood count, Thyroid hormones panel, Erythrocyte sedimentation rate, creatinine, and glomerular filtration rate were all found to be normal.

Outcome and follow up

Once lithium had been completely ceased, there was complete resolution of blepharospasm, photosensitivity and tremor.

Quetiapine was started as the medication of choice for treatment of her ongoing recurrent depressive disorder at a dose of 100 mg nocte following Royal Australian and New Zealand College of Psychiatry guidelines. This was very well tolerated and showed significant improvement in her mood.

Discussion

Pathogenesis and clinical classification

The pathogenesis of blepharospasm is not fully understood yet; however, it is believed to be multifactorial, involving a complex interplay of various competing factors including genetic predisposition, environmental stressors and underlying psychiatric conditions such as obsessive-compulsive disorder and anxiety. 6 Functional and anatomical abnormalities, including space-occupying lesions and imbalances of key neurotransmitters including dopamine, serotonin and acetylcholine, have also been implicated. 6

For this particular case, the clinical timeline – specifically the onset of side effects within weeks of lithium initiation and immediate resolution upon withdrawal of the agent – points to an acute or subacute medication dystonia rather than a ‘tardive’ phenomenon. Tardive dyskinesia (TD) is traditionally characterised by delayed onset following prolonged exposure to dopamine antagonists. While some literature categorises lithium-associated blepharospasm as ‘tardive’, 11 the rapid recovery in this case suggests that the mechanisms of late-onset supersensitivity are unlikely. Instead, an early-onset sensitisation of receptors or a direct dose-related toxic effect is a more plausible explanation for this acute presentation.

Differential diagnosis: Evaluating concomitant medications

A critical question is whether the blepharospasm and photophobia observed resulted from the patient’s other psychotropic agents, particularly lurasidone or the prescribed antidepressants. While antipsychotics are known triggers for extrapyramidal side effects (EPSEs), several factors strengthen the attribution to lithium:

Risk factor profile: The risk of developing TD is significantly elevated by factors such as older age (over 65), female gender, first-generation antipsychotic use, and high-dose/long-term exposure.2,7 While this patient’s demographic profile included some risk factors, her lurasidone dose remained stable and conservative throughout the treatment period.

Temporal stability: The most compelling evidence lies in the treatment timeline. The patient’s regimen of lurasidone and venlafaxine had been maintained at a steady state for years prior to the introduction of lithium. Crucially, the blepharospasm resolved entirely upon the cessation of lithium, despite the patient continuing the same dose of lurasidone. Antidepressant considerations: A systematic review indicates that venlafaxine is associated with a significantly lower rate of EPSEs compared to other antidepressant classes. 8 Furthermore, while mirtazapine has been linked to EPSEs in particular, reversible dyskinesias (typically these side effects resolve within 4 weeks of cessation), 9 the symptoms in this patient did not emerge until lithium was introduced and Mirtazapine had already been ceased for almost 8 weeks.

Lithium’s adverse side effects profile has been found to be extensive and diverse, impacting nearly every major body system. Parkinson-like symptoms such as dyskinesia and akathisia, as well as cerebellar symptoms, have been extensively documented as potential neurological side effects despite lithium being within the therapeutic range. Thus, providing biological plausibility for the occurrence of blepharospasm not only as a potential side effect but also as a clinical manifestation of lithium toxicity. 5

A case report published by Kumar and Grover 9 raised a direct link between lithium use and blepharospasm in a 41 year old male with a diagnosis of bipolar affective disorder with a concurrent history of chronic alcohol consumption. They hypothesised that there had been a reduction in dopamine receptors in the striatum or limbic system giving rise to extrapyramidal side effects. 9 In this case, it is important to note that the patient’s lithium level was below the therapeutic range and the side effect subsided after cessation of the offending drug.

Similarly, a literature review on lithium-associated movement disorder suggested lithium-induced dystonia as a result of a dopamine-acetylcholine imbalance. 10 Pharmacological studies suggest that lithium increases acetylcholine concentrations, potentially by inhibiting acetylcholinesterase, an enzyme that normally breaks down acetylcholine. Lithium is also believed to decrease dopamine release from the presynaptic terminal. 9

The authors also highlighted the possible implication of Gamma-aminobutyric acid (GABA) in the pathogenesis of dystonic features. Lithium significantly increased GABA levels by increasing GABA release and upregulating GABA-B receptors. Thus, disrupting the balance within the basal ganglia, ultimately leading to a disruption in the thalamocortical drive, which manifests as sustained muscle contractions. 9

Based on the patient’s history, photophobia could be considered the primary pathology, with blepharospasm occurring secondary to it. While both symptoms appeared to emerge simultaneously, the patient was unable to establish a definitive chronological order of onset. Furthermore, although the spasms noticeably worsened in the presence of bright light, she also experienced them in relatively dark environments and at night.

Lithium-associated blepharospasm is reported as a late onset and rare side effect. It is usually categorised as ‘tardive’ or late-onset dyskinesias. 11 However, based on the striking temporal correlation between lithium initiation, symptom onset, and rapid resolution – occurring independently of the stable antipsychotic and antidepressant regimen – lithium is the most probable cause of this acute dystonic presentation. Early-onset sensitisation of receptors or a similar factor is possible, but we are unable to comment on its plausibility or impact.

Conclusion

Blepharospasm is a rare but distressing side effect due to lithium that can occur during the early phase of treatment. Clinicians should remain vigilant for movement disorders even at low therapeutic levels, as the condition appears to be self-limiting upon cessation of the offending agent.
