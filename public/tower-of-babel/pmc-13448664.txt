Morphometric, biochemical, and mineral elements richness in the leaves of two pear species (Pyrus elaeagnifolia and Pyrus communis)
Authors: Aydın Uzun, Ahmet Sümbül, Mehmet Yaman, Ercan Yıldız, Hasan Talha Ünsal, Yusuf Murat Keçe, Adem Güneş, Kadir Uğurtan Yılmaz, Ramazan Bıçar, Merve İnanç, Yazgan Tunç, Ali Khadivi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study was conducted to determine the morphometric characteristics, mineral composition, and bioactive compound richness of the leaves of wild pear (Pyrus elaeagnifolia) genotypes naturally occurring in the Central Anatolia region and of commonly cultivated pear (Pyrus communis L.) varieties. To the best of our knowledge, the biochemical and mineral composition of the leaves of P. elaeagnifolia, a species endemic to Anatolia, has not been fully characterized. The study findings revealed significant genotypic variation in all measured characters. Petiole length varied between 3.38 (104) and 39.30 mm (Deveci), petiole thickness varied between 0.65 (27) and 1.93 mm (44), leaf width varied between 7.85 (27) and 30.65 mm (30), and leaf length varied between 24.98 (27) and 68.71 mm (104). Based on the mineral element content of the leaves, calcium (Ca), sulfur (S), phosphorus (P), and potassium (K) were identified as the dominant elements. In terms of mineral element content, P. elaeagnifolia was richer than P. communis. In leaves, Ca content varied between 96.37 (40) and 309.17 (58) mg/kg, K content varied between 47.18 (35) and 71.57 (36) mg/kg, P content varied between 33.93 (47) and 98.66 (30) mg/kg, and S content varied between 103.59 (47) and 149.92 (30) mg/kg dry weight. Total phenolic content (TPC) varied between 97.85 (115) and 165.34 (49) mg GAE g− 1 FW (milligrams of gallic acid equivalent per gram of fresh weight), total flavonoid content (TFC) varied between 26.95 (53) and 49.60 (102) mg QE g− 1 FW (milligrams of quercetin equivalent per gram of fresh weight), and antioxidant activity (AA) varied between 33.00% (67) and 59.50% (11–103),indicating considerable variation in bioactive compounds among genotypes and cultivars. In the principal component analysis (PCA), six principal components with eigenvalues ≥ 1.00 were identified, and the first three principal components explained 50.35% of the total variation. PCA and hierarchical clustering analyses have shown that genotypes/cultivars are clearly separated according to morphometric, biochemical characteristics, and mineral element content, and that these characteristics are effective tools in identifying genetic resources. The data obtained show that wild pear leaves have the potential to be used as a natural raw material in the food supplement, pharmaceutical, and cosmetic industries, and can be evaluated as a genetic resource in breeding programs.

Introduction

Türkiye’s diverse climate, spanning temperate, subtropical, and even tropical zones, combined with its unique geographical position, makes it a major gene center for numerous fruit species. For centuries, this rich land has fostered the growth of a wide array of fruit cultivars, both cultivated and wild, contributing to its valuable and extensive genetic resources43. Wild edible fruits have been the primary food source used by people for centuries, and these fruits were widely used in food and health areas for the Anatolian people9. Wild pear (P. elaeagnifolia), which belongs to the Rosaceae family and grows naturally in the arid areas of Anatolia’s Central and Eastern regions, is one of the popular wild edible fruits of Türkiye2. Anatolia is a significant gene center for the wild pear (P. elaeagnifolia), locally known as ‘Ahlat’ in Türkiye.

Wild pear, a popular fruit in terms of taste and aroma, is consumed fresh and dried in Anatolia. Wild pear leaves are also used as tea54. Wild pear fruits have high water retention capacity thanks to biochemical contents; therefore, wild pear fruits are used as a curative in treating diarrhea3. Wild pear fruits and leaves have been used for centuries for medicinal purposes among the public in treating heart diseases, diarrhea, blood purification, the vascular system, poisonous insect bites, the nervous system, teeth, and gum diseases23. It is known that wild pear leaves, which have diuretic properties, effectively treat bladder inflammation, high blood pressure, and kidney stones55.

Plant leaves are a valuable source of health-supporting compounds, including antioxidants, polyphenols, and vitamins. Polyphenols, in particular, are recognized for their potent antioxidant properties and other bioactive effects. Polyphenols are essential for fighting oxidative stress, mitigating inflammation, and supporting cardiovascular health. By mitigating oxidative damage to cells in several diseases, chronic conditions such as cancer, neurodegenerative diseases, and heart disease, polyphenols can significantly contribute to lowering the risk of cardiovascular disease34,37. People’s interest in minerals increases daily as their effects on treating various diseases are understood16. The leaves of fruit species such as apple, pear, and quince are richer in Ca, Mg, Fe, and K than their fruits. It is well documented that the mineral content of plant leaves, particularly in terms of K and Ca (3 to 6 times), is significantly higher than that found in the fruits of the same plant52. The human body needs mineral elements to maintain and stabilize daily homeostasis. Minerals are indispensable for many of the body’s biological processes, such as regulating cell signaling, nerve function, muscle function, bone integrity, fluid balance, and metabolic functions53. Foods rich in antioxidants are effective in preventing and treating mineral-related metabolic diseases41,42.

A limited number of studies on parts such as fruits, branches, and leaves of different Pyrus species have proven that plants of this species have rich biochemical and mineral content2,22,36. Wild pear leaves are a promising food ingredient due to their rich biochemical and mineral content. Their unique composition allows them to be incorporated into foods to support overall health and aid in disease prevention. Moreover, given their abundance of bioactive compounds, wild pear leaves also hold significant potential as valuable resources for the pharmaceutical and cosmetic industries52. Biochemical components and mineral contents found in plant leaves can be used as natural raw materials to enrich processed fruits’ health and sensory values44. Adding apple leaves to cloudy apple drinks positively impacts the final product’s biochemical composition (phenolic acids, dihydrochalcones, flavonols, etc.). This significantly boosts the drink’s antioxidant capacity28.

Biochemical compounds are crucial to a plant’s defense system18. Plants produce polyphenols and store them in various parts to protect against stress factors such as pests and UV rays. Interestingly, the concentration of these protective compounds is not in all parts of the plant; they tend to accumulate in the leaves7,45. Leaves generally accumulate more biochemical compounds than fruits. This is mainly due to increased exposure of leaves to biotic and abiotic stress factors and direct sunlight20. Polyphenols do more than scavenge free radicals and stop their production; they are also linked to various anti-inflammatory, antimicrobial, anti-cancer, and cardiovascular activities, etc52. In many Asian countries, including Japan, Korea, and Vietnam, the consumption of certain leaves as a source of bioactive compounds is common46. Therefore, leaves emerge as a promising new raw material source for the development of both nutraceuticals and functional food ingredients52.

Exploring new food sources and their bioactive components remains a vital area of research, especially as the global population continues to grow. This field is crucial for understanding how diet can promote health52. Therefore, it is essential to investigate wild pear leaves as one of the natural sources of bioactive compounds and mineral elements. Although the biochemical and mineral composition of P. communis fruits has been well-studied, there has been a recent surge in interest regarding the investigation of the leaves of this species for their biochemical and mineral content. To our knowledge, no studies on the leaf contents of the P. elaeagnifolia species were identified in the literature. Despite the considerable potential of wild plant species, there’s a notable deficiency of literature concerning the biochemical and mineral composition of plant leaves. This study was conducted to address a gap in the literature by examining the morphometric properties, mineral content, total phenolic content, total flavonoid content, and antioxidant activities of P. elaeagnifolia and P. communis leaves, which are widely distributed in Central Anatolia.

Materials and methods

Plant material

The study was conducted in the “Pear Adaptation Parcel” in (38°42’45” N 35°32’28” E), the Agricultural Research and Application Center of Erciyes University, Republic of Türkiye. Geographic location of the studied pear genotypes and cultivars is shown in Fig. 1, generated using ArcGIS software version 10.110. The climate of the region is continental, with hot and dry summers and cold and snowy winters. The annual mean rainfall in the area is approximately 560 mm, and there are significant temperature differences between day and night. In the study, 27 wild pear genotypes from the species P. elaeagnifolia (3, 11–103, 12, 22, 27, 30, 32, 35, 36, 37, 40, 44, 47, 49, 53, 58, 65, 67, 69, 77, 102, 104, 106, 112, 114, 115 and Bodur Ahlat) and two commercial cultivars from the species P. communis (Deveci and Ankara) were used as study material. The genotypes constituting the study material were selected from the province of Kayseri, located in the center of Central Anatolia. Pear genotypes and cultivars that are 7 years old were grown in 3 × 3 m planting intervals. Annual cultural maintenance of pear trees (irrigation, fertilization, disease and pest control, etc.) was carried out regularly. The study investigated the morphometric characteristics, mineral content, and biochemical properties of leaves from both wild pear genotypes and cultivated pear cultivars. Leaf samples were collected from healthy, fully developed, and mature leaves (at the fruit ripening stage) in 2024 and 2025. To ensure homogeneity and minimize variation, leaf samples were collected from the middle sections of the trees, the sunlit areas, and from different sides of the trees. For each genotype/cultivar, three trees were selected, and each tree was considered as one replicate (n = 3). The experiment was arranged in a completely randomized design with three replications per genotype/cultivar.

The specimens were formally identified by Prof. Dr. Aydın Uzun. Herbarium voucher specimens, labeled PE-2156 and PC-2156, have been deposited in the publicly accessible herbarium of Erciyes University, Faculty of Agriculture, Kayseri, Türkiye.

Morphometric analysis

Analyses were planned in three replications and were performed on 100 leaves in each replication. The morphometric characteristics of the leaves, such as leaf length (LL), leaf width (LW), petiole length (PL), and petiole thickness (PT), were measured using a precision digital caliper (Mitutoyo® Absolute Digimatic Caliper, 0–150 mm range, ± 0.01 mm accuracy).

Biochemical analysis

Extraction

Biochemical compounds of the leaves were obtained by the maceration method in a methanol solution at 25 °C for 24 h in a shaker. After the resulting solution was filtered with the help of filter paper (110 mm ø, Whatman No. 2), the supernatant was centrifuged at 11,200×g for 15 min15. The methanol extracts obtained were used to determine the biochemical content (total phenolic content, total flavonoid content, and antioxidant activity) of wild pear and pear leaves.

Total phenolic content (TPC): TPC of leaves from wild pear genotypes and cultivated pear varieties was determined using the Folin–Ciocalteu colorimetric method, following the protocol outlined by Lamuela-Raventós29. Briefly, 500 µL of fresh leaf extract was combined with 4.1 mL of distilled water, 100 µL of Folin–Ciocalteu reagent, and 1 mL of 2% sodium carbonate (Na₂CO₃). The mixture was incubated in the dark at room temperature (24 ± 1 °C) for 2 h, after which absorbance was recorded at 760 nm using a UV–Vis spectrophotometer (Brand: Unico, Model: UV/Vis 2100) The absorbance values were subsequently converted to GAE using a standard calibration curve prepared with gallic acid. The results were expressed as mg GAE g− 1 FW.

Total flavonoid content (TFC): TFC in leaves of wild pear genotypes and cultivated pear varieties was determined following the protocol described by Demir et al.8. Briefly, 1000 µL of each leaf extract was mixed with 3.3 mL of methanol, followed by the addition of 0.1 mL of 10% aluminum chloride hexahydrate (AlCl₃·6 H₂O) and 0.1 mL of 1 M potassium acetate (CH₃COOK) solution. The reaction mixtures were incubated at room temperature (24 ± 1 °C) for 2 h, and absorbance was measured at 415 nm using a UV–Vis spectrophotometer (Brand: unico, Model: UV/Vis 2100). The flavonoid content was quantified using a standard calibration curve prepared with quercetin and expressed as mg QE g− 1 FW.

Antioxidant activity (AA)

The AA of leaf extracts was determined using the DPPH (2,2-diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl) radical scavenging assay, as described by Sharma and Bhat39. Briefly, 100 µL of each leaf extract was mixed with 2.9 mL of ethanol and 1 mL of 0.26 mM DPPH solution. The reaction mixture was vortexed and incubated in the dark at room temperature for 30 min to allow radical scavenging activity. Absorbance was then recorded at 517 nm using a UV–Vis spectrophotometer (Systonic 119, Haryana, India). Mineral content analysis

Leaf mineral analysis, including aluminum (Al), calcium (Ca), potassium (K), magnesium (Mg), manganese (Mn), sodium (Na), nickel (Ni), phosphorus (P), lead (Pb), and sulfur (S) of wild pear genotypes and cultivated pear varieties was conducted in three replications, with 100 leaves analyzed per replication. Healthy and fully developed leaves, representative of each tree and sampled from all sides of the canopy, were used for the analysis.

Initially, the collected leaves were finely ground into powder. Subsequently, 0.25 g of the ground leaf material was digested with 0.5 mL of nitric acid (HNO₃) and 2 mL of hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂), following the procedure described by Miller32. The digested samples were then oven-dried at 68 °C for 48 h using a forced-air drying oven (Nükleon NKD1000, Ankara, Turkey)57. The drying process was followed by a three-stage heating protocol as reported by Mertens31: (i) 5 min at 145 °C with 75% relative humidity, (ii) 10 min at 180 °C with 90% relative humidity, and (iii) 10 min at 100 °C with 40% relative humidity.

Finally, mineral concentrations were measured in terms of dry weight (mg/kg DW) using inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectrometry (ICP–OES; Perkin-Elmer Optima 2100 DV, Shelton, CT 06484–4794, USA), as described by Kızılkaya et al.27.

Statistical analysis

To increase data reliability and minimize temporal variation, statistical analyses were performed using the average of data from both years. The means of the data obtained in the study were subjected to variance analysis (ANOVA) with the JMP Pro 17 statistical software package. Differences between means were compared using the Least Significant Difference (LSD) test at a 5% significance level (p ≤ 0.05). To evaluate the variations among the traits, minimum (Min), maximum (Max), mean, standard deviation (SD), and coefficient of variation (CV) values were calculated. PCA and hierarchical clustering analysis were performed to reveal the effectiveness of the features examined in the study and the diversity of the individuals examined in the study. PCA and heatmap hierarchical clustering analysis were carried out using the JMP Pro 17 statistical software package (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, ABD). To prevent scale discrepancies between the data, the data were standardized, and PCA and hierarchical heatmap analysis were performed. Heatmap hierarchical clustering was performed using the Ward method based on Euclidean distance.

Results and discussion

Morphometric analysis

Studies on leaf characteristics of wild pear (P. elaeagnifolia) genotypes are limited. To our knowledge, this study described the morphometric characteristics, mineral contents, and biochemical contents of wild pear leaves for the first time in the literature. To evaluate the changes in leaf characteristics, biochemical characteristics, and mineral composition, min., max., mean, SD, and CV values were calculated. CV is a straightforward statistical parameter that is not dependent on measurement units and allows for comparative evaluation of variability across traits with different scales26. CV indicates a significant degree of variation among the studied genotypes and cultivars. Characteristics with a CV greater than 20% indicate more significant differences among genotypes, making them helpful in distinguishing between genotypes. High CV values suggest a wider selection range, whereas low CV values denote greater stability among genotypes25. Statistically significant differences regarding leaf characteristics were detected among genotypes and cultivars (Table 1). The variation (CV) of leaf characteristics was observed in petiole length (69.30%), leaf width (31.69%), leaf length (26.87%), and petiole thickness (26.51%), respectively. As no studies utilizing similar statistical methods exist for P. elaeagnifolia, our findings were compared with research conducted on other Pyrus species. Compared with the studies conducted on other Pyrus species such as P. spinosa (CV = 25.23–45.62%)50, P. glabra (CV = 17.27–36.63%)35, P. syriaca (CV = 19.40–36.80%)26, and P. communis (CV = 14.96–24.19%), P. pyrifolia (CV = 3.82–15.76%), P. glabra (CV = 15.73–40.46%), and P. syriaca (CV = 12.08–36.07%)21, our results show a relatively high variability in leaf characteristics.

Petiole length varied between 3.38 (104) and 39.30 mm (Deveci), with a mean value of 13.86 ± 9.61 mm. Petiole thickness varied between 0.65 (27) and 1.93 mm (44), with a mean value of 1.15 ± 0.31 mm. Leaf width varied between 7.85 (27) and 30.65 mm (30), with a mean value of 17.91 ± 5.68 mm. Leaf length varied between 24.98 (27) and 68.71 mm (104), with a mean value of 44.54 ± 11.97 mm. Overall, leaf dimensions did not differ markedly between species, although P. elaeagnifolia leaves tended to be smaller than those of P. communis cultivars. However, it was observed that the leaves of the genotypes belonging to the species P. elaeagnifolia were relatively smaller than the leaves of the cultivars belonging to the species P. communis. Khadivi et al.26 reported leaf length between 22.70 and 70.75 mm, leaf width between 9.18 and 35.20 mm, petiole length between 7.84 and 47.88 mm, and petiole thickness between 0.54 and 1.69 mm in the study conducted on the P. syriaca species. Vidakovic et al.50 reported leaf length between 1.34 and 7.32 cm, leaf width between 0.20 and 1.86 cm, and petiole length between 0.06 and 3.62 cm in the study conducted on the P. spinosa species. Parvin et al.35 reported leaf length between 3.53 and 10.26 cm, leaf width between 0.83 and 2.40 cm, petiole length between 1.33 and 4.03 cm, and petiole thickness between 0.70 and 1.40 cm in the study conducted on the P. glabra species. Jalilian et al.21 reported leaf length between 2.74 and 22.46 cm, leaf width between 1.15 and 36.07 cm, petiole length between 1.36 and 40.46 cm, and petiole thickness between 0.81 and 34.34 cm in the study conducted on four different Pyrus species (P. communis, P. pyrifolia, P. glabra, and P. syriaca). Leaves are paramount for absorbing sunlight during photosynthesis. Consequently, larger leaf dimensions can promote increased primary metabolite production, thereby creating favorable conditions for secondary metabolite synthesis24, which is consistent with our study findings.

Biochemical analysis

Data on the biochemical contents of wild pear genotypes and pear cultivars are presented in Table 2, and statistically significant differences were determined between the biochemical contents. These differences reveal the biochemical diversity of the genotypes and cultivars examined under the same ecological and climatic conditions in the same experimental plot. The CV value of biochemical contents ranged from 14.34% (TPC) to 19.88% (TFC). The TPC value of the leaves varied between 97.85 (115) and 165.34 (49) mg GAE g-1, while the average TPC value was determined as 127.10 mg GAE g-1. The highest TPC was followed by 155.37 (40) mg GAE g-1, 150.49 (53) mg GAE g-1, 149.27 (35) mg GAE g-1, 144.60 (3) mg GAE g-1, and 142.77 (114) mg GAE g-1, respectively. The TFC value of the leaves varied between 26.95 (53) and 49.60 (102) mg QE g− 1, while the average TFC value was determined as 38.12 mg QE g− 1. The highest TFC was followed by 47.63 (112) mg QE g− 1, 47.38 (49) mg QE g− 1, 46.60 (32) mg QE g− 1, 45.54 (40) mg QE g− 1, and 45.13 (114) mg QE g− 1, respectively. The AA value of the leaves varied between 33.00% (67) and 59.50% (11–103), while the average AA value was determined as 45.71%. The highest AA was followed by 57.50% (69), 55.50% (77), and 55.00% (22, 44, 53), respectively. To the best of our knowledge, no studies have been found that investigate the biochemical composition of P. elaeagnifolia leaves. Wojdyło et al.52 determined TPC between 2004.7 and 3764.6 mg/100 g and TFC between 2393.5 and 5108.9 mg/100 g in leaves of pear cultivars. The same researchers determined TPC between 92.3 and 191.7 mg/100 g and TFC between 3155.7 and 3552.7 mg/100 g in apple leaves; TPC between 251.9 and 498.7 mg/100 g and TFC between 1653.2 and 2310.6 mg/100 g in quince leaves. These results show that pear leaves have higher values than other pome fruits regarding biochemical content.

Various biotic and abiotic stress factors, such as cultivation location, environmental conditions, drought resistance, and frost resistance, increase the phenolic content of leaves compared with fruits20,48. Leaves exposed to intense sunlight exhibit a higher concentration of polyphenolic compounds, likely due to increased gene expression and enhanced phenolic biosynthesis. Notably, the content of flavonol derivatives in sun-exposed leaves can be three times greater than in shaded leaves20. Flavonols, consumed in daily diets at levels typically ranging from 5 to 40 mg, are known to decrease the incidence of cardiovascular diseases and inhibit the proliferation of cancer cells5. When evaluated from a health perspective, biochemical compounds together offer remarkable advantages. Phenolics and flavonoids, in particular, are well-established antioxidants with proven anti-inflammatory and anticancer properties. These biochemical compounds work together to neutralize free radicals and help prevent diseases related to oxidative stress12. Antioxidants play a crucial role in neutralizing free radicals, thereby reducing oxidative stress. Oxidative stress is a major contributor to chronic diseases like cardiovascular disorders, diabetes, and cancer40. Phenolic and flavonoid compounds are considered as crucial as primary antioxidants due to their powerful free radical scavenging capabilities and anti-inflammatory properties30,56. The study revealed that the leaves of both wild pear genotypes and cultivated pear cultivars are rich in biochemical compounds, indicating their significant potential as a valuable source of natural bioactive compounds. These leaves show promise for inclusion in foods, dietary supplements, or nutraceuticals, offering potential benefits for human health. Investigating the bioavailability, clinical efficacy, and therapeutic potential of these compounds may create new opportunities for innovative uses of pear leaves in health-promoting products and medical treatments.

Principal component analysis (PCA)

PCA is a powerful statistical technique. It highlights the most essential features by determining differences in the characteristics studied. Researchers commonly use PCA to explain how much impact specific characteristics have or to illustrate variations among individuals within a study43. The first three principal components save significant time characterizing genotypes and cultivars19. PCA is a popular tool among breeding and population genetics researchers because of its powerful capabilities. When using PCA, it’s recommended to consider components with eigenvalues greater than 1 to evaluate the significance of the principal component19,33. Six components with eigenvalues greater than 1 in the study explained 74.73% of the total variation (Table 4). The effect of the first three components is more than 25% of the total variation, which shows the heterogeneity in the gene pool33. The first three principal components explained 50.35% of the variation. PCA1 explained 22.90% of the variation, PCA2 explained 17.30% of the variation, and PCA3 explained 10.15% of the variation. The effects of the examined traits in the first three principal components differed. The degree of impact of the features discussed on the principal components is considered significant if it is 0.30 and above6. This approach is used to identify essential features in the data and select principal components that explain a significant portion of the total variation1. The leaf length (0.42), TFC (0.41), LW (0.39), and petiole length (0.30) characteristics showed a high positive effect on the first principal component, which explained 22.90% of the total variance. The S (0.42), Ca (0.41), Mg (0.40), and Na (0.36) characteristics showed a high positive effect on the second principal component, which explained 17.30% of the total variance. While AA (0.44) and Mn (0.34) features had high positive effects on the third principal component, which explained 10.15% of the total variance, P (-0.43) and K (-0.35) features had high negative loadings. In studies conducted on different pear species, the first three principal components were reported as 28.06% in P. syriaca species by Khadivi et al.26, 94.62% in P. spinosa species by Vidakovic et al.50, 44.35% in P. glabra species, and 44.00% in P. syriaca species by Parvin et al.35, while Jalilian et al.21 reported the two main components as 60.25% in four different pear species. It is thought that the different results between the studies may be due to the variability of the study materials (genotypes and cultivars) and the diversity of the characteristics examined.

Hierarchical cluster analysis

Hierarchical cluster analysis, represented through a heatmap, was employed to classify wild pear genotypes, pear cultivars, and leaf-related characteristics based on similarity patterns. The heatmap displays a color-coded matrix that visually conveys trait intensities and clustering trends. By minimizing within-group variance, this method effectively identifies natural groupings and is commonly used to analyze complex multivariate datasets11,13,14,43. The analysis was performed using Ward’s minimum variance clustering method and Euclidean distance as the similarity metric. The resulting dendrogram enabled the grouping of wild pear genotypes and pear cultivars, and characteristics according to expression similarities. The heatmap color gradient, ranging from blue to red, visually indicates the increasing magnitude of characteristic values of wild pear genotypes and pear cultivars. The results obtained from both PCA and hierarchical cluster analysis demonstrated clear trends across pear genotype and cultivars. As a result of the hierarchical clustering analysis based on the morphometric, biochemical, and mineral properties of the leaves of wild pear genotypes and pear cultivars, two main clusters were formed (Fig. 3). The two main groups were divided into two sub-clusters within themselves. Conclusion

This study presents the first comprehensive characterization of the morphometric, biochemical, and mineral properties of leaves from wild pear (P. elaeagnifolia) genotypes as well as two commercially important pear varieties (P. communis cv. Deveci and Ankara). Genotypes such as ‘77’ with high mineral content levels and ‘49’ with high biochemical content show significant potential for food and health applications. Genotype 49, with high total phenolic (165.34 mg GAE g-1 FW) and total flavonoid (47.38 mg QE g− 1 FW) contents, shows significant potential for functional food and nutraceutical applications. Genotype 77, with high levels of Ca (302.04 mg/kg), Mg (3.87 mg/kg), and S (141.57 mg/kg), shows significant potential for use in dietary supplements or the fortification of functional food products. However, some genotypes accumulate relatively high levels of Al and Pb, which are toxic trace metals. Comprehensive safety assessments should be conducted for these genotypes in terms of food or health-related applications. PCA and hierarchical clustering analyses grouped genotypes based on morphometric, biochemical, and mineral element traits, demonstrating that these traits are effective tools for identifying genetic resources. In conclusion, the findings revealed that wild pear leaves showed significant variation in morphometric characteristics, phenolic, flavonoid, and antioxidant content, and mineral content. Rich in biochemical compounds and mineral elements, wild pear leaves are a valuable natural raw material source for traditional medicine, food supplements, cosmetics, and pharmaceuticals. Future research should focus on the identification and quantification of specific phenolic compounds, assessments of bioavailability and bioaccessibility, in vitro and in vivo studies of biological activity (e.g., antimicrobial, anti-inflammatory, anticancer), and the development of standardized leaf extracts for industrial use.
