Eight-year tuberculosis epidemic trends in the Republic of Congo, a high TB burden country: progress and gaps towards end-TB targets
Authors: Freisnel Hermeland Mouzinga, Darrel Ornelle Elion Assiana, Mita Naomie Merveille Dello, Breli Bonheur Ngouama, Franck Hardain Okemba Okombi, Baurel Arnaud Akiera, Martin P Grobusch, Alain Maxime Mouanga, Viny Andzi Elenga, Etienne Nguimbi, Francine Ntoumi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEvaluating progress towards WHO End Tuberculosis Strategy goals is crucial for high burden countries like the Republic of Congo. This study analysed trends in tuberculosis (TB) incidence, treatment, care quality and operational research activities from 2016 to 2023 to assess the country’s control efforts.MethodsAn 8-year retrospective study of TB incidence/mortality trends was performed using a Joint Point Analysis V.5.2.0. We extracted annual national and WHO TB programme data for the period under review.ResultsFrom 2016 to 2023, the TB incidence rate decreased by 2.6% (annual percentage change (APC)=−0.33, 95% CI− 0.5 to −0.005; p<0.05). The proportion of bacteriologically confirmed cases significantly increased (APC=4.2, 95% CI 0.9 to 7.7; p<0.001), while treatment coverage rose by 22% (APC=2.81, 95% CI 1.1 to 4.5; p<0.05). The proportion of notified patients with TB tested for HIV significantly increased (APC=25, 95% CI 2.6 to 52.63; p<0.05), but the rate of patients with TB testing HIV positive decreased significantly (APC=−13.9, 95% CI −23.9 to −2.6; p<0.05). The proportion of unsuccessful treatment outcomes showed a non-significant decline, with an APC of 4.90% (95% CI −11.7% to 2.3%; p=0.1), while TB-related deaths increased non-significantly, with an APC of 3.76% (95% CI −1.3% to 9.2%; p=0.12). Patients with TB/HIV on ART increased by 51% (APC=6.4, 95% CI −23.9 to −2.6; p<0.05). No operational research activity has been carried out throughout the review period.ConclusionThe observed progress was insufficient, as the Republic of Congo failed to meet the 20% TB incidence reduction target for 2020 and is unlikely to achieve the 50% reduction goal for 2025. More investment in case detection, diagnosis, treatment quality and the implementation of operational research activities is needed to achieve global goals.Keywords: Health policies and all other topics, Incidence, Tuberculosis, Universal Health Care

Introduction

Despite being preventable, treatable and curable, with a global treatment coverage of approximately 70%, tuberculosis (TB) continues to affect around 10 million people annually and kill about 3 individuals every minute.1 2 Urgent action is needed to achieve the WHO End TB strategy by 2035, launched in 2015 and adopted by all United Nations member states, which aims to end the TB epidemic by 2035.3 The strategy outlines progressive milestones (2020: respect reduction of 35% in deaths and 20% in incidence; 2025: 75% fewer deaths, 50% lower incidence) and ambitious targets (2030: 90% fewer deaths, 80% lower incidence; 2035: 95% fewer deaths, 90% lower incidence), while ensuring no family faces catastrophic TB-related costs compared with the 2015 levels.3 Although a net global reduction of around 8.7% in TB incidence and 19% in TB-related deaths has been observed between 2015 and 2021, these remain far from the WHO End TB Strategy milestone of 2020. This indicates that both the WHO End TB Strategy milestones for 2020 on reducing TB incidence and mortality compared to the 2015 baseline were globally missed.14,6 It is concerning to note that the incidence of TB rose by 3.9% between 2020 and 2022, reversing the downward trend observed in recent years. This rise was likely due mainly to the disruption of health services caused by the COVID-19 pandemic.14,8

Studies examining national trends towards WHO targets are crucial in high TB burden countries as they make it possible to assess both the epidemic trends and the remaining challenges to achieving universal health coverage. These studies also play a critical role in re-defining national priorities while highlighting gaps and bottlenecks in TB management, research activities and innovation. By understanding local trends and challenges, it becomes possible to implement more targeted and effective interventions, enhance collaboration among stakeholders and adapt policies to address emerging issues, such as those caused by the COVID-19 pandemic.59,11

Yet, in the Republic of Congo, which is one of the 30 high-TB burden countries, no study of this kind had been carried out to date, neither before nor after the COVID-19 pandemic. The aim of this study was therefore to assess the burden of TB and trends in TB incidence and mortality in the Republic of Congo from 2016 to 2023 as the 2015 baseline data were not available.

Additionally, the study aimed to provide data on the characteristics of TB treatment, quality of TB care and the operational research activities or survey conducted across the country prior to and after the COVID-19 period.

Data sources and analysis

TB annual data from 2016 to 2023 were extracted from the National Tuberculosis Programme (PNLT) report and the WHO annual TB report to calculate the annual percentage change in the mortality and incidence rate at national level. Additional data were gathered from field investigations or returns, routine data collections and available population-based data sources to analyse the change in the annual treatment characteristics and operational research activities. TB notification data were obtained from the PNLT DHIS2 surveillance system and reflect confirmed cases reported through routine programme operations. TB incidence estimates presented in this study were those published by the PNLT, produced in collaboration with the WHO country office. These were analysed separately from the notification data.

The programme collects aggregated data quarterly from Diagnostic and Reporting Centres across the 12 departments of the country using the DHIS2 (District Health Information Software 2) database. Facility Focal Point Persons collect all disaggregated sociodemographic data from patients at their respective facilities before compiling the data into hard copies. Completed data are then submitted to the Departmental TB Coordinator, who performs quality checks and aggregates the data from all reporting facilities within the department. The aggregated data are then sent to the PNLT national office, where data from all 12 departments across the country are managed and stored in the DHIS2 database.

Data for mortality analysis were also collected from the PNLT. The PNLT receives relevant patient information through official facility TB treatment registers or the DHIS2 database. These records track important patient details, including the date when treatment (therapeutic or preventive) was initiated, as well as the outcomes such as death, treatment failure, loss to follow-up, completion of treatment and cure. No formal screening or eligibility assessment was applied to individual studies, as the analysis was based on aggregated national data available from official health programmes.

A Joinpoint regression analysis provided by the US National Cancer Institute (Using Joinpoint Regression Programme, V.5.2.0) was applied to calculate the annual percentage change (APC) and its 95% CI.12 This analysis mainly focused on incidence and other dependent parameters, such as mortality, loss-to-follow-up, treatment failures, treatment completion and diagnostic coverage. The age-standardised rates were adjusted to the global norm to ensure comparability within the group. The Joinpoint regression analysis was based on a grid search method with a minimum number of two observations from a Joinpoint. The parametric method and permutation test were employed for model selection. In this model, period in calendar years was the only independent variable, while the variables mentioned above (mortality, loss-to-follow-up, treatment failures, etc) were treated as dependent variables, assuming homoscedasticity over time for each test. The calculated APC represents the average yearly rate of change, reflecting the trend’s slope over time.

Finally, research data on TB in the Republic of Congo were gathered by searching for published articles (both review and original) on PubMed and INHARI website. The search was conducted in English using the following keywords: ‘Tuberculosis’, ‘Republic of Congo’, ‘Brazzaville’, ‘operational research’, ‘TB control’ and ‘Survey’, with the intention to evaluate the third pillar of the WHO End TB programme, which focuses on intensified research and innovation.

Operational research was defined as any strategy, interventions, tool or knowledge applied to enhance the quality, coverage, effectiveness or performance of the TB health programme in which the research was conducted.13

Patient and public involvement

Patients and the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination plans of this research.

Result

Overall APC trend in TB incidence

Between 2016 and 2023, the overall TB incidence rate in the Republic of Congo decreased by 2.6%, from 379 to 369 cases per 100 000 inhabitants. The APC over the entire study period was −0.33 (APC=−0.33; (95% CI − 0.5 to −0.005); p=0.025), reflecting the average yearly decline. This decline occurred in three distinct phases: first, from 2016 to 2018 (APC=−0.56), followed by a slight increase from 2018 to 2021 (APC=0.08), and then a more significant decline from 2021 to 2023 (APC=−1.07) (figure 1A).

(A) Trend in the annual tuberculosis incidence rates from 2016 to 2023. (B) Annual TB incidence trend in females. (C) Annual TB incidence trend in males (M). APC, annual percentage change; TB, tuberculosis.

Throughout the review period, TB incidence rates were consistently higher in males (418–437 per 100 000) than in females (318–333 per 100 000). While both genders showed a general decline in incidence, the decrease was statistically significant only in females (APC −0.6278; 95% CI −1.0533 to 0.2005; p=0.011484) (figure 1B,C).

APC by diagnostic approaches and the number of TB testing or diagnosis and treatment centres

A significant shift in TB diagnostic approaches was observed, revealing an inverse relationship between clinically diagnosed and bacteriologically confirmed cases over the 8-year period. The proportion of clinically diagnosed TB cases significantly declined, with an APC of −3.35 (95% CI −5.76 to −0.8; p<0.001). Conversely, bacteriologically confirmed cases showed a significant annual increase, with an APC of 4.2 (95% CI 0.9 to 7.7; p<0.001). The sharpest decline in clinically diagnosed cases occurred between 2021 to 2023, with an APC of −10.58, while bacteriologically confirmed cases rose markedly during the same period (APC 15.90) (figure 2A).

(A) Graph showing the annual proportion of patients diagnosed bacteriologically and clinically from 2016 to 2023. (B) Graph illustrating the annual change in the number of TB diagnosis centre in the country. APC, annual percentage change; TB, tuberculosis.

From 2016 to 2023, the APC in the number of TB testing or diagnosis and treatment centres across the country increased by 5.94 (95% CI −2.2.6 to 12.8; p=0.06). The most significant rise occurred between 2021 and 2023, with an APC of 30.84 (95% CI 15.4 to 48.2; p<0.05) (figure 2B).

A pairwise comparison of TB notification rates between children (0–14 years) and adults (15 years and above) revealed an increasing trend for both groups. However, the rate of increase was significantly higher among adults, with an APC of 5.10 compared with 1.20 for children (p<0.05) (online supplemental figure 1 and table 1).

APC by TB treatment and outcomes

Over the entire review period, treatment coverage increased by around 20.8%, with an APC of 2.81 (95% CI 1.1 to 4.5; p<0.05). From 2016 to 2021, the APC increased by 1.68, but after 2021, a significant rebound was observed, with the APC rising to 6.9 (figure 3A).

(A) Joinpoint regression showing trends in TB treatment coverage. (B) Joinpoint regression showing trends in TB annual treatment outcomes. (C) Joinpoint regression showing trends in TB annual unsuccessful treatment outcomes (died and failed). (D) Joinpoint regression showing trends in TB annual unsuccessful treatment outcomes (lost to follow-up). APC, annual percentage change; TB, tuberculosis.

Treatment success showed an insignificant increase with an APC of 1.7 (95% CI −1.2 to 4.8; p=0.2), switching from 70% in 2016 to 81% in 2023. Regarding unsuccessful outcomes, an insignificant increase in deaths trends was observed, with an APC of 3.7 (95% CI −1.3 to 9.2; p=0.12). However, a significant decline in patients lost to follow-up was observed between 2021 and 2023, with an APC of −15.8 (95% CI −24.6 to −5.99; p<0.05) (figure 3B–D).

APC in TB/HIV integrated services

HIV testing rate among patients with TB increased significantly over time, with an APC of 25.2 (95% CI 2.6 to 52.63; p<0.05). The proportion of notified patients with TB tested for HIV rose from 29% in 2016 to 82.2% in 2023. Conversely, the trend of patients with TB testing HIV positive declined significantly, with an APC of −13.9 (95% CI −23.9 to −2.6; p<0.05) (figure 4A,B).

Joinpoint regression showing TB/HIV data in (A) HIV diagnosis coverage in patients with TB. (B) Proportion of patients with TB who tested HIV positive. (C) Proportion of patients on ART. (D) Proportion of patients with HIV on cotrimoxazole. APC, annual percentage change; ART, antiretroviral therapy; TB, tuberculosis.

The proportion of patients with TB co-infected with HIV on antiretroviral therapy (ART) increased from 43.3% in 2016 to 85.1% in 2023, with an APC of 6.4 (95% CI 0.08 to 13.15; p<0.05). From 2016 to 2021, the APC was 4.015 (95% CI −16.1 to 29.1; p=0.6) before rising to 15.08 from 2021 to 2023 (95% CI −56.13 to 201.9; p=0.67). For patients with TB receiving cotrimoxazole (an antibiotic for opportunistic infections), the APC showed an insignificant increase of 5.9 (95% CI −1.9 to 14.30; p=0.5), rising from 38.2% in 2017 to 78.1% in 2023, with a notable APC surge of 21.84 from 2021 to 2023 (figure 4C,D; online supplemental figure 2 and table 1).

Drug-resistant (DR) TB

Due to limited data available on TB drug resistance from the years prior to and during the COVID-19 pandemic (2016–2019 and 2020–2021), we considered the timeframe of 2022 to 2023 to analyse the drug resistance situation in the Republic of Congo.

In 2022, a total of 13 489 TB cases were notified, with 4125 patients (30.6%) benefiting from RR testing. In 2023, among 14 370 notified TB cases, 5914 (41.2%) benefited from RR testing, reflecting a relative increase of 34.6% in testing coverage. Despite increased testing, the RR detection rate decreased from 3.2% (131/4125) in 2022 to 3.7% (216/5914) in 2023, a relative decline of 26.9%. Of those diagnosed, 61.1% (131 cases, 86 successes) were initiated on treatment in 2022, compared with 95.1% (216 cases, 165 successes) in 2023. This represents a relative increase of 55.7% in treatment coverage and an improvement in treatment success from 65.6% to 76.6%. However, given the limited 2-year window and small sample size, no statistical testing was applied (as join point requires at least three data points for trend analysis), and the observed differences should be interpreted descriptively rather than inferentially (figure 5).

National operational research activities or survey

To evaluate the efforts made to achieve the third pillar of the WHO End TB programme, which focuses on intensified research and innovation, this review included articles published between 2016 and 2023, sourced from PubMed or INHARI. A total of eight articles with data collected in the country were identified, of which seven were original research articles and one a review.14,20 Notably, only one article addresses operational research. This operational research article provides a detailed explanation of managing cutaneous TB, emphasising the importance of establishing a histological diagnosis and administering specific treatment for the condition.19

Discussion

The purpose of this article was to provide a comprehensive overview of the current TB situation in the Republic of Congo, highlighting both areas of progress and aspects that need improvement to meet the goals set by the WHO End TB strategy. A significant nationwide 2% reduction in TB incidence was observed. This decline could reflect the general upward trend in the programme’s activities, including the increase in TB diagnosis and treatment coverage, which rose from 54% in 2016 to 65% in 2023. However, part of the decline in incidence may also reflect under-reporting, particularly from 2016 to 2021, when there were only 40 testing or diagnosis and treatment centres across the country’s 12 departments, highlighting a significant diagnostic capacity gap.

Efforts to address this issue began in 2021, leading to a significant increase in testing and treatment Centres, from 40 in 2021 to 73 in 2023, greatly enhancing the country’s diagnostic infrastructure. Additionally, a new sample transportation network from peripheral areas to designated diagnostic sites was established, improving accessibility and efficiency. Owed to these efforts, the national programme has received endorsement through the implementation of the Quality Management System, using the SLIPTA (Stepwise Laboratory Quality Improvement Process Towards Accreditation) checklist, as well as through the strengthening of the TB laboratory network, with the TB-Lab checklist. These recognitions further underscore the positive impact of the TB programme in its management. Despite these improvements, the national annual decline in TB incidence remains at −0.33%, well below the global target of a 4% annual decline set for 2020. This underscores the urgent need for sustained and intensified efforts to meet the global TB elimination goals. While the magnitude of decline is lower, it follows a similar downward trend with a pooled analysis of central African countries reporting annual TB incidence declines of around 1.4–2.5% in Cameroon, 1.1–1.9% in Chad, 1.3%2.4% in Central African Republic and 0–1.2% in DRC.21 We also observed a disproportionate drop in the incidence of TB in women compared with men. As well as equal TB diagnostic and treatment care being provided (to the best of our knowledge) across the country, this could indeed reflect underlying gender-related factors, including differences in healthcare-seeking behaviours and treatment adherence. Based on day-to-day observations in TB centres, feedback from healthcare providers and data from the literature, men tend to defer seeking care longer than women, and male patients are generally the worst adherents to TB treatment.22 23

When looking at the APC by TB diagnostic approach, a notable shift was observed in the trends of bacteriologically confirmed patient with TB, alongside a decrease in clinically diagnosed patients. The APC of bacteriologically confirmed cases increased from 4.2 (2016–2021) to 15.9 (2021–2023), which can be seen as a positive development towards more accurate and reliable TB diagnosis. However, given the high proportion of clinically diagnosed patients with TB in certain high TB burden countries, such as Tanzania (56%), Uganda (49.8%) and Kenya (49.7%),24 South Africa (35.9%)25 and China (61.8%),26 the decreasing trend in clinically diagnosed cases may reflect a low index of suspicion among healthcare practitioners, particularly for subclinical TB, making diagnosis more challenging.24 27

Over the review period, treatment coverage improved significantly, with a 20.8% overall increase, reflecting expanded access to TB treatment. However, the treatment success rate showed only a modest rise, from 70% in 2016 to 81% in 2023, suggesting limited progress in improving treatment outcomes.

Trends in unsuccessful outcomes revealed a slight annual increase in deaths (3.7%) from 2016 to 2023, while the rate of patients lost to follow-up declined significantly from 2021 to 2023 (APC −15.8%). This improvement may be linked to efforts addressing previous challenges, such as the persistent shortage of anti-TB drugs, particularly between 2012 and 2017. During this period, the Global Fund grant was specifically awarded to cover around 30% of the treatment needs, leaving the Congolese government to cover the remaining costs, which were often unmet. For example, in 2016, no public funds were disbursed, likely hindering patient follow-up and treatment completion.14 16 However, funding dynamics have since improved. In 2023, the Global Fund expanded its support, providing funding for laboratory supplies, infrastructure renovations and patient monitoring, amounting to over US$2 million. The Congolese government also made a substantial contribution, fulfilling 77.28% of its commitments to the TB programme. This helped cover almost all drug needs (US$1.3 million) and facilitated the implementation of various programme activities. As a result, no health entity in the country reported a shortage of medicines throughout 2023,16 and the country recorded its lowest TB incidence both during the study period and since 2000.

Analysis of TB/HIV data reveals a significant upward trend in the rate of annual HIV testing (APC=25) among patients with TB. Conversely, HIV positivity among patients with TB declined sharply, from 29.9% in 2016 to 11.5% in 2023. This inverse trend suggests that the decrease in HIV-positive TB cases is likely due to improved screening and testing practices rather than an actual reduction in HIV prevalence as reported in the WHO TB report.1 Before 2019, testing was limited to presumptive HIV cases; with the introduction of routine testing for all patients with TB, along with expanded access to antiretroviral treatment (rising from 43% in 2016 to 81% in 2023) and co-trimoxazole (increasing from 36% in 2016 to 78% in 2023), a greater number of HIV-negative individuals are now being tested, leading to a lower observed rate of co-infection. Continued collaboration between TB and HIV programmes is essential to sustain these gains and align the Republic of Congo’s efforts with global health targets.

While the findings suggest progress in testing, treatment coverage and better outcomes among patients with DR TB, long-term surveillance is needed to assess the sustainability and significance of these changes. Therefore, the observed differences should be interpreted descriptively rather than inferentially as only two points were analysed.

Understanding the underlying causes of these trends is essential to inform and refine future strategies for controlling DR TB. Also, the lack of data regarding resistance to other critical anti-TB drugs such as pyrazinamide, ethambutol, bedaquiline, levofloxacin, clofazimine, prothionamide, delamanid, linezolid and cycloserine is also challenging.

The analysis of the dynamism of Congolese researchers in the TB sector revealed only one published article in PubMed and INHARI, which aligns with the third pillar of the who end TB.19 This third pillar calls for intensification of research and innovation. For global or regional TB research and innovation to be effective, proactive engagement from the researchers is essential. However, no national surveys were found, which may partially explain why overall TB challenges are not adequately managed. Such studies clearly define the problem and offer operational solutions. This low proactivity of researchers at national level may also be explained by the absence of laboratories or centres equipped for TB culture. With the national programme laboratory expected to be operational by the second trimester of 2025, there is hope that it will serve as a hub for generating more data, better aligning with regional and global trends.

A key limitation of this study is its reliance on aggregated national TB reports and WHO programme data without external validation with independent surveys, which may be subject to biases in completeness, accuracy and consistency. Incomplete or inconsistent reporting from diagnostic centres across the 12 departments could result in data gaps, even after quality checks in the DHIS2 database. Potential inaccuracies may stem from manual data entry or variations in reporting practices between departments, while selection bias could occur if certain subpopulations (eg, marginalised groups) were under-reported. Possible under-notification, particularly in underserved areas, which may result in underestimation of actual incidence, notification and mortality. Short time-series which could limit trend inference or accurately model join-points over time. The exclusion of non-English and regionally indexed studies could have led to the omission of relevant Congolese research.

The overall findings provide both encouraging insights and highlight key areas requiring further focus. Based on the results and critical aspects not adequately addressed in the existing reports, the following recommendations are proposed to accelerate progress towards ending TB in the country: (1) continued increase in TB case finding and establishment of a contact tracing system; (2) setting up a comprehensive follow-up system; (3) expand molecular and drug susceptibility testing; (4) promote operational research; (5) ensure TB preventive treatment for high-risk population groups; (6) increase national funding for TB to guarantee sustainability; (7) implement a multisectoral accountability framework for TB as described by WHO28; (8) conduct catastrophic costs surveys affecting patients with TB and their families; and (9) conduct countrywide surveys on transmission dynamics of circulating clones.

Conclusion

The study highlighted areas of slight improvement, especially based on the results from 2021 to 2023 such as increased coverage of treatment and HIV testing, as well as improved ART uptake for co-infected patients and overall areas that need attention such as increased treatment quality and implementation of operational surveys to reach the universal health coverage.
