Endogenous salicylic acid levels in Arabidopsis impact the growth-promoting activity of the beneficial rhizobacterium Pseudomonas simiae WCS417r
Authors: Israel D K Agorsor
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivePlant defence against pathogens typically requires salicylic acid (SA) biosynthesis or accumulation or the activation of the SA signalling pathway. However, SA overaccumulation is known to impair plant development, and previous work has shown that SA biosynthesis or signalling can interfere with the activities of plant–beneficial microbes but in a microbial species-dependent manner. This study investigated the effects of endogenous levels of SA in Arabidopsis on the growth-promoting activity of the beneficial rhizobacterium Pseudomonas simiae WCS417r (hereinafter Ps WCS417r).ResultsWild-type levels of SA limited Ps WCS417r-mediated plant growth, as the SA-deficient Arabidopsis mutant sid2-2 accumulated more biomass in response to Ps WCS417r treatment than did the wild-type plants in response to Ps WCS417r treatment, and the SA-overaccumulator cpr5 yielded less biomass than did the wild-type plants. Analysis of previously generated and publicly available plant gene expression datasets revealed modest downregulation of EPS1, a key gene that contributes to SA accumulation, in the roots of Arabidopsis wild-type plants treated with Ps WCS417r. These results suggest that SA accumulation strongly limits the potential of some plant growth-promoting rhizobacteria, potentially minimizing their effectiveness in ensuring sustainable agriculture that is less reliant on chemical fertilizers.Keywords: Salicylic acid, Arabidopsis thaliana, Plant growth-promoting rhizobacteria, Pseudomonas simiae WCS417r, Plant growth-promotion

Introduction

The ability of beneficial rhizobacteria to promote plant growth sustainably has long been demonstrated [1–3]. Additionally, several fundamental biological mechanisms governing these plant–beneficial microbe interactions have been identified [4, 5]. However, large-scale field deployment of these rhizobacteria has faced hurdles. This includes the unpredictability of results from their application [6], usually attributed to their low rhizosphere competence, among other factors [7]. Several challenges thus remain to be addressed before large-scale, commercial adoption can take off, possibly via what is envisioned as “microbiome-assisted agriculture” [8].

A classic problem plants face is whether to allocate resources for growth or for defence against pathogens, the so-called growth-defence trade-off concept [9]. The plant hormone salicylic acid (SA) mediates resistance against several plant pathogens [10, 11] and is required for systemic acquired resistance (SAR) in plants [12]. However, SA accumulation can also impair plant growth, potentially by repressing auxin signalling [11], and thus also potentially affecting the activity of beneficial microbes that promote plant growth at least in part by activating the plant auxin signalling pathway. Plants accumulating SA, while resistant to biotrophic pathogens, are also typically stunted [9]. SA-deficient transgenic Arabidopsis NahG plants (carrying the salicylate hydroxylase gene NahG [13]), and sid2 mutants (mutated for the ICS1 gene, which encodes isochorismate synthase, a regulator of the SA biosynthesis pathway [14]), under some conditions, grow more robustly than wild-type plants [15]. Yet, they are also highly susceptible to pathogens [14, 16].

An important question is whether SA can interfere with beneficial microbe-mediated plant growth. Indeed, previous work revealed that SA modulates the composition of the root microbiome, excluding several bacterial families from the root microbiome of Arabidopsis cpr5 mutants that constitutively produce SA [17]. Furthermore, SA depletion in NahG plants affects plant growth-promoting bacteria (PGPB)-mediated plant growth differently, with the impact generally being PGPB-dependent [18]. Thus, an investigation into the extent to which different PGPB may be impacted by plant SA biosynthesis or signalling in mediating plant growth is warranted. Pseudomonas simiae WCS417r (hereinafter Ps WCS417r) has previously been shown to elicit both induced systemic resistance and growth in Arabidopsis [19–21]. The objective of this study was to investigate whether Ps WCS417r is affected by SA in mediating Arabidopsis growth.

Materials and methods

Plant materials

For all the experiments, wild-type Arabidopsis plants were Arabidopsis thaliana Col-0. The sid2-2 and cpr5 mutants were obtained from the Danna Lab (University of Virginia) Arabidopsis seed stock collection, and were gifts from Mary Wildermuth at the University of California, Berkeley, and Xinnian Dong at Duke University, respectively. The SA content quantifications confirming SA deficiency and overaccumulator phenotypes, respectively, have been previously and extensively reported e.g., [13–17].

Rhizobacterial strain and growth conditions

P. simiae WCS417r (previously known as Pseudomonas fluorescens WCS417r) was maintained on LB plates amended with 50 µg/ml rifampicin. For root inoculation, a single colony was randomly picked from the LB plate and grown overnight in approximately 100 mL of LB at 28 °C with shaking at 230 rpm until the OD600 reached 0.4–0.8. The cells were harvested, washed three times in sterile water, and adjusted to the required inoculation titre.

Plant growth and rhizobacterial treatment

Seedling assays: Seeds were surface sterilized via 10% bleach three times for two minutes each, followed by three washes with sterile water, and subsequently resuspended in 0.1% PhytoAgar (PlantMedia, Dublin, OH, USA; Cat# 40100072-2) and stratified in the dark at 4 ᴼC for at least two days.

For experiments with wild-type seedlings only (Fig. 1), approximately 15 seeds were plated onto autoclaved 3MM paper cut to fit a 100 mm × 100 mm square plate (Fisher Scientific; Cat# FB0875711A) and wetted with 6 mL of the appropriate inoculum. Filter-sterilized full-strength Hoagland’s solution and Ps WCS417r cells (OD600 = 0.4) were mixed together in equal volumes and applied as the inoculum (i.e., Ps WCS417r, OD600 = 0.2), and full-strength Hoagland’s solution and sterile water were mixed together in equal volumes for vehicle control. The plates were sealed with parafilm and incubated vertically in a growth chamber (Conviron Adaptis 1000, Canada) (at 25 ± 2°C, 75% RH, 16 h light/8 h dark, and 100 µmoles/m2/s light intensity) for 17 days.

For experiments with wild-type and mutant seedlings (Fig. 2), seeds were plated onto 1x MS agar plates (100 mm × 100 mm; Fisher Scientific; Cat# FB0875711A) composed of 4.4 g/L Murashige and Skoog Basal Medium with vitamins (PhytoTech; M519), 0.5% sucrose (Sigma; S7903), 0.5 g/L MES (Sigma; M8250), and 0.7% PhytoAgar (PlantMedia; Cat# 40100072-2), pH 5.7. The plates were sealed with parafilm and incubated vertically in a growth chamber for 7 days. Uniformly growing seedlings were transferred to autoclaved 3MM paper in square plates and wetted with 5 mL of filter-sterilized half-strength Hoagland’s solution (vehicle control) or 5 mL of half-strength Hoagland’s solution + Ps WCS417r (OD600 = 0.2). There were 6 seedlings per plate, which were incubated horizontally for 7 days and processed for shoot biomass data.

Adult plant assays: Approximately five stratified seeds were sown in peat pellets (Jiffy-7, Shippagan, Canada) hydrated in tap water. Before sowing the seeds, a hydrated pellet was either inoculated with 5 mL tap water (control) or with Ps WCS417r (OD600 = 1.0) to 1 × 108 CFU/g pellet (equivalent to 5 mL of inoculum). The trays were covered with a translucent plastic dome to maintain high humidity. These plants were transferred to growth chambers under controlled conditions at 25 ± 2°C, 75% RH, 9 h light/15 h dark, and 100 µmoles/m2/s light intensity. Seedlings were thinned out leaving one seedling per pellet at the end of week two, and bottom-irrigated with tap water twice a week and with half-strength Hoagland’s solution once a week. Shoot biomass data were collected at the end of week 5.

Shoot biomass quantification

Shoots were excised with a razor blade (seedlings) or a pair of scissors (adult plants) at the root‒shoot junction. Shoots from all germinated seedlings per plate were pooled and weighed (Fig. 1) or weighed individually (Fig. 2). All measurements were taken to the nearest 0.1 mg (Mettler Toledo, USA). Root colonization quantification

To quantify Ps WCS417r root colonization, root tissues from all seedlings that germinated per plate (approx. 16 seedlings) were excised and collected separately into autoclaved 2.0 mL Eppendorf tubes using sterilized razor blades and tweezers, and the fresh weight (FW) was determined. Subsequently, 400 µL of autoclaved water was added to each sample, in addition to two tungsten beads cleaned with ethanol, and the root tissues were lysed (Qiagen Retsch TissueLyser II) at the freq. of 25/sec. for 5 min. One hundred microlitres of each lysate sample was used for 10-fold serial dilutions in sterile water. Five (5) microlitres of the dilutions were plated on LB agar (amended with 50 µg/ml rifampicin) and incubated for 24 h at 28 °C, after which CFUs were counted. The CFUs are presented as CFUs/mg root FW.

Evaluation of publicly available gene expression datasets for expression patterns of SA biosynthetic/accumulation/signalling genes

A publicly available gene expression dataset from Stringlis et al.., [22], generated from 22-day-old Arabidopsis wild-type Col-0 roots treated with Ps WCS417r for six (6) hours, was searched for the expression patterns of the Arabidopsis SA biosynthetic/accumulation/signalling genes ICS1, EPS1 and NPR1 via their Arabidopsis Genome Initiative (AGI) locus codes from TAIR (i.e., AT1G74710, AT5G67160, and AT1G64280, respectively). The searches were performed via the “find function” in Excel.

Statistical analyses

Data analyses were performed via JASP open-source software v 0.18.1.0 and Excel, and graphs were generated via Excel. Where appropriate, a two-sided Student’s t-test was performed for statistical comparison of two means, and a one-way ANOVA was used for comparison of more than two means. Statistical significance was set to P values < 0.05.

Results

Ps WCS417r promotes Arabidopsis growth under various growth conditions

Previous studies established the plant growth-promoting activity of Ps WCS417r. To advance the goal of the current work, this phenotype for Ps WCS417r was confirmed under various growth conditions. Specifically, Arabidopsis growth in response to Ps WCS417r inoculation was examined at the seedling stage and for adult plants via a plate-based assay for seedlings and peat pellets for adult plants. Under both conditions, Arabidopsis plants inoculated with Ps WCS417r grew larger than non-inoculated controls did (Fig. 1A and B).

Ps WCS417r-mediated Arabidopsis growth is enhanced by low SA levels and impaired by high SA levels

Next, to test whether wild-type SA levels in Arabidopsis could limit Ps WCS417r-mediated growth, both wild-type plants and sid2-2 mutants were inoculated with Ps WCS417r. Arabidopsis cpr5 mutants that overaccumulate SA [13] were also inoculated. It was found that inoculated wild-type and sid2-2 plants grew larger than the respective non-inoculated controls did (Fig. 2A and B). However, the increase in biomass was greater in the sid2-2 mutants than in the wild-type plants (Fig. 2C and D). Consistent with these findings, compared with wild-type plants, cpr5 mutants presented less biomass in response to inoculation (Fig. 2C and D). Taken together, these results suggest that SA accumulation negatively regulates Ps WCS417r-mediated Arabidopsis growth.

Ps WCS417r root colonization profile remains intact across genotypes

Salicylic acid may act directly to limit pathogen proliferation in plants [23]. Furthermore, some bacterial families are enriched in the root endosphere of SA-deficient Arabidopsis mutants and depleted in SA-overaccumulating mutants, which is consistent with the observation that exogenous SA application can deplete bacterial colonization of root tissues [17]. To determine whether the Ps WCS417r-mediated plant growth responses in the sid2-2 and cpr5 mutants were due to altered root tissue colonization profiles, Ps WCS417r colonization of wild-type, sid2-2 and cpr5 roots was analysed. No significant differences were found among the root colonization profiles (Fig. 3). Accordingly, previous work has shown that Ps WCS417r-mediated Arabidopsis growth is not necessarily related to the direct occupancy of root tissues by Ps WCS417r, as volatile compounds secreted by Ps WCS417r contribute to modifying the plant phenotype [24].

Analysis of publicly available gene expression datasets highlights the regulation of SA accumulation in Ps WCS417r―Arabidopsis interactions

To obtain insights into the role of endogenous SA in Ps WCS417r―Arabidopsis interactions, plant gene expression datasets derived from control (i.e., mock-inoculated) and Ps WCS417r-inoculated wild-type Col-0 Arabidopsis plants [22] were analysed. The goal was to uncover potential shifts in the expression patterns of genes involved in SA biosynthesis/accumulation and signalling pathways upon Ps WCS417r inoculation. This revealed a modest downregulation of the expression of EPS1 (AT5G67160; ENHANCED PSEUDOMONAS SUSCEPTIBILITY 1) 6 h after Ps WCS417r treatment but no evidence of changes in the expression of ICS1 (AT1G74710; ISOCHORISMATE SYNTHASE 1) (Table 1).

In plant defence, EPS1 is thought to function upstream of SA [25], and pathogen-induced SA accumulation is impaired in eps1 mutants [26]. Recent evidence suggested that EPS1, along with PBS3 (AT5G13320; AVRPPHB SUSCEPTIBLE 3), completes the SA biosynthetic pathway in Arabidopsis [27]. Strikingly, root treatment with Ps WCS417r, while suppressing SA accumulation, upregulated the expression of NPR1 (AT1G64280; NONEXPRESSOR OF PATHOGENESIS-RELATED GENES 1), a key regulator of SAR [28], 6 h after treatment (Table 1). These expression patterns are consistent with the finding that Ps WCS417r triggers induced systemic resistance in Arabidopsis leaves, which primes the plant to defend against pathogen attack, a defence mechanism that occurs independently of SA accumulation [19, 20] but is dependent on NPR1 [20]. Taken together, the results presented here suggest that endogenous SA accumulation in Arabidopsis limits Ps WCS417r-mediated Arabidopsis growth.

Discussion

This work reports that the plant growth-promoting capacity of P. simiae WCS417r is dependent on plant endogenous SA levels, in agreement with previous reports that plant SA levels can shape the outcome of plant―beneficial microbe interactions in a microbial species-dependent manner. For example, in an in vitro assay [18], Arabidopsis wild-type seedlings grew to an average of 8.3 mg without rhizobacterium inoculation, but inoculation with Bacillus pumilus SE-34 yielded nearly three times the biomass, at 24.4 mg. However, Arabidopsis NahG seedlings (in which SA is degraded) yielded, on average, 36.2 mg without rhizobacterium inoculation (highlighting that under certain growth conditions, reduced SA levels can increase plant growth). Surprisingly, NahG seedlings yielded only 21.3 mg on average following SE-34 inoculation, indicating an approximately 40% decrease in biomass accumulation. On the other hand, following Pseudomonas fluorescens 89B-61 inoculation, the NahG seedlings grew to an average size of 43.3 mg. These findings indicate that the rhizobacteria B. pumilus SE-34 and P. fluorescens 89B-61 are differently sensitive to SA and that SA affects their ability to promote plant growth.

In the present study, SA deficiency improved Ps WCS417r-mediated growth in both seedlings and adult plants, whereas SA overaccumulation led to impaired growth. Notably, while SA-deficient mutants can grow larger than wild-type plants as discussed above, under some growth conditions, such as low light and low sulfur, their biomass accumulation remains below that of wild-type plants [29, 30]. These findings may explain the observation that, in the present study, under the nutrient-limited conditions utilized, sid2-2 plants were not necessarily larger than wild-type plants (Fig. 2A and B). It is not yet clear why beneficial microbes are differentially sensitive to SA. However, SA may modulate microbial community composition through direct or indirect effects, thereby impacting microbial physiology differently [17]. Interestingly, the work reported here suggests that the altered SA accumulation profiles of sid2-2 and cpr5 mutants do not affect overall Ps WCS417r colonization of root tissues. Whereas Ps WCS417r cells predominantly colonize the root surfaces of Arabidopsis [21], the root colonization profiling method used in this study would also account for endophytic colonization. Importantly, it remains unknown whether SA accumulation affects Ps WCS417r physiology or metabolism in a way that may be relevant for the plant growth-promoting trait of Ps WCS417r. Although exogenous SA application has been suggested as a strategy for recruiting beneficial microbes [31], there are conflicting reports on the impact of exogenous SA application on microbial diversity in the rhizosphere or bulk soil [17, 31, 32]. Future studies could explore the effects of exogenous SA on Ps WCS417r.

Moreover, Ps WCS417r modifies Arabidopsis root architecture, enabling lateral root development by activating the auxin signalling pathway [23], which contributes to plant growth, as a relatively large root surface area mediates the uptake of plant nutrients from the growth medium. Perhaps sid2-2 mutants may gain relatively more root biomass in response to Ps WCS417r inoculation than do wild-type plants. Unfortunately, these data were not collected in the present study. Importantly, SA accumulation is known to repress auxin signalling as a mechanism to restrict pathogen growth, and SA-overaccumulating plants present auxin deficiency/insensitivity phenotypes [11]. Thus, the impaired Ps WCS417r-mediated plant growth observed in cpr5 mutants may be attributed to the potential perturbation of auxin signalling or sensitivity in cpr5 plants. Furthermore, Ps WCS417r suppresses defence responses in Arabidopsis roots [4, 33], suggesting that SA-activated defences may interfere with the activities of some plant growth-promoting rhizobacteria. Other plant beneficial bacteria are known to activate NPR1-dependent systemic resistance in their host plants. These include Pseudomonas putida LSW17S, Bacillus cereus AR156, and Bacillus amyloliquefaciens [34–37]. Yet, these do not necessarily suppress SA accumulation. This may indicate that only Ps WCS417r (and perhaps a few others) suppress SA accumulation while activating the NPR1-dependent defences. Thus, while some beneficial rhizobacteria may cope with plant SA-mediated defences to promote plant growth [38], even wild-type, steady-state endogenous levels of SA, as well as SA accumulation, can limit beneficial rhizobacteria-mediated plant growth.

Conclusion

This study revealed that endogenous levels of SA in Arabidopsis wild-type plants limited Ps WCS417r-mediated plant growth, and this effect was exacerbated by SA overaccumulation. Consistent with these findings, SA depletion enhanced Ps WCS417r-mediated growth. Gene expression data analysis suggested that the suppression of SA accumulation, but not SA signalling, may be crucial for enabling Ps WCS417r-mediated Arabidopsis growth. The results highlight a potential double-edged sword role of SA, which, being important for plant defences, can also interfere with the plant growth-promoting activity of some plant beneficial bacteria.

Limitations

This study established correlations between Arabidopsis SA levels and Ps WCS417r-mediated Arabidopsis growth. Of note, although SA quantification in plant tissues was not performed directly, these quantifications have been extensively reported in the earliest and subsequent studies reporting the SA phenotype of these mutants [13–17]. Moreover, our previous work in which SA content of the mutants was not quantified confirmed the phenotypes expected for these SA-deficient/SA-overaccumulator mutants [39, 40]. An additional limitation is that direct evidence regarding the molecular pathways affected by SA during the Arabidopsis–Ps WCS417r interaction has not been provided. In this regard, further studies are needed to understand the effects of other plant growth-limiting factors, such as auxin signalling and its interplay with SA, in shaping this phenotype.
