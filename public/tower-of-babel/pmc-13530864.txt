Endodontic and Surgical Management of External Cervical Root Resorption: A Case Report
Authors: Fatemeh Bonyadimanesh, Mohammadreza Abrishami
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThis study describes the treatment of tooth #9 exhibiting external cervical root resorption (ECR) and apical root resorption. A therapeutic approach—including root canal therapy with MTA apexification, surgical debridement of resorptive tissue at the ECR site, and sealing of the defect—was necessary to eliminate the infection.Keywords: apexification, mineral trioxide aggregate, root canal therapy, root resorption

Introduction

Root resorption is the progressive loss of dental hard tissue driven by osteoclastic activity. It occurs when physical or chemical insults disrupt the protective cementoblast and osteoblast layers, compromising the structural integrity of both internal and external root surfaces. In permanent dentition, root resorption has been linked to a range of causes, including trauma, impaction of teeth, cystic lesions, and endocrine disorders such as hyper/hypoparathyroidism and hyper/hypophosphatemia [1]. The clinical manifestation of root resorption is highly variable, and considerable strides have been made in classifying these lesions based on their location, the type of resorptive process involved, and the suspected underlying etiology [2]. One of the most poorly understood forms of resorption is external cervical root resorption (ECR), a subtle yet often aggressive type of external root resorption that typically begins with damage to the cementum, followed by involvement of the underlying predentin, and may eventually extend into the dentin and root canal system [3]; based on Heithersay's classification, these lesions are categorized into four classes according to their extent and invasiveness, ranging from small, superficial defects with shallow dentinal penetration (Class 1) to extensive resorptive lesions with deep dentinal involvement and possible pulpal communication (Class 4) [1].

ECR has previously been described using various terms, including invasive cervical, asymmetric internal, progressive intradental, peripheral cervical, and cervical external resorption [4]. This form of resorption is a dynamic pathological process characterized by its location at the cervical region of the tooth, just below the epithelial attachment and coronal part of the bone, which is referred to as the zone of connective tissue attachment [5]. While the precise etiology of ECR remains unclear, factors such as orthodontic treatment, trauma, bleaching, and iatrogenic damage have been recognized as significant predisposing factors [6]. In recent years, there has been a heightened focus on histopathological analysis, with the hope that it may offer insights into the underlying cause and pave the way for novel therapeutic approaches [7].

The early detection of ECR is often challenging, as the lesion's location makes it easy to overlook during a routine visual examination. As a result, ECR is frequently discovered incidentally and can easily be misdiagnosed as a carious lesion [2]. Traditional radiographs are also insufficient for diagnosing internECR due to the masking effect of the surrounding tooth structure and overlying alveolar bone, as well as the unpredictable shape of the resorptive lesion, which can lead to delayed recognition until significant resorption has already occurred [8, 9]. Cone‐beam computed tomography (CBCT) has proven to be an invaluable tool in providing a more precise and detailed representation of these lesions. Once ECR is diagnosed, treatment options are diverse and depend on the extent and severity of the condition.

The management of ECR typically involves two primary therapeutic approaches: the internal approach and the external approach. Selection of the treatment modality depends on lesion severity, accessibility, and proximity to or involvement of the root canal system. The internal approach is indicated when the defect can be accessed through the root canal or when endodontic treatment is required, whereas the external approach is preferred for lesions that are accessible surgically and require direct visualization for complete debridement and restoration [5]. This study aims to present the endodontic and surgical management of a maxillary incisor with a history of trauma, which exhibited both ECR and apical root resorption.

Case History/Examination

A 45‐year‐old male presented to the Department of Endodontics of Mashhad Dental School with the chief complaint of discomfort upon biting and tooth discoloration in tooth #9 (Figure 1A). The patient's medical history was non‐contributory, with no known systemic conditions or allergies. He had previously received a composite resin restoration to treat an uncomplicated crown fracture of the incisal edge resulting from trauma.

Clinical and Radiographic Examination

Upon clinical examination, tooth #9 demonstrated sensitivity to percussion and palpation, bleeding on probing on the palatal aspect, normal probing depth, and no mobility (Figure 1A–C). Pulp testing confirmed the tooth was non‐vital, with no response to either thermal or electric stimuli.

Radiographic examination revealed an irregular, mottled radiolucency involving the cervical region of tooth #9, consistent with ECR. Additionally, apical root resorption was evident (Figure 1D). To further assess the extent of the lesion, CBCT was performed (Figure 1D–F). The pulpal and periapical diagnosis of the tooth was determined to be a pulpless and infected root canal system with chronic apical periodontitis. The resorptive lesion was classified as Heithersay's Class III ECR defect (characterized by deeper invasion of resorbing tissue into the coronal third of the root) and Patel's 1Ap ECR defect (where “1” indicates a supracrestal lesion, “A” indicates circumferential spread of 90 degrees or lower, and “p” indicates pulpal involvement) [1].

Treatment

The treatment objectives for this case were threefold: to arrest the resorption process, to restore the root's healthy architecture, and to prevent future resorption. Given the location and size of the defect, the treatment plan involved performing root canal therapy (RCT), followed by surgical repair of the resorbed root. The patient was informed of the proposed treatment plan. Written informed consent from the patient was obtained for publication according to journal guidelines.

Nonsurgical Endodontic Treatment

Before the surgical intervention, endodontic therapy was initiated. After administration of local anesthesia (lidocaine 2% with 1:100,000 epinephrine; Darou Pakhsh Pharmaceutical Manufacturing Co., Tehran, Iran) and rubber dam isolation, RCT of tooth #9 was commenced. The access cavity was prepared, and the working length was determined using an apex locator (Propex IQ, Dentsply Maillefer, Ballaigues, Switzerland), then confirmed with an intraoral periapical radiograph (Figure 2). Coronal preflaring was performed with an orifice shaper (3 M ESPE, MN, USA), and apical preparation was completed up to size #40 (0.04; M3‐Pro Gold, United Dental, China). The canal was irrigated with normal saline; sodium hypochlorite and EDTA were not used because the ECR lesion had not yet been sealed and these irrigants could potentially cause tissue irritation. Calcium hydroxide (Morvabon, Tehran, Iran) was placed as an intracanal dressing for 2 weeks. During the second visit, the tooth was asymptomatic, and the calcium hydroxide was removed by light instrumentation. The apical foramen diameter corresponded to a #60 K‐file. Due to the presence of external apical root resorption and the inability of gutta‐percha to provide an adequate apical seal in canals with an apical diameter exceeding #55 [10], a Mineral Trioxide Aggregate (MTA) plug (ProRoot MTA; Dentsply Sirona, Charlotte, NC, USA) was placed. The material was delivered into the apical third using the MAP One system (PD Dental, Switzerland), followed by the placement of a temporary restoration. A 1‐week interval was allowed before the surgical phase to ensure adequate setting and sealing of the MTA plug prior to obturation of the remaining canal with gutta‐percha. This interval also allowed clinical monitoring of complete symptom resolution prior to proceeding with the surgical phase.

Surgical Management of ECR

After a week, the surgical management of the ECR lesion was initiated. Following the administration of a nasopalatine nerve block (2% lidocaine with 1:500,000 epinephrine; Darou Pakhsh Pharmaceutical Manufacturing Co., Tehran, Iran), an intra‐sulcular incision was performed to design an envelope flap. A full‐thickness mucoperiosteal flap was subsequently reflected and stabilized using a traction suture (Figure 3).

Surgical management of external cervical resorption (ECR) and restoration of the defect with glass ionomer cement. The final periapical radiograph was taken after completing the root canal treatment to confirm a favorable seal.

Thorough mechanical debridement of the defect was performed to remove the granulomatous tissue (Figure 3), using a combination of manual curettes and ultrasonic tips. Ultrasonic tips are particularly effective in the presence of fibro‐osseous and granulomatous tissue within the resorption cavity, where manual curettes may fail to completely remove all lesion components from a single access point [3]. A biopsy specimen of the granulation tissue was obtained and submitted for histopathological evaluation. Afterwards, a 90% aqueous solution of trichloroacetic acid (TCA) was used for chemical debridement and removing the tissue remnants. A microbrush was dipped in TCA, excess liquid was removed, and the solution was carefully applied to the resorptive site. Adjacent areas were protected with glycerol to prevent accidental contact with the TCA. The solution was allowed to remain in contact with the exposed root surface for 3 min, after which it was thoroughly rinsed off with sterile saline [3]. Undermined dentin or enamel at the peripheries of the cavity was removed using a high‐speed handpiece. The temporary filling material was removed, and a tapered gutta‐percha cone (#40/0.04; Meta Biomed, South Korea) was placed in the canal to maintain patency during the restoration process in the upcoming surgery (Figure 3). The communication was then sealed with glass ionomer (GI) cement (GC Corporation, Tokyo, Japan), and the original anatomic contour of the root was restored (Figure 3). After ensuring the material had set, the flap was repositioned and sutured.

Following gutta‐percha removal, the canal was irrigated with 10 mL of 3% sodium hypochlorite and 5 mL of 17% EDTA. A final rinse with 10 mL of saline was performed, and the canal was dried using sterile paper points. The RCT was completed with thermoplastic gutta‐percha (Figure 3). Due to the potential of bleaching to trigger resorptive processes [11], this procedure was avoided. The patient was referred for permanent composite resin restoration, as the remaining tooth structure was sufficient to retain the restoration without additional reinforcement.

Histopathological Findings

Microscopic examination of the biopsy specimen revealed granulation tissue consisting of fine collagen fibers, fibroblast cells, blood vessels, and inflammatory cell infiltration—predominantly lymphocytes and plasma cells (Figure 4).

Conclusion and Results (Outcome and Follow‐Up)

At the one‐year follow‐up, the patient remained asymptomatic and the tooth discoloration had resolved significantly (Figure 5). Radiographic assessment showed a considerable resolution of the radiolucent lesion, and the filling remained well‐adapted to the root surface (Figure 5), suggesting successful repair of the resorptive defect and a stable seal at the restoration interface.

Discussion

This case describes an instance of ECR combined with external apical root resorption. Due to a pulpless, infected root canal system and chronic apical periodontitis, the tooth required apexification with an MTA plug and non‐surgical RCT.

The pathogenesis and etiology of ECR remain poorly understood [12]. Known predisposing factors include trauma, periodontal therapy, and internal bleaching agents [13, 14]. Trauma is the second‐most prevalent inducer of ECR, accounting for 28.5% of cases when combined with other factors, and approximately 12% when it is the sole factor [13]. In this case, the patient reported a history of trauma resulting in tooth chipping and a subsequent composite resin restoration. The concurrent presentation of cervical and external apical resorption is uncommon and suggests a severe initial insult, as both lesions require damage to the protective precementum and periodontal ligament (PDL). Loss of these protective barriers facilitates root resorption under pathological conditions [15]. Consistent with a cohort study by Jebril et al. [1], where nearly half of the participants reported a history of trauma, this case involved a maxillary incisor. This reinforces the hypothesis that trauma is a primary etiological factor, as maxillary incisors are the teeth most frequently subjected to dental injuries [16].

The patient's chief complaint included pain during mastication and tooth discoloration. Radiographic imaging revealed chronic apical periodontitis and apical root resorption. Clinically, ECR lacks a pathognomonic presentation in its early stages; it often begins insidiously and is typically discovered incidentally during routine examinations [5]. Jebril et al. [1] noted that most patients are asymptomatic at consultation, with 50% of affected teeth still responding to pulpal sensibility testing. These findings support the characterization of ECR as a slow‐progressing disease that involves the pulp only in advanced stages [7]. Consequently, meticulous examination and regular follow‐up of traumatized teeth are vital to prevent progression.

A hallmark sign of ECR is the “pink spot” in the cervical third, caused by highly vascular granulomatous tissue visible through the enamel. While pathognomonic, this sign is relatively rare. In this case, the tooth exhibited a grayish hue, indicating pulp tissue mortification [12, 17].

Standard 2D radiography often fails to detect labio‐lingual defects [6]. While the initial periapical radiograph showed cervical radiolucency, CBCT was essential to define the lesion's true dimensions and circumferential spread. This aligns with evidence that CBCT reveals more advanced ECR than 2D imaging in 38% of cases [18]. Furthermore, 3D assessment can detect pulp perforations missed by 2D films. The European Society of Endodontology (ESE) recommends CBCT whenever the diagnosis is uncertain or surgical intervention is planned [6].

Treatment of ECR is tailored to the lesion's severity, with the primary goal being the total removal of resorptive tissue and restoration of the defect. Treatment options include flap reflection, debridement, and restoration using materials such as glass ionomer (GI) cement, composite resin [19], or calcium silicate‐based cements, including MTA [20], Biodentine [21, 22, 23], and calcium‐enriched mixture (CEM) [20]. Alternatively, Geristore may be utilized [24].

In this case (Heithersay Class III), we combined a surgical approach with non‐surgical RCT. Given the necrotic canal and wide apical foramen, an apexification approach was utilized to create a definitive apical stop. Conventional RCT in canals with an open apex (ISO > 55) has a lower success rate (< 70%) compared to smaller apices (> 80%) [10]. Placing an MTA plug creates an artificial stop and seals the root terminus. MTA was selected for its proven bioactivity: it induces hard tissue formation and has excellent sealing ability. Although MTA carries a known risk of tooth discoloration [25], no discoloration was observed in this case due to MTA, since the material was confined to a plug at the root apex. By isolating the canal with MTA, we prevented the extrusion of filling material into the periapical tissues, establishing a stable foundation prior to addressing the cervical resorption. The ESE and supporting literature emphasize the necessity of securing the root canal, via non‐surgical RCT or apexification, before surgical repair when pulpal involvement is present [6, 26]. Other clinical studies involving ECR with pulp involvement have initiated endodontic therapy before surgery, occasionally completing it intra‐surgically [3, 22, 27].

Apexification was followed by an external surgical approach, utilizing operative techniques that adhered to established protocols for the surgical management of ECR. Both physical and chemical debridement were performed to ensure the comprehensive removal of granulomatous tissue. While physical debridement facilitates direct visualization and cavity shaping, mechanical instruments may fail to reach intricate channels, undercuts, and lacunae. This can leave residual clastic cells and resorptive tissue that may sustain or reactivate the resorptive process [28]. To address this, 90% TCA was applied to the lesion surface. TCA promotes coagulative necrosis of hyperplastic, invasive tissue and penetrates less accessible recesses, effectively cauterizing residual tissue in contact with the PDL. Theoretically, this chemical adjunct inactivates clastic cells and reduces the risk of recurrence [3, 23]. While some researchers, such as Jebril et al. [1], limited debridement to physical methods due to the risk of soft tissue injury from improper isolation, this study prioritized the protection of adjacent soft tissues to allow for safe TCA application. Notably, TCA significantly demineralizes dentin. As Schwartz et al. [26] observed, the treated surface must be planed away to create a fresh dentin substrate before placing restorative materials. We adhered to this protocol by removing the superficial demineralized layer; failure to refresh the dentin would result in a lack of available calcium for glass‐ionomer bonding and a compromised resin bond.

The selection of a restorative material for the cervical defect depends on handling characteristics, sealing ability, and biocompatibility. In this case, conventional GI cement was used to fill the defect and re‐establish the root contour. GI was selected for its clinical ease of use in a surgical field, chemical adhesion to dentin, fluoride release, and established periodontal compatibility [27]. Although bioactive calcium‐silicate cements like MTA and Biodentine are frequently advocated for ECR repair [26, 29, 30], the supracrestal and accessible nature of this specific defect made GI a more practical choice for achieving a contoured and polished external surface. Following the sealing of the communication and intra‐canal irrigation, the RCT was completed.

At the 1‐year follow‐up, proper healing of the defect was observed. The patient remained asymptomatic, and radiographic examinations confirmed the absence of periradicular pathology during the follow‐up period.

Conclusion

This case highlights that ECR can present atypically, such as with gray discoloration and concomitant apical resorption, rather than the classic “pink spot.” An integrated surgical‐endodontic approach—combining meticulous debridement, an MTA apical plug, and GI restoration—effectively preserved the tooth. These findings emphasize the need for tailored treatment protocols when managing complex ECR manifestations.
