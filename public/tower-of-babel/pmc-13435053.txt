General method for generating functionalized arylsilyllithium species
Authors: Natsumi Hammyo, Rikuro Takahashi, Hajime Ito
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Although silyllithium species are valuable reagents in organosilicon chemistry due to their versatile reactivity and utility, especially in Si–Si bond-forming reactions, access to functionalized variants remains limited. In particular, while dimethylphenylsilyllithium is widely used, functionalized arylsilyllithium species remain extremely rare because classical lithium-mediated reduction of chlorosilanes is largely restricted to simple substrates. Here, we report a general method for accessing silyllithium species bearing functionalized aryl groups, enabled by the activation of silylboranes with organolithium reagents. By tuning the activator to match the electronic properties of the substrate, both electron-rich and electron-poor dialkylarylsilyl systems are converted into the corresponding silyllithium species. In situ11B{1H} and 29Si{1H} NMR studies support a boron–metal exchange pathway involving borate intermediates. Notably, these species are inaccessible via conventional lithium-mediated reduction of chlorosilanes. Furthermore, their reactions with chlorosilanes afford disilanes and oligosilanes in moderate to good yields, broadening the scope of silicon-based transformations and enabling access to electronically diverse organosilicon compounds.

Introduction

Silyllithium species are valuable reagents in organic synthesis owing to their versatile reactivity and broad range of applications.1–49 Compared to carbon, silicon has a lower electronegativity, and thus the generation of silyllithium species, i.e., silicon-centered anions, from electronically positive silicon atoms is inherently more challenging.22–32 The synthesis of various silyllithium reagents has already been reported via classical approaches that include the lithium-mediated reduction of chlorosilanes and metal–lithium exchange from preformed silylmetal reagents such as silylmercury, silylstannane, or disilane compounds (Scheme 1A).22–32 Notably, triphenylsilyllithium (Ph3SiLi),33,34 dimethylphenylsilyllithium (Me2PhSiLi),33,34 trimethylsilyllithium (TMSLi),35 triethylsilyllithium (TESLi),36 tri-tert-butylsilyllithium [(t-Bu)3SiLi],37 and tris(trimethylsilyl)silyllithium (TMS3SiLi),38,39 have been synthesized using these approaches. In addition, Tamao has reported the preparation and reaction of α-alkoxy-, α-amino-, and α-sulfur-substituted silyllithium compounds.30,40–44 Among these silyllithium species, Me2PhSiLi is the most widely used as it can be readily generated from the corresponding chlorosilane (Me2PhSiCl) by reduction with lithium metal (Scheme 1B, X = H).34 In this classical reduction method, the treatment of chlorosilanes with lithium metal generally leads to the formation of disilane compounds as intermediates, from which the silyllithium species are generated.22–29 However, this method is considerably limited in its substrate scope. For instance, many trialkylsilyl chlorides are not suitable for this method given the difficulties associated with reducing the resulting disilane to the corresponding trialkylsilyl lithium species.22–29 It is widely recognized that the presence of at least one phenyl group on the silicon atom is essential for the reduction of the intermediate disilane to form a silyllithium species in the lithium-mediated reduction of chlorosilane (Scheme 1B, X = H).22–29 However, silyllithium species cannot be generated from chlorosilanes bearing electron-donating substituents (Scheme 1B, X = EDG) using these methods, as these substituents are less capable of stabilizing the negative charge compared to the phenyl group. Fleming has previously reported on the “failure in several attempts to prepare arylsilyllithium reagents”.50 In particular, Fleming noted that the simple replacement of a phenyl group by a more electron-donating p-tolyl group resulted only in the formation of the corresponding disilane, without further conversion to the silyllithium reagent.50 Indeed, in our previous study, the reduction of dimethyl(2-methoxyphenyl)chlorosilane with lithium metal led only to the formation of the corresponding disilane, confirming experimentally that the desired silyllithium species was not formed under these conditions.48 Although Corey has addressed this issue using transmetalation of the disilanes with MeLi in THF/HMPA (Scheme 1C), the reported examples were limited to dimethylarylsilyl systems bearing methyl or methoxy substituents on the aryl ring, and amino-substituted arylsilanes were not investigated in that study.51 Furthermore, this method requires the use of HMPA.51 In contrast, chlorosilanes bearing electron-withdrawing substituents (Scheme 1B, X = EWG) with high electron-accepting ability should undergo single-electron reduction by lithium; however, their subsequent behavior has not yet been explored, and their decomposition to complex mixtures has been observed in our studies (for details, see the Results and discussion section). Introducing electron-withdrawing and other functional groups into oligosilanes is important because such substituents can modulate their electronic and photophysical properties, enabling the design of donor–acceptor-type oligosilanes with tunable charge-transfer characteristics for silicon-based functional materials.52,53

To access silyllithium species beyond the scope of the classical method that utilizes the lithium reduction of chlorosilanes, silylboranes have recently emerged as particularly versatile and stable precursors for the generation of silyllithium species.21,45–49 In 2001, Tamao and Kawachi reported an approach for the generation of silyllithium species from silylboranes via a boron–metal exchange reaction (Scheme 1D).45 In this method, the silylborane is activated by an organolithium reagent, such as MeLi, to form a borate complex that then undergoes boron–metal exchange, leading to the elimination of Me–B(pin) and the formation of the corresponding silyllithium species.45,46 Tamao and Kawachi also demonstrated that the silyllithium species underwent a subsequent reaction with chlorosilanes to afford the corresponding disilane compounds via Si–Si bond formation.45 Although they applied this method to alkoxysilylborane, dimethylphenylsilylborane, and triphenylsilylborane, the tolerance of functional groups on the aryl ring was not examined in that work.45 We have substantially expanded this chemistry recently by developing transition-metal-catalyzed Si–H borylation reactions, thus enabling the synthesis of a variety of unprecedented silylboranes, including trialkylsilylboranes,46 hydrosilylboranes,47 anisylsilylboranes,48 and optically active silylboranes.49 Furthermore, we successfully generated the corresponding silyllithium species from these silylboranes.21,46–49 Moreover, we established a general method for the synthesis of complex oligosilanes based on iterative chain elongation using methoxyphenyl- or hydrogen-substituted silylboranes as building blocks.48 Thus, silylboranes represent a relatively stable and structurally diverse class of compounds that can serve as useful precursors for the generation of silyllithium species as silicon-centered nucleophiles. However, a general method for generating silyllithium species with functionalized aryl groups that possess either electron-donating or -withdrawing substituents has not yet been established. Although the reactivity of the silylboranes under such conditions is not yet fully understood, it is conceivable that silylboranes bearing sensitive electron-deficient substituents may not tolerate the harsh conditions typically required for reactions with alkyllithium reagents.

In the present study, we have succeeded in generating diverse dialkylarylsilyllithium species from silylboranes, in which the aryl groups are functionalized with an electron-donating group (EDG) or an electron-withdrawing group (EWG) (Scheme 1C and E). By employing an appropriate organolithium activator for the silylboranes (MeLi/TMEDA for those with EDG and PhLi for those with EWG), we have achieved the generation of the corresponding silyllithium species bearing substituents such as amino, cyano, ester, and halogen groups. Notably, our experiments also demonstrated that these silyllithium species cannot be generated by classical reduction methods using chlorosilanes and lithium metal.22–32,34 Furthermore, we have successfully synthesized various functionalized disilanes and oligosilanes through reactions of the diverse silyllithium species with chlorosilanes. 11B and 29Si NMR analyses suggested that the reaction proceeds via a borate complex, leading to the generation of silyllithium species while maintaining both the electron-donating and electron-withdrawing groups intact.

Results and discussion

Optimization of the generation of silyllithium species and the Si–Si bond-formation reaction using a silylborane with an EDG

We initially conducted a study to optimize the conditions for the generation of silyllithium species from dialkylarylsilylboranes that carry an electron-donating substituent on the phenyl group. To ensure sufficient stability of the corresponding silylboranes, ethyl and isopropyl substituents were selected.21,48 All reactions were carried out in THF using silylborane 1a which bears a diphenylamino group (EDG = -NPh2; Table 1). After the addition of the activator at −78 °C, the reaction mixture was stirred for 30 minutes, followed by the addition of triethylsilyl chloride (Et3SiCl). The solution was then stirred for 2 h at the indicated temperature to afford the corresponding disilane. Based on the reported conditions for the generation of silyllithium species from silylboranes, the reaction was initially conducted using MeLi as an activator.21,45–49 Using the procedure described above, the desired disilane product (2a) was obtained in moderate yield (50%; Table 1, entry 1). However, a large amount of starting material 1a remained unreacted. To enhance the reactivity, we investigated the use of additives in combination with the activators. The combination of hexamethylphosphoric triamide (HMPA) and MeLi significantly improved the yield of 2a (71%), indicating increased reactivity (Table 1, entry 2). Addition of 1,3-dimethyl-3,4,5,6-tetrahydro-2(1H)-pyrimidinone (DMPU) was also effective, albeit that the yield of 2a slightly decreased (67%; Table 1, entry 3). An excellent yield was observed when N,N,N′,N′-tetramethylethylenediamine (TMEDA) was used with MeLi (85%, Table 1, entry 4). These coordinating additives are thought to enhance the reactivity of MeLi and to promote the formation of a monomeric silyllithium species that exhibits increased reactivity toward the chlorosilane, thus leading to improved yields of the corresponding disilane.15,23,31,54–59 Changing the reaction temperature to −20 °C instead of 0 °C resulted in a decreased yield of 2a (72%; Table 1, entry 5), indicating a reduced reactivity of the silyllithium species. Equally, increasing the temperature to room temperature or 40 °C also reduced the yield of 2a (78% and 45%, respectively; Table 1, entries 6 and 7, respectively), suggesting that the silyllithium species is unstable at higher temperatures.

Substrate scope of the generation of silyllithium species and the Si–Si bond-formation reaction using silylboranes with EDGs

With the optimal conditions in hand, a series of silylboranes with EDGs was examined to explore the substrate scope of the Si–Si bond-formation reaction (Table 2). The reaction of a silylborane bearing a p-diethylaminophenyl group and bulky diisopropyl group with Et3SiCl provided the corresponding product (2b) in high yield (79%). The optimized conditions were then applied to a silylborane bearing an electron-donating carbazole group. A longer reaction time (4 h) was required after the addition of the chlorosilane and the desired product (2c) was obtained in moderate yield (40%). The moderate yield of 2c should most likely be attributed to incomplete conversion of the starting silylborane, as unreacted starting material was recovered after the reaction. A silyllithium species bearing a tolyl group, which is difficult to access via the classical lithium-metal reduction of the corresponding chlorosilane,50 could also be generated from the corresponding silylborane, affording the desired disilane (2d) in moderate yield (37%). This relatively low yield is probably due to the formation of an unidentified byproduct that may arise from side reactions at the benzylic position. Dimethyl(2,6-dimethoxyphenyl)silylborane 1e was sufficiently stable to be isolated by silica-gel chromatography, suggesting that the 2,6-dimethoxyphenyl group most likely kinetically stabilizes the silylborane through steric shielding of the Si–B bond.48 Under the optimized conditions, 1e afforded the corresponding disilane (2e) in excellent yield (97%). Furthermore, we found that this reaction was applicable to the synthesis of various disilane compounds, including the reaction between bulky silyl compounds. Reactions of bulky diisopropyl(4-methoxyphenyl) silylborane with triisopropylsilylchloride afforded sterically hindered disilane 2f in 68% yield after stirring for 4 h. When Me2PhSiCl, BnMe2SiCl, or Ph3SiCl were used as the silicon electrophile, the corresponding disilane products (2g–2i) were obtained in moderate to good yields (66%, 58%, and 70%, respectively).60 Pleasingly, the optimized conditions could also be applied to the synthesis of oligosilane compounds. The reaction of silylborane 1a with 0.5 equivalent of 1,2-dichloro-1,1,2,2-tetramethyldisilane afforded the desired tetrasilane 2j in 38% yield.

Optimization of the generation of silyllithium species and the Si–Si bond-formation reaction using a silylborane with an EWG

Next, we optimized the conditions for the generation of silyllithium species from a silylborane with an EWG. The reactions were carried out in THF using silylborane 1g, which bears a cyano group as the EWG at the para position of the phenyl ring (Table 3). After the addition of the activator at −78 °C, the reaction mixture was stirred for 30 minutes, followed by the addition of Et3SiCl. Following previously reported conditions, we initially examined MeLi as the activator.21,45–49 However, despite the high conversion of the starting material, the desired compound (2k) was obtained only in low yield (41%; Table 3, entry 1), suggesting that decomposition occurred through nucleophilic attack of the activator to the electrophilic cyano group. Probably for the same reason, a lower yield was obtained when n-BuLi was used instead of MeLi (36%; Table 3, entry 2). In order to reduce the reactivity of the activator, MeMgBr was employed, albeit that the reaction scarcely afforded the disilane (<1%; Table 3, entry 3).45 Finally, we found that the reaction using PhLi resulted in the formation of the desired product (2k) in good yield (65%; Table 3, entry 4). These results suggest that the choice of an appropriate base as the activator is crucial for the formation of the borate complex, especially in the presence of a potentially reactive EWG. PhLi is less nucleophilic than alkyllithium reagents, and thus less prone to attack the EWG. However, despite its low nucleophilicity PhLi can still participate in the formation of the borate complex and the generation of the corresponding silyllithium species because the presence of an electron-deficient aryl group on silicon increases the electrophilicity of the boron atom (Table 3, entry 4). Changing the reaction temperature to 0 °C or −20 °C instead of room temperature resulted in decreased yields of 2k (58%; Table 3, entries 5 and 6), indicating that the silyllithium species has low reactivity. We found that PhLi stock solutions, which were prepared from phenylbromide and Li wire and stored in a Schlenk tube, gave poor and irreproducible results, possibly due to gradual degradation or changes in the effective concentration during storage. In order to ensure the reproducibility of the reaction, PhLi needed to be prepared immediately prior to use from phenylbromide and sec-BuLi (81%; Table 3, entry 7). Increasing the temperature to 40 °C reduced the yield of 2k (<73%; Table 3, entry 8), suggesting that the silyllithium species is unstable at higher temperatures.

Substrate scope of the generation of silyllithium species and the Si–Si bond-formation reaction using silylboranes with EWGs

Under the optimized conditions for electron-poor silylanions, various silylboranes with EWGs were tested in order to examine the substrate scope of the Si–Si bond-formation reaction (Table 4). The reaction of a silylborane bearing a 4-cyanophenyl and a bulky diisopropyl group with Et3SiCl afforded the corresponding product (2l) in good yield (75%). In addition, halogen substituents such as fluorine and chlorine were tolerated, affording the desired products in high yield (2m: 90%; 2n: 83%). Furthermore, we found that this reaction was applicable to a silylborane bearing a bulky ester group. The reaction of a silylborane containing a tert-butyl ester moiety with Et3SiCl afforded disilane 2o in 78% yield. While bulky ester groups were tolerated under the reaction, replacement of the tert-butyl ester with a sterically less hindered ethyl ester led to a complex mixture, probably due to nucleophilic attack of PhLi on the less hindered ester group (2p). When Me2PhSiCl or BnMe2SiCl were used as the silicon electrophile, the corresponding disilanes were obtained in moderate to good yield (2q: 40%; 2r: 68%). However, reactions with sterically demanding chlorosilanes remained challenging. Even when the reaction mixture was stirred for a longer time at room temperature after addition of the chlorosilane, the reaction with Ph3SiCl gave a complex mixture without formation of the desired product (2s), whereas the reaction with (i-Pr)3SiCl afforded the desired disilane (2t) in 10% isolated yield. These results suggest that the silyllithium species generated from silylboranes bearing EWGs are not sufficiently nucleophilic to react efficiently with bulky chlorosilanes. For similar steric reasons, the corresponding tetrasilane (2u) was not obtained.

Generation of silyllithium species and Si–Si bond-formation reaction using a trialkylsilylborane

We next applied the reaction to a trialkylsilylborane bearing a tert-butyl ester group (Scheme 2).46 When MeLi and TMEDA were employed as the activators, the corresponding trialkylsilyllithium species was generated, and the reaction with Me2PhSiCl afforded disilane 2v in 36% NMR yield (Scheme 2A). This result indicates that a trialkylsilylborane bearing an ester group remains a challenging substrate under the present conditions. Competitive decomposition of the ester group by MeLi may lower the efficiency of generation of the corresponding trialkylsilyllithium species. In contrast, when silylborane 1m was subjected to the optimized reaction conditions for silylboranes with EWGs, the corresponding disilane (2v) was not obtained, and starting silylborane 1m was recovered in 69% yield (Scheme 2B). These results indicate that, in comparison with dialkylarylsilylboranes bearing an EWG on the phenyl group, the boron atoms in trialkylsilylboranes lack sufficient electrophilicity to be activated by the less nucleophilic PhLi. In fact, only 31% of PhB(pin), which should be produced in an equimolar amount when the silyllithium species is formed, was observed by NMR analysis. Therefore, these reaction conditions rely on a synergistic effect in which PhLi serves as a mild activator that does not attack the electrophilic substituents on the phenyl group, while those substituents simultaneously enhance the electrophilicity of the boron atom.

Mechanistic studies

To demonstrate the superiority of the generation of silyllithium species from silylboranes over the traditional lithium-mediated reduction of chlorosilanes,22–32,34 we tested whether the silyllithium species could be generated from the corresponding chlorosilanes with functionalized aryl groups (Scheme 3A). For that purpose, chlorosilane 3a, bearing an electron-donating diphenylamino group, was exposed to Li wire in THF at 0 °C for 25 h. However, the reaction afforded a complex mixture, and no trace of the corresponding hydrosilane, which would indicate the presence of the protonation product of the silyllithium species, was observed by either GC or NMR analyses. When chlorosilane 3b, which bears an cyano group as the EWG, was treated with Li wire, the reaction also led to a complex mixture and the corresponding hydrosilane was not detected. In contrast, under our optimized conditions for the generation of silyllithium species from silylboranes, the Si–Si coupling products 2a and 2k, derived from the corresponding silyllithium species, were successfully obtained in 82% and 67% yield, respectively. These results clearly highlight the promising synthetic potential of our approach in generating silyllithium species with electronically diverse substituents and for directly accessing disilanes and oligosilanes, which are difficult to obtain through the conventional lithium-mediated reduction of chlorosilanes.

Then, we performed in situ11B{1H} and 29Si{1H} NMR experiments to confirm the formation of a borate complex and a silyl anion from the reactions of the dialkylarylsilylboranes bearing EDGs and EWGs with the optimal activators (Scheme 3B; for details, see the Supporting Information).46,49,61,62 Treatment of silylborane 1a with 1.0 equiv. of MeLi and TMEDA in THF at −78 °C led to a new 11B signal at δ 8.5 ppm that was attributed to the borate complex.46,49 When the temperature was increased, we observed a new small signal for Me–B(pin) at δ 33.6 ppm, indicating the generation of a silyllithium species.46,49 In the 29Si{1H} NMR spectrum, a new 29Si signal at δ −10.0 ppm indicating the presence of a silyllithium species was detected. The reaction mixture obtained from silylborane 1g and 1.0 equiv. of PhLi in THF at −78 °C showed two new 11B signals, i.e., a small peak at δ 6.2 ppm, attributed to the boronate complex, and a major peak at δ 30.7 ppm, consistent with Ph–B(pin), indicating the generation of a silyllithium species.46,49 In the 29Si{1H} NMR spectrum, the 29Si signal (from δ −20.5 to δ −13.0 ppm), which was attributed to the silyllithium species, was detected at −100 °C.

Finally, to obtain more direct evidence for the formation of silyllithium species, deuterium quenching experiments were conducted (Scheme 3C). Treatment of silylboranes 1e and 1i with the corresponding activators, followed by quenching with DCl or D2O, afforded the deuterated silanes S1e and S1i in 93% and 79% NMR yield, respectively, with >99% deuterium incorporation. These results provide strong evidence for the generation of the corresponding silyllithium species under the applied activation conditions.

Conclusions

Herein, we have developed a general and mild method for the generation of silyllithium species that bear functionalized aryl groups from silylboranes through activation with organolithium reagents. By carefully selecting the activator according to the electronic nature of the silylboranes and the resulting silyllithium species, both EDG- and EWG-substituted dialkylarylsilylboranes can be successfully converted into the corresponding silyllithium species. Mechanistic studies using in situ11B{1H} and 29Si{1H} NMR spectroscopy provided evidence for the formation of borate complexes and silyllithium species, supporting a boron–metal exchange pathway. Furthermore, the generated silyllithium species readily reacted with various chlorosilanes to afford disilanes and oligosilanes in moderate to good yield. Notably, these silyllithium species are inaccessible via the classical lithium-mediated reduction of chlorosilanes, highlighting the distinct advantage of the present method. Given the long-standing limitations in accessing functionalized silyllithium reagents, this work establishes a broadly applicable platform for the synthesis of electronically diverse organosilicon compounds, especially disilanes and oligosilanes, and is expected to expand the utility of silyllithium chemistry in organic synthesis.
