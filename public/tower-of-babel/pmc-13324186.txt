Speech-based digital endpoints track ALS progression and align with standard clinical outcomes: evidence from the VRG50635 trial
Authors: Michael Neumann, Hardik Kothare, Meredith Bartlett, Oliver Roesler, Christiane Suendermann-Oeft, Abhishek Hosamath, Lakshmi Arbatti, David Pautler, David Suendermann-Oeft, Diego Cadavid, Robert H Scannevin, Ines Hoffmann, Anil Tarachandani, Tara Haley, Shane Raines, Philip Van Damme, Pentti Tienari, Eino Solje, Manu Jokela, Angela Genge, Colleen O’Connell, Ruben P A van Eijk, Leonard H van den Berg, Vikram Ramanarayanan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWe report on the utility of speech-based digital endpoints measured during a Phase 1b study of VRG50635 in Amyotrophic Lateral Sclerosis (ALS). Fifty-four participants with ALS were enrolled and participated in an 8-week pretreatment run-in, followed by three 8-week dosing periods and an 8-week follow-up. They completed a speech assessment every two weeks in the clinic or at home. We observed moderate to high correlations between digital measures of speech timing and articulatory motor function, and the ALS Functional Rating Scale-Revised, slow vital capacity and plasma neurofilament light chain. Furthermore, speech measures can show functional decline before the ALSFRS-R does, while also capturing differences between participants with bulbar symptoms and those without. The results support the feasibility and utility of digital speech endpoints to study disease impact in ALS clinical trials.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-48100-6.Subject terms: Medical research, Neurology, Neuroscience

Introduction

Amyotrophic Lateral Sclerosis (ALS) is a progressive motor neuron disease causing muscular atrophy and loss of voluntary motor control in persons with ALS (pALS), with a median survival of 3–5 years1 after disease onset. Despite major investments in clinical research, only 6 out of 38 placebo-controlled ALS trials with over 100 participants (1996–2022) have shown statistically significant evidence of efficacy2. Key reasons cited for this lack of significant progress in ALS drug development include the lack of sensitive and meaningful biomarkers, poor clinical trial design, lack of power to control type 1 and type 2 errors, and diagnostic delays leading to late enrollment3. This underscores the importance of identifying sensitive, meaningful, and frequently assessable biomarkers to improve ALS trial design.

The clinical standard to measure disease progression in ALS is the ALS Functional Rating Scale-Revised (ALSFRS-R)4, a questionnaire comprising 12 questions across four functional domains impacted by ALS5: bulbar, fine motor, gross motor and respiratory. The ALSFRS-R is the most common primary endpoint in clinical trials in ALS including phase 2 and phase 36. However, there is evidence that it tracks disease progression in a non-linear manner and lacks sensitivity to change in the early stages of bulbar disease onset7–9. Van Unnik et al. have pointed out that such clinician reported-based outcomes have “limited ability to detect subtle changes over time”10.

Speech-based digital health technology (DHT) based endpoints hold potential to provide objective, sensitive, feasible, meaningful, and more frequent outcome measures to capture change due to disease progression and therapeutic intervention11–14. Several DHTs have been employed in recent years in observational studies and interventional clinical trials as exploratory endpoints, including at-home spirometry15, actigraphy10,16, hand-held dynamometry17, and digital speech assessments14,18,19. Of these, speech measures, which provide an inexpensive, non-invasive and feasible way to frequently and remotely capture manifold health information, have shown great promise for monitoring of neurological and mental health11,20–23. Green et al.18 and Shefner et al.19 showed improvements in timing-related speech measures in the active cohorts of clinical ALS trials involving dextromethorphan/quinidine and pridopidine, respectively. The former included speech data collected at sites during patient visits and the latter collected data remotely using a smartphone application. However, in both studies, speech recordings were collected only a handful of times (4 and 5 times, respectively). In this study, we recorded speech from patients every two weeks for 40 weeks at home or in the clinic, giving us a more granular insight into disease progression through changes in speech function. Indeed, many studies have demonstrated the efficacy of speech measures that capture how a given disease impacts the different domains of speech performance, including motor, anatomical, cognitive, linguistic and affective20,24–26. In ALS, speech measures have been shown to be powerful in early detection of bulbar symptoms27–33 and in tracking the progression of bulbar decline in pALS12,13,34–37.

This study aimed to evaluate digital speech measures as exploratory endpoints in a Phase 1b, prospective, open-label, single-arm, multiple ascending dose proof of concept study of the PIKfyve inhibitor VRG50635 in ALS during a pre-treatment period followed by a within participant dose escalation treatment period. This paper does not report on the topline results of the trial; a detailed description of those, including primary and secondary endpoints, will be published separately38. Topline results of this trial investigated whether chronic VRG50635 dosing in people with sporadic or genetic ALS is safe and tolerated, whether it does achieve central and peripheral target engagement across escalating doses, and whether such engagement favorably affects NfL, glial biomarkers, and clinical progression as measured by ALSFRS-R. The primary and secondary results argue against a therapeutic benefit of the intervention in ALS at studied exposure. Therefore, the present study focuses primarily on the longitudinal analysis of digital speech based exploratory endpoints and their utility in characterizing disease progression.

Results

Fifty-four pALS (sporadic n=37; genetic n=17) were enrolled in this Phase 1b, proof of concept study of VRG50635. The study was registered at ClinicalTrials.gov under the registration ID NCT06215755 on 2024-01-22. The study was conducted in four countries (Canada, Belgium, Finland, the Netherlands). The present work presents post-hoc exploratory analyses of digital speech endpoints using data collected in the VRG50635 trial. These analyses were not prespecified in the original statistical analysis plan, and results should be considered exploratory.

Adherence to the speech assessments per schedule of activities was 79% (86% when including sessions that were out of the visit window, which were included in the present study). Out of 54 participants, 50 completed the 8-week run-in period and entered the treatment period. Of these, 17 discontinued the study early after a maximum of 62 days of treatment. Notably, the dropout rate was much higher in the bulbar affected cohort with a median follow-up duration of 14 weeks, compared to 36.7 weeks in the bulbar unaffected cohort (see Table 1).

We observed strong significant correlations between the change in PP/SVC and the change in speech measures related to timing and articulatory kinematics (RPSD, ; DDKSC, ), pausing (RPPPT, ), and respiration (SPID, ). With reduced PP/SVC, participants need longer to read the reading passage text (RPSD increases), make more and longer pauses (RPPPT increases), produce less syllables during DDK (DDKSC decreases), and the maximum phonation time (SPID) decreased. Furthermore, RPCTA was moderately correlated with PP/SVC, suggesting that a reduction in PP/SVC coincides with a worsening in timing alignment and intelligibility of speech.

For the ALSFRS-R bulbar subscore we observed strong significant correlations with the same five measures (, and additionally for SITPPT () and DDKCTV (), which means that with declining ALSFRS-R bulbar scores participants make more and longer pauses during sentence reading and exhibit greater irregularity in producing syllables during the DDK task. Correlations with the ALSFRS-R total score and the speech subscore showed similar patterns.

For NfL we also observed significant moderate correlations with the same five speech measures (RPSD, ; RPCTA, ; RPPPT, ; DDKSC, ; SPID, ), indicating longer speaking duration paired with longer and more pauses, a worsening in timing alignment, a reduction in syllable production during DDK, and decreased maximum phonation time associated with increased NfL levels.

When considering the bulbar affected and unaffected subcohorts separately (supplementary Fig. A1), we observed notable differences in the correlations. In the bulbar affected group, speech measures had weaker correlations with the ALSFRS-R subscores, while correlations with PP/SVC remained high for RPPPT, DDKSC, and SPID. In the bulbar unaffected group, we observed moderate to strong correlations between RPSD, RPPPT, DDKCTV, SPID and ALSFRS-R subscores as well as PP/SVC, and weaker correlations for RPCTA compared to the entire study cohort.

For all the measures presented, the average slope based on all visits was significantly different from zero (), including for NfL and PP/SVC (supplementary Fig. A2). This indicates that the speech outcome measures capture aspects of functional decline in speech related to disease progression over the period of 40 weeks.

LMEs fitted only on data from in-clinic visits (every eight weeks) show that less frequent measurements lead to underestimated slopes for all clinical and speech outcome measures (except RPSD, which was instead overestimated). Furthermore, with less frequent measurement sampling the slopes of some speech measures—DDKSC, RPCTA, and RPPPT—were not significantly different from zero. These observations demonstrate that more frequent measurements allow for better and more robust statistical estimates of change. While the ALSFRS-R may be administered more frequently, too, this has direct consequences on clinician burden and cost in a clinical trial. Furthermore, there is evidence that more frequent ALSFRS-R monitoring has only limited benefits in relation to increased patient and clinician burden39,40.

When comparing the bulbar affected and unaffected cohorts, we observed that except for DDKSC and NfL, the average group trajectories were significantly different from each other, with the bulbar affected cohort experiencing a steeper decline overall. Supplementary Fig. A3 shows the LME trajectories for individuals with sporadic ALS and those with genetic ALS separately. All outcome measures show similar slopes for these two groups, except for DDKSC (steeper decline for sporadic ALS group, ).

Participant-level measurement sensitivity

To evaluate the rate of change at the individual participant level, we visualized the slopes for all participants separately, see Fig. 3.

Although the ALSFRS-R bulbar score remained stable for 14 participants (all in the bulbar unaffected group) over the 40-week period, as indicated by an absolute slope smaller than 0.01 points/month (top left panel of Fig. 3), objective speech measures revealed measurable changes in many of these individuals during the same time period. DDKSC declined for most participants, with the exception of positive slopes for three subjects. RPSD captured an increase in speaking duration for one half of these individuals, while the other half shows a slight reduction. The slopes for RPCTA, RPPPT, and SPID do not show clear patterns for those individuals with stable ALSFRS-R bulbar score. Notably, some participants show only a small rate of change in the ALSFRS-R bulbar score, but consistent large absolute slopes in all other endpoints, including PP/SVC and NfL, e.g. P37 and P39. The individual slopes for PP/SVC and NfL also capture substantial changes in the majority of participants.

In addition, we estimated the rate of change based on only the 8-week run-in period, prior to the first treatment (see supplementary Fig. A4). This was done to characterize early baseline trajectories. We observed slightly different patterns: DDKSC increased for most participants, suggesting a potential learning effect for this task during the first assessment sessions. RPPPT on the other hand shows a positive slope for most participants, including the bulbar unaffected group, indicating early signs of functional decline (more and longer pauses).

Overall, these findings provide evidence that on the individual participant level, speech outcome measures can capture deterioration—but also improvement—even when the clinical gold standard, the ALSFRS-R, remains unchanged.

Discussion

The findings of this Phase 1b study of VRG50635 show that speech measures exhibit moderate to high correlations in their rate of change with ALSFRS-R, PP/SVC, and NfL. This provides strong evidence of concurrent validity. This study did not address potential treatment effects because the topline results (under review38) argue against any therapeutic benefit of the intervention. In fact, administration of VRG50635 was shown to increase NfL, so the changes in NfL during treatment periods cannot solely be attributed to natural disease progression. The present study focused on evaluation of digital speech outcome measures and their utility in characterizing disease progression as compared to the ALSFRS-R, PP/SVC and NfL, without factoring in treatment effect, and therefore with the implicit assumption that all endpoints were impacted similarly across the pre-treatment and treatment periods. Given the topline results, this assumption appears reasonable for the ALSFRS-R, but has limited validity for changes in NfL.

The following five speech measures exhibited a significant decline during the 40 weeks of part 1 and part 2 of the study and showed moderate to high significant correlations in their rate of change with established outcome measures: speaking duration of the reading passage (RPSD), number of syllables produced during diadochokinesis (DDKSC), percent pause time during the reading passage (RPPPT), canonical timing alignment of the reading passage (RPCTA), and the maximum phonation time of sustained phonation of /i/ (SPID). These results are in line with previous findings from observational studies of speech measures in ALS13,14,18,36. It is expected that not all selected speech measures correlate with clinical outcomes or biomarkers, especially in a cohort of predominantly limb onset, bulbar unaffected pALS. Specifically, we observed low correlations for harmonics-to-noise ratio (DDKHNR and SITHNR), fundamental frequency (RPMFF), and word count in the picture description task (PDWC). Stronger correlations in the bulbar unaffected cohort compared to the bulbar affected group for some measures (see supplementary Fig. A1) are partly explained by distributional differences in the slope estimates for the ALSFRS-R scores. In the affected group, slope relationships were more heterogeneous across participants. In the unaffected group, a ceiling effect in ALSFRS-R bulbar subscores produced a bimodal slope distribution, with a subset of participants showing near-zero change alongside stable speech trajectories, and others showing a clear co-variation between certain speech measures and ALSFRS-R (bulbar) decline. These findings suggest that correlation-based evidence should be interpreted in the context of subgroup structure and disease stage.

Speech measures (and DHTs in general) provide a substantial advantage over traditional survey-based outcomes in that they are continuous-valued and have higher resolution. Although the ALSFRS-R bulbar subscore showed a statistically significant decline in the study cohort (Fig. 2a), the average slope of − 0.04 points per week means that it would take on average 25 weeks to observe a 1-point change in this cohort, which is by definition the minimal detectable change. In addition to providing frequently administrable, objective, and sensitive measurements, another benefit of digital speech outcome measures is their cost efficiency—when implemented in an automated and scalable manner—and the low patient burden, especially if the assessments can be administered remotely at home. Our linear mixed-effects (LME) model analysis demonstrates the importance of frequent measurements. If outcome measures would have been captured only at the in-clinic visits every 8 weeks instead of biweekly, the slopes would have been underestimated across most endpoints—a serious risk in clinical trials, where an underestimation of treatment effects could potentially lead to the failure of the study in question.

The findings should be interpreted in the context of the study’s assumptions and limitations. Linear mixed-effects models do not account for early discontinuation and assume missing data at random41. Because dropout likely relates to disease progression, estimated slopes may underestimate the true rate of decline. Furthermore, doing speech assessments remotely at home poses the risk of uncontrolled recording environments, background noise and other potential confounding factors. Quality reviews in this study have ensured high data quality and have shown good adherence to task instructions. However, background noise was present in some recordings, which can affect certain outcomes like voice quality measures. Furthermore, potential learning or practice effects of repeated task stimuli were not accounted for explicitly in this study. We hypothesize that an increase in DDKSC during the 8-week run-in period was likely attributable to participants becoming more familiar with the task during the first assessments. Rotating stimuli (e.g., different pictures that were presented each time for the picture description task) can mitigate this, but additional approaches to account for practice effects should be explored in future work. Another consideration is the minimal clinically important difference (MCID), not addressed here as it is more relevant to phase 3 trials and clinical practice. MCID thresholds are typically derived by linking outcome changes to an anchor of meaningfulness, e.g., the ALSFRS-R42, though we previously showed that this method limits the granularity and we explored perceptual ratings of listener effort as an alternative anchor43. We showed that MCID estimates varied depending on the method, anchor, and dataset, underscoring the need for future work to define clinically meaningful change in speech measures across populations.

To conclude, the presented findings argue for a more wide-spread adoption of digital speech outcome measures in ALS clinical trials. The study provides evidence that objective speech measures capture disease progression and are sensitive to early changes. In general, speech assessments can be administered frequently at home and across different languages, arguing for their inclusion as effective digital outcome measures in clinical trials.

Methods

Participants and clinical assessments

Fifty-four participants (37 male, 17 female, mean age: 58.9 ± 9.1 years) were enrolled in this Phase 1b study of VRG50635 in four countries (Canada, Belgium, Finland, the Netherlands). A detailed study overview can be found in a separate publication38. The study was divided into three parts:

Part 2: 32-week dose escalation of VRG50635 daily at 400 mg, 600 mg, and 800 mg for 8 weeks each plus an 8-week extension adjusted based on tolerability

Part 2: 32-week dose escalation of VRG50635 daily at 400 mg, 600 mg, and 800 mg for 8 weeks each plus an 8-week extension adjusted based on tolerability

Part 3: 40-week long-term extension at the highest tolerated dose and post end-of-treatment visit

Part 3: 40-week long-term extension at the highest tolerated dose and post end-of-treatment visit

Fifty-four participants entered Part 1; 50 were dosed at 400 mg, and 40 and 34 escalated to 600 mg and 800 mg, respectively. Twenty-one participants entered Part 3. In this study, we analyzed data from Part 1 and Part 2 due to the limited long term extension data available. Independent Ethics Committees reviewed and approved the protocol and amendments, informed consent form, and other relevant documents before study initiation, which was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and International Council on Harmonisation (ICH) Good Clinical Practice guidelines. In Europe, approvals were granted by the University Medical Center Utrecht, the Netherlands (13 February 2024); UZ Leuven, Belgium (approval 11 March 2024); Helsinki University Hospital, Finland (approval 11 March 2024); the University of Eastern Finland (approval 20 February 2024); and the Wellbeing Services County of Southwest Finland (approval in 2024). In Canada, IRB approvals were granted by McGill University, The Royal Institution for the Advancement of Learning (approval 29 November 2024) and by Horizon Health Network, Regional Health Authority B (approval 6 March 2024). An independent Data Safety Monitoring Committee reviewed overall safety and dose escalation. Participants provided written informed consent prior to study participation.

The ALSFRS-R was administered every four weeks and King’s ALS staging44 every eight weeks. Slow vital capacity (SVC)—a spirometric measure of vital capacity whose rate of decline predicts clinically meaningful markers of ALS decline such respiratory failure, tracheostomy, or death45—and Plasma Neurofilament Light Chain (NfL)—an axonal-injury protein, which is elevated in ALS and is a robust prognostic biomarker for disease severity and survival46—were measured every two weeks. SVC measurements are represented as percent predicted SVC (PP/SVC), derived using the GLI-2012 reference equations47. For a subcohort analysis, we stratified participants into bulbar affected and bulbar unaffected based on the King’s staging assessment of the bulbar region at the first treatment visit. King’s staging information was not available for four participants. For three of the four, the ALSFRS-R bulbar subscore was used instead, treating participants with a bulbar subscore as bulbar affected. The remaining subject was not included in the subcohort analysis because no information was available (Fig. 4).

Speech assessments

Participants completed biweekly speech assessments at home or in the clinic using the Modality assessment platform48–50, where Tina, a virtual guide, engaged them in a structured conversation (see Fig. 4). The Modality platform is compliant with the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA) and the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR; European Union). The assessment elicits different types of speech samples that have been shown to capture different aspects of ALS progression14,51–54. The following tasks were included for analysis: (a) read speech (sentence intelligibility test (SIT), 5–8 words; reading passage (RP)), (b) measure of diadochokinesis (DDK, rapidly repeating the syllables /pɑtɑkɑ/), (c) sustained phonation of /i/, and (d) free speech in form of a picture description task (PD). SIT sentences were selected from a pool of 58 sentences, so that participants were not presented the same stimuli in consecutive assessments. The longer reading passage text was the same throughout all assessments. Note that the clinical utility of this conversational assessment platform has also been demonstrated in prior work examining observational history datasets across multiple disease areas over and above ALS14,55, including Parkinson’s Disease56,57, mild cognitive impairment58, schizophrenia59, depression60 and autism spectrum disorder61.

Extraction of speech measures

The Modality platform is equipped with analytics modules that automatically extract measures to capture information from speech (including energy, timing, voice quality, spectral, linguistic features). The open source software Praat (v6.2.17)62 and Montreal Forced Aligner (v2.0.0.a22)63 were used to extract speech measures from segmented audio signals. Linguistic features were computed using the Python package spaCy (v3.7.2). They are based on automatic transcriptions obtained with AWS Transcribe. Table 2 provides an overview of the speech measures and the assessment tasks from which each measure was extracted. We define these task-measurement combinations as pre-specified outcome measures. These measures were chosen based on prior work that demonstrated their sensitivity and clinical utility in capturing progressive decline in ALS14,36. For the SIT task, which consists of four separate sentences that participants are asked to read aloud, measures were mean aggregated across sentences (e.g., SITPPT denotes the average percent pause time across the four sentences).

To remove outlier values from speech measures, we employed a distribution-based outlier detection algorithm64. Outliers might occur due to high-intensity background noise or incorrectly performed tasks. First, all values that are more than five standard deviations away from the sample mean were removed. These are considered extreme outliers, which potentially skew the distribution mean. The value of five standard deviations was empirically chosen after analyzing the data distributions. Then, the mean value for each measure was re-computed and values outside standard deviations were flagged as outliers and removed from any further analysis. In addition to this automatic data pre-processing step, manual quality control was performed by human raters to ensure high data quality. For every assessment session, a sample of at least three audio recordings was reviewed to identify any problems with background noise, audio quality or adherence to task instructions.

Statistical analysis methods

The rate of change in outcome measures was analyzed using linear mixed-effects models (LME)65 with random intercepts and slopes by participant ID, fitted with the nlme package in R. Fixed effects were estimated for the intercept and slope. LMEs allow us to estimate the group level trajectories and to derive best linear unbiased predictors (BLUP)66 of random effects to represent the rate of change for each individual. To investigate differences between bulbar affected and unaffected participants, we applied a two-group LME with fixed effects for cohort, time, and their interaction, and random intercepts and slopes for each participant.

We examined correlations between speech measures and clinical variables, including the ALSFRS-R, PP/SVC, and NfL. Specifically, Spearman correlation was calculated based on the rate of change in all variables, as being represented by BLUPs for each participant. For the ALSFRS-R, which assesses function in four domains (speech and swallowing, fine motor function, gross motor function, and respiration), we focus on the bulbar subscore (first three questions, ranges from 0 to 12), the speech subscore (first question, ranges from 0 to 4), and the total score (ranges from 0 to 48). To account for the multiple comparisons, we applied Benjamini–Hochberg correction67 to control the false discovery rate at 5%. This approach was chosen because the speech measures are inter-correlated.

Furthermore, we compared individual differences for each participant in the decline of speech measures with the corresponding decline of the ALSFRSR-R bulbar subscore, PP/SVC, and NfL to investigate whether speech measures can show changes earlier than these endpoints for individual subjects.

To assess the short-term stability of each speech outcome measure, we computed test–retest reliability using recordings from the third and fourth assessments of the pre-treatment run-in period, which were separated by approximately two weeks. This fixed visit pair was chosen to ensure a balanced design with one measurement pair per participant, and to avoid potential practice effects that may affect the earliest visits. For each measure, test–retest reliability was quantified using a two-way intraclass correlation coefficient for single measurements, ICC(2,1), which estimates the agreement between the two recordings within each pair.
