Clinical outcomes associated with Korean medicine home-visit care for patients with cognitive impairment: a multi-center retrospective observational study
Authors: Chan-Young Kwon, Jihyeon Lee, Yujin Han
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This multi-center retrospective study investigated associations between a 4-month Korean medicine (KM) home-visit program and pain, behavioral and psychological symptoms of dementia (BPSD), cognitive function, and caregiver burden in 92 patients with cognitive impairment (Global Deterioration Scale stages 2–5) assessed at baseline, 2 months, and 4 months. Complete case analysis was the primary analytical approach. Significant improvements were observed in Pain Assessment in Advanced Dementia: median 4 to 2, p < 0.001) and neuropsychiatric symptom severity (Neuropsychiatric Inventory Questionnaire: 3 to 2, p = 0.006). While Mini-Mental State Examination for Dementia Screening remained stable; the Clock Drawing Test showed a non-significant trend toward improvement (p = 0.098). Caregiver burden (12-item Zarit Burden Interview [ZBI-12]) showed a non-significant trend toward reduction (p = 0.074). Subgroup analyses showed exploratory within-group signals for ZBI-12 (mild cognitive impairment group) and CDT (dementia group); between-group differences were not significant. Improvements in BPSD severity strongly correlated with reduced caregiver distress (rho = 0.721, p < 0.001). A 4-month KM home-visit program was associated with significant reductions in pain and BPSD, with stable cognitive outcomes. These hypothesis-generating findings support integrating KM services into community-based dementia care and the design of future controlled trials.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-54209-5.

Introduction

As of December 2024, South Korea officially transitioned into a super-aging society, with the population aged 65 and above surpassing 20% of the total. This rapid demographic shift has made the management of cognitive impairments, particularly dementia, one of the most pressing public health priorities1. By 2025, the number of dementia patients in South Korea is estimated at approximately 970,000 (prevalence 9.17%), projected to exceed 2 million by 20402. Dementia substantially diminishes quality of life for patients and imposes significant burden on family caregivers through associated behavioral and psychological symptoms of dementia (BPSD) and physical pain, leading to rising socioeconomic costs3–5 Recent analyses of Korean newspaper reports indicate that prolonged caregiving burden has, in some cases, been associated with extreme outcomes including suicide or homicide, highlighting caregiver burden as both a clinical and social issue6.

While South Korea’s dementia management system has primarily focused on early screening through Dementia Relief Centers and financial subsidies, important gaps remain7. These include inadequate post-diagnostic management strategies, limited medical interventions for BPSD, and poor coordination between healthcare and welfare services7. Recent evidence indicates that the availability of home-visit medical personnel, such as physicians and nurses, is more effective at reducing mortality and institutionalization rates than expanding hospital beds or long-term care facilities alone8. These findings reveal the limitations of facility-oriented resource allocation for supporting aging in place (AIP) and suggest the need to transition toward integrated, home-based medical care systems.

Korean medicine (KM) home-visit care integrates multimodal interventions, including acupuncture, Chuna therapy, and herbal medicines, to address cognitive decline, BPSD, and musculoskeletal pain in patients with cognitive impairment9–11. These interventions contribute to neuroprotection and improved cerebral blood flow, potentially attenuating cognitive decline12. Furthermore, they offer a non-pharmacological alternative to psychotropics, reducing concerns about adverse drug reactions in home-based settings10,11. Despite these potential benefits, objective evidence on the clinical effectiveness of KM home-visit interventions remains limited. While home-based environments pose challenges for standardized evaluation and longitudinal tracking compared to clinical settings, systematic data collection from real-world practice is needed to enhance service quality and inform future policy development.

This study analyzed retrospective chart data from patients with cognitive impairment receiving KM home-visit care to evaluate the clinical impact of four-month interventions on pain, cognitive function, BPSD, clinical global impressions, and caregiver burden. By examining real-world clinical data, we aim to provide evidence that can inform future healthcare policies and care systems for patients with cognitive impairment.

Methods

Study design

This multi-center retrospective observational study was conducted and reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology guidelines (Supplementary File 1)13. It evaluated the clinical effectiveness of KM home-visit services across six regional KM home-based medical centers participating in the “Long-term Care Home-based Medical Center Pilot Project” commissioned by the Ministry of Health and Welfare of South Korea. Given the multimodal and individualized nature of the intervention, this study is best characterized as a pragmatic evaluation of a real-world KM home-visit service rather than a controlled efficacy trial of any specific therapeutic component. The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Dong-eui University Korean Medicine Hospital (Approval No.: DH-2025-23; Approval Date: November 26, 2025). Following the Declaration of Helsinki14, all participants received study information and provided written informed consent.

Eligibility criteria

Participants were selected by screening medical records of patients with cognitive impairment who received KM home-visit care between August 2025 and January 2026. Inclusion criteria were: (1) aged 60 years or older; (2) diagnosed by KM doctors with Global Deterioration Scale (GDS) stages 2 to 5; (3) received regular home-visit services for at least four months; and (4) had longitudinal follow-up data at baseline (T0), 2 months (T1), and 4 months (T2).

Patients were excluded if they had severe mental illness or acute medical conditions that prevented standardized clinical evaluation. Patients who discontinued the intervention during the observation period due to hospitalization or institutionalization were also excluded. To reflect the home-visit environment and minimize selection bias, cases with missing values in specific assessment items were retained if evaluation data were available for each scheduled time point.

Clinical management and quality control

Multidisciplinary teams consisting of a KM doctor, a nurse, and a social worker provided care according to the “Long-term Care Home-based Medical Center Pilot Project” guidelines. Personalized care plans were developed following initial consultations and comprehensive geriatric assessments. KM doctors provided integrated services, including acupuncture, moxibustion, cupping, and herbal prescriptions, at least once monthly. The clinical protocol adopted the framework of the Busan Metropolitan City Dementia Prevention Project, which effectively suppresses progression from mild cognitive impairment (MCI) to dementia.15 Within this framework, a specific set of cognitive-enhancing acupoints, including GV20, EX-HN1, PC6, HT7, PC8, and ST36, was recommended (Fig. 1)15. The final selection was left to the KM doctor’s clinical discretion to allow individualized treatment. Nurses conducted bi-monthly visits for nursing care and medication guidance (a formally designated nursing service encompassing verification of medication adherence, management of remaining medications, and monitoring for adverse drug reactions, but not including dose adjustment recommendations), while social workers facilitated community resource linkage. KM doctors are legally authorized to prescribe Korean herbal medicines and perform KM procedures, but are not licensed to prescribe conventional pharmacological agents such as anti-dementia drugs, antipsychotics, or analgesics; all interventions delivered were strictly within the scope of KM. Participation in the program was voluntary and initiated by patients or their families.

To ensure data consistency across centers, we implemented a comprehensive quality control strategy. All evaluators completed web-based training modules developed by the researchers, covering assessment procedures and documentation standards. Training materials remained accessible on the online platform throughout the study for ongoing reference. While treatment individualization was permitted, core assessment procedures remained uniform. To address implementation challenges, the research team maintained real-time communication with participating centers through a dedicated consultation platform, enabling immediate support for protocol clarification and troubleshooting.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using Python (version 3.12) with pandas, scipy, and numpy libraries. Prior to analysis, the missing data pattern was assessed using Little’s MCAR test (χ² = 199.882, df = 175, p = 0.096), supporting that missing data were consistent with an MCAR mechanism26. Missingness in caregiver-administered instruments (NPI-Q and ZBI-12) showed near-complete overlap (Cohen’s κ = 0.921), indicating that missing data were structurally attributable to the absence of a primary caregiver among participants living alone. Baseline characteristics did not significantly differ between participants with and without caregiver assessments (all p > 0.05). Complete case analysis (CCA) was adopted as the primary analysis; median imputation was retained as a sensitivity analysis. The non-parametric Friedman test analyzed longitudinal changes across three time points (T0, T1, T2), with post-hoc Wilcoxon signed-rank tests applying Bonferroni correction (significance threshold: p < 0.0167)27. Hodges-Lehmann estimates with 95% confidence intervals were calculated for T0-to-T2 changes.

Clinical effect sizes (r) were calculated using the formula r = |Z| / √N, with values of 0.1, 0.3, and 0.5 representing small, medium, and large effects, respectively. Relationships between clinical improvements (Δ) were examined using Spearman correlation. Subgroup analysis stratified participants into MCI (GDS 2–3) and dementia (GDS 4–5) groups17. Within-group changes were evaluated using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test, and between-group differences in change scores were compared using the Mann-Whitney U test. For all other analyses, p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Spearman rank correlation of change scores (ΔT0→T2) was conducted using pairwise CCA, whereby each variable pair was analyzed using all cases with observed data for both variables. Between-group differences in change scores were compared using the Mann-Whitney U test.

Data visualizations were generated using Python (version 3.12) with Matplotlib and Seaborn libraries.

Results

Participant characteristics

Of 99 patients who initiated KM home-visit care with a chief complaint of cognitive concerns, seven were excluded: five due to a GDS score of 1 or missing baseline GDS data, and two due to hospitalization or institutionalization during follow-up (Supplementary File 2). The final analysis included 92 participants. Mean age was 83.21 ± 7.83 years (range: 62–97), with 55 males (59.8%) and 37 females (40.2%). At baseline, median GDS score was 3 (interquartile range [IQR]: 2–5). The sample included 58 participants (63.0%) in the MCI group (GDS 2–3) and 34 (37.0%) in the dementia group (GDS 4–5) (Table 1). (4) Global clinical status: Under CCA, GDS distributions changed significantly (Friedman test: χ² = 7.724, p = 0.021; N = 89; Hodges-Lehmann estimate: 0.0, 95% CI: 0.0 to 0.0; r = 0.22). CGI-S showed a significant overall change (χ² = 6.157, p = 0.046; N = 87; Hodges-Lehmann estimate: 0.0, 95% CI: 0.0 to 0.5; r = 0.25), with post-hoc analysis showing a significant increase at 4 months compared to 2 months (T1 vs. T2: p = 0.005, r = 0.30), reflecting disease progression. CGI-I scores showed a significant trend (p = 0.001; N = 87; r = 0.36).

Correlations among changes in clinical outcomes

Spearman correlation analysis based on pairwise CCA revealed strong positive correlations among BPSD-related change scores. Improvement in NPI-Q severity was highly correlated with improvements in symptom count (rho = 0.833, p < 0.001; N = 54) and caregiver distress (rho = 0.721, p < 0.001; N = 54). NPI-Q symptom count and caregiver distress were also significantly correlated (rho = 0.527, p < 0.001; N = 54). An inverse correlation was identified between changes in caregiver burden (ZBI-12) and patient depressive symptoms (GDS-SF; rho = − 0.301, p = 0.027; N = 54). No significant correlations were observed between pain and BPSD or caregiver burden outcomes (all p > 0.05) (Fig. 3).

Subgroup analysis

Subgroup CCA showed significant PAINAD improvements in both MCI (p < 0.001, r = 0.65, N = 57) and dementia groups (p < 0.001, r = 0.40, N = 32). NPI-Q severity showed a within-group signal in the dementia subgroup (p = 0.018, r = 0.43, N = 20) but not in MCI (p = 0.167, N = 33). ZBI-12 showed a weak Friedman signal in the MCI subgroup (p = 0.033, N = 33); however, no pairwise post-hoc comparison reached significance after Bonferroni correction. CDT showed an exploratory within-group signal in the dementia subgroup (p = 0.049, r = 0.31, N = 21). Between-group comparisons of change scores were not statistically significant for any outcome (PAINAD: p = 0.105; NPI-Q Severity: p = 0.345; ZBI-12: p = 0.600; CDT: p = 0.536), and all subgroup findings should be interpreted as exploratory only (Supplementary File 5).

Discussion

A four-month KM home-visit care program was associated with significant reductions in pain and BPSD, alongside trends in caregiver burden reduction. These findings suggest that KM home-visit services may contribute to stabilizing the caregiving environment for patients with cognitive impairment.

Pain reduction (PAINAD) showed the largest effect size (r = 0.56) across all outcomes. While pain in patients with dementia is often under-recognized due to communication barriers, unaddressed pain can trigger BPSD28. Although pain management is typically considered necessary for BPSD control28,29, our correlation analysis found no significant association between pain reduction and improvements in other clinical outcomes. This suggests that KM interventions may address pain and BPSD through independent mechanisms, though the retrospective design limits causal inference.

Previous studies have shown that herbal medicine, including Yokukansan, can reduce BPSD11. Consistent with this evidence, we observed significant reductions in NPI-Q severity (p = 0.006) and caregiver distress (p < 0.001) under the primary CCA. NPI-Q symptom count showed a non-significant trend (p = 0.086) and should be interpreted cautiously. Importantly, BPSD severity improvement was strongly correlated with reduced caregiver distress (rho = 0.721, p < 0.001), confirming that reductions in BPSD are closely linked to caregiver relief. Since BPSD is a major driver of caregiver burnout and early institutionalization3–5, these improvements suggest that KM home-visit care may help support AIP.

While MMSE-DS scores remained stable, CDT scores showed a non-significant trend toward improvement under the primary complete case analysis (p = 0.098; Hodges-Lehmann: +0.5, 95% CI: 0.0 to 2.0). While MMSE-DS tracks global cognitive status, CDT more sensitively captures visuospatial construction, executive function, and attention—domains directly linked to functional abilities in daily life30. The absence of significant CDT change under CCA, alongside an exploratory within-group signal in the dementia subgroup (p = 0.049, r = 0.31), suggests that executive function may remain partially responsive to intervention even in advanced stages, though this should be regarded as hypothesis-generating only. These cognitive outcomes likely reflect stabilization rather than improvement, which may itself represent a clinically meaningful finding in the context of a progressive neurodegenerative condition.

The slight increase in CGI-S (3 to 4, p = 0.046) likely reflects disease progression during the four-month study period. However, the significant improvement in CGI-I (p = 0.001, r = 0.36) indicates that KM home-visit care provides clinical benefits in managing distressing symptoms despite ongoing neurodegeneration. These findings suggest the program serves as a supportive intervention that stabilizes the caregiving environment by addressing core clinical burdens such as pain and BPSD. It should be noted that CGI-S and CGI-I were assessed by the treating KM doctors rather than independent raters, introducing a potential source of expectation bias that may have influenced ratings in a favorable direction. The CGI-S increase from 3 to 4 reflects worsening absolute disease severity consistent with disease progression, whereas CGI-I captures symptomatic change relative to baseline; these two measures capture distinct dimensions—disease trajectory versus symptomatic response—and should not be interpreted as contradictory19.

Although caregiver burden (ZBI-12) did not reach statistical significance under the primary CCA (p = 0.074; Hodges-Lehmann: −1.5, 95% CI: −3.5 to 1.0; r = 0.18), the sensitivity analysis using median imputation indicated a significant reduction (p < 0.001). Both results should be considered together. While patient pain and BPSD improved significantly by 2 months, in the sensitivity analysis, caregiver burden decreased significantly only at 4 months. This time lag suggests that sustained clinical stability in the patient is needed before medical interventions translate into caregiver relief31. Also, an inverse correlation was observed between changes in caregiver burden and patient depressive symptoms (ZBI-12 × GDS-SF: rho = − 0.301, p = 0.027), suggesting complex relational dynamics within the caregiving dyad that warrant further investigation. These findings warrant replication in prospective studies with adequate power.

The absence of significant change in depressive symptoms (GDS-SF) and loneliness scores should not be interpreted simply as a lack of psychosocial benefit from KM home-visit care. Loneliness in older adults with cognitive impairment is a multidimensional construct embedded within complex socioeconomic and relational contexts; intervention strategies targeting loneliness require approaches that extend beyond social isolation alone32. Furthermore, loneliness, apathy, resilience, and cognitive function interact dynamically—loneliness mediates the relationship between resilience and apathy, with cognitive function further moderating this pathway33—suggesting that meaningful psychosocial change is unlikely to emerge over a short four-month period without socially embedded or psychologically targeted components. The stability of GDS-SF and loneliness scores over four months may therefore reflect prevention of further deterioration rather than a failure of the intervention34,35, and longer-term, integrated medico-social approaches will be needed to address these outcomes.

Subgroup CCA analysis showed that pain (PAINAD) improved significantly in both MCI (p < 0.001, r = 0.65, N = 57) and dementia groups (p < 0.001, r = 0.40, N = 32), suggesting KM home-visit care may benefits patients regardless of cognitive decline severity. NPI-Q severity showed a within-group signal in the dementia subgroup (p = 0.018, r = 0.43, N = 20) but not in the MCI subgroup (p = 0.167, N = 33). ZBI-12 showed a weak Friedman signal in the MCI subgroup (p = 0.033, N = 33); however, no pairwise post-hoc comparison reached significance after Bonferroni correction, and this should be considered a weak exploratory signal only. CDT showed an exploratory within-group signal in the dementia subgroup (p = 0.049, r = 0.31, N = 21). Critically, between-group comparisons of change scores were not statistically significant for any outcome (PAINAD: p = 0.105; NPI-Q Severity: p = 0.345; ZBI-12: p = 0.600; CDT: p = 0.536), and all subgroup observations should be interpreted as exploratory only. Adequately powered, prospective studies with pre-specified subgroup hypotheses are required to formally evaluate stage-specific treatment effects.

This study has several limitations. First, the retrospective observational design without a control group limits causal inference regarding the specific effects of KM interventions. Second, the sample size (N = 92) may have limited statistical power for subgroup analyses, and the four-month follow-up is relatively short for a degenerative condition. Third, heterogeneity in visit frequency and specific interventions among the six participating centers may affect generalizability. Fourth, the requirement for complete four-month participation may have introduced survivor selection bias, as patients who required hospitalization or institutionalization during the follow-up period were excluded, potentially overstating observed benefits. Future prospective studies should implement intention-to-treat frameworks to account for all enrolled participants regardless of completion status. Fifth, CGI-S and CGI-I were assessed by treating KM doctors rather than independent raters, introducing potential expectation bias. Sixth, concurrent use of conventional medical services was not controlled or documented; the potential influence of concurrent conventional care cannot be excluded. Finally, the study population was limited to GDS stages 2–5; applicability to more severe dementia (GDS stages 6–7) remains to be established. Despite these limitations, this study provides real-world evidence on KM home-visit care effectiveness using data from primary care settings, contributing to the evidence base for integrating KM services into community-based dementia care systems.

In conclusion, a four-month KM home-visit care program was associated with significant reductions in pain and BPSD, alongside trends in caregiver burden reduction. Despite ongoing cognitive decline, KM services may help stabilize the family caregiving environment. These hypothesis-generating findings from a pragmatic real-world evaluation support integrating KM services into community-based dementia care systems and the design of future controlled trials to confirm effectiveness.
