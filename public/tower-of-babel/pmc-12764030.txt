Effects of lower limb neural mobilisation on reactive agility, balance, and physical performance: a randomised controlled trial
Authors: Selim Mahmut Gunay, Ece Erturk
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeNeural mobilization (NM) is commonly applied to enhance neural tissue mobility and reduce pain, but its effects on physical performance in healthy individuals remain uncertain. This randomised controlled trial investigated the effects of lower limb NM, applied alone or in combination with dynamic stretching, on reactive agility (RA), balance, flexibility, and muscle strength in healthy young women.Materials and methodsSixty healthy female participants were randomly assigned to three groups: the NM group, the NM + dynamic stretching (NMDS) group, and the dynamic stretching only (DS) group. Interventions were performed three times per week for two weeks under physiotherapist supervision. Performance was assessed using the RA test and vertical jump, while dynamic balance was evaluated with the Y balance test. In addition, hamstring flexibility and lower extremity isometric muscle strength were measured.ResultsResults showed significant main effects of time across all parameters (p < 0.05), with large effect sizes for balance, flexibility, and muscle strength. Notably, the NMDS group demonstrated superior improvements in Y Balance Test performance (F(2,57) = 8.83; p < 0.001; η²=0.237) compared with NM and DS. Hamstring flexibility also improved most prominently in the NMDS group, with a 21% increase. Although RA, vertical jump, and muscle strength improved within all groups, no significant group × time interactions were detected for these measures (p > 0.05). No adverse events occurred.ConclusionThese findings support the use of NM, especially when integrated with dynamic stretching, as a safe and effective intervention for enhancing neuromotor control, performance optimisation, and injury risk reduction in healthy individuals.Trial registrationNCT06951048; Registration date: 22 April 2025; (https://clinicaltrials.gov/ct2/show/NCT06951048).Keywords: Neural mobilisation, Dynamic stretching, Reactive agility, Balance, Flexibility

Introduction

Neural mobilisation (NM) is a treatment method based on mobilising neural tissue through manual techniques or exercise-based approaches. In this approach, the nervous system is moved relative to the surrounding tissues via mechanical and/or neurophysiological pathways, aiming to reduce symptoms [1]. NM techniques can be applied to facilitate the gliding of peripheral nerves over adjacent structures or to improve range of motion without increasing nerve tension [2].

Two neurodynamic mobilisation techniques are defined: sliding (gliding) and tensioning. Sliding targets peripheral nerve mobilisation through alternating movements in at least two joints; one joint’s movement increases nerve tension while the other reduces it, promoting neural tissue movement relative to surrounding structures. Tensioning involves simultaneous movement of two joints, each increasing nerve tension, thereby producing greater overall mechanical loading on the neural tissue [3, 4].

Human and animal studies have reported that NM reduces intraneural edema, improves intraneural fluid distribution, decreases thermal and mechanical hyperalgesia, and reverses increased immune responses [5]. Although NM is widely used to enhance the functionality of neural structures and reduce disease-related symptoms, in recent years its application in asymptomatic individuals has been investigated for its effects on flexibility and performance parameters, as well as for injury prevention [6, 7]. Based on these findings, it has been suggested that incorporating NM into pre-sport warm-up routines may help prevent injuries. Nunes et al. applied lower limb nerve mobilisation in asymptomatic individuals to examine changes in functional performance and balance parameters. In their study, they applied NM (tensioning and mobilisation) to the sciatic, tibial, and femoral nerves in healthy individuals in a single session and reported no positive or negative change in performance, suggesting that dosage and duration of application might influence the results obtained [8]. Waldhelm et al., investigating the acute effects of neural gliding exercises on athletic performance in healthy individuals, reported that although bilateral sciatic neural gliding exercises applied to the lower limbs did not surpass a DS protocol in terms of performance, they resulted in greater improvement in hamstring flexibility [9]. Similarly, Sharma et al. stated that adding neural tensioning or mobilisation to static stretching for the lower limbs provided greater improvement in hamstring flexibility compared to static stretching alone [10]. However, dynamic performance assessment was not conducted in that study. In another study examining the effects of neurodynamic gliding techniques on hamstring flexibility in healthy football players, significant increases in hamstring flexibility were reported in the neurodynamic intervention group compared to the control group [11]. D’souza et al., evaluating the short-term effects of different NM techniques on hamstring flexibility in recreational football players, found significant improvements in both neural tensioning and NM groups, with no significant difference between the groups in sit-and-reach and active knee extension test results [12]. Similarly, in a study by Ferrera et al., the acute effects of neural gliding and neural tensioning exercises on static postural control and jumping in football players were investigated. Both techniques resulted in significant improvements in the measured parameters, with no difference between techniques [13].

NM techniques can be used as part of assessment, treatment, or warm-up programs [4]. In this context, NM assessment and mobilisation techniques may be used to identify injury risk factors [14, 15] and to support performance, either in combination with other interventions or as a standalone option [11, 16].

Although the literature indicates that NM techniques contribute to flexibility in asymptomatic individuals, their effects on functional performance, balance, and posture vary. Methodological differences between studies, and the lack of an optimal application duration and protocol definition, are considered sources of these inconsistencies. Indeed, the literature includes interventions ranging from 5 to 20 repetitions lasting 2 min to applications lasting up to 8 weeks [16–18]. Furthermore, the effect of NM on reactive agility (RA)—a parameter considered important for sports performance—has not yet been evaluated. The small number of studies conducted on healthy populations and the methodological differences in existing research create uncertainty regarding the effectiveness of NM techniques.

The aim of this study is to investigate the effects of lower limb NM techniques on RA, balance, and physical performance in healthy individuals. In this context, we hypothesise that neural mobilisation could be an effective intervention contributing to improvements in reactive agility, balance, and physical performance parameters in healthy individuals.

Materials and methods

Participants

This study included 60 healthy female participants with no health problems that would prevent participation in NM or DS protocols. Even if participants were physically fit to perform exercise, the following conditions were defined as exclusion criteria: history of lumbar or lower extremity surgery within the past year, diagnosis of scoliosis, presence of localised pain in the lumbar region or lower extremities within the past 3 months, and a history of neurodegenerative or neuromuscular disease. Participants were informed that those experiencing discomfort during NM or DS interventions would be excluded from the study. In addition, individuals who missed two consecutive exercise sessions or more than three sessions in total were excluded from the study and not included in the data analysis.

Ethical approval

This study was approved by the Bursa Uludağ University Health Research Ethics Committee (Ethics Committee Approval Number: 2025/4 − 1). Before participant recruitment, the study was registered with the ClinicalTrials.gov system (Registration number: NCT06951048; Registration date: 22 April 2025; https://clinicaltrials.gov/ct2/show/NCT06951048). All procedures were conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, and written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Randomisation and blinding

This study was designed as a superiority, three-arm, parallel-group randomised controlled trial with an allocation ratio of 1:1:1. Participants to be included in the study were selected from healthy females who met the inclusion and exclusion criteria. The random allocation sequence was generated by an independent researcher not involved in data collection or analysis. Eligible participants were assigned equally into one of three groups using a computer-assisted program with a simple random sampling method: NM group, Neural Mobilisation + Dynamic Stretching Group (NMDS), and DS Group. The randomisation process was conducted by an independent researcher who was not involved in data collection or analysis. Participants were not informed about the purpose and expected effects of the interventions to prevent expectancy bias.

The study was reported in accordance with the CONSORT statement for presenting parallel-group randomised trials and NM interventions [19]. Figure 1 shows the study design and group allocation.

Intervention

The exercise interventions, consisting of NM and dynamic stretching, were applied in three separate groups, each with a different exercise protocol, three times a week for two weeks. All sessions were delivered in a controlled exercise environment by an experienced physiotherapist with 10 years of clinical practice. Before each session, all participants performed a warm-up protocol consisting of 3 min of low-intensity jogging.

Neural mobilisation group

Participants in this group underwent specific NM techniques targeting the sciatic, femoral, and tibial nerves. The techniques determined for each nerve were based on sliding principles in accordance with the anatomical course of the nerve, applied as 4 sets of 10 repetitions. Exercises were performed sequentially for both lower limbs, with a 30-second rest between sets. The selected exercise dose was determined based on previous evidence demonstrating that similar dosages of neural mobilisation effectively improve lower-limb flexibility without adverse effects [16].

Sciatic nerve mobilisation

The participant lay in a side-lying position, with the limb to be treated positioned uppermost. The hip was positioned in approximately 90° flexion, the knee in extension, and the ankle in dorsiflexion. During the exercise, the head and ankle moved in opposite directions to achieve neurodynamic sliding (head extension – ankle dorsiflexion; head flexion – ankle relaxation) [3, 20].

Femoral nerve mobilisation

The participant was positioned in a side-lying position with the upper leg designated as the target limb, while the lower hip and knee were flexed to 90° for support. The physiotherapist stood behind the participant and extended and adducted the target hip until a slight increase in lumbar lordosis was observed and the foot touched the floor. Femoral nerve mobilisation was performed by synchronising hip and knee movements with head and cervical spine position changes in opposite directions; specifically, cervical flexion was applied during hip extension, and cervical extension during hip flexion, to create a neural sliding effect along the pathway. Throughout the procedure, the participant maintained a pain-free and comfortable range of motion, while the physiotherapist applied gentle, rhythmic movements aimed at enhancing neural tissue mobility [20, 21].

Tibial nerve mobilisation

The participant lay supine. The hip and knee were positioned in 45° flexion. With the ankle dorsiflexed and everted, and the toes in flexion, the limb was positioned. Mobilization was performed by synchronizing head flexion – toe extension and head extension – toe flexion [8, 20].

Neural Mobilisation + Dynamic Stretching (NMDS) Group

Participants in this group performed the same NM protocol as the NM group, followed by DS exercises. The DS program consisted of active straight leg raise (SLR), vertical jump, and 10-meter sprint exercises. Each exercise was performed in 3 sets of 10 repetitions. The NM and DS protocols were performed consecutively in the same session.

Dynamic Stretching (DS) Group

The DS group performed only the DS protocol for two weeks. This program, targeting the lower extremities, consisted of SLR, vertical jump, and 10-meter sprint exercises, performed in 3 sets of 10 repetitions.

SLR gait exercise

Participants were instructed to stand upright in an erect posture. The arms were either extended forward at shoulder height or kept relaxed at the sides. The exercise was performed by raising one leg forward without bending the knee to achieve hip flexion. During the movement, the trunk was maintained in an upright position, and excessive flexion or extension of the lumbar region was avoided. Each repetition involved alternating leg lifts to create a walking-like rhythm with straight-leg raises. The exercise tempo was controlled, with each lifting and lowering cycle lasting approximately 1–2 s. The range of motion was adjusted according to each participant’s hamstring flexibility, and care was taken to ensure that no pain or discomfort occurred during the exercise.

Rhythmical vertical jump exercise

This exercise was a low-amplitude plyometric activity designed to improve the elastic properties of the lower-limb muscle–tendon unit—particularly the gastrocnemius, soleus, and Achilles tendon—and to enhance the neuromuscular system’s ability to perform rapid, repetitive contractions. Participants stood upright with the arms at the sides and the trunk maintained in an erect position. During the exercise, knee flexion was kept to a minimum, and rhythmic, low-intensity jumps were performed using only ankle plantar flexion. The heels did not touch the ground; brief, repetitive jumps were executed using forefoot contact only. The jump height was maintained at approximately 5–10 cm. The rhythm was set to approximately one to two jumps per second, and the exercise was performed using a metronome set between 120 and 140 beats per minute.

10-meter sprint exercise

Participants performed a 10-meter sprint on a flat surface marked with start and finish lines. The starting position was set in a semi-crouched stance with the forefeet in contact with the ground. Each sprint was self-initiated, and a 15–20 s rest interval was provided between trials. Participants were instructed to maintain maximal acceleration throughout the sprint distance and to avoid decelerating before crossing the finish line.

Participants did not receive any other concomitant care or interventions during the trial.

Assessment

All assessments were performed before the intervention (pre-test) and on the third day following the completion of the interventions (post-test), and the data were recorded. To prevent acute physiological responses from influencing the assessment findings, a 72-hour waiting period was implemented after the final intervention session before conducting the post-test measurements. Before the formal assessments, all participants received standardised instructions and were given brief practice trials for each test to ensure correct execution and minimise potential learning effects between pre- and post-assessments.

Reactive agility

To assess RA, a Y-shaped course was set up. Y reactive agility tests are reliable measures that can distinguish between semi-professional and amateur performance levels [22]. The course was arranged using three cones placed 3 m apart, with additional cone markers indicating the midpoint and the 1.5-meter distance from the starting line to standardise cue delivery. Participants started the test from a position 50 cm behind the starting line.

During the assessment, participants sprinted 3 m in a straight line, and at the midpoint of the course, upon receiving a directional cue from the assessor, they changed direction approximately 45° to the right or left to reach the finish line. The change-of-direction command was delivered by the assessor based on the participant’s approach to the 1.5-meter cone marker, determined visually rather than by an automated system. This approach was chosen because, as noted that human-based stimuli provide greater ecological validity and reliability for assessing RA in sport-specific contexts [23].

All trials were video-recorded using a high-frame-rate camera positioned laterally to the course. Sprint times were later extracted by analysing the video footage to ensure precise identification of the start and finish points. Timing began simultaneously with the initiation of the sprint and stopped when the participant crossed the finish line. Each participant performed the test three times, and the best performance time was used in the statistical analysis. All measurements were conducted under identical environmental conditions and by the same assessor.

Vertical jump test

To assess lower-limb explosive power, the vertical jump test was administered following a standardised field-based protocol. After explaining the test purpose, the procedure was demonstrated, and participants were allowed several practice trials before testing. Each participant stood upright next to a flat wall, positioned with the inner shoulder approximately 15 cm away from the wall.

The standing reach height was determined by having the participant extend the arm closest to the wall upward as high as possible and make a mark with chalk applied to the fingertips. This served as the baseline height for calculating jump performance. Participants then performed a single-leg countermovement jump on the dominant leg, incorporating an arm swing to allow a natural take-off motion. The countermovement depth was self-selected and performed within a comfortable, pain-free range without any preparatory step or pause.

The goal was to achieve the greatest possible vertical displacement from a stationary position while maintaining balance on the supporting leg. At the highest point of the jump, participants touched the wall again, leaving a second chalk mark. The vertical jump height was determined by measuring the distance between the standing reach mark and the highest jump mark. Each participant performed three maximal trials, and the best score was recorded for analysis [24, 25].

Y balance test

To assess balance, the Y Balance Test was performed for the dominant lower limb. During the test, participants were instructed to reach as far as possible in the anterior, 135° posteromedial, and 135° posterolateral directions on a prearranged apparatus. After six practice trials in each direction, three test trials were conducted, and the highest score in each direction was used for evaluation. Lower limb length was also measured to obtain the composite lower limb balance score, which was used in the data analysis [26, 27].

Straight leg raise test

Active SLR testing was used to determine hamstring flexibility. The participant was instructed to raise the test limb straight, without knee flexion, three times. Using a goniometer, the highest hip flexion value obtained was recorded in degrees [28].

Isometric muscle strength

In this study, the Lafayette Manual Muscle Testing System (Model-01165, Lafayette Instrument Company, Lafayette, IN, USA) was used to evaluate isometric muscle strength and power of the lower limb. Following bilateral exercises, assessments were performed only on the dominant lower limb, and these data were used in the analyses.

Knee extension strength

Participants were seated with hips and knees at 90° flexion. A hand-held dynamometer was placed ~ 2 cm proximal to the lateral malleolus on the intermalleolar region. They performed maximal isometric knee extension for 5 s. Knee Flexion Strength: In the same position, with knees extended, the dynamometer was placed ~ 2 cm proximal to the lateral malleolus on the posterior leg. Participants performed maximal knee flexion against resistance for 5 s. Hip Flexion Strength: Seated with hips and knees at 90° flexion, the dynamometer was placed just proximal to the knee on the anterior thigh. Participants performed maximal isometric hip flexion for 5 s. For all tests, three maximal contractions were recorded, and the average value was analysed. Force (N) was multiplied by tibial shaft length (tibial tuberosity to medial malleolus, m) to calculate peak torque (Nm) [29, 30].

Statistical analysis

All data were analysed using IBM SPSS Statistics 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). The Shapiro–Wilk test was used to assess the normality of data distribution. Descriptive statistics for normally distributed continuous variables were expressed as arithmetic mean ± standard deviation (X̄ ± SD), while non-normally distributed variables were expressed as median (minimum–maximum).

To compare baseline (pre-test) demographic characteristics and performance measures between groups, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used for normally distributed variables, and the Kruskal–Wallis test for non-normally distributed variables.

Within-group pre–post changes were assessed using paired-samples t-tests. For between-group comparisons, ANOVA was performed, and Bonferroni-adjusted post-hoc analyses were conducted for variables showing significant differences.

Effect sizes were reported as Cohen’s d for within-group analyses and eta squared (η²) for between-group analyses. Effect sizes were interpreted as follows: for Cohen’s d, 0.2 small, 0.5 medium, 0.8 or above large; for η², 0.01 small, 0.06 medium, 0.14 or above large. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05 [31, 32].

Sample size calculation was performed using G*Power 3.1 software. In the a priori power analysis for one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA: Fixed effects, omnibus, one-way), an effect size (f) of 0.45, alpha level (α) of 0.05, statistical power (1–β) of 0.80, and 3 groups were assumed. According to these parameters, a minimum of 51 participants was required. Considering potential dropouts, 20 participants were included in each group, for a total of 60 healthy female participants. This sample size preserved the statistical power of the study and ensured the reliability of between-group comparisons.

There were no missing data in the analysed dataset. No interim analyses were planned or conducted, and no stopping guidelines were specified for this trial.

Results

Data from 60 participants who met the criteria and completed the study were analysed. No participants were lost to follow-up or excluded after randomisation in any of the groups. The baseline demographic characteristics and performance measurements of the participants are presented in Table 1. No statistically significant differences were found between the groups in terms of age, height, weight, body mass index (BMI), or any of the performance-related parameters (p > 0.05). This indicates that the groups had similar baseline characteristics prior to the intervention and that randomisation was successfully implemented.

In RA measurements, the main effect of time was significant (F(1,57) = 106.72; p < 0.001; η²=0.652), indicating a very large effect size. A significant improvement was observed from pre- to post-test across all groups, with the reduction in agility time in the NMDS group demonstrating a comparatively greater clinical effect. The main effect of group was also significant (F(2,57) = 3.47; p = 0.04; η²=0.109), reflecting a medium effect size. Accordingly, general differences were identified between groups irrespective of time. However, the group × time interaction was not significant (F(2,57) = 0.27; p = 0.76; η²=0.009), indicating that the magnitude of improvement was comparable across the three groups.

Y balance measurements revealed a significant main effect of time (F(1,57) = 213.22; p < 0.001; η²=0.789), representing a very large effect size. Significant improvements were observed in all groups from pre- to post-test. The main effect of group was not significant (F(2,57) = 0.61; p = 0.55; η²=0.021), indicating that group means did not differ irrespective of time. In contrast, the group × time interaction was significant (F(2,57) = 8.83; p < 0.001; η²=0.237), with a large effect size, indicating that the extent of improvement differed among groups. Bonferroni post hoc tests revealed that the NMDS group showed greater improvements in Y balance scores compared with the NM and DS groups.

Vertical jump measurements demonstrated a statistically significant main effect of time (F(1,57) = 7.31; p = 0.01; η²=0.114), reflecting a medium effect size. This result indicated significant improvements from pre- to post-test across all groups. The main effect of group was not significant (F(2,57) = 0.32; p = 0.72; η²=0.011), suggesting no general differences between groups. Similarly, the group × time interaction was not significant (F(2,57) = 2.03; p = 0.14; η²=0.066), indicating that the magnitude of improvement did not significantly differ among groups, despite a small-to-medium effect size.

SLR test assessing hamstring flexibility demonstrated a statistically significant main effect of time (F(1,57) = 39.36; p < 0.001; η²=0.408), corresponding to a large effect size. Significant improvements were observed across all groups from pre- to post-test. Descriptive data indicated that the NMDS group achieved the greatest gain, with a 21% increase in flexibility. However, the main effect of group was not significant (F(2,57) = 0.13; p = 0.88; η²=0.005), suggesting no general differences among groups. The group × time interaction was also not significant (F(2,57) = 0.48; p = 0.62; η²=0.017).

Isometric muscle strength assessments revealed significant main effects of time across all parameters (quadriceps: F(1,57) = 25.14; p < 0.001; η²=0.306; hamstring: F(1,57) = 19.42; p < 0.001; η²=0.254; hip flexors: F(1,57) = 22.67; p < 0.001; η²=0.284), all representing large effect sizes. Significant improvements were observed from pre- to post-test in all groups. Descriptive analyses indicated that the NMDS group achieved the greatest gains, with increases of 18% in quadriceps strength, 16% in hamstring strength, and 19% in hip flexor strength. However, the main effects of group were not significant (quadriceps: F(2,57) = 0.41; p = 0.67; η²=0.014; hamstring: F(2,57) = 0.52; p = 0.6; η²=0.018; hip flexors: F(2,57) = 0.36; p = 0.70; η²=0.012), indicating no general differences among groups. Similarly, the group × time interactions were not significant (p > 0.05), suggesting that improvements in muscle strength did not significantly differ across groups.

Harms

No adverse events were reported during the intervention period. Participants were systematically monitored for any discomfort, pain, or injury during and after each session by the supervising physiotherapist, and any such events would have been recorded and reported.

Discussion

This randomised controlled trial is one of the first comprehensive interventional studies in the literature to simultaneously examine the effects of lower limb NM techniques on RA, balance, and physical performance in healthy female individuals. The findings demonstrated that all interventions produced significant within-group improvements. Between groups, however, notable differences were observed, particularly in the balance parameter, with the combination of NM and DS producing significant and moderately effective improvements. In contrast, no significant between-group differences were found for other performance indicators such as RA, vertical jump, and isometric muscle strength. Based on these results, NM performed alone improved performance, but when combined with dynamic stretching—a proven intervention—it provided additional benefits, particularly for balance outcomes.

RA refers to unplanned movements determined by physical, technical, and perceptual–cognitive characteristics. It is essentially characterised as sprints involving directional changes triggered by a specific stimulus [33]. The most critical parameters affecting RA performance are decision-making speed and movement speed. The NM and DS exercises used in our study may be considered potential approaches for improving RA performance. The literature includes interventions that enhance RA through cognitive and perceptual mechanisms [34, 35], as well as plyometric training programs that contribute to the physical and motor components of agility [36]. In this context, the NM and DS interventions in our study, applied alone or in combination, may be considered as exercise options capable of positively contributing to RA performance in athletes through improvements in physical and motor abilities. Zwierko et al. (2023) reported that reactive manoeuvres in volleyball require athletes to continuously adapt their movements according to complex visuospatial stimuli, ensuring neural synchronisation between perceptual–cognitive functions and motor responses. Therefore, reactive agility performance depends directly on the efficiency of perceptual, cognitive, and motor integration at the neural level [37]. Therefore, reactive agility performance depends directly on the efficiency of perceptual, cognitive, and motor integration at the neural level. The improvement in reactive agility observed after neural mobilisation may be related to enhanced neural conduction and afferent feedback mechanisms. As suggested by González-Matilla et al. (2022), peripheral and central mechanisms—such as changes in blood flow, local sensitivity, sensorimotor function, cortical activity, intraneural edema, and inflammation—may explain the beneficial neurophysiological effects of neural mobilisation. These mechanisms may collectively contribute to faster sensory–motor integration and more efficient neuromuscular responses during unplanned movements [38].

Balance is achieved by integrating visual, vestibular, and somatosensory inputs into the central nervous system to produce appropriate motor responses. NM applications reduce neural tension and adhesions, improving intraneural circulation and axonal transmission. This may influence proprioceptive and reflex feedback mechanisms, thereby indirectly affecting balance [38, 39]. While previous literature has reported that NM has no significant acute effects on dynamic balance or physical performance, its potential to support neural integrity when combined with other therapeutic approaches has been emphasised [8]. Kurt et al. found that adding NM techniques to conservative treatment in patients with low back pain did not provide additional benefits for gait or static balance [40]. In our study, all groups demonstrated significant balance improvements after two weeks, with the NMDS group showing greater improvement than the other groups. This supports the view that NM may play a role in enhancing the effects of other interventions. Future studies are warranted to explore the potential effects of NM on neuromotor control and proprioceptive feedback systems, particularly with long-term follow-up.

Good hamstring flexibility is essential for sports performance and health [41, 42]. Moreover, one cause of flexibility loss leading to injury has been attributed to reduced mechanosensitivity in neural tissue [43]. In our study, significant improvements were observed in the SLR test in all groups, indicating that both NM and DS approaches can positively affect hamstring flexibility of the lower extremity. The literature suggests that application dosage and session frequency are determining factors for effectiveness, with low-to-moderate session numbers yielding more favourable results [44]. In our study, all intervention groups showed significant improvement, with the largest clinical effect size observed in the NMDS group. Therefore, in athletic populations, NM may be applied alone or combined with DS to improve flexibility. Furthermore, NM techniques could be integrated into other exercise and training programs to help reduce injury risk.

Athletes with good flexibility are known to perform better in acceleration, sprint, jump, and agility tests [45]. Although the two-week intervention period in this study may have been insufficient to achieve substantial improvements in balance and physical performance, the observed increase in flexibility may have contributed to performance changes. Overmoyer et al. reported a significant relationship between Y Balance Test scores and lower extremity flexibility in healthy and active young adults, as well as an association between flexibility asymmetries in certain directions and test scores [46].

In the literature, the effects of NM on muscle strength vary depending on the protocol, target muscle group, and combination with other techniques. Some studies have reported significant increases in strength, while others have reported only improvements in pain and function [47]. In the present study, NM and its combinations provided measurable increases in lower limb muscle strength, although the magnitude and distribution of these effects varied by muscle group. NM may moderately increase muscle strength in healthy individuals, as supported by the large effect size observed for knee flexion. This effect is thought to be due to increased nerve conduction velocity and improved motor unit activation [48].

This study is among the first randomised controlled trials to assess the simultaneous effects of NM on multiple performance parameters—RA, balance, flexibility, and muscle strength—in healthy individuals. The inclusion of three intervention protocols, successful randomisation, and comparable baseline groups strengthened internal validity. Effect size reporting, reliable measurement methods, and detailed protocol descriptions enhanced clinical relevance, methodological rigour, and reproducibility. Findings show NM can selectively improve multiple physical parameters, with notable gains in balance and flexibility, supporting neuromotor control and movement efficiency. NM appears to be an effective, evidence-based intervention for performance optimisation and injury risk reduction. Future studies should explore NM combined with training programs to assess additive effects.

Limitations

This study has some limitations. The sample consisted exclusively of healthy young female individuals, which limits the generalizability of the findings to other age groups, sexes, or clinical populations. Furthermore, the intervention period was limited to two weeks, preventing the assessment of NM’s long-term effects and neuromuscular adaptation processes. The absence of gold-standard measurement systems for performance assessments may have influenced the reliability of the results. Therefore, future studies with more diverse samples and longer follow-up periods are needed to better evaluate the effectiveness and lasting effects of NM in different populations.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study demonstrated that lower limb NM techniques, whether applied alone or combined with dynamic stretching, produced significant improvements—particularly in balance and flexibility—in healthy young women. Although within-group improvements were observed in RA, muscle strength, and other performance parameters, no significant between-group differences were found for these measures. These findings indicate that neural mobilisation may serve as a complementary strategy within training programs to enhance performance and potentially support injury prevention through improved neuromuscular function.
