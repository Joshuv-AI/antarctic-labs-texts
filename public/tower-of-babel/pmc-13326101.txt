Determination of rational drug use, health literacy, and cyberchondria levels among teachers
Authors: Leyla Şahin, Nursel Üstündağ Öcal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AimThis study aims to determine the levels of rational drug use, health literacy, and cyberchondria among teachers.MethodThis study was conducted with 325 teachers working in Yerköy district of Yozgat province. Data were collected using the Rational Drug Use Scale, the Health Literacy Scale, the Cyberchondria Severity Scale, and a sociodemographic questionnaire. The analyses were performed using SPSS 26.0; Descriptive statistics were calculated, and inferential analyses, including correlation, regression, and group comparison tests, were conducted to address the study objectives.ResultsA total of 325 teachers participated (51.1% female; mean age 35.2 ± 5.8 years). Most reported good or very good health (80%), while 28% had a chronic disease. The mean Rational Drug Use score was 31.2 ± 4.8, with 61.8% showing insufficient knowledge. Health literacy was adequate in 62.5% of participants (mean 37.8 ± 5.9), and cyberchondria levels were moderate (mean 68.4 ± 13.1). Women and younger teachers showed higher cyberchondria and health literacy, respectively. Better health perception, higher parental education, absence of chronic disease, and physician consultation were associated with higher rational drug use and health literacy, and lower cyberchondria. Logistic regression indicated that higher health literacy, better perceived health, and consulting a physician significantly increased the likelihood of adequate rational drug use. Multiple linear regression showed that lower rational drug use, lower health literacy, and poorer perceived health predicted higher cyberchondria.ConclusionThis study revealed that teachers’ e-health literacy may play an important role in reducing cyberchondria tendencies.

Introduction

The World Health Organization (WHO) defines rational drug use as “the provision of appropriate medicines to individuals according to their clinical needs and individual characteristics, in the correct dose and duration, at the lowest cost, and in an easily accessible manner” [19, 69]. Rational drug use (RDU) is a systematic approach that begins with establishing an accurate diagnosis and includes selecting the most effective treatment according to the patient’s characteristics, initiating therapy, monitoring outcomes, and evaluating the results [19].

Health literacy, according to the World Health Organization (WHO), refers to the cognitive and social skills that determine individuals’ motivation and ability to access, understand, and use health information in ways that promote and maintain good health [51, 71]. The Institute of Medicine defines health literacy as the capacity to obtain, process, and understand basic health information and services needed to make appropriate health decisions. The American Medical Association (AMA) describes the concept as a set of skills that includes basic reading and numerical abilities necessary to function effectively within the healthcare environment. These skills also encompass the ability to read and understand prescribed medications, appointment slips, and other health-related materials [71].

Teacher health literacy is defined as “teachers’ capacity to obtain, interpret, and understand basic health information and services, and their competence to use such information and services in ways that enhance students’ learning of health concepts and skills.” Teacher health literacy can be considered the counterpart of student health literacy. While students are consumers of health information in the classroom, teachers act as providers of health information [18].

Cyberchondria is regarded as the reflection of the concept of hypochondria—defined as fear and anxiety arising from misinterpretation of bodily sensations—into the online environment. Cyberchondria is described as excessive or repetitive online searching for health-related information due to health anxiety and concerns, which paradoxically increases rather than alleviates these anxieties [17].

In Türkiye, teachers not only deliver academic content but also serve as influential actors in shaping the health behaviors of students and the wider community. Therefore, understanding their tendencies toward rational drug use, health literacy, and cyberchondria is critical for improving public health. Since teachers interact extensively with children and young people, providing them with health information at an early age can contribute to improved public health, the adoption of healthy lifestyle behaviors, and reduced healthcare cost [33].

In this context, there is a need for research that evaluates the effects of teachers' health literacy and cyberchondria levels on rational drug use from a cultural perspective.

Because students tend to model their teachers' attitudes and behaviors, teachers play a crucial role in raising healthy generations [63].

Studies conducted provide significant general information on rational drug use, health literacy, and cyberchondria.A study conducted with teachers in Turkey showed that 35% of participants had problematic levels of health literacy, and 17% had low levels [26].

Another study showed that 9.6% of participating teachers had insufficient health literacy, and 42.2% had problematic-limited literacy [30].

Low e-health literacy levels can make it difficult for individuals to accurately and consciously evaluate health-related information, leading to health anxiety and cyberchondria. This can cause individuals to misinterpret health problems, self-diagnose, resort to incorrect treatments and unlicensed healthcare services, or make unnecessary visits to emergency rooms, thus placing an additional burden on the healthcare system [35].

Disproportionate and repetitive use of the internet for obtaining health information triggers cyberchondria [49].

Research aim

This study aimed to assess teachers’ levels of rational drug use, health literacy, and cyberchondria within a public health perspective. It further sought to examine the relationships between rational drug use behaviors, health literacy, and cyberchondria severity. The study also investigated the potential role of e-health literacy in reducing maladaptive health information–seeking behaviors. In addition, sociodemographic factors associated with these variables were explored. By identifying determinants of health-related behaviors among teachers, this study aims to contribute to the development of public health interventions that promote rational drug use and improve health literacy at the community level.

Materials and methods

Study design

This study is designed as a descriptive cross-sectional study.

Population and sample

The research was conducted within a defined population of N = 1,450 teachers working in public schools affiliated with the Ministry of National Education in the central district and other districts of Yozgat province.

A power analysis was performed using G*Power 3.1.9.7 with assumptions of α = 0.05, 1–β = 0.95, medium effect size (f2 = 0.15), and three predictors. The minimum required sample size for multiple regression was calculated as n = 172. However, to strengthen representativeness and to compensate for potential data loss (missing responses, outliers, refusal to participate, technical problems), a target sample of n = 350 was planned. The study used a simple random sampling method. During data collection, 350 questionnaires were distributed; 325 were deemed valid and included in the analyses.

Data collection tools

Data were collected through face-to-face questionnaires, with an average completion time of 20–25 min.

Rational Drug Use Scale (RDU)

The Rational Drug Use Scale (RDU), developed by Demirtaş et al. [16], is a 21-item knowledge-based instrument consisting of 10 true and 11 false statements. False statements (items 2, 5, 6, 9, 10, 13, 15, 16, 17, 19, 20) are reverse-coded. Scoring is as follows: True = 2, I do not know = 1, False = 0 (reverse items scored oppositely, False = 2). Total scores range from 0 to 42, with scores ≥ 35 indicating sufficient knowledge. The original internal consistency was Cronbach’s α = 0.79,in this study, α = 0.81. The scale demonstrated high psychometric performance in the teacher population, with item-total correlations ranging from 0.45 to 0.72 and confirmed factorial validity.

Turkish Health Literacy Scale-32 (TSOY-32)

The Turkish Health Literacy Scale-32 (TSOY-32), adapted into Turkish by Okyay et al. [52], is a 32-item 5-point Likert-type scale (0 = very difficult, 4 = very easy). It consists of two dimensions: Treatment and Service (17 items) and Disease Prevention (15 items), encompassing processes of accessing, understanding, appraising, and applying health information. The index score is calculated using the formula [(mean − 1) × (50/3)], resulting in a score between 0 and 50. Categories are defined as: 0–25 inadequate, > 25–33 limited, > 33–42 sufficient, and > 42–50 excellent. The original Cronbach’s α was 0.86; in this study, total α = 0.88, Treatment and Service α = 0.85, and Prevention α = 0.84.

Cyberchondria Severity Scale (CSS-33)

The Cyberchondria Severity Scale (CSS-33), developed by McElroy and Shevlin [46] and adapted into Turkish by Uzun and Zencir [68], is a 33-item 5-point Likert-type scale (1 = never, 5 = always). It includes five subscales: Compulsion (8 items), Excessive Anxiety (8), Excessiveness (8), Reassurance Seeking (6), and Mistrust of Medical Professionals (3). Total scores range from 33 to 165. The original Cronbach’s α was 0.89, and the Turkish adaptation reported α = 0.87. In this study, total α = 0.90, with subscale α values ranging between 0.76 and 0.82. All scales demonstrated high internal consistency: RDU α = 0.81, TSOY-32 total α = 0.88 (subscales 0.84–0.85), CSS-33 total α = 0.90 (subscales 0.76–0.82), all exceeding the 0.70 threshold.

Sociodemographic form

The sociodemographic form consisted of 15 structured questions covering demographic variables (age, gender, marital status, parental education level, teaching branch), socioeconomic variables (perceived income level), and health-related variables (smoking/alcohol use, known chronic disease, regular pharmacological treatment, suspicion of undiagnosed illness, first action taken in case of a health problem, frequency of physician visits in the last 12 months, and general health perception).

Data analysis

Data were entered into Excel and transferred to SPSS 26.0 for analysis. The missing data rate was 1.8%. Score calculations were performed strictly according to scale instructions: 11 items were reverse-coded in the RDU; the index formula was applied in TSOY-32; total and subscale scores were calculated for CSS-33. All calculations were double-checked.

Normality was assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test, Q-Q plots, histograms, and boxplots. Since the CSS-33 total and Excessive Anxiety subscale showed p < 0.05, 25 outliers (|Z|> 3.29), identified via boxplots and Mahalanobis distance, were systematically removed, resulting in a final sample of n = 325. Subsequently, normality was achieved through log₁₀(CSS + 1) transformation (K-S p = 0.152). This transformation was selected to address the right-skewed distribution of cyberchondria scores (skewness = 0.5). Alternative transformations (square root, Box-Cox) were compared, and the log transformation provided the best model fit.

During data cleaning, no duplication, inconsistency, systematic error, or response bias was detected. Reverse coding in the RDU was manually verified in 10 randomly selected participants, and TSOY-32 index calculations were automatically checked for the entire sample.

Ethical approval

Ethical approval for the study was obtained from the Ethics Committee of Yozgat Bozok University Faculty of Social and Human Sciences (Decision No: 19/58 Date: 21.11.2024). All procedures were conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and national ethical guidelines for data protection.

Results

The mean age of the participants was 35.2 ± 5.8 years (range: 26–45). The gender distribution was balanced, with 51.1% female (n = 166) and 48.9% male (n = 159). The majority of participants were married (61.8%), and 47.1% reported a high perceived income level.

Overall health perception was generally positive; 50.2% rated their health as “good,” and 29.8% as “very good.” The average number of physician visits within the last year was 3.8 ± 1.9 (range: 0–10).

The distribution of teaching branches was relatively balanced: primary school teachers (24.0%), science teachers (20.9%), social sciences teachers (19.1%), foreign language teachers (16.9%), and other branches (19.1%). Parental education levels were high, with 68% of mothers and 72% of fathers holding a university degree.

Regular smoking was reported by 8% of participants, and alcohol use was also 8%. The prevalence of known chronic diseases was 28%, with the most common conditions being hypertension (12%) and diabetes (8%). Regular medication use was reported by 25%, and 32% indicated suspicion of an undiagnosed illness.

Significant differences emerged when scale scores were compared according to demographic variables.

Women scored higher than men on cyberchondria (CSS-33 log: 1.84 > 1.82, t = 2.45, p = 0.015, d = 0.25, small-to-moderate effect), particularly on the Excessive Anxiety subscale (1.28 > 1.25, t = 3.10, p = 0.002, d = 0.34). Married participants appeared to have higher knowledge levels in rational drug use (RDU) compared to single participants (31.8 > 30.4, t = 2.15, p = 0.032, d = 0.29).

The younger age group (26–35 years) demonstrated higher health literacy scores than the older group (38.5 > 36.9, F = 5.42, p = 0.021, η2 = 0.016, small effect). General health perception was a strong determinant: a gradient relationship was observed for RDU (very good 33.1 > very poor 28.5, F = 6.78, p < 0.001, η2 = 0.078, moderate effect), TSOY-32 (39.2 > 34.5, F = 3.25, p = 0.013, η2 = 0.038), and CSS-33 (reverse pattern: very poor 1.88 > very good 1.82, F = 5.15, p = 0.001, η2 = 0.060). Post-hoc Bonferroni tests confirmed the ordered group differences.

Participants with higher parental education levels had higher RDU and TSOY-32 scores and lower CSS-33 scores. Smokers were disadvantaged across all scales. Those with a known disease, regular medication use, or suspicion of an undiagnosed illness exhibited lower RDU and TSOY-32 scores and higher CSS-33 scores.

Regarding the first action taken in response to a health problem, those who preferred visiting a physician had the highest RDU and TSOY-32 scores and the lowest CSS-33 scores (F = 4.20/3.10/9.80, p < 0.05, η2 = 0.019–0.057). In contrast, participants who first searched the internet showed the highest level of cyberchondria.

In terms of teaching branch, social sciences teachers had the lowest CSS-33 scores (1.80), whereas science teachers had the highest (1.86) (F = 3.45, p = 0.009, η2 = 0.041, small effect).

Discussion

The mean age of the sample was 35.2 ± 5.8 years, reflecting the concentration of teachers in Turkey within the mid-career period. A relatively young teacher profile offers greater adaptability to digital health tools; however, when combined with family responsibilities (e.g., a marriage rate of 61.8%) and professional workload, it may lead to postponed health decisions and increased stress levels. This demographic pattern is consistent with national data indicating that approximately 55% of teachers in Turkey are between 30–40 years of age, which is above the Organization for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD) average of 40% [15]. Demirel Yazıcı and Cemaloğlu [15] suggested that the concentration of young teachers affects the relationship between teaching experience and burnout, whereas Sarıkaya and Görgülü [61] noted that professional stress is significantly higher in the early career period. Indicators from OECD and EU countries also show that teacher employment in developing countries tends to be concentrated at earlier ages, which may be associated with workload and stress levels [60]. In this context, Turkey’s teacher assignment system contributes to a younger teacher profile,however, there is a long-term need for supportive programs on professional stress management, health awareness, and health literacy [15, 18, 42, 60, 61].

Although the gender distribution was balanced (51.1% female), female teachers had significantly higher cyberchondria log total scores than males. This difference may be associated with family and emotional burdens, gender roles, and empathy-based cognitive processes influencing health anxiety among women. Uysal Toraman et al. [67] reported that during the pandemic, female teachers in Turkey had higher cyberchondria levels than male teachers,this finding suggests that gender-based stress differences may be associated with cyberchondria. Higher scores in the subscales of excessive worry and compulsion among female teachers indicate greater sensitivity to negative interpretation of digital health information, whereas cultural pressure and “strong male” norms may delay the recognition of health anxiety in male teachers.Patrick and Robertson [56] also emphasized that a lack of emotional expression in men negatively affects health behaviors. These gender-based differences have been similarly reported in the post-pandemic teacher health literature, indicating that stress and anxiety levels are higher in female teachers and that psychoeducational and mindfulness-based approaches may be particularly beneficial for women [29, 59, 67], Katsarou et al. [34] systematically demonstrated that stress and anxiety levels were higher among female teachers compared to males during the COVID-19 period and evaluated cyberchondria as a gender-differentiated phenomenon.

Married teachers demonstrated significantly higher rational drug use scores compared to single teachers. This finding suggests that family responsibilities may enhance pharmacological awareness and learning motivation, while intra-family communication and shared health-related decision-making may facilitate information exchange. Conversely, the suspicion of undiagnosed illness was higher among single teachers, implying that a lack of social support may lead individuals to engage in self-diagnosis and increase internet-based information seeking. These results indicate that family structure can influence health behavior direction. While marriage may serve as a protective factor through social support, single teachers may experience professional isolation, and digital health-seeking behaviors may be more pronounced. Lorcu and Bayer [42] reported that marital status is significantly associated with health literacy levels among teachers. This finding suggests that family support may function as a buffering mechanism that strengthens health-related knowledge. Similarly, Ezirmik et al. [20] highlighted the role of social support in reducing health anxiety. Sentell et al. [62] found that married individuals in the U.S. exhibited higher health literacy and more positive health behaviors. Nomaguchi and Milkie [50] reported that family relationships enhance psychological resilience, while Craig and Churchill [12] demonstrated, within the Theory of Planned Behavior framework, that social norms (e.g., family support) strengthen intentions toward health-related behaviors. Accordingly, strengthening social support networks and peer groups among single teachers could have a balancing effect on access to health information and cyberchondria levels.

Approximately 70% of parents had a university degree, indicating a socioeconomically advantaged sample. However, the weak association between parental education and rational drug use suggests limitations in the intergenerational transfer of health awareness. Individual motivation, access to information, and digital literacy skills may be more influential than parental education. Similarly, Bröder et al. [10] highlighted that while parental education supports health literacy, its effect varies depending on individual and environmental conditions.

Physician consultation frequency was significantly higher among participants with higher perceived income, whereas teachers with lower income perception reported lower consultation rates. This finding suggests that economic inequalities may restrict access to healthcare services and redirect individuals toward alternative information sources.

Berkman et al. [8] systematically showed that low health literacy negatively affects health outcomes, which aligns with limitations observed in teacher health behaviors despite high academic qualifications. Sørensen et al. [64] reported a positive relationship between educational level and health literacy in European HLS-EU data but noted variability across countries and professions. Fleary et al. [23] similarly highlighted that parental education supports health behavior but that individual learning and access skills are decisive. Taken together, in high-stress professions such as teaching, the advantages conferred by parental education have limited translation into health behaviors, indicating that digital literacy and individual awareness programs play a crucial role in bridging this gap [8, 10, 23, 64].

Teachers with higher perceived income reported significantly more frequent physician visits. In contrast, teachers with lower perceived income had reduced access to healthcare, suggesting that economic inequalities limit healthcare access and may drive individuals toward alternative information sources. In the lower-income group, increased internet-based information seeking may elevate exposure to misinformation and increase health anxiety. This aligns with the predominance of middle-income socioeconomic structures and rural–urban disparities in Central Anatolia. In rural areas, cost and transportation barriers limit healthcare use, making the internet a primary information source, posing risks to information accuracy.

Fang et al. [21] reported that low in come perception reduced physician access among teachers in mountainous regions of China. Likewise, Marmot [44] emphasized the negative impact of low income on health behaviors, while Adler et al. [2] noted that social determinants exacerbate health inequalities. Kajawu et al. [32] and Alvaredo et al. [3] similarly demonstrated that economic disparities affect access to healthcare resources and information.

Overall health perception was generally good; however, teachers with negative health perception had moderately higher cyberchondria scores. This indicates that health perception is not merely a cognitive assessment but also involves emotional and behavioral components. Positive health perception may act as a psychological buffer, reducing anxiety, whereas negative perception may increase health-related anxiety. Boehm and Kubzansky [11] meta-analytically demonstrated positive effects of positive psychology on physiological health outcomes, and Steptoe et al. [65] reported that subjective well-being reduces anxiety levels. Pressman et al. [57] indicated that positive affect supports health behaviors.

The mean number of physician visits was 3.8, consistent with the annual recommendation of 2–4 visits for adults proposed by the World Health Organization [70]. The WHO global monitoring report emphasizes 2–4 primary care visits annually as a target for universal health coverage. Thus, the average of 3.8 among teachers is within the recommended range, though rural location and heavy teaching loads may limit access. A positive relationship between physician visits and cyberchondria scores was observed, indicating that health anxiety drives both online information seeking and physician verification. Teachers with limited access due to intensive schedules rely more on the internet, reinforcing the information verification loop. Laxminarayan et al. [39] reported that physician-seeking behavior is associated with anxiety levels. Barkley et al. [5] found increased online information seeking among groups with limited primary care access, McMorrow et al. [45] observed that access barriers elevate health anxiety. Collectively, these findings indicate that heavy workloads among teachers make the internet a primary health information source, with WHO [70] emphasizing access-focused programs to strengthen universal health coverage.

Regarding teaching branches, social sciences teachers had lower cyberchondria scores compared to science teachers. This difference may reflect branch-specific cognitive and emotional thinking styles influencing health anxiety. Social sciences teachers’ contextual and empathetic thinking styles may promote flexible interpretation of health information, whereas science teachers’ symptom-focused and analytical thinking may increase anxiety levels. Jennings and Greenberg [31] reported higher emotional awareness and stress management skills among social sciences teachers. Laudonia et al. [38] noted that action research in science education may enhance analytical focus. Hamre and Pianta [27] emphasized that teachers’ cognitive and emotional styles influence behavioral outcomes, while Mishra [48] highlighted that constructivist approaches in social sciences education strengthen contextual thinking.

Although smoking prevalence was low (8%), teachers who smoked had significantly higher cyberchondria scores. This may indicate that online health searches are more frequently used as a stress-coping mechanism and that nicotine dependence may elevate anxiety levels. McNeely et al. [47] showed that school connectedness reduces alcohol and nicotine use among youth, suggesting that social connectedness and stress among teachers may indirectly influence health behaviors.

Teachers with known chronic diseases (28%) had lower rational drug use scores and higher cyberchondria scores, indicating that experiential knowledge does not always reduce anxiety, and insufficient systematic health education may create knowledge gaps. Regular medication users (25%) had lower health literacy scores, suggesting that knowledge deficits negatively impact medication adherence in chronic disease management. Beaunoyer et al. [9] reported associations between digital information seeking and health anxiety in chronic illness,Fried et al. [24] noted that online searches increase uncertainty and anxiety in decision-making. Adhikari Baral and Baral [1] demonstrated improved medication adherence through self-care education in type 2 diabetes. Özer et al. [53] reported that knowledge deficits increase dosing errors among regular medication users. These findings indicate that insufficient systematic education in chronic disease management can exacerbate anxiety and adherence problems.

The rate of undiagnosed disease suspicion was high (32%), and this group demonstrated significantly higher cyberchondria scores. This finding indicates that internet-based self-diagnosis may increase exposure to misinformation and symptom amplification tendencies.

While visiting a physician was the first-line action, rational drug use scores were higher in this group; conversely, individuals who prioritized online information first exhibited higher cyberchondria levels. This indicates that behavioral choices influence health outcomes and that digital search cycles may exacerbate anxiety. Rowlands et al. [58] reported that online symptom searches increase exaggeration tendencies, particularly among individuals with undiagnosed concerns. Kirschner and De Bruyckere [36] demonstrated that information filtering skills in the digital age are limited and that multitasking reduces information quality. Gandla et al. [25] defined cyberchondria as a novel form of health anxiety, with internet search loops reinforcing it. Papp-Zipernovszky et al. [55] highlighted that digital health literacy determines the quality of information seeking, with high-literacy individuals having shorter and more focused searches.

The total rational drug use score was above the theoretical midpoint but below the adequacy threshold. The predominance of insufficient knowledge levels indicates that even within an educated population, risks of pharmacological misuse and self-medication persist. This aligns with national literature and underscores the need to integrate pharmacology-based modules into curricula, especially considering teachers’ role-model status. Atik [4] reported low awareness of rational antibiotic use among teacher candidates, highlighting misconceptions such as antibiotic use for the common cold. Çakmak and Pakyüz [13] found that knowledge deficits were associated with dosage errors. Dost and Üner [18] indicated that increasing teachers’ health literacy enhances their role-model effect and knowledge. Tosun and Hoşgör [66] found that e-health literacy predicts rational drug use, while misinterpretation of digital sources increases self-medication risk.

Health literacy was generally adequate; however, higher treatment-related scores compared to prevention-related scores suggest a reactive health approach. This indicates that time constraints, access limitations, and motivational barriers may restrict proactive behaviors among teachers, highlighting the need for preventive health education in the post-pandemic period. Dost and Üner [18] reported that teachers’ health literacy is predominantly treatment-oriented, while preventive behaviors (e.g., screening and vaccination) remain secondary. Liu et al. [41] found that occupational burnout reduces proactive health-seeking behaviors. Fleary et al. [23] demonstrated that health literacy strongly predicts preventive behaviors and that teachers can serve as positive role models for students. Huang et al. [28] noted that e-health literacy supports medication adherence but that preventive dimensions remain weaker.

Cyberchondria levels were generally moderate, with the “excessive anxiety” subdimension scoring highest and “mistrust of doctors” scoring lowest. This suggests that digital information overload may trigger symptom amplification, while trust in healthcare professionals remains relatively preserved. Intensive internet use emerged as a factor increasing cyberchondria levels. Fernández-Batanero et al. [22] reported that educational technologies may increase stress and burnout among teachers, potentially intensifying health anxiety in digital contexts. Bayzan [7] found that digital citizenship behaviors reduce privacy concerns but may increase anxiety during online health searches. Uysal Toraman et al. [67] reported high levels of cyberchondria and coronavirus anxiety among teachers during the pandemic, with overall cyberchondria at a moderate level and elevated scores in distrust and excessive behavior subscales.

Baştuğ and Eryılmaz-Baştuğ [6] indicated moderate cyberchondria levels among teachers. Ezirmik et al. [20] found a negative, low-intensity, statistically significant correlation between SCÖ total scores and self-perceived health status. Demir et al. [14] noted the highest average in the excessiveness subscale.

The younger age group (26–35) demonstrated advantages in digital access contributing to health literacy, whereas the older group (36–45) showed balancing advantages through experiential knowledge. This balance may reflect easier online information access among younger teachers and practical experience among older teachers.

Perceived health status differences were pronounced: participants with higher well-being perception had higher rational drug use and health literacy scores and lower cyberchondria scores. This suggests that subjective well-being may exert a protective cognitive and emotional effect, indicating that perception-enhancing interventions should be prioritized in teacher health programs.. Yalman and Yaşar [72] reported that health literacy predicts healthy lifestyle behaviors, with stronger rational drug use awareness in high well-being groups. Yılmaz and Kıl [73] found health literacy positively affects rational drug use, while low health perception is associated with higher health anxiety and cyberchondria. Kutcher et al. [37] reported that mental health literacy programs improve subjective well-being and reduce anxiety symptoms among teachers.

Correlation analysis revealed a moderate positive relationship between rational drug use and health literacy, while both variables were negatively associated with cyberchondria. These findings suggest that health literacy may function as a cognitive buffer; however, a substantial portion of variance appears to be explained by other variables such as stress, digital use frequency, and health perception. Tosun and Hoşgör [66] found e-health literacy enhances rational drug use and is negatively related to cyberchondria.Paakkari and Okan [54] noted that health literacy reduced anxiety levels after the pandemic, although stress partially mediated this relationship. Liu et al. [41] indicated that reactive and proactive components of health literacy influence cyberchondria and that health perception may act as a mediator. Bröder et al. [10] reported that health literacy supports preventive behaviors among youth, although its effects in adult populations such as teachers may vary contextually. These findings align with theoretical assumptions that cognitive representations regulate health behaviors.

Regression models satisfied robust statistical assumptions. Cyberchondria was negatively predicted by rational drug use, health literacy, and perceived health status. Conversely, rational drug use was positively predicted by health literacy, perceived health status, and frequency of physician consultation. These results demonstrate the practical significance of the variables and the importance of negative predictive value in early intervention design. Findings are consistent with theoretical expectations derived from the Health Belief Model and the Theory of Planned Behavior, providing an evidence-based foundation for digital health literacy–based program development. Ma et al. [43] reported that school-based literacy interventions reduce health anxiety and strengthen behavioral intention. Lewis et al. [40] reported that digital literacy training predicts behavioral change and may be effectively adapted to teacher samples.

Conclusion

The findings indicate that improving health literacy and rational drug use behaviors may reduce cyberchondria levels among teachers. Given the influence of demographic factors, tailored interventions focusing on e-health literacy should be developed for teachers. Integrating digital health literacy into teacher training programs may contribute to both individual well-being and public health outcomes.
