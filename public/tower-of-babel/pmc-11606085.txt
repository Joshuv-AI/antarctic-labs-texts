Examining the potential causal relationships among smoking behaviors, blood DNA methylation profiles, and the development of coronary heart disease and myocardial infarction
Authors: Wenhua Li, Pan Dong, Yixiao Li, Jiaxin Tang, Siyang Liu, Ling Tu, Xizhen Xu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSmoking has been identified as a standalone risk factor for coronary heart disease (CHD) and myocardial infarction (MI), but the precise underlying mechanisms remain incompletely elucidated.ResultsIn this study, we conducted a two-sample Mendelian randomization analysis to examine the impact of smoking behaviors (including smoking initiation, age of smoking initiation, cigarettes per day, and smoking cessation) and smoking-related DNA methylation at CpG sites on CHD and MI based on the UK Biobank dataset. Additionally, we included the FinnGen and Biobank Japan datasets as replications and performed a meta-analysis to combine the results from different data sources. We further validated our results using genetic colocalization analysis. In genomic analysis, we provided compelling evidence on the association between genetically predicted smoking initiation and increased susceptibility to CHD and MI. In epigenetic analysis, we identified 11 smoking-related CpG sites linked to CHD risk and 10 smoking-related CpG sites associated with the risk of MI based on the UK Biobank dataset. Subsequently, some of these CpG sites were further replicated using the FinnGen or BBJ datasets. Ultimately, a meta-analysis was conducted to integrate findings from various data sources (3 for CHD, and 2 for MI), revealing that 7 of 11 CpG sites were linked to CHD risk; whereas, 7 of 10 CpG sites were associated with MI risk. Furthermore, we performed genetic colocalization analysis and found that cg19744173 (FBLN7), cg00395063 (ARHGEF12), and cg16822035 (MCF2L) exhibited robust evidence of colocalization with coronary heart disease; whereas, cg19529732 (DIABLO), cg26405020 (FES), and cg08940075 (CNN3) demonstrated strong colocalization evidence with the risk of myocardial infarction.ConclusionsOur research offers a novel insight into the impact of smoking on the susceptibility to CHD and MI through the lens of epigenetic DNA methylation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13148-024-01791-y.Keywords: Smoking, Smoking-related methylation, Coronary heart disease, Myocardial infarction

Background

Coronary heart disease (CHD), in particular myocardial infarction (MI), are the most common cause of mortality among cardiovascular diseases [1–3]. Genetic and environmental influences are significant contributors to the pathogenesis of CHD. Numerous observational studies have demonstrated tobacco smoking as a prominent, independent risk factor for CHD [4–16]. The risk of CHD escalates with both the quantity of cigarettes consumed daily and the duration of smoking. It was reported that smokers exhibited a 1.6-fold higher likelihood of developing CHD compared to non-smokers [17]. The relative risk of nonfatal acute myocardial infarction (AMI) in smokers was 2.95 times more than in non-smokers [18].

Epigenome-wide association studies (EWAS) have revealed that smoking behaviors can induce changes in DNA methylation throughout the entire epigenome [19]. Meanwhile, modifications in DNA methylation have been associated with the development of CHD. Multiple studies have demonstrated differences in DNA methylation profiles in peripheral blood, arterial tissues, and myocardial tissue in individuals with CHD. For example, Hiltunen et al. (2002 and 2003). noted a decreasing trend of genomic DNA methylation in atheromatous plaque samples obtained from humans, ApoE−/− mice fed a high-fat diet, and New Zealand rabbits fed a high-cholesterol diet, with these changes being associated with the severity of atherosclerosis [20, 21]. Castro et al. (2019) observed a notable reduction in the methylation levels of peripheral blood leukocytes in patients with atherosclerosis [22]. Subsequent studies have consistently validated the correlation between the genomic methylation of peripheral blood cells and CHD [22–25]. Therefore, we hypothesized that DNA methylation could serve as a reactive epigenetic mechanism that links genetic predisposition to CHD and MI with smoking exposure.

Mendelian randomization (MR), a study design commonly utilized in epidemiology, employs genetic variants as instrumental variables to evaluate the causal impact of exposures on outcomes. This approach is deemed to be advantageous over observational studies as it mitigates confounding factors and reverse causation. In the present study, we conducted a two-sample MR analysis to examine the association between genetic predisposition to smoking behaviors and the risk of CHD and MI, as well as the impact of genetically predicted smoking-related methylation at CpG sites on CHD and MI risk. We further validated our results using genetic colocalization analysis.

Methods

Study design

This is a two-sample Mendelian randomization study (MR) which used summary statistics from relevant GWAS, and Fig. 1 provides an overview of design of the present study. Three vital assumptions in MR analysis must be satisfied: (1) the instrumental variables are associated with exposure; (2) the effects of instrumental variables on exposure are the only factor that affects the outcome; and (3) instrumental variables are independent of exposure and outcome confounders [26]. We firstly performed a two-sample MR to estimate the association of different smoking behaviors with CHD and MI risk. Subsequently, we assessed the causal relationship between smoking-related DNA methylation at CpG sites and the risk of CHD and MI. Thirdly, colocalization analysis was employed to evaluate whether two associated signals (CpG sites and diseases risk) were consistent with a shared causal variant. Finally, we replicated the significant CpG sites using different sources of outcomes.

Schematic diagram of the study design. GWAS, genome-wide association study; EWAS, Epigenome-wide association study; mQTLs, methylation quantitative trait loci; MR, Mendelian randomization; SNP, single-nucleotide polymorphism

Exposure GWAS

In this study, we utilized a comprehensive meta-analysis of genome-wide association studies (GWAS) on tobacco use, which encompassed data from over 1.2 million individuals of European ancestry [27]. Smoking initiation phenotypes included age of initiation of regular smoking (age of smoking initiation; n = 341,427) and a binary phenotype indicating whether an individual had ever smoked regularly (smoking initiation; n = 1,232,091). Heaviness of smoking was measured with cigarettes per day (cigarettes per day; n = 337,334). Smoking cessation (n = 547,219) was a binary variable contrasting current versus former smokers.

2623 known smoking-related DNA methylation at CpG sites were identified in a large epigenome-wide association study meta-analysis conducted by the CHARGE Consortium, which included 15,907 blood-derived DNA samples from participants in 16 cohorts (including 2433 current, 6518 former, and 6956 never smokers) [28]. For this, we used a catalog of SNPs associated with CpG sites in the Genetics of DNA Methylation Consortium (GoDMC) to identify conditionally independent mQTLs. Methylation quantification was conducted using Illumina Infinium HumanMethylation450 BeadChip on bisulfite-converted genomic DNA from 36 cohorts totaling 27,750 European samples in whole blood [29].

Outcome GWAS—coronary heart disease

We extracted summary statistics of coronary heart disease from a large meta-analysis (Table S1), which included 122,733 cases and 424,528 controls from the UK Biobank resource and CARDIoGRAMplusC4D (Coronary ARtery DIsease Genome Wide Replication and Meta-analysis (CARDIoGRAM) plus The Coronary Artery Disease (C4D) Genetics) [30]. Coronary heart disease was defined using the following ICD 10 codes: I21–I25 covering ischemic heart diseases and the following Office of Population Censuses and Surveys Classification of Interventions and Procedures, version 4 (OPCS-4) codes: K40-K46, K49, K50 and K75 which includes replacement, transluminal balloon angioplasty, and other therapeutic transluminal operations on coronary artery and percutaneous transluminal balloon angioplasty and insertion of stent into coronary artery. Self-reported coronary heart disease was also used in the definition.

We also included two other datasets as the replication. The first was from FinnGen consortium, which consisted of 21,012 coronary heart disease cases and 197,780 controls. FinnGen is a large public–private partnership aiming to collect and analyze genome and health data from Finnish biobank participants. The GWAS was adjusted for age, sex, 10 principal components, and genotyping batch [31]. The second dataset was from BioBank Japan (BBJ), BBJ started developing one of the world’s largest disease biobanks, creating a foundation for research aimed at achieving medical care tailored to the individual traits of each patient. In our study, we included 29,319 coronary heart disease cases and 212,453 controls [32].

Outcome GWAS—myocardial infarction

We used a summary GWAS combined with the UK Biobank and CARDIoGRAMplusC4D in a fixed-effects meta-analysis that included a total of 61,000 myocardial infarction cases and 577,000 controls, and 8,126,035 single-nucleotide polymorphisms common to both datasets [33]. Additionally, dataset from FinnGen was also used in our study as a replication, which included 12,801 myocardial infarction cases and 187,840 controls.

Genetic instrument selection

The genetic variants used as instrumental variables for the exposure in MR analyses should be uncorrelated, and strongly associated (p < 5 × 10–8) with the exposure of interest. Of the SNPs obtained from the GWASs of smoking behaviors and mQTLs, we further selected only SNPs with a minor allele frequency > 0.01, which were not in LD (r2 < 0.001 and clumping window within 10,000 base pairs based on European 1000 Genomes Project reference panel) for MR analyses. We calculated the F statistic for each instrumental variable to assess the strength and avoid weak instrument bias. As commonly used, the cutoff was set to F statistic > 10 [34–36]. Additionally, if one selected SNP in the first step was not present in outcome data, we used its proxy SNP with r2 > 0.8 instead. If more than one proxy SNPs were available, we chose the one with the maximum r2 and minimum P value associated with the exposure.

Statistical analysis

We performed two-sample MR analysis of smoking behaviors and smoking-related DNA methylation at CpG sites on coronary heart disease and myocardial infarction risk primarily using the inverse-variance weighted (IVW) method under the multiplicative random effects model. The random effects IVW method assumes the validity of all genetic variants as instrumental variables in a concise manner and addresses heterogeneity through Wald ratio estimates [37]. The results were presented as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) indicating diseases risk per one genetic predicted smoking behaviors and smoking-related DNA methylation at CpG sites standard deviation (SD) change. To account for multiple testing, we applied p-value correction using the Bonferroni method. Associations with an adjusted p-value of less than 0.05, as determined using the random effects IVW model or Wald ratio model, were considered statistically significant. Different cohort results were meta-analyzed using a random effects model.

To test the third MR assumption, we applied MR-Egger to detect possible violations of instrumental variable assumptions due to directional horizontal pleiotropy [38]. Additionally, we used weighted median which is a median of the weighted estimates and provides a consistent effect even if 50% of instrumental variables are pleiotropic [39]. Heterogeneity was tested using Cochran’s Q test in the IVW and MR-Egger methods.

For results that passed our MR threshold (adjusted p-value < 0.05), we performed colocalization using the COLOC package in R. Colocalization assesses the probability of a shared causal variant (PP.H4) between the disease GWAS and instruments for the smoking-related DNA methylation at CpG sites of interest. Colocalization was performed using with default priors (prior probability of initial trait association was 1 × 10–4, prior probability of shared causal variant across two traits was 1 × 10–5). The PP.H4 higher than 80% was considered as strong evidence of colocalization. All analyses were undertaken with R Software 3.6.1.

Results

Genetically predicted smoking behaviors and the risk of CHD and MI

Based on the established selection criteria for instrumental variables, a total of 131 single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) were utilized as instrumental variables for 4 kinds of smoking behaviors. These included 7 instrumental variables for age of smoking initiation, 23 instrumental variables for cigarettes per day, 8 instrumental variables for smoking cessation, and 93 instrumental variables for smoking initiation. The F statistics ranged from 29.8 to 953, with specific information on the selected instrumental variables provided in Table S2.

Our MR analysis indicated a statistically significant association between genetically predicted smoking initiation and an increased risk of CHD based on the IVW approach. (OR 1.236; 95% CI 1.126–1.357; p = 8.85E-6). This association was replicated using the weighted median method (Table S3). However, no significant relationships were observed between the age of smoking initiation (OR 0.79; 95% CI 0.225–2.758; p = 0.708), cigarettes per day (OR 1.058; 95% CI 0.98–1.142; p = 0.151), smoking cessation (OR 1.07; 95% CI 0.873–1.311; p = 0.517) and the risk of CHD (Fig. 2).

Forest plot of genetically predicted associations observed with smoking behaviors and coronary heart disease and myocardial infarction. CI, confidence interval; OR, odds ratio

Positive associations were observed between genetically predicted smoking initiation (OR 1.381; 95% CI 1.271–1.5; p = 2.77E-14) and smoking cessation (OR 1.195; 95% CI 1.039–1.375; p = 0.012) and an increased risk of MI. These associations were further supported through sensitivity analyses, including the weighted median method. However, genetically predicted age of smoking initiation (OR 0.617; 95% CI 0.349–1.089; p = 0.096) and cigarettes per day (OR 1.068; 95% CI 0.974–1.172; p = 0.162) did not show significant associations with MI (Fig. 2, Table S3).

Genetically predicted smoking-related DNA methylation at CpG sites on CHD and MI risk

In order to evaluate the causal relationship between DNA methylation at smoking-related CpG sites and the occurrence of CHD and MI, we utilized the identified mQTLs from the GoDMC database and performed two-sample Mendelian randomization and colocalization analysis. Following the selection of instrumental variables, a total of 1947 smoking-related CpG sites were included in the analysis of CHD; while, 2057 CpG sites were included for MI. Thus, we established significance thresholds of p < 2.57E-05 (0.05/1947) and p < 2.43E-05 (0.05/2057) for CHD and MI, respectively.

We observed that 11 smoking-related CpG sites were associated with CHD beyond a stringent Bonferroni threshold (Table 1 and Table S4). Genetically predicted methylation at 4 smoking-related CpG sites were significantly associated with higher risk of CHD, including cg16822035 (MCF2L) (OR 1.09; 95% CI 1.052–1.123), cg00395063 (ARHGEF12) (OR 1.26; 95% CI 1.145–1.379), cg07257768 (MDC1) (OR 1.84; 95% CI 1.411–2.395) and cg07723659 (SYMPK; RSPH6A) (OR 1.17; 95% CI 1.087–1.254). Whereas methylation at the rest of seven CpG sites, including cg02560388 (OR 0.65; 95% CI 0.587–0.726), cg07932199 (ATXN2) (OR 0.73; 95% CI 0.648–0.814), cg26099045 (OR 0.78; 95% CI 0.716–0.856), cg19744173 (FBLN7) (OR 0.91; 95% CI 0.873–0.947), cg23061027 (PRRT1) (OR 0.86; 95% CI 0.802–0.917), cg21201401 (LIME1) (OR 0.79; 95% CI 0.713–0.877), and cg18419271 (OR 0.85; 95% CI 0.793–0.918), were significantly associated with decreased risk of CHD. For the replication of these CpG sites convincing associated with CHD risk, we observed three CpG sites, including cg19744173 (FBLN7) (PP.H4 = 0.903), cg00395063 (ARHGEF12) (PP.H4 = 0.959) and cg16822035 (MCF2L) (PP.H4 = 0.971), exhibited strong evidence of colocalization (Table 1). Ten smoking-related CpG sites were found to be associated with MI after Bonferroni adjustment. Specifically, genetically predicted methylation at 6 CpG sites, including cg26405020 (FES) (OR 1.74; 95% CI 1.502–2.014), cg13668129 ( HNRNPUL1) (OR 1.17; 95% CI 1.104–1.241), cg08940075 (CNN3) (OR 2.07; 95% CI 1.573–2.734), cg07723659 (SYMPK; RSPH6A) (OR 1.21; 95% CI 1.126–1.31), cg22006825 (HNRNPUL1) (OR 1.18; 95% CI 1.093–1.265) and cg17551891 (MAD1L1) (OR 1.08; 95% CI 1.044–1.122), were significantly linked to a higher risk of MI. While genetically predicted methylation at 4 CpG sites, including cg02560388 (OR 0.62; 95% CI 0.555–0.695), cg07932199 (ATXN2) (OR 0.73; 95% CI 0.643–0.832), cg19529732 (DIABLO) (OR 0.75; 95% CI 0.657–0.845), and cg23061027 (PRRT1) (OR 0.85; 95% CI 0.782–0.913), were significantly associated with a decreased risk of MI. Furthermore, our analysis revealed strong evidence of colocalization for three CpG sites, including cg19529732 (DIABLO) (PP.H4 = 0.976), cg26405020 (FES) (PP.H4 = 0.985), and cg08940075 (CNN3) (PP.H4 = 1) (Table 1).

Replication results

To enhance the credibility of our findings, we incorporated additional independent datasets for further examination.

In the FinnGen cohort, we identified notable correlations between cg07932199 (ATXN2) (OR 0.66; 95% CI 0.536–0.807), cg18419271 (OR 0.74; 95% CI 0.619–0.884), and cg02560388 (OR 0.73; 95% CI 0.602–0.892) and decreased CHD risk (Fig. 3). In order to explore the potential impact of these smoking-related CpG sites on the Asian population, a comprehensive GWAS dataset from BBJ was utilized for MR analysis. The findings indicated a significant association between genetically predicted cg00395063 (ARHGEF12) (OR 1.25; 95% CI 1.084–1.433) and cg16822035 (MCF2L) (OR 1.05; 95% CI 1.005–1.101) and an elevated risk of CHD; while, cg02560388 (OR 0.66; 95% CI 0.566–0.762) and cg23061027 (PRRT1) (OR 0.83; 95% CI 0.71–0.964) were linked to a decreased risk of CHD. Subsequently, we conducted a meta-analysis to consolidate the outcomes of three cohorts, revealing that 7 of 11 smoking-related CpG sites were significantly correlated with CHD.

On the other hand, cg07723659 (SYMPK; RSPH6A) (OR 1.22; 95% CI 1.046–1.428) exhibited a notable genetic predisposition linked to heightened susceptibility to MI; whereas, cg02560388 (OR 0.62; 95% CI 0.49–0.789), cg07932199 (ATXN2) (OR 0.61; 95% CI 0.478–0.783), and cg19529732 (DIABLO) (OR 0.65; 95% CI 0.508–0.84) were linked to a decreased risk of MI in the FinnGen cohort. Discussion

In our study, we investigated the association of smoking behaviors with CHD and MI at the genomic and epigenomic levels by MR analysis and further validated the results by genetic colocalization analysis. In the genomic analysis, we provided convincing evidence supporting the pathogenic effect of smoking initiation on CHD and MI. In the epigenetic analysis, we observed that 11 smoking-related CpG sites were linked to CHD risk based on the UK Biobank dataset; while, 10 smoking-related CpG sites were associated with the risk of MI. Subsequently, some of these CpG sites were further replicated using the FinnGen or BBJ datasets. Finally, we conducted a meta-analysis to combine the results from different data sources (3 for CHD, and 2 for MI), and we found 7 of the 11 CpG sites were correlated with the risk of CHD; while, 7 of the 10 CpG sites were associated with the risk of MI. In addition, we performed genetic colocalization analysis and found that cg19744173 (FBLN7), cg00395063 (ARHGEF12), and cg16822035 (MCF2L) showed strong colocalization evidence with CHD; while, cg19529732 (DIABLO), cg26405020 (FES), and cg08940075 (CNN3) showed strong colocalization evidence with the risk of MI.

MR analysis at the genome level provided convincing evidence that smoking initiation contributes to CHD and MI risk. Numerous observational studies have demonstrated that smoking is a major, independent risk factor for CHD and MI. For example, studies have shown that smokers were 1.6 times more likely to develop CHD and 2.95 times more likely to suffer a nonfatal acute myocardial infarction than non-smokers [17, 18], which was consistent with our study. The evidence supporting the association between cigarettes per day and age of smoking initiation and the risk of CHD and MI may be weak due to limited statistical power. In our study, we found no significant correlation between smoking cessation and the risk of CHD. However, it is noteworthy that smoking cessation was associated with an increased risk of MI. Considering our results of smoking initiation indicated an overall association between smoking and an increased risk of MI, which was supported by subsequent epigenetic MR analysis, this finding did not affect our overall conclusions. Previous studies have also found similar contradictory findings. For instance, a study about exploring the cross cancer effect of smoking and its fingerprints in blood DNA methylation on multiple cancers revealed that smoking cessation was significantly associated with an increased risk of colorectal cancer [40]. The study by Amiya A. Ahmed et al. (2015) indicated that among the 4154 participants without baseline acute myocardial infarction history, Former heavy smokers (≥ 32 pack-years; after > 15 years of abstinence) remained an elevated risk relative to current smokers (aHR, 1.35; 95% CI, 0.92–1.97) [41]. This may be attributed to several factors. First, People might quit smoking because they have early symptoms of disease (for example, cardiovascular issues or respiratory problems) or have been diagnosed with a condition (for example, early stages of cancer or chronic obstructive pulmonary disease). Therefore, the association between quitting smoking and increased disease risk could reflect the fact that these people are already at higher risk due to pre-existing conditions, not necessarily because quitting causes harm. In this case, quitting smoking appears correlated with the disease, but it’s the underlying health issues that lead to both the cessation and the increased risk. Second, the effectiveness of smoking cessation is affected by since cessation, consumed doses, duration of smoking phases and so forth. And smoking cessation is often a delayed decision made after years of exposure to smoking-related harm. By the time people quit, they may have already accumulated significant biological damage from smoking. This damage might take years or even decades to repair, or may never fully heal. In addition, smoking leads to chronic inflammation [42], and it can take time for the body to recover from this after cessation. During this period of post-cessation inflammation, the risk of certain diseases (like cardiovascular diseases) may remain elevated. Collectively, the timeline for cardiovascular diseases risk reduction following smoking cessation might be influenced by cumulative exposure to smoking [43]. The uncertainty surrounding the time course of cardiovascular diseases risk reduction post-smoking cessation may lead to an underestimation of cardiovascular diseases risk among former smokers [43]. Evidence from the British Regional Heart Study 12 indicated that the risk of myocardial infarction can persist for over 20 years after smoking cessation in men [44]. In an observational cohort study involving 8,770 participants, the risk of cardiovascular disease in former heavy smokers remained significantly elevated for at least 5–10 years, and potentially up to 25 years after cessation when compared to never smokers [43]. Third, due to the phenomenon of recidivism among former smokers, cardiovascular events that occur in individuals who have resumed smoking were inaccurately classified as events among former smokers rather than current smokers [45]. This misclassification consequently led to an overestimation of the risk associated with former smokers. However, more evidence is needed to explain this question.

Epigenetic analysis revealed that 11 smoking-related CpG sites were linked to CHD in the UK Biobank database. Among these, 3 CpG sites including cg02560388, cg07932199 (ATXN2) and cg18419271 were found to be replicated in the FinnGen database; while, 4 CpG sites including cg02560388, cg16822035 (MCF2L), cg00395063 (ARHGEF12) and cg23061027 (PRRT1) were replicated in the BBJ database. Furthermore, 7 CpG sites including cg02560388, cg07932199 (ATXN2), cg16822035 (MCF2L), cg00395063 (ARHGEF12), cg23061027 (PRRT1), cg07723659 (SYMPK; RSPH6A) and cg18419271 remained associated with CHD following a meta-analysis of the results from all three databases. Additionally, 3 CpG sites including cg19744173 (FBLN7), cg00395063 (ARHGEF12), and cg16822035 (MCF2L) were further confirmed by genetic colocalization. Ten smoking-related CpG sites have been associated with MI. Among these, 4 CpG sites including cg02560388, cg07723659 (SYMPK; RSPH6A), cg07932199 (ATXN2), and cg19529732 (DIABLO) were found to be replicated in the FinnGen database. Following a meta-analysis of the results from both databases (data in the BBJ were absent), cg02560388 remained significantly associated with MI. Furthermore, 3 CpG sites including cg19529732 (DIABLO), cg26405020 (FES), and cg08940075 (CNN3) were validated through genetic colocalization analysis.

Our studies identified cg00395063 (ARHGEF12) and cg16822035(MCF2L) to be significantly correlated with an elevated risk of CHD. ARHGEF12 gene encodes a Rho guanine nucleotide exchange factor 12 (RhoGEF12), which has been implicated in hypertension [46, 47], a well-established risk factor for CHD. Additionally, a genome-wide gene association analysis in silico has identified a potential link between the ARHGEF12 gene and coronary artery disease, with proposed mechanisms underlying this association based on in silico functional analysis [48]. MCF2L, a member of the Dbl family, functions as a guanine exchange factor that may serve as a link between signaling pathways through Rac1 and RhoA. A rare variant in MCF2L was identified through exclusion linkage analysis in a pedigree exhibiting premature atherosclerosis [49]. Moreover, MCF2L has been observed in human coronary artery segments from patients with varying degrees of atherosclerosis, but not in non-diseased arteries [49], providing additional evidence for its potential involvement in CHD. The findings from our methylation MR and genetic colocalization analysis offered a new perspective on the involvement of genes ARHGEF12 and MCF2L in the pathogenesis of CHD through DNA methylation.

Our studies indicated that cg19744173 (FBLN7) was linked to a decreased risk of CHD. The Fibulin family is a class of extracellular matrix proteins, and some members of this family are involved in the regulation of cardiovascular diseases such as ischemia–reperfusion, myocardial hypertrophy, and vascular remodeling. FBLN7 encodes protein Fibulin-7 and is a member of the Fibulin family. It was found that Fibulin 7 protein and mRNA levels were significantly increased in the mice with MI [50]. Moreover, in the same study, Fibulin 7 deletion attenuated post‐MI cardiac remodeling, leading to improved cardiac function and reduced myocardial fibrosis; while, overexpression of Fibulin 7 resulted in the opposite effects [50]. Our study introduced a novel epigenetic viewpoint, proposing that Fibulin7 could decrease the risk of MI through DNA methylation modification.

In our research, we observed a significant association between cg26405020 (FES) and cg08940075 (CNN3) and increased MI risk. FES is a tyrosine kinase involved in different cellular processes such as migration and differentiation. Based on targeted methylation profiling of coronary artery disease loci in whole blood, FES was found to harbor CpGs identified in current smokers in the Rotterdam Study [51]. Irina Zorkoltseva et al. (2022) provided evidence that FES was associated with coronary artery disease using an SMR/HEIDI analysis [48]. Additionally, by jointly modeling eQTL and GWAS datasets, Boxiang Liu et al. (2018) reported that FES may modulate the risk of coronary artery disease through human coronary artery smooth muscle cells [52]. Furthermore, Rachel R Huxley et al. (2022) found that reducing the expression of FES in monocytes through experimental methods could enhance monocyte migration. In the same study, knockout of FES resulted in larger atherosclerotic plaque sizes and an increased abundance of monocytes/macrophages within the plaques in a mouse model [53]. Our findings supportively show that smoking may contribute to CHD by altering the methylation modifications of FES. Evidence that could link CNN3 to CHD or MI is scanty, but our study offered robust evidence for its pathogenic effects on MI by MR and colocalization analysis.

Our study showed that cg19529732 (DIABLO) was associated with a decreased risk of MI. DIABLO, also recognized as SMAC, is a mitochondrial protein that functions as a suppressor of XIAP and promotes apoptosis by counteracting XIAP [54]. It has been reported that DIABLO protein expression was elevated in an ischemia–reperfusion rat model [55], corroborating our findings and suggesting a potential involvement of DIABLO in MI.

Additionally, cg13668129 and cg22006825 are both located in the HNRNPUL1 gene, and were linked to increased MI risk in our study. HNRNPUL1 is known to be involved in various cellular processes, such as RNA transport, processing, and transcriptional regulation. Research conducted by Dov Shiffman et al. (2006) revealed that an HNRPUL1 variant was associated with early-onset MI in 3 consecutive case–control studies [56]. Jeroen B. van der Net et al. (2008) confirmed the relationship between the rs11881940 polymorphism in HNRPUL1 gene and CHD [57]. These findings collectively indicate the significant role of HNRNPUL1 in the development of CHD and MI.

Furthermore, our study found associations between cg07932199 (ATXN2), cg23061027 (PRRT1), and cg07723659 (SYMPK; RSPH6A) with both CHD and MI risk. Despite limited existing evidence on associations between these three genes and CHD and MI, our research offered additional support from an epigenetic perspective of methylation. Further investigation into related pathways is warranted for a more comprehensive understanding of these relationships.

Our study has several advantages. First, a two-sample MR analysis was performed to evaluate the associations between smoking behaviors, blood DNA methylation, and the susceptibility to CHD and MI, thereby mitigating the potential for reverse causation and minimizing confounding effects. Subsequently, we examined the relationship between genetically predicted smoking-related CpG sites and CHD and MI in different independent data sources, enhancing statistical robustness and reinforcing the validity of our results through consistent estimates across diverse study populations. In addition, we performed a meta-analysis to combine the results from different data sources to reduce population structure bias. Finally, we applied genetic colocalization to further validate the role of some important CpG sites in CHD and MI. There were also some limitations to our study. Data on methylation were sourced from peripheral blood samples, potentially yielding distinct methylation profiles compared to tissue-specific samples [58], and lacked longitudinal information. Future investigations utilizing myocardial tissue-derived data are recommended. Additionally, compared with CpG sites, CpG islands are regions of DNA with a higher frequency of CpG dinucleotides, often located near gene promoters, and they play a more crucial role in gene regulation, particularly in the context of DNA methylation [59, 60]. Thus, analyzing CpG islands, as opposed to individual CpG sites, may offer broader insights into the epigenetic regulation of genes, as changes in methylation patterns across entire CpG islands can have significant effects on gene expression. In our study, we could get access DNA methylation data for CpG sites associated with smoking, but could not get access DNA methylation data for smoking-associated CpG islands. So, we might not repeat our analysis on CpG islands in this study.

Conclusion

The results of our research demonstrated a genetic correlation between smoking initiation and the risk of CHD and MI. Additionally, our findings suggest that DNA methylation at CpG sites may serve as a potential mechanism underlying this association. Specifically, DNA methylation at certain CpG sites, including cg19744173 (FBLN7), cg00395063 (ARHGEF12), and cg16822035 (MCF2L), was linked to CHD; while, methylation at cg19529732 (DIABLO), cg26405020 (FES), and cg08940075 (CNN3) was associated with MI.
