Post-polypectomy surveillance documentation and guideline concordance: A retrospective clinical audit of physician documentation at a tertiary Saudi hospital
Authors: Muhammad F. Mubarak, Mohammed A. Alabduljabbar, Mohammed S. Almousa, Ahmad Y.H. Ghesheyan, Ibraheem M. Alkanhal, Sultan K. AlSubaie, Ammar S. Alotaibi, Resheed Alkhiari, Suliman Alshankiti
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPost-polypectomy surveillance is essential for colorectal cancer prevention, but adherence to guidelines varies and data from KSA are limited. This study evaluated surveillance documentation and guideline concordance at King Saud University Medical City.MethodsA retrospective audit included patients aged 18–75 years who underwent screening colonoscopy with polypectomy. Documented surveillance intervals were compared with USMSTF 2020 guidelines, and absence of a documented plan was considered a primary quality metric.ResultsAmong 207 patients, 74.9% had no documented surveillance plan. Among the 52 patients (25.1%) with a plan, only 29 (14.0% overall) were guideline-concordant, whereas 20 (9.7%) were over-surveyed and 3 (1.4%) under-surveyed. The majority of plans (45/52) were documented by gastroenterologists and the remaining seven by primary care physicians.ConclusionSurveillance documentation is markedly limited, with suboptimal adherence, highlighting the need for structured interventions to improve guideline-based practice.

Objectives •To evaluate the rate at which primary care physicians (PCPs) and gastroenterologists document post-polypectomy surveillance plans following screening colonoscopy, and to assess whether documented plans are concordant with the U.S. Multi-Society Task Force (USMSTF) 2020 post-polypectomy surveillance guidelines.•To compare the documentation and provision of surveillance plan recommendations between PCPs and gastroenterologists.•To assess the associations between physician adherence to USMSTF 2020 surveillance recommendations and relevant clinical and demographic factors.

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) ranks as the third most common cancer globally, accounting for approximately 10% of all diagnosed cancer cases.1 In KSA, CRC is the leading cancer in males and third most common in females (9.7%).2 In many cases, CRC begins as colon polyps, which are abnormal growths that develop on the inner surface of the colon.3 Colon polyps belong to two categories: benign non-neoplastic polyps and neoplastic polyps with a higher malignancy risk.4 Most CRCs originate from adenomatous polyps. The progression from benign adenomatous polyps to invasive cancer typically occurs over several years, providing a crucial window for intervention.3 This significant window for intervention highlights the importance of colonoscopy. Adenoma removal during colonoscopy reduces the incidence of CRC by 76–90% compared with that when polyps are left untreated, and mortality by 53% compared with the general population.5,6 Regular surveillance is necessary to identify and manage polyps before they progress into invasive cancer, improving patient outcomes and reducing the impact of CRC. Thus, in the present study, we focused on primary care physicians (PCPs) and gastroenterologists because they determine these critical surveillance intervals. Previous studies have shown that there are variations in physician adherence rates to post-polypectomy surveillance guidelines. However, previous studies have not fully evaluated physician adherence to these guidelines in KSA, highlighting a significant gap in the regional literature. In particular, Naftali et al. demonstrated that before an intervention, only 65% of the surveillance recommendations following polypectomy by consultant gastroenterologists were consistent with international consensus guidelines.7 After implementing measures such as reaffirmation of guidelines and standardized reporting tools, the adherence rate improved to 78%. Moreover, Clarke et al. reported a high rate of adherence (>85%) to guidelines among academic gastroenterologists.8 Conversely, studies involving PCPs demonstrated a tendency toward over-surveillance. Boolchand et al. found that 61% of PCPs recommended premature surveillance for hyperplastic polyps and 71% for single tubular adenomas. It should be noted that the participants in their study consisted of 48% internists and 52% family practitioners, reflecting a balanced representation of primary care specialties.9 Similarly, Olds et al. noted that only 11% of PCPs adhered to the surveillance interval guideline for patients with two 6 mm tubular adenomas.10 This tendency toward over-surveillance was further corroborated by Mysliwiec et al., who showed that surveillance colonoscopies were frequently performed outside the guideline recommendations, particularly for hyperplastic polyps and low-risk lesions such as small adenomas.11 Furthermore, Patell et al. found significant deficiencies in terms of knowledge of surveillance guidelines among various medical specialties. In particular, 50% of physicians felt confident in their knowledge, but only 8% accurately applied the guidelines to clinical vignettes.12 This disparity in adherence to guidelines between gastroenterologists and PCPs, where PCPs exhibit a tendency toward over-surveillance, unnecessarily strains colonoscopy resources. Thus, in the present study conducted at King Saud University Medical City (KSUMC), we evaluated surveillance documentation and guideline concordance among PCPs and gastroenterologists. The findings obtained in this study may help to inform future quality improvement efforts and promote the more appropriate use of colonoscopy resources.

Materials and Methods

Study design and setting

This study was based on a retrospective clinical audit conducted at KSUMC in Riyadh, KSA, between December 2024 and December 2025, where PCPs/family medicine practitioners and gastroenterologists are responsible for determining post-polypectomy surveillance recommendations. The target population consisted of patients aged 18–75 years who underwent screening colonoscopy and polypectomy at KSUMC. We initially screened records for 506 colonoscopies performed between 2023 and 2024, where 299 records (59.1%) were excluded because patients had inadequate or poor bowel preparation, incomplete colonoscopy or pathology documentation, or conditions requiring specialized surveillance protocols such as inflammatory bowel disease (Figure 1), leaving 207 patients in the final analytic cohort. Although 59.1% of the records were excluded, the final cohort of 207 patients exceeded the minimum required sample size of 196. Exclusions were based on predefined criteria to limit baseline confounding and improve internal validity.Figure 1CONSORT flow diagram with excluded patients and final patients included and appropriateness of assigned documented surveillance colonoscopy post colorectal polypectomy.

Sample size

The minimum required sample size was estimated using the single-proportion formula, assuming a guideline-concordant documentation rate of 50% based on a systematic review and meta-analysis that pooled 16 real-world studies of post-polypectomy surveillance interval adherence, with a desired absolute precision of ±7% at a 95% confidence level. This yielded a requirement of approximately 196 records. All 207 colonoscopy records that met the inclusion and exclusion criteria during the study period were included, so the final cohort exceeded the minimum amounts and effectively represented a census of the accessible population at our institution.13

Data collection and variables

Demographic information, colonoscopy findings, polyp characteristics (type, size, number, and histology), documented family history of CRC, and the surveillance interval recommended by the responsible physician were extracted for each patient. Data were extracted by five trained investigators using a standardized electronic data abstraction form with predefined variable definitions and coding rules (Appendix A). Before data collection, all data extractors attended a structured orientation and calibration session led by the board-certified gastroenterologist principal investigator, where the abstraction form and coding rules were reviewed and applied to example cases to ensure consistent use across the team. To assess the consistency of extraction, the board-certified gastroenterologist principal investigator independently reviewed a random sample of 22 records from the analytic cohort, which showed that 21 of 22 records (95.5%) were initially concordant, and the single discrepant case was discussed in the presence of all data collectors and the principal investigator. Following deliberation, the difference in classification was considered minor and unlikely to affect the overall findings, and the original extraction was retained. The responsibility for surveillance was attributed to the physician who authored the colonoscopy report or the physician who recorded the surveillance recommendation if a separate clinic follow-up note existed.

Surveillance classification and primary quality metric

Documented surveillance intervals were extracted from the colonoscopy report or associated clinic follow-up notes and assessed against the USMSTF 2020 post-polypectomy surveillance guidelines to determine adherence. The absence of a documented surveillance plan in these sources was classified as “no surveillance plan documented” and treated as a primary quality metric because the electronic medical record at KSUMC is the standard medium for communicating surveillance decisions between providers and for retrieving prior plans at subsequent visits. In this context, failure to record a surveillance interval in the chart represented a documentation gap with potential implications for continuity of care and given the retrospective design, this measure reflected documentation failure rather than definitive evidence that a clinical surveillance decision was not made.

Ethical considerations and data security

This study involved secondary data, so patients were not directly recruited and all eligible cases were identified through the hospital's electronic medical record system. All data were anonymized and de-identified prior to analysis, securely stored, and accessed only by authorized personnel. Ethical approval was obtained from the Institutional Review Board at KSUMC, and all confidentiality and regulatory standards were strictly followed.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using JASP software (JASP Team Version 0.19.0, 2024). Categorical variables were expressed as frequencies and percentages. Continuous variables were expressed as means and standard deviations. Chi-square and Fisher's exact tests were used for categorical variables. A p-value less than 0.05 was considered to indicate a statistically significant difference.

Results

Analysis of the baseline characteristics of the cohort of 207 patients showed that they had a mean age (± standard deviation) of 59.9 years (±9.0 years), and the gender distribution was 60.4% male and 39.6% female. The polyp burden was characterized in extreme detail across type, number, size, and histology. The most common polyp type identified was 1–2 adenomas smaller than 10 mm, which was found in nearly half of the patients (48.3%). This was followed by adenomas with advanced histology, including tubulovillous adenoma, villous adenoma, or high-grade dysplasia, which were present in 30 patients. Rarer findings included patients with 5–10 adenomas (3.9%), sessile serrated polyps of any kind (2.9%), and traditional serrated adenomas (0.5%). In terms of counts, the majority of patients had either a single polyp (49.8%) or two polyps (27.5%). Moreover, higher polyp counts included 3–4 polyps found in 33 patients, 5–10 polyps in 11 patients, and more than 10 polyps in only three patients. In terms of size, most polyps were diminutive, measuring less than 5 mm (52.2%), followed by small polyps of 5–9 mm (34.3%). Larger polyps of 10–19 mm were found in 20 patients, and large polyps measuring ≥20 mm were found in only eight patients. Physician adherence to post-colonoscopy surveillance guidelines and plan provision.

The data were used to comprehensively analyze the adherence to established guidelines for post-polypectomy surveillance. The majority of patients (74.9%, 155/207) had no surveillance plan documented in the colonoscopy report or follow-up clinic notes following screening colonoscopy with polypectomy. Among the minority who had a plan (52 patients, 25.1%), the actual intervals prescribed by physicians deviated from the intervals recommended by clinical guidelines. According to the guidelines, the most frequently recommended surveillance intervals are 7–10 years (46.4%), followed by 10 years (16.4%) and 3 years (22.7%). However, the most common intervals provided were 3 years (9.1%), 5 years (4.3%), and 1 year (3.3%), with a variety of other intervals. Overall, a documented surveillance plan appropriate to the guidelines was found for only 29 patients. Furthermore, 20 patients were subjected to “over-surveillance” and a small group of three patients received “under-surveillance.” Gastroenterologists provided surveillance plans for 45 patients whereas PCPs only provided plans for seven patients.

Analysis using the chi-square test detected a statistically significant association between guideline-recommended and physician-prescribed surveillance intervals (χ2(35) = 51.4, p = 0.036). When the guidelines recommended a 3-year interval, the most common actual interval was also 3 years (n = 10). When the recommendation was 3–5 years, the actual intervals were most often 3 years (n = 3) or 3–5 years (n = 4). However, when the recommended interval was 7–10 years, the most frequent actual interval selected was 3 years (n = 6), followed by 7 years (n = 4). Moreover, for the 52 cases provided with a follow-up plan, we examined the association between physician adherence to guidelines in screening and multiple other factors. Gender and adherence to guidelines were not statistically significant (p = 0.57), or first degree family history of CRC (p = 0.72). Discussion

In this study, we assessed the documentation of post-polypectomy surveillance plans and the guideline concordance of these plans among PCPs and gastroenterologists at KSUMC using the USMSTF 2020 recommendations as the reference standard. The study design was a retrospective audit of medical records and it allowed evaluation of formal documentation but not direct assessment of clinical reasoning or pathology interpretation when no plan was recorded. The following important observations were made: for the majority of patients, no post-polypectomy surveillance plan was documented in the colonoscopy report or clinic notes, and when a plan was documented, guideline-concordant intervals were achieved for only about half of cases. Adherence to recommended intervals did not have clear associations with clinical or demographic variables. Overall, these findings indicate that there were two main challenges in our setting: insufficient documentation of surveillance recommendations, and inconsistent application of guideline-based intervals among the minority of patients for whom a plan was recorded.

In our cohort, 74.9% of patients had no documented post-polypectomy surveillance plans. Among all 207 patients, only 14.0% ultimately received an appropriate interval according to USMSTF 2020, whereas 9.7% were over-surveyed and 1.4% were under-surveyed (Figure 3). Naftali et al. reported that 76% of patients received a clearly documented surveillance recommendation pre-intervention, where 65% of the documented plans were concordant with the guidelines. After implementing standardized reporting and guideline reinforcement, the documentation rate improved to 86% and concordance to 78%.7 Our documentation rate of 25.1% is markedly lower, but adherence among documented plans (55.8%) is comparable to the pre-intervention figure reported by Naftali et al. and to pooled international estimates (≈50%).7,13 Similarly, Clarke et al. reported adherence rates exceeding 85% among academic gastroenterologists.8 These comparisons demonstrate that the primary challenge in our setting is ensuring guideline-concordant intervals when plans are documented as well as significantly improving the rate at which surveillance plans are documented in the medical records.Figure 3Accuracy of surveillance timing if provided or not in corcodance with USMSTF 2020.

The pattern of deviation from the guideline recommendations observed in our cohort reflects a similar tendency toward over-surveillance that has been described in previous PCP-focused studies, although this pattern was less pronounced in our cohort. Boolchand et al. reported that PCPs frequently recommended intervals that were shorter than the guidelines, particularly in cases of hyperplastic polyps and single tubular adenomas, with 61% and 71% of respondents choosing intervals shorter than the guideline suggestions, respectively.9 Mysliwiec et al. further demonstrated that surveillance of colonoscopies was often performed in excess of the guideline recommendations, especially for hyperplastic polyps and other low-risk lesions.11 However, the magnitude of over-surveillance observed in our cohort (38.5%) was lower than those reported in several PCP-focused studies, and this difference may reflect variations in practice settings because the surveillance recommendations in our institution were generated within a mixed care environment that included both PCPs and gastroenterologists. Therefore, differences in clinical workflows, guideline familiarity, or institutional practices may have contributed to the relatively lower rate of over-surveillance observed in our cohort.

Most of the documented surveillance plans were provided by gastroenterologists, who accounted for 45 of the 52 plans (21.7% of all patients), whereas PCPs documented plans in only seven cases (3.4%). It is important to note that this difference in documentation rates did not allow a formal statistical comparison of guideline concordance between the two specialties, with only seven PCP-documented plans, as any comparison would have been severely underpowered and inference from these data would not have been effective. This finding reflects a difference in who documented the plans and not necessarily in who discussed them with patients, and should be interpreted with caution.

Our findings have notable implications for service delivery at KSUMC. Over-surveillance exposes patients to unnecessary procedural risks and anxiety, and contributes to avoidable strain on already limited colonoscopy resources, especially in a healthcare system facing an increasing burden of CRC.1,2 By contrast, the high proportion of patients without a documented surveillance plan highlights a gap in the formal recording of follow-up recommendations, which may limit continuity of care and the ability to track adherence to guidelines over time. Missing the opportunity to provide accurate and consensus and evidence-based surveillance plans to patients undergoing polypectomy during colonoscopy for CRC screening compromises the ability to avoid CRC development in under-surveyed or non-surveyed patients.14 Addressing these issues will require interventions that extend beyond individual clinician efforts. Naftali et al. indicated that reinforcing guidelines and embedding structured reporting tools can significantly improve adherence.7 In our setting, similar strategies, such as integrating automated surveillance calculators into the electronic medical record, standardizing colonoscopy and pathology report templates to include a mandatory surveillance recommendation field, and providing focused education sessions targeted at both PCPs and gastroenterologists, may help convert the current variability into consistent, guideline-based practice.

Overall, these findings highlight a substantial gap in the documentation of post-polypectomy surveillance plans at KSUMC, with suboptimal guideline adherence among documented cases. Therefore, improving documentation practices and reinforcing guideline-based surveillance recommendations should be key targets for quality improvement.

Limitations

This study had several important limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, a key limitation of this retrospective study is that the observed 74.9% rate of undocumented surveillance plans reflects documentation failure rather than clinical decision-making failure. Plans may have been formulated but not recorded in the electronic medical records, communicated verbally to patients, or documented in external systems that were not accessible for this review. Electronic medical record documentation gaps directly threaten care continuity by risking loss to follow-up, but our findings should not be overinterpreted as evidence that clinicians failed to make appropriate surveillance decisions in these cases.

Second, only seven surveillance plans were documented by PCPs compared with 45 by gastroenterologists. This profound imbalance prevented any formal statistical comparison of guideline concordance between the two specialties as it would have been statistically invalid. Thus, conclusions about the performance of PCPs versus gastroenterologist in applying guideline intervals could not be drawn from these data.

Third, the study did not assess physician-level variables, such as years of experience, subspecialty training, clinical workload, or familiarity with the USMSTF 2020 guidelines, which may have contributed to variations in surveillance recommendations. Understanding these factors could provide deeper insights into why deviations from the guidelines occurred.

Finally, the study was conducted in a single tertiary care center, which may limit the generalizability of our findings to other institutions with different workflows, levels of guideline integration, or patterns of shared care between PCPs and gastroenterologists.

Conclusion

This study showed that most patients at KSUMC did not receive a documented post-polypectomy surveillance plan, and concordance with USMSTF 2020 guidelines was inconsistent among those who did. The study design based on a retrospective audit of medical records assessed the documentation rates and accuracy of documented intervals, but it did not permit conclusions to be reached about clinical reasoning in undocumented cases. Guideline concordance did not vary significantly across patient or polyp characteristics among those who received a plan. The near-absence of PCP-documented plans (n = 7) prevented any specialty-level comparison of concordance, highlighting a structural gap in post-colonoscopy documentation rather than a measurable difference in guideline knowledge. These findings demonstrate the need to improve both the rate of surveillance plan documentation and the accuracy of interval selection when plans are provided. Implementing structured reporting tools, reinforcing guideline use, and integrating decision-support systems may help standardize practices and enhance the quality of CRC prevention in our institution.
