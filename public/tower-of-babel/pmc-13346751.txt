Federated multimodal learning for privacy-preserving blood donor profiling and personalized recall strategy optimization
Authors: Li Li, Jiaojiao Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBlood supply shortages remain a persistent challenge for transfusion services, yet effective donor recall is hampered by fragmented data across institutions, limited profiling depth, and increasing privacy regulations. This paper proposes a federated multimodal learning framework that enables distributed blood centers to collaboratively train a shared donor profiling model without exchanging raw records. The framework integrates three modality-specific encoders — for demographic attributes, behavioral donation sequences, and textual feedback — unified through a cross-modal attention mechanism with adaptive gating. Differential privacy is embedded into the federated training pipeline via local gradient clipping and calibrated Gaussian noise injection, with cumulative privacy expenditure tracked through a moments accountant. Building on the fused donor embeddings, a multi-task recall optimization model jointly predicts optimal contact timing and communication channel, while a hierarchical clustering scheme translates predictions into tiered intervention protocols. Experiments on 127,463 donor records partitioned across six simulated blood centers demonstrate that the proposed method achieves a profiling F1 score of 83.7% and a recall conversion rate of 31.2%, approaching centralized performance within approximately 2% points. Ablation analysis confirms that the behavioral sequence modality contributes the strongest discriminative signal, while cross-modal attention yields a 4.3-point F1 improvement over naive concatenation. Introduction

Maintaining an adequate and stable blood supply is foundational to modern healthcare, yet the gap between clinical demand and actual availability has proven stubbornly difficult to close. Transfusion services across the globe face this tension in varying degrees, and the situation in China is particularly instructive. Seasonal troughs—most pronounced during winter and summer—tend to overlap with peak surgical caseloads, generating supply-demand mismatches that directly endanger patient outcomes1. The problem, however, runs deeper than seasonal cycles. Collection infrastructure clusters disproportionately in urban cores, leaving rural and semi-urban communities with chronic shortfalls, and the donor base tilts heavily toward younger age groups whose long-term retention has been discouraging2. Faced with these compounding pressures, blood service authorities have begun moving away from passive, broadcast-style recruitment and toward data-driven recall strategies that specifically target lapsed or eligible repeat donors.

Conventional recall approaches, unfortunately, carry well-known weaknesses. Most blood centers function as informationally isolated units; each maintains its own donor database, and jurisdictional or regulatory barriers make merging or cross-referencing these records impractical3. This fragmentation—what practitioners commonly call the “data silo” problem—sharply curtails any institution’s ability to assemble comprehensive donor profiles that capture behavioral patterns across organizational boundaries. Privacy concerns compound the difficulty: pooling sensitive health and behavioral data in a single repository opens donors to non-trivial re-identification risks, a vulnerability that standard anonymization methods only partially address4. On top of these structural barriers, most profiling pipelines draw on a narrow slice of available information—mainly demographic fields and donation-history counts—while ignoring richer signals buried in communication logs, survey responses, and spatiotemporal movement records5. Profiles built on such a limited feature set inevitably produce recall campaigns that lack the precision needed for genuine personalization.

Several recent developments, viewed collectively, suggest that a more capable paradigm is within reach. Federated learning—conceived originally to let multiple parties train a joint model without sharing raw data—has matured quickly and now sees active deployment in healthcare settings as diverse as electronic health record analytics and medical image segmentation6. When differential privacy is layered on top of a federated architecture, the combination provides formal mathematical guarantees against inference attacks on individual contributions, which goes a long way toward building trust among institutional participants7. Concurrently, multimodal data fusion has delivered notable performance gains in recommendation systems and user modeling by jointly mining textual, categorical, and sequential data streams8. Within blood transfusion research specifically, machine learning has been applied to forecast donation intervals, flag high-risk lapse cohorts, and gauge individual willingness to donate under different contextual conditions9. Personalized recommendation engines rooted in collaborative filtering and deep sequential architectures have likewise been repurposed for health behavior nudging, with early results that look promising10.

Despite this momentum, a conspicuous gap remains at the intersection of privacy-preserving collaboration and multidimensional donor characterization. The majority of federated learning studies in healthcare have concentrated on single-modality clinical data, leaving the heterogeneous and institution-specific feature spaces typical of blood donation ecosystems largely unaddressed11. Multimodal donor profiling efforts, on the other hand, generally presuppose centralized data access and therefore do not translate straightforwardly into federated environments where communication budgets and privacy constraints introduce additional architectural demands12. The handful of studies that have tried to bridge these two streams have mostly stopped at the conceptual level, offering neither rigorous algorithmic formulations nor empirical evidence grounded in realistic blood service datasets13. A clear need exists, then, for research that simultaneously tackles cross-institutional privacy collaboration and expressive, multi-source donor representation within one coherent analytical framework.

The present study addresses this need by putting forward a federated multimodal learning architecture designed expressly for privacy-preserving blood donor profiling and recall optimization. At its core, our work rests on the premise that truly effective recall requires donor portraits capturing not just static demographics but also dynamic behavioral trajectories, psychological engagement cues, and contextual environmental factors—and that building such portraits should never come at the cost of individual privacy. On the theoretical side, we extend federated optimization theory to handle heterogeneous modality alignment across decentralized nodes, yielding fresh insights into convergence behavior when gradient noise varies by modality. On the practical side, the framework offers blood service organizations a concrete path toward collaborative intelligence: participating institutions can tap into a richer shared model while retaining full custody over their local data assets.

The remainder of this paper is organized around four interconnected research components. First, we design a federated multimodal fusion framework that enables distributed blood centers to collaboratively train a shared donor representation model without transmitting raw records. Second, we integrate a differential privacy enhancement mechanism into the federated training pipeline, calibrating noise injection to balance privacy guarantees against model utility in a principled manner. Third, we develop a multi-granularity donor profiling method that fuses demographic, behavioral, and engagement modalities into a unified embedding space, capturing both long-term preference structures and short-term state transitions. Fourth, we formulate a personalized recall strategy optimization model that maps donor profiles to tailored intervention actions — including channel selection, message framing, and timing — via a constrained policy learning approach14. The principal contributions of this work lie in three areas: the introduction of a modality-aware federated aggregation scheme that respects cross-institutional feature heterogeneity, the co-design of differential privacy budgets with multimodal representation quality, and the end-to-end integration of privacy-preserving profiling with recall policy optimization in a unified system. Together, these contributions advance the state of the art in both federated health analytics and blood service management, offering a replicable template for privacy-conscious, data-rich donor engagement.

Federated learning and privacy protection mechanisms

At its core, federated learning allows multiple data holders to train a shared model collaboratively while each participant keeps its raw data strictly local15. Three canonical paradigms have crystallized over the past several years: horizontal federated learning, in which participants share the same feature space but hold different sample populations; vertical federated learning, where overlapping users carry complementary feature sets across institutions; and federated transfer learning, which loosens both sample and feature overlap assumptions to handle highly heterogeneous distributions16. For blood donation networks—regional centers that typically collect similar variables from non-overlapping donor pools—horizontal federation is the most natural fit.

Multimodal learning and feature fusion methods

Multimodal learning seeks to build joint representations from heterogeneous information sources — structured tabular records, free-text narratives, and temporal event sequences, among others20. Each modality demands its own encoding strategy: embedding layers and feed-forward networks handle categorical and numerical fields; pre-trained language models transform textual inputs into dense semantic vectors; and recurrent or temporal convolutional architectures capture sequential dependencies in behavioral logs21.

Three fusion paradigms dominate the literature. Early fusion concatenates modality-specific features before any shared layers process them, which is straightforward yet prone to noise amplification when modalities differ sharply in dimensionality or informativeness. Late fusion trains independent modality branches and merges their decision-level outputs, preserving modality-specific patterns at the cost of missing fine-grained cross-modal interactions. Hybrid fusion interpolates between these extremes, allowing intermediate representations to interact at selected network depths22.

In healthcare applications, multimodal fusion has already shown tangible benefits — from combining clinical notes with lab results for patient outcome prediction to integrating wearable sensor streams with electronic health records for chronic disease monitoring25. These precedents suggest that blood donor profiling, which similarly involves structured demographics, textual feedback, and longitudinal donation sequences, stands to gain substantially from a well-designed multimodal architecture.

Historically, donor profiling has drawn on three pillars: demographic attributes (age, sex, occupation), behavioral indicators (donation frequency, inter-donation intervals, seasonal patterns), and psychological constructs—altruism, self-efficacy, perceived social norms—that shape underlying motivation26. Nevertheless, three persistent obstacles limit the practical impact of these methods. First, donor records remain siloed across administratively independent blood centers, preventing any single institution from accessing the full behavioral picture. Second, most existing profiles are coarse-grained — reducing complex motivational and contextual signals to a handful of categorical labels — which limits the personalization depth achievable in recall campaigns. Third, tightening data protection regulations impose strict compliance boundaries that conventional centralized modeling pipelines cannot satisfy without substantial re-engineering31. It is precisely at this confluence of challenges — cross-institutional isolation, representational shallowness, and privacy mandates — that the present study positions its contribution, aiming to unify federated collaboration with rich multimodal profiling for recall optimization.

A matter deserving explicit treatment rather than tacit glossing-over concerns the interaction between local differential privacy and the parameter upload phase itself. In the current implementation, each client transmits its noise-perturbed parameter vector individually, and the server then performs weighted averaging across the K = 6 contributions. Cryptographic secure aggregation—in the sense of Bonawitz et al.19, where masked updates reveal only the aggregate to the server—has not been integrated at this stage of the system. With only six participants, this design leaves a residual exposure to single-point inference by a curious or compromised coordinator: local DP perturbation attenuates but does not wholly eliminate the attack surface. We flag this openly rather than letting it pass unremarked, and we treat the integration of masking-based secure aggregation as a concrete direction for near-term follow-up work, discussed further among the limitations in Sect. 5.

In all experiments reported below, we set γ = 0.5, assigning equal weight to channel classification and timing regression; a sensitivity analysis of this parameter appears in Sect. 4.3. This multi-task design allows the shared embedding layers to capture representations beneficial to both tasks simultaneously, improving generalization particularly when labeled recall outcomes are sparse.

One practical concern worth confronting head-on is that cross-entropy and mean-squared-error operate on fundamentally incommensurate numerical scales; a naive mixture would let whichever term happens to be larger quietly dominate the gradient signal and starve its counterpart of learning capacity. To keep both heads on comparable footing, we normalize the timing target ti to the [0,1] interval through min-max scaling computed over the training partition, which bounds the MSE contribution within a controlled range. We then verified empirically: after the first training epoch, the cross-entropy term settles at roughly 1.42 ± 0.08, while the normalized MSE sits at 0.58 ± 0.04, with their respective gradient L2 norms differing by less than a factor of 1.4. Under these conditions, γ = 0.5 functions as an honest equal-weighting rather than a de facto domination of one objective, a reading that Table 3’s stability across γ ∈ [0.3, 0.7] further corroborates. We also explored homoscedastic uncertainty weighting41 as an alternative, but found it yielded marginally equivalent performance at the cost of two additional learnable parameters, leaving fixed weighting as the more parsimonious choice for this application.

Experimental setup and profiling performance evaluation

Our experimental dataset consists of 127,463 anonymized donor records drawn from six municipal blood centers and covering the period from January 2018 to December 2023. Each record contains the three modalities defined in Sect. 3.1: demographic attributes, behavioral donation sequences, and textual feedback entries. To simulate a realistic federated environment, we partitioned the dataset across virtual clients corresponding to the originating centers, preserving their natural demographic imbalances. A Dirichlet-based allocation with concentration parameter further controlled the degree of non-IID skew across clients, with smaller values inducing sharper distributional divergence43. Within each client, an 80/10/10 split separated training, validation, and test sets chronologically to prevent temporal leakage.

All models were trained on a single server equipped with four NVIDIA A100 GPUs (40 GB each) under a simulated federated configuration; the six virtual clients ran as isolated processes on co-located hardware rather than on physically separate machines. Some readers may reasonably wonder why four A100s were marshaled for a model carrying only 2.38 million parameters, and the concern is fair enough to address directly. The model itself is modest: peak per-client GPU memory sits around 4.8 GB, and aggregate GPU utilization averaged roughly 31% during federated training rounds, with transient peaks near 58% during hyperparameter sweeps. The abundance of compute served a different purpose entirely—parallelizing six virtual clients running concurrently across five random-seed replicates while sweeping the full hyperparameter grid described later in Table 5. On a single mid-tier GPU, the same experimental volume would have stretched across several weeks of wall-clock time, which proved impractical within our revision schedule. For a genuine multi-center deployment, each blood center would require nothing more than a single workstation-class GPU, and many centers could likely manage with a well-provisioned CPU-only server. The hardware reported here reflects experimental throughput convenience, not operational necessity. We recognize that this design does not reproduce the communication latency, bandwidth limitations, or computational heterogeneity of a genuine multi-center deployment, and we present an explicit communication overhead analysis later in this section. Because losing two of six heterogeneous clients per round would ordinarily raise serious concerns about gradient variance and client drift—and it is a concern we take seriously—we now report exact variance statistics in Table 8 (presented later in Sect. 4.3 alongside the other robustness diagnostics). Measured across rounds 40–80 (post warm-up, pre-convergence), per-coordinate gradient variance rises from 0.021 under full participation to 0.058 under 30% dropout, roughly a 2.8-fold increase, yet remains comfortably below the 0.15–0.25 regime that typically heralds diverging client drift in standard FedAvg46. Three factors, in our reading, account for this measured stability: the FedProx proximal term (µ = 0.01) anchors local updates against the most recent global state; the cross-modal attention gate redistributes representational mass when certain clients’ data distributions shift, acting as an implicit regularizer; and the Dirichlet α = 1.0 setting, while non-IID, does not push heterogeneity into the extreme regime (α < 0.3) where drift effects typically compound. We should be candid, though, that under α = 0.1 combined with 30% dropout, preliminary experiments show the F1 gap widening to roughly 3.5 points—still below catastrophic levels, but a caveat worth naming honestly. Recall strategy optimization effectiveness and privacy protection analysis

To gauge the practical value of the proposed personalized recall model, we compared it against three baseline strategies: a fixed-cycle approach that contacts all eligible donors at uniform 90-day intervals (Fixed-Cycle), a rule-based method that triggers recall based on blood type shortage thresholds and last donation date (Rule-Based), and a centralized personalized model trained on pooled data without federated privacy constraints (Central-Personal). The experimental results reported in Sect. 4.1 through 4.3 carry implications that extend beyond the numerical metrics themselves, touching on both the theoretical understanding of federated multimodal systems and the operational realities of blood service management.

One may reasonably ask why the federated multimodal design closes so much of the gap to centralized training. In our assessment, the answer hinges on the complementary character of the three modality streams. Demographics supply a stable, low-variance scaffold of donor identity, yet they carry no temporal sensitivity. Behavioral sequences track evolving engagement dynamics but become uninformative when a donor has only one or two visits on record. Textual feedback, sparse as it often is, encodes motivational and attitudinal signals that neither of the other channels can see. What the cross-modal attention mechanism accomplishes when it operates over these three streams jointly goes well beyond simple averaging: it selectively amplifies whichever modality happens to be most diagnostic for a particular donor at a particular moment. The ablation findings confirm this reading—removing any single modality degrades performance, but the magnitude of the damage is context-dependent, and the attention gate compensates by redistributing representational weight to the surviving channels. We contend that this adaptive reweighting is the critical differentiator separating our fusion approach from simpler concatenation or late-fusion alternatives.

Practitioners should interpret the privacy-utility tradeoff with care. Our finding that an operating point around preserves over 93% of noise-free performance is reassuring, but blood center administrators must weigh this against their specific regulatory environment. Institutions subject to stringent personal health information statutes may prefer tighter budgets (), accepting a somewhat coarser recall granularity in exchange for stronger formal guarantees. Conversely, centers operating under cooperative data-sharing agreements with informed donor consent might tolerate or higher, capturing nearly the full performance benefit. The key takeaway is not a single recommended value but rather the shape of the tradeoff curve itself — the elbow region between and — which allows each deployment to make an informed, context-sensitive decision.

We should openly acknowledge several limitations of this work. First, although our dataset encompasses 127,463 records, all six contributing centers belong to a single national blood service system. Whether these findings transfer to transfusion organizations operating under markedly different donation cultures, regulatory frameworks, or demographic compositions has yet to be empirically tested. Second, the communication overhead estimates presented in Table 7 reflect idealized bandwidth assumptions. In a genuine Wide Area Network (WAN) deployment, practical delays are likely to exceed the theoretical values reported here, owing to factors such as latency jitter caused by variable routing paths, the computational cost of cryptographic handshakes (e.g., TLS/SSL negotiations) required for secure parameter transmission, and general network instability including packet loss and congestion events. Our federated experiments, executed on co-located hardware, cannot fully reproduce these effects; physically distributed testbeds remain a priority for future validation. Third, the modality-missingness stress test in Table 13, while informative, relies on random masking. Real-world text availability may correlate with donor engagement levels in ways that synthetic masking fails to capture. Fourth, the attention-weight visualizations in Fig. 9 offer a preliminary window into model behavior, but the architecture largely operates as a black-box predictor. More expressive interpretability mechanisms—concept-based explanation modules, counterfactual reasoning dashboards, or input-attribution maps—would bolster clinical trust and warrant dedicated study. Fifth, the privacy pipeline as currently configured relies exclusively on local differential privacy, without cryptographic secure aggregation on the parameter upload path. In a K = 6 federation this design leaves a residual attack surface against single-client inference by a compromised coordinator—a gap that masking-based secure aggregation protocols, such as Bonawitz et al.19 or its more recent variants, would close. Integrating such protocols is a priority for subsequent engineering work, and we flag it here rather than burying it. Sixth, the cost figures reported in Table 10 reflect retrospective simulation under institutional unit-cost assumptions rather than prospective A/B validation. While the relative ordering among strategies conveys real efficiency differences, the absolute CNY values should be read as estimates; a live prospective trial remains the proper vehicle for establishing exact operational economics. Seventh, the client-dropout robustness analysis in Sect. 4.1 was conducted primarily under moderate non-IID conditions (Dirichlet α = 1.0). Preliminary runs at α = 0.1 suggest the F1 gap widens to approximately 3.5 points under 30% dropout—still manageable, but a scenario deserving systematic study before deployment in federations exhibiting extreme demographic divergence.

These caveats notwithstanding, the practical outlook for the framework is encouraging. Its modular design—local encoding separated cleanly from global aggregation—allows individual centers to join the federation incrementally, without overhauling existing data infrastructure. The tiered recall prioritization maps onto operational workflows that blood service staff already understand, which lowers the adoption barrier considerably. Privacy guarantees anchored in formal differential privacy theory, rather than ad hoc anonymization heuristics, provide a defensible compliance posture as regulatory scrutiny of health data analytics continues to tighten. Looking forward, incorporating real-time donor feedback loops and dynamic privacy budget adjustment could further sharpen both the responsiveness and the trustworthiness of federated donor management systems.

Conclusion

This paper tackled the intertwined challenges of building accurate blood donor profiles and optimizing personalized recall strategies under stringent privacy constraints. We proposed a federated multimodal learning framework that enables cross-institutional collaboration while keeping individual donor records strictly local. Four tightly coupled components—federated architecture design, differential privacy integration, multimodal donor profiling, and multi-task recall optimization—were each validated through systematic experiments on a dataset exceeding 127,000 donor records distributed across six simulated blood centers.

Three principal findings stand out. First, the federated multimodal fusion framework—built around cross-modal attention and adaptive modality gating—reaches profiling accuracy within 2% points of a centralized upper bound, all while preserving strict data isolation across participating institutions. Behavioral sequences emerged as the single most discriminative modality, yet the attention mechanism’s capacity to dynamically offset modality-level sparsity—drawing on textual and demographic cues for donors with thin donation histories—proved indispensable for maintaining consistent performance across varied donor subpopulations. Second, the differential privacy enhancement, which pairs local gradient clipping with calibrated Gaussian noise injection, incurs only marginal utility loss at a privacy budget of . The elbow-shaped tradeoff curve that our experiments revealed gives practitioners a concrete basis for calibrating protection strength against institutional risk tolerance. Third, formulating personalized recall optimization as a multi-task learning problem over timing and channel selection lifts conversion rates by roughly 12% points relative to rule-based approaches, and the hierarchical priority clustering converts model outputs into operationally actionable recall schedules.

In terms of originality, this study offers three contributions: a modality-aware federated aggregation scheme designed for heterogeneous feature distributions across decentralized nodes; a co-designed privacy-utility balancing mechanism that formally bounds cumulative privacy loss without catastrophic accuracy degradation; and a complete end-to-end pipeline linking privacy-preserving profiling to multi-task recall decision-making inside a single trainable system. Taken together, these advances push forward the methodological frontier of federated health analytics and provide blood service organizations with a replicable blueprint for privacy-conscious, data-rich donor engagement. More broadly, the framework shows that meaningful collaborative intelligence is attainable even in health domains where data sensitivity and institutional fragmentation have long obstructed joint modeling.

Several promising avenues remain open for future work. Integrating cryptographic secure aggregation on top of the current DP scheme would close the residual single-point inference risk in small-K federations and strengthen the privacy guarantees to a more defensible operational posture. Incorporating additional modalities—geospatial mobility traces, wearable physiological indicators, social network interaction patterns—could enrich donor representations further, though it would also demand solutions to considerably greater modality alignment complexity. Exploring personalized federated aggregation, in which the global model adapts its weighting scheme to each client’s local distribution, may unlock additional robustness gains under non-IID conditions, particularly in the α ≤ 0.3 regime where gradient variance effects start to compound visibly. A prospective A/B validation of the recall strategy—replacing our retrospective cost simulation with actual operational deployment across a multi-center pilot—would convert our economic projections into established figures and, equally valuable, expose edge cases not readily visible in retrospective data. Strengthening model interpretability through attention visualization dashboards or concept-based explanation modules would deepen clinical trust and smooth regulatory review. Perhaps most critically, transitioning from simulated federation to genuine multi-center deployment—complete with real network latency, asynchronous update schedules, and institutional governance negotiations—represents the indispensable next step toward confirming the practical viability of this system in operational blood management environments.
