Dual activation of MC3R and MC4R drives weight loss and reduces food intake in male primates with obesity
Authors: Jillian L. Seiler, Anna C. Impastato, Emma Xiaoyu Zhang, Kade J. Kelley, Thomas L. Bennett, Bradley Studnitzer, Claudia R. Prindle, Benjamin H. Rajewski, Barry A. Badeau, Xinjian Jiang, Russell Potterfield, Jordan Y. Delev, Daniel L. Marks
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The melanocortin system plays a central role in regulating hunger and satiety, making it an attractive target for treating metabolic disease. However, the limited clinical success of selective melanocortin-4 receptor (MC4R) agonists has prompted investigation into whether concurrent melanocortin-3 receptor (MC3R) and MC4R activation may more effectively engage this pathway for the treatment of general obesity. Here we show that selective MC3R agonism modulates food intake in a state-dependent manner, and that co-agonism of MC3R and MC4R produces greater metabolic effects than selective MC4R agonism alone, consistent with non-redundant and cooperative roles. Using novel peptides in male nonhuman primates and rodents, we develop 710GO, an orally available MC3R/MC4R dual agonist that induces significant weight loss in primates with diet-induced obesity. Oral 710GO demonstrates limited weight rebound, compatibility with GLP-1–based therapies, and a favorable preclinical safety profile. These findings support combined MC3R/MC4R agonism as a promising approach for next-generation obesity therapeutics.

Introduction

The recent successes of glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1s) in treating obesity address a previously unmet demand for weight loss therapeutics. However, side effects and variable efficacy underscore the need for mechanistically differentiated approaches to support a massive and diverse patient population1–3. The melanocortin (MC) family of receptors has long represented an alternative metabolic target to GLP-1s for treating obesity, but drugs targeting MC receptors are currently limited to treating obesity caused by rare genetic conditions4,5. A greater understanding of the MC family is required to unlock its potential for weight loss in individuals with general obesity.

The MC family of receptors is a highly conserved neuroendocrine signaling network that plays a critical role in energy homeostasis6–10. It is comprised of five G protein-coupled receptors (MC1R-MC5R) that are endogenously activated by peptides derived from the proopiomelanocortin (POMC) precursor polypeptide—α-, β-, γ-MSH and ACTH11. One well-known endogenous antagonist of the MC family, Agouti-related peptide (AgRP), acts primarily on two receptors—MC3R and MC4R12. These are the most highly expressed MCRs in the brain, with significant expression in areas of the hypothalamus that regulate food intake and peripheral metabolism (e.g., the arcuate nucleus (ARC) and paraventricular nucleus (PVN))6,13. MC3R is expressed presynaptically on POMC/AgRP terminals that regulate GABA-mediated inhibition onto postsynaptic MC4R-expressing PVN neurons, highlighting the direct ability for MC3R to modulate MC4R activity14. While agonism (e.g., α-MSH) of the central melanocortin system causes reduced food intake and weight loss, antagonism (e.g., AgRP) causes increased food intake and weight gain15. Notably, both of these peptides simultaneously target MC3R and MC4R. Assuming these two receptors have non-redundant functions, their shared ligands and hypothalamic expression pattern indicate a potentially cooperative role in metabolism.

Despite evidence for these receptors’ synergy, early rodent studies predominately suggest the paramount role of MC4R over MC3R in body weight regulation16–20. Furthermore, MC4R loss-of-function (LoF) mutations were identified as the most common cause of monogenic obesity in humans21,22. These observations, combined with the limited pharmacological evaluation of MC3R, likely primed pharmaceutical efforts to focus on MC4R-selective agonists for treating obesity. All of these early MC4R-selective programs, with the exception of setmelanotide, were terminated after failing to demonstrate safe, translational efficacy in the clinic23. These findings have been interpreted as reflecting limited therapeutic windows, wherein adverse effects prevented dosing at efficacious levels in humans23.

Since these pharmaceutical program failures, studies investigating MC3R have continued to support its non-redundant role in metabolism. In humans, MC3R LoF mutations are rare, but are significantly more prevalent in individuals with obesity24. These MC3R-deficient phenotypes implicate the receptor in controlling energy partitioning as it relates to growth, puberty, and body composition25–27. In rodents, Mc3r knockouts demonstrate increased fat mass and reduced body lengths, although effects on food intake vary by species28,29. Perturbations to MC3R in rodent models after homeostatic challenge led to abnormal refeeding14,30. Across species, sexually dimorphic effects of MC3R have also been observed in metabolic phenotype and reproductive development17,31–33. These findings position MC3R as a critical target for metabolic regulation. However, these discoveries surfaced after the early 2000s, when pharmaceutical programs were already clinically evaluating MC4R-selective candidates.

Recently, mifomelatide (TCMCB07), a first-in-class MC3R/MC4R dual antagonist, was developed to treat involuntary weight loss, and showed increases in participant body weight in a phase I clinical trial34–37. In this work, we investigate MC3R/MC4R dual agonism as a potential mechanism for safe and efficacious weight loss in general obesity. We design and synthesize novel peptides to probe the distinct and combined roles of MC3R and MC4R across metabolic states in male nonhuman primates and rodents. We further develop 710GO (EMBC-710GO), an orally available MC3R/MC4R dual agonist, and evaluate its effects on food intake, body weight, and safety. Together, these studies provide insight into the relative contributions of MC3R and MC4R and support combined receptor activation as a strategy for obesity therapeutics.

Results

Design and characterization of MC3R and MC4R peptide agonists

To evaluate the effects of MC3R and MC4R on food intake and body weight, we designed and synthesized a series of peptides with differing selectivity profiles. The compounds were then evaluated in functional assays measuring cyclic adenosine monophosphate (cAMP) production, as the receptors are known to exert their effects on metabolic regulation via Gs protein-dependent pathways (Fig. 1, Table 1, Supplementary Fig. 1)38,39. Functional activity of α-MSH was comparable to reported values, validating the assay40. All previous clinical molecules demonstrated full MC4R agonism (Emax ≥ 100%) and selectivity for MC4R over MC3R. Additionally, we designed MC3R- and MC4R-selective compounds (EBMC-40-187 and EBMC-03-47, respectively) and compounds with full agonist activity on both MC3R and MC4R (710GO) and mixed activity as an MC3R partial antagonist/MC4R full agonist (707GO; Supplementary Fig. 2). Many melanocortin compounds, including our own, also have activity on MC1R and MC5R. EBMC-03-47 has activity on MC1R (EC50 = 0.407 nM, Emax = 102%) and MC5R (EC50 = 0.391 nM, Emax = 102%), whereas EBMC-40-187 is active on MC1R (EC50 = 3.38 nM, Emax = 100%) with minimal activity on MC5R (EC50 > 1000 nM). We use “MC3R-selective” and “MC4R-selective” to compare a compound’s relative activity on these two receptors. MC3R and MC4R agonists differentially affect NHP body weight

MC3R is implicated as a key regulator of energy homeostasis and is critical for providing an upper bound to weight gain during exposure to energy-dense food14. To investigate the roles of MC3R and MC4R in the context of homeostatic bounds, we subcutaneously administered MC4R-selective agonist EBMC-03-47, MC3R-selective agonist EBMC-40-187, and their combination to male NHPs in different metabolic states: ad libitum/lean and fasted/lean. Food intake was measured over 24 h following drug administration and was normalized to each animals' own vehicle baseline.

In lean animals that were fed ad libitum, MC3R or MC4R agonism did not significantly alter food intake compared to control after 24 h (Fig. 2A). Total food consumption during the first 2 h was highly variable but low, leading to large changes in cumulative food intake that normalized after 3 h (Supplementary Fig. 4). In NHPs that were fasted overnight, treatment with selective agonists alone and in combination resulted in short-term decreases in food intake (Fig. 2B). MC4R agonism reduced food intake by 20.0 ± 2.4% in the first hour after dosing but returned to baseline after 4 h. The effect of MC3R agonism was more pronounced, reducing cumulative food intake by 46.0 ± 7.1% in the first hour. This reduction was persistent; after 24 h, cumulative food intake was still 14.3 ± 1.6% lower than control. Co-administration of both agonists caused the largest reduction of cumulative food intake for the first 3 h, averaging 54.9 ± 18.9% lower than control. However, these levels returned to baseline after 4 h. Compared to ad libitum conditions, modest homeostatic challenge (fasting) increased the food intake reduction of melanocortin agonism. We then investigated the differences in MC3R/MC4R agonism under conditions of prolonged homeostatic dysregulation, as in obesity, as we hypothesized that these receptors may play differential roles in a more chronic setting. An MC3R agonist, MC4R agonist, and a combination of the two were administered subcutaneously to NHPs with diet-induced obesity (DIO) daily. These animals were provided with ad libitum high-fat diet, and food intake and body weight were measured daily for 15 days. On day ten, doses were increased to investigate dose responsiveness. Over the duration of the study, cumulative food intake decreased across all groups compared to vehicle treatment, although with large individual variability (Fig. 2C). MC3R agonism decreased cumulative food intake by 1.4 ± 8.9% compared to vehicle, whereas MC4R agonism reduced food intake by 11.2 ± 12.1%. Consistent with the lean fasted NHPs in the acute feeding study, co-administration of MC3R and MC4R agonists resulted in the greatest reduction in cumulative food intake—a 19.6 ± 21.3% decrease compared to vehicle. These results point to a cooperative effect of MC3R and MC4R agonism on food intake in NHPs with diet-induced obesity consuming an energy-dense diet.

The observed food intake reduction from MC3R/MC4R co-agonism was well coupled with significant decreases in body weight (Two-way ANOVA, Ftreatment × time (3.65, 18.25) = 5.34, p < 0.01, η2 = 0.16; Fig. 2D, Supplementary Fig. 4). Co-administration of selective agonists significantly reduced body weight by 7.8 ± 1.7% over the course of the study (p < 0.05). This decrease in body weight was substantial compared to selective MC4R agonism, which only reduced body weight by 5.6 ± 1.8%. Interestingly, selective MC3R agonism resulted in 1.5 ± 2.4% increase in body weight, despite slightly reducing food intake. At day 0 and the completion of the dosing period, NHPs were scanned with dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DEXA) to characterize their body composition and we observed a significant effect of treatment (Two-way ANOVA, Ftreatment (3, 15) = 6.32, p < 0.01, η2 = 0.23). While MC3R agonism alone caused a 3.65 ± 3.50% reduction in fat mass compared to baseline, NHPs also experienced a 6.85 ± 1.11% increase in lean mass, explaining their net body weight increase (Fig. 2E). Co-agonism and MC4R agonism alone caused the same amount of lean mass loss (2.83 ± 3.96% and 2.76 ± 1.63%, respectively), but co-agonism caused almost twice as much fat mass loss as MC4R agonism (16.58 ± 7.23% and 8.64 ± 2.54%, respectively). While MC4R agonism resulted in weight loss, these data suggest that co-agonism with MC3R leads to greater total weight loss and synergistic fat mass loss. Comparable results were obtained in rats, with the notable exception that selective MC3R agonism elicited weight loss (Supplementary Figs. 5 and 8). This species-specific divergence may reflect evolutionary differences in melanocortin metabolic regulation41–43.

MC3R/MC4R co-agonism lowers weight and food intake in NHPs

While our data suggest complementary MC3R/MC4R co-agonism can improve weight loss, other literature demonstrates that de-activating MC3R (through pharmacological antagonism or gene knockout) can facilitate weight loss and inhibit weight regain in lean rodents14,31,44. These contradictory proposed roles of MC3R necessitated further investigation of MC3R activity in conjunction with MC4R agonism. To evaluate this, we subcutaneously administered two compounds—710GO and 707GO—to fasted NHPs with diet-induced obesity. While both compounds are full MC4R agonists with similar pharmacokinetic (PK) properties, 710GO is a potent MC3R agonist and 707GO is a partial MC3R antagonist (Table 1, Supplementary Tables 2-4, Supplementary Figs. 10 and 11). Food intake was measured for 72 h after subcutaneous injection and plotted alongside the animals’ respective vehicle baseline (Fig. 3A). NHPs treated with 710GO experienced a non-significant reduction in food intake that peaked at 0.5 h post food presentation but was still 42.5 ± 8.0% less than vehicle by 72 h (Fig. 3B). NHPs administered 707GO briefly trended towards an increase in food intake but normalized back to vehicle levels of cumulative food intake by 72 h. We then set out to determine whether this acute interaction between MC3R and MC4R translates to body weight changes with repeated dosing. We administered 710GO and 707GO daily via oral gavage to NHPs with diet-induced obesity for 35 days. NHPs treated with 710GO consumed 20.3 ± 1.1% fewer calories compared to their pre-treatment baseline, whereas NHPs treated with 707GO consumed 13.4 ± 1.3% fewer calories (Fig. 3C). Both treatment groups showed statistically significant reductions in caloric consumption across the study duration (One-way ANOVA, Ftreatment(2, 105) = 130.1, p < 0.0001, η2 = 0.71), as well as on specific days, with 710GO-treated animals showing more days of significant difference. Consistent with selective agonist experiments, subchronic studies in rats show a reduction in food intake and body weight in response to both 710GO and 707GO (Supplementary Table 1, Supplementary Figs. 6, 8-9). In addition to caloric consumption, treatment also had a significant effect on body weight across the study duration (Two-way ANOVA, Ftreatment x time(2.94, 52.86) = 13.71), p < 0.0001, η2 = 0.12). By day 36, 710GO-treated NHPs had lost 7.5 ± 1.2% body weight compared to vehicle, but 707GO-treated NHPs did not demonstrate significant weight change (Fig. 3D, Supplementary Fig. 7B). These results further suggest that MC3R agonism works cooperatively with MC4R agonism to produce larger reductions in body weight and food intake.

Chronic 710GO administration reduces body weight in NHPs with obesity

After establishing the efficacy of dual MC3R/MC4R agonism for weight loss in NHPs with obesity, we investigated the effects of chronic administration. NHPs with diet-induced obesity were given 710GO (10 mg/kg) or saline by oral gavage over the course of 15 weeks. Food intake and body weight measurements were taken daily. NHPs with diet-induced obesity were offered three meals per day, including high-fat chow, fruit, and normal chow. Likely due to the high-calorie diet provided, NHPs with diet-induced obesity receiving saline gained 4.6 ± 0.4% body weight over the course of the study. There was a significant effect of the treatment on body weight across the study duration (Two-way ANOVA, Ftreatment(1,12) = 9.74, p < 0.01, η2 = 0.34). 710GO preserves lean mass and mitigates GI side effects

Given the reported concerns of GLP-1 therapy, we specifically evaluated 710GO for weight rebound, loss of lean mass, and GI side effects2,45,46. After dosing concluded in our 15-week study, NHPs with diet-induced obesity remained on high-fat diet and were monitored for an additional 6 weeks. Two weeks after cessation of treatment, NHPs treated with 710GO lost an additional 0.39% of their initial body weight. By 6 weeks after treatment cessation, they had only regained 34.2% of the total weight lost during treatment. This rate of weight regain was not significantly different when compared to animals receiving placebo (Fig. 4B). In the 35-day study, 710GO-treated NHPs with diet-induced obesity lost an average of 518.1 ± 132.7 g of fat mass as measured by DEXA scan, corresponding to 90.1% of the total mass lost (Fig. 4C). Notably, 710GO did not significantly decrease lean mass in NHPs with diet-induced obesity, with lean mass only constituting 9.93% of the total mass lost (Fig. 4C). 710GO-treated animals appeared healthy, with no observed vomiting or diarrhea. We then evaluated 710GO as an adjuvant to semaglutide for weight loss in NHPs with diet-induced obesity over 19 days, and found that treatment had a significant effect on body weight over the study duration (Two-way ANOVA, Ftreatment × time(4.48, 26.85) = 19.53, p < 0.0001, η2 = 0.2). Co-administration of semaglutide and 710GO decreased body weight by 7.6 ± 0.7%, significantly more than 4.1 ± 0.8% when semaglutide was administered alone (p < 0.05) (Fig. 4D, Supplementary Fig. 7D). Not only was the combination treatment significantly more effective than semaglutide alone, it also did not induce vomiting or GI distress (Fig. 4D). This effect is consistent with prior treatments of other melanocortin agonists in combination with GLP-1 drugs in both nonclinical and clinical settings47,48.

710GO demonstrates a safe cardiac profile in male NHPs with obesity

Melanocortin agonists have previously been limited by cardiac side effects49. To investigate the effect of 710GO on cardiac parameters, multiple doses were orally administered to NHPs implanted with telemetry devices. Over a 24-h monitoring period, 710GO did not induce changes in cardiac parameters, including systolic blood pressure (SBP), diastolic blood pressure (DBP), heart rate (HR), and corrected QT interval (QTc) at doses up to 60 mg/kg (Fig. 4E-H). In comparison, setmelanotide (an FDA-approved melanocortin agonist) significantly increased nighttime DBP and SBP and decreased body temperature several hours after a 3 mg/kg subcutaneous dose (Supplementary Fig. 12). Both 710GO (60 mg/kg, PO) and setmelanotide (3 mg/kg, SC) were dosed six times higher than their previously established efficacious doses in NHPs50. Additionally, 710GO did not result in hERG inhibition (Supplementary Fig. 13). These results demonstrate that 710GO safely modulates the melanocortin system without increasing sympathetic tone and may also have a greater therapeutic window than setmelanotide.

Discussion

The respective contributions of MC3R and MC4R to energy homeostasis have long remained elusive. Historically, MC4R was viewed as the primary mediator of food intake and weight regulation, while MC3R was considered ancillary16–20. While humans with MC4R LoF mutations display frank hyperphagia and obesity, MC3R LoF variants typically present with a mild overweight phenotype22,24. Yet, recent work suggests that MC3R functions as a rheostat, which modulates downstream melanocortin tone based on peripheral cues of energetic state14. To dissect this interplay, we evaluated selective MC3R, MC4R, and dual MC3R/MC4R agonists in NHPs under both acute and chronic metabolic perturbations.

In metabolically normal, lean animals under homeostatic conditions, selective activation of either receptor had little effect on suppressing feeding, while dual MC3R/MC4R agonism produced a modest reduction of food intake. However, during acute fasting, MC3R agonism alone markedly suppressed refeeding behavior in lean animals, whereas MC4R agonism alone did not. Combined MC3R/MC4R activation further enhanced this anorectic effect, indicating that MC3R plays a dominant role in responding to a transient energy deficit and that dual MC3R/MC4R activation amplifies this response.

In NHPs with obesity undergoing subchronic dosing, receptor contributions diverged: selective MC4R agonism reduced food intake and body weight, consistent with its established role in long-term energy regulation, while dual MC3R/MC4R agonism produced greater and more durable reductions across both metrics. In contrast, selective MC3R agonism did not decrease food intake. This finding aligns with previous studies where Mc3rKO animals exposed to obesogenic challenges vastly overshoot an appropriate set point compared to wildtype controls, suggesting that MC3R is necessary for setting an upper limit on metabolic regulation14. Additionally, an increase in lean mass was seen in response to MC3R agonism, leading to a slight increase in body weight. This finding is consistent with the previously characterized role of MC3R in modulating energy partitioning17.

These findings delineate a potential functional division of labor within the melanocortin system. MC3R acts as a contextual modulator, tuning the magnitude of the response in line with metabolic state, while MC4R drives sustained changes in feeding and body weight. Together, they operate as an integrated network, coordinating adaptive and chronic control of body weight. Thus, modulating MC3R adjusts the boundary, thereby allowing for more profound weight gain or loss through MC4R activation above the previously established metabolic set point.

One additional hypothesis emerging from these data is that obesity may blunt responsiveness to tonic α-MSH–mediated food inhibition, rendering melanocortin signaling partially desensitized51,52. MC4R is known to have high basal activity, which may contribute to the observed reduction in food intake when selectively agonizing MC3R in lean fasted animals53. However, in NHPs with obesity, a loss of high constituent MC4R tone renders selective MC3R agonism insufficient to lower food intake. Under such conditions, supraphysiological MC3R and MC4R agonism may help restore this lost sensitivity by amplifying melanocortin tone or re-establishing inhibitory thresholds, thereby resulting in more effective MC4R signaling.

Our findings also contextualize prior clinical programs. Early MC4R-selective agonists achieved only limited efficacy, even after prolonged treatment54,55. More recent compounds, such as setmelanotide and bivamelagon, retain some potency on MC3R and produced greater weight loss, supporting the hypothesis that MC3R engagement enhances MC4R-driven outcomes56,57. However, limitations in dosing due to potential off-target adverse effects, including increased sympathetic tone, likely prevent full receptor activation and clinically-relevant weight loss in general obesity58. To overcome these limitations, we developed 710GO, an equipotent dual MC3R/MC4R agonist. High potency at both receptors allows for efficacy at lower exposures, a C-terminal extension minimizes off-target interactions, and non-canonical amino acids enhance physiological stability59,60. These design features collectively enable precise central melanocortin engagement with improved efficacy and tolerability.

While promising, these studies were conducted with modest sample sizes and showed large interindividual variability. The compounds used in this study are selective relative to MC3R and MC4R; however, they also have varying activity on MC1R and MC5R which may impact their effect on the observed metabolic and safety profiles. We note that neither MC1R nor MC5R have been directly linked to changes in food intake or body weight, but the contributions of these receptors cannot be entirely ruled out61,62. EBMC-40-187 and EBMC-03-47 were dose-matched to yield similar systemic PK profiles, but differences in central penetrance and receptor actuation may also affect activity. While compounds in this study were administered systemically and only to male animals due to availability, we recognize that MC3R in particular has demonstrated both a sexually dimorphic and anatomically heterogenous impact on various behaviors; future studies will need to carefully parse out these differential effects. Across studies, rodent body weight was always tightly correlated to changes in food intake, whereas in NHPs, the two metrics sometimes diverged. Species differences may reflect important evolutionary and ecological pressures that led to greater, longer-term energy demands. In such cases, more nuanced mechanisms for metabolic control are required, including alterations in energy partitioning and storage. These observations warrant further investigation. Additionally, differences in the pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic profiles of dual MC3R/MC4R agonism via administration of two separate compounds vs one compound should be considered. Regardless, in the present studies, dual agonism consistently resulted in the greatest reduction in both food intake and body weight, irrespective of species. While the data presented here are directionally consistent with a model of MC3R/MC4R cooperativity, we acknowledge that the current pharmacological datasets do not yet fully disentangle receptor-specific mechanisms or definitively establish the nature of this interaction. As such, the mechanistic framework proposed should be interpreted as a working model supported by convergent observations across conditions rather than a resolved pathway. Further studies, including more targeted mechanistic experiments, are neccessary to more precisely define the relative and potentially context-dependent contributions of each receptor. Future work, including a first-in-human clinical trial, will evaluate the translational potential of MC3R/MC4R dual activation.

Collectively, these findings position MC3R/MC4R dual activation as a differentiated mechanism that re-establishes the melanocortin system as a promising target for obesity treatment. Dual MC3R/MC4R agonism recapitulates the normal physiological integration required for effective and sustainable weight regulation. By engaging both receptors, 710GO achieves efficacy at lower exposures and thus a broader safety margin than prior MC4R-selective compounds. Moreover, these data support the innate cooperation between melanocortin and incretin systems which lends itself to combination therapy with lower doses, greater efficacy, and reduced side effects. Additionally, we observe minimal weight rebound and preferential loss of fat mass relative to lean mass with orally-administered 710GO, which could provide a mechanism of durable, healthier weight loss. After decades of limited success in melanocortin-based obesity pharmacotherapy, these findings offer a renewed path forward that ultimately warrants investigation in clinical studies.

Methods

Cynomolgus macaque husbandry

All protocols and any amendments or procedures involving the care or use of animals in these studies were reviewed and approved by WuXi AppTec Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) prior to the initiation of such procedures (IACUC No. 24043001). Only male cynomolgus macaques (Macaca fascicularis) were used in these studies due to limited availability of female macaques. Animals were individually housed in stainless-steel mesh cages in accordance with local standard and Testing Facility SOPs. Lean animals were supplied with a certified Monkey Diet twice each day, except for overnight fasting for acute refeeding experiments where specified. NHPs with diet-induced obesity were fed a high fat diet for the entirety of the study, including standard chow (3.5 kcal/g) as the morning meal and high-fat chow (4.31 kcal/g) as the afternoon meal. In addition, animals received fruit (0.52 kcal/g) daily as nutritional enrichment. Facilities were kept at a humidity between 40% to 70% at temperatures ranging from 16°C to 26 °C with at least 8 air changes/h. An electronic time-controlled lighting system was used to provide a 12-h light/12-h dark cycle. The animals were supplied with fresh reverse-osmosis (RO) water using an automated watering system. Veterinary care was available and provided to the animals throughout the course of the study. Trained veterinary professionals were specifically instructed to monitor animals for behaviors related to nausea, including hypersalivation, retching, and emesis. Any decisions about removing animals from studies for health reasons were made at the judgement of veterinary staff. In all studies, technicians were trained to account for food not consumed due to spillage or potential known sickness behaviors such as food shredding, food hoarding, or pica.

Selective and combined agonism acute feeding studies in cynomolgus macaques

Fifteen non-naïve adult (ages 4-6 years old), male cynomolgus macaques (Macaca fascicularis) were weighed (starting body weight 3–6 kg) and baseline food intake was measured for 6 days prior to the start of dosing. Animals were then grouped such that the average food intake, age and body weight in each group was roughly equivalent. The study was performed in a within-subjects design where each animal acts as its own control by receiving a single administration of each dose level, including vehicle. Macaques were given SC, QD, either vehicle (300 mM mannitol in sWFI), EBMC-40-187 (an MC3R-selective agonist), EBMC-03-47 (an MC4R-selective agonist), or combination EBMC-40-187 and EBMC-03-47 (n = 3, per dose group, except where one animal that was removed from the ad libitum combination dose group based on a ROUT outlier analysis, Q = 1%). In both the EBMC-40-187 and the combination dosing groups, EBMC-40-187 was administered at 8 and 15 mg/kg. In both the EBMC-03-47 and the combination dosing groups, EBMC-03-47 was administered at 3 and 6 mg/kg. Food was weighed at 0.5 h, 1 h, 1.5 h, 2 h, 3 h, 4 h, 24 h post-first dosing (Day 1). Animals were fed 2 h after dosing, so the measurement at 0.5 h means 2.5 h after dosing. Macaques were regularly observed via cage-side observation for the duration of the study.

MC3R selective agonism, MC4R selective agonism, and MC3R/MC4R co-agonism sub-chronic feeding study in cynomolgus macaques

Twenty-five naïve adult (ages 9–20 years old), male cynomolgus macaques (Macaca fascicularis) with diet-induced obesity with starting body weights >8.5 kg were weighed and baseline food intake was measured for 6 days prior to the start of dosing. Animals were then grouped such that the average food intake, age and body weight in each group was roughly equivalent, with extra animals being released back into the colony. Macaques were given SC, QD, either vehicle (300 mM mannitol in sWFI), EBMC-40-187 (an MC3R-selective agonist), EBMC-03-47 (an MC4R-selective agonist), or combination EBMC-40-187 and EBMC-03-47 for 24 days (n = 5). In both the EBMC-40-187 and the combination dosing groups, EBMC-40-187 was administered at 4 mg/kg for days 1–10 and 8 mg/kg for days 11–24. In both the EBMC-03-47 and the combination dosing groups, EBMC-03-47 was administered at 1.5 mg/kg for days 1–10 and 3 mg/kg for days 11–24. Food intake was monitored by meal (morning, afternoon, and evening). Body weight was measured weekly and on day 24 (the final day of dosing). Animals were fasted overnight for the collection of blood for clinical chemistry before the start of the study and every 2 weeks until the end of dosing. At days −7 and 25, animals were anesthetized and body composition was measured via DEXA scan. Macaques were monitored by regular cage-side observation for the duration of the study.

MC3R/MC4R co-agonism (710GO) and MC4R agonism/MC3R partial antagonism (707GO) acute feeding studies in cynomolgus macaques

Six non-naïve adult (ages 10–25 years old), male cynomolgus macaques (Macaca fascicularis) were weighed (starting body weight >8 kg) and baseline food intake was measured for 6 days prior to the start of dosing. Animals were then grouped such that the average food intake, age and body weight in each group was roughly equivalent. The study was performed in a within-subjects design where each animal acts as its own control by receiving a single administration of each dose level, including vehicle. Macaques were given SC, QD, either vehicle (saline), 710GO or 707GO (3 mg/kg, n = 2 per dose group). Food intake was monitored for 72 h post-dose. Macaques were regularly observed via cage-side observation for the duration of the study.

MC3R/MC4R co-agonism (710GO) and MC4R agonism/MC3R partial antagonism (707GO) sub-chronic feeding study in cynomolgus macaques

Forty non-naïve adult (ages 6–22 years old), male cynomolgus macaques (Macaca fascicularis) with diet-induced-obesity and starting body weight >8.5 kg were weighed and food intake was measured for 6 days prior to the start of the study. Animals were then grouped such that the average food intake, age and body weight in each group was roughly equivalent, with extra animals being released back into the colony. They were then administered either vehicle (saline, n = 16), 710GO (n = 15), or 707GO (n = 8). For the 710GO and 707GO groups, macaques were administered 10 mg/kg PO, QD for days 1–8, 20 mg/kg PO, QD for days 9–14, and 15 mg/kg PO, BID for days 15–35. Food intake was monitored by meal. Body weight was measured weekly. Animals were fasted overnight for the collection of blood for clinical chemistry before the start of the study and every 2 weeks until the end of dosing. Macaques were monitored by regular cage-side observation for the duration of the study.

Chronic dual-agonist (710GO) feeding study

Fourteen non-naïve adult (ages 10–22 years old), male cynomolgus macaques (Macaca fascicularis) with diet-induced-obesity with starting body weights >8 kg, were administered either vehicle (n = 4) or 710GO by oral gavage. The 710GO treatment group was split into two cohorts. The first 710GO cohort (n = 4) received 10 mg/kg for days 1–91. The second 710GO cohort (n = 6) received 10 mg/kg for days 1–51, 20 mg/kg for days 52–91, and 30 mg/kg for days 92–105. The 710GO results from the study are described as the combined data from both 710GO cohorts. Food intake was monitored by meal. Body weight was measured weekly, and weight rebound was assessed for 6 weeks after cessation of the treatment phase. Blood labs were taken at the start of the study and every 2 weeks until the end of dosing. One animal was dropped from the saline group due to nonspecific illness. Body composition was measured by MRI at the beginning and end of the treatment phase of the second 710GO cohort.

Subchronic combination 710GO + GLP-1 feeding study in cynomolgus macaques

Sixteen non-naïve adult (ages 6-22 years old), male cynomolgus macaques (Macaca fascicularis) with diet-induced-obesity with starting body weights >8 kg, were treated with vehicle (saline, n = 8), semaglutide (n = 3, one animal was removed from the study for nonspecific illness) or combination 710GO and semaglutide (n = 4). Semaglutide was administered at a dose of 0.005 mg/kg SC, BIW for week 1, and 0.01 mg/kg SC, BIW for weeks 2–3. 710GO was administered 10 mg/kg PO, QD for days 1–19. The combination group was treated with semaglutide (0.005 mg/kg SC, BIW) and 710GO (10 mg/kg, PO, QD) for week 1 and semaglutide (0.01 mg/kg SC, BIW) and 710GO (10 mg/kg, PO, QD) for weeks 2–3. Food intake was monitored by meal. Body weight was measured weekly. Blood labs were taken at the start of the study and every 2 weeks until the end of dosing. Macaques were monitored by regular cage-side observation for the duration of the study.

Cardiac safety studies of 710GO in cynomolgus macaques

Five non-naïve adult (ages 6-9 years old), male cynomolgus macaques (Macaca fascicularis) with starting body weights between 7 and 9 kg were surgically implanted with telemetry devices to monitor body temperature and cardiac parameters including SBP, DBP, HR, and QTc. Each animal (n = 5) received vehicle (sWFI) and 710GO at doses of 3, 10, 30, and 60 mg/kg, PO, in a Latin square design. Setmelanotide was dosed 3 mg/kg, SC (n = 3). Animals were dosed in the morning; body temperature and cardiac parameters were measured every 30 min over a 24-h monitoring period.

Compound procurement

Peptides and small molecules were either synthesized at WuXi AppTec or purchased from commercial vendors.

Cyclic AMP assays

CHO-K1 cells (from EuroScreenFast) expressing recombinant human receptor grown prior to the test in media without antibiotic were detached by gentle flushing with PBS-EDTA (5 mM EDTA), recovered by centrifugation and resuspended in assay buffer (KRH: 5 mM KCl, 1.25 mM MgSO4, 124 mM NaCl, 25 mM HEPES, 13.3 mM Glucose, 1.25 mM KH2PO4, 1.45 mM CaCl2, 0.5 g/l BSA, supplemented with 1 mM IBMX or 25 mM Rolipram). Dose response curves were performed in parallel with the reference compounds. For agonist tests (384 wells), 5 mL of cells were mixed with 5 mL of test compound at concentrations from 0.0001 to 1000 nM and then incubated 30 min at room temperature. After addition of the lysis buffer containing cAMP-d2 and anti-cAMP cryptate detection reagents, plates were incubated 1 h at room temperature, and fluorescence ratios were measured according to HTRF kit specifications. For antagonist tests (384 wells): 5 mL of cells were mixed with 5 mL of test compound at concentrations from 0.0001 to 1000 nM and reference agonist NDP-α-MSH for a final concentration corresponding to the historical EC80. Plates were then incubated for 30 min at room temperature. After addition of the lysis buffer containing cAMP-d2 and anti-cAMP cryptate detection reagents, plates were incubated 1 h at room temperature, and fluorescence ratios were measured according to HTRF kit specifications.

Statistical methods

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism (v10.6.1). Sample sizes (n) refer to the number of animals in each group. Data are shown as mean ± SEM. For comparisons among more than two groups, one- or two-way ANOVA or mixed-effects analyses followed by either Dunnett’s (when comparing only to vehicle group), uncorrected Fisher’s LSD, or Tukey’s multiple-comparison test were applied. All statistical tests were two-sided. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Specific n, test used and p-value are reported in all figure legends. For all in vivo studies, n refers to the number of individual animals (biological replicates). Exact p-values for all statistical comparisons are provided in the Source Data file. η2 was used to report effect sizes for F tests and were calculated in GraphPad Prism.

General peptide synthesis

Peptides were synthesized on Rink amide resin using standard Fmoc SPPS, utilizing orthogonal Alloc, Allyl, tBu, Boc, Trt, and Pbf side chain protecting groups. Fmoc-deprotection was carried out using 1–2 bed volume (BV) of 20% piperdine in DMF (20 min, 2×). Resin bound peptides were washed with DMF (2–3 BV, 6×). Coupling steps utilized either AA/HBTU/DIEA (3:3:6) or AA/DIC/HOBt (3:3.3:3) in DMF followed by a DMF wash step (2–3 BV, 5×). For cyclized peptides, Alloc/Allyl side chain protecting groups were deprotected under N2 with 20.0 eq phenylsilane and 0.2 eq Pd(PPh3)4 in DCM (2–3 BV) for 1.5 h at rt. The resin was then drained and washed 5× with DCM and 5× with DMF. Peptides were either cyclized with DIC:HOBt (3:6) or HATU:DIEA (2:4) in DMF. Peptides were cleaved from the resin using a cleavage cocktail of TFA/3-MPr/Tis/H2O (92.5/2.5/2.5/2.5) at 10 mL/g peptide resin for 2 h at rt. After cleavage, peptides were precipitated with cold isopropyl ether (10:1 ether:cocktail). Two iterative steps of centrifugation and ether wash were performed before drying under vacuum.

All peptides were purified using RP-HPLC (5 μm, C18, 110 Å) using linear gradients of MeCN in H2O (modified with 0.075% TFA). Gradient and run time vary by elution property of the peptide. After purification, peptides were frozen and lyophilized. RP-HPLC spectra for all purified peptides were acquired (1.9 mm, C18, 2.1 × 50 mm) using a linear 10%–80% gradient of MeCN in H2O (modified with 0.075%–0.1% TFA) over 2 min.
