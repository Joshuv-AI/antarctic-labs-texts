Correlated cell movements drive epithelial finger formation
Sander C. Kammeraat, Yann-Edwin Keta, Paul Appleton, Ian P. Newton, Tanniemola B. Liverpool, Rastko Sknepnek, Inke Näthke, Silke Henkes

Abstract
Sheets of epithelial cells form protective barriers in multicellular organisms. When damaged, finger-like protrusions form at the advancing edge, closing the damaged area. Due to the resemblance to fluid spreading, existing models typically invoke instability mechanisms to explain the onset of fingers. Combining in vitro experiments on freely expanding MDCK cell monolayers with simulations of the self-propelled Voronoi model and an active viscoelastic theory, we show that instead fingers form spontaneously due to emergent, correlated cell motion within the cell layer and simply represent long-lived active fluctuations of the boundary. Simulations and theory both quantitatively match spatiotemporally correlated cell motion measured in the interior of the monolayer. To capture finger formation, we model the actomyosin cable at the advancing edge as a contractile semi-flexible polymer driven by correlated active noise representing the interior. The model not only exhibits spontaneous finger formation but also quantitatively predicts tangent-tangent and roughness correlation functions of the edge in space and time, as well as fluctuation spectra. Our results, therefore, indicate that correlated cell movements lead to robust finger formation, without the need for any feedback mechanism, suggesting that leader cells, cell-cell signalling, and division modulate an intrinsic process instead of causing it. 1 1 1 Author contributions: SCK carried out the image analysis and ran the simulations. Significance statement

Epithelia form protective barriers in multicellular organisms. To maintain homeostasis, they must be able to regenerate and heal damaged areas. This occurs through collective cell migration, during which finger-like protrusions commonly appear. Whether these protrusions are driven by specialised leader cells, biochemical cues, or generic physical interactions remains unclear. Integrating in vitro imaging, agent-based simulations, and continuum modelling, we show that correlated active cell motion alone suffices to produce fingers. Leader cells, signalling, and proliferation modulate, but do not trigger, this pattern. Our results show that the key mechanism underlying a complex biological process can be understood using a general framework of the physics of dense active matter.

Structural integrity of the epithelial layers that form barriers in multicellular organisms

producing several different potential mechanisms. Yet, no consensus has been reached about the exact biophysical mechanism underlying this phenomenon.

Several models have been proposed where leader cells at the tips of the fingers, with prominent lamellipodia that generate traction forces

Models introducing signalling

Recent studies increasingly focused on the role of the entire monolayer in finger formation, reflecting the increased recognition of the role of mechanics in the collective behaviour of tissues
Ladoux and Mège 2017
.
In particular, it was shown that the mechanism controlling finger formation must originate from the dynamics of cells in the bulk behind the fingers
Vishwakarma et al. 2018
, and that leader cells cannot drag more than one or two follower cells
Rossetti et al. 2024
;
Lee 2024
, and that the boundary and interior length scales are coupled
Vazquez et al. 2022
. From a modelling point of view, cell-cell alignment in the interior has been shown to generate finger structures, either on its own
Basan et al. 2013
;
Khataee et al. 2020
;
Büscher et al. 2020
or in conjunction with leaders
Sepúlveda et al. 2013
;
Tarle et al. 2015
or cell division
Bonilla et al. 2020
.
Moreover, at the continuum scale, the interplay between an imposed velocity gradient and contractile intercellular stresses is known to generate boundary instabilities
Lee and Wolgemuth 2011
;
Nesbitt et al. 2017
;
Alert et al. 2019
;
Trenado et al. 2021
.

In this paper, we propose a simple mechanism for the formation of fingers, as illustrated in Fig.
1
.
Starting from observations of correlated cell movements over length scales exceeding multiple cell sizes
Garcia et al. 2015
;
Petitjean et al. 2010
;
Henkes et al. 2020
, we show that uncorrelated active cell crawling, together with elastic interactions between cells, results in multicellular-scale flow patterns in the cell monolayer behind the advancing edge
Henkes et al. 2020
. Building on our previous work
Henkes et al. 2020
and including the mechanical effects of the multicellular actomyosin cable on finger formation
Jeong et al. 2023
;
Reffay et al. 2014
, we develop a cell-level computational model and a viscoelastic continuum theory of the advancing edge that both describe finger formation as the interplay between these correlated cell movements and the contractile actomyosin cable.

Using data from an MDCK wound healing assay imaged over large length scales and long times, we established that fingers slowly develop and reach a steady state, but without the growth of a characteristic length scale typical of a finite-wavelength instability.
The model and the continuum theory are able to quantitatively reproduce velocity correlations and finger properties observed in experiments.
Our findings, therefore, indicate that epithelial fingers are highly persistent fluctuations arising from active driving of the advancing edge by the interior of the monolayer.

Results - monolayer interior

To understand the origin and behaviour of the fingers, we studied both the properties of the cells in the bulk of a confluent epithelial monolayer and the behaviour of the advancing edge invading the free space. We begin by examining the behaviour in the bulk.

Experiments

We studied the velocity correlations and finger formation experimentally using Madin-Darby canine kidney (MDCK) cell monolayers (Methods).
After extracting the velocity field using Particle Image Velocimetry (PIV) on these images (SI Appendix, section

Active Vertex Model

We represent the cell sheet through a version of the vertex model
Nagai and Honda 2001
;
Farhadifar et al. 2007
;
Fletcher et al. 2014
, where the sheet is represented as a two-dimensional Voronoi tiling
Li and Sun 2014
;
Bi et al. 2016
;
Barton et al. 2017
, extended to include a dynamic monolayer edge.
The edge is modelled as a semi-flexible polymer under tension to mimic the contractile nature of the actomyosin cable(s).
It can dynamically grow and shrink, making it a good computational model to study finger formation. The total energy of our model can be written as the sum of the mechanical energy of cells in the bulk,
E_{\mathrm{VM}}
, with an area term of modulus
K_{A}
and a perimeter term of modulus
K_{P}
, see Eq. (
9
), and the deformation energy of the advancing edge,
E_{\textrm{edge}}
, with stretching modulus
k_{s}
and bending modulus
k_{b}
, see Eq. (
10
), giving in total
E_{\text{AVM}}=E_{\text{VM}}+E_{\text{edge}}
(see Methods for details).

The model is made active by adding self-propulsion to each cell. Although the individual crawling of epithelial cells in a confluent monolayer on a substrate is a complex process, involving cryptic lamellipodia
Farooqui and Fenteany 2005
, the observed velocity correlations in the sheet can be successfully captured by assuming that the cells crawl independently as simple Active Brownian Particles (ABPs)
Henkes et al. 2020
, with constant speed
v_{0}
in a direction set by the unit-length vector
\hat{\mathbf{n}}
that follows rotational diffusion and is uncorrelated between cells.
We extended the original AVM
Barton et al. 2017
to include internal dissipative effects in the form of pair-friction between cells, as a form of local viscosity. The importance of such effects was recently highlighted
Tong et al. 2023
;
Rozman et al. 2024
, and we show below that they are essential to reproduce the experimental spatial velocity correlations. Altogether, the resulting overdamped equation of motion is a force balance between dissipative, mechanical, and active forces, and reads

Origin of the bulk velocity correlations

To understand velocity correlations and reproduce them in the AVM, we used a model of the cell monolayer based on dense active matter
Caprini et al. 2020
;
Mandal et al. 2020
;
Keta et al. 2022
, and extended an existing continuum model
Henkes et al. 2020
with viscoelasticity
Szamel and Flenner 2021
. The overdamped equation of motion for the displacement field
\mathbf{u}(\mathbf{r},t)
is represented as a continuous elastic solid (SI Appendix, section
I
),

\displaystyle\zeta_{c}\dot{\mathbf{u}}=

\displaystyle B\nabla(\nabla\cdot\mathbf{u})+\mu\nabla^{2}\mathbf{u}+\zeta_{c}v_{0}\mathbf{\hat{n}}

(2)

\displaystyle+K\nabla(\nabla\cdot\dot{\mathbf{u}})+\eta\nabla^{2}\dot{\mathbf{u}},

where we identify the velocity
\mathbf{v}(\mathbf{r},t)\equiv\dot{\mathbf{u}}(\mathbf{r},t)
.
Here,
\zeta_{c}
is the substrate friction coefficient, and the monolayer is assumed to have elastic bulk and shear moduli
B
and
\mu
. Similar to the AVM, we assume that the cells move independently as simple ABPs. In continuum, this takes the form of an active driving field
\zeta_{c}v_{0}\mathbf{\hat{n}}(\mathbf{r},t)
, which is uncorrelated in space with a cutoff
a
at the cell scale, but persistent in time with persistence time
\tau
. The vector field
\hat{\mathbf{n}}(\mathbf{r},t)
follows the same stochastic dynamics as described by Eq. (
1b
).
In addition, we now also include viscous terms: a bulk viscosity
K
and a shear viscosity
\eta
that correspond to the pair friction coefficient
\zeta_{\text{pair}}
in the AVM. As we show below, they are essential to reproduce the experimental spatial and temporal velocity correlations.

Analysing Eq. (
2
) in spatio-temporal Fourier space
Henkes et al. 2020
(SI Appendix, section
I.A
) decouples the equation into longitudinal and transverse modes. Using the Lorentzian correlations of the driving field and then transforming back to real space, we obtain the
spatial (i.e. equal time) velocity correlation function for the viscoelastic isotropic continuum model,

\displaystyle\left\langle\mathbf{v}(\mathbf{r},0)\dotproduct\mathbf{v}(\mathbf{0},0)\right\rangle=\frac{v_{0}^{2}a^{2}}{4\pi}\frac{K_{0}(r/\xi_{\parallel,p})-K_{0}(r/\xi_{\parallel,d})}{\xi_{\parallel,p}^{2}-\xi_{\parallel,d}^{2}}

(3)

\displaystyle+\frac{v_{0}^{2}a^{2}}{4\pi}\frac{K_{0}(r/\xi_{\perp,p})-K_{0}(r/\xi_{\perp,d})}{\xi_{\perp,p}^{2}-\xi_{\perp,d}^{2}},

where
r\equiv|\mathbf{r}|
is the magnitude of vector
\mathbf{r}
, and
K_{0}
denotes the
0^{\mathrm{th}}
modified Bessel function of the second kind.
This decaying correlation function is composed of two contributions. The first is from the longitudinal modes, with characteristic length scales
\xi_{\parallel,p}=\sqrt{\left[(B+\mu)\tau+(K+\eta)\right]/\zeta_{c}}
from the persistent driving and
\xi_{\parallel,d}=\sqrt{\left(K+\eta\right)/\zeta_{c}}
from the substrate and internal dissipation.
The second contribution is from the transverse modes, also having two characteristic length scales

\xi_{\perp,p}=\sqrt{\left(\mu\tau+\eta\right)/\zeta_{c}}
from the driving and
\xi_{\perp,d}=\sqrt{\eta/\zeta_{c}}
from the dissipation.
By comparing Eq. (
3
) and Ref.
Henkes et al. 2020
we see that including bulk and shear viscosities
K
and
\eta
leads to the two extra hydrodynamic length scales
\xi_{\parallel,d}
and
\xi_{\perp,d}
, while modifying the two original length scales
\xi_{\parallel,p}
and
\xi_{\perp,p}
of active elastic origin which retain their dominant
\sqrt{\tau}
scaling.

We note that
K_{0}(r/\xi)\sim-\log(r/\xi)
in the limit
r\to 0
, which diverges. The differences as in Eq. (
3

r\to 0
.
Therefore, the role of internal dissipative effects is to regularise short-range correlations, which is instrumental to reproducing the smooth experimental velocity correlations. Likewise, AVM simulations without pair dissipation show large velocity fluctuations between neighbouring cells
Bi et al. 2016
;
Henkes et al. 2020
.

In summary, the effect of the elastic and viscous interactions between the cells is to turn the spatially
uncorrelated
, but persistent, driving from the crawling cells into a spatially
correlated
velocity field.

Results - finger formation

Having established that the AVM and continuum theory can both reproduce the experimentally observed velocity correlations in the bulk, we now focus on the behaviour of the advancing edge of the monolayer in the experiments and the AVM.
To do so, we extracted the contour of the advancing edge as a function of time from experiments (Fig.
1
and SI Appendix, sections
II
and
III.D
).
Consistent with other experimental results
Poujade et al. 2007
;
Vishwakarma et al. 2018
, the fingers developed over a few hours after the insert has been lifted, and cells were allowed to spread (Movies
S1
and
S2
). The fingers were persistent, i.e. they stayed in place relative to the mean border position for many hours (Fig.
3
a), although we did observe occasional retractions. We observed a wide variety in the width and amplitude of the fingers.

In the AVM, for sufficiently small values of the spring constant
k_{s}
, we obtained similar finger formation (Movies
S3
and
S4
, and Fig.
3

We will show later that including cell division and flattening in the AVM reproduces the average border progression observed in the experiments. The AVM simulations also showed the long lifetime of fingers and the variety of finger sizes.

We now compare simulated and measured advancing edges quantitatively by defining three boundary correlation functions, which allow us to constrain the remaining parameters of the AVM, the boundary spring constant

Polymer and height equation theory for finger formation

We seek to understand the origin of the fingers, and their properties as they
arise from the interplay between the bulk velocity fluctuations and the contractile edge of the cell monolayer.
Motivated by the actomyosin cable linking cells and its boundary implementation in the AVM, we model the edge as a semiflexible polymer driven by the motion of the cells of the interior, with overdamped equations including pair dissipation

\zeta\dot{\mathbf{r}}_{i}+\zeta_{\mathrm{pair}}(\dot{\mathbf{r}}_{i+1}+\dot{\mathbf{r}}_{i-1}-2\dot{\mathbf{r}}_{i})=-\nabla_{\mathbf{r}_{i}}E_{\mathrm{edge}}+\zeta\mathbf{v}^{f}_{i}.

(7)

Here
i
labels points in the polymer with periodic boundary conditions with horizontal length
L_{x}\!=\!$3000\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro m}$
, matched to the experiment, and where
\mathbf{v}^{f}_{i}
is the driving by the velocity field of the monolayer with correlations given by Eq. (
3
). We observed an excellent match between the active polymer description and the AVM simulations (Fig.
3
c and Fig.
4
c, f and i, and Movie
S6
). This shows that a simple driven model with no feedback instabilities is able to quantitatively reproduce finger formation. Note, however, that we had to fit a very small value of spring constant
k_{s}
, likely reflecting the effectively viscoelastic relaxation mechanism of the cable in both experiment and AVM (SI Appendix, section
II.D
).

To gain insight into the origin of finger length and time scales, we derive an equivalent continuum formulation starting from a (stretched) worm-like chain
Marko and Siggia 1995
;
van Saarloos et al. 2024
extended to include correlated active driving.
In the limit of small fluctuations and the absence of overhangs, the active polymer can be described by an equation of motion for its vertical height
h
along a horizontal direction
x
(SI Appendix, section
II
)),

\displaystyle\zeta_{h}\partial_{t}h(x,t)={}

\displaystyle\lambda\frac{\partial^{2}h(x,t)}{\partial x^{2}}-\kappa\frac{\partial^{4}h(x,t)}{\partial x^{4}}

(8)

\displaystyle+\eta_{h}\frac{\partial^{2}\partial_{t}h(x,t)}{\partial x^{2}}+\zeta_{h}v^{f}(x,t),

referred to below as the height equation, where we have assumed overdamped dynamics for the advancing edge with
friction coefficient
\zeta_{h}
. The line tension
\lambda
and bending stiffness
\kappa
in the worm-like chain contribute the first two terms. We also included pair-dissipation (viscosity
\eta_{h}
), like in
Khatri and McLeish 2007
. The boundary is driven by the stochastic velocity field
v^{f}(x,t)
from the cell movements in the interior, for which we derive an auxiliary one-dimensional equation based on the viscoelastic model (SI Appendix, section
II.D
). As with the two-dimensional continuum model for the velocity correlations, we can directly map the AVM parameters to the height equation parameters, except for again needing a much lower value for the line tension
\lambda
(SI Appendix, section
II.D
).

Simulations of the height equation exhibit boundary fluctuations (Fig.
3
c and Movie
S7
) that capture the length scales of experiment and simulations and persist over multiple hours, though by construction, not overhangs.
The height equation analytically reproduces the tangent-tangent correlation of the experiment and simulations (Fig.
4
c). We also observed a Fourier spectrum (Fig.
4
f) and relative roughness correlation (Fig.
4
i) that matches the AVM for length scales larger than
100\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro m}
.

We can understand the dominant length and time scales through analysis of the correlations of the linear theory (SI Appendix, section
III
, see Eq. (
S64
)). The tangent-tangent correlation contains five different pairs of poles on the imaginary axis at wave vectors
q_{i}
(Eq. (
S86
)), setting five length scales
\xi_{i}=2\pi/q_{i}
.
The largest and dominant of these length scales is for
q_{1}=\sqrt{\frac{\zeta}{\mu\tau+\eta}}
, the same as the shear length scale of the interior velocity correlations.
In the overdamped one-dimensional dynamics of the edge, mode
q
relaxes with a time scale
\tau_{q}\sim q^{-2}
. The finger lifetime is then dominated by relaxation at scale
q_{1}
, explaining their longevity.
In the height correlator, there is a leading
1/q^{2}
term that becomes dominant in the infinite time, infinite system size limit. The length scales (Fig.
4
f and i) are thus coarsening over the course of the experiment, albeit very slowly. In contrast, the tangent-tangent correlations reach a true steady state since
\hat{\bm{t}}\sim\bm{\nabla}h
, and so do not contain the
1/q^{2}
- in fact, their thermal limit is the strong force expansion of the WLC model
van Saarloos et al. 2024
.

There is a notable difference between the height equation and the two-dimensional active polymer simulations (Fig.
4
f and i). It emerges at short length scales (i.e. high values of the wavevector
q
) and increases with time, with the polymer but not the height equation matching the AVM and with the experiment resembling the longest times.
We attribute this discrepancy to nonlinear effects stemming from the coupling, at large deformations, between height and transverse fluctuations along the stretchable polymer chain. It will be interesting to classify this nonlinearity, which does not appear to be the KPZ term
Kardar et al. 1986
and may instead be effectively thermal
Caballero et al. 2022
.
As the experiment does not start from a perfectly straight boundary (Fig.
3
a and SI Appendix, Fig.
S12
), these effects appear immediately (see Fig.
S6
for simulations with initial roughness). It is responsible for shifting the short-time roughness exponent from approximately
2
to
1
, consistent with previous observations
Rapin et al. 2021
.

Cell proliferation and flattening

The finger formation can be thought of as a roughening effect of the advancing edge in addition to the average expansion of the sheet. Previous experiments showed
Poujade et al. 2007
that inhibiting cell division did not abolish finger formation for
t<$10\text{\,}\mathrm{h}$
, indicating that proliferation is not the driving factor. This is consistent with our base model. However, it is important to determine whether the interface is affected by the effects of proliferation over longer time scales.
To this end, we included stochastic cell division in the AVM. Cells grow by slowly increasing their preferred area
A_{i,0}(t)
over time, with
A_{i,0}
at time
t=$0\text{\,}\mathrm{h}$
set to vary slightly between cells to prevent synchronised division (Methods). The division rate scales with
A_{i}(t)-A_{i,0}
where
A_{i}(t)
is the cell’s actual area
Bell and Anderson 1967
;
Barton et al. 2017
. This leads to a tissue with a uniform growth rate and spatially randomly distributed divisions.

An additional process that has to be considered in this context is cell flattening, which occurs immediately after the barrier is released.
This response occurs right after the removal of the containment, during which the cells lose height and expand in area
Serra-Picamal et al. 2012
.
We model it in the AVM by changing the initial dimensions of the cell strip, so that the initial average cell area was
\overline{A}(t=0)=0.90A_{0}
. This puts the tissue initially in a slightly compressed state, consistent with observations in tissues and cultured epithelial cells
Eisenhoffer and Rosenblatt 2013
;
Zulueta-Coarasa and Rosenblatt 2022
, causing the initial cells to expand in the first few hours after barrier removal.

The expanding AVM (Movie
S5
) matches well to the experimental mean border progression for the first
5\text{\,}\mathrm{h}
(Fig.
5
c), with contributions from both cell expansion and division (Fig.
5
b). It then starts to deviate, as after this time, the experiments reveal decreased border progression, which we do not reproduce. A more detailed model of cell division and spreading is needed to capture this crossover. Importantly, the properties of the interface (tangent-tangent correlations, spectrum and roughness) remain quantitatively consistent with the simulations without division (SI Appendix, Fig.
S8
).

The addition of the two mechanisms also produces a velocity gradient towards the edge of the tissue in the AVM (SI Appendix, section
I.C
).
This causes the temporal velocity autocorrelation in
2
a to develop a heavier tail at long times, a trend similar to that observed in the experiments (SI Appendix, Fig.
S2
).

Discussion and Conclusion

Here we have shown how correlated cell movements can drive finger formation at the boundary of cell sheets.
Using experiments on epithelial MDCK monolayers, as well as simulations of the Active Vertex Model (AVM) calibrated to reproduce their collective behaviour, we showed that finger formation on the boundary reaches a steady state and leads to long-lived fingers of a variety of sizes.

Our results do not support the existence of a finite-wavelength instability in finger formation. Instead, we reproduced experiments and simulations quantitatively by combining the space and time correlated collective cell motion in the interior with the contractile effect of the multicellular contractile actomyosin cable surrounding the tissue.
The characteristic length and time scales of the fingers are controlled by the stiffness of the tissue and the contractility of the actomyosin cable, as well as by the persistence time of active cell crawling.

It puts our results into a similar class of active interfaces as the active nematic filaments of
Adkins et al. 2022
;
Zhao et al. 2024
, but it remains to be seen how it relates to other active interface problems
Cates and Nardini 2025
.

Our model makes the experimental prediction that finger length and time scales are directly set by the spatiotemporal correlations of the interior and the actomyosin cable, and their growth rate by the activity. As has been highlighted before, the internal correlation length is sensitive to density
Garcia et al. 2015
and changes in the substrate stiffness
Vazquez et al. 2022
, in the latter case also directly affecting the finger length scale. In our fitted model, the hydrodynamic length scale
\xi_{\perp,d}=\sqrt{\eta/\zeta_{c}}
and which has been linked to the correlation length
Garcia et al. 2015
;
Vazquez et al. 2022
, is only approximately two cell diameters. Ultimately, we predict that modifications that change either cell-cell adhesion
K_{P}
, either friction coefficient, crawling motility
v_{0}
, or the properties of the actomyosin cable
\lambda
will changes both interior and finger length and time scales according to our theory, dominated by the active elastic length scale
\xi_{\perp,p}=\sqrt{(\mu\tau+\eta)/\zeta_{c}}
.

At this point, the question arises how our results connect to the many feedback mechanisms that have been observed experimentally (notably leader cells
Vishwakarma et al. 2018
and plithotaxis), and proposed theoretically (e.g. curvature enhanced motility
Mark et al. 2010
). Our model can be considered as a fluctuation-driven
null model
, i.e. the basic phenomenology arising from
only
uncorrelated motility and viscoelastic mechanics. The critical conclusion is that an unexpectedly large proportion of experimental observations are well explained by such a simple model. We hypothesise that additional feedback, acting on the background of the uncorrelated driving, is responsible for the
details
of the phenomenology observed. For example, leader cells arise at the tip of forming fingers from the local mechanical environment
Vishwakarma et al. 2018
and may then amplify their formation.

Methods

Experiments

Madin-Darby canine kidney (MDCK) cells were seeded into Ibidi two-well culture inserts
ibi ny
which had been attached to TTP 40mm
\oslash
Tissue Culture Petri Dishes (93040T). Each well had a 0.22
{\mathrm{cm}}^{2}
surface area. A 70
\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro L}
cell suspension was added to each so that the cells were fully confluent after an overnight incubation. To initiate cell movement and create an empty space, i.e. “wound”, the insert was removed, leaving cells free to spread (Fig.
1
a). To record the moving edge, 5 overlapping fields of view along each free edge were imaged every 5 minutes for 72
\mathrm{h}
– this was to ensure the cells could be observed for the maximum time and had reached and gone past the edge of the field of view. Consequently, the analysis was over a shorter period (usually 40
\mathrm{h}
) and included only the time points when the edges were in field of view. The 5 fields of view were stitched together post-acquisition. The recording microscope was a Nikon TiE with a Nikon Plan Fluor ELWD 20x NA0.45 Ph1 ADL objective (using phase contrast). Images were recorded with Photometrics Cascade II 1K camera. Heating to 37
\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolCelsius}
and 5%
\mathrm{CO_{2}}
during the recording were provided by an Okolab incubation system.

AVM simulations

We extended the Active Vertex Model (AVM)
Barton et al. 2017
, a variation of the self-propelled Voronoi model
Li and Sun 2014
;
Bi et al. 2016
with a dynamic cell sheet boundary.
This model represents a confluent epithelial cell monolayer by a two-dimensional polygonal Voronoi tiling, with cell centres located on the vertices of the dual Delaunay triangulation, and it is part of the wider class of vertex models (VM)
Nagai and Honda 2001
;
Farhadifar et al. 2007
;
Fletcher et al. 2014
.
Cells in the interior experience the standard vertex model potential

E_{\mathrm{VM}}=\sum_{\mathrm{C}=1}^{N_{\mathrm{C}}}\left[\frac{K_{A}}{2}(A_{\mathrm{C}}-A_{0})^{2}+\frac{K_{P}}{2}(P_{\mathrm{C}}-P_{0})^{2}\right],

(9)

modelling elastic cell-cell interactions with area modulus
K_{A}
(cell volume incompressibility) and perimeter modulus
K_{P}
(cell-cell adhesion and cell contractility), as well as target (i.e. preferred) area
A_{0}
and target (i.e. preferred) perimeter
P_{0}
. As in the original AVM
Barton et al. 2017
, we add a short-range soft core repulsive potential to avoid problems with highly obtuse triangles in the dual Delaunay triangulation.

The advancing edge of the cell monolayer acts as a semi-flexible polymer under contraction, surrounding the tissue and closing the finite Voronoi tesselation. Its energy is

E_{\mathrm{edge}}=\sum_{i}\frac{k_{s}}{2}\left|\mathbf{r}_{i+1}-\mathbf{r}_{i}\right|^{2}+\sum_{i}\frac{k_{b}}{2}(\theta_{i}-\pi)^{2},

(10)

where index
i
labels all points on the advancing edge,
a
is the rest length of the bond connecting two consecutive boundary points, and
\theta_{i}
is the angle between the two bonds meeting at the boundary point
i
.
k_{s}
and
k_{b}
are corresponding stiffnesses (i.e. spring constants). The inclusion of the angular springs is motivated by numerical stability, and
k_{b}
is taken to be a constant sufficiently strong to prevent problems with the dual Delaunay triangulation.

To include the dynamics into the model, we assume that cells crawl independently as simple Active Brownian Particles (ABPs)
Henkes et al. 2020
. Model cells crawl at a constant speed
v_{0}
in a direction that follows rotational diffusion
i.e.
\mathbf{F}_{i}^{\mathrm{act}}=\zeta v_{0}\hat{\mathbf{n}}_{i}
in which
\zeta
is the substrate friction coefficient and
\mathbf{\hat{n}}_{i}=\cos(\phi_i)\hat{\mathbf{x}}+\sin(\phi_i)\hat{\mathbf{y}}
is the polarity vector of cell
i
. Angle
\phi_{i}
follows rotational diffusion,
\dot{\phi_{i}}=\sqrt{2/\tau}\,\eta_{i}
, where
\eta_{i}
is a Gaussian white noise with zero mean
\langle\eta_{i}(t)\rangle=0
and unit variance
\langle\eta_{i}(t)\eta_{j}(0)\rangle=\delta_{ij}\,\delta(t)
, where
\tau
is the persistence time.

We extended the original AVM to include internal dissipative effects, resulting in the equation of motion (
1
). The dynamics of the cell sheet are obtained by integrating over time using the Euler-Maruyama method. The internal dissipation mixes velocities on the left-hand side in Eq. (
1a
), which requires solving a linear system of equations in the form
\sum_{j}M_{ij}\mathbf{v}_{j}=\mathbf{F}_{i}
in each time step. The symmetric matrix
M_{ij}
is sparse, and the system can be efficiently solved using the Eigen3 library
Guennebaud et al. 2010
.
The boundary points follow the same equation of motion but without self-propulsion, and the boundary can dynamically grow and shrink, making the boundary effectively viscoelastic
Barton et al. 2017
.

The initial condition for the non-dividing AVM simulations is a rectangular box of
3400\times 650

{\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro m}}^{2}
. We place 12,861 cells and 1,620 boundary points so that the average area of the cells is
171\text{\,}{\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro m}}^{2}
. The boundary points are placed along the edge of the box with a spacing of
5\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro m}
. The cells are placed randomly inside the box and then relaxed by using Brownian dynamics with a soft-core repulsive potential to set the initial Voronoi seeds. The subsequent AVM simulation is run for 40 hours with a time step of 0.0001 h. The results for the non-dividing AVM are the result of 31 independent simulations with different seeds for both the initialisation and AVM simulation steps.

We ran 10 independent expanding AVM simulations of initial size
2000\times 650

{\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro m}}^{2}
, containing initially 8399 cells and 1069 boundary points spaced
5\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro m}
, such that the initial cell area was on average
154

{\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro m}}^{2}
. The threshold area for each cell to divide was set to its initial, compressed, area. The growth and division rates, as defined in
Barton et al. 2017
, are set to match the experimental border progression (Fig.
5
c).

Appendix III
Theory

I
Velocity field of the interior

I.A. Derivation of bulk velocity field

The velocity field can be derived by modelling the cell sheet as an active (visco)elastic solid.
We follow the derivation
Henkes et al. 2020
and add pair dissipation.
The equation of motion for the displacement field
\bm{u}
within the cell sheet reads

\displaystyle\zeta\bm{\dot{u}}(\bm{r},t)=\zeta v_{0}\bm{\hat{n}}(\bm{r},t)+\nabla\cdot\bm{\overleftrightarrow{\sigma}},

(S1)

where
\zeta
is a friction coefficient, which we write without the
c
subscript of the main text for ease of notation,
\zeta v_{0}\hat{\bm{n}}
is a self-propulsion force field with
v_{0}
a self-propulsion velocity.

For an isotropic material, we associate a bulk modulus
B
to uniform expansion and a shear modulus
\mu
to shear deformation, hence we write the elastic part of the stress tensor as

\displaystyle\sigma_{\alpha\beta}^{E}=B\delta_{\alpha\beta}\varepsilon_{\gamma\gamma}+2\mu\left(\varepsilon_{\alpha\beta}-\frac{1}{d}\delta_{\alpha\beta}\varepsilon_{\gamma\gamma}\right),

(S2)

where the (linear) strain tensor is given by
\varepsilon_{\alpha\beta}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\partial_{\beta}u_{\alpha}+\partial_{\alpha}u_{\beta}\right)
.

Similarly, we write a viscous contribution to the stress tensor with bulk viscosity
K
and shear viscosity
\eta
,

\displaystyle\sigma_{\alpha\beta}^{V}=K\delta_{\alpha\beta}\dot{\gamma}_{\gamma\gamma}+2\eta\left(\dot{\gamma}_{\alpha\beta}-\frac{1}{d}\delta_{\alpha\beta}\dot{\gamma}_{\gamma\gamma}\right),

(S3)

with the strain rate tensor
\dot{\gamma}_{\alpha\beta}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\partial_{\beta}\dot{u}_{\alpha}+\partial_{\alpha}\dot{u}_{\beta}\right)
. Adding
\nabla\cdot\bm{\overleftrightarrow{\sigma}}=\nabla\cdot\bm{\overleftrightarrow{\sigma}}^{E}+\nabla\cdot\bm{\overleftrightarrow{\sigma}}^{V}

\zeta\dot{\bm{u}}(\bm{r},t)=\zeta v_{0}\hat{\bm{n}}(\bm{r},t)+B\nabla(\nabla\cdot\bm{u}(\bm{r},t))+\mu\nabla^{2}\bm{u}(\bm{r},t)+K\nabla(\nabla\cdot\dot{\bm{u}}(\bm{r},t))+\eta\nabla^{2}\dot{\bm{u}}(\bm{r},t).

(S4)

We introduce the Fourier transform

\displaystyle\bm{U}(\bm{q},\omega)

\displaystyle=\int\differential t\,e^{\mathrm{i}\omega t}\tilde{\bm{u}}(\bm{q},t)=\int\differential\bm{r}\int\differential t\,e^{\mathrm{i}\bm{q}\cdot\bm{r}+\mathrm{i}\omega t}\,\bm{u}(\bm{r},t),

(S5a)

\displaystyle\bm{u}(\bm{r},t)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{2}}\int\differential\bm{q}\,e^{-\mathrm{i}\bm{q}\cdot\bm{r}}\,\tilde{\bm{u}}(\bm{q},t)=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{3}}\int\differential\bm{q}\,e^{-\mathrm{i}\bm{q}\cdot\bm{r}}\int\differential\omega\,e^{-\mathrm{i}\bm{q}\cdot\bm{r}-\mathrm{i}\omega t}\bm{U}(\bm{q},\omega),

(S5b)

and express Eq. (
S4
) in the spatiotemporal Fourier space

\displaystyle-\mathrm{i}\omega\zeta\bm{U}(\bm{q},\omega)=\zeta v_{0}\hat{\bm{N}}(\bm{q},\omega)-(B-\mathrm{i}\omega K)\left[\bm{U}(\bm{q},\omega)\cdot\bm{q}\right]\bm{q}-(\mu-\mathrm{i}\omega\eta)\left[\bm{q}\cdot\bm{q}\right]\bm{U}(\bm{q},\omega).

(S6)

We introduce
\bm{U}_{\parallel}=(\bm{U}\cdot\hat{\bm{q}})\hat{\bm{q}}
and
\bm{U}_{\perp}=\bm{U}-\bm{U}_{\parallel}
the longitudinal and transverse components of
\bm{U}
, where
\hat{\bm{q}}=\bm{q}/q
and
q=|\bm{q}|
, then rewrite Eq. (
S6
)

\displaystyle-\mathrm{i}\omega\zeta\begin{bmatrix}\bm{U}_{\parallel}\\
\bm{U}_{\perp}\end{bmatrix}=\zeta v_{0}\begin{bmatrix}\hat{\bm{N}}_{\parallel}\\
\hat{\bm{N}}_{\perp}\end{bmatrix}-\begin{bmatrix}(B+\mu-\mathrm{i}\omega(K+\eta))q^{2}&0\\
0&(\mu-\mathrm{i}\omega\eta)q^{2}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\bm{U}_{\parallel}\\
\bm{U}_{\perp}\end{bmatrix}.

(S7)

This system of equations is decoupled for longitudinal and transverse components. We thus write

\displaystyle\bm{U}_{\parallel}(\bm{q},\omega)

\displaystyle=\frac{\zeta v_{0}\bm{N}_{\parallel}(\bm{q},\omega)}{\left[B+\mu\right]q^{2}-\left[K+\eta\right]\mathrm{i}\omega q^{2}-\mathrm{i}\omega\zeta},

(S8a)

\displaystyle\bm{U}_{\perp}(\bm{q},\omega)

\displaystyle=\frac{\zeta v_{0}\bm{N}_{\perp}(\bm{q},\omega)}{\mu q^{2}-\mathrm{i}\eta\omega q^{2}-\mathrm{i}\omega\zeta}.

(S8b)

I.B. Temporal autocorrelation function

We derive the temporal autocorrelation function. We first focus on the longitudinal component, and will infer the transverse component by identification while setting
B=0
and
K=0
.

We define the longitudinal velocity temporal autocorrelation function

\left\langle\bm{v}_{\parallel}(\bm{r},t)\cdot\bm{v}_{\parallel}(\bm{r},t^{\prime})\right\rangle=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{6}}\iiiint\differential\bm{q}\,\differential\bm{q^{\prime}}\,\differential\omega\,\differential\omega^{\prime}\,(-\omega\omega^{\prime})\left\langle\bm{U}_{\parallel}(\bm{q},\omega)\cdot\bm{U}_{\parallel}(\bm{q^{\prime}},\omega^{\prime})\right\rangle e^{-\mathrm{i}(\bm{q}+\bm{q^{\prime}})\cdot\boldsymbol{r}}e^{-\mathrm{i}\omega t}e^{-\mathrm{i}\omega^{\prime}t^{\prime}},

(S9)

and note that
Henkes et al. 2020

\displaystyle\left\langle\hat{\bm{N}}_{\parallel}(\bm{q},\omega)\hat{\bm{N}}_{\parallel}(\bm{q^{\prime}},\omega^{\prime})\right\rangle=\frac{1}{2}\left\langle\hat{\bm{N}}(\bm{q},\omega)\hat{\bm{N}}(\bm{q^{\prime}},\omega^{\prime})\right\rangle=(2\pi)^{3}a^{2}\frac{\tau}{1+\omega^{2}\tau^{2}}\delta(\bm{q}+\bm{q^{\prime}})\delta(\omega+\omega^{\prime}),

(S10)

where

\displaystyle\left\langle\bm{v}_{\parallel}(\bm{r},t)\cdot\bm{v}_{\parallel}(\bm{r},t^{\prime})\right\rangle=\frac{a^{2}\zeta^{2}v_{0}^{2}\tau}{(2\pi)^{3}}\iint\differential\bm{q}\,\differential\omega\,F(\bm{q},\omega)\,e^{-\mathrm{i}\omega(t-t^{\prime})},

(S11)

with

\displaystyle F(\bm{q},\omega)

\displaystyle=\frac{\omega^{2}}{\left[B+\mu\right]^{2}q^{4}+\left(\left[K+\eta\right]q^{2}+\zeta\right)^{2}\omega^{2}}\frac{1}{1+\tau^{2}\omega^{2}}=\frac{\omega^{2}}{\frac{\zeta^{2}}{\tau^{2}}\Big[\frac{(B+\mu)^{2}\tau^{2}}{\zeta^{2}}q^{4}+\left(\frac{K+\eta}{\zeta}q^{2}+1\right)\tau^{2}\omega^{2}\Big]\Big[1+\tau^{2}\omega^{2}\Big]}

(S12)

where we have introduced the following longitudinal length scales,

\displaystyle\xi_{\parallel,p}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{(B+\mu)\tau+K+\eta}{\zeta}},

(S13a)

\displaystyle\xi_{\parallel,d}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{K+\eta}{\zeta}}.

(S13b)

We use the following relations between the Lorentzian and the exponential via the Fourier transform

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\differential t\,e^{\mathrm{i}\omega t}\,e^{-|t|/\tau}

\displaystyle=\frac{2\tau}{1+\tau^{2}\omega^{2}},

(S14a)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\differential\omega\,e^{-\mathrm{i}\omega t}\frac{1}{1+\tau^{2}\omega^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\tau}e^{-|t|/\tau},

(S14b)

therefore we take the inverse Fourier transform in time in Eq. (
S11

\displaystyle\left\langle\bm{v}_{\parallel}(\bm{r},t)\cdot\bm{v}_{\parallel}(\bm{r},t^{\prime})\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{a^{2}v_{0}^{2}}{2(2\pi)^{2}}\int\differential\bm{q}\,\frac{(\xi_{\parallel,p}^{2}-\xi_{\parallel,d}^{2})q^{2}\,e^{-\frac{(\xi_{\parallel,p}^{2}-\xi_{\parallel,d}^{2})q^{2}}{(\xi_{\parallel,d}^{2}q^{2}+1)\tau}|t-t^{\prime}|}-(\xi_{\parallel,d}^{2}q^{2}+1)\,e^{-|t-t^{\prime}|/\tau}}{\Big[(\xi_{\parallel,p}^{2}-\xi_{\parallel,d}^{2})^{2}q^{4}-(\xi_{\parallel,d}^{2}q^{2}+1)^{2}\Big]\Big[\xi_{\parallel,d}^{2}q^{2}+1\Big]}

(S15)

where we have introduced

\zeta_{q,\parallel}=\zeta+(K+\eta)q^{2}.

(S16)

For

II
Derivation of the height equation

This document derives the height equation theory for the cell sheet boundary. The motivation for studying the height equation is on the one hand to validate the hypothesis that correlated cell movements driving the contractile boundary leads to finger formation and then to lay out the relation between the different time scales (
e.g.
the short persistence time of cell driving and the long persistence time of fingers) and length scales. On the other hand, this study aims to provide quantitative comparisons between experiment, AVM, and theory.

The latter goal constrains the setup of the height equation.
In the experiments and AVM simulations, we only have access to a piece of the boundary, namely, a piece of horizontal length
L_{x}
. The idea is to treat this piece of the boundary as a worm-like chain of horizontal length
L_{x}
with periodic boundary conditions.

For the sake of clarity, we first quote the equation of motion in time
t
for the single-valued vertical height
h
of the boundary as a function of the horizontal direction
x
,

\zeta_{h}\partial_{t}h(x,t)=\lambda\partial_{x}^{2}h(x,t)-\kappa\partial_{x}^{4}h(x,t)+\eta_{h}\partial_{x}^{2}\partial_{t}h(x,t)+\zeta_{h}v^{f}(x,t),

(S23)

referred to below as the height equation, and then explain the meaning of each term.
We assume overdamped dynamics for the boundary with friction coefficient
\zeta_{h}
. The line tension
\lambda
and bending stiffness
\kappa
contribute the first two terms on the right-hand side and these terms will be derived from a worm-like chain in section
II.A
. Motivated by the bulk dynamics and the AVM model, we also add pair-dissipation (through viscosity
\eta_{h}
) to the height equation. The boundary is driven by the stochastic velocity field
v^{f}(x,t)
from the bulk of the cell sheet. The characterisation of this stochastic velocity field by its correlation functions can be found in section
I
.

II.A. Worm-like chain contribution

We consider a worm-like chain model of a polymer, parametrise the position along the polymer with the arc length
s
, and write its free energy
van Saarloos et al. 2024
as

E_{\text{uns}}=\int_{0}^{L}\differential s\,\frac{1}{2}\kappa\left(\partial_{s}^{2}\bm{r}\right)^{2},

(S24)

where
L
is the total length of the polymer and
\kappa
its bending stiffness.

We stretch the polymer to a horizontal length
L_{x}
. This global constraint
Byron and Fuller 2012
can be written as

\int_{0}^{L}\differential s\,\partial_{s}\bm{r}\cdot\bm{\hat{x}}=L_{x},

(S25)

and can be enforced by adding it to the free energy with a Lagrange multiplier
\lambda

E=\int_{0}^{L}\differential s\,\frac{1}{2}\kappa\left(\partial_{s}^{2}\bm{r}\right)^{2}-\lambda\left(\int_{0}^{L}\differential s\,\partial_{s}\bm{r}\cdot\bm{\hat{x}}-L_{x}\right).

(S26)

We are interested in the fluctuations with respect to an initial horizontal configuration, i.e.
\bm{r}(s,t=0)=x(s)\bm{\hat{x}}
. We therefore decompose
\bm{r}(s)
into a parallel (along
x
-axis) and a perpendicular (along
y
-axis) component
Liverpool 2003

\bm{r}(s)=(s-r_{\parallel}(s))\bm{\hat{x}}+r_{\perp}(s)\bm{\hat{y}},

(S27)

which also yields

\displaystyle\partial_{s}\bm{r}(s)

\displaystyle=(1-\partial_{s}r_{\parallel})\bm{\hat{x}}+\partial_{s}r_{\perp}\bm{\hat{y}},

(S28a)

\displaystyle\partial_{s}^{2}\bm{r}(s)

\displaystyle=-\partial_{s}^{2}r_{\parallel}\bm{\hat{x}}+\partial_{s}^{2}r_{\perp}\bm{\hat{y}}.

(S28b)

Using this decomposition, the free energy Eq. (
S26
) takes the form

E=\int_{0}^{L}\differential s\,\left(\frac{\kappa}{2}\left[(\partial_{s}^{2}r_{\parallel})^{2}+(\partial_{s}^{2}r_{\perp})^{2}\right]-\lambda(1-\partial_{s}r_{\parallel})\right)+\lambda L_{x},

(S29)

which is the free energy of the polymer only if
s
is the arc length. This requirement constrains the fluctuations as the tangent vector has to be of unit norm:

(1-\partial_{s}r_{\parallel})^{2}+(\partial_{s}r_{\perp})^{2}=1.

(S30)

II.B. Limit of small fluctuations

Note that Eq. (
S30
) is a
local
constraint and generally allows expressing one of the degrees of freedom in terms of the others, reducing the total number of degrees of freedom
Byron and Fuller 2012
. In the limit of small fluctuations, this is straightforward to do by solving the constraint equation for
\partial_{s}r_{\parallel}
,

\partial_{s}r_{\parallel}=1-\sqrt{1-(\partial_{s}r_{\perp})^{2}}=\frac{1}{2}(\partial_{s}r_{\perp})^{2}+\frac{1}{8}(\partial_{s}r_{\perp})^{4}+\mathcal{O}((\partial_{s}r_{\perp})^{6}),

(S31)

and then approximating
\partial_{s}r_{\parallel}\approx\frac{1}{2}(\partial_{s}r_{\perp})^{2}
.

In the limit of small fluctuation, we thus obtain, dropping the constant term
\lambda L_{x}
, the following expression for the free energy

E=\int_{0}^{L}\differential s\,\left(\frac{\kappa}{2}(\partial_{s}^{2}r_{\perp})^{2}+\frac{\lambda}{2}(\partial_{s}r_{\perp})^{2}-\lambda\right)\equiv\int_{0}^{L}\differential s\,f(s,\partial_{s}r_{\perp},\partial_{s}^{2}r_{\perp}).

(S32)

Passive forces are then given by the variation of this free energy

\frac{\delta E}{\delta r_{\perp}}=\frac{\partial f}{\partial r_{\perp}}-\frac{\differential}{\differential s}\left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial\partial_{s}r_{\perp}}\right)+\frac{\differential^{2}}{\differential s^{2}}\left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial\partial_{s}^{2}r_{\perp}}\right)=-\lambda\partial_{s}^{2}r_{\perp}+\kappa\partial_{s}^{4}r_{\perp}.

(S33)

Note that we used here that
\lambda
is constant.
In the absence of overhangs, we can write
s(x)
, and in the limit of small fluctuations,
\partial_{s}x=1-\partial_{s}r_{\parallel}
, so that higher order derivatives with respect to
s
can be approximated as derivatives with respect to
x
. Thus, after identifying
h(x,t)\equiv r_{\perp}(x,t)
, we finally obtain

\frac{\delta E}{\delta h}=-\lambda\partial_{x}^{2}h(x,t)+\kappa\partial_{x}^{4}h(x,t).

(S34)

II.C. From 2d to 1d

We consider that the bulk dynamics generate the driving fluctuating velocity field
v^{f}
of the boundary.
In the case of a flat boundary,
e.g.
oriented in the direction of
\hat{\bm{x}}
, we would expect these fluctuations to have the following spectrum

\left\langle V^{f}(q,\omega)V^{f}(q^{\prime},\omega^{\prime})\right\rangle=\iiiint\differential x\,\differential t\,\differential x^{\prime}\,\differential t^{\prime}\,e^{\mathrm{i}qx+\mathrm{i}\omega t}\,e^{\mathrm{i}q^{\prime}x^{\prime}+\mathrm{i}\omega^{\prime}t^{\prime}}\,\left\langle(\bm{v}(\bm{r},t)\cdot\hat{\bm{y}})(\bm{v}(\bm{r}+x\hat{\bm{x}},t^{\prime})\cdot\hat{\bm{y}})\right\rangle.

(S35)

We introduce
\theta
such that
\bm{q}=q\cos\theta\,\hat{\bm{x}}+q\sin\theta\,\hat{\bm{y}}
, then we write the above velocity correlations

\displaystyle\left\langle(\bm{v}(\bm{r},t)\cdot\hat{\bm{y}})(\bm{v}(\bm{r}+x\hat{\bm{x}},t^{\prime})\cdot\hat{\bm{y}})\right\rangle

(S36)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{4}}\iint\differential\bm{q}\,\differential\bm{q}^{\prime}\,e^{-\mathrm{i}\bm{q}\cdot\bm{r}}e^{-\mathrm{i}\bm{q}^{\prime}\cdot(\bm{r}+x\hat{\bm{x}})}\,\left[\left\langle\tilde{\bm{v}}_{\parallel}(\bm{q},t)\cdot\tilde{\bm{v}}_{\parallel}(\bm{q}^{\prime},t^{\prime})\right\rangle\cos^{2}\theta+\left\langle\tilde{\bm{v}}_{\perp}(\bm{q},t)\cdot\tilde{\bm{v}}_{\perp}(\bm{q}^{\prime},t^{\prime})\right\rangle\sin^{2}\theta\right],

where we have assumed
\left\langle\tilde{\bm{v}}_{\parallel}(\bm{q},t)\cdot\tilde{\bm{v}}_{\perp}(\bm{q}^{\prime},t^{\prime})\right\rangle=0
.
Given the derivations of sections
I.C
and
I.B
, we write the spectra as following

\displaystyle\left\langle\tilde{\bm{v}}_{\parallel}(\bm{q},t)\cdot\tilde{\bm{v}}_{\parallel}(\bm{q}^{\prime},t^{\prime})\right\rangle

\displaystyle=G_{\parallel}(q,|t-t^{\prime}|)\,\delta(\bm{q}+\bm{q}^{\prime}),

(S37a)

\displaystyle\left\langle\tilde{\bm{v}}_{\perp}(\bm{q},t)\cdot\tilde{\bm{v}}_{\perp}(\bm{q}^{\prime},t^{\prime})\right\rangle

\displaystyle=G_{\perp}(q,|t-t^{\prime}|)\,\delta(\bm{q}+\bm{q}^{\prime}),

(S37b)

such that we may write Eq. (
S36
) as

\displaystyle\left\langle(\bm{v}(\bm{r},t)\cdot\hat{\bm{y}})(\bm{v}(\bm{r}+x\hat{\bm{x}},t^{\prime})\cdot\hat{\bm{y}})\right\rangle=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{4}}\iint\differential q\,q\,\differential\theta\,e^{-\mathrm{i}qx\cos\theta}\left[G_{\parallel}(q,|t-t^{\prime}|)\cos^{2}\theta+G_{\perp}(q,|t-t^{\prime}|)\sin^{2}\theta\right]

(S38)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2(2\pi)^{3}}\int\differential q\,q\,\left[G_{\parallel}(q,|t-t^{\prime}|)\,\big(J_{0}(|qx|)+J_{2}(|qx|)\big)+G_{\perp}(q,|t-t^{\prime}|)\,\big(J_{0}(|qx|)-J_{2}(|qx|)\big)\right],

where we have used

\int_{0}^{2\pi}\differential\theta e^{-\mathrm{i}qx\cos\theta}\cos^{2}\theta=\frac{1}{2}2\pi(J_{0}(|qx|)+J_{2}(|qx|)),

(S39)

with
J_{2}
the 2
nd
Bessel function of the first kind.
Crucially, Eq. (
S38
) shows that

\left\langle(\bm{v}(\bm{r},t)\cdot\hat{\bm{y}})(\bm{v}(\bm{r}+x\hat{\bm{x}},t^{\prime})\cdot\hat{\bm{y}})\right\rangle\neq\left\langle\bm{v}_{\perp}(\bm{r},t)\cdot\bm{v}_{\perp}(\bm{r}+x\hat{\bm{x}},t^{\prime})\right\rangle=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{3}}\int\differential q\ q\,G_{\perp}(q,|t-t^{\prime}|)\,J_{0}(|qx|),

(S40)

therefore we cannot directly use the fluctuations of the 2d bulk velocity field to inform 1d boundary fluctuations.

Therefore, as a matter a simplicity, we will consider that the driving fluctuating velocity field
v^{f}
is generated by the following chain model

\zeta v^{f}(x,t)=\zeta\partial_{t}y(x,t)=\mu\partial_{x}^{2}y(x,t)+\eta\partial_{x}^{2}\partial_{t}y(x,t)+\zeta v_{0}n_{y}(x,t).

(S41)

In this equation,
\zeta v_{0}n_{y}(x,t)
is the
y
-component of the ABP active driving field in
Henkes et al. 2020
. It is correlated in time, and uncorrelated in space

\displaystyle\left\langle n_{y}(x,t)n_{y}(x^{\prime},t^{\prime})\right\rangle=\frac{1}{2}a\,\delta(x-x^{\prime})\,e^{-|t-t^{\prime}|/\tau},

(S42)

with
a
a coarse-graining length equivalent to the lattice spacing (see the supplementary of
Henkes et al. 2020
for more details).

We write Eq. (
S41
) in
(q,\omega)
-space

-\mathrm{i}\zeta\omega Y(q,\omega)=-\mu q^{2}Y(q,\omega)+\mathrm{i}\eta\omega q^{2}Y(q,\omega)+\zeta v_{0}N_{y}(q,\omega),

(S43)

which yields

V^{f}(q,\omega)=-\mathrm{i}\omega Y(q,\omega)=\frac{-\mathrm{i}\omega\zeta v_{0}N_{y}(q,\omega)}{\mu q^{2}-\mathrm{i}\eta\omega q^{2}-\mathrm{i}\zeta\omega}.

(S44)

II.D. Mapping AVM boundary to height equation + noise-generating chain

Here, we explain the mapping of the AVM boundary parameters to the height equation + noise-generating chain parameters. We do so by coarse-graining the boundary polymer of the AVM to the height equation. For simplicity, we only consider the forces between the boundary points and assume that the interaction with the interior can be modelled by the advection with the velocity field of the interior.
We define the position vector of each boundary point by

\bm{r}_{i}
, where
i
runs from 1 to
N
, the number of boundary points. The link/bond vectors are defined as
\bm{R}_{i}=\bm{r}_{i+1}-\bm{r}_{i}
, where
i
runs from 1 to
N
.

Figure S10:
Evolution of the mean distance between boundary points in the AVM for matched simulations of the main text.

The linear spring potential with zero rest length of the AVM can be written as

U_{s}=\frac{k_{s}}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{N}|\bm{R}_{i}|^{2}.

(S45)

The angular spring potential in the AVM is based on the angle
\theta_{i}
between two link vectors and is given by

\displaystyle U_{b,\mathrm{AVM}}=\frac{k_{b,\mathrm{AVM}}}{2}\sum_{i=0}^{N-2}\theta_{i}^{2}.

(S46)

This bending potential does not map exactly to the fourth order bending term in the height equation. For that end, we have to instead take

U_{b}=\frac{k_{b}}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(\bm{R}_{i+1}-\bm{R}_{i}\right)^{2},

(S47)

which does approximate the bending potential of the AVM when the angle
\theta
between link vectors is small (limit of small fluctuations) and
R_{i}\approx R_{i+1}\approx R\equiv a
(constant tension):

U_{b}=\frac{k_{b}}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left[|\bm{R}_{i+1}|^{2}+|\bm{R}_{i}|^{2}-2|\bm{R}_{i+1}||\bm{R}_{i}|\cos(\theta_i)\right]\approx\frac{k_{b}}{2}2\sum_{i=0}^{N-2}\left[R^{2}-R^{2}\left(1-\frac{1}{2}\theta_{i}^{2}\right)\right]=\frac{k_{b}}{2}R^{2}\sum_{i=0}^{N-2}\theta_{i}^{2}.

(S48)

In figure
S10
, we show that we can indeed approximate the bond lengths by a constant
a(t)\approx a(0)=5

\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro m}
in the matched AVM simulations.

For periodic boundary conditions, one can then show (using the gradients of
U_{s}
and
U_{b}
) that the equation of motion for each boundary point is given by

\displaystyle\zeta\dot{\bm{r}}_{i}=k_{s}\left(\bm{r}_{i+1}-2\bm{r}_{i}+\bm{r}_{i-1}\right)-k_{b}\left(\bm{r}_{i+2}-4\bm{r}_{i+1}+6\bm{r}_{i}-4\bm{r}_{i-1}+\bm{r}_{i-2}\right)+\zeta_{\mathrm{pair}}\left(\bm{r}_{i+1}-2\bm{r}_{i}+\bm{r}_{i-1}\right)+\bm{f}_{i},

(S49)

where
\bm{f}_{i}=\zeta\bm{v}(\bm{r}_{i})
is the advection by the velocity field of the interior at position
\bm{r}_{i}
.

Dividing by the friction coefficient in the AVM and approximating the discrete differences by second and fourth order derivatives, we obtain the approximation

\displaystyle\dot{\bm{r}}(s)=\frac{k_{s}}{\zeta a^{2}}\partial_{s}^{2}\bm{r}(s)-\frac{k_{b}}{\zeta a^{4}}\partial_{s}^{4}\bm{r}(s)+\frac{\zeta_{\mathrm{pair}}}{\zeta a^{2}}\partial_{s}^{2}\partial_{t}\bm{r}(s)+\frac{\bm{f}(s)}{\zeta}.

(S50)

As explained in the derivation of the height equation, section
II
, we thus get in the limit of small fluctuations around an initially flat boundary:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}h(x,t)=\frac{k_{s}}{\zeta a^{2}}\partial_{x}^{2}h(x,t)-\frac{k_{b}}{\zeta a^{4}}\partial_{x}^{4}h(x,t)+\frac{\zeta_{\mathrm{pair}}}{\zeta a^{2}}\partial_{x}^{2}\partial_{t}h(x,t)+v^{f}(x,t),

(S51)

from which we deduce the discrete-to-continuum mapping
\frac{k_{s}}{\zeta a^{2}}=\frac{\lambda}{\zeta_{h}}
,
\frac{k_{b}}{\zeta a^{4}}=\frac{\kappa}{\zeta_{h}}=\frac{k_{b,\mathrm{AVM}}}{\zeta a^{2}}
, and
\frac{\zeta_{\mathrm{pair}}}{\zeta a^{2}}=\frac{\eta_{h}}{\zeta_{h}}
. However, we found that the estimated line tension and bending stiffness on the boundary of the AVM should be taken roughly a factor of 25 smaller, to match with theory, likely stemming from the relaxation effects when new points are added to the AVM boundary, see
Barton et al. 2017
for details.

We note that the spacing between boundary points
a(t)\approx a(0)=5

\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro m}
in the AVM, is different from the coarse-graining length scale
\sqrt{A_{0}}
used for the interior length scale in section
I.D
. This has implications for the mapping of the AVM parameters to the height equation noise-generating chain,

v^{f}(x,t)=\frac{\mu_{h}}{\zeta_{h}}\partial_{x}^{2}y(x,t)+\frac{\eta_{h}}{\zeta_{h}}\partial_{x}^{2}\partial_{t}y(x,t)+v_{0,h}n_{y}(x,t),

(S52)

as we will now discuss.

First of all, the driving strength
v_{0,h}
is not equal to
v_{0}
used in the AVM, this is because the driving of the boundary points by the cells of the interior is on a length scale
\sqrt{A_{0}}
instead of the boundary length scale
a
. By discretizing Eq. (
S52
) on the length scale
\sqrt{A_{0}}
and then reparametrizing to the length scale
a
, one finds that
v_{0,h}=\frac{a}{\sqrt{A_{0}}}v_{0}
. Strictly speaking, this method also results in a viscosity parameter that is
\frac{a^{2}}{A_{0}}
smaller, but we approximate this to be the same as the viscosity term of the height equation, and checked for the height spectrum that it made only a small difference at scales smaller than the cell size. We use the equivalent
2d
version of this support chain for the
2d
polymer simulations mentioned in the main text, and find
\mu_{h}/\zeta_{h}=$600\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro m}$
by hand from fitting the real space spatial correlation of the 2d polymer noise generating chain velocity to the real space velocity correlations of the AVM (Supporting Figures,
II.D
).

II.E. Steady state velocity correlations

We compute the steady state velocity correlation functions which enter the analysis of the height equation.
To that end we first compute the spectrum of the driving field
n_{y}

\left\langle N_{y}(q,\omega)N_{y}(q^{\prime},\omega^{\prime})\right\rangle=\iiiint\differential x\,\differential t\,\differential x^{\prime}\,\differential t^{\prime}\,\left\langle n_{y}(x,t)n_{y}(x^{\prime},t^{\prime})\right\rangle=4\pi^{2}\tau a\frac{\delta(q+q^{\prime})\delta(\omega+\omega^{\prime})}{1+\tau^{2}\omega^{2}},

(S53)

where the second equality derives from Eqs. (
S42
) and (
S14a
).

With the use of Eqs. (
S44
) and (
S53
), we readily obtain

\displaystyle\left\langle V^{f}(q,\omega)V^{f}(q^{\prime},\omega^{\prime})\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{4\pi^{2}\tau a\omega^{2}\zeta^{2}v_{0}^{2}}{\Big[\mu^{2}q^{4}+\left(\zeta+\eta q^{2}\right)^{2}\omega^{2}\Big]\Big[1+\tau^{2}\omega^{2}\Big]}\,\delta(q+q^{\prime})\,\delta(\omega+\omega^{\prime})

(S54)

\displaystyle=4\pi^{2}\tau av_{0}^{2}\zeta^{2}\frac{\tau^{2}\omega^{2}/\zeta^{2}}{\Big[(\xi_{p}^{2}-\xi_{d}^{2})^{2}q^{4}+(\xi_{d}^{2}q^{2}+1)\tau^{2}\omega^{2}\Big]\Big[1+\tau^{2}\omega^{2}\Big]}\,\delta(q+q^{\prime})\,\delta(\omega+\omega^{\prime})

which has the same form as Eq. (
S12
) and where we have introduced the following length scales

\displaystyle\xi_{p}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{\mu\tau+\eta}{\zeta}},

(S55a)

\displaystyle\xi_{d}

\displaystyle=\frac{\eta}{\zeta},

(S55b)

identically to Eq. (
S20
).
Similarly to Eq. (
S15
), we then compute the two-time velocity spatial spectrum

\displaystyle\left\langle\tilde{v}^{f}(q,t)\tilde{v}^{f}(q^{\prime},t^{\prime})\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\pi av_{0}^{2}\,\delta(q+q^{\prime})\,\frac{(\xi_{p}^{2}-\xi_{d}^{2})q^{2}\,e^{-\frac{(\xi_{p}^{2}-\xi_{d}^{2})q^{2}}{(\xi_{d}^{2}q^{2}+1)\tau}|t-t^{\prime}|}-(\xi_{d}^{2}q^{2}+1)\,e^{-|t-t^{\prime}|/\tau}}{\Big[(\xi_{p}^{2}-\xi_{d}^{2})^{2}q^{4}-(\xi_{d}^{2}q^{2}+1)^{2}\Big]\Big[\xi_{d}^{2}q^{2}+1\Big]}

(S56)

\displaystyle=\frac{\pi av_{0}^{2}}{\tau}\delta(q+q^{\prime})\frac{\zeta^{2}}{\zeta_{q}^{2}}\frac{\frac{\mu q^{2}}{\zeta_{q}}e^{\frac{-\mu q^{2}}{\zeta_{q}}|t-t^{\prime}|}-\frac{1}{\tau}e^{-\frac{1}{\tau}|t-t^{\prime}|}}{\left[\mu q^{2}/\zeta_{q}-1/\tau\right]\left[\mu q^{2}/\zeta_{q}+1/\tau\right]}.

where we have introduced

\zeta_{q}=\zeta+\eta q^{2}.

(S57)

III
Analysis of the height equation

III.A. Solving the height equation in
(q,t)
-space

Using the Fourier transform conventions listed in Eq. (
S5
), the height equation, Eq. (
S23
), takes the following form in
(q,t)
-space

\displaystyle(\zeta+\eta q^{2})\partial_{t}\tilde{h}(q,t)=-\left(\lambda q^{2}+\kappa q^{4}\right)\tilde{h}(q,t)+\zeta\tilde{v}^{f}(q,t).

(S58)

which may be rewritten, using Eq. (
S16
),

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\tilde{h}+\frac{\lambda q^{2}+\kappa q^{4}}{\zeta_{q}}\tilde{h}=\frac{\zeta}{\zeta_{q}}\tilde{v}^{f}.

(S59)

Considering
e^{\left(\lambda q^{2}+\kappa q^{4}\right)t/\zeta_{q}}
as integrating factor, we write

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\left(\tilde{h}e^{\left(\lambda q^{2}+\kappa q^{4}\right)t/\zeta_{q}}\right)=\frac{\zeta}{\zeta_{q}}\tilde{v}^{f}e^{\left(\lambda q^{2}+\kappa q^{4}\right)t/\zeta_{q}},

(S60)

and therefore by integration

\displaystyle\tilde{h}(q,t)=\frac{\zeta}{\zeta_{q}}\int_{0}^{t}\differential t^{\prime}\,\tilde{v}^{f}(q,t^{\prime})e^{-\left(\lambda q^{2}+\kappa q^{4}\right)(t-t^{\prime})/\zeta_{q}},

(S61)

where we have used the initial condition
h(x,0)=0
thus
\tilde{h}(q,0)=0
.

Average border progression.

Since
\left\langle\hat{v}^{f}(q,t^{\prime})\right\rangle=0
, we have by linearity of the Fourier transform

\displaystyle\left\langle\tilde{h}(q,t)\right\rangle

\displaystyle=0,

(S62a)

\displaystyle\Big<h(x,t)\Big>

\displaystyle=0,

(S62b)

i.e. no average border progression.

III.B. Transient Spatial Fourier spectrum

Using equation Eq. (
S61
), we get for the transient height spectrum

\displaystyle\left\langle\tilde{h}(q,t)\tilde{h}(q^{\prime},t)\right\rangle=\frac{\zeta^{2}}{\zeta_{q}^{2}}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{t}\differential t_{1}\,\differential t_{2}\,\left\langle\tilde{v}^{f}(q,t_{1})\tilde{v}^{f}(q^{\prime},t_{2})\right\rangle e^{-\left[\lambda(q^{2}\left[t-t_{1}\right]+q^{\prime 2}\left[t-t_{2}\right])+\kappa(q^{4}\left[t-t_{1}\right]+q^{\prime 4}\left[t-t_{2}\right])\right]/\zeta_{q}}.

(S63)

We assume that bulk velocity correlations have reached a steady state well before the release of the confinement.
This allows us to use the steady state result for
\left\langle\tilde{v}^{f}(q,t_{1})\tilde{v}^{f}(q^{\prime},t_{2})\right\rangle
Eq. (
S56
) and write

\displaystyle\left\langle\tilde{h}(q,t)\tilde{h}(q^{\prime},t)\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{\pi av_{0}^{2}}{\tau}\delta(q+q^{\prime})\frac{\zeta^{4}}{\zeta_{q}^{4}}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{t}\differential t_{1}\,\differential t_{2}\frac{\frac{\mu q^{2}}{\zeta_{q}}e^{\frac{-\mu q^{2}}{\zeta_{q}}|t_{1}-t_{2}|}-\frac{1}{\tau}e^{-\frac{1}{\tau}|t_{1}-t_{2}|}}{\left[\mu q^{2}/\zeta_{q}-1/\tau\right]\left[\mu q^{2}/\zeta_{q}+1/\tau\right]}e^{-\left[\lambda q^{2}+\kappa q^{4}\right]\left[2t-t_{1}-t_{2}\right]/\zeta_{q}},

(S64)

\displaystyle=\frac{\pi av_{0}^{2}\delta(q+q^{\prime})}{\tau\left[\mu q^{2}/\zeta_{q}-1/\tau\right]\left[\mu q^{2}/\zeta_{q}+1/\tau\right]}\frac{\zeta^{4}}{\zeta_{q}^{4}}H(q,t),

where we find

\displaystyle H(q,t)=

\displaystyle\left(1-e^{-2\left[\lambda q^{2}+\kappa q^{4}\right]t/\zeta_{q}}\right)\frac{\lambda q^{2}+\kappa q^{4}}{\zeta_{q}}\frac{(1/\tau)^{2}-(\mu q^{2}/\zeta_{q})^{2}}{\left[\left[\lambda q^{2}+\kappa q^{4}\right]^{2}/\zeta_{q}^{2}-\left(1/\tau\right)^{2}\right]\left[\left[\lambda q^{2}+\kappa q^{4}\right]^{2}/\zeta_{q}^{2}-\left(\mu q^{2}/\zeta_{q}\right)^{2}\right]}

\displaystyle+\left(1+e^{-2\left[\lambda q^{2}+\kappa q^{4}\right]t/\zeta_{q}}-2e^{-\left[\lambda q^{2}+\kappa q^{4}\right]t/\zeta_{q}-\mu q^{2}t/\zeta_{q}}\right)\left[\frac{\mu q^{2}/\zeta_{q}}{\left[\lambda q^{2}+\kappa q^{4}\right]^{2}/\zeta_{q}^{2}-\left(\mu q^{2}/\zeta_{q}\right)^{2}}\right]

(S65)

\displaystyle-\left(1+e^{-2\left[\lambda q^{2}+\kappa q^{4}\right]t/\zeta_{q}}-2e^{-\left[\lambda q^{2}+\kappa q^{4}\right]t/\zeta_{q}-t/\tau}\right)\left[\frac{1/\tau}{\left[\lambda q^{2}+\kappa q^{4}\right]^{2}/\zeta_{q}^{2}-\left(1/\tau\right)^{2}}\right].

Infinite time limit.

For all
q^{2}\neq 0
, we find

\displaystyle\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}H(q,t)=

\displaystyle\frac{\lambda q^{2}+\kappa q^{4}}{\zeta_{q}}\frac{(1/\tau)^{2}-(\mu q^{2}/\zeta_{q})^{2}}{\left[\left[\lambda q^{2}+\kappa q^{4}\right]^{2}/\zeta_{q}^{2}-\left(1/\tau\right)^{2}\right]\left[\left[\lambda q^{2}+\kappa q^{4}\right]^{2}/\zeta_{q}^{2}-\left(\mu q^{2}/\zeta_{q}\right)^{2}\right]}

\displaystyle+\left[\frac{\mu q^{2}/\zeta_{q}}{\left[\lambda q^{2}+\kappa q^{4}\right]^{2}/\zeta_{q}^{2}-\left(\mu q^{2}/\zeta_{q}\right)^{2}}\right]

\displaystyle-\left[\frac{1/\tau}{\left[\lambda q^{2}+\kappa q^{4}\right]^{2}/\zeta_{q}^{2}-\left(1/\tau\right)^{2}}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(\lambda q^{2}+\kappa q^{4})/\zeta_{q}+1/\tau}-\frac{1}{(\lambda q^{2}+\kappa q^{4})/\zeta_{q}+\mu q^{2}/\zeta_{q}}.

(S66)

This means that we can conclude

\displaystyle\left\langle\tilde{h}(q)\tilde{h}(q^{\prime})\right\rangle

\displaystyle\equiv\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\left\langle\tilde{h}(q,t)\tilde{h}(q^{\prime},t)\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{\pi av_{0}^{2}\delta(q+q^{\prime})}{\tau\left[\mu q^{2}/\zeta_{q}-1/\tau\right]\left[\mu q^{2}/\zeta_{q}+1/\tau\right]}\frac{\zeta^{4}}{\zeta_{q}^{4}}\left[\frac{1}{(\lambda q^{2}+\kappa q^{4})/\zeta_{q}+1/\tau}-\frac{1}{(\lambda q^{2}+\kappa q^{4})/\zeta_{q}+\mu q^{2}/\zeta_{q}}\right],

(S67)

for any
q^{2}\neq 0
.

III.C. Transient roughness

We define the roughness
w(t)
as the ensemble average of the standard deviation of the height at time
t
:

\displaystyle w^{2}(t)\equiv\left\langle\overline{\left(h(x,t)-\overline{h(x,t)}\right)^{2}}\right\rangle=\left\langle\overline{h^{2}(x,t)}\right\rangle-\left\langle\left(\overline{h(x,t)}\right)^{2}\right\rangle,

(S68)

where the overline
\overline{A}
refers to the spatial average, and brackets
\langle A\rangle
to the ensemble (noise) average.
By writing the spatial average as an integral, we see that we can exchange the order of averaging, so we use Eq. (
S64
) to first find

\displaystyle\left\langle h^{2}(x,t)\right\rangle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{2}}\iint\differential q\,\differential q^{\prime}\left\langle\tilde{h}(q,t)\tilde{h}(q^{\prime},t)\right\rangle e^{-\mathrm{i}qx}e^{-\mathrm{i}q^{\prime}x}

(S69)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{2}}\int\differential q\frac{\pi av_{0}^{2}}{\tau\left[\mu q^{2}/\zeta_{q}-1/\tau\right]\left[\mu q^{2}/\zeta_{q}+1/\tau\right]}\frac{\zeta^{4}}{\zeta_{q}^{4}}H(q,t)

where we have used the Dirac delta to evaluate the integral over
q^{\prime}
.
And then

\displaystyle\left\langle\overline{h^{2}(x,t)}\right\rangle=\overline{\left\langle h^{2}(x,t)\right\rangle}=\lim_{L\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{L}\int_{-L/2}^{L/2}\differential x\left\langle h^{2}(x,t)\right\rangle=\lim_{L\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{L}L\left\langle h^{2}(t)\right\rangle=\left\langle h^{2}(t)\right\rangle.

(S70)

By symmetry of the equations of motion of both the height and support chain and the fact that the noise is spatially uncorrelated, we expect the average spatial height of the boundary to be zero, hence

\displaystyle\left\langle\left(\overline{h(x,t)}\right)^{2}\right\rangle=0.

(S71)

So to calculate the roughness, we (numerically) calculate the integral

\displaystyle\left\langle h^{2}(t)\right\rangle=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{2}}\int\differential q\frac{\pi av_{0}^{2}}{\tau\left[\mu q^{2}/\zeta_{q}-1/\tau\right]\left[\mu q^{2}/\zeta_{q}+1/\tau\right]}\frac{\zeta^{4}}{\zeta_{q}^{4}}H(q,t)

(S72)

with

\displaystyle H(q,t)=

\displaystyle\left(1-e^{-2\left[\lambda q^{2}+\kappa q^{4}\right]t/\zeta_{q}}\right)\frac{\lambda q^{2}+\kappa q^{4}}{\zeta_{q}}\frac{(1/\tau)^{2}-(\mu q^{2}/\zeta_{q})^{2}}{\left[\left[\lambda q^{2}+\kappa q^{4}\right]^{2}/\zeta_{q}^{2}-\left(1/\tau\right)^{2}\right]\left[\left[\lambda q^{2}+\kappa q^{4}\right]^{2}/\zeta_{q}^{2}-\left(\mu q^{2}/\zeta_{q}\right)^{2}\right]}

\displaystyle+\left(1+e^{-2\left[\lambda q^{2}+\kappa q^{4}\right]t/\zeta_{q}}-2e^{-\left[\lambda q^{2}+\kappa q^{4}\right]t/\zeta_{q}-\mu q^{2}t/\zeta_{q}}\right)\left[\frac{\mu q^{2}/\zeta_{q}}{\left[\lambda q^{2}+\kappa q^{4}\right]^{2}/\zeta_{q}^{2}-\left(\mu q^{2}/\zeta_{q}\right)^{2}}\right]

(S73)

\displaystyle-\left(1+e^{-2\left[\lambda q^{2}+\kappa q^{4}\right]t/\zeta_{q}}-2e^{-\left[\lambda q^{2}+\kappa q^{4}\right]t/\zeta_{q}-t/\tau}\right)\left[\frac{1/\tau}{\left[\lambda q^{2}+\kappa q^{4}\right]^{2}/\zeta_{q}^{2}-\left(1/\tau\right)^{2}}\right].

Transient relative roughness correlation.

We define the relative roughness as in
Rapin et al. 2021

\displaystyle B_{S}(x,t)\equiv

\displaystyle\left\langle\overline{\left(h(x+x^{\prime},t)-h(x^{\prime},t)\right)^{2}}\right\rangle

(S74)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\left\langle h^{2}(t)\right\rangle-2\left\langle\overline{h(x+x^{\prime},t)h(x^{\prime},t)}\right\rangle,

where we numerically calculate

\displaystyle\left\langle\overline{h(x,t)h(x^{\prime},t)}\right\rangle=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{2}}\int\differential q\frac{\pi av_{0}^{2}}{\tau\left[\mu/\zeta_{q}-1/\tau\right]\left[\mu q^{2}/\zeta_{q}+1/\tau\right]}\frac{\zeta^{4}}{\zeta_{q}^{4}}H(q,t)e^{-\mathrm{i}q(x-x^{\prime})}.

(S75)

Transient temporal roughness correlation.

We define a temporal roughness correlation of the following form,

\displaystyle B_{T}(\Delta t,t)\equiv\frac{\langle\overline{\Delta h(x,t+\Delta t)\Delta h(x,t)}\rangle}{\sqrt{\langle\overline{\Delta h(x,t+\Delta t)^{2}}\rangle}\sqrt{\langle\overline{\Delta h(x,t)^{2}}\rangle}},

(S76)

with
\Delta h(x,t)\equiv h(x,t)-\overline{h(x,t)}
. Noting that the terms in the denominator are just the variances of the height at the respective times, we can also write

\displaystyle B_{T}(\Delta t,t)=\frac{\langle\overline{\Delta h(x,t+\Delta t)\Delta h(x,t)}\rangle}{\sqrt{w^{2}(t+\Delta t)}\sqrt{w^{2}(t)}}.

(S77)

We, therefore, need

\displaystyle\langle\tilde{h}(q,t)\tilde{h}(q^{\prime},t^{\prime})\rangle=\frac{\zeta^{2}}{\zeta_{q}^{2}}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{t^{\prime}}\differential t_{1}\differential t_{2}\left\langle\tilde{v}^{f}(q,t_{1})\tilde{v}^{f}(q^{\prime},t_{2})\right\rangle e^{-\left[\lambda(q^{2}\left[t-t_{1}\right]+q^{\prime 2}\left[t^{\prime}-t_{2}\right])+\kappa(q^{4}\left[t-t_{1}\right]+q^{\prime 4}\left[t^{\prime}-t_{2}\right])\right]/\zeta_{q}}.

(S78)

If we now introduce
t^{\prime}=t+\Delta t
, we find eventually

\displaystyle\langle\tilde{h}(q,t)\tilde{h}(q^{\prime},t+\Delta t)\rangle=

\displaystyle\frac{\zeta^{2}}{\zeta_{q}^{2}}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{t+\Delta t}\differential t_{1}\differential t_{2}\left\langle\tilde{v}^{f}(q,t_{1})\tilde{v}^{f}(q^{\prime},t_{2})\right\rangle e^{-\left[\lambda(q^{2}\left[t-t_{1}\right]+q^{\prime 2}\left[t-t_{2}\right])+\kappa(q^{4}\left[t-t_{1}\right]+q^{\prime 4}\left[t-t_{2}\right])\right]/\zeta_{q}}e^{-\left[\lambda q^{\prime 2}+\kappa q^{\prime 4}\right]\Delta t/\zeta_{q}}.

(S79)

Plugging in the velocity correlations, one obtains (using Mathematica), the following result

\displaystyle\langle h(q,t)h(q^{\prime},t+\Delta t)\rangle=\frac{\pi av_{0}^{2}}{\tau}\frac{\zeta^{4}}{\zeta_{q}^{4}}\zeta_{q}^{3}\tau^{2}\delta(q+q^{\prime})G(q,t),

(S80)

where
G(q,t,\Delta t)\equiv G_{1}(q,t,\Delta t)+G_{2}(q,t,\Delta t)+G_{3}(q,t,\Delta t)+G_{4}(q,t,\Delta t)
, and

\displaystyle G_{1}(q,t,\Delta t)\equiv f_{1}(q)\left(e^{-\left[\lambda q^{2}+\kappa q^{4}\right]t/\zeta_{q}-(t+\Delta t)/\tau}+e^{-\left[\lambda q^{2}+\kappa q^{4}\right](t+\Delta t)/\zeta_{q}-\Delta t/\tau}-e^{-\Delta t/\tau}\right),

\displaystyle G_{2}(q,t,\Delta t)\equiv f_{2}(q)\left(e^{-\left[\lambda q^{2}+\kappa q^{4}\right](t+\Delta t)/\zeta_{q}-\mu q^{2}t/\zeta_{q}}+e^{-\left[(\lambda+\mu)q^{2}+\kappa q^{4}\right]t/\zeta_{q}-\mu q^{2}\Delta t/\zeta_{q}}-e^{-\mu q^{2}\Delta t/\zeta_{q}}\right),

\displaystyle G_{3}(q,t,\Delta t)\equiv f_{3}(q)e^{-\left[\lambda q^{2}+\kappa q^{4}\right]\Delta t/\zeta_{q}},

\displaystyle G_{4}(q,t,\Delta t)\equiv f_{4}(q)e^{-\left[\lambda q^{2}+\kappa q^{4}\right](2t+\Delta t)/\zeta_{q}},

where the
\{f_{i}(q)\}
are rational functions of
q
.
By taking the inverse Fourier transform of
\langle h(q,t)h(q^{\prime},t+\Delta t)\rangle
we then obtain
\langle\overline{\Delta h(x,t+\Delta t)\Delta h(x,t)}\rangle
.

III.D. Transient Slope-slope and tangent-tangent correlation

Denote the slope of the height function by

\displaystyle u(x,t)\equiv\partial_{x}h(x,t),\quad\text{and}\quad\tilde{u}(q,t)=-\mathrm{i}q\tilde{h}(q,t).

(S81)

The slope-slope correlation in
(q,t)
-space is thus, using Eq. (
S64
),

\displaystyle\boxed{\left\langle\tilde{u}(q,t)\tilde{u}(q^{\prime},t)\right\rangle=\left\langle-qq^{\prime}\tilde{h}(q,t)\tilde{h}(q^{\prime},t)\right\rangle=q^{2}\frac{\pi av_{0}^{2}\delta(q+q^{\prime})}{\tau\left[\mu q^{2}/\zeta_{q}-1/\tau\right]\left[\mu q^{2}/\zeta_{q}+1/\tau\right]}\frac{\zeta^{4}}{\zeta_{q}^{4}}H(q,t).}

(S82)

From slope-slope correlation to tangent-tangent correlation.

The tangent-tangent correlation function is defined as

\displaystyle C(s,t)\equiv\left\langle\overline{\bm{\hat{t}}(s+s^{\prime},t)\cdot\bm{\hat{t}}(s^{\prime},t)}\right\rangle,

(S90)

where the spatial average is over
s^{\prime}

If we assume the absence of overhangs (as we did for the derivation of the height equation), we can write

and write the tangent vector in components:

\displaystyle\bm{\hat{t}}(s,t)=

\displaystyle\partial_{s}x(s)\bm{\hat{x}}+\partial_{s}y(s)\bm{\hat{y}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\partial x}{\partial s}\left[\bm{\hat{x}}+\frac{\partial y}{\partial x}\bm{\hat{y}}\right].

(S91)

\displaystyle\bm{\hat{t}}(s+s^{\prime},t)\cdot\bm{\hat{t}}(s^{\prime},t)=\frac{\partial x}{\partial s}\bigg\rvert_{s+s^{\prime}}\frac{\partial x}{\partial s}\bigg\rvert_{s^{\prime}}\left[1+\frac{\partial y}{\partial x}\bigg\rvert_{s+s^{\prime}}\frac{\partial y}{\partial x}\bigg\rvert_{s^{\prime}}\right].

(S92)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial x}{\partial s}\bigg\rvert_{s+s^{\prime}}\frac{\partial x}{\partial s}\bigg\rvert_{s^{\prime}}\approx 1-\frac{1}{2}\left[\frac{\partial y}{\partial x}\right]_{s+s^{\prime}}^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\left[\frac{\partial y}{\partial x}\right]_{s^{\prime}}^{2}.

(S93)

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle\overline{1-\frac{1}{2}\left[\frac{\partial y}{\partial x}\right]_{s+s^{\prime}}^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\left[\frac{\partial y}{\partial x}\right]_{s^{\prime}}^{2}+\frac{\partial y}{\partial x}\bigg\rvert_{s+s^{\prime}}\frac{\partial y}{\partial x}\bigg\rvert_{s^{\prime}}}

\displaystyle=1-\overline{\frac{\partial y}{\partial x}\bigg\rvert_{s^{\prime}}\frac{\partial y}{\partial x}\bigg\rvert_{s^{\prime}}}+\overline{\frac{\partial y}{\partial x}\bigg\rvert_{s+s^{\prime}}\frac{\partial y}{\partial x}\bigg\rvert_{s^{\prime}}}.

(S94)

II
Image processing

Having curated the experimental dataset, the next step was to (pre)process the images so that the boundary line could be extracted and the PIV could be performed. Since the imaging setup was such that it followed the boundary over time, the images were essentially taken in a comoving reference frame with respect to the moving cell front. As we are interested in the absolute movement of the cell front, we needed to shift the images to the lab frame. In addition, we occasionally noticed a small horizontal drift of the field of view in the image sequences which we attribute to a small microscope drift. We corrected for this and the comoving frame by tracking a reference point (one of the corners in the field of view) in the images and using the location of this point to translate every image to an absolute reference frame, see figures
S13
and
S14
.

III

III.A. Zoning

Earlier experiments, e.g.
Petitjean et al. 2010
;
Reffay et al. 2014
;
Vishwakarma et al. 2018
revealed gradients in the velocity and force fields as a function of the distance from the euclidean distance from a fingertip. To analyze this in our experiments we divided the tissue in the images into dynamic zones, see figure
S17
. The zones move in synchrony with the fingers, so that we could obtain velocity statistics at different locations behind the fingers. The finger region is determined from the maximum and minimum of the boundary line at any time. As the minimum position of the boundary is subject to (slow) fluctuations, we define a buffer zone to cleanly distinguish a finger zone and a region further behind the region between the minimum of the boundary line and 100 pixels below the mean of the boundary line (
$1\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro m}$\mathrel{\widehat{=}}1.5385
pixels). The velocity statistics of the region further away from the boundary than the buffer zone is shown in
S1
.

III.B. PIV

We followed the work by Petitjean et al
Petitjean et al. 2010
in determining the window size for the PIV: the maximal local displacement should be smaller than half of the size of the smallest window.
Like Petitjean et al
Petitjean et al. 2010
, we took
30\mathrm{\mu m}
/h as an overestimation of the speed of the cells. Since the time between frames is 5 minutes and
$1\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro m}$\mathrel{\widehat{=}}1.5385
pixels, the maximal displacement is 3.8 pixels between frames. As this is very close to 4 pixels, we decided to use 16 pixels and not 8 as the smallest window size because this still gives sub cellular size resolution (average cell area is around
170\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro m}
). The PIV analysis is carried out with the use of PIVlab
Thielicke and Sonntag 2021
for MATLAB with successively smaller window sizes: 64 to 32 to 16 pixels. We show in figure
S18
an excerpt of the PIV field.

S17

III.C. Rotational tilt correction

Most experiments started with a slanted boundary, see for example the initial frame of experiment 01 in figure
S13
. We corrected for this by, for each experiment separately, fitting a linear line through the boundary of the first frame and using the angle of this line to rotate the boundary coordinates in all frames. We also did this for the position and velocity vectors of the PIV vectors. We validated the rotational tilt correction by measuring the average speed in the horizontal direction (along the long tissue axis) and the vertical direction before and after the rotational tilt correction. The rotational tilt correction led to an increase in the vertical speed in exchange for a smaller horizontal speed. This is consistent with the fact that initially the movement of the border cells is mostly oriented towards the free space
Poujade et al. 2007
.
