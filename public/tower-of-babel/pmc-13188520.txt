Age-related differences in adherence to inhaled corticosteroids among adult patients with asthma: a national cohort study in South Korea
Authors: Hee Sun Park, Jungkuk Lee, Hasung Kim, Seong-Dae Woo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundUnderstanding age-related differences in adherence to inhaled corticosteroids (ICSs) is crucial to optimize asthma management.ObjectivesTo explore differences in adherence levels among different age groups of adult patients with asthma in South Korea from January 2016 to December 2020.MethodsWe performed a population-based cohort study using data from South Korea’s National Health Insurance Service, including individuals aged 18 to 79 who began using ICS between 2016 and 2020. Adults with asthma were categorized into three age groups: young adults (18–39 years of age), middle-aged adults (40–59 years of age), and older adults (60–79 years of age). The primary outcomes were medication adherence, indicated by the proportion of days covered (PDC), and persistence. Additionally, asthma outcomes, including the risk of asthma exacerbation and pneumonia, were analyzed.ResultsAmong the 15,367 included patients, 4,237 were young adults, 6,132 were middle-aged adults, and 4,998 were older adults. Older adults had a significantly higher PDC (0.56 ± 0.20) than middle-aged (0.53 ± 0.20) and young adults (0.52 ± 0.20; P < .001). Additionally, older adults had a 29.4% lower risk of discontinuation compared with young adults (adjusted hazard ratio [aHR], 0.706; 95% confidence interval [CI], 0.667–0.747; P < .001) and a 13.4% lower risk compared with middle-aged adults (aHR, 0.866; 95% CI, 0.821–0.913; P < .001). Despite higher medication adherence, older adults experienced a higher risk of severe asthma exacerbations (aHR, 1.848; 95% CI, 1.568–2.178; P < .001) than young adults.ConclusionOlder adults demonstrated better adherence to ICS-containing inhalers but also had higher risks of severe exacerbations and pneumonia, highlighting the necessity for age-specific management strategies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12890-026-04284-1.Keywords: Age groups, Asthma, Corticosteroids, Inhaler, Medication adherence

Introduction

Asthma, a chronic respiratory condition affecting approximately 300 million people globally, considerably affects patients’ quality of life and healthcare resources [1]. Despite the well-established benefits of inhaled corticosteroids (ICSs) in asthma management, adherence to prescribed medications remains a critical challenge, leading to poor disease control and increased healthcare utilization [2, 3]. Understanding the factors influencing adherence, particularly age-related differences, is essential to improve treatment outcomes and patients’ quality of life [4].

Previous studies have highlighted various demographic and clinical factors that affect medication adherence in patients with asthma [5–8]. Age is one such factor that has garnered attention, with evidence suggesting that adherence patterns may vary across age groups [9, 10]. However, comprehensive real-world data of such age-related adherence differences at a national level are limited.

With this study, we sought to explore previously unaddressed aspects by analyzing the variations in adherence to ICS therapy among adult patients with asthma in South Korea, utilizing data from the National Health Insurance Service (NHIS). By categorizing patients into three age groups—young adults (18–39 years), middle-aged adults (40–59 years), and older adults (60–79 years)—we sought to provide a detailed description of how adherence varies with age. Furthermore, within the same cohort, we aimed to describe age-specific risks of severe asthma exacerbations and pneumonia in real-world practice. We therefore hypothesized that adherence to ICS-containing inhalers and the risks of severe asthma exacerbations and pneumonia would differ across age groups in real-world clinical practice.

Material and methods

Data source

We drew upon the comprehensive NHIS database in South Korea for this study, which encompasses the vast majority of the Korean population. The NHIS dataset includes detailed information on healthcare utilization, sociodemographics, diagnoses (based on the 10th revisions of the International Classification of Diseases [ICD-10]), and prescriptions. This investigation received ethical clearance from the Institutional Review Board of Chungnam National University Hospital (IRB No. 2022–04–114). Given the retrospective nature of the research, the requirement for written informed consent was waived, and all participant information was anonymized to protect patient privacy.

Study population and design

In this study, we selected individuals aged 18 to 79 who had a new asthma diagnosis and began using ICS between January 2016 and December 2019. The data on which a patient first received a prescription for an ICS-containing inhaler was defined as the index date. Patients were a priori categorized into three age groups (18–39, 40–59, and 60–79 years), representing young, middle-aged, and older adults in line with prior respiratory epidemiologic work [11, 12]. For inclusion in the study, patients needed to have at least four prescriptions of ICS-containing inhalers within a year after the index date, ensuring that only those receiving ongoing maintenance ICS therapy were included. Exclusion criteria were applied sequentially, as outlined in Fig. 1. Patients were excluded if they used systemic steroids within 60 days prior to the index date; had fewer than three prescriptions of ICS-containing inhalers within a year of the index date; were diagnosed with malignant diseases, cystic fibrosis, or chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD); or used biologics at any point during the study period, to restrict the cohort to patients treated with ICS-based controller therapy alone. After applying these criteria, the final study cohort comprised 15,367 patients (Fig. 2).

Definitions and outcomes

Asthma was identified using the ICD-10 codes J45–J46. Asthma-related medications, including ICSs and ICS/LABA combinations, were identified using ingredient codes in the NHIS database (Supplementary Table 1, Additional File). The primary outcomes were medication adherence and persistence. Adherence was assessed via the proportion of days covered (PDC), determined by dividing the total days the medication was on hand by the overall duration of observation. Persistence was measured from the index date until discontinuation, which was marked by a gap of 120 days or more since the last prescription. A 120-day permissible gap was selected to reflect real-world follow-up and prescribing practices in South Korea, where asthma care quality is evaluated partly based on whether patients attend outpatient visits at least three times per year. Secondary outcomes included the incidence of severe asthma exacerbation and pneumonia. Severe asthma exacerbation was defined as hospitalization or emergency department visits for asthma, followed by the administration of systemic corticosteroids at a daily dose of 15 mg or an equivalent for three or more consecutive days. In this study, systemic corticosteroids were defined as prednisolone, methylprednisolone, dexamethasone, hydrocortisone, and triamcinolone, as identified by their ingredient codes in the NHIS database. Pneumonia was identified based on ICD-10 codes J12–J18. Comorbidities were defined according to ICD-10 codes and included hypertensive disorder, diabetes mellitus, hyperlipidemia, pneumonia, renal impairment, liver disease, acute respiratory disease, gastroesophageal disease, urinary tract infection, rheumatoid arthritis, osteoarthritis, and dementia (Supplementary Table 2, Additional File). Furthermore, we applied the Charlson Comorbidity Index to assess mortality risk stemming from these comorbidities before the index date.

Statistical analyses

Baseline characteristics across different age groups were analyzed using χ2 tests for categorical data and t-tests for continuous data. To estimate hazard ratios (HRs) for the risk of asthma exacerbation and pneumonia, Cox proportional-hazards models were utilized, adjusting for age, sex, and region of residence, comorbidities, and medication use. The proportional-hazards assumption was tested using Schoenfeld residuals; the assumption was satisfied for all variables included in the model. We employed logistic regression models to investigate medication adherence at PDC thresholds of ≥ 0.5 and ≥ 0.8, as previously mentioned [13]. Cumulative incidence rates across different age groups were depicted using Kaplan–Meier survival curves. All statistical procedures were carried out using SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA), and significance was set at a P-value of < 0.05.

Results

Study population

In this study, 15,367 patients with asthma who initiated ICS-containing inhaler therapy were included. These patients were categorized into three age groups: 4,237 young adults (18–39 years), 6,132 middle-aged adults (40–59 years), and 4,998 older adults (60–79 years). The baseline characteristics of these groups are detailed in Table 1, with mean ages of 30.9 ± 5.4 years for the youngest group, 50.2 ± 5.7 years for the middle-aged group, and 67.4 ± 5.4 years for the oldest group. Significant differences were observed across age groups in terms of sex, regional distribution, and all comorbidities (P < 0.001), whereas no significant differences were observed in medication use patterns, with ICS/long-acting beta agonist combinations being common across all ages.

Adherence to ICS therapy

Adherence to ICS therapy, measured via the PDC, varied significantly across the age groups (P < 0.001; Table 2). The mean PDC values were highest in the older-adult group (0.56 ± 0.20), followed by those in the middle-aged- (0.53 ± 0.20) and young-adult groups (0.52 ± 0.20). Additionally, the percentages of adults achieving a PDC ≥ 0.5 and ≥ 0.8 differed among the groups (both P < 0.001,) being highest among older adults (52.3% and 15.8%, respectively), followed by those in middle-aged (43.8% and 12.7%, respectively) and young adults (42.9% and 11.9%, respectively).

Older adults also exhibited better persistence with ICS therapy. The risk of discontinuation was 29.4% lower among older adults than that among young adults (adjusted HR, 0.706; 95% confidence interval [CI], 0.667–0.747; P < 0.001) and 13.4% lower than that among middle-aged adults (adjusted HR, 0.866; 95% CI, 0.821–0.913; P < 0.001) (Fig. 3).

Asthma exacerbations and pneumonia

The cumulative incidence of asthma exacerbations varied among the age groups. Older adults had a significantly higher risk for severe asthma exacerbations than young (adjusted HR, 1.848; 95% CI, 1.568–2.178; P < 0.001) and middle-aged (adjusted HR, 1.319; 95% CI, 1.115–1.559; P < 0.001) adults (Fig. 4A, Table 3). The risk of pneumonia was also higher among older adults than that among young (adjusted HR, 1.681; 95% CI, 1.452–1.945; P < 0.001) and middle-aged adults (adjusted HR, 1.272; 95% CI, 1.125–1.439; P < 0.001) (Fig. 4B, Table 3).

Discussion

This study provides valuable insights into the age-related differences in adherence to ICSs among adult patients with asthma in South Korea, highlighting the significant impact of age on medication adherence and associated clinical outcomes. The results demonstrate that older adults exhibit higher adherence to ICS therapy than their younger counterparts, as evidenced by higher mean PDC values and lower rates of discontinuation. However, despite this higher adherence, older adults also experienced a greater incidence of severe asthma exacerbations and pneumonia, suggesting that age-related differences in baseline risk substantially influence asthma outcomes in this population. To our knowledge, few prior studies have characterized age-related patterns of adherence and persistence together in a large, nationwide cohort of adult ICS initiators.

The higher adherence observed among older adults may be attributed to several factors, including increased disease awareness, more frequent healthcare interactions, and a greater perceived need for consistent medication use [10]. Older adults are more likely to have regular contact with healthcare providers, which may reinforce the importance of medication adherence and lead to better management of their condition [5, 14]. Despite their higher adherence, older adults had a significantly higher risk of severe asthma exacerbations and pneumonia than younger patients. This paradoxical result suggests that factors other than adherence, such as the physiological changes associated with aging, the burden of comorbidities, and potential differences in the severity of asthma, may exert a significant influence on asthma outcomes [15, 16]. The aging process is associated with immunosenescence, heightened susceptibility to infection, and alterations in lung function, all of which may contribute to the higher rates of exacerbations and pneumonia observed among older adults [17]. Furthermore, the management of asthma in older adults may be complicated by the presence of multiple comorbidities, such as cardiovascular diseases and diabetes, which can interfere with asthma control and increase the risk of adverse outcomes. Taken together, these considerations suggest that the coexistence of higher adherence and worse outcomes in older adults in our cohort most likely reflects a higher baseline risk profile—driven by more severe or long-standing asthma and a greater comorbidity burden.

The results of this study have important clinical implications. Although improving adherence to ICS therapy is critical for effective asthma management, especially in older adults, the broader context of asthma care must also be considered in this population. Clinicians should be aware of the potential challenges associated with aging, including the increased risk of comorbidities and the need for a more comprehensive approach to disease management. This may involve closer monitoring, tailored treatment plans that address both asthma and comorbid conditions, and patient education to ensure that older adults are equipped to manage their asthma effectively.

In contrast, the younger age groups, while exhibiting lower adherence rates, experienced fewer severe exacerbations and instances of pneumonia. This may be owing to their generally better overall health, fewer comorbidities, and potentially less severe forms of asthma. However, the lower ICS adherence of younger adults remains a concern, as it may lead to poor long-term asthma control and increased healthcare use if not addressed [5, 18–20]. Strategies to improve adherence of younger patients may need to focus on education, lifestyle integration of medication routines, and the reduction of barriers to consistent medication use. At a health system and policy level, these age-specific patterns suggest that adherence strategies should be tailored by age group, with younger adults targeted through education, digital reminders, and reduced cost or access barriers, and older adults through integrated care models that address asthma, comorbidities, and infection prevention.

One notable strength of this investigation lies in the use of a large, nationally representative sample and rigorous statistical techniques to assess how age and adherence might influence asthma outcomes. However, several limitations also need to be considered. First, our retrospective study design and reliance on prescription data to measure adherence mean that we may not have fully captured actual medication use, and the study did not account for other factors, such as the inhaler technique, which can influence the effectiveness of ICS therapy. Second, because this study drew on claims data from the Korean NHIS, we lacked direct measures or robust proxies of baseline asthma severity, prior exacerbations, and asthma control status, which may have led to residual confounding of age-related differences in adherence and outcomes. Third, we used a relatively permissive 120-day permissible gap between prescriptions to reflect real-world follow-up patterns, which may overestimate persistence and introduce some age-related bias. Fourth, as this research was conducted in South Korea, its findings may not extend to populations in other regions where healthcare systems and cultural views on medication use differ. Future research could include larger, multi-country, prospective studies with more detailed clinical characterization to address these limitations and provide more comprehensive insights into asthma management across diverse populations.

In conclusion, this study highlights the importance of age as a significant factor in adherence to ICS therapy among adult patients with asthma and highlights the need for age-specific strategies to improve asthma management. Although older adults exhibited better medication adherence, they also faced greater risks of severe exacerbations and pneumonia, indicating the need for a comprehensive care approach that addresses both adherence and the unique challenges associated with aging.
