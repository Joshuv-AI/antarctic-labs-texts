Multifunctional Hybrid Nanomedicines for Neurodegenerative Diseases: A Paradigm Shift from Rational Design to Clinical Translation
Authors: Yuqin Wu, Mukun Wang, Tianpeng Li, Zhihui Huang, Min Wu, Tingting Sun, Hailin Cong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractEffective drug delivery to the central nervous system remains a persistent challenge owing to the blood–brain barrier (BBB), heterogeneous pathological microenvironments, and the limited capacity of conventional carriers to achieve precise spatiotemporal control over drug release. Multifunctional hybrid nanomedicines (MHNs), defined here as engineered nanosystems that integrate two or more functional modules, such as targeted delivery, controlled release, and/or theranostic capabilities within a single platform, offer a transformative approach to neurotherapeutic design. This review traces the evolution of MHNs from passive carriers to stimuli-responsive, “smart” delivery platforms and presents a unified design framework built upon four synergistic strategies: (1) surface engineering for receptor-mediated BBB transcytosis and site-specific accumulation; (2) biomimetic surface modification to prolong systemic circulation and evade immune-mediated clearance; (3) controlled release systems responsive to endogenous stimuli (pH, redox gradients, pathologically elevated metal ions) or exogenous triggers (light, magnetic fields), with particular emphasis on metal-coordination bonds as tunable, microenvironment-sensitive release switches; and (4) theranostic integration enabling image-guided drug delivery and real-time therapeutic monitoring. The construction strategies, synergistic mechanisms, mechanistic principles, and therapeutic functions underpinning these four approaches are systematically examined in the context of neurodegenerative diseases. The current clinical translation landscape corresponding to each approach is also comprehensively surveyed. Furthermore, while most integrated MHN platforms remain at the preclinical stage, the principal challenges confronting MHNs are critically assessed, and prospective development trajectories associated with each approach are identified and discussed, thereby providing a substantive reference framework for the next-generation design of MHNs with effective spatiotemporal controlled release capabilities.

Introduction

Neurodegenerative diseases (NDs), including Alzheimer’s disease (AD), Parkinson’s disease (PD), Huntington’s disease (HD), and amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS), are characterized by progressive neuronal loss and irreversible functional decline. Their pathogenesis involves a complex interplay of genetic, environmental, and age-related factors, leading to shared hallmarks such as aberrant protein aggregation, neuroinflammation, oxidative stress, and critically blood-brain barrier (BBB) impairment.1,2 This multifactorial nature renders conventional single-target therapies largely ineffective, often due to compensatory pathways and dose-limiting systemic toxicity.3 Moreover, the BBB excludes over 98% of potential therapeutics, severely limiting drug delivery to the brain.4 Even recently approved monoclonal antibodies for AD exhibit modest efficacy and carry risks of adverse imaging abnormalities, underscoring an urgent, unmet need for strategies that can efficiently navigate the BBB or alternative route, selectively target diseased microenvironments, and concurrently address multiple pathological cascades.5,6

Nanomedicine has emerged as a transformative paradigm to meet this challenge, evolving from passive carriers to active, “smart” platforms capable of targeted delivery and environmental responsiveness.7,8 Among these, multifunctional hybrid nanomedicines (MHNs) refer specifically to integrated nanotherapeutic systems engineered through the synergistic combination of hybrid carrier platforms and rationally integrated functional components. The carrier platforms hierarchically integrate lipids, polymers, and/or biologics (eg, engineered exosomes, modified AAV vectors, nanomaterial-armored bacteria, liposomal systems, and polymeric/inorganic nanoparticles), while the functional components encompass surface targeting ligands, biomimetic coatings, disease-responsive release mechanisms, and diagnostic reporters (eg, PET tracers) or external stimulus modalities (eg, focused ultrasound). By design, such synergistic integration enables MHNs to co-encapsulate diverse cargoes (eg, small molecules, oligonucleotides), respond to disease-specific stimuli, traverse the BBB, and achieve real-time theranostic feedback. With several candidates now entering early-phase clinical trials for NDs, MHNs represent the forefront of precision nanomedicine.9,10 By design, MHNs can co-encapsulate diverse cargoes (eg, small molecules, oligonucleotides), respond to disease-specific stimuli, and incorporate targeting ligands for BBB traversal. However, the rapid expansion of this field has led to a plethora of innovative but dispersed designs. A critical gap remains: the lack of a unifying conceptual framework to rationalize the design of these complex systems, relate their engineering to specific pathological targets, and guide future development toward clinical success.

To fill this gap, this review moves beyond conventional, component-oriented classifications. We propose and elaborate a function-driven, modular design framework centered on four synergistic cores: (1) Surface Engineering for precise targeting and trafficking, (2) Biomimetic Technology for immune evasion and enhanced biocompatibility, (3) Intelligent Responsiveness to endogenous or exogenous triggers for spatiotemporal control, and (4) Integrated Theranostics for simultaneous diagnosis and treatment. Importantly, these four cores are conceived not as mutually exclusive technology categories, but as interconnected functional dimensions of MHN design. Their progressive convergence and rational integration underpin the transition from conventional nanocarriers toward multifunctional hybrid systems capable of addressing multiple biological barriers and pathological processes simultaneously. Accordingly, our framework considers both the established roles of individual functional modules and the potential complementary or synergistic advantages that may arise from their rational integration. This framework is not merely descriptive; it provides a rational blueprint for constructing next-generation MHNs tailored to the multifaceted pathology of NDs.

To contextualize this framework and chart the path forward, the review is structured as follows: First, we trace the evolutionary trajectory from foundational brain-targeted nanocarriers to the emergence and key milestones of MHNs, illustrating how progressive functionalization and hybridization have shaped increasingly sophisticated multifunctional platforms. We then systematically dissect each of the four core paradigms, detailing their design principles, mechanisms, and roles in combating NDs pathology. Building on this paradigmatic analysis, we examine clinical translation status and persistent challenges. Finally, we project future directions, highlighting emerging tools that promise closed-loop, adaptive neurotherapeutics. The underlying systematic literature search strategy is detailed in the Supplementary methods.

Evolution of MHNs for Brain-Targeted Therapies

The concept of nanomedicine emerged in the late 1970s with the introduction of liposomal vesicles as carriers for small-molecule drugs.11 These early systems were primarily monofunctional, designed for passive encapsulation and release, lacking capabilities for active targeting or theranostic integration. A pivotal milestone occurred in 1995 with the clinical approval of Doxil®, a PEGylated liposome that leveraged the Enhanced Permeability and Retention (EPR) effect to achieve prolonged circulation and passive targeting to systemic solid tumors. While Doxil itself was a monofunctional therapeutic agent, its success validated liposomal bilayers as versatile scaffolds and critically demonstrated the clinical viability of nano-engineering for improved drug delivery.12 This breakthrough spurred subsequent efforts to incorporate additional, orthogonal functionalities, such as imaging agents and targeting moieties, thereby paving the way for the later development of MHNs in the 2000s, which strategically integrate diverse components (eg, organic and inorganic materials) within a single nanoarchitecture. In 2002, Kreuter’s group demonstrated that apolipoprotein-E-functionalized poly (butyl cyanoacrylate) nanoparticles could cross the BBB via low-density lipoprotein receptor-mediated transcytosis, achieving a seven-fold increase in brain uptake of dalargin.13 This work established surface-ligand-directed brain targeting, a principle later extended to ligands such as transferrin, RVG-29, and angiopep-2 peptides14 (Figure 1). In the late 2000s, a new wave of biomimetic nanotherapeutic carriers emerged. One representative example involves recombinant adeno-associated virus serotype 2 encoding glutamic acid decarboxylase, which restores inhibitory GABA ergic circuitry in the subthalamic nucleus through localized GABA synthesis.15 Another typical biomimetic nanoplatform features a hybrid coating constructed by fusing natural red blood cell and platelet membranes, thereby integrating the complementary functions of both cell types. This design endows the carrier with the prolonged circulation and immune-evasion properties of erythrocytes, together with the lesion-homing and targeting capabilities characteristic of platelets. Consequently, it achieves efficient multifunctionality on a single nanoplatform, providing a versatile and intelligent vehicle for targeted therapy.16 Recent studies describe a biomimetic MHN featuring three integrated functions: “sponge-like adsorption,” “targeted delivery,” and “microglial reprogramming.” Its hybrid cell membrane coating (MES23.5 cell membrane/ IFN-γ-treated RAW264.7 cell membrane) serves as an intelligent homing system and decoy sponge, wherein MES23.5 cell membrane targets inflamed microglia via VCAM-1 binding, while IFN-γ-treated RAW264.7 cell membrane-rich in IL1R1 and TNFR1-neutralizes excess neuroinflammatory cytokines, thereby dampening harmful immune signaling; the coating then facilitates endocytosis of the core nanoparticle (Cu2-xSe nanoparticle). Inside microglia, Cu2-xSe nanoparticle simultaneously inhibits excessive α-synuclein uptake via RAGE blockade and boosts mature cathepsin D-dependent lysosomal degradation, achieving extracellular inflammation clearance and intracellular α-synuclein aggregate removal, ultimately reprogramming pathogenic microglia into pro-resolution phenotypes and markedly improving PD outcomes17 (Figure 1).

During this period, advancements in stimuli-responsive chemistry enabled the development of sophisticated metal-organic hybrid nanoparticles, primarily for oncology applications, while redox-responsive nanomedicines emerged for NDs research. For example, a siRNA delivery system employs a pH-responsive diblock copolymer synthesized via controlled reversible addition fragmentation chain transfer polymerization, self-assembling into ~40 nm spherical micelles with a low critical micelle concentration (~2 μg/mL), a cationic dimethylaminoethyl methacrylate shell for efficient siRNA complexation, and a hydrophobic/pH-sensitive core (dimethylaminoethyl methacrylate / propylacrylic acid / butyl methacylate) that ensures structural stability and promotes endosomal escape. The micelles maintain integrity upon siRNA binding, achieve ~90% cellular uptake, and enable potent gene silencing.18 The next developed nanotheranostic system (Congo Red/Rutin-multiple functional nanoparticles) employs ultrasmall PEGylated magnetic nanoparticles (~13 nm) that exhibit excellent biocompatibility, prolonged blood circulation, and enhanced MRI contrast performance. Congo red is covalently conjugated for specific targeting of Aβ plaques via β-sheet recognition, while Rutin is linked through a H2O2-responsive boronate ester bond, enabling on-demand drug release in the plaque microenvironment rich in ROS.19 By 2020s, the field achieved a landmark with spatiotemporal precision and gene-editing capabilities. Gong et al developed a disulfide-crosslinked silica nanocapsule designed for intracellular GSH-responsive release after brain-targeted delivery. The surface is sequentially modified with glucose and rabies virus-derived peptide (RVG29) to enable stepwise BBB penetration and neuron-specific targeting: glucose promotes GLUT1-mediated transcytosis across the BBB, while RVG29 mediates neuronal uptake via nicotinic acetylcholine receptors. After internalization into neurons, high intracellular GSH triggers rapid nanocapsule disassembly, achieving on-demand release of payloads (eg, CRISPR-Cas9 ribonucleoprotein) directly inside target cells. This dual-ligand, stimuli-responsive system provides precise and high genome-editing efficiency with minimal off-target effects, offering a promising non-invasive platform for systemic treatment of NDs20 (Figure 1).

Recent advancements have focused on the development of an “all-in-one” MHN. Albumin nanoparticles co-encapsulating clioquinol (a metal chelator) and donepezil (an acetylcholinesterase inhibitor) were surface-functionalized with TAT peptide for cytosolic delivery and GM1 ganglioside for neuronal targeting. Intranasal administration restored memory performance to wild-type levels within 30 days in preclinical models.21 In 2024, Kantor developed a CRISPR/dCas9-based epigenome editing platform using an AAV-delivered “all-in-one” vector. This system combines dSaCas9, a KRAB-MeCP2 repressor, and gRNA to target gene promoters, inducing heterochromatin (eg, H3K9me3) for sustained gene silencing. It achieved 70% suppression of apolipoprotein-E expression, reducing disease-related markers22 (Figure 1).

Viewed collectively, these milestones delineate a continuous evolution from foundational nanocarriers to increasingly multifunctional and hybrid architectures, in which surface engineering, biomimicry, stimuli responsiveness, and theranostic capabilities progressively converge. This evolutionary trajectory provides the conceptual basis for the four function-driven design paradigms discussed in the following sections.

Synergistic Design Strategies for MHNs: The SIM-Map Framework

In the evolving landscape of ND therapeutics, single-function nanocarriers are increasingly inadequate for addressing the complex pathological network involving the BBB, neuroinflammation, and protein aggregation. Recent advancements have shifted focus toward MHNs, emphasizing a “synergistic design and integrated evaluation” paradigm.23 Departing from conventional linear “material-structure” analyses, we introduce the “Synergy-Integration Modular Map” (SIM-Map) framework, rooted in synergistic enhancement and integrated burden reduction. The SIM-Map organizes MHN developments into four synergistic functional modules: (1) Surface Engineering, (2) Biomimetic Camouflage, (3) Intelligent Response, and (4) Theranostic Integration. The integrated module, encompassing characterization, synergistic mechanisms, and integrated interfaces, to highlight both horizontal functional synergy and vertical system integration (Figure 2). By transforming fragmented research into a cohesive “synergy-integration” knowledge chain, the SIM-Map elucidates critical synergistic nodes and integration gaps, offering a replicable narrative framework for the iterative design of next-generation nanomedicines tailored to NDs. Surface Engineering: Manipulating the Nano-Bio Synergistic Interface

Surface engineering of MHNs orchestrates the nano-bio interface to achieve synergistic functionality and integrated therapeutic outcomes for NDs. By precisely tailoring nanoparticle physicochemical properties, such as size, charge, hydrophilicity, and ligand functionalization, surface modifications enhance brain delivery while synergizing with biomimetic camouflage, intelligent response, and theranostic integration modules of the SIM-Map framework (Figure 2). These designs balance stealth properties for prolonged circulation with active targeting for precise lesion engagement, forming a versatile platform that optimizes delivery efficiency, biocompatibility, and clinical translatability (Figure 3). Passive Targeting and Stealth Properties

Passive targeting strategies prioritize minimizing nonspecific interactions to confer “stealth” properties and extended systemic circulation, thereby enhancing nanoparticle accumulation in pathological brain regions through disease-dependent vascular permeability and related mechanisms. The EPR effect, originally validated in systemic solid tumors, historically informed this prolonged-circulation paradigm.24 The cornerstone approach is PEGylation, where methoxy-poly(ethylene glycol) (mPEG, typically ≥2–5 kDa) is grafted onto the nanoparticle surface to form a hydrophilic, sterically shielding layer. This hinders serum protein adsorption (by up to ~90% in specific model systems) reduces macrophage uptake by >90% under optimized dense-brush conditions, and markedly extends circulation half-lives, reaching ~15–20 hours in mice and >24 hours in humans for selected long-circulating formulations25,26 (Figure 4a). To mitigate potential PEG immunogenicity, alternatives include zwitterionic polymers such as poly(carboxybetaine) or poly(2-methyl-2-oxazoline) (PMOXA) brushes, which provide superior oxidative stability and absent anti-PEG antibody recognition.27 Complementary charge neutralization, achieved through zwitterionic coatings, surface acetylation, or short-chain phosphatidylcholine insertion, yields zeta potentials of −5 to +5 mV, attenuating electrostatic adhesion to negatively charged endothelial glycocalyx and minimizing complement activation.28 For example, chitosan-modified nanoparticles with near-neutral surfaces (+3 mV) exhibit 2.3-fold higher brain accumulation than cationic variants (+25 mV) in Alzheimer’s models,29 while polyzwitterion-coated PLGA nanoparticles (70 nm, neutral charge) achieve 40% higher Aβ plaque targeting.30 These “inert” coatings also serve as a scaffold for downstream functionalities, such as incorporating pH-responsive linkers for triggered release in acidic neuroinflammatory environments or alkyne/maleimide handles for oriented ligand conjugation30 (Figure 4b). Active Targeting

Building on this stealth foundation, surface engineering evolves into a programmable “docking platform” that exploits ligand–receptor interactions amplified at pathological sites. MHNs are decorated with antibodies, peptides, aptamers, or vitamin analogues targeting over-expressed receptors such as TfR, LRP-1, nAChR, or FR-β on brain endothelium and diseased cells, enabling receptor-mediated transcytosis across the BBB and lesion-specific retention31,32 (Figure 5a). Examples include antibody-decorated liposomes that bind extracellular Aβ protofibrils with sub-nanomolar avidity, accelerating microglial clearance and reducing cortical amyloid,33 and dual-peptide PEG–PLA nanoassemblies (H102: NAP at 2:1 ratio) that simultaneously recognize amyloid plaques and synaptic adhesion molecules, synergistically lowering Aβ and hyper-phosphorylated tau while restoring memory deficits34 (Figure 5b). Advanced designs incorporate sheddable PEG blocks that expose hidden peptides in response to upregulated MMP-2 in gliotic niches, pH-sensitive histidine-rich domains for endosomal escape, or “don’t-eat-me” CD47 peptides to evade microglial phagocytosis35–37 (Figure 5c). A typical example of receptor-mediated brain delivery is lactoferrin transcytosis across the BBB. Blockade with receptor-associated protein (RAP), an antagonist of the low-density lipoprotein receptor-related protein (LRP) pathway, reduced lactoferrin transport by approximately 70%, suggesting the involvement of LRP-associated mechanisms in this process.38 Separately, TAT-modified lipid nanocarriers have been developed to enhance the intranasal delivery of glial cell line-derived neurotrophic factor (GDNF) to the brain in experimental PD,38 whereas nanoparticles co-functionalized with lactoferrin and borneol have been employed to improve the nose-to-brain delivery and therapeutic efficacy of dopamine.39 The brain-targeting potential of lactoferrin functionalization has also been demonstrated with lactoferrin-modified PEG-co-PCL nanoparticles, which enhanced intranasal delivery of the neuroprotective peptide NAP across multiple brain regions.40 Notably, carnosic acid-loaded chitosan nanoparticles increased brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) levels in the brain by approximately 2.7-fold compared with the corresponding control treatment41 (Figure 5c). Collectively, these ligand-decorated surfaces act as a central command hub, orchestrating circulation time, cellular zip-code recognition, organelle trafficking, and on-demand release, providing a translatable strategy for precision therapy in neurodegeneration while integrating seamlessly with other SIM-Map modules. Biomimetic Technology: Nature-Inspired Delivery

To achieve superior biocompatibility beyond conventional PEGylation or ligand grafting, the field has shifted toward nature-inspired delivery systems that incorporate entire biological modules to navigate the neurovascular microenvironment. These biomimetic constructs replicate the surface topology, membrane viscoelasticity, and molecular recognition signatures of endogenous vectors, such as exosomes, erythrocytes, platelets, or stem cells, transforming the BBB from a barrier into a selectively permeable interface.42–45 In preclinical models of AD, PD, and ALS, such systems yield up to one order of magnitude higher cerebral accumulation, reduced microglial activation, and efficient delivery of proteins, nucleic acids, and small molecules across cerebral endothelial cells.46,47

This paradigm evolves along a deliberate continuum: an initial “surface-modification class” that cloaks scalable synthetic cores with natural membranes or protein mosaics;48,49 a hybrid tier combining biomimetic camouflage with engineered chemical functionalities; and a “carrier class” that deploys intact extracellular vesicles/exosomes or living vectors, coopting their full lipid bilayers, cytosolic cargo, and trafficking circuits wholesale.50,51 These tiers are not mutually exclusive but form a versatile design spectrum, allowing precise matching to the pathophysiological needs of individual NDs (Figure 6a–e). Biomimetic Surface Decoration and the Continuum Toward Hybrids

Biomimetic surface decoration marks a shift from chemical conjugation (eg, synthetic polymers or covalent linkers for stealth/ligands) to direct translocation of natural cell membranes or fragments onto polymeric cores. This preserves intrinsic proteins, glycolipids, and antigens for extended circulation, immune evasion, and organ tropism, while adding a soft, deformable lipid shell with native-like bending modulus and fluidity, enabling receptor-mediated transcytosis across the BBB far more efficiently than rigid PEGylated systems43,52,53 (Figure 6f–j).

Surface topology becomes dynamic and raft-like, reorganizing under shear stress or ligand binding to expose cryptic peptides (eg, angiopep-2) on demand. This behavior unattainable with traditional chemistry.54,55 Membrane viscoelasticity enhances margination, deformation, and endosomal escape; erythrocyte-mimicking nanoparticles show lower storage modulus than PLGA-PEG analogs, prolonging vascular residence and enabling brain parenchymal accumulation without mannitol-induced BBB disruption.56–58 Inherited molecular signatures add disease-modulating effects, such as platelet–CCR2 hybrids homing to Aβ plaques while sequestering pro-inflammatory CCL2 to reprogram the microenvironment52 (Figure 6f–j).

This camouflage synergizes with synthetic chemistry: natural membrane benefits (prolonged circulation, innate targeting from erythrocyte/macrophage sources)59,60 combine with stimuli-responsive cores (eg, ROS-cleavable dendrimers or metal-phenolic networks) for controlled release.61,62 Co-modification of macrophage membranes with targeting peptides outperforms either alone,63 enabling hybrid systems that layer precise chemical functionalities beneath lipid bilayers for stealth, pathological homing, and context-dependent release, bridging to more advanced tiers.

Progression to Living Vectors

At the holistic end of the continuum, intact biological entities, such as viruses, bacteria, or macrophages, serve as living “plug-and-play” chassis, engineered to detect disease signals, amplify diagnostics, and deliver payloads while maintaining viability and safety.64 Viral vectors exploit optimized capsids/glycoproteins for axonal retrograde transport and neuron-selective transduction; lentiviral/AAV capsids displaying RVG or ApoE peptides achieve >10-fold higher hippocampal transgene expression than lipid nanoparticles, packaging CRISPR–dCas9 for epigenetic silencing of α-synuclein or BACE-1.22,65 Bacterial examples include attenuated Salmonella typhimurium, leveraging flagellar motility and chemotaxis to target neuroinflammation,66 with autolysis triggered by low Mg2⁺ to release CRISPR–Cas9 under inflammation-sensitive promoters.67 Hybrid “viro-exosome” systems, such as exosome-associated AAV vectors, enhance targeted gene delivery and immune modulation by encapsulating viral capsids within engineered exosomes, achieving superior cellular specificity and therapeutic efficacy at lower doses than unmodified AAV vectors,68 and hUC MSC–derived exosome–nanoliposome hybrids potently suppress α-synuclein fibrillation, secondary nucleation, disaggregation, and neurotoxicity while facilitating BBB crossing and cellular uptake.69

Exosomal vectors enhance synergy via ultrasound assistance and epigenetic response: RVG-targeted CRISPRi-exosomes enable focused ultrasound-mediated blood-brain barrier opening and CRISPRi-induced methylation of the α-Synuclein gene, reducing α-synuclein levels in PD models.70 Commensal vectors enhance synergy via olfactory localization and targeted payload response: engineered Lactobacillus plantarum WCFS1 retaining OppA proteins enables intranasal nose-to-brain delivery through N-acetyl heparan sulfate recognition on olfactory epithelium cilia, facilitating localized secretion and brain accumulation of neurotrophic factors for CNS-targeted therapeutic applications.71 On-demand synthesis occurs via hypoxia-inducible circuits in attenuated Salmonella typhimurium for anti-TNF-α nanobodies within the tumor microenvironment,72 and via constitutive secretion systems in commensal Escherichia coli Nissle 1917 for anti-TNF-α nanobody delivery at mucosal inflammatory sites.73 Safety incorporates biocontainment strategies, including the use of attenuated food-grade chassis strains (such as Lactococcus lactis MG1363) with CRISPR-based kill switches, auxotrophic markers, or thyA deletion to ensure transient colonization and prevent persistence.74 Design follows a “sense-process-act-erase” framework adapted for the gut-brain axis: select robust gut-colonizing chassis, eliminate unnecessary virulence or pathogenicity elements, add cue-responsive circuits (eg, optogenetic light-inducible promoters for red-light control), encode neuroprotective therapeutics/reporters (such as GLP-1 analogs or p62), and implement clearance.75 These platforms enable sustained local production with in situ amplification via bacterial replication in the gut, facilitating gut-brain axis modulation, reduced neuroinflammation and protein aggregation (Aβ/α-synuclein), improved cognitive/motor function, and lower systemic doses compared with injected biologics, though with regulatory/manufacturing challenges. Emerging advanced systems, such as optogenetically or magnetically controlled CRISPR-edited probiotics, merge bacterial programmability, precise colonization control, immune modulation, and self-regulation, exemplifying intelligent biomimetic medicine for NDs across the continuum.76

Intelligent Responsiveness: Precise Spatiotemporal Control

Building on bionic architectures that confer endothelium-crossing and lesion-targeting abilities, microenvironment-responsive nanomedicines introduce a second layer of spatiotemporal precision. These systems exploit the pathological hallmarks of ND lesions: acidic pH, redox imbalance, enzyme overexpression, or hypoxia, as intrinsic triggers for cargo release. Upon detection of endogenous (pH, redox, enzyme, or hypoxia) or exogenous (light, ultrasound, or magnetic field) cues, or externally induced thermal stimulation, the carriers undergo stimulus-dependent physicochemical changes, such as protonation, bond cleavage, hypoxia-activated bioreduction, phase transition, charge reversal, or hydrophilicity switching, resulting in swelling, disassembly, or cargo liberation precisely at the diseased site.77–79 This on-demand, lesion-confined release maximizes local drug concentration while minimizing exposure of healthy brain tissue.80 To address BBB efflux pumps and multifaceted neurodegenerative mechanisms, co-delivery of therapeutics and complementary modulators can be synchronized via the same trigger, jointly enhancing brain penetration and counteracting neuronal apoptotic pathways.78 Representative building blocks include pH-sensitive polyhistidine, enzyme-cleavable peptides, reduction-labile disulfides, and thermosensitive lipids, which integrate active targeting with stimulus responsiveness to yield safe, efficient platforms for ND therapy.77,81 Coordination chemistry has recently emerged as a unifying design principle bridging endogenous and exogenous responsiveness. The tunable bond energies (50–400 kJ mol−1), reversible lability, and well-defined geometry of metal–ligand interactions enable simultaneous microenvironment sensing, logical signal processing, and on-demand therapeutic action, effectively functioning as a “molecular processor” within MHNs.82–84 This coordination logic permeates the trigger categories discussed below.

Endogenous Triggers (pH/Redox/Enzyme/ Hypoxia)

Endogenous-responsive systems harness the biochemical signatures of neurodegeneration: elevated ROS, protease overexpression, acidic compartments, and hypoxia, to convert pathological cues into release signals.80,85–87 The core design principle is consistent across triggers: incorporate a labile or switchable unit that remains stable in circulation but responds sharply to the target stimulus, integrate it into a carrier capable of transporting therapeutics or scavengers, and ensure drugs accumulation in lesions via passive or active targeting strategies.77,80 This yields two key benefits: site-specific drug release and localized microenvironment remodeling (ROS scavenging, lysosomal pH correction, fibril disruption, enzyme inhibition), effects unattainable with conventional delivery.85–87

Redox-responsive systems are the most established. Diselenide, thioketal, or boronic ester linkages incorporated into mesoporous silica or polymeric micelles undergo rapid cleavage at the elevated H2O2 (>50 µM) levels associated with oxidative stress in Parkinson’s and Alzheimer’s lesions, with representative diselenide systems releasing >80% payload over approximately 3–6 days88,89 (Supplementary Table 1). Although efficient and irreversible, redox activation lacks inherent spatial exclusivity and requires orthogonal cues (pH, enzyme, magnetic guidance) or anatomical routes (intranasal) for selectivity.90 Coordination chemistry expands redox strategies beyond covalent cleavage. For example, tannic acid–Mn2⁺ clusters remain stable under physiological conditions but disassemble in elevated GSH environments, enabling site-specific release91 (Supplementary Table 2). Coordination also enables active engagement of disease-relevant metals: triphenylphosphonium–quercetin conjugates coordinate mitochondrial Fe2⁺ to form nanocomplexes that sequester iron and suppress lipid peroxidation, mitigating ferroptosis.82 Similarly, bipyridine-decorated hydrogen-bonded organic frameworks (HOFs) selectively extract Cu2⁺ from neurotoxic Aβ–Cu complexes while activating NADH peroxidase-like activity to restore redox homeostasis92 (Supplementary Table 1). These approaches couple payload release with metal chelation and catalytic ROS detoxification.

Enzyme-responsive carriers could exploit overexpressed proteases (cathepsin B, AEP, MMP-9) via cleavable peptide linkers, alongside esterase-responsive systems targeting aberrantly active AChE through acetylcholine ester-based gatekeeping mechanisms.93–95 Catalytic kinetics provide signal amplification (varying widely by system), and the strict substrate specificity of AEP confers high target/non-target selectivity in preclinical models, though direct quantitative comparisons across systems remain system-dependent and require careful contextualization.94,96 Release kinetics are comparatively slow (t½ typically 1–3 h, varying by linker design and local enzyme concentration) and are subject to inter-individual enzyme variability.97 Coordination chemistry complements enzyme platforms in diagnostics, as illustrated by Zr4⁺-phosphate affinity in MOF nanoplates for ultrasensitive kinase detection (0.35 mU mL−1),98 and metal-catalyzed C=C cleavage for NIR fluorophore activation without net metal consumption99 (Figure 7a–e, Supplementary Table 1). pH-responsive carriers target endosomal (~5.5) or inflamed-tissue (6.2–6.8) acidity using poly (β-amino ester) s or nucleolipid groups that trigger proton-sponge or charge-reversal mechanisms, or biodegradable polyesters such as PLGA whose acid-catalyzed hydrolysis is accelerated in these environments. These systems have demonstrated utility in neurodegenerative models, including gene delivery to astrocytes and dopaminergic neurons100–102 (Supplementary Table 1). Coordination offers an alternative: pH-labile Mn-templated topological structures (eg, trefoil knots and Borromean rings) undergo lysosomal acidification-triggered disassembly to release Mn2⁺ ions, and providing high-relaxivity T1-MRI contrast for integrated theranostics, which demonstrated in glioblastoma models.103

Hypoxia-responsive nanomedicine targets brain-tissue hypoxia (<5% O2) in NDs, driven by vascular dysfunction, mitochondrial impairment, or neuroinflammation, using nitroimidazole derivatives, azobenzene linkers, or quinone-based polymers that undergo enzyme-mediated bioreductive cleavage (nitroreductase or azoreductase) under low-oxygen tension.104,105 These carriers remain stable in normoxic environments but trigger rapid nanoparticle disassembly or shell degradation, enabling spatiotemporal release of neuroprotective payloads such as superoxide dismutase mimics, curcumin analogs, or anti-amyloidogenic agents directly into hypoxic microenvironments while facilitating BBB penetration via receptor-mediated or adsorptive transcytosis.106,107 In addition, hypoxia-labile metal–organic frameworks or azo-linked coordination polymers undergo reductive disassembly to release Mn2⁺ or Fe2⁺ ions, providing high-relaxivity T1-MRI contrast for integrated theranostics, as shown in preclinical brain disorder models.108

Exogenous Triggers (Light/US/Magnetic)

Exogenous-responsive systems treat the diseased brain as a multiphysics environment in which externally applied energies enable precise nanoscale control over drug release and therapeutic action. Functional nanomaterials transduce light, ultrasound, or magnetic fields into localized biological effects, overcoming BBB restrictions and improving delivery efficiency.78 Photothermal agents (gold nanorods, IR780-loaded nanostructures) convert NIR light into heat, transiently enhancing BBB permeability via thermosensitive channels (eg, TRPV1) while triggering release from thermoresponsive matrices109 (Supplementary Table 1). Alternatively, photo-oxidation agents such as AuNCs@HSA-B convert NIR light into singlet oxygen, enabling direct oxidative modification of Aβ monomers to inhibit fibrillogenesis and alleviate neurotoxicity110 (Supplementary Table 1). Magnetic nanoparticles generate hyperthermia under alternating magnetic fields, disaggregating Aβ plaques, promoting microglial clearance, and enabling magnetothermal neuromodulation111,112 (Supplementary Table 1). Coordination chemistry integrates multiple functions into single platforms. The IR780-Mn@TA-TPL system uses a tannic acid–Mn2⁺ scaffold to combine T1-MRI contrast, NIR photothermal conversion, fluorescence imaging, and neuron targeting; NIR irradiation triggers localized release while dual-modal imaging monitors combined antioxidant and anti-tau therapy in AD models.91 Ultrasound-responsive carriers exploit inertial cavitation (microbubbles) for transient BBB opening or piezoelectric materials (eg, MoS2@Bi2S3) for non-thermal ROS generation and antioxidant catalysis.113 Microbubble-encapsulated “cluster-bomb” architectures achieve >70-fold increases in siRNA delivery with reversible BBB opening lasting up to 48 h.114 (Supplementary Table 1).

Comparative evaluations of stimuli-responsive nanomedicines for NDs highlight the complementary strengths of endogenous triggers (redox, enzymatic, or pH gradients characteristic of inflamed brain microenvironments) and exogenous physical stimuli (near-infrared light, focused ultrasound, magnetic fields, or thermal modulation), with hybrid platforms achieving enhanced spatiotemporal precision through concurrent activation requirements. Multi-responsive designs, such as ROS-cleavable nanoparticles paired with NIR photothermal agents115 (Figure 7f–I, Supplementary Table 1), enzyme-targeted nanobubbles integrated with focused ultrasound for transient BBB opening116 (Supplementary Table 1), or pH-sensitive magnetoliposomes,117 enable logic-gate-like control that reduces premature release and improves therapeutic indices in preclinical Alzheimer’s and Parkinson’s models, although challenges in scalability, biocompatibility, and clinical translation remain.

Integrated Theranostics: A Platform for Precision Medicine

Theranostic MHNs serve as closed-loop “see-and-treat” platforms by seamlessly integrating two or more of the paradigms: surface functionalization approaches outlined in Surface Engineering: Manipulating the Nano-Bio Synergistic Interface, the endogenous and exogenous responsiveness detailed in Intelligent Responsiveness: Precise Spatiotemporal Control, and the biomimetic camouflage of Biomimetic Technology: Nature-Inspired Delivery into a unified nanoscale vector. Their modular “core–shell–corona” blueprint comprises a therapeutic core (lipid, PLGA, or SPION; 30–80 nm),118–120 and an imaging shell encoding quantitative contrast (NIR-II fluorophores, Gd-DOTA, or ultrasmall SPIONs) pre-calibrated so that the same signal guiding positioning also potentially serves as a surrogate for local drug concentration121–123 (Supplementary Table 2); and a PEG corona bearing dual ligands: CRT (transferrin receptor) and QSH (Aβ-plaque specific). Consistent with the dual-ligand benefits, SDP@Cur-CRT/QSH nanoparticles outperform their mono-ligand counterparts in multiple endpoints, including Aβ plaque volume reduction (1.23 mm3 vs 1.73 mm3), attenuation of neuroinflammation (NLRP3/IL-18/CD68 downregulation, p<0.05–0.001), and cognitive recovery in APP/PS1 mice (improved Morris water maze performance), while exhibiting excellent colloidal stability (PDI 0.174–0.245, ζ ≈ −19.3 mV)124 (Supplementary Table 2).

The core advancement of this architecture lies in the intrinsic coupling of imaging and therapy: the physicochemical trigger that initiates drug release simultaneously activates or modulates the contrast signal, thereby establishing a closed-loop feedback cycle that permeates every stage from initial targeting to efficacy evaluation. At the entry phase, ligand–receptor binding facilitates transcytosis across the BBB, a process that can be monitored in real time via NIR-II imaging (penetration depth ~3 mm, SNR ≈ 7.4)125,126(Figure 8a–j, Supplementary Table 2). Once the nanoparticles reach the pathological microenvironment, responsive chemistries from Intelligent Responsiveness: Precise Spatiotemporal Control, such as ROS-cleavable boronate esters, pH-labile linkages, or enzyme-sensitive gates, trigger drug release while simultaneously unquenching or inducing a wavelength shift in the tethered probe, enabling direct, pixel-level correlation between therapeutic payload release and target engagement96,102,127 (Supplementary Table 2). The resulting signal can be read out through multiple modalities, such as T1/T2 relaxivity, fluorescence lifetime, or PET intensity, to modulate pharmacodynamic effects in real time, permitting precise dose escalation or de-escalation.128 Achieving this multimodal readout without mutual quenching, however, requires spatial separation of contrast moieties by 2–4 nm, which increases the hydrodynamic diameter by 5–8 nm and may narrow the window for efficient BBB penetration.129 Figure 8Two representative theranostic MHNs for NDs. Exogenous triggers further extend the reach of this feedback architecture. Focused ultrasound, typically applied within the transcranially feasible range of 200 kHz to 1.5 MHz at acoustic pressures of approximately 0.3–0.7 MPa with burst durations on the order of 5–10 ms,130,131 enhances nanoparticle accumulation across the BBB by several-fold to over tenfold depending on nanoparticle size and formulation, as quantified through complementary imaging modalities including PET, MRI, and ICP-OES. PET-based quantification in particular has demonstrated up to 14-fold increases in brain accumulation of radiolabeled liposomal constructs following FUS-mediated BBB opening, with the option to terminate sonication once the signal reaches a predefined threshold, thereby minimizing unnecessary BBB disruption132,133 (Figure 8k–p, Supplementary Table 2). Intranasal administration offers a complementary route that can likewise be monitored noninvasively: following administration, the earliest detectable accumulation occurs preferentially in the olfactory bulb within approximately 30 minutes, while peak concentrations in deeper regions such as the hippocampus are typically reached at 4 hours or beyond, reflecting the kinetics of axonal transport through the cribriform plate.134,135 Overall nose-to-brain delivery efficiency remains modest, generally in the low single-digit percentage range of the administered dose. Near-infrared fluorescence imaging at the cribriform plate has been explored as a potential noninvasive indicator of olfactory uptake and may, in principle, serve as a surrogate signal for guiding infusion timing, though its application as a reliable closed-loop infusion control trigger remains to be formally validated.136 In both cases, however, the fidelity of the feedback loop depends on the temporal alignment between imaging-probe stability and the pharmacokinetics of the therapeutic core, a significant constraint for slowly diffusing payloads such as siRNA–lipid complexes whose release kinetics may extend beyond 48 h, well past the optical window of many fluorophores.137,138

Disease-specific studies illustrate the translational potential of the closed-loop paradigm. In AD, NBGQDs-ICG nano-assemblies cross the BBB under NIR irradiation, generating singlet oxygen that inhibits Aβ42 fibrillation by 91%, disassembles preformed fibrils by 87%, and augments microglial clearance.139 In PD, the neutrophil membrane-biomimetic nanozyme R-NM@Fe-Ic displays enhanced dual SOD/CAT-like activity (2.28-fold greater ROS scavenging), attenuates α-synuclein aggregation, promotes Parkin/PINK1-mediated mitophagy, and preserves 72.4% of dopaminergic neurons (vs 23.6% with L-DOPA).140 For either indication, however, regulatory acceptance of imaging surrogates as biomarkers for dose adjustment will demand rigorous demonstration that signal intensity reliably predicts downstream pharmacodynamic outcomes, a relationship that remains vulnerable to off-target radiometal binding and inter-patient variability in probe metabolism.128,141 Nevertheless, by incorporating quantitative, trigger-coupled contrast at every stage of targeting, activation, and efficacy evaluation, this theranostic platform potentially unifies the strategies from 2.1–2.3 into a cohesive, closed-loop precision-medicine framework for neurodegenerative proteinopathies.

MHNs for NDs: Advances in Clinical Applications

The clinical translation of MHNs remains tightly linked to broader advances in nanoplatforms and brain delivery technologies for NDs. Accordingly, conventional liposomes, focused ultrasound, and viral vectors are not presented here as MHNs per se, but as clinically relevant precursor or enabling platforms that illustrate key translational principles, including biocompatibility, cargo delivery, barrier modulation, and surface functionalization. Their inclusion provides clinically relevant benchmarks for understanding how individual enabling technologies may inform the design and translation of more integrated multifunctional hybrid systems. Early passive liposomes largely failed in NDs due to non-specific distribution, inadequate parenchymal penetration, and limited ability to engage complex pathological cascades. Newer candidates that incorporate one or more functional features (eg, targeting ligands, stimuli-responsive elements, or co-loaded therapeutics) have progressed to mid- and late-stage clinical testing. However, most integrate only one or two such features; fully multifunctional MHNs with integrated multimodal capabilities largely remain preclinical.77 Even when biomarker target engagement is achieved, it rarely translates into clinically meaningful functional or cognitive improvements. This gap is compounded by extremely high attrition rates (>95–99% for ND disease-modifying therapies), logistical and regulatory barriers, manufacturing and scalability challenges, and the practical difficulties of chronic dosing in elderly, frail populations. In stark contrast to oncology where the EPR effect, more permissive tumor microenvironments, shorter treatment timelines, and clearer endpoints facilitate progress. While ND pipelines must overcome sequential BBB and parenchymal hurdles, decades-long insidious pathology, profound disease heterogeneity, and the need for sustained, safe engagement with slowly evolving neurodegenerative cascades.142

Surface Engineering for Receptor-Mediated BBB Transcytosis and Site-Specific Accumulation

Liposome-based active immunotherapies exemplify surface engineering focused on peripheral epitope presentation rather than direct nano-transcytosis. ACI-24.060 (NCT05462106, ABATE Phase 1b/2) displays tetrapalmitoylated Aβ1-15 on MPLA-adjuvanted liposomes to elicit Th2-biased IgG against pathological Aβ oligomers (including pyroGlu-modified species). Interim safety data through December 2024 confirmed no serious adverse events and no ARIA in prodromal AD and Down-syndrome cohorts; full 6- and 12-month amyloid-PET and cognitive readouts remain pending until primary completion (estimated June 2026). Takeda secured an exclusive option in 2024, yet historical precedent (eg, AN1792 halted by aseptic meningoencephalitis) and uncertainty regarding whether peripheral antibody titers will achieve meaningful plaque clearance or slow cognitive decline underscore persistent risks143 (Figure 9a, Supplementary Table 3). Similarly, ACI-35.030 / JNJ-64042056 (NCT06544616, Retain Phase 2b) presents p-Tau396-404 peptides on the same scaffold. Phase 1b/2a data demonstrated rapid, sustained IgG responses and biomarker shifts with excellent tolerability; however, in February 2026 Janssen voluntarily paused recruitment to evaluate logistical aspects (including enrollment challenges in biomarker-defined preclinical cohorts), unrelated to safety (Figure 9b). These liposomal vaccines achieve immune targeting of pathological conformers but rely on downstream antibody crossing rather than carrier-mediated transcytosis. Balanced perspective: small early cohorts, reliance on surrogate endpoints, and the persistent biomarker–clinical disconnect leave efficacy unproven against hard functional outcomes in adequately powered trials144 (Supplementary Table 3). Biomimetic Surface Modification to Prolong Systemic Circulation and Minimize Immune Clearance

Representative preclinical biomimetic platforms highlight inflammation-homing and immune-evasion strategies with high translational promise. One notable example is UDCA-NVs-TPP, a macrophage-membrane nanovesicles loaded with ursodeoxycholic acid (UDCA) and surface-modified with triphenylphosphonium (TPP) for mitochondrial targeting in MPTP of PD mouse models (Figure 10). This construct leverages macrophage-derived membranes for ICAM-1-mediated inflammation homing and natural stealth properties, while TPP enables mitochondrial guidance and UDCA provides antioxidant/mitochondrial rescue. In MPTP mice, UDCA-NVs-TPP restores ATP production, quenches reactive oxygen species, and significantly improves motor scores (eg, reduced pole-climbing time and enhanced hanging ability) without overt toxicity, simultaneously addressing key pathological cores. Nevertheless, substantial translation risks remain. The data derive exclusively from acute toxin-based models that imperfectly recapitulate the genetic heterogeneity, slow chronic progression, α-synuclein pathology, and comorbidities of human idiopathic PD. No clinical-stage candidates of this class currently exist. GMP-scale production of cell-derived vesicles, batch-to-batch consistency, potential long-term immunogenicity with repeated dosing, and regulatory complexities surrounding living-membrane biologics constitute major chemistry, manufacturing, and control as well as safety hurdles145 (Supplementary Table 3). Overall, most biomimetic platforms, despite compelling preclinical efficacy, have yet to demonstrate the prolonged circulation, consistent stealth, and durability required for chronic ND therapy. Controlled Release Systems Responsive to Endogenous or Exogenous Stimuli

Exogenous physical triggers dominate clinical-stage controlled delivery. MRI-guided focused ultrasound with microbubbles (FUS-BBB, multiple Phase 1/2 trials (eg, WVU ExAblate AD expansions and NCT06158789) and ongoing AD combination studies) is a non-nanomaterial procedural platform that transiently (typically within 24–48 h) opens tight junctions, enabling parenchymal entry of co-administered agents or, alone, enhancing microglial phagocytosis and glymphatic clearance146 (Figure 11, Supplementary Table 3). Early studies report safe, reversible opening and focal Aβ/tau reductions (5–50%); larger programs test repeated hemispheric opening on cognitive composites (completion ~2029). However, the approach remains focal rather than global, logistically demanding (specialized MRI centers, anesthesia considerations, multi-hour sessions), and burdensome for elderly frail patients requiring repeated treatments over years. Sample sizes are often small (<20), many lack sham controls or are underpowered for efficacy, and long-term cumulative risks (chronic inflammation, vascular remodeling, microhemorrhage) are incompletely characterized147 (Figure 11, Supplementary Table 3). Theranostic Integration Enabling Image-Guided Drug Delivery and Real-Time Therapeutic Monitoring

Theranostic features appear sporadically. FUS inherently couples BBB opening with real-time MRI guidance, while [11C]MODAG-001 exemplifies a complementary strategy by repurposing a therapeutic lead compound for PET-based monitoring of α-synuclein aggregates.148 Preclinical/early clinical work explores co-loaded imaging agents or bio-orthogonal click-to-release systems, yet fully integrated dual-function MHNs delivering drug while reporting engagement/response have not reached registrational stages148,149 (Figure 12). Regulatory, manufacturing, and dosimetry complexities, combined with the chronic nature of NDs, slow progress. This core remains a critical unmet need for personalized therapy. Collectively, clinical efforts concentrate on surface-engineered immunoliposomes (core 1) and exogenous BBB-opening platforms (core 3), with biomimetic cloaking (core 2) and true theranostics (core 4) lagging. Very few candidates integrate multiple cores, and true MHNs in late-stage testing are scarce despite preclinical advancement142 (Figure 12a). This disease-specific stratification of mechanistic priorities reflects not merely biological heterogeneity but also the differential maturation of surrogate endpoint frameworks endorsed by regulatory agencies. The accelerated approvals granted to anti-amyloid monoclonal antibodies on the basis of amyloid PET and cerebrospinal fluid biomarker surrogacy have structurally incentivized nanocarrier designs capable of augmenting antibody CNS penetration, thereby reinforcing core 1 dominance within AD development portfolios.151 Conversely, the absence of analogous validated surrogate endpoints for α-synuclein pathology, TDP-43 proteinopathy, or superoxide dismutase-1 aggregation in PD and ALS, respectively, constrains the evidentiary pathway available to MHN developers targeting these indications, effectively elevating the clinical and financial risk associated with first-in-class theranostic candidates152 (Figure 12b).

Persistent Challenges and Emerging Perspectives

The four paradigms: surface engineering for receptor-mediated transcytosis, biomimetic immune evasion, stimuli-responsive controlled release, and theranostic integration, have individually generated substantial advances in targeted nanomedicine for NDs. Despite substantial progress, clinical translation for CNS disorders remains hindered by three interconnected challenges: (1) navigating multiple anatomical constrictions (BBB, perivascular spaces, tortuous interstitial matrix); (2) synchronizing drug release with the dynamic spatiotemporal evolution of disease microenvironments; and (3) achieving artifact-free, high-fidelity tracking of drug release in deep brain parenchyma. Overcoming these requires moving beyond passive accumulation toward intelligent, adaptive MHN architectures capable of autonomous navigation, microenvironment-triggered payload deployment, and real-time theranostic feedback.142,153 The development in this field generally follows two directions: breakthroughs within a single framework, and the optimization of framework integration.

From Receptor Targeting to Transcytosis Pathway Engineering: The Next Frontier of Surface Engineering

The current practice of surface engineering for BBB transcytosis operates predominantly at the level of ligand–receptor affinity: selecting a targeting moiety, conjugating it to the nanocarrier surface, and relying on endogenous receptor internalization machinery to initiate transcytotic transport. This approach treats the transcytosis pathway as a fixed biological conduit to be accessed, rather than as a malleable cellular process to be actively modulated (Figure 13a). The logical next frontier of surface engineering is a transition from receptor engagement to active transcytosis pathway programming, which deliberately steering internalized nanocarriers away from lysosomal degradation routes and toward abluminal exocytosis through surface-encoded intracellular trafficking signals154–156 (Figure 13b). This transition is grounded in an emerging understanding of the molecular determinants of transcytotic routing: the intracellular fate of receptor-bound nanocarriers is determined not solely by receptor identity but by the geometry of receptor clustering at the point of endocytic pit formation, the density and spatial arrangement of ligands on the nanocarrier surface, and the mechanical properties of the nanocarrier itself, all parameters directly amenable to surface engineering control.157,158 Anisotropic nanocarriers whose ligand display is geometrically configured to induce multivalent receptor clustering in patterns that favor clathrin-independent, Rab11-positive recycling endosome routing over Rab7-positive late endosome/lysosome routing represent a plausible near-term design objective159 (Figure 13b). Concurrently, the incorporation of endosomal escape-promoting surface motifs, pH-sensitive/ endosomal fusogenic peptides or proton sponge polymer brushes that activate specifically within endosomal compartments, would allow surface engineering to extend its functional scope from extracellular receptor engagement to intracellular compartment navigation, addressing the transcytotic misdirection problem that currently limits brain accumulation to below 1% of the injected dose160–162 (Figure 13).

At a larger scale, the extrapolation of surface engineering principles beyond the BBB to the sequential anatomical barriers of the CNS, including the glia limitans, the ECM mesh, and synaptic cleft interfaces,163 anticipates a future in which nanocarrier surfaces are programmed with sequential, spatially activated targeting codes: a first ligand layer that mediates BBB transcytosis, a second that is exposed after BBB crossing to engage ECM-penetrating integrins, and a third that activates upon cell-surface contact to mediate neuronal or glial internalization (Figure 13c). This hierarchical surface reprogramming concept, while technically demanding, is logically continuous with current surface engineering practice and represents its natural developmental trajectory.

From Immune Evasion to Immunological Reprogramming: Biomimetic Modification as Active Neuroimmune Modulation

Biomimetic surface modification has been conceptualized primarily as a defensive strategy: replicating the molecular signals of endogenous cells to avoid immune detection and prolong circulation. The inherent logic of this framework, however, contains an underexploited offensive dimension. Biological membranes do not merely suppress immune recognition; they actively communicate with and reprogram immune cell behavior through surface-displayed cytokines, adhesion molecules, and immunomodulatory receptors. Extending biomimetic modification from passive camouflage to active immunological instruction represents a conceptually natural and clinically significant evolutionary step164–166 (Figure 14a and b). In the context of NDs, where neuroinflammation driven by chronically activated microglia and infiltrating peripheral immune cells is a primary driver of disease progression, nanocarriers whose biomimetic surfaces display carefully curated immunomodulatory signals, such as anti-inflammatory cytokines, tolerogenic checkpoint ligands such as PD-L1 or CD200, or M2-polarizing signal complexes, could simultaneously achieve prolonged circulation, targeted CNS accumulation, and therapeutic modulation of the neuroinflammatory microenvironment, all from a single surface modification layer.167 This prospect transforms biomimetic modification from a pharmacokinetic tool into a direct therapeutic modality, blurring the boundary between drug delivery and immunotherapy in a manner that is uniquely enabled by the membrane-mimetic surface design framework (Figure 14c).

Furthermore, the deliberate engineering of biomimetic surfaces that interact productively with, rather than resist, the protein corona offers a predictive design target grounded directly in the framework’s logic. If the corona composition can be pre-determined by surface chemistry design, and if corona proteins can be selected to present BBB-transcytosis-promoting ligands (eg, ApoE for LRP1-mediated entry) or neuroprotective signals to immune cells, then the corona transitions from an adversary of biomimetic design to its most powerful ally, a dynamically assembled, biologically authentic outer shell that is functionally superior to any synthetic coating achievable by current fabrication methods.168,169 The systematic mapping of surface chemistry–corona composition–biological outcome relationships, enabled by high-throughput proteomics and machine learning-assisted data interpretation, makes this prospect technically tractable within the coming decade (Figure 14).

From Binary Stimulus-Response to Molecular Computation: The Evolution of Controlled Release

The current generation of stimuli-responsive controlled release systems operates on a fundamentally binary logic: a pathological stimulus exceeds a defined threshold, a structural change occurs in the nanocarrier, and payload is released. This architecture, while a substantial advance over passive diffusion-driven systems, is limited in its capacity to discriminate between the spatially heterogeneous, temporally dynamic, and molecularly complex microenvironments of NDs, where individual stimuli (elevated ROS, mildly acidic pH, elevated metal ions) may be present transiently, partially, or in combinations that vary across patients, brain regions, and disease stages (Figure 15a). The logical extension of stimuli-responsive design is toward nanocarriers capable of molecular computation, integrating multiple concurrent input signals according to Boolean or weighted logic rules to produce release decisions of far greater specificity than any single-stimulus system can achieve.170,171 Metal-coordination chemistry is particularly well-positioned to anchor this evolution. The coordination environment of transition metals is exquisitely sensitive to the identity, concentration, and combination of competing ligands, properties that can be exploited to engineer release switches that respond not to a single pathological metal ion in isolation, but to specific metal-ion ratios or combinations diagnostic of particular ND pathologies. The ratio of Cu2⁺ to Zn2⁺, for instance, is altered in a characteristic pattern in AD amyloid plaque microenvironments relative to normal tissue; an AND-gate release system requiring simultaneous Cu2⁺ elevation above threshold A AND Zn2⁺ elevation above threshold B would achieve lesion specificity that neither single-metal trigger could provide independently.172–175 More ambitiously, coordination framework nanocarriers incorporating multiple metal nodes with distinct coordination chemistries could perform sequential signal integration that a first metal node responding to pH shift, a second undergoing ligand exchange upon Cu2⁺ elevation, and a structural rearrangement occurring only when both events have been registered, constituting a molecular and gate implemented entirely in inorganic coordination chemistry without requiring biological components or external energy input (Figure 15b). From Carrier Tracking to Molecular Pharmacodynamic Imaging: The Maturation of Theranostic Integration

The most consequential and technically achievable near-term advance within the theranostic integration framework is the elimination of the structural decoupling between carrier signal and pharmacodynamic outcome, the transition from systems that image where the drug goes to systems that image whether the drug is working. This transition requires the development of pharmacodynamically activatable reporters: imaging agents whose signal is not constitutive but is directly modulated by the molecular consequence of therapeutic action at the target site91,178,179 (Figure 16). The design logic for such reporters follows naturally from the theranostic framework: just as stimuli-responsive release systems are engineered around the chemistry of pathological microenvironmental cues, pharmacodynamically activatable reporters would be engineered around the chemistry of therapeutic outcomes. It could expect that a nanocarrier delivering a BACE1 inhibitor for AD therapy could co-incorporate a FRET-based fluorescent reporter whose emission intensity changes as amyloid precursor protein processing shifts from amyloidogenic to non-amyloidogenic cleavage, providing an optical readout of drug mechanism engagement rather than merely carrier localization.180,181 Also, a nanocarrier delivering dopamine precursors or monoamine oxidase inhibitors for PD could incorporate an electrochemically active reporter whose redox state is coupled to local dopamine concentration, providing a pharmacodynamic readout directly proportional to therapeutic restoration of dopaminergic tone.182–184 The chemical architecture for such reporters (eg, FRET pairs, electrochemical redox couples, aggregation-induced emission probes) is well-established; what remains is the conceptual and engineering integration of reporter chemistry with therapeutic mechanism, a design challenge that is solvable within the framework’s existing toolkit (Figure 16).

At the multimodal scale, the integration of photoacoustic, MRI, and radionuclide contrast functions within a single nanoplatform, each modality contributing complementary spatiotemporal information about different aspects of drug distribution and pharmacodynamic response, anticipates a future of hierarchically informative theranostic imaging in which whole-brain biodistribution is resolved at the MRI scale, regional drug release is detected by photoacoustic signal changes, and cellular-level pharmacodynamic engagement is confirmed by activatable fluorescent reporters, all from a single administered nanoplatform.185,186 This multimodal theranostic prospect is logically continuous with current single-modality theranostic design and requires no conceptual leap beyond the careful co-engineering of compatible contrast agents within an appropriately architected nanocarrier scaffold (Figure 16).

Cross-Framework Convergence I: The Self-Reporting Targeted Release System

The most immediate and realizable convergent prospect arising from the intersection of the four frameworks is the proposed concept of the self-reporting targeted release system: an envisioned nanoplatform in which surface engineering (Paradigm 1) directs site-specific accumulation, biomimetic coating (Paradigm 2) ensures delivery of an intact, functional particle to the target site, metal-coordination-gated release (Paradigm 3) triggers payload deployment specifically in the pathological microenvironment, and an activatable theranostic reporter (Paradigm 4) generates a signal change that confirms, in real time, that release has occurred and that the drug is engaging its molecular target31,187–189 (Figure 17). Cross-Framework Convergence II: Disease-Stage-Adaptive Multifunctional Nanoplatforms

The longitudinal progression of NDs, from prodromal neurochemical changes, through early synaptic dysfunction, to overt neurodegeneration and late-stage gliosis, presents a challenge that no single framework can address alone but that the convergence of all four could, in principle, offer a conceptual pathway toward resolving: the design of nanoplatforms whose multifunctional behavior adapts autonomously to disease stage without requiring reformulation or changes in administration protocol.77,192

This prospect is grounded in the recognition that each framework contributes a parameter that is, in principle, dynamically regulable by the disease microenvironment. Surface engineering ligands that target receptors upregulated specifically in neuroinflammatory conditions (eg, VCAM-1, P-selectin on activated brain endothelium) would provide enhanced accumulation precisely when and where disease activity is greatest, without accumulating non-specifically in quiescent brain regions.193 Biomimetic coatings incorporating disease-stage-sensitive membrane proteins that expressed at higher density on nanocarrier surfaces as disease biomarkers in the patient’s plasma increase, through a diagnostically guided reformulation cycle, would modulate immune interaction profiles in response to systemic disease state.194 Metal-coordination release switches calibrated to the metal dyshomeostasis magnitudes characteristic of different Braak stages would release greater payload fractions as disease advances and metal dysregulation intensifies, providing pharmacologically appropriate dose escalation without any change in the administered nanocarrier dose.174 Theranostic reporters tracking pharmacodynamic endpoints: amyloid burden, synaptic density markers, dopaminergic tone, would provide the longitudinal monitoring data necessary to inform each of these adaptive responses (Figure 18). While the full realization of this vision lies beyond current fabrication capability, each of its component adaptive functions is supported by existing chemistry, and their incremental integration represents a coherent conceptual research trajectory grounded entirely within the four frameworks’ own developmental logic (Figure 18).

Cross-Framework Convergence III: The Theranostic Feedback Loop as a Design Principle

Beyond its role as a monitoring tool, theranostic integration, when combined with the other three frameworks, suggests a design principle of broad applicability: the therapeutic action enabled by Paradigms 1–3 produces a microenvironmental change, reduced metal ion concentration, normalized pH, decreased ROS, that is itself the signal driving the theranostic readout of Paradigm 4. This establishes the conceptual basis for a natural negative feedback architecture in which successful therapy diminishes the stimulus driving further drug release, and theranostic monitoring confirms the transition from pathological to normalized microenvironment195,196 (Figure 19). Exploiting this feedback architecture as a deliberate design principle, engineering nanocarriers in which the theranostic signal is not merely a passive reporter but is structurally coupled to release gate closure, could enable self-limiting therapeutic systems that autonomously reduce payload delivery as pharmacodynamic targets are approached, minimizing overdose risk without requiring external monitoring or clinician intervention. A coordination-framework nanocarrier that releases chelating drug in response to elevated Cu2⁺, while simultaneously monitoring local Cu2⁺ concentration via a ratiometric photoacoustic reporter, could in principle incorporate a Cu2⁺-depletion-sensitive gate closure mechanism, ceasing release as local copper returns to physiological levels and reopening as copper re-accumulates if therapeutic effect wanes. This self-regulating, theranostically coupled release system represents not a speculative technological breakthrough but a logical extrapolation of chemical principles already demonstrated individually across the four frameworks, whose integration constitutes the field’s most compelling near-term convergent design objective197,198 (Figure 19).

In conclusion, the compartmentalized advancement of the four cores: surface-engineered targeting, biomimetic cloaking, stimuli-responsive modulation, and theranostic integration, while analytically convenient, obscures the extent to which their long-term clinical utility is interdependent. Current evidence supports the individual and, in selected systems, combinatorial advantages of these strategies in addressing specific barriers associated with NDs, including BBB dysfunction, immune dysregulation, heterogeneous proteopathic lesions, and the pharmacokinetic constraints of chronic treatment. However, whether their systematic integration will translate into superior long-term clinical outcomes remains to be established and should be regarded as a forward-looking design hypothesis rather than an established therapeutic principle.

Accordingly, future development should move beyond the simple sequential addition of functional modules toward rational architectural co-design guided by disease-stage-specific biological requirements. From a translational perspective, priority should be given to demonstrating long-term safety under repeated dosing, reproducible and scalable manufacturing, pharmacokinetic and biodistribution consistency, validation in clinically relevant disease models, and a favorable benefit–complexity balance that can support regulatory evaluation. These considerations will ultimately determine whether increasingly MHNs can progress from mechanistically attractive experimental platforms to clinically viable interventions for NDs.
