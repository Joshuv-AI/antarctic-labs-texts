Low early post-operative pain is a strong predictor of favorable outcomes after rotator cuff repair
Authors: Maxime Antoni, Houcine Bagga, Oleg Moisa, Geoffroy Nourissat
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Hypothesis and/or BackgroundPost-operative pain following arthroscopic rotator cuff repair (ARCR) is highly variable. While some patients require strong analgesia, including opioids, others report minimal discomfort. Identifying predictors of low pain early after surgery may help tailor analgesic strategies and anticipate functional outcomes. This study aimed to identify independent predictors of low pain at one month after ARCR and to evaluate whether early low pain predicts a more favorable clinical course during the first post-operative year.MethodsA retrospective single-center, single-surgeon study included 178 patients who underwent primary ARCR between 2022 and 2023. Patients with partial or revision repairs, patch augmentation, tendon transfers, or isolated subscapularis tears were excluded. Low pain was defined as a visual analog scale (VAS) ≤3 at one month without analgesic use. Multivariate logistic regression identified predictors of low pain. Clinical outcomes (Constant score, American Shoulder and Elbow Surgeons, Subjective Shoulder Value, range of motion, strength, and VAS), return to work/sport, complications, and revisions were compared between patients with and without low pain. Propensity score matching (1:1) was performed to reduce confounding.ResultsThree independent predictors of low pain at one month were identified: absence of subscapularis tendon involvement (P = .03), absence of pre-operative analgesic use (P = .001), and lower baseline VAS pain score (P = .049). Patients with low pain at one month (n = 58; 32.6%) had significantly higher Constant, American Shoulder and Elbow Surgeons, and Subjective Shoulder Value scores, as well as lower VAS pain at all post-operative time points up to 1 year (P < .05 for all). At 1 year, between-group differences exceeded the Minimal Clinically Important Difference for all patient-reported outcome measures. No differences were observed in return-to-work, return-to-sport, complication, or revision rates.ConclusionLow pain at 1 month after ARCR was independently associated with absence of subscapularis involvement, absence of pre-operative analgesic use, and lower baseline pain. Patients experiencing early low pain follow a consistently more favorable clinical trajectory, achieving superior functional outcomes throughout the first post-operative year.

Post-operative pain following arthroscopic rotator cuff repair (ARCR) can be substantial, particularly during the early recovery period.24 Effective pain control is essential not only for patient comfort but also to facilitate rehabilitation, improve compliance with physical therapy, and potentially influence long-term functional outcomes.7,11 A wide range of analgesic strategies are currently employed, typically combining general and/or regional anesthesia with post-operative administration of oral or intravenous medications.21,24 Despite these options, pain management remains highly variable among patients: while some require strong analgesia, including opioids, others report only mild discomfort.19 This heterogeneity highlights the importance of tailoring analgesic regimens to each patient's individual needs in order to optimize care and avoid overtreatment.

The use of opioids in the post-operative setting has come under increasing scrutiny due to the well-documented risks of dependence, misuse, and opioid-related mortality. In several countries, opioid overprescription has become a major public health issue, leading to a paradigm shift toward more judicious and selective use. In this context, the ability to anticipate which patients are likely to require opioids and which may safely avoid them has become a clinical priority.14 Several pre-operative factors have been associated with higher post-operative pain levels, including prior opioid use, chronic pain conditions, anxiety or depression, and disadvantaged socioeconomic status.17,25 However, less is known about the significance of post-operative pain patterns themselves. In particular, it remains unclear whether low pain levels during the early recovery phase reflect a consistently favorable trajectory, both in terms of pain and overall clinical outcomes.12

The primary objective of this study was to identify patient- or surgery-related factors associated with low pain levels at 1 month post-operatively following ARCR. The secondary objective was to determine whether low pain at 1 month could serve as an early predictor of a more favorable clinical course over the first post-operative year.

We hypothesized that patients experiencing low pain at 1 month would demonstrate superior outcomes over time, including higher functional scores and consistently lower pain levels at subsequent follow-up visits.

Methods

Study design and population

This retrospective, single-center, single-surgeon study included a consecutive series of patients who underwent primary arthroscopic repair of full-thickness rotator cuff tears over a 2-year period (2022–2023). Patients treated with partial repairs, revision rotator cuff repair, concomitant superior capsular reconstruction, tendon transfer, or patch augmentation were excluded, as were patients with isolated subscapularis tears or with incomplete medical records. The study was conducted in accordance with institutional ethical standards and the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to inclusion.

Radiographic evaluation

All patients underwent cross-sectional imaging (magnetic resonance imaging [MRI] or computed tomography [CT] arthrography in cases of contraindication to MRI) to assess tendon lesions pre-operatively. These lesions were classified into several types: isolated supraspinatus tear in cases of distal and anterior rupture of the supraspinatus tendon; posterosuperior rotator cuff tear in cases of posterior extension; anterosuperior rotator cuff tear in cases of anterior extension; and isolated subscapularis tear. Tendon retraction in the coronal plane was classified according to Patte,18 and fatty muscle infiltration was assessed using the Goutallier classification.9

Surgical procedure

All procedures were performed by an experienced shoulder surgeon. Patients were positioned in the beach chair position under general anesthesia combined with an interscalene nerve block. The operative arm was supported using a mobile arm holder. All surgeries were performed arthroscopically. A complete rotator cuff repair was systematically performed, using either a single-row or double-row technique depending on the size and retraction of the tear. Acromioplasty was conducted when indicated by acromial morphology on radiographs and intraoperative findings, while tenotomy or tenodesis of the long head of the biceps was performed in nearly all cases.

Post-operatively, the operated arm was immobilized in a sling with the elbow at the side for a duration of 4 weeks. Passive range-of-motion exercises were initiated immediately after surgery, active mobilization at 6 weeks, and strengthening exercises were started at 3 months. Return to work and sports was permitted as soon as the patient felt able, provided that post-operative rehabilitation and immobilization protocols were followed.

Clinical evaluation

Patient demographics and relevant medical history were recorded (Table I). Clinical evaluations were performed pre-operatively and at 1, 3, 6, and 12 months post-operatively. Range of motion and pain, assessed using a visual analog scale (VAS), were recorded at each post-operative follow-up visit. Functional outcomes were assessed using the Constant score (CS),3 the American Shoulder and Elbow Surgeons (ASES) score,20 and the Subjective Shoulder Value (SSV)8 at 3, 6, and 12 months post-operatively. Shoulder strength was measured with a handheld dynamometer at 90° of abduction in the scapular plane. The primary outcome was defined as low post-operative pain at 1 month, corresponding to a VAS score ≤3 without analgesic consumption.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation, while categorical variables were expressed as counts and percentages.

Baseline characteristics between the initial and final cohorts were compared using univariate analyses. The normality of continuous variables was assessed with the Shapiro-Wilk test and homogeneity of variances with Levene's test. Depending on data distribution, continuous variables were compared using the unpaired Student's t-test, Welch's t-test, or Mann-Whitney U test. Categorical variables were compared using the chi-squared test or Fisher's exact test, as appropriate. All statistical tests were 2-sided, with a significance threshold set at P < .05.

A multivariate logistic regression analysis was conducted to identify independent predictors of low pain at 1 month. Multicollinearity, heteroskedasticity, and normality of residuals were evaluated using the Belsley-Kuh-Welsch technique, the Breusch-Pagan test, and the Shapiro-Wilk test, respectively. The results of the multivariable model are reported as odds ratios with 95% confidence intervals and associated P values.

Patients were divided into 2 groups based on their pain level and analgesic use at 1 month: (1) those reporting a VAS score ≤3 without analgesic use, and (2) those with a VAS >3 and/or ongoing analgesic consumption. Post-operative outcomes—including patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs), revision rates, complications, return to work, and return to sport—were compared between groups. To minimize potential confounding, a 1:1 propensity score matching without replacement was performed. Propensity scores were estimated using a logistic regression model incorporating the following normalized covariates: tear pattern, pre-operative analgesic use (yes/no), pre-operative VAS pain score, pre-operative CS, pre-operative ASES score, and pre-operative SSV. Tear patterns were categorized as isolated supraspinatus tears, defined as distal and anterior tears without posterior extension beyond 1 cm; posterosuperior tears, defined as tears extending posteriorly and involving the infraspinatus; and anterosuperior tears, defined as tears involving the superior cuff and the subscapularis. All analyses were conducted using EasyMedStat (version 3.42; Paris, France; www.easymedstat.com).

Results

Of the 207 patients who underwent primary rotator cuff repair during the inclusion period, 22 were lost to follow-up, and 7 had incomplete records, resulting in a final cohort of 178 patients (57.8% male, mean age 58.9 ± 9.6 years) for statistical analysis. The final included cohort (n = 178) was broadly comparable to excluded patients (n = 29) regarding baseline demographic, clinical, imaging, and surgical characteristics. The only significant difference was observed for tobacco status, with a higher proportion of smokers among excluded patients. At 1 year, clinical outcomes remained significantly superior in the low-pain group, with differences exceeding the Minimal Clinically Important Difference (MCID) for the Constant (10.4 points)16 and ASES (11.1 points)5 scores, while the difference in SSV approached but did not exceed the MCID (13.0 points).15 Shoulder range of motion (forward flexion, abduction, external rotation) and strength were also significantly greater in this group.

However, no significant differences were observed between groups regarding return-to-sport or return-to-work rates during the first post-operative year. Similarly, complication and revision surgery rates were comparable between groups.

Discussion

The principal finding of this study is that low pain levels at 1 month following ARCR—defined as a VAS score ≤3 without analgesic consumption—were associated with a consistently more favorable clinical trajectory throughout the first post-operative year. Patients in the low-pain group demonstrated significantly superior functional outcomes, including Constant, ASES, and SSV scores, with differences exceeding the MCID at each follow-up visit. These findings suggest that early post-operative pain may serve as a clinically relevant prognostic marker, with potential implications for post-operative monitoring and individualized pain management strategies.

Post-operative pain following rotator cuff repair is known to be highly variable, particularly during the early recovery phase.24 Effective pain control during the first post-operative weeks is critical, as excessive pain may delay rehabilitation, impair adherence to physical therapy, and contribute to the development of secondary shoulder stiffness.7,11 Several studies have shown that early post-operative pain is associated with an increased risk of capsular adhesions and prolonged functional limitation, supporting the rationale for early identification and proactive management of patients at risk.7,11 In addition, psychosocial factors such as pain catastrophizing, kinesiophobia, and unrealistic pre-operative expectations are known to influence post-operative pain perception and recovery, further emphasizing the multifactorial nature of post-operative pain.13,22

In the present study, involvement of the subscapularis tendon emerged as an independent predictor of higher pain levels at 1 month. Tears involving the subscapularis are generally associated with more complex tear patterns and longer operative times. Increased surgical duration may reflect both tear severity and prolonged exposure to factors such as electrocautery use and arthroscopic fluid pressure, which may contribute to post-operative soft tissue edema and pain. Furthermore, subscapularis repair itself may increase early post-operative discomfort due to anterior shoulder tension and the need for stricter post-operative protection. Our findings are consistent with previous reports suggesting that subscapularis involvement negatively impacts early post-operative pain and recovery.2

The absence of pre-operative analgesic use was also identified as an independent predictor of low post-operative pain. This observation aligns with existing literature demonstrating that patients using analgesics—particularly opioids—prior to surgery often present with worse pre-operative functional scores and experience higher rates of post-operative complications and revisions.1,6,10 Pre-operative opioid use has been associated with opioid tolerance, altered pain perception, and prolonged post-operative analgesic requirements.1 Similarly, lower baseline pre-operative pain levels were independently associated with lower post-operative pain, a relationship that has been consistently reported in the literature.4 These findings suggest that optimization of pre-operative pain control may positively influence post-operative recovery.

From a clinical standpoint, these results support the value of pre-operative risk stratification based on readily available factors such as baseline pain intensity, pre-operative analgesic use, subscapularis involvement, and psychosocial context. Identifying patients at higher risk for post-operative pain may allow surgeons to provide more accurate pre-operative counseling and implement targeted, individualized analgesic strategies. Such an approach could help reduce unnecessary opioid prescriptions, thereby minimizing the risk of dependence, while ensuring adequate pain control in patients who are likely to require more intensive analgesic support.

Early post-operative pain assessment may also serve as a useful monitoring tool. Patients reporting low pain levels at 1 month may be reassured regarding their recovery trajectory, whereas persistent or excessive pain at this time point could be considered an early warning sign, prompting closer surveillance, adjustment of rehabilitation protocols, or further investigation to prevent stiffness or suboptimal outcomes.

Interestingly, despite sustained functional differences between groups up to 12 months post-operatively, no significant differences were observed in return-to-work or return-to-sport rates. These endpoints are influenced by numerous external factors, including occupational demands, socioeconomic context, and patient motivation, which may explain the absence of statistically significant differences.23 Nonetheless, we observed a nearly 2-fold lower return-to-work rate and more than twice the complication rate in the high-pain group at 1 year, although these differences did not reach statistical significance, likely due to limited sample size.

This study has several limitations. Its retrospective, single-center, single-surgeon design limits the generalizability of the findings, although it ensures homogeneity in surgical technique and post-operative management. Importantly, data were collected prospectively, minimizing recall bias and strengthening data reliability. While multivariate analysis was used to adjust for confounding variables, residual confounders, particularly psychological and psychosocial factors, could not be fully accounted for. In addition, post-operative advanced imaging was not routinely performed to assess tendon healing after repair, which prevented evaluation of whether the quality or integrity of repair healing influenced post-operative outcomes. Finally, psychosocial factors, particularly anxiety and depressive symptoms, which are known to influence post-operative recovery, were not included in the statistical analyses.

Future prospective studies are warranted to validate these findings and to assess whether personalized analgesic pathways based on pre-operative risk stratification can effectively reduce post-operative pain, limit opioid use, and improve functional outcomes following rotator cuff repair.

Conclusion

Absence of subscapularis tendon tear, absence of pre-operative analgesic use, and lower baseline VAS scores were identified as independent predictors of low pain levels at 1 month post-operatively following ARCR. Patients with low pain levels at 1 month exhibited significantly better clinical outcomes and consistently lower pain scores throughout the entire first post-operative year. At 1 year, all evaluated PROMs remained significantly superior in this group, with differences exceeding the MCID. These findings may help optimize pre-operative patient selection, tailor perioperative pain management strategies, and improve patient counseling regarding expected post-operative outcomes.
