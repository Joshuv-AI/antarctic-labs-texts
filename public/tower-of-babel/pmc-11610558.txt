Peptostreptococcus Anaerobius enhances dextran sulfate sodium-induced colitis by promoting nf-κB-NLRP3-Dependent macrophage pyroptosis
Authors: Xu-Hang Shen, Jing Guan, De-Peng Lu, Shao-Cheng Hong, Li Yu, Xi Chen
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTEvidence indicates that gut microbiota is crucial in ulcerative colitis (UC) development. Increased Peptostreptococcus species abundance is linked to UC, but its role and mechanisms in intestinal inflammation are not well understood. This study used a dextran sulfate sodium (DSS)-induced colitis model in mice, and different bacterial strains were administered via gavage. We assessed clinical manifestations, colonic barrier function, gut microbiota composition, and levels of inflammatory cytokines, NOD-like receptor family pyrin domain-containing 3 (NLRP3) signaling molecules, and pyroptosis-related proteins. Mouse bone marrow-derived macrophages (BMDMs) were infected with Peptostreptococcus anaerobius at different time points and multiplicities of infection (MOI). Cell viability and the expression of NLRP3 signaling molecules and pyroptosis-associated proteins were assessed. The inhibitors C29, TAK-242, and MCC950 were employed for Toll-like receptor (TLR) and NLRP3 signaling pathways. It was observed that P. anaerobius exacerbated intestinal inflammation and barrier injury in DSS-induced colitis in mice. Additionally, P. anaerobius contributed to gut microbiota dysbiosis during colitis progression. P. anaerobius induced the expression of NLRP3 signaling molecules and pyroptosis-associated proteins in mouse colitis tissues. In vitro assays demonstrated that P. anaerobius activated NLRP3 inflammasome and evoked gasdermin D-mediated pyroptosis and interleukin (IL)-1β secretion in macrophages. Furthermore, TLR2 and TLR4 were identified as key mediators of P. anaerobius-induced macrophage pyroptosis via activation of the Nuclear Factor-kappa B (NF-κB)-NLRP3 pathway. In conclusion, P. anaerobius promotes macrophage pyroptosis and IL-1β secretion through the TLR2/4-NF-κB-NLRP3 signaling axis, thereby aggravating colitis. P. anaerobius may represent a potential risk factor for UC development.KEYWORDS: Peptostreptococcus anaerobius, ulcerative colitis, macrophage, pyroptosis, NF-κB

Introduction

Inflammatory bowel diseases (IBDs), including Crohn’s disease (CD) and ulcerative colitis (UC) are associated with numerous complex factors [1]. Commensal bacteria play a crucial role in maintaining a symbiotic relationship by shaping and modulating host physiological processes. Disruptions in the gut microbiota have been linked to chronic inflammation, such as IBD [2]. Gut microbiota dysbiosis leads to the enrichment of harmful bacteria and compromises the integrity of the intestinal barrier, which is strongly influenced by the gut microbiota. The key role of the gut flora in the pathogenesis of IBDs has long been postulated. UC is believed to result from an excessive inflammatory response mediated by the immune system, leading to microbial dysbiosis and intestinal barrier disruption [3].

Peptostreptococcus anaerobius, a Gram-positive anaerobic bacterium, is an oral and commensal gut microbe. Species within the genus Peptostreptococcus are implicated in various inflammatory diseases, including septic arthritis [4], endocarditis [5], and periodontitis [6]. Additionally, P. anaerobius has been identified as significantly enriched in the stool and mucosal microbes of colorectal cancer patients [7]. It induces reactive oxygen species (ROS) production, enhances cholesterol biosynthesis, and activates pro-oncogenic factors and pathways, promoting tumor cell proliferation via interaction with Toll-like receptor (TLR) 2/4 on host cells [8,9]. Increased abundance of Peptostreptococcus species have been observed in patients with UC or CD[10–12]. However, it remains unclear whether P. anaerobius enrichment promotes UC pathology. Moreover, P. anaerobius stimulates TLR2 and TLR4 activation, which in turn enhances the production of tumor necrosis factor (TNF) and interleukin (IL)-6 in macrophages [13]. Macrophages are key effectors of the innate immunity system during infection. The NOD-like receptor family pyrin domain-containing 3 (NLRP3) inflammasome plays a significant role in the host defense against microbial invasion in macrophages [14,15]. NLRP3 inflammasome activation is often associated with inflammation and cell death, particularly pyroptosis, a type of proinflammatory programmed cell death mediated by cleaved gasdermin D (GSDMD) [16,17]. Therefore, it is crucial to investigate the molecular mechanisms underlying P. anaerobius-induced macrophage pyroptosis.

The objective of this study was to explore the effect of P. anaerobius on intestinal inflammation, barrier injury, and the regulation of macrophage pyroptosis in a murine model of dextran sulfate sodium (DSS)-induced colitis. Additionally, we examined the alterations in the intestinal microbiota caused by P. anaerobius and DSS to elucidate the underlying mechanisms. Our study revealed that P. anaerobius contributes to DSS-induced colitis by promoting macrophage pyroptosis and exacerbating gut dysbiosis.

Materials and methods

Bacterial strains and culture

P. anaerobius (27337) and Escherichia coli MG1655 (700926) were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection. P. anaerobius was cultured in Wilkins-Chalgren anaerobe broth (Thermo Fisher Scientific) under anaerobic conditions (10% H₂, 10% CO₂, 80% N₂) at 37°C using an anaerobic workstation (Yuejin, China). E. coli MG1655 was cultured in lysogeny broth under aerobic conditions at 37°C.

Animal model

All animal studies were approved by the Animal Experimentation Ethics Committee of Anhui Medical University (No. LLSC20231251) and adhered to the ARRIVE guidelines. Specific pathogen-free C57BL/6 mice (female, 6 weeks old) were purchased from Shanghai GemPharmatech Co., Ltd. and maintained in a specific pathogen-free environment. After 7 days of adaptive feeding, mice were administered an antibiotic cocktail in drinking water (0.2 mg/mL ampicillin, neomycin, metronidazole, and 0.1 mg/mL vancomycin) for 2 weeks and then a total of 32 mice randomly assigned to four groups (n = eight per group). The P. anaerobius and E. coli groups were gavage-fed P. anaerobius or E. coli MG1655 (1 × 109 colony-forming units) in 200 μL phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) daily for 2 weeks. The PBS group was gavaged with 200 μL PBS daily for 2 weeks, with colony-forming units measured using the optical density value. After 1 week of gavage feeding, mice received 2.5% DSS (MP Biomedicals) in drinking water for 7 days to induce colitis, while the negative control group received regular water. Mice were weighed daily. If the animal developed dyspnea, weight loss of more than 20%, or showed signs of mortality, they were euthanized immediately. After the experiment, mice were euthanized, the whole colon was excised and measured, and the distal colon was collected for further analysis.

Measurement of disease activity index (DAI)

Disease severity was assessed daily using a scoring system based on weight loss, stool consistency, and fecal bleeding. DAI scores were calculated as follows: body weight loss (0: none; 1: 1–5%; 2: 5–10%; 3: 10–20%; 4: >20%), fecal blood (0: negative; 2: positive hemoccult; 4: gross bleeding), and stool consistency (0: well-formed pellets; 2: loose stools; 4: diarrhea). The total DAI score was the sum of these individual scores.

Histology scoring

Distal colon tissues were fixed overnight in 10% formalin at room temperature, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned. The sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin for pathological analysis. Histological scores were calculated based on inflammation severity (0: none; 1: mild; 2: moderate; 3: severe), level of inflammatory involvement (0: none; 1: mucosa; 2: mucosa and submucosa; 3: transmural), and extent of epithelial/crypt damage (0: none; 1: basal 1/3; 2: basal 2/3; 3: crypt loss; 4: crypt and surface epithelial destruction).

Alcian blue/periodic acid-Schiff stain

Colon tissues were processed and subjected to pathological analyses. Sections were stained with Alcian blue/periodic acid-Schiff stain for histological visualization, and blue-stained goblet cells were counted under a microscope.

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM)

Colon tissues were fixed with 2.5% glutaraldehyde and further fixed with 1% osmium tetroxide. Dehydration was performed with increasing ethanol and propylene oxide concentrations. Samples were embedded, sectioned into 50-nm slices, and stained with 3% uranyl acetate and lead citrate. Images were acquired using Talos L120C G2 TEM (FEI).

Fluorescein isothiocyanate (fitc) – dextran 4 intestinal permeability assay

Mice were fasted for 3 h before the colonic permeability assay and then given 10 mg of FITC-dextran 4 (Sigma). Whole blood was collected 4 h later and centrifuged at 12 000 rpm for 10 min to obtain serum. The serum was diluted 4 folds, and 100 μL of each sample diluent was added to 96-well plates. FITC-dextran levels were measured using fluorometry with 485 nm excitation/528 nm emission.

Cell culture

Bone marrow was extracted from the femur and tibia of mice. The cells were isolated and resuspended in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% Fetal Bovine Serum, 100 U/mL penicillin/streptomycin, and 20 ng/mL recombinant mouse M-CSF. Cells were seeded at 1.5 × 106 per well in a 6-well plate with 2 mL of medium and cultured for 6 days. The medium was refreshed every three days, and floating, non-adhesive cells were removed. For pharmacological treatment, inhibitors were added 1 h before P. anaerobius infection.

Infection of bone marrow-derived macrophages (BMDMs) with P. anaerobius

Fresh, overnight P. anaerobius cultures were resuspended in PBS. BMDMs were seeded at 0.5 × 106 per well in a 12-well plate for 24 h and incubated with P. anaerobius at the indicated MOI. After 6 h of incubation in DMEM, the medium was discarded, and the cells were washed three times with 1× PBS to exclude extracellular bacteria. The cells were then incubated again with fresh DMEM supplemented with 10 ng/μL gentamicin to kill residual bacteria. At various time points post-infection, supernatants were collected for lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), and cytokine assays and cell lysates were prepared for Western blotting.

Cell counting kit-8 (CCK-8) cell viability assay

Cell viability was assessed using a CCK-8 kit (Beyotime, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Live cells were quantified by measuring the production of orange formazan dye, which is proportional to cell number, with absorbance read at 460 nm. Cell viability was expressed as the percentage of live cells relative to the total number of cells in the control.

LDH cytotoxicity assay

LDH release was assayed using an LDH Cytotoxicity Assay Kit (Beyotime, China) following the manufacturer’s protocol. Supernatants were harvested to quantify the LDH release. LDH release into the medium was determined by measuring the absorbance at 490 nm. Relative LDH release was expressed as the percentage of LDH activity in the supernatants of cultured cells (medium) compared to the total LDH (from media and cells) and used as an index of cytotoxicity.

RNA extraction and quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qRT-pcr)

RNA was extracted from macrophages or freshly frozen colon tissues using TRIzol reagent (TaKaRa) as per the manufacturer’s instructions. Reverse transcription was performed with 500 ng of RNA using PrimeScript RT Master Mix (TaKaRa). The synthesized complementary DNA was analyzed by qRT-PCR using TB Green Premix Ex Taq II (TaKaRa) and specific primers on a LightCycler 480 RT-PCR system (Roche). Each reaction was performed in triplicate in 10 μL volumes. Gene expression was normalized to the housekeeping gene GAPDH, and relative RNA levels were calculated using the comparative cycle threshold method (2−ΔΔCT). Primer sequences are listed in Supplementary Table 1.

Western blotting analysis

Cell samples were lysed with 1× RIPA lysis buffer (Beyotime, China) containing phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride and a phosphatase inhibitor (1:1000). Colon tissue samples were homogenized in 1× RIPA lysis buffer with phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride and phosphatase inhibitor (1:1000) using a tissue breaker. The mixtures were centrifuged at 4°C for 15 min at 12,000 rpm, and the supernatant was collected. Protein concentration was measured using a bicinchoninic acid protein assay kit (Beyotime, China). Proteins were separated by electrophoresis, transferred to polyvinylidene fluoride membranes, blocked with 5% skim milk for 1 h, and incubated with primary and secondary antibodies. Bands were visualized using enhanced chemiluminescence detection reagents (Thermo Fisher) and quantified with ImageJ software. Antibodies used are listed in Supplementary Table 2.

Immunofluorescence staining

Paraffin colon tissue slides underwent heat-induced antigen retrieval and were treated with sodium borohydride to reduce autofluorescence. Following blocking with 5% bovine serum albumin in PBS, the slides were incubated with primary antibodies in a humidified chamber at 4°C overnight. After washing, the slides were incubated with fluorophore-labeled secondary antibodies at room temperature for 1 h. Fluorescence was then detected using a fluorescence microscope. Antibodies used are listed in Supplementary Table 2.

Immunohistochemistry analysis

Paraffin-embedded colon tissue slides underwent heat-induced antigen retrieval and peroxidase blocking to eliminate endogenous hydrogen peroxide. After blocking with 10% sheep serum in PBS, the slides were incubated with primary antibodies in a humidified chamber at 4°C overnight. They were then incubated with horseradish peroxidase-labeled secondary antibodies at room temperature for 30 min and stained with 3,3’-diaminobenzidine substrate solution. Antibodies used are listed in Supplementary Table 2.

16s ribosomal RNA (rRNA) gene sequencing and data analysis

Fecal samples were collected with sterile cotton swabs and placed in 2 mL sterile tubes. They were sent to the Novogene Sequencing Center in Tianjin, China, for DNA extraction and sequencing. Genomic DNA was extracted using the QIAamp Fast DNA Stool Mini Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany). The V3–V4 region of the 16S rRNA gene was amplified with universal primers (341F, CCTAYGGGRBGCASCAG; 806 R, GGACTACNNGGGTATCTAAT) and sequenced using the NovaSeq system (Illumina) with a 2 × 250-base pair protocol. Sequencing data were processed by filtering adapters and barcodes, combining paired reads, and removing low-quality sequences and chimeras. Clean data were analyzed using the QIIME2 pipeline, with sequences aligned and annotated using the SILVA database (v138). A phylogenetic tree was constructed, and beta diversity metrics were calculated with the core-metrics-phylogenetic method at a sampling depth of 10,000.

Microarray data acquisition and analysis

The genomic spatial event datasets GSE75214 and GSE38713 were obtained from the Gene Expression Omnibus database and are based on the GPL6244 and GPL570 platforms for probe annotation, respectively [18,19]. In the GSE38713 dataset, 36 mucosal samples were collected from patients with active UC, patients with UC in remission, and healthy controls. In the GSE75214 dataset, 74 samples were classified as active UC, 23 samples as UC patients in remission, and 11 as controls using the same categorization method. Subsequently, transcriptional expression data of the genes of interest from the datasets were extracted and visualized using boxplots. Visualization was performed using the ggplot2 and ggpubr packages in R version 4.2.2 for the three aforementioned groups.

Statistical analysis

GraphPad Prism 9.0.0 was used for statistical analysis in this study. Normally distributed data were compared using the unpaired Student’s t-test, one-way analysis of variance, or two-way analysis of variance. Non-normally distributed data were compared using the Kruskal–Wallis test. Data are expressed as the mean ± standard error of the mean, as indicated in the figure legends. Statistical tests were two-tailed, and p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

P. anaerobius aggravated the phenotype in DSS-induced colitis

A mouse model of acute colitis was established using 2.5% DSS in drinking water. As illustrated in (Figure 1(a)), mice were initially administered an antibiotic cocktail in their drinking water for 2 weeks to deplete the intestinal microbiota. Following this, mice were gavaged with PBS, E. coli, or P. anaerobius for 14 days, and 2.5% DSS was administered for 7 days to induce colitis after a one-week gavage period. Mice receiving plain drinking water served as the negative control. To assess the severity of colitis, we measured body weight, DAI, colon length, spleen weight, histological score, and goblet cell count.

Our results showed that mice gavage-fed P. anaerobius (DSS + P. anaerobius) exhibited greater weight loss and higher DAI (Figure 1(b, c)) during DSS treatment compared to other groups. Colon shortening and splenomegaly were significantly less pronounced in the DSS + P. anaerobius group than in the DSS + PBS and DSS + E. coli groups (Figure 1(d-f)). Histological analysis of the colons stained with hematoxylin and eosin-stained revealed a higher histology score in the DSS + P. anaerobius group (Figure 1(g, h)). Additionally, the number of goblet cells was significantly reduced in the DSS + P. anaerobius group compared to the DSS + PBS and DSS + E. coli groups (Figure 1 (i,j)). These findings suggest that P. anaerobius exacerbates DSS-induced colitis.

P. anaerobius promoted the inflammatory response in the colon

The inflammatory response mediated by the intestinal epithelial and immune cells is crucial in the development of colitis [20]. IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α, IL-18 are key proinflammatory cytokines released from activated monocytes. Additionally, IL-17 secreted by CD4+ T helper 17 (Th17) and CD8+ cytotoxic T (Tc17) cells contributes to tissue inflammation through the release of proinflammatory cytokines and the stimulation of neutrophil chemotaxis. Macrophages and T regulatory cells are significant sources of IL-10, which plays a crucial modulatory role in inflammation.

To evaluate the impact of P. anaerobius on the inflammatory response in colon tissue, we measured the expression levels of representative inflammatory cytokines (IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α, IL-18, IL-10, and IL-17a) at the transcriptional level. Mice in the DSS + P. anaerobius group exhibited increased expression of IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α, IL-18, and IL-17a while showing a decreased expression of IL-10 compared to the DSS + PBS and DSS + E. coli group (Figure 2).

P. anaerobius exacerbated intestinal barrier injury in DSS-induced colitis

Given the critical role of barrier dysfunction in colitis development, we assessed the barrier function of the colon in our animal model. TEM was employed to visualize the structure of the intestinal epithelial cells. Additionally, immunofluorescence staining was used to examine the expression of tight junction proteins, specifically Claudin-1, Occludin, and zonula occludens-1, to further investigate epithelial barrier function. The TEM and immunofluorescence staining images revealed that P. anaerobius exacerbated epithelial injury in the colon (Figure 3(a, b)).

To measure intestinal permeability, we administered FITC-dextran to the mice and assessed permeability levels. The DSS + P. anaerobius group exhibited higher intestinal permeability compared to the DSS + PBS and DSS + E. coli groups (Figure 3(c)). Tight junctions, crucial for maintaining epithelial barrier integrity, were examined by measuring the expression levels of Claudin-1, Occludin, and zonula occludens-1 at both the messenger RNA and protein levels. Our results indicated that mice in the DSS + P. anaerobius group had lower expression levels of these tight junction proteins compared to those in the DSS + PBS and DSS + E. coli groups (Figure 3(d-j)).

P. anaerobius involved in gut microbiota dysbiosis in DSS-induced colitis

To assess the impact of P. anaerobius on gut microbiota alterations, we analyzed the bacterial composition in fecal samples from mice subjected to various treatments using 16S rRNA gene sequencing. Microbiota composition was compared between mice gavaged with P. anaerobius, those gavaged with E. coli and PBS, and normal controls, all evaluated at the same time point. The Principal Coordinates Analysis (PCoA) plot, based on the unweighted UniFrac distance matrix, revealed distinct clustering patterns in the intestinal bacterial communities of mice at different time points. Mice gavaged with P. anaerobius exhibited significant differences from the other three control groups. In contrast, no notable differences were observed among the mice gavaged with E. coli, PBS, and the normal controls (Figure 4(a, b)). PCoA, based on both the Bray-Curtis and Jaccard matrices of bacterial taxonomy, showed similar results (Supplementary Figure S1A-D). The relative abundance of bacterial taxa exhibited substantial changes among mice subjected to different treatments. At the phylum level, mice treated with P. anaerobius exhibited an accumulation of Patescibacteria, while the relative abundances of Firmicutes and Verrucomicrobiota were significantly reduced. (Supplementary Figure E, F). The relative abundance of Verrucomicrobiota was significantly reduced, and increased proportions of Actinobacteriota and Patescibacteria were observed in P. anaerobius-treated mice compared with those in PBS-treated mice. At the genus level, the relative abundances of Escherichia-Shigella, Enterobacter, Blautia, and Enterococcus were increased, and Muribaculaceae, Akkermansia, Lactobacillus, Dubosiella, and Clostridia_UCG −014 were decreased in P. anaerobius-treated mice at the end of the experiment compared with those in adaptive feeding (Figure 4(c, e)). The relative abundance of Akkermansia was decreased, and Lactobacillus and Lachnospiraceae_NK4A136_group were increased in P. anaerobius-treated mice in comparison with those in PBS-treated mice, Escherichia-Shigella was increased in P. anaerobius-treated mice compared to that in the control group (Figure 4(d, f)). Linear discriminant analysis and effect size analyses were conducted to identify differential bacterial taxa among the groups with different treatments. Compared to mouse stools after 3 days of adaptive feeding, the enrichments of Escherichia−Shigella, Blautia, Staphylococcus, Flavonifractor, Citrobacter, Phreatobacter, and Pseudonocardia were investigated in feces of P. anaerobius-treated mice at the end of the experiment. In contrast, the abundance of genera that are typically beneficial for gut health was significantly reduced. These include Ruminococcaceae, Muribaculum, Odoribacter, Rikenellaceae_RC9, Parabacteroides, Bifidobacterium, Alistipes, Dubosiella, Lactobacillus, and Muribaculaceae (Figure 4(g)). P. anaerobius-treated mice showed significant enhancements in Clostridia_UCG −014 and Faecalibaculum when compared to the negative control group (Supplementary Figure S4G) and a signature of the enrichment of Flavonifractor compared to the PBS group (Supplementary Figure S1H, I). Additionally, PICRUSt2 was utilized to predict the metabolic pathways of the gut microbiota. These predicted pathways were analyzed both horizontally and vertically across the different treatment groups (Figure 4(h, i)). Pathways associated with fatty acid biosynthesis (PWY-5989, PWY0–862, PWY-7664, PWY-6282, and PWYG-321) were found to be upregulated in the P. anaerobius-treated mice. Conversely, pathways related to nucleoside and nucleotide biosynthesis (PWY-5686, PWY-6126, and PWY-7229), as well as those involved in glycolysis and gluconeogenesis (ANAGLYCOLYSIS-PWY, GLYCOLYSIS, PWY-5484, and GLUCONEO-PWY), were downregulated. Compared to the PBS group, purine nucleotide degradation-related pathways (PWY-6608, SALVADEHYPOX-PWY, P164-PWY, and PWY-6353) and tetrapyrrole biosynthesis-related pathways (PWY-5188 and PWY-5189) were also downregulated in the P. anaerobius-treated mice. Additionally, GDP-sugar biosynthesis-related pathways (PWY-5659 and PWY-7323) were upregulated in both PBS-treated and P. anaerobius-treated mice compared to those in the E. coli group.

P. anaerobius upregulated expression of NLRP3 signaling and pyroptosis-associated proteins in DSS-induced colitis

Increasing evidence has indicated that dysregulated inflammasome activity plays a crucial role in the development of chronic intestinal inflammation [21,22]. Inflammasome activation serves as a critical defense mechanism against bacterial infections through innate immune responses, including the activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome and inflammatory cell death. The assembly of the NLRP3 inflammasome and subsequent activation of Caspase-1 lead to the cleavage of GSDMD and pro-IL-1β into their mature forms. This process results in the pyroptosis-dependent secretion of IL-1β, which is a fundamental event in the pathogenesis of colitis [23]. To verify this hypothesis, we analyzed the expression of NLRP3, Caspase-1, and GSDMD using the Gene Expression Omnibus database in two separate expression datasets derived from endoscopically obtained intestinal mucosal biopsies from patients with UC and healthy controls. Compared to healthy controls, the expression of NLRP3/Caspase-1/GSDMD signaling proteins was elevated in the colons of patients with UC, especially in patients with active UC (Figure 5(a)). To further explore the mechanism underlying the effect of P. anaerobius on DSS-induced colitis, we detected the expression of NLRP3 signaling and pyroptosis-associated proteins using Western blotting and immunohistochemical staining. The results showed that the expression of NLRP3, cleaved-Caspase-1, GSDMD-N, and mature IL-1β were highly increased in the colon of DSS + P. anaerobius compared with DSS + PBS and DSS + E. coli group (Figure 5(b-i)). To further investigate NLRP3 inflammasome-dependent pyroptosis in macrophages in vivo, we assessed the co-localization of NLRP3 with the macrophage marker F4/80. Double-immunolabeling of mouse colon tissues revealed that NLRP3 activation was predominantly observed in macrophages (Figure 5(j)).

P. anaerobius activated NLRP3 inflammasome and induced GSDMD-mediated pyroptosis and IL-1β secretion in macrophages

NLRP3 is expressed in the immune cells of the lamina propria and is absent in epithelial cells in active UC [24]. Macrophages are the primary type of innate immune cells activated in colitis and play crucial roles in the progression of infection. To investigate the effects of P. anaerobius on NLRP3 inflammasome activation and GSDMD-mediated pyroptosis, we used mouse BMDMs. The BMDMs were infected with P. anaerobius at different time points and MOI. The viability of macrophages infected with P. anaerobius was assessed using CCK-8 cell viability and LDH release assays. Cytotoxic effects were investigated by varying the duration of infection and MOI (Figure 6(a, b, i, j)). To analyze the impact of P. anaerobius on NLRP3 inflammasome activation and pyroptosis in the macrophage, we measured the expression of NLRP3, Caspase-1, GSDMD, and IL-1β in extracts or culture supernatants from infected macrophages using Western blotting. Compared with uninfected cells, P. anaerobius significantly increased the expression of NLRP3, Caspase-1 p10, GSDMD-N, and IL-1β after 2, 4, and 6 h post-infection at MOI 80 (Figure (6c-h)), and at MOI 20, 40, and 80 after 6 h post-infection (Figure 6(k-p)). These results indicate that P. anaerobius activates the NLRP3 inflammasome, leading to GSDMD-mediated pyroptosis and IL-1β secretion in macrophages.

P. anaerobius-induced macrophage pyroptosis was associated with TLRs and NLRP3 inflammasome

Inflammasomes, which are signaling platforms assembled by cytosolic pattern recognition receptors (PRR), may activate Caspase-1. NLRP3 inflammasome assembly is regulated through priming by TLR ligands and subsequent activation signals. GSDMD, a crucial mediator of pyroptosis, is transcriptionally activated by both NF-κB and the NLRP3 inflammasomes. When extracellular signals associated with pyroptosis activate NLRP3 inflammasomes, activated Caspase-1 cleaves GSDMD into its N- and C-terminal fragments. This cleavage results in cell swelling and the activation of pro-IL-1β and pro-IL-18, converting them into their mature forms. Additionally, our findings indicate that P. anaerobius activates TLR2/4 and NF-κB signaling pathways in the context of DSS-induced colitis (Supplementary Figure S2). C29 is a TLR2 inhibitor that specifically targets the BB loop pocket within the TIR domain of TLR2. This inhibition effectively blocks hTLR2/1 and hTLR2/6 signaling [25]. TAK-242 was a selective TLR4 inhibitor by binding to Cys747 of the TLR4 intracellular domain, which could inhibit Nitric Oxide, TNF-α, and IL-6 production and downregulate the expression of TLR4 downstream signaling molecules Myeloid Differentiation Primary Response Protein 88 and TIR-domain-containing adapter-inducing interferon-β [26]. MCC950 is a specific inhibitor of the NLRP3 inflammasome that directly interacts with the Walker B motif of the NATCH domain to inhibit adenosine triphosphate hydrolysis [27]. To determine the involvement of TLRs in NLRP3 inflammasome-mediated signaling activation and GSDMD-mediated pyroptosis in P. anaerobius-infected macrophages, the cells were pre-incubated with C29, TAK-242, or MCC950 before P. anaerobius infection. BMDMs without any treatment were used as negative controls. The effects of the inhibitors on TLR2 and TLR4 expression in BMDMs were detected before P. anaerobius infection (Supplementary Figure S3). First, the expression levels of TLR2 and TLR4 in P. anaerobius-induced macrophages were measured. The expression levels of TLR2 and TLR4 increased after P. anaerobius infection (Figure 7(a-c)). After one hour of pretreatment with C29, TAK-242, and MCC950, BMDMs infected with P. anaerobius at an MOI of 80 for 6 h were assessed for cell viability and protein expression in both the culture supernatant and whole-cell lysates. The results showed that cell viability tested by CCK-8 and LDH release assays were significantly increased in the C29, TAK-242, and MCC950 pretreatment groups compared to the infection group without inhibitor pretreatment (Figure 7(d, e)). The expression of NF-κB, NLRP3, Caspase-1, GSDMD, and IL-1β of extracts or culture supernatants from P. anaerobius-infected macrophages following different pretreatments were detected using Western blotting. The phospho-NF-κB was significantly reduced in macrophages pretreated with C29, TAK-242, and MCC950 compared to macrophages infected with P. anaerobius without any inhibitor pretreatment. Pretreatment with these inhibitors notably suppressed the P. anaerobius-induced activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome, GSDMD-mediated pyroptosis, and secretion of IL-1β, compared to the group infected with P. anaerobius alone (Figure 7(f-l)).

Discussion

Previous investigations have indicated that microbial dysbiosis plays a significant role in the development of IBDs. Analysis of 16S rRNA gene sequencing data from patient samples revealed a notably high abundance of Peptostreptococcus species in individuals with UC and CD [10–12]. This study demonstrated that P. anaerobius enriched in the inflammatory intestine was potentially pathogenic for UC, and P. anaerobius supplementation could aggravate DSS-induced experimental colitis. A recent study reported that the abundance of P. anaerobius was higher in human colon tumor tissues and adenomas compared to control samples [8]. P. anaerobius adheres to colonic epithelial cells and initiates an oncogenic PI3K-Akt-FAK cascade to promote tumor cell proliferation [9]. However, the interactions between P. anaerobius and the host immune system remain unclear. In this study, we confirmed that P. anaerobius activates NLRP3 inflammasome and induces GSDMD-mediated pyroptosis and IL-1β secretion in macrophages to accelerate intestinal inflammation. Furthermore, we found that TLR2 and TLR4 are involved in P. anaerobius-induced macrophage pyroptosis by activating the NF-κB-NLRP3 pathway.

During intestinal inflammation, PRR signaling promotes mucosal repair and restores immune homeostasis. PRR stimulation facilitates the clearance of pathogens from the intestine [15]. As traditional PRRs of the innate immune system, TLRs primarily convert signals from bacteria in the microbiota into functional changes in innate immune cells. However, it is widely believed that Gram-positive bacteria lack cell wall lipopolysaccharides, which are the agonistic ligands for TLR4. Despite this, some studies have shown that lipopolysaccharides are not the only exotoxins capable of activating TLR4. For example, glycosylphosphatidylinositols from Toxoplasma gondii and toxins from Staphylococcus aureus can also activate TLR4 [28,29]. Different commensal species, including Bacteroides uniformis, Bacteroides ovatus, P. anaerobius, and Clostridium histolyticum, have been reported to engage in the activation of TLR2 and TLR4 and to stimulate canonical NF-κB signaling. This activation has been analyzed through the study of receptors, intracellular signaling components, and transcription factors [13]. The effect of P. anaerobius activation is mediated through interaction with TLR2/4 on host cells to induce ROS production, increase cholesterol biosynthesis, and activate pro-oncogenic factors and pathways to promote tumorigenesis of colorectal cancer [8]. The interaction between P. anaerobius and colorectal cancer cells (HT-29 and Caco-2) via integrin α2/β1-PI3K-Akt-NF-κB signaling pathway has also been reported [9]. However, the effect of P. anaerobius on macrophages remains unclear. In this study, we confirmed that P. anaerobius induces macrophage pyroptosis and IL-1β secretion. This process is associated with the activation of TLR2 and TLR4. In comparison, the administration of E. coli MG1655, a nonpathogenic strain closely related to the K12 wild-type, did not elicit the same response [30]. We used E. coli MG1655 as a control for investigating the effects of the pathogenic P. anaerobius, focusing on the distinction between pathogenic and nonpathogenic bacteria rather than categorizing them as Gram-negative versus Gram-positive or coccus versus bacillus. Similar experimental designs, using E. coli MG1655 as a control in studies of IBDs and colorectal cancers, have been reported in previous research [31,32].

Several recent reviews have highlighted the contributions of various inflammasome platforms, specifically the NLRP3, NAIP/NLRC4, NLRP6, and non-canonical caspase-4/11 pathways expressed in intestinal epithelial cells. These pathways play significant roles in the inflammasome-mediated modulation of gastrointestinal function, influenced by both resident and exogenous bacteria [33,34]. Although NLRP3 inflammasome has also been reported to have a protective effect on the intestinal epithelial cells during experimental colitis [35], it is crucial for the activation of macrophages and is considered to be a proinflammatory signaling pathway in IBDs [36]. Moreover, the attenuation of macrophages and NLRP3 inflammasome activation has been shown to alleviate UC [37,38]. Interestingly, NLRP3 activation has also been implicated in the protective effect of Akkermansia muciniphila on acute colitis. In A. muciniphila-treated mice, the expression levels of NLRP3, caspase-1 p20, and IL-1β p17 were upregulated [39]. In this study, we demonstrated that the activation of NLRP3 inflammasome is responsible for P. anaerobius-induced proinflammatory effect by promoting macrophage pyroptosis and IL-1β secretion. We speculated that NLRP3 plays different roles in the activation of beneficial and harmful bacteria. It is worth noting that Candida albicans can hijack inflammasome-GSDMD pathway-mediated pyroptosis to promote its escape from macrophages, suggesting that GSDMD may be a potential therapeutic target in pathogen-induced inflammatory diseases [40]. Activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome, which contributes to the inflammatory response via macrophage pyroptosis, has also been observed in other inflammatory diseases, such as allergic rhinitis [41], chronic obstructive pulmonary disease [42], and Mycobacterium tuberculosis-induced infection [16]. As an important transcriptional regulator, NF-κB plays a crucial role in the regulation of inflammation response by inducing NLRP3 expression [22,43]. It has been shown that MCC950, a specific inhibitor of NLRP3, can reduce inflammation and NF-κB activation by inhibiting NLRP3 in a murine model of non-alcoholic fatty liver disease or primary dysmenorrhea [44,45]. These findings are consistent with our results. MCC950 intervention strongly attenuated the activation of NF-κB in P. anaerobius-infected macrophages, which indicated that the NF-κB pathway plays a critical role in P. anaerobius-induced macrophage pyroptosis via NF-κB-NLRP3 pathway and that inhibiting NLRP3 inflammasomes could downregulate NF-κB activation.

We observed that P. anaerobius-treated and P. anaerobius-colonized mice exhibited a disruption of microbial homeostasis. The microbial structure shifted during the progression of P. anaerobius-infected experimental colitis, characterized by enrichment of inflammatory microbial communities. A significant increase in the relative abundance of gut pathogens (Escherichia Shigella, Staphylococcus, Pseudomonas, and Flavonifractor) was observed in the feces of P. anaerobius-treated mice at the end of the experiment. Escherichia Shigella, Staphylococcus, and Pseudomonas are common pathogens involved in infections and inflammation [46–48]. Flavonifractor can be categorized as an enriched species in the early stages of colorectal cancer [49]. In contrast, the relative abundance of beneficial bacteria, such as Muribaculaceae, Odoribacter, Rikenellaceae RC9, Alistipes, and Akkermansia, decreased in P. anaerobius-treated mice. Previous studies have reported that reduced proportions of Muribaculaceae are associated with increased disease severity in mice [50]. Additionally, the enrichment of Alistipes and Odoribacter species increased in patients with UC in remission following fecal microbiota transplantation [51]. Alistipes species have also been identified as a potential diagnostic biomarker for CD in a recent report [52]. Furthermore, Rikenellaceae RC9, Alistipes, and Akkermansia have been recognized as beneficial for the production of short-chain fatty acids and play a protective role in alleviating intestinal inflammation [53–55]. This suggests that the harmful effects of P. anaerobius administration on the intestinal mucosa may be indirectly caused by the disruption of gut commensal bacteria. However, previous studies have shown that the human macrophage NLRP3 inflammasome could be directly activated by staphylococcus aureus extracellular vesicles [56], which induces pyroptosis of epithelial cells via activation of the ROS-NLRP3 signaling pathway [57]. This highlights the direct destructive impact of pathogenic bacteria on the host.

Conclusion

In conclusion, we confirmed that P. anaerobius can promote macrophage pyroptosis and IL-1β secretion through the involvement of TLR2/4, as well as activation of the NF-κB-NLRP3 pathway, thereby exacerbating experimental colitis. Our study strongly suggests that P. anaerobius is a potential risk factor for the development of IBD.
