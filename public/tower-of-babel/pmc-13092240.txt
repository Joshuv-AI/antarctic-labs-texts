Efficacy and Safety of Anthracyclines Combined with Temozolomide Regimens for the Treatment of Advanced Leiomyosarcoma: A Retrospective Study
Authors: Huijing Tan, Jianguo Zhou, Susheng Shi, Liming Jiang, Lijie Zuo, Aijiang Su, Huike Wang, Yuting Sun, Wenjuan Xuan, Yihebali Chi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLeiomyosarcoma (LMS) is an aggressive soft tissue sarcoma with high rates of hematogenous metastasis and recurrent relapse, with a 5-year survival rate of 17.0%. This study evaluated the clinical efficacy of an anthracycline plus temozolomide (ATEM) regimen in advanced LMS and explored the feasibility of dynamic circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) monitoring for early detection of disease progression.MethodsThis retrospective study included patients with advanced LMS who received ATEM between September 2018 and December 2024. The efficacy, including complete response (CR), partial response (PR), stable disease (SD), or progressive disease (PD), objective response rate (ORR), and disease control rate (DCR), as well as safety, were assessed. The median progression-free survival (mPFS) and median overall survival (mOS) were calculated using Kaplan–Meier survival curves. A subset of patients treated with TEM-containing regimens between December 2023 and December 2024 underwent longitudinal ctDNA monitoring.ResultsForty-seven patients were included, with a median follow-up of 39.5 months. The mPFS was 12.0 months and mOS was 37.5 months. One patient achieved clinical CR, 9 achieved PR, 33 had SD and 4 experienced PD. The ORR was 21.3% (10/47), and DCR was 91.5% (43/47). The most common adverse events were leukopenia (42.6%), neutropenia (32.0%), and thrombocytopenia (14.9%). Grade 4 neutropenia occurred in three patients, including one case complicated by febrile neutropenia. All cases were managed successfully with supportive treatment. No treatment-related deaths were observed. In this pilot investigation of 7 patients, ctDNA levels consistently reflected clinical disease status and treatment response.ConclusionATEM regimen demonstrated promising efficacy in advanced LMS, with survival outcomes comparable to or better than in prior first-line treatments. ctDNA monitoring showed promise as a non-invasive biomarker for disease monitoring and treatment guidance. Prospective, multi-center studies are warranted to validate these findings.Keywords: temozolomide, advanced leiomyosarcoma, anthracyclines, chemotherapy, ctDNA

Introduction

Soft tissue sarcoma (STS), arising from mesenchymal tissues, represents approximately 0.8% of all human malignancies.1 Leiomyosarcoma (LMS) constitutes 24% of STS and predominantly affects middle-aged to elderly individuals (40–60 years old). LMS frequently originates in the retroperitoneum (23.0%) and gynecological organs (33.0%). This subtype is clinically aggressive, with a high tendency for hematogenous spread and a risk of late relapse that may occur years to decades after initial surgery. The current standard management prioritizes surgical resection as the foundation for local disease, while anthracycline-based chemotherapy serves as the first-line systemic treatment for advanced, unresectable, or metastatic STS.2

Retroperitoneal LMS (RLMS), a deep-seated LMS subtype, presents diagnostic and therapeutic challenges due to its concealed anatomical location and low rates of complete surgical resection. RLMS is associated with a worse prognosis compared to other LMS subtypes, with a 5-year survival rate of 50.0%–60.0%.1 It also demonstrates high rates of local recurrence and metastasis to organs, such as the liver and lungs. Uterine LMS (uLMS) is rare, accounting for <5.0% of uterine malignancies but approximately 50% of uterine sarcoma subtypes. The reported diagnostic detections rates are 56.4% for standard ultrasound (US), 88.4% for consultant US, and 87.5% for magnetic resonance imaging (MRI).3 Compared with RLMS, uLMS shows relatively higher chemosensitivity.4,5 However, approximately 50.0% of patients with high-grade uLMS experience recurrence within two years, predominantly with extrapelvic metastases involving the abdominopelvic cavity, liver, lungs, bones, or distant subcutaneous/soft tissue.

Anthracycline and/or ifosfamide (AI)-based regimens have remained the first-line standard treatment for metastatic, recurrent, or unresectable LMS for decades.2 However, for patients who progress to first-line therapy, no established standard for second-line therapy currently exists. Available options include single-agent therapies, such as gemcitabine (GEM), dacarbazine (DTIC), temozolomide (TEM), trabectedin (Trab), and eribulin, or combination regimens, such as GEM plus docetaxel (TXT) or GEM–DTIC. Despite these treatments, overall prognosis remains poor, with a median overall survival (mOS) of 8.0–13.0 months following first-line initiation, and a 5-year survival rate of only 17.0%.6

Temozolomide (TEM), an oral alkylating agent traditionally used in glioblastoma and melanoma, exerts antitumor activity via DNA methylation. Several Phase II trials in advanced STS have demonstrated its efficacy, particularly in unresectable uterine/non-uterine LMS following first-line failure. Compared to the outcomes in broader STS populations, TEM achieved notably improved efficacy in LMS, including an objective response rate (ORR) of 18.0% vs. 8.0%, stable disease (SD) of 27.0% vs. 8.0%, disease control rate (DCR) of 45.0% vs. 16.0%, median progression-free survival (mPFS) of 3.9 vs. 2.0 months, and mOS of 30.8 vs. 13.0 months. Also, it was reported that most adverse events (AEs) are manageable in LMS.7–9

Doxorubicin, a key anthracycline agent, mediates its cytotoxic effects through the inhibition of DNA/RNA synthesis. Notably, epirubicin (EPI) has demonstrated reduced cardiotoxicity and hematologic toxicity compared to doxorubicin (ADM/A) while maintaining comparable efficacy in advanced STS.10 In recent years, pegylated liposomal doxorubicin (PLD) has emerged as a promising treatment option for metastatic STS. When combined with anlotinib, PLD has shown promising activity, with an ORR of 40.7%, DCR of 81.5%, and mPFS of 7.0 months.11 Compared to conventional doxorubicin, PLD offers similar efficacy but with reduced hematologic toxicity, alopecia, and cardiotoxicity, although clinicians should remain alert for potential hypersensitivity reactions. Therefore, we hypothesized that combining anthracyclines with temozolomide improves disease control in advanced LMS compared to historical outcomes of standard therapies. Accordingly, this study aimed to evaluate the efficacy and safety of EPI or PLD combined with TEM in this patient population.

There is increasing interest in circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) as a minimally invasive biomarker for real-time disease monitoring. ctDNA consists of tumor-derived fragments of cell-free DNA in peripheral blood and can reflect tumor-associated genomic alterations, including mutations, rearrangements, copy number variations, and methylation abnormalities. Tumor-informed ctDNA assay use molecular profiling of an individual patient’s tumor to select high-confidence, patient-specific variants for longitudinal tracking, thereby improving specificity and reducing interference from non-tumor-related variants. This approach enables dynamic assessment of treatment response and clonal evolution, although it may fail to capture newly emergent alterations outside the tracked set. Given the rarity and molecular heterogeneity of STS, the feasibility and clinical utility of ctDNA monitoring in STS remains unclear. Therefore, we further explored the feasibility of serial ctDNA monitoring to detect disease progression in a subset of patients with advanced LMS.

Materials and Methods

Participants

This single-center retrospective study was conducted between September 2018 and December 2024 at the National Cancer Center/National Clinical Research Center for Cancer/Cancer Hospital, Chinese Academy of Medical Sciences (CAMS) and Peking Union Medical College (PUMC). The study protocol was approved by the institutional review board and conducted in full compliance with Good Clinical Practice (GCP) guidelines and the Declaration of Helsinki. All patient data were anonymized to ensure confidentiality.

Eligible patients were retrospectively enrolled based on the following inclusion criteria: (1) histopathologically confirmed diagnosis of unresectable or metastatic LMS according to WHO classification;12 (2) Basic information such as age, tumor site, tumor size, presence of metastasis, and metastatic site are obtainable and clear,13–15 and baseline radiologic assessment available showing ≥1 measurable lesion according to RECIST 1.1; (3) Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) performance status of 0–2; (4) age ≥ 18 years at baseline; and (5) estimated life expectancy > 3.0 months.

Treatments

EPI was administered via continuous intravenous infusion at a dose of 60–70 mg/m2 for 1–4 days, evenly distributed over 96 hours. PLD was administered as an intravenous infusion at a dose of 30–40 mg/m2 on day 1. TEM was orally administered at 200 or 300 mg/d based on body surface area (≥1.7 m2, 300 mg; <1.7 m2, 200 mg) on days 1–5. The combination of EPI/PLD with TEM is referred to as the ATEM regimen. This regimen is prioritized for anthracycline-naïve patients. Treatment was delivered in 21-day cycles (3 weeks).

Treatment continued until the sign of progressive disease (PD), the occurrence of intolerable AEs, or the cumulative dose of anthracycline reached the dose limit (EPI or PLD not exceeding 900 mg/m2). Dose adjustments were made based on toxicity and patient tolerance. Following an intensive phase (typically no more than 8 cycles depending on disease control and tolerability), patients proceeded to one of two maintenance phases with TEM: single-agent maintenance therapy, in which TEM was orally administered at 200 or 300 mg/day on days 1–5 of a 21-day cycle; or combination maintenance therapy (TEM plus thalidomide [Thal]), in which TEM was administered as described above, while Thal was administered once nightly on days 1–7, with dose escalation to 200 mg on days 8–14, and 300 mg from day 15 onward, and continued until PD or the occurrence of intolerable AEs.

Between December 2023 and December 2024, a subset of patients initiating ATEM regimens underwent whole-exome sequencing (WES) to complete tumor genomic profiling and analysis, with an aim to identify patient-specific somatic single nucleotide variants (SNVs). WES was performed across the whole exome with a mean sequencing depth of approximately 200× for tumor tissue and 100× for matched normal samples. Subsequently, personalized, tumor-informed ctDNA testing was performed to preliminarily investigate the temporal dynamics of ctDNA monitoring for disease progression in advanced LMS patients. Plasma ctDNA was assessed using targeted deep sequencing (RACEseq) with an average sequencing depth of ~20,000×. For reproducibility, ctDNA positivity was determined using a pre-specified detection threshold based on the number of authentic mutation sites detected. A sample was classified as ctDNA-positive if two or more authentic mutation sites, consistent with those found in the primary tumor tissue, were detected, after excluding background noise interference. Conversely, the sample was classified as ctDNA-negative if zero or one authentic mutation site was detected (Supplementary Figure 1).

Outcomes

Efficacy was evaluated every two treatment cycles according to RECIST version 1.1.16 Responses were categorized as complete response (CR), partial response (PR), SD, or PD.17 ORR was defined as the sum of CR and PR, and DCR was defined as the sum of CR, PR, and SD. Progression-free survival (PFS) was the time from the initiation of the chemotherapy regimen to PD or death from any cause. Overall survival (OS) was the time from the initiation of the chemotherapy regimen to death from any cause. AEs were graded according to the NCI-CTCAE 4.0 standard for common drug toxicity classification developed by the National Cancer Institute (NCI). The primary endpoints included PFS, OS, ORR, and the safety profiles characterized by AEs were exploratory endpoints.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 22.0 or R software version 4.0.1. Variables were compared using the chi-square test. 95% CIs were constructed using the Wald method. The HR was estimated using a univariate Cox regression model. Analysis for PFS was performed using a Cox regression model. Time-to-event data were used to estimate the survival rates and generate Kaplan–Meier survival curves. Graphs were drawn using GraphPad Prism software version 10.1.2.

Results

Baseline Characteristics

In total, 47 patients were included in the study who had advanced LMS and received an anthracycline-based regimen combined with TEM (ATEM). Among them, 29 cases received ATEM as first-line therapy, 11 as second-line, 5 as third-line, and 2 as fourth-line treatment. Polyethylene glycol-conjugated granulocyte colony-stimulating factor (G-CSF) was administered if necessary.

The cohort included 9 males and 38 females, with ages ranging from 27 to 66 (median: 53) years old. The primary lesion was located in the retroperitoneum in 24 cases (including originating from the inferior vena cava/renal vein), in the uterus in 11 cases, and in other locations in 12 cases, such as the elbow, wrist, nasal cavity, atrium, stomach, pancreas, mesentery, abdominal pelvis and thigh. Outcomes

Among all the participants, two patients were in the intensive treatment phase, and four patients were still receiving maintenance treatment with TEM alone. The other treatment conditions of all 47 patients in this study are shown in Figure 1.

Flowchart of the present study.

Among the 47 patients evaluated, one patient (2.1%, 1/47) achieved CR, 9 patients (19.1%, 9/47) achieved PR, 33 patients (70.2%, 33/47) had SD, and 4 patients (8.5%, 4/47) experienced PD. The ORR was 21.3% (10/47) and DCR was 91.5% (43/47).

The median follow-up duration was 39.5 months. The mPFS was 12.0 (95% CI: 8.3–15.7) months (Figure 2A), and the mOS was 37.5 (95% CI: 25.1–29.9) months (Figure 2B). Among the patients, 4 exhibited primary drug resistance after 2 cycles of treatment, and 3 patients underwent surgical resection after achieving PR (Figure 1). Excluding these latter 7 patients, the overall PFS for the remaining patients was from 4 months onward, with a minimum of 6 treatment cycles. The longest PFS exceeded 55.0 months and the longest OS exceeded 65.5 months during outpatient follow-up, following post-line treatment. Patients who achieved a PR showed a longer mPFS (16.5 vs. 12.0 months, p=0.162) and mOS (58.5 vs. 32.0 months, p=0.060) than those who had SD, although without statistical significance (Figure 2C and Figure 2D). The swimmer plot shows the time to tumor response and treatment and the follow-up periods for each patient (Figure 3).

The relationships between the baseline factors and PFS were analyzed. As shown in Table 2 and Supplementary Figure 2, there was a significant difference between patients with and without lymph node metastasis (LNM) in terms of both mPFS (13.0 vs. 5.3 months, p=0.0029) and mOS (40.5 vs. 18.5 months, p=0.0013). Other factors even showed statistically significant correlations (Table 2), but the differences were not further reflected in the specific PFS numerical values, possibly due to the small sample size and the limited statistical power. Patients with uLMS did not show better PFS outcomes compared to those with RLMS. This may be attributed to their baseline characteristics, such as a higher proportion of high Ki-67 expression or a greater proportion of patients receiving the ATEM regimen in later lines of therapy.

Analysis of the ORR and DCR in Advanced LMS Patients

There was a significant association between LNM metastasis and PFS (Figure 4A). LNM is uncommon in STS, and routine lymph node dissection is generally not performed during surgery. However, when LNM was present, the prognosis tended to be worse; Although patients with Ki-67 expression levels higher than 50% showed a trend toward a higher ORR (26.7% vs. 19.0% vs. 12.5%), their PFS tended to be shorter (Figure 4B). PFS was the least favorable in patients with a tumor size exceeding 15.0 cm (Figure 4C). Additionally, a negative MGMT pathological IHC expression was associated with a trend toward improved PFS (Figure 4D).

Adverse Events

Among the 47 patients, the most common AEs were leukopenia (42.6%), neutropenia (32.0%), thrombocytopenia (14.9%), anemia (8.5%), mild liver function increase (23.4%), nausea (53.2%), vomiting (34.0%), fatigue (19.1%), rash (12.8%), and oral mucositis (10.6%). The majority of AEs were grade 1–2 in severity and generally within the tolerable range. One case developed grade 4 neutropenia accompanied by fever, while two cases experienced grade 4 neutropenia. All the cases were managed successfully with supportive treatment. No treatment-related deaths were observed.

Relationship Between ctDNA Dynamics and Disease Progression

This analysis represents an initial exploratory investigation of ctDNA in a limited cohort. Among the four patients with radiographically SD, longitudinal ctDNA monitoring revealed a trend toward decreasing levels or minimal fluctuations over time (Figure 5A and B). In contrast, all three patients experiencing radiographically confirmed disease progression exhibited concurrent elevations in ctDNA levels at the time of progression documentation (Figure 5C). This temporal concordance suggests a potential association between rising ctDNA levels, as measured by personalized assays, and disease progression in this cohort of advanced LMS patients.

Discussion

In the present study, we evaluated the efficacy and safety of TEM combined with EPI or PLD in patients with advanced LMS. Our data showed that the mPFS was 12.0 months and the mOS was 37.5 months, with an ORR of 21.3% and a DCR of 91.5%. Moreover, the AEs were all manageable. Moreover, the dynamics of ctDNA levels appeared to correlate with changes in tumor burden and clinical disease progression.

Anthracyclines are still the standard first-line treatment for LMS with late metastasis, relapse, and refractory or no operation opportunity. At present, there are no standard second-line or later chemotherapy regimens for patients with advanced LMS. TEM monotherapy has demonstrated promising efficacy in advanced STS following first-line chemotherapy.7–9 EPI and PLD are more tolerated than traditional anthracyclines while maintaining comparable efficacy.10 Therefore, TEM combined with EPI or PLD is the optimal choice in clinical practice.

When contextualized against prior evidence (Table 4), our outcomes appear clinically encouraging. The European Soft Tissue and Bone Sarcoma Group (STBSG) of the EORTC conducted a retrospective study comparing doxorubicin combined with dacarbazine (DoDa), doxorubicin plus ifosfamide (DI), and doxorubicin alone (Do) in advanced soft tissue sarcoma. The DoDa regimen demonstrated superior PFS and ORR compared to DI or Do, with a mPFS of 9.2 months, mOS of 36.8 months, and ORR of 30.9%.18 A single-center retrospective study of TEM combined with EPI in advanced LMS reported an mPFS of 10.0 months, ORR of 53.3%, and a DCR of 100.0%,19 which provided the basis for this study to continue to explore the optimization scheme. The landmark LMS-04 Phase III trial evaluated doxorubicin plus trabectedin versus doxorubicin alone as a first-line therapy for metastatic/advanced uterine and soft tissue LMS. The combination significantly improved the PFS and OS, with an mPFS of 12.2 vs. 6.2 months (p<0.0001), and mOS of 33.1 vs. 23.8 months (p=0.0253). The ORR and DCR of the combination group were 36.5% and 91.9%, respectively. The results suggest that doxorubicin plus trabectedin might be a first-line treatment option for metastatic/advanced uterine and soft tissue LMS.20,21 However, uncontrollable hepatotoxic events were observed, and trabectedin has not yet been listed in mainland China. Within these constraints, our ATEM results (mPFS 12.0 months; mOS 37.5 months; DCR 91.5%) are broadly comparable to outcomes reported with intensified first-line anthracycline-based combinations. Given the retrospective design and heterogeneous treatment lines in our cohort, these findings should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating rather than definitive evidence of superiority; nevertheless, they support prospective validation of ATEM as a potentially effective and tolerable option in advanced LMS.

Summary of the Publications on STS/LMS Treatment

The role of anthracyclines as first-line therapy has also been debated in the context of alternative cytotoxic regimens and emerging combination strategies (Table 4). A study comparing GEM-TXT to GEM monotherapy favored the combination in terms of the ORR, PFS, and OS.22 However, subsequent research found doxorubicin monotherapy non-inferior to GEM–TXT, reaffirming anthracyclines as the standard first-line option.23 A single-arm phase II trial on the first-line treatment of advanced STS patients with anlotinib combined with EPI reported an ORR of 13.3%, DCR of 80.0%, and mPFS of 11.5 months, supporting the further exploration of antiangiogenic–chemotherapy combinations.24 Several trials have evaluated novel combinations with immunotherapy. A multicenter, single-arm, prospective trial on the first-line treatment of unresectable metastatic or locally advanced STS patients (50% dedifferentiated liposarcoma) with penpulimab combined with anlotinib and epirubicin showed a mPFS of about 11.0 months, with the mOS not yet reached.25 Overall, the combination of immunotherapy with chemotherapy and anti-angiogenic therapy has shown promise in the treatment of STS patients. However, several critical challenges remain unresolved, including the optimal timing for administration, whether these therapies should be administered sequentially or concurrently, and the underlying mechanisms of therapeutic synergy. In current clinical practice, the choice of initial treatment still heavily depends on the physician judgment and the institutional protocols. Critical questions persist though: Is the traditional approach of combining chemotherapy and anti-angiogenic therapy with immunotherapy superior to sequential or alternating regimens? What constitutes the most effective combination strategy? And which patient subgroups would benefit most? Given the complexity and heterogeneity of STS, a deeper understanding of their pathophysiology and the identification of relevant biomarkers are essential to maximize the patient benefit from these emerging therapies.

In a phase I/II trial evaluating PD-L1 inhibition combined with doxorubicin as the first-line treatment of STS (n=44), the ORR was 31.7% (including one case of CR, and 12 cases of PR), mPFS was 8.2 months, and the mOS was 24.1 months, with grade 3–4 AEs primarily being myelosuppression and anemia.26 A phase I/II study investigating pembrolizumab combined with doxorubicin as the first-line therapy for unresectable/metastatic sarcoma reported an ORR of 22%, DCR of 81% (59% achieving SD), and a 6-month PFS rate of 73%, which was higher than the historical rate of 40%.27 Recently published results from a phase II trial evaluating eribulin plus pembrolizumab in 57 patients with advanced STS showed 12-week PFS rates of 36.8%, 69.6%, and 52.6% in LMS, LPS, and UPS/other STS cohorts, respectively.28

At the 2024 American Society of Clinical Oncology (ASCO) Annual Meeting, the results of the single-arm phase II trial (SAIS study) were presented. This trial evaluated sintilimab plus doxorubicin and ifosfamide (AI) as the first-line treatment for advanced UPS, SS, myxoid liposarcoma (MLPS), and DDLPS patients. Among the 41 evaluable patients, the ORR was 68.3%, the mPFS reached 8.9 months, and the mOS was 19.5 months, suggesting the synergistic effects of the immunochemotherapy effects.29 Separately, the Spanish Sarcoma Research Group (GEIS) Ib phase study investigated doxorubicin and dacarbazine combined with nivolumab as a first-line therapy for advanced LMS patients (January 2002 to July 2023). In their 23 evaluable patients, the mPFS was 8.7 months (PR 56.5%, SD 39.1%, PD 4.4%), with a 6-month PFS rate of 80%. The regimen demonstrated safety, feasibility, and promising clinical activity, warranting further validation in Phase II.30 Currently, immune checkpoint inhibitor–chemotherapy combinations represent a favorable strategy in advanced STS, though robust evidence remains lacking to confirm its superiority over standard chemotherapy alone (Table 4). Further studies are needed to identify optimal regimens with enhanced efficacy and manageable toxicity.

We also explored clinical and pathological factors associated with outcomes in our cohort. Patients achieving PR tended to have longer PFS and OS than those with SD, supporting the clinical observation that objective tumor shrinkage may translate into improved long-term outcomes even in advanced LMS. LNM was associated with significantly shorter PFS, consistent with the notion that nodal involvement, uncommon in sarcoma and therefore not routinely addressed surgically, often reflects aggressive tumor biology. A higher Ki-67 proliferation index (>50%) was associated with a higher ORR but a trend toward shorter PFS, suggesting that highly proliferative tumors may be more initially chemosensitive yet prone to early relapse. Tumor size >15.0 cm was associated with limited PFS benefit, implying that higher baseline tumor burden adversely affects prognosis. Finally, MGMT-negative immunohistochemistry showed a trend toward improved PFS, which is biologically plausible for an alkylating agent such as TEM; however, this observation remains exploratory and requires validation in larger, prospectively characterized cohorts.

Maintenance therapy represents another important clinical consideration for sustaining disease control after induction therapy. In this study, two basic maintenance therapies (TEM alone or TEM combined with Thal) were applied. Only three patients received TEM combined with Thal: one patient received the maintenance for 15.0 months. Another patient discontinued Thal after 2 cycles due to suffering from palpitations and headache, and continued on TEM monotherapy for 9.0 months. A third patient maintained combination maintenance for 7.0 months. Following 6–8 cycles of intensive chemotherapy, the patients who were non-progressive or alive transitioned to maintenance therapy with TEM monotherapy, with the average time to initiation of maintenance being 6.4 months. Four patients achieved their best tumor response during the maintenance phase: two patients in the combination group achieved their best responses at 2.0 and 6.0 months into maintenance, with subsequent disease stabilization for 7.0 and 9.0 months, respectively; while two patients in the TEM monotherapy group achieved their best responses at 2.0 and 7.0 months, followed by stable durations of 9.0 and 3.0 months, respectively. These findings highlight the potential value of maintenance therapy in sustaining disease control after the initial response, warranting further clinical application and validation.

Previous studies on maintenance strategies in advanced LMS have yielded mixed results (Table 4). A study on the second-line treatment of advanced LMS using gemcitabine (GEM) plus pazopanib followed by pazopanib maintenance after the failure of first-line anthracyclines reported a mPFS of 6.5 months and ORR of 23.8%, with limited benefit observed in LMS patients.31 In contrast, a study evaluating anlotinib maintenance following first-line anthracycline-based chemotherapy in advanced STS reported a mPFS of 9.1 months, with a DCR of 93.0% among 15 LMS patients.32 Additionally, the 2024 ESMO Congress presented results from the EREMISS study, providing the first evidence-based data for maintenance therapy in advanced non-adipocytic STS (ASTS). With LMS being the most common subtype (59%), the study demonstrated that regorafenib significantly improved the mPFS (5.6 vs. 3.5 months, HR=0.51) in patients achieving SD/PR after first-line doxorubicin chemotherapy, particularly in LMS and in patients unable to tolerate continued chemotherapy. While the mOS reached 27.6 months without statistical significance, this study introduced a novel maintenance strategy for ASTS, pending larger phase III trials to confirm the long-term survival benefits and molecular biomarker-guided precision approaches. Notably, 28% (18/64) of REGO patients discontinued treatment due to toxicity, with the most common grade ≥3 clinical AEs being fatigue (9%), arterial hypertension (8%), and skin rash (8%).33

Thalidomide (Thal), used as part of the dual-agent maintenance in our study, offers potential ancillary benefits, such as sedation, antiemetic effects, and anxiety-related insomnia relief. Its proposed antitumor mechanisms include anti-angiogenesis, immune regulation, and inducing tumor cell apoptosis. In our cohort, two of the three patients receiving dual-agent maintenance achieved their best tumor responses during treatment (at 2.0 and 6.0 months), followed by prolonged disease stabilization (7.0 and 9.0 months), with total maintenance durations of 9.0 and 15.0 months, respectively. While promising, Thal use requires careful monitoring due to its known AEs, and supportive care is critical to maintaining treatment adherence and the patients’ quality of life. Our data support the feasibility and potential benefit of maintenance therapy in advanced LMS, particularly after initial disease control with intensive therapy. Both TEM monotherapy and the combination of TEM plus Thal merit further investigation to optimize the balance between efficacy and tolerability.

Finally, we explored the feasibility of ctDNA monitoring in LMS. LMS is characterized by complex copy number alterations and relatively few recurrent single-nucleotide variants, which may complicate ctDNA assay design and detection sensitivity. Despite these challenges, our pilot data suggest that ctDNA kinetics may reflect tumor burden changes and clinical progression. Given the molecular heterogeneity across STS subtypes, ctDNA performance is likely subtype- and assay-dependent; tumor-informed approaches may be particularly useful for longitudinal tracking when patient-specific alterations can be reliably identified. Larger prospective studies are needed to determine analytical sensitivity, optimal sampling intervals, and the clinical value of ctDNA for response assessment, minimal residual disease detection, and early progression identification in LMS. This study has several limitations to note. First, as a retrospective study, the data collection was susceptible to selection bias, making it difficult to fully control for confounding factors. Second, the small sample size may have led to insufficient statistical power, potentially limiting the generalizability of the conclusions. Third, the single-center design may have introduced institutional biases, restricting the external validity of the findings, also incomplete medical records or inadequate pathological descriptions of baseline characteristics such as Ki-67 and LNM further limit the analysis. Accordingly, our findings should be viewed as hypothesis-generating real-world evidence that supports prospective multicenter validation using standardized regimens and predefined endpoints.

Conclusions

In summary, this single-center retrospective study of ATEM chemotherapy in patients with advanced LMS demonstrated encouraging clinical benefits an mPFS of 12.0 months and mOS of 37.5 months with manageable toxicity. Prospective, large-scale, multi-center clinical trials using standardized anthracycline-based regimens are needed to further evaluate the efficacy of ATEM compared to conventional first-line treatments. Our preliminary findings indicate a consistent pattern linking ctDNA kinetics to disease progression. However, given the exploratory nature and small sample size of this study, larger-scale, prospective validation is warranted to rigorously evaluate the clinical utility of ctDNA monitoring for prognostic stratification and early detection of progression in advanced LMS.
