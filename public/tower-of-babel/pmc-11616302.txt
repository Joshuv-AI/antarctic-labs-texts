Genomic and clinical characterization of pediatric lymphoepithelioma-like carcinoma
Authors: Yi Que, Xiuxia Lu, Suying Lu, Feifei Sun, Jia Zhu, Yu Zhang, Juan Wang, Junting Huang, Wei Liu, Fenghua Wang, Liping Li, Li Zhang, Min Gao, Zijun Zhen, Yizhuo Zhang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPediatric lymphoepithelioma-like carcinoma (pLELC) is a rare neoplasm with unclear prognosis, genome, and tumor microenvironment. Our study aims to elucidate its genomic and clinical characteristics.MethodsForty-one pLELC patients were enrolled at Sun Yat-sen University Cancer Center from 2012 to 2023. Kaplan–Meier analysis was utilized to estimate progression-free survival (PFS) and overall survival (OS). Baseline plasma protein levels from 16 patients and 9 health controls were analyzed using a Olink proteomic platform and whole exon sequence (WES) was performed on 11 tumor samples from 10 pediatric patients. Immunohistochemistry (IHC) for PD-L1was performed, and the infiltration of CD4+ or CD8+ T cells was evaluated.ResultsPatients receiving anti PD-1 in combination with chemotherapy had a 1-year PFS of 100%, while the 2-year PFS was 72.92% (95%CI: 46.80‒100%). The 1-year OS for patients receiving anti PD-1 in combination with chemotherapy was 100%, and the 2-year OS was 87.5% (95%CI: 67.34–100%). Significant upregulation of immune checkpoint molecules was detected including LAG-3, PD-L1, and galectin-9 in LELC group by proteomic analysis (P < 0.05). The mutational landscape of pediatric LELC presented more genes mutated in pathways associated with immune, DNA repair, cell cycle and NOTCH. Pathway analysis of mutational profiles indicated DNA repair pathway and SWI/SNF complex were potential drug targets for pLELC patients. All the pediatric LELC patients evaluated exhibited positive PD-L1 expression and CD4+/CD8+ T cells infiltration.ConclusionsOur findings indicate a promising response rate associated with the combination of PD-1 antibody treatment and chemotherapy in pediatric patients with LELC, providing a theoretical basis for targeting DNA repair pathways. These outcomes suggest that clinical trials involving immune checkpoint inhibitors are warranted in pediatric patients with LELC.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12967-024-05921-1.

Introduction

Lymphoepithelioma-like carcinoma is an exceptionally rare malignancy among pediatric populations and is characterized by its histopathological similarity to Epstein-Barr virus (EBV)-associated nasopharyngeal carcinoma. Zhang et al. conducted a study exploring the link between EBV and thymic LELC in 23 pediatric patients and revealed that 12 patients (52%) tested positive for EBV, and all children with available EBV data were found to be associated with EBV infection [1]. Currently, there is a lack of systematic reports on pLELC, and most are case studies. Previous studies have reported nearly 17 cases of thymic LELC in patients under 18 years of age, with a 69% mortality rate and an average survival time of 12.6 months after diagnosis [2–4].

LELC rarely occurs in children and is even rarer in adults. In adult LELC, Hong et al. described the genomic alterations in adult pulmonary LELC, elucidating potential tumorigenesis mechanisms and providing evidence that pulmonary LELC is a distinct type of lung cancer resembling adult nasopharyngeal carcinoma (NPC) [5]. He et al. identified a clinically actionable subset of patients with EBV-associated lymphoepithelioma-like cholangiocarcinoma who exhibited a promising response to PD-1 antibody therapy [6]. Additionally, Huang et al. reported that EBV-associated lymphoepithelioma-like cholangiocarcinomas, accounting for 6.6% of intrahepatic cholangiocarcinomas (ICCs), present unique etiological characteristics, clinicopathological characteristics, and molecular genetics and are characterized by a significantly elevated tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes, high PD-L1 expression, and large numbers of CD8+ intratumoral lymphocytes [7].

However, for pLELC, the clinicopathological features, prognosis, and molecular characteristics remain unknown, necessitating the urgent development of novel therapeutic agents to further increase the survival rates of patients suffering from this fatal disease. In this study, we first reported on the clinical characteristics and prognosis of pLELC patients and explored the real-world efficacy data collected over 10 years in our center. We examined the plasma protein profile and identified the genomic landscape, including single-nucleotide variations (SNVs) and copy number variations (CNVs), of pLELC patients using plasma proteomics and WES. Additionally, we comprehensively compared the genomic profiles of pLELC with those of other relevant cancer types, such as pediatric NPC and adult LELC, to further enhance our understanding of this malignancy. Furthermore, we discovered that alterations in the DNA repair pathway and the SWI/SNF superfamily complex pathway might serve as novel drug targets for pLELC. This study aimed to contribute to the effort to improve outcomes for children with LELC and offer new insights that could lead to more effective treatment regimens and, ultimately, better prognostic outcomes for these patients.

Materials and methods

Patients and ethical concerns

This study evaluated patients with pLELC at Sun Yat-sen University Cancer Center from May 2012 to August 2023. The patients without clinical record data were excluded. This study was approved by the Ethics Review Board of Sun Yat-sen University Cancer Center (B2023-363-01), and all the patients provided informed consent.

All patients underwent pathological diagnosis in accordance with the WHO classification criteria, and all pLELC patients were confirmed by both morphological and immunohistochemical features. The tumors showed characteristic LELC patterns with atypical cells lacking squamous differentiation markers. Immunohistochemical analysis confirmed the epithelial origin and LELC features, with positive staining for CK and p40, and keratin (AE1/AE3). Negative markers included LCA, vimentin, CD30, and S-100. Positive Epstein-Barr encoding region (EBER) detection via situ hybridization confirmed EBV association, strengthening the LELC diagnosis.

Thymic LELC is graded according to the Masaoka staging system. LELC in other parts is classified according to the eighth edition of the International Association TNM staging system. Retrospective analysis of electronic medical records provided clinical data, encompassing demographic information (gender, age), clinical staging, tumor location, and tumor size. Prognostic data for OS, PFS, and objective response rate (ORR) were collected through telephone interviews and comprehensive assessments involving clinical examination and CT scans, adhering to the Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors 1.1.

The OS was determined as the duration from the initial treatment until death due to any cause. Among the entire cohort of 41 pLELC patients, PFS was defined as the time elapsed from the initial treatment to either clinical or radiological progression or death. For patients who had no recorded events or follow-up information, the date of their last contact, which was December 15, 2023, was recorded as the censoring date.

Protein expression levels detection

Baseline plasma samples were collected from 16 patients with pLELC, while plasma from 9 healthy children served as controls. We utilized the Olink Proseek multiplex ultrasensitive platform immuno-oncology panel, which encompasses 96 biomarkers (Olink Target 96 immune oncology panel), the protein expression levels in both the patient and control sera were accurately quantified. Subsequently, the log2-transformed normalized protein expression values were plotted over time.

DNA extraction

Genomic DNA was extracted from 11 surgical resected or needle biopsy samples of tumor tissues obtained from 10 patients (One patient with both primary and metastatic tissue). These samples were then subjected to WES. The integrity of the extracted DNA was verified using agarose gel electrophoresis, while the concentration of DNA was precisely quantified using a Qubit fluorometer. Additionally, the purity of the DNA was assessed by NanoDrop, with the OD260/OD280 ratio serving as an indicator.

WES

Whole-exome capture libraries were constructed by randomly fragmenting DNA into 150–250 bp segments using ultrasound. Fragments were then repaired, A-tailed, and ligated with indexed adapters. Incomplete ligation products were removed using magnetic beads. PCR amplification with adapter-complementary primers formed sequencing libraries. For WES, Agilent SureSelect probes hybridized with the libraries. Hybridized segments were captured and purified using magnetic beads, followed by a second PCR amplification and purification, resulting in the final sequencing libraries. Quality control involved measuring library concentration with Qubit and analyzing fragment size distribution with an Agilent analyzer. Libraries passing quality checks were then sequenced using the NGS platform’s PE150 strategy, which sequences both ends of each fragment for 150 bp.

Somatic mutation detection

For the raw sequences, we performed quality control using the default parameters of FastQC (v0.11.9) software, with Q30 values exceeding 80% for all samples. Subsequently, employing the default parameters of the fastp software (v0.22.0), adapters, low-quality bases, and ambiguous reads were trimmed [8]. Concurrently, reads exhibiting low-quality bases and those with a high proportion of ambiguous bases in the original sequences were discarded, ensuring that the read length met a minimum threshold of 75 bp. Next, we aligned the cleaned sequencing data to the hg19 reference genome using BWA's MEM algorithm [9]. The repeats introduced by PCR amplification were further identified and removed. On the basis of paired alignment files (tumor samples and matched blood samples), variant detection and genotyping of SNVs and InDels were performed with sentieon software [10] with default parameters. The specific rules for mutation screening were as follows: (1) Exclude variations from triallelic site; (2) Variations are deemed insignificant and filtered out if the PV value exceeds 0.25 and the PV2 value also exceeds 0.25, or if INFO/STR equals 1 while the PV value surpasses 0.05; (3) Discard variations where FMT/FOXOG equals 1, indicating a directional bias; (4) Eliminate variations exhibiting an SOR value greater than 3, as this signifies a notable strand bias; (5) Exclude variations with a quality score falling below the established threshold (QUAL = 30); (6) Filter out short tandem repeat variants with fewer than 10 repeats; (7) Remove variations located in noisome regions; (8) Exclude germline variations where the mutation frequency in paired samples divided by the mutation frequency in tumor samples is greater than or equal to 0.2. ANNOVAR was utilized to annotate the identified mutation sites. Variant sites were annotated using ANNOVAR [11]. Then, R package maftools 2.10.0 was applied to visualize the data.

Mutational signature analysis

Mutational signature was identified using the BayesNMF algorithms. Firstly, the NMF is run on a range of values and measures are taken to determine the optimal number of signatures (n = 3). The best possible signature is the value of a significant decrease in symbiosis correlation. Then, we ran the main function and plotted the signatures. To increase the confidence of the findings, the detected signatures were compared with the known mutational processes from the COSMIC database (http://cancer.sanger.ac.uk/cosmic/signatures) and the similarity was shown in heatmap.

Integrated pathway analysis

We screened out the frequently altered genes (somatic mutations and SCNAs) and performed GO and KEGG pathway enrichment using DAVID bioinformatics Resource 6.8.

Comparison analysis

We downloaded the SNV/InDels data of pNPC and aLELC from the published paper [5, 12]. We carried out the statistics and comparison of mutant genes with R package maftools 2.10.0. The tumor mutation burden (TMB) was determined based on the quantification of somatic mutations, coding mutations, base substitution mutations, and indel mutations per megabase of the examined genome. To minimize noise, synonymous mutations within the exon regions were incorporated into the calculation, while mutations occurring in areas of rearrangements, fusions, CNV, and noncoding alterations were excluded [13, 14]. Specifically, the MAF file encompassing all mutation data acquired from WES was inputted into the maftools software, which subsequently calculated the TMB value for each sample utilizing its designated TMB calculation formula (tmb_table_wt_log = tmb(maf = maf))for analysis.

Copy number variant detection

We utilized the CNVkit (0.9.9) software [15] to detect large fragments of CNVs. The analytical process can be succinctly summarized as follows: (1) The read coverage of the sample was calculated for each bin; (2) The read coverage for the sample under investigation was adjusted for GC content and capture edge, yielding the copy number ratio in comparison to the reference sample; (3) The Circular Binary Segmentation (CBS) algorithm was employed to merge adjacent bins into chromosomal segments based on the copy number ratio, thereby extracting the copy number change information for each segment; (4) Generally, regions with absolute log2 copy number ratios at least 0.58 (= log2 (1.5)) were viewed as losses (deletions) or gains (amplifications). The results were subsequently transformed and reanalyzed using GISTIC 2.0 [16], followed by the use of maftools to export the analytical findings.

Evaluation of PD-L1 by IHC

Tumor tissues from 10 patients with pLELCs were analyzed using IHC to detect the expression and clinical significance of PD-L1. IHC was performed using rabbit monoclonal antibodies specific to PD-L1 (clone E1L3N; CST). PD-L1 tumor proportion score (TPS) testing was performed per the institutional standard of care, utilizing an FDA-approved assay.

Multiplex immunofluorescence (mIF) staining

We conducted mIF staining utilizing the seven-color Fluorescence Kit (Panovue, Beijing, China) in accordance with the manufacturer’s protocol [14]. Antibodies used for mIF staining included CD8 (ZA-0508, dilution 1:100; ZSJQB Co., Ltd.) and CD4 (ZA-0519, dilution 1:100; ZSJQB Co., Ltd.). The stained slides were meticulously examined using a Vectra Polaris multispectral imaging platform and subsequently analyzed through the HALO digital pathologic platform.

Statistical analysis

Two-tailed Student’s t-test and Fisher’s exact test were used for continuous and discrete variables, respectively. Pearson’s Chi-squared test was used for comparison of response rate difference. Responses were evaluated according to the Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors, version 1.1. The Kaplan–Meier method was used to plot survival curves, and survival was compared using the log-rank test. The statistical tests were two-tailed, and differences were considered statistically significant at P < 0.05. All statistical analysis was done with standard R packages.

Results

Clinicopathological features

Overall, 41 patients were retrospectively enrolled in this study from May 2012 to August 2023. Among the patients, 39 of 41 tested positive for EBER via immunohistochemistry, with the status of EBER remaining unknown for two patients. The median follow-up period spanned 26 months, ranging from 5 to 138 months. The median age of the patients was 15 years, ranging from 5 to 24 years. The primary disease sites were predominantly located in the thymic/mediastinum (53.7%, 22/41), followed by the parotid or submandibular glands (34.1%, 14/41), and other locations (12.2%, 5/41). Twenty-seven patients presented with tumors measuring 5 cm or larger, 10 patients had tumors smaller than 5 cm, and the tumor size for 4 patients remained unknown. The majority of patients were staged at IV (73.2%, 30/41).

The characteristics of the LELC patients are summarized in Table 1. Thirty-one patients received radiotherapy as their first-line treatment, whereas 10 patients did not. Among the patients who received first-line therapy for their tumors, three individuals underwent surgery and radiation as their initial treatment, excluding chemotherapy. Among the other patients, ten patients received chemotherapy subsequent to radiation and surgery, three patients were treated with a combination of chemotherapy and surgery, eighteen patients underwent chemotherapy concurrently with radiation, and seven patients received chemotherapy alone. When the group receiving chemotherapy combined with surgery was compared with the group receiving chemotherapy combined with surgery and radiation therapy, the hazard ratio (HR) for PFS is 5.088 (95% CI: 0.3289–78.7), indicating a significant difference (P = 0.0393) (Additional file 1, Figure S1A). Similarly, for OS, the HR was 9.135 (95% CI: 1.136–7345.0), P = 0.0019 (Additional file 1, Fig. S1B). Furthermore, when the chemotherapy only group was compared with the group receiving chemotherapy plus radiation therapy, the HR for PFS was 5.980 (95% CI: 1.272–28.12), P = 0.0047 (Additional file 1, Figure S1A). For OS, the HR was 10.19 (95% CI: 0.3283–316.0), P = 0.0039 (Additional file 1, Figure S1B). Protein levels comparison between pLELC and normal samples

Since patients who received PD-1 antibody combined with chemotherapy exhibited relatively better outcomes, we aimed to further explore the immune microenvironment of pLELC. Recent studies have identified proteins as crucial liquid biopsy markers that reflect both the patient's immune system status and tumor activity [17, 18]. Consequently, we employed Olink proteomics technology to define the immune-tumor-related protein profiles. Blood samples were available for 16 patients when they were diagnosed with LELC, with 9 normal pediatric plasma samples serving as controls. Thirty significantly upregulated or downregulated tumor immunity-related plasma proteins were detected when healthy controls (HCs) were compared with children with LELC (Fig. 2A). The differentially expressed proteins detected by proteomic analysis are shown in the volcano maps (Fig. 2B), among these, immune checkpoint molecules, such as LAG-3, PD-L1, galectin-9, CD40 and IL-6, were significantly upregulated, whereas ICOSLG and ARG1 were downregulated. Notably, we detected significantly elevated levels of the CX3CL1 protein, a chemokine that is expressed in both soluble and membrane-bound forms, which has been implicated in mediating inflammation and tumor immune invasion [19, 20]. This elevation was found to be strongly associated with poorer prognosis in terms of PFS and OS (P < 0.05; Additional file 3, Figure S3A-C). Moreover, linear regression analysis revealed a positive correlation between CX3CL1 protein levels and the plasma protein levels of PD-L1(R = 0.67, P = 0.0043), PD-L2 (R = 0.6, P = 0.0013), LAG-3 (R = 0.72, P = 0.0015), and galectin-9 (R = 0.69, P = 0.0034) (Additional file 3, Figure S3D). Gene Ontology (GO) pathway analysis revealed activated pathways related to the cellular response to chemokine and cell chemotaxis and downregulation of the regulation of T-cell proliferation and activation in the LELC group compared with the control group, which validated the significant upregulation of immune checkpoint inhibitory molecules in the plasma of pediatric LELC patients (Fig. 2D). We classified the differentially altered plasma proteins on the basis of their cellular origin and found that T cells and macrophages were the main cell subsets that were primarily affected (Fig. 2E, Additional file 4, Table S1).

PD-L1 expression level and immune infiltration in pLELC

To further explore the immune microenvironment of pLELC, immunohistochemistry of PD-L1 and mIF staining of CD4 and CD8 were performed on 12 tissue samples from 10 patients, among which two patients had both primary tissue and metastatic tissue. All the tissue samples had a PD-L1 TPS > 1%. Among these, 11 (91.7%) samples had PD- L1 TPS > 20% and 2 (16.7%) samples had PD-L1 TPS > 50%. For patient #13, who presented the highest PD-L1 TPS of 53.53%, the tissue sample was obtained from a liver metastasis. The PD-L1 TPS for the primary site in patient #5 and #14 were 42.3% and 46.0%, respectively, while the percentages of staining in the metastatic tissue were 31.2% and 44.4%, respectively (Additional file 5, Figure S4).

To compare the abundance of immune infiltration in different tissues, we quantitatively assessed the distribution of immune cells according to criteria defined in previous studies [21], and we calculated the number of T cells per mm2 in any three regions of the tumor. The average densities of CD4-positive and CD8-positive T cells in these tissues were 166 /mm2 and 26 /mm2, respectively, ranging from 20 to 591 and 18 ~ 902. Patients #2 and #12 presented the highest infiltration of CD4+/CD8+ cells. Specifically, patient #5 had CD4+ T cell densities of 207 /mm2 and CD8+ T cell densities of 902/mm2 at the primary site, and 384 /mm2 and 295 /mm2 at the metastasis site, whereas patient #14 had densities of 40 /mm2 and 72 /mm2 at the primary tissue, and 167 /mm2 and 255 /mm2 at the metastasis site respectively, indicating disparities in the tumor immune microenvironment between the primary and metastasis sites (Additional file 6, Figure S5). Furthermore, a positive correlation was observed between the infiltration of CD4+ and CD8+ T cells (r = 0.64, P = 0.0244). Within the tumor microenvironment, a greater proportion of tumor cells expressed PD-L1 expression, and an increased infiltration of CD4+ and CD8+ T cells was observed in the tumor microenvironment, indicating potential applications for anti-PD-1 or anti-PD-L1 therapy.

Integrated genomic analysis of pLELC

To discover the molecular characteristics of pLELC, WES analysis was performed on the genomic DNA of 11 pLELC tumors and paired blood controls at a mean coverage of 200 fold, respectively. Overall, SNV/Indel mutations from 3153 genes were detected, with an average mutation of 364 mutations per person (ranging from 6 to 1105). A total of 4004 somatic mutations were identified, including 3631 non-silent, 205 silent and 168 short insertions and deletions. Specifically, these mutations included 214 nonsense mutations, 40 frameshift deletions, 22 frameshift insertions, 78 inframe deletions, 28 inframe insertions, 3417 missense mutations, 201 splicing mutations and 3 nonstop and 1 translation start site mutation. The predominant somatic mutation types were C:G > T:A transitions, followed by C:G > G:C and C:G > A:T changes. We subsequently analyzed mutational signatures in pLELC based on 30 curated COSMIC mutational signatures defined in the COSMIC database [22, 23], and a total of three independent mutation signatures were screened out in pLELC, which were similar to COSMIC signatures 1, 6 and 13 (Fig. 3B). Signature 1 was associated with apolipoprotein B messenger RNA editing catalytic polypeptide-like (APOBEC) cytidine deaminases activity in tumor evolution [24] and signature 2 was associated with an endogenous mutational process initiated by spontaneous deamination of 5-methylcytosine, which has been reported in other cancer types [25]. In addition, mutational signature analysis revealed enrichment of defective DNA mismatch repair (MMR), which is related to neoantigens in cancer and is thought to present exceptional responsiveness to immune checkpoint blockade (ICB) [26, 27]. GO enrichment analysis also suggested that these mutated genes were associated mainly with the regulation of DNA repair (Additional file 8, Figure S6; Additional file 9, Table S3). The most highly represented genes were TTN, which was mutated in six different patients, and NEB, MUC16 and TRRAP which, were each altered in five different patients. TRRAP and ANKRD36C mutations were detected in patients younger than 15 years old, and TTN, MUC16 and NEB mutations were detected in primary tumors (Fig. 3C).

We observed that the mutational landscape of pLELC presented a moderate similarity with that of pNPC. TTN was the most frequently mutated gene in these three types of tumors, with a slightly greater frequency than TP53. Pathway analysis of mutational profiles

Furthermore, integrated analysis of mutational profiles revealed core signalling pathways, including those involved in DNA damage or replication arrest, and pathways associated with the SWI/SNF superfamily type complex. The regulation of the DNA repair pathway was altered primarily by deletion of ATM, followed by mutation or deletion of ATR, and deletion of TRRAP. The SWI/SNF complex pathway was frequently dysregulated, mainly due to mutation or deletion of SMARCA4 (Fig. 5A). We also assessed CNVs with GISTIC software, and after filtering with q values < 0.05, 59 significant focal copy number alterations (9 amplifications and 50 deletions) were identified with a total of 8–18,125 genes altered (Additional file 10, Table S4; Additional file 11, Table S5). CNV loss was predominant in pLELCs and the significantly deleted regions included 11p15.5, 14q32.33 and 2p11.1, which were observed in 9 (82%) of the 11 patients. Notably, genes associated with DNA repair including ATM, ATR, ATRIP, and TP53I3, were identified in the corresponding deleted regions (Fig. 5B). Furthermore, the prognostic value of SMARCA4 and ATR in pLELC patients was explored. The results revealed that the patients with mutant SMARCA4 or ATR had a shorter PFS than those with a wild type status (Fig. 5C, D). There was no significant difference in OS between the two groups, which was probably due to the small sample size (Additional file 12, Figure S7). Application of ICIs and PARP inhibitors in pediatric LELC patients

Patient #11 was a 15-year-old girl who presented with a mass on the right clavicle. A CT scan at the local hospital revealed multiple masses in the right cervical IV and V regions, bilateral clavicular regions, and the mediastinum, with the largest mass measuring approximately 9.0 × 6.7 cm. Lymph node aspiration and pathological examination confirmed the diagnosis of lymphoepithelioma-like carcinoma. PET/CT imaging revealed active metabolism in multiple enlarged lymph nodes in the right cervical IV and V regions, bilateral clavicular regions, mediastinum, and cardiophrenic angle, with invasion of the right upper and middle lung lobes and adjacent pericardium. The patient subsequently underwent six cycles of chemotherapy with the TPF regimen, followed by mediastinal tumor resection after four cycles. Postsurgical adjuvant radiotherapy was administered with a PGTV of 60.2 Gy/28F and a PTV of 50.4 Gy/28F. Four months after treatment cessation, a follow-up examination revealed enlarged lymph nodes posterior to the cardiophrenic angle and posterior to the inferior vena cava, suggesting tumor recurrence and metastasis. The patient subsequently received six cycles of combination therapy with a PD-1 inhibitor and the GP regimen. A complete response was achieved (Fig. 6A, B). Another patient presented with chest and back pain and was diagnosed with LELC by pathological biopsy of the mass adjacent to the anterior mediastinum. PET‒CT scans revealed a high metabolic mass in the anterior mediastinum. The patient was diagnosed with stage IVb disease of the anterior mediastinum, accompanied by multiple metastases in the lymph nodes, liver, spleen, and bones. The sequence of the tumor revealed ATM gene mutation, TP53 gene mutation, CHD2 mutation, and a TMB of 28.1 mut/Mb. Following five cycles of treatment with the GP plus PD-1 regimen, the tumor showed a partial response. Subsequently, a resection surgery was attempted on the anterior mediastinum, but owing to the proximity of the tumor to large blood vessels, only partial removal was feasible, leaving residual tumor tissue. Postoperatively, the patient underwent radiotherapy, during which the PARP inhibitor olaparib was administered. Upon re-evaluation, the tumor has now achieved durable remission (Additional file 13, Figure S8).

Discussion

LELC is a rare malignancy, and its pathogenesis in children and adolescents remains unclear. As evidenced by Wu et al. [29], a causal association has been reported between LELC and the EBV. Notably, the presence of EBV DNA and nuclear RNA has been observed in biopsy samples of LELC [4, 30]. Moreover, the efficacy of PD-1 antibodies in treating pLELC has not been definitively established. To investigate the clinical and molecular characteristics of this type of tumor, we firstly conducted a study focusing on pediatric and adolescent patients with lymphoepithelioma-like carcinoma over the past 10 years.

Consistent with previous study, we established the pivotal role of radiotherapy in the management of LELC and found that radiotherapy improves local control of the disease [31]. Additionally, we observed a correlation between the size of the primary tumor and its prognostic implications. The anatomical locations of LELC diverge between adults and children, exhibiting a wide range of sites in adults. Specifically, in adults, LELC can occur in multiple sites, including the lungs [32], hepatobiliary system [33–35], urinary tract [36, 37], and gastrointestinal tract [38, 39]. Additionally, there have been reports of LELC occurring in the parotid and salivary glands in adults [40, 41]. However, in children LELC primarily occurs in the thymus, with occasional cases in the lungs [2]. Our study has identified that LELC in children predominantly affects the thymus and parotid glands, with only one case involving the lungs.

The immunosuppression-related proteins in the plasma are significantly upregulated in pLELC compared to healthy individuals. Pathway enrichment analysis reveals that the activation and proliferation of T cells are downregulated, suggesting that these patients have a compromised immune function and may potentially benefit from immunosuppressants such as PD-1 and LAG-3 monoclonal antibodies. Additionally, our study highlights the significance of the downregulation of ICOSLG, which serves as a ligand for ICOS and functions as a costimulatory signal for T-cell proliferation and cytokine secretion. Therefore, its decreased expression also indicates a downregulation of immune function. Several studies have reported the role of ICOS/ICOSL in regulating immune function and indicate that the expression of ICOSL in plasma may serve as a prognostic marker for LELC, but further large-scale studies are needed to confirm this [42, 43].

We also observed a statistically significant elevation in the TMB levels among pLELC patients, compared to both adult LELC and pediatric NPC patients. Moreover, all the patients evaluated exhibited PD-L1 expression and immune cells infiltration indicate a potential therapeutic benefit from PD-1 antibodies in pediatric LELC. It indicates the response of immune checkpoint inhibitor in pLELC. Interestingly, through an integrated analysis of mutational profiles, we discovered that the DNA repair pathway and the SWI/SNF complexes pathway are two of the pivotal signaling pathways, which have indicated potential target therapy. Prior studies have shown that ATR and ATM, being key proteins for DNA damage repair, play crucial roles in regulating cancer growth and progression [44]. Furthermore, mutations in subunits like SMARCA4, SMARCB1, or ARID1A in SWI/SNF complexes may benefit from EZH2 inhibitors [45, 46].

Given the close relationship between EBV and lymphoepitheliomatoid carcinoma in children, the underlying mechanism of tumorigenesis is of special interest. Among the genes most frequently mutated in the WES analysis of pLELC tumors, TTN, MUC16, NEB, and TRRAP are particularly noteworthy. Notably, MUC16 has been detected in EBV-associated lymphoepithelioma-like cholangiocarcinoma (EBV-LELCC) [47]. The somatic mutation of TTN, which was altered in 55% of all samples, has been found to be associated with high immunogenicity and an inflammatory tumor microenvironment (TIME), suggesting that its mutation may serve as a potential predictive marker for patients eligible for immune checkpoint inhibitors [48, 49]. However, further investigation of these mutated genes is needed to pave the way for the development of targeted therapies for pLELC patients.

One significant limitation of our current study is that the methodologies employed did not encompass additional genomic data, such as transcriptome profiling or single-cell sequencing. Additionally, we performed DNA extraction and exome sequencing based on paraffin-embedded tissue sections containing tumor tissue. However, the influence of the infiltrating immune cells among them cannot be excluded. Therefore, to eliminate this potential bias, it may be necessary to study tumor cells and immune cells separately by performing single-cell sequencing in the future. Furthermore, as our focus was primarily on the clinical aspects and somatic mutation patterns of this unique tumor, we did not explore the precise viral integration sites or the intricate mechanisms underlying EBV-induced carcinogenesis. However, our research has still yielded valuable insights that can guide further exploration in this field.

Conclusion

In summary, our study has provided a comprehensive overview of the clinical and molecular characteristics of pediatric LELC. It highlights potential tumorigenic mechanisms and establishes pediatric LELC as a distinct cancer that differs from pediatric NPC and adult LELC. Furthermore, it indicates a promising response rate associated with the combination of PD-1 antibody treatment and chemotherapy in pediatric patients with LELC and offers a theoretical basis for targeting DNA repair pathways.
