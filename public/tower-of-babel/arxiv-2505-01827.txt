Priorconditioned Sparsity-Promoting Projection Methods for Deterministic and Bayesian Linear Inverse Problems Thanks: \monthyeardate August 24, 2026 \corresponding Jonathan Lindbloom
Jonathan Lindbloom, Mirjeta Pasha, Jan Glaubitz, Youssef Marzouk

Abstract
High-quality reconstructions of signals and images with sharp edges are needed in a wide range of applications. To overcome the large dimensionality of the parameter space and the complexity of the regularization functional, sparisty-promoting techniques for both deterministic and hierarchical Bayesian regularization rely on solving a sequence of high-dimensional iteratively reweighted least squares (IRLS) problems on a lower-dimensional subspace. Generalized Krylov subspace (GKS) methods are a particularly potent class of hybrid Krylov schemes that efficiently solve sequences of IRLS problems by projecting large-scale problems into a relatively small subspace and successively enlarging it. We refer to methods that promote sparsity and use GKS as S-GKS. A disadvantage of S-GKS methods is their slow convergence. In this work, we propose techniques that improve the convergence of S-GKS methods by combining them with priorconditioning, which we refer to as PS-GKS. Specifically, integrating the PS-GKS method into the IAS algorithm allows us to automatically select the shape/rate parameter of the involved generalized gamma hyper-prior, which is often fine-tuned otherwise. Furthermore, we proposed and investigated variations of the proposed PS-GKS method, including restarting and recycling (resPS-GKS and recPS-GKS). These respectively leverage restarted and recycled subspaces to overcome situations when memory limitations of storing the basis vectors are a concern. We provide a thorough theoretical analysis showing the benefits of priorconditioning for sparsity-promoting inverse problems. Numerical experiment are used to illustrate that the proposed PS-GKS method and its variants are competitive with or outperform other existing hybrid Krylov methods.

Introduction

Recovering high-quality signals and images from indirect, incomplete, and noisy observations is a common yet challenging problem in various applications. The task is often modeled as a linear inverse problem

{\bf b}={\bf A}{\bf x}+{\bf e},

(1)

where
{\bf b}\in\mathbb{R}^{M}
denotes the observed data,
{\bf x}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
is the unknown parameter vector (e.g., the signal or the vectorized image),
{\bf A}\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times N}
is a known linear forward operator, and
{\bf e}\in\mathbb{R}^{M}
corresponds to noise.

The inverse problem
eq.

1
is typically
ill-posed
, resulting in the solution of
eq.

1
being non-unique, not existing at all, or being highly sensitive. One way to overcome ill-posedness is through
regularization
, wherein one instead seeks the solution to a nearby regularized inverse problem:

\argmin_{{\bf x}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}}\left\{\mathcal{F}({\bf x}{;{\bf b}})+\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{x})\right\},

(2)

where
\mathcal{F}
and
\mathcal{R}
denote the data-fidelity and regularization term, respectively.
The choice of
\mathcal{F}
is informed by the assumptions about the data-generating process and noise characteristics.
For simplicity, we assume that
{\bf e}
is a realization of standard normal noise, i.e.,
{\bf e}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I})
, yielding
\mathcal{F}({\bf x}{;{\bf b}})=\|{\bf A}{\bf x}-{\bf b}\|_{2}^{2}
.

\mathbf{A}\leftarrow\mathbf{C}^{-1}\mathbf{A}

\mathbf{b}\leftarrow\mathbf{C}^{-1}\mathbf{b}

The regularization term

{\bf x}

A common assumption is that
{\bf x}
is sparse or has a sparse representation
\boldsymbol{\Psi}{\bf x}

\boldsymbol{\Psi}\in\mathbb{R}^{K\times N}

For instance,
\boldsymbol{\Psi}

Sparsity for
\boldsymbol{\Psi}{\bf x}

\mathcal{R}
as some scaled surrogate for the
\ell_{0}
-“norm”
\|\boldsymbol{\Psi}{\bf x}\|_{0}

An alternative to the above deterministic regularization setting is the

to inverse problems
[

,

where we treat the unknown parameters as random variables and impose a prior distribution on them.
For instance, assuming additive standard normal noise

1

\pi(\mathbf{b}|{\bf x})\ \propto\ \exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\|{\bf A}{\bf x}-\mathbf{b}\|^{2}_{2}\right)

Assuming further a prior distribution
\pi^{0}({\bf x})
for the random variable of interest
{\bf x}
, Bayes’ rule prescribes a formula for the posterior density
\pi_{\rm pos}({\bf x}|{\bf b})\ \propto\ \pi({\bf b}|{\bf x})\pi^{0}({\bf x})

Furthermore, the role of the regularization term is now taken by a prior distribution
\pi^{0}({\bf x})

{\bf x}
—in this case, that it has a sparse representation.
Finally, the sought-after posterior density
\pi_{\rm pos}({\bf x}|{\bf b})
is provided by Bayes’ rule as
\pi_{\rm pos}({\bf x}|{\bf b})\ \propto\ \pi({\bf b}|{\bf x})\pi^{0}({\bf x})

A particularly potent class of sparsity-promoting priors is the generalized sparse Bayesian learning (GSBL) priors

\pi^{0}(\boldsymbol{\theta})

Here,
\boldsymbol{\theta}=[\theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{K}]^{T}
is a vector of auxiliary hyper-parameters that encode the sparsity profile of
\boldsymbol{\Psi}{\bf x}

Our contribution

We propose a novel priorconditioning strategy that can be used in sparsity-promoting techniques in both deterministic and Bayesian settings for ill-posed linear inverse problems. For each case, we present the weights and describe how priorconditioning can be used in the context of reweighting. Furthermore, we propose variations of our method that employ restarting and recycling to overcome memory limitations. Specifically, this paper’s main contributions can be summarized as follows:

1.

Driven by the need for computationally feasible methods for large-scale linear inverse problems, we propose a priorconditioning strategy (referred to as “PS-GKS”) that is based on GKS and can be used to efficiently solve IRLS problems arising from the deterministic or Bayesian setting.
This allows us to efficiently and automatically select the model parameters (see below for details).

2.

To overcome the computational bottleneck of computing the pseudoinverse needed for the priorconditioning, we propose a prior conditioned CG method that is then further accelerated by GPU, making it applicable for large-scale inverse problems.

3.

A comprehensive comparison to other sparsity-promoting methods that utilize Krylov subspaces is provided.
In particular, we compare our PS-GKS method with competing methods based on the Golub-Kahan bidiagonalization and the flexible Golub-Kahan process.
Numerical experiments in 1D and 2D (X-ray computerized tomography (CT) applications) illustrate our proposed method’s performance.

We observe that our PS-GKS method significantly improves the existing S-GKS method by substantially reducing the number of iterations required for convergence.
Such improvement is observed throughout several reweighting strategies used in both the deterministic and Bayesian formulation.

Application to deterministic and Bayesian inverse problems

We outline two motivating examples of sparsity-promoting methods for linear inverse problems that rely on efficiently solving a sequence of IRLS problems: One deterministic method arising from the MM approach to
\ell^{p}
-regularization and one Bayesian method arising from MAP estimation with sparsity-promoting GSBL priors.

2.1
Deterministic regularization and the MM approach

A common technique to promote sparsity in
\boldsymbol{\Psi}\mathbf{x}
is to seek the solution to the deterministic problem

\argmin_{{\bf x}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}}\left\{\mathcal{J}({\bf x})\right\},\quad\mathcal{J}({\bf x})=\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{b}\right\rVert}_{2}^{2}+\frac{\mu}{p}\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\Psi}\mathbf{x}\right\rVert}_{p}^{p},

(4)

with
0<p\leq 1
.
Notably, the objective
\mathcal{J}
is generally neither smooth nor convex.
The popular MM approach
[
36
,
35
]
addresses this challenging structure of
\mathcal{J}
by successively minimizing a sequence of smooth approximations— so-called quadratic tangent majorants—of the original functional, resulting in an IRLS problem of the form
eq.

3
.
Specifically, a smooth approximation of the
p
-norm
in
eq.

4
is given by
\|{\bf z}\|_{p}^{p}\approx\sum_{k}\phi_{p,\varepsilon}({\bf z}_{k})
with
\phi_{p,\varepsilon}({\bf z}_{i})=({\bf z}_{k}^{2}+\varepsilon^{2})^{p/2}
being an approximation of
|{\bf z}_{k}|^{p}
and
\varepsilon>0
.
The first step to building a quadratic tangent majorant consists of constructing the smoothed functional

\mathcal{J}_{\varepsilon}({\bf x})=\|{\bf A}{\bf x}-{\bf b}\|_{2}^{2}+(\mu/p)\sum_{k}\phi_{p,\varepsilon}([\boldsymbol{\Psi}{\bf x}]_{k}).

Let
{\bf x}_{\ell}
be an available approximation of the desired solution. Assuming an adaptive quadratic majorant, we select the weighting matrix as

{\bf W}_{\ell+1}=\text{diag}\left({(\boldsymbol{\Psi}{\bf x}_{\ell})^{2}+\varepsilon^{2}}\right)^{\frac{p-2}{4}},

(5)

which yields the following quadratic tangent majorant for
\mathcal{J}_{\varepsilon}({\bf x})
:

\mathcal{M}({\bf x},{\bf x}_{\ell})=\displaystyle{\frac{1}{2}}\|{\bf A}{\bf x}-{\bf b}\|^{2}_{2}+\displaystyle{\frac{\mu}{2}}\|{\bf W}_{\ell+1}\boldsymbol{\Psi}{\bf x}\|^{2}_{2}+c,

(6)

where
c
is a constant that is independent of
{\bf x}
and
{\bf x}_{\ell}
.

The new approximation of the solution,
\mathbf{x}_{\ell+1}
, is then obtained by minimizing
eq.

6
by a standard method such as CGLS. The process of defining and minimizing a new quadratic tangent majorant is repeated, resulting in a sequence of IRLS problems as in
eq.

3
where the weights are given as in
eq.

5
.

2.2
Bayesian inverse problems: GSBL and the IAS approach

We next demonstrate how IRLS naturally arise in sparsity-promoting Bayesian approaches to linear inverse problems using hierarchical priors.
For simplicity, we focus on efficient MAP estimation within the GSBL approach
[
27
,
68
,
26
]
using the popular IAS algorithm
[
10
,
12
,
8
,
43
]
.
Notably, combining the IAS algorithm with the proposed PS-GKS method later allows us to automate the selection of the rate parameter
\vartheta
(which serves as a regularization parameter) of the generalized gamma hyper-prior, reducing the need for manual fine-tuning.

In the Bayesian approach
[
61
,
11
]
, the inverse problem
eq.

1
is framed as a statistical inference problem based on the posterior distribution, which combines the likelihood function
f(\mathbf{x};\mathbf{b})
implied by
eq.

1
with a prior density
\pi^{0}
that encodes our structural beliefs about
\mathbf{x}
.
Consider the data model
eq.

1
with whitened noise
\mathbf{e}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I})
.
In this case, the likelihood is
f(\mathbf{x};\mathbf{b})\propto\exp(-\frac{1}{2}\mathinner{\!\left\lVert{\bf A}\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{b}\right\rVert}_{2}^{2})
.
To formulate the prior density
\pi^{0}
, we again assume that
\boldsymbol{\Psi}\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{K}
is sparse.
A particularly potent class of sparsity-promoting priors that we consider in this work are the GSBL priors
\pi^{0}(\mathbf{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})=\pi^{0}(\mathbf{x}|\boldsymbol{\theta})\pi^{0}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
, combining a conditional Gaussian prior
\pi^{0}(\mathbf{x}|\boldsymbol{\theta})
and a generalized gamma hyper-prior
\pi^{0}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
, where
\boldsymbol{\theta}=[\theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{K}]
is a vector of auxiliary hyper-parameters.
Specifically, we assume that the
k
th component of
\boldsymbol{\Psi}\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{K}
is independently normal-distributed with mean zero and variance
\theta_{k}
, i.e.,
[\boldsymbol{\Psi}\mathbf{x}]_{k}|\theta_{k}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\theta_{k})
.
The variance
\theta_{k}
is also modeled as a random variable, which is generalized gamma-distributed, i.e.,
\theta_{k}\sim\mathcal{GG}(r,\beta,\vartheta)
with parameters
r\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
,
\beta>0
, and
\vartheta>0
.
The resulting GSBL posterior density
\pi^{\mathbf{b}}
for
(\mathbf{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})
conditioned on
\mathbf{b}
follows from Bayes’ theorem and is given by

\displaystyle\pi^{\mathbf{b}}(\mathbf{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})\propto\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\mathinner{\!\left\lVert{\bf A}\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{b}\right\rVert}_{2}^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\mathbf{D}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{-1/2}\boldsymbol{\Psi}\mathbf{x}\right\rVert}_{2}^{2}-\sum_{k=1}^{K}\left[\left(\frac{\theta_{k}}{\vartheta}\right)^{r}-\left(r\beta-\frac{3}{2}\right)\log\theta_{k}\right]\right)

(7)

with diagonal matrix
\mathbf{D}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}={\rm diag\!}\left(\boldsymbol{\theta}\right)
.

The
MAP estimate

(\mathbf{x}^{\mathrm{MAP}},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\mathrm{MAP}})
of
\pi^{\mathbf{b}}(\mathbf{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})
is the maximizer of the joint posterior in
eq.

7
.
Equivalently, the MAP estimate is the minimizer of the negative logarithm of the joint posterior, i.e.,
(\mathbf{x}^{\mathrm{MAP}},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\mathrm{MAP}})=\argmin_{\mathbf{x},\boldsymbol{\theta}}\left\{\mathcal{J}(\mathbf{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})\right\}
with
\mathcal{J}=-\log\pi^{\mathbf{b}}(\mathbf{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})
.
A prevalent strategy to approximate the minimizer of
\mathcal{J}
is to use block-coordinate descent methods
[
67
,
3
]
that aim to minimize
\mathcal{J}
by alternatingly minimizing
\mathbf{x}
and
\boldsymbol{\theta}
.
For MAP estimation of the GSBL posterior, the same strategy is leveraged by IAS.
We refer to
[
7
,
43
]
for details on the
\boldsymbol{\theta}
-update, which can be efficiently performed by finding the root of a simple quadratic function if
r=\pm 1
and by solving an ordinary differential equation in all other cases.
Furthermore, the
\mathbf{x}
-update reduces to solving a quadratic optimization problem

\mathbf{x}_{\ell+1}=\argmin_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}}\left\{\mathinner{\!\left\lVert{\bf A}\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{b}\right\rVert}_{2}^{2}+\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\mathbf{D}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\ell+1}}^{-1/2}\boldsymbol{\Psi}\mathbf{x}\right\rVert}_{2}^{2}\right\}.

(8)

To place the IAS algorithm into the IRLS form of
eq.

3
, we perform the change of variables
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\ell+1}=\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\ell+1}/\vartheta
, transforming
eq.

8
into

\mathbf{x}_{\ell+1}=\argmin_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}}\left\{\mathinner{\!\left\lVert{\bf A}\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{b}\right\rVert}_{2}^{2}+\vartheta^{-1}\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\mathbf{D}_{\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\ell+1}}^{-1/2}\boldsymbol{\Psi}\mathbf{x}\right\rVert}_{2}^{2}\right\}.

(9)

Notably,
eq.

9
will allow us to introduce the proposed PS-GKS method into the IAS algorithm, resulting in the automated selection of the rate parameter
\vartheta
of the generalized gamma hyper-prior, removing the need for manually fine-tuning it.

Background

As demonstrated above, IRLS problems arise naturally in various approaches to linear inverse problems.
We now review hybrid projection methods, specifically the GKS approach, for efficiently solving IRLS problems of the form
eq.

3
.
Furthermore, we demonstrate the limitations of existing GKS methods, which subsequently motivate the development of priorconditioning strategies in the context of hybrid methods.

3.1
Projected IRLS and GKS

For large-scale problems with thousands or millions of unknowns, the computational bottleneck of the IRLS problem
eq.

3
is that we have to repeatedly solve high-dimensional least squares problems.
This becomes extremely costly as iterative methods may require a large number of matrix-vector products (matvecs) with
\mathbf{A}
and
\boldsymbol{\Psi}
to produce a solution of sufficient quality
[
55
]
.
Moreover, traditional techniques
[
64
,
30
]
for selecting an appropriate regularization parameter
\mu
in
eq.

4
rely on solving
eq.

4
for a potentially large number of different
\mu
-values, further increasing computational costs.
To alleviate the computational burdens of solving IRLS problems, various hybrid projection methods have been introduced
[
15
]
, which replace
eq.

3
with a sequence of projected IRLS problems:

{\bf x}_{\ell+1}=\argmin_{{\bf x}\in\mathcal{V}_{\ell}}\left\{\mathinner{\!\left\lVert{\bf A}{\bf x}-{\bf b}\right\rVert}_{2}^{2}+\mu_{\ell+1}\mathinner{\!\left\lVert{\bf W}_{\ell+1}\boldsymbol{\Psi}{\bf x}\right\rVert}_{2}^{2}\right\},\quad\ell=0,1,2,\ldots,

(10)

where
\{\mathcal{V}_{\ell}\}_{\ell\geq 0}
is a nested sequence of low-dimensional approximation spaces.
We denote their dimensions by
D_{\ell}=\operatorname{dim}(\mathcal{V}_{\ell})
, also called
basis sizes
.
The heuristic behind this approach is that solving each projected,
D_{\ell}
-dimensional problem—including regularization parameter selection—is significantly cheaper compared to the original problem
eq.

3
.

Here, we focus on projection methods based on generalized Krylov subspaces (GKS) for
\{\mathcal{V}_{\ell}\}_{\ell\geq 0}
.
The GKS approach was introduced in
[
39
]
to solve
eq.

4
with
p=2
.
Subsequently,
[
41
,
35
]
combined the GKS method with a projected IRLS scheme of the form
eq.

10
to solve
eq.

4
for any
0<p<2
, called the
MM-GKS
approach.

For generality, we consider MM-GKS as a subset of the broader class of
sparsity-promoting GKS (S-GKS) methods
for solving
eq.

10
.
These methods employ GKS for the subspaces and allow for any sparsity-promoting weights, including those derived from the GSBL approach in
Section

2.2
.
Specifically, the S-GKS method begins by selecting an initial
\mathcal{V}_{0}
and
\mathbf{x}_{0}
.
A typical choice for
\mathcal{V}_{0}
is the standard Krylov subspace
\mathcal{V}_{0}=\mathcal{K}_{h}(\mathbf{A}^{T}\mathbf{A},\mathbf{A}^{T}\mathbf{b})={\rm span}\{({\bf A}^{T}{\bf A})^{0}{\bf A}^{T}{\bf b},\ldots,({\bf A}^{T}{\bf A})^{h-1}{\bf A}^{T}{\bf b}\}
with a relatively small
h
(for instance,
h=5
see
[
55
,
53
]
). At the
(\ell+1)
th iteration, one then computes
\mathbf{W}_{\ell+1}
,
\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{\ell+1}=\mathbf{W}_{\ell+1}\boldsymbol{\Psi}
, and the economic QR factorizations
\mathbf{A}\mathbf{V}_{\ell}=\mathbf{Q}_{\mathbf{A}}\mathbf{R}_{\boldsymbol{\Psi}},\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{\ell+1}\mathbf{V}_{\ell}=\mathbf{Q}_{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}\mathbf{R}_{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}
. Here,
\mathbf{V}_{l}
is a matrix whose columns form an orthonormal basis for
\mathcal{V}_{l}
.
It can be computed using, for instance, the Golub–Kahan bidiagonalization
[
28
, §10.4]
.
Using
\mathcal{V}_{l}
allows to re-write the constraint problem
eq.

10
as the unconstrained problem

\displaystyle\min_{\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{D_{\ell}}}\left\{\|\mathbf{A}\mathbf{V}_{\ell}\mathbf{z}-\mathbf{b}\|_{2}^{2}+\mu_{\ell+1}\|\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{\ell+1}\mathbf{V}_{\ell}\mathbf{z}\|_{2}^{2}\right\}.

(11)

One then substitutes the QR decompositions into
eq.

11
to obtain

\min_{\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{D_{\ell}}}\left\{\|\mathbf{R}_{\mathbf{A}}\mathbf{z}-\mathbf{Q}_{\mathbf{A}}^{T}\mathbf{b}\|_{2}^{2}+\mu_{\ell+1}\|\mathbf{R}_{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}\mathbf{z}\|_{2}^{2}\right\}.

(12)

A regularization parameter selection method is then applied to choose
\mu_{\ell+1}
in
eq.

12
, which we comment on in
Section

4.4
.
Next,
eq.

12
is solved and the solution
\mathbf{z}_{\ell+1}
is mapped back to the original
N
-dimensional space via
\mathbf{x}_{\ell+1}=\mathbf{V}_{\ell}\mathbf{z}_{\ell+1}
. If convergence has not yet been confirmed, the residual vector
\mathbf{r}_{\ell+1}=\mathbf{A}^{T}(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{V}_{\ell}\mathbf{z}_{\ell+1}-\mathbf{b})+\mu_{\ell+1}\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{\ell+1}^{T}(\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{\ell+1}\mathbf{V}_{\ell}\mathbf{z}_{\ell+1})
is incorporated into the subspace of the next iteration.
This is done by by computing
{\bf v}_{\rm new}=(\mathbf{I}_{N}-\mathbf{V}_{\ell}\mathbf{V}_{\ell}^{T})\mathbf{r}_{\ell+1}/\|(\mathbf{I}_{N}-\mathbf{V}_{\ell}\mathbf{V}_{\ell}^{T})\mathbf{r}_{\ell+1}\|_{2}
, setting
\mathbf{V}_{\ell+1}=[\mathbf{V}_{\ell},\mathbf{v}_{\text{new}}]
, and choosing
\mathcal{V}_{\ell+1}=\operatorname{col}(\mathbf{V}_{\ell+1})
.
The above S-GKS iteration is repeated until a pre-specified convergence criterion is satisfied.
We summarize the S-GKS method in
algorithm

2
.

Remark 2
(Restarting and recycling).

Several computational experiments
[
55
,
53
,
43
]
have demonstrated that many iterations may be necessary for convergence when S-GKS is applied to large-scale problems.
In this case, the computational costs of using a high-dimensional subspace quickly become prohibitive.
Furthermore, the associated storage requirement can easily exceed the memory capacity.
Recently, restarted and recycled variants of S-GKS
[
5
,
53
]
have been proposed: once the basis size reaches a dimension limit
D_{\text{max}}
, the basis is compressed into a smaller subspace of dimension
D_{\text{min}}
.
The resulting “restarted” S-GKS (resS-GKS) and “recycled” S-GKS (recS-GKS) method have a
\mathcal{O}(D_{\ell}N)
memory requirement and
\mathcal{O}(D_{\ell}^{2}M)
computational cost per iteration with
D_{\ell}=D_{\text{min}}+\ell\bmod(D_{\text{max}}+1)
, which can be significantly cheaper than S-GKS for a large iteration index
\ell
.

4
Proposed method

We observed above that neither S-GKS nor its restarted or recycled variants yielded satisfactory results for reconstructing a piecewise constant signal.
To overcome this limitation, we introduce a new projection method that constructs prior conditioned generalized Krylov subspaces within a transformed space defined by the sparsifying transformation
\boldsymbol{\Psi}
.
The resulting method, which we call
priorconditioned S-GKS (PS-GKS)
, incurs a higher computational cost per iteration than S-GKS due to operations involving the precondition.
However, this additional expense is offset by the method’s ability to produce sparser and more accurate solutions within an approximation subspace of modest dimension.

4.1
Priorconditioning for the full-scale problem

First, we review the priorconditioning technique applied to a single full-scale least squares problem in
eq.

3
.
The crux of the technique is to seek a transformation under which the solution can be expressed in terms of a Tikhonov problem with regularization transformation equal to the identity.
Such a transformation performs a whitening by the prior and hence is referred to as
priorconditioning
.

It is well known that a transformation satisfying this property is provided by the standard form transformation for least squares problems
[
19
,
31
]
:
Let
\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{\ell+1}=\mathbf{W}_{\ell+1}\boldsymbol{\Psi}
and
\mathbf{K}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times P}
be a full-rank matrix whose columns form an orthonormal basis for
\operatorname{ker}(\boldsymbol{\Psi})
.
Then, the standard form transformation applied to
eq.

3
yields

\displaystyle\argmin_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}}\left\{\|\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{b}\|_{2}^{2}+\mu\|\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{\ell+1}\mathbf{x}\|_{2}^{2}\right\}=(\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{\ell+1})_{\mathbf{A}}^{\dagger}\left(\argmin_{\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{K}}\left\{\|\overline{\mathbf{A}}_{\ell+1}\mathbf{z}-\overline{\mathbf{b}}\|_{2}^{2}+\mu\|\mathbf{z}\|_{2}^{2}\right\}\right)+\mathbf{x}_{\text{ker}},

(13)

where
\mathbf{x}_{\text{ker}}=\mathbf{K}(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{K})^{\dagger}\mathbf{b}\in\operatorname{ker}(\boldsymbol{\Psi})
,
\overline{\mathbf{A}}_{\ell+1}=\mathbf{A}(\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{\ell+1})_{\mathbf{A}}^{\dagger}
,
\overline{\mathbf{b}}=\mathbf{b}-\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}_{\text{ker}}
,
(\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{\ell+1})_{\mathbf{A}}^{\dagger}=\mathbf{E}\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{\ell+1}^{\dagger}
, and
\mathbf{E}=\mathbf{I}_{N}-\mathbf{K}(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{K})^{\dagger}\mathbf{A}
.
Furthermore,
(\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{\ell+1})_{\mathbf{A}}^{\dagger}
is known as the oblique (
\mathbf{A}
-weighted) pseudoinverse of
\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{\ell+1}
; see
[
31
]
.
We note that the singular values associated with the least squares problems appearing in
eq.

13
may be very different; hence
(\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{\ell+1})_{\mathbf{A}}^{\dagger}
functions as a precondition.

Remark 3
.

Notable simplifications arise if
\boldsymbol{\Psi}^{-1}
exists, in which case
(\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{\ell+1})_{\mathbf{A}}^{\dagger}=\boldsymbol{\Psi}^{-1}\mathbf{W}_{\ell+1}^{-1}
, or if
\boldsymbol{\Psi}
has full column rank, in which case
(\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{\ell+1})_{\mathbf{A}}^{\dagger}=\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{\ell+1}^{\dagger}
.
In either case,
\mathbf{x}_{\text{ker}}=\mathbf{0}_{N}
, eliminating the need for the matrix
\mathbf{K}
.
Assuming
P>0
, we observe that
\mathbf{A}\mathbf{K}
forms a “skinny” full-rank matrix, whose pseudoinverse can be efficiently computed—even for large-scale problems—using the economic QR decomposition of
\mathbf{A}\mathbf{K}
.
In contrast, computing
\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{\ell+1}^{\dagger}
is more demanding for large problems and may, itself, require an iterative approach.
We discuss such efficient iterative approaches in
Appendix

F
.

4.2
Analysis of priorconditioning

We provide a theoretical analysis showing the benefits of priorconditioning in
eq.

13
for sparsity-promoting inverse problems.
To the best of our knowledge, such benefits have only been acknowledged heuristically in the literature
[
6
,
63
,
18
,
43
]
except for
[
46
]
, where an analysis was provided for the case
\boldsymbol{\Psi}=\mathbf{I}_{N}
.
Importantly, our investigation makes no assumptions on
\boldsymbol{\Psi}
and permits it to be rank-deficient.

Henceforth, let
\lambda_{i}(\mathbf{X})
denote the
i
th largest eigenvalue of a square matrix
\mathbf{X}
in descending order (counting multiplicities).
We begin by examining the suboptimal performance of the existing S-GKS method.
By the Poincaré separation theorem
[
38
, Corollary 4.3.16]
, the condition number of the projected least-squares problem in
eq.

11
cannot exceed that of the original (full-scale) problem in
eq.

3
.
However, the condition number of
eq.

3
itself can be extremely large, especially when the weight vector
\mathbf{w}
captures the sparsity pattern accurately.

theorem

4
below sheds light on this phenomenon.

Theorem 4
.

Let
\mathbf{Q}^{\text{st}}_{\mu}=\mathbf{A}^{T}\mathbf{A}+\mu\boldsymbol{\Psi}^{T}\mathbf{W}^{2}\boldsymbol{\Psi}
,
\mathbf{W}=\operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{w})
, and
R=\operatorname{rank}(\boldsymbol{\Psi})
.
Then, the first
R
largest eigenvalues of
\mathbf{Q}^{\text{st}}_{\mu}
satisfy

\displaystyle\lambda_{N}(\mathbf{A}^{T}\mathbf{A})+\mu\lambda_{R}(\boldsymbol{\Psi}^{T}\boldsymbol{\Psi})\lambda_{i+(N-R)}(\mathbf{W}^{2})\leq\lambda_{i}(\mathbf{Q}^{\text{st}}_{\mu})\leq\lambda_{1}(\mathbf{A}^{T}\mathbf{A})+\mu\lambda_{1}(\boldsymbol{\Psi}^{T}\boldsymbol{\Psi})\lambda_{i}(\mathbf{W}^{2})

(14)

for
i=1,\ldots,R
, and the remaining
N-R
eigenvalues satisfy

\displaystyle\lambda_{N}(\mathbf{A}^{T}\mathbf{A})\leq\lambda_{i}(\mathbf{Q}^{\text{st}}_{\mu})\leq\lambda_{1}(\mathbf{A}^{T}\mathbf{A})+\mu\lambda_{1}(\boldsymbol{\Psi}^{T}\boldsymbol{\Psi})\lambda_{i}(\mathbf{W}^{2})

(15)

for
i=R+1,\ldots,N
.

Proof.

The statement follows from standard bounds on the eigenvalues of sums of symmetric matrices and a generalization of Ostrowski’s theorem (see
theorem

10
).

theorem

4
implies the following lower bound for the condition number of
\mathbf{Q}_{\mu}^{\text{st}}
:

\kappa\left(\mathbf{Q}_{\mu}^{\text{st}}\right)\geq\frac{\lambda_{N}(\mathbf{A}^{T}\mathbf{A})+\mu\lambda_{N}(\boldsymbol{\Psi}^{T}\boldsymbol{\Psi})\lambda_{1}(\mathbf{W}^{2})}{\lambda_{1}(\mathbf{A}^{T}\mathbf{A})+\mu\lambda_{1}(\boldsymbol{\Psi}^{T}\boldsymbol{\Psi})\lambda_{N}(\mathbf{W}^{2})},

(16)

assuming
\operatorname{rank}(\boldsymbol{\Psi})=N
.
The lower bound
eq.

16
reveals the dependency of the condition number on the scaling in
\mathbf{W}^{2}
and the regularization parameter
\mu
.
Consider the typical situation where
\mathbf{W}={\rm diag\!}\left(\mathbf{w}\right)
accurately encodes the sparsity profile, i.e.,
w_{k}\approx 0
if
[\boldsymbol{\Psi}\mathbf{x}_{\text{truth}}]_{k}=0
(which is true for most of the entries) and
w_{k}\gg 0
otherwise.
In this case,
\lambda_{1}(\mathbf{W}^{2})\gg\lambda_{N}(\mathbf{W}^{2})
and the right hand side of
eq.

16
implies a detremantally large condition number for
\mathbf{Q}_{\mu}^{\text{st}}
and the S-GKS method, provided that
\mu
is not too small.

In contrast, we next show in
theorem

5
that the normal equations for the priorconditioned formulation of the RHS of
eq.

13
enjoys a clustered spectrum.

Theorem 5
.

Let
\mathbf{Q}_{\mu}^{\text{pr}}=\overline{\mathbf{A}}^{T}\overline{\mathbf{A}}+\mu\mathbf{I}_{K}
with
\overline{\mathbf{A}}=\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{W}\boldsymbol{\Psi})_{\mathbf{A}}^{\dagger}
where
(\mathbf{W}\boldsymbol{\Psi})_{\mathbf{A}}^{\dagger}=\mathbf{E}(\mathbf{W}\boldsymbol{\Psi})^{\dagger}
is the oblique (
\mathbf{A}
-weighted) pseudoinverse of
\mathbf{W}\boldsymbol{\Psi}
as in
Section

4.1
, and let
R=\operatorname{rank}(\boldsymbol{\Psi})
.
Then, the first
R
largest eigenvalues of
\mathbf{Q}_{\mu}^{\text{pr}}
satisfy

\displaystyle\mu\leq\lambda_{i}(\mathbf{Q}_{\mu}^{\text{pr}})\leq\mu+\min\left\{c_{1}\lambda_{i}(\mathbf{A}^{T}\mathbf{A}),c_{2}\lambda_{i}(\mathbf{W}^{-2})\right\}

(17)

for
i=1,\ldots,R
, where
c_{1}=\lambda_{1}(\mathbf{W}^{-2})/\lambda_{R}(\boldsymbol{\Psi}^{T}\boldsymbol{\Psi})
and
c_{2}=\lambda_{1}(\mathbf{A}^{T}\mathbf{A})/\lambda_{R}(\boldsymbol{\Psi}^{T}\boldsymbol{\Psi})
are constants independent of
i
.
Moreover, the remaining
K-R
eigenvalues of
\mathbf{Q}_{\mu}^{\text{pr}}
are all equal to
\mu
.

Proof.

See
Appendix

A
.

theorem

5
implies the following upper bound for the condition number of
\mathbf{Q}_{\mu}^{\text{pr}}
:

\displaystyle\kappa(\mathbf{Q}_{\mu}^{\text{pr}})\leq 1+\frac{\lambda_{1}(\mathbf{A}^{T}\mathbf{A})\lambda_{1}(\mathbf{W}^{-2})}{\mu\lambda_{R}(\boldsymbol{\Psi}^{T}\boldsymbol{\Psi})}.

(18)

Comparing
eq.

18
with
eq.

16
, we see that the linear system resulting from priorconditioning is typically significantly better conditioned than the original one.
In particular, the upper bound
eq.

18
for priorconditioned linear systems is independent of the relative scaling of the weights
\mathbf{w}
and improves for increasing
\mu
.
This observation indicates that the proposed priorconditioning is particularly advantageous for strongly sparsity-promoting approaches.

It is important to note that not only the condition number but also the clustering of eigenvalues will significantly influence the performance of iterative methods such as CG.

This is due to the polynomial best approximation property of CG
[
16
, Lemma 3.14]
, which states for a generic system
\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{u}=\mathbf{v}
that the
\ell
th iteration satisfies

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{u}_{\ell}\|_{\mathbf{Q}}=\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\in\mathcal{P}_{\ell}\\
p(0)=1\end{subarray}}\|p(\mathbf{Q})(\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{u}_{0})\|_{\mathbf{Q}},

(19)

where
\mathcal{P}_{\ell}
denotes the set of polynomials of degree at most
\ell
. That is, CG implicitly fits a polynomial to the spectrum of
\mathbf{Q}
.
The resulting polynomial best approximation is expected to be more accurate when the spectrum of
\mathbf{Q}
is clustered. Hence, better clustering leads to accelerated convergence for CG with fewer basis vectors.
From
theorem

5
, the spectrum of
\mathbf{Q}_{\mu}^{\text{pr}}
is comprised of
R
eigenvalues decaying to
\mu
at least as rapidly as
\mu+c_{1}\lambda_{i}(\mathbf{A}^{T}\mathbf{A})
or
\mu+c_{2}\lambda_{i}(\mathbf{W}^{-2})
(whichever is faster), along with the eigenvalue
\mu
repeated
K-R
times.
Consequently,
\mathbf{Q}_{\mu}^{\text{pr}}
can have at most
\operatorname{rank}(\mathbf{A})+1
distinct eigenvalues.
Furthermore, if the weights
\mathbf{w}
are close to encoding a sparse solution with
S
“small” components (identifying the support) and
K-S
“large” components, then we expect the spectrum to exhibit at most
S
+1 clusters.
In summary, our above analysis indicates that priorconditioning becomes particularly effective when (i) the weights strongly promote sparsity, (ii) the singular values of
\mathbf{A}
decay fast, or (iii) the regularization parameter
\mu
is increased. This should be compared to
theorem

4
, which indicates that the eigenvalues of
\mathbf{Q}_{\mu}^{\text{st}}
span a wide range of values with no particular clustering.

4.3
Projected IRLS via priorconditioned generalized Krylov subspaces

We now introduce our new PS-GKS method, which incorporates priorconditioned generalized Krylov subspaces into the projected IRLS scheme in
eq.

10
.
The main idea is to replace
eq.

10
with

{\bf x}_{\ell+1}=\mathbf{x}_{\text{ker}}+(\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{\ell+1})_{\mathbf{A}}^{\dagger}\left(\argmin_{{\bf z}\in\mathcal{V}_{\ell}}\left\{\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\overline{{\bf A}}_{\ell+1}{\bf z}-{\bf b}\right\rVert}_{2}^{2}+\mu_{\ell+1}\mathinner{\!\left\lVert{\bf z}\right\rVert}_{2}^{2}\right\}\right),

(20)

where
\{\mathcal{V}_{\ell}\}_{\ell\geq 0}
is a nested sequence of low-dimensional subspaces. A key distinction of our proposed PS-GKS from existing S-GKS methods is that we define the subspace in the
K
-dimensional space of “increments” rather than in the
N
-dimensional native space of
{\bf x}
.
Consequently, we refer to these subspaces as
priorconditioned generalized Krylov subspaces
.

Our proposed PS-GKS method operates as follows:
We begin by selecting an initialization
\mathbf{x}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
.
We then compute the associated weight matrix
\mathbf{W}_{0}
and weighted sparsifying transformation
\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{0}=\mathbf{W}_{0}\boldsymbol{\Psi}
.
Next, we determine the contribution to the solution from
\ker(\boldsymbol{\Psi})
as
\mathbf{x}_{\text{ker}}=\mathbf{K}(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{K})^{\dagger}\mathbf{b}
and define the pseudoinverses
\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{0}^{\dagger}
and
(\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{0})_{\mathbf{A}}^{\dagger}
.
Letting
\overline{\mathbf{A}}_{\ell}\coloneqq\mathbf{A}(\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{\ell})_{\mathbf{A}}^{\dagger}
, we generate an initial subspace
\mathcal{V}_{0}=\mathcal{K}_{h}(\overline{\mathbf{A}}_{0}^{T}\overline{\mathbf{A}}_{0},\overline{\mathbf{A}}_{0}^{T}\overline{\mathbf{b}})\subset\mathbb{R}^{K}
for a relatively small
h
, e.g.,
h=5
.
(See
remark

7
for more details on choosing the initial subspace.).

At the
(\ell+1)
th iteration of the PS-GKS method, we begin by computing the new weight matrix
\mathbf{W}_{\ell+1}
and the weighted sparsifying transformation
\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{\ell+1}=\mathbf{W}_{\ell+1}\boldsymbol{\Psi}
—as in the S-GKS approach.
We then project the stand form IRLS subproblem on the right-hand side of
eq.

13
onto the lower-dimensional subspace
\mathcal{V}_{\ell}
to obtain the projected problem

\displaystyle\argmin_{\mathbf{z}\in\mathcal{V}_{\ell}}\left\{\|\overline{\mathbf{A}}_{\ell+1}\mathbf{z}-\overline{\mathbf{b}}\|_{2}^{2}+\mu_{\ell+1}\|\mathbf{z}\|_{2}^{2}\right\}.

(21)

To solve
eq.

21
, we insert the economic QR factorization
\overline{\mathbf{A}}_{\ell+1}\mathbf{V}_{\ell}=\mathbf{Q}_{\overline{\mathbf{A}}}\mathbf{R}_{\overline{\mathbf{A}}}
, yielding

\displaystyle\argmin_{\mathbf{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{D_{\ell}}}\left\{\|\mathbf{R}_{\overline{\mathbf{A}}}\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{Q}_{\overline{\mathbf{A}}}^{T}\overline{\mathbf{b}}\|_{2}^{2}+\mu_{\ell+1}\|\mathbf{u}\|_{2}^{2}\right\}.

(22)

After selecting
\mu_{\ell+1}
using a regularization parameter selection method (see
Section

4.4
), we recover the full-scale solution to the transformed problem as
\mathbf{z}_{\ell+1}=\mathbf{V}_{\ell}\mathbf{u}_{\ell+1}
.
Furthermore, we get an estimate of the solution to the original problem as
\mathbf{x}_{\ell+1}=(\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{\ell+1})_{\mathbf{A}}^{\dagger}\mathbf{z}_{\ell+1}+\mathbf{x}_{\text{ker}}
.
The remaining steps in the iteration follow that of the S-GKS method.
The main difference is that the subspace enlargement is performed in the transformed space.
Specifically, the residual vector is
\mathbf{r}_{\ell+1}=\overline{\mathbf{A}}_{\ell+1}^{T}(\overline{\mathbf{A}}_{\ell+1}\mathbf{V}_{\ell}\mathbf{z}_{\ell+1}-\overline{\mathbf{b}})+\mu_{\ell+1}\mathbf{V}_{\ell}\mathbf{z}_{\ell+1}
for the proposed PS-GKS method.
Finally, the PS-GKS iteration is repeated until some convergence criterion is satisfied.

Remark 6

At the same time, building the QR factorization of

D_{\ell}
matvecs with
\boldsymbol{\Psi}
and
\mathcal{O}(D_{\ell}^{2}K)
additional flops—we have an additional factor
D_{\ell}
because
\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{\ell+1}
changes at each iteration.
2
2

2

.

\mathbf{A}\mathbf{V}_{\ell}

\mathbf{A}

\mathbf{A}\mathbf{V}_{\ell-1}

[

We now provide a few details on how the DP can be used to select

in the priorconditioned projected problem

Since
\mathbf{x}_{\ell+1}=(\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{\ell+1})_{\mathbf{A}}^{\dagger}\mathbf{V}_{\ell}\mathbf{u}_{\ell+1}+\mathbf{x}_{\text{ker}}
and
\mathbf{A}(\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{\ell+1})_{\mathbf{A}}^{\dagger}\mathbf{V}_{\ell}=\mathbf{Q}_{\overline{\mathbf{A}}}\mathbf{R}_{\overline{\mathbf{A}}}
, the DP rule for

is to select
\mu_{\ell+1}
as the root of

\displaystyle\varphi(\mu)

4.5
Restarting and recycling PS-GKS

As discussed in
remark

2
, the storage requirements of performing many iterations of S-GKS or PS-GKS can easily exceed the memory capacity in some applications.
Furthermore, for PS-GKS, we have to perform
\mathcal{O}(D_{\ell})
additional matvecs with
\mathbf{A}
and
(\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{\ell+1})_{\mathbf{A}}^{\dagger}
at each iteration, which can increase the computational costs for large
\ell
. For these reasons, we proposed to combine the PS-GKS method with restarting/recycling strategies—similar to those described in
remark

2
for existing S-GKS methods. The resulting restarted PS-GKS (resPS-GKS) method has memory requirements and computational costs of
\mathcal{O}(D_{\ell}K)
and
\mathcal{O}(D_{\ell}^{2}M)
flops per iteration, respectively, where
D_{\ell}=1+\ell\mod D_{\text{max}}
.

At the same time, the resulting recycling PS-GKS (recPS-GKS) method has memory requirements and computational costs of
\mathcal{O}(D_{\ell}K)
and
\mathcal{O}(D_{\ell}^{2}M)
flops per iteration, respectively, where
D_{\ell}=D_{\text{min}}+\ell\mod(D_{\text{max}}+1)
.

Several strategies may be used to perform the basis compression step in recycled PS-GKS.
In our implementation, we consider a truncated SVD (tSVD) method based on the matrix
\mathbf{H}_{\ell+1}=[\mathbf{R}_{\overline{\mathbf{A}}}^{T},\mu_{\ell+1}^{1/2}\mathbf{I}_{D_{\ell}}]^{T}
, which is inspired by
[
37
,
53
]
.
Suppose we want to compress the basis
\mathbf{V}_{\ell}\in\mathbb{R}^{K\times D_{\text{max}}}
.
We begin by computing the rank-(
D_{\text{min}}-1)
truncated SVD
\mathbf{H}_{\ell+1}\approx\hat{\mathbf{U}}\hat{\mathbf{S}}\hat{\mathbf{W}}^{T}
with
\hat{\mathbf{U}}\in\mathbb{R}^{2D_{\text{max}}\times D_{\text{min}}-1}
,
\hat{\mathbf{S}}\in\mathbb{R}^{D_{\text{min}}-1\times D_{\text{min}}-1}
, and
\hat{\mathbf{W}}\in\mathbb{R}^{D_{\text{max}}\times D_{\text{min}}-1}
.
Next, we compute a new basis matrix
\tilde{\mathbf{V}}=\mathbf{V}_{\ell}\hat{\mathbf{W}}\in\mathbb{R}^{K\times D_{\text{min}}-1}
.

We then form
\tilde{\mathbf{z}}=(\mathbf{z}_{\ell+1}-\tilde{\mathbf{V}}\tilde{\mathbf{V}}^{T}\mathbf{z}_{\ell+1})/\|\mathbf{z}_{\ell+1}-\tilde{\mathbf{V}}\tilde{\mathbf{V}}^{T}\mathbf{z}_{\ell+1}\|_{2}
and replace the basis with
\mathbf{V}_{\ell}=[\tilde{\mathbf{V}}\,\,\tilde{\mathbf{z}}]\in\mathbb{R}^{K\times D_{\text{min}}}
. Overall, the above compression routine costs
\mathcal{O}(D_{\text{max}}^{3}+D_{\text{max}}^{2}K)
flops, making it inexpensive compared to the computational cost of a single iteration of PS-GKS when the basis is large.

5.1.1
Sensitivity of the MM methods w.r.t.
\varepsilon

For methods using MM weights, it is well known that the value of
\varepsilon
used in defining the smoothed approximation to the
\ell_{p}
norm may drastically affect the performance of various methods—a value of
\varepsilon
that is too large promotes sparsity only weakly, while a value of
\varepsilon
that is too small may yield numerical instabilities in the method.
Here, we argue that methods that make use of priorconditioning (including PS-GKS, PS-GKB, and flexible methods) are relatively insensitive to the
\varepsilon
parameter when compared to S-GKS.
To demonstrate this, we compare the dependency of the final RRE and Gini index (after 150 iterations) for several methods on the value of the
\varepsilon
parameter in
fig.

4
.

Figure 4
:
Test 1. Sensitivity of 1D cosine problem results w.r.t. the MM
\varepsilon
parameter in terms of the final RRE (left) and Gini index (right).

We observe that the quality of the S-GKS solution initially improves as
\varepsilon
decreases, but then degrades once
\varepsilon
is reduced below a certain critical threshold.
Since this threshold is not known a priori, this highlights the role of
\varepsilon
as an additional tuning parameter for the S-GKS method with MM weights.
In contrast, we observe that the performance of the PS-GKS, PS-GKB, and flexible methods (except FGK) is comparatively insensitive with respect to
\varepsilon
, which can thus safely be set to a small value without degrading performance.

6
Conclusion and outlook

In this paper we propose priorconditioning methods that can be used in both deterministic and Bayesian setting for ill-posed inverse problems with sparsity-promoting priors. Namely, we develop prior conditioned sparsity generalized Krylov subspace PS-GKS methods that when used in the context of reweighting exhibits superior reconstructions at a relatively small number of iterations. We further propose variations of the PS-GKS method, including restarting and recycling (resPS-GKS and recPS-GKS).
Such methods allow us to improve the computed solution quality and the computational time by reducing memory requirements, and automatically select a regularization parameter at a reduced number of iterations compared to the original S-GKS.
For the Bayesian setting, we can estimate one of the hyperprior parameters automatically. While we only focus on the GSBL priors, our work has potential to be extended to other hierarchical priors and further can be used for more efficient posterior characterization, i.e., UQ for the sparsity-promoting priors. We provide a theoretical analysis showing the benefits of priorconditioning in for sparsity-promoting inverse problems.

As future work we consider extension to the dynamic inverse problems setting which we anticipate to present computational difficulties especially in efficient estimation of the priorconditioner. Addressing computational concerns that arise from the need to estimate pseudoinverses of large and complex matrices is left as future work. Our preliminary results on multigrid methods show that we can avoid the need for GPU usage and still maintain low computational time – a direction which we aim to pursue for the dynamic inverse problems setting
[
55
,
40
]
.

Appendix F
Computation of pseudoinverses

The main computational obstacle introduced by the PS-GKS method when compared with S-GKS is the requirement of computing matvecs with the pseudoinverses
\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{\ell}^{\dagger}
and
(\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{\ell}^{\dagger})^{T}
. We assume here that
\boldsymbol{\Psi}^{-1}
is not invertible. Recall that it in general
(\mathbf{W}_{\ell}\boldsymbol{\Psi_{\ell}})^{\dagger}\neq\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{\ell}^{\dagger}\mathbf{W}_{\ell}^{-1}
,
3
3

3

Notable exceptions of when
(\mathbf{W}_{\ell}\boldsymbol{\Psi_{\ell}})^{\dagger}=\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{\ell}^{\dagger}\mathbf{W}_{\ell}^{-1}
holds include when
\boldsymbol{\Psi}
is invertible or has linearly independent rows.
which means that an offline computation of
\boldsymbol{\Psi}^{\dagger}
is not immediately of use.

For problems of sufficiently low dimension (small
N
), one may employ an approximation to the pseudoinverse such as

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{\ell}^{\dagger}\approx(\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{\ell}^{T}\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{\ell}+\delta\mathbf{I}_{N})^{-1}\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{\ell}^{T},\quad(\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{\ell}^{\dagger})^{T}\approx\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{\ell}(\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{\ell}^{T}\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{\ell}+\delta\mathbf{I}_{N})^{-1},

(62)

for some small
\delta>0
. This is particularly convenient when
\boldsymbol{\Psi}^{T}\boldsymbol{\Psi}
possesses banded structure, since in this case matvecs with the inverses in
eq.

62
may be applied in
\mathcal{O}(B^{2}N)
flops using a banded Cholesky factorization
[
57
]
, where
B
denotes the bandwidth.

For problems of high dimension (large
N
), iterative methods provide an effective means of computing matvecs with the pseudoinverses. One such method was recently proposed in
[
23
,
25
]
, where the matvec
\boldsymbol{\xi}=\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{\ell}^{\dagger}\mathbf{y}
is computed by applying the LSQR
[
50
]
or LSMR
[

\boldsymbol{\Psi}^{\dagger}

at each instance. The matrix

Appendix G
Priorconditioned GKB and FGK methods

The methods discussed in the paper discussed so far fall under the umbrella of GKS-type hybrid projection methods; our PS-GKS method is the first of GKS-type that utilizes priorconditioned subspaces. There exists a competing class of priorconditioned Golub-Kahan (GK) -type hybrid projection methods based on the Golub-Kahan bidiagonalization (GKB) algorithm or the flexible Golub-Kahan (FGK) decomposition
[
14
]
.

G.1
GKB method

It is straightforward to devise an analogue of our PS-GKS based on the GKB process, which we will refer to as PS-GKB. Such a method was first proposed in
[
25
]
. At the
\ell
th iteration of PS-GKB we form the priorconditioned problem

\displaystyle\argmin_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}}\left\{\|\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{b}\|_{2}^{2}+\mu_{\ell}\|\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{\ell}\mathbf{x}\|_{2}^{2}\right\}=(\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{\ell+1})_{\mathbf{A}}^{\dagger}\left(\argmin_{\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{K}}\left\{\|\overline{\mathbf{A}}_{\ell}\mathbf{z}-\overline{\mathbf{b}}\|_{2}^{2}+\mu_{\ell}\|\mathbf{z}\|_{2}^{2}\right\}\right)+\mathbf{x}_{\text{ker}}

(67)

where
\overline{\mathbf{b}}=\mathbf{b}-\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}_{\text{ker}}
and
\mathbf{x}_{\text{ker}}=\mathbf{K}(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{K})^{\dagger}\mathbf{b}
. Next, instead of projecting the problem in the RHS of
eq.

67
onto a generalized Krylov subspace as in PS-GKS, we instead project it onto
\operatorname{col}(\mathbf{V}_{\ell})
where
\mathbf{V}_{\ell}
arises from the Golub-Kahan bidiagonalization

\displaystyle\overline{\mathbf{A}}_{\ell}\mathbf{V}_{\ell}=\mathbf{U}_{\ell+1}\mathbf{B}_{\ell},\quad\overline{\mathbf{A}}_{\ell}^{T}\mathbf{U}_{\ell+1}=\mathbf{V}_{\ell+1}\hat{\mathbf{B}}_{\ell+1}^{T}

(68)

for matrices
\mathbf{U}_{\ell+1}\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times(\ell+1)}
,
\mathbf{V}_{\ell+1}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times(\ell+1)}
with orthonormal columns and lower bidiagonal matrices
\mathbf{B}_{\ell}\in\mathbb{R}^{(\ell+1)\times\ell},\hat{\mathbf{B}}_{\ell+1}\in\mathbb{R}^{(\ell+1)\times(\ell+1)}
. Using
eq.

68
, the projected problem can be expressed as

\displaystyle\mathbf{y}_{\ell}=\argmin_{\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{\ell}}\left\{\|\mathbf{B}_{\ell}\mathbf{y}-\|\overline{\mathbf{b}}\|_{2}\mathbf{e}_{1}\|_{2}^{2}+\mu_{\ell}\|\mathbf{y}\|_{2}^{2}\right\}

(69)

where
\mathbf{e}_{1}=[1,0,\ldots,0]^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{\ell+1}
and the solution at the
\ell
th iteration is recovered as
\mathbf{x}_{\ell}=\mathbf{x}_{\text{ker}}+(\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{\ell})_{\mathbf{A}}^{\dagger}\mathbf{V}_{\ell}\mathbf{y}_{\ell}
. The parameter
\mu_{\ell}
can be selected using a regularization parameter selection method such as DP. This process is repeated for increasing
\ell
, where a new weighting matrix
\mathbf{W}_{\ell}
is computed at each iteration. We note that the
\ell
th iteration of PS-GKB requires
\mathcal{O}(\ell)
matvecs with
\mathbf{A}/\mathbf{A}^{T}
and
(\boldsymbol{\Psi_{\ell}})_{\mathbf{A}}^{\dagger}/((\boldsymbol{\Psi_{\ell}})_{\mathbf{A}}^{\dagger})^{T}
which is the same as the
\ell
th iteration of PS-GKS.

G.2
FGK methods

FGK hybrid projection methods are based on the flexible preconditioning framework of
[
48
,
58
,
59
]
. The main differences between FGK methods and the PS-GKB method are that FGK methods require only a single matvec with
\mathbf{A}/\mathbf{A}^{T}
and
(\boldsymbol{\Psi_{\ell}})_{\mathbf{A}}^{\dagger}/((\boldsymbol{\Psi_{\ell}})_{\mathbf{A}}^{\dagger})^{T}
in each iteration, and that the approximation subspace of FGK methods depends on the entire history of weight matrices
\{\mathbf{W}_{j}\}_{j\leq\ell}
, while the approximation subspace of PS-GKB depends on only the latest weight matrix
\mathbf{W}_{\ell}
.

Assuming for the moment that
\boldsymbol{\Psi}^{-1}
exists, the FGK process produces the factorization

\displaystyle\mathbf{A}\mathbf{Z}_{\ell}=\mathbf{U}_{\ell+1}\mathbf{M}_{\ell},\quad\mathbf{A}^{T}\mathbf{U}_{\ell+1}=\mathbf{V}_{\ell+1}\mathbf{S}_{\ell+1},

(70)

where the columns of
\mathbf{U}_{\ell+1}
and
\mathbf{V}_{\ell+1}
are orthonormal,
\mathbf{M}_{\ell}
is upper Hessenberg, and
\mathbf{S}_{\ell+1}
is upper triangular. The approximation subspace for the solution at the
\ell
th iteration is taken to be
\operatorname{col}(\mathbf{Z}_{\ell})
, where several choices of
\mathbf{Z}_{\ell}
appear in the literature. When
\boldsymbol{\Psi}^{-1}
exists, the choice
\mathbf{Z}_{\ell}^{(a)}=[\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{1}^{-T}\mathbf{v}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{\ell}^{-T}\mathbf{v}_{\ell}]
corresponds to that of
[
14
]
. For general
\boldsymbol{\Psi}
, the choice

\displaystyle\mathbf{Z}_{\ell}^{(b)}=\begin{bmatrix}(\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{1})_{\mathbf{A}}^{\dagger}((\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{1})_{\mathbf{A}}^{\dagger})^{T}\mathbf{v}_{1}\quad\cdots\quad(\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{\ell})_{\mathbf{A}}^{\dagger}((\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{\ell})_{\mathbf{A}}^{\dagger})^{T}\mathbf{v}_{\ell}\end{bmatrix}

(71)

has been proposed in
[
25
,
22
]
and is what we employ for the FGK methods in this investigation. We assume throughout that the FGK process is break-down free, i.e., that
\operatorname{rank}(\mathbf{Z}_{\ell})=\ell
for each
\ell
.

In this investigation, we only consider FGK methods corresponding to the “first-regularize-then-project” framework
[
30
, §6.4]
. Included in this framework are the IRW-LSQR method of
[
22
]
and the F-TV method method of
[
25
]
. Such FGK methods utilize the projected problem

\displaystyle\mathbf{x}_{\ell}=\mathbf{x}_{\text{ker}}+\argmin_{\mathbf{x}\in\operatorname{col}(\mathbf{Z}_{\ell})}\left\{\|\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{b}\|_{2}^{2}+\mu_{\ell}\|\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{\ell}\mathbf{x}\|_{2}^{2}\right\},

(72)

in the
\ell
th iteration. Inserting
eq.

70
into the minimization in
eq.

72
produces the equivalent problem

\displaystyle\mathbf{y}_{\ell}=\argmin_{\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{\ell}}\,\,\|\mathbf{M}_{\ell}\mathbf{y}-\|\mathbf{b}\|_{2}\mathbf{e}_{1}\|_{2}^{2}+\mu_{\ell}\|\mathbf{R}_{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}\mathbf{y}\|_{2}^{2}

where

.
