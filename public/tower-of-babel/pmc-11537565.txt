Novel, Low-cost Ear Elevation Splint for Auricular Construction
Authors: Emily S. Chwa, Breanna Baltrusch, Erin Claussen, Sophia Allison, Nikhil D. Shah, Akira Yamada
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Summary:Modern approaches to autogenous auricular reconstruction involve a multistage approach where ear elevation is an independent procedure. The goal is to project the reconstructed ear at an anatomically accurate and symmetric auriculocephalic angle, but postoperative scar contraction may adversely affect the long-term outcomes. An ear elevation splint was developed to provide rigid support for the auricle during the acute healing period to promote lasting elevation. The splint is low cost, made from commonly used orthotic materials, and can be conveniently customized for each patient in less than 15 minutes.

Takeaways

Question: How can ear projection loss after auricular reconstruction be prevented with postoperative dressings?

Findings: A novel ear elevation splint created using commonly available, inexpensive orthotic materials was able to provide rigid support for ear projection.

Meaning: Craniofacial surgeons can quickly create an inexpensive ear elevation splint for patients after auricular reconstruction to provide rigid support and prevent projection loss.

EAR ELEVATION IN AURICULAR RECONSTRUCTION

Success of congenital microtia repair should be based on the anatomic landmarks, placement, and projection of the reconstructed ear. After advancements in autogenous auricular reconstruction by Tanzer and Brent, Nagata1 condensed the multistage procedure into his 2-stage technique. The senior author uses a 2-stage autogenous approach, after Nagata with several modifications.2,3 All approaches involve ear elevation as an independent step, where the reconstructed ear must maintain projection at an anatomically correct and bilaterally symmetrical auriculocephalic angle.

Maintaining long-term projection of the auricle can be compromised due to insufficient auricular support or scar contracture. In a study of 309 patients who underwent total auricular reconstruction with a modified Nagata technique, 22.3% had insufficient projection or a shallow auriculocephalic sulcus at a median follow-up of 26 months.4

To combat these adverse outcomes, the senior author collaborated with orthotists to develop an ear elevation splint that maintains desirable auricular projection with external support. After the elevation procedure, the ear is dressed with a bolster suture for 2 weeks. After the bolster suture is removed, the splint is then placed along the posterior ear to be worn continuously for one month. This project was deemed nonhuman research by the Lurie Children’s institutional review board.

The materials above are commonly used by orthotists and cost approximately $35 per patient.

Seven patients with microtia (age range 11–59 years) have been treated with this ear elevation splint. A modified version of the splint can be made to only elevate the upper auricle. All patients did not have notable reduction in projection or shallowing of the sulcus at a median follow-up period of 10 months (range 4–15 months) (Fig. 3). Patients reported compliance with the splint and did not endorse any severe discomfort that compromised the ability to wear the splint as instructed. No pressure sores or injury to the surrounding skin occurred.

DISCUSSION

The ear elevation splint was developed to maintain projection in autologous auricular reconstruction during the postoperative healing period. It provides rigid support to the elevated auricle compared with traditional dressing and counters the pressure from scar contracture.

Advancements in operative technique to promote lasting elevation include the use of costal cartilage blocks by Nagata1 or more experimental approaches such as bone cement5 or MedPor beneath the auricle. All patients in this article underwent auricular reconstruction based on the Nagata technique with modifications by the senior author, where costal cartilage blocks support posterior framework and is covered with a temporoparietal fascial flap followed by skin graft from either the scalp or the chest. Due to the synthetic composition of MedPor, there is a higher rate of extrusion and infection.6 The use of temporoparietal fascia over the sulcus by Nagata1 improved skin graft take and prevented skin contracture. However, hypertrophic scarring remains an issue because it decreases the sulcus depth.

Despite these advancements, the study by Lee and Oh4 showed that a quarter of patients who had auricular reconstruction with the Nagata technique still had unfavorable ear projection at follow-up. Not only does reduced ear projection impair the aesthetic outcomes of the reconstruction, it can also render the sulcus nonfunctional, such as the inability to wear glasses. This splint adds to postoperative care for auricular reconstruction by promoting lasting ear elevation and keeping the skin taut to prevent scar contracture.7

The study also identified hemifacial microsomia and undergoing ear elevation within 12 months of canaloplasty as independent risk factors for decreased auricular elevation. For these patients, in addition to those with known risk factors for scar contracture or hypertrophic scarring, implementing this splint is especially valuable.

There is ongoing controversy regarding the clinical effectiveness of splinting therapy, but numerous in vivo studies have made splinting therapy a mainstay of preventing scar contracture following trauma or surgery, especially burn rehabilitation.8 This concept should be applied accordingly to ear elevation.

Of note, there are several other methods and materials that can be used in the creation and customization of a postelevation splint for patients undergoing auricular reconstruction. These include a dental impression compound, silicone Foley catheter-based splint, other thermoplastic splints, and 3-dimensional printed ear splint.9–11 Alternative materials to Aquaplast include high-molecular-weight polyethylene compound, high-molecular-weight polypropylene compound, and an ethylene vinyl acetate compound, which all become moldable at higher temperatures. (See table, Supplemental Digital Content 2, which lists the advantages and drawbacks for each material or technique, http://links.lww.com/PRSGO/D596.) Compliance of patients wearing postoperative molds and splints is all relatively high regardless of material used. Cost of splint, availability of materials, and patient preference all should inform the physician’s decision.

Although the senior author uses this splint for 1 month, there are limited data on the ideal duration. The remodeling phase can last for months, and scar contracture occurs more often in skin grafts due to their inability to expand.7,12 Patients follow up with the senior author weekly for 6 weeks after ear elevation, and the senior author has noted this to be a critical time period in hypertrophic scarring and wound contracture. Thereafter, patients are seen monthly to monitor any changes and ensure appropriate projection is maintained (Figs. 3, 4). (See figure, Supplemental Digital Content 3, which displays a lateral view of a 12-year-old boy 7 months postoperative, http://links.lww.com/PRSGO/D597.) New onset scar contracture would be unlikely in this small cohort, but the duration of postoperative ear elevation splinting may change based on the senior author’s evolving experience.

A photograph of a 12-year-old boy now 7 months postoperative with appropriately deep sulcus after successful use of the ear elevation splint.

Although a formal study with objective methodology is needed to determine any statistically significant improvements, the splint is a convenient, low-cost, low-risk, and noninvasive addition to postoperative management that offers stronger support than typical dressing with clinical observation of maintained ear projection over time. Craniofacial surgeons can either collaborate with in-house orthotists to make the splints in the clinic or acquire the very few materials necessary for the splint and create it themselves.
