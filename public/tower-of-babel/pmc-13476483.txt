Opportunistic Genetic Testing for Familial Hypercholesterolemia in Premature Acute Coronary Syndrome
Authors: Nicol Vaizman, Lubomira Cermakova, Matthew Cooper, Adam I Kramer, Kyle Hui, Linda M Jackson, Thomas M Roston, GB John Mancini, Christopher B Fordyce, Iulia Iatan, Liam R Brunham
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundFamilial hypercholesterolemia (FH) is common among patients with premature acute coronary syndrome (ACS) but remains underdiagnosed and undertreated.ObjectivesThe ACCURATE (Advancing Cardiac Care Unit-based Rapid Assessment and Treatment of hypErcholesterolemia) study evaluated whether opportunistic genetic testing in patients with premature ACS increases FH diagnosis and lipid-lowering therapy use.MethodsACCURATE was a nonrandomized, prospective study that recruited 2 sequential cohorts of patients. In phase 1, patients were treated according to usual standard of care, with no genetic testing. In phase 2, patients underwent FH genetic testing with results returned to treating physicians. Eligible patients were <60 years old admitted with ACS and had low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) ≥4 mmol/L (≥155 mg/dL). The primary endpoint was a new diagnosis of definite FH at 15 months post-ACS. Secondary endpoints were changes in lipid-lowering therapy and lipid levels.ResultsOverall, 40 patients in phase 1 and 100 patients in phase 2 completed the study, with similar baseline characteristics. A diagnosis of definite FH at follow-up occurred in 10% of patients in phase 2 vs 0% in phase 1. At 15 months, patients in phase 2 were more likely to achieve an LDL-C <1.8 mmol/L (73% vs 53%, P = 0.04), had lower mean LDL-C (1.65 mmol/L vs 1.93 mmol/L [64 mg/dL vs 75 mg/dL]) (P = 0.03), and were more likely to be receiving statin therapy (95% vs 83%, P = 0.04) or statin-ezetimibe combination therapy (52% vs 30%, P = 0.02).ConclusionsOpportunistic genetic testing of young adults with ACS was associated with increased FH diagnosis, more intensive lipid-lowering treatment, and improved lipid target attainment. (Advancing Cardiac Care Unit-based Rapid Assessment and Treatment of hypErcholesterolemia [ACCURATE]; NCT05218005).Key words: acute coronary syndrome, familial hypercholesterolemia, genetic testing

Heterozygous familial hypercholesterolemia (FH) is an autosomal semidominant disorder characterized by lifelong elevation of low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) and increased risk of atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD). FH results from pathogenic variants in the low-density lipoprotein receptor (LDLR), apolipoprotein B (APOB), proprotein convertase subtilisin/kexin type 9 (PCSK9), or low density lipoprotein receptor adaptor protein 1 (LDLRAP1), and an FH-causing variant is identified in 60% to 80% of individuals with clinically diagnosed FH.1 FH is estimated to affect ∼1 in 313 in the general population globally.2,3 However, < 10% of individuals with FH are diagnosed, and it remains undertreated.2, 3, 4

One approach to identify patients with FH is through opportunistic screening of patients presenting with an acute coronary syndrome (ACS). This approach is premised on the observation that FH is present in 1 in 14 individuals who experience an ACS at age <60 years, suggesting that the diagnostic yield in this population would be considerably higher compared to population screening.5 To date, genetic testing for FH has primarily been evaluated in stable outpatient populations.6 However, the diagnostic yield and clinical utility of genetic testing for FH in the ACS setting has not been prospectively evaluated, which may in part relate to the challenges of conducting genetic research in the cardiac intensive care unit setting.7

We designed the ACCURATE (Advancing Cardiac Care Unit-based Rapid Assessment and Treatment of hypErcholesterolemia) study to assess the diagnostic yield, feasibility, and clinical utility of genetic testing for FH in the acute cardiac care setting. Here, we report the primary results of ACCURATE.

Methods

Study design

The design and rationale of the ACCURATE study have been described previously.8 In brief, ACCURATE was a nonrandomized prospective study conducted at 2 quaternary care hospitals in Vancouver, Canada: St. Paul’s Hospital and Vancouver General Hospital. Recruitment occurred between January 2022 and May 2024. The design consisted of 2 sequentially recruited cohorts to allow comparison between a control group receiving standard of care with no genetic testing (phase 1), and an intervention group that underwent FH genetic testing (phase 2) (Figure 1, Supplemental Figure 1). All patients were followed prospectively for 15 months after discharge from their index ACS event. Data collection included demographics, medical histories, family history, laboratory values, and lipid-lowering therapy (LLT) use. Recurrent cardiovascular events were defined as nonfatal myocardial infarction (MI), unstable angina, coronary revascularization (elective or unplanned), and death. Data sources included review of laboratory records and consultation records during the index hospitalization and at follow-up encounters. All patients provided written informed consent. ACCURATE was approved by the Providence Health Care Research Ethics Board (certificate #H21-00116) and is registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT05218005). The trial was conducted in accordance with the provisions of the Declaration of Helsinki, the International Conference on Harmonization “Good Clinical Practices.”

Study population and baseline characteristics

Eligible participants were <60 years old admitted with a diagnosis of a ST-segment elevation MI or non–ST-segment elevation MI. Inclusion required either: 1) untreated LDL-C ≥4.0 mmol/L (155 mg/dL); 2) LDL-C ≥2.5 mmol/L (97 mg/dL) if on a statin for ≥1 month prior to presentation; or 3) non-HDL cholesterol (non-HDL-C) ≥4.6 mmol/L (178 mg/dL). Exclusion criteria were inability or unwillingness to provide informed consent and insufficient clinical or laboratory data. Recruitment took place during the COVID-19 pandemic. Due to pandemic-related delays in recruitment, the phase 1 cohort was supplemented with eligible participants (n = 4) recruited for the SAVE BC (Study to Avoid CardioVascular Events in BC) registry, a British Columbia-based prospective premature ASCVD registry, who met the same inclusion criteria and were recruited during the same time period as ACCURATE.9 Baseline lipid levels were defined as the value obtained at the index ACS admission or the highest value within 1 year preceding the event. For patients with triglycerides above 4.5 mmol/L (399 mg/dL) but <9 mmol/L (797 mg/dL), LDL-C was estimated using the Sampson-National Institutes of Health (NIH) equation.10 For patients on LLT ≥1 month before admission, untreated (baseline) LDL-C was imputed.11 High-intensity statin was defined as atorvastatin 40 or 80 mg or rosuvastatin 20 or 40 mg daily. Treatment intensification during follow-up was defined as any increase in LLT dose, including initiation or up-titration of statins, addition of ezetimibe, or initiation of a PCSK9 inhibitor (PCSK9i).

The Dutch Lipid Clinic Network Score (DLCNS) was calculated for all patients to classify their clinical probability of FH. The presence of tendon xanthomas and corneal arcus were not systematically recorded by treating physicians and were scored as 0. Patient reported family history of dyslipidemia was scored as 1 point. When the degree of relation was unknown or beyond first-degree, no points were assigned. Scores were categorized as unlikely (<3), possible (3-5), probable (6-8), or definite (≥8) FH. For comparability across phases, a baseline clinical DLCNS was calculated for all patients (phase 2 scored without genetic results). For phase 2, the DLCNS was updated by adding 8 points for an FH-causing variant identified to quantify post-genetic testing DLCNS reclassification.

Genetic testing and return of results for phase 2

Saliva samples were collected during hospitalization for phase 2 patients, and DNA was extracted using standard protocols as previously described (Supplemental Figure 2).8 Sequencing was performed using a targeted next-generation sequencing panel covering the major FH-associated genes (LDLR, APOB, PCSK9, LDLRAP1) and additional FH phenocopy genes (ABCG5/8). Variant annotation including population, functional, and FH-affected cases previously reported and in silico predictive algorithms was performed on the VarSeq (Golden Helix) software. Variants were classified according to the 2015 American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics standards and 2022 ClinGen FH Variant Curation Expert Panel criteria.8,12,13 Previously reported variants were checked in the ClinVar database, and FH Variant Curation Expert Panel curated classifications were used when available (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/clinvar/). Variants of uncertain significance were reported but not considered diagnostic.

Endpoints and statistical analysis

The primary endpoint was the number of patients with a new diagnosis of definite FH.8 The secondary endpoints were the proportion of patients who underwent intensification of LLT, the lowest LDL-C level achieved, and the proportion of patients achieving guideline-recommended lipid targets in the 15 months after the index ACS.8 The exploratory endpoint was the number of recurrent cardiovascular events in the 15-month follow-up.

Patient characteristics were summarized as mean ± SD for continuous variables and as counts with percentages for categorical variables. Between-group comparisons were performed using the Wilcoxon rank sum test (non-normally distributed variables) for continuous variables and Fisher exact test for categorical variables. The normality of variable distribution was verified using the Shapiro-Wilk test. A 2-tailed P value <0.05 was considered statistically significant. For the primary endpoint (the proportion of patients classified as definite FH at follow-up), a 1-tailed P value < 0.05 was considered significant based on the hypothesis that genetic testing would increase but not decrease the number of FH diagnoses. Corresponding 2-tailed P values are provided for completeness. Statistical analyses were performed in R version 4.1.1.

Results

Baseline characteristics

A total of 140 eligible participants were included, comprising 40 patients in phase 1 and 100 patients in phase 2 (Figure 1, Supplemental Figure 1). Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics were similar between phases (Table 1). Across both phases, the mean age was 50.8 years old (SD: 6.8; range: 31-60) and 88% of participants were male. The most common self-reported ethnicities were European (51%), South Asian (14%), and East Asian (11%). Cardiovascular risk factors were common, with dyslipidemia in 62%, smoking in 56%, hypertension in 44%, diabetes in 24%, and obesity in 31%. Family history of premature ASCVD was reported in 45% of patients across both phases and 72% reported a family history of dyslipidemia.

FH genetic testing in phase 2

All 100 patients in phase 2 successfully underwent targeted sequencing of FH-associated genes. The median time from discharge to genetic test report generation was 13 days (range: 0 to 72), and the median time to report distribution to cardiovascular specialists was 17 days (range: 3 to 73). Eighty-five percent of reports were distributed to specialists in <30 days from the date of discharge for the index ACS.

Pathogenic or likely pathogenic variants in FH-associated genes were identified in 8 out of 100 patients (8%). All genetic variants identified were present in the heterozygous state (Supplemental Table 1). Six patients had single-nucleotide variants including 5 in LDLR and 1 in PCSK9, and 2 carried copy-number variants, both in LDLR.

Among the 8 individuals with a pathogenic or likely pathogenic variant, genetic testing led to a reclassification of diagnosis in 88% (7/8) of patients (McNemar exact P = 0.016; 95% CI: 2.9-13.9). Upward reclassification occurred among patients initially classified as unlikely FH (n = 1), possible FH (n = 5), or probable FH (n = 1) (Supplemental Tables 2 and 3). At 15 months post-ACS, the proportion of patients with a new diagnosis of definite FH was 0% in phase 1 and 10% in phase 2 (1-tailed P = 0.03, 2-tailed P = 0.06) (Figure 2).

Lipid-level outcomes and target attainments

At baseline, LDL-C levels were not significantly different between phase 1 and 2 (Supplemental Table 4). Follow-up lipid profiles within 15 months post-ACS discharge were available for 36/40 (90%) of patients in phase 1 and 89/100 (89%) of patients in phase 2. Mean LDL-C at follow-up was significantly lower in phase 2 (1.66 ± 0.97 mmol/L; 95% CI: 1.44-1.85 mmol/L) (64 ± 38 mg/dL; 95% CI: 56-71 mg/dL) compared to phase 1 (1.93 ± 0.86 mmol/L; 95% CI: 1.64-2.22 mmol/L) (75 ± 33 mg/dL; 95% CI: 63-86 mg/dL) (P = 0.03) (Table 2).

Lipid-lowering therapy outcomes and patterns

Prior to admission, 6/40 (15%) of patients in phase 1 and 20/100 (20%) of patients in phase 2 were receiving statin therapy (Table 1). At the time of discharge from the index ACS, most patients were prescribed a high-intensity statin (36/40 in phase 1 [90%; 95% CI: 76.9%-96.0%]) vs 96/100 in phase 2 (96%; 95% CI: 90.2%-98.4%; P = 0.20) (Table 2). A greater proportion of patients in phase 2 were prescribed ezetimibe at discharge (6/40 in phase 1 [15%; 95% CI: 7.1%-29.1%]) vs 38/100 in phase 2 (38%; 95% CI: 29.1%-47.8%; P = 0.01). Combination LLT at discharge was less frequent in phase 1 than phase 2 (4/40 [10%; 95% CI: 4.0%-23.1%] vs 37/100 (37%; 95% CI: 28.2%-46.8%); P = 0.002). PCSK9i use at baseline was low in both phases (0% to 1%).

Deintensification of LLT occurred in 8/40 (20%; 95% CI: 10.5%-34.8%) in phase 1 vs 12/100 (12%; 95% CI: 7.0%-19.8%) in phase 2 (P = 0.3). At 15-month follow-up, statin use was significantly higher in phase 2 compared with phase 1 (95% vs 83%, P = 0.04). High-intensity statin use was numerically higher in phase 2 but did not reach statistical significance (90% vs 78%, P = 0.06); a similar pattern was observed for ezetimibe use (52% vs 35%, P = 0.09). PCSK9i use at 15 months remained low and was similar between phases (8% in phase 1 vs 5% in phase 2; P = 0.7). Dual therapy (statin + ezetimibe) was more common in phase 2 (52% vs 30%; P = 0.02), while triple therapy (statin + ezetimibe + PCSK9i) was exclusively used in phase 2 and uncommon overall (4% vs 0% in phase 1, P = 0.6). Any combination therapy was numerically higher in phase 2 compared to phase 1 (53/100 [53%] vs 15/40 [37.5%]; P = 0.1) (Table 2).

Subgroup analyses

Within phase 2, lipid levels, LDL-C target attainment, and LLT use among patients with a diagnosis of definite FH (n = 10) compared to those with probable/possible/unlikely FH (n = 90) are summarized in Table 3. Patients with a diagnosis of definite FH were more likely to receive combination therapy at discharge (80% vs 32%; P = 0.005). At follow-up, mean LDL-C was numerically lower in those with definite FH, but LDL-C target attainment was comparable with LDL-C <1.8 mmol/L achieved in 4/6 (67%) definite FH vs 56/76 (74%) in probable/possible/unlikely FH patients (P = 0.7). PCSK9i use was higher in patients with a diagnosis of definite FH (30% vs 2%; P = 0.007), and triple therapy was more common (30% vs 1%; P = 0.003). Recurrent cardiovascular events

Recurrent ACS or elective revascularization during the 15-month follow-up period occurred in 12.5% (5/40) of patients in phase 1 vs 7% (7/100) of patients in phase 2 (P = 0.3) (Supplemental Table 5). Death occurred in 0% (0/40) of patients in phase 1 and 2% (2/100) in phase 2 (P = 1).

Discussion

The ACCURATE study investigated the diagnostic yield and clinical utility of opportunistic genetic testing in patients <60 years old presenting with ACS. The key findings of this study were that opportunistic genetic testing identified pathogenic or likely pathogenic FH variants in 8% of patients with premature ACS, increased the diagnosis of definite FH at follow-up from 0% in the control phase to 10% in the intervention phase, and was associated with lower follow-up LDL-C levels, greater LDL-C target attainment, and higher use of statin and statin-ezetimibe therapy.

We identified pathogenic or likely pathogenic variants in 8% of patients in phase 2 (8/100), corresponding to a prevalence of 1 in 13 in patients with ACS <60 years. This is similar to the estimate of 1 in 14 patients with ACS <60 years from a previous meta-analysis and is 25 times higher than the general population prevalence of FH (1 in 311), supporting the concept that opportunistic genetic testing for FH in the ACS population has a high diagnostic yield.2,5 Our genetic yield of 8% is consistent with studies of young patients with elevated LDL-C.14 Other studies using similar inclusion criteria have reported diagnostic yields in patients with premature coronary artery disease ranging from 3.4% to 26.9%.15, 16, 17, 18, 19, 20, 21 Conversely, lower yields have been reported in cohorts with higher competing prevalence of traditional ASCVD risk factors.16,22,23

Genetic test results in ACCURATE were provided to the treating specialist physicians in <30 days in 85% of cases, establishing that genetic testing for FH is feasible within a clinically meaningful time frame. This aligns with European Society of Cardiology (ESC) and American College of Cardiology/American Heart Association (ACC/AHA) guideline recommendations to reassess LDL-C levels and optimize LLT within 4 to 8 weeks after ACS.24,25 Notably, the return of results within this early postdischarge window may also be important as long-term adherence and intensification of LLT remain suboptimal in secondary prevention, and early prescribing of combination therapy influences long-term outcomes.26, 27, 28 However, delayed report return in a small number of cases highlights an important implementation challenge, including difficulty confirming the responsible physician or follow-up appointment and no primary care physician in 4% of patients. This suggests that rapid genetic testing alone is insufficient in informing clinical decisions without a structured return-of-results pathway in post-ACS care. These findings also highlight the feasibility of implementing genomic technologies in the cardiac intensive care unit setting, which has been identified as a research priority in this population.7 It is important to note that implementation of such an approach will require formal assessment of cost-effectiveness and scalability. The economic value of FH genetic testing in this setting is likely to extend beyond the index hospitalization, as a genetically positive result could prompt targeted cascade testing of first-degree relatives, leading to initiation of LLT and prevention of cardiac events. A health technology assessment by Ontario Health found that genetic testing for individuals with a clinical diagnosis of possible FH was cost-effective compared with clinical evaluation alone.29

Genetic testing in ACCURATE led to substantial diagnostic reclassification. Indeed, 6 of 8 (75%) patients who were found to have a pathogenic or likely pathogenic FH-causing variant would not have been identified as having FH using standard clinic criteria such as the DLCNS. This suggests that genetic testing has the potential to identify patients with FH who may otherwise elude clinical detection. An additional advantage of genetic testing in the ACS population is that LDL-C levels may be depressed at the time of an ACS, which could lead to false negative clinical diagnoses but would not impact genetic diagnosis.30

Compared with phase 1, patients in phase 2 had increased persistence of statins at follow-up, a lower mean LDL-C at follow-up, and a higher proportion of patients achieved LDL-C <1.8 mmol/L. This suggests that genetic testing was associated with improvements in patient care and guideline-recommended lipid attainment. A causal relationship between genetic testing and improved patient care cannot be inferred from these findings due to the nonrandomized nature of the ACCURATE study. However, previous studies have shown genetic testing for FH led to greater LDL-C reductions at 24 weeks and were accompanied by earlier intensification of LLT after disclosure.31 It is therefore conceivable that genetic testing in ACCURATE contributed to greater rates of statin persistence and lipid target attainment. Furthermore, PCSK9i use was low overall in this cohort, despite the high-risk secondary prevention setting. This likely reflects real-world prescribing patterns in Canada, where cost, access, and reimbursement are barriers to the uptake of these agents.32 Notably, patients reclassified as definite FH in phase 2 had significantly higher PCSK9i use than those with probable, possible or unlikely FH, significantly higher use of triple therapy with a statin, ezetimibe and PCSK9i, and numerically greater use of ezetimibe. These observations are consistent with the concept that genetic testing for FH can support treatment intensification, and that the improvements in lipid control in phase 2 were driven in part by more intensive treatment of patients diagnosed with FH.

Our findings complement those of the recent MVP-ROAR (Million Veteran Program - Return of Actionable Results) randomized clinical trial, in which disclosure of FH genetic testing among an outpatient veteran population was associated with a nonsignificant greater reduction in LDL-C levels and numerically higher treatment intensification at 6 months.33 In contrast, ACCURATE enrolled a hospitalized ACS cohort with a substantially higher pretest probability of FH and demonstrated both a high diagnostic yield and improved LDL-C target attainment.

Study Limitations

There are limitations that need to be considered. First, ACCURATE used a prospective, 2-phase nonrandomized design with sequential cohorts instead of individual randomization. The differences between phases might reflect temporal trends in ACS care, clinical practice, or unmeasured confounding variables rather than the genetic testing intervention itself. For example, it is possible that more intensive LLT in phase 2 was driven by overall improvements in quality of care over time. Second, recruitment occurred during the COVID-19 pandemic and was affected by lower rates of hospital admissions and restrictions on in-patient recruitment. Third, although the small sample size and 15-month follow-up were sufficient to assess feasibility and diagnostic yield, they were underpowered for subgroup analyses (eg, age, sex, ACS type, ancestry) and clinical outcomes. The observed trends toward greater combination LLT use and less LLT deintensification in phase 2 are clinically relevant but did not reach statistical significance. Post hoc estimates suggest that 161 and 329 participants per phase would be required to detect significant differences in the endpoints of combination LLT use and LLT deintensification, respectively, with 80% power. Larger cohorts are therefore needed to confirm whether opportunistic FH genetic testing improves long-term LLT intensification and persistence after premature ACS. Fourth, lipoprotein(a) [Lp(a)] testing was not systematically performed in the ACCURATE study. In addition to monogenic FH, elevated Lp(a) is an important genetically-determined casual risk factor for premature ASCVD and can further enhance risk among patients with FH.34 Fifth, patient-reported experience measures were not collected, and future studies should assess patient and provider experience to optimize the acceptability, communication, and clinical integration of opportunistic FH genetic testing. Finally, we did not specifically evaluate cascade testing, which would be an additional benefit of genetic diagnosis. However, information provided with the genetic test reports recommended cascade testing.

Conclusions

Opportunistic genetic testing for FH during ACS is feasible and increased the diagnosis of FH (Central Illustration). Genetic testing was associated with greater use of statin therapy and combination LLT compared to standard of care, with greater LDL-C target attainment. Incorporating FH genetic testing during hospitalization for premature ACS may help to address the ongoing underdiagnosis of FH and improve long-term outcomes in this high-risk patient population.

COMPETENCY IN MEDICAL KNOWLEDGE: ACS is an important clinical setting in which FH may first manifest. In the ACCURATE study, opportunistic genetic testing for FH during hospitalization with ACS increased definite FH diagnoses, most of which would not have been classified by clinical criteria alone. Genetic testing was associated with greater LLT use and improved LDL-C target attainment at 15-month follow-up. These findings support genetic testing for young adults in acute cardiac care as a strategy to improve preventive cardiovascular medicine.

TRANSLATIONAL OUTLOOK: Genomic technologies provide the opportunity to improve rates of diagnosis and allow more refined stratification of patients. However, genomic testing has not had a major clinical impact in the cardiac intensive care unit setting to date. In this study, we showed that implementation of genetic testing in the cardiac intensive care unit setting was associated with improvements in diagnosis and treatment of FH. Future research, including larger randomized studies and formal cost-effectiveness studies, will further support the clinical implementation of genomic testing.
