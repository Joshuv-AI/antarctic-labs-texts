Fragmenting Filaments and Evolving Cores - Insights from Dust Polarisation Study of a filament in Northern Orion B Facilities: JCMT, Herschel, IRSA
Kshitiz K. Mallick, Doris Arzoumanian, Satoko Takahashi, Ray S. Furuya, Yoshiaki Misugi, Yoshito Shimajiri, Kate Pattle, Shu-ichiro Inutsuka

Abstract
We present an analysis of polarised dust emission at 850 µm for a parsec long filament in the northern part of the Orion B molecular cloud. The region was observed by the JCMT SCUBA-2/POL-2 polarimeter. The filament has a line mass ( ∼ \sim 80 M ⊙ \mathrm{M_{\odot}} pc -1 ) larger than the critical (magnetic) line mass ( ∼ \sim 37 M ⊙ \mathrm{M_{\odot}} pc -1 ); and hosts one starless, three prestellar, and four protostellar cores, with masses in the range 0.13 to 9.13 M ⊙ \mathrm{M_{\odot}} . The mean (debiased) polarisation fraction of the filament and core pixels was calculated to be 5.3 ± \pm 0.3% and 3.2 ± \pm 0.3%, respectively, likely reflecting their distinct physical conditions. The polarisation fraction for the cores does not depend on the type of core, and was found to decrease with increasing column density, varying from 6-11% at the filament edges to 1 − 0.1 + 0.7 {}^{+0.7}_{-0.1} % in the denser parts ( N H2 \mathrm{N_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{H2}}}} ≳ \gtrsim 2 × \times 10 22 cm -2 ). Magnetic field orientation of the protostellar cores, in contrast to prestellar cores, appears to be relatively aligned with the magnetic field orientation of the local filament in this region. Using the Davis-Chandrasekhar-Fermi formalism the plane-of-sky magnetic field strength for the protostellar cores ( ∼ \sim 39-110 µ G) was found to be higher than that of the prestellar cores ( ∼ \sim 22-61 µ G); and weakest for the starless core ( ∼ \sim 6 µ G). The average value for the filament was found to be ∼ \sim 31 µ G. The magnetic field-volume density relation for the prestellar/starless cores and protostellar cores suggests a transition from weak field case to strong field case as the cores evolve from prestellar to protostellar phase.

I
Introduction

Star formation is a complex phenomena requiring simultaneous examination of
multiple competing processes such as gravity, turbulence, and magnetism

(
35
;
53
;
23
;
60
)
.
While gravity and turbulence have been studied to a significant degree

(
34
)
, investigation of magnetic field is work in progress

(
71
)
, driven over the past decade by developments
in instrumentation
(
72
)
,
enabling polarisation measurements to catch up with photometric and
spectroscopic observations.
Observations by
Planck

(
79
)
have already shown the
ubiquitous presence of (dust) polarised emission throughout the Galaxy,
indicating the magnetized nature of the interstellar medium (ISM).
The first results from
Planck
demonstrated the close relationship
between the magnetic field structure and the density structures, suggesting
the crucial role of the magnetic fields in regulating the dynamics of the ISM

(
78
;
81
;
84
;
80
)
.

Analysis of magnetic field associated with Galactic star formation requires
observations of cold molecular clouds as well as structures present therein,
such as filaments, hubs, ridges
(
68
;
31
)
,
with scales ranging from a few parsec to tenths of parsec

(
45
;
71
)
.
Examination of filaments especially assumes relevance as
Herschel

far-infrared (FIR) observations of dust emission have revealed the ISM
to be threaded by filamentary structures
(
2
)
.
Filaments have also been hypothesised to be an intermediate step in the
collapse of molecular clouds towards dense cores

(
40
)
, and their importance in star formation is
borne out by the significant number of cores found associated with them

(
3
;
46
;
48
)
.
The latter suggests that filament evolution may be significantly
intertwined with the initial conditions of star formation.
Hence, it is imperative to study magnetic fields associated with filaments,
particularly those hosting dense cores, in order to understand their dynamical
evolution and fragmentation into cores.

In this paper, we have carried out a polarisation study of a relatively
secluded filament

(
9
)
,
unassociated with the main molecular complex, in the northern part of the

Orion B (catalog )
molecular cloud complex.
The location is associated with the
Planck
Galactic Cold Clump
(PGCC)
G203.21-11.20 (catalog )

(
83
;
82
)
,
and has only been a part of a few statistical studies of Orion at FIR and
submillimeter wavelengths. Observations by JCMT (James Clerk Maxwell Telescope)
at 850
µm

(
99
)

found four cores associated with the PGCC, named as

G203.21-11.20E1 (catalog )
,

G203.21-11.20E2 (catalog )
,

G203.21-11.20W1 (catalog )
, and

G203.21-11.20W2 (catalog )
.
According to ALMA observations, G203.21-11.20W2 has been classified as a hot
corino
(
55
;
39
)
.
Dense gas tracers such as N
2
H
+

+
, H
13
CO
+
, and HCN

(
98
;
100
)
, and isotopologues of CO

(

have also been detected at the location of

PGCC G203.21-11.20 (catalog )
.
Despite the above, while the Orion B cloud complex is a well-investigated
high-mass star forming region
(
10
)
, this particular
filament shows a lack of investigation in literature, lacking even
observations in
Spitzer

(
94
)
.
More recently, the filament was found to harbour multiple cores in the
Herschel Gould Belt Survey

(
3
;
46
, HGBS,)
.
The core masses from the HGBS catalog
(for the filamentary region studied in this paper)
cover nearly two orders of magnitude, ranging from 0.13-9.13
\mathrm{M_{\odot}}
.
Thus this filament presents a suitable location to
study the magnetic field structure across a filament fragmenting into
cores at different evolutionary stages.
The above mentioned molecular
tracer studies show the systemic velocity of the PGCC associated with the
filament to be
\sim
10
\mathrm{km\,s^{-\!1}}
, similar to the rest of the Orion B complex

(
75
;
29
)
,
and thus we adopt a distance of 400 pc usually used for Orion B and also
used in the HGBS study
(

We present the 850

dust polarisation observation carried out
using POL-2 polarimeter on Submillimeter Common-User Bolometer Array 2

(

The thermal dust emission at FIR is theorised to be linearly polarised

alignment of dust grains with their major axis perpendicular to the field

93

92

The magnetic field properties can be thus inferred from such observations for

III
Polarisation Results

III.1
Calculation of Polarisation Parameters

Using the stokes I, Q, and U data, and their respective variances
(Section
II.1
), the observed polarisation
parameters – polarisation intensity (
\emph{PI}
), polarisation fraction (
\emph{PF}
), and
the polarisation angle (
\Theta
) are calculated using the following expressions :

\displaystyle\emph{PI}=

\displaystyle\sqrt{Q^{2}+U^{2}}

(1)

\displaystyle\emph{PF}=

\displaystyle\frac{\emph{PI}}{I}

(2)

\displaystyle\Theta=

\displaystyle\mathrm{\frac{1}{2}~tan^{-1}}\frac{U}{Q}

(3)

While U and Q are scalars, for
\Theta
to match with the sign convention of
the polarised waves

(
14
;
32
;
96
)
,
the quantity (U/Q) is not treated as a pure ratio, but the signs are taken
into account to map the angle in the
“correct quadrant”.
The polarisation angles lie in the range (-90,90) degrees. It
should be noted that according to the IAU convention, angles are measured east
of north. In the FIR regime, the orientation of the plane-of-sky magnetic field
(
\theta
) is subsequently obtained by rotating
\Theta
by 90°

(
79
;
36
)
, i.e.

\theta=\Theta+\pi/2.

(4)

To account for the positive bias of the polarisation measurements

(
91
)
, we calculated the debiased polarisation
intensity and polarisation fraction as follows :

\displaystyle PI_{deb}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sqrt{Q^{2}+U^{2}-0.5\,(\sigma_{{}_{Q}}^{2}+\sigma_{{}_{U}}^{2})},

(5)

\displaystyle PF_{deb}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{PI_{deb}}{I}.

(6)

Error propagation yields the following expressions for the uncertainties :

\sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle\theta}=\sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle\Theta}=\frac{1}{2}\frac{\sqrt{Q^{2}\sigma_{{}_{U}}^{2}+U^{2}\sigma_{{}_{Q}}^{2}}}{Q^{2}+U^{2}}

(7)

\sigma_{{}_{PI_{deb}}}=\frac{\sqrt{Q^{2}\sigma_{{}_{Q}}^{2}+U^{2}\sigma_{{}_{U}}^{2}}}{PI_{deb}}

(8)

\sigma_{{}_{PF_{deb}}}=PF_{deb}\sqrt{\frac{\sigma_{{}_{PI_{deb}}}^{2}}{PI_{deb}^{2}}+\frac{\sigma_{{}_{I}}^{2}}{I^{2}}}

(9)

Having obtained a map of
PI_{deb}
,
PF_{deb}
, and
\theta
, the subsequent task is to
discern reliable measurements for further analysis. Towards this end, we use
stokes I and
PF_{deb}
. Since pixels for which
PF_{deb}
>
20% are likely
unrealistic
(
64
;
12
;
79
)
,
we confine our pool of usable pixels to those with
PF_{deb}
\leq
20%.

PF_{deb}
for
the region was found to decline with increasing SNR (signal-to-noise
ratio), and hence we choose a liberal criteria of SNR(
PF_{deb}
)
\geq
1 for
selecting our pixels
(we use the notation SNR(
PF_{deb}
)
\equiv
PF_{deb}
/
\sigma_{PF_{deb}}
).
The validity of this selection criteria is discussed in
Appendix
B
.
All such usable pixels were found to have a high SNR in I – SNR(I)
\geq
9 –
hence no further trimming on the basis of SNR of stokes I was deemed necessary.

To summarise, we obtain 198 pixels
(pixel size
\sim
12″, beam size
\sim
14″)
in our region of interest which satisfy the combined criteria :

PF_{deb}
\leq
20%, SNR(I)
\geq
9, and SNR(
PF_{deb}
)
\geq
1.

III.5
Polarisation Fraction vs. Total Intensity

The relationship between the polarisation fraction and total intensity is used
to assess the dust grain alignment efficiency
with respect to
the visual extinction
or optical depth. As per the theory of radiative torque alignment

(
51
;
37
;
95
;
42
)
,
dust grain alignment depends on the
wavelength-to-grain size ratio. In regions of high dust extinction, short
wavelength radiation is attenuated leading to a loss in alignment of smaller
grains. The polarisation fraction serves as an indicator of the dust grain
alignment efficiency, with total intensity I acting as a proxy for visual
extinction. This relationship typically shows a power-law dependence of the
form
\emph{PF}\propto I^{-\alpha}
, where
0\leq\alpha\leq 1
.
The limit
\alpha=0
gives
\emph{PI}\propto I
, which implies that all grains
are equally aligned along each line of sight resulting in a constant
polarisation fraction.
The other limit,
\alpha=1
, implies a constant polarisation intensity
irrespective of the total intensity. Such a case has been interpreted to arise
due to polarised emission from a thin layer of low density material on the
surface of the cloud, overlaying an unpolarised interior

(
74
;
50
)
. The
intermediate values of
\alpha
imply that polarisation fraction decreases with
increasing intensity and thus signals depolarisation.
This empirical relation is referred to as
“polarization hole”  and among its
possible causes are :
loss in grain alignment at high extinction,
complex field geometry within the telescope beam being integrated over
(“field tangling”)
in the line of sight (LOS), and dust grain
destruction in the vicinity of protostars
(
71
)
.

For the calculation of
\alpha
, we use the Ricean Mean Model method of

74

(
73
, also see appendix of)
. In this method, the
relation between the polarisation fraction and the intensity is
given by :

\emph{PF}=\emph{PF}_{\sigma_{\!{}_{\mathrm{QU}}}}\left(\frac{I}{\sigma_{\!{}_{\mathrm{QU}}}}\right)^{-\alpha},

(10)

where,

\sigma_{\!{}_{\mathrm{QU}}}
is the rms noise level of the data;

\emph{PF}
{}_{\sigma_{\!{}_{\mathrm{QU}}}}
is the polarisation fraction at this noise level of the
data (i.e.
\emph{PF}
(I=
\sigma_{\!{}_{\mathrm{QU}}}
)=
\emph{PF}
{}_{\sigma_{\!{}_{\mathrm{QU}}}}
); and

\alpha
is the power law index in the range
0\leq\alpha\leq 1
.
For a region with N pixels,
\sigma_{\!{}_{\mathrm{QU}}}
can be obtained as follows :

\sigma_{\!{}_{\mathrm{QU}}}=\frac{1}{2N}\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(\sqrt{\sigma_{\!{}_{\mathrm{Q,i}}}^{2}}+\sqrt{\sigma_{\!{}_{\mathrm{U,i}}}^{2}}\right),

(11)

where
\sigma_{\!{}_{\mathrm{Q,i}}}^{2}
and
\sigma_{\!{}_{\mathrm{U,i}}}^{2}
represent the variances of the
i-th

pixel.
\sigma_{\!{}_{\mathrm{QU}}}
is thus a property of the dataset, and the aim is to obtain

\emph{PF}
{}_{\sigma_{\!{}_{\mathrm{QU}}}}
and
\alpha
.

Since polarisation fractions are Rice distributed, the mean of the Rice
distribution gives us
(see
74
, for details)
:

\emph{PF}=\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2}}\!\left(\!\frac{I}{\sigma_{\!{}_{\mathrm{QU}}}}\!\right)^{\!-\!1}\!\mathcal{L}_{{}_{\frac{1}{2}}}\!\!\left(\frac{-\emph{PF}_{\sigma_{\!{}_{\mathrm{QU}}}}^{\,2}}{2}\!\left(\!\frac{I}{\sigma_{\!{}_{\mathrm{QU}}}}\!\right)^{\!2(1-\alpha)}\right),

(12)

where
\mathcal{L}_{1/2}
is the Laguerre polynomial of order 1/2. Apart from
equation
10
, the above equation also assumes

\sigma_{\!{}_{\emph{PF}}}
=
\sigma_{\!{}_{\mathrm{QU}}}
/I.
We fit Equation
12
using the observed (i.e.
non-debiased) polarisation fraction and the total intensity I, keeping

\emph{PF}
{}_{\sigma_{\!{}_{\mathrm{QU}}}}
and
\alpha
as free parameters. This fit was carried out
using all the pixels in our region of analysis, i.e. no trimming of dataset
was carried out on the basis of SNR criteria on I or
\emph{PF}
.
Only
\emph{PF}

\leq
20%
was applied, as pixels with
\emph{PF}

>
20% are likely unphysical (see
Section
III.1
).
The fitting was carried out using the
scipy
routine
curve_fit
.
The error in
\emph{PF}
was also taken into account for the fit.
Figure
6
shows the model fit for the filament and the
extended-core regions separately. Solid green line denotes the Ricean Mean
Model fit. The obtained parameters are given in Table
2
.

To compare with analyses in literature,
the figures also show
the null hypothesis (dotted line), and
the single-power law (blue dashed line).
For the null hypothesis,
\emph{PF}
{}_{\sigma_{\!{}_{\mathrm{QU}}}}
=0, and thus

\emph{PF}

=\sqrt{\pi/2}\left(I/\sigma_{\!{}_{\mathrm{QU}}}\right)^{-1}
for all I.

The single-power law is fit is carried out using
PF_{deb}
and using only the
pixels which satisfy a high SNR criteria. A high SNR criteria is used as
the polarisation fraction tends to a Gaussian distribution in the high SNR
limit, and in a low SNR limit, the power-law index of
-
1 will always be
obtained.
Debiased values are used as not doing so would artificially inflate
\alpha
.
We refer to
74
for a detailed discussion of
the above.
The fit is calculated using the equation

(
74
)
:

PF_{deb}=PF_{\sigma_{\!{}_{\mathrm{QU}}}}\left(\frac{I}{\sigma_{\!{}_{\mathrm{QU}}}}\right)^{-\alpha}.

(13)

Only the pixels which satisfied the (commonly taken) high SNR criteria –
SNR(
PF_{deb}
)
>
3, SNR(I)
>
10, and
\sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle PF_{deb}}\!<5\%

– were utilised.

The reduced
\chi^{2}
for is calculated as follows (given
n
datapoints
used for fitting) for the two models :

\displaystyle\chi^{2}_{\mathrm{reduced}}=\frac{\chi^{2}}{n-2}=\frac{\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\frac{\emph{PF}_{model}-\emph{PF}_{i}}{\sigma_{{}_{\emph{PF}_{i}}}}\right)^{2}}{n-2}.

(14)

The same has also been reported in Table
2
to assess
the goodness-of-fit for each curve.
The reduced
\chi^{2}
for the null hypothesis is given by

\chi^{2}_{\mathrm{reduced}}=\chi^{2}/n
, and it was calculated using
the same pixels and values as for the Ricean Mean Model (i.e. observed).

III.6
Histogram of Relative Orientations

The HRO (Histogram of Relative Orientations) is used to assess the relative
orientation of the magnetic field vectors with respect to the column density
structures
(
89
)
. We adopt the steps for calculation from

80
. A brief description of the procedure follows.
The angle between the tangent to the iso-column density contours and the

\mathrm{B_{pos}}
vector can be given by :

\displaystyle\phi

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle arctan\frac{|\bm{\nabla\mathrm{N_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{H2}}}}}\times\bm{E}|}{\bm{\nabla\mathrm{N_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{H2}}}}}.\bm{E}}

(15)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle arctan\frac{|\bm{\nabla\mathrm{N_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{H2}}}}}||\bm{E}|sin\phi}{|\bm{\nabla\mathrm{N_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{H2}}}}}||\bm{E}|cos\phi},

(16)

where,

\bm{\nabla\mathrm{N_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{H2}}}}}
is the gradient of the column density map, and
\bm{E}

is the polarisation vector.
The gradient is perpendicular to the iso-
\mathrm{N_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{H2}}}}
contours and the polarisation
vector (
\bm{E}
) is perpendicular to the
\mathrm{B_{pos}}
vector in FIR. Hence
\phi

– the angular difference between the gradient and the polarisation –
gives us the angular difference of the tangent to the column density contour
and magnetic field vector.
The gradient is given by the following convolution operation
(denoted by
\otimes
) :

\displaystyle\bm{\nabla\mathrm{N_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{H2}}}}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(G_{x}^{l}\otimes\mathrm{N_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{H2}}}})\bm{\hat{x}}+(G_{y}^{l}\otimes\mathrm{N_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{H2}}}})\bm{\hat{y}}

(17)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle g_{x}\bm{\hat{x}}+g_{y}\bm{\hat{y}},

(18)

where, G
{}_{x}^{l}
and G
{}_{y}^{l}
are the gaussian derivative kernels (of kernel size

l\times l
) along the x and y-axis, respectively. The orientation angle of
the gradient is then given by :

\psi=arctan(-g_{x}/g_{y}).

(19)

Application of the convolution operation given in Equation
17
on
the column density map gives us the (pixel-by-pixel) x- and y-components of the
gradient map, which are then used to obtain a map of the gradient angle

\psi
using Equation
19
. We utilized the
scipy
task

gaussian_filter
with a kernel size of 3 pixel
\times
3 pixel for
our purpose.
The pixel-by-pixel (relative) difference of the gradient angle map (
\psi
)
and the polarisation angle map (
\theta
) gives us relative orientation
(
\phi
) map.
Due to the half-vector nature of the polarisation vector, the difference
between the gradient and the polarisation
angle has to be mapped to a 0-90°  interval. Also, since we are only
concerned with the relative orientation between the polarisation (half-)vector
and the gradient vector,
\phi
is physically equivalent to
-\phi
, and the
angular difference can be mapped to (0,90) rather than (-90,90) without any
loss of generality
(
88
)
. For example,
\phi=0
indicates
that the magnetic field is parallel to the (tangent of the) column density
contour, and
\phi=\pm 90
indicates that it is perpendicular.
The pixel-by-pixel map of
\phi
is then used to construct the histogram.

Figure
7
shows the HROs for the filament (in blue) and the
extended-cores (in red). The HRO for all the pixels is also shown by dashed
black line.
Error
\sigma_{k}
for the
k-th
bin was calculated using the following

(
80
)
:

\displaystyle\sigma_{k}^{2}=h_{k}\left(1-\frac{h_{k}}{h_{tot}}\right),

(20)

where
h_{k}
is the count of the
k-th
bin, and
h_{tot}
is the total
count.

The HRO shows two peaks for the overall region - first peak in the
22.5 to 33.75 degree bin and a second peak in the 45 to 56.25 degree bin.
The second peak is clearly due to the orientation of the filament pixels,
as is also seen in the blue histogram. The first peak appears to be due to
the combined effect of core and filament pixels, and thus it can be surmised
that core pixels are partly aligned with the filament pixels.

III.7
Magnetic Field using DCF

III.7.1
Calculation

We utilize the widely used Davis-Chandrasekhar-Fermi (DCF) formalism

(
21
;
13
)
to calculate the magnetic field
strength in the extended-core regions and the associated neighbourhoods.
The DCF method works on the assumption that over and
above the underlying large-scale mean field direction, there are perturbations
in the magnetic field on small-scale due to the non-thermal motions of the gas.
The turbulence generated by this non-thermal motion is Alfvenic in nature.
The dispersion in the deviation of polarisation angles from the mean field
direction can be used to calculate the plane-of-sky magnetic field (
\mathrm{B_{pos}}
)
using the formula
(
17
)
:

\mathrm{B}_{\mathrm{pos}}^{\textsc{dcf}}\approx 9.3\,\frac{\sqrt{\mathrm{n_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{H2}}}}}~~\Delta\!\mathrm{V_{NT}}}{\delta\theta}

(21)

where

\mathrm{n_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{H2}}}}
is the number density of hydrogen molecules (in cm
-3
),

\Delta\!\mathrm{V_{NT}}
is the linewidth (
fwhm
) of non-thermal
velocity component (in
\mathrm{km\,s^{-\!1}}
), and

\delta\theta
is the dispersion in polarisation angles (in degrees).
The resultant
\mathrm{B}_{\mathrm{pos}}^{\textsc{dcf}}
is in microgauss.

The non-thermal velocity linewidth is calculated using the expression

(
28
;
26
)
:

\Delta\!\mathrm{V_{NT}}=\sqrt{(8\,ln2)\times(\sigma_{tot}^{2}-c_{s}^{2})}

(22)

where
\sigma_{tot}
is the total velocity dispersion,
c
s
(
=\sqrt{kT/\bar{m}}
) is the speed of sound,
k is the Boltzmann constant,
T is the temperature, and

\bar{m}
is the mean molecular weight of the medium.

Taking T as 10 K and
\bar{m}
as 2.33 amu
(
44
)
, we
calculated the sound speed to be
\sim
0.19
\mathrm{km\,s^{-\!1}}
.

\sigma_{tot}
was calculated using the
\mathrm{N_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{H2}}}}
vs.
\sigma_{tot}
plot from

7
.

\Delta\!\mathrm{V_{NT}}
was found to lie in the range 0.2-0.7
\mathrm{km\,s^{-\!1}}
,
and seems to be consistent with other core and filament studies in
literature

(
49
;
50
;
87
;
97
)
.
The number density (
\mathrm{n_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{H2}}}}
) was calculated by dividing the column density
value by
W_{fil}=
0.1 pc, which has been measured to be the width of
the filament

(
9
;
5
;
4
)
.
Thus, for each of the extended-core regions, we first obtained the mean

\mathrm{N_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{H2}}}}
using their constituent pixels in the column density map, which
was then used to calculate
\mathrm{n_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{H2}}}}
and
\Delta\!\mathrm{V_{NT}}
.
Archival N
2
H
+
(J=1-0) observations of
PGCC G203.21-11.20 (catalog )

(
98
)
, which
encompass core 3 to 6 regions, show the non-thermal linewidth to be in the
range
\sim
0.6-0.7
\mathrm{km\,s^{-\!1}}
. Thus our estimates (for these four cores) are not
only consistent with observations, but also the error in

\Delta\!\mathrm{V_{NT}}
considered for further calculation
(
\sim
0.12
\mathrm{km\,s^{-\!1}}
, see Appendix
D.2
)
takes into account the range of variation.
For angular dispersion, the circular standard deviation of the pixel angles
was used as a measure.

Subsequently, for each of the extended-cores in Figure

III.7.3
Sources of Uncertainty in the DCF method

The classical DCF method is expected to give reliable estimations of the
magnetic field for
\delta\theta<
25°

(
70
;
19
)
. Though this is not so for
cores 3, 4, 7, and 8; the departure from 25°  is significant
for 3 and 8, making their magnetic field strength estimations less robust
than that for the rest of the core regions.
Similarly, for the neighbourhoods, except for core 6, all others were found
to possess large angular dispersions.

The DCF method assumes an equipartition
of the turbulent kinetic energy and the turbulent magnetic energy.
While the assumption is expected to be satisfied in the sub-Alfvenic regions;
for
super-Alfvenic regions, the magnetic field perturbations cannot keep up with
the strong turbulent motions and the assumption breaks down

(
25
)
. Using DCF in such a case would lead to
an overestimation of the magnetic field strength

(
57
;
58
)
. According to our calculation later
in the paper (in Section
IV.2.2
and
Table
3
), cores 3 and 4 are likely to be super-Alfvenic,
and hence their field strengths could be overestimated.

Finally, we note that the efficacy of DCF for estimating field strengths of
protostellar objects could be affected by phenomena such as outflows and
gravitational collapse. However, any detailed modelling to quantify these
effects is outside the scope of this paper.

III.8
Magnetic Field assuming compressible ISM

The classical DCF method has the assumption of incompressible fluids
in its calculation. However, the ISM is compressible and hence the field
strength should be calculated using compressible turbulence.
While DCF (Section
III.7
) is the mainstay for
further calculations in the paper, we estimate the field strength assuming
compressible turbulence for comparison.
We use the method provided by
85
for this purpose.
The following expression from
16
was used to calculate
the magnetic field :

\mathrm{B}_{\mathrm{pos}}^{\textsc{st}}\approx 1.76\,\frac{\sqrt{\mathrm{n_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{H2}}}}}~~\Delta\!\mathrm{V_{NT}}}{\sqrt{\delta\theta}}

(23)

where the symbols on the right hand side retain the same meaning as in Equation

21
, and
\mathrm{B}_{\mathrm{pos}}^{\textsc{st}}
is the field strength in microgauss.
The values for the extended-cores and their neighbourhoods are
also listed in Table
1
. While the change
in
\mathrm{B_{pos}}
is well within the error limits, no clear pattern can be discerned
whether the
85
method leads to a larger or smaller
field strength overall.

Figure
8
shows the field strength
variation with column density. Despite a quantitative change in strengths,
the figure shows that that the relative trend between the cores remains almost
same.
In both the low-density (
\sim
0.6-1
\times
10
22
cm
-2
) and
high-density regimes (
\sim
2.5-3
\times
10

the increase in
\mathrm{B_{pos}}
is less steep than that for DCF method.
In the intermediate range (
\sim

\times

cm
-2
), the field

is a increase in
\mathrm{B_{pos}}
with
\mathrm{N_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{H2}}}}
.
This method also contains uncertainties, and we refer to

58

discussion on the method and its shortcomings.

following expression

(

\small M^{B}_{{}_{\begin{subarray}{c}line,\\
crit\end{subarray}}}\!\!\simeq\!\left[\!2.24\!\left(\frac{R_{o}}{0.05\,\mathrm{pc}}\right)\left(\!\frac{B_{o}}{10\,\mu G}\!\right)\!\right]\mathrm{M_{\odot}}\mathrm{pc}^{-1}+1.66\frac{\sigma_{{tot}}^{2}}{G}

(24)

where
M
{}^{B}_{line,crit}

\mathrm{M_{\odot}}
pc
-1

R

is the radius of the filament;
B

is the magnetic field in the filament; and

\sigma_{{tot}}

Since the width of the filament is taken as 0.1 pc, hence we take
R
o
as 0.05 pc. We take B
o
to be the average of

\mathrm{B_{pos}}
values for the neighbourhoods of the cores
(B
{}^{\textsc{dcf}}_{pos,ngbr}
in
Table
1
), which comes out to be

\sim
31.2
µ
G.
Using the mean
\mathrm{N_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{H2}}}}
of the contiguous filamentary structure detected
above 5
\sigma
in stokes I image
(see Appendix
C
and Figure
11
),

\sigma_{{tot}}
was determined to be
\sim
0.28
\mathrm{km\,s^{-\!1}}
from the

\mathrm{N_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{H2}}}}
vs.
\sigma_{tot}
relation of
7
.
Thus, M
{}^{B}_{line,crit}
for the filament is calculated to be

\sim
37
\mathrm{M_{\odot}}
pc
-1
.
Taking the transverse line profile of the filament along the north-south
direction in the column density map, we obtained the transverse extent of
the filament, and subsequently the total mass of the filament as

\sim
100
\mathrm{M_{\odot}}
. The resulting line mass, taking the length to be

\sim

\mathrm{M_{\odot}}
pc
-1

is thermally and magnetically supercritical.

IV.2.1
Mass-to-Flux Ratio

The mass (M) to flux (
\Phi
) ratio (
\lambda
) in terms of critical value of
(1/2
\pi\sqrt{G}
) is given by

(
69
;
17
)
:

\displaystyle\lambda_{obs}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 7.6\times 10^{-21}\left(\frac{\mathrm{N_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{H2}}}}}{B}\right),

(25)

where,

\mathrm{N_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{H2}}}}
is the column density of the core (in cm
-2
) from the HGBS
catalog, and
B is the magnetic field (in
µ
G)
calculated in Section
III.7
.

We calculate
\lambda_{obs}
assuming B=
\mathrm{B_{pos}}
and using both

\mathrm{B}_{\mathrm{pos}}^{\textsc{dcf}}
and
\mathrm{B}_{\mathrm{pos}}^{\textsc{st}}
.
The results are given in Table
3
.
While the protostellar cores (2, 5, 6, and 7) span the domain from
subcritical (core 2,
\lambda_{obs}<1
) to
transcritical (core 5 and 7,
1\lesssim\lambda_{obs}\lesssim 2
) to
supercritical (core 6,
\lambda_{obs}\gtrsim 2
);
all the prestellar cores (1, 3, and 4) are found to be supercritical; and
the sole starless core (core 8) is subcritical.
A transcritical or supercritical core implies that magnetic field support
is not sufficient to resist against gravity.

If one were to utilise the total field strength
|\textbf{B}|
instead of

\mathrm{B_{pos}}
– given by

|

|
=(
4/\pi
)
\mathrm{B_{pos}}
(
17
)
–
in Equation
25
,
as well as apply the statistical correction due to the orientation of the

\lambda=\lambda_{obs}/3

(
17
)
,

The prestellar cores 3 and 4 can be said to be transcritical in such a case,

\lambda^{\textsc{dcf}}\sim 1.2
and
\sim 0.9
, respectively.

IV.2.2
Energy Balance

In this section, we look at the different energy terms. It should be noted that
the energy values calculated here contain significant uncertainties, and should
be treated as a representative of their order of magnitude.
The Alfven velocity of each of the cores was calculated using the following
expression (in cgs system) :

V_{A}=B/\sqrt{4\pi\rho}=B/\sqrt{4\pi(\text{\textmu}_{\mathrm{H2}}\mathrm{m_{H}}\mathrm{n_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{H2}}}})},

(26)

where

\text{\textmu}_{\mathrm{H2}}
(=2.8) is the molecular weight per hydrogen molecule

(
44
)
,

\mathrm{m_{H}}
is the mass of hydrogen atom,
B is the magnetic field strength in Gauss,

\rho
is the density (in g cm
-3
), and

\mathrm{n_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{H2}}}}
is the volume number density of the region.
Subsequently, the Alfvenic Mach number is given by :

M_{A}=\sigma_{NT}/V_{A},

(27)

where
\sigma_{NT}
for each core is that calculated
in Section
III.7
.
The magnetic energy term can be then obtained as:

\displaystyle E_{B}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{B^{2}}{8\pi}\,V=\frac{1}{2}mV_{A}^{2},

(28)

where,
B and V are the magnetic field strength and volume (of the core) in cgs
units, respectively; and

m
is the mass of the core.
We use the right hand side expression with V
A
for calculation.
The total kinetic energy (KE) term (E
KE
) can be expressed as a sum of
thermal KE (E
{}_{{}_{KE,T}}
) and non-thermal KE (E
{}_{{}_{KE,NT}}
) as :

\displaystyle E_{{}_{KE}}\!=\!\frac{3}{2}m\,\sigma_{{}_{total}}^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{3}{2}m\,c_{s}^{2}\!+\!\frac{3}{2}m\,\sigma_{{}_{NT}}^{2}

(29)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle E_{{}_{KE,T}}\!+\!E_{{}_{KE,NT}}.

(30)

Assuming the cores to be spheres with effective radius R
(‘observed core radius’  from the HGBS catalog),
the gravitational (potential) energy (E
G
) is :

\displaystyle E_{G}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{3}{5}\frac{G\,m^{2}}{R},

(31)

where,
G is the gravitational constant.
The computed values for the respective cores are given in
Table
3
.

V
Discussion

Our analysis of the region reveals the complex nature of magnetic fields which
thread this filamentary cloud region, and affect its dynamics as well as of the
cores embedded therein.

V.1
The fragmenting filament

The approximately 1.25 pc long filamentary structure displays a line mass
(
\sim
80
\mathrm{M_{\odot}}
pc
-1
) much larger than the critical magnetic line mass
(
\sim
37
\mathrm{M_{\odot}}
pc
-1
) and is thus expected to be in a fragmentation
and collapse stage.
The presence of cores at different evolutionary stages probably attest to this
fact.

The magnetic field half-vectors from
Planck
show their orientation
to be perpendicular to the filament’s overall longitudinal direction
(see Figures
1
and
2
). This is
in line with observations in literature wherein
\mathrm{B_{pos}}
has been mostly found
perpendicular to high-density supercritical filaments

(
76
;
78
)
.
Albeit at low-resolution, the vectors do not show any
reorientation of
\mathrm{B_{pos}}
from perpendicular to parallel

(
30
, ‘U-shaped’  morphology,)

as density increases at the filament crest.
Even the JCMT vectors give no such indication.
Three out of four Cores in the dense central part of the filament – namely
cores 3, 4, and 6 – show their constituent pixels’ JCMT vectors
(Figure
2
) to be mostly aligned with the
Planck

vectors, i.e. perpendicular to the filament’s overall longitudinal direction.
This could indicate no longitudinal flow of material along
the filament to its “high-density”  part.
We add the caveat that the vectors in core 5 could be partially aligned with
the filament crest orientation locally
(see Figures
11
, and
12
).
The overall magnetic field orientation of the cores with respect to the
filament crest does not appear to show any particular trend
(see Figure
12
).
As such, it seems to agree with simulations which suggest
random orientation, positing that the randomness of the magnetic fields in
the cores is inherited from the transonic velocity fluctuations of their
host magnetized filaments during the fragmentation

(
63
;
62
)
.

According to evolutionary stage, the filamentary cloud consists of
1 starless core, 3 prestellar cores, and 4 protostellar cores.
Except for core 1, the rest (cores 2 to 8) lie along the contiguous
filamentary structure.
The prestellar cores 3 and 4, and the starless core 8 appear to have their
major axis aligned along the filament longitudinal axis. This is also borne
out by simulations wherein the cores formed along a fragmenting filament have
ellipsoidal shapes with longer axis aligned with filament axis

(
63
)
.
Protostellar core 5 has no 70
µm
detection according to the HGBS catalog,
unlike other protostellar cores; yet is associated with an outflow according
to ALMA observations. Hence it is likely the youngest among protostellar cores
in this filamentary region. Core 5 is also the location of highest column
density in the region, albeit only marginally larger than that for
(protostellar) core 6

(
55
, identified as a hot corino in literature;)
.
For core 6, the outflow

(from
55
, position angle = 149.1 deg)

does not appear to be aligned with either the orientation of the core
(
\sim
16 deg) or
\mathrm{B_{pos}}
(
\sim
18
\pm
2 deg)
(see Figure
2
and Table
1
).

The starless and prestellar cores – i.e. cores 1, 3, 4, and 8 – have larger
angular dispersions than protostellar cores (
\delta\theta_{\mathrm{extCore}}

in Table
1
). This is likely so as the field lines are
too weak to resist the effect of turbulence in younger stages, and as the
gravitational collapse proceeds, the field lines become more ordered due to
the dissipation of turbulence
(
19
)
. This can be clearly
seen on a visual examination of the field lines as well
(see Figure
2
).

V.2
Depolarisation in the cores

Examination of polarisation parameters for the filament and the core regions
shows a smaller polarisation fraction for the cores. The polarisation fraction
as a function of intensity is given by
\emph{PF}\propto I^{-\alpha}
, wherein we
calculated the value of
\alpha
for the
filament (0.71
\pm
0.08) and the cores (0.85
\pm
0.10)
separately by applying the Ricean Mean Model (Section
III.5
).
The
\alpha
values, along with the smaller polarisation fraction of the
cores, suggest a combination of loss of grain alignment and change in
structure of the field between the filament and the cores.

Looking at only the core values, we find that the polarisation
parameters for the protostellar cores 5, 6, and 7 are similar to that
of their respective neighbourhoods, in contrast to prestellar cores
(Figure
5
).
This suggests that among the possible causes of depolarisation
– namely, loss in grain alignment, dust grain destruction in the vicinity
of protostars, and integration over field geometry along the LOS

(
71
)
–
the latter might not be the cause for
depolarisation in the protostellar cores.
For this, we refer to the
“two-component model along the LOS”
from
81
.
According to this model, the depolarisation factor due to the rotation of

\mathrm{B_{pos}}
is
\sim
0.9-1 for a small angle difference from the background.
Hence it is reasonable to conclude that the depolarisation for the
protostellar cores (5, 6, and 7) is likely due to the loss in grain alignment
and/or dust grain destruction in the vicinity of these protostars.

V.3
Transition from weak to strong field with core evolution ?

The B-n relation for the cores shows a clear distinction
between the power law indexes for the prestellar/starless cores and the
protostellar cores (Figure
9
).
The index for the prestellar/starless cores
(
\kappa

\sim
0.66
\pm
0.09)
indicates a weak field case.
The value is consistent with that found by
18
using
Zeeman
observations (
\kappa\approx 0.65
), as well as the index calculated by

66
(0.66
\pm
0.05) using a literature-compiled
sample of 17 dense cores (using DCF method).
Though it should be noted that
66
conclude from
their
analysis that a weak field is not a requisite for their calculated relation,
and the relation can be statistically explained on the basis of distribution
of mass and volume density for a sample of approximately magnetically
critical cores which are strongly bound and spheroidal with a large range
of radii.

On the other hand, the more evolved protostellar cores
(
\kappa

\sim
0.42
\pm
0.12)
appear to be consistent with the strong field case.
The overall value (
\kappa

\sim
0.55
\pm
0.15) lies between the two limits,
and is very similar to the index of 0.57 found by
56

using (recalculated) previous polarised dust observations of a sample at
clump and core scales (using DCF).

This analysis indicates a possible scenario wherein the prestellar/starless
core stage could be dominated by weak field case, and there is a
‘switch’  to strong field domain as the core
moves along its evolutionary path to protostellar stage.
Supporting evidence for this is also
provided by our mass to flux ratio (
\lambda
) values
(Table
3
).
According to
19
,
\lambda
for the cores in the
strong field case is
\sim
1 or only slightly
>
1.
But for the weak field case, the region is supercritical, and
\lambda
may
take any value
>
1. That we see
\lambda^{\textsc{dcf}}_{obs}
for
prestellar cores larger than the protostellar cores on an average in
Table
3
could be a reflection of this.
Moreover, if corrections for total magnetic field and orientation are applied
(Section
IV.2.1
), only the prestellar cores (3 and 4)
appear to be critical and rest subcritical.
It is also noteworthy that the magnetic field strengths for the
prestellar/starless cores here are also weaker than those for the
protostellar cores.

Hence it could be that as a (prestellar) core permeated by a weak magnetic
field collapses with flux-conservation
(
61
)
, then at some
stage during the collapse, the (cross-sectional) area will become small but
the total flux being the same, the field strength associated will now be
higher. At this stage, the prestellar core is probably dense enough to
transition to a protostellar stage, and the field strong enough to be called
strong field case.

Another possible reason for the different slope of the protostellar
cores and the prestellar/starless cores, as well as for the larger
mass to flux ratio of the prestellar cores could be a difference of condition
of the respective local regions. The eastern part of the filament
(see Figure
1
) which harbours the prestellar
cores 3 and 4 (see Figure
2
) appears to to be
joined by a secondary filament from its north. The flow of material along
the secondary filament to the eastern part of the main filament could be
affecting the physical conditions there. The western part with protostellar
cores 5, 6, and 7 is devoid of such an effect.

V.4
Limitations of our Dust Polarisation Analyses

This study is mainly limited by low sensitivity in many parts of the filament,
leaving pixel gaps for some of the cores and their neighbourhood regions,
resulting in poor statistics.
Moreover SCUBA-2/POL-2 DAISY mapping is only reliable for the central region
of the field of observation, and thus one cannot take a complete
view of the larger region which shows secondary filaments joining the main
filamentary structure (see Figure
1
) in this study.

Furthermore, calculation of magnetic field strengths using the DCF method
(and its variants) assumes turbulence to be the reason for distortion of the
field over its mean background value. It discounts complicated structures
which can arise in evolved cores due to phenomena such as outflows and
gravitational collapse (like hourglass morphology)
(also see Section
III.7.3
). Lastly, the magnetic
field strength which is used in this work is the plane-of-sky
component. Our study thus lacks any information about three dimensional
structure of the magnetic field.
This leads to incomplete information about the measured properties
in some instances, such as
serendipitous orientation of the field vector exactly along the line-of-sight
wherein no polarisation would be observed;
and averaging out of field vectors along the line-of-sight, to mention a few.

VI
Summary and Conclusions

In this paper, we have carried out an analysis of polarised dust emission
at 850
µm
from a filament
(also associated with
PGCC G203.21-11.20 (catalog )
)
in the northern part of the Orion B molecular cloud complex.
The region was observed from JCMT SCUBA-2/POL-2 polarimeter.
The main findings can be summarised as follows :

1.

The line mass of the nearly 1.25 pc long filamentary structure is calculated
to be
\sim
80
\mathrm{M_{\odot}}
pc
-1

of
\sim

\mathrm{M_{\odot}}
pc

for the magnetized filament
(
\mathrm{B_{pos}}
\sim
31

G),
thus making it unstable to gravitational collapse.
The filament is found to be fragmented into 1 starless, 3 prestellar, and
4 protostellar cores.

2.

The total 198 detected pixels at our SNR criteria were classified into 125
filament and 73 core pixels. The mean
\theta
for the filament and the
cores was calculated to be 50
\pm
3 degrees and 30

\sim

\sim

3.

The polarisation fraction of the cores was found to be correlated with their
location rather than their type. Cores (3 to 6) in the denser part
(
\mathrm{N_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{H2}}}}
\gtrsim
2

\sim

\sim

4.

Protostellar cores were found to have their magnetic field orientation

5.

The Ricean Mean Model fit to the relation PF
\propto
I
-α

\alpha

\pm

\pm

This result, along with the smaller polarisation fraction of the cores,
suggests a combination of loss of grain alignment and change in structure
of the field between the filament and the cores.

6.

The prestellar cores and the (sole) starless core were found to have larger
angular dispersion than the protostellar cores.
Using DCF,
the plane-of-sky field strength (
\mathrm{B}_{\mathrm{pos}}^{\textsc{dcf}}

\sim

8.

The B-n relation (
\mathrm{B_{pos}}
\propto

\mathrm{n_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{H2}}}}

\pm

The fitting suggests a transition from weak field case to strong field case

The study of magnetic field in a single fragmenting filament populated by
cores at various evolutionary stages has provided us important insights on
the intertwined nature of (prestellar to protostellar) core evolution and
(weak to strong) field transition.
To better understand the fragmentation of magnetized filaments and subsequent
evolution of cores, prospective work would not only require polarisation
mapping of the larger FoV, but also
at a resolution which allows one to study structures and phenomena
associated with protostellar objects, such as hourglass morphology and
effect of outflows.
Furthermore, core-wise analysis of magnetic fields could be extended to other
filamentary molecular clouds for sampling different environments.
The role of current facilities like ALMA;
upcoming submillimeter facilities like the
Large Submillimeter Telescope (LST) and
Atacama Large Aperture Submillimeter Telescope (AtLAST);
and instruments like the PRIMA Polarimetric Imager and
the next generation JCMT/SCUBA-3
would be crucial in future polarisation studies of star forming regions.
