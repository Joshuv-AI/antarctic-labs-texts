Current landscape of migraine care with expert consensus from East Asia
Authors: Shuu-Jiun Wang, Tsubasa Takizawa, Jiying Zhou, Mi Ji Lee, Daisuke Danno, Min Kyung Chu, Byung-Kun Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMigraine is a debilitating neurovascular disorder affecting over one billion people globally. While reported prevalence in Asia is lower than in Western countries, significant regional variations and unique clinical presentations necessitate a tailored approach to management. From the Advisory Board Meeting of the East Asian Network for Migraine, expert insights and current literature from China, Japan, South Korea, and Taiwan were synthesized to establish a unified perspective on the epidemiological and sociocultural contexts of the region. Patients in these East Asian countries exhibit distinct clinical features, such as a lower frequency of migraine with aura and photophobia, but a higher incidence of osmophobia compared to Western populations. Despite these clear phenotypic differences, significant gaps in care persist. Acute treatments like triptans remain underutilized, and while emerging CGRP-targeting therapies show promise, their adoption is hindered by high costs and limited accessibility. Furthermore, the region faces critical challenges, including a lack of region-specific clinical trials, culturally appropriate assessment tools, and unified clinical guidelines. Optimizing migraine care in East Asia requires a shift toward personalized, culturally sensitive strategies. Experts reached a consensus on the urgent need for collaborative research to address existing disparities and improve patient outcomes. By aligning clinical practice with the unique sociocultural landscapes of these nations, this statement provides a foundational framework for enhanced policy development and improved migraine management across East Asia.Keywords: Asia, Eastern; Cost of illness; Disease management; Epidemiology; Guideline; Health service accessibility; Migraine disorders; Policy making; Therapeutics

Introduction

Migraine is a debilitating neurovascular disorder affecting approximately 1.1 billion people worldwide, accounting for a global prevalence of 14% [95% uncertainty interval (UI): 12.9 to 15.2], with a higher incidence among females than males [1]. The global burden of disease (GBD) 2015 study ranked migraine as the seventh leading cause of disability worldwide, whereas the 2019 GBD study elevated it to the second leading cause of disability globally [2, 3]. Its increasing prevalence elevates the disease burden, healthcare costs and socioeconomic impact [4]. Despite extensive research on its pathophysiology, migraine remains underdiagnosed and treatment options are still limited globally [5, 6].

This unmet need has driven increasing interest in more effective management strategies. Pharmacological treatment is generally categorized into acute therapies, which aim to alleviate migraine attacks, and preventive therapies, which reduce the frequency, duration, and intensity of attacks. Recent research, particularly the identification of calcitonin gene-related peptide (CGRP) as a key mediator of migraine pathophysiology has driven the development of CGRP-targeted therapies. Among these, certain CGRP receptor antagonists have demonstrated efficacy in both acute and preventive treatment settings [7]. Building on these therapeutic advances, many countries have established clinical guidelines tailored to their healthcare systems to optimize migraine care.

The International Headache Society (IHS) has developed practical recommendations for migraine management in 2024, complementing previously published domestic and international guidelines for migraine treatment [8, 9]. Despite efforts to include diverse populations, these recommendations can be further substantiated by incorporating the distinct clinical and epidemiological features of East Asians. Migraine prevalence, for instance, is generally lower in Asia, estimated at approximately 9%, compared to 13% in North America and 15% in Europe [10, 11]. However, the inclusion of probable migraine (PM) may increase the prevalence estimates in East Asia. For example, a Korean study reported a combined prevalence of definite migraine and PM of 17.5% [12]. Furthermore, treatment practices also vary considerably. While triptans are a mainstay of acute therapy in Western countries, their usage remains significantly lower in East Asia-with the notable exception of Japan (51.4%) [13]. In contrast to the high prevalence of use in France (92.5%), the United States (42.2%), and Germany (39.1%), triptans are prescribed far less frequently in South Korea (28.2%), Taiwan (6.0%), and China (7.7%) [14–18]. These likely reflect different epidemiological characteristics, sociocultural factors, healthcare systems, and patient preferences. To address these challenges, there is an unmet need to develop tailored strategies for migraine management in East Asia.

To this end, the East Asian network for migraine organized an advisory board meeting in September 2024 (Seoul, Republic of Korea) with seven neurologists and headache specialists from China, Japan, South Korea, and Taiwan, all of whom are prominent leaders in their respective national headache societies and distinguished researchers in the field. The objective was to raise awareness of gaps in migraine care, characterize the unique clinical and demographic profile of East Asian patients, and explore disparities and health inequities across the region.

In this article, we report the comprehensive review of significant trends in migraine prevalence, incidence, prognosis, as well as genetic and sociocultural factors influencing the condition in East Asian populations. We also present the treatment patterns and guidelines for migraine management with regional variations in the use. This expert review highlights the unmet needs in migraine care in East Asia and explores the challenges and opportunities for better migraine management in the region.

Methods

The content discussed in Advisory Board Meeting of the East Asian Network for Migraine was described in detail. To provide additional context to the patient experiences discussed during the meeting, relevant published literature was identified via a comprehensive PubMed search. This search, conducted without time restrictions, utilized combinations of the following keywords: “migraine epidemiology,” “East Asian network for migraine,” “disease burden,” “migraine advisory board meeting,” “migraine management,” “treatment patterns,” “migraine prevalence,” “diagnosis,” “treatment,” “quality of life,” “socioeconomics,” and “East Asia.”

Ethics committee approval was not required under local regulations, as the study did not entail primary data collection involving human participants. All the meeting participants were fully informed of the meeting’s objectives and actively participated in the development of this publication, meeting all International Committee of Medical Journal Editors (ICMJE) criteria for authorship to this publication.

Results

Epidemiology of migraine in East Asia

Prevalence and clinical features

Over the past three decades, the global prevalence of migraine has shown slight increase, with an estimated annual percentage change (EAPC) of 0.09 [95% confidence interval (CI) 0.07–0.1]. In contrast, during the same period, East Asia experienced a more pronounced rise in migraine prevalence (EAPC 0.31, 95% CI 0.27–0.34). This trend is particularly notable given that East Asia started from a relatively low age-standardized prevalence rate (ASPR) (10,819.5 per 100,000) in 1990 (estimated from the reported percentage change and 2019 value), compared to the global (13,865.6 per 100,000), Western Europe (17,852.5 per 100,000) and Southeast Asia (16,220.2 per 100,000). Despite the relatively marked increase in age-standardized prevalence in East Asia, the overall prevalence remained lower than the global average in 2021 (16,485.5 vs. 19,962.3 per 100,000) [19]. Although the rise in migraine incidence can be attributed to change in risk factors such as rate of metabolic disease, or poor sleep behaviors, the observed increase may also partly reflect improved diagnostic awareness and data reporting. Nevertheless, the persistently lower prevalence compared with global estimates suggests a distinct epidemiologic profile of migraine in East Asia.

Among East Asian countries, China had an ASPR of 14,887.8 per 100,000 population in 1990, which increased to 16,463.6 per 100,000 in 2021, reflecting a moderate upward trend with an EAPC of 0.32. Taiwan’s ASPR was 17,404.4 per 100,000 in 1990, rising to 18,132.3 per 100,000 in 2021, with an EAPC of 0.16. Japan maintained a relatively stable ASPR throughout the observed period, increasing slightly from 15,459.5 per 100,000 in 1990 to 15,881.2 per 100,000 in 2021 (EAPC 0.12). In contrast, the ASPR for migraines in South Korea was 18,042 per 100,000 population in 1990, but it declined to 17,436.1 per 100,000 population in 2021. Migraine epidemiological data from East Asia in the 1990s may have been affected by limited healthcare infrastructure, regional variations in diagnostic practices, and underreporting, leading to inconsistencies in the data. Thus, while these data illustrate broad temporal trends in prevalence, direct comparisons should be interpreted with caution within the context of these methodological limitations.

Beyond these temporal trends and methodological considerations, it is also important to examine broader regional differences in migraine prevalence between East Asia and Western countries. Epidemiological studies have consistently reported that migraine prevalence in East Asia is lower than that reported in Western countries. The 1-year prevalence of migraine in adult Asians aged 18 years or older is 9%, which is lower than Europe 15%, and North America (13%) [20]. In contrast, the prevalence of chronic migraine (CM) or chronic daily headache (CDH) does not differ substantially between Asia, the United States and Europe [21–25]. Meanwhile, the higher occurrence of PM [12], which does not fully meet the diagnostic criteria for migraine, suggests that the current diagnostic frameworks might not fully capture the specific clinical presentations in East Asian populations. This discrepancy likely arises because current diagnostic criteria have limited regional fit, and East Asian patients present with different symptom constellations. Clinical features of migraine also vary across regions, with East Asian populations demonstrating notable differences compared to Western cohorts. For instance, migraine with aura is reported in 13% of cases in South Korea, 12.5% in Taiwan, compared to approximately 30% in Europe and North America [26–30]. Similarly, photophobia is less frequently observed in Asia (45.7%), with rates as low as 32.6% in Japan, 42.3% in South Korea, and 67.4% in Taiwan, compared to much higher rates in the US (80–90%) [27, 31–35]. In contrast, nausea is most commonly reported as the most bothersome symptom (MBS) among South Korean and Taiwanese patients with migraine, followed by phonophobia and osmophobia, whereas in Europe and in the US, photophobia is identified as the primary MBS [36–39]. These differences highlight the need to adapt migraine care strategies to diverse populations that reflect the clinical and epidemiological characteristics of East Asians.

Incidence and risk factors

In 2019, the global incidence of migraine increased to 87.6 million cases (95% UI: 76.6–98.7), representing a 40.1% increase from 1990, with India, China, the United States, and Indonesia together accounting for 43.6% of cases [40]. Notably, countries such as Singapore, Italy, and other high-income nations showed rapid increases in incidence, whereas regions like tropical Latin America experienced a decline [average annual percentage change (AAPC): -0.12, 95% CI: -0.13 to -0.12] [19].

Regional differences in migraine incidence rates across East Asian countries were also notable. China reported an incidence rate of 1,415.6 per 100,000 population (AAPC 0.24), while Japan showed similar rates of 1,502.4 and 1,489.7 per 100,000 (AAPC 0.03 and − 0.12%, respectively). Compared to these countries, South Korea exhibited both the highest incidence rate (1,540.3 per 100,000) and the most significant downward trend (AAPC − 0.37%) [41].

Migraine incidence is influenced by diverse risk factors, including stress and sociocultural conditions [42]. For instance, China ranks second globally in the absolute number of migraine attacks, which may be attributed to urbanization and changes of lifestyle factors (smoking, obesity, lower physical activity, etc.) [40, 43]. Academic pressures, including enrollment in higher education and competition, are also recognized risk factors in China–a trend mirrored in most other East Asian countries. In addition, in South Korea, stress from competitive educational and work environments, along with irregular eating and sleeping patterns, has been linked to migraine onset [44]. In Japan, social stigma represents a barrier to effective migraine management, with many individuals reporting difficulties coping with the condition due to guilt about burdening colleagues and concerns over strained interpersonal relationships. Because of these challenges, 72% of individuals did not seek medical consultation for their migraine [45]. Such sociocultural differences contribute significantly to variations in migraine incidence across countries. A narrative review of 42 studies from 13 Asian countries on migraine triggers in Asia highlights regional differences shaped by geography and culture [20]. The study reported that East Asia experiences more pronounced seasonal changes, making weather-related factors such as fluctuations in temperature, humidity, barometric pressure, and even typhoon-related triggers [46]. In contrast, Western Asia differs in cultural and religious practices, with fasting during Ramadan and lower alcohol consumption influencing the pattern of dietary triggers. These differences underscore the need to consider regional characteristics when understanding and managing migraine in Asians.

Specific genetic polymorphisms are also implicated in migraine susceptibility. The PRDM16 (rs2651899) gene, associated with oxidative stress and neurogenesis, is linked to migraine with aura (OR: 1.32; p = 0.04) [47, 48]. Additionally, the TRPM8 (rs10166942) gene, involved in cold sensation, is associated with increased migraine risk (OR: 1.36; p < 0.0001) [49]. The NOTCH3 p.R544C variant, linked to cerebral autosomal dominant arteriopathy with subcortical infarcts and leukoencephalopathy (CADASIL), is prevalent in Taiwan, Southern China and Jeju island in South Korea, and does not appear to increase migraine risk in these regions [50–52]. Studies in Taiwan have identified variants such as rs146094041 linked to early-onset (≤ 12 years) and specific migraine subtypes, underscoring the influence of regional genetic factors [53]. Additionally, rs146778855, rs117608715, and rs150592309 were common in migraine with aura, while rs181024055 was more common in migraine without aura. These findings suggest that regional genetic factors may significantly influence migraine expressions and prevalence. Although multiple genetic polymorphisms have been associated with migraine susceptibility in East Asian populations, genetic testing, at present, is not recommended for routine diagnosis, risk stratification, or treatment selection in patients with migraine. Most pharmacologic decisions continue to rely on clinical characteristics, comorbidities, attack frequency, and treatment response rather than genotype. Future large-scale, multiethnic prospective studies are needed to determine whether genetic profiling may eventually inform personalized migraine therapy in East Asian populations.

Migraine can progress to CM or CDH if not adequately managed. A narrative review reported that significant number of individuals with episodic migraine (EM) transition to CM over time and a study from Taiwan found CDH and CM prevalence rates of 3.2% and 1.7%, with one-third of CDH cases persisting for more than two years [22, 42]. Chronification is driven by factors such as headache frequency, comorbidities (e.g., depression, anxiety), and particularly medication overuse. Medication overuse was identified as a primary contributor to chronification, overuse of acute headache medications—more than 10–15 days per month for at least three months—can perpetuate symptoms and lead to medication overuse headache (MOH) [54, 55].

Applicability of international guidelines to the East Asian population

Acute treatment and management

Acute migraine management guidelines have been developed and updated by various organizations worldwide, reflecting advances in evidence and clinical practice. In East Asia, the guidelines from China, Japan, South Korea, and Taiwan collectively offer a region-specific approach to acute therapy, emphasizing tailored treatment according to attack severity and individual needs.

Acute treatment guidelines across all four East Asian countries are largely consistent, focusing on stratified care; early intervention, use of antiemetics when necessary, and preventing MOH [56–62]. For mild to moderate attacks, all four countries consistently recommend non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) (e.g., ibuprofen, naproxen, diclofenac, aspirin) as the first-line agents. Sumatriptan, the only triptan on World Health Organization (WHO)’s essential medicine list, was approved in all four countries [8]. Acetaminophen is considered a viable alternative to NSAIDs in less disabling cases. For patients who present to the emergency department with acute migraine, intravenous dexketoprofen or intramuscular diclofenac may be considered as non-oral options. The combination of ergotamine (a class of medications called ergot alkaloids) and caffeine is recommended as a second-line drug for the acute treatment of moderate-to-severe migraine attack in Taiwan [62, 63]. To enhance efficacy of NSAIDs, combination therapies such as acetaminophen–aspirin–caffeine or isometheptene mucate–dichloralphenazone–acetaminophen are also endorsed by local treatment guidelines. To manage nausea and vomiting associated with migraine or its medications, adjunctive antiemetics (e.g., metoclopramide for oral and parenteral use, or prochlorperzaine parenteral) are suggested. However, 5-HT3 antagonists (ondansetron) are not recommended for this purpose [8]. Chinese, Korean and Taiwanese guidelines recommend limiting acute medication use to a maximum of two days per week to reduce the risk of MOH [57, 61, 63]. Notably, in Taiwan, parenteral steroids with intravenous fluid therapy are recommended for patients with status migrainosus―a severe attack that is not relieved by acute treatment and persist for more than 72 h [44, 56, 58].

Overall, the acute treatment guidelines of the four East Asian countries are largely consistent with key international recommendations, positioning NSAIDs and triptans as the cornerstones of therapy, while also incorporating adjunctive antiemetics and non-oral routes for refractory cases. Similar to the American Headache Society (AHS), the European Academy of Neurology/International Headache Society (EAN/IHS), and National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE), the East Asian guidelines emphasize early intervention, stratified care, and the avoidance of opioids and barbiturates to prevent MOH [64–66].

At the same time, several region-specific distinctions remain. For example, despite the strong international endorsement of triptans, their actual prescribing rate and adherence are still low in Taiwan and South Korea, likely reflecting side-effect concerns and limited awareness among patients and clinicians [67]. Compared to Taiwan (6%), South Korea (10.8%) and Japan (51.4%) [13, 68, 69], the prescription rate of triptans in China is significantly lower―accounting for only 3% of claims in the CHIRA database [70]. This is due to systemic issues such as triptans being reimbursed only as second-line treatments, cost barriers, limited formulation options, and cultural factors.

Japan and China have updated their guidelines to acknowledge newer agents such as rimegepant (December 2025) and lasmiditan (2022), closely aligning with the latest AHS and IHS updates [8, 64], whereas the acute-treatment guidelines in South Korea and Taiwan have not yet been updated to include these agents.

While medication overuse is widely recognized as an important contributor to migraine chronification, the specific pharmacological classes contributing to MOH differ between Western and East Asian populations. In Western countries, where triptans are established as first-line treatments with relatively high prescription rates, triptan overuse has been reported as a common contributor to MOH development [71]. By contrast, triptan prescription rates remain relatively low in East Asia. Instead, the medications most commonly implicated in MOH in this region are non-prescription over-the-counter (OTC) analgesics—particularly NSAIDs and caffeine-containing combination drugs [72, 73]. Regardless of the specific medication type involved, addressing medication overuse through comprehensive patient education and appropriate acute treatment selection is crucial for improving long-term outcomes. In this context, acute treatments with a lower risk of MOH, such as gepants, may be particularly valuable in East Asia, where reliance on OTC analgesics is common [74].

Preventive treatment and management

Guidelines for migraine prevention share a common foundation worldwide, but important contrasts exist between western recommendations and those in East Asia, particularly in diagnostic thresholds, drug availability, reimbursement, and prescribing practices. International guidelines, such as those from the IHS, AHS and European Headache Federation (EHF) provide a broad framework based on evidence tiers and practical applicability.

Preventive therapy is generally indicated for patients with frequent and/or disabling migraine, inadequate acute treatment response, or at risk of MOH. A commonly cited initiation threshold is ≥ 2–4 migraine days per month, which is consistent across both Western and Asian guidelines, reflecting shared principles. However, beyond this common ground, regional variations shape practice patterns. While international guidelines (EHF/AHS) now recommend CGRP targeted therapies as first-line preventive therapy, East Asian access remains restricted. Local regulatory and reimbursement policies often require patients to fail multiple prior conventional treatments (Table 1), creating a significant gap between global clinical evidence and regional patient accessibility.

In East Asia, countries have developed region-specific guidelines that adapt international standards to local realities. Among four guidelines of East Asian countries, South Korean guidelines align most closely with international standards, recommending prevention when attacks are frequent, acute therapy is ineffective, or MOH prevention is necessary. A headache diary is strongly advised to monitor efficacy, side effects, and compliance. In China, the guidelines recommend prevention in patients with > 2 severe attacks per month, > 3 migraine days with partial impairment, poor acute treatment response, MOH risk, or comorbidities such as depression and sleep disorders [56, 75]. Japanese guidelines even extend eligibility to those with persistent migraine despite acute therapy, or certain subtypes that risk permanent neurological sequelae, applying a similar ≥ 2 attacks or ≥ 3 days threshold [58, 59]. Lastly, Taiwan sets a slightly higher cutoff, recommending preventive therapy at ≥ 4 headache days per month or for patients with profound disability, complicated migraine with debilitating aura, or migrainous infarction [63].

Preventive pharmacological options also show overlap with regional nuances. For example, beta-blockers (propranolol, metoprolol), antiepileptics (topiramate, valproic acid), antidepressants (amitriptyline), and calcium channel blockers are consistently recommended as foundational therapies. However, the availability of each drug differs. While flunarizine is widely used in China, Taiwan, and South Korea, it is unavailable in Japan where lomerizine is commonly used instead. Dosing practices also diverge—amitriptyline is typically prescribed at doses of 25–150 mg in Europe [9], but it is given at lower doses in East Asia (25–75 mg in China, 10–30 mg in Japan, 2.5–50 mg in South Korea, 10–75 mg in Taiwan), reflecting regional tolerability and clinical habits [58, 62, 76].

The introduction of new therapies highlights further contrasts. While CGRP-targeted monoclonal antibodies (CGRP mAbs) are internationally recognized as promising preventive options, their use for CM prevention varies across East Asian countries due to approval and reimbursement differences. In Japan, the reimbursement threshold for CGRP mAbs is much lower than South Korea or Taiwan. Nevertheless, the required 30% co-payment (approximately 12,000 Japanese Yen (80 US Dollars) is still burdensome for many patients. In a survey conducted by the Japanese Headache Society, only 8.3% of its members reported prescribing at this threshold; most clinicians tended to initiate treatment after failure of two or more preventive medications and when patients had a somewhat higher number of headache days [77]. In contrast, Taiwan has no co-payment, but its reimbursement criteria are very strict, and the coverage period is limited to only six months with a mandatory break for at least 6 months. In South Korea, as in Taiwan, reimbursement thresholds are high; however, many patients carry private health insurance, so out-of-pocket costs are often mitigated. Such country-specific regulatory differences also arise from variations in drug approvals across countries. A prime example is onabotulinumtoxinA, which is used globally for preventing chronic migraine but has not yet been approved for this indication in Japan, unlike in South Korea and Taiwan. These examples show that actual accessibility of treatment and preventive medications for CM in East Asia is heavily influenced by factors like healthcare policies, cost-sharing systems, reimbursement regulations, and drug approval for indications, despite the existence of international or national guidelines.

Availability and approval of worldwide acknowledged non-pharmacological CM prevention strategies, including neuromodulation, cognitive-behavioral therapy, and acupuncture also vary across East Asian countries. Lastly, to reduce or avoid problems caused by overuse of CM treatment medication, East Asian guidelines consistently emphasize the importance of patient education. In particular, most guidelines recommend considering a gradual taper after a sustained period of symptom control—typically after maintaining effective prophylaxis for 3 to 6 months for EM and 6 to 12 months for CM.

Unmet needs in migraine care and migraine-related quality of life evaluation in East Asia

Unmet needs in acute treatment

Across the four East Asian countries, at least two oral triptans are available; moreover, non-oral formulations are also available except in South Korea. Despite the availability, access to triptans is often restricted due to high costs and limited national insurance coverage. The 2024 data from the Japan Medical Data Center Claims Database indicated that 95.6% of migraine patients received acute treatment, while 20.8% received preventive treatment. Acetaminophen/NSAIDs (54.8%) had the highest prescription rate as an initial treatment, followed by triptans (51.4%), notably high triptan usage [13]. This is a high proportion, considering that this data captures only hospital prescriptions, and it has been reported that many migraine sufferers in East Asia use over-the-counter medications instead of prescription drugs [11, 13]. Among patients followed for over a year, only 3.7% displayed patterns suggesting MOH. The 2021 Japanese OVERCOME study, a cross-sectional, population-based web survey assessing the epidemiology, treatment, and management of migraine among Japanese adults, found that 80.4% of respondents had used over the counter acute migraine medication at some point, while only 20.1% had used triptan [78]. Additionally, approximately half of respondents (53.2%) were currently using acute prescription medication, primarily NSAIDs (36.7%) [78].

From 2018 to 2022, analgesics were the most commonly prescribed antimigraine medications in China, with a 5-year average of 70.23%, compared to 26.41% for migraine-specific agents (triptans and ergotamine) and 3.36% for antiemetics [79]. NSAIDs (28.03%), caffeine-containing agents (22.15%), and opioids (primarily codeine/ibuprofen combinations, 16.00%) were the predominant analgesics, while oral triptans (26.12%) were the leading migraine-specific drugs, accounting for 62.21% of total costs [79]. Notably, caffeine-containing products (such as acetaminophen/caffeine, aminopyrine/caffeine, acetaminophen/propylantipyrine/caffeine, aspirin/acetaminophen/caffeine, or aminopyrine/phenacetin/phenobarbital/caffeine) and opioids were more frequently used as acute treatments in China compared to other East Asian countries.

The study published in 2014 utilized Taiwan’s national health insurance database to analyze the refill and 2-year retention rates for newly prescribed triptans [67]. Among the 13,951 participants receiving a new triptan prescription (99.9% sumatriptan), 34.3% adhered to the triptan at the first refill, 0.01% switched to another triptan, and 40.9% switched to a non-triptan acute migraine medication. The 2-year retention rate was notably low at 4.0% [67].

Similarly, in South Korea, trends in triptan use from 2002 to 2019 showed significant growth according to a large claims database [69]. The total number of prescribed triptan tablets increased 24-fold (from 3,785 to 90,934), total prescription cases saw a 17-fold rise (from 432 to 7,382), and the utilization rate among migraine patients jumped from 1.6% to 10.8%, representing a 6.8-fold increase over the period. Despite this growth, the overall prescription rate of triptans remains low, indicating potential underutilization and underscoring the need for improved migraine management strategies across all medical fields. The substantial variation in prescribing medications across different specialties emphasizes the critical need for enhanced migraine education among non-neurologists [69].

Ditans and gepants represent two novel classes of acute migraine-specific therapies with distinct mechanisms of action. Lasmiditan, the first-in-class 5-HT1F receptor agonist, offers an alternative to triptans without vasoconstrictive effects and was approved by the FDA in 2019 [80]. It was then approved in Japan and South Korea in 2022 and 2023, respectively. Gepants—small-molecule CGRP receptor antagonists—have demonstrated efficacy for acute migraine treatment. Notably, they are not known to cause MOH [81]. Across East Asia, rimegepant is approved for the acute treatment of migraine in China, Japan, South Korea, and Taiwan. However, reimbursement status varies. While Japan has already implemented insurance coverage―allowing patients to pay only a 30% co-payment―the other three countries have yet to provide similar financial support. Other agents in this class, including ubrogepant and zavegepant, are not yet available in any of the four East Asian countries, despite demonstrated efficacy and safety in Western populations. Thus, while these therapies are widely recognized as promising alternatives to traditional acute treatments, their adoption in East Asia remains limited, reflecting regional differences in regulatory approval, healthcare accessibility, and market availability.

Unmet needs in preventive treatment

Despite the availability of novel preventive therapies, significant unmet needs remain in East Asian migraine care. Clinical trial and real-world data consistently demonstrate that the 50% responder rates for CGRP mAbs plateau at around 60%, leaving a substantial proportion of patients insufficiently treated [82]. Some patients respond only after prolonged treatment, suggesting that longer evaluation periods are necessary, but this delay also underscores the limitations of existing options and the need for more effective strategies [82]. CGRP mAbs are not reimbursed in China, whereas in Taiwan and South Korea they are approved primarily for CM under strict criteria (Table 1), requiring demonstration of at least 50% efficacy over 12-week period, which poses challenges for long-term use. In contrast, in Japan, CGRP mAbs can be prescribed for patients with ≥ 4 monthly migraine days without restrictions on treatment duration. However, 30% co-payment hinders their utilization.

Regardless of wide availability of preventive therapies, their utilization in East Asia remains relatively low [68, 83]. Survey data show that only 23.7% of East Asian migraine patients consistently use preventive medications, and overall satisfaction with these treatments is just 29.5% [84]. A global CaMEO-I study similarly reported that while most respondents met the AHS 2021 criteria for preventive therapy, 84.5% were not using it, with this treatment gap being particularly pronounced in Asia [85]. In Japan, for instance, 98% of migraine sufferers had never received preventive medications [45, 78]. Consistent findings were reported in a survey of outpatient neurology clinics across eight Asian countries, where 87.5% of patients used acute medications but only 29.2% used preventive medications [86]. These findings highlight a consistent pattern of under-treatment that continues to shape the clinical landscape.

Delayed initiation of preventive therapy is another major concern, often driven by both clinical inertia and cultural influences. Physicians may hesitate to escalate treatment beyond acute medications even for patients with frequent or disabling attacks, while cultural norms such as gaman in Japan, which values endurance and patience, contribute to under-reporting of symptoms and postponement of care [87]. This paradox is illustrated by survey data showing that Japanese patients report low migraine severity scores yet experience some of the highest levels of work impairment globally, reflecting a tendency to endure disability rather than seek timely treatment [78, 84, 87].

Optimizing preventive treatment outcomes requires not only the initiation of appropriate prophylactic agents but also the effective management of pre-existing medication overuse [88]. However, in East Asia, the clinical transition from acute medication overuse to a stable preventive regimen is often hindered by systemic inadequacies [55]. Despite the clinical necessity of managing MOH, there is currently a significant lack of structured withdrawal support systems across most of East Asia. Specifically, specialized inpatient and outpatient withdrawal programs are rarely available, and clinical guidelines for systematic drug discontinuation remain underdeveloped in this region. This systemic gap is further exacerbated by barriers to access and reimbursement for advanced therapies. For instance, the high insurance barriers for CGRP mAbs in South Korea and Taiwan impose significant restrictions on their use, reducing the real-world utilization of evidence-based treatment [59]. Consequently, clinicians face a dual challenge: the absence of insurance coverage for intensive withdrawal management and the limited accessibility of novel preventives, both of which contribute to the poor adherence and suboptimal outcomes observed with traditional oral preventives.

Rimegepant is approved across all four East Asian countries; however, in China it is limited to acute treatment and not yet approved for prevention. Atogepant is available in South Korea and Taiwan for prevention of migraine but is not currently reimbursed. However, in Japan, atogepant became available in April 2026, and insurance covers the preventive indication. These developments highlight progress but also reveal significant delays and regional disparities in access, leaving many East Asian patients without timely access to new preventive options.

Beyond issues of access and adherence, central sensitization is increasingly recognized as a major barrier to effective prevention and a driver of chronification. Patients with clinical features such as neck pain, cranial autonomic symptoms, and high interictal burden are more likely to develop refractory migraine [89]. While CGRP mAbs act mainly through peripheral mechanisms due to their limited CNS penetration, gepants, although also primarily acting peripherally, may have some degree of central activity and thus hold potential benefits for patients with central sensitization and refractory migraine [90].

Experts in the region increasingly recognize the artificial divide between acute and preventive treatments. Emerging therapies such as gepants, which can be used in both contexts, offer the potential for more flexible treatment strategies that better reflect the fluctuating course of migraine. For example, the Chinese guidelines for the treatment of acute migraine emphasize that rimegepant not only manages acute migraine symptoms but also effectively reduces headache frequency. Continued efforts are therefore underway in East Asia to address these unmet needs by improving access to novel therapies, promoting earlier initiation of preventive treatment, and adopting more patient-centered, flexible approaches to care.

Quality of life (QoL) assessment surveys and unmet needs in East Asia

In clinical trials, it is crucial to use appropriate tools for assessing quality of life, which is a key indicator. The AHS, the American Academy of Neurology, the EHF, and the EAN not only provide guidelines for the management of migraines but also recommend various migraine assessment tools. These include the Migraine-Specific Quality of Life Questionnaire (MSQ) Version 2.1, Migraine Disability Assessment Scale (MIDAS), and Headache Impact Test (HIT-6) [91]. Currently, East Asia, similar to the global context, also extensively utilizes the types mentioned earlier in various studies, including clinical trials [92, 93]. Particularly, the MIDAS questionnaire and MSQv2.1 are widely used for research purposes, their application in routine clinical practice remains limited due to time constraints in busy outpatient settings [37, 78, 93–97]. Large-scale epidemiological studies (AMPP and CaMEO) on migraine, commonly employed the MIDAS questionnaire [98]. The MIDAS data are utilized in various applications, including the estimation of economic loss resulting from reduced work productivity among employees [45].

In Japan, the OVERCOME study has employed the migraine interictal burden scale-4 (MIBS-4) questionnaire to evaluate the psychological, social, and emotional burden experienced between migraine attacks [84, 99, 100]. Additional questionnaire included the Work Productivity and Activity Impairment-Migraine (WPAI-M), Impact of Migraine on Partners and Adolescent Children (IMPAC), Patient Health Questionnaire and Generalized Anxiety Disorder (GAD-7). In Taiwan, HIT-6, HADS, and BDI are commonly used in clinics, while MSQv2.1 and MIBS-4 are reserved for research purposes. In South Korea, the HIT-6, and PHQ-9 have been well validated and are commonly used in routine clinical assessment of migraine patients, whereas the MSQv2.1 is primarily applied in research settings. Although most of the translated questionnaires of these measures are well validated and commonly used in migraine research, further studies are needed to assess their cultural relevance and psychometric performance within these Asian migraine populations. Furthermore, many patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs) have been translated from English into local languages; however, linguistic nuances or imprecise wording often arise during translation, making them difficult to interpret and potentially skewing the recorded scores.

Recently, migraine has gained recognition as a critical public health issue [101]. Population-based data from Japan (OVERCOME study) demonstrated that a substantial proportion of individuals with migraine have never consulted a physician (42.6%) [73]. Although the lifetime rate of medical consultation for migraine in South Korea did not differ greatly between the 2009 (30.8%) and 2018 (34.2%) surveys, international trends demonstrated an increase in consultation rates in the United States, from 16% in 1984 to 47% in 1999 and 80% in 2016. With improvements in South Korea’s socioeconomic conditions and healthcare system, together with awareness campaigns by the South Korean Headache Society, further increases in lifetime consultation rates are anticipated, which may ultimately enhance diagnosis and treatment [44].

According to 2016 data, the prevalent cases of migraine in these four East Asian countries that participated in this study amount to approximately 162 million, representing 15.5% of the 1.044 billion total global prevalent cases of migraine [102]. Given the population size and the scale of the migraine prevalence, clinical trials targeting East Asian populations may provide additional values in migraine treatment research [95]. Additionally, there is a need for the development of tools that offer a more culturally sensitive approach to assessing the everyday impacts of migraines, as well as comparative studies with commonly used tools. Addressing these unmet needs through targeted clinical trials and culturally appropriate assessment tools is crucial for improving migraine management in East Asia.

Future directions

Recently, global guidelines and research efforts have made strides in including more diverse populations, striving to offer a holistic understanding of migraine management across different demographics. While the core principles of guidelines from IHS, AHS, EAN, AAN, and EHF are broadly applicable to East Asian populations, local adaptation is necessary to account for differences in drug availability, reimbursement structures, and clinical practice patterns. This expert review identifies region-specific unmet needs in migraine management in East Asia and suggests addressing these gaps through collaborative research initiatives and the development of tailored, region-specific treatment guidelines. It also emphasizes that migraine management should consider regional contexts, including cultural, socioeconomic, and healthcare system differences across East Asia, including China, Japan, South Korea, and Taiwan.

To achieve these goals, more real-world evidence generation in East Asian population must be performed as well as clinical trials specific to the region. Developing a continuous collaborative network of experts in East Asia and the broader Asian region could facilitate studies where retrospective or prospective data can be collected. This would allow for the examination of treatment failure rates, medication sequences, and outcomes for conventional oral preventives, triptans, and CGRP-pathway agents (mAbs and gepants) and onabotulinumtoxinA. These findings would provide insights into the practical challenges faced by both clinicians and patients.

Another key challenge leading to disparities in migraine management within East Asia is the variability in access to migraine medications, particularly newer therapies like CGRP-targeted agents. While regulatory approvals are expanding, region-specific barriers include high costs, limited insurance coverage, and regulatory differences among countries. Development of a consensus statement or position paper on management of migraine in East Asia can advocate for broader access to these treatments. Addressing optimal treatment through patient stratification and re-establishing reimbursement criteria could unify migraine management in East Asia and influence national policies and insurance frameworks. Furthermore, migraine management can be improved through government initiatives such as the introduction of affordable generic migraine-specific medications, expansion of telemedicine services to improve access in rural areas, and enhanced insurance coverage for these treatments.

Given the unique etiological landscape of MOH in East Asi, characterized by high accessibility to OTC analgesics, current management strategies face significant limitations. Therefore, to enhance the management of migraine in East Asia, future efforts must focus on establishing robust withdrawal support infrastructures. This includes the development of standardized protocols for both inpatient and outpatient settings, alongside the expansion of insurance benefits to cover professional withdrawal assistance [103]. Addressing these systemic gaps is essential to translate clinical consensus into effective treatment outcomes for the growing number of patients suffering from medication-induced migraine chronification.

Culturally and regionally specific QoL assessment tools may further improve migraine management in East Asia. Current tools, such as MIDAS and HIT-6, are instrumental in assessing the impact of migraines on patients’ lives. Current measures may not comprehensively reflect the disability and burden experienced by Asian patients, as cultural variations influence perceptions of work and daily responsibilities. Additionally, accurate documentation of work or school days lost is often affected by cultural attitudes toward employment and education. For example, most East Asian patients, particularly those in China and Japan, often continue working despite experiencing headaches. This high prevalence of presenteeism may lead to an underestimation of the true burden of migraine, as the impact is hidden behind continued attendance rather than reflected in absenteeism. Development of an Asia-specific tool can better evaluate the burden of migraine and facilitate more accurate comparisons between populations.

These collaborations can be further expanded into other Asian regions such as Southeast Asia, where limited established headache societies and a relatively small number of specialists may pose challenges to the development of migraine-specific initiatives. Incorporating these countries into regional collaborations could not only enhance data collection, support educational programs, and foster the establishment of local guidelines, but also improve the migraine patient care in these regions. It can also enhance physician training, particularly for primary care providers, and increasing public awareness through multi-national educational campaigns.

Overall, collaborative research to generate region-specific data, development of tailored assessment tools, dedicated clinical trials in East Asian populations and continuous development of expert consensus guidelines for East Asian would enhance migraine management for these patients. Consensus-building efforts, such as Delphi studies, can address shared barriers to effective migraine treatment across diverse healthcare systems.

Conclusions

In summary, while East Asian migraine care shares core principles with international guidance, it may benefit from context-specific adaptation. Key priorities are: (1) bridging access gaps—delayed approvals relative to Western markets and reimbursement-policy and care-pathway constraints; (2) advancing region-specific clinical trials and real-world evidence; (3) standardizing education for patients and clinicians to promote timely initiation and rational tapering; and (4) developing and validating culturally sensitive QoL measures. However, a limitation of this study is that its findings are primarily based on data from China, Japan, South Korea and Taiwan; therefore, caution is warranted when extrapolating these conclusions to other Asian countries with different clinical and regulatory landscapes. Sustained, multi-country collaboration—including consensus statements aligned with payer criteria—could be a valuable step in translating evidence into standardized, high-quality care for patients across East Asia.
