Telemedicine Use Amongst US Children and Young Adults With Autism: Utilization, Costs and Common Conditions
Authors: Gonzalo Ramirez-Pulido, Tonghui Xu, Summer Chavez, Omolola Adepoju
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe management of autism and other comorbidities among children and young adults places a substantial economic and logistical burden on families. Telemedicine offers potential benefits for children and young adults with autism by improving access to care, reducing costs, and supporting families in managing ongoing treatment needs, all highlighted during the COVID-19 pandemic. While telemedicine has shown positive benefits in neurotypical pediatric populations, limited evidence supports and describes the use of telemedicine for children with autism.ObjectiveThis study examined children and young adults with autism and assessed the association between telemedicine services and annual combined out-of-pocket (OOP) medical costs among children and young adults with autism.DesignCross-sectional data from the U.S. nationally representative Medical Expenditure Panel Survey (MEPS) were analyzed. Independent variables included demographics, medical conditions, utilization patterns, and healthcare expenditures. A weighted log-linear regression model estimated combined medical OOP costs, and a binary weighted logistic regression examined factors associated with telemedicine use.DataThe study cohort included 189 children and young adults with autism aged 1–21 years from 2020 to 2023.ResultsAmong them, 62 (32.8%) used telemedicine. By 2023, telemedicine utilization among children and young adults with exceeded those who did not use telemedicine. Telemedicine utilization did not have a significant effect on OOP costs (EXP(β) = 1.30, p = 0.17). Insurance type showed a strong effect, with individuals without Medicaid having much higher odds of incurring OOP costs than those with Medicaid (EXP(β) = 15.91, p < 0.001). Black or African American children had significantly lower odds of telemedicine use compared to non-Hispanic White children (Exp(β) = 0.17, p < 0.05).ConclusionTelemedicine use was not significantly associated with combined OOP medical costs among children and young adults with autism. However, differences were observed in telemedicine use and OOP costs.

Introduction

The prevalence of autism in the U.S. has increased over the last two decades, with an estimated 1 in 31 children having autism. 1 The disorder is characterized by limitations in social communication and interaction, as well as repetitive and restricted behaviors or activities. 2 In the U.S., the average age of autism diagnosis is over 4 years old, with 27% of children remaining undiagnosed by age 8. 3 Early diagnosis is critical for children with autism as it facilitates early intervention, which improves developmental outcomes. 4 These interventions consist of individualized therapies that address behavior, language, and social communication. 4 Individuals and their families may also benefit from linkages with other programs and supportive services. 4 Because autism is a lifelong condition that may require intensive services and support across healthcare, it is associated with substantial economic burden, with an estimated $3.6 million in lifetime social costs for one individual. 5 Caregivers and close relatives of children with autism often face financial challenges, as insurance coverage frequently falls short of meeting the full range of their healthcare needs. 6 These access and systemic barriers highlight the need for innovative approaches to delivering access to care.

Given these access challenges, telemedicine has emerged as a promising tool to improve access to autism-related services, especially for families in rural or underserved areas or for those who face challenges in accessing traditional clinical settings. 7 Previous studies have examined telehealth-based services in different contexts such as applied behaviour analysis, in-home autism assessment, provider and caregiver experiences with telehealth assessments, and parent-mediated telehealth interventions.8-11 These studies highlight that telemedicine may be a feasible practice for autism-related care. Telemedicine also has the potential to reduce logistical barriers related to travel and provider shortages.

Prior literature has shown that individuals with autism experience higher costs than those without, with higher healthcare expenditures among autistic individuals. 12 Another study examining claims-based evidence shows that children with autism have higher utilization and expenditures compared to children without neurodevelopmental disorders. 13 Telemedicine has been studied in children and young adults with autism to treat behavioral health concerns, anxiety therapy, physical activity, and sleep quality.14-17 These studies highlight how telemedicine may be a feasible modality for this vulnerable population. Yet, less is known about how children and young adults with autism use telemedicine in real-world healthcare settings, despite earlier research investigating telemedicine for autism diagnosis, evaluation and intervention. Limited evidence exists describing whether telemedicine use is associated with differences in healthcare utilization, healthcare costs, or out-of-pocket costs. 18

In this study, we aim to describe the sociodemographic characteristics of children and young adults with autism (defined as under 21 years of age) who use telemedicine compared to those who do not use telemedicine. We examine the association between telemedicine services for autism treatment and annual combined out-of-pocket (OOP) medical costs among children and young adults with autism. Finally, we identify sociodemographic factors influencing the use of telemedicine services.

Methods

Data and Design

The Medical Expenditure Panel Survey (MEPS) is a large, nationally U.S. representative survey of families and individuals conducted annually by the Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality (AHRQ). 19 MEPS data consists of (a) demographic information, (b) medical conditions and events, (c) medical expenditures, and (d) insurance. Starting in 2020, MEPS began collecting data on telemedicine. For combined OOP costs, medical event files were pooled, and duplicate person–event records were removed using the DUPERSID–EVNTIDX identifier to avoid double counting. Combined OOP costs were linked to demographic data and analyzed using Full-Year Consolidated File weights and pooled PSU/stratum variables. 20

The cohort in our study includes children and young adults aged 1 to 21 years, drawn from four years of cross-sectional consolidated MEPS data. The inclusion criteria were: (a) individuals identified in the MEPS dataset using the three-digit ICD-10 code F84 for autism, and (b) binary responses (yes/no) to the question regarding whether an outpatient medical event was a telemedicine visit. This study was reviewed and approved by the University of Houston Institutional Review Board (STUDY00005254).

Variable and Measurements

The independent variable included sociodemographic characteristics gender, age, race/ethnicity, insurance, region, family income based on the federal poverty level (FPL), parents’ education level categorized according to years of education, employment status, mother’s marital status, and clinical characteristics (the five most frequently occurring medical conditions identified by three-digit ICD-10 codes). There were two distinct outcome variables: (a) a binary indicator of telemedicine usage (used telemedicine vs. did not use telemedicine) and (b) total annual combined OOP medical costs. The combined OOP cost was the sum of outpatient, inpatient, emergency, prescribed medication, and home health services costs associated with autism, and the five most frequently occurring co-occurring medical conditions identified in the dataset: attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD; ICD-10: F90), sleep disorders (ICD-10: G47), asthma (ICD-10: J45), anxiety (ICD-10: F41), and depression (ICD-10: F32-F33). These medical conditions were based on respondent reports recorded by the interviewer using a condition pick-list with ICD-10-CM codes. Since the sample size was small, we focused on the top five conditions to capture the most common co-occurring conditions without overcomplicating the statistical models. Missing data were observed for parent employment (9%), continuous mental health status (1%), insurance status (4%), mother’s marital status (7%), and continuous highest parent education year (7%). Categorical variables were imputed using the most frequent value, and continuous variables were imputed using the mean.

Data Analysis

In the preliminary analysis, we conducted a correlation analysis including all study variables to evaluate the possibility of a social gradient in autism diagnosis. We also ran a variance inflation factor (VIF) model to assess potential multicollinearity. Descriptive statistics were computed, followed by weighted chi-square tests for categorical variables and weighted t-test for continuous variables to compare telemedicine users and non-users, as well as patients with multiple conditions.

To model OOP costs (Model 1), we used a weighted log-linear regression model and included a binary indicator for telemedicine use as an independent variable. Because the OOP cost variable was right-skewed and included both zero values and extremely high outliers, we added $1 to observations with zero OOP costs and applied the log transformation simultaneously. 21 This approach allows the coefficients’ signs to reliably indicate whether higher OOP costs are associated with higher or lower values of the independent variables. The coefficient estimates for each sociodemographic factor from the regression models were then expressed as the exponential of the beta coefficient (i.e., Exp(β)). Model 2 was conducted using survey logistic regression, and we reported adjusted odds ratios (ORs). All adjusted regression analyses were survey-weighted and accounted for the complex sampling design, weighting structure, and inflation adjustments of the MEPS data. All costs were reported in December 2023 U.S. dollars using the U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics Consumer Price Index (CPI) Inflation Calculator. In addition, we reported the model fit in Table A.1 (Supplementary materials). For the weighted log-linear regression model, we reported the adjusted R-squared and Root Mean Square Error (Root MSE). For the survey logistic regression model, we reported the max-rescaled R-square and area under the curve (AUC). All analyses were conducted using SAS 9.4, and findings were considered significant at p < 0.05.

Result

Preliminary Analysis

For the preliminary analysis, the correlation matrix across all study variables revealed no strong relationships that would raise concerns about a social gradient or multicollinearity. The highest correlation was between household parent status and college experience (r = 0.45), while the correlation between parental education and FPL was relatively weak (r = 0.36), suggesting a low risk of a potential social gradient. The VIF results also showed no evidence of high multicollinearity, as all VIF values were below 5.

Statistical Description, Chi-Squared and T-Test

Table 1 presents the descriptive statistics for autism patients and their corresponding telemedicine use. Among 189 patients with autism, 62 (32.8%) children and young adults used telemedicine. Most patients in the study were male (75.7%) compared to females (24.3%) (p < 0.01). More than half of the study sample (60.3%) reported family income <200% below the FPL (p = 0.05). The racial and ethnic composition consisted mainly of Hispanic patients (48.7%) and followed by non-Hispanic Whites (28.6%). Hispanic patients represented a higher proportion of the telemedicine group compared with the non-telemedicine group (56.5% vs. 44.9%), but the subsample size was smaller in the telemedicine group. More Black or African American patients were in the group who did not use telemedicine compared to the telemedicine group (16.5% vs. 4.8%) (p < 0.01). The study sample was highly educated, with 58.2% parents reporting college as the highest level of education. The proportion of college-educated parents was greatest in the group that did not use telemedicine (63.8%) compared with the telemedicine group (46.8%) (p < 0.01). Compared with those who did not use telemedicine, telemedicine users had higher proportions of ADHD (59.7% vs. 38.6%; p < 0.01), sleep disorders (21.0% vs. 18.1%; p < 0.01), and anxiety (33.9% vs. 18.1%; p < 0.001). Telemedicine users also had higher proportions of at least one co-occurring medical condition (25.8% vs. 19.7%) and multiple co-occurring medical conditions (45.2% vs. 33.1%) compared with non-users (p < 0.01). In addition, a lower proportion of telemedicine users rated their mental health status as excellent, very good, or good compared with those who did not use telemedicine (62.9% vs. 75.6%; p < 0.001).

We also report the average weighted OOP medical costs for children and young adults with autism, stratified by reason for the telemedicine encounter in Table 1. For the entire study sample, the estimated weighted annual average OOP medical cost was $807.80. The average OOP medical cost was not statistically significantly different between telemedicine users and non-users ($755.35 vs. $833.30; p = 0.63). Similarly, the average number of annual total medical visits was 35 for the total sample. The average number of annual medical visits was also similar between telemedicine users and non-users, with 34 visits among telemedicine users and 36 visits among non-users (p = 0.53).

Discussion

In this study examining telemedicine use and associations with OOP medical expenditures among children and young adults with autism, we found several key results. Telemedicine utilization was more likely among those aged 6-12 years, those with more than one co-occurring medical condition, and among children with reported poor/fair mental health. Conversely, Black children and those with parents holding a college degree were less likely to use telemedicine. Notably, our findings did not show that telemedicine use reduced combined OOP costs. Higher OOP costs were significantly associated with higher family income, autistic children aged 6-17 years, parental college education, non-Medicaid insurance, single mother households, and those with multiple co-occurring medical conditions, while Hispanic autistic children were less likely to have higher OOP costs.

Evaluating the impact of telemedicine on OOP costs for autistic children and youth can be very complex, as costs can vary based on the severity of autism.22-24 Although our model shows that telemedicine has no significant influence on combined OOP costs, this finding may reflect variations in service needs, care intensity, and access to specialized providers among children with differing autism severity. Telehealth cost savings are not being observed across settings consistently and may depend on whether telehealth replaces travel or more expensive in person care. 22 Additionally, increased telemedicine utilization may supplement rather than replace in-person visits, which could explain why telemedicine was not associated with lower OOP costs. The number of comorbidities was the strongest correlate of telemedicine use and OOP expenses among children and youth with autism. With over 60% of the study population reporting one or more chronic conditions, including asthma, mental health conditions, and ADHD, 25 children with multiple comorbidities were more likely to utilize telemedicine, likely reflecting their greater need for ongoing care. Although direct evidence on autism-specific cases is limited, broader studies show that children with multiple chronic conditions generally face higher combined OOP medical expenses. 26 Other studies have found that low-income families with children who have multiple conditions experience higher OOP costs. 27

Additionally, middle- and high-household income was associated with greater combined OOP medical costs for children and young adults with autism, which is consistent with patterns from previous studies.28,29 We believe that Medicaid coverage largely explained this difference — in our study, 68% of families had Medicaid coverage. This combination of modest income and Medicaid coverage likely (1) reduced their OOP burden compared to families with less than a college education, (2) limited their expenditure capacity on healthcare compared to higher income families, and (3) may reflect differences in access to care, as higher income families can pay for more readily available services. While Medicaid may cover basic services, families with higher incomes may be better financially situated to participate in more frequent therapies and to build more robust care networks. Prior research primarily focused on expenditures directly related to autism, found families with children covered by Medicaid incurred substantially lower combined OOP medical costs compared to those with private, other insurances,30,31 or no insurance. 32 Our results further illustrate that Medicaid also reduces OOP medical costs when considering expenses associated with both autism and co-occurring conditions. Additionally, children with autism living in single-mother families had higher odds of incurring OOP costs. Single mothers may face challenges, multiple barriers accessing care such as inadequate medical insurance, which could contribute to higher OOP expenses.

Telehealth use should be interpreted within the context of COVID-19 during 2020 and 2021. During this period telehealth had a rapid expansion given that health systems sought to maintain care while limiting in-person visits reducing risk of transmission. Therefore, the increase in telehealth use in our study may reflect pandemic-derived changes rather than the usual patterns of care among children and young adults with autism. CDC reported a 154% increase in telehealth visits during the last week of March 2020 compared to the same week in 2019. 33 In another example, Patel et al. found that telehealth visits increased from 0.8 to 17.8 visits per 1000 enrollees from January 2020 to June 2020 while in-person visits decreased by approximately 30%. 34 Cantor et al. found in a national claims-based study of mental health services from 2019 to 2022 that telehealth visits remained 10 times higher than pre-pandemic levels during the postacute phase. 35 These findings need to be interpreted as reflecting healthcare utilization during the pandemic period of rapid telehealth expansion. This context may also explain why telemedicine was not associated with lower OOP costs because telehealth might have substituted in-person visits.

Although access to mental health services benefited from the momentum of telemedicine during the COVID-19 pandemic, special attention should be paid to the considerable burden of mental health comorbidities amongst children and young adults with autism. In our study, those who reported poor or fair mental health had the highest odds of using telemedicine. Some autistic individuals may be less able to present to an appointment in person or may feel less comfortable in a medical setting, making telemedicine a useful alternative to in-person care. 36 Moreover, telemedicine can reduce children’s anxiety and stress by allowing them to remain in a predictable and familiar environment. 37 In terms of services for autism, there have been positive findings with a greater likelihood to adopt telemedicine, enhanced parental coaching, and training to deliver autism treatment, leading to reduced caregiver burden as well as improvements in communication skills and behavior.6,18,36,38,39 Despite the gains of telemedicine during the pandemic, disparities in telehealth access and usage continue to persist among people of color, those with limited technology literacy, older adults, and people with disabilities.40,41 This was consistent with our study, which found that Black/African children were less likely to use telemedicine. Considering the critical contribution of telemedicine to increasing access to care, special attention should be given to ensuring the digital divide does not widen amongst already at-risk groups. Development of interventions to address equity within health technology among vulnerable autism populations is necessary.

Limitations

There are several important limitations of the present study. The data analyzed in this study were primarily self-reported and therefore may be subject to common survey-related biases, including recall bias, reporting bias, and residual confounding. However, MEPS has been widely used to estimate average healthcare costs across different populations. In addition, because the study sample size was small and the proportion of missing data was low, we did not use multiple imputation. Instead, missing values were handled using high-frequency category replacement for categorical variables and mean replacement for continuous variables, which was considered to pose a low risk of bias. Therefore, although these limitations should be considered when interpreting the findings, they are unlikely to substantially change the overall conclusions of the present study. Third, the first limitation is the small total sample size. Although our data set spanned four years, the total sample size was only 189, which may have introduced bias into the study, and a single outlier value may have affected the robustness of the results. In addition, our study includes individuals under the age of 22 (13%), whereas most related studies focus on children under 18 years with autism. Therefore, differences in age range may introduce some bias in interpretation. Fourth, although the MEPS dataset contains a rich set of variables, we included only common covariates and the top five most common co-occurring medical conditions. This approach may have excluded less common medical conditions or other relevant factors that could influence OOP costs. Additionally, the COVID-19 pandemic may have influenced the study findings. During the pandemic, telemedicine use increased substantially, while in-person healthcare utilization have decreased. This shift in healthcare delivery could have affected both telemedicine utilization patterns and OOP medical costs, potentially introducing bias into the results. However, because the pandemic period represents an important context for understanding telemedicine use among children and young adults with autism, these data were retained. Finally, to retain zero-cost observations while addressing the right-skewed distribution of cost data, we used a log(OOP cost + 1) transformation. Although this approach has been used in the cost-of-care literature, adding an arbitrary constant may introduce minor bias when retransformed estimates are interpreted on the original cost scale. To address these limitations, we plan to increase the sample size by incorporating data from subsequent years, to apply machine learning feature selection methods to better identify important predictors, use two-part models instead of the current log-transformed cost model, and apply generalized linear regression models to examine healthcare utilization.

Conclusion

Overall, although our study indicates that telemedicine use may not influence combined OOP medical costs among children and young adults with autism, telemedicine has important benefits and may serve as an appropriate alternative care option for autism-related treatment and other medical conditions. Despite the rapid expansion of telehealth during the COVID-19 pandemic, racial/ethnic disparities in telemedicine use persisted, with Black or African-American children and young adults having significantly lower odds of telemedicine utilization. These disparities may influence equitable access to telemedicine-based services for autism. Future research efforts should consider implementation and evaluation of strategies to increase uptake of telemedicine in these groups. Health services researchers may consider exploring other relationships between telemedicine, healthcare utilization and patient outcomes.
