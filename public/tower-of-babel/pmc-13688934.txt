Analysis of risk factors for unplanned readmission following acute appendicitis surgery in children
Authors: Yue Wang, Haiyan Lei, Hongxi Guo, Wu Wang, Huan Li, Hongqiang Bian, Xufei Duan, Qin Guo, Jun Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAcute appendicitis (AA) is the most common acute abdominal condition in children. The rate of unplanned readmission within 30 days after appendectomy ranges from 4% to 13%, significantly increasing the healthcare burden and affecting patient outcomes. Identifying independent risk factors for readmission is crucial for optimizing management of high-risk pediatric patients; however, relevant evidence in the Chinese pediatric population remains limited.MethodsThis study employed a single-center retrospective cohort design, enrolling 5,016 pediatric patients who underwent appendectomy for AA at Wuhan Children’s Hospital between July 2013 and February 2025. Perioperative variables were collected via the electronic medical record system, including demographic characteristics, inflammatory markers (WBC, CRP), surgical approach (laparoscopic/open), and appendicitis classification (complicated appendicitis /uncomplicated appendicitis). The primary outcome was unplanned readmission within 30 days after discharge following appendectomy. Multivariate logistic regression analysis was performed to identify independent risk factors (SPSS 27.0), with collinearity diagnostics to ensure model stability. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was used to determine optimal cutoff values for continuous variables.ResultsThe overall readmission rate was 2.4% (118/5,016), with 17.8% (21/118) requiring reoperation. Primary readmission causes included intra-abdominal infection (39.8%), intestinal obstruction (36.4%), and surgical site infection(SSI) (17.8%). 71.2% of readmissions occurred within ≤ 14 days post-discharge (median: 11 days). Independent risk factors included open surgery (aOR = 6.92, 95% CI: 3.65–13.13, P < 0.001), CRP ≥ 53.36 mg/L (aOR = 4.28, 95% CI: 2.62–6.98, P < 0.001), complicated appendicitis (aOR = 1.56, 95% CI: 1.03–2.37, P = 0.037), and operative time > 63.5 min (aOR = 1.82, 95% CI: 1.25–2.66, P = 0.002).ConclusionOpen surgery, elevated CRP (≥ 53.36 mg/L), complicated appendicitis, and prolonged operative time are independent risk factors for readmission following pediatric appendectomy. For children exhibiting these high-risk characteristics, individualized discharge follow-up strategies (e.g., early outpatient evaluation, enhanced complication monitoring) are recommended to reduce readmission risk. Future research should develop predictive risk models and validate the effectiveness of intervention measures.

Background

Acute appendicitis (AA) is the most common cause of acute abdominal pain in children, with a lifetime cumulative risk estimated between 7% and 9%, typically peaking at ages 11–12 [1]. As a potentially serious condition, timely diagnosis and intervention are crucial. Currently, appendectomy remains the gold standard treatment for the vast majority of pediatric AA cases, as it effectively eliminates the source of infection and prevents complications [2, 3]. Despite being one of the most established and routine procedures in general surgery, unplanned readmissions following appendectomy remain a significant concern. These unexpected readmissions not only significantly increase the economic burden and resource consumption on healthcare systems but are also closely associated with poor postoperative outcomes and morbidity in pediatric patients, causing additional physical and emotional distress for children and their families [4, 5]. Literature reports indicate that the unplanned readmission rate within 30 days after pediatric appendectomy ranges from 4% to 13% [4–6], highlighting the prevalence and clinical significance of this issue.

Unplanned readmissions often signal the failure to effectively manage postoperative complications (such as intra-abdominal infections, intestinal obstruction, or SSI) or the emergence of new issues. Therefore, thoroughly analyzing the underlying risk factors contributing to this adverse outcome is crucial for optimizing clinical practice and improving pediatric patient prognosis. By identifying high-risk pediatric populations, clinicians can implement more targeted monitoring strategies, individualized treatment plans (such as more aggressive antimicrobial therapy and detailed discharge instructions), and closer follow-up arrangements during the perioperative period and at discharge. This approach not only effectively reduces unplanned readmission rates and enhances healthcare quality and safety but also facilitates more rational allocation of medical resources. However, despite recognition of the severity of readmission issues, identifying and validating specific risk factors for unplanned readmission following pediatric appendectomy—particularly across different geographic and healthcare center contexts—requires further in-depth research. While existing studies provide readmission rate data, systematic analyses of independent predictors influencing readmission, especially within the Chinese pediatric population, remain relatively limited.

Based on this, the present study aims to systematically identify and evaluate independent risk factors closely associated with unplanned readmission within 30 days following appendectomy for AA in children through a retrospective analysis of large-sample data from a single center. We anticipate that the findings will provide robust evidence-based support for early clinical identification of high-risk pediatric patients, development of individualized intervention strategies, and reduction of unplanned readmission rates.

Methods

Research design and subjects

This study employed a single-center retrospective cohort design. The study population comprised all pediatric patients with AA who underwent appendectomy in the General Surgery Department of Wuhan Children’s Hospital between July 1, 2013, and February 28, 2025. The research protocol was reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of Wuhan Children’s Hospital, with informed consent waived due to the retrospective nature of the study.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion Criteria: (1) Age ≤ 18 years; (2) Clinical and imaging diagnosis (abdominal ultrasound ± CT) consistent with AA; (3) Undergoing emergency appendectomy (laparoscopic or open).

Exclusion Criteria: (1) Concurrent major abdominal surgery (e.g., intestinal resection and anastomosis, enterostomy); (2) Initial non-surgical management (antibiotics and/or percutaneous abscess drainage); (3) Non-acute appendicitis surgery (e.g., interstitial appendectomy); (4) Appendicitis patients who did not undergo surgery; (5) Incomplete clinical data (missing key variables such as CRP, WBC, or operative time).

Data collection

The following perioperative variables were collected via the hospital’s Electronic Medical Record (EMR) system: gender, age, body mass index (BMI), symptom duration (hours from onset of abdominal pain to admission), vital signs upon admission (temperature, heart rate, respiratory rate), and inpatient laboratory parameters: White blood cell count (WBC, ×10⁹/L), C-reactive protein (CRP, mg/L), hemoglobin (g/L). In the absence of a validated Pediatric Appendicitis Score (PAS), white blood cell (WBC) count and C-reactive protein (CRP) level at admission were used as the primary objective surrogate markers for preoperative disease severity in this study. Surgical approach (laparoscopic appendectomy, open appendectomy), operative time (minutes, defined as skin incision to completion of suturing), intraoperative appendicitis classification: Classified according to international consensus standards: complicated appendicitis and uncomplicated appendicitis, total hospitalization duration (days, from admission to discharge), unplanned readmission within 30 days post-discharge (yes/no) (No active post-discharge follow-up (e.g., telephone interview) was performed. Readmission events were identified solely by screening the hospital’s EMR system; therefore, only readmissions to our institution within 30 days of discharge were recorded. Readmissions from other hospitals for which electronic medical records were not generated at this hospital were not included in this analysis.), reason for readmission, time interval between initial discharge and readmission (days), management approach for readmission (conservative treatment/reoperation), readmission hospitalization duration (days).

Definition

Complicated appendicitis

AA complicated by abscess formation, appendiceal gangrene, a mass around the appendix, or appendiceal perforation with or without peritonitis constitutes complicated appendicitis.

Reoperation

Defined as any unplanned surgical intervention requiring a return to the operating room under general anesthesia during the readmission period. This included surgical drainage of intra-abdominal abscess (laparoscopic or open), adhesiolysis for intestinal obstruction, exploratory laparotomy/laparoscopy, or wound exploration/debridement under general anesthesia.

Primary outcome measure

The primary outcome measure is unplanned readmission within 30 days of discharge following appendectomy, defined as readmission to this hospital within 30 days of initial discharge due to complications related to appendectomy (such as infection, intestinal obstruction, or incisional issues).

Grouping

Patients were divided into two groups based on whether they experienced unplanned readmission within 30 days postoperatively: Group A (readmission group): Patients who experienced unplanned readmission. Group B (non-readmission group): Patients who did not experience unplanned readmission.

Surgical standards and quality control

All procedures are performed under the supervision of attending physicians or associate chief physicians holding intermediate-level titles or higher, with assistance from resident physicians, ensuring standardized surgical techniques. Laparoscopic appendectomy is the routine procedure of choice. The decision to convert to open surgery is based on preoperative and intraoperative assessments (e.g., severe adhesions, inflammatory encapsulation obscuring anatomy, difficult hemorrhage control). All appendectomy specimens undergo routine histopathological examination to confirm diagnosis and classification (complicated/uncomplicated). The final definition of complicated appendicitis integrates intraoperative findings and the pathology report.

Perioperative management

Diagnostic process

All patients undergo a standard evaluation upon admission, including a detailed medical history, physical examination, blood tests (complete blood count, CRP, coagulation function), and abdominal ultrasound. If diagnosis is uncertain, an abdominal contrast-enhanced CT scan is performed.

Antibiotic use

Antibiotic therapy followed the hospital’s antimicrobial stewardship program based on Surgical Infection Society/Infectious Diseases Society of America (SIS/IDSA) guidelines [11], using standardized electronic order sets for antibiotic selection, dosing, and duration. While limited patient-specific adjustments (e.g., allergy, renal function, culture results) were permitted under the guideline framework, these were not based on individual physician preference but on clinical indications.

Postoperative monitoring

Vital signs and abdominal findings are routinely monitored postoperatively. Inflammatory markers (WBC, CRP) are rechecked as needed.

Discharge criteria

In our center, discharge following appendectomy was guided by a set of standardized clinical criteria: (1) afebrile (< 37.5 °C) for at least 24 h without antipyretics; (2) a clear downward trend in postoperative C-reactive protein (CRP) and white blood cell (WBC) levels without evidence of persistent or progressive infection; (3) tolerance of oral intake without vomiting or abdominal distension, with return of bowel function (passage of flatus or stool); (4) adequate pain control with oral analgesics alone; and (5) no other complications requiring intervention, such as active intra-abdominal infection.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS Statistics software (version 27.0). Descriptive statistics: Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (Mean ± SD) based on data normality; categorical variables were expressed as frequency and percentage (n (%)). Intergroup comparisons: Independent samples t-tests were used for variables meeting normality and homogeneity of variance; otherwise, the Mann-Whitney U test was employed. Multivariate analysis: Binary logistic regression was used to identify independent risk factors for unplanned readmission. For continuous variables (e.g., age, body temperature, WBC, CRP, operative time), receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis determined optimal cutoff values for predicting readmission. Optimal thresholds were identified by maximizing Youden’s index (sensitivity + specificity − 1). These cutoff values were subsequently converted into dichotomous variables for use in univariate and multivariate analyses. Results were expressed as adjusted odds ratios (aOR) with 95% confidence intervals (95% CI). Model Fitting and Calibration: Model goodness-of-fit was assessed using the Hosmer-Lemeshow test (P > 0.05 indicating adequate fit), and model discriminatory power was evaluated by calculating the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC). Variance inflation factors (VIF) were calculated for all variables in the final model to assess multicollinearity, with VIF < 5 considered acceptable. Any statistical test yielding P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Results were finally presented in tabular and textual formats.

Results

From July 2013 to February 2025, a total of 5,016 pediatric patients with AA underwent appendectomy at Wuhan Children’s Hospital and were included in the final analysis. Baseline characteristics of the study population are detailed in Table 1. Male patients predominated (3,243 cases, 64.7%), while female patients numbered 1,773 (35.3%). The mean age was 8.18 ± 3.15 years (range: 0.27–17.91 years). The vast majority of procedures (4,942, 98.5%) initially employed a laparoscopic approach, with 74 cases (1.5%) converted to open appendectomy during surgery. The overall open conversion rate was 74 cases (1.5%). The mean operative time was 58.4 ± 12.63 min. Based on intraoperative and pathological findings, 1,759 cases (35.1%) were classified as complicated appendicitis (gangrene, perforation, and/or abscess), while 3,257 cases (64.9%) were uncomplicated appendicitis. Within 30 days of discharge following surgery, 118 patients (2.4%) were readmitted to our institution due to appendectomy-related complications (Group A). Because readmissions to outside hospitals were not tracked, this figure represents a minimum incidence rather than the true population readmission rate. The remaining 4,898 patients (97.6%) did not experience readmission at our institution within 30 days (Group B).

Table 1 details the comparative results of demographic, clinical, and surgical characteristics between the two patient groups: The mean age of patients in the readmission group (Group A) (7.55 ± 3.02 years) was significantly lower than that of the non-readmission group (Group B) (8.20 ± 3.15 years) (P = 0.026). Group A’s mean temperature at admission (37.5 ± 0.97 °C) was significantly higher than Group B’s (37.1 ± 0.84 °C) (P < 0.001). Group A’s mean WBC count at admission (14.9 ± 5.01 × 10⁹/L) was significantly higher than Group B’s (12.8 ± 6.13 × 10⁹/L) (P < 0.001). Group A’s admission CRP (99.1 ± 62.45 mg/L) was significantly higher than Group B’s (55.8 ± 64.97 mg/L) (P < 0.001). Regarding surgical approach, Group A’s laparoscopic surgery rate was significantly lower than Group B’s (88.1% vs. 98.8%, P < 0.001). The open surgery rate in Group A was significantly higher than that in Group B (11.9% vs. 1.2%, P < 0.001). The primary reason for open surgery in most cases was severe inflammation/adhesions impeding safe laparoscopic manipulation. The proportion of complicated appendicitis in Group A (57.6%, 68/118) was significantly higher than in Group B (34.5%, 1,691/4,898) (P < 0.001). The mean operative time in Group A (61.3 ± 11.15 min) was significantly longer than that in Group B (58.3 ± 12.66 min) (P = 0.012). No statistically significant differences were observed between the two groups in gender distribution, body mass index (BMI), hemoglobin level at admission, duration of symptoms (time from abdominal pain onset to admission), or length of initial hospitalization (all P > 0.05).

Table 2 lists the causes of unplanned readmissions within 30 days of discharge following appendectomy. The most common causes included intra-abdominal infection (47 cases, 39.8%), intestinal obstruction (43 cases, 36.4%), and SSI (21 cases, 17.8%). Among the 118 patients with unplanned readmission, 50 (42.4%) were classified as having uncomplicated appendicitis. In this subgroup, the most common causes for readmission were intestinal obstruction (22/50, 44%) and SSI (16/50, 32%), whereas intra-abdominal infection was observed in only 8 patients (16%). This pattern differed notably from the complicated appendicitis subgroup (n = 68), in which intra-abdominal infection was the predominant cause (39/68, 57%). These findings suggest that readmissions in patients with uncomplicated appendicitis are driven primarily by postoperative surgical complications (e.g., adhesions, SSI) rather than by infectious progression of the original disease. Other causes, such as enteritis and incisional hernia, accounted for a lower proportion. Figure 1 shows the timing of unplanned readmissions following appendectomy discharge. Most readmissions (71.2%, 84/118) occurred within the first two weeks after initial discharge, with a median time to readmission of 11 days post-discharge. Mean readmission length of stay was 9.7 ± 5.98 days (range: 1–41 days). Among readmitted patients, 82.2% (97/118) were successfully managed with conservative treatment (intravenous antibiotics, bowel rest, supportive care). The remaining 17.8% (21/118) required reoperation under general anesthesia, including adhesiolysis for small bowel obstruction (n = 12), surgical drainage of intra-abdominal abscesses (n = 5), intestinal perforation repair (n = 2), purulent omentectomy (n = 1), and laparoscopic incisional hernia repair (n = 1).

Discussion

This retrospective cohort analysis of 5,016 pediatric patients with AA systematically investigated risk factors and clinical characteristics associated with unplanned readmission within 30 days of discharge following appendectomy. Our key findings include: (1) The 30-day unplanned readmission rate was 2.4%, with 17.8% of readmitted patients requiring reoperation; (2) Intra-abdominal infection (39.8%) and intestinal obstruction (36.4%) were the primary causes of readmission, occurring predominantly within two weeks post-discharge (71.2%); (3) Multivariate analysis further confirmed that open appendectomy, complicated appendicitis, baseline CRP ≥ 53.36 mg/L, and operative time > 63.5 min were independent risk factors for readmission. Previous studies have also reported operative time as an independent risk factor for readmission [7]. These findings provide important evidence-based guidance for identifying high-risk patients, optimizing perioperative management, and developing post-discharge follow-up strategies.

The readmission rate observed in this study (2.4%) was significantly lower than the average rate (4.8%) reported in a recent meta-analysis of multinational studies [8]. This discrepancy may be attributed to the relatively longer postoperative hospitalization duration and strict discharge criteria at our center, which facilitated more effective control of early complications. However, over 70% of readmissions occurred within 14 days of discharge, suggesting that most complications had underlying signs present during the initial hospitalization, necessitating enhanced early identification and intervention. Intra-abdominal infection was the primary cause of readmission, consistent with previous pediatric studies [6, 9, 10]. This underscores the importance of continuous postoperative monitoring for infection signs and optimized antimicrobial stewardship (strictly adhering to SIS/IDSA guidelines [11]). Intestinal obstruction was the second most common cause in this study (36.4%). Its overall incidence (0.8%) aligns with the reported range of intestinal obstruction after pediatric appendectomy in the literature (0.16%-10.7%) [12–14], suggesting that although its incidence is low, it often leads to readmission when it occurs.

Comprehensive risk factor assessment is crucial for identifying patients at high risk for postoperative complications, optimizing clinical decision-making, and allocating resources. Multivariate analysis revealed that pediatric patients undergoing open appendectomy faced nearly a sevenfold increased risk of readmission compared to those undergoing laparoscopic surgery (aOR = 6.92, 95% CI: 3.65–13.13). This finding aligns with multiple studies [7, 15], underscoring the significant advantage of laparoscopic surgery as the preferred approach in reducing infectious complications and readmission risk. However, it must be recognized that the choice of open surgery often reflects more severe local inflammation (e.g., dense adhesions, abscess formation), which itself indicates a high risk of complication [6]. Therefore, this association may be partly attributable to disease severity rather than solely to differences in surgical approach. Nevertheless, children requiring open surgery should be considered extremely high-risk patients, warranting enhanced postoperative monitoring and follow-up after discharge.

Children with complicated appendicitis (gangrene, perforation, abscess) exhibited significantly elevated readmission risk (aOR = 1.56). This aligns with the disease’s nature—tissue necrosis, high bacterial contamination load, and intense local inflammatory response substantially increase postoperative infectious complications (e.g., abdominal abscess) and adhesive intestinal obstruction risks [6]. This finding reaffirms disease severity as a core driver of adverse outcomes. In this large pediatric cohort, an admission CRP level ≥ 53.36 mg/L was established as a strong independent predictor of readmission (aOR = 4.28). As an acute-phase reactant protein, elevated CRP levels directly reflect the intensity of systemic inflammation and correlate closely with tissue damage and bacterial load [6].This threshold (53.36 mg/L) provides a simple, objective laboratory marker for early clinical identification of high-risk pediatric patients.

Surgery duration > 63.5 min was also independently associated with readmission risk (aOR = 1.82). Prolonged surgery duration often reflects increased technical difficulty (e.g., severe adhesions, anatomical challenges) or complex intraoperative management (e.g., extensive pus irrigation, difficult hemostasis) [7], indirectly indicating disease severity and surgical trauma. It serves as a real-time indicator for surgeons to assess postoperative risks. Furthermore, our study indicates that a portion (17.8%) of unplanned readmitted patients required reoperation under general anesthesia, underscoring the severity of complications in this subgroup. This finding highlights the importance of early identification and management of high-risk patients to potentially prevent progression to the most severe outcomes. Given the significantly higher readmission rate within 2 weeks post-discharge for most patients (Fig. 1), improving post-discharge care and patient education could reduce readmission rates.

Based on the above findings, we propose the following clinical practice recommendations: (1) Risk-Stratified Follow-up: Children with any one or multiple risk factors—open surgery, complicated appendicitis, CRP ≥ 53.36 mg/L at admission, or operative time > 63.5 min—should be considered high-risk for readmission. For these patients, we recommend earlier outpatient follow-up (e.g., within 1 week of discharge) rather than prolonged inpatient observation. This approach balances the need for close monitoring with the goal of minimizing unnecessary hospitalization. (2) Prioritize laparoscopic techniques: Whenever technically feasible and safe, laparoscopic appendectomy should remain the preferred surgical approach for AA(especially in children) to minimize readmission risk. (3) Enhanced Discharge Education: Develop personalized discharge instructions for high-risk families, emphasizing education on recognizing early signs of abdominal infection (persistent fever, worsening abdominal pain, vomiting) and intestinal obstruction (abdominal distension, cessation of flatus and bowel movements). Provide clear guidance on the importance of prompt return to care.(4) Accessible Post-Discharge Support: Establish convenient postoperative consultation channels (telephone/online platforms) to enable families to promptly report concerns, potentially averting complications before they necessitate readmission.

It must be emphasized that we are not advocating for an extension of the already relatively long initial length of stay in this study cohort. On the contrary, we recognize that the current length of stay exceeds international standards, which in itself may be a target for optimization. In Western countries, same-day discharge for patients with uncomplicated appendicitis has become increasingly common [16]. This reflects differences between China and Western countries in healthcare systems, reimbursement models, and clinical practices. It is worth noting that despite the relatively long initial observation period, 2.4% of patients still required readmission, with a median readmission time of 11 days post-discharge. This suggests that the clinical manifestations of many complications are delayed, extending even beyond the duration of our extended hospital stay. Future research should explore whether safely shortening the length of stay for low-risk patients, while strengthening post-discharge support for high-risk patients, can optimize treatment outcomes and resource utilization within the Chinese healthcare context. Based on the identified risk factors, we propose establishing a standardized clinical pathway for pediatric appendicitis to systematically reduce readmission rates. This pathway incorporates preoperative (admission CRP ≥ 53.36 mg/L), intraoperative (complicated appendicitis, operative time > 63.5 min, open conversion), and postoperative elements for risk stratification. High-risk patients would receive enhanced monitoring including early outpatient follow-up (within 1week post-discharge), reinforced discharge education focused on recognizing early signs of infection or obstruction, and accessible post-discharge consultation channels. Low-risk patients would follow routine protocols. The effectiveness of this proposed pathway in reducing readmissions while optimizing care requires validation through future prospective studies or quality improvement initiatives.

The primary strengths of this study lie in its large sample size (n = 5,016) and comprehensive analysis of multiple perioperative variables, providing reliable data specific to the Chinese pediatric population. However, the following limitations should be considered: (1) Retrospective, single-center design: Results may be constrained by the completeness of medical records and the diagnostic and treatment practices of a single institution; caution is required when extrapolating conclusions. (2) Limitations of the readmission definition and follow-up methods: No active post-discharge follow-up was conducted; all readmission events were passively identified through the hospital’s electronic medical record system. Only readmissions due to complications that resulted in the patient’s return to our hospital were counted, while cases transferred to other hospitals were excluded, which may have led to an underestimation of the actual readmission rate. Therefore, the reported readmission rate of 2.4% should be considered a minimum estimate. However, Wuhan Children’s Hospital is the sole tertiary pediatric surgical referral center for the Wuhan metropolitan area and surrounding regions. Most community hospitals and emergency departments in this region routinely transfer pediatric surgical emergencies to our center, which partially mitigates the risk of missing out-of-hospital readmissions. Nevertheless, we cannot rule out the possibility that some patients—particularly those residing far from the hospital—may have sought care at other medical institutions.(3) Antibiotic therapy was administered in accordance with the hospital’s antimicrobial stewardship program, which follows the Surgical Infection Society/Infectious Diseases Society of America (SIS/IDSA) guidelines. Although the medical records documented specific antibiotic regimens, dosages, and the duration of post-discharge oral antibiotic therapy, they did not consistently provide complete data on post-discharge antibiotic adherence, which constitutes a limitation of this study. However, recent evidence suggests that post-discharge antibiotic duration may not be the primary driver of readmission risk. In a study of children with perforated appendicitis, Cobb et al. [17] found no significant difference in 30- or 60-day readmission rates between patients discharged with no antibiotics, short-course antibiotics (1–7 days), or longer-course antibiotics (8 + days). This suggests that when adequate surgical source control is achieved, the impact of post-discharge antibiotic variation on readmission may be limited. Nevertheless, we strongly recommend that future prospective studies incorporate detailed antibiotic data to definitively address this question and to determine whether specific antibiotic strategies could further mitigate risk in the high-risk subgroups we have identified (e.g., those with open surgery or extremely elevated CRP).(4) Lack of long-term outcome and quality-of-life data: The study focused solely on 30-day readmissions and did not evaluate the impact of readmission events on children’s long-term recovery, quality of life, or healthcare costs. (5) Potential misclassification of appendicitis type: We did not perform a systematic re-review of pathology slides for patients classified as having uncomplicated appendicitis. Although all diagnoses were based on standardized intraoperative findings and routine histopathological examination, the possibility of occult perforations or microabscesses cannot be entirely excluded. Future studies with centralized pathology review are warranted.

Conclusion

This study confirmed that the rate of unplanned readmission within 30 days after appendectomy for AA in children was 2.4%, with nearly one-fifth requiring reoperation. Intra-abdominal infection and intestinal obstruction were the primary causes, and most readmissions occurred in the early post-discharge period (≤ 14 days). Open appendectomy, complicated appendicitis, baseline CRP ≥ 53.36 mg/dL, and operative time > 63.5 min were identified as independent risk factors for readmission. These findings underscore the necessity of individualized perioperative management, enhanced post-discharge monitoring, and early intervention for high-risk pediatric patients, particularly those undergoing open surgery, presenting with severe inflammatory response (CRP ≥ 53.36 mg/L), or having complex pathology. Future research should focus on developing risk prediction tools and evaluating the effectiveness of targeted interventions.
