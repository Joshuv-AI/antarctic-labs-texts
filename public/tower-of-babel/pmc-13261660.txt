Structural basis for the activation of an LRR receptor by a single-disulfide-bridged peptide signal
Authors: Yifan Bai, Pei Wei, Lijun Wang, Cheng Chi, Cuicui Du, Zhifu Han, Jinfang Yu, Xiao-Quan Wang, Shuguo Hou, Yu Xiao
Published: 2026

Dear Editor,

Intramolecular disulfide bonds can modulate overall peptide rigidity and improve structural stability, thereby serving as essential post-translational modifications for signaling small secreted peptides (SSPs). Despite the widespread distribution of signaling SSPs with a single intramolecular disulfide bond, such as oxytocin, urotensin Ⅱ, vasopressin, and somatostatin in animals, few analogous SSPs have been characterized in plants (Daly and Wilson, 2021). To date, only delta-like phytocytokines (DEPs) (Wang et al., 2025) have been functionally characterized as such SSPs in plants. SMALL PHYTOCYTOKINES REGULATING DEFENSE AND WATER LOSS (SCREW) (Liu et al., 2022)/CTNIPs (Rhodes et al., 2022) are phytocytokines that activate immune responses (e.g., mitogen-activated protein kinase [MAPK] phosphorylation) and regulate stomatal closure through PLANT SCREW UNRESPONSIVE RECEPTOR (NUT)/HAESA-LIKE 3 (HSL3), which belongs to the leucine-rich repeat receptor-like kinase (LRR-RLK) XI subfamily. SCREW peptides activate NUT by promoting its heterodimerization with BRASSINOSTEROID INSENSITIVE 1-ASSOCIATED KINASE 1 (BAK1) (Liu et al., 2022). However, whether the biological function of SCREW2 depends on conformational changes induced by its disulfide bond and the underlying mechanisms governing the assembly of NUT–SCREW–BAK1 complexes remain elusive.

To address these questions, we purified the extracellular LRR domains of Arabidopsis NUT and BAK1 (NUTLRR and BAK1LRR, respectively) from insect cells and chemically synthesized the 23-residue SCREW2 peptide (Supplemental Figures 1A–1C; Supplemental Tables 1 and 2). Isothermal titration calorimetry (ITC) assays confirmed that SCREW2 (naturally oxidized form, unless otherwise specified) and SCREW2C–C (synthesized with a preformed C58–C68 disulfide bond) bind NUTLRR with comparable Kd values of 8.49 and 4.20 μM, respectively (Figure 1A and Supplemental Figures 2A and 2B). Both peptides also exhibited high activity in MAPK assays (Figure 1B). In contrast, unoxidized SCREW2 and SCREW2CC/SS (C58S/C68S) showed no detectable binding (Supplemental Figures 2A and 2B). Consistently, MAPK activation was significantly reduced when triggered by reducing agent-treated SCREW2 or SCREW2CC/SS (Figure 1B). Multimers were detected neither in naturally oxidized, chemically synthesized SCREW2 peptides nor in transiently expressed SCREW2 precursor proteins in plants, even following exogenous supply of H2O2 or treatment with flagellin 22, an immune elicitor known to induce endogenous reactive oxygen species production, suggesting that SCREW2 does not form intermolecular disulfide bonds either in vitro or in vivo (Supplemental Figures 1C and 1D). These results indicate that intramolecular disulfide bond formation is essential for SCREW2 activity and binding to NUT. Subsequently, we reconstituted the NUTLRR–SCREW2–BAK1LRR complex using gel filtration (Figure 1C) and resolved its cryo-electron microscopy (cryo-EM) structure at 3.48 Å resolution (Figure 1D and Supplemental Figures 3A–3E; Supplemental Table 3). In this structure, SCREW2 acts as a molecular glue, stabilizing the NUTLRR–BAK1LRR heterodimer (Figure 1D and Supplemental Figure 4A). NUTLRR adopts a superhelical architecture, with 15 N–glycans distributed along its 22–LRR solenoid (Figure 1D and Supplemental Figure 4A). The electron density map clearly reveals that SCREW2 adopts a cyclic loop conformation spanning residues P49–N70, stabilized by a disulfide bond between C58 and C68 (Figure 1E). The concave surface of NUT LRR3–15 binds SCREW2, whereas the descending lateral side of NUT LRR16–22 contacts the concave surface of the BAK1 LRRs (Figure 1D–1G). Notably, structural superposition with the HAE LRR–INFLORESCENCE DEFICIENT IN ABSCISSION (IDA)–SOMATIC EMBRYOGENESIS RECEPTOR-LIKE KINASE 1LRR (SERK1RR) complex reveals that the ligand-binding pocket of NUTLRR extensively overlaps with that of HAELRR. This shared pocket is occupied by a similar PVPxSGPN/S motif located in IDA/IDA-LIKEs and the N-terminal region of SCREWs (P49–N56), explaining the close phylogenetic relationship between NUT (HSL3) and HAE/HSL1/HSL2 (Santiago et al., 2016; Roman et al., 2022) (Supplemental Figures 5A and 5B). Furthermore, the cyclic loop structure of SCREW2 exhibits a larger contact area with BAK1 than does IDA with SERK1 (300.4 vs. 248 Å2), which may enhance co-receptor recruitment and stabilize the ternary complex (Supplemental Figure 5C).

Extensive interactions mediate recognition of SCREW2 by NUTLRR. In addition to the known RxR motif, numerous ligand-interacting residues on NUT differ from those on HAE/HSL1/HSL2 (Supplemental Table 4; Supplemental Figure 5D). In the N-terminal region, SCREW2P49, SCREW2S53, and SCREW2N56 form hydrogen bonds with NUTLRR Q120, NUTLRR Y214/H239, and NUTLRR D288/S290, respectively. SCREW2N56 also forms van der Waals contacts with NUTLRR F267. In the central segment (G57–P62), hydrogen bonds are detected between SCREW2T59 and NUTLRR E360, as well as between SCREW2N60 and NUTLRR Y386. Additionally, SCREW2I61– P62 insert into a hydrophobic region formed by NUTLRR residues (G382, V384, L405, T406 and Y429). Within the C-terminal region (R63–N70), SCREW2R63 forms a hydrogen bond with NUTLRR R358 and SCREW2H69 forms a salt bridge with NUTLRR E218 (Figure 1G). For SCREW2-induced NUTLRR–BAK1LRR heterodimerization, the CTNIP motif of SCREW2 is sandwiched between NUTLRR and the recruitment loop of BAK1LRR. Simultaneously, BAK1LRR T52 inserts directly into the center of the SCREW2 cyclic loop and forms hydrogen bonds with SCREW2T59 and the main chain of SCREW2G66 (Figure 1F). These interactions bring BAK1LRR into close proximity with NUTLRR, facilitating further extensive contacts between NUTLRR and BAK1LRR (Supplemental Figure 4B).

To verify the structural observations, we replaced key residues at the interaction interface and evaluated the effects both in vitro and in vivo (Supplemental Figures 1A and 1B; Supplemental Table 1). ITC assays showed that SCREW2 binding was abolished by NUTLRR E360Y (Supplemental Figures 2A and 2B). Except for the binding of SCREW2 N60W to NUTLRR, the binding affinities of SCREW2 T59W, I61W, and P62W for NUTLRR, as well as those of SCREW2 for NUTLRR G382R, T406Y, and S290A/F267A, were reduced by approximately 2- to 7-fold (Supplemental Figures 2A and 2B). Consistently, SCREW2 variants showed reduced activity in inducing MAPK phosphorylation in Arabidopsis, and SCREW2-induced MAPK activation was substantially impaired in Nicotiana benthamiana transiently expressing NUT mutants (Figure 1H and 1I). Moreover, mutations in BAK1LRR (T52W and T52G/L53G/V54G) abolished BAK1LRR binding to NUTLRR–SCREW2 in ITC assays (Supplemental Figures 1A, 1B, 2A, and 2B), and SCREW2-induced NUTLRR–BAK1LRR interaction was also disrupted when these mutations were introduced into BAK1 in coimmunoprecipitation assays (Figure 1J). Furthermore, we demonstrated that the disulfide bridge and residues N56 and I61 are critical for SCREW2 function in suppressing abscisic acid and flagellin 22 induced stomatal closure (Figure 1K). Collectively, these data underscore the importance of key residues in the NUTLRR–SCREW2–BAK1LRR interaction.

Phylogenetic analysis of LRR-RLKs across 38 representative plant species identified a monophyletic NUT superclade containing 127 members within the LRR-RLK XI family (Supplemental Figures 6A–6C and 7A). Most members carry 22 LRR repeats, with minor variations, suggesting their potential capacity to recognize SCREW-like peptides (Supplemental Figures 6D and 7B; Supplemental Table 5). Phylogenetic analysis indicated that NUT initially emerged in gymnosperms and subsequently underwent pronounced lineage-specific expansion in angiosperms (Supplemental Figure 7A). We divided the NUT superclade into two groups, the gymnosperm and angiosperm groups, and ten subgroups (a–j) (Supplemental Figure 6C). In dicots, excluding basal lineages, NUT homologs separate into two branches, the NUT clade (subgroup g) and a sister clade, termed the NUT-like (NUTL) clade (subgroup f). Most species, such as tomato and soybean, retain members in both clades, whereas Arabidopsis lacks NUTL. In monocots, excluding basal lineages, NUT homologs similarly cluster into two sister branches (subgroups i and j). Notably, in Poaceae species, the subclades have undergone further expansion, as indicated previously (Chihiro et al., 2021; Rhodes et al., 2022), and divide into at least four branches. Of these, DEP receptor 1 (DEPR1) from subgroup i and OsRLK5 (subgroup j) recognize the DEP peptide and immunopeptide peptide, respectively (Sun et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2025), whereas DCC RECEPTOR 1 (DCCR1) from subgroup i recognizes DUAL CYSTEINES IN C-TERMINUS (DCC) peptides with two consecutive C-terminal cysteines (Yin et al., 2025) (Supplemental Figure 7C). Moreover, we identified numerous SCREW/DEP or SCREW/DEP-like peptides in these representative plant species (Supplemental Figures 8A–8C; Supplemental Table 6). Phylogenetic analysis clearly separated dicot SCREWs and monocot DEPs into two distinct groups (Supplemental Figure 8C), and amino acid frequency analysis revealed that residues corresponding to SCREW2 V50 and I61 clearly distinguish SCREW peptides from DEP peptides (Supplemental Figure 8D).

Sequence alignment and amino acid frequency analysis revealed that NUT ligand-binding residues are highly conserved in the NUT clade but diverge in the NUTL/DCCR/DEPR/RLK5 clades (Supplemental Figure 9A). Structural comparison of the NUTLRR–SCREW2–BAK1LRR and DEPRLRR–DEP2–SERK2LRR complexes revealed several conserved interaction modes, such as NUTD288/S290–SCREW2N56, DEPR1D272/S274–DEP2N11, and the SCREW2CTNIP/DEP2CTNNP motifs sandwiched by NUT–BAK1/DEPR–SERK2, but also numerous differences (Supplemental Figures 9B–9D). For example, SCREW2 I61 from the SCREW2CTNIP motif interacts with NUTG382, which is strictly conserved only in the NUT clade, whereas DEP2 N16 from the DEP2CTNNP motif additionally forms hydrogen bonds with DEPRS369. Notably, compared with SCREW2, DEP2 exhibits a longer N-terminal region and a larger cyclic loop, which are incompatible with the ligand-binding pocket of NUT (Supplemental Figure 9C). In the structural alignment, significant conformational clashes occur between DEP2A7,G8 and NUTH239,Y214, as well as between DEP2P20 and NUTR358 (Supplemental Figure 9E). Consistently, further ITC assays confirmed that there was no detectable interaction between DEP2 and NUTLRR (Supplemental Figures 2B and 2C). These findings suggest that the receptor-binding pockets of the dicot NUT–SCREW and monocot DEPR–DEP systems have undergone substantial divergence. Given that the kinase domains of NUT and DEPR are also likely incompatible, future characterization of their intracellular divergence will be essential to fully explain why DEP2 can induce MAPK phosphorylation across a broad range of Poaceae species but not in the dicot Arabidopsis (Wang et al., 2025). Taken together, our results demonstrate that, although receptors from the NUT, NUTL, DEPR, DCCR, and RLK5 clades all belong to the NUT superclade, their extracellular receptor-ligand recognition modes have undergone substantial diversification.

We then performed a bioinformatic search of all Arabidopsis SSPs to identify other putative peptides encoded by Arabidopsis with one disulfide bridge that contributes to chain folding. Thirty-one candidate peptides exhibiting an intrinsically disordered conformation and containing two cysteines capable of forming a disulfide bridge were identified (Supplemental Table 7). Intriguingly, several members of the SERINE-RICH ENDOGENOUS PEPTIDE family also possess two spatially adjacent cysteine residues with potential bonding capacity. Notably, AT2G25125.1 and AT2G01379.1 appear to define a new candidate peptide family whose conformational folding relies on a single disulfide bridge, as their sequences consistently comprise a signal peptide, a variable region, and a conserved C-terminal mature region (Supplemental Figures 10A and 10B).

In parallel with our study, two independent studies reported crystal and cryo-EM structures of the binary NUTLRR–SCREW2 complex (Jiménez-Sandoval et al., 2025; Jung et al., 2025). Together with these two studies, our structural and functional analysis of the NUTLRR–SCREW2–BAK1LRR complex shows that SCREW2 adopts a single disulfide-bridged cyclic structure that is essential for NUT recognition and BAK1 recruitment, extending current understanding beyond the prevailing model that the LRR-RLK XI subfamily recognizes only linear peptides (Figure 1L). Previous studies have largely focused on cysteine-rich peptides, such as LURE-type cysteine-rich peptides, epidermal patterning factors, and small cysteine-rich peptides, which contain multiple disulfide bonds and adopt stable α/β folds (Matsubayashi, 2014). These disulfide bonds are essential for maintaining their structural integrity. In contrast, SSPs harboring only a single disulfide bond remain poorly characterized. Our structural and biochemical analyses show that SCREW adopts a single disulfide-stabilized loop conformation that is required for binding to the concave surface of the NUT LRR domain. Bioinformatic and structural predictions further suggest that Arabidopsis may encode additional peptides with similar features. These findings align with previous reports indicating that the wheat genome encodes over 500 SSPs harboring 2 cysteine residues (Tian et al., 2022). Such disulfide-stabilized structures may be highly sensitive to cellular redox fluctuations. Thus, plants may regulate peptide-mediated signaling by coupling redox fluctuations, particularly reactive oxygen species dynamics, to the reversible activation of these peptides, thereby enhancing environmental adaptability. In conclusion, further investigation of single disulfide-bridged peptides and their cognate receptors, within and beyond the NUT superclade, is warranted.
