Enhancing Pickering emulsion stabilization of insoluble rice bran natural complexes: Structure-dependent interfacial regulation by catechin monomers
Authors: Han Cui, Helin Li, Enyan Yu, Xiaojuan Wu, Lizhong Lin, Wei Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Rice bran is an abundant agricultural by-product rich in proteins, polysaccharides, and polyphenols, exhibiting potential for Pickering emulsion stabilizers. However, these nutrients predominantly exist as insoluble rice bran natural complexes (IRBNC), which are underutilized due to their compact structure, limited wettability, and poor interfacial activity. To overcome these limitations and optimize the interfacial properties of IRBNC for enhanced emulsification, three catechin monomers with varying numbers of phenolic hydroxyl groups and galloyl moieties—epicatechin (EC), epigallocatechin (EGC), and epicatechin gallate (ECG) were selected as structural models to modify IRBNC. Appropriate concentrations of EC (25 mg/g), EGC (50 mg/g), and ECG (25 mg/g) decreased the ordered structure of proteins in IRBNC, shifted particle wettability from highly hydrophobic (127.015°) to amphiphilic (ranging from 86.976° to 95.695°), loosened the compact layered microstructure, thereby improving interfacial activity (equilibrium interfacial tension: 20.43 to 16.19 mN/m). Consequently, Pickering emulsion stabilized by IRBNC modified with appropriate concentrations of EC, EGC, and ECG exhibited smaller average droplet size (from 59.94 μm to 46.32 μm) and lower creaming index values (from 23.9% to 14.5%) compared with unmodified IRBNC. Moreover, appropriate catechin modification inhibited the formation of lipid oxidation products in IRBNC-stabilized Pickering emulsions during storage. Notably, modification efficiencies followed a structure-dependent trend (ECG > EGC > EC), with ECG exhibiting the strongest effect due to its galloyl substitution and higher phenolic hydroxyl density. Overall, appropriate catechin modification, particularly with ECG, represents an effective strategy to tailor the structural and interfacial properties of IRBNC for enhanced Pickering emulsion stabilization.

Introduction

Rice bran (RB) is a by-product generated during the processing of rice into polished rice, with a global annual output of over 63 million tons (Zhuang et al., 2019). Due to the high oil content (12–23%), RB is primarily utilized for RB oil production, whereas the defatted RB obtained after oil extraction contains abundant protein (about 15.85%), polysaccharides (about 57.93%), and phenolics (about 8.26 mg/g) (Li et al., 2024b). The proteins, bioactive peptides, dietary fibers and phenolic compounds isolated from defatted RB have attracted increasing research interest due to their functional properties and potential food-related applications (Bonifacino et al., 2020; Chen et al., 2025; Senna et al., 2025). During the extraction of soluble protein and bioactive polysaccharides, a large amount of RB residue (about 52.51%) was produced, in which most of the unutilized proteins and insoluble polysaccharides combined to form the insoluble rice bran natural complex (IRBNC) (Li et al., 2025a). Due to the strong interactions among the IRBNC components, traditional separation techniques are difficult to extract, resulting in low utilization of defatted RB (Li et al., 2024b). Therefore, the overall utilization of IRBNC rather than through hierarchical extraction is a new strategy to achieve high-value utilization of RB resources.

Unlike artificially synthesized complexes, natural complexes of plant origin are formed by cross-linking of proteins, polysaccharides, and polyphenols during plant growth, resulting in the construction of complex multiscale synergistic networks (Mnich et al., 2020). Consequently, compared with synthetic complexes, natural complexes exhibit higher structural integrity, richer intermolecular interactions, and more stable lamellar structures, providing a unique structural basis for subsequent interfacial modulation and functionalization (Liu et al., 2023b, 2025). However, natural complexes binding ratios and spatial distribution within natural complexes could not be precisely controlled, which led to structures with uneven particle size distribution or insufficient intermolecular association (Liu et al., 2025). The structural characteristics hindered the formation of continuous and dense interfacial films, thus limiting the application in emulsion.

To improve the wettability and interfacial stabilization capacity of IRBNC in emulsion systems, mechanical pretreatment has been employed to disrupt particle aggregated structures and promote the formation of an ordered network structure, thereby regulating surface structural rearrangement and the exposure of hydrophilic groups, improving particle hydrophilicity and ultimately enhancing Pickering emulsion stability (Li et al., 2024b). However, mechanical treatment typically primarily affects particle size and morphology, whereas it has limited capacity to modulate surface chemistry and interfacial affinity (Ghelichi et al., 2023). Moreover, emulsion stability is a multidimensional concept, involving physical stability and oxidative stability during storage (Schröder et al., 2021). To further improve the overall stability of emulsion systems, tea polyphenols have been widely incorporated because of their excellent antioxidant capacity and their ability to interact with proteins and polysaccharides through hydrogen bonding, hydrophobic interactions, and covalent conjugation (Wang et al., 2022b; Li et al., 2023). These interactions can induce conformational rearrangement of complexes, alter particle wettability and aggregation behavior, while simultaneously forming a dense antioxidant interfacial barrier that inhibits lipid oxidation. Consequently, both the physical and oxidative stabilities of the resulting emulsions are significantly improved. Nevertheless, most current studies have focused on soluble protein–polyphenol complexes or have employed mixed polyphenol extracts, while systematic investigations on the structure-dependent effects of individual catechin monomers on insoluble natural complexes such as IRBNC remain scarce (Yuan et al., 2025; Dai et al., 2024). In particular, the distinct contributions of phenolic hydroxyl groups and galloyl moieties to the interfacial modulation of insoluble particles have not been clearly distinguished.

Among tea polyphenols, catechins account for approximately 60–80% of the total tea polyphenol content (Luo et al., 2024). Catechins comprise several flavan-3-ol monomers, among which epicatechin (EC), epigallocatechin (EGC), epicatechin gallate (ECG), and epigallocatechin gallate (EGCG) are present in high amounts and are representative in terms of structural characteristics such as the number of phenolic hydroxyl groups and the presence or absence of galloyl groups (Luo et al., 2024), which is beneficial for comparing the effects of different catechin structures on the modification effect and mechanism of action of IRBNC. Our previous study reported EGCG-modified IRBNC exhibited enhanced interfacial viscoelasticity, strengthened interfacial film formation, and improved Pickering emulsion stability (Li et al., 2025a). However, it remains unclear whether these beneficial effects are unique to EGCG or depend universally on specific catechin structural features, namely hydroxyl group density and galloyl substitution, and their subsequent impacts on interfacial adsorption and emulsion stabilization.

To systematically elucidate these structure-dependent mechanisms, EC, EGC, and ECG were selected as representative catechin monomers with distinct structural characteristics to establish a structure-function relationship between catechin molecular architecture and IRBNC modification. We hypothesized that differences in hydroxyl group density and galloyl substitution would result in distinct interaction behaviors with IRBNC, leading to different degrees of structural rearrangement, wettability regulation, and interfacial adsorption. These changes would ultimately determine the physical and oxidative stability of Pickering emulsions stabilized by the modified particles. Accordingly, this study systematically investigated the interactions between IRBNC and different concentrations of EC, EGC, and ECG, as well as the effects of these catechin monomers on the structural characteristics and emulsion stabilization performance of IRBNC. This study provides new insights into the structure-dependent modification of natural protein–polysaccharide complexes by catechins and offers a theoretical basis for the rational design of highly stable Pickering emulsion systems.

Materials and methods

Materials

Fresh rice bran (RB) derived from newly harvested Huang Hua Zhan rice was supplied by Jinquan Rice Factory (Ningxiang County, China). EC (98%, CAS No. 490-46-0), EGC (98%, CAS No. 970-74-1), and ECG (98%, CAS No. 1257-08-5) were purchased from Shanxi Dongshuo Biotechnology (Xi'an, China). Analytical reagents were sourced from Sinopharm Chemical Reagent (Beijing, China).

IRBNC preparation

Following Li et al. (2024b), the defatted RB was dispersed in deionized water (1:10, w/w), adjusted to pH 9.0 using NaOH (2.0 mol/L), while the mixture was continuously stirred at 500 rpm in a 40°C water bath for 4 h. The dispersion was centrifuged at 8000 × g for 20 min at 4°C. The obtained precipitate was re-dispersed in deionized water at a ratio of 1:5 (w/w), followed by pH adjustment to 4.0 using 2.0 mol/L HCl. The acidic dispersion was autoclaved (120°C, 1 h), and then centrifuged (7000 × g, 15 min, 4°C). The resulting precipitate was redispersed in deionized water (1:5, w/w), adjusted to pH 7.0 with 2.0 mol/L NaOH, dialyzed against deionized water for 72 h, and freeze-dried to obtain IRBNC, which consisted of proteins (22.54%), carbohydrates (66.48%), and phenolic compounds (11.05 mg/g).

Preparation of EC-IRBNC, EGC-IRBNC, and ECG-IRBNC complexes

Following Li et al. (2025a), IRBNC dispersions (2.0 wt%) were first subjected to shear mixing at 10,000 rpm for 5 min, followed by ultrasonication at 25 kHz and 450 W for 30 min (JY99-IIDN, Ningbo Scientz Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Ningbo, China), and finally treated by high-pressure homogenization at 80 MPa for two passes (D-6 L, PhD Technology LLC, USA). EC, EGC, and ECG were added at mass ratios of 25, 50, and 100 mg/g (catechin/IRBNC), and the mixtures were reacted at pH 12.0 for 2 h. The dispersions were subsequently adjusted to pH 7.0 and dialyzed for 60 h to obtain EC-IRBNC, EGC-IRBNC, and ECG-IRBNC complexes.

Structural characterization of EC-IRBNC, EGC-IRBNC, and ECG-IRBNC complexes

Bound phenol content

EC-IRBNC, EGC-IRBNC, and ECG-IRBNC complexes were dispersed in 2 mol/L NaOH solution (1:100, w/v) and hydrolyzed for 60 min. The complex dispersions were acidified to pH 2.0 with concentrated HCl and extracted with ethyl acetate. After evaporation of the organic phase at 45°C, the residue was re-dissolved in 10 mL of 80% (v/v) methanol. The bound phenol content was quantified using the Folin-Ciocalteau assay at 760 nm with EC, EGC, and ECG used as standards.

Soluble protein content

IRBNC dispersion (1 mL, 2.5 wt%) was centrifuged at 10, 000 × g for 20 min. The soluble protein concentration in the supernatant was determined by the Bradford's method. The soluble protein content of EC-IRBNC, EGC-IRBNC, and ECG-IRBNC complexes was calculated as:(1)Solubleproteincontent=CV/M

where C, V, and M were soluble protein concentration (mg/mL), supernatant volume (mL), and weight of EC-IRBNC, EGC-IRBNC, and ECG-IRBNC complexes (g), respectively.

Free amino (-NH2) content

Free-NH2 content was determined through the O-phthalaldehyde (OPA) method. EC-IRBNC, EGC-IRBNC, and ECG-IRBNC complexes concentrations of 10 mg/mL were centrifuged at 10,000 × g for 10 min. A 400 μL aliquot of the supernatant was mixed with 3 mL of OPA reagent and allowed to react for 2 min. Fluorescence intensity was recorded using an F-4600 fluorescence spectrometer (Hitachi High-Tech Corporation, Tokyo, Japan) at excitation and emission wavelengths of 340 and 430 nm, respectively. Glycine was used as the standard.

Free sulfhydryl (-SH) content

Free-SH content was evaluated using Ellman's method as reported by Li et al. (2021) after centrifugation of the EC-IRBNC, EGC-IRBNC, and ECG-IRBNC complexes dispersions (10,000 × g, 20 min).

Surface hydrophobicity (H0)

Following Li et al. (2025b), the EC-IRBNC, EGC-IRBNC, and ECG-IRBNC complexes (0.005–0.5 mg/mL) were reacted with 1-phenylaminonaphthalene-8-sulfonic acid. The excitation and emission wavelengths were 390 nm and 470 nm, respectively.

ζ-potential

Following Li et al. (2025a), the EC-IRBNC, EGC-IRBNC, and ECG-IRBNC complexes were adjusted to a concentration of 0.1 mg/mL with 5 mmol/L phosphate buffer at pH 7.0. The ζ-potential of the dispersions was then determined using a Nano ZS Zeta-sizer (Malvern Instruments Ltd., Malvern, UK).

Average particle size and specific surface area (SSA)

The average particle size and SSA of IRBNC particles were measured according to the method described by Lin et al. (2022) using a LS13 320 laser particle size analyzer (Beckman Coulter Ltd., Miami, FL, USA).

X-ray diffraction (XRD) spectroscopy

Following Li et al. (2024b), XRD patterns were recorded using a D/Max-2200 X-ray diffractometer (Rigaku Denki, Japan) operated at 40 kV and 30 mA over a 2θ range of 4–50° at a scanning rate of 10°/min.

Fourier transform-infrared (FT-IR) spectroscopy

Following Li et al. (2021), FT-IR spectra of EC-IRBNC, EGC-IRBNC, and ECG-IRBNC complexes were collected in the wavenumber range of 4000 to 400 cm−1 with the Thermo Nicolet iS5 FT-IR spectrometer (Massachusetts, USA). Samples were mixed with KBr (1:99, w/w), and ground into pellets. The spectral data were analyzed using PeakFit 4.12 software.

Microstructure

Using a modified method from Ke and Li (2024), the microscopic morphology of EC-IRBNC, EGC-IRBNC, and ECG-IRBNC complexes were observed using a scanning electron microscope (Carl-Ziess SMT, Germany) operated at an accelerating voltage of 3 kV, with magnifications of 200× and 1000×.

Contact angle (θ) interfacial tension and interfacial pressure (π)

Contact angle (θ) and interfacial tension were measured using a DSC350 optical contact angle analyzer (Nayan Technology (Shanghai) Co., Ltd., China) at 25°C.

For contact angle measurement, the powder sample was compressed into a pellet (5 mm diameter, 2 mm thickness). A 5 μL deionized water droplet was placed on the pellet surface, and the static contact angle was recorded and analyzed using the instrument software. For interfacial tension measurement, the pendant drop method was used. The 1.0 wt% particle dispersion was formed into a droplet and suspended in corn oil. The π was calculated as follows:(2)π=γ0‐γtwhere γ0 represents the interfacial tension of ultrapure water (30.13 mN/m)

Characterization of stability of Pickering emulsions

Pickering emulsions preparation

Based on the method of Li et al. (2024b), emulsions with a particle concentration of 1.0 wt% and an oil volume fraction (0.2) were prepared. The mixtures were homogenized at 15,000 rpm for 2 min (IKA, UK) and further treated by ultrasonication (25 kHz, 450 W, 30 min) in an ice-water bath (4°C).

Average droplet size

Based on the method of Lin et al. (2022), emulsion droplet size was measured using a laser diffraction particle size analyzer (Beckman Coulter, USA).

ζ-potential

The emulsions were diluted 500-fold using phosphate buffer (5 mmol/L, pH 7.0), and ζ-potential of the emulsions was measured using a Nano ZS Zeta-sizer (Malvern Instruments, UK).

Confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM)

Fluorescent dyes including Nile Red (0.1%, w/v), fluorescein isothiocyanate (0.1%, w/v), and calcofluor white (0.1%, w/v) were separately added (20 μL) to 1 mL of emulsion for staining. Then, 10 μL of stained emulsion was mounted on glass slides and stored at 4°C overnight. Microstructures were observed using a confocal laser scanning microscope (CLSM, LSM 980, Carl Zeiss, Germany), and fluorescence signals were collected at excitation/emission wavelengths of 561/610 nm for Nile Red, 488/525 nm for FITC, and 405/450 nm for Calcofluor White.

Rheological properties

Following Li et al. (2024b) and modified, rheological measurements were performed using a DHR-2 rheometer (TA Instruments, USA) with a 60 mm parallel plate geometry at 25°C and a 500 μm gap. Flow behavior was evaluated over a shear rate range of 0.1–100 s−1. Dynamic viscoelastic properties were determined by strain and frequency sweep tests within the linear viscoelastic region, and storage (G’) and loss (G’’) moduli were recorded.

Hydroperoxides content

Freshly prepared emulsions were stored at 45°C in the dark for 30 days, with samples taken periodically for analysis during this period. Emulsions (0.05 mL) was mixed with 1.0 mL of isooctane-isopropanol mixture (1:1, v/v) and vortexed for 30 s, centrifuged (5000 × g, 5 min). The supernatant (0.15 mL) was mixed with 5.85 mL of methanol–butanol mixture (2:1, v/v), 50 μL of freshly prepared Fe2+ solution, and 50 μL of ammonium thiocyanate (3.94 mol/L). After vortexed for 30 s, the reaction mixture was incubated in the dark for 20 min, and the absorbance was measured at 510 nm. The concentration of lipid hydroperoxides was calculated using a standard curve prepared with cumene hydroperoxide. The Fe2+ solution was freshly prepared by mixing equal volumes of 0.132 mol/L BaCl2 (in 0.4 mol/L HCl) and 0.144 mol/L FeSO4, and the supernatant was used for analysis.

Thiobarbituric acid reactive substances (TBARS) concentration

Following Wang et al. (2022a) and modified, the emulsion sample (0.4 mL) was mixed with TBA (5 mL) heated at 100°C for 15 min, then centrifuged (1000 × g, 15 min), and the absorbance of the supernatant was measured at 532 nm. The concentration was calculated using a standard curve of 1,1,3,3-tetraethoxypropane. The TBA reagent was prepared by dissolving 15% (w/v) trichloroacetic acid and 0.375% (w/v) thiobarbituric acid in 0.25 mol/L HCl.

Creaming index (CI%)

Following Li et al. (2024b), emulsions were stored for 30 d. The overall height of the emulsion (HT) and the height of the separated clear phase (HTL) were determined from the sample bottle. The CI was then calculated according to the following equation:(3)CI(%)=HTL/HT×100

Statistical analysis

All experiments were performed in triplicate, and the results are presented as mean values. Statistical analysis was carried out using one-way ANOVA followed by Duncan's multiple range test with IBM SPSS 22.0 software.

Results and discussion

Structural characteristics of EC-IRBNC, EGC-IRBNC, and ECG-IRBNC complexes

Bound phenol content

As shown in Fig. 1A, the bound phenol contents of EC-IRBNC, EGC-IRBNC, and ECG-IRBNC significantly increased with increasing addition levels of EC, EGC, and ECG (P < 0.05), demonstrating effective binding of catechin monomers to IRBNC. The binding ratios of EC, EGC, and ECG to IRBNC markedly decreased with increased addition levels (Fig. 1B, P < 0.05), indicating that binding of EC, EGC, and ECG to IRBNC might have reached a saturation state at 25 mg/g, while the excess EC, EGC, and ECG remained in the free form within the dispersion. Li et al. (2025a) reported a similar saturation phenomenon was observed in the EGCG-modified IRBNC system. At the same addition, ECG exhibited the highest binding efficiency to IRBNC, followed by EGC and EC. Under alkaline conditions, catechins may be oxidized to quinone-like intermediates, which could participate in interactions with proteins and polysaccharides through both covalent and non-covalent pathways (Jakobek, 2015; Schieber, 2018). The galloyl group in ECG may provide additional reactive sites compared with EC (two hydroxyls) and EGC (three hydroxyls), which may explain the superior binding affinity. Compared with the stronger interaction reported for EGCG in a previous study (Li et al., 2025a), these findings suggested that phenolic hydroxyl density and galloyl substitution may influence the interaction behavior between catechins and IRBNC. In addition, preferential binding of galloylated catechins was likewise reported for catechin-β-lactoglobulin complexes (Yuan et al., 2025).Fig. 1Effect of EC, EGC, ECG modifications on bound phenol content (A) and binding rate (B) of IRBNC. Soluble protein content

As shown in Fig. 2, when the amount of catechin monomer added was 25 mg/g, the soluble protein content of EC-IRBNC, EGC-IRBNC and ECG-IRBNC significantly increased (P < 0.05). The increase may be attributed to alkaline-induced disruption of intermolecular interactions within IRBNC, which led to the exposure of protein fractions within the IRBNC, together with the introduction of hydrophilic phenolic hydroxyl groups from catechin monomers that interacted with the exposed proteins, thereby increasing protein solubility (Li et al., 2025a). However, when the addition of EGC and ECG exceeded 50 mg/g and 25 mg/g, respectively, no further significant increase was observed, suggesting that the capacity of EGC and ECG to promote protein solubilization had reached saturation. In contrast, the soluble protein content of EC-IRBNC continued to increase with increasing EC addition, likely due to weaker interactions between EC and IRBNC.Fig. 2Effect of EC, EGC, ECG modifications on soluble protein content of IRBNC.

Effect of EC, EGC, ECG modifications on soluble protein content of IRBNC.

At an addition level of 25 mg/g, significant differences in the soluble protein content were observed among the three complexes (Fig. 2, P < 0.05). At an addition of 50 mg/g, the soluble protein content of EGC-IRBNC and ECG-IRBNC was significantly higher than that of EC-IRBNC, while no significant difference was observed between EGC-IRBNC and ECG-IRBNC. Moreover, compared with the previously reported EGCG-IRBNC which showed a marked increase in soluble protein content from 10.13 to 35.10 mg/g (Li et al., 2025a), EC, EGC, and ECG showed a relatively weaker enhancement of soluble protein content. These results suggest that structural differences among EC, EGC, and ECG may influence their interaction behavior with IRBNC, and that the extent of these effects depends on the catechin addition level.

Free-NH2 content and free-SH content

The free-NH2 and free-SH contents of EC-IRBNC, EGC-IRBNC, and ECG-IRBNC significantly decreased as the addition of EC, EGC, and ECG increased (Fig. 3A and B) (P < 0.05), suggesting the involvement of amino and sulfhydryl groups in the interactions between catechins and IRBNC. Under alkaline conditions, EC, EGC, and ECG may be oxidized into quinone intermediates, which can subsequently react with nucleophilic amino and sulfhydryl groups in proteins, thereby decreasing the contents of free-NH2 and free-SH groups (Ke and Li, 2024). As shown in Fig. 3A, at the same catechin addition level, significant differences in free-NH2 content were observed among EC-IRBNC, EGC-IRBNC, and ECG-IRBNC (P < 0.05). The free -NH2 content of EGCG-IRBNC was significantly lower than EC-IRBNC, EGC-IRBNC, and ECG-IRBNC, indicating stronger interactions between EGCG and IRBNC (Li et al., 2025a). At a catechin addition of 25 mg/g, the free-SH content of ECG-IRBNC was significantly lower than that of EC-IRBNC and EGC-IRBNC (Fig. 3B, P < 0.05). At an addition of 50 and 100 mg/g, no significant difference in the free-SH content was found between EGC-IRBNC and ECG-IRBNC, whereas both were significantly lower than that of EC-IRBNC (Fig. 3B, P < 0.05). These differences may be attributed to the higher hydroxyl group density in EGC and ECG relative to EC, resulting in stronger interactions with IRBNC. Similar phenomena have been reported for the covalent binding of protocatechuic acid and gallic acid to whey protein (Fei et al., 2023). In addition, high molecular weight phenolics possess more binding sites, making them more susceptible to protein interactions and polysaccharide surface binding (Peng et al., 2023).Fig. 3Effect of EC, EGC, ECG modifications on free amino group content (A), and free sulfhydryl group content (B) of IRBNC.

Effect of EC, EGC, ECG modifications on free amino group content (A), and free sulfhydryl group content (B) of IRBNC.

H0

As shown in Fig. 4, the H0 of EC-IRBNC, EGC-IRBNC, and ECG-IRBNC significantly reduced with the addition of EC, EGC, and ECG (P < 0.05). The results showed that modification with EC, EGC, and ECG enhanced the hydrophilicity of IRBNC. The phenomenon may be attributed to the introduction of abundant hydrophilic hydroxyl groups by the catechins, as well as possible structural rearrangements within IRBNC induced by catechin interactions. These changes may have contributed to the burial of hydrophobic regions or increased exposure of hydrophilic groups on the particle surface, thereby reducing H0 (Wang et al., 2022a).Fig. 4Effect of EC, EGC, ECG modifications on surface hydrophobicity of IRBNC.

Effect of EC, EGC, ECG modifications on surface hydrophobicity of IRBNC.

As shown in Fig. 4, at catechin concentrations of 25 mg/g and 50 mg/g, the H0 of ECG-IRBNC was significantly lower than that of EC-IRBNC and EGC-IRBNC (P < 0.05), while no significant difference in H0 was observed between EC-IRBNC and EGC-IRBNC. At a concentration of 100 mg/g for all three catechins, significant differences in H0 were observed among the three complexes (P < 0.05). These results indicated that the H0 of IRBNC was governed by both catechin type and addition level. The lower H0 observed for ECG-IRBNC may be associated with its greater interaction capacity with IRBNC and the introduction of additional hydrophilic groups (Ma et al., 2025). Furthermore, the stronger interactions between ECG and IRBNC may induce more pronounced structural rearrangement, which could reduce the accessibility of hydrophobic domains and consequently decrease the binding of ANS probes to hydrophobic sites on the particle surface (Dai et al., 2022; Guo et al., 2024). At higher addition levels (100 mg/g), these structural variations among EC, EGC, and ECG became more amplified, leading to distinct discrepancies in their hydrophobicity regulation capacity.

ζ-potential

As shown in Fig. 5, the ζ-potential of IRBNC was −22.07 mV, indicating that IRBNC was negatively charged. The ζ-potential of EC-IRBNC, EGC-IRBNC, and ECG-IRBNC gradually decreased with increasing addition of the three catechin monomers (Fig. 5). The results showed that EC, EGC, and ECG modifications increased the negative charge density of IRBNC. The phenomenon was attributed to deprotonation of phenolic hydroxyl groups of catechins under alkaline conditions, forming phenoxide anions (-O-) and converting electrically neutral molecules into negatively charged species (Yan et al., 2021). Therefore, the ζ-potential of EC-IRBNC, EGC-IRBNC, and ECG-IRBNC continued to decrease with the increase of catechin monomer addition.Fig. 5Effect of EC, EGC, ECG modifications on ζ-potential of IRBNC.

Effect of EC, EGC, ECG modifications on ζ-potential of IRBNC.

As shown in Fig. 5, when the three catechin monomers were added at 25 mg/g, the ζ-potential of ECG-IRBNC was significantly lower than that of EC-IRBNC and EGC-IRBNC, whereas a significant difference between the ζ-potential of EC-IRBNC and ECG-IRBNC was observed at 50 mg/g (P < 0.05), indicating that different catechin monomers had different abilities to introduce negative charges. ECG contains a galloyl group and a relatively high density of phenolic hydroxyl groups, which may contribute to the introduction of additional negative charges under alkaline conditions and consequently lead to stronger electrostatic repulsion (Yuan et al., 2025).

Average particle size and SSA

As shown in Fig. 6A, the average particle size of EC-IRBNC, EGC-IRBNC, and ECG-IRBNC gradually increased with increasing additions of EC, EGC, and ECG. The phenomenon may be associated with dimer or polymer formation through reactions between quinones generated from catechin oxidation and unoxidized polyphenols, thereby leading to particle aggregation (Chen et al., 2023). In contrast, the SSA of EC-IRBNC, EGC-IRBNC, and ECG-IRBNC gradually decreased with increasing catechin addition, whereas significant reductions for EC-IRBNC and EGC-IRBNC occurred at 50 and 100 mg/g (Fig. 6B, P < 0.05). The phenomenon may be related to particle aggregation and physical dilution effects, leading to a reduced accessible surface area.Fig. 6Effect of EC, EGC, ECG modifications on average particle size (A) and specific surface area (B) of IRBNC.

Effect of EC, EGC, ECG modifications on average particle size (A) and specific surface area (B) of IRBNC.

As shown in Fig. 6A, at EC, EGC, and ECG addition levels of 25 and 50 mg/g, the average particle sizes of EGC-IRBNC and ECG-IRBNC were significantly larger than that of EC-IRBNC (P < 0.05). At an addition of 100 mg/g, the average particle size of EC-IRBNC and EGC-IRBNC was significantly larger than that of ECG-IRBNC (Fig. 6A, P < 0.05). Correspondingly, significant differences in the SSA were also observed among EC-IRBNC, EGC-IRBNC, and ECG-IRBNC (Fig. 6B, P < 0.05). These differences may be attributed to variations in the interaction strength between EC, EGC, ECG, and IRBNC. Owing to the presence of a galloyl group, ECG and IRBNC exhibited stronger interactions at higher addition level. In contrast, EC and IRBNC showed the weakest interaction and tended to form smaller particles.

XRD

As shown in Fig. 7A−C, IRBNC exhibited a broad diffraction peak at approximately 22°, indicating a typical cellulose I-type semi-crystalline structure (Song et al., 2021). With increasing additions of EC, EGC, and ECG, the peak position of IRBNC remained essentially unchanged, whereas the diffraction intensity gradually decreased and the peak became broader, indicating reduced crystallinity and an increased amorphous fraction. These changes could be attributed to the interactions between EC, EGC, ECG and IRBNC, which disrupted the intermolecular and intramolecular hydrogen bonding network within cellulose microcrystals and the surrounding protein–polysaccharide matrix (Yong et al., 2020). The similar phenomenon was also reported for protein and gallic acid, ferulic acid, coumaric acid, and chlorogenic acid complexes (Işçimen, 2025). Compared with EC-IRBNC, EGC induced a more pronounced reduction in diffraction intensity, which may be related to its higher hydroxyl group density and greater potential for intermolecular interactions. The most significant attenuation and broadening of diffraction peaks were observed for ECG-IRBNC, indicating the strongest disruption of the ordered structure of IRBNC, which may be related to the additional galloyl group enhancing interactions such as hydrogen bonding and π–π stacking, consistent with reports that galloylated catechins exhibit stronger binding effects than non-galloylated catechins (Ma et al., 2025). Moreover, the characteristic crystalline diffraction peaks of EC, EGC, and ECG were not detected in the modified IRBNC samples, and only a single amorphous diffraction peak was observed, further demonstrating that EC, EGC, and ECG interacted with IRBNC rather than existing as separate crystalline phases. The reduced crystallinity and increased amorphous regions may decrease the structural rigidity of IRBNC particles, thereby enhancing particle flexibility and facilitating particle adsorption at the oil–water interface, which may contribute to improved interfacial stabilization performance.Fig. 7Effect of EC, EGC, ECG modifications on XRD (A: EC-IRBNC; B: EGC-IRBNC; C: ECG-IRBNC) of IRBNC.

FT-IR spectroscopy

As shown in Fig. 8A–C, compared with IRBNC, the absorption intensity corresponding to -OH stretching vibrations (3700–3000 cm−1) decreased in EC-IRBNC, EGC-IRBNC, and ECG-IRBNC, indicating the involvement of hydroxyl groups in intermolecular interactions. This result may be associated with hydrogen-bonding interactions between catechins and IRBNC (Zhao et al., 2023). The absorption intensity of the -OH stretching vibration in ECG-IRBNC significantly decreased more than that in EC-IRBNC and EGC-IRBNC, indicating a greater extent of intermolecular interactions. The absorption peak associated with C-H stretching vibration at 2924 cm−1 was weakened after EC, EGC, and ECG modifications, which may be associated with structural changes induced by interactions between EC, EGC, ECG and IRBNC (Zhao et al., 2023). Meanwhile, the characteristic peak associated with C=O stretching vibration (1730 cm−1) was weakened after EC, EGC, and ECG modifications (Fig. 8A–C), suggesting the possible involvement of carbonyl-containing groups in the interaction process (Li et al., 2025a). Among the EC-IRBNC, EGC-IRBNC and ECG-IRBNC, the most pronounced weakening of the C=O band was induced by ECG, indicating higher influence on carbonyl-related absorption characteristics. Similar characteristic band attenuation has also been reported in EGCG-IRBNC (Li et al., 2025a), suggesting that esterified catechins (ECG and EGCG) may exhibit stronger interaction tendencies than non-galloylated catechins (EC and EGC). Overall, FT-IR results demonstrated that hydrogen bonding and other non-covalent interactions substantially contributed to the interactions between catechins and IRBNC, while the interaction strength was closely associated with structural differences among EC, EGC, and ECG.Fig. 8Effect of EC, EGC, ECG modifications on FT-IR spectrum (A: EC-IRBNC; B: EGC-IRBNC; C: ECG-IRBNC), and protein secondary structure (D: EC-IRBNC; E: EGC-IRBNC; F: ECG-IRBNC) of IRBNC.

Protein secondary structure

As shown in Fig. 8D–F, with increasing additions of EC, EGC, and ECG, the α-helix content initially decreased and then increased, the β-turn content showed the opposite trend, and β-sheet content continuously decreased, indicating that catechin modification altered the secondary structure of the protein in IRBNC. Notably, α-helix and β-sheet represent ordered structures, while β-turn and random coil are associated with disordered conformations. Therefore, the reduction in ordered structures accompanied by an increase in disordered conformations suggests that appropriate EC (25 mg/g), EGC (50 mg/g), and ECG (25 mg/g) modifications induced a transition toward a more disordered protein conformation, indicating partial unfolding and enhanced conformational flexibility of protein molecules. Similar reductions in β-sheet and α-helix contents were reported for β-lactoglobulin complexes with catechin, gallocatechin gallate, EC, EGC, ECG, and EGCG, which were attributed to progressive disruption of the highly ordered hydrogen-bonding network stabilizing β-sheet structures by interactions between catechins and proteins (Yuan et al., 2025). Among the three catechins, ECG induced the most pronounced changes in protein secondary structure, followed by EGC and EC, owing to structural differences. Furthermore, compared to EC, EGC, and ECG, previous studies have shown that EGCG modification significantly reduces the ordered secondary structure of the IRBNC (Li et al., 2025a). Consistently, esterified catechins (ECG and EGCG) were reported to induce a more disordered conformation in gluten proteins than non-galloylated catechins (EC and EGC) (Ma et al., 2025).

Microstructure

As shown in Fig. 9A–D, the particle sizes of EC-IRBNC, EGC-IRBNC, and ECG-IRBNC increased with increasing additions of EC, EGC, and ECG, indicating that catechin modification induced IRBNC aggregation, which may be associated with enhanced interactions among particles following catechin incorporation (Li et al., 2024a). At 1000× magnified image (Fig. 9D−H), the structure became loose and porous at 25 mg/g for EC and ECG, at 50 mg/g for EGC, which may be associated with structural rearrangement of the IRBNC bonding with catechin (Yong et al., 2022). However, when the concentrations of EC, EGC, and ECG were further increased, the structure of complexes became compact again, probably due to strengthened intermolecular interactions at higher catechin concentrations (Xie et al., 2020). Owing to the structural features, ECG induced the formation of a highly cross-linked porous network at an addition level of 25 mg/g. In contrast, EGC exhibited weaker interactions with IRBNC and required a higher concentration (50 mg/g) to promote the development of a relatively loose porous structure. Although EC also showed weaker interaction strength, the smaller molecular size and lower steric hindrance facilitated penetration into the IRBNC, thereby enabling the formation of a loose porous structure at 25 mg/g.Fig. 9Effects of EC, EGC, ECG modifications on the microstructure of IRBNC (A, E: Control; B, F: EC-IRBNC; C, G: EGC-IRBNC; D, H: ECG-IRBNC, A-D: 200× magnified image; E-H: 1000× magnified image).

Wettability

The θ of original IRBNC was 127.015° (Fig. 10A), indicating strong hydrophobicity, which is unfavorable for emulsion stabilization. With increasing additions of EC, EGC, and ECG, the θ values of EC-IRBNC, EGC-IRBNC, and ECG-IRBNC progressively decreased (Fig. 10B–D), demonstrating that catechin modification enhanced IRBNC hydrophilicity by introducing abundant hydrophilic hydroxyl groups and effectively converting hydrophobic particles into amphiphilic particles (Li et al., 2025a; Yan et al., 2024). When θ approaches 90°, particles are more readily adsorbed at the oil–water interface, which can contribute to the stabilization of Pickering emulsions through steric barriers against droplet aggregation (Liu et al., 2023a). At an addition level of 25 mg/g, the θ value of ECG-IRBNC reached 91.053°, whereas EC-IRBNC reached 95.695°, both close to 90°; at 50 mg/g, the θ value of EGC-IRBNC decreased to 86.976°, approaching the optimal range (Fig. 10B–D). These results, together with previous data showing that EGCG modification reduced the θ of IRBNC from 139.8° to 78.5° (Li et al., 2025a), indicate that EC, EGC, and ECG effectively shifted IRBNC from a highly hydrophobic state toward a more amphiphilic character, with distinct efficiencies at identical addition levels. Moderate catechin addition favored amphiphilicity and emulsion stabilization, whereas excessive addition drove the particles toward strong hydrophilicity, which was detrimental to emulsion stability. Thus, the tailored wettability of IRBNC by catechin monomer modification played a critical role in modulating particle interfacial behavior, which was decisive for achieving desirable Pickering emulsion stabilization.Fig. 10Effects of EC, EGC, ECG modifications on the wettability (A: Control; B: EC-IRBNC; C: EGC-IRBNC; D: ECG-IRBNC), interfacial tension (E) and π (F) of IRBNC.

Interfacial tension and π

The interfacial tension of IRBNC and the catechin-modified complexes rapidly decreased and then stabilized (Fig. 10E), indicating the progressive adsorption of particles at the oil–water interface and the formation of a stable interfacial layer. In contrast, π exhibited an opposite trend, rapidly increasing at the early stage and gradually leveling off (Fig. 10F), further demonstrated the continuous occupation of the interface by particles and the progressive development of interfacial coverage. Compared with native IRBNC, all catechin-modified samples exhibited significantly lower equilibrium interfacial tension and higher equilibrium π values. Notably, the equilibrium interfacial tension decreased from 20.43 mN/m for native IRBNC to 16.19 mN/m after catechin modification, confirming that catechin conjugation effectively enhanced the interfacial activity and interfacial adsorption behavior of IRBNC. At an addition of 25 mg/g, EC-IRBNC displayed a slightly faster initial decrease in interfacial tension and a more rapid rise in π than ECG-IRBNC. This might be attributed to the smaller molecular size and lower steric hindrance of EC, which facilitated faster diffusion and initial adsorption. Conversely, the galloyl group and higher phenolic hydroxyl density of ECG likely induced stronger binding affinity and moderate particle association, which may have marginally slowed initial adsorption. However, ECG-IRBNC demonstrated superior overall interfacial performance, characterized by a contact angle closer to 90° (91.053°), lower surface hydrophobicity, and an optimized loose porous microstructure at 25 mg/g, which enabled more effective long-term packing and film formation.

With increasing catechin concentration, the interfacial tension of all complexes first decreased and then increased, while π showed a corresponding inverse trend (Fig. 10F), indicating an optimal modification level beyond which excessive crosslinking and increased hydrophilicity reduced effective adsorption. This structure-dependent behavior is in agreement with Huang et al. (2024) and Tang et al. (2025). While smaller molecules such as EC favored faster initial adsorption, catechins with higher hydroxyl density and galloyl groups (ECG) achieved more effective overall interfacial tension reduction and stronger interfacial films through optimized amphiphilicity.

Average droplet size and ζ-potential

As shown in Fig. 11A, with increasing EC, EGC, and ECG additions, the average droplet sizes of EC-IRBNC, EGC-IRBNC, and ECG-IRBNC stabilized emulsions initially decreased and subsequently increased. At the appropriate catechin addition, the average droplet sizes reached 48.63, 46.51, and 46.32 μm for EC-IRBNC, EGC-IRBNC, and ECG-IRBNC stabilized emulsions, respectively, indicating that appropriate catechin modification promoted the formation of smaller and more uniform droplets. Moderate EC, EGC, and ECG rendered IRBNC closer to amphiphilic (90°), which may facilitate adsorption at the oil–water interface and enhance inter-droplet electrostatic repulsion, thereby inhibiting aggregation and reducing droplet size (Liu et al., 2023a). In contrast, excessive addition promoted complexes aggregation, resulting in flocculation and increased droplet size (Tang et al., 2025). At an addition of 25 mg/g, ECG-IRBNC stabilized emulsion exhibited the smallest droplet size (P < 0.05), at addition concentrations of 50 mg/g and 100 mg/g, EGC-IRBNC stabilized emulsion significantly reduced droplet sizes than the EC-IRBNC and ECG-IRBNC stabilized emulsion (P < 0.05). Significant differences in droplet size were observed among the three emulsions at 25 mg/g and 50 mg/g, suggesting that structural differences among catechins influenced their interactions with IRBNC and consequently affected emulsion formation behavior. ECG had the strongest interaction with IRBNC, minimizing droplet size even at low concentrations. Although the smaller molecular weight and lower steric hindrance of EC partially compensated for the weaker interaction, the reduction in droplet size remained less pronounced than that induced by ECG.Fig. 11The average droplet size (A) and ζ-potential (B) of emulsions stabilized by catechin-IRBNC particles.

The average droplet size (A) and ζ-potential (B) of emulsions stabilized by catechin-IRBNC particles.

As shown in Fig. 11B, the ζ-potential of emulsions stabilized by EC-IRBNC, EGC-IRBNC and ECG-IRBNC initially decreased and then increased with EC, EGC, and ECG additions, indicating that moderate addition enhanced the negative surface charge of droplets. The phenomenon may be associated with the introduction of additional ionizable phenolic hydroxyl groups through catechin-IRBNC interactions under alkaline conditions, thereby increasing the negative charge density of the particles. Simultaneously, the decrease in free-NH2 and -SH groups may also contribute to changes in surface charge distribution (Li et al., 2023). Excessive catechin addition may induce particle aggregation, hinder effective interfacial arrangement, and alter the distribution of charged groups on the droplet surface (Tang et al., 2025). At an addition of 25 mg/g, the ζ-potentials of EC-IRBNC and ECG-IRBNC emulsions reached minimum values, with ECG-IRBNC exhibiting the lowest potential. In contrast, the ζ-potential of EGC-IRBNC emulsion was lowest at 50 mg/g and 100 mg/g (Fig. 6B). These differences may be related to variations among EC, EGC, and ECG, particularly the presence of the galloyl group and differences in phenolic hydroxyl group density, which could influence charge dissociation behavior and particle interactions. Meanwhile, the relatively lower steric hindrance of EC may partially facilitate particle arrangement at the oil-water interface.

CLSM

As shown in Fig. 12, the emulsion stabilized by unmodified IRBNC exhibited large and heterogeneous oil droplets with weak and discontinuous interfacial fluorescence, indicating insufficient particle adsorption and poor interfacial coverage. In contrast, all catechin-modified systems exhibited smaller and more uniform droplets with enhanced and more continuous interfacial fluorescence signals, suggesting improved droplet dispersion and interfacial assembly. The distribution of green fluorescence surrounding the oil droplets suggested that protein components in IRBNC were preferentially associated with the oil–water interface and contributed to interfacial layer formation, which may be related to their amphiphilic characteristics (Li et al., 2024b). Meanwhile, the presence of polysaccharide fluorescence around the droplets indicated that polysaccharide components participated in the formation of interfacial and continuous-phase structures, potentially providing additional steric stabilization (Huang et al., 2024). The rearrangement of polysaccharide components at the interface may be associated with the reduced crystallinity of cellulose-rich structures observed by XRD. The disruption of ordered crystalline domains increased the amorphous fraction and reduced structural rigidity of IRBNC particles, which may facilitate polysaccharide chains mobility and redistribution during interfacial assembly, thereby promoting the formation of a more continuous and compact interfacial layer. Therefore, the synergistic contribution of protein-mediated interfacial adsorption and polysaccharide-associated structural reinforcement may facilitate the formation of a more stable interfacial layer, thereby reducing droplet aggregation and improving emulsion stability. At optimal modification levels of EC (25 mg/g), EGC (50 mg/g), and ECG (25 mg/g), the emulsions displayed the most homogeneous microstructures and the most continuous interfacial fluorescence signals, indicating efficient particle adsorption and formation of compact interfacial layers. With further increase in catechin concentration (50–100 mg/g), droplet heterogeneity and discontinuous interfacial signals became more pronounced, suggesting that excessive modification may induce particle aggregation and reduce effective interfacial mobility, thereby weakening emulsion stability (Tang et al., 2025). Among the three systems, the ECG-modified emulsion showed a more uniform droplet distribution and stronger interfacial fluorescence, whereas EC exhibited relatively weaker interfacial organization and EGC required a higher dosage to achieve an optimal structure, reflecting differences in molecular structure and interfacial interaction behavior.Fig. 12The CLSM images of emulsions stabilized by catechin-IRBNC particles.

Rheological properties

The apparent viscosities of all emulsions decreased with increasing shear rate, showing typical shear-thinning behavior (Fig. 13A–C), indicating that emulsions stabilized by EC-IRBNC, EGC-IRBNC, and ECG-IRBNC behaved as non-Newtonian fluids. The apparent viscosities of the three emulsions initially increased and subsequently decreased with increasing catechin concentration, suggesting that moderate catechin addition increased droplet flow resistance, whereas excessive addition produced the opposite effect. Moderate catechin incorporation promotes the formation of smaller and more uniformly distributed emulsion droplets, thereby strengthen droplet–droplet interactions and contributing to an increase in the apparent viscosity of the emulsion (Han et al., 2024). Hydroperoxides content

As shown in Fig. 14A, during a 30 days storage period, the peroxide values of emulsions stabilized by EC-IRBNC, EGC-IRBNC, and ECG-IRBNC initially decreased and then increased with increasing catechin content, indicating that catechins effectively inhibited primary lipid oxidation. This enhanced oxidative stability may be attributed to the combined effects of catechin antioxidant activity and the physical barrier effect of IRBNC particles. The phenolic hydroxyl groups of catechins can scavenge free radicals and reactive oxygen species, thereby delaying lipid oxidation. Meanwhile, the modified IRBNC particles adsorbed at the oil–water interface may form a protective interfacial layer, reducing the contact between oil droplets and pro-oxidative factors and thus further improving oxidative stability. However, further increases in catechin concentration resulted in higher hydroperoxide contents, which may be associated with excessive particle aggregation and altered interfacial structures, thereby weakening the protective effect of the interfacial layer. Among the three catechins, ECG exhibited the strongest inhibitory effect on peroxide formation. This result may be related to the presence of the galloyl group and the higher phenolic hydroxyl group density of ECG, which are generally associated with enhanced antioxidant activity (Wang et al., 2022b, 2023).Fig. 14The hydroperoxides content (A) and TBARS content (B) of the emulsions during the 30-day storage.

TBARS values

As shown in Fig. 14B, the TBARS values of EC-IRBNC, EGC-IRBNC, and ECG-IRBNC stabilized emulsions first decreased and then increased during storage with increasing catechin content, indicating that catechins can effectively inhibit the decomposition of lipid hydroperoxides into secondary oxidation products. The improvement in oxidative stability may be attributed to the combined effects of catechin antioxidant activity and the interfacial barrier formed by IRBNC particles. Catechins can inhibit lipid oxidation by scavenging free radicals through their phenolic hydroxyl groups, while IRBNC particles adsorbed at the oil–water interface may restrict the contact between oil droplets and pro-oxidative factors and reduce the migration of oxidation-related compounds, thereby suppressing the formation of secondary oxidation products. Similarly, results have been reported in EGCG-modified myofibrillar protein stabilized water-in-oil emulsion, where catechin incorporation improved oxidative stability (Wang et al., 2022b). However, at high addition levels, TBARS values increased, indicating reduced oxidative stability of the emulsions. The phenomenon may be associated with excessive catechin-induced particle aggregation and altered interfacial microstructures, which weakened the protective effect of the interfacial layer. Moreover, excessive catechin addition has been reported to exhibit potential pro-oxidant effects, which may further accelerate secondary lipid oxidation through complex oxidation reactions involving phenolic compounds (Wang et al., 2023). Among the three emulsions, the ECG-IRBNC stabilized emulsion exhibited the lowest TBARS value, indicating the strongest inhibition of secondary oxidation products. This result may be related to the presence of the galloyl group and the higher phenolic hydroxyl group density of ECG. Overall, these results demonstrate that the oxidative stability of Pickering emulsions was determined by the synergistic effects of catechin structure-dependent antioxidant capacity and IRBNC particle-mediated interfacial protection.

In addition, color differences were observed among emulsions stabilized by IRBNC modified with different catechins, which may be associated with structure-dependent oxidation reactions of catechins during storage. Mechanism analysis

Catechin modification regulates the structural properties of IRBNC in a structure- and dosage-dependent manner. Structural differences among EC, EGC, and ECG, together with variation in addition level, determine their interaction behavior with IRBNC. As shown in Fig. 16, different catechin structures interact with IRBNC to varying degrees, but at the optimal addition level (EC: 25 mg/g; EGC: 50 mg/g; ECG: 25 mg/g), protein flexibility increases, crystallinity decreases, and particle networks become more porous, as previously reported for polyphenol-induced modification of rice bran components and protein–polyphenol systems (Li et al., 2021; Xie et al., 2020). These structural transitions enhance particle amphiphilicity, promotes particle diffusion and adsorption at the oil–water interface, together with the formation of a compact interfacial layer (Lin et al., 2022; Liu et al., 2023a; Xiao et al., 2018). The interfacial layer acts as a physical barrier restricting oxygen diffusion and limiting contact between lipid substrates and prooxidants, thereby suppressing lipid oxidation in emulsified systems (Han et al., 2024; Fei et al., 2023). Enhanced interfacial coverage further reduces oil droplet exposure to the aqueous phase, improving oxidative stability of emulsions (Wang et al., 2022b). Emulsions stabilized by modified particles exhibit reduced droplet size, improved microstructural uniformity, and enhanced viscoelastic behavior, consistent with previous studies on protein–polyphenol and polysaccharide–protein Pickering emulsions (Huang et al., 2024; Li et al., 2024b; Liu et al., 2023a).Fig. 16Mechanism diagram of catechin monomers enhancing the stability of Pickering emulsions stabilizing IRBNC through structure-dependent interfacial structure modulation.

Crucially, the regulatory efficiency is governed by the specific molecular structures of the catechin monomers. ECG, featuring a galloyl moiety and the highest phenolic hydroxyl density, exhibits the strongest binding affinity with IRBNC, promoting the tightest interfacial packing and superior emulsion stability. Conversely, EC facilitates faster interfacial migration due to lower steric hindrance, whereas EGC requires a higher concentration (50 mg/g) to achieve comparable structural rearrangement owing to the intermediate hydroxyl density of EGC. However, regardless of the catechin type, excessive addition (50–100 mg/g) induces over-crosslinking and particle self-aggregation. The resulting over-crosslinking shifts particle wettability toward excessive hydrophilicity and impairs interfacial mobility, ultimately compromising Pickering emulsion stability (Li et al., 2025a). Overall, the present findings reveal the interconnected structure–dosage–function relationship governing IRBNC particle assembly, interfacial behavior, and the subsequent performance of the complexes in Pickering emulsions.

Conclusions

The study systematically compared EC, EGC, and ECG for the modification of IRBNC to elucidate the relationships among catechin molecular structure, IRBNC structural reorganization, interfacial properties, and Pickering emulsion stability. Catechin modification increased the bound phenolic content and decreased the free amino and sulfhydryl contents of IRBNC, accompanied by reduced crystallinity and altered protein secondary structure, indicating substantial structural reorganization. These changes further regulated particle aggregation and shifted IRBNC surface wettability from highly hydrophobic toward a more favorable wettability range, which facilitated interfacial adsorption and contributed to improved Pickering emulsion stability. Moderate catechin addition enhanced particle adsorption at the oil–water interface and promoted the formation of an effective interfacial layer, thereby reducing droplet size and improving physical stability, while the phenolic hydroxyl groups and antioxidant capacity of catechins enhanced oxidative stability. Excessive catechin addition, however, promoted particle aggregation and weakened interfacial stabilization, resulting in reduced emulsion stability. The three catechins exhibited distinct structure-dependent effects on particle structure, interfacial properties, and emulsion stability, with ECG showing the most pronounced overall effect. The enhanced performance was associated with its greater ability to regulate IRBNC molecular structure, particle aggregation, wettability, and interfacial properties, thereby contributing to superior emulsion stabilization. These findings demonstrate that catechin molecular structure plays an important role in determining catechin-IRBNC interactions and subsequent emulsion properties. Overall, the systematic comparison of EC, EGC, and ECG establishes a structure-property relationship linking catechin molecular structure with IRBNC reorganization, interfacial behavior, and Pickering emulsion stability, providing a theoretical basis for the rational design of natural Pickering stabilizers. Further molecular and interfacial characterization may clarify the underlying interaction pathways and interfacial assembly mechanisms. Future studies integrating advanced molecular and interfacial characterization techniques may provide deeper insights into the interaction pathways between catechins and IRBNC and the assembly behavior of particles at oil–water interfaces, thereby facilitating the rational development of natural Pickering stabilizers with tailored functionalities.
