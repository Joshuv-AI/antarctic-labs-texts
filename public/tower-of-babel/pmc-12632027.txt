Cardiometabolic risk reduction with digital twinning in patients with type 2 diabetes
Authors: Seyed Arsalan Seyedi, Juan P. González-Rivas, Pranav Mellacheruvu, Ananya Mellacheruvu, Seyed Pedram Aledavood, Alireza Esteghamati, Jeffrey I. Mechanick
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Digital twin (DT) technology—real-time, data-driven virtual models of individuals—offers transformative potential in managing cardiometabolic-based chronic disease (CMBCD). The novel CMBCD model configures a progression from interacting adiposity, dysglycemia, hypertension, dyslipidemia, and residual drivers to certain cardiovascular diseases (CVDs) such as atherosclerosis, heart failure, and atrial fibrillation. Insulin resistance and type 2 diabetes (T2D) are central to this cascade, requiring management tactics where DT serves as a precision-medicine tool. A systematic search across PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, Scopus, and Cochrane identified 12 eligible studies (retrospective cohort, randomized clinical trials, and frameworks) to explore the role of DT in T2D and CMBCD. Due to data duplication and lack of homogeneity, meta-analysis was not feasible, prompting this narrative synthesis. DT-guided benefits were identified across the CMBCD spectrum: (1) adiposity – body mass index reduction by 1.8 kg/m² with decreased visceral adiposity; (2) dysglycemia – hemoglobin A1c reduction as much as 1.8% with 89% achieving glycemic targets, as well as fewer medications used, time-in-range (TIR) improved 69.7% to 86.9%, and time-above-range decreased; (3) hypertension – normal blood pressure cases increased from 46.4% to 63.1% and outperforming standard care; (4) dyslipidemia – triglycerides decreased 18.8% and high density lipoprotein (HDL) increased 6.8%; (5) residual – metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease improvements; and (6) complications – improved chronic kidney disease, retinopathy, and cataract prediction, comprehensive CVD risk reduction, and medication de-escalation. In sum, DT appears promising for comprehensive and personalized T2D care and cardiometabolic risk reduction. Broader validation in diverse populations, refined implementation strategies, and eventual systematic reviews and meta-analyses are needed.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40842-025-00242-8.

Introduction

A DT is a virtual representation of a physical object, structure, or system that integrates real-time data from the subject it mirrors. This technology enables performance analysis, simulations, and the identification of potential issues in its physical counterpart [1]. The concept of DT originated in the 1960s when NASA employed basic twinning techniques for space programming during the Apollo missions [2]. The term “digital twin” was formally introduced in 1997 by Hernández and Hernández [3] in the context of urban planning and highway design.

Recently, DT technology has expanded into the healthcare sector, where it is transforming medical practice by facilitating personalized treatment, disease modeling, predictive analytics, drug development, and medical training [4]. Detailed virtual models of individual patients allow healthcare professionals to simulate and refine tailored treatment options, resulting in more effective therapies [5]. For instance, DTs can simulate the effects of medications or lifestyle changes on a patient’s unique physiology, enhancing the precision of care. Disease modeling with DTs improves researchers’ understanding of disease progression and supports the development of novel treatments [6]. Real-time data updates enhance predictive analytics, allowing for timely adjustments to patient care [6]. In drug development, DTs simulate drug interactions within the human body, accelerating the testing process [7]. Additionally, virtual replicas of patient anatomy offer a risk-free platform for medical training, including procedural skills, without compromising patient safety.

The potential of DT technology is particularly significant in managing chronic conditions such as T2D [8]. Traditional T2D management often relies on generalized guidelines, which may not fully address individual variations in disease progression or treatment response [9]. Comprehensive T2D extends beyond simple glycemic control to prevention/management of complications, particularly CVD [10]. More specifically, cardiometabolic risk is related to the development/progression of certain CVDs (atherosclerotic CVD [ASCVD], heart failure [HF], and atrial fibrillation [AF]) that have metabolic drivers. The CMBCD model provides a comprehensive framework for preventing CVD at primary, secondary, and tertiary levels. By identifying insulin resistance as the primary factor in disease progression, it highlights the importance of addressing modifiable risk factors to reduce patient suffering and societal expenses [10]. In brief, CMBCD breaks down interacting metabolic drivers (adiposity-based chronic disease [ABCD], dysglycemia-based chronic disease [DBCD], hypertension-based chronic disease [HBCD], dyslipidemia-based chronic disease [LBCD], and residual risks (e.g., metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease [MASLD] [11]) into four pathophysiological stages (risk, predisease, disease, and complications), in the context of ASCVD/HF/AF, modified by social determinants of health (SDOH) and transcultural factors [10]. This complications-centric, holistic approach highlights the importance prevention, especially focusing on the central role of insulin resistance.

DT technology provides personalized, data-driven insights as specific clinical recommendations. In T2D care, DTs can predict blood glucose responses, enhance monitoring, detect specific complication risks, generate tailored treatment plans, enable disease reversal, and help design clinical trials. Nevertheless, formal aggregated evaluations of DT technology in T2D management remain underexplored, with limited evidence in the current literature [12]. As the CMBCD model offers a pragmatic framework for cardiometabolic risk, this review reexamines the evolving evidence on DT technology to provide updated conclusions and insights into comprehensive T2D management across the CMBCD spectrum.

Methods

This review is based on the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-analysis (PRISMA) Statement 2020 [13]. The article’s protocol was registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO): CRD420251005313.

Search strategy

Web of Science, Embase, PubMed, Cochrane Library, and Scopus databases were searched systematically until January 2025. Search strings were related to the DT technology and T2D (Supplemental Table 1). This systematic search was performed without any language restrictions.

Study selection

Eligibility criteria

Only original and peer-reviewed studies evaluating the impact of DT technology on the management of T2D in adult participants were included.

Exclusion criteria

Studies without adequate information about DT as the intervention or participants’ diagnosis of T2D were excluded. In addition, preprint papers, review articles, editorial letters, reports, dissertations, conference abstracts, and book chapters were excluded. For eligibility and exclusion, two authors (S.A.S. and S.P.A.) independently screened the title/abstract and for selected studies, reviewed the full texts. In case of disagreement, these authors discussed findings to reach an agreement. If unable, a third author (P.M.) resolved the disagreement.

Data extraction

Two authors (S.A.S. and S.P.A.) independently extracted data from the included studies. If they could not reach an agreement regarding an item, a third author (P.M.) resolved the disagreement. The data extracted from articles were the first author’s name, publication year, design of the study, location of the study, total number of participants, model, DT procedure, DT data required, DT application, and key outcomes.

Quality assessment

Evaluating the risk of bias was considered necessary to establish the reliability and validity of the aggregated evidence presented in this systematic review. In view of the systematic nature of our search and the potential limitations of the included studies—including overlapping cohorts and variations in study design—a structured quality assessment was carried out.

Two authors (S.A.S. and P.M.) carried out an independent review of risk of bias, with disagreements resolved by a third author (S.P.A.). To appraise randomized controlled trials (RCTs), we applied the Cochrane Risk of Bias 2.0 (RoB 2.0) tool, which evaluates bias across five discrete domains: (1) randomization process, (2) deviations from intended interventions, (3) missing outcome data, (4) measurement of the outcome, and (5) selective reporting. Each of these domain is assessed individually, and an overall judgment of “low risk,” “some concerns,” or “high risk” is then assigned, thereby providing a standardized framework to identify and quantify potential bias within RCTs [14].

For observational studies, we used the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS). The tool rates potential bias across three major domains: (1) study group selection, (2) cohort comparability, and (3) outcome assessment. The NOS assigns a score from 0 to 9 points, with predefined cut-offs used to categorize studies as high risk (0–3), moderate risk (4–6), or low risk (7–9). This scoring system allows semi-quantitative study quality assessment and ensures consistency across evidence [15].

By applying these complementary tools, we aimed to compare both randomized and observational studies objectively in a transparent manner, allowing for a more accurate interpretation of the robustness of our findings.

Results

Study characteristics

A total of 129 studies were identified, of which 69 were exact duplicates, leaving 60 unique articles. After screening titles and abstracts, 45 articles were excluded. The remaining 15 articles underwent full-text evaluation, resulting in the exclusion of three articles. Ultimately, 12 studies were included (Fig. 1). Among the included studies, six were retrospective cohort studies [16–21], three were RCTs [22–24], and three presented frameworks proposing reliable architectures for implementing DT technology in the management of T2D [25–27]. However, due to potential bias—specifically, the repeated use of the same cohort across five studies—and a lack of data homogeneity, conducting a meta-analysis was not feasible; therefore, a narrative synthesis was undertaken instead. The studies were conducted across various countries, including India, the Netherlands, Singapore, and the U.S. The sample sizes of the included cohort studies and RCTs ranged from 64 to 7,072 participants, with a total of 11,296 with T2D (Table 1). The findings of these nine studies (Table 1) and the three frameworks (Table 2) are given below, categorized across the CMBCD spectrum, followed by aggregated quality assessments of the body of evidence.

Cardiometabolic risk reduction

Abnormal adiposity

ABCD comprises derangements in adiposity amount (e.g., body mass index [BMI]) and distribution (e.g., waist circumference [WC]). With DT intervention, significant mean (standard deviation [SD]) BMI reductions of -2.7 (± 2.3), -1.7 (± 2), and − 1.8 (± 3.39) were observed from baseline measurements in the DT groups of DT precision treatment program [16], DT precision nutrition program [19], and Joshi et al. [24] study, after follow-up periods of one-year, 90-days, and one-year, respectively (p < 0.001). Two studies documented the weight loss using DT, with 4.8 (± 5.7) kg weight reduction (p < 0.001) in study by Shamanna et al. [16] In another study by Joshi et al., [24] 73.8% of individuals achieved a ≥ 5% in weight loss and 41.6% experienced a weight loss of ≥ 10% (p < 0.001). In addition, mean WC in DT group (n = 232) experienced a reduction of 9.5 (± 7.7) after one year.

Dysglycemia

In a retrospective study of 1,853 patients in a DT-targeted program, the average hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c) decreased by 1.8% (p < 0.001), with 89% of the patients reaching their glycemic goals (HbA1c < 7.0%) and an average reduction of 1.5 anti-diabetic medications per patient. Moreover, continuous glucose monitor data showed that TIR increased from 69.7% (± 30.7) to 86.9% (± 24.5) (p < 0.001), suggesting a potential positive effect of DT on overall glycemic control. Time-above-range was reduced (p < 0.001), though time-below-range slightly increased compared to baseline, particularly for glucoses below 54 mg/dL (p < 0.001). Patients also lost a mean weight of 4.8 kg (p < 0.001) [16].

A subsequent retrospective study of a DT-enabled program supported the above findings by evaluating glycemic variability, BMI, blood pressure changes, and antihypertensive medication use in 64 patients with T2D over 90 days. The mean (SD) glucose percentage coefficient of variation (%CV) was 20.58% (10.22) on the first day of the program. Moreover, mean %CV (SD) was 15.75% (6.10) during days 24 to 30 (p < 0.001), 16.62% (5.96) during days 54 to 60 (p < 0.001), and 17.37% (6.00) during days 84 to 90 of the program (p < 0.05). Over the course of 90-days, the mean %CV (SD) was 17.34% (± 4.36%) (p < 0.05) [18].

A retrospective cohort study evaluating the impact of DT-enabled precision nutrition on glycemic control in T2D reported significant benefits. Among 64 patients with T2D, DT-enabled dietary recommendations showed favorable outcomes including a mean HbA1c reduction of 1.9% (p < 0.001) at 90 days, and 30% of the patients achieved an HbA1c of < 6.5% without any rise in medication use. Fasting glucose levels decreased by 22 mg/dL (p < 0.001), while 57 patients had TIR > 70% after 90 days. Furthermore, the Homeostatic Model Assessment of Insulin Resistance (HOMA-IR) decreased significantly by 56.9% from 7.4 (± 3.5) to 3.2 (± 2.8) (p < 0.001) [17].

The impact of 90 days of DT-enabled nutritional on T2D remission was investigated in a retrospective cohort study. Among 463 individuals, 74% experienced reduction in medication use or HbA1c levels, with 31% achieving partial remission (HbA1c < 6.5% without glucose-lowering medications for at least three months), 11% complete remission (HbA1c < 5.7% without glucose-lowering medication for at least three months) [28], and an overall 21.2% reduction in average HbA1c [19].

A comparative study on DT for predictive glycemic control in T2D remission showed that personalized nutrition may stabilize glucose levels and reduce glycemic variability. In 319 subjects receiving DT intervention (n = 233) vs. standard of care (SC; usual dietary counseling) (n = 86), DT intervention was associated with significant reduction in HbA1c from 9.0 (± 1.9) to 6.1 (± 0.7) (p < 0.001) compared to SC from 8.5 (± 1.9) to 8.2 (± 1.6) (p > 0.05). At the 1-year mark, 94% of the DT group stopped T2D medication use, with 72.7% achieving T2D remission. In contrast, all participants in the SC group continued their T2D medication, and no cases of T2D remission were observed [23].

HBCD

In a study by Shamanna et al. [23], a substantial reduction in systolic blood pressure (SBP) was observed from 140.2 (± 15.8) to 127.1 (± 8.3) mmHg (P = 0.02) and a notable reduction in diastolic blood pressure (DBP) from 94.7 (± 11.7) to 86.8 (± 6.4) mmHg (P = 0.014) in the DT intervention group after six months of follow-up. In addition, a significant HTN remission rate of 60% was observed in the DT group, with twelve patients discontinuing their anti-HTN medications [23].

Another study using DT showed significant findings regarding blood pressure changes, and anti-HTN medication use in 64 patients with T2D. Following 90 days of follow-up, SBP and DBP decreased by 10.1 mmHg (p < 0.01) and 3.6 mmHg (p < 0.01), respectively. In addition, baseline anti-HTN medication use was present in 23 (35.94%) patients, of which, 20 patients were medication free after 90 days on DT use (p < 0.0001) [18].

In a 1-year RCT involving 319 patients, DT interventions significantly lowered SBP (-7.6 mm Hg vs. -3.2 mm Hg; p < 0.01) and DBP (-4.3 mm Hg vs. -2.2 mm Hg; p < 0.05) compared to SC. The proportion of individuals with normal BP in the DT group increased from 108 (46.4%) to 147 (63.1%), presenting superior performance compared to SC where normal BP participants increased only from 25 (30%) to 28 (33%). Furthermore, 30 participants from the DT group discontinued their anti-HTN medications at the end of the 1-year mark, while all SC participants remained on their anti-HTN medications, demonstrating that DT-facilitated interventions enhance BP control while reducing medication burden [22]. Collectively, DT interventions were associated with improvements in blood pressure control and medication reduction across diverse cohorts.

LBCD

With DT use, Shamanna et al. [18] reported mean (SD) HDL cholesterol levels increased over time, from 42.9 (± 10.5) mg/dL at baseline to 44.1 (± 14.2) mg/dL at 30 days and 48.0 (± 12.2) mg/dL at 90 days. Low-density lipoprotein (LDL) cholesterol increased from 103.7 (± 33.9) mg/dL at baseline to 120.2 (± 39.9) mg/dL at 30 days, followed by a slight decrease to 117.5 (± 47.9) mg/dL at 90 days. Non-HDL cholesterol increased from 138.1 (± 37.5) mg/dL at baseline to 154.9 (± 41.9) mg/dL at 30 days, and then declined to 144.1 (± 52.0) mg/dL at 90 days. Total cholesterol values were 181.0 (± 39.2) mg/dL at baseline, 201.0 (± 48.0) mg/dL at 30 days, and 192.1 (± 51.0) mg/dL at 90 days. Triglyceride levels decreased progressively from 186.2 (± 103.8) mg/dL at baseline to 157.7 (± 92.9) mg/dL at 30 days, and 135.1 (± 77.5) mg/dL at 90 days. The triglyceride-to-HDL ratio changed from 4.7 (± 3.4) at baseline to 4.9 (± 7.4) at 30 days, and declined to 3.1 (± 2.1) at 90 days. [18] In another cohort of 463 individuals using DT-enabled precision nutrition, mean triglycerides decreased by 18.8% (p < 0.001, N = 244), mean HDL increased by 6.8% (p < 0.001, N = 244), and changes in very low-density lipoprotein and apolipoprotein B were not significant throughout the study [19].

Residual risk

Joshi et al. [24] also evaluated liver fibrosis indices with DT-enabled precision nutrition in T2D. In a one-year RCT of 319 patients, these interventions resulted in lower liver fibrosis scores, with the proportion of patients at one year with a normal score and fatty liver index increasing from 11.8% to 67.4% and 13.6% to 54.3%, respectively (compared to 16% to 9.9% and 15.9% to 13.4%, respectively, with SC; p < 0.001). Also at 1 year, nine of the ten DT patients with abnormal fibrosis (FIB)-4 scores at baseline became normal (compared to only two or the eight SC patients; p < 0.001 between groups). [24]

Joshi et al. also evaluated the fatty liver and MAFLD by Individualized nutrition using DT in T2D patients. A one-year RCT of 319 patients, 233 on DT, and 86 on SC revealed that nutrition interventions guided by DT resulted in a significant reduction of hepatic fat content and a significant decrease in liver fibrosis scores. At 1 year, more patients in the DT group achieved a liver fat content < 6% by magnetic resonance imaging proton density fat fraction than in the SC group (129 [83.2%] vs. 17 [34%], respectively) [24].

Cardiometabolic-based chronic disease complications

Chronic kidney disease

A DT model with generalized metabolic flux (GMF) analysis was efficacious in chronic kidney disease (CKD) prediction and diagnosis in patients with T2D [20]. Among 1,333 patients with T2D from Singaporean and a North American cohorts, this model detected CKD with an Area Under the Curve (AUC) of 0.80 (95% confidence interval (CI): 0.74–0.85) and 0.82 (95% CI: 0.79–0.84), respectively [20]. The model further validated its robustness by accurately predicting CKD in two multi-center Singaporean cohorts—with/without complete patient data—achieving AUCs of 0.86 and 0.75 (95% CI: 0.73–0.77), respectively [20]. Notably, the GMF maintained consistent accuracy for long-term CKD prediction over three, five, and ten years with AUCs of 0.75 (95% CI: 0.73–0.77), 0.75 (95% CI: 0.72–0.77), and 0.73 (95% CI: 0.71–0.75), respectively [20].

Ophthalmic

The DT model with GMF analysis also predicted ophthalmic complications. In a study of 806 patients with T2D, the model identified diabetic retinopathy with an AUC of 0.84 (sensitivity 80%, specificity 71%) or cataracts with an AUC of 0.79 (sensitivity 80%, specificity 62%), respectively. Prediction of three-year diabetic retinopathy and cataract occurrence had AUCs of 0.81 (sensitivity 78%, specificity 70%) and 0.93 (sensitivity 87%, specificity 95%), respectively [21].

Cardiovascular

In a cohort study of DT use, the resting heart rate mean beats/minute (range), as an indicator of cardiovascular health, decreased from baseline of 75.0 (65—85) to 90-day of 71.0 (62—85) (p < 0.0001) [17]. In another cohort (N = 179 patients with T2D), mean (SD) 10-year ASCVD risk scores were significantly reduced from 12.2% (± 12.2%) at baseline to 10.6% (± 11.0%) at final follow-up visit (p < 0.001) [19].

Digital twin frameworks presented for type 2 diabetes care

Zhang et al. [25] constructed a comprehensive DT framework for T2D management that integrates real-time patient data with machine learning (ML) models and physiological simulations. This framework was validated using data from 1,131 patients with T2D at six months and 639 at one year. The framework’s primary strength lies in its integration of multiple data sources, including continuous glucose monitoring (CGM), dietary logs, and medication adherence, which have enabled a wider view of patients’ health. However, significant limitations were high computational demands and densely sampled longitudinal data, limiting accessibility across resource-poor areas.

A specialized DT framework designed specifically for elderly patients with T2D addressed the unique challenges of diabetes management in older populations by accounting for age-related physiological changes and comorbidities [26]. Implementation of this framework (n = 15) resulted in TIR improvements from 3% to 79% to 86%-97% with a 14%-29% reduction in insulin requirements [26]. Though responsive to the needs in geriatric populations, the use of geriatric databases may not be translatable to younger populations, limiting generalizability and necessitating validation across age groups [26].

Distribution-based sub-population selection (DSPS) represents another DT approach in T2D, focusing on the in-silico reproduction of clinical trial outcomes to optimize treatment strategies. In a retrospective analysis of 1,100 patients, DSPS accurately simulated treatment responses with an 89% concordance rate to real-world clinical outcomes (p < 0.001). This framework enabled precision medicine by predicting which sub-populations will respond best to specific interventions, reducing trial-and-error in treatment planning. Another major strength of DSPS is in personalized drug selection and clinical decision-making. The constraints of simulation platforms include the absence of precise physiological simulation, oversimplification of behavioral aspects, and assuming perfect intervention adherence by patients. These limitations emphasize the necessity for constant enhancement and validation of simulation platforms to include additional sophisticated and realistic physiological and behavioral elements [27].

Quality assessment

This review includes three RCTs that were evaluated using the RoB-2 tool and rated two of the studies as having low risk of bias and one study as having some concern. The concern in the third study was from the missing outcome data domain, where the follow-up outcomes had missing data that were not fully addressed in the analysis (Supplemental Table 2). In addition, there were six observational studies assessed with the NOS tool, of which three received a score of eight out of nine stars, and three received a score of nine stars. Overall, all studies demonstrated a low risk of bias (Supplemental Table 3).

Discussion

This study investigated the clinical significance of DT technology interventions on cardiometabolic health in patients with T2D. Reductions in BMI, waist circumference, and weight indicate improved adiposity, a key T2D and cardiovascular risk factor. Decreased HbA1c and increased TIR reflect better glycemic control, lowering microvascular and macrovascular risks, and blood pressure and hypertension remission suggest reduced cardiovascular risk and medication needs. Moreover, favorable lipid profile changes may lower atherosclerosis risk, and reduced liver fibrosis and hepatic fat support DT-guided nutrition in managing NAFLD. In addition, DT models for prediction of CKD and eye complications offer potential for early risk assessment and personalized care to prevent T2D complications. Additionally, the generalizability of these findings is supported by the diversity of cohort sizes (64 to > 7,000 participants) and studies conducted across multiple countries (e.g., India, the Netherlands, Singapore, and the U.S.), spanning retrospective cohorts and randomized controlled trials.

Using continuous data analyses, both real-time and historical, DT technology may transform T2D management and improve CMBCD risk via personalization of treatment strategies. Specifically, DT-enabled interventions are associated with DBCD risk reduction in terms of better glycemic control and higher rates of T2D remission. Beyond this, DT-enabled personalized nutrition mitigated various markers of ABCD, HBCD, LBCD, residual risks such as MASLD, and complications particularly CVD-related.

The capabilities of DT technology are more widely reported for its transformative features in managing T2D. A significant application includes patient-centered DTs that take into account multiple sources of information related to patients, including CGM data, dietary records, physical activity, and medication adherence [16, 29]. In addition, DT frameworks can generate predictions, support management, and mitigate CMBCD progression in terms of complication prevention [20, 22].

The routine incorporation of DT into preventive healthcare for T2D has great potential reducing the global burden of T2D. Leveraging DT technology may provide innovative scalable, dynamic, and data-driven approaches to chronic metabolic disease management, potentially reducing healthcare costs, polypharmacy, symptom burdens, and disability [30, 31]. Moreover, DT systems may enable aggregate population-level analytics using continuous data collection to support many different healthcare systems in identifying best practice approaches to target cohorts at various risk levels—a customizable paradigm [25].

This ability to analyze and create analytics best-practices for precision public health, allows for precision targeting based on various SDOH and transcultural factors [25, 31]. Accordingly, future studies on groups with distinct SDOH who are at the highest risk for DBCD development and progression are warranted.

The remote monitoring aspect of DT may also improve access in low resource areas, to address some of the inequities with traditional delivery methods of care delivery [30]. The knowledge from RCTs demonstrating that DT provides assistance in achieving remission with T2D and resolution of comorbidities will create opportunities for policy change with attention on reimbursement and infrastructure for equitable, cost-effective diabetes prevention and management [31].

DT frameworks

DT frameworks can be effectively integrated into existing healthcare infrastructures by leveraging real-time data from electronic health records (EHRs), wearable devices, and continuous monitoring tools to create personalized, dynamic patient models [32]. These models enable disease management through predictive approaches and simulation of treatment responses, thus enhancing precision medicine in conditions like T2D. This integration requires established interoperability standards for seamless data exchange, robust data security to protect patient privacy, and scalable cloud-based platforms for high computational demands on densely sampled longitudinal data [33, 34]. Embedding DTs within clinical workflows may support clinicians with decision-making, optimize resource assignment, and facilitate remote monitoring, ultimately may improve patient outcomes and healthcare efficiency [35]. However, successful integration must address challenges such as data quality, system interoperability, computational resources, and regulatory compliance to ensure accessibility in different healthcare systems.

Developing basic simulation and predictive models through digital twinning necessitates various input data, including real-time physiological data like CGM and heart rate monitoring, clinical imaging (MRI, CT scans), lab test outcomes, and EHRs [36]. Moreover, information regarding patient behavior, lifestyle, genomic, and proteomic data enhance model precision. Integrating these diverse data types enables adaptive, customized simulations that represent individual health status for efficient disease management and prognosis [35, 37].

ML models play a key role in the development of DTs by allowing precise simulations and predicting capabilities. These models include deep learning (DL) (e.g., neural networks (NNs), long short-term memory (LSTM) networks), gradient boosting algorithms (e.g., gradient boosting machine (GBM), CatBoost), and tree-ensemble methods (e.g., random forest (RF)). While NNs and LSTM are proficient at handling time-series, high-dimensional healthcare data, and effectively capturing temporal dependencies, they require large, well-annotated datasets, careful regularization, and extensive external validation before deployment in clinical workflows. Gradient boosting algorithms including GBM and CatBoost, and tree-ensemble methods including RF are both popular for their excellent predictive accuracy on organized clinical data and their capacity to manage heterogeneous types of features [38, 39]. The incorporation of internet of things devices delivers continuous real-time data that support these models for ongoing personalization [40].

Computational requirements differ based on model complexity; DL and LSTM models typically require significant graphics processing unit (GPU) resources and memory because of extensive NN structures and the necessity for sequential data handling. Gradient boosting techniques (e.g., CatBoost, GBM) and tree-ensemble bagging methods (e.g., RF) might need significant processing power for extensive datasets or real-time predictions. Successful DT frameworks typically depend on scalable, cloud-based infrastructure to fulfill these requirements, guaranteeing the smooth incorporation of ML models with healthcare systems for ongoing monitoring and support in decision-making [32, 38–40].

Strengths and limitations

Although some overlap with previous reviews may exist [41, 42], this review is distinguished by its systematic search of the current literature to assess the emerging progress of DT technology in reducing CMBCD risk among individuals with T2D. In addition, exploring and presenting DT frameworks for T2D care adds to the novelty of this study. The comprehensive assessment in this study yielded promising findings regarding the implementation of DT technology for future T2D care. However, several limitations should be addressed. A significant number of the included studies were conducted by the same team of investigators with some cohort overlaps, potentially introducing bias. Although these studies employed distinct DT models and assessed different primary outcomes, overlapping cohorts and the evaluation of some similar secondary outcomes may lead to substantial bias and affect the overall quality of findings. Moreover, the presence of multiple studies conducted by the same research team may introduce author bias, which warrants further investigation through cohort studies conducted by independent teams. Additionally, many studies lacked a proper validation process, which is crucial for integrating any technology into clinical care. Moreover, some studies did not include a control group to compare intervention results with SC, limiting the ability to validate the significance of DT. Additionally, three studies that did include a control arm did not use CGM in the control group, making it difficult to isolate the effects of the DT intervention.

Conclusions

This paper emphasizes the significant potential of DT technology to revolutionize T2D management for reducing CMBCD risk. Utilizing real-time patient information, DT models facilitate tailored interventions and accurate treatment enhancement. Incorporating DT into clinical practice may enhance results in several cardiometabolic domains; however, additional validation and wider accessibility are required. Future research should aim to incorporate CGM in control groups to more effectively isolate the effects of DT.
