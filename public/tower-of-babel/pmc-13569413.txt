Oral nano-delivery of a gut microbial metabolite enhances T cell stemness for cancer immunotherapy
Authors: Kai Han, Young Seok Cho, Mariko Takahashi, Xingwu Zhou, Hannah E Dobson, Kim Hutchings, Yuesong Wu, Youngseo Na, Fang Xie, Julia Crowther, Jinmei Wu, Jin Xu, Chuan Lee, Himani Jasewicz, Yujin Kim, Minal Nenwani, Olamide Animasahun, Fulei Wuchu, Anthony Andren, Harrison Wong, Emma Camp, Ziye Wan, Qi Wu, Li Zhang, Cheng Xu, Katherine Dong, Yao Xu, Anna Schwendeman, Grace Y Chen, Yuying Xie, Costas A Lyssiotis, Martin Clasby, Deepak Nagrath, Yu Leo Lei, James J Moon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractGut microbial metabolites play crucial roles in regulating systemic immunity, but their mechanisms and limited drug-like properties remain unresolved. Here we report an oral nano-formulation that leverages gut microbial metabolites to modulate T cell metabolism and amplify antitumour immunity. Through an in vitro screening of gut microbial metabolites, we identified 3,4-dihydroxybenzoic acid that improved adoptive T cell therapy and enhanced CD8+ T cell stemness by suppressing glycolysis and regulating the Akt-mTORC1-Myc pathway. To harness the potency of 3,4-dihydroxybenzoic acid for systemic cancer immunotherapy, we engineered a 3,4-dihydroxybenzoic acid prodrug nano-emulsion, significantly increasing its oral absorption and half-life. In multiple murine tumour models, the oral nano-emulsion enhanced the expansion of antigen-specific, stem-like CD8+ T cells, sensitizing tumours to anti-PD-1 blockade and exerting robust antitumour efficacy. By integrating nanotechnology with microbial-metabolite-based immunotherapy, this study establishes a mechanistic link between the gut microbiota and T cell immunity, offering a promising approach for cancer immunotherapy.Subject terms: Drug delivery, Nanoparticles, Biomedical engineering

Main

Immune checkpoint blockade (ICB) antibodies have revolutionized cancer immunotherapy, but their overall low response rates in patients remain a major challenge1. Emerging evidence suggests that microbial metabolites are critical for the crosstalk between the gut microbiota and systemic immunity through the regulation of T cell phenotype and effector functions2–5, suggesting their therapeutic potential to improve antitumour immunity. However, the role of microbial metabolites in T cell immunity and fitness remains largely unexplored, particularly in the context of ICB cancer therapy.

We previously reported that oral inulin gel enhances antitumour immunity and synergizes with ICB via the gut microbiota–short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs)–CD8+ T cell axis6. Interestingly, we observed that inulin gel substantially altered a library of non-SCFA microbial metabolites, leading to our hypothesis that these metabolites may control T cell immunity. In particular, gut microbial metabolites may serve as a rich source of new compounds for drug development, as they offer favourable safety profiles, well-defined chemical structures, scalability for manufacturing and quality control7. However, many microbial metabolites are highly hydrophilic with a low molecular weight, showing poor oral absorption and pharmacokinetic (PK)8 properties with a short plasma half-life and high clearance rate. Their clearance from the systemic circulation can occur within minutes, thereby limiting their bioavailability and effective regulation of T cell immunity in vivo9.

In this paper, we report a microbial-metabolite-based oral nano-emulsion that regulates T cell metabolism and triggers robust T cell immune responses in multiple mouse tumour models and preserves the stemness of CD8+ T cells (Fig. 1a). By utilizing an in vitro screening of these altered non-SCFA metabolites on inulin gel administration, we identified 3,4-dihydroxybenzoic acid (DHB; also known as protocatechuic acid) as a key microbial metabolite that promotes the generation of memory T cells via the suppression of glycolysis. To improve the oral absorption and PK properties of the metabolite, we developed DHB into a synthetic prodrug in an oral nano-emulsion formulation, which substantially increased its half-life and bioavailability. The oral DHB prodrug formulation sensitized mice to anti-PD-1 blockade by boosting antigen-specific stem-like T cells in vivo, and demonstrated how to improve the drug-like properties of microbial metabolites. As small-molecule prodrugs have been widely used in modern pharmaceutics, our DHB prodrug formulation shows promising translational potential for systemic cancer immunotherapy.

DHB induces stem-like CD8+ T cell differentiation

We have previously reported that oral inulin gel plus α-PD-1 IgG therapy expanded CD8+ T cells with stemness in part by modulating the gut microbiome and SCFA levels6. As the metabolomics data6 revealed that inulin gel plus α-PD-1 IgG therapy also altered various non-SCFA microbial metabolites, we sought to examine whether these non-SCFA microbial metabolites could regulate the phenotype of CD8+ T cells towards stem-like CD8+ T cells, known to promote long immunological memory; possess features of self-renewal, persistence and multipotency; and improve ICB efficacy10. In particular, we focused on glycerol, N-acetylneuraminate, γ-aminobutyric acid (GABA), pantothenate, disodium sebacate, hippurate, 5-aminoimidazole-4-carboxamide ribonucleotide, ferulate and DHB, based on our prior metabolomics data showing their markedly altered levels in the faeces after treatment with inulin gel plus α-PD-16. Some other substantially altered metabolites were excluded because of their potential toxicity (for example, phenylacetaldehyde), the reported immune-activating effects (for example, ursodeoxycholate11, linoleate12 and arginine13). Splenocytes from naïve OT-I mice were activated with SIINFEKL antigen peptide, followed by incubation with these microbial metabolites and interleukin-2 (IL-2) (Fig. 1b). Among these microbial metabolites, DHB significantly increased the frequencies of CD44+CD62L+CD8+ central memory T (Tcm) cells (Fig. 1c and Supplementary Fig. 1a) and T cell factor 1+ (Tcf1) CD8+ T cells, in which CD62L and Tcf1 were encoded by the stem-like markers Sell and Tcf7, respectively, and reported to be positively associated with ICB outcome in cancer patients14 (Fig. 1d and Supplementary Fig. 1b). Moreover, DHB maintained T cell viability even when IL-2 was withdrawn for 36 h, compared with all the other groups (Fig. 1e and Supplementary Fig. 1c). In line with this stem-like phenotype, DHB treatment decreased the frequency of CD44+CD62L−CD8+ T effector memory cells, GzmB+CD8+ T cells and IFN-γ+CD8+ T cells, indicating less terminal effector differentiation (Supplementary Fig. 2 and Extended Data Fig. 1a,b); however, DHB-treated T cells exhibited a robust IFN-γ recall response on antigen restimulation (Fig. 1f and Supplementary Fig. 2). In addition, DHB-treated OT-I CD8+ T cells showed an increased expression of Sca-1, a hematopoietic stem cell antigen marker within memory-precursor-like T cells15,16 (Fig. 1g and Extended Data Fig. 1c). DHB treatment also upregulated the expression of Forkhead O transcription factor 1 (Foxo1) and decreased Foxo1 phosphorylation, indicating that DHB engages Foxo1 to sustain stem-like T cell function and maintenance8,17,18 (Fig. 1h and Extended Data Fig. 1d). Besides, DHB did not affect the proliferation of CD8+ T cells (Supplementary Fig. 3). Interestingly, several DHB isomers, including 2,4-DHB and 2,3-DHB, also increased the generation of CD8+ Tcm cells (Fig. 1i). However, among these DHB isomers, only DHB significantly enhanced IL-2 secretion when OT-I splenocytes were incubated with the SIINFEKL peptide and DHB isomers (Fig. 1j), suggesting the unique molecular activity of DHB. Additionally, OT-I CD8+ T cells incubated with DHB and anti-CD3/anti-CD28 antibodies expanded CD44+CD62L+ Tcm cells (Extended Data Fig. 1e), indicating that DHB-mediated Tcm expansion was antigen independent. Moreover, DHB induced the differentiation of CD44+CD62L+CD8+ Tcm cells in a dose-dependent manner (Extended Data Fig. 1f).

DHB reprograms T cell metabolism and promotes stemness

T cell activation and differentiation are largely controlled by their immunometabolic adaptation19. Hence, we performed targeted metabolomics analysis to delineate the potential mechanism of DHB-induced stem-like T cell differentiation. The DHB treatment of OT-I CD8+ T cells decreased many intermediate metabolites within the canonical metabolic pathways, including glycolysis, glutaminolysis and citric acid cycle, compared with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) control (Fig. 2a and Extended Data Fig. 2a). This metabolically quiescent status corresponds to the distinct metabolic profile in stem-like memory CD8+ T cells20. Specifically, DHB substantially reduced the levels of glucose-6-P, fructose 6-P, P-enolpyruvate and pyruvate in the glycolytic pathway (Extended Data Fig. 2b). Moreover, Seahorse assay for bioenergetics performed on CD8+ T cells isolated from lymph nodes of CT26-tumour-bearing mice treated with DHB showed that DHB treatment decreased the extracellular acidification rate (ECAR) of CD8+ T cells (including basal ECAR, glycolytic capacity and glycolytic reserve) in vivo (Fig. 2b and Extended Data Fig. 2c), indicating the suppression of glycolysis. In addition, in vitro DHB treatment of OT-I CD8+ T cells decreased ECAR as well as the oxygen consumption rate (reflecting oxidative phosphorylation21; Extended Data Fig. 2d). Taken together, these results suggested that DHB suppresses glycolysis in CD8+ T cells and skews CD8+ T cells into a metabolically quiescent status.

Next, we examined the impact of metabolic changes on DHB-induced stem-like T cell differentiation using a series of metabolic pathway inhibitors (Fig. 2c). We tested the effects of bis-2-(5-phenylacetamido-1,3,4-thiadiazol-2-yl)ethylsulfide (BPTES), which depletes glutamate for the TCA cycle22; UK5099, a mitochondrial pyruvate carrier (MPC) inhibitor23; etomoxir44, a fatty acid oxidation inhibitor24; 2-deoxy-ᴅ-glucose (2-DG)24, a glycolysis inhibitor; and oligomycin, known to promote glycolysis by inhibiting ATP synthase and oxidative phosphorylation25 (Extended Data Fig. 2e). Treatment with BPTES, UK5099 or etomoxir did not alter DHB-induced Tcm differentiation (Fig. 2c). By contrast, 2-DG increased the frequency of Tcm in the PBS group to a comparable level as the DHB treatment (Fig. 2c), highlighting the critical role of glycolysis inhibition in CD8+ Tcm differentiation. In particular, oligomycin markedly decreased the level of CD8+ Tcm in the PBS control, but not in the DHB group (Fig. 2c). Consistently, GSK2837808A, a lactate dehydrogenase inhibitor26 that blocks the conversion of pyruvate to lactate, promoted Tcm generation to a comparable level to that observed in DHB-treated T cells (Extended Data Fig. 3a).

To delineate the underlying mechanism of the DHB-mediated suppression of glycolysis and Tcm differentiation, we focused on reduced glutathione (GSH). This was based on the dramatic decrease (~97%) in the intracellular GSH level in DHB-treated T cells, compared with PBS-treated T cells (Fig. 2a). To examine how DHB treatment decreased the GSH levels, we performed biomolecular mass spectrometry with triple-quadrupole (QqQ) tandem mass spectrometry (MS/MS) on GSH incubated with free DHB or DHB-quinone oxidized by nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate (NADPH). The analysis revealed rapid DHB-GSH adduct formation via Michael addition reaction between the thiol group of GSH and DHB (Fig. 2d and Extended Data Fig. 4a,b). Interestingly, among its various isomers, only DHB formed a stable adduct showing the highest signal intensity, as the ortho-catechol position favours the formation of stable quinone intermediates (Extended Data Fig. 4c,d). In vitro GSH trapping assay further confirmed the reduced level of intracellular GSH in DHB-treated OT-I CD8+ T cells (Fig. 2e). GSH is a major determinant of intracellular redox potential that regulates T cell immunity via the Akt/mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR) pathway27. Partial loss of intracellular GSH by buthionine sulfoximine (BSO; known as a glutamate cysteine ligase inhibitor28) increased the Tcm to a similar level observed in DHB-treated T cells (Extended Data Fig. 3b). Immunoblot analysis revealed that DHB treatment decreased the phosphorylation of Akt (Ser473) and mTOR (Ser2448) in OT-I CD8+ T cells (Fig. 2f and Supplementary Fig. 4), indicating the DHB-mediated downregulation of the Akt-mTORC1 pathway. In line with these findings, treatment with the mTOR inhibitor rapamycin induced CD8+ Tcm cells to a level comparable to that observed in DHB-treated cells (Extended Data Fig. 3a)27. Moreover, DHB substantially decreased the expression of Myc, a crucial transcription factor that activates glycolysis and glutaminolysis28 (Fig. 2f and Supplementary Fig. 4). These results showed that DHB decreases intracellular GSH by forming an adduct with GSH and reprograms T cell metabolism to switch off glycolysis via the Akt-mTORC1-Myc pathway.

We performed RNA-sequencing (RNA-seq) analyses to examine DHB-mediated transcriptional control of activated OT-I CD8+ T cells. Bulk RNA-seq analysis showed that DHB markedly increased the expression levels of genes associated with progenitor-like signature, including Ly6a, Ccr7, Sell and Tcf7 (Fig. 2g). In addition, single-cell RNA-sequencing (scRNA-seq) analysis of DHB-treated OT-I CD8+ T cells revealed 13 distinct clusters (Fig. 2h). Among those clusters, DHB-induced stem-like T cells were identified in cluster 3 with a high expression of Tbx21, Tcf7, Tox, Mki67, Ikzf2 and bcl6, and cluster 6 with a high expression of Tcf7, Sell, Tox, Ly6c2, bcl6 and Pecam1 (Fig. 2h,i and Extended Data Fig. 5a). In cluster 6, we also noted a small but pronounced increase in the expression levels of Tox, which suggests that DHB-induced stem-like CD8+ T cells shared molecular features with progenitor exhausted T cells.

Using the same cells analysed by scRNA-seq described above, we additionally performed paired single-cell assay for transposase-accessible chromatin with high-throughput sequencing (scATAC-seq) to assess the impact of DHB on chromatin accessibility at single-cell resolution (Extended Data Fig. 5b,c). DHB-treated CD8+ T cells showed increased accessibility at the target gene loci of Tcf7, Sell and Ccr7 compared with the PBS control, consistent with the transcriptomic data (Fig. 2j). In particular, Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) performed on cluster 3 plus cluster 6 showed a substantial downregulation of glycolysis (normalized enrichment score (NES), −1.49) as well as hypoxia (NES, −1.60) pathways (Fig. 2k–m and Extended Data Fig. 5d,e). As hypoxia contributes to increased glycolysis and T cell exhaustion and hinders the effectiveness of immunotherapy29, these results suggest the potential role of DHB in promoting T cell stemness and preventing T cell exhaustion. Meanwhile, DHB-induced stem-like T cells in cluster 3 plus cluster 6 exhibited decreased chromatin accessibility for Pdcd1 and Icos, known to drive T cell exhaustion30,31 as well as increased chromatin accessibility for Sell and Nr4a1, a recently reported transcription factor that promotes the stemness of T cells and progenitor exhausted T cells-related genes32 (Fig. 2n and Extended Data Fig. 5f). Furthermore, DHB-induced stem-like T cells consistently showed an increase in chromatin openness in enhancer regions of Tcf7, Ccr7 and Sell (Extended Data Fig. 5g). These data show that DHB induces a unique epigenetic landscape that supports progenitor-like gene expression programs.

Oral DHB Prodrug 201 augments the efficacy of α-PD-1 therapy

On the basis of the strong potential of DHB to induce stem-like CD8+ T cells in vitro (Figs. 1 and 2) and accumulating evidence demonstrating stem-like CD8+ T cells as the crucial determinant for successful ICB immunotherapy33, we set out to examine whether DHB can improve the antitumour efficacy of systemic α-PD-1 IgG and how to further improve its therapeutic potential. In an ICB-resistant spontaneous CDX2-Cre NLS-APCfl/fl colorectal cancer model34, the oral administration of DHB combined with α-PD-1 IgG therapy markedly reduced the burden of colon adenocarcinoma (Fig. 3a). On the other hand, in CT26 subcutaneous (s.c.) tumour model, oral DHB administration did not improve the antitumour efficacy of α-PD-1 IgG (Fig. 3b), potentially due to the low oral bioavailability of DHB. By contrast, increasing the local concentration of DHB by direct intratumoural administration into CT26 s.c. tumour resulted in similar antitumour efficacy as systemic α-PD-1 monotherapy (Fig. 3b). The combination of intratumoural DHB administration and systemic α-PD-1 IgG significantly delayed the growth of CT26 s.c. tumour (Fig. 3b). Taken together, these results suggested that DHB, when administered appropriately, could achieve strong synergy with α-PD-1 ICB.

Encouraged by these results, we sought to optimize the oral delivery of DHB for systemic cancer immunotherapy. DHB is a hydrophilic small molecule that can be cleared rapidly in vivo. Prodrug is a well-established strategy to modify the parent drug for increased hydrophobicity, protection of functional groups and improved PK profiles. We synthesized seven variants of DHB prodrugs and found that Prodrugs 863, 142 and 201 were well encapsulated with high stability in an oral nano-emulsion formulated with oleic acid/Tween 80 (ref. 35) (Extended Data Fig. 6). Oral administration of these prodrug nano-emulsion formulations improved the therapeutic efficacy of α-PD-1 IgG in CT26 s.c. tumour-bearing mice (Fig. 3c). In particular, oral prodrug 201 formulation (Prodrug 201) combined with α-PD-1 therapy exerted the strongest antitumour efficacy and significantly increased the frequency of CD8+ T cells specific to AH1, an immunodominant epitope from CT26 gp70 (H-2Ld-restricted SPSYVYHQF), among the peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs; Fig. 3c,d). On the basis of these results, we focused on Prodrug 201 in the subsequent studies.

Prodrug structures and formulation preparation.

Prodrug 201 nano-emulsion exhibited a homogeneous spherical morphology with a hydrodynamic size of ~160 nm with a polydispersity index of 0.19 (Fig. 3e). We examined the PK profiles of Prodrug 201 administered orally. First, liquid chromatography MS/MS analysis of Prodrug 201 exposed to rat plasma showed that ethyl-DHB (M4) and DHB were the major metabolized products of Prodrug 201 (Extended Data Fig. 7a,b), supporting our prodrug-based strategy. PK studies in rats demonstrated that the half-life of DHB in the plasma after oral gavage is only 0.4 h with a plasma concentration (Cmax) of 34.7 ng ml−1. In sharp contrast, the oral administration of Prodrug 201 markedly extended the plasma half-life to 4.1 h, increased the Cmax to 182 ng ml−1, improved the area under the curve by 14.3-fold, and improved the oral bioavailability (%F) by 9.72-fold, compared with free DHB (Fig. 3f and Extended Data Fig. 7c). This improved oral absorption and PK profile of Prodrug 201 led to greater antitumour efficacy in various murine tumour models. Compared with free DHB plus α-PD-1 therapy, oral Prodrug 201 plus α-PD-1 combo-therapy exerted significantly enhanced antitumour efficacy in mice with established α-PD-1-resistant NOOC1 squamous cell carcinoma (Fig. 3g and Extended Data Fig. 7d) as well as in mice bearing B16F10 melanoma (Fig. 3h), demonstrating the superiority of Prodrug 201 over the parent drug, DHB. Besides, in both spontaneous CDX2-Cre NLS-APCfl/fl colorectal cancer model and mammary-specific polyomavirus middle T antigen overexpression mouse model (MMTV-PyMT), oral Prodrug 201 boosted the therapeutic efficacy of α-PD-1 antibody, resulting in markedly reduced tumour burden in the colon or abdomen/axilla (Fig. 3i and Extended Data Fig. 8). In particular, the MMTV-PyMT model closely recapitulates human breast cancer progression36, including metastasis, thereby underscoring the translational relevance of the Prodrug 201 combination therapy.

Prodrug 201 promotes antigen-specific stem-like CD8+ T cells

On the basis of the strong antitumour efficacy of oral Prodrug 201 combined with α-PD-1, we further investigated its impact on antitumour immune responses. In CT26-tumour-bearing mice, oral Prodrug 201 plus α-PD-1 therapy effectively suppressed tumour growth and prolonged the animal survival (Fig. 4a–c). Analyses of splenocytes from CT26-tumour-bearing mice on day 25 and tumour-free survivors on day 65 showed that the stemness features persisted in antigen-specific, AH1-tetramer+CD8+ T cells with higher frequencies of CD44+CD62L+AH1-tetramer+CD8+ T cells and Sca-1+PD-1−AH1-tetramer+CD8+ T cells in tumour-free survivors on day 65 (Extended Data Fig. 9). Moreover, when tumour-free survivors in the oral Prodrug 201 plus α-PD-1 group were rechallenged with CT26 tumour cells on the contralateral flank on day 90, they were completely resistant to tumour growth (Fig. 4d). This indicated that oral Prodrug 201 plus α-PD-1 therapy induced a long-term protective antitumour response. In particular, this combination therapy induced negligible changes in the body weight or complete blood count in CT26-tumour-bearing mice (Supplementary Figs. 5 and 6), indicating good biosafety profile of the oral Prodrug 201 plus α-PD-1 therapy.

Next, we examined which immune cell type was responsible for the antitumour efficacy of the combination therapy. Depletion of natural killer (NK) cells or CD4+ regulatory T cells (Tregs) with α-Asialo GM1 antibody or α-CD25 antibody, respectively, had only minor effect on the antitumour effect of the Prodrug 201 plus α-PD-1 therapy (Fig. 4e). By contrast, the administration of α-CD8 antibody abrogated the antitumour efficacy of the combination therapy (Fig. 4e), suggesting CD8+ T cells as the key antitumour effector cells. Indeed, oral Prodrug 201 plus α-PD-1 combo-therapy significantly increased the frequencies of AH1 antigen-specific CD8+ T cells, CD44+CD62L+CD8+ Tcm cells and CD44+CD62L−CD8+ Tem cells in PBMCs (Fig. 4f–h), compared with α-PD-1 monotherapy. The combination therapy also promoted IFN-γ response from CD8+ T cells on in vitro stimulation with AH1 epitope (Fig. 4i) and increased AH1-specific CD44+CD62L+CD8+ Tcm cells, and CD44+CD62L−CD8+ Tem cells (Fig. 4j,k).

Moreover, in CT26-tumour-bearing mice, oral Prodrug 201 plus α-PD-1 combo-therapy markedly increased the intratumoural levels of IFN-γ, interferon-inducible chemokine (C-X-C motif) ligand 10 (CXCL10) and granzyme B, compared with PBS treatment (Fig. 5a,b and Extended Data Fig. 10a). The combination therapy also drove the substantial infiltration of CD45+ leucocytes, CD3+ T cells and CD8+ T cells into the tumour microenvironment (TME), whereas increasing the ratio of M1-like macrophages to M2-like macrophages and decreasing immune suppressive Tregs (Fig. 5c–f and Extended Data Fig. 10b–e). Prodrug 201 plus α-PD-1 also elevated the frequencies of CD3+ and CD8+ T cells in tumour-draining lymph nodes (tdLNs), compared with α-PD-1 monotherapy (Extended Data Fig. 10f,g).

Immunological profiling of the TME showed that Prodrug 201 plus α-PD-1 substantially increased the frequencies of CD44+CD62L+CD8+ T cells and AH1-specific CD8+ T cells (Fig. 5g,h and Extended Data Fig. 10h). Meanwhile, the combination therapy significantly expanded the intratumoural frequencies of AH1-specific CD8+CD44+CD62L+ T cells, AH1-specific Tcf1+CD8+ T cells, AH1-specific Foxo1+CD8+ T cells, AH1-specific Sca-1+CD8+ T cells and AH1-specific Sca-1+PD-1−CD8+ T cells, compared with PBS treatment (Fig. 5i–m). Similar results were also observed in NOOC1 and B16F10 tumour models, where tumour-infiltrating CD8+ T cells exhibited high levels of CD62L and Tcf1 expressions following the Prodrug 201 plus α-PD-1 combo-therapy (Extended Data Fig. 10i–k).

We also examined the impact of the combo-therapy on tdLNs (Fig. 5n–p). Prodrug 201 plus α-PD-1 increased the CD44−CD62L+ and CD44+CD62L+ subsets among AH1-specific CD8+ T cells in tdLNs, compared with PBS or α-PD-1 groups (Fig. 5n). In addition, the combination therapy increased the stem-like biomarkers in AH1-specific CD8+ T cells in tdLNs, as evidenced by the increased frequency of Sca-1+PD-1−CD8+ T cells and decreased frequency of Sca-1−PD-1+ CD8+ T cells, compared with the PBS group (Fig. 5o and Extended Data Fig. 10l). Specifically, among these AH1-tetramer+Sca-1+PD-1−CD8+ T cells, the frequencies of CD44+CD62L+CD8+ T cells and CD44−CD62L+CD8+ T cells were significantly higher in the combo-therapy group, compared with α-PD-1 group (Fig. 5p and Extended Data Fig. 10l). In particular, CD44−CD62L+Sca-1+PD-1−CD8+ T cells are stem-like CD8+ T cells characterized by their longevity and stemness15,16. Collectively, our data showed that Prodrug 201 is a potent oral immunomodulator that expands antigen-specific stem-like CD8+ T cells in the systemic compartment as well as in the TME, thereby contributing to the antitumour efficacy of α-PD-1 ICB therapy.

DHB improves adoptive T cell immunotherapy

Lastly, as another application of DHB-based expansion of stem-like CD8+ T cells, we evaluated whether DHB treatment can improve the therapeutic efficacy of adoptive T cell therapy (ACT). Recent studies suggest that Foxo1 plays a critical role in promoting T cell longevity through stem-like programming, underscoring its importance in T cell therapy8,37. DHB treatment of OT-I CD8+ T cells ex vivo significantly increased the frequency of CD127+PD-1−CD8+ T cells (Fig. 6a), a subset of T cells reported as long-lived memory T cells38. Within this subset of T cells, DHB significantly increased the expression of Foxo1 and Tcf1 (Fig. 6b,c) and concomitantly upregulated Ki67, a proliferation marker (Fig. 6d). We tested if OT-I CD8+ T cells expanded ex vivo in the presence of DHB could exert strong antitumour efficacy in B16F10-OVA-bearing mice. Adoptive transfer of DHB-trained OT-I CD8+ T cells significantly delayed B16F10-OVA tumour growth and induced complete tumour regression in two out of ten mice, whereas the adoptive transfer of control OT-I CD8+ T cells failed to slow the tumour growth (Fig. 6e). The analysis of T cells in the TME on day 21 revealed that ACT with DHB-trained OT-I CD8+ T cells significantly increased intratumoural infiltration of transferred (Thy1.1+) CD8+ T cells and their Ki67 expression (Fig. 6f,g). Besides, the adoptive transfer of DHB-trained OT-I CD8+ T cells also inhibited NOOC1-OVA tumour growth, with increased frequencies of Tcf1+CD8+ T cells, and CD44+CD62L+CD8+ T cells among antigen-specific CD8+ T cells in PBMCs (Supplementary Fig. 7).

On the basis of these results, we evaluated whether DHB-based Prodrug 201 could improve ACT in situ. Indeed, in the B16F10-OVA tumour model, the oral administration of Prodrug 201 increased the antitumour efficacy of adoptive OT-I CD8+ T therapy (Supplementary Fig. 8a,b). Smart-sequencing-based bulk RNA-seq analysis of tumour-infiltrating OVA-specific OT-I CD8+ T cells further revealed that genes associated with glycolysis, inflammatory signalling, oxidative stress and dysfunctional T cell states were downregulated, including Acacb39, Slc16a5 (ref. 40), Ifnab41, Nos1 (ref. 42), Pla2g5 (ref. 43) and multiple Klra family members44 (Supplementary Fig. 8c,d). Taken together, DHB or Prodrug 201 treatment endows CD8+ T cells with a persistent stem-like phenotype and enhances their tumour infiltration, thereby improving their antitumour efficacy.

Conclusions

In summary, we have synthesized a DHB-derived prodrug nano-emulsion and shown that oral prodrug therapy induced antigen-specific stem-like T cell responses in vivo and amplified the therapeutic efficacy of α-PD-1 immunotherapy in multiple ICB-refractory murine tumour models. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first demonstration of a microbial-metabolite-based oral drug designed to improve stem-like T cell immunity. Our oral prodrug formulation with a well-defined composition and chemical structure is well positioned for large-scale manufacturing, quality control and patient compliance, highlighting its strong translational potential. More broadly, since microbial metabolites play critical roles in host-immune interactions and are associated with various immune-related diseases, this study provides a general pathway for harnessing microbial metabolites in innovative drug discovery and development.

Methods

In vitro differentiation and survival of OT-I T cells with various metabolites

Splenocytes from OT-I mice (6–8 weeks old) were obtained aseptically. The cells were cultured in 96-well plates (1 × 105 cells per well). For the CD8+ Tcm differentiation study, cells were activated with SIINFEKL peptide (4 × 10−9 M) in the presence of various metabolites (100 µM of N-acetylneuraminate, disodium sebacate, DHB, pantothenate, GABA, glycerol, 5-aminoimidazole-4-carboxamide ribonucleotide or ferulate) for 3 days. Then, the cells were washed several times with a T cell medium and further cultured with IL-2 (10 ng ml−1, PeproTech) in the presence of metabolites for 5 days. The suspension cells were collected, blocked with CD16/32 antibody, and stained with the fixable viability dye eFluor 450, FITC-CD44 rat anti-human/mouse (clone IM7, eBioscience), PE-CD62L monoclonal antibody (clone MEL-14, BioLegend) and APC-CD8 rat anti-mouse (clone 53-6.7, BD Biosciences). For CD8+ T cell survival study, cells were activated with an antigen peptide as above, followed by thorough washing, and incubation in the presence of IL-2 for 2 days. Metabolites were added during this incubation (100 µM of N-acetylneuraminate, disodium sebacate, DHB, pantothenate, GABA, glycerol or 5-aminoimidazole-4-carboxamide ribonucleotide). Then, the cells were cultured in the absence of both IL-2 and metabolites for 36 h. CD44+CD62L+ and live T cells (stained with Annexin V-APC and PI) were detected via flow cytometry. For the Tcf1+CD8+ differentiation study, cells were activated with antigen peptide as above, followed by thorough washing, and cultured with metabolites in the presence of IL-2 for 3 days. Cells were stained with the PE-Tcf1/Tcf7 rabbit monoclonal antibody (clone C63D9, Cell Signaling Technology). For the isolated CD8+ T cell differentiation study, CD8+ T cells isolated from the spleen of OT-I mice were activated with antigen peptide as above, washed with the T cell medium, and further cultured with DHB in the presence of IL-2 for 5 days. For isolated CD8+ T cell co-cultured with BSO, rapamycin or GSK2837808A, CD8+ T cells were isolated from splenocytes in C57BL/6 mice with CD8+ T cell isolation kit, cultured in 96-well plates (1 × 105 cells per well), and activated with anti-CD3 and anti-CD28 (1 µg ml−1) antibodies in the presence DHB (100 µM), BSO (25 µM), rapamycin (10 nM) or GSK2837808A (15 µM) for 3 days. Then, the cells were washed twice and further cultured with IL-2 (10 ng ml−1) in the presence of the same drug mentioned above for 4 days. Cells were stained with BV570-anti-mouse CD8a antibody (BioLegend, clone 53-6.7), PerCP-Cy5.5-anti-mouse CD127 antibody (Fisher Scientific, clone A7R34), FITC-anti-mouse CD279 (PD-1) antibody (eBioscience, clone RMP1 30), PE-anti-mouse/human/rat Foxo1 antibody (Cell Signaling, clone C29H4), APC-anti-mouse/human Tcf1/Tcf7 antibody (Cell Signaling, clone C63D9), AF700-anti-mouse Ki67 antibody (BioLegend, clone 16A8) and live/dead fixable near-IR (Invitrogen, number L10119). For the anti-CD3/CD28 antibodies activation setting, CD8+ T cells were isolated and seeded in a hamster IgG precoated plate at a density of 105 cells per well. DHB, anti-CD3/CD28 antibodies (1 µg ml−1) and IL-2 (100 U ml−1) were added to activate the T cells. After 48 h, cells were washed for two times, and the cells were further incubated with DHB and IL-2 (10 ng ml−1) for 5 days. Tcm cells were quantified by flow cytometry.

In vivo cancer immunotherapy of prodrug formulation plus α-PD-1

For the formulation screening study in vivo, s.c. CT26 tumour model was established as mentioned above. The formulation containing either Prodrug 201, Prodrug 863 or Prodrug 142 (molar equivalent to 0.11 mg of DHB per dose) was orally administered every other day starting on day 7. α-PD-1 antibody (100 µg per dose) was injected intraperitoneally (i.p.) on days 9, 12, 15 and 19. The PBMCs were obtained on days 17 and 22 and stained with PE-tagged peptide-MHC-I tetramer (H-2Ld-restricted SPSYVYHQF). In the B16F10 tumour model, 3 × 105 B16F10 cells were inoculated in C57BL/6 mice (5–6 weeks old), Prodrug 201 or free DHB was orally administered every other day starting on day 6. α-PD-1 antibody (100 µg per dose) was injected i.p. on days 7, 9, 11 and 14. In the NOOC1 tumour model, 1.5 × 105 NOOC1 cells in the matrix gel were inoculated in C57BL/6 mice, Prodrug 201 or free DHB was orally administered every other day starting on day 8. α-PD-1 antibody (100 µg per dose) was injected i.p. on days 14, 20 and 26. For antitumour efficacy comparison between Prodrug 201 and Prodrug 201 plus α-PD-1, Prodrug 201 was orally administered every other day, starting on day 7. α-PD-1 antibody (100 µg per dose) was injected i.p. on days 10, 14, 18 and 22. The PBMCs were collected on days 14 and 20 for further analysis. The body weight, tumour length and width, and animal survival were recorded. For the CT26 tumour rechallenge study, the surviving animals after Prodrug 201 plus α-PD-1 treatment were inoculated with 1.5 × 105 CT26 tumour cells on the contralateral flank by s.c. injection on day 90, and the tumour growth was monitored for additional 60 days.

Statistical analysis

The results are expressed as the mean ± s.e.m. A one-way or two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by Bonferroni’s multiple comparisons test was used for testing differences among groups. Data were approximately normally distributed, and variance was similar between the groups. The experiments were repeated multiple times as independent experiments, as indicated in the figure captions. A complete dataset from one representative, independent experiment is shown in the figures. No samples were excluded from the analyses. Statistical significance is indicated as *P < 0.05. GraphPad Prism 10 was used for the statistical analyses.
