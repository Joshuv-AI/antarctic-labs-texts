Taking Model-Complete Cores
Manuel Bodirsky, Bertalan Bodor, Paolo Marimon

Abstract
. A first-order theory T T is a model-complete core theory if every first-order formula is equivalent modulo T T to an existential positive formula; the core companion of a theory T T is a model-complete core theory S S such that every model of T T maps homomorphically to a model of S S and vice-versa. Whilst core companions may not exist in general, they always exist for ω \omega -categorical theories. We show that many model-theoretic properties, such as stability, NIP, simplicity, and NSOP, are preserved by moving to the core companion of a theory. On the other hand, we show that the classes of theories of structures interpretable over ( ℕ , = ) ({\mathbb{N}};=) and over ( ℚ , < ) ({\mathbb{Q}};<) are both not closed under taking core companions. The first class is contained in the class of theories of ω \omega -stable first-order reducts of finitely homogeneous relational structures, which was studied by Lachlan in the 80’s. We conjecture the two classes to be equal.



1.
Introduction

A theory
T
has quantifier elimination if every first-order formula is equivalent to a quantifier-free formula modulo
T
. Model-completeness is a natural weakening of quantifier elimination: instead of requiring that every formula is equivalent to a quantifier-free formula, we ask
that they are equivalent to existential formulas.
Some theories
T
might not be model-complete, but might have a
companion
which is model-complete:
S
is called a
companion
of
T
if
S
and
T

have
the same universal consequences
(equivalently, every model of
T
embeds in a model of
S
and vice-versa).
A
model companion of
T
is a companion
S
of
T
which is model complete.
If
T
has a model companion
S
, then
S
is unique up to equivalence of theories (see, e.g.,
[
42
]
).

There is also a positive version of the concept of a model companion, inspired by the theory of graph homomorphisms:
we say that a theory
T
is a
core theory
if every existential formula is equivalent to an existential positive formula
T
. A theory
S
is the
core companion
of
T
if
S
and
T
have the same universal negative consequences (equivalently, every model of
T
maps homomorphically to a model of
S
and vice-versa), and
S
is a model-complete core theory.
Again, if
T
has a core companion
S
, then
S
is unique up to equivalence of theories
[
11
]
.

In this article,
we are often especially interested in model companions and core companions of
\omega
-categorical theories. A theory is
\omega
-categorical
if it has a unique countable model up to isomorphism, and a structure is called
\omega
-categorical if its first-order theory is. Examples of structures with
\omega
-categorical theories are all finite structures,
(\mathbb{N};=)
,
(\mathbb{Q};{<})
, the Rado graph, and all infinite vector spaces over finite fields. By Saracino’s theorem, every
\omega
-categorical theory has a model companion
S
, and
S
is
\omega
-categorical
[
58
]
.
If
\mathfrak{A}
is the
countable model of an
\omega
-categorical theory
T
,
then
T
is model-complete if and only if
the automorphism group
\operatorname{Aut}(\mathfrak{A})
is dense in the monoid of elementary self-embeddings of
\mathfrak{A}
with respect to the topology of pointwise convergence (a consequence of the theorem of Engeler, Svenonius, and Ryll-Nardzewski; see
[
42
,
12
]
). Every
\omega
-categorical theory also has a model-complete core
companion
[
13
,
11
]
. If
\mathfrak{A}
is the countable model of an
\omega
-categorical theory
T
, then
T
is a model-complete core theory if and only if
\operatorname{Aut}(\mathfrak{A})
is dense in the endomorphism monoid
\operatorname{End}(\mathfrak{A})
. Moreover, two
\omega
-categorical theories have the same universal negative consequences if and only if their countable models
\mathfrak{A}
and
\mathfrak{B}
are
homomorphically equivalent
, i.e.,
there exists a homomorphism from
\mathfrak{A}
to
\mathfrak{B}
and vice versa.
In particular, every finite structure
\mathfrak{B}
is homomorphically equivalent to a finite structure
\mathfrak{C}
all of whose endomorphisms are automorphisms, and which is called the
core
of
\mathfrak{B}
in the theory of graph homomorphisms, database theory, and constraint satisfaction (see, e.g.,
[
41
]
).

Typically, the model-companion and core companion of
T
are
simpler
than
T
. The simplification for the model companion is usually modest.
For example, the first-order theory of the countable dense linear order with start point but no endpoints, i.e., the first-order theory of
({\mathbb{Q}}_{\geq 0};<)
, is not model-complete, and its model companion is the first-order theory of
({\mathbb{Q}};<)
; it is simpler in the sense that for each
n\geq 1
, it has strictly fewer types over the empty set. For the core companion the model-theoretic simplification can be more radical. For example, the first-order theory
T_{R}
of the Rado graph
R
is model-complete (it even has quantifier elimination), but not a core theory, since
R
contains infinite cliques
K_{\omega}
, and endomorphisms
e\colon R\to K_{\omega}
which are not embeddings.
The core companion of
T_{R}
is the theory of
K_{\omega}
, which is
\omega
-stable (Definition
3.26
), while
T_{R}
even has the independence property (IP) (Definition
3.10
).
In the following, we will say that a structure is model-complete (or a model-complete core) if its theory is model-complete (or has a model-complete core theory).
A structure
\mathfrak{C}
is a
companion
(
model companion
,

core companion
) of a structure
\mathfrak{B}
if the first-order theory of
\mathfrak{C}
is a companion (model companion,
core companion) of the first-order theory of
\mathfrak{B}
.

In some applications of model theory, we are only interested in the existential or existential positive theory of a given structure
\mathfrak{B}
. For instance,
if we want to study the decidability the existential theory of
\mathfrak{B}
(which is for instance undecidable for
({\mathbb{N}};+,*,1)

[
53
]
and not known to be decidable for
\mathfrak{B}=({\mathbb{Q}};+,*,1)

[
1
]
), then obviously any companion of

\mathfrak{B}
has the same existential theory. Another example
is the study of the computational complexity of
\operatorname{CSP}(\mathfrak{B})
, the
constraint satisfaction problem
of
\mathfrak{B}
, which can be seen as the problem of deciding the primitive positive theory of
\mathfrak{B}
. If two structures satisfy the same universal negative sentences, or if they are even homomorphically equivalent, then they have the same CSP.
In these applications,
the fact that the model companion or core companion of
T
is structurally simpler than
T
is the reason why these concepts are so useful:
for instance, instead of studying the complexity of
\operatorname{CSP}(\mathfrak{B})
, it might be simpler to study the complexity of the
\operatorname{CSP}
of the core companion of
\mathfrak{B}
.

If we want to apply this method systematically to study
\operatorname{CSP}(\mathfrak{B})
for structures
\mathfrak{B}
from some class of structures
\mathcal{C}
, we ideally need
that
{\mathcal{C}}
satisfies two conditions:

•

every structure in
{\mathcal{C}}
has a core companion, and

•

the core companion of
{\mathcal{C}}
is again in the class
{\mathcal{C}}
.

If these two conditions are satisfied, then we have reduced our task to the model-complete cores in
{\mathcal{C}}
, which is often simpler.
It should be mentioned that the task to determine the model-complete core can come with considerable combinatorial problems: already if
\mathfrak{B}
is a finite structure, the question whether
\mathfrak{B}
is a core is coNP-complete
[
40
]
. Also the following question remains open.

Question 1.1

(Question (5) in
[
15
]
)
.

Does there exist a finitely homogenizable structure (for the definition, see Section
2
) whose model-complete core is not finitely homogenizable?

Contributions

In this article, we show that many classes of interest in model theory, such as
\omega
-stability, stability,
\mathrm{NIP},\mathrm{NSOP}
,
and simplicity (in the model theoretic sense) are closed under taking model-complete cores; we develop general methods for proving this.

However, some other properties, such as total categoricity, are not closed under taking model-complete cores.
One of the main results of the present paper is the resolution of an open question from
[
9
]
(Section 10):
we show that the class of structures that are (first-order) interpretable over a countably infinite structure with the empty signature (short:
interpretable over equality
) is
not
closed under taking model-complete cores. This is surprising, because the class is tame from a model-theoretic perspective, and at first sight appears to be quite robust.
It is the central class of structures studied in an active area of theoretical computer science which studies automata theory with atoms and orbit-finite computation (
[
22
,
48
,
23
,
24
,
21
,
38
]
).

Interestingly, the model-complete core of our counterexample has an interpretation in
({\mathbb{Q}};<)
.
In fact, every structure interpretable over equality is
\omega
-stable,
and hence its model-complete core is
\omega
-stable as well.
Moreover, they are reducts of homogeneous structures with finite relational signature, and all
\omega
-stable reducts of homogeneous structures with finite relational signature are interpretable over
({\mathbb{Q}};<)
(Theorem
5.2
).
We conjecture that the class of all
\omega
-stable structures that are interdefinable with a homogeneous structure in a finite relational signature (and which we call
Lachlan’s class
, see Section
5
) equals precisely the class of structures that are interdefinable with the model-complete core of a structure interpretable over equality. Structures interpretable over
({\mathbb{Q}};<)
are frequently studied in the area of computation with atoms that we have already mentioned above.

Our final result shows that the class of structures interpretable over
({\mathbb{Q}};<)
is not closed under taking model-complete cores as well. A concrete counterexample is the so-called
generic permutation
, which is the model-complete core
of a structure with a two-dimensional interpretation over
({\mathbb{Q}};<)
, but which is itself not interpretable over
({\mathbb{Q}};<)
.
The generic permutation plays an important role in the recent systematic study of NIP structures that are homogeneous in a binary signature
[
67
,
67
,
56
]
.

The typical strategy to prove that a structure
\mathfrak{A}
is not interpretable in a structure
\mathfrak{B}
is by exhibiting a model-theoretic property which is preserved by interpretations, and to prove that this property holds in
\mathfrak{B}
, but not in
\mathfrak{A}
. Alternative strategies in the
\omega
-categorical context (see, for example,
[
71
, Theorem A.21]
) rely on type counting arguments, with
\mathfrak{B}
having too fast growth-rate in its number of types to be interpretable in
\mathfrak{A}
. Neither of these strategies can work to prove that a structure
\mathfrak{A}
does not interpret its model-complete core
\mathfrak{B}
: essentially all model theoretic properties which are preserved under interpretations that hold for
\mathfrak{A}
will hold for
\mathfrak{B}
(Section
3
), and
\mathfrak{B}
only has a slower growth-rate in its number of types than
\mathfrak{A}
. Indeed, proving non-interpretability for structures which are very similar from a model theoretic perspective is a major challenge. To overcome it, we develop a toolkit of techniques and ideas of a group-theoretic nature to prove non-interpretability. We believe this to be another major contribution of this paper.

The closure of the class of structures with a first-order interpretation over
({\mathbb{Q}};<)
under taking model-complete cores and reducts is quite interesting:
it is quite robust (e.g., closed under interpretations) and contained in the class of first-order reducts of NIP homogeneous structures with a finite relational signature of maximal arity two. We do not know an example that would separate these two classes.

2.
Preliminaries

We write
[n]
for the set
\{1,\dots,n\}
. We refer the reader to
[
42
]
for
an introduction to first-order logic (which for us always includes equality) and
proofs of the various model-theoretic facts mentioned below.

All the structures in this article are assumed to be
relational
, i.e., they have a signature
\tau
which consists of relation symbols only.
If
R\in\tau
is a relation symbol of arity
k
, and
\mathfrak{A}
is a
\tau
-structure, we write
R^{\mathfrak{A}}\subseteq A^{k}
for the respective relation of
\mathfrak{A}
, and we write
\operatorname{Dom}(\mathfrak{A})
for the domain of
\mathfrak{A}
(usually,
\operatorname{Dom}(\mathfrak{A})
will also be denoted by
A
).

A structure
\mathfrak{B}
obtained from
\mathfrak{A}
by dropping some of the relations is called a
reduct
of
\mathfrak{A}
, and conversely
\mathfrak{B}
is called an
expansion
of
\mathfrak{A}
.
An expansion
\mathfrak{B}
of
\mathfrak{A}
is called a
first-order expansion
if all relations of
\mathfrak{B}
are first-order definable in
\mathfrak{A}
. We say that a structure
\mathfrak{B}
is a
first-order reduct
of a structure
\mathfrak{A}
if
it is a reduct of a first-order expansion of
\mathfrak{A}
. Two structures are called
interdefinable
if they are first-order reducts of each other.
If
\mathfrak{A}
is a structure and
B\subseteq A
, then we write
\mathfrak{A}|_{B}
for the structure with domain
B
and all the relations of the form
R\cap B^{n}
where
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
and
R
is definable (without parameters) in
\mathfrak{A}
.

A structure
\mathfrak{B}
is called
finitely bounded
if there are finitely many finite structures
\mathfrak{F}_{1},\dots,\mathfrak{F}_{n}
such that a finite structure
\mathfrak{A}
embeds into
\mathfrak{B}
if and only if it does not embed
\mathfrak{F}_{i}
for some
i\in[n]
.

A structure
\mathfrak{A}
is called
homogeneous
if every isomorphism between finite substructures of
\mathfrak{A}
can be extended to an automorphism of
\mathfrak{A}
. (some authors use
ultrahomogeneous
to prevent confusion with other notions of homogeneity; in our context, however, there is no such danger of confusion). We call a structure
finitely homogeneous
if it is homogeneous and has a finite relational signature, and we call it
finitely homogenizable
if it is interdefinable with finitely homogeneous structure.
Finitely homogeneous structures are
\omega
-categorical
, i.e., their first-order theory has at most one countable model up to isomorphism (see, e.g.,
[
42
, Theorem 7.4.1]
).

It is often useful to study
\omega
-categorical structure with notions from permutation group theory.
If
G
is a permutation group on a set
A
, then
an
orbit of
G
on
A^{k}
(or an
orbit of
k
-tuples
) is a set of the form

\{(\alpha(a_{1}),\dots,\alpha(a_{k}))\mid\alpha\in G\}
for some
(a_{1},\dots,a_{k})\in A^{k}
.
In an
\omega
-categorical structure
\mathfrak{A}
, the first-order definable relations of arity
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
are precisely the finite unions of
orbits of
n
-tuples
of
\operatorname{Aut}(\mathfrak{A})

[
42
, Corollary 7.3.5]
.

We shall use the standard convention of denoting tuples of variables by overlined letters at the end of the alphabet
\bar{x},\bar{y},\bar{z},\dots
and tuples of elements by overlined letters at the beginning of the alphabet
\bar{a},\bar{b},\bar{c}\dots
.

Sometimes it will be best to think in terms of theories rather than structures. Given a first-order theory
T
in the signature
\tau
, a model
\mathfrak{A}
of
T
, and
C\subseteq\mathfrak{A}
, let
\tau\cup\{C\}
be the expansion of the signature
\tau
by constant symbols naming each element of
C
. A
type
(or
\tau
-type
if
\tau
is not clear from the context) in the variables
\bar{x}
over
C
is a maximally consistent set
p
of
\tau\cup\{C\}
-formulas with free variables belonging to
\bar{x}
. We write
S_{\bar{x}}(C)
fir the set of all types in the variable
\bar{x}
over
C
and we just write
S_{1}(C)
when
\bar{x}
is a single variable. Given a
k
-tuple
(a_{1},\dots,a_{k}):=\bar{a}\in A^{k}
, its
type over
C
is the set of all
\tau\cup\{C\}
-formulas satisfied by
\bar{a}
in the variables
(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})
(where for each
1\leq i\leq k
,
x_{i}
substitutes
a_{i}
). We say that
\bar{a}

realizes the type

p\in S_{\bar{x}}(C)
if
p
is the type of
\bar{a}
over
C
. If
\mathfrak{A}
is
\omega
-categorical, then all types in finitely many variables over finitely many parameters are realized in
\mathfrak{A}
(this property is known as being
\omega
-saturated
[
42
, Exercise 7.2.11]
). Moreover, two
k
-tuples of
\mathfrak{A}
have the same type (over
\emptyset
) if and only if they are in the same orbit of
\operatorname{Aut}(\mathfrak{A})

[
42
, Corollary 7.3.3]
.

2.1.
Positive theories and core companions

In Section
3
, we will be looking at properties preserved when taking the core companion of a theory. In particular, we will study properties preserved amongst theories with the same universal negative consequences. This naturally leads us to the study of existential positive formulas (which are preserved under homomorphisms) and more broadly of positive model theory. This branch of model theory has recently received substantial attention (see, for example,
[
7
,
32
,
43
,
11
]
), and the way positive model theoretic properties are defined is a fundamental inspiration to our method to prove that traditional model theoretic properties are preserved when moving to the core companion. In this paper we will adopt the notation of
[
44
]
and we direct the reader to this introductory text and
[
15
, Chapter 2]
for proofs of the facts we mention below.

Definition 2.1
.

An
existential positive
formula
\phi(\bar{y})
is a formula of the form
\exists\bar{x}\;\psi(\bar{x};\bar{y})
, where
\psi(\bar{x};\bar{y})
is quantifier-free and contains no negations.

Definition 2.2
.

A
positive theory
is a set of
h
-inductive sentences
, i.e., sentences of the form

\forall\bar{x}(\phi(\bar{x})\rightarrow\psi(\bar{x})),

where
\phi(\bar{x})
and
\psi(\bar{x})
are existential positive. An
h
-universal sentence
(sometimes known as a universal negative sentence) is a sentence of the form

\forall\bar{x}(\phi(\bar{x})\rightarrow\bot),

where
\phi(\bar{x})
is positive quantifier-free.

Definition 2.3
.

Let
T
be a positive theory and let
\phi(\bar{x})
be an existential positive formula. We say that an existential positive formula
\psi(\bar{x})
is
an obstruction of
\phi(\bar{x})
if

T\vdash\forall\bar{x}((\phi(\bar{x})\wedge\psi(\bar{x}))\rightarrow\bot).

Definition 2.4
.

A model
\mathfrak{A}
of a positive theory
T
is called
positively closed
if every homomorphism
f\colon\mathfrak{A}\to\mathfrak{B}
, where
\mathfrak{B}\vDash T
, is an
immersion
, i.e., is such that for every existential positive formula
\phi(\bar{x})
and every tuple
\bar{a}
from
A
,

\mathfrak{A}\vDash\phi(\bar{a})\text{ if and only if }\mathfrak{B}\vDash\phi(f(\bar{a})).

For any model
\mathfrak{A}
of a positive theory
T
there is a positively closed model
\mathfrak{B}\vDash T
such that
\mathfrak{A}
maps homomorphically to
\mathfrak{B}

[
7
, Lemma 1.20]
.

For the following fact see, for example,
[
16
, Proposition 2.1.14]
:

Fact 2.5
.

Let
S
and
T
be first-order theories. The following are equivalent:

•

every
h
-universal consequence of
T
is also an
h
-universal consequence of
S
; and

•

every model of
S
maps homomorphically to a model of
T
.

Definition 2.6
.

A first-order theory
T
is called
model complete
if every embedding between models of
T
is elementary. A first-order theory
S
is a
core theory
if homomorphisms between models of
S
are embeddings. In particular, we say that
T
is a
model-complete core
theory if homomorphisms between its models are elementary embeddings.

By the
positive fragment
of a first-order theory we mean its set of
h
-inductive consequences. model-complete core theories are axiomatised by their positive fragment
[
11
, Proposition 4.17]
. A positive theory
S
is called Boolean if all of its models are positively closed
[
33
]
(see
[
44
]
for a more detailed discussion). Sometimes this notion is referred to as “positively model complete”
[
39
]
. Note that if
S
is a model-complete core theory, then the positive fragment of
S
is Boolean. Conversely, if a positive theory is Boolean, then it is a model-complete core theory.

Definition 2.7

(
[
11
]
)
.

A first-order theory
S
is the
core companion
of a first-order theory
T
if:

•

S
is a model-complete core theory;

•

S
and
T
have the same
h
-universal consequences.

It follows from Fact
2.5
that
if
S
and
T
have the same
h
-universal consequences, then every model of
S
has a homomorphism to a model of
T
and vice-versa.

2.2.
Interpretations

Our terminology and notation follows standard conventions in model theory; we refer for instance to Hodges
[
42
]
or Tent and Ziegler
[
68
]
.

Definition 2.8
.

A first-order reduct is called
quantifier-free
if all defining formulas can be chosen to be quantifier-free.
We say that
\mathfrak{A}
and
\mathfrak{B}
are
interdefinable
if they are reducts of one another, and that they are
bidefinable
if
\mathfrak{B}
is isomorphic to a structure which is interdefinable with
\mathfrak{A}
.

Remark 2.9
.

If at least one of the structures
\mathfrak{A}
and
\mathfrak{B}
is countable and
\omega
-categorical, then

•

\mathfrak{A}
and
\mathfrak{B}
are interdefinable if and only if
\operatorname{Aut}(\mathfrak{A})=\operatorname{Aut}(\mathfrak{B})
, and

•

\mathfrak{A}
and
\mathfrak{B}
are bidefinable if and only if
\operatorname{Aut}(\mathfrak{A})
and
\operatorname{Aut}(\mathfrak{B})
are isomorphic as permutation groups.

Definition 2.10
.

Let
\mathfrak{A}
and
\mathfrak{B}
be structures. We say that
I
is an
interpretation
of
\mathfrak{B}
in
\mathfrak{A}
if there exists some
d\in\omega
and such that
I
is a partial surjective map from
A^{d}
to
B
such that for every atomic formula
\varphi(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})
in the signature of
\mathfrak{B}
the relation

I^{-1}(R)\coloneqq\{(a_{1}^{1},\dots,a_{1}^{d},\dots,a_{k}^{1},\dots,a_{k}^{d}):\mathfrak{B}\models\varphi(I(a_{1}^{1},\dots,a_{1}^{d}),\dots,I(a_{k}^{1},\dots,a_{k}^{d}))\}

is definable in
\mathfrak{A}
.
The number
d
above is called the
dimension
of the interpretation.
We say that
\mathfrak{A}

interprets

\mathfrak{B}
, or
\mathfrak{B}
is
interpretable
in
\mathfrak{A}
if there exists an interpretation of
\mathfrak{B}
in
\mathfrak{A}
. For a fixed structure
\mathfrak{A}
,
we write
\I(\mathfrak{A})
for the class of structures which are interpretable in it.

Let us consider some interpretation
I
as in the definition above. By applying the definition for the formula
(x=x)
we obtain that the domain set of
I
needs to be definable. Moreover, by applying the definition for the formula
(x=y)
we also obtain that the kernel of the map
I\colon\operatorname{Dom}(I)\rightarrow B
also needs to be definable in
\mathfrak{A}
. This essentially means that the domain of
\mathfrak{B}
can be thought of as a definable quotient of some definable substructure of some power of
\mathfrak{A}
, and every relations of
\mathfrak{B}
needs to be represented by some set of tuples which is also definable in
\mathfrak{A}
.
Note that the interpretability relation is transitive, i.e., if
\mathfrak{B}\in\I(\mathfrak{A})
and
\mathfrak{C}\in\I(\mathfrak{B})
then
\mathfrak{C}\in\I(\mathfrak{A})
.

Definition 2.11
.

An interpretation (of
\mathfrak{B}
in
\mathfrak{A}
)
I
is called
full
if every relation
R
for which
I^{-1}(R)
is definable in
\mathfrak{A}
is definable in
\mathfrak{B}
.

It is clear from the definition above a structure
\mathfrak{B}
is interpretable in
\mathfrak{A}
if and only if
\mathfrak{B}
has some expansion which has a full interpretation in
\mathfrak{A}
.

Remark 2.12
.

Note that if
I
is a
d
-dimensional interpretation of
\mathfrak{B}
in
\mathfrak{A}
, then the following is a well-defind continuous action of
\operatorname{Aut}(\mathfrak{A})

on
B
: for
\alpha\in\operatorname{Aut}(\mathfrak{A})

3.3.
Model theoretic properties defined through type-counting

Certain model theoretic properties such as
\omega

\mathrm{NIP}
can be characterised in terms of type counting
[

We will see below that given a theory
T
and its core companion
S
, the supremum of the number of types that can be attained over a set of parameters of size
\lambda
can only decrease when moving from
T
to
S
. Hence, model theoretic properties which can be defined in terms of type counting are preserved by going to the core companion.

Lemma 3.25
.

Let
S
and
T
be first-order theories in a signature
\tau
such that
S
is the core companion of
T
. Let
\mathfrak{A}^{\star}
and
\mathfrak{B}^{\star}
be, respectively, monster models for

and
T
with a homomorphism
f\colon\mathfrak{A}^{\star}\to\mathfrak{B}^{\star}
. Let
C
be a small subset of
\mathfrak{A}^{\star}
. Let
S_{1}(C)
be the space of
1
-types of
S
over
C
. Then,

|S_{1}(C)|\leq|S_{1}(f(C))|\;.

Proof.

Let
\mu=|S_{1}(C)|
. Let
(a_{i})_{i\leq\mu}
be a set of elements of
\mathfrak{A}^{\star}
, each realising a distinct type in
S_{1}(C)
. For each

, let
\mathrm{tp}^{+}(a_{i}/C)
be the restriction of
\mathrm{tp}(a_{i}/C)
to existentially positive formulas. Since
S
is a model-complete core theory, every formula is equivalent modulo

to an existential positive formula. In particular, writing
S_{1}^{+}(C)
for the space of types over

restricted to existential positive formulas, the “forgetful” map

restricting each type is a bijection. However, since

is a homomorphism and respects existentially positive formulas, the types
(\mathrm{tp}(f(a_{i})/f(C))|i\leq\mu)
are all distinct, yielding the desired statement.
∎

Monadic model theoretic properties

In this subsection, we prove that the two main monadic model theoretic properties of monadic stability and monadic
\mathrm{NIP}
are preserved by going to the core companion. Such properties are of interest in model theory
[
4
]
, permutation group theory (in the
\omega
-categorical context)
[
27
,
19
]
, and structural graph theory (in their generalisation to hereditary classes)
[
34
]
.

Definition 3.29
.

Let
\mathcal{P}
be a patterned property. We say that a first-order theory
T
is monadically
\mathrm{N}\mathcal{P}
if all expansions of a model of
T
by unary predicates are still
\mathrm{N}\mathcal{P}
.

The two main monadic properties are monadic stability and monadic
\mathrm{NIP}
. We note that other classes (such as monadically
\mathrm{NSOP}
or monadically simple) just collapse to monadic stability
[
36
]
.

The definition of monadic
\mathrm{NIP}

prima facie
does not seem amenable to being shown to be preserved by going to the core companion of a theory by the same methods we used for patterned properties. However, there is an equivalent characterisation of monadic
\mathrm{NIP}
which has a similar enough form to patterned properties to be amenable to our techniques in terms of not admitting a pre-coding configuration
[
25
]
. The definition we give below is not the original (of
[
25
, Definition 3.10]
), but a simplified variant which is proven to be equivalent to the original in Lemma 1.7 of
[
26
]
:

Definition 3.30
.

A
pre-coding configuration
consists of a formula
\phi(\bar{x},\bar{y},z)
with
|z|=1
, and a sequence
\mathcal{I}:=(\bar{d}_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}
such that for all
s<0<t
from
\mathbb{Z}
, there is
c_{(s,t)}
such that

(3.1)

\displaystyle\vDash\phi(\bar{d}_{s},\bar{d}_{t},c_{(s,t)});

(3.2)

\displaystyle\text{ for all }v>t,

\displaystyle\vDash\neg\phi(\bar{d}_{s},\bar{d}_{v},c_{(s,t)});\text{ and}

(3.3)

\displaystyle\text{for all }u<s,

\displaystyle\vDash\neg\phi(\bar{d}_{u},\bar{d}_{t},c_{(s,t)})\;.

Fact 3.31

(Theorem 1.1 & Proposition 2.11 in
[
25
]
)
.

The following are equivalent for a first-order theory
T
:

(1)

T
is monadically
\mathrm{NIP}
;

(2)

T
does not admit a pre-coding configuration.

Fact 3.32

(
[
4
]
)
.

Let
T
be a first-order theory. The following are equivalent:

(1)

T
is monadically stable;

(2)

T
is stable and monadically
\mathrm{NIP}
.

Proposition 3.33
.

Let
S
be the core companion of
T
. Then

(1)

if
T
is monadically
\mathrm{NIP}
, then so is
S
;

(2)

if
T
is monadically stable, then so is
S
.

Proof.

The second statement follows from the first by Fact
3.32
and the fact that stability is preserved by taking the core companion (Theorem
3.22
). We prove the first statement by contrapositive. Suppose that
S
is not monadically
\mathrm{NIP}
. Then, it admits a pre-coding configuration given by a formula
\phi(\bar{x},\bar{y},z)
, a sequence
(\bar{d}_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}
, and
(c_{(s,t)}|s<0<t\in\mathbb{Z})
. Since
S
is a model-complete core theory, we may take
\phi
to be an existential positive formula. We also take
\psi(\bar{x},y,z)
to be an existential positive formula which is equivalent modulo
S
to
\neg\phi(\bar{x},\bar{y},z)
(and so is an obstruction of
\phi(\bar{x},\bar{y},z)
). Take a homomorphism
f\colon\mathfrak{A}^{\star}\to\mathfrak{B}^{\star}
where
\mathfrak{B}^{\star}
is a monster model for
T
. Since existential positive formulas are preserved by homomorphisms and the fact that
\psi
is an obstruction of
\phi
is also true in
T
, we still have that
(f(\bar{d}_{i}))_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}
and
(f(c_{(s,t)})|s<0<t\in\mathbb{Z})
witness that
\phi(\bar{x},\bar{y},z)
has a pre-coding configuration. In particular,
T
is not monadically
\mathrm{NIP}
by Fact
3.31
.
∎

Proposition
3.33
can also be phrased in the following way in the
\omega
-categorical setting.

Corollary 3.34
.

Let
\mathfrak{A}
be a countable
\omega
-categorical structure, and let
\mathfrak{B}
be the model-complete core of
\mathfrak{A}
. Then

(1)

if
\mathfrak{A}
is monadically
\mathrm{NIP}
, then so is
\mathfrak{B}
;

(2)

if
\mathfrak{A}
is monadically stable, then so is
\mathfrak{B}
.

We remark that the second claim of Corollary
3.34
also follows from an orbit growth argument, as it already has been observed in
[
19
]
. This uses a characterization of
\omega
-categorical monadically stable structures in terms of unlabelled growth.

Definition 3.35
.

Let
\mathfrak{A}
be an
\omega
-categorical structure. We denote by
u_{n}(\mathfrak{A})
the number of orbits of the natural action of
\operatorname{Aut}(\mathfrak{A})
on
n
-element subsets of
\mathfrak{A}
. The sequence
(u_{n}(\mathfrak{A}))_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is called the
unlabelled growth
of
\mathfrak{A}
.

Theorem 3.36

(
[
27
]
, Theorem 1.1)
.

Let
\mathfrak{A}
be a countable
\omega
-categorical structure. Then
\mathfrak{A}
is monadically stable if and only if
\mathfrak{A}
is stable and for all
c>1
we have
u_{n}(\mathfrak{A})<c^{n}
if
n
is large enough.

We know that if
\mathfrak{A}
is
\omega
-categorical and
\mathfrak{B}
is its model-complete core then
u_{n}(\mathfrak{B})\leq u_{n}(\mathfrak{A})
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
(see for instance
[
18
]
, Lemma 2.2.69). Therefore, Theorem
3.36
indeed implies that
\omega
-categorical monadically stable structures are closed under taking model-complete cores. We mention that a different orbit growth characterization (using labelled growth) of
\omega
-categorical monadically stable structures is given in
[
17
]
which also can be used to show item
2
of Corollary
3.34
.

For
\omega
-categorical monadically
\mathrm{NIP}
structures no characterization is known in terms of orbit growth functions, however the following has been conjectured in
[
27
]
(Conjecture 2).

Conjecture 3.37
.

Let
\mathfrak{A}
be a countable
\omega
-categorical structure. Then
\mathfrak{A}
is monadically
\mathrm{NIP}
if and only if
u_{n}(\mathfrak{A})<c^{n}
for some
c\in\mathbb{R}
.

Note that given Conjecture
3.37
the same argument as above would also imply the first item of Corollary
3.34
.

3.5.
Finer features in an
\omega
-stable setting

In later sections we will be studying various classes of structures which are
\omega
-stable (such as structures interpretable in
(\mathbb{Q};=)
, or Lachlan’s class). We have already shown that
\omega
-stability is preserved when going to the model-complete core. The finer structure of definable sets in
\omega
-stable theories can be studied through the notion of Morley rank. Indeed, all
\omega
-stable theories are totally transcendental (in the sense that Morley rank is defined for all formulas), and for countable theories
\omega
-stability and total trancendentality are the same property
[
68
, Theorem 5.2.6]
. In this subsection we prove that Morley rank behaves well under the operation of going to the core companion. We also prove that the core companion of a strongly minimal theory is still strongly minimal.

Lemma 3.38
.

Let
S
be the core companion of
T
. Let
\phi(\bar{x};\bar{y})
be an existential positive formula and
a
be a tuple of arity
|\bar{y}|
from the monster model
\mathfrak{A}^{\star}
of
S
. Let
f\colon\mathfrak{A}^{\star}\to\mathfrak{B}^{\star}
be a homomorphism from
\mathfrak{A}^{\star}
to a monster model
\mathfrak{B}^{\star}
of
T
. If
\phi(\bar{x};\bar{a})
has infinitely many realisations in
\mathfrak{A}^{\star}
, so does
\phi(\bar{x};f(\bar{a}))
in
\mathfrak{B}^{\star}
.

Proof.

As usual, we prove this by contrapositive. Let
(\phi(\bar{b}_{i},\bar{a}))_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
be distinct realisations of
\phi(\bar{x},\bar{a})
in
\mathfrak{A}^{\star}
. Since
S
is a model-complete core theory,
\bar{x}_{1}\neq\bar{x}_{2}
(where
|\bar{x}_{1}|=|\bar{x}_{2}|=|\bar{x}|
) is equivalent to an existential positive formula
\psi(\bar{x}_{1},\bar{x}_{2})
. In particular,
\psi(\bar{b}_{i},\bar{b}_{j})
holds for all
i\neq j
in
\mathfrak{A}^{\star}
and so we have that
\psi(f(\bar{b}_{i}),f(\bar{b}_{j}))
holds in
\mathfrak{B}^{\star}
for all
i\neq j
. Since this is still an obstruction of
\bar{x}_{1}=\bar{x}_{2}
in
T
,
\phi(\bar{x},\bar{a})
has infinitely many realisations in
\mathfrak{B}^{\star}
.
∎

Definition 3.39
.

Let
T
be a first-order theory. Let
\phi(\bar{x};\bar{y})
be a
\tau
-formula. Let
b
be a tuple of arity
|\bar{y}|
. We define the Morley rank of
\phi(\bar{x};\bar{b})
by induction on ordinals as follows:

•

\mathrm{MR}(\phi(\bar{x};\bar{b}))\geq 0
if
\phi
is consistent;

•

\mathrm{MR}(\phi(\bar{x};\bar{b}))\geq\beta+1
if there are
(\phi_{i}(\bar{x}))_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
which imply
\phi
and which are pairwise inconsistent and such that
\mathrm{MR}(\phi_{i})\geq\alpha
for all
i\in\mathbb{N}
;

•

\mathrm{MR}(\phi(\bar{x};\bar{b}))\geq\lambda
for
\lambda
a limit ordinal if
\mathrm{MR}(\phi)\geq\gamma
for all
\gamma<\lambda
.

If
\phi
is inconsistent, we define
\mathrm{MR}(\phi)=-\infty
. If
\mathrm{MR}(\phi)>\alpha
for all ordinals
\alpha
, we set
\mathrm{MR}(\phi)=\infty
. Otherwise, there is some
\alpha
such that
\mathrm{MR}(\phi)\geq\alpha
but
\mathrm{MR}(\phi)\not\geq\alpha+1
and we set
\mathrm{MR}(\phi)=\alpha
. When working with multiple theories, we may write
\mathrm{MR}_{T}
to denote the Morley rank in the theory
T
. We say that a theory
T
is
totally transcendental
if all consistent formulas (with parameters from the monster model) have ordinal Morley rank.

It is clear how to define the positive version of Morley rank:

Definition 3.40
.

Let
S
be a positive theory with the joint homomorphism property. Let
\phi(\bar{x};\bar{y})
be an existential positive
\tau
-formula. Let
b
be a tuple of arity
|\bar{y}|
(from the positive monster model of
S
). We say

•

\mathrm{MR}^{+}(\phi(\bar{x};\bar{b}))\geq 0
if
\phi
does not imply
\bot
;

•

\mathrm{MR}^{+}(\phi(\bar{x};\bar{b}))\geq\beta+1
if there are existential positive
(\phi_{i}(\bar{x}))_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
which imply
\phi
such that for each
i<j\in\mathbb{N}
,
\phi_{j}
is an obstruction of
\phi_{i}
and such that
\mathrm{MR}^{+}(\phi_{i})\geq\alpha
for all
i\in\mathbb{N}
;

•

\mathrm{MR}^{+}(\phi(\bar{x};\bar{b}))\geq\lambda
for
\lambda
a limit ordinal if
\mathrm{MR}(\phi)\geq\gamma
for all
\gamma<\lambda
.

Then, we define the positive Morley rank of a formula exactly as in Definition
3.39
.

The lemma below could also be deduced from Lemma
3.46
and
[
72
, Proposition 7.36]
.

Lemma 3.41
.

Let
S
be the core companion of
T
. Let
\phi(\bar{x};\bar{y})
be an existential positive formula and
a
be a tuple of arity
|\bar{y}|
from the monster model
\mathfrak{A}^{\star}
of
S
. Let
f\colon\mathfrak{A}^{\star}\to\mathfrak{B}^{\star}
be a homomorphism from
\mathfrak{A}^{\star}
to a monster model
\mathfrak{B}^{\star}
of
T
. Then, the Morley rank of
\phi(\bar{x};\bar{a})
in
S
is smaller or equal to the Morley rank of
\phi(\bar{x};f(\bar{a}))
in
T
. In particular, if
T
is totally transcendental, so is
S
.

Proof.

In
S
every formula is equivalent to an existential positive formula. Thus, every formula is assigned both a Morley rank and a positive Morley rank and the two coincide.

We shall prove by induction on ordinals that if
\mathrm{MR}(\phi(\bar{x},\bar{a}))\geq\alpha
in
S
, then
\mathrm{MR}(\phi(\bar{x},f(\bar{a})))\geq\alpha
in
S
. Since
f
is a homomorphism from
\mathfrak{A}^{\star}
into
\mathfrak{B}^{\star}
, if
\phi(\bar{x},\bar{a})
is consistent in
S
, so is
\phi(\bar{x},f(\bar{a}))
in
T
. Hence, we have dealt with the base case. Now, since the case of limit ordinals is trivial, we only need to deal with the case of
\alpha+1
. Suppose that
\mathrm{MR}(\phi(\bar{x},\bar{a}))\geq\alpha+1
. Since in
S
,
\mathrm{MR}^{+}
and
\mathrm{MR}
agree, there are existential positive
(\phi_{i}(\bar{x};\bar{a}_{i}))_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
implying
\phi(\bar{x};\bar{a})
and which are pairwise inconsistent. By inductive hypothesis, the
(\phi_{i}(\bar{x};f(\bar{a}_{i})))_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
witness the fact that
\mathrm{MR}(\phi(\bar{x};f(\bar{a}))\geq\alpha+1
, completing the proof. Finally, suppose by contrapositive
S
is not
\omega
-stable. Then, some existential positive formula
\phi(\bar{x};\bar{a})
is assigned Morley rank
\infty
. But then, taking an appropriate homomorphism
f\colon\mathfrak{A}^{\star}\to\mathfrak{B}^{\star}
, we get that
\phi(\bar{x},f(\bar{a}))
also has Morley rank
\infty
, meaning that
T
is not totally transcendental either.
∎

Remark 3.42
.

Given a formula
\phi(\bar{x};\bar{b})
with
\mathrm{MR}(\phi(\bar{x};\bar{b}))=\alpha
, where
\alpha
is an ordinal, let the
Morley degree
of
\phi(\bar{x};\bar{b})
,
\mathrm{dM}(\phi(\bar{x};\bar{b}))
, be the largest number
n\in\mathbb{N}
such that there are
\phi_{1}(\bar{x}),\dots,\phi_{n}(\bar{x})
implying
\phi
, pairwise inconsistent and with
\mathrm{MR}(\phi_{i}(\bar{x}))=\alpha
(such number exists; cf.
[
68
, Lemma 2.6.4]
). We say that a first-order theory
T
is
strongly minimal
if
(\mathrm{MR}_{T}(x=x),\mathrm{dM}(\phi(x=x)))=(1,1)
. Note that
T
is strongly minimal if and only if every definable subset (with parameters) of its monster model
\mathfrak{A}^{\star}
is either finite or cofinite. If a positive theory is not a model-complete core theory, there may not be a reasonable notion of positive Morley degree of an existential positive formula of ordinal positive Morley rank. Moreover, let
S
be the core companion of
T
and let
\mathfrak{A}^{\star}
and
\mathfrak{B}^{\star}
be monster models of the respective theories with a homomorphism
f\colon\mathfrak{A}^{\star}\to\mathfrak{B}^{\star}
. Let
\phi(\bar{x};\bar{b})
be an existential positive formula with
\bar{b}
being parameters from
\mathfrak{A}^{\star}
. Then, it is possible both that
\mathrm{dM}(\phi(\bar{x};\bar{b}))<\mathrm{dM}(\phi(\bar{x};f(\bar{b}))
and that
\mathrm{dM}(\phi(\bar{x};\bar{b}))>\mathrm{dM}(\phi(\bar{x};f(\bar{b}))
. For the first case, let
T
be the theory of an equivalence relation
E_{1}
with two classes. Its core companion
S
is the theory of an equivalence relation
E_{1}
with a single class. In particular,
\mathrm{dM}_{T}(x=x)=2
and
\mathrm{dM}_{S}(x=x)=1
. For the second case, let
T^{\prime}
be the expansion of
T
by a relation
E_{1}^{\neg}
for the negation of
E_{1}
and by an equivalence relation
E_{2}
which agrees with
E_{1}
on one of its classes, and which refines the other class into infinitely many classes. The core companion
S^{\prime}
of
T^{\prime}
is the theory where the equivalence relation
E_{2}
coincides with
E_{1}
, which still partitions the domain in two classes and is still negated by
E_{1}^{\neg}
. Now,
(\mathrm{MR}_{S}(x=x),\mathrm{dM}_{S}(x=x))=(1,2)
. But
(\mathrm{MR}_{T}(x=x),\mathrm{dM}_{T}(x=x))=(2,1)
.

In spite of the fact that the Morley degree of a theory does not necessarily behave well when moving to the core companion, strong minimality is still preserved.

Corollary 3.43
.

Let
S
be the core companion of
T
. If
T
is strongly minimal, then so is
S
.

Proof.

Assume that
\mathrm{MR}_{T}(x=x)=1
. We prove by contrapositive that if
\mathrm{dM}_{S}(x=x)>1
, then
\mathrm{dM}_{T}(x=x)>1
. Firstly, by the previous lemma,

1\leq\mathrm{MR}_{S}(x=x)\leq\mathrm{MR}_{T}(x=x)=1\;.

If
S
is not strongly minimal, this is witnessed by two existential positive formulas
\phi_{1}(x,\bar{a}_{1})
and
\phi_{2}(x,\bar{a}_{2})
which are jointly inconsistent and both of Morley rank
\geq 1
. By Lemma
3.38
, since
\phi_{1}
and
\phi_{2}
are non-algebraic (i.e., have infinitely many realisations) in
\mathfrak{A}^{\star}
,
\phi_{1}(x,f(\bar{a}_{1}))
and
\phi_{2}(x,f(\bar{a}_{2}))
are non-algebraic in
\mathfrak{B}^{\star}
. Hence, they both have Morley rank
\geq 1
and they are still jointly inconsistent, yielding that
T
is not strongly minimal.
∎

3.6.
Trace definability of the core companion

In later sections, we will see that the classes of structures interpretable in
(\mathbb{N},=)
or in
(\mathbb{Q},<)
are not closed under taking core companions. In particular, it is possible that no model of a theory
T
interprets a model of the core companion
S
of
T
. Nevertheless, in this subsection, we show that the core companion
S
of a theory
T
is interpretable in a weak sense in
T
, through the notion of
trace definability

[
71
,
72
]
. Trace definability is a weak notion of interpretation that recently received attention in model theory.
We show that if a complete theory
T
has a model-complete core companion
S
, then
T
trace defines
S
. Several model theoretic properties can be defined in terms of not trace defining certain theories. Since trace definability is a transitive relation, showing that a theory trace defines its core companion implies that all properties which can be defined in terms of not trace defining a given theory (or class of theories) are preserved when moving to the core companion.

Definition 3.44
.

Let
\mathfrak{A}
and
\mathfrak{B}
be first-order structures in signatures
\sigma
and
\tau
respectively. A
trace definition
of
\mathfrak{B}
in
\mathfrak{A}
is a map
\mathfrak{t}:\mathfrak{B}\to\mathfrak{A}^{m}
for some
m\in\mathbb{N}
such that for any
\tau\cup\{B\}
-formula
\phi(\overline{x})
with
|\overline{x}|=n
, there is a
\sigma\cup\{A\}
-formula
\psi(\overline{y})
with
|\overline{y}|=nm
such that for any tuple
\overline{a}\in A^{n}
, we have that

\mathfrak{B}\vDash\phi(\overline{a})\Leftrightarrow\mathfrak{A}\vDash\psi(\mathfrak{t}(\overline{a})).

We say that
\mathfrak{A}

trace defines

\mathfrak{B}
through the map
\mathfrak{t}
. We say that a complete theory
T
trace defines another complete theory
S
if some model of
S
is trace definable in some model of
T
.

It is easy to see that trace definability is a transitive relation and that if
\mathfrak{A}
interprets
\mathfrak{B}
, then it also trace defines
\mathfrak{B}

[
71
]
. In particular, we can think of trace definability as a weak
form
of interpretation. When a theory
T
trace defines another theory
S
, several properties are
transfered
from
T
to
S
. We list below some of the main properties preserved under trace definability. We refer the reader to
[
71
]
for the various definitions. Several of these properties are used to analyse the finer structure of stable and
\mathrm{NIP}
theories
[
65
,
67
,
2
,
6
]
.

Fact 3.45

(
[
71
]
)
.

Suppose that
T
trace defines
S
. If
T
has one of the following properties, then
S
also does: stability,
\mathrm{NIP}
,
k
-
\mathrm{NIP}
, being totally transcendental, superstability, strong dependence, having finite of
U
-rank, having finite Morley rank, having finite
\mathrm{dp}
-rank, having finite
\mathrm{op}
-rank, linear
\mathrm{VC}
-density bounds, having the strong Erdős-Hajnal
property, having near linear Zarankiewicz bounds.

Lemma 3.46
.

Let
T
be be a first-order theory and
S
be its core companion. Let
\mathfrak{B}
be a model of
S
and
\mathfrak{t}\colon\mathfrak{B}\to\mathfrak{A}
be a homomorphism from
\mathfrak{B}
into some model
\mathfrak{A}
of
T
. Then
\mathfrak{t}
is a trace definition.
In particular,
T
trace defines
S
.

Proof.

Let
\tau
be the signature of
S
and
T
.
Notice that since
S
is a model-complete core theory,
\mathfrak{t}
is injective. In fact, there is some existential positive formula
\psi(x,y)
which is an obstruction to
x=y
in
S
. Since
\mathfrak{t}
preserves
\psi
and since
\psi
is still an obstruction to
x=y
in
T
, this implies that
\mathfrak{t}
is injective. Now, consider an existential positive
\tau
-formula
\phi(\overline{x},\overline{y})
.
Since
S
is a model-complete core theory,

\neg\phi(\overline{x},\overline{y})
is equivalent to an
existential positive formula

\theta(\overline{x},\overline{y})
modulo
S
.
Take
\overline{b}\in B^{|\overline{y}|}
. Notice that for
\overline{a}\in A^{|\overline{x}|}
,

\mathfrak{B}\vDash\phi(\overline{a},\overline{b})\Rightarrow\mathfrak{A}\vDash\phi(\mathfrak{t}(\overline{a}),\mathfrak{t}(\overline{b})),

and

\mathfrak{B}\vDash\neg\phi(\overline{a},\overline{b})\Leftrightarrow\mathfrak{B}\vDash\theta(\overline{a},\overline{b})\Rightarrow\mathfrak{A}\vDash\theta(\mathfrak{t}(\overline{a}),\mathfrak{t}(\overline{b}))\Rightarrow\mathfrak{A}\vDash\neg\phi(\mathfrak{t}(\overline{a}),\mathfrak{t}(\overline{b})),

where the last implication follows since
\theta
is still an obstruction to
\phi
in
T
. In particular, from the above implications we obtain that
\mathfrak{B}
is trace defined in
\mathfrak{A}
, as desired.
∎

Putting together Fact
3.45
and Lemma
3.46
, we get:

Corollary 3.47
.

Suppose that
S
is the core companion of the theory
T
. If
T
has one of the following properties, then
S
also does: stability,
\mathrm{NIP}
,
k
-
\mathrm{NIP}
, being totally transcendental, superstability, strong dependence, having finite of
U
-rank, having finite Morley rank, having finite
\mathrm{dp}
-rank, having finite
\mathrm{op}
-rank, having linear
\mathrm{VC}
-density bounds, having the strong Erdős-Hajnal property, having near linear Zarankiewicz bounds.

4.
Properties not preserved by taking model-complete cores

The previous section might leave the impression that virtually every meaningful model-theoretic property
is preserved by taking model-complete cores. However, this is not the case; we start with an instructive example that shows that total categoricity is not preserved by taking model-complete cores (Theorem
4.1
). We do not know whether total categoricity is preserved by the model companion.

Other classes of structures that are not preserved by taking model-complete cores are the class of all structures interpretable over equality (Section
4.2
), and the class of all structures interpretable over
({\mathbb{Q}};<)
(Section
4.3
). The latter is not even preserved by taking model companions.

4.1.
Total categoricity

A theory is called
totally categorical
if it has up to isomorphism precisely one model of each infinite cardinality.
A structure is called
totally categorical
if its first-order theory is.
It is well known that totally categorical structures are not closed under taking reducts. An example for this is the digraph
\mathfrak{A}
with infinitely many disjoint directed edges, which is totally categorical. However, let us consider the first-order reduct
\mathfrak{A}
of this structure which only has two unary predicates, one for all sources and one for all targets. The theory of this structure has 3 non-isomorphic models of size
\omega_{1}
, because we can pick the size of the unary relations to be
\omega
or
\omega_{1}
as long as at least one of the relations has size
\omega_{1}
. This shows that
\mathfrak{A}
is not totally categorical.

We now construct a totally categorical structure whose model-complete core is not
totally categorical.

Theorem 4.1
.

The class of totally categorical structures is not closed under taking core companions.

Proof.

Let
A:={\mathbb{N}}\times\{0,1,2\}
. Let
\mathfrak{A}
be the structure with domain
A
and whose relations are defined as follows (also see Figure
1
).

\displaystyle U_{i}

\displaystyle\coloneqq{\mathbb{N}}\times\{i\}\text{ for }i\in\{0,1\}

\displaystyle N

\displaystyle\coloneqq\{(u_{1},u_{2})\in(U_{1})^{2}\cup(U_{2})^{2}\mid u_{1}\neq u_{2}\}

\displaystyle R

\displaystyle\coloneqq\{((n,0),(n,1)):n\in{\mathbb{N}}\}\cup\{((n,0),(n,2)):n\in{\mathbb{N}}\}

\displaystyle\quad\cup\{((0,0),(n,1)):n\in{\mathbb{N}}\}\cup\{((0,0),(n,2)):n\in{\mathbb{N}}\}.

Let
\mathfrak{B}
be any model of the theory of
\mathfrak{A}
. Then, in
\mathfrak{B}
,
(U_{0},U_{1},U_{2})
is a partition of
B
and
R
is a union of a bijection from
U_{0}
to
U_{1}
, a bijection from
U_{0}
to
U_{2}
and
\{v\}\times(U_{1}\cup U_{2})
for some unique vertex
v\in U_{0}
. In particular
|U_{0}|=|U_{1}|=|U_{2}|
. It is easy to see that if
|B|=\kappa
then
\mathfrak{B}
is isomorphic to the structure whose domain is
\kappa\times\{0,1,2\}
and whose relations are defined the same way as for
\mathfrak{A}
. In particular
|B|
determines
\mathfrak{B}
up to isomorphism, i.e.,
\Th(\mathfrak{B})=\Th(\mathfrak{A})
is totally categorical.

Now, let
C={\mathbb{N}}\times\{1,2\}\cup\{(0,0)\}
and
\mathfrak{C}=\mathfrak{A}|_{C}
. Any endomorphism of
\mathfrak{C}
is a union of
\operatorname{id}(\{(0,0)\})
and self-injections of
U_{1}
and
U_{2}
which clearly can be approximated by automorphisms of
\mathfrak{C}
. Thus,
\mathfrak{C}
is a model-complete core. Let us now observe that the map
A\rightarrow C
which fixes every element in
U_{1}\cup U_{2}
and maps every element of
U_{0}
to
\{(0,0)\}
is a homomorphism from
\mathfrak{A}
to
\mathfrak{C}
. Therefore,
\mathfrak{C}
is the model-complete core of
\mathfrak{A}
. Note, however, that
\mathfrak{C}
is interdefinable with the disjoint union of two infinite sets and a one-element structure. Then the same argument as above shows that
\Th(\mathfrak{C})
has 3 models of size
\omega_{1}
, and thus it is not totally categorical.
∎

4.2.1.
The Johnson graph

To prove that
\mathfrak{Y}
(see (
4.1
)) is a model-complete core and not interpretable in
({\mathbb{Q}};=)
, we need to revisit basic facts about the so-called Johnson graph (and its finite covers).

Definition 4.6
.

The
Johnson graph
(denoted by
\mathfrak{J}
) is the graph whose vertex set is
{{\mathbb{Q}}\choose 2}
(that is, the set of
2
-element subsets of
{\mathbb{Q}}
), and where two (distinct) vertices
x,y\in{{\mathbb{Q}}\choose 2}
are adjacent, i.e., connected by an edge, if and only if
x\cap y
is nonempty.
We use the notation
E
for the set of edges, and
N
for the set of nonedges of
\mathfrak{J}
, i.e.,

\displaystyle E

\displaystyle:=\{(x,y\in{{\mathbb{Q}}\choose 2}^{2}:|x\cap y|=1\}

\displaystyle\text{ and }\quad N

\displaystyle:=\{(x,y\in{{\mathbb{Q}}\choose 2}^{2}:|x\cap y|=0\}.

If
\alpha
is a permutation of
{\mathbb{Q}}
, then the natural action of
\alpha
on
{{\mathbb{Q}}\choose 2}
is denoted by
\tilde{\alpha}
.

We chose the domain set of
\mathfrak{J}
to be
{{\mathbb{Q}}\choose 2}
, but clearly we could use any countable set instead of
\mathbb{Q}
.

Remark 4.7
.

It is well known and easy to see that

\operatorname{Aut}(\mathfrak{J})=\{\tilde{\alpha}:\alpha\in\operatorname{Sym}(\mathbb{Q})\}
(see, e.g.,
[
10
]
).

Notation 4.8
.

We denote by
P_{a}
the set
\big\{\{a,b\}:b\in{\mathbb{Q}}\setminus\{a\}\big\}
.

Lemma 4.9
.

Let
a\in{\mathbb{Q}}
, and let
e\in\operatorname{End}(\mathfrak{J})
. Then there exists a unique
b\in{\mathbb{Q}}
such that
e(P_{a})\subseteq P_{b}
.

Proof.

Let
c_{1},c_{2},c_{3},c_{4}\in{\mathbb{Q}}\setminus\{a\}
be distinct. Then the elements
e(\{a,c_{1}\})
,
e(\{a,c_{2}\})
,
e(\{a,c_{3}\})
, and
e(\{a,c_{4}\})
are pairwise adjacent in
\mathfrak{J}
. It is easy to verify that this is only possible if there exists a number
b
which is contained in
e(\{a,c_{i}\})
for all
i\in\{1,2,3,4\}
, that is,
e(\{a,c_{i}\})\in P_{b}
. If we choose any
c_{4}^{\prime}\in{\mathbb{Q}}\setminus\{a,c_{1},c_{2},c_{3}\}
, then the same argument shows that
\{a,c_{4}^{\prime}\}\in P_{b}
. This implies that
e
maps
P_{a}
into
P_{b}
. The uniqueness of
b
is then obvious.
∎

The Johnson graph is not a core, because it is homomorphically equivalent to an infinite clique. However, it becomes a model-complete core if we expand it by the relation
N
.

Lemma 4.10
.

The structure
\big({{\mathbb{Q}}\choose 2};E,N\big)
is a model-complete core.

Proof.

We show that
\operatorname{End}({{\mathbb{Q}}\choose 2};E,N)=\overline{\operatorname{Aut}({{\mathbb{Q}}\choose 2};E,N)}=\overline{\operatorname{Aut}(\mathfrak{J})}
. Let
e
be an endomorphism of
({{\mathbb{Q}}\choose 2};E,N)
. Then
e
is also an endomorphism of
\mathfrak{J}
. By Lemma
4.9
,
there exists a map
\alpha\colon{\mathbb{Q}}\rightarrow{\mathbb{Q}}
such that
P_{a}
is mapped to
P_{\alpha(a)}
. We claim that
\alpha
is injective. Arbitrarily choose distinct
a,b,c,d\in{\mathbb{Q}}
.
Then,
(\{a,c\},\{b,d\})\in N
, and thus
(e(\{a,c\}),e(\{b,d\}))\in N
, that is,
e(\{a,c\})\cap e(\{b,d\})=\emptyset
. By definition,
e(\{a,c\})\in P_{\alpha(a)}
and
e(\{b,d\})\in P_{\alpha(b)}
, that is,
\alpha(a)\in e(\{a,c\})
and
\alpha(b)\in e(\{b,d\})
. It follows that
\alpha(a)\neq\alpha(b)
, which shows that
\alpha
is injective.

Let
F
be a finite subset of
{{\mathbb{Q}}\choose 2}
and let
F^{\prime}:=\cup F
. Then
F^{\prime}
is a finite subset of
{\mathbb{Q}}
. Since
\alpha
is injective, it follows that there exists a permutation
\beta
of
{\mathbb{Q}}
so that
\beta|_{F^{\prime}}=\alpha|_{F^{\prime}}
. Then
\tilde{\beta}\in\operatorname{Aut}(\mathfrak{J})
. We claim that
\tilde{\beta}|_{F}=e|_{F}
. Let
\{a,b\}\in F
. Then
a,b\in F^{\prime}
, and by definition
\alpha(a),\alpha(b)\in e(\{a,n\})
. Since
\alpha
is injective,

e(\{a,b\})=\{\alpha(a),\alpha(b)\}=\{\beta(a),\beta(b)\}=\tilde{\beta}(\{a,b\}).

This proves that
\operatorname{End}({{\mathbb{Q}}\choose 2};E,N)=\overline{\operatorname{Aut}(\mathfrak{J})}
. Therefore,
({{\mathbb{Q}}\choose 2};E,N)
is a model-complete core.
∎

4.2.2.
Finite covers of the Johnson graph

Let
\mathfrak{A}
and
\mathfrak{B}
be structures with domains
A
and
B

and let
\pi\colon A\to B
be a function.
The
kernel
of
\pi
is the equivalence relation
\{(a_{1},a_{2})\mid\pi(a_{1})=\pi(a_{2})\}\subseteq A^{2}
.
If
\pi
is surjective and the kernel of
\pi
is preserved by
\operatorname{End}(\mathfrak{A})
, we write
\mu_{\pi}
for the mapping from

\operatorname{End}(\mathfrak{A})
to
B\to B
defined as follows.
If
e\in\operatorname{End}(\mathfrak{A})
and
b\in B
, we choose
a\in\pi^{-1}(b)
(which we can since
\pi
is surjective). Then

\mu_{\pi}(e)(b):=\pi(e(a))
is well-defined since the kernel of
\pi
is preserved by
e
.

Definition 4.11
.

Let
\mathfrak{A}
and
\mathfrak{B}
be structures with domains
A
and
B
.
A
finite covering map
\pi\colon\mathfrak{A}\to\mathfrak{B}
is a
surjective function
\pi\colon A\to B
such that

•

the preimage of each
w\in B
under
\pi
is finite,

•

the kernel of
\pi
is preserved by
\operatorname{End}(\mathfrak{A})
, and

•

the image of
\operatorname{Aut}(\mathfrak{A})
under
\mu_{\pi}
equals
\operatorname{Aut}(\mathfrak{B})
.

Remark 4.12
.

Note that finite covering maps
\pi\colon\mathfrak{A}\to\mathfrak{B}
are usually defined using a variant of
\mu_{\pi}
, namely the version where it is only defined on
\operatorname{Aut}(\mathfrak{A})
(see, for example,
[
37
,
9
]
).
For us it will be practical to apply
\mu_{\pi}
also to endomorphisms. Note that the assumption in Definition
4.11
that the kernel of
\pi
should be preserved by
\operatorname{End}(\mathfrak{A})
rather than
\operatorname{Aut}(\mathfrak{A})
is not a severe restriction,
because if replace
\mathfrak{A}
and
\mathfrak{B}
with their expansions by all first-order definable relations, and the kernel of
\pi
being preserved by
\operatorname{Aut}(\mathfrak{A})
, then it is also preserved by
\operatorname{End}(\mathfrak{A})
.

Lemma 4.13
.

Let
\pi\colon Y\rightarrow{{\mathbb{Q}}\choose 2}
be given by
(a,b,m)\mapsto\{a,b\}
. Then
\pi\colon\mathfrak{Y}\to\mathfrak{J}
is a finite covering map.
Moreover,
every
e\in\operatorname{End}(\mathfrak{Y})
preserves the relation

D:=\{((a,b,m),(a,b,n))\mid a,b\in{\mathbb{Q}},a<b,m,n\in\mathbb{Z}_{4}\}

and
\mu_{\pi}(e)
is an endomorphism of
({{\mathbb{Q}}\choose 2};E,N)
.

Proof.

Clearly, the preimage of every element
\{a,b\}\in{{\mathbb{Q}}\choose 2}
under
\pi
is finite. We first show that the kernel of
\pi
is preserved by
\operatorname{End}(\mathfrak{Y})
by proving that it is existentially positively definable in
\mathfrak{Y}
:
we have
\pi(x)=\pi(y)
if and only if

x=y\vee R(x,y)\vee R(y,x)\vee\exists z\big(R(x,z)\wedge R(z,y)\big).

Next, we show that
\mu_{\pi}(e)
preserves the relations
E
and
N
for every
e\in\operatorname{End}(\mathfrak{Y})
.

Let
(\{a,b\},\{c,d\})\in E
, that is,
|\{a,b\}\cap\{c,d\}|=1
. We may assume without loss of generality that
a<b
and
c<d
. By definition,
((a,b,m),(c,d,n))\in E
for some
m,n\in\{0,1\}
. Then
(e((a,b,m)),e((c,d,n)))\in E
, which implies that

|\mu_{\pi}(e)(\{a,b\})\cap\mu_{\pi}(e)(\{c,d\})|=|\pi(e(a,b,m))\cap\pi(e(c,d,n))|=1,

that is,
(\mu_{\pi}(e)(\{a,b\}),\mu_{\pi}(e)(\{c,d\}))\in E
.

Let
(\{a,b\},\{c,d\})\in N
, that is,
\{a,b\}\cap\{c,d\}=\emptyset
. We may again assume that
a<b
and
c<d
. By definition,
((a,b,0),(c,d,0))\in N
, and thus
(e((a,b,0)),e((c,d,0)))\in N
. This implies that
\mu_{\pi}(e)(\{a,b\})\cap\mu_{\pi}(e)(\{c,d\})=\emptyset
, that is,
(\mu_{\pi}(e)(\{a,b\}),\mu_{\pi}(e)(\{c,d\}))\in N
.

Now we show that
\pi
is a finite covering map. A map
\gamma\colon Y\to Y
is an automorphism of
\mathfrak{Y}
if and only if
\gamma
is bijective and both
\gamma
and
\gamma^{-1}
are
endomorphisms of
\mathfrak{Y}
. In this case,
\mu_{\pi}(\gamma)
is also bijective, and it follows from the second part of the lemma, which we have already shown above, that both
\mu_{\pi}(\gamma)
and
(\mu_{\pi}(\gamma))^{-1}=\mu_{\pi}(\gamma^{-1})
are endomorphisms of
({{\mathbb{Q}}\choose 2},E,N)
. Therefore,
\mu_{\pi}(\gamma)\in\operatorname{Aut}({{\mathbb{Q}}\choose 2},E,N)=\operatorname{Aut}(\mathfrak{J})
.

Conversely, if
\gamma^{\prime}
is an automorphism of
\mathfrak{J}
then it is of the form
\gamma^{\prime}=\tilde{\alpha}
for some
\alpha\in\operatorname{Sym}({\mathbb{Q}})
(Remark
4.7
). Consider the function
\gamma
which maps

to
(\alpha(a),\alpha(b),m)
if
\alpha(a)<\alpha(b)
and to
(\alpha(b),\alpha(a),m+1)
if
\alpha(b)<\alpha(a)
. It is clear that
\gamma
preserves
D
, and its action on
{{\mathbb{Q}}\choose 2}
is
\gamma^{\prime}
. We claim that
\gamma
is an automorphism of
\mathfrak{Y}
. The only nontrivial thing we have to check is that
\gamma
preserves the relation
E
. Let
((a,b,m),(c,d,n))\in E
. Then
(a,b)_{m}=(c,d)_{n}
. Let
u:=\alpha((a,b)_{m})=((c,d)_{n})
. If
\alpha(a)<\alpha(b)
then

, and if
\alpha(b)<\alpha(a)
then
u=(\alpha(b),\alpha(a))_{m+1}
. Similarly, if
\alpha(c)<\alpha(d)
then
u=(\alpha(c),\alpha(d))_{n}
, and if
\alpha(d)<\alpha(c)
then
u=(\alpha(d),\alpha(c))_{n+1}
. By checking all four cases one can verify that
(\gamma(a,b,m),\gamma(c,d,n))\in E
. This implies that the action of
\operatorname{Aut}(\mathfrak{Y})
on

is exactly the automorphisms group of
\mathfrak{J}
, and concludes the proof that

is a finite covering map.
∎

For

\circlearrowright_{S}

Proof.

It is clear that every element of

such that

and
\alpha(b)=a

Lemma 4.19
.

Let

is a closed normal subgroup of

4.3.

For

Proposition 4.24

The structure

is homogeneous, finitely bounded, has a binary signature, is Ramsey, and NIP.

Proof (sketch).

The proof of homogeneity and finite boundedness is essentially the same as the proof of Theorem 19 in
[

]

The natural action of

Lemma 4.25

Let

Let

(\mathbb{Q};<,S,T)
and
\mathfrak{A}
are bi-embeddable. Let us consider the map

e\colon\mathbb{Q}\mapsto\mathbb{Q}\times[2],r\mapsto\begin{cases}(r,1)&if $r\in S$,\\
(r,2)&if $r\in T$.\end{cases}

This embeds
(\mathbb{Q};<,S,T)
into
\mathfrak{A}
. The existence of an embedding in the other direction follows from the fact that
\operatorname{Age}(\mathfrak{A})\subseteq\operatorname{Age}(\mathbb{Q};<,S,T)
.
∎

Note that
(\mathbb{Q};<)
interprets the structure
\mathfrak{A}
with domain
A=\mathbb{Q}\times[2]
from Lemma
4.30
.
The following lemma establishes a connection between
(\mathbb{Q};<,S,T)
and
S(2)
.

Lemma 4.31
.

Let
c\in\mathbb{S}
be arbitary. Then
(S(2);c)
interprets
(\mathbb{Q};<,S,T)
, and
S(2)
is isomorphic to a quantifier-free reduct of
(\mathbb{Q};<,S,T)
.

Proof.

Let us consider the structure
(\mathbb{S}\setminus\{c\};<,S,T)
where
S=\{a:c\prec a\}
,
T=\{a:c\succ a\}
and

\displaystyle<\,\coloneqq\{(a,b):\bigl((a\in S\Leftrightarrow b\in S)\wedge a\prec b\bigr)\vee\bigl((a\in S\Leftrightarrow b\in T)\wedge a\succ b\bigr)\}.

We can think of the relation
<
in the following way. We identify each point in
T
with its opposite point and then consider the restriction of the order defined on the half-circle containing
S
going from
c
to its opposite point. This implies immediately that
<
linearly orders
S\cup T=\mathbb{S}\setminus\{c\}
and both
S
and
T
are dense according to
<
. Therefore,
(\mathbb{S}\setminus\{c\};<,S,T)
is isomorphic to
(\mathbb{Q};<,S,T)
, and thus
(\mathbb{Q};<,S,T)
is interpretable in
(S(2);c)
.

For the second claim we can reverse the construction, i.e., we can define
\prec
as

\prec\,\coloneqq\{(a,b):\bigl(a<b\wedge(a\in S\Leftrightarrow b\in S\bigr)\vee\bigl(a>b\wedge(a\in S\Leftrightarrow b\in T\bigr)\}

This defines
S(2)|_{\mathbb{S}\setminus c}
which, by the homogeneity of
S(2)
, is isomorphic to
S(2)
.
∎

Remark 4.32
.

Our constructions given in the proof of Lemma
4.31
come from the correspondence described in Proposition 6.1 in
[
29
]
.

Corollary 4.33
.

S(2)
is the model companion of a structure in
\I(({\mathbb{Q}};<))
.

Proof.

By Lemma
4.30
we know that
({\mathbb{Q}};<,S,T)
is the model companion of a structure
\mathfrak{A}
with an interpretation in
({\mathbb{Q}};<)
, and by Lemma
4.31
we know that
S(2)
is isomorphic to a quantifier-free reduct of
({\mathbb{Q}};S,T)
. Hence, if we use the same formulas to form a quantifier-free reduct
\mathfrak{B}

of
\mathfrak{A}
, then
the same embeddings that show that
({\mathbb{Q}};<,S,T)
is a companion of
\mathfrak{A}
show that
S(2)
is a companion of
\mathfrak{B}
.
Since the structure

S(2)
is homogeneous, it is the model companion of
\mathfrak{B}
, and hence a model companion of a structure with a first-order definition in
({\mathbb{Q}};<)
.
∎

4.3.2.
Non-interpretability results

Here
we show that none of the three structures
(\mathbb{Q};<)*(\mathbb{Q};<)
,
(\mathbb{Q};<,S,T)
, and
S(2)
is interpretable in
(\mathbb{Q};<)
. By Lemmas
4.29
,
4.30
, and Corollary
4.33
we know that each of these structures are model companions of structures interpretable in
(\mathbb{Q};<)
, so
any of these three structures shows that
\I((\mathbb{Q};<))
it not closed under taking model companions.

We first show that every order which is definable in
\mathcal{J}^{<}(d)
equals a lexicographic order up to a permutation of the coordinates and the reversal of the order of some of the coordinates. We say that an interval of
\mathbb{Q}
is
non-degenerate
if it contains at least 2 elements.

Lemma 4.34
.

Let
d\in\mathbb{N}
, let
I_{1}<I_{2}<\dots<I_{d}
be some non-degenerate intervals in
\mathbb{Q}
,
and let
\prec
be an order which is first-order definable in
(\mathbb{Q}_{\nearrow}^{d};<_{1},\dots,<_{d})
. Then there are
k\in[d]
and
R\in\{\prec,\succ\}
such that for all
\bar{a},\bar{b}\in\mathcal{I}\coloneqq\prod_{i=1}^{d}I_{i}
the relation
a_{k}<b_{k}
implies
\bar{a}R\bar{b}
.

Proof.

We first prove the following statement by induction on
m
:

There exists some
j\in[m]
and
R\in\{\prec,\succ\}
such that for all
\bar{a},\bar{b}\in{\mathcal{I}}
such that
a_{j}<b_{j}
and
a_{i}=b_{i}
for all
i>m
we have
\bar{a}R\bar{b}
.
(\ast)

For
m=d
we obtain the statement of the lemma.

For
m=0
there is nothing to prove. For the induction step from
m-1
to
m
, let us fix some tuple
\bar{c}\in\mathbb{Q}_{\nearrow}^{d}
, and let
j
be the index for which the induction hypothesis holds. We define

\displaystyle\gamma\colon

\displaystyle I_{j}\times I_{m}\rightarrow\mathbb{Q}^{d}

\displaystyle(a_{1},a_{2})\mapsto(c_{1},\dots,c_{j-1},a_{1},c_{j+1},\dots,c_{m-1},a_{2},c_{m+1},\dots,c_{d}).

Claim.
For all
(a_{1},a_{2}),(b_{1},b_{2})\in I_{j}\times I_{m}
either
\operatorname{tp}(a_{1},b_{1})
or
\operatorname{tp}(a_{2},b_{2})
determines the
\prec
-order of
\gamma(a_{1},a_{2})
and
\gamma(b_{1},b_{2})
.

Pick some elements
s_{1},t_{1},u_{1},v_{1}\in I_{j}
and
s_{2},t_{2},u_{2},v_{2}\in I_{m}
with
s_{1}<t_{1}<u_{1}<v_{1}
and
s_{2}<t_{2}<u_{2}<v_{2}
. Suppose first that the pairs
(\gamma(t_{1},t_{2}),\gamma(v_{1},v_{2}))
and
(\gamma(t_{1},t_{2}),\gamma(v_{1},s_{2}))
are ordered in the same way according to
\prec
. We assume that
\gamma(t_{1},t_{2})\prec\gamma(v_{1},v_{2})
, the other case is symmetric. Then
\operatorname{tp}(\gamma(t_{1},t_{2}),\gamma(u_{1},u_{2}))=\operatorname{tp}(\gamma(t_{1},t_{2}),\gamma(v_{1},v_{2}))
and
\operatorname{tp}(\gamma(u_{1},u_{2}),\gamma(v_{1},t_{2}))=\operatorname{tp}(\gamma(t_{1},t_{2}),\gamma(v_{1},s_{2}))
. Therefore,
\gamma(t_{1},t_{2})\prec\gamma(u_{1},u_{2})\prec\gamma(v_{1},t_{2})
. This implies that for all
a_{1},a_{2},b_{1},b_{2}
as in our assumption, if
a_{1}<b_{1}
, then
\gamma(a_{1},a_{2})\prec\gamma(b_{1},b_{2})
. Similarly we are done in the case when the pairs
(\gamma(t_{1},t_{2}),\gamma(v_{1},v_{2}))
and
(\gamma(t_{1},t_{2}),\gamma(s_{1},v_{2}))
are ordered in the same way according to
\prec
. In the remaining case we get that the pairs
(\gamma(t_{1},t_{2}),\gamma(s_{1},v_{2}))
and
(\gamma(t_{1},t_{2}),\gamma(v_{1},s_{2}))
are ordered in the same way according to
\prec
. However, this is impossible, because
\operatorname{tp}(\gamma(t_{1},t_{2}),(v_{1},s_{2}))=\operatorname{tp}(\gamma(s_{1},v_{2}),(t_{1},t_{2}))
.

By reversing the order
\prec
if necessary we can assume without loss of generality that either
a_{1}<b_{1}
or
a_{2}<b_{2}
implies
\gamma(a_{1},a_{2})\prec\gamma(b_{1},b_{2})
for all
a_{1},a_{2},b_{1},b_{2}
as above. We handle the two cases separately.

Case 1.
a_{1}<b_{1}
implies
\gamma(a_{1},a_{2})\prec\gamma(b_{1},b_{2})
for all
(a_{1},a_{2}),(b_{1},b_{2})\in I_{j}\times I_{m}
.
First note that if
c^{\prime}\in I_{m}
and
a_{1}<b_{1}
then for
\bar{u}=\gamma(a_{1},c^{\prime}),\bar{v}=\gamma(a_{2},c^{\prime})
we have
\bar{u}\prec\bar{v}
and
(\bar{u}=\bar{v})\supseteq[d]\setminus[m-1]
. Thus, by the induction hypothesis for all tuples
\bar{u},\bar{v}\in I
with
u_{k}<v_{k}
and
u_{i}=v_{i}
for all
i\geq m
we have
\bar{u}\prec\bar{v}
.

Let us now consider some tuples
\bar{u},\bar{v}\in{\mathcal{I}}
such that
u_{k}<v_{k}
and
u_{i}=v_{i}
for all
i\geq m+1
. We have to show that
\bar{u}\prec\bar{v}
. Let us pick a
\bar{w}\in{\mathcal{I}}
such that
u_{k}<w_{k}<v_{k},w_{m}=u_{m}
and
w_{i}=v_{i}
for
i\neq j,m
. Then by our argument above it follows that
\bar{u}\prec\bar{w}
. On the other hand, if we pick some
(a_{1},a_{2}),(b_{1},b_{2})\in I_{j}\times I_{m}
such that
a_{1}<b_{1}
and
\operatorname{tp}(a_{2},b_{2})=\operatorname{tp}(u_{m},v_{m})=\operatorname{tp}(w_{m},v_{m})
then we have
\operatorname{tp}(\bar{w},\bar{v})=\operatorname{tp}(\gamma(a_{1},a_{2}),\gamma(b_{1},b_{2}))
. By our assumption we have
\gamma(a_{1},a_{2})\prec\gamma(b_{1},b_{2})
. Therefore
\bar{w}\prec\bar{v}
. Combining this with the previous inequality we obtain
\bar{u}\prec\bar{v}
.

Case 2.
a_{2}<b_{2}
implies
\gamma(a_{1},a_{2})\prec\gamma(b_{1},b_{2})
for all
(a_{1},a_{2}),(b_{1},b_{2})\in I_{j}\times I_{m}
.
By the induction hypothesis there exists some
R\in\{<,>\}
such that for all tuples
\bar{u},\bar{v}\in{\mathcal{I}}
if
u_{k}Rv_{k}
then
\bar{u}\prec\bar{v}
. Let
\bar{u},\bar{v}\in{\mathcal{I}}
such that
u_{m}<v_{m}
and
u_{i}=v_{i}
for
i\geq m+1
. We have to show that
\bar{u}\prec\bar{v}
. Let
\bar{w}\in{\mathcal{I}}
such that
w_{m}=u_{m},u_{k}Rw_{k}
and
v_{i}=w_{i}
for
i\neq j,m
. Then by the definition of
R
we have
\bar{u}\prec\bar{w}
. On the other hand, if we pick some
(a_{1},a_{2}),(b_{2},b_{2})\in I_{j}\times I_{m}
such that
b_{1}<b_{2}
and
\operatorname{tp}(a_{1},a_{2})=\operatorname{tp}(w_{k},v_{k})
then
\operatorname{tp}(\bar{w},\bar{v})=\operatorname{tp}(\gamma(a_{1},b_{1}),\gamma(a_{2},b_{2}))
. This implies
\bar{w}\prec\bar{v}
, and therefore
\bar{u}\prec\bar{v}
.
∎

Lemma 4.35
.

Let
d\in\mathbb{N}
and let
\prec
be an order which is first-order definable in
\mathcal{J}^{<}(d)
. Then there are
R_{1},\dots,R_{d}\in\{<,>\}
and
\sigma\in\operatorname{Sym}([d])
such that for all
\bar{a},\bar{b}\in\mathbb{Q}_{\nearrow}^{d}
we have
\bar{a}\prec\bar{b}
if and only if there exists some
j\leq d
such that

\{\sigma(1),\dots,\sigma({j-1})\}\subseteq(\bar{a}=\bar{b})
, and
a_{\sigma(j)}R_{j}b_{\sigma(j)}
.

Proof.

We show by induction on
m\in{\mathbb{N}}
,
m\leq d
, that we can choose some
R_{1},\dots,R_{m}\in\{<,>\}
and distinct
\sigma(1),\dots,\sigma(m)\in[d]
such that for all
\bar{a},\bar{b}\in\mathbb{Q}_{\nearrow}^{d}
, whenever there exists some
j\leq m
such that
(\bar{a}=\bar{b})\supseteq\{\sigma(1),\dots,\sigma(j-1)\}
and
a_{\sigma(j)}R_{j}b_{\sigma(j)}
, then
a_{j}\prec b_{j}
.
({\dagger}_{m})
.

For
m=d
we obtain the statement of the lemma.

We prove the statement by induction on
m
. For
m=0
there is nothing to prove.

For the induction hypothesis let us assume we already chose some
R_{1},\dots,R_{m}
and
\sigma(1),\dots,\sigma(m)
such that
({\dagger}_{m})
holds. We introduce the following notation for “shuffling” tuples.
For
\bar{u}\in\mathbb{Q}^{d-m}
and
\bar{c}\in\mathbb{Q}^{m}
we write
\bar{u}\oplus_{\sigma}\bar{c}
for the tuple whose
\sigma(i)
-th coordinate is
c_{i}
for all
i\in[m]
, and such that the tuple formed by listing all the other coordinates in order is
\bar{u}
. Let us fix
\bar{c}\in\mathbb{Q}^{m}
such that

D\coloneqq\{\bar{u}:\bar{u}\oplus_{\sigma}\bar{c}\in\mathbb{Q}_{\nearrow}^{d}\}\neq\emptyset.

Note that in this case
\{\bar{u}\oplus_{\sigma}\bar{c}:\bar{u}\in\mathbb{Q}^{d-m})\}\cap\mathbb{Q}_{\nearrow}^{d}
is a
\prec
-interval by the induction hypothesis. Let us choose some non-degenerate intervals
I_{1}<\dots<I_{d-m}
of
\mathbb{Q}
such that
\mathcal{I}\coloneqq\prod_{i=1}^{d-m}I_{i}\subseteq D
. Note that for
\bar{u},\bar{v}\in E
the
((<_{ij}))_{i,j\in[d]}
-type of the pair
(\bar{u}\oplus_{\sigma}\bar{c},\bar{v}\oplus_{\sigma}\bar{c})
is uniquely determined by the
((<_{i}))_{i\in[d]}
-type of the pair
(\bar{u},\bar{v})
. This implies that the order

\prec^{*}\coloneqq\{(\bar{u},\bar{v}):\bar{u}\oplus_{\sigma}\bar{c}\prec\bar{v}\oplus_{\sigma}\bar{c})

is definable in
(I;<_{1},\dots,<_{d})
. Therefore, by Lemma
4.34
we obtain that there exists some
k
and some
R\in\{<,>\}
such that
u_{k}Rv_{k}
implies
\bar{u}\prec^{*}\bar{v}
for all
\bar{u},\bar{v}\in\mathcal{I}
. Since all sequences of intervals
(I_{1},\dots,I_{d-m})
as above can be mapped to each other by an automorphism of
\operatorname{Aut}(\mathbb{Q})
fixing
\bar{c}
, it follows that in fact the value
k
does not depend on our particular choice of the intervals
I_{i}
.

Now we show that in fact for all
\bar{u},\bar{v}\in D
the relation
u_{k}Rv_{k}
implies
\bar{u}\prec^{*}\bar{v}
. For simplifying the notation we assume that
R
is
<
; the proof of the other case is analogous. So suppose that
u_{k}<v_{k}
. We define the tuples
\bar{u}^{0},\dots,\bar{u}^{d},\bar{v}^{0},\dots,\bar{v}^{d}

as follows. Let
J

be a inclusion-wise minimal non-degenerate interval whose endpoints are elements of
\bar{c}
. Let
u_{i}<u_{i+1}<\dots<u_{j}
be the elements of
\bar{u}
which are contained in
J
. Note that in this case
v_{i}<v_{i+1}<\dots<v_{j}
are also exactly the elements of
\bar{v}
in
J
. Let us pick some elements
w_{i}<\dots<w_{j}
such that

•

if
\ell<k
then
w_{\ell}<\min(u_{i},v_{i})

•

if
\ell>k
then
w_{\ell}>\max(u_{j},v_{j})

•

if
\ell=k
then
u_{k}<w_{k}<v_{k}
.

For
\ell\in\{i,\dots,j\}\setminus\{k\}
we define

u^{n}_{\ell}\coloneqq\begin{cases}w_{\ell}&if $\ell<\min(k,i+n)$ or $\ell>\max(k,j-n)$\\
u_{\ell}&otherwise.\end{cases}

The element
v^{n}_{\ell}
is defined analogously. Finally, we define the elements
u^{n}_{k}
and
v^{n}_{k}
in such a way that

u_{k}=u^{0}_{k}<u^{1}_{k}<\dots<u^{d}_{k}<u_{k+1},w_{k},

and

v_{k}=u^{0}_{k}>v^{1}_{k}>\dots>v^{d}_{k}>v_{k-1},w_{k}.

Note that by definition all tuples
\bar{u}^{n}
,
\bar{v}^{n}
, and
\bar{w}
are in
D
, because they all have the same type over
\bar{c}
. Moreover, it follows from our choice that for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
the intervals
[[u^{n}_{\ell},u^{n+1}_{\ell}]]:\ell\in[d-m]
are pairwise disjoint. By making these intervals slightly bigger we are in the setting of Lemma
4.34
. Since
{u^{n}_{k}<u^{n+1}_{k}}
, we have that
\bar{u}^{n}\prec^{*}\bar{u}^{n+1}
. A similar argument shows that
\bar{u}^{d}\prec^{*}\bar{w}
. Therefore, we get
\bar{u}\prec^{*}\bar{w}
. By repeating the argument for the
v
-tuples we obtain
\bar{w}\prec^{*}\bar{v}
. Therefore,
\bar{u}\prec^{*}\bar{v}
, as we claimed.

Now we can finish the induction step. We pick
\sigma({m+1})
to be the position of the
k
-th coordinate after applying the shuffling map
\bar{u}\mapsto\bar{u}\oplus_{\sigma}\bar{c}
, and we put
R_{m+1}\coloneqq R
. Then clearly
\sigma(m+1)\not\in\{\sigma(1),\dots,\sigma(m)\}
. Now let
\bar{a},\bar{b}\in\mathbb{Q}_{\nearrow}^{d}
be arbitrary. If
(\bar{a}=\bar{b})\not\supseteq\{\sigma(1),\dots,\sigma(m)\}
then we are done by the induction hypothesis. Otherwise, there exist some
\bar{u},\bar{v}\in\mathbb{Q}_{\nearrow}^{d-m}
and
\bar{c}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{Q}_{\nearrow}^{m}
such that
\bar{a}=\bar{u}\oplus\bar{c}^{\prime}
and
\bar{b}=\bar{v}\oplus\bar{c}^{\prime}
. Let us assume that
a_{\sigma(m+1)}Rb_{\sigma(m+1)}
. We have to show that
\bar{a}\prec\bar{b}
. This is enough to show in the case where
\bar{c}^{\prime}=\bar{c}
. In this case
u_{k}<v_{k}
, and therefore by our claim above
\bar{u}\prec^{*}\bar{v}
, that is,
\bar{a}=\bar{u}\oplus\bar{c}\prec\bar{v}\oplus\bar{c}=\bar{b}
.
∎

Our next goal is to show that
(\mathbb{Q};<,S,T)
is not interpretable in
(\mathbb{Q};<)
. In our first step we want to use Lemma
4.25
but we want to reduce the number
\ell
as much as possible. In order to do this, assuming that
(\mathbb{Q};<,S,T)
is a reduct of
\mathcal{J}^{<}(\bar{d})
, we colour the elements of
\mathbb{Q}
according to their first coordinates. We first need the following general observation about colourings of
\mathbb{Q}
.

Lemma 4.36
.

Let
\lambda\colon\mathbb{Q}\rightarrow[n]
be a colouring of
\mathbb{Q}
such that
\lambda(S)\cap\lambda(T)=\emptyset
. Then there exists a non-degenerate interval
I
and some colours
s\in\lambda(S)
and
t\in\lambda(T)
such that both
\lambda^{-1}(s)
and
\lambda^{-1}(t)
are dense in
I
.

Proof.

We prove this by induction on
n
. For
n=2
there is nothing to prove. Otherwise, by relabelling the colours we can assume that
\lambda(S)\subseteq[k]
and
\lambda(T)\subseteq[n]\setminus[k]
for some
k\in[n-1]
. Then either
k\geq 2
or
n-k\geq 2
. Without loss of generality we may assume that the latter holds.

Let us first assume that
\lambda^{-1}(n)
is dense in
\mathbb{Q}
. In this case, let
\lambda^{\prime}\coloneqq a\mapsto\min(\lambda(a),n-1)
, and let us apply the induction hypothesis to the coloring
\lambda^{\prime}
. We can conclude that there exists some non-degenerate interval
I
and some
s\in S
such that
\lambda^{-1}(s)
is dense in
I
, and by our assumption
\lambda^{-1}(n)
is dense in
I
.

Now let us assume that
\lambda^{-1}(n)
is not dense in
\mathbb{Q}
.
Then we can find some non-degenerate interval
I
such that
\lambda^{-1}(n)\cap I=\emptyset
. By shrinking the interval
I
further we can assume that it is open.
Then
(I;{<}|_{I},S\cap I,T\cap I)
is isomorphic to
(\mathbb{Q};<,S,T)
and thus we can apply the induction hypothesis to the colouring
\lambda|_{I}
which by our assumption avoids the colour
n
.
∎

Now we are ready to show one of the main results of this section.

Theorem 4.37
.

(\mathbb{Q};<)
does not interpret
(\mathbb{Q};<,S,T)
.

Proof.

Suppose for contradiction that
\mathbb{Q}
interprets
(\mathbb{Q};<,S,T)
. By Lemma
4.25
we know that there exists a
\bar{d}\in{\mathbb{N}}^{\ell}
,
a first-order reduct
\mathfrak{C}
of

\mathcal{J}^{<}({\bar{d}})
,
and an isomorphism
\alpha\colon\mathfrak{C}\to(\mathbb{Q};<,S,T)
. Let us now consider the colouring
\lambda:=\pi_{1}\alpha^{-1}\colon{\mathbb{Q}}\to[\ell]
. Note that for a given
i\in[\ell]
all elements in
\pi_{1}^{-1}(i)
lie in the same orbit in
\operatorname{Aut}(\mathfrak{C})
. This implies that
\lambda(S)\cap\lambda(T)=\emptyset
, and thus we can apply Lemma
4.36
. Let
s\in S,t\in T
and
I^{\prime}
be the interval from the conclusion of Lemma
4.36
. We can assume without loss of generality that
s=1
and
t=2
. Let
\mathfrak{B}=\mathcal{J}^{<}(d_{1},d_{2})\subseteq\mathcal{J}^{<}(\bar{d})
, and let
\prec\coloneqq\alpha_{B}^{-1}(<)
and
I\coloneqq\alpha_{B}^{-1}(I^{\prime})
. Then by our construction
\prec
is definable in
\mathfrak{B}
,
I
is a
\prec
-interval in
B
, and
\pi_{1}^{-1}(1)
and
\pi_{1}^{-1}(2)
are both dense in
(I;\prec)
. By abusing notation we also write
S=\pi_{1}^{-1}(1)
and
T=\pi_{1}^{-1}(2)
.

Let us now apply Theorem
4.35
to the order
{\prec}|_{S}
.
We can conclude that there exist some non-degenerate subinterval
J^{\prime}
of
I
, some
j_{1}\in[d_{1}]
, and a relation
R_{1}\in\{<,>\}
such that for all
(1,a),(1,b)\in J^{\prime}
we have
(a=b)\supseteq[d_{1}]\setminus\{j_{1}\}
and
a\prec b
if and only if
a_{j_{1}}R_{1}b_{j_{1}}
. By repeating the argument for the order
{\prec}|_{T}
we can find some non-degenerate subinterval
J
of
J^{\prime}
, some
j_{2}\in[d_{2}]
, and a relation
R_{2}\in\{<,>\}
such that for all
(2,a),(2,b)\in J
we have
(a=b)\supseteq[d_{2}]\setminus\{j_{1}\}
and
a\prec b
if and only if
a_{j_{2}}R_{2}b_{j_{2}}
. Define

\alpha_{i}\colon J\cap S\rightarrow\mathbb{Q},(1,a)\mapsto a_{j_{1}},\alpha_{2}\colon J\cap T\rightarrow\mathbb{Q},(2,a)\mapsto a_{j_{2}},\alpha\coloneqq\alpha_{1}\cup\alpha_{2}.

Note that the maps
\alpha_{1}
and
\alpha_{2}
are both either order-preserving or order-reversing, and the images
\alpha_{1}
and
\alpha_{2}
are both intervals in
(\mathbb{Q};<)
. Let

A\coloneqq\{a_{1},\dots,a_{j_{1}-1},a_{j_{1}+1},\dots,a_{d_{1}},b_{1},\dots,b_{j_{2}-1},b_{j_{2}+1},\dots,b_{d_{2}}\}

for either (or equivalently for all)
(1,a),(2,b)\in J
. By shrinking the interval
J
even further we can assume without loss of generality that the image of
\alpha
is disjoint from
A
. Note that in this case the type of any pair
(u,v)\in J^{2}
is determined by the types of
u
and
v
and
(\alpha(u),\alpha(v))
. Now if the intervals
\alpha(J\cap S)=\alpha_{1}(J\cap S)
and
\alpha(J\cap T)=\alpha_{2}(J\cap T)
are disjoint then each pair
(u,v)\in(J\cap S,J\cap T)
has the same type with respect to
\mathfrak{B}
and thus also in
(I,\prec,S,T)
which contradicts our assumption that both
S
and
T
are dense in
J
. So we can assume that this is not the case, i.e.,
\alpha(J\cap S)\cap\alpha(J\cap T)\neq\emptyset
. Let
r\in\alpha(J\cap S)\cap\alpha(J\cap T)
and put
u\coloneqq\alpha_{1}^{-1}(r),v\coloneqq\alpha_{2}^{-1}(r)
. We can assume without loss of generality that
u\prec v
. Since
S
is dense in
J
we obtain that there exists some
w\in J\cap S
such that
u\prec w\prec v
. Let us first assume that
\alpha_{1}(w)>\alpha_{1}(u)
, the other case is analogous. In this case
\alpha_{1}
is order-preserving. Let
u^{\prime}\in J\cap S
be arbitrary. If
\alpha_{1}(u^{\prime})\leq\alpha_{1}(u)
then
u^{\prime}\preceq u\prec v
. On the other hand, if
\alpha_{1}(u^{\prime})>\alpha_{1}(u)
then the pairs
(w,v)
and
(u^{\prime},v)
have the same type with respect to
\mathfrak{B}
. In particular,
u^{\prime}\prec v
. Since the same argument can be repeated for all
v\in T
we obtained that for all
(u,v)\in(J\cap S)\times(J\cap T)
the inequality
u\prec v
holds. In this case however neither
S
nor
T
is dense in
J
, a contradiction.
∎

Lemma
4.30
and Theorem
4.37
imply the following.

Corollary 4.38
.

\I((\mathbb{Q};<))
is not closed under taking model companions.

We next show that
(\mathbb{Q};<)
using Theorem
4.37
that the generic permutation and the dense local order are also not interpretable in
(\mathbb{Q};<)
.

Lemma 4.39
.

(\mathbb{Q};<)*(\mathbb{Q};<)
interprets
(\mathbb{Q};<,S,T)
.

Proof.

We write
<_{1}
and
<_{2}
for the two orders of
(\mathbb{Q};<)*(\mathbb{Q};<)
. Let
I=\operatorname{id}_{D}
where

D=\{(a,b)\in(\mathbb{Q};<)*(\mathbb{Q};<):a<_{1}b\}.

We define the relations
<_{1},S,T
on
D
as follows.

\displaystyle(a,b)<(c,d)

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow(a<c\vee(a=c\wedge b<d))

\displaystyle(a,b)\in S

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow a<_{2}b

\displaystyle(a,b)\in T

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow a>_{2}b.

Clearly,
I
is an interpretation of
(D;<,S,T)
in
(\mathbb{Q};<)*(\mathbb{Q};<)
. We now show that
(D;<,S,T)
is isomorphic to
(\mathbb{Q};<,S,T)
. It is clear that
(D;<)
is isomorphic to
(\mathbb{Q};<)
and
(S,T)
is a partition of
D
. It remains to show that
S
and
T
are dense in
D
. For this it is enough to show that
S
and
T
intersects all intervals of the form
J=[(a,b),(a,c)]:a<b<c
. By the genericity of
(\mathbb{Q};<)*(\mathbb{Q};<)
we can find
d,e
such that
a<_{1}b<_{1}d<_{1}e<_{1}c
and
d<_{2}b<_{2}e<_{2}
. Then by definition
(a,d),(a,e)\in J,(a,e)\in S,(a,d)\in T
, so
J\cap S,J\cap T\neq\emptyset
. This shows that
(D;<,S,T)
is isomorphic to
(\mathbb{Q};<,S,T)
which implies that
(\mathbb{Q};<)*(\mathbb{Q};<)
interprets
(\mathbb{Q};<,S,T)
.
∎

Corollary 4.40
.

(\mathbb{Q};<)
does not interpret
(\mathbb{Q};<)*(\mathbb{Q};<)
.

Proof.

Follows from Theorem
4.37
and Lemma
4.39
.
∎

Corollary 4.41
.

(\mathbb{Q};<)
does not interpret
S(2)
.

Proof.

Suppose for contradiction that
(\mathbb{Q};<)
interprets
S(2)
. Let
c\in\mathbb{S}
. Then by Corollary
2.23
we know that
(\mathbb{Q};<)
also interprets
(S(2),c)
, and thus by the first statement of Lemma
4.31
also
(\mathbb{Q};<,S,T)
. However, this is impossible by Theorem
4.37
.
∎

Finally, we show a strengthening of Corollary
4.41
which will be relevant in our discussion in Subection
4.3.3
.
By
[
8
, Section 9]
, we know that the structure
S(2)
has five nontrivial first-order reducts. However, four of these are bidefinable with reducts of
(\mathbb{Q};<)
. The automorphism group of the remaining one can be written as the union of
\operatorname{Aut}(S(2))
and the set of anti-automorphism of
S(2)
, i.e., the bijections of
S(2)
reversing
\prec
. On the relational side, this first-order reduct, similarly as in the case of
(\mathbb{Q};<)
, can be described by the betweenness relation
\operatorname{Betw}
defined as

\operatorname{Betw}\coloneqq\{(x,y,z)\in\mathbb{S}^{3}:x\prec y\prec z\vee z\prec y\prec x\}.

For the sake of completeness we provide a proof for this correspondence, and then we show that
(\mathbb{S},\operatorname{Betw})
is not interpretable in
(\mathbb{Q};<)
.

Proposition 4.42
.

\operatorname{Aut}(\mathbb{S};\operatorname{Betw})
is the union of the set of automorphisms and anti-automorphisms of
S(2)
.

Proof.

Clearly, every automorphism and anti-automorphism of
S(2)
preserves
\operatorname{Betw}
.
For the other direction, choose
\alpha\in\operatorname{Aut}(\mathbb{S};\operatorname{Betw})
and

a,b,c,d\in\mathbb{S}
arbitrarily such that
a\prec b
and
c\prec d
. We have to show that the pairs
(\alpha(a),\alpha(b))
and
(\alpha(c),\alpha(d))
are ordered in the same way. It follows from the definition of
\prec
that we can find
e,f\in\mathbb{S}
such that
a\prec b\prec e\prec f\prec c\prec d
. Then
(a,b,e),(b,e,f),(e,f,c),(f,c,d)\in\operatorname{Betw}
. Since
\alpha
preserves
\operatorname{Betw}
, this implies that either

\displaystyle\alpha(a)

\displaystyle\prec\alpha(b)\prec\alpha(e)\prec\alpha(f)\prec\alpha(c)\prec\alpha(d)

\displaystyle\text{ or }\quad\alpha(a)

\displaystyle\succ\alpha(b)\succ\alpha(e)\succ\alpha(f)\succ\alpha(c)\succ\alpha(d),

which implies what we wanted to show.
∎

Proposition
4.42
immediately implies the following.

Corollary 4.43
.

Let
c,d\in\mathbb{S},c\neq d
. Then
\operatorname{Aut}(\mathbb{S};\operatorname{Betw})_{c,d}=\operatorname{Aut}(\mathbb{S};\prec)_{c,d}
; in particular,
(\mathbb{S};\prec)
is a first-order reduct of
(\mathbb{S};\operatorname{Betw},c,d)
.

Corollary 4.44
.

(\mathbb{Q};<)
does not interpret
(\mathbb{S};\operatorname{Betw})
.

Proof.

Suppose for contradiction that
(\mathbb{Q};<)
interprets
(\mathbb{S};\operatorname{Betw})
. Let
c,d\in\mathbb{S},c\neq d
. Then by Corollary
2.23
we know that
(\mathbb{Q};<)
also interprets
(\mathbb{S};\operatorname{Betw},c,d)
, and thus also
S(2)
by Corollary
4.43
. This contradicts Lemma
4.41
.
∎

4.3.3.
Slow unlabelled growth and interpretability

Recall that we denote the number of orbits of the natural action of
\operatorname{Aut}(\mathfrak{A})
on
n

by

By Theorem 10.4 in

we know (by stating the contrapositive) that if an
\omega
-categorical structure
\mathfrak{A}
is not interpretable in

then there exists some polynomial

for all

is

meaning that outside

is close to being as slow as possible. A slightly slower unlabelled growth is attained by

and its betweenness reduct. In the case of

[

. As far as we know

has the slowest possible unlabelled growth outside

Conjecture 4.45
.

Let
\mathfrak{A}
be an
\omega
-categorical structure which is not interpretable in

. Then

We note that the theorem we cited above and Conjecture

. Then there exists some polynomial
p

Interestingly,

also shows up as the primitive structure with the slowest known unlabelled growth apart from

and

Conjecture 4.47

[

Let

5.
Lachlan’s Class

As we have seen in the previous section,
the class of structures interpretable over equality is not closed under taking model-complete cores. The closure of this class under taking model-complete cores is contained in an interesting class of structures that was introduced and studied by Lachlan; in fact, we conjecture that Lachlan’s class
equals
the
class of structures that are interdefinable with model-complete cores of structures interpretable over equality (Conjecture
5.4
).

Adapting terminology from Remark
3.42
, we say that a structure
\mathfrak{A}
is
strongly minimal
if its theory is. Note that in a strongly minimal
\omega
-categorical structure
\mathfrak{A}
all definable subsets (with parameters) are finite or cofinite. We say that
\mathfrak{A}
is
strictly minimal
if it is strongly minimal and has primitive automorphism group.
The following is a consequence/reformulation of the Cherlin-Mills-Zilber theorem about strictly minimal sets (
[
30
]
, Theorem 2.1).

Theorem 5.1
.

Let
\mathfrak{A}
be a strictly minimal
\omega
-categorical structure. Then one of the following holds.

(1)

\mathfrak{A}
is interdefinable with
(A;=)
.

(2)

\mathfrak{A}
is a first-order reduct of some infinite affine space over a finite field.

(3)

\mathfrak{A}
is a first-order reduct of some infinite projective space over a finite field.

Following
Lachlan
[
49
]
, we denote by
\mathcal{D}
the class of
\omega
-categorical
\omega
-stable structures
\mathfrak{A}
such that all strictly minimal structures which are interpretable in
\mathfrak{A}
with parameters are bidefinable with a pure set, and we call it
Lachlan’s class
. This class can be characterized in many equivalent ways as shown by the following theorem.

Theorem 5.2
.

For any structure
\mathfrak{A}
, the following are equivalent.

(1)

\mathfrak{A}\in\mathcal{D}
.

(2)

\mathfrak{A}
is stable and interpretable over
({\mathbb{Q}};<)
.

(3)

\mathfrak{A}
is
\omega
-stable and a first-order reduct of a finitely homogeneous relational structure.

(4)

\mathfrak{A}
is
\omega
-stable and a reduct of a finitely bounded homogeneous Ramsey structure.

Proof.

The equivalence (
1
)
\Leftrightarrow
(
2
) follows from
[
49
]
, Theorem 4.6.

The implication (
2
)
\Rightarrow
(
4
) follows Corollary
2.18
.

The implication (
4
)
\Rightarrow
(
3
) is trivial.

(
3
)
\Rightarrow
(
1
). This is essentially due to
[
51
, Theorem3.4]
. We show the contrapositive. Let us assume that
\mathfrak{A}\not\in\mathcal{D}
. Then by Theorem
5.1
we know that
\mathfrak{A}
interprets a nontrivial first-order reduct of the affine space or the projective space. The automorphism group of these are contained in
P\Gamma L(\omega,q)
or
A\Gamma L(\omega,q)
for some
q
(i.e., the group of semi-projective/semi-affine transformations). Since
|P\Gamma L(\omega,q):PGL(\omega,q)|=|A\Gamma L(\omega,q):AGL(\omega,q)|
is finite (it is the dimension of
\mathbb{F}_{q}
over its prime field), by adding finitely many constants we obtain that
\mathfrak{A}
interprets some infinite projective of affine space over a finite field. If we add a constant to the affine space we get a vector space, and if we fix a line in a projective space and a point outside this line we also get a vector space. Thus, in both cases we obtain that
\mathfrak{A}
interprets an infinite vector space. However, by Theorem 1.1 in
[
51
]
we know that a finitely homogeneous relational structure cannot interpret any infinite group, a contradiction.
∎

Corollary 5.3
.

Lachlan’s class
\mathcal{D}
is closed under taking model-complete cores.

Proof.

Follows from the equivalence (
1
)
\Leftrightarrow
(
4
) in Theorem
5.2
, Corollary
3.27
and Theorem
2.16
.
∎

Conjecture 5.4
.

Lachlan’s class equals to the class of structures that are interdefinable with a model-complete core of a structure interpretable over equality.

Note that a positive answer to Conjecture
5.4
would imply a positive answer to Walsberg’s question of whether every structure in Lachlan’s class is
trace minimal
(i.e., trace definable in the theory of
(\mathbb{Q},=)
, cf.
[
71
, Section 9.4]
and
[
72
, p.157]
). Conjecture
5.4
can equivalently be phrased as follows.

Conjecture 5.5
.

Every structure in Lachlan’s class is homomorphically equivalent to a structure interpretable over equality.

To prove that Conjecture
5.5
is equivalent to Conjecture
5.4
, we first prove the following lemma.

Lemma 5.6
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a class of

\omega
-categorical
structures that is closed under taking first-order reducts. Then every structure in
\M({\mathcal{C}})
is homomorphically equivalent to some structure in
\mathcal{C}
.

Proof.

Let
\mathfrak{A}
be a structure
which is interdefinable with the model-complete core
\mathfrak{C}
of some structure
\mathfrak{B}\in\mathcal{C}
.
We may assume that
\mathfrak{C}
is a substructure of
\mathfrak{B}
.
The definition of
\mathfrak{A}
in
\mathfrak{C}
can be made existential positive, because
\mathfrak{C}
is a model-complete core.
Let
\mathfrak{D}
be the reduct of
\mathfrak{B}
obtained by evaluating the existential positive formulas in
\mathfrak{B}
rather than
\mathfrak{C}
. By assumption,
\mathfrak{D}\in{\mathcal{C}}
.
Moreover,
\mathfrak{A}
is a substructure of
\mathfrak{D}
,
and the homomorphism from
\mathfrak{B}
to
\mathfrak{C}
is also a homomorphism from
\mathfrak{D}
to
\mathfrak{A}
. Hence,
\mathfrak{A}
and
\mathfrak{D}
are homomorphically equivalent.∎

Proposition 5.7
.

Conjecture
5.4
is equivalent to Conjecture
5.5
.

Proof.

Lemma
5.6
shows that Conjecture
5.4
implies Conjecture
5.5
.
Now assume Conjecture
5.5
.
Let
\mathfrak{A}
be in Lachlan’s class.
The expansion

\mathfrak{A}^{*}
of
\mathfrak{A}
by all first-order definable relations is also contained in Lachlan’s class.
Moreover,
\mathfrak{A}^{*}
is
a model-complete core, and by Conjecture
5.5
is is homomorphically equivalent to a structure interpretable over equality. Thus,
\mathfrak{A}^{*}
is the model-complete core of a structure interpretable over equality, and

\mathfrak{A}^{*}
is interdefinable with

, which proves Conjecture

.
∎

6.
Conclusion

Theorem
6.1
below summarises various results of this article.

Let
\mathcal{E}
be the class of all structures that are first-order reducts of binary finitely bounded homogeneous NIP Ramsey structures.

Theorem 6.1
.

We have the following inclusions and strict inclusions.

\I(({\mathbb{Q}};=))\subset\MI(({\mathbb{Q}};=))\subseteq\mathcal{D}\subset\I((\mathbb{Q};<))\subset\MI((\mathbb{Q};<))\subseteq{\mathcal{E}}

Proof.

The inclusion
\I(({\mathbb{Q}};=))\subseteq\MI(({\mathbb{Q}};=))
is trivial, since the expansion of every structure in
\I(({\mathbb{Q}};=))

by all first-order definable relations is
a model-complete core.
The inclusion is strict by Corollary
4.38
.
To see the inclusion
\MI(({\mathbb{Q}};=))\subseteq{\mathcal{D}}
, first recall that
every structure in
\I(({\mathbb{Q}};=))

is stable (Theorem
3.1
) and hence in
{\mathcal{D}}
by Theorem
5.2
. The claim now follows, because
{\mathcal{D}}
is closed under taking model-complete cores (Corollary
5.3
).

The inclusion
{\mathcal{D}}\subseteq\I((\mathbb{Q};<))
holds by Theorem
5.2
, and the inclusion is strict, because
(\mathbb{Q};<)
itself is not stable and therefore not in
{\mathcal{D}}
.
The inclusion
\I((\mathbb{Q};<))\subseteq\MI((\mathbb{Q};<))
is again trivial, and strict by Corollary
4.38
.
Finally, for the inclusion
\MI((\mathbb{Q};<))\subseteq{\mathcal{E}}
see Remark
4.26
.
∎

Conjecture
5.4

Question 6.2

Is it true that the class

This question is open even if we drop the requirements in the definition of

Question 6.3

Is every homogeneous NIP structure with a finite relational signature of arity at most two interdefinable with a structure in

Note that Question
