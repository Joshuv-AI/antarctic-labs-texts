Computing an approximation of the partial Weyl closure of a holonomic module
Hadrien Brochet

Abstract
. The Weyl closure is a basic operation in algebraic analysis: it converts a system of differential operators with rational coefficients into an equivalent system with polynomial coefficients. In addition to encoding finer information on the singularities of the system, it serves as a preparatory step for many algorithms in symbolic integration. A new algorithm is introduced to approximate the partial Weyl closure of a holonomic module, where the closure is taken with respect to a subset of the variables. The method is based on a non-commutative generalization of Rabinowitsch’s trick and yields a holonomic module included in the Weyl closure of the input system. The algorithm is implemented in the Julia package MultivariateCreativeTelescoping.jl and shows substantial speedups over existing exact Weyl closure algorithms in Singular and Macaulay2 .



1.
Introduction

Algebraic analysis is the area of mathematics that studies systems of linear partial differential equations (PDEs)
with polynomial coefficients from an algebraic point of view.
In this context, such systems are represented by a left ideal in the
n
th Weyl algebra

(1)

W_{\mathbf{x}}=K[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]\langle\partial_{1},\dots,\partial_{n}\rangle.

One important application of algebraic analysis is the study of parametric integrals through the construction of systems of PDEs that they satisfy.
This has been shown to be always possible when the integrand
f
is holonomic
(
23
)
,
meaning that it satisfies a “maximally overdetermined" system of PDEs.
Algorithms for performing this computation exist
(
37
;
31
;
9
)
.
However, these algorithms require as input generators of the annihilator of the integrand in
W_{\mathbf{x}}
:

(2)

\ann_{W_{\mathbf{x}}}(f)=\{P\in W_{\mathbf{x}}\mid P\cdot f=0\}.

A common approach to compute
\ann_{W_{\mathbf{x}}}(f)
is to first compute
the annihilator
\ann_{W_{\mathbf{x}}(\mathbf{x})}(f)
of
f
in the rational Weyl algebra

(3)

W_{\mathbf{x}}(\mathbf{x})=K(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\langle\partial_{i},\dots,\partial_{n}\rangle

and then recover
\ann_{W_{\mathbf{x}}}(f)
via the intersection

(4)

\ann_{W_{\mathbf{x}}}(f)=\ann_{W_{\mathbf{x}}(\mathbf{x})}(f)\cap W_{\mathbf{x}}.

This intersection computation is a particular instance of the Weyl closure defined as follows.
Let
S\subset W_{\mathbf{x}}^{r}
be a left submodule for some integer
r
.
The Weyl closure of
S
, denoted by
\cl(S)
, is the left
W_{\mathbf{x}}
-module

(5)

\cl(S)=W_{\mathbf{x}}(\mathbf{x})S\,\cap\,W_{\mathbf{x}}^{r}.

Although an algorithm for computing Weyl closures exists
(
40
)
,
its practical complexity is often a bottleneck in symbolic integration,
motivating the search for efficient approximation approaches.

A recent integration algorithm
(
9
)

assumes that the parameters
\mathbf{t}=(t_{1},\dots,t_{m})
of the integrals are rational variable, while the
integration variables
\mathbf{x}
are polynomial variables, and takes as input generators of

\ann_{W_{t,\mathbf{x}}(\mathbf{t})}(f)
where

W_{t,\mathbf{x}}(\mathbf{t})=\mathbb{K}(\mathbf{t})[\mathbf{x}]\langle\partial_{\mathbf{t}},\partial_{\mathbf{x}}\rangle
.
This motivates extending the definition of Weyl closure to partial Weyl closure.
Let
S\subseteq W_{\mathbf{t},\mathbf{x}}(\mathbf{t})^{r}
be a left submodule.
The partial Weyl closure of
S
with respect to
\mathbf{x}
is

(6)

\cl_{\mathbf{x}}(S)=W_{\mathbf{t},\mathbf{x}}(\mathbf{t},\mathbf{x})S\cap W_{\mathbf{t},\mathbf{x}}(\mathbf{t})^{r},

where
W_{\mathbf{t},\mathbf{x}}(\mathbf{t},\mathbf{x})=\mathbb{K}(\mathbf{t},\mathbf{x})\langle\partial_{\mathbf{t}},\partial_{\mathbf{x}}\rangle
.

Related work

The problem of computing generators of
\cl(S)
given
S\subset W_{\mathbf{x}}^{r}
was first introduced by Chyzak and Salvy
(
14
)

for the case
r=1
, under the name of extension/contraction problem.
This problem was later solved by Tsai for general
r
in his PhD thesis
(
41
;
40
)
.
He showed
(
40
, Theorem 2.2.1)
that the Weyl closure of
S\subset W_{\mathbf{x}}^{r}
can be computed by inverting a single polynomial
p
vanishing on the singular locus of
S
:

(7)

\cl(S)=W_{\mathbf{x}}[1/p]S\cap W_{\mathbf{x}}^{r}.

This yields an algorithm when combined with the localization algorithm of Oaku, Takayama, and Walther
(
30
)

and Oaku’s algorithm for computing the singular locus of
S

(
32
)
.

The Weyl closure is also related to desingularization.
For
n=1
the singularities of an operator
L\in W_{x}
are the roots of its leading coefficient,
and a desingularization of
L
at a singularity
\alpha
is an operator
L^{\prime}\in W_{x}
for which there exist
M\in W_{x}(x)
such that
L^{\prime}=ML
and
L^{\prime}
does not have a singularity at
\alpha
.
This notion was extended to systems of PDEs by Chen, Kauers, Li and Zhang
(
12
)
.
The Weyl closure corresponds to the smallest ideal containing all desingularizations of the initial system.
Algorithms for the desingularization of Ore and differential operators in the univariate case have been studied extensively; see, for instance,
(
2
;
13
;
22
;
43
)
.

The computation of annihilators of holonomic functions in the rational Weyl algebra
W_{\mathbf{x}}(\mathbf{x})
is already well understood.
Systems of differential equations are known for basic functions and algorithmic closure by sum, product and more
(
17
;
38
)

exist for deducing systems for more complicated ones.
These algorithms are implemented in
Maple

(
15
)
,
Mathematica

(
26
)
, and
Sage

(
24
)
.
Although similar closure properties for sum and product exist in the Weyl algebra
(
31
)
(see also
(
34
, Sec.3)
),
their computational efficiency seem unclear. For example, the closure by product requires to double the number of variables and eliminate half of them by Gröbner basis computation, which is known to be costly.

Contributions

The notion of holonomicity is extended to
W_{\mathbf{t},\mathbf{x}}(\mathbf{t})
-modules
and a new algorithm is proposed for computing an holonomic approximation of the partial Weyl closure.
That is, given a finite rank module
S\subset W_{\mathbf{t},\mathbf{x}}(\mathbf{t})^{r}
, the algorithm computes a module
S^{\prime}
such that
S^{\prime}\subseteq\cl(S)

and
W_{\mathbf{t},\mathbf{x}}(\mathbf{t})^{r}/S^{\prime}
is holonomic.
The algorithm proceeds by iteratively constructing a sequence of modules

(8)

S_{1}\subseteq S_{2}\subseteq\cdots\subseteq\cl(S)

with the property that there exists
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
such that
S_{\ell}=\cl(S)
.
The condition
S_{i}=\cl(S)
could, in principle, be verified using an extension of the
b
-function associated with a weight vector
(
36
, Chapter 5.1)
.
However, computing this function is known to be expensive, so a weaker condition is used instead, yielding an approximation algorithm.
One possible choice is to stop at the smallest
i\in\mathbb{N}
such that
W_{\mathbf{t},\mathbf{x}}(\mathbf{t})^{r}/S_{i}
is holonomic.
Note that, in practice, obtaining only a holonomic approximation does not seem to be an issue for symbolic integration;
closure algorithms under sums and products, both in the polynomial and rational Weyl algebra, are already not guaranteed to return the full annihilator.

The algorithm is based on a generalization of Rabinowitsch’s trick to D-modules,
which consists in introducing a new variables
T
thought of as
1/p
for some
p\in\mathbb{K}(\mathbf{t})[\mathbf{x}]
,
together with the additional equation
pT-1=0
.
The commutation rule between
T
and
\partial_{\mathbf{x}},\partial_{\mathbf{t}}
are not well suited for computing Gröbner basis
in
W_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{t}}(\mathbf{t})[T]^{r}
for an order eliminating
T
.
This problem is tackled by computing Gröbner bases in
W_{\mathbf{t},\mathbf{x}}(\mathbf{t})[T]^{r}
seen as an infinite-rank
W_{\mathbf{t},\mathbf{x}}(\mathbf{t})
-module
(
37
)
.
Then, saturations of
S
with respect to
p
are computed iteratively until the stopping condition is reached.
Lastly, the algorithm has been implemented in the
Julia
package MultivariateCreativeTelescoping.jl
(
11
)
.
It compares very favorably to existing implementation, both because of a better Gröbner basis implementation and because of an algorithmic improvement.

Compared with Tsai’s approach, the present algorithm avoids several computationally expensive steps.
Tsai’s algorithm first computes generators of the
W_{\mathbf{x}}
-module
W_{\mathbf{x}}[1/p]S
using the localization algorithm
of Oaku, Takayama, and Walther
(
30
)
, and then recovers the Weyl closure
by performing the intersection
W_{\mathbf{x}}[1/p]S\cap W_{\mathbf{x}}^{r}
.
The localization step requires the computation of a
b
-function associated with a weight vector of a suitable ideal,
which is known to be difficult in general.
Moreover, simplifications with respect to the polynomial
p
are postponed until the final intersection step.
In particular, when
S
is an ideal annihilating a function
f
, the localization algorithm returns an ideal
S^{\prime}

together with an integer
\ell
such that
S^{\prime}
annihilates
f/p^{\ell}
, which typically leads to a significant blow-up
in the size of expressions compared to
S
and
\cl(S)
.
This issue is avoided in the present algorithm by performing simplifications with respect to
p
as early as possible
through iterative saturation.

2.
Preliminaries

2.1.
Weyl algebras and holonomicity

Polynomial and rational Weyl algebra

Let
\mathbb{K}
be a field of characteristic zero. Let
W_{\mathbf{x}}
denote the
n
th Weyl algebra

\mathbb{K}[\mathbf{x}]\langle\bm{\partial}_{\mathbf{x}}\rangle
with generators
\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
and

\bm{\partial}_{\mathbf{x}}=(\partial_{1},\dots,\partial_{n})
, subject to the relations

\partial_{i}x_{i}=x_{i}\partial_{i}+1,\quad x_{i}x_{j}=x_{j}x_{i},\quad\partial_{i}\partial_{j}=\partial_{j}\partial_{i},\quad x_{i}\partial_{j}=\partial_{j}x_{i}\quad(i\neq j).

The rational Weyl algebra
W_{\mathbf{x}}(\mathbf{x})=\mathbb{K}(\mathbf{x})\langle\bm{\partial}_{\mathbf{x}}\rangle
is defined similarly,
with the commutation rules

\partial_{i}\partial_{j}=\partial_{j}\partial_{i},\quad\partial_{i}r(\mathbf{x})=r(\mathbf{x})\partial_{i}+\frac{\partial r(\mathbf{x})}{\partial x_{i}},

for all
r(\mathbf{x})\in\mathbb{K}(\mathbf{x})
and
i\in\{1,\dots,n\}
.
More generally, we consider the mixed Weyl algebra
W_{\mathbf{t},\mathbf{x}}(\mathbf{t})
,
where
\mathbf{t}
are rational variables and
\mathbf{x}
are polynomial variables.

Any element
P\in W_{\mathbf{t},\mathbf{x}}(\mathbf{t})
admits a unique decomposition

P=\sum_{{\bm{\alpha}},{\bm{\beta}},{\bm{\gamma}}}a_{{\bm{\alpha}},{\bm{\beta}},{\bm{\gamma}}}(\mathbf{t})\,\mathbf{x}^{\bm{\alpha}}\bm{\partial}_{\mathbf{x}}^{\bm{\beta}}\bm{\partial}_{\mathbf{t}}^{\bm{\gamma}},

where the sum is finite and

a_{{\bm{\alpha}},{\bm{\beta}},{\bm{\gamma}}}(\mathbf{t})\in\mathbb{K}(\mathbf{t})
.

The degree of
P
is defined by

\deg(P)=\max\{|{\bm{\alpha}}|+|{\bm{\beta}}|+|{\bm{\gamma}}|\mid a_{{\bm{\alpha}},{\bm{\beta}},{\bm{\gamma}}}(\mathbf{t})\neq 0\}.

This definition extends naturally to free left modules.
Throughout this article, all modules are left modules, and the adjective “left” is omitted when no confusion may arise.
A submodule
S\subset W_{\mathbf{t},\mathbf{x}}(\mathbf{t})^{r}
is said to be of finite rank
if the vector space

W_{\mathbf{t},\mathbf{x}}(\mathbf{t},\mathbf{x})^{r}\big/\big(W_{\mathbf{t},\mathbf{x}}(\mathbf{t})\,S\big)

is finite-dimensional over
\mathbb{K}(\mathbf{t})
.
A module is finite-rank if and only if its Weyl closure is holonomic

(
38
)
.

Holonomic
W_{\mathbf{x}}
-modules.

Let
S
be a submodule of
W_{\mathbf{x}}^{r}
, and set
M=W_{\mathbf{x}}^{r}/S
.
The Bernstein filtration
(
4
)

of the algebra
W_{\mathbf{x}}
is the sequence of
\mathbb{K}
-vector spaces
(\mathcal{F}_{m})_{m\geq 0}

defined by

\mathcal{F}_{m}=\{P\in W_{\mathbf{x}}\mid\deg(P)\leq m\}.

A filtration of
M
adapted to
(\mathcal{F}_{m})_{m\geq 0}
is given by the sequence
of
\mathbb{K}
-linear subspaces
\Phi_{m}\subseteq M
defined by

\Phi_{m}=\{P\cdot e_{i}+S\mid P\in\mathcal{F}_{m},\ 1\leq i\leq r\},

where
P\cdot e_{i}+S
denotes the image of
P\cdot e_{i}
in the quotient

W_{\mathbf{x}}^{r}/S
.
These filtrations are compatible with the algebra and module structures:

\mathcal{F}_{m}\mathcal{F}_{m^{\prime}}\subseteq\mathcal{F}_{m+m^{\prime}}
and

\mathcal{F}_{m}\Phi_{m^{\prime}}\subseteq\Phi_{m+m^{\prime}}
.

There exists a unique polynomial
p\in\mathbb{K}[m]
, called the Hilbert polynomial of
M
,
such that
\dim_{\mathbb{K}}(\Phi_{k})=p(k)
for all sufficiently large
k
.
The dimension of
M
is defined as the degree
d
of
p
.
The integer
d
clearly lies between
0
and
2n
. Bernstein proved that if

M
is non-zero, then
d\geq n

(
4
)
.
The module
M
is said to be holonomic if
d=n
or if
M=0
.

The holonomicity of
M
can be tested algorithmically from a Gröbner basis of
S
by means of
Theorem

1
.
Although the precise origin of this criterion is unclear, one implication appears in Zeilberger
(
42
)
,
and the second condition was used as the definition of holonomicity by Kauers
(
25
)
.
A complete proof is given in
(
10
, Theorem 80)
.

Theorem 1

(Folklore)
.

Let
S
be a non-trivial submodule of
W_{\mathbf{x}}^{r}
, let
G
be a Gröbner basis of
S
,
and let
e_{1},\dots,e_{r}
be the canonical basis of
W_{\mathbf{x}}^{r}
.
The following statements are equivalent:

(1)

The module
W_{\mathbf{x}}^{r}/S
is holonomic.

(2)

For every subset
A\subseteq\{\mathbf{x},\bm{\partial}\}
with
|A|=n+1
and every
i\in\llbracket 1,r\rrbracket
,
the vector space
S\cap\mathbb{K}\langle A\rangle e_{i}
is non-zero.

(3)

For every subset
A\subseteq\{\mathbf{x},\bm{\partial}\}
with
|A|=n+1
and every
i\in\llbracket 1,r\rrbracket
,
there exists
g\in G
such that
\lm(g)\in\mathbb{K}\langle A\rangle e_{i}
.

The notation
\llbracket 1,r\rrbracket
in the above theorem denotes the set
\{1,\dots,r\}
.

2.2.
Singular Locus

Principal symbol

Let
S\subset W_{\mathbf{t},\mathbf{x}}(\mathbf{t})^{r}
be a left submodule, and let
(\bm{0},\bm{1})

denote the weight vector assigning weight
0
to the variables
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}

and weight
1
to the variables
\partial_{x_{1}},\dots,\partial_{x_{n}},\partial_{t_{1}},\dots,\partial_{t_{m}}

(see
(
36
, Chapter 1.2)
for a gentle introduction when
m=0
).
Let

P=\sum_{{\bm{\alpha}},{\bm{\beta}},{\bm{\gamma}},i}a_{{\bm{\alpha}},{\bm{\beta}},{\bm{\gamma}},i}(\mathbf{t})\,\mathbf{x}^{{\bm{\alpha}}}\bm{\partial}_{\mathbf{x}}^{{\bm{\beta}}}\bm{\partial}_{\mathbf{t}}^{{\bm{\gamma}}}e_{i}\in W_{\mathbf{t},\mathbf{x}}(\mathbf{t})^{r},

with
a_{{\bm{\alpha}},{\bm{\beta}},{\bm{\gamma}},i}(\mathbf{t})\in\mathbb{K}(\mathbf{t})
and set

c=\max\left\{|{\bm{\beta}}|+|{\bm{\gamma}}|\;\middle|\;\exists\,{\bm{\alpha}},i\text{ such that }a_{{\bm{\alpha}},{\bm{\beta}},{\bm{\gamma}},i}\neq 0\right\}.

The principal symbol (or initial form) of
P
with respect to

(\bm{0},\bm{1})
is the element in
\mathbb{K}[\mathbf{x},{\bm{\xi}},{\bm{\zeta}}]^{r}
defined by

(9)

\ini_{(\bm{0},\bm{1})}(P)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\bm{\alpha}},i\\
|{\bm{\beta}}|+|{\bm{\gamma}}|=c\end{subarray}}a_{{\bm{\alpha}},{\bm{\beta}},{\bm{\gamma}},i}\,\mathbf{x}^{{\bm{\alpha}}}{\bm{\xi}}^{{\bm{\beta}}}{\bm{\zeta}}^{{\bm{\gamma}}}e_{i},

where
\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{n},\zeta_{1},\dots,\zeta_{m}
are commuting variables corresponding to

\partial_{x_{1}},\dots,\partial_{x_{n}},\partial_{t_{1}},\dots,\partial_{t_{m}}
.

Initial module and characteristic variety

The initial module

\ini_{(\bm{0},\bm{1})}(S)\subset\mathbb{K}[\mathbf{x},{\bm{\xi}},{\bm{\zeta}}]^{r}

is the ideal generated by the initial forms of all elements of
S
.
The characteristic variety of the
W_{\mathbf{t},\mathbf{x}}(\mathbf{t})
-module

M=W_{\mathbf{t},\mathbf{x}}(\mathbf{t})^{r}/S

is defined as

\charv(M)=V\!\left(\ini_{(\bm{0},\bm{1})}(S)\right)\;\subset\;\mathbb{C}^{2n+m}.

Singular locus

The singular locus of
S
is a set-theoretic overestimation
of the singularities (with respect to
\mathbf{x}
) of the solutions of
S
.
It plays a crucial role in the study of Weyl closure, as stated in
Theorem

1
.
Formally, it is defined as the Zariski closure of the image of

\charv(M)\setminus\{\xi_{1}=\cdots=\xi_{n}=0,\zeta_{1}=0,\dots,\zeta_{m}=0\}

under the coordinate projection

\mathbb{C}^{2n+m}\to\mathbb{C}^{n},\qquad(\mathbf{x},{\bm{\xi}},{\bm{\zeta}})\mapsto\mathbf{x}.

Equivalently, it is defined as the zero set of the elimination ideal

\sing(I)=V\left(\left(\ini_{(\bm{0},\bm{1})}(S):\langle\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{n},\zeta_{1},\dots,\zeta_{m}\rangle^{\infty}\right)\cap\mathbb{C}[\mathbf{x}]\right),

where, for a module
A\subset\mathbb{K}[\mathbf{x},{\bm{\xi}},{\bm{\zeta}}]^{r}
, and an ideal
B\subset\mathbb{K}[\mathbf{x},{\bm{\xi}},{\bm{\zeta}}]
the saturation of
A

with respect to
B
is given by

(10)

A:B^{\infty}=\left\{a\in\mathbb{K}[\mathbf{x},{\bm{\xi}},{\bm{\zeta}}]^{r}\;\middle|\;\exists\,k\in\mathbb{N}\text{ such that }B^{k}a\subset A\right\}.

An algorithm for computing the singular locus was proposed by
Oaku
(
32
)
.

3.
Holonomic
W_{\mathbf{t},\mathbf{x}}(\mathbf{t})
-modules

In view of
Theorem

1
, the notion of holonomicity can be extended to

W_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{t}}(\mathbf{t})
-modules as follows.

Definition 1.

Let
M=W_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{t}}(\mathbf{t})^{r}/S
be a
W_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{t}}(\mathbf{t})
-module and let
e_{1},\dots,e_{r}
be the canonical basis of
W_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{t}}(\mathbf{t})^{r}
.
The module
M
is said to be holonomic if for every subset

A\subseteq\{\mathbf{x},\partial_{\mathbf{x}},\partial_{\mathbf{t}}\}
with
|A|=n+1

and every
i\in\llbracket 1,r\rrbracket
, the intersection
S\cap\mathbb{K}(\mathbf{t})\langle A\rangle e_{i}
is non-zero.

When
m=0
this definition coincides with the standard definition of holonomic
W_{\mathbf{x}}
-modules by
Theorem

1
,
and when
n=0
it coincides with the definition of D-finite modules,
that is, modules that are finite-dimensional over
\mathbb{K}(\mathbf{t})
.
The equivalence between the second and third point of
Theorem

1
,
which gave an algorithmic criterion for testing holonomicity, naturally extends to this setting.

Lemma 2
.

Let
S
be a non-trivial
W_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{t}}(\mathbf{t})
-module and let
G
be a Gröbner basis of
S
.
The module
M=W_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{t}}(\mathbf{t})^{r}/S
is holonomic if and only if for every subset
A\subseteq\{\mathbf{x},\partial_{\mathbf{x}},\partial_{\mathbf{t}}\}

with
|A|=n+1
and every
i\in\llbracket 1,r\rrbracket
,
there exists
g\in G
such that
\lm(g)\in\mathbb{K}(\mathbf{t})\langle A\rangle e_{i}
.

The classical equivalence between D-finiteness and holonomicity
(
23
;
38
)
,
also generalizes to the present setting.

Theorem 3
.

Let
S
be a submodule of
W_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{t}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{t})^{r}
. The following statements are equivalent:

(1)

W_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{t}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{t})^{r}/S
is
D
-finite.

(2)

W_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{t}}(\mathbf{t})^{r}/(S\cap W_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{t}}(\mathbf{t})^{r})
is holonomic as a
W_{\mathbf{t},\mathbf{x}}(\mathbf{t})
-module

(3)

W_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{t}}^{r}/(S\cap W_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{t}}^{r})
is holonomic as a
W_{\mathbf{t},\mathbf{x}}
-module

Proof.

The theorem is proved in the case
r=1
; the general case follows by the same arguments but requires heavier notation.
It is well known that (1) and (3) are equivalent:
the implication
(1)\Rightarrow(3)
is proved by
constructing a suitable filtration
(
38
, Appendix)
,
and the converse is a straightforward application of

Theorem

1
.
It therefore suffices to prove
(3)\Rightarrow(2)
and
(2)\Rightarrow(1)
. They are consequences of
Definitions

1
and
1
.
Only the implication
(3)\Rightarrow(2)
is detailed; the other one is analogous.

Let
A\subseteq\{\mathbf{x},\partial_{\mathbf{x}},\partial_{\mathbf{t}}\}
be a set of cardinality
n+1
.
Then
A\cup\{\mathbf{t}\}
has cardinality
n+m+1
.
Applying
Theorem

1
to
S\cap W_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{t}}^{r}
and the set
A\cup\{\mathbf{t}\}
yields that the
\mathbb{K}
-vector space

\mathbb{K}\langle A\cup\{\mathbf{t}\}\rangle\cap\left(S\cap W_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{t}}^{r}\right)

is non-zero.
This implies that the
\mathbb{K}(\mathbf{t})
-vector space

\mathbb{K}(\mathbf{t})\langle A\rangle\cap\left(S\cap W_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{t}}(\mathbf{t})^{r}\right)

is non-zero, which proves (2).
∎

4.
Partial Weyl closure by saturation

The goal of this section is to prove
Theorem

1
,
which states that the partial Weyl closure of a module
S\subset W_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{t}}(\mathbf{t})^{r}

can be computed by inverting a single polynomial vanishing on the singular locus of
S
.
A similar result was proved by Tsai
(
40
, Theorem 2.2.1)

in the case where no parameter
\mathbf{t}
is present.
The argument given here is a straightforward extension of his approach to this mixed setting.

Theorem 1
.

Let
S
be a submodule of
W_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{t}}(\mathbf{t})^{r}
and let
f\in\mathbb{K}(\mathbf{t})[\mathbf{x}]

be a polynomial vanishing on
\sing(S)
.
Then,

(11)

\cl_{\mathbf{x}}(S)=W_{\mathbf{t},\mathbf{x}}(\mathbf{t})[1/f]S\cap W_{\mathbf{t},\mathbf{x}}(\mathbf{t}).

The proof of
Theorem

1
relies on
Lemma

2
, which is
a mixed Weyl algebra analogue of a classical result in
D
-module theory
(see, for instance,
(
20
, Lemma 6)
).

Lemma 2
.

Let
f\in\mathbb{K}(\mathbf{t})[\mathbf{x}]
and let
S
be a submodule of
W_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{t}}(\mathbf{t})[1/f]^{r}
such that

(12)

M=W_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{t}}(\mathbf{t})[1/f]^{r}/S

is finitely generated as a
\mathbb{K}(\mathbf{t})[\mathbf{x},1/f]
-module.
Then
M
is torsion-free over
\mathbb{K}(\mathbf{t})[\mathbf{x},1/f]
.
Equivalently, for any
a\in\mathbb{K}(\mathbf{t})[\mathbf{x},1/f]
and

g\in W_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{t}}(\mathbf{t})[1/f]^{r}
, the implication

ag\in S\Rightarrow g\in S

holds.

Proof.

Proof of
Lemma

2
.
Let
T
be the torsion module

(13)

T=\{m\in M\mid\exists p\in\mathbb{K}(\mathbf{t})[\mathbf{x},1/f],\,pm=0\}.

We want to prove that
T
is
\{0\}
.
We start by proving that it is a submodule of
M
.
The stability under left multiplication by
\mathbf{x}_{i}
and
1/f
is immediate.
Let
\delta\in\{\bm{\partial}_{\mathbf{x}},\bm{\partial}_{\mathbf{t}}\}
, let
m\in T
,
and choose
p\in\mathbb{K}(\mathbf{t})[\mathbf{x},1/f]
such that
pm=0
.
Multiplying by
\delta
yields

(14)

\delta pm=0=(\delta\cdot p)m+p(\delta\cdot m)

Since

.
Hence there exists

.
Therefore

Since

Let

be generators of

such that

Note that the

in the denominator.
Then the polynomial

annihilates every element of

.
Applying any

gives

0=\delta Pm=(\delta\cdot P)m+P(\delta\cdot m).

Since

, it follows that

annihilates

and hence annihilates

.
Iterating this argument for suitable choices of

, we conclude that

1
annihilates

.
∎

Theorem

1
.

The inclusion

with

can be reformulated in a more algorithmic way using left saturation module

The left saturation module of

with respect to the polynomial

.

The set
S:(f)^{\infty}
is a left submodule of
W_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{t}}(\mathbf{t})^{r}

Proof.

The set
S:(f)^{\infty}
is clearly non-empty, contained in

and stable under addition.
It remains to prove the stability under left multiplication by elements of
W_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{t}}(\mathbf{t})

The stability under multiplication by
\mathbb{K}(\mathbf{t})[\mathbf{x}]

We now check the stability under the action of

Let

and

such that

then a computation shows that

The right-hand side belongs to

.

Let

Example 5
.

We present a simple example illustrating the computation of a Weyl closure.
Consider the rational function
f(x,y)=1/(x^{2}-y^{3})
. It is annihilated by the two operators

(18)

g_{1}=\partial_{x}(x^{2}-y^{3}),\quad g_{2}=\partial_{y}(x^{2}-y^{3}),

which generate a finite-rank ideal in
W_{x,y}
.
These two operators actually generate the annihilator of
f
in
W_{x,y}(x,y)
.
However, they do not generate a holonomic ideal.
A third operator annihilating
f
can be obtained from

g_{1}
and
g_{2}
using a division by
x^{2}-y^{3}
:

(19)

3x\partial_{x}+2y\partial_{y}+1=\frac{1}{x^{2}-y^{3}}(3xg_{1}+2yg_{2}).

The ideal generated by
g_{1},g_{2},g_{3}
corresponds the Weyl closure of

,
which is also the annihilator of

in

.

5.
The algorithm

An algorithm is now described for computing a holonomic approximation of the
partial Weyl closure
\cl_{\mathbf{x}}(S)
.
Following Rabinowitsch’s trick
(
35
)
,
a new variable
T
is introduced, which formally represents
1/f
.
The variable
T
commutes with
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{t}
, and satisfies for all

\ell\in\{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{t}\}

(20)

\partial_{\ell}T=T\partial_{\ell}-f_{\ell}T^{2},

where
f_{\ell}=\frac{\partial f}{\partial\ell}
.
These relations define a new algebra
W_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{t}}(\mathbf{t})[T]^{r}
, whose quotient by

U_{f}=\sum_{i=1}^{r}W_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{t}}(\mathbf{t})[T]\,(fT-1)e_{i}

is isomorphic to
W_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{t}}(\mathbf{t})[1/f]^{r}
.

As shown in
Lemma

1
, left saturation modules can be computed by
eliminating the variable
T
in a suitable submodule of this algebra.

Lemma 1
.

Let
S
be a submodule of
W_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{t}}(\mathbf{t})^{r}
and
f
be a polynomial of
\mathbb{K}(\mathbf{t})[\mathbf{x}]
, then

S:(f)^{\infty}=\left(W_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{t}}(\mathbf{t})[T]S+U_{f}\right)\cap W_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{t}}(\mathbf{t})^{r}.

The proof is based on the following lemma, whose immediate consequence is

(21)

U_{f}=\sum_{i=1}^{r}(fT-1)W_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{t}}(\mathbf{t})[T]e_{i}

Lemma 2
.

The ideal generated by
fT-1
is two-sided, that is

W_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{t}}(\mathbf{t})[T](fT-1)=(fT-1)W_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{t}}(\mathbf{t})[T]

Proof.

The direct inclusion is a consequence of the equality

\partial_{\ell}(Tf-1)=(Tf-1)(\partial_{\ell}+Tf_{\ell})

for
\ell\in\{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{t}\}

and the reverse inclusion follows from the analog formula

(Tf-1)\partial_{\ell}=(\partial_{\ell}-Tf_{\ell})(Tf-1).

∎

Lemma

1
can now be proved.

Proof.

To simplify the notation, the result is proved for
r=1
only, omitting
e_{1}
.
In this case,
U_{f}
becomes
U_{f}=W_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{t}}(\mathbf{t})[T](fT-1)
. Let us begin with the direct inclusion.
Let
L\in S:(f)^{\infty}
, then there exists
i\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
such that
f^{i}L\in S
. Hence,
T^{i}f^{i}L

decomposes as

T^{i}f^{i}L=(T^{i}f^{i}-1)L+L\in W_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{t}}(\mathbf{t})[T]S.

Besides
fT-1
divides
T^{i}f^{i}-1
, which proves by the remark before
Lemma

2
that
(T^{i}f^{i}-1)
is in
U_{f}
.
This proves that
L
is in
W_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{t}}(\mathbf{t})[T]S+U_{f}
. Because
L
is in
W_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{t}}(\mathbf{t})
by definition,
it belongs to the intersection.

Let us now prove the reverse inclusion. Let
L\in(W_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{t}}(\mathbf{t})[T]S+U_{f})\cap W_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{t}}(\mathbf{t})
,
then
L
decomposes as

L=\sum_{i=1}^{p}T^{i}g_{i}+a

with
p\in\mathbb{N}
,
g_{1},\dots,g_{p}\in S
and

. Then

f^{p}L=\sum_{i=1}^{p}(T^{i}f^{i}-1)f^{p-i}g_{i}+\sum_{i=1}^{p}f^{p-i}g_{i}+f^{p}a=g+\tilde{a}

with
g=\sum_{i}f^{p-i}g_{i}\in S
and
\tilde{a}=f^{p}a+\sum_{i=1}^{p}(T^{i}f^{i}-1)f^{p-i}g_{i}
.
Using again that
fT-1
divides
T^{i}f^{i}-1
and
Lemma

2
, we obtain that
\tilde{a}
is in
U_{f}
.
However, neither
f^{p}L
nor
g
involves the variable
T
,
thus
\tilde{a}
is in
U_{f}\cap W_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{t}}(\mathbf{t})
and must be zero. This proves that
f^{p}L
is in
S
.
∎

Lemma

1
suggests a strategy for computing the partial Weyl closure using saturation.
In the commutative case this would be done
by fixing a monomial order eliminating
T
and computing a Gröbner basis with respect to this order.
This is however not possible in this non-commutative setting as there is no order on
W_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{t}}(\mathbf{t})[T]^{r}
eliminating
T

that is compatible with multiplication, in the sense that the product of leading terms is the leading term of the product (recall
Eq.

20
).
This problem is tackled by seeing
W_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{t}}(\mathbf{t})[T]^{r}
as an infinite-rank
W_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{t}}(\mathbf{t})
-module
and performing Gröbner basis computation on truncations of this infinite rank module.
This idea of computing Gröbner bases of truncations of an infinite-rank module has already
been used by Takayama in his integration algorithm
(
37
)
.
As in his original algorithm, the termination criterion used here is based on holonomicity and does not
guarantee that the full saturation module is obtained.

A basis of the
W_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{t}}(\mathbf{t})
-module
W_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{t}}(\mathbf{t})[T]^{r}
is
\{T^{j}e_{i}\mid j\in\mathbb{N},i\in\llbracket 1,r\rrbracket\}
.
The basis elements
T^{0}e_{i}
will be identified with
e_{i}
. The degree of
g\in W_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{t}}(\mathbf{t})[T]^{r}
in
T
,
denoted
\deg_{T}(g)
, is defined as the largest integer
j
that appears in the decomposition of
g
with non-zero coefficient in this basis.
Let
G
be a finite subset of
W_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{t}}(\mathbf{t})[T]^{r}
and
\ell
be the largest degree in
T
appearing in
G
.
Then
W_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{t}}(\mathbf{t})G
is included in the finitely generated
W_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{t}}(\mathbf{t})
-module
\sum_{i=0}^{\ell}\sum_{j=1}^{r}W_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{t}}(\mathbf{t})T^{i}e_{j}

in which it is possible to compute Gröbner bases over
W_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{t}}(\mathbf{t})
.
Coupled with
Lemma

1
this leads to
Section

5
.

.

Section

of a

-module included in

First, note that

By

Let

For this reason, benchmarks for the Gröbner basis implementations used in these three systems are first presented.

All benchmarks were performed on a cluster, where each node is equipped with two Intel(R) Xeon(R) E5-2660 v2 CPUs and 256 GB of RAM.

All computations were run on a single thread when the cluster was not under heavy load,
