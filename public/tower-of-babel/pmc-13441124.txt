Staged Resection and Reconstruction of a 60-kg Giant Plexiform Neurofibroma Involving the Posterior Trunk and Thigh
Authors: Ahmet Bicer, Hande Elif Karadag, Ege Topaloglu, Recep Atakan Gokturk, Efe Uysal, Huseyin Kaya, Celal Cinar, Ilkben Gunusen, Asuman Sargin, Samed Kanbir
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Summary:Neurofibromatosis type 1 is a genetic disease that causes the development of numerous neurofibromas, with large plexiform forms being rare but associated with significant reconstructive challenges. Giant plexiform neurofibromas involving the posterior trunk and lower limbs, especially those exceeding 30 kg, are extremely rare. We report the case of a 41-year-old man with neurofibromatosis type 1 who had a massive plexiform neurofibroma weighing 60 kg. The tumor originated from the posterior trunk and extended to the gluteal and posterior thigh regions, exceeding his own body weight. Branches of the right profunda femoris artery, bilateral iliolumbar arteries, and the bilateral internal iliac arteries were embolized preoperatively in an attempt to control intraoperative bleeding. The tumor was resected in 2 stages and was reconstructed sequentially with negative pressure wound therapy, split-thickness skin grafts, and regional flaps. The postoperative period was complicated by repeated infections and multiple flap failures. However, the final wound closure and stable soft-tissue condition were eventually achieved through a staged multidisciplinary approach. This case highlights the importance of staged resection and individualized reconstructive techniques for the surgical treatment of exceptionally large plexiform neurofibromas, particularly in high-risk patients.

Plexiform neurofibromas (PNFs) are benign but highly vascular tumors associated with neurofibromatosis type 1 (NF1). Giant PNFs (>30 kg) are extremely rare1–3 and pose significant anesthetic and surgical challenges. We present the management of an exceptionally large 60-kg neurofibroma.

CASE PRESENTATION

A 41-year-old man with NF1 presented with a massive PNF extending from the posterior trunk to the gluteal and right posterior thigh region (Fig. 1), causing severe deformity and complete immobility. Treatment had been delayed due to limited access to specialized care and socioeconomic constraints.

Preoperative appearance of the 60-kg PNF extending from the posterior trunk to the gluteal and right posterior thigh regions.

Preoperative evaluation revealed severe mitral regurgitation with an ejection fraction of 20%, markedly elevating anesthetic risk. The etiology remained unclear; NF1-related cardiovascular involvement and chronic tumor-related hemodynamic burden were considered, without a definitive causal relationship. Because of tumor size, computed tomography and magnetic resonance imaging could not be performed; preoperative Doppler ultrasonography demonstrated a hypervascular soft-tissue mass.

Preoperative positioning trials were performed to assess tolerance. Immediately before surgery, selective embolization1 of branches of the right profunda femoris artery, bilateral iliolumbar arteries, and bilateral internal iliac arteries was performed by the interventional radiology team with the aim of reducing intraoperative bleeding. Lower extremity perfusion remained intact; further angiography was avoided to limit contrast burden.

The first staged partial excision was performed by the plastic and orthopedic surgery teams using a plasma knife and unipolar electrocautery. The specimen (Fig. 2) weighed 60 kg; the patient’s postoperative body weight was 40 kg. The right tensor fasciae latae muscle and a portion of the right gluteus maximus muscle were resected because of tumor involvement. Despite embolization, massive intraoperative bleeding required 5 units of erythrocyte suspension. Because of hemodynamic instability, the inferior gluteal–perineal extension was intentionally left in place. The resulting defect of the posterior trunk, gluteal region, and right posterior thigh (Fig. 3) was temporarily covered with split-thickness skin grafts (STSGs) and negative pressure wound therapy (vacuum-assisted closure).4,5 STSGs were harvested from uninvolved skin overlying the tumor due to limited available donor sites.

The excised 60-kg tumor specimen showing its massive size and highly vascular surface after partial resection.

Immediate postoperative defect exposing the posterior trunk, gluteal, and femoral musculature.

A diverting colostomy was performed on postoperative day 18 to prevent fecal contamination of the gluteal wound. A second excision was performed on postoperative day 30, removing the residual gluteal–perineal component. (See figure, Supplemental Digital Content 1, which displays the residual gluteal–perineal tumor component remaining after the initial excision, before the second resection, https://links.lww.com/PRSGO/F146.) The wound was managed conservatively.

Chronic preoperative immobilization and tumor weight had caused prolonged pressure over the right greater trochanter. After debulking, soft-tissue breakdown revealed underlying bone necrosis, resulting in trochanteric exposure. (See figure, Supplemental Digital Content 2, which displays preoperative appearance before biceps femoris muscle flap reconstruction, showing extensive granulating tissue and exposure of the greater trochanter, https://links.lww.com/PRSGO/F147.) On postoperative month 2, a pedicled biceps femoris muscle flap,6 measuring 15 × 10 cm, was rotated to the greater trochanteric defect and covered with STSGs from the right posterior thigh. The donor site was closed primarily. Early venous congestion resulted in partial flap necrosis, managed with serial bedside debridements and local wound care.

Persistent bone exposure prompted reconstruction with a right-sided pedicled anterolateral thigh fasciocutaneous flap,7 measuring 15 × 12 cm, rotated into the defect. The donor site was resurfaced with STSGs. Skin substitutes were avoided because of cost and the anticipated need for further grafting. The same right posterior thigh STSG donor sites were reused. Concomitant trochanteric bone debridement was performed. Within the first postoperative day, severe venous congestion developed; despite immediate take-back and repositioning of the flap into its donor bed, progressive ischemia resulted in total flap necrosis. After clear demarcation, the necrotic tissue was debrided on postoperative day 5. The donor site was resurfaced with STSGs from the left anterior thigh.

Further bone debridement was performed and vacuum-assisted closure therapy reinstated. Subsequently, a right-sided pedicled vertical rectus abdominis myocutaneous flap8,9 based on the deep inferior epigastric artery, measuring 15 × 8 cm, was rotated to the greater trochanteric defect. The abdominal donor site was closed primarily. The flap demonstrated stable perfusion without venous congestion or dehiscence. No clinically significant donor site morbidity was observed, likely related to the persistent immobility. Residual granulating areas were later resurfaced with STSGs from the left scapular region.

Histopathology confirmed a benign PNF with clear margins and no malignant transformation. Tumor size was recorded on the excised specimens, measuring 108 × 66 cm in the initial resection and 22 × 17 cm in the subsequent excision. No recurrence was observed at 19-month follow-up.

The patient necessitated several intensive care admissions. Wound cultures demonstrated multidrug-resistant organisms; antimicrobial therapy was adjusted accordingly. Follow-up postoperative echocardiography showed improved left ventricular function (ejection fraction 50%) with moderate mitral regurgitation.

At discharge, the patient’s systemic condition had markedly improved, with stable flap perfusion and small residual areas requiring local care (Fig. 4). Although nonambulatory, he progressed from complete bed dependence to independent bed mobility and wheelchair use, with improvement in self-care and psychological well-being. At postoperative month 19, the patient weighed 58 kg. He is currently undergoing structured orthopedic rehabilitation with the goal of eventual ambulation.

At 19 months postoperatively, the patient demonstrates stable reconstruction and satisfactory contour, with small residual granulating areas being managed with local dressings.

DISCUSSION

Giant PNFs are rare and surgically challenging because of size, hypervascularity, and systemic compromise. Preoperative selective embolization reduced but did not eliminate bleeding, supporting staged resection in extreme cases and allowing partial control of intraoperative blood loss in highly vascular tumors.

Management of trochanteric exposure was particularly demanding. Complications included partial necrosis of the biceps femoris flap and total necrosis of the anterolateral thigh flap, likely multifactorial, related to systemic compromise, altered venous outflow, and regional perfusion changes after embolization. Escalation to a more proximal pedicled vertical rectus abdominis myocutaneous flap from a nonembolized vascular territory provided durable coverage in a compromised field. Management required balancing surgical goals with the patient’s systemic limitations. This case highlights the importance of staged resection and careful selection of reconstructive options from reliable vascular territories in extensive PNFs.

CONCLUSIONS

Exceptionally large PNFs may pose surgical and reconstructive challenges related to hypervascularity, extensive tissue loss, and systemic fragility. Staged, individualized, and multidisciplinary approaches are essential. Preoperative embolization should be considered adjunctive rather than definitive for bleeding control. In our experience, a proximal pedicled flap from a nonembolized and likely tumor-unaffected vascular territory allowed stable wound closure after failure of regional pedicled flaps, underscoring the value of flexible, case-specific strategies rather than fixed algorithms.
