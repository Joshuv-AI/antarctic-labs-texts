State of Surgical Readmissions Within the Military Health System (2020–2023)
Authors: Felicia Yeboah Denteh, Amanda L. Banaag, Hongyan Wu, Andrew J. Schoenfeld, Tracey Pérez Koehlmoos
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Objective:To evaluate the current state of healthcare disparities within the Military Health System, comparing Department of Defense facilities to treatment outsourced to the civilian sector.Background:Racial disparities in healthcare outcomes remain a critical concern in the US, even in the setting of universal insurance provisions. Despite prior studies suggesting reduced disparities within the Military Health System, challenges such as reduced surgical volume, staffing shortages, and the COVID-19 pandemic may have exacerbated inequalities, particularly among racial and ethnic minorities.Methods:We performed a retrospective cohort study of healthcare claims data evaluating 90-day readmission in adult TRICARE beneficiaries undergoing surgical procedures between 2020 and 2023. The outcome of interest was readmission to an inpatient facility within 90-days of the index surgery, irrespective of the original site of service. Multivariable logistic regression and interaction analyses assessed the association of race, surgical care settings, Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI) score, and patient characteristics with 90-day readmission.Results:A total of 262,344 surgeries among 244,008 beneficiaries were identified for inclusion. Black patients with CCI of 0 were more likely to experience 90-day readmissions to private sector hospitals (adjusted odds ratio [AOR] = 1.19; 95% confidence interval [CI] = 1.17–1.22) and direct care facilities (AOR = 1.08; 95% CI = 1.05–1.11). Readmission in direct care was appreciated for Hispanic patients with a CCI of 1 (AOR = 1.14; 95% CI = 1.13–1.15).Conclusion:We found significant increases in 90-day readmissions among Black and Hispanic patients based on CCI score within the direct care setting. We believe this reflects recent challenges that have impacted both staffing and surgical volume within these facilities.

INTRODUCTION

Racial disparities are known to persist in publicly funded environments of care, like the Veterans Administration, and academic health networks.1–7 Universal insurance has often been posited as a mitigating factor for healthcare disparities8–10 although most studies in universally-insured US populations are confounded by reliance on data from elderly (Medicare) individuals or economically disadvantaged populations (Medicaid/state-level assistance programs). Previous research conducted using data from the Military Health System (MHS) over the time period 2006 to 2014 suggested the universally insured and equal access design of this system significantly reduced healthcare disparities after surgery among racial minorities and those of reduced socioeconomic status.8

However, in as much as that time, the MHS has faced a number of existential challenges, including consolidation under a single managing entity (the Defense Health Agency), transition to a new universal medical record system, a push to move more healthcare responsibilities to the civilian sector, increased emphasis on military-medical preparedness as opposed to the provision of regular medical care, staffing shortages and the system-wide stress of managing the COVID-19 pandemic.9–13 The MHS overall reported close to a 36% reduction in meaningful surgical procedures over the early phase of the COVID-19 pandemic,10 coupled with a 26% decrease in surgical procedures during 2015 to 2019, even before the onset of the pandemic.11

In healthcare networks that are struggling, the most vulnerable populations are the ones that show the signs of experiencing worsening outcomes first.7,14,15 I8n this context, we sought to evaluate the current state of healthcare disparities within the MHS, comparing Department of Defense facilities (direct care) to treatment outsourced to the civilian sector. We hypothesized that, in light of the known reductions in surgical volume and staffing reported in previous research, worsening healthcare disparities would be appreciated among racial and ethnic minorities in direct care when compared to the civilian setting.

METHODS

Utilizing health care claims data from the MHS Data Repository, a retrospective cohort study was performed on all TRICARE beneficiaries ages 18-64 years who received 1, or more, of a selected battery of surgeries between fiscal years 2020-2023 (October 1, 2019 – September 30, 2023). The MHS Data Repository is a central data repository for all health care delivered to MHS beneficiaries at military treatment facilities (direct care) or civilian fee-for-service facilities (private sector care) through TRICARE insurance. International Classification of Diseases, tenth revision, procedure codes and Current Procedural Terminology codes were used to identify individuals who received 1 or more of a representative panel of surgical interventions including abdominal aortic aneurysm repair/reconstruction, appendectomy, bariatric surgery, coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG), cataract surgery, cholecystectomy, colectomy, inguinal hernia repair, mastectomy, rotator cuff repair, spinal surgery (discectomy, cervical myelopathy, or spinal metastatic disease), total hip arthroplasty, total knee arthroplasty, and transurethral resection of the prostate. These selected procedures include characteristic general surgery, vascular surgery, neurosurgery, urology, and orthopedic interventions with intentional variation in intensity and complexity. This battery of procedures has previously been used in the study of surgical healthcare policy within the MHS and other healthcare settings.8,10,12 Patients with multiple surgeries were included as long as each surgery occurred within 6 or more months apart. Patients with any neoplasm in the year prior or with a history of any surgery in the 6-months before the index surgery were excluded from analysis.

The primary outcome was 90-day readmission after the index surgery, as a proxy for healthcare quality. Readmission was defined as any inpatient admission to a direct or private sector hospital within 90-days of the index surgery, irrespective of the original site of service.

Demographic characteristics including sex, age, race, medical comorbidities (classified using the Charlson comorbidity index16) marital status, beneficiary category, sponsor rank as a proxy for socioeconomic status,8,13,17 and service branch were obtained at the time of surgery. Additionally, the care setting (direct or private sector) where the index procedure was performed was also abstracted. In line with prior studies using military claims,8–12 race was categorized as White, Black, Asian or Pacific Islander, Native American or Alaska Native, and other. Missing race was handled using imputation calculated by reweighted estimated equations.18 Ethnicity was constructed as a mutually exclusive category in parallel, denoting Hispanic patients. Sponsor rank was used as a measure for socioeconomic status, with those of junior enlisted rank representative of lower socioeconomic status.8,17 The environment of care of the index surgery was considered the primary predictor and 90-day readmissions the primary outcome, with race and ethnicity the main explanatory factors. As such, we performed an interaction analysis in the setting of multivariable logistic regression testing to evaluate the impact that race, the Charlson comorbidity index score and the care setting of surgical care had on 90-day readmissions. All other variables abstracted were used as covariates in these analyses. To account for multiple surgeries per patient, each model was clustered on the patient’s unique identification number. Statistical significance was set, a-priori, at adjusted odds ratio (AOR), and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) exclusive of 1.00 in multivariable analysis, with P < 0.05.

We followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology reporting requirements and all analyses were conducted in SAS 9.4. This study was found exempt by the Institutional Review Board of the Uniformed Services University of the Health Sciences.

RESULTS

A total of 262,344 surgeries among 244,008 adult TRICARE beneficiaries were identified for inclusion with 78.99% of all surgeries performed at a private sector facility (Supplemental Table 1, see https://links.lww.com/AOSO/A606). The majority of surgeries were in females (52.15%), with pluralities in adults ages 55 to 64 (45.74%), White race (46.65%), senior enlisted sponsor rank (59.73%) and dependents (44.98%). Over 85% of the cohort had a Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI) score of zero (Supplemental Table 1, see https://links.lww.com/AOSO/A606). The most common procedure overall was spinal surgery (19.07%); however, cholecystectomy was the most common procedure in direct care (Table 1). Approximately 10% of surgeries were associated with a 90-day readmission event, including 10.25% of procedures performed in the private sector and 10.13% of direct care interventions.

After multivariable analysis that adjusted for confounders, in the MHS on the whole we observed disparities in the odds of 90-day readmission among patients of Black race (AOR = 1.13; 95% CI = 1.11–1.15; P < 0.0001; Supplemental Table 2, see https://links.lww.com/AOSO/A607) as compared to Whites. Lower odds of readmission were identified among patients of Asian/Pacific Islander (AOR = 0.70; 95% CI = 0.67–0.73; P < 0.0001) and American Indian/Alaskan Native (AOR = 0.80; 95% CI = 0.73–0.87; P < 0.0001) race. No significant difference was appreciated for Hispanic patients (AOR = 1.01; 95% CI = 0.99–1.04; P = 0.32). In addition, there was no significant difference in the odds of readmission between direct care hospitals and private institutions (AOR = 1.01; 95% CI = 0.99–1.02; P = 0.47).

The results of the interaction testing revealed significant interactions between race and care setting (P < 0.001), and between race and CCI score (P < 0.0001; data not shown). Subsequent multivariable regressions, with control for CCI score, were performed to examine the interaction between race and care setting on 90-day readmissions. In the interaction analysis Black patients with CCI of 0 were more likely to experience 90-day readmissions to private sector hospitals (AOR = 1.19; 95% CI = 1.17–1.22; P < 0.0001) and direct care facilities (AOR = 1.08; 95% CI = 1.05–1.11; P < 0.0001; Table 2). Black patients with a CCI of 1 were at a significantly greater likelihood of readmission in the private sector (AOR = 1.08; 95% CI = 1.05–1.11; P < 0.0001), but had lower odds of readmission in direct care (AOR = 0.090; 95% CI = 0.84–0.96; P = 0.0008). For Black patients with a CCI of 2 or higher, there was no significant difference in readmission within direct care (AOR = 1.05; 95% CI = 0.98–1.13; P = 0.14) and lower odds of readmission in the private sector (AOR = 0.89; 95% CI = 0.85–0.94; P < 0.0001). Significant differences in readmission were not encountered among Hispanic patients in most CCI categories, with the exception of higher odds in direct care for those with a CCI of 1 (AOR = 1.14; 95% CI = 1.13–1.15; P < 0.0001) and lower odds in the private sector for individuals with a CCI of 2 or higher (AOR = 0.92; 95% CI = 0.85–0.99; P = 0.02).

DISCUSSION

This investigation examined our hypothesis that organizational alterations and operational changes in the MHS between 2019 and 2023 exerted a disproportionate effect on surgical care received by racial and ethnic minorities. Prior research had maintained that the provision of universal health insurance combined with the design of the MHS led to sizable reductions in healthcare disparities appreciated in the civilian sector, including reductions in mortality, complications, readmissions and failure to rescue after surgery.4,8,9 The ability to maintain such high-quality care is highly predicated on appropriate staffing and adequate procedural-based volume within direct care facilities.19 However, surgical volume in the direct care setting dropped substantially over the last 2 decades, with more cases being referred to civilian hospitals.11 This trend was exacerbated further during the COVID-19 pandemic, with surgical experience in direct care facilities reportedly declining by 36%.10

This is the first study we are aware of to examine racial disparities in surgical outcomes within the different environments of care (eg, direct and private sector) of the MHS subsequent to the COVID-19 pandemic. Our study is advantaged by reliance on a representative battery of characteristic surgical interventions, a large sample of over 250,000 surgeries and a national scope. Given the representative nature of the population covered by the MHS,8,17,19 and broad racial, ethnic, educational, vocational and socioeconomic diversity, we believe that the findings can serve as an informative model for other large healthcare networks that operate using a tiered system that combines large, tertiary referral centers with smaller community hospitals.

Overall, it was reassuring to see that there was no significant difference in the odds of readmission overall between the direct care and private sector settings. At the same time, we did appreciate modest, but statistically significant increases in the likelihood of readmission among Black patients in the private sector and direct care, and Hispanic patients in direct care. For Black and Hispanic patients with 2 or more comorbidities, superior outcomes (ie, significantly lower odds of readmission) were appreciated in private sector hospitals. The findings for Black patients with CCI of 0 in direct care (AOR = 1.08; 95% CI = 1.05–1.11) and for Hispanic patients with CCI of 1 (AOR = 1.14; 95% CI = 1.13–1.15) were significantly different. Although there were significant differences detected for the other racial categories, because the signal overall is a reduction in 90-day readmissions (Supplemental Table 2, see https://links.lww.com/AOSO/A607), we do not consider these findings as representative of healthcare disparities prima facie.

The current findings for direct care should be contrasted with earlier studies conducted on data from care provided in the MHS for the years 2006 to 2014 and involving Emergency General Surgery, cardiothoracic, orthopaedic, urology, and neurosurgical conditions.8,9 In these prior studies, no significant differences for readmission among Black patients were appreciated for the direct care setting or the MHS network.8,9 We believe this reduction in care quality for racial and ethnic minorities is reflective of the workforce and staffing challenges, and reductions in meaningful procedural volume, that have confronted the MHS over the last decade and especially in the most recent 3 to 5 years that span the COVID-19 pandemic.10–12 Minority patients are likely the first to experience the effects of a struggling healthcare network due to previously recognized challenges in navigating healthcare utilization, reduced healthcare literacy, socioeconomic challenges that impact capacity to travel and greater risks of healthcare segregation.14,15

We believe these results hold an important, 2-pronged, message for the MHS and the healthcare system as a whole. Foremost, previously high-performing institutions in terms of eliminating health disparities may experience declinations in performance in the setting of external challenges that impact staffing and procedural volume, even if their focus and treatment philosophy remain unaltered. Second, even if the overall performance of a system in terms of surgical care quality seems to be stable, certain segments of the population, including both intrinsically lower- and higher-risk racial and ethnic minorities may be experiencing healthcare disparities. As such, we recommend appropriate staffing, improved procedural volume and regular stratified assessments based on race, ethnicity, and risk category to ensure adequate provision of surgical care across all aspects of the population. This message would seem to be as important for direct care facilities at this time as it is for civilian hospitals.

We recognize several study limitations. First, our work represents a retrospective analysis of MHS claims, which introduces inherent challenges due to the administrative nature of the data.17,18 Race and ethnicity data are self-reported and relatively complete for active-duty members but may be incomplete for dependent beneficiaries. We addressed this issue to the fullest extent possible using multiple imputation methods as recommended in prior research.17,18 Second, the study period covered only 3 years (2020–2023), limiting its ability to assess longer-term trends. Third, the stresses on the MHS over the last 5 to 6 years have contributed to worrisome signs of reduced healthcare delivery efficiency within the MHS and this has resulted in some signals of worsening healthcare disparities which were not previously present in the time period 2006 to 2010.8 Fourth, we are unable to ascertain the decisions behind patients who were retained for treatment in direct care as opposed to those who were referred to the civilian sector. Within recent years there has been a general move to increase referrals to the civilian setting,10–12 which we believe would limit the prospect for confounding by indication to impact our results. Similarly, we are unable to understand the reasons behind readmission in a clinically granular way. In this context, we cannot completely ascertain whether lower rates of readmission among racial minorities may represent reduced access to care, or healthcare discrimination in specific settings. Finally, our investigation focused solely on 90-day readmissions as a proxy measure for healthcare quality. We did not assess other outcomes such as mortality, complications or failure to rescue.

In conclusion, in contrast to prior research on the MHS, we found significant increases in 90-day readmissions among Black and Hispanic patients based on CCI score within the direct care setting. We believe this reflects recent challenges that have impacted both staffing and surgical volume within direct care facilities. These results implicate worsening healthcare disparities as an early signal of healthcare networks that are experiencing reductions in surgical care quality.
