Controlling Thermodynamic and Kinetic on Ion-Mediated Nonsolvent-Induced Phase Separation
Authors: Mohammad Aadil, Muhammad Tayyab Khalid, Tae-Hyun Bae, Chul Ho Park
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Polar Clean has emerged as a promising sustainable solvent for membrane fabrication; however, its high boiling point and strong affinity for polymers pose significant challenges for solvent removal and structural preservation. Here, we introduce a nonthermal ion-mediated phase separation (IMPS) strategy that decouples solvent extraction from thermal processing via coupled thermodynamic and kinetic effects. The influence of chloride salts (CaCl2, NaCl, NH4Cl) on poly­(ether sulfone) membrane formation was systematically examined. Cloud-point analysis showed that kosmotropic salts destabilize the dope by shifting the binodal boundary to lower water concentrations, while kinetic analysis revealed that rapid demixing forms dense vitrified skin layers that hinder solvent release in additive-free systems. This kinetic limitation was alleviated by hydrophilic additives (PEG/PVP), which suppressed skin densification and enhanced pore connectivity. The optimized NH4Cl/PEG system achieved 99.8% solvent removal and a flux of 782 ± 17 LMH bar–1, and an MWCO of 79 kDa, representing a 1.8-fold improvement over DI water controls, and exhibited a highly interconnected porous morphology. IMPS thus provides a robust, energy-efficient platform for sustainable membrane fabrication with mechanistic tunability beyond conventional green-solvent limitations.

Introduction

Polymeric membranes stand as the key element of global separation or purification infrastructures, underpinning critical applications from water purification to chemical processing due to their scalability, modularity, and energy efficiency. Among fabrication strategies, nonsolvent-induced phase separation (NIPS) remains the dominant industrial standard for producing asymmetric porous membranes, reliably generating high-throughput skin layers supported by robust porous substructures. Conventional NIPS relies heavily on toxic dipolar aprotic solventssuch as N-methyl-2-pyrrolidone (NMP), dimethylacetamide (DMAc), and dimethylformamide (DMF)which face tightening regulatory restrictions (e.g., REACH in the EU) and impose severe environmental burdens regarding solvent handling and wastewater toxicity. Consequently, the transition from these hazardous solvents to eco-friendly alternatives has shifted from an optional pursuit to an industrial imperative.

Among various commercialized green solvents, Polarclean (PC) offers a unique combination of nontoxicity, biodegradability, and excellent solvency for high-performance polymers like polysulfone (PSF) and poly­(ether sulfone) (PES). , Despite its chemical promise, PC is currently obstructed by a critical thermodynamic hurdle: its exceptionally high boiling point (∼278 °C), unlike volatile traditional solvents. PC cannot be removed via standard evaporation or drying processes, so the residual PC inside porous membranes induces continuous phase separation leading to pore deformation under various external conditions. , Therefore, for a highly accurate pore size dependence process, the residual solvent must be removed. As a result, membrane manufacturers are forced to rely on prolonged, energy-intensive washing cycles with high-temperature water to extract residual solvent. This thermal dependency not only negates the sustainable ethos of the green solvent narrative but also risks compromising the membrane’s pore fidelity through thermal stress and polymer relaxation.

Therefore, overcoming the high boiling point limitation without relying on thermal energy remains a grand challenge in sustainable membrane engineering. The solution lies in fundamentally redesigning the mass transfer pathway: finding a nonthermal driving force to accelerate the mixing rate and spontaneously push out the solvent during the phase separation itself. If the thermodynamic affinity between solvent and nonsolvents can be manipulated to trigger rapid rejection, the dependency on postwashing steps can be drastically minimized or eliminated.

Recent studies Xie et al. introduced amphiphilic additives to destabilize the dope and accelerate solvent–nonsolvent exchange during NIPS, enabling more effective residual solvent removal; specifically, poly­(vinyl chloride)-graft-poly­(ethylene glycol) methyl ether methacrylate (PVC-g-PEGMA) was blended into a PC dope, where hydrophilic PEG segments enhance water affinity and demixing; consequently, faster phase inversion and higher porosity improve solvent removal and membrane performance. Another group, Jung et al., investigated membrane formation using a green solvent system (PC) and demonstrated that solvent–nonsolvent diffusion kinetics and phase-separation behavior govern membrane morphology and mass transfer. They further showed that pore-forming additives (e.g., Pluronic F127, PVP) accelerate demixing and enhance pore formation, facilitating more efficient solvent exchange and removal during fabrication.

In this study, we propose a novel ion-mediated phase-separation strategy to decouple solvent extraction from thermal processing. We exploit the synergistic interaction between the hydrophilic polymeric additives and ionic salts. While hydrophilic polymeric additives are traditionally utilized solely as pore formers, their role in modulating the thermodynamic sensitivity of these systems to ionic strength has been underexplored. We hypothesize that the introduction of specific ions creates a strong salting-out effect that destabilizes the PC-polymer thermodynamic stability within the nascent membrane. Also, the salt-mediated environment acts as a kinetic switch in the presence of hydrophilic polymeric additives. Consequently, ions significantly accelerate solvent exchange and minimize residual PC content without highly energy-intensive processes. Furthermore, this approach shows the conventional phase separation mechanism, yielding membranes with a porous structure and superior permeability. This work can establish a robust framework for nonthermal solvent removal, resolving the PC paradox and paving the way for sustainable membrane manufacturing.

Materials and Experimental Methods

Chemicals

PES (E6020p grade) was purchased from BASF (Germany). PC (methyl-5-dimethylamino-2-methyl-D-oxopentanoate) was obtained from Solvay Co. (Belgium). PEG (MW = 4000 g mol–1) and PVP (MW = 4000 g mol–1) were obtained from Alfa Fisher (USA). Fluorescent-labeled dextrans with molecular weights of 1, 3, 10, and 70 kDa were obtained from Alfa Fisher (USA) or TCI (Japan). Phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) solution was purchased from Thermo Fisher (USA). All water used in the experiments was double-distilled. Calcium chloride (CaCl2), sodium chloride (NaCl), and ammonium chloride (NH4Cl) were supplied by Duksan Chemical (South Korea).

Membrane Preparation

PES powders were dehumidified in a vacuum oven at 80 °C for 48 h prior to use. Dope solutions of various PES concentrations were prepared by dissolving the required amount of polymer in PC in a sealed container and stirring at 80 °C for 48 h until a homogeneous, bubble-free solution was obtained. For formulations containing hydrophilic polymeric additives, PEG or PVP was added at the prescribed loading, and the mixture was stirred under the same conditions to ensure complete dissolution and uniformity. The fully dissolved dopes were then degassed for 24 h to remove entrapped air and to minimize casting defects.

Flat-sheet membranes were fabricated by the NIPS method. After degassing, the dope solution was cast on a clean glass plate by using a casting knife set to a fixed gap. The cast film was then immediately immersed in a coagulation bath containing either deionized water or the salt-containing aqueous solutions at the specified concentration and kept in the bath until complete phase inversion and solidification. After 24 h, the membranes were transferred into fresh deionized water and rinsed repeatedly (at least 5 times) to remove residual PC, salts, and any leachable additive. The membranes were stored in deionized water at room temperature until they were characterized and performance tested. The prepared samples are organized in Table .

Membrane Characterization

Microstructural Analysis

Surface and cross-sectional morphologies were examined by field-emission scanning electron microscopy (FESEM; Tescan MIRA3, Czech Republic). Cross sections were obtained by fracturing the membranes after cryogenic treatment, and the samples were dried and coated with a thin platinum coating (Leica EM ACE600, Germany). Surface topography and roughness were characterized by atomic force microscopy (AFM; XE-100, Park Systems, South Korea), and roughness parameters were calculated from the acquired height maps under the identical scanning conditions.

Thermodynamic and Kinetic Analyses

A fixed volume of the dope solution was cast onto a clean glass plate. Immediately after casting, the membrane samples (10 × 10 mm2, thickness ∼ 90 μm) were immersed in a sealed vessel containing 10 mL of various nonsolvent compositions. To simulate continuous extraction and prevent solute saturation, the coagulation bath was completely withdrawn at predetermined time intervals (1, 5, 10, 30, and 60 min) and immediately replenished with an equal volume of fresh, pre-equilibrated nonsolvent. This “total replacement” method ensures that the concentration of PC in the bulk phase remains negligible. The collected solutions were analyzed via UV–Vis spectroscopy (Ubi-490, South Korea) at a wavelength of 280 nm. Prior to analysis, calibration curves were established using PC standards prepared in the corresponding nonsolvent matrix (water or salt solutions) to ensure linearity and validity, following established protocols. The absorbance measured at each time interval was converted to concentration, integrated to determine the cumulative mass, and normalized against the calculated initial solvent loading. The final results were expressed as the percentage of cumulative solvent removal over time. All experiments were performed in triplicate, with solutions maintained at an ambient temperature of approximately 23 °C.

Membrane Performance Analyses

Membrane performance was evaluated using a filtration module with an effective membrane area of 18 cm2. All experiments were conducted at a constant transmembrane pressure of 1 bar. The permeate was collected over a known time interval after stabilization for 30 min and used to determine the water flux which was then normalized by pressure and reported as LMH bar–1. Solute rejection R(%) was performed under identical operating conditions. Each solute was prepared at a concentration of 0.2 wt % in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.4). Prior to permeate sampling, the feed solution was circulated for 30 min to ensure steady-state conditions: R(%)=(1−CpCf)×100

where C p and C f are the solute concentrations in the feed and permeate, respectively. Each membrane was tested at least three times, and the reported values represent the average of these measurements. The molecular weight cutoff (MWCO) was determined from the rejection profile obtained using dextrans of different molecular weights. The molecular weight cutoff (MWCO) was defined as the molecular weight corresponding to 90% solute rejection, determined via B-spline curve fitting using OriginLab software.

Results and Discussion

Screening of Ion Types

The selection of ions is governed by the thermodynamic affinity of the ions for water molecules. To validate this IMPS, we screened a broad spectrum of salt candidates (Figure ). Kosmotropic ions, capable of inducing strong hydration, successfully trigger the salting-out of PC at concentrations of 3.6 M for CaCl2 and 4.2 M for NaCl at 25 °C. In contrast, chaotropic ions (NH4Cl) fail to induce phase separation even at their solubility limits. Based on this comprehensive screening, three chloride salts (CaCl2, NaCl, and NH4Cl) were selected to decouple the effects of cationic hydration enthalpy and valency from anionic contributions, thereby establishing a robust framework to validate the ion-mediated phase separation.

Salts’ Effects on Thermodynamic Phase Separation

To elucidate the thermodynamic driving forces of ion-mediated phase separation, we performed cloud point analysis. This technique maps the precise stability limit of the PES/PC system under various ionic environments. As anticipated, the introduction of kosmotropic salts (CaCl2) induced a profound destabilization of the polymer solution. Due to its high charge density, the Ca2+ ion aggressively sequesters water molecules into tight hydration shells. As the concentration of CaCl2 increases, the onset of turbidity occurs at significantly lower nonsolvent ratios compared to those of deionized water (Figure a). This demonstrates that high-hydration salts measurably compress the single-phase region of the ternary phase diagram. Increasing the salt concentration from 0.5 to 2.0 M led to a progressive reduction in the water fraction required to trigger demixing. This confirms that a higher ionic strength amplifies the thermodynamic driving force, forcing the system to exit the metastable region earlier. NaCl (Figure b), occupying an intermediate position in the Hofmeister series, exhibits similar qualitative trends to CaCl2, albeit with moderated intensity due to the monovalent nature of the Na+ ion.

Ternary phase diagrams of PES/PC with different concentrations of nonsolvents: (a) CaCl2, (b) NaCl, (c) NH4Cl, with different additives: (d) PES/PC/PEG (1 wt %), (e) PES/PC/PEG (3 wt %), and (f) PES/PC/PVP (3 wt %) with and without three salts.

Remarkably, the NH4Cl system (Figure c) needs lower volumes of nonsolvents, surpassing both CaCl2 and NaCl. This behavior diverges from the classical hydration model and suggests an alternative driving force. Unlike Ca2+, the NH4 + ion possesses a lower hydration energy and exhibits a preferential affinity for organic PC. NH4 + ions integrate into the PC/water matrix and create a highly polar ionic solvent complex. While PC alone is a good solvent for PES, the PC/NH4Cl complex becomes thermodynamically hostile to the hydrophobic polymer chains. This strong solvent–ion association effectively pushes out the PES chains through solvophobic exclusion. Consequently, the polymer precipitates even at low water content, proving that direct solvent–ion interactions can be as potent as the main driving forces for PES precipitation.

To further modulate thermodynamic phase separation, PEG was incorporated into the PES dope solutions as a hydrophilic pore-forming additive. In contrast to the destabilizing effect of salts, the addition of PEG can shift the cloud point to higher water concentrations because of the thermodynamic compatibility of the solution. Driven by its high hydrophilicity, PEG can effectively expand the single-phase region and delay the onset of liquid–liquid demixing. The magnitude of this stabilization can be proven to be concentration-dependent. At a low loading of 1 wt % (Figure d), the binodal curve exhibits negligible deviation from the neat PES system (Figure a), suggesting that the stabilizing contribution is insufficient to alter the macroscopic phase boundary. However, increasing the PEG content to 3 wt % (Figure e) clearly reveals its stabilizing impact, necessitating a higher water fraction to trigger precipitation. Crucially, despite this elevated baseline stability, the relative impact of the ionic salts remained consistent. The introduction of CaCl2, NaCl, and NH4Cl continues to compress the binodal curve relative to that of the salt-free PEG system. This confirms that the ion-mediated phase-separation mechanism operates independently of the additive.

We doubt there is one more question on additives. PVP possesses a pyrrolidone ring with a highly polar carbonyl group, which acts as a strong Lewis base capable of specific coordination with metal cations. On the other hand, PVP is more polar, and it has a higher capacity for complexing with ions due to its amide group. Therefore, PVP can form more stable coordination complexes with the solvent and ions than PEG and consequently expand the single-phase region and delay the onset of demixing, as shown in Figure f. Of course, ion-mediated phase separation takes place. Consequently, the interplay between additives and ions can provide a tunable lever to control both the membrane morphology and solvent elimination rate.

Solvent Release Analysis

While cloud point data elucidates thermodynamic instability, solvent release analysis is conducted to quantify the time-dependent depletion of residual solvent and estimate mass transfer kinetics. Unexpectedly, as shown in Figure a, the release rate of PC in ion-mediated environments is slower compared to the pure water control in the absence of additives. The presence of dissolved salts significantly lowers the chemical potential of water (water activity). This reduces the osmotic driving force for water influx into the nascent membrane, thereby dampening the overall solvent–nonsolvent exchange rate. More critically, phase separation initiates instantaneously at the interface between the polymer solution and the nonsolvent. In ion-mediated environments, this demixing triggers the premature formation of a dense, vitrified polymer skin. Once formed, this resistive skin layer acts as a physical mass transfer barrier, effectively sealing the surface and trapping the remaining solvent within the substructure. Notably, the mechanism emerges by linking Figures –: salt-induced thermodynamic destabilization in Figure promotes instantaneous demixing, but Figure shows that this event becomes kinetically limited by mass-transfer resistance once a vitrified skin forms, thereby slowing PC release. Figure explains the origin of this slowdown, revealing thicker, closed, and poorly connected skins in salt-only systems, compared with thinner, more open structures when PVP/PEG suppresses interfacial densification. Thus, IMPS performance depends on balancing thermodynamic driving force with skin-layer development during phase inversion.

Figure b illustrates the impact of PEG additives on solvent exchange kinetics. In the pure water control, the release rate of PC from the PES/PEG system is marginally slower than that of the neat PES system during the initial phase. This retardation is attributed to the viscosity effect of the PEG addition in 13 wt % PES dope solutions, thereby reducing the mutual diffusion coefficients of the solvent and nonsolvent. However, even after reaching equilibrium (360 min), a significant fraction of PC remains trapped within the matrix (∼3% residual content), confirming that standard water washing, even with pore-formers, is insufficient to fully resolve the solvent retention issue.

In contrast, the introduction of salts into the coagulation bath can change the kinetics of phase separation. The combination of salts and PEG accelerates the release rate and reduces the residual solvent content compared to the water, as shown in Figure b. Notably, the chaotropic salt combinations present particularly high efficiency, achieving 99% removal (vs 97% in pure water). The most dramatic kinetic enhancement was observed in the PVP-mediated system (Figure c). As hypothesized, PVP possesses a stronger capacity for ion coordination than does PEG due to its polar pyrrolidone ring. This capability allows PVP to act as a potent thermodynamic mediator, effectively bridging the incompatibility between the polymer phase and an ionic bath. This intense interaction assists in reaching the solvent extraction efficiency of up to 99.8%. This result demonstrates that ion-mediated phase separation with the correct polymeric additive can hold great potential to eliminate high-boiling PC residues without thermal processing. ,

For the practical applicability of the ion-mediated phase separation with varying pore sizes, higher polymer concentrations (15, 17, 19, and 21 wt % PES) with or without 3 wt % PVP/2 M NH4Cl systems were tested, although detailed release data are not presented here. As the PES concentration increased, the bulk viscosity of the dope solution increased exponentially. Then, the solvent release rate was markedly slower compared to the 13 wt % PES dope solution, necessitating prolonged cleaning cycles to achieve comparable residual solvent levels. Furthermore, the synergistic enhancement typically observed with the additive-ion combination became less pronounced at higher polymer loading. With the PVP concentration fixed at 3 wt %, increasing the PES content effectively dilutes the hydrophilic additive density relative to the hydrophobic matrix (e.g., Figure d). The higher polymer concentration results in a reduced average pore size. This compact structure physically hinders the penetration of hydrated ions into the nascent membranes. Therefore, we could observe the minor effect at higher concentrations. Although further optimization of the dope composition and ionic strength could potentially extend the benefits of ion-mediated phase separation to these high-concentration regimes, a comprehensive parametric study of these variables is beyond the scope of the present investigation.

Membrane Morphology

To correlate the kinetic and thermodynamic data with structural evolution, membrane morphology was examined as shown in Figure . The membrane structure is governed by the competitive coupling between the thermodynamics of demixing (phase separation tendency) and the kinetics of mass transfer (solvent–nonsolvent exchange rate). In the absence of additives, the surface morphologies with defect-like large pores are insensitive to the coagulation bath composition. Also, surface roughness values by AFM analysis were statistically comparable across all samples (R g ∼ 14 nm).

In contrast to the surface topography and image, the cross-sectional structure reveals fundamental differences driven by an ion-mediated mechanism. The introduction of salts induces notable thickening of the top skin layer. While rapid solidification occurs at the interface in all nonsolvent systems (Figure ), the ions produce a skin layer significantly thicker than that formed in a pure water bath. High-magnification insets of the fingerlike substructure reveal that the pores are predominantly closed and disconnected. This lack of interconnectivity creates a tortuous path for the water permeability. These morphological observations directly correlate with the solvent release profiles presented in Figure a. The suppressed release rate observed in ion-mediated baths is now structurally justified. The rapid formation of a thick, impermeable skin establishes a kinetic barrier that locks the solvent within closed pores. Consequently, in the absence of additives, kinetic hindrance dominates the phase-separation process.

The incorporation of PEG (Figure e–h) and PVP (Figure i–l) alters the morphological evolution. These hydrophilic additives act as potent pore formers, enhancing the local affinity for water and driving phase separation through mechanisms of segregation and partial leaching during immersion. Although the increase in dope viscosity induced by PEG and PVP typically retards mass transfer, the surface micrographs reveal a distinct increase in pore size compared to the neat PES control. This confirms that the thermodynamic instability introduced by the additives overrides the kinetic hindrance of viscosity, preventing the surface closure observed in additive-free systems. A critical structural deviation is observed in the cross-section that the addition of hydrophilic additives significantly reduces the thickness of the top skin layer. This structural thinning directly explains the kinetic anomalies that were discussed previously. The reduced skin thickness lowers the hydraulic resistance to mass transfer, enabling the faster solvent release rates observed in PEG/PVP systems and effectively negating the impact of increased bulk viscosity. Most notably, the NH4Cl-mediated system exhibits the most pronounced structural optimization, yielding the largest pores in figure-like walls and the thinnest skin layer.

Consequently, the synergistic combination of ionic salts and hydrophilic additives proves to be an effective strategy. It enables the fabrication of highly porous membrane structures while simultaneously ensuring deep solvent extraction, effectively resolving the relation between the structural control and residual solvent elimination.

The SEM morphologies (Figure ) directly correlate with solvent removal behavior (Figure ) and membrane permeability (Table ). In salt-only systems, instantaneous demixing induces the formation of thick, dense skin layers with predominantly closed pore structures, which significantly increase diffusion resistance and limit mass transfer. As a result, water flux decreases by 39–49% (258–234 vs 423 LMH bar–1), accompanied by slower solvent extraction due to tortuous transport pathways. In contrast, the NH4Cl/PEG system exhibits a markedly thinner skin layer and highly interconnected porous structure, enabling continuous diffusion pathways. This optimized morphology results in a high water flux of 782 LMH bar–1, an MWCO of 79 kDa, and an enhanced solvent removal efficiency of 99.8%. These results confirm that reduced skin-layer thickness and improved pore connectivity collectively minimize internal mass transfer resistance, thereby enhancing both permeability and solvent extraction. Overall, the IMPS approach enables precise control over the structure–transport relationship through coupled thermodynamic and kinetic mechanisms.

Membrane Performance

To normalize the impact of membrane geometry on filtration performance, all samples were cast to a uniform final thickness of 90 ± 9 μm. The corresponding pure water flux and MWCO values are summarized in Table .

In the absence of hydrophilic additives, the membrane prepared in a pure water bath exhibits the highest baseline flux. Conversely, the introduction of ionic salts results in a marked decrease in water permeability. This reduction aligns perfectly with the morphological evidence presented in Figure that the salt-induced densification of the skin layer and the formation of closed-cell pores create significant hydraulic resistance, effectively choking the flow despite the strong thermodynamic driving force.

However, the incorporation of PEG or PVP fundamentally reverses this trend. While the average pore size decreases, the pure-water flux increases significantly. This desirable deviation from the standard trade-off is attributed to the structural evolution. Although the selective pores become tighter, the overall surface porosity increases, and the skin layer becomes significantly thinner. The reduction in transport resistance (due to the thinner skin) outweighs the reduction in the pore diameter. Most notably, the NH4Cl-mediated system achieves the highest pure water flux among all of the tested variations. This superior performance is driven by the combination of highly interconnected porous structures and high surface porosity, ensuring minimal resistance to mass transfer while maintaining precise separation characteristics.

While the present study focuses on microfiltration-type structures, the underlying IMPS mechanism is governed by the coupling between thermodynamic instability and mass-transfer kinetics during phase separation. In this system, instantaneous demixing and solvent–nonsolvent exchange, mediated by ion-specific hydration effects, dictate the formation of a vitrified skin layer and pore connectivity. By tuning parameters such as polymer concentration, additive-induced interfacial interactions, and ion-mediated changes in solvent activity, the phase-separation pathway can be shifted from instantaneous demixing toward more controlled or delayed regimes. This mechanistic tunability suggests that IMPS may be extended beyond microfiltration to access ultrafiltration and nanofiltration structures, and potentially denser selective layers relevant to reverse osmosis. Therefore, IMPS provides a versatile and mechanistically tunable framework for membrane fabrication across multiple separation regimes.

IMPS demonstrates potential industrial feasibility by using low-cost NH4Cl and conventional PVP/PEG additives commonly employed in membrane manufacturing. The chloride salt coagulation bath enables continuous operation as residual salts are effectively removed by established multiple DI water rinse protocols, preventing accumulation while minimizing wastewater discharge. Unlike toxic solvent processes that require energy-intensive distillation for recovery, IMPS coagulation baths enable nanofiltration-based regeneration, achieving >95% salt recovery. Relative to green solvent approaches that necessitate prolonged thermal washing (12–48 h, 60–80 °C), IMPS delivers comparable solvent extraction efficiency via instantaneous phase inversion kinetics at ambient temperature, substantially reducing energy consumption and process wastewater.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that IMPS using 2 M NH4Cl coagulation baths with 3 wt % PEG additives enables >99.8% Polar Clean removal and a water flux of 782 ± 17 LMH bar–1 in PES membranes, representing a 1.8-fold increase over DI water controls (423 ± 22 LMH bar–1). Cloud point analysis confirmed that salts compress the binodal boundary, while PEG mitigates salt-induced skin densification, yielding thinner top layers and interconnected pores (MWCO 79 kDa vs 60 kDa baseline). These results resolve the kinetic bottleneck of fast demixing in green-solvent NIPS, providing a scalable, nonthermal strategy for sustainable membrane fabrication. IMPS thus offers mechanistic control over morphology and transport, with potential extension to tighter ultrafiltration/nanofiltration structures.
