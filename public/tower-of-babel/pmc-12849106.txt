A systematic review of EEG-based biomarkers for depression, anxiety, and bipolar disorder: trends in explainable artificial intelligence (XAI)
Authors: Lijiang Zhai, Min Zhao, Jia Zhang, Muhammad Jamil, Rida Naz, Caina Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMood and anxiety disorders, including major depressive disorder (MDD), bipolar disorder (BD), and anxiety disorders, affect millions worldwide and pose significant diagnostic challenges. Electroencephalography (EEG) has emerged as a non-invasive neurophysiological tool for identifying biomarkers of these disorders. However, the adoption of deep learning and machine learning in EEG analysis introduces interpretability concerns that limit clinical trust and applicability. Explainable Artificial Intelligence (XAI) offers transparent insights into model decisions, aiming to improve trust and clinical adoption of AI tools.ObjectiveThis systematic review aims to critically evaluate the landscape of EEG-based biomarkers for depression, anxiety, and bipolar disorder as informed by XAI techniques. Specifically, it explores the nature of EEG features selected through explainable models, their physiological plausibility, diagnostic performance, methodological quality, and clinical translatability.MethodsFollowing PRISMA guidelines, a comprehensive literature search was conducted across PubMed, Scopus, IEEE Xplore, Web of Science, and Embase databases for studies published between 2015 and 2025. Inclusion criteria were studies employing EEG-derived features for mood disorder classification using machine learning or deep learning, coupled with at least one post-hoc XAI method (e.g. SHAP, LIME, attention maps). A total of 145 studies were included. Thematic synthesis was organized into key domains: EEG protocol and feature extraction, model performance, XAI methods, interpretability outcomes, risk of bias, translational barriers, and publication trends.ResultsThe review revealed that spectral features (e.g. alpha and beta power), functional connectivity metrics (e.g. PLI, PCC), and time-domain measures (e.g. Hjorth parameters) were the most commonly exploited EEG features. XAI-enhanced models consistently identified physiologically valid biomarkers such as frontal alpha asymmetry in MDD and frontal–parietal beta connectivity in BD. Machine learning models—including SVM, Random Forest, and deep neural networks—achieved high diagnostic accuracies (75–99%) across studies. SHAP and LIME were the most widely used interpretability methods, offering feature importance explanations that aligned well with known pathophysiological patterns. However, approximately 65% of studies exhibited high risk of bias due to small sample sizes, lack of external validation, and methodological inconsistencies. Ethical and translational concerns included limited clinician trust, lack of regulatory frameworks, and potential algorithmic bias. Publication trends showed exponential growth in EEG-XAI research post-2020, particularly in studies targeting depression.ConclusionEEG-XAI frameworks represent a transformative approach to neuropsychiatric diagnostics by coupling physiological insights with algorithmic transparency. While explainable models enhance diagnostic confidence and interpretability, their clinical utility is contingent upon methodological rigor, ethical safeguards, and cross-disciplinary validation. Future research should prioritize multi-site data harmonization, larger cohorts, and longitudinal designs to facilitate the safe and effective deployment of EEG-XAI tools in clinical psychiatry.Clinical trial numberNA.Supplementary informationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12888-025-07589-3.

Introduction

Electroencephalography (EEG) is a widely used non-invasive technique for investigating neural dynamics in psychiatric disorders [1]. Although anxiety disorders are diagnostically distinct from mood disorders under DSM-5 and ICD-11, they are included in this review because of their high prevalence, shared neurophysiological features with mood disorders, and frequent co-occurrence in EEG-based AI studies. The application of EEG in the study of depression, anxiety, and bipolar disorder has gained considerable momentum, driven by its ability to reflect dynamic changes in brain function associated with these conditions [2]. Traditional methods of EEG analysis often rely on visual inspection or basic quantitative measures, which may be limited in their capacity to detect the intricate patterns and subtle nuances in the data [3]. In response to these limitations, researchers are increasingly turning to advanced signal processing techniques and machine learning (ML) algorithms to extract more informative features from EEG recordings.

Deep learning methodologies, in particular, have demonstrated significant promise in decoding complex brain states and classifying psychiatric disorders using EEG signals [4]. The success of deep learning in EEG analysis stems from its capacity to automatically learn hierarchical data representations, capturing intricate relationships that conventional approaches may overlook [5]. However, the inherent “black box” nature of deep learning models poses a barrier to clinical implementation, as the rationale behind their predictions is often opaque [6]. To overcome this challenge, explainable artificial intelligence (XAI) methods have been developed to improve transparency and interpretability of AI models, supporting clinical trust [7].

XAI techniques help identify which EEG features or patterns are most relevant for diagnosis or prediction, offering insights into the neural mechanisms associated with psychiatric conditions [8]. The convergence of EEG-based biomarkers and XAI holds substantial promise for improving our understanding and treatment of depression, anxiety and bipolar disorder

The application of deep learning to EEG signal decoding has seen increasing research interest [9]. Deep neural networks such as convolutional neural networks (CNNs) and long short-term memory (LSTM) networks are being widely applied in psychiatric classification tasks [10]. These models offer powerful tools for large-scale data analysis, enabling both prediction and classification in biomedical domains [11]. The use of deep belief networks and other architectures for EEG signal analysis has also contributed to improved physiological understanding [12].

Key to the effectiveness of ML algorithms in EEG analysis is the selection of relevant features and preprocessing strategies [13]. In the context of psychiatric disorders, these models can assist in identifying individuals at risk, monitoring disease progression, and predicting treatment outcomes. Importantly, the ability to interpret model decisions is vital for clinical trust and integration. Many ML models, especially deep learning models, lack interpretability, which hinders their adoption in real-world settings [8, 14].

While XAI is still a nascent field, especially in psychiatric EEG research, its growth is rapid and promising. Techniques such as SHAP values, LIME, and attention mechanisms are enabling researchers to pinpoint EEG features that distinguish between mental health conditions [7]. By improving interpretability, XAI enhances clinical decision-making, supports targeted intervention strategies, and promotes personalized care.

Nevertheless, applying XAI to deep learning models comes with computational costs, especially for large EEG datasets. There is a pressing need to develop computationally efficient XAI approaches that remain clinically meaningful [15]. Moreover, evaluation frameworks to ensure the clinical utility of these explanations are currently underdeveloped [16]. Clinically validated studies are essential to demonstrate whether XAI-enhanced EEG analysis truly improves diagnostic accuracy and treatment outcomes [17].

XAI is especially critical in healthcare due to the ethical and regulatory need for transparency and fairness [18, 19]. Without explainability, clinicians may hesitate to adopt even highly accurate models [20]. Transparent models are more likely to be accepted by clinicians, regulators, and patients alike [21]. Most current XAI work focuses on interpreting black-box models, but the shift toward inherently interpretable models is gaining traction in domains such as medical imaging and psychiatric diagnostics [22, 23].

Finally, while global feature importance values help summarize model behavior, they often fail to represent subgroup or individual-level predictions [15]. Tools like FeatureExplorer may enhance prediction performance but often neglect explainability. The rationale for this review stems from this gap: many EEG-based ML models for psychiatric disorders lack interpretability, hindering clinical applicability. This review aims to address that gap by systematically evaluating the integration of XAI techniques in EEG biomarker research across depression, anxiety, and bipolar disorder [24].

Objectives

This systematic review aims to provide a comprehensive overview of the current state of research on explainable AI in EEG-based studies of depression, anxiety, and bipolar disorder. The goal is to systematically investigate how explainable AI techniques are employed to enhance the interpretability of machine learning models utilizing EEG data for the identification and classification of depression, anxiety, and bipolar disorder. This review seeks to achieve a comprehensive understanding of the methodologies, applications, and outcomes associated with explainable AI in the context of EEG-based analysis for mental health disorders. By examining the existing literature, we aim to identify key trends, challenges, and opportunities in this emerging field and the review seeks to outline the prevalent XAI methodologies employed in conjunction with EEG data, elucidating their strengths, limitations, and applicability to the study of depression, anxiety, and bipolar disorder. This includes examining diverse XAI techniques such as feature importance analysis, rule extraction, and visualization methods, and evaluating their effectiveness in enhancing the interpretability of machine learning models.

Methodology

We performed a systematic literature search in major bibliographic databases—PubMed/MEDLINE, IEEE Xplore, Scopus, Web of Science, and Embase—limited to English-language, peer-reviewed articles published between 2015 and 2025. The inclusion of Embase ensured broader coverage of biomedical and clinical research, particularly studies relevant to psychiatric and neurological disorders. A combination of controlled-vocabulary terms (MeSH in PubMed) and free-text keywords was used. Study selection

Two reviewers (XX and YY) independently screened all retrieved records by title/abstract and then full text, using predefined eligibility criteria. Disagreements were resolved through discussion and consensus, with a third reviewer (ZZ) consulted if needed. Inter-rater agreement was high (Cohen’s κ = 0.81 at title/abstract stage, κ = 0.84 at full-text stage), indicating strong reliability. Exclusion criteria included: (i) non-peer-reviewed articles, (ii) studies not using EEG, (iii) studies not applying AI/ML methods, (iv) reviews, editorials, and conference abstracts, and (v) studies without quantitative performance results.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Eligible studies were original research articles involving adult (≥18 years) human participants diagnosed with major depressive disorder, generalized anxiety disorder, or bipolar disorder that applied a machine learning or deep learning algorithm to classify or predict one of these conditions using EEG-derived features. Each included study explicitly incorporated at least one post-hoc explainability method (e.g., SHAP, LIME, attention or saliency mapping) to interpret model outputs and reported quantitative performance metrics (e.g., accuracy, AUC, sensitivity, specificity). We excluded reviews, meta-analyses, case reports, editorials, conference abstracts, and commentaries; studies that relied solely on non-EEG modalities (e.g., fMRI, MEG); animal or in vitro investigations; and any study that did not include a model interpretability or explainability component.

Exclusion criteria were: (i) non-peer-reviewed articles, (ii) studies not using EEG or not applying AI/ML methods, (iii) studies without performance metrics, (iv) reviews/editorials, and (v) non-human studies

Risk of bias and quality assessment

We assessed methodological quality and risk of bias using three established tools: Quality Assessment of Diagnostic Accuracy Studies 2 (QUADAS-2), Prediction model Risk of Bias ASsessment Tool (PROBAST), and Methodological Index for Non-Randomized Studies (MINORS).

QUADAS-2 primarily evaluates four domains—patient selection, index test, reference standard, and flow/timing. In our review, this tool frequently flagged issues related to patient selection and index test domains, particularly in studies that used retrospective EEG datasets without clear diagnostic thresholds or blinded review procedures.

PROBAST focuses on risk of bias in prediction model studies across domains including participants, predictors, outcomes, and analysis. Most concerns were identified in the analysis domain, often due to limited model validation or exclusion of confounding variables.

MINORS, designed to evaluate non-randomized studies, consistently highlighted weaknesses in prospective data collection, sample representativeness, and baseline comparability.

Results

Study characteristics

This section presents a comprehensive demographic and methodological summary of EEG-based XAI techniques in diagnosing and analyzing depression, anxiety, and bipolar disorder. These studies span 2015–2025 and represent a broad range of machine learning approaches, EEG signal processing techniques, and clinical applications.

Distribution by disorder

Out of the 145 included studies, the majority (85 studies) focused on major depressive disorder (MDD), reflecting its global prevalence and the growing demand for non-invasive, neurophysiological biomarkers in its diagnosis and treatment. A total of 35 studies targeted various forms of anxiety disorders, including generalized anxiety disorder (GAD), panic disorder, and social anxiety disorder (SAD), demonstrating the rising use of EEG-based models in identifying psychophysiological patterns associated with anxiety. Meanwhile, 25 studies explored bipolar disorder (BD), covering both depressive and manic phases. These studies often employed comparative models to distinguish BD from other mood and anxiety disorders using machine learning and XAI-driven EEG analysis [17, 25].

Geographic distribution

The geographic origin of the studies reveals a strong international interest in EEG-based XAI applications across diverse regions:

Asia accounted for approximately 40% of the studies, led by contributions from China, India, South Korea, and Japan. Research from this region often employed advanced deep learning architectures and large EEG datasets for depression and anxiety detection [7, 26–28].

Europe contributed roughly 35%, particularly from countries such as Germany, the United Kingdom, Poland, and Greece. European studies emphasized interpretability and ethical deployment of XAI models in clinical neuropsychiatry [17, 29, 30].

North America comprised about 20%, primarily from the United States and Canada, focusing on translational research, biomarker validation, and early treatment prediction in psychiatric disorders [31].

The remaining 5% of studies came from Australia, South America, and Africa, showing emerging interest in the use of EEG signals and interpretable AI tools in mental health diagnostics.

This global distribution highlights the interdisciplinary and international nature of this research field, with growing emphasis on population-specific EEG biomarkers and culturally informed AI models.

Publication years and trends

The temporal trend of publications reflects the increasing academic and clinical interest in integrating EEG analysis with machine learning and explainability frameworks:

2015–2017: A small number of foundational studies were published during this period, focusing largely on signal processing techniques and basic classification models for psychiatric diagnosis [30, 32].

2018–2020: There was a noticeable growth in research activity as more studies began employing convolutional neural networks (CNNs), long short-term memory networks (LSTMs), and attention-based models for EEG classification tasks [1, 30]. Importantly, this phase also marked the initial use of XAI methods like SHAP and LIME in psychiatric EEG analysis.

2021–2025: This period saw an exponential increase in publications, accounting for over 60% of the total included studies. Recent works leveraged graph neural networks (GNNs), hybrid multimodal approaches, and advanced anomaly detection frameworks with embedded XAI mechanisms for personalized mental health monitoring [25, 28, 33, 34]. The surge aligns with growing computational capabilities, open EEG datasets, and clinical interest in deploying AI for individualized diagnostics.

EEG recording protocols and feature extraction

Most reviewed EEG-AI studies (depression, anxiety, bipolar) used multichannel scalp recordings (standard 10–20 montage) in resting-state conditions. For example, Shen et al. (2021) analyzed a large multi-center resting EEG dataset, and Huang et al. (2019) collected 10 min eyes-closed resting EEG from first-episode MDD patients. Similarly, Liu et al. (2020) recorded 10-minute resting EEG from GAD patients using six frontal electrodes. Task-based protocols were less common; Kapitány-Fővény et al. (2021) compared inner vs. overt speech tasks, and Yang et al. (2022) explicitly found the eyes-closed resting paradigm yielded the highest accuracy (79.4%) for classifying bipolar depression. In summary, nearly all studies used eyes-closed or eyes-open rest, with only a few incorporating cognitive/emotional tasks.

Electrode coverage varied but generally focused on frontal, temporal, parietal and occipital regions. Qi et al. (2020) used 16 channels spanning frontal (Fp1, Fp2, F3, F4, F7, F8), central (C3, C4), parietal (P3, P4, Pz), and occipital (O1, O2) sites. Ravan et al. (2018) identified key discriminative features at frontal (e.g. FC5) and centroparietal (CPz) sites, while Shen et al. highlighted left-parietal and occipital electrodes for depression. Commonly used frequency bands spanned delta (≈0.5–4 Hz) up through beta (13–30 Hz), often including alpha (8–13 Hz) and sometimes gamma ( > 30 Hz). For instance, Shen et al. (2021) filtered 0.01–40 Hz, and Suzuki’s consumer-EEG study covered 1–128 Hz. Beta-band features were frequently salient: Qi et al. (2020) reported significantly higher beta power in MDD, and Ahmadi et al. (2021) found discriminatory Hjorth activity in beta/gamma at frontal sites. In contrast, alpha-band connectivity changes were noted in anxiety studies. Overall, spectral power differences (especially in alpha/beta) and connectivity measures in those bands were most commonly exploited.

EEG features extracted fell into several categories. Spectral features (absolute/relative band power, PSD) were most prevalent. For example, Huang et al. used relative delta/theta/alpha power ratios (achieving 88.2% accuracy for MDD), and Suzuki et al. (2020) used PSD and differential entropy (91.6% macro-F1). Functional connectivity metrics were also widely used: Liu et al. extracted undirected (PLI, PCC) and directed (PDC) connectivity from six frontal channels and achieved 98.1% accuracy with a Deep Forest model. Yang et al. employed PLI differences across multiple bands, with RF classification (~79.4% accuracy) best for eyes-closed data. Temporal/nonlinear dynamics features appeared in fewer studies: Ahmadi et al. computed time-domain Hjorth parameters (activity, mobility, complexity) in beta/gamma bands, achieving 92.05% accuracy. Entropy measures were relatively rare: Qi et al. (2020) computed fuzzy entropy for GAD vs. DD (Depressive Disorder), finding higher beta-band FE in DD. EEG-Based ML and DL models in depression, anxiety, and bipolar disorders

Table 8 summarizes 18 selected EEG studies (2015–2025) using classical ML and DL models for depression, anxiety, or bipolar disorder. Methods ranged from traditional classifiers (SVM, RF, KNN) to deep networks (CNN, LSTM, GCN, transformer). For example, Zhang et al. [54] developed a 2D CNN–LSTM hybrid yielding 95.1% accuracy (AUC 0.98) in MDD screening. Xu et al. [55] used a multi-range CNN with residual and attention (MRCNN-RSE) on six-channel frontal EEG, achieving 98.48% accuracy. Similarly, advanced approaches like graph convolutional networks reached ~98–99% accuracy. Traditional ML also performed well: Al-Ezzi et al. (2023) used SVM on effective-connectivity features to identify social anxiety levels (accuracy 92.8%, sensitivity 95.3%, specificity 94.1%), and Suárez et al. (2025) applied Random Forest to distinguish bipolar vs. controls (accuracy 93.4%, AUC 0.93, sensitivity 93.5%, specificity 93.3%). Each study reports metrics such as accuracy, AUC, sensitivity and specifies the clinical classification task (e.g. MDD vs. healthy, anxiety severity) and insights (see Table 6).

Explainable AI techniques & interpretability outcomes

In recent EEG studies of mood and anxiety disorders, explainable AI (XAI) methods have been applied to reveal which EEG features drive classification. Recent years have seen growing use of explainable artificial intelligence (XAI) tools such as SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) and Local Interpretable Model-agnostic Explanations (LIME) to improve model interpretability in EEG-based mental health studies. Other techniques such as Partial Dependence Plots (PDP) and Gradient-weighted Class Activation Mapping (Grad-CAM) have also been increasingly applied to provide transparent and physiologically meaningful insights into model behavior. In our review, we found several studies employing these techniques. For example, Shen et al. (2025) used a Random Forest with SHAP-based feature selection to grade depression severity, achieving ~75% accuracy; SHAP highlighted left-parietal beta and occipital alpha power as top predictors of more severe depression. In bipolar disorder (BD), Saghab Torbati et al. [63] applied LIME to a linear SVM model and found beta- and gamma-band activity in frontal/occipital electrodes were key for distinguishing BD from controls. Similarly, attention-based deep networks have been used primarily for depression: Chen et al. [41] reported a multi-dimensional attention CNN with 95.1% accuracy for MDD vs. control, while Lu et al. [64] employed a graph CNN with frequency attention (GFACNN) to predict antidepressant response, significantly outperforming other models. Visual saliency maps have also been applied: Metin et al. [65] used CNNs and Class Activation Mapping to achieve 95.9% accuracy in BD vs. control, with CAMs highlighting prefrontal channels (F4, F7, F8, C3) as most discriminative. Across studies, key EEG features emerged consistently: frontal beta/gamma power and inter-hemispheric correlations in depression, and limbic/prefrontal rhythms in BD. Overall, SHAP and LIME were used in several studies (mostly depression and BD), attention-based models in many depression studies, and saliency/CAM primarily in BD. Figure 11 illustrates the number of studies using each XAI method for each disorder. These findings suggest that XAI analyses often confirm known EEG biomarkers (e.g. increased frontal beta in MDD) and reveal new spatial patterns, thereby enhancing model transparency and guiding clinical interpretation.

Methodological quality and risk of bias in EEG-XAI studies

To assess the robustness and reproducibility of EEG-based XAI findings in mood and anxiety disorders classification, we conducted a structured bias evaluation using three established tools: QUADAS-2, PROBAST, and MINORS. Table 8 presents a synthesis of 23 newly identified peer-reviewed studies published between 2015 and 2025, all indexed in Web of Science and meeting the predefined inclusion criteria. Each study was evaluated for risk of bias across patient selection, model development, and outcome interpretation domains.

A subset of studies—including those by Liu et al. [25], Sameti et al. [76], and Al-Ezzi et al. (2023)—demonstrated moderate risk of bias, particularly under PROBAST, often due to unclear handling of missing data and potential overfitting without cross-validation on independent cohorts. For example, Sameti et al. employed SHAP-based feature explanations and graded depression severity effectively, yet failed to implement a fully blinded assessment protocol, raising potential concerns under QUADAS-2.

Conversely, only a few studies, such as Demir et al. [43] and Breakspear et al. [44], demonstrated low-to-moderate risk profiles across all three assessment tools. These studies implemented larger sample sizes, rigorous feature selection, cross-validation, and prospective designs, increasing their methodological credibility and generalizability.

Risk domains

QUADAS-2 flagged frequent issues in patient selection and index test domains, particularly in studies using retrospective EEG datasets without clear diagnostic thresholds or blinded review.

PROBAST commonly identified concerns in the analysis domain, often due to limited model validation or exclusion of confounding variables.

MINORS, designed for non-randomized designs, consistently highlighted weaknesses in prospective data collection, sample representativeness, and baseline comparability.

Implications for clinical translation

The presence of high bias levels, despite excellent reported performance metrics (e.g., accuracies > 95%), underscores a critical gap between technical performance and clinical deployability. Without rigorous study design and transparent methodology, even highly accurate models—such as those by Umair et al. [51] or Wu et al. [78]—may lack sufficient validity for real-world use. Furthermore, the lack of external testing datasets and cross-site validation remains a major barrier to translating EEG-XAI classifiers into routine clinical workflows.

Discussion

This systematic review synthesized evidence from recent EEG-based XAI studies on depression, anxiety, and bipolar disorder. A consistent pattern across included studies was that EEG biomarkers highlighted by explainability methods overlapped with known neurocognitive networks. For instance, frontal alpha asymmetry and posterior alpha reductions [27]; Shen et al., 2025) can be mapped onto the default mode network (DMN), which has been repeatedly associated with rumination and self-referential processing in major depression. Similarly, alterations in frontal–parietal beta and gamma connectivity [49, 65] correspond to dysfunction within the frontoparietal network (FPN), supporting models of impaired executive control and emotion regulation in mood and anxiety disorders.

Studies on bipolar disorder [61], [93] revealed changes in frontal–temporal connectivity and nonlinear EEG dynamics, which are consistent with abnormalities in the salience network (SN). These findings support the hypothesis that aberrant SN functioning contributes to difficulties in switching between DMN and FPN activity, a hallmark of bipolar pathology. Anxiety-related investigations [25, 48] highlighted enhanced frontal beta activity and reduced alpha power, aligning with hypervigilance and heightened cortical arousal that are neurobiologically linked to SN dysfunction [89–96].

Importantly, explainability techniques such as SHAP Shen et al., 2025, LIME [49], and attention mechanisms [41, 54] consistently identified EEG features that were neurobiologically plausible and clinically relevant. For example, SHAP highlighted left-parietal beta and occipital alpha features for depression severity (Shen et al., 2025), while LIME confirmed the discriminative role of frontal beta-band Hjorth parameters in bipolar disorder [49]. These convergences reinforce the translational value of XAI, showing that interpretable models can bridge statistical prediction with mechanistic insight.

Despite promising results, several limitations remain. Most studies were based on relatively small, single-site, and private datasets [62, 68]; Li et al., 2022), raising concerns about reproducibility and external validity. While larger datasets such as MODMA have been used in some cases (Wei Liu et al., 2024 [42]; ), open multi-site repositories remain scarce. This reliance on small and homogeneous samples also increases the risk of algorithmic bias, as demonstrated in recent fairness-focused work [36]. Furthermore, most designs were cross-sectional, limiting insight into longitudinal dynamics such as treatment response [29].

Taken together, this review suggests that EEG biomarkers interpreted through XAI approaches map meaningfully onto large-scale networks such as the DMN, FPN, and SN, thereby enhancing both interpretability and clinical relevance. By aligning EEG findings with neuroscience frameworks, EEG-based XAI can support not only accurate classification but also mechanistic understanding of psychiatric disorders. Future work should prioritize larger, multi-site, and multimodal datasets, incorporate longitudinal designs, and integrate explainability with clinically interpretable network-level models to advance translational impact.

Review questions and answers

In what ways do XAI-driven explanations improve clinician decision-making, diagnostic confidence, and patient stratification compared to models without interpretability?

Explainable models demonstrably improve clinical interpretability and decision-making compared to black-box EEG classifiers. By revealing which EEG features drive predictions, XAI transforms opaque classifiers into decision-support tools that align with known neurophysiology. XAI approaches significantly enhanced model transparency, enabling the identification of disorder-specific EEG patterns and supporting more informed clinician decision-making and improved diagnostic confidence. In other words, when EEG models highlight key features (e.g. through SHAP or LIME importance rankings), clinicians gain insight into why a patient is classified as depressed, anxious, or bipolar. This transparency supports targeted interventions and patient stratification: e.g., SHAP-based models have been used not only for binary diagnosis but also for grading depression severity, where the most important EEG features (frontal/parietal beta, occipital alpha) align with known biomarkers of severe depression. Explainable models enhance diagnostic confidence and interpretability, enabling clinicians to trust and act upon automated EEG-based predictions. In sum, XAI-driven explanations bridge the gap between raw EEG metrics and clinical judgment, thereby improving the validity of EEG biomarkers in practice (e.g., by confirming that high frontal alpha asymmetry corresponds to MDD, which bolsters clinician confidence).

Which EEG-derived features (spectral, time-frequency, connectivity) selected by XAI approaches show the strongest reproducibility and physiological validity across diverse datasets, and how do these explainable selections align with established neural mechanisms of each disorder?

Across EEG-XAI studies, spectral power and connectivity features dominate, and XAI consistently highlights those with strong physiological grounding. The review finds that roughly half of studies use spectral-power metrics and half use connectivity measures, whereas entropy or nonlinear metrics are rare. Importantly, the features identified by XAI are typically reproducible and neurobiologically plausible. For example, many XAI analyses pinpoint frontal alpha asymmetry and frontal-parietal beta power as top predictors: Explainable models consistently identified physiologically valid biomarkers, such as frontal alpha asymmetry in MDD and frontal–parietal beta connectivity in BD. Indeed, SHAP and LIME attributions have repeatedly surfaced classic depression/anxiety markers – Several XAI studies highlighted increased parietal alpha activity and altered limbic rhythm connectivity, suggesting abnormal attentional regulation and emotion processing mechanisms in mood and anxiety disorders. For instance, SHAP-based feature selection in a recent MDD study highlighted left-parietal beta and occipital alpha power as most important, matching well-established MDD biomarkers [27]. Likewise, LIME applied to anxiety models often flags elevated occipital beta and frontal gamma features, aligning with arousal-related pathophysiology. In sum, XAI-selected features show both reproducibility across tasks and biological validity: by comparing attributions across datasets, researchers find that the same spectral/connectivity bands recur as most important, and these correspond to established neural signatures of each disorder. This convergence gives confidence that XAI is not “inventing” spurious signals but rather surfacing reproducible EEG biomarkers that can be validated against known neurobiological models of mood and anxiety disorders.

What ML algorithms have been used to classify mood and anxiety disorders based on EEG data, and what are their performance metrics?

EEG-based mood-disorder classifiers employ a wide range of machine learning algorithms, often achieving very high performance. In the reviewed studies, traditional classifiers (e.g. SVM, Random Forest, KNN) and deep learning models (CNNs, RNNs/LSTMs, graph CNNs, Transformers) are both common. For example, Zhang et al. [54] used a hybrid CNN–LSTM network to screen for MDD with 95.1% accuracy, and Xu et al. [55] deployed an attention-enhanced multi-band CNN to reach 98.5% accuracy. Graph-based and ensemble methods have similarly attained upper-90% accuracies in many tasks. Notably, these high accuracies are consistent across model types: pooled results show average accuracies often exceeding 90% (with many studies reporting > 95%) for distinguishing patients from controls. Traditional ML models also perform well – for instance, SVMs on connectivity features achieved ~92–94% accuracy in social anxiety and bipolar screening. Table 6 in the review (and Figs. 9–10) summarize this performance: deep CNNs dominate recent studies and often yield the highest scores, but even simpler models (e.g. Random Forest on band-power features) frequently yield robust classification (e.g. 93.4% accuracy for bipolar vs. control). In summary, a diverse array of algorithms (from linear SVMs to complex CNNs) has been applied to EEG mood-disorder data, with reported metrics spanning roughly 75–99% accuracy, demonstrating the technical feasibility of EEG-based diagnosis when data and labels are well-controlled.

Which XAI methods have been applied to EEG-based models, and how do they enhance model transparency?

A broad range of post-hoc XAI methods have been applied to EEG models, all aimed at illuminating model decision rules. The most frequently used techniques are feature-importance methods (SHAP, LIME) and saliency/attention methods. For example, many studies applied SHAP or LIME to classical ML models: Saghab Torbati et al. [63] used LIME on an SVM to reveal that frontal beta and occipital gamma power were key for bipolar detection; Shen et al. (2025) used SHAP with a Random Forest to show left-parietal beta and occipital alpha as top predictors of depression severity. Deep networks commonly used attention mechanisms and saliency maps: for instance, Metin et al. [65] applied Class Activation Mapping (CAM) to a CNN, which highlighted prefrontal electrodes (F4, F7, F8, C3) as discriminative in bipolar vs. Control. Attention-augmented CNNs were also reported (e.g. a “multi-dimensional attention” network achieved 95.1% accuracy by automatically weighting spatial–temporal EEG features.

Together, these XAI tools have enhanced model transparency by linking predictions back to EEG physiology. SHAP/LIME explain individual predictions through feature-importance scores, while attention and saliency visualize salient channels or time–frequency regions. Studies using these XAI methods often confirm known EEG biomarkers (e.g., increased frontal beta in MDD) and reveal new spatial patterns, thereby enhancing model transparency and guiding clinical interpretation. By producing intuitive explanations—such as highlighting specific frequency bands or electrode sites—XAI techniques make the models’ reasoning more accessible to clinicians, which is critical for trust, interpretability, and clinical validation. In all cases, by producing intuitive explanations (e.g. highlighting specific frequency bands or electrode sites), XAI techniques make the models’ reasoning accessible to clinicians, which is critical for trust and validation.

What is the methodological robustness of EEG-XAI studies, and what biases may affect their findings?

The methodological rigor of EEG-XAI studies is generally poor, with a majority exhibiting high risk of bias. A structured bias assessment (using QUADAS-2, PROBAST, and MINORS) found that ≈65% of studies had high bias in at least one domain. The most common issues were very small sample sizes, absence of external or independent validation, and incomplete reporting of study design. For example, many high-performing studies (e.g. Pu et al., 2020; Ravan et al. 2018) scored poorly on patient-selection and blinding criteria. QUADAS-2 often flagged retrospective EEG data without clear diagnostic thresholds, while PROBAST highlighted missing data handling and overfitting concerns. Only a few recent works (e.g. Demir et al., 2019; Breakspear et al., 2017) achieved low-to-moderate bias by using larger, multicenter cohorts and rigorous cross-validation.

These biases have serious implications: despite reported accuracies above 95% in many studies, high bias levels underscore a critical gap between technical achievement and clinical deployability. In other words, the impressive performance metrics may not generalize due to methodological flaws such as overfitting on single-site data. Figure 7 further illustrates this issue, showing consistently low methodological quality scores across studies, including those employing XAI approaches. In summary, while EEG-XAI methods show considerable promise, their reproducibility and validity are currently limited by common sources of bias. Future research should prioritize larger and more diverse samples, external validation, and transparent reporting to achieve clinically robust and generalizable models.

What are the translational barriers and ethical implications for deploying EEG-XAI biomarkers in clinical practice?

Translating EEG-XAI biomarkers into practice faces multiple barriers and ethical concerns. Many studies note that even accurate models suffer from limited clinician trust and the absence of regulatory guidelines. For instance, the review highlights “ethical and translational concerns” such as limited clinician trust, lack of regulatory frameworks, and algorithmic bias. In practice, clinicians may hesitate to adopt an EEG-based decision aid if its rationale is not clear – an issue XAI partly mitigates but cannot fully solve. Other barriers include data privacy (EEG data can be sensitive neurophysiology), the need for standardized protocols, and the potential for demographic bias (e.g. models trained on one population may not generalize).

Ethically, transparency is crucial: without explainability, clinicians may hesitate to adopt even highly accurate models. Conversely, well-explained EEG models are more likely to be accepted by clinicians and regulators. However, XAI alone does not automatically ensure fairness or privacy. Governance frameworks, regulatory oversight, and interdisciplinary validation are essential for the ethical deployment of these technologies in clinical settings. The authors emphasize that governance frameworks and interdisciplinary validation are needed for ethical deployment. For example, standardizing how EEG data are collected and anonymized is vital, as is auditing algorithms for bias across sex, age, or ethnicity. In summary, while XAI provides a pathway toward ethically responsible AI (by improving transparency), deploying EEG-XAI tools safely will require addressing clinician education, regulatory approval processes, and fairness considerations alongside technical development.

The field of EEG-XAI biomarker research for mood and anxiety disorders has grown exponentially over the past decade. Although the majority of studies focus on MDD, research on BD and GAD has increased steadily since 2020. This reflects a broadening of EEG-XAI applications beyond mood disorders to transdiagnostic affective conditions (Fig. 11).

Limitations

This review has several limitations. First, the literature search was restricted to English-language publications, which may have led to the exclusion of relevant studies reported in other languages, potentially introducing language bias. Second, the review was not preregistered in a public database such as PROSPERO or OSF, which may limit transparency and reproducibility. Third, only peer-reviewed journal articles were included, excluding preprints and grey literature, which may bias the findings toward more established research. Finally, although every effort was made to conduct a comprehensive search, relevant studies may still have been missed due to indexing limitations or inconsistent terminology in the EEG-XAI field. This review was not preregistered in a public registry such as PROSPERO because it did not involve a meta-analysis. While preregistration is not mandatory for this type of review, future work may benefit from registering protocols to further enhance methodological transparency.

Conclusion

This systematic review critically examined the integration of explainable artificial intelligence (XAI) in EEG-based classification of depression, anxiety, and bipolar disorder across 145 peer-reviewed studies from 2015 to 2025. Our findings reveal a growing yet methodologically heterogeneous body of research where the convergence of neural biomarkers and interpretable machine learning models offers unprecedented opportunities for enhancing psychiatric diagnostics. Notably, the use of spectral features—particularly alpha and beta band powers—and functional connectivity metrics emerged as recurrent and physiologically plausible EEG correlates across disorders. These features, when prioritized through SHAP, LIME, attention mechanisms, or saliency maps, not only achieved high classification accuracies (often > 90%) but also reinforced established neurobiological models of affective dysregulation, such as frontal alpha asymmetry in MDD and hyperconnectivity in GAD.

XAI tools significantly enhanced model transparency, transforming opaque classifiers into clinically interpretable decision-support systems. This interpretability—through local and global feature attributions—allowed for the identification of disorder-specific neurophysiological patterns, thereby facilitating more informed clinician decision-making and improved diagnostic confidence. Yet, despite the promising accuracy metrics, our risk-of-bias assessment revealed that over 65% of the studies demonstrated high or unclear methodological risk, often due to limited sample sizes, poor validation practices, or unbalanced datasets. These methodological deficits limit the generalizability of current EEG-XAI findings and underscore the need for more robust, multicenter, and longitudinal studies.

Furthermore, the translational pathway from algorithm to clinic remains fraught with barriers, including lack of external validation, concerns about patient data privacy, ethical considerations related to algorithmic bias, and insufficient clinician trust in black-box systems. While XAI addresses some of these challenges by offering interpretability, it does not inherently eliminate them. Ethical deployment of EEG-XAI tools in mental healthcare will require rigorous governance frameworks, cross-disciplinary collaboration, and ongoing empirical validation.

In conclusion, XAI-enhanced EEG modeling represents a promising frontier in psychiatric neuroinformatics, offering not only accurate but explainable assessments of complex depressive disorders. Future research should focus on improving methodological rigor, expanding disorder-specific biomarker validation, and advancing the clinical applicability of interpretable AI systems. By bridging neuroscience, machine learning, and clinical psychiatry, EEG-XAI models hold the potential to transform mood and anxiety disorders diagnostics into a more personalized, transparent, and scientifically grounded domain.
