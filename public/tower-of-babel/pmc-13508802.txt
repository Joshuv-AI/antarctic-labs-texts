Machine Learning Techniques to Predict Fetal Nutritional Status
Authors: Neema Mduma, Hudson Laizer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesMalnutrition remains the leading cause of child mortality in Tanzania, with over 34% of children under 5 years of age affected by stunting and approximately 5% experiencing acute malnutrition. This study aimed to develop a machine learning model to predict fetal nutritional status using maternal and clinical data, thereby enabling early risk identification for health workers and parents and facilitating timely intervention. To enhance practical applicability, the model was deployed within a mobile application to provide accessible, real-time predictions that support prompt clinical and behavioral responses.MethodsUsing a dataset collected in Tanzania, the performance of multiple binary classification algorithms—logistic regression, multi-layer perceptron, random forest, extreme gradient boosting, and light gradient boosting machine (LightGBM)—was compared using the geometric mean and F-measure. These models were trained on clinical data from 11,703 pregnant women to predict fetal nutritional status based on maternal and clinical variables.ResultsThe results indicated that the LightGBM algorithm achieved the best overall performance in predicting fetal nutritional status. The most influential predictors included maternal age, weight, fetal age, hemoglobin level, number of meals per day, medical history, and education level. Additionally, 93% of respondents reported satisfaction with the application’s predictive functionality, supporting its potential utility for early intervention in low-resource settings.ConclusionsThese findings highlight the potential of data-driven approaches to address public health challenges in maternal and child health. The proposed model may enable healthcare providers to make timely, informed decisions that improve maternal and fetal outcomes, ultimately contributing to the reduction of child malnutrition in Tanzania.Keywords: Nutrition, Machine Learning, Maternal, Children, Tanzania

Introduction

Malnutrition is the leading cause of child mortality in Tanzania [1,2]. According to the 2015–2016 Demographic and Health Survey, approximately 3.1 million children under 5 years of age are affected by stunting, representing a prevalence of 34%, while 5% of children under 5 experience acute malnutrition, defined as wasting or low weight-for-height [3,4]. Child malnutrition is driven by multiple factors, including maternal malnutrition, suboptimal infant feeding practices, poor-quality healthcare, and limited access to nutrition information [5,6]. Tanzania also faces a double burden of malnutrition among women of reproductive age, with 28% classified as overweight or obese and 10% as underweight [7,8]. Both extremes are associated with adverse fetal outcomes: undernutrition is linked to intrauterine growth restriction and low birth weight, whereas overweight and obesity are associated with complications such as gestational diabetes and macrosomia, which also affect child health outcomes. Malnutrition during early childhood has long-term consequences, including impaired physical and cognitive development and an increased risk of mortality [9,10]. Therefore, ensuring adequate nutrition among children under 5 years of age is essential, as malnutrition is associated with long-term cognitive deficits, reduced educational attainment, decreased adult productivity, and broader economic losses [11,12]. Traditional approaches for assessing fetal nutritional status, including clinical evaluation, maternal anthropometry, and ultrasound, have notable limitations, such as restricted access, subjective interpretation, and limited capacity for early prediction. These limitations underscore the need for accessible, data-driven predictive tools.

Machine learning has gained increasing attention across multiple sectors, including education, healthcare, and agriculture [13,14], and has demonstrated strong potential for predicting outcomes and identifying at-risk populations in clinical settings [15,16]. Its application in maternal and child health supports early risk detection and timely intervention. In this context, predicting fetal nutritional status using machine learning offers a promising approach to addressing malnutrition by providing healthcare workers with early, actionable insights for targeted care [17]. Given the high prevalence of child malnutrition and ongoing maternal health challenges in Tanzania, the integration of machine learning into this domain may play an important role in strengthening the healthcare system by enabling data-driven, scalable, and contextually relevant preventive strategies [18].

Literature Review

In Tanzania, fetal nutritional status is traditionally assessed using clinical and anthropometric methods. Common approaches include monitoring maternal weight gain, body mass index, mid-upper arm circumference, and fetal growth through ultrasound imaging [19]. These indicators are used to identify intrauterine growth restriction and to predict the risk of low birth weight. Hemoglobin levels are also routinely assessed to detect maternal anemia, which is strongly associated with impaired fetal growth [20].

Despite their importance in evaluating fetal development and maternal health, these methods have several limitations, particularly in low-resource settings. Access to routine ultrasound and comprehensive antenatal screening is often limited in rural and underserved areas due to shortages of equipment and trained personnel [5]. Although maternal anthropometric indicators are practical and widely used, they may fail to capture micronutrient deficiencies or the cumulative effects of poor maternal health [21]. Furthermore, these approaches are primarily reactive rather than predictive, as they typically identify problems only after clinical symptoms have emerged, thereby limiting their effectiveness in preventing malnutrition-related outcomes [22].

Currently, predictive tools are rarely used in routine maternal care to assess fetal nutritional risk prior to the onset of complications. This gap highlights the potential value of machine learning techniques, which can integrate clinical and demographic variables to identify early risk patterns. When adapted to local contexts, such models may enhance prenatal care in Tanzania by providing data-driven support for early intervention.

Methods

Dataset Description

Data were collected through maternal questionnaires and detailed clinical evaluations conducted at four clinics located in the Urambo and Babati districts of Tanzania. Figure 1 illustrates the geographic distribution of the study sites, which represent areas with differing levels of healthcare accessibility and nutritional profiles. These sites were selected to capture demographic and nutritional diversity, with Urambo representing a rural setting with limited healthcare services and Babati representing an area with comparatively better access to care. This contrast facilitated the collection of a wide range of maternal and clinical data, thereby enhancing the generalizability of the model. Data collection was conducted from July to October 2024. Ethical approval was obtained, informed consent was secured from all participants, and all data were anonymized to ensure privacy and compliance with ethical standards.

Map of the study area showing Urambo and Babati districts in Tanzania.

Strict data handling and confidentiality protocols were maintained throughout the study. Access to the dataset was restricted to authorized researchers, and all data were stored in encrypted formats on secure servers hosted by the Nelson Mandela African Institution of Science and Technology. Data sharing followed institutional policies and national ethical guidelines to ensure participant confidentiality. No personal or location-specific identifiers were included during model training or mobile application deployment.

The dataset comprised 11,703 records from pregnant women and included 15 variables (Table 1), which were used to train and validate the model. Data were collected during routine antenatal visits, primarily between the second and third trimesters of pregnancy (approximately 20–36 weeks of gestation). Maternal clinical and sociodemographic information, including age, weight, hemoglobin level, and dietary habits, was recorded at the time of data collection. The outcome variable, fetal nutritional status, was assessed at delivery or at the end of pregnancy using fetal growth parameters and gestational age-adjusted weight percentiles. In this study, fetal nutritional status was defined as a binary outcome (normal vs. undernourished) based on established fetal growth indicators, including estimated fetal weight and gestational age-adjusted percentiles, in accordance with World Health Organization and Tanzanian Ministry of Health guidelines. Classification labels were validated by attending clinicians using standard antenatal and delivery records to ensure accuracy and consistency.

Prior to model development, several preprocessing steps were applied to ensure data quality. Missing values were imputed using the mean for numerical variables and the mode for categorical variables. Numerical features were normalized to ensure consistency across algorithms. Feature importance was assessed using a random forest model implemented in scikit-learn, and the final model was trained using the most influential variables. To address class imbalance, the synthetic minority over-sampling technique was applied to improve model generalizability across classes.

Classification Algorithms

Fetal nutritional status was predicted using binary classification algorithms, as the outcome variable was categorized into two classes. Because these algorithms differ in their underlying modeling approaches, five methods were compared: logistic regression (LR), multi-layer perceptron (MLP), random forest (RF), extreme gradient boosting (XGBoost), and light gradient boosting machine (LightGBM).

The selection of algorithms was guided by their technical performance characteristics, contextual relevance, and suitability for the classification problem. LR was included because of its well-established utility in modeling binary outcomes. MLP was selected for its flexibility and ability to capture complex non-linear relationships. RF was included because it can improve predictive accuracy by aggregating the outputs of multiple decision trees. To strengthen the comparative framework and incorporate more advanced machine learning approaches, XGBoost and LightGBM were also evaluated. The inclusion of multiple algorithms enabled a robust comparison and ensured that the final model selection was informed by both predictive performance and clinical interpretability.

Experimental Procedure

The dataset was divided into training (60%), validation (20%), and test (20%) sets, as summarized in Figure 2, which outlines the experimental workflow and the cross-validation and evaluation procedures used to assess model reliability. In this study, 10-fold out-of-bag cross-validation was performed, and all selected classification algorithms were run 10 times using different training, validation, and test splits. The dataset was partitioned into 10 approximately equal subsets; during each run, the model was trained on nine subsets and evaluated on the remaining subset. Performance results from all folds were then averaged to obtain the final evaluation metrics. An independent test set was used for final assessment of the best-performing models to ensure that no data leakage occurred at any stage.

Experimental procedure showing the distribution of the dataset into training, validation, and testing sets for model development and evaluation.

Mobile Application Development

The machine learning model that achieved the most satisfactory predictive performance was deployed in a mobile application developed using the Rapid Application Development approach, which emphasizes rapid prototyping and continuous user feedback (Figure 3). This approach was selected to promote active stakeholder involvement throughout the development process. The application was built in Android Studio and refined iteratively in response to user feedback until the final version met user expectations. Furthermore, the application was developed in Swahili to enable local users who are not familiar with English to interact with it easily.

Although RF showed strong validation performance, some metrics differed across models; for example, the test-set Gm was slightly higher for MLP, reflecting differences in how individual algorithms balanced sensitivity and specificity across datasets. Among all models evaluated, LightGBM was identified as the most satisfactory model for predicting fetal nutritional status because it demonstrated consistently strong performance across both validation and test sets, indicating stable generalizability. Its selection for deployment was based on a balanced assessment of Fm, Gm, and overall generalization performance rather than on any single metric alone.

To enhance reliability, all algorithms were evaluated using 10-fold cross-validation, and the averaged results reduced the influence of random variation. Although LightGBM achieved the best overall performance, its advantage over XGBoost was relatively small. In addition, the analysis was limited by the size of the dataset and its regional coverage, which may restrict the generalizability of the findings to broader populations.

Mobile Application Results

The mobile application, named “LisheYetu,” was developed to deploy the best-performing model. The application allows users to enter the variables that contributed most strongly to the prediction of fetal nutritional status. It also incorporates user-requested features, including reminders for upcoming clinic visits, information on nearby health facilities with contact details for relevant practitioners, and recommendations for locally available seasonal foods (Figure 5).

Discussion

The results of this study indicate that advanced ensemble methods, particularly LightGBM and XGBoost, outperformed more traditional algorithms such as RF, MLP, and LR in predicting fetal nutritional status using maternal and clinical features. LightGBM achieved the highest test scores for both Gm and Fm, indicating strong generalizability and consistent performance across data splits. XGBoost also performed well, further supporting its utility for structured clinical data. Although RF performed strongly on the validation set, its lower test-set performance suggests possible overfitting or reduced robustness for this dataset.

From a clinical perspective, these findings highlight the potential of machine learning models to improve early identification of at-risk pregnancies and to support targeted nutritional interventions within antenatal care programs. Integrating machine learning tools into Tanzania’s maternal health system could improve screening efficiency, resource allocation, and overall care delivery, particularly in underserved areas where routine monitoring remains limited. By translating clinical and demographic indicators into predictive insights, the model may assist healthcare providers in prioritizing high-risk pregnancies for timely intervention, thereby supporting data-driven decision-making, strengthening nutrition policy implementation, and promoting more equitable maternal healthcare across Tanzania.

Feature importance rankings identified maternal age, weight, fetal age, hemoglobin level, number of meals per day, medical history, and education level as the most influential predictors of fetal nutritional outcomes. Clinically, these variables are plausibly related to fetal growth and development. Maternal age, whether adolescent or advanced, is associated with increased risk of low birth weight and impaired fetal growth because of biological and age-related complications. Meal frequency reflects dietary adequacy, and infrequent meals may indicate undernutrition and limited nutrient availability for fetal development. Hemoglobin level reflects anemia status, which is closely associated with restricted fetal growth and adverse pregnancy outcomes.

Maternal weight and fetal age reflect nutritional reserves and developmental progression, whereas education level and medical history may serve as indicators of health literacy, healthcare access, and long-term health behaviors that influence pregnancy outcomes. These findings are consistent with previous research identifying maternal nutrition, health status, and socioeconomic factors as key determinants of fetal growth and health outcomes [20].

The influence of these top-ranked features can be further understood within the Tanzanian context. Maternal education plays an important role in shaping health-seeking behavior, dietary knowledge, and adherence to antenatal care recommendations. Women with higher levels of education are more likely to access nutrition information, diversify household diets, and support adequate maternal and fetal nutrition. Similarly, the number of meals consumed per day reflects both household food security and dietary practices; low meal frequency may indicate underlying economic constraints and food insufficiency, which remain common in rural areas of Tanzania. Maternal hemoglobin levels and weight also reflect broader public health challenges, including anemia, micronutrient deficiencies, and limited access to nutrient-rich foods.

The study also introduced a mobile application designed to assist nutrition specialists, pregnant women, and healthcare practitioners in predicting fetal nutritional status. A similar approach was reported by Hebden et al. [23], who described mobile applications for nutrition and physical activity behavior change that were associated with improved dietary habits [24]. The application developed in the present study was designed to reflect both technical requirements and user needs in order to maximize usability and contextual relevance. With smartphone use in Tanzania exceeding 22 million users [25], mobile technology may offer a practical and accessible platform for delivering this type of health intervention.

A preliminary feasibility assessment was conducted to evaluate the practicality of deploying the model through the LisheYetu mobile application. The assessment considered smartphone penetration, internet connectivity, user digital literacy, and integration with existing maternal health systems in Tanzania. The mobile application was designed to operate effectively in both online and offline modes. Its lightweight design enables users in areas with limited or unreliable internet connectivity to access its key features. Its lightweight design also supports compatibility with low-cost Android devices, and user feedback obtained during pilot testing suggested good usability and acceptability. However, large-scale implementation would require collaboration with health authorities to establish data governance procedures, support regular model updating, and provide user training to promote sustainability and ethical compliance.

In conclusion, the LightGBM algorithm demonstrated strong performance in predicting fetal nutritional status. Despite these promising results, the findings should be interpreted cautiously because of the dataset’s limited diversity and the absence of longitudinal data. Future work should focus on expanding data collection across multiple regions and incorporating dietary intake and environmental variables. In addition, hyperparameter optimization may further improve predictive performance and strengthen the use of data-driven approaches to address maternal and child health challenges.
