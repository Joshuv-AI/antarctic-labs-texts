Analysis of rational prescription of medicines by village clinics: a repeated cross-sectional study of a province in eastern China
Authors: Chunyan Song, Zhongming Chen, Wenqiang Yin, Zhiqiang Feng, Xiaoning Hao
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Abstract ABSTRACTObjectiveTo analyse the rational prescription of medicines by village clinics following China’s implementation of the New Healthcare System Reform (NHCSR).DesignRetrospective study.SettingVillage clinics in a province in eastern China.Primary and secondary outcome measuresOur evaluation was based on the core drug use indicators reported in the study ‘How to Investigate Drug Use in Health Facilities: Selected Drug Use Indicators’.ResultsThe level of rational use of medicines (RUMs) in village clinics improved in 2018 compared with 2009; the average number of drugs per prescription decreased from 3.66 to 2.77, the percentage of prescriptions including antibiotics decreased from 65.40% to 46.00%, and the percentage of prescriptions including injections decreased from 55.75% to 37.48% (all p<0.01), whereas the percentage of drugs prescribed from the list of essential medicines increased from 66.50% to 80.34%, and the percentage of drugs prescribed by generic names increased from 61.29% to 70.65% (all p<0.01).ConclusionThe level of RUMs improved after the implementation of the NHCSR, however, it was found that there was still a gap between the level of RUMs in 2018 and the international reference value. Financial investments, electronic prescription systems, improved supervision and regular training are essential actions that must be implemented.Keywords: Primary Care, HEALTH SERVICES ADMINISTRATION & MANAGEMENT, Health Services

Introduction

The rational use of medicines (RUMs) is the use of the most effective medicines with the fewest side effects and the least cost.1 The irrational use of medicines remains a major public health problem worldwide. The WHO estimates that >50% of medicines are prescribed, dispensed or sold inappropriately.2 Medicines that are inaccurately prescribed or taken or are of poor quality can result in the wastage of scarce resources and widespread health hazards.2

The irrational use of medicines is common in China, particularly in rural areas, and polypharmacy and overprescription of antibiotics remain common.3,7 In addition, studies have reported concerns regarding the prescription of village clinics (VCs) in some areas, including improper drug administration channels, abuse of intravenous injection methods and an excessive number of prescription drugs.8 9 VCs are the foundation of the three-tiered health service system in rural areas in China; therefore, they perform basic medical diagnosis and treatment of common and frequently occurring diseases, provide basic public health services to rural residents, and are crucial to the promotion and protection of the health of rural residents. Since 2017, the annual number of visits to VCs has exceeded 1.3 billion, accounting for >31% of total visits to primary healthcare institutions.10

In 1993, the WHO and the International Network for Rational Use of Drugs developed drug use indicators, including core and complementary indicators.11 Compared with complementary drug use indicators, core drug use indicators are more informative and feasible, less likely to fluctuate over time or according to location, and easier to measure drug use.12,14 The core drug use indicators are five prescription indicators with reference values.15

In March 2009, the Chinese government launched the New Healthcare System Reform (NHCSR) and established the National Essential Medicine System (NEMS). The NEMS is the core of promoting the RUMs.16 To improve the level of RUMs in primary medical institutions, the NEMS proposes establishing a system of priority and rational use of essential medicines, requiring all government-run primary medical institutions to supply national essential medicines for use.

As of 2019 there were 16 prefecture-level cities in Shandong province (with 17 prefecture-level cities in total in 2018 and before), a province located in eastern China with a population of 100.7 million individuals (including 38.76 million individuals in rural areas) and a per capita gross domestic product of ¥70 653. NEMS was implemented in Shandong province on 1 March 2010. As of June 2011, NEMS had been implemented in all government-run primary medical institutions in Shandong province.

Since the implementation of the NHCSR, changes in RUMs in medical institutions have increasingly attracted the attention of health management researchers. A study by Chen revealed that the rational use of outpatient medications in township hospitals improved after the implementation of NEMS.17 Consistent with previous findings, the findings of a study by Song et al revealed a positive correlation between NEMS and RUMs.18 However, contrasting findings have been reported in other studies. The results of a study by Liu et al showed that the reforms neither radically changed the behaviour of medical workers nor affected the prescription rate of antibiotics.19 Lu et al found that NEMS was unlikely to positively impact RUMs.20

Previous studies fully focused on RUMs after the implementation of the NHCSR in China; however, most were conducted based on investigations in the early stage of NHCSR and were mainly cross-sectional studies that mostly focused on hospitals. Insufficient attention has been paid to VCs, which are the primary healthcare institutions for rural residents. Therefore, it is imperative to conduct multistage surveys of VCs. In the present study, we aimed to investigate the changes in RUMs based on prescriptions by VCs through tracking surveys conducted in 2009, 2012 and 2018, and to explore the possible reasons for these changes from the perspective of NHCSR. The results of the present study provide up-to-date evidence and a reference for improving the level of RUMs based on prescriptions by VCs.

Methods

Study design and setting

The data in this study were obtained from three surveys conducted in Shandong province in 2009, 2012 and 2018, respectively. In 2009, a baseline survey was conducted using a multistage sampling method. First, according to the economic level, Jinan, Rizhao and Jining were selected from the 17 prefecture-level cities in Shandong province, representing economically developed, moderately developed and underdeveloped regions, respectively. Two counties with different economic levels, three townships and two VCs were randomly selected from each prefecture-level city, county and township, respectively. Follow-up surveys were conducted in 2012 and 2018; however, due to urbanisation and regional health development in recent years, some VCs were abolished or merged and could not be traced, resulting in a substantial reduction in the sample size in 2018.

Sampling and sample size determination

Thirty-six, 33 and 17 VCs were surveyed in 2009, 2012 and 2018, respectively.

Regarding the prescription sampling method, the number of prescriptions by VCs in May, June and July 2009 was determined using the principle of mechanical sampling. One prescription was taken every ((N/50)–1) (where N is the total number of prescriptions by a VC for the designated month).21 If a prescription was damaged, the previous prescription was selected, and the number of prescriptions by each VC was 30–50. The same method was used in 2012 and 2018 to investigate and track prescriptions by VCs during the same months (May, June and July). After removing prescriptions with missing items, non-standard writing or illegible handwriting, 818, 735 and 763 valid prescriptions were obtained in 2009, 2012 and 2018, respectively (table 1).

Patient and public involvement

Statistical analysis

First, a descriptive analysis was performed on the RUMs based on prescriptions. Then, the Kruskal-Wallis H and χ2 tests were used to analyse the changes in RUMs based on prescriptions in 2009, 2012 and 2018.

Results

Data on patients who received prescriptions were analysed. The average age of these patients was 39.01±23.10 years (n=522), 39.54±23.26 years (n=503) and 47.31±22.40 years (n=651) in 2009, 2012 and 2018, respectively. The most common diseases in the patients were respiratory diseases, accounting for 57.32% (n=137), 56.56% (n=194) and 43.87% (n=308) of all cases in 2009, 2012 and 2018, respectively.

In 2009, 2012 and 2018, the average number of drugs per prescription was 3.66, 3.29 and 2.77, respectively, the percentage of prescriptions including antibiotics was 65.40%, 62.31% and 46.00%, respectively, and the percentage of prescriptions including injections was 55.75%, 49.66% and 37.48%, respectively, all of which showed a decreasing trend (p<0.01). Furthermore, in 2009, 2012 and 2018, the percentage of drugs prescribed from the essential medicine list was 66.50%, 78.50% and 80.34%, respectively, and the percentage of drugs prescribed using generic names was 61.29%, 70.90% and 70.65%, respectively, all of which initially showed an increasing trend and then stabilised (p<0.01) (table 3).

By analysing the RUMs based on prescriptions by VCs in 2009, 2012 and 2018, we found that the level of RUMs based on prescriptions improved. However, compared with the international reference values recommended by WHO,15 the RUMs in VCs in 2018 did not meet the recommended value. The average number of drugs per prescription, the percentage of prescriptions including antibiotics, and the percentage of prescriptions including injections were all higher than the international reference values recommended by the WHO. Conversely, the percentages of drugs prescribed from the essential medicine list and using generic names were far lower than the international reference values recommended by the WHO.

Discussion

This study has shown that since the implementation of the NHCSR, RUMs based on prescriptions by VCs has somewhat improved. And it provides quantitative baseline data for monitoring and improving the level of RUMs based on prescriptions by VCs.

First, data analysis revealed that the average number of drugs per prescription decreased significantly after the implementation of the NHCSR. This finding is similar to that of Yin et al, who found that the average number of drugs per prescription decreased in all regions surveyed, including a 0.29 decrease in Zhejiang province in China between 2009 and 2010.22 The average number of drugs per prescription not only reflects drug usage in medical institutions but also the prescription behaviour of medical personnel.23 Studies have shown that the amount of prescription medication is consistent with the phenomenon in the actual medical service process that some medical personnel issue ‘large prescriptions’ (over five types of drugs per prescription) in pursuit of economic benefits.17 24 Owing to the implementation of the NEMS, the drug income of doctors (including health professionals holding the certificates of practising physicians, practising physician assistants and VC doctors) in VCs mainly depends on subsidies for essential medicines, which have improved unreasonable drug use behaviour driven by economic interests. In addition, after the implementation of the NEMS and with an increase in relevant training, the level of RUMs based on prescriptions by doctors in VCs improved.25 Another contributing factor could be that the average number of drugs per prescription, which is one of the normative assessment contents of prescriptions, is regularly checked by the health management department, which somewhat standardises the prescription behaviour of doctors in VCs. Therefore, the number of prescription drugs showed a downward trend from 3.66 in 2009 to 3.29 in 2012 and then to 2.77 in 2018.

Second, the present study showed that after the implementation of the NHCSR in China, the percentage of drugs prescribed from the essential medicine list significantly increased from 66.50% in 2009 to 80.34% in 2018. This finding is consistent with that of Song et al, who surveyed the use of drugs in 146 township health centres in four provinces of China and found that the percentage of drugs prescribed from the essential medicine list increased from 63.33% in 2009 to 79.89% in 2010.18 Prioritising the use of essential medicines is crucial to promoting RUMs and is also the original aim of the NHCSR for implementing the NEMS.26 Shandong province implemented NEMS on 1 March 2010, and as of June 2011, NEMS had been implemented in all government-run primary medical institutions in Shandong province. Therefore, the percentage of drugs prescribed from the essential medicine list was significantly higher in 2012 than in 2009.

Third, the percentage of prescriptions including antibiotics decreased significantly after the implementation of NHCSR. This finding is consistent with that of Song et al and Zhu. Song et al investigated the use of drugs in primary medical institutions in Heilongjiang province and found that the rate of antimicrobial drug use decreased from 53.29% in 2008 to 38.62% in 2013.27 Furthermore, Zhu found that after the implementation of NEMS in primary medical institutions, the rate of antimicrobial drug use decreased from 58.01% to 44.48%.28 The results of the present study showed that the percentage of prescriptions including antibiotics decreased significantly in 2018 compared with 2012. Since the implementation of the NHCSR, the government has continuously strengthened the management of the clinical application of antibiotics in medical institutions and has successively introduced relevant policy documents promoting the rational application of antibiotics, including the ‘Management Measures for Clinical Application of Antibacterial Drugs’ and ‘Guiding Principles for Clinical Application of Antibacterial Drugs (2015 Edition)’. The number of antimicrobial drugs purchased and prescribed in medical institutions has been strictly controlled. Therefore, the rate of antimicrobial drug use continued to decline in 2012 and 2018 compared with 2009. Notably, the more doctors in VCs receive essential medicine training, the more they attach importance to RUMs.29 After the implementation of NEMS, the health management department strengthened its training in essential medicine. This has somewhat improved the level of RUMs among doctors in VCs and reduced the percentage of prescriptions including antibiotics.

Fourth, the present study revealed that the percentage of prescriptions including injections decreased significantly after the implementation of the NHCSR. This finding is consistent with that of Zhao et al and Wu et al. Zhao et al conducted a survey of prescriptions by VCs and found that the percentage of prescriptions including injections decreased from 56.9% to 49.3% after the implementation of NEMS.30 Consistent with previous results, the results of a study by Wu et al showed that the percentage of prescriptions including injections in a primary medical institution in Anhui province decreased from 51.59% to 37.04% after the implementation of NEMS.31 Because of the particularity of the dosage form of the injection drug, once an adverse reaction occurs, the harm to the human body is more serious than that caused by the oral dosage form. According to the Annual Report for National Adverse Drug Reaction Monitoring,32 in 2021, the incidence of injection-induced adverse drug reactions (ADRs) accounted for 55.3% of ADR/event reports, far exceeding the incidence of ADRs induced by oral and intramuscular administration. Related studies have shown that the prescription rate of injections is not only associated with the supervision of local health management departments and the reasonable level of medication prescribed by medical personnel but also with the degree of financial subsidies provided by local governments to VCs in which NEMS was implemented.33 After the implementation of the NHCSR, NEMS was implemented in medical institutions at the grass roots, increasing financial investment and promoting awareness of RUMs among medical personnel, thereby reducing the prescription rate of injections. In addition, studies have shown that the use of injections is directly proportional to that of antibiotics.34 This finding is consistent with that of the present study, which showed that the use of injections and antibiotics declined simultaneously after the implementation of the NHCSR.

However, the present study indicated that the percentage of prescriptions including antibiotics and that of prescriptions including injections in 2018 remained lower than the international reference value, which is consistent with the findings of Meng et al, who investigated the prescriptions by VCs in 2019 and found that the prescription rates of antimicrobial drugs and injections were 36.50% and 28.40%, respectively.35 First, the abuse of antibacterial drugs and injections has always been a major concern in China’s medical and health fields. Medication habits portrayed by medical personnel over a long period cannot be fundamentally changed in a short period.36 Second, regulations are in place in some regions that charge general diagnosis and treatment fees based on the content of rural medical services, which spur doctors in VCs to prescribe antibiotics and injections.37 Third, some rural residents have poor medication habits, as they believe that antibiotics are ‘universal drugs’ and actively request doctors in VCs to prescribe antibiotics. To ‘make quick decisions’ and ‘get better quickly,’ they prefer to use intravenous infusion. This finding is similar to that of Tang et al.38

Finally, the results of the present study revealed that after the implementation of the NHCSR, the percentage of drugs prescribed using generic names increased significantly from 61.29% in 2009 to 70.65% in 2018. This finding is consistent with that of Lin et al. In a meta-analysis, Lin et al found that the percentage of drugs prescribed using generic names increased by 14.1% after the implementation of NEMS.39 The use of generic drug names can reduce the economic benefits of drugs to medical personnel, protect patients’ legitimate rights and interests (such as the right to purchase drugs independently) and promote fairness in health services.40 Research has shown that the higher the rate of using generic drug names, the higher the safety and rationality of patient medication.41 The increase in the percentage of drugs prescribed using generic names may be associated with the implementation of NEMS. Notably, some doctors in VCs have improved RUMs by prescribing drugs using their generic names. However, there was little change in the percentage of drugs prescribed using generic names between 2012 and 2018. This is primarily because prescriptions by VCs are mostly handwritten by doctors. For convenience and speed, doctors in VCs often use abbreviations or trade names to replace some complex and lengthy generic drug names, such as ‘0.9% NS’ instead of 0.9% sodium chloride injection, ‘5% GS’ instead of 5% glucose injection, and ‘654–2’ instead of raceanisodamine hydrochloride injection. It is difficult to completely change this long-standing writing habit within a short period. In addition, the health management department did not set up an assessment and inspection content for the use of generic drug names, only verbally requiring standardised writing or the use of generic drug names. The lack of supervision also resulted in a lack of significant improvement in the rate of using generic drug names in VCs.

This study has some limitations. First, as the influencing factors were not investigated, the reasons for the changes in RUMs based on prescriptions by VCs may not be limited to those mentioned in this study. Second, since this study mainly analysed the RUMs in VCs, the characteristics and training of doctors in VCs were not analysed. Finally, given that the VCs were relatively scattered, it was difficult to conduct a large-sample follow-up survey. Owing to the influence of the sample size, the results of this study may have limited representativeness for RUMs based on prescriptions by VCs in China.

Conclusions

The level of RUMs based on prescriptions by VCs improved after the implementation of the NHCSR. However, it was found that there was still a gap between the level of RUMs in 2018 and the international reference value, which reflected that there was still much room for improvement in RUMs at the grass-roots level. Therefore, based on the findings and discussion in the present study, we put forward several suggestions. First, the pharmacy administration should be further strengthened at the grass-roots level, including introduction of an electronic prescribing system and regular assessment of medication use. Second, the government needs to increase financial investment and ensure the implementation of NEMS. Finally, it is necessary to conduct RUMs training, enhance the awareness of RUMs among doctors in VCs and correct the bad medication habits of rural residents through health education.
