From Prevention to Rescue
Authors: Ammar M. Killu, Mayra Guerrero
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Corresponding Author

Transcatheter mitral valve replacement (TMVR) has emerged as an important option for select patients with severe mitral annular calcification (MAC) and symptomatic mitral valve dysfunction who are poor candidates for conventional surgery. Early experience from the TMVR in MAC Global Registry established procedural feasibility but also underscored the price of this anatomy, with a 1-year mortality of 54%.1 More contemporary prospective data from the MITRAL (Mitral Implantation of TRAnscatheter vaLves) trial refined this experience, showing 1-year mortality of 34.5% (transatrial 38.5%, transseptal 26.7%), with the vast majority of survivors in NYHA functional class I or II at 1 year.2 Longer-term follow-up has reinforced the clinical value of this approach among survivors, with sustained symptom improvement and stable valve function in the MITRAL cohort at 5 years.3 These data define both the promise of TMVR in MAC and its persistent anatomic price. Chief among these limitations is left ventricular outflow tract (LVOT) obstruction.

Against that backdrop, the present report by Katrapati et al4 is important not simply because it is novel, but because it advances a practical and timely idea: radiofrequency (RF) septal modification may serve not only as a pre-emptive strategy to enable TMVR, but also as a rescue therapy after TMVR when clinically significant LVOT obstruction has already declared itself. This distinction matters—in a field where candidacy is often determined by millimeters and gradients, this case reminds us that procedural success sometimes depends less on perfect prediction than on having an adaptable bailout strategy.

TMVR in MAC: Progress With an Anatomic Price

The case is instructive on several levels. A predicted neoLVOT of 219 mm2 would not traditionally be viewed as prohibitive, yet the patient developed a marked postprocedural gradient the following day. Although contemporary imaging has transformed preprocedural planning, no single cut point can fully capture the physiology of LVOT compromise after TMVR. This is a reminder that LVOT risk after TMVR is not binary. Indeed, the neoLVOT is not a fixed anatomic number, it is a dynamic geometry influenced by various factors, including septal thickness, valve skirt position, ventricular size, aortomitral angulation, loading conditions, and phase-specific flow. What matters is not merely the area, but the mechanism.

From Prevention to Precision Rescue

Alcohol septal ablation remains an important option, but it is constrained by septal perforator anatomy and carries a meaningful risk of conduction injury and pacemaker implantation. LAMPOON (Intentional Laceration of the Anterior Mitral Leaflet to Prevent Left Ventricular Outflow Tract Obstruction During Transcatheter Mitral Valve Implantation), BATMAN (Balloon-Assisted Translocation of the Mitral Anterior Leaflet), and, more recently, SESAME (Septal Scoring Along the Midline Endocardium) have expanded the armamentarium considerably, but they do not solve the same problem in every patient and are laborious procedures. Leaflet-directed strategies are most effective when the dominant mechanism is anterior leaflet displacement into the LVOT. Septal strategies become especially relevant when the limiting substrate is septal bulk, basal septal protrusion, or anticipated interaction between the transcatheter valve stent and the septum.

Our group and collaborators have helped build the septal-modification pathway that increasingly makes TMVR procedures feasible. The first described case and subsequent case series have demonstrated that pre-emptive RF modification could enlarge the outflow tract sufficiently to permit TMVR in otherwise ineligible patients.5,6 This case also illustrates why RF septal modification is attractive. Unlike alcohol septal ablation, RF lesions are not limited by coronary anatomy. They can be sculpted at the exact site of neoLVOT compromise. The use of a transseptal platform, intracardiac echocardiography, and zero-fluoroscopic lesion delivery further underscores how mature electrophysiology tools can be repurposed for structural complications. Electroanatomic mapping, when used, offers an additional advantage by helping define the conduction system and potentially lowering the risk of iatrogenic heart block. In that sense, RF ablation offers something uniquely valuable in structural intervention: anatomy-independent, focal, image-guided tissue modification. Unlike those previous RF reports, which were pre-emptive, the present case extends the concept into the true bailout setting after valve implantation, representing an important step forward.

Enthusiasm, however, should be matched by humility. RF septal reduction after TMVR is conceptually elegant, but several important questions remain unanswered. What lesion set is optimal? Should the target be limited to the point of prosthetic-skirt contact, or should a broader basal septal segment be modified? How much of the immediate gradient reduction reflects stunning, and how much reflects durable remodeling? Could edema paradoxically worsen obstruction in selected patients? Should invasive gradient assessment be incorporated routinely? And how should operators balance efficacy against the competing risks of heart block, thromboembolism, transient worsening from edema, or collateral injury to adjacent structures or the prosthesis itself? These are not reasons for hesitation; they are the agenda for the next phase of investigation.

Prospective multicenter registries will now be essential to define patient selection, lesion strategy, procedural endpoints, conduction outcomes, temporal remodeling, and durability. More broadly, TMVR programs increasingly need a dedicated LVOT toolbox rather than a single favored technique.

This report is therefore more than a technical success. It represents the next step in a progression that this field, and this group, have helped shape: recognition of LVOT obstruction as a central barrier to TMVR, refinement of imaging-based risk prediction, development of transseptal TMVR for MAC, introduction of prophylactic LVOT modification, and now demonstration that catheter-based RF septal therapy may rescue a patient after obstruction has already occurred. That is how new paradigms emerge: first as a workaround, then as a reproducible strategy, and eventually as part of routine planning. For selected patients with post-TMVR LVOT obstruction, RF septal modification may be poised to become exactly that.
