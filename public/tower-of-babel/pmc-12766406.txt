Protocol for isolation of lipid droplets and mitochondria from acute myeloid leukemia cells for lipidomic analysis
Authors: Ran Zhao, Liang Zhang, Shuxin Dong, Kazuharu Kamachi, Saurabh Kumar Gupta, Hiroki Akiyama, Jalen Nguyen, G Lavender Hackman, Michael Andreeff, Alessia Lodi, Jo Ishizawa
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryLipid droplets (LDs) are dynamic organelles critical for lipid and energy homeostasis. LD dysregulation is implicated in various diseases, including acute myeloid leukemia (AML), which exhibits aberrant lipid metabolism and chemoresistance. Here, we describe an effective protocol for the co-isolation of LDs and mitochondria from lipid-deficient cell types, e.g., AML cells. We detail procedures for the isolation, quality verification, and results from mass spectrometry-based lipidomic analysis. This protocol enables the functional study of organellar lipidomic regulation in AML.Subject areas: cell biology, cell isolation, cell separation/fractionation, mass cytometry, cancer

Before you begin

Lipid droplets (LDs) are dynamic organelles that actively regulate lipid and energy homeostasis.1,2 LDs, which are derived from endoplasmic reticulum and are driven by triacylglycerol (TAG) synthesis, consist of hydrophobic neutral lipids, mainly triacylglycerols (TAG) and sterol esters, surrounded by a phospholipid monolayer embedded with peripheral proteins.3 This protocol is for the extraction and isolation of lipid droplets (LDs) from AML cell lines (e.g., OCI-AML3, MOLM13), with optional co-isolation of mitochondria. AML cells are cultured in suspension and collected by centrifugation. A large culture is required to effectively isolate LDs due to the inherently lower LD load in AML cells compared with adipose or liver tissues. A snap-freezing step is employed to promote the release of intracellular contents during cellular homogenization. Following homogenization, low-speed (2,000 × g) centrifugation is first used to remove the nuclear fraction. Then, owing to the low density (<1 g/mL) of neutral lipids in LDs, mostly TAGs, high-speed (16,000 × g) centrifugation is used to separate LDs from the post-nuclear supernatant (PNS). Samples must be maintained at ∼4°C throughout the extraction steps to preserve LD integrity. Our protocol allows the simultaneous isolation of LDs and mitochondria from the same sample and eliminates the need for ultracentrifugation and specialized equipment. While this protocol was developed and used in AML cell models, this workflow can be applied to the study of other cell models and is an accessible tool to investigate the role of LDs in AML and the interplay between LDs and mitochondria (Figure 1).

Innovation

LDs’ lipid composition is fundamental to their roles in lipid flux and homeostasis regulation. Mass spectrometry (MS)-based lipidomics, an essential tool for characterizing lipid species in LDs and these species’ roles in cell physiology,4,5 requires the effective isolation of LDs from cellular components. Despite emerging interest in the role of lipid metabolism in hematologic malignancies, the role of LDs in AML remains underexplored, partly due to the lack of established methods for isolating LDs from AML cells. Most existing protocols for isolating LDs focus on lipid-rich tissues or solid tumor cells, and require specialized instruments or ultracentrifugation.6,7,8,9 Importantly, isolating LDs from lipid-deficient cells, such as AML cells,10 presents a significant challenge. Meanwhile, no established protocol for isolating LDs from AML cells has been reported. Our protocol allows for the simple and efficient isolation of LDs from AML cells for lipidomic analysis, as we have found that such high speeds may not be essential for extracting LDs from human AML cells. Uniquely, our protocol also establishes a branching workflow to simultaneously isolate intact mitochondria from the same sample. As mitochondria are a known primary therapeutic target in AML, and given the close interplay between LDs and mitochondria, this dual approach facilitates investigation of the interlinking functions between LDs and mitochondria in AML disease progression and therapeutic response.

Expected outcomes

Successful LD isolation yielded a final suspension of opaque-white LDs in HIB. A neutral lipid assay, such as Colorimetric Triglyceride Assay Kit (Cayman Chemicals 10010303) can be used to quantify TAGs to verify LD yield. The LDs isolated from 3 × 107 cells OCI-AML3 and MOLM13 cells contain ∼2.2 μg TAGs and ∼4.0 μg TAGs, respectively (Figure 3A).

Immunoblotting revealed a high signal of PLIN2 exclusively in the LD fraction, and a high TOMM20 signal only in the mitochondria fraction but not in PNS, cytosolic, or whole cell samples (Figure 3B), supporting the successful isolation of LDs and crude mitochondria.

Fluorescence imaging of isolated LDs stained with BODIPY 493/503 (green) and anti-PLIN2-CF568 (red) showed co-localization of the signals of LDs and PLIN2 in the ∼1 μm round spherical structures. Proper co-localization of both colors represents an intact LD phospholipid monolayer with contained neutral lipids (Figure 3C).

MS-lipidomic analysis of OCI-AML3 derived samples was performed as a downstream and separate workflow, showing highly enriched (∼90%) TAGs in the LDs, consistent with LD’s main function of neutral lipid storage. We found that TAGs account for 45% and 31% of the total number of identified lipid species in LDs and whole cell lysates, respectively (Figure 4A), while accounting for 90% (LD) and 18% (WCL) of the lipid composition (Figure 4B), confirming the purity of the isolated LDs. In addition to glycerolipids (including both TAGs and diacylglycerols, DAGs), sphingolipids (including sphingomyelin, SM; hexosylceramide HexCer; and ceramide, Cer) and glycerophospholipids (phosphatidylcholine, PC; phosphatidylethanolamine, PE; phosphatidylinositol, PI; phosphatidylserine, PS, lysophosphatidylcholine, LPC) account for 32% and 18% of the total number of lipid species identified in LDs, respectively.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Whole-cell pellets, and mitochondrial and LD fractions were extracted using a modified Bligh-Dyer procedure, as described previously.11,12,13 Each sample was spiked with diluted SPLASH Lipidomix Mass Spec Standard (Avanti Polar Lipids, #330707) and tetramyristoyl cardiolipin (Avanti Polar Lipids, #710332), as lipid internal standards. Untargeted lipidomics was performed using a Vanquish ultra-high-performance liquid chromatography system coupled to an Orbitrap IQ-X Tribrid mass spectrometer (Thermo Scientific). A Kinetex 2.6 μm C18 100 Å LC column (150 × 2.1 mm, #00F-4462-AN, Phenomenex) was used for chromatographic separation. The column temperature was maintained at 45°C. Mobile phase A was composed of 60:40 acetonitrile:water with 10 mM ammonium formate and 0.1% formic acid; mobile phase B consisted of 90:10 isopropanol:acetonitrile with 10mM ammonium formate and 0.1% formic acid. The LC gradient was set to 30% B at 0 minutes, 43% B at 2 minutes, 55% B at 2.1 minutes, 65% B at 12 minutes, 85% B at 18 minutes, 100% B at 20 minutes, and 30% B at 25 minutes and held until 31 minutes. The flow rate was 260 μL/minute with a 5 μL injection volume. Lipidomics data were acquired in both positive and negative modes. Data were acquired using the following source parameters: sheath gas: 40 arb; auxiliary gas: 8 arb; sweep gas: 0.5 arb; spray voltage, 3.5 kV for positive mode and 2.5 kV for negative mode acquisition. For MS/MS analysis, high-energy collisional dissociation (HCD) was performed using a stepped collision energy of 25, 30, and 35eV. Collision-induced dissociation (CID) was triggered upon detection of phospholipid polar fragments within a 10 ppm mass tolerance. A subsequent MS3 CID event was triggered when a fatty acid neutral loss was detected within a 5 ppm mass tolerance. Quality control (QC) samples were generated separately for each group (whole cell, mitochondria, and LDs; 3 separate QC samples) and for all sample types (1 additional total QC sample containing all sample types) by combining equal volumes of all replicates within each group or all samples. Repeated measurements of the QC sample were run every 5 samples with a randomized order to monitor the signal stability of the internal standards. Coefficients of variation were calculated for the QC samples, and signals exceeding 25% coefficient of variation were omitted from the analysis. Lipid species assignments were performed based on accurate mass values, retention times, and MS/MS fragmentation. For lipid quantification, signal intensities were normalized to that of the corresponding internal standard. The lipid classification was based on the LIPID MAPS nomenclature.16 The p-value for cardiolipin percentage in WCL vs mitochondria (Figure 4C) was calculated with a two-tailed Student’s t test.

Limitations

This protocol was optimized for AML suspension cell lines. Isolating LDs from primary AML samples may be challenging due to limited sample size and lower lipid load.

Previous reports state that snap freezing of cells may lead to disruption of larger LDs of adipocytes, and samples should not be frozen to avoid shearing and breaking of LDs in fatty tissues, such as adipocytes that contain supersized LDs.6 While the shearing of LDs caused by snap freezing remains a concern, AML cells are not known to contain supersized LDs and should be less prone to LD damage by freezing. The snap-freezing step enhances LD isolation yield from AML cells, and fluorescence imaging results suggest that the integrity of isolated LDs is mostly maintained.

The mitochondrial fraction will contain MAMs, therefore, ER markers (e.g., calreticulin) are supposed to be detected in immunoblotting of the mitochondrial fraction (Figure 3B). Additional clean-up, such as Percoll gradients centrifugation, may be required for applications needing highly purified mitochondria. Minor cellular membrane contamination may not be fully excluded despite repeated washing of LDs due to density.

Given the dynamic nature of LD isolation and variability of lipid species across cell types, and even the same cell type under different states, it is difficult to directly compare our LD extraction protocol with previous studies.
