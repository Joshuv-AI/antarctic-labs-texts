Storage tank detection in remote sensing images based on circular bounding boxes and large selective kernel
Authors: Yu Liu, Yong Wan, Weimin Huang, Zihao Zhan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAccurate storage tank detection in remote sensing images is vital for monitoring methane emissions, a potent greenhouse gas, from the oil and gas industry. Existing methods, such as traditional geometric and spectral feature-based approaches, suffer from high false detection rates due to background variations and imaging conditions, while deep learning models like YOLO series and EfficientDet struggle with small objects, multi-scale features, background interference, and regression sensitivity, leading to missed detections and false positives. This study introduces a novel method integrating circular bounding boxes and a Large Selective Kernel (LSK) to address these gaps. Circular bounding boxes, aligned with storage tanks’ typical circular shape, stabilize Intersection over Union (IoU) for small objects, while LSK dynamically adjusts the receptive field to leverage contextual information effectively. Implemented on a YOLO-v10 framework and evaluated on a comprehensive dataset comprising DIOR, NWPUU_RESISC45, NWPU VHR-10, TGRS-HRRSD, and a self-built dataset (totaling 3568 images and 46075 storage tanks), our approach achieved a precision of 0.911, recall of 0.902, and mean Average Precision (mAP@0.5) of 0.931. These results represent improvements of 2.0% in precision, 2.7% in recall, and 1.8% in mAP@0.5 over the state-of-the-art YOLO-v10 baseline, offering a robust tool for pinpointing methane emission sources and supporting environmental sustainability efforts in the oil and gas sector.Keywords: Circular bounding box, Methane, Storage tanks, Remote sensing, Target detection.Subject terms: Energy infrastructure, Information technology

Introduction

Methane, a potent greenhouse gas with 25 times the global warming potential of CO₂, significantly contributes to climate change. Human activities account for 60% of atmospheric methane emissions, of which the oil and gas industry is a major contributor1,2. Specifically, oil and gas storage tanks are among the primary sources within this sector, responsible for up to 94.1% of methane emissions in the oil and gas industry3. Therefore, accurately identifying storage tanks is critical for pinpointing emission sources and supporting methane mitigation efforts. However, wide and dispersed geographic distribution of storage tanks, combined with the fact that the coordinates of many tanks are not recorded, means that simple statistical screening is insufficient for accurately locating all storage tanks. With the increasing resolution of remote sensing technology in recent years4, along with its inherent advantages of wide coverage and fast data acquisition, remote sensing has become an essential tool for detecting and identifying oil and gas storage tanks5.

In addressing the challenge of detecting storage tank targets, several studies have employed a combination of conventional image processing methodologies, including morphological processing, Hough transform, clustering, and minimum distance classifiers. These methodologies have been utilized to leverage spatial and spectral characteristics for the purpose of high-resolution remote sensing image detection of storage tank targets. However, storage tanks are typically located in complex backgrounds. Traditional geometric and spectral feature-based methods have limited performance under background and imaging condition variations, resulting in a high false detection rate in storage tank detection6, primarily due to their inability to handle variations in lighting, shadows, and similar circular structures. Therefore, deep learning-based methods have progressively emerged as the prevailing approach, exemplified by the YOLO series (e.g., YOLOv5, YOLOv7) and EfficientDet, which have garnered extensive application. These methods incorporate attention mechanisms (e.g., CBAM, SimAM, multi-head attention), feature enhancement modules (e.g., C3, CARAFE), and small object detection heads to enhance detection capabilities in complex backgrounds of remote sensing images7,8. Deep learning methods perform better in detecting storage tanks in complex backgrounds, but further optimization is needed to improve detection accuracy and efficiency. One of the key challenges is the presence of many small objects in remote sensing images, and small object detection has long been one of the most significant challenges in storage tank detection methods9,10. Small object detection continues to face many difficulties due to the limited size of small objects, fewer visual and geometric cues, and the lack of large-scale datasets for small objects. Some of the challenges include the complexity of multi-scale feature learning11, the impact of background interference and dense targets12, the absence of contextual information13, and the sensitivity of the regression branch14. For storage tanks, remote sensing images typically exhibit multi-scale target characteristics, where both large and small storage tanks may be present simultaneously. Suppose the focus is solely on improving the detection performance for small objects (e.g., by lowering the Intersection over Union (IoU) threshold). Then in such a case, it may decrease the detection model’s overall performance15. Therefore, an efficient multi-scale object detection method is required to accurately identify large and small storage tanks in remote sensing images.

Storage tanks generally appear as circular objects from the overhead perspective of remote sensing imagery. However, many human activity traces on the ground appear circular when viewed from above, which can lead to false identification of these features as storage tanks. This issue arises because conventional object detection methods mainly focus on the pixels within the target boundary while neglecting the surrounding background pixel information, which could be useful. Contextual information plays a crucial role in improving detection accuracy by providing additional cues to distinguish targets from similar backgrounds, reducing false positives. The receptive field must be expanded to effectively incorporate background information, typically increasing the convolutional kernel size. However, this approach can result in losing small object features16,17. Therefore, it is necessary to dynamically adjust the receptive field based on the scene requirements, reducing the false detection rate of the object detection method.

Recent advances in remote sensing object detection include oriented bounding box methods18 and contextual enhancement techniques19, yet challenges like regression sensitivity and background noise persist. To solve the problems of false detections and missed detections in storage tank target detection tasks in remote sensing images, this paper proposes a detection method based on circular bounding boxes combined with a large selective kernel (LSK). Circular bounding boxes better align with the morphological characteristics of storage tanks in remote sensing images, and theoretical analysis shows that circular bounding boxes provide greater IoU stability during slight positional shifts, which helps improve the recall of small objects. A circular bounding box detection framework is introduced to solve traditional rectangular bounding boxes’ regression branch sensitivity issue. Meanwhile, the LSK adaptively selects convolution kernel sizes, dynamically adjusting the model’s receptive field to efficiently utilize background information in remote sensing images, thereby improving detection accuracy. By integrating these two methods, we aim to significantly enhance oil and gas storage tank detection performance, providing robust technical support for identifying potential greenhouse gas emission sources.

The major contributions of this work are: (1) addressing IoU instability for small circular objects, thereby reducing missed detections and improving recall in multi-scale scenarios; (2) enabling adaptive receptive field adjustment to mitigate background interference, enhancing precision in complex environments without sacrificing efficiency. These advancements target the unique gaps in storage tank detection, such as regression sensitivity and improper contextual utilization, distinguishing our work from general object detection methods. However, limitations include the method’s specificity to circular objects, potentially limiting generalization to non-circular targets, and increased computational complexity from LSK, which may demand more resources. Future research could explore shape-adaptive bounding boxes and optimize LSK efficiency.

Related works

This section provides a concise overview of prior research relevant to storage tank detection in remote sensing images, focusing on three key areas: traditional and deep learning methods for storage tank identification, advancements in small object detection, and techniques for dynamically adjusting receptive fields. These areas highlight the evolution of detection strategies, their limitations in addressing small, circular targets like storage tanks, and the motivation for our proposed enhancements, which build upon these foundations to improve accuracy and robustness.

Storage tank target detection from remote sensing images

Storage tank detection from remote sensing images has made significant progress with the advancement of deep learning. Traditional methods are simple and computationally efficient, but they rely on geometric and spectral features. When local target distribution patterns or imaging conditions change, these methods become limited, leading to high false positives in different backgrounds. Hierarchical residual learning branches have shown effectiveness in identifying storage tanks in large-scale, high-resolution images with strong generalization ability20. However, it is computationally intensive. Improved models like EfficientDet7 and Faster R-CNN21 have significantly enhanced detection accuracy, but they still fail to resolve the issue of slow inference speeds caused by computational intensity. By extracting contour shape cues and using sparse reconstruction to generate saliency maps, storage tanks can be accurately located while minimizing interference from high-contrast backgrounds22. However, given the effects of satellite angle and occlusion, this method is not capable of precisely identifying all tanks within complex backgrounds.

These methods have made notable strides in improving detection accuracy, but they do not fundamentally address the challenges of detecting small objects and mitigating the interference from complex background information in remote sensing images23. Therefore, there is still room for further optimization in existing methods to overcome these two major difficulties. The current research contributes by integrating shape-specific bounding boxes and adaptive kernels, outperforming prior works in handling small objects and backgrounds.

Small object detection in remote sensing images

Small object detection in remote sensing images has long been challenging. In recent years, researchers have proposed various methods to improve the accuracy and robustness of small object detection24–26. The introduction of the YOLO-based algorithm significantly enhanced the capability of small object detection and real-time performance, leading to improved algorithms designed specifically for small object and multi-scale target detection. These improvements have been widely applied in various remote sensing target detection scenarios27–30. Subsequent advancements have also emphasized enhancing the precision of models in detecting small-scale targets31, including mini-YOLO variants that optimize for lightweight detection in constrained environments32,33.

The above YOLO-based object detection methods focus on improving small object detection performance through multi-scale feature learning. However, few scholars have attempted to address challenges such as background interference, dense object distribution, missing contextual information, and the sensitivity of the regression branch. To tackle the issue of complex background interference in remote sensing images, CRNet was proposed18, which replaces traditional bounding boxes with circular bounding boxes to reduce background interference in remote sensing imagery. However, the present study primarily emphasizes experimental validation of the method’s effectiveness, while the theoretical principles underlying this approach remain insufficiently explored, which also constitutes one of the key research emphases of this paper.

Dynamic adjustment of receptive field

In remote sensing image object detection, background information is not always irrelevant, the surrounding image context may carry important information to enhance target detection accuracy34. This issue has received significant attention from researchers. The Receptive Field Expansion Block35 was designed to capture contextual features surrounding each object and propagate them to the feature maps at all levels of the feature pyramid. Additionally, improvements to RFB include using Kronecker convolution for feature extraction, which enhances the global semantic and local contextual information on the feature map by expanding the coverage of individual pixels over the original image36. However, extending the receptive field inevitably introduces interference from background information. Multi-scale convolutions aggregate features at different kernel sizes for richer representations but lack dynamic selection; dilated convolutions expand fields without increasing parameters but can cause gridding artifacts. In contrast, the LSK Network (LSKNet) dynamically adjusts the spatial receptive field based on the target’s size, allowing for more efficient utilization of background information in remote sensing images, thereby improving detection accuracy37. Empirical comparisons in our main experimental results (Sect. 5.3) show LSK outperforms fixed multi-scale (YOLO-v10) or dilated approaches (RetinaNet) in precision for our dataset, justifying its selection.

Although the above methods have achieved remarkable results for a specific problem, they have overlooked key challenges in small object detection—multi-scale complexity, background interference, dense targets, context absence, and regression instability.

Problem statement

Missed storage tank detection caused by sensitivity in the regression branch

Fixed IoU thresholds in storage tank detection amplifies regression sensitivity for small objects. While IoU evaluates prediction accuracy during training, its inherent geometric sensitivity causes drastic fluctuations for minor positional shifts (Fig. 1). These instabilities lead to loss function misguidance and subsequent exclusion of small storage tanks during model optimization, resulting in false negatives.

IoU variations for bounding boxes of different sizes with the same offset.

False target detection caused by improper receptive field setting

Fixed receptive fields in object detection models amplify false detections for storage tanks. While expanding the field aids target verification through background context (Fig. 2), excessive enlargement introduces noise. This forces the model to misinterpret ambiguous structures as storage tanks. A dynamic receptive field selection mechanism is thus critical to balance context utilization and noise suppression.

Methodology

This section presents two methods for addressing the two key issues mentioned in Sect. 3. Circular bounding boxes (Sect. 4.1–4.4) solve the problem of missed storage tank detection due to regression branch sensitivity. The LSK module solves the problem of false target detection due to an improper receptive field setting (Sect. 4.5). The proposed method combines these by modifying the YOLO-v10 framework: circular bounding boxes replace rectangular ones in data processing, anchors, and loss functions, while LSK integrates into the backbone for adaptive feature extraction, enabling end-to-end optimization for circular targets in complex scenes. We selected YOLO-v10 as the base due to its state-of-the-art real-time performance and NMS-free design.

Circular bounding box

Although general object detection algorithms are designed to identify various objects in multiple scenarios, such as autonomous driving, security monitoring, and retail, tank detection presents unique challenges and opportunities. It is mainly used to identify cylindrical tanks in aerial or satellite images, which appear as circles when viewed from above. This contrasts with general object detection, which processes objects of arbitrary shapes and sizes; therefore, tank detection datasets consist primarily of circular targets with consistent shapes, varying sizes, and complex backgrounds. To adapt to these characteristics, our method uses circular bounding boxes, which better match the geometric properties of storage tanks than the rectangular bounding boxes commonly used in general object detection. This design better matches the shape of the targets and enhances detection performance by reducing background noise and improving localization accuracy. Storage tanks typically appear circular in overhead remote sensing images. Compared to rectangular bounding boxes, circular bounding boxes (formed by inscribed circles of rectangles) reduce redundant background area (Fig. 3) and mitigate IoU instability for small objects.

Data preprocessing

We first adjusted the data processing section to accommodate the detection requirements of circular bounding boxes. In the original YOLO-v10, the target annotations typically include the class label, the center coordinates of the rectangular box, the width, and the height of the box (x, y, w, h). However, for circular bounding box detection, the annotation information is modified to include the class label, the center coordinates, and the radius (x, y, r). Therefore, we need to calculate the center coordinates and radius of the inscribed circle within the rectangular bounding box, in order to convert the dataset into circular bounding box annotations. Specifically, the x and y coordinates remain unchanged, and the radius r is taken as half of the shorter side of the rectangle.

To ensure the quality of circular bounding box annotations, we validated the conversion process from rectangular to circular annotations. The radius was calculated as half the shorter side of the rectangular box, and center coordinates were retained. To address potential labeling bias, particularly for irregular or partially occluded tanks, we conducted a manual review of the dataset to verify the accuracy of the inscribed circle annotations. For partially occluded tanks, where the circular shape may be incomplete, we used a semi-automated tool to adjust the center and radius based on visible tank boundaries, ensuring alignment with the true circular shape. In cases of severe occlusion (less than 50% of the tank visible), annotations were flagged for exclusion to avoid introducing bias. This validation process minimized errors but may still face challenges with highly irregular tanks, which are less common in our dataset but warrant further investigation in future work.

Still, with these adjustments, all four-dimensional data processing flows involving (x, y, w, h) in the model optimization and detection framework were adapted to the three-dimensional (x, y, r) format. Additionally, modifications were made to the anchor box structure and the loss function to accommodate circular bounding boxes and the new data dimension.

Although YOLO-v10 is a non-maximum suppression (NMS)-free object detection method, it still employs an initial anchor box structure to facilitate model optimization, helping the model better predict the location and size of objects during training. The purpose of using K-Means clustering is to determine representative anchor box radii that cover varying target sizes efficiently, optimizing for multi-scale detection.

Like the clustering of rectangular box widths and heights in the YOLO model, multiple anchor box radii are required to cover detection targets of varying sizes. We apply the K-Means clustering algorithm to the ground-truth radii in the training dataset to determine a representative set of anchor box radii . Specifically, to effectively cover most target sizes, we initially selected 9 anchor boxes as the number of clusters K, following the empirical approach used in the original YOLO model. To further optimize the number of clusters, we applied the elbow method to determine the most appropriate K. By calculating the clustering error for different numbers of clusters, we selected the number of clusters at the inflection point of the loss curve as the final K value. After clustering, the center values of each cluster represent the corresponding anchor box radii. These radii are used as anchor box scales for subsequent target detection model training.

At the same time, to ensure a uniform distribution of anchor boxes on the feature map, each point on the feature map is associated with multiple anchor boxes, each corresponding to a different radius. Large selective kernel

In this paper, we combine the LSK with the circular boundary frame detection framework to further improve the performance of tank target detection in remote sensing images. The circular bounding box has the advantage of reducing the interference of redundant background information. Still, its effect depends on the proper use of background information and the ability to suppress it. By incorporating LSK, the size of the convolution kernel can be selected adaptively, and the utilization efficiency of background information can be further optimized based on a circular boundary box. LSK originated from LSKNet, and its core idea is to combine the multi-scale convolution kernel with the selection mechanism to enhance the flexibility and robustness of the network.

LSK constructs large convolutional kernels by decomposing them into a series of depth-wise convolutions with progressively increasing kernel sizes and dilation rates. Specifically, the receptive field size of the i-th convolution is represented as Eq. (15).

After obtaining features with receptive fields at different scales, the LSK module dynamically selects the most relevant spatial context areas for different targets using a spatial selection mechanism. This mechanism first extracts spatial feature descriptors through average pooling and max pooling, and then uses convolutional layers to transform these descriptors into spatial attention maps. Each spatial attention map is then converted into a spatial selection mask through a sigmoid activation function, which is used to weight the features corresponding to the decomposed large convolution kernel as Eq. (17). This dynamic adjustment allows smaller kernels for small targets to preserve details and larger ones for big targets to capture context.

Through the above mechanism, the LSK module can adaptively adjust the network’s receptive field to better capture the contextual information required by different targets. In complex backgrounds, this dynamic adjustment capability enables the network to reduce background interference, improve detection accuracy for small objects, and, at the same time, effectively leverage more contextual information for large targets, thereby enhancing overall object detection performance.

Experimental environment and setting

The experiment was run in the following hardware environment: Intel Xeon(R) Gold 6226R@2.90 GHz 2.89 GHz CPU, 256GB RAM, NVIDIA Quadro RTX 6000 GPU, Windows 10 Professional Edition, CUDA 11.1 and Pytorch 1.10.1. Training uses a 0.01 initial learning rate, 0.01 multiplier, 3-epoch warm-up, 0.937 momentum, and early stopping after 100 epochs without improvement.

The evaluation indexes in this paper are Precision (P), Recall Rate (R), and Mean Average Precision (mAP). P represents the probability that the model predicts correctly, and R represents the proportion of the sample the model predicts in the real sample. mAP is a more comprehensive evaluation index in the target detection task, which can evaluate the detection ability of the model more comprehensively. We use mAP@0.5 to evaluate the model’s performance, representing the mean accuracy at an IoU threshold 0.5.

Main experimental results

In the model training stage, we adopted the dynamically adjusting the learning rate to accelerate the training convergence and avoid falling into the local optimal solution. Specifically, we initialized the learning rate at 0.01 to facilitate rapid learning at the start. To ensure more fine-tuned convergence, using a learning rate multiplier at 0.01 to gradually reduce the learning rate during training. We also employed a warm-up period of 3 epochs to stabilize the training in the early stages. During this warm-up, the learning rate started from a low value and gradually increased to the initial value of 0.01. Additionally, the momentum was initialized at 0.937 to help accelerate gradient descent and avoid oscillations in the optimization process. To prevent the model from overfitting in the later stage, we adopted the early stop method during the training stage. When there was no performance improvement of the model within 100 consecutive epochs, the training was stopped, and the model with the best performance was taken as the final result. Finally, the model reached the early stop condition after 153 training epochs. The changes in its indicators are shown in Fig. 9. In the early stages, the validation loss fluctuates significantly compared to the training loss, but it gradually stabilizes as the number of epochs increases. During the initial training stages, factors such as overfitting, differences in data distribution, learning rate settings, and gradient noise cause the model to perform inconsistently on the validation set. As the number of epochs increases, however, the model learns more generalizable features, the optimization process converges, and the model adapts to the distribution of the validation set. This results in a gradual decrease in the fluctuation of the validation loss and eventual stabilization.

We speculate that the possible reason is that most storage tanks in remote sensing images are light-colored, such as white, while there are fewer dark-colored tank samples in the training set. As a result, the model may lack sufficient training data for dark circular objects, leading to misidentifications.

Discussion

The experimental results demonstrate that the combination of circular bounding boxes and LSK achieves the optimal performance of the proposed method in the storage tank detection task (accuracy: 0.911; recall rate: 0.902; mAP@0.5: 0.931). Compared to baselines like YOLO-v10 (mAP@0.5: 0.915), our method improves by 1.8%, primarily due to better handling of small objects (via circular boxes) and backgrounds (via LSK), unlike Faster R-CNN (0.846) which suffers from slower speed and regression issues, or SSD (0.894) limited by fixed anchors. The distinction arises from our shape-specific design and adaptive kernels, tailored to circular tanks, whereas others are general-purpose and less efficient for this domain. The physical and engineering significance of these improvements can be further understood from the following aspects:

(1) The function of circular bounding boxes is as follows: The incorporation of circular bounding boxes into the YOLO-v10 model led to an enhancement in the recall rate from 0.878 to 0.890, signifying a substantial improvement in the detection capability of diminutive storage tanks. From a physical perspective, this enhancement can be attributed to the high degree of congruence between circular bounding boxes and the geometry of storage tanks, which minimizes the impact of superfluous background pixels in conventional rectangular bounding boxes. Theoretical analysis (Sect. 4.1) demonstrates that circular bounding boxes demonstrate reduced variability in IoU calculations for small objects. From an engineering perspective, this translates to diminished sensitivity to positional deviations, consequently leading to a reduction in false negatives. This characteristic is of particular importance for small storage tanks in remote sensing images, as these targets typically occupy only a small number of pixels and are easily missed by traditional methods due to regression instability. Regarding generalizability, while circular boxes reduce background coverage, the model still learns from diverse training data including partial backgrounds around circles, allowing robustness to noise as validated in simulations (Fig. 6).

(2) The role of LSK is twofold: first, its incorporation into YOLO-v10 enhances the accuracy from 0.893 to 0.901, thereby demonstrating its efficacy in reducing false detections. From an engineering perspective, LSK adapts to the multi-scale characteristics of storage tanks by dynamically adjusting the receptive field. The capacity of small storage tanks to preserve details is contingent upon a smaller receptive field, while the capacity of large storage tanks or targets in complex backgrounds to obtain contextual information is enhanced by a larger receptive field. This adaptability corresponds physically to the model’s selective utilization of background information, enabling it to better distinguish storage tanks from similar circular objects (e.g., the stadium in Fig. 8), thereby enhancing detection accuracy.

(3) The following section will explore the synergistic effects of the two methods. The employment of circular bounding boxes in conjunction with LSK has been demonstrated to enhance the model’s performance, as evidenced by the enhancement in accuracy and recall rates to 0.911 and 0.902, respectively. This synergistic effect indicates that the two methods complement each other in addressing the challenges of storage tank detection (e.g., small object misses and false positives in complex backgrounds). The utilization of circular bounding boxes provides prior knowledge regarding the shape, while LSK enhances the model’s adaptability to targets and backgrounds of various scales. This combination offers an efficient engineering solution for methane emission monitoring, achieving a balance between high coverage (high recall) and high reliability (high accuracy).

(4) To better understand the limitations of our proposed method, we analyzed common failure cases observed in the test results, as shown in Fig. 10. A notable misdetection example is the misclassification of a nearly circular stadium as a storage tank, primarily due to its similar shape and high contrast in remote sensing imagery. This error likely stems from the underrepresentation of dark-colored circular objects in the training dataset, as most storage tanks are light-colored (e.g., white or metallic). Other failure cases include missed detections of partially occluded tanks, where shadows or overlapping structures obscure key features, and false positives in densely clustered scenes, where small circular objects are mistaken for tanks. To mitigate these issues, future improvements could involve augmenting the dataset with more diverse samples, including dark-colored circular objects and occluded tanks, to enhance model robustness. Additionally, incorporating color and texture features into the LSK module could help distinguish tanks from similar structures, reducing false positives. These strategies aim to address the specific challenges of complex backgrounds and morphological similarities in remote sensing imagery.

The significance of these improvements extends beyond mere metric improvements. The proposed methodology directly addresses two fundamental challenges in the field of storage tank detection in remote sensing images. Firstly, it considers the regression sensitivity of small objects, and secondly, it explores the interference from complex backgrounds. By enhancing detection performance, this method provides the oil and gas industry with a more precise tool for locating methane emission sources, thereby supporting the implementation of environmental monitoring and climate change response strategies. Potential limitations include limited generalization to non-circular objects, as the circular design is tailored to tanks; future directions could involve hybrid bounding boxes for diverse shapes and further LSK optimizations for real-time applications.

Conclusion

This paper presents a method for storage tank detection from remote sensing images based on circular bounding boxes combined with LSK, aiming to tackle the problems of missed mall objects and false detections in complex backgrounds commonly encountered in traditional detection methods. Circular bounding boxes are introduced to reduce missed detections of small objects, which mitigate the issue of missed small objects due to regression branch sensitivity as shown in complete theoretical proof provided in this study. LSK is employed to dynamically adjust the receptive field to address false positives in complex backgrounds, enabling selective use of background context information from remote sensing images, thereby enhancing detection accuracy. Experimental results show that, in the specific application scenario of storage tank detection in remote sensing images, the proposed method significantly improves precision, recall, and mAP@0.5 compared to both classical and latest object detection methods, achieving scores of 0.911, 0.902, and 0.931, respectively. This method addresses two key challenges in storage tank target detection: sensitivity to small objects and complex background interference. The technology under discussion offers an efficient solution for storage tank localization in the petroleum and natural gas industry, facilitating the monitoring and tracking of greenhouse gas emissions. This, in turn, supports efforts to mitigate climate change. In the future, this framework is expected to be extended to detect more methane emission facilities, and plans are underway to expand the dataset to enhance the model’s ability to distinguish between storage tanks and similar background objects.
