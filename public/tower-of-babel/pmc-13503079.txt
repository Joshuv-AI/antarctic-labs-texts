Exploration of the mechanism of key odour substances in porcine large intestine by enzyme-alkali-alcohol ternary composite treatment: Efficacy of deodorisation treatments and impact on quality
Authors: Jiaxin Chen, Xiaoyun Gu, Wenwen Hou, Shiyu Liu, Jie Zhao, Jinxing Liu, Hongbin Lin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
In this study, the key off-odour compounds in porcine large intestine were identified using headspace solid-phase microextraction coupled with gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (HS-SPME-GC–MS), through which thirty volatile compounds were detected, and a combined deodorisation process was subsequently developed. Odour activity value (OAV) analysis revealed eight key off-odour substances (OAV ≥ 1), whereas gas chromatography-olfactometry-mass spectrometry (GC-O-MS) further confirmed that 4-methylphenol, indole and 3-methylindole were the predominant contributors to the characteristic faecal and animal odours. In single-factor experiments, the deodorisation effects of ethanol, sodium hydroxide and papain were evaluated, and an optimal combined process was established, consisting of 1.2% papain (45 min), 1.2 g/L NaOH (30 min) and 35% ethanol (45 min). This combined treatment synergistically reduced the total content of the key off-odour compounds by 82.60%, markedly outperforming each individual method. Moreover, it improved the quality of the final product, as evidenced by reduced hardness and chewiness, and a 28.04% decrease in cooking loss. Collectively, these findings provide a scientific basis for quality enhancement by elucidating the key odour sources and the underlying deodorisation mechanisms.

Introduction

Livestock, including pigs, cattle, sheep, chickens, ducks and geese, constitute major sources of dietary protein worldwide, and demand for animal-derived protein continues to increase together with improving living standards. However, the slaughter and processing of these animals generate substantial quantities of underutilised by-products such as blood, offal, bones, skin and feathers. Among these, by-products from pigs are particularly significant, accounting for ∼30% of live weight and underscoring their considerable potential for further development (Al-Zohairi et al., 2023).

The porcine large intestine, a common pork by-product, serves as a key ingredient in traditional dishes such as Jiangyou Feichang and Jiuzhuan Dachang. With rising market demand for such product, processing scale has expanded accordingly. However, the widespread utilisation remains constrained because of a strong off-odour that develops during processing and culinary application. This undesirable odour complicates processing and directly impairs flavour acceptance, thereby limiting the product's potential for further development and market expansion within the meat industry (Lebret & Čandek-Potokar, 2022). Correspondingly, Bearth et al. (2021) observed that, although most consumers cognitively acknowledge animal offal as a food ingredient, their actual acceptance at the table remains limited due to its characteristic off-odours. Studies have further shown that this issue hinders the efficient utilisation and industrial valorisation of livestock and poultry by-products worldwide (de Araujo, Araujo, Patarata and Fraqueza, 2022). Consequently, a systematic characterisation of the odour profile of meat by-products such as porcine large intestine, together with the development of highly effective deodorisation methods, is essential.

Currently, the commonly used methods for removing off-odours are mainly divided into three categories: physical, chemical and biological enzymatic methods. Physical methods primarily rely on the inherent physical properties of odour compounds and their interactions with the matrix to achieve separation. Taking organic solvent extraction as an example, solvents such as ethanol utilise their hydrophobic properties to dissolve and remove lipid-soluble odour compounds, in accordance with the ‘like dissolves like’ principle. Liu et al. (2024) demonstrated the efficacy of ethanol in removing off-odour substances from tilapia. Conversely, chemical methods primarily rely on chemical reactions to modify the structure or state of odour molecules. Alkaline treatment, for example, utilises the saponification and hydrolysis effects of alkalis such as sodium hydroxide (NaOH) to break down fatty acids and other precursors of undesirable odours. This method has shown remarkable efficacy in the deodorisation efficiency of surimi, markedly reducing the content of volatile off-odour compounds such as hexanal and 1-octen-3-ol (Phetsang et al., 2021). However, biological enzymatic methods employ the specific catalytic action of enzymes to transform odour components. This process modifies the odour profile by increasing the relative content of aromatic compounds while decreasing that of aldehydes and ketones (Dai et al., 2024). For example, studies have shown that papain specifically cleaves peptide bonds at the C-terminus of arginine and lysine residues. This action effectively reduces the formation of bitter peptides derived from hydrophobic amino acid sequences, thereby improving the overall flavour of the product (He, Wang, Wang, Wang and Liao, 2025).

Although substantial progress has been made in the deodorisation research for livestock and poultry, most studies have focused on aquatic species and conventional muscle-based foods, leaving animal by-products such as porcine large intestine comparatively underexplored. Among the limited studies available, research has predominantly evaluated single deodorisation methods or focused exclusively on sensory evaluation, without systematically comparing the efficacy of different mechanistic approaches or examining their potential synergistic interactions. Furthermore, the principal off-odour compounds responsible for the characteristic faecal and animal notes of porcine large intestine remain only partially identified, and the molecular mechanisms through which physical, chemical and enzymatic treatments mitigate these compounds have yet to be fully elucidated.

First, a comprehensive analytical strategy integrating headspace solid-phase microextraction coupled with gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (HS-SPME-GC–MS), gas chromatography-olfactometry-mass spectrometry (GC-O-MS), odour activity value (OAV) calculation and odour recombination and omission tests was employed to systematically identify and validate the key odour compounds in porcine large intestine. Second, a ternary synergistic deodorisation process combining sequential enzymatic, alkaline and ethanol treatments was developed and optimised. In this process, papain was first applied for preliminary hydrolysis, facilitating the release of odour substances bound within the protein matrix. Subsequently, NaOH was used to neutralise key odour compounds, namely 4-methylphenol, indole and 3-methylindole, by converting them into their corresponding sodium salts. Finally, the samples were washed in an aqueous ethanol solution. Ethanol facilitated the extraction of residual odour substances according to the ‘like dissolves like’ principle, while the aqueous phase dissolved and removed the hydrophilic sodium salts generated during the treatment. Furthermore, the impact of different deodorisation methods on the eating quality of porcine large intestines was systematically assessed. By elucidating the mechanisms underlying each treatment step and their synergistic interactions, this study not only offers a practical and scalable deodorisation strategy for processing porcine large intestine but also establishes a theoretical and technical foundation for valorising other livestock by-products that face comparable odour challenges.

Materials and methods

Materials

Fresh porcine large intestines were purchased from Hongguang Food Market in Chengdu, Sichuan Province and transported to the laboratory on ice within 1 h. The following reagents were obtained from suppliers: n-alkanes (C7-C40) from Shanghai Xuejing Electronic Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China); 2-methyl-3-heptanone (95%) from Sigma-Aldrich (MO, USA); papain (100,000 U/g) from Henan Wanbang Chemical Technology Co., Ltd. (Henan, China); and food-grade sodium hydroxide from Henan Hengyu Biological Technology Co., Ltd. (Henan, China). All other chemicals used were of analytical grade.

Sample preparation

Sample preparation consisted of two parts: pre-treatment and the preparation of an odourless matrix. First, the submucosal adipose tissue was excised from fresh porcine large intestine. The samples were then rinsed with clean water, blotted dry, portioned into bags, and stored at −20 °C until use. Concurrently, food-grade caprylic/capric triglyceride was selected as the odourless matrix for subsequent analyses.

Analysis and identification of volatile compounds in porcine large intestine

Analysis of volatile compounds

The sample was pretreated according to the method of (Zhang, Zang et al., 2023) with appropriate modifications. A 3 g portion of the large intestine sample was placed into a 20 mL headspace vial, to which 10 μL of 0.0816 mg/mL 2-methyl-3-heptanone (internal standard) solution was added. HS-SPME was performed using a 75 μm CAR/PDMS fibre (Supelco, Inc., Bellefonte, USA) under the following conditions: equilibration in a 60 °C water bath for 20 min, adsorption for 30 min, and desorption in the GC injection port for 3 min.

Subsequent analysis was conducted using GC–MS (GC–MS-QP2020NX, Shimadzu Corporation, Kyoto, Japan). A DB-WAX polar column (30 m × 0.25 mm × 0.25 μm) was employed. High-purity helium served as the carrier gas at a constant flow rate of 1.0 mL/min in splitless injection mode. The temperature programme was held at 40 °C for 2 min, ramped to 150 °C at a rate of 5 °C/min, then further increased to 240 °C at 6 °C/min and held for 3 min. Detection was performed with an EI ion source at an ion source temperature of 230 °C, electron energy of 70 eV, interface temperature of 250 °C, and a mass scan range of m/z 40–400.

GC-O-MS analysis

Based on the method of Ning et al. (2025) with appropriate modifications, analysis was conducted using a GC–MS system equipped with an olfactory detection port (ODP 3, Gerstel, Mülheim an der Ruhr, Germany). Sample pretreatment and GC–MS analytical conditions were as detailed in Section 2.3.1. To prevent the condensation of volatile components, the temperatures of the olfactory detection port and the transfer line were maintained at 80 °C and 250 °C, respectively. Volatile flavour compounds were assessed by a panel of five trained assessors (20–25 years of age) using the time-intensity method, with each assessor working independently. The retention time, odour intensity, and a specific odour descriptor for each perceived compound were recorded. Odour intensity was rated on a five-point scale: 0 (not detected), 1 (weak but recognisable), 2 (moderate and easily recognisable), 3 (strong), 4 (very strong), and 5 (extremely strong). The identification of a compound required confirmation by at least three assessors, based on matching retention times and descriptors. To minimise sensory fatigue, a mandatory rest interval of 30–60 s was implemented between samples. Compounds with a flavour intensity ≥ 2 were designated as potentially key contributors. The study was reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of Xihua University, and informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to the commencement of the sessions.

Volatile compound analysis and OAVs calculation

To identify volatile odour compounds, the mass spectra obtained from GC–MS and GC-O-MS analyses were matched against the NIST 20 database (National Institute of Standards and Technology, Gaithersburg, MD, USA). A similarity index (SI) of ≥80 was used for the preliminary screening of components. The retention index (RI) for each target compound was then calculated using the retention times of a homologous series of C7-C40 n-alkanes obtained under identical chromatographic conditions. Semi-quantitative determination was performed using the internal standard method, following the procedure of Li et al. (2024), with 2-methyl-3-heptanone selected as the reference material to establish the quantitative baseline. The concentration of each target compound was then calculated based on the principle that the response factor is proportional to concentration in gas chromatography, using the ratio of its characteristic peak area to that of the internal standard. Flavour recombination verification

Based on the method of Yao et al. (2025) with minor modifications, a 3 g aliquot of the odourless matrix was accurately weighed into a 20 mL headspace vial. Key off-odour compounds were then spiked into this matrix at their determined concentrations to construct the recombinant sample. Following a 10 min equilibration period at 25 °C for both the recombinant sample and the original large-intestine sample (which served as a blank control), a descriptive sensory analysis was performed to systematically compare their odour profiles. For each sample, the three evaluations provided by each panellist were averaged. These mean values were then used to construct the sensory profile diagram.

The experiment was conducted by ten professional evaluators familiar with the flavour profile of porcine large intestine, with all sensory tests performed in a dedicated sensory analysis laboratory. Prior to formal analysis, the volatile odour characteristics of porcine large intestine were defined through sensory attribute identification. After multiple rounds of screening and validation, four representative descriptors, ‘grassy’, ‘mushroom’, ‘fatty’, and ‘putrid’, were selected as evaluation indicators, alongside an assessment of overall flavour intensity. Each sensory descriptor was referenced to a corresponding standard compound: n-hexanol for grassy, 1-octen-3-ol for mushroom, nonanal for fatty, and 3-methylindole for putrid. Sensory intensity was scored using a nine-point scale, where 1 represented extremely weak and 9 represented extremely strong. This study was reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of Xihua University, and informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to the study.

Determination of key off-odour compounds by omission analysis

To evaluate the actual impact of individual compounds on the overall odour of porcine large intestine, an omission model system was constructed. Following the ISO 4120:2004 (E) standard (Sensory analysis – Methodology - Triangle test), each assessor was presented with three coded samples and asked to identify the odd sample (ISO 4120, 2004). The complete recombinant sample was then compared with the corresponding omission sample; a significant difference was interpreted as indicating that the omitted compound was a key off-odour substance.

Key off-odour compounds removal

Papain, sodium hydroxide and ethanol were employed for off-odour removal from porcine large intestine, using the residual content of volatile off-odour compounds as the evaluation index. Single-factor experiments were conducted under the following conditions: (i) papain treatment, examining the effects of different enzyme concentrations (0%, 0.3%, 0.6%, 0.9%, 1.2% and 1.5%) and treatment durations (15, 30, 45, 60 and 75 min); (ii) NaOH treatment, assessing the effects of different mass concentrations (0, 0.4, 0.8, 1.2, 1.6 and 2.0 g/L) and treatment durations (0, 10, 20, 30, 40 and 50 min); and (iii) ethanol treatment, evaluating the effects of different concentrations (0%, 5%, 15%, 25%, 35% and 45%) and treatment durations (5, 15, 25, 35 and 45 min). Based on the single-factor results, the optimal sequence of the three treatments was determined by comparing all possible permutations. The enzyme-alkali-alcohol sequence, which consisted of enzymatic treatment followed by a water rinse, alkali treatment, a second water rinse, and finally ethanol soaking, was ultimately selected.

Determination of key off-odour compounds

The key volatile off-odour compounds were analysed according to Section 2.3.1.

Electronic nose analysis

A 5 g sample from each treatment group (optimal conditions of each individual method and the combined method) was accurately weighed into a headspace vial. After equilibration in a water bath, the sample was analysed using an electronic nose (Airsense Analytics GmbH, Schwerin, Germany) equipped with an array of sensors of varying specificities. Each sensor type responds to a distinct profile of volatile compounds (detailed in Table S1). Each treatment group was analysed in six replicate runs. The instrument was operated with the following parameters: detection time, 120 s; purge time, 130 s; preparation time, 5 s; internal and inlet flow rates, both 300 mL/min (Liu et al., 2025). The sensor response data were automatically recorded by the built-in software based on the steady-state signals. From the exported data, three time points (60–62 s) with relatively stable response values were selected and averaged for subsequent principal component analysis (PCA) and radar plot construction.

Sensory analysis

Sensory evaluation was conducted with minor modifications to the method of Zhang et al. (2020). Tests were performed in a standard sensory laboratory at a controlled temperature of 25 ± 2 °C. Samples were sealed in odour-free brown glass bottles, equilibrated for 10 min, and presented to a panel of ten trained assessors (five male, five female, aged 20–25 years) in individual testing booths. All panelists were familiar with the typical odour profile of porcine large intestine and had demonstrated ability proficiency in sensory discrimination. The detailed scoring criteria are provided in Table S2.

Texture analysis

The textural properties (hardness and chewiness) of the samples were determined following the method of Cheng et al. (2022) with slight modifications. Samples were cut into 1 cm × 1 cm × 3 cm cubes and analysed using a TA-XT plus texture analyser (Lotun Science Co., Ltd., Xiamen, China). The instrument settings were as follows: pre-test speed 2 mm/s, test speed 1 mm/s, post-test speed 1 mm/s, trigger force 5 g, compression distance 10 mm, and interval time 5 s.

Cooking loss determination

Following the method of Wang et al. (2023) with minor modifications, the weight of the untreated raw sample was recorded as m1. After different treatments, the sample was vacuum-sealed, heated in a 90 °C water bath for 10 min, cooled to room temperature, surface moisture was wiped off, and the weight was recorded as m2. The cooking loss rate was calculated using the following formula:(3)Cooking loss%=m1−m2/m1×100%

Statistical analysis

All analyses were conducted in triplicate, with results presented as mean ± standard deviation. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 26 (IBM SPSS Statistics, Chicago, USA). One-way ANOVA was employed to compare mean differences, with Tukey's honestly significant difference (HSD) test applied for post hoc analysis at a significance level of P < 0.05. Hierarchical clustering and correlation analyses between the contents of characteristic volatile off-odour content and the quality indices of different samples were performed using the open-source platform ChiPlot (https://www.chiplot.online).

Hydrocarbons were the most abundant and diverse class of compounds identified in this study, which may primarily derive from the oxidation of fatty acid alkoxy radicals. Although these compounds typically have relatively high odour thresholds, they can act as flavour precursors and, under specific conditions, may be converted into more potent low-threshold volatiles such as aldehydes and ketones. These secondary products are considered potential contributors to off-odours in meat products and can influence the overall flavour profile (Tatiyaborworntham et al., 2022). The detected alcohols varied considerably in their odour contributions. For instance, 1-Hexanol and octanol, impart grassy and irritating notes, respectively, which collectively contribute to the unpleasant odour character of the samples (Li et al., 2025). In contrast, 1-octen-3-ol may play a more critical role. It is generated through the oxidation of arachidonic acid (C20:4n-6) and other polyunsaturated fatty acids, such as linoleic acid (C18:2n-6) and α-linolenic acid (C18:3n-3). Furthermore, its low odour threshold could enable it to exert a more pronounced influence on the overall characteristic flavour of porcine large intestine. Geng et al. (2022) has confirmed it to be the primary source of fishy off-odour.

Aldehydes were another key fraction of the volatile profile of porcine large intestine, dominated by nonanal, (Z)-9-hexadecenal, pentadecanal and (Z)-13-octadecenal. These aldehydes are generated primarily from the peroxidation of unsaturated fatty acids. Nonanal, a representative oxidation product, is a critical flavour component in meat products, imparting a distinct fatty note. Owing to their low odour thresholds, aldehydes can stimulate olfactory receptors even at trace concentrations and thus play a decisive role in determining the overall odour profile of porcine large intestine (Xu, Jiang, Du et al., 2022).

Phenols, heterocycles, ketones and esters also contribute to the distinctive odour of porcine large intestine. Phenols are mainly formed by microbial metabolism or amino acid degradation. They may impart porcine large intestine a characteristic animal odour that significantly influences the overall aroma profile. Among heterocycles, indole and 3-methylindole (skatole) are well-known malodourants frequently detected in meat products. These compounds are generated through microbial metabolism of tryptophan in the intestine and readily accumulate in animal fat (Ma et al., 2021). Ketones are produced via oxidation of unsaturated fatty acids and also represent common volatile odourants in food systems (Zhou et al., 2022). Esters were also detected in the samples. These compounds can originate from lipid degradation and subsequent esterification with alcohols. However, owing to their typically high odour thresholds, esters may contribute relatively little to the overall flavour profile.

Identification of off-odour compounds based on GC-O-MS and OAVs

The contribution of volatile flavour compounds to the overall odour is generally determined by their concentration and odour threshold. When the OAV is ≥ 1, the compound is considered to have a direct impact on the overall flavour and can be defined as a key odourant (Wang, Ma, Duan et al., 2025). Thus, the OAV can quantify each compound's contribution to the overall odour profile (Duan et al., 2023). As shown in Table 2, eight volatile components with OAVs ≥ 1 were identified, which collectively define the overall odour profile of porcine large intestine. Among these, 3-methylindole (OAV = 114.52; faecal odour), β-myrcene (OAV = 70.78; balsamic odour), and 4-methylphenol (OAV = 36.98; animalic note) made the most significant contributions and were the predominant contributors to the off-odour. In comparison, nonanal (grassy/fatty), 1-octen-3-ol (mushroom/earthy), indole (foul odour), d-limonene (citrus), and 1-hexanol (grassy) exhibited relatively lower OAV values (ranging from 2.76 to 8.83), serving as important modifiers that enriched the complexity of the overall odour profile. The GC-O-MS results further corroborated these findings. As shown in Fig. S2, the experimental mass spectrum of each compound matched well with its corresponding NIST library reference spectrum, with characteristic fragment ions clearly labelled (e.g., m/z 56 for 1-Hexanol, m/z 57 for 1-Octen-3-ol, m/z 107 for 4-Methylphenol, m/z 41 for Nonanal, m/z 41 for β-Myrcene, m/z 68 for d-Limonene, m/z 117 for Indole, m/z 130 for 3-Methylindole). As detailed in Table 2, six volatile compounds were clearly perceived by olfactory assessment. Among these, 4-methylphenol, indole, and 3-methylindole exhibited high odour intensities. The reasons for this phenomenon may be as follows. On the one hand, as a digestive organ, porcine large intestine harbours intestinal microbial communities capable of converting tyrosine and L-tryptophan from food residues into 4-methylphenol via biotransformation pathways such as decarboxylation (Squires et al., 2020). Meanwhile, L-tryptophan can also be decarboxylated by these intestinal microbes to form 3-methylindole, which accumulates in the intestinal tissue and imparts a distinctive faecal odour. Additionally, lipid components can be oxidised by enzymes such as lipoxygenase to generate short-chain aldehydes such as 1-octen-3-ol and nonanal (Zeng et al., 2020). Zhang et al. (2023) identified 4-methylphenol and 3-methylindole as the primary off-odour contributors in porcine large intestine. Similarly, Del Bianco et al. (2020) also reported these two compounds as key contributors to the characteristic odour of mutton.

Verification of key off-odour compounds by recombination analysis

The odour profile of a sample is not a simple superposition of all volatile compounds, but rather arises from interactions among the compounds themselves and with the food matrix. Based on the OAV and GC-O-MS results, recombination model experiments were conducted to further verify the contribution of the eight key volatile compounds to the overall odour of porcine large intestine. The 3-methylindole, 4-methylphenol, indole, 1-hexanol, nonanal and 1-octen-3-ol were added to an odourless matrix at their measured concentrations to construct the recombination model.

As shown in Fig. 1, the odour radar plot of the recombinant model closely matched the overall profile of the original porcine large intestine sample. In particular, the intensities of the ‘fatty’ and ‘mushroom’ attributes were nearly identical to those of the raw sample, indicating that the level of contribution of nonanal and 1-octen-3-ol were largely restored. The recombinant model successfully reproduced the dominant off-odour character, and its overall acceptability did not differ significantly from that of the authentic sample, confirming that 3-methylindole and 4-methylphenol play a dominant role in the sample's odour profile. This discrepancy may be attributed to interactions among odour compounds. First, compounds with high odour intensity in the original sample (3-methylindole and indole) may have exerted a sensory masking effect on substances with lower odour intensity such as 1-hexanol (Wang et al., 2024). This is difficult to replicate in an artificial recombinant model. Second, trace compounds that may synergistically enhance the grassy note could have been present in the original system but were not incorporated into the recombinant model, resulting in a weaker grassy note. Despite these minor deviations, the recombinant model largely matched the authentic sample in both dominant odour characteristics and overall acceptability, confirming that the six key compounds identified collectively constitute the primary odour profile of porcine large intestine.

Determination of key off-odour compounds by omission analysis

The result indicated that these three compounds were indispensable constituents responsible for the characteristic off-odour of porcine large intestine. Determination of key off-odour content

The effects of papain, sodium hydroxide and ethanol on key off-odour compounds in the porcine large intestine were systematically investigated under different treatment conditions. As shown in Fig. 2, the concentrations of the three key off-odour compounds (4-methylphenol, indole, and 3-methylindole) decreased significantly under all treatments (P < 0.05). During ethanol treatment specifically, the content of these compounds decreased consistently across the conditions tested. Fig. 2a demonstrates that ethanol concentration significantly affected the total content of off-odour compounds. Increasing the concentration to 35% reduced the total content from 620.94 μg/kg to 237.25 μg/kg (P < 0.05), with no further significant reduction beyond this point. As shown in Fig. 2b, processing time also influenced off-odour removal. The content of off-odour compounds continued to decline with increased processing time, reaching a minimum of 179.40 μg/kg at 45 min.Fig. 2Influence of treatment concentration and time on the key off-odour compounds. Ethanol (a, b), sodium hydroxide (c, d), and papain (e, f), comparison among the three individual treatments at their optimal conditions and the combined treatment (g).Fig. 2

Influence of treatment concentration and time on the key off-odour compounds. Ethanol (a, b), sodium hydroxide (c, d), and papain (e, f), comparison among the three individual treatments at their optimal conditions and the combined treatment (g).

This may be attributed to the enhanced polar solvating capacity of ethanol at concentrations up to 35%, which disrupts hydrogen bonds and hydrophobic interactions between the off-odour compounds and the intestinal tissue (Zhou et al., 2019), thereby facilitating the transfer of compounds such as 4-methylphenol and indole from the tissue into the ethanol phase. Beyond this threshold, the extractable pool of target off-odour compounds becomes progressively depleted, resulting in a plateau in total content. At a fixed ethanol concentration, the removal rate of off-odour compounds from the porcine large intestine follows a rapid-to-slow trend over time. Initially, the high concentration gradient could drive the rapid dissolution and diffusion of off-odour compounds into the ethanol. As processing continues, the concentration of off-odour compounds within the tissue decreased while increased in the ethanol phase, thereby reducing the removal rate until it stabilised after approximately 45 min.

During sodium hydroxide treatment, the total content of the three off-odour compounds decreased gradually with increasing NaOH concentration (Fig. 2c). When the concentration was raised to 1.2 g/L, the total content fell to 261.60 μg/kg. Notably, a further increase in NaOH concentration to 1.6 g/L resulted in no significant change (P > 0.05). When treatment duration was varied (Fig. 2d), the off-odour content continued to decline over time, reaching a minimum of 229.92 μg/kg after 30 min. Two mechanisms may underlie this phenomenon. First, NaOH could induce conformational changes in intestinal proteins, causing tertiary structure unfolding and thereby weakening protein-off-odour compound interactions (Liu et al., 2024; You et al., 2025). This may promote the dissociation and release of off-odour compounds. Second, as a strong base, NaOH can neutralise weakly acidic compounds such as phenols and indoles, reducing the content of volatile off-odour compounds.

Zhang, Xiong, et al. (2023) likewise reported that alkali treatment significantly reduces the levels of undesirable volatile compounds. At a fixed NaOH concentration, prolonged treatment allowed sufficient time for alkali penetration and completion of the corresponding chemical reactions, ultimately achieving maximum off-odour removal efficiency. However, when treatment exceeded 30 min, the off-odour compounds content increased again, probably because the combined effect of strong alkali and extended treatment causing excessive protein degradation, which generated additional volatile off-odour compounds (Pan et al., 2026).

During papain treatment, the off-odour compounds content initially decreased, then increased, and finally decreased again with increasing enzyme dosage (Fig. 2e). When the treatment time was varied, as observed with the previous two treatments, the total off-odour compounds content gradually decreased during the initial stage (0–45 min) (Fig. 2f), and further extension of the treatment time led to stabilisation of off-odour compounds levels. At low concentrations, papain effectively hydrolyses the protein matrix, releasing entrapped off-odour compounds (Zhao et al., 2020). With increasing concentration, a transient aggregation of odour molecules may occur, causing a temporary rise in the measured content. However, at high enzyme levels, the potent hydrolysis not only degrades proteins but also generates free amino acids. These amino acids may modulate the aroma profile via dissociation of off-odour compound aggregates, ultimately reducing their overall content (Xu, Jiang, Liu, et al., 2022). At a fixed enzyme dosage, extended reaction time progressively hydrolyses the off-odour compounds-encapsulating protein matrix, continuously releasing them from the tissue until the system stabilises.

Based on the three single-factor experiments, the optimal combined process was determined as follows: treatment with 1.2% papain for 45 min, 1.2 g/L NaOH for 30 min, and 35% ethanol for 45 min. As shown in Fig. 2g, each individual treatment significantly reduced the total off-odour compounds content (P < 0.05), with reductions of 65.08% (ethanol), 53.70% (NaOH), and 50.71% (papain). The combined treatment achieved an 82.60% reduction, demonstrating a marked synergistic effect over any single treatment.

Electronic nose analysis

The electronic nose simulates the human olfactory system by using sensors to rapidly characterise the overall volatile odour profile of a sample. This approach enables rapid identification of both simple and complex odours while overcoming limitations inherent in human assessors, such as strong subjectivity and susceptibility to fatigue (Fu et al., 2025). The electronic nose radar plots of samples treated under the optimal conditions of each individual method (1.2% papain for 45 min, 1.2 g/L NaOH for 30 min, and 35% ethanol for 45 min), together with the sample treated with the combined method, were presented in the Fig. 3a. The odour profile of the composite treatment group was the most expansive and complex, indicating that its volatile composition was the most abundant. Concurrently, in the corresponding score plot (Fig. 3b), the NaOH and ethanol treatment points clustered tightly along PC1, indicating that their overall odour profiles were similar and the deodorising effects were limited. The papain-treated group was distributed in a band along the PC2 axis, demonstrating a better deodorising effect. Notably, the combined treatment points were the most widely dispersed. Furthermore, the loading plot (Fig. 3c) showed that sensors W1S, W3S, and W1C were positioned farthest from the origin, making the most prominent contribution. According to the sensor response characteristics (Table S1), this indicated that the key odour differences among the samples stemmed primarily from their responses to aromatic compounds, benzenoids, hydrides, and alkanes.Fig. 3E-nose radar plot (a), PCA score plot (b) of the electronic nose data and PCA loading plot (c).Fig. 3

This distribution pattern likely stems from the distinct mechanisms and removal efficiencies of the treatments: NaOH may act via alkali neutralisation, whereas ethanol may act via solvent dissolution, respectively (Liu et al., 2024). However, their limited efficacy results in only minor alterations to the sample odour profiles, reflected in the high overlap with the control in the radar chart (Fig. 3a) and the tightly clustered points in Fig. 3b. Papain hydrolyses the protein matrix to release off-odour compounds embedded in the tissue (Dong et al., 2025), though its effectiveness may fluctuate. This results in a moderately expanded profile in Fig. 3a and dispersed short bands in Fig. 3b.

The composite treatment adopts a synergistic sequence in which enzymatic hydrolysis is followed by alkaline treatment and ethanol extraction. Initially, papain hydrolyses the protein structure; NaOH then further disrupts the protein network; finally, ethanol treatment enhances tissue permeability and promotes the release of degradation products. This sequence not only achieves highly efficient removal of the original off-odour compounds but also likely causes extensive degradation of tissue proteins and fats. This complex process may consequently generate and release a broader variety and higher concentration of new volatile compounds (such as aldehydes, ketones, and aromatic derivatives). The overall outcome is manifested as the significantly expansive profile in Fig. 3a and the widely dispersed, elongated band-like distribution of sample points in Fig. 3b. This result directly corroborates the conclusion in Section 3.2.1 that the combined process possesses efficient odour-removal capacity.

As shown in Fig. 4a, both ethanol and NaOH treatments reduced sample hardness and chewiness, with values decreasing to 1656.69 g and 1390.99, respectively. Papain treatment led to a further reduction to 1406.14 g and 1180.53 g, respectively. The observed textural changes can be attributed to the distinct mechanisms through which each treatment disrupts protein structure: ethanol treatment induces protein denaturation and alters spatial conformation (Lee et al., 2024); NaOH disrupts disulfide and ionic bonds, leading to protein dissolution and extension (Cheng et al., 2019). Both treatments primarily affect non-covalent interactions that maintain the tertiary structure, resulting in similar textural modifications (Duan et al., 2026). In contrast, papain specifically hydrolyses peptide bonds in myofibrillar proteins (Wang, Ma, Jiang et al., 2025), enhancing the water-holding capacity and consequently producing a more substantial reduction in hardness. The combined treatment achieved efficient removal of off-odour components through a stepwise synergistic process. First, papain performed preliminary enzymatic hydrolysis, disrupting part of the protein structure; NaOH then provided an alkaline environment that induced deeper dissociation and increased solubility (Shang et al., 2022); finally, ethanol treatment enhanced tissue permeability and facilitated the complete leaching of degradation products. This process significantly reduced the hardness and chewiness, indicating effective structural weakening that favours off-odour removal and exhibits a pronounced synergistic deodorisation effect. Ribeiro et al. (2023) also reported that papain increases myofibrillar protein solubility and enhances patty tenderness. Similarly, Han et al. (2025) demonstrated that the synergistic effect of papain and ultrasound reduces hardness and chewiness, improving product tenderness.

Cooking loss

Cooking loss, a key indicator of water-holding capacity in meat products, directly reflects their ability to retain moisture during thermal processing (Zhao et al., 2024). As shown in Fig. 4b, the effects of different treatments on cooking loss in porcine large intestine varied markedly. The cooking loss in NaOH and papain treatments were reduced 17.47% and 23.87%, respectively, whereas in ethanol treatment were increased 5.08%. Notably, the combined treatment achieved the highest reduction, at 28.04%. This may be attributed to excessive protein denaturation and disrupts the protein network structure (Furutani & Izawa, 2022), thereby weakening its water-retention capacity. In contrast, sodium hydroxide destabilises the tertiary protein structure, exposing hydrophilic groups and enhancing overall hydration (Du et al., 2021). Papain, on the other hand, cleaves peptide bonds, creating more space and channels for water immobilisation.

Meanwhile, the polar amino acid side chains exposed by enzymolysis strengthen hydrogen bonding and ionic interactions with water molecules (Shang et al., 2022; Yang et al., 2026). This explains the respective 17.47% and 23.87% reductions in cooking loss observed with these two treatments. The combined treatment integrates the synergistic mechanisms of ethanol-enhanced penetration, alkali-induced protein disruption, and enzymatic hydrolysis, collectively establishing a more stable water-holding system that maximally inhibits moisture loss during cooking and achieves a significant 28.04% reduction. Zhang, Zhu, et al. (2023) demonstrated that papain was more effective in reducing cooking loss when combined with other methods than when used alone.

Conclusion

This study utilised the combined technology of HS-SPME-GC–MS and GC-O-MS to accurately identify the characteristic off-odour substances of porcine large intestine, which were mainly 3-methylindole (with the highest OAV), 4-methylphenol (with the highest content) and indole. The odour reconstitution and omission experiments further confirmed that these three substances were the primary contributors to the characteristic off-odour of porcine large intestine. To effectively remove the aforementioned odours, this study developed a combined treatment, which successively utilised papain (at 1.2%, treated for 45 min), NaOH (at 1.2 g/L, treated for 30 min) and ethanol (at 35%, soaked for 45 min). This process achieves the efficient and coordinated removal of key odour substances through a three-step collaborative mechanism: deep hydrolysis of the protein matrix by papain to release odours, disruption of protein structure and neutralisation of key odour compounds by NaOH and ethanol-mediated breaking of interactions between odour substances and tissues to achieve final dissolution. The total reduction rate of odour substances reached 82.60%, and the deodorisation effect is significantly better than that of any single treatment. Moreover, the combined treatment significantly improved the texture and water retention properties of the product, reducing the hardness and chewiness by a large margin. The cooking loss decreased by 28.04%, and the product achieved the highest overall acceptance in the sensory evaluation. In summary, this study clarified and verified the effectiveness of the enzyme–alkali–alcohol synergistic odour-removal mechanism, providing a robust theoretical foundation and a practical technical pathway for odour control and industrial processing of porcine large intestine and other similar animal by-products.
