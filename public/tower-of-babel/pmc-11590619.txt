Clinical decision making and moral distress among intensive care units nurses in Iran
Authors: Arefeh Arash, GHolamreza Mahmoodi-shan, Fatemeh Mehravar, Zahra Sabzi, Hamideh Mancheri
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIntensive care units are often presented as environments where ethical issues are common and decisions can determine the life or death of patients, and these units have unique challenges due to critical health care. In these units, the relationship between the medical team and the patient’s relatives, their refusal of treatment, informed consent causes the nurses to have conflict in their decision making, therefore, this study aims to determine the level of clinical decision-making and moral distress and the relationship between them in intensive care units nurses.MethodsThis cross-sectional study was conducted with a descriptive-analytical approach in 2023 in Gorgan city. The number of 198 nurses in the intensive care units of Gonbad Kavos hospitals in the north of Iran were investigated and evaluated using the Corley Moral Distress questionnaire (2002), and Laurie’s Clinical Decision questionnaire (2001) in 2023. Independent T-test and anova analysis of variance were used for bivariate analysis. The significance level in this study was considered 0.05.ResultsThe results of the study showed that the mean and standard deviation of clinical decision making was 60.98 ± 10.25 (analytical-systematic level) and moral distress was 92.2 ± 23.61 (moderate level). There was a statistically significant relationship between clinical decision-making and nurses’ moral distress (P < 0.001 and r = 0.370). The moral distress score had a statistically significant relationship with marriage, employment status, education level, age, work experience and duration of employment in the special department. Also, the clinical decision score had a statistically significant relationship with employment status, education level, age and work experience.ConclusionAccording to these results, it seems that more attention should be paid to the moral distress of nurses in intensive care units, and it is necessary to improve their decision-making towards intuitive interpretation, which is done by designing and compiling training programs and workshops for nurses can be done, so that we can provide optimal nursing services to the patients of this department.Keywords: Nursing, Moral distress, Clinical decision-making, Intensive care units

Introduction

Nurses are the main providers of care and play an important role in the health care system, and they are often the first group to be aware of changes in patients’ conditions, and by implementing quick interventions, they can save patients from accidents that lead to harm and reduce death [1]. Considering the scope of nursing science, their sensitive responsibilities and the complex clinical conditions of patients, it is necessary for nurses to be able to make the right decision in the shortest possible time to save the patient’s life. Among the important roles of nurses in decision-making departments it is right in times of crisis. Clinical decision-making can be considered interventions and solutions that help to improve the clinical condition of the patient from the current condition to the final optimal condition. Timely decision-making accelerates the progress of the treatment process of patients, takes care of them more and reduces the costs of treatment and is a key factor in providing safe and efficient care [2]. Decision making in nursing is a complicated process. Clinical decision-making is the process of obtaining mental and objective data in relation to the patient’s condition and evaluating the data to choose the best course of action to achieve the desired goals and is considered the basic part of the nursing profession [3]. Correct decisions can be made It helps to improve the quality of care, reduce the duration of illness and disability, reduce costs, and make optimal use of resources, and wrong decisions can cause injury and loss and reduce the quality of care [4].

Studies show that decision-making skills among nurses are noticeably decreasing. It has even been mentioned in some studies that nurses do not have good clinical decision-making ability [5, 6]. Some studies conducted in Iran have also reported the level of clinical decision-making of Iranian nurses at an average level [7].

Important factors in clinical decision-making by nurses can be mentioned, including experience and intuition, decision-making context and environment, patient identification, clinical reasoning and critical thinking [8, 9]. In addition, dealing with new and complex technology such as monitors heart, unstable and severely ill patients and lack of resources, It puts a heavy burden on intensive care nurses to find the best clinical choices and affects their decision-making ability [10]. Therefore, the special nature of these departments requires that the nurses working in these departments must be competent decision makers and the clinical environment should be provided in such a way that the nurses can act more independently and find the solutions that come to their mind to solve the patients’ problems [7].

The decision-making process for patients in special care units can cause moral distress in nurses. The concept of moral distress was first presented by Jumpton, and then it was investigated and expanded by many researchers. He initially examined the concept of moral distress in 1938 and stated that nurses experience moral distress when faced with situations in which they are aware of the correct action but are unable to do it [11]. According to Corrado and Molinaro, moral distress refers to feelings of guilt, powerlessness, and anger that health care professionals experience in situations where they cannot act in accordance with their professional and personal values [12].

Nurses working in intensive care units are frequently faced with unique ethical dilemmas that include the lack of end-of-life conversations, concerns about optimal patient pain management, and conflicting concerns with physicians about patient treatment that require decisions that are often made in It is in conflict with their personal and professional values [13]. Moral distress is a phenomenon that can have different effects on nurses, patients and health systems and lead to harmful consequences. This phenomenon can cause the nurse to have a conflict in the field of patient care and avoid confronting the patient and providing him with quality care [14]. These conditions provide the basis for defects in the recovery process and increase the duration of hospitalization [15]. Also, in addition to the mentioned cases, moral distress, if continued, causes emotional reactions such as anger, despair, guilt, physical disorders such as headaches and sleep disorders in nurses [16].

Al-Turfi et al.‘s study (2022), which was conducted in Iraq, showed that there was a significant relationship between moral distress and clinical decision-making by nurses in intensive care units [17]. The study by Tahmasebi et al. (2021) which was conducted in Iran showed that there was no statistically significant relationship between clinical decision-making and moral distress of nurses working in the pediatric department [18]. In a study conducted by Kalani et al. (2023), it was shown that there is an inverse and significant relationship between moral distress and clinical competence and skill application [19].

There is no scientific consensus regarding the relationship between clinical decision-making and moral distress in intensive care nurses, and due to the few studies conducted regarding the relationship between clinical decision-making and moral distress in special care nurses, it is necessary to conduct this research. Intensive care units are often presented as environments where ethical issues are common and the decisions of the treatment team can determine the life or death of patients and the length of their treatment, and these units have unique challenges due to Critical health care. In these units, the relationship between the medical team and the patient’s companions, their refusal of treatment, informed consent causes the nurses to have conflict in their decision making. Therefore, considering the characteristics of special care units and the lack of sufficient studies on decision-making patterns and moral distress, this study was conducted with the aim of investigating the relationship between clinical decision-making and moral distress in nurses of intensive care units of Gonbad hospitals in 2023.

Design

This study is cross-sectional and descriptive-analytical in which the research samples are nurses who work in the intensive care units (CCU, ICU and dialysis) of hospitals affiliated to Golestan University of Medical Sciences (province in the north of Iran) were working in Gonbad Kavus city (Hospital of the Payambarazam, Shahid Motahari and shohada). The sampling method in this study was stratified and Convenience from each stratum. The sampling method in this study was stratified and Convenience from each stratum. Quota sampling is a non-probability sampling technique in which participants are selected based on pre-defined quotas for certain characteristics, such as age, gender, education level, or type of hospital [20]. After estimating the total sample size, based on the number of nurses in each hospital and its share, a portion of the total sample was considered for that hospital (Table 1).

B) Corley moral distress questionnaire (2002)

Corley moral distress questionnaire (2002)

Moral distress scale (MDS) is the first instrument which was initially developed to measure the frequency and intensity of MD in intensive care nurses in 2001 by Corley and colleagues [22]. 17 Original MDS was designed [23]. The psychometric properties of MDS were examined among 214 intensive care nurses from American hospitals, and test result indicated a Cronbach’s alpha o.98 for individual responsibility, 0.82 for not in patient’s best interest, and 0.84 for deception, respectively. After validated, MDS has been extensively used to detect the frequency and intensity of MD in intensive care nurses. Total variance explained by the three factors was 19.38. The theta test was 0.96 for the entire instrument [22]. This questionnaire has 36 items and is formulated on a seven-point Likert scale (absence: zero score, moderate: 3 score and severe: 6 score). To calculate the overall score of the questionnaire, the scores of all questionnaire items are added together and the score range of this questionnaire is between 0 and 216. The higher the score obtained from this questionnaire, the higher the level and severity of moral distress and vice versa [18, 24].

This valid and reliable questionnaire has been used in many researches in special care nurses in our country [18, 24]. Beikmoradi and his colleagues first prepared and adjusted this questionnaire in the form of translation and re-translation using previous quantitative and qualitative studies in Iran and other countries and based on the cultural and social conditions of the country. A questionnaire organized to determine the validity using the content validity method was examined by 17 professors of nursing and midwifery (Tehran and Hamedan University of Medical Sciences), health, statistics and medicine of Hamedan University of Medical Sciences in terms of achieving the research objectives. And it was approved after the necessary amendments and changes. To determine the reliability of the questionnaire, the retest method was used. The designed questionnaire was completed by 20 people with the same characteristics as the research community. Two weeks later, the questionnaires were completed again by the same people. Then Pearson’s correlation coefficient was calculated for two sets of questionnaires (r = 0.82). Also, Cronbach’s alpha coefficient was calculated to determine the internal reliability of the instrument (α = 0.93) [24].

Cronbach’s alpha in our study for Moral Distress Questionnaire was 0.78 respectively, which indicates high reliability.

C) Laurie’s clinical decision questionnaire (2001)

Laurie’s clinical decision questionnaire (2001)

Lauri’s 24-item clinical decision-making questionnaire is a shortened version of the original 56-item instrument [25] .The 56-item tool was created by Lauri and Salanterä based on various decision-making theories and previous studies on nursing decision-making. The results of studies using a 56-item tool showed that nurses use four types of decision-making models (analytical, analytical-intuitive, intuitive-analytical and intuitive).The reliability scores of the factors were also high (alpha = 0.85–0.91 [26] .Equivalent to the original instrument, the 24-item instrument had four subscales, Respondents answered each question on a 5-point Likert type scale with response options of “always (score 5), often (score 4), average (score 3), rarely (score 2), never (score 1)”. These items were scored from 1 to 5 so the lowest scores measured analytical decision making and the highest scores intuitive decision making. Respondents can get scores from 24 to 120, where a score 24– 67 indicate indicates systematic analytical decision-making, and a score between 68 and 77 is the second level of decision-making, i.e., intuitive analysis, and a score 78–120 indicates the third level of clinical decision-making, i.e., interpretive intuition [18, 26, 27]. The scores for responses to odd items were reversed; thereby, the response option of “never” would be scored as 5 and also indicate a highly intuitive approach. A low total score described analytical decision making and a high-score intuitive decision making [27].

The questionnaire served as a valid and reliable instrument for evaluating clinical decision-making. The results of the exploratory factor analysis accounted for 69.07% of the variance, and the Cronbach’s alpha value was 0.92, which indicated a satisfactory level of internal Consistency [26, 28]. Its reliability and validity have been confirmed in many studies in our country. As Javadi et al. reported that Lori’s clinical decision-making items were approved by English professors and nurses after editing and proofreading. The decision-making items were also confirmed in a preliminary study on 15 nurses of Rasht medical training centers, who were randomly selected, using the internal correlation method and with Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of 78% [29]. Also, in order to measure the reliability of this questionnaire in his study, Karimi Naqander gave the questionnaire to 20 people and the internal correlation coefficient of Cronbach’s alpha was calculated for this questionnaire to be 0.8 [30]. Tahmasebi et al. They reported 0.8 through internal consistency with Cronbach’s alpha [18].

Cronbach’s alpha in our study for Clinical Decision Questionnaire was 0.89 respectively, which indicates high reliability.

After approving the proposal and obtaining permission from the vice president of research and ethics committee (code of ethics: IR.GOUMS.REC.1402.401), the researcher went to the hospitals affiliated to Golestan University of Medical Sciences in Kavous Gonbad and after coordinating and obtaining permission from the head of the center, she went to the intensive dunits. According to the proportional allocation of each hospital and ward, nurses who met the entry criteria were selected as available. The objectives of the research were explained to them, their written consent was obtained and they were asked to complete the relevant questionnaires.

Statistical analysis

The information was entered in SPSS software version 26. Mean and standard deviation statistics were used to describe quantitative variables, and frequency and frequency percentage were used to describe qualitative variables.

Independent T-test and ANOVA analysis of variance were used for bivariate analysis. The significance level in this study was considered 0.05. According to the main purpose of the study, which is to determine the relationship between clinical decision-making and moral distress, Pearson’s correlation test was used.

Results

In this study, 198 nurses working in the intensive care unit in hospitals affiliated to Golestan University of Medical Sciences in Gonbad Kavus city participated in 2023. 31.8% of nurses were male and 71.7% were married. Most of them had a bachelor’s degree and 2% were morning shift nurses. 61.1% of nurses were in medium economic status and 57.1% of nurses were in official employment status and the rest were in plan, contract and company.

The mean score of clinical decision-making and moral availability in nurses was reported as 60.98 ± 10.25 (range 31 to 81) and 92.2 ± 23.61 (range 25 to 195). Pearson’s correlation test showed that there was a direct and significant statistical correlation between the average scores of clinical decision making and moral distress (p = 0.0001 and r = 0.370). In other words, the results of this study showed that nurses who have higher clinical decision-making also experienced more moral distress.

As shown in Table 2, the average scores of clinical decision-making and moral distress in nurses with a bachelor’s degree were higher than those with a master’s degree, and this difference was statistically significant. Also, the average score of clinical decision-making in nurses with contractual employment status has the highest value and the average score of moral distress in nurses with a contract has the highest score. Moral distress score was higher in single nurses than married ones (P = 0.047). Most nurses (198 and %62.2) had systematic analytical decision-making, 65 nurses (%32.8) had intuitive decision-making, and 9 nurses (%4.5) had interpretive decision-making.

Discussion

The results of the analysis of the findings show that the mean score of the moral distress of nurses is 92.2 ± 23.61, which is in the average range, which is in line with the study of Al-Turfi, Tahmasebi and Beikmoradi [17, 18, 24]. Behbodhi et al. (2017) in their study with the aim of evaluating the severity of moral distress of nurses working in the children’s department with the nurses of the pediatric intensive care department in the hospitals of Mazandaran province, found moral distress in 75% of the pediatric department nurses and 58.8% of the intensive care department nurses. Reported children at a low level, which is not consistent with the results of the present study [31]. Among its reasons, we can mention factors such as cultural diversity and differences in the values and opinions of nurses in different places. In the study of Lake and his colleagues, inconsistent with the results of the present study, the amount of moral distress in the nurses of the intensive care department was significantly higher than that of other departments [32]. In this study, the risk of transmission of corona virus to the family members of nurses and care of patients with corona virus without the presence of family members and patients who died without the presence of family and clergy were among the most important factors causing moral distress.

The results of the analysis of the findings show that the mean score of nurses’ clinical decision-making is 60.98 ± 10.25, that their decision-making is of a systematic analytical type. In Parker’s study, nurses’ decision-making progressed towards systematic analytical and analytical-intuitive decision-making with training. According to this study, nurses should be trained to make systematic analytical decisions. The evidence showed that nurses who use systematic analytical decision-making act faster than other nurses and this type of decision-making reduces mortality [33]. Clinical decision-making in Iranian nurses in most of the conducted studies is at an average and intuitive level [34–38], which Tahmasebi believes in this context is the time limitation of nurses due to their busy work that spends a lot of time on care services in medical centers. And also the fatigue caused by it has caused nurses to distance themselves in the use of scientific sources and evidence in the field of clinical decision-making in the provision of patient care, and the fading of the use of evidence-based studies has caused an increase in the use of intuitive care among nurses [18]. The results of our study are inconsistent with the study of Maharmeh et al. (2016). The results of this study showed that nurses used more intuitive decision-making in clinical settings, and one of the most important influencing factors in this field is expressed by the experiences of nurses [10].

The analysis of the findings revealed that there is a significant relationship between moral distress and clinical decision-making of nurses working in the intensive care department in hospitals affiliated to Golestan University of Medical Sciences in Kavos Gonbad, so that nurses who have higher moral distress have higher decision scores. That is, they move towards intuitive decision-making and use clinical experiences and personal beliefs and values.

In this regard, Al-Turfi et al.‘s (2022) showed that there is a positive and direct significant relationship between clinical decision-making and moral distress of Iraqi nurses [17].

While the results of the study by Escolar-Chua et al. (2018) showed that nurses who expressed a higher level of moral distress reported more weakness in making decisions about how to provide care to patients hospitalized in the intensive care unit [39]. In this study, the level of moral distress experienced by nurses was higher and it seems that higher moral distress causes weakness in clinical decision making. The results of Mehlis et al.‘s study (2018) in Germany also showed the relationship between moral distress in nurses and clinical decision-making in cancer patients hospitalized in the intensive care unit at the end of life [40]. In the final stage of life, due to the physical and critical conditions in which they are, many difficulties have been created in the field of clinical decision-making, which has a direct effect on the moral distress of nurses.

In our country, Tahmasebi and his colleagues conducted a study in 2021 entitled “Investigating the relationship between clinical decision-making and moral distress in pediatric intensive care nurses”. In this study, no significant relationship was found between clinical decision-making and moral distress, which was inconsistent with the results of this study [18]. His difference in the results may be due to the difference in the statistical population studied. In this study, the level of induced moral distress was higher in nurses, and it seems that the type of illness of patients hospitalized in the intensive care unit and their conditions have a direct effect on clinical decision-making and moral distress of nurses, as well as children and infants to a large extent. They are not able to express their problems and participate in nurses’ decision-making, and nurses should use their clinical experiences more.

The results of the studies show that the level of moral distress in unmarried nurses was significantly higher than that of married nurses. In the study of Hashemi Parast et al., consistent with the results of the present study, the level of moral distress in single nurses was more intense and frequent [41]. In the explanation of obtaining such results, it can be said that since single nurses are more likely to be younger than married nurses and have less clinical experience, their moral distress decreases with increasing level of awareness and clinical experience.

Also, in our study, the mean moral distress in nurses with a plan employment status was significantly higher than other nurses’ employment status, which results are in line with the study of Hashemi Parast et al., who stated that the severity of moral distress in nurses was related to their employment conditions; As the nurses who were officially employed experienced less intensity of distress [41], in explaining this issue, it seems that official nurses are more likely to perform better than plan nurses due to their work experience and more experience in distress management. While in the study of Beikmoradi there was no significant relationship between employment status and moral distress [24] and it is not consistent with the present study.

In the explanation of other findings of our study, there was no correlation between moral distress and age, gender, work experience in the clinic in the study of Beikmoradi and colleagues, consistent with the results of the present study [24].

In the study of Hashemi Parast et al., which is not consistent with the results of the present study, it was found that with increasing age and work experience, the frequency and intensity of moral distress experienced by nurses decreases [41]. The results of AL taker et al. (2018), inconsistent with the results of the present study, showed higher moral distress of female nurses in caring for patients hospitalized in intensive care units [42] that individual-cultural and social differences, organizational structure and care processes and The treatment of the research community can be one of the reasons for this difference in the findings.

Another result of this study was the statistically significant relationship between clinical decision-making and age, so that clinical decision-making becomes intuitive-analytical with increasing age. The study of Mirsaeidi and Shahraki Moghadam showed that the amount of clinical decision-making in different age groups has a significant statistical difference, and participation in decision-making increases with increasing age and work experience [35, 43]. In explaining this issue, it can be said that increasing age and increasing work experience causes more participation in the decision-making process. Study by Qudsi that is not aligned with the present study showed that there is no significant relationship between age and clinical decision-making [44]. Among the reasons for the inconsistency of these studies with the present study, factors such as cultural diversity and differences in the values and opinions of nurses in different parts of the country can be mentioned.

Amoroso et al. (2019) did not report a significant statistical relationship between the demographic indicators of American nurses working in the intensive care unit (age, gender, clinical experience) with clinical decision-making, in line with the results of the present study [45]. The results of Mousavi Nasab et al.‘s study, in line with the results of the present study, indicated the absence of any significant relationship between work experience, age and gender of nurses working in different hospital departments with clinical decision-making among them [46].

Limitation

The fact that the questionnaire is self-reported can be one of the limitations of the research, which is affected by the mental and psychological conditions of the participants. The researcher has solved this limitation to some extent by explaining the objectives of the research, giving enough time and completing the questionnaire during the break.

Conclusion

The present study showed that the clinical decision-making of nurses working in intensive care units is of a systematic analytical type and the level of moral distress is moderate, and there is a positive and statistically significant relationship between clinical decision-making and moral distress. Since the level of moral distress and the type of clinical decision-making affect the quality of care and patient safety, it seems that more attention should be paid to the moral distress of nurses in special care units and their decision-making should be promoted towards intuitive interpretation. Therefore, nurses should receive specialized training in clinical decision-making using different techniques, which can be done by designing and developing educational programs and workshops. Also, develop the teaching and learning process by allocating enough time for clinical training and creating opportunities to observe and discuss clinical issues.
