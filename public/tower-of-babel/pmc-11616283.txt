Deep vein thrombosis and aneurysm co-occurrence: a case report and review of the literature
Authors: Meghdad Ghasemi Gorji, Amirhossein Rajabi, Sina Sohrabizadeh, Mahtab Asgari, Ali Rafiei, Fardin Karbakhsh Ravari

Introduction

Deep vein thrombosis (DVT) and aortic aneurysms represent critical vascular conditions with distinct pathophysiological mechanisms. DVT involves the formation of blood clots within deep calf veins, while aortic aneurysms result from weakened segments in the aorta [1–6].

However, intriguingly, there exists a rare interplay between these two entities. Case reports suggest that arterial aneurysms can occasionally precipitate DVT by compressing nearby veins, disrupting blood flow, and potentially leading to clot formation [7–22]. In this case report, we delve into this unique relationship, exploring a case involving coexisting DVT, aortic aneurysm, and iliac artery aneurysm under low-risk circumstances.

Case presentation

A 20-year-old Iranian female patient with a prior history of left lower limb deep vein thrombosis (DVT) at age 19 years underwent a coagulation factor evaluation. Test results for factor V Leiden mutation, antithrombin, and other coagulation factors were negative, and no underlying cause for the DVT was identified. The patient received anticoagulation therapy consisting of rivaroxaban, 10 mg, twice daily, for 6 months.

One year later, at the age of 20 years, the patient presented with abdominal and flank pain accompanied by pallor. Upon initial presentation with abdominal and flank pain, the patient displayed notable pallor, suggestive of anemia secondary to acute blood loss. Vital signs revealed hypotension with a blood pressure of 85/50 mmHg and tachycardia, with a heart rate of 120 beats per minute. Clinical examination revealed tenderness in the right lower abdominal quadrant with guarding, but no rebound tenderness was observed. Initial laboratory tests indicated a significant drop in hemoglobin to 6 g/dL, further supporting hemorrhagic anemia likely owing to the intraabdominal and pelvic hemorrhage. Other laboratory values showed mildly elevated white blood cell counts. C-reactive protein (CRP) levels were within the normal range. Renal and liver function tests were within normal limits, and electrolytes were balanced.

Following initial evaluations and a high clinical suspicion of intraabdominal and pelvic hemorrhage, a computed tomography (CT) angiography was performed after stabilizing the patient. The imaging revealed significant dilation of the right common iliac artery, accompanied by rupture and mural thrombi along the arterial wall (Fig. 1). This finding confirmed the presence of an isolated right common iliac aneurysm with rupture, necessitating immediate surgical intervention to prevent further complications.

Isolated rupture of right iliac artery aneurysm (red arrow)

Intraoperatively, given the complete rupture of the right common iliac, closure of the right internal iliac artery was achieved with an 8-mm Dacron graft extending from the origin of the right common iliac to the right external iliac. Following the procedure, the patient’s pulse was restored, and she was transferred to the intensive care unit (ICU), where she was stabilized.

Postoperatively, the patient regained consciousness with a restored pulse; however, severe abdominal pain persisted, necessitating analgesic management. A follow-up CT scan revealed no new abnormalities. Suspecting inflammation, hydrocortisone was administered, which relieved the pain. She was discharged on corticosteroids, and subsequent rheumatological testing returned normal results.

Approximately 6 weeks later, the patient presented with symptoms indicative of small bowel obstruction. Initial conservative management was attempted, but as abdominal distension worsened and a large pancreatic pseudocyst was visualized on a CT scan, a laparotomy was performed to relieve obstructive symptoms caused by the cyst’s compression on the intestinal tract (Fig. 2). The pseudocyst was successfully drained. Postoperatively, the patient continued to produce 600–700 cc of secretions daily even after a month, prompting the addition of oral pancreatin therapy. Rheumatological tests and repeat coagulation factor assessments remained negative. Evaluation for the potential necessity of a distal pancreatectomy to address the pseudocyst is ongoing.

Pancreatic pseudocyst (red arrow) and the right iliac artery, bypassed using graft (yellow arrow), are shown

Pathological examination of the aneurysm sac identified only an inflammatory aneurysm with no additional findings. During the treatment course, the patient also experienced a right ureteral stone, managed conservatively. C-reactive protein (CRP) levels remained within the normal range. The patient is currently maintained on anticoagulant therapy. A follow-up CT scan showed a patent graft after 1 month, but graft thrombosis was noted after 2 months. Despite this finding, the patient remains asymptomatic, with no evidence of organ ischemia or compromised blood flow.

Discussion

Deep vein thrombosis (DVT) affects the venous system in the lower limbs. It initiates clot formation in a deep calf vein, leading to an obstructive condition that hampers the venous reflux mechanism [1].

Iliac artery aneurysms are rare, more commonly observed in older men, and have a relatively slow progression but a higher risk of rupture as they increase in size. Early detection and proper management are crucial owing to the significantly higher mortality rate associated with emergency surgical interventions [23]. Embolization of the internal iliac artery (IIA) is a common occurrence during endovascular abdominal aortic aneurysm (AAA) repair, and unilateral IIA embolization is well tolerated, enabling effective endovascular treatment options for patients with AAA and iliac artery aneurysms [24].

Furthermore, some researchers have reported cases of iliac artery aneurysms presenting with lower extremity deep vein thrombosis [20] or cases in which an external iliac artery aneurysm causes severe venous obstruction [15]. It seems that the association of DVT with any vascular aneurysm (whether venous or arterial aneurysm) is more common in men than in women. Among the 16 patients who had an aneurysm and DVT at the same time, 14 of them were men. Most patients were in the sixth to seventh decade of life, although there were cases from the third, fourth, and fifth decade [25–36]. Cardiovascular diseases, hypertension, hyperlipidemia, and smoking were the most common underlying risk factors among people who had DVT and aneurysms, although most people had no underlying disease [25–27, 29–31, 33, 36].

In certain instances, arterial aneurysms have resulted in flow disturbances and deep vein thrombosis (DVT) by exerting pressure on the veins [25, 27–30, 36]. Most patients presenting with both DVT and arterial aneurysms concurrently typically had an aneurysm in the abdominal aorta (AAA), particularly in the infrarenal aorta [27–29, 36]. It is widely acknowledged that AAA can obstruct the small bowel and the ureter. Cases of DVT due to compression by an AAA are uncommon [37]. This condition is frequently associated with fibrosis caused by inflammatory AAA. AAA without fibrosis has been reported to cause IVC compression and DVT when exceeding 6 cm in width. Inflammatory AAAs are more likely to manifest symptoms (80%), while it is uncommon for noninflammatory aneurysms to be symptomatic (7–30%) [17]. This case illustrates that the IVC compression by the AAA is severe enough to cause flow abnormalities with unilateral DVT [29].

In one of the cases, DVT was linked to venous aneurysm [26]. Venous aneurysms, especially those affecting the inferior vena cava (IVC), are rare. A 2008 literature review showed that IVC involvement was observed in only 3 of 29 reported venous aneurysms [38]. The patient with a venous aneurysm and DVT was younger than those with arterial aneurysms, [26] at 33 years old. The most common types of IVC aneurysms associated with DVT are type II–IV. Therefore, an IVC aneurysm should be considered in the differential diagnosis of the etiology of DVT [26].

Behçet’s disease was observed in 3 out of 15 cases. Behçet’s disease (BD) is a complex vasculitis with various vascular manifestations, including venous thrombosis, arterial thrombosis/aneurysm/pseudoaneurysm, and co-associated venous thrombosis and arterial lesions. The same inflammatory process appears responsible for arterial and venous involvement in patients with BD [39]. One of these three cases had Hughes–Stovin syndrome. Hughes–Stovin syndrome is an incomplete form of Behçet’s syndrome characterized by recurrent thrombophlebitis, deep vein thrombosis, and multiple pulmonary and bronchial aneurysms. Patients with Hughes–Stovin syndrome typically do not present with oral or genital ulcerations, skin lesions, iritis, or arthralgia, unlike those with Behçet’s disease [33].

Another case report of DVT and aneurysm involves chronic traumatic arteriovenous fistula (AVF). Chronic traumatic AVF is linked to unrecognized or untreated penetrating vascular injury. Closure of all AVFs appeared to be the optimal treatment option [31].

The remaining case reports are associated with recurrent nontyphoidal Salmonella bacteremia. Mycotic aneurysms are a well-recognized complication of nontyphoidal Salmonella bacteremia; the risk is elevated in patients with atherosclerotic disease. The infrarenal abdominal aorta is the most common site of infection, and its compressive mechanisms can cause DVT [40].

A femoral artery aneurysm (FAA) is an uncommon peripheral vascular aneurysm that can lead to thrombosis, embolism, and life-threatening rupture if left untreated. The manifestations of FAA range from the discovery of an asymptomatic mass during regular physical examination to the emergence of acute limb-threatening ischemia. Recently, a case involving FAA resulting in deep vein thrombosis and compartment syndrome has been documented. While the conventional treatment for FAA typically involves an open surgical approach, in this instance, an endovascular intervention was undertaken owing to the significant surgical mortality risk [41].

In another case report, the authors describe a patient found to have an undiagnosed abdominal aortic aneurysm. The patient presented to the emergency department with sudden swelling in one leg following the rupture of the aneurysm into the vena cava. Upon conducting an abdominal examination, concerns arose about potential intraabdominal pathology, which was subsequently confirmed through a bedside ultrasound examination, leading to a computed tomography evaluation of the aorta. The patient was expeditiously transferred for urgent surgical intervention and experienced a favorable recovery outcome. This case serves as a sobering reminder to healthcare providers that, while unilateral leg swelling is a prevalent symptom encountered in frontline settings, deep vein thrombosis is not the sole important differential diagnosis to consider [42].

Conclusion

The association of deep vein thrombosis (DVT) with vascular aneurysms, whether venous or arterial, appears to be a rare occurrence. The majority of reported cases involve men, particularly in the sixth to seventh decade of life. Underlying risk factors such as cardiovascular diseases, hypertension, hyperlipidemia, and smoking are commonly observed in these individuals. It is crucial to consider the possibility of aneurysm-related flow disturbances and compression effects on veins when evaluating patients with concurrent DVT and aneurysms. Additionally, the presence of Behçet’s disease, Hughes–Stovin syndrome, chronic traumatic arteriovenous fistula, and recurrent nontyphoidal Salmonella bacteremia as potential contributors to this association should not be overlooked. Further research and awareness are needed to better understand and manage this rare but clinically significant phenomenon.
