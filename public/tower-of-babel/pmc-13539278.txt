A novel method to assess delay discounting indicates greater ambivalence for delayed rewards
Authors: Esther Kim, Rebecca Kurnellas, Sergej Grunevski, Julia Felton, Richard Yi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractInspired by Warren K. Bickel, who introduced numerous procedural and conceptual innovations to the study of delay discounting, the present study seeks to further establish the validity of a delay discounting task that is able to assess degree of preference when making a choice. We examine the construct of ambivalence, which may provide methodological and conceptual advancements in the study of delay discounting. In a sample of 105 participants, individuals used a 100‐point scale to indicate not only preference between immediate and delayed rewards but also the degree of that preference. Using this approach, both the magnitude effect and the date/delay effect were replicated, further establishing the validity of the task. Following conversion of the degree of preference to an ambivalence score, we examined associations between rate of delay discounting and between‐individual levels of ambivalence, finding that lower rates of delay discounting were associated with greater ambivalence. We also compared within‐individual ambivalence when the immediate reward versus the delayed reward was preferred, finding greater ambivalence was observed when the delayed reward was preferred. We speculate that the construct of ambivalence may offer some new opportunities in the conceptual understanding of delay discounting and the application of this work to health‐relevant behaviors.Keywords: Ambivalence, delay discounting, preference

INTRODUCTION

A useful construct in the study of decision making is delay discounting, which reflects the decrease in the subjective value of an outcome as a function of its delay (Odum, 2011). Given the diversity of daily decisions that require a trade‐off between immediate and delayed outcomes, delay discounting has significant implications for a wide range of behaviors that influence health, wealth, and happiness. And in many instances, this proposition has proved to be accurate, with higher rates of delay discounting (indicative of a greater decrease in the value of delayed outcomes) being associated with behaviors such as financial mismanagement, pathological gambling, compromised health and safety risk, a variety of psychiatric disorders, and increased risk of death (Amlung et al., 2019; Boyle et al., 2013; Dixon et al., 2003; Hamilton & Potenza, 2012; Mishra & Lalumière, 2017).

Warren K. Bickel, which this special issue of JEAB memorializes, is inarguably one of the most important behavioral scientists to apply delay discounting to the study of health behaviors. One area in which Bickel has been particularly influential is the development of methods that have enhanced the theoretical understanding of delay discounting and its application to health‐related behaviors. These innovative methods include discounting tasks using nonmoney rewards (Epstein et al., 2010; Madden et al., 1999) as well as multiple reward types (i.e., cross‐commodity: Bickel, Landes, et al., 2011; Moody et al., 2017), inclusion of context effects (Charlton et al., 2013; Radu et al., 2011), exploration of past outcomes (Fontes et al., 2025; Yi et al., 2006), and methods to facilitate data collection (Koffarnus & Bickel, 2014; Yi et al., 2010) and manage data (Johnson & Bickel, 2008; Franck et al., 2019). Moreover, the construct of delay discounting is central to Bickel's competing neurobehavioral decision systems hypothesis (Bickel et al., 2016) and reinforcer pathology model (Bickel et al., 2020; Bickel, Jarmolowicz, et al., 2011). It is in that spirit of innovation that we seek to further establish the construct of ambivalence in the study of delay discounting.

Ambivalence in delay discounting

With some exception (see Collado et al., 2017; Weatherly et al., 2010), a significant majority of the research implementing delay discounting methods use binary choice procedures that assume a constant degree of certainty when participants make choices. Grunevski et al. (2022) have previously examined this assumption by using four different methods to evaluate whether degree of ambivalence (for the present purposes, the inverse of certainty about the preferred option) varied within individuals and across items in the Monetary Choice Questionnaire (MCQ; Kirby et al., 1999). That study not only confirmed that ambivalence was variable within‐subject but also that the variability was systematic, with greater ambivalence scores for trials “near” the participant discount rate k. That is, as the subjective values of the immediate and delayed outcomes approached equivalence for a specific participant, that participant exhibited greater ambivalence when making those choices. The present study seeks to further establish the validity of a degree of preference paradigm in a delay discounting task as well as to consider the possibility that degree of ambivalence may be associated with preference for the immediate or delayed outcome.

The current study

Using the 100‐point continuous sliding scale task (Condition A4 from Grunevski et al., 2022), participants indicated both the preferred option in a delay discounting task (similar to traditional binary choice tasks) as well as the degree of preference for that option. As the degree of preference is a novel modification of established delay discounting procedures, it is important to evaluate whether it reproduces well‐established findings from the delay discounting literature implementing the conventional binary choice paradigm. Accordingly, our first goal was to replicate two distinct effects observed in validated binary choice tasks: (1) the magnitude effect (Benzion et al., 1989; Grace & McLean, 2005; Green et al., 1997; Myerson & Green, 1995; Thaler, 1981), where smaller rewards are discounted more steeply than larger rewards, and (2) the date/delay effect (DeHart & Odum, 2015; Keidel et al., 2024; Read et al., 2005), where rewards are discounted more steeply when delays are presented as time intervals (e.g., 30 days) rather than equivalent calendar dates (e.g., August 1). Our second goal was to explore whether greater or lesser ambivalence is associated with preference for immediate versus delayed outcomes.

A total of 177 individuals (Mean age = 33.81 years, SD = 8.03) were recruited from the Amazon Mechanical Turk (MTurk) crowdsourcing platform. Eligible participants were between the ages of 18 and 65, currently living in the United States, had a human intelligence task (HIT) approval rate of 95% or higher, and had more than 100 HITs approved (see Table 1 for participant demograhpics).

Measures

Monetary Choice Questionnaires

The small (nine items) and large (nine items) magnitude conditions of the 27‐item Monetary Choice Questionnaire (MCQ; Kirby et al., 1999) were used to assess delay discounting, resulting in an 18‐item assessment. Replicating the magnitude effect requires comparisons between a minimum of two reward magnitude conditions. Therefore, the small and large magnitude conditions were included to maximize sensitivity to magnitude differences while abbreviating the task. Immediate monetary rewards ranged from $11 to $78, and delayed monetary rewards ranged from $25 to $85, with delays between 7 and 186 days. The two versions of this 18‐item task differed in whether delays to the delayed reward were presented as calendar dates (date‐framed condition, e.g., $80 on December 1) or as the equivalent delay from the date of the session (delay‐framed condition, e.g., $80 in 30 days). The immediate option always appeared on the left side, and the delayed option always appeared on the right side.

Participants responded to each delay discounting item by moving an indicator along a horizontal line to indicate which option they preferred and the degree of preference (Figure 1). On each trial, the indicator defaulted to the midpoint of the scale, such that participants started each response centered on the horizontal line. This 100‐point horizontal line was marked so that the extreme left indicated Strongly Prefer Left Option and the extreme right indicated Strongly Prefer Right Option. The half‐way point between the extreme and midpoint indicated Slightly Prefer the respective option. As we needed an indicator of preference to determine discount rate (as in the binary choice task), leaving the indicator at the line midpoint (i.e., no preference) was not accepted.

Procedure

The survey was administered online using Qualtrics. Participants first read over an information statement and provided informed consent. All procedures were approved by the university's institutional review board.

Participants were randomly assigned to either the date‐framed (n = 90) or delay‐framed (n = 87) MCQ condition. Trials were randomized, with the one attention check item appearing in the middle of each task (i.e., “Would you prefer $25 today or $80 today?”). Upon completion of the entire session, participants were compensated the recommended pay rate requested by MTurk workers—$0.10 per minute, resulting in a mean compensation of $2.00 given a mean completion time of 10.4 minutes (Chandler and Shapiro, 2016).

Data analysis

Discount rates (k)

The position of the indicator was converted to a 100‐point scale, where “0” represented the indicator at the far left and “100” indicated the indicator at the far right. To evaluate the degree of ambivalence on specific trials relative to the discount rate (as in Grunevski et al., 2022), trials at each magnitude were rank ordered by their associated k (lowest to highest, see Table 2). A participant's presumed switch point (from immediate to delayed) was then identified based on the k value (calculated as described above). The trial that theoretically represented a participant's final choice of the immediate outcome before switching to the delayed outcome based on their discount rate was designated as Left 1 (L1), and the trial that represented a participant's first choice of the delayed outcome following a switch from the immediate outcome was designated as Right 1 (R1). Trials L2 and L3 and R2 and R3, were similarly designated. For example, if a participant's k for a specific magnitude was 0.065, which falls between the 7th (0.041) and 8th (0.10) ranked k, then the 7th trial was designated L1 and the 8th trial was designated R1 for that participant, with the 6th trial and the 9th trial designated as L2 and R2, respectively.

Exclusion criteria

Data were screened to ensure participant engagement. Participants who failed the attention‐check item were excluded (date = 16, delay = 9), and participants who exhibited no variability in preference (i.e., preferred only the immediate option or only the delayed option across all trials across both magnitudes; date = 24, delay = 23) were excluded. In total, 40 participants were excluded from the date condition and 32 from delay condition. Following these exclusions, the final analytic sample consisted of 105 participants (delay condition: n = 55; date condition: n = 50).

To test our replication of the magnitude and date/delay effects, a mixed‐effects model was conducted on the ln(k) values using date/delay condition (date vs. delay), magnitude condition (small vs. large), and their interaction as fixed effects, with participant included as a random intercept. All categorical variables were sum coded before mixed‐effect modeling. We report b, the unstandardized coefficients of our regression models. To evaluate whether the inclusion of interaction terms improved model fit, a likelihood‐ratio test was conducted comparing the full mixed‐effects model (including all interactions) with a reduced model examining only the main effects. A sensitivity analysis was conducted to evaluate the potential influence of participant exclusions on the primary findings using the full sample prior to exclusions (N = 177). To test associations between ambivalence and rate of delay discounting, Pearson correlations were calculated between participants' mean ambivalence scores and their ln(k) values, separately for each magnitude. Prior to examining trial‐level ambivalence scores, ambivalence score distributions were examined separately for the date and delay conditions and were found to be positively skewed in both. To normalize those distributions, a constant (1) was added to all scores and then log transformed—log10(ambivalence score + 1). The mixed‐effect model was then run on these log‐transformed ambivalence scores with side (left vs. right), distance from switch point (1, 2, 3), magnitude (small vs. large), and condition (date vs. delay) as fixed effects, along with all two‐way, three‐way, and four‐way interactions, and participant as a random intercept. Follow‐up pairwise comparisons on significant main effects and interactions were conducted using estimated marginal means.

To assess the potential influence of group differences on delay discounting, demographic characteristics were compared between the date and delay conditions. No statistically significant differences were observed between conditions in sex, race, ethnicity, educational level, employment status, income, or age. Given the small number of racial minorities, race was collapsed into White versus non‐White, education level into bachelor's degree or higher versus less than a bachelor's degree, employment status into full‐time and not full‐time, and income into <50, 50–75, ≥75 K. Age was compared using Welch's t test, sex, ethnicity, race, education level, and income were compared using chi‐square tests, and employment status was compared using Fisher's exact test.

DISCUSSION

The current study first sought to replicate two established effects in the delay discounting literature, the magnitude and date/delay effects, using our novel degree of preference task. Consistent with a significant body of literature (Benzion et al., 1989; DeHart & Odum, 2015; Keidel et al., 2024; Myerson & Green, 1995; Read et al., 2005; Thaler, 1981), we replicated both effects, as we found significantly lower discount rates in the large reward magnitude than in the small reward magnitude and in the date‐framed condition relative to the delay‐framed condition. Given the replication of these effects, as well as the replication and extension of findings from Grunevski et al. (2022), we believe this further establishes the validity of the degree of preference task for the study of delay discounting.

Our second goal was to explore ambivalence scores from this task, specifically the possibility that preference (for the immediate or delayed outcome) was associated with degree of ambivalence. By using the continuous scale in the present task rather than assuming their invariance using binary choices, this study allowed us to explore the role of ambivalence in intertemporal choice. This added granularity in decision process reveals systematic patterns of ambivalence that are not detectable using binary choice tasks. We found that discount rates were negatively correlated with average ambivalence scores across all date/delay and magnitude conditions, indicating that relative preference for the delayed option was associated with greater ambivalence.

It is possible that this result reflects an association between individual difference variables, where individuals with lower discount rates tend to be more ambivalent. While an interesting possibility on its own, we also evaluated within‐individual association by examining a possible difference in ambivalence when participants preferred the immediate outcome versus the delayed outcome, focusing our interest on trials in which the highest ambivalence scores were expected (and observed). Our comparison of ambivalence scores from L and R trials indicate that participants exhibit greater ambivalence on trials in which they chose the delayed option than when they chose the immediate option. This result, which expands on the findings of Grunevski et al. (2022), may provide an important theoretical insight on intertemporal decision making. Specifically, it suggests that heightened ambivalence when preferring a delayed reward may serve as a mechanism underlying one of the most well‐documented challenges of self‐control: preference reversals.

Preference reversals (Green et al., 1994; Rachlin & Green, 1972) refer to switches from preference for a larger later reward over a smaller sooner reward when both rewards are delayed to preference for the smaller sooner reward as time passes and the smaller sooner reward becomes immediate or nearly immediate. Although these reversals are predicted by nonexponential mathematical discounting models (Yi et al., 2009), the fact that people are more ambivalent when choosing delayed rewards may explain a psychological mechanism underlying these reversals. Specifically, if ambivalence is a proxy for the degree to which preference can be “persuaded” toward an alternative, greater ambivalence when choosing a delayed reward suggests that preference for the delayed reward may be inherently less stable and a change in preference is more likely than a preference for the immediate reward. Our results suggest that vulnerability to analogs of preference reversals in the real world, such as relapse to drug use, may reflect greater ambivalence about the delayed outcome (e.g., drug abstinence) even when it is preferred to the immediate outcome. Individuals may strongly value long‐term health or stability yet still experience greater conflict when engaging in behavior consistent with this preference, increasing the likelihood of reversal in the presence of immediate alternatives (e.g., drug use). This interpretation aligns with clinical evidence that proxies for low ambivalence (higher commitment and engagement) are predictors of treatment success for conditions like substance use disorders (Groshkova, 2010).

While the current study provides a novel approach to studying delay discounting, we note some limitations. First, we note that the sliding scale always oriented the immediate and delayed rewards to the left and right, respectively. This does introduce the possibility that side bias could have influenced both indicated preference as well as the degree of preference. That said, it should also be noted that a fixed relative positioning of the options in a delay discounting task is common and that the positioning of the indicator at the start of each trial required a centering response—that is, the pointer needed to be moved to the centered indicator to move it. Second, we acknowledge the limitation of our recruitment methods. Crowdsourcing through MTurk does introduce a number of challenges, including a nonrepresentative sample and the possibility of poor data quality. On the issue of data quality, one attention check may have been insufficient to ensure attention throughout the assessment of delay discounting. We believe that the exclusion of data from participants who failed the attention check as well as those who exhibited no variability in their responses, partially addresses reasonable concerns about data quality, although we acknowledge some concerns may remain. A related concern is the positively skewed distribution of ambivalence scores. Even with our effort to normalize the distribution (adding a constant and log10 transformation), a high frequency of zeros did not fully allow for distribution normalization. While not specifically an indicator of poor data quality, it does result in a technical violation of the normality assumption of our comparison of ambivalence scores. This does not necessarily invalidate the results of the analyses, but it is nonetheless a caveat. Another limitation of the present study is that response latency was not recorded. Prior work has suggested that response time may serve as an additional indicator of ambivalence in intertemporal choice (e.g., Basile & Toplak, 2015). Our previous work has examined multiple direct measures of ambivalence, including the slider‐based measure used here, and demonstrated consistent patterns, with ambivalence peaking near the preference switch point (Grunevski et al., 2021). Future studies would benefit from examining the correspondence between ambivalence scores and response latency to clarify how these measures capture uncertainty in delay discounting.

Despite these limitations, we believe the proposed degree of preference task offers potential in the future study of delay discounting using a fixed‐item paradigm like the MCQ as well as titrating delay discounting tasks implementing adjusting‐delay and adjusting‐amount procedures. Incorporating a measure of ambivalence into adjusting tasks could allow the task to converge on indifference more efficiently by adjusting values based on the degree of preference such that lower ambivalence leads to larger adjustments. Related methodological innovations could include complementary measures of ambivalence including response latency (e.g., Basile & Toplak, 2015) and tracking of pointer trajectory (Schneider et al., 2015).

We are interested in the prospect of examining whether degree of ambivalence is an indicator of “persuadability.” Individual differences in ambivalence may signal who is likely to be influenced by interventions targeting a change in rate of delay discounting as well as facilitating identification of which interventions are likely to affect which individuals. Within individuals, ambivalence might indicate the opportunities and constraints on whether certain choices by an individual can be changed; is there a “window of persuasion” within which an intervention might capitalize on high ambivalence to change behavior/preference or the intervention must reduce ambivalence so that behavior/preference is less vulnerable to a change? While these questions are yet unanswered, we believe that the exploration of these possibilities is a worthwhile pursuit, and our effort in this domain is informed and inspired by Warren Bickel and his enormous contributions to the application of basic behavioral science to clinical outcomes.
