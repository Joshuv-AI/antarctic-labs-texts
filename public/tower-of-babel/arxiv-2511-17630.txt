Can we use LLMs to bootstrap reinforcement learning? - A case study in digital health behavior change
Nele Albers, Esra Cemre Su de Groot, Loes Keijsers, Manon H. Hillegers, Emiel Krahmer

Abstract
Personalizing digital applications for health behavior change is a promising route to making them more engaging and effective. This especially holds for approaches that adapt to users and their specific states (e.g., motivation, knowledge, wants) over time. However, developing such approaches requires making many design choices, whose effectiveness is difficult to predict from literature and costly to evaluate in practice. In this work, we explore whether large language models (LLMs) can be used out-of-the-box to generate samples of user interactions that provide useful information for training reinforcement learning models for digital behavior change settings. Using real user data from four large behavior change studies as comparison, we show that LLM-generated samples can be useful in the absence of real data. Comparisons to the samples provided by human raters further show that LLM-generated samples reach the performance of human raters. Additional analyses of different prompting strategies including shorter and longer prompt variants, chain-of-thought prompting, and few-shot prompting show that the relative effectiveness of different strategies depends on both the study and the LLM with also relatively large differences between prompt paraphrases alone. We provide recommendations for how LLM-generated samples can be useful in practice.

Introduction

While effective for changing various health behaviors including ones related to lifestyle
Singh et al. 2024
and mental well-being
Groot et al. 2023
, digital health applications likely work best when they can be personalized and adapt to the individual wishes and needs of users whenever they arise
Kocaballi et al. 2019
;
Singh et al. 2024
. Technically, this kind of personalized interactions can be achieved using reinforcement learning (RL)
Sutton and Barto 2018
, a machine learning approach where an intelligent agent learns to make decisions by taking actions, observing the impact these actions have on users, and adjusting its behavior accordingly. Crucially, this method relies on data on users, in the form of rewards and transitions between user states, to determine the impact actions have. But what if no human user data is available? In this paper we explore whether large language models (LLMs) can be used to generate useful interaction samples for digital health behavior change settings.

LLMs have recently shown promise in simulating patients in conversations with mental health trainees
Wang et al. 2024
, nursing students
Agatsuma et al. 2024
, and medical students
Brügge et al. 2024
. This means that even though LLMs are trained to predict the next word for a given text, they have the potential to generate cognition and behavior simulating those of humans. While some approaches explicitly integrate cognitive models with LLMs (e.g.,
Wang et al. 2024
), others use relatively simple prompts (e.g.,
Agatsuma et al. 2024
;
Brügge et al. 2024
). The latter highlights the potential of LLMs to generate interaction samples more or less out of the box, without the need to carefully craft simulations based on data from related studies (e.g.,
Li et al. 2024
;
Trella et al. 2024
;
Coughlin et al. 2024
) or try to derive expert- or theory-based rules which are commonly not available at a sufficiently detailed level
Klasnja et al. 2015
.

To examine whether it is beneficial to use out-of-the-box LLMs to simulate the behavior of users in digital behavior change applications, we compare the interaction samples generated with LLMs to those collected in four previous behavior change studies. In these former studies, an RL agent chose different persuasive strategies for quitting smoking
Albers et al. 2023
, proposed different activities for quitting smoking
Albers et al. 2025
and mental well-being
de Groot et al. 2025
, and decided whether to add human feedback when preparing for quitting smoking
Albers et al. 2025
. For each of these studies, we assess how useful LLM-generated samples would be for two use cases. In the first use case, we want to learn effective actions to take in each state (i.e., a policy) that we can use as a warm start for a new application. So we determine how well an LLM-based optimal policy performs compared to one learned from the real behavioral data. In the second use case, we want to use the generated samples to gain insights into the rewards and state transitions of different actions. This could, for example, help to make design choices regarding the actions to use. Hence, we investigate how similar the LLM-based reward and transition functions are to those estimated from the four former studies’ real behavioral samples.

We perform an extensive evaluation, including five different LLMs, a shorter and a more elaborate prompt version, ten paraphrases per prompt version, and an exploration of chain-of-thought prompting, few-shot prompting, and changing the model temperature. Our findings show that LLM-generated samples can lead to policies and reward and transition functions that outperform baselines. Asking human raters to predict rewards and next states (Fig.
1
) further shows that LLM-generated samples reach the performance of human-generated samples. The gap remaining between both LLM- and human-generated samples on the one hand and real behavioral samples on the other hand, however, highlights the difficulty in accurately predicting human behavior without real behavioral data. Moreover, we find based on an analysis of shorter and longer prompt versions, chain-of-thought prompting, and few-shot prompting that the relative effectiveness of these prompting strategies largely depends on the study, LLM, and use case. The only exception is few-shot prompting, which generally leads to improvements for at least one of the use cases. We provide recommendations for how LLM-generated samples can be useful in practice.

Related work

Tailoring digital health applications for behavior change

While meta-analyses show that digital health applications are effective for areas such as quitting smoking
Fang et al. 2023
;
Li et al. 2024
, increasing mental well-being
Groot et al. 2023
, and reducing physical inactivity and unhealthy eating
Singh et al. 2024
, users commonly do not adhere to these applications over a longer period of time or stop using them entirely after a while
Kelders et al. 2020
;
Jabir et al. 2024
;
Shams et al. 2023
. Instead of developing one-size-fits-all interventions, tailoring interventions to the distinct needs of specific (groups of) users could potentially improve adherence and reduce dropout
Singh et al. 2024
. As such, there are a variety of approaches to adapt
what
the applications offer,
when
,
how
, and
with whom
. For example,
Dierikx et al. 2024
and
Gasparetti et al. 2020
adapted daily step goals,
Wang et al. 2021
and
Trella et al. 2023
tailored the timing of notifications for physical activity and oral self-care,
Bertolotti et al. 2019
and
Di Massimo et al. 2019
chose differently framed messages for healthy eating, and
Piette et al. 2023
and
Forman et al. 2023
learned when to add human support in digital applications for reducing opioid analgesic misuse risk and supporting weight loss. Group-level tailoring is thereby achieved by accounting for dynamic factors or states (e.g., available time, sleep quality, motivation, self-efficacy, and mood
Dierikx et al. 2024
) as well as more stable user characteristics (e.g., personality
Halko and Kientz 2010
, culture
Oyibo et al. 2018
, motivation type
Alqahtani et al. 2023
, stage of behavior change
Oyebode and Orji 2023
). Tailoring to individuals, either alone (e.g.,
Kaptein et al. 2015
) or combined with group-level tailoring (e.g.,
Tomkins et al. 2021
), is also common.

Computational methods for tailoring to users

Various computational methods have been used to tailor elements of digital health applications to users, including classification-based methods and algorithms comparing user behavior to norms and guidelines
Iris ten Klooster et al. 2024
. For example,
Hors-Fraile et al. 2019
used a recommender system to choose motivational messages for quitting smoking,
Albers et al. 2023
selected testimonials for becoming more physically active based on a linear regression model, and
Ranjbartabar et al. 2021
chose relational cues for a conversational agent based on users’ preferences for examples provided before the interaction. An especially promising approach is reinforcement learning (RL) due to its ability to 1) learn which intervention options are optimal by trying them out, a process called exploration, 2) account for current user states when choosing intervention options as well as the effects of the latter on future states and thus the effectiveness of future intervention options, and 3) adapt to changes in these effects arising in a live application as long as some level of exploration remains. As such, RL has been applied to domains such as oral self-care (e.g.,
Trella et al. 2025
), physical activity coaching (e.g.,
Wang et al. 2021
), and smoking cessation (e.g.,
Albers et al. 2022
), with initial results highlighting the potential of these algorithms to make behavior change support more effective.

Designing reinforcement algorithms for health behavior change

There is, however, not “one” RL algorithm. For example, while
Albers et al. 2022
choose a
persuasive strategy
that maximizes the
engagement
with the intervention
every few days
,
Wang et al. 2021
determine
multiple times per day
whether to send a running
notification
with the goal of maximizing a person’s
behavior change
. More generally, designers of RL algorithms need to make a variety of design choices
Trella et al. 2022
. This includes the decision times (e.g., send a message every day vs. every hour), the variables to include in the state description, the possible intervention options, the reward to be maximized, and the algorithm itself (e.g., whether to account only for current or also for future user states). Although behavior change theories and domain experts can provide guidance in making some of these choices (e.g.
Potter et al. 2023
), they often cannot specify human behavior dynamics at a level of detail that is granular enough to predict the long-term effects of similar alternatives
Klasnja et al. 2015
. Therefore, since testing many algorithm variants with real users is both costly and undesirable due to the real, negative consequences that sub-optimal intervention options can have for people, simulations are often used to evaluate different algorithm variants
Weimann and Gißke 2024
;
Greene et al. 2023
. However, creating such simulations at a sufficient level of detail requires data on related human behavior, either for creating the model (e.g.,
Wang et al. 2021
;
Li et al. 2024
;
Mintz et al. 2020
) or for validating a literature-based model (e.g.,
Jabeen et al. 2021
). Such data is often not publicly available
Weimann and Gißke 2024
.

LLMs taking the role of human participants

The alternative for both testing different algorithm variants and pretraining new algorithms before deployment that we explore in this paper is generating human interaction samples with LLMs. Previous studies have provided promising results for using LLMs to take the role of human participants in various settings. For example,
Bavaresco et al. 2025
show that LLMs can, in some cases, be adequate annotators for NLP tasks, which could speed up the (initial) evaluation of NLP models even if the LLMs should first be validated against human annotations for a particular task. Similarly,
Hämäläinen et al. 2023
show that LLMs can generate believable descriptions about experiencing video games as art, which could be useful for piloting new studies. And
Brügge et al. 2024
use LLMs to simulate patients for medical students’ training, giving medical students the opportunity to easily and cheaply prepare for medical conversations with a large number and a variety of case scenarios. There are inherent limitations to applying LLMs in these settings. For example, LLM-generated responses might contain factual errors or show less diversity than actual human responses
Hämäläinen et al. 2023
. They might also be less representative of people with a lower socioeconomic position or racial minorities
Kim and Lee 2024
or have gender biases (e.g.,
Bajaj et al. 2024
). However, the goal in each of these cases is not to entirely replace human participants, but to make better use of their time by identifying more promising NLP models, study questions, or conversation techniques in advance. These more promising variants can then be validated with real behavioral data as a next step. Examining how effective LLMs are for generating interaction samples to gain initial insights on RL models for digital behavior change applications is the goal of this paper.

To summarize, research has shown that while RL is a promising approach to create digital applications for behavior change that are effectively tailored to users and their states, it is in practice difficult to make all required design choices in an informed way when no human data is available. Since previous studies indicate that LLMs can potentially take the role of human participants, we investigate in this paper whether LLMs can also generate samples of humans interacting with digital applications for behavior change that are useful for designing RL approaches.

Experimental setup

To examine whether LLMs can generate useful samples for digital health behavior change settings, we take an offline RL approach on four different behavior change studies (Fig.
2
). Specifically, we ask LLMs to imagine a user in a state
s_{t}
receiving an action
a_{t}
and predict for the user the reward
r_{t}
and next state
s_{t+1}
after receiving
a_{t}
. Let’s take the behavior change study on giving human feedback illustrated in Fig.
1
as example. A user’s state is described by three state features that could influence whether human feedback is effective in the sense that it increases people’s effort spent on smoking cessation activities. This includes how important preparing to quit is to the user, how confident they are to do it, and how much they would appreciate getting human feedback. So an LLM would, for example, be asked to imagine being a user with low perceived importance, high confidence and low feedback appreciation (
s_{t}
) who gets human feedback (
a_{t}
). For this user, the LLM would then predict the effort spent on a smoking cessation activity (
r_{t}
) and the next values for the three state features (
s_{t+1}
).

Figure 2
:
Overview of our offline RL approach applied to four different behavior change studies. Data collection: In the absence of real behavioral data, data is collected by prompting an LLM to imagine a user in state
s_{t}
receiving action
a_{t}
. Training: The data is used to train an RL agent. Deployment: The trained agent is tested in a simulation of real users created using previously collected real behavioral data.

All generated samples of the form
\langle s_{t},r_{t},a_{t},s_{t+1}\rangle
are used to learn a policy for an RL agent that describes which action to optimally take in which state. For the first use case, this policy is then evaluated in a simulation of real users that is created using real behavioral data from the behavior change study. For the second use case, we also look specifically at the reward and transition functions estimated from the generated samples in the process of learning a policy. These reward and transition functions, together also referred to as dynamics, describe the rewards and state transitions for all state-action combinations. We repeat this process for different settings: 1) using chain-of-thought prompting, 2) changing the temperature of the LLM, and 3) using few-shot prompting. Moreover, we compare against samples generated by asking human raters to make predictions analogous to the ones made by LLMs.

Models

We evaluated five diverse open-weight models: Llama-3.2-3B-Instruct, Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct, Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct
Meta AI 2024
, Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.3
Mistral AI 2023

Dietrich and Hollstein 2025
). Since our goal was to test an out-of-the-box approach to generating samples, we used these models with a seed for reproducibility and otherwise largely the default parameter settings (Table
A1

Prompts

For each behavior change study, we wrote a prompt that describes the study from the perspective of users, consisting of a description of 1) the study, 2) the user’s current state, 3) the chosen action, and 4) a question about either the reward or the next state, together with output formatting instructions (Fig.
3
). We created separate prompts for the reward and next state due to LLM output formatting difficulties when asking for both together. The Appendix shows the prompt templates for both the reward and the next state for all studies.

Figure 3
:
Example prompt for the reward for study 3 on human feedback for quitting smoking. The part in italics is only shown for the extensive prompt version.

Since small changes to the prompt can lead to drastic changes in model outputs
Mizrahi et al. 2024
;
Sclar et al. 2024
, we performed all our experiments with 10 prompt variants. We created these by asking ChatGPT to rephrase the initial prompt while keeping the same meaning and subsequently correcting any inaccuracies introduced in this process (e.g., changed state variable names). Moreover, for studies 1–3, we created an extensive prompt version by providing more information on the content users received from the behavior change application (e.g., the full text of the preparatory activity assigned to the user or of the persuasive message sent to the user) if this content would be known before conducting a study. Note that we could not provide the full text of human feedback messages in the extensive prompts for study 3, because these messages would not be available in advance. For study 4, due to the very short text of the challenges, we did not vary the prompt length. Furthermore, to explore the effect of chain-of-thought prompting
Wei et al. 2022
, we also created a chain-of-thought version of the prompts (Fig.
3
), again in 10 variations. We obtained these chain-of-thought prompts by asking ChatGPT to turn the base prompt into a chain-of-thought prompt and correcting the resulting prompt for introduced errors. Lastly, to examine the effect of few-shot prompting
Brown et al. 2020
, we added between two and ten randomly chosen real behavioral samples with the same action as the one we were currently generating samples for (Fig.
3
).

Human-generated samples: Asking human raters to generate samples

If we find a difference in performance between the LLM-generated and the real behavioral samples, we do not know if this difference is due to LLMs being an unsuitable approach for predicting behavior or simply due to an inherent gap between predicting and actually performing a behavior. To better understand how good LLMs are at predicting behavior, we hence also asked human raters to generate samples. Specifically, we asked people to provide reward and next state predictions for 32 state-action combinations from one of the four studies (Fig.
A9
in the Appendix). We provided the information contained in the base prompt for studies 1–3 and the single prompt length we have for study 4. Participants were recruited via convenience sampling as well as through the student participant pool at the Tilburg School of Humanities and Digital Sciences and allocated to the four studies such that studies with more actions had a proportionally higher chance of being chosen. All participants had to give digital informed consent before providing predictions. Participants were included as long as they passed at least one attention check question, and data from participants who failed more than one attention check question was removed. Overall, we obtained between about 76 (study 2, 14 actions) and 123 (study 3, 2 actions) samples per action for the four studies. The study was reviewed and approved by the Research Ethics and Data Management Committee of the Tilburg School of Humanities and Digital Sciences, belonging to Tilburg University, with code REDC 2025.37. Before data collection, the study was preregistered in the Open Science Framework (OSF)
Albers and Krahmer 2025
. Analogously to LLM-generated samples, we call the resulting samples human-generated samples. This is not to be confused with the real behavioral samples, which, while also stemming from humans, are not generated in the same sense.

How good are the learned reward and transition functions?

Setup

In the second use case, we want to use the generated samples to gain insights into the rewards and state transitions (i.e., dynamics) of different actions. Therefore, we computed how close the dynamics estimated based on the LLM samples are to the dynamics estimated from the real behavioral data. For this, we computed mean absolute errors (i.e.,
L_{1}
-errors) between the real and generated reward and transition functions for different numbers of samples per action. To contextualize the performance, we computed the human baseline as well as simple data-independent baselines. The latter are assuming that people spend the mean reward for each state-action combination for the reward, and assuming that all transitions are equally likely or that people stay in their state for the transitions. Moreover, we show the results of drawing the same number of samples from the real behavioral samples (i.e., from an oracle). We provide means and 95% credible intervals based on the 10 prompt variants for the LLM-generated samples and based on 10 random draws for the real behavioral samples.

Setup

We again show means and 95% credible intervals based on the 10 prompt variants for the generated samples and 10 random draws for the real behavioral samples. Does few-shot prompting help?

Setup

To see whether few-shot prompting leads to better performing policies and more accurately estimated dynamics, we repeated our analyses one more time with between two and ten real behavioral samples added to the prompt for Llama-3.3-70B. These real samples were randomly drawn from all real samples with the same action that we were currently asking the LLM to make predictions for. Since we did not have ethical approval to transfer the real behavioral samples from study 4 to the server where the LLMs were run, we focused solely on the first three behavior change studies.

Discussion

Based on generating samples with five different open-weight LLMs for four different behavior change studies, we find that LLM-generated samples make it possible to learn policies that outperform worst and random baselines in human data-based simulations. Moreover, reward and transition functions estimated from the generated samples are generally closer to those estimated from the real behavioral samples than heuristics such as assuming people stay in their state or that all transitions are equally likely. This suggests that LLMs can be a useful tool for generating interaction samples for digital health behavior change settings.

Effect of different prompting strategies

We find that chain-of-thought prompting neither consistently improves nor deteriorates the LLM-generated samples. One possible reason for this is that chain-of-thought prompting might be less effective for our models, in line with
Bajaj et al. 2024
finding that chain-of-thought prompting helped only for the smaller and older models they tested. Moreover,
Song et al. 2025
saw for multimodal LLMs that chain-of-thought prompting helped for some tasks such as ordering tasks but not others such as event relation inference. It could hence be that step-by-step reasoning is not necessarily helpful when it comes to predicting human responses to behavior change support strategies. In light of the findings by
Liu et al. 2025
that step-by-step reasoning has sometimes similar negative effects for humans and LLMs, it would be interesting to see in the future whether the effects of step-by-step reasoning are also similar for humans and LLMs for our studies. Notably, our results are based on ten slightly different chain-of-thought prompts. It is thus possible that a single fine-tuned chain-of-thought prompt outperforms our original prompt. Our goal, however, was to see if a simple application of chain-of-thought prompting would be beneficial, as prompt fine-tuning is difficult in our envisioned setting where there is no real behavioral data. We find this not to be the case. Similarly, providing more information in the prompt or changing the temperature also has no consistent effect for our studies and models. Changing the temperature thereby has hardly any effect at all. This is good news for our envisioned setting where fine-tuning based on real behavioral data is difficult, as results should be relatively insensitive to the temperature setting. Lastly, providing between five and ten real examples per action tends to help for estimating the transition function, with mixed effects for the policy performance and reward estimation.

Comparing LLM and human rater predictions

Both the performance of policies and accuracy of dynamics based on LLM-generated samples reach those of human raters who were asked to make predictions for people described in scenarios, suggesting that remaining differences between generated and real behavioral samples are due to a gap between envisioning and actually doing behavior rather than a limitation specific to using LLMs rather than human raters. Humans are not necessarily good at predicting their behavior due to biases such as the optimism bias, according to which people tend to be overly optimistic about themselves and their future
Weinstein 1980
. And we even went one step further by asking people to predict the behaviors of
others
described in scenarios. Even though such scenario-based predicting of human behavior is often used in behavior change settings as an initial low-cost and low-risk evaluation step (e.g.,
de Vries et al. 2016
;
Ghantasala et al. 2023
;
Tielman et al. 2019
), it is evident from our observed gap between human-predicted and real behavioral samples that this is not an easy task for humans. Based on previous work finding that LLMs mirror certain human cognitive biases (e.g., temporal biases
Song et al. 2025
or heuristics for syllogistic inference
Bertolazzi et al. 2024
), it would be interesting to investigate if this is also the case for our studies. Anecdotally, some human raters remarked after their participation that to better predict human behavior, they would need to know more about the people in the scenario. While one would not always know the exact target population in our envisioned setting, future work could examine whether adding user characteristics such as age or occupation sampled from a plausible range improves both LLM and human prediction performance. If such user characteristics should also be part of the RL algorithm, however, care needs to be taken of potential biases in LLM responses based on factors such as socioeconomic position (e.g.,
Kim and Lee 2024
), gender (e.g.,
Bajaj et al. 2024
), or culture (e.g.,
Naous et al. 2024
).

Relative performance of LLMs

Regarding the relative performance of the different models, no single LLM outperforms all other ones for all studies and metrics. Llama-3.3-70B is, however, usually one of the best-performing models. That no single LLM always performs best and the trend that larger Llama models tend to outperform their smaller counterparts is, for example, also what
Testoni and Calixto 2025
observed for their estimation of uncertainty for medical question answering. Part of the reason why it is difficult to judge which models are best in our case is also that while the mean metrics of models often differ, their 95% credible intervals based on different prompt variants overlap, thus not providing a clear indication that one model is better than another. This underlines the importance of evaluating LLMs with different prompt variants
Mizrahi et al. 2024
;
Sclar et al. 2024
. In our case, making slight changes to the prompt also led to sometimes drastic changes in the degree to which the models were following the required output format, making it necessary to add more logic for preprocessing the model outputs. This was done to avoid that observed differences are primarily due to different output formatting abilities rather than actual differences in the prediction of human responses. In case one is also interested in the former, the additional preprocessing could be removed.
Song et al. 2025
, for example, saw that one of the reasons for unsatisfactory performance of their multimodal LLMs in temporal grounding and reasoning tasks is poor output formatting.

Practical implications

We set out this paper with the idea that in the absence of real behavioral data, generating interaction samples with LLMs could be useful for building RL models. Either to train a policy that can be used as warm start for a new application (use case 1), or to gain insights into the actual rewards and transitions that can, for example, be used to make design choices such as which actions to use (use case 2). Our findings show that LLMs can be a useful tool for generating samples in the absence of any real behavioral data. If a few real samples are available (e.g., five or ten per action), it seems to generally be useful to incorporate these into the prompt when aiming to estimate the transition function. And once between 40 and 200 real samples per action are available, these real samples clearly outperform any number of LLM-generated samples. Since we do not find consistent effects of providing more or less study information, providing more information beyond a minimum does not seem to be necessary, which is helpful if not all study details are known yet. Slight prompt paraphrases do, however, lead to different reward and transition functions, suggesting that using more than one might help to not get extreme results.

Regarding model choice, Llama-3.3-70B generally performed best across studies and use cases. While it is possible that other LLMs, for example reasoning-based ones (e.g.,
Muennighoff et al. 2025
;
Guo et al. 2025
), perform better than the five LLMs we tested in our proof-of-concept study, it is unlikely that the improvements are large. This is because our best LLMs tend to already reach the performance of human raters. Besides performance, a model choice should also take the costs of using LLMs into account. This includes the actual price of the required resources researchers or their institutes need to pay, but also externalized costs such as energy consumption, carbon emissions, and the use of clean water and minerals (e.g.,
Luccioni et al. 2023
;
Luccioni et al. 2024a
;
Luccioni et al. 2024b
), the effects of which tend to be disproportionally felt by already marginalized groups
Agyeman et al. 2003
. It could also be that traditional simulation approaches, while requiring domain knowledge and programming expertise to build, are ultimately cheaper when all costs are considered. LLMs have also been explored as a way to reduce the amount of expertise required to build such simulations
Khanshan et al. 2024
, which while increasing the environmental cost would likely still result in fewer required LLM inferences than full LLM-based simulations.

Final remarks

Overall, we show based on four diverse behavior change studies that an out-of-the-box approach to generating RL samples with LLMs can help to gain some insights into effective actions and the rewards and state transitions associated with them. Our answer to the effects of different prompting strategies is generally “it depends” - effects often differ between studies and use cases. Only few-shot prompting seems to offer a more consistent advantage, but also primarily for estimating state transitions. We see our findings as supporting the idea of using LLM-generated samples as a tool when designing a new RL algorithm and encourage future work on how the LLM-generated samples can be effectively combined with real behavioral samples when they ultimately become available. Moreover, since predicting the effects of different actions chosen in a certain context forms the basis of tailoring in general, our approach also holds promise for tailoring approaches for digital health behavior change other than reinforcement learning.
