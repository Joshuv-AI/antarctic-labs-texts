KAT6A inhibitor-loaded and anti-PD-L1-coated manganese metal–organic framework nanoplatform for enhanced colorectal cancer immunotherapy via cGAS-STING activation
Authors: Helei Wang, Chao Ji, Cheng Xie, Zongyang Li, Hongqiao Cai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractColorectal cancer immunotherapy remains limited by insufficient innate immune activation, poor tumor immune infiltration, and resistance to immune checkpoint blockade. Herein, we developed a KAT6A inhibitor-loaded and anti-PD-L1-coated manganese metal-organic framework (MnMOF) nanoplatform for enhanced colorectal cancer immunotherapy through coordinated cGAS-STING activation and PD-L1 blockade. The KAT6A inhibitor (Ki) PF-9363 was loaded into MnMOF, followed by surface modification with anti-PD-L1 antibody to obtain Ki@MnMOF@P. The nanoplatform exhibited efficient Ki loading, pH-responsive drug release, and retained PD-L1 blocking activity. In vitro, Ki@MnMOF@P showed enhanced cellular uptake and selective cytotoxicity against HCT116 cells while maintaining low toxicity toward normal colonic epithelial NCM460 cells. In a CT26 colorectal tumor-bearing mouse model, systemic administration of Ki@MnMOF@P significantly inhibited tumor growth, reduced tumor weight, and prolonged survival. Moreover, Ki@MnMOF@P achieved favorable tumor accumulation without obvious systemic toxicity. Mechanistically, Ki@MnMOF@P suppressed KAT6A expression, promoted cGAMP production, enhanced cGAS expression, and increased STING phosphorylation in tumor tissues. Besides, the nanoplatform remodeled the tumor immune microenvironment by elevating IFN-β, CXCL10, TNF-α, IL-6, and IFN-γ levels and increasing CD8+ and CD4+ T-cell infiltration. These findings demonstrate that Ki@MnMOF@P integrates KAT6A inhibitor and manganese-mediated cGAS-STING activation and anti-PD-L1-mediated checkpoint blockade, providing a promising nanomedicine strategy for colorectal cancer immunotherapy.Keywords: CRC, MOF, KAT6A inhibitor, cGAS-STING activation, Tumor immunotherapy

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) is one of the most prevalent and lethal malignancies worldwide (Steup et al., 2025). The high incidence, recurrence risk, and metastatic potential of CRC impose a substantial clinical burden (Yu et al., 2025). Surgery, chemotherapy, radiotherapy, targeted therapy, and immune checkpoint blockade have improved patient outcomes (Yin et al., 2025). However, the therapeutic efficacy of immunotherapy in CRC is highly dependent on tumor immune phenotype (Zhang et al., 2025a). Patients with high microsatellite instability and mismatch repair-deficient CRC can benefit from immune checkpoint inhibitors, whereas the majority typically exhibit limited responses to PD-L1 blockade (Ying et al., 2026; Xu et al., 2025; Fumet et al., 2026). This poor responsiveness is closely associated with weak innate immune activation, limited cytotoxic T-cell infiltration, and an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment (Chu et al., 2025). Therefore, the development of therapeutic strategies capable of converting immunologically “cold” CRC tumors into “hot” tumors is urgently needed (Liu et al., 2026). The cyclic GMP-AMP synthase-stimulator of interferon genes (cGAS-STING) axis plays a central role in initiating type I interferon-mediated immune responses (Hooftman et al., 2026; Shim et al., 2025). Activation of cGAS-STING signaling can increase the secretion of immune-stimulatory cytokines and facilitate cytotoxic T lymphocyte infiltration, thereby bridging innate and adaptive antitumor immunity (Wang et al., 2025). Since direct drug delivery remains challenging because of the pharmacokinetic properties and limited tumor accumulation, nanoplatforms that can activate cGAS-STING signaling in a tumor-preferential manner provide an attractive strategy for CRC immunotherapy (Liu et al., 2023).

Manganese-based nanomaterials have recently attracted increasing attention as immunomodulatory agents, because Mn2+ can amplify cGAS-STING activation and enhance type I interferon responses (Zhang et al., 2025b; Yang et al., 2024; Zhong et al., 2025). In particular, manganese metal-organic framework (MnMOF) possesses several advantages, including high drug-loading capacity, structural flexibility, and tumor microenvironment-responsive degradation (Zheng et al., 2023). MnMOF can serve not only as drug carriers but also as bioactive immunostimulatory components by releasing Mn2+ within tumor cells. This dual function makes MnMOF promising for integrating drug delivery, innate immune activation, and immune microenvironment remodeling. Epigenetic regulation also plays an important role in tumor immune evasion. Lysine acetyltransferase 6 A (KAT6A), a member of the MYST family of histone acetyltransferases, has been implicated in tumor progression (Sharma et al., 2023; Liu et al., 2024). Recent evidence indicates that inhibition of KAT6A can activate interferon-related immune responses and enhance antitumor immunotherapy in CRC, partly through activation of the cGAS-STING pathway (Han et al., 2025). Therefore, combining KAT6A inhibition with Mn2+-mediated cGAS-STING activation may provide a rational strategy to enhance immunotherapy in CRC.

Programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) is an important immune checkpoint molecule expressed on the surface of tumor cells (Lin et al., 2023). In CRC, tumor-associated PD-L1 can bind to programmed death-1 (PD-1) on activated T cells, thereby suppressing T-cell-mediated cytotoxicity and facilitating tumor immune escape (Tong et al., 2024). Blockade of the PD-1/PD-L1 axis remains critical for restoring T-cell-mediated tumor killing (Guan et al., 2024). Anti-PD-L1 antibodies can relieve PD-L1-mediated immune suppression and enhance cytotoxic T-cell activity (Huseni et al., 2023). However, free antibodies and small-molecule immunomodulators often suffer from insufficient tumor accumulation and limited coordination between immune priming and checkpoint blockade. Surface coating of nanoplatforms with anti-PD-L1 offers a potential means to improve the spatial and temporal coordination of different immunotherapeutic mechanisms (Qin et al., 2024). Although immune checkpoint blockade has improved cancer therapy, its efficacy in most CRC patients remains limited by weak innate immune activation, insufficient cytotoxic T-cell infiltration, and an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment. Previous strategies using immune checkpoint inhibitors, epigenetic modulators, or STING-activating nanomaterials individually have shown potential, but their therapeutic efficacy is often restricted by poor tumor accumulation, insufficient immune priming, or lack of coordination between innate immune activation and checkpoint blockade. Therefore, integrating multiple complementary immunomodulatory mechanisms into a single tumor-targeted nanoplatform may provide a more effective approach for converting immunologically ‘cold’ CRC tumors into ‘hot’ tumors. Herein, we developed a KAT6A inhibitor (Ki)-loaded and anti-PD-L1-coated manganese MOF nanoplatform (Ki@MnMOF@P) for enhanced CRC immunotherapy via cGAS-STING activation (Fig. 1). In this system, the MnMOF was designed to act as both a drug carrier and a Mn2+-releasing immunostimulatory component, while the encapsulated KAT6A inhibitor was used to amplify interferon-related immune activation. Anti-PD-L1 coating was introduced to facilitate interaction with PD-L1-positive tumor-associated cells and provide immune checkpoint blockade. This integrated nanoplatform could promote cGAS-STING signaling, cytotoxic T-cell infiltration, and ultimately improve antitumor efficacy against CRC. This study provides a multifunctional nanomedicine strategy for combining manganese-mediated and KAT6A inhibitor- mediated cGAS-STING activation, and PD-L1 blockade in CRC immunotherapy.

Materials and Methods

Synthesis of MnMOF

The MOF was synthesized by a hydrothermal method (Zhu et al., 2026). The hydrothermal method was selected for MnMOF synthesis because it provides a controlled reaction environment that promotes coordination between Mn2+ ions and carboxylate ligands. This method could lead to the formation of a stable and crystalline MOF framework. This method also facilitates the generation of nanoscale particles with porous structures, which are beneficial for subsequent Ki loading and pH-responsive release. Briefly, (CH₃COO)₂Mn·4H₂O (0.47 g) were separately dissolved in 30 mL of deionized water, while H₃BTC (1.26 g) was dissolved in 30 mL of absolute ethanol. The solutions were mixed and transferred into a Teflon-lined autoclave, followed by reaction at 120 °C for 12 h. After cooling to room temperature, the product was collected, washed three times with deionized water and ethanol, and dried at 100 °C for 12 h.

Synthesis of Ki@MnMOF@P

The MnMOF was dispersed in the KAT6A inhibitor PF-9363 (HY-132283, MedChemExpress, China) solution, and stirred at room temperature for 24 h, and the Ki@MnMOF is obtained (Chen et al., 2026). For antibody functionalization, the carboxyl groups of anti-PD-L1 antibody (BE0383, Bio X Cell, NH, USA) were activated using 1-ethyl-3-(3-dimethylaminopropyl)carbodiimide hydrochloride (EDC, 1 mM) and N-hydroxysuccinimide (NHS, 1 mM) in MES buffer for 30 min at room temperature. Subsequently, amine-terminated thiolated polyethylene glycol (SH-PEG-NH₂, molecular weight 5000 Da) was added, and the reaction was allowed to proceed to form SH-PEG–anti-PD-L1 through amide-bond formation (Liu et al., 2025a). After incubation with Ki@MnMOF for 12 h at 4 °C, the final Ki@MnMOF@P was obtained.

Characterization of nanoparticles

The morphology of Ki@MnMOF@P was observed by transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and scanning electron microscopy (SEM). Hydrodynamic diameter, polydispersity index (PDI), and zeta potential were measured using dynamic light scattering and electrophoretic light scattering. Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) was performed to identify characteristic functional groups and confirm the chemical structure of the nanoparticles. X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis was used to evaluate the crystalline structure of MnMOF-based nanoparticles. To assess colloidal stability, Ki@MnMOF@P was dispersed in PBS, cell culture medium, or serum-containing medium, and PDI changes were monitored over time.

Evaluation of anti-PD-L1 blocking activity

The PD-1/PD-L1 blocking activity of free anti-PD-L1 antibody and Ki@MnMOF@P was evaluated using a commercial PD-1/PD-L1 binding inhibition assay. PBS and Ki@MnMOF were used as negative controls. The inhibition rate was calculated based on the absorbance relative to the untreated control.

Determination of drug loading and encapsulation efficiency

Drug loading was evaluated with different initial concentrations of Ki solution. After loading, the nanoparticles were separated by centrifugation, and the amount of unloaded Ki in the supernatant was measured by HPLC. Encapsulation efficiency and loading capacity were calculated using the following equations: Encapsulation efficiency (%) = [(total Ki - free Ki) / total Ki] × 100%; Loading capacity (%) = [(loaded Ki) / weight of Ki-loaded nanoparticles] × 100%.

In vitro Ki release assay

The release behavior of Ki from Ki@MnMOF and Ki@MnMOF@P was evaluated with different pH values, including physiological pH 7.4, mildly acidic pH 6.5, and acidic pH 5.5. The released Ki concentration was measured by HPLC. Cumulative release was calculated and plotted as a function of time.

Cell culture

Human colorectal cancer HCT116 cells and normal human colonic epithelial NCM460 cells were obtained from American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, Manassas, USA). Cells were cultured in RPMI-1640 supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin–streptomycin. All cells were maintained at 37 °C in a humidified incubator containing 5% CO2.

In vitro cytotoxicity assay

The cytotoxicity of different formulations was evaluated in NCM460 and HCT116 cells. Cells were seeded into 96-well plates and cultured overnight. The medium was then replaced with fresh medium containing PBS, MnMOF, Ki, Ki@MnMOF, or Ki@MnMOF@P at different concentrations. After incubation for 24 h, cell viability was measured using a standard CCK-8 assay. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader, and cell viability was calculated relative to the PBS-treated control group. A dose–response curve was plotted, and the half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC₅₀) was calculated using nonlinear regression analysis. Based on the curve, 2 nM was selected as the subsequent concentration.

Live/dead staining

HCT116 cells were seeded and treated with PBS, MnMOF@P, Ki, Ki@MnMOF, or Ki@MnMOF@P. After incubation, the cells were washed with PBS and co-stained with calcein-AM and propidium iodide (PI) for 30 min at 37 °C. Live cells and dead cells were visualized by fluorescence microscopy. The percentage of dead cells was quantified using Image J from randomly selected fields.

Cellular uptake assay

To evaluate cellular uptake, Ki@MnMOF and Ki@MnMOF@P were labeled with Nile red. NCM460 and HCT116 cells were incubated with Nile red-labeled nanoparticles for a predetermined period. After incubation, the cells were washed with PBS and observed using fluorescence microscopy. Fluorescence intensity was quantified using Image J.

Mn2+ quantification

NCM460 and HCT116 cells were incubated with Ki@MnMOF or Ki@MnMOF@P at different nanoparticle concentrations. After treatment, the cells were washed thoroughly with PBS, collected, and digested using nitric acid. The intracellular manganese content was quantified by inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectroscopy. The Mn2+ level was normalized to cell number.

Establishment of the CT26 tumor-bearing mouse model

The study was approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of Jilin University First Hospital, and complied with national ethical guidelines for laboratory animal care. CT26 colorectal cancer cells were collected during the logarithmic growth phase and suspended in sterile PBS. Cells were subcutaneously injected into the flank of mice (8-week-old female BALB/c mice purchased from Charles River company, China) to establish a CT26 colorectal tumor-bearing mouse model. When tumor volumes reached about 100 mm3, mice were randomly divided into five treatment groups: PBS, MnMOF (bare manganese MOF), MnMOF@P (MOF coated with anti-PD-L1), Ki@MnMOF (Ki-loaded MOF), and Ki@MnMOF@P (Ki-loaded MOF coated with anti-PD-L1). Ki was prepared (5 mg/kg), which was administered via tail vein injection at a volume of 100 μL, and the corresponding concentrations of each nanodrug formulation were adjusted to ensure the equivalent dose of delivered Ki. The treatments were administered via intravenous injection of tail vein. Tumor volume was measured every 2 days, and tumor size was calculated using the formula: Tumor volume = (length × width2) / 2. The body weight of the mice was monitored throughout the study.

Ex vivo fluorescence imaging

To evaluate tumor-targeting ability and biodistribution, Ki@MnMOF@P was labeled with Cy5.5 fluorescent dye and intravenously injected into CT26 tumor-bearing mice. After 24 h, mice were sacrificed, and tumors and major organs, including the heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney, were collected. Ex vivo fluorescence imaging was performed using an in vivo imaging system.

Biodistribution analysis and biosafety evaluation

For Ki biodistribution analysis, tissue samples were homogenized and analyzed by HPLC. Systemic biosafety was evaluated by monitoring body weight changes during treatment. At the end of the experiment, blood samples were collected for hematological and serum biochemical analyses. Liver function was assessed by measuring alanine aminotransferase, aspartate aminotransferase, and albumin levels. Renal function was evaluated by measuring blood urea nitrogen levels. Hematological parameters, including white blood cell and red blood cell counts, were also analyzed. Major organs, including the heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney, were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, embedded in paraffin, sectioned, and stained with hematoxylin and eosin for histopathological examination (Cai et al., 2025).

Immunofluorescence staining

Tumor sections were deparaffinized, rehydrated, subjected to antigen retrieval, blocked with serum, and incubated with primary antibodies against KAT6A, cGAS, phosphorylated STING, phosphorylated TBK1, and phosphorylated IRF3 at 4 °C overnight. After washing, sections were incubated with fluorescence-labeled secondary antibodies and counterstained with DAPI. Fluorescence images were obtained using a fluorescence microscope. Quantitative analysis was performed using ImageJ software.

Detection of cGAMP and immune-related cytokines

Tumor tissues were collected after treatment and homogenized in cold lysis buffer. The levels of cGAMP and immune-related cytokines were measured using commercial ELISA kits according to the manufacturers' protocols. IFN-β, CXCL10, TNF-α, IL-6, and IFN-γ levels were quantified to evaluate cGAS-STING activation and antitumor immune stimulation. Cytokine concentrations were normalized to tissue weight.

Analysis of tumor-infiltrating T cells

Tumor tissues were minced into small fragments and digested with collagenase-containing digestion buffer to obtain single-cell suspensions. After filtration and red blood cell lysis, cells were blocked and stained with anti-PE-CD3, anti-FITC-CD4, anti-APC-CD8, and anti-PC7-CD45 fluorophore-conjugated antibodies (1:200) for 30 min at 4 °C in the dark, washed with PBS, and analyzed on a flow cytometer. Data were analyzed using FlowJo. CD45+CD3+CD4+ and CD45+CD3+CD8+ T cell populations were gated based on control. Briefly, cell debris was first excluded based on forward scatter and side scatter parameters. Single cells were then gated using FSC-A versus FSC-H to remove cell doublets and aggregates. The leukocyte population was identified by gating CD45+ cells. Within the CD45+ population, T lymphocytes were further defined as CD3+ cells. Subsequently, CD4+ helper T cells and CD8+ cytotoxic T cells were identified from the CD45+CD3+ T-cell population as CD45+CD3+CD4+ and CD45+CD3+CD8+ cells, respectively.

Survival analysis

For survival studies, CT26 tumor-bearing mice were treated with PBS, MnMOF, MnMOF@P, Ki@MnMOF, or Ki@MnMOF@P according to the established dosing schedule for 60 days. Mice were monitored regularly, and survival time was recorded. Mice were euthanized when tumor volume exceeded the ethical limit or when humane endpoint criteria were met. Survival curves were generated using the Kaplan–Meier method.

Statistical analysis

All quantitative data are presented as mean ± standard deviation. Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism 8.0. Comparisons between two groups were analyzed using Student's t-test, while comparisons among multiple groups were performed using one-way analysis of variance followed by an appropriate post hoc test. Survival curves were compared using the log-rank test. A value of P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results and discussion

Preparation and characterization of Ki@MnMOF@P

The preparation process of the KAT6A inhibitor-loaded and anti-PD-L1-coated manganese MOF nanoplatform is illustrated in Fig. 2A. MnMOF was first synthesized through a hydrothermal method using manganese acetate and H3BTC as precursors. The KAT6A inhibitor PF-9363 (written as Ki) was subsequently loaded into the MnMOF framework to obtain Ki@MnMOF. To further introduce immune checkpoint blockade functionality, anti-PD-L1 antibody was coated onto the surface of Ki@MnMOF, yielding the final Ki@MnMOF@P nanoplatform. The morphology was examined by TEM and SEM. As shown in Fig. 2B and C, the nanoparticles exhibited a relatively uniform spherical morphology with a rough and porous surface structure. The average particle size of Ki@MnMOF@P was 104.1 ± 23.6 nm (Fig. 2D). The prepared nanoparticles were within the nanoscale range suitable for tumor accumulation and cellular uptake. The hydrodynamic diameters and surface charges of MnMOF, Ki@MnMOF, and Ki@MnMOF@P were further compared to verify the stepwise modification process. After drug loading and anti-PD-L1 coating, the hydrodynamic diameter showed a slight increase, further supporting the successful construction of Ki@MnMOF@P without obvious aggregation (Fig. 2E). The surface charge changes also confirmed the modification of the nanoplatform. As shown in Fig. 2F, MnMOF exhibited a positive zeta potential of approximately +3.5 mV. After Ki loading, the zeta potential slightly decreased to around +1.0 mV. Notably, after anti-PD-L1 coating, the zeta potential shifted to approximately −10.0 mV, which may be attributed to the introduction of PEGylated antibody molecules on the nanoparticle surface (Karimi et al., 2025). The chemical structure of the nanoparticles was further characterized by FTIR spectroscopy, with characteristic absorption bands corresponding to the organic ligand and functional groups of the MOF structure (Fig. 2G). The characteristic bands associated with the carboxylate groups and the Mn—O coordination structure, absorption features derived from Ki molecules, and PEGylated antibody components, confirming successful functionalization. The XRD pattern of Ki@MnMOF@P exhibited several distinct diffraction peaks, indicating the crystalline nature of the nanoplatform (Fig. 2H). The main diffraction peaks (6.9°, 9.8°, 12.3°, 14.3°, 18.8°, 20.6°, 22.0°, 28.5°, 32.3°) of Ki@MnMOF remained after Ki loading and anti-PD-L1 coating, confirming the structural stability of the nanoplatform after functionalization. XPS spectrum of Ki@MnMOF@P showed characteristic Mn, C, O, and N signals, confirming the presence of the manganese-based MOF framework and supporting the successful incorporation of organic components (Fig. S1). The anti-PD-L1 of Ki@MnMOF@P was measured to be 0.9 μg/mL by plotting a standard curve, and the anti-PD-L1 conjugation efficiency was calculated to be 90.0% (Fig. S2). To evaluate whether the anti-PD-L1 coating retained its biological activity after conjugation, the PD-1/PD-L1 blocking ability was assessed (Rui et al., 2023). As shown in Fig. 2I, PBS and Ki@MnMOF showed negligible inhibition, whereas free anti-PD-L1 antibody exhibited a high inhibition rate of approximately 90%. Importantly, Ki@MnMOF@P maintained a comparable inhibition rate, indicating that anti-PD-L1 retained its immune checkpoint blocking activity after being coated onto the nanoplatform. The colloidal stability of Ki@MnMOF@P was further investigated by monitoring the polydispersity index (PDI) in different physiological media. As shown in Fig. 2J, Ki@MnMOF@P maintained a relatively low PDI in PBS, culture medium, and serum-containing medium, with no significant increase in dispersion index. Ki@MnMOF@P maintained stable particle size and surface charge in these media (Fig. S3). These results indicated that the nanoplatform possessed good colloidal stability under physiological and biological conditions (Salehiabar et al., 2023). Taken together, these characterization results demonstrated the successful construction of Ki@MnMOF@P with uniform nanoscale morphology, appropriate particle size, stable dispersion, and retained PD-L1 blocking activity, providing a solid basis for subsequent cellular and antitumor immunotherapy studies.

Drug loading and selective cytotoxicity of Ki@MnMOF@P

The drug-loading behavior was evaluated using different concentrations of the KAT6A inhibitor Ki. BET results showed the reduced pore parameters after Ki loading, suggesting drugs were successfully incorporated into the internal pores of MnMOF (Fig. S4). As shown in Fig. 3A, the encapsulation efficiency gradually decreased as the initial Ki concentration increased from 1 to 5 mg/mL. In contrast, the loading capacity increased in a concentration-dependent manner, reaching approximately 55% at 5 mg/mL (Fig. 3B). These results indicated that the nanoplatform possessed favorable drug-loading ability and could effectively serve as a carrier for Ki delivery. The biocompatibility and tumor-selective cytotoxicity of the nanoplatform were then evaluated in normal human colonic epithelial NCM460 cells and human CRC HCT116 cells (Tan et al., 2025). As shown in Fig. 3C, MnMOF, Ki, Ki@MnMOF, and Ki@MnMOF@P showed minimal cytotoxicity toward NCM460 cells, with cell viability remaining above 85% even at the highest tested concentration. These results suggested that the nanoplatform had relatively low toxicity toward normal colonic epithelial cells. In contrast, the Ki-containing formulations exhibited obvious dose-dependent cytotoxicity against HCT116 cells (Fig. 3D). MnMOF alone showed only slight inhibition of HCT116 cell viability, indicating that the blank carrier had limited direct cytotoxicity. Free Ki markedly reduced HCT116 cell viability, confirming the antitumor activity of KAT6A inhibition. Ki@MnMOF also showed significant cytotoxicity, demonstrating that the loaded Ki could be effectively delivered and released from the MnMOF carrier, while Ki@MnMOF@P produced stronger inhibition. Live/dead staining further confirmed the selective killing effect of nanoplatform. As shown in Fig. 3E, PBS- and MnMOF@P-treated cells displayed predominantly green fluorescence, indicating high cell viability. In contrast, Ki-, Ki@MnMOF-, and Ki@MnMOF@P-treated groups exhibited increased red fluorescence, corresponding to elevated dead cell. Quantitative analysis showed that Ki@MnMOF@P induced the highest percentage of dead cells (Fig. 3F). This result was consistent with the cell viability assay and demonstrated the enhanced cancer cell-killing ability of Ki@MnMOF@P nanoplatform. A schematic illustration of the selective antitumor effect is shown in Fig. 3G. Ki@MnMOF@P could preferentially interact with CRC cells, leading to effective cancer cell killing. In contrast, the nanoplatform showed minimal effects on normal colonic epithelial cells. Collectively, these results demonstrated that Ki@MnMOF@P possessed efficient Ki loading and selective cytotoxicity against CRC cells, supporting its potential for subsequent immunotherapeutic applications.

pH-responsive release, cellular uptake, and tumor-targeting ability of Ki@MnMOF@P

The pH-responsive release behavior and tumor-targeting ability of Ki@MnMOF@P are illustrated in Fig. 4 A. Because the tumor microenvironment is mildly acidic, the Ki@MnMOF@P nanoplatform was expected to promote Ki release under acidic conditions while maintaining relative stability under physiological conditions (Bian et al., 2023; Liu et al., 2025b). As shown in Fig. 4B, both Ki@MnMOF and Ki@MnMOF@P exhibited sustained Ki release over 96 h, and the cumulative release reached approximately 80%. The similar release profiles of Ki@MnMOF and Ki@MnMOF@P indicated that anti-PD-L1 coating did not markedly interfere with Ki release from the MnMOF framework. The pH-dependent release profile was further evaluated under different pH conditions. As shown in Fig. 4C, Ki release was accelerated under acidic conditions, with the highest cumulative release observed at pH 5.5, followed by pH 6.5 and pH 7.4. This result suggested that the MnMOF nanoplatform could respond to acidic tumor-related environments and promote drug release, which may contribute to enhanced intracellular Ki delivery in tumor cells. The accelerated Ki release under acidic conditions may be attributed to the acid-triggered structural degradation of MnMOF. Specifically, protonation of the carboxylate ligands in the acidic environment weakens the Mn–ligand coordination bonds and may resulte in partial framework loosening or collapse. This behavior may facilitate Mn2+ release and promote the diffusion of Ki from the MOF pores. Besides, Ki@MnMOF@P exhibited accelerated Mn2+ release under acidic conditions and acid-triggered decrease of hydrodynamic size were observed (Fig. S5). To investigate the cellular uptake behavior, Nile red-labeled nanoparticles were incubated with normal human colonic epithelial NCM460 cells and human colorectal cancer HCT116 cells. In NCM460 cells, both Ki@MnMOF@P and Ki@MnMOF showed weak red fluorescence signals, and no obvious difference was observed between the two groups (Fig. 4D-E). In contrast, a markedly enhanced uptake of Ki@MnMOF@P was observed in HCT116 cells. As shown in Fig. 4F-G, Ki@MnMOF@P-treated HCT116 cells exhibited much stronger red fluorescence than Ki@MnMOF-treated cells, and quantitative analysis showed significantly higher fluorescence intensity of Ki@MnMOF@P. Besides, pre-blocking of PD-L1 with free anti-PD-L1 antibody markedly reduced the intracellular fluorescence signal of HCT116 cells treated with Ki@MnMOF@P (Fig. S6). The enhanced cellular uptake of Ki@MnMOF@P in HCT116 cells may be partly associated with the interaction between surface anti-PD-L1 and PD-L1 expressed on tumor cells. However, because PD-L1 can also be expressed by immune and stromal cells in the tumor microenvironment, the anti-PD-L1 coating should be interpreted as promoting PD-L1-associated tumor microenvironment interaction rather than exclusive cancer-cell-specific targeting. The Mn2+ content was further measured to confirm the delivery of MnMOF-derived manganese into cells. In NCM460 cells, both Ki@MnMOF and Ki@MnMOF@P induced only a slight increase in Mn2+ content as the nanoparticle concentration increased, and no significant difference was observed (Fig. 4H). In HCT116 cells, Ki@MnMOF@P resulted in a much higher Mn2+ content than Ki@MnMOF in a concentration-dependent manner (Fig. 4I). These results were consistent with the fluorescence uptake data and further confirmed the selective accumulation of anti-PD-L1-coated nanoparticles in CRC cells. Finally, the in vivo tumor-targeting ability of Ki@MnMOF@P was evaluated using ex vivo fluorescence imaging of major organs and tumors. As shown in Fig. 4 J, the strongest fluorescence signal was detected in the tumor tissue, whereas relatively weaker signals were observed in major organs, except for moderate liver accumulation. This biodistribution pattern indicated that Ki@MnMOF@P possessed favorable tumor accumulation ability. Taken together, these results demonstrated that Ki@MnMOF@P exhibited pH-responsive Ki release, enhanced uptake by CRC cells, increased Mn2+ delivery, and efficient tumor-targeting performance, supporting its potential for tumor-preferential activation of the cGAS-STING pathway and enhanced CRC immunotherapy (Fig. 4).

In vivo antitumor efficacy of Ki@MnMOF@P

The in vivo antitumor efficacy of Ki@MnMOF@P was further evaluated using a CT26 subcutaneous colorectal tumor-bearing mouse model (Xue et al., 2025). As shown in Fig. 5A, The mice were randomly divided into different treatment groups and intravenously administered PBS, MnMOF, MnMOF@P, Ki@MnMOF, or Ki@MnMOF@P. Tumor growth was monitored throughout the treatment period, and the mice were sacrificed on day 21 for further analysis. Individual tumor growth curves are shown in Fig. 5B-F. Tumors in the PBS group grew rapidly, indicating aggressive tumor progression without therapeutic intervention. Treatment with MnMOF alone showed only limited inhibition of tumor growth, suggesting that the blank MnMOF carrier had weak antitumor activity. Compared with MnMOF, MnMOF@P moderately delayed tumor progression, which may be attributed to the anti-PD-L1-mediated immune checkpoint blockade. Ki@MnMOF produced a stronger tumor-inhibitory effect than MnMOF and MnMOF@P, indicating that the delivery of the KAT6A inhibitor contributed to tumor growth suppression. Notably, Ki@MnMOF@P exhibited the most pronounced antitumor efficacy, with tumor growth being markedly inhibited during the observation period. The average tumor growth curves further confirmed the superior therapeutic effect of Ki@MnMOF@P (Fig. 5G). Ki@MnMOF@P significantly reduced tumor progression compared with all other groups. Consistent with the tumor volume results, the final tumor weights were significantly lower in the Ki@MnMOF@P group than in the other treatment groups (Fig. 5H). Ki@MnMOF also reduced tumor weight compared with PBS and MnMOF, but its inhibitory effect was weaker than that of Ki@MnMOF@P. To further clarify the contribution of each therapeutic component in Ki@MnMOF@P, additional control groups including free anti-PD-L1, free Ki, and Ki combined with anti-PD-L1 were evaluated (Fig. S7). Tumor weight analysis demonstrated Ki@MnMOF@P -treated mice exhibited that the lowest tumor weight, even lower than Ki combined with anti-PD-L1. These findings indicated that anti-PD-L1 coating further enhanced the therapeutic performance of the Ki-loaded MnMOF nanoplatform, likely by improving tumor targeting and restoring T-cell-mediated antitumor immunity. Histological analysis of tumor tissues was performed using H&E staining as shown in Fig. 5I, further confirming the potent antitumor effect in vivo. Taken together, these results demonstrated that Ki@MnMOF@P effectively inhibited colorectal tumor growth in vivo. The superior antitumor efficacy of Ki@MnMOF@P can be attributed to the integrated therapeutic functions of the nanoplatform, including Mn2+-mediated immune stimulation, KAT6 A inhibitor delivery, and anti-PD-L1 checkpoint blockade. These findings support Ki@MnMOF@P as a promising multifunctional nanoplatform for enhanced colorectal cancer immunotherapy.

Biodistribution and biosafety evaluation of Ki@MnMOF@P

The biodistribution behavior of Ki@MnMOF@P was investigated to further verify whether anti-PD-L1 coating could enhance tumor accumulation of the nanoplatform. As shown in Fig. 6A, Ki@MnMOF@P exhibited a higher Ki level in tumor tissues than Ki@MnMOF, indicating that the anti-PD-L1 coating improved the tumor-targeting ability of the nanoplatform. In contrast, relatively low Ki accumulation was observed in the major organs, including the heart, lung, spleen, and kidney. Although liver accumulation was detected, which is commonly associated with the clearance of nanomaterials by the reticuloendothelial system, Ki@MnMOF@P still showed preferential drug enrichment in tumor tissue (Cao et al., 2023). The time-dependent tumor accumulation profile further confirmed the targeting advantage of Ki@MnMOF@P. As shown in Fig. 6B, the Ki concentration in tumor tissues increased after administration and reached a relatively high level between 2 and 4 h. Compared with Ki@MnMOF, Ki@MnMOF@P showed significantly higher tumor accumulation at the main observation time points, suggesting that anti-PD-L1 functionalization enhanced tumor retention and delivery efficiency. These results were consistent with the in vitro cellular uptake and ex vivo fluorescence imaging results, supporting the role of anti-PD-L1 coating in improving CRC tumor targeting. Furthermore, manganese was mainly distributed in the liver, with relatively higher levels detected at early time points, and the kidney and spleen also exhibited moderate manganese accumulation, whereas relatively lower levels were observed in the heart and lung (Fig. S8). Notably, manganese levels in all investigated organs gradually declined over time and approached baseline levels at 168 h post-injection. The systemic biosafety of different formulations was then evaluated. During the treatment period, no obvious body weight loss was observed in any group (Fig. 6C), indicating that the administered formulations did not induce apparent systemic toxicity. H&E staining of major organs, including the liver, kidney, heart, lung, and spleen, further showed no obvious pathological damage or structural abnormalities after treatment with MnMOF, MnMOF@P, Ki@MnMOF, or Ki@MnMOF@P (Fig. 6D). These histological results suggested that the nanoplatforms possessed good tissue compatibility in vivo. Blood biochemical and hematological analyses were performed to further assess liver, kidney, and systemic toxicity. As shown in Fig. 6E–G, the levels of alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), and albumin (ALB) showed no significant abnormal changes among the treatment groups, indicating that liver function was not markedly affected. In addition, blood urea nitrogen (BUN) levels remained comparable across all groups (Fig. 6H), suggesting no obvious renal toxicity. Hematological parameters, including white blood cell (WBC) and red blood cell (RBC) counts, also remained within a similar range among the groups (Fig. 6I and J), indicating that the treatments did not induce notable hematological toxicity. Taken together, these results demonstrated that Ki@MnMOF@P achieved enhanced tumor accumulation while maintaining favorable systemic biosafety. The anti-PD-L1-coated MnMOF nanoplatform effectively improved tumor-targeted delivery of the KAT6A inhibitor without causing obvious damage to major organs or disrupting liver, kidney, and hematological functions. These findings further support the potential of Ki@MnMOF@P as a safe and effective nanoplatform for CRC immunotherapy.

Ki@MnMOF@P activates the cGAS-STING pathway and prolongs survival

To investigate the mechanism underlying the enhanced antitumor efficacy of Ki@MnMOF@P, the activation of the cGAS-STING pathway was evaluated in tumor tissues. As illustrated in Fig. 7A, the therapeutic design of Ki@MnMOF@P involves two complementary mechanisms: Mn2+ released from MnMOF can promote cGAMP production and activate STING signaling, while the loaded KAT6A inhibitor Ki can reduce KAT6A-associated signals and further enhance cGAS-STING-associated immune activation. As shown in Fig. 7B, strong KAT6A signals were observed in the PBS, MnMOF, and MnMOF@P groups, whereas Ki@MnMOF and Ki@MnMOF@P markedly reduced KAT6A signals. Quantitative analysis further confirmed that KAT6A levels were significantly decreased in the Ki-containing groups compared with the non-Ki groups (Fig. 7C). These results demonstrated that Ki was effectively delivered into tumor tissues and retained its KAT6A-inhibitory activity after loading into the MnMOF nanoplatform. The level of cGAMP, a key second messenger generated by cGAS activation, was then measured. As shown in Fig. 7D, MnMOF, MnMOF@P, Ki@MnMOF, and Ki@MnMOF@P all increased cGAMP levels compared with PBS, indicating that Mn-based formulations could activate upstream cGAS signaling. Consistently, immunofluorescence staining showed that cGAS expression was markedly enhanced in tumors treated with Ki@MnMOF and Ki@MnMOF@P, whereas only weak signals were detected in the PBS, MnMOF, and MnMOF@P groups (Fig. 7E). Quantitative analysis confirmed the significant upregulation of cGAS in the Ki-containing treatment groups (Fig. 7F). These results suggested that KAT6A inhibition played an important role in enhancing cGAS pathway activation within the tumor microenvironment. STING activation was further assessed by detecting phosphorylated STING (p-STING). As shown in Fig. 7G, PBS-treated tumors displayed minimal p-STING fluorescence, whereas MnMOF and MnMOF@P moderately increased p-STING signals. Notably, Ki@MnMOF and Ki@MnMOF@P induced much stronger p-STING expression. Quantitative analysis showed that the p-STING level was significantly elevated in the Ki@MnMOF and Ki@MnMOF@P groups compared with PBS and blank MnMOF-based groups (Fig. 7H). These findings indicated that Ki@MnMOF@P effectively activated the cGAS-STING signaling cascade in vivo. Moreover, Ki@MnMOF@P treatment markedly increased pTBK1 and pIRF3 expression compared with control groups, indicating enhanced activation of the downstream signaling cascade following cGAS-STING stimulation (Fig. S9). The current study was designed to establish the activation profile and immunological consequences of the Ki@MnMOF@P-mediated cGAS-STING pathway, whereas definitive pathway dependency studies using genetic or pharmacological blockade represent an important next step. Finally, the survival benefit of different treatments was evaluated. As shown in Fig. 7I, mice treated with Ki@MnMOF@P exhibited prolonged survival compared with the other groups, with a significant improvement in overall survival (P = 0.001). This result was consistent with the tumor growth inhibition data and further confirmed the therapeutic advantage of the integrated nanoplatform. Taken together, these results demonstrated that Ki@MnMOF@P could effectively suppress KAT6A expression, enhance cGAMP production, activate cGAS and STING phosphorylation, and prolong survival in CT26 tumor-bearing mice. The enhanced therapeutic outcome was likely attributed to the activation of cGAS-STING signaling by Mn2+ release and KAT6A inhibition. These findings further support Ki@MnMOF@P as a promising multifunctional nanoplatform for cGAS-STING-activated colorectal cancer immunotherapy.

Ki@MnMOF@P remodels the tumor immune microenvironment

To further clarify the immunotherapeutic mechanism of Ki@MnMOF@P, immune activation and tumor immune microenvironment remodeling were evaluated after different treatments. As illustrated in Fig. 8A, Ki@MnMOF@P was designed to integrate tumor targeting, Mn2+-mediated cGAS-STING activation, KAT6A inhibition, and PD-L1 blockade. This combined strategy was expected to enhance innate immune activation, promote antitumor cytokine secretion, and increase T-cell-mediated immune responses in tumor tissues. The secretion of cGAS-STING-related cytokines was first examined by ELISA. As shown in Fig. 8B and C, MnMOF and MnMOF@P moderately increased the levels of IFN-β and CXCL10 compared with PBS, indicating that Mn2+ released from MnMOF could activate innate immune signaling. Ki@MnMOF and Ki@MnMOF@P further enhanced IFN-β and CXCL10 production, suggesting that KAT6A inhibition contributed to the amplification of interferon-related immune responses (Kim et al., 2024; Elewaut et al., 2025). The levels of inflammatory and immunostimulatory cytokines were also evaluated. As shown in Fig. 8D and E, TNF-α and IL-6 levels were increased after treatment with MnMOF-based formulations, especially in the Ki-containing groups. Ki@MnMOF and Ki@MnMOF@P induced markedly higher cytokine secretion than PBS, suggesting enhanced immune stimulation within the tumor microenvironment. Spleen weight was then measured as an indicator of systemic immune activation. As shown in Fig. 8F, the spleen weight increased after treatment with MnMOF@P, Ki@MnMOF, and Ki@MnMOF@P, with the Ki@MnMOF@P group showing the most obvious increase. This result suggested that the integrated nanoplatform activated systemic antitumor immunity. The activation of adaptive antitumor immunity was further assessed by measuring IFN-γ secretion and T-cell populations (Peng et al., 2022). As shown in Fig. 8G, Ki@MnMOF@P significantly increased IFN-γ levels compared with the other groups, indicating enhanced T-cell-mediated immune activation. Moreover, Ki@MnMOF@P markedly increased the proportions of both CD8+ T cells and CD4+ T cells in tumor tissues (Fig. 8H and I). Compared with Ki@MnMOF, the higher T-cell infiltration observed in the Ki@MnMOF@P group may be attributed to the anti-PD-L1 coating, which relieved PD-L1-mediated immune suppression and promoted T-cell antitumor activity. Taken together, these results demonstrated that Ki@MnMOF@P effectively remodeled the tumor immune microenvironment by enhancing cGAS-STING-related cytokine secretion, increasing IFN-γ production, and promoting CD8+ and CD4+ T-cell infiltration. The improved immune activation was attributed to the coordinated effects of Mn2+-mediated innate immune stimulation, KAT6A inhibitor-mediated cGAS-STING amplification, and anti-PD-L1-mediated checkpoint blockade. These findings further explain the superior antitumor efficacy of Ki@MnMOF@P in colorectal cancer immunotherapy. Recent studies have demonstrated that multifunctional nanoplatforms capable of integrating multiple therapeutic interactions can achieve enhanced antitumor efficacy through coordinated regulation of tumor cells and the immune microenvironment, especially cGAS-STING signal (Sun et al., 2021; Wang et al., 2023; Li et al., 2022; Huang et al., 2023). By combining different therapeutic modalities, including immune activation, checkpoint blockade, and tumor microenvironment modulation, these nanosystems can overcome the limitations associated with single-agent therapies (Table S1). Inspired by these advances, the present Ki@MnMOF@P platform was designed to simultaneously inhibit KAT6A, activate Mn2+-mediated cGAS-STING signaling, and block PD-L1-mediated immune suppression, thereby achieving synergistic antitumor immunotherapy.

Despite the promising therapeutic efficacy of Ki@MnMOF@P in the current study, several challenges remain before clinical translation. The long-term fate, metabolism, and clearance of manganese released from the nanoplatform require further systematic investigation. In addition, scalable synthesis with strict quality control, batch-to-batch reproducibility, and optimization of antibody conjugation efficiency remain important considerations for clinical manufacturing. Future studies using orthotopic, metastatic, and patient-derived tumor models will be necessary to further validate the therapeutic potential and immune-regulatory effects of this platform. Moreover, comprehensive evaluation of pharmacokinetics, immunogenicity, and long-term toxicity will be essential for advancing Ki@MnMOF@P toward clinical application.

Conclusion

In this study, we successfully developed a KAT6A inhibitor-loaded and anti-PD-L1-coated manganese metal–organic framework nanoplatform, Ki@MnMOF@P, for enhanced colorectal cancer immunotherapy through cGAS-STING pathway activation. The MnMOF framework served not only as an efficient carrier for Ki delivery but also as a manganese-based immunostimulatory component. Surface coating with anti-PD-L1 further endowed the nanoplatform with immune checkpoint blockade capability and improved tumor-targeted accumulation. The prepared Ki@MnMOF@P exhibited efficient drug-loading capacity, pH-responsive Ki release, and retained PD-1/PD-L1 blocking activity. In vitro experiments demonstrated that Ki@MnMOF@P enhanced cellular uptake in colorectal cancer cells and showed selective cytotoxicity against tumor cells while maintaining low toxicity toward normal colonic epithelial cells. In vivo, Ki@MnMOF@P significantly inhibited CT26 tumor growth, reduced tumor weight, and prolonged survival. The Ki@MnMOF@P showed good systemic biosafety without obvious body weight loss, or abnormal liver, kidney, and hematological toxicity. Mechanistically, Ki@MnMOF@P effectively suppressed KAT6A expression, promoted cGAMP production, enhanced cGAS expression, and increased STING phosphorylation in tumor tissues. Moreover, the treatment remodeled the tumor immune microenvironment by elevating IFN-β, CXCL10, TNF-α, IL-6, and IFN-γ levels and increasing CD8+ and CD4+ T-cell infiltration. These results indicate that the superior antitumor efficacy of Ki@MnMOF@P through KAT6A inhibitor-mediated and Mn2+-mediated cGAS-STING activation, and anti-PD-L1-mediated checkpoint blockade. Overall, Ki@MnMOF@P provides a promising multifunctional nanomedicine strategy for improving colorectal cancer immunotherapy.
