ARID1A loss drives gastric signet ring cell carcinoma by regulating mucin production and secretion
Authors: Hongyu Liu, Ailing Zhong, Zhenghao Lu, Limin Gao, Mengsha Zhang, Jiaxin Li, Yingjie Wang, Yiyun Wang, Lanxin Zhang, Jiabo Zhang, Xiangyu Pan, Xuelan Chen, Jingyao Chen, Siyu He, Xinyuan Wang, Xudong Wan, Xintong Deng, Tingfa Peng, Jian Wang, Jiajia Du, Kun Yang, Kai Liu, Xinzu Chen, Xiaolong Chen, Zhe Feng, Baohong Wu, Jing Xu, Linyong Zhao, Weihan Zhang, Shengyong Yang, Yuan Wang, Lu Chen, Zhihong Xue, Chengjian Zhao, Lunzhi Dai, Feifei Na, Jiankun Hu, Yu Liu, Chong Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Signet ring cell carcinoma (SRCC) is a lethal malignancy with distinct histologic features, characterized by accumulated mucins in the cytoplasm which compress nuclei. Gastric SRCC is the most common SRCC whose incidence is increasing in recent years. The molecular mechanisms underlying the histopathology remain poorly understood. Here, we report that AT-rich interactive domain-containing protein 1 A (ARID1A), one of the most frequently mutated genes in gastric SRCC, functions as a bona fide tumor suppressor. Its loss, together with Trp53 and Pten loss, drives SRCC in mice. Mechanistically, Arid1a loss upregulates the expressions of mucins through the competing BRD9-containing ncBAF complex. And mucin secretion is impaired by the downregulation of Scin, a direct target of Arid1a in SRCC. Inhibition of Brd9 ameliorates the malignancy of SRCC. Thus, our study reveals dual roles of ARID1A in both mucin production and secretion, providing new mechanistic insights and potential therapeutic vulnerabilities in SRCC.

Introduction

Signet ring cell carcinoma (SRCC) is a histologically distinct form of highly malignant adenocarcinoma, characterized by a large vacuole and the resulting cell periphery-localized nucleus, resembling a signet ring1,2. The most common SRCC tumors occur in the stomach, and less frequently in colorectum and other sites3–5. Gastric SRCC accounts for approximately 10% of all gastric cancers, with an increasing incidence in recent years, and is associated with poor prognosis6–9. It is believed that this special histologic appearance is due to large amounts of mucins produced by SRCC cells and their deficient ability to export them10–15. However, the molecular mechanisms underlying these malignancy-associated abnormalities remain poorly understood.

Recent progress in cancer genomics have identified a spectrum of genomic alterations in SRCC9,16,17. In addition to TP53, the most frequently mutated gene in both SRCC and non-SRCC tumors, ARID1A and CDH1 exhibit particularly high mutation rates in SRCC. Other alterations, such as CLDN18-ARHGAP26/6 fusions, are also detected in approximately 20% of SRCC patients9,18,19. CDH1 encodes the cell-cell adhesion molecule E-cadherin, and its heterozygous loss significantly promotes SRCC formation in mice treated with N-methyl-N-nitrosourea (MNU), whereas it has a limited effect on tubular adenoma development20. Similarly, CDH1 knockout in human gastric organoids induces morphological changes similar to SRCC21–24. It has also been suggested that downregulation of Wnt signaling facilitates the SRCC morphology driven by CDH1 mutations. CLDN18 encodes a tight junction protein, while ARHGAP26/6 function as RHOA inhibitors, and the CLDN18-ARHGAP26/6 fusion appears to enhance the migration and chemoresistance of gastric cancer cells, features consistent with the clinical characteristics of SRCC patients9. In contract, the potential functions of other SRCC-associated genes, including ARID1A, remain less well understood.

ARID1A, a core member of the SWI/SNF chromatin remodeling complex, is frequently mutated in multiple human cancers, including ovarian clear cell carcinoma (50%), uterine endometrioid carcinoma (29%), and gastric cancer (29%)25,26. Arid1a loss alone can drive invasive colon adenocarcinoma in mice, whereas it suppresses tumorigenesis in the small intestine of the Apcmin mice27. In liver cancer, ARID1A has been reported to promote tumor initiation but restrain progression and metastasis at later stages28. ARID1A knockout can also transform human gastric organoids29. However, whether and how ARID1A deficiency plays a functional role in SRCC, particularly its distinctive histopathology, remains to be investigated.

In this study, we develop a strategy to generate primary SRCC mouse models using genome-edited gastric organoids. We find that Arid1a loss promotes SRCC formation by directly impairing the secretory process and indirectly enhancing mucin gene expression.

Results

ARID1A loss cooperates with TP53 and PTEN deficiency to promote SRCC initiation and progression

It is necessary to first analyze the transcriptomic profiles of SRCC patients in order to construct clinically relevant mouse models, given that gastric SRCC possesses distinct transcriptomic characteristics compared to other pathological types of gastric cancer. Thus, we analyzed genetic data from 171 cases of diffuse-type gastric carcinoma (DGC) and gastric signet ring cell carcinoma (SRCC) obtained from the cBioPortal database and found that ARID1A was altered in 39% of cases (66/171). Notably, ARID1A alterations frequently co-occurred with TP53 (50%, 86/171) and PTEN (18%, 30/171) alterations (Supplementary Fig. 1a and Supplementary Dataset 1). To investigate the functional role of ARID1A in SRCC, we utilized a previously established gastric cancer mouse model that combines CRISPR/Cas9-mediated genome editing with organoid culture.

Since ARID1A, TP53, and PTEN alterations frequently co-occur in patient samples, we first generated gastric organoids from adult Trp53−/−; Cas9-EGFP mice. We then introduced an sgRNA targeting Pten, thereby producing Trp53−/−; sgPten organoids. Subsequently, these organoids were transduced with either a non-targeting sgScramble (sgScr) control or sgArid1a, yielding two engineered models: Trp53−/−; sgPten; sgScr (TP) and Trp53−/−; sgPten; sgArid1a (TPA) organoids (Supplementary Dataset 2)30,31. Because the sgRNA-expressing plasmid co-expressed mCherry, successfully infected organoids exhibited red fluorescence, allowing their tracking and analysis. Mutations in Pten and Arid1a were confirmed by a T7 endonuclease I mismatch detection assay (Supplementary Fig. 1b and Supplementary Dataset 2), and loss of ARID1A protein expression was further validated by Western blotting (Supplementary Fig. 1c). Morphologically, TPA organoids exhibited more prominent hollow structures, with cavities filled with mucin as revealed by Alcian Blue-Periodic acid Schiff (AB-PAS) staining (Fig. 1a). Meanwhile, we isolated and expanded monoclonal organoids derived from the TP and TPA gastric organoids (Supplementary Fig. 1d, e). These monoclonal organoids recapitulated the phenotypes observed in polyclonal cultures: TPA clones exhibited significantly increased proliferation and distinct morphological features compared with TP clones (Supplementary Fig. 1f, g). Collectively, these results indicate that monoclonal TPA organoids are phenotypically comparable to bulk TPA organoids, further indicating our strategy for investigating the function of ARID1A.

To further elucidate the role of Arid1a, we performed subcutaneous transplantation of TP and TPA organoids and compared their growth rates in vivo. Loss of Arid1a markedly accelerated tumor growth at the subcutaneous site, whereas the control group displayed only modest expansion (Fig. 1d–f). Histopathological analysis revealed that TPA-derived tumors exhibited characteristic morphological features of SRCC, including cytoplasmic mucin accumulation and nuclear compression (Fig. 1g). Immunohistochemical (IHC) staining confirmed the loss of ARID1A and the overexpression of MUC1 in TPA tumors (Fig. 1g, h). In contrast, the TP tumors mainly displayed generalized hyperplasia. Furthermore, AB-PAS staining of tumor tissues showed a marked increase in positively stained cells in the TPA group (Fig. 1g). Together, these findings identify Arid1a loss as a key driver of tumor initiation and progression.

Orthotopic gastric SRCC with Arid1a loss displayed massive mucin production

To better understand the biological function of ARID1A in SRCC, we orthotopically injected TP and TPA gastric organoids into the gastric submucosa of recipient mice to model clinical gastric tumorigenesis and monitored tumor formation32–34. Orthotopic transplantation of TP gastric organoids exhibited minimal tumorigenic capacity, with successful engraftment observed in only one recipient mouse. In contrast, TPA organoid recipients developed robust tumors characterized by histologically confirmed invasive gastric adenocarcinoma lesions. Specifically, mice transplanted with TPA organoids developed tumors significantly faster and exhibited shorter overall survival compared with control recipients (TPA-1: median 67 days after transplantation; p = 1.0E-4 and TPA-2: median 142 days after transplantation; p = 4.4E-3, Fig. 2a). Biopsy analysis confirmed that the TPA gastric organoids gave rise to a single gastric lesion showing specific mCherry expression, indicating their origin from the transplanted organoids (Fig. 2b). To clarify the pathological classification of TPA tumors, we performed hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining, which serves as a key diagnostic criterion for tumor type identification. The results showed that the lesions were predominantly composed of tumor cells with prominent cytoplasmic vacuoles and eccentrically displaced crescent-shaped nuclei, consistent with the typical morphology of SRCC (Fig. 2e, f). Furthermore, AB-PAS staining revealed marked mucin enrichment within the cytoplasm of most tumor cells (Fig. 2e). Given that mucin accumulation in SRCC is readily detectable by transmission electron microscopy (TEM)11, we next performed TEM analysis of TP and TPA tumor tissues. TEM revealed numerous cytoplasmic vacuoles with nuclei compressed to the periphery-defining ultrastructural characteristics of SRCC (Fig. 2e). IHC analysis further showed that MUC1, a mucin family protein, was markedly upregulated in TPA tumors compared with TP tumors (Supplementary Fig. 2a, b). In contrast, the epithelial differentiation marker E-cadherin (CDH1) exhibited comparable expression levels between TP and TPA tumor cohorts (Supplementary Fig. 2a, b), suggesting that the underlying mechanism of ARID1A-deficient SRCC may not involve the CDH1 deficiency pathway35,36. Consistent with the features observed in clinical SRCC patients, TPA tumors displayed metastatic potential to distal sites, whereas no detectable metastases were observed in TP tumors (Supplementary Fig. 2c, d). The metastatic lesions exhibited the same signet ring cell carcinoma morphology as the primary tumors (Supplementary Fig. 2c, d).

Collectively, these results demonstrate that the ARID1A-deficiency-driven orthotopic SRCC-like model faithfully recapitulates the histological and pathological features of human SRCC.

ARID1A deficiency disrupts SCIN-dependent secretion in SRCC

We further performed RNA-seq on TP and TPA monoclonal organoids. Principal component analysis (PCA) revealed that the transcriptomes of TPA clones were clearly distinct from those of TP clones (Supplementary Fig. 3a), with monoclonal TPA organoids clustering together with bulk TPA organoids, and monoclonal TP organoids clustering with bulk TP organoids. Notably, the upregulated mucin genes identified in TPA monoclonal organoids were markedly increased (Supplementary Fig. 3b). GSEA further demonstrated that genes up or downregulated in bulk TPA organoids were correspondingly enriched in TPA clones relative to TP organoids (Supplementary Fig. 3c). Collectively, these phenotypic and molecular results indicate that monoclonal TPA organoids closely resemble bulk TPA organoids (Supplementary Fig. 1d–g), thereby validating our strategy for investigating the biological function of ARID1A.

Signet ring cells are defined by excessive intracellular mucin accumulation; however, how ARID1A contributes to this phenotype remains unknown. Based on this SRCC phenotype, we hypothesized that it results from both increased mucin production and defective secretion (Fig. 3d). Given that ARID1A functions as a chromatin remodeler, we performed ARID1A CUT&Tag (Cleavage Under Targets and Tagmentation) analysis and identified 14,864 ARID1A-regulated genomic regions (Fig. 3e and Supplementary Dataset 6). Subsequently, we performed ATAC-seq (Assay for Transposase-Accessible Chromatin using sequencing) on TP and TPA gastric organoids and identified significantly altered chromatin accessibility between the two groups (Fig. 3f and Supplementary Dataset 5). ATAC gain regions in the TPA organoids were more frequently located in promoter regions compared with ATAC loss regions (Supplementary Fig. 3d). And the tornado plots showed that the ATAC maintain regions had much less ARID1A binding than those with ATAC loss or gain, suggesting that they were not regulated by ARID1A (Supplementary Fig. 3e). GO enrichment analysis revealed that genes within ATAC loss regions were enriched in pathways related to epithelial differentiation and epidermal development, whereas genes within ATAC gain regions were enriched in ERK, TGF-β, and other growth-related signaling pathways (Supplementary Fig. 3f, g). Notably, analysis showed that 56.6% of genes with decreased chromatin accessibility upon Arid1a knockout were directly bound by ARID1A (Fig. 3f, g and Supplementary Dataset 5, 6). These genes were enriched in pathways related to gastric gland morphogenesis, including epithelial cell proliferation and gland development (Fig. 3h).

Among these genes, Scin (Scinderin)—an exocytosis-related gene that encoding an actin-severing protein essential for vesicular trafficking37—was directly regulated by ARID1A (Fig. 3i and Supplementary Dataset 5 and 6) and significantly downregulated following Arid1a loss (Fig. 3j). Consistently, analysis of the TCGA-STAD cohort revealed significantly reduced SCIN expression in ARID1A-mutant tumors compared with ARID1A wild-type controls (Fig. 3k). To determine whether ARID1A directly regulates Scin and its potential influence on the extracellular secretory pathway, we introduced Scin mutations into TP tumor organoids using CRISPR/Cas9-mediated gene editing, generating TP-sgScr and TP-sgScin models (Supplementary Fig. 3h). We quantified the size and numbers of tumor organoids with sgScr or sgScin. The results showed that there were more organoids with Scin loss than the control and they also had significantly increased size, comparing the sgScr ones (Supplementary Fig. 3i, j). Subsequently, we assessed their morphological changes. TP-sgScin organoids exhibited a vacuolar phenotype accompanied by mucin overexpression (Fig. 3l, m). Moreover, TP-sgScin organoids displayed a significantly higher frequency of SRCC-like morphological features compared with TP-sgScr controls (Fig. 3n).

To further investigate whether ARID1A directly regulates SCIN, we performed RNA-seq analyses to compare the transcriptomic similarity between TPA and TP-sgScin tumor organoids. Loss of Scin in TP organoids led to gene expression changes that significantly overlapped with those in TPA organoids (Supplementary Fig. 3k). In particular, both Scin- and Arid1a-deficient organoids shared a set of downregulated genes predominantly involved in protein transport, localization, and secretion (Supplementary Fig. 3l). Collectively, these results suggest that ARID1A directly regulates SCIN. The loss of this regulation impairs normal cellular secretion, thereby contributing to the initiation of SRCC.

ARID1A deficiency directly induces SCIN downregulation and disrupts cellular secretion

Subsequently, we performed a functional rescue experiment by overexpressing SCIN in TPA tumor cells (vector control and SCIN OE). Collectively, these findings suggest that ARID1A directly regulates SCIN transcription, which influence mucin secretion and thereby affect the development of SRCC.

ARIDIA loss increased the expressions of mucins through a BRD9-mediated indirect mechanism

Since Arid1a is an ATP-dependent chromatin helicase, its loss is expected to downregulate its direct downstream targets, as we showed above in the case of Scin and other contexts37. Notably, emerging evidence suggests that the ARIDIA-containing cBAF may compete with other BAF complexes, including the BRD9- containing ncBAF38. To test this hypothesis, we first measured the chromatin binding of BRD9 in TP and TPA organoids using CUT&Tag (Supplementary Fig. 5a and Supplementary Dataset 7). Remarkably, 73.8% of genes located within regions that gained chromatin accessibility following Arid1a loss were bound by BRD9 (Fig. 5c). Consistently, BRD9 binding signals were significantly enhanced at a subset of genomic loci following Arid1a loss (Fig. 5d). Moreover, analysis of BRD9 binding within ATAC gain regions revealed a predominance of peaks with increased BRD9 occupancy (Supplementary Fig. 5b). BRD9 binding was predominantly localized to distal intergenic regions (40.66% in TP and 40.42% in TPA) and promoter regions (10.95% in TP and 22.97% in TPA) (Supplementary Fig. 5c, d). We next applied DiffBind to identify BRD9 gain, maintained, and loss regions in TPA organoids relative to TP organoids and subsequently characterized their genomic features. BRD9 gain regions exhibited increased genomic accessibility, whereas BRD9 loss regions showed decreased accessibility, suggesting that BRD9 plays a critical role in gene regulation upon ARID1A loss (Supplementary Fig. 5e). Consistent with these findings, GO enrichment analysis revealed significant enrichment of cell cycle, TGF-beta, hypoxia, and growth-related pathways in BRD9 gain regions (Supplementary Fig. 5f). Of note, the regions with both ATAC and BRD9 binding gain contained multiple pro-tumor genes, such as Meis3 and Plcb4, besides mucins (Supplementary Fig. 5g), which further suggest that BRD9 may facilitate the pro-tumorigenic transcriptional programs associated with ARID1A loss.

Notably, genes that displayed increased chromatin accessibility upon Arid1a loss and were bound by BRD9 were significantly enriched in SRCC-related pathways (Fig. 5e). Integrative analysis identified 2479 genes with increased chromatin accessibility upon Arid1a loss that were both bound and transcriptionally regulated by BRD9. This set included mucin family genes (Fig. 5f), which exhibited enhanced BRD9 occupancy, elevated chromatin accessibility, and increased expression following Arid1a knockout (Fig. 5g, h).

To evaluate the therapeutic potential of BRD9 degradation for SRCC, we found that dBRD9 exerted a stronger effect than 5-Fu in TPA tumor organoids, which were relatively resistant to conventional chemotherapy (Fig. 6d and Supplementary Fig. 6d). Furthermore, dBRD9 treatment induced significant regression of the histological and morphological features of TPA tumor organoids, including reduced structural complexity, cribriform growth, and stratification (Fig. 6e), whereas TP organoids remained unaffected (Supplementary Fig. 6e). The proportion of SRCC-like and AB-PAS-positive organoids was significantly decreased in the dBRD9-treated groups compared with dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO)-treated controls (Fig. 6f, g).

We further assessed the efficacy of dBRD9 treatment in vivo using Arid1a-deficient SRCC mouse model (Fig. 6h). TPA tumor organoids were subcutaneously transplanted into recipient mice, followed by daily treatment with either vehicle or dBRD9 (50 mg/kg). dBRD9 treatment significantly inhibited the growth of Arid1a-deficient tumors (Fig. 6i). Histological analysis using H&E and AB-PAS staining revealed that BRD9 inhibition reduced the proportion of signet ring cells compared with the vehicle-treated group (Fig. 6j, k). The data presented herein suggest that BRD9 inhibition represents a promising therapeutic strategy for modulating the morphological and biological characteristics of SRCC.

Collectively, these data suggest that BRD9 inhibition represents a promising therapeutic strategy for modulating the morphological and biological characteristics of SRCC. This finding is particularly significant, as current treatment guidelines for gastric adenocarcinoma lack effective options for managing SRCC. Targeting BRD9 may thus offer a novel approach to improve therapeutic outcomes for patients with this challenging gastric cancer subtype.

Discussion

Gastric SRCC is a subtype of gastric malignancies, and its study has been hindered by limited animal models. Here, we applied a recently developed strategy with genome-edited organoids to generate primary, orthotopic, and genetic drivers-defined SRCC models in mice. Similar strategies, referred to as Genetically Engineered Organoid-initiated Mouse Models (GEOMMs), have been applied to other malignancies, including lung, esophageal, bladder, and colorectal30,31,41,42. Recently, we also developed GEOMMs of gastric adenocarcinoma and found that different genetic alterations and microenvironments can significantly influence their progression and metastasis32. Using a similar approach, we also established an Ezh2-driven mouse model of gastric squamous cell carcinoma34. Compared with conventional genetically engineered mouse models (GEMMs), GEOMMs provide a more efficient platform for functional characterization of novel genetic alterations. This advantage makes them valuable for dissecting the functions of the numerous human cancer-associated genes revealed by cancer genomics studies.

Using this strategy, we found that gastric organoids with Arid1a loss, together with Trp53 and Pten deficiencies, exhibited tumor characteristics of SRCC when implanted both subcutaneously and orthotopically in the stomach. These findings not only validate ARID1A, one of the most frequently mutated genes in SRCC, as a bona fide tumor suppressor of gastric SRCC in mice but also provide a strategy for investigating the functions of other SRCC-associated genes. We found that CDH1 also plays an important role in SRCC. However, we didn’t observe a significant change of CDH1 levels in the TPA tumors, which was consistent with the IF staining results reported by ref. 29. Only about 10% gastric SRCC patients have CDH1 mutations9,16. And single cell RNA-seq analyses found that “it was revealed that the CDH1 expression level was essentially the same between M/PDA and SRCC cells”14. Despite the unchanged CDH1 levels, the TPA tumors displayed typical SRCC histopathological features, including intracytoplasmic mucins and nucleus pushed to the edge and these SRCC-like cells consisted of about 80% of all tumor cells. We also observed increased metastases in the TPA mice, consistent with the features observed in clinical SRCC patients14.

ARID1A is frequently disrupted in many other types of human cancers as well43–45. However, its roles and underlying mechanisms in tumorigenesis and progression are complex and context-dependent. For example, ARID1A exhibits opposing functions in the initiation and progression of liver cancer through regulating oxidative stress and chromatin accessibility, respectively28. In liver cholangiocarcinoma, Arid1a loss cooperates with Kras mutation to promote tumorigenesis via repression of the TGF-β pathway46, whereas activation of the YAP pathway is essential for Arid1a deficiency-induced pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma47. In the urothelium, ARID1A loss induces a transcriptional-translational conflict, resulting in uncontrolled proliferation, clonogenic growth, and bladder cancer progression48.

Given the well-characterized roles of the ARID1A-containing SWI/SNF complex in chromatin remodeling and transcriptional regulation, ARID1A loss has been expected to cause a global reduction in chromatin accessibility28,29,49. However, accumulating evidence indicates that, at least in some tumors, ARID1A deficiency instead increases chromatin accessibility, possibly through the dysregulation of other chromatin remodeling factors50–52. Consistently, our study demonstrates that Arid1a loss led to hyperproliferation in our model, which is consistent with previous studies in many other types of cancers, while the detailed mechanisms were context-dependent28,29,53. GSEA and RNA-seq analyses revealed that TPA tumors displayed significantly enriched gene signatures related to MAPK and PI3K-AKT signaling, consistent with transcriptional activation of these pathways. Comparable signatures were also observed in SRCC patients and were associated with cell proliferation. In particular, we found that secretory genes such as Scin were directly downregulated, while mucin genes were indirectly increased in Arid1a-loss-induced SRCC. This mechanism differs from that reported by Lo et al., who showed that combined ARID1A and TP53 loss drove diffuse gastric cancer in human gastric organoids but did not observe an SRCC-like phenotype29. Interestingly, their Alcian blue staining revealed mucin proteins predominantly localized to the organoid lumen, whereas in our models, AB-PAS staining demonstrated marked intracellular mucin retention together with transcriptional and epigenomic evidence linking ARID1A loss to mucin regulation. Although the precise basis for these differences remains unclear, potential explanations include distinct genetic contexts (TP53 loss alone versus combined Trp53 and Pten loss), the accumulation of unidentified mutations during prolonged organoid culture.

Given the prevalence and tumorigenic functions of ARID1A mutations, there has been attractive to explore potential therapeutic strategies for ARID1A-altered tumors. Previous studies have shown that ARID1A-inactivated ovarian carcinomas exhibit defects in the mevalonate pathway and are therefore sensitive to statin treatment, alone or in combination with immune checkpoint blockade54. Multi-omics analyses have suggested that ARID1A-mutated gastric cancers may be responsive to bromodomain and NF-κB inhibition55. We found that Arid1a loss leads to Brd9-mediated upregulation of mucin genes. Thus, we propose that targeting Brd9 or the non-classic BAF complex may represent an effective therapeutic strategy for SRCC, which is characterized by excessive mucin accumulation. Our finding are consistent with observations in other cancer types, including sarcoma39, rhabdoid tumor56, and liver cancer57. It will be of great interest to test inhibitors of the non-classic BAF complex in patients harboring ARID1A mutations. Nevertheless, we also note that ARID1A may play complex roles in both malignant cells and nonmalignant cells, such as its function in tumor-infiltrating T cells58. Therefore, therapeutic strategies targeting ARID1A-deficient tumors should be carefully evaluated for potential systemic effects.

Methods

Ethics statement

For studies involving mice described below, all animal procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of West China Hospital. The maximal tumor volume permitted under the approved protocol was 1500 mm³. Tumor size was monitored regularly throughout the study, and no tumors from any mice exceeded this predefined limit.

Mice

All these mice were bred and kept in the SPF animal facility of Sichuan University, and no mouse was excluded in the experiments. Trp53−/− mice from The Jackson Laboratory (Stock No: 002101, RRID: IMSR_JAX:002101) and Trp53−/−; Cas9-expressing mice obtained by breeding Trp53−/− mice with Cas9 knock-in mice from The Jackson Laboratory (Stock No: 026179, RRID: IMSR_JAX:026179) were served as donor mice (both in C57BL/6 background). BALB/cA-nu mice were purchased from Beijing HuaFukang Biological Technology Co., Ltd. (6–8-week-old).

Mouse organoid culture

Mouse organoid cultures were established and maintained as described previously32. Specifically, normal mice stomach was mechanically minced into pieces of 5-mm3 cubes and incubated in DPBS with 2.5 mM EDTA on ice. After incubation, the released gastric gland fractions were resuspended with Matrigel (Corning, Cat# 356237) and seeded in 48-wells plate. Tumor tissues were harvested and washed with ice-cold DPBS, and then minced into pieces. Incubating the minced tumor tissue in digestion medium containing 0.5 mg/ml collagenase IV (GIBCO, Cat# 17104-019) and 1.0 mg/ml collagenase I (GIBCO, Cat# 17100-017) in DMEM/F12 medium at 37 °C until it dissociates into single cells or cell clusters. Tumor cells were collected by centrifugation and resuspended with Matrigel. Organoid culture medium was DMEM/F12 (GIBCO, Cat# C11330500BT) supplemented with 2 mM GlutaMAX (GIBCO, Cat# 35050-061), 1% Penicillin and Streptomycin (GIBCO, Cat# 15140-122), 1× B27 (GIBCO, Cat# A3582801), 1× N2 (GIBCO, Cat# 17502048), 10 mM Y-27632, 200 ng/ml FGF10 (Peprotech, Cat# 100-26-1000), 500 nM A-8301 (Peprotech, Cat# 9094360), 50 ng/ml mouse recombinant EGF (Peprotech, Cat# AF-100-15-1000), 100 ng/ml mouse recombinant noggin (Peprotech, Cat# 120-10C-250), 10 mM Nicotinamide (Sigma, Cat# N0636), 1 mM N-acetylcysteine (Sigma, Cat# A9165), 10% Wnt3a condition medium, 10% R-spondin1 condition medium). Organoid medium was refreshed every 2–3 days.

Organoid genome editing

sgRNAs designed on the website of DNA2.0 Gene Design & Synthesis (https://www.atum.bio/pipeline/dna) were cloned into the lentiviral constructs U6-sgRNA-EFS-mCherry-P2A-puro. Human SCIN cDNA sequences were obtained from the https://asia.ensembl.org/index.html, digested by BglII (Cat#R0144S) and EcoRI (Cat#R0101S) endonuclease from New England BioLabs, and followed by cloning into the retroviral plasmid MSCV-cDNA-IRES-GFP. The sequences of gRNAs and primers targeting Pten, Arid1a, Scin, and Brd9 are listed in Supplementary Dataset 2. Lentivirus packaging, infection, and verification were done as previously reported31.

Generation of clonal organoid lines

After washing with PBS, organoids were dissociated using TrypLE™ dissociation reagent at 37 °C for 10 min. Cell aggregates were removed by passing the suspension through a 35 μm cell strainer, and the filtrate was centrifuged at 400 × g for 5 min at 4 °C to collect the cell pellet. The pellet was resuspended in culture medium supplemented with 10 μM Y-27632. Cells were then distributed into individual wells of a 96-well plate by serial dilution. The plate had been pre-coated with 10 μL of Matrigel and overlaid with 100 μL of organoid culture medium. Wells containing a single cell were confirmed by microscopic examination. After 8–10 days, wells containing a single organoid were dissociated with TrypLE™ and re-plated for clonal expansion.

Organoid subcutaneous transplantation

Trp53−/−;sgPten;sgScramble (TP) and Trp53−/−;sgPten;sgArid1a (TPA) gastric organoids, TP-sgScramble and TP-sgScin tumor organoids, TPA tumor organoids were dissociated into single cells using TrypLE™ Express, resuspended in Matrigel (Corning #356231), and mixed with PBS at a 1:1 ratio. Cell viability was confirmed >90% by trypan blue exclusion. The cell suspension (2 × 105 cells in 50 μL total volume) was aspirated into pre-chilled 29 G insulin syringes and injected subcutaneously into the right flank of 6–8-week-old BALB/cA-nu mice (n = 6–7 per group). Tumor growth was monitored. Tumor sizes were periodically measured using vernier calipers, and tumor volume was calculated using the formula: 0.52 × width2 × length (mm3). Refer to the previous article for detailed methods32.

Organoid orthotopic transplantation

For the orthotopic model, organoids were collected and aspirated into prechilled 29 G insulin syringes. 6 to 8-week-old nude mice were continuously anesthetized via a nose cone connected to an isoflurane vaporizer. An abdominal incision was made using sterile surgical scissors, and the stomach was gently exteriorized with forceps. Organoids (5–8 × 10⁵ cells in 50 μL Matrigel/PBS suspension) were injected into the submucosal layer of the lower gastric corpus using a 29 G insulin syringe. The stomach was repositioned into the abdominal cavity, and the abdominal wall was closed with 5–0 sutures. Postoperative mice were monitored until fully ambulatory. Mice was monitored weekly.

H&E and immunohistochemistry staining

Tumor tissues and organoids were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde overnight, then subjected to gradient dehydration, embedded in paraffin, and preserved at room temperature. The standard protocol was used for H&E and immunohistochemistry staining. Imaging was performed using a Pannoramic MIDI. The H-score, a semiquantitative assessment of immunohistochemical staining intensity and distribution, was calculated for each tumor sample to evaluate protein expression. The staining intensity was visually graded on a scale of 0 to 3: 0 (Negative or no staining), 1+ (Weak staining), 2+ (Moderate staining), and 3+ (Strong staining). For each intensity grade (0, 1+, 2+, 3+), the percentage of tumor cells exhibiting that specific intensity was estimated. Western blotting

TP and TPA gastric organoid lysates were extracted in RIPA buffer (Beyotime, Cat# P0013) supplemented with protease inhibitors (Beyotime, Cat# P1045), followed by SDS–PAGE electrophoresis and transfer onto PVDF membranes (Millipore). Primary antibodies were applied in 5% non-fat milk and incubated overnight at 4 °C. HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies were applied at a 1:10,000 dilution. Images were obtained using NcmECL Ultra Reagent (NCM Biotech).

RNA extraction and RT-qPCR

Total RNA was extracted from TP, TPA-1, TPA-2, TP-sgScr, TP-sgScin−1, TP-sgScin−2, TPA-sgScr, TPA-sgBrd9-1, and TPA-sgBrd9−2 tumor organoids for RT-qPCR. Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat# 15596026), and complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized using the HiScript-III RT SuperMix kit (Vazyme, Cat# R323-01) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Real-time quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR) was performed using Fast SYBR Green Master Mix (Vazyme, Cat# Q711-02/03) on the StepOne Real-Time PCR System (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Applied Biosystems QuantStudio 3). Muc1, Muc5ac, mouse Scin, and human SCIN gene primers for RT-qPCR are listed in Supplementary Dataset 2.

Organoid proliferation assay

Organoids were digested into single cells using TrypLE™ (GIBCO, Cat# 12605-028). A total of 5000 cells were seeded into each well of a 96-well plate. After 5 days of culture, organoids were photographed using an Olympus IX73P2F. The size of organoids was measured using ImageJ (Version 1.51).

Antibodies

The Recombinant Anti-MUC1 antibody (Cat# ab45167, RRID: AB_776552) was obtained from Abcam; ARID1A/BAF250A (D2A8U) Rabbit mAb (Cat# 12354, RRID: AB_2637010) and E-Cadherin (4A2) Mouse mAb (Cat# 14472, RRID: AB_2728770) were obtained from Cell Signaling Technology. The BRD9 polyclonal antibody (Cat# A303-781A, RRID: AB_11218396) was obtained from Thermo Fisher Scientific.

In vitro treatment

Tumor organoids from TP and TPA mice were dissociated using TrypLE™ and seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 5000 cells per well. After 24 h of culture, the organoids were treated with varying concentrations of dBRD9 (GlpBio, Cat# 2341840-98-8) (0, 0.001, 0.01, 0.1, 1, 10, and 100 μM). After 72 h of treatment, the organoids were photographed and quantified using CCK8. The ratio of signet ring cell organoids to total organoids was calculated from at least three independent areas. Cell viability was determined by normalizing to the DMSO group.

In vivo treatment

TPA tumor organoids was cultured and performed subcutaneous transplantation with 50,000 cells per site. Tumor volume was then monitored using vernier calipers. When the subcutaneous tumors reached approximately 100 mm³, the mice were randomly divided into two groups for intraperitoneal drug administration: one group received vehicle control (10% DMSO + 40% PEG300 + 5% Tween-80 + 45% saline) and the other received the dBRD9 compound (MedChemExpress, Cat# 2170679-45-3) treatment group. Daily dosing was administered at 50 mg/kg, with tumor volume measurements recorded every three days using vernier calipers throughout the treatment period. Mice were sacrificed and analyzed at the indicated time points.

RNA-seq analysis

Total RNA was extracted from gastric TP and TPA tumor organoids, TP tumor organoids (sgScr and sgScin), and TPA tumor organoids treated with vehicle or dBRD9 for transcriptome profiling. RNA-seq libraries were sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform (150-bp paired-end reads). After removing the adapters, ploy-N, and low-quality reads, clean data were aligned against the reference mouse genome (mm10) using STAR_2.6.0. Following this, raw counts were normalized and significance scores. as well as log2-fold change values, were calculated using the DESeq2 (v.1.22.2). The differentially expressed genes were used to perform Gene Ontology enrichment analyses with the R package clusterProfiler (v.3.10.1) (RRID:SCR_016884). Furthermore, Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) was employed to identify significantly enriched pathways. Preranked function was performed to clarify the overall transcriptome similarity between mouse models and tumor tissues of SRCC patients. Data visualization was conducted using the R packages ggpubr, ggplot2, pheatmap, and Vennerable.

Constructing ATAC-seq libraries

Gastric TP and TPA organoids (5 × 104 cells/each group) were harvested to construct ATAC-Seq libraries using a Chromatin Profile Kit (Novoprotein, Cat. N248-01A). 1 mL ice-cold PBS suspended organoids, then centrifuged at 500 × g at 5 min. The supernatant was removed and 50 μL lysis buffer gently resuspended organoids. Tubes were placed on ice for 5 min and added into 950 μL wash buffer, then gently blown 5 times, which was centrifuged at 500 × g for 5 min at 4 °C. The sediment was added into a 40 μL fragmented mixture and transferred to PCR tubes for 30 min at 37 °C. Then 10 μL stop buffer was used to end the reaction for 5 min at 55 °C. The DNA extracted beads were used for purifying fragments. Transposed DNA fragments were amplified using the PCR system from this kit. PCR conditions: 72 °C for 3 min, 98 °C for 30 s, followed by 9–18 cycles of 98 °C for 15 s, 60 °C for 15 s and 72 °C for 10 s, 72 °C for 2 min. Amplified libraries were purified with the DNA Clean Beads.

ATAC-seq analysis

Assay for Transposase-Accessible Chromatin using sequencing (ATAC-seq) was performed on nuclei isolated from TP and TPA gastric organoids to profile genome-wide chromatin accessibility. The Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform was utilized to sequence the library. To remove adapters from the 150 bp paired-end raw data, NGmerge was employed with the option “NGmerge -a -v -n 20”. Bowtie2 software was used to construct an index for the mouse genome (mm10), followed by alignments using the option “–very-sensitive -X2000 -x mm10”. Duplicates were removed using Picard’s MarkDuplicates tool. Mitochondrial genome contamination was eliminated using SAMtools and awk commands, resulting in bam files. Normalized bw files were generated using deepTools with the option “bamCoverage -bs = 1 –normalizeUsing BPM” for subsequent visualization in the Integrated Genome Viewer (IGV). Macs3 hmmratac standard workflow was utilized for peak calling in ATAC-seq. The genomic distribution of accessibility sites was determined using ChIPseeker with the option “annotatePeak”, and the TSS region “tssRegion” was set as (−3000, 3000). The “gappedPeak” files were transformed into GrangesList forms, and the peaks were converted into consensus counts. Data normalization and differential comparison were performed using DESeq2. Regions of differential accessibility were identified for downstream analyses using a p-value cutoff of 0.05 (Wald test).

Constructing CUT&Tag-seq libraries

Gastric TP and TPA organoids (1 × 10⁵ cells/per group) were harvested to construct CUT&Tag libraries. TP and TPA organoids were used for BRD9 and ARID1A CUT&Tag. Organoids were harvested in 1.5 mL tubes for the CUT&Tag assay using the NOvoNGS CUT&Tag 3.0 High-Sensitivity Kit (Novoprotein, Cat. N259-YH01-01A). A total of 10 μL of ConA beads were added to each tube and incubated with cells for 10 min at 25 °C. The supernatant was removed and the primary antibody buffer with the appropriate primary antibody was added and incubated for 2 h at 25 °C or overnight at 4 °C. The supernatant was removed, and the secondary antibody buffer was added and incubated for 1 h at 25 °C. Beads were washed twice. The chromatin was combined with ChiTag™ 2.0 Transposome, then fragmented using Tagmentation Buffer for 1 h at 37 °C. ChIP DNA was purified using DNA Clean Beads and amplified using the PCR system from this kit. PCR conditions: 72 °C for 3 min, 98 °C for 30 s, followed by 10–25 cycles of 98 °C for 15 s, 60 °C for 15 s, and 72 °C for 10 s, then 72 °C for 2 min. Amplified libraries were purified using DNA Clean Beads. The purified ChIP DNA was used for library preparation.

CUT&Tag analysis

CUT&Tag (Cleavage Under Targets and Tagmentation) assays were conducted on chromatin isolated from TP and TPA gastric organoids using antibodies against ARID1A, BRD9, or control IgG. The Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform was employed to perform genomic sequencing. Following quality control by the fastp standard workflow, the Bowtie2 software was utilized to construct an index for the mouse genome (mm10) and align paired-end clean data of 150 bp length using the options “–local –very-sensitive-local –no-unal –no-mixed –no-discordant –phred33 -I 10 -X 700”. The SAMtools program was utilized to convert sam files to sorted bam files. Picard’s MarkDuplicates tool was used to remove duplicates with the option “REMOVE_DUPLICATES = true”. Normalized bw files were generated using deepTools with the option “bamCoverage -bs = 1 –normalizeUsing BPM” for further visualization in the Integrated Genome Viewer (IGV). Peak calling was performed using the MACS2 and MACS3 software with the option “narrow -q 1e-5 -f BAMPE -g mm –keep-dup all”. ChIPseeker was employed to identify the genomic distribution of the peaks, with the TSS region “tssRegion” set as (−3000, 3000) for annotation. The “narrowPeak” files were transformed into GrangesList forms, and the peaks were converted into consensus counts. Data normalization and differential comparison were performed using DESeq2. Regions of differential modification were identified for downstream analyses using a p-value cutoff of 0.05 (two-sided Wald test).

Referred SRCC patient transcriptome data analysis

We analyzed the genetic alteration data of 171 diffuse gastric carcinoma samples (including 144 diffuse-type adenocarcinomas and 27 signet ring cell carcinoma) obtained from the cBioPortal (https://www.cbioportal.org/) dataset. Subsequently, we investigated the occurrence of genetic alterations in ARID1A, TP53, and PTEN, encompassing various types of mutations (truncating, in-frame, missense, and splice site mutations), copy number variations (shallow deletions, deep deletions, gains, and amplifications), as well as structural variations in this dataset of individuals. Transcriptome data and corresponding clinical information of GC patients (SRCC, n = 12; Intestinal, n = 74) were obtained from The Cancer Genome Atlas Stomach Adenocarcinoma (TCGA-STAD) project. Transcriptome data of gastric cancer cell lines were acquired from the Cancer Cell Line Encyclopedia (CCLE) database (SRCC, n = 5; other types, n = 14), microarray data and corresponding Lauren classifications were acquired from GSE26899 (Diffuse-type, n = 21; Intestinal-type, n = 59).

GSEA was performed using the clusterProfiler package for the SRCC/DGC versus ITGC comparison in the TCGA-STAD cohort and for the TPA versus TP comparison in tumor organoids. For organoid RNA-seq data, mouse gene symbols were converted to human orthologs before analysis. Hallmark, C2, and C5 gene sets from MSigDB v7.1 were used. The preranked function from GSEA software was performed to clarify the overall transcriptome similarity between mouse models and tumor samples of GC patients. To define SRCC signature genes, we used the standard DESeq2 pipeline to identify genes significantly upregulated in the combined SRCC and diffuse-type gastric cancer group relative to intestinal-type gastric cancer samples from the TCGA-STAD cohort (P < 0.05 and log2 fold change > 1).

Data visualization

The R packages ggpubr, ggplot2, and ggpaired were used to create box plots to visualize differences in gene expression and chromatin accessibility levels. Heatmaps of differentially expressed genes were generated using the pheatmap package. GO enrichment results were presented using bar plots and dot plots created with ggplot2. DeepTools was used to visualize protein-binding and chromatin accessibility profiles. Matrix computation of protein-binding and accessibility peaks was performed using the “computeMatrix reference-point –referencePoint center -b 3000 - a 3000 - skipZeros” option.

Statistical analysis

Organoid diameter and number assays, tumor measurements, RT-qPCR, morphological statistics, and in vitro treatment were analyzed for statistical significance using two-tailed unpaired parametric Student’s t-tests (Prism 9.0, GraphPad software). The data distribution was assumed to be normal, but this was not formally tested. Statistical test methods, sample sizes, and p-values are indicated in the corresponding figure legends. For the in vitro treatment experiments, all samples were randomly assigned to vehicle or treatment groups. Tumor measurements were performed blindly. The measurements of organoid shapes were analyzed blindly. For other in vivo and in vitro experiments, the researchers were not blinded while performing the experiments. No data were excluded from the study. Statistical significance on RNA-seq, ATAC-seq, and CUT&Tag data was determined using unpaired two-tailed t-test, Wilcoxon rank-sum test, Wald test, hypergeometric distribution, or Likelihood-ratio test. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05. All the quantification and visualization of omics data analysis were performed on R v3.6.
