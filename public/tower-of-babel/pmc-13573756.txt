New York state resistance to federal regression of sexual and reproductive rights
Authors: Danielle Greene, Diana Romero, Holly Farkas
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
As anti-rights actors have consolidated political power in the US, restrictions on sexual and reproductive health and rights have expanded through judicial, legislative, legal, and fiscal mechanisms. Danielle Greene and colleagues examine how New York’s experience illustrates both the potential and the limits of state level resistance

For nearly a quarter of a century, anti-rights forces in the US have consolidated national and local power with the goal of undermining sexual and reproductive health and rights (SRHR). These efforts (in the form of executive orders, Supreme Court decisions, national and local legislation, and discontinuation of funding) restrict access to care, individual choice, bodily autonomy, and quality of care and disrupt the overall public health system nationally and locally. Recently, US health policies have sharply diverged from the scientific evidence supporting SRHR.

Across the US, SRHR advocates and progressive states have initiated local efforts of resistance, including changes to state constitutions and passage of protective state level and local level legislation, defeated referendums proposing SRHR restrictions, and targeted local funding allocations. This paper is part of a collection of articles on the impacts of shifts in the multilateral system on SRHR. Building on the descriptions of anti-rights ideology and the impact on global multilateral governance that this collection offers, we assess changes in US SRHR policy and the mitigation response in New York State. Although New York may be more liberal, better funded, and more organised than other localities experiencing similar threats, the strategies it has used may inform and inspire local efforts elsewhere.

The larger context

The federalist structure of the US government relies on a capitalist approach to social welfare. The US provides a safety net, not a baseline, to pay for healthcare, food, housing assistance, and/or income support for specific vulnerable populations (that is, people who are living with disabilities or severe illness, low income adults, senior citizens, and children). Lack of specific protections for gender rights in the US Constitution, combined with poor enforcement of the equal protection clause for race, resulted in a legal gap through which decades long efforts to restrict SRHR and safety net programmes have succeeded, long before the consolidation of US executive power in 2016. The movement to the far right politically has been supported by an organised approach to placing “reliably conservative” staffers in congressional offices, elected positions, judgeships, and federal appointments. This has culminated in current high level positions for many authors of Project 2025, an anti-rights governance blueprint (fig 1).1 Through this long term strategy, three conservative justices were appointed to the Supreme Court of the United States between 2017 and 2020, which redistributed the political and philosophical balance of the court. This led to the Dobbs decision that ended nationwide protections for abortion, thereby giving decision making power to individual states.

Two hundred and sixty six presidential declarations (executive orders) released between the 2025 inauguration and June 2026 provide further evidence of the long term coordinated anti-rights strategy to unravel SRHR and align with Project 2025. Of these, 26 targeting bodily autonomy, reproductive and economic justice, civil rights, diversity, and sexual orientation and gender identity were issued on Inauguration Day in January 2025.2 National and local judicial, legislative, legal, and fiscal attacks on SRHR, including data suppression and privacy violations, have followed. In response, SRHR advocates, progressive civil society, and likeminded state and local governments are using collective action along with legal, legislative, and fiscal strategies to mitigate these harms and protect SRHR.

Threats and actions

Judicial rulings, legislation, and state constitutions

The ripple effects of the Dobbs decision have caused confusion within and among states’ social and health infrastructure, particularly in the 41 states with varying degrees of abortion restrictions.3 Abortion restrictions are associated with increased infant mortality, worse maternal mental health (itself associated with 20% of maternal mortality), and obstetrics/gynaecology provider shortages.4 5 An example of this confusion is the impact of local interpretations of the federal Emergency Medical Treatment and Labour Act (EMTALA), which requires hospitals to provide stabilising treatment to anyone presenting at an emergency room. Although 2022 guidance confirmed that EMTALA covered abortion, providers in abortion restricted states remain uncertain about what constitutes permissible medical care.6 This has forced continuation of non-viable pregnancies, resulting in permanent uterine damage jeopardising future pregnancies and presenting additional maternal health risks.6

An abortion access state since 1970, New York responded to escalating federal SRHR attacks in 2019 by safeguarding reproductive rights state-wide through the Reproductive Health Act.7 This act made the right to abortion an affirmative right in state health law, as opposed to the previous penal code that made abortion not a crime.8 New York proceeded to codify reproductive rights in its state constitution, resulting in a 2024 amendment protecting the right to abortion and reproductive care by forbidding discrimination against “pregnancy and pregnancy outcomes, and reproductive healthcare and autonomy,” as well as “sexual orientation, gender identity, gender expression.”7 The second clause protects against post-Dobbs restrictions on access to gender affirming care for minors. Federal funding cuts and New York restoration

The 2025 federal funding bill (HR1) suspended all public health insurance payments to providers who perform abortions, including payments for women’s health and primary care, even though the use of federal funds to pay for abortion has been prohibited since 1976.10 Nationally, nearly 500 SRHR providers have closed or are planning to since payments were withheld, exacerbating economic and social challenges particularly for rural and vulnerable populations lacking maternal, sexual, reproductive, and LGBTQ+ healthcare.11 Recent guidelines for competitive family planning funding from the federal government emphasise family formation. This contradicts the 1970 congressional intent for these funds—to support comprehensive sexual and reproductive healthcare—and reflects the executive orders’ restriction of diversity and sexual orientation and gender identity.12 Civil society groups filed joint lawsuits challenging the funding language.12 Civil society groups filed joint lawsuits challenging the new guidelines, as has a 23 state coalition.12 13

A previous disruption in the family planning programme required administrative and physical separation between federally funded services and abortion services. New York reacted by replacing federal funding with state funds to maintain comprehensive family planning services; it later established abortion access centres to connect individuals seeking care with local providers, funding, and insurance enrolment, as well as transportation and housing for people coming from abortion restricted states. One such entity, the NYC Abortion Access Hub, answered 4158 enquiries in its first year.14 When this disruptive rule affecting the family planning funding was overturned, New York accepted new federal funding. However, faced with additional fiscal threats, New York is again replacing federal cuts with state funds.15 In response to these disruptions, New York has sought to maintain continuity in public health data collection at the state and local level.

Data suppression, privacy violations, and shield laws

Funding cuts, executive orders limiting civil rights, and termination of thousands of federal employees, resulting in closure of federal agency offices and programmes have disrupted US health data collection and linkage systems while creating new linkages that violate privacy regulations, target immigrants, and disenfranchise citizens and legal residents.2 16 17 Datasets began disappearing shortly after the 2025 presidential inauguration, along with the removal of thousands of governmental web pages that used “banned words,” such as women, sexuality, pregnant person, gender, female, and LGBTQ+.18 Remaining data sources may have been modified — for example, by changing terminology or categories of gender.19

A clear example is the 2025 cessation of the national Pregnancy Risk Assessment Monitoring System (PRAMS), which has monitored maternal and infant health in partnership with city and state health departments across the US since 1987. PRAMS data have documented geographical and racial/ethnic disparities in maternal mortality within the US, as well as its higher maternal and infant mortality rates compared with other high income nations.20 PRAMS has been credited with reducing infant deaths by approximately 50% in the US between 1987 and 2022.21 The substantially revised PRAMS web pages were recently restored by court order after the federal government was sued by advocates. A banner now appears across the top of each PRAMS web page, and many others, illuminating the ideology influencing the federal public health system:

“Per a court order, HHS is required to restore this website to its version as of 12:00 AM on January 29, 2025. Information on this page may be modified and/or removed in the future subject to the terms of the court’s order and implemented consistent with applicable law. Any information on this page promoting gender ideology is extremely inaccurate and disconnected from truth. The Trump Administration rejects gender ideology due to the harms and divisiveness it causes. This page does not reflect reality and therefore the Administration and this Department reject it.”22

Over time, the lack of data to inform health needs and outcomes will lead to less funding and fewer interventions, exacerbate health inequities, and render certain populations invisible. The NYC Health Department established the Maternal, Infant and Perinatal Survey to replace PRAMS for the 2026 New York City birth cohort. Matching the survey questions to the last completed national data collection (2023) allows comparison and continuity with previous datasets. However, the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention PRAMS has stated that requests for previous data are not being processed.

In addition to suppressing and changing research and surveillance data, both the federal government and abortion restrictive states are actively working to challenge health information privacy laws. Texas and Louisiana filed criminal charges against out-of-state medication abortion and telehealth providers as well as the in-state patients who sought care from them.23 The US government’s federal personnel office is seeking access to current and former employees’ private insurance medical records.17 This could lead to targeting, discrimination, and termination on the basis of employees’ histories of mental health problems, substance use, reproductive health, or gender affirming care seeking.

Twenty two states have enacted provider shield laws to protect against out-of-state criminal charges.24 Eight states have shield laws that are specific to telehealth.24 New legislation enacted in New York in 2026 prevents anti-rights actors from accessing online health information and further strengthens access to medication abortion amid federal efforts to restrict it.15 23 However, privacy protections for electronic health records, provider protections for prescribing medication abortion, and gender affirming care failed to pass (table 1).15 State level responses have increasingly focused on creating legal protections to limit the impact of restrictive policies, although these approaches have important limitations.

Collective action and legal challenges

In the face of federal hostility, SRHR advocates are creating their own infrastructure of coalitions and legal responses to the dismantling of SRHR. 25 Twenty three governors have committed to protect public health and reproductive rights in their states, using the power of non-partisan coalitions (for example, Reproductive Freedom Alliance, Governors Public Health Alliance). Multistate health department collaboratives, such as the nine states and two cities in the Northeast Public Health Collaborative and the four states in the West Coast Health Alliance, aim to ensure coordinated regional approaches to safeguard and protect population health and rights (personal communication). Providers, feeling that “the mere act of providing sexual and reproductive healthcare is an act of defiance,” are joining with SRHR advocates and progressive civil society in resistant partnerships.26

Attorneys general, along with SRHR advocates and progressive civil society organisations, have collectively filed more than 750 lawsuits against the federal government since January 2025.26 As shown by the cases highlighted in this paper, such actions have the potential to pause or reverse a harmful policy. Joining a lawsuit, providing testimony, or submitting an amicus (“friend of the court”) brief by entities not involved in the case allows organisations and individuals to show support. As of June 2026, only 4% of final decisions upheld the administration’s policies.27 Still, lawsuits are expensive, may take years, and can be lost. However, non-governmental legal organisations with missions centred around reproductive rights and justice, such as the Center for Reproductive Rights, the Lawyering Project, If/When/How, and the National Women’s Law Center, are continuing to take states to court to challenge, for example, state level abortion bans on the grounds of unclear exceptions for the health of the pregnant person.

Discussion and conclusions

New York has taken multiple actions to protect SRHR, yet many vulnerable populations remain at risk of harm. Although New York laws and regulations prevent providers from withholding treatment on the basis of gender identity, two local hospital systems discontinued gender affirming youth care when confronted with the federal threat to cease all public insurance payments to them if the services continued.28 A federal attempt to access personal identifying clinical records from these programmes is now being challenged in court. McGovern and colleagues in this collection describe the impact of the multi-year coordinated attack on gender affirming care.29 In the 2025-26 New York State legislative cycle, five bills banning the inclusion of transgender athletes in school sports were introduced and defeated. These bills were not unique; across 43 states, 530 anti-LGBTQ+ bills were introduced in 2026. These have ranged from barriers to accurate IDs, bans on free speech and expression, and gender affirming healthcare restrictions to weakening student/educator rights and civil rights law.30 A new national guidance is under review that would require all federal scientific grants to be approved by political appointees and align with administration priorities. If enacted, these reviews will further diminish the scientific integrity of health research and healthcare for all, with likely disparate effects on vulnerable populations pertaining to SRHR.31

Myriad actions from different sectors of US society demonstrate collective resistance. Although legal and policy related efforts can help to preserve access, their impact is nevertheless constrained by funding limitations, health system capacity, and providers’ ability to sustain service delivery under restrictive conditions. That said, efforts undertaken by varied actors in New York, as described in this paper, can serve as a model for other progressive actors and states. These approaches may not be realistic in more restrictive states and nations; however, the emerging consequences of anti-SRHR policies—such as declining maternal and infant health, provider shortages, and increased risk taking behaviour among young people—may instead be the catalyst for backlash.

Although state based actions might only change the landscape of SRHR in one state, the collective actions through multistate lawsuits or civil society led challenges to federal actions have resulted in pauses or reversals of national policy. Regional efforts such as the Northeast Public Health Collaborative may restore fraying health system infrastructure through collective regional planning and participation in data collection efforts such as the Maternal, Infant and Perinatal Survey.

The cumulative effect of each narrow success builds the potential for larger scale mobilisation and impact. Although the New York actions to preserve and reclaim access to SRHR may demonstrate incremental progress, they map out a path towards what can be increasingly impactful collective action. These small triumphs represent the coalescence of different sectors with a common goal, showing how the power of collaboration between SRHR advocates and progressive civil society can reclaim and preserve SRHR for all people.
