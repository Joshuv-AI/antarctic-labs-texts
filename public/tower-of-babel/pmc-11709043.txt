Assessment of satisfaction of using socially marketed health consumables at government settings among beneficiaries in a rural area of Hooghly District, West Bengal
Authors: Aditi Sur, Bobby Paul, Lina Bandyopadhyay, Monalisha Sahu, Rivu Basu, Ankush Banerjee
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroduction:India is committed to achieving universal health care for all by 2030. The objective of social marketing is to promote public health and its goal is to improve health for all, but there are some challenges like irregular availability, quality issue, inadequacy of marketing causes under- utilization of the government supplied health consumables.Objectives:Present study aims to find out the usage pattern and assess the perceived satisfaction level of beneficiaries of using various socially marketed health consumables at government settings and explore the perception of healthcare personnel regarding barriers to its usage in a rural area of India.Materials and Methods:A community-based, Mixed-Methods study (Convergent parallel design) was done in the service area of the Rural Health Unit and Training Centre (RHU&TC), Singur of Hooghly district, West Bengal, between January 2023 and December 2023, where the quantitative component was carried out by face-to-face interview among 150 beneficiary household respondents selected by two-stage cluster sampling; and qualitative component was done by 4 Key informant interviews (KII) among healthcare personnel (medical officer, public health nurse, pharmacist). Statistical data were analyzed by descriptive statistics using SPSS 16 version and Microsoft Excel for the quantitative part, and thematic analysis was done for the qualitative part. Institutional Ethics Committee clearance was obtained.Results:Ever use of government-supplied contraceptives like Chhaya (Ormeloxifene), Antara (Injectable contraceptive), emergency contraceptive pills, and intrauterine contraceptive devices (IUCD) was done by only 15.3%, 10.7%, 7.3%, 32% of eligible beneficiary households, respectively. The major reasons cited for irregular use were unawareness of availability of the products (Chhaya = 76.3%, Antara = 64.9%, EC pills = 46.7%), poor faith regarding quality (Male condom = 40%, Generic medicines = 70%), inadequate promotion (IUCD = 53.9%). The majority of users of male condoms (90%), IFA tablets (52.9%), ORS (51.1%), and generic medicines (55.7%) were not satisfied after using those products. Key informant interviews among healthcare personnel revealed avoidance of using modern contraceptive methods and frequent unavailability of some socially marketed health consumables were the predominant causes of reduced uptake of those products by beneficiaries.Conclusions:Proper social marketing strategies, adequate promotion, regular awareness campaigns, and tailored training of field healthcare workers are required to improve the acceptability, availability, and marketing of these health consumables.

Introduction

India is embarking on an ambitious target of achieving Universal Health care for all by 2030, which is fundamental to achieving the other Sustainable Development Goals. Universal health coverage ensures all people have access to quality health services including prevention, promotion, treatment, rehabilitation, and palliation without financial hardship. Three essential elements are covered—access, quality, and financial protection.[1] On the other side, socially marketed health consumables are an example of the implementation of social marketing as a public health intervention. Social marketing is the design, implementation, and control of programs calculated to influence the acceptability of social ideas and involving considerations of product planning, pricing, communication, distribution, and marketing research.[2] Through influencing health-related behaviors, promoting the adoption of new health-related products or services, and making changes to social, environmental, economic, and policy factors influencing people’s health, social marketing is targeting to achieve the goal of improved health for all.[23]

In India, various health programs have been launched under National Health Mission like National Rural Health Mission to provide accessible, affordable and quality health care to the rural population, especially the vulnerable groups.[4] Despite significant progress since the launch of NRHM and different health programs, challenges still remain in terms of inadequate accessibility, irregular availabilities of health care services and health consumables, poor quality control and lack of affordability. India’s high out-of-pocket (OOP)-health spending, one of the highest in the world is reflecting those challenges in a comprehensive manner.[1] It is highly concerning that India is one of countries with highest OOPE (48.21%) compared to global average (18.1%) as of 2019. Almost half of all health spending in India is still paid by patients themselves directly at the point of treatment.[5] Though major chunk of socially marketed health consumable are provided free or at minimal cost under the Health Ministry’s largest cashless health insurance scheme—the Ayushman Bharat Jan Arogya Yojana, there is still underutilization of government supplied health consumables that have been contributed to India’s high out of pocket health expenditure.

There is a scarcity of studies that exhaustively depict the causes of underutilization and beneficiaries’ satisfaction status with socially marketed health consumables. The qualitative research component of this study explores the underlying reasons for the underutilization of socially marketed health consumables through key informant interviews among healthcare personnel, revealing nuanced insights and contextual challenges. This qualitative understanding is crucial for identifying barriers and informing targeted interventions. Complementing this, the mixed methods approach integrates quantitative data on usage and satisfaction with qualitative insights on barriers to usage. By combining both methods, this study holistically assesses the usage patterns and satisfaction status of beneficiaries using socially marketed health consumables from government settings and explores the perceptions of healthcare personnel regarding various barriers to their usage in Singur, aiming to enhance their uptake in the rural areas of Hooghly District, West Bengal.

Methodology

Study design

To gain an in-depth understanding of the topic, a community based Mixed method study was conducted using the convergent parallel design. The research process can be symbolized as quantitative and qualitative (QUAN+QUAL; Morse, 1991).

Study setting and participants

This study was conducted from January 2023 to December 2023 in the service area of Rural Health Unit and Training Centre (RHU&TC), Singur of Hooghly District, West Bengal. For quantitative strand, the households having adult beneficiaries, who were residing in the study area for at least 1 year were selected. Household without eligible couple were excluded from this study. The healthcare personnel, that is, medical officers, public health nurse (PHN) and pharmacist providing health care services in RHU&TC for at least 1 year, were selected for key-informant-interviews (KII) for the qualitative strand of the study. Only those who were willing and gave informed written consent were included in this study.

Sample size determination

As there is definite scarcity of studies with holistic approach and majorities of socially marketed health consumables taken in this study have been used in family planning methods, so that, for sample size estimation of the quantitative strand, NFHS-5 data was considered most reliable. According to National Family Health Survey-5 (NFHS-5) data, the prevalence of contraception use in Hooghly district is 74.9%.[6] So sample size for this study was calculated taking P = 0.749, confidence level = 95%, Z value 1.96 and absolute error of precision 10%, using the standard Cochran’s formula, that is, sample size = Z2pq/d2= {(1.96)2 × 0.749 × 0.251}/(0.1)2 = 72.19, that is, 73. After applying the design effect of 2, it came (73 × 2) =146. As cluster sampling technique was done with 15 clusters (primary sampling unit), (146/15) =9.7, that is, 10 households (secondary sampling unit) were selected from each cluster. So, the final sample size was approximately (15 × 10) = 150. The sample size for the qualitative strand was determined based on the data saturation.

Sampling technique

The participants of the quantitative strand were selected via a two-stage cluster sampling technique. In the first stage, 15 villages (primary sampling unit) among 64 villages under RHU&TC were selected via probability proportional to size method, followed by the second stage, where 10 households (secondary sampling unit) were selected from each cluster by simple random sampling. In a simple-random technique, the houses were selected by a random number table. From each selected household, the head of the family was approached for an interview. If the head of the family was not a member of an eligible couple, then the particular questions, regarding the usage pattern of the family planning products were asked to a member of an eligible couple of that same family. In households with more than one eligible couple, only the couple with a longer duration of marriage was included in this study.

For the qualitative strand of the study, four healthcare personnel, that is, one medical officer, two public health nurses (PHN) and one pharmacist, providing healthcare services in RHU&TC, Singur for at least 1 year, were selected via purposive sampling.

Simultaneously, for the qualitative strand of the study, four key informant interviews (KIIs) were conducted among the healthcare personnel providing healthcare services, that is, medical officer (K1 = working for 7 years in RHU&TC), public health nurses (K2 = working for 8 years in RHU&TC, K3 = working for 5 years in RHU&TC) and pharmacist (K4 = working for 3 years in RHU&TC) regarding their perspective on the barriers to the use of socially marketed health consumables among beneficiaries. The participants were interviewed using a predesigned pre-tested semi-structured key informant interview guide. All KIIs were audio-recorded with prior consent obtained from the participants. Data collection was done till the point of data saturation. The time duration for each interview was around 20 minutes.

Data analysis

Quantitative data was analyzed using Microsoft Excel 2016 and Statistical Package for Social Sciences software (version 16). Descriptive statistics for the predictor variables and the outcome variables (satisfaction status of using socially marketed health consumables among beneficiaries) were shown by bar diagram, frequency table, and mean.

Qualitative data was analyzed with the help of thematic analysis. Codes were used to extract information from the KIIs. The assessment was done regarding the use of socially marketed health consumables among beneficiaries from the transcripts of the recorded KIIs. The responses were coded based on the guides developed before. Simultaneously, the field notes were also considered side by side to put the codes in the appropriate context. Similar codes were put together and themes were generated.

The qualitative and quantitative strands of the study were integrated at the reporting and interpretation levels. Here, we have tried to merge the quantitative and qualitative data by directly comparing the quantitative statistical results and qualitative findings. Permission was taken from the Institutional Ethics Committee. Informed written consent was taken from each participant before data collection and confidentiality was maintained throughout the process.

Results

Among the 150 household respondents, the mean age of the study participants was 40.43 years (±12.85 years). More than half respondents were female (52.7%). Majority of them were Hindu (70%), and married (73.3%). Regarding socioeconomic status, (income calculated using BG Prasad scale, CPI = 125.1, for January 2022), one-third of them belong to class III (37.3%) followed by class IV (30%).[7] Approximately one-third of respondents had completed secondary education (32.7%).

Discussion

This mixed method study aimed to assess the satisfaction status of beneficiaries of using socially marketed health consumables from government settings and the related perception of healthcare personnel involved in healthcare services. As there is a lack of published studies that have depicted the wholesome picture in a single frame, but, after an intensive literature search, it has been found that there are few individual studies available, which reflect similar findings.

Our study showed that only 10.7% of eligible beneficiaries had ever used Antara (an injectable contraceptive), whereas experiencing side effects after using it, had been reported as a predominant cause of dissatisfaction. The major perceived causes of not using it were unaware of availability and inadequate promotion. A study done by Bairagya A et al.[8] in Nadia district West Bengal, showed quite similar findings, that only one-third of the study population used injectable contraceptives, and side effects like irregular menstrual symptoms was the main cause for dissatisfaction. In the conclusion part, the author also explained the importance of social marketing and the role of frontline health workers in promoting the use of injectable contraceptive methods. Quite similar findings were obtained from the study done by Donta B et al.,[9] that explained, the most common reason for discontinuing oral pills, and intrauterine devices was due to side effects.

A study done by Ajayi AI et al.[10] supports the findings of our study that poor faith and the unavailability of condoms were the main reasons for the inconsistent use of condoms. Our study also reported that poor faith regarding the quality of condoms was one of the predominant reasons for not using them.

The present study shows ever use of emergency contraceptive pills was done by only 7.3% of beneficiaries and unaware of availability, and poor supply was identified as a major cause of underutilization of EC pills. A study done by Sahu M et al.[11] in a rural area of Delhi reflect similar findings, that ever use of emergency contraception was 9.7% and lack of knowledge about EC pills among study participants were identified as a major cause of lack of use.

In this study, majority of ORS (44.5%) users were not satisfied due to irregular availabilities followed by unattractive packaging, poor quality, and side effects. A study done by Gogoi M et al.[12] in Assam showed 55% of mothers strongly agreed that side effects and poor taste of ORS are some barriers towards the practice of ORS. A study done by Fagbamigbe AF et al.[13] also revealed unavailability, unaffordability, and poor awareness are the topmost barriers to utilization of ORS.

Our study shows that majority of IFA tablet users (75%) were not satisfied due to irregular availabilities followed by unattractive packaging and side effects. A community-based mixed method study done by Mishra JP et al.[14] showed that the main reasons for nonadherence to the IFA supplementation were unavailability of the supplements, side effects, and lack of awareness.

The present study also shows that the majority of users of EC pills (41.3%), male condoms (59.3%), and ORS (54.7%) had procured it from the chemist/private, which reflects high out-of-pocket (OOP)- health spending among beneficiaries.

The findings from the qualitative part of our study also strengthen our quantitative findings. Both the qualitative and quantitative parts of our study had convergent findings. It is the responsibility of the health care system to organize awareness generation activities about socially marketed health consumables. But our study showed that there were many gaps in the health care system itself that holds it back from executing its work effectively. Lack of adequate promotion and irregular awareness programs led to the up-growth of poor knowledge and misconception among beneficiaries. A study done by Ochako R et al.[15] showed similar findings that lack of awareness and knowledge about modern contraceptive methods, fear of side effects, and adverse reactions of modern contraceptive methods were the major barriers to use.

Our study also explores the unavailability or stock out of health products and inequitable distribution that contributes to India’s high OOP-health spending.[1] A study done by Ezezika O et al.[16] revealed that lack of ORS supply, poor distribution, low awareness of ORS usage, and poor training of health workers limit the use of ORS. A few of recent articles reported about frequent drug shortage or stock- out at government hospitals, which leads to delay in achieving WHO’s target of universal healthcare.[1718]

From the present study, stigma, lack of source of information and low education level were identified as the major barriers to use of these socially marketed health consumables. A study done by Donta B et al.[9] showed identical findings that, the most significant barriers to condom use were, embarrassment in collecting condom and social stigma. Similar findings were obtained from the study done by Mishra N et al.,[19] which showed the presence of stigma, shame, fear and misconceptions regarding the use of IUD rooting from the lack of knowledge and poor service quality also limits its adoption by women. Another study done by Sanneving L et al.[20] also revealed that contraceptives are less used by illiterate women compared to more educated women, also supports the findings of our study. Among this study’s limitations, most of the responses were recall-based, so bias might be possible, and the sample size was small. Additionally, beneficiaries were not included in the key-informant interviews.

Conclusion

The lack of awareness, poor knowledge among beneficiaries regarding the use of those socially marketed health consumables, inadequate promotion, inequitable distribution, and frequent stock-out of a few socially marketed health consumables are reported in this study. So, proper marketing strategies, health education, and information-education-communication are needed to improve the utilization of socially marketed health consumables. To ensure the regular availability and equitable distribution system, good inventory of the products-timely indent, and maintenance of a buffer stock is also needed. The regular awareness campaigns at the community level for motivation and counselling regarding the use of modern contraceptive methods and combating the stigma is the utmost necessity. Primary health care serves as a vital platform for health education, awareness promotion, and equitable access to essential products. Leveraging its infrastructure, social marketing can effectively reach target populations, bridge knowledge gaps, and distribute health consumables, empowering communities for informed decision-making and improved public health outcomes. Apart from this, tailored training of field health care workers and finally, periodic in-built evaluation of every tire of service delivery system and consistent administrative surveillance at the policy-making level is required for improving the quality, availability, and marketing of the socially marketed health consumables to make the public health delivery system fully functional and accountable to the community.
