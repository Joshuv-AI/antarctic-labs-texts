A staged vision-force collaborative framework for precision robotic insertion of metallic valve components
Authors: Teng Wu, Dong Zhang, Bin Liu, Huan Wu, Lingen Ren
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Precision robotic insertion of metallic valve components remains challenging because specular reflection, depth loss, and partial occlusion degrade visual localization near the hole entrance, while contact further reduces the reliability of vision. The resulting residual pose errors often cause impact, side loading, jamming, and insertion failure. To address this issue, we propose a staged vision–force collaborative framework (SVFC) for precision insertion of metallic valve components. The key idea of SVFC is to treat metallic insertion as a sensing-reliability handover process: vision is used to compress the global pose error into a force-recoverable local neighborhood, while force feedback subsequently takes over for residual centering and contact regulation. The framework divides the process into three sequential phases: vision-guided coarse alignment, force-guided residual pose correction, and compliant insertion under variable-parameter admittance control. During residual correction, the joint held by the gripper is driven along a local Archimedean spiral trajectory within the vision-defined neighborhood, and centering is determined using a convergence criterion based on the root-mean-square values of lateral forces and torques. During insertion, virtual stiffness and damping are adjusted online according to contact state to suppress transient impact and mitigate jamming under different fit conditions. Experiments on representative clearance-fit and interference-fit tasks show that the proposed method improves insertion success rate, reduces insertion time, and lowers peak contact forces compared with baseline methods, indicating that explicit coordination between vision-dominant alignment and force-dominant contact regulation is beneficial for metallic insertion.

Introduction

Precision insertion (peg-in-hole / insertion) is a core operation in advanced manufacturing systems and is widely encountered in aerospace, energy equipment, industrial valve assembly, and complex electromechanical products1–4. As manufacturing systems continue to evolve toward higher consistency, reliability, and autonomy, robotic assembly is shifting from conventional rigid execution to intelligent assembly paradigms that integrate perception, decision-making, and compliant control. For rigid metallic parts such as valve components, assembly tolerances are becoming increasingly tight, and even sub-millimeter or sub-degree pose deviations can induce impact, side loading, jamming, or outright failure at the hole-entry stage5. Achieving efficient and stable centering and insertion under safe contact conditions therefore remains a central challenge in precision robotic insertion.

From a theoretical perspective, insertion is not merely a position-tracking problem; rather, it is a quasi-static interaction process jointly governed by geometric constraints, friction, and contact stability6. Classical studies showed that compliant support can markedly increase tolerance to initial misalignment, whereas impedance control, formal descriptions of compliant motion, and assembly-oriented compliance control established the methodological basis for regulating robot–environment interaction under constraints7–9. More broadly, contact modes and geometric constraints are decisive for manipulation outcomes, implying that once the task becomes contact-dominated, position accuracy or single-modality perception alone is usually insufficient to ensure stable assembly10.

Before contact, vision remains one of the most effective means for object recognition, 6D pose estimation, and coarse alignment. The theory of visual servoing provides a general closed-loop framework for error-driven adjustment based on image features or pose estimates. Subsequent advances, including DeepIM11, deep pose estimation trained with synthetic data12, Pix2Pose13, and the BOP benchmark14, have improved the accuracy, robustness, and comparability of 6D pose estimation in complex scenes. For insertion tasks, multi-view vision15, YOLO with layout features16, and learning-based visual servoing have been applied to hole localization, connector alignment, and high-precision peg-in-hole assembly17. In addition to learning-based methods, classical computer vision techniques such as feature-based detection18, contour extraction and template matching19 have also been widely used for object localization and alignment in structured industrial environments. However, for metallic valve components, illumination variations, specular reflection, local depth loss, partial occlusion, and camera–robot calibration errors often leave non-negligible residual pose errors in the pre-insertion neighborhood. Once contact occurs, vision becomes even less informative about the true contact state and hole-wall constraints. In this work, these effects are treated as application-level challenges rather than as independent vision problems.

Accordingly, force/torque sensing and compliant control are typically introduced during the contact phase to compensate for the limitations of vision. Previous studies have shown that force signals can more directly reflect the evolution of contact constraints and residual misalignment, from learning compliant assembly motions from demonstration and adapting manipulation skills to reference force profiles to using time-series force information for state recognition and hole finding in inclined-hole insertion20–23. At the control level, variable impedance24, variable admittance25, and learning-based impedance policies26 have been widely explored to reduce contact impact, suppress jamming, and improve insertion efficiency. In parallel, piecewise strategies, decoupled control, and trajectory optimization for high-precision insertion have also reported promising results27,28. Nevertheless, under practical assembly conditions, friction disturbances, variations in fit types, and rapid switching between contact states can still cause fixed-parameter controllers or strategies that rely only on local force directions to misclassify contact or converge unreliably.

Given the complementarity of vision before contact and force sensing during contact, vision–force collaboration has become an important direction for precision insertion. Prior studies have fused visual and force information for batch precision assembly, autonomous industrial assembly, and insertion under uncertainty, demonstrating the value of multimodal collaboration for robust assembly29,30. More broadly, recent studies on robotic positioning and robot-side accuracy enhancement have also highlighted the importance of metrology-aware sensor fusion, adaptive calibration, and residual error compensation for improving robotic precision and robustness in practical industrial settings31,32. For residual error correction and contact search, contact-state-aware search strategies and partial spiral force trajectories indicate that the coordinated design of state recognition, search, and insertion critically affects centering efficiency and assembly success33,34. More recently, ForceVLA35 has further highlighted the importance of force as a primary modality in contact-rich manipulation, especially under occlusion and dynamic uncertainty.

At a broader system level, perception and control modules for robotic assembly are often organized within modular software architectures that support the integration of sensing, planning, and control components. However, such system-level frameworks primarily address software orchestration and module interoperability, rather than the task-level sensing handover problem that arises when metallic insertion transitions from a vision-dominant pre-contact regime to a force-dominant contact regime.

Therefore, the novelty of this work lies in the task-oriented coordination mechanism that explicitly organizes this sensing handover. The visual stage compresses global pose errors into a force-recoverable neighborhood, after which force-guided spiral centering and contact-state-dependent variable-admittance control complete the insertion. This differs from conventional sequential integration strategies.

To address these issues, we propose a staged vision–force collaborative framework (SVFC) for precision insertion of metallic valve components. Unlike conventional multimodal insertion pipelines that mainly concatenate visual localization and compliant control, the proposed framework is organized around the sensing handover problem that arises near reflective metallic hole entrances. Specifically, the visual stage is not intended to achieve final insertion-ready precision, but to compress the global pose error into a local force-recoverable neighborhood in which contact cues become more reliable than vision. Within this neighborhood, residual centering error is further reduced through force-guided Archimedean spiral exploration, and the final insertion is completed by contact-state-dependent variable-admittance regulation. In this way, SVFC establishes a physically grounded coordination mechanism between visual perception and force feedback, thereby improving contact safety, insertion robustness, and execution efficiency.

It should be noted that, in this work, the term “metallic” implicitly involves practical visual degradation effects such as specular reflection, local depth loss, and partial occlusion near the hole entrance. We do not treat reflectivity as an independent vision problem or claim that the visual detector alone resolves these effects. Instead, the proposed framework focuses on reducing the dependence on vision near contact by transferring the dominant role from vision to force feedback.

Specifically, this work makes three contributions. First, we reformulate reflective metallic insertion as a sensing-reliability handover problem and develop a staged vision–force collaborative framework in which visual perception performs global-to-local error compression, while force feedback takes over for residual centering and contact regulation after the system enters a bounded force-recoverable neighborhood near the hole entrance. Second, we propose a force-guided residual centering strategy that performs bounded local exploration through an Archimedean spiral trajectory and determines near-axis alignment using force/torque convergence cues, thereby improving the reliability of contact-stage correction under reflective and partially occluded conditions. Third, we develop a contact-state-dependent variable-admittance insertion strategy that regulates compliance differently across contact phases, so as to suppress transient impact, mitigate jamming risk, and maintain stable insertion under both clearance-fit and interference-fit conditions.

Methods

This section describes the principle and implementation of SVFC. To address severe reflection, visual degradation near the hole entrance, and the difficulty of reliably eliminating residual pose errors once contact occurs, the insertion process is decomposed into three consecutive phases: vision-guided coarse alignment, force-guided residual pose error correction, and compliant insertion under variable-parameter admittance control. Each phase is associated with a dominant sensing modality, a control objective, and a switching criterion, allowing the system to adaptively allocate the roles of vision and force feedback as task uncertainty evolves. In the implementation, all comparative experiments were conducted under a unified set of detector, calibration, search, and control configurations. Specifically, the visual detector was trained offline using reflective valve images collected under the experimental illumination conditions, and the eye-to-hand camera-to-robot transformation was calibrated before the experiments and kept fixed throughout all trials. The PBVS controller was executed with a fixed gain and iteration limit, while the spiral search was performed in the local insertion plane with a constant step size and sliding-window RMS evaluation of force/torque signals. In addition, contact-state switching and admittance parameter adjustment followed predefined thresholds and scheduling logic that remained unchanged across all experiments.

Overall structure of the collaborative assembly framework

Precision insertion tasks exhibit distinctly different sources of uncertainty across different execution phases. Under non-contact or weak-contact conditions, global pose error dominates the alignment accuracy. In contrast, during the contact and insertion stages, local geometric misalignment, frictional effects, and the evolution of contact states become the primary factors influencing the process. Based on this observation, this study proposes a staged vision–force collaborative insertion method (SVFC), whose core idea is to dynamically allocate the dominant roles of visual perception and force feedback at different stages of the task.

Staged insertion process in the SVFC framework.

As illustrated in Fig. 1, the SVFC insertion process is divided into three sequential stages: a vision-guided coarse alignment stage, a force-guided residual pose correction stage, and a compliant insertion and stability control stage. At the early stage of insertion, the global pose information provided by visual perception is used to guide the robot gripper to rapidly approach the target assembly region. When visual accuracy becomes limited and the joint tip enters the neighborhood of the hole entrance, force feedback is introduced to correct the residual pose error. During the final insertion phase, a compliant control strategy is employed to achieve stable and reliable insertion under continuous contact constraints.

Force-guided residual correction stage

After vision-guided coarse alignment, millimeter-level residual pose deviations may still exist between the joint and the valve-body hole due to camera measurement noise and the accumulation of robot calibration errors. Such errors are difficult to further eliminate at the vision level, yet they can induce eccentric loading, jamming, or insertion failure during the contact phase. Therefore, before compliant insertion, we introduce a force-guided residual correction stage, whose workflow is shown in Fig. 4. After initial contact between the joint tip and the hole entrance, a wrist-mounted six-axis force/torque sensor is used to perceive contact features such as lateral forces and overturning moments, thereby inferring axis offset and orientation errors. An Archimedean-spiral blind-area search is then executed within the neighborhood of the vision-specified pre-insertion pose, enabling online reduction of residual local alignment errors and fine centering at the hole entrance.

In a local planar coordinate frame established with the hole center obtained from vision-guided coarse alignment as the origin, the robot gripper drives the joint to perform small-amplitude search motions along an Archimedean spiral.

It should be emphasized that the Archimedean spiral search in this study is a planar local centering strategy rather than a full six-degree-of-freedom pose search. The purpose of the preceding vision-guided coarse alignment stage is to reduce both translational and angular misalignment to a bounded neighborhood before contact. Within this neighborhood, the dominant remaining source of insertion failure is the lateral centering error at the hole entrance. Therefore, the force-guided search is implemented only in the local insertion plane, while the residual angular error is assumed to remain sufficiently small for subsequent compliant insertion.

Compliant insertion and stable control stage

After the residual pose correction stage is completed, the joint has been guided to the vicinity of the hole entrance and reaches a near-axis alignment state. However, due to assembly tolerances and the structural compliance of the gripping mechanism, small residual pose errors may still exist during the insertion process. Therefore, in the insertion stage, a compliant insertion strategy is constructed, consisting of contact constraint modeling, contact state identification and stage switching, and variable-parameter admittance control. This strategy enables the robot gripper-joint assembly to adaptively adjust its pose under continuous contact constraints, thereby achieving a stable and reliable insertion process.

Based on the above model, the external force measured by the wrist force/torque sensor during insertion can be primarily interpreted as the hole-wall reaction induced by the total lateral compression. It should be noted that this simplified contact model is mainly valid under low-speed quasi-static insertion conditions, where inertial effects remain negligible and residual angular misalignment is small. Similar quasi-static approximations relating lateral misalignment to contact force have been commonly adopted in peg-in-hole insertion and compliant assembly studies. However, under large angular errors, strong frictional effects, or high-speed contact, the interaction may involve multi-point contact and dynamic effects, which are not fully captured by the present model.

Variable-admittance compliant insertion control

During the insertion contact stage, the direction and magnitude of the lateral contact force reflect the direction and magnitude of the current axis misalignment, and can therefore be used to guide the adjustment of the gripper-joint assembly pose. Based on this principle, admittance control is adopted to map the external contact force to a pose correction of the TCP of the gripper-joint assembly, thereby achieving compliant insertion based on force feedback. The admittance model used in this study is defined as Eq. (17):17\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$M\left( t \right)\mathop x\limits^{{\cdot \cdot }} +B\left( t \right)\mathop x\limits^{\cdot } +K\left( t \right)\mathop x\limits^{{}} ={F_{ext}}$$\end{document}

Here x represents the pose deviation of the TCP of the gripper-joint assembly, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathop x\limits^{\cdot }$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathop x\limits^{{\cdot \cdot }}$$\end{document} denote the velocity and acceleration, respectively, Fext is the external contact force measured by the force/torque sensor, and M(t), B(t) and K(t) represent the time-varying virtual mass, damping, and stiffness parameters, respectively. In practical control, the insertion process mainly involves low-speed quasi-static motion. Therefore, the inertial term can be neglected and Eq. (17) can be simplified to Eq. (18):

By decomposing the TCP motion of the gripper-joint assembly into the insertion plane, the lateral pose adjustment relationship can be obtained as Eq. (19):19\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\dot {x}_ \bot }=B_{ \bot }^{{ - 1}}\left( t \right)\left( {{F_{ext}} - {K_ \bot }\left( t \right){x_ \bot }} \right)$$\end{document}

From this formulation, it can be observed that the lateral correction motion of the gripper-joint assembly is mainly determined by the measured contact force direction. Under the constraint of the hole wall, the system generates pose corrections along the direction that reduces the alignment error, thereby suppressing jamming and impact. Furthermore, the parameters B(t) and K(t) are dynamically adjusted according to the contact state identified online.

Experiment

In this section, a series of experiments were conducted to verify the effectiveness of the proposed staged vision–force collaborative framework (SVFC). The experimental evaluation was carried out from three aspects: the stability of visual coarse alignment, the capability of residual pose error correction, and the overall insertion performance. First, under different specular reflection conditions, the proposed visual coarse localization method was compared with PCR-PoseNet39, ContourPose40, and P2HNet17. Under identical PBVS control parameters, the coarse localization accuracy, convergence time, and success rate were analyzed to evaluate the influence of visual pose estimation on closed-loop visual servoing performance. Second, within the pre-insertion neighborhood after visual coarse alignment, an Archimedean spiral blind search strategy based on force feedback was introduced. The proposed method was compared with random search, circular search paths, and a contact-force-gradient-based correction method41. The lateral force/torque magnitude and search time were recorded to quantify the efficiency of residual error correction and the stability of the contact process. Finally, ablation experiments were conducted on two representative valve assembly tasks with clearance fit and interference fit. The assembly success rate, maximum insertion force, and average insertion time were recorded to comprehensively evaluate the performance of SVFC in precision insertion tasks.

Experimental apparatus

The architecture of the experimental system is shown in Fig. 7(a). The experimental platform employs a GCR5 robotic manipulator with a repeatability of ± 0.02 mm. A six-axis force/torque sensor (FA7155A) was mounted on the robot wrist. The sensor has a force capacity of 70 N (torque capacity of 5 N·m), with a nonlinearity of 0.5% and hysteresis of 0.5%. A PGC-140 parallel gripper was installed at the end of the force/torque sensor. The gripper has a positioning repeatability of ± 0.03 mm, a maximum vertical static load of 300 N, and a permissible moment load of 7 N·m. The vision system consists of a Gemini-Pro binocular depth camera and a white light source. The illumination source provides two brightness modes: Normal and High. All algorithms were implemented in Python 3.9 and executed on a workstation equipped with an Intel(R) Core(TM) i7-10750 H CPU @ 2.60 GHz.

The selected valve bodies and joints, as well as their fitting relationships, are representative of typical valve assembly components. Detailed dimensional information is listed in Table 2. The upper edge of the valve hole has a chamfer of 0.1 mm, and the nominal hole depth is 3 mm. Both the valve body and the joint are manufactured from 45 steel (Fig. 8).

Experimental evaluation of the force-guided residual error correction strategy

In this section, the force-guided residual correction strategy is evaluated empirically through contact observables rather than through an independently measured geometric capture boundary. Specifically, the experiments examine whether representative residual offsets within the PBVS handover neighborhood can be driven toward a force-based centering condition with reduced lateral force and overturning moment responses. Therefore, this validation should be interpreted as evidence of local recoverability under the tested conditions, rather than as a metrology-grade derivation of the exact admissible capture boundary.

To verify the compatibility of the proposed Archimedean spiral force-guided search strategy with the overall framework, comparative experiments were conducted under identical visual alignment conditions. The proposed method was compared with three baseline strategies: random search, circular radial expansion search, and a gradient-based correction method using the contact force direction.

The experiments were performed using Valve (A) and Joint (a). Three groups of initial lateral and orientation errors were considered: ±1 mm, ± 1°, ± 2 mm, ± 1°, and ± 3 mm, ± 1°. For each group of initial errors, the residual error correction process was repeated 10 times. During the search process, the joint tip maintained slight axial contact with the valve hole entrance surface. Each method executed its corresponding search trajectory within the insertion plane until the unified force-based centering criterion was satisfied. The root-mean-square (RMS) magnitudes of the lateral contact force and lateral torque, denoted as Flat, rms and Mlat, rms, respectively, were recorded to evaluate the reliability of centering and the safety of the contact interaction.

This empirical observation is consistent with the geometric properties of the Archimedean spiral trajectory, which provides deterministic coverage while avoiding over-reliance on instantaneous force-direction estimation under noisy early-contact conditions.

When the system detects a rapid increase in insertion force or an obstruction of depth advancement (stage Sd), the virtual stiffness K is further decreased and the damping B is increased to weaken the contact constraint and reduce the risk of oscillation. After recovery, the process enters stage Se, where the parameters stabilize to support continued insertion.

These results demonstrate that SVFC reduces the peak axial insertion force by adaptively adjusting the virtual stiffness and damping parameters. Consequently, the insertion process becomes more compliant, significantly reducing potential damage to the workpiece caused by abrupt force variations.

At the same time, SVFC also exhibited the lowest mean peak insertion force and smaller force dispersion, which indicates that the proposed staged strategy improves not only insertion reliability and efficiency, but also contact stability during the insertion process.

In comparison, OV shows a significant decrease in success rate in both tasks, indicating that relying solely on visual coarse alignment cannot reliably eliminate residual pose errors and often leads to jamming. Although VF maintains a relatively high success rate, the insertion time is longer, suggesting that force guidance without a complete search-and-compliance strategy is insufficient for stable insertion. VFF improves the success rate and shortens the insertion time because it includes visual coarse alignment, force-guided search, and fixed-parameter admittance control. Therefore, VFF can be regarded as the strongest ablation baseline.

Compared with VFF, SVFC reduced the mean insertion time from 2.7 s to 1.1 s in Group A and from 3.3 s to 2.3 s in Group B, corresponding to reductions of 59.3% and 30.3%, respectively. The mean peak insertion force was also reduced from 5.3 N to 3.0 N in Group A and from 8.5 N to 4.5 N in Group B, corresponding to reductions of 43.4% and 47.1%, respectively. These results indicate that the contact-state-dependent variable-admittance strategy further improves insertion efficiency and contact safety beyond the improvement obtained by force-guided search alone. The success-rate comparison should be interpreted cautiously because of the limited number of trials and the small number of failures; nevertheless, SVFC achieved successful insertion in all tested trials under both fit conditions. Consistent with the insertion-stage analysis in Sect. 3.4, the proposed method also exhibits reduced lateral contact loading during insertion, further supporting the claim that the state-dependent admittance strategy suppresses jamming-related off-axis interaction, rather than merely reducing axial force peaks.

Discussion

Through multiple groups of experiments, the proposed SVFC framework shows improved performance in error convergence, contact safety, and insertion efficiency under the tested metallic valve insertion conditions. These improvements are mainly attributed to the explicit handover from vision-dominant coarse alignment to force-dominant residual centering and contact-state-dependent compliant insertion.

In the visual coarse alignment stage, the combination of ROI depth restoration and PCA-based axis estimation significantly improves stability and robustness of the estimated axis direction under challenging illumination and surface conditions. As a result, PBVS can more easily enter its effective convergence region, thereby providing more reliable initial conditions for the subsequent force-guided correction stage. These results indicate that the engineering robustness of the vision stage directly affects the upper performance limit of the overall collaborative strategy.

For residual pose error correction, the Archimedean spiral blind search combined with a mechanical convergence criterion enables a transition from passive force response to active geometric exploration. Experimental results show that, compared with random or non-search strategies, the proposed approach exhibits greater stability in both search efficiency and suppression of lateral contact forces. This observation suggests that bounded search under visual constraints can effectively reduce the risks caused by contact uncertainty.

During the insertion stage, the introduction of variable-parameter admittance control based on contact-state identification allows the system to achieve a dynamic balance between impact suppression and insertion efficiency. Compared with fixed-parameter strategies, the proposed method significantly reduces the peak insertion force and improves the success rate under interference-fit conditions. These results verify the necessity of adaptive compliant regulation during the insertion of the joint into the valve hole.

An important practical consideration is the trade-off between robustness and execution time. Although the proposed SVFC framework introduces a staged structure involving visual coarse alignment, force-guided residual correction, and compliant insertion, the additional complexity is mainly at the control and coordination level, rather than in hardware. The same robot, RGB-D sensor, and force/torque sensor are used throughout the process, and no additional sensing modalities are required.

Experimental results in Table 9 show that, despite the multi-stage structure, SVFC achieves shorter average insertion times compared with simplified variants such as VFF. This is mainly because the force-guided residual correction stage reduces misalignment before deep insertion, thereby avoiding repeated jamming, excessive contact forces, and recovery motions that would otherwise increase overall execution time.

From a practical perspective, the proposed staged strategy is particularly beneficial in scenarios involving reflective metallic components, close-range visual degradation, tight tolerances, or interference fits, where direct insertion based on visual alignment or fixed compliance is prone to failure or instability. In contrast, in highly structured environments with reliable visual localization and larger clearances, simpler insertion strategies may still be preferable due to their lower implementation complexity.

Overall, the proposed framework improves the success rate, insertion efficiency, and contact stability of precision insertion tasks involving representative metallic valve components under the tested conditions. However, the generality of the framework should be interpreted with caution because the present experiments were conducted on a limited set of valve structures, fit types, and illumination settings.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the force-guided residual correction stage was evaluated mainly through contact observables, including lateral force and overturning moment, rather than through independent post-correction geometric measurement of the residual pose. Therefore, the recoverability demonstrated in this study should be interpreted as empirical local recoverability under the tested conditions. Second, the Archimedean spiral search was implemented in the local insertion plane and did not perform a full six-degree-of-freedom pose search. Its effectiveness therefore relies on the preceding vision-guided stage to reduce the angular error to a bounded range before contact. Third, the contact-state thresholds and admittance scheduling parameters were empirically selected and kept fixed in the experiments. Although this design supports fair comparison among methods, adaptive threshold optimization may be required for broader industrial deployment. Finally, stronger hole-entry non-planarity, burrs, asymmetric chamfers, and local surface defects were not systematically investigated and may bias the force-based centering cues.

Future work will address these limitations by introducing independent measurement of post-correction residual pose errors, adaptive threshold and admittance-parameter optimization, richer local contact modeling, and broader validation across more diverse metallic assembly geometries and surface conditions.

Conclusion

This study addressed positioning errors and contact uncertainty in robotic precision insertion of reflective metallic valve components by proposing a staged vision–force collaborative framework (SVFC). The central idea of the framework is to coordinate the sensing handover from vision-dominant coarse alignment to force-dominant residual centering and contact-state-dependent compliant insertion.

Experimental results under representative clearance-fit and interference-fit conditions show that SVFC improves insertion success rate, shortens insertion time, and reduces peak insertion force compared with the tested baseline methods. These results suggest that explicitly coordinating visual alignment, force-guided local correction, and variable-admittance insertion is beneficial for reflective metallic insertion tasks under the tested conditions.

The current framework still relies on empirically selected thresholds, assumes low-speed quasi-static contact, and has been validated mainly on representative metallic valve components under limited fit and illumination settings. In addition, the force-guided local correction stage was evaluated mainly through contact-force observables rather than independent post-correction geometric measurements. Future work will focus on adaptive parameter optimization, direct measurement of residual pose after force-guided correction, richer contact modeling, and broader validation under more diverse hole-entry geometries, surface qualities, and industrial assembly conditions.
