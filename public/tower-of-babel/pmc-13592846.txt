An optical fiber caffeine sensor using a molecularly imprinted polymethacrylic acid membrane coated on optical fiber surface as a transducer
Authors: Josiah W Miller, Jason D McCaslin, Shiquan Tao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCaffeine is a neurotransmitter, important to human health. This compound exists in many food products, has been added to soft drinks, pharmaceutical drugs. For many applications, a field deployable technique for quick detecting caffeine is attractive. In this paper, we report a selective optical fiber caffeine sensor using a molecularly imprinted polymer (MIP) membrane as a transducer. The membrane extracts/concentrate caffeine from sample solutions significantly improved sensor’s sensitivity with a 95 times sensitivity improvement. The sensor achieved a detection limit of 2.0 μg/mL. This sensor was used to directly analyze caffeine in soft drink products without interference from sample matrix.Keywords: Caffeine, Molecularly imprinted polymer, Optical fiber caffeine sensor, Polymethacrylic acid, Soft drink analysis

1. Introduction

Caffeine is a compound associated with our daily life. This compound is a neurotransmitter and keeps people staying awake [1,2]. The uptake sources of caffeine are mainly from what people drinking, including brewed coffee or tea solution, soft drinks [3,4]. Caffeine has also been added to other food and pharmaceutical drug products as supplemental for keeping people awake [5–7]. However, some unwanted effects of caffeine to human health have also attracted public attention, and some scientists recommended limiting caffeine consumption. For example, USA Food & Drug Administration recommended a 400 mg/day consumption limit for a normal adult [8]. Due to the significance of this compound in food and pharmacy, a field deployable technique for quick detecting caffeine concentration in difference food and pharmacy products is very attractive for product quality control or for production process monitoring.

Caffeine absorbs UV light with peak absorption wavelength at 210 nm and 274 nm [9–11]. This property can be used for analyzing caffeine concentration in samples of simple sample matrices. However, direct analysis of caffeine in complex samples with intrinsic UV/Vis absorption spectrometry is difficult due to the existence of severe spectral interferences. A separation technique, such as solvent extraction, solid phase microextraction (SPME), high performance liquid chromatography (HPLC), is needed to isolate this compound from sample matrix before detection [11–14]. Indirect optical spectroscopic methods have also been reported for analyzing caffeine in different samples. These methods involve caffeine chemical reactions with reagents, which generate colored products and can be detected with optical absorption or optical fluorescence spectrometry [15–17]. These indirect optical spectrometric methods also suffered from interference issue because many other compounds in samples can cause similar reactions. The adsorption of caffeine on silver nanoparticle (Ag-NP) surface causes the change of surface plasmon spectrometric signal or surface enhanced Raman scattering signal [18,19]. These indirect methods have been reported for detecting caffeine. However, the silver NP can absorb many nitrogen-containing organic compounds and results in similar surface plasmon spectrometric signal. Thus, these methods are not selective.

Caffeine can be oxidized on an electrode at appropriately applied electric potential. This oxidation reaction results in a current signal, which can be used as a sensing signal for quantitatively analyzing caffeine [20–22]. However, electrochemical sensors for caffeine sensing also have interferences from coexisting sample matrix, and direct analyzing caffeine in food samples with electrochemical techniques, such as cyclic voltammetry, linear sweeping, is very challenging. Although many efforts were devoted to modify electrode surface property, such as coating noble metal NPs, carbon nanotubes and sol–gel derived silica NPs, the achievement of these efforts was very much limited [20,23–25].

Molecularly imprinted polymer (MIP) is a new development in material science. A MIP is made by cross linking a monomer in the existence of template molecules [26,27]. After polymerization, the template molecules were imbedded inside the formed polymer. These imbedded template molecules can be removed out from the MIP with the assistance of a solvent or a chemical reagent. This results in a polymer having vacant with geometric shape and chemical bonding sites that are complementary to the template molecule. Such MIP can be used as SPME material to selectively extract template molecules but block out other species in a complex sample. Therefore, MIP has been reported for removing environmental pollutants in wastewater treatments [27–29]. In chemical analysis, MIPs have been used as SPME materials or as HPLC stationary phases for insolating target analyte from sample matrix before detection [27,30,31].

MIP is especially useful in chemical sensor development. A chemical sensor developed for field application of detecting an analyte requires the sensor to be highly selective, because it is difficult to use a separation procedure before using the sensor for detection in field applications. Integrating a MIP as a separation element can make a sensor specifically responses only to the analyte, but does not response to co-existing sample matrix. This advantage has been demonstrated in several reported chemical sensors having MIPs integrated as a component of the sensors [18,19,32–36]. MIP integrated electrochemical sensors for detecting caffeine have also been reported [32–34]. MIP bonded nanoparticles have been reported for detecting caffeine using surface plasmon spectrometry or surface acoustic wave as detection techniques [18,35,36].

In recent works, we developed evanescent wave optical fiber chemical sensors (EW-OFCS) using chitosan MIP membranes coated on surface of optical fibers as transducers [37–39]. These sensors demonstrated high selectivity and sensitivity in detecting organic acids. In such a sensor, the chitosan MIP selectively extracts a targeted organic acid because the polymer has amine groups to react with a weak organic acid, which concentrates the organic acid into the membrane and significantly improves sensor’s sensitivity. In this work, we integrated a polymethacrylic acid (PMAA) MIP membrane with caffeine template as a transducer of an EW-OFCS caffeine sensor. The PMAA-MIP membrane has pores with shape that is complementary to caffeine, and has weak carboxylic acid on the inner surface of pores that bond to caffeine, which is a weak base. This PMAA-MIP membrane can selectively extract/concentrate caffeine molecule from a sample solution into the membrane, and improve sensor’s sensitivity and selectivity.

2. Experimental

2.1. Chemicals

Deionized (DI) water was used for preparing aqueous solutions used in this work. Methacrylic acid (MAA, (H2C‗C(CH3)COOH, 99%, Sigma–Aldrich, Saint Louis, USA), ethylene glycol dimethacrylate (EGDMA, CH2‗C(CH3)COOCH2− CH2OCOC(CH3)‗CH2, 98%, Sigma―Aldrich) and azobisisobutyronitrile (AIBN, C8H12N4, 12 wt.% in acetone, Sigma―Aldrich) were used for preparing PMAA polymers. 3-(trimethoxysilyl)propyl methacrylate (TMOSPMA, H2C‗C(CH3)CO2(CH2)3Si (OCH3)3, 98%, Sigma―Aldrich) was used for introducing methacrylate function group on silica fiber surface. A TMOSPMA coating solution was prepared by mixing equal volume of the TMOSPMA solution and acetone. Caffeine (C8H10N4O2, anhydrous, 99%, Sigma―Aldrich) was used for preparing caffeine templated PMAA-MIP. Caffeine standard solutions used in this work were prepared by dissolving solid caffeine reagent in DI water and appropriate dilution. Tetraethyl orthosilicate (TEOS, Si(OC2H5)4, reagent grade, 98%, Sigma―Aldrich) was used for preparing silica sol solution. All other reagents used in this work were reagent grade and was used without further purification.

A PMAA-MIP synthesis solution was prepared by mixing 1.36 mL MAA, 10 mL EGDMA, 1.0 mL AIBN in acetone solution, 10 mL chloroform and 0.50 g caffeine. The composition of this solution was adopted by referring to a published work on caffeine-templated PMAA-MIP synthesis [35,36]. The mixture was sonicated for 5 min to dissolve caffeine. Nitrogen gas was bubbled through the mixture solution in a small reagent bottle for 15 min to drive out oxygen dissolved in the solution.

2.2. Instrument

An optical fiber compatible UV/Vis spectrometer (USB4000, OceanOptics, Inc., Dunedin, FL, USA) and an optical fiber compatible deuterium/tungsten combo light source (DH-2000, OceanOptics, Inc.) were used for measuring fiber optic EW absorption spectrometric spectrum and intensity spectrum of light guided through an optical fiber. The USB4000 optical fiber spectrometer was connected to a computer with a dedicated USB connector. A computer program (OceanView, OceanOptics, Inc) provided by the instrument maker was used to record the optical spectroscopic spectrum. A FTIR spectrometer (Nicolet iS5, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Madison, WI, USA) was used to study PMAA polymers made in this work. Attenuated total reflection (ATR) FTIR spectroscopy with a diamond crystal as an ATR element was used to record FTIR spectra of the prepared polymer powders. An UV/Vis spectrometer (UV2450, Shimadzu, Tokyo, Japan) was used with 1 cm quartz cuvettes for recording optical absorption spectra of sample solutions and for analyzing caffeine concentration in sample solutions.

2.3. Preparing a bent optical fiber probe (BOFP)

In this work, an optical fiber (FG200AEA, fiber core/cladding diameter = 200/220 μm, Thorlabs, Newtown, NJ, USA) with enhanced UV transmittance was used to make the fiber optic sensor probe, because the PMAA-MIP-EW-OFCS detects the optical spectrometric signals in UV wavelength range as a sensing signal. An “U” shaped BOFP was prepared by following a procedure reported in our previous publications [37–39]. In brief, the center of a short piece (30 cm) of the optical fiber was inserted into a micro butane torch flame. About 1 cm length of the central part of the fiber’s protection jacket and cladding was burned off. The bare fiber core was further forced to bend in the flame to form a “U” shaped structure. The “U” shaped part of the optical fiber core was soaked in a K2Cr2O7/H2SO4 washing solution, which was made by adding 10 g solid K2Cr2O7 into 20 mL concentrated H2SO4 in a small glass bottle, for 5 min to wash off any organic species on bent fiber core surface, then rinsed with DI water. The bent fiber core was inserted into a 50% hydrofluoric acid (caution: hydrofluoric acid is poisoning and should be handled with care) solution for 15 min to etched off some fiber core material in order to make a BOFP of smaller fiber core diameter. This HF etching process was adopted because an EW-OFCS has higher sensitivity with a smaller optical fiber core [40,41]. The HF-etched fiber core probe was rinsed with DI water, then, soaked in a 1 M NaOH solution for 20 min and rinsed with DI water again. The prepared BOFP was air-dried before coating with a polymer membrane.

2.4. Preparing silica sol solution

In this work, a silica sol solution was prepared by hydrolyzing TEOS with hydrochloric acid as catalyst. 2.0 mL of TEOS, 1.0 mL of DI water and 0.3 mL of 0.1MHCl were added into a small reagent bottle. The mixture solution was stirred with a magnetic stirrer bar for 2 h. A clear solution, called silica sol solution, was obtained from this reaction. This solution was prepared immediately before use.

2.5. Coating PMAA-MIP on BOFP surface

Two methods were investigated for synthesizing PMAA-MIP as a membrane on BOFP surface. In the first method, a prepared BOFP was first dried by heating the BOFP in a small glass bottle while flowing dry nitrogen gas through the bottle for 10 min [42]. The dried BOFP was then inserted into the TMOSPMA coating solution for 1 h. The TMOSPMA coated BOFP was then rinsed with methanol and DI water in sequence. This TMOSPMA-coated BOFP was used for coating PMAA-MIP by following the procedure described below.

2.0 mL of the PMAA-MIP synthesis solution were transferred into a small reagent bottle, which was pre-de-oxygenated by flowing nitrogen gas through the bottle for 5 min. The TMOSPMA-coated BOFP, which was fixed in a silicone septum of a top-hole screw cap, was inserted into the reagent bottle. The reagent bottle was air-tight with the silicone septum lined top-hole screw cap to prevent air leakage into the bottle, and the gas inside the reagent bottle was nitrogen during reaction process. The bent part of the BOFP was about 3 mm above the PMAA-MIP synthesis solution liquid level. The reagent bottle was then placed on top of a heating plate which had a setting temperature of 80 °C. The reagent bottle was shaken manually every 10 s while heating. Some PMAA-MIP synthesis solution was coated on the bent part of the BOFP from the manual shaken process. At around 40 min after heating, the transparent solution changed to be white-cloudy in the reagent bottle, which indicating the formation of PMAA-MIP polymer. The heating temperature was lowered to 60 °C, and the reagent bottle with the coated BOFP was kept on the heating plate overnight.

In the second method, PMAA-MIP was first synthesized by heating the PMAA-MIP synthesis solution in a small reagent bottle at 60 °C overnight [18,35,36]. The obtained polymer powder was grinded with mortar and pestle. The grinded powder was sieved with a #400 sieve (pore size, 38 μm). A coating mixture was made by mixing 50 mg of the sieved PMAA-MIP powder with 1.0 mL of silica sol solution. The mixture was sonicated for 5 min before coating the material on surface of a BOFP. A dip coating method [37–39] was followed to coat the PMAA-MIP/silica sol solution mixture by manually dipping the bent part of the BOFP into the mixture and pulling out slowly. The coated probe was let air-dry for 20 s. Three coating operations were performed. The coated probe was let for gelation in ambient air or in a humid air overnight. In the case of gelation in a humid air, the coated probe, which was fixed on the silicone septum of a top-hole screw cap, was inserted into a small reagent bottle which was half-filled with DI water. The bent part of the coated BOFP was about 5 cm above the water level.

A non-MIP PMAA was also prepared following the procedure of the second method, but did not have caffeine added into the PMAA synthesis solution. The obtained non-MIP PMAA powder was mixed with silica sol solution to form a coating mixture. A BOFP coated with non-MIP PMAA coating was prepared following above described coating procedure.

2.6. FTIR study of PMAA MIP polymer

The PMAA-MIP powder and non-MIP PMAA powder were used as samples in recording FTIR spectrum. A small quantity of a powdered polymer was placed onto the ATR adaptor of the iS5 FTIR spectrometer, and pressed in tight contact with the diamond crystal, and then an IR transmittance spectrum was recorded with air as reference sample.

2.7. Optical fiber EW spectrometry monitoring the washing out of caffeine template from PMAA-MIP membrane

The two ends of a PMAA-MIP coated BOFP were connected with the USB-4000 optical fiber spectrometer and the DH-2000 light source respectively by using SMA connectors. The bent part of the fiber probe was inserted into DI water in a small bottle. Light intensity spectrum was recorded as a reference when the bent probe was just inserted into DI water for measuring optical fiber EW absorption spectrum. Optical fiber EW absorption spectrum was monitored while the bent fiber probe was kept in DI water for washing out caffeine from the MIP membrane.

2.8. PMAA-MIP-EW-OFCS detecting caffeine in a sample solution

Light intensity spectrum was recorded as a reference after washing out caffeine template molecules from the PMAA-MIP membrane with DI water. The bent optical fiber probe was then inserted into a sample solution. An optical fiber EW absorption spectrum was recorded after 2 min the fiber probe was inserted into the sample solution. The difference of optical fiber EW absorbance at 274 nm (Abs274) from the optical fiber EW absorbance at 450 nm (Abs450) was used as the sensing signal (Abs274-Abs450) for quantitively detecting caffeine concentration in the sample solution.

2.9. PMAA-MIP-EW-OFCS analyzing caffeine in soft drink samples

Caffeine concentration in three soft drink products, Pepsi, Dr Pepper and Mountain Dew, purchased from a local grocery store, was analyzed by using the sensor of this work. A 5.0 mL aliquot of an original soft drink sample was 4-times diluted to make a 20.0 mL test sample solution. Three test sample solutions were made for each of the soft drink products. In analyzing caffeine in such a test sample, the PMAA-MIP coated BOFP was just inserted into the sample solution, and the optical fiber EW absorbance spectrum was recorded. The baseline shift corrected optical fiber EW absorbance (Abs274-Abs450) was used as the sensing signal for quantitative detecting caffeine concentration in the test sample solutions.

3. Results and discussion

3.1. ATR FTIR spectra of non-MIP PMAA and PMAA-MIP samples proving caffeine as MIP’s template molecules

Fig. 1 shows FTIR spectra of three substances, non-MIP PMAA, MIP-PMAA and pure caffeine. The peaks in the non-MIP PMAA demonstrate –C‗O stretch vibration (1721 cm−1), C‗C stretch vibration (1640 cm−1), –CH2–stretch vibration (1455 cm−1) and O–C(=O) stretch vibration (1148 cm−1) in the crosslinked polymer. These FTIR peaks were also reported in PMAA-MIP literatures [43–46]. PMAA and PMAA-MIPs in these reported works were used as drug delivery substances, SPME material (PMAA-MIP) or as a transducer of a sensor (PMAA-MIP). There are several new peaks in the PMAA-MIP’s FTIR spectrum when compared with the FTIR spectrum of non-MIP PMAA. These peaks have wavenumber at 745 cm−1 (C–C‗O), 1239 cm−1 (C–N), 1653 cm−1 (C‗O), and 1700 cm−1 (C‗N), which agree with caffeine’s IR spectrum peaks reported in a literature [47]. These FTIR peaks also appeared in the FTIR spectrum of a pure caffeine sample recorded in this work. It can be concluded that these new peaks in PMAA-MIP’s FTIR spectrum are from caffeine. Therefore, the FTIR spectra clearly indicate that caffeine was embedded inside the PMAA-MIP polymer as template.

ATR-FTIR spectra of non-MIP PMAA, PMAA-MIP and pure caffeine.

3.2. Optical fiber EW absorption spectrometry monitoring washing out of caffeine template molecules from MIP membrane

Caffeine template molecules was washed out of the PMAA-MIP membrane when the coated BOFP was inserted into DI water. This washing out took 3 h. The washing out process was monitored by observing the optical fiber EW absorption spectrum. The light intensity passed through the MIP-coated BOFP when probe was just inserted into DI water was recorded as a reference for recording the optical fiber EW absorption spectrum. The absorbance spectrum at that moment was recorded as baseline spectrum showing in Fig. 2 (A). During the washing out process, a negative absorption spectrum was recorded. The negative absorption spectrum has a peak negative absorption wavelength at 274 nm, which agrees with caffeine’s UV absorption spectrum. After 3 h the BOFP was inserted into DI water, the absorption spectrum (Fig. 2 (B)) stabilized and negative peak absorbance at 274 nm reached −1.9.

Fiber optic EW absorption spectrum of an caffeine-templated PMAA-MIP membrane coated BOFP recorded before (A) and after (B) caffeine template molecules were washed out of the MIP membrane.

3.3. Selecting a method for coating PMAA-MIP on BOFP

The two methods for coating PMAA-MIP on BOFP was compared. These methods were adopted in referring to published works which developing PMAA-MIP surface acoustic wave sensors for detecting caffeine [35,36] or coating a PMAA-MIP membrane on silica fiber surface for SPME applications [42]. PMAA-MIP membranes made from both methods were stable on the BOFP surface, and caffeine can be washed off without washing off the MIP polymers. The response of PMAA-MIP-EW-OFCS sensors made from these two methods exposed to standard solutions of different caffeine concentration were investigated. Fig. 3 (A) shows the recorded spectral response of a BOFP prepared with the first method. Fig. 3 (B) shows the recorded spectral response of a BOFP prepared with the second method. Both sensors showed EW absorption spectrum with peak absorption wavelength at caffeine’s peak absorption wavelength (274 nm). The relationships of these sensors’ sensing signal (Abs274-Abs450) with caffeine concentration are shown in Fig. 4. The sensitivity of the sensors made from these two methods was comparable.

Spectral responses of two PMAA-MIP-EW-OFCS sensors exposed to caffeine standard solutions of different concentration. The PMAA-MIP membrane preparation methods of these two sensors were different as described in the text. A: the spectral response of a BOFP prepared with the first method. B: the spectral response of a BOFP prepared with the second method.

Calibration curves show the linear relationship of the two sensors’ response to caffeine concentration in aqueous sample solutions. The sensitivity of these two sensors is comparable.

In considering the fact that the coating thickness of an EW-OFCS affects the sensor’s sensitivity. However, the PMAA-MIP coating thickness in the first method is very difficult to control, but the coating thickness of the second method is controllable. Therefore, the second method was adopted in this work to make PMAA-MIP coating on BOFP surface in this work. This MIP powder synthesis and mixed polymer coating approach is similar to those in reported works of developing PMAA-MIP electrochemical and surface acoustic wave caffeine sensors [33,35,36].

For the gelation process after coating the PMAA-MIP mixed silica sol solution, it was found that it took >24 h to wash out the caffeine embedded inside the MIP if the coated BOFP was gelatinized in ambient air. The embedded caffeine molecules can be washed out within 3 h if the coated probe was gelatinized in humid air. The later method was adopted in this work to prepare the PMAA-MIP coated BOFP.

In addition to sol gel derived silica sol solution, three more polymer solutions were investigated for trapping the PMAA-MIP and coating the mixture polymer membranes on BOFP for sensing caffeine. These are polyvinyl chloride (dissolved in methylene dichloride), poly(methyl methacrylate) (dissolved in tetrahydrofuran), polyvinyl alcohol (dissolved in DI water) crosslinked by glutaraldehyde. However, it was found that the BOFP made by using these mixture polymers did not show response to caffeine in a sample solution. The big difference between sol–gel derived silica and these polymers is that the silica nanoparticles are hydrophilic, while the three organic polymers are hydrophobic. Caffeine in an aqueous solution sample can not permeate into the hydrophobic polymers and can not interact with EW of light guided through the BOFP.

3.4. PMAA-MIP SPME improving EW-OFCS sensitivity for detecting caffeine

Optical fiber EW absorption spectrometry is a relatively insensitive technique for detecting optical absorption signal, because the limited interaction pass-length [40,41]. Although the BOFP technique adopted in this work helped increases the sensitivity of EW-OFCS, it’s sensitivity was still limited. This is demonstrated in spectrum (A) in Fig. 5. This absorption spectrum was recorded when a bare BOFP (do not have any coating) was exposed to a 2.0 mg/mL caffeine standard solution. Spectrum (B) in Fig. 5 was recorded when the PMAA-MIP coated BOFP of this work was exposed to a 0.20 mg/mL caffeine standard solution. Comparing these two spectra, one can conclude that by using the PMAA-MIP to extract/concentrate caffeine from a sample solution into the membrane, the sensitivity of PMAA-MIP-EW-OFCS increased by 95 times. Spectrum (C) in Fig. 5 was recorded when a non-MIP PMAA coated BOFP was exposed to a 2.0 mg/mL caffeine standard solution. Comparing this spectrum with spectrum (A), it is clear that the non-MIP PMAA membrane has very limited effect of concentrating caffeine from sample solution to the membrane.

3.5. PMAA-MIP selective SPME improving selectivity of EW-OFCS for detecting caffeine

One problem of using traditional UV/Vis spectrometry in real-world sample analysis is the spectral interference from species co-existing with an analyte in a sample. The spectral interference can be reduced in EW-OFCS using non-MIP polymer membranes, because the membrane can block out some molecules from entering the membrane. However, spectral interference still exists in reported EW-OFCS using polymer membrane coatings [48,49]. In this work, a PMAA-MIP was used for extracting caffeine into the membrane. The selective extraction of caffeine by the MIP membrane improved sensor’s selectivity. Fig. 6 shows optical absorption spectra obtained when a PMAA-MIP-EW-OFCS (Fig. 6 (A)) and a non-MIP PMAA membrane coated BOFP (Fig. 6 (B)) were exposed to standard solutions of different compounds. These spectra clearly demonstrate that a PMAA-MIP-EW-OFCS has better selectivity compared with a non-MIP PMAA-EW-OFCS. A selectivity factor (SF), which describes the relative sensitivity of the sensor toward caffeine to an interference species, is defined as the ratio of absorbance at 274 nm caused by caffeine to the absorbance at the same wavelength (274 nm) caused by an interference species has same concentration as that of caffeine. The selectivity of MIP based sensors is decided by two factors: 1), selective extraction of template molecules into the polymer; and 2), selectively detecting compound(s) extracted into the polymer. When discussing selectivity of MIP based sensors, most published papers focused on the pores in MIP that having shape and bonding sites which are complementary to the template molecule. This leads to the conclusion that MIP extract only template molecules from sample solutions in some published literature, especially in some review papers [32,33]. However, there are function groups in MIP that are not associated with the imprinted pores. For example, within the MIP of this work, there are MAA units in the pores that are imprinted with caffeine, but there are also MAA units in the formed polymer that are not imprinted with caffeine. Although the MAA units imprinted with caffeine are very selective in extracting caffeine, the non-imprinted MAA units in the polymer can also form hydrogen bond with organic acid or form salt with organic base, and extract these compounds into the MIP. This was demonstrated in the recorded absorption spectra (Fig. 6 (A)) in this work. Similar results were also observed in our previous works with molecularly imprinted chitosan polymers for organic acid sensor development [37–39]. Although MIP based chemical sensors were claimed to be highly selective, cross responses from co-existing species with chemical property similar to that of analyte have also been observed by others in their research works [18,34,35]. In PMAA-MIP-EW-OFCS of this work, two factors improved the sensor’s selectivity: MIP selective extraction of analyte and optical spectroscopic discrimination. The MIP selective extraction concentrated caffeine into membrane, improved sensor’s sensitivity toward sensing caffeine than other compounds, and increased sensor’s SF for sensing caffeine. Optical spectroscopic selectivity further discriminated caffeine from other compounds even they were extracted together with caffeine into MIP membrane.

Theophylline and caffeine are both methylxanthines, differ by only a single methyl group. These two compounds have similar chemical and optical properties. Caffeine’s peak absorption wavelength is 274 nm, and theophylline’s peak absorption wavelength is 278 nm. Compared with a non-MIP PMAA membrane, the MIP-PMAA membrane has higher selectivity in concentrating caffeine, and an increased SF was resulted as shown in Table 1. This sensor’s SF for sensing caffeine against theophylline is comparable to that in a reported PMAA-MIP caffeine sensor using a UV/Vis absorption spectrometry as a detection technique [18]. The difficult of discriminating these two compounds with MIP-based sensors was demonstrated in another reported work [19], which used theophylline a template for preparing PMAA-MIP for detecting caffeine using surface enhance Raman spectrometry as a detection technique. It was understood that interference from theophylline was not completely eliminated by using PMAA-MIP membrane as transducer of this sensor, and the sensor is not recommended to be used for analyzing caffeine in theophylline containing samples, such as browed tea, coffee solution samples.

3.6. PMAA-MIP-EW-OFCS reversibility, response time

The PMAA-MIP-EW-OFCS of this work is a reversible sensor, because the MIP extraction is a reversible process, and the intrinsic optical absorbance by caffeine itself was measured as a sensing signal. There is no chemical reaction involved in the sensor’s measurement process. Fig. 7 shows the experimental results demonstrating the reversibility of the sensor. In this test, the sensor probe was alternatively exposed to a 40 μg/mL caffeine standard solution and DI water, and the sensing signal (Abs274-Abs450) was continuously monitored with time. This test result demonstrates the sensor’s reversibility and stability. The response time of the sensor is defined as a time needed for the sensor to reach 90% of its full-scale response after exposed to a sample solution. The response time of this sensor is calculated to be 0.5 min from test result showing in Fig. 7.

3.7. Calibration curve and detection limit

The spectral response of this sensor to caffeine standard solutions of different concentration showing in Fig. 3 (B) was used for establishing a calibration curve for quantitative analysis of caffeine in aqueous sample solutions. The difference of absorbances at 274 nm and 450 nm (Abs270-Abs450) was used as the sensing signal of this sensor, because the movement of sensor probe from one solution to another sample solution can cause a change of optical fiber’s position and shift the baseline of the optical signal. A calibration curve was established using a least square regression method with spectral response data showing in Fig. 3 (B). The obtained calibration curve with calibration equation is shown in Fig. 4 (Sensor#1).

The detection limit of this sensor is defined as caffeine concentration in a sample solution which causes a sensing signal that is three times of standard deviation (STDV) of blank sample’s sensing signal. The STDV of blank sample using data in Fig. 7 is calculated to be 0.0012. The detection limit, calculated from the STDV and slope of the calibration curve, is 2.0 μg/mL. The reported traditional UV/Vis spectrometric methods for analyzing caffeine [8–10] have detection limits that are lower than the detection limit of this sensor. The sensitivity of optical absorption spectrometry is proportional to the interaction pass-length. In optical fiber EW-OFCS, the interaction pass-length is in micrometer range, very much limited [40,41]. Although the MIP membrane extraction concentrated caffeine from a sample solution into the MIP membrane, the sensitivity of this sensor is still not as high when compared with traditional UV/Vis absorption spectrometry. PMAA-MIP has been investigated for caffeine sensors using different detection techniques. These sensors have detection limit comparable or better than the detection limit of the sensor of this work as showing in Table 2.

The sensitivity improvement by PMAA-MIP membrane extraction of this sensor is limited to be 95. This is compared with several thousand times sensitivity improvement by chitosan MIP membrane extraction of organic acids in our previously reported works [37–39]. It was believed that the difference of transducer membrane preparation methods of these two MIP-based sensor techniques has significant impact on the sensitivity improvement. In the sensor of this work, the PMAA-MIP were prepared as solid particles which passed through a #400 sieve (pore size of 38 μm). These particles were distributed in a porous silica membrane. Therefore, the concentration of MIP particles in the membrane of this sensor was much lower than that of the chitosan MIP based sensors. Another factor affects the sensitivity of this sensor is the size of the PMAA-MIP particles (38 μm), which is much larger than the penetration depth of EW of light passing through the BOFP, which is comparable with light’s wavelength. In such a case, many caffeine molecules extracted to the PMAA-MIP particles do not make contribution of EW absorbance signal.

3.8. PMAA-MIP-EW-OFCS analyzing caffeine in soft drink products

The sensor of this work was used to analyzing caffeine concentration in three soft drink products, Pepsi, Dr Pepper and Mountain Dew, purchased from a local grocery store. The extraction step includes adding 0.20 mL of 20% Na2CO3 solution to 4.0 mL of the 16-time diluted test sample solution, then adding 4.0 mL of CH2Cl2 in a small testing tube with screw cap. 4. Conclusions

An optical fiber caffeine sensor was developed by integrating a MIP membrane with BOFP fiber optic EW absorption spectrometry. The MIP membrane coated on the surface of an BOFP selectively extracts caffeine from a sample solution into the membrane, increases the sensor’s sensitivity and selectivity. This makes direct analysis of caffeine in complex samples, such as soft drinks, possible. The developed sensor was used to directly analyzing caffeine in soft drink products, and analytical results agree well with results obtained by using a traditional solvent extraction UV/Vis absorption spectrometric method. Due to the existence of spectral interference species, it is difficult to directly analyze caffeine in some soft drink products using traditional UV/Vis spectrometry, and a separation technique was normally applied prior to detection. With the sensor of this work, direct analyzing caffeine in the soft drink products is possible because a separation element (MIP membrane) was integrated into the sensor.
