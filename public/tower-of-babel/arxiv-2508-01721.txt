Abstract
The rotational evolution of a strongly magnetized neutron star (NS), accreting or isolated, is driven by external torques of different nature. In addition to the torques, even the tiniest deformations of the NS crust can affect its rotation through asymmetries in its inertia tensor. Several factors may be responsible for the deformations, including strong magnetic fields, internal stresses, or local heating. The main effect produced by the deformations is the so-called free precession: the motion of the rotational axis with respect to the crust. We consider the evolution of a triaxially deformed isolated NS with a strong dipolar magnetic field for a broad range of parameters, taking into account the magnetic field decay. We show that the combination of pulsar torques and free precession results in a considerable broadening of the distribution of magnetic obliquity angles (the angle between the magnetic and rotational axes) and creates a population of objects where the rotational axis does not align with the magnetic axis at all but enters a limit-cycle regime. The combination of free precession and magnetic torques can also explain the observed distribution in pulsar braking indices by creating a periodic oscillation in the magnetic obliquity.

I
Introduction

Rotation-powered pulsars is a numerous class of isolated neutron stars (NS). They are believed to have a dipolar magnetosphere, whose axis is misaligned with the rotation axis by an angle
\chi
, known as the
magnetic angle
. Its time change, along with the spin frequency
\Omega
, its time derivative
\dot{\Omega}
, and other higher-order timing parameters, provides a description of the star’s rotational evolution.

Radiopulsars are subject to magnetospheric torques and exhibit a gradual spin down over time scales of

\tau_{\mathrm{psr}}\sim|\Omega/\dot{\Omega}|\sim
millions of years, as expected by theory (
A. Spitkovsky 2006
, see also
P. Abolmasov et al. 2024
and
A. Lyne & F. Graham-Smith 2012
for a review). At the same time, when treated as rigid spherical bodies, misaligned rotators are expected to undergo progressive alignment of the spin and magnetic axes
(
A. Philippov et al. 2014
)
, and to precess around the magnetic axis with a period of
\sim(\Omega R_{\mathrm{NS}}/c)\tau_{\mathrm{psr}}
, where
R_{\mathrm{NS}}
is the neutron star radius.
This period is
10^{3}-10^{4}
times shorter than
\tau_{\mathrm{psr}}
for a typical pulsar, and this type of precession is usually referred to as “radiative precession” in the pulsar literature
(
A. Melatos 2000
;
V. S. Beskin & A. A. Zheltoukhov 2014
)
. If, in addition, the inertia tensor of the neutron star is nonisotropic, then other precessional components are at play.

Several mechanisms that might cause biaxial and triaxial deformations of the NS have been discussed so far
(
J. P. Ostriker & J. E. Gunn 1969
;
P. Goldreich 1970
;
A. Melatos 2000
;
D. I. Jones & N. Andersson 2001
;
L. Arzamasskiy et al. 2015
, e.g.)
.
These include: (i) uncompensated elastic deformation that ensues the crystallization of the star
(
P. Goldreich 1970
;
A. Melatos 2000
)
;
(ii) uneven heating of the neutron star crust due to magnetic field decay or accretion in a binary system
(
L. Bildsten 1998
;
G. Ushomirsky et al. 2000
;
J. A. Pons et al. 2007
;
J. A. Pons et al. 2009
)
;
and (iii) distortion of the NS crust by the tension of a sufficiently strong internal magnetic
field
(
B. Haskell et al. 2008
, e.g.)
.
The magnitudes of the deformation predicted by these models span a wide range,
from
\varepsilon\sim 10^{-14}
to
\varepsilon\sim 10^{-5}
, where
\varepsilon=\Delta I/I
,
I
is the net moment of inertia of the star, and
\Delta I
is its variation between different directions. The specific value depends on the details of the NS interior physics.

If
\varepsilon
is large enough, the star will tend to precess freely with a period
\sim 2\pi/\varepsilon\Omega\ll\tau_{\mathrm{psr}}
.
This can manifest itself observationally as periodic changes in the pulsar timing parameters
(
D. I. Jones & N. Andersson 2001
)
. At least for two isolated pulsars, free precession was proposed as an explanation for a periodicity known from the observations: B1828-11
(
T. Akgün et al. 2006
)
(see, however,
I. H. Stairs et al. 2019
); and B1642-03
(
T. V. Shabanova et al. 2001
)
.
The decadal
increase in the separation between the main and secondary pulses of the Crab pulsar (for
0.62^{\circ}
per century,
A. Lyne et al. 2013
), which seems to imply a rapid increase of
\chi
, can also be naturally explained by precession
(
L. Arzamasskiy et al. 2015
)
.
But perhaps the most compelling evidence for a free precession of an NS comes from the recent X-ray polarization measurements of Her X-1 made by the Imaging X-ray Polarimetry Explorer (IXPE,
P. Soffitta et al. 2021
) probing the spin geometry of the neutron star
(
J. Heyl et al. 2024
)
. The 35-day variability in the X-ray pulse frequency of Her X-1 also supports the precession
(
D. Kolesnikov et al. 2022
)
.

In addition, some gamma-ray and fast radio bursts exhibit rapid quasi-periodic oscillations in their light curves, which, although
debatable, have been attributed to precessional motions of a central magnetar
(
Y. Levin et al. 2020
;
B. Zhang et al. 2024
)
.

Whether NSs can be modeled as rigid bodies is questionable. In reality, the inner crust is coupled to neutron superfluid vortices that are pinned to nuclei in the crustal lattice.
In the case of absolute pinning, free precession is dominated by the superfluid component
(
J. Shaham 1977
)
. However, in the
presence of external torque, the superfluid may follow the precession of the crust via thermal vortex creep
(
A. Alpar & H. Oegelman 1987
)
.
Some evidence of this exists in Her X-1
(
J. Heyl et al. 2024
)
.
Our analysis ignores any coupling to the core and to the superfluid within the crust, and we only briefly touch upon it in Sec.
V
.

Although only sufficiently strong deformations,
\varepsilon\sim 10^{-8}-10^{-5}
, can cause precessional motions with periods
of the order of months to decades, as observed in the objects mentioned above, even much smaller deformations,
\varepsilon\lesssim 10^{-10}
,
can substantially affect the long-term evolution of radio pulsars.
If common across the pulsar population, they should also affect their statistical properties.
This motivates a comprehensive evolutionary analysis of a population of deformed NSs.
This is the primary goal of this paper.

Previous studies have focused on either spherical stars driven solely by electromagnetic torques, or on deformed stars with a negligible contribution of radiative precession
(
P. Goldreich 1970
;
D. I. Jones & N. Andersson 2001
, e.g)
.
Other studies included both, but focused on very specific cases
(
A. Melatos 2000
;
J. J. Zanazzi & D. Lai 2015
;
L. Arzamasskiy et al. 2015
)
.
Most attention was paid to very specific combinations of parameters such as biaxial stars, aligned or orthogonal magnetic axis orientation.
Radiative precession was often ignored or treated in a simplified way.
No systematic analysis of the parameter space was performed. The magnetic field decay has never been included in the study.

In this paper, we conduct a systematic study of the rotational evolution of isolated NSs for a broad range of parameters, including the possibility of magnetic field decay, and discuss the observational implications. We identify several evolutionary pathways and demonstrate that the long-term evolution of radiopulsars can naturally account for the anomalous braking indices and their correlation with the pulsar age, as observed, as well as a quasi-isotropic distribution of magnetic angles.

The paper is organized as follows. In Section
II
, we derive and analyze equations describing the evolution of a deformed precessing neutron star.
In Section
III
, the long-term evolution of such a precessing star is considered for a wide range of NS parameters, including the effect of magnetic field decay. Section
IV
discusses the observational implications. A brief account of the role of neutron superfluid in the inner crust, which is ignored in our analysis, is given in Section
V
. We summarize in Section
VI
.

Physics of neutron star precession

II.1
Euler’s equations

Let us treat the neutron star as a non-spherical rigid body

\boldsymbol{\omega}

I_{0}

Assuming
I_{0}=const
, one can rewrite Euler’s equations (
3

\boldsymbol{\dot{\Omega}}+\boldsymbol{\dot{\omega}}=(\boldsymbol{\omega}\times\boldsymbol{\Omega})+\boldsymbol{n}.

(6)

Here, the dot denotes the time derivative, and
\boldsymbol{n}=\boldsymbol{N}/I_{0}
.

\boldsymbol{n}=\boldsymbol{n}_{\Omega}+\boldsymbol{n}_{\chi}+\boldsymbol{n}_{\mu}=n_{\Omega}\boldsymbol{s}_{3}+n_{\chi}\boldsymbol{s}_{2}+n_{\mu}\boldsymbol{s}_{1}.

(7)

The orthonormal vector basis
\boldsymbol{s}_{i}
(
i=1,2,3

\boldsymbol{m}=\boldsymbol{\mu}/\mu

\chi=\cos^{-1}\left(\frac{\boldsymbol{m}\cdot\boldsymbol{\Omega}}{\Omega}\right)

\boldsymbol{s}_{3}=\boldsymbol{\Omega}/\Omega,

(9)

\boldsymbol{s}_{2}\sin\chi=(\boldsymbol{m}\times\boldsymbol{s}_{3})\times\boldsymbol{s}_{3}=\boldsymbol{s}_{3}\cos\chi-\boldsymbol{m},

(10)

\boldsymbol{s}_{1}\sin\chi=(\boldsymbol{s}_{3}\times\boldsymbol{m})=(\boldsymbol{s}_{2}\times\boldsymbol{s}_{3})\sin\chi,

(11)

7
) is parallel to the spin axis and accounts for the rotational energy losses, i.e., the spin-down itself. The second term is responsible for magnetic angle evolution, while the third term, which is proportional to
n_{\mu}
, causes rotation of the spin axis around the magnetic axis with the angular velocity of

\boldsymbol{\omega}_{\mathrm{rad}}=\varepsilon_{\mu}\Omega\cos\chi\boldsymbol{m},

(12)

where

\varepsilon_{\mu}=-\frac{n_{\mu}}{\Omega^{2}\sin\chi\cos\chi}

(13)

is a convenient dimensionless parameter representing the strength of the torque component
\boldsymbol{n}_{\mu}
. In the pulsar literature, this type of precession is often referred to as “radiative” precession, and the torque
\boldsymbol{n}_{\mu}
as the “anomalous” or “radiation-precession” torque
(
P. Goldreich 1970
;
M. L. Good & K. K. Ng 1985
;
A. Melatos 2000
)
. We shall henceforth refer to this type of forced precession as radiative.

With the decomposition (
7
) and definition (
12
), one can re-write Euler’s equations (
6
) in the form

\dot{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}+\dot{\boldsymbol{\omega}}=\left(\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{\mathrm{p}}\times\boldsymbol{\Omega}\right)+\boldsymbol{n}_{\chi}+\boldsymbol{n}_{\Omega},

(14)

where

\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{\mathrm{p}}=\boldsymbol{\omega}+\boldsymbol{\omega}_{\mathrm{rad}}

(15)

is the full precessional angular velocity. The precessional time scale is
T_{\mathrm{p}}\sim 2\pi/\Omega_{\mathrm{p}}
, respectively. Projecting Eq. (
14
) on
\boldsymbol{s}_{3}
and
\boldsymbol{s}_{2}
, one can obtain a set of two equations describing, respectively, the spin down and magnetic angle evolution of the neutron star.

II.2
Pulsar torque

The components of the torque
\boldsymbol{n}
acting on an isolated active radiopulsar have been obtained numerically and analytically. Generally, this torque is proportional to

n_{\mathrm{psr}}=-\frac{\mu^{2}}{I_{0}R_{\mathrm{LC}}^{3}},

(16)

where
R_{\mathrm{LC}}=c/\Omega
is the radius of the light cylinder. Recent MHD and PIC studies of pulsar magnetospheres
(
A. Spitkovsky 2006
;
A. Tchekhovskoy et al. 2013
;
A. Philippov et al. 2014
)
, yield the following torque components:

n_{\Omega}=n_{\mathrm{psr}}(k_{0}+k_{1}\sin^{2}\chi),

(17)

and

n_{\chi}=k_{2}n_{\mathrm{psr}}\sin\chi\cos\chi.

(18)

The dimensionless coefficients
k_{i}
in these expressions were also estimated numerically and found to be of order unity, with a small spread within the interval 1..2, depending on the particular setup
(
A. A. Philippov et al. 2015
;
J. Pétri 2020
)
. Hereafter we adopt
k_{0}=k_{1}=k_{2}=1
.

The precessional component of the torque,
n_{\mu}
, has also been found to be non-zero for an isolated neutron star. It has been derived earlier
(
L. Davis & M. Goldstein 1970
;
M. L. Good & K. K. Ng 1985
;
A. Melatos 2000
;
V. S. Beskin & A. A. Zheltoukhov 2014
, e.g.)
and may be interpreted, at least partially, as a manifestation of the inertia of the stellar magnetic field.
The most complete consideration of it was provided in the analytical work by
V. S. Beskin & A. A. Zheltoukhov 2014

n_{\mu}=-\xi n_{\mathrm{psr}}\frac{R_{\mathrm{LC}}}{R_{\mathrm{NS}}}\sin\chi\cos\chi.

(19)

The factor

\xi=\frac{8}{15}-\frac{1}{5}\frac{R_{\mathrm{NS}}}{R_{\rm in}}

(20)

was calculated under the assumption that the field is uniform within some radius
R_{\rm in}\leq R_{\mathrm{NS}}
and dipolar outside it. Here, we adopt
R_{\rm in}=R_{\mathrm{NS}}

\xi=1/3

\varepsilon_{\mu}=-\frac{\mu^{2}}{3I_{0}R_{\mathrm{NS}}c^{2}},

(21)

\Omega

B_{12}=B/10^{12}\,

\varepsilon_{\mu}\approx-9.2\times 10^{-13}B^{2}_{12}I_{0,45}^{-1}R_{\mathrm{NS,12}}^{5}.

\dot{\Omega}=n_{\mathrm{psr}}

II.3
Free precession

For a freely precessing star (
\boldsymbol{n}\equiv 0
), Eq. (
14
) reduces to

\dot{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}+\dot{\boldsymbol{\omega}}=(\boldsymbol{\omega}\times\boldsymbol{\Omega}).

(29)

Projecting the resultant equations onto
\boldsymbol{\omega}
leads to a constant of motion

\lambda_{0}=\frac{\boldsymbol{\Omega}\cdot\boldsymbol{\omega}}{2\Omega^{2}}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\varepsilon_{1}\sin^{2}\theta\cos^{2}\varphi+\varepsilon_{3}\cos^{2}\theta\right).

(30)

Here we have assumed that
\varepsilon_{1,3}\ll 1
and both remain constant. These assumptions will be used further in the text.

The geometric meaning of
\lambda_{0}
is as follows. The tip of the spin vector
\boldsymbol{\Omega}
rotates in the principal frame along trajectories of constant
\lambda_{0}
(
the polhodes
) with a precessional period
T_{\mathrm{p}}\sim 2\pi/\varepsilon_{\mathrm{d}}\Omega
.
The set of available precession orbits depends on the ratio between
\varepsilon_{1}
and
\varepsilon_{3}
.
In Figure
3
, we show examples of the precessional orbits identified by their normalized
\lambda_{0}
parameter values

\lambda_{\mathrm{0,norm}}=\frac{\lambda_{0}-\lambda_{\mathrm{0,min}}}{\lambda_{\mathrm{0,max}}-\lambda_{\mathrm{0,min}}}\in[0,1],

(31)

with
\lambda_{\mathrm{0,min}}
and
\lambda_{\mathrm{0,max}}
being the minimal and maximal values of
\lambda_{0}
for a particular choice of the deformation parameters
\varepsilon_{1},\varepsilon_{3}
.

It is evident from Eq. (
30
) that there are four precession poles (extrema of
\lambda_{0}
): two along the minor principal axis
\boldsymbol{e}_{1}
, and two along the major principal axis
\boldsymbol{e}_{3}
. They are depicted by bold plus signs in Figure
3
. With our convention,
\varepsilon_{1}<0
, the corresponding values of
\lambda_{0}
at these locations are
\lambda_{0,\mathrm{min}}=\varepsilon_{1}/2
and
\lambda_{0,\mathrm{max}}=\varepsilon_{3}/2
. The saddle points (X-points), that correspond to
\lambda_{0}=0
, are located on the intermediate axis
\boldsymbol{e}_{2}
and are marked by crosses. In the case of an oblate (
\varepsilon_{1}=0
) or prolate (
\varepsilon_{3}=0
) biaxial deformations,
\lambda_{0,\mathrm{min}}
or
\lambda_{0,\mathrm{max}}
are equal to zero, respectively.

Generally, the spin axis tends to precess around the axis of minimal or maximal inertia, while the intermediate axis
\boldsymbol{e}_{2}
remains unstable. This is a well-known result of classical mechanics.

The combined effect of the two precessional motions

A real neutron star is expected to have a nonzero radiative precessional torque
n_{\mu}
, which contributes to the full precessional angular velocity (Eq.

\lambda_{0}
,

III
Long-term evolution of a precessing neutron star

III.1
Overview

Figure 5:
Qualitative summary for asymptotic evolutionary states of a precessing magnetized neutron star. Here,
\boldsymbol{\Omega}
denotes the angular velocity vector and
\chi
the magnetic angle. For a biaxial star,
\alpha
is the angle between the magnetic and symmetry axes.

A precessing NS evolves on time scales much longer than its precession period due to the other torque components and secular evolution of the parameters of the star.
After considerable time, its precession orbits evolve towards either static points or attractor limit circles.
We begin with a qualitative description of the main results. Readers who are not interested in the gory details can skip the rest of this section. The main observational consequences will be discussed in Sec.
IV
.

The long-term evolution of the precessional motion of a NS depends on the ratio
|\varepsilon_{\mu}|/\varepsilon_{\mathrm{d}}
, the orientation of
\boldsymbol{m}
, and the initial orientation of the rotation axis
\boldsymbol{s}_{3}
with respect to the principal frame. It takes place on the pulsar spin-down time scale,

\tau_{\mathrm{psr}}\gg T_{\mathrm{p}}\gg P.

(33)

Generally, three regimes can be identified (see Figure
5
). When
|\varepsilon_{\mu}|\gg\varepsilon_{\mathrm{d}}
, the rotation axis gradually approaches the magnetic axis (
\chi\to 0
).
When
|\varepsilon_{\mu}|\ll\varepsilon_{\rm d}
, the rotation axis of
a biaxial
star evolves towards either the deformation axis or the deformation equator, depending on the angle
\alpha
between the magnetic and symmetry axes.
Triaxiality in this regime either makes
\boldsymbol{\Omega}
aligned with one of the extremal principal axes (
\boldsymbol{e}_{1,3}
), or keeps it precessing along an attractor orbit. In the former case
\chi
approaches a final non-zero value, while in the latter case it wobbles on a precessional timescale with a finite amplitude
\Delta\chi
. Finally, in the intermediate regime,
|\varepsilon_{\mu}|\sim\varepsilon_{\mathrm{d}}
, we find that the rotation axis either approaches one of the precession poles, which are offset from both the magnetic and the principal axes (see Figure
3
), or, as in the weakly magnetized case, enters an attractor state where the long-term evolution approaches a stable limit cycle. The different evolutionary pathways are summarized in Figure
5
.

Only in highly magnetized stars (
|\varepsilon_{\mu}|\gg\varepsilon_{\rm hd}
)
\chi
evolves according to the standard spin-down model. For the majority of pulsars, we expect the spin and magnetic axes to remain separated essentially at all times.

III.2
Secular evolution of
\lambda

Figure 6:
Precession-averaged
\dot{\lambda}
calculated for an oblate biaxial star with magnetic moment tilted at
45^{\circ}
relative to the deformation axis. For
|\varepsilon_{\mu}|\gg\varepsilon_{\mathrm{d}}
,
\lambda_{\mathrm{norm}}=0
at the magnetic poles and
1
at the magnetic equator, while for
|\varepsilon_{\mu}|\ll\varepsilon_{\mathrm{d}}
,
\lambda_{\mathrm{norm}}=0
at the deformation equator and
1
at the deformation (symmetry) axis. White arrows show the direction of evolution of
\lambda
. As seen, in the case of strong radiative precession, the spin axis tends to align with the magnetic axis. On the other hand, when free precession dominates, it aligns with the symmetry axis instead. An attractor exists in a certain range of parameters where
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{d}}\gtrsim\mathrm{few}\times|\varepsilon_{\mu}|
, and is marked by the solid black line. The black dashed line marks the locus of saddle points on the map.

Upon projecting Euler equations (
14
) on
\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{\mathrm{p}}
and taking into account the above assumptions (
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{d}}\ll 1
and constant,
\varepsilon_{\mu}=const
,
\boldsymbol{m}=const
), one obtains an equation for the long-term evolution of
\lambda
(see Appendix
A
for a detailed derivation):

\Omega^{2}\frac{\rm d\lambda}{\rm dt}=\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{\mathrm{p}}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}_{\chi}.

(34)

This equation indicates that
\lambda
tends to be nearly conserved on the precessional time scales, and its longer-term evolution is governed by the external torque component
n_{\chi}
, which is responsible for the magnetic angle evolution.
In a more explicit form,

\displaystyle\tau_{\mathrm{psr}}\frac{\rm d\lambda}{\rm dt}

\displaystyle=\varepsilon_{\mu}\sin^{2}\chi\cos^{2}\chi\,+

(35)

\displaystyle\qquad{}\varepsilon_{1}\frac{\Omega_{1}}{\Omega}\left(m_{1}\cos\chi-\frac{\Omega_{1}}{\Omega}\cos^{2}\chi\right)\,+

(36)

\displaystyle\qquad{}\varepsilon_{3}\frac{\Omega_{3}}{\Omega}\left(m_{3}\cos\chi-\frac{\Omega_{3}}{\Omega}\cos^{2}\chi\right),

(37)

where
\tau_{\mathrm{psr}}
is the pulsar spin-down time, Eq. (
26
), and
m_{k}=\boldsymbol{m}\cdot\boldsymbol{e}_{k}
are the projections of the magnetic axis onto the principal axes
\boldsymbol{e}_{k}
. The three terms on the right-hand side of this equation are related to different precessional motions: the first represents the radiative precession, while the others are related to the contribution of the deformation of the star.

According to Eq. (
37
),
\lambda
changes on the spin-down timescale
\tau_{\mathrm{psr}}
, meaning that the spin axis gradually shifts from one precessional orbit to another.
From Eq. (
34
), one also infers that
\lambda
always remains constant when the spin and precessional axes become aligned. Indeed, as
\boldsymbol{n}_{\chi}=n_{\chi}\boldsymbol{s}_{2}
is orthogonal to
\boldsymbol{\Omega}
by construction,
\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{\mathrm{p}}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}_{\chi}=0
when
\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{\mathrm{p}}\parallel\boldsymbol{\Omega}
. This condition is fulfilled at the poles of the precession (
\dot{\varphi}=\dot{\theta}=0
), which coincide with points of extrema of
\lambda
. The last statement stems from the relations

\frac{\partial\lambda}{\partial\theta}=-\frac{1}{\Omega}\sin\theta\,\dot{\varphi},\quad\mbox{and }\quad\frac{\partial\lambda}{\partial\phi}=\frac{1}{\Omega}\sin\theta\,\dot{\theta},

(38)

that hold when
n_{\chi}=0
.
In other words, Eq. (
34
) (or
37
) is the generalization of the standard equation
\Omega\dot{\chi}=n_{\chi}
, that governs the evolution of
\chi
for a spherical neutron star.

The difference in precessional and spin-down time scales allows to reduce the evolution of
\boldsymbol{\Omega}
to the evolution of
\lambda
.
The latter is easier to solve with help of averaging within one precessional period (or one precessional orbit):

\langle\dot{\lambda}\rangle=\frac{1}{T_{\mathrm{p}}}\oint_{\lambda=const}\dot{\lambda}\rm dt.

(39)

Such averaging can be obtained either analytically or numerically, depending on the system’s configuration.

Below, we study in detail the long-term evolution of the magnetic angle of a deformed star by averaging Eq. (
37
) and assuming different deformations and magnetic fields.

III.3
Evolution of a spherical star: the control case

The formalism developed in the previous sections may be used to reproduced the alignment process of a spherical pulsar.
A spherical star has
\varepsilon_{1}=\varepsilon_{3}=0
, hence
\lambda=\varepsilon_{\mu}\cos^{2}\chi/2
. Eq. (
37
) then takes the form

\tau_{\mathrm{psr}}\dot{\lambda}=\varepsilon_{\mu}\sin^{2}\chi\cos^{2}\chi,

(40)

which, upon using
\dot{\lambda}=-\varepsilon_{\mu}\cos\chi\sin\chi\dot{\chi}
, reduces to the standard magnetic angle evolution law:

\Omega\dot{\chi}=n_{\mathrm{psr}}\sin\chi\cos\chi.

(41)

As
\varepsilon_{\mu}<0
,
\lambda
gradually approaches its minimal value
\lambda_{\min}=\varepsilon_{\mu}/2
over the spin-down time. On the star’s surface, points of minimal
\lambda
coincide with the magnetic poles. This process represents standard pulsar magnetic alignment.

III.6
Effects of magnetic field decay

Figure 11:
Spin axis trajectory for a triaxial star with magnetic field decay. For this simulation
\varepsilon_{\mu,0}=-3\varepsilon_{d}
has been adopted. The magnetic field history is shown by the red line in the bottom plot (and the corresponding right vertical axis). During rapid magnetic field decay, the spin axis follows the changing precession axis and ultimately aligns with one of the principal axes. This leads to a gradual increase in the magnetic angle. The radius of the precessional orbit remains practically unaffected during the magnetic field decay, as it occurs much faster than the spin-down time. In the upper panel, the gray contours and the precession poles correspond to the initial, strongly magnetized, configuration.

The rotational evolution of a neutron star, as described above, assumes a vital simplification: the constancy of its magnetic field. However, since it is expected to decay on timescales shorter than spin-down time
(
A. P. Igoshev et al. 2021
, e.g.)
, the parameter
\varepsilon_{\mu}\propto\mu^{2}
cannot be taken strictly constant. Instead, the precessional orbit of a deformed star, similar to those shown in Figure
4
, should evolve with time from larger to smaller values of
|\varepsilon_{\mu}|
. There are basically three distinct tracks: if the hydrostatic deformation

\varepsilon_{\mathrm{d}}
is always smaller than
|\varepsilon_{\mu}|
, the evolution will be similar to that of a spherical star. If
|\varepsilon_{\mathrm{\mu}}|/\varepsilon_{\mathrm{d}}<1
, the evolution will be similar to that in the left column of the table in Figure
5
. The third possibility is that
|\varepsilon_{\mathrm{\mu}}|/\varepsilon_{\mathrm{d}}
transitions from
>1
initially to
<1
at a later time.
We already know the shapes of the precessional orbits for all possible combinations of
\varepsilon_{\mu}
and
\varepsilon_{\rm d}
; the question is how
\lambda
is affected by the evolution of these parameters.

Formally, Eq. (
37
) must be supplemented in this case with a new term on the right-hand side

\tau_{\mathrm{psr}}\frac{\rm d\lambda}{\rm dt}=-\dot{\varepsilon}_{\mu}\tau_{\mathrm{psr}}\cos^{2}\chi+\tau_{\mathrm{psr}}\left(\frac{\rm d\lambda}{\rm dt}\right)_{\varepsilon_{\mu}=const}.

(44)

For a particular set of initial conditions, this evolutionary equation can be integrated numerically if needed.
However, even without formally solving this equation, the effect of the magnetic field decay can be qualitatively understood.
Relationships (
38
) imply that if
n_{\chi}=0
, the scalar
\lambda
has an analogy to a Hamiltonian of a one-dimensional oscillator described with generalized coordinate
q\equiv\varphi
, momentum
p\equiv\cos\theta
, so that the canonical equations
\partial\lambda/\partial q=-p^{\prime}
and
\partial\lambda/\partial p=q^{\prime}
are exactly fulfilled. Here, prime denotes the
\partial/(\Omega\partial t)
derivative. In this analogy,
\lambda
is the total energy of such an oscillator, while contours plotted in Figures
3
and
4
are its phase portraits. The external torque
n_{\chi}
can then be imagined as a dissipative term responsible for the energy losses. At the same time,
\varepsilon_{\mu}
is one of the parameters of such an oscillator. If
\varepsilon_{\mu}
changes on timescales longer than the precession timescale
2\pi/\Omega_{\mathrm{p}}
, but shorter than the dissipative timescale
\tau_{\mathrm{psr}}
, then the classical adiabatic invariant,

J=\frac{1}{2\pi}\oint_{\lambda=const}\cos\theta d\varphi,

(45)

should exist. It has a geometric meaning of the surface area enclosed by the
\boldsymbol{\Omega}
trajectory in the
\cos\theta-\varphi
plots. Therefore, during the evolution of the magnetic field, the spin axis will successively follow trajectories that enclose approximately the same area. For a tight and approximately circular orbit, conservation of the enclosed area also implies conservation of its angular radius.
At later stages, such a trajectory would show a
\chi
variation amplitude of about the initial diameter of its orbit.

If the magnetic field is strong enough, then the precession poles are close to the magnetic ones.
Decaying field will make the precession poles move from the magnetic axis towards one of the principal axis of inertia.
Hence, the average magnetic angle will increase, driving the pulsar towards a nearly orthogonal rotator. We illustrate this effect in Figure
11
. The contours of constant
\lambda_{\mathrm{norm}}
in this plot correspond to the initial state of
\varepsilon_{\mu,0}=-3\varepsilon_{\mathrm{d}}
(which, taking
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{d}}=\sqrt{2}\times 10^{-13}
, corresponds to
\mu_{0}\approx 1.4\times 10^{30}
G cm
3
).

To model the magnetic field evolution, we adopted a phenomenological law based on the numerical results of
D. N. Aguilera et al. 2008

B=B_{0}\frac{\exp(-t/\tau_{\mathrm{Ohm}})}{1+(\tau_{\mathrm{Ohm}}/\tau_{\mathrm{Hall}})\left(1-\exp(-t/\tau_{\mathrm{Ohm}})\right)},

(46)

where
\tau_{\mathrm{Ohm}}=10^{7}
years is the characteristic time of Ohmic decay and
\tau_{\mathrm{Hall}}=10^{4}\left(10^{15}{\rm\,G}/B_{0}\right)
years is the characteristic time scale of the Hall cascade
(
A. P. Igoshev 2019
;
A. P. Igoshev et al. 2021
)
. We assume that the magnetic field decays in accordance with this relationship until it reaches
0.05B_{0}
(after approximately three e-foldings), and then remains constant. This behavior mimics a hypothetical Hall attractor stage
(
K. N. Gourgouliatos & A. Cumming 2014a
;
K. N. Gourgouliatos & A. Cumming 2014b
)
.

During the period of rapid magnetic field decay (as indicated by the red line in the bottom plot of the figure), the average magnetic angle increases due to the motion of the precession pole. At the same time, the amplitude of the
\chi
variations,
\Delta\chi\sim
few degrees, remains nearly constant. This reflects the constancy of the adiabatic invariant (
45
).
If the magnetic field evolves faster than the spin-down time scale, it also sets the time for
\chi
evolution.
The amplitude of the effect depends on the mutual orientation of the magnetic field and the principal axes.

We conclude that a decaying magnetic field in a deformed neutron star can give rise to a gradual misalignment of the magnetic and spin axes. This is a consequence of the declining radiative precession. Old, weakly magnetized neutron stars tend to precess around one of their principal axes, but the size of the precessional orbit is likely to be defined by the earlier evolution.

IV
Observational implications

The precessional time of
\sim(10^{-4}..10^{-2})\tau_{\mathrm{psr}}
discussed above is much longer than the typical timespan of neutron star observations (
\lesssim 50
years). Such a slow precession cannot be observed directly as, e.g., a periodic component in the timing solution. However,
wobbling of the magnetic angle during the course of evolution can, nonetheless, affect the statistics of pulsar observables. Firstly, it affects the distribution of
\chi
across the NS populations. Secondly, it impacts the observables associated with high-order derivatives of
\Omega
that are sensitive to
\chi(t)
. In this section, we discuss the basic observational consequences of NS long-period precession, which can explain certain peculiarities in the available data.

IV.1
Statistics of pulsar braking indices

Eqs (
17
) and (
18
), describing the spin-down of a pulsar, may be used to predict the first and second derivatives of its spin frequency. The derivatives may then be used to calculate the so-called
braking index
4
4

4

This dimensionless parameter characterizes the physics of pulsar spin-down. In particular, if one assumes a phenomenological spindown evolution that obeys a simple power-law form,
\dot{\Omega}=K\Omega^{n}
, where
K=const
, then
n_{\mathrm{obs}}=n
.
,

n_{\mathrm{obs}}=\frac{\ddot{\Omega}\Omega}{\dot{\Omega}^{2}}.

(47)

In the most general form (assuming only
I_{0}=const
), it is

n_{\mathrm{obs}}=3-4\left(\frac{\dot{\mu}}{\mu}+f(\chi)\dot{\chi}\right)\tau_{\mathrm{ch}},

(48)

where
\tau_{\mathrm{ch}}=-\Omega/2\dot{\Omega}
, and

f(\chi)=\frac{\sin\chi\cos\chi}{1+\sin^{2}\chi}.

(49)

For pulsars with constant magnetic field

n_{\mathrm{obs}}=3+2\frac{\sin^{2}\chi\cos^{2}\chi}{(1+\sin^{2}\chi)^{2}}\in 3-3.25,

(50)

which is a clear prediction for the spin-down of a spherical neutron star. Monotonically decaying magnetic field in isolated radiopulsars may lead to larger positive values of
n_{\mathrm{obs}}\sim
few
\times 10^{2}

(
A. P. Igoshev & S. B. Popov 2020
)
. However, for hundreds of known isolated pulsars, the braking indices reach absolute values of up to
\sim 10^{6}
and are negative in approximately half of the cases. This is known as the problem of
anomalous braking indices

(
S.-N. Zhang & Y. Xie 2012
, e.g.)
.

Unfortunately, estimating
\ddot{\Omega}
(and, hence,
n_{\mathrm{obs}}
) from the observations is difficult.
While Eqs. (
17
) and (
18
) predict a smooth power-law spin-down on the time scale of
\tau_{\mathrm{psr}}
, the real rotational evolution of the majority of isolated pulsars exhibits irregularities known as “timing noise” –
quasi-periodic variations on time scales ranging from months to years
(
G. Hobbs et al. 2010
, e.g.)
. This phenomenon has been extensively studied over the past several decades, and numerous theories have been proposed to explain it
(
A. Bransgrove et al. 2025
;
P. E. Boynton et al. 1972
;
J. M. Cordes & D. J. Helfand 1980
;
F. D’Alessandro et al. 1995
;
J. O. Urama et al. 2006
;
G. Hobbs et al. 2010
;
S.-N. Zhang & Y. Xie 2012
;
A. Lyne et al. 2013
;
Z. W. Ou et al. 2016
)
. Currently, there is no broad consensus on the specific cause of the timing noise.

From the perspective of the evolutionary model considered here, it is worth pointing out that the timing noise significantly influences the higher-order derivatives of the rotational frequency, including
\ddot{\Omega}=\rm d^{2}\Omega/\rm dt^{2}
. Thus,
n_{\mathrm{obs}}
,
when estimated over a short observational timespan, can lie well outside the predicted range
3-3.25
. Although timing noise is thought to be the primary cause of anomalous
n_{\mathrm{obs}}
, evolutionary effects accumulated over timescales vastly longer than the observational span – specifically, precession – can also cause large deviations of
n_{\mathrm{obs}}
from the value predicted by the standard model. It is therefore imperative to disentangle long-term evolutionary effects in observational data from timing noise. In what follows, we offer a method for selecting out pulsars in which
\ddot{\Omega}
is likely to be
dominated by long-term evolution. We stress that even in pulsars that exhibit timing noise, long-term evolutionary effects might be important.

Such an asymmetry may be even more prominent, since the pulsar life time depends on the magnetic angle: nearly orthogonal rotators switch off more rapidly

Among more than 4000 known pulsars,

V
The effect of pinned superfluid

Thus far, we treated the NS as a deformed solid body. In reality, the NS interior, where the density approaches the nuclear density, likely
contains a neutron superfluid component threaded by an array of quantized vortices
(
G. Baym et al. 1969
)
. The bulk of the neutron superfluid resides in the core,
at densities
\rho\gtrsim 2\times 10^{14}
gr cm
-3
, while a small fraction coexists with the crystalline lattice in the inner solid crust.
The crust and the superfluid within the crust are thought to be strongly coupled through vortex creep - the pinning and unpinning of vortices to the
nuclei in the crustal lattice
(
P. W. Anderson & N. Itoh 1975
;
F. D’alessandro 1996
;
B. Haskell & A. Melatos 2015
;
M. Antonelli et al. 2022
)
. This vortex creep model has become the leading explanation for the ”glitch” activity observed in the Crab and Vela pulsars (see, e.g.,
B. Haskell & A. Melatos 2015
and references therein).

As originally shown by Shaham
(
J. Shaham 1977
)
(see also
D. I. Jones & N. Andersson 2001
;
B. Link & C. Cutler 2002
), in case of absolute pinning, superfluid neutron vortices, constrained
to move rigidly with the crust, exert
a large torque on the crust, resulting in rapid precession. The internal torque exerted on the crust by the pinned superfluid can be added to the
Euler equations by noting that the angular momentum of the superfluid, henceforth denoted by
\boldsymbol{L}_{s}
,
is fixed in a frame rotating with the star, viz.,
d\boldsymbol{L}_{s}/dt=0
. With this constraint imposed, Euler equations reduce to

\frac{d\boldsymbol{L}_{c}}{dt}+\boldsymbol{\Omega}\times(\boldsymbol{L}_{c}+\boldsymbol{L}_{s})=\boldsymbol{N},

(56)

where
\boldsymbol{\Omega}
and
\boldsymbol{L}_{c}
are the angular velocity and angular momentum of the crust (and other components tightly coupled to the crust), given by Eqs. (
4
) and (
5
), respectively, and

\boldsymbol{N}
denotes the external torque acting on the crust (Eq.
7
). Thus, the dynamics of precession in this case has an additional
effective torque
\boldsymbol{N}_{s}=-\boldsymbol{\Omega}\times\boldsymbol{L}_{s}=-I_{s}\,\boldsymbol{\Omega}\times\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{s}
, where
I_{s}
is the moment
of inertia of the pinned superfluid and
\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{s}
its angular velocity vector.
Using Eq. (
5
), we obtain

\boldsymbol{\dot{\Omega}}+\boldsymbol{\dot{\omega}}=(\boldsymbol{\omega}\times\boldsymbol{\Omega})+\boldsymbol{n}-\frac{I_{s}}{I_{0}}\,(\boldsymbol{\Omega}\times\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{s}).

(57)

For values
I_{s}/I_{0}\gtrsim 10^{-2}
inferred from analysis of glitches and other considerations
(
B. Link et al. 1999
;
A. Cheng et al. 1976
)
, we find that the RHS of the latter equation
is largely dominated by the internal torque,
producing a precessional angular frequency
\Omega_{p}\sim(I_{s}/I_{0})\Omega_{s}\gtrsim 10^{-2}\Omega
, as originally found by
J. Shaham 1977
.

The fast precessional period (minutes to hours) imposed by absolute pinning is inconsistent with the much longer precessional periods inferred in some neutron stars,
notably Hercules X-1. It has long been suspected that the superorbital period of 35 days observed in Hercules X-1 is due to free precession of the neutron star.
Recent X-ray polarization measurements with the Imaging X-ray Polarimetry Explorer (IXPE), which probe the spin geometry of the neutron star, strongly support this view
J. Heyl et al. 2024
. Nearly free precession might be possible if the internal torque exerted on the crust by the pinned superfluid is balanced by external torques. In this case a steady state exists in which the pinned superfluid follows the precessional motion of the crust through thermal vortex creep
(
A. Alpar & H. Oegelman 1987
)
.
The relaxation time to this equilibrium state depends on the ratio between the pinning and thermal energies, and is on the order of a few hundred to
a few thousand years for Her X-1. How this extends to other systems under different conditions is unclear at present. A full treatment of the
spin evolution of NS should take into account the coupling between the crust, the core and the superfluid within the crust in a self-consistent manner. Such analysis is beyond the scope of this paper and is left for future work.

VI
Summary

In this work, we have conducted a systematic investigation of the long-term rotational evolution of a deformed isolated neutron star, paying particular attention to the dynamics of the magnetic angle
\chi
(the angle between the rotation and magnetic axes). We have shown that
in contrast to the gradual monotonic alignment (
\chi\to 0
) found in the case of a spherical star, sufficiently large deformation can lead to high-amplitude cyclic variations of
\chi
over the precessional time
T_{\mathrm{p}}\approx 2\pi/\Omega_{\mathrm{p}}
, with an amplitude that evolves on the spin-down timescale
\tau_{\mathrm{psr}}\approx I_{0}\Omega/N_{\Omega}
.
We identify several evolutionary regimes distinguished
by the ratio between the “effective magnetic deformation”
\varepsilon_{\mu}
(Eq.
22
) and the deformation
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{d}}
representing the anisotropy of the star’s inertia tensor.
Most importantly, our model reproduces the peculiar distribution of the pulsar braking indices as a natural consequence of the precession-timescale evolution of a population of realistically deformed pulsars. It can also affect the distribution of magnetic angles. The main evolutionary tracks and
observational consequences can be summarized as follows:

•

When
|\varepsilon_{\mu}|\gg\varepsilon_{\mathrm{d}}
, the evolution is essentially the same as that of a spherical star;
\chi
declines monotonically up to a complete alignment of the spin and magnetic axes.

•

If
|\varepsilon_{\mu}|\ll\varepsilon_{\mathrm{d}}
, the evolution of the spin axis depends on the type of deformation, as summarized in Fig.
5
.
In particular, for triaxial deformation, an attractor state exists in which
\chi
wobbles with a finite amplitude.

•

In the intermediate case,
|\varepsilon_{\mu}|\sim\varepsilon_{\mathrm{d}}
, the rotation axis either reaches a precession pole or ends up in an attractor orbit.
In either case, the spin and magnetic axes remain well separated at all times.

•

In situations where the magnetic field decays over a timescale comparable or shorter than the spin-down time, the NS may switch its evolutionary track from a radiative-precession-dominated to one dominated by free precession. In this case, shown in Figure
11
, the angular size of the precession orbit remains approximately constant.

•

At deformation levels
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{d}}\lesssim 10^{-10}
, the expected oscillations of
\chi
can account for the abnormally huge braking indices of old pulsars. The observed growth of the braking index
n_{\mathrm{obs}}
with the characteristic pulsar age,
\tau_{\mathrm{ch}}=-\Omega/2\dot{\Omega}
, is well reproduced for a realistic pulsar population.

•

Synthetic distributions of
\chi
computed for a population of deformed stars tend to be uniform in the angle or even isotropic (for the case of triaxial deformations and magnetic field decay). Compared to the expectations for spherical pulsar populations, they show a lack of nearly aligned and an excess of nearly orthogonal rotators, in good agreement with the observations.

The method developed in this paper for analyzing rotational evolution introduces a new powerful approach for studying and understanding the dynamics and statistics of the magnetic obliquities of isolated neutron stars.

Appendix A
The evolution equation for the scalar
\lambda

Let
\Omega_{k}=\boldsymbol{\Omega}\cdot\boldsymbol{e}_{k}
be the projections of the angular velocity onto the principal axes (
k=1..3
).
Then, from the definition of the scalar parameter
\lambda
(Eq.
32
), we obtain

2\Omega^{2}\lambda=\boldsymbol{\Omega}\cdot\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{\mathrm{p}}=\varepsilon_{1}\Omega_{1}^{2}+\varepsilon_{3}\Omega_{3}^{2}+\varepsilon_{\mu}(\boldsymbol{\Omega}\cdot\boldsymbol{m})^{2},

(A1)

and

2\frac{\rm d}{\rm dt}\left(\Omega^{2}\lambda\right)=(\boldsymbol{\dot{\Omega}}\cdot\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{\mathrm{p}})+(\boldsymbol{\Omega}\cdot\boldsymbol{\dot{\Omega}_{\mathrm{p}}}).

(A2)

On the other hand, from Eqs. (
12
) and (
15
), one can write out the components of the total precessional angular velocity in the principal frame as

\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{\mathrm{p}}=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\varepsilon_{1}\Omega_{1}+\varepsilon_{\mu}(\boldsymbol{\Omega}\cdot\boldsymbol{m})m_{1}\\
\varepsilon_{\mu}(\boldsymbol{\Omega}\cdot\boldsymbol{m})m_{2}\\
\varepsilon_{3}\Omega_{3}+\varepsilon_{\mu}(\boldsymbol{\Omega}\cdot\boldsymbol{m})m_{3}\\
\end{array}\right),

(A3)

where
m_{k}
are projections of the unit vector
\boldsymbol{m}
onto principal axes. Assuming
\varepsilon_{1,3}=const
,
\varepsilon_{\mu}=const
, and
m_{k}=const
, one gets

\boldsymbol{\Omega}\cdot\boldsymbol{\dot{\Omega}_{\mathrm{p}}}=\varepsilon_{1}\Omega_{1}\dot{\Omega}_{1}+\varepsilon_{3}\Omega_{3}\dot{\Omega}_{3}+\sum_{k}\varepsilon_{\mu}(\boldsymbol{\dot{\Omega}}\cdot\boldsymbol{m})\Omega_{k}m_{k}=\frac{\rm d}{\rm dt}\left(\Omega^{2}\lambda\right),

(A4)

as
\sum_{k}\Omega_{k}m_{k}\equiv\boldsymbol{\Omega}\cdot\boldsymbol{m}
.
Taking equation (
A2
) into account, one gets

\boldsymbol{\dot{\Omega}}\cdot\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{\mathrm{p}}=\boldsymbol{\Omega}\cdot\boldsymbol{\dot{\Omega}}_{\mathrm{p}}.

(A5)

This allows to rewrite the equation (
A2
) as

2\Omega\dot{\Omega}\lambda+\Omega^{2}\dot{\lambda}=(\boldsymbol{\dot{\Omega}}\cdot\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{\mathrm{p}}).

(A6)

Let us first project Euler’s equations (
14
) onto the spin axis
\boldsymbol{s}_{3}
:

(1+\varepsilon_{1})\dot{\Omega}_{1}\frac{\Omega_{1}}{\Omega}+\dot{\Omega}_{2}\frac{\Omega_{2}}{\Omega}+(1+\varepsilon_{3})\dot{\Omega}_{3}\frac{\Omega_{3}}{\Omega}=n_{\Omega},

(A7)

which in case of a slightly deformed star (
|\varepsilon_{1,3}|\ll 1
)
gives

(\boldsymbol{\dot{\Omega}}\cdot\boldsymbol{s}_{3})=\dot{\Omega}\approx n_{\Omega}.

(A8)

On the other hand, if
\varepsilon_{k}=const
,

\boldsymbol{\dot{\Omega}}\cdot\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{\mathrm{p}}+\boldsymbol{\dot{\omega}}\cdot\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{p}=(1+\varepsilon_{1})\dot{\Omega}_{1}\Omega_{\mathrm{p,1}}+\dot{\Omega}_{2}\Omega_{\mathrm{p,2}}+(1+\varepsilon_{3})\dot{\Omega}_{3}\Omega_{\mathrm{p,3}}\approx\boldsymbol{\dot{\Omega}}\cdot\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{\mathrm{p}}.

(A9)

Thus, the Euler equations projected onto the precessional axis
\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{\mathrm{p}}
may be rewritten as

2\Omega\dot{\Omega}\lambda+\Omega^{2}\dot{\lambda}=n_{\Omega}(\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{\mathrm{p}}\cdot\boldsymbol{s}_{3})+n_{\chi}(\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{\mathrm{p}}\cdot\boldsymbol{s}_{2}),

(A10)

where

n_{\Omega}(\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{\mathrm{p}}\cdot\boldsymbol{s}_{3})=\dot{\Omega}\left(\varepsilon_{1}\frac{\Omega_{1}^{2}}{\Omega}+\varepsilon_{3}\frac{\Omega_{3}^{2}}{\Omega}+\varepsilon_{\mu}\frac{(\boldsymbol{\Omega}\cdot\boldsymbol{m})^{2}}{\Omega}\right)=2\dot{\Omega}\Omega\lambda.

(A11)

Finally, one obtains the evolutionary equation for
\lambda
under the assumption of constant deformations
\varepsilon_{k}
and fixed
\boldsymbol{m}
in a form

\Omega^{2}\dot{\lambda}=n_{\chi}(\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{\mathrm{p}}\cdot\boldsymbol{s}_{2})=\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{\mathrm{p}}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}_{\chi}.

(A12)

Then, recapping that
n_{\chi}=n_{\mathrm{psr}}\sin\chi\cos\chi
(Eq.
18
) and
\boldsymbol{s}_{2}\sin\chi={\boldsymbol{s}}_{3}\cos\chi-\boldsymbol{m}
, (Eq.
10
) one gets

\Omega^{2}\dot{\lambda}=n_{\mathrm{psr}}\Omega\lambda\cos^{2}\chi-n_{\mathrm{psr}}(\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{\mathrm{p}}\cdot\boldsymbol{m})\cos\chi,

(A13)

or

\tau_{\mathrm{psr}}\dot{\lambda}=\varepsilon_{\mu}\sin^{2}\chi\cos^{2}\chi+\varepsilon_{1}\frac{\Omega_{1}}{\Omega}\left(m_{1}\cos\chi-\frac{\Omega_{1}}{\Omega}\cos^{2}\chi\right)+\varepsilon_{3}\frac{\Omega_{3}}{\Omega}\left(m_{3}\cos\chi-\frac{\Omega_{3}}{\Omega}\cos^{2}\chi\right),

(A14)

where
\tau_{\mathrm{psr}}=-\Omega/n_{\mathrm{psr}}
is the spin-down timescale. In another form, if the definition of
\lambda
is taken into account:

\tau_{\mathrm{psr}}\frac{\rm d\lambda}{\rm dt}=(\varepsilon_{\mu}-2\lambda)\cos^{2}\chi+\left(\varepsilon_{1}\frac{\Omega_{1}}{\Omega}m_{1}+\varepsilon_{3}\frac{\Omega_{3}}{\Omega}m_{3}\right)\cos\chi,

(A15)

This equation is useful for analytically considering the precession of an isolated neutron star.
