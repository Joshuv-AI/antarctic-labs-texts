Environmental sustainability evidence in critical care medicine: a scoping review
Authors: Marko Balan, Thomas Bodley, Kiyan Heybati, Jiawen Deng, Melany Gaetani, Christina Maratta, Michelle Swab, Karen E A Burns, Srinivas Murthy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeWe sought to conduct a scoping review to summarize the existing literature on environmental sustainability in critical care medicine, methods for measuring intensive care unit (ICU)-related environmental impact, and interventions to improve ICU environmental sustainability.SourceWe conducted a systematic search of four databases to identify studies published before October 2024 pertaining to environmental sustainability and assessment of the carbon footprint of ICUs and manually screened selected journals.Principle findingsThe combined search strategy identified 1,967 potentially eligible articles. We conducted a full-text review of 149 articles, of which 99 met eligibility criteria. Articles were categorized into five domains: waste management (n = 31), carbon footprint (n = 20), sustainable medication practices (n = 9), energy delivery/consumption (n = 4), and other (n = 39), with four studies classified into two themes owing to overlap in content. Daily material waste in the ICU ranged from 4.9 to 15.2 kg per patient per day. Most studies reported poor waste sorting practices, and several studies reported reductions in waste generation with multicomponent interventions. Three carbon-footprint-themed studies calculated the carbon footprint of ICUs either through life-cycle assessments or material flow analysis. Eleven studies quantified carbon emission reductions using either prospective or theoretical stewardship interventions related to equipment, medications, or ICU care processes.ConclusionsIn this scoping review, we found a rapidly expanding yet fragmented body of evidence on ICU environmental sustainability. Important gaps exist in evidence to guide clinicians on practical bedside strategies and understanding how to implement them to drive meaningful, sustainable change.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12630-026-03142-2.Keywords: carbon footprint, critical care medicine, environmental impact, environmental sustainability, intensive care, scoping review

Human activities are the primary cause of the climate crisis, with more than a million deaths, four billion injuries, and numerous health-related consequences due to climate-related geophysical disasters over a 20-year period.1 Nevertheless, the system tasked with responding to increased climate-related health care demands is also contributing to the problem with carbon emissions from health care systems, which contribute up to 5% of the net global carbon emissions.2 The intensive care unit (ICU) has some of the highest per-patient resource use rates of any health care environment.3,4 When compared with other acute care units, an ICU can generate 1.3 times more solid waste and 3.1 times more greenhouse gases per patient per day,5 providing the opportunity for ICUs to have a central role in initiating and improving sustainability in health care.4,6

There have been several recommendations for sustainable practice in the ICU internationally.4,7,8 Despite campaigns and recommendations, there is a paucity of data summarizing evidence for specific initiatives and actions that clinicians can take to decrease the environmental impact of health care. It is also unclear which initiatives can be applied to the ICU setting, creating the need for specialty-specific recommendations.6 To address sustainability practices in critical care, we sought to conduct a scoping review to summarize the existing research on environmental sustainability, methods for measuring ICU-related environmental impact, and interventions to improve ICU environmental sustainability. We selected the scoping review methodology to enable provision of a broad perspective of the ICU sustainability evidence.

Methods

This scoping review is reported in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-analyses Extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) framework.9 We did not publish our protocol.

Literature search strategy

The initial search strategy was developed in Ovid MEDLINE® (United States National Library of Medicine, Bethesda, MD, USA/Wolters Kluwer N.V., Alphen aan den Rijn, Netherlands) by a health sciences librarian (M. S.) in collaboration with subject experts (M. B. and S. M.). The search combined Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) and keyword terms relating to ICU settings and environmental sustainability. The search strategy was then adapted for each included database: Ovid MEDLINE, Cumulative Index to Nursing and Allied Health Literature (CINAHL; via EBSCOhost, EBSCO Information Services, Ipswich, MA, USA), Embase® (Elsevier, Amsterdam, Netherlands), and Scopus (Elsevier). The date of the most recent search was 4 October 2024. Full search strategies are tabulated in Electronic Supplementary Material (ESM) eTables 1–4. Database searches were conducted from inception to October 2024.

One author (M. B.) manually reviewed the online indexes of eight critical care and/or climate change and health journals (American Journal of Respiratory and Critical Care Medicine, CHEST, Critical Care, Critical Care Medicine, Journal of Climate Change and Health, Annals of Intensive Care, Critical Care and Resuscitation, and Pediatric Critical Care Medicine) from January 2020 to September 2024.

Eligibility criteria

We included all publications, regardless of the type of article, that assessed or discussed environmentally sustainable practices in ICU settings and/or the environmental footprint of ICUs. We did not apply language restrictions. Both adult and pediatric ICUs were eligible. We excluded articles that discussed critical care procedures without explicit mentions of ICU settings. For example, studies that included acute care interventions relevant to the ICU but performed in non-ICU settings such as the operating room or emergency department were excluded.

Study selection

Two authors (J. D. and K. H.) performed title and abstract screening in duplicate with data tracking in Covidence (Veritas Health Innovation, Melbourne, VIC, Australia).10 Disagreements were resolved via consensus, and if agreement could not be reached, the article was retained. Records deemed eligible by both reviewers were subsequently retrieved and underwent full-text screening in duplicate. One author (M. B.) identified articles through review of online journal publication indexes, and those judged to meet eligibility criteria were also included.

Data extraction

Data abstraction was performed by four reviewers (M. B., T. B., J. D., and K. H.) using a standardized extraction sheet. Extracted data included basic study information such as article type, journal name, publication year, and study design, as well as study objectives, main findings, and conclusions. Studies were categorized into five domains developed a priori: 1) waste management, 2) carbon footprint, 3) energy delivery/consumption, 4) sustainable medication practices, and 5) other/unclassified. Study data were reviewed by one author (M. B.) to identify intervention-based and implementation studies which were categorized by study type and domain. Non-English articles were translated by an author with fluency in the publication language or using Google Translate (Google LLC, Mountan View, CA, USA).11

Results

Our literature search identified 1,955 articles. An additional 12 articles were included from manual review of selected journal issues for 1,967 articles. Review of titles and abstracts of the articles led to the subsequent screening of 149 full-text articles, of which 99 were included in the review (Fig. 1). The most common reason for article exclusion was non-ICU-specific setting followed by absence of sustainability-related content.

Summary of articles

From 1993 to 2024, 99 articles describing the carbon footprint or sustainability initiatives within adult, pediatric, and neonatal ICUs were published (Table 1). Over the last decade, sustainability publications in critical care have increased nearly fivefold, with 69 studies published from 2020–2024 compared with 15 in the preceding 5 years (Fig. 2). Nearly half of all publications (48/99) were secondary research articles, with the largest single category (n = 36) being nonreview articles (i.e., perspectives, commentaries, or editorials).

The largest number of articles pertained to the theme of waste management (n = 31; 31%). Carbon footprint was the second most common theme, with n = 20 (20%) included articles. Medication practices and energy delivery/consumption were relatively uncommon, comprising only n = 9 (9%) and n = 4 (4%) of the total articles included, respectively. Thirty-nine articles (39%) did not fit predominantly into one of the themes. Four articles were classified into two themes owing to considerable overlap in content (Table 1). Five articles were non-English publications, four of which were amenable to translation by Google Translate,11 and one was translated by one of the authors who reads Chinese as a native language (J. D.). Twenty-two (22%) articles evaluated interventions or implementation strategies, predominantly through quality improvement studies within the waste management and sustainable medication practices domains (Table 2). Several studies quantified material waste generation in the ICU with reported rates of total waste between 4.9 and 15.2 kg per patient per day.12–21 The most common types of waste were general and infectious (regulated medical waste). Many studies evaluated the accuracy of sorting practices and recycling potential, reporting that between 8.7% and 44% of materials were considered recyclable.17,18,22 Four papers focused on specific aspects of ICU-related waste including sharps disposal, infectious waste, unused supplies, or waste generated with renal replacement therapy.16,19,20,23

Carbon footprint

One-fifth of included articles (n = 20) related to the carbon footprint of ICU care (Table 4). Moreover, three articles used life-cycle assessment (LCA) or material flow analysis to evaluate the carbon footprint of critical care provision or processes,5,28,29 11 articles compared carbon emission reductions from completed or proposed stewardship interventions,30–40 and one randomized controlled trial (RCT) reported a reduction in carbon footprint.41 Four reviews, including one systematic review and one scoping review, summarized the carbon footprint of adult and pediatric/neonatal critical care.4,42–44

Carbon emissions reported from the three hybrid LCAs undertaken in two included articles measured 88 kg CO2 equivalents (CO2e) per patient per day (Australian ICU),29 178 kg CO2e per patient per day (American ICU),29 and 138 kg CO2e per bed per day.5 Differences in estimates were largely attributable to differences in energy sources due to different heating, ventilation, and air conditioning requirements as a result of geographical climates, in addition to some variation in the LCA system boundaries. In all three LCAs, the largest contributors to greenhouse gas emissions were energy (including electricity), gas, and consumables, underscoring the significant impact of heating, ventilation, and air conditioning particularly in hospitals with predominantly nonrenewable energy sources. Carbon emissions from material waste were measured in one article amounting to 12 kg CO2e per patient per day primarily composed of disposable gloves (49,900 kg CO2e), syringes (27,000 kg CO2e), and disposable medical clothing (24,300 kg CO2e).28

Most of the carbon footprint articles (15/20) recorded (n = 4) or projected (n = 11) the reduction in carbon emissions (footprint) through prospective interventions and theoretical comparisons. One prospective multiyear intervention to reduce unnecessary blood testing across four ICUs resulted in an annual reduction of 18,000 kg CO2e.35 A theoretical 10-fold reduction in carbon emissions was reported when switching from disposable to reusable blood pressure cuffs,36 while switching from intravenous to oral levetiracetam to treat seizures in a 15-bed ICU theoretically could save 1,480 kg CO2e annually.37 Finally, potential carbon savings were also identified by using smaller intravenous bags when possible.39

Studies using experimental designs were uncommon. We identified only one (5%) well-designed noninferiority RCT that reported that oral phosphate (vs intravenous) replacement in hypophosphatemia was safe and cost-effective and reduced the estimated carbon footprint 20-fold.41

Sustainable medication practices

Nine (9%) of the included articles addressed sustainable medication practices, most of which (n = 6; 67%) were interventional, plus an additional theoretical comparison study. Classes of medications encompassed in sustainable medication practices included antimicrobials,45 anticonvulsants,37 vasopressors,46 electrolyte replacement,41 inhaled nitric oxide,47 and analgesics/antipyretics.48 Four articles evaluated potential waste and cost savings of different drug preparation or administration practices,22,46,49,50 and three studies evaluated enteral vs intravenous administration.37,41,48

Energy delivery/consumption

Four (4%) articles discussed power delivery and consumption. One study reported on the development of a model to accurately predict electricity consumption in an ICU in the UK on the basis of patient acuity, organ support, and workplace practices.51 Another study compared start-up, standby, and operating mode energy consumption and estimated annual electricity savings of as much as 1.2 tons of CO2e savings by turning off ventilators 80% of the time when they were not in use.52 The remaining two articles described the use of solar rooftop photovoltaic panels in India to power an open care radiant warmer in a neonatal ICU53 and modeled the integration of an “electric spring” to improve power system quality in an ICU under various conditions.54

Other

Of the included articles, 39% did not clearly fit any of the prespecified domains. Of these, n = 34 (87%) were secondary research articles, including 28 commentaries, perspectives, editorials, or brief reports and 6 reviews, discussing the effects of critical care medicine on climate change and providing suggestions for sustainability initiatives. These covered a wide range of levels from bedside clinical care7,55–63 to unit/organizational level,64–66 and the infrastructure and physical design of ICUs.67–70

Only five (13%) of the unclassified articles included original research. Three of these were qualitative studies examining definitions, perspectives, and sustainability change ideas of ICU clinicians (largely nurses).71–73

Over a quarter of the unclassified articles were published in critical care nursing journals and/or focused primarily on ICU nursing practices,60,62,64,67,69,71–75 while five were published in pediatric/neonatology journals or from pediatric/neonatal ICU perspectives.62,68,69,76,77

Discussion

In this scoping review, we identified 99 unique articles related to environmental sustainability in critical care medicine across more than four domains. Despite an increasing number of publications focused on sustainability, there is a paucity of high-quality evidence available to guide ICU practitioners on specific sustainability practices. Furthermore, only two articles were primary research studies completed in Canada, highlighting a national gap in this area of investigation.78,79 This review underscores the need to prioritize intervention-based sustainability research in the ICU since existing work shows high resource consumption, high environmental impact of ICU-related activities, and a multitude of sustainability opportunities. Intervention-based studies would be well supported by research to identify behavioural change strategies to effectively incorporate sustainability interventions within the ICU.80 Intervention and behaviour change studies identified in this review included a limited number of implementation strategies,81 perhaps related to feasibility, resource constraints, and the narrow locus of control of involved clinicians. Future work should focus on developing implementation strategies and specific and effective interventions to improve environmental sustainability in critical care.

Although previous reviews have been published on sustainability,7,8 this scoping review summarizes the published literature specific to environmental sustainability in critical care. Our findings complement those of existing reviews,4,43,61,66 including those that include resource use and cost in critical care.82 Our findings also complement prior reviews in other medical specialties including surgery/anesthesiology83–85 and radiology,86 by identifying similar sustainability domains and highlighting opportunities for intervention. Further, the present review provides a broad overview of environmental sustainability initiatives and a framework of sustainability domains to help guide future research in this area. Our findings support published statements and emerging guidance on sustainability initiatives in critical care medicine.87–89

Our review shows a fivefold increase in the number of sustainability publications in critical care medicine over the last decade. As high-quality, quantitative data increase and our understanding of the carbon footprint of critical care services develops, practical and easily accessible platforms for information sharing will be imperative to drive meaningful change. There is already a living database of pharmaceutical and health care process life-cycle analyses which may be helpful in providing accurate assessments of environmental impacts;90 nevertheless, a wider scope and better understanding of the limitations and generalizability of available data are required to implement sustainability initiatives.

Only half of all publications were primary research articles, with most of these reporting observational studies, mainly from Europe and North America. Furthermore, of all articles included, only 22 involved interventions. These observations highlight the need for further study into which critical care processes are modifiable and the efficacy of specific sustainability interventions. Life-cycle assessments, although highly valuable, are neither necessary nor feasible for each process performed or item used in ICUs. Future studies should focus on highlighting the potential environmental benefits of eliminating low-value care and environmental impact assessments of tested interventions integrated within clinical trials. Among the largest theme categories, waste and carbon emissions, a mere 52% and 10% of included articles were interventional, respectively. Much of the interventional work was performed within the realm of sustainable medication practices and initiatives. This finding may reflect the early stages of descriptive research in this field; nevertheless, it may also suggest the presence of institutional barriers that hinder larger-scale sustainability initiatives such as a lack of leadership support, de-prioritization of resources for environmental changes, lack of guidance/perceived expertise in the field, or even low buy-in within the specialty. Many interventions may also be constrained by national regulations and regional/local services and thus are typically beyond the direct influence of bedside ICU clinicians. Understanding and addressing these barriers will be fundamental to long-lasting sustainable change, in addition to multidisciplinary collaboration and broadening sustainability initiatives beyond the walls of the ICU to the hospital and health care system. The overlap between critical care medicine and anesthesiology offers an ideal opportunity for crossdiscipline collaboration on sustainability initiatives.61 For example, existing literature from other specialties can be applied in ICU settings related to environmental impact of reusable vs single-use laryngoscopes,91 pulse oximeters,92 anesthetic gases,93 and procedure kits.94,95

In this review, we identified a wide range of effective interventions varying in feasibility and impact, many of which have been proposed by others.7,8 These include small-scale changes in daily bedside practice that can be easily applied by clinicians immediately, such as improving waste sorting, minimizing unnecessary supplies waste, powering off standby electronic equipment, choosing equivalent enteral/oral medications over intravenous, and establishing multidisciplinary green teams. Larger-scale, more impactful potential interventions identified include advocating for more efficient heating, ventilation, and air conditioning systems; renewable energy supply to hospitals; institutional/regional environmental sustainability strategies; and sustainable procurement practices. Nevertheless, our findings highlight a lack of studies comparing efficacy of different sustainability interventions or implementation strategies.

Finally, we identified several gaps in the literature. These include the impacts (and mitigation strategies) of commonly used pharmaceuticals and procedural equipment in the ICU. Few studies investigated reusable equipment or circular economy interventions. Collectively, these components of daily ICU care result in significant resource use and carbon emissions28 and represent ideal processes for sustainability teams to address. Furthermore, the paucity of data from low- and middle-income countries represents a missed opportunity for the global critical care community to learn from innovations in low-resourced areas. A global crisis demands global solutions, with broad collaboration including clinical, educational, research, and organizational efforts to reduce the ecologic footprint of critical care. Success will depend on the collective efforts of individual health care clinicians and the leadership of international critical/intensive care societies.88

Limitations

This scoping review has several limitations. First, we focused on critical care publications. As such, the search did not include studies conducted in other environments (operating rooms or emergency departments) that may also be relevant for the provision of acute care. Further, we did not include studies conducted in the ICU without explicit mention of environmental or sustainability concepts. Consequently, we may have overlooked articles that described environmental benefit, although not explicitly stated, such as studies specifically evaluating reduction of low-value care within ICUs, a topic of a recent review.82 The a priori-determined four-domain framework in this review has not been validated in other studies or health care settings; other frameworks may be appropriate, particularly for sustainability interventions as proposed by others.7,8 Additionally, we did not appraise the risk of bias in the included studies, which would have necessitated use of different instruments and was beyond the scope of the current review.

Conclusions

In this scoping review, we identified several publications in different domains. Nevertheless, despite the rapidly increasing body of research in the field of ICU sustainability, important gaps exist in evidence to guide ICU clinicians and health care teams regarding specific sustainability practices to implement at the bedside and the role of implementation science to drive change towards greater sustainability. This underscores the need to apply and integrate environmental sustainability research in the ICU amid data supporting high resource consumption and carbon emissions and an increasing call to identify sustainability opportunities and practices across clinical, educational, research, and organizational domains. Future work should focus on sustainable interventions and identification of potential barriers to implementation.
