The proportion of moderate-to-vigorous-intensity physical activity to total volume of physical activity is associated with cognitive and mental health in middle-aged and older adults
Authors: Xiuguo Ge, Xiaoxiang Shen, Kening Xue, Dongmeng Cai, Zhiyuan Liu, Xiaoguang Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposePhysical activity (PA) intensity remains a key determinant in managing chronic conditions. This study investigated how the proportion of moderate-to-vigorous physical activity (MVPA) relative to total volume is independently associated with cognitive function and depressive symptoms in middle-aged and older adults.MethodsA cross-sectional analysis was conducted utilizing data from 17,057 middle-aged and older adults in the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study. We used a Chinese version of the International Physical Activity Questionnaire to assess PA. Cognitive function and depressive symptoms were evaluated using the Telephone Interview for Cognitive Status and the Center of Epidemiologic Studies Depression Scale. The association between the proportion of MVPA to total PA volume and cognitive and mental health was tested using multivariable binary logistic regression analyses.ResultsThe findings indicated a suggested U-shaped pattern: 1%-33% MVPA was associated with the lowest odds of impairment particularly among older adults, for both cognitive function (adjusted odds ratio [aOR]: 0.48, 95% CI: 0.35, 0.67) and depressive symptoms (aOR: 0.57, 95% CI: 0.43, 0.76). Conversely, 67%-100% MVPA was associated with an increase in prevalence, particularly among male participants, where the favorable association with depression was no longer statistically significant (aOR: 1.01, 95% CI: 0.83, 1.22). Notably, adjusting for socioeconomic status unmasked significant favorable associations in middle-aged adults previously obscured by negative confounding.ConclusionThis study finds that MVPA is significantly linked to mental and cognitive health in older adults. Our findings identify a range associated with the lowest odds for MVPA proportion, specifically 1%-33% MVPA, that is associated with the lowest odds of impairment for cognitive impairment and depressive symptoms.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40359-026-04931-6.Keywords: Cognitive function, Depressive symptoms, Moderate-to-vigorous-intensity physical activity, Older adults, Physical activity

Introduction

China stands at the epicenter of a seismic demographic shift: global population aging. With projections indicating that the population aged 65 and older will soon surpass 400 million, the strain on public health infrastructure is becoming unprecedented [1]. Yet, extended longevity brings with it a parallel rise in neuropsychiatric multimorbidity, most notably the dual burden of cognitive impairment and late-life depression. China currently shoulders roughly 25% of the global dementia burden [2, 3], and depressive symptoms plague nearly one-fifth of its elderly citizens, severely eroding their functionality and quality of life [4, 5]. Far from being isolated issues, these conditions often feed into one another, creating a cycle that accelerates functional decline and deepens dependency [6]. Given that pharmacological solutions remain limited and the economic weight of long-term care continues to mount, the search for modifiable lifestyle factors that can fortify cognitive and mental resilience is not only important but has also become an urgent global imperative [7].

Physical activity (PA) has long been recognized as a cornerstone non-pharmacological strategy for fostering healthy aging. A wealth of epidemiological data, supported by robust meta-analyses, consistently underscores the shield that regular PA provides against both depressive symptoms and cognitive decline [8–10]. From a biological perspective, these benefits are thought to stem from enhanced neuroplasticity, which is driven by the upregulation of brain-derived neurotrophic factor, as well as reduced systemic inflammation and improved cerebral vascular function [11, 12]. In line with this evidence base, the World Health Organization has largely prioritized the accumulation of absolute volume, recommending a baseline of 150 min of moderate-intensity activity (MPA) weekly to curb chronic disease risks [13]. As a result, the scholarly landscape has been dominated by studies examining the dose-response relationship between total PA volume and health outcomes, yielding a consensus that “more is generally better” [14].

However, traditional metrics like total duration or energy expenditure (METs) obscure a critical dimension: the intensity composition. Because these absolute metrics treat different activity patterns with identical energy expenditure equally, they fail to isolate the specific effects of physical effort. The primary strength of using the moderate-to-vigorous PA (MVPA) proportion is its ability to mathematically uncouple the intensity of activity from total volume. When total volume is held constant, it remains an open question how the relative balance between MVPA and light-intensity PA (LPA) shapes neuropsychiatric outcomes. Recent insights derived from compositional data analysis posit that the structure of daily activity may be just as strongly linked to health as the absolute amount [15–18]. Although existing literature suggests that vigorous-intensity PA (VPA) confers superior benefits for mortality reduction compared to moderate intensity [19, 20], its specific implications for preserving mental and cognitive resilience in older adults remain far less distinct.

While leisure-time activity is widely championed, high-intensity occupational PA shows a more complex characteristic. Often characterized by repetitive strain, inadequate recovery, and psychosocial stress, it may actually undermine cardiovascular and mental well-being [21, 22].

This distinction is nowhere more critical than in China, where a vast cohort of rural older adults continues to shoulder the burden of labor-intensive agricultural work [23]. For this specific demographic, blanket recommendations for “higher intensity” which are devoid of contextual nuance, risk becoming counterproductive, potentially exacerbating physiological wear rather than building resilience. Yet, the intensity composition associated with better neuropsychiatric health in Asian populations remains critically under-explored, particularly concerning potential non-linear dose-response thresholds and demographic nuances [24, 25].

Therefore, the present study utilized data from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) to investigate the independent associations of MVPA with neuropsychiatric health. Our analysis was guided by three specific objectives: first, to examine how the proportion of MVPA correlates with the twin risks of depressive symptoms and cognitive impairment; second, to map potential non-linear dose-response trends in search of an intensity balance linked to the lowest prevalence of impairment; and third, to explore sociodemographic heterogeneity, comparing age and sex cohorts to account for distinct activity domains (such as agricultural labor for men versus social exercises for women) that may uniquely shape the intensity-health relationship.

Methods

Study design and participants

We conducted a cross-sectional analysis utilizing the fourth wave (2018) of the CHARLS, a nationally representative longitudinal survey designed to track the health of China’s middle-aged and elderly population. To ensure a comprehensive representation of residents aged 45 and older, the survey implements a multistage stratified probability-proportional-to-size sampling strategy. Geographically, the cohort has a broad reach, spanning 28 provinces and encompassing 150 counties or districts alongside 450 distinct villages or urban communities. Comprehensive protocols detailing the study design and sampling framework are documented elsewhere [23].

Starting with an initial cohort of 19,816 individuals recruited in the 2018 wave, we refined the dataset for analysis through a strict sequential filtering process. We first excluded participants younger than 45 years or those with unrecorded age data (n = 554). Subsequently, we removed individuals with missing values for the primary variables of interest, including PA (n = 76), cognitive function (n = 1,492), and depressive symptoms (n = 637). This selection procedure yielded a final analytic sample comprising 17,057 participants. A visual flowchart detailing participant retention is provided in Supplementary Figure S1, while a sensitivity analysis comparing the characteristics of included versus excluded participants can be found in Supplementary Table S1.

Assessment of PA

We evaluated PA profiles utilizing the International Physical Activity Questionnaire (IPAQ) Short Form, an instrument that has demonstrated robust validity and reliability within Chinese cohorts [26, 27]. Respondents were prompted to recall the frequency and average daily duration of any PA sustained for at least 10 consecutive minutes over the previous week. The assessment captured three distinct intensity tiers: VPA, defined as exertion requiring hard physical effort and heavy breathing; MPA, involving moderate effort; and LPA, which was restricted to walking activities. Specific items comprising the modified IPAQ are detailed in Supplementary Table S2.

We quantified the weekly volume for each intensity by multiplying frequency by daily duration, subsequently deriving the total PA volume as the aggregate sum of VPA, MPA, and LPA [28]. Assessment of cognitive function

We assessed cognitive performance using a global composite score derived from the modified Telephone Interview for Cognitive Status (TICS), a tool validated for its psychometric robustness in older Chinese adults [30]. This assessment battery encompassed three key domains: orientation, gauged by the participant’s ability to identify the current date and season (range: 0–5); episodic memory, measured via immediate and delayed recall of a 10-word noun list (range: 0–20); and attention/calculation, evaluated through a serial sevens subtraction task (range: 0–5). A cumulative global score was derived by summing these domain-specific values (total range: 0–30), where higher scores signify superior cognitive function. Detailed scale items are provided in Supplementary Table S3. Aligning with established thresholds in CHARLS literature [31], we defined cognitive impairment using a cutoff of < 14.

Assessment of depressive symptoms

We measured depressive symptomatology using the 10-item Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression Scale (CES-D-10), an instrument with established psychometric validity among older adults [32]. This inventory captures the frequency of specific affective experiences over the preceding week, ranging from restless sleep and loneliness to difficulty concentrating and a pervasive sense that “everything is an effort”. Respondents rated each item on a 4-point Likert scale, spanning from 0 (rarely or none of the time) to 3 (most or all of the time). To ensure scoring consistency, the two items reflecting positive affect (“hopeful about the future” and “happy”) were reverse-coded prior to summation. Cumulative scores range from 0 to 30, with higher tallies signaling elevated symptom severity. Adhering to validated cut-offs for the Chinese elderly population [33, 34], we classified a total score of ≥ 10 as indicative of clinically significant depressive symptoms. The complete item set is detailed in Supplementary Table S4.

Covariates

Covariate selection was anchored in established literature and its theoretical pertinence to neuropsychiatric etiology [35–39]. We accounted for key sociodemographic indicators, including age (continuous) and sex, alongside categorical variables such as marital status (married/cohabiting vs. divorced/widowed/never married), educational attainment (stratified from illiteracy to ≥ high school), and residence type (urban, urban-rural integration zone, or rural), occupational status (unemployment/retirement, agricultural, non-agricultural), per capita household income (low income, medium income, high income). Lifestyle profiles were captured through smoking status (never, former, current), alcohol consumption frequency, and nocturnal sleep duration (categorized as < 6 h, 6–8 h, or ≥ 8 h). To index physical health status, we constructed a somatic comorbidity score by summing self-reported, doctor-diagnosed chronic conditions (e.g., hypertension, diabetes, cardiovascular disease, arthritis). Crucially, to prevent analytical circularity, psychiatric and memory-related disorders were strictly excluded from this calculation. The resulting comorbidity burden was stratified into three levels: 0, 1, or ≥ 2 conditions. Social participation was operationalized as engagement in at least one communal activity (such as interacting with friends, playing mahjong/chess, or attending community clubs) during the preceding month. Finally, to disentangle the specific effects of intensity composition from total volume, we included the absolute quantity of PA (minutes/week) as a continuous covariate in all multivariate models.

Statistical analysis

Characteristics of the study participants were stratified according to the proportion of MVPA to total PA volume (categorized as: No activity, 0%, 1–33%, 34–66%, and 67–100%). Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages, with differences across groups assessed using chi-square tests.

To estimate odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs), we employed multivariable binary logistic regression models. Crucially, all analyses incorporated robust standard errors clustered at the community ID level to rigorously account for the complex sampling design and intragroup correlations. Our modeling strategy proceeded in two stages: a crude model followed by a fully adjusted model that controlled for sociodemographic, lifestyle factors, health status, social participation, and total PA volume. To capture distinct facets of the association, we adopted a dual-reference approach: first, using the inactive group to benchmark general activity benefits; and second, using the 0% MVPA group to specifically isolate the incremental value of increasing intensity among active individuals.

We further conducted stratified analyses to explore heterogeneity by sex and age (middle-aged: 45–64 years or older adults: ≥ 65 years). To probe for non-linear dose-response patterns, we plotted prevalence rates with 95% CIs and initially tested for non-linearity by including a quadratic term in the logistic regression models. Finally, the robustness of our primary findings was tested through several sensitivity analyses, including applying stricter diagnostic criteria (CES-D-10 score ≥ 12; TICS score < 10) and excluding potentially confounding subgroups, such as illiterate participants or those without chronic conditions, to minimize reverse causality. The detailed results of these sensitivity analyses are presented in Supplementary Table S5 and Supplementary Table S6.

All statistical computations were executed using Stata 18.5 (StataCorp, College Station, TX, USA), with statistical significance set at a two-sided alpha of p < 0.05.

Results

Table 1 presents the characteristics of the 17,057 study participants, stratified by the proportion of MVPA. Statistical analysis revealed heterogeneity across the five intensity groups regarding all sociodemographic, lifestyle, and health indicators (all p < 0.001). Compared to the active groups, participants in the “No activity” group were generally older (21.4% aged ≥ 75 years), more likely to be female (54.9%), and had a higher proportion of illiteracy (31.2%) and comorbidity (≥ 2 chronic conditions: 19.3%). Conversely, the group with the highest MVPA proportion (67%-100%) displayed a demographic profile consistent with rural manual labor: these individuals resided overwhelmingly in rural areas (80.6%) and reported the highest prevalence of current smoking (28.7%).

As illustrated in Fig. 1, a distinct inverse trend between PA intensity and disease burden was observed with advancing age. Specifically, the average contribution of MVPA (represented by bars, left axis) undergoes a steady erosion, dropping from 47.0% among middle-aged participants (45–54 years) to just 27.0% in the oldest cohort (≥ 75 years). In parallel, the burden of neuropsychiatric disease (plotted on the right axis) climbs steadily. This trend is most alarming for cognitive impairment (red dashed line), which exhibits a precipitous surge, with the prevalence skyrocketing from 29.0% in the youngest demographic to 72.4% among the oldest-old. Depressive symptoms (blue solid line) track a similar upward path, though the gradient is more tempered, rising from 34.2% to 40.4%.

Figure 2 visualizes the complex, non-linear relationship between MVPA proportion and cognitive impairment and depressive symptoms. Instead of a simple linear gradient, the data indicated a suggested U-shaped pattern for both cognitive impairment and depressive symptoms. The prevalence of impairment was lowest within the 1%-33% MVPA, where prevalence rates were lowest for both cognitive impairment (29.0%) and depressive symptoms (34.2%). In sharp contrast, a discernible increase in prevalence was observed in the 67%-100% MVPA group. Here, prevalence rates surged to 72.4% for cognitive impairment and 40.4% for depression, representing levels that not only rival but, in some cases, surpass those observed in completely inactive individuals.

Table 2 details the multivariable associations stratified by sex. When benchmarking against “No activity”, engaging in PA was consistently associated with lower odds of both outcomes for men and women alike. However, the narrative becomes more nuanced when we switch the reference to the 0% MVPA group to isolate the specific value of intensity: here, distinct sex-specific heterogeneity emerges. Women exhibited a clear non-linear response regarding cognitive impairment. While the 1%-33% MVPA showed the strongest association with lower odds relative to inactivity (adjusted odds ratio [aOR]: 0.54, 95% CI: 0.39, 0.74), this advantage was partially eroded in the highest intensity bracket. Men, by contrast, followed an “L-shaped” trend. The favorable association remained significant even at the 67%-100% MVPA (aOR: 0.50, 95% CI: 0.39, 0.65). This sex disparity was even more stark for depressive symptoms. Relative to 0% MVPA, men in the 67%-100% MVPA category saw the favorable association become non-significant (aOR: 1.01, 95% CI: 0.83, 1.22).

Similarly, women also experienced an attenuation of associations at the highest intensity; while 1%-33% MVPA was significantly associated with lower odds of depression (aOR: 0.73, 95% CI: 0.60, 0.90), this advantage was lost in the 67%-100% group (aOR: 1.04, 95% CI: 0.87, 1.24).

Table 3 delineates the associations stratified by age cohort. Among middle-aged adults (45–64 years), we uncovered a “masking association” influenced by socioeconomic confounders, particularly regarding cognitive impairment. In crude models referencing the 0% MVPA group, 67%-100% MVPA was paradoxically associated with higher odds of cognitive impairment (crude odds ratio [OR]: 1.11, 95% CI: 0.99, 1.25). After adjustment, this association was reversed, becoming statistically significant (aOR: 0.79, 95% CI: 0.66, 0.96). For depressive symptoms in this demographic, benefits were more narrowly confined: a significant favorable association was evident only in the 1%-33% MVPA group (aOR: 0.74, 95% CI: 0.61, 0.90), with higher intensity compositions not being significantly associated with further lower odds. The narrative shifts for older adults (≥ 65 years), where associations followed a distinct U-shaped trend, a pattern most pronounced for cognitive impairment.

While a favorable association remained in the 67%-100% MVPA, the magnitude of the association was less pronounced and not statistically significant (aOR: 0.81, 95% CI: 0.64, 1.02). Discussion

Our primary finding indicates that an MVPA proportion of 1%-33% shows a strong association with lower odds of cognitive impairment and depressive symptoms. This challenges the prevailing assumption that “higher intensity is invariably better” [13, 19]. Instead of a linear payoff, our data map distinct U-shaped dose-response trends across both cognitive impairment and depressive symptoms. Crucially, this favorable association was attenuated in the 67%-100% MVPA group, which showed higher odds of impairment compared to the 1%-33% MVPA group. In specific subgroups, this plateau even shifted into a notable increase in the prevalence of impairment [40]. However, the significant baseline heterogeneity across MVPA groups (Table 1) suggests that these associations may partly reflect underlying sociodemographic differences rather than a pure dose-response effect. Collectively, these patterns suggest that intensity maximization may not be ideal, supporting instead the potential value of a “balanced activity portfolio”. For aging populations, a regimen anchored in light activity and punctuated by moderate bouts is associated with better outcomes than exclusive reliance on high-intensity exertion.

The non-linear patterns and increased impairment prevalence within the 67–100% MVPA group likely reflect a combination of methodological artifacts and sociocultural factors. Methodologically, deriving a proportion from two self-reported metrics can compound measurement errors. For individuals with low total activity, small recall inaccuracies may disproportionately inflate the MVPA proportion. Consequently, this observed increase in prevalence may partially stem from random error amplification rather than a true biological threshold. Socioculturally, extreme MVPA proportions in the CHARLS cohort predominantly represent labor-intensive agricultural work rather than leisure exercise. This aligns with the “PA paradox” [21], which distinguishes between the neuroprotective effects of recreational activity and the potentially toxic effects of high-intensity occupational exertion. Physiologically, unremitting strenuous labor, which is characterized by repetitive strain and inadequate recovery [22, 41], may trigger systemic inflammation and elevated cortisol, exerting a neurotoxic toll that counteracts the benefits of exercise [42]. Psychologically, engaging in heavy labor for survival reflects a lack of autonomy. This makes such physical exertion a marker of socioeconomic hardship linked to depression, rather than a neuroprotective benefit. This dynamic vividly reflects the sociocultural fabric of rural China, where older adults frequently remain the agricultural backbone. However, given the lack of direct measures distinguishing occupational from leisure PA, the link between extreme MVPA proportions and agricultural strain remains an interpretative hypothesis.

To explain the distinct “L-shaped” trend observed in women, we hypothesize that their activity profiles might be more frequently characterized by domestic roles or communal recreations like the ubiquitous “square dancing”. Distinct from heavy labor, these modalities typically combine manageable physiological exertion with high levels of social connectivity [43], which may synergistically buffer against depression and cognitive decline [44]. By weaving social support into the fabric of physical movement, these activities show a favorable association across the entire intensity spectrum, preventing the “wear and tear” observed in men [45]. While the statistical contrast is clear, whereby men exhibit a suggested U-shaped pattern and women maintain an L-shaped association, this sociocultural divergence remains an interpretative speculation that requires future qualitative and quantitative validation.

Our age-stratified analysis reveals a “masking phenomenon” driven by negative confounding among middle-aged adults (45-64 years). In crude models, high MVPA burdens paradoxically appeared to harm cognitive function. Yet, adjusting for socioeconomic factors revealed a significant association with lower odds. This likely stems from China's socioeconomic context, where middle-aged adults in the 67%-100% MVPA group are disproportionately manual laborers with limited education [46]. As a strong risk factor for cognitive decline and a proxy for diminished “cognitive reserve” [47], low education may obscure the favorable associations of PA in unadjusted models [3]. Accounting for these confounders separates the biological benefits of movement from the disadvantages of low socioeconomic status. For older adults (≥ 65 years), however, the favorable associations remained robust across all models. This stability likely reflects a post-retirement transition in activity domains. At this stage, high-intensity exertion shifts from a survival necessity (e.g., labor) to a discretionary choice (e.g., exercise), decoupling physical effort from socioeconomic hardship [48].

Our sensitivity analysis of participants with chronic multimorbidity yielded a notable finding. In this vulnerable demographic, 0% MVPA was not significantly associated with better cognitive outcomes. However, the 1%-33% MVPA proportion showed a significant, favorable association. This supports the hypothesis of a “physiological intensity threshold”, representing the minimum activation level potentially required for individuals with impaired physiological resilience [9]. Chronic disease creates an environment of systemic inflammation and vascular dysfunction.

Thus, the gentle stimulus of LPA may be insufficient to counteract this pathological burden [49]. MVPA, by contrast, appears necessary to generate the hemodynamic shear stress [50] and stimulate the release of neurotrophic factors [51, 52], which ultimately drive endothelial repair and neurogenesis [53]. Clinically, these findings suggest that the generic advice to “simply move more” may be insufficient for multimorbid older adults [54]. Instead, a specific dose of MVPA may be important to breach this pathological threshold and support cognitive health.

Several limitations inherent to the study design warrant acknowledgment. First, the cross-sectional nature of the data precludes definitive causal inferences.

Consequently, reverse causality remains an unavoidable limitation; for example, individuals experiencing early cognitive decline or depressive symptoms may naturally reduce their PA. While sensitivity analyses support the consistency of our findings, they cannot establish temporal directionality. Second, our primary exposure relies on a calculated ratio derived from self-reported metrics, which may amplify inherent measurement errors. Furthermore, ratio-based exposures introduce mathematical coupling and interpretational ambiguity. Identical MVPA proportions can represent vastly different absolute activity levels (e.g., 30 vs. 300 minutes both yielding 100%). Although we adjusted for total PA volume, defining what extreme categories (e.g., 67–100%) represent in real-world behaviors remains challenging. Third, despite adjusting for multiple covariates, residual confounding remains likely. We lacked specific data on occupational PA, limiting our ability to disentangle the effects of intentional exercise from occupational strain. Additionally, utilizing a composite comorbidity count may not fully capture the unique confounding effects of specific conditions, such as chronic lung disease, kidney disease, or obesity. Future studies should control for individual conditions to improve participant comparability. Finally, while our large sample size and the use of relative proportions may partially mitigate some systematic recall biases, they do not resolve the fundamental limitations of the study design. Although our sample is restricted to China, the country's unique dual urban-rural structure still offers a valuable paradigm for other nations undergoing similar demographic transitions.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study finds that MVPA is significantly associated with mental and cognitive health in older adults. Our findings identify a range associated with the lowest odds for MVPA proportion, specifically 1%-33% MVPA, that is associated with the most favorable reduction in risks for depressive symptoms and cognitive impairment. In contrast to the linear assumption that higher intensity invariably confers greater benefits, our results suggest a nuanced dose-response relationship.

Specifically, we observed potentially higher odds of impairment associated with 66-100% MVPA and limited benefit from exclusively 0% MVPA in certain subgroups.

Future study is essential to clarify the benefits of moderate MVPA and the potential harms of excessive strain. Establishing these causal links is a prerequisite for incorporating a “balanced activity portfolio” into public health guidelines for older adults.
