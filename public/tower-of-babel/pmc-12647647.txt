Ophthalmology education and systemic disease integration in Syrian medical schools: a cross-sectional assessment of knowledge gaps
Authors: Saja Karaja, Khayry Al-Shami, Ayham Qatza, Omran Faour
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Ophthalmology plays a crucial role in medical practice by addressing vision impairment and detecting systemic diseases. However, ophthalmology education has declined globally despite the high prevalence of preventable blindness. A cross-sectional survey examined demographics, clinical experience, and training perspectives among Syrian fifth- and sixth-year medical students and ophthalmology residents in Syria. Data were collected over three months using Google Forms and analyzed with SPSS. Ethical approval and informed consent were obtained. Among 500 medical trainees (age range 18–35 years, with balanced gender distribution), 281 participants (56.2%) reported limited clinical exposure to systemic diseases, while only 103 participants (20.6%) demonstrated high awareness of ocular manifestations. Participants most frequently recognized ocular manifestations of hypertension and diabetes. Awareness and diagnostic confidence increased significantly with age and clinical experience (p < 0.05). This study reveals significant gaps in trainees’ understanding of ocular manifestations of systemic diseases. These findings emphasize the urgent need for improved ophthalmology education to enhance diagnostic and management skills.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-25831-6.

Introduction

Ophthalmology significantly impacts quality of life, with vision impairment widely recognized as highly debilitating1. The World Health Organization reports that 2.2 billion people globally have vision impairment or blindness, with approximately 1 billion cases being preventable or treatable2. Many systemic diseases present with ocular manifestations as the first indicators of underlying pathology. Healthcare practitioners require adequate ophthalmological training to recognize and manage these conditions effectively. Such training improves public health outcomes3,4. Medical schools inadequately prepare students in ophthalmology despite its clinical importance. Ophthalmic conditions account for 1.5–11.8% of primary care consultations and 20% in patients over 65; however, medical schools globally are reduceing ophthalmology teaching time. A 2024 systematic review demonstrated declining ophthalmology education globally, with approximately 75% of medical students reporting low confidence in ophthalmological knowledge and 67% feeling inadequately skilled in ocular assessments. This educational deficit may compromise patient outcomes through delayed diagnosis and missed systemic disease indicators. Medical education requires enhanced ophthalmological training incorporating systemic disease recognition and practical assessment skills1. Syrian medical education follows a six-year curriculum similar to international systems but faces unique challenges from prolonged conflict and infrastructure damage. While global literature documents declining ophthalmology education standards, Syrian institutions experience additional barriers including faculty emigration and resource limitations. This study addresses Syrian-specific knowledge gaps while contributing to understanding of ophthalmology education challenges in crisis-affected settings.

Methods

Study design and population

This cross-sectional study surveyed medical students from seven Syrian public universities (Damascus, Hama, Aleppo, Tishreen, Homs, Tartous, and Al-Furat) and private institutions. The cross-sectional design enabled simultaneous assessment across multiple training levels while maintaining methodological validity for educational gap identification. Given documented infrastructure constraints in Syrian medical education, this approach provided the most feasible systematic assessment method. Participants included fifth- and sixth-year medical students who had completed ophthalmology curricula and ophthalmology residents in training. Syrian medical curricula incorporate ophthalmology education during the fifth and sixth clinical years according to academic frameworks. Participants were included based on curriculum exposure rather than demonstrated competency. Data collection occurred from October 12, 2024, to January 12, 2025.

Survey administration

Researchers distributed the survey via Google Forms to eligible participants. The survey link was distributed through email, mobile messaging, and social media platforms (primarily Telegram).

Sample size and demographics

Sample size was calculated using cochran’s sample size formula. The formula used a 95% confidence level (Z = 1.96), 5% margin of error (e), and a 50% population proportion (p) assumption. Study instruments

The anonymous survey contained no identifiable data and comprised five sections. Participants provided informed consent before accessing survey questions. The questionnaire included five sections: (1) demographic characteristics; (2) general awareness of systemic disorders with ophthalmic manifestations; (3) specific knowledge of systemic diseases and associated ocular symptoms; (4) clinical practice patterns and perceived importance of ophthalmological knowledge; and (5) opinions on improving ophthalmology expertise and identifying educational challenges.

Data quality control

Questionnaires were translated from English to Arabic for Syrian participants and are provided as Supplementary Files 1 and 2. Back-translation to English verified translation accuracy and consistency. Pilot testing with 30 participants demonstrated good internal consistency (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.82). The online questionnaires included written instructions and required approximately 10 min to complete.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was performed using SPSS version 22 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Continuous variables are presented as means ± standard deviations, and categorical variables are presented as frequencies and percentages. Chi-square tests assessed associations between categorical variables. A p-value of ≤ 0.05 was considered significant.

Ethical considerations

The study obtained informed consent from all participants and adhered to ethical research standards. The first question in the questionnaire was designed to ask participants whether they agreed to participate in the survey.

Table 3 summarizes encounters with systemic-ocular diseases and associated diagnostic confidence. Encounters were reported by 58.6% (n = 293). Confidence levels were distributed as follows: high in 4.2% (n = 21), moderate in 42.8% (n = 214), uncertain in 48.8% (n = 244), and low in 4.2% (n = 21). Diabetes mellitus was cited by 97.4% (n = 487) as a key systemic disease with ocular sequelae. Hypertension and systemic lupus erythematosus were cited by 96.2% (n = 481) and 86.2% (n = 431), respectively. Only 49.4% (n = 247) recognized sarcoidosis as a relevant systemic condition.

Table 3 also lists the specific ocular manifestations reported. The most commonly cited hypertension-related manifestations were retinopathy, 95.6% (n = 478), and optic neuropathy, 37.2% (n = 186). Reported SLE manifestations were as follows: dry-eye syndrome, 64.4% (n = 322); retinal vasculitis, 45.6% (n = 228); uveitis, 39.8% (n = 199); and scleritis, 26.8% (n = 134). The diabetes-related sequelae identified were as follows: retinopathy, 96.0% (n = 480); cataract, 55.8% (n = 279); glaucoma, 47.6% (n = 238); and vitreous hemorrhage, 33.8% (n = 169).

Table 4 examines associations between demographics and awareness levels. High awareness increased with age: 18–24 years, 17.3%; 25–30 years, 36.6%; and 31–35 years, 50.0%. The trend was significant (χ² = 22.98, p = 0.007). Gender differences emerged in awareness and confidence. High awareness was greater in males (25.2%) than in females (16.1%), whereas moderate awareness predominated in females (63.7% vs. 53.2%). The difference was significant (χ² = 14.65, p = 0.005). Institutional differences were not significant (χ² = 29.72, p = 0.377). Nonetheless, high awareness rates were highest at Tartous University, 28.5% (n = 2/7); private universities, 27.0% (n = 13/48); and Aleppo University, 26.4% (n = 18/68). Hama University showed high awareness in 12.4% (n = 18/145) and moderate awareness in 61.3% (n = 89/145). Awareness differed by training stage: fifth-year students, 8.7% (n = 19/217) versus PGY4 residents, 85.7% (n = 6/7). Among residents, 85.7% (n = 6/7) of PGY4 trainees demonstrated high awareness. The progression in knowledge levels was significant (χ² = 81.65, p < 0.001).

Discussion

This cross-sectional study of 500 medical trainees from Syrian universities examines ophthalmology education and its integration with systemic disease knowledge. The study identifies significant gaps in trainees’ exposure, awareness, and diagnostic confidence. These gaps vary by demographic variables, training levels, and institutional affiliations. Five primary themes emerge: (1) knowledge gaps in medical curricula as a global challenge; (2) demographic influences on ophthalmological knowledge; (3) exposure modalities and knowledge acquisition; (4) curricular gaps in systemic-ocular integration; and (5) curriculum recommendations for future directions. These themes highlight significant educational challenges and propose corresponding solutions.

Knowledge gaps in medical curricula as a global challenge

These results align with global trends in ophthalmology curricula gaps and highlight region-specific patterns requiring targeted educational interventions. A significant proportion of participants had inadequate ophthalmology exposure during medical training. Although 66.4% of respondents had access to research, mentorship, or clinical observation, 33.6% had no prior exposure, indicating a substantial curriculum gap. These findings confirm documented evidence of declining ophthalmology content in medical curricula. A United States study found reduced ophthalmology content in medical school curricula5. Research from Saudi Arabia revealed significant gaps in the ophthalmology curriculum: 35.8% in basic topics, 61.3% in clinical content, 26.4% for prevalent diseases, and 39.6% for common emergencies6. In this study, 58.6% of respondents demonstrated moderate awareness of systemic disease symptoms, while only 20.6% showed high awareness of systemic diseases causing ocular symptoms. These findings indicate significant disparities in exposure and awareness, highlighting the crucial need to improve ophthalmology training in systemic disease manifestations. A United Kingdom study reported major disparities in curriculum organization and teaching methods, failing to meet the International Council for Ophthalmology (ICO) recommendations7. These challenges reflect international issues in vision health education, rather than isolated or local ones.

Demographic influences on ophthalmological knowledge

Age-related proficiency development

Knowledge acquisition was positively correlated with age and training level. Awareness of systemic-ocular disease associations increased with age: 17.33% (ages 18–24), 36.62% (25–30), and 50.0% (31–35). This pattern reflects the incremental integration of clinical experience, as senior trainees accumulate patient interactions and specialized coursework. Residents showed markedly higher proficiency: 85.71% of PGY4 residents had high awareness, compared to 8.76% of fifth-year medical students. Limitations in early exposure particularly affected students from smaller medical schools and underrepresented minorities8. These findings underscores the impact of residency programs, with PGY4 residents showing higher awareness than preclinical students. These trends were statistically significant (χ² = 81.65, p < 0.001), supporting the value of clinical immersion.

Gender disparities in diagnostic confidence

Gender representation was nearly equal (50.8% female, 49.2% male); however, awareness levels differed significantly between groups. Males demonstrated higher high-awareness rates than females (25.20% vs. 16.14%). Conversely, females showed higher moderate awareness rates (63.78% vs. 53.25% for males). This gender disparity requires investigation of underlying sociocultural and academic factors. This finding reflects documented international patterns where female medical students report lower confidence despite equivalent performance9. The Syrian cultural context adds complexity to these findings. Research demonstrates that gender influences educational perceptions and self-reporting behaviors among Syrian medical students10. These differences may explain why female participants demonstrated moderate awareness levels despite potentially equivalent knowledge, suggesting cultural influences on confidence reporting rather than competency deficits. This phenomenon extends beyond cultural considerations to include documented systemic bias. Huh et al. demonstrated that female postgraduate year-four residents (n = 452) received lower medical knowledge ratings at the midyear evaluation compared to male peers. However, this difference disappeared by graduation. The findings emphasize that biased assessments can negatively influence career opportunities for trainees, with long-term consequences stemming from these initial disparities11. The observed awareness gap may reflect both educational differences and potentially systemic biases in knowledge evaluation in ophthalmology training. A comprehensive review identified eight domains of ongoing gender inequity in ophthalmology: leadership; research and academics; income; surgical exposure and subspecialization; harassment; career satisfaction; mentorship; and family obligations. Furthermore, the review indicates that female ophthalmologists and trainees report significantly different training experiences compared to their male counterparts. Specifically, women encounter fewer operative opportunities, increased bullying and harassment, limited mentorship access, and different expectations regarding family responsibilities12. These experiential differences may affect knowledge acquisition and confidence in knowledge expression. These factors may explain why female participants in the current study demonstrated moderate rather than high awareness levels.

Challenges to ophthalmology education

Gender disparities reflect broader systemic challenges affecting ophthalmology education. Syrian medical curricula incorporate systemic-ocular disease content; however, implementation has been compromised by infrastructure damage and faculty attrition. Systematic evaluation reveals that while curricula include ophthalmology content, teaching varies significantly across institutions due to faculty shortages and equipment loss13. Consequently, 33.6% of participants reported no meaningful ophthalmology exposure despite official curriculum requirements. The international literature suggests several contributing factors: curriculum time constraints, absence of mandatory rotations, and resource limitations. The Syrian context exacerbates these challenges through infrastructure constraints and faculty emigration. Implementation deficits align with international patterns, where only 20–36% of medical schools globally adequately implement ICO-recommended content14. Syrian institutions face additional conflict-specific challenges that exacerbate these educational gaps. Research consistently highlights curriculum standardization deficits. Shah et al. identified the absence of ophthalmology education guidelines from the Liaison Committee on Medical Education, the organization responsible for curriculum development and medical institutions accreditation15. Although the ICO has provided guidelines for ophthalmology practice, its non-mandatory status results in limited adoption by medical schools. Studies indicate that only 20% of Canadian medical schools incorporate ICO guidelines into their core curricula16. This absence of mandatory guidelines results in significant variability in ophthalmology programs content and duration among institutions. Tartous University trainees exhibited higher awareness rates than Hama University trainees (28.57% vs. 12.41%). Institutional differences did not reach statistical significance (χ² = 29.72, p = 0.377). However, the observed variation may indicate differences in resource access or curricular prioritization among Syrian medical schools that warrant further investigation. Moxon et al. reported that while 94.7% of medical institutions in the United States and Canada provide ophthalmology exposure, the format varies significantly: 78.9% offer only preclinical coursework, 13.7% provide both preclinical and clinical coursework, and 7.4% focus exclusively on clinical exposure5. Chan et al. assessed medical school ophthalmology curricula and found significant variation in instructional duration across Canadian institutions17. Furthermore, private university participants (9.6% of the sample) demonstrated intermediate performance, suggesting inadequate pedagogical frameworks that may contribute to theoretical-practical knowledge gaps. Ophthalmology education programs remain inconsistent across countries, creating significant challenges for the field.

Exposure modalities and knowledge acquisition

Clinical and academic pathways

Prior clinical exposure significantly determined student knowledge levels. Among trainees with prior clinical experience in systemic disease management, 24.20% demonstrated high awareness compared to 17.79% in the non-exposed group. Medical school curriculum and clinical ophthalmology rotations were the most frequently cited resources for ophthalmology learning, while research ranked third among reported resources. Training methods including didactic lectures, case presentations, and slide demonstrations offer various advantages, although their efficacy varies18,19. Li et al. demonstrated that supplementary ophthalmology lectures significantly enhanced third-year students’ knowledge and interest in teaching20. Moreover, extended lecture duration may enhance student proficiency and foster greater interest in the field21,22. Similarly, mentors, mandatory rotations, and elective courses served as valuable educational resources. Approximately 35.7% of Canadian medical schools require an ophthalmology rotation, typically lasting under two weeks16. Furthermore, our finidngs support integrating research activities into medical training. Regular academic exposure (56.8%) and research participation (2.2%) synergistically enhanced competency. Research-involved trainees demonstrated significantly higher awareness levels (47.37%). A United States study of residents and medical students found that 63% identified research as their preferred learning resource23. These findings underscore the importance of fostering a research-oriented mindset through specialized programs, mentorship, and curriculum integration. Collectively, these data indicate limited ophthalmology learning opportunities for medical students. Addressing this gap requires an integrated ophthalmology-systemic disease curriculum and increased clinical exposure opportunities. Early clinical exposure significantly enhances student interest and career consideration.

The relationship between confidence and competence

This study identified a significant gap between clinical exposure levels and diagnostic confidence. Despite 58.6% of participants encountering systemic diseases with ocular manifestations, 48.8% could not identify this connection. Only 4.2% reported high diagnostic confidence. Similarly, United Kingdom studies demonstrate that inadequate undergraduate education creates critical knowledge gaps in ophthalmology, particularly affecting emergency department staff24. Additionally, 63.9% of senior house officers reported insufficient confidence in managing ocular emergencies25. Consequently, clinical exposure alone proved insufficient for developing diagnostic competencies. This gap may be attributed to an overemphasis on pathology memorization combined with insufficient differential diagnosis and clinical reasoning training.

Furthermore, results demonstrate a strong correlation between diagnostic confidence and competence. Participants with higher confidence scores in ocular symptom identification demonstrated significantly greater awareness of the systemic-ocular connection. A Canadian survey of 386 first-year residents found that 64% of them had minimal ophthalmology exposure, with 76.2% receiving one week or less of training. While residents mastered basic skills such as visual acuity assessment and pupillary reflexes, they lacked competency in fundoscopy, slit-lamp examination, and intraocular pressure assessment26. These findings warrant the development of targeted training interventions and mentorship programs to enhance student self-efficacy in ophthalmic skills. The statistical significance (χ² = 187.48, p < 0.001) underscores that confidence develops through repeated clinical experiences rather than innate ability.

Curricular gaps in systemic-ocular integration

Participants’ knowledge of ocular manifestations varied significantly across different systemic diseases. Participants demonstrated high awareness of hypertension-hypertensive retinopathy (95.60%) and diabetes-diabetic retinopathy (96.00%). However, awareness was lower for glaucoma-related manifestations (47.60%) and scleritis (26.80%). This disparity likely reflects the curricula’s emphasis on common conditions while neglecting rare systemic-ocular associations. This curricular narrowing predisposes trainees to diagnostic anchoring bias, limiting recognition of unusual autoimmune or infectious presentations. The substantial proportion of respondents limiting ocular manifestations to diabetes and hypertension is concerning. These findings indicate the need for comprehensive training in systemic disease-ocular associations. Without fundamental ophthalmic skills, primary care physicians may fail to distinguish between stable conditions and ocular emergencies, potentially causing irreversible vision loss before specialist referral27. Direct ophthalmoscopy may reveal serious systemic diseases, including papilledema indicating elevated intracranial pressure or space-occupying lesions, cytomegalovirus retinitis, and endocarditis that require further investigations28,29. While participants demonstrated adequate knowledge of major systemic disease-ocular relationships, targeted education in rare conditions would improve diagnostic accuracy.

Curriculum recommendations for future directions

The current study highlights critical areas for enhancing ophthalmology education among Syrian medical trainees. The findings reveal knowledge gaps, limited clinical exposure, and minimal research participation. The data inform targeted solutions that align with empirical evidence and the scientific literature.

Research-enhanced medical training

Integrating active research components into medical training represents a vital strategy for improving ophthalmology competency. The data support this approach: 47.37% of participants involved in research activities demonstrated higher awareness levels compared to those receiving the standard curriculum only. Research opportunities such as scientific journal clubs and capstone projects foster critical thinking, clinical reasoning, and scholarly engagement relevant to systemic-ocular disease.

Digital microlearning and interactive online platforms accommodate modern learner preferences. These platforms offer accessible, concise modules on evolving themes such as biologics-associated ocular toxicity30. Empirical evidence demonstrates that supplementary research exposure enhances knowledge retention and motivates sustained interest in ophthalmology.

Clinical exposure and curriculum standardization

Structured clinical exposure improves diagnostic awareness among trainees. Those with relevant clinical experience achieved higher proficiency rates than those without exposure (24.20% versus 17.79%). This finding supports mandatory ophthalmology clinical rotations that integrate bedside teaching, simulation, and multidisciplinary case conferences to bridge theory and practice.

Performance variability across institutions, with awareness rates from 12.41% to 28.57%, indicates the need for nationwide curriculum standardization. Standardization efforts should incorporate ICO guidelines to ensure consistent educational quality and comprehensive systemic-ocular disease integration.

Study limitations and strengths

This study is the first comprehensive assessment of ophthalmology education knowledge gaps among Syrian medical trainees. It provides essential baseline data for educational reform initiatives. The sample size of 500 participants exceeded the minimum requirement and achieved balanced demographic representation across seven public and private institutions, enhancing generalizability within Syria. The cross-sectional design enabled simultaneous assessment across multiple training levels, from fifth-year medical students to fourth-year postgraduate residents. Conducting this study during Syrian medical education reconstruction provides valuable insights for policy development and curriculum planning. Several methodological limitations affect interpretation of findings. The use of only closed-ended survey items limited deeper insights into participants’ reasoning and contextual barriers in Syrian medical education. Online survey distribution through digital platforms prevented precise response rate calculation and may have introduced selection bias. The cross-sectional design precludes causal inferences about relationships between educational interventions and knowledge outcomes. The survey relied on self-reported awareness rather than objective knowledge assessment, potentially introducing response bias. Ongoing Syrian conflict conditions may have influenced exposure levels and educational access in ways not captured by this assessment, affecting generalizability to stable environments.

Conclusion

This study highlights critical gaps in ophthalmology education and demonstrates the urgent need for enhanced hands-on clinical training, small-group learning, and mentorship. A competency-based curriculum aligned with ICO guidelines may reduce demographic and curricular disparities while improving proficiency in managing systemic diseases affecting the eye. Integrating interdisciplinary approaches and innovative educational technologies would enhance student preparedness and improve healthcare delivery. Future research should evaluate the long-term effects of these interventions and explore trainee experiences to advance ophthalmology education globally.
