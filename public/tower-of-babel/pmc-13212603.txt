SlSAM3 interacts with SlACS4 to facilitate brassinosteroid signaling-mediated tomato (Solanum lycopersicum) fruit coloring
Authors: Wu Xuetong, Li Ailing, Chen Huan, Liu Xingping, Weibiao Liao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Brassinosteroids (BRs) are among the plant hormones that regulate climacteric fruit ripening. However, the precise molecular mechanisms underlying this regulatory process remain poorly characterized. In this study, exogenous 24-epibrassinolide (EBR) prominently promoted tomato fruit coloring and ethylene production, with a notable induction of S-adenosyl-L-methionine synthetase (SlSAM3) expression. Genetic knockout and overexpression of SlSAM3 demonstrated its pivotal role in accelerating tomato fruit coloration. Notably, EBR failed to promote coloration in slsam3 mutants, underscoring that SlSAM3 is required for BR to promote tomato fruit coloration. The results showed that interaction between SlSAM3 and 1-aminocyclopropane-1-carboxylate synthase (SlACS4) is enhanced by EBR. addition, Furthermore, transient silencing and overexpression of SlACS4 in wild-type and slsam3 mutants revealed that SlACS4 is a key contributor to tomato fruit coloration, while SlSAM3 is required for SlACS4 to exert its full promotive effect. Collectively, this study reveals an unique mechanism in which EBR promotes tomato fruit coloration by inducing SlSAM3 expression and facilitating its interaction with SlACS4, highlighting a distinct layer of crosstalk between BR and ethylene signaling pathways.

Introduction

Tomato (Solanum lycopersicum L.) is a globally important vegetable crop and a key model system for studying fruit ripening1. As a representative climacteric fruit, tomato ripening is primarily regulated by ethylene, which drives the respiratory climacteric and initiates a cascade of ripening phenotypes. These include chlorophyll degradation, carotenoid accumulation, fruit softening, changes in acidity, starch-to-sugar conversion, and the production of aroma volatiles2–4. This complex process is orchestrated by plant hormones and numerous ripening-related genes. Color, a critical indicator of fruit ripeness and appearance quality, is influenced by the biosynthesis of chlorophyll and carotenoids. Key structural genes involved in these pathways have been identified, and manipulating their expression has been shown to significantly impact pigment accumulation in plant5,6. MED25 knockdown resulted in increased total chlorophyll content and reduced carotenoid and lycopene levels, ultimately delaying tomato fruit ripening7. Additionally, SlZHD17, a zinc-finger homeodomain transcription factor, interacts with SlMYB72 to regulate chlorophyll and carotenoid metabolism in tomato8. SlZHD17 transcriptionally activates SlSGR, SlPSY1, and SlZISO, which inhibit chlorophyll degradation and plastid transformation while promoting carotenoid accumulation, thereby mediating fruit ripening. These findings highlight the importance of identifying regulatory factors in pigment metabolism to unravel the complex network governing fruit ripening.

Although fruit ripening is regulated by a complex network of multiple phytohormones9,10, ethylene has long been established as the primary trigger for climacteric fruit ripening11,12. Manipulating key genes in ethylene biosynthesis or signaling pathways through mutation or downregulation can effectively disrupt ethylene production or signaling, thereby delaying fruit ripening13,14. However, ethylene biosynthesis during tomato fruit ripening is influenced by multiple hormones1,10,15. For instance, methyl jasmonate induces the expression of NAC transcription factors (NAC2, NAC3), which activate the ethylene synthesis gene ACS1, enhancing ethylene biosynthesis in kiwifruit10. LeNCED1 acts as a primary inducer by initiating ABA biosynthesis during the early stages of fruit ripening15. The accumulation of ABA stimulates ethylene production, playing a pivotal role in tomato fruit ripening. Additionally, endogenous brassinosteroid (BR) levels exhibits distinct dynamic changes during the early ripening stages of both climacteric and non-climacteric fruit, indicating that BR’s regulatory mechanisms vary depending on fruit type and developmental stage16–18. Exogenous BR application experiments demonstrated that its effects on fruit ripening are dose-dependent19. BR treatment promotes the expression of SlACS and SlACO genes, as well as ethylene production, in tomato fruit20. Interestingly, overexpression of the BR biosynthetic gene DWARF in tomato increases endogenous BR and ethylene levels, leading to earlier fruit ripening21, highlighting the regulatory role of endogenous BR in this process. BR treatment also activates the expression of PuBZR1 and enhances its interaction with PuACO1, inhibiting PuACO1 activity19. Additionally, PuBZR1 transcriptionally represses PuACO1, PuACS1, and PuERF2, reducing ethylene production and delaying fruit ripening. Furthermore, BR binding to BRI1 and BAK1 activates receptor kinases, initiating a signaling cascade that results in the dephosphorylation and nuclear translocation of SlBZR1 in tomato1. SlBZR1 directly activates the transcription of ethylene biosynthesis genes SlACO1 and SlACO3, enhancing ethylene production and carotenoid accumulation, thereby accelerating tomato fruit ripening. Currently, research on the roles of BR and ethylene in fruit ripening primarily focuses on how key transcription factors in the BR signaling pathway directly regulate the expression of ethylene biosynthesis-related genes (e.g., SlACO and SlACS family genes). This regulation modulates ethylene production, thereby influencing the fruit ripening process. However, beyond its effects on key genes in the classical BR signaling pathway, the specific mechanisms of interaction between BR and multiple proteins remain poorly understood and require further investigation.

The molecular mechanisms underlying BR-mediated regulation of fruit color development remain poorly understood. While BR is known to modulate genes in the classical BR signaling pathway to influence fruit ripening, its regulatory effects on additional genes and proteins are largely unexplored. Here, we confirmed the promotive effect of exogenous 24-epibrassinolide (EBR) on fruit ripening and identified a key rate-limiting enzyme gene (SlSAM3), which encoded the synthase of the ethylene precursor S-adenosylmethionine (SAM). To investigate its function, we constructed SlSAM3 knockout mutants and overexpression lines using CRISPR/Cas9 gene editing technology and genetic transformation, respectively. To explore the synergistic mechanism between BR and ethylene in fruit ripening, EBR was applied to the SlSAM3 knockout mutants and overexpression lines, and the progression of fruit ripening was monitored. Further research revealed that EBR significantly enhances the interaction between SlSAM3 and SlACS4, a key gene involved in ethylene biosynthesis. This indicates that BR may regulate tomato fruit ripening by activating a complex and finely tuned signaling cascade network. These findings provide distinct insights into the molecular crosstalk between BR and ethylene signaling during tomato fruit ripening.

Results

Exogenous EBR promotes tomato fruit coloring and ethylene production

The results indicated that EBR treatment notably boosted tomato fruit coloration and firmness at 39 and 45 dpa compared to untreated control, with the 2 µM concentration showing the most noticeable effect (Supplementary Fig. 1). Consistent with these preliminary observations, exogenous EBR exhibited dose-dependent effects on tomato fruit coloration: 2 µM EBR significantly promoted pigmentation, while contrastingly, 4 µM notably repressed it. Thus, the 2 µM EBR was selected for all subsequent experiments to investigate its effects on tomato fruit ripening. Fruit treated with 2 µM EBR exhibited a notable reduction in Chla, Chlb and total chlorophyll content (Fig. 1A,C). As shown in Fig. 1D-F, treatment with 2 µM EBR significantly elevated the carotenoid and lycopene content, and stimulated ethylene production in tomato fruit. In contrast, treatment with brassinazole (BRZ), an inhibitor of BR biosynthesis, significantly delayed fruit coloration and suppressed ethylene production. Furthermore, compared with the control, EBR treatment dramatically enhanced the expression of genes associated with chlorophyll degradation as well as carotenoid and lycopene synthesis in tomato fruit compared to control, especially at the 39 and 45 dpa (Supplementary Fig. S2). Subsequently, we focused on the differences in fruit development from color break to color transition stages between 35 and 45 dpa. EBR induces SlSAM3 expression during tomato fruit ripening

Previous study revealed that SlFERL interacted with the key enzyme S-adenosylmethionine synthetase 1 (SlSAM1) to accelerate fruit ripening22. Given that EBR treatment enhanced ethylene production in tomato fruit (Fig. 1F), and considering that S-adenosylmethionine synthetase acts as the rate-limiting enzyme in the ethylene biosynthesis pathway by producing the precursor substance for ethylene synthesis, it is likely that EBR treatment influences SlSAMs activity during the fruit ripening process. As an initial step in understanding the regulation of ethylene production and fruit ripening, we identified a total of three SAMs genes in tomato (Supplementary Fig. S3). Among the three SAM synthase genes examined, SlSAM3 showed the most pronounced response: its expression was significantly induced by EBR and suppressed by BRZ across fruit developmental stages, with the induction amplitude substantially greater than those of SlSAM1 and SlSAM2. (Fig. 2A-C). In contrast, SlSAM1 and SlSAM2 exhibited relatively moderate or stage-specific responses to EBR and BRZ treatments (Fig. 2A, B). Moreover, SlSAM3 expression level was gradually induced during fruit development and ripening, showing higher transcript abundance at BR, BR + 5, and RE stages compared with the MG stage (Fig. 2D-F), whereas SlSAM1 and SlSAM2 displayed more limited variation among tissues and stages (Fig. 2D, E). SlSAM3 gene accelerates tomato fruit coloring

To investigate the function of SlSAM3 in regulating color development and ethylene content during tomato fruit ripening, we established transgenic lines in tomato cv. Micro-Tom, consisting of CRISPR/Cas9-mediated SlSAM3 knockout mutants (slsam3#8 and slsam3#9) and SlSAM3-overexpressing lines (OE-SAM3#1 and OE-SAM3#2) (Fig. 3). The slsam3#8 and slsam3#9 mutants carry 4-bp and 7-bp deletions, respectively, resulting in frameshift mutations in the SlSAM3 coding region (Fig. 3A and Supplementary Fig. S4), and a consequent sharp decrease in transcript levels (Fig. 3D). This observation may be associated with nonsense mediated mRNA decay (NMD), a common cellular pathway that degrades transcripts containing premature termination codons, such as those generated by the frameshift mutations23,24. Successful overexpression of SlSAM3 in OE-SAM3 lines was confirmed by immunoblot analysis using an anti-HA antibody (with untagged wild type (WT) as a negative control; Fig. 3B and Supplementary Fig. S5), and further validated by a significant increase in SlSAM3 transcript levels detected by RT-PCR (Fig. 3E). We recorded the ripening characteristics of OE-SAM3#1, OE-SAM3#2, slsam3#8 and slsam3#9 fruit. Compared with WT plants, fruit ripening was accelerated in SlSAM3-overexpressing lines, whereas it was delayed in slsam3 mutants (Fig. 4). At 36 dpa, fruit from the SlSAM3-overexpressing lines displayed broken coloration, whereas those from the WT and slsam3 mutants remained at mature green stage (Fig. 4A). In contrast, WT tomato fruit initially broke color at 39 dpa as showed in previous results, indicating that SlSAM3 overexpression accelerated ripening by 3 d. Conspicuously, compared to WT, the OE-SAM3#1 and OE-SAM3#2 fruit exhibited enhanced chlorophyll degradation, whereas the slsam3#8 and slsam3#9 fruit showed reduced degradation (Fig. 4B, D). Additionally, carotenoid and lycopene accumulation was significantly higher in OE-SAM3#1 and OE-SAM3#2 lines compared to WT, while slsam3#8 and slsam3#9 mutants showed diminished accumulation (Fig. 4E, F). Similarly, ethylene content was elevated in OE-SAM3#1 and OE-SAM3#2 fruit relative to WT, whereas slsam3#8 and slsam3#9 mutants produced less ethylene (Fig. 4G). qRT-PCR analysis showed the transcript levels of SlACS2, SlACS4, SlACO1 and SlACO3 were increased in OE-SAM3#1 and OE-SAM3#2 fruit compared to WT fruit, peaking at 45 dpa (Fig. 4H,K). Importantly, SlACS4 exhibited the most pronounced increase in transcript abundance among these genes (Fig. 4I). We consider the upregulation of multiple ethylene biosynthesis genes in SlSAM3-overexpressing fruits to be an indirect consequence of enhanced ethylene synthesis. This interpretation aligns with the well-documented phenomenon of ethylene-mediated positive feedback on SlACS and SlACO expression during climacteric fruit ripening. Coincidentally, the expression of the genes associated with chlorophyll degradation and carotenoid and lycopene synthesis was significantly up-regulated at 36 and 45 dpa in SlSAM3-overexpressing lines, whereas slsam3 mutants exhibited a contrasting pattern (Supplementary Fig. S6). EBR promotes tomato fruit coloration in a SlSAM3-dependent manner

In this study, EBR appeared to accelerate tomato fruit coloring and ethylene production, potentially by regulating SlSAM3 expression (Fig. 2C). Building on previous findings, we tentatively confirmed that the SlSAM3 gene could boost color development and ethylene production during tomato fruit ripening. To investigate whether EBR accelerates this process by modulating SlSAM3, we externally sprayed EBR to fruit of SlSAM3 knockout mutants and SlSAM3-overexpression lines. We investigated the mechanism of fruit ripening by monitoring ripening characteristics in WT, SlSAM3-overexpressing lines and slsam3 mutants treated with EBR. Notably, EBR application failed to enhance coloration in slsam3#8 and slsam3#9 mutant fruit (Fig. 5A). Similarly, EBR did not rescue the reduced lycopene and carotenoid levels, increased chlorophyll accumulation, or restore the diminished ethylene level in the slsam3 mutant fruits (Fig. 5B-G). Unlike in the mutant, EBR further accelerated fruit coloration in the SlSAM3-overexpressing lines (Fig. 5A). In contrast, EBR treatment also aggravated the rise of lycopene and carotenoid, the acceleration of chlorophyll loss and the boosted ethylene content in the SlSAM3-overexpression fruits (Fig. 5B-G). In comparison to the control, EBR treatment markedly enhanced the transcriptional levels of genes involved in chlorophyll degradation, carotenoid biosynthesis, and lycopene biosynthesis in the SlSAM3-overexpressing fruit, but had no effect on the expression of these genes in slsam3 mutant fruit (Supplementary Fig. S7). Parallel findings revealed that EBR treatment robustly activated SlACS2, SlACS4, SlACO1, and SlACO3 in OE-SAM3#1 and OE-SAM3#2 fruit, while no significant changes in ethylene biosynthesis-related gene expression were detected in slsam3 mutants under EBR treatment (Fig. 5H,K). A notable observation was the 50% and 70% upregulation of SlACS4 transcription at 36 dpa and 42 dpa, respectively, in SlSAM3-overexpressing lines following EBR exposure (Fig. 5I). Interaction between SlSAM3 and SlACS4 is enhanced by EBR

To further uncover the mechanism by which SlSAM3 modulates ethylene-mediated fruit coloration, we explored whether SlSAM3 interacts with the key components in the ethylene signaling pathway. Previous results showed that SlACS4 expression was significantly upregulated in SlSAM3-overexpressing fruits (Fig. 4I). Moreover, EBR treatment was observed to dramatically induce SlACS4 expression in SlSAM3-overexpressing fruit (Fig. 5I). Considering the expression patterns of the SlSAM3 and SlACS4 genes in tomato fruit coloration, we hypothesized that SlSAM3 regulates tomato fruit coloration, potentially through the involvement of SlACS4. To validate this hypothesis, SlSAM3 and SlACS4 were cloned into pGADT7 and pGBKT7 vectors for a yeast two-hybrid (Y2H) assay. Yeast cells containing pGADT7-SAM3 and pGBKT7-ACS4 exhibited robust growth on SD-Leu-Trp-His-Ade medium, confirming the interaction between SlSAM3 and SlACS4 (Fig. 6A). To further validate the interaction between SlSAM3 and SlACS4 in vivo, a coimmunoprecipitation (CoIP) assay was conducted. GFP-ACS4 was transiently co-expressed with SAM3-HA or empty-HA (as a negative control). Following immunoprecipitation using anti-GFP agarose conjugate, GFP-ACS4 was co-immunoprecipitated only when co-expressed with SAM3-HA, but not with empty-HA, confirming the interaction between SlSAM3 and SlACS4 in planta (Fig. 6B and Supplementary Fig. S8). Notably, EBR treatment markedly increased the amount of SlSAM3 co-immunoprecipitated with SlACS4 (Supplementary Fig. S9), indicating that BR signaling enhances their protein-protein interaction. To investigate the effect of EBR on the SlSAM3-SlACS4 interaction, a firefly luciferase (Luc) complementation imaging assay was performed. SAM3-nLuc (SlSAM3 fused to the N-terminus of Luc) and cLuc-ACS4 (SlACS4 fused to the C-terminus of Luc) were co-infiltrated into Nicotiana benthamiana leaves. Luminescence signals were specifically detected in the SAM3-nLuc+cLuc-ACS4 co-expression regions but not in the negative controls, further supporting the interaction between SlSAM3 and SlACS4 in plant (Fig. 6C, D). Following co-infiltration with nLuc-SAM3 and cLuc-ACS4, tobacco leaves were treated with EBR and BRZ, and the bioluminescence signals were evaluated. EBR treatment resulted in stronger luminescence signals in SAM3-nLuc+cLuc-ACS4 co-expression regions, with a 3.5-fold increase in signal intensity (Fig. 6 E, F). Conversely, BRZ treatment reduced the protein interaction signal. To investigate the localization of the SlSAM3-SlACS4 interaction, a bimolecular fluorescence complementation (BiFC) assay was conducted. SlACS4 tagged with pSPYCE and SlSAM3 labeled with pSPYNE were co-infiltrated into tobacco leaves for transient expression. YFP fluorescence was primarily localized to the plasma membrane in tobacco leaf cells co-expressing ACS4-cYFP and SAM3-nYFP (Fig. 6G). EBR treatment significantly enhanced the YFP fluorescence signal, while BRZ treatment weakened it. Functional interplay between SlSAM3 and SlACS4 in tomato fruit coloration

To further elucidate the role of SlACS4 in SlSAM3-mediated fruit ripening, we employed virus-induced gene silencing (VIGS) and transient overexpression approaches to modulate SlACS4 expression in both WT and slsam3 knockout backgrounds. Agrobacterium cultures carrying constructs for SlACS4 silencing or overexpression were injected into WT and slsam3 mutant fruit at the mature green (MG) stage. Fruit color changes were monitored at 3 and 7 d post-injection, respectively. In the WT background, silencing of SlACS4 (TRV-ACS4) significantly delayed the color transition in tomato fruit, whereas overexpression of SlACS4 (35S::ACS4) accelerated fruit coloration (Fig. 7A). Consistent with these phenotypic observations, TRV-ACS4 significantly reduced, while 35S::ACS4 significantly increased the carotenoid and lycopene contents in the WT background (Fig. 7B). These results indicated that SlACS4 positively contributes to the regulation of fruit coloration. Notably, in the slsam3 mutant background, silencing SlACS4 (TRV-ACS4) resulted delayed the color transition relative to the empty-vector (pTRV1 + pTRV2) control (Fig. 7A), although this effect was weaker than that observed in the WT background. While overexpression of SlACS4 (35S::ACS4) in the WT background markedly accelerated fruit ripening, this promotive effect was significantly suppressed in the slsam3 background (Fig. 7A). Consistently, transient overexpression of SlACS4 significantly increased carotenoid and lycopene contents in WT fruits, but the magnitude of this increase was markedly reduced in slsam3 background (Fig. 7B). Conversely, SlACS4 silencing reduced pigment accumulation across both genetic backgrounds. These findings suggest that SlSAM3 is required for the full promotive effect of SlACS4 on fruit ripening, highlighting a functional interplay between SlSAM3 and SlACS4 in regulating tomato fruit coloration. In the slsam3 background, the downregulation of genes associated with chlorophyll degradation and carotenoid synthesis by TRV-ACS4 was less pronounced compared to the WT background (Fig. 7C). Similarly, the upregulation of these genes by slsam3/35S::ACS4 was significantly attenuated relative to 35S::ACS4 in the WT background (Fig. 7C). In line with this, the expression levels of genes involved in chlorophyll degradation (SlSGR) and carotenoid biosynthesis (SlPSY1, SlPSY2, SlPDS, SlZDS) were strongly induced by SlACS4 overexpression in WT fruits but showed a weaker transcriptional response in the slsam3 mutant (Fig. 7C). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that SlACS4 plays an important role in regulating tomato fruit coloration, while SlSAM3 is required for SlACS4 to exert its full promotive effect. Discussion

Climacteric fruit exhibits a characteristic peak in respiratory rate and a “burst” of ethylene release at the onset of ripening25–27. Ethylene, a master regulator of climacteric fruit ripening26, markedly delays ripening when its biosynthesis is reduced24. BR, a kind of polyhydroxylated steroid plant hormone, significantly contributes to the ripening of climacteric fruit by modulating ethylene synthesis. In our study, exogenous EBR treatment notably boosted tomato fruit coloration and ethylene production (Fig. 1). Similarly, exogenous EBR enhances the accumulation of carotenoid volatile substances and improved post-harvest storage quality in tomato23. Furthermore, exogenous BR exerts a dose-dependent effect on delaying pear fruit ripening, while also blocking ethylene production in the process19. Such dose-dependent responses are typical of hormone signaling, where excessive concentrations may trigger negative feedback and inhibit downstream processes, providing an explanation for the reduced coloration observed at high EBR levels (Supplementary Fig. S1). Nevertheless, endogenous BR dynamics were found to have no substantial role in modulating mango fruit ripening17. This phenomenon also exists in bananas28. This suggests that endogenous BR levels may remain relatively stable or fluctuate within a narrow range that is insufficient to markedly affect ethylene biosynthesis. Unlike before, the exogenous BR application conversely reduces ethylene production and maintains postharvest fruit quality and markedly postpones the ageing of jujube fruit16. This suggests that exogenous BR application exerts distinct effects on the ripening of post-harvest fruit compared to pre-harvest fruit. While it inhibits ethylene production in postharvest fruit, it enhances ethylene synthesis in preharvest fruit, though this effect is species-dependent. Consistent with previous studies, our data confirmed that exogenous EBR treatment promotes tomato fruit coloration and increases ethylene production. However, the downstream molecular events that connect EBR signaling to the regulation of ethylene biosynthesis during fruit ripening remain poorly understood. We found that EBR marked induced SlSAM3 expression during fruit ripening (Fig. 2), with a stronger and more consistent response than SlSAM1, suggesting a dominant role for SlSAM3 among SAM synthase family members in this process. Previous study demonstrated that the interaction between SlFERL and SlSAMS1 results in elevated SAM accumulation and enhanced ethylene production, thereby promoting tomato fruit ripening22. This indicates that the SAM synthase gene is involved in ethylene biosynthesis to regulate tomato fruit ripening; however, the regulatory effects of BR on this gene remain poorly understood. Our results demonstrated that SlSAM3 is induced by EBR, suggesting that SlSAM3 may serve as a molecular link between BR signal transduction and ethylene biosynthesis. The induction may explain how EBR enhances ethylene production in tomato, although additional transcriptional regulators may also be involved.

Given the central role of SlSAM3 in SAM production and ethylene biosynthesis, it is reasonable to speculate that SlSAM3 may directly influence fruit coloration during ripening. To test this hypothesis, we generated loss-of-function mutants (slsam3) via CRISPR/Cas9 and overexpressing lines (OE-SAM3) via transgenic approaches. Molecular characterization confirmed a significant reduction of SlSAM3 transcripts in the mutants, likely due to nonsense-mediated mRNA decay (NMD)29,30 (Fig. 3D), and a substantial increase in transcript levels in the OE-SAM3 lines (Fig. 3B, E). A key limitation of this study is the use of CRISPR/Cas9 derived frameshift alleles. While the strong loss of function phenotypes validates their functional relevance, it should be acknowledged that we cannot exclude the production of stable, C terminally truncated proteins, which might exert dominant negative or neomorphic effects. Complementary strategies, such as generating full length deletions or employing RNA interference (RNAi) for knockdown, would provide valuable orthogonal future validation. Notably, the overexpression of SlSAM3 obviously accelerated tomato fruit coloring (Fig. 4). whereas the promotive effect of EBR was abolished in slsam3 mutant (Fig. 5), suggesting that SlSAM3 is required for BR-mediated fruit ripening. However, even when overexpressed, SlSAM3 does not fully saturate the regulatory pathway, thereby allowing BR signaling to further potentiate downstream ethylene production and fruit coloration. The SlCYP90B3 gene, a rate-limiting enzyme in BR biosynthesis, governs tomato fruit ripening through ethylene-dependent mechanisms, emerging as a target for improving tomato quality in terms of appearance, nutrition and flavor6. Similarly, our results identified SlSAM3 as a key rate-limiting enzyme gene in ethylene biosynthesis and a potential target of BR signaling. EBR further accelerated fruit coloration, aggravated the rise of lycopene and carotenoid, the acceleration of chlorophyll loss and improved the ethylene production in the SlSAM3-overexpressing lines (Fig. 5). 28-homobrassinolide (HBR), partially rescues lycopene accumulation in ap2a mutant fruit, supporting a role for BR downstream of AP2a31. Together, these findings suggest that SlSAM3 may act as a key mediator linking BR signaling to ethylene biosynthesis during fruit ripening. Previous studies have indicated BR signaling relies on a cascade of phosphorylation events to regulate the functions of the transcription factors BZR1 and BES1, which in turn control the expression of BR-responsive genes32,33. In addition, BZR1-centered BR signaling is critical for increasing carotenoid levels, improving fruit quality, and facilitating ripening in tomato34. SlBZR1 and SlBES1 function redundantly to regulate tomato fruit softening, with SlBZR1 taking a dominant role in carotenoid accumulation and fruit ripening1. However, it remains unclear whether exogenous BR is involved in the regulation of fruit ripening by SlBES and SlBZR1. Furthermore, EBL accelerated the ripening of postharvest tomato fruit, possibly by regulating the SlBAM1 and SlPIF3 genes35. However, it is currently unclear whether the SlBAM1 and SlPIF3 genes actually regulate tomato fruit ripening. Thus, many proteins controlled by BR during fruit ripening still await identification and characterization. Notably, although SlSAM3 is a key downstream mediator for BR-mediated regulation in tomato fruit coloration and ethylene biosynthesis, the upstream regulators within the BR pathway that directly control SlSAM3 expression are yet to be identified. To our knowledge, this study represents the unique report on the role of SlSAM3 in fruit development and provides the evidence that BR enhances SlSAM3 to promote tomato fruit ripening.

Previous results demonstrated that EBR promotes fruit coloration in a SlSAM3-dependent manner. To explore the underlying mechanism, we examined the expression of several key ethylene biosynthetic genes, including SlACS1, SlACS4, SlACO1, and SlACO4, in SlSAM3-overexpressing lines, among which SlACS4 exhibited the most pronounced transcriptional induction (Fig. 4I). Given that SlSAM3 encodes a metabolic enzyme rather than a transcriptional regulator, we propose that the elevated SlACS4 expression is an indirect consequence of enhanced ethylene biosynthesis and subsequent feedback regulation. Thus, we cannot exclude the possibility that increased ethylene levels in SlSAM3-overexpressing fruits contribute to the transcriptional activation of ethylene biosynthetic genes through this feedback loop. Although SlACS2, SlACO1, and SlACO3 were also upregulated in response to SlSAM3 or EBR treatment, these genes function at different enzymatic steps or cellular contexts. Given that SAM serves as the direct substrate for ACS enzymes but not for ACOs, we focused our interaction analyses on SlACS4 as the most biologically relevant candidate. Notably, the pronounced transcriptional response of SlACS4 further led us to investigate whether SlSAM3 coordinates with SlACS4 at the protein level. While the present study centered on the highly responsive SlACS4, future work should also explore whether SlSAM3 similarly modulates other ACS isoforms, such as SlACS3, in regulating ethylene homeostasis during fruit ripening. Additional regulatory layers beyond the SlSAM3-SlACS4 axis may also be involved and warrant further investigation. Consistent with this hypothesis, our protein-protein interaction assays revealed that SlSAM3 physically interacts with SlACS4, a key enzyme in ethylene biosynthesis. Although luciferase complementation assays (LCA) have intrinsic limitations depending on the choice of negative controls, the interaction was consistently corroborated by multiple independent approaches, including Y2H, CoIP, and BiFC analyses (Fig. 6). Importantly, the transcriptional regulation of multiple ethylene biosynthesis genes does not necessarily imply direct protein-level interactions with SlSAM3. Prior reports found that S-adenosylmethionine synthase proteins interact with diverse partners to modulate ethylene biosynthesis22,36. For example, SlFERL1 interacted with SlSAMS1, resulting in increased SAM accumulation and elevated ethylene production, thereby accelerating tomato fruit ripening22. Nevertheless, MdFERL1/6 interacted with MdSAMS to suppress ethylene production and delayed apple fruit ripening36. These findings suggest the S-adenosylmethionine synthase gene may exert regulatory effects through protein–protein interactions in species- or context-dependent manner. Additionally, the Rice dwarf virus (RDV)-encoded protein Pns11 enhanced the susceptibility of rice seedlings to RDV by interacting with OsSAMS1, boosting its enzymatic activity and resulting in elevated levels of SAM, ACC, and ethylene37. The interaction between AtFER and AtSAM1/AtSAM2 negatively regulated SAM levels and ethylene biosynthesis in Arabidopsis, potentially through mechanisms involving ethylene production, polyamine signaling, and methylation38. These studies imply that interaction with SlSAMS protein is an important regulatory layer controlling ethylene biosynthesis. However, they failed to utilize knockout or overexpression of the SAMS gene to definitively validate its regulatory role in fruit ripening. In our study, we utilized CRISPR/Cas9 and genetic transformation techniques to knock out and overexpress the SlSAM3 gene, clearly demonstrating that EBR promoted SlSAM3, which further promotes ethylene synthesis to accelerate tomato fruit coloration (Figs. 4,5). Furthermore, EBR enhanced the physical interaction between SlSAM3 and SlACS4 (Fig. 6). By incorporating CoIP assays under EBR treatments, our study provides biochemical evidence that BR signaling dynamically regulates the SlSAM3-SlACS4 interaction. Notably, BiFC analysis revealed a physical interaction between SlSAM3 and SlACS4, with fluorescence signals detected predominantly at the plasma membrane. This presents an apparent paradox, given that the enzymatic conversions of methionine to SAM (by SAM synthase) and SAM to ACC (by ACS) are established cytosolic events. This discrepancy could be attributed to the inherent nature of the BiFC assay, which can stabilize transient interactions and potentially sequester recombinant fluorescent complexes at membrane-proximal sites. Thus, the observed plasma membrane localization may reflect a stabilization site for detection rather than the principal site of enzymatic function. Our study identifies SlSAM3 as an essential metabolic hub linking BR signaling to ethylene biosynthesis during fruit ripening. While we demonstrate that BR induces SlSAM3 expression and enhances its functional coordination with SlACS4, the precise upstream mechanisms by which the BR signaling cascade regulates SlSAM3 remain to be fully elucidated. Future work should investigate whether canonical BR transcription factors (e.g., BZR1/BES1) directly target the SlSAM3 promoter, and whether BR-associated kinases modulate SlSAM3 activity, stability, or partnership with SlACS4 via post-translational modifications. Clarifying these mechanisms will finalize the BR-to-ethylene signaling pathway and yield a more integrated understanding of how hormonal cues reprogram metabolism to drive developmental change. Additionally, although our findings demonstrated that a physical interaction between SlSAM3 and SlACS4, whether this interaction is functionally required for SlSAM3-mediated fruit ripening remained unknown. To address this question, we further assessed the functional dependency of SlSAM3 on SlACS4 during tomato fruit coloration. By transiently silencing and overexpressing SlACS4 in both WT and slsam3 backgrounds, we further showed that SlSAM3 is required for SlACS4 to exert its full promotive effect. (Fig. 7). While this study establishes a functional link between SlSAM3 and SlACS4 in mediating BR-induced fruit coloration, future investigations could further define the specificity and completeness of this regulatory axis. A direct genetic test, such as expressing individual SlACS isoforms (e.g., SlACS2, SlACS3, and SlACS4) in the slsam3 mutant background, would clarify whether SlACS4 plays a unique role or functions redundantly within the ACS family to restore the ripening phenotype. Additionally, exploring whether SlSAM3 interacts with or modulates other ethylene biosynthetic components (e.g., ACO enzymes) through similar or distinct mechanisms will provide a more comprehensive understanding of how SAM metabolism is channeled into the ripening program. Combining such genetic approaches with spatial proteomics could also map the dynamic interactome of SlSAM3 under BR signaling, revealing potential additional partners involved in fine-tuning ethylene production during fruit maturation. Additionally, we proposed a model to summarize the outcomes of this study (Fig. 8). EBR strongly induces SlSAM3 gene expression during fruit ripening. Knockout and overexpression of SlSAM3 suggest that BR might accelerate fruit ripening through a mechanism involving SlSAM3. Furthermore, BR also enhances the physical interaction between SlSAM3 and SlACS4, which may contribute to SlSAM3-mediated promotion of fruit ripening. Thus, EBR acts as a positive regulator by inducing SlSAM3 expression and enhancing its physical interaction with SlACS4. Methods

Plant materials and growth conditions

Wild-type tomato (S. lycopersicum L. cv. Micro-Tom) and transgenic lines in this background (two CRISPR-Cas 9 mutant lines slsam3#8 and slsam3#9, and two OE lines OE-SAM3#1 and OE-SAM3#2) were grown in growth chambers under controlled conditions: a 12 h photoperiod, 25 °C/20 °C (day/night) temperature, and 600 µmol m-2 s-1 photosynthetic photon flux density. Flowers were marked at the anthesis stage, and the amount of marked fruit per plant was limited to six or fewer. Fruit was collected at various stages after 30-50 days post-anthesis (dpa). Based on established criteria (Giovannoni, 2004), fruit maturity stages—green mature (MG), breaker (B), pink (P), and red (R)—are classified according to changes in fruit color39. Three biological replicates of tomato fruit were collected at these stages for analysis. Pericarp tissues were collected, rapidly frozen in liquid nitrogen, and maintained at −79°C until further processing. Additionally, N. benthamiana plants, used for transient expression assays, were grown in growth chambers with 16-h light/8-h dark cycles at 25 °C/20 °C (day/night) temperature.

Gene isolation and sequence analysis

To identify potential SlSAMS genes in tomato, the whole-genome data (SL3.0) and annotation files (SL3.0) for tomato were downloaded from NCBI (https://www.NCBI.nlm.nih.gov/; accessed on 5 June 2023)40. Tomato coding sequences (CDS) were extracted using the “Gtf/Gff3 Sequences Extract” feature of TBtools software (v1.09876), and the “Batch TranSlate CDS to Protein” function was used to convert CDSs into protein sequences41. The protein sequences of Arabidopsis-SlSAMS genes were used as queries in a BLASTp analysis against the proteomes of tomato, barley, rice, potato and tobacco to identify SlSAMS proteins. The presence of the S-adenosylmethionine synthetase domain in all predicted SlSAMS proteins was verified using the Conserved Domain Database (https: //www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/Structure/cdd/cdd.shtml). Members of the tomato SlSAMS gene family were identified and named SlSAMS genes. The protein sequences of tomato, Arabidopsis, barley, rice, potato and tobacco were combined into a single file, and an evolutionary tree was constructed using MEGA11 software (v11.0.13). The neighbor-joining method was applied with 1000 bootstrap replicates, and all other parameters were set to default values.

Vector construction and plant transformation

To obtain tomato SlSAM3-overexpressing lines, the open reading frame (ORF) of the SlSAM3 gene was inserted into the Asc I and Kpn I sites of the pFGC1008-HA vector using homologous recombination. The overexpression construction, driven by the CaMV 35S constitutive promoter, was introduced into the tomato cv. Micro-Tom variety via Agrobacterium tumefaciens GV3101-mediated genetic transformation. Transformed OE-SAM3 lines were screened based on hygromycin B resistance. Positive transgenic plants were confirmed by protein extraction followed by immunoblot analysis using an anti-HA antibody. Total proteins were extracted and separated by SDS-PAGE, then transferred onto PVDF membranes. The membranes were probed with a primary anti-HA antibody (Abmart, M20021, dilution 1:5000), followed by incubation with an HRP-conjugated secondary mouse antibody (Coherion, CSA2108, dilution 1:5000). Signals were detected using the NcmECL Ultra-sensitive chemiluminescence detection kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions, and the chemiluminescent signals were recorded using an imaging system. Due to sample limitations, conventional internal loading control was not included for this immunoblot. However, equal loading was assessed visually by Ponceau S staining of the membrane immediately after transfer, which confirmed comparable loading across samples. In addition, the primary conclusion regarding transgene expression is further supported by complementary transcript-level data. Two independent homozygous lines from the T1 generation were utilized in the study. Primer information is provided in Supplementary Table S1.

Generation of slsam3 mutants using CRISPR/Cas9 technology

Tomato slsam3 mutants were generated using CRISPR/Cas9 gene editing and tissue culture techniques. The 20 bases upstream of NGG in the SlSAM3 gene CDS region was selected as the target sequence using the CRISPR-P website (http://cbi.hzau.edu.cn/cgi-bin/CRISPR)5. The sequence of the SlSAM3 gene and the target sequences predicted are presented in Supplementary Table S2. The target sequence was reverse-complemented to form sgRNA, which was imported into CRISPR/sgRNA vectors and then ligated into the CRISPR/Cas9 binary vector pHEE401. The construct was introduced into tomato cv. Micro-Tom by infecting leaf explants with A. tumefaciens GV3101. Transgenic lines were screened based on hygromycin B resistance. After successful transformation, genomic DNA was extracted from transgenic plants using Fast Plant DNAzol (Zhenbai Biotechnology, Hangzhou, China), and DNA fragments (~500 bp) flanking the CRISPR/Cas9 target site were amplified by PCR using gene-specific primers. The PCR products were purified and subjected to Sanger sequencing. The obtained sequences were aligned with the WT reference sequence using snapgene software to identify mutations at the target site. Two independent homozygous mutant lines (slsam3#8 and slsam3#9) were identified, carrying 4-bp and 7-bp deletions in the coding region of SlSAM3, respectively. The T1 progenies that contained homozygous mutation and lost the Cas9 during segregation were selected for harvesting seeds. T2 generation plants of the corresponding lines were used in the experiment.

Chemical treatments

The fruit of WT, slsam3 mutants and SlSAM3-overexpression lines were sprayed with 2 µM EBR (S18014, Shanghai yuanye Bio-Technology Co., Ltd) and brassinazole (BRZ, V10373, Shanghai yuanye Bio-Technology Co., Ltd) continuously for one week starting at the fruit setting stage, while the control group was sprayed with distilled water. After the initial treatment, the fruit was allowed to grow until the MG stage, where they were subjected to another week of continuous spraying with 2 µM EBR and BRZ, with the control group again treated with distilled water. For preliminary experiments, WT fruit was collected at 30 (MG), 39 (B), 45 (P), and 50 (R) dpa. In subsequent experiments, the fruit of WT, slsam3 mutants and SlSAM3-overexpression lines were harvested at 36 and 42 dpa for further analysis.

Total RNA isolation and real-time quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR) analysis

Total RNA was extracted from harvested fruit samples using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The RNA was reverse transcribed into cDNA using 5 × Evo M-MLVRT Master Mix (AG, Hunan, China), following the manufacturer’s protocol. RT-qPCR was performed using 2× SYBR Green Pro-Taq HS Premix (AG, Hunan, China) on a Light Cycler®96 Real-Time PCR system (Roche, Switzerland) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Relative gene expression was calculated using 2 −ΔΔT method, with SlACTIN (NC_015447) serving as the internal control42. All primers used in this study are detailed in Supplementary Table S1.

Chromaticity and firmness analysis of tomato fruit

Fruit color was evaluated by measuring the L*, a* and b* values using a chroma meter CR-400 (Konica Minolta Inc, Japan). Measurements were taken at three different points on each fruit, and the instrument was calibrated with a standard white plate prior to testing. Tomato fruit firmness was measured using a Universal TA Texture Analyzer (Tengba Inc, Shanghai, China) with slight modifications, and the results were recorded in Newtons (N)41.

Measurement of chlorophyll content

The chlorophyll pigments were extracted using 95% ethanol in the dark for 24 h3. Absorbance values were measured at 665 and 649 nm using a UV spectrophotometer (Shimadzu UV-1780, Kyoto, Japan). Chlorophyll content was calculated using the following formulas: Chlorophyll a = 13.95 × A665 - 6.68 × A649; Chlorophyll b = 24.96 × A649 - 7.32 × A665; Total chlorophyll content = Chlorophyll a + Chlorophyll b.

Measurement of carotenoid and lycopene content

Approximately 0.5 g of sample was collected and homogenized in 5 mL of extraction solvent consisting of hexane, acetone, and ethanol in a volume ratio of 1:1:1. The homogenate was vortexed thoroughly and incubated in the dark for 20 min and centrifuged at 12,000 g for 10 min4. The upper organic phase containing pigments was carefully collected and subsequently dried with a rotary evaporator4. The pigment was dissolved in a mixture of acetonitrile and methanol (30:75, v/v), followed by filtration through a 0.22 μm membrane filter prior to analysis. Carotenoid and lycopene content was quantified using high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC). Separation was performed on a reverse-phase C18 column, and compounds were detected at appropriate wavelengths (e.g., 450 nm for carotenoids and 472 nm for lycopene). Quantification was conducted using external standard calibration curves generated with authentic standards.

Measurement of ethylene content

Harvested fruit was enclosed in 500 mL gas-tight bottle and incubated at room temperature for 12 h. The collected gas (1 mL) is then injected and analyzed by SP-3420 gas chromatograph (Beifenruili, Beijing, China) for analysis5. The ethylene concentration was assessed by comparing the peak area of the sample with that of a known concentration ethylene standard.

Yeast two-hybrid assay

The CDSs of SlSAM3 and SlACS4 were inserted into pGADT7 and pGBKT7 vectors, respectively, to construct the bait (AD-SAM3) and prey (BD-ACS4) plasmids. These constructions were co-transformed into the yeast strain Y2H Gold for the Y2H assay. Transformed yeast cells were first selected on synthetic dropout (SD) medium lacking leucine and tryptophan (SD/-Leu/-Trp) to ensure the presence of both plasmids. Protein-protein interactions were assessed by spot-plating the yeast cultures onto SD medium lacking leucine, tryptophan, histidine, and adenine (SD/-Leu/-Trp/-His/-Ade), supplemented with varying concentrations of Aureobasidin A (AbA) according to a dilution gradient. The primers used are listed in Table S1.

Bimolecular fluorescence complementation assay

The full-length coding sequences of SlSAM3 and SlACS4 (excluding the stop codons) were cloned into the pSPYNE-35S and pSPYCE-35S vectors, respectively, using Xba I and BamH I restriction sites. The resulting constructs were transformed into the A. tumefaciens strain GV3101. Subsequently, the transformed A. tumefaciens cultures were infiltrated into leaves of N. benthamiana. The infiltrated leaves were treated with EBR and BRZ and then observed under a confocal microscope (Zeiss LSM 710, Leica Wetzlar, Germany) at 3 d post-infiltration. The primers used for cloning are detailed in Supplementary Table S1.

Luciferase complementation assay

The ORFs of SlSAM3 and SlACS4 were cloned into the pCAMBIA1300-nLUC and pCAMBIA1300-cLUC vectors, respectively, using Sal I and Kpn I restriction sites. The resulting constructs, SAM3-nLUC and ACS4-cLUC, were transformed into A. tumefaciens strain GV3101. The infiltrated leaves were treated with EBR and BRZ. LUC signalling was detected in vivo at 3 d post-infiltration using a PlantView100 (BLT photon technology, Guangzhou, China). The primers used in this assay are detailed in Table S1.

Coimmunoprecipitation analysis

For the Co-IP assay, the SlSAM3 was ligated into the pFGC1008-HA vector, facilitating the expression of SlSAM3 protein fused with an HA tag and driven by the CaMV 35S promoter. The SlACS4 was cloned into the pSuper1300-GFP vector. As mentioned above, Agrobacterium containing SAM3-HA and ACS4-GFP was co-transformed into tobacco. After infiltration, tobacco plants were irrigated with nutrient solution supplemented with 50 µM EBR for 3 d, and the infiltrated tobacco leaves were collected for CoIP experiments. Total proteins were extracted from tobacco leaves and then incubated with GFP agarose beads (ChromoTek, Wuhan, China) at 4 °C with gentle rotation for immunoprecipitation. After washing, the bound proteins were eluted and subjected to SDS-PAGE followed by immunoblot analysis. Input proteins and their immunoprecipitated counterparts were analyzed by immunoblotting, employing antibodies specific to GFP (CST, #2555, dilution 1:5000) or HA (Abmart, M20021, dilution 1:5000) to confirm protein expression and interactions. The sequences of all primers employed in this experiment are provided in Table S1.

Virus-induced gene silencing and transient expression

Tobacco rattle virus (TRV)-based vectors (pTRV1/2) were used for virus-induced gene silencing (VIGS) of SlACS47. The constructions pTRV1 + pTRV2 (hereafter referred to as TRV) and pTRV1 + pTRV2-ACS4 (TRV-ACS4) were transformed into A. tumefaciens strain GV3101 and infiltrated into WT and slsam3 mutant fruit at the MG stage. In parallel, the CDS of SlACS4 was cloned into the pSuper1300-GFP vector under the control of the CaMV 35S promoter (35S::ACS4). The empty pSuper1300-GFP vector (35S::) and 35S::ACS4 constructs were transformed into GV3101 and infiltrated into WT and slsam3 mutant fruit at the MG stage. Tomato fruit was photographed and collected for subsequent analysis on days 3 and 7 post-injection. All primers utilized in this study are listed in Table S1.

Statistics and reproducibility

All experiments were performed with three biologically independent replicates, where each replicate represents samples collected from independent plants or fruits. Data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA), Microsoft Excel, and GraphPad Prism (GraphPad Software Inc., San Diego, CA, USA). Differences among multiple groups were analyzed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s multiple comparison test, with significant differences indicated by different letters (P < 0.05). Comparisons between two groups were performed using Student’s t-test. Statistical significance was defined as follows: P < 0.05, *; P < 0.01, **; P < 0.001, ***; and P < 0.0001, ****.
