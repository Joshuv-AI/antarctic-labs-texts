Avijit Mandal, Henry D. Pfister

Abstract
The question of whether Reed–Muller (RM) codes achieve capacity on binary memoryless symmetric (BMS) channels has drawn attention since it was resolved positively for the binary erasure channel by Kudekar et al. in 2016. In 2021, Reeves and Pfister extended this to prove the bit-error probability vanishes on BMS channels when the code rate is less than capacity. In 2023, Abbe and Sandon improved this to show the block-error probability also goes to zero. These results rely on the symmetry and nested structure of RM codes. In this work, we focus on binary-input symmetric classical-quantum (BSCQ) channels and the Holevo capacity. For a BSCQ, we consider observables that estimate the channel input in the sense of minimizing the mean-squared error (MSE). Using an orthogonal decomposition of minimum MSE (MMSE) observables under a weighted inner product, we derive a recursion for the extrinsic MMSE of a code bit. Consequently, for Reed–Muller code sequences whose rates remain below the Holevo capacity by a fixed positive amount, any prescribed set of 2 o ⁡ ( log ⁡ N ) 2^{o(\sqrt{\log N})} bits can be decoded sequentially with the probability of any error tending to zero.



I
Introduction

In 1954, Reed–Muller codes were introduced by Muller
[
19
]
, and their majority-logic decoding was developed by Reed
[
24
]
. Since then, Reed–Muller codes have played an important role in coding theory, communication, information theory, and theoretical computer science
[
4
,
2
]
. A central question has been whether Reed–Muller codes achieve capacity on natural families of channels.

For classical binary memoryless symmetric channels, this question has seen substantial recent progress. In 2016, Kudekar et al. showed that sequences of Reed–Muller codes whose rates converge to a fixed value in
(0,1)
achieve capacity on the binary erasure channel
[
12
,
13
]
. This result was subsequently leveraged and generalized in several directions
[
31
,
3
,
9
]
. In 2021, Reeves and Pfister proved that Reed–Muller codes achieve capacity on binary memoryless symmetric channels with respect to bit-error probability
[
25
,
27
]
. In 2023, Abbe and Sandon strengthened this to block-error probability for binary memoryless symmetric channels
[
1
]
. These results rely on two structural features of Reed–Muller codes: their large symmetry group and their nested projection structure. The simplified and generalized framework in
[
22
]
is the starting point for the present work.

Coding for classical-quantum channels was first studied by Holevo
[
11
]
and by Schumacher and Westmoreland
[
32
]
, who showed that reliable communication is possible up to the Holevo capacity using collective measurements across long codeword blocks. This is analogous to Shannon’s channel-coding theorem in that it identifies the fundamental communication limit, but the decoding problem is substantially different. The output of a classical-quantum channel is a quantum state, the optimal measurement may be collective, and different candidate codewords generally correspond to noncommuting density matrices. Thus, even for a classical binary linear code, the extrinsic observation of a single bit is not a scalar likelihood-ratio channel but a binary quantum hypothesis-testing problem.

Explicit code constructions and structured decoders for classical-quantum channels have been studied in several directions. Polar codes give near-explicit constructions approaching the Holevo information with quantum successive-cancellation decoding
[
34
,
8
]
.
Belief propagation with quantum messages was introduced as a quantum analogue of classical message passing for pure-state classical-quantum channels
[
28
]
, with later work proving optimality on tree factor graphs and developing efficient approximate implementations
[
23
]
. More recent work studies belief propagation with quantum messages and paired-measurement belief propagation with quantum messages for symmetric classical-quantum channels and polar constructions
[
6
,
14
,
17
,
16
]
.

The present paper is complementary to this line of work.
Rather than designing a low-complexity decoder, we analyze the bitwise optimal decoding measurement for Reed–Muller codes on binary-input symmetric classical-quantum channels.
The goal is to understand whether the symmetry and nesting mechanisms that underlie Reed–Muller capacity results on classical binary memoryless symmetric channels have a quantum analogue. The main challenge is to replace the classical Boolean decoding function and its Fourier analysis by an operator-valued object that can be compared across nested Reed–Muller codes.

At a high level, the proof follows the same philosophy as recent Reed–Muller code analyses on binary memoryless symmetric channels
[
27
,
22
]
, but each classical object must be replaced by a quantum analogue. In the classical setting, the extrinsic information for a bit can be represented by a Boolean or real-valued decoding function, and the recursive Reed–Muller argument bounds correlations between
two looks
at this function coming from two nested copies of a shorter Reed–Muller code. In the classical-quantum setting, the corresponding object is a Hermitian observable acting on the extrinsic quantum systems. We choose this observable to minimize mean-squared error in estimating the input bit. Its orthogonal decomposition with respect to a weighted GNS inner product plays the role of the Fourier expansion of a classical decoding function. The new correlation inequality for quantum observables is then the operator analogue of the classical two-look correlation bound.

In this work, we focus on binary-input symmetric classical-quantum channels and show that Reed–Muller codes achieve vanishing bit-error probability below the Holevo capacity in a small-set sense. More precisely, for suitable Reed–Muller code sequences of rate below the Holevo capacity, any set of
2^{o(\sqrt{\log N})}
coordinates can be decoded sequentially with vanishing error probability.
This result establishes that the Reed–Muller symmetry-and-nesting arguments survive in the noncommutative classical-quantum setting when the classical decoding function is replaced by a quantum observable.

The proof has the following key steps.
First, an EXIT-area argument uses the gap between the code rate and the Holevo capacity to bound the extrinsic entropy.
This entropy bound is then converted into a nontrivial gap for the mean-squared error of the bitwise extrinsic channel.
Next, we use the Reed–Muller nesting structure to compare the optimal mean-squared-error observables associated with two projected copies of a shorter Reed–Muller code.
This gives a recursive relation for the extrinsic mean-squared error.
Lastly, we convert the mean-squared-error bound into a Helstrom bit-error bound and apply Gao’s quantum union bound to obtain the small-set decoding result.
The key technical ingredient is a correlation bound for the mean-squared-error observables.
Its proof uses GNS orthogonal decompositions and the transitive symmetry inherited from the Reed–Muller code.

The main contributions are as follows.

1.

We formulate binary classical–quantum estimation as a mean-squared-error optimization over Hermitian observables, characterize the optimal observable, and relate the resulting minimum mean-squared error to the Helstrom error probability.

2.

We establish an EXIT-area identity for transitive codes on binary-input symmetric classical–quantum channels and use it to relate the rate gap below the Holevo capacity to the extrinsic uncertainty of a single coordinate.

3.

We develop a weighted GNS orthogonal decomposition for quantum observables and obtain a new correlation inequality for observables with overlapping supports and transitive symmetry.

4.

We apply these tools to the Reed–Muller nesting structure to prove an explicit decay bound for the optimal bit-error probability below the Holevo capacity and show that any set of
2^{o(\sqrt{\log N})}
coordinates can be decoded sequentially with vanishing error probability.

Establishing a vanishing block-error probability at all rates below the Holevo capacity remains an important direction for future work.
Such a result would also resolve the long-standing open problem of whether Reed–Muller codes achieve strong secrecy on the binary symmetric channel (BSC).
By classical–quantum channel duality
[
29
,
30
]
, achieving vanishing block-error probability on a binary pure-state channel is dual to strong secrecy for the dual code on the (dual) binary symmetric channel.
Since the dual of a Reed–Muller code is again a Reed–Muller code, proving that Reed–Muller codes achieve vanishing block-error probability on binary pure-state channels (at all rates below capacity) would provide the secrecy component needed to show that nested Reed–Muller codes achieve the secrecy capacity of the binary symmetric wiretap channel.

Existing results show that nested Reed–Muller codes can simultaneously provide reliability and strong secrecy at rates close to, but bounded away from, the secrecy capacity
[
21
]
.
In fact, by duality,
[
21
]
provides the current best bound for the pure-state noise threshold of Reed–Muller codes for vanishing block-error probability.
Thus, a vanishing block-error probability result for Reed–Muller codes (at rates below capacity) on binary pure-state channels would not only advance classical–quantum coding theory, but would also resolve the problem of achieving the binary symmetric wiretap secrecy capacity with nested Reed–Muller codes under the strong-secrecy criterion.

In quantum mechanics, a system with

In quantum mechanics, any

II-D

Symmetric Binary-Input Classical-Quantum Channels

A classical-quantum (CQ) channel
W\colon\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{D}^{d}
defined by
x\mapsto\rho^{x}
takes a classical binary input
x\in\mathcal{X}
and produces a quantum state represented by the density matrix
\rho^{x}
. For the classical input distribution
p_{\mathcal{X}}(x)
, the associated classical quantum state
\rho_{XY}
is denoted by

\displaystyle\rho_{XY}=\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}p_{\mathcal{X}}(x)\big(\outerproduct{x}{x}_{X}\otimes\rho^{x}_{Y}\big).

For a classical input
X
and a quantum output
Y
, the quantum mutual information (or Holevo information) is given by

\displaystyle I(X;Y)_{\rho_{XY}}=S\left(\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}p_{\mathcal{X}}(x)\rho^{x}\right)-\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}p_{\mathcal{X}}(x)S(\rho^{x}),

where
S(\rho)=-\Tr(\rho\log_2 \rho)
is the von Neumann entropy.

Definition 3
.

A binary-input CQ channel
W\colon\{0,1\}\rightarrow\mathbb{D}^{d}
is defined by
x\mapsto\rho^{x}
.
If there is a unitary
U

such that
\rho^{1}=U\rho^{0}U^{\dagger}
, then we call this a binary-input symmetric CQ (BSCQ) channel.

For the BSCQ channel

with quantum output states
\rho^{0}
and
\rho^{1}
and input distribution
(1-p,p)

I(W)
, is

denoted by

\displaystyle I(W)\coloneqq I(X;Y)_{\rho_{XY}}=S((1-p)\rho^{0}+p\rho^{1})-S(\rho^{0})

where we used the fact that the von Neumann entropy is unitarily invariant, i.e.,
S(\rho^{1})=S(\rho^{0})
.

Since the channel is symmetric, the capacity is achieved at

and thus the Holevo capacity of the BSCQ channel is given by

Likewise, the channel entropy

\displaystyle H(W)

\displaystyle\ \coloneqq H(X|Y)_{\rho_{XY}}

\displaystyle\ =H(X)_{\rho_{XY}}+H(Y|X)_{\rho_{XY}}-H(Y)_{\rho_{XY}}

Similarly, the conditional min-entropy is denoted by

\displaystyle H_{\min}(W)

\displaystyle\ =H_{\min}(X|Y)_{\rho_{XY}}

\displaystyle\ =-\log_{2}\left(\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{2}\left\|(1-p)\rho^{0}-p\rho^{1}\right\|_{1}\right)

Definition 5

(Helstrom measurement)
.

Consider the measurement that distinguishes between two quantum states
\rho^{0},\rho^{1}\in\mathbb{D}^{d}
when
\rho^{0}
has prior probability
1-p
.
This measurement minimizes the error probability
[
10
]
by using projection operators onto the eigenspaces of

D=(1-p)\rho^{0}-p\rho^{1}.

The resulting “Helstrom error probability” is denoted by

\displaystyle P_{e}(\rho^{0},\rho^{1},p)\coloneqq\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{2}\left\|(1-p)\rho^{0}-p\rho^{1}\right\|_{1}.

Let
\Pi_{+}
,
\Pi_{-}
, and
\Pi_{\circ}
be the spectral projectors of
D
for its positive, negative, and zero eigenspaces. Every optimal binary projective Helstrom measurement has the form
\Pi_{0}=\Pi_{+}+Q
and
\Pi_{1}=\Pi_{-}+(\Pi_{\circ}-Q)
, where
Q
is any orthogonal projector with
Q\leq\Pi_{\circ}
.
All such choices have the same average error.
When symmetry between the measurements for the
0/1

E_{0}=\Pi_{+}+\Pi_{\circ}/2
and
E_{1}=\Pi_{-}+\Pi_{\circ}/2
.

From
[
33
]

\displaystyle P_{e}(\rho^{0},\rho^{1},p)=1-2^{-H_{\min}(W)}.

For the uniform prior, this relation gives

\displaystyle P_{e}\left(\rho^{0},\rho^{1},\frac{1}{2}\right)=\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{2}T(\rho^{0},\rho^{1}),

where
T(\rho^{0},\rho^{1})\coloneqq\frac{1}{2}\left\|\rho^{0}-\rho^{1}\right\|_{1}
is the trace distance.

For a linear code

\bm{c},\bm{c}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}^{l}_{0}
, then

\displaystyle l(\bm{c}+\bm{c}^{\prime})=l(\bm{c})+l(\bm{c}^{\prime})=0.

Thus
\bm{c}+\bm{c}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}^{l}_{0}
, which implies
\mathcal{C}^{l}_{0}
is a linear subspace of code
\mathcal{C}
, hence a subcode. Since the functional
l
is nonzero,
\mathcal{C}^{l}_{1}
is an affine coset of
\mathcal{C}^{l}_{0}
.
The following properties hold for
\mathcal{C}^{l}_{0}
and
\mathcal{C}^{l}_{1}
:

1.

\mathcal{C}=\mathcal{C}^{l}_{0}\bigsqcup\mathcal{C}^{l}_{1}
,

2.

\mathcal{C}^{l}_{0}\cap\mathcal{C}^{l}_{1}=\emptyset
,

3.

|\mathcal{C}^{l}_{0}|=|\mathcal{C}^{l}_{1}|=\frac{|\mathcal{C}|}{2}
.

A standard example is the linear function
l(\bm{c})=c_{0}
that returns the first bit in the codeword.

Now, we establish a symmetry result that is the CQ analogue of the classical symmetry satisfied by linear codes on symmetric channels.
For
\bm{c}\in\mathcal{C}
, we define the unitary

\displaystyle U^{\bm{c}}=\bigotimes_{i=0}^{N-1}U^{c_{i}}.

Since
U^{2}=\mathbb{I}
, we have
(U^{\bm{c}})^{\dagger}=U^{\bm{c}}
for all
\bm{c}\in\mathcal{C}
.
The codewords of
\mathcal{C}
are transmitted over a BSCQ channel to give
W\colon x\rightarrow U^{x}\rho^{0}U^{x}
with
x\in\{0,1\}
.

We assume a uniform distribution on the codewords of
\mathcal{C}
and will show that conditioning on the value of the linear functional
l
gives an average state that is invariant under the
U^{\bm{c}}
action for all
\bm{c}\in\mathcal{C}^{l}_{0}
.
Consequently, the corresponding Helstrom projector can be chosen invariant under this action, and expectations over the coset can be evaluated with respect to the all-zero codeword.

Consider the task of discriminating the transmitted codewords on BSCQ channel
W
based on whether
\bm{c}\in\mathcal{C}^{l}_{0}
or
\bm{c}\in\mathcal{C}^{l}_{1}
. Then we construct the average density matrices
\sigma^{l}_{x}
for
x\in\{0,1\}
as below

\displaystyle\sigma^{l}_{x}=\frac{2}{|\mathcal{C}|}\sum_{\bm{c}\in\mathcal{C}^{l}_{x}}U^{\bm{c}}(\rho^{0})^{\otimes N}U^{\bm{c}}.

Lemma 6
.

Let
\Pi_{+}^{l},\Pi_{-}^{l},\Pi_{\circ}^{l}
be the positive, negative, and zero spectral projectors for
\sigma_{0}^{l}-\sigma_{1}^{l}
.
For every
\bm{h}\in\mathcal{C}_{0}^{l}
and
s\in\{+,-,\circ\}
,
U^{\bm{h}}\Pi_{s}^{l}U^{\bm{h}}=\Pi_{s}^{l}
. For every
\bm{g}\in\mathcal{C}_{1}^{l}
,
U^{\bm{g}}\Pi_{+}^{l}U^{\bm{g}}=\Pi_{-}^{l}
,
U^{\bm{g}}\Pi_{-}^{l}U^{\bm{g}}=\Pi_{+}^{l}
, and
U^{\bm{g}}\Pi_{\circ}^{l}U^{\bm{g}}=\Pi_{\circ}^{l}
. Moreover, if an observable
F
is invariant under conjugation by every
U^{\bm{h}}
,
\bm{h}\in\mathcal{C}_{0}^{l}
, then
\Tr(F\sigma_0^l)=\Tr(F(\rho^0)^{\otimes N})
. The same statements hold after puncturing coordinates, with the unitaries and product state restricted to the retained output systems.

Proof.

Observe that for
\bm{c}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}^{l}_{0}

\displaystyle U^{\bm{c}^{\prime}}\sigma^{l}_{0}U^{\bm{c}^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{|\mathcal{C}|}\sum_{\bm{c}\in\mathcal{C}^{l}_{0}}U^{\bm{c}^{\prime}}U^{\bm{c}}(\rho^{0})^{\otimes N}U^{\bm{c}}U^{\bm{c}^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{|\mathcal{C}|}\sum_{\bm{c}\in\mathcal{C}^{l}_{0}}U^{\bm{c}^{\prime}+\bm{c}}(\rho^{0})^{\otimes N}U^{\bm{c}^{\prime}+\bm{c}}

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{|\mathcal{C}|}\sum_{\bm{c}^{\prime\prime}\in\mathcal{C}^{l}_{0}}U^{\bm{c}^{\prime\prime}}(\rho^{0})^{\otimes N}U^{\bm{c}^{\prime\prime}}

\displaystyle=\sigma^{l}_{0}.

Similarly
U^{\bm{c}^{\prime}}\sigma^{l}_{1}U^{\bm{c}^{\prime}}=\sigma^{l}_{1}
for
\bm{c}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}^{l}_{0}
since
\mathcal{C}^{l}_{1}
is an affine coset of
\mathcal{C}^{l}_{0}
. Therefore for all
\bm{c}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}^{l}_{0}
,
U^{\bm{c}^{\prime}}
commutes with
\sigma^{l}_{0}-\sigma^{l}_{1}
, since

\displaystyle U^{\bm{c}^{\prime}}(\sigma^{l}_{0}-\sigma^{l}_{1})U^{\bm{c}^{\prime}}=\sigma^{l}_{0}-\sigma^{l}_{1}.

By definition
\Pi^{l}_{+}
is the orthogonal projector to the positive eigenspace of
\sigma^{l}_{0}-\sigma^{l}_{1}
. Since
U^{\bm{c}^{\prime}}
commutes with
\sigma^{l}_{0}-\sigma^{l}_{1}
for all
\bm{c}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}^{l}_{0}
,
U^{\bm{c}^{\prime}}
preserves the positive eigenspace. Thus

\displaystyle U^{\bm{c}^{\prime}}\Pi^{l}_{+}U^{\bm{c}^{\prime}}=\Pi^{l}_{+},\quad\forall\bm{c}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}^{l}_{0}.

Hence this yields

\displaystyle\Tr\left(\Pi^{l}_{+}\sigma^{l}_{0}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{|\mathcal{C}|}\sum_{\bm{c}\in\mathcal{C}^{l}_{0}}\Tr\left(U^{\bm{c}}\Pi^{l}_{+}U^{\bm{c}}(\rho^{0})^{\otimes N}\right)

\displaystyle=\Tr\left(\Pi^{l}_{+}(\rho^{0})^{\otimes N}\right).

If
\bm{c}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}_{1}^{l}
, translation by
\bm{c}^{\prime}
interchanges the cosets
\mathcal{C}_{0}^{l}
and
\mathcal{C}_{1}^{l}
, so
U^{\bm{c}^{\prime}}\sigma_{0}^{l}U^{\bm{c}^{\prime}}=\sigma_{1}^{l}
and
U^{\bm{c}^{\prime}}\sigma_{1}^{l}U^{\bm{c}^{\prime}}=\sigma_{0}^{l}
. Hence
U^{\bm{c}^{\prime}}(\sigma_{0}^{l}-\sigma_{1}^{l})U^{\bm{c}^{\prime}}=-(\sigma_{0}^{l}-\sigma_{1}^{l})
, which swaps the positive and negative spectral projectors and preserves the zero spectral projector.
Finally, if
F
is invariant under translation by
\bm{h}\in\mathcal{C}_{0}^{l}
, then

\Tr(F\sigma_0^l)=\frac{2}{|\mathcal{C}|}\sum_{\bm{c}\in\mathcal{C}_{0}^{l}}\Tr(U^{\bc}FU^{\bc}(\rho^0)^{\otimes N})=\Tr(F(\rho^0)^{\otimes N}).

Restricting every tensor product and unitary to a punctured output system gives the punctured statement.
∎

III
Minimum Mean-Squared Error (MMSE) for Quantum Binary Hypothesis Testing

In this section, we introduce an estimation formulation of binary CQ hypothesis testing that will be used later in the proof.
For a binary CQ state, the MMSE is obtained by optimizing over Hermitian observables on the quantum output system. This is useful for two reasons. First, the optimal observable can be expanded in the GNS basis introduced in later sections. Second, the resulting MMSE can be compared with the Helstrom error probability, which is the bit-error probability for the induced binary hypothesis test.

III-A

MMSE Achieving Observable

For a binary-input CQ channel
W
with input distribution
(1-p,p)
, we combine the input and output into the binary CQ state

\displaystyle\rho_{XY}=(1-p)\ket{0}\bra{0}_{X}\otimes\rho^{0}_{Y}+p\ket{1}\bra{1}_{X}\otimes\rho^{1}_{Y},

where
\rho^{0},\rho^{1}\in\mathbb{D}^{d}
.
A quantum observable on
Y
is defined by a Hermitian matrix
M_{Y}
and represents the process of projectively measuring the
Y
-state in the eigenbasis of
M_{Y}
and returning the eigenvalue associated with the outcome.
Let

O_{X}=(\ket{0}\bra{0}_{X}-\ket{1}\bra{1}_{X})\otimes\mathbb{I}_{Y}

be the quantum observable associated with the binary input, and let

O_{Y}=\mathbb{I}_{X}\otimes M_{Y}

be any Hermitian observable supported within
Y
.
Optimizing over Hermitian
M_{Y}
jointly optimizes the projective measurement and the associated real-valued estimates.
Define

\displaystyle\rho_{Y}

\displaystyle=\Tr_{X}(\rho_{XY})=(1-p)\rho^{0}+p\rho^{1},

\displaystyle D

\displaystyle=(1-p)\rho^{0}-p\rho^{1}.

Then the mean-squared error associated with
M_{Y}
is

\displaystyle f(\rho_{XY},M_{Y})

\displaystyle\coloneqq\Tr\big(\rho_{XY}(\obs_X-\obs_Y)^2\big)

\displaystyle=1-2\Tr(D\measo_Y)+\Tr(\rho_Y\measo_Y^2).

(1)

Lemma 7

Consider the CQ state

(2)

For any

(3)

The minimum value is

MMSE Relation for Symmetric BSCQ Channels

Lemma 8
.

Consider a BSCQ channel with outputs
\rho^{0}
and
\rho^{1}
, where
\rho^{1}=U\rho^{0}U^{\dagger}
with
U^{2}=\mathbb{I}
, and a uniform random input
X
.
Let
M_{Y}^{*}
be an MMSE-achieving observable.
Then

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}(X|Y)_{\rho_{XY}}=\Tr\!\left(\rho^{0}(\mathbb{I}_{Y}-M_{Y}^{*})^{2}\right).

(6)

Moreover, the canonical minimizer, which vanishes on
\ker\rho_{Y}
, satisfies
UM_{Y}^{*}U^{\dagger}=-M_{Y}^{*}
.

Proof.

For
p=1/2
, define

\rho_{Y}=\frac{\rho^{0}+\rho^{1}}{2},\qquad D=\frac{\rho^{0}-\rho^{1}}{2}.

The channel symmetry implies

U\rho_{Y}U^{\dagger}=\rho_{Y},\qquad UDU^{\dagger}=-D.

Note that, if
P
is the support projector for
\rho_{Y}
, then
UPU^{\dagger}=P
and
U
preserves
\supp(\rho_{Y})
.

Since
M_{Y}^{*}
satisfies

\rho_{Y}M_{Y}^{*}+M_{Y}^{*}\rho_{Y}=2D,

applying the mapping
M\mapsto UMU^{\dagger}
to both sides shows that

\rho_{Y}(-UM_{Y}^{*}U^{\dagger})+(-UM_{Y}^{*}U^{\dagger})\rho_{Y}=2D.

It follows that
-UM_{Y}^{*}U^{\dagger}
solves the same equation.
Since the solution of the Sylvester equation is unique when restricted to the support of
\rho_{Y}
and
UPU^{\dagger}=P
, the projection of
M_{Y}^{*}
onto the support of
\rho_{Y}
is also antisymmetric under the mapping.
Therefore, we assume without loss of generality that

UM_{Y}^{*}U^{\dagger}=-M_{Y}^{*}.

It follows that

\Tr(\rho_Y\measo_Y^*)=0,\qquad\Tr(D(\measo_Y^*)^2)=0,

because
\rho_{Y}
is invariant under
U
whereas
M_{Y}^{*}
and
D
are antisymmetric.

Since
\rho^{0}=\rho_{Y}+D
, we have

\displaystyle\Tr(\rho^0(\mI-\measo_Y^*)^2)

\displaystyle=1-2\Tr(\rho^0\measo_Y^*)+\Tr(\rho^0(\measo_Y^*)^2)

\displaystyle=1-2\Tr(D\measo_Y^*)+\Tr(\rho_Y(\measo_Y^*)^2).

This is exactly the MSE attained by
M_{Y}^{*}
.
∎

The recursion will be stated for
\mathcal{M}
, but the final coding theorem is stated in terms of bit-error probability.
Therefore, we need inequalities that relate the MMSE of the optimal observable to the Helstrom error probability.
The next lemma gives both directions needed later.
One direction converts MMSE decay into bit-error decay, and the other converts an initial entropy bound into an initial MMSE bound.

III-C

Relation Between MMSE and Helstrom Error

Lemma 9
.

Consider a BSCQ with outputs
\rho^{0}
and
\rho^{1}
, where
\rho^{1}=U\rho^{0}U^{\dagger}
with
U^{2}=\mathbb{I}
, and a uniform random input
X
(i.e.
p=1/2
).
Then, the MMSE
\mathcal{M}(X|Y)_{\rho_{XY}}
satisfies

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}(X|Y)_{\rho_{XY}}

\displaystyle\ \geq 2P_{e}\left(\rho^{0},\rho^{1},\frac{1}{2}\right)

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}(X|Y)_{\rho_{XY}}

\displaystyle\ \leq 4P_{e}\left(\rho^{0},\rho^{1},\frac{1}{2}\right)\left(1-P_{e}\left(\rho^{0},\rho^{1},\frac{1}{2}\right)\right).

Proof.

Let

M_{Y}^{*}=\sum_{j}m_{j}P_{j}

be the spectral decomposition of the MMSE-achieving observable, where the
P_{j}
are orthogonal projectors. Define

p_{j}^{x}\coloneqq\Tr(\rho^x P_j),\qquad p_{j}\coloneqq\frac{p_{j}^{0}+p_{j}^{1}}{2}

and let
J
be the set of indices
j
such that
p_{j}>0
.
For this fixed projective measurement, the MSE becomes

f(\rho_{XY},M_{Y}^{*})=\sum_{j\in J}p_{j}\left(1-2m_{j}\frac{p_{j}^{0}-p_{j}^{1}}{p_{j}^{0}+p_{j}^{1}}+m_{j}^{2}\right).

Hence, for fixed
j\in J
, the minimizing coefficient is

m_{j}=\frac{p_{j}^{0}-p_{j}^{1}}{p_{j}^{0}+p_{j}^{1}}

and we define
\eta_{j}\coloneqq(1-m_{j})/2
.
Thus, we find

\mathcal{M}(X|Y)_{\rho_{XY}}=\sum_{j\in J}4p_{j}\eta_{j}(1-\eta_{j}).

For the same measurement
\{P_{j}\}
, the optimal classical decision rule has error probability

P_{e}^{\{P_{j}\}}=\sum_{j\in J}p_{j}\min(\eta_{j},1-\eta_{j}).

Since
4t(1-t)\geq 2t
for every
t\in[0,1/2]
, we observe that

\mathcal{M}(X|Y)_{\rho_{XY}}\geq 2P_{e}^{\{P_{j}\}}\geq 2P_{e}\!\left(\rho^{0},\rho^{1},\tfrac{1}{2}\right)

because the Helstrom error is the minimum possible.

For the upper bound, let
\Pi_{0}
be a Helstrom projector and define

\widetilde{M}_{Y}=\alpha(2\Pi_{0}-\mathbb{I}_{Y}).

Since
(2\Pi_{0}-\mathbb{I}_{Y})^{2}=\mathbb{I}_{Y}
, the MSE of this trial observable is

\displaystyle f(\rho_{XY},\widetilde{M}_{Y})

\displaystyle=1-2\alpha\,\Tr\!\left(D(2\Pi_{0}-\mathbb{I}_{Y})\right)+\alpha^{2}.

By Helstrom’s formula,

\Tr\!\left(D(2\Pi_{0}-\mathbb{I}_{Y})\right)=\frac{1}{2}\|\rho^{0}-\rho^{1}\|_{1}=1-2P_{e}\!\left(\rho^{0},\rho^{1},\tfrac{1}{2}\right).

Choosing
\alpha=1-2P_{e}(\rho^{0},\rho^{1},\tfrac{1}{2})
yields

f(\rho_{XY},\widetilde{M}_{Y})=4P_{e}\!\left(\rho^{0},\rho^{1},\tfrac{1}{2}\right)\left(1-P_{e}\!\left(\rho^{0},\rho^{1},\tfrac{1}{2}\right)\right).

Since
M_{Y}^{*}
minimizes the MSE, the same upper bound holds for
\mathcal{M}(X|Y)_{\rho_{XY}}
.
∎

IV
Reed–Muller Codes over BSCQ Channels

In this section, we apply the binary CQ setup to the extrinsic channel induced by the
RM
code.
A uniformly random codeword
\bm{X}\in\mathcal{C}
is transmitted through independent uses of the BSCQ channel, and the receiver estimates
X_{0}
using only the quantum systems
Y_{\sim 0}
.
The two conditional output states of this induced binary channel are obtained by averaging over the codewords with
x_{0}=0
and
x_{0}=1
.

Since
RM
codes are transitive and we consider transmission over memoryless BSCQ channels, any performance metric for a single bit must take the same value for all bits.
Thus, without loss of generality, we focus on bit 0 for our analysis.
Recent analyses of

[
27

26
,
1

22
]
.

RM
code decoding for a single bit from the extrinsic observation over BSCQ channels.

Consider the
RM
code
\mathcal{C}=\textnormal{RM}(r,m)
of length
N=2^{m}
.
As discussed in Proposition
2
(c), one can partition the
n=N-1
non-zero indices into disjoint sets
A,B,C,D\subseteq[N]\setminus\{0\}
such that the code projections
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}=\mathcal{C}|_{\{0\}\cup A\cup B}
and
\mathcal{C}^{\prime\prime}=\mathcal{C}|_{\{0\}\cup A\cup C}

\textnormal{RM}(r,m-1)

Let
\bm{X}
denote a uniform random codeword from
\mathcal{C}
and
\bm{Y}
denote the

quantum systems that define the output when transmitting
\bm{X}
through independent BSCQ channels.
We use
Y_{\sim 0}
to denote the quantum systems used for extrinsic estimation of
X_{0}
.
Then, the associated CQ state is given by

\displaystyle\rho_{\bm{X}Y_{\sim 0}}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{C}|}\sum_{\bm{x}\in\mathcal{C}}\outerproduct{\bm{x}}{\bm{x}}_{\bm{X}}\otimes\rho^{x_{\sim 0}}_{Y_{\sim 0}},

where
\rho^{x_{\sim 0}}_{Y_{\sim 0}}=\rho^{x_{1}}_{Y_{1}}\otimes\dots\otimes\rho^{x_{n}}_{Y_{n}}

For codes
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
,
\mathcal{C}^{\prime\prime}
the extrinsic CQ states
\rho_{X_{\{0\}\cup A\cup B}Y_{A\cup B}}
and
\rho_{X_{\{0\}\cup A\cup C}Y_{A\cup C}}

To obtain the error probability of decoding bit-0 of
\mathcal{C}
via extrinsic observation of the quantum system
Y_{\sim 0}
, we compute

\displaystyle\rho_{X_{0}Y_{\sim 0}}

\displaystyle\ =\Tr_{X_{\sim 0}}\left(\rho_{\bm{X}Y_{\sim 0}}\right)=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{z\in\{0,1\}}\outerproduct{z}{z}_{X_{0}}\otimes\overline{\rho}^{z}_{Y_{\sim 0}},

(7)

\displaystyle\overline{\rho}^{z}_{Y_{\sim 0}}=\frac{2}{|\mathcal{C}|}\sum_{\bm{x}\in\mathcal{C},x_{0}=z}\rho^{x_{\sim 0}}_{Y_{\sim 0}}.

Thus, the extrinsic bit-error probability for
\mathcal{C}
is given by
P_{b}(\mathcal{C})=P_{e}\left(\overline{\rho}^{0}_{Y_{\sim 0}},\overline{\rho}^{1}_{Y_{\sim 0}},\frac{1}{2}\right).

For codes
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
,
\mathcal{C}^{\prime\prime}
the extrinsic CQ states
\overline{\rho}^{x}_{Y_{A\cup B}},\overline{\rho}^{x}_{Y_{A\cup C}}
and bit-error probabilities
P_{b}(\mathcal{C}^{\prime}),P_{b}(\mathcal{C}^{\prime\prime})

Let
\Pi_{+},\Pi_{-},\Pi_{\circ}
be the positive, negative, and zero spectral projectors of
\overline{\rho}^{0}_{Y_{\sim 0}}-\overline{\rho}^{1}_{Y_{\sim 0}}
so that the optimal two-outcome POVM is defined by
E_{0}=\Pi_{+}+\Pi_{\circ}/2
,
E_{1}=\Pi_{-}+\Pi_{\circ}/2
.
The all-ones symmetry exchanges
E_{0}
and
E_{1}
, whereas every zero-coset symmetry fixes each effect.
This implies that

\displaystyle P_{b}(\mathcal{C})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\Tr\left(E_{1}\overline{\rho}^{0}_{Y_{\sim 0}}\right)+\frac{1}{2}\Tr\left(E_{0}\overline{\rho}^{1}_{Y_{\sim 0}}\right)

\displaystyle=\Tr(E_1\overline\rho^0)

\displaystyle=\Tr(E_1(\rho^0)^{\otimes n}).

The second step holds because the all-ones unitary interchanges the conditional states and satisfies
U^{\otimes n}E_{0}U^{\otimes n}=E_{1}
and the third step follows because the strengthened punctured form of Lemma
6
applies to
E_{1}
, which is invariant under conjugation by codewords in the zero coset.
Thus, the extrinsic bit-error probability may be evaluated under the all-zero transmitted codeword.
Also, the balanced POVM is defined here only to make the
0/1
exchange symmetry exact.
Any projective allocation of the zero eigenspace has the same average Helstrom error and may be used instead when a sequence of projectors is required.

Thus, one can analyze the extrinsic bit-error probability by considering only the extrinsic observation from the all-zero codeword
(\rho^{0})^{\otimes n}
.
Since the all-zero word is one term in the
x_{0}=0
coset average,
\supp((\rho^{0})^{\otimes n})\subseteq\supp(\overline{\rho}^{0}_{Y_{\sim 0}})
.
Also
\supp(\overline{\rho}^{0}_{Y_{\sim 0}})\subseteq\supp((\overline{\rho}^{0}_{Y_{\sim 0}}+\overline{\rho}^{1}_{Y_{\sim 0}})/2)
.
Therefore, in the rank-deficient case, the MMSE observable for the extrinsic channel is uniquely determined on every vector that can contribute to the all-zero expectations used below.

Let
W_{\mathrm{extr}}^{\mathcal{C}}:\{0,1\}\to\mathbb{D}
,
x_{0}\mapsto\overline{\rho}^{x_{0}}_{Y_{\sim 0}}
, denote the binary CQ channel induced by
\rho_{X_{0}Y_{\sim 0}}
. This is the BSCQ channel for decoding bit
0
of
\mathcal{C}
from the extrinsic quantum system
Y_{\sim 0}
.
In particular, addition of the all-ones codeword interchanges the cosets with
x_{0}=0
and
x_{0}=1
.
Thus, the tensor product
U^{\otimes N-1}
of the symmetry unitary for
W
maps
\overline{\rho}^{0}_{Y_{\sim 0}}
to
\overline{\rho}^{1}_{Y_{\sim 0}}
.

V
Extrinsic Information Transfer Functions

An EXIT function describes how the information about one coordinate changes as the observations of the other coordinates are gradually erased.
This provides a way to relate the uncertainty of the extrinsic observation
Y_{\sim 0}
to the rate of the code.
In particular, we introduce an erasure interpolation between the original BSCQ channel and a channel whose output is always erased.
Along this interpolation, the change in the conditional entropy of the transmitted codeword can be decomposed into single-coordinate extrinsic information terms.
For a transitive code, these terms are the same for every coordinate, which gives an EXIT-area identity for a single coordinate.
We use this identity to convert a rate gap
C-R
into a nontrivial bound on the extrinsic uncertainty of one coordinate.

For the BSCQ channel
W
,
let
W_{t}
denote the BSCQ channel in which the quantum output of
W
is erased with probability
t
.
For the channel input vector
\bm{x}=(x_{0},\ldots,x_{N-1})
, the corresponding quantum output on quantum systems
\bm{Y}=(Y_{0},Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{N-1})
is defined by the density matrix

W_{t}(\bm{x})=W_{t}(x_{0})\otimes W_{t}(x_{1})\otimes\cdots\otimes W_{t}(x_{N-1}).

We will sometimes drop the
t
subscript when
t=0
or alternatively write
\bm{Y}(t)
to emphasize the dependence on
t
.

Let
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathcal{X}^{N}
be a length-
N
code for this CQ channel.
For a random codeword
\bm{X}\in\mathcal{C}
, drawn according to
P_{\bm{X}}(\bm{x})
, the resulting density matrix is

\rho_{\bm{X}\!\bm{Y}(t)}=\sum_{\bm{x}\in\mathcal{C}}P_{\bm{X}}(\bm{x})\left(\ket{\bx}\bra{\bx}\otimes W_{t}(\bm{x})\right).

We follow the convention of
[
35
]
and denote the mutual information by

I\big(\bm{X};\bm{Y}(t)\big)_{\rho_{\bm{X}\!\bm{Y}(t)}}.

The erasure parameter
t
provides an interpolation path from the fully observed case to complete erasure.
Differentiating the conditional entropy along this path decomposes the total information into single-coordinate extrinsic contributions.
For transitive codes, these contributions are identical across coordinates, so the global mutual information per bit is equal to the area under the bitwise extrinsic information curve.

Lemma 10
.

For a binary code
\mathcal{C}
with codewords chosen from the distribution
P_{\bm{X}}
on a binary-input CQ channel, we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}I(\bm{X};\bm{Y})_{\rho_{\bm{X}\!\bm{Y}}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\int_{0}^{1}\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}I\big(X_{i};Y_{i}\mid Y_{\sim i}(t)\big)_{\rho^{i}_{\bm{X}\!\bm{Y}(t)}}\,dt,

where
\rho^{i}_{\bm{X}\!\bm{Y}(t)}
is the density matrix for the system where
Y_{i}
does not experience erasures.
If the automorphism group of
\mathcal{C}
is transitive (i.e.,
\mathcal{C}
has transitive symmetry)
and the codeword distribution is uniform, then this reduces to

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}I(\bm{X};\bm{Y})_{\rho_{\bm{X}\!\bm{Y}}}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}I\big(X_{0};Y_{0}\mid Y_{\sim 0}(t)\big)_{\rho^{0}_{\bm{X}\!\bm{Y}(t)}}\,dt.

Proof.

First, we note that

\displaystyle I(\bm{X};\bm{Y})_{\rho_{\bm{X}\!\bm{Y}}}

\displaystyle=H(\bm{X})-H(\bm{X}|\bm{Y})_{\rho_{\bm{X}\!\bm{Y}}}

\displaystyle=H(\bm{X}|\bm{Y}(1))_{\rho_{\bm{X}\!\bm{Y}(1)}}-H(\bm{X}|\bm{Y}(0))_{\rho_{\bm{X}\!\bm{Y}(0)}}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\frac{d}{dt}H(\bm{X}|\bm{Y}(t))_{\rho_{\bm{X}\!\bm{Y}(t)}}\,dt.

Next, we assume that the erasure rate for each system is different (e.g., the erasure rate for
Y_{i}
is
t_{i}
) and use the law of the total derivative to write

\displaystyle\ \frac{d}{dt}H(\bm{X}|\bm{Y}(t))_{\rho_{\bm{X}\!\bm{Y}(\bm{t})}}

\displaystyle\ =\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}\frac{d}{dt_{i}}H(\bm{X}|\bm{Y}(\bm{t}))_{\rho_{\bm{X}\!\bm{Y}(\bm{t})}}\bigg|_{t_{0}=t_{1}=\cdots=t_{N-1}=t},

where
\bm{t}=(t_{0},t_{1},\ldots,t_{N-1})
is the vector of erasure rates.
To simplify this expression, we use the chain rule of entropy and the definition of an erasure channel to write

\displaystyle\ H(\bm{X}|\bm{Y}(\bm{t}))_{\rho_{\bm{X}\!\bm{Y}(\bm{t})}}

\displaystyle\ =H(X_{i}|\bm{Y}(\bm{t}))_{\rho_{\bm{X}\!\bm{Y}(\bm{t})}}+H(X_{\sim i}|\bm{Y}(\bm{t}),X_{i})_{\rho_{\bm{X}\!\bm{Y}(\bm{t})}}

\displaystyle\ =(1-t_{i})H(X_{i}|Y_{\sim i}(t_{\sim i}),Y_{i})_{\rho^{i}_{\bm{X}\!\bm{Y}(\bm{t})}}

\displaystyle\ \;\,+t_{i}H(X_{i}|Y_{\sim i}(t_{\sim i}))_{\rho^{i}_{\bm{X}\!\bm{Y}(\bm{t})}}\!\!+H(X_{\sim i}\mid Y_{\sim i}(t_{\sim i}),X_{i})_{\rho_{\bm{X}\!\bm{Y}(\bm{t})}},

where
\rho^{i}_{\bm{X}\!\bm{Y}(\bm{t})}=\rho_{\bm{X}\!\bm{Y}(\bm{t})}|_{t_{i}=0}
is the density matrix of the same system except
Y_{i}
does not experience erasures.
Thus, we see

\displaystyle\ \frac{d}{dt_{i}}H(\bm{X}|\bm{Y}(\bm{t}))_{\rho_{\bm{X}\!\bm{Y}(\bm{t})}}

\displaystyle=H(X_{i}|Y_{\sim i}(t_{\sim i}))_{\rho^{i}_{\bm{X}\!\bm{Y}(\bm{t})}}-H(X_{i}|Y_{\sim i}(t_{\sim i}),Y_{i})_{\rho^{i}_{\bm{X}\!\bm{Y}(\bm{t})}}

\displaystyle=I(X_{i};Y_{i}|Y_{\sim i}(t_{\sim i}))_{\rho^{i}_{\bm{X}\!\bm{Y}(\bm{t})}}.

Thus, we observe that

\displaystyle\ \frac{d}{dt}H(\bm{X}|\bm{Y}(t))_{\rho_{\bm{X}\!\bm{Y}(\bm{t})}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}\frac{d}{dt_{i}}H(\bm{X}|\bm{Y}(\bm{t}))_{\rho_{\bm{X}\!\bm{Y}(\bm{t})}}\bigg|_{t_{0}=t_{1}=\cdots=t_{N-1}=t}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}I(X_{i};Y_{i}|Y_{\sim i}(t_{\sim i}))_{\rho^{i}_{\bm{X}\!\bm{Y}(\bm{t})}}.

Since the channel uses have full permutation symmetry when
t_{i}=t
for all
i\in[N]
, if the code has transitive symmetry and the codeword distribution is uniform, then the summand does not depend on
i
and one gets the stated simplification.
∎

Lemma
10
expresses the normalized information in terms of the extrinsic information of a single coordinate.
The next step is to lower bound the integrand using the channel capacity and the information already available from the other coordinates.
This converts the rate condition
R<C
into a pointwise upper bound on the extrinsic entropy
H(X_{0}|Y_{\sim 0})_{\rho_{\bm{X}\!\bm{Y}}}
.

Lemma 11
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a transitive binary linear
[N,K]
code with
R=K/N>0
and let
\bm{X}
be uniform on
\mathcal{C}
. Then,

H(X_{0}|Y_{\sim 0})_{\rho_{\bm{X}\!\bm{Y}}}\leq 1-(C-R).

Proof.

We start by writing

\displaystyle C

\displaystyle=I(X_{0};Y_{0})_{\rho_{X_{0}\!Y_{0}}}

\displaystyle=H(Y_{0})_{\rho_{X_{0}\!Y_{0}}}-H(Y_{0}|X_{0})_{\rho_{X_{0}\!Y_{0}}}

\displaystyle=H(Y_{0})_{\rho^{0}_{\bm{X}\!\bm{Y}(t)}}-H(Y_{0}|X_{0},Y_{\sim 0}(t))_{\rho^{0}_{\bm{X}\!\bm{Y}(t)}}

\displaystyle=I(Y_{0};X_{0},Y_{\sim 0}(t))_{\rho^{0}_{\bm{X}\!\bm{Y}(t)}}

\displaystyle=I(Y_{0};Y_{\sim 0}(t))_{\rho^{0}_{\bm{X}\!\bm{Y}(t)}}+I(Y_{0};X_{0}|Y_{\sim 0}(t))_{\rho^{0}_{\bm{X}\!\bm{Y}(t)}}

\displaystyle\leq I(X_{0};Y_{\sim 0}(t))_{\rho^{0}_{\bm{X}\!\bm{Y}(t)}}+I(Y_{0};X_{0}|Y_{\sim 0}(t))_{\rho^{0}_{\bm{X}\!\bm{Y}(t)}}

\displaystyle\leq I(X_{0};Y_{\sim 0})_{\rho_{\bm{X}\!\bm{Y}}}+I(Y_{0};X_{0}|Y_{\sim 0}(t))_{\rho^{0}_{\bm{X}\!\bm{Y}(t)}},

where the first inequality follows from the Markov structure
Y_{0}-X_{0}-Y_{\sim 0}(t)
and, conditional on
X_{0}
, this implies
Y_{0}
is independent of
Y_{\sim 0}(t)
because the physical channel is memoryless.
The second inequality holds because
Y_{\sim 0}(t)
is a degraded version of
Y_{\sim 0}
and
\rho^{0}_{\bm{X}\!\bm{Y}}=\rho^{0}_{\bm{X}\!\bm{Y}(0)}=\rho_{\bm{X}\!\bm{Y}}
.
Now, we can use the EXIT area theorem to write

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}H(\bm{X})

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{N}I(\bm{X};\bm{Y})

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}I\big(X_{0};Y_{0}\mid Y_{\sim 0}(t)\big)_{\rho^{0}_{\bm{X}\!\bm{Y}(t)}}\,dt

\displaystyle\geq\int_{0}^{1}\big(C-I(X_{0};Y_{\sim 0})_{\rho_{\bm{X}\!\bm{Y}}}\big)dt

\displaystyle=C-I(X_{0};Y_{\sim 0})_{\rho_{\bm{X}\!\bm{Y}}}

Since the code is transitive and
R>0
, every
X_{i}
is uniform on
\{0,1\}
(i.e., no code bits are identically zero).
This shows that
H(X_{0})=1
and implies the desired conclusion
R\geq C-1+H(X_{0}|Y_{\sim 0})_{\rho_{\bm{X}\!\bm{Y}}}
.
∎

The entropy bound above identifies when the extrinsic channel for one coordinate is already informative.

VI
Orthogonal Decomposition of Observables

Decoder error can be naturally expressed in terms of quantum observables.
Thus, we introduce a Fourier-type decomposition for quantum observables with respect to the product background state.
This decomposition enables the proof of a correlation inequality for two observables that depend on distinct but overlapping subsets of quantum systems.

To show a vanishing bit-error probability for
RM
codes on BSCQ channels, we rely on a suitable choice of inner product to decompose quantum observables. For our analysis, we adopt the complex inner product established from Gelfand–Naimark–Segal (GNS) construction
[
5
]
.
For any full-rank
\rho\in\mathbb{D}^{d}
and
F,G\in\mathbb{C}^{d\times d}
, consider the GNS inner product on
\mathbb{C}^{d\times d}
defined by

\left\langle F,G\right\rangle_{\rho}\coloneqq\Tr(\obsg^{\dagger} \rho\obsf).

For the Hilbert space
\mathbb{C}^{d\times d}
over
\mathbb{C}
, there exists an orthonormal basis
\{\Omega_{0},\Omega_{1},\ldots,\Omega_{d^{2}-1}\}
with
\Omega_{0}=\mathbb{I}
.
For example, one can apply Gram-Schmidt to any basis whose first element is
\mathbb{I}
.
Although the GNS inner product can be complex, the
\Omega_{0}
-coefficient of a Hermitian
F
is real because
\left\langle F,\Omega_{0}\right\rangle_{\rho}=\Tr(\rho\obsf)
and

\left\langle F,\Omega_{0}\right\rangle_{\rho}^{\dagger}=\left\langle\Omega_{0},F\right\rangle_{\rho}=\Tr(\obsf^\dagger\rho)=\Tr(\rho\obsf).

Since this inner product is positive definite only if
\rho
is full-rank, a modified version is defined in Lemma
12
for any
\rho
.

For
n
qudits, this extends to
\mathbb{C}^{d^{n}\times d^{n}}
with the inner product

\left\langle F,G\right\rangle_{\rho^{\otimes n}}\coloneqq\Tr(\obsg^{\dagger} \measgn\obsf)

and the single-system basis extends to an orthonormal basis
\{\Omega_{\bm{s}}\}_{\bm{s}\in[d^{2}]^{n}}
for
\mathbb{C}^{d^{n}\times d^{n}}
.
Specifically, for
\bm{s}\in[d^{2}]^{n}
, we have

\Omega_{\bm{s}}\coloneqq\Omega_{s_{0}}\otimes\cdots\otimes\Omega_{s_{n-1}}.

Using this basis, we can write any observable
F\in\mathbb{C}^{d^{n}\times d^{n}}
as

\displaystyle F=\sum_{\bm{s}\in[d^{2}]^{n}}\widehat{F}_{\bm{s}}\Omega_{\bm{s}},\qquad\widehat{F}_{\bm{s}}\coloneqq\left\langle F,\Omega_{\bm{s}}\right\rangle_{\rho^{\otimes n}}.

The study of quantum boolean functions is closely related to the orthogonal decomposition of observables.
Until now, most work in this area is restricted to the uniform background measure where
\rho=\mathbb{I}

[
18
]
.
For classical boolean functions, there is a well-developed theory beyond this (e.g.,
p
-biased measures).
In this context, Parseval’s theorem implies that

\left\|F\right\|_{\rho^{\otimes n}}^{2}=\Tr(\obsf^{\dagger} \rho^{\otimes n} \obsf)=\sum_{\bm{s}\in[d^{2}]^{n}}|\widehat{F}_{\bm{s}}|^{2}.

Thus, we find that

\text{Var}(F)_{\rho^{\otimes n}}=\left\|F\right\|_{\rho^{\otimes n}}^{2}-\left|\left\langle F,\mathbb{I}\right\rangle_{\rho^{\otimes n}}\mkern-1.0mu\right|^{2}=\sum_{\bm{s}\in[d^{2}]^{n}:\bm{s}\neq\bm{0}}|\widehat{F}_{\bm{s}}|^{2}.

The above description shows that
\left\langle F,\mathbb{I}\right\rangle_{\rho^{\otimes n}}=\widehat{F}_{\mathbf{0}}
is real for Hermitian
F
.

We say that an observable
F
on
n
systems is
supported within
the subset
A\subseteq[n]
if we can write
F=F_{A}\otimes\,\mathbb{I}_{\sim A}
where
F_{A}\in\mathbb{S}^{d^{|A|}}
and the tensor product is taken to preserve the original order of the quantum systems.
Its minimal support is the smallest such
A
.
For an arbitrary subset
A\subseteq[n]
, we define
\mathcal{S}_{A}
to be the collection of non-trivial strings
\bm{s}\in[d^{2}]^{n}
supported within
A
,

\mathcal{S}_{A}\coloneqq\{\bm{s}\in[d^{2}]^{n}\,|\,s_{i}=0,\forall i\notin A\}-\{\mathbf{0}\}.

We also note that
\Omega_{\bm{s}}=\Omega_{\bm{s}_{A}}\otimes\,\Omega_{\bm{s}_{\sim A}}
, where the tensor product is taken to preserve the original order of the quantum systems.

Now, we will see the benefit of choosing our single-system basis with
\Omega_{0}=\mathbb{I}
.
Based on this choice, if
F
is supported within a non-empty set
A
and
\bm{s}\neq\mathbf{0}
, then it follows that

\displaystyle\widehat{F}_{\bm{s}}

\displaystyle=\Tr\left((\Omega_{\bm{s}_{A}}\otimes\Omega_{\bm{s}_{\sim A}})^{\dagger}\rho^{\otimes n}(F_{A}\otimes\mathbb{I}_{\sim A})\right)

\displaystyle=\Tr\left(\Omega_{\bm{s}_{A}}^{\dagger}\,\rho^{\otimes|A|}\,F_{A}\right)\Tr\left(\Omega_{\bm{s}_{\sim A}}^{\dagger}\,\rho^{\otimes|\sim A|}\,\mathbb{I}_{\sim A}\right)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\Tr\left(\Omega_{\bm{s}_{A}}^{\dagger}\,\rho^{\otimes|A|}\,F_{A}\right)&\text{if $\bm{s}\in\mathcal{S}_{A}$}\\
0&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

because
\Tr\big( \basis{\bs_{\sim A}}^{\dagger} \, \rho^{\otimes|\sim A|} \, \mI_{\sim A} \big)=0
if
\bm{s}\notin\mathcal{S}_{A}\cup\{\mathbf{0}\}
.

The preceding GNS construction assumes that
\rho
is full rank.
But, when
\rho
is rank deficient, the inner product is semidefinite on the full operator space, but may be positive definite on a subspace.
In general, all GNS inner products depend only on the left-supported representatives
PF
and
PG
, where
P
is the projection onto
\supp(\rho)
.
The next lemma shows that restricting to these representatives gives a genuine Hilbert space and preserves all elements necessary for the correlation inequality.

Lemma 12
.

Let
\rho\in\mathbb{D}^{d}
and let
P
be the orthogonal projector onto
\supp(\rho)
. For
F,G\in\mathbb{C}^{d\times d}
, we have

\displaystyle\langle F,G\rangle_{\rho}=\langle PF,PG\rangle_{\rho}.

Moreover,
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\rho}
is positive definite on the subspace spanned by left-support GNS representatives,

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}=\{F\in\mathbb{C}^{d\times d}:PF=F\}.

Restricting observables to this subspace gives a well-defined Hilbert space of dimension
d\operatorname{rank}(\rho)
.
Such a space always contains an orthonormal basis of the form

\displaystyle\{\Omega_{0},\Omega_{1},\ldots,\Omega_{d\operatorname{rank}(\rho)-1}\},\qquad\Omega_{0}=P.

For
n
qudits, the tensor-product basis
\{\Omega_{\bm{s}}\}_{\bm{s}\in[d\operatorname{rank}(\rho)]^{n}}
is orthonormal with respect to
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\rho^{\otimes n}}
, and every
F\in\mathbb{C}^{d^{n}\times d^{n}}
satisfies

\displaystyle P^{\otimes n}F=\sum_{\bm{s}\in[d\operatorname{rank}(\rho)]^{n}}\widehat{F}_{\bm{s}}\Omega_{\bm{s}},\qquad\widehat{F}_{\bm{s}}:=\langle F,\Omega_{\bm{s}}\rangle_{\rho^{\otimes n}},

with Parseval identity

\displaystyle\left\|F\right\|_{\rho^{\otimes n}}^{2}=\sum_{\bm{s}\in[d\operatorname{rank}(\rho)]^{n}}|\widehat{F}_{\bm{s}}|^{2}.

Proof.

Since
P
is the support projection of
\rho
, we have

\displaystyle P\rho=\rho P=\rho.

Thus, we have

\displaystyle\langle F,G\rangle_{\rho}

\displaystyle=\Tr(G^\dagger\rho F)

\displaystyle=\Tr((PG)^\dagger\rho(PF))

\displaystyle=\langle PF,PG\rangle_{\rho}.

If
PF=F
and
\langle F,F\rangle_{\rho}=0
, then

\displaystyle\Tr(F^\dagger\rho F)=0.

Since
\rho
is positive definite on
\supp(\rho)
, this implies
F=0
.
Hence, the inner product is positive definite on
\mathcal{D}
. Since
\mathcal{D}
is finite-dimensional, it is a Hilbert space.

The tensor-product statement follows from

\displaystyle\langle\Omega_{\bm{s}},\Omega_{\bm{t}}\rangle_{\rho^{\otimes n}}=\prod_{i=0}^{n-1}\langle\Omega_{s_{i}},\Omega_{t_{i}}\rangle_{\rho}=\delta_{\bm{s},\bm{t}}.

Therefore the expansion of
P^{\otimes n}F
and Parseval’s identity follow from ordinary orthonormal-basis expansion.
∎

Expanding
\left\langle F,G\right\rangle_{\rho^{\otimes n}}
in the orthonormal basis
\{\Omega_{\bm{s}}\}_{\bm{s}\in[d^{2}]^{n}}
and simplifying, we get

\displaystyle\ \left|\left\langle F,G\right\rangle_{\rho^{\otimes n}}\right|

\displaystyle\ =\left|\left\langle\sum_{\bm{s}\in[d^{2}]^{n}}\widehat{F}_{\bm{s}}\Omega_{\bm{s}},\sum_{\bm{t}\in[d^{2}]^{n}}\widehat{G}_{\bm{t}}\Omega_{\bm{t}}\right\rangle_{\rho^{\otimes n}}\right|

\displaystyle\ =\Bigg|\left\langle\left(\widehat{F}_{\mathbf{0}}\Omega_{\mathbf{0}}+\sum_{\bm{s}\in\mathcal{S}_{A}}\widehat{F}_{\bm{s}}\Omega_{\bm{s}}\right),\left(\widehat{G}_{\mathbf{0}}\Omega_{\mathbf{0}}+\sum_{\bm{t}\in\mathcal{S}_{A}}\widehat{G}_{\bm{t}}\Omega_{\bm{t}}\right)\right\rangle_{\rho^{\otimes n}}

\displaystyle\ \quad+\left\langle\sum_{\bm{s}\in\mathcal{S}_{AB}-\mathcal{S}_{A}}\widehat{F}_{\bm{s}}\Omega_{\bm{s}},\sum_{\bm{t}\in\mathcal{S}_{AC}-\mathcal{S}_{A}}\widehat{G}_{\bm{t}}\Omega_{\bm{t}}\right\rangle_{\rho^{\otimes n}}

\displaystyle\ \quad+\left\langle\left(\widehat{F}_{\mathbf{0}}\Omega_{\mathbf{0}}+\sum_{\bm{s}\in\mathcal{S}_{A}}\widehat{F}_{\bm{s}}\Omega_{\bm{s}}\right),\sum_{\bm{t}\in\mathcal{S}_{AC}-\mathcal{S}_{A}}\widehat{G}_{\bm{t}}\Omega_{\bm{t}}\right\rangle_{\rho^{\otimes n}}

\displaystyle\ \quad+\left\langle\sum_{\bm{s}\in\mathcal{S}_{AB}-\mathcal{S}_{A}}\widehat{F}_{\bm{s}}\Omega_{\bm{s}},\left(\widehat{G}_{\mathbf{0}}\Omega_{\mathbf{0}}+\sum_{\bm{t}\in\mathcal{S}_{A}}\widehat{G}_{\bm{t}}\Omega_{\bm{t}}\right)\right\rangle_{\rho^{\otimes n}}\Bigg|

\displaystyle\ =\left|\left\langle\left(\widehat{F}_{\mathbf{0}}\Omega_{\mathbf{0}}+\sum_{\bm{s}\in\mathcal{S}_{A}}\widehat{F}_{\bm{s}}\Omega_{\bm{s}}\right),\left(\widehat{F}_{\mathbf{0}}\Omega_{\mathbf{0}}+\sum_{\bm{t}\in\mathcal{S}_{A}}\widehat{F}_{\bm{t}}\Omega_{\bm{t}}\right)\right\rangle_{\rho^{\otimes n}}\right|

\displaystyle\ =|\widehat{F}_{\mathbf{0}}|^{2}+\sum_{\bm{s}\in\mathcal{S}_{A}}|\widehat{F}_{\bm{s}}|^{2},

where the cross terms cancel by orthonormality because
A,B,C
are disjoint and the only nonconstant basis strings that occur in both expansions are those in
\mathcal{S}_{A}
.
More specifically,
\left\langle\Omega_{\bm{s}},\Omega_{\bm{t}}\right\rangle_{\rho^{\otimes n}}=0
if
(\bm{s},\bm{t})\in(\mathcal{S}_{AB}-\mathcal{S}_{A})\times(\mathcal{S}_{AC}-\mathcal{S}_{A})
,
(\bm{s},\bm{t})\in\mathcal{S}_{A}\times(\mathcal{S}_{AC}-\mathcal{S}_{A})
, or
(\bm{s},\bm{t})\in(\mathcal{S}_{AB}-\mathcal{S}_{A})\times\mathcal{S}_{A}
.

For
\bm{s}\in[d^{2}]^{n}
and
i\in[n]
, define the indicator function

\displaystyle Q_{\bm{s}}(i)\coloneqq\begin{cases}1\quad\text{if $s_{i}\neq 0$}\\
0\quad\text{if $s_{i}=0$}\end{cases}

and the sums

\displaystyle\eta_{i}(F)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\sum_{\bm{s}\in\mathcal{S}_{AB}}Q_{\bm{s}}(i)\frac{|\widehat{F}_{\bm{s}}|^{2}}{|\bm{s}|},\qquad|\bm{s}|\coloneqq\sum_{i\in[n]}Q_{\bm{s}}(i).

Then, expanding
\left\|F\right\|_{\rho^{\otimes n}}^{2}
in the
\{\Omega_{\bm{s}}\}_{\bm{s}\in[d^{2}]^{n}}
basis shows that

\displaystyle\left\|F\right\|_{\rho^{\otimes n}}^{2}

\displaystyle\ =|\widehat{F}_{\mathbf{0}}|^{2}+\sum_{\bm{s}\in\mathcal{S}_{AB}}|\widehat{F}_{\bm{s}}|^{2}

\displaystyle\ =|\widehat{F}_{\mathbf{0}}|^{2}+\sum_{i\in A\cup B}\sum_{\bm{s}\in\mathcal{S}_{AB}:s_{i}\neq 0}\frac{|\widehat{F}_{\bm{s}}|^{2}}{|\bm{s}|}

\displaystyle\ =|\widehat{F}_{\mathbf{0}}|^{2}+\sum_{i\in A\cup B}\eta_{i}(F).

For any
\tau\in\mathrm{Sym}(F_{AB})
, we can extend by the identity outside
A\cup B
so the extension lies in
\mathrm{Sym}(F)
and preserves
\mathcal{S}_{AB}
because
F=F_{AB}\otimes\mathbb{I}_{\sim(A\cup B)}
.
Thus, the extended
\tau
gives

\displaystyle\eta_{\tau(i)}(F)

\displaystyle\ =\sum_{\bm{s}\in\mathcal{S}_{AB}}Q_{\bm{s}}(\tau(i))\frac{|\widehat{F}_{\bm{s}}|^{2}}{|\bm{s}|}

\displaystyle\ \stackrel{{\scriptstyle(a)}}{{=}}\sum_{\bm{s}\in\mathcal{S}_{AB}}Q_{\tau\bm{s}}(\tau(i))\frac{|\widehat{F}_{\tau\bm{s}}|^{2}}{|\tau\bm{s}|}

\displaystyle\ =\eta_{i}(F),

where
(a)
holds because
\tau\mathcal{S}_{AB}=\mathcal{S}_{AB}
holds because
\tau
must stabilize the support of
F
and one can change the variable of summation to
\tau\bm{s}
.
Since
\mathrm{Sym}(F_{AB})
is transitive, one can see that
\eta_{i}(F)
does not depend on
i\in A\cup B
and it follows that

\displaystyle\eta_{i}(F)

\displaystyle=\bar{\eta}(F)\coloneqq\frac{1}{|A|+|B|}(\|F\|_{\rho^{\otimes n}}^{2}-|\widehat{F}_{\mathbf{0}}|^{2}),\;i\in A\cup B.

Thus, we get the inequality

\displaystyle\frac{|A|}{|A|+|B|}\left(\left\|F\right\|_{\rho^{\otimes n}}^{2}-|\widehat{F}_{\mathbf{0}}|^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=|A|\bar{\eta}(F)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in A}\eta_{i}(F)

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{i\in A}\sum_{\bm{s}\in\mathcal{S}_{A}:s_{i}\neq 0}\frac{|\widehat{F}_{\bm{s}}|^{2}}{|\bm{s}|}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\bm{s}\in\mathcal{S}_{A}}|\widehat{F}_{\bm{s}}|^{2},

where the inequality holds because the sum is over
\bm{s}\in\mathcal{S}_{A}
rather than
\bm{s}\in\mathcal{S}_{AB}
.
Combining the above equations gives the stated result

\displaystyle\left|\left\langle F,G\right\rangle_{\rho^{\otimes n}}\right|

\displaystyle\leq|\widehat{F}_{\mathbf{0}}|^{2}+\frac{|A|}{|A|+|B|}\left(\left\|F\right\|_{\rho^{\otimes n}}^{2}-|\widehat{F}_{\mathbf{0}}|^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\kappa\left\|F\right\|_{\rho^{\otimes n}}^{2}+(1-\kappa)\big|\!\left\langle F,\mathbb{I}\right\rangle_{\rho^{\otimes n}}\!\big|^{2},

where the equality holds because
\widehat{G}_{\bm{s}}=\widehat{F}_{\bm{s}}
for
\bm{s}\in\mathcal{S}_{A}
.
∎

]
. This is discussed in

A generalized version of Lemma

is established in Appendix

Lemma 17
.

There is a canonical MMSE-achieving

Proof.

Let

Finally, we can extend this observable by the identity on

∎

\displaystyle\Tr\!\left(\overline{\rho}^{0}_{Y_{\sim 0}}(\mathbb{I}-M)^{2}\right)=\Tr\!\left((\rho^{0})^{\otimes n}(\mathbb{I}-M)^{2}\right),

Lemma 19
.

For the
RM
code
\mathcal{C}=\textnormal{RM}(r,m)
and disjoint sets

A,B,C\subseteq[N]\setminus\{0\}
such that the two code projections

\mathcal{C}^{\prime}=\mathcal{C}|_{\{0\}\cup A\cup B},\qquad\mathcal{C}^{\prime\prime}=\mathcal{C}|_{\{0\}\cup A\cup C}

are equal to
\textnormal{RM}(r,m-1)
, if we have
\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{C}^{\prime})\in[0,1)
, then the extrinsic MMSEs satisfy

\frac{\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{C})}{1-\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{C})}\leq\frac{3}{4}\frac{\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{C}^{\prime})}{1-\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{C}^{\prime})}.

Proof.

From the definition of
\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{C})
we get

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{C})

\displaystyle\ =f(\rho_{X_{0}Y_{\sim 0}},M_{\sim 0})

\displaystyle\ =\Tr\left((\rho^{0})^{\otimes n}\left(\mathbb{I}_{Y_{\sim 0}}-M_{\sim 0}\right)^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\ \stackrel{{\scriptstyle(a)}}{{\leq}}\Tr\left((\rho^{0})^{\otimes n}\left(\mathbb{I}_{Y_{\sim 0}}-\alpha(M_{AB}+M_{AC})\right)^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\ =1-2\alpha\Tr\left((\rho^{0})^{\otimes n}(M_{AB}+M_{AC})\right)

\displaystyle\ \quad+\alpha^{2}\Tr\left((\rho^{0})^{\otimes n}(M_{AB}+M_{AC})^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\ =1-4\alpha(1-\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{C}^{\prime}))+2\alpha^{2}(1-\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{C}^{\prime}))

\displaystyle\ \quad+2\alpha^{2}\real(\Tr\left(M_{AB}(\rho^{0})^{\otimes n}M_{AC}\right)\big),

where
(a)
is true because
M_{\sim 0}
is the optimal MMSE observable.
Using Lemma
16
, we can bound the cross term with

\left|\left\langle M_{AB},M_{AC}\right\rangle_{(\rho^{0})^{\otimes n}}\mkern-1.0mu\right|\leq\kappa(1-\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{C}^{\prime}))+(1-\kappa)(1-\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{C}^{\prime}))^{2},

where
\kappa
is specified later.
This gives

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{C})

\displaystyle\leq 1+2\alpha(\alpha-2)(1-\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{C}^{\prime}))

\displaystyle\quad+2\alpha^{2}\left[\kappa(1-\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{C}^{\prime}))+(1-\kappa)(1-\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{C}^{\prime}))^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=1-2(1-\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{C}^{\prime}))\left[2\alpha-\alpha^{2}\big(2-(1-\kappa)\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{C}^{\prime})\big)\right].

The right-hand side is minimized by choosing

\alpha^{*}=\frac{1}{2-(1-\kappa)\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{C}^{\prime})},

and this results in the bound

\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{C})\leq\frac{(1+\kappa)\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{C}^{\prime})}{2-(1-\kappa)\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{C}^{\prime})}.

Applying the increasing map
\phi(x)=x/(1-x)
on
[0,1)
yields

\frac{\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{C})}{1-\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{C})}\leq\frac{1+\kappa}{2}\frac{\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{C}^{\prime})}{1-\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{C}^{\prime})}.

For the given RM setup, we have
\kappa\leq 1/2
and this gives the stated result.
∎

VIII
Achieving Vanishing Bit-Error Probability

In this section, we establish the primary result on vanishing bit error probability by combining the results from previous sections.

From our definition of extrinsic channel

\displaystyle H(X_{0}|Y_{\sim 0})_{\rho_{X_{0}\!Y_{\sim 0}}}=H(W^{\mathcal{C}}_{\text{extr}}).

For
W^{\mathcal{C}}_{\text{extr}}
, we can revisit the relationship between the Helstrom error probability and the conditional min-entropy
H_{\min}(W^{\mathcal{C}}_{\text{extr}})=H_{\min}(X_{0}|Y_{\sim 0})_{\rho_{X_{0}\!Y_{\sim 0}}}
to see that

1-P_{e}\left(\overline{\rho}^{0}_{Y_{\sim 0}},\overline{\rho}^{1}_{Y_{\sim 0}},\frac{1}{2}\right)=2^{-H_{\min}(X_{0}|Y_{\sim 0})_{\rho_{X_{0}\!Y_{\sim 0}}}}.

The conditional min-entropy also satisfies the inequality

H(X_{0}|Y_{\sim 0})_{\rho_{X_{0}\!Y_{\sim 0}}}\geq H_{\min}(X_{0}|Y_{\sim 0})_{\rho_{X_{0}\!Y_{\sim 0}}}

and this implies that

P_{e}\left(\overline{\rho}^{0}_{Y_{\sim 0}},\overline{\rho}^{1}_{Y_{\sim 0}},\frac{1}{2}\right)\leq 1-2^{-H(X_{0}|Y_{\sim 0})_{\rho_{X_{0}Y_{\sim 0}}}}.

Thus, an upper bound on
H(X_{0}|Y_{\sim 0})_{\rho_{X_{0}Y_{\sim 0}}}
gives an upper bound on the Helstrom error probability of the extrinsic channel.
Applying Lemma
9
then gives the corresponding upper bound on the MMSE.
The next lemma states this conversion in the form needed to initialize the RM recursion.

Lemma 20
.

The MMSE of
X_{0}
from the observations of the quantum systems
Y_{\sim 0}
satisfies

\displaystyle\ \mathcal{M}{\left(X_{0}\mid Y_{\sim 0}\right)}_{\rho_{X_{0}Y_{\sim 0}}}

\displaystyle\ \leq 4\cdot 2^{-H(X_{0}|Y_{\sim 0})_{\rho_{X_{0}Y_{\sim 0}}}}\big(1-2^{-H(X_{0}|Y_{\sim 0})_{\rho_{X_{0}Y_{\sim 0}}}}\big).

Thus, if
H(X_{0}|Y_{\sim 0})_{\rho_{X_{0}Y_{\sim 0}}}\leq 1-\frac{\delta}{\ln 2}
for
\delta\in[0,\ln 2]
, then the MMSE satisfies
\mathcal{M}{\left(X_{0}\mid Y_{\sim 0}\right)}_{\rho_{X_{0}Y_{\sim 0}}}\leq 1-\delta^{2}
and

\mathcal{M}{\left(X_{0}\mid Y_{\sim 0}\right)}_{\rho_{X_{0}Y_{\sim 0}}}\!\leq 1-(\ln 2)^{2}(1-H(X_{0}|Y_{\sim 0})_{\rho_{X_{0}Y_{\sim 0}}})^{2}.

Proof.

We know that

P_{e}\left(\overline{\rho}^{0}_{Y_{\sim 0}},\overline{\rho}^{1}_{Y_{\sim 0}},\frac{1}{2}\right)=\frac{1-T}{2},

where

T=\frac{1}{2}\|\overline{\rho}^{0}_{Y_{\sim 0}}-\overline{\rho}^{1}_{Y_{\sim 0}}\|_{1}\geq 1-2\left(1-2^{-H(X_{0}|Y_{\sim 0})_{\rho_{X_{0}Y_{\sim 0}}}}\right).

Thus, we find that

\displaystyle\ \mathcal{M}{\left(X_{0}\mid Y_{\sim 0}\right)}_{\rho_{X_{0}Y_{\sim 0}}}

\displaystyle\leq 4P_{e}\left(\overline{\rho}^{0}_{Y_{\sim 0}},\overline{\rho}^{1}_{Y_{\sim 0}},\frac{1}{2}\right)\left(1-P_{e}\left(\overline{\rho}^{0}_{Y_{\sim 0}},\overline{\rho}^{1}_{Y_{\sim 0}},\frac{1}{2}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\leq 1-T^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 4\cdot 2^{-H(X_{0}|Y_{\sim 0})_{\rho_{X_{0}Y_{\sim 0}}}}\big(1-2^{-H(X_{0}|Y_{\sim 0})_{\rho_{X_{0}Y_{\sim 0}}}}\big).

For the last part, the upper bound on the MMSE monotonically increases to 1 as the conditional entropy increases to 1.
By choosing
H(X_{0}|Y_{\sim 0})_{\rho_{X_{0}Y_{\sim 0}}}=1-\frac{\Delta}{\ln 2}
for
\Delta\in[0,\ln 2]
, we see that the RHS expression equals

2e^{\Delta}-e^{2\Delta}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{2\Delta^{k}-2^{k}\Delta^{k}}{k!}\leq 1-\Delta^{2}

using the
k=0,1,2
terms because the rest are negative.
This implies the final claim that

\mkern-1.5mu\mathcal{M}(X_{0}|Y_{\sim 0})_{\rho_{X_{0}Y_{\sim 0}}}\!\leq 1\mkern-0.5mu-\mkern-0.5mu(\ln 2)^{2}(1\mkern-1.5mu-\mkern-1.5muH(X_{0}|Y_{\sim 0})_{\rho_{X_{0}Y_{\sim 0}}})^{2}.\!\qed

Lemma
20
gives the initial condition for the base code
\mathcal{C}_{0}
once
R(\mathcal{C}_{0})<C
.
The recursive inequality from Lemma
19
is then applied repeatedly along the sequence
\mathcal{C}_{k}=\textnormal{RM}(r,m+k)
.
It is convenient to iterate the recursion after applying the increasing map
x\mapsto x/(1-x)
, which turns the recursion into a geometric bound.

Lemma 21
.

Consider the sequence of
RM
codes such that
\mathcal{C}_{0}=\textnormal{RM}(r_{m},m)
and

\mathcal{C}_{k}=\textnormal{RM}(r_{m},m+k)
. If
\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{C}_{0})\leq 1-\delta
for
\delta>0
, then, for all
k\geq 0
, we have

\frac{\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{C}_{k})}{1-\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{C}_{k})}\leq\left(\frac{3}{4}\right)^{k}\frac{1-\delta}{\delta}.

Equivalently, this gives

\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{C}_{k})\leq\frac{(1-\delta)(3/4)^{k}}{\delta+(1-\delta)(3/4)^{k}}\leq\frac{1-\delta}{\delta}\left(\frac{3}{4}\right)^{k}.

Proof.

By Lemma
19
, for every
j\geq 0
,

\frac{\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{C}_{j+1})}{1-\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{C}_{j+1})}\leq\frac{3}{4}\frac{\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{C}_{j})}{1-\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{C}_{j})}.

Iterating from
\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{C}_{0})\leq 1-\delta
shows that

\frac{\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{C}_{k})}{1-\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{C}_{k})}\leq\left(\frac{3}{4}\right)^{k}\frac{1-\delta}{\delta}.

Solving this inequality for
\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{C}_{k})
gives

\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{C}_{k})\leq\frac{(1-\delta)(3/4)^{k}}{\delta+(1-\delta)(3/4)^{k}}\leq\frac{1-\delta}{\delta}\left(\frac{3}{4}\right)^{k}.\qed

Now, we can state the main quantitative theorem.
The theorem combines the RM rate estimate for the sequence
\mathcal{C}_{k}=\textnormal{RM}(r,m+k)
, the initial MMSE bound obtained from the rate gap
C-R(\mathcal{C}_{0})
, and the recursive decay from Lemma
21
.
The last step uses Lemma
9
to pass from
\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{C}_{k})
to
P_{b}(\mathcal{C}_{k})
.

Theorem 22
.

Consider a BSCQ channel
W
with Holevo capacity
C
.
For any
r,m\in\mathbb{N}
with
r<m
, consider the
RM
code sequence

\mathcal{C}_{k}=\textnormal{RM}(r,m+k)
.
Then
R(\mathcal{C}_{k})\geq R(\mathcal{C}_{0})-k/(2\sqrt{m})
and, if
R(\mathcal{C}_{0})<C
, we have

P_{b}(\mathcal{C}_{k})\leq\frac{1}{2}\frac{(1-\delta)(3/4)^{k}}{\delta+(1-\delta)(3/4)^{k}}\leq\frac{1-\delta}{2\delta}\left(\frac{3}{4}\right)^{k},

where
\delta=(\ln 2)^{2}(C-R(\mathcal{C}_{0}))^{2}>0
.

Proof.

The code rate satisfies

R(\mathcal{C}_{k})\geq R(\mathcal{C}_{0})-\frac{k}{2\sqrt{m}},

for all
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}

[
22
]
.
Next, Lemmas
11
and
20
imply

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{C}_{0})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{M}(X_{0}|Y_{\sim 0})_{\rho_{X_{0}Y_{\sim 0}}}

\displaystyle\leq 1-(\ln 2)^{2}\big(1-H(X_{0}|Y_{\sim 0})_{\rho_{\bm{X}\!\bm{Y}}}\big)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 1-(\ln 2)^{2}(C-R(\mathcal{C}_{0}))^{2},

where
C
is the BSCQ channel capacity.
Thus, we can choose
\delta=(\ln 2)^{2}(C-R(\mathcal{C}_{0}))^{2}
and apply Lemma
21
to see that

\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{C}_{k})\leq\frac{(1-\delta)(3/4)^{k}}{\delta+(1-\delta)(3/4)^{k}}.

Finally, Lemma
9
gives
2P_{b}(\mathcal{C}_{k})\leq\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{C}_{k})
, and hence

P_{b}(\mathcal{C}_{k})\leq\frac{1}{2}\frac{(1-\delta)(3/4)^{k}}{\delta+(1-\delta)(3/4)^{k}}\leq\frac{1-\delta}{2\delta}\left(\frac{3}{4}\right)^{k}.\qed

The theorem is stated for a fixed base code and a sequence obtained by increasing the blocklength parameter. To obtain the informal asymptotic statement, one chooses the base code rate below
C
by a fixed amount and then lets
k
grow proportionally to
\sqrt{m}
. The RM rate estimate controls the loss in rate, while the factor
(3/4)^{k}
gives exponential decay in
\sqrt{m}
.
Formalizing this argument gives Theorem
1
.

Proof of Theorem
1
.

To recover Theorem
1
from Theorem
22
, we use the rate analysis ideas from
[
27
, p. 924]
.
First, for large enough
m
, fix a target rate gap
\eta>0
and choose
k=\lfloor(\eta/2)\sqrt{m}\rfloor
and
m^{\prime}=m-k
.
As
m
is large enough, we assume
k/(2\sqrt{m^{\prime}})\leq\eta/3
.
Since increasing
r
changes the rate by
2^{-m^{\prime}}\binom{m^{\prime}}{r}
, whose maximum is
O(1/\sqrt{m^{\prime}})
, there is an
r\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\mathcal{C}_{0}=\textnormal{RM}(r,m^{\prime})
satisfies
\eta/4<C-R(\mathcal{C}_{0})\leq\eta/2
.
Apply Theorem
22
to get
C-R(\mathcal{C}_{k})\leq\eta
,
\delta\geq(\ln 2)^{2}(\eta/4)^{2}
, and

P_{b}(\mathcal{C}_{k})\leq\frac{1-\delta}{2\delta}\exp\!\left(-\frac{1}{2}\ln(4/3)\eta\sqrt{m}+O(1)\right).

Thus, for any
c<\frac{1}{2}\ln(4/3)
, there is an
m_{0}
such that, for all
m>m_{0}
,
P_{b}(\mathcal{C}_{k})\leq e^{-c\eta\sqrt{m}}
.
∎

The bound above is for one fixed coordinate.
Since RM codes are transitive, the same bit-error bound holds for every coordinate.
Therefore, for any chosen coordinate set, we can apply the corresponding single-coordinate projective Helstrom measurements sequentially to the received state.
Gao’s quantum union bound controls the cumulative error despite the disturbance caused by earlier measurements
[
7
]
.

Lemma 23
.

Let
S\subseteq[N]
. For each
i\in S
, let
\Pi_{i,+},\Pi_{i,-},\Pi_{i,\circ}
be the spectral projectors of the
i
-th extrinsic Helstrom difference, choose any projector
Q_{i}\leq\Pi_{i,\circ}
, and form the optimal binary projective completion
\Pi_{i,0}=\Pi_{i,+}+Q_{i}
,
\Pi_{i,1}=\Pi_{i,-}+(\Pi_{i,\circ}-Q_{i})
, extended by the identity on
Y_{i}
. For a transmitted codeword
\bm{x}
, Gao’s union bound gives

\mathbb{P}\{\text{Error on any bit in $S$}\mid\bm{x}\}\leq 4\sum_{i\in S}\Tr((\mI-\Pi_{i,x_i})\rho_{\bY}^{\bx}).

Averaging over a uniformly transmitted codeword gives

\mathbb{P}\{\text{Error on any bit in $S$}\}\leq 4\sum_{i\in S}P_{b,i}(\mathcal{C})=4|S|P_{b}(\mathcal{C}),

where the last step uses transitivity.
Since
N=2^{m}
and
P_{b}(\mathcal{C})\leq e^{-c\eta\sqrt{m}}
, this tends to zero when
|S|=2^{o(\sqrt{\log N})}
.

IX
Conclusion

While
RM
codes have been studied extensively on BMS channels, comparatively little is known about their behavior on classical–quantum channels. In this paper, we develop a framework for analyzing
RM
codes over CQ channels through their bitwise optimal measurements. Although our primary goal was to establish vanishing bit-error probability for
RM
codes, the proof required a new correlation bound for quantum observables with suitable support and symmetry properties. We also developed an MMSE formulation for estimating a binary classical variable from a quantum system. Using the correlation bound, we showed that, when the code rate has a fixed gap below the Holevo capacity as the length increases, the MMSE and bit-error probability of each coordinate decay as
e^{-c\sqrt{\log N}}
. Together with the quantum union bound
[
7
]
, this implies that any set of
2^{o(\sqrt{\log N})}
coordinates can be decoded sequentially with vanishing error probability.

The principal remaining question is whether this result can be strengthened to vanishing block-error probability at all rates below the Holevo capacity. Such an extension would be significant beyond classical–quantum coding.
Binary pure-state channels provide a particularly compelling test case for such an extension. Through classical–quantum channel duality, a capacity-achieving block-decoding theorem in this setting would translate into a strong-secrecy result for dual Reed–Muller constructions on the corresponding binary symmetric wiretap channel
[
29
,
30
]
. Existing work comes close to this goal but does not yet establish secrecy capacity in full generality
[
21
]
. Thus, developing techniques that bridge bitwise optimality and collective block decoding is a natural next step, with consequences for both quantum decoding and classical wiretap coding.

Appendix A
Properties of MMSE

Definition 24
.

For states
\rho,\sigma\in\mathbb{D}^{d}
with
\mathrm{supp}(\rho)\subseteq\mathrm{supp}(\sigma)
, define the symmetric logarithmic derivative (SLD) quantum
\chi^{2}
divergence to be

\chi^{2}_{\mathrm{SLD}}(\rho\|\sigma)\coloneqq\Tr\!\left((\rho-\sigma)\Omega_{\sigma}^{-1}(\rho-\sigma)\right),

where

\Omega_{\sigma}(A)\coloneqq\frac{1}{2}(\sigma A+A\sigma)
is the SLD Fisher information operator and
\Omega_{\sigma}^{-1}
denotes the inverse on the support of
\sigma
.
If the support condition fails, set
\chi^{2}_{\mathrm{SLD}}(\rho\|\sigma)=\infty
.
When
\rho
and
\sigma
commute, this reduces to the classical
\chi^{2}
divergence

\chi^{2}(P\|Q)=\sum_{y:Q(y)>0}\frac{(P(y)-Q(y))^{2}}{Q(y)}

for their common eigenvalue distributions
P
and
Q
.

Lemma 25
.

For the uniform binary CQ state

\rho_{XY}=\frac{1}{2}\outerproduct{0}{0}_{X}\otimes\rho^{0}_{Y}+\frac{1}{2}\outerproduct{1}{1}_{X}\otimes\rho^{1}_{Y},

let
\bar{\rho}=(\rho^{0}+\rho^{1})/2
.
Then, we have

1-\mathcal{M}(X|Y)_{\rho_{XY}}=\chi^{2}_{\mathrm{SLD}}(\rho^{0}\|\bar{\rho}).

When
\rho^{0}
and
\rho^{1}
commute, the same identity holds for the classical
\chi^{2}
divergence between their eigenvalue distributions
P_{0}
and
P_{1}
with
\bar{P}=(P_{0}+P_{1})/2
:

\displaystyle 1-\mathcal{M}(X|Y)

\displaystyle=\chi^{2}(P_{0}\|\bar{P}).

Proof.

For the uniform input distribution,
\rho_{Y}=\bar{\rho}
and

D=(\rho^{0}-\rho^{1})/2=\rho^{0}-\bar{\rho}
.
By Lemma
7
, the optimal observable satisfies

\Omega_{\bar{\rho}}(M_{Y}^{*})=D
, and

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}(X|Y)_{\rho_{XY}}

\displaystyle=1-\Tr(D\measo_Y^*)=1-\Tr\!\left(D\Omega_{\bar{\rho}}^{-1}(D)\right)

\displaystyle=1-\chi^{2}_{\mathrm{SLD}}(\rho^{0}\|\bar{\rho}).

Since
\rho^{1}-\bar{\rho}=-D
, the same formula holds with
\rho^{1}
in place of
\rho^{0}
.
When the states commute, diagonalizing them simultaneously turns
\Omega_{\bar{\rho}}^{-1}
into entrywise division by
\bar{P}
, which results in
\chi^{2}(P_{0}\|\bar{P})
.
∎

Appendix B
KMS Inner Product and Canonical Basis

This appendix records the KMS analogue of the GNS construction for comparison but it is not needed for the proof of the main coding theorem.
For
\rho\in\mathbb{S}_{++}^{d}
and
F,G\in\mathbb{S}^{d}
, the KMS inner product on
\mathbb{S}^{d}
is denoted by

\left\langle F,G\right\rangle_{\rho}^{\mathrm{KMS}}\coloneqq\Tr(\obsg^{\dagger} \rho^{\frac{1}{2}} \obsf\rho^{\frac{1}{2}}).

By the cyclic property of the trace, the KMS inner product is always real for
F,G\in\mathbb{S}^{d}
.
Thus, the set
\mathbb{S}^{d}
with this inner product is a Hilbert space over
\mathbb{R}
.
This space has dimension
d^{2}
because each element of
\mathbb{S}^{d}
is defined by exactly
d^{2}
real parameters.

For the KMS inner product, one can find an orthonormal basis
\{\Omega_{0}^{\prime},\Omega_{1}^{\prime},\ldots,\Omega_{d^{2}-1}^{\prime}\}
for the vector space
\mathbb{S}^{d}
over
\mathbb{R}
, where
\Omega_{i}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{S}^{d}
for
i\in[d^{2}]
and
\Omega_{0}^{\prime}=\mathbb{I}
.
For example, one can apply Gram-Schmidt to any basis whose first element is
\mathbb{I}
.
Choosing
\Omega_{0}^{\prime}=\mathbb{I}
guarantees that

\left\langle F,\Omega_{0}^{\prime}\right\rangle_{\rho}^{\mathrm{KMS}}=\left\langle F,\mathbb{I}\right\rangle_{\rho}^{\mathrm{KMS}}=\Tr(\rho\obsf).

For
F,G\in\mathbb{S}^{d^{n}}
satisfying
F=\otimes_{i=0}^{n-1}F_{i}
and
G=\otimes_{i=0}^{n-1}G_{i}
, one can define

\left\langle F,G\right\rangle_{\rho,n}^{\mathrm{KMS}}\coloneqq\prod_{i=0}^{n-1}\left\langle F_{i},G_{i}\right\rangle_{\rho}^{\mathrm{KMS}}=\prod_{i=0}^{n-1}\Tr( \obsg_i^{\dagger} \rho^{\frac{1}{2}}\obsf_i\rho^{\frac{1}{2}}).

Similar to the GNS inner product, the extension to the full space satisfies

\left\langle F,G\right\rangle_{\rho^{\otimes n}}^{\mathrm{KMS}}=\Tr(\obsg^{\dagger} (\measgn)^{\frac{1}{2}} \obsf(\measgn)^{\frac{1}{2}})

and the single-system orthonormal basis naturally extends to an orthonormal basis for
\mathbb{S}^{d^{n}}
using the inner product on the full space.
Specifically, for
\bm{s}\in[d^{2}]^{n}
, we define the basis elements

\Omega_{\bm{s}}^{\prime}\coloneqq\Omega_{s_{0}}^{\prime}\otimes\cdots\otimes\Omega_{s_{n-1}}^{\prime}

and observe that

\displaystyle\left\langle\Omega_{\bm{s}}^{\prime},\Omega_{\bm{t}}^{\prime}\right\rangle_{\rho^{\otimes n}}^{\mathrm{KMS}}

\displaystyle\ =\prod_{i=0}^{n-1}\Tr( \basis{t_i}'^{\dagger}(\rho)^{\frac{1}{2}}\basis{s_i}'(\rho)^{\frac{1}{2}} )

\displaystyle\ =\prod_{i=0}^{n-1}\delta_{s_{i},t_{i}}=\delta_{\bm{s},\bm{t}}

For an observable
F\in\mathbb{S}^{d^{n}}
, let
\widehat{F}_{\bm{s}}^{\mathrm{KMS}}=\left\langle F,\Omega_{\bm{s}}^{\prime}\right\rangle_{\rho^{\otimes n}}^{\mathrm{KMS}}
so that we get the KMS decomposition for
F
as

F=\sum_{\bm{s}\in[d^{2}]^{n}}\widehat{F}_{\bm{s}}^{\mathrm{KMS}}\Omega_{\bm{s}}^{\prime}.

On the other hand, it also satisfies Parseval’s theorem as

\displaystyle\left(\left\|F\right\|_{\rho^{\otimes n}}^{\mathrm{KMS}}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\ =\Tr(\obsf^{\dagger} (\measgn)^{\frac{1}{2}} \obsf(\measgn)^{\frac{1}{2}})

\displaystyle\ =\sum_{\bm{s}\in[d^{2}]^{n}}|\widehat{F}_{\bm{s}}^{\mathrm{KMS}}|^{2}.

Thus, we find that the variance relation

\displaystyle\text{Var}_{\mathrm{KMS}}(F)_{\rho^{\otimes n}}=

\displaystyle\ \left(\left\|F\right\|_{\rho^{\otimes n}}^{\mathrm{KMS}}\right)^{2}-\left(\left\langle F,\mathbb{I}\right\rangle_{\rho^{\otimes n}}^{\mathrm{KMS}}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\ =\sum_{\bm{s}\in[d^{2}]^{n}:\bm{s}\neq\bm{0}}|\widehat{F}_{\bm{s}}^{\mathrm{KMS}}|^{2}.

Using these properties, we can also obtain the transitive symmetry lemma for observables
F
and
G
under the KMS inner product.

Lemma 26
.

For pairwise disjoint sets
A,B,C\subseteq[n]
with
|B|=|C|
and
\kappa=|A|/(|A|+|B|)
, let
F
and
G
be observables supported within
A\cup B
and
A\cup C
respectively so that

and

.
Suppose a permutation
\pi\in S_{n}
satisfies
\pi(i)=i
for

and
\mathrm{SW}_{\pi}\,F\,\mathrm{SW}_{\pi}^{\dagger}=G
.
Then, if
\mathrm{Sym}(F_{AB})
is transitive on

, we have

\displaystyle\left|\left\langle F,G\right\rangle_{\rho^{\otimes n}}^{\mathrm{KMS}}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\kappa\left(\left\|F\right\|_{\rho^{\otimes n}}^{\mathrm{KMS}}\right)^{2}+(1-\kappa)\left(\left\langle F,\mathbb{I}\right\rangle_{\rho^{\otimes n}}^{\mathrm{KMS}}\right)^{2}.

Proof.

The proof of Lemma

applies to this case simply by changing all GNS inner products and norms to KMS inner products and norms.

Appendix C
Correlation Inequality Lemma

For completeness, we now give a two-observable GNS correlation bound that does not require one observable to be a permuted copy of the other. It strengthens the structural viewpoint of Lemma
16
but is not used in the main recursion.

Lemma 27
.

Consider disjoint sets
A,B,C\subseteq[n]
with
\kappa_{1}=\frac{|A|}{|A|+|B|}
and
\kappa_{2}=\frac{|A|}{|A|+|C|}
. Let
F
and
G
be observables on the full
ABC
system supported
A\cup B
and
A\cup C
, respectively. Then, if
\mathrm{Sym}(F_{AB})
is transitive on
A\cup B
and
\mathrm{Sym}(G_{AC})
is transitive on
A\cup C
, then we have

\displaystyle\left|\left\langle F,G\right\rangle_{\rho^{\otimes n}}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\kappa_{1}\left\|F\right\|_{\rho^{\otimes n}}^{2}+(1-\kappa_{1})\big|\!\left\langle F,\mathbb{I}\right\rangle_{\rho^{\otimes n}}\!\big|^{2}}

\displaystyle\quad\times\sqrt{\kappa_{2}\left\|G\right\|_{\rho^{\otimes n}}^{2}+(1-\kappa_{2})\big|\mkern-4.0mu\left\langle G,\mathbb{I}\right\rangle_{\rho^{\otimes n}}\mkern-4.0mu\big|^{2}}.

Proof.

Using the expansion of
F,G
in the orthonormal basis
\{\Omega_{\bm{s}}\}_{\bm{s}\in[d^{2}]^{n}}
as shown in the proof of Lemma
16
, we get

\displaystyle\ \left|\left\langle F,G\right\rangle_{\rho^{\otimes n}}\mkern-1.0mu\right|

\displaystyle\ =\left|\left\langle\left(\widehat{F}_{\mathbf{0}}\Omega_{\mathbf{0}}+\sum_{\bm{s}\in\mathcal{S}_{A}}\widehat{F}_{\bm{s}}\Omega_{\bm{s}}\right),\left(\widehat{G}_{\mathbf{0}}\Omega_{\mathbf{0}}+\sum_{\bm{t}\in\mathcal{S}_{A}}\widehat{G}_{\bm{t}}\Omega_{\bm{t}}\right)\right\rangle_{\rho^{\otimes n}}\right|

\displaystyle\ \leq\left\|\widehat{F}_{\mathbf{0}}\Omega_{\mathbf{0}}+\sum_{\bm{s}\in\mathcal{S}_{A}}\widehat{F}_{\bm{s}}\Omega_{\bm{s}}\right\|_{\rho^{\otimes n}}\left\|\widehat{G}_{\mathbf{0}}\Omega_{\mathbf{0}}+\sum_{\bm{t}\in\mathcal{S}_{A}}\widehat{G}_{\bm{t}}\Omega_{\bm{t}}\right\|_{\rho^{\otimes n}}

\displaystyle\ =\sqrt{|\widehat{F}_{\mathbf{0}}|^{2}+\sum_{\bm{s}\in\mathcal{S}_{A}}|\widehat{F}_{\bm{s}}|^{2}}\sqrt{|\widehat{G}_{\mathbf{0}}|^{2}+\sum_{\bm{t}\in\mathcal{S}_{A}}|\widehat{G}_{\bm{t}}|^{2}}.

On the other hand, from the transitive symmetry of the observables
F_{AB}
and
G_{AC}
and using Lemma
14
, we get

\displaystyle\ \left\|F\right\|_{\rho^{\otimes n}}^{2}=|\widehat{F}_{\mathbf{0}}|^{2}+(|A|+|B|)\overline{\eta}(F)

\displaystyle\ \left\|G\right\|_{\rho^{\otimes n}}^{2}=|\widehat{G}_{\mathbf{0}}|^{2}+(|A|+|C|)\overline{\eta}(G).

Thus, the following inequalities hold for
F
and
G
:

\displaystyle\ \frac{|A|}{|A|+|B|}\left(\left\|F\right\|_{\rho^{\otimes n}}^{2}-|\widehat{F}_{\mathbf{0}}|^{2}\right)\geq\sum_{\bm{s}\in\mathcal{S}_{A}}|\widehat{F}_{\bm{s}}|^{2},

\displaystyle\ \frac{|A|}{|A|+|C|}\left(\left\|G\right\|_{\rho^{\otimes n}}^{2}-|\widehat{G}_{\mathbf{0}}|^{2}\right)\geq\sum_{\bm{s}\in\mathcal{S}_{A}}|\widehat{G}_{\bm{s}}|^{2}.

Finally, we obtain

\displaystyle\left|\left\langle F,G\right\rangle_{\rho^{\otimes n}}\mkern-1.0mu\right|

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{|\widehat{F}_{\mathbf{0}}|^{2}+\sum_{\bm{s}\in\mathcal{S}_{A}}|\widehat{F}_{\bm{s}}|^{2}}\sqrt{|\widehat{G}_{\mathbf{0}}|^{2}+\sum_{\bm{t}\in\mathcal{S}_{A}}|\widehat{G}_{\bm{t}}|^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\kappa_{1}\left\|F\right\|_{\rho^{\otimes n}}^{2}+(1-\kappa_{1})\big|\!\left\langle F,\mathbb{I}\right\rangle_{\rho^{\otimes n}}\mkern-4.0mu\big|^{2}}

\displaystyle\qquad\cdot\sqrt{\kappa_{2}\left\|G\right\|_{\rho^{\otimes n}}^{2}+(1-\kappa_{2})\big|\!\left\langle G,\mathbb{I}\right\rangle_{\rho^{\otimes n}}\mkern-4.0mu\big|^{2}}.\qed

Remark 28
.

Lemma

also holds for the KMS inner product and the same proof applies simply by changing all GNS inner products and norms to KMS inner products and norms.
