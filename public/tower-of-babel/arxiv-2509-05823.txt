Abstract
Motivated by Tweedie’s formula for the Compound Decision problem Robbins 1951 , we examine the theoretical foundations of empirical Bayes estimators that directly model the marginal density m ⁡ ( y ) m(y) . Our main result shows that polynomial log-marginals of degree k ≥ 3 k\geq 3 cannot arise from any valid prior distribution in exponential family models, while quadratic forms correspond exactly to Gaussian priors. This provides theoretical justification for why certain empirical Bayes decision rules, while practically useful, do not correspond to any formal Bayes procedures. 1
Empirical Bayes and Compound Decision

Consider an experiment in which unknown parameters, say
\bm{\mu}=(\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{n})
, gives rise to observations
Y_{i}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{ind}}}{{\sim}}f(y\mid\mu_{i})
,
i=1,2,\ldots,n
, and the goal is to estimate
\bm{\mu}
under a loss function
\ell(y,\mu)
, e.g., the squared-error loss
n^{-1}\left|\left|\hat{\bm{\mu}}-\bm{\mu}\right|\right|^{2}
. We assume that the
Y_{i}
’s are exchangeable, i.e., invariant under permutation of the indices, which implies conditional independence and identical nature by de Finetti’s theorem. For Gaussian
f(\cdot)
, this problem is known as the normal means, or Gaussian sequence, or the Gaussian compound decision problem. In the compound decision problem
(
Robbins 1951
)
, the estimates (or any decision
\delta_{i}
about
\mu_{i}
) is allowed to depend on all observations
{\bf y}=(y_{1},\ldots,y_{n})
, under the compound risk
{\mathbb{E}}_{\bm{\theta}}\{\ell(\delta,\bm{\theta})\}
. The compound decision problem, together with Stein’s shrinkage phenomenon
(
Stein 1956
)
, shows that by allowing individual decisions/estimates to depend on the entire sequence, substantial reduction of total loss can be achieved. It is easy to see that the means
\mu_{i}
are typically less extreme than the observations
y_{i}
; as Efron
(
Efron 2011
)
emphasizes, such regression to the mean is essentially the selection-bias or “winner’s curse” phenomenon. The empirical Bayes methodology, introduced by
(
Robbins 1956
)
, provides a statistical procedure where the goal of approximating the ideal or oracle Bayes rule is nearly achieved without specifying a prior
(
Zhang 2003
;
Efron 2011
)
. As noted by
Zhang 2003
, the compound decision problem and the empirical Bayes methodology have been called the ‘two breakthroughs’ in post-war statistics by
(
Neyman 1962
)
, as they have influenced a lot of modern statistical methods, especially in the context of high-dimensional data.

One of the main attractions of the Empirical Bayes framework for the Compound Decision problem is that it can result in risk reduction without solving the deconvolution problem of estimating the common conditional distribution
F
. Rather, one could work with the ‘observable’ marginal density
m(y)
, and use either parametric or nonparametric models to estimate it, and use the estimated
\hat{m}(y)
as a plug-in in the Empirical Bayes approach. For example, assuming
\mu_{i}
’s to be normally distributed with zero mean and unknown variance
\sigma_{0}^{2}
, leads to a Gaussian marginal for the
Y_{i}
’s. Using a method of moments estimator for the
\sigma_{0}^{2}
leads to the famous, classical James–Stein estimator
\delta(y)=(1-(n-2)/\sum_{i=1}^{n}y_{i}^{2})y

(
Stein 1956
)
. Difficulty in choosing a parametric model for the marginal, leads the way to more flexible nonparametric models such as
Kiefer & Wolfowitz 1956
, which is easily seen to be convex and can benefit hugely from recent advances in convex optimization tools, as shown by
Koenker & Mizera 2014
. Yet another approach is proposed by Efron
(
Efron 2008
;
Efron 2012
;
Efron 2011
)
, using ‘Lindsey’s method’, which consists of estimating the marginal distribution from a Poisson regression of an integer order
K
, based on Lindsey’s Method that models the histogram bin counts of the observed
{\bf y}
-values as Poisson random variables.
Efron 2011
’s formulation yields locally adaptive shrinkage based on the local shape of the marginal histogram, while making no parametric assumptions on the prior. This has inspired subsequent developments in the Empirical Bayes/Compound Decision problem by nonparametric density estimates of the marginal
(
Simon & Simon 2013
;
Wager 2014
, e.g.,)
. As emphasized by
Efron 2014
, empirical Bayes admits two complementary strategies:
f-modeling
, which models the marginal density
m(\cdot)
on the observation (
y
) scale, and
g-modeling
, which models the prior
F
on the parameter (
\mu
) scale. The former have been relied upon heavily in applications, especially after
Robbins 1956
, while the latter have been predominant in the theoretical EB literature
(
Laird 1978
;
Jiang & Zhang 2009
)
.

Given the popularity and performance of EB methods, a natural question from a Bayesian perspective is:
for what class of prior distributions could a particular empirical-decision rule arise?
In this article, we provide partial answers. First, if
\log m(y)
is a polynomial of degree
>2
, no prior can produce
m(y)
via Tweedie’s formula; the only polynomial cases compatible with Bayes are degrees
\leq 2
, with
K=2
corresponding to a Gaussian prior. Second, beyond the convexity constraint on
c(y)=\tfrac{1}{2}y^{2}+\log m(y)
as required by
Koenker & Mizera 2014
, we point out that a marginal is a Gaussian convolution if and only if it extends (in a neighborhood of the smoothing parameter) to a bounded solution of the heat equation
u_{t}=u_{xx}
by the Hirschman-Widder Weierstrass representation theorem
(
Hirschman & Widder 1955
, Thm. VIII.6.3)
.

Tweedie’s formula

In the simplest normal means model, let
\mu_{i}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{iid}}}{{\sim}}F

Y_{i}\mid\mu_{i}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{ind}}}{{\sim}}\mathcal{N}(\mu_{i},1)

Efron 2011

The posterior distribution under this model would be:

Efron 2011

Polson 1991

{\mathbb{E}}(\mu\mid y)=m-\tau^{2}\frac{\partial}{\partial\hat{\mu}}\log p(\hat{\mu}\mid a),

(2)

Polson 1991

Let

\beta

\mathbb{E}[\bm{\beta}\mid{\bf y}]=({\bf X}^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}{\bf X})^{-1}\,{\bf X}^{\top}\Big(\Sigma^{-1}{\bf y}+\nabla_{{\bf y}}\log m({\bf y})\Big).

Let the prior for the precision

SURE:
Tweedie’s formula is also related to the Stein’s unbiased risk estimation
(
Stein 1981

framework for achieving a finite sample unbiased estimate of the prediction risk. For the normal means model,
{\bf y}\sim\mathcal{N}(\bm{\mu},\sigma^{2}I)

\left|\left|\hat{{\bf y}}-{\bf y}\right|\right|^{2}+2\sigma^{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\partial}{\partial y_{i}}{\hat{y}_{i}}

Bhadra et al. 2019

\mathrm{SURE}(\delta)=1+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Big\{s_{F}(Y_{i})^{2}+2\,\frac{\partial}{\partial y}s_{F}(Y_{i})\Big\}.

Choosing

Ghosh et al. 2025
further provide a unified ‘

(

)

(

for learning the prior, and establishes near-parametric convergence rates for regret/Fisher divergence with oracle inequalities under misspecification.

2
Avoiding Deconvolution

All empirical Bayes methods, thus, share the goal of estimating the mixing measure
F
, irrespective of the loss function used
(
Gu & Koenker 2016
)
. The deconvolution problem, i.e., estimating
F
, was referred to as ‘estimating the inestimable’ by Robbins
(
Koenker & Gu 2024
)
, and has a long history, as pointed out by
Ritov 2024
. However, the typical reason is to provide a solution to the normal means or compound decision problem
(
Robbins 1956
)
, i.e., a good estimate for the normal means
(\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{n})
something of interest in many different applications. For this problem, deconvolution is often an intermediate goal, but not the primary objective.

We do not attempt a full review of the vast literature on the normal-means problem. Instead, we focus on a specific class of empirical-Bayes estimators called empirical decision rules
(
Koenker & Mizera 2014
)
. An empirical decision rule attempts to estimate the posterior mean function
{\mathbb{E}}(\mu_{i}\mid y_{i})
directly. Such a rule implicitly invokes the existence of some prior distribution
f_{0}
but does not attempt to estimate it
(
Efron 2011
)
.

To understand this empirical-Bayes approach, return to the simple normal-mean model
(y_{i}\mid\mu_{i})\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu_{i},1)
. Absent any prior information that would distinguish the
\mu_{i}
, a natural assumption is that they arise exchangeably from some prior,
\mu_{i}\sim F
. An important result discussed by
Robbins 1956
holds that the posterior mean can be written as the unbiased estimate plus a Bayes correction:

E(\mu|y)=y+\frac{\partial}{\partial y}\log m(y),

(4)

where
m(y)=\int\varphi(y\mid\mu)dF(\mu)
is the marginal density of the data under this prior. The prior does not appear explicitly, but its effect is incorporated in
m(y)
.
Efron 2011
nicely summarizes its appeal: “
The crucial advantage of Tweedie’s formula is that it works directly with the marginal density
,” thereby offering the statistician the opportunity to avoid deconvolution entirely.

2.1
Nonparametric Maximum Likelihood

Among methods that directly estimate the marginal,
Brown & Greenshtein 2009
advocate for replacing
m(y)
by a kernel density estimate of it, while the usage of a nonparametric maximum likelihood estimator
(e.g.
Kiefer & Wolfowitz 1956
, NPMLE)
for
m(y)
has been investigated by various authors including
Jiang & Zhang 2009
;
Saha & Guntuboyina 2020
;
Greenshtein & Ritov 2022
.
Koenker & Mizera 2014
argue that recent advances in convex optimization has paved the way for wider usage and applicability of the Kiefer–Wolfowitz algorithm for the nonparametric empirical Bayes compound decision problems. Ritov
(
Ritov 2024
)
shows that the Tweedie plug-in based on the nonparametric MLE of the marginal (equivalently, the Kiefer-Wolfowitz NPMLE for the mixing law) yields a decision rule (based on the Tweedie’s formula) that is minimax in both the empirical Bayes and compound-decision formulations, establishes concentration for the NPMLE decision, and links its risk directly to SURE - thereby providing a justification for NPMLE without oracle arguments.

In other words, the key insight here is that, since we observe
m(y)
directly, a non-parametric approach to estimate
m(y)
and use that as a plug-in in Tweedie’s formula is a natural solution. Moreover, since (
4
) only involves the marginal
m(y)
, one can bypass priors entirely and estimate:

\hat{\mu}_{i}^{\text{TW}}\;=\;y_{i}+\partial_{y}\log\hat{m}(y_{i}),

where
\hat{m}
is any nonparametric estimate of
m
(e.g., a kernel density, a Kiefer–Wolfowitz NPMLE Gaussian mixture, or a shape-constrained fit), thus avoiding deconvolution and explicit prior modeling
(
Brown & Greenshtein 2009
;
Kiefer & Wolfowitz 1956
;
Koenker & Mizera 2014
;
Jiang & Zhang 2009
;
Saha & Guntuboyina 2020
;
Greenshtein & Ritov 2022
)
. Writing
\ell(y)=\log m(y)
and
s(y)=\ell^{\prime}(y)
, one may estimate
\ell
by minimizing a score-matching/SURE objective with a smoothness penalty,

\min_{\ell}\;\Big\{\;\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\big[s(y_{i})^{2}+2\,s^{\prime}(y_{i})\big]\;+\;\rho\,\mathcal{R}(\ell)\;\Big\},\qquad\mathcal{R}(\ell)=\int\big(\ell^{\prime\prime}(y)\big)^{2}\,dy,

optionally under shape constraints such as convexity of
c(y)=\tfrac{1}{2}y^{2}+\ell(y)
on a grid (linear inequalities), which yields a convex quadratic program and directly returns the Tweedie plug-in
\hat{\mu}_{i}^{\text{TW}}

(
Ghosh et al. 2025
, see also the SURE/score-matching equivalence in)
.

2.2
Lindsey’s method

Efron 2011
argues that the Tweedie’s formula
y+\frac{\partial}{\partial y}\log m(y)
requires a smoothly differentiable estimate of the log marginal
\log m(y)
, and describes a Poisson regression technique, called the ‘Lindsey’s method’
(
Efron 2004
;
Efron 2011
)
and
(
Efron 2012
, ch.5)
. The Lindsey’s method assumes that the log-marginal is a
K
-th degree polynomial, for some integer
K
. That is, it estimates the
\mu_{i}
by first estimating the marginal density directly using a polynomial or spline on the log scale, e.g.,

\hat{m}(y)=\exp\left(\hat{\beta}_{0}+\sum_{k=1}^{K}\hat{\beta}_{k}y^{k}\right).

(5)

The
\beta_{k}
’s are estimated via a Poisson regression (using usual generalized linear model tools) to the histogram counts after binning the data. Under this form of the marginal density, the posterior mean function is easily computed by taking the log and differentiating. Following on the work of
Efron 2011
,
Koenker & Mizera 2014
considered an estimator based on the observation that the posterior mean function
{\mathbb{E}}(\mu\mid y)
in Tweedie’s formula (
4
) is non-decreasing in
y
. This implies that the function

c(y)=\frac{1}{2}y^{2}+\log m(y)

is convex, a result which holds not merely for the Gaussian case, but for any exponential-family observational model, regardless of the prior
F
. However, an unconstrained or traditional kernel-based estimate of
m(y)
does not ensure convexity of
c(y)
. As discussed before,
Koenker & Mizera 2014
proposed to estimate
m(y)
non-parametrically.

3
A Bayesian view of empirical decision rules

From a Bayesian perspective, a natural question is:
for what class of prior distributions could a particular empirical-decision rule arise?
In the case of Efron’s estimator, we provide an explicit answer in the following theorem:
there are no such priors, save the trivial case of a Gaussian prior
(and a quadratic log marginal). We state this as the following theorem:

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
\varphi(y\mid\mu)=\exp\{\mu y-\tfrac{1}{2}\mu^{2}\}\,\varphi(y)
be the
\mathcal{N}(\mu,1)
exponential-family form, where
\varphi
is the
\mathcal{N}(0,1)
density, and let
m(y)=\int\varphi(y\mid\mu)\,dF(\mu)
be the marginal induced by a prior
F
on
\mu\in\mathbb{R}
. If
\log m(y)
is a polynomial of degree
K\geq 3
on
\mathbb{R}
, then there is no such prior
F
. In other words, for all prior measures
F
supported on
\mathbb{R}
, and for all nonzero choices of
\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{K}
,

\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\phi(y\mid\mu)~dF(\mu)\neq\exp\left({\beta}_{0}+\sum_{k=1}^{K}{\beta}_{k}y^{k}\right),\quad\text{if}\quad K\geq 3.

If
K=2
, then
F
must be (possibly degenerate) Gaussian.

Hence, Efron’s Empirical Bayes rule is therefore not a formal Bayes rule, in that it cannot arise from a valid application of Tweedie’s formula under any prior. The only exception is
K=2
, implying that
F
is Gaussian. Interestingly, when
\log m(y)
is quadratic (
K=2
), Tweedie’s formula gives
{\mathbb{E}}(\mu_{i}\mid Y_{i}=y)=(1-1/V)y
for a normal marginal
\mathcal{N}(0,V)
, and replacing
1/V
by the unbiased estimator
(n-2)/\sum_{j=1}^{n}y_{j}^{2}
yields the James–Stein estimator
(
Efron 2011
)
.

Koenker & Mizera 2014
cast two EB estimators as convex programs: (i) a direct MLE of the marginal density
m
under the shape constraint that
c(y)=\tfrac{1}{2}y^{2}+\log m(y)
is convex : this enforces monotonicity of
\delta(y)=y+m^{\prime}(y)/m(y)=c^{\prime}(y)
and yields a piecewise-linear
c
, so
\delta(y)=c^{\prime}(y)
is piecewise constant; and (ii) a Kiefer–Wolfowitz NPMLE of the mixing law
F
via a finite-dimensional convex dual whose solution is atomic (with at most
n
support points), with interior-point implementations delivering substantial speedups over EM while retaining excellent risk performance. For the
Koenker & Mizera 2014
estimator, all Gaussian convolutions do satisfy the convexity of
c
, but convexity is only necessary, not sufficient. Additional conditions are given by the following result adapted from Theorem VIII.6.3 of
Hirschman & Widder 1955
.

Theorem 3.2

(
Hirschman & Widder 1955
)
.

Let
u:\mathbb{R}\times(0,\infty)\mapsto\mathbb{R}
be
C^{2}
in
x
,
C^{1}
in
t
, and bounded for each
t>0
.
Then the following are equivalent:

1.

u
solves the heat equation
u_{t}=u_{xx}
on
\mathbb{R}\times(0,\infty)
and, for some bounded Borel measure
\mu
,

u(x,t)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{4\pi t}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{-(x-y)^{2}/(4t)}\,d\mu(y)\qquad(t>0).

2.

For each fixed
t>0
,
u(\cdot,t)
is the Weierstrass transform (Gaussian convolution) of a bounded function; in particular
m_{t}(x):=u(x,t)
is a Gaussian convolution.

If, in addition,
\mu
has a bounded density
f
, then
u(\cdot,t)=\phi_{t}*f
with
\phi_{t}
the
N(0,2t)
kernel. Consequently, for a single marginal
m(x)=u(x,t_{0})
(e.g.,
t_{0}=1
), being a Gaussian convolution is stronger than having

m(\cdot)
must extend to a bounded solution of the heat equation in a time neighborhood of
t_{0}
.

Remark 3.3
.

One-parameter antecedents.
For one-parameter models, early work showed important rigidity phenomena: for a class where the sample total is sufficient for any sample size, the fiducial distribution of the parameter cannot coincide with any Bayesian posterior under any prior
(
Grundy 1956
)
. In one-parameter natural exponential families,
Sampson 1975
shows that the moment generating function (as a function of the natural parameter) uniquely characterizes the family and provides necessary and sufficient conditions for such MGFs. In one-parameter settings where the posterior expectation of the population mean is a linear function of the sample observation,
Goldstein 1975
proves that the moments of the prior distribution are uniquely determined, giving precise uniqueness relations for such linear Bayes rules. Finally, in a one-parameter exponential family under squared-error loss,
Goldstein 1977
shows that the only Bayes estimators whose 1
-
Lipschitz (contraction) transforms are also Bayes are the linear (affine) estimators in the canonical sufficient statistic.

is a polynomial on
\mathbb{R}
, then

\log M_{H}(y)=\log m(y)-\log\varphi(y)=:P(y)

is also a polynomial (since,
-\log\varphi(y)=\tfrac{1}{2}y^{2}+\mathrm{const}
is quadratic).
Both
M_{H}(z)
and
e^{P(z)}
are entire and agree for all real
z
; by the identity theorem for entire functions,
M_{H}(z)\equiv e^{P(z)}
on
\mathbb{C}
(see, e.g.,
Ahlfors 1979
). Evaluating on the imaginary axis and normalizing yields a characteristic function:

\phi_{\widetilde{H}}(t):=\frac{M_{H}(it)}{M}=\exp\!\bigl(P(it)-\log M\bigr)=:\exp Q(t),

so
\log\phi_{\widetilde{H}}
is a polynomial.

By Marcinkiewicz’s theorem, if a characteristic function has the form
\phi(t)=\exp\{Q(t)\}
with
Q
a polynomial on
\mathbb{R}
, then
\deg Q\leq 2
. Moreover,
\deg Q=0,1
correspond to a degenerate distribution and
\deg Q=2
to a (nondegenerate) Gaussian distribution
(
Bryc 1995
, Thm. 2.5.3)
; see also
Lukacs 1970
and
Marcinkiewicz 1939
.
Therefore
\deg P\leq 2
, so
\deg\log m(\cdot)\leq 2
. In particular, no prior
F
can produce a marginal with polynomial
\log m
of degree
K\geq 3
.
(Marcinkiewicz’s theorem applies here since
\phi_{\tilde{H}}(t)=M_{H}(it)/M
is a bona fide characteristic function.)

If
\log M_{H}(y)=\alpha y^{2}+\beta y+\gamma
, then

m(y)=\varphi(y)\,e^{\alpha y^{2}+\beta y+\gamma}=C\exp\!\Bigl(-\tfrac{1}{2}(1-2\alpha)\,y^{2}+\beta y\Bigr),

with
C=e^{\gamma}/\sqrt{2\pi}
. Since
m
is integrable,
1-2\alpha>0
, so
m
is a (nondegenerate) Gaussian density.
Write
Y=\mu+\varepsilon
with
\varepsilon\sim N(0,1)
independent of
\mu\sim F
. Then
Y
is normal and
\varepsilon
is nondegenerate normal. By Cramér’s decomposition theorem,
\mu
must be (possibly degenerate) normal
(
Bryc 1995
, Thm. 2.5.2)
; see also
Cramér 1936
. Hence,
F
is Gaussian.

∎

Remark 3.4
.

The argument extends verbatim to any LEF (linear exponential family) with quadratic carrier log-density, since Step 2 only uses that
\log f_{0}(y)
is quadratic to pass from
\log m
polynomial to
\log M_{H}
polynomial. For general LEFs this implication is even more restrictive.
Background on characteristic functions and cumulants (used implicitly in Marcinkiewicz/Cramer arguments) is collected in
Feller 1971
.

4
Conclusion

Why care whether an empirical Bayes estimator corresponds to a formal Bayes procedure, that is, whether it can arise from a hierarchical model with an actual prior? Proper Bayes rules bring structural guarantees: admissibility under a proper prior with finite Bayes risk, coherence of the posterior, and, in favorable cases, least-favorable-prior minimaxity. A standard result (see Lehmann and Casella) states: let
R(\theta,\delta)={\mathbb{E}}_{\theta}L(\theta,\delta(X))
be the risk and
r(\delta,\Pi)=\int R(\theta,\delta)\,d\Pi(\theta)
the Bayes risk for a proper prior
\Pi
; if
\delta_{\Pi}
is Bayes with respect to
\Pi
and
r(\delta_{\Pi},\Pi)<\infty
, then
\delta_{\Pi}
is admissible. Admissibility itself is a weak filter and does not, by itself, recommend a procedure: as
Strawderman 2021
notes, “
Admissibility is a relatively weak optimality property, and the fact that a procedure is admissible does not give strong evidence that it should be adopted. Typically, there are a very large number of admissible procedures in any given problem, including all procedures which are unique Bayes with respect to any proper prior.
” Our results mark the boundary for Tweedie-based rules: polynomial
\log m
of degree greater than two cannot be Bayes, the quadratic case corresponds to a Gaussian prior, and a marginal is a Gaussian convolution only if it extends locally in the smoothing parameter to a bounded solution of the heat equation. We hope to have shown that Empirical Bayes and NPMLE plug-ins optimize observable criteria such as SURE and regret, but they do not automatically inherit the guarantees that come with a proper Bayes formulation.
