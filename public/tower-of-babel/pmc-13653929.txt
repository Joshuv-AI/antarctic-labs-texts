Targeting α6β1 integrin signaling with pranlukast to modulate venous endothelium function and reduce post-thrombotic vein wall injury
Authors: Alessia L Taylor, Oscar Moreno, Catherine E Luke, Mattea Pellerito, Antonio Pellerito, Nehali Patel, Qing Cai, Sabrina Rocco, Daniel D Myers, Thomas Wakefield, Andrea T Obi, Peter Henke
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPost-thrombotic syndrome (PTS) develops in 20% to 50% of patients following deep vein thrombosis (DVT) due to persistent vein wall injury, inflammation, and fibrotic remodeling. Current anticoagulant therapies do not directly address PTS pathogenesis and carry bleeding risks. We characterized the extracellular matrix (ECM) components and evaluated whether pharmacological inhibition of α6β1 integrin with pranlukast (PLK) could modulate post-thrombotic vein wall injury in a preclinical DVT model.MethodsECM protein composition was characterized in C57BL/6 mice by Western blotting of inferior vena cava (IVC) harvested after IVC ligation from 4 to 21 days, identifying the laminin-α6β1 integrin axis as a therapeutic target. The presence of α6β1 was confirmed via immunofluorescence in post-thrombotic mice up to 21 days. PLK, a candidate α6β1 integrin antagonist, was tested in vitro and in vivo. Venous endothelial cells were treated with PLK to assess transcription of integrins (Itga6 and Itgb1) and inflammation markers (sele). In vivo, C57BL/6 mice received PLK or vehicle before and after IVC ligation. Thrombus characteristics, vein wall remodeling, integrin protein expression, and cellular composition were evaluated.ResultsLaminin, fibrin, thrombomodulin, and fibronectin were present in the post-thrombotic IVC in mice. The ECM protein laminin and its primary receptor, α6β1 integrin (CD49f/CD29), were highly expressed up to 21 days post-thrombosis in the vein wall. In vitro, PLK treatment significantly decreased transcription of Itga6, Itgb1, and sele after 24 hours. PLK did not affect thrombus formation, with comparable thrombus weights and lengths across groups, demonstrating a safety profile. The administration of PLK was associated with decreased levels of α6 and β1 integrins in the vein wall and thrombus, as well as a significant reduction in vein wall thickness (PLK, 44.95 ± 5.09 μm; dimethyl sulfoxide, 56.84 ± 6.54 μm; P = .0081) at 8 days and in proinflammatory monocytes (CCR2+) compared with control (PLK, 14.73 ±1.69; dimethyl sulfoxide, 23.15 ± 2.38; P < .0001).ConclusionsThis study demonstrates that targeting α6β1 integrin with PLK reduces vein wall fibrotic injury following experimental DVT without altering thrombus formation. These findings identify a novel, nonanticoagulant therapeutic strategy for PTS prevention that warrants further investigation in chronic models and translational studies.Keywords: Pranlukast, Post-thrombotic syndrome, Deep vein thrombosis, α6β1 integrin, Vein wall fibrosis, Venous endothelial cells

Deep vein thrombosis (DVT) affects up to 900,000 Americans annually, causing approximately 60,000 to 100,000 deaths per year.1 Unresolved DVT leads to post-thrombotic syndrome (PTS) in 20% to 50% of patients, characterized by chronic pain, edema, skin changes, and venous ulceration that significantly impair quality of life.2,3 The pathophysiology of PTS involves injury to the venous wall via local inflammation [nuclear factor kappa-light-chain-enhancer of activated B cells (NF-κB), tumor necrosis factor-α (TNFα), interleukin-6 (IL-6), and IL-1β], loss of endothelial cells, and increased venous wall stiffness and thickness (increased collagen remodeling in response to transforming growth factor-β).4,5 Current anticoagulant therapies are effective in preventing thrombus extension and pulmonary embolism, but do not directly address the underlying vein wall pathology and are associated with an increased risk of bleeding.6 Once vein wall damage occurs, characterized by increased thickness, stiffness, and valvular dysfunction, patients face higher risks of recurrent DVT and obstruction and often require lifelong anticoagulation.

Two key factors linked to thrombosis resolution are restoration of luminal endothelial coverage (re-endothelialization/angiogenesis)7,8 and extracellular matrix (ECM) reorganization. During DVT, laminin, a major basement membrane glycoprotein, interacts with multiple cell surface receptors to modulate collagen remodeling [via α2β1/α3β1/matrix metalloproteinase (MMP) signaling], wound healing (α3β1/α6β4/keratinocyte migration and adhesion), angiogenesis (endothelial α3β1/α6β4 signaling), and coagulation (platelet α6β1/vessel wall adhesion).9,10 Initial αIIbβ3 integrin antagonists, such as abciximab (Reopro), eptifibatide (Integrilin), and tirofiban (Aggrastat), were developed as antithrombotic therapeutics but also induced integrin conformational changes to a high-affinity state, which can cause thrombocytopenia and elevate bleeding risk.11 Other strategies to reduce thrombogenesis, like glycoprotein VI (GPVI) blocking, also interfere with ECM-platelet signaling. This approach increases bleeding risk by impairing the recognition of the exposed matrix, which is crucial for identifying vascular injury and starting hemostasis.12

Conversely, targeting laminin-integrin interactions appears not to raise bleeding risk, suggesting that it could be a safer alternative,9 providing an attractive alternative approach for DVT treatment. More specifically, α6β1 integrin (CD49f/CD29) is expressed on platelets, endothelial cells, leukocytes, and fibroblasts.13,14 Pranlukast (PLK), originally developed as a cysteinyl leukotriene receptor antagonist for asthma treatment, has been identified through in silico screening as a potential antagonist of α6β1 integrin.15 Antagonists of integrins have been shown to reduce fibrogenesis in idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis, supporting their potential as agents to reduce PTS.16 This study characterizes ECM components in the post-thrombotic vein wall using a well-established murine model of stasis-induced DVT. We hypothesized that pharmacological inhibition of α6β1 integrin, which is overexpressed in post-thrombotic veins, could modulate vein wall injury without altering thrombogenesis, possibly providing an additional therapeutic option.

Methods

Study design

This study consisted of two parts. Part I examined ECM remodeling in the stasis mouse model of venous thrombosis (VT). Samples from the inferior vena cava (IVC) were collected on days 4, 14, and 21 and analyzed via Western blot for laminin, fibronectin, fibrin, CD31, thrombomodulin, GPVI, α3 integrin/CD49c, α6 integrin/CD49f, β1 integrin/CD29, β4 integrin/CD104, and β6 integrin/ITGB6. An additional early time-course group, sampled on days 2, 4, and 8, was used to verify laminin expression by Western blot. These experiments highlighted the laminin-α6β1 integrin pathway as a possible therapeutic target. Part II tested PLK as an α6β1 integrin inhibitor using venous endothelial venous cells and the in vivo IVC stasis thrombosis model. Outcomes studied included thrombus characterization, vein wall changes by histology, integrin protein expression using immunofluorescence, and immunohistochemistry analysis of endothelial, inflammatory, macrophage, and fibroblast cell populations. The experimental workflow is illustrated in Fig 1. Candidate compounds targeting the α6β1 integrin were selected based on previous evidence of CD49f antagonism in cancer models, using molecular docking and virtual screening.15 PLK was chosen because it is a safe, U.S. Food and Drug Administration-approved drug with predicted binding affinity for integrins.

Ethical considerations

All animal experiments were conducted following University of Michigan Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee policies and U.S. Government Principles for the Use and Care of Vertebrate Animals in Testing, Research, and Training. The study is reported in accordance with Animal Research: Reporting of In Vivo Experiments guidelines.17

Animal surgical procedures

Adult male C57BL/6 mice (The Jackson Laboratory), aged 8 to 12 weeks, were used for all in vivo experiments. The total IVC ligation (stasis) model was performed as described earlier.18,19 In brief, mice were anesthetized with 2.5% isoflurane, and eye lubricant was applied to prevent ocular damage. After abdominal disinfection and sterile preparation, a midline laparotomy was performed. The infrarenal IVC was carefully dissected away from the aorta, and posterior venous branches were cauterized using low-temperature, fine-tip Bovie cautery. Side branches were ligated using 7-0 DemeLENE suture, followed by complete ligation of the IVC. The abdominal muscle layers were closed with continuous sutures, skin was closed with wound clips, and animals were monitored in a recovery cage with heat support until fully recovered.

Western blotting

Previous poststasis mice IVC were dissected, separating them from the aorta and perivascular tissue under microscopic visualization, and harvested at 4, 14, and 21 days (n = 3-5), snap frozen, and lysed in radioimmunoprecipitation assay buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, PI89900) containing protease and phosphatase inhibitors, as previously described in a diluting concentration of 60,000 μg/g of sample.20,21 In brief, frozen samples were processed using a tissue bullet blender homogenizer (BBX24, Next Advance Lab) in low-temperature room settings at 4°C, and store at −80°C after processing. Protein was quantified using the bicinchoninic acid Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 23227). Equal amounts of protein were loaded into a 10% Tris-Glycine gel. Membranes were blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin in Tween-Tris Saline before antibody incubation. We used β-actin (1/2000, Santa Cruz sc-47778), Laminin (1/1000, Abcam ab11575), 59d8 Fibrin (1/1000, donated by Dr Charles Esmon), platelet endothelial cell adhesion molecule-1 (PECAM-1)/CD31 (1/1000, Abcam ab28364), thrombomodulin (1/1000, Abcam ab187075), fibronectin (1/24, Abcam ab2413), GPVI (1/1000, Invitrogen PA5-20582), α3 integrin/CD49c (1/1000, Abcam ab190731), α6 integrin/CD49f [1/1000, Cell Signaling Technology (CST) 3750S], β1 integrin/CD29 (1/1000, CST 4706S), β4 integrin/CD104 (1/1000, Abcam ab182120), and β6 integrin/ITGB6 (1/1000, GeneTex GTX118323) primary antibodies to characterize the ECM components in the post-thrombotic vein. Anti-rabbit secondary antibodies conjugated with horseradish peroxidase, were used at a 1:2000 dilution. We further characterized Laminin expression in a second set of post-thrombotic animals at 2, 4, and 8 days post-IVC stasis in mice. Chemiluminescent detection was used for protein quantification and imaging (Bio-Rad, ChemiDoc Imaging System). Sample densitometry normalizing to β-Actin was performed in Image Lab (Bio-Rad, version 6.1.0).

Drug administration protocol

PLK was dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) and diluted in normal saline for intraperitoneal injection. Mice were randomized to two groups: vehicle control (DMSO) and PLK (1 mg/kg). Pretreatment was administered 1 day before surgery, followed by twice-daily injections throughout the study. Mice were euthanized at 2 or 8 days poststasis (n = 3-5/group) for tissue harvest. Under microscope visualization, the IVC, thrombus, and aorta were harvested en bloc at the level of the IVC ligature (superior boundary) and iliac veins (inferior boundary).

Histology and immunohistochemistry

For histological analysis, tissues were formalin-fixed for 8 hours, paraffin-embedded, and sectioned. Slides were obtained from the midsection of the thrombosed IVC, avoiding oblique cuts resulting in an elliptical shape. Hematoxylin and eosin and Masson trichrome staining were performed at the Orthopaedic Research Laboratories Histology Core. Slides were imaged using a brightfield microscope (Nikon Eclipse E400). In DMSO- or PLK-treated mice, we harvested IVC at 2 and 8 days. Slides were deparaffinized and rehydrated, then subjected to Tris-ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid antigen retrieval (Abcam, ab93684), and blocked with normal serum (CST, 5425). Primary antibodies for endothelial cells PECAM-1/CD31 (1:4000, Abcam ab28364), macrophage CCR2 (1:200, Novus Biologicals NBP1-67700), CD206 (1:4000, Abcam ab300621), and fibroblast DDR2 (1/500, Abcam ab76967) were incubated overnight at 4°C. A horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibody was applied, followed by 3,3′-diaminobenzidine substrate according to the manufacturer's instructions (Vector Laboratories Inc). The slides were counterstained with hematoxylin (Fisher Chemical, AC411165000), washed, dried, and cover-slipped. Cells were quantified blindly by counting positive cells in eight high-power fields (×1000 magnification) around the IVC wall using a Nikon B400 brightfield microscope.22,23 Masson’s trichrome staining was used to measure vein wall thickness (VWT). Images were captured at ×100 magnification with a Nikon microscope and analyzed blindly using ImageJ (National Institutes of Health, version 1.54 g). Eight equidistant radial measurements along the vessel circumference were taken from the lumen to the adventitia of the vessel (morphological appearance as blue from the IVC collagen) and then averaged.22,24

Immunofluorescence

In the 8-day DMSO- or PLK-treated group, slides were deparaffinized and rehydrated, then subjected to Tris-ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid antigen retrieval (Abcam, ab93684), and blocked with normal serum (CST, 5425). Primary antibodies CD31 555-conjugated (CST, 34624) in a 1:100 dilution, DDR2 (Abcam, ab76967) in a 1:100 dilution, integrin α6 (Abcam, ab181551), integrin β1 (Abcam, ab179471), labeled for 488-, 594-, and 750-nm channels (Proteintech, FlexAble 2.0 CoraLite Plus) were incubated overnight at 4°C. After washing with phosphate-buffered saline (3 × 5 minutes), slides were mounted using Prolong Gold with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (Thermo Fisher Scientific, P36931) and coverslipped (Electron Microscopy Sciences, Micro Cover glass 72204-04). Images were acquired and processed using an automated fluorescence microscope (Keyence, BZ-X1000) from the Jobst Laboratories at the University of Michigan. Cells were quantified blindly by counting 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole-positive cells on ×20 OME.TIFF stitched images in QuPath (Queen's University Belfast, 0.7.0-arm64).

Thrombus characterization

Thrombus burden was assessed precisely by measuring thrombus weight on a high-precision scale and length via microscope visualization at harvest.25,26 Weight-to-length ratios were calculated to normalize for variation in thrombus size.27

Cell culture and in vitro experiments

Immortalized murine venous endothelial cells (American Type Culture Collection, CRL-2586) were cultured under standard conditions (37°C, 5% CO2) in complete growth medium. Cells were treated with PLK at 50 or 100 μM, or with vehicle control (DMSO) for 24 hours. For transcription analysis, total ribonucleic acid was extracted using standard protocols, and quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction was performed for Itga6 and Itgb1 using validated primers.

Statistical analysis

Comparisons between treated and untreated groups were performed using unpaired t-test, Mann-Whitney U-test, or Brown-Forsythe analysis of variance as appropriate based on data distribution. A P value <.05 was considered statistically significant. Data were reported as means ± standard deviation. All analyses were performed using Prism software (GraphPad, version 11.1.0). Scientific illustrations were designed using BioRender.com.

Results

Part I. In vivo ECM remodeling in the stasis mouse model of VT

Laminin and α6β1 integrin are prominent components of the thrombus matrix

We first characterized the ECM composition in our stasis VT mouse model. Laminin subunit gamma-3 (LAMC3) was identified via Western blotting as a prominent protein in the IVC and thrombus, with persistently high concentrations detected between 4 and 21 days. Other matrix components quantified included fibrin, PECAM-1, thrombomodulin, fibronectin, and GPVI (Fig 2, A and B). The presence of laminin at additional time points (2, 4, and 8 days) in a different set of animals with stasis VT confirmed these findings. Western blotting of the post-thrombotic vein wall confirmed the presence of α6β1 integrin and showed high overexpression up to day 21 (Fig 2, C). Other integrins, such as α3, β3, and β6, were not overexpressed when compared with β-actin (Fig 2, D). Trichrome staining of the IVC wall demonstrated that the increase in these ECM components is proportional to progressive fibrosis of the post-thrombotic vein over time (Fig 2, E).24 These findings support targeting α6β1 integrin as a therapeutic strategy for DVT and PTS.

Part II. Integrin α6β1 expression is increased following IVC thrombosis and reduced by PLK treatment

Immunofluorescence analysis demonstrated α6β1 integrin expression in the vein wall and thrombus following IVC stasis from days 4 to 21 (Fig 3, A). These findings demonstrate dynamic temporal regulation of α6β1 integrin following DVT, with prominent expression within the thrombus and peak expression during the subacute phase around day 8. This expression profile supports day 8 as a relevant time point for evaluating pharmacologic modulation of the α6β1 integrin pathway. In venous endothelial cells, PLK treatment significantly reduced gene expression of Itga6 (∼30%; P = .0027), Itgb1 (∼20%; P = .0101), and the inflammatory marker sele (∼30%; P = .0032) compared with controls (Fig 3, B). Consistent with these transcriptional findings, in mice treated with PLK α6 integrin-positive cells were reduced in the vein wall (PLK, 680.8 ± 621.6; control, 1854 ± 895.4; P = .0315) and thrombus (PLK, 1874 ± 1591; control, 5279 ± 2376; P = .0204; Fig 3, C and D) compared with control. Similarly, β1 integrin-positive cells were reduced in the vein wall (PLK, 308.0 ± 410.4; control, 1596 ± 1078; P = .0323) and thrombus (PLK, 2028 ± 752.8; control, 6046 ± 3643; P = .0421; Fig 3, C and D).

PLK does not affect thrombus formation or resolution in vivo

To evaluate whether integrin inhibition affects thrombosis, we assessed thrombus characteristics in PLK-treated mice. At day 2 postligation, thrombus weight, length, and weight-to-length ratios were comparable across all treatment groups. PLK mean clot weight/length of 0.02323 (g/cm) did not increase thrombus formation compared with the vehicle control 0.02410 (g/cm; P = .5558). At 8 days, the mean clot weight/length (P = .747) and additional thrombi measurements were also nonsignificant between PLK and the control group (Fig 3, D). These findings indicate that PLK’s effects are not mediated through direct anticoagulant or thrombolytic mechanisms.

PLK reduces vein wall thickness following DVT and modulates macrophage phenotype in the thrombus and vein wall

VWT was quantified as a measure of post-thrombotic fibrotic remodeling in Masson's trichrome staining. After 2 days, no significant differences in VWT were observed between groups. However, by day 8, mice treated with high-dose PLK (1 mg/kg) showed a significant 26.45% reduction in VWT. The VWT in the PLK-treated group was 44.95 ±5.09 μm and in the vehicle (DMSO) control group, 56.84 ±6.54 μm (P = .0081; Fig 4, A), consistent with attenuated fibrotic injury.

Immunohistochemical analysis of endothelial cell (CD31) luminal coverage showed no significant difference between PLK (0.78 ± 0.09) and control (0.80 ± 0.08; Fig 4, B). Conversely, macrophage markers revealed altered expression patterns in PLK-treated mice. CCR2+ expression, associated with proinflammatory monocyte/macrophages, was reduced in the PLK group (14.73 ± 1.69), compared with the control group (23.15 ± 2.38; P < .0001). Conversely, CD206, a marker of anti-inflammatory monocyte/macrophages, showed a nonsignificant increase in expression (PLK, 0.02 ± 0.003; control, 0.02 ± 0.002; P = .1164). DDR2, a tyrosine kinase receptor primarily expressed in fibroblasts involved in fibrotic remodeling, was not affected by PLK treatment (PLK, 19.75 ± 4.89; control, 19.75 ± 4.30; P = .8141; Fig 4, B). The significant reduction of both integrin subunits following PLK treatment, in the absence of a significant reduction in CD31-positive cells, supports an on-target modulation of α6β1 integrin expression rather than a generalized loss of endothelial cells in the post-thrombotic IVC.

Discussion

The α6β1 integrin serves as one of the principal receptors for vascular laminins and has been implicated in platelet adhesion, activation, and arterial thrombosis.9 This study demonstrates that α6β1 integrin is expressed in post-thrombotic veins and that targeting this integrin with PLK reduces vein-wall fibrotic injury following experimental DVT without compromising thrombus formation or resolution (Fig 5). This pharmacological strategy, using a U.S. Food and Drug Administration-approved drug, limits vein wall fibrosis after DVT while avoiding the neonatal lethality because of excessive hemostatic risk associated with complete genetic α6-integrin deletion,10 thereby enhancing translational relevance.

Other cysteinyl leukotriene receptor antagonists, such as montelukast, have also been shown in retrospective U.S. Department of Veterans Affairs cohorts to reduce venous thromboembolism risk by 15.9% (hazard ratio, 0.841) among patients with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, suggesting this class of drugs for venous thromboembolism prophylaxis.28 PLK identification as an α6β1 integrin antagonist through in silico screening and validation in breast cancer models15 provided the rationale for its repurposing in venous disease.

The dissociation between thrombus characteristics and vein-wall remodeling observed in our study is clinically significant. Our findings suggest that PLK acts on the vein wall rather than the thrombus itself, potentially offering an adjunctive therapy. We also confirmed how integrin signaling can influence macrophage polarization,22,23 reducing proinflammatory CCR2+ and increasing CD206+ expression. We believe that PLK may create a microenvironment more favorable to tissue repair with reduced inflammation and less fibrosis. This may be associated with the previously demonstrated PLK nuclear factor kappa-light-chain-enhancer of activated B cells inhibitory effects, leading to a reduction in IL-6.29 Monocyte-derived IL-6 can promote thrombus resolution through MMP9-dependent proteolysis and neovascularization, whereas sustained pathological IL-6 signaling leads to late vein-wall fibrotic remodeling.22,23

Limitations

Our study has several limitations. First, the experiments presented were conducted in male mice, in which thrombus formation differs from that in humans. In the preclinical stasis model, a short follow-up period (8 days) captures subacute remodeling but may not fully recapitulate chronic PTS. This is the rationale for administering PLK before thrombus formation. For clinical relevance, it is necessary to demonstrate that this agent can be effective when administered after DVT has developed, because in practice, it would not be given before DVT but only after diagnosis. Future studies should include longer time points (14 and 21 days) to better mirror chronic human DVT, aiming to better characterize therapeutic rather than prophylactic strategies.30

Second, although we observe reduced transcription and protein expression of Itga6 and Itgb1, the mechanism of PLK’s effects on additional integrin expression, altered epigenetic processes,31 and other PLK-mediated downstream changes32 remains to be determined and warrants further investigation using multiomics approaches, including transcriptomic, proteomic, epigenomic, and spatial profiling. To extend these mechanistic observations to specific cell populations, future studies should focus on a knock-out model specific for endothelial, fibroblast, and platelet integrin expression. Additional ECM changes during DVT that were not assessed in this study include the activity of MMPs4,5 and tissue inhibitors of metalloproteinases that can modulate angiogenesis, relaxation of the IVC, and late structural changes in the blood vessel architecture.33 Further evaluation of mechanotransduction pathways associated with focal adhesion kinase (FAK) and activation of the SMAD2/4-YAP/TAZ pathway34 should be explored, as they offer new insights into the link between inflammatory ECM remodeling and fibrosis via integrin activation during DVT.35 These pathways could present new therapeutic targets and should be investigated further in future studies.

Conclusions

Our study characterizes the main ECM components in a preclinical DVT model and identifies PLK as a promising therapeutic candidate for reducing post-thrombotic vein wall fibrosis. Targeting α6β1 integrin decreased VWT and lowered proinflammatory markers without impacting thrombogenesis. This approach offers a focused strategy to address the unmet need in post-DVT management. Further development of integrin-targeted therapies may provide new options for patients at risk for this debilitating complication of VT.
