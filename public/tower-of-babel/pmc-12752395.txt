Substance use disorder prevalence in Sierra Leone: a sex-disaggregated analysis using the World Health Organization equity data
Authors: Augustus Osborne
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSubstance use disorders (SUDs) pose notable public health challenge worldwide, with disparities often observed across demographic groups. Introduction

Substance use disorders (SUDs) are of global public health concern, contributing substantially to the burden of disease, disability, and mortality [1]. SUDs encompass a range of conditions related to the harmful use of psychoactive substances, including alcohol and drugs, and are often associated with a myriad of adverse health, social, and economic outcomes [2]. Globally, an estimated 35 million people suffer from SUDs, with only a small fraction receiving adequate treatment or care [3]. The consequences of SUDs extend beyond individual health, affecting families, communities, and national economies [3]. In low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) like Sierra Leone, the challenges posed by SUDs are exacerbated by limited healthcare resources, stigma, and a lack of comprehensive prevention and treatment programs [4]. Understanding the prevalence and distribution of SUDs in Sierra Leone, particularly through a sex-disaggregated lens, is critical for designing targeted interventions and addressing health inequities. Despite the growing recognition of the importance of sex-based analyses in global health, there is limited evidence on how SUDs affect males and females differently in Sierra Leone, leaving a critical gap in the literature. Given the gross lack of country-specific data on substance use and substance use disorders in Sierra Leone and much of West Africa, this study leverages global data sources such as the World Health Organization (WHO) reports and the Health Equity Assessment Toolkit (HEAT). These resources provide critical epidemiological insights and help address knowledge gaps where local surveillance systems are limited. By utilizing these databases, the study aims to inform evidence-based interventions and policies in settings where reliable local data are not yet available.

Previous studies on SUDs have primarily focused on high-income countries, where robust surveillance systems and healthcare infrastructure enable the collection of reliable data [5]. These studies have highlighted significant sex-based differences in SUD prevalence, with males consistently exhibiting higher rates of substance use and related disorders compared to females [6, 7]. However, the situation in LMICs, including Sierra Leone, remains poorly understood due to a lack of reliable, up-to-date, and comparable data. A few regional studies in sub-Saharan Africa have suggested that cultural norms, gender roles, and socioeconomic factors influence substance use patterns, leading to disparities in prevalence and access to care [8–10]. For example, societal expectations may discourage substance use among females, resulting in underreporting and limited recognition of their needs in public health strategies [11, 12]. While these studies provide valuable insights, they are often limited in scope, methodology, or geographic coverage, making it difficult to draw comprehensive conclusions about the burden of SUDs in specific contexts like Sierra Leone. Moreover, there are no existing data on SUDs in Sierra Leone which are needed to inform effective policies and interventions.

This study addresses the critical gap in the literature by providing a comprehensive analysis of SUD prevalence in Sierra Leone using data from the WHO equity data repository. By leveraging the WHO Health Equity Assessment Toolkit (HEAT), this study offers reliable, standardized, and geographically complete estimates of SUD prevalence, disaggregated by sex, over a two-decade period (2000–2019). This focus is particularly important given the limited data on alcohol and drug use among females in the region. While it is well established that women often have less access to treatment and care for SUDs, there remains a need to better understand the factors contributing to these disparities. Insights from this study can inform the development of gender-sensitive interventions and national alcohol policies, which are currently under consideration in several West African countries.

Methods

Study design and data source

This study utilized a cross-sectional design to examine the prevalence of substance use disorders in Sierra Leone over a two-decade period (2000–2019), with a specific focus on sex-disaggregated trends and inequalities. All data were obtained from the World Health Organization Equity Data repository, a comprehensive global database that provides standardized, high-quality health-related data for monitoring health inequities across countries. The WHO equity data repository integrates data from multiple sources, including national surveys, censuses, and administrative records, and applies rigorous methodologies to ensure comparability across time and populations.

The WHO repository consolidates standardized health estimates using inputs from national household surveys (e.g., DHS, MICS), censuses, administrative records, and globally harmonized analytical frameworks (including alignment with WHO Global Health Estimates and GBD-consistent methods where applicable). Estimates are age-standardized to a standard population to support comparability across time and settings. WHO applies quality assurance, metadata documentation, and harmonization procedures before making indicators available in HEAT. While this harmonization enhances comparability, estimates may still reflect differences in source data completeness and uncertainty across countries and years; I therefore report 95% uncertainty intervals and acknowledge remaining limitations.

The dataset used in this study included age-standardized prevalence estimates of substance use disorders (per 100,000 population) for males and females in Sierra Leone. These estimates were derived using WHO-defined methods for calculating age-standardized rates, which adjust for differences in age structure between populations and over time. The data were stratified by sex to enable an in-depth analysis of sex-based disparities in substance use disorder prevalence. The study period, 2000–2019, was selected to capture long-term trends and provide insights into potential changes in prevalence and inequality over time.

Outcome measure and dimension of inequality

The primary outcome measure in this study was the age-standardized prevalence of substance use disorders, expressed as the number of cases per 100,000 population. Substance use disorders were defined per the WHO International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision (ICD-10), encompassing mental and behavioural disorders due to psychoactive substance use (including alcohol and drug use disorders) as aggregated in WHO standardized estimates [13]. The prevalence indicators are model-based, age-standardized rates per 100,000 population, aligned with ICD-10 diagnostic groupings. The prevalence estimates were analyzed separately for males and females to examine sex-based differences in the burden of substance use disorders.

The dimension of inequality assessed in this study was sex, a key demographic variable that is critical for understanding disparities in health outcomes. Inequality measures were calculated to quantify the magnitude and persistence of sex-based differences in substance use disorder prevalence. Specifically, the absolute difference (D) and prevalence ratio (R) were used to compare the burden of substance use disorders between males and females. Additionally, the population attributable fraction (PAF) and population attributable risk (PAR) were calculated to assess the population-level impact of sex-based differences on the overall burden of substance use disorders.

Data analysis

The analysis for this study was conducted using the WHO Health Equity Assessment Toolkit (HEAT), which provides access to standardized, pre-computed estimates and inequality metrics derived from the WHO Equity Data Repository. For Sierra Leone (2000–2019), I selected the sex dimension and extracted: (1) age-standardized SUD prevalence (per 100,000) for females and males; and (2) inequality measures (absolute difference, prevalence ratio, population attributable fraction, population attributable risk) automatically calculated by HEAT. I performed no independent statistical computations outside HEAT to preserve alignment with WHO standardization. My role comprised specifying the analytic scope, extracting and verifying outputs, organizing and visualizing results, and interpreting trends and disparities.

For this study, the HEAT tool was used to extract sex-disaggregated prevalence estimates of substance use disorders (per 100,000 population) in Sierra Leone for the period 2000–2019. The tool automatically calculates inequality measures, including absolute differences and prevalence ratios, to quantify disparities between males and females. Additionally, the HEAT tool computes population-level impact measures, such as the population attributable fraction and population attributable risk, to assess the contribution of sex-based disparities to the overall burden of substance use disorders.

The downloaded results from the HEAT tool included age-standardized prevalence estimates for males and females, as well as all inequality measures. These results were presented in tabular and graphical formats, enabling a clear visualization of trends and disparities over time. Since the HEAT tool performs all statistical analyses and ensures the accuracy and comparability of results, no independent statistical analysis was conducted for this study. The results were directly interpreted and reported as provided by the HEAT tool.

Results

Prevalence of substance use disorders in Sierra Leone

Figure 1 illustrates the age-standardized prevalence of substance use disorders (per 100,000 population) in Sierra Leone from 2000 to 2019. The prevalence remained relatively stable between 2000 and 2015, fluctuating slightly around 821.6 to 824.5 per 100,000 population. However, a notable increase is observed between 2015 and 2019, with the prevalence rising sharply to 845.1 per 100,000 population in 2019. This upward trend in the latter years suggests a potential escalation in the burden of substance use disorders, warranting further investigation into contributing factors and targeted public health interventions.

The age-standardized prevalence of substance use disorders in Sierra Leone between 2000 and 2019 is presented in Table 1. Overall, males consistently exhibited higher prevalence rates compared to females across all years studied. In 2019, the prevalence among males was 975.6 per 100,000 population (95% CI: 844.2–1112.6), whereas for females, the prevalence was 718.3 per 100,000 population (95% CI: 612.9–848.5). This disparity persisted throughout the study period, with males maintaining a prevalence approximately 250 per 100,000 population higher than females.

The trend analysis shows that the prevalence of substance uses disorders remained relatively stable over the two decades, with only minor fluctuations. Among females, the prevalence ranged from 710.0 per 100,000 in 2000 (95% CI: 602.0–834.5) to 718.3 per 100,000 in 2019 (95% CI: 612.9–848.5). Similarly, among males, the prevalence ranged from 952.4 per 100,000 in 2000 (95% CI: 821.6–1085.0) to 975.6 per 100,000 in 2019 (95% CI: 844.2–1112.6).

Although males consistently exhibited higher prevalence rates of SUDs compared to females over the two-decade period, the overall prevalence remained relatively stable, with an increase noted in more recent years. Notably, the calculated inequality measures, including Population Attributable Fraction (PAF) and Population Attributable Risk (PAR), indicated that gender did not substantially affect the overall population-level burden of SUDs.

The absolute difference in prevalence between males and females was substantial and remained relatively stable, ranging from − 234.7 in 2005 to -257.3 in 2019. These values should be interpreted cautiously, recognizing that PAF/PAR for a non-modifiable demographic attribute (sex) may be less informative for burden reduction than absolute and relative disparity metrics.

Discussion

This study provides a comprehensive analysis of the prevalence of SUDs in Sierra Leone between 2000 and 2019, highlighting sex-based disparities using data from the WHO Equity Data repository. The findings reveal a relatively stable age-standardized prevalence of SUDs over the two decades, fluctuating slightly between 821.6 and 824.5 per 100,000 population from 2000 to 2015. However, a notable increase was observed between 2015 and 2019, with the prevalence rising to 845.1 per 100,000 population in 2019. This upward trend suggests an escalating burden of SUDs in recent years, potentially reflecting changes in substance availability, socioeconomic conditions, or healthcare access. Importantly, the study also highlights persistent sex-based disparities, with males consistently exhibiting higher prevalence rates than females across all years. In 2019, the prevalence among males was 975.6 per 100,000 population compared to 718.3 per 100,000 population among females, a disparity that has remained stable over time. Despite these differences, inequality measures such as population attributable fraction and population attributable risk indicate that sex-based differences did not significantly alter the overall burden of SUDs in the population.

By leveraging WHO’s standardized, age-adjusted estimates via HEAT, the analysis benefits from harmonized definitions, comparability over time, and publicly documented methods. However, as with all model-based estimates, outputs are contingent on the completeness and quality of underlying data sources in Sierra Leone, the assumptions in global estimation frameworks, and the uncertainty reflected in 95% intervals. It therefore emphasizes absolute and relative disparity metrics (D and R) for equity interpretation and treats PAF/PAR estimates with caution.

The findings of this study align with global and regional research identifying SUDs as a significant public health challenge, particularly among males. Previous studies have consistently demonstrated higher prevalence rates of substance use and related disorders among males compared to females [6, 7]. This disparity has been attributed to a combination of biological, social, and cultural factors. For instance, males are more likely to engage in risk-taking behaviours, including substance use, and may face fewer societal restrictions compared to females [14]. In sub-Saharan Africa, cultural norms often discourage substance use among females, leading to underreporting and limited recognition of their needs in public health strategies [15]. The consistent prevalence ratio of approximately 0.7 observed in this study mirrors findings from other LMICs, where males are 1.3 to 1.4 times more likely to experience SUDs than females [16, 17]. However, unlike some studies that report a narrowing of sex-based disparities over time [18, 19], the gap in Sierra Leone has remained stable, suggesting that gender-specific factors influencing substance use patterns have persisted in this context.

The observed increase in SUD prevalence from 2015 to 2019 is concerning and warrants further investigation. Similar trends have been reported in other LMICs, where economic challenges, urbanization, and increased availability of substances have contributed to rising prevalence rates [20]. In Sierra Leone, the aftermath of the civil war (1991–2002) and the Ebola epidemic (2014–2016) may have exacerbated socioeconomic vulnerabilities, creating conditions conducive to substance use [21]. Additionally, limited access to mental health and substance use treatment services may have contributed to the observed increase in prevalence [4]. While this study does not explore the specific drivers of this trend, it underscores the need for further research to identify and address the underlying factors.

The observation that gender did not significantly influence the burden of SUDs, as reflected by the PAF and PAR values, suggests that while prevalence rates differ by sex, the impact on population-level burden remains limited. This finding may be attributable to underlying social, cultural, or health system factors, and underscores the need for further investigation into the drivers of these patterns. Understanding these dynamics will be critical for designing effective, equitable interventions and policies.

Public health implications

The findings of this study have important implications for public health policy and practice in Sierra Leone. The persistent sex-based disparities in SUD prevalence highlight the need for targeted interventions that address the specific needs of males and females. For males, prevention efforts should focus on reducing substance availability, promoting healthy behaviours, and addressing social norms that encourage substance use. For females, interventions should aim to overcome barriers to access and reduce stigma associated with substance use and treatment. Importantly, the stable prevalence rates observed among females suggest that their burden of SUDs has been underrecognized in public health strategies, necessitating greater attention to their needs.

The increase in SUD prevalence in recent years underscores the urgency of strengthening prevention and treatment programs in Sierra Leone. Public health efforts should prioritize early identification and intervention for individuals at risk of SUDs, particularly in underserved and vulnerable populations. Expanding access to evidence-based treatment services, including counselling, medication-assisted therapy, and rehabilitation programs, is critical for reducing the burden of SUDs. Furthermore, addressing the social determinants of health, such as poverty, unemployment, and lack of education, is essential for preventing substance use and promoting recovery.

The findings also emphasize the importance of incorporating sex-disaggregated data into public health surveillance and research. By examining sex-based differences in SUD prevalence and outcomes, policymakers can design more equitable and effective interventions. The use of inequality measures, such as absolute differences and prevalence ratios, provides valuable insights into the magnitude and consistency of disparities, enabling targeted resource allocation and program planning.

Strengths and limitations

This study has several strengths. It provides a comprehensive analysis of SUD prevalence in Sierra Leone over a two-decade period, utilizing standardized and reliable data from the WHO Equity Data repository. The use of sex-disaggregated data and inequality measures offers valuable insights into the burden of SUDs and the disparities between males and females. Additionally, the study contributes to the limited body of literature on SUDs in Sierra Leone, addressing a critical gap in knowledge and providing a foundation for future research and policy development.

However, the study also has limitations. First, the reliance on secondary data from the WHO HEAT may limit the ability to explore the underlying drivers of the observed trends and disparities. For example, data on specific substances, socioeconomic factors, and healthcare access were not available, preventing a more nuanced analysis. Second, the use of age-standardized prevalence estimates may obscure variations in SUD prevalence across different age groups, which could provide important insights for targeted interventions. Because the WHO repository compiles and harmonizes data from multiple sources, residual heterogeneity in source data completeness and quality may influence national estimates. Although WHO standardization promotes comparability, cross-country differences in inputs and uncertainty remain. Furthermore, HEAT computes inequality measures for non-modifiable demographic categories (e.g., sex), which may yield PAF/PAR values of limited interpretability for burden reduction planning; hence, the study prioritize absolute and relative gaps for equity assessment.

Future research directions

Future research should aim to address the limitations of this study by incorporating primary data collection and exploring the specific factors driving trends and disparities in SUD prevalence. Longitudinal studies that examine the impact of socioeconomic, cultural, and policy changes on substance use patterns would provide valuable insights for prevention and treatment efforts. Additionally, research on the availability, accessibility, and effectiveness of substance use treatment services in Sierra Leone is needed to inform program planning and resource allocation. Finally, studies that examine the intersection of substance use with other health and social issues, such as mental health, gender-based violence, and poverty, would contribute to a more comprehensive understanding of the burden of SUDs and the strategies needed to address it.

Conclusion

This study highlights the stable yet concerning burden of SUDs in Sierra Leone over two decades, with a notable increase in prevalence from 2015 to 2019. The persistent sex-based disparities underscore the need for targeted interventions that address the specific needs of males and females. The findings highlight the urgent need for further research on substance use among women, as well as the barriers they face in accessing care. Addressing these gaps is essential for informing equitable public health strategies and supporting ongoing policy development across West Africa.
