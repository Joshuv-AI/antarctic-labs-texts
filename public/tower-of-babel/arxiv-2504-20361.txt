Hydrostatic equilibrium in multi-Weyl semimetals
Jewel Kumar Ghosh, Francisco Peña-Benítez, and Patricio Salgado-Rebolledo

Abstract
We study the hydrostatic equilibrium of multi-Weyl semimetals, a class of systems with Weyl-like quasi-particles but anisotropic dispersion relation ω 2 ∼ k ∥ 2 + k ⟂ 2 ​ n \omega^{2}\sim k_{\parallel}^{2}+k_{\perp}^{2n} , with n n a possitive integer. A characteristic feature of multi-Weyl systems is the lack of Lorentz invariance, instead, they possess the reduced spacetime symmetry ( S ​ O ​ ( 1 , 1 ) × S ​ O ​ ( 2 ) ) ⋉ ℝ 4 (SO(1,1)\times SO(2))\ltimes\mathbb{R}^{4} . In this work we propose a covariant formulation for the low energy theory, allowing for a minimal coupling of the fermion field to external geometric background and U ⁡ ( 1 ) U(1) gauge field. The non-Lorentzian structure of the field theory demands introducing an Aristotelian spacetime analogous to the so-called stringy Newton-Cartan geometry [ 1 ] . Our proposal allows for a systematic study of the hydrostatic properties via the derivation of the partition function of the system. In addition to multi-Weyl models, our formulation can be applied to systems with similar spacetime symmetry groups, such as Bjorken flow.

4
Multi-Weyl particles in curved spacetime

The computation of correlation functions of conserved charges in quantum field theory can be accessed via the so-called generating functional or effective action of the system. Such a functional is obtained after integrating out the matter content that has been minimally coupled to external background fields. In particular, the expectation value of conserved currents associated with spacetime symmetries is obtained after coupling the system to a curved background. Generically, Lorentzian fields are coupled to pseudo-Riemannian geometries, Galilean particles to Newton-Cartan spaces, whereas theories lacking boost invariance need to be coupled to the so-called Aristotelian spacetime

47

39
]

However, the class of systems we are interested in does not fit within these examples, and the appropriate geometry compatible with the reduced boost and rotational symmetry of the problem needs to be constructed. To do so, we will gauge the spacetime and internal symmetry group of the problem under consideration.

\mathcal{F}=d\mathcal{A}+\mathcal{A}\wedge\mathcal{A}=T^{A}P_{A}+\frac{1}{2}\mathcal{F}^{AB}\,M_{AB}+F\,Q

(4.8)

where we have defined curvatures

\mathcal{F}^{AB}=\mathcal{F}^{(S)}\,\epsilon^{AB}+\mathcal{F}^{(L)}\,\varepsilon^{AB}=d\Omega^{AB}\,,\qquad F=dA\,,

(4.9)

and the torsion two-form

T^{A}=De^{A}\,,

(4.10)

with the exterior covariant derivative defined as

D=d+A\,\mathcal{P}[Q]+\frac{1}{2}\Omega^{AB}\,\mathcal{P}[M_{AB}]\,,

(4.11)

with
\mathcal{P}[Q]
and
\mathcal{P}[M_{AB}]
being the corresponding representations for the charge, boost and angular momentum generators. In particular, it acts on vector- and fermion-valued fields as follows

\displaystyle DV^{A}=dV^{A}+\Omega^{A}\,_{B}V^{B}\,,

(4.12a)

\displaystyle D\Psi=\left[d+iA+\frac{1}{2}(\Omega+i\,n\,\omega)\tau^{3}\right]\Psi\,.

(4.12b)

In addition, we introduce inverse vierbeins
E_{A}
, satisfying the orthogonality relation

\displaystyle e^{A}{}_{\mu}E_{B}{}^{\mu}=\delta^{A}_{B},

\displaystyle e^{A}{}_{\mu}E_{A}{}^{\nu}=\delta^{\nu}_{\mu}\,,

(4.13)

and the flat geometry of the previous section is recovered after setting
e^{A}=dx^{A}
,
\Omega^{AB}=A=0
. Since the vierbeins are invertible we can introduce the volume form

dV=e^{0}\wedge e^{1}\wedge e^{2}\wedge e^{3}=|e|d^{4}x\,.

(4.14)

Using
\epsilon_{AB}
and
\varepsilon_{AB}
we can define the two forms

\displaystyle\epsilon=\frac{1}{2}\epsilon_{AB}e^{A}{}\wedge e^{B}{}\,,\qquad\varepsilon=\frac{1}{2}\varepsilon_{AB}e^{A}{}\wedge e^{B}{}\,,

(4.15)

and express the volume form as
dV=\epsilon\wedge\varepsilon
. On the other hand the three dimensional area element reads

(dS)_{A}=\epsilon_{A}\wedge\varepsilon-\varepsilon_{A}\wedge\epsilon\,,

(4.16)

with
\epsilon_{A}=\epsilon_{AB}\,e^{B}
and
\varepsilon_{A}=\varepsilon_{AB}\,e^{B}
.

Notice that the vierbeins and their inverse can be used to trade tensors into Lie algebra valued tensors and viceversa, in particular, using
\eta_{AB}
,
g^{A}_{B}
, and
h^{A}_{B}
we can defined the set of tensors

\displaystyle g^{\mu}_{\nu}=g^{A}_{B}E_{A}\,^{\mu}e^{B}\,_{\nu}\,,\quad h^{\mu}_{\nu}=h^{A}_{B}E_{A}\,^{\mu}e^{B}\,_{\nu}\,,\quad G_{\mu\nu}=e^{A}\,{}_{\mu}e_{A\nu}\,,\quad G^{\mu\nu}=E^{A\mu}E_{A}\,^{\nu},

(4.17)

satisfying

\displaystyle g^{\mu}_{\nu}+h^{\mu}_{\nu}=\delta^{\mu}_{\nu}\,,\quad G^{\mu\beta}G_{\beta\nu}=\delta^{\mu}_{\nu}\,,

(4.18)

similarly to the case of tangent space indices we will use
G^{\mu\nu}
and
G_{\mu\nu}
to raise and lower curved space indices.

4.2
Gauge transformations

The transformation law of the connection under infinitesimal diffeomorphisms and gauge transformations with parameters
\chi=(\xi,\Lambda)
, where

\Lambda=\frac{1}{2}\lambda^{AB}M_{AB}+\lambda^{(Q)}Q

(4.19)

and
\lambda^{AB}=\epsilon^{AB}\lambda^{(S)}+\varepsilon^{AB}\lambda^{(L)}
, reads

\delta_{\chi}\mathcal{A}=\mathfrak{L}_{\xi}\mathcal{A}+\mathcal{D}\Lambda

(4.20)

where
\mathcal{D}=d+[\mathcal{A},\;]
.
Evaluating this transformation for each field, we find

\displaystyle\delta_{\chi}e^{A}=\mathfrak{L}_{\xi}e^{A}-\lambda^{A}\,_{B}\,e^{B}=D\xi^{A}+\bar{\mathcal{G}}^{A}-\bar{\lambda}^{A}\,_{B}e^{B}{}\,,

(4.21a)

\displaystyle\delta_{\chi}\Omega^{A}\,_{B}=\mathfrak{L}_{\xi}\Omega^{A}\,_{B}+d\lambda^{A}\,_{B}=d\bar{\lambda}^{A}\,_{B}+\bar{\mathcal{E}}^{A}\,_{B}\,,

(4.21b)

\displaystyle\delta_{\chi}A=\mathfrak{L}_{\xi}A+d\lambda^{(Q)}=d\bar{\lambda}^{(Q)}+\bar{E}\,,

(4.21c)

where we introduced new parameters

\displaystyle\bar{\lambda}^{AB}

\displaystyle=\lambda^{AB}+\iota_{\xi}\Omega^{AB}\,,\qquad\bar{\lambda}^{(Q)}=\lambda^{(Q)}+\iota_{\xi}A\,,

(4.22)

and the “electric” fields

\displaystyle\bar{\mathcal{G}}^{A}=\iota_{\xi}T^{A}\,,\qquad\bar{\mathcal{E}}^{AB}

\displaystyle=\iota_{\xi}\mathcal{F}^{AB}=\iota_{\xi}\mathcal{F}^{(S)}\,\epsilon^{AB}+\iota_{\xi}\mathcal{F}^{(L)}\,\varepsilon^{AB}\,,\qquad\bar{E}=\iota_{\xi}F\,.

(4.23)

These relations can be easily obtained by applying Cartan’s magic formula
\mathfrak{L}_{\xi}\mathscr{F}=\iota_{\xi}d\mathscr{F}+d\iota_{\xi}\mathscr{F}
, where
\mathscr{F}
is a differential form.

It is convenient to extend the individual Lie algebras of diffeomorphisms and gauge transformations by introducing the bracket

[\chi^{\prime},\chi]=\delta_{\chi^{\prime}}\chi=(\mathfrak{L}_{\xi^{\prime}}\xi,\mathfrak{L}_{\xi^{\prime}}\Lambda-\mathfrak{L}_{\xi}\Lambda^{\prime})\,,

(4.24)

after some tedious derivations it is possible to prove that

[\delta_{\chi^{\prime}},\delta_{\chi}]=\delta_{[\chi^{\prime},\chi]}\,,

(4.25)

and that
\bar{\lambda}^{(S)}
,
\bar{\lambda}^{(L)}
,
\bar{\lambda}^{(Q)}
transform as scalar functions.

4.3
Intrinsic and extrinsic torsion

A spacetime with
SO(1,1)\times SO(2)
structure group defines a generalized Aristotelian geometry
[
49
]
exhibiting a foliation analogous to the string version of Newton-Cartan geometry
[
1
,
50
,
51
]
. In contrast to standard Riemannian geometry, not all components of the torsion depend on the spin connection. This becomes manifest when splitting the torsion two-forms (
4.10
) in the following way

T^{A}=\mathring{T}^{A}+\hat{T}^{A}

(4.26)

where

\mathring{T}^{a}=\frac{1}{2}T^{a}{}_{ij}e^{i}\wedge e^{j}+T^{(ab)}{}{}_{i}e_{b}\wedge e^{i},\qquad\mathring{T}^{i}=\frac{1}{2}T^{i}{}_{ab}e^{a}\wedge e^{b}+T^{(ij)}{}{}_{a}e_{j}\wedge e^{a},

(4.27)

and

\hat{T}^{a}=\frac{1}{2}T^{a}{}_{bc}e^{b}\wedge e^{c}+T^{[ab]}{}{}_{i}e_{b}\wedge e^{i},\qquad\hat{T}^{i}=\frac{1}{2}T^{i}{}_{jk}e^{i}\wedge e^{j}+T^{[ij]}{}{}_{a}e_{j}\wedge e^{a}\,.

(4.28)

From now on, without a loss of generality, we assume that
g^{A}_{B}=\mathrm{diag}(1,1,0,0)
and
h^{A}_{B}=\mathrm{diag}(0,0,1,1)
and we introduce indices
a=0,1
and
i=2,3
to distinguish between the longitudinal and transverse components of
SO(1,1)\times SO(2)
tensors
7
7

7

Note that this definition must be adapted when applied to the results of Section
3
, where the indices are split as
a=0,3
and
i=1,2
.
.
The components of
\mathring{T}
can be expressed solely in terms of the non-holonomic coefficients

\Sigma_{AB}{}{}^{C}=-2E_{A}{}^{\mu}E_{B}{}^{\nu}\partial_{[\mu}e^{C}{}_{\nu]},

(4.29)

defined via the Lie brackets of the inverse vierbeins

\displaystyle[E_{A},E_{A}]=\Sigma_{AB}\,^{C}E_{C}\,.

(4.30)

This yields

\mathring{T}^{a}{}_{ij}=-\Sigma_{ij}{}{}^{a}\,,\quad\mathring{T}^{i}{}_{ab}=-\Sigma_{ab}{}{}^{i}\,,\quad\mathring{T}^{(ab)i}=\Sigma^{i(ab)}\,,\quad\mathring{T}^{(ij)a}=\Sigma^{a(ij)}\,.

(4.31)

Consequently, setting the components of
\mathring{T}^{a}
and
\mathring{T}^{i}
to zero does not contribute to expressing any spin connection components in terms of the tetrad and its derivatives. Instead, it imposes constraints on the geometry. To avoid such constraints and preserve the independence of all vierbein components, these torsion components will not be set to vanish and will be referred to as intrinsic torsion. In contrast,
\hat{T}^{A}
depends on the spin connection components, which can be expressed as

\displaystyle\hat{T}^{a}{}_{bc}=-\Sigma_{bc}{}{}^{a}-2\epsilon^{a}{}_{[b}E_{c]}{}^{\mu}\Omega_{\mu}\,,\qquad\hat{T}^{i}{}_{jk}=-\Sigma_{jk}{}{}^{i}-2\varepsilon^{i}{}_{[j}E_{k]}{}^{\mu}\omega_{\mu}\,,

(4.32a)

\displaystyle\hat{T}^{[ab]i}=-\Sigma^{i[ab]}-\epsilon^{ab}\Omega_{\mu}E^{i\mu}\,,\qquad\hat{T}^{[ij]a}=-\Sigma^{a[ij]}-\varepsilon^{ij}\omega_{\mu}E^{a\mu}\,.

(4.32b)

with
\epsilon_{0}\,^{1}=\epsilon_{1}\,^{0}=\varepsilon_{2}\,^{3}=-\varepsilon_{3}\,^{2}=1
. Thus, when
\hat{T}^{A}
vanishes,
\Omega
and
\omega
can be expressed in terms of the vierbein. These components define the extrinsic torsion of the geometry, which may or may not be set to zero depending on whether the spin connections are treated as independent fields.

It is important to notice that the space-time manifold is not a product manifold of the form
\mathcal{M}_{4}=\mathcal{M}_{1,1}\times\mathcal{M}_{2}
, with
\mathcal{M}_{1,1}
and
\mathcal{M}_{2}
pseudo-Riemannian and Riemannian spaces, respectively. If it were, there would be no intrinsic torsion, and the number of independent fields (
\delta^{a}_{\mu}\delta^{b}_{\nu}g_{ab},\delta^{i}_{\mu}\delta^{j}_{\nu}h_{ij}
) would be
6
, not matching with the number of independent (conserved) spacetime-related operators of the theory (
T^{\mu}\,_{\nu}
), which is
10
. The proper way of sourcing all the operators requires a generic four dimensional manifold whose tangent space can be decomposed locally as
T_{x}\mathcal{M}_{4}=\mathbb{R}^{1,1}\times\mathbb{R}^{2}
. This is the curved space-time we consider here. On a manifold with such a structure it is convenient to define a covariant derivative satisfying the property
\nabla_{X}Y_{\parallel}=P_{\parallel}(\nabla_{X}Y)
and
\nabla_{X}Y_{\perp}=P_{\perp}(\nabla_{X}Y)
, with
P_{\parallel}
,
P_{\perp}
the projectors to the Lorentzian and Euclidean slices of the tangent space respectively, i.e.
Y_{\parallel}=P_{\parallel}(Y)
,
Y_{\perp}=P_{\perp}(Y)
. If we recall that torsion is the failure of the connection to match the Lie bracket

\displaystyle T(X,Y)

\displaystyle=\nabla_{X}Y-\nabla_{Y}X+[X,Y]\,,

(4.33)

we can derive the following set of consistency conditions

\displaystyle\mathrm{if}\quad X=P_{\parallel}(X),\quad Y=P_{\parallel}(Y):\qquad P_{\perp}(T(X,Y))

\displaystyle=[X,Y]_{\perp}\,,

(4.34)

\displaystyle\mathrm{if}\quad X=P_{\perp}(X),\quad Y=P_{\perp}(Y):\qquad P_{\parallel}(T(X,Y))

\displaystyle=[X,Y]_{\parallel}\,,

(4.35)

\displaystyle\mathrm{if}\quad X=P_{\parallel}(X),\quad Y=P_{\perp}(Y):\qquad P_{\parallel}(T(X,Y))

\displaystyle=-\nabla_{Y}X+[X,Y]_{\parallel}\,,

(4.36)

\displaystyle P_{\perp}(T(X,Y))

\displaystyle=\nabla_{X}Y+[X,Y]_{\perp}\,.

(4.37)

Using the basis (
4.13
) satisfying,
P_{\parallel}(E_{a})=E_{a}
,
P_{\parallel}(E_{i})=0
,
P_{\perp}(E_{i})=E_{i}
,
P_{\perp}(E_{a})=0
, we conclude that

\displaystyle T_{iab}

\displaystyle=[E_{a},E_{b}]_{i}\,,

(4.38)

\displaystyle T_{aij}

\displaystyle=[E_{i},E_{j}]_{a}\,,

(4.39)

\displaystyle T\,_{(ab)i}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}([E_{b},E_{i}]_{a}+[E_{a},E_{i}]_{b})\,,

(4.40)

\displaystyle T\,_{(ij)a}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}([E_{j},E_{a}]_{i}+[E_{i},E_{a}]_{j})\,.

(4.41)

where, in the last two lines, we have used
\nabla_{X}E_{a}=X^{\mu}\omega_{\mu}\epsilon_{a}\,^{b}E_{b}
and
\nabla_{X}E_{i}=X^{\mu}\Omega_{\mu}\varepsilon_{i}\,^{j}E_{j}
. These are precisely the intrinsic torsion components (
4.27
). Thus, we conclude that, contrary to the Lorentzian/Riemannian case, the only components of the torsion field that we are free to set to zero are the ones given in (
4.28
), namely,

T^{\mu}\,_{\nu\rho}e_{a\mu}E_{b}\,^{\nu}E_{c}\,^{\rho}\,,\qquad T^{\mu}\,_{\nu\rho}e_{[a\mu}E_{b]}\,^{\nu}E_{i}\,^{\rho}\,\qquad T^{\mu}\,_{\nu\rho}e_{i\mu}E_{j}\,^{\nu}E_{k}\,^{\rho}\,,\qquad T^{\mu}\,_{\nu\rho}e_{[i\mu}E_{j]}\,^{\nu}E_{a}\,^{\rho}\,.

(4.42)

Therefore, the Aristotelian geometry possesses a nonzero torsion.

As is common in non-Lorentzian geometry
[
49
,
47
,
52
,
53
]
, the components of the intrinsic torsion can be expressed in terms of the so-called “extrinsic curvatures”

K^{a}{}_{\mu\nu}=-\frac{1}{2}\mathcal{L}_{E^{a}}h_{\mu\nu},\qquad K^{i}{}_{\mu\nu}=-\frac{1}{2}\mathcal{L}_{E^{i}}g_{\mu\nu}\,,

(4.43)

satisfying the following properties

\displaystyle K^{(a}{}_{\mu\nu}E^{b)\nu}=0=K^{(i}{}_{\mu\nu}E^{j)\nu}\,,

(4.44a)

\displaystyle K^{abc}=K^{a}{}_{\mu\nu}E^{b\mu}E^{c\nu}=0\,,

(4.44b)

\displaystyle K^{ijk}=K^{i}{}_{\mu\nu}E^{j\mu}E^{k\nu}=0\,,

(4.44c)

where the first relation implies, in particular, the identities

K^{a}{}_{\mu\nu}E_{a}{}^{\nu}=K^{i}{}_{\mu\nu}E_{i}{}^{\nu}=0\,.

(4.45)

In terms of Eqs. (
4.43
), the intrinsic torsion can be written as

\mathring{T}^{a}=K_{ij}{}{}^{a}e^{i}\wedge e^{j}+K^{iab}e_{b}\wedge e_{i}\,,\qquad\mathring{T}^{i}=K_{ab}{}{}^{i}e^{a}\wedge e^{b}+K^{aij}e_{j}\wedge e_{a}\,.

(4.46)

Using the relations (
4.43
) it is possible to understand
K^{a}{}_{\mu\nu}
and
K^{i}{}_{\mu\nu}
as the projections of the following object

\displaystyle K^{\rho}{}_{\mu\nu}

\displaystyle=E_{a}{}^{\rho}K^{a}{}_{\mu\nu}+E_{i}{}^{\rho}K^{i}{}_{\mu\nu}

(4.47)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}g^{\rho\lambda}\left(2\partial_{(\mu}h_{\nu)\lambda}-\partial_{\lambda}h_{\mu\nu}\right)+\frac{1}{2}h^{\rho\lambda}\left(2\partial_{(\mu}g_{\nu)\lambda}-\partial_{\lambda}g_{\mu\nu}\right)\,.

Note that the identities (
4.45
) imply that
K^{\rho}{}_{\mu\nu}
has a vanishing trace

K^{\rho}{}_{\mu\rho}=0\,.

(4.48)

4.5
Affine connection

It is possible to introduce an affine connection
\gamma^{\rho}{}_{\mu\nu}
by defining a covariant derivative
\nabla_{\mu}
through a vierbein postulate of the form

\nabla_{\mu}e^{A}{}_{\nu}=\partial_{\mu}e^{A}{}_{\nu}+\Omega^{A}{}_{B\mu}\,e^{B}{}_{\nu}-\gamma^{\rho}{}_{\mu\nu}e^{A}{}_{\rho}=0\,.

(4.56)

Such equations completely determine
\gamma^{\rho}{}_{\mu\nu}
, which can be written as

D_{\mu}\chi^{A}=e^{A}{}_{\nu}\nabla_{\mu}\chi^{\nu}\,,\qquad\nabla_{\mu}\chi^{\nu}=E_{A}{}^{\nu}D_{\mu}\chi^{A}\;.

(4.57)

Following (
4.49
), we split the affine connection as

\gamma^{\rho}{}_{\mu\nu}=\mathring{\gamma}^{\rho}{}_{\mu\nu}+\kappa_{\mu\nu}{}{}^{\rho}\,,

(4.58)

where
\mathring{\gamma}^{\rho}{}_{\mu\nu}
is the affine connection associated to
\mathring{\Omega}
and
\mathring{\omega}
, whereas
\kappa_{\mu\nu}{}{}^{\rho}
is the contorsion tensor associated to the contorsion forms
\mathcal{K}
and
\kappa
. This means that the connection
\mathring{\gamma}^{\rho}{}_{\mu\nu}
carries intrinsic torsion and
\kappa_{\mu\nu}{}^{\rho}
is the contorsion tensor associated only with the extrinsic torsion, i.e., the part of the torsion that can be set to zero without imposing constraints on the vierbein. In order to find the explicit form of
\mathring{\gamma}^{\rho}{}_{\mu\nu}
we replace Eqs. (
4.49
) and (
4.58
) in (
4.56
) to find

\mathring{\gamma}^{\rho}{}_{\mu\nu}=E_{A}{}^{\rho}\left(\partial_{\mu}e^{A}{}_{\nu}+\mathring{\Omega}^{A}{}_{B\mu}e^{B}{}_{\nu}\right)\,,\qquad\kappa_{\mu\nu}{}^{\rho}=\mathcal{K}^{A}{}_{B\mu}E_{A}{}^{\rho}e^{B}{}_{\nu}\,.

(4.59)

After replacing the solution for the spin connection (
4.55
) in (
4.59
), the affine connection with vanishing extrinsic torsion can be written as

\displaystyle\mathring{\gamma}^{\rho}{}_{\mu\nu}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}g^{\rho\lambda}\left(\partial_{\mu}g_{\lambda\nu}+\partial_{\nu}g_{\mu\lambda}-\partial_{\lambda}g_{\mu\nu}\right)

(4.60)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}h^{\rho\lambda}\left(\partial_{\mu}h_{\lambda\nu}+\partial_{\nu}h_{\mu\lambda}-\partial_{\lambda}h_{\mu\nu}\right)+\left(g^{\rho\alpha}g_{\nu\lambda}+h^{\rho\alpha}h_{\nu\lambda}\right)K^{\lambda}{}_{\mu\alpha}\,,

where we have used the definition (
4.47
). The fact that
K^{\rho}{}_{\mu\nu}
satisfies the traceless condition (
4.48
) can be used to show that the covariant divergence of a vector
V^{\mu}
with respect to the extrinsic-torsion-free affine connection
\mathring{\gamma}^{\rho}{}_{\mu\nu}
satisfies the usual property

(\mathring{\nabla}_{\mu}-\mathring{T}^{\rho}{}_{\rho\mu})V^{\mu}=\partial_{\mu}V^{\mu}+\mathring{\gamma}^{\rho}{}_{\mu\rho}\,V^{\mu}=\frac{1}{|e|}\partial_{\mu}\left(|e|\;V^{\mu}\right)\,.

(4.61)

which guarantees that the divergence theorem holds in this type of geometry even though
\mathring{\gamma}^{\rho}{}_{\mu\nu}
is not the standard Levi-Civita connection.

5
Effective action and conserved currents

Previous construction facilitates coupling the multi-Weyl fermions to external gauge fields and geometry, the minimally coupled theory has action

S=\int\,\textrm{Re}\,\bar{\phi}\left[i\not{D}_{\parallel}+\,m^{1-n}(i\not{D}_{\perp})^{n}\right]\phi\,dV\,,

(5.1)

where

\not{D}_{\parallel}\phi=E_{a}{}^{\mu}\gamma^{a}D_{\mu}\phi,\qquad\not{D}_{\perp}=E_{i}{}^{\mu}\gamma^{i}D_{\mu}\phi,\qquad\gamma^{A}=(\sigma^{1},-i\sigma^{2},\mathbb{\sigma}^{0},-i\sigma^{3})\,,

(5.2)

and
D_{\mu}\phi
is given in Eq. (
4.12
). The energy-momentum, spin, angular, and U(1) current operators are defined from the variation of the action with respect to the external fields

\delta S=\int_{\mathcal{M}}\left(\delta e^{A}\wedge\star\mathcal{T}_{A}+\delta\Omega\wedge\star\mathcal{S}+\delta\omega\wedge\star\mathcal{J}+\delta A\wedge\star J\right)\,,

(5.3)

in particular the current operators for the system read

\displaystyle\star\mathcal{T}\,_{A}

\displaystyle=-Re\left[i\bar{\phi}\,\star\gamma_{\parallel}D_{A}\phi+m^{1-n}i^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}(-1)^{j-1}\bar{\phi}(\overleftarrow{\not{D}_{\perp}})^{j-1}\star\gamma_{\perp}\,D_{A}\not{D}_{\perp}^{n-j}\phi\right]

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\quad+\mathcal{L}(dS)_{A}\,,

(5.4a)

\displaystyle\star\mathcal{S}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}Re\left[i\bar{\phi}\,\star\gamma_{\parallel}\sigma^{3}\phi+m^{1-n}i^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}(-1)^{j-1}\bar{\phi}(\overleftarrow{\not{D}_{\perp}})^{j-1}\star\gamma_{\perp}\sigma^{3}\not{D}_{\perp}^{n-j}\phi\right]\,,

(5.4b)

\displaystyle\star\mathcal{J}

\displaystyle=-\frac{n}{2}Re\left[\bar{\phi}\,\star\gamma_{\parallel}\sigma^{3}\phi-m^{1-n}i^{n+1}\sum_{j=1}^{n}(-1)^{j-1}\bar{\phi}(\overleftarrow{\not{D}_{\perp}})^{j-1}\star\gamma_{\perp}\sigma^{3}\not{D}_{\perp}^{n-j}\phi\right]\,,

(5.4c)

\displaystyle\star J

\displaystyle=-Re\left[\bar{\phi}\,\star\gamma_{\parallel}\phi-m^{1-n}i^{n+1}\sum_{j=1}^{n}(-1)^{j-1}\bar{\phi}(\overleftarrow{\not{D}_{\perp}})^{j-1}\star\gamma_{\perp}\not{D}_{\perp}^{n-j}\phi\right]\,,

(5.4d)

where we have defined
\star\gamma=\gamma^{A}(dS)_{A}
. On the other hand, their expectation values can be obtained from the generating functional

\mathcal{Z}[e^{A},\Omega^{AB},A]=-\log\int D\bar{\Psi}D\Psi e^{-S_{E}[\bar{\Psi},\Psi;e^{A},\Omega^{AB},A]}\,,

(5.5)

\delta_{\chi}\mathcal{Z}=W_{\text{anom}}[e^{A},\Omega^{AB},A;\chi]\,,

(5.6)

with
W_{\text{anom}}
containing the gauge and gravitational anomalies of the system, nonetheless a detailed analysis of the hydrodynamic description including anomalous contributions escapes the scope of this paper and will be left for future studies, therefore, the existence of
W_{\text{anom}}
will be ignored.

The Ward identities of the system can be obtained by explicitly evaluating the left-hand-side of Eq. (
5.6
) which takes the form

\delta_{\chi}\mathcal{Z}=-\int_{\mathcal{M}}\left(\xi^{A}\frac{\delta\mathcal{Z}}{\delta\xi^{A}}+\bar{\lambda}^{(S)}\frac{\delta\mathcal{Z}}{\delta\bar{\lambda}^{(S)}}+\bar{\lambda}^{(L)}\frac{\delta\mathcal{Z}}{\delta\bar{\lambda}^{(L)}}+\bar{\lambda}^{(Q)}\frac{\delta\mathcal{Z}}{\delta\bar{\lambda}^{(Q)}}\right)+\int_{\mathcal{M}}d\star\tilde{N}\,,

. Therefore, each term in Eq. (

\displaystyle D\star\mathcal{T}\,_{A}-T_{A}^{B}\wedge\star\mathcal{T}\,_{B}-\mathcal{F}_{A}^{(S)}\wedge\star\mathcal{S}-\mathcal{F}_{A}^{(L)}\wedge\star\mathcal{J}

\displaystyle=F_{A}\wedge\star J\,,

(5.8a)

\displaystyle d\star\mathcal{S}

\displaystyle=\star\mathcal{T}\,^{A}\wedge\epsilon_{A}\,,

(5.8b)

\displaystyle d\star\mathcal{J}

\displaystyle=\star\mathcal{T}\,^{A}\wedge\varepsilon_{A}\,,

(5.8c)

\displaystyle d\star J

\displaystyle=0\,,

(5.8d)

where we have used (
4.16
), the gauge field transformations (
4.21
), and defined the one-form

\mathcal{F}_{A}\equiv\iota_{E_{A}}\mathcal{F}=T^{B}_{A}P_{B}+\frac{1}{2}\left(\mathcal{F}^{(S)}_{A}\,\epsilon^{BC}+\mathcal{F}^{(L)}_{A}\,\varepsilon^{BC}\right)M_{BC}+F_{A}\,Q\,,

(5.9)

in terms of the
SO(1,1)\times SO(2)
curvature (
4.8
). Moreover, if the system possesses a symmetry generated by Killing parameters
\chi_{K}=(\xi_{K},\lambda_{K}^{AB},\lambda_{K}^{(Q)})
we can interpret the effective action as the hydrostatic partition function of the system

\mathcal{Z}=\int_{\mathcal{M}}\star\mathcal{P}\,,

(5.10)

where
\mathcal{P}
is a local function of the spacetime coordinates that we interpret as the hydrostatic pressure. Therefore, after equating (
5.7

) and using the equations of motion we obtain that the Noether current

N=\mathcal{T}\,_{A}\xi_{K}^{A}+\mathcal{S}\bar{\lambda}_{K}^{(S)}+\mathcal{J}\bar{\lambda}_{K}^{(L)}+J\bar{\lambda}_{K}^{(Q)}-\mathcal{P}\xi_{K}\,,

(5.11)

must be conserved, i.e.

d\star N=0\,.

(5.12)

6
Thermal equilibrium and partition function

In order to have the notion of hydrostatic equilibrium in the system, a set of Killing parameters are needed on the manifold such that the system can reach a steady regime with the external forces and local hydrodynamic variables compensating each other. Given Killing parameters
\chi_{K}=(\xi_{K},\lambda_{K}^{AB},\lambda_{K}^{(Q)})
with
\xi_{K}
future-oriented, in what follows we will define the hydrostatic variables.

First we note that the projectors
g^{A}_{B},h^{A}_{B}
allow one to decompose the tangent spaces as
T_{p}\mathcal{M}=\mathbb{R}^{1,1}_{\parallel}\oplus\mathbb{R}^{2}_{\perp}
. In particular, using the vector field
\xi_{K}
we can construct vectors
u^{A},v^{A}
satisfying

u_{A}u^{A}=-1\,,\qquad v_{A}v^{A}=1\,,\qquad u_{A}v^{A}=0\,,

(6.1)

belonging to the longitudinal and transverse subspaces in
T_{p}\mathcal{M}
respectively, and defined as

\beta u^{A}=g^{A}_{B}\xi_{K}^{B}\,,\quad\quad\bar{\alpha}\,v^{A}=\,h^{A}_{B}\xi_{K}^{B}\,.

(6.2)

with the inverse temperature
\beta=T^{-1}
and (reduced) momentum’s chemical potential
\bar{\alpha}=\beta\alpha
defined as

\beta^{2}=-g(\xi_{K},\xi_{K})\,,\qquad\bar{\alpha}^{2}=h(\xi_{K},\xi_{K})\,.

(6.3)

The fluid velocity can be expanded as

U^{A}=u^{A}+\alpha\,v^{A}\,.

(6.4)

At this point we find it convenient to introduce the projectors

\displaystyle\Delta^{A}\,_{B}

\displaystyle=\delta^{A}_{B}+U^{A}u_{B}\,,

(6.5a)

\displaystyle P^{A}\,_{B}

\displaystyle=\Delta^{A}\,_{B}-v^{A}\hat{v}_{B}\,,

(6.5b)

where
\hat{v}_{A}=v_{A}+\alpha u_{A}
, such that

\Delta^{A}\,_{B}
projects transverse to the fluid velocity, whereas
P^{A}\,_{B}
projects transverse to both the fluid velocity and the momentum’s chemical potential (
\alpha v^{A}
).
In addition, we define also the boost-rotations, and
U(1)
(reduced) chemical potentials as

\bar{\mu}^{AB}=\bar{\mu}^{(S)}\epsilon^{AB}+\bar{\mu}^{(L)}\varepsilon^{AB}\equiv\bar{\lambda}^{AB}_{K}\,,\qquad\bar{\mu}\equiv\bar{\lambda}^{(Q)}_{K}\,.

(6.6)

Note that the structure of the tangent space and the properties of the degenerate epsilon tensors
\epsilon_{AB},\varepsilon_{AB}
allow one to construct out of the Killing vector
\xi_{K}
, a complete set of vectors
(U,\tilde{U},\tilde{V},V)
and dual co-vectors
(u,\tilde{u},\tilde{v},\hat{v})
defined as follows

\displaystyle u=u^{A}e_{A}\,,\quad\tilde{u}=u^{A}\epsilon_{A}\,,\quad\tilde{v}=v^{A}\varepsilon_{A}\,,\quad\hat{v}=v+\alpha u\,,

(6.7a)

\displaystyle\tilde{U}=g^{-1}\tilde{u}\,,\quad\tilde{V}=h^{-1}\tilde{v}\,,\quad V=h^{-1}v\,,\quad v=v^{A}e_{A}.

(6.7b)

with
g^{-1},h^{-1}
the degenerate inverse ‘metrics’. On this basis, both
g
and
h
take the form

g=-u\otimes u+\tilde{u}\otimes\tilde{u}\,,\qquad h=v\otimes v+\tilde{v}\otimes\tilde{v}.

(6.8)

The hydrostatic relations are derived by expressing the Killing conditions
\delta_{\chi_{K}}\mathcal{A}=0
, with the components of
\delta_{\chi}\mathcal{A}
given in Eq. (
4.21
), in terms of the hydrodynamic variables. In particular, the variation of the vierbeins
e^{A}
implies that

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\xi_{K}}g

\displaystyle=0\,,

(6.9a)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\xi_{K}}h

\displaystyle=0\,.

(6.9b)

Since
g
and
h
are written in terms of the 1-forms
u,\tilde{u},v,\tilde{v}
we compute first their Lie derivatives

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\xi_{K}}u

\displaystyle=\beta a^{0}-d\beta\,,

\displaystyle\qquad a^{0}

\displaystyle=\iota_{U}du\,,

(6.10a)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\xi_{K}}\tilde{u}

\displaystyle=\beta a^{1}\,,

\displaystyle\qquad a^{1}

\displaystyle=\iota_{U}d\tilde{u}\,,

(6.10b)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\xi_{K}}\tilde{v}

\displaystyle=\beta a^{2}\,,

\displaystyle\qquad a^{2}

\displaystyle=\iota_{U}d\tilde{v}\,,

(6.10c)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\xi_{K}}v

\displaystyle=\beta a^{3}+d\bar{\alpha}\,,

\displaystyle\qquad a^{3}

\displaystyle=\iota_{U}dv\,.

(6.10d)

Using these we can find the Lie derivative of the ‘metrics’ as follows

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\xi_{K}}g=\left[-\beta\left(u\otimes a^{0}+a^{0}\otimes u\right)+u\otimes d\beta+d\beta\otimes u+\beta\left(\tilde{u}\otimes a^{1}+a^{1}\otimes\tilde{u}\right)\right],

(6.11a)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\xi_{K}}h=\left[\beta\left(v\otimes a^{3}+a^{3}\otimes v\right)+v\otimes d\bar{\alpha}+d\bar{\alpha}\otimes v+\beta\left(\tilde{v}\otimes a^{2}+a^{2}\otimes\tilde{v}\right)\right].

(6.11b)

Therefore, the hydrostatic conditions are obtained from all the independent projections of Eqs. (
6.9a
) and (
6.9b
) respectively. In particular, the scalar projections
\mathcal{L}_{\xi_{K}}g\,(U,U)
,
\mathcal{L}_{\xi_{K}}h\,(U,U)
,
\mathcal{L}_{\xi_{K}}g\,(U,V)
,
\mathcal{L}_{\xi_{K}}h\,(U,V)
,
\mathcal{L}_{\xi_{K}}g\,(V,V)
and
\mathcal{L}_{\xi_{K}}h\,(V,V)
imply

\displaystyle\iota_{U}d\beta

\displaystyle=0\,,

\displaystyle\iota_{U}d\alpha

\displaystyle=0\,,

(6.12a)

\displaystyle\iota_{V}(\beta a^{0}-d\beta)

\displaystyle=0\,,

\displaystyle\iota_{V}(\beta a^{3}+d\bar{\alpha})

\displaystyle=0\,.

(6.12b)

On the other hand, the vector projectors are obtained from
\mathcal{L}_{\xi_{K}}g\,(U,\eta)

\mathcal{L}_{\xi_{K}}h\,(U,\eta)

\mathcal{L}_{\xi_{K}}g\,(V,\eta)

\mathcal{L}_{\xi_{K}}h\,(V,\eta)

P^{\mu}_{\ \nu}\eta^{\nu}=\eta^{\mu}

\displaystyle=0\,,

\displaystyle=0\,,

\displaystyle=0\,.

(6.13b)

The tensor equations finally read

\displaystyle(a^{1}\otimes\tilde{u}+\tilde{u}\otimes a^{1})(\eta,\gamma)

\displaystyle=0\,,

(6.14a)

\displaystyle(a^{2}\otimes\tilde{v}+\tilde{v}\otimes a^{2})(\eta,\gamma)

\displaystyle=0\,,

(6.14b)

with

With the notion of equilibrium being established, now we discuss the zeroth-order hydrodynamics. As we discussed before, the zeroth-order hydrostatic partition function must be a functional of the scalars constructed with no derivative. There are five zeroth-order scalars that characterize the hydrostatic system: the Lorentzian norm of the Killing vector
(\xi_{K}^{A})
defines a temperature
T
, whereas the Euclidean norm defines a chemical potential
\alpha
. On top of these, the system possesses three chemical potentials:
\mu^{(S)}
and
\mu^{(L)}
corresponding to boost-rotation charges, and
\mu
corresponding to the U
(1)
charge.
Combining all these together, at the zeroth order the hydrostatic partition function is given by:

\mathcal{Z}=\int\mathcal{P}\left(T,\alpha,\mu^{(S)},\mu^{(L)},\mu\right)dV\,.

(6.19)

\displaystyle J^{\mu}=\rho U^{\mu}\,

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}^{\mu}=\rho^{(S)}U^{\mu},

(6.20b)

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}^{\mu}=\rho^{(L)}U^{\mu},

(6.20d)

where we have defined

\displaystyle s

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial\mathcal{P}}{\partial T}\,\quad p=\frac{\partial\mathcal{P}}{\partial\alpha},\quad\rho^{(S)}=\frac{\partial\mathcal{P}}{\partial\mu^{(S)}}\,,\quad\rho^{(L)}=\frac{\partial\mathcal{P}}{\partial\mu^{(L)}}\,,\quad\rho=\frac{\partial\mathcal{P}}{\partial\mu},

(6.21)

and the Gibbs-Duhem relation Eq. (
2.13
)
that incorporates the spin densities
\tilde{\mathcal{E}}+\mathcal{P}=Ts+\rho\mu+\rho^{(S)}\mu^{(S)}+\rho^{(L)}\mu^{(L)}
has been used. Moreover, the entropy current is obtained by inserting the constitutive relations into Eq. (

N^{\mu}=-sU^{\mu}.

(6.22)

7
Discussion and Outlook

In this work, we have developed a covariant framework to describe the hydrostatic equilibrium of mWSMs, which are characterized by their anisotropic dispersion relations and topological charge. Our formalism, rooted in the gauging of the reduced spacetime symmetry group
(SO(1,1)\times SO(2))\ltimes\mathbb{R}^{4}
, leads to a non-Lorentzian geometry tailored to capture the physical behavior of these systems. A pivotal achievement of this study is the construction of a new class of non-Lorentzian curved spacetime background that naturally incorporates the anisotropic properties of mWSMs, facilitated through the coupling of multi-Weyl fermions to external gauge and geometric fields.
One of the notable features emerging from our analysis is that these spacetimes are intrinsically torsionful, forcing a distinction between intrinsic and extrinsic torsion components in the underlying geometry.

Our derivation of the hydrostatic partition function at zeroth order lays the groundwork for understanding non-dissipative transport in mWSMs. It demonstrates that despite the absence of Lorentz invariance, the system retains a robust structure enabling the definition of equilibrium thermodynamic variables such as temperature, chemical potentials, and pressure. These variables, through a carefully defined set of Killing parameters, ensure the compatibility of our geometric formalism with hydrostatic equilibrium conditions.
Furthermore, we emphasize that while our motivation stemmed from condensed matter realizations of mWSMs, the formalism possesses broader applicability. Specifically, it extends naturally to ultra-relativistic hydrodynamic systems such as Bjorken flow, which share a similar symmetry structure. This underscores the utility of our framework as a general tool for analyzing systems with reduced Lorentz symmetry in both high-energy and condensed matter physics.

Our work paves the road to the construction of a fully non-dissipative hydrodynamic theory for these systems. Moreover, it allows for a systematic analysis of anomaly-induced transport, especially the one related to expected gravitational anomalies of the system
[
7
]
. In materials such as Graphene and Weyl semimetals, straining manifests in the form of pseudo-gauge fields and generates an effective curved geometry
[
54
,
55
,
56
]
; it would be interesting to understand how these phenomena generalize to the case of mWSMs. In fact, recent studies find that pseudo-gauge fields do not emerge from strain in mWSMs
[
8
,
13
]
, therefore, we may wonder whether all the effect of strain fields is encapsulated in the geometric fields of our theory.

Furthermore, our formalism is applicable to exploring out-of-equilibrium dynamics via the Schwinger-Keldish effective action formalism
[
23
,
57
]
or using gauge/gravity duality
[
58
,
59
,
60
,
61
,
62
,
63
]
, which presents an exciting avenue. Ultimately, we anticipate that the symmetry-based approach adopted here will catalyze further developments in the hydrodynamic modeling of anisotropic and non-boost-invariant systems.
