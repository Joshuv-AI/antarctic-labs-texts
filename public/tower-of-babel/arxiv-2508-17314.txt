Extension of a problem of Euler in Lorentz-Minkowski plane
Muhittin Evren Aydin, Rafael López

Abstract
. In this paper we study curves in Lorentz-Minkowski space 𝕃 2 \mathbb{L}^{2} that are critical points of the moment of inertia with respect to the origin. This extends a problem posed by Euler in the Lorentzian setting. We obtain explicit solutions for stationary curves in 𝕃 2 \mathbb{L}^{2} distinguishing if the curve is spacelike or timelike. We also give a method to carry stationary spacelike curves into stationary timelike curves and vice versa via symmetries and inversions about the lightlike cone. Finally, we solve the problem of maximizing the energy among all spacelike curves joining two given points which are collinear with the origin.

1.
Introduction of the problem

In 1744, Euler proposed the study of curves
\gamma
in the Euclidean plane that minimize or maximize the energy

(1)

E_{\alpha}[\gamma]=\int_{\gamma}|\gamma(s)|^{\alpha}\,ds,

where
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
is a parameter and
s
is the arc-length parameter
[
4
]
. When
\alpha=0
, the energy
E_{0}[\gamma]
is the length of
\gamma
. For
\alpha=2
, the energy
E_{2}[\gamma]
is the moment of inertia of
\gamma
with respect to the origin of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. Assuming that
\gamma
does not contain the origin, the critical points of
E_{\alpha}
are characterized in terms of the curvature
\kappa
of the curve, namely,

(2)

\kappa=\alpha\frac{\langle N,\gamma\rangle}{|\gamma|^{2}},

where
N
is the unit normal vector of
\gamma
. Recently, the authors together A. Bueno have extended this problem to the other two Riemannian space forms, the sphere
\mathbb{S}^{2}
and the hyperbolic plane
\mathbb{H}^{2}

[
1
]
.

In this paper, we extend Euler’s variational problem to the Lorentzian two-dimensional space form. The Lorentz-Minkowski plane
\mathbb{L}^{2}
is defined by
\mathbb{L}^{2}=(\mathbb{R}^{2},\langle,\rangle)
where
\langle,\rangle
is the Lorentzian metric
\langle,\rangle=dx^{2}-dy^{2}
and
(x,y)
are the canonical coordinates of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. Given two points
P_{1},P_{2}\in\mathbb{L}^{2}
, the problem is to determine the curve
\gamma\colon I\to\mathbb{L}^{2}
with minimum or maximum energy
E_{\alpha}
among all curves joining
P_{1}
and
P_{2}
.

Important differences appear in the Lorentzian context. First, in order for the arc-length
ds
to make sense in (
1
), it is necessary to restrict the study to spacelike curves. In addition, since, for
\alpha=0
, the energy
E_{0}[\gamma]
reduces to the length of the curve
\gamma
, it is natural in this context to find curves which maximize the energy
E_{\alpha}
, in contrast to the Euclidean case, where the problem concerns minimizers of the energy.

As in the Euclidean setting, we require that
0\not\in\gamma(I)
,
s\in I\subset\mathbb{R}
, to assure differentiability of the functional
E_{\alpha}
. However, another difference arises in the Lorentzian setting concerning the term
|\gamma|^{\alpha}
in (
1
). In
\mathbb{L}^{2}
, the quantity
\langle\gamma,\gamma\rangle
is not necessarily positive, because for a point
p=(x,y)\in\mathbb{L}^{2}
, we have
\langle p,p\rangle=x^{2}-y^{2}
, which may be positive, zero, or negative. Thus, we will take
|\gamma|^{\alpha}=|\langle\gamma,\gamma\rangle|^{\alpha/2}
. In consequence, we need to assume that the curve
\gamma
does not intersect the lightlike cone
\mathcal{C}=\{p\in\mathbb{L}^{2}\colon\langle p,p\rangle=0\}
.

The compliment of
\mathcal{C}
in
\mathbb{L}^{2}
consists of four connected components, hence
\gamma
must be included in one of these components. We set

\mathcal{C}^{-}=\{p\in\mathbb{L}^{2}\colon\langle p,p\rangle<0\},\qquad\mathcal{C}^{+}=\{p\in\mathbb{L}^{2}\colon\langle p,p\rangle>0\}.

Each of the sets
\mathcal{C}^{-}
and
\mathcal{C}^{+}
is formed by two components. Depending on whether
\gamma(I)
is contained in
\mathcal{C}^{-}
or in
\mathcal{C}^{+}
, we have
|\langle\gamma,\gamma\rangle|=-\langle\gamma,\gamma\rangle
or
|\langle\gamma,\gamma\rangle|=\langle\gamma,\gamma\rangle
, respectively. This makes to distinguish in which set the curve is contained.

Following the standard calculus of variations (see Sect.
2
), a spacelike curve
\gamma\colon I\subset\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{L}^{2}\setminus\mathcal{C}
is a critical point of the energy
E_{\alpha}
if and only if

(3)

\kappa=-\alpha\frac{\langle N,\gamma\rangle}{|\langle\gamma,\gamma\rangle|}.

Eq. (
3
) leads to an interesting observation. Although the problem was initially formulated only for spacelike curves, the same equation also holds for timelike curves. This allows to introduce the following definition, which applies to both types of curves.

Definition 1.1
.

Let
\gamma\colon I\to\mathbb{L}^{2}
be a spacelike or timelike curve in
\mathbb{L}^{2}
satisfying
\gamma(I)\cap\mathcal{C}=\emptyset
. We call
\gamma
an
\alpha
-stationary surface of
E_{\alpha}
if

(4)

\kappa=-\alpha\frac{\langle N,\gamma\rangle}{|\langle\gamma,\gamma\rangle|}.

If the value of
\alpha
is not specified, such curves will simply be called stationary curves.

A particular emphasis in this paper is to highlight the differences from the Euclidean case and the specific features that appear in the Lorentzian setting. After the derivation of Eq. (
4
) for spacelike curves in Sect.

2
, we will show examples of stationary curves with constant curvature, namely straight lines and circles.
We also give an application of the maximum principle to control the value of
\alpha
for
\alpha
-stationary spacelike curves.
In Sect.
3
we establish two types of connections between
\alpha
-stationary curves. First, by using the symmetry
(x,y)\mapsto(y,x)
about the lightlike cone, we carry stationary spacelike curves contained in
\mathcal{C}^{+}
(resp.
\mathcal{C}^{-}
) to stationary timelike curves contained in
\mathcal{C}^{-}
(resp.
\mathcal{C}^{+})
(Thm.
3.1
). On the other hand, the inversion
p\mapsto\frac{p}{\langle p,p\rangle}
gives a connection between
\alpha
-stationary curves with different values of
\alpha
(Thm.
3.2
). Both results will be useful in the computation and derivation of properties of stationary curves. In Sect.
4
, we obtain the explicit parametrizations of all stationary curves in
\mathbb{L}^{2}
. Finally, in Sect.
5
, we provide a first approach to the problem of maximizing the energy
E_{\alpha}
, obtaining the classification of the maximizers in the case the two given points which are collinear with the origin.

2.
The Euler-Lagrange equation and examples

In this section, we begin with the derivation of the Euler-Lagrange equation for the energy (
1
), next show examples of stationary curves, and finally apply the maximum principle to estimate the value of
\alpha
for
\alpha
-stationary spacelike curves that are far away from the lightlike cone.

The Euler-Lagrange equation follows from standard arguments in calculus of variations. Let
\gamma\colon I\to\mathbb{L}^{2}
be a spacelike curve, and assume that locally
\gamma
is given by
y=y(x)
,
x\in[a,b]
. The condition that
\gamma
is spacelike implies that
y^{\prime}(x)^{2}<1
for all
x\in[a,b]
. As a first step, we consider the case that
\gamma
is included in
\mathcal{C}^{-}
, that is,
x^{2}-y(x)^{2}<0
for all
x\in[a,b]
. We take the unit normal vector
N
of
\gamma
to be

(5)

N=\frac{(y^{\prime},1)}{\sqrt{1-y^{\prime 2}}}.

The energy functional (
1
) can be written as

(6)

E_{\alpha}[\gamma]=\int_{a}^{b}(y^{2}-x^{2})^{\alpha/2}\sqrt{1-y^{\prime 2}}\,dx:=\int_{a}^{b}J(x,y,y^{\prime}).

Applying the Euler-Lagrange equation

\frac{\partial J}{\partial y}=\frac{d}{dx}\left(\frac{\partial J}{\partial y^{\prime}}\right)
,
we obtain

(7)

\alpha\frac{xy^{\prime}-y}{\sqrt{1-y^{\prime 2}}}=-(y^{2}-x^{2})\frac{d}{dx}\left(\frac{y^{\prime}}{\sqrt{1-y^{\prime 2}}}\right).

Since

\langle N,\gamma\rangle=\frac{xy^{\prime}-y}{\sqrt{1-y^{\prime 2}}},

and the second parenthesis in the right hand-side is the curvature
\kappa
of
\gamma
, Eq. (
7
) becomes

\kappa=\alpha\frac{\langle N,\gamma\rangle}{\langle\gamma,\gamma\rangle}.

This equation coincides with (
4
).

In case that
\gamma(I)
is contained in
\mathcal{C}^{+}
, the arguments are similar. Now the energy changes to

E_{\alpha}[\gamma]=\int_{a}^{b}(x^{2}-y^{2})^{\alpha/2}\sqrt{1-y^{\prime 2}}\,dx,

obtaining that critical points of the energy are characterized by the equation

\kappa=-\alpha\frac{\langle N,\gamma\rangle}{\langle\gamma,\gamma\rangle}.

Again, this equation coincides with (
4
).

Although the energy (
1
) does not make sense for timelike curves, Eq. (
4
) still holds. For timelike curves, we apply a similar argument. Indeed, if the curve is
y=y(x)
, the timelike condition is
y^{\prime 2}>1
. For this, we consider the same expression (
6
) but replace the term
\sqrt{1-y^{\prime 2}}
with
\sqrt{y^{\prime 2}-1}
.

If
\gamma
is contained in
\mathcal{C}^{+}
, the computation of the Euler-Lagrange equation yields

\kappa=-\alpha\frac{\langle N,\gamma\rangle}{x^{2}-y^{2}}=-\alpha\frac{\langle N,\gamma\rangle}{\langle\gamma,\gamma\rangle}.

This formula coincides with (
4
). In the case that
\gamma(I)\subset\mathcal{C}^{-}
, the same relation is obtained, except with the opposite sign on the right-hand side. Therefore, this confirms that the extension of the Def.
1.1
for timelike curves is valid.

Remark 2.1
.

It is clear that linear isometries of
\mathbb{L}^{2}
, as well as dilations of
\mathbb{L}^{2}
, preserve the solutions of (
4
).

We present some examples of stationary curves among the family of curves of
\mathbb{L}^{2}
with constant curvature. Notice that by curvature we mean the (geodesic) curvature of a curve as submanifold of
\mathbb{L}^{2}
. If
\gamma
is a non-degenerate curve in
\mathbb{L}^{2}
parametrized by arc-length and
\nabla
is the Levi-Civita connection on
\mathbb{L}^{2}
, the Weingarten formula is
Av=-\nabla_{v}N
where
A
is the shape operator,
v
is a tangent vector and
N
is the unit normal vector of
\gamma
. Then the curvature is defined by
\kappa=-\epsilon\,\mbox{trace}(A)
, where
\epsilon=\langle\gamma^{\prime},\gamma^{\prime}\rangle
. Then

(8)

\begin{split}\kappa&=-\epsilon\,\mbox{trace}(A)=-\epsilon(\langle A\gamma^{\prime},\epsilon\gamma^{\prime}\rangle=\langle\nabla_{\gamma^{\prime}}N,\gamma^{\prime}\rangle=\langle N^{\prime},\gamma^{\prime}\rangle\\
&=-\langle N,\gamma^{\prime\prime}\rangle.\end{split}

We now describe the two types of circles in
\mathbb{L}^{2}
depending on its causal character. A hyperbolic circle in
\mathbb{L}^{2}
with radius
r>0
and center
p_{0}
is defined as one of the two components of

\mathbb{H}^{1}(p_{0};r)=\{p\in\mathbb{L}^{2}:\langle p-p_{0},p-p_{0}\rangle=-r^{2}\}.

Each components is contained in a distinct component of
\mathcal{C}^{-}
. This curve is spacelike with curvature
\kappa=\frac{1}{r}
for the orientation
N(p)=(p-p_{0})/r
.

Similarly, a pseudocircle of radius
r>0
centered at
p_{0}
is defined by one of the two components of

\mathbb{S}^{1}_{1}(p_{0};r)=\{p\in\mathbb{L}^{2}:\langle p-p_{0},p-p_{0}\rangle=r^{2}\}.

Each component lies in one of the components of
\mathcal{C}^{+}
. A pseudocircle is a timelike curve with curvature
\kappa=\frac{1}{r}
for the orientation
N(p)=-(p-p_{0})/r
. For both types of circles, when the center is the origin, we denote them simply by
\mathbb{H}^{1}(r)
and
\mathbb{S}^{1}_{1}(r)
, respectively.

We provide some examples of stationary curves.

Example 2.2
.

(1)

All non-degenerate straight lines in
\mathbb{L}^{2}
passing through the origin
(0,0)
are
\alpha
-stationary curves, for any value of
\alpha
.

(2)

Hyperbolic circles centered at the origin. They are
\alpha
-stationary curves for
\alpha=1
and for all values of
r
.

(3)

Pseudocircles centered at the origin. They are
\alpha
-stationary curves for
\alpha=1
for all values of
r
.

In the following result, we find all straight lines, hyperbolic circles and pseudocircles that are
\alpha
-stationary curves: see Fig.

.

Non-degenerate straight lines crossing

(2)

(3)

The only pseudocircles that are

(a)

Pseudocircles
\mathbb{S}^{1}_{1}(r)

Proof.

It is straightforward that the only straight lines that are stationary curves are those ones crossing the origin.

(1)

We are looking for which hyperbolic circles are
\alpha
-stationary curves. Let
\mathbb{H}^{1}(p_{0};r)
be parametrized by
\gamma(s)=(x_{0},y_{0})+r(\sinh(s),\pm\cosh(s))
, where
p_{0}=(x_{0},y_{0})
. Suppose that
\mathbb{H}^{1}(p_{0};r)\subset\mathcal{C}^{-}

4

(1-\alpha)\langle\gamma,\gamma\rangle+\alpha\langle\gamma,p_{0}\rangle=0,

which can be written as

-(\alpha-2)rx_{0}\sinh(s)\pm(\alpha-2)ry_{0}\cosh(s)+x_{0}^{2}-y_{0}^{2}+(\alpha-1)r^{2}=0.

If
p_{0}=(0,0)
, then
\alpha=1
. Otherwise, we have
\alpha=2
and
\langle p_{0},p_{0}\rangle=-r^{2}
.
In such a case, there exists a
s_{0}
such that

x_{0}+r\sinh s_{0}=0,\quad y_{0}\pm r\cosh s_{0}=0,

namely, one of the components of
\gamma
crosses
(0,0)
. Differentiating
\langle\gamma(s),\gamma(s)\rangle
twice, we conclude
2\langle\gamma^{\prime}(s_{0}),\gamma^{\prime}(s_{0})\rangle>0

\langle\gamma(s),\gamma(s)\rangle>0

\gamma(s)\in\mathcal{C}^{+}
. This is not possible. The other component is included in
\mathcal{C}^{-}
, proving the result.

Suppose now
\mathbb{H}^{1}(p_{0};r)\subset\mathcal{C}^{+}
. Now Eq. (
4
) is

(1+\alpha)\langle\gamma,\gamma\rangle-\alpha\langle\gamma,p_{0}\rangle=0,

or equivalently,

(\alpha+2)rx_{0}\sinh(s)\mp(\alpha+2)ry_{0}\cosh(s)+x_{0}^{2}-y_{0}^{2}-(\alpha+1)r^{2}=0.

Notice that if
p_{0}=(0,0)
, then
\mathbb{H}^{1}(r)
is included in
\mathcal{C}^{-}
, which it is not possible. Thus
p_{0}\not=(0,0)
and consequently,
\alpha=-2
and
\langle p_{0},p_{0}\rangle=-r^{2}
. The component of
\mathbb{H}^{1}(p_{0};r)
that crosses
(0,0)
is contained in
\mathcal{C}^{+}
but the other is contained in
\mathcal{C}^{-}
.

(2)

Let
\mathbb{S}^{1}_{1}(p_{0};r)
be a pseudocircle, which it is parametrized by
\gamma(s)=(x_{0},y_{0})+r(\pm\cosh(s),\sinh(s))
. Suppose that
\mathbb{S}^{1}_{1}(p_{0};r)
is contained in
\mathcal{C}^{-}
. Then (
4
) gives

(1+\alpha)\langle\gamma,\gamma\rangle-\alpha\langle\gamma,p_{0}\rangle=0.

This equation can be written as

\pm(\alpha+2)rx_{0}\cosh(s)-(\alpha+2)ry_{0}\sinh(s)+x_{0}^{2}-y_{0}^{2}+(\alpha+1)r^{2}=0.

The case
p_{0}=(0,0)
is not possible because this pseudocircle is included in
\mathcal{C}^{-}
. Thus
p_{0}\not=(0,0)
, hence
\alpha=-2
and
\langle p_{0},p_{0}\rangle=r^{2}
. Only one component is included in
\mathcal{C}^{-}
: the other one is included in
\mathcal{C}^{+}
, which must be discarded.

Suppose that
\mathbb{S}^{1}_{1}(p_{0};r)
is contained in
\mathcal{C}^{+}
. Now (
4
) gives

(1-\alpha)\langle\gamma,\gamma\rangle+\alpha\langle\gamma,p_{0}\rangle=0,

or equivalently,

\pm(2-\alpha)rx_{0}\cosh(s)+(\alpha-2)ry_{0}\sinh(s)+x_{0}^{2}-y_{0}^{2}-(\alpha-1)r^{2}=0.

If
p_{0}=(0,0)
, then we have
\alpha=1
. Otherwise,
p_{0}\not=(0,0)
, it follows that
\alpha=2
and
\langle p_{0},p_{0}\rangle=r^{2}
. Only one component is included in
\mathcal{C}^{+}
which, in addition, crosses

We finish this section with an application of the maximum principle for elliptic equations. Eq. (
4
) is elliptic when the curve is spacelike and the maximum principle can be applied: the argument is the same that in the Euclidean context (
[
3
, Prop. 2.5]
). First, for a curve
\gamma
, we define

\overline{\kappa}=\kappa+\alpha\frac{\langle N,\gamma\rangle}{|\langle\gamma,\gamma\rangle|}.

Given
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
, the maximum principle asserts that if two spacelike curves
\gamma_{i}
,
i=1,2
, which are tangent at a common point
s=s_{0}
, and if
\gamma_{1}
lies above
\gamma_{2}
around
p
according to the orientation by the (coincident) normal vectors at
s_{0}

\overline{\kappa}_{1}(s_{0})\geq\overline{\kappa}_{2}(s_{0})
. If, in addition,
\overline{\kappa}_{i}
are constant and
\overline{\kappa}_{1}=\overline{\kappa}_{2}
, then
\gamma_{1}=\gamma_{2}
in a neighborhood of
s=s_{0}
.

Thanks to the maximum principle, it can be proved that in Euclidean plane, the only closed stationary curves are circles centered at the origin (
\alpha=-1
). This result cannot have a counterpart in Lorentzian setting because there are not closed spacelike curves. However, we will use hyperbolic circles
\mathbb{H}^{1}(r)
to compare with
\alpha

\alpha
. For this, we give the following definition. A set
S\subset\mathbb{L}^{2}
is said to be far away from the lightlike cone if there is
\delta>0
such that

S\subset\mathcal{C}_{\delta}:=\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{L}^{2}\colon x^{2}-(y-\delta)^{2}<0\}.

The notion of far away from the lightlike cone can be expressed in terms of causality of
\mathbb{L}^{2}
. It is equivalent to say that
S
is contained in the future (resp. past) causal of the point
p_{\delta}:=(0,\delta)
, which is denoted in the literature by

(resp.

Theorem 2.4
.

Let
\gamma
be a properly immersed
\alpha
-stationary spacelike curve and contained in
\mathcal{C}^{-}
. If
\gamma
is far away from the lightlike cone, then

.

Proof.

Since
\gamma
is connected, and after the linear isometry
(x,y)\mapsto(x,-y)
, we can assume that
\gamma
is contained in the component of
\mathcal{C}_{\delta}
given by
\mathcal{C}_{\delta}^{+}=\{(x,y)\in\mathcal{C}_{\delta}\colon y\geq\delta\}
. The argument is similar if
\gamma
is included in the other component.
Denote by
\mathbb{H}^{1}_{+}(r)
the component of the hyperbolic circle
\mathbb{H}^{1}(r)
contained in
\mathcal{C}^{-}\cap\{y\geq 0\}
. Notice that
\mathbb{H}^{1}_{+}(r)
is asymptotic to the two branches of
\mathcal{C}\cap\{y\geq 0\}
and its vertex is
(0,r)
. Let

be sufficiently small so that
\gamma(I)\cap\mathbb{H}^{1}_{+}(r)=\emptyset
. This is possible because
(0,r)\to(0,0)
as

and the boundary of
\mathcal{C}_{\delta}^{+}
and the half-cone

are at finite positive distance. In fact, the distance between
\gamma(I)
and
\mathbb{H}^{1}_{+}(r)
is positive and finite.

Let us increase the value of

,
r\nearrow\infty
. Then there is a first number

such that
\mathbb{H}^{1}_{+}(r_{0})
and
\gamma(I)
touch. Let

be a common point of
\gamma(I)\cap\mathbb{H}^{1}_{+}(r_{0})
. By considering the unit normal vector

at
\mathbb{H}^{1}_{+}(r_{0})
, the set
\gamma(I)
lies above
\mathbb{H}^{1}_{+}(r_{0})
. For the parameter
\alpha
, the maximum principle at the common contact point

gives

0=\overline{\kappa}_{\gamma}(s_{0})\geq\overline{\kappa}_{\mathbb{H}^{1}_{+}(r_{0})}(s_{0})=\frac{1}{r}-\alpha\frac{1/r_{0}}{r_{0}^{2}}=\frac{1-\alpha}{r_{0}},

which implies

. However, the case

cannot occur. In such a situation, both curves
\gamma
and
\mathbb{H}^{1}_{+}(r_{0})
have the same constant value for
\overline{\kappa}
and
\overline{\kappa}=0
. The maximum principle would imply that
\gamma
coincides with the hyperbolic circle
\mathbb{H}^{1}_{+}(r_{0})
. This is not possible because
\mathbb{H}^{1}_{+}(r_{0})
is not far away from the lightlike cone.

3.
Two relationships between stationary curves

Prop.
2.3
provides similar statements between stationary (spacelike and timelike) circles: compare (2a) with (3a) and (2b) with (3b) respectively. This can be obtained from a more general viewpoint as we now show.

Define the map

\Phi\colon\mathbb{L}^{2}\to\mathbb{L}^{2},\quad\Phi(x,y)=(y,x).

This map is an involution, that is,
\Phi^{2}=1_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}
. It is clear that
\Phi
maps spacelike (timelike) vectors to timelike (spacelike) vectors. Moreover,
\langle\Phi(p),\Phi(p)\rangle=-\langle p,p\rangle
. Consequently,
\Phi
carries spacelike curves contained in
\mathcal{C}^{+}
(resp.
\mathcal{C}^{-}
) to timelike curves contained in
\mathcal{C}^{-}
(resp.
\mathcal{C}^{+}
). The reverse process also holds: timelike curves are carried to spacelike curves. Thus, the map
\Phi
has a well-defined behaviour for stationary curves.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
\gamma
be an
\alpha
-stationary spacelike curve contained in
\mathcal{C}^{-}
(resp.
\mathcal{C}^{+}
). Then
\widetilde{\gamma}=\Phi\circ\gamma
is an
\alpha
-stationary timelike curve contained in
\mathcal{C}^{+}
(resp.
\mathcal{C}^{-}
).

Proof.

Let
\gamma
be an
\alpha
-stationary spacelike curve contained in
\mathcal{C}^{-}
, parametrized by arc-length. Then we have the relations

\widetilde{\gamma}=\Phi\circ\gamma,\quad\widetilde{N}=\Phi\circ N.

It follows
|\langle\gamma,\gamma\rangle|=|\langle\widetilde{\gamma},\widetilde{\gamma}\rangle|
. Due to
\Phi^{2}=1_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}
, we derive

\langle N,\gamma\rangle=\langle\Phi\widetilde{N},\Phi\widetilde{\gamma}\rangle=-\langle\widetilde{N},\widetilde{\gamma}\rangle.

By using (
8
), we compute

\widetilde{\kappa}=-\langle\widetilde{\gamma^{\prime\prime}},\widetilde{N}\rangle=-\langle\Phi\circ\gamma^{\prime\prime},\Phi\circ N\rangle=\langle\gamma^{\prime\prime},N\rangle=-\kappa.

Finally, by (
4
), we have

\widetilde{\kappa}=-\kappa=\alpha\frac{\langle N,\gamma\rangle}{|\langle\gamma,\gamma\rangle|}=-\alpha\frac{\langle\widetilde{N},\widetilde{\gamma}\rangle}{|\langle\widetilde{\gamma},\widetilde{\gamma}\rangle|}.

This proves that
\widetilde{\gamma}
is an
\alpha
-stationary timelike curve contained in
\mathcal{C}^{+}
. The argument is similar when
\gamma
is an
\alpha
-stationary spacelike curve contained in
\mathcal{C}^{+}
.
∎

From this result, the statements of Proposition
2.3
follow as a consequence, see also Fig.
1
.

Another interesting map in
\mathbb{L}^{2}
for
\alpha
-stationary curves is the inversion with respect to the lightlike cone, defined by

\Psi\colon\mathbb{L}^{2}\setminus\mathcal{C}\to\mathbb{L}^{2},\quad\Psi(p)=\frac{p}{\langle p,p\rangle}.

Notice that
\Psi
preserves the components of both
\mathcal{C}^{+}
and
\mathcal{C}^{-}
.
We prove that
\Psi
maps
\alpha
-stationary curves to stationary curves, but with different value of
\alpha
.

First, we introduce hyperbolic polar coordinates, which are the Lorentzian analogue of the polar coordinates in the Euclidean plane. Given a point
p=(x,y)\in\mathbb{L}^{2}
with
\langle p,p\rangle\not=0
, there exist
\rho>0
and
\varphi\in\mathbb{R}
such that

\begin{split}p=\rho(\sinh\varphi,\pm\cosh\varphi)&\quad\mbox{if $p\in\mathcal{C}^{-}$}\\
p=\rho(\pm\cosh\varphi,\sinh\varphi)&\quad\mbox{if $p\in\mathcal{C}^{+}$.}\\
\end{split}

The sign
\pm
appears because each of the sets
\mathcal{C}^{-}
and
\mathcal{C}^{+}
is formed by two components. Applying the linear symmetries of
\mathbb{L}^{2}
given by
(x,y)\mapsto(x,-y)
and
(x,y)\mapsto(-x,y)
, we can restrict to the choice of the positive sign in the hyperbolic polar coordinates.

Let
\gamma=\gamma(s)=(x(s),y(s))
be a spacelike curve included in
\mathcal{C}^{-}
. Let
\rho=\rho(s)
be a smooth function such that

\gamma(s)=\rho(s)(\sinh(s),\cosh(s)).

The curvature
\kappa
is given in terms of
\rho
by

\kappa=\frac{\rho\left(\rho^{\prime\prime}+\rho\right)-2\rho^{\prime 2}}{\left(\rho^{2}-\rho^{\prime 2}\right)^{3/2}},

where the unit normal vector is

N=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\rho^{2}-\rho^{\prime 2}}}(\sinh(s)\rho+\cosh(s)\rho^{\prime},\cosh(s)\rho+\sinh(s)\rho^{\prime}).

The spacelike condition is
\rho^{2}-\rho^{\prime 2}>0
. Then Eq. (
4
) is

(9)

\rho\rho^{\prime\prime}+(\alpha-2)\rho^{\prime 2}+(1-\alpha)\rho^{2}=0.

If
\gamma
is contained in
\mathcal{C}^{+}
, the spacelike condition is
\rho^{\prime 2}-\rho^{2}>0
, and Eq. (
4
) is

(10)

\rho\rho^{\prime\prime}-(\alpha+2)\rho^{\prime 2}+(1+\alpha)\rho^{2}=0.

If
\gamma
is a timelike curve, then Eq. (
4
) coincides with (
9
) when
\gamma
is contained in
\mathcal{C}^{+}
and with (
10
) when
\gamma
is contained in
\mathcal{C}^{-}
.

Once we have the equations for the
\alpha
-stationary curves, we now state the following result about the inversion with respect to the lightlike cone (see
[
6
]
for the Euclidean counterpart).

Theorem 3.2
.

Let
\gamma
be a non-degenerate curve contained in
\mathbb{L}^{2}\setminus\mathcal{C}
, and let
\widetilde{\gamma}
denote its inverse,
\widetilde{\gamma}=\Psi\circ\gamma
. Then
\widetilde{\gamma}
and
\gamma
have the same causal character. Moreover:

(1)

\gamma
is an
\alpha
-stationary curve contained in
\mathcal{C}^{-}
if and only if
\widetilde{\gamma}
is an
(2-\alpha)
-stationary curve contained in
\mathcal{C}^{-}
.

(2)

\gamma
is an
\alpha
-stationary curve contained in
\mathcal{C}^{+}
if and only if
\widetilde{\gamma}
is an
(-2-\alpha)
-stationary curve contained in
\mathcal{C}^{+}
.

Proof.

By Theorem
3.1
, it suffices to consider spacelike curves. Suppose that
\gamma
is contained in
\mathcal{C}^{-}
. In hyperbolic polar coordinates, the inverse curve
\widetilde{\gamma}
can be written as
\widetilde{\gamma}(s)=\frac{1}{\rho(s)}(\sinh(s),\cosh(s))
. Thus
\widetilde{\rho}=1/\rho
. In terms of
\widetilde{\rho}
, Eq. (
9
) is equivalent to

\widetilde{\rho}\widetilde{\rho}^{\prime\prime}-\alpha\widetilde{\rho}^{\prime 2}+(\alpha-1)\widetilde{\rho}^{2}=0.

Since
\widetilde{\gamma}
is included in
\mathcal{C}^{-}
, a comparison with Eq. (
9
) yields that
\widetilde{\gamma}
is an
(2-\alpha)
-stationary spacelike curve. The argument is similar when
\gamma
is contained in
\mathcal{C}^{+}
.
∎

Theorem 4.1
.

Up to a linear isometry and a dilation of
\mathbb{L}^{2}

\alpha
-stationary spacelike curves contained in

\gamma(s)=\rho(s)(\sinh(s),\cosh(s))

(11)

\rho(s)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\cosh((\alpha-1)s)^{\frac{1}{\alpha-1}},&\alpha\not=1\\

Moreover,

(1)

If
\alpha>1

\gamma
are asymptotic to a vertical translation of the lightlike cone
\mathcal{C}
.

(2)

If
\alpha=1

\gamma
is a hyperbolic circle

centered at the origin or the two branches of
\gamma
are asymptotic to
\mathcal{C}
.

(3)

If
0<\alpha<1
, then
\gamma
meets tangentially
\mathcal{C}
at two points.

(4)

If
\alpha<0
, then
\gamma
intersects orthogonally
\mathcal{C}
at two points.

Proof.

Let
\gamma=\gamma(s)=(\gamma_{1}(s),\gamma_{2}(s))
be an
\alpha
-stationary spacelike curve included in
\mathcal{C}^{-}
. Let
\rho=\rho(s)
be a smooth function such that

\gamma(s)=\rho(s)(\sinh(s),\cosh(s)).

We know that
\rho
satisfies (
9
) with
\rho^{2}-\rho^{\prime 2}>0
. This equation can be solved, obtaining

\rho(s)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}c_{2}\left(e^{(\alpha-1)s}+c_{1}e^{-(\alpha-1)s}\right)^{\frac{1}{\alpha-1}},&\alpha\not=1\\
c_{2}e^{c_{1}s},&\alpha=1,c_{1}^{2}<1.\end{array}\right.

where

if

and

if
\alpha=1
.

We separate the case
\alpha\not=1
. Consider the rigid motion
R_{t}(x,y)=(\cosh(t)x+\sinh(t)y,\sinh(t)x+y\cosh(t))

where
\theta=s+t

11
). Let

\rho(s)=(\cosh((\alpha-1)s))^{\frac{1}{\alpha-1}}.

As a consequence, the curve
\gamma
is symmetric about the
y
-axis.

(1)

Case
\alpha>1
. It is clear that
\lim_{s\to\infty}\gamma(s)=\infty
. In order to prove that it is asymptotic to a lightlike cone, we have

\lim_{s\to\pm\infty}\frac{\gamma_{2}^{\prime}(s)}{\gamma_{1}^{\prime}(s)}=\pm 1,

which gives the slope of the asymptote. Moreover,

\lim_{s\to\infty}(\gamma_{2}(s)-\gamma_{1}(s))=2^{\frac{1}{1-\alpha}}.

This implies that the lines of equation

are asymptotic to
\gamma

\gamma
is asymptotic to
(0,2^{\frac{1}{1-\alpha}})+\mathcal{C}
which is a vertical translation of
\mathcal{C}
.

(2)

Case
0<\alpha<1
. A computation gives

\lim_{s\to\pm\infty}\gamma(s)=(\pm 2^{\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}},2^{\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}})

and

\lim_{s\to\pm\infty}\frac{\gamma_{2}^{\prime}(s)}{\gamma_{1}^{\prime}(s)}=\pm 1.

This proves that the intersection of
\gamma
with
\mathcal{C}
is tangential.

(3)

Case
\alpha<0
. We have

\lim_{s\to\pm\infty}\gamma(s)=(\pm 2^{\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}},2^{\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}}),

proving
\gamma
tends to
\mathcal{C}

\lim_{s\to\pm\infty}\frac{\gamma_{2}^{\prime}(s)}{\gamma_{1}^{\prime}(s)}=\pm 1,

the intersection (at the limit) between
\gamma
and
\mathcal{C}
is orthogonal.

Suppose now
\alpha=1
. If
c_{1}=0
, then
\gamma
is the hyperbolic circle
\mathbb{H}^{1}(-c_{2}^{2})

c_{1}\not=0
, then
\lim_{s\to\pm\infty}\frac{\gamma_{2}^{\prime}(s)}{\gamma_{1}^{\prime}(s)}=\pm 1

\lim_{s\to\pm\infty}\gamma_{2}(s)-(\pm)\gamma_{1}(s)=0
. This proves that the two branches of
\gamma
are asymptotic to
\mathcal{C}
.

5.
The problem of maximizing the energy

We go back to the initial Euler’s problem in
\mathbb{L}^{2}
: given two points
P_{1},P_{2}\in\mathbb{L}^{2}
, find a spacelike curve
\gamma
which maximizes the energy
E_{\alpha}
. As usually, the first approach is finding the critical points of this energy joining both points, which are potential maximizers of
E_{\alpha}
. In
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, the problem of finding minimizers of
E_{\alpha}
was solved by Mason for the value
\alpha=2

[
7
]
and a general approach for all values of
\alpha
has been recently obtained in
[
2
]
. In these results, techniques as the extremal fields in the sense of Weierstrass are employed
[
5
]
.

A simple but illustrative case is when
P_{1}
and
P_{2}
are collinear with the origin
0=(0,0)
. A possible maximizer is the straight line joining both points. Since we focus on the spacelike case, this line is assumed to be spacelike. Thus, both points must be contained in
\mathcal{C}^{+}
; otherwise, the line joining
P_{1}
and
P_{2}
would meet
\mathcal{C}
, which we want to avoid. Let
S_{12}
denote the segment joining
P_{1}
and
P_{2}
. Under this situation, we prove the following result.

Theorem 5.1
.

Let
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
. Let
P_{1}
and
P_{2}
be two given points contained in
\mathcal{C}^{+}
. If
P_{1}
and
P_{2}
are collinear with
0
and
0\not\in S_{12}
, then the maximizer of the energy
E_{\alpha}
is the segment
S_{12}
.

Proof.

Without loss of generality, we may assume that
P_{1}
and
P_{2}
lie in the component of
\mathcal{C}^{+}
included in
x>0
. We use hyperbolic polar coordinates,
(\rho,\theta)\equiv\rho(\cosh\theta,\sinh\theta)
. For a spacelike curve
\gamma(s)=(\rho(s),\theta(s))
contained in
\mathcal{C}^{+}
, we have

E_{\alpha}[\gamma]=\int_{a}^{b}\rho^{\alpha}\sqrt{\rho^{\prime 2}-\rho^{2}\theta^{\prime 2}}\,ds
.

Up to a linear isometry, a dilation of
\mathbb{L}^{2}
and renaming the points, we can assume
P_{1}=(1,0)
and
P_{2}=(r,0)
with
r>1
. First, we find the value of
E_{\alpha}[S_{12}]
. We parametrize
S_{12}
in hyperbolic polar coordinates by
s\mapsto(\rho(s),\theta(s))
, where
\rho(s)=s
,
\theta(s)=0
and
s\in[1,r]
. Then

E_{\alpha}[S_{12}]=\int_{1}^{r}s^{\alpha}\,ds=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}r^{\alpha+1}-1,&\alpha\not=-1\\
\log(r)&\alpha=-1.\end{array}\right.

Let
\gamma
be any spacelike curve joining
P_{1}
and
P_{2}
,
s\in[a,b]
. Using
\theta^{\prime 2}\geq 0
, we have

E_{\alpha}[\gamma]=\int_{a}^{b}\rho^{\alpha}\sqrt{\rho^{\prime 2}-\rho^{2}\theta^{\prime 2}}\,ds\leq\int_{a}^{b}\rho^{\alpha}\rho^{\prime}\,ds=E_{\alpha}[S_{12}].

∎

We now consider the case that
P_{1}
and
P_{2}
are collinear with
0
and
0\in S_{12}
.

Theorem 5.2
.

Let
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
. Let
P_{1}
and
P_{2}
be two given points contained in
\mathcal{C}^{+}
which are collinear with
0
and
0\in S_{12}
.

(1)

If
\alpha>-1
, the maximizer of the energy
E_{\alpha}
is the segment
S_{12}
.

(2)

If
\alpha\leq-1
, there is not a maximizer of the energy
E_{\alpha}
joining both points.

Proof.

Without loss of generality, we suppose that in hyperbolic polar coordinates,
P_{1}=(1,0)
and
P_{2}=(-r,0)
,
r>0
.

(1)

Case
\alpha>-1
. Let
\gamma=\gamma(s)
,
s\in[a,b]
, be any spacelike curve joining
P_{1}
and
P_{2}
. Choose a point
Q=\gamma(c)
such that the lengths of
\gamma_{|[a,c]}
and
\gamma_{|[c,b]}
are both less than those of
S_{10}
and
S_{02}
, respectively, where
S_{10}
and
S_{02}
are the segments joining
P_{1}
and
0
and
0
and
P_{2}
. Then
E_{\alpha}[\gamma_{|_{[a,c]}}]\leq E_{\alpha}[S_{10}]
and
E_{\alpha}[\gamma_{|_{[c,b]}}]\leq E_{\alpha}[S_{02}]
. Notice that
E_{\alpha}[S_{10}]
and
E_{\alpha}[S_{02}]
are finite because
\alpha>-1
. Thus
E_{\alpha}[\gamma]\leq E_{\alpha}[S_{10}]+E_{\alpha}[S_{02}]=E_{\alpha}[S_{12}]
.

(2)

Case
\alpha\leq-1
. It is immediate that
E_{\alpha}[S_{12}]=\infty
, proving the non-existence of maximizers.

∎
