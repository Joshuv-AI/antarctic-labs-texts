Boosting VBF Reconstruction at Muon Colliders
Carlos Henrique de Lima

Abstract
Forward muon detection at high-energy muon colliders is crucial for resolving the underlying electroweak process. This work proposes a novel strategy using asymmetric beam energies to boost one of the forward muons into the detector acceptance, enhancing the ability to distinguish between W W - and Z Z -initiated vector boson fusion processes. We demonstrate the capabilities of such an asymmetric collider using VBF Higgs production at 3 and 10 TeV muon colliders with modest boost asymmetries. Asymmetric beam configurations can partially recover the physics potential lost in forward regions when detector coverage is limited.

Detecting forward muons is highly challenging in current muon collider designs. Most of the limitation comes from the shielding necessary to handle the beam-induced background (BIB) at these colliders

In this letter, we motivate an alternative experimental design, utilizing asymmetric beam energies to facilitate the measurement of these forward muons. The asymmetric energy boosts the angle of the particle produced in the collision. The asymmetry means that the forward muons traveling in the opposite direction of the boost can now be inside the detector. While the muons traveling in the direction of the boost get further forward, meaning that, under this design, invisible searches are still not optimal. Nevertheless, detecting one muon is enough
Han et al. 2021a
to significantly improve the sensitivity for new physics.

While several approaches have focused on improving shielding geometries or extending detector coverage, these rely on substantial progress in their development. The strategy proposed in this work is minimal in terms of the detector design, relying instead on kinematic manipulation. Eventually, at multi-TeV collisions, the muons generated in VBF can be so forward that their distribution peaks close to the beam pipe, and no shielding and detector improvement can access this region. In contrast, an asymmetric energy configuration can help to tackle this regime. The effect of a beam asymmetry using current detector design parameters can be seen in Figure

We demonstrate this idea using VBF Higgs production at a 3 TeV and 10 TeV center-of-mass energy collider with moderate asymmetries with boost
\beta=\frac{E_{1}-E_{2}}{E_{1}+E_{2}}=[0.5,\,0.9]

Z
-initiated process also increases. This enhances the ability to extract Higgs–gauge boson couplings compared to the symmetric scenario. Even modest asymmetries can improve the discovery reach on electroweak coupled physics without the need for significant progress in detector and shielding design. Beyond VBF Higgs production, asymmetric collisions offer a framework to recover physics in otherwise inaccessible regions.

An asymmetric muon collider consists of two beams with unequal energies, introducing a net boost between the center-of-mass (COM) frame and the laboratory frame. The lab-frame angle
\theta_{\text{lab}}
of outgoing particles is related to the COM angle
\theta
by

\cos\theta_{\rm lab}=\frac{\cos\theta+\beta}{1+\beta\cos\theta}\,.

(1)

where
\beta
is the boost velocity (in units of the speed of light). This transformation compresses forward particles moving aligned with the more energetic beam and expands the acceptance of backward-going particles. It is worth noting that this effect does not apply to the BIB, since they are not interacting with the other beam.

The tradeoff is that final-state particles emitted in the boost direction are compressed into even smaller angles, potentially exiting the detector coverage as shown in Figure
1
. Consequently, reconstructing invisible VBF final states remains challenging, even in asymmetric configurations, as it is not possible to recover both muons. Nonetheless, the net gain is significant: a single reconstructed forward muon is often sufficient to identify the
Z
-fusion component, as the
W
-fusion channel lacks visible leptons.

III
VBF Higgs

In a muon collider, a single Higgs can be produced by both
W
and
Z
fusion, where the
W
is the dominant production channel, but produces forward neutrinos, and the
Z
is subdominant and produces forward muons:

\displaystyle\mu^{+}\,\mu^{-}

\displaystyle\rightarrow\bar{\nu}_{\mu}\,\nu_{\mu}\,H\,,

(2)

\displaystyle\mu^{+}\,\mu^{-}

\displaystyle\rightarrow\mu^{+}\,\mu^{-}\,H\,.

(3)

The VBF topology of these processes means that the muons have a forward angle of typically
\theta_{\mu}\approx m_{Z}/E_{\mu}
. As the energy of the beam increases, most of the distribution of the muons gets concentrated below
10
degrees and thus both processes become indistinguishable as shown in Figure
2
. This, in turn, means that the reach for physics that is produced by
Z
fusion gets obscured by the
W
fusion process. The same is true for the decay products of the Higgs particle. As the process has a
p_{T}
that is dictated by the vector boson mass, as the energy increases, more forward are the decay particles from VBF produced states, as it is possible to see in Figure.
2
.

As the Higgs is produced on-shell, the scale of the process is set by its mass. This eliminates the need to consider complicated electroweak parton distribution functions
Han et al. 2021b
;
*Han:2022laq
. Thus, VBF Higgs is a process that the current Monte Carlo tools can handle, and we can showcase the advantages of being able to reconstruct at least one of the forward muons. As the asymmetric configuration can only recover one of the forward muons, we consider the visible decay channel, more specifically
H\rightarrow b\bar{b}
. We simulate both processes using
MadGraph5_aMC@NLO

Alwall et al. 2014
interfaced with
Pythia8.2

Sjöstrand et al. 2015
for showering and hadronization.

We need to both measure and differentiate the
W
and
Z
initiated process, so we consider two separate analyses, following
Han et al. 2021a
, which are combined in the end:

•

Inclusive channel: No muons detected, and thus both
W
and
Z
fusion are the signal.

•

Exclusive
1\mu
channel: Only events from
Z
fusion are the signal.

Since most of the effects of the asymmetric beam are related to only the detector geometry, we use a simplified detector simulation implemented in
DELPHES

de Favereau et al. 2014
. We also do not include the beam energy spread, as the goal of this work is to compare the different colliders with the same parameters.

The dominant background comes from
Z
processes of the form

\displaystyle\mu^{+}\mu^{-}

\displaystyle\rightarrow\bar{\nu}_{\mu}\nu_{\mu}Z(jj)\,,

(4)

\displaystyle\mu^{+}\mu^{-}

\displaystyle\rightarrow\mu^{+}\mu^{-}Z(jj)\,.

(5)

The background can be easily handled by implementing the following basic kinematic cuts:

\displaystyle p_{T}^{b}

\displaystyle>30\,\text{GeV}\,,\,10^{\circ}<\theta_{b/\mu}^{\text{lab}}<170^{\circ}\,,

(6)

\displaystyle m_{\text{recoil}}

\displaystyle>200\,\text{GeV}\,,\,110\,\text{GeV}<m_{b\bar{b}}<140\,\text{GeV}\,.

(7)

As the cuts have sufficient distinguishing power, it is not necessary to tag the
b
-flavor, as noted in
Han et al. 2021a
. Although this increases the background, it can still be easily removed after the cuts.

The separate measurement of these two processes can be used to determine their respective Gauge-Higgs coupling. We can parametrize the contribution of new physics at low energies of this process using the kappa framework, where the Gauge-Higgs coupling is modified by a factor
\kappa_{V}=1+\Delta\kappa_{V}
. The total cross-section can be written as

\displaystyle\sigma_{\text{inc}}

\displaystyle=(1+\Delta\kappa_{W})^{2}\sigma_{W}+(1+\Delta\kappa_{Z})^{2}\sigma_{Z}^{\text{inc}}\,,

(8)

\displaystyle\sigma_{1\mu}

\displaystyle=(1+\Delta\kappa_{Z})^{2}\sigma_{Z}^{1\mu}\,.

(9)

A combined likelihood fit of
\kappa_{W}
and
\kappa_{Z}
is done by constructing a Poisson log-likelihood for each analysis. The
95\%
C.L regions for the different configurations of symmetric and asymmetric colliders can be seen in Figure.
3
. The important feature of these plots is the relative behavior between the accelerator setups. There may be differences in the efficiency of the detector that change the overall size of these curves, but the relative behavior is fixed, as they are measured assuming the same detector design.

There are some interesting behaviors that it is possible to notice from Figure.
3
. First, the effects of the asymmetry are less pronounced for lower energies. In these regions, enough muons are being detected by the tails of the distribution to obtain a good reach for both Gauge-Higgs couplings, with a slight correlation that is not possible to resolve. As the asymmetry increases, this degenerate direction gets resolved, and the search becomes statistically limited. The situation is more extreme at higher energies, where the tails of the distribution do not produce enough detectable muons, such that there is a loss of sensitivity in a direction in the
\Delta\kappa_{W}
vs
\Delta\kappa_{Z}
plane. This degeneracy gets resolved as the beam asymmetry increases.

Another thing to notice is that the symmetry configurations considered for the larger values of
\beta
have significantly higher energy, but get significantly worse in the disentangling of the correlations between the Gauge-Higgs couplings. While the reach for
\kappa_{W}
scales in these scenarios, there is effectively no reach for individual
\kappa_{Z}
measurement, where all the bound comes only from the inclusive analysis.

IV
Conclusion

This work proposes the use of asymmetric beam energies as a simple but powerful strategy to improve forward muon reconstruction at high-energy muon colliders. In the multi-TeV regime, vector boson fusion and scattering become the dominant processes to study the electroweak sector, but their characteristic forward leptons are often lost due to shielding constraints. We demonstrated that introducing a modest Lorentz boost via beam asymmetry can effectively shift one of the forward muons from
Z
-fusion into the detector acceptance region, enabling their reconstruction without altering detector geometry.

Reconstructing a single forward muon is often sufficient to isolate the
Z
-fusion component of VBF, separating it from the dominant
W
-fusion background. This not only improves precision in Higgs–
Z
coupling measurements but also enhances sensitivity to new physics in VBF topologies.

Beyond the specific case of VBF Higgs production, asymmetric beam configurations offer a general framework to recover physics in otherwise inaccessible regions of phase space. They warrant broader consideration in collider design discussions, especially in scenarios constrained by detector geometry or shielding requirements. These results motivate further exploration of asymmetric beams in other forward-dominated processes, such as vector boson scattering and di-Higgs production.
