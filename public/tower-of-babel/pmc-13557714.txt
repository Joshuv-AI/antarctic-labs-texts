A computational framework for proteome-wide target profiling of natural products: mechanistic and therapeutic insights into ginsenosides
Authors: Dohyeon Kim, Charuvaka Muvva, Jung-Seok Yang, Hak Cheol Kwon, Cheol-Ho Pan, Kyungsu Kang, Taejung Kim, Ki Cheong Park, Keunwan Park
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractNatural products (NPs) serve as valuable sources of pharmacologically active compounds, yet their therapeutic potential remains constrained by incomplete knowledge of their molecular targets. To address this, a computational framework based on evolutionary chemical binding similarity (ECBS) is developed to generate proteome-wide NP-target binding profiles. By integrating evolutionarily related ligands and optimizing chemical pairing strategies, the framework constructs target-specific ECBS models covering 6203 protein targets with enhanced predictive performance. As a proof of concept, this approach is applied to ginsenosides, a structurally diverse NP class with broad pharmacological relevance. The analysis reveals comprehensive ginsenoside–target associations, enabling functional clustering, inference of mechanisms of action, and identification of chemotype-specific targets. Experimental validation confirms a novel direct interaction with protein kinase C isoforms by a direct binding assay, and provides phenotypic (indirect) evidence consistent with activity at the sarcoplasmic/endoplasmic reticulum calcium adenosine triphosphatase (SERCA), demonstrating the practical utility of the approach. To enhance data accessibility and exploration, GinsenBank (http://ginseng.aslla.net) is developed as an open web resource providing predicted binding profiles, molecular clustering, multi-target analysis, and drug similarity assessment. These findings highlight the potential of proteome-scale NP-target profiling to accelerate the discovery of novel therapeutic applications for NPs.

Introduction

Natural products (NPs) have historically played a crucial role in drug discovery due to their diverse pharmacological activities [1]. NP research has traditionally relied on activity-guided approaches, where bioassays identify extracts or compounds with bioactivity [2–4], accumulating data from the NP-activity perspective as seen in Natural Product Activity and Species Source (NPASS) [5] or COlleCtion of Open Natural prodUcTs (COCONUT) [6] databases.

However, while effective for identifying bioactive NPs, this approach struggles to identify molecular targets, which are essential for elucidating mechanisms of action (MoAs), predicting off-target effects, and designing rational derivatives [7, 8]. For instance, ginsenoside Rd, a saponin derived from Panax ginseng, has been shown to exert protective effects in muscle wasting conditions, and its MoA directly binding to signal transducer and activator of transcription 3 (STAT3), a transcription factor involved in inflammation and muscle catabolism, was proposed by computational target prediction followed by an experimental validation [9]. This interaction provided a mechanistic explanation for its therapeutic potential.

Another example is a carbohydrate-lectin interaction in immune regulation, where lectins bind glycans with low affinity (mM range) to regulate phagocytosis, cell adhesion, and cancer immunotherapy [10]. Although traditional NP discovery often overlooks weak interactions, emerging evidence shows that weak, multivalent interactions frequently drive complex biological activities [11, 12]. These findings highlight that weak yet specific NP-target interactions can yield meaningful biological consequences when amplified by avidity or downstream signaling. Consequently, mapping comprehensive NP-target-activity networks is critical for modern NP-based drug discovery. Since experimental proteome-wide validation of these intricate interactions is unfeasible, robust computational approaches are essential. Demonstrating their utility, our recent work employed computational methods to identify the potential binding targets of fucoidan, providing a theoretical framework to explore its MoA and proposing a target-directed strategy for immune modulation and drug delivery [13].

In this study, we developed a method for proteome-wide target-binding profiles (TBPs) by constructing multiple evolutionary chemical binding similarity (ECBS) [14] models. Extending our previous work [15], we re-trained models using evolutionarily related chemical pairs with validated inactive and random negative (rN) data. We found that diversifying chemical pairing schemes substantially improved predictive performance. Implementing this optimized strategy across 6203 targets allowed for the probabilistic profiling of target interactions for any given compound. Each TBP is defined as a high-dimensional vector of these predicted probabilities, providing a quantitative landscape of the compound’s global interactions (Fig. 1A). We hypothesize that this profile represents functional relatedness beyond structural similarity, enabling MoA inference and functional clustering. TBP analysis of an NP class can also suggest chemotype-specific targets and novel activities.

Schematic workflow for predicting the functional and therapeutic effects of NPs based on TBPs; (A) ECBS models, each trained on 6203 protein targets, were applied to a chemical compound to generate TBPs that mimic proteome-scale chemical perturbations; the TBPs were then used to identify primary targets underlying the known biological activities of NPs and to compare them based on TBP correlations, representing their overall similarity in terms of target binding; (B) for a given NP class, comparative analysis of TBP matrix enables the categorization of targets into common targets (those contributing to shared biological functions) and specific targets (those uniquely associated with distinct NP subgroups); the target information derived from TBPs was then used for subsequent functional target analyses, disease association studies, and comparisons with FDA-approved drugs to elucidate target-based NP–disease relationships.

To demonstrate the utility of this system, ginsenosides were selected as a representative NP class due to their structural diversity, well-characterized biological activities, and significance in NP research. TBP analysis showed that functional similarities among ginsenosides are better captured by TBPs than by chemical structure alone, supported by literature co-occurrence patterns. Mapping disease associations via these predicted targets suggested novel biological roles for ginsenosides, which we subsequently confirmed through experimental validation by identifying the sarcoplasmic/endoplasmic reticulum calcium adenoside triphosphatase (SERCA) and protein kinase C (PKC) families as direct targets. These results demonstrate that proteome-wide TBPs link NPs to disease-associated targets and predict unexplored therapeutic activities. We also constructed the GinsenBank database (http://ginseng.aslla.net) to facilitate analysis.

Results and discussion

Overview of a target-based approach for natural product research

Most NP studies rely on bioactivity-guided approaches, which are often limited by their inability to identify molecular targets underlying MoA [2]. Since many NPs affect multiple targets or pathways [16], pinpointing primary functional targets remains challenging. We therefore investigated proteome-wide NP-target interactions by deploying 6203 target-specific ECBS models to conduct comprehensive TBP analysis.

A TBP consists of probability scores estimating the binding propensities of a query compound across 6203 protein targets, predicted using pre-trained ECBS models optimized through diversified chemical pairing schemes. Analyzing TBPs uncovers binding targets linked to known bioactivities and disease relationships beyond chemical similarity (Fig. 1A). This approach hypothesizes that NPs with similar TBPs exert similar biological activities by capturing functionally meaningful binding characteristics, regardless of chemical structure.

Molecular targets identified within the TBPs of an NP class are stratified by average prediction scores into “common targets” for the class and “specific targets” for subgroups clustered by TBP similarity (Fig. 1B). Common targets exhibit consistently high scores across the class, while group-specific targets show elevated scores only within certain subgroups, reflecting binding preferences from unique structural features. Both target sets were analyzed for biological functions, associated diseases, and similarities to known drugs to provide insights into the MoA of each NP class (Fig. 1B).

Optimization of evolutionary chemical binding similarity models to calculate target-binding profile

To construct TBP prediction models, chemical-target binding data from ChEMBL and BindingDB were integrated into a training dataset covering 6203 targets. Target-specific ECBS models were trained using diversified chemical pairing schemes to enhance prediction performance (Fig. 2), refining the original method [15] by expanding chemical pair data and incorporating experimentally confirmed true negatives alongside rNs.

Performance of re-trained ECBS models with diversified chemical pairing schemes; (A) a simplified example illustrating the evolutionary relationships of chemicals, defined by their targets and protein family information; T1 represents a target of interest, while T2 represents a target belonging to the same protein family; the chemicals are categorized into active compounds (P), evolutionarily related compounds (fP), and randomly selected inactive compounds (rN); those compounds were paired in different combinations and used to build ECBS models to test prediction performance; (B) the predictive performance of ECBS models trained with diverse chemical pairing data was evaluated for each test target using the PR-AUC and visualized as boxplots; for comparison, QSAR-like binary classification models based on single compounds (P or fP versus rN) were also constructed; five different chemical pairing schemes were combined (marked as “1”) and evaluated using the same test dataset; the composition of the training data for each model is indicated in the matrix below the boxplot, in which each row is labelled by compound type (P, fP, rN) or pairing scheme (P-P, P-fP, P-rN, fP-fP, fP-rN, rN-rN)

Chemical data for each target were categorized as positive (binding the target), family-based positive (fP) (binding a homologous target), or negative (randomly or experimentally confirmed non-binding) (Fig. 2A). Compounds were then paired as evolutionarily related or unrelated to build target-specific ECBS models via classification-based similarity learning. We evaluated combinations of evolutionarily related and unrelated chemical pairs to select the best-performing configurations.

Compared to single-compound representations, chemical pair representation enables more precise tailoring of chemical relationships to specific prediction tasks [17]. Incorporating evolutionary information from homologous proteins into ECBS models provides a baseline for conserved binding features, complementing target-specific data and mitigating sparse training data for understudied targets. For validation, we also constructed a traditional binary classifier from individual compound data and compared its performance with the ECBS variants trained on diverse chemical pair data (Fig. 2B).

Model accuracy was evaluated on 593 targets with at least 10 positive data points (see Methods). For comparison, scores from models trained on single-compound data were converted to similarity scores by calculating score difference, ensuring a test set identical to that of the ECBS models. We used the area under the precision-recall curve (PR-AUC) as the primary metric, as it offers a more sensitive assessment of true binders within a large non-binders pool. Performance variability was summarized using boxplots (Fig. 2B).

Among the models trained with single-compound data, the combination of target-specific positives (P), fP, and rN achieved the highest AUC, whereas the model with only fP and rN showed the lowest performance. In chemical pair models, those including P–fP consistently demonstrated strong performance, further enhanced by adding negative pairs (fP–rN and rN–rN). Models utilizing P–P, P–fP, and fP–rN (with or without rN–rN) achieved the best performance with mean PR-AUC above 0.92 on the rN and above 0.87 on the true negative test sets (Supplementary Table S1). Conversely, models lacking these negative pairing types (P–P, P–rN, fP–fP) showed lower accuracy, indicating that pairing fP with negatives provides critical information for predicting chemical–target interactions. Paired Wilcoxon signed-rank tests confirmed that the model trained with P–P, P–fP, fP–rN, and rN–rN pairs significantly outperformed the single-compound baselines and all schemes lacking this core pairing combination (Benjamini–Hochberg-corrected P < .05). This model exhibited consistently high predictive performance across both random-negative and experimentally validated inactive test sets (Fig. 2B). Its performance also remained consistently high across representative protein families in both test sets (Supplementary Fig. S1), indicating that the improvement is systematic rather than confined to well-studied targets or particular families. This model was therefore selected for calculating TBPs in subsequent analyses.

Target-binding profiling for ginsenosides

To validate our approach, ginsenosides were selected as a representative NP because their target-binding MoAs remain largely uncharacterized, despite their broad biological significance. A literature search of the Reaxys database [18] using the keyword “ginsenoside” revealed 164 associated biological activities, including antineoplastic, anti-inflammatory, and cardioprotective effects (Table 1), and identified 1392 distinct ginsenoside compounds with unique International Chemical Identifier Keys (InChIKeys). However, investigation of experimental evidence for binding affinity (Ki, Kd, and IC50) showed that only 12 protein targets, such as 14-3-3 protein zeta/delta (UniProt ID: P63101), Annexin A2 (P07355), and Programmed cell death protein 1 (Q15116) exhibited Kd values below 3 μM across 14 ginsenosides. Similarly, while a ChEMBL search identified various ginsenoside-related bioactivity assays, studies validating specific target-binding affinity were highly limited (Table 1). To analyze these unexplored target-based activities, we calculated TBPs for 60 representative ginsenosides (Supplementary Table S2) and performed the analyses described below.

Target-binding similarity with approved drugs

To evaluate potential functional similarities, we first identified Food and Drug Administration (FDA)-approved drugs with TBPs similar to those of ginsenosides. Such comparative analyses can facilitate the identification of shared MoAs between NPs and established therapeutics. We applied the ECBS models to 2252 FDA-approved drugs from DrugBank to generate comparable TBPs, using Pearson correlation to quantify functional similarity. The top 50 drugs with the highest correlation per ginsenoside were selected for detailed analysis.

The majority of these highly correlated drugs were steroids or steroid derivatives (Fig. 3A), along with several glycoside-type compounds. This finding aligns with the structural nature of ginsenosides, which feature a steroid-like triterpenoid backbone with diverse sugar moieties. Notably, cardiac glycosides such as digoxin (DrugBank ID: DB00390), digitoxin (DB01396), deslanoside (DB01078), and ouabain (DB01092) showed the highest correlations across all ginsenosides. These drugs act on ATP1A1 (UniProt ID: P05023), which is also identified as a common ginsenoside target below, suggesting the possibility that ginsenosides possess cardiotonic activity mediated by sodium/potassium-transporting ATPase subunits. Overall, the correlation between known ginsenoside functions and pharmacologically similar drugs validates TBPs for predicting or explaining NP therapeutic activities.

Correlation between ginsenoside activity and target-binding

We examined whether TBP similarity correlates more strongly with ginsenoside biological activities than conventional structure similarity. This analysis highlights the functional relevance of target-binding predictions and supports the hypothesis that compounds with similar TBPs exhibit comparable biological activities by capturing functionally relevant binding characteristics. To test this, we scored ginsenoside–disease associations by calculating the product of the TBP-derived binding score and the target–disease association score from OpenTargets [19]. A higher score indicates a greater likelihood that a ginsenoside is linked to a disease through target binding.

To validate these scores, we identified FDA-approved drugs sharing targets and indications with the 60 ginsenosides. The drug–ginsenoside pairs revealed that higher similarity, whether structural or TBP-based, corresponded to greater disease association scores (Fig. 3B). Notably, TBP similarity correlated more strongly with disease association scores (r = 0.79, P = .012) than structural similarity (r = 0.66, P = .053). For instance, ginsenoside F3 had a high association score (0.9429) with atrial fibrillation via ATP1A1. Ouabain, an approved ATP1A1 inhibitor for atrial fibrillation, showed high TBP similarity with ginsenoside F3 (TBP similarity 0.967) despite a very low structural similarity (Tanimoto coefficient 0.216).

To further validate our predictions, we analyzed ginsenoside–disease associations using literature co-occurrence in PubMed. The results demonstrated a positive correlation between higher predicted association scores and increased co-occurrence frequencies (Fig. 3C and Supplementary Tables S3 and S4), confirming that TBPs effectively capture functional information relevant to ginsenoside activity and associated diseases. These results indicate that TBP-encoded interactions and derived disease association scores are consistent with both known ginsenoside bioactivities and clinical evidence.

Characterization of common targets of ginsenosides

Having analyzed the overall relationships between TBPs and biological functions, we next investigated predicted targets to elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying ginsenoside activity. Common targets with consistently high TBP scores across the 60 ginsenosides were prioritized by average rank (Supplementary Table S5). The top 50 targets (Fig. 4) underwent protein class, gene enrichment, and target–disease network analyses to identify associated Gene Ontology (GO) terms and potential therapeutic indications.

Hierarchical protein class analysis of common targets

To systematically map the functional landscape of ginsenoside targets, predicted common targets were categorized into a hierarchical classification system (Fig. 4A). The pharmacological space is predominantly occupied by enzymes and primary active transporters, followed by membrane receptors, ion channels, secreted proteins, and surface antigens. Notably, subfamily-level profiling revealed a pronounced convergence on specific homologous families, particularly kinase groups (e.g. AGC, comprising protein kinase A, G, and C families, and CAMK, calcium/calmodulin-dependent protein kinase) and P-type ATPases (e.g. Na+/K+ and Ca2+ ATPases) (Table 2). Given their structurally conserved ligand-binding domains and functional similarities [20], these homologous families represent key mechanistic hubs mediating the polypharmacological effects of ginsenosides.

Within the transporter, the P-type ATPase family (e.g. ATP1A1, ATP1A2, ATP1B1, ATP2A1, and ATP2A2) represents a highly enriched target cluster. These proteins maintain electrochemical gradients essential for muscle contraction, neuronal excitability, and cellular calcium homeostasis [21]. Their modulation provides a direct mechanistic rationale for the well-documented cardio-protective [22, 23] and anticancer [24] activities of ginsenosides, consistent with our disease association profiles (Supplementary Tables S3 and S4). Similarly, the prominent enrichment of the AGC kinase family (e.g. PRKCA, PRKCD, and PRKCH, encoding protein kinase C alpha, delta, and eta, respectively) among enzymes highlights a critical regulatory node. AGC kinases govern key intracellular signaling cascades controlling cell proliferation, apoptosis, and immune responses [25]. Although these high ECBS scores were obtained for the mouse and rat orthologs of the AGC kinase family rather than for the human proteins, this result can be explained by applying the concept of the ECBS. The fundamental premise of the ECBS is that structural and functional similarities between homologous proteins extend to their binding ligands, given that evolutionarily conserved pockets share common 3D pharmacophore features. Accordingly, although these orthologs yielded higher scores because more experimental binding evidence is available for the mouse and rat AGC kinases than for their human counterparts, ginsenosides would be expected to bind to the evolutionarily related human AGC kinase family relatively more strongly than to unrelated targets. Therefore, targeting this kinase subnetwork offers a compelling molecular hypothesis for the broad immunomodulatory and anticancer efficacies of ginsenosides [26–29].

Gene enrichment analysis for common targets

To elucidate the biological roles of common ginsenoside targets, functional enrichment analysis was performed using StringDB (Search Tool for the Retrieval of Interacting Genes/Proteins) [30]. Enriched GO biological processes are primarily distributed across two interconnected functional axes: fundamental cellular homeostasis and the regulation of muscle contractility (Fig. 4B). The most populated categories (highest number of genes) encompass essential transport mechanisms, notably cellular cation homeostasis and regulation of transport. Additional highly significant terms were associated with cardiovascular physiology, including regulation of cardiac conduction, striated muscle contraction, and muscle contraction by calcium ion. This functional landscape reflects the representation of membrane transport proteins, specifically P-type ATPases and ion channels. Collectively, these results suggest a coherent MoA wherein ginsenosides modulate key ion transporters to govern downstream cellular electrophysiology and cardiac muscle dynamics.

Target–disease network analysis

A target–disease association network was constructed to explore the potential disease associations of ginsenosides (Fig. 4C and Supplementary Table S6). The network exhibits a bipartite topology, segregating into cardiovascular and oncological clusters. Na+/K+-transporting ATPases (ATP1A1, ATP1A2, and ATP1B1) serve as central hubs linked to cardiovascular pathologies, including heart failure and atrial fibrillation. This suggests a potential mechanism for ginsenoside-mediated cardioprotection via the modulation of ion homeostasis. Conversely, Ca2+-transporting ATPases (ATP2A1, ATP2A2) and PKC isoforms (PRKCA, PRKCD, and PRKCH) form a dense subnetwork associated with diverse malignancies, such as breast, ovarian, and head and neck cancers. Furthermore, the network captures pleiotropic interactions with aging and neurodegenerative diseases. Collectively, these systems-level associations align with reported ginsenoside bioactivities and clinical indications [26, 31, 32].

Target-binding profile-based clustering of ginsenosides

While ginsenosides share a common steroid-like backbone [33], they exhibit structural diversity, particularly in the sugar moieties at the C-3 and C-20 [34, 35], defining distinct structural subtypes thought to drive functional differences. To classify ginsenosides into functional subclasses, we performed hierarchical clustering based on the pairwise correlations of their TBPs. Based on the resulting cluster tree, we divided the compounds into six distinct clusters with characteristic binding patterns (Fig. 5A and B). These clusters partially align with traditional structural categorizations (Supplementary Figs S2 and S3), indicating that TBPs capture functional factors beyond chemical structures alone.

Functional subclasses of ginsenosides based on TBPs; (A) heatmap of pairwise correlations among ginsenosides based on TBPs, showing six distinctive ginsenoside clusters; (B) visualization of variations among specific ginsenoside clusters based on principal component analysis (PCA) of their TBPs; (C) boxplots of molecular descriptors across groups, including molecular weight, AlogP, and QED (from left to right); statistical significance was assessed using the Kruskal–Wallis test, followed by post hoc Dunn’s test, excluding Groups 4 and 5 due to their small sample sizes (n = 2).

Cluster composition analysis revealed varying degrees of structural homogeneity. Groups 1 and 4 primarily consist of ocotillol (OT)- and oleanane-type ginsenosides, respectively, indicating that for these specific scaffolds, structural topology tightly governs the TBP (Fig. 5A and Supplementary Fig. S2). In contrast, Groups 2, 3, and 6 contained heterogeneous mixtures of protopanaxadiol (PPD) and protopanaxatriol (PPT) types, both of which share a dammarane-type tetracyclic triterpenoid backbone. Nonetheless, Groups 2 and 6 were enriched in PPD-type compounds (62.5% and 100%), while Group 3 had a higher proportion of PPT-type ginsenosides (59%) (Supplementary Fig. S2).

To evaluate the biological relevance of these functional groupings, we examined structurally divergent compounds that co-clustered. For instance, compound K (PPD-type) and ginsenoside Rh1 (PPT-type), both in Group 3, exhibit similar anti-inflammatory and neuroprotective effects [36–40], which are difficult to infer from structure alone. Likewise, ginsenosides Rd (PPD-type) and Rg1 (PPT-type) co-segregated into Group 2, and both are known to exert hepatoprotective effects against acute liver injury [41, 42]. Thus, TBP-based clustering successfully uncovers hidden pharmacological equivalence, enabling the prediction of shared bioactivities across disparate structural scaffolds.

Finally, we assessed whether these TBP-based clusters occupy distinct physicochemical spaces by comparing molecular descriptors including molecular weight, hydrophobicity (AlogP, the atom-based calculated logarithm of the octanol-water partition coefficient), and the quantitative estimate of drug-likeness (QED) (Fig. 5C). The outlying clusters exhibited distinct physicochemical profiles: Group 1 displayed the highest QED scores, Group 6 possessed the largest molecular weights, and Group 5 was highly hydrophobic. Most interestingly, while Groups 2 and 3 both represent PPD/PPT mixtures, they are physicochemically distinct; Group 2 compounds generally exhibit higher molecular weights but lower ALogP and QED compared to Group 3. These findings indicate that TBP clustering implicitly captures fundamental physicochemical determinants that govern the target-binding thermodynamics of ginsenosides.

Characterization of cluster-specific targets and associated disease networks

To delineate the unique pharmacological signatures of each cluster, we identified cluster-specific targets and their associated disease networks. Targets were classified as cluster-specific if they demonstrated significantly elevated binding within a group, satisfying an average ECBS score >0.6 and a margin of at least 0.05 over all other clusters. These cutoffs were chosen to reflect the underlying score distributions: a score of 0.6 corresponds to approximately the top 5% of predicted ECBS values, and a between-cluster margin of 0.05 exceeds the 95th percentile of a null distribution obtained by permuting cluster labels, thereby selecting targets that are both strongly and specifically predicted for a given cluster. A sensitivity analysis confirmed that the identified cluster-specific targets are robust to moderate changes in these cutoffs (Supplementary Fig. S4). This thresholding yielded 110 highly specific targets (Supplementary Table S7), whose functional classifications (Fig. 6A), target-binding scores (Fig. 6B), and disease associations (Fig. 6C and Table 3) were subsequently profiled.

In Group 1, carbonic anhydrases (CAs), a family of lyases involved in diverse physiological and pathological processes [43], were identified as cluster-specific targets (Fig. 6A). The heatmap (Fig. 6B) visualizes the target-binding probability of Group 1 compounds toward these CAs. Network analysis (Fig. 6C) links these targets to nervous system disorders (epilepsy, obstructive sleep apnea) and ocular pathologies (glaucoma). Given the paucity of direct experimental evidence regarding CA modulation by ginsenosides, these predictions propose a novel MoA underlying their polypharmacology, warranting prioritized experimental validation.

The oleanane-type ginsenosides in Group 4 demonstrated specific interactions with G protein-coupled receptors, notably muscarinic acetylcholine receptor M3 (CHRM3), apelin receptor (APLNR), and C-C chemokine receptor type 5 (CCR5), which map to human immunodeficiency virus/acquired immunodeficiency syndrome (HIV/AIDS), chronic respiratory, and urinary disorders (Fig. 6A and C). Specific tyrosine protein phosphatases, such as protein tyrosine phosphatase non-receptor type 2 (PTPN2), also emerged as targets linked to glioblastoma and breast adenocarcinoma. These target-inferred oncology links (Supplementary Table S8) are corroborated by our literature text-mining (Supplementary Table S4); for instance, ginsenoside Ro, the prototypical Group 4 compound, co-occurs significantly with terms such as cancer (10 records) and inflammation (10). Thus, our in silico predictions provide a plausible mechanistic rationale for the observed activities of Group 4 compounds.

Group 5, a minor but physicochemically distinct cluster containing a synthetic ginsenoside Rh2 derivative, showed specific interaction with peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma (PPARG) and delta (PPARD) (Fig. 6C and Supplementary Table S7). This PPAR signaling node connects Group 5 to metabolic disorders, notably diabetes mellitus (Fig. 6C). This target–disease mapping offers a rational starting point for investigating synthetic ginsenoside derivatives in metabolic syndrome therapies.

Group 6, composed of malonylated ginsenosides including malonylginsenoside Rd and Rc, exhibited strong associations with diverse cancers, most notably glioblastoma multiforme, breast adenocarcinoma, and lung adenocarcinoma (Fig. 6C and Supplementary Table S7). The targets underlying these associations included protein kinase C epsilon (PRKCE), C-C chemokine receptor type 6 (CCR6), NF-κB inhibitor alpha (NFKBIA), and endothelial PAS domain protein 1 (EPAS1). Although the chemical and pharmacological characterization of malonylginsenosides remains limited, our literature text-mining nonetheless captured evidence of their relevance to cancer, with malonylginsenoside Rd and Rc, each co-occurring with cancer, in a single record (Supplementary Table S4).

In contrast, owing to their mixed PPD/PPT structural compositions and overlapping TBPs, Groups 2 and 3 lacked strictly defined cluster-specific targets. While no targets unique to Group 3 could be identified, melanocortin 5 receptor (MC5R) and xanthine dehydrogenase (XDH) emerged as the only Group 2-specific targets, with MC5R showing notable associations with various cancers. Furthermore, adenosine monophosphate (AMP)-activated protein kinase subunits (PRKAG1, PRKAA2, and PRKAB1, encoding the γ1, α2, and β1 subunits, respectively), which are key CAMK family members involved in oncology and metabolic regulation, were identified as highly specific targets shared between Group 2 and 6 (Fig. 6C). These predicted results are consistent with the extensive literature reporting thousands of co-occurrences between Group 2 ginsenosides (e.g. Rc, Rb1, Rd, and Rg1) and various cancers, including breast, lung, and prostate cancers (Supplementary Table S4). Importantly, this shared target profile allows us to infer that the relatively understudied malonylginsenosides may also share similar associations with these cancers. Together with the common targets, these cluster-specific profiles demonstrate the utility of TBP analysis to uncover hidden molecular interactions and expand the NP-target–disease interactome.

Experimental validation for novel ginsenoside–target interactions

Our computational model assigned high binding scores to well-established interactions, such as ginsenoside Rh2-mediated inhibition of Na+/K+ ATPase [44] and PPD binding to 14-3-3 protein zeta/delta [45]. Encouraged by this predictive accuracy, we sought to experimentally validate high-scoring, novel targets that currently lack literature support. To identify optimal candidates, we implemented a systematic filtering pipeline: common targets were ranked by predicted binding scores, prioritizing those supported by high-scoring homologous proteins. Targets with previously well-documented ginsenoside associations were excluded via exhaustive PubMed text-mining. Through this process, the PKC family (specifically PRKCD and PRKCH) and SERCA were selected for experimental validation.

Protein kinase C family

Competitive binding assays for PRKCD and PRKCH were performed using 26 ginsenosides (Supplementary Fig. S5A). Affinity was estimated via the percent of control (POC) ratio, where lower values indicate stronger competitive binding. Most ginsenosides exhibited weak affinity at 10 μM. For example, ginsenoside Ro showed POC values of 82% for PRKCD and 94% for PRKCH, while 20(R)-ginsenoside Rg3 showed 88% for both. Despite weak initial binding, ginsenoside Ro and 20(R)-ginsenoside Rg3 were selected for further analysis at higher concentrations to obtain a more accurate measure of binding affinity. Dissociation constants (Kd) were measured using 1/3 serial dilutions starting from 3 mM. For ginsenoside Ro, Kd values were 160 μM for PRKCD and 255 μM for PRKCH (Fig. 7A), providing the first evidence of a direct interaction between ginsenoside Ro and PKC. In contrast, 20(R)-ginsenoside Rg3 exhibited no detectable binding to either PRKCD or PRKCH (Supplementary Fig. S5B).

Experimental validation of ginsenoside activities on PKC subtypes and breast cancer cell viability; (A) binding assays of ginsenoside Ro with PKC subtypes PRKCD and PRKCH; average dose–response curves from duplicate assays were used to determine Kd values; the x-axis represents ligand concentration (nM), and the y-axis indicates relative inhibitory activity obtained from the KdELECT service; (B) cell viability assays in MDA-MB-231 breast cancer cells to evaluate the effect of ginsenosides on cancer cell growth in relation to SERCA activity; eight ginsenosides reduced cell viability below 50% at 100 μM.

While experimental constraints limited testing to two compounds, the detectable binding of ginsenoside Ro suggests other ginsenosides may also interact with the PKC family. Elucidating these interactions could provide insights into the anticancer or anti-inflammatory mechanisms of ginsenosides, particularly given the established associations between PRKCs and diseases such as acute myeloid leukemia [46–48].

Sarcoplasmic/endoplasmic reticulum calcium ATPase

To explore the predicted SERCA-modulatory roles of ginsenosides, we evaluated their anticancer activity using a breast cancer cell line. Because pharmacological inhibition of SERCA is well-documented to disrupt calcium homeostasis and induce apoptosis in breast cancer cells [49], this cell viability assay serves as a biologically relevant phenotypic proxy for evaluating SERCA-targeted efficacy.

Among the 41 ginsenosides tested, eight reduced cell viability below 50% (Fig. 7B). Of these, seven belonged to Group 2 [ginsenoside F2, Rk1, gypenoside (G) L, and notoginsenoside (NG) Fe] or Group 3 (ginsenoside CK, Rh2, and Rh3), while G-A was ungrouped (Supplementary Fig. S6). Notably, Group 3 ginsenosides exhibited stronger cytotoxicity (IC50 4–10 μM) than Group 2 (IC50 10–340 μM). This differential potency aligns with our TBP models, suggesting that Group 3 compounds may possess structural features favoring SERCA-mediated cytotoxicity.

Importantly, these results provide the first experimental evidence for the cytotoxic activity of ginsenoside Rh3, G-L, and NG-Fe against breast cancer cells. While biophysical assays are required to confirm direct binding to SERCA, these phenotypic outcomes support our in silico target-binding predictions as a robust framework for uncovering novel pharmacological activities of NPs.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that proteome-wide computational target profiling can uncover previously unrecognized NP-target and NP-disease associations. By predicting potential therapeutic activities and identifying drugs with similar functional profiles, this strategy provides a systematic framework for generating testable hypotheses and accelerating NP-based drug discovery. Experimental validation confirmed a previously unreported direct interaction of ginsenosides with the PKC-family and provided phenotypic evidence consistent with SERCA-mediated activity. Furthermore, we established GinsenBank (http://ginseng.aslla.net/), a web database integrating 1392 unique ginsenosides and 16 477 associated experimental records from Reaxys database, to facilitate systematic drug discovery (Supplementary Fig. S7). This approach is broadly applicable to other NP classes, facilitating the identification of novel bioactive compounds through target-centric analyses. Despite these advances, limitations remain. Although the predictive power of ECBS has been supported by multiple studies [9, 15, 50], TBP accuracy is restricted by the limited availability of high-quality training data for NPs, as demonstrated with ginsenosides. To improve sensitivity and precision, particularly in capturing subtle structural variations, more comprehensive binding datasets and refined predictive models will be required.

Materials and methods

Preparation of chemical-target binding data

Chemical-target binding data were retrieved from ChEMBL and BindingDB. For both databases, binding affinity measurements (Kd, Ki, and IC50) below a 10 μM cutoff were defined as positive interactions. Compounds were integrated by matching external identifiers and InChIKeys. Affinity values reported with a “>” sign or labeled as “not active” were regarded as non-binding. In total, 5 909 245 binding and 548 712 non-binding data points were collected. A total of 6203 targets with at least one positive binding record were retained for model construction.

Model optimization and performance test for chemical-target prediction

Target-specific ECBS and conventional binary classification models were evaluated using the curated dataset. For performance assessment, we selected targets satisfying two criteria: (i) at least 10 active chemical compounds, and (ii) at least 10 fP compounds (ligands of targets sharing the same family ID). To prevent bias toward large protein families, up to 10 targets per family ID were randomly selected for testing. Family IDs were retrieved from multiple sequence databases, including Gene3D, The Institute for Genomic Research’s database of protein families (TIGRFAM), Simple Modular Architecture Research Tool (SMART), protein families (Pfam), SUPERFAMILY, and PRINTS. In total, 593 targets were selected for evaluation and hyperparameter optimization.

For training, target-specific and fP compounds were clustered using Tanimoto coefficient (256-bit Molecular ACCess System (MACCS) fingerprints), and cluster centers were used as positive training data. The similarity cutoff for clustering Ps was fixed at 0.5, while the cutoff for fPs was treated as a tuning parameter (family clustering cutoff) and tested at 0.25, 0.5, and 0.75 to optimize the contribution of evolutionary information. The number of fPs was also used as a tuning parameter (family multiplier). After clustering, fP compounds were randomly sampled at one-, five-, or 10-fold the number of Ps. Cluster members not used for training were assigned to the positive test set.

Negative chemical data were generated by randomly sampling chemicals from the collected dataset, but only those whose known targets had no evolutionary relationship to the target of interest were retained as negative samples. The number of negatives was fixed at four times the number of positives, consistent with our previous study [15]. For the test set, the same negative sampling strategy was applied. In addition, an alternative negative set of experimentally confirmed inactive compounds was used to compare model performance across different negative data types. The resulting test set was used to evaluate all model configurations, including single-compound versus paired data, with or without fPs, and under different chemical pairing schemes.

By clustering the initial chemical data, the number of training instances was reduced while retaining the most informative data points through uniform sampling. This approach produced a larger test set, consisting of cluster members, enabling a more realistic assessment of model performance. On average, the test set with rNs was 9.86 times larger than the training set, whereas the test set with true negatives was 4.48 times larger. All classification similarity learning models for ECBS and conventional binary classification models were implemented using the “ranger” Random Forest R package with default parameters, except that class weights were adjusted according to the ratio of positive and negative data.

Predictive performance was evaluated using PR-AUC for each model. To compare performance across models, a paired Wilcoxon signed-rank test was applied across the 593 targets, followed by Benjamini–Hochberg correction for multiple testing (Supplementary Table S1). Additionally, performance was stratified by representative protein families based on the ChEMBL protein classification and evaluated separately for the rN and true negative sets.

Calculation of target-binding profile

To predict binding scores using optimized ECBS models, we curated reference ligand sets for 6203 targets, comprising an average of 37 known active binders per target. For a query compound, pairwise ECBS scores were calculated against all ligands within a target’s reference set, and the maximum similarity score was assigned as the final target-binding probability. Consequently, the TBP of each compound is defined as a 6203D vector of continuous values ranging from 0 to 1. Using this pipeline, we generated TBPs for 60 representative ginsenosides using structure-data format (SDF) files from the ChEMBL database [51] (Supplementary Table S2), alongside 2252 FDA-approved drugs from DrugBank for comparative analysis.

Furthermore, to systematically construct the GinsenBank database, we independently collected a broader dataset of ginsenosides from the Reaxys database. Because Reaxys aggregates records from diverse publications and patents that differ in data sources, research institutions, and experimental purposes, the same ginsenoside can appear multiple times. To eliminate such structural redundancies, we filtered the records based on unique InChIKeys, yielding a final, non-redundant set of 1392 ginsenoside structures. Finally, we applied the identical pipeline to compute the 6203D TBPs for all 1392 compounds, thereby expanding the GinsenBank database to cover a much broader ginsenoside chemical space than the initial ChEMBL data.

Target–disease association evidence

We utilized OpenTargets [19] to identify relationships between targets and diseases. Because ECBS predicts physical compound–protein interactions, we focused the analysis on evidence that directly reflects pharmacological modulation (the “drug,” “pathways,” and “systems biology” categories) and excluded genetic evidence, which reflects a gene’s role in disease etiology rather than its potential as a therapeutically engageable target. This prioritizes disease relevance interpretable in terms of compound-mediated effects while avoiding the uncertainty of indirect genetic associations. Using the selected evidence, we recalculated and normalized association scores to a 0–1 range using a harmonic sum, following the original OpenTargets scoring system.

Incorporating such genetic associations could nevertheless be a valuable complementary strategy in future work. Consistent with this rationale, when genetic evidence is additionally included, genetic disorders that our framework does not prioritize appear among the top associations in Tables 2 and 3. For example, ATP1A2 is associated not only with cardiovascular diseases highlighted in the present study, such as atrial fibrillation and heart failure, but also with familial and sporadic hemiplegic migraine, which is caused by ATP1A2 mutations rather than pharmacological modulation.

Construction of the ginsenoside–disease association network

To map the pharmacological landscape of ginsenosides, we integrated predicted TBPs with validated target–disease association data sourced from OpenTargets. A quantitative ginsenoside–disease association score was calculated as the product of the ECBS target-binding probability and the corresponding OpenTargets association score (both normalized to 0–1). Using these composite scores, a network was constructed, focused on homologous protein families identified as common or cluster-specific targets. To ensure stringency and biological relevance, only the top five highest-scoring diseases were retained for each target node. In the network visualization, node dimensions represent either the ECBS probability (for targets) or the association score (for diseases), while edge thickness is proportional to the calculated target–disease association scores.

Correlation analysis between ginsenosides and drugs

To evaluate the similarity between ginsenosides and FDA-approved drugs, we compared the TBPs of 60 ginsenosides and 2252 drugs using Pearson correlation. Since most drugs interact with specific targets, predicted scores for most targets are low, which can artificially inflate correlation values. Indeed, due to the characteristics of TBPs, where most targets have low scores and only a few exhibit high scores, cosine similarity tends to yield systematically higher similarity values compared to Pearson correlation (Supplementary Fig. S8A and B). To address this, we selected the top 1%, bottom 1%, and a random 1% sample from the remaining targets for each compound to calculate the correlation coefficients. We then identified the top 50 correlated drugs for each ginsenoside, resulting in a unique set of 79 drugs. Additionally, to assess the sensitivity of the similarity metric, we also selected the top 50 correlated drugs for each ginsenoside using cosine similarity and Euclidean distance. Evaluating the overlap of these selected drug sets via Jaccard similarity revealed no major differences in the primary outcomes (Supplementary Fig. S8C). Therapeutic indications, targets, and classes were annotated using DrugBank.

Ginsenosides sample preparation for experimental validation

The 41 ginsenosides for experimental validation were sourced from ChemScene [CK, Ra1, Rb3, 20(S)-Rg3, G-XVII, F2, F1, F3, F4, F5, Rf, Rg2, Rh1, Rh2, Rk1, NG-R1, NG-R2, Vinaginsenoside R4, NG-Fe, 20(R)-Rg2, 20(R)- Rh1, Rg6, Rg5, Rk3, Ro, Pseudoginsenoside F11, G-A, G-L, G-XLIX], TargetMol (Rb1, Rb2, Rc, Rd, Re, 20-O-Glucoginsenoside Rf, Rg1, NG-Ft1, PPT, 20(R)-Rg3, Rg4), and ChemFaces (Rh3). All compounds had a purity ≥98% and were used without further purification. These were categorized by structural type: PPDs, protopanaxtriols, oleanolic acid, and OT types.

Competitive ligand binding assay for protein kinase C family

Experimental validation of ginsenoside binding to PRKCD and PRKCH was performed using the scanELECT and KdELECT services from DiscoverX [52]. In scanELECT, kinase binding is detected by comparing the amount of kinase captured on a solid support in the presence of both test and control ligands. Inhibition was estimated by evaluating the POC at 10 μM. Dissociation constant (Kd) values were determined using KdELECT by fitting a dose–response curve using the Hill equation.

Cell viability assay for sarcoplasmic/endoplasmic reticulum calcium ATPase

Cell viability was calculated by the 3-(4,5-Dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide (MTT) assay; cells were seeded in 96-well plates at 7.8 × 103 cells per well and cultured overnight to achieve over 80% confluency. Cells were incubated for the indicated times prior to the determination of cell proliferation using the MTT reagent (Roche, Basel, Switzerland; 11465007001) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The viability of MDA-MB-231 cells was measured after treatment with a range of ginsenoside concentrations (0.1 nM–100 μM) to evaluate dose-dependent effects. Absorbance was measured at 550 nm. Viable cells were counted by trypan blue exclusion. The detailed protocol can be found in a previous article [53]. Data were indicated as a percentage of the signal observed in vehicle-treated cells and are shown as the means ± SEM of triplicate experiments.
