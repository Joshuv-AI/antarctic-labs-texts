Molecular mechanisms underlying early functional divergence in snake venom inferred from the genomes of two pitviper lineages
Authors: Andrew J Mason, Pedro G Nachtigall, Darin R Rokyta, Christopher L Parkinson, Felipe G Grazziotin, Inácio L M Junqueira-de-Azevedo, H Lisle Gibbs
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe molecular mechanisms that underlie adaptive divergence in complex traits can be assessed in traits like animal venoms where variation in gene sequence and expression levels can be directly linked to functional divergence in phenotypes. We used novel metrics for measuring functional divergence based on amino acid variation to assess the impact of coding sequence evolution, gene duplication and loss, and expression variation on the divergence in venom between sister species in two lineages of pitvipers.ResultsIn both lineages, coding sequence and expression variation made significant contributions to overall functional divergence whereas genic variation was less important. Locus-specific analyses of two multigene families that encode important venom proteins (serine proteases and metalloproteases) showed that (1) There were differences between lineages in the distributions of locus effect size on functional divergence between Sistrurus (many loci with similar effects) and Bothrops (loci with either small or large effects); (2) A small number of loci were under strong positive selection, but only in serine proteases was the intensity of selection positively correlated with contributions to functional divergence in venom. (3) Patterns of overall serine protease and metalloprotease expression differed between lineages, but there was no association between expression levels of individual genes and contributions to divergence.ConclusionsOur results show that the genetic underpinnings of early adaptive divergence in snake venoms are multifaceted and vary across lineages. Broadly, sequence and expression divergence both have substantial effects on functional divergence and each of these mechanisms has greater impacts than genic variation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12915-025-02465-8.Keywords: Phenotypic divergence, Functional variation, Comparative genomics, Venom, Pitvipers, Bothrops, Sistrurus

Background

Understanding the genetic mechanisms underlying adaptation in complex phenotypes is a central goal of evolutionary biology [1–4]. Questions that are the focus of attention are as follows: First, what are the relative roles of coding sequence mutation and regulatory evolution? Sequence divergence through coding mutations is the primary mechanism leading to novel adaptive functions in proteins and is responsible for many well-known phenotypic variants that represent adaptations [2, 5–8]. However, expression variation arising through the evolution of regulatory elements has also been predicted to have a significant role in generating morphological and developmental adaptations because, unlike coding sequence evolution, regulatory evolution may be less constrained by pleiotropic effects [9–11]. The greater number of genomic elements involved in gene regulation also presents a wider range of mutational targets, which may facilitate the rapid evolution of gene expression [12, 13]. However, the relative contribution of each mechanism to adaptive divergence remains unclear.

A second issue is establishing the relative contribution of large versus small-effect mutations as the basis for adaptive divergence [1]. Early evolutionary theory emphasized adaptation through incremental, small effect mutations [4] whereas more recent empirical work has revealed many cases where adaptation occurs through variants of large effect [14, 15] leaving the role of mutation effect size on adaptive divergence unresolved.

Finally, the extent to which adaptive variation evolves via parallel or convergent genetic processes compared to contingent genetic processes remains an open question [16–18]. Recent genomic studies have demonstrated instances of parallelism in the genetic basis for adaptive traits, yet there remain many cases where adaptive phenotypes have evolved with divergent genomic architectures [18–20]. As a result, additional studies that address the relative importance of each process to the evolution of adaptations are needed.

A challenge to understanding the genetics of adaptation for most traits is that the genotype–phenotype relationship is complex, which makes the relationships between genetic variation and functional variation phenotype hard to identify [21]. Animal venoms are a system that overcomes this limitation because as secreted protein cocktails their composition can be measured and variation in components can be linked to specific molecular mechanisms [22–24]. Among the venomous lineages, the venom of many pitvipers has been broadly characterized in molecular detail due to their medical relevance [25–32]. These studies have uncovered a limited number of specialized venom gene families that generate the proteins that comprise the venom phenotype, and recent reports show that such variation correlates with diversity in diet [33, 34]. As such, these genes lend themselves to focused studies understanding the mechanisms and forces that shape functional divergence. Recent studies on snake venom have demonstrated not only substantial phenotypic variation between species that presumably reflects adaptive divergence, but also in the genomic architecture that underlies this variation [35–38], and the complex nature of selection acting to generate divergence within species [39–44]. Despite these advances, a knowledge gap persists between our understanding of how particular genetic mechanisms generate functional divergence, and the relative roles that these mechanisms play in differentiating venoms at deep and shallow evolutionary timescales.

Assessing the role that different molecular mechanisms play in adaptive divergence requires identifying a common response variable that reflects functional divergence in the phenotype [1]. For protein-based phenotypes like venom or other enzymatic secretions, a possible solution is to decompose the phenotype into a community of amino acid k-mers [34]. These k-mer communities can then be used to estimate functional variation between samples that can be traced to specific molecular sources. When combined with transcriptomic and/or proteomic data, this approach can measure k-mer characteristics like divergence as an additive product of specific molecular sources. Such an approach has recently been used to demonstrate evidence of adaptive variation of snake venoms in response to different diets [33, 34].

Here we use this strategy to evaluate the contribution of different molecular mechanisms to functional divergence in venom between sister species in two pitviper lineages. Specifically, we compared venom divergence of sister taxa in two pitviper lineages belonging to a clade of South American lanceheads (genus Bothrops) and to the North American pygmy rattlesnakes (genus Sistrurus) (Fig. 1). We use genomic and transcriptomic data to assess four main questions: (1) What are the relative contributions of gene sequence variation, expression level, and genic variation to divergence in each lineage? (2) What are the effect sizes of individual loci? (3) What role did selection play in the evolution of venom differences between these sister species? (4) Do the patterns differ between phylogenetically distinct lineages of pitvipers in which venom divergence has occurred between recently evolved species? Our study provides a rare opportunity to directly evaluate the relative importance of different molecular mechanisms in generating adaptive divergence between closely related species. Our goal is to provide a “big picture” analysis of the molecular underpinnings of early divergence in venom that complements more focused analyses of specific mechanisms on venom protein families [45, 46].

Results

Genomic structure of venom genes

The focal Bothrops and Sistrurus species whose venom phenotypes were analyzed in this study are shown in Fig. 1 (three species of Bothrops and three species of Sistrurus; Additional file 1: Table S1). Each pitviper lineage consisted of two sister species and the most closely related outgroup. These species triplets were chosen to ensure that our comparisons between each set of ingroup species were capturing the earliest stages of interspecific divergence in venom in each lineage.

We assembled and annotated high-quality draft genomes for these six species of snakes. Using our approach, we identified > 15,000 genes in each species (Additional file 1: Table S2) including all major venom gene families which are the focus of our study. We supplemented the automated identification of venom genes using annotation software with the manual review of putative venom toxin genes [35, 38, 47, 48]. These efforts yielded many putative venom toxins in line with other studies [35, 36, 38, 39, 49, 50] (Additional file 1: Table S2, Additional file 1: Fig. S1 and S2).

Genic variation was particularly pronounced in four toxin families that make up a large proportion of viper venoms (PLA2s, SVMPs, SVSPs, and CTLs; Additional file 1: Fig. S1 and S2) suggesting an important role for gene family gain and loss as a mechanism for functional differences in venom between species [51–53]. Genes from each of these families are generally localized in a single tandem array on a single chromosome [35, 38, 46, 54].

In terms of specific gene families, SVMP loci were the most diverse. We identified between 22 and 39 SVMPs in Bothrops species with B. cotiara having many more SVMP homologs than B. alternatus or B. fonsecai. We annotated between 13 and 20 SVMPs in Sistrurus with S. catenatus exhibiting the fewest SVMPs and S. tergeminus exhibiting the most (Additional file 1: Fig. S1 and S2). There were moderate levels of diversity in SVSP loci: Bothrops species contained between 9 and 14 SVSPs while Sistrurus species genomes contained between 11 and 16 SVSPs (Additional file 1: Fig. S1, and S2). For PLA2s loci, all Bothrops species exhibited two PLA2s, although only a single PLA2 was orthologous between B. alternatus, B. cotiara, and B. fonsecai (Additional file 1: Fig. S1). In contrast, Sistrurus species exhibited between three and six PLA2s, with an expansion of PLA2s occurring in S. catenatus (Additional file 1: Fig. S2). Finally, Bothrops species exhibited 6–11 CTLs while Sistrurus species contained only 5–6 CTLs with little variation among species (Additional file 1: Fig. S1 and S2).

Genetic mechanisms underlying functional dissimilarity in venom

To assess how different genetic mechanisms contributed to functional divergence in venom, we introduce a new metric—Functional divergence (ΔFD)—for assessing the functional divergence among samples of coding sequences in terms of amino acid divergence. The ∆FD value is a summary statistic for the divergence in amino acid (AA) k-mer communities for any two groups, estimated using the Bray–Curtis dissimilarity index (Fig. 2, Methods, Additional file 2: Supplemental Methods) [55–89]. In simulations of gene families with varying magnitudes of amino acid variation (Fig. 3a), gene duplication (Fig. 3b), and differential expression (Fig. 3c), ∆FD demonstrated a positive logarithmic response to increasing variation between gene families. This response is more linear at lower levels of sequence divergence between paralogs (Fig. 3a and b). This characteristic makes ∆FD most sensitive to early signatures of functional divergence, as would be expected for recently diverged species that are the subjects of our comparisons.

For our empirical analyses, we first calculated ∆FDseq, ∆FDgene, and ∆FDexpr in a hierarchical manner for all venom gene families (Fig. 2, Methods, Additional file 2: Supplemental Methods). Because this comparison includes both single and multicopy gene families, we also compared ∆FD values for four multiple copy venom gene families where gene duplication and loss can both occur. This subset of venom genes provides a set of genes where all three genetic mechanisms can potentially contribute to functional divergence in venom. For additional biological context, we also compared of ∆FDseq and ∆FDgene from venom genes to those of genome wide non-venom genes. In all datasets, we tested for significant contributions for each component of ∆FD treating each gene family as a replicate. For each homologous group of venom genes, we also examined the contribution of ∆FDseq and ∆FDgene as a percent of ∆FDexpr, which we consider the “total” functional divergence because it accounts for the combined effects of all three mechanisms.

First, we considered how functional divergence emerged among all genes that contribute to the venom phenotype. In Bothrops, the median ∆FDseq for toxin families was 0.15 representing a median 43% of the overall functional divergence (Fig. 4a). Sistrurus toxin families exhibited a median ∆FDseq of 0.57 among orthologs representing a median 71% of the overall functional divergence (Fig. 4b). The ∆FDseq values of both lineages were higher for toxins than nontoxins (Additional file 1: Fig. S3); Bothrops nontoxins showed a median ∆FDseq of 0.07 and Sistrurus nontoxins showed a median ∆FDseq of 0.25.

In Bothrops, the median ∆FDgene was 0.23, or a median 72% of the overall functional divergence and explains an additional 29% of the overall functional divergence. The increase compared to ∆FDseq was marginally significant based on a one-sided Wilcoxon test (p = 0.09; Fig. 4a). Genic variation did not significantly increase ∆FD in Sistrurus based on a one-sided Wilcoxon test (median ∆FDgene 0.64; p = 0.29; Fig. 4b), accounting for a median contribution of 75% of overall functional divergence and represents an additional 4% of functional divergence. Like sequence comparisons, ∆FDgene values of both lineages were higher for toxins than nontoxins (Additional file 1: Fig. S3) as Bothrops nontoxins showed a median ∆FDgene of 0.11 and Sistrurus nontoxins showed a median ∆FDgene of 0.27.

Finally, expression had a significant effect on k-mer community divergence with ∆FDexpr being significantly higher than ∆FDgene for Bothrops (median ∆FDexpr 0.40, p = 0.02; median 28% additional variation explained) and Sistrurus (median ∆FDexpr = 0.83, p < 0.001; median 25% additional variation explained) (Fig. 4a, b). In summary, these comparisons suggest that for the overall venom phenotype, sequence, and expression divergence both have substantial effects on functional divergence, but while each of these mechanisms has greater impacts than genic variation in Sistrurus, genic variation and expression may have comparable effects in Bothrops.

Restricting comparisons to the multigene toxin families (CTL, PLA2, SVMP, and SVSP) we observed a different pattern in Bothrops while a similar one in Sistrurus. The median ∆FDseq values and percent contributions were like those calculated for all genes for both Bothrops (median ∆FDseq = 0.15, median of 33% of the overall functional divergence; Fig. 4c) and Sistrurus (median ∆FDseq = 0.64, median of 68% of the overall functional divergence; Fig. 4d). In Bothrops, however, the median ∆FDgene of complex gene families was 0.46, a median of 102% of the overall functional divergence —a much higher value than what was calculated for all venom toxins, many of which are single-copy genes with no genic variation. Despite this marked increase, the change compared to ∆FDseq was only marginally significant based on a one-sided Wilcoxon test (p = 0.09; Fig. 4c) although this could be due to the small sample size of complex gene families. ∆FDgene of complex gene was also elevated in Sistrurus compared to the overall venom (median ∆FDgene of complex gene families = 0.76; Fig. 4d) albeit not significantly (p = 0.56); however, it presented a similar median percent contribution to overall functional divergence (80%).

The effect of expression on functional divergence between multigene toxin families differed as well. In Bothrops, the median ∆FDexpr was lower than the median value of ∆FDgene, (accounting for a median 2% reduction on average to overall divergence) but this difference was not significant (p = 0.10). In Sistrurus, the median ∆FDexpr of complex gene families was higher than ∆FDgene, (median value = 0.95) and marginally significantly (p = 0.06) and contributed, 20% of the overall functional divergence between species. These results show a small effect decreasing functional divergence in Bothrops but an effect of increasing functional divergence in Sistrurus.

Overall, the comparison of the complex CTL, PLA2, SVMP, and SVSP families within these lineages illustrates the variable effects of different genetic mechanisms both among gene families and between lineages. Although genic variation plays a moderate role in functional divergence of venom in Bothrops (Fig. 4a), the multigene families showed substantial divergence (Fig. 4c). Even the response of different gene families varied across mechanisms in Bothrops with families like CTLs experiencing incrementally higher divergence across mechanisms, SVMPs, and SVSPs exhibiting a substantial increase due to genic variation and little effect, or reduced divergence, through expression. Sistrurus, on the other hand, exhibits a more consistent pattern of divergence through all three genetic mechanisms and across all gene families, with similar increases in divergence for all gene families (Fig. 4b, d).

Locus effect size distributions in complex venom gene families

We compared the distribution of ∆FD′gene values for SVMP and SVSP gene families to directly assess the contributions of individual genes to overall functional diversity (∆FD′gene) as a measure of locus effect size on adaptive divergence. There was a striking difference in the shape of this distribution between pitviper lineages with the shape of ∆FD′gene distributions being bi-modal in Bothrops and mono-modal in Sistrurus for both SVMP and SVSP lineages, suggesting lineage-specific “strategies” for generating functional divergence (Fig. 5a and b). Specifically, in Bothrops SVMP ∆FD′gene values were bimodal (p < 0.001) with one cluster of values centered below zero, while the other was centered above zero. This pattern indicates that there is a subset of genes that contribute to divergence (those with positive ∆FD′gene values) while another subset is functionally conserved (those with negative ∆FD′gene values) and does not contribute to functional divergence. In contrast, SVMP ∆FD′gene values in Sistrurus appeared more unimodal and gently negatively skewed with a median centered slightly above 0 (biomodality test, p = 0.07) indicating that most genes are contributing to functional divergence albeit to varying extents. Similar patterns were observed in SVSPs with Bothrops ∆FD′gene values being bimodal (p < 0.002), while Sistrurus ∆FD′gene values formed a more unimodal distribution (p = 0.21) although there was some evidence of local clustering of ∆FD′gene values (Fig. 5b). These results suggest that, for homologs in these two multigene families, each pitviper lineage has evolved different effect size distributions: For Sistrurus, all loci fall into a single effect distribution (“many loci with similar effects” model) whereas for Bothrops the roughly bimodal distributions for locus effect sizes are consistent with a dualistic, small effect/large effect locus model.

Selection and functional divergence in complex venom gene families

We assessed selection on individual loci in homologs that made up SVMP and SVSP gene families in both pitviper lineages. The overall distribution of dN/dS values showed similar distributions across both gene families and across species with most locus-specific values centered around a dN/dS value of 1 (Fig. 6a and b). Specifically, in SVMPs, most gene-specific dN/dS values were estimated as being close to 1 for all species (Fig. 6a, Additional file 1: Table S3) indicating that most loci were evolving under weak positive or negative selection, or neutrally. Few SVMP loci had high dN/dS values indicating strong positive selection (Additional file 1: Table S3) and there were no loci with high dN/dS values observed for B. fonsecai, or S. tergeminus. Distributions of gene-specific dN/dS values did not differ between sister taxa for SVMPs (Bothrops − WSVMP = 277.5, pSVMP = 1; Sistrurus − WSVMP = 81.5, pSVMP = 0.52).

Similarly, in SVSPs most gene-specific dN/dS values were either near or less than 1 for all species (Fig. 6b, Additional file 1: Table S4). B. cotiara exhibited no SVSP homologs with a high value of dN/dS. As for SVMPs, we did not observe significant differences in the distribution of dN/dS between species based on a Wilcoxon test (Bothrops − WSVSP = 56, pSVSP = 1; Sistrurus − WSVSP = 49.5, pSVSP = 1).

Although we did not observe differences in the overall dN/dS estimates between sister taxa, in SVMPs, we observed four instances where orthologous genes showed greater than a tenfold change in dN/dS across Bothrops species and one instance in Sistrurus (Additional file 1: Table S5). dN/dS values were not consistently higher or lower for either species of Bothrops or Sistrurus, as determined with a paired Wilcoxon test (Fig. 6a, Table S5). SVSPs showed a similar pattern: there were several instances where orthologous genes showed greater than a tenfold change in dN/dS in Bothrops and one instance in Sistrurus (Additional file 1: Table S6). However, there was only a marginal species effect on SVSP dN/dS in Bothrops with a trend towards higher dN/dS in B. fonsecai (Additional file 1: Table S6). No such pattern was detected for Sistrurus (Additional file 1: Table S6). Overall, these findings suggest that the intensity and type of selection varies among specific loci and the magnitude of selection on these gene families does not vary by species.

Finally, the relationship between the magnitude of selection on individual venom loci and ∆FD′gene differed between the SVMP and SVSP gene families across both lineages. In SVMPs, we found evidence for a species by dN/dS interaction, which appeared to be driven by a strong effect of species rather than a relationship between ∆FD′gene and dN/dS (Fig. 6c, Additional file 1: Table S7). Specifically, genes with low dN/dS in S. catenatus and B. fonsecai exhibited low ∆FD′gene values, but ∆FD′gene values were more uniformly distributed across all other groups. In contrast, in SVSPs, although we similarly inferred a marginally significant interaction effect (Additional file 1: Table S8), we observed a strong positive association between dN/dS and ∆FD′gene (Fig. 6d, Additional file 1: Table S8). This pattern suggests that positive selection facilitates functional divergence in SVSPs but not in SVMPs in both pitviper lineages.

Expression and functional divergence in complex gene families

The overall expression of specific complex venom gene families tends to differ between species (Fig. 1). We found evidence of differential gene family expression for SVMPs between Bothrops and Sistrurus species, and for SVSPs in Sistrurus only. B. fonsecai showed significantly higher SVMP expression than B. cotiara in Bothrops (WSVMP = 175, p = 0.004; Fig. 7a) while in Sistrurus, S. tergeminus showed significantly higher SVMP expression than S. catenatus (WSVMP = 42, p = 0.004; Fig. 7a). For SVSPs, we did not detect a significant difference between sister taxa in Bothrops (WSVSP = 55, p = 0.40; Fig. 7b) but in Sistrurus, S. tergeminus exhibited significantly higher SVSP expression than S. catenatus (WSVSP = 17, p = 0.002; Fig. 7b). The significant differences in the distribution of expression values for these taxa suggest that overall differences in expression of gene families are contributing to functional divergence in venom.

We observed numerous substantial (> log2) fold changes for orthologous SVMPs in Bothrops and Sistrurus, although few orthologs were found to be significantly differentially expressed as evaluated using DESeq2, likely due to small sample sizes (Additional file 1: Table S9). To test whether one or the other species in a sister pair showed consistently higher or lower orthologous gene expression, we used a paired Wilcoxon test. Here, the paired Wilcoxon evaluated whether the difference between ortholog expression in two species was significantly different from zero. The paired Wilcoxon revealed a significant effect in Sistrurus as most orthologs were more highly expressed in S. tergeminus than S. catenatus (Fig. 7a; Additional file 1: Table S9). This finding suggests that expression divergence in Sistrurus may occur through coordinated regulatory evolution affecting many or all SVMP genes. No such effect was detected in Bothrops (Fig. 7a; Additional file 1: Table S9). Expression patterns in SVSP ortholog comparisons were similar to that observed in SVMPs. Several orthologs were inferred to have greater than a twofold difference in expression between sister taxa by DESeq2 (Fig. 7b; Additional file 1: Table S10). However, few were significantly differentially expressed (Additional file 1: Table S10). Paired Wilcoxon tests similarly showed no species effect in Bothrops, but significantly higher expression of orthologous SVSPs in S. tergeminus compared to S. catenatus (Additional file 1: Table S10).

We also compared gene expression estimates for SVMP and SVSP gene families to their ∆FD′gene values to test whether genes that had a greater contribution to functional divergence through sequence evolution were more highly expressed. As noted above, ∆FD′gene values are derived only from coding sequence variation of orthologs and paralogs in a community and are thus independent of differential expression. This comparison, therefore, allowed us to understand if and how these two key molecular mechanisms interact to generate functional divergence of the SVMP and SVSP families in these species. Particularly, we were interested in testing for a positive relationship between gene expression and ∆FD′gene values, which would suggest that expression disproportionately favors more functionally divergent genes.

For SVMPs, we found no relationship between expression and ∆FD′gene values for Sistrurus (p = 0.11; R2 = 0.06; Fig. 7c, d). We did, however, find a significant negative relationship between expression and ∆FD′gene values for Bothrops (p = 0.002), although the proportion of variance explained remained low (R2 = 0.15). This relationship most likely reflects a lack of low expression in the group of SVMPs with negative ∆FD′gene values (Fig. 7b). Supporting this observation, if the positive ∆FD′gene and negative ∆FD′gene subsets of the Bothrops data are compared independently, we recover a significant positive relationship between expression and positive ∆FD′gene values explaining a low proportion of variation (p = 0.05; R2 = 0.11) and no relationship between expression and negative ∆FD′gene values (p = 0.39; R2 = 0.006).

In SVSPs, we failed to detect a significant relationship between expression and ∆FD′gene values for Sistrurus (p = 0.529; R2 = 0), and we detected a marginally significant negative relationship in Bothrops (p = 0.068) that explained a small amount of variance (R2 = 0.10). In contrast to SVMPs, testing positive ∆FD′gene and negative ∆FD′gene subsets of the Bothrops data independently shows no evidence for a significant relationship between expression and either positive ∆FD′gene and negative ∆FD′gene values. In summary, these results demonstrate that within each gene family, there is little evidence of an expression bias favoring more functionally divergent genes.

Discussion

Coding sequence evolution is a driver of functional divergence in venom

The relative importance of mutations causing protein sequence variation versus regulatory mutations that impact gene expression as mechanisms underlying adaptive evolution has long been debated [2, 9]. A growing number of genomic studies have documented regulatory variants associated with adaptation [90–95]. These findings are consistent with predications that many types of phenotypic evolution will be associated with regulatory mutations that present larger mutational targets than protein coding genes and are less constrained by pleiotropic effects [9, 12, 96–100]. Contrary to this prediction, our results show that coding sequence evolution played a substantial role in functional change in overall venom composition, contributing more than 40% of the relative functional divergence in both lineages studied.

In addition, as predicted ([52, 100], sequence diversification through gene family evolution also played a role in functional divergence, although its overall contribution is no greater than sequence evolution or expression. Genomic studies increasingly demonstrate evidence of gene duplication facilitating adaptation through initial functional redundancy and co-option at both short- and long-time scales [50, 101–104]. Our findings are consistent with this expectation, and genic variation likely mediates some important functional differences. For example, in SVMP and SVSP gene families, we observed numerous paralogs exhibiting similar contributions to functional divergence, suggesting that diversification is both rapid and pervasive. Similarly, we observed that genic variation in PLA2 content explained differences in both Bothrops and Sistrurus lineages, as observed in other pitviper species [54, 105]. In Bothrops genic variation explained the entirety of their venoms’ PLA2 content and in Sistrurus, S. catenatus uniquely exhibited crotoxin-like PLA2 homologs.

The surprising importance of sequence evolution in venom genes may be because several features may make venom genes more “evolvable” than genes that underlie other adaptive phenotypes that show limited divergence [106]. First, the specialized function and generally localized expression of venom toxins to the venom gland means that coding sequence mutations are unlikely to have pleiotropic effects with negative fitness consequences in other tissues. This characteristic leads to reduced evolutionary constraint that allows venom genes to evolve more freely than other non-venom genes which play more significant and centralized roles in other key physiological processes. Such characteristics can explain why we expected and observed elevated divergence among venom toxin genes when compared to nontoxin genes in both lineages. Second, venom is an adaptation that allows snakes to kill and digest diverse prey which often have disparate physiological processes and/or resistance systems that must be overcome [33]. Sequence variation in venom protein coding genes may be required to expand enzymatic function of snake venom toxins, allowing venoms to evolve greater potency and/or effectiveness against diverse prey [34, 107–111]. Together these features (reduced constraint and strong selection) may explain why sequence evolution in venom plays such an important role in functional diversification in venom even at shallow evolutionary timescales.

However, we also note that, consistent with other studies, our results provide indirect evidence for the importance of regulatory evolution in snake venom genes [37, 45, 112]. Increases in overall expression play an important role in generating differences in venom of the species analyzed here. Surprisingly, this shift is general and non-specific rather than being targeted towards loci that contributed more functional divergence between species as is evident in the lack of a relationship between locus-specific values of ∆FD′gene and locus-specific levels of expression in SVMP and SVP gene families. The reason for this lack of specificity is unknown, but we suggest that expression in these families may act to optimize other functional properties of venom such as, for example, overall diversity, greater efficiency, or toxicity.

Role of selection in venom divergence

Venoms have long been regarded as rapidly adapting phenotypes in part because of the pervasive positive selection that has been shown to act upon gene families [113–116]. However, as greater resolution genomic data have become available, evidence has accrued that contemporary selection on venom toxins take diverse forms including purifying and balancing selection, and the documentation of loci evolving under neutral process [39, 40, 44]. Our findings support these results and argue against a primary role for pervasive positive selection as the driver of venom divergence, especially across recent evolutionary time. For example, we observed only a small proportion of SVMP and SVSP genes showing evidence of strong positive selection—only SVSPs showed evidence for a positive relationship between strength of selection and functional divergence in SVSP proteins. No such relationship was detected in SVMPs, which suggests that genes under neutral or negative selection still contribute to functional divergence in venom proteins.

There may be multiple reasons for a lack of evidence supporting pervasive positive selection driving early functional divergence. Venoms are key life-history characters and are expected to respond rapidly to selection, but it may be that only one or a few components of the composite phenotype are needed to “fine tune” a venom to solve a new ecological “problem” [110]. Additionally, although venom duplication is often associated with diversification, the proximate selection pressure favoring duplication may be an effect like increased expression [46, 99, 117], in which case duplicated genes would likely experience relaxed constraint rather than positive selection. Despite the recent increases in genomic and taxonomic representation in venom systems, our findings illustrate the substantial gap that still exists between understanding how the ecological and selective pressures species populations experience translate into macroevolutionary patterns of venom variation.

Convergent and contingent patterns in genic variation and expression

A key question in evolutionary biology is whether functionally similar adaptations arise through parallel or convergent processes or follow distinct (contingent) evolutionary pathways in different lineages [118]. Our lineage-by-lineage comparison of venom evolution suggests that at broad evolutionary scales (i.e., in the snake venom system) similar mechanisms act as generators of functional divergence while the contributions of specific mechanisms differ between lineages. Many genomic studies have documented the re-use of specific loci and even molecular variants in adaptation through convergent or parallel evolutionary processes [20, 119–121]. Broadly, we observed similar patterns in the role of the different molecular mechanisms in each lineage. Coding sequence variation alone established > 40% of the overall functional divergence in both lineages, with expression having a similar effect increasing the proportion of variance. These findings are consistent with generally conserved roles of the various molecular mechanisms (coding sequence variation, regulatory evolution, and genic variation) in the pitviper venom system.

Nonetheless, the relative effects of some mechanisms varied between lineages, which support a role of contingency in functional divergence. Contingency is expected to be more common across both deep time and scenarios with less selective constraints, for example, biological systems with greater genetic redundancy [16, 18, 122]. Our comparisons fit both criteria as our focal lineages diverged at least 15 mya by many estimates [33, 53, 114, 123–126] and the expansive toxin families create genetic redundancy [52, 53, 127, 128].

In line with these expectations, the relative role of gene duplication and expression was quite variable across toxin families. The PLA2 toxin gene family provides an interesting case study, as functional divergence of PLA2s in Bothrops was solely a function of genic variation, but all three genetics mechanisms played a role in the Sistrurus PLA2 gene family. The strong effects of all three mechanisms in Sistrurus PLA2s may reflect the functional versatility of these proteins which can exhibit an array of enzymatic functions [129, 130]. Although the relatively small number of individual genes sampled here limits our ability to identify broadly selective pressures affecting PLA2s, their functional diversity and near ubiquitous presence in snake venoms make them an ideal candidate group for a macro-scale evolutionary investigation.

In terms of gene expression, there were similar differences observed within SVMP and SVSP gene families. In both toxin families, orthologous toxins were consistently higher expressed in S. catenatus compared to S. tergeminus whereas in Bothrops the specific variations in gene expression were much more variable between taxa. These differing patterns suggest that although expression is facilitating divergence in both lineages, it may be playing subtly different roles and possibly evolving through different types of mutations. Specifically, the higher expression variability in Bothrops may reflect a series of small, gene-specific mutations fine-tuning expression. Differences in Sistrurus, on the other hand, could emerge through mutation in a common regulatory element that alters the expression of numerous genes in a tandem array [37]. Our limited data set precludes an effective test of these hypotheses but would be a valuable addition in the future.

Although this study focused on comparing recently diverged sister species to minimize phylogenetic effects, there are many uncontrolled factors that could underlie the contingent patterns we observed. For example, though sister taxa in both lineages were recently diverged, S. catenatus and S. tergeminus were estimated to diverge 1–2 million years earlier than B. cotiara and B. fonsecai. In the scale of evolutionary time, 1–2 million years is a small amount of time, but when considered relative to the species’ total divergence it is non-trivial. As such, we cannot rule out the possibility that the roles of our focal evolutionary mechanisms shift after approximately 3 million years.

Alternatively, the observed patterns of contingency might reflect other lineage-specific characteristics. For example, differences in the lineages’ historical population sizes and/or genetic connectivity which affect genetic diversity and genomic background could limit the effectiveness of some mechanisms [131, 132]. Lineage-specific differences in ecology and selection pressures might also lead to different genetic responses [17, 98]. Determining which if any of these characteristics affects the evolutionary function of different genetic mechanisms will require much more substantial lineage sampling and phylogenetically controlled approaches but would be a promising avenue for investigation.

Genomic tools for assessing phenotypic divergence.

A gap in studies of the molecular basis of adaptations is linking specific mutations with their functional consequences [1]. For traits that can be plausibly represented by amino acid diversity, the ∆FD and ∆FD′ metrics represent a novel approach that explicitly link phenotypic diversity to variation in gene sequences. These metrics can be estimated in a hierarchical manner to assess the relative contribution of different molecular mechanisms that potentially underlie this variation. Further, they can be readily calculated from any community of amino acids and have specific predictions associated with their values, which makes them comparable across a variety of biological scenarios. We use these values to compare functional divergence among venoms, but similar approaches could be extended to comparing divergence among tissues or various experimental treatments. We note that this approach still makes significant assumptions about the links between amino acid composition and physiological function of venom.

For example, we have used divergence in the translated transcriptome communities as a surrogate for the divergence in the venom phenotype, due to the expected correlation between transcriptome and proteome in snake venoms [24]. Such a relationship may not be true for many systems, and the extent to which our metric of “functional divergence” captures ecologically meaningful variation is unclear. Additionally, our approach does not account for mechanisms of variation like post-transcriptional or post-translational modification, which may have effects even in venom systems [133]. Nonetheless, we expect these novel metrics will have utility in the era of comparative genomics by establishing a common basis for measuring putatively functional variation in many systems.

Finally, we point to the need to more thoroughly investigate how the ∆FD metric used here responds to different types of k-mer variation that, if different between species could introduce bias into measures of divergence. For example, three type of variation that could hypothetically have this effect are (1) “end” effects whereby a concentration of nonsynonymous substitutions at the ends of genes have a disproportionate impact on k-mer divergence; (2) indel effects in which indels have a smaller impact on ∆FD values than do nonsynonymous substitutions (Additional file 1: Fig. S6) and; 3) “spacing” effects in which the spacing between mutations also could impact ∆FD values (Additional file 1: Fig. S7). These instances show subtle mathematical biases of ∆FD, but at a finer scale than what we investigate here. A useful approach would be to conduct studies with simulated datasets to explore the relative impact that each of these phenomena has on ∆FD metric as a way of understanding if differences in phenomena are present between the species being compared how they can introduce bias into estimates of functional divergence. Empirically, we have explored whether some of these phenomena are present in our data sets, and we find that the impacts on measures of differentiation are negligible. This seems reasonable because the process of mutation which generates this variation is likely similar between these closely related species and so seems unlikely to us to generate biases in real-world datasets.

Conclusions

Through the comparative genomic analyses of two pitviper lineages, we dissected the genetic underpinnings of early functional divergence in an adaptive phenotype. Our results support a prominent role for protein sequence evolution, as a mechanism for functional divergence in venom at recent timescales despite a smaller mutational target relative to expression. These patterns argue for convergence in the role of protein evolution in venom diversification although evolutionary contingency was observed in the analysis of individual genes in diverse gene families.

Methods

Sample preparation

We sequenced and assembled draft genomes and venom gland transcriptomes for B. cotiara, B. fonsecai, Sistrurus miliarius, and S. tergeminus (Additional file 2: Supplemental Methods). Broadly our methods followed those described in [35] for Sistrurus species and [48] for Bothrops species. These data were then combined with the recently generated S. catenatus [55] and B. alternatus genomes [48]. Details on the specimens and datasets used from each species are available in Additional file 2: Table S1.

For each sample, we extracted high molecular weight (HMW) DNA from blood stored in ethanol (Additional file 2: Supplemental Methods). HMW DNA from Sistrurus species was then used to generate Pacbio continuous long read (CLR) and Illumina short-read sequence data (Additional file 2: Supplemental Methods). HMW DNA from each Bothrops species was used to generate PacBio HiFi reads for genome assembly. PacBio genomic sequence data for all species was generated at the University of Delaware Sequencing and Genotyping Center and short-read genomic data for Sistrurus species was generated at the Florida State University (FSU) College of Medicine’s Translational Science Laboratory.

For functional genome annotation and to estimate expression of venom and other genes, RNA was extracted from snake venom glands following methods described in [60] (see Additional file 2: Supplemental Methods for further details). Briefly, venom glands were harvested from euthanized specimens 4 days after venom extraction and stored in RNAlater. Total RNA was extracted from the gland following a standard Trizol extraction protocol and converted to cDNA using the NEB magnetic bead mRNA isolation kit and NEB Next Ultra I cDNA library prep kit (Additional file 2: Supplemental Methods). The cDNA libraries for Sistururus specimens were sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 at the FSU College of Medicine’s Translational Science Laboratory and libraries for Bothrops specimens were sequenced on an Illumina Hiseq 1500 at the Instituto Butantan sequencing facility.

Bioinformatic processing

Illumina short-read data were first trimmed and quality-filtered with trimgalore! v0.6.4 [58]. Short-read RNAseq data were additionally merged by their 3’ ends using PEAR v0.9.6 to aid in transcript assembly [59].

Reference genomes for the Sistrurus species were assembled using the MaSuRCA v4.3.1 hybrid genome assembler [63] on the Ohio Super Computing cluster [64]. We then used RagTag v.2.1.0 [66] to scaffold the S. miliarius and S. tergeminus assemblies to the S. catenatus genome, a near chromosome-level assembly derived from Pacio CLR and HiC data (Additional file 2: Supplemental Methods). Bothrops HiFi data were assembled using hifiasm v0.16.1 [67] and polished using Inspector v1.0.1 [134].

For all species, venom gland RNA seq data were assembled as transcripts for genome annotation following [62] (Additional file 2: Supplemental Methods). Species-specific repeat libraries were inferred with RepeatModeler v.2.0.1 and then used for annotation and repeat masking with RepeatMasker v.4.1.0 [68]. Sistrurus genomes were annotated using the iterative Maker annotation and AUGUSTUS training strategy used in [35, 38] (Additional file 2: Supplemental Methods). We used a combined set of SWISS-Prot protein sequences from Anolis carolinensis, Ophiophagus hannah, and all Crotalus species (downloaded July 2020) as protein-evidence for annotation.

Bothrops genomes were annotated using the Funannotate pipeline [71], which integrates the ab initio predictors AUGUST, SNAP, GeneMark-ES, and GlimmerHMM to build gene models. We used the venom gland transcriptomic data of the species as transcript evidence and the protein sequences available for the Tetrapoda clade in Uniprot (downloaded July 2022) and NCBI (downloaded July 2022) databases as protein evidence. We also performed the functional annotation step using InterProScan5 v.5.54 [72]. Venom genes in Bothrops were annotated using ToxCodAn-Genome v1.0, a tool designed specifically to annotate toxin genes by considering all genomic features of toxin-containing regions [48].

For all species, venom gene annotations were manually reviewed and curated to ensure the best possible accuracy and equivalence among venom comparisons. Documentation of the general approach and recommendations for toxin annotation review can be found on ToxCodAn’s “Guide to Toxin Annotation” page (documented on the ToxCodAn-Genome “Guide to Toxin Annotation” (https://github.com/pedronachtigall/ToxCodAn-Genome/tree/main/Guide)).

To estimate gene expression, we mapped venom gland RNAseq reads to their respective genomes with HiSat2 v.2.1.0 and estimated read counts with featureCounts [80]. We tested for differential expression among orthologous sequences using DESeq2 v.1.36.0 [82] (Additional file 2: Supplemental Methods).

Homology inference

We designed a bioinformatic pipeline to identify homologous groups of sequences among all genomes, reconstruct the gene family phylogeny, and importantly, identify gene duplication and loss events (Additional file 1: Fig. S8). First, we inferred homologous groups of CDS among each taxon’s genomes using OrthoFinder v.2.5.4 [73] with an inflation parameter of 1.3. We also provided sequences from the Python bivittatus genome [74] to serve as outgroup sequences, especially for venom gene families. Next, gene family trees were inferred based on nucleotide alignments of coding sequences using MAFFT v.7.480 [75] and IQTree v.1.6.12 [76] with automated model selection. We then reconciled gene trees using generax v.2.0.4 [77] to infer gene duplications and losses (Additional file 2: Supplemental Methods). Finally, because previous work indicated that traditional methods of duplication/loss inference could be prone to misidentify incompletely sampled multi-copy gene families [78], we created a custom python script to improve duplication/loss classifications within a gene family based on branch length ratios (Additional file 2: Supplemental Methods). We did this because reconciliation methods are based strictly or primarily on comparisons of gene tree topology to the “true” species tree topology, neglecting branch length information that might improve the accuracy of inference [135].

Briefly, our script identified cases where a gene duplication was inferred based on a species-incongruent topology with a very short internode (here defined as ≤ 10 × shorter than their parent branch) as cases that were more likely a speciation event with incomplete lineage sorting (Additional file 1: Fig. S9). We also identified and corrected cases where especially long branches (here defined as ≥ 5 × longer than their parent branch) descended from a “speciation” event, which likely represented duplications and/or losses in multiple lineages (Additional file 1: Fig. S9).

Homology classification

For downstream analyses, we also classified genes identified in these assemblies by their evolutionary genomics relationships [136]. Here, homologs refer to toxin genes derived from the common ancestral gene which may be shared among all species, occur within a subset, or even single lineage. We use the term gene-specific to refer to metrics inferred for each homologous gene in each species. A critical step for calculating how functional divergence arises through specific mechanisms (see below) is classifying genes within a homologous group as orthologs or paralogs.

Broadly, we defined orthologs as gene copies that are inferred to be “shared” between sister species pairs and whose divergence occurs because of speciation while paralogs are defined as gene copies that occur in only one taxon of a sister pair, due to either a gene duplication or a gene loss event. However, our classification of “orthologs” refers to a specific subtype of orthology that has been alternately defined as “true ortholog” [137], “positional ortholog” [138], and “primary ortholog” [139–141] (Additional file 1: Fig. S10). This concept of ortholog recognizes that, following a lineage-specific gene duplication, a shared genomic context is expected between the original gene and its orthologs that is not equivalent for the derived duplicates [141].

Consequently, in cases of within-species duplication (i.e., in-paralogs), we classify the original, conserved gene copy as an “ortholog,” while the derived copy resulting from duplication is considered a “paralog” (Additional file 1: Fig. S10). We use this nontraditional classification because it recognizes the fact that genomic events such as gene duplications and losses may have immediate phenotypic impacts; a feature which is not considered by the classical one-to-one and many-to-one ortholog classification schema. Functionally, we distinguish between duplicate copies by considering the least diverged, and thus most similar, copy as the primary ortholog while more divergent copies are classified as paralogs [142, 143] (Additional file 1: Fig. S10).

Metrics of functional divergence

A goal of our study was to assess the contributions of different components of sequence-based divergence, either alone or weighted by expression, to functional differences in venom composition between species. To do this, we introduce a new metric—Functional divergence (FD)—for assessing the functional divergence among samples of coding sequences in terms of amino acid divergence. More specifically, FD is assessed using the measures ∆FD and its derivative ∆FD′ which summarize the divergence between two amino acid communities and the relative contribution of individual genes to this divergence, respectively. Together, these metrics assess divergence among amino acid k-mer samples that arise through nonsynonymous substitutions between the coding loci being compared either in sequence divergence alone or sequence divergence weighted by levels of expression. This method builds upon previous work which established a functional link between the amino acid k-mer communities of snake venom and snake diets [34]. This metric can be used in any biological system where phenotypes can be characterized using amino acid diversity.

The ∆FD value is a summary statistic for the divergence in amino acid (AA) k-mer communities for any two groups, estimated using the Bray–Curtis dissimilarity index (Fig. 2a; Additional file 2: Supplemental Methods, Additional file 1: Fig. S11). We chose to use the Bray–Curtis dissimilarity index for two reasons. First, it is a widely used metric with well understood characteristics for measuring differentiation [144–148]. Second, it is calculated using the same primary data as other community ecology metrics that have been effective for modeling venom protein communities by treating amino acid k-mers as “species” [34]. ∆FD is a bounded value ranging from 0 to 1 with low values of ∆FD indicating that two AA k-mer communities are similar while high values indicate AA k-mer communities are different. The derivative value ∆FD′ assesses the contribution of distinct components (e.g., genes) to divergence, relative to the ∆FD of the entire sample. ∆FD′ values are calculated as the expected change in ∆FD if all k-mers associated with a given component were removed from the community (see formula in Additional file 2: Supplemental Methods). Negative ∆FD′ values indicate a given component makes a k-mer sample more similar while positive ∆FD′ values indicate a component contributes to divergence.

An important feature of the ∆FD summary statistic is that it can be used to assess the relative and additive contribution of specific molecular mechanisms (sequence divergence, gene gain and/or loss, and expression variation) on overall divergence in venom estimated (Fig. 2). In this framework, the ∆FD of sequence divergence is considered nested within the ∆FD of sequence divergence and genic variation. Similarly, ∆FD of sequence divergence and genic variation is considered nested within ∆FD of sequence divergence, genic variation, and expression.

For example, to estimate the impact of gene gain or loss on functional divergence we can compare ∆FD based only on orthologous gene sequences from a given homologous group (∆FDseq; Fig. 2a) with the level of divergence that arises through sequence divergence and gene duplication and loss (genic variation) combined (∆FDgene). Finally, the abundance of a given k-mer can be weighted by its estimated expression, a metric which evaluates how differential expression affects k-mer community divergence (∆FDexpr). To explore these characteristics, we performed a limited set of simulations varying the magnitude of the effect for the general mechanisms underlying ∆FDseq, ∆FDgene, and ∆FDexpr (Additional file 2: Supplemental Methods).

To calculate ∆FDseq, ∆FDgene, and ∆FDexpr for each of our sister taxon comparisons, we first extracted and translated coding sequences for species from each genome. Next, following [34], we reduced each AA sequence to its composition of AA 20-mer and combined AA-k-mers based on orthogroup assignment (Additional file 2: Supplemental Methods). We then calculated ∆FDseq, ∆FDgene, and ∆FDexpr as described above using TPM10K [84] as the weighting metric for expression level. As a precaution to ensure our inferences were based on genetic sources that contributed to venom phenotypes (as opposed to serving other physiological functions), we restricted our comparisons to genes that showed some level of species-specific gene expression in at least one individual represented in our RNAseq dataset. The final Bray–Curtis dissimilarity index was calculated using the vegdist function of the vegan package in R [83].

To better understand the relationships among mechanisms associated with genetic variation and our metrics of functional divergence, we wrote a python script to simulate different magnitudes of sequence variation, gene duplication, and differential gene expression. For each simulation, we calculated functional divergence and then evaluated the behavior of these metrics in relation to features of gene sequences and expression divergence. In the first scenario, we simulated random amino acid sequence variation across pairs of sequences 139, 259, and 600 amino acids in length allowing 1–100% of sites to vary between them. These sequence lengths represent small, medium, and large venom components (e.g., PLA2s, SVSPs, and SVMPs, respectively). Divergent sites were chosen at random from a uniform distribution and functional divergence was calculated from the resultant AA kmer communities. We simulated 10 replicates for each length and each percent divergence.

In the second scenario, we started with two sequences with 10% of differing sites. We then allowed between 1 and 20 random gene duplications to occur, with each duplicate gene accruing 10% divergence in amino acid sequence from its parent. Amino acid sequences were simulated with a length of 600 sites to match snake venom metalloproteinases. Gene duplications were ascribed to genes within each simulated family randomly and then diverged from their parent gene by a proportion of sites ranging from 0–0.4. Divergent sites were chosen at random from a uniform distribution and functional divergence was calculated from the resultant AA kmer communities.

In the third scenario, we started with two gene families with 5 paralogs each with 10% of sites differing between gene families. We then simulated differential expression between gene families ranging from 6 to 102,400 increasing by factors of two (e.g., 100, 200, 400 …). The base gene families each included 5 paralogs (600 amino acids long with 10% of amino acid sites differing between families). Expression of genes in gene family one was uniformly assigned a value of 100, and the expression of paralogs in gene family two was determined by the simulated difference in expression.

Comparing contributions of sequence, genic, and expression variation

To test how different genetic mechanisms contribute to functional divergence of venom gene families, we compared ∆FDseq, ∆FDgene, and ∆FDexpr values within each lineage. Specifically, we compared ∆FDseq and ∆FDgene to assess the impact of genic variation arising from gene gain and/or loss using Wilcoxon tests. To test for a significant contribution of gene expression on divergence, we compared ∆FDgene and ∆FDexpr within each lineage using Wilcoxon tests. To estimate the relative impact of different genetic mechanisms to overall functional variation, we also calculated the percent of ∆FDexpr, accounted for by either ∆FDseq or ∆FDgene for each homologous group. These values represent the percent contributions to functional divergence of sequence evolution alone and sequence evolution integrated with gene gain/loss, respectively. We then summarized the contribution for each mechanism on the divergence of venom by taking the median of the percent contributions. These calculations and comparisons include all genes (including single copy genes) contributing to the venom phenotype. To gain a better understanding of the role of gene gain and loss, we conducted the same analyses on a reduced dataset consisting of complex venom multi-gene families that are known to be highly expressed in viper venoms, namely CTLs, PLA2s, SVMPs, and SVSPs.

Finally, since venom gene products are secreted proteins, we expected they might experience reduced selective constraint and higher functional divergence when compared to gene products involved in cell, tissue, and/or organ maintenance. To test this and provide context for interpreting the venom-specific results, we compared ∆FDseq and ∆FDgene, distributions of venom toxin gene families to those of nontoxin gene families for each lineage. Since our RNAseq data was derived only from venom gland tissue, we excluded comparing the ∆FDexpr for toxins and nontoxins since the majority of nontoxins will serve key functions outside the venom gland and are likely to be lowly expressed in venom gland tissue [85]. As above, we tested for a significant difference between gene groups with a Wilcoxon test.

Tests for selection on coding sequence differentiation in genomic clusters

To test if selection was related to functional divergence, we examined how selection on coding sequences varied among lineages and genes for two large, tandemly arrayed toxin families: the SVMPs and SVSPs. These families are both major components of pitviper venoms which have each expanded from a single common ancestral gene and therefore comprise a single homologous group of toxin genes [46, 85].

We inferred gene-specific values of dN/dS for SVMPs and SVSPs and tested for significant differences in dN/dS and significant differences among conserved orthologs between sister taxa in each lineage. Our goal was to determine if selection, especially positive selection, was a key factor in the evolution of functional divergence of SVMP and SVSP paralogs. An abundance of very high or low dN/dS estimates would suggest that selection is acting strongly to favor phenotypic divergence or conservation, respectively. On the other hand, a distribution of dN/dS values centered around 1 would suggest a significant role for weak selection and/or neutral evolution. We focused on orthologous genes within each gene family because they provide an internal control in the form of comparable loci to examine the consistency in the role of selection through the comparison of dN/dS estimates between sister taxa.

To obtain gene-specific dN/dS estimates, we estimated dN/dS across the gene family with branch model 1 in CODEML [86]. This approach allowed us to estimate selection on species-specific paralogs in addition to the orthologous genes in a species pair. For each gene family, to test for differences between species in the overall distribution of gene-specific dN/dS values we used a two-sided Wilcoxon test in R [87]. We restricted our comparisons to genes in which we observed at least four substitutions to ensure there was mutation from which to draw a reliable dN/dS estimate. Additionally, we specifically tested whether dN/dS differed among orthologs of each species pair with a paired Wilcoxon test. In contrast to an overall test, a significant difference in paired dN/dS would indicate a uniform response in one lineage, as would be expected if all orthologs were responding to a similar selection pressure.

Functional divergence of coding sequence differentiation in genomic clusters

To link variation in adaptive sequence evolution directly to functional divergence, we calculated ∆FD′ values for each gene in a gene family (hereafter ∆FD′gene). ∆FD′gene values were used to measure the contributions of only sequence-based divergence for both orthologous and paralogous genes. In the case of a species-specific paralog, ∆FD′gene reflected the loss of a single sequence, while in the case of paired orthologs ∆FD′gene reflected the loss of both sister species’ sequence.

To test whether selection was related to functional divergence of venom, we assessed the distribution of gene-specific ∆FD′gene values between lineages. Positive ∆FD′gene values would indicate that most genes in a gene family contribute to the functional divergence between species, whereas negative values would suggest that most genes are highly conserved and do not contribute to functional divergence.

We visualized the distribution of ∆FD′gene values due to coding sequence and genic variation in R and tested the modality of each distribution with the modetest function of the multimode package [88]. The test for modality distinguished whether diversity within a toxin family emerged uniformly or reflected different responses among genes leading to a multimodal distribution.

We also tested whether ∆FD′gene values varied among genes with differing classes of selection effects (negative, neutral, or positive selection) to determine whether functional divergence in gene families was facilitated by selection versus occurring through high mutation rates under neutral evolution. If higher ∆FD′gene values were significantly associated with higher dN/dS values, this would suggest that selection is favoring functional divergence among venom genes. A lack of a relationship between ∆FD′gene and dN/dS would suggest that functional divergence may occur through neutral evolution or relaxed selection rather than selection favoring gene diversification. We tested the relationship between ∆FD′gene and dN/dS in SVMPs and SVSPs with a linear regression summarized using the Anova function of the car package in R [89].

Tests for differential expression in genomic clusters

We tested how gene expression impacted functional divergence by comparing how gene expression varied among genes and species for the SVMP and SVSP gene families. Differing distributions of gene expression would support the idea that functional divergence occurs between broad differential expression of gene families. The lack of a difference in expression distributions would instead suggest that either little differentiation occurred between lineages, or that while the expression of specific genes may differ between species, the average expression of genes within a family was stable or conserved. This latter case would suggest a “trade-off” in the expression of distinct genes within a gene family, which may be fine-tuning the final venom phenotype.

We also tested for species-specific expression effects in orthologs to provide insight on whether expression was optimized on a “per-gene” basis or broadly across many genes in a family. Detecting a species-specific effect on ortholog expression would mean all genes in a gene family were upregulated in one or the other species, suggesting that expression is being broadly optimized across the gene family. A lack of a species effect on ortholog expression would indicate a more nuanced, gene-specific role of differential expression.

To test these hypotheses, we obtained relative expression estimates for each gene based on RNAseq read mapping (see above; Additional file 2: Supplemental Methods). We then tested for differences between sister taxa in the average expression of genes in each venom gene family based on center log-ration transformed TPM10K estimates. Differences in the distributions of gene expression were tested with a Wilcoxon test in R, while a paired Wilcoxon test was used to test for a uniform expression response in orthologs.

Finally, we were also interested in whether expression was used to accentuate functional divergence established in coding sequences through elevated expression. To determine if higher gene expression was associated with more functionally divergent sequences, we tested for a relationship between relative expression and ∆FD′gene values with a linear regression. As ∆FD′gene values account only for divergence through sequence differences, a significant relationship between these variables would indicate biased gene expression towards either more similar (negative relationship) or dissimilar (positive relationship) genes (Additional file 1: Fig. S12). A lack of significance would suggest that expression does not disproportionately emphasize genes that have a greater impact on functional divergence. Because we found evidence for multimodality in the ∆FD′gene distributions of Bothrops SVMPs and SVSPs, we ran our linear regressions on the full distribution, and on the positive and negative ΔFD′gene subsets independently.
