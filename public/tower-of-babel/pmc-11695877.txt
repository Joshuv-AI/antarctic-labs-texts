Targeted Heart Rate Control in Sepsis: A Promising Path or a Double-edged Sword?
Authors: Kiran K Gudivada
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractHow to cite this article: Gudivada KK. Targeted Heart Rate Control in Sepsis: A Promising Path or a Double-edged Sword? Indian J Crit Care Med 2024;28(12):1093–1095.Keywords: Beta-blockers, Heart rate, Mortality, Sepsis

In 2017, sepsis accounted for 19.7% of global mortality, causing 11 million deaths and contributing to one in every five fatalities. In India, the incidence of sepsis stands at 540–640 cases per lakh population, resulting in 89.6 lakh cases and a mortality rate of 25–30%.1 Among the strategies explored to reduce sepsis-related mortality, targeted heart rate control using β-blockers has gained significant attention over the last decade. This approach, first studied in 1969, continues to be an intriguing area of research.2

Adrenergic Stress in Critical Illness: Role of β-blockers

Critical illnesses like sepsis, trauma, and burn injuries induce complex physiological changes, impacting hemodynamics, immune function, metabolism, and coagulation. This triggers the release of endogenous catecholamines, often leading to excessive sympathetic stimulation, compounded by exogenous catecholamines used in critical care.3,4 Prolonged adrenergic stress can impair autonomic function, worsening outcomes in multi-organ failure.5 β-blockers have emerged as a potential therapy to address this dysregulation, particularly persistent tachycardia, which remains a key indicator of poor prognosis. By mitigating the harmful effects of adrenergic overload and immune modulation, β-blockers may improve outcomes in critically ill patients.6

Which Subset of the Sepsis Population Benefits Most from β-blockers?

β-blockers have shown promise in improving outcomes in certain subsets of septic patients. Those who present with persistent tachycardia despite treatment of underlying causes seem to benefit the most from β-blocker therapy.7 This is particularly true when the autonomic nervous system is dysregulated, which often occurs in the later stages of sepsis. The optimal timing for initiating β-blocker therapy in sepsis is still a topic of debate. Given the risks of hypotension and impaired perfusion associated with β-blockers, therapy should be considered when the patient is hemodynamically stable, typically after initial resuscitation, and when the catecholamine burden remains high despite other interventions.8

What are the Therapeutic Targets?

Heart rate control remains a key target in the management of critically ill patients, especially those with sepsis. Tachycardia is an independent risk factor for mortality, and managing heart rate may improve survival outcomes. Interestingly, relative bradycardia has been linked to lower mortality in septic shock. This may be explained by the detrimental effects of tachycardia, such as a shortened diastolic phase, which impairs coronary perfusion and increases the risk of ischemia.9,10

While there is no universally accepted target for heart rate control, there is growing support for individualized targets based on the patient's condition and the stage of illness. In patients with early shock, a higher heart rate may be necessary to compensate for reduced perfusion, while later-stage patients may benefit from lower heart rates to improve organ perfusion.11

What Dose and Type of β-blockers have Maximum Efficacy?

Short-acting β-1 selective blockers, such as esmolol and landiolol, are commonly used for managing tachycardia in septic patients. These drugs have a rapid onset and offset, making them an attractive option in critically ill patients. The appropriate dose depends on the patient's clinical condition and response to therapy. A careful titration approach is recommended to avoid excessive bradycardia or hypotension, which can worsen outcomes.7,12

Non-selective β-blockers, although less commonly used, may have potential benefits in certain septic patients. A retrospective study indicated that non-selective β-blockers given before hospital admission were linked to lower mortality in sepsis, highlighting their role in potentially restoring autonomic balance.13

Benefits of β-blockers in Sepsis and Beyond

Studies have shown that β-blockers may offer several benefits in sepsis, including improved cardiac function, reduced myocardial injury, and lower mortality. Patients with brain injuries, trauma, and burns have also shown lower mortality and reduced muscle catabolism when treated with β-blockers.14

Evidence

Heliste et al. showed a long-term mortality benefit from β-blocker treatment compared to placebo or standard care, although no significant difference in short-term mortality was observed. This lack of short-term benefit may be attributed to variability in individual studies, particularly differences in illness severity and potential reporting biases.12 A consistent finding among these studies is a significant reduction in heart rate with β-blocker treatment, compared to controls. However, no significant difference was observed in mean arterial pressure (MAP) at 48 or 72 hours, nor was there any notable variation in vasopressor load at these time points.7,12

β-blockers can induce hypotension and negative isotropic effects, which are unfavorable in critically ill patients. Despite this, earlier clinical trials and physiological reasoning suggest that organ perfusion may be more critical than a specific MAP target. This highlights the disconnect between macro- and micro-hemodynamics, where relying solely on macrohemodynamic parameters such as MAP can lead to misleading conclusions. Microcirculatory dysfunction in shock is a strong prognostic marker for poor outcomes, but assessing microcirculatory flow in clinical settings remains challenging. Reported hemodynamic data often lacked detail, hindering definitive conclusions.4

To improve the reliability of research, more directly measurable outcomes are needed. However, clinical indicators like capillary refill time or mottling scores are highly subjective, and other methods, such as StO2, near infra-red spectroscopy (NIRS), and the peripheral perfusion index, are limited to research settings. Future studies could focus on composite outcomes, acknowledging that this approach may require larger sample sizes.

The absence of large, high-quality randomized controlled trials (RCTs) further complicates the generation of robust evidence in this field. Issues such as heterogeneity in measurements, inconsistent reporting of outcomes, and poor methodology of individual studies make it challenging to conduct reliable meta-analyses. Moreover, inadequate reporting of co-administered drugs affecting hemodynamics, such as steroids, diuretics, and fluids, adds to the complexity. Small sample sizes increase susceptibility to bias, and the lack of published study protocols compounds these challenges.

A significant challenge in critical care research lies in the reporting of outcomes, especially mortality, which is often not the primary endpoint in many studies. This limitation reduces the statistical power of individual studies. However, pooling data through meta-analyses can enhance study power, and advanced statistical methods like trial sequential analysis (TSA) help estimate type I and type II errors, allowing for more reliable conclusions.

In this issue of the journal, I commend the authors for their meticulous analysis of this topic.15 Their use of advanced statistical tools such as TSA, combined with Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) certainty of evidence, adds rigor to their findings. Their conclusion that “targeted heart rate control strategy is not justifiable in routine clinical practice and should be confined to research settings” is well-supported. Also, the “low” certainty of evidence for mortality is reflective of poor methodology in the published literature. Interestingly, while the authors reported statistically significant p-values, the TSA results highlighted uncertainty. The TSA α-spending adjusted confidence interval (CI) includes no effect (0.53–1.15), further reinforcing the argument for a limited impact. Sensitivity analysis also indicated the fragility of the results; for instance, the exclusion of the Ivabradine study rendered the findings non-significant, suggesting a lack of robustness. Furthermore, only two studies in the analysis had mortality as the primary outcome, leaving the majority of studies underpowered to detect mortality-related effects.

On calculating the fragility index of this meta-analysis, it was found to be “1”. This implies that altering the outcome of a single patient in the treatment arm—from survival to death—would render the p-value for the mortality estimate statistically non-significant (p > 0.05). This underscores the delicate nature of the evidence.16 The TSA results, which show that the required information size (RIS) was not achieved, point to a potential for type I error. This indicates the need for a large, adaptive, platform-based, multi-armed trial that includes ivabradine and short-acting β-blockers to provide more definitive insights into this critical question.

Conclusion

β-blockers show promise in managing adrenergic stress in select subsets of septic patients, particularly those with persistent tachycardia and autonomic dysregulation. However, the current evidence is questionable, with low certainty in mortality benefits and a high risk of bias due to underpowered studies. Future research should focus on well-designed, large-scale trials with comprehensive endpoints to clarify their role. Until then, β-blockers should remain confined to research settings, ensuring patient safety while exploring their potential.
