(C

) Complex workload characteristics.
In this work, we focus on large language model (LLM) inferencing. Different LLM use cases could have diverse input and output lengths, and latency SLOs, and could be run with different tensor parallelism (TP), GPU frequency, and load (requests per second or RPS) configurations. The configuration on which it runs decides the fate of the request—the power it consumes and the end-to-end (E
2

(C
3
) Knobs with varying overheads.
The TP and GPU frequency knobs discussed above have overheads to different degrees. While changing GPU frequencies take milliseconds via
nvidia-smi
, changing TP degree could take a few seconds to minutes due to the multiple steps involved—waiting for existing requests to complete, re-sharding the model weights, transferring them to the GPUs involved, and synchronizing the GPUs. Tuning these knobs online in a cross-site setting, where each site could also have power variability, keeping in mind their varying overheads, needs careful planning.

(C
4
) Latency versus power trade-off.
Given the power and compute constraint, one could run the AI Greenferencing system to minimize either the aggregate request latency or the aggregate power consumption. We discuss in §
5
how one could tradeoff latency for power and vice versa. While we leave it to the AI operator to decide on the operating point, AI Greenferencing design needs to be aware of both choices.

Request scheduler.
This component is in charge of dispatching the inference requests, as they arrive in the arrival queue, across multiple sites. It consumes the output of
Planner-S
to learn the weights with which it performs a weighted round-robin (WRR) routing for each class (input/output length) of requests across clusters of GPUs assigned to individual classes across sites. To assign classes to requests, it uses a
Request Class Predictor
that takes as inputs the request and the model, predicts the output length and, hence, the class. Our predictor consists of an Albert/DistilBert/Longformer
(
Lan et al. 2020
;
Sanh et al. 2020
;
Beltagy et al. 2020
)
layer for input embeddings followed by a regressor, and could predict the output length bucket (small/medium/long) with an accuracy of
99.95\%+
(predicting buckets is easier than predicting absolute output lengths). Hence, following the footsteps of DynamoLLM
(
Stojkovic et al. 2025
)
, we treat output length as an oracle in our experiments. The
Request Scheduler
also has a
packing heuristic
that we describe next.

Packing heuristic.
If the
Request Scheduler
observes online that the load for a request class for a GPU instance is lower than expected, it moves a fraction of the smaller requests (e.g., LS) to this class (e.g., LM) for better performance. It starts with the larger requests, reassigns, and move to the smaller ones, freeing up resources at each step.

(

;

;

;

;

explored renewable-powered DCs leveraging solar power, battery, grid draw, and scheduling optimization. Key differences with AI Greenferencing are: (
1
)
Driver:
We aim to unlock more AI compute motivated by the growing power demand. (
2
)
Power source:
We focus on wind energy as solar generation needs to rely on batteries or generators heavily. (
3
)
Right-sizing:
We recommend smaller compute in larger wind farms, a strategy that significantly reduces power uncertainty. (
4
)
Cross-site:
Our work leverages power complementarity across sites. (
5
)
Workload:
AI inferencing is more amenable to power fluctuations than traditional workloads.

Moving compute to power.
Virtual Battery & SkyBox
(
Agarwal et al. 2021
;
Sun et al. 2024
)
use renewable power complementarity across sites to run compute. Differences with AI Greenferencing: (
1
)
Provisioning:
We focus on compute right-sizing and intelligent routing software alongside power complementarity. (
2
)
Scalability:
While SkyBox does not leverage sites with large variations in generation, we could still include them. (
3
)
Workload:
SkyBox focuses on heavier VM placement and migration. We rather focus on intelligent routing of AI inferencing requests.

Power-aware AI Inferencing.
DynamoLLM
(
Stojkovic et al. 2025
)
minimizes energy/power consumption in AI inferencing by tuning TP degree and GPU frequencies. While we build
Heron
on top of this work, the key differences are: (
1
)
Cross-site:
Our work focuses on cross-site routing and not site-specific scheduling. (
2
)
Assumptions:
DynamoLLM assumes no power or compute limitations, while we are constrained on both at wind sites. (
3
)
Approach:
We are power-variability aware.

7.
Discussions

Batteries/generators.
They help alleviate some of the wind power uncertainty at the cost of round-trip loss, hybrid energy mix, and higher CAPEX. Here, we stress-tested
Heron
without using any of these additional modalities and rather leverage right provisioning, power complementarity, and application/infrastructure knobs to tackle power uncertainty.

Beyond wind.
We leave exploring other sustainable energy sources for AI workloads as future work. Solar farms face
\sim
50\%
unavailability, requiring costly battery/generator backup, increasing CAPEX and OPEX. While nuclear energy is on the radar of big AI players
(
Terrell 2024
;
Crownhart 2024
)
for its stable base load, it has its own challenges – (
1
) high CAPEX, (
2
) nuclear waste management, (
3
) public perception and regulations, (
4
) restricted zones, and (
5
) long setup times.

GPU phase-out.
With GPU leaders like NVIDIA announcing a new generation almost every year, hyperscalars need concrete plans to phase-out the quickly aging GPUs. AI Greenferencing could help ship these GPUs to wind farms and run a fraction of the relevant AI workload at low CAPEX.

Autonomous maintenance.
Alongside training farm-local labor on basic cluster maintenance, one could also engage cheap robots in these MDCs for basic repairing tasks
(
Hong et al. 2024
)
.

Elephants versus mice.
In AI Greenferencing, a large wind farm paired with smaller deployments might cause imbalances, requiring reroutes beyond the smaller sites’ capacity. Fig.
2
shows DCs have diverse wind farms within low network latency, enabling sizeable deployments to handle power uncertainty effectively.

Security of the models.
An AI inferencing provider can either deploy their own compute or leverage
3^{rd}
party compute (startups
(
sol 2025
;
win 2025
;
wes 2025
)
co-locating compute in wind farms). With the latter approach, the AI provider needs to resort to privacy-preserving model-splitting techniques
(
Fu et al. 2024
;
Guo et al. 2021
)
.

8.
Conclusion

AI Greenferencing brings AI compute to wind energy sources, bypassing power delivery challenges and meeting rising AI demand sustainably. We present a feasibility study and a right-sizing strategy to mitigate wind power uncertainties. Our cross-site router efficiently manages residual uncertainties, delivering up to
1.8
\times
the goodput of state-of-the-art solutions.
This work does not raise any ethical issues.
