Function evaluation of intelligent interaction voice prompting system on personal protective equipment removal for medical staff
Authors: Sha Yang, Min-Rui Li, Hui-Lan Zhang, Jin-Jun Xia, Hong-Xia Luo, Zhong-Lan You, Ting-Ting Li, Jian-Ping You
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis study aimed to evaluate the effectiveness of an intelligent interaction voice prompting system (IIVPS) for guiding medical staff in the removal of personal protective equipment (PPE).MethodsA total of 152 medical staff members were enrolled via convenience sampling. Using a one-group pretest-posttest design, system effectiveness was evaluated by comparing pre- and post-intervention PPE removal accuracy, psychological stress, and operational fatigue, with nonparametric tests for statistical analyses. User experiences and suggestions for improvement were collected via questionnaires and face-to-face interviews.ResultsAll participants achieved correct PPE-removal procedure adherence while using the IIVPS. The use of the IIVPS was associated with a significant increase in PPE-removal accuracy from 78% to 100% (P < 0.001). The median psychological stress score decreased from 3.00 (3.00, 4.00) to 2.00 (1.00, 2.00) (Z = -8.610, P < 0.001), and the median operational fatigue score decreased from 3.00 (3.00, 4.00) to 1.50 (1.00, 2.00) (Z = -9.172, P < 0.001). Significant differences in system mastery were observed based on PPE training history and familiarity (P < 0.05), while learning acceptance varied by age, work experience, and PPE familiarity (P < 0.05). Analysis of qualitative feedback revealed overwhelmingly positive responses (95.39%), with key themes including “effective operational guidance” and “stress reduction”.ConclusionsThe IIVPS may effectively guide medical staff in standardised PPE removal and potentially reduce psychological stress and operational fatigue. However, this study’s one-group design and short-term evaluation limit causal inferences, and long-term effectiveness requires verification via randomised controlled trials (RCTs). Background

Personal protective equipment (PPE) plays a role in mitigating occupational exposure risks for medical staff handling infected patients and contaminated materials. It is integral to isolation protocols, helps reduce nosocomial infections, and ensures the safety of healthcare workers [1]. However, the effectiveness of PPE depends on strict adherence to operational procedures; any deviation from these protocols can significantly compromise its protective benefits [2]. Occupational exposure risks may increase because of improper removal techniques, inadequate protection, or inappropriate PPE use (e.g., unnecessary layering that increases heat stress and fatigue). Studies have indicated that PPE-removal errors constitute a major occupational hazard [3], particularly as fatigue from prolonged clinical duties can increase procedural non-compliance and susceptibility to infection, thereby elevating infection risk [4, 5]. For example, in a study simulating exposure to infection, 46% of participants’ skin and clothing were contaminated during PPE removal [6]. In response, the World Health Organization (WHO) has emphasised the importance of standardising PPE-removal procedures to minimise infection risk [7]. Notably, traditional training models rely on manual supervision and periodic exercises, which have limitations such as retention decline over time and variability in procedure adherence [8].

Recent advances in artificial intelligence (AI) have enabled novel approaches for optimising medical workflows. Specifically, intelligent voice interaction systems that employ speech recognition and natural language processing have demonstrated efficacy in surgical navigation and telemedicine through real-time feedback mechanisms [9]. Building on these technological developments, this study explores the innovative application of an intelligent interaction voice prompting system (IIVPS) designed to standardise PPE-removal protocols. This study aimed to evaluate whether the IIVPS could improve the accuracy of PPE removal and reduce psychological stress and operational fatigue among medical staff. This initiative demonstrates the potential for AI-driven transformation of PPE management, aiming to advance medical safety practices and to offer a novel training tool for clinical settings.

Methods

Study design and setting

A one-group pretest-posttest design was used to evaluate the effectiveness of the IIVPS in guiding PPE removal among medical staff. The primary outcome was PPE-removal accuracy, and secondary outcomes included psychological stress, operational fatigue, system mastery score, and learning acceptance score. Data were collected before and after the IIVPS intervention.

Participants and sampling

Participants were recruited between January and September 2023 via convenience sampling from five medical institutions in Chongqing, China: one Grade III-A (tertiary) hospital’s infectious disease department, three Grade II-A (secondary) hospitals’ fever clinics, and one county-level Centre for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC). Eligibility criteria: (1) licensed healthcare workers (doctors, nurses, laboratory technicians, disinfection personnel); (2) engaged in infectious disease prevention or patient contact; (3) voluntary participation with written informed consent. Exclusion criteria: unable to complete the questionnaire or PPE-removal operation. Because this was a convenience sample, the sample size was determined by the number of eligible and consenting staff available during the study period; no a priori power calculation was performed. Participation was voluntary, and all 152 participants enrolled in this study provided written informed consent.

Intervention programme

The IIVPS (Patent No. ZL 202110120557.9) was developed by the research group to guide medical staff through the correct procedures for PPE removal and hand hygiene, particularly under demanding conditions. The system has been piloted in several medical units (details available upon request/with citation if applicable). The system hardware comprises several components: a housing (containing a hand sanitizer reservoir and pump), a main control board, two proximity sensors (one located at the bottom, the other on the side of the housing), a display screen (front-mounted), a speaker, and a power supply module. A computer program stored on the main control board manages the operation of the speaker, pump, display, and sensors. The operational workflow of the IIVPS is illustrated in Fig. 1.

As the core intervention tool, the IIVPS aims to standardise PPE removal (based on the standard 9-step protocol for Level 2 PPE based on the relevant national/CDC-level guidance used in China (Level 2 PPE, 9-step doffing sequence), which details the sequence for hand hygiene, gown, gloves, and mask/eyewear removal to minimise self-contamination), thereby reducing occupational exposure risk. Participant training

An intervention team of six members was set up. All study team members received standardised training on the PPE-removal procedure and use of the system. Prior to use, the participants were systematically trained to use the system to ensure that they were familiar with it. Volunteers were first provided with the device instruction manual and the accompanying PPE manual. Three senior nurses from the Infectious Diseases Unit explained the operating procedures, conducted on-site demonstrations and provided guidance on installing the device in the PPE-removal area. This ensured that each participant could operate the system in a standardised and regulated manner. Photographic sequences demonstrating the IIVPS in use during PPE doffing are provided in Supplementary Figure S1.

Evaluation indicators

The study used: (1) A general information questionnaire covering socio-demographics (sex, age, education, work experience) and participation in epidemic prevention tasks. (2) A survey on daily PPE-removal practices, assessing prior PPE experience, training history, and familiarity with procedures. (3) A survey on the IIVPS user experience, evaluating ease of learning/mastery, perceived impact on psychological tension (defined as self-reported feelings of anxiety or stress during PPE removal), willingness to use, and willingness to recommend the system. (4) An PPE-removal operation correctness evaluation form, which was a standardized 15-item checklist used to record adherence to procedural steps and assess operational correctness. (5) Open-ended questions to collect qualitative feedback on overall impressions and suggestions for system improvement. Comprehensive psychometric validation (e.g., factor analysis, test–retest reliability) was not performed, which is addressed as a study limitation.

PPE-removal accuracy: Assessed via a 15-item standardised checklist (1 point per correct step, accuracy = total score/15 × 100%). Psychological stress was assessed using a single-item 5-point Likert rating (1 = no stress to 5 = extreme stress); content validity was reviewed by experts (CVI = 0.92). Operational fatigue: 5-point Likert scale (1 = no fatigue, 5 = extreme fatigue; CVI = 0.92). System Mastery Score: 5-point Likert scale (1 = very easy to master, 5 = very difficult; expert-validated). A lower score indicates greater perceived ease of mastery. Learning Acceptance Score: 5-point Likert scale (1 = completely unacceptable, 5 = completely acceptable; expert-validated). Qualitative feedback: Two open-ended questions (“Overall, how do you feel after using the system?” “What needs improvement?“) and face-to-face interviews.

Data collection methods

The PPE-removal process of the study participants was recorded through on-site observation and video recording, and on-site scoring was performed by trained researchers to ensure the objectivity and consistency of the scoring, and to summarize the correctness of the operation and adherence to safety protocols designed to minimize exposure risk. Hand hygiene duration was measured using the system’s built-in proximity sensors, which automatically timed the hand-rubbing process and provided an audible alert if the duration fell short of the 20-second standard, ensuring compliance monitoring. Prior to the IIVPS intervention, a baseline assessment of PPE removal accuracy was conducted. Each participant performed the complete PPE removal procedure without any prompting or guidance. Their performance was evaluated in real time by trained members of the research team using a standardized 15-item checklist. The assessment was based on direct observation and video recording. The same checklist, observation protocols, video-recording procedures, and researcher scoring criteria were applied identically during the post-intervention assessment to ensure comparability.

Quantitative data regarding baseline characteristics, pre-intervention stress/fatigue levels, post-intervention stress/fatigue levels, and user experience with the IIVPS (including ease of use, clarity, and willingness to use/recommend) were collected from all 152 participants via questionnaires administered via an online survey platform (Questionnaire Star/Wenjuanxing, China). Additionally, qualitative data regarding participants’ overall impressions and suggestions for improvement were gathered through open-ended questions included in the online survey and face-to-face interviews conducted with participants. The questionnaire used in this study was specifically developed by the research team, and the full questionnaire is provided as Supplementary Material (Supplementary File 1).

Quality control

To ensure standardisation and consistency of the research process, a detailed study operation manual was prepared, clarifying the specific requirements and procedures for each step. All researchers involved in implementing the intervention and collecting data received professional training. This training covered the following: familiarity with the study design and research protocol; proficient operation of the IIVPS; and thorough understanding of the standardized procedures and assessment criteria for PPE removal.

Statistical analysis

IBM SPSS software (version 27.0; Chicago, IL, USA) was used for statistical analysis and data description, with a significance level set at α = 0.05 (two-tailed). Continuous variables are described using mean and standard deviation when normally distributed; otherwise, median (IQR) was reported. Within-group comparisons of non-normally distributed data (e.g., pre- vs. post-intervention scores) were performed using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test. Categorical variables are expressed as frequencies and percentages. Between-group comparisons were conducted using the Mann-Whitney U test for two independent groups and the Kruskal-Wallis H test for more than two groups. For any significant results from the Kruskal-Wallis H test, post-hoc pairwise comparisons were performed using Dunn’s test with Bonferroni correction to control for multiple comparisons. Responses from the two open-ended questions, which asked participants about the system’s strengths and suggested improvements, were analysed using inductive thematic analysis. The analysis followed a systematic process: (1) Two researchers independently read and re-read the responses to achieve familiarization. (2) They generated initial codes to identify key phrases and concepts. (3) Codes were collaboratively sorted into potential themes. (4) Themes were reviewed and refined to ensure they accurately represented the data set. (5) The themes were clearly defined and named. Any discrepancies in coding or theme identification were resolved through discussion until consensus was reached. Finally, illustrative quotes were selected to represent the key themes in the results.

Results

Participant characteristics and compliance

In total, 152 medical staff members participated in the study. Among the included participants, 73 (48.03%) were from the infectious-disease departments, and 79 (51.97%) were from other non-infectious departments. The average participant age was 33.09 ± 9.42 years, and the mean years of work experience was 8.09 ± 8.47 years. Regarding the intervention, all participants (100%) successfully completed the PPE-removal procedure utilising the IIVPS. Observations confirmed that during system use, all participants correctly followed the standardised procedures as guided by the system’s voice prompts. No sequence errors or missed steps were observed according to the checklist criteria and rater observation. Additionally, all participants complied with hand hygiene protocols, achieving hand-rubbing/hand-hygiene durations greater than 20 s (Table 1).

Baseline PPE experience and perceptions

The results showed that 59 (38.82%) medical staff members in this study had experience of using PPE, of whom 42 had participated in anti-epidemic missions. Of the medical staff, 111 (73.03%) had received PPE training; however, 107 (70.39%) still forgot the operation steps, and only 55 (36.19%) were familiar with the operation procedure. Additional analysis of baseline responses/observations indicated that the most common errors involved the removal process, especially the wrong order of PPE removal and insufficient frequency of PPE replacement during work (where applicable), and time spent on key steps during removal. Most medical staff members (78.29%) felt nervous during PPE removal and were worried about contamination. Among the 152 medical staff members, 85.53% mentioned that they were tired or fatigued while using PPE (Table 2).

User experience with the system

Thematic analysis of open-ended responses revealed four key themes: (1) Enhanced guidance and safety (e.g., ‘The voice prompts stopped me from skipping the hand hygiene step’); (2) Reduced anxiety: “More reassuring than manual operation, no need to worry about forgetting steps”; (3) High practicality; and (4) Suggestions for improvement (e.g., system fluency and multi-language support). Use of the IIVPS was associated with an increase in PPE-removal accuracy from 78% to 100%. The results indicated high overall user satisfaction and acceptance of the IIVPS for PPE removal. Regarding perceived difficulty in mastering the system (System Mastery Score), the median score was 3 (IQR: 2–3). A lower score indicates greater ease. Notably, 116 participants (76.32%) rated the system as ‘very easy’ or ‘easy’ to master (scores of 1 or 2), indicating high overall perceived usability despite some variability in ratings. Regarding the clarity of the voice guidance for the removal process, 131 participants (86.18%) perceived it as “very clear” or “clear”.

Most participants expressed willingness to use and recommend the system. Specifically, 136 (89.47%) reported willingness to use, and 140 (92.11%) reported willingness to recommend. Qualitative feedback from open-ended responses and interviews was overwhelmingly positive, with 145 participants (95.39%) highlighting the system’s effectiveness in operational guidance, stress reduction, safety enhancement, and practical utility. A few users suggested potential improvements to system fluency.

Comparison of psychological stress and operational fatigue before and after using the system

The median score for psychological stress during PPE removal was 3.00 (3.00, 4.00) before using the system and 2.00 (1.00, 2.00) after using the system, indicating a statistically significant reduction (Z = -8.610, P < 0.001). Similarly, the median scores for operational fatigue decreased from 3.00 (3.00, 4.00) before using the system to 1.50 (1.00, 2.00) after use, with the difference being statistically significant (Z = -9.172, P < 0.001).

Comparison of system usage among medical staff with different characteristics

Significant differences were observed in the ease of mastering the system among the medical staff regarding whether they had received PPE-related training and their familiarity with PPE use (P < 0.05). Additionally, perceived clarity of the system’s operational guidance differed among participants based on age, years of work experience, and familiarity with PPE usage (P < 0.05). For the significant Kruskal-Wallis H tests, post-hoc pairwise comparisons were conducted using Dunn’s test with Bonferroni correction. For age groups, the Learning Acceptance Score was higher in the 40–49 age group compared to the 20–29 age group (adjusted P = 0.019). No other pairwise comparisons for age groups were significant. For work experience groups, the Learning Acceptance Score was higher in the 20–40 years group compared to the 0–10 years group (adjusted P = 0.038). No other pairwise comparisons for work experience groups were significant. Familiarity with PPE procedures also showed significant overall effects on both System Mastery and Learning Acceptance Scores (P < 0.05). Post-hoc analyses revealed that for the System Mastery Score, participants who were ‘Completely familiar’ reported lower scores than those who were ‘Completely unfamiliar’ (adjusted P = 0.014). For the Learning Acceptance Score, scores in the ‘Completely familiar’ and ‘Somewhat familiar’ groups were both higher than in the ‘Completely unfamiliar’ and ‘Neutral’ groups (all adjusted P < 0.05) (Table 3).

Discussion

Association between IIVPS use and PPE-removal accuracy

Occupational exposure remains a critical risk that threatens the health and safety of medical staff. Through open-ended questions regarding previous PPE usage among the 152 participants, 147/152 (96.71%) participants reported that the doffing procedures were complex, expressing concerns about errors or improper execution potentially leading to contamination and exposure. Notably, 70.39% of the participants admitted to forgetting steps during PPE removal. However, after using the system, all 152 participants were able to perform the doffing procedures correctly, with no omissions or sequence errors. The system’s clear voice prompts guided the medical staff step-by-step through the process, significantly reducing the likelihood of forgotten steps. This finding is consistent with previous research indicating that intelligent assistance systems can effectively enhance accuracy and compliance in complex operational tasks [10]. Furthermore, the real-time feedback function of the system provided timely guidance, further minimising the risk of operational errors. Supporting this, a systematic review of 24 studies involving 2278 participants found that additional voice instructions could reduce doffing errors and contamination spots [4]. Moreover, the system integrates hand hygiene guidance with instructions for PPE removal. If the handwashing duration is insufficient, the system triggers an audible alert through the main control board to ensure compliance with hand hygiene protocols. Consequently, all 152 participants in this study demonstrated proper hand hygiene practices, with handwashing durations exceeding 20 s.

IIVPS appears to reduce psychological stress during PPE removal

Traditional PPE doffing procedures typically rely on visual aids or written instructions, which often contribute to anxiety and stress among medical staff owing to the complexity and multistep nature of the process, particularly in high-pressure environments, such as infectious disease wards or isolation areas [8, 11]. In contrast, the system provides clear real-time auditory instructions based on standardised PPE-removal protocols, replacing traditional visual cues. This approach significantly reduces concerns regarding missed steps, incorrect sequences, and contamination risks. This finding is consistent with those of previous studies on the effectiveness of intelligent assistance systems in alleviating psychological stress among medical staff [12, 13], further demonstrating that the IIVPS can ease anxiety during complex operational tasks in the medical field.

Furthermore, the voice guidance function of the system helps to reduce the cognitive load on medical staff during PPE-removal procedures. Traditional procedures often require operators to recall a sequence of steps mentally, which can lead to distractions and operational errors. In contrast, the IIVPS provides real-time auditory prompts, minimises the memory burden, and allows medical staff to focus more effectively on the task at hand, thereby reducing psychological pressure.

High user acceptance and potential utility of the IIVPS

The IIVPS demonstrated user-friendly operation with clear voice prompts and strong instructional guidance. All participants successfully completed the PPE-removal procedure without errors while using the system, highlighting its effectiveness in guiding correct performance. This outcome aligns with findings suggesting that technological assistive strategies, like the audio-visual aid described by Kaur et al. [14], can enhance PPE-removal practices. The IIVPS is a straightforward technological assistive strategy that enhances the correct rate of PPE removal by medical staff. Furthermore, the system showed high user acceptance. A high proportion of participants reported willingness to use and recommend the IIVPS (89.47% and 92.11%, respectively) to both personally use and recommend the IIVPS to colleagues, indicating satisfaction with its instructional quality and perceived utility. This high acceptance, coupled with its potential to reduce the need for direct human supervision during doffing, suggests the IIVPS may reduce reliance on direct supervision during doffing, potentially improving workflow efficiency; this should be evaluated in future studies.

Given its effectiveness and positive user reception, the IIVPS appears suitable for deployment in diverse settings requiring PPE use, such as fever clinics, isolation wards, various laboratory environments (including mobile BSL-3 (P3) laboratories), mobile imaging units, screening sites, and quarantine facilities, in addition to infectious disease departments. Furthermore, it can be integrated into medical training programmes as an intelligent teaching tool [15, 16], complementing other educational platforms to expand its functionality and provide a new approach for optimising PPE training.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the single-group pre-post design without a control group precludes causal inference. The observed improvements cannot be definitively attributed to the IIVPS alone, as alternative explanations—including learning effects, Hawthorne effects, and observer bias—cannot be ruled out. Notably, while all participants demonstrated correct PPE removal post-intervention, this 100% adherence rate should be interpreted with caution, particularly given that participants were volunteers who received structured training beforehand. Thus, this finding reflects performance under trained, observed, and supervised conditions and may not generalize directly to routine clinical settings. Second, outcome measurement relied on self-reported single-item scales for stress and fatigue and observational checklists for accuracy, rather than validated multi-item instruments or objective contamination assessment. This limits the ability to assess internal consistency and construct validity. Third, subgroup analyses, though revealing descriptive differences, were not adjusted for multiple comparisons beyond post-hoc testing, and the absence of multivariate adjustment precludes causal interpretation of group effects. Additionally, because no a priori power calculation was performed, subgroup analyses may be underpowered, and findings should be interpreted as descriptive rather than confirmatory. Fourth, the evaluation was short-term and focused solely on Level 2 PPE removal; long-term effectiveness and applicability to other protection levels remain unexamined. Future research should address these limitations by employing randomized controlled designs, incorporating objective outcome measures (e.g., fluorescent markers for contamination assessment), using validated multi-item instruments for psychological and experiential outcomes, conducting comprehensive psychometric validation of assessment tools, and evaluating long-term effectiveness and broader applicability across different PPE protocols.

Conclusions

This study evaluated the IIVPS among 152 medical staff. The findings suggest that the system may effectively guide users toward accurate PPE removal and was associated with reduced self-reported psychological stress and operational fatigue, with high user acceptance. However, these conclusions are tempered by the study’s limitations, including its one-group pre-post design, short-term evaluation, and reliance on self-reported and observational measures rather than objective contamination data. Therefore, future randomized controlled trials and longitudinal studies are needed to verify its long-term effectiveness and generalizability. Notwithstanding these limitations, the IIVPS shows promising potential as a tool for clinical application and PPE training, potentially contributing to healthcare workers’ occupational safety.
