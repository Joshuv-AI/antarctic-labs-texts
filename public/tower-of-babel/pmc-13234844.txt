A nucleic acid regulation strategy under mechanical stress for intervertebral disc degeneration treatment
Authors: Wenchao Wang, Wenxiang Huo, Dingfei Qian, Peihong Hou, Cheng Su, Shengyao Sun, Yawei Yao, Jinlong Ma, Linhao Hou, Jiayan Wu, Yongming Chen, Fanqi Hu, Tianyu Zhao, Xuesong Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The nucleus pulposus (NP), the core shock-absorbing component of the intervertebral disc (IVD), plays a vital role in the pathogenesis of intervertebral disc degeneration (IVDD). Here, we uncover that degenerative NP tissue under mechanical stress is characterized by upregulated PIEZO1 and accumulation of cell-free DNA (cfDNA). Specific inhibition or knockout of PIEZO1 suppresses the cfDNA-induced NP degeneration. Mechanistically, PIEZO1-mediated calcium overload triggers mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) leakage, initiating a cascade that culminates in MAFB-dependent activation of the NOD-like receptor thermal protein domain associated protein 3 (NLRP3) inflammasome pathway. Targeting the PIEZO1-cfDNA-MAFB-NLRP3 axis, we developed a hydrogel nano composite system (HA-F127@MCC950 SiNPs) containing amino-functionalized SiO2 nanoparticles (SiNPs) loaded with pyroptosis antagonist MCC950, embedded within hyaluronic acid (HA) and pluronic F127 (F127). The engineered hydrogel possesses dual effects in cfDNA scavenging and NLRP3 suppression in NP cells (NPCs). Moreover, this approach markedly attenuated NP degeneration progression in rat models of cfDNA-induced and temporary compression model (TCM)-induced IVDD. Collectively, this nucleic acid clearance strategy provides new insights for IVDD treatment.

Introduction

Intervertebral disc degeneration (IVDD) serves as the primary cause of lower back pain (LBP) in the elderly [1,2], imposing a substantial burden on global disability and healthcare systems [3]. Present clinical management strategies for IVDD are predominantly oriented towards palliative treatments to alleviate symptoms, fail to reverse the pathological progression of disc degeneration [4]. Despite their promise, stem cell therapy and biomaterial implants are confined to repairing and regenerating damaged tissue, while overlooking the upstream pathogenic drivers [[5], [6], [7], [8], [9]]. Critically, the hostile microenvironment in degenerated intervertebral disc (IVD) further compromises the restorative function of therapeutic interventions, fundamentally complicating treatment of IVDD [[10], [11], [12]].

Mechanical stress, a key driver of IVDD, acts primarily by inducing degeneration of the nucleus pulposus (NP), which serves as the core functional unit for pressure distribution within the IVD [[13], [14], [15]]. PIEZO1 orchestrates the transduction of mechanical inputs into biochemical signals and is implicated in multiple disease pathologies [[16], [17], [18]]. Notably, PIEZO1 activation drives nucleus pulposus cells (NPCs) apoptosis, thus promoting the progression of IVDD [19,20]. Moreover, targeting PIEZO1 is emerging as a promising therapeutic strategy for IVDD [21].

Inflammation, which promotes mitochondrial oxidative stress and disrupts extracellular matrix (ECM) homeostasis, is closely related to IVDD [22]. Pyroptosis, a typical kind of cell death, is intrinsically linked to inflammatory activation [23]. Recent evidence indicates that the activation of NLRP3/apoptosis-associated speck-like protein containing a CARD (ASC)/Caspase-1/IL-1β cascade axis is critically involved in the process of IVDD [24]. Furthermore, accumulating studies are exploring the therapeutic targeting of the pathway for IVDD treatment [[25], [26], [27], [28]]. Excitedly, PIEZO1-dependent mechanotransduction potentiates inflammation by engaging the NLRP3 inflammasome [29,30].

Cell-free DNA (cfDNA), including cf-nucleus DNA (cf-nDNA) and cf-mitochondria DNA (cf-mtDNA), a known biomarker for inflammation-related diseases [31,32], is linked to the presence of LBP in patients with IVDD [33]. In particular, mtDNA is released from damaged mitochondria through BAK/BAX macropores into the cytosol [34,35], where it acts as a damage associated molecular pattern (DAMP) to activate the NLRP3 inflammasome [[36], [37], [38]]. Moreover, the mtDNA-NLRP3 axis has been implicated in the pathogenesis of acute liver injury [39], diabetic cardiomyopathy [40], and NP degeneration [41]. Additionally, mechanical stress-induced PIEZO1 activation has been implicated in osteoarthritis progression, leading to mitochondrial calcium overload and subsequent mtDNA release [42]. Thus, targeting nucleic acid-sensing pyroptosis pathways under mechanical stimulation constitutes a novel approach to IVDD treatment. However, the absence of effective tools hampers the implementation of this strategy.

Given the crucial role of cfDNA in the molecular mechanisms of inflammatory diseases, efforts to remove it have spurred a surge in research in recent years [[43], [44], [45], [46]]. Obviously, due to their electrostatic adsorption of anionic cfDNA, cationic polymers are promising candidates for its clearance. In addition, the favorable biocompatibility and excellent drug-loading properties of hyaluronic acid (HA) hydrogel make it an ideal candidate for a delivery platform in IVDD therapy [47,48]. Furthermore, the thermoresponsive sol-gel transition of pluronic F127 enables its utilization in bone defects [49] and vascular embolism [50].

Here, we first investigated cfDNA accumulation and PIEZO1 activation in human, mouse and rat degenerated NP tissues under mechanical load, and then the upstream regulatory role of PIEZO1 on cfDNA was examined by applying its activator Yoda1 and inhibitor GsMTx4. Subsequently, using transcriptome sequencing as a guide and Piezo1/Mafb genetic knockout mice, we elucidated the nosogenesis by which the PIEZO1-cfDNA-MAFB axis activates the NLRP3 inflammasome during NP degeneration. Accordingly, HA-F127 NPs system was developed for simultaneously sequestering cfDNA (via amino-functionalized SiO2) and inhibiting NLRP3 (via MCC950). Ultimately, the therapeutic efficacy of HA-F127 Si@MCC950 NPs against NP degeneration was validated in rat models of IVDD induced by cfDNA and TCM.

Results

Mechanical stress-mediated NP pyroptosis is featured by upregulated PIEZO1 and cfDNA

Recognizing that excessive mechanical loading is a major risk factor for IVDD, NP specimens were collected from patients with lumbar disc herniation (LDH), stratified according to their distinct signal intensities on T2-weighted Magnetic Resonance (T2WI) (Fig. S1A). Specifically, late-stage degenerated discs (Grade V) were characterized by tissue atrophy (Fig. S1B), increased histological score, loss of COL2, and elevated PIEZO1 and cfDNA (Fig. 1A and Fig. S2A–B), relative to their earlier-stage counterparts (Grade III). To recapitulate abnormal local mechanical pressure, lumbar spine instability (LSI) mice model and rat temporary compression model (TCM) were established (Fig. S1C and D). The collapse of intervertebral disc height (IDH) and the elevation in Pfirrmann grade of the IVD were observed on Fig. S1C–G. Furthermore, the reduced NPCs count, decreased AGGRECAN, and increased MMP3 in the NP tissues further confirmed the established model's validity and consistency (Fig. S1H and I). Notably, immunostaining showed a coherent degenerative pattern, evidenced by diminished COL2, elevated histological score, and enhanced signals of PIEZO1 and cfDNA (Fig. 1B–C and Fig. S2A–B). To investigate the link between PIEZO1 and cfDNA, mice with LSI were treated with either Yoda1 (a PIEZO1 agonist) or GsMTx4 (a PIEZO1 inhibitor). As shown in Fig. S2C, Yoda1 elevated cfDNA levels, whereas GsMTx4 conversely suppressed it. Next, rat primary NPCs (rNPCs) were isolated to assessed the PIEZO1-cfDNA axis in vitro. Consistently, immunofluorescence results mirrored the regulatory trend observed in vivo (Fig. S2D). Critically, to identify the cell death mechanism driving NP degeneration under mechanical load, human primary NPCs (hNPCs) were extracted and exposed to a 1 MPa mechanical stimulus for 24 h. The results indicated that mechanical stress promoted NP pyroptosis, manifested by increased pyroptotic vesicles (Fig. 1D), as well as upregulation of NLRP3, the Cleaved GSDMD/GSDMD ratio, and the Cleaved Caspase-1/Caspase-1 ratio (Fig. 1E and G). Furthermore, Western blot and immunofluorescence analysis showed that mechanical stimulation also triggered ferroptosis and apoptosis in hNPCs. Ferroptosis was evidenced by increased PTGS2, decreased GPX4, and enhanced lipid peroxidation (Fig. S2E and F). Apoptosis was demonstrated by a higher Cleaved Caspase-3/Caspase-3 ratio and more Tunel-positive cells (Fig. S2G and H). Subsequently, to investigate which cell death mechanism plays a dominant role, we treated hNPCs with VX-765 (a pyroptosis inhibitor), Fer-1 (a ferroptosis inhibitor), or Z-VAD-FMK (an apoptosis inhibitor). The results showed that pyroptosis inhibition significantly reduced lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) release (Fig. 1F) and restored COL2 expression (Fig. S2I). PIEZO1-dependent calcium overload elicits mitochondrial stress and mtDNA leakage

Under 1 MPa of mechanical stress, hNPCs exhibited increased PIEZO1 expression (Fig. 1E and G) and abnormal cfDNA accumulation (Fig. 2A), prompting us to investigate the source of cfDNA. Given that PIEZO1 is a mechanosensitive ion channel and that mitochondria are primary source of cytoplasmic DNA, we hypothesized that PIEZO1 activation promotes cfDNA accumulation by facilitating mtDNA release. To test this, we first isolated and purified cytosolic mtDNA from hNPCs exposed to 1 MPa of mechanical stimulation. The qualitative analysis by agarose gel electrophoresis (Fig. 2B) and quantitative analysis by RT-qPCR (Fig. 2C) showed that mechanical loading significantly increased mtDNA release. Furthermore, immunofluorescence analysis demonstrated that Piezo1 silencing via small interfering RNA (siRNA) (Fig. S3A) inhibited the mechanical load-induced accumulation of cfDNA in the cytoplasm of rNPCs (Fig. 2D and G). As the preferential Ca2+ selectivity of PIEZO1, we next investigated the role of Ca2+ in cfDNA accumulation. Notably, as shown in Fig. 2E and F, Yoda1 treatment induced Ca2+ influx in rNPCs, accompanied by the opening of the mitochondrial permeability transition pore (mPTP). Subsequently, we utilized BAPTA-AM to chelate intracellular calcium and CsA to inhibit mPTP opening. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) revealed that interventions with either BAPTA-AM or CsA alleviated the mitochondrial cristae disorganization induced by H2O2 and Yoda1 (Fig. 2H). Accordingly, JC-1 (Fig. S3C), MitoTracker Green (Fig. 2I), DCFH-DA (Fig. S3D), and Mito-SOX (Fig. 2J) assays demonstrated that calcium chelation or mPTP inhibition restored mitochondrial homeostasis, confirmed by rescued mitochondrial membrane potential (MMP), normalized mitochondrial length, and reduced ROS production. Consistent with their roles in blocking the calcium-mPTP axis, both BAPTA-AM (in vitro) and CsA (in vitro and vivo) significantly attenuated cfDNA levels, as detected by immunofluorescence (Fig. S3E and F). PIEZO1-triggered release of mtDNA activates MAFB and NLRP3

To delineate the downstream signaling of the PIEZO1-cfDNA axis, RNA sequencing was conducted. As evidenced by Heatmap visualization, Yoda1 significantly promoted the expression of Mafb and Nlrp3 in rNPCs (Fig. 3A). Furthermore, CpG 1826 (CpG), a typical oligodeoxynucleotide, was synthesized to verify the function of cfDNA. The immunohistochemical results showed that both CpG intervention and TCM modeling significantly upregulated MAFB and NLRP3 expression, with the most pronounced effect observed in the combination group (Fig. 3B). Moreover, Western blot analysis showed that Yoda1 and CpG respectively upregulated MAFB and NLRP3 in a time-dependent and concentration-dependent manner (Fig. S4A–D), and their combined intervention produced the most marked effect (Fig. 3C). Next, to genetically interrogate the role of PIEZO1 in controlling MAFB and NLRP3 expression, we utilized a conditional knockout (CKO) strategy. Piezo1 CKO mitigated the LSI-induced accumulation of cfDNA (Fig. 3D and E) and consequently blunted the upregulation of the MAFB and NLRP3 (Fig. 3F). Building on these findings, the Tail-looping model was employed to confirm the PIEZO1-cfDNA-MAFB-NLRP3 pathological axis. cfDNA drives NP pyroptosis and degeneration dependent on MAFB

As a transcription factor, MAFB is involved in various biological regulatory processes. To determine whether MAFB participates in cfDNA-induced NLRP3 inflammasome activation, we employed both overexpression and knockdown strategies for functional validation. Following transfection with lentivirals (Lv), siRNA, or plasmid (p), Western blot analysis were first performed to verify the transfection efficiency in rNPCs (Fig. S4E–G). Next, immunofluorescence analysis demonstrated that Mafb overexpression mediated by lentivirus (Lv-Mafb) upregulated NLRP3 while concurrently suppressing COL2 expression (Fig. 4A). In addition, siRNA-mediated knockdown of Mafb (si-Mafb) prevented cfDNA accumulation under mechanical stress (Fig. S4H). Subsequently, Western blot data demonstrated that cfDNA triggered a pyroptotic response, marked by elevated levels of NLRP3, Cleaved GSDMD/GSDMD, Cleaved Caspase-1/Caspase-1, IL-1β, and ASC, which was significantly attenuated upon si-Mafb treatment (Fig. 4B and C). Moreover, the suppression of Mafb effectively inhibited cfDNA-mediated NLRP3 inflammasome activation, and concomitantly restored COL2 levels, as confirmed by immunofluorescence (Fig. 4D). Crucially, the promoter dual luciferase reporter assay showed that luciferase activity was significantly increased following exogenous Mafb overexpression. Next, knockout-rescue experiments were performed to validate the involvement of MAFB in the PIEZO1-NLRP3 axis. As shown in Fig. S4I and J, silencing Piezo1 or Mafb alone each inhibited Yoda1-induced pyroptosis activation, whereas combined silencing of both genes achieved the greatest inhibition. Besides, plasmid-mediated Mafb overexpression (p-Mafb) reversed the si-Piezo1-induced inhibition of pyroptosis (Fig. S5A and B). Furthermore, Mafb CKO mice were further constructed to verify its function in PIEZO1-cfDNA-NLRP3 pathway. As shown in Fig. 4F, Mafb knockout reversed the deleterious effects of CpG in the Tail-looping model, rescuing AGGRECAN expression and attenuating NLRP3 and IL-1β level, highlighting its pivotal bridging role in this signaling cascade. Critically, adeno-associated virus (AAV)-mediated Mafb overexpression (AAV-Mafb OE) counteracted the effects of Piezo1 CKO, specifically by increasing NLRP3 and IL-1β levels while reducing COL2 expression, as evidenced by immunohistochemistry (Fig. 4G).

Specifically, as cytosolic mtDNA is a canonical activator of the cGAS-STING pathway, we examined whether mechanical stress-induced pyroptosis activation involves this pathway. As shown in Fig. S5C–F, neither mechanical (1 MPa) nor chemical (Yoda1) stimulation significantly activated this pathway in hNPCs. Remarkably, we observed that under mechanical stress, hNPCs exhibit increased MAFB expression and decreased GSK-3β expression (Fig. S5G and H). Notably, treatment with Laduviglusib (a GSK-3β inhibitor) promoted MAFB expression, whereas DIF-3 (a GSK-3β agonist) inhibited the cfDNA mediated upregulation of MAFB (Fig. S5I–J). Therefore, we suggest that PIEZO1-dependent cfDNA accumulation suppresses GSK-3β to activate MAFB [51], leading to pyroptosis, which occurs independently of the cGAS-STING pathway. In conclusion, our findings may provide the PIEZO1-cfDNA-GSK-3β-MAFB-NLRP3 axis as a promising therapeutic target for the treatment of IVDD.

Development and characterization of HA-F127@MCC950 SiNPs

To target this mechanism, we engineered an injectable, thermal-responsive hydrogel to co-deliver SiO2 nanoparticles (SiNPs) and the NLRP3 inhibitor MCC950, achieving cfDNA scavenging and pyroptosis inhibition in NPCs. Firstly, oxidized hyaluronic acid (OHA) was synthesized via the sodium periodate oxidation method and the 1H NMR (600 MHz) analysis confirmed the degree of aldehyde formation reached 10% (Fig. S6A and B). Oxyamino terminated Pluronic F127 (AOP127) was prepared via the route shown in Fig. S6C and 1H NMR analysis verified the successful modification of F127 molecular termini, with a terminal functionalization ratio of 97.5% (Fig. S6D). OHA and AOP127 were each dissolved in PBS to prepare 2.5% (w/v) solutions. The solutions were then mixed at a 3:1 vol ratio (functional group molar ratio = 1:1), resulting in the formation of an HA-F127 hydrogel. As shown in Fig. 5A, the HA-F127 hydrogel exhibited excellent thermal responsiveness. Next, two types of amino-functionalized SiNPs were synthesized (Fig. S6E), and their DNA adsorption capacity were detected by agarose gel electrophoresis. As shown in Fig. 5B and S6F, the optimal adsorption ratio of diamino SiNPs were slightly lower than that of monoamino ones. Nevertheless, the former has a significantly higher bound amino group content. At the same microsphere mass fraction, diamino SiNPs showed higher adsorption capacity and are thus preferred. In addition, 80 nm SiNPs were chosen for their highest potential (Fig. S6G) and gelation property (Fig. S6H). TEM verified the successful synthesis of diamino SiNPs with uniform particle sizes (Fig. 5C). Next, the self-healing and injectability properties of HA-F127 SiNPs were evaluated. As depicted in Fig. 5E and F, two separated hydrogel composite fused into one continuous gel after 5 min of contact at 37 °C, and the gel could be extruded smoothly through a 20-gauge needle to form the letters “ABC”. Meanwhile, in vitro stability assays of the hydrogel demonstrated that the HA-F127 SiNPs exhibited a slight mass reduction over time following swelling, a phenomenon presumably attributable to partial dissolution of the matrix (Fig. S6I). Besides, compression (Fig. 5G) and dynamic cyclic loading tests (Fig. 5H) revealed the load-bearing capacity of HA-F127 SiNPs. With respect to the DNA adsorption assay of the hydrogel, the results demonstrated that, in comparison with HA-F127, HA-F127 SiNPs exhibited an adsorption ratio of up to 90% for CpG following 700 min of incubation (Fig. 5I). Based on this, MCC950 was incorporated into the hydrogel system. As shown in Fig. 5J, the release ratio of MCC950 reached 90% within 8 h, irrespective of the presence of SiNPs in the system. Accordingly, dynamic rheological analysis were performed on the HA-F127@MCC950 SiNPs. Frequency (0.1-100 Hz) and strain (0.01-500%) sweep tests were conducted. The near-constant values of the storage (G′) and loss (G’’) modulus within the tested ranges (up to 10 Hz and 10% strain) demonstrated the hydrogel's ability to retain stability across a relatively wide operational window (Fig. 5K and L). Then, a constant frequency of 1 Hz and strain of 1% were applied. The temperature sweep from 4 °C to 37 °C at a heating rate of 1 °C/min was used to evaluate the thermal response. Data from Fig. 5M and N demonstrated that following gelation at 37 °C, the hydrogel network persisted and maintained its structural integrity for a duration of at least 40 min. Furthermore, the cytotoxicity of the hydrogel system was assessed. Both live/dead cell staining (Fig. S6J and K) and CCK8 assay (Fig. S6L) confirmed that the HA-F127@MCC950 SiNPs exhibited no significant impact on NPCs survival, demonstrating favorable biocompatibility. Finally, colocalization analysis of SiNPs and CpG was performed in NPCs. Immunofluorescence imaging (Fig. 5O) confirmed the colocalization of FITC-labeled SiNPs (green) with Cy5-labeled CpG (red), indicating that the SiNPs exhibited strong cfDNA-binding capability.

Evaluation of HA-F127@MCC950 SiNPs in cfDNA-induced IVDD

Encouraged by the ability of HA-F127@MCC950 SiNPs to modulate pyroptosis and inflammation in vitro, we further investigated their effects in ECM synthesis and degradation. Western blot analysis revealed that HA-F127@MCC950 SiNPs mitigated the CpG-induced decrease in COL2 and increase in MMP3 expression (Fig. S7B). Next, the IR808-labeled HA-F127@MCC950 SiNPs were locally injected into the IVD for tracking. As shown in Fig. 7B and C, the introduction of SiO2 significantly lowered cfDNA levels in NP tissue. Moreover, the addition of MCC950 enabled the system to confer greater protection against cfDNA-induced NP damage than HA-F127 SiNPs alone, indicated by the decreased histological score, restoration of COL2 loss, and reduced MMP3 upregulation. Discussion and conclusion

In this study, we first reported that degenerated NP specimens from patients with LDH exhibit a distinct immunohistochemical profile, featuring notably elevated PIEZO1 expression coupled with substantial cfDNA accumulation. Since PIEZO1 is a mechanosensitive channel [52] and mechanical overload is a key driver of IVDD [14], our findings reinforce the link between cfDNA accumulation and pathologic mechanical stress. Next, both LSI mouse [53] and TCM rat models [54] were established to respectively mimic the abnormal load experienced by NP tissue. Consistent findings in both models prompted us to directly test PIEZO1's role concerning cfDNA through its pharmacological activation (Yoda1) and inhibition (GsMTx4). Excitedly, in contrast to the promoting effect of Yoda1 on cfDNA accumulation, GsMTx4 treatment significantly suppressed it. Similarly, Piezo1 silencing (via si-Piezo1 or Piezo1 CKO) inhibited mechanical stress-mediated cfDNA upregulation. However, directly inhibiting PIEZO1 to treat cfDNA-related IVDD is unlikely to be feasible, due to its essential roles in processes such as vascular development and cell migration [55]. Moreover, given the inevitability of mechanical stress on the IVD in upright humans, PIEZO1 may not represent a rational therapeutic target for cfDNA clearance.

Having established that PIEZO1 regulates cfDNA levels, we next turned to investigate the origin of the cfDNA. Functioning primarily as a calcium-permeable channel, PIEZO1 responds to mechanical stress by inducing mitochondrial Ca2+ overload, thereby promoting mtDNA release and driving osteoarthritis progression [42]. Consistently, qualitative and quantitative analysis of mtDNA (mt-ND1, mt-CO2, and D-loop) under mechanical stress, together with the use of Ca2+ chelators (BAPTA-AM) and mPTP blockers (CsA), confirmed our hypothesis. Notably, relevant studies have shown that mtDNA release occurs through the BAK/BAX macropores [34,56]. Inspirationally, a biomimetic nanovesicle system loaded with BAI1 (BAX/BAK inhibitor) has been developed to counteract cfDNA-mediated IVDD [48]. Nevertheless, the connection between BAK/BAX and autophagy, supporting cellular energy metabolism and function, remains unclear [57]. In practical terms, the therapeutic inhibition of BAX/BAK carries the risk of unintended adverse consequences or off-target effects.

After confirming that pyroptosis (rather than ferroptosis or apoptosis) is the primary driver of NP degeneration under mechanical stress, we conducted a correlation study between the PIEZO1-cfDNA axis and pyroptosis. Results confirmed that PIEZO1 activation promotes cfDNA-induced NLRP3 upregulation. While Piezo1 CKO suppressed mechanical load-induced NLRP3 activation in the LSI model, this therapeutic effect was reversed by CpG intervention in the Tail-looping model. Thus, intercepting the cfDNA-NLRP3 axis seems crucial for treating NP degeneration mediated by PIEZO1. Admittedly, relevant studies have demonstrated that cytosolic mtDNA escape can trigger NPCs pyroptosis via the cGAS-STING-NLRP3 axis [41]. However, we do not consider this a conflict with our findings that cfDNA activates pyroptosis in a MAFB-dependent manner, because our conclusions were drawn under conditions of abnormal mechanical stress rather than oxidative stress. Observably, therapeutic systems designed to intervene in key pathogenic pathways have gained considerable traction in recent years. For instance, Ce DNase nanoparticles loaded with C-176 have been developed for ischemic stroke treatment by suppressing the cGAS-STING pathway [45]. However, due to concerns over its broad physiological roles, such as osteoclast differentiation [58], MAFB was not selected as a breakthrough point to avoid undesirable outcomes. Complementally, cfDNA activates MAFB via GSK-3β inhibition [51]. Similarly, in light of the metabolic regulatory role of GSK-3β [59], we did not regard it as an intervention target.

With the pathogenic mechanism clarified, the research then turns to the selection and assessment of a therapeutic system. Our previous research has shown that epigallocatechin-3-gallate (EGCG)-based nanodrugs could alleviate osteoarthritis progression [60]. Besides, nanozyme hydrogels and biomimetic SiNPs were widely applied in cartilage regeneration [61,62]. Although various strategies for cfDNA clearance have been developed, most primarily leverage the inherent anionic nature of cfDNA [44,46]. HA hydrogel is commonly employed as drug delivery system due to its notable hydrophilicity, viscoelasticity, biocompatibility, and biodegradability [47,[63], [64], [65]]. Furthermore, owing to its remarkable temperature responsiveness, F127 has become a key component in various drug delivery applications [49]. Based on this, the HA-F127@MCC950 SiNPs platform was engineered to achieve dual functions: cfDNA clearance and NLRP3 inflammasome inhibition, alongside ensured biocompatibility (Appendix A, Appendix A). Consequently, the HA-F127@MCC950 SiNPs system has been proven to alleviate NP degeneration in rat puncture and TCM models.

Notably, an extreme “burst release” phenomenon of MCC950 was observed in Fig. 5J, with over 90% of the payload released within 8 h, indicating a release profile that is not sustained in the classical sense. In our pathogenic model, mechanical stress drives chronic cfDNA release, which continuously activates the NLRP3 inflammasome. MCC950 acts as an initial “circuit breaker” – it rapidly inhibits NLRP3 within the first few hours after injection, halting the acute pyroptotic cascade. Meanwhile, the SiNPs remain entrapped in the HA-F127 hydrogel for at least 21 days and continuously scavenge cfDNA. Once cfDNA is cleared, the upstream trigger for NLRP3 activation is eliminated. Therefore, even after MCC950 is cleared, the inflammasome cannot be re-activated because the danger signal (cfDNA) is no longer present. This is analogous to a “hit-and-run” or “therapeutic braking” mechanism – a short pulse of inhibitor can achieve long-term suppression if the persistent driver is removed. Actually, several studies have shown that short-term NLRP3 inhibition can durably alter the course of chronic inflammatory diseases [26,66]. This is consistent with our RT-qPCR results in Fig. 6G. At 12 h, the HA-F127@MCC950 SiNPs group showed significantly lower inflammatory factor levels than the HA-F127 SiNPs group, owing to the rapid release of MCC950. At 48 h, no elevation of inflammatory factors was observed in the HA- F127@MCC950 SiNPs group; conversely, due to continuous cfDNA adsorption by SiO2, inflammatory factor levels in the HA-F127 SiNPs group returned to baseline at 72 h. Therefore, our system does not rely on sustained MCC950 release, but rather on a dual mechanism: fast initial NLRP3 blockade combined with long-term cfDNA clearance by SiNPs. However, the dual functions of this system do not necessarily imply a synergistic effect between MCC950 and SiNPs, as a quantitative analysis of such synergy (e.g., combination index calculation) has not been conducted.

Obviously, the adsorption ability of SiNPs for cfDNA is mainly attributed to their positive charge, but this also brings two major problems: the degradation of SiO2 and the off-target effect caused by their binding to the negatively charged endogenous extracellular matrix components (e.g., AGGRECAN). In fact, nonporous amorphous silica is not “non-biodegradable” – it degrades slowly into safe products. The US-FDA classifies silicon dioxide as a “generally regarded as safe (GRAS)” substance. Furthermore, several studies have demonstrated that sub-100 nm silica nanoparticles can be partially degraded and cleared via urine and bile over time [67]. While we acknowledge that degradation in the avascular IVD is slower than in highly vascularized tissues, the long-term safety of silica nanoparticles has been extensively investigated [68], and our safety data demonstrated no detectable local or systemic toxicity. In addition, in our system, the SiNPs are embedded within the HA-F127 hydrogel prior to injection. HA is a polyanionic polymer with abundant carboxyl groups that electrostatically interact with the positively charged amino groups on the SiNPs. Consequently, the composite hydrogel surface exhibits a near-neutral, as confirmed by our zeta potential measurement. Therefore, the risk of off-target is minimal. However, we propose to perform the following experiments in a follow-up study to fully address this issue, including a 6-month intradiscal injection in healthy rats with histopathological evaluation of the IVD and surrounding tissues; ICP-MS quantification of silicon content in the IVD, regional lymph nodes, and plasma to assess long-term retention and systemic exposure; AGGRECAN-SiNPs binding assay to quantitatively assess the risk of electrostatic interaction with the native ECM.

This study has several limitations. Firstly, despite the confirmation of direct Mafb binding to the Nlrp3 promoter by dual luciferase reporter assay, the precise binding sites remain unexplored. Secondly, regarding cfDNA clearance efficiency, only RT-qPCR-based quantification of mtDNA and nDNA was performed; more precise detection methods, such as high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC), were not employed. Thirdly, the small sample size (n = 4) in the in vivo experiments may increase the risk of chance findings. Fourthly, no in vivo validation was performed in non-human primates, nor was any preclinical pharmacokinetic or pharmacodynamic analysis conducted, which hinders clinical translation.

In summary, mechanical stress activates the PIEZO1-mediated accumulation of cfDNA, which triggers NLRP3-dependent IVDD. Conceptionally, HA-F127@MCC950 SiNPs system was developed to block the PIEZO1-cfDNA-NLRP3 axis, confirming their efficacy in delaying NP degeneration.

Materials and methods

Human samples

NP specimens and corresponding imaging data were collected from surgical subjects with LDH.

Conditional knockout mice and genotyping

Piezo1flox/flox, Mafbflox/+ and Col2CreERT+/+ mice were generated and provided by Cyagen (USA). The Piezo1 CKO and Mafb CKO mice, with genotypes of Piezo1flox/flox Col2CreERT+/− and Mafbflox/flox Col2CreERT+/− respectively, were bred using the strategy detailed in Fig. S8D, E and G. Genotyping was conducted using the One Step Mouse Genotyping Kit (Wengene, WG-MG-1000) with primers listed in Table S1, and the results are presented in Fig. S8F.

Rat needle puncture model and disc injection

Puncture model was established at the Co6/7, Co7/8, Co8/9, and Co9/10 intervertebral discs using a 20-gauge needle under X-ray guidance (RV-32D, China). The needle was inserted vertically into the tail disc, advanced to a depth of at least 5 mm, rotated 360°, and then left in place for 30 s to ensure degenerative effects. Subsequently, 2 μL of PBS, CpG (0.6 μg/μL), MCC950 (1 mg/mL), HA-F127, SiNPs, HA-F127@MCC950, HA-F127 SiNPs or HA-F127@MCC950 SiNPs were injected into the corresponding puncture site. The animals were sacrificed 2 months after the procedure.

Rat TCM model and intradiscal introduction

The TCM procedure was performed with Ilizarov-type apparatus guided by X-ray (RV-32D, China), which applied a controlled axial force to the Co6/7, Co7/8, and Co8/9 intervertebral disc spaces [69]. Subsequently, a 2 μL volume of PBS, MCC950 (1 mg/mL), HA-F127, SiNPs, HA-F127@MCC950, HA-F127 SiNPs or HA-F127@MCC950 SiNPs were administrated into disc. The animals were sacrificed 1 months after the procedure.

Radiological evaluation analysis

Intervertebral disc height (IDH) was determined using X-ray and micro-CT (Scanco Viva-CT80, Switzerland) with the following resolution: 14 μm, 70 kVp, and 114 μA. Disc signal intensity was assessed on T2-weighted MRI (PharmaScan, USA) with the following settings: echo time 33 ms, repetition time 2500 ms, field of view 36 mm × 36 mm, and slice thickness 0.3 mm.

Histology and immunohistochemical analysis

For histological evaluation, specimens were fixed, decalcified, and embedded before being sectioned and stained with hematoxylin-eosin (HE, Servicebio, G1005) and safranin O-fast green (SOFG, Servicebio, G1053) to analyze NP morphological structure. To evaluate the homeostasis of the ECM and the expression of target proteins, immunohistochemistry and immunofluorescence staining were further performed. The antibodies are detailed in Table S2. Images were acquired using a microscope (Nikon, ECLIPSE E100).

Isolation of NP cells (NPCs)

Rat NPCs (rNPCs) and human NPCs (hNPCs) were isolated from the tail NP tissues of 4-week-old SD rats and from NP specimens of surgical patients with LDH (Grade III) respectively following enzymatic digestion with 0.25% EDTA-trypsin (Gibco) and 0.2 mg/mL Type II collagenase (Sigma, V900892).

Cell transfection

All si-RNA and plasmids synthesis were performed by Genebio (Shandong, China), and lentivirus synthesis by Genechem (Shanghai, China). For siRNA transfection, 50 nM siRNA targeting Mafb (si-Mafb) or Piezo1 (si-Piezo1), along with negative control siRNA (si-NC) were transfected into rNPCs via Lipofectamine 3000 reagent (ThermoFisher, L3000015) for 48 h. For plasmids transfection, 1 μg plasmid targeting Mafb (p-Mafb) or negative control plasmid (p-NC) were transfected into rNPCs using Lipofectamine 3000 and P3000 reagent for 8 h. For lentivirus transduction, 1 × 107 TU/mL lentivirus targeting Mafb (Lv-Mafb) or negative control lentivirus (Lv-NC) was used to transduce rNPCs for 24 h.

Cell mechanical loading

To simulate physical mechanical stimulation, hNPCs were cultured using a programmable air compression system (Cell and Force, China).

LDH release assay

Given that LDH release levels significantly increase during pyroptosis, the LDH release assay was performed according to the manufacturer's instructions (Beyotime, C0016). Briefly, the supernatant of hNPCs was collected after intervention, transferred to a 96-well plate, and mixed with the LDH detection working solution. The mixture was then incubated for 30 min at room temperature in the dark. Relative quantification was achieved by measuring the absorbance at 490 nm (Flash, SuPerMax 3100).

Western blot analysis

Proteins were extracted from NPCs, separated by SDS-PAGE, and transferred onto PVDF membranes. After blocking, the membranes were incubated with specific primary antibodies followed by secondary antibodies. The target protein bands were ultimately detected using a chemiluminescence imaging system (Tanon 5200, Shanghai). The antibodies are listed in Table S3.

RT-qPCR analysis

Total RNA extraction and reverse transcription were performed using the RNA-Quick Purification Kit (ES Science, RN001) and HiScript III All-in-one RT SuperMix (Vazyme, R333-01), respectively. mRNA expression levels were quantified using TB Green Premix Ex Taq (TaKaRa, RR420A) on the CFX Duet Real-Time PCR System (Bio-Rad). The primer sequences are listed in Table S4.

Immunofluorescence analysis

After fixation and permeabilization, NPCs were blocked and then incubated with specific primary antibodies, followed by their respective secondary antibodies and DAPI (Solarbio, C0065). Fluorescence images were captured with a laser confocal microscope (Nikon, CrestOptics X-Light V3). The antibodies are listed in Table S5.

Dual luciferase reporter gene assay

After co-transfection with the plasmid containing the NLRP3 promoter region and the synthesized plasmid overexpressing Mafb or NC (blank vector as control) for 48 h, hNPCs were harvested. The relative activity of Firefly luciferase to Renilla luciferase was then measured using a Dual Luciferase Reporter Assay Kit (Vazyme, DL101-01).

Measurement of nuclear DNA and cytosolic mtDNA

According to the manufacturer's instructions (Bestbio, BB-36021), after lysing the hNPCs, one quarter of the cell suspension was used to extract total cellular DNA, and the remaining part was used to isolate nuclear and cytoplasmic components (including mitochondria). Half of the obtained cytosolic fractions were then used to extract total cytosolic DNA, while the other half were further centrifuged at 12000 × g for 20 min at 4 °C to obtain pure cytosolic fractions (excluding mitochondria). DNA from each fraction was purified using a DNA extraction kit (TIANGEN, DP316). Subsequently, general PCR (Vazyme, P222) was performed, followed by agarose gel electrophoresis to visualize the DNA abundance, and RT-qPCR to quantitatively analyze DNA content. The levels of pure nuclear DNA and cytosolic mtDNA were normalized to the total cellular DNA and total cytosolic DNA, respectively. The primer sequences are listed in Table S4.

Preparation and characterization of hydrogel

OHA and AOP127 (containing 10 wt% amino-functionalized SiNPs) solutions were mixed at a 3:1 vol ratio to fabricate HA-F127 SiNPs hydrogel. To visualize and quantify the injectability and self-healing behavior, a tracer dye was mixed into the hydrogel. DNA adsorption capacity assay of SiNPs with different N/P ratios was evaluated by agarose gel electrophoresis. Besides, HA-F127 SiNPs were characterized by electron microscope and Zeta-Nanosizer. Next, MCC950 (MCE, HY-12815), dissolved in PBS, was combined with HA-F127 SiNPs to achieve a final concentration of 1 mg/mL. Drug release was assessed by monitoring the analyte concentration at 230 nm using HPLC.The dynamic rheological properties of the hydrogel were evaluated using HAAKE MARS III (ThermoFisher, USA) [70].

In vitro test for hydrogel stability

The hydrogel (200 μL) was incubated with PBS (1 mL) at 37 °C for 2 h. The degradation rate (DR) and swelling rate (SR) were calculated respectively using the following equations: DR (%) = DR (%) = (W0-Wt)/W0 × 100%; SR (%) = SR (%) = (Wt-W0)/W0 × 100%, where W0 is the initial weight of the sample and Wt is the weight at the measured time point.

Compression and fatigue cycle tests

All tests were performed under ambient conditions using a universal testing machine (Go-tech, GT-7001). Notably, in the compression and fatigue cycle experiments, cylindrical hydrogel samples with a height of 5 mm and a diameter of 10 mm were prepared, and the loading and unloading speeds were both set at 5 mm/min. Subsequently, the compressive resistance and fatigue resistance were recorded.

Statistical analysis

Data from at least three independent biological replicates are expressed as mean ± SD. Statistical analyses were performed in GraphPad Prism 9, employing Student's t-test for two-group comparisons or One-way ANOVA followed by Tukey's multiple comparisons test for multi-group comparisons. Significance levels are denoted as: ns (not significant), ∗P < 0.05, ∗∗P < 0.01, ∗∗∗P < 0.001, and ∗∗∗∗P < 0.0001.
