The 3D microstructural analysis of gustatory papillae and taste buds in the dog (Canis lupus familiaris, Canidae, Carnivora)
Authors: Barbara Plewa, Kinga Skieresz-Szewczyk, Hanna Jackowiak
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Microstructural LM and SEM studies of fungiform (Fu), vallate (Vp), and foliate papillae (Fo) were conducted on the entire dog tongue. To demonstrate tongue areas with particular sensitivity to taste stimuli, 3D reconstruction technique of serial histoslides of papillae in Amira™ software was used for the first time. Obtained data indicated the presence of caudally bent Fu’s, in which the diameter and the number of taste buds (Tbs) per single papilla increased caudally, and amounted to 29. In Fu’s distinguished four types of connective tissue cores with variable distribution of Tbs. Paired Vp’s were characterized by a decrease in diameter caudally, together with the number of Tbs from 1285 to 332. Fo’s with irregular folia had a variable distribution of Tbs, which amounted to 1444 per papilla. The analysis of the total number of Tbs on particular tongue parts indicated their higher number on the lingual apex, as the area of food preselection and the caudal part of the lingual body, with 93% of all Tbs, which is essential in taste analysis during food crushing before rapid swallowing. The 3D method establishes a new standard for precise structural and functional research of gustatory papillae and their taste buds in mammals.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-54210-y.

Introduction

Basic knowledge of the morphology of the dog’s tongue is mainly described in textbooks and a few scientific reports for veterinarians, biologists, and pet owners1–8. Characteristic anatomical features of the elongated, well-protrusible tongue are the median sulcus, which extends along the midline of the dorsal surface of the lingual mucosa from the rounded apex to the caudal part of the lingual body, and the lyssa, located beneath the mucosa on the ventral surface of the tongue3,6. In the lingual mucosa of dogs, there are three types of gustatory papillae, namely fungiform (Fu’s), vallate (Vp’s), and foliate papillae (Fo’s), and two types of mechanical papillae, i.e., filiform papillae (Fi’s) and conical papillae (Co’s)2–4,7,8. In earlier studies of dogs, information about Fo’s was described generally, without microstructural analysis1,4,5.

In wild-living canids, anatomical and microstructural studies were conducted in the silver and arctic fox, the raccoon dog, the Japanese marten, the Japanese badger, the least weasel, the black-backed jackal, and the wolf and, in relation to dogs, show such similarities in anatomy as an elongated tongue with a rounded apex and dorsally the median sulcus, as well as numerous Fu’s, 2–3 symmetrical pairs of Vp’s, and posterolateral distributed Fo’s9–16. In the Japanese badgers, the least weasel, and the black-backed jackal noted the lack of Fo’s13–15.

Dogs are facultative carnivores; however, due to their adaptation to a high-starch diet, some authors consider them omnivores17–19. The most characteristic features of a dog’s feeding behavior include rapid swallowing of large food particles, as a legacy from its wolf ancestor, and bite force, which is greatest on the molar teeth17,18,20. Dogs were presented as highly sensitive mammals capable of distinguishing sweet, sour, salty, and bitter tastes4,21–24. Moreover, Kurihara (2020)24 reported that taste responses to umami induced by synergism and sodium chloride levels in food are similar to those of humans, a consequence of domestication and adaptation to human food.

Although knowledge of the sense of taste in dogs, a popular pet, is increasing, many information gaps remain regarding the microstructure of gustatory papillae and the taste bud system. Previous studies conducted in various feeding groups of mammals, i.e., herbivorous (ruminants and lagomorphs), and carnivores (cats), using a three-dimensional reconstruction technique, have provided new perspectives on the microstructural analysis of the gustatory papillae and taste bud system25–27. An important aspect of these studies was the identification of variation in the shape of the internal connective tissue skeleton in gustatory papillae, as well as a pattern of the distribution and number of Tbs on surface papillae along the tongue. Based on the results, the average number of Tbs per tongue part was estimated, and the areas of the tongue important for taste analysis were identified. Application of the 3D technique in herbivorous mammals revealed marked underestimation of Tb numbers in all papilla types and across the entire tongue compared with previous LM- and SEM-based microstructural analyses limited to a single papilla.

The aim of the present study was to conduct a microstructural LM and SEM analysis of Fu’s, Vp’s, and Fo’s with a 3D reconstruction technique to demonstrate tongue areas with particular sensitivity to taste stimuli in dogs. The computer-aided 3D reconstruction method will allow to (i) describe the microstructural diversity of CTCs of gustatory papillae on each part of the tongue, (ii) determine the arrangement and number of Tbs in each type of gustatory papillae, and (iii) estimate of the number of Tbs on the studied tongue parts as well as on the entire tongue of the dog.

Results

In the studied dogs, the elongated tongue exhibited a median sulcus on the dorsal surface of the lingual mucosa, extending from the apex of the tongue to approximately half the tongue’s length, or, in 3 cases, to the entire length. The mean tongue length was ca. 10.4 cm, while the tongue width was from around 2.2 cm on the lingual apex to 3.8 cm on the lingual body. On the dorsal surface of the tongue, three types of gustatory papillae, i.e., fungiform papillae (Fu’s), vallate papillae (Vp’s), and foliate papillae (Fo’s), and two types of mechanical papillae, namely filiform papillae (Fi’s) and conical papillae (Co’s) were observed.

Fungiform papillae

The Fu’s were arranged evenly on the dorsal surface of the apex, the rostral and caudal part of the lingual body, and the area between the rows of Vp’s (Figs. 1a and b, 2a, 3a and 4a). Only in one studied specimen, a single flattened Fu was observed on the ventral surface of the tongue (Fig. 1f). The papillae were generally rounded in outline with convex dorsal surfaces and densely surrounded, but not covered by keratinized processes of mechanical Fi’s (Figs. 1c, 2b, 3b and 4b). The Fu’s were caudally bent from half of their height; only a few of them, located between Vp’s, were straight (Figs. 5a, d, g and j, 2d, 6a and b and 7a and b). The total number of Fu’s along the whole tongue ranged from 190 to 267.

On the edge of the apex of the tongue, around 9 Fu’s were arranged linearly with an average diameter of 250.0 μm and height of 336.4 μm (Supplementary material 1 A). The massive Fi’s surrounding Fu’s had one sharpened main process and 4–6 smaller lateral processes, bending toward the dorsal surface of the tongue, or were directed laterally (Figs. 1c, d). The Fu’s were covered by multilayered keratinized epithelium, with a mean thickness of 43.9 μm, while the horny layer was 4.9 μm (Figs. 1a, d; Supplementary material 1B).

The shape of the CTCs was club-like, resulting from the increase in CTC diameter from the base to the top (Supplementary material 1 C, Figs. 1h and 5b and e). The average CTC height was 269.5 μm (Supplementary material 1 A). On the apical part of Fu’s CTCs, small collagenous protrusions and circular depressions were observed (Figs. 1h and 5e). The dorsal surface of CTCs provided a platform for Tbs, which were located caudally or caudo-laterally. The number of Tbs, determined on the 3D models, equalled 7 and 9, whereas their average diameter was 35.5 μm (Figs. 5e, f).

On the dorsal surface of the tongue, from the apex to the rostral part of the lingual body, approximately 128 Fu’s were distinguished. Their diameter amounted to 143.3 μm on the apex and 220.7 μm on the rostral part of the lingual body, whereas the height of the papillae was 385.8 μm and 358.1 μm, respectively (Supplementary material 1 A). The Fi’s were pointed caudally and structured from a single main process and 3–8 smaller lateral processes (Figs. 1c and 2a and b). The average thickness of the multilayered epithelium covering the Fu’s was 43.0 μm on the lingual apex, and 56.2 μm on the rostral part of the lingual body, while the mean thickness of the horny layer equalled 6.0 μm and 7.3 μm, respectively (Figs. 5g and j and 2d; Supplementary material 1B).

On the apex of the tongue, both club-like and columnar-like CTCs were identified, whereas only finger-like CTCs occurred in the rostral lingual body (Figs. 1g, 5h and k and 2e and f). In the club-like type of the CTC, the dorsal diameter was wider than the medial one, in the columnar-like type, these two diameters were comparable, while in the finger-like type, the diameter decreased from the base to the papilla top (Supplementary material 1 C). The mean height of the CTC was 334.4 μm on the lingual apex and 297.7 μm on the rostral part of the lingual body (Supplementary material 1 A). On the dorsal surface of the CTCs located on the apex of the tongue, shallow depressions were visible (Figs. 1g, 5h and k and 2c, e and f). Figure 1e presents some taste pores on the dorsal surface of Fu’s.

In Fu’s, on the lingual apex, 3 and 6 Tbs were distributed evenly or caudally, while on the rostral part of the lingual body, 2 and 4 Tbs were arranged medially or latero-medially (Figs. 5h, i, k and l and 2e–h). The average diameter of Tbs in the rostral part of the tongue amounted to 32.7 μm.

On the surface of the caudal part of the lingual body, with the area between Vp’s, approximately 89 Fu’s were observed, whereof 23 Fu’s occurred between Vp’s (Fig. 4a, b). The average diameter and height of Fu’s on the caudal part of the lingual body were 329.9 μm and 544.5 μm, respectively, while Fu’s located between Vp’s had 550.7 μm in diameter and 599.1 μm in height (Supplementary material 1 A). The Fi’s were bent caudally and structured from one main process and 2–4 smaller lateral processes, which were reduced in the area between Vp’s (Figs. 3a–c and 4b). The Fu’s were covered by multilayered keratinized epithelium with a mean thickness of 65.5 μm on the caudal part of the lingual body and 70.7 μm between Vp’s, whereas the thickness of the horny layer equalled 6.1 μm and 5.0 μm, respectively (Figs. 6a and b and 7a and b; Supplementary material 1B).

In both the caudal part of the lingual body and the area between Vp’s, columnar-like types of CTC were observed, with similar diameters in the medial and dorsal parts (Figs. 6c and d and 7c; Supplementary material 1 C). Additionally, in the area between Vp’s, the CTCs of Fu’s included a balloon-like type, characterized by a wider medial part than dorsal part (Fig. 7d; Supplementary material 1 C). The height of the CTCs on the caudal part of the lingual body, and between Vp’s was 479.0 μm, and 533.1 μm, respectively (Supplementary material 1 A). On the lateral surfaces of the columnar-like types of Fu’s CTCs, longitudinal lateral laminae were observed. In the area between Vp’s, the lateral laminae were shorter and restricted to the upper area of the CTC (Figs. 3e, 6d and 7c and e). Moreover, on the dorsal edges of CTCs located on the caudal part of the lingual body, circular in outline depressions were observed (Fig. 3f). In turn, on the entire dorsal and lateral surfaces of balloon-like CTCs, small collagenous protrusions were noticed (Figs. 7d, f).

SEM electronogram showed the dorsal surface of Fu’s with few taste pores (Fig. 3d). On the 3D models of Fu’s, on the caudal part of the lingual body, 6 and 12 Tbs were observed caudally or evenly on the dorsal surface of the CTCs (Figs. 6c–f and 7c and e). In the Fu’s between Vp’s, Tbs were arranged either evenly or along lateral and caudal edges of the CTCs (Figs. 7d, f). The number of Tbs varied and amounted to 8 and 29. The Tbs in Fu’s with the columnar-like type of the CTC were wide and circular in outline, with a diameter ranging from 28.9 μm to 53.2 μm (Figs. 6b and 7a). In turn, in the Fu’s with the balloon-like CTC, Tbs were slender, with diameters ranging from 17.0 μm to 37.3 μm (Fig. 7b).

Animations presenting different types of Fu’s CTCs with Tbs arrangement are in the Supplementary materials 3–6.

Vallate papillae

The Vp’s were often arranged in pairs diverging on the right and left sides of the caudal surface of the lingual body in oblique lines in the form of an inverted V letter with a caudal truncated apex. In the studied dogs, the number of these papillae ranged from 3 to 5 (Fig. 4a). The rostrally and caudally situated pairs of Vp’s were oval or rounded in outline (Fig. 4a). The rostral pair of the papillae was characterised by higher dimensions in relation to the caudal ones. The short and long diameters of the rostral Vp’s reached 2.3 mm and 2.8 mm, respectively, while the dimensions of caudal papillae were equal and amounted to 2.2 mm (Supplementary material 2 A).

The continuous papillary furrow separated the papillary body from the papillary ridge (Figs. 4b, c). The ridge of the papillae was composed of conjoined bases of Co’s, whose pointed tips were visible on the caudal part of the papillae (Figs. 4a, b). The diameter width of the papillary body in rostrally and caudally situated Vp’s was 1,091.0 μm and 866.9 μm, respectively. In turn, the height of the papillary body in the rostral and caudal papillae amounted to 929.9 μm and 1,096.7 μm, respectively (Supplementary material 2 A). The width of the papillary ridge reached 531.9 μm in the rostral Vp’s and 557.0 μm in the caudal ones (Supplementary material 2 A). The average width of the papillary furrow in rostral and caudal Vp’s was 54.4 μm and 70.3 μm, respectively (Supplementary material 2 A).

The Vp’s were covered by non-keratinized multilayered epithelium, with a thickness of 69.8 μm on the dorsum of the papillary body, 66.7 μm in the lateral surface of the papillary body, as well as 82.6 μm on the dorsum of the papillary ridge, and 57.1 μm on the lateral surface of the papillary ridge (Fig. 8a; Supplementary material 1B).

The oval in outline CTC of the body of Vp was described as mushroom-like (Figs. 4d and 8a–c). The width of the CTC papillary body in rostral Vp was 1.6 mm, and its height was 877.6 μm, whereas in the caudal Vp, these values were 1.0 mm and 1,066.5 μm, respectively (Supplementary material 2 A). On the dorsal surface of the papillary body CTC, 2–3 longitudinal and circular depressions were noticed, whereas the lateral surfaces of the CTC were smooth (Figs. 4d and 8b, d and e). Figure 4d presents a discontinuous CTC of the papillary ridge, which in the caudal part of the Vp’s, consisted of separate collagen skeletons of Co’s. On the dorsal surface of the CTC of the papillary ridge, rounded depressions were observed, while the lateral surfaces were smooth (Fig. 4d).

Taste buds in Vp’s were present in three areas, i.e., the lateral and dorsal surface of the papillary body and the lateral surfaces of the papillary ridge (Figs. 8a–f). On the 3D model of the rostral, bigger Vp’s, out of 1,285 Tbs were present. Most of the Tbs, that is 1,272, were distributed in 4–23 rows on the lateral surfaces of the papillary body, whereas only 6 Tbs occurred on its dorsal surface, and 7 Tbs were located on the lateral surface of the papillary ridge (Figs. 8c–f). The total number of Tbs on the caudal, smaller in size Vp was 332, of which 270 Tbs were arranged in 2–8 rows on the lateral surfaces of the papilla body, 52 Tbs occurred in two strands on the dorsal surface of the papillary body, and 10 Tbs were located on the lateral surfaces of the papillary ridge (Figs. 8a, b, d–f). The diameter of Tbs in Vp’s ranged from 21.3 μm to 57.7 μm.

Foliate papillae

The Fo’s were located on both sides of the caudo-lateral surfaces of the lingual body. The papillae were round in outline and were surrounded rostrally, caudally, and dorsally by Co’s (Fig. 9a). The Fo’s were divided into 7–8 separate folia with irregular courses. Occasionally, some folia exhibited an S-shaped (Figs. 9a and 10b). Dorsally, a single folium was fused with the base of Co’s (Fig. 9a). The average diameter of Fo’s was 3.5 mm.

The width and height of a single folium were diverse. Generally, the narrow folia were higher than the wide ones (Figs. 9d–f; Supplementary material 2B). The width of a narrower folium was ca. 104.0 μm, while in wider folium was ca. 1.1 mm (Supplementary material 2B). The height of a single folium ranged from 104.0 μm to 718.8 μm, whereas the papillary furrows between folia have a width from 14.5 to 86.3 μm (Supplementary material 2B). It should be added that the wide and short folia were surrounded by shallow furrows, while the narrow and high folia by deeper furrows (Figs. 9e–g). The average depth of the shallow and deep furrows reached 239.6 μm and 506.8 μm, respectively. Figure 10b and d show that some folia splitin the middle into two parts and then fusetogether again. Some folia can even split into 3 narrower folia. The observation of the macerated tissue samples and LM cross-sections revealed that serous glands were opened not only at the bottom of the papillary furrows, but also on the dorsal surface of the folia and located in the surroundings of Fo’s (Figs. 9c–g). Furthermore, the LM cross-sections indicated openings on the dorsal surface of folia leading to depressions with lymph follicles in the CTC of folia (Fig. 9g). The Fo’s were covered by non-keratinized multilayered epithelium, which was thicker on the dorsal surface of the folia than on the lateral surfaces, with a thickness of 87.2 μm and 50.2 μm, respectively (Figs. 9e–g; Supplementary material 1B).

The CTC of the Fo’s was irregular in shape and varied between individual studied specimens (Figs. 9c and d and 10a and b). The single folium generally consisted of a single CTC lamina with a width and height of 491.9 μm and 449.4 μm, respectively (Figs. 9c–g and 10a and b; Supplementary material 2B). On the dorsal surface of some folia, longitudinal and sometimes deep depressions were noticed, which divided one folium into two, but only over a short distance (Figs. 9c and 10c, e and f). In the area of the depressions, short and sharp collagenous processes were visible (Fig. 9c). The CTCs on the lateral surfaces of folia were smooth (Figs. 9d and 10c and f).

The Tbs in Fo’s were distributed on the dorsal and lateral surfaces, either along the entire length of folia or restricted to 1/3 or 2/3 of the length (Fig. 10a–f). The SEM electronograms revealed the presence of some taste pores on the dorsal surface of folia (Fig. 9b). In some folia, Tbs occurred only on one side, while in others, they were present only on the upper part of the folia (Figs. 9e–g and 10c–f). The 3D model of Fo’s with wider and shorter folia revealed the presence of 1,074 Tbs, of which 952 Tbs arranged in 3–16 rows were noted on its dorsal surface, while 122 Tbs distributed into 2–8 rows were on the lateral surfaces of folia (Figs. 10a, c, d; Supplementary material 2B). Figures 10b, e, and f present a 3D model of the Fo’s with narrower but higher folia, containing as many as 1,814 Tbs, among which only 576 located in 2–9 rows were present on the dorsal surface of the folia, whereas the 1,238 Tbs arranged in 2–13 rows were distributed on the lateral surfaces (Fig. 10c; Supplementary material 2B). The diameter of Tbs ranged from 22.4 μm to 58.9 μm.

An animation presenting Fo CTC with Tbs arrangement is in Supplementary material 8.

Statistical analysis

The statistical analysis revealed that significant differences in the number of Fu’s were only between the dorsal surface of the apex and the area between Vp’s, as well as between the caudal surface of the lingual body and the area between Vp’s.

Significant differences in the diameter of Fu’s were observed between all tongue parts, except between the edge of the tongue and the caudal part of the lingual body. Similarly, significant differences in the papillae height were between all tongue parts, with the exception of the edge of the tongue and the rostral part of the lingual body, the caudal part of the lingual body, and the area between Vp’s.

No statistically significant differences were found in the thickness of the multilayered epithelium covering Fu’s between the edge of the tongue and the dorsal surface of the apex, between the dorsal surface of the apex and the rostral part of the body, as well as between the rostral part of the lingual body and the area between Vp’s. In contrast, significant differences in the thickness of the horny layer were distinguished between the edge of the tongue and the rostral part of the lingual body, between the edge of the tongue and the caudal part of the lingual body, and between the rostral part of the lingual body and the area between Vp’s. Differences among other tongue parts were also statistically significant.

No statistically significant differences in the number and diameter of Tbs in Fu’s.

Discussion

In accordance with the research objectives, for the first time in dogs, a computer-aided three-dimensional analysis of the microstructure of fungiform papillae (Fu’s), vallate papillae (Vp’s), and foliate papillae (Fo’s) and their taste buds (Tbs) was performed. To date, such 3D reconstruction techniques for microstructural analysis of gustatory papillae in carnivores have been applied in the cat26.

Generally, the pattern of distribution of Fu’s on the dorsal surface of the lingual apex and the body in dogs was similar to that of other, earlier studied canine species5,9–12,14,16. However, only in the dog and the ferret (Mustelidae), Fu’s were also arranged along the edge of the lingual tip28. In turn, the Fu’s located between Vp’s are species-specific to dogs.

Summarizing the numeric data of Fu’s in the dog revealed ca. 226 Fu’s, of which 137 (60%) were located on the rostral part of the tongue. Of the 89 Fu’s counted on the caudal part of the lingual body, 23 Fu’s (25%) were located between Vp’s. Comparison of these results with the 220 Fu’s reported in cats indicates a comparable total number of these papillae in these carnivorous species26. Quantitative analysis showed that the diameter and height of canine Fu’s generally increase from the lingual apex to the caudal part of the lingual body. The caudally situated Fu’s were 54% wider and 38% higher than the rostrally located ones. Exceptionally, on the tongue edge, the papillae were 43% wider and 12% shorter than those on the dorsal surface of the apex, which was statistically proven. A caudally increasing diameter of Fu’s was also described only in the ferret, while in silver and arctic foxes, the papillae diameters decreased from the tongue edge to the lingual body9,12,28. Iwasaki and Sakata (1985)5 reported that in the beagle dog, the diameters of Fu’s ranged from 300 to 400 μm, with no differences among tongue parts.

Along the tongue, Fu’s were densely surrounded by mechanical filiform papillae (Fi’s) with numerous keratinized processes, whose number and size decrease caudally. However, an important observation from a functional perspective was the comparable heights of Fu’s and Fi’s, which allow full exposure of Fu’s surface with Tbs, enabling effective taste perception even during rapid food transport along the tongue.

The 3D reconstruction of Fu’s and their microstructures on particular tongue parts allowed for the visualization of four types of internal CTCs, which constitute a skeleton for nerves, blood vessels, and a superficial platform for Tbs. Among club-like, columnar-like, finger-like, and balloon-like CTC, the most common were the club-like and columnar-like types. The morphometric analysis confirmed that, together with the previously mentioned increase in the height of Fu’s, its internal CTC increased caudally by approximately 40%. Columnar-like CTCs were observed to be the highest, regardless of the tongue part. In our previous 3D study on Fu’s in short-haired cats, four types of CTC were also distinguished. Similar to the dog, columnar- and club-like CTC types were characterized; however, the other mushroom- and bud-like CTC types were species-specific to short-haired cats26. Among carnivores, only Yoshimura et al. (2009)13 described columnar-like CTCs in Japanese badgers.

The SEM and 3D analysis of the surface of Fu’s CTCs revealed that the presence of lateral laminae and protrusions as structures providing stronger anchoring of the epithelium depends on the tongue part. On the caudal part of the tongue, both lateral laminae and protrusions were observed, whereas rostrally, only protrusions on the dorsal surface of the CTC were noticed. Contrary to dogs, observation of Fu’s CTCs in the short-hair cat revealed the presence of lateral collagenous laminae or protrusions on each of the described CTC types26. In the Japanese marten and the black-backed jackal, CTC had several depressions on the dorsal surface or was smooth dorsally and laterally11,14.

An important point of the current microstructural studies was to determine the exact distribution and number of Tbs. Using 3D reconstruction techniques, it was precisely determined that Tbs were mostly grouped caudally or latero-medially on Fu’s surface, although some Fu’s had evenly dispersed Tbs. These data showed that positioning Tbs in groups is directly related to the caudal bending of Fu’s and may facilitate efficient taste analysis of small food particles and water.

A comparative analysis of the number of Tbs in a single Fu showed that the number of Tbs increased caudally with increasing Fu diameter from 3 to even 29. The highest number of Tbs was observed in the widest Fu located between Vp’s, and was from 58% to 79% greater compared with data in the remaining Fu’s. However, the differences were not statistically significant. Previously, Holliday (1940)1 reported that the maximum number of Tbs per Fu was 6. As in a dog, in the short-haired cat, the number of Tbs and the diameter of Fu increase caudally, and this increase has been determined to be 38%26. The diameter of Tbs on the rostral part of the tongue was generally 17% smaller than that observed on the caudal part of the tongue. However, due to the high variability in the number of Tbs in individual Fu’s located between Vp’s, differences in Tb diameter of up to 38% were also established.

The 3D characterization of the taste bud system of Fu’s in dogs was intended to estimate the average number of Tbs in all Fu’s on the entire tongue, which on the dorsal surface of the lingual apex, rostral, and caudal parts of the lingual body equaled 324, 195, and 1,008, respectively. As a result, on the entire tongue was 1,527 Tbs in Fu’s, whereas the previous data provided by Holliday (1940)1, determined on serial sections with a thickness of 20 μm, showed an underestimated number of Tbs in Fu, amounting to 1,444. A similar analysis of the number of Tbs in a short-haired cat indicates 5,703 Tbs in all Fu26.

As mentioned in the Introduction, in Carnivores, and in dogs as well, 2–3 pairs of Vp’s occur in symmetrical pairs along forming a V-shaped oblique line. However, in the ferret described by Takemura et al. (2009)28 indicated a species-specific number of Vp’s ranged from 8 to 12. A characteristic feature of Vp’s in dogs was a decreasing diameter of the papillae in the caudal direction, which was also described in the silver fox and ferret9,28. In turn, in the arctic fox, this pattern reversed12. Generally, the Vp’s are surrounded by the continuous ridge formed by the single or fused Co’s4,9,10,12. In the arctic fox, the Vp’s in the 3rd pair were surrounded by two rings of the connected Co’s, whereas in the studied dog, only on the caudal part of the papillary ridge, separate Co’s were observed12. According to Takemura et al. (2009)28, the papillary ridge in ferrets was fully discontinuous.

A characteristic feature of the mushroom-like Vp CTC was the presence of rounded and longitudinal outline depressions on the dorsal surface of the papillary body, along with varying in size circular depressions on the dorsal surface of the papillary ridge. The CTC was covered by a multilayered epithelium lacking a horny layer. In Japanese badgers, the Vp CTC was covered with small protrusions, whereas in black-backed jackals, many additional ditches/grooves were observed13,14.

El-Sharaby et al. (2014)29 noted that Tbs in dogs’ Vp’s are located on the dorsal and lateral surfaces of the papillary body, as well as in the lateral epithelium of the papillary ridge, findings that correspond to our observations in dogs. Our 3D models of Vp revealed that the distribution and number of Tbs per Vp appear to depend on papilla size; thus, in smaller Vp, there were 74% fewer Tbs than in larger ones. In smaller Vp’s, 15% of all Tbs occurred on the dorsal surface of the papillary body, while in the bigger Vp’s, it was only 1%. Regardless of rostrally and caudally positioned Vp’s, the diameters of Tbs were comparable. Considering that approximately 4 Vp’s occurred in dogs, the estimated total number of Tbs reached 3,234, which is about 92% higher than previously reported in puppies by Holliday (1940)1.

Among wild-living Canidae, Fo’s have been reported in the silver and arctic fox, wolf, and raccoon, and in Mustelidae only in Japanese martens9–12,16. In domestic dogs, only some authors mentioned their presence1,4,5. Earlier, Chibuzo (1979)4 reported 8–13 folia in dogs’ Fo’s, which exceeds the values of 7–8 folia described in this study. In other canids, the number of folia varies and amounts to 4–5 in silver fox and wolf, as well as 7–9 folia in the arctic fox9,12,16. A common feature of Fo’s in wild Canidae, also confirmed in present studies, was the fusion of the folia with the bases of Co’s9,10. Only Chibuzo (1979)4 and Haligur et al. (2018)16 described a typical, symmetrical, parallel arrangement of the folia in Fo’s, which contrasts with the irregular structure of the folia observed in our studies. 3D models enabling observation on the dorsal surface of some folia, longitudinal depressions with short collagenous protrusions as structures strengthened the anchorage of the non-keratinized multilayered epithelium. It was determined that the irregular structure of Fo’s resulting from the characteristic folia course, tendency to folia splitting/fusing, and the variable folia height and width directly affects the arrangement of Tbs. The obtained data showed that Fo’s with wider and lower folia had 88% of Tbs on the dorsal surface, whereas the papillae with higher and narrower folia had 68% of Tbs on the lateral surfaces of the folia. The estimated total number of Tbs in the two Fo’s was 2,888.

Quantitative analysis of Tbs across all types of gustatory papillae and along the entire tongue estimated the total number at 7,649. Comparing these results to observations conducted by Holliday (1940)1 in the puppy, who estimated 1,706 Tbs only in Fu’s and Vp’s, without Fo’s, were 78% higher. The 3D study of two types of gustatory papillae in short-haired cats revealed that the estimated number of Tbs was higher than in dogs, reaching 8,26526.

With regard to the Tbs number on the particular tongue parts and the tongue functions during food intake, two tongue areas with an increased number of Tbs and, consequently, greater sensitivity to taste stimuli have been identified: (1) the lingual apex, which is functionally associated with the initial contact and preselection of food and, in the case of dogs, with the perception of the water taste, and (2) caudal surface of the body of the tongue, where determined 93% of the total number of Tbs. This, together with biomechanical studies of canine bite force, indicating that dogs bite harder on their molars regardless of breed, highlights the important role of the caudal part of the tongue in the taste analysis of crushed food20. The caudal part of the tongue is also the site of precise analysis of pre-processed, saliva-mixed food, shaped into a bolus ready to be swallowed. Our studies in the short-haired cat showed that only 58% of all Tbs were present caudally26. A possible reason for that may be the absence of Fo’s in the cat, and the distinction of three tongue areas with a higher number of Tbs, i.e., the lingual apex, the lateral surfaces of the tongue, and the caudal part of the tongue26.

To conclude, this study is the first attempt to conduct microstructural research on dog gustatory papillae from particular parts of the tongue using LM, SEM, and 3D reconstruction techniques. Based on the obtained results, the variability in the shapes of Fu’s papillae and their CTCs was stated, as well as the exact distribution and number of Tbs in each type of papilla. The quantitative data received were used to estimate the total number of Tbs across the entire tongue and to identify tongue areas with increased sensitivity to taste stimuli. The applied 3D method proved essential for a detailed, proper microstructural analysis of gustatory papillae and the taste bud system.

Materials and methods

The research material consisted of tongues from five adult domestic crossbreed dogs (Canis lupus familiaris), 2 females and 3 males, with an average body weight of 8–10 kg and an age of 6–9 years. The tongue tissues were collected from dogs euthanized due to medical reasons, excluding chronic or gastrointestinal diseases. The euthanasia procedure was performed by veterinarians at three veterinary clinics in Poznań, using the chemical agent Morbital (pentobarbital sodium and pentobarbital) from Biowet (Puławy, Poland) in a dose adjusted to the animal’s body weight (0.3–0.6 ml/kg BW). The tongues were dissected by veterinarians after the animal was euthanized and fixed in 10% neutral formaldehyde.

The macroscopic observations and morphometric analysis were performed using a digital microscope ZIP FHD (TAGARNO, ver. 1.1.3.1).

Tissue samples of 1 cm2 were dissected from the dorsal and ventral surfaces of the apex, rostral, and caudal parts of the lingual body, from the area of Vp’s, posterolateral surfaces of the lingual body, and prepared for the observation in the light microscope (LM), scanning electron microscope (SEM), and NaOH maceration for removal of mucosal epithelium. From each tongue part, 3–5 tissue samples were dissected.

Light microscopy (LM) observation

For the LM observations, the tissue samples were dehydrated in an increasing series of ethanol (70–96%, 15–20 min) and methyl benzoate, infiltrated by Blue paraffin®. At the end, the samples were serially cut into 5 μm slices on a rotary microtome (Leica RM 2055), stained using the Masson-Goldner trichrome method, and closed with a cover slide using synthetic resin DePeX. Serial histological slices were examined under a light microscope (Axioskop 2 Plus, Zeiss) using a digital camera (Gryphax). Depending on the papillae size, the documentation was performed at magnifications of x1.25 and x5.

The three-dimensional analysis of gustatory papillae, connective tissue cores (CTCs), and taste buds (Tbs)

The 3D models of all gustatory papillae were made from 5 μm serial histological slices documented under a light microscope. The number of slices used for a single 3D model was 32–125 for Fu’s, 307–328 for Vp’s, and 323–505 for Fo’s. A special record of all histoslides, including the lost and unsuitable ones, was prepared, and 3D reconstructions of gustatory papillae, their CTCs, and Tbs proceed in a 3D Visualization, Analysis, and EM Software called Amira (Thermo Scientific™, ver. 2020). The pixel sizes for histoslides at magnifications x1.25 and x5 were 1.86 μm × 1.86 μm and 0.48 μm × 0.48 μm, respectively. The 3D modeling started with converting all colored slices to black-and-white. The next step was alignment, recalculation, and histoslides segmentation. During the segmentation step, each structure, i.e., the outline of the papillary epithelium, CTCs, and Tbs, was manually marked on each histological section. The last step was resampling and generating 3D models of specific gustatory papillae, including their CTCs and all Tbs.

The scanning electron microscope (SEM) observation

For SEM observation, tissues were dehydrated in an increasing series of ethanol (70–96%, each for 10–15 min), a 1:1 ethanol-acetone solution (5 min), and 100% acetone (5 min). Later, the samples were dried in a critical point dryer using liquid CO2 (Critical Point Dryer K850, LEICA, Wetzlar, Germany), mounted on aluminum stubs with double-sided carbon tape or silver pasta, sputtered with a gold coat in Sputter Coater S150B, Edwards, and observed under SEM EVO 10 (Zeiss, Germany) at the accelerating voltage of 15 kV.

The 10% NaOH maceration technique

For CTC observation of gustatory papillae in a scanning electron microscope (SEM), the tissue samples were immersed in a 10% NaOH solution at room temperature for 8–10 days, with 4 solution changes. After removing the epithelium from the connective tissue skeleton, the samples were rinsed five times in tap water and dehydrated in an increasing series of ethanol (70–96%, each 5 min), a 1:1 solution of ethanol-acetone (5 min), and 100% acetone (5 min). Later, they were dried in a critical point dryer using liquid CO2 (Critical Point Dryer K850, LEICA, Wetzlar, Germany), mounted on aluminium stubs with double-sided carbon tape or silver paste, sputtered with a gold coat in Sputter Coater S150B (Edwards), and observed under SEM EVO 10 (Zeiss, Germany) at the accelerating voltage of 15 kV.

The histomorphometrical analysis

For Fu’s, Vp’s, and Fo’s, the measurements were done on 5 to 20 cross-sections of their medial parts. On each histological cross-section of the gustatory papilla, around 5 measurements of the height and diameter of the papillae, the height and diameter of their CTCs, the thickness of the multilayered epithelium and the horny layer, as well as the diameter of the taste buds were made. Additionally, the CTCs of Fu’s were measured in the basal, medial, and dorsal parts. Due to the visible differences in furrow width in the Vp’s, measurements were also taken at the basal, medial, and dorsal parts. All measurements were conducted using the MultiScan v.18.03 computer system (CSS, Warsaw, Poland). For the estimation of the total number of taste buds on particular tongue parts and on the entire tongue, an average number of taste buds on each tongue part was used.

The statistical analysis

The statistical analysis of quantitative data regarding the number of Fu’s, their height, diameter, as well as thickness on the multilayered epithelium with the horny layer, and the number and diameter of Tbs in Fu’s was performed in Microsoft Excel 2010. It was conducted using ANOVA, with a significance threshold of 0.05. Statistically significant results (p < 0.05) were further analyzed using a Post-Hoc Test (Bonferroni Correction), with a statistical threshold set at 0.01. Any result of p < 0.01 was described as statistically significant.

The anatomical nomenclature of the tongue structures was taken from Nomina Anatomica Veterinaria (2017) and Nomina Histologica Veterinaria (2017)30,31.
