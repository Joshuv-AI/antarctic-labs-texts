Exploration of the multi-component, multi-target, and multi-pathway mechanism of Rubia yunnanensis in inhibiting breast cancer based on network pharmacology, molecular docking, and experimental validation
Authors: Xiaojia Ma, Luyao Wang, Yanbo Hao, Wenying Chai, Min Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundBreast cancer (BC) is a malignancy associated with high morbidity and mortality. Rubia yunnanensis (RY), a traditional Chinese medicinal herb, has demonstrated inhibitory effects against various tumor types, yet its role in BC remains undefined.ObjectiveThis study investigated the effect of RY on BC and elucidated its underlying molecular regulatory mechanisms.MethodsThe role of RY in BC was assessed through CCK-8, Transwell, flow cytometry, Western blot, xenograft modeling, and immunohistochemistry assays. We employed ultra-high-performance liquid chromatography-quadrupole time-of-flight tandem mass spectrometry (UPLC-Q-TOF/MS), the PubChem and TCMSP database, and literature mining to identify the active ingredients of RY. Potential targets for these active ingredients were predicted using TargetPrediction database, while BC-regulated targets were collected from the GeneCards, DrugBank, and TTD databases. A protein-protein interaction network analysis identified core targets, followed by GO and KEGG enrichment analyses using the DAVID database. Molecular docking was performed to validate the binding affinity of the active ingredients for these core targets. Finally, Western blot analysis was used to detect the relative protein expression levels of the core targets.ResultsRY significantly reduced the viability of MDA-MB-231 and BT-549 cells, inhibited their migratory capacity, promoted apoptosis, and upregulated the expression of the E-cadherin, while downregulating the expression of N-cadherin and vimentin. These effects were concentration-dependent. In vivo studies demonstrated that RY treatment inhibited BC growth, reduced the Ki67-positive rate and the expression of N-cadherin and vimentin in mouse BC tissues, and promoted the expression of E-cadherin. Furthermore, UPLC-Q-TOF/MS analysis and database mining identified 26 active components in RY and 312 potential targets related to its action against BC. The core targets for RY in BC treatment include TP53, AKT1, IL-6, TNF, STAT3, CTNNB1, EGFR, MYC, ESR1, and CASP3. Enrichment analysis indicated significant involvement of the PI3K-AKT and MAPK signaling pathways. The key active components of RY demonstrated strong binding potential with these core target genes, namely TNF, ESR1, CASP3, EGFR, STAT3, MYC, CTNNB1, AKT1, TP53, and IL-6.ConclusionThis study reveals that RY exhibits therapeutic potential on BC through multi-ingredient, multi-target, and multi-pathway mode of action.

Introduction

Breast cancer (BC) is the most commonly diagnosed malignancy in women. In 2022, there were 2.3 million new BC cases worldwide, with an incidence that continues to rise and a trend toward affecting younger populations [1]. Clinical management of BC typically involves surgical resection, radiotherapy, chemotherapy, endocrine therapy, and targeted therapy to reduce mortality and recurrence [2]. Nevertheless, patient prognosis remains poor within five years post-treatment, and recurrence rates stay high [3]. Consequently, identifying novel therapeutic approaches and potential biological targets is critical for improving BC treatment and prognosis.

Chinese herbal medicine has been used to treat diseases in China for thousands of years. As a component of comprehensive anti-tumor therapy, traditional Chinese medicine (TCM) is widely employed and has demonstrated efficacy [4,5]. The active ingredients in TCM exert multi-target, multi-pathway, and multi-level anti-tumor activities, offering the distinct advantages of low toxicity, synergistic effects, and cost-effectiveness. Numerous TCM compounds, patent medicines, and monomeric constituents are now commonly used in the clinical management of tumors [6,7]. For instance, Yang et al. [8] showed that Kinsenoside inhibits triple-negative BC cell growth by suppressing DGAT1-mediated lipid droplet formation to induce ferroptosis. Similarly, red ginseng polysaccharide exhibits anticancer activity in lung and BC by inhibiting GPX4 and triggering ferroptosis [9].

Rubia yunnanensis (RY), derived from the dried root and rhizome of Rubia yunnanensis Diels, exhibits recuperative, anti-inflammatory, anti-oxidant, and anti-tumor properties [10,11]. The growing economic and medicinal importance of this plant is well established, with studies demonstrating its efficacy against gastrointestinal, cardiovascular, and immune-related disorders [[12], [13], [14]]. For instance, Li et al. [11] found that Rubioncolin C, a natural constituent of RY, induces apoptosis and autophagy in triple-negative BC cells and inhibits pulmonary metastasis in mice. Nevertheless, the principal bioactive components of RY and their specific targets in BC remain undefined.

Advances in bioinformatics have enabled network pharmacology and molecular docking to elucidate how TCM acts at the molecular level, supplying quantitative scientific data for its characteristically multi-ingredient, multi-target, and multi-pathway mechanisms. This study investigated the effects of RY on BC cells and in animal models, while ultra-high-performance liquid chromatography-quadrupole time-of-flight tandem mass spectrometry (UPLC-Q-TOF-MS/MS) was employed to analyze its effective constituents. Network pharmacology identified key targets and regulatory pathways associated with both BC and RY, revealing novel molecular targets for BC diagnosis and therapy.

Materials and methods

Preparation of RY extract

Thirty grams of RY was soaked in purified water for 0.5-1 h and decocted twice, first for 0.5 h and again for 0.3 h. The two decoctions were combined, concentrated, and filtered to yield a final concentration of 1 g crude drug per mL. The prepared extract was stored under refrigeration.

Cell culture

MDA-MB-231 (Cat: CL-0150) and BT-549 (Cat: CL-0041) cell lines were kindly provided by Wuhan Pricella Biotechnology Co., Ltd. Cells were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), 10 μg/mL insulin, and 1% penicillin-streptomycin solution. All cultures were maintained in a humidified incubator at 37 °C with 5% CO2.

Cell treatment

MDA-MB-231 and BT-549 cells were treated for 24 h and 48 h with RY at concentrations of 0, 5, 10, 20, 40, 80, 160 μg/mL RY, or with 4 μM paclitaxel (PTX, MedChem Express, Monmouth Junction, NJ, USA).

CCK-8

The treated MDA-MB-231 and BT-549 cells were incubated with 10 μL of CCK-8 reagent (Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) per well for 4 h. The optical density value of each well was measured at a wavelength of 450 nm and recorded.

Transwell

MDA-MB-231 and BT-549 cells were suspended in serum-free medium and treated with RY and PTX. A 100 μL aliquot of each cell suspension (1 × 106 cells/mL) was seeded into the upper chamber of a Transwell insert (Corning, Corning, NY, USA). The lower chamber was filled with medium containing 20% FBS. Following a 24-h incubation, the cells that had migrated to the lower chamber were fixed with formaldehyde for 30 min. These cells were stained with crystal violet (Beijing Solarbio, China) for 3 min and subsequently rinsed with water. Three random fields per well were imaged and quantified using a microscope.

Flow cytometry

Cell apoptosis was assessed using an Annexin V-FITC/PI apoptosis kit (Elabscience Biotechnology, China). Cells were collected, trypsinized, and centrifuged at 1000 rpm for 5 min at 4 °C. The pelleted cells were resuspended in binding buffer after counting. This suspension was incubated with Annexin V-FITC and PI in the dark for 10 min prior to analysis by flow cytometry.

Western blot

RIPA lysate (Beijing Solarbio Science & Technology Co., Ltd., China) was used to lyse the cells and tumor tissues completely. Protein concentrations were measured spectrophotometrically. The samples were separated by sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis and transferred onto polyvinylidene fluoride membranes via wet transfer. Membranes were blocked with 5% skim milk for 1 h, rinsed with TBST, and incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. After further TBST washes, the membranes were incubated with a secondary antibody for 2 h. Finally, enhanced chemiluminescence reagent was applied to the membranes for 2 min in the dark, and signals were detected using a gel imaging system.

Animals and xenograft model

This study was conducted in compliance with the Animal Research: Reporting of In Vivo Experiments (ARRIVE) guidelines 2.0 and was approved by the Experimental Animal Ethics Committee of Yunnan Labreal Biotechnology Co., Ltd (PZ20230305).

Mice were treated with different concentrations of RY to observe whether RY exerts toxic effects in normal mice. Twenty-four BALB/c nude mice (6-8 weeks old, 20 ± 2 g) were obtained from Hunan Silaike Jingda Experimental Animal Co., Ltd. The mice were randomly assigned to four groups. Each group received a daily intraperitoneal injection of RY at 0, 2.5, 12.5, or 25 g/kg. The control group (0 g/kg) was injected with an equivalent volume of normal saline. The appearance and behavioral performance of the mice were observed during RY treatment, and their body weight was measured daily. Six hours after the injection on day 14, the mouse livers were harvested for analysis.

Eighteen female BALB/c nude mice (6-8 weeks old, 20 ± 2 g) were obtained from Human Silaike Jingda Experimental Animal Co., Ltd. The mice were acclimatized under specific pathogen-free conditions for 7 days. The cells (8 × 106 cells/mouse) were injected subcutaneously into the right flank of each mouse. Mice were randomly assigned to one of three groups: Ctrl, RY, and PTX groups. Beginning seven days after cell inoculation, the RY group received intraperitoneal injections of 2 g/kg RY every three days [10]. The PTX group was administered 10 mg/kg PTX intraperitoneally on the same schedule. Tumor growth was monitored, and all mice were sacrificed for tissue collection on day 31.

Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E)

Mouse liver tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned. The sections were deparaffinized and hydrated with distilled water. The sections were stained with hematoxylin until the nuclei appeared blue-purple, treated with hydrochloric acid ethanol, and subsequently stained with eosin for the cytoplasm. After stepwise dehydration, the sections were mounted with neutral balsam and photographed under a microscope.

Immunohistochemical (IHC) staining

The proportion of Ki67-positive cells in the tissues was determined by IHC staining. Following fixation, the tumor tissues were embedded in paraffin, sectioned, and dewaxed. Antigen retrieval was performed on the sections using citric acid buffer, and a hydrophobic barrier pen was used to circle the tissue areas. The sections were incubated with the primary antibody, the corresponding secondary antibody, DAB chromogen, and hematoxylin. Finally, the dehydrated sections were mounted with neutral balsam. Images were acquired using a microscope.

UPLC-Q-TOF-MS/MS analysis

The main active constituents of RY (Yunnan Shenwei Sprui, China) were identified using UPLC-Q-TOF-MS/MS. For extraction, 0.2 g of RY powder was combined with 25 mL of 75% methanol, weighed, and sonicated for 30 min. After sonication, the mixtures were allowed to equilibrate to room temperature, re-weighed, and reconstituted with methanol to compensate for any solvent loss. The solutions were filtered through a 0.22 μm membrane, and the filtrates were collected for analysis. These samples were subsequently analyzed by UPLC-Q-TOF-MS/MS (Waters ACQUITY H-Class, Waters, Milford, MA, USA; Triple TOF 6600+, Waltham, MA, USA) to characterize the chemical composition of RY.

Analysis results of chemical constituents of RY.

Target acquisition of RY active ingredients

The SMILES notations of active ingredients were imported into Swiss TargetPrediction database (http://www.swisstargetprediction.ch/index.php) for Homo sapiens to predict potential targets. Targets with a probability score greater than zero were retained as predicted targets for each ingredient. Additional target information for each compound was retrieved from the TCMSP database. The targets identified from both databases were consolidated, duplicates were removed, and the resulting RY target genes were standardized using the UniProt database (https://www.uniprot.org/). An ingredient-target network was subsequently constructed with Cytoscape 3.9.1 software. Ingredients exhibiting a median Degree value or higher were defined as the core constituents of RY.

Prediction of BC targets and acquisition of intersection targets

BC-related targets were retrieved by searching the GeneCards, DrugBank, and TTD databases using the keyword “breast cancer”. To enhance relevance, the screening criteria in the GeneCards database were set to a relevance score >5. Protein targets from the DrugBank database were converted to Gene Symbols using the UniProt database. After removing duplicates, BC-related targets were obtained. The active ingredient targets of RY and the BC-related targets were input into the Jvenn online tool to construct a Venn diagram, yielding the intersection targets of RY and BC.

Analysis of protein-protein interaction (PPI) network

The intersection target was imported into the STRING 11.5 database (https://string-db.org) and Cytoscape 3.9.1 software to construct the PPI network. Targets with a Degree value at or above the median were defined as core targets.

Gene ontology (GO) and kyoto encyclopedia of genes and genomes (KEGG) enrichment analysis

The intersection targets were analyzed for GO and KEGG enrichment using the DAVID database (https://david.ncifcrf.gov/). The resulting GO and KEGG results were preprocessed and subsequently visualized with the micro-information platform (https://www.bioinformatics.com.cn/).

Molecular docking

Molecular docking validation was performed using AutoDock software to verify the interactions between the core active ingredients identified through network pharmacology analysis and the core targets. The three-dimensional structures of the target proteins were retrieved from the RCSB Protein Data Bank (https://www.rcsb.org/). The three-dimensional structures of the small molecule ligands were obtained from the TCMSP database, the PubChem database, or generated via conversion using Open Babel software. Complexes with a binding energy lower than −5 kcal/mol were considered to possess potential binding activity. The docking conformations of these complexes were further visualized and analyzed using PyMOL software.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis and graphing were performed using GraphPad Prism 8.0. Data were represented as “mean ± standard deviation”. Differences among multiple groups were analyzed by one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey's test. A P-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

After 48 h, proliferation was significantly lower at concentrations of 10, 20, and 40 μg/mL compared to the 24-h time point. Treatment with the positive control PTX also reduced proliferation. Analysis using the AAT Bioquest IC50 calculator determined the IC50 values for MDA-MB-231 cells to be 92.17 μg/mL at 24 h and 28.26 μg/mL at 48 h. Transwell, flow cytometry, and Western blot analyses demonstrated that RY (10-80 μg/mL) and PTX inhibited cell migration (Fig. 1C and D), promoted apoptosis (Fig. 1E and F), and increased E-cadherin expression while suppressing N-cadherin and vimentin protein levels in both cell lines (Fig. 1G–J). These findings indicate that RY suppresses the malignant phenotype of MDA-MB-231 and BT-549 cells.

All mice in each group remained in good general condition during the administration period, with no significant adverse reactions observed. We subsequently investigated the effect of RY on tumor growth in a mouse model. Xenograft experiments demonstrated that both RY and PTX inhibited tumor growth (Fig. 2B and C). Tumors in the RY and PTX groups exhibited significantly lower volume and weight than those in the control group (Fig. 2D and E). Discussion

BC is a common and life-threatening disease for women, with an incidence in China of approximately 600 per 100,000 individuals. Its primary etiological factors include heredity, environmental influences, and hormone secretion. Current treatment primarily involves surgery, chemotherapy, molecular therapy, endocrine therapy, and various neoadjuvant approaches, which are frequently associated with serious adverse effects such as nausea, vomiting, and cognitive decline [17]. TCM, either alone or in combination with other chemotherapeutic agents, represents a novel strategy for BC treatment that can improve patient survival rates and quality of life. Our findings demonstrate that RY inhibited the proliferation, migration, and epithelial-mesenchymal transition of MDA-MB-231 and BT-549 cells, suppressed tumor growth, and induced apoptosis. We employed UPLC-Q-TOF-MS/MS, network pharmacological analysis, and molecular docking to identify the active components and key targets through which RY exerts its therapeutic effect on BC.

RY is a distinctive ethnic medicine in China with abundant medicinal resources. Long-term clinical application has yielded numerous effective prescriptions derived from RY. Cheng et al. [18] reported that an RY alcohol extract counteracted the decreased activity of HT22 cells induced by oxygen-glucose deprivation/reoxygenation, suppressed oxidative stress, and demonstrated a protective effect against cerebral ischemia-reperfusion injury in vitro. The cyclopeptide RA-V, isolated from RY, induces oxidative and endoplasmic reticulum stress in colorectal cancer cells. It promotes DNA damage and reduces cancer cell drug resistance by inhibiting the Nrf2 protein [19]. Rubiarbonol G, an arborinane-type triterpenoid from RY, activates the MAPK family ERK1/2 and JNK signaling pathways, thereby inducing apoptosis and G0/G1 phase arrest in HeLa cells [20]. Separately, RA-XII from RY inhibits tumor growth and metastasis in a mouse model of colorectal cancer by suppressing lipogenesis [21].

Network pharmacology identifies associations among targets, diseases, and drugs to inform drug development. Using UPLC-Q-TOF-MS/MS analysis, we identified 31 active ingredients in RY, including apioside, apigenin-7-O-neohesperidoside, rhoifolin, and RA XII. Database screening yielded 26 of these as effective ingredients. A PPI network analysis revealed that key regulatory targets for BC among these effective ingredients include TP53, AKT1, IL-6, TNF, and STAT3. TP53 is a critical tumor suppressor gene that encodes the 53 kDa P53 protein. This protein exists in two primary forms: the wild-type (WT-p53) and the mutant (MUT-P53). Through its core DNA-binding domain, WT-p53 binds to target genes to induce the repair of DNA-damaged cells and trigger apoptosis, thereby eliminating damaged and cancerous cells to exert its anticancer function [22]. MUT-P53 typically arises from a single residue substitution within its core DNA binding domain, which abolishes its ability to bind the functional target genes of WT-P53 and consequently eliminates its tumor-suppressive function, thereby promoting tumor development and progression [23]. The TP53 mutation rate in BC cells is approximately 30%. This loss of functional p53 drives the reprogramming of preadipocytes, shifting them toward an inflammatory state and fostering an immunosuppressive immune microenvironment, which collectively facilitates BC progression [24]. AKT1 is a key downstream target in the PI3K/AKT pathway, regulating processes such as cell proliferation, migration, invasion, and apoptosis to promote BC progression [25,26]. The pleiotropic cytokine IL-6 facilitates the transition from inflammation to tumorigenesis. Secreted by both tumor cells and stromal cells within the tumor microenvironment, IL-6 serves as a critical mediator of intercellular communication [27]. In BC, elevated IL-6 secretion drives tumor growth and promotes the differentiation of BC stem cells [28]. TNF is a principal mediator of inflammation that promotes the growth, infiltration, and metastasis of tumor cells, playing a significant role in both the immune system and tumor progression [29,30]. By inducing NF-κB, TNF-α downregulates LYRM7 levels, which in turn promotes mitochondrial oxidative stress and the growth and metastasis of BC cells [31]. STAT3, a member of STAT protein family, is phosphorylated by receptor kinases in response to cytokines and growth factors, leading to the formation of homodimers or heterodimers. These complexes then translocate to the nucleus, where STAT3 functions as a transcription factor [32,33]. The inhibitor YZ-35 effectively blocks STAT3 pathway activation, thereby suppressing tumor growth and cancer stem cell activity in BC [34]. These molecular targets are central to the therapeutic action of RY against BC.

GO enrichment analysis of the core targets revealed that the targets of RY against BC are associated with phosphorylation, protein phosphorylation, positive regulation of cell proliferation, ATP binding, identical protein binding, and protein kinase activity. These processes are implicated in the therapeutic action of RY against BC. KEGG pathway enrichment analysis showed that the core targets are primarily enriched in pathways in cancer, the PI3K-AKT signaling pathway, the MAPK signaling pathway, and chemical carcinogenesis-reactive oxygen species pathways. The PI3K-AKT signaling pathway is activated in BC to promote tumor cell proliferation and metastasis, making it a promising therapeutic target for inhibitor development [35,36]. MAPK, a serine-threonine protein kinase, is activated by cytokines, neurotransmitters, and hormones [37]. The pathway serves as a central node for numerous cellular signals and regulates critical processes including proliferation, inflammation, and apoptosis [38]. In triple-negative BC, MAPK pathway activation drives disease progression [39].

In conclusion, this study confirmed that RY effectively inhibits the growth and metastasis of BC and induces apoptosis at both cellular and animal levels. By integrating UPLC-Q-TOF-MS/MS, network pharmacology, and molecular docking analysis, the potential active components, key targets, and related pathways of RY against BC were preliminarily identified. The screened active components can serve as lead compounds, providing a material basis for the development of novel anti-tumor natural products with independent intellectual property rights. Their multi-target mode of action also offers a potential strategy to overcome tumor drug resistance. However, the current research still has certain limitations. First, the core predicted targets and signaling pathways, such as PI3K-AKT and MAPK, have not yet been experimentally validated for their biological functions in BC. Subsequent functional verification at the cellular and animal levels is required using molecular biology techniques such as gene knockout and overexpression. Second, the toxicity studies of RY are currently based only on small-scale animal experiments, and the results cannot yet be extrapolated to clinical settings. The safety of its clinical application still requires systematic evaluation. Furthermore, due to the lack of clinical samples and prospective clinical trial data, the actual efficacy of RY in humans, the optimal target population, and its long-term safety remain to be further elucidated.
