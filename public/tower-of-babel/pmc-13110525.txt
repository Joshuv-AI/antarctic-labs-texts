Comparison of the effects of eHealth-delivered sedentary breaks versus physical activity promotion interventions on sedentary time: protocol for a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Siqing Chen, Xiaofei Nie, Edward Giovannucci, Lili Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionProlonged sedentary behaviour (SB) is an independent risk factor for adverse health outcomes, with current WHO guidelines emphasising both increased physical activity (PA) and reduced sitting time. While electronic health (eHealth) interventions offer scalable solutions, the comparative effectiveness of two dominant strategies: sedentary break interventions (frequent interruptions of sitting) versus PA promotion (structured activity sessions) remains unclear. This systematic review and meta-analysis protocol aims to compare the effectiveness of interventions targeting breaks in SB versus increased PA in reducing sedentary time through meta-analysis of randomised controlled trials (RCTs) comparing either intervention to a control condition, while exploring key moderating factors including participant characteristics, intervention type, duration, eHealth delivery mode, theoretical basis, use of behaviour change techniques (BCTs), PA intensity and SB frequency.Methods and analysisThis protocol follows the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis Protocols (PRISMA-P) guidelines. A comprehensive search will be conducted in PubMed, Embase, Web of Science and the Cochrane Library from inception to 31 July 2025, with language restrictions limited to English and Chinese publications. RCTs comparing eHealth-delivered interventions promoting sedentary breaks with those increasing PA in adults will be included. The primary outcome is sedentary time (objectively measured or self-reported), and secondary outcomes include health outcomes such as cardiometabolic markers, fatigue and well-being. Two reviewers (SC and XN) will independently screen studies, extract data and assess risk of bias using validated tools. Meta-analyses will be performed if sufficient homogeneous data are available, comparing changes in sedentary time. Subgroup analyses will explore effects by participant characteristics including gender (male vs female), age groups (<60 vs ≥60 years), intervention type (sedentary breaks vs PA), duration (short-term ≤3 months vs medium-term >3 to <6 months vs long-term ≥6 months), eHealth platform/delivery mode (eg, smartphone apps vs websites vs wearable device prompts vs text messages), theoretical basis (eg, theory-driven interventions vs non-theoretical interventions), use of BCTs (eg, interventions incorporating BCTs vs interventions without BCTs), SB break frequency (eg, every 30 min vs every 60 min) and PA promotion intensity (eg, light vs moderate vs vigorous). A narrative synthesis will be conducted for studies unsuitable for quantitative analysis.Ethics and disseminationEthical approval is not required as this study involves secondary analysis of published data. Findings will be disseminated through peer-reviewed publication and conference presentations.PROSPERO registration numberCRD420251042994.

Introduction

Sedentary behaviour (SB), characterised by any waking activity with an energy expenditure ≤1.5 metabolic equivalents (METs) while in a sitting, reclining or lying posture,1 has emerged as a significant independent risk factor for adverse health outcomes. Extensive epidemiological evidence associates high levels of sedentary time to increased cardiovascular disease,2 3 type 2 diabetes,4 obesity,5 all-cause mortality6 and poorer mental well-being,7 with these hazards operating independently of physical activity (PA) levels.8 Traditional face-to-face interventions have shown limited effectiveness in achieving beneficial SB change.911

Addressing SB requires scalable, accessible and cost-effective interventions. Electronic health (eHealth) refers to health services that are delivered or enhanced through electronic devices, the internet and related digital technology.12 13 eHealth tools can deliver behaviour change strategies (eg, prompts, self-monitoring, feedback, goal setting, education) directly to individuals in their daily environments, overcoming barriers to time, location and cost associated with traditional face-to-face interventions.14 The reach, adaptability and potential for automation make eHealth particularly suited for promoting changes in habitual behaviours like SB.9 Behaviour change techniques (BCTs), defined as specific, replicable components of interventions designed to alter behaviour, are central to understanding how eHealth strategies achieve outcomes.15 16

The WHO emphasises both increasing PA, with a specific quantitative target of at least 150 min of moderate-to-vigorous exercise per week, and reducing sedentary time.17 In contrast, the Canadian 24-Hour Movement Guidelines provide specific quantitative recommendations for SB alone, advising that adults should limit sedentary time to 8 hours or less per day, including no more than 3 hours of recreational screen time.18 This distinction highlights a key difference in how these guidelines address SB: the WHO addresses sedentary time alongside PA as part of a combined recommendation, whereas the Canadian guidelines target SB independently with its own quantitative benchmark.

Accordingly, eHealth interventions designed to reduce sedentary time have generally followed two distinct approaches aligned with these guidelines. The first is ‘sedentary break’ interventions, which aim to directly interrupt prolonged sedentary time through prompts. The second is ‘PA promotion’ interventions, which focus on increasing moderate-to-vigorous PA and indirectly reducing sedentary time through changes in time allocation. These two strategies differ fundamentally in their mechanisms: the former directly addresses the physiological harms of SB (eg, reduced venous blood flow, suppressed muscle energy metabolism), while the latter indirectly offsets sedentary effects by increasing overall activity levels.19 Although current eHealth interventions targeting SB have predominantly focused on promoting PA,20 emerging studies find that simply meeting PA targets may not sufficiently counteract the harms of prolonged sitting.8 Preliminary evidence suggests that SB-specific interventions (eg, short, frequent standing breaks) are more effective in reducing sedentary time than PA-focused promotion strategies.152123 However, the existing evidence base is predominantly composed of studies focused on PA promotion, with relatively few interventions specifically targeting SB,18 and no systematic review has directly compared the effectiveness of these two intervention approaches.20 Therefore, it remains unclear whether encouraging frequent, brief interruptions is more, less or equally effective as promoting dedicated activity sessions for reducing overall sedentary time within the eHealth context.20

Given the significant health hazards of SB, the growing adoption of eHealth solutions,9 14 and the distinct nature of sedentary breaks and PA promotion strategies,17 a rigorous synthesis of the evidence comparing their effectiveness specifically for reducing sedentary time is crucial. This systematic review and meta-analysis will synthesise evidence from randomised controlled trials (RCTs) to compare the effectiveness of eHealth-delivered interventions focused on sedentary breaks versus PA promotion for reducing total sedentary time in adults, while exploring key moderating factors including participant characteristics, intervention delivery mode, theory, BCTs, duration, intensity and frequency.

Methods and analysis

Registration and study design

This systematic review and meta-analysis protocol has been registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO registration number: CRD420251042994) and will adhere to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analysis Protocols (PRISMA-P) guidelines24 (online supplemental table 1). Any protocol amendments will be documented and updated in PROSPERO. The study design follows the PICOS framework (Population, Intervention, Comparison, Outcome, Study Design) to ensure methodological rigour.

Eligibility criteria for selecting studies

Types of study

We will include: RCTs. Exclusion criteria: observational studies (cohort, case-control, cross-sectional); reviews, meta-analyses, study protocols, conference abstracts; non-peer-reviewed articles.

Types of participants

Adults (≥18 years), irrespective of health status (general population, office workers, individuals with chronic conditions, etc). Children and adolescents (<18 years) will be excluded for the following reasons: (1) authoritative guidelines provide different quantitative recommendations for SB across age groups, the Canadian 24-Hour Movement Guidelines recommend no more than 2 hours of recreational screen time per day for children and adolescents, compared with 3 hours for adults,18 indicating that SB targets are age-specific and should be addressed separately; (2) children and adolescents are in key stages of growth and development, with differences in musculoskeletal systems, metabolic function and cognitive development compared with adults, which may influence both the physiological mechanisms of SB and their responses to eHealth interventions. Including these populations could introduce unnecessary heterogeneity and compromise the validity and applicability of findings to the target adult population.

Types of interventions

We will comprehensively compare two eHealth-delivered intervention strategies: sedentary break interventions, which target the interruption of prolonged sitting (eg, via app notifications or web-based alerts prompting standing), and PA promotion interventions, which aim to increase overall PA time using digital tools.

Types of comparison

Control groups (usual care or no intervention). Studies using active comparators that do not align with the two intervention types of interest (eg, eHealth interventions targeting other health behaviours such as diet or sleep, or combined interventions targeting both PA and SB) will be excluded, as they would not allow for answering our research question.

Types of outcomes

Primary outcomes

Sedentary time (objectively measured (eg, accelerometry) or self-reported (eg, questionnaire)); encompassing various forms of SB including occupational sitting (eg, desk work), leisure-time sitting (including television viewing and screen time) and transportation-related sitting.

Secondary outcomes

Health outcomes (eg, cardiometabolic markers, fatigue, well-being).

Methods and analysis

Search Strategy

A comprehensive literature search will be performed across multiple databases, including PubMed, Embase, Web of Science and Cochrane from inception to 31 July 2025. The search strategy will incorporate Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) and free-text terms related to telemedicine, exercise, SB and study designs (RCTs). The search will be limited to studies published in English and Chinese, as the review team is proficient in these languages, ensuring accurate data extraction and quality assessment. Studies published in other languages will be excluded. Two independent reviewers (SC and XN) will execute the search, followed by a manual ‘snowball’ search of reference lists from included studies and relevant systematic reviews to ensure comprehensive coverage. The detailed search strategies are available in online supplemental table 2.

Study selection

All identified records will be imported into EndNote X9 for duplicate removal. Two reviewers (SC and XN) will independently screen titles and abstracts, followed by full-text assessment against predefined eligibility criteria. No automation tools will be used for study selection; all screening will be performed manually by the reviewers. Discrepancies will be resolved through discussion or consultation with a third reviewer (LY). The selection process will be documented in a PRISMA flowchart, which will include the number of records identified, screened, excluded and included at each stage, along with reasons for exclusion.

Data extraction procedures

A standardised extraction form will be used to collect study characteristics (eg, first author, publication year, country, study design), participant details including sample size, mean age, sex distribution (male vs female), baseline health status, and occupational context; intervention specifics such as intervention type (sedentary breaks vs PA promotion), eHealth platform/delivery mode (eg, smartphone apps; websites; wearable device prompts; text messages), intervention duration (categorised as short-term ≤3 months vs medium-term >3 to <6 months vs long-term ≥6 months), and intervention intensity/frequency, capturing break frequency (eg, every 30 min vs every 60 min) for sedentary interventions and PA promotion target volume/intensity (eg, light vs moderate vs vigorous) for activity-based strategies; comparator details (eg, control type, description); and for PA intensity, we will adopt the standard MET thresholds based on the Compendium of Physical Activities (ref): light-intensity PA (1.6–2.9 METs), moderate-intensity PA (3.0–5.9 METs) and vigorous-intensity PA (≥6.0 METs). Where studies report intensity using other descriptors (eg, ‘brisk walking,’ ‘jogging’), we will map these to the corresponding MET-based categories according to established compendium values. Comparator details (eg, control type, description); BCTs, theory, outcome measures (eg, sedentary time); and follow-up duration. Missing or unclear data will be requested from study authors. Two reviewers (SC and XN) will perform extraction independently, with verification by a third reviewer.

Risk of bias and quality assessment

Methodological quality will be evaluated using the Revised Cochrane Risk of Bias Tool (RoB 2.0) for RCTs.25 The Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) guidelines26 will be used to rate the overall quality of evidence for primary outcomes, considering risk of bias, consistency, directness, precision and publication bias (online supplemental table 3). Two reviewers (SC and XN) will conduct assessments independently, with disagreements resolved through consensus (LY). The level of agreement between reviewers will be documented.

Data synthesis and analysis

Meta-analysis will be conducted using StataMP V.17 where studies report sufficiently similar outcomes. Continuous outcomes will be analysed using mean differences (MD) or standardised mean differences (SMD) with 95% CIs, depending on measurement consistency.27 Heterogeneity will be assessed via the I² statistic, with a fixed-effects model applied if I² ≤50% and a random-effects model otherwise.27 Reading through previous meta-analyses of eHealth-delivered SB interventions, we identified significant heterogeneity in participant characteristics, intervention designs and outcomes reported in the literature.9 15 20 If possible, subgroup analyses will explore effects by participant characteristics including gender (male vs female), age groups (<60 vs ≥60 years), intervention type (sedentary breaks vs PA), duration (short-term ≤3 months vs medium-term >3 to <6 months vs long-term ≥6 months), eHealth platform/delivery mode (eg, smartphone apps vs websites vs wearable device prompts vs text messages), theoretical basis (eg, theory-driven interventions vs non-theoretical interventions), use of BCTs (eg, interventions incorporating BCTs vs interventions without BCTs), intervention intensity/frequency (eg, break frequency: every 30 min vs every 60 min) and PA promotion target volume (eg, light vs moderate vs vigorous) intensity.

To mitigate the risk of type I error due to multiple comparisons, subgroup analyses will be conducted only when each subgroup category contains at least three studies.28 Findings from these analyses will be interpreted as exploratory and hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory.28 We will clearly report the number of studies and participants contributing to each subgroup analysis. If data are insufficient for planned subgroup analyses, we will provide a narrative summary of the available evidence instead. Publication bias will be evaluated using funnel plots and Egger’s test,29 with adjustments made via the Trim and Fill method if needed. Sensitivity analyses will examine the influence of individual studies on overall results.30

Discussion

To date, existing clinical guidelines, while acknowledging the health risks of sedentary lifestyles, lack comprehensive recommendations on detailed strategies for reducing SB, particularly regarding the potential of digital health tools in promoting behaviour change.17 This underscores the urgent need for evidence-based guidance on leveraging eHealth technologies to interrupt prolonged sitting and increase PA. Against this backdrop, this systematic review and meta-analysis will directly compare eHealth-delivered interventions focused on sedentary breaks with those promoting PA. These findings may provide evidence to inform the development of clinical guidelines, facilitating the integration of eHealth strategies into practices aimed at curbing SB. Specifically, the study will clarify whether sedentary break interventions, PA promotion or a combination of both is most effective for reducing sedentary time. This evidence may then be translated into practical recommendations for clinicians, public health practitioners and eHealth developers, enabling them to design and implement targeted interventions that leverage the unique capabilities of digital health platforms. Ultimately, these efforts could help bridge the current gap in guidelines and support the consideration of effective eHealth strategies to combat SB.

The exclusive focus on eHealth-delivered interventions, encompassing mobile apps, wearables, web platforms and short message service, will shed light on the distinct mechanisms through which digital tools implement SB reduction strategies.14 The study will analyse how BCTs15 embedded within specific eHealth features, such as automated activity detection (self-monitoring), context-aware prompts triggered by prolonged sitting (action planning), personalised feedback loops (feedback on behaviour), gamification elements (rewards, graded tasks), and integrated social support differentially operationalise behaviour change mechanisms between sedentary break and PA promotion interventions.25 Understanding these differences is crucial for elucidating how these features might moderate the effectiveness of each strategy in reducing sedentary time, providing essential insights for optimising future eHealth interventions.

The current ambiguity in SB intervention strategies is evident in existing guidelines,17 18 which only provide principle-based recommendations (eg, reduce sedentary time) for addressing SB, lacking refined, population-specific protocols: For high-risk groups such as older adults, pregnant individuals and patients with chronic diseases, more frequent breaking strategies (eg, moving every 30 min) are needed. However, guidelines do not specify quantitative standards for frequency or intensity. To determine the effectiveness of different eHealth-delivered interventions, the planned subgroup analyses will dissect the determinants of efficacy by stratifying outcomes across multiple dimensions. These include participant demographics, such as age groups (<60 vs ≥60 years) and sex, and intervention implementation characteristics, such as delivery modality, SB break frequency, PA intensity, measurement method of sedentary time (device-measured vs self-reported), the study duration, BCTs, and theoretical basis (eg, theory-driven vs non-theoretical designs) will also be considered. This stratified approach is essential, as different populations may respond differently to these interventions; for example, older adults or individuals with mobility limitations may benefit more from sedentary break strategies, while younger populations might achieve greater reductions in sedentary time through structured PA programmes.20 Simultaneously, evaluating both device-measured and self-reported sedentary time will clarify how measurement methods influence observed effects, enabling a more comprehensive understanding of how eHealth delivery modalities and BCTs, theoretical frameworks interact to modulate the success of each strategy in reducing SB.

Heterogeneity within the evidence base is anticipated and will be a central focus of the planned analyses. Key sources of heterogeneity are expected to include variations in intervention design, such as differing definitions and prescriptions of sedentary breaks (eg, duration, frequency) and diverse goals and intensities in PA promotion.27 Differences in the eHealth modalities employed and variations in participant populations are also likely contributors. An additional source of heterogeneity stems from the measurement of sedentary time itself. Studies may use objective measures (eg, accelerometry) reporting minutes per day, or self-reported measures (eg, questionnaires) reporting hours per day. To address this, we will extract and document the measurement method used in each included study. In the meta-analysis, we will prioritise objectively measured sedentary time where available. If both objective and self-reported measures are reported, we will conduct sensitivity analyses to examine whether effect estimates differ by measurement method. If quantitative synthesis is not feasible due to measurement heterogeneity, we will provide a narrative synthesis describing how different measurement approaches may influence observed effects. The planned subgroup and sensitivity analyses will systematically explore these sources of heterogeneity, aiming to provide detailed insights into which strategy may prove most effective for reducing sedentary time, for whom, and under what specific eHealth delivery conditions. These insights could ultimately contribute to the development of tailored digital interventions and offer evidence to inform existing public health guidelines with granular, digitally implementable SB reduction.

Overall, this systematic review and meta-analysis will provide evidence that may inform future updates of clinical guidelines by clarifying the comparative effectiveness of eHealth-delivered sedentary break versus physical activity promotion interventions for reducing sedentary time. However, several methodological limitations warrant consideration, including potential publication bias, substantial heterogeneity in intervention protocols and eHealth delivery modes. Furthermore, the exclusion of non-English language publications may restrict the applicability of the findings across diverse global populations.

Ethics and dissemination

As this study synthesises existing published data, no ethical approval is required. Findings will be disseminated through publication in peer-reviewed journals in the fields of behavioural medicine, digital health and public health. Results will also be presented at national and international conferences to reach clinicians, researchers and policymakers working in behavioural medicine and related fields. The transparent reporting of methods and results will enable replication and facilitate incorporation of findings into clinical practice guidelines.
