Dynamic and quasi static stiffness characterization of a lamination stack of an electric motor
Authors: V Szabó, B Vehovszky, ZG Gazdagh
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe development of electric motors for automotive applications requires precise material models to simulate structural strength and NVH (Noise, Vibration, and Harshness) properties. Modeling the behavior of lamination stacks, composed of stacked steel plates, presents significant challenges. This study conducted dynamic and quasi-static experiments at various preload levels on an unmodified automotive lamination stack. Significant discrepancies were identified between stiffness values obtained from static and dynamic measurements. Consequently, using dynamically obtained stiffness values in static models, and vice versa, leads to inaccuracies and should be avoided. These results enhance the precision and efficiency of simulations used in the design and optimization of electric motors.Keywords: Lamination stack, Static stiffness, Dynamic stiffness

Introduction

With increasingly stringent vehicle emission regulations worldwide, electromobility is gaining popularity due to its potential to reduce local emissions. Designing electric motors for passenger cars poses unique challenges compared to their use in power plants, household appliances, and electric trains [1], [2]. These motors must operate across a wide temperature range, be lightweight, and exhibit high power density and efficiency to ensure proper vehicle dynamics and range. Additionally, they must endure numerous repetitive thermal and structural loads [3] and possess optimal acoustic properties [4]. Affordability is also crucial for market success. These requirements, among others, are established early in the development process.

Virtual testing using simulations is the most efficient and economical method to accelerate the design process of new products. Simulations can reduce development costs by minimizing the number of costly prototypes and hardware tests. Predicting weak spots or unwanted behaviors before building a prototype prevents unsuccessful hardware tests. Additionally, virtual testing helps understand the phenomena behind problematic behaviors of parts and systems, enabling designers to achieve high-quality products in a shorter period [5].

The lamination stack is a key component of an electric motor. It is composed of sheets made of steel featuring a varnish coating on the surface of each sheet. This varnish provides electrical isolation to prevent the formation of eddy currents in the lamination stack during motor operation [6]. From the mechanical perspective, the consequence of this complex structure is a strong nonlinear stiffness behavior [7].

Linear material models accurately describe behavior for a wide range of applications, including NVH analyses. Using linearity in simulations simplifies the mathematical modeling of structures and makes it easier to analyze their behavior. In linear systems, the relationship between inputs and outputs is proportional and additive. Several structures in practice behave linearly within a certain range of loads or displacements. Linear dynamics enable forecasting the response of a structure to different types of loading, which is critical in the design process. However, not all structures behave linearly. Nonlinear analysis may be necessary to predict the correct physical behavior in such cases. Static nonlinear material properties describing the stiffness of a lamination stack in 3D are required as inputs for structural strength models of electric machines for load cases where deformations / stresses due to axial compression are relevant. A similar situation arises when the magnitude of the loads / deformations renders linear methods inappropriate. For example, in the rotor of an asynchronous electric motor with an aluminum cage, different thermal expansion coefficients of the components cause the lamination stack to be compressed after casting the rotor cage around the stack [8]. This manufacturing procedure results in an axial stress state frozen into the rotor, which significantly influences simulation results. Accurate strength simulations of such a rotor also require accounting for nonlinear phenomena. [7], [9].

One way to obtain material properties of lamination stacks is to calculate them using composite theory. Laminated structures are used in various industries due to their excellent mechanical properties [10]. Predicting the behavior or damage of such structures can require extensive experiments on the components due to their complex structure and sometimes surprising behavior (e.g., negative Poisson's ratio [11]) For lamination stacks, only the material properties and thickness of steel and varnish are measured, and the macroscopic material properties of the stack are derived. Baloglu et al. [12] compared and validated three different methods (Rule of Mixture, Analytical Homogenization, Numerical Homogenization) against an experimental modal analysis on an uncompressed stack. The study found good correlation for some eigenmodes and moderate for others, explainable by the structure of the stack. Other extensive studies in this topic have been conducted by Schwarzer [13] and Luchscheider [14].

The stiffness of a lamination stack can be measured either statically [15], [16], [17] or calculated from dynamic experiments [18], [19], [20]. In static methods, the specimen is slowly compressed, and the compression force and displacement are measured, resulting in a full nonlinear loading-unloading curve with a high range of compression force. In dynamic methods, the stack is placed between two heavy push plates, preloaded by bolts to a constant level. A dynamic excitation force, much smaller than the preload force, is applied. In resonance cases, the excitation force can be neglected, and only the inertia forces of the push plates are considered, which are still much smaller than the static preload. An example of this measurement technique is described in [21], where the same method is used as in this paper. Stiffness is then obtained in two ways in our research: by fitting a numerical model to match the eigenfrequencies obtained from the experiment or by calculating it from the inertia force and displacement data.

Multiple examples of direct lamination stack stiffness measurements have been published. Loos et al. [22] performed compression and shear tests on smaller specimens. Baloglu [23] tested a simplified geometry with similar dimensions of to a stator. Luchscheider [14] used a similar setup and additionally studied creep effects of a specimen prepared from a lamination stack. In these cases, the experiments were carried out on smaller specimens. The cutting technology used to manufacture the sheets significantly influences the mechanical properties of the lamination stack [24]. Cutting and preparing specimens from larger stacks can influence their nonlinear behavior for multiple reasons: new cut edges are created, the sheets can suffer out-of-plane plastic deformation during the cutting process, cutting the whole stack deforms the varnish, thermal effects change the material properties of the stack, and the release of inherent stress due to cutting may result in off-plane deformation [14]. Therefore, this research utilizes an unmodified lamination stack to avoid altering its mechanical properties. To the knowledge of the authors, no other publications have examined unmodified stacks.

Material parameters are evaluated differently for static and dynamic behavior. In static measurements, the load on the specimen is applied gradually and remains constant over time. In dynamic measurements, the load is applied rapidly and repeatedly, causing cyclic loading. The dynamic behavior of materials is more complex and difficult to predict than their static behavior and can be affected by factors such as the rate of loading, temperature, and duration of loading. Dynamic excitation acts on an elastic body when a significant part of the work done by external force is converted to kinetic energy. When the amplitude of dynamic load is high, the relative velocity of plastic deformation prevents it from being completed. In such cases, the structure can sustain higher dynamic stress compared to static without damage [25]. Engineers must consider these differences when designing structures or components expected to operate under dynamic loading conditions to ensure they can withstand the expected loads without failure.

This paper compares the stiffness properties of a single specimen of a lamination stack at three different preload states using three methods: static stiffness measurements, analytical derivation of material properties from a dynamic experiment, and fitting the parameters of an FEM model to reproduce the results of the same dynamic experiment. The comparison aims to determine the validity of using dynamically obtained material stiffness properties for static modeling, and vice versa.

Theory

Stiffness behavior of lamination stacks

For structural strength modeling of lamination stacks using the Finite Element Method, the most commonly used technique involves solid elements. The mechanical behavior is described using a special, transversal isotropic material model, either with constant material parameters [13], [14], [19], [22], [23], or parameters that depend on the stress state [17]. This technique is widely used in NVH and strength simulations due to its relatively low computational power requirements, high model reliability, and good convergence. However, it has limitations: it cannot reproduce axial nonlinearity unless material coefficients are computed as functions of the axial load in a custom material model. Additionally, this model treats the assembly of individual sheets as a single continuum, making realistic calculation of in-plane plastic behavior of the sheets impossible. This limitation is significant when simulating damage caused by extreme rotational speeds of a rotor, such as when determining the maximum RPM the rotor can withstand.

Transversal isotropic material models are a simplified version of orthotropic ones. They feature similar material properties in two directions, and a different characteristic in the third direction. The layered structure of the lamination stack causes it to behave significantly differently compared to homogeneous isotropic materials. For pure radial loads (Fig. 1 loading mode a) the stack behaves similarly to a solid steel block. An example of this is the pure rotational loading of a synchronous motor. In other modes (Fig. 1 loading modes b and c) the structure of the stack significantly influences the stiffness behavior, which strongly depends on the axial compression state [22]. This study focuses on loading mode b.

Main load cases for the transversal isotropic loading of a steel stack: loading mode a – pure radial loading, loading mode b – pure axial compression, loading mode c – shear [15].

Fig. 2 shows a symbolic representation of a stress-strain curve obtained by testing the specimen for pure axial compression. The curve can be characterized as progressive. This nonlinearity is caused by several properties of the lamination stack. First, the mechanical properties of the varnish between the sheets are completely different from the stiffness of steel, and its thickness is not perfectly even. The sheets are not perfectly planar: when compressed together, the unevenness needs to be compensated by pressure. As the contacts between the sheets close, the stiffness of the structure increases [27]. Additional influences on stiffness result from the manufacturing process: the cut edges of the sheets, surface roughness [9], [16], and the joining technique [12]. The smoothness of the edges of the sheets, once cut into their final shape, depends on the cutting procedure used (e.g., laser cutting delivers better results than stamping) [24]. Pressing the sheets together results in a nonlinear, elastoplastic behavior. The causes of this nonlinearity are thoroughly discussed in [15]. All these factors impact the contact characteristics between the sheets and, ultimately, the macroscopic stiffness behavior of the stack, as well as the equivalent E- and G-moduli.

Loading and unloading stress-strain curve derived from a compression test of a lamination stack, and the concept of the E-Modules for loading Ei,loading and unloading Ei,unloading obtained at a specific LCi preloaded state of the stack.

For all cases, linearized equivalent E-moduli can be derived from the stress-strain curves (illustrated in Fig. 2). It is important to note that in the case of lamination stacks, the loading and unloading curves are different, leading to a pair of E-moduli at a single preload state (ELCx,loading and ELCx,unloading).

Loading and unloading the specimen results in a hysteresis consisting of separate loading and unloading curves [27]. This hysteresis varies in each load cycle. During the initial cycles, the difference between the resulting curves is larger, especially for the loading curve. As the cyclic test progresses, the stress-strain curves stabilize. If the required precision of a simulation allows, the loading and unloading curves can be approximated by a single curve: either an average or the unloading curve of the first cycle, as shown in [28].

Methods

Experimental modal analysis in a preloaded state

The measured system consists of thick steel push plates with the rotor segment placed between them (Fig. 5). The experimental setup was inspired by a previous work on the stiffness and damping characteristics of the lamination stack of an electric rotor [37]. Although the method used is suitable for both linear and nonlinear characterization, this study focuses on comparing dynamic properties to static ones rather than on nonlinear dynamics. The lamination stack specimen is placed between two push plates. Sensors measure the input force and corresponding accelerations at the corners of the push plates. This type of measurement is called a dilatation test. The measurements were made using Simcenter SCADAS Mobile Frontend and Simcenter Testlab software developed by Siemens. The identification of material properties based on the analyzed results depends on the test setup geometry and its masses, as the two-degree-of-freedom (DOF) system's resonance depends on the masses of the push plates and the rotor segment stiffness.

The dynamic experiment.

Initially, an Experimental Modal Analysis (EMA) measurement was conducted with one excitation and ten response points, examining axial compression mode. The data was processed using Multiple Input Multiple Output (MIMO) Frequency Response Functions (FRFs) module in Simcenter Test.Lab software, where standard measures of linearity were checked: reciprocity and magnitudes of FRFs at different force excitation levels. This helps identify the targeted resonance frequency of the 2DOF system. After ensuring that the system response was linear, a stepped sine excitation was applied with a sampling rate set to 102.4 kHz to obtain a detailed response of the laminated structure.

The experimental analysis was conducted in two steps: first, a faster stepped-sine excitation was used in a wider frequency range (approximately 400 - 3000 Hz, depending on the measured stack width) in 1 Hz steps and 20 cycles per step to characterize the dynamic behavior and identify the main resonances. In the second phase, a +/- 50 Hz frequency band was excited around the axial compression mode with 0.1 Hz step size and 40 cycles per step, aiming to achieve a more detailed, less distorted resonance peak (see Fig. 6). Both measurements were conducted with a controlled excitation force (1 N by default). The time data for further evaluation was then selected from the middle of the resonance peak, capturing approximately 20 vibration cycles, elapsing around 10 milliseconds.

The excitation force and acceleration response time and frequency domain data.

Understanding the influence of preload over the stiffness and damping characteristic is important in rotor dynamics, as the axial preload changes in operation. The damping part of the dynamic hysteresis is stable around the resonance.

The FRF based EMA and its standard linearity checks show minor nonlinearity in the examined setup of the current study (see Fig. 11). In other lamella configurations, such as laminated stacks with magnetic inserts, the system exhibits strongly nonlinear behavior. The weak nonlinear behavior in the case of FRF measurements can be easily averaged out by using burst random excitation with a large sample size of measurement blocks. Using a sufficient number of blocks for averaging the data, the linearity, reciprocity, and coherence checks show good agreement, and EMA works within a certain excitation range and constant preload.

The excitation time data and effect of excitation force on FRF amplitude.

Quasi static stiffness characterization of the lamination stack

Direct measurement of the stiffness involves compressing the specimen and measuring the force and displacement response. The method used in this research uses a real automotive lamination stack without cutting it, as described in detail in [15]. The specimen was compressed at room temperature (20 °C), using a hydraulic cylinder (Fig. 7) to a maximum of Ftest, which serves as a reference load magnitude. The loading rate was limited to 1 kN/s to avoid dynamic / strain rate dependent effects. The displacements were measured at 4 different locations, the results of which were averaged during postprocessing. The tangents of the resulting stiffness curves were calculated for both the loading and unloading curve using the principle illustrated in Fig. 2 for three load cases (LC1,LC2,LC3). Results are reviewed later in the chapter “Evaluation of the results”.

The measurement setup used to obtain the axial stiffness curve of the specimen [15].

Finite element method based calculation of material properties fitted to the dynamic experiment

This technique aims to calibrate the material properties of an FEM model - a digital twin of the experiment described in the previous chapter in order to match selected eigenfrequencies and eigenmodes of the physical experiment. The outputs of the procedure are the material properties of the stack, required to virtually reproduce the dynamic behavior of the real experiment. The method described here differs from other published procedures in the fact that it allows the quick semi-automatized characterization of the material at different preload levels. For the FEA Abaqus R2016 Hotfix 21 was used.

After performing the experiment described in the previous chapter, two typical eigenmodes were chosen to be matched virtually using the FEM model, illustrated in Fig. 9. The model consisted of the following components:

•Two pieces of steel plates made of steel, using a linear elastic material model

•the lamination stack modeled as a solid cylinder, using a transversal isotropic material model, where direction 3 corresponds the normal direction (perpendicular to the plates)

Top: the normal (left hand side) and shear (right hand side) eigenmodes used in the calibration process of the transversal isotropic material model plotted from Abaqus, bottom: the corresponding visualization of the eigenmodes from the dynamic experiments.

The parts were connected through *TIE constraints: the plates and the stack from both sides, the bolt heads and the plates, and the nuts and the plates. This constraint acts like a contact with infinite stiffness, equivalent to meshing the parts together through coincident nodes - providing a non-separating bonded interface between the parts. The plates have been fitted with larger holes than the bolt diameter. Virtual experiments have been performed using models where the plates are fixed to the bolts inside the holes, and have been compared to no contact inside the bolt holes. At higher preload levels, this feature had minimal influence. However, at lower preload states, having a solid bond between the holes and the bolts resulted in an unrealistic coupling in the shear results. As a conclusion, this bond has been deactivated in the models used to obtain the results in this research.

The first step in the optimization loop (Fig. 10) began by modifying the material properties of the FEA deck, and starting a static analysis applying the preload on the M8 bolts. The analysis was run with the *NLGEOM=ON switch active, which is necessary in Abaqus for the preload state to be inherited into the frequency analysis step [26]. In the frequency analysis step, all boundary conditions were removed, except for the preload sections of the bolts, and the *FREQUENCY analysis was performed in a free floating configuration.

FEA optimization workflow to approximate material properties using experiment results.

The main criterion for the choice of eigenmodes was the maximal correlation of the eigenfrequency of eigenmodes to the specific material properties of the stack. The material properties E1, E2, ν12, ν13, ν23, G12 show minimal correlation to the eigenfrequencies of the selected normal fnormFEM and shear fshearFEM eigenmodes. In case of ν13 and ν23 (which are equal), for example, extreme values of 0.01 and 0.5 have been tested. The difference in the results is minimal: 0.17 Hz for the shear, and 0.2 Hz for the normal eigenfrequency, meaning that the influence of these parameters is negligible.

The focus of the current procedure is the determination of E3, in order to match the behavior of the dynamic experiment. The fnormFEM is primarily correlated to E3, but has a moderate dependency on G13 = G23 (from here on referred to as Gx3). The dependencies of fshearFEM are on the other hand the contrary; mainly correlated to Gx3, but still significantly influenced by E3. By using both fnormFEM and fshearFEM as targets and E3 and Gx3 as input variables, the latter material properties were approximated using an optimization algorithm.

During the optimization procedure, the order of eigenmodes was not constant, it changed depending on the material properties input into the optimization loop. For example, using a low E3 resulted in the normal eigenmode (Fig. 9) being output as mode 2, and in case of E3 set at the upper boundary in the optimizer, as mode 5. In order to automatically identify the correct eigenmodes, the modal displacement outputs (ui) were exported at selected nodes of the FEM model for all modes, and identification of the proper eigenmodes was achieved using a custom post processing script written in python. As an example, in case of the normal eigenmode, the formula for the identification was Max(u3cp1−u3cp2), where u3cpi correspond to the modal displacement in the normal direction (3) of the nodes at the middle of the outer side of plate i. The physical interpretation of the criterion is the largest distance between the middle points of the plates.

Results

Evaluation of dynamic measurement data

The dynamic hysteresis is plotted for a single cycle in Fig. 12. It shows the measured restoring force versus displacement hysteresis loop for the pushplate-stack-pushplate setup at compression resonance. The blue dots are the sampled data points. The area of the blue curve represents the cyclic dissipation and the orange dashed line - fitted using least squares method - shows the stiffness line of the system. The present results, together with the force linearity check (see Fig. 11, prove the basically linear behavior of the measured system.

The stiffness of the stack was determined from the system stiffness using Equation (16). That stack stiffness value was then compared to the results of the quasi-static measurement.

The restoring force surface method can help understand the dynamic behavior of the system, but it only captures a snapshot of the moment and does not account for changes that may occur during operating conditions. Furthermore, in the presented work, it was executed at one frequency - in resonance condition - which depends on the preload state. To gain a full insight into the dynamic stiffness behavior, i.e., to capture its frequency dependency, the measurement technique must be extended to include, for example, the study of embrittlement. Embrittlement refers to the loss of ductility and flexibility in the material, which can cause it to become brittle and prone to cracking or breaking.

The effect of stack thickness was also examined. Assuming homogeneous material stiffness and constant cross-sectional area, the stiffness is inversely proportional to the material thickness. This theoretical correlation may be overridden by contact surface-related or other inhomogeneity effects. The measured effect of stack thickness is in good agreement with the theoretical correlation (see Fig. 13), proving that the stack can be considered as a homogeneous material in the examined setup.

Evaluation of quasi static measurement data

In order to measure the static σ−ϵ curves in a stabilized loading-unloading hysteresis, 5 cycles have been performed using the method explained in section 3.2.

A curve was fitted on the σ−ϵ curves resulting from the 5th cycle of the experiments. The first derivative of the analytical curves was calculated at the loading and unloading points resulting in the properties values Elstat and Eustat, at the 3 different load / preload states: LC1,LC2,LC3. From the E-moduli, the stiffness of the stack has been calculated.

The relative magnitude of the load cases LC on the static loading-unloading curves is shown in Fig. 14, along with the relative E-moduli calculated at the intersection of the loading / unloading curves and lines showing the LCx loads. It can be noted that the overall preload level used for the load cases examined here is relatively low. There are two reasons for this. First, the accuracy of the RFS method is optimal only for lower preload values. Additionally, the static measurement setup was designed to operate at the load levels the stack can experience during operation in a motor. LC3, the highest load, is only 15% of the maximum preload magnitude σtest used in the static experiment. The magnitude of LC2 is half of LC3, LC1 the quarter. The results, after being converted into stiffness values, are compared to dynamically measured values in the next chapter.

Comparison of the dynamic and static stiffness results

The axial stiffness results obtained using the methods explained above are compared in Fig. 15. The value calculated using the RFS method in LC2 serves as the reference value - 100%.

Comparison of the stiffness results at the lowest (LC1), medium (LC2) and highest (LC3) preload state, using the dynamic RFS result of LC2 as reference.

At LC2, the FEA obtained stiffness results closely match the RFS reference results, totaling 113%. The quasi static stiffnesses, however, are considerably lower: 43% for loading, 55% for unloading. The difference can be contributed to viscoelastic phenomena, possibly the dynamic behavior of the varnish.

For LC3, the dynamically derived stiffness results are close to each other: 315% for RFS, 331% for FEM. The gap between the static and dynamic results increases dramatically: the static loading value is 81%, the unloading 91%.

At the lower load case LC1, the dynamic RFS stiffness (52%) is close to the static results: loading 27%, unloading 40%. However, the FEA method resulted in an extremely low stiffness value (10%). The longitudinal eigenmode (left hand side of Fig. 9) in the measurement setup has a very low eigenfrequency in this case, to which the model was calibrated. Apparently, as the preload is lowered, the influence of the stack stiffness on this eigenfrequency of the whole setup decreases radically.

To test this theory, a series of simulations has been performed: multiple values of axial compression stiffness values E3 have been tested at LC1 preload. The resulting curve is shown in Fig. 16, where the stiffness values have been normalized, as before (using Reference Dynamic RFS LC2 in Fig. 15). The curve shows, that as E3 is decreased, the longitudinal eigenfrequency of the whole experiment setup FEM model converges to approximately 1000 Hz - the eigenfrequency of the measurement setup without the stiffness of the stack. This is a lower limit for the FEM model in the upper configuration, and any axial eigenfrequency around it would mean unrealistically low stack stiffness values. However, it has been observed during the experiments, that at low preload levels the contact between the bolts and plates is not ideal [38]. This means, that modeling them as a bonded structure at low preload levels leads to overestimation of the bolt contact stiffness. This theory can explain the good match between RFS and FEM at the higher preload load cases LC2 and LC3, and the mismatch at LC1. To counter this effect, the contact type has been changed to penalty contact with a linear pressure-overclosure characteristic, where the penalty contact multiplier is the number used to define the contact stiffness, as described in [26]. The E3 coefficient has been set to the value determined using the RFS method. The dependency of the axial eigenfrequency as a function of the contact stiffness has been shown in Fig. 17. Here, increasing the contact stiffness results in an asymptotic curve for the axial eigenfrequency, with approximately 1650 Hz for a perfectly rigid contact. Further increase in contact stiffness over this has practically no influence on the resulting eigenfrequency. Lowering the contact stiffness leads to realistic stack stiffness results and confirms the influence of the bolt contact stiffness as described in the previous paragraph.

One possible reason why material parameters can differ under dynamic and quasi static loading is due to the effect of strain rate. When subjected to dynamic loading, the strain rate experienced by a material is much higher than under static loading conditions. This can cause changes in the microstructure of the material, affecting its mechanical properties. For example, at higher strain rates, some materials may exhibit an increase in strength and stiffness, while others may become more ductile and exhibit a decrease in strength [39]. This is especially true for polymers, like the varnish [40]. Another reason for the difference in material behavior between static and dynamic loading is due to the effect of fatigue. Repeated loading and unloading cycles can cause microstructural changes and material degradation, leading to a reduction in strength and stiffness. This phenomenon is relevant for inhomogeneous material structures, like additively manufactured parts [41] or composite materials [42].

Conclusions

The goal of this research was to compare the dynamically measured and the static axial compression stiffness of a single unmodified automotive lamination stack. The static stiffness was obtained through a quasi-static compression experiment, while the dynamically determined stiffness was derived from a dynamic measurement based on the RFS method. Further evaluation was conducted via parameter fitting of a dynamic FEM model of the measured system to the first normal and shear eigenmodes of the experiment. The objective was to compare stiffness values obtained from static and dynamic measurements by identifying similarities and understanding differences.

The dynamic mechanical response of the epoxy resin coated steel lamination stack at a relatively low preload state shows good correlation to static values. The difference between dynamic and static stiffness becomes greater with increasing preload. At higher preload states, the stiffness derived from the dynamical experiments reaches 3-4 times the value of the static stiffness, as shown in Fig. 18. In the applied range of preload in dynamic case, there is a linear relationship between static stiffness and preload. The dynamically determined stiffness shows a higher order relationship between preload and stiffness. This leads to the conclusion that intermixing static and dynamically determined stiffnesses at higher preload ranges, typically at load levels experienced during the operation of asynchronous machine rotors, results in low precision and is not recommended. The dynamic response differs significantly from the static behavior at high preload states, which cannot be neglected.

Trendlines illustrating the growing gap between dynamically and quasi statically determined axial stiffness values, as preload is increased. Reference point for the preload: LC2. Reference for the relative stiffness: the dynamic RFS result at LC2.

Regarding the static E-moduli derived from the static loading and unloading curves at specific preload values show a difference of approximately 10-15%. If a precision of this magnitude is acceptable in a simulation, an approximating curve / E-module can be used, instead of Eload and Eunload.

The stiffness values derived from dynamical experiments using FEA, at LC2 and LC3 show good correlation with the dynamic measurement based stiffness results, the differences are within 10%. However, at LC1 preload the difference is significant. Therefore the derivation of selected dynamic material properties from the results of the shown experimental modal analysis using FEA modeling is possible using the automated method presented in this article. The results show that the influence of the stiffness of the lamination stack on the eigenfrequencies increases considerably as more preload is applied. Calculation of dynamic material stiffnesses using the restoring force surface method has a good correlation with the FEA results at higher preload rates using rigid contacts. As the preload decreases, the contact under the bolt heads becomes softer due to a decrease in the contact stiffness between the bolts and the plate. Taking this phenomenon into account in FEM is possible using a softened pressure-overclosure contact formulation.

The DoE of the FEM model of this measurement setup reveals that when using a transversal isotropic material model, the Poisson numbers have a negligible influence on the axial and shear eigenfrequencies.

Future work

In a dynamic system consisting of steel sheets coated with epoxy resin, the sheets remain almost rigid, while most of the deformation occurs in the epoxy resin. The dynamic study shows a linear stiffness characteristic along with viscous damping-like behavior. However, the study highlights the importance of focusing on changes in the epoxy resin's material parameters, particularly thermal embrittlement caused by continuous high-temperature loading. Future work should aim to prove that the restoring force surface method can track such changes that affect the dynamic parameters.
