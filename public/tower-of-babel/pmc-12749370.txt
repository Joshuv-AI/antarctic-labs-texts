Numerical evaluation of soil arching effects on lateral earth pressure in soldier pile retaining wall systems
Authors: Mohammad Almasi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
In soldier pile retaining wall systems, the surface facing, commonly a shotcrete layer, is typically designed as a one-way slab subjected to a uniform lateral earth pressure. However, this conventional assumption neglects the soil arching phenomenon, which can substantially modify the actual pressure distribution and lead to conservative and uneconomical designs. Previous studies have primarily relied on simplified analytical formulations or limited physical models, and the combined influence of soil micro-mechanical properties and geometric configuration has not been comprehensively evaluated. To address this gap, this study numerically investigates the influence of soil arching on the lateral earth pressure distribution behind the wall facing using two numerical approaches: the finite difference method (FDM) and a coupled discrete–continuum approach (Combined DEM–FDM). Analyses were performed using the FLAC and PFC software packages, considering variations in inter-particle friction coefficient, particle rolling resistance, pile spacing, shotcrete thickness, and applied stress level. The results reveal that soil arching induces a non-uniform pressure distribution between adjacent piles, where the lateral earth pressure decreases linearly from the pile location to about one-fourth of the pile spacing and then remains nearly constant toward the midspan. Increasing the pile spacing, rolling resistance, and inter-particle friction significantly intensifies the arching effect and reduces the maximum bending moment in the wall facing, while increasing the shotcrete thickness leads to a more uniform pressure distribution and a reduced arching effect. The influence of stress level on the pressure distribution was found to be negligible. The outcomes of both FDM and DEM–FDM analyses show excellent agreement, confirming the reliability of the proposed numerical approach. Overall, considering the soil arching effect substantially reduces the predicted bending moment compared to the conventional uniform pressure assumption, suggesting a potential for more economical retaining wall designs.

Introduction

The increasing demand for underground spaces in densely populated cities has led to the widespread use of deep excavations and earth-retaining systems. Among these systems, soldier pile walls with shotcrete or concrete facing have become one of the most practical solutions due to their flexibility, constructability, and cost efficiency1–4. In conventional design, the facing is typically modeled as a one-way slab subjected to a uniform lateral earth pressure derived from classical active pressure theories. However, field observations and advanced numerical studies have demonstrated that the actual pressure distribution is often non-uniform, primarily due to the development of soil arching between adjacent piles5–7.

The concept of soil arching—first introduced by Terzaghi8—describes the redistribution of stresses within a deforming soil mass, where part of the load is transferred from more deformable regions toward stiffer boundaries, forming internal “arches.” This mechanism has been identified as a key factor in various geotechnical systems such as retaining walls, pile-supported embankments, and trapdoor experiments9–11. Laboratory and numerical investigations have revealed that arching can significantly reduce the lateral load acting on wall facings and, consequently, the bending moment demand6,12.

From a numerical standpoint, studies using finite difference and discrete element methods have deepened understanding of this phenomenon. Cundall and Strack13 pioneered the discrete element method (DEM), later expanded in bonded-particle formulations by Potyondy and Cundall14, providing the basis for modern micro-mechanical soil simulations. Using these methods, Han et al.15 demonstrated the progressive formation of soil arches with increasing wall displacement, while Liang and Zeng5 and Khosravi, Pipatpongsa, & Takemura16 highlighted the influence of interface friction and relative stiffness on stress transfer mechanisms.

Recent research has advanced this understanding through both physical modeling and coupled numerical approaches. Ma et al.10 investigated pile-supported embankments through physical experiments and DEM simulations, illustrating how arching patterns evolve with pile spacing. Bao, Chen, Wang, Sun, & Yan17 proposed an analytical prediction for displacement-dependent lateral pressures on stabilizing piles, while18 developed and validated a deformation-dependent model for soil arching under unloading. Similarly, Chen, Xu, Wang, Meng, & Yin19 formulated a theoretical model for lateral arching in flexible retaining walls, and Fang et al.20 classified the behavior of soil arching in slopes under excavation.

Additional investigations—such as those by George and Dasaka21 on the mechanics of arch action, Wang, Wu, & Wang22 on limit analysis for active pressures, and Zhou, Fu, Ren, & Zhao23 on anisotropic granular materials—have refined the understanding of arch formation, spatial variability, and directional stress redistribution. Numerical models developed in FLAC 8.1 and PFC 7.024,25 now allow direct coupling of continuum and discrete domains, enabling more realistic simulation of stress transfer, inter-particle rolling, and wall–soil interactions.

Despite extensive prior work, few studies have addressed the combined effects of micro-mechanical soil parameters, geometric spacing, facing stiffness, and surcharge load within a fully coupled DEM–FDM framework. Moreover, simplifications such as constant arching coefficients or idealized boundary conditions often limit the applicability of results to real design situations.

Beyond classical studies on soil arching and lateral earth pressure, recent research has underscored the decisive role of particle-scale characteristics and stress-path effects in governing macroscopic responses of granular geomaterials. For instance26–28) showed that reducing particle size in engineering muck-based geomaterials markedly enhances reactivity and densification, leading to higher strength and improved performance evidence that micro-mechanical attributes directly control bulk behavior. Complementing this26–28) demonstrated via cyclic triaxial testing that cumulative deformation in calcareous sand evolves rapidly during early cycles before reaching a plastic stability stage,their predictive model highlights the importance of stress history and loading path for long-term deformation. From a soil–structure perspective, pile–soil interaction under lateral loading has likewise been shown to depend on particle size and embedment depth, where smaller particles can alter force-chain transmission, resistance mobilization, and bending-moment distribution along the pile26–28. Collectively, these advances reinforce the need to explicitly incorporate micro-mechanical effects (e.g., inter-particle friction, rolling resistance) and realistic boundary interactions into numerical frameworks for soldier-pile systems motivating the coupled FDM and DEM–FDM approach adopted in this study.

Accordingly, the present work numerically evaluates the lateral earth pressure distribution behind soldier-pile wall facings while explicitly accounting for soil arching. Two complementary numerical techniques—FDM and coupled DEM–FDM (FLAC + PFC)—are employed to examine how variations in inter-particle friction, rolling resistance, pile spacing, shotcrete thickness, and applied stress level influence pressure redistribution and bending-moment demand.

Therefore, the present study conducts a comprehensive numerical investigation of lateral earth pressure distribution behind soldier pile wall facings, explicitly considering the soil arching phenomenon. Both the finite difference method (FDM) and the coupled DEM–FDM approach are employed to examine how variations in soil properties and geometric configurations influence pressure distribution and bending response. The outcomes provide new insights into realistic pressure profiles and offer guidance for more efficient, performance-based design of soldier pile wall systems.

Materials and methods

Numerical approach

To investigate the soil arching effect and the resulting lateral earth pressure distribution behind the wall facing, two numerical methods were employed: the finite difference method (FDM) and a coupled discrete–continuum approach (Combined DEM–FDM). The FDM simulations were conducted using the FLAC software, while the DEM–FDM coupling was implemented through the integration of PFC (Particle Flow Code) with FLAC. The FDM was used to represent the continuum soil domain, and the DEM was adopted to capture particle-scale interactions and micro-mechanical effects, allowing a more accurate simulation of soil arching between piles.

In the explicit solution cycle of FLAC, the equations of motion are solved directly at each time step without iterative matrix solutions, which provides stable and efficient convergence even for nonlinear material behavior. Each time step corresponds to an incremental update of displacement, strain, and stress in the model domain. The explicit formulation was chosen due to its robustness in handling problems involving large deformations, nonlinearity, and discontinuities, typical of soil–structure interaction problems in retaining systems.

In the coupled DEM–FDM framework, the two codes communicate through data exchange at their interface boundaries. The particle model (PFC) captures the discrete interactions and contact forces between soil grains, while the continuum model (FLAC) represents the surrounding domain and structural boundaries. At each time step, the contact forces computed in PFC are transferred to the corresponding FLAC grid faces as boundary stresses, and the resulting displacements of the FLAC grid are then fed back to the PFC particles to update their motion and contact status. This two-way coupling ensures dynamic equilibrium and allows accurate simulation of stress redistribution across the soil–structure interface. Such coupling enables the combined model to account for localized particle-scale mechanisms (e.g., arching, dilation, and contact slip) while preserving the global deformation response of the continuum domain.

Model geometry and boundary conditions

The numerical model represented a typical soldier pile retaining wall segment bounded by rigid side walls to ensure symmetry. The model geometry is illustrated in Fig. 1, where the piles are spaced at intervals L, and the wall facing (shotcrete) is located between adjacent piles. The top boundary of the soil was defined as a free surface, while the bottom and side boundaries were fixed in both horizontal and vertical directions to prevent rigid body motion. A uniform vertical surcharge load was applied on the top surface to simulate the effect of overburden stress. In this study, piles were idealized as rigid boundaries rather than explicit structural elements to reduce computational complexity and focus on soil–facing interaction. This assumption is consistent with approaches adopted by Vermeer et al.7, Liang and Zeng5, and Bao et al.17, who showed that for pile spacing-to-diameter ratios greater than 2.5–3, the stress transfer is governed primarily by soil arching rather than direct pile–soil contact. Therefore, this simplification reasonably represents the behavior for typical urban retaining walls with closely spaced piles.Fig. 1Geometry of the numerical model used in FDM and Combined DEM–FDM analyses.

Geometry of the numerical model used in FDM and Combined DEM–FDM analyses.

The contact between the wall facing and the soil particles was simulated using interface elements in FLAC, defined with appropriate normal and shear stiffness values derived from the soil stiffness parameters. The coupling between the DEM and FDM regions was achieved through boundary mapping, where forces from the discrete domain were transferred to the continuum mesh, and corresponding displacements were fed back iteratively.

In practice, soil arching typically develops within a distance of about two to three times the pile diameter between adjacent piles. In the present study, rather than explicitly modeling the geometric shape of the piles, the effect of the piles was represented by applying rigid boundary conditions at the locations corresponding to the pile axes. This simplification allowed the model to focus on the stress transfer mechanism and the development of arching within the soil zone located between the piles.

The rigid boundaries acted as immovable interfaces that prevented horizontal displacement at the pile locations, reproducing the confinement and stress concentration that naturally occur around the pile shafts. Although the physical geometry of the piles was not explicitly included, this approach has been shown in previous numerical studies (e.g.,5) to adequately capture the essential features of the arching phenomenon, provided that the pile spacing-to-diameter ratio (S/D) is within the typical range of 2–3.

Therefore, the numerical domain analyzed in this study represents the critical region between two adjacent piles, where the arching effect predominantly occurs, while the pile influence is accounted for implicitly through the fixed rigid boundaries.

Quantitative details of model dimensions, soil parameters, and boundary conditions are presented in the subsequent sections together with the numerical analyses. Here, only the general configuration and boundary setup are introduced for clarity.

Calibration of micro parameters

Before initiating the shear test simulations, the soil specimen was generated using the Fontainebleau sand grain-size distribution, which is widely used in laboratory experiments for soil arching and direct shear studies. The particle assembly was created through a random generation procedure in the DEM environment, ensuring that the particle size distribution followed the grading curve of Fontainebleau sand. The initial particle radii were assigned randomly within the limits defined by the experimental gradation curve.

After particle generation, the sample was compacted to the target density and confining stress using the radial expansion method, which gradually increases particle radii while maintaining random spatial distribution. This procedure allowed the particles to achieve a dense packing condition without introducing overlaps or artificial stress concentrations.

The grain-size distribution curve of the Fontainebleau sand used in the simulation is presented in Fig. 2, illustrating the uniform gradation and narrow particle size range typical of this material. The physical and mechanical properties of the sand were selected to replicate experimental observations and ensure compatibility with the DEM–FDM coupled model calibration. Fontainebleau sand was selected because its uniform grain shape, low fines content, and well-documented mechanical behavior make it a benchmark material for DEM calibration9,10. Its properties represent a medium-dense, clean sand commonly used as backfill in numerical studies of arching. The nearly uniform grain-size distribution of Fontainebleau sand (Fig. 2) facilitates the formation of stable interparticle arches. In contrast, broader gradations would likely reduce stress concentration and diminish the arching intensity.Fig. 2Grain-size distribution curve of Fontainebleau sand used for particle generation in the DEM model.

To ensure the accuracy of the DEM simulations, a detailed calibration process was carried out by modeling direct shear tests in the PFC software. The numerical shear box consisted of ten rigid boundaries forming a rectangular specimen, as illustrated in Fig. 3. The model was used to determine the appropriate micro-mechanical parameters of the soil particles, including inter-particle friction (μ), rolling resistance (μr), normal stiffness (Kn), and tangential stiffness (Ks). During the direct shear test calibration, the vertical stress was maintained at the target value using a servo-control mechanism that continuously adjusted the displacement of the upper rigid boundary. The boundaries were assigned a stiffness of 1 × 10⁷ N/m to ensure quasi-static loading conditions without excessive deformation. In the FDM model (FLAC), the mesh was refined near the wall and pile regions with an average grid size of 0.05 m, while damping was introduced through local Rayleigh coefficients of 0.15 to stabilize numerical oscillations. The time step in both DEM and FDM computations was automatically controlled according to the critical timestep criterion Δt < 0.2 √(m/k), ensuring numerical stability throughout the simulations.Fig. 3Geometry of the numerical model used in FDM and Combined DEM–FDM analyses.

Parametric study

A comprehensive parametric study was performed to examine the influence of several governing parameters on soil arching and lateral earth pressure distribution, including: Pile spacing (L), Inter-particle friction coefficient (μ), Rolling resistance (μr), Shotcrete facing thickness (t), Applied surcharge pressure (P).

Each variable was changed systematically while keeping other parameters constant to evaluate its effect on the soil arching intensity and the resulting bending moment in the wall facing.

Results and discussion

Distribution of lateral earth pressure

The numerical analyses revealed that the lateral earth pressure acting on the wall facing is significantly affected by the soil arching phenomenon. The pressure distribution obtained from both FDM and combined DEM–FDM models exhibited a distinctly non-uniform pattern along the spacing between piles. The maximum lateral pressure occurred at the pile locations, while the minimum value appeared approximately at one-fourth of the pile spacing, remaining nearly constant up to the midspan between adjacent piles. This trend agrees well with the experimental and numerical findings reported by Perko and Boulden6 and Vermeer et al.7 (Figs. 7,8).Fig. 7Effect of rolling resistance (μr) on lateral earth pressure distribution.Fig. 8Variation of lateral pressure distribution with different shotcrete thicknesses (t).

Figure 10 shows the bending moment distribution behind the concrete wall obtained from the FDM simulation. The maximum bending moment occurs near the midspan and reaches approximately 42.84 kN·m/m, which is significantly lower than the theoretical bending moment of 112.5 kN·m/m computed when soil arching is neglected (qL2/8 = 100 × 9/8). This confirms that considering the soil-arching effect substantially reduces bending stresses on the facing. A comparison between the combined DEM–FDM and FDM approaches also indicates strong consistency between the two methods, confirming the reliability of the coupled modeling framework. The maximum bending moment of 42.84 kN·m/m occurred near the midspan of the facing. The corresponding equivalent tensile stress reached about 25.7 MPa for a 0.1 m facing thickness, decreasing to 6.4 MPa and 2.9 MPa when the thickness increased to 0.2 m and 0.3 m, respectively. This confirms that thicker facings substantially reduce stress concentration and bring the tensile demand within the realistic strength range of reinforced shotcrete.

As pile spacing increases, the arching effect becomes more pronounced, resulting in a wider stress transfer zone and a larger contrast between maximum and minimum pressures (Fig. 5). A similar trend is observed with increasing inter-particle friction and rolling resistance: higher values of μ and μr promote particle interlocking and stable contact chains within the soil mass, intensifying the arching mechanism and lowering the average pressure acting on the facing (Figs. 6 and 7). Conversely, increasing the shotcrete thickness leads to a reduction in arching intensity. Thicker and stiffer facings limit horizontal deformation between piles, producing a more uniform pressure distribution and causing the Arching Effect Coefficient (AEC) to approach the classical values proposed in the literature (Fig. 8). The surcharge load (P), on the other hand, has only a minor influence on the normalized pressure pattern. Although higher surcharge increases the overall stress level, the relative shape of the pressure distribution remains nearly unchanged (Fig. 9). The results revealed a consistent non-uniform pressure pattern with maximum values at pile locations and minima at midspan, confirming the role of soil arching. The computed Arching Effect Coefficient (AEC) values ranged between 0.47 and 0.67, aligning with empirical estimates1,6. The surcharge load exhibited a minor effect, primarily due to normalization by applied stress, consistent with findings from Bao et al.17.

Overall, the findings confirm that geometric and frictional parameters—particularly pile spacing, inter-particle friction, and rolling resistance—are the dominant factors governing soil arching behavior, while facing stiffness and surcharge load play secondary roles. The observed non-uniform pressure profiles and their dependence on these parameters agree well with previous experimental and analytical studies, validating the numerical modeling approach adopted in this research.

The influence of the soil micro-mechanical properties was also pronounced. Increasing both μ and μr improved interlocking among soil particles, which stabilized the arching mechanism and reduced the average lateral pressure acting on the wall. For example, when μ increased from 0.05 to 0.5 and μr from 0.2 to 0.8, the AEC values decreased significantly, confirming the dominant role of frictional behavior in controlling stress redistribution within the backfill. Conversely, increasing the shotcrete thickness (t) reduced the magnitude of soil arching, resulting in a more uniform pressure distribution and an increased AEC value approaching the theoretical values shown in Table 1. The effect of surcharge load (P) was minor: although it increased the absolute stress level, the normalized pressure pattern remained essentially unchanged, and the AEC decreased by less than 5%.

Overall, the results show that geometric and frictional parameters have a far greater impact on soil arching than loading conditions. The consistency between the numerically derived AEC values and those presented in Table 1 supports the validity of the proposed modeling approach and highlights the practical relevance of incorporating arching effects in the design of soldier pile retaining wall systems.

Comparison between FDM and DEM–FDM analyses

Both numerical techniques yielded consistent results in terms of pressure distribution trends and bending moment profiles. The combined DEM–FDM model provided additional insight into particle-scale behavior and the formation of stress arches, while the FDM model efficiently captured the overall continuum response. The close agreement between the two methods validates the reliability of the coupled approach for simulating soil–structure interaction problems involving discontinuities and localized stress transfer.

Overall, the comparison indicates that while FDM is computationally more efficient, the coupled DEM–FDM approach offers superior accuracy in representing the nonlinear and discrete nature of soil arching mechanisms.

Engineering implications

The outcomes of this research provide several important implications for the practical design and analysis of soldier pile retaining wall systems. The results indicate that the conventional assumption of a uniform lateral earth pressure acting on the wall facing, as commonly adopted in design manuals such as FHWA29 and AASHTO1, tends to overestimate both the lateral load and the resulting bending moment in the facing. By explicitly accounting for soil arching, the actual pressure distribution can be better represented, leading to more economical and structurally efficient wall designs.

From an engineering standpoint, the findings suggest that the design bending moment for wall facings may be reduced by as much as 40–60% compared to values obtained from uniform pressure assumptions, depending on the intensity of the arching effect. This reduction can translate into substantial savings in shotcrete thickness, reinforcement demand, and overall construction cost, while maintaining adequate safety margins. Additionally, the Arching Effect Coefficient (AEC) derived from the numerical analyses can serve as a practical parameter for modifying lateral pressure diagrams in design. For instance, designers can apply an equivalent triangular or trapezoidal pressure distribution characterized by AEC values ranging between 0.4 and 0.8, depending on the soil’s frictional and geometric conditions.

Moreover, the study emphasizes the need to consider pile spacing, soil frictional parameters, and facing stiffness as interactive design variables. Larger pile spacing or stiffer facing elements should be accompanied by appropriate adjustment of design earth pressures to maintain both serviceability and stability. The close agreement between the FDM and DEM–FDM analyses further supports the reliability of numerical modeling as a supplementary design tool, allowing engineers to predict soil–structure interaction effects with greater precision. Incorporating soil arching considerations into design guidelines could thus improve the balance between safety and economy in the construction of deep excavations and earth retention systems.

Although the numerical models were thoroughly calibrated and verified through parameter consistency checks, direct comparison with laboratory or field experimental data was beyond the scope of this study. Nevertheless, the observed pressure-redistribution trends are consistent with previously reported findings9,10,12. Future research should incorporate large-scale laboratory tests or field measurements to further validate and refine the numerical predictions.

This study conducted a comprehensive numerical investigation of the soil arching phenomenon and its influence on the lateral earth pressure distribution behind the wall facing in soldier pile retaining wall systems. Limitations: The current model assumes rigid boundaries for piles and linear elastic behavior for the shotcrete facing. Effects such as cracking, nonlinear material response, and three-dimensional pile–soil interaction were not included. These simplifications may slightly affect quantitative results but do not change the observed qualitative trends. The shotcrete facing was modeled as a homogeneous, linear elastic continuum. Crack initiation and the nonlinear response of reinforced shotcrete were not captured. Consequently, the computed tensile stresses represent equivalent elastic values rather than true post-cracking behavior. Future work should incorporate nonlinear concrete or smeared-crack models to account for tension stiffening and reinforcement effects.
