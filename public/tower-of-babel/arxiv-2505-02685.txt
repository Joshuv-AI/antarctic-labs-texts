On the Spectral Expansion of Monotone Subsets of the Hypercube
Yumou Fei, Renato Ferreira Pinto Jr

Abstract
We study the spectral gap of subgraphs of the hypercube induced by monotone subsets of vertices. For a monotone subset A ⊆ { 0 , 1 } n A\subseteq\{0,1\}^{n} of density μ ⁡ ( A ) \mu(A) , the previous best lower bound on the spectral gap, due to Cohen [ Coh16 ] , was γ ≳ μ ⁡ ( A ) / n 2 \gamma\gtrsim\mu(A)/n^{2} , improving upon the earlier bound γ ≳ μ ​ ( A ) 2 / n 2 \gamma\gtrsim\mu(A)^{2}/n^{2} established by Ding and Mossel [ DM14 ] . In this paper, we prove the optimal lower bound γ ≳ μ ⁡ ( A ) / n \gamma\gtrsim\mu(A)/n . As a corollary, we improve the mixing time upper bound of the random walk on constant-density monotone sets from O ⁡ ( n 3 ) O(n^{3}) , as shown by Ding and Mossel, to O ⁡ ( n 2 ) O(n^{2}) . Along the way, we develop two new inequalities that may be of independent interest: (1) a directed L 2 L^{2} -Poincaré inequality on the hypercube, and (2) an “approximate” FKG inequality for monotone sets.

Introduction

Suppose
G=(V,E)
is a good expander graph, so that a random walk on the vertices of
G
is

fast mixing
, i. e. converges quickly to its stationary distribution. When is a random walk on a
subgraph of
G
also fast mixing? More precisely, what kinds of subgraph restrictions preserve good
expansion?

This paper studies the case of the hypercube graph
H_{n}
, where vertices
x,y\in\{0,1\}^{n}
are
connected by an edge if and only if they differ in exactly one coordinate. Recall that the

lazy random walk
on
H_{n}
has mixing time
\Theta(n\log n)
. Given a subset
A\subseteq\{0,1\}^{n}
of vertices, we consider the random walk on
\{0,1\}^{n}

censored
to
A
.

Definition 1.1

(Censored random walk,
[
DM14
]
)
.

Given
A\subseteq\{0,1\}^{n}
, the
random walk on
\{0,1\}^{n}
censored to

A
is defined as follows. On state
x\in A
, sample
i\in[n]
uniformly
at random and let
y
be the vertex obtained by flipping the
i
-th bit of

x
. Then

1.

If
y\in A
, flip a coin and either stay at
x
or move to
y
(each with probability
1/2
).

2.

If
y\not\in A
, stay at
x
(in which case we call this a
censored
step).

Without further guarantees on
A
, the censored random walk may mix well or
extremely poorly even when
A
is large and connected, as the following two
examples illustrate:

Example 1

(Subcube)
.

Let
S\subset[n]
be a set of indices, and let
A
be the subcube given by
the vertices
x\in\{0,1\}^{n}
satisfying
x_{i}=0
for all
i\in S
. Then the
censored random walk is essentially a random walk on the smaller cube

\{0,1\}^{n^{\prime}}
, where
n^{\prime}\vcentcolon=n-|S|
, except that only an

O(n^{\prime}/n)
-fraction of the transitions are not censored. Thus the
censored random walk has mixing time
O(\tfrac{n}{n^{\prime}}\cdot n^{\prime}\log n^{\prime})=O(n\log n)
.

Example 2

(Middle slice bridge)
.

Let
x^{*}\in\{0,1\}^{n}
be an arbitrary vertex with Hamming weight
|x^{*}|=\lfloor n/2\rfloor
, and consider the set
A\vcentcolon=\left\{x\in\{0,1\}^{n}:|x|\neq\lfloor n/2\rfloor\right\}\cup\{x^{*}\}
. A spectral argument shows that the
mixing time of the censored random walk is exponential in
n
.

Thus, it is natural to ask: what properties of
A
ensure fast mixing? In

[
DM14
]
, Ding & Mossel initiated the study of random walks
censored to
monotone
sets
A
1

A
is called monotone if
x\in A
implies
y\in A
whenever
x\preceq y
, where the latter denotes the

x\preceq y
if
x_{i}\leq y_{i}

and showed that, when

fast mixing. Concretely, letting
\mu
denote the uniform distribution on

\{0,1\}^{n}
, they proved

(
[

)
.

Let
A\subseteq\{0,1\}^{n}
be a non-empty monotone set. Then the random walk on

\{0,1\}^{n}
censored to
A
has mixing time

t_{\mathsf{mix}}\leq 512\cdot\left(\frac{n}{\mu(A)}\right)^{2}\log(4\cdot 2^{n}\mu(A)).

When the density
\mu(A)
is a constant, the above implies a mixing time bound of
O(n^{3})
. In particular, for the uncensored special case
A=H_{n}
, this result only yields an upper
bound of
O(n^{3})
on the mixing time, versus the optimal
\Theta(n\log n)
; this suggests the
potential for improving upon
Theorem

1.2
, and indeed
[
DM14
]
asked the following question.

Question 1.3

(
[
DM14
, Question 1.1]
)
.

Suppose
\mu(A)\geq\varepsilon
for some constant
\varepsilon>0
. Is it true that
t_{\mathsf{mix}}\leq O_{\varepsilon}(n\log n)
?

Our main result makes progress on this question by showing an
O(n^{2})
mixing time bound for monotone sets
A
of constant density.

Theorem 1.4
.

Let
A\subseteq\{0,1\}^{n}
be a non-empty monotone set. Then the random walk on

\{0,1\}^{n}
censored to
A
has mixing time

t_{\mathsf{mix}}\leq\frac{2n}{\mu(A)}\cdot\log(4\cdot 2^{n}\mu(A))\,.

1.1
Spectral gap

It is well-known that the mixing time of a Markov chain is related to the
spectral gap
of its generator (see e.g.
[
LP17
]
), or equivalently the
spectral expansion
of the underlying graph. The Poincaré inequality (see e.g.
[
ODo14
]
) for the hypercube states that the spectral expansion of the (lazy) hypercube
H_{n}
is exactly
1/n
, which implies the (non-tight) mixing time bound
O(n^{2})
for the lazy random walk on
H_{n}
.

Thus, a natural question related to mixing under censoring is the
robustness
of the classical Poincaré inequality under vertex removal from
H_{n}
. Specifically, does the spectral expansion of
H_{n}
remain on the order of
1/n
if only a small fraction of the vertices are removed, or does it exhibit a significant deviation?
Example

2
demonstrates that the spectral expansion can shrink to exponentially small values if the removed set is arbitrary. Our goal is to show that when the removed set of vertices is monotone (and not too large), the spectral expansion remains at least on the order of
1/n
.

For the purpose of clearer comparison with the classical Poincaré inequality, we introduce the Dirichlet form of a function on
A
. Note that in the case
A=H_{n}
, the following definition is exactly the “influence”
[
ODo14
, Definition 2.27]
of the function
f:\{0,1\}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
.

Definition 1.5
.

Fix a monotone set
A\subseteq\{0,1\}^{n}
. For all
f:A\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, we define

\mathcal{E}_{A}(f)\vcentcolon=\frac{1}{4}\cdot\underset{x\in A}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(f(x)-f(x^{\oplus i})\right)^{2}\cdot\mathds{1}\left[x^{\oplus i}\in A\right]\right].

Here
x^{\oplus i}
denotes the binary string obtained by flipping the
i
-th bit of
x
.

We can now state our “robust” version of the Poincaré inequality.

Theorem 1.6
.

Let
A\subseteq\{0,1\}^{n}
be a non-empty monotone set. We have for all
f:A\rightarrow\mathbb{R}

{\mathrm{Var}}_{A}\left[f\right]\leq\frac{1}{1-\sqrt{1-\mu(A)}}\cdot\mathcal{E}_{A}(f).

Here
{\mathrm{Var}}_{A}\left[f\right]
stands for the variance of
f(x)
where
x
is a uniformly random element of
A
.

Note that in the case
A=H_{n}
, the above theorem recovers the Poincaré inequality on the
hypercube. We remark that
Theorem

1.4
follows directly from
Theorem

1.6
due to
standard Markov chain theory (e.g.
[
LP17
, Theorem 12.4]
), so the rest of the paper focuses mainly of proving
Theorem

1.6
.

Theorem

1.6
can also be stated as a lower bound on the spectral gap of
H_{A}
—the
subgraph of
H_{n}
induced by
A
, with a self-loop added to vertex
x
for each edge
\{x,y\}
of the hypercube with

x\in A
and
y\not\in A
(which counts as
1
toward the degree of
x
). For convenience of reference, in this paper we
define
the spectral gap using the language of
Theorem

1.6
.

Definition 1.7
.

Let
A\subseteq\{0,1\}^{n}
be a monotone set with at least 2 elements. We define

\gamma(H_{A})\vcentcolon=\frac{1}{n}\cdot\inf_{f\not\in\mathsf{const}_{A}}\frac{\mathcal{E}_{A}(f)}{{\mathrm{Var}}_{A}\left[f\right]},

where
f
ranges over all non-constant functions from
A
to
\mathbb{R}
.

Now
Theorem

1.6
can be stated as
\gamma(H_{A})\geq\frac{1}{n}\left(1-\sqrt{1-\mu(A)}\right)\gtrsim\mu(A)/n
, for monotone sets
A
with
|A|\geq 2
.

1.2
Proof overview: previous work

We begin by briefly describing the proof of
Theorem

1.2
in
[
DM14
]
. The proof in
[
DM14
]
also analyzes the spectral expansion of
H_{A}
, achieving the lower bound
\gamma(H_{A})\gtrsim\mu(A)^{2}/n^{2}
.

By Cheeger’s inequality, to obtain a lower bound on
\gamma(H_{A})
, it suffices to lower bound the
bottleneck ratio

\phi(H_{A})\vcentcolon=\min_{S\subseteq A}\frac{|E(S,A\setminus S)|}{\min\{|S|,|A\setminus S|\}},

where
E(S,A\setminus S)
denotes the set of edges
\{x,y\}
of
H_{n}
with
x\in S
and
y\in A\setminus S
. By an isoperimetric inequality of the hypercube, there is good lower bound on the number of boundary edges connecting a vertex in
S
to a vertex in
\{0,1\}^{n}\setminus S
. However, it is not immediately clear how many of these edges actually lead to
A\setminus S
. The crucial observation of
[
DM14
]
is that if an a edge from a vertex
x\in S
goes “upward”—that is, if its other endpoint
y\in\{0,1\}^{n}\setminus S
satisfies
y\succeq x
—then by the monotonicity of
A
, we must have
y\in A\setminus S
.

Coincidentally, there is a “directed isopetrimetric inequality”
[
GGLRS00
, Theorem 2]
, developed by the property testing community, which provides a lower bound on exactly the number of such
upward
boundary edges. Specifically, it gives a lower bound on the number of edges connecting a vertex
x\in S
to a vertex
y\in\{0,1\}^{n}\setminus S
with
y\succeq x
in terms of the
distance
of
S
to monotonicity. The precise notion of distance is less important than the key fact that, when
|A|
is not too small, at least one of
S
and
A\setminus S
must be far from any monotone set—i.e., it must have a large distance to monotonicity—due to the FKG inequality
[
FKG71
]
. As a result, we can lower bound the number of upward boundary edges from either
S
or
A\setminus S
. Since both sets of upward edges are subsets
E(S,A\setminus S)
, we can thus arrive at a lower bound on
|E(S,A\setminus S)|
and hence
\phi(H_{A})
.

The work
[
DM14
]
actually achieves the optimal bound
\phi(H_{A})\gtrsim\mu(A)/n
on the bottleneck ratio. However, this only translates to a quadratically worse bound
\gamma(H_{A})\gtrsim\mu(A)^{2}/n^{2}
for the spectral expansion, due to the loss incurred by applying Cheeger’s inequality. One natural idea is to avoid using Cheeger’s inequality by directly bounding the
spectral gap
\gamma(H_{A})
. In this direction,
[
Coh16
]
used a
canonical path argument to show the bound
\gamma(H_{A})\gtrsim\mu(A)/n^{2}
,
which improves upon
Theorem

1.2
by a factor of
\mu(A)
. However,
this improvement is only effective when
\mu(A)\ll 1
and the dependence on
n
remains suboptimal.

Directed Poincaré inequality.

As indicated in
Section

1.2
, directed isoperimetric
inequalities aim to lower bound the number of “upward boundary edges” from a
set
S
to
\{0,1\}^{n}\setminus S
in terms of the “distance” of
S
to
monotonicity; see
Section

1.6
for more background.

For our application, we require a directed isoperimetric inequality in the
L^{2}

setting, which is the setting associated with the classical Poincaré inequality
and the spectral gap. The first step is to define, for any

f:\{0,1\}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, its
L^{2}
-distance to monotonicity and
its “upward boundary edges”.

Definition 1.8

(Distance to monotonicity)
.

For a function
f:\{0,1\}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, we define

\mathsf{dist}_{2}^{\mathsf{mono}}(f)\vcentcolon=\inf_{g\in\mathsf{mono}}\sqrt{\underset{x\in\{0,1\}^{n}}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\big(f(x)-g(x)\big)^{2}\right]},

where
g
ranges over all monotone increasing functions from
\{0,1\}^{n}
to
\mathbb{R}
.

Definition 1.9

(Upward boundary)
.

For all
f:\{0,1\}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, we define

\mathcal{E}^{-}(f)\vcentcolon=\frac{1}{4}\cdot\underset{x\in\{0,1\}^{n}}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{n}\min\left\{0,f(x^{i\rightarrow 1})-f(x^{i\rightarrow 0})\right\}^{2}\right].

Here for
x\in\{0,1\}^{n}
and
b\in\{0,1\}
,
x^{i\rightarrow b}
stands for the string
(x_{1},\dots,x_{i-1},b,x_{i+1},\dots,x_{n})
.

We are now ready to state our directed
L^{2}
-Poincaré inequality.

Theorem 1.10

(Directed Poincaré inequality)
.

For all functions
f:\{0,1\}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, we have

\mathsf{dist}_{2}^{\mathsf{mono}}(f)^{2}\leq\mathcal{E}^{-}(f).

Approximate FKG inequality.

The classical FKG inequality of
[
FKG71
]
states that if
f,g:\{0,1\}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
are monotone increasing functions and
x
is a uniformly random element of
\{0,1\}^{n}
, then the random variables
f(x)
and
g(x)
are nonnegatively correlated. It is well-known that this statement holds for increasing functions over a broader class of partially ordered sets (posets). In our proof, we crucially need a lower bound on the correlation ratio of any two increasing functions
A\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, where the set
A
is partially ordered by the natural partial order of the hypercube. However, it is easy to see that the FKG inequality does not generally hold on this poset. Thus, we seek an “approximate” version of the FKG inequality, where we are content with a correlation ratio bounded away from
-1
, rather than necessarily nonnegative.

Definition 1.11

(Approximate FKG ratio)
.

Fix a monotone set
A\subseteq\{0,1\}^{n}
with at least 2 elements. We define the
approximate FKG ratio
of the poset
A
to be

\delta(A):=\min\left\{0,\inf_{f,g\in\mathsf{mono}_{A}\setminus\mathsf{const}_{A}}\frac{\mathrm{Cov}_{A}\left[f,g\right]}{\sqrt{{\mathrm{Var}}_{A}\left[f\right]\cdot{\mathrm{Var}}_{A}\left[g\right]}}\right\},

where
f
and
g
range over all non-constant monotone increasing functions from
A
to
\mathbb{R}
. Here,
\mathrm{Cov}_{A}\left[f,g\right]
stands for the covariance of the random variable pair
(f(x),g(x))
where
x
is a uniformly random element of
A
.

Theorem 1.12

(Approximate FKG inequality)
.

For any monotone set
A\subseteq\{0,1\}^{n}
with at least 2 elements, we have
\delta(A)\geq-\sqrt{1-\mu(A)}
.

1.4
The case of small
A
: fast mixing requires good FKG ratio

The bound in
Theorem

1.6
gives only a spectral gap bound
\gamma(H_{A})\gtrsim\mu(A)/n
for the random walk censored to
A
. The dependence on
n
is clearly optimal: even in the case
A=H_{n}
, the spectral gap is exactly
1/n
. The next example shows that the asymptotic dependence on
\mu(A)
is also optimal.

Example 3

(
[
DM14
, Example 1.3]
)
.

Assume
n/4\leq m\leq n/2
and consider
A=\{x\in\{0,1\}^{n}:x_{1}=\dots=x_{m}=1\}\cup\{x\in\{0,1\}^{n}:x_{m+1}=\dots=x_{2m}=1\}
, the union of two subcubes. In this case,
\mu(A)\sim 2^{-m+1}
. Let
f:A\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be defined by
f(x)=1
if
x_{1}=\dots=x_{m}=1
and
f(x)=-1
otherwise. Then
\mathcal{E}_{A}(f)\sim m\cdot 2^{-m}
and
{\mathrm{Var}}_{A}\left[f\right]\sim 1
, so
{\mathrm{Var}}_{A}\left[f\right]\gtrsim\mu(A)^{-1}\cdot\mathcal{E}_{A}(f)
. Standard Markov chain theory (e.g.
[
LP17
, Theorem 7.4]
) shows that the mixing time of the random walk censored to
A
is exponentially large in
n
.

Remarkably,
Example

3
is also where the approximate FKG inequality fails badly—if we let
A
be the union of two subcubes as in
Example

3
and consider the indicator functions of the two subcubes, it is easy to see that they are both increasing functions on the poset
A
but are very anti-correlated (i. e. the approximate FKG ratio
\delta(A)
is very close to
-1
).

Our results actually reveal that, when
A
is a monotone set, torpid mixing happens
if and only if
the approximate FKG ratio of
A
is close to
-1
.

Theorem 1.13
.

Let
A\subseteq\{0,1\}^{n}
be a monotone set with at least 2 elements. Then for all functions
f:A\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, we have

(1+\delta(A))\cdot{\mathrm{Var}}_{A}\left[f\right]\leq\mathcal{E}_{A}(f).

Theorem 1.14
.

Let
A\subseteq\{0,1\}^{n}
be a monotone set with at least 2 elements. Then for some non-constant function
f:A\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, we have

(1+\delta(A))\cdot n\cdot{\mathrm{Var}}_{A}\left[f\right]\geq\mathcal{E}_{A}(f).

The two theorems above imply that
(1+\delta(A))/n\leq\gamma(H_{A})\leq 1+\delta(A)
, which means the approximate FKG ratio of
A
characterizes the spectral gap of
H_{A}
up to a factor of
n
.

Remark 1
.

The case
A=H_{n}
demonstrates that the lower bound
(1+\delta(A))/n\leq\gamma(H_{A})
is tight. Moreover, there are examples indicating that the upper bound
\gamma(H_{A})\leq 1+\delta(A)
is tight up to a constant factor—for instance, when
A=\{x\in\{0,1\}^{n}:|x|\leq 1\}
.

1.5
Open problems

Our work leaves two avenues for potential improvement, roughly corresponding to
two regimes in the size of the set
A
. We discuss each of these directions in
turn.

Large
A
.

When
\mu(A)\geq\varepsilon
for some fixed constant
\varepsilon>0
, we establish the tight asymptotic bound
\gamma(H_{A})\gtrsim 1/n
. However, this only yields the mixing time bound
t_{\mathsf{mix}}=O_{\varepsilon}(n^{2})
, which does not resolve
1.3
. In many problems, an
O(n\log n)
mixing time bound can be obtained by proving a log-Sobolev inequality instead of an
L^{2}
-Poincaré inequality. It is plausible that our techniques could be further adapted to establish a log-Sobolev inequality, similar to how we extended the argument of
[
DM14
]
from the discrete setting to the
L^{2}
setting. Are there analogous versions of the directed isoperimetric inequality and the approximate FKG inequality in the log-Sobolev setting? We leave these as open questions.

Small
A
.

When no additional structure is imposed, the random walk censored to
A
may mix very slowly if
\mu(A)\ll 1
(e. g.
Example

3
). In many problems of interest, however,
A
possesses some form of structure, and the goal is to obtain a good bound on the mixing time, aiming for an efficient approximate sampling algorithm. For example, when
A
is a halfspace, i. e. defined by
A=\{x\in\{0,1\}^{n}:a_{1}x_{1}+\dots+a_{n}x_{n}\geq b\}
for nonnegative numbers
a_{1},\dots,a_{n},b
,
[
MS04
]
proves a mixing time bound of
n^{9/2+o(1)}
which yields a sampling algorithm for 0-1 knapsack solutions.

Our work (
Theorems

1.13
and
1.14
) reveals that the deciding factor for whether rapid mixing holds is the
approximate FKG ratio
of
A
rather than the size of
A
. However, we do not know how to leverage additional structure of
A
in a direct study of its approximate FKG ratio, and we leave the development of new tools for this purpose as an interesting direction for future work.

1.6
Related work

Mixing time of censored random walks.

Markov Chain Monte Carlo methods are the object
of extensive study in mathematics, statistical physics and theoretical computer science, and the
question of mixing time of random walks lies at the core of algorithms for approximate sampling and
counting; see e. g.
[
Jer98
,
Gur16
,
MT06
,
LP17
]
. In settings featuring combinatorial
structure such as in sampling matchings, independent sets, or spanning forests of a graph, or their
natural generalizations to the more algebraic setting of matroids, a
basis exchange
or

down-up
random walk is usually employed, and spectral arguments are used to bound the mixing
time; see e. g.
[
Jer03
,
ALGV19
,
CGM19
,
AL20
]
.

Our work focuses on the setting where the set
A
may not enjoy such rich
structure, and instead is only guaranteed to be monotone. As discussed above,
our results improve upon the spectral gap and mixing time bounds shown by the
previous works of
[
DM14
,
Coh16
]
. In the special case
where the monotone set
A
is additionally promised to contain every
x\in\{0,1\}^{n}
with Hamming weight at least
(\tfrac{1}{2}-\varepsilon)n
, i. e. the middle
layers of the hypercube, one may expect the censored and uncensored random walks
to behave similarly, and indeed in this case
[
Mat02
]
gave the optimal

\Omega_{\varepsilon}(1/n)
bound for the spectral gap and log-Sobolev constant of
the censored random walk, which implies the optimal
O_{\varepsilon}(n\log n)

mixing time bound.

Besides combinatorial or algebraic structure, one may also ask what

geometric
structure affords fast mixing. As mentioned above,

[
MS04
]
studied the censored random walk when the set
A
is a
halfspace, i. e.
A=\{x\in\{0,1\}^{n}:a_{1}x_{1}+\dots+a_{n}x_{n}\leq b\}
for
nonnegative numbers
a_{1},\dots,a_{n},b
, which corresponds to 0-1 knapsack
solutions. Intuitively, such set
A
should not contain bottlenecks even if it
is very small, and indeed
[
MS04
]
showed a mixing time bound of

n^{9/2+o(1)}

Approximate FKG inequality

The goal of this section is to prove
Theorem

1.12
, where we need to lower bound the correlation ratio between two monotone increasing functions on
A
. We first note that the case where the functions take values in
\{0,1\}
is easy. Indeed, we have the following simple lemma.

Lemma 2.1
.

Assume that
f,g:A\rightarrow\{0,1\}
are monotone increasing functions. Then we have
\underset{x\in A}{\mathbb{E}}\left[f(x)g(x)\right]\geq\mu(A)\cdot\underset{x\in A}{\mathbb{E}}\left[f(x)\right]\cdot\underset{x\in A}{\mathbb{E}}\left[g(x)\right]
.

Proof.

Let
B=\{x\in A:f(x)=1\}
and
C=\{x\in A:g(x)=1\}
. By the monotonicity of
f
and
g
, the sets
B
and
C
are both monotone subsets of the hypercube
\{0,1\}^{n}
. By the classical FKG inequality
[
FKG71
]
we know that
\mu(B\cap C)\geq\mu(B)\cdot\mu(C)
. Therefore,

\underset{x\in A}{\mathbb{E}}\left[f(x)g(x)\right]=\frac{\mu(B\cap C)}{\mu(A)}\geq\mu(A)\cdot\frac{\mu(B)}{\mu(A)}\cdot\frac{\mu(C)}{\mu(A)}=\mu(A)\cdot\underset{x\in A}{\mathbb{E}}\left[f(x)\right]\cdot\underset{x\in A}{\mathbb{E}}\left[g(x)\right].\qed

It is straightforward to deduce from
Lemma

2.1
that for monotone increasing functions
f,g:A\rightarrow\{0,1\}
, the desired approximate FKG inequality

\mathrm{Cov}_{A}\left[f,g\right]\geq-\sqrt{1-\mu(A)}\cdot\sqrt{{\mathrm{Var}}_{A}\left[f\right]\cdot{\mathrm{Var}}_{A}\left[g\right]}

holds.

The main challenge in
Theorem

1.12
lies in extending this idea to real-valued functions. In fact, the problem can be reduced to proving the following statement, which involves purely random variables rather than any structural property of the partially ordered set
A
.

Theorem 2.2
.

Let
(X,Y)
be a pair of real-valued random variables with bounded second moment. Suppose there is a constant
c\in[0,1)
such that for all
a,b\in\mathbb{R}
,

\mathbb{P}\left[X\geq a,Y\geq b\right]\geq c\cdot\mathbb{P}\left[X\geq a\right]\cdot\mathbb{P}\left[Y\geq b\right],

(1)

then we must have

\mathrm{Cov}\left[X,Y\right]\geq-\sqrt{(1-c)\cdot\mathrm{Var}\left[X\right]\cdot\mathrm{Var}\left[Y\right]}.

(2)

Proof of
Theorem

1.12
assuming
Theorem

2.2
.

Let
x
be a uniformly random element of
A
and let
X=f(x)
and
Y=g(x)
. Thus
\mathrm{Var}\left[X\right]={\mathrm{Var}}_{A}\left[f\right]
,
\mathrm{Var}\left[Y\right]={\mathrm{Var}}_{A}\left[g\right]
, and
\mathrm{Cov}\left[X,Y\right]=\mathrm{Cov}_{A}\left[f,g\right]
.

Now for each pair of
a,b\in\mathbb{R}
, if we define
f_{a},g_{b}:A\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
by

f_{a}(x)\vcentcolon=\mathds{1}\left[f(x)\geq a\right]\qquad\text{and}\qquad g_{b}(x)\vcentcolon=\mathds{1}\left[g(x)\geq b\right],

since they are clearly monotone increasing 0/1-valued functions, we can apply
Lemma

2.1
to
f_{a}
and
g_{b}
to deduce that

\mathbb{P}\left[X\geq a,Y\geq b\right]\geq\mu(A)\cdot\mathbb{P}\left[X\geq a\right]\cdot\mathbb{P}\left[Y\geq b\right].

If
\mu(A)=1
, then
A=\{0,1\}^{n}
and the conclusion follows from the classical FKG inequality. If
\mu(A)<1
, we apply
Theorem

2.2
to the random variable pair
(X,Y)
with constant
c=\mu(A)
, which yields exactly the desired conclusion.
∎

The remainder of this section is devoted to proving
Theorem

2.2
, which is surprisingly nontrivial. To illustrate the complexity of this inequality, we note that equality in (
2
) holds for a wide range of joint distributions of
(X,Y)
beyond the case captured by
Lemma

2.1
, i.e. where

and

take only two possible values.

Let

follow a discrete distribution supported on the grid

\mathbb{P}\left[X=0,Y=3\right]=\mathbb{P}\left[X=3,Y=0\right]=\frac{1}{15},\qquad\text{and}\qquad\mathbb{P}\left[X=2,Y=2\right]=\frac{4}{15}.

as well.

2.1
A symmetric model

A key challenge in
Theorem

2.2
lies in its lack of “centrosymmetry” with respect to
(X,Y)
. While the assumption (
1
) does not remain invariant under the substitution
X\mapsto-X
and
Y\mapsto-Y
, the conclusion is unaffected by such substitutions. This raises an intriguing question: what is the “symmetric” information inherent in (
1
) that leads to the conclusion?

In this subsection, we present an approach that effectively extracts the “symmetric” information from (
1
). To this end, we first define two Borel measures on
[0,1]
induced by
X
and
Y
.

Definition 2.3
.

Let
\varphi_{X}:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow[0,1]
be the Borel-measurable map defined by
a\mapsto\mathbb{P}\left[X\geq a\right]
, and then for each Borel set
E\subseteq[0,1]
, let
\alpha(E)
be the Lebesgue measure of the inverse image
\varphi_{X}^{-1}(E)
. The countable additivity of
\alpha
easily follows from the countable additivity of the Lebesgue measure.

The measure
\alpha
is referred to as the push-forward of the Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{R}
by the map
a\mapsto\mathbb{P}\left[X\geq a\right]
. Similarly define the Borel measure
\beta
on
[0,1]
to be the push-forward of the Lebesgue measure by the map
b\mapsto\mathbb{P}\left[Y\geq b\right]
.

The definition of push-forward measures naturally leads to the following “change of variable” formula, which is a standard fact in measure theory.

Proposition 2.4

(
[
Bog07
, Theorem 3.6.1]
)
.

Suppose
\varphi
is a Borel-measurable map from
\mathbb{R}
to
[0,1]
, and suppose
\lambda
is the push-forward of the Lebesgue measure under
\varphi
. Then for any Borel-measurable function
f:[0,1]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{\geq 0}
, we have

\int_{0}^{1}f(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\lambda(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(\varphi(a))\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}a.

We then define the reverse of a measure, which corresponds to the substitution
X\mapsto-X
.

Definition 2.5
.

If
\lambda
is a Borel measure on
[0,1]
, we let
\lambda^{\,\mathsf{R}}
be the Borel measure on
[0,1]
defined by
\lambda^{\,\mathsf{R}}(E)=\lambda(\{1-x:x\in E\})
, for Borel subsets
E
of
[0,1]
.

The following definition is the crucial tool in our proof of
Theorem

2.2
.

Definition 2.6
.

Fix a constant
c\in[0,1)
. We define the operator
K_{c}(\cdot,\cdot)
by

K_{c}(\lambda,\nu):=\int\int\min\left\{\sqrt{1-c}\cdot xy,\frac{(1-x)(1-y)}{\sqrt{1-c}}\right\}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\lambda(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\nu(y),

for Borel measures
\lambda,\nu
on
[0,1]
. For
c=0
, we omit the subscript and use the shorthand
K\vcentcolon=K_{0}
.

The next two propositions demonstrate that the operator
K_{c}(\cdot,\cdot)
is able to capture the variances and covariance of
X
and
Y
.
Proposition

2.8
is the key place where “symmetric” information is extracted from the condition (
1
).

Proposition 2.7
.

We have
\mathrm{Var}\left[X\right]=K(\alpha,\alpha^{\,\mathsf{R}})
. Similarly,
\mathrm{Var}\left[Y\right]=K(\beta,\beta^{\,\mathsf{R}})
.

Proof.

Writing expected values as integrations of cumulative distribution functions (the “layer cake representation”), we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}\left[X\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[X^{2}\right]-\mathbb{E}\left[X\right]^{2}

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\Big(\mathbb{P}\left[X\geq a,X\geq b\right]-\mathbb{P}\left[X\geq a\right]\mathbb{P}\left[X\geq b\right]\Big)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}a\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}b

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\min\Big\{\mathbb{P}\left[X\geq a\right]\Big(1-\mathbb{P}\left[X\geq b\right]\Big),\mathbb{P}\left[X\geq b\right]\Big(1-\mathbb{P}\left[X\geq a\right]\Big)\Big\}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}a\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}b

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}\min\{x(1-y),y(1-x)\}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\alpha(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\alpha(y)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}\min\{xy,(1-x)(1-y)\}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\alpha(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\alpha^{\,\mathsf{R}}(y)=K(\alpha,\alpha^{\,\mathsf{R}}),

where the fourth equality above follows from
Proposition

2.4
.
∎

Proposition 2.8
.

Assuming (
1
), we have
\mathrm{Cov}\left[X,Y\right]\geq-\sqrt{1-c}\cdot K_{c}(\alpha,\beta)
.

Proof.

In a similar way to the proof of
Proposition

2.7
, we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{Cov}\left[X,Y\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[XY\right]-\mathbb{E}\left[X\right]\mathbb{E}\left[Y\right]

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\Big(\mathbb{P}\left[X\geq a,Y\geq b\right]-\mathbb{P}\left[X\geq a\right]\mathbb{P}\left[Y\geq b\right]\Big)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}a\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}b.

(3)

Note that on one hand, by (
1
) we have

\mathbb{P}\left[X\geq a,Y\geq b\right]-\mathbb{P}\left[X\geq a\right]\mathbb{P}\left[Y\geq b\right]\geq-(1-c)\mathbb{P}\left[X\geq a\right]\mathbb{P}\left[Y\geq b\right].

(4)

On the other hand, by union bound we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[X\geq a,Y\geq b\right]-\mathbb{P}\left[X\geq a\right]\mathbb{P}\left[Y\geq b\right]

\displaystyle\geq 1-\mathbb{P}\left[X<a\right]-\mathbb{P}\left[Y<b\right]-\mathbb{P}\left[X\geq a\right]\mathbb{P}\left[Y\geq b\right]

\displaystyle=-\Big(1-\mathbb{P}\left[X\geq a\right]\Big)\Big(1-\mathbb{P}\left[Y\geq b\right]\Big).

(5)

Plugging (
4
) and (
5
) into (
3
), we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{Cov}\left[X,Y\right]

\displaystyle\geq-\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\min\Big\{(1-c)\mathbb{P}\left[X\geq a\right]\mathbb{P}\left[Y\geq b\right],\Big(1-\mathbb{P}\left[X\geq a\right]\Big)\Big(1-\mathbb{P}\left[Y\geq b\right]\Big)\Big\}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}a\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}b

\displaystyle=-\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}\min\{(1-c)xy,(1-x)(1-y)\}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\alpha(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\beta(y)

\displaystyle=-\sqrt{1-c}\cdot K_{c}(\alpha,\beta),

where the first equality above follows from
Proposition

2.4
.
∎

2.2

Proof of

2.2

2.9

Using

Technical preparations

Throughout this subsection, we let
c
be a fixed constant in the range
[0,1)

h

that will be useful in our proof of

2.9

Definition 2.10
.

We define a function
h:[0,1]\times[0,1]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
by

h(x,y):=-\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x}\frac{x}{\sqrt{1-cx}}\right)\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}y}\frac{1-y}{\sqrt{1-cy}}\right).

Proposition 2.11
.

The function
h
is continuous and nonnegative on
[0,1]\times[0,1]
and for all
x,y\in[0,1]
,

\int_{0}^{x}\int_{y}^{1}h(r,s)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r=\frac{x(1-y)}{\sqrt{(1-cx)(1-cy)}}.

Proof.

The integration of
h
follows from definition, and straightforward calculation shows

h(x,y)=\frac{(2-cx)(2-c-cy)}{4(1-cx)^{3/2}(1-cy)^{3/2}}\geq 0.\qed

Definition 2.12
.

We define a function
q:[0,1]\rightarrow[0,1]
by

q(x):=\frac{1-x}{1-cx}.

Proposition 2.13
.

The function
q
is continuous and monotone decreasing, and
q(q(x))=x
. For every
x,y\in[0,1]
, we also have

\frac{x(1-y)}{\sqrt{(1-cx)(1-cy)}}=\frac{q(y)(1-q(x))}{\sqrt{(1-cq(x))(1-cq(y)}}.

Proof.

The definition of
q
reduces to
1-x-q(x)+cxq(x)=0
, from which it is clear that
q
is an involution. Furthermore, we have the equivalent identities
(1-q(x))=x(1-cq(x))
, and
(1-cx)(1-q(x))=(1-c)x
. Multiplying the two identities gives

(1-c)\cdot x^{2}(1-cq(x))=(1-q(x))^{2}(1-cx),

and hence

\frac{x}{\sqrt{1-cx}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-c}}\cdot\frac{1-q(x)}{\sqrt{1-cq(x)}}.

(6)

Substituting
q(y)
for

gives

\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-c}}\cdot\frac{1-y}{\sqrt{1-cy}}=\frac{q(y)}{\sqrt{1-cq(y)}}.

(7)

Multiplying the above two identities yields the conclusion.
∎

\displaystyle\quad\sqrt{K(\alpha,\alpha^{\,\mathsf{R}})K(\beta,\beta^{\,\mathsf{R}})}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}\sqrt{(1-cx)(1-cy)}\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}\mathds{1}\left[r\leq x\leq s,\;q(s)\leq y\leq q(r)\right]h(r,s)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\alpha(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\beta(y)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}\sqrt{(1-cx)(1-cy)}\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}\mathds{1}\left[r\leq x\leq s,\;r\leq q(y)\leq s\right]h(r,s)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\alpha(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\beta(y)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}\mathds{1}\left[x\leq q(y)\right]\sqrt{(1-cx)(1-cy)}\cdot\frac{x(1-q(y))}{\sqrt{(1-cx)(1-cq(y))}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\alpha(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\beta(y)

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}\mathds{1}\left[x>q(y)\right]\sqrt{(1-cx)(1-cy)}\cdot\frac{q(y)(1-x)}{\sqrt{(1-cx)(1-cq(y))}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\alpha(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\beta(y)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}\mathds{1}\left[x\leq q(y)\right]\cdot\sqrt{1-c}\cdot xy\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\alpha(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\beta(y)

(using equation (
6
))

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}\mathds{1}\left[x>q(y)\right]\cdot\frac{(1-x)(1-y)}{\sqrt{1-c}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\alpha(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\beta(y)

(using equation (
7
))

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}\min\left\{\sqrt{1-c}\cdot xy,\frac{(1-x)(1-y)}{\sqrt{1-c}}\right\}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\alpha(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\beta(y)=K_{c}(\alpha,\beta).

In the second to last equality above, we used the fact that

\sqrt{1-c}\cdot xy\leq\frac{(1-x)(1-y)}{\sqrt{1-c}}\quad\text{if and only if}\quad x\leq q(y).\qed

3
From directed to undirected isoperimetry

In this section, we provide a proof of
Theorem

1.13
assuming
Theorem

1.10
. Throughout the section, we fix a monotone set
A\subseteq\{0,1\}^{n}
with at least 2 elements.

3.1
Domain extension

Since the target result,
Theorem

1.13
, focuses solely on the subset
A
of the hypercube, while
Theorem

1.10
applies only to functions defined on the entire hypercube, we first introduce a simple method for extending function domains to the whole hypercube.

Definition 3.1
.

We define an operator
T
that extends any function
f:A\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
to the function
T[f]:\{0,1\}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
defined by

T[f](x)=\begin{cases}\min_{y\in A}f(y),&\text{if }x\not\in A,\\
f(x),&\text{if }x\in A.\end{cases}

By defining the value of the function outside of the original domain
A
to be sufficiently small, the extension operator enjoys the following two useful properties that allow us to access the power of
Theorem

1.10
.

Proposition 3.2
.

For every function
f:A\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
we have

\mu(A)\cdot\mathcal{E}_{A}(f)=\mathcal{E}^{-}(T[f])+\mathcal{E}^{-}(T[-f]).

Proof.

Since
T[f]
is constant on
\{0,1\}^{n}\setminus A
, and since
T[f](x)\leq T[f](y)
for all
x\in\{0,1\}^{n}\setminus A
and
y\in A
, we know that for
x\in\{0,1\}^{n}
and
i\in[n]
,

T[f](x^{i\rightarrow 1})<T[f](x^{i\rightarrow 0})\quad\text{can hold only if}\quad x,x^{\oplus i}\in A.

So we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}^{-}(T[f])

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}\cdot\underset{x\in\{0,1\}^{n}}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{n}\min\left\{0,T[f](x^{i\rightarrow 1})-T[f](x^{i\rightarrow 0})\right\}^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}\cdot\underset{x\in\{0,1\}^{n}}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{n}\min\left\{0,T[f](x^{i\rightarrow 1})-T[f](x^{i\rightarrow 0})\right\}^{2}\cdot\mathds{1}\left[x,x^{\oplus i}\in A\right]\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{\mu(A)}{4}\cdot\underset{x\in A}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{n}\min\left\{0,f(x^{i\rightarrow 1})-f(x^{i\rightarrow 0})\right\}^{2}\cdot\mathds{1}\left[x^{\oplus i}\in A\right]\right]

Applying the above argument to
T[-f]
instead of
T[f]
, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}^{-}(T[-f])

\displaystyle=\frac{\mu(A)}{4}\cdot\underset{x\in A}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{n}\min\left\{0,-f(x^{i\rightarrow 1})+f(x^{i\rightarrow 0})\right\}^{2}\cdot\mathds{1}\left[x^{\oplus i}\in A\right]\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{\mu(A)}{4}\cdot\underset{x\in A}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{n}\max\left\{0,f(x^{i\rightarrow 1})-f(x^{i\rightarrow 0})\right\}^{2}\cdot\mathds{1}\left[x^{\oplus i}\in A\right]\right].

Adding the above two equations together yields

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}^{-}(T[f])+\mathcal{E}^{-}(T[-f])

\displaystyle=\frac{\mu(A)}{4}\cdot\underset{x\in A}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(f(x^{i\rightarrow 1})-f(x^{i\rightarrow 0})\right)^{2}\cdot\mathds{1}\left[x^{\oplus i}\in A\right]\right]

\displaystyle=\mu(A)\cdot\mathcal{E}_{A}(f).\qed

Proposition 3.3
.

For every function
f:A\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, there exists a monotone increasing function
g:A\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
such that

\left\|f-g\right\|_{2}\leq\mu(A)^{-1/2}\cdot\mathsf{dist}_{2}^{\mathsf{mono}}(T[f]),

where the
L^{2}
-norm is the norm in the inner product space
L^{2}(A)
.

Proof.

Since the collection of all monotone increasing real-valued functions on
\{0,1\}^{n}
form a closed set in the Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{\{0,1\}^{n}}
, there exists a monotone increasing function
\widetilde{g}:\{0,1\}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
such that
\left\|T[f]-\widetilde{g}\right\|_{2}=\mathsf{dist}_{2}^{\mathsf{mono}}(T[f])
, where the
L^{2}
-norm is the norm in the space
L^{2}(\{0,1\}^{n})
. Now note that the restriction
g\vcentcolon=\widetilde{g}|_{A}
is a monotone increasing function on
A
. Therefore,

\displaystyle\left\|f-g\right\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=\underset{x\in A}{\mathbb{E}}\left[(f(x)-g(x))^{2}\right]=\underset{x\in A}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\Big(T[f](x)-\widetilde{g}(x)\Big)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mu(A)^{-1}\cdot\underset{x\in\{0,1\}^{n}}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\Big(T[f](x)-\widetilde{g}(x)\Big)^{2}\right]=\mu(A)^{-1}\cdot\mathsf{dist}_{2}^{\mathsf{mono}}(T[f])^{2}.\qed

3.2
Correlation analysis

In this subsection, we lay some groundwork about correlation of functions (or equivalently, random variables) that will help prove
Theorem

1.13
. We begin with the following natural definition of correlation ratios.

Definition 3.4
.

For non-constant functions
g,h:A\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, we define

\rho(g,h)\vcentcolon=\frac{\mathrm{Cov}_{A}\left[g,h\right]}{\sqrt{{\mathrm{Var}}_{A}\left[g\right]\cdot{\mathrm{Var}}_{A}\left[h\right]}}.

The following triangle-inequality-type lemma is going to be important in the proof of
Theorem

1.13
. Conceptually, the lemma says that if functions
g
and
h
on
A
are not very correlated with each other (that is,
\rho(g,h)
is bounded away from 1), then
f
cannot be very correlated with both
g
and
h
at the same time. In particular, we will later use the the lemma in the case where
g
is a monotone increasing function and
h
is a monotone decreasing function, which cannot be very correlated if
\delta(A)
is bounded away from
-1
.

Proposition 3.5
.

Consider three non-constant functions
f,g,h:A\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
. We have

\max\{0,\rho(f,g)\}^{2}+\max\{0,\rho(f,h)\}^{2}\leq 1+\max\{0,\rho(g,h)\}.

Proof.

We may without loss generality assume that
{\mathrm{Var}}_{A}\left[f\right]={\mathrm{Var}}_{A}\left[g\right]={\mathrm{Var}}_{A}\left[h\right]=1
. In this case,
\mathrm{Cov}_{A}\left[f,g\right]=\rho(f,g)
,
\mathrm{Cov}_{A}\left[f,h\right]=\rho(f,h)
and
\mathrm{Cov}_{A}\left[g,h\right]=\rho(g,h)
.

If
\rho(f,g)<0
then the conclusion trivially holds since
\max\{0,\rho(f,h)\}^{2}\leq 1
. Similarly if
\rho(f,h)<0
, the conclusion is also trivial. In the following, we assume that
\rho(f,g)\geq 0
and
\rho(f,h)\geq 0
.

Consider the matrix

B\vcentcolon=\begin{bmatrix}1&\rho(f,g)&\rho(f,h)\\
\rho(f,g)&1&\rho(g,h)\\
\rho(f,h)&\rho(g,h)&1\end{bmatrix}.

For each vector
\bm{\lambda}=(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\lambda_{3})\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
, we know
\bm{\lambda}^{T}B\bm{\lambda}={\mathrm{Var}}_{A}\left[\lambda_{1}f+\lambda_{2}g+\lambda_{3}h\right]\geq 0
. So
B
is a positive semi-definite matrix. This means
\det B\geq 0
, and we can expand it into

1+2\rho(f,g)\rho(f,h)\rho(g,h)\geq\rho(f,g)^{2}+\rho(f,h)^{2}+\rho(g,h)^{2}.

(10)

If
\rho(g,h)<0
, then (
10
) implies
1\geq\rho(f,g)^{2}+\rho(f,h)^{2}
and we arrive at the conclusion. In the following we assume
\rho(g,h)\geq 0
.

Expanding the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality
{\mathrm{Var}}_{A}\left[f\right]\cdot{\mathrm{Var}}_{A}\left[g+h\right]\geq\mathrm{Cov}_{A}\left[f,g+h\right]^{2}
, we have

2+2\rho(g,h)\geq(\rho(f,g)+\rho(f,h))^{2}\geq 4\rho(f,g)\rho(f,h).

(11)

Multiplying both sides of (
11
) by
\rho(g,h)/2
and then adding it to (
10
), we get the desired conclusion

1+\rho(g,h)\geq\rho(f,g)^{2}+\rho(f,h)^{2}.\qed

The following definition serves to interpret correlation ratios in terms of
L^{2}
distances.

Definition 3.6
.

For functions
f,g:A\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, we define

\tau(f,g)\vcentcolon=\min_{a\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0},b\in\mathbb{R}}\left\|f-(ag+b)\right\|_{2},

where the
L^{2}
-norm is the norm in the inner product space
L^{2}(A)
.

Proposition 3.7
.

Consider two non-constant functions
f,g:A\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
. We have

\tau(f,g)^{2}=\Big(1-\max\{0,\rho(f,g)\}^{2}\Big)\cdot{\mathrm{Var}}_{A}\left[f\right].

Proof.

Note that

\displaystyle\tau(f,g)^{2}

\displaystyle=\min_{a\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0},b\in\mathbb{R}}\left\|f-(ag+b)\right\|_{2}^{2}=\min_{a\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}}{\mathrm{Var}}_{A}\left[f-ag\right]

\displaystyle=\min_{a\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}}\Big(a^{2}\cdot{\mathrm{Var}}_{A}\left[g\right]-2a\cdot\mathrm{Cov}_{A}\left[f,g\right]+{\mathrm{Var}}_{A}\left[f\right]\Big).

(12)

If
\rho(f,g)<0
, then
\mathrm{Cov}_{A}\left[f,g\right]<0
, and the quadratic polynomial in the right hand side of (
12
) is minimized at
a=0
. Therefore
\tau(f,g)^{2}={\mathrm{Var}}_{A}\left[f\right]
, as desired.

If
\rho(f,g)\geq 0
, then
\mathrm{Cov}_{A}\left[f,g\right]\geq 0
, and the quadratic polynomial in the right hand side of (
12
) is minimized at
a=\mathrm{Cov}_{A}\left[f,g\right]/{\mathrm{Var}}_{A}\left[g\right]
. Therefore (
12
) simplifies to

\tau(f,g)^{2}=-\frac{\mathrm{Cov}_{A}\left[f,g\right]^{2}}{{\mathrm{Var}}_{A}\left[g\right]}+{\mathrm{Var}}_{A}\left[f\right]=(1-\rho(f,g)^{2})\cdot{\mathrm{Var}}_{A}\left[f\right],

as desired.
∎

Spectral theory and heat flow for directed graphs

In this section, as a first step toward proving our directed Poincaré inequality for the hypercube
(
Theorem

1.10
), we first set up a framework that applies to the more general case of directed
weighted graphs. Specifically, we revisit and extend the study of directed analogues of classical
concepts from spectral graph theory such as the Laplacian operator, the Dirichlet energy, and the
heat flow; define a directed notion of spectral gap for weighed directed graphs; and show that
bounding this
dynamical spectral gap
suffices for proving a directed Poincaré inequality.
Then, in the next section,
Theorem

1.10
will follow as an application once we establish a
bound on the directed spectral graph of the directed hypercube graph.

Prior work on spectral theory for directed graphs.

There has been extensive prior work
developing spectral graph theory beyond the classical setting of undirected graphs, toward capturing
directed graphs and hypergraphs. Early work of
[
Fil91
,
Chu05
]
associated a certain Hermitian
matrix with each directed graph, and showed a Cheeger-type inequality based on the eigenvalues of
that matrix, and many subsequent works have built upon that foundation; we refer to the recent
thesis
[
Tun25
]
for a thorough review, and here we mention two recent lines of work that are
closest to our setting. One line of works
[
LTW23
,
LTW24
,
Tun25
]
has developed a theory of

reweighted eigenvalues
capturing expansion properties of directed graphs and hypergraphs,
proved Cheeger inequalities for these settings, and devised efficient algorithms for graph
partitioning. Another line of works
[
Yos16
,
Yos19
,
FSY21
,
IMTY22
]
has pursued similar goals by
analyzing a
nonlinear Laplacian
operator and the heat equation associated with it.

While our interest in a spectral theory for directed graphs is related to these previous works (and
indeed we will build upon the approach of
[
Yos16
]
), our focus is slightly different. In a
nutshell, while prior works have focused on spectral characterizations of good expansion of a
directed graph
G
as captured by directed versions of Cheeger inequalities (for edge conductance or
vertex expansion) and mixing time of random walks, our focus will be on the quality of
G
as the
“substrate” for a dynamical process; we will consider
G
a good directed spectral expander if it
affords fast convergence for that process. In particular, our perspective allows for
directed
acyclic graphs
to be considered good expanders, which is a stark departure from prior perspectives
– as we briefly explain next.

Indeed, a central focus of prior works has been to establish Cheeger inequalities of the type

\vec{\lambda}_{2}\lesssim\vec{\phi}(G)\lesssim\sqrt{\vec{\lambda}_{2}}\,,

(14)

where
\vec{\lambda}_{2}
denotes a relevant second eigenvalue related to the directed weighted graph

G
, and the edge conductance
\vec{\phi}(G)
of
G
is

\vec{\phi}(G)\vcentcolon=\min_{\emptyset\neq S\subsetneq V}\frac{\min\left\{w(\delta^{+}(S)),w(\delta^{+}(V\setminus S))\right\}}{\min\left\{\vol_{w}(S),\vol_{w}(V\setminus S)\right\}}\,,

where
\delta^{+}(S)
denotes the outgoing edge boundary of
S
and
\vol_{w}(S)
denotes the total
weighted degree of all vertices in
S
. Now, if
G
is not strongly connected, then in general there
exists a set
S
with positive volume but no outgoing edges, which makes
\vec{\phi}(G)
and thus

\vec{\lambda}_{2}
zero. In particular, this is the case for the directed hypercube graph which we
are interested in, so if we hope to show a non-trivial directed Poincaré inequality via a spectral
argument, such a quantity
\vec{\lambda}_{2}
will not do.

Our approach.

The type of spectral theory for directed graphs we study in this section was first developed by

[
Yos16
]
in the context of network analysis. In that work,
[
Yos16
]
defined a nonlinear
Laplacian operator acting on real-valued functions defined on the vertices of a directed graph,
showed that this operator induces a dynamical process that is a directed analogue of the classical
heat flow on graphs, proved that this operator has nontrivial eigenvalues, and established a Cheeger
inequality like (
14
) for this setting. While
[
Yos16
]
focused on the
implications of directed spectral theory for graph partitioning and related problems in network
analysis, we focus on the dynamical properties of the heat flow on directed graphs – namely its
convergence to a
monotone
limit, and its connection to the directed Poincaré inequality.

Let us briefly motivate and preview the main ideas in our argument. In classical spectral graph
theory, given a graph
G=(V,E)
, the following four concepts play a central role:

1.

The Laplacian operator
\mathscr{L}
, which acts on function
f
by outputting another function

\mathscr{L}f
.

2.

The Dirichlet energy functional
\mathcal{E}
, which associates with each
f
an energy
\mathcal{E}(f)\geq 0
measuring the “local variance” of
f
along edges of
G
.

3.

The heat flow semigroup
S_{t}
, which captures a dynamical process which starts at some
initial state
f
and has its rate of change governed by the Laplacian:
\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}S_{t}f=\mathscr{L}S_{t}f
. The heat flow informally “sends mass” along each edge of
G
from the vertex
with higher
f
-value to the vertex with lower
f
-value, causing the system to converge to
an equilibrium state.

4.

The Poincaré inequality, which states that
\mathrm{Var}\left[f\right]\leq\frac{1}{\lambda}\,\mathcal{E}(f)
. The
best constant
\lambda
is called the
spectral gap
of
G
.

The appearance of the Poincaré inequality above hints at the relevance of this theory to our goal of
proving a directed Poincaré inequality, and we mentioned above that our strategy toward this goal
will be to define a directed version of the spectral gap. As a motivation for this strategy, we
recall that the spectral gap ties together essentially all of the elements of the list above;
indeed, as summarized in
[
Han14
, Theorem 2.18]
, the following are equivalent given a constant

c\geq 0
:

1.

Poincaré inequality:
\mathrm{Var}\left[f\right]\leq c\mathcal{E}(f)
for all
f
.

2.

Variance decay:
\mathrm{Var}\left[S_{t}f\right]\leq e^{-2t/c}\mathrm{Var}\left[f\right]
for all
f,t
.

3.

Energy decay:
\mathcal{E}(S_{t}f)\leq e^{-2t/c}\mathcal{E}(f)
for all
f,t
.

To prove a directed Poincaré inequality, we replace the Laplacian operator
\mathscr{L}
with an operator

\mathscr{L}^{-}
which, intuitively, only “sends mass” from vertex
u
to vertex
v
if
(u,v)
is a
directed edge and
f(u)>f(v)
, i. e.
f
violates monotonicity along edge
(u,v)
; the dynamical
system induced by
\mathscr{L}^{-}
is the
directed heat flow
on
G
, which was studied by

[
Yos16
]
. The directed heat flow is precisely the
gradient system
for the directed energy
functional
\mathcal{E}^{-}(f)
, i. e. the upward boundary from
Definition

1.9
. Thus, this system
intuitively “corrects” the local violations of monotonicity as quickly as possible, and indeed it
converges to a monotone function as
t\to\infty
.

This directed theory cannot fully analogize the classical situation above; for example, variance
decay fails to hold, because non-constant monotone functions are (non-unique!) stationary solutions.
Instead, we will
define
the
dynamical spectral gap
of
G
as the best constant
characterizing the (directed) energy decay, as in
Item

3
above, and then show that
(the directed version of)
Item

3
implies (the directed version of)

Item

1
.

As mentioned in the introduction, recent work of
[
Fer24
]
also proved a directed
Poincaré inequality – for functions defined on the continuous cube
[0,1]^{n}
– using a dynamical
argument. Indeed, that work also took as its starting point the connections between the heat flow
and the Poincaré inequality, and showed that the natural directed version of the heat flow in
continuous space enjoys exponential energy decay, which implies a directed Poincaré inequality for
that setting. Our proof is conceptually similar to the proof of
[
Fer24
]
, but our techniques differ
in at least two ways: 1)
[
Fer24
]
required analytical arguments
from the theory of partial differential equations (PDEs), while we are able to study our dynamical
process as an ordinary differential equation (ODE) thanks to the finite-dimensional nature of our
problem; and 2)
[
Fer24
]
used tools from optimal transport theory to tensorize their
one-dimensional result, while we obtain a multidimensional inequality directly
by studying the directed heat flow as a gradient system. In this last
regard, our proof also bears resemblance to, and is inspired by prior work of
[
KLLR18
]
on the
so-called
Paulsen problem
from operator theory, where a “movement decay” property of a
suitable dynamical system was used to bound the distance between the initial and equilibrium states
of that system.

V
, we denote by
L^{2}(V)
the Hilbert space obtained by endowing the set of
V\to\mathbb{R}
functions with the inner product
\left\langle f,g\right\rangle\vcentcolon=\underset{x\in V}{\mathbb{E}}\left[f(x)g(x)\right]
, where the
expectation is taken with respect to the uniform distribution over
V
. This inner product induces
the norm
\|f\|=\sqrt{\left\langle f,f\right\rangle}=\sqrt{\mathbb{E}\left[f^{2}\right]}
.

For each
u\in V
, we write
e_{u}\in L^{2}(V)
for the standard basis vector given by
e_{u}(v)\vcentcolon=\mathds{1}\left[u=v\right]
.

4.1
The directed Laplacian

Let
G=(V,w)
be a directed weighted graph, where
w:V\times V\to[0,+\infty)
is function
specifying the weights of edges in
G
. By convention, we say that
(u,v)\in V\times V
is an
edge in
G
when
w(u,v)>0
. We say
G
is
undirected
is
w
is a symmetric function.

Definition 4.1

(Directed Laplacian
[
Yos16
]
)
.

The
directed Laplacian operator
of
G
is the operator
\mathscr{L}^{-}=\mathscr{L}^{-}_{G}:L^{2}(V)\to L^{2}(V)
given by

\mathscr{L}^{-}f\vcentcolon=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{u,v\in V}w(u,v)\left(f(u)-f(v)\right)^{+}(e_{v}-e_{u})

(15)

for each
f\in L^{2}(V)
.

Given
f\in L^{2}(V)
, we say an edge
(u,v)
is
f
-monotone
if
f(u)\leq f(v)
, and we say
it is
f
-antimonotone
if
f(u)>f(v)
. We say
f
is monotone
if every edge
(u,v)

of
G
is
f
-monotone. If we think of
f
as the distribution of “mass” over the vertices
V
and of

\mathscr{L}^{-}\bm{f}
as the rate of change of
\bm{f}=\bm{f}(t)
over time
t
, then

Definition

4.1
posits that mass flows along the
f
-antimonotone edges, from the
heavier vertex to the lighter one. When
G
is undirected, this process is the standard heat flow on

G
, and indeed
Definition

4.1
recovers the standard graph Laplacian in this case:

Observation 4.2
.

If
G
is undirected, then
\mathscr{L}^{-}_{G}
is (half of) the standard (unnormalized)
Laplacian operator
\mathscr{L}_{G}
of
G
. Indeed, we can see the action of
\mathscr{L}^{-}_{G}
on
f\in L^{2}(V)

as follows: 1) remove the
f
-monotone edges
(u,v)
from
G
; 2) view the resulting graph
G^{\prime}
as undirected; and 3) apply the standard Laplacian operator
\mathscr{L}_{G^{\prime}}
to
f
.

Remark 2
.

In spectral graph theory, one typically defines
the Laplacian operator of an undirected graph as
-\mathscr{L}_{G}
in our notation, i. e. by replacing

e_{v}-e_{u}
with
e_{u}-e_{v}
in
Definition

4.1
. Our notation follows instead the
tradition from probability theory (see e. g.
[
BGL14
,
Han14
]
), which has the advantages 1) that

\mathscr{L}^{-}
itself, rather than
-\mathscr{L}^{-}
, will be the generator of the heat semigroup – our main
object of interest; and 2) of consistency with the analytic setting, where we have the Laplacian
operator
\Delta
for smooth functions in Euclidean space.

We note that unlike the standard Laplacian operator, the operator
\mathscr{L}^{-}
is nonlinear and not self-adjoint in general. Instead, one may think of
\mathscr{L}^{-}
as a “piecewise linear” operator on
L^{2}(V)
, which is in particular Lipschitz continuous.

Lemma 4.3
.

The operator
\mathscr{L}^{-}:L^{2}(V)\to L^{2}(V)
is Lipschitz continuous.

Proof.

Since all norms are equivalent in a finite-dimensional space, it suffices to let
f,g\in L^{2}(V)
differ on a single point
z\in V
and show that, for all
u\in V
,
\left\lvert(\mathscr{L}^{-}f)(u)-(\mathscr{L}^{-}g)(u)\right\rvert\leq M\left\lvert f(z)-g(z)\right\rvert
for some constant
M>0
, which may depend on
the graph
G
but not on
f
or
g
.

By
Definition

4.1
, we have

(\mathscr{L}^{-}f)(u)=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{v\in V}\left[-w(u,v)\left(f(u)-f(v)\right)^{+}+w(v,u)\left(f(v)-f(u)\right)^{+}\right]

and

(\mathscr{L}^{-}g)(u)=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{v\in V}\left[-w(u,v)\left(g(u)-g(v)\right)^{+}+w(v,u)\left(g(v)-g(u)\right)^{+}\right]\,.

By the triangle inequality and the assumption that
f
and
g
differ only on coordinate
z
,

\displaystyle\left\lvert(\mathscr{L}^{-}f)(u)-(\mathscr{L}^{-}g)(u)\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\sum_{v,v^{\prime}\in V:z\in\{v,v^{\prime}\}}w(v,v^{\prime})\left\lvert\left(f(v)-f(v^{\prime})\right)^{+}-\left(g(v)-g(v^{\prime})\right)^{+}\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\sum_{v,v^{\prime}\in V:z\in\{v,v^{\prime}\}}w(v,v^{\prime})\left\lvert\left(f(v)-f(v^{\prime})\right)-\left(g(v)-g(v^{\prime})\right)\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\left\lvert f(z)-g(z)\right\rvert\sum_{v,v^{\prime}\in V}w(v,v^{\prime})\,.\qed

4.2
The energy functional

As in the case of the standard Laplacian operator, the directed Laplacian naturally induces an

energy functional
(or Dirichlet form). The following definition corresponds to the Rayleigh
quotient defined in
[
Yos16
]
.

Definition 4.4

(Energy functional)
.

The
directed Dirichlet energy
functional
\mathcal{E}^{-}:L^{2}(V)\to\mathbb{R}
is given by

\mathcal{E}^{-}(f)\vcentcolon=-\left\langle f,\mathscr{L}^{-}f\right\rangle\,.

The directed Dirichlet energy measures the local violations of monotonicity along edges of
G
, and
it is indeed always non-negative, as shown in the following proposition (which is similar to
Lemma 4.3 of
[
Yos16
]
for the normalized nonlinear Laplacian).

Proposition 4.5

(Energy functional measures local violations)
.

For each
f\in L^{2}(V)
, it holds that

\mathcal{E}^{-}(f)=\frac{1}{2}\underset{u\in V}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\sum_{v\in V}w(u,v)\left(\left(f(u)-f(v)\right)^{+}\right)^{2}\right]\,.

Proof.

We have

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}^{-}(f)

\displaystyle=-\left\langle f,\mathscr{L}^{-}f\right\rangle=-\left\langle f,\frac{1}{2}\sum_{u^{\prime},v\in V}w(u^{\prime},v)\left(f(u^{\prime})-f(v)\right)^{+}(e_{v}-e_{u^{\prime}})\right\rangle

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\underset{u\in V}{\mathbb{E}}\left[f(u)\left(\sum_{u^{\prime}\in V}w(u^{\prime},u)\left(f(u^{\prime})-f(u)\right)^{+}-\sum_{v\in V}w(u,v)\left(f(u)-f(v)\right)^{+}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\underset{u\in V}{\mathbb{E}}\left[f(u)\sum_{v\in V}\Big(w(v,u)\left(f(v)-f(u)\right)^{+}-w(u,v)\left(f(u)-f(v)\right)^{+}\Big)\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2|V|}\sum_{u,v\in V}w(u,v)f(u)\left(f(u)-f(v)\right)^{+}-\frac{1}{2|V|}\sum_{u,v\in V}w(v,u)f(u)\left(f(v)-f(u)\right)^{+}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\underset{u\in V}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\sum_{v\in V}w(u,v)\left(\left(f(u)-f(v)\right)^{+}\right)^{2}\right]\,.\qed

Observe that since
\mathscr{L}^{-}
is “piecewise linear”, the energy functional
\mathcal{E}^{-}
is a “piecewise quadratic” functional on
L^{2}(V)
. Furthermore, at each point
f\in L^{2}(V)
the Laplacian
\mathscr{L}^{-}f
points at the direction opposite to the gradient of
\mathcal{E}^{-}
. To formalize this viewpoint, we first need to introduce some standard definitions to clarify what we mean by the gradient of a functional on

L^{2}(V)
, such as
\mathcal{E}^{-}
. We recall the following standard definition.

Definition 4.6

(Fréchet derivative and gradient)
.

We say that
F:L^{2}(V)\to\mathbb{R}
is
Fréchet differentiable
at
f\in L^{2}(V)
if there
exists a bounded linear operator
A:L^{2}(V)\to\mathbb{R}
such that

\lim_{\|h\|_{2}\to 0}\frac{\left\lvert F(f+h)-F(f)-Ah\right\rvert}{\|h\|_{2}}=0\,.

In this case, we call
DF(f)\vcentcolon=A
the
Fréchet derivative
of
F
at
f
. Moreover,
by the Riesz representation theorem there exists a unique vector
v\in L^{2}(V)
satisfying

Ah=\left\langle h,v\right\rangle

(16)

for all
h\in L^{2}(V)
. We call
\nabla F(f)\vcentcolon=v
the
gradient
of
F
at
f
.
Finally, we say that
F
is (Fréchet)
C^{1}
if it is Fréchet differentiable at every
f\in L^{2}(V)
and the map
f\mapsto DF(f)
(from
L^{2}(V)
to the space of bounded linear operators

L^{2}(V)\to\mathbb{R}
) is continuous.

The following proposition follows from standard real analysis.

Proposition 4.7
.

Suppose
F:L^{2}(V)\to\mathbb{R}
has continuous partial derivatives with respect to the standard
basis
\{e_{u}\}_{u\in V}
, that is, for each
u\in V
the
L^{2}(V)\to\mathbb{R}
function

\partial_{u}F(f)=\lim_{t\to 0}\frac{F(f+te_{u})-F(f)}{t}

is continuous. Then
F
is (Fréchet)
C^{1}
and its gradient
\nabla F:L^{2}(V)\to L^{2}(V)
is
given by

(\nabla F(f))(u)=|V|\cdot\partial_{u}F(f)

for each
f\in L^{2}(V)
and
u\in V
.

We highlight that since the inner product on
L^{2}(V)
used in (
16
) differs from the standard inner product on the finite dimensional Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{V}
by a factor of
|V|
, the gradient of
F
also differs from the partial derivatives of
F
by a factor of
|V|
.

We are now equipped to show that
\mathcal{E}^{-}
is a convex
C^{1}
functional, and that the Laplacian

\mathscr{L}^{-}
is (half of) the negative gradient of
\mathcal{E}^{-}
.

Lemma 4.8
.

The functional
\mathcal{E}^{-}:L^{2}(V)\to[0,+\infty)
is
C^{1}
and convex.

Proof.

We define a function
g:\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
by

g(x,y)\vcentcolon=\frac{1}{2}\left((x-y)^{+}\right)^{2}\,.

(17)

Class
C^{1}
.
By
Proposition

4.5
, it suffices to show that for each
u,v\in V
, the
L^{2}(V)\to\mathbb{R}
function
f\mapsto\left(\left(f(u)-f(v)\right)^{+}\right)^{2}
is

C^{1}
. By
Proposition

4.7
, it suffices to show that this function has continuous partial
derivatives, which is equivalent to showing that
g
has continuous partial derivatives. This is
indeed the case, with
\partial_{1}g(x,y)=(x-y)^{+}
and
\partial_{2}g(x,y)=-(x-y)^{+}
.

Convexity.
By
Proposition

4.5
and recalling that the weights
w(u,v)

are non-negative, it again suffices to show that the function
g
is convex. Indeed, let
(x_{1},y_{1}),(x_{2},y_{2})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
and
\lambda\in[0,1]
. Then, using the convexity of the functions

z\mapsto z^{+}
and
z\mapsto z^{2}
along with the fact that the latter function is increasing
for
z\geq 0
, we have

\displaystyle g\left(\lambda(x_{1},y_{1})+(1-\lambda)(x_{2},y_{2})\right)=\frac{1}{2}\left[\left[(\lambda x_{1}+(1-\lambda)x_{2})-(\lambda y_{1}+(1-\lambda)y_{2})\right]^{+}\right]^{2}

\displaystyle\quad=\frac{1}{2}\left[\left[\lambda(x_{1}-y_{1})+(1-\lambda)(x_{2}-y_{2})\right]^{+}\right]^{2}\leq\frac{1}{2}\left[\lambda(x_{1}-y_{1})^{+}+(1-\lambda)(x_{2}-y_{2})^{+}\right]^{2}

\displaystyle\quad\leq\lambda g(x_{1},y_{1})+(1-\lambda)g(x_{2},y_{2})\,.\qed

Lemma 4.9
.

For any
f\in L^{2}(V)
, we have
\mathscr{L}^{-}f=-\frac{1}{2}\nabla\mathcal{E}^{-}(f)
.

Proof.

Recall that the function
g
in (
17
) has partial derivatives
\partial_{1}g(x,y)=(x-y)^{+}
and

\partial_{2}g(x,y)=-(x-y)^{+}
. Now, by
Proposition

4.5
, for each
u\in V
, the
partial derivative
\partial_{u}\mathcal{E}^{-}(f)
(in the notation of
Proposition

4.7
) is

\displaystyle\partial_{u}\mathcal{E}^{-}(f)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|V|}\sum_{v\in V}\left[w(u,v)\partial_{1}g(f(u),f(v))+w(v,u)\partial_{2}g(f(v),f(u))\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|V|}\sum_{v\in V}\left[w(u,v)\left(f(u)-f(v)\right)^{+}-w(v,u)\left(f(v)-f(u)\right)^{+}\right]\,.

Thus, by
Proposition

4.7
, the gradient
\nabla\mathcal{E}^{-}(f)
(which exists by

Lemma

4.8
) is given by

(\nabla\mathcal{E}^{-}(f))(u)=|V|\cdot\partial_{u}\mathcal{E}^{-}(f)=\sum_{v\in V}\left[w(u,v)\left(f(u)-f(v)\right)^{+}-w(v,u)\left(f(v)-f(u)\right)^{+}\right]

for each
u\in V
.
On the other hand, by
Definition

4.1
,

(\mathscr{L}^{-}f)(u)=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{v\in V}\left[-w(u,v)\left(f(u)-f(v)\right)^{+}+w(v,u)\left(f(v)-f(u)\right)^{+}\right]

for each
u\in V
, so
(\mathscr{L}^{-}f)(u)=-\frac{1}{2}(\nabla\mathcal{E}^{-}(f))(u)
as desired.
∎

4.3
Directed heat flow

As previewed in the
Section

4.1
, the directed Laplacian operator can be thought of as the
rate of mass transfer along
f
-antimonotone edges of
G
in a dynamical process. Let us make this
notion precise.

Given any
f\in L^{2}(V)
, we define the
directed heat flow
on
G
with
initial state

f
as the dynamical system given by the initial value problem (IVP)

\bm{f^{\prime}}(t)=\mathscr{L}^{-}\bm{f}(t)\quad\text{for all }t\geq 0\,,\qquad\bm{f}(0)=f\,.

(18)

Since the operator
\mathscr{L}^{-}
is Lipschitz by
Lemma

4.3
, a standard existence
and uniqueness theorem for ordinary differential equations (ODEs) implies that this IVP has a unique
solution
\bm{f}:[0,+\infty)\to L^{2}(V)
; see e. g.
[
Tes12
, Corollary 2.6]
. This can also be
shown using the theory of maximal monotone operators, as done by
[
IMTY22
]
for the heat flow on
hypergraphs, and by
[
Yos19
]
in a study that generalizes both the directed graph setting of

[
Yos16
]
and the hypergraph setting of
[
IMTY22
]
.

Moreover, the directed heat flow enjoys the following semigroup structure. Define the operator
family
\left(P_{t}\right)_{t\geq 0}
, with
P_{t}:L^{2}(V)\to L^{2}(V)
for each
t\geq 0
, as follows:
for each
f\in L^{2}(V)
, let
\bm{f}:[0,+\infty)\to L^{2}(V)
be the solution to the IVP
(
18
), and let

P_{t}f\vcentcolon=\bm{f}(t)\,.

Then it immediately follows that
P_{t}
satisfies the properties of a semigroup, namely

1.

P_{0}
is the identity operator.

2.

P_{s}P_{t}=P_{s+t}
for all
s,t\geq 0
.

3.

\lim_{t\to 0}P_{t}f=f
for all
f\in L^{2}(V)
(this follows from the differentiability
of the solution
\bm{f}(t)
).

We call
P_{t}
the
directed heat semigroup
operator. Note that
P_{t}
is a nonlinear operator.

Observation 4.10

(Monotone functions are stationary solutions)
.

If
f\in L^{2}(V)
is monotone, then
\mathscr{L}^{-}f=0
and hence
P_{t}f=f
for all
t\geq 0
.

Since monotone functions are stationary solutions to the directed heat flow (
4.10
),
while any non-monotone
f
has non-zero Laplacian
\mathscr{L}^{-}f
(see
Proposition

4.5
), it
is natural to expect that
P_{t}f
always converges to a monotone function as
t\to\infty
. This is
indeed the case, because the directed heat flow is a
gradient
system
for the
convex
energy functional
\mathcal{E}^{-}
.

Proposition 4.11

(Directed heat flow is gradient system)
.

For all
f\in L^{2}(V)
and
t\geq 0
, we have

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}P_{t}f=-\frac{1}{2}\nabla\mathcal{E}^{-}(P_{t}f)\,.

Proof.

By the definition of
P_{t}
we have
\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}P_{t}f=\mathscr{L}^{-}P_{t}f
. The claim then follows from
Lemma

4.9
.
∎

Corollary 4.12

(Convergence to monotone equilibrium)
.

For every
f\in L^{2}(V)
, there exists a (unique) monotone
f^{*}\in L^{2}(V)
such that
P_{t}f\to f^{*}
as
t\to\infty
.

Proof.

It is a standard fact that since
\mathcal{E}^{-}
is a convex differentiable function which attains its
minimum, its gradient system (which is given by
P_{t}
) converges to a minimizer
f^{*}
; see e. g.
[
AC84
, Chapter 3, Theorem 2]
. By
Proposition

4.5
, such a minimizer is
monotone.
∎

In light of
Corollary

4.12
, we may define the following limit operator.

Definition 4.13

(Monotone equilibrium)
.

We define the operator
P_{\infty}:L^{2}(V)\to L^{2}(V)
by
P_{\infty}f\vcentcolon=\lim_{t\to\infty}P_{t}f
for each
f\in L^{2}(V)
, and call
P_{\infty}f
the
monotone equilibrium
of
f
.

Remark 3
.

Since
P_{\infty}f
is monotone, it is constant in each strongly connected component of
G
.

4.4
Dynamical spectral gap

In this subsection, we associate with the directed Laplacian operator
\mathscr{L}^{-}=\mathscr{L}_{G}^{-}
a
quantity
\lambda^{-}=\lambda^{-}(G)
, the
dynamical spectral gap
of
G
, as a natural directed
generalization of the classical (undirected) case. In particular,
\lambda^{-}(G)
characterizes the
rate of energy decay in the directed heat flow as
P_{t}f
converges to its monotone equilibrium, and
this also implies a directed Poincaré inequality linking the distance between the initial and
equilibrium states to the energy of the initial state (i. e. its violations of monotonicity).

In the classical theory, the spectral gap
\lambda(G)
associated with the classical Laplacian
operator
\mathscr{L}_{G}
is

\lambda(G)=\inf_{f\neq\vec{0}:\;\mathbb{E}\left[f\right]=0}\frac{-\left\langle f,\mathscr{L}_{G}f\right\rangle}{\|f\|_{2}^{2}}\,,

(19)

which is the best constant for which the Poincaré inequality

\mathrm{Var}\left[f\right]\leq\frac{1}{\lambda(G)}\mathcal{E}(f)

holds, where the classical Dirichlet energy is
\mathcal{E}(f)=-\left\langle f,\mathscr{L}_{G}f\right\rangle
. As mentioned at the
beginning of this section, the spectral gap
also characterizes the rate of
exponential decay of the variance toward its minimum value under the heat flow. That is,
\lambda(G)
is the best constant for
which

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\mathrm{Var}\left[\widetilde{P_{t}}f\right]\leq-2\lambda(G)\mathrm{Var}\left[\widetilde{P_{t}}f\right]

(20)

holds for all
f\in L^{2}(V)
and
t\geq 0
, where
\widetilde{P_{t}}
is the undirected heat flow. It also similarly characterizes the exponential decay of
the Dirichlet energy, i. e.
\lambda(G)
is the best constant for which

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\mathcal{E}(\widetilde{P_{t}}f)\leq-2\lambda(G)\mathcal{E}(\widetilde{P_{t}}f)

(21)

holds for all
f\in L^{2}(V)
and
t\geq 0
. We refer the reader to
[
Han14
]
for a comprehensive
reference.

We wish to define a natural notion of spectral gap for the directed Laplacian
\mathscr{L}^{-}
, towards the
goal of obtaining a directed Poincaré inequality. One attempt is to take the directed analogue of
the ratio in (
19
), namely

\frac{\mathcal{E}^{-}(f)}{\|f\|_{2}^{2}}=\frac{-\left\langle f,\mathscr{L}^{-}f\right\rangle}{\|f\|_{2}^{2}}\,.

(22)

(This would essentially be the unnormalized version of the spectral gap defined by
[
Yos16
]

for the normalized nonlinear Laplacian.) However, this quantity faces the issue that any
monotone function
f
has
\mathcal{E}^{-}(f)=0
, which makes the ratio zero
5
5

5

While for strongly
connected directed graphs only constant functions are monotone, which renders the objection
void, this is not the case for general directed graphs.
​​. Even if we restricted our
attention to non-monotone
f
, in general
\|f\|_{2}^{2}
could still be made arbitrarily large while
keeping
\mathcal{E}^{-}(f)>0
bounded, by putting large values of
f(u)
that do not introduce violations
of monotonicity. Therefore (
22
) does not seem like an informative quantity
for our purposes.

Another way to see the conceptual issue is that, unlike in the classical case, in the directed
setting we cannot hope to have variance decay as in (
20
), since
the monotone equilibrium
P_{\infty}f
may have strictly positive variance. However, as our next
attempt will reveal, we
can
establish the decay of Dirichlet energy as in
(
21
), which will suffice to give a directed Poincaré inequality
for
G
.

We start by observing that, in the classical case, the ratio

\inf_{f:\mathcal{E}(f)>0}\frac{\|\mathscr{L}f\|_{2}^{2}}{\mathcal{E}(f)}

(23)

equals the smallest
non-zero
eigenvalue of the classical Laplacian
-\mathscr{L}_{G}
, so for a

connected
graph it also equals the spectral gap
\lambda(G)
. Now, its directed analogue

\inf_{f:\mathcal{E}^{-}(f)>0}\frac{\|\mathscr{L}^{-}f\|_{2}^{2}}{\mathcal{E}^{-}(f)}

(24)

seems like a reasonable candidate – intuitively, note that not only does the constraint
\mathcal{E}^{-}(f)>0
rule out monotone functions
f
, but also,
f
-monotone edges
(u,v)
with large
f(v)-f(u)

contribute nothing to the denominator, suggesting that (
24
) does not have the
same shortcoming as (
22
)
6
6

6

For example, let
G
consist of two disjoint edges
(u,v)
and
(u^{\prime},v^{\prime})
, and consider the
sequence
(f_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
with
f_{i}(u)=0
,
f_{i}(v)=i
,
f(u^{\prime})=1
, and
f(v^{\prime})=0
. Then

\mathcal{E}^{-}(f_{i})/\|f_{i}\|_{2}^{2}\to 0
as
i\to\infty
, while
\|\mathscr{L}^{-}f_{i}\|_{2}^{2}/\mathcal{E}^{-}(f_{i})
remains constant.

​​. Another way to see the intuition behind (
24
) is that, while its
classical counterpart (
23
) only differs from
(
19
) by ignoring the zero eigenvalues coming from the single
pathological case of disconnected graphs, the directed (
24
) ignores many more
uninformative situations and captures the connectivity of
G
in the appropriate, directed sense.

Therefore, we define

Definition 4.14
.

The
dynamical spectral gap
of
G
is the quantity
\lambda^{-}(G)\in[0,+\infty]
given by

\lambda^{-}(G)\vcentcolon=\inf\left\{\frac{\|\mathscr{L}^{-}f\|_{2}^{2}}{\mathcal{E}^{-}(f)}\;\;\Big|\;\;f\in L^{2}(V)\,,\,\,\mathcal{E}^{-}(f)>0\right\}\,.

The next proposition shows that every graph has a non-zero dynamical spectral gap.

Proposition 4.15
.

The dynamical spectral gap
\lambda^{-}(G)
is strictly positive, and it is a real number unless

G
consists of isolated vertices only.

Proof.

If
G
contains at least one edge
e=(u,v)
, then any function
f\in L^{2}(V)
with
f(u)=1

and
f(v)=0
has
\mathcal{E}^{-}(f)>0
, so
\lambda^{-}(G)<+\infty
.

We now show that
\lambda^{-}(G)>0
. The idea is that, for each function
f\in L^{2}(V)
, the
action of
\mathscr{L}^{-}
on
f
is the same as the action of the standard Laplacian on a subgraph of

G
(made undirected) that preserves only the
f
-antimonotone edges (as noted in

4.2
). The number of such subgraphs is at most
|V|!
, i. e. the number
of permutations induced by
f
, so
\lambda^{-}(G)
is lower bounded by the minimum non-zero
eigenvalue out of finitely many graphs.

Formally, let
n\vcentcolon=|V|
, fix any
f\in L^{2}(V)
satisfying
\mathcal{E}^{-}(f)>0
, and let
\pi:V\to[n]
be a bijection such that
f_{\pi^{-1}(1)}\leq f_{\pi^{-1}(2)}\leq\dotsm\leq f_{\pi^{-1}(n)}
. Let
G_{\pi}=(V,w_{\pi})
be the undirected, weighted graph defined as follows:
for each
u,v\in V
,

w_{\pi}(u,v)\vcentcolon=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{2}w(u,v)&\text{if }\pi(u)>\pi(v)\\
\frac{1}{2}w(v,u)&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

Let
\mathscr{L}_{\pi}\vcentcolon=\mathscr{L}_{G_{\pi}}
denote the standard Laplacian operator for
G_{\pi}
. We claim that

\mathscr{L}^{-}f=\mathscr{L}_{\pi}f
. First, by definition of the standard Laplacian, for any
z\in V
we have

(\mathscr{L}_{\pi}f)(z)=\sum_{v\in V}w_{\pi}(z,v)\left(f(v)-f(z)\right)\,.

On the other hand,
Definition

4.1
and the definition of
w_{\pi}
yield

\displaystyle(\mathscr{L}^{-}f)(z)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{u,v\in V}w(u,v)\left(f(u)-f(v)\right)^{+}(e_{v}(z)-e_{u}(z))

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{u:\pi(u)>\pi(z)}w(u,z)\left(f(u)-f(z)\right)-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{v:\pi(z)>\pi(v)}w(z,v)\left(f(z)-f(v)\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{v:\pi(v)>\pi(z)}w_{\pi}(v,z)\left(f(v)-f(z)\right)+\sum_{v:\pi(z)>\pi(v)}w_{\pi}(v,z)\left(f(v)-f(z)\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{v\in V}w_{\pi}(v,z)\left(f(v)-f(z)\right)=(\mathscr{L}_{\pi}f)(z)\,,

so indeed
\mathscr{L}^{-}f=\mathscr{L}_{\pi}f
. Let
\lambda_{\pi}
denote the smallest non-zero eigenvalue of

-\mathscr{L}_{\pi}
, which exists because
G_{\pi}
contains at least one edge since
f
is not monotone. Then

\frac{\|\mathscr{L}^{-}f\|_{2}^{2}}{\mathcal{E}^{-}(f)}=\frac{\|\mathscr{L}_{\pi}f\|_{2}^{2}}{-\left\langle f,\mathscr{L}^{-}f\right\rangle}=\frac{\|\mathscr{L}_{\pi}f\|_{2}^{2}}{\left\langle f,-\mathscr{L}_{\pi}f\right\rangle}\geq\lambda_{\pi}\,.

Let
\lambda^{*}\vcentcolon=\min_{\pi}\lambda_{\pi}
, where
\pi
ranges over all bijections from
V
to
[n]

for which
-\mathscr{L}_{\pi}
has a non-zero eigenvalue. Then
\lambda^{*}
is strictly positive, and for each

g\in L^{2}(V)
with
\mathcal{E}^{-}(g)>0
, we have

\frac{\|\mathscr{L}^{-}g\|_{2}^{2}}{\mathcal{E}^{-}(g)}\geq\lambda^{*}\,.

Thus
\lambda^{-}(G)>0
as claimed.
∎

Next, we observe that
\lambda^{-}(G)
characterizes the rate of exponential decay of
\mathcal{E}^{-}(P_{t}f)
.
The key fact is that, in a gradient system, the rate of energy decay is governed by the rate of
change of the state.

Proposition 4.16
.

Let
f\in L^{2}(V)
. Then the function
t\mapsto\mathcal{E}^{-}(P_{t}f)
is differentiable and, for all

t\geq 0
,

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\mathcal{E}^{-}(P_{t}f)=-2\left\|\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}P_{t}f\right\|_{2}^{2}\,.

Proof.

By the chain rule (for the Fréchet derivative) and
Proposition

4.11
,

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\mathcal{E}^{-}(P_{t}f)=\left\langle\nabla\mathcal{E}^{-}(P_{t}f),\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}P_{t}f\right\rangle=\left\langle-2\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}P_{t}f,\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}P_{t}f\right\rangle=-2\left\|\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}P_{t}f\right\|_{2}^{2}\,.\qed

Corollary 4.17
.

Let
f\in L^{2}(V)
. Then the function
t\mapsto\mathcal{E}^{-}(P_{t}f)
satisfies, for all
t\geq 0
,

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\mathcal{E}^{-}(P_{t}f)\leq-2\lambda^{-}(G)\cdot\mathcal{E}^{-}(P_{t}f)\,.

(25)

Furthermore,
\lambda^{-}(G)
is the largest constant for which
(
25
) holds for all
f,t
.

Proof.

By
Proposition

4.16
,

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\mathcal{E}^{-}(P_{t}f)=-2\left\|\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}P_{t}f\right\|_{2}^{2}=-2\|\mathscr{L}^{-}P_{t}f\|_{2}^{2}\,.

By
Definition

4.14
,
\lambda^{-}(G)\leq\|\mathscr{L}^{-}P_{t}f\|_{2}^{2}/\mathcal{E}^{-}(P_{t}f)
, whence (
25
) follows.
Conversely, for every
\varepsilon>0
, by definition of infimum we can find
g\in L^{2}(V)
such
that
\|\mathscr{L}^{-}g\|_{2}^{2}/\mathcal{E}^{-}(g)<\lambda^{-}(G)+\varepsilon
, in which case we
have (recalling that
P_{0}g=g

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\mathcal{E}^{-}(P_{0}g)=-2\left\|\mathscr{L}^{-}P_{0}g\right\|_{2}^{2}>-2\left(\lambda^{-}(G)+\varepsilon\right)\mathcal{E}^{-}(P_{0}g)\,,

so the constant
\lambda^{-}(G)
is indeed tight.
∎

We can now show that the dynamical spectral gap mediates an upper bound on the distance from the

Theorem 4.18

(Directed Poincaré inequality)

For all

\|f-P_{\infty}f\|_{2}^{2}\leq\frac{1}{\lambda^{-}(G)}\,\mathcal{E}^{-}(f)\,.

Proof.

Since

satisfies the
claimed upper bound for all

\|f-P_{t}f\|_{2}=\left\|\int_{0}^{t}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}P_{t}f\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t\right\|_{2}\leq\int_{0}^{t}\left\|\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}P_{t}f\right\|_{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t=\int_{0}^{t}\sqrt{-\frac{1}{2}\cdot\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\mathcal{E}^{-}(P_{t}f)}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t\,.

We now appeal to the exponential rate of decay of

via a straightforward calculus lemma stated below (

\|f-P_{t}f\|_{2}\leq\sqrt{\frac{1}{2}}\cdot\frac{2}{\sqrt{2\lambda^{-}(G)}}\sqrt{\mathcal{E}^{-}(P_{0}f)}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\lambda^{-}(G)}}\sqrt{\mathcal{E}^{-}(f)}\,.\qed

We used the following lemma, whose proof we defer to

Let

The dynamical spectral gap of the directed hypercube

Lower bound.

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}\underset{x\in\{0,1\}^{n}}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left((f(x^{i\to 1})-f(x^{i\to 0})^{-})\right)^{2}\right]=\mathcal{E}^{(i)}(f)\,.

(26)

\mu(A)^{-1/2}\cdot\mathsf{dist}_{2}^{\mathsf{mono}}(T[f])\leq\tau(f,g).

We are now ready to prove
Theorem

1.14
.

Proof of
Theorem

1.14
.

We divide into two cases.

Case 1:

\delta(A)=0
. We pick an arbitrary monotone increasing function
g:A\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, and we claim that
n\cdot{\mathrm{Var}}_{A}\left[g\right]\geq\mathcal{E}_{A}(g)
, certifying
Theorem

1.14
. In fact, we have

\displaystyle\quad\mathcal{E}_{A}(g)

\displaystyle=\mu(A)^{-1}\cdot\mathcal{E}^{-}(T[g])+\mu(A)^{-1}\cdot\mathcal{E}^{-}(T[-g])

(by
Proposition

3.2
)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{n}{\mu(A)}\cdot\mathsf{dist}_{2}^{\mathsf{mono}}(T[g])^{2}+\frac{n}{\mu(A)}\cdot\mathsf{dist}_{2}^{\mathsf{mono}}(T[-g])^{2}

(by
Corollary

6.2
)

\displaystyle\leq n\cdot\tau(g,g)^{2}+n\cdot\tau(-g,g)^{2}

(by
Corollary

6.4
)

\displaystyle=0+n\cdot{\mathrm{Var}}_{A}\left[g\right]=n\cdot{\mathrm{Var}}_{A}\left[g\right]

(by
Proposition

3.7
).

(27)

Case 2:

\delta(A)<0
. Since the set of all monotone increasing functions
g:A\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
such that
{\mathrm{Var}}_{A}\left[g\right]=1
is compact in the Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{A}
, there exist functions
g,h
in this set such that
\mathrm{Cov}_{A}\left[g,h\right]
attains its minimum value, subject to
g
and
h
being monotone increasing and having variance 1. By
Definition

1.11
, it is easily seen that this minimum value equals
\delta(A)
.

We then consider the function
g-h
. Since
\mathrm{Cov}_{A}\left[g,h\right]=\delta(A)<0
, it is easy to see that
g-h
is a non-constant function. We claim that the function
g-h
certifies
Theorem

1.14
, i.e.

(1+\delta(A))\cdot n\cdot{\mathrm{Var}}_{A}\left[g-h\right]\geq\mathcal{E}_{A}(g-h).

(28)

In fact, we have

\displaystyle\quad\mathcal{E}_{A}(g-h)=\mu(A)^{-1}\cdot\mathcal{E}^{-}(T[g-h])+\mu(A)^{-1}\cdot\mathcal{E}^{-}(T[h-g])

(by
Proposition

3.2
)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{n}{\mu(A)}\cdot\mathsf{dist}_{2}^{\mathsf{mono}}(T[g-h])^{2}+\frac{n}{\mu(A)}\cdot\mathsf{dist}_{2}^{\mathsf{mono}}(T[h-g])^{2}

(by
Corollary

6.2
)

\displaystyle\leq n\cdot\tau(g-h,g)^{2}+n\cdot\tau(h-g,h)^{2}

(by
Corollary

6.4
)

\displaystyle=n\cdot\left(2-\max\{0,\rho(g-h,g)\}^{2}-\max\{0,\rho(h-g,h)\}^{2}\right)\cdot{\mathrm{Var}}_{A}\left[g-h\right]

(by
Proposition

3.7
).

(29)

We then calculate
\rho(g-h,g)
by
Definition

3.4
. Since
{\mathrm{Var}}_{A}\left[g\right]={\mathrm{Var}}_{A}\left[h\right]=1
and
\mathrm{Cov}_{A}\left[g,h\right]=\delta(A)
, we have

\rho(g-h,h)=\frac{\mathrm{Cov}_{A}\left[g-h,g\right]}{\sqrt{{\mathrm{Var}}_{A}\left[g-h\right]}}=\frac{1-\delta(A)}{\sqrt{2-2\delta(A)}}=\sqrt{\frac{1-\delta(A)}{2}}\geq 0.

Similarly, we have
\rho(h-g)=\sqrt{(1-\delta(A))/2}\geq 0
. Plugging the values of
\rho(g-h,g)
and
\rho(h-g,h)
into (
29
) yields the desired inequality (
28
).
∎
