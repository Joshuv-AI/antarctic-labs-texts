A gut vascular barrier damage-based scoring system predicts postoperative outcomes in Crohn’s disease
Authors: Xiao-jun Chen, Yi Guo, Hui Peng, Hu-fei Zhang, Yi-nan Zhang, Zi-Meng Liu, Xu-Yu Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe gut vascular barrier (GVB) is essential for maintaining intestinal homeostasis. However, its role in patients undergoing surgery for Crohn’s disease (CD) remains unclear. Thus, our study aimed to explore the association between GVB and disease severity and postoperative outcomes in CD patients, as well as to develop a scoring system to predict postoperative prognosis.MethodsEndothelial plasmalemma vesicle–associated protein (PV-1) is a typical biomarker of GVB dysfunction. The expression of PV-1 was measured in intestinal mucosa of patients with Crohn’s disease (CD) and controls scheduled for surgery. The correlation between PV-1 expression and clinical data was analyzed. Moreover, a scoring system based on PV-1 was developed to predict the postoperative outcomes.ResultsGVB damage was detected in CD patients. Levels of intestinal PV-1 expression were markedly higher in inflamed parts of CD patients than in non-inflamed parts and in control patients (both P < 0.05). Levels of PV-1 were positively correlated with preoperative disease activity indexes and postoperative clinical outcomes. Importantly, a new scoring system based on PV-1 was established to predict an adverse total postoperative prognosis or a longer length of stay, and it showed better discrimination (Areas under the receiver operating characteristic curves (AUCs) = 0.808, 0.902, P < 0.001).ConclusionGVB damage was found in CD patients and was associated with the severity of disease and postoperative outcomes. A scoring system based on GVB damage is promising in predicting poor postoperative outcomes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12876-026-04892-5.Keywords: Ileocolon resection, Gut vascular barrier, Crohn’s disease, Postoperative outcomes

Introduction

Crohn’s disease (CD), the main type of inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), is a chronic inflammatory disease that affects all parts of the gastrointestinal tract [1]. It is more prevalent in industrialized countries, particularly in North America and Western Europe, with an estimated incidence of 6.3–23.8 and prevalence of 96.3-318.5 per 100 000 people in North America. It appears to have increased steadily worldwide [2, 3]. The development of CD involves a mucosal immune reaction and intestinal barrier dysfunction, resulting in long-term gut inflammation [1, 4]. The intestinal tract is often damaged due to its relapsing and remitting lesions and transmural inflammation. The majority of people present with an inflammatory phenotype at diagnosis, however, if they fail to respond medical treatment or progress to complicated disease phenotypes, half of them will develop complications (e.g., bowel obstruction, abscesses, fistulas), which often require surgical intervention [2–4]. In Europe, it is estimated that 37–61% of patients will require surgery within 10 years of diagnosis, though these rates have declined partly in due to the use of anti-TNF agents [5–7].

Unfortunately, surgery is not curative, and clinical recurrence occurs in about 50% of patients [4]. Up to 20% of patients require reoperation 5–10 years after the diagnosis. It should be noted that the costs for CD are higher than ulcerative colitis(UC) and surgical fees account for the highest of hospitalization costs [5, 8]. Previous studies have shown that, compared with the American College of Surgeons National Surgical Quality Improvement Program surgical risk calculator (NSQIP-SRC), disease-specific severity scores (Harvey-Bradshaw Index, HBI) are more strongly associated with surgical complications in CD patients. However, the area under the curve (AUC) was low (0.593 for NSQIP-SRC and 0.665 for HBI) on the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve, indicating poor predictive accuracy [9]. Thus, it is necessary to establish more useful tools to help clinicians assess surgical complication risk and make shared decisions.

The gut barriers are essential for regulating intestinal homeostasis. The gut vascular barrier (GVB) is an important anatomical barrier that lies beyond the epithelial barrier in the intestines of both mice and humans. It plays a central role in limiting the toxins in the intestinal lumen from reaching the liver and the systemic circulation [10–13]. Endothelial plasmalemma vesicle-associated protein-1(PV-1), a component of intestinal vascular endothelium, correlates with impaired GVB function and acts as a specific biomarker for GVB disruption [14, 15]. Previous studies have shown that GVB dysfunction is associated with several pathological conditions, including ankylosing spondylitis, liver metastases of colorectal cancer, celiac disease and inflammatory bowel disease [11, 16–18]. Furthermore, increased expression of microvascular adhesion molecules and impaired mucosal immunity have been observed in patients with CD [19, 20]. However, the role of GVB in CD severity remains unclear. Based on the aforementioned evidence, we hypothesized that CD may disrupt GVB integrity. Meanwhile, mucosal PV-1 may be a promising biomarker for determining the severity of disease and predicting postoperative prognosis in CD-related surgery.

Taken together, the objective of the current study is to determine whether GVB damage is present in CD patients. Additionally, we aimed to analyze the correlation between PV-1, an indicator of GVB dysfunction, and disease severity, perioperative prognosis, as well as its ability to predict postoperative outcomes.

Methods

Subjects selection

We conducted a prospective observational study enrolling patients diagnosed with CD and bowel cancer who needed surgery at our hospital from October 2021 to July 2023. CD was determined on the basis of conventional clinical, endoscopic, radiological and histological criteria [21]. Patients diagnosed with CD who were scheduled for enterectomy were included. Control subjects were selected from individuals who underwent right hemicolectomy or ileal resection operations. The exclusion criteria were as follows: patients over 75 years or under 18 years of age; those with other gastrointestinal tumors or autoimmune disease; those with only colonic lesions removed during surgery; those with missing computed tomography reports 90 days before surgery; those with history of multiple chemotherapy treatments before surgery; those with important organ insufficiency, as well as those who refused to participate in the study or cooperate.

Human sample collection

Specimens of macroscopically inflamed tissue (lesional, L) and non-inflamed (non-lesional, NL) tissue from the ileum were collected intraoperatively from patients with CD who required surgery due to complications. As controls, we obtained specimens of healthy ileal tissue (HC) from the margins of patients undergoing surgery for intestinal cancer (Figure S1). All biopsies were divided into two parts: one was embedded in optimal cutting temperature compound (OCT) and stored at -80℃ for subsequent immunofluorescence staining, and the other was used for mRNA extraction and reverse transcription quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR).

RNA extraction and qRT-PCR

RNA was isolated from human intestinal biopsies using a Universal MicroRNA Purification Kit (EZBioscience, USA). The RNA was then transcribed into cDNA using a Reverse Transcription Kit (EZBioscience, USA). Quantitative real-time PCR was performed using SYBR Green Master Mix (EZBioscience, USA) on a LightCycler 480 II (Roche, Switzerland). The primer sequences used in this study are listed below:

PV1: 5’CTCTTCATGGTCTATGGCAACG3’ and 5’GCGAGCATTCAGCCACATC3’; β-actin: 5’CATGTACGTTGCTATCAGGC3’ and 5’CTCCTTAATGTCACGCACGAT3’. Relative mRNA quantification was quantified using the 2^(−ΔΔCT) method.

Immunofluorescence imaging

The intestines were embedded in an optimal cutting temperature compound (OCT) and stored at -80 °C. Cryosections were blocked by incubating them in 1% BSA for 30 min. The sections were then incubated with the primary antibodies of target at 4 °C overnight, washed three times with PBS, and incubated with fluorochrome-labeled secondary Antibodies for 2 h at room temperature. The sections were washed three times with PBS. The following primary antibodies were used: anti-human PV-1 (1:100, NB100-77668, Novus Biologicals, CO, US) and anti-human CD34 (1:100, ab81289, Abcam, MA, US). Nuclei were counterstained with 4’, 6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI). The sections were then covered and observed under a laser scanning confocal microscope. ImageJ software was used for image analysis and fluorescence quantification.

Clinical data collection

Demographic data and CD-relevant information were recorded. Disease location and behavior were recorded according to the Montreal classification. The Crohn’s Disease Activity index (CDAI) and the Harvey-Bradshaw Index (HBI) were used to evaluate disease activity. According to the criteria, CDAI < 150 defined as remission, 150–219 as mild activity, 220–450 as moderate activity, and > 450 as severe activity [22]. As for HBI score, 0–3 suggested remission, 4 with mild activity, 5–8 with mild activity and ≥ 9 with severe activity [23]. Besides, we collected preoperative and postoperative serum inflammatory indexes, including C-reactive protein (CRP), erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR), α-antitrypsin (AAT), and platelet (PLT) counts, as well as nutritional indicators, including hemoglobin (Hb), serum albumin (ALB, and prealbumin (PA). Furthermore, we summarized indicators of short-term postoperative outcomes, including the prognostic nutritional index (PNI = 10×ALB (g/dL) + 0.005 × total peripheral lymphocyte count (TLC) (per mL)), total hospital stay, postoperative hospital stay, days of postoperative intravenous antibiotic use, days of fever, maximum fever temperature, grade of antibiotics, and postoperative complications(e.g., gastrointestinal bleeding, wound infection or disruption, anastomotic fistula, pneumonia, bacteremia, and sepsis shock) [24–26], and ICU admission after surgery.

Development of the scoring system

To define the role of PV-1 in clinical outcomes after CD-related surgery, poor overall outcomes were defined as follows: a postoperative length of stay (LOS) longer than the median LOS, a greater than median number of days of postoperative intravenous antibiotic use, a body temperature higher than 39℃, the use of special-grade antibiotics, admission to the intensive care unit (ICU) or the development of any postoperative complications mentioned above [9, 24]. A longer postoperative hospital stay was defined as> the median of LOS after surgery for CD [27]. A higher risk of infection was defined as follows: postoperative intravenous antibiotic use longer than the median, body temperature higher than 39℃, use of special-grade antibiotics or development of an infectious complication [24] (e.g., wound infection, extra-abdominal infection, or sepsis shock). The CD population was then divided into three types of two groups according to the above definitions: a normal LOS group and a longer LOS group; a low infectious group and a high infectious group; and a favorable total prognosis group and a poor total prognosis group. Univariate and multivariate analyses were performed to identify statistically significant variables, and logistic regression coefficients for these variables were converted into points. Then a scoring system was established to predict postoperative outcomes.

Statistical analysis

All analyses were performed using the SPSS Statistics 25.0. Continuous variables with a normal distribution were presented as the mean±standard deviation (SD), and non-normal variables were reported as the median (interquartile range). Categorical data were presented as n (%). Differences in continuous data among groups were analyzed by using the Student’s t-tests or one-way ANOVA test if the values followed a normal distribution. Otherwise, Mann-Whitney U-tests or Kruskal-Wallis tests were used. Categorical variables were analyzed using the chi-square test. Spearman’s rank correlation test was used for correlation analysis. Logistic regression analysis was conducted to investigate the association between intestinal PV-1 expression and clinical disease severity or postoperative length of stay. The multivariate logistic regression coefficients for each factor in the final model were converted into a score. Weighted points were calculated by dividing each regression coefficient by the smallest regression coefficient among the variables. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves and the areas under the ROC curves (AUCs) were analyzed to evaluate predictive potential for postoperative outcomes. All graphs were drawn using GraphPad software 9.0. A two-sided p value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Demographic characteristics of CD and Control subjects

A total of 158 surgeries were included in the study. 49 patients were excluded due to exclusion criteria, and 56 CD patients and 53 control patients who required intestinal surgeries were enrolled. 10 patients in CD group and 6 control patients with missing data were excluded from this study. Ultimately, the analysis included forty-six CD patients and forty-seven control individuals. The standard flow diagram of the trial is displayed in Fig. 1. There were no significant differences in sex, PLT, PA between the CD and control groups (P > 0.05). The PV-1 expression in intestinal mucosa is increased in CD patients

As shown in Fig. 2(A), PV-1 expression in the ileum of CD patients and control subjects was examined by qRT-PCR. PV-1 mRNA levels were markedly elevated in inflamed sites of CD patients than in controls (P < 0.01). Additionally, we found that PV-1 mRNA levels were significantly higher in macroscopically inflamed tissues than in non-inflamed tissues of CD samples (P < 0.05). We also performed an immunofluorescence analysis to compare PV-1 expression. The intensity of PV-1 protein expression in inflamed tissues of CD specimens was significantly higher than in the other two groups (Fig. 2B, quantified in Fig. 2C). However, there was no significant difference in PV-1 expression between the NL and control groups in the above experiments (all P > 0.05).

Correlation between PV-1 and preoperative disease activity in CD patients

Since CDAI and HBI reflect the severity of clinical disease, we first examined their correlation with gut PV-1 levels. As shown in Fig. 3, the results revealed a stronger positive correlation between intestinal PV-1 expression and HBI (r = 0.498, P < 0.0001) and CDAI (r = 0.476, P = 0.001). Additionally, PV-1 was also positively correlated with CRP (r = 0.346, P = 0.019), AAT (r = 0.321, P = 0.029), and negatively correlated with Hb (r =-0.293, P = 0.048) and PA (r =-0.324, P = 0.028). Surprisingly, ESR (r = 0.175, P = 0.760) and ALB (r=-0.275, P = 0.065) were not significantly associated with PV-1 in this study.

The correlation of PV-1 mRNA levels was assessed with the following: (A) CDAI, (B) HBI, (C) C-reactive protein (CRP), (D) α-antitrypsin (AAT), (E)hemoglobin (Hb), and (F)prealbumin (PA). The correlations between these indexes were assessed by Spearman’s rank correlation test.

Next, we compared several baseline metrics, including age, gender, BMI, location, behavior and PV-1 expression between the “remission/ mild” group and “moderate/ severe” group using univariate difference analysis (Table S2). Parameters with significant differences between the groups were applied to a multivariate logistic regression analysis. The results suggested that, after adjusting for significant influencing and confounding variables, location in ileocolon (OR = 9.34, 95% CI 1.11–78.22, P = 0.035) and intestinal PV-1 expression (OR = 6.93, 95% CI 1.55–30.90, P = 0.011) remained independent risk factors for moderate or severe activity (Table S3).

Correlation between PV-1 and clinical outcomes

To further study the postoperative effects of PV-1 on patients with CD, we compared indicators of clinical outcomes between the CD and control groups. We found significant statistical differences in the grade of antibiotics, total hospital stay, postoperative hospital stay, and days of postoperative intravenous antibiotic use between groups (all P < 0.05, Table S4). However, there were few differences in CRP, WBC, neutrophil percent, days of fever, postoperative complications and ICU admission after surgery. Then, we performed a correlation analysis of clinical prognostic indicators. Figure 4 shows positive correlations between PV-1 levels and postoperative hospital stay (r = 0.341, P = 0.02), days of postoperative intravenous antibiotic use (r = 0.389, P = 0.007) and maximum temperature of fever (r = 0.482, P = 0.023). However, there were no significant associations between PV-1 levels and total hospital stay or PNI (all P > 0.05).

Predictive potential of PV-1 in clinical outcomes after CD-related surgery

Based on evidence of moderate relationships between PV-1 mRNA and postoperative outcomes (Spearman rho 0.3–0.5), we constructed a new scoring system to predict longer postoperative LOS. The median postoperative LOS was 10 days (IQR 8–13 days). Thus, we defined a longer LOS as greater than 10 days and divided the patients into two groups: those with a LOS of 10 days or less and those with an LOS greater than 10 days. First, we performed a univariate analysis to identify the underlying risk factors of poor prognosis. The results showed that PV-1 expression, ALB levels ≤ 30 g/L and a higher ASA classification significantly affected the length of hospital stay (all P < 0.05, Table S5).

Then, we used the three statistically significant variables for multivariate regression analysis to develop a scoring system that predicts longer LOS (Table 2). The Hosmer–Lemeshow test indicated that the model fit was good (χ2 = 4.35, P = 0.738). PV-1 expression was not statistically significant in the multivariate analysis. However, it was included in the scoring system because incorporating it into the final regression model improved the performance (AUC 0.902 vs. 0.845, 95% CI: 0.814–0.989), achieving a sensitivity of 88.0% and a specificity of 88.5%. Compared with CDAI scores, the new scoring system displayed a greater AUC (Fig. 5a). Next, we applied this scoring system to predict total postoperative prognosis and risk of infection. For total postoperative prognosis, the model achieved an AUC of 0.808 (P < 0.001, 95% CI: 0.682–0.934) and the cutoff score, calculated by Youden’s index, was 4.5. This score had a sensitivity of 61.5% and a specificity of 95.0%. Nevertheless, the model was less effective at predicting a higher risk of infection. In this case, the model achieved an AUC of 0.696 (P < 0.05, 95% CI: 0.539–0.854) and the cut-off score was calculated with a sensitivity of 57.1% and a specificity of 84.0%. Finally, we found that the risk of a poor outcome increased with an increased score (Fig. 5).

Discussion

The intestine is exposed to a variety of foods and microorganisms and plays an indispensable role in absorbing nutrients and maintaining homeostasis, which depends on the morphological and functional integrity of the gut barrier [10]. In 2015, the gut vascular barrier (GVB) was proposed as the final barrier layer [28]. An increase in PV-1 is one of the factors that promote GVB dysfunction [29]. Many studies suggest the protective effects of the GVB and endothelium, but these effects are still controversial in Crohn’s disease [12]. Recently, bacterial translocation associated with GVB impairment has been found in human studies of ulcerative colitis (UC) and in DSS-colitis animal models, promoting the accumulation of inflammatory cells in distal organs such as the liver and brain [30]. Our study demonstrated that PV-1 levels were elevated in the intestinal tissues of CD patients compared to control subjects, especially in the inflamed mucosa of the gut (Fig. 2). We also found that PV-1 expression positively correlates with clinical disease activity (CDAI and HBI scores and CRP) and the degree of inflammation but negatively correlates with Hb and prealbumin levels (Fig. 3). In general, these conclusions may suggest that GVB disruption occurs in the intestines of CD patients, leading to larger substances entering the bloodstream and triggering an immune response.

It has been reported that PV-1 can be a biomarker for a poor prognosis in patients with colorectal cancer, including recurrence and liver metastases [17]. Our results displayed that high PV-1 mRNA levels may predict a poor perioperative prognosis (Fig. 4). Additionally, we found that levels of PV-1 mRNA, ALB and ASA classification were associated with prolonged LOS in the univariate analysis (Table S5). Although PV-1 expression was not identified as a statistically significant result in the multivariate analysis, incorporating it into the scoring system showed improved differentiation performance (AUC = 0.902 vs. 0.845). This is the first prospective study to attempt to estimate the association between GVB dysfunction and postoperative outcomes. In line with previous studies, a high ASA classification has been strongly associated with postoperative complications and is commonly used to predict LOS in CD-related surgery [31, 32]. Patients with a higher ASA grade may have a severe intestinal penetrating lesion and a poorer preoperative physiological condition. Additionally, nutritional indexes such as low ALB have also been found to be associated with postoperative infectious complications [33, 34]. Malnutrition may affect abdominal wound recovery and increase the risk of infection. In our cohort, however, none of the CD patients had used steroids before surgery, and only one patient had undergone strictureplasty. This makes it difficult to assess the impact of these factors on postoperative outcomes.

Most research has focused on the role of the National Surgical Quality Improvement Program surgical risk calculator (NSQIP-SRC) in predicting surgical risks, but it does not precisely predict risks associated with CD-related surgery [9, 35]. Compared with the previous model [31], this scoring system more effectively differentiates patients with poor prognoses, including those with longer LOS and poorer total postoperative outcomes after CD-related surgery (Fig. 5). Several factors, including Albumin levels, PV-1 expression, and ASA classification, have been correlated with these outcomes. We also uncovered that the risk of an adverse postoperative prognosis increases with higher scores according to the scoring system (Fig. 5).

The primary strength of this study lies in its pioneering assessment of the association between PV-1 and disease characteristics and short-term surgical outcomes in CD patients. Although it can be challenging to obtain specimens preoperatively, intestinal mucosal tissue samples can be collected during preoperative gastrointestinal endoscopy to better assess postoperative risks prior to surgery. Additionally, the proposed model is a preliminary finding. In the future, our team will evaluate serum PV-1 levels in CD patients or explore non-invasive biomarkers of GVB damage to improve the model’s clinical applicability.

Our study has several limitations. First, the CD group and the control group were not matched for age and BMI, which may impact our conclusions. However, we selected this group as controls because we needed to account for the variable of surgical intervention. There were relatively few patients with other gastrointestinal diseases that required surgery, such as intussusception. To minimize confounding factors, we compared non-inflamed intestinal tissue from CD patients with relatively normal tissue from cancer patients and found no statistically significant differences. Furthermore, multivariate logistic regression analysis revealed that age and BMI were not critical confounding factors influencing disease activity. Rather, PV-1 levels were precisely correlated with disease severity (Table S2). Second, the small patient sample size and the observational study at a single institution are inadequate to draw absolute conclusions. Furthermore, we will investigate the specific mechanisms of GVB dysfunction through animal experimentation and evaluate the long-term outcomes of patients. To improve prediction ability, we will also include larger cohorts of patients undergoing CD surgery and develop a validation cohort to confirm the effectiveness of the scoring system.

Conclusion

Our findings suggest that intestinal levels of PV-1 are significantly elevated in CD patients and are positively correlated with disease severity and clinical outcomes, providing new therapeutic targets for CD treatment. These findings may provide new therapeutic targets for CD treatment. Besides, a PV-1-based scoring system can differentiate postoperative outcomes with a higher specificity. Therefore, our study sheds light on measuring PV-1 mRNA in the prediction of poor postoperative prognosis and highlights the need for prospective assessment of GVB in the postoperative management of CD patients.
