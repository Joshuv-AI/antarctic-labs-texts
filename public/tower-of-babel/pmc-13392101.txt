Prevalence and carrier frequency of Canavan disease in a South Indian community with implications for research and public health
Authors: Rekha Aaron, Aaron Chapla, M Jayaprabha, Janane Elangovan, Roopa Kunthavai Chellappa, Asha Krishnamurthy, Ramya Raghavan, Mrinalini Peters, Maya Thomas, Sumita Danda
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCanavan disease (CD) is a progressive leukodystrophy characterized by the spongy degeneration of white matter in the brain. Only three reports from India have been elucidated the ASPA gene mutation in patients diagnosed with CD. At our center, two genetically confirmed cases of juvenile Canavan disease have been identified. Both patients possess a novel genetic variant in the ASPA gene, c.526G > A (p. Gly176Ser), which has been classified as pathogenic. A preliminary study conducted at our centre suggested the presence of a founder effect within this population. Hence, the current study aimed to analyse the cross-sectional prevalence and carrier frequency of the disease and confirm the founder effect. Five hundred seventy-five blood samples were collected from individuals in this community after obtaining informed, written consent. The ASPA gene, specifically exon 3, was analysed utilizing an in-house developed, cost-effective allele-specific PCR approach in all samples. Three short tandem repeat (STR) markers—D17S1828, D17S919, and D17S1298—were employed to examine the founder effect of the mutation by fragment analysis. The carrier frequency in our study population was found to be 1 in 23, higher than that reported in the Jewish population. The allele-specific PCR technique proved to be sensitive and cost-effective for mass screening. All identified carriers displayed specific variations in the 23 heterozygous allelic repeats. At the same time, the affected individuals manifested homozygous alleles for the D17S1828 marker, confirming that this variant is indigenous to the population under study. The D17S919 marker (18/19 repeats) was absent in the control cohort but present in the community under investigation, further substantiating the founder effect. Furthermore, the study identified other potential genetic disorders in this community that warrant further investigation. This study underscores the critical importance of carrier screening in communities characterized by high consanguinity to mitigate the prevalence of recessive disorders in future generations. Introduction

Canavan disease [CD] or Aspartoacylase deficiency is a progressive neurodegenerative disorder, inherited as an autosomal recessive disorder linked with a gene locus ASPA on chromosome 17, which encodes the aspartoacylase enzyme. This zinc carboxypeptidase converts N-acetyl-L-aspartic acid [NAA] to aspartate and acetate. The absence of this enzyme results in the accumulation of NAA in the brain. ASPA is primarily found in oligodendrocyte progenitor cells and oligodendrocytes in the brain, with smaller amounts also detected in microglia and the brainstem. A recent study by Wei et al., has shown that the pathogenesis of CD is not solely because of NAA accumulation but also through other NAA-associated signaling mechanisms1. The severity of clinical presentation depends on the degree of ASPA residual activity. Genetic testing for pathogenic variants in the ASPA gene is crucial for a definitive diagnosis of Canavan disease (CD). Over 50 mutations have been discovered in the human ASPA gene, mostly involving missense variants in the coding region. These mutations typically lead to the loss of ASPA enzymatic activity. The onset and severity of CD depend on the specific mutation present.

A clear genotype-phenotype correlation is now possible in the mildest cases with the highest residual activity of the enzyme2. NAA serves as a molecular marker and plays a vital role in maintaining the metabolic integrity of oligodendrocytes. Infantile ASPA deficiency is prevalent in Ashkenazi Jews and less common in the non-Jewish population. The overall prevalence of CD is 1 in 200,000 to 400,000 (Mendes et al. 2017), whereas the prevalence in the Ashkenazi population is around 1 in 500 to 1 in 6700. In Jewish patients with CD, two primary mutations, c.854 A > C (p.Glu285Ala) and c.693 C > A (p.Y231X), have been identified in 98% of cases3. Conversely, the most common mutation in non-Jewish patients is c.914 C > A (p.Ala305Glu) (40%–48% of CD alleles). This mutation has been associated with severe and mild Canavan disease phenotypes.

There are three clinically distinct phenotypes of CD, congenital form with severe symptoms in the first few weeks of life; the infantile/neonatal form, the most common form in which the disease is apparent by 6 months of age; and the juvenile form/mild form, in which the disease is evident by the age of 4 or 5 years. Early signs of the disease include irritability and hypotonia with poor head control. From 3 to 6 months, macrocephaly is evident with delayed head control, followed by progressive delay in achieving age-appropriate milestones. As the disease progresses, optic atrophy develops, leading to increased irritability, sleep disturbances, feeding difficulties, and seizures in affected children. The prognosis remains poor, with a high rate of mortality reported in early childhood4. However, individuals with milder forms of the disease can survive into their second decade of life and beyond.

In contrast to the infantile CD, the juvenile variant is less common and presents with a normal period in infancy or only mild delay in motor milestones. Most children with mild disease are normocephalic. They present with variable symptoms, including progressive ataxia, nystagmus, visual difficulties, and other neurological manifestations. Failing to perform an MRI of the brain with MRS or check urine NAA levels significantly increases the risk of missing a CD diagnosis in mild cases.

Only three reports from India have characterized the ASPA mutation in patients with CD5,6. Mild/Juvenile CD is less common than severe CD (Clayton et al. 2020). Previously identified two cases of Juvenile Canavan disease in the Devanga Chettiyar community near Thiruvanamalai district, Tamil Nadu have been confirmed with the presence of a novel homozygous missense variant c.526G > A (p.Gly176Ser) proximal to the donor splice site. This pathogenic variant was found to be segregating with the disease in the same community. Hence, we have aimed to study the prevalence of CD and carrier status in this closely knitted community. The community is spread across three neighboring villages. Due to inbreeding, there is a suspected higher incidence of CD in this region, with at least 22 to 24 cases exhibiting a similar phenotype over the last 15 to 20 years. However, there is no information available to definitively confirm these cases as CD. The earlier study suggested a possible high prevalence of mild or juvenile CD in this community. They identified that the founder effect for this variant may have originated from a common ancestor7. Therefore, we aimed to study the cross-sectional prevalence and carrier frequency of CD and to confirm the founder effect by screening a group of controls & cases from the same community by microsatellite analysis.

Patients & methods

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB MIN NO:12018 dated 24.04.2019) and initiated after receiving approval from the DPHPM (Director of Public Health & Preventive Medicine) and the primary health center. All experiments were performed in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations.

To screen for the specific disease-causing variant in the ASPA, we developed a cost-effective method using Allele-specific PCR. All samples underwent DNA extraction followed by variant screening using Allele-specific PCR.Genomic DNA was extracted from peripheral blood using the standard Qiagen kit method. We designed primers for the ASPA gene exon 3 (ENST00000263080.3) and also primers specific for the variant. In addition to that, we designed Allele-specific PCR (AS-PCR) primers for the ASPA novel variant c.526G > A (p.Gly176Ser) using Primer3 software. AS-PCR was first standardized in patient and healthy samples using EmeraldAmp® GT PCR Master Mix (cat no.RR310A, Takara Bio Inc, Japan), validated by Sanger sequencing following the standard protocol and the PCR products were analysed on 2% agarose gel. Further, PCR was performed in all patients, family members, and samples from the same community to confirm the zygosity of the variant. All samples were sequenced by amplifying exon 3 with a specific set of primers. Linkage analysis is typically conducted by typing short tandem repeat markers to identify the gene locus and the haplotype associated with a pathogenic variant. We used three STR markers D17S1828, D17S919 & D17S1298, which are located around 10.2 cM-14.69 cM near the ASPA gene locus, to identify the haplotype pattern to elucidate the founder effect of the mutation. PCR was standardized using fluorescent-labeled primers, followed by fragment analysis with LIZ-600 as the size standard. The repeats were calculated manually based on the peak size. We included 20 carriers,30 non carriers and 20 samples from Vellore Tamilnadu region(a total of 70 samples) for STR analysis.

The sample size was calculated, assuming a proportion p=0.5 using a confidence level of 95% (corresponding to a Z-score of 1.96) and a desired precision (margin of error) of ± 5%. Therefore, a minimum of 385 participants were required to achieve the desired precision and confidence level. Prevalence rates were calculated as the proportion of cases from total sample size. For allele frequency difference per STR locus was calculated using Chi-square test.

Results

Following multiple outreach camps and community education initiatives, a total of 575 blood samples were collected from three villages. Participation was voluntary and based on informed consent. In our study, we were unable to determine the prevalence of the disease since we did not identify any new cases. However, we found that the carrier frequency in our study population (25/575) was higher than that reported in the Jewish population (1 in 23, compared to 1 in 40 of the Jewish community).

An intriguing finding was that two affected cases were related five generations back, and none of their other family members had the disease where the inbreeding is common (Fig. 3). This highlights the likelihood of disease occurrence in a close community is rare even though the carrier frequency is high. We hypothesize that the lower prevalence of the disease may be attributed to either our inability to confirm previous cases (estimated to be 15–20 cases) or to the presence of similar phenotypes that resulted in fatalities during the second or third decades of life, with only a subpopulation being screened. Therefore, genetic screening is crucial in such indigenous communities where multiple genetic disorders exist.

For microsatellite analysis, the PCRs using fluorescently labelled primers were standardized in healthy samples and fragment analysis was done as per standard protocol. We analysed 20 heterozygous carriers, 30 non-carriers & 2 homozygous cases from the same community for three STR markers and compared the data with 20 non-carriers from another part of Tamilnadu (Vellore).

The D17S919 marker 18/19 repeats were found to be absent in the Vellore cohort confirming the founder effect. While we did not identify any new cases of CD, we were able to trace other probable genetic diseases in this community, for which the genetic causes are currently unknown and have not been evaluated at our hospital before. Targeted genetic testing confirmed that these cases were negative for the ASPA variant.

In two instances, exome sequencing revealed the genetic causes of early infantile epileptic encephalopathy due to an SCN8A gene variant in one case, and primary microcephaly related to an ASPM gene variant in another patient. This highlights the need for exome sequencing in the remaining cases to identify the possible causes of other genetic disorders which might be unique to the community.

Discussion

This is the first kind of study from India elucidating the prevalence of CD in an indigenous community. The incidence of CD is higher among the Ashkenazi Jewish population, although it has been reported in other ethnic groups with significantly lower frequencies. The definite prevalence of CD and clinical presentation in India are poorly determined. Gowda et al. reported retrospective data of 12 CD cases where six cases had mutations in the ASPA gene, the most extensive case series from India to date6. A Pilot study from India on NGS-based carrier screening for 88 diseases in 200 samples showed 0.5% Canavan disease carrier8. While international studies offer valuable epidemiological data and evidence-based screening methods, research in India is still limited, revealing significant gaps in diagnosis and public health responses. A recent study has reported variability among individuals with the same pathogenic p.G274R variant. This variability ranges from severe phenotypes, including axial hypotonia starting at three months of age, to a case of an 11-year-old with atypical mild symptoms.Another report describing three affected children from two Greek families with an unusually mild course of Canavan disease, with a compound heterozygous mutation in two individuals (Y288C; F295S) and a homozygous p.G274R variant. The above studies indicate that the p.G274R mutation might be a hot spot variant in other populations9–12.

An earlier study from our center identified two cases with the same mutation in exon 3 of the ASPA gene, and later recognized that they both belonged to the same community. To our surprise, we recently found another patient at our clinic with the same mutation from a different region of Tamilnadu. We later confirmed that this family also belongs to the same community.This suggests that this mutation may be more common than previously recognized, possibly due to families relocating to various areas, leading to an underestimation of the underestimation of true incidence.

In response to our camps and counseling, the community is currently much aware of genetic diseases and the higher probability of having affected generations if they marry within the community, hence the need for genetic screening is imperative in such an indigenous population. Through this study, we could determine the carriers and counsel them to prevent future generations from being affected by the disease.

The outcomes of this work have the potential to significantly enhance global health and well-being by addressing critical gaps in rare disease detection and care. Currently, the lack of comprehensive epidemiological data impedes effective public health planning, optimal resource allocation, and the development of targeted therapies for rare genetic disorders. This is particularly evident in low- and middle-income countries like India, where community-based studies and screening programs for rare diseases such as Canavan disease and other leukodystrophies are virtually nonexistent.

By conducting community-based genetic studies, researchers can identify population-specific variants and disease patterns, enabling the development of tailored newborn screening protocols. Early diagnosis through such programs can open pathways to timely interventions, including prenatal diagnosis, preimplantation genetic diagnosis (PGD), newborn screening, and emerging therapeutic options such as gene therapy or enzyme replacement. This proactive approach can drastically reduce the burden of disease, improve long-term outcomes, and enhance the quality of life for affected individuals and their families. Moreover, this research fosters awareness and builds genetic literacy within communities, promoting informed reproductive choices and reducing stigmatization of rare disorders.

Conclusion

This study underscores the critical role of carrier screening in communities with high consanguinity to educate future generations about autosomal recessive disorders which can impact the health and economy of a society. Although the project commenced with a focus on Canavan disease, it uncovered indications of additional genetic disorders within the community, signaling a promising avenue for further research. Notably, the carrier frequency observed was higher than that found among Ashkenazi Jews, which suggests a potential under representation of prevalence due to the scarcity of community-based studies on rare diseases like Canavan. Study highlights an exciting opportunity for enhanced community awareness and preventative measures in genetic health.
