A nomogram for suicide-specific mortality risk stratification in patients with tongue cancer: a Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER)-based study, 2004–2017
Authors: Sha-Sha Du, Hai-Ping You, Yu-Ling Zhang, Nan-Jun Chen, Chen Ren, Heng-Wen Sun, Fei Xu, Xin Hua
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAlthough suicide represents an important cause of mortality among cancer patients, prediction models specifically developed for tongue cancer patients remain unavailable. This study aimed to identify suicide-related risk factors and develop an individualized risk stratification model for tongue cancer patients. This study aimed to identify factors associated with suicide-specific mortality and develop an internally validated risk stratification model for patients with tongue cancer.MethodsPatients diagnosed with tongue cancer between 2004 and 2017 were identified from the Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) database. Eligible patients were randomly allocated to training and validation cohorts at a ratio of 7:3. Cox proportional hazards regression was used to identify potential predictors and construct a nomogram. Model discrimination was evaluated using the concordance index (C-index) and time-dependent receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves with corresponding area under the curve (AUC) values. Calibration was assessed by comparing predicted and observed suicide-specific mortality.ResultsAmong the 25,024 eligible tongue cancer patients, 96 suicide-related deaths were identified during follow-up, corresponding to a cumulative suicide mortality rate of 0.38%. Multivariate Cox regression analysis revealed that sex and age (P<0.001) were independent predictors, and race, tumor (T) stage, and income (P≤0.2) were potential significant factors for suicide in tongue cancer patients. The nomogram had a good performance for the prediction of suicide-specific mortality both in the training {C-index =0.762 [95% confidence interval (CI): 0.710–0.815]} and validation sets [C-index =0.761 (95% CI: 0.673–0.849)]. ROC curve demonstrated acceptable performance in both the training and validation sets, with 3-year AUCs of 0.784 and 0.774, 5-year AUCs of 0.762 and 0.788, and 10-year AUCs of 0.729 and 0.783, respectively. Moreover, the calibration plot indicated moderate accuracy of the nomogram in both datasets.ConclusionsOverall, this study successfully identified risk factors for suicide among tongue cancer patients and developed a nomogram to offer risk assessment to mitigate the risk of suicide in this patient population.Keywords: Tongue cancer; suicide; nomogram; risk factors; Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER)

Introduction

Oral cavity and pharynx cancer, ranking as the eighth most prevalent malignancy globally, is projected to account for approximately 58,450 new cases and 12,230 fatalities in 2024, predominantly occurs in the anterior 2/3 of the tongue (1). Previous studies have investigated suicide risk among patients with various malignancies, including head and neck cancer and other cancer types (2). However, most previous studies have focused on identifying population-level risk factors rather than developing individualized risk stratification models. Tongue cancer is a unique malignancy because impairment of speech, swallowing function, appearance, and social interaction caused by the disease and its treatment may result in substantial psychological burden. Therefore, developing a specific risk stratification model for suicide mortality among tongue cancer patients may provide additional insights into identifying vulnerable populations. Especially, oral tongue squamous cell carcinoma has a comparatively poor prognosis, attributed to the abundant lympho-vascular network and the absence of a robust barrier to impede tumor dissemination. However, the causes of death among tongue cancer patients are not solely attributable to the cancer itself but are more significantly influenced by psychological factors. The tongue plays a crucial role in functions such as speech, mastication and deglutition, and postoperative disfigurement further contributes to anxiety, depression, and other psychological issues (3). It is precisely these distinctive challenges that make tongue cancer one of the malignancies with the highest suicide rates (4).

Given the alarming global incidence of suicide, which results in approximately 800,000 deaths each year (5,6), it is imperative to acknowledge that cancer patients, including those with tongue cancer, are at an elevated risk for suicide compared with the general population (7,8). It is particularly salient to recognize that research has delved into the potential risk factors associated with suicidal ideation and behavior among various cancer types, including lung cancer (9,10), head and neck cancer (11,12), gastric cancer (13), kidney cancer (14), and bladder cancer (15).

Nevertheless, existing methods fall short in evaluating the risk of suicide, as they fail to account for the complex interplay of tumor-specific and patient-related variables on an individual basis. To bridge this gap, the development of a predictive tool that integrates these multifaceted factors is essential for a more precise estimation of suicide risk in cancer patients. One such approach could involve the application of the Cox proportional hazards model, which can be effectively conveyed through the use of nomograms for enhanced clinical utility (16). Nomograms have become a prevalent instrument for individualized prognostication of clinical outcomes (15,17,18). Designed as an intuitive visual tool, the nomogram developed in this research helps clinicians quickly identify high-suicide-risk patients during hectic clinical practice, so that timely psychological interventions can be initiated.

In this study, our objective was to identify the risk factors linked to suicidal outcomes in patients diagnosed with tongue cancer. Additionally, we endeavored to construct and validate a predictive model designed to assess the suicide risk within this specific patient cohort. We present this article in accordance with the TRIPOD reporting checklist (available at https://tcr.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/tcr-2026-1088/rc).

Methods

Patient selection and variables

All data were meticulously extracted from the Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) database, a comprehensive resource for cancer incidence and survival data in which the patients’ data were public and anonymous. Our study cohort comprised patients diagnosed with tongue cancer from 2004 to 2017, identified through the SEERStat software (version 8.4.3) utilizing the Incidence-SEER 17 Research Data. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments.

Comprehensive data for each patient were procured, including demographic details, clinicopathologic characteristics, treatment modalities, and follow-up information. The demographic variables included age at diagnosis (categorized as ≤70 or >70 years), sex (male or female), race (white, black, or other), and marital status (married or unmarried). Economic status was stratified by income levels (<55,000, 55,000–75,000, or >75,000, representing low, middle, or high income). Clinicopathologic features comprised histological grade (I, II, III, or IV), primary tumor status (T stage: T1, T2, T3, or T4), nodal involvement (N stage: N0, N1, N2, or N3), and distant metastasis status (M0 or M1), all classified in accordance with the 6th or 7th edition of the American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) staging manual. Given the constraints in converting data from the AJCC 6th edition to the 7th or 8th edition, we opted for the AJCC 6th edition [2004–2015] and 7th edition [2016–2017], given our study’s time frame [2004–2017]. Treatment data encompassed surgical intervention, chemotherapy, and radiotherapy. The primary endpoint of our study was the incidence of suicide among the study population. The reporting of this study conforms to the RECORD statement (19).

Statistical analyses

Patients were randomly assigned into training and validation cohorts at a ratio of 7:3. The training cohort was used for model development, whereas the validation cohort was used for internal validation of model performance. The baseline characteristics of these groups were evaluated using Student’s t-test for continuous variables, which were reported as mean ± standard deviation (SD). For categorical variables, comparisons were made using the Chi-squared test, with results presented as frequencies and proportions. All statistical analyses were conducted using R software (version 4.2.1). Statistical significance was defined as two-tailed P values <0.05, except for the univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses, where a more stringent threshold may be applied as deemed appropriate. In this study, we employed both univariate and multivariate Cox proportional hazards models to identify potential predictors of the suicide outcome. Utilizing a stepwise selection process, we refined our predictive model. Considering that suicide was a rare outcome, the number of observed suicide events relative to the number of regression parameters was considered when interpreting the stability of the multivariable prediction model; a more liberal threshold (P≤0.2) was employed for variable selection to minimize the risk of overlooking potential predictors. The risk scores for each predictor were derived from the multivariate Cox model and graphically represented as nomograms.

To assess the survival curves, Kaplan-Meier curves were constructed, and the differences between groups were statistically evaluated using the log-rank test. Model discrimination was evaluated using the concordance index (C-index) and time-dependent receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves with corresponding area under the curve (AUC) values. These measures were used to assess predictive discrimination rather than to define direct clinical decision thresholds.

The predictive accuracy of the nomogram in the training cohort was ascertained by comparing the predicted 3-, 5-, and 10-year suicide-specific mortality rates with the observed rates in a calibration plot. This analysis was mirrored in the validation cohort, where the predictive performance was assessed using the C-index, ROC curve, and calibration plot to ensure the model’s generalizability and reliability.

Results

Baseline characteristics

A total of 25,024 tongue patients (training cohort: 17,520 patients; validation cohort: 7,504 patients) from the database between January 1, 2004, and December 31, 2017, were eligible for inclusion. Among all eligible tongue cancer patients included in this study, 96 suicide-related deaths were identified during follow-up, corresponding to a cumulative suicide mortality rate of 0.38%. The study population was predominantly male (69.0 %, n=17,269), aged under 70 years (18,720, 74.8%), identified as white (21,679, 86.6%), married (20,427, 81.6%) and high income (12,710, 50.8%). A comparison of clinical characteristics and demographics between the training and validation cohorts revealed no significant differences (all P>0.05), as detailed in Table 1.

Univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses of suicide-specific mortality

In the training cohort, significant variables including age (P<0.001) and sex (P<0.001), and variables that met the prespecified significance threshold (P≤0.2) in the univariate Cox model, such as race, histological grade, T stage, income and surgery, were entered into the multivariate Cox regression model (Table 2). Kaplan-Meier curves for age, sex, race, T stage, and income are presented in Figure 1. Multivariate analysis identified age [≤70 vs. >70 years, hazard ratio (HR) =0.41; 95% confidence interval (CI): 0.25–0.69, P<0.001], sex (male vs. female, HR =10.45; 95% CI: 3.25–33.55, P<0.001) and race (black vs. white, HR =0.24; 95% CI: 0.03–1.72, P=0.20), T stage (T4 vs. T1, HR =1.69; 95% CI: 0.80–3.59, P=0.20), income (low vs. high, HR =1.69; 95% CI: 0.82–3.49, P=0.20 and middle vs. high, HR =1.49; 95% CI: 0.89–2.48, P=0.10) as variables that met the prespecified significance threshold (P≤0.2).

Development of a novel prognostic model based on independent indicators

We have developed an innovative prognostic model in the form of a nomogram for the individualized prediction of tongue cancer suicide-specific mortality at 3-, 5-, and 10-year intervals, based on five key indicators identified through multivariate modeling (Figure 2). Following a diagnosis, the total score for each patient can be determined by summing the scores attributed to each of the five prognostic factors. Subsequently, the nomogram can be used to estimate the probabilities of suicide-specific mortality at the specified time points by aligning the calculated total score with the corresponding survival rate scale. This approach provides a personalized assessment tool for clinicians to predict suicide risk over time.

Validations of predicting nomograms

We compared C-index and ROC curves to assess the accuracy of predicted suicide-specific mortality months. The C-index of the nomogram for suicide-specific mortality months in the training and validation sets was 0.762 (95% CI: 0.710–0.815) and 0.761 (95% CI: 0.673–0.849), respectively.

The nomogram demonstrated satisfactory accuracy as assessed by the ROC curve analysis in both the training and validation cohorts, with 3-year AUCs of 0.784 and 0.774, 5-year AUCs of 0.762 and 0.788, and 10-year AUCs of 0.729 and 0.783, respectively (Figure 3). The 3-, 5-, and 10-year suicide probabilities were depicted in the calibration plots in the training and validation sets (Figure 4), which demonstrated a relatively precise predictive performance.

Discussion

This study presents the first suicide risk prediction model developed specifically for patients with tongue cancer, a population not addressed in prior model-building efforts (20). By leveraging a comprehensive dataset from the SEER program, we have delineated five key predictors (age, sex, race, income and T stage) of suicide risk within this cohort. This discovery is pivotal, as it lays the groundwork for a more nuanced understanding of suicide risk stratification in oncology. Building on these insights, we have crafted a novel prognostic model encapsulated in an accessible and robust nomogram. The internal validation of this model substantiates its efficacy, as evidenced by the calibration plots that reveal a strong alignment between predicted and observed outcomes. The nomogram’s design integrates multiple prognostic factors, a feature that enhances its precision in calculating individualized survival probabilities. It stands as a clinically relevant instrument, streamlining the process of personalized treatment planning and prognostic forecasting. Our model offers a straightforward, cost-effective, and non-invasive method for the early identification and management of suicide risks in patients with tongue cancer.

Our study’s findings underscore the influence of age as a significant prognostic marker, with patients aged 70 years or older exhibiting a notably higher risk of suicide. Elderly patients, particularly those over the age of 70, are confronted with a unique set of challenges that may heighten their risk of suicide (21). The advanced age may bring a confluence of physical decline, reduced social engagement, and potentially a greater number of comorbidities, which can exacerbate the psychological impact of a cancer diagnosis. This age group may also experience a sense of loss, such as the death of peers and a reduced sense of purpose, which can contribute to feelings of hopelessness and isolation. The finding suggests a substantial reduction in suicide risk for younger patients, highlighting the importance of age-specific support strategies.

Consistent with previous studies on various cancer populations (22,23), we found that gender emerged as a striking predictor of suicide risk, with male patients showing a markedly higher risk compared to their female counterparts. The pronounced disparity in suicide risk between male and female patients suggests that gender plays a complex role in the psychological response to cancer. Other studies have also found that this may be related to male patients suffering more social pressures, family burdens and their own psychological problems (24,25). Additionally, societal expectations and norms may influence how men perceive their self-worth in the context of illness, potentially leading to a higher risk of suicide. This points to a critical need to understand the gender-specific psychological and social factors that may contribute to this disparity. This includes exploring the role of societal expectations, help-seeking behaviors, and the impact of cancer on traditional gender roles.

Racial differences in suicide risk may reflect a myriad of social, cultural, and economic factors that influence mental health outcomes. The varying degrees of social support, cultural attitudes towards mental health, and the historical experiences of different racial groups can shape the individual’s resilience and coping mechanisms in the face of cancer. Understanding these nuances is essential for developing culturally sensitive interventions.

The progression of cancer to advanced stages often signifies a more severe prognosis and may require more aggressive treatments, which can be physically and emotionally taxing. Patients with advanced cancer may grapple with a sense of helplessness and a decreased quality of life, which can heighten the risk of suicidal ideation. The physical suffering, existential concerns, and the perception of being a burden to others may contribute to suicidal thoughts.

Socioeconomic status is a critical determinant of health outcomes, including mental health. Patients from lower-income backgrounds may face financial strain due to the costs of cancer treatment, which can add to the emotional distress. Limited financial resources can restrict access to comprehensive care, including mental health services, potentially leaving these patients without the necessary support to cope with the psychological toll of cancer.

Our study introduces a novel nomogram for predicting suicide risk in tongue cancer patients, a tool that addresses a significant gap in the current clinical management of this patient population. The alarming global incidence of suicide, coupled with the psychological distress often associated with cancer diagnosis and treatment, underscores the urgency of our findings. Unlike previous studies that have broadly examined cancer patients, our research specifically targets oral tongue cancer, providing a more nuanced understanding of suicide risk within this demographic. The clinical translational significance of our study is profound, offering a paradigm shift towards personalized care in oncology. Our predictive model for suicide risk serves as a critical tool for early identification of vulnerable tongue cancer patients, enabling preemptive and targeted mental health interventions. By personalizing care plans and fostering open dialogue about psychological well-being, our model enhances patient-provider communication, facilitating a more holistic approach to cancer treatment. The integration of mental health services becomes a natural progression, with our findings guiding resource allocation and informing health policies to ensure efficient and effective patient care. Furthermore, our study catalyzes education and training for healthcare professionals, elevating their ability to recognize and manage suicide risk. It also paves the way for future research and development, encouraging the creation of innovative interventions and therapeutic strategies. Ultimately, our model’s application value lies in its potential to revolutionize clinical decision-making, enrich patient outcomes, and cultivate a more empathetic and responsive healthcare system that acknowledges mental health as an indispensable aspect of overall well-being.

The study has some limitations. Firstly, the retrospective nature of our research, while providing valuable insights, is subject to the inherent biases and incomplete data that come with the reliance on existing medical records. This may have influenced the comprehensiveness of our suicide risk factor analysis. Secondly, suicide is a relatively rare outcome among patients with tongue cancer, and the limited number of events may influence the stability and generalizability of predictive models. Although the number of events-per-variable was approximately 10.7 in the final model, the results should still be interpreted cautiously. In addition, deaths from other causes may act as competing events that influence the occurrence of suicide. Because the present study focused on identifying factors associated with suicide-specific mortality using available population-based cancer registry data, further studies incorporating additional competing-risk approaches and independent validation cohorts are warranted to further refine suicide risk estimation. Thirdly, another limitation is that the model was internally validated using a randomly selected cohort from the same population-based database. Although this approach provides an initial assessment of model performance, external validation in independent populations with different geographic regions, healthcare systems, or time periods is necessary to confirm the generalizability of the nomogram before its potential clinical application. Fourthly, the SEER database lacks detailed information regarding the extent of tongue resection, including partial glossectomy, hemiglossectomy, subtotal glossectomy, or total glossectomy. Moreover, postoperative functional outcomes, such as speech, swallowing ability, dietary intake, sleep quality, and quality-of-life measurements, were unavailable. These factors may significantly influence psychological distress and suicide risk among patients with tongue cancer. Future studies incorporating detailed surgical and functional assessments are warranted to improve the accuracy of suicide risk prediction models. Lastly, Suicide is a complex psychosocial outcome influenced by multiple factors beyond cancer-related characteristics. However, several important suicide-related variables, including depression, anxiety disorders, substance abuse, previous suicide attempts, family psychiatric history, and psychological assessment results, are unavailable in the SEER database. Therefore, the present nomogram should be interpreted as a population-level risk stratification tool based on available demographic, socioeconomic, and clinical characteristics rather than a comprehensive model incorporating all potential psychological determinants of suicide risk. Furthermore, because suicide was a rare outcome in this cohort, the predictive performance of the model should be interpreted cautiously, and further validation in independent populations is warranted. Despite these limitations, our study provides a foundational step towards a more nuanced understanding of suicide risk in tongue cancer patients and underscores the importance of further investigation in this critical area of oncology.

Conclusions

We have crafted and substantiated a straightforward yet robust model aimed at personalized prediction and risk stratification for patients afflicted with tongue cancer. This model may provide preliminary risk stratification information and assist in identifying tongue cancer patients who may benefit from further psychological assessment. As a prognostic instrument, it holds the promise of equipping oncologists with the necessary foresight to implement timely preventative measures. Furthermore, it can facilitate the referral of patients identified as high risk to psychiatric professionals for a comprehensive evaluation and management of potential underlying mental health conditions. The implementation of this predictive model may also contribute to alleviating the overall burden of tongue cancer by averting preventable fatalities.
