Human adipose-derived mesenchymal stromal cells in vitro expansion for regenerative applications: tracking small genetic variants reveals dynamic changes within a genetically stable framework
Authors: Simona Neri, Isabella Bartolotti, Elena Pedrini, Cristina Manferdini, Elisa Assirelli, Francesco Ursini
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe extended use of mesenchymal stromal cells (MSC) for cellular therapies raises safety concerns, and preliminary controls are needed before clinical application to guarantee patient safety. In vitro expansion is frequently required to obtain sufficient material, with an increased risk of appearance and selection of genetically altered or senescent cells, which could impair not only safety but also the biological potential and therapeutic efficacy of these cells. In vitro manipulated MSCs must adhere to the guidelines of Good Manufacturing Practices including the verification of genomic stability, transformation potential and senescence. Several tests are currently used, mainly karyotyping, which enables the detection of large alterations. Small alterations, albeit potentially important, are usually not evaluated, except for selected known mutations in a few crucial genes.MethodsHere, the potential of Next Generation Sequencing (NGS) to detect small DNA alterations was exploited as a reliable tool to monitor the genetic stability of cells manipulated prior to therapeutic application. The acquisition of small somatic mutations during in vitro culture of human adipose-derived mesenchymal stromal cells from 23 osteoarthritis patients was investigated at different times from passage 1 to passage 10 using targeted NGS of a panel of human cancer-related genes. Moreover, copy number variations in selected genes were analyzed using both the ‘Coverage Analysis’ plugin and digital PCR.ResultsOverall genetic stability was maintained, despite the emergence of sporadic variants, some with pathogenic potential or of uncertain significance, reflecting dynamic changes in culture. No copy number variations occurred during in vitro culture.ConclusionsTime in culture is confirmed as a key variable influencing the acquisition of genetic alterations, underscoring the importance of minimizing expansion times to preserve genetic stability. Given the potential impact of small genetic alterations on cellular function and the high next generation sequencing processivity and throughput, exploring incoming mutations in MSC cells via NGS turns out to be not only a perspective research pursuit, but also a robust strategy for surveillance of genetic stability. Integrating this approach into quality control guidelines would support standardized monitoring of the genomic integrity of manipulated MSCs prior to clinical application.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13287-026-05065-7.Keywords: Genetic stability, Mesenchymal stromal cells, In vitro expansion, Biosafety, Targeted next generation sequencing, Variant detection, Copy number variations

Background

Mesenchymal stromal cell (MSC)-based therapy has attracted enormous attention, since it provides patients with new options for previously untreatable diseases. MSC efficacy mostly relies on their paracrine action, mediated by the release of soluble factors and exosomes with trophic, anti-inflammatory and anti-apoptotic activity, on their homing ability in the sites of tissue injury, and on their immunomodulatory activity [1, 2].

The clinical use of MCSs is widely diffused in all countries for a broad range of therapeutic applications including cancer, cardiovascular diseases, degenerative and immune diseases, as attested by thousands of registered clinical trials (https://clinicaltrials.gov/) [3]. One of the most investigated conditions for MSC-based regenerative approaches is knee osteoarthritis (OA), a prevalent, multifactorial degenerative joint disease representing an increasing global burden [64]. It is characterized by cartilage degradation, synovial inflammation, and subchondral bone remodeling, ultimately leading to pain and functional impairment [4]. MSCs from multiple sources have shown promise in mitigating OA symptoms and disease progression (reviewed in [5–7]. In particular, MSCs from adipose tissue (adipose-derived MSCs, ASCs) have gained attention due to their relative abundance, ease of harvest, and potent immunomodulatory and anti-inflammatory capacities, with accumulating evidence supporting their safety and therapeutic potential in reducing joint inflammation and promoting cartilage repair [5, 8].

Prior to use, MSC treatments often include in vitro cell manipulation, raising the possibility of acquiring modifications that could have therapeutic implications [9]. Since MSCs are not abundant in tissues, culture expansion is the far more frequent kind of cell manipulation, needed to obtain enough material useful for treatment [10]. Cell expansion is hampered by the acquisition of mutations (genetic and epigenetic) as well as by replicative senescence, with the associated risk of positive selection for genetically altered or senescent cells. Both can significantly alter cellular biological properties and compromise safety, biological potential, and therapeutic efficacy (reviewed in [9, 11]. During in vitro growth, there is an accumulation of oxidative stress and a reduced DNA synthesis and repair efficiency that promote DNA damage accumulation [11, 12]. Moreover, cell expansion drives senescence by progressively reducing replication and differentiation ability, with acquisition of a senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP) [13, 14] corresponding to a shift from repair and homeostasis to pro-inflammatory and pro-aging functions [15]. MSC age-related functional decline also affects MSC immunosuppressive efficacy by downregulating PD-L1 expression [16]. MSC senescence was also related to an acquired ability to promote proliferation and migration of resident tumor cells [14, 17, 18].

Overall, in vitro expansion implies specific risks of transformation and functional alteration. Thus, monitoring the alterations that cells undergo during culture before clinical application is crucial to guaranteeing the safety of MSC-based therapies and, ultimately, patient safety. Specific legal frameworks have been established to define the minimal requirements for the clinical use of manipulated cell therapy products, which are classified as Advanced Therapy Medicinal Products (ATMPs). In Europe, stem cell therapies are regulated under the ATMP framework (https://www.ema.europa.eu/en/human-regulatory/overview/advanced-therapies/legal-framework-advanced-therapies), including Regulation EC n. 1394/2007; Directive 2001/83/EC, and the EMA/CAT Guidelines on safety and quality of MSC-based therapies (EMA/CAT/80183/2014) [9, 19]. Similar regulatory principles apply worldwide, with expanded cell therapies overseen by the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) in the US and the Pharmaceuticals and Medical Devices Agency (PMDA) in Japan.

MSC preparations are considered ATMPs when they undergo substantial manipulation, such as in vitro expansion, or when intended for non-homologous use. They must be produced under Good Manufacturing Practice (GMP) conditions and authorized by the European Medicines Agency (EMA). Currently, only a few MSC therapies have received EMA approval, such as Darvadstrocel for Crohn’s disease [20]. In general, MSC-based products are evaluated case by case due to their heterogeneity and complexity. It is recommended to adopt a risk-based strategy, aiming at identifying various risks with respect to quality, safety and efficacy. Identity, purity, potency, tumorigenicity, senescence and genomic stability are among the recommended controls. Despite its comprehensive framework, the EU’s regulation of ATMPs lacks exclusive and standardized Genetic Stability Testing. Karyotyping is widely accepted and the most frequently used since aneuploidies and chromosomal abnormalities occur in culture and, at the same time, they are a hallmark of cancer [9, 21, 22]. However, karyotyping only detects large alterations, in the order of megabases of DNA, and lacks sensitivity for detecting smaller defects down to the size of a single or few bases which could pose a transformation risk or affect cell behavior. Given the numerous potential changes occurring in vitro and the lack of thorough investigations that have identified genetic instability at specific loci, a major need for technological advances in biosafety molecular monitoring can be envisaged. Next Generation Sequencing (NGS) enabled a new paradigm allowing for massive parallel sequencing of millions bases with unprecedented potential. It sensitively and cheaply detects mutations in a limited time. This technique has already demonstrated its potential for translation into clinical practice, thanks to its efficiency, sensitivity and smartness [23–26].

Here, we have leveraged this technology as a tool to monitor genomic stability in terms of small genetic alterations to assess the safety of mesenchymal stromal cells prior to therapeutic application. While most existing data focus on large cytogenetic alterations, our study aims to provide a more detailed evaluation of genetic stability at single base detection level. We tracked the somatic mutation spectrum and copy number variations in cancer-related genes of ASCs from knee OA patients, at different times of in vitro expansion (from P0 until P10), using Next Generation Sequencing (NGS) and digital PCR (dPCR). Results pointed to a general stability, even if sporadic mutations of unknown or potential pathogenic effect occur.

Methods

Specimens

Subcutaneous abdominal fat (30 ± 1 g) was obtained from 24 donors affected by knee OA (50–80 years of age; BMI≤30) undergoing liposuction under local anesthesia, when recruited for the ADIPOA2 clinical trial phase IIb European project (Grant agreement number 643809). The study was approved by the Rizzoli Orthopedic Institute Ethical Committee, and all patients provided their written informed consent (Protocol number ADIPOA2 0007498).

Cell isolation and culture

Clinical grade ASCs were isolated from lipoaspirates according to Good Manufacturing Practice (GMP) as previously described [21, 27]. Briefly, after fat digestion with collagenase (45 min at 37 °C) and centrifugation (10 min at 600 RCF), the stromal vascular fraction (SVF) was seeded into a CellStack chamber (Corning, USA) at the density of 4 × 103 cells/cm2 in α-minimum essential medium (αMEM) (Gibko) supplemented with 2% platelet lysate (PLP), 100 µg/ml Penicillin-Streptomycin (Life Technologies, USA). The medium was changed every 24 h during the first three days of cultivation, after which it was changed every 72 h. Cells were harvested by trypsinization at fixed intervals, typically near confluence but not necessarily at full confluence, to standardize culture conditions across all donors, then partially reseeded at the density of 2000 cells/cm2 and partially harvested for further analyses. Cell passages performed by the analysed cell populations are shown in Table 1. Cell viability and count were performed at each split by eosin exclusion dye. Cell morphology was observed throughout the culture period.

Replication rate

To evaluate the effect of in vitro expansion on the acquisition of somatic mutations, the ASC replicative history in culture was recorded as Cumulative Population Doublings (CPD). Population doublings (PD) were calculated at each split by the formula NH/NI = 2PD, where NH is the number of recovered and NI the number of seeded cells [30]. CPD (starting from P0) were obtained by adding the PD calculated at each passage to the previous PD.

NGS-targeted sequencing

Total DNA was obtained from ASC pellets (50–200,000 cells) using the QIAamp DNA Mini Kit (Qiagen GmbH, Germany) and quantified with the Qubit 2.0 Fluorometer (Life Technologies, USA) after staining with the Qubit dsDNA HS Assay kit (Invitrogen, USA). One of the 24 ASC samples expanded in culture (sample 10) was excluded from subsequent analyses due to poor DNA quality. The somatic mutation spectrum of 23 ASC samples comparatively analyzed at different culture passages was assessed by targeted panel sequencing in known cancer mutation hotspots using the Ion Ampliseq Cancer Hotspot Panel v2 (CHPv2) (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) including 207 amplicons, covering 2855 mutations frequently occurring in 50 cancer-related genes. Each sample (10 ng) was processed using the Ion AmpliSeq Library Kit 2.0 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) according to manufacturing procedures; the Ion Chef platform (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) was used to automate the template preparation and to load the Ion 316 Chip Kit v2 BC (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). The sequencing reaction was then performed on the Ion Torrent Personal Genome Machine (PGM) System (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). When the average coverage was below 1000X and DNA was available, samples were sequenced twice in different runs. The alignment of the sequences with hg19 reference genome and base calling were performed using the Torrent Suite software (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). The identification of variants was performed using two different commercial applications: the Ion Reporter™ software (Version 5.20, Thermo Fisher Scientific) and a specific somatic workflow from the SEQNEXT application (Version 5.4.1, JSI medical systems GmbH Germany). All the variants were annotated according to the nomenclature used by the Human Genome Variation Society (HGVS) and then classified according to the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics classification (ACMG) [31]. We used the Franklin web-based platform (https://hsf.genomnis.com/home) to assess variant pathogenicity. As part of its prediction pipeline, Franklin integrates multiple in silico tools, including SIFT, FATHMM, DANN, MetaLR, REVEL, MutationAssesser, PolyPhen-2, MutationTaster, PrimateAI, BayesDel, AlphaMissense, Eve, Varity, SpliceAI, dbscSNV, and GERP. For additional splicing predictions, we also used Human Splicing Finder (https://hsf.genomnis.com/home, GenOmnis). To limit false positive calls related to homopolymer sequencing errors [32], data analysis was focused on the single-nucleotide variants (SNVs) detection. Regarding the minimum required coverage, we considered what suggested in a previous study [33]: to have false positive probability of 0.001 and true positive of 0.999, a > 5% Variant Allele Frequency (VAF) was required at 562 average coverage depth and > 3% VAF at 1,650 (together with the recommended threshold of at least 30 mutated reads). According to what reported, we selected a 3% VAF cutoff to balance between sensitivity and the risk of false positives, as detection below ca.2% VAF is more prone to artefacts in standard target panels and can be difficult to distinguish from background noise. Technical studies [34, 35] have already illustrated that the reproducibility of calls below 2% is lower and false positive increase. Then, variants in the 1–3% VAF range were called only if confirmed in replicates. Variants below 1% VAF, detectable using ultra-sensitive approaches, have uncertain clinical significance, particularly for MSCs with limited proliferative capacity and in vivo persistence. Variants were considered true calls if they respected the criteria, in addition to a minimal depth of coverage > 250 reads per tested target [36] and if called by both data analysis softwares.

Although the study is primarily focused on the evaluation of small genetic alterations, the ‘coverage Analysis’ plugin was used to identify clear loss of one or more full amplicons by evidencing areas of missing data. In cases of missing or markedly reduced coverage, the results were confirmed by performing a technical replicate in an independent sequencing run. Moreover, the amplicon depth of each sample normalized to the total number of reads) was compared with the mean normalized depth of the same amplicon defined in a previously established wild-type baseline, as described in a previous study [37]. As depth-of-coverage approaches are typically less reliable for the detection of large duplication (harder to be distinguished from technical variability in targeted sequencing data), the assessment of CNV gains will require the use of alternative methodologies such as qPCR, MLPA (when available), SNP arrays, or CNV-dedicated analysis pipelines.

Digital PCR (dPCR)

Due to the high frequency of TP53 mutations reported in human cancer [38, 39], the TP53 gene was also explored for the presence of copy number variations (CNVs) by dPCR. To detect low frequency variants, the presence/absence of CNVs in TP53 was verified using the QuantStudio 3D Digital PCR System platform (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). DNAs from the first and last culture passage of each ASC sample were analyzed using a specific TaqMan probe for the detection of CNVs in exon 4 of TP53 (location: hg38, Chr.17:7668402–7687550; Cat. No. Hs06423639_cn, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA); the probe was previously tested and validated on 6 DNAs with regular biallelic status. A second probe was used to target RNAseP gene (Cat.No. 4403326, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA), as housekeeping control. The reaction mix was created using QuantStudio™ 3D Digital PCR Master Mix (Cat. No. 4482710, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA), nuclease-free water, and both probes, according to the QuantStudio™ 3D Digital PCR System User Guide. All the DNA samples were diluted to 10 ng/µl and tested together with no-template control. Each DNA sample was added to the mix and then introduced in a Digital PCR 20 K Chip by QuantStudio™ 3D Digital PCR Chip Loader (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The data were analyzed using the QuantStudio™ 3D AnalysisSuite™ Software (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) and results were expressed as number of copies/µl.

Results

Cell characterization (morphology, replication rate, phenotype, and differentiation potential)

To evaluate the impact of in vitro expansion on ASCs, we first assessed their morphological features, proliferative capacity, phenotype, and differentiation potential.

The percentage of CFU-f was comparable at passages P0 and P1, with no significant changes (Fig. 1A). Over the course of the culture, the cells appeared as a uniform mono-layer population with stable fibroblast-like morphology, enlarging with culture time (Fig. 1B).

Throughout the observation period, none of the ASC preparations reached replicative senescence or completely stopped growing. All samples were cultured up to P3; ten samples were expanded to P6, four of which up to P10, and one up to P14. Growth curves showed discrete heterogeneity, emphasizing interindividual variability in replication rate (Supplementary Fig. 1). As expected, the proliferation rate gradually slowed down over time while maintaining an increasing trend. Mean PD accomplished at end P1, P4, P6, and P10, were 1.02 ± 0.41, 3.69 ± 1.32, 5.44 ± 1.44, and 7.87 ± 0.52, respectively (Fig. 1C).

ASCs antigenic characteristics (Fig. 1D) and differentiation potential (Fig. 1E) were proven, according to internationally accepted criteria for defining MSCs [29, 40]. ASCs had a classical phenotype: at the end of the first passage, they showed very low expression of CD14, CD31 and CD45 (0.54 ± 0.25%, 0.42 ± 0.20% and 0.20 ± 0.13%, respectively); they showed a fraction of the population expressing CD34 (9.23 ± 6.55%), and they all expressed CD13, CD73, CD90, and CD105 (98.83 ± 0.43, 96.72 ± 1.94, 97.43 ± 2.71, 97.46 ± 1.89, respectively). CD14, CD31 and CD45 negativity was maintained both at P1 (0.10 ± 0.08%, 0.38 ± 0.52% and 0.34 ± 0.49%, respectively) and P3 (0.25 ± 0.46%, 0.38 ± 0.52% and 0.22 ± 0.44%, respectively) as well as CD13, CD73, CD90, and CD105 positivity was maintained both at P1 (99.34 ± 0.44, 98.73 ± 0.73, 99.10 ± 0.60, 98.00 ± 1.64, respectively) and P3 (98.95 ± 0.93, 96.56 ± 3.22, 94.57 ± 3.70, 98.18 ± 1.03, respectively). CD34 marker progressively disappeared (Fig. 1D).

Using specific differentiation media, ASCs showed osteogenic and chondrogenic differentiation potential, confirming their multipotency (Fig. 1E).

Somatic mutation acquisition in culture

Using targeted NGS, we tracked the acquisition of somatic mutations across different culture passages. For each sample at each analyzed culture passage, the mean depth, the uniformity of coverage, and the on-target percentage are detailed in Supplementary Table 1. Due to low coverage (90.33x and 0.133x), Sample 19 (P1) and the replicated analysis of Sample 11 (P4) were excluded from the study.

Thirty-four variants classified as polymorphisms or ‘benign/likely benign’ variants (listed in Supplementary Table 2) were detected in several samples at a frequency of approximately 50% or 100%, revealing their constitutive presence at germline level, respectively in hetero- or homozygosity. In all cases, the allele frequency was consistently maintained across culture passages.

Nine variants, whose low frequency ranging from 2.2 to 11.3% indicates their somatic origin, have been detected in 10 ASC samples at different culture times. Detailed information on the detected variants, samples and culture passages at which they appeared, together with variant allele frequency (VAF), is shown in Table 2. The detection of low-frequency variants at individual passages suggests the presence of sub-clonal or emerging somatic events during in vitro culture.

Out of the 23 ASC cases that were examined, 13 did not exhibit any variants. Of the 10 cases that did, 8 only acquired one variant, 1 sample acquired two, and 1 sample acquired three.

The FBXW7 gene variant (c.1524 A > G, p.Gln508Gln) was the most frequently observed, being present in 5 different ASC donors. In three of these 5 cases (samples 14, 18, and 21), the variant was absent at the beginning of the culture (P0-P1) and appeared after P5; in the two remaining cases (samples 16 and 23), the variant was already detectable at passage P1 and persisted in subsequent passages. In these latter cases, it is not possible to determine whether the variant was already present in vivo or was acquired during the early stages of culture (between P0 and P1). Although the variant is synonymous, its low frequency in the general population leads to its classification as a variant of uncertain significance. In addition, advanced in silico prediction tools (Human Splicing Finders, GenOmnis), which also consider the effect of alterations in ‘non-canonic’ auxiliary splicing sequences, have predicted a potential deleterious effect, with the generation of a novel splice donor site, potentially affecting transcript maturation.

All the other detected variants were absent at P0 and only appeared in subsequent culture passages.

Considering the clinical significance (variant effect), we detected only one clearly pathogenic variant (KRAS: c.182 A > T, p.Gln61Leu; ClinVar VCV000045116). This variant was absent at P0 and P4 and occurred at a frequency of 2.3% at P6 of Sample 2 (Table 2). According to ClinVar information (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/clinvar/variation/45116/), it is associated to non-small cell lung carcinoma, neoplasm of the large intestine, thyroid tumor, medullary thyroid cancer and multiple myeloma.

Four somatic variants, HRAS c.81T > C (p.His27His), IDH1 c.315 C > T (p.Gly105Gly), ABL1 c.823-59T > C, and KIT c.1621 A > C (p.Met541Leu), were predicted as having benign impact. They were also detected as germline variants (see Supplementary Table 2).

Four genetic somatic alterations, not detected as germline variants, are of uncertain significance (VUS): FGFR3 c.1200 C > G (p.Ser400Arg), FBXW7 c.1524 A > G (p.Gln508Gln), KIT c.1973G > A (p.Gly658Glu), CDKN2A c.396G > T (p.Ala132Ala).

Copy number variations

The ‘Coverage Analysis’ plugin was used to generate visual coverage maps. These coverage maps had compared each other and with a reference sample (CTRL), evidencing the presence of some missing (or extremely low coverage) data in chromosome 4 of all the culture passages of Sample 7: 7(P0), 7(P4), 7(P6) (Fig. 2); the deletion was validated by performing technical replicates in separate sequencing runs. Going into detail, the missing data included a 78 nt region in exon 18 (chr4: g.55602674–55602751) of KIT gene, suggesting a deletion already present in vivo. This observation was confirmed using a previously validated algorithm to identify large deletion by NGS data comparing the normalized coverage of the amplicon with its mean normalized coverage in a group of control samples [37]. To better define the length of the deletion, essential for the variant interpretation, a qPCR or full-gene NGS analysis could be employed. KIT pathogenic variants are associated to various cancer types (e.g. gastrointestinal stromal cell, germ cell tumors, acute myeloid leukemia, systemic mastocytosis, piebaldism) [41].

Since TP53 is a master regulator of genomic stability, the first and last culture passages for each sample were also analyzed to evaluate the presence of CNVs in TP53 gene. Digital PCR analysis did not reveal any CNVs, thereby excluding the presence of large deletions or amplifications in this genomic region (Supplementary Fig. 2).

Discussion

The therapeutic application of MSCs raises several safety concerns, primarily the potential risk of tumorigenicity, along with issues related to therapeutic efficacy. These concerns are particularly relevant when MSCs undergo extensive in vitro manipulation, most commonly expansion, prior to clinical use. Our investigation to assess the impact of in vitro expansion on the accumulation of small genetic alterations in human ASCs from 23 knee OA donors contributes to the growing body of evidence concerning the genetic stability of human MSCs during in vitro manipulation.

The extent of expansion and specific culture conditions critically influence MSC phenotype, functionality, and senescence onset [42, 43]. In the present study, cells were cultured well beyond standard expansion duration to stress them and facilitate the detection of potential alterations. It is well established that prolonged in vitro expansion induces cellular senescence, accumulation of DNA damage and a decline in both DNA repair efficiency [13, 21, 44] and chromatin stability [45]. Bao et al. [46] demonstrated that extended MSC culture leads to the downregulation of key DNA repair genes, along with increased residual DNA damage and a reduction in DNA damage recognition capacity. While cell expansion-induced senescence can act as a safeguard mechanism against malignant transformation by blocking cell replication, it may also compromise MSCs functionality, enhance transformation risk, and impair MSCs function and therapeutic potential (reviewed in [11]). These findings raise concern that, under specific selective pressures such as prolonged culture, certain clones may bypass growth arrest.

Even if MSCs do not become transformed per se, they can indirectly promote tumour progression by supporting the proliferation and dissemination of pre-existing resident pre-malignant or malignant cells. MSCs may exert a dual role in the host, depending on their activation state—either a tumour-suppressive MSC1 or a tumour-promoting MSC2 phenotype [47, 48]—as well as on their senescent state. Cellular senescence can act as a tumour suppressor by blocking the expansion of damaged cells through growth arrest, or as a tumour promoter via the senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP), which releases mediators in the tumour microenvironment that stimulate cancer cell migration and proliferation [17, 18, 49, 50].

Genomic stability assessments for MSC biosafety typically rely on conventional karyotyping, with some studies using higher-resolution methods such as comparative genomic hybridization (CGH) and gene expression analyses [9]. However, these approaches fail to detect point mutations or small indels, which may potentially affect cell behaviour. In this study, targeted NGS was employed to enhance mutation detection sensitivity. When combined with complementary techniques for detecting larger genomic alterations, NGS provides a more comprehensive genomic profile, ultimately enabling more reliable assessments of cell suitability for clinical applications. Its high-throughput, low DNA input requirement, and high sensitivity streamline mutation analysis in both research and clinical precision medicine settings [51, 52]. NGS is particularly well-suited for high-resolution tracking of heterogeneous cell populations, such as culture-expanded MSCs, where random somatic mutations may be lost or become fixed over time through negative or positive selection, respectively. Despite its advantages, NGS is not yet a formal regulatory requirement for genetic stability assessments.

In this study, we analysed a panel of 50 cancer-related genes; however, custom panels can be tailored for specific applications based on the intended use of the cells, their origin, and existing research on relevant susceptibility genes or recurrent mutations. This flexibility makes targeted sequencing a highly versatile and adaptable tool.

Our results demonstrated a low incidence of genetic alterations, confirming overall genetic stability even after prolonged culture, consistent with literature (reviewed in [9]) and in agreement with our previous studies on infrapatellar fat pad and subcutaneous ASCs. In those studies, techniques such as karyotyping, microsatellite instability analysis, and telomere length and activity assessments were employed [12, 21]. These methods are time-consuming, whereas NGS significantly reduces processing time while providing comprehensive datasets.

Among the nine somatic variants detected, four were classified as benign and likely culture-acquired (P4 or P10), suggesting prolonged culture promotes the accumulation of mutations. Four variants were VUS: two synonymous and two missense mutations. The most frequent synonymous variant occurred in the FBXW7 gene (the others were identified in isolated cases), detected in five samples from early culture to passage 10. FBXW7 encodes an E3 ubiquitin ligase substrate involved in the degradation of oncogenic proteins and acting as a tumour suppressor; several pathogenic mutations in this gene have been linked to multiple cancer types [53]. Although functional studies are needed, advanced in silico prediction tools suggest that the c.1524 A > G (p.Gln508Gln) variant may alter splicing by activating a novel donor site. Notably, FBXWT expression was decreased in OA cartilage [54], as well as related to cellular senescence [55]. Based on what described, it is necessary to pay attention in presence of synonymous variants; in fact, even if they are supposed to be harmless, their impact on pre-mRNA splicing, mRNA stability and transcription efficiency cannot be excluded [56].

Another synonymous mutation was found in CDKN2A gene, a tumour suppressor gene involved in cell cycle regulation and frequently mutated in cancers [57] This variant appeared in one sample at P4 and remains unreported, warranting further study. The observed missense variant in the FGFR3 gene (an oncogene linked to various cancers [58]) is not described, with unknown clinical significance.

A VUS was also detected in the KIT gene, coding for a receptor tyrosine kinase involved in cell proliferation and survival and whose mutations are common in GIST, melanoma, and leukaemia [41]. This variant is not described and results of unknown significance.

Only one clearly pathogenic variant was identified at P6 in a single donor sample, namely the c.182 A > T (p.Gln61Leu) mutation in the KRAS GTPase proto-oncogene, involved in signal transduction and cell proliferation. It is a well-known oncogenic driver mutation that constitutively activates KRAS and affects downstream signalling, contributing to malignant transformation [59, 60]. Its presence, even at low frequency, represents a potentially relevant clinical risk and cannot be overlooked, supporting the exclusion of the affected cell batch from clinical use under a precautionary, risk-based approach. The detection of this common variant, observed here only once, allows only speculation as to whether it reflects intrinsic donor characteristics (notably the donor harbored no other variants) or culture-related events. Moreover, its significance remains to be established with dedicated functional studies.

In general, the sporadic occurrence in vitro of low-frequency oncogenic variants raises important questions regarding the appropriate sensitivity of NGS-based release testing for MSC products. At present, there is no consensus on variant allele frequency thresholds that should be considered clinically relevant in this context. While ultra-deep sequencing enables the identification of very small subclones, the biological and clinical significance of such findings remains uncertain, reinforced by the limited engraftment, proliferation, and long-term persistence of MSCs in vivo [61], and by the unlikelihood that a single oncogenic mutation would be sufficient to drive neoplastic transformation. Consequently, the mere presence of rare oncogenic variants does not necessarily translate into increased clinical risk.

Nevertheless, stochastic clonal expansion during in vitro culture or after administration cannot be entirely excluded, especially for alterations affecting key oncogenic pathways. Therefore, a precautionary, risk-based approach should prevail, and cell preparations carrying potentially pathogenic variants should be excluded until the safety of the mutation is demonstrated, especially when the use is intended for non–life-threatening conditions. Further functional and longitudinal studies are required to define clinically meaningful thresholds and to clarify the phenotypic and biological consequences of low-frequency variants. In this context, targeted NGS, although not currently mandated by regulatory frameworks, may complement conventional cytogenetic analyses to further enhance patient safety.

No copy number variants were observed in TP53. As a limitation, we note that our digital PCR analysis was restricted to exon 4, chosen as a representative central exon to allow sensitive detection of low-frequency copy number changes involving the entire gene or the central region. Consequently, it does not detect partial copy number alterations confined to regions outside exon 4. Nevertheless, somatic TP53 deletions in cancer typically involve large genomic segments rather than isolated exons, making exon 4 a reliable proxy [62].

Using the depth-of-coverage approach, a deletion of at least 78 nucleotides in exon 18 of the KIT gene was detected in one sample at P0 and was consistently identified in all subsequent passages of the same sample, indicating an in vivo origin. Although the exact length of the deletion is unknown, it is located within an exon encoding part of the tyrosine kinase domain and may therefore impair kinase activity, alter protein stability, or lead to transcript degradation, potentially resulting in reduced signaling or, less predictably, aberrant kinase activity, also depending on the reading frame. This rare and poorly characterized variant with a likely more heterogeneous effect contrasts with the more frequently reported exon 11 mutations and exon 9 duplications, which are well-established gain-of-function alterations driving constitutive KIT activation [63].Based on previous observations and on its presumed in vivo origin, we could speculate that this deletion may, in principle, be tolerated and compatible with a somatic variant in non-malignant tissues, depending on its functional impact whose elucidation will require targeted functional assays.

Overall, the limited number of minor alterations observed, although rare, confirms that prolonged culture may increase the risk of acquiring somatic mutations. Excluding P0 variants (likely reflecting in vivo events), most mutations emerged from P6 onwards, suggesting that mutation accumulation is related to culture duration and cumulative cell divisions. Therefore, limiting expansion to early passages ([2–64]) may help mitigate the risk of acquiring potentially harmful mutations.

In three cases (two VUS and the deletion in the KIT gene), variants were detected from the onset of culture, suggesting their occurrence in vivo. Given that certain variants may undergo a specific selective pressure in culture, it appears essential to monitor not only mutations arising in vitro, but also pre-existing in vivo mutations, which may be positively selected in vitro.

Somatic variants were identified in 10 of 23 donors: eight donors carried a single variant, one donor carried two variants, and one donor carried three. In contrast, no somatic variants were detected in the remaining 13 donors, highlighting interindividual variability in genomic stability and/or susceptibility to mutation acquisition. This variability may relate to intrinsic genomic instability, underlying genetic background, age, or prior medical history. These observations aligns with Borgonovo et al. [65], who reported that MSCs derived from a donor with a cancer history (initially cytogenetically normal) acquired clonal trisomies and translocations during in vitro culture, suggesting an intrinsic genetic instability specific to that donor. MSCs from Fanconi anemia patients also displayed a propensity to acquire spontaneous chromosomal breakages in vitro [66]. Similarly, we previously observed the emergence of aneuploidies during culture in only one of a series of ASC samples [21], again suggesting a donor-specific susceptibility. Interestingly, in the present study, the same ASC sample did not exhibit any point mutations or microdeletion, even if it should be noted that the CNV analysis was limited to deletions evidenced in the visual coverage maps, which may have led to underestimation of the actual deletion burden. In any case, these findings suggest that the propensity to acquire genomic alterations may not be generalized, but rather restricted to specific donors and types of changes. These findings emphasize the translational relevance of donor-dependent variability, the complexity of interpreting genomic stability, and the importance of the donor’s genetic background and specific risk profile, reinforcing the need for a personalized, case-by-case assessment. While systematic pre-stratification of donors based on predictive mutation signatures is currently not feasible, particular attention may be warranted for donors with a history of neoplastic disease or other conditions associated with genomic instability. Future studies may consider patient/donor pre-screening or stratification as a strategy to mitigate biosafety risks and support careful, individualized evaluation of MSC products.

Looking forward, NGS could be integrated into standard MSC release testing to improve genetic monitoring and strengthen regulatory frameworks. Customized gene panels, rather than broad cancer panels, may more efficiently detect potentially deleterious changes. Functional studies on cultures carrying rare variants, such as the KRAS mutation identified in this study, will be essential to assess effects on proliferation, senescence, differentiation, and overall therapeutic potential. Such integrative approaches could refine risk-based assessments and support safer clinical applications of MSCs.

Conclusions

This study discusses the monitoring of small-scale genetic alterations as a valuable approach to perform a comprehensive genomic stability assessment of mesenchymal stromal cells manipulated prior to their therapeutic use. Among the available methodologies, panel-based mutation detection using high-throughput sequencing emerges as a technically suitable and sensitive strategy to support the safe and efficient clinical translation of MSC-based therapies. Genetic stability was generally maintained, but mutation burden increased over time as the likelihood of acquiring potentially pathogenic mutations. Therefore, the reduction of expansion can greatly reduce their occurrence.

Findings also highlight the importance of case-by-case assessment to monitor both mutations acquired in vitro and pre-existing in vivo variants, that may subsequently be selected during expansion.
