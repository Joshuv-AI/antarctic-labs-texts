IndiVNet A region adaptive semantic image segmentation for autonomous driving in unstructured environments
Authors: Pritam Chakraborty, Anjan Bandyopadhyay, Siddhartha Bhattacharyya, Jan Platos
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Autonomous navigation in developing regions is challenged by heterogeneous traffic, dynamic occlusions, and weak road structure. Existing segmentation models, largely trained on structured Western datasets, struggle to generalize under these conditions. To address this gap, we propose IndiVNet, a semantic segmentation architecture tailored for unstructured Indian driving environments. IndiVNet introduces a progressive dilation encoder (6\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\rightarrow$$\end{document}16) that captures fine-grained details and broad contextual cues without inducing oversparsity. Evaluated on the India Driving Dataset (IDD), it achieves 69.98% mIoU, outperforming CNN and Transformer baselines, and reaches 73.2% mIoU on CAMVID, demonstrating strong cross-domain generalization. By combining contextual adaptability with real-time efficiency, IndiVNet offers a scalable, region-aware solution for robust autonomous navigation in complex environments.

Introduction

The global push towards autonomous vehicle technology has been notably transformative. In this progress, the unique challenges presented by India’s diverse and intricate driving conditions have been overlooked. Unlike the roads in the USA or Europe, Indian roadways are characterized by complexity, congestion, and distinct cultural nuances1–4. The inadequacy of conventional segmentation models for effectively addressing these challenges has become evident, as they are often designed for Western environments5–8. In recognition of this critical gap, our research is motivated by the need to develop a segmentation model explicitly designed for autonomous navigation on Indian roads. The intricacies of the Indian driving landscape demand specialized attention, considering the diverse terrains, erratic traffic patterns, and unique cultural aspects. Compounding this challenge is the scarcity of datasets representative of Indian driving conditions, with existing datasets primarily composed of images from Western countries.

Indian road conditions differ significantly from structured Western environments, presenting unique challenges for semantic segmentation. Firstly, Indian traffic is highly unstructured, with frequent violations of lane discipline and poorly defined road boundaries, complicating spatial reasoning. Secondly, the traffic composition is heterogeneous, encompassing vehicles, two-wheelers, pedestrians, animal-driven carts, and informal transport modes, increasing intra-class variability. Thirdly, road scenes often exhibit visual clutter and occlusions due to street vendors, religious gatherings, and irregularly parked vehicles, introducing substantial noise. Finally, infrastructure variability, such as abrupt changes in road width, surface quality, and markings, further increases scene complexity. Conventional models such as BiSeNet, FANet, and ICNet9–11, which are primarily trained on structured datasets like Cityscapes and CamVid12,13, show degraded performance on Indian datasets like India Driving Dataset (IDD)14. Their limited adaptability to heterogeneous and visually noisy environments restricts their applicability in unstructured road scenarios, as demonstrated in Table 3.

While large-scale global benchmarks such as Cityscapes, Mapillary Vistas, and BDD100K exist, they include only a limited subset of images representing Indian or unstructured traffic conditions. To overcome these limitations, we draw upon the India Driving Dataset14, a valuable resource crafted by IIIT Allahabad. This dataset, specifically curated for the nuances of Indian roads, serves as a fundamental element in our quest to enhance the performance of segmentation models in this specific context.

The core motivation behind this research stems from the notable absence of work focused on adapting segmentation models to the complexities of Indian roads. The disparities in terrain, traffic patterns, and cultural influences demand a tailored approach. The motivation is further accentuated by the limited applicability of existing models, which are designed for Western environments, and the scarcity of datasets representative of Indian driving scenarios. This research aims to fill this void, utilizing the India Driving Dataset to pave the way for segmentation models optimized for the unique challenges posed by India’s diverse and crowded roadways.

This paper presents an enhanced approach to semantic segmentation, specifically designed for Indian driving conditions, by incorporating extensive dilution techniques. This broadens the field of view to capture rich contextual information. Secondly, it aims to explore the utilization of diverse backbones, including various convolutional neural networks, to capture low-level features crucial for accurate and effective segmentation. This research seeks to address the inadequacies of existing segmentation models in the Indian driving landscape by proposing a novel methodology that integrates comprehensive dilution strategies and diverse backbones, contributing to the advancement of autonomous vehicle technologies in this unique context.

Literature review

Unlike object detection, which localizes discrete objects using bounding boxes, or instance segmentation, which differentiates multiple instances of the same class, semantic segmentation assigns a categorical label to each pixel, providing dense scene understanding. This work focuses solely on semantic segmentation for road-scene perception under unstructured Indian conditions. Semantic segmentation is used as a pre-processing or post-processing step in various computer vision tasks15. Image segmentation is a key phase in scene understanding, crucial for interpreting the surrounding environment16. The safe and secure navigation of intelligent driving systems relies on scene understanding, as it enables the interpretation of the behavior of surrounding vehicles and pedestrians.

Before the development of deep neural networks, handcrafted features such as histograms of oriented gradients (HOG)17 were major components of classical semantic segmentation techniques. These features were input into random forests, support vector machines, and naïve Bayes classifiers. The success of these conventional techniques in pixel-level classification tasks was limited by their emphasis on manually created features rather than an understanding of the data structure18.

Semantic segmentation performance greatly increased with the development of deep learning, especially with convolutional neural networks (CNNs) like VGG, ResNet, Xception, and GooLeNet19–21. U-shaped architectures, such as ResNet18-UNet and VGG16-UNet22,23 established new standards in this arena. For example, Audebert et al.24 proposed a model for vehicle extraction, which increased inference time but improved overall accuracy by 10% by utilizing the VGG16 pre-trained network. Typically, encoder-decoder architectures were used in semantic segmentation. While the decoder network used transposed convolutions to recover fine-grained details from the output, the encoder network down-sampled the input to identify higher-level characteristics. These details were preserved through skip links between the respective encoder and decoder levels. VGG16 fared better than ResNet and E-Net19,25 among various feature extractors; nevertheless, an ensemble method incorporating many extractors could further improve performance.

Complying with traffic regulations facilitated autonomous cars’ navigation in controlled traffic conditions. However, in unstructured environments, such as those frequently found in developing countries like India autonomous navigation becomes more difficult due to the lack of lane information and defined traffic regulations26. AI algorithms built to manage unstructured traffic are critical to the success of autonomous vehicles in these areas.

Large annotated datasets are essential for training deep learning models in autonomous driving; however, collecting such datasets is both expensive and time-consuming. Most existing datasets assume structured environments in mostly high-income countries, which do not accurately represent the chaotic traffic conditions found in regions like India. To fill this gap, the India Driving Dataset (IDD)14 was introduced at NCVPRIPG 2019, capturing images from unstructured driving environments. Several models have been proposed to address these challenges the Modified U-Net (Mod-UNet)27 introduced, which effectively combined low-level and high-level features to segment vehicles within dense and unstructured traffic. Similarly, a paper introduced the EfficientNet-UNet28 architecture, which leveraged EfficientNet for enhanced feature extraction and achieved superior IoU scores in segmentation tasks.

To facilitate real-time segmentation on datasets such as Cityscapes, Mapillary Vistas, and India Driving Dataset (IDD)12,14,29, Porzi and his team introduced a segmentation head that combines multi-scale features extracted from a feature pyramid network (FPN)30 with contextual information using a lightweight module inspired by DeepLab. Singh et al.31 conducted a comprehensive analysis of various models for object detection, semantic segmentation, and instance segmentation, highlighting the unique challenges posed by unstructured traffic datasets. Furthermore, Baheti and his team enhanced the DeepLabv3+32 framework by incorporating dilated residual networks to improve segmentation performance, particularly for small objects within the India Driving Dataset (IDD).

Despite the progress, obtaining commercial faith in deep-learning-based models for semantic segmentation remained difficult due to their opaque nature. Grad-CAM and other Explainable AI (XAI) techniques33 were used to interpret the output of these models. Grad-CAM was applied to multiple CNN models by using gradients to build localization maps that emphasized significant areas in the picture for concept prediction.

Unstructured traffic datasets continued to receive little research attention. The current study suggests an inception-based U-Net model with Grad-CAM34,35 to evaluate the findings of semantic segmentation on images obtained from unstructured traffic settings to overcome this issue. The segmented images are reconstructed by the decoder, while the inception module acts as the encoder for feature extraction. The data has been demonstrated by Grad-CAM to aid in interpretation and clarification36.

Proposed network architecture

The proposed IndiVNet architecture is designed specifically for semantic segmentation in unstructured Indian driving conditions. At a high level, IndiVNet combines a ResNet-50 backbone with our novel Indienco encoder, which introduces a progressive dilation strategy to capture multi-scale contextual features while preserving fine spatial details. The architecture is lightweight yet robust, enabling the accurate segmentation of heterogeneous traffic scenes under varying illumination and occlusion conditions. In the following subsections, we first describe the Indienco encoder, which is responsible for multi-scale context aggregation, and then present the overall IndiVNet network, which integrates low-level and high-level features for precise pixel-wise classification.

While IndiVNet uses conventional building blocks such as convolution, max-pooling, and dilated convolution, the novelty lies in how these operations are organized and progressively applied. Specifically, our progressive dilation strategy incrementally increases dilation rates (6–16) to balance local detail with long-range context, avoiding oversparsity issues found in traditional dilated CNNs. In addition, the encoder is designed in a region-aware manner, focusing more capacity on critical semantic regions (e.g., vehicles, pedestrians) that dominate Indian traffic scenes. This structural innovation progressive dilation combined with dataset-driven region prioritization differentiates IndiVNet from prior architectures that employ the same basic primitives in a fixed or global way.

Indienco encoder

The ‘Indienco’ is a custom encoder designed for semantic segmentation tasks. It applies a series of dilated convolutions to an input tensor, capturing multi-scale information. The dilation rate varies across layers, allowing the model to capture features at different scales. This module enhances the model’s ability to maintain high-resolution spatial information while also capturing larger-scale contextual details. Justification for Using Dilation Rates from 6 to 16 We use dilation rates ranging from 6 to 16 in incremental steps. The choice of 10 dilation rates strikes a balance between two critical objectives. The first is to expand the effective receptive field to cover large objects and distant regions, and the second is to preserve sufficient spatial resolution to segment smaller objects, such as pedestrians, two-wheelers, or roadside obstacles. Through empirical validation, we observed that smaller dilation rates (below 6) limited the model’s ability to perceive broader scene context, resulting in poor segmentation of distant objects. Conversely, extremely large dilation rates (above 16) resulted in excessive sparsity within convolutional filters, degrading the detection of fine details. Therefore, a progressive multi-scale approach, spanning 10 dilation rates from 6 to 16, has been adopted to ensure dense contextual encoding without sacrificing fine spatial features.

The outputs of all the dilated convolution layers, along with the output of the pooling layer and the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$1 \times 1$$\end{document} convolution layer, are concatenated together along the channel dimension. The final output tensor has the same spatial dimensions as the input but a different number of channels. Each channel in the output can be interpreted as the model’s confidence that a particular class or object is present at each location in the input. The varying dilation rates allow the model to capture features at different scales without increasing the computational complexity of the model. This makes it particularly useful for tasks such as semantic segmentation, where it is crucial to maintain high-resolution spatial information while also capturing larger-scale contextual information.

Experiment

Dataset description

India driving dataset (IDD)

The India Driving Dataset (IDD)14, introduced in 2018, is designed to address the unique challenges posed by unstructured traffic environments prevalent in developing countries, such as India, where road rules are often loosely enforced and infrastructure is inconsistent. It comprises 10,003 finely annotated images distributed across 26 semantic classes, capturing a wide spectrum of real-world complexities, including:Ambiguous or poorly defined road boundariesHeterogeneous traffic participants (e.g., pedestrians, auto-rickshaws, livestock)High intra-class variability and occlusionDiverse lighting and weather conditionsThe dataset comprises both coarsely annotated (approximately 26,000 images) and finely annotated (10,003 images) subsets. In this study, we exclusively utilize the finely annotated subset containing 10,003 high-quality images, as it provides dense pixel-level semantic labels suitable for robust model training and evaluation.

The dataset was collected over 182 driving sequences and is partitioned into 6,993 training, 981 validation, and 2,029 test images. To facilitate focused analysis and improve generalization, the 26 original classes are consolidated into seven critical semantic categories: drivable area, non-drivable area, living beings, vehicles, roadside objects, far objects, and sky. This restructuring supports robust benchmarking in chaotic, real-world traffic scenarios that differ significantly from the structured environments of many Western datasets.

CamVid

The CamVid dataset13 serves as a classic benchmark for urban scene understanding and is widely adopted for semantic segmentation research. Image preprocessing

In this work, images and their corresponding annotation masks are loaded utilizing TensorFlow’s tf.io.read_file along with decoding functions such as tf.image.decode_jpeg and tf.image.decode_png. As part of the preprocessing step, special attention is given to the “not labeled” class; pixels assigned a value of 255 are remapped to a label value of 7 to maintain consistency with the predefined number of classes. To support faster model convergence during training, image pixel values are normalized to a [0, 1] range. Both images and masks are resized to a uniform resolution of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$256 \times 256$$\end{document} pixels using TensorFlow’s tf.image.resize method. Additionally, data augmentation strategies, including random horizontal flipping, are applied to the training data to improve model robustness and generalization capability.

Since the datasets used in this study have different native image resolutions, all input images and their corresponding segmentation masks were standardized to a fixed dimension of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$256 \times 256$$\end{document} pixels. Bilinear interpolation was employed for resizing the RGB images, while nearest-neighbor interpolation was used for resizing the segmentation masks to preserve class integrity. To reduce distortion, a center-crop operation was applied prior to resizing when aspect ratios varied significantly.

Experimental setup

Our experiments utilize the following computational setup: We employ a batch size of 8 with a buffer size of 1000 during training. The model has been implemented using Google Colaboratory’s infrastructure, which provided 12.7 GB of system RAM, 15.0 GB of GPU memory, and 78.2 GB of available disk storage. All input images have been resized to 256\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\times$$\end{document}256 resolution before processing. The training procedure consists of 50 epochs, with the encoder network initialized using weights pre-trained on ImageNet and subsequently fine-tuned on the India Driving Dataset (IDD). For optimization, we adopt the Adam optimizer with a fixed learning rate of 0.001 and implement Sparse Categorical Cross-Entropy as our loss function. The training dynamics, including accuracy and loss progression, are visualized in Figs. 7, 8, respectively. The complete source code and implementation are publicly accessible through our GitHub repository:https://github.com/Pritamresearch/IndiVnet.

IndiVNet employs a categorical cross-entropy loss function applied over softmax activations. The softmax function11\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \text {Softmax}(z_i) = \frac{e^{z_i}}{\sum _{j} e^{z_j}} \end{aligned}$$\end{document}is smooth and differentiable for all finite inputs \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$z_i$$\end{document}.

The corresponding cross-entropy loss12\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \mathscr {L}(y, \hat{y}) = -\sum _{i} y_i \log (\hat{y}_i) \end{aligned}$$\end{document}is also differentiable except at points where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hat{y}_i = 0$$\end{document}, which are avoided in practice through the properties of softmax. Therefore, the overall loss function is differentiable almost everywhere, enabling stable gradient-based optimization. Evaluation metrics

For evaluating segmentation performance, we employ Mean Intersection over Union (mIoU) rather than standard accuracy metrics. Traditional accuracy measurements can be misleading in segmentation tasks due to uneven class distributions and varying object sizes within scenes. For instance, “road” pixels typically dominate urban scenes, while “pedestrian” pixels are far fewer yet equally important.

mIoU addresses these limitations by measuring the precise overlap between predictions and ground truth across all classes. It accounts for three critical error types: false positives (misclassified pixels), false negatives (missed detections), and true positives (correct predictions). The class-specific IoU is calculated as13\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} IoU = \frac{TP}{TP + FN + FP} \end{aligned}$$\end{document}The overall mIoU metric then averages these values across all N classes as14\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} mIoU = \frac{1}{N}\sum _{i=1}^{N} IoU_i \end{aligned}$$\end{document}This approach provides a balanced evaluation, regardless of class frequency or object size, making it particularly valuable for complex urban scenes with diverse elements, such as vehicles, infrastructure, and dynamic objects.

The strong performance of IndiVNet, particularly on complex road scenes in the India Driving Dataset (IDD), can be attributed to several key design choices. First, the Indienco encoder introduces a progressive dilation strategy that expands the receptive field without compromising spatial detail, enabling the model to capture global context while preserving fine object boundaries. This is crucial for segmenting heterogeneous road scenes with significant visual clutter and occlusions. Second, the integration of low-level feature fusion allows the network to retain finer spatial information from early layers, which enhances boundary localization and improves segmentation of small and partially occluded objects frequent occurrences in Indian traffic conditions. Finally, IndiVNet has been developed using a dataset-driven approach, specifically targeting the characteristics of the India Driving Dataset (IDD), which reflects the real-world variability of Indian roads. This specific design enables the model to achieve a higher mIoU compared to several conventional models trained primarily on structured datasets. These architectural innovations collectively contribute to IndiVNet’s robustness and adaptability in challenging real-world environments.

Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs), such as IndiVNet, utilize dilated (or atrous) convolutions to expand the receptive field without increasing the number of parameters or computational cost. Specifically, a 3 \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\times$$\end{document} 3 kernel with a dilation rate of 3 effectively covers a 7 \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\times$$\end{document} 7 area, allowing the network to capture broader contextual information while maintaining computational efficiency.

In contrast, Transformer architectures utilize self-attention mechanisms that compute pairwise interactions between all input tokens, resulting in a time and memory complexity that is quadratic with respect to the input size (i.e., O(N\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$^{2}$$\end{document})). This quadratic scaling can pose challenges for real-time applications and resource-constrained environments.

TOPSIS analysis of the proposed model

To evaluate the performance of our proposed model, IndiVnet, we performed a TOPSIS (Technique for Order Preference by Similarity to Ideal Solution) analysis45, which is widely used for multi-criteria decision-making problems. The objective of this analysis is to compare the performance of various models in terms of three criteria: mIoU, Dice Score, and Resolution. The decision matrix is created with models as alternatives and performance metrics as criteria. The metrics considered are mIoU (%), Dice Score (%), and Resolution (pixels). The models are listed in Table 3.

Three primary factors contribute to the model’s this performance. Firstly, inherent biases in the training data significantly impact the results. The India Driving Dataset (IDD) contains only 4.7% night scenes compared to 29% encountered in real-world driving conditions. Culturally specific objects, such as bullock carts, represent less than 0.1% of pixel annotations. Secondly, architectural constraints of the current implementation present challenges: the dilated convolution design struggles with abrupt occlusion boundaries, and the fixed dilation rates lack adaptability to varying object densities in complex traffic scenarios. Finally, sensor limitations in the training data restrict performance, as the model processes RGB-only input without LiDAR or thermal information that could improve nighttime detection. Additionally, 8-bit quantization during image preprocessing leads to the loss of critical low-light details that could otherwise enhance dark scene segmentation. These interconnected factors collectively explain the observed performance gaps in occlusion handling, night-time operation, and rare object detection.

The fluctuations observed in the validation accuracy and loss (Fig. 6) primarily arise from class imbalance within the India Driving Dataset (IDD), where dominant classes, such as road and sky, occupy a disproportionate number of pixels compared to minority categories, like pedestrians or animal carts. This imbalance can cause transient spikes in validation loss, despite the training loss steadily decreasing. To mitigate these effects, future work will explore class-balanced loss formulations such as focal loss and median-frequency balancing, along with adaptive data augmentation targeting underrepresented semantic categories. Additionally, progressive learning rate scheduling and mini-batch re-weighting will be incorporated to stabilize validation dynamics. These measures can reduce oscillations while enhancing the stability of minority-class segmentation. Nonetheless, we note this phenomenon as a limitation inherent to real-world Indian traffic datasets, where achieving true class balance is nearly impossible.

The FPS decreases moderately from 17 to 16 because dilated convolutions increase the effective receptive field without additional parameters, offering a highly favourable accuracy–efficiency trade-off. Increasing the encoder channel width to 512 filters (Stage–3) further improves accuracy to 69.98% mIoU (+2.26), but FPS decreases from 16 to 15. Although wider feature maps impose additional memory and computation, the gain in representational richness stabilises object boundaries and rare-class predictions. This trade-off is considered acceptable because the model continues to operate near real time.

Replacing the simple decoder with the lightweight refinement decoder (Stage–4) improves accuracy slightly (70.48% mIoU, +0.50) while reducing FPS from 15 to 14. The additional cost arises from multi-level feature fusion and refinement, but the improved spatial consistency and edge sharpness justify the overhead, especially for safety-critical perception tasks.

Incorporating class-balanced loss weighting (Stage–5) increases rare-class IoU significantly (from 46.2% to 51.1%) with negligible impact on both mIoU (70.32%) and FPS (14). This addition was chosen because IDD contains multiple long-tail classes, and improved robustness across these categories is desirable even without a global accuracy increase.

The cumulative ablation reveals that IndiVNet’s design prioritises a strong accuracy–efficiency balance. The most impactful component is progressive dilation, which yields large accuracy gains with minimal speed penalty. Subsequent enhancements contribute smaller accuracy improvements but refine important aspects such as boundary precision and long-tail performance. The final model achieves 70.48% mIoU at 14 FPS, which represents an optimal trade-off between segmentation quality and real-time feasibility for unstructured autonomous driving environments.

Conclusion and future scope

This study introduced IndiVNet, a region-aware semantic segmentation framework designed specifically for the visual and structural complexities of Indian driving environments. By combining progressive dilation encoding with a context–spatial fusion decoder, IndiVNet effectively balances global contextual reasoning with fine-grained boundary precision. The model achieves a mean IoU of 69.98% on the India Driving Dataset, outperforming several strong lightweight baselines while operating at a lower input resolution. Beyond performance metrics, this work highlights the value of geography-aware model design in autonomous driving research. The findings underscore that region-specific priors, cultural scene elements, and environment-dependent feature distributions are essential for developing robust perception systems capable of operating reliably in diverse real-world traffic ecosystems.

Despite its strong performance, IndiVNet is subject to several limitations. The data distribution of IDD poses inherent challenges: night-time images constitute only 4.7% of the dataset, whereas real-world driving involves nearly 29% low-illumination conditions, limiting the model’s ability to generalise to dark scenes. Similarly, culturally specific objects such as bullock carts appear in less than 0.1% of annotated pixels, restricting reliable detection of rare but safety-critical classes. Architecturally, the fixed dilation rates in the Indienco encoder struggle with abrupt occlusion boundaries and do not adapt dynamically to variations in object density. Sensor constraints further limit performance, as the dataset provides only 8-bit RGB imagery without depth, LiDAR, or thermal cues, and quantisation-induced luminance loss reduces fidelity in low-light regions. These factors collectively explain the remaining gaps in night-time segmentation, rare object detection, and occlusion handling.

To address these challenges and extend the capabilities of IndiVNet, several research directions are planned. First, incorporating spatial and channel attention mechanisms or lightweight transformer modules may improve discrimination in occlusion-heavy or visually cluttered scenes. Second, integrating attention-guided dynamic feature recalibration could allow the network to selectively emphasise contextually important regions during segmentation. Third, large-scale self-supervised pretraining on unlabeled Indian driving data promises improved domain robustness and generalisation to unseen conditions. Fourth, edge-oriented optimisations such as quantisation-aware training, structured pruning, and mixed-precision inference will be explored to support real-time deployment on embedded platforms. Finally, developing a cross-domain continual learning framework would enable the model to adapt incrementally to regional, seasonal, and infrastructural variations without catastrophic forgetting. Together, these directions form a comprehensive roadmap for advancing region-specific semantic segmentation in complex, real-world autonomous driving scenarios.
