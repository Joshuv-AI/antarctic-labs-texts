Functional Dissociation of Mismatch Negativity From Late Discriminative Negativity Across the Adult Lifespan
Authors: Ricky Chow, Jennifer A. Bugos, Claude Alain, R. Shayna Rosenbaum
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe mismatch negativity (MMN) is elicited by infrequent acoustic changes (such as pitch, frequency, and duration of tones) and by violations of higher‐order, abstract auditory patterns. In these latter cases, the MMN can be accompanied by a lesser‐known event‐related potential termed the late discriminative negativity (LDN). To date, examination of the LDN has been primarily centered on childhood development; whether the LDN is reliably elicited across the adult lifespan and how its functional significance differs from the MMN remain unclear. This study examined the MMN and LDN recorded from 92 adults (18–86 years, 47 females) elicited by changes in pitch contour within short (500 ms) five‐tone sequences. Subsequent incidental memory for the standard and deviant tone sequences was tested against similar lures and dissimilar foils. Data‐driven analyses showed that the LDN was reliably visible from young to older adulthood and remained stable through adulthood, in contrast to age‐related declines in MMN. Furthermore, MMN amplitude was robustly associated with age and subsequent memory performance, whereas LDN amplitude showed weak and inconsistent associations with age and hearing acuity. Results extend prior developmental work by revealing a reliable LDN throughout adulthood and are consistent with the LDN as a marker of prediction error for higher‐order, gestalt properties of auditory perception. Taken together, the MMN and LDN are both generated by discrepancies between incoming sensory input and predicted regularities but are temporally distinct and show differential associations with age and memory performance, suggesting a functional dissociation between early and late indices of oddball detection.

Introduction

A classic demonstration of the mismatch negativity (MMN)—an early, negative deflection associated with deviance detection (Näätänen et al. 2007, 2019; Näätänen and Michie 1979)—features an early (i.e., 150–250 ms) frontocentral negativity elicited with auditory oddball paradigms, where standard and deviant tones differ in some acoustic feature, such as pitch, duration, or intensity. Research has further shown that the MMN can also be elicited from abstract, higher‐order violations of regularities, such as standard and deviant tone sequences differing in pitch contour (i.e., the temporal arrangement or pattern of tones within a sequence as in Alain et al. 1998, 1999; Fujioka et al. 2004; Tervaniemi et al. 2001, 2006; Trainor et al. 2002; see Paavilainen 2013 for review). In some cases, oddball paradigms involving such higher‐order violations feature a subsequent deviance‐related negativity, termed the late discriminative negativity (LDN, Cheour et al. 2001; Peter et al. 2012; Zachau et al. 2005; sometimes referred to as the late negativity [LN] in studies of vowel or phoneme discrimination—Yu et al. 2017). The LDN shares similarities with the MMN in scalp distribution, and both are elicited without explicit attention to stimulus changes (Liaukovich et al. 2022; Shestakova et al. 2003). However, the conditions that reliably elicit the LDN, and how the LDN relates to the MMN in terms of its functional significance, remain unclear (Wetzel and Schröger 2014). The current study aimed to examine deviance‐related potentials generated using a pattern‐deviant oddball paradigm reported from a previous study (Chow et al. 2025), with a systematic focus on examining the functional significance of the LDN in relation to the MMN across the adult lifespan.

Research involving the LDN has predominantly focused on infant and childhood development (Bishop et al. 2011; Čeponienė, Kushnerenko, et al. 2002; Čeponienė, Yaguchi, et al. 2002; Cheour et al. 2001; Datta et al. 2010; Gumenyuk et al. 2004; Halliday et al. 2014; Martynova et al. 2003; Maurer et al. 2003; Morr et al. 2002; Shafer et al. 2005; Shestakova et al. 2003; Yang et al. 2022; see Wetzel and Schröger 2014 for review), with limited attention to how it manifests across the adult lifespan. In children, the LDN was first reported in 5‐ to 10‐year‐olds as peaking at about 400–430 ms in response to oddball violations involving word stimuli and was originally termed the “late” MMN (Korpilahti et al. 1995). Since then, studies using various oddball paradigms with phonological or complex stimuli have revealed an LDN following the MMN, peaking at about 300‐ to 600‐ms post‐stimulus onset (Bishop et al. 2011; Cheour et al. 2001; Shafer et al. 2005; Sharma et al. 2025; Zachau et al. 2005). One study reported an LDN in 4‐ to 6‐year‐old children, recorded using an oddball paradigm with French vowels or musical tones, which increased in amplitude following training (Moreno et al. 2015), though other intervention studies have revealed mixed effects (Kujala et al. 2024; Putkinen et al. 2013).

In young adults, the LDN has been reported for violations of pitch contour (Zachau et al. 2005) and duration‐related stress patterns in tone pairs (Peter et al. 2012). It has been observed in studies using five‐tone sequences (Liaukovich et al. 2022), naturalistic, spectrally complex auditory stimuli (Horváth et al. 2009), and interleaving tone sequences differing in underlying patterns (Bendixen et al. 2012). One study with older adults reported an MMN at a somewhat later latency than expected (which the authors inferred as a possible LDN) using tone triplets with an occasional deviant within only the second tone (Neubert et al. 2021). Other paradigms have elicited an LN in young adult listeners examining the effect of language experience on phonological or lexical tone discrimination (i.e., monolinguals and bilinguals), finding effects of linguistic experience on both the MMN and LN to vowel contrasts and lexical tone contrasts to unfamiliar languages (Datta et al. 2020; Jakoby et al. 2011; Kaan et al. 2007, 2008; Yu et al. 2017, 2018). One study reported smaller LDN amplitudes to phonological vowel contrasts in a combined sample of adolescents and young adults (14–23 years) compared to children (8–12 years; Hommet et al. 2009). Another study in young adults revealed that the LDN amplitude was modulated by phonological complexity of deviants (David et al. 2020). Although research has identified maturational changes in this late negativity (e.g., from infancy to 4 years of age in Yu et al. 2019), the functional significance of the LDN in healthy adults and its trajectory from young to older adulthood remains unclear.

The functional significance of the LDN may be analogous to that of the MMN in the context of predictive coding (Friston 2005, 2018) and as a signal of prediction error—that is, an index sensitive to perceptual differences between predicted and actual sensory input (Baldeweg 2007; Friston 2005; Garrido et al. 2009; see Winkler and Czigler 2012 for review). According to this framework, the brain generates predictions about sensory inputs based on an internal model of past experiences. Predictive coding models propose a hierarchical architecture in which bottom‐up sensory inputs are compared to top‐down predictions, and mismatches propagate these prediction errors to higher‐order levels (Friston 2005; Wacongne et al. 2011). In auditory oddball paradigms, an internal model formed from regularities of standard stimuli is violated by a quantifiable acoustic difference of the rare deviant stimulus, thereby eliciting the MMN (Garrido et al. 2009; Wacongne et al. 2012; but see May 2021). However, the functional significance of the LDN may differ from that of the MMN. For instance, the LDN may be related to a transfer process from short‐ to long‐term memory (Peter et al. 2012; Zachau et al. 2005), selective attention (Mueller et al. 2008), automatic preparation for detecting changes to subsequent stimuli (Alho et al. 1994; Näätänen et al. 1982), or reorienting of attention towards task‐irrelevant or back to task‐relevant information (Gumenyuk et al. 2004; Mueller et al. 2008; Shestakova et al. 2003; Wetzel et al. 2004). Another interpretation is that the LDN reflects higher‐order (i.e., global) changes in auditory discrimination (Čeponienė et al. 2004; Huang et al. 2024; Zachau et al. 2005). Indeed, a separate “late” deviance‐related negativity tends to be generated from violations of the higher‐order structure of complex, multi‐tone sequences (Peter et al. 2012; Zachau et al. 2005), naturalistic sounds (Horváth et al. 2009), lexical tone contrasts (Kaan et al. 2007, 2008; Yu et al. 2017, 2018), and vowel contrasts (Datta et al. 2020; Yu et al. 2019), suggesting that violations in abstract regularities elicit an early and a later negativity. Using computational methods, prior work has proposed that the MMN and LDN may index prediction error of local and global auditory features, respectively (Huang et al. 2024). In this view, the MMN may show sensitivity to local perceptual violations (such as early detection of violations in pitch or initial violations of pitch contour relations) and the LDN to global perceptual violations of gestalt features (i.e., auditory prediction of local features that accrues over time; Schneider et al. 2022; Wacongne et al. 2011, 2012), such as detection of violations of overall pitch contour.

More recently, the MMN and LDN were elicited in a study with young adults that tested for transfer of sensory memory to long‐term memory (Herman et al. 2023), specifically memory precision (i.e., mnemonic discrimination—the ability to distinguish between similar items or events in memory). In this study, individual differences in the pattern‐deviant MMN (from five‐tone sequences) were shown to be sensitive to a subsequent recognition test for standard and deviant tone sequences (i.e., old target sequences) against highly similar lure sequences (i.e., novel tone sequences that were similar in mean frequency and in pitch contour). A larger MMN amplitude was associated with better memory precision for the same stimuli that elicited this signal. Visual inspection of the waveforms revealed a second, later negativity, which was not analyzed in that study and was referred to as the LDN. Chow et al. (2025) extended this work by showing age‐related degradations in sensory representations across the adult lifespan, as measured by the complex MMN. In this study, enhanced MMN amplitudes to pattern deviants were associated with enhanced precision in subsequent memory discriminability while controlling for age. Furthermore, MMN amplitude was shown to predict lure discriminability beyond the predictive value of age and hearing acuity. Critically, through mediation analyses, MMN amplitude was found to partially explain age‐related declines in subsequent precision in memory performance, but not for the general old/new effect. Similar to Herman et al. (2023), the LDN was also visible in middle‐aged and older adults, but analyses of LDN amplitude in relation to age, peripheral hearing ability, and behavioural performance were beyond the scope of that study.

In the present study, we investigated whether the LDN is present across the adult lifespan, as well as the functional similarities and differences between the MMN and LDN by comparing their respective effects with age, hearing acuity, and their relationship with subsequent performance on an incidental test of episodic memory. The relationship between MMN and LDN amplitudes with age was not examined in Chow et al. (2025), nor how each component differentially changes with age and perceptual abilities (i.e., hearing acuity and perceptual discrimination) and therefore represent novel analyses. Knowledge of the relationship between the MMN and LDN that considers age and hearing acuity will contribute to the literature on whether the LDN may be a reliable marker of perceptual processing and sensitivity to episodic memory across the adult lifespan. To this end, new analyses were conducted on the same sample of participants (92 adults, 18–86 years, 47 females) reported in Chow et al. (2025) who were administered an oddball paradigm that generated a pattern‐deviant MMN and the LDN. We expected that the LDN, as a negativity elicited by higher‐order violations of regularities, would index factors that influence global, gestalt perception, such as old–new recognition performance (as indexed by target–foil discriminability) rather than violations of local properties of sensory input.

Materials and Methods

Participants

Middle‐aged and older adults (i.e., participants aged between 35 and 86 years) were recruited through the participant database at the Rotman Research Institute, as well as via online advertisements and community outreach within the Greater Toronto Area in Ontario, Canada. Young adults (i.e., participants aged between 18 and 35 years) were recruited from the York University undergraduate research participant pool and the surrounding community; their data are detailed in Herman et al. (2023). Whereas middle‐aged and older adults received monetary compensation, young adult participants were compensated either with monetary payment or course credit applicable to their undergraduate introductory psychology course. Eligibility requirements for all participants included self‐reported fluency in English and no history of neurological conditions (such as stroke, transient ischemic attack, or traumatic brain injury), formal diagnoses of mood disorder, substance use disorders, or learning disabilities. Participants were excluded if they were currently undergoing chemotherapy or radiation therapy to the head or neck region or if they were taking medications known to influence cognitive functioning (e.g., antidepressants or antipsychotics). Additional exclusion criteria included the use of hearing aids or self‐reported hearing impairments. Furthermore, none of the middle‐aged or older participants had undergone a neuropsychological evaluation within the 6 months prior to testing. For those aged 60 or older, eligibility was confirmed by a score above 30 on the modified version of the Telephone Interview for Cognitive Status (TICS‐m) to ensure the absence of substantial cognitive decline.

All participants underwent pure‐tone audiometry to confirm age‐appropriate hearing levels (HL). Young adults exhibited normal bilateral pure‐tone audiometric (PTA) thresholds, with values consistently below 25 dB HL across octave frequencies ranging from 250 to 8000 Hz. Data from one middle‐aged and two older participants were excluded due to moderate hearing loss, defined as average thresholds exceeding an average of 35 dB HL between 250 and 1000 Hz in both ears. Additionally, data from two middle‐aged individuals were excluded due to asymmetric hearing thresholds between the ears. All participants reported either normal or corrected‐to‐normal vision.

Data were excluded from two young adults, three middle‐aged adults, and two older adults due to a failure to follow task instructions. Data from one young and one middle‐aged participant were excluded after falling asleep during the EEG recording. Furthermore, data from four young adults were excluded based on below‐chance performance on the pitch contour discrimination task conducted during the back‐to‐back discrimination phase.

The final sample for data analysis consisted of 32 older adults (60–86 years, 15 female), 30 middle‐aged adults (36–59 years, 14 female), and 30 young adults (18–32 years, 18 female), as reported in Chow et al. (2025). A chi‐square test revealed no significant differences in sex distribution across the groups, χ2(2, N = 87) = 2.11, p = 0.348. The experimental protocol was approved by the Research Ethics Boards of York University and the Rotman Research Institute at Baycrest Centre, and all participants provided informed written consent.

Neuropsychological Assessment

Older adult participants and a subset of middle‐aged adults completed the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA; Nasreddine et al. 2005) as a brief screening tool for global cognitive functioning. To evaluate symptoms of anxiety and depression, all participants also completed the Generalized Anxiety Disorder 7‐item scale (GAD‐7; Spitzer et al. 2006) and the Patient Health Questionnaire 8‐item scale (PHQ‐8; Kroenke et al. 2009), respectively. Participants aged 60 and above were administered additional neuropsychological assessments to confirm age‐appropriate cognitive functioning. Older adult participants completed the Vocabulary and Matrix Reasoning subtests from the Wechsler Abbreviated Scale of Intelligence (Wechsler 2008) as estimates of crystallized and fluid intelligence. The Digit Symbol Coding subtest from the Wechsler Adult Intelligence Scale, 3rd Edition (WAIS‐III), was used to assess processing speed. Psychomotor speed and executive functions, such as cognitive flexibility and inhibition, were evaluated with the Trail‐Making Test (Reitan and Wolfson 1985). Memory performance was measured via the California Verbal Learning Test, Third Edition (CVLT‐3; Delis et al. 2017), along with the Incidental and Free Recall components of the WAIS‐III Digit Symbol Coding Test. The WAIS‐IV Digit Span subtest (Wechsler 2008) was used to assess auditory attention and working memory. To examine language and executive functioning further, the Verbal Fluency subtest from the Delis–Kaplan Executive Function System (D‐KEFS; Delis et al. 2012) was administered, measuring phonemic and semantic fluency. Finally, because the stimuli in the main experimental paradigm differed in pitch contour, the Musical Ear Test (Wallentin et al. 2010) was used to evaluate musical aptitude in both melodic and rhythmic domains. Task Procedures

All participants completed two computerized tasks designed to assess mnemonic discrimination (i.e., precision in long‐term memory). To confirm that the older adult cohort exhibited performance consistent with previous samples, participants first completed the Mnemonic Similarity Task (MST), which assesses visual mnemonic discrimination using images of everyday objects and is recognized as a task that reliably places demands on pattern separation—a process that distinguishes separate yet similar events or items in memory (Stark et al. 2013). Following the MST, participants completed the Auditory MMN Memory Task (AMMT)—a novel task of mnemonic discrimination for unfamiliar auditory stimuli, previously referred to as the Auditory Pattern Separation Task in Herman et al. (2023). The MST was always administered before the AMMT, and participants were offered an optional break between tasks. Older adults aged over 60 completed these tasks on a different day from their neuropsychological assessment to minimize fatigue. All older participants completed both sessions within a 2‐month window, and no data were lost due to participant attrition.

MST

The MST was included to verify that participants exhibited mnemonic discrimination performance comparable to that reported in previous studies. The task consists of two main phases: an incidental encoding phase and a subsequent recognition test. During the encoding phase, participants viewed 128 images of everyday objects and judged whether each was an “indoor” or “outdoor” object. This was followed by a test phase comprising 192 images, during which participants were asked to classify each item as “old,” “similar,” or “new” in the context of the encoding phase. Test phase stimuli consisted of previously seen items (old targets), entirely new items (foils), and items that were perceptually similar but not identical to those shown during encoding (lures). Participants were randomly assigned to one of two equivalent stimulus sets (Set C or Set D). Responses were made using a computer keyboard. Older adults completed the MST in a double‐walled, sound‐attenuated booth using a desktop computer, although EEG was recorded—these EEG data will be reported elsewhere. Middle‐aged and younger adult participants performed the task on a Windows 7 laptop in a quiet room without the experimenter present. Each image was displayed for 2 s in both the encoding and test phases, with a 500‐ms ISI.

AMMT

The AMMT (Herman et al. 2023) consisted of three phases: an incidental encoding phase, a forced‐choice test phase, and a back‐to‐back discrimination phase. The stimuli included six distinct auditory tone sequences: two target types (one standard, one deviant), three lure types, and one foil type. Each tone sequence comprised five pure tones with each tone lasting 100 ms (i.e., 500 ms for the entire sequence) and had differing temporal arrangements of the tones so that they varied in pitch contour. Each tone sequence was generated using Audacity software (Version 2.3.3) with a 10‐ms fade‐in and 10‐ms fade‐out to prevent auditory clipping. Target and lure sequences were designed to be highly similar in pitch contour while maintaining overall pitch content. The foil sequence was made of the same pitches but down an octave so that it was perceptually distinct from the rest of the stimulus set. The frequency arrangements of the five tones in each tone sequence type are displayed in Table 2. The pitches were arranged to avoid forming recognizable musical scales or melodies, resulting in sequences that were perceptually amelodic and lacked tonal organization, thereby minimizing the influence of musical experiences.

Oddball Phase and EEG Acquisition

Participants were passively presented with two types of auditory tone sequences—a standard and a deviant—in a randomized order while watching a silent film for 25 min. Participants were instructed to attend to the movie, and adherence to the protocol was monitored by an experimenter via webcam from outside the sound‐attenuated booth. Across the session, the standard sequence was presented in 700 trials, whereas the deviant appeared in 300 trials. These trials were presented in a randomized order in a single continuous run, with breaks offered upon request. Data quality was monitored throughout EEG acquisition by a researcher outside the sound‐attenuated booth using real‐time visualization software. Each tone sequence lasted 500 ms from onset to offset and was followed by a jittered ISI ranging from 900 to 1150 ms with a rectangular distribution of 50‐ms steps. The sequences were designed to be similar in average frequency but distinct in pitch contour, allowing for perceptual discrimination.

EEG was recorded using 66 Ag/AgCl scalp electrodes with a BioSemi ActiveTwo amplifier system (BioSemi V.O.F., Amsterdam, the Netherlands). Electrode placement followed the international 10–20 system, with a common mode sense active electrode and a driven right leg passive electrode serving as ground references. Ten additional electrodes were positioned below the hairline to enhance coverage and monitor ocular activity. These included placements at both mastoids, the pre‐auricular points, lateral points beside the outer canthus of each eye, and inferior to the orbit of each eye, in addition to two extra frontotemporal sites. EEG data were continuously digitized at a sampling rate of 512 Hz with a bandwidth of DC to 100 Hz and stored offline for subsequent analysis.

Forced‐Choice Test Phase

Following the encoding phase, participants were tested on their memory for the tone sequences using a forced‐choice recognition task. This test phase included the same standard and deviant sequences from the encoding phase (i.e., targets), along with novel sequences that were either similar in pitch contour (lures) or dissimilar in pitch contour and overall pitch (foils). To ensure reliance on long‐term memory rather than short‐term memory, the test began after a brief 2‐ to 3‐min break during which participants received instructions from the experimenter.

Participants were asked to indicate via keyboard whether each auditory sequence (i.e., probe) had been previously heard during the encoding phase. The test set included the two original target sequences, three lure sequences that were matched in mean frequency but differed in pitch contour, and one foil sequence that differed in both frequency and contour. Each of the six sequence types (two targets, three lures, and one foil) was probed 10 times in a randomized order, resulting in 20 target trials, 30 lure trials, and 10 foil trials, for a total of 60 trials. The task was self‐paced, and each trial included a 500‐ms interval between the probe and the presentation of the response prompt.

Perceptual Discrimination Task

After the forced‐choice test phase, participants completed a back‐to‐back perceptual discrimination task to confirm their ability to perceptually distinguish among the different auditory sequences. This phase was not a primary focus of the current study; rather, it served to verify that participants could differentiate targets, lures, and foils during the earlier forced‐choice test phase from short‐term memory. In each trial, participants were presented with a pair of tone sequences played in succession, separated by a 500‐ms ISI. Participants were then asked whether the two sequences were the same or different. Each tone sequence was paired with itself (i.e., identical pair) and with every other sequence. The task had three conditions: identical, similar, and dissimilar pairs. Similar pairs were denoted as nonidentical sequence pairs between each target and lure sequences. Dissimilar pairs were nonidentical sequence pairs that included the foil sequence and all other sequence types. All nonidentical pairs were presented in both possible orders. In the identical condition, each of the six identical sequence pairs was presented four times in total (24 trials). Each unique non‐identical pair was presented twice, yielding 84 total trials: 24 identical, 40 similar, and 20 dissimilar trials. The task followed a forced‐choice format, was self‐paced, and responses were made using a keyboard.

Data Preparation

EEG Preprocessing

Event‐related potential (ERP) preprocessing was conducted using BESA Research software (Version 7.1; MEGIS GmbH, Gräfelfing, Germany). Continuous EEG recordings were re‐referenced to the average reference and digitally filtered using a 0.53‐Hz high‐pass filter (forward, 6 dB/octave) and a 40‐Hz low‐pass filter (zero phase, 24 dB/octave). EEG channels exhibiting substantial artifacts or signal drift—mainly due to head or body movements—were corrected using spherical spline interpolation (Picton et al. 2000), with no more than 10% of channels interpolated per dataset. To address ocular artifacts, eye movements were identified using a spatial components method based on the approach developed by Berg and Scherg (1994) and Ille et al. (2002). Spatial topographies that best accounted for blinks and vertical/lateral eye movements were calculated and subtracted from the continuous EEG to reduce contamination.

EEG data were then segmented into 1000‐ms epochs for each participant, including a 200‐ms pre‐stimulus baseline. Epochs were automatically screened for artifacts, and those with fluctuations exceeding ±60 μV (peak‐to‐peak) were excluded from further analysis. As previously reported in Chow et al. (2025), the percentage of trials excluded did not significantly differ across age groups: For young adults, an average of 7.58% (SD = 5.12%) of standard trials and 7.88% (SD = 5.48%) of deviant trials were rejected; for middle‐aged adults, 10.17% (SD = 10.61%) of standard and 10.54% (SD = 10.82%) of deviant trials were excluded; and for older adults, 7.20% (SD = 6.44%) of standard and 7.25% (SD = 6.33%) of deviant trials were removed. A one‐way ANOVA showed no significant group differences in trial rejection rates, F(2, 89) = 1.42, p = 0.248, ηp 2 = 0.031. Remaining artifact‐free epochs were averaged separately for each stimulus type (standard and deviant) and baseline‐corrected using the 200‐ms pre‐stimulus interval to avoid influence from preceding trials. The mismatch difference waveform was computed for each participant by subtracting the averaged standard waveform from the deviant waveform.

Behavioural Measures

In the MST test phase, trials were excluded from analysis if participants failed to respond within the 2500‐ms response window. Additionally, to control for inadvertent key presses, responses made within 200 ms were removed from accuracy calculations. On average, 3.77% (SD = 5.78%) of trials were excluded per participant, 2.01% (SD = 1.77%) for young adults, 3.63% (SD = 4.26%) for middle‐aged adults, and 5.68% (SD = 8.62%) for older adults. A Kruskal–Wallis test showed no significant differences in exclusion rates across groups, H(2) = 5.29, p = 0.071. Old–new recognition performance was assessed by subtracting the proportion of false alarms to foils (i.e., “old” responses to unstudied items) from the hit rate for targets (“old” responses to studied items). Mnemonic discrimination was quantified using the Lure Discrimination Index (LDI), defined as the difference between the proportion of lures correctly identified as “similar” and the proportion of foils incorrectly labeled as “similar” (i.e., similar | lure – similar | foil). Data for these analyses were based on a subset of 31 older adults, as one participant did not complete the MST due to color blindness, and data from another participant were excluded due to being an outlier on both LDI and old–new recognition scores. As reported in Chow et al. (2025), there was a group difference in LDI score (F(2, 88) = 14.10, p < 0.001, η p 2 = 0.243) where older adults showed lower LDI scores compared to middle‐aged (p < 0.001) and young adults (p < 0.001), and there was no difference between young and middle‐aged adults (p = 0.456). No group difference was found for old–new recognition, H(2) = 0.13, p = 0.935, η p 2 = 0.009.

In the AMMT forced‐choice test phase, two primary performance metrics were derived: target–foil discriminability (a standard old–new recognition measure calculated as z (old | targets) – z (old | foils)) and target–lure discriminability (reflecting mnemonic discrimination, calculated as z (old | targets) – z (old | lures)). Both metrics were computed using loglinear d‐prime values adjusted for extreme proportions following the method proposed by (Hautus 1995) using the psycho R package (Makowski 2018). Although standard tone sequences were presented more frequently than deviant ones during encoding, Wilcoxon signed‐rank tests revealed no significant differences in hit rates between these two target types: older adults, z = −0.419, p = 0.675; middle‐aged adults, z = −0.815, p = 0.415; young adults, z = −1.835, p = 0.064. Moreover, a repeated‐measures ANOVA on hit rates showed no significant interaction between age group and target subtype, F(2, 89) = 0.283, p = 0.755, η p 2 = 0.006.

For the back‐to‐back perceptual discrimination task of the AMMT, accuracy was calculated separately for three conditions: (1) correctly identifying identical pairs as same, (2) identifying similar pairs (i.e., target–lure or nonidentical lure pairs) as different, and (3) identifying dissimilar pairs (i.e., foil sequence paired with either target or lure sequence) as different. Data from one older adult were excluded due to not understanding instructions. There was a group difference in overall performance on the back‐to‐back discrimination phase, H(2) = 23.85, p < 0.001, η p 2 = 0.174, with young adults showing higher discrimination rates compared to middle‐aged (p = 0.039) and older adults (p < 0.001) and no difference between middle‐aged and older adults (p = 0.055; for a more detailed analysis of this phase, please see Chow et al. 2025). All behavioral preprocessing and statistical analyses were carried out in R (Version 4.2.3).

Data Analysis

ERPs

Visual inspection of the ERP waveforms revealed two prominent negative deflections in response to deviant stimuli compared to standard ones. The first was a negative component occurring approximately 200‐ to 400‐ms post‐stimulus onset, prior to stimulus offset, and was identified as the MMN. A second, later negative deflection was observed between 550‐ and 700‐ms post‐onset and was labeled the LDN. Using a data‐driven approach, cluster‐based permutation testing was applied to confirm the presence of the MMN and LDN. A two‐tailed paired‐samples permutation t‐test was conducted to compare responses to standard versus deviant stimuli across the whole sample. To evaluate age‐related differences in MMN amplitude, an independent‐samples permutation t‐test was also performed on the MMN difference waveforms, comparing young and older adults—representing the extremes of the adult age spectrum. ERP data were analyzed using BESA Statistics (Version 2.1; MEGIS GmbH, Gräfelfing, Germany), which provides cluster‐based permutation testing and topographic ANOVA for a data‐driven statistical approach that accounts for all electrodes and time points while rigorously correcting for multiple comparisons.

Each permutation analysis involved two main stages. First, parametric t‐tests were computed at each electrode and time point: within‐subject t‐tests for standard versus deviant comparisons and between‐subject t‐tests for cross‐group comparisons. Data points falling below an initial cluster‐forming alpha threshold of 0.05 were grouped into spatiotemporal clusters. The t‐values within each cluster were summed to produce a cluster‐level statistic (cluster value). In the second phase, Monte Carlo permutation testing was conducted. This involved randomly reassigning condition or group labels across 5000 permutations to generate a null distribution of maximum cluster values. The observed cluster values were then compared against this distribution to calculate Monte Carlo p‐values, representing the proportion of permutations in which the random cluster exceeded the observed one. This approach identifies statistically significant spatiotemporal clusters that reflect amplitude differences between conditions or groups. For cluster identification, spatial neighbors were defined as electrodes within a 4‐cm radius. For a comprehensive explanation of these permutation methods as implemented in BESA Statistics, see Maris and Oostenveld (2007). Permutation testing was followed by additional analyses confirming the presence of the LDN across the entire sample (within‐subjects ANOVA between standard and deviant trials), which were not conducted previously in Chow et al. (2025). Results of these analyses within each group (paired‐sample t‐tests for young, middle‐aged, and older adults) were previously reported in the supplementary materials of Chow et al. (2025). Amplitudes were averaged across a cluster of seven frontal and frontocentral electrodes (AFz, F1, Fz, F2, FC1, FCz, and FC2) averaged across a time window informed by visual inspection and permutation testing (i.e., 550–700 ms) and comprise the same values used for correlation and regression analyses as described below.

Correlation and Regression Analyses

Peak latencies for the MMN and LDN were also subjected to analyses (which were not examined in Chow et al. 2025). Latency extraction was done after additional smoothing with a low‐pass 20‐Hz filter (zero phase, 24 dB/octave) given individual variability on this difference‐wave measure. For the MMN, peak latency was defined as the time point of maximal negativity within a broadly defined window of 150–450 ms; for the LDN, peak latency was similarly extracted within a window of 500–750 ms. These peak latencies were averaged over a cluster of seven electrodes from frontal and frontocentral sites (AFz, F1, Fz, F2, FC1, FCz, and FC2) consistent with Herman et al. (2023) and Chow et al. (2025). Two‐tailed bivariate Pearson correlations were conducted between each component and age.

As for associations with MMN or LDN with behavioural performance, two‐tailed bivariate Pearson correlations were conducted between MMN or LDN mean amplitude on one hand and behavioural performance on the other while controlling for age. Behavioural measures subjected to analyses include subsequent memory performance on the AMMT forced‐choice test phase (i.e., d‐prime for target–lure or target–foil discriminability) and hit rates for discriminating back‐to‐back stimulus pairs on the AMMT perceptual discrimination task (i.e., accuracy on discriminating between similar pairs and dissimilar pairs). As the MMN largely tends to be maximal at frontal and frontocentral electrode sites, the mean amplitudes of the MMN and LDN for each participant were derived from the same cluster of seven electrodes from frontal and frontocentral sites averaged across time windows informed by visual inspection and permutation testing, that is, 200–400 ms for the MMN and 550–700 ms for the LDN.

Correlation coefficients are accompanied by bias‐corrected and accelerated (BCa) 95% confidence intervals (CIs), computed using 1000 bootstrap resamples. Due to the number of pairwise correlations examined, we also report their corresponding Bayes' factors (BF10). In line with standard practice, a BF10 greater than 10 is interpreted as strong support for the alternative hypothesis, whereas a BF10 less than 0.10 is taken as strong evidence for the null (van Doorn et al. 2021). Because MMN and LDN amplitudes are typically negative at frontocentral sites, more negative values typically denote a larger MMN or LDN amplitude. However, for clarity in interpretation, the sign of MMN and LDN amplitude values has been reversed (i.e., negative values are expressed as positive, and vice versa). As a result, a positive correlation coefficient indicates that a greater MMN or LDN amplitude is associated with higher discriminability or greater bilateral hearing thresholds (i.e., worse hearing acuity).

Linear regression models were then conducted to examine the independent contributions of age and hearing thresholds on MMN and LDN amplitude. Bilateral pure‐tone thresholds for each participant were calculated by averaging thresholds from 250 to 1000 Hz across both ears, with this range selected to match the frequency content of the tone sequence stimuli. Both predictors were mean‐centered prior to analysis to reduce multicollinearity; all variance inflation factors (VIFs) were below 3 (age = 2.22, hearing threshold = 2.60, age by hearing interaction = 1.27). The initial additive model included age and hearing acuity as predictors of MMN or LDN amplitude. An age by hearing interaction was added to a second model to test for moderation effects. Nonparametric bootstrapping was conducted using 5000 iterations. BCa 95% CIs were computed for each regression coefficient.

Correlation and Regression Analyses

Peak Latency Analysis

Across the adult lifespan, MMN peak latencies showed strong evidence of age‐related slowing, r(90) = 0.306, 95% BCa CI [0.150, 0.468], p = 0.003, BF10 = 10.04. Peak latencies of the LDN were also prolonged with older age, r(90) = 0.283, 95% BCa CI [0.120, 0.454], p = 0.006, BF10 = 5.21. A one‐tailed Fisher's z‐test showed that these correlation coefficients were not significantly different from one another, z = 0.187, p = 0.426.

Age and Hearing Acuity

As shown in Figure 3, MMN amplitude declined with age across the adult lifespan, r(90) = −0.460, BCa CI [−0.614, −0.264], p < 0.001, BF10 = 4562.78. Given that a nonlinear pattern emerged upon closer visual inspection of the data, we tested for nonlinear effects of age on MMN amplitude using a generalized additive model (GAM). The smooth term for age was significant (effective degrees of freedom, edf = 2.48, F = 12.38, p < 0.001), indicating a nonlinear age‐related pattern rather than a strictly linear association. Model comparison indicated mixed results, with the GAM providing a better fit based on Akaike Information Criterion (AIC; GAM = 71.87; quadratic = 74.25; linear = 76.87), whereas Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC) values were similar across models (GAM = 83.98; quadratic = 84.34; linear = 84.43), suggesting that the observed nonlinearity was modest in magnitude.

ERP amplitudes were derived from a cluster of seven frontal and frontocentral electrodes (AFz, F1, Fz, F2, FC1, FCz and FC2) from 200 to 400 ms post‐stimulus onset for the MMN and 550 to 700 ms for the LDN.

In contrast, the LDN remained stable with age, with evidence for a null correlation between LDN amplitudes and age in years, r(90) = −0.086, 95% BCa CI [−0.309, 0.152], p = 0.415, BF10 = 0.181. A one‐tailed dependent‐samples Fisher's z‐test showed that these correlation values were significantly different, z = −3.31, p < 0.001. Hearing acuity also showed clear age‐related declines, with increased PTA thresholds with age when constraining for thresholds that overlapped in frequency of the AMMT tone sequence stimuli (i.e., averaged from 250 to 1000 Hz, r(90) = 0.723, p < 0.001). This correlation remained significant even when extended to higher frequencies (i.e., averaged from 250 to 8000 Hz) that represent age‐related presbycusis (r(90) = 0.834, p < 0.001). In examining whether deviance‐related potentials were associated with hearing acuity separate from the effects of age, partial correlation analyses showed a significant correlation between LDN amplitude and bilateral hearing thresholds while controlling for age, r(89) = 0.23, p = 0.031; however, this finding was contradicted by 95% bootstrapped confidence intervals (BCa CI [−0.03, 0.40]). The partial correlation between MMN amplitude and hearing thresholds while controlling for age did not reach significance, r(89) = 0.102, 95% BCa CI [−0.071, 0.279], p = 0.336.

Our previous work (Chow et al. 2025) examined the relationship between MMN and behavioural performance while controlling for age and hearing acuity using a series of a priori forward stepwise regression models. These analyses demonstrated that MMN amplitude uniquely predicted mnemonic discrimination performance over and above age and PTA thresholds. In the present study, we shift the focus to age‐related changes in MMN and LDN amplitudes, examining how these components differentially decline with age and hearing loss. Specifically, we use multiple linear regression models with age, hearing acuity, and their interaction entered simultaneously to assess their independent contributions to MMN and LDN amplitude. Table 5 displays a summary of regression coefficients for each model. For the MMN, the initial additive model with age and PTA thresholds was significant, F(2, 89) = 12.53, p < 0.001, adjusted R 2 = 0.20. As expected, older age predicted smaller MMN amplitudes, B = −0.012, p < 0.001, 95% BCa CI [−0.016, −0.006]. Hearing acuity was not a significant predictor, B = 0.007, p = 0.336, 95% BCa CI [−0.005, 0.022]. When the age by hearing interaction term was added to the model, it did not significantly improve model fit over the initial model, ΔF(1, 88) = 0.02, p = 0.878, ΔR 2 < 0.001, and the age by hearing interaction was not significant, B = −4.46 × 10−5, p = 0.878, 95% BCa CI [−0.0007, 0.0006]. Therefore, the simpler model without the interaction was retained.

In summary, MMN amplitudes showed robust age‐related declines that were independent of the peripheral hearing acuity. In contrast, LDN amplitude remained stable across the adult lifespan, with Bayesian analyses supporting the absence of an age effect. Interestingly, higher bilateral PTA thresholds were associated with greater LDN amplitudes even after controlling for age; furthermore, older age was significantly predictive of smaller LDN amplitudes. However, these individual associations, albeit significant, were not theoretically meaningful given that the model did not reach significance, and age and hearing acuity explained only a small amount of the variance in LDN amplitude (i.e., 3.7%). No moderating effect of age and hearing acuity was found for either MMN or LDN amplitude. Therefore, analyses show weak independent associations of the LDN with age and hearing acuity.

AMMT Perceptual Discrimination Task

Given the skewed distributions of performance on the perceptual discrimination task, Spearman's rho (ρ) correlations are reported. Data from one older adult were excluded due to misunderstanding task instructions. Clear ceiling effects were observed in the identical condition (with 55 of 91 participants with a perfect score). As for the similar condition, zero‐order Spearman correlations indicated a positive association between MMN amplitude and hit rates for similar pairs (ρ(89) = 0.24, 95% BCa CI [0.02, 0.42]). However, this association was no longer evident after controlling for age (ρ(89) = 0.06, 95% BCa CI [−0.15, 0.25]), suggesting that the observed relationship was driven by shared age‐related variance. MMN amplitude was not significantly associated with discriminating dissimilar pairs (ρ = 0.10, 95% BCa CI [−0.11, 0.33]). LDN amplitude showed no significant association with discriminating similar pairs (ρ = −0.07, 95% BCa CI [−0.28, 0.15]) or dissimilar pairs (ρ < 0.01, 95% BCa CI [−0.22, 0.21]).

Discussion

The present study aimed to examine whether the LDN is reliably generated across the adult lifespan and how this deviance‐related potential may be distinct from the MMN. Many studies using oddball paradigms have typically elicited this deviance‐related component incidentally when the primary research focus was the MMN (including our own, Chow et al. 2025; Herman et al. 2023), with more recent studies now systematically examining the LDN as a primary research aim (e.g., Datta et al. 2020; David et al. 2020; Schneider et al. 2022). We show that the LDN and MMN were each presented as prominent deviant‐related negativities that appear to share similar frontocentral scalp distributions and that both components were reliably generated in young to older adulthood. Despite these descriptive similarities, the LDN showed a functional dissociation from the MMN. Across age, the MMN showed expected decreases in amplitude, whereas the LDN was stable into older adulthood. Furthermore, both components showed differential sensitivities to peripheral hearing acuity and subsequent memory performance: The MMN was sensitive to episodic memory for oddball stimuli, whereas the LDN showed a small but negligible relationship with hearing acuity. This functional dissociation suggests that the LDN and MMN index different stages of automatic auditory discrimination and are differentially sensitive to features of auditory perception and memory.

Contexts of LDN Generation

Our findings extend those of earlier studies reporting LDN in children and young adults by showing reliable LDN generation across the adult lifespan. Contexts of LDN generation from prior research have been unclear and further complicated by methodological differences, including the types of stimuli used—ranging from naturalistic sounds such as vowel phonemes to simple tones (Korpilahti et al. 2001; Shestakova et al. 2003)—as well as variations in attentional demands (e.g., active vs. passive listening tasks; Horváth et al. 2009; Liaukovich et al. 2022). Given the relatively late latency of the LDN found in the present study, this component may reflect prediction error generated by violations of expectations in response to deviations from the overall pitch contour near the offset of the 500‐ms tone sequence. The time course of this deviant‐related processing (i.e., 550–700 ms) suggests that automatic predictive coding mechanisms operating on sensory memory persevere beyond the first few hundred milliseconds past the conventional time window of the MMN. Furthermore, the LDN in the present study was generated using a passive paradigm with instructions to attend to a muted film. Findings are consistent with other studies in young adults that have reported the LDN in passive listening situations without a target detection task (Horváth et al. 2009; Liaukovich et al. 2022). Our findings therefore provide converging evidence that the LDN can be reliably generated across the adult lifespan, including middle‐aged and older adults, and in contexts that minimize the influence of attention reorienting and do not require effortful deployment of sustained attention or goal‐oriented behaviour.

Although the MMN has been shown to be generated from violations of abstract regularities, the LDN does not always follow in such cases. Some pattern‐deviant oddball paradigms employed violations in a single auditory feature in multi‐tone sequences (i.e., the pitch of an individual tone) rather than manipulating the entire temporal arrangements. For instance, in the abstract‐feature oddball paradigm employed by Tervaniemi et al. (2006), deviants were created by replacing only the penultimate tone or the last tone of a five‐tone sequence. Therefore, prediction error in these contexts may have been generated from violations of pitch expectations rather than of overall pitch contour—because only a single feature differed between the incoming and predicted sensory input—which may explain the absence of a clear LDN response. In another study, deviants with a predictable statistical temporal structure have been shown to modulate the MMN and P3a (a later deviance‐related positivity associated with attentional capture), which suggests higher‐level implicit learning of statistical regularities; however, no LDN was observed (Lecaignard et al. 2015). Some other studies examined a pattern‐deviant MMN in young and middle‐aged adults using two tones (500 and 2000 Hz) that alternated regularly in frequency (standard) or were repeated (deviant), where no late negativity was possible due to a relatively shorter epoch and ISI (Alain et al. 1998, 1999). Other studies demonstrating an LDN used higher‐order violations of abstract features, such as in naturalistic spectrally rich sounds that include a pitch glide component (Horváth et al. 2009) and interleaved sound sequences (Bendixen et al. 2012). We propose that the LDN reliably accompanies the MMN when generated by higher‐order violations of abstract regularities, such as changes in pitch contour of a tone sequence, feature‐complex stimuli, and vowel phonemes.

Functional Dissociation of the LDN From MMN

Our findings align with the proposed role of the LDN as a neural correlate for global violations in auditory processing. The latency of the LDN (approximately 550–700 ms) occurred after the offset of the tone sequence, whereas the earlier latency of the MMN suggests it was elicited by prediction errors based on low‐level acoustic features—specifically, pitch differences in the first two tones—prior to the offset of the tone sequence. This MMN occurred in the time window of 200–400 ms, which is relatively later than typical elicited responses (Näätänen et al. 2007; Pakarinen et al. 2007), and may be due to the use of pattern deviants (Bendixen et al. 2012; Paavilainen 2013). We propose that the MMN from the current study may reflect prediction errors arising from fine‐grained, detailed representations in sensory memory, whereas the LDN may show greater sensitivity to violations of global, gestalt‐like acoustic structures that operate on slightly longer timescales (Huang et al. 2024; Schneider et al. 2022). Evidence for this temporal dissociation is further supported by studies that manipulate local versus global regularities (where labels of “local” and “global” effects are used rather than a strict labelling of the MMN or LDN per se, such as in Bekinschtein et al. 2009). Research with marmosets has also supported such a hierarchical predictive coding framework; deviance detection of higher‐order auditory regularities across longer timescales involved greater contributions from anterior temporal and prefrontal areas, whereas midbrain and sensory regions were more sensitive to local, prediction errors operating on shorter timescales (Chao et al. 2018; Gelens et al. 2024; Jiang et al. 2022). Previous research has shown a network of bilateral auditory sources and inferior frontal involvement in MMN, which is proposed to also contribute to the LDN (Cheour et al. 2001; Huang et al. 2024; Rinne et al. 2000). Further evidence has suggested that prediction errors based on global auditory features can enhance accuracy and reaction times in target detection (Coy et al. 2023). In a hierarchical predictive coding framework, the LDN may represent prediction errors established from encoding higher‐level auditory features, which have been proposed to be associated with greater frontal contributions or greater distributed representations compared to MMN generation (Deouell 2007; Garrido et al. 2008; Huang et al. 2024; Wacongne et al. 2012).

Differential Associations With Auditory Perception and Memory

Findings indicated that, although MMN amplitude was more closely linked to age than to hearing threshold, the LDN was not substantially explained by either age or peripheral hearing abilities. Furthermore, the MMN did not show a clear relationship with hearing acuity when controlling for age. The relationship between MMN and peripheral hearing abilities has been mixed, as some studies report attenuated MMN amplitudes (Cai et al. 2015; Chen et al. 2016) or earlier latencies (Randazzo et al. 2023) with greater age‐related hearing loss, though another study reported no difference between older adults with normal hearing and hearing loss (Brückmann et al. 2021). In this research, the MMN was shown to be simultaneously associated with both age and hearing thresholds, but the independent contributions of each factor were not examined. Future research should clarify the independent contributions of hearing acuity and age to the conventional pitch‐deviant MMN. In contrast, the LDN showed a suppressor‐like pattern with age and pure‐tone thresholds, such that amplitude declines with increasing age but increases with increasing hearing loss. However, these associations, though statistically significant, were small in magnitude and accounted only for marginal variance in LDN amplitude (i.e., 3.7%) and are therefore not theoretically meaningful. These findings raise the possibility that the LDN may be more resistant to the effects of age or peripheral hearing abilities (compared to the MMN) given its putative role as a late index of gist‐like representation of deviance detection.

The present study offers a direct examination of the relationship between LDN amplitude and long‐term memory performance. Although our findings implicate the LDN as a largely perceptual ERP component, its proposed role as an index of gist‐based representations in deviance detection may extend to episodic memory retrieval. From this perspective, the LDN may support broad categorical or familiarity‐based judgments—such as old–new recognition performance—rather than mnemonic discrimination that relies on fine‐grained discrimination. However, our results provide no evidence for a relationship between the LDN and either mnemonic discrimination (i.e., target–lure discriminability) or old–new recognition (i.e., target–foil discriminability). The current findings are inconsistent with previous accounts proposing that the LDN indexes transfer of a sensory memory trace to long‐term memory (Peter et al. 2012; Zachau et al. 2005). It appears that the MMN, rather than the LDN, represents this transfer, given its unique association with subsequent memory performance for oddball stimuli as reported in the present analyses. Findings reinforce the interpretation that the fidelity of long‐term memory representations is linked to the fidelity of sensory representations indexed by the MMN rather than the LDN. Our results are also inconsistent with the view that this component reflects attentional reorienting towards task‐relevant information or task‐irrelevant distraction as proposed in prior accounts (Gumenyuk et al. 2004; Mueller et al. 2008; Shestakova et al. 2003; Wetzel et al. 2004). If the LDN indeed indexed such a reorienting process, one would expect this component to be correlated with behavioral measures—yet no such associations were observed. Results indicate that the LDN is unlikely to reflect mechanisms relevant for attentional orienting or long‐term memory and instead highlight the MMN as a more reliable predictor of subsequent memory performance across the adult lifespan. Nevertheless, further developmental research is necessary to fully understand the role of the LDN on perceptual processing.

Implications of the LDN to Aging

MMN amplitudes decline in healthy aging (Cheng et al. 2013; Ruzzoli et al. 2012); we extend this age‐related decline to pitch contour or pattern deviants, consistent with age‐related degradations of the fidelity of sensory representations (in this paper, likely pitch contour information from the first two tones). Further exploratory analyses showed evidence for a nonlinear decline across age, with a visually apparent steeper decline from young to early middle age (i.e., between 20 and 40 years of age)—though cohort effects are possible (e.g., from variability in lifetime auditory experience). These findings further reinforce age‐related decrements in sensitivity to prediction error signals generated within sensory memory, even for higher‐order, abstract predictions. In contrast, the LDN remained relatively stable in amplitude across age. This relationship is in stark contrast to other ERPs that decline in amplitude with age, including sensory‐evoked potentials across multiple sensory domains (Alain et al. 2022) and the ERP correlates of inhibitory control (Kropotov et al. 2016; Rabi et al. 2022; van Dinteren et al. 2014). The stability of the LDN in aging is therefore an unexpected finding that raises questions about whether aging is associated with preserved fidelity of higher‐order auditory gestalt features. As expected, MMN peak latencies were prolonged in older age, consistent with prior research (Chen et al. 2016; Cooper et al. 2006; Näätänen et al. 2012). LDN peak latencies also showed a similar pattern of age‐related slowing at a similar rate as the MMN.

Age‐related declines in automatic detection of low‐level, detailed auditory features, yet preserved detection of global auditory features, may also explain patterns of memory retrieval in aging that are influenced by perception. In healthy aging, declines in mnemonic discrimination of highly similar stimuli or overlapping representations are expected, whereas the old–new effect in recognition is usually spared (Koen 2022; Koen and Yonelinas 2016; Korkki et al. 2020; Stark et al. 2013). Intact perception of higher‐level gestalt representations in aging is in line with research showing that older adults increasingly rely on schema and familiarity‐ or gist‐based retrieval strategies (Devitt and Schacter 2016; Hess 1990; Koen and Yonelinas 2016; Schacter et al. 1997) and global feature‐matching strategies to aid memory retrieval (Huffman and Stark 2017). Therefore, one might expect declines in automatic discrimination of low‐level perceptual details (as indexed by the MMN) to mediate mnemonic discrimination, whereas global‐feature processing may account for old–new recognition performance. Indeed, in the present study, the MMN predicted age‐related declines in target–lure discriminability (first reported in Chow et al. 2025); however, no association was found between LDN and target–foil discriminability. This absence of an association may be due to limitations of the present auditory paradigm, namely, variability in foil hit rates (as discussed in Chow et al. 2025). Future research should further investigate how local and global prediction errors contribute to age‐related changes in memory retrieval.

Given that the present paper only examined LDN generation across the adult lifespan, it is yet unclear whether the LDN generated from deviance detection from newborns and children arises from the same mechanisms, given developmental changes in auditory discrimination and language acquisition. Indeed, one study reported that LDN peak amplitudes to pitch deviants were higher in adolescent children compared to both young and older adults (Mueller et al. 2008). However, the absence of a clear LDN in this adult sample (likely due to the use of simple pitch deviants) supports the view that the LDN is more reliably elicited by higher‐order auditory regularities. Further, the inconsistent nomenclature surrounding this late deviance‐related potential likely reflects its limited representation in the literature and continued uncertainty regarding its status as a distinct component from the MMN. The breadth of conditions that elicit such a component complicates strong functional or mechanistic dissociations from the MMN. Although research has referred to this late negativity component as an LDN (Bishop et al. 2011; Cheour et al. 2001; Gumenyuk et al. 2004; Halliday et al. 2014; Martynova et al. 2003; Moreno et al. 2015; Mueller et al. 2008; Peter et al. 2012), this negativity has been referenced by other labels depending on the paradigm and area of study. For example, this component has been referred to as such as the “late” MMN in comparison to the “early” MMN (Bauer et al. 2009; Haigh et al. 2019; Halliday et al. 2014; Korpilahti et al. 1995, 2001; Zachau et al. 2005), a late negativity (Datta et al. 2020; Strotseva‐Feinschmidt et al. 2015; Wottawa et al. 2022; Yu et al. 2017, 2018), an MMN to global versus local rule violations (Horváth et al. 2001), a separate subcomponent of the MMN (Huang et al. 2024), or a frontocentral negativity in response to global rule violations (Hsu et al. 2026). In some cases, authors avoid the term MMN altogether, instead referring to early and late mismatch responses (Grundei et al. 2023; Yu et al. 2019). Although these responses differ from the MMN in latency and have been associated with higher‐level global stimulus properties, such as phoneme identity or phonological representations of speech stimuli (Datta et al. 2020; Liu et al. 2014; Wottawa et al. 2022; Yu et al. 2017, 2019), late deviance‐related potentials are not specific to abstract or global rule violations (see Bendixen et al. 2012; Hsu et al. 2026; Schröger et al. 2007; van Zuijen et al. 2006). The breadth of conditions that elicit such a component complicates strong functional or mechanistic dissociations from the MMN. Accordingly, the present study adopts the term LDN descriptively, consistent with the dissociation reported in speech discrimination studies of healthy and clinical developmental populations (Alonso‐Búa et al. 2006; Cheour et al. 2001; Hommet et al. 2009; Liu et al. 2014; Martynova et al. 2003; Moreno et al. 2015; Sharma et al. 2025). Although the MMN and LDN from the present study show morphological similarities (viz., a frontocentral deflection elicited by oddball detection), our findings also suggest a degree of functional dissociation between the two responses while remaining consistent with prior conceptualizations of the LDN as a “late” MMN.

Limitations and Future Directions

Despite descriptive estimates of MMN and LDN scalp topography in the present study, whether these components share similar neural generators is unclear. Importantly, similarities in scalp topography do not necessarily imply shared or identical underlying neural sources. Research has shown that the MMN is generated based on a network comprising the right inferior frontal gyrus and bilateral sources in the primary auditory cortices and superior temporal gyri (Cooray et al. 2014; Deouell 2007). LDN generation may also share a similar network of neural generators and potentially engage in greater frontal or higher‐order auditory association areas at the source level when higher‐order predictions are violated (Huang et al. 2024; Wacongne et al. 2011). Future research should therefore examine functional connectivity between frontal and temporal sources that may differ in strength or source localization between MMN and LDN. The extent to which the LDN also differs from other deviance‐related potentials remains to be investigated. For example, the reorienting negativity (RON), a component elicited by active oddball paradigms with a distractor task (Berti and Schröger 2001; Schröger and Wolff 1998), is a late component that occurs in a time window that overlaps with the LDN (i.e., 400–700 ms; Wetzel et al. 2006) and that is likely specific to attentional reorienting. As the pattern‐deviant MMN increases in amplitude with larger magnitudes of prediction error (e.g., Alain et al. 1994), future manipulations may involve salience, task relevance, or hearing acuity to modulate the strength of the sensory memory trace and examine subsequent effects on long‐term memory. In addition, the forced‐choice memory paradigm used in the present study may not have optimally captured higher‐order or gist‐based processes hypothesized to relate to the LDN (as evidenced by variability in foil hit rates rather than expected ceiling effects), potentially contributing to null behavioural associations. Furthermore, given the cross‐sectional nature of the present study, conclusions about within‐individual aging trajectories and causal relationships between MMN/LDN measures and memory performance cannot be drawn.

Future studies may examine the extent to which personal experience with multilingualism, particularly with tonal languages, may influence the MMN and LDN given that multilingualism history of participants was not collected and represents a limitation of the present study. The amplitude of deviance‐related potentials was shown to be modulated by bilingualism status and language proficiency in adults (Datta et al. 2020; Jakoby et al. 2011). Speakers of Mandarin and other tonal languages may show larger MMN to pitch glides or pitch contours, as well as greater lateralization, compared to their monolingual peers (Bidelman and Chung 2015; Chandrasekaran et al. 2007). Recent research has also suggested lateralized auditory processing in aging, especially for complex auditory stimuli (Angenstein 2024), which were not investigated in the present study. The stimuli used here comprised abstract, pre‐experimentally unfamiliar amelodic tone sequences, which are not expected to elicit strong hemispheric asymmetries relative to speech‐ or language‐based stimuli. Indeed, MMN and LDN showed peak maxima at midline electrode sites, with largely bilateral scalp topographies across age groups (Figure 2; see also Chow et al. 2025). The absence of lateralized analyses nevertheless represents a limitation and can be explored in future research with more linguistically or acoustically complex stimuli. Future research may also continue to investigate the LDN in the context of schizophrenia and other disorders of psychosis. Indeed, in one study, the pattern‐deviant MMN and accompanying LDN (characterized as a “late” MMN) was found to be reduced in amplitude in individuals with schizophrenia, with greater LDN attenuation associated with worse scores on measures of social cognition (Haigh et al. 2019). Other research has investigated paradigms with auditory deviants of local and global regularities in people with disorders of consciousness (i.e., patients in a minimally conscious state or those in a vegetative state), finding that neural responses to global violations predicts better prognosis and later recovery (Perez et al. 2021). Future research should directly test the putative role of the LDN as a domain‐general marker of prediction error for higher‐order regularities, as well as its sensitivity to transfer from sensory memory to gist‐based long‐term memory.

Conclusion

The purpose of the present paper was to examine the MMN and LDN across the adult lifespan and to examine how these two electrophysiological markers of prediction error differ in their relationships with age, hearing acuity, and subsequent episodic memory performance. Given past speculative interpretations of the LDN, we conclude that this component is reliably elicited by higher‐order abstract regularities even in passive listening conditions and does not require an attentional component. Although the LDN has been a prominent marker of changes in auditory processing throughout childhood development, the current findings show that this component is not specific to early development and can be elicited in adulthood. Although the MMN and LDN appear to share scalp topographies, the current results further show a temporal and functional dissociation with age, hearing acuity, and subsequent long‐term memory representations for oddball stimuli, with the MMN showing greater sensitivity to age and long‐term memory and the LDN showing weak associations with age and hearing acuity. These findings reinforce the sensitivity of predictive coding mechanisms to low‐level acoustic features, such as pitch, which are reflected in the MMN, as well as to higher‐order, abstract features of overall gestalt changes, such as pitch contour, reflected in the LDN.
