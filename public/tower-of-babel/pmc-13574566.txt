The ImproveCareNow Registry: Prediction of different remission rates in pediatric ulcerative colitis
Authors: Jonathan Van Hecke, Gigi Veereman, Koen Huysentruyt, Lucas Wauters, Jeremy Adler, Shehzad Saeed, Elisabeth De Greef, and the ImproveCareNow Pediatric IBD Learning Health System
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesIdentifying predictors of clinical remission in pediatric ulcerative colitis (UC) will impact the therapeutic approach. We aimed to predict time to first (TFR), corticosteroid‐free (TCSFR, ≥3 months corticosteroid withdrawal), and sustained remission (TSR, ≥6 months).MethodsThis retrospective study analyzed de‐identified prospectively collected data from 2383 patients enrolled in ImproveCareNow (2007–2023). We included UC patients under 18 years at diagnosis. Remission was defined as a Pediatric UC Activity Index (PUCAI; <10 points). The Statistical Package for the Social Sciences version 29 with multiple imputation for missing data was used for statistical analysis. Cox regression identified predictors at diagnosis/enrollment (T0) or first visit (T1) on the outcomes (n = 1785). This study received ethics committee approval (23135‐ICN_UC).ResultsMean TFR was 177.7 days, predicted by disease extent (p > 0.05) at T0 and history of severe UC (hazard ratio [HR] = 1.142, p = 0.05) before inclusion. In patients not reaching remission at T1 (subgroup; n = 964), TFR was predicted by PUCAI (confounder; moderate HR = 0.867 or severe disease HR = 0.697, p < 0.1) at T1 and history of severe UC (HR = 1.292, p = 0.007) before T1 (mean 25 days SEM 0.7). Mean TCSFR was 569.6 days, predicted by PUCAI (only mild HR = 0.728 and moderate HR = 0.559, p < 0.001) and use of corticosteroids (HR = 0.668, p < 0.001) at T1 (mean 32 days SEM 0.9). Mean TSR was 631.0 days, predicted by PUCAI (only mild HR = 0.721 and moderate HR = 0.541, p < 0.001) and the use of oral aminosalicylates (HR = 0.793, p = 0.003) at T1 (mean 30 days SEM 0.7).ConclusionsSeveral clinical parameters in early disease course predict remission with increasing PUCAI as useful negative predictor.

INTRODUCTION

The disease course of patients with inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) varies even among individuals with the same IBD subtype (i.e., ulcerative colitis [UC] or Crohn's disease [CD]). 1 Identifying clinical predictors could help predict an unfavorable disease course and impact therapeutic strategies shortly after diagnosis. 2 In pediatric UC, disease progression and early treatment response within 3 months of diagnosis are important for long‐term prognosis. 1 The Predicting Response to Standardized Pediatric Colitis Therapy (PROTECT) study found that clinical remission early in the disease course, as measured by the Pediatric UC Activity Index (PUCAI, <10 points), was a prognostic predictor of both short‐ and long‐term remission. 3 , 4 , 5 Moreover, PUCAI has been shown to correlate well with the endoscopic disease severity. 6

However, few studies have examined predictors of time to first (TFR), corticosteroid‐free (TCSFR), or sustained remission (TSR) in pediatric UC patients. The PROTECT study reported clinical remission rates at 1 month (31%–67%) and corticosteroid‐free remission rates at 3 months (15%–53%) and at 1 year (38%). 3 The length of corticosteroid‐free remission or need for colectomy are important markers of therapeutic effectiveness with an impact on long‐term outcomes and quality of life. 7 The aim of this study was to predict TFR, TCSFR, and TSR in a large multicenter network of pediatric UC patients.

METHODS

Ethics statement

This research was not preregistered with(out) an analysis plan in an independent, institutional registry. The protocol for this study was approved by the ImproveCareNow Research Committee and the Ethics Committee of the Universitair Ziekenhuis Brussel (Brussels, Belgium) with protocol number 23135_ICN‐UC. The study protocol was established following the General Data Protection Regulation.

Patient population

This prospective study analyzed patient information provided by the ImproveCareNow (ICN) Network. The ICN Network consists of more than 100 centers in mainly the United States of America, but also the United Kingdom, Belgium, and Qatar. The ICN registry was formed in 2007 8 and contains prospectively collected information of existing and newly diagnosed IBD patients.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

This study only included newly diagnosed pediatric UC patients under 18 years of age at diagnosis. Patients had to be enrolled in ICN and have at least one follow‐up visit at a participating care center within 3 months diagnosis. This study included all registered patients between 2007 (start of ICN) and 2023 (April 23rd, date of protocol submission to EC).

The general exclusion criteria for all outcomes were: (i) patients older than 18 years of age at diagnosis of UC, (ii) patients with a change in disease phenotype after inclusion in ICN, (iii) patients with CD or IBD type unclassified, (iv) patients without a diagnosis specifying an IBD‐subtype within 3 months (92 days) of inclusion in ICN, (v) patients without inclusion and/or first visit in ICN within 3 months (92 days) of diagnosis. In addition, patients needed to have at least four follow‐up visits within 2 years of follow‐up to be included for analysis. There was no predefined interval between the visits. A de‐identified dataset was provided by ICN already taking into account specific criteria (i–iv).

Variables description

Upon inclusion in ICN and at each follow‐up visit, a case report form was completed. There were no pre‐arranged visit times. This study used data collected at diagnosis/enrollment in ICN (T0) and at the first follow‐up visit (T1), including age, sex, disease extent following the Paris classification 9 and disease severity following physician's global assessment (PGA) or PUCAI. PUCAI was not available at diagnosis. However, prior severe disease was reported at inclusion in ICN. The following cut‐offs were used for PUCAI: inactive (<10 points), mild (10–34 points), moderate (35–64 points), and severe disease (≥65 points). 10 Clinical remission was defined as a PUCAI of less than 10 points. 11 At each visit, the time from diagnosis, age, body mass index (BMI), and several laboratory parameters (albumin, C‐reactive protein (CRP), erythrocyte sedimentation rate [ESR], and hematocrit) were also registered. BMI was recalculated to z‐scores using the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention growth charts. 12 Treatment was classified according to the use of antibiotics, aminosalicylates, systemic corticosteroids, immunomodulators, and biologics. Small molecules (only tofacitinib) were not included in the analysis. The stratification of corticosteroid use by route of administration was not present.

Study outcomes

The outcomes of this study were TFR, TCSFR, and TSR. Corticosteroid‐free remission was defined as clinical remission (PUCAI < 10) without the use of any corticosteroids for at least 3 months (91 days). 13 Sustained remission was arbitrarily defined as clinical remission (PUCAI < 10) for at least 6 months (182 days). Demographic and clinical parameters (e.g. disease activity, laboratory values, and treatment) in early disease course (≤3 months) were analyzed to determine their relationship with the remission rates. To minimize a follow‐up gap, only patients with at least four follow‐up visits within 2 years after diagnosis were included.

Statistical analysis

De‐identified data were extracted into Microsoft Excel 2016 for cleaning and calculation of additional variables (e.g., different remission rates) and then into the International Business Machines Corporation Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) version 29 for statistical analysis. 14 PUCAI was categorized, as defined above. The missing values pattern of the data was analyzed and because of up to 37.2% of missing data in predictors, multiple imputation with 20 imputations was applied to variables with <50% missingness (Table S1). 15 , 16 Biologicals could therefore not be imputed. Outcomes for this study were analyzed on both original data and pooled imputed data according to Rubin's rules. 16

Descriptive statistics were calculated at baseline (T0) and first visit (T1) (see Tables S2 and S3). Normality was assessed using the Kolmogorov‐Smirnov or Shapiro–Wilk test. Group differences in continuous variables were analyzed using independent t‐tests or Mann–Whitney U tests, as appropriate.

The influence of age at diagnosis, gender, disease extent, health insurance, the history of severe UC, laboratory values, PGA, PUCAI, and treatment at T0 and/or T1 on TFR, TCSFR, and TSR was tested using a manual forward stepwise Cox regression analysis. Both significant (α < 0.05) and non‐significant (α < 0.1) variables from the univariate analyses were included in the multivariate Cox regression analysis (α < 0.05). Model selection was based on the Akaike Information Criterion 17 and Likelihood Ratio Tests. Basic assumptions for Cox regression analysis were verified (i.e., independence, no outliers, multicollinearity). The proportional hazard assumption was checked graphically via a log‐minus‐log curve and statistically by creating time‐dependent covariates. Confounding was assessed by reintroducing excluded variables based on the significance and χ 2‐value of the model, significance of the coefficients and clinical relevance. Interaction terms were not possible with multiple imputed data. Unless stated elsewhere, the performed statistical tests in this study were two‐tailed with a significance level α of 0.05.

RESULTS

Patient characteristics

Patient information was available for 6257 pediatric UC patients. Of these, 5853 patients (93.5%) were diagnosed with UC and were younger than 18 years at diagnosis. No patients were excluded based on another diagnosis or a change in IBD subtype. Ultimately, 2383 patients (38.1%) remained eligible for the study (Figure 1) with 1785 patients (28.5%) having at least four follow‐up visits within 2‐year follow‐up. Demographic information at T0 and T1 of the analyzed patients (n = 1785) is shown in Table 1. Most of them had pancolitis (63.0%) and 29.0% experienced at least one episode of severe disease prior to inclusion in ICN. PUCAI scores were recorded at 90.3% of visits. Mean follow‐up was 43.9 months (standard deviation [SD] 27.9), with a mean of 12.0 total visits (SD 7.2), including 7.2 visits (SD 3.1) within 2 years after diagnosis. Table 2 shows TFR, TCSFR, and TSR at different time points after inclusion in ICN.

TFR

Of the 1706 patients (95.6%) reaching clinical remission during their follow‐up in ICN, the median TFR was 77.0 days (95% confidence interval [CI] 72.1–81.9) and the mean TFR was 177.7 days (95% CI 165.0–190.4). Graphical representation of TFR is shown in Figure S1. Table 3 shows the results of the multivariate Cox regression analysis for all outcomes with full model results for all potential predictors in Tables S4–S7.

For the whole group (n = 1706), disease extent (E2 HR = 1.023, E3 HR = 0.931, and E4 HR = 1.186, p > 0.05) at T0 and a history of severe UC (HR = 1.142, 95% CI [1.000–1.305], p = 0.05) before T0 (mean 23 days after diagnosis, standard error of the mean [SEM] 0.5) predicted TFR. Furthermore, predictors at T0 and T1 were searched in a subgroup of patients (n = 964) with clinical remission after T1 (mean TFR 252.8 days). For this group, a history of severe UC before T1 and the confounder PUCAI at T1 (mean 25 days after diagnosis SEM 0.7) were potential predictors of TFR. The history of severe UC before T1 was significantly associated with a faster TFR (HR = 1.292, 95% CI [1.075–1.553], p = 0.007). PUCAI at T1, as a confounder, showed a non‐significant relationship with TFR for moderate (HR = 0.867, 95% CI [0.738–1.019], p = 0.083) and severe disease (HR = 0.697, 95% CI [0.464–1.047], p = 0.081) as compared to mild disease. The percentage of patients using corticosteroids at T1 stratified by PUCAI was 84.2%, 72.4%, and 60.1% for severe, moderate, and mild disease, respectively. Infliximab was the most used biologic at T1 (12.8%–20.8%). Pancolitis was present in around 56.7%–61.4% of patients. The presence of pancolitis was associated with a higher corticosteroid use (p < 0.001). The use of corticosteroids by PUCAI and disease extent is presented in Figure S2.

The presence of pancolitis, at either T0 or T1, was associated with an increased likelihood of reaching TFR faster (HR = 1.275 and HR = 1.312, respectively). Conversely, the use of oral aminosalicylates was associated with a slower TFR (HR = 0.821). Other parameters at T1, such as albumin, BMI, and hematocrit also showed some significance in predicting TFR, but they did not meet all basic assumptions. The models without multiple imputation showed similar results (Tables S5 and S6).

TCSFR

Of the 1785 patients eligible for the outcome, 763 patients (42.7%) reached corticosteroid‐free remission somewhere in the disease course. The mean TCSFR was 569.6 days (95% CI: 537.5–601.6), whereas the median survival time based on a Kaplan–Meier curve was 421.0 days (95% CI: 391.5–450.5). The prediction of TCSFR is displayed in Table 3, Figure S3 and Tables S8 and S9.

The final model included the predictor variables PUCAI and use of corticosteroids at T1 (mean 32 days after diagnosis, SEM 0.9). Because of their important time‐varying effect (p < 0.05 for proportional hazards), they were also included separately in a time‐dependent model (Tables S10 and S11). The baseline model showed that PUCAI at T1 was significantly associated with an increased risk of reaching TCSFR later for mild (HR = 0.728, 95% CI [0.617–0.860], p < 0.001) and moderate disease (HR = 0.559, 95% CI [0.436–0.717], p < 0.001) but not for severe disease (HR = 0.662, 95% CI [0.349–1.255], p = 0.202) compared to inactive disease. This was also the case for the corticosteroid use at T1 (HR = 0.668, 95% CI [0.567–0.788], p < 0.001). Around 49.5% (n = 182) of patients with inactive, 54.6% (n = 154) with mild, 68.4% (n = 63) with moderate and 82.4% (n = 18) with severe disease used corticosteroids at T1. PUCAI and the use of corticosteroids were correlated (ρ = 0.130, p = 0.001). The model without multiple imputation showed similar results but also included disease extent at T1 (Table S8).

TSR

Of the 1785 patients eligible for the outcome analysis, 1231 patients (69.0%) reached sustained remission somewhere in the disease course. The mean TSR was 631.0 days (95% CI: 607.6–654.3), whereas the median time based on a Kaplan–Meier curve was 498.0 days (95% CI: 471.8–524.2). The prediction of TSR is displayed in Table 3, Figure S4 and Tables S12 and S13.

The final model included PUCAI and the use of oral aminosalicylates at T1 (mean 30 days after diagnosis, SEM 0.7). Because of their important time‐varying effect (p < 0.05 for proportional hazards), they were also included separately in a time‐dependent model (Tables S10 and S11). The baseline model showed that PUCAI at T1 was associated with an increased likelihood of reaching TSR later for mild (HR = 0.721, 95% CI [0.627–0.829], p < 0.001) and moderate disease (HR = 0.541, 95% CI [0.447–0.654], p < 0.001) but not for severe disease (HR = 0.631, 95% CI [0.378–1.055], p = 0.078) compared to inactive disease. Furthermore, the use of oral aminosalicylates at T1 was associated with a slower TSR (HR = 0.793, 95% CI [0.680–0.924], p = 0.003). PUCAI and use of oral aminosalicylates were not correlated (ρ = −0.055, p = 0.093). The model without multiple imputation included disease extent, PUCAI and history of severe UC at T1 (Table S12).

DISCUSSION

This study provides insight into the TFR, TCSFR, and TSR, as well as the prediction of these outcomes in a large multicenter network of pediatric UC patients. These time points reflect real‐world treatment responses in patients with UC.

In our cohort, patients reached clinical remission for the first time approximately 6 months after diagnosis. We found that patients experiencing severe UC early after diagnosis tended to reach clinical remission more quickly. Data of the Canadian Children IBD Network indicated that 61% of patients presenting with severe UC as first presentation achieved corticosteroid‐free clinical remission at 1 year. 18 However, acute severe UC at diagnosis remains a risk factor for colectomy and for a more aggressive disease course. 11 , 19

Disease extent and disease severity (PUCAI) also appear to influence clinical remission. Patients with pancolitis, for example, achieved faster remission, likely reflecting the early use of intensive therapy such as with corticosteroids. The pediatric IBD Ahead Program confirmed that disease extent at diagnosis does not predict relapse but may predict treatment response. 19 Furthermore, patients with more severe disease (PUCAI ≥ 35) after 1 month of treatment tended to reach clinical remission later. This indicates that children with severe UC at or shortly after diagnosis are usually treated rapidly and intensively, 20 while the absence of remission 1 month after diagnosis remains a risk factor for persistent disease activity. 3 , 4 Early clinical remission is thus an important indicator of responsiveness to initial therapy and may inform early treatment decisions, including whether treatment should be optimized or escalated in patients with delayed or absent responses.

Furthermore, patients reached corticosteroid‐free remission approximately 1.6 years after diagnosis in our cohort. The PROTECT study reported 3‐month and 1‐year corticosteroid‐free remission in 34% and 38% of patients, respectively, 3 , 4 although that study defined remission as corticosteroid withdrawal for at least 4 weeks. 3 In our cohort, patients with mild or moderate disease 1 month after diagnosis were more likely to experience a delayed corticosteroid‐free remission. For patients with severe disease this could not be observed, likely due to the smaller sample size. In the PROTECT study, patients with a lower baseline disease activity (PUCAI < 35), higher albumin levels and early clinical remission after 4 weeks were more likely to achieve corticosteroid‐free remission at 3 months. 4

Twelve‐month remission was predicted by slightly higher baseline PUCAI (PUCAI < 45), higher hemoglobin and early remission after 4 weeks. 3 Consistent with these results, our study showed that non‐remission after 1 month (PUCAI > 10) is associated with a higher risk of not achieving corticosteroid‐free remission, emphasizing the importance of early clinical remission. Additionally, ongoing corticosteroid use after 1 month was linked to a longer time to reach corticosteroid‐free remission. Poor response to corticosteroids early in the disease course has been consistently identified as a key predictor of worse outcomes, including lower rates of corticosteroid‐free remission and higher colectomy risk. 21 Further research is needed to evaluate the impact of biologicals on these outcomes.

Additionally, patients reached sustained remission approximately 1.7 years after diagnosis. The clinical relevance of sustained remission, defined as clinical remission for at least 6 months, may be reflected in a durable therapeutic response extending beyond induction and post‐induction effects as well as its ability to capture early disease maintenance. Previous studies on sustained steroid‐free remission, such as Schechter et al., reported rates of 34% at 6‐ and 12‐month follow‐up. 22 In our cohort, pediatric patients with mild or moderate, but not severe, disease 1 month after diagnosis were more likely to reach sustained remission later. Moreover, the use of oral aminosalicylates 1 month after diagnosis was also associated with delayed achievement of sustained remission, likely due to the non‐inclusion of rectal therapy and association with disease extent. While a history of severe UC and disease extent at 1 month showed no significant relationship with TSR, they may still have influenced PUCAI scores. Consistent with previous findings, early disease activity, as measured by PUCAI at 8 weeks, remains a useful predictor of sustained steroid‐free remission. 23 Other studies using the same definition of sustained remission could not be identified.

The strengths of this study include the identification and prediction of remission rates in a large group of pediatric UC patients from a multicenter network. Multiple imputation was used to create reliable models that corrected for missing data and represented individual parameters. The presence of PUCAI as a predictor of different remission rates indicates its value in clinical practice. However, PUCAI was not available at diagnosis, eliminating another potential predictor. Our findings indicated the need for an effective therapeutic strategy to achieve clinical remission as soon as possible. This is important because early clinical remission, within 4 weeks of diagnosis, is associated with better long‐term outcomes, 3 , 4 , 19 as shown with the top‐down therapy resulting in lower relapse rates and similar rates of sustained remission in pediatric UC. 24

Limitations of this study include the non‐standardized data collection and missing data. Although a Case Report Form was used, data were not collected at predefined time points. Due to the strict exclusion criteria, which could have introduced selection bias, the remaining population (28.5%) was large enough to draw reliable conclusions. Multiple imputation was used to account for missing data, with a maximum of 37.2% missing in one of the potential predictors. By using the minimum of four visits at 2‐year follow‐up, we also tried to minimize the follow‐up gap to obtain reliable results for TCSFR and TSR, which is evident from the mean of more than seven visits. However, there may be a selection bias towards a group of patients who are more severely ill and are followed up more closely. Nevertheless, the minimum of four visits would likely only lead to the exclusion of patients who are not well monitored throughout their disease course.

Furthermore, the prediction of different remission rates could have been influenced by theoretical correlation between included variables in the final model. However, we studied when to expect and how to predict different remission rates which is innovative. Nevertheless, this study did not consider patients who experienced a significant clinical response (≥ 20‐point drop in PUCAI or a PUCAI < 10). 11 Such an outcome would be useful for comparing the effects of different therapies. Moreover, an endoscopic assessment of remission would have provided valuable additional information alongside the clinical outcomes. Correcting the models for treatment could have provided more insight into the relationship between the speed at which treatment is initiated and UC severity.

CONCLUSION

This study emphasizes that clinical parameters early in the disease course are useful for predicting both short‐ and long‐term remission. Both for corticosteroid‐free and sustained remission, active disease (PUCAI > 10) after 4 weeks was associated with poorer outcomes. Therefore, we recommend that newly diagnosed UC patients should be closely monitored with prompt adjustment of therapy if there is no significant clinical response. Physicians should treat patients with UC intensively from the time of diagnosis, even when the disease appears to be mild. Furthermore, physicians should pay attention to other potential risk factors of short‐ and long‐term non‐remission.
