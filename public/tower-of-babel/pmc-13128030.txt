Development and Validation of a Natural Language Processing Algorithm Using Electronic Health Record Data to Identify Breast Cancer Patients With Low Social Support
Authors: Candyce H. Kroenke, Rhonda Aoki, Lauren Mammini, Stacey Alexeeff, Salene M.W. Jones, Lawrence H. Kushi, Jessica Mogk, Shaila Strayhorn-Carter, David Mosen, David Cronkite, the Kaiser Permanente Northern California Clinician Board
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
PURPOSESocial support is important in the management of breast cancer. We developed a series of structured variables or modules from electronic health record (EHR) data to form the basis for the development of EHRsupport, an EHR-based measure of social support.METHODSWe built a natural language processing (NLP) algorithm from clinical notes in 7,989 women diagnosed from January 2006 to September 2021 with invasive breast cancer. Our team reviewed charts, developed a lexicon, manually crafted rules, applied the NLP algorithm to 565,258 EHR notes and 68,760 patient messages −1 to +3 months around diagnosis, combined terms into separate modules, and tuned (N = 40), updated, and tested (N = 100) these versus chart review. We further developed modules from structured data sources.RESULTSWe identified and developed 11 modules from unstructured data: spouse/partner status, parenthood status, (clinical) visit support, living situation (alone or with others), friend or other support, positive social support, negative (lack of) social support, deceased person, transportation issues, relationship conflict or stress, and social isolation. Module data availability ranged from 1.4% for social isolation to 92.0% for spouse/partner. Modules were accurate (0.81–0.95). The most highly available groups had moderate to excellent F1 scores (0.75–1.00), precision or positive predictive value (0.61–1.00), and recall or sensitivity (0.75–1.00). Recall and precision were more variable among rarer modules. Five percent of patients lacked an in-state emergency contact and 3% of patients had diagnostic codes for low support at any time point.CONCLUSIONThe EHRsupport algorithm accurately identified social support data, supporting the development of a clinical tool that can be used to identify patients with low social support.

INTRODUCTION

Social support is defined as the perception and reality of the exchange of assistance through social relationships.1 Social ties are the persons in one’s network that may provide social support. Patients with breast cancer with high social support and supportive social ties have more optimal treatment and treatment adherence,2–4 better quality of life5–8 and coping,9 and lower cancer mortality.1,10–18

Because of the critical nature of social support in managing breast cancer during the acute treatment phase, clinicians at Kaiser Permanente Northern California (KPNC) have historically documented information in the electronic health record (EHR) at diagnosis about patients’ personal relationships and living situations that may affect their social support. Their goal is to refer patients to additional resources that may enhance their care if they lack support. Recent directives for distress19 and social support screening at large health care organizations have further augmented data availability in the EHR.

Investigators have developed algorithms to extract information on social support; the number of algorithms assessing social support or isolation is low20 but growing.21–29 Methods, including types of notes accessed, approaches to lexicon development,30 and the types of terms developed,31 vary as does the prevalence of social support issues in patients and whether these are related to clinical outcomes of interest. A few algorithms have been evaluated in cancer.21,27,29 Zhu’s algorithm identified 1.6% of patients with prostate cancer as being socially isolated. The prevalence of low social support in Bhatt et al27 was 6%, and the indicator was not related to mortality outcomes in patients with cancer. In Johnson et al,29 the prevalence of low social support was 8%; the indicator was related to mortality in hematologic cancer. No work has been done in breast cancer although it is the most common type of cancer in women. Furthermore, given that 25% of the community population age >65 years indicates social isolation,32 current algorithms may underestimate the prevalence of social support issues in patient populations, which may limit utility of measures in both clinical practice and research, including the ability to differentiate risk in patients. We therefore report a comprehensive approach to the extraction and organization of EHR data from unstructured clinical notes as well as from structured sources, informed by social support terminology from previous literature, into a series of formatted modules that will enable the development of an aggregate EHRsupport measure, a computable, EHR-based measure of social support. This approach was designed to enable (1) exhaustive capture of data and thus more complete determination of population risk; (2) risk differentiation of patients beyond dichotomous categorization; and (3) separate examination of modules in research.

METHODS

This section outlines processes used to develop social support modules from unstructured EHR text data using natural language processing (NLP) and from structured data. The study protocol was approved by the KPNC institutional review board.

Study Population

Women diagnosed with incident invasive breast cancer between 2006 and the end of June 2020 were identified from the KPNC Cancer Registry; those diagnosed between July 2020 and the end of September 2021 were identified through the KPNC Breast Cancer Tracking System (BCTS).33 We identified 37,457 women with incident breast cancer including 35,222 with tumor data in the virtual data warehouse (VDW) who were diagnosed from 2006 to the end of June 2020 and 2,297 women identified by the BCTS team who were diagnosed from July 2020 to the end of September 2021. We used VDW tumor data for the 62 women with both sets of data. Samples used in the study are summarized in the Data Supplement (Table S1).

Patients in Whom the Corpus and Lexicon Were Built

For initial algorithm development, we created a corpus of 134,738 notes authored from 1 month before to 1 month after diagnosis for 3,255 women from the BCTS lists who were diagnosed from November 2018 to August 2020.

Study Population for NLP Algorithm Development

From the full population, we conducted a stratified random sample of N = 8,000 patients, including random samples of 500 women per diagnosis year with notes authored from 1 month before up to 3 months after an invasive breast cancer diagnosis. We excluded 11 male patients, resulting in a total of 7,989 patients in a training data set in which we developed the NLP algorithm.

Tuning and Testing Samples

We generated two tuning data sets of 20 persons each in whom we performed chart review—to test and further develop manual rules. We generated these samples from multiple stratified random samples (by diagnostic year, race, ethnicity, number of notes within window, marital status, parenthood status, clinic, stage, and comorbidity). We further generated a list of N = 100 patients (nonoverlapping with the 7,989 patients in the training data set) from a series of stratified random samples of women (by diagnostic year, race and ethnicity, number of notes within the window, spouse/partner status, parenthood status, clinic based on availability of breast care coordinator notes, stage, and comorbidity) for final validation. This approach was designed to ensure representation in each diagnosis year, race and ethnic group, and social support scenario (eg, what support is documented when a patient is unmarried and has no children).

The Pathways Cohort

Finally, to enable evaluation of a simple score, we included women from the Pathways Study, a prospective cohort study of 4,504 women diagnosed in 2005–2013 with stage I to IV invasive breast cancer34 nested within the larger KPNC population who completed the validated, 19-item Medical Outcomes Study-Social Support (MOS-SS)35 measure approximately 2 months following diagnosis (N = 4,194) excluding those who did not have at least one mention of a social support term in their chart (N = 49) for a total of N = 4,145 in the analytic data set.

Data Sources and Lexicon Development

Guided by conversations with clinician experts, review of previous social support literature, and direct examination of the EHR (described below), we explored unstructured and structured sources of data.

Unstructured Data

Identification of data.

Consultation with content experts.

We asked breast cancer leadership including surgeons, oncologists, and breast care coordinators to act as a project advisory board and met with them to learn about ongoing KPNC directives and plans for assessing social support, clinical workflows, and systematicity of and approach to documentation of social support information. Although any clinician could add information about social support to the EHR, breast care coordinators (nurse navigators in other clinical settings) primarily collect this information. We asked breast cancer coordinators their approaches to data collection and their ideas regarding data that flag social support issues.

Breast care coordinators use a breast intake form, which includes prompts regarding the patient’s social situation (eg, social networks, marital status, living situation), and a distress screener, based on the National Comprehensive Cancer Network Distress Management Guidelines,36 within a month after diagnosis; this assessment includes availability of, or issues with, social support. Clinicians have recorded data on social support since the advent of Epic in 2006, although documentation has increased over time with directives to systematically collect information. The new use of screeners and forms since the mid-teens enhanced data availability. Clinicians also informed us that they have unstructured conversations about social support; documentation occurs in templates or free text notes.

We also consulted with other KP staff (research teams, information technology experts) to identify other EHR sources or approaches to assessment of social support issues.

Chart review/exploration of provider notes.

We (R.A., E.M.K., C.K.) conducted an initial chart review of a full set of clinical notes from 1 month before to 1 month after the date of diagnosis in 18 patients, randomly selected from more recent (August 2019, n = 6 and June 2020, n = 12) records. This was done to determine language used to describe social support based on constructs commonly used in the literature (social support, quality of social support, social ties, types of ties, social isolation, loneliness, living situation); this was informed by a review conducted by a content expert on the team (C.H.K.)1 and on other items clinicians mentioned indicated lack of social support (lack of visit support, lack of emergency contact). We looked for language regarding social ties and living situation (living alone or with others). We also searched for general indications of social support (“good support,” “support system”) as well as support during clinical visits (“here today with,” “accompanied by”). We searched for information about the quality of women’s relationships including positive (“loving,” “connected”) and negative (“estranged,” “infidelity”). Because some women may rely on religious or community support, we searched for related terms but found little indication of these in text notes.

Initial algorithm development.

For each social support term in our lexicon, in notes from the 3,255 women from the BCTS population uploaded to a SQL server, we generated a random sample of 20 substrings using a full text index to find our term of interest, and then we created data fields containing the 50 characters before and after the term. We manually evaluated each substring to determine whether or not the term of interest described social support and whether we could use simple string searches or needed to develop more complex rules (example in the Data Supplement, Fig S1). We calculated the percent of relevant mentions for each term to determine which ones could be identified using named entity recognition methods and which might require more complicated processing. Terms with low relevance (<50%) were dropped unless they reflected terms commonly documented in the EHR in which case we evaluated ways to improve the relevance of critical terms. This process enabled the development of the initial algorithm.

Algorithm development.

Using Python, we developed an NLP algorithm consisting of manually constructed rules based on regular expressions to capture relevant mentions of each term, omit nonrelevant mentions, and differentiate between positive (available) and negative (lack) indications of social support. Rules worked well when syntax and verbiage for a term (eg, has children—children: 0 v children: 1) were consistently described in notes. All terms had complex rules. Even the capture of information for specific snippets sometimes required complex rules, which was resource-intensive and may require future updates as clinical documentation evolves (ie, brittleness). In addition to clinical notes, we discovered that patient messages (messages sent through the online patient portal) contained information on social support, so we included those messages in the corpus. In algorithm development, we also applied the 24 terms previously used by Zhu et al21 to identify socially isolated patients in prostate cancer.

We applied the algorithm to all notes around diagnosis; the most common notes included progress notes (25.1%), telephone encounters (18.2%), messages (11.2%), procedures (8.7%), staff notes (8.3%), scans (2.8%), and long operative notes (2.0%). In total, we developed 11 distinct modules from unstructured data (Table 1).

Tuning and Final Validation of the NLP Algorithm

We conducted two rounds of tuning (20 charts each) on provider notes −1 to +3 months around diagnosis. We compared the results of the algorithm with chart review, evaluated performance metrics, and refined rules with each tuning. For the final validation, we reviewed 100 charts and evaluated performance metrics.

We created a keyword search approach to conduct chart reviews. We created an extensive list of keywords for each of the modules using terms from previous literature, those discovered through lexicon development based on the full chart review of 18 patients, and those found through iterative updating during tuning. We confirmed that this method produced the same information as full chart review −1 to +3 months around diagnosis in an additional, randomly selected set of five patients and proceeded with the more efficient keyword approach. The final data collection tool included fields relevant to modules and instructions for data capture. Reviewers (L.M., E.H.K.), with monitoring (R.A., C.H.K.), conducted searches and documented note dates, relevant snippets and appropriate context, whether the note occurred around diagnosis, and whether mentions were relevant.

Structured Data

Informed by previous work37,38 and clinician communication, we also identified and developed data from more structured sources including diagnostic (International Classification of Diseases versions 9 and 10) codes signifying social support issues and lack of emergency contact in state—data available in the VDW.

Statistical Analysis

We generated descriptive statistics for the NLP algorithm testing population and described availability of modules, overall and by diagnosis year. If the NLP rules captured information on a given concept group, data were considered available and were otherwise considered missing. We computed accuracy, F1 scores, recall (sensitivity), and precision or positive predictive value of modules against chart review, during data tuning and in testing. Finally, we generated a simple sum of modules in patients from the Pathways Study, categorized them, and evaluated the mean and standard deviation (SD) of the MOS-SS measure by level of social support. This was done as an initial assessment of the validity of the score as a screening tool.

RESULTS

In the 7,989 women in the study, who had a total of 565,258 text notes and 68,760 patient messages, the mean age was 60.7 years (SD, 13.2); 55.8% had stage I breast cancer, 29.4% had stage II cancer, and 12.5% had stage III or IV cancer; 58.9% were non-Hispanic White, 7.0% were Black, 12.1% were Hispanic, 16.9% were Asian or Pacific Islander, 0.6% were American Indian or Alaska Native, 0.6% were Pacific Islander, 3.9% were more than one race, and 0.1% were of unknown race; and 59.0% had any comorbidity. Of these women, 7,850 (98.3%) patients had at least one note referring to some aspect of social support within the −1 to +3 months time window around diagnosis (Table 2).

Availability of Social Support Modules in the EHR and Data Missingness

We examined the availability of social support documentation in unstructured data. From highest to lowest availability, modules from text notes included (1) spouse/partner status (92.0%), (2) parenthood status (89.2%), (3) visit support (84.2%), (4) living situation (61.7%), (5) friend or other support (49.3%), (6) positive social support (46.8%), (7) deceased person (40.7%), (8) transportation issues (14.9%), (9) relationship conflict or stress (7.8%), (10) negative social support (4.6%), and (11) social isolation (1.4%; Table 3). Availability of data increased over time since the mid-teens, particularly for living situation, positive social support, and transportation issues. Data availability in recent years was higher (Fig 1).

Among structured variables, data on emergency contact were available in 98.8% of patients with breast cancer; the lack of this data implies risk. We further considered the lack of availability of an in-state emergency contact (5.1%) as an indicator of low social support. We also identified several diagnostic codes signifying problems with social support, social isolation, or social strain (Data Supplement, Table S2). Only 3% (N = 30) ever had any mention of these codes in the EHR at any time, however.

Validation of Modules Against Chart Review

In the final validation, modules were accurate (0.81–0.95). Performance was higher for modules with >40% data availability (F1: 0.75–1.00; precision: 0.61–1.00; recall: 0.75–1.00) and more variable for less commonly available modules (F1: 0.28–0.89; recall: 0.25–0.33; precision: 0.22–0.67; Table 4).

Evaluation of a Simple Sum of Modules Versus the MOS-SS Measure

Ranging from 3 to 12 points (with higher EHRsupport score denoting higher social support), MOS-SS scores were higher in patients with higher sum scores (Table 5). About 7% of the population had a score of 6 or less denoting very low social support; 20% had a score of 7 or less.

DISCUSSION

We described the identification, extraction, and organization of social support data in the EHR, as well as validation of resulting social support modules. Although we considered both unstructured and structured sources of data, the most relevant and systematically available data were located in free text notes around diagnosis. Overall data availability for social support was high at 98% although data availability varied by module. Our NLP algorithm accurately identified social support data in the EHR. This study used a comprehensive approach to the development of social support modules in a population-representative sample of patients with breast cancer.

Investigators have used varied approaches to the development of NLP-based algorithms, including different approaches to lexicon development.21–29 Depending on the comprehensiveness of the lexicon or whether the focus is on determining whether social support is explicitly indicated to be limited or adequate, approaches may underestimate the prevalence of low social support. In the development of Moonstone, Conway et al22 described the complexity of the often indirect language used to describe social support; capturing that language depends on knowledge of how it may be described, both in common use and among clinicians such as social workers who have knowledge of the research literature, as well as factors that may negatively affect support. Including a content expert on the team with knowledge of the social support research literature was helpful to capture relevant information. Chart review was also critical to the identification of data. By developing a multiplicity of modules related to social support, our approach was designed to increase transparency regarding the information included in the algorithm and to improve flexibility of its use. For example, although the lowest EHRsupport score category included 7% of patients, similar to other algorithms, recategorization enabled population capture closer to community estimates of social isolation. Given the comprehensive inclusion of terms, our approach may help to identify additional patients who may benefit from social support resources.

Accuracy of modules was high. Recall and precision were high for the more systematically available modules and lower for rarer modules. The algorithm missed some nuanced language used to describe social support issues. However, lower performance was also due to chance and a disproportionate effect on statistics of a small number of discordant findings; performance was higher during tuning (Table 4).

Clinicians were most likely to document information on tangible factors such as spouse/partner status, parenthood status, visit support, and living situation, although subjective data on positive social support, as well as data on living situation and availability of transportation, became increasingly available since the mid-teens with directives19 for this screening within KP. On the basis of data availability patterns, clinicians appeared to look for positive evidence of social support at diagnosis. Although the lower availability of negative modules might imply a lack of systematic data collection, clinicians appeared to document such information when they felt patients had substantial issues, which may be appropriate.

The most systematically available modules should be least prone to bias. However, given imperfect data capture, researchers may wish to perform sensitivity analyses comparing associations of modules with outcomes of interest by factors that influence data availability including stage, clinic, or time period. Although metrics for the modules were similar across time for most modules in post hoc analysis, they improved for positive social support since 2015 (data not shown) with greater data availability, which may enhance research quality.

Among strengths, in the black box environment of artificial intelligence/machine learning approaches, our manual approach to the development of an NLP algorithm is intuitive, easy to grasp, and straightforward to troubleshoot. Moreover, given the extensiveness of the algorithm, it may be applied in any health condition or health system successfully to the degree that clinicians encode information about social support in similar ways. Further work is needed to evaluate this potential. Another strength is the modular design which increases flexibility of its use depending on research and clinical goals.

A limitation is the potential brittleness of the algorithmic rules, as well as the need to develop, maintain, and update rules although this does not suggest that large language models will necessarily perform better39 since they may be less able to identify relevant information in the case of jargon, acronyms, and abbreviations, common in clinical notes, whereas this can be readily accounted for in rule-based methods. Future research should consider whether these data are available in other cancers and health conditions and in men.

In summary, we developed an accurate, high-performing algorithm from EHR data to develop modules that can be used in population health research and to build a predictive tool to help clinicians identify patients with low social support.
