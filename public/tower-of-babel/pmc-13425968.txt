Modulating mesenchymal stromal cells chondrogenesis through TGF-β1 pre-conditioning during monolayer expansion in pellet culture and in a 3D mechanical load model
Authors: Maja Schlittler, Guoliang Chen, Lauma Ievina, Laura Mecchi, Franziska L. Breulmann, Martin J. Stoddart, Elena Della Bella
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundBone marrow–derived mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) represent a promising source for cell-based regenerative cartilage therapies. However, in vitro chondrogenic differentiation of MSCs varies widely between donors, posing a significant challenge to the clinical translation of these therapies. Here, we explored the potential of pre-conditioning MSCs with TGF-β1 during monolayer expansion in order to enhance chondrogenic differentiation in poorly responsive MSCs and reduce donor-to-donor variability.MethodsMSCs from six donors were expanded in monolayer using standard methods. For the final two days of expansion, half of the cells were pre-conditioned with 10 ng/mL TGF-β1, whereas the others were kept in standard expansion medium. MSCs were then detached for 28 days of chondrogenic differentiation in standard pellet culture in chondrogenic medium or in a 3D mechanically induced chondrogenesis model in polyurethane-fibrin scaffolds. After 28 days, samples were collected for gene expression analysis, sulphated glycosaminoglycan (sGAG) assay, DNA quantification and histological evaluations (Safranin-O/Fast green staining).ResultsTwo days of TGF-β1 pre-conditioning during MSC expansion increased COL2A1 expression after 28 days of pellet culture (P = 0.0217), but also elevated COL10A1 (P = 0.0293), leaving the COL2A1/COL10A1 ratio unchanged (P = 0.8522). There was a trend for an increased COL2A1/COL1A1 ratio and an increased ACAN expression (P = 0.0837). Pre-conditioning had no effect on sGAGs retained in the pellets or released into the medium over the 28 days but slightly enhanced the Safranin-O staining on histological sections. When chondrogenesis was mechanically induced without exogenous TGF-β1, pre-conditioning had no effect on COL2A1, COL10A1, COL1A1, or ACAN gene expression. Similarly, no differences were observed in sGAG content (retained or released) or in Safranin-O staining.ConclusionsTGF-β1 pre-conditioning enhanced some chondrogenic markers in static pellet culture, but these effects were small and did not translate to the more clinically relevant model of mechanically induced chondrogenesis. Furthermore, TGF-β1 pre-conditioning was associated with increased COL10A1 expression, indicating a concurrent promotion of hypertrophic differentiation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12967-026-08719-5.

Background

Articular cartilage defects caused by traumatic injury and/or degenerative disease are a major cause of joint pain and mobility impairment [1]. As cartilage has a poor intrinsic regenerative capacity, untreated defects can lead to chronic complaints and a significant reduction in the patients’ quality of life [2]. Although great progress has been made in the surgical treatment of damaged cartilage, the outcomes of current procedures, such as microfracture and osteochondral autograft transplantation are still highly variable and unpredictable [3]. Cell-based therapies, like autologous chondrocyte implantation, have been clinically applied for decades but their success rate is limited by the small number of harvestable cells, the associated donor site morbidity, and the low in vitro expansion potential of primary chondrocytes [4]. In search of an alternative cell source for cell-based treatments, mesenchymal stromal cells (MSCs) have received much attention as they can be readily isolated from bone marrow aspirates and expanded in vitro over several passages while maintaining their multilineage potential [5]. To induce a chondrocyte-like phenotype, MSCs are typically expanded in monolayer and then differentiated in pellet culture with the addition of 10 ng/mL exogenous Transforming Growth Factor β (TGF-β), a key regulator of cartilage homeostasis [6, 7]. Unfortunately, the chondrogenic commitment of MSCs in response to TGF-β is difficult to predict as it varies considerably between cells obtained from different donors and even between passages of cells from the same donor [8, 9]. Identifying and eliminating the sources of this variation are essential for the successful clinical translation of MSC-based therapies [10]. A frequently overlooked factor in this context is that MSC fate can be influenced as early as during monolayer expansion [11]. Evidence from a pilot study suggests that pre-conditioning MSCs with exogenous TGF-β1 during monolayer expansion may prime cells from otherwise non-responsive donors for successful chondrogenic differentiation [8]. MSC expansion medium is commonly supplemented with 10% foetal bovine serum (FBS), which inherently contains TGF-β proteins. However, the concentration can fluctuate between products and even batches, potentially contributing to the observed donor-to-donor variability in the chondrogenic response [12].

The overall aim of this study was to investigate the effect of TGF-β1 in the expansion medium on MSC chondrogenesis. In a pilot experiment, we first quantified TGF-β1 levels of different serum batches used for MSC expansion and assessed their impact on chondrogenic differentiation. Next, we explored the potential of pre-conditioning responsive MSCs with TGF-β1 during monolayer expansion to enhance chondrogenic differentiation in pellet culture as well as in a more clinically relevant model where chondrogenesis was mechanically induced without the addition of exogenous TGF-β1.

Methods

Isolation and monolayer expansion of human MSCs

Bone marrow aspirates were harvested in EDTA tubes from the vertebral body of six human donors (two males, four females, 49 ± 21 years) undergoing spinal surgery. Human MSCs were isolated from bone marrow aspirates with informed consent and full ethical approval (Ethik-Kommission der Albert-Ludwigs-Universität Freiburg 135/14). Mononuclear cells were isolated from bone marrow using Histopaque®-1077 density separation as previously described [6], and seeded at a density of 53,000 cells/cm2 in MSC growth medium (Minimum Essential Medium α (α-MEM; Gibco, 12000063) containing 2.2 g/L sodium bicarbonate (Sigma-Aldrich, 31437), 10% FBS (see specification below), 1% Penicillin-Streptomycin (100 U/mL penicillin, 100 µg/mL streptomycin; Gibco, 15140-122) and 5 ng/mL basic Fibroblast Growth Factor (bFGF; Biosynth, 30R-AF015)). Cells were allowed to adhere at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere (5% CO2, 95% relative humidity) for 96 h, after which non-adherent cells were removed, and the medium was refreshed. When colonies reached approximately 80% confluence, cells were replated at a density of 3000 cells/cm2. Medium was refreshed three times per week until the cells again reached ~ 80% confluence, after which they were cryopreserved in FBS containing 8% dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO; Sigma-Aldrich, D2438) and stored in liquid nitrogen until use.

For monolayer expansion, MSCs were thawed in MSC growth medium at a density of 3000 cells/cm2. The FBS batches used in this study were as follows: pilot experiments were performed with serum batches A1 (Seraplus, Pan-Biotech, Aidenbach, Germany, P30-3702, lot: P121812), A2 (Seraplus, P30-3702, lot: P200905), and B (Corning, 35-079-CV, lot: 35079016), while serum batch B was used for the main study. Medium was refreshed three times per week, and cells were passaged upon reaching 80% confluence. Cells were used for experiments at passage four. For the final two days of monolayer expansion, half of the cells from each donor were pre-conditioned with 10 ng/mL TGF-β1 (Fitzgerald Industries, 30R-AT072) (+) while the remainder were kept in regular medium (-). At the end of monolayer expansion, cells were detached, and one million cells from each donor and condition were frozen in TRI Reagent (Molecular Research Center, TB126) for day 0 (D0) gene expression analysis. The remaining cells were seeded into pellets or scaffolds for chondrogenic differentiation.

Chondrogenic differentiation in pellet culture

For pellet culture, 2 × 105 cells per well were seeded in V-bottomed 96-well plates (Sterilin, 612V96) in 200 µL chondrogenic (CG) medium consisting of DMEM 4.5 g/L glucose (Gibco, 52100021), 1% Penicillin-Streptomycin (Gibco, 15140122), 1% ITS™ Premix (Corning, 354352), 1x MEM Non-Essential Amino Acids Solution (Gibco, 11140035), 50 µg/mL ascorbic acid 2-phosphate (Sigma-Aldrich, A8960), 100 nM dexamethasone (Sigma-Aldrich, D2915), and 10 ng/mL recombinant human TGF-β1 (Fitzgerald Industries, 30R-AT072). For undifferentiated controls, chondropermissive (CP) medium, formulated identically as CG but without TGF-β1, was used. Pellets were formed by centrifugation for 5 minutes at 500 g and the outermost wells of the plate were filled with PBS to minimize edge effects. The medium was refreshed three times per week, and the conditioned medium was collected and frozen for subsequent analysis. Pellets from the pilot experiment were collected after 21 days, whereas pellets from the main study were harvested after 28 days in culture.

Mechanically induced chondrogenic differentiation in polyurethane-fibrin scaffolds

For mechanically induced chondrogenesis, cells were seeded into custom-made cylindrical polyurethane scaffolds, as described elsewhere [13]. Briefly, polyester-urethane scaffolds were synthesized from hexamethylene diisocyanate, poly(1-caprolactone) diol, and isosorbide diol (1,4:3,6-dianhydro-D-sorbitol). Pores were generated using a salt-leaching phase inversion technique, resulting in interconnected pores with diameters of 90–300 μm. Polyurethane sponges were then cut into cylindrical scaffolds (8 mm diameter, 4 mm height) using water-jet technology (CUTEC AG, Basel, Switzerland). Scaffolds were sterilized in a cold cycle at 37 °C with ethylene oxide prior to use.

Five million cells were asymmetrically seeded in each scaffold with 90% of the cells distributed throughout the scaffold in a fibrinogen-thrombin solution and the remaining 10% of the cells added on top of the scaffold [14]. For seeding, 4.5 × 106 cells were resuspended in 75 µL Fibrinogen from human plasma (50 mg/mL; Sigma-Aldrich, F4883) and added to a sterile Eppendorf tube lid. The scaffold was then added to the lid together with 75 µL Thrombin from human plasma (2 U/mL, Sigma-Aldrich, T4393) and squeezed several times to allow equal distribution of the cells throughout the scaffold. Fibrinogen-thrombin (25 mg/mL and 1 U/mL final concentrations, respectively) gels were left to solidify for one hour at 37 °C, 5% CO2 and 90% relative humidity. The remaining 0.5 × 106 cells were resuspended in CP medium and seeded on top of the scaffolds. Constructs were incubated for another hour before they were placed in custom-made PEEK holders [15] together with 2.5 mL CP or CG medium supplemented with 50 µM ε-amino-caproic acid (EACA) to inhibit fibrin degradation. Scaffolds were assigned to one of three experimental groups: free-swelling undifferentiated controls cultured in CP medium (CP-FS); scaffolds subjected to mechanical loading in CP medium (CP-L); or free-swelling chondrogenic controls cultured in CG medium (CG-FS).

For mechanical loading, a previously described pin-on-ball bioreactor system was used [15]. Unconfined dynamic compression (1 Hz, 0.4–0.8 mm) combined with shear stress (1 Hz, ± 25°) was applied on top of a static offset strain of 0.4 mm, corresponding to cyclic loading between 10% and 20% strain. Mechanical loading was performed for one hour per day, five days per week over a course of 28 days. Medium was changed three times per week, and conditioned medium was collected at every medium change and stored at -20 °C for later analyses. On day 28, all scaffolds were cut vertically into two halves, and replicates from each condition were collected for biochemical analyses and histology as described below (Fig. 1).

TGF-β1 quantification by ELISA

The amount of active TGF-β1 released into the cell culture medium over the 28 days was quantified using the human TGF-beta 1 DuoSet ELISA (R&D systems, DY240) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. To determine the total (i.e., active and latent) amount of TGF-β1, an aliquot of each sample was activated by acidification before ELISA. Total TGF-β1 in FBS batches was quantified using the same procedure. Absorbance was measured using an Infinite® 200 PRO microplate reader (Tecan, Männedorf, Switzerland).

Gene expression analysis

One million MSCs were collected of each donor after monolayer expansion (D0) in 1 mL TRI Reagent (Molecular Research Center; TB126) and stored at -80 °C until RNA extraction. On day 28, triplicate pellets and scaffold halves were harvested and homogenized in 1 mL TRI Reagent in a MM 200 Mixer Mill (Retsch & Co., Haan, Germany) to facilitate RNA extraction. Samples were briefly centrifuged to remove debris and then also stored at -80 °C. After RNA extraction, RNA was quantified using a NanoDrop One Spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher) and 1 µg of total RNA was then reverse transcribed with the TaqMan reverse transcription kit (Applied Biosystems; N8080234) using random hexamers.

Quantitative real-time PCR (RT-qPCR) was performed on a QuantStudio 6 Flex Real-Time PCR system (Applied Biosystems) using TaqMan Gene Expression Master Mix (Applied Biosciences; 4370074) and primers of interest (Tables 1 and 2). Gene expression values were normalized to the expression of RPLP0 of the respective sample, and data were analysed using the 2−ΔCt method.

Quantification of sulphated glycosaminoglycans (sGAGs) and DNA

Pellets (in triplicates) and scaffold halves were digested overnight at 56 °C in 0.5 mg/mL Proteinase K (Roche; 315852001). After inactivation for 10 min at 95 °C, scaffolds were squeezed and discarded. Samples were stored at -20 °C until sGAG and DNA quantification. The amount of sGAGs retained in the scaffolds or pellets was quantified in duplicates by dimethylmethylene blue (DMMB) dye binding assay at pH 1.5 using chondroitin sulfate A sodium salt from bovine trachea (Sigma; C9819-5G) as a standard [16]. The amount of sGAGs released into the medium over the 28 days was also quantified using the DMMB assay at pH 3. Absorbance was measured using an Infinite® 200 PRO microplate reader (Tecan, Männedorf, Switzerland). All sGAG values were normalized to the amount of DNA in the pellets or scaffolds, which was quantified using the Quant-iT PicoGreen dsDNA Assay kit (Invitrogen; 7589).

Histological staining

On day 28, pellets and scaffolds were washed with PBS and fixed in 4% formaldehyde for 48 h at room temperature. After washing in PBS, samples were transferred to 70% ethanol and embedded in paraffin. Sections of 5 μm thickness were prepared using the HM 335 S microtome (Microm, Thermo Fisher). For Safranin-O/Fast Green staining, slides were deparaffinized, stained in Weigert’s Iron Hematoxylin Solution (HT-107/109, Sigma Aldrich; 10 min; dark) and rinsed first in lukewarm tap water (10 min) and then in distilled water (10 min). Sections were stained for 6 min in 0.02% Fast green FCF (C.I. 42053) (Sigma-Aldrich, 104022), followed by 20 s in 1% Acetic Acid and 12 min in Safranin O (C.I. 50240) (Sigma-Aldrich, 1041330025). Slides were differentiated in 70% ethanol until black/blue colours faded and red colours became visible (10 s–6 min) and then in 96% ethanol (2–30 s). After dehydration in 100% ethanol (2 × 4 min), slides were immersed in Xylene (2 × 2 min). Coverslips were mounted using Eukitt® Quick-hardening mounting medium (Sigma-Aldrich, 03989). Brightfield images were acquired using a Axioscan 7 Slide Scanner (Carl Zeiss) with a 20x/0.8 objective. Safranin O and Fast Green staining was quantified using a custom image analysis script written in R. Background was removed from images by calculating pixel saturation using a Hue, Saturation, Value (HSV) colour model; pixels with saturation > 0.08 were classified as tissue. An automatic bounding box was generated from the tissue mask, and all further analysis was restricted to the cropped region. RGB values within the cropped image were converted to HSV using direct per pixel calculations. Safranin O-positive pixels were identified as tissue pixels with high saturation (> 0.25) and red hues (H < 0.1 or H > 0.9). Fast Green–positive pixels were identified as tissue pixels with high saturation (> 0.25) and green-to-teal/blue green hues (0.25 < H < 0.68). Tissue area and stain positive areas were measured as pixel counts. Stain abundance was expressed as the fraction of total tissue area stained positive for Safranin O or Fast Green. Downsampled binary masks were generated for visual quality control of tissue detection and stain segmentation (Supplementary Fig. 1).

Statistical analyses

MSCs from six donors were used for the experiments. Pellet and scaffold chondrogenesis experiments were performed with at least three technical replicates per condition, and all assays were conducted in duplicates. Paired t-tests were applied to compare pre-conditioned and unconditioned MSCs, while comparisons between conditions (CP-FS, CG-FS, and CP-L) or between FBS batches were performed using one-way ANOVA with Tukey’s post hoc test. Data are presented as mean ± SD, and differences were considered statistically significant at P < 0.05. Statistical analyses were carried out using GraphPad Prism 10 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA).

Results

TGF-β concentration in FBS and its effect on chondrogenic differentiation of MSCs

Since FBS has been reported to contain variable amounts of TGF-β, we quantified total TGF-β1 levels in two batches of the same serum (A1 and A2) and in one batch of a different serum (B) by ELISA. Serum B contained more than three times the amount of TGF-β1 compared to A1 and A2 (5787 ± 736 pg/mL vs. 1482 ± 79 pg/mL (P = 0.0043) and 1856 ± 55 pg/mL, respectively (P = 0.0056); Fig. 2A). Next, we conducted a pilot experiment using MSCs from a single donor to compare chondrogenic differentiation following expansion in sera A1 or B. When chondrogenesis was induced at passage two, no clear effect of the different sera was observed on histological staining after 21 days of differentiation (Fig. 2B). However, when cells were transferred to pellet culture at passage three, pellets of MSCs that were expanded in serum B had a larger diameter and more intense Safranin-O/Fast Green staining compared to MSCs expanded in A1 (Fig. 2C).

Effect of TGF-β pre-conditioning on day 0 gene expression

Based on the above-mentioned observations, we hypothesized that pre-conditioning MSCs with exogenous TGF-β1 during expansion enhances their chondrogenic differentiation potential at later passages. MSCs from six donors were expanded until passage three. For each donor, half of the cells were pre-conditioned with 10 ng/mL TGF-β1 during the final two days of monolayer expansion, before being seeded in pellets or polyurethane-fibrin scaffolds at passage four for 28 days of chondrogenic differentiation (Fig. 1).

To evaluate the impact of pre-conditioning on potential predictive markers of chondrogenic differentiation, gene expression analyses were conducted on cells harvested at the end of monolayer expansion (D0). Gene expression values were normalized to the RPLP0 expression of the respective sample. We first analysed the expression of various TGF-β receptors (TGFBRs) on D0, as these have been shown to predict the chondrogenic potential of MSCs [9]. TGF-β1 pre-conditioning did not alter TGFBR1 expression (P = 0.3600; Fig. 3A) but significantly reduced TGFBR2 expression (P = 0.0048; Fig. 3B), leading to a higher TGFBR1/TGFBR2 ratio (P = 0.0077; Fig. 3C). Expression of ACVRL1, the gene encoding ALK1, another type I TGFBR, remained unaffected (P = 0.3821; Fig. 3D), as did the TGFBR1/ACVRL1 and ACVRL1/TGFBR2 ratios (P = 0.8252 and 0.4579, respectively). Further analysis of D0 samples revealed an increased expression of COL1A1 (P = 0.0058), COL10A1 (P = 0.0146) and BAX (P = 0.0382) in pre-conditioned cells and a downregulation of ALPL (P = 0.0401). No significant difference was observed for COL2A1 (P = 0.3376), ACAN (P = 0.3440), SOX9 (P = 0.6909), RUNX2 (P = 0.3000), NFKB1 (P = 0.8199), RELA (P = 0.9002), IKBKG (P = 0.4229), TAK1 (P = 0.2306), PTGS2 (P = 0.1141), and BCL2 (P = 0.1518) expression on D0 between the groups.

Effect of TGF-β1 pre-conditioning on chondrogenic markers after 28 days of chondrogenic differentiation in pellet culture

Next, we investigated whether two days of TGF-β1 pre-conditioning during monolayer expansion would influence chondrogenic outcomes after 28 days of differentiation in static pellet culture. COL2A1 gene expression was significantly higher in pre-conditioned MSCs (CG-FS +) than in unconditioned cells (CG-FS -) (Fig. 4A; P = 0.0217). However, because COL10A1 expression was also significantly increased in pre-conditioned cells (Fig. 4B; P = 0.0293), the COL2A1/COL10A1 ratio did not differ between groups (Fig. 4C; P = 0.8522). Pre-conditioning had no significant effect on COL1A1 expression (Fig. 4D; P = 0.2161), and although the COL2A1/COL1A1 ratio was higher in five of six donors in the pre-conditioned group, this difference did not reach statistical significance (Fig. 4E; P = 0.0903). Of the other chondrogenic markers tested, ACAN was slightly but not significantly elevated in the pre-conditioned group (P = 0.0837; Fig. 4F), while ALPL, COMP, MMP13, RUNX2, and SOX9 expression remained unaffected by pre-conditioning (P = 0.6343, 0.2769, 0.1342, 0.1443, and 0.1285, respectively).

Next, we evaluated whether TGF-β1 pre-conditioning affects the production of sGAGs, a key structural component of the cartilage extracellular matrix (ECM). No statistically significant differences were observed between pre-conditioned and unconditioned cells for sGAGs retained in the pellets (Fig. 5A; P = 0.3416), released into the medium over 28 days Fig. 5B; P = 0.2726), or for the combined total of both (Fig. 5C; P = 0.7181). Discussion

Bone marrow-derived MSCs are the primary cell type recruited during microfracture treatment and a potential source for cell-based cartilage repair [5]. However, MSCs from different donors exhibit unpredictable and highly variable responses to chondrogenic stimulation, which remains a major limitation for the clinical applicability of such therapies [10]. Methods to reduce donor-to-donor variability are therefore highly warranted.

MSC fate can already be influenced during monolayer expansion, and differences in this phase may contribute to the heterogeneity observed in chondrogenic outcomes [11]. MSC expansion medium is commonly enriched with 10% FBS with additional Fibroblast Growth Factor 2 (FGF2), which supports cell adhesion and proliferation and preserves stemness during in vitro expansion [17]. However, FBS is a poorly defined supplement containing TGF-β proteins and other growth factors at concentrations that vary between products and even between batches [17]. To quantify differences between products, we tested the TGF-β1 concentration in FBS obtained from two manufacturers and in two batches of the same product. One of the tested sera contained more than three times the amount of TGF-β1 of the others, which, when added at 10% to the expansion medium, corresponds to ~ 0.58 ng/mL compared to ~ 0.15–0.19 ng/mL TGF-β for the remaining products. In a pilot test, we evaluated whether the type of serum used during expansion can affect the outcome of subsequent chondrogenic differentiation. When MSCs were expanded until passage two and then transitioned to chondrogenesis in pellet culture, expansion with different sera had no visible effect on chondrogenic differentiation. However, when chondrogenesis was induced at passage three, MSCs expanded in the serum containing more TGF-β1 produced larger pellets with more intense Safranin-O staining, suggesting that TGF-β in the expansion medium may help counteract the loss of chondrogenic potential with successive passages. In a previous study, we obtained similar results by supplementing the expansion medium of MSCs from two poorly responsive donors with TGF-β1, which restored their chondrogenic phenotype [8].

Based on these findings, we here hypothesized that pre-conditioning MSCs with exogeneous TGF-β1 during the expansion phase would enhance subsequent chondrogenic differentiation and thereby reduce donor-to-donor variability. To test this, we expanded MSCs from six donors and pre-conditioned half of the cells with 10 ng/mL TGF-β1 for the final two days of monolayer expansion. Chondrogenic differentiation was then induced by transferring MSCs to pellet culture at passage three and stimulating them for 28 days with CG medium containing 10 ng/mL TGF-β1. The pro-chondrogenic effect of TGF-β1 is mediated through activation of canonical TGF-β signalling. Active ligand binds to the TGFBR2 on the cell surface, which subsequently recruits, phosphorylates, and activates the TGFBR1, also known as ALK5. The activated TGFBR1 then phosphorylates Smad2 and Smad3, which form a complex with Smad4. This complex translocates into the nucleus, where it regulates the expression of chondrogenic genes [18]. Notably, ligand-bound TGFBR2 can also initiate non-canonical signalling by recruiting the alternative type I receptor ALK1. This pathway activates Smad1, Smad5 and Smad8, and has been associated with increased MMP-13 expression, cartilage hypertrophy, and the progression of osteoarthritis [9, 18, 19]. We recently showed that the relative expression of TGFBRs can predict the chondrogenic potential of MSCs. Specifically, cells with a high TGFBR1/TGFBR2 ratio at the time of pellet seeding displayed increased COL2A1 expression and a higher COL2A1/COL10A1 ratio after 28 days of chondrogenic stimulation compared to cells with a low ratio [9]. Moreover, silencing the TGFBR2 increased the TGFBR1/TGFBR2 ratio and restored the chondrogenic phenotype in non-responsive donors, potentially by shifting pathway activation towards Smad2/3 phosphorylation and limiting signalling via the Smad1/5/8 pathway [9]. Instead of silencing receptors, studies in other cell types suggest that TGF-β stimulation alone can modulate the TGFBR profile by promoting receptor cycling [20]. Other studies describe a negative feedback mechanism in which TGFBR2 activation represses TGFBR2 gene expression in pancreatic epithelial cell lines [21] and in articular chondrocytes [22], although it is unknown whether this also occurs in MSCs. To determine whether pre-conditioning could elicit a similar effect in MSCs, we here analysed TGFBR gene expression in pre-conditioned and non-conditioned cells on D0. Pre-conditioning increased the TGFBR1/TGFBR2 ratio in all six donors, primarily through a reduction in TGFBR2 expression. Consistent with our previous study, an increased TGFBR1/TGFBR2 ratio was associated with elevated COL2A1 expression, with a trend toward increased ACAN expression and an elevated COL2A1/COL1A1 ratio after 28 days of pellet culture in CG medium. However, in contrast to our earlier findings, COL10A1 expression was also increased, resulting in an unchanged COL2A1/COL10A1 ratio, indicating a shift toward unfavourable hypertrophic differentiation rather than the formation of stable articular cartilage [9].

sGAGs are widely used as markers for chondrogenic differentiation due to their critical role in cartilage matrix formation and their temporal expression patterns during MSC chondrogenesis. We therefore quantified sGAG production in pre-conditioned versus non-conditioned MSCs during chondrogenic differentiation. Although no differences were observed in sGAGs released into the medium or sGAGs retained within the pellets, histological sections showed slightly more intense Safranin-O staining in pre-conditioned cells. In our previous study, we reported similar results, although the effect of pre-conditioning was considerably more pronounced, likely due to the specific selection of non- and poorly responsive donors, whereas in the present study none of the donors were completely non-responsive [8]. This, in turn, could be related to our use of FBS with the higher TGF-β1 concentration (Serum B) during expansion, which may have unintentionally pre-conditioned the control cells and masked the presence of poorly responsive donors. In addition, in our previous study, we pre-conditioned the cells for five instead of two days, which could also explain the stronger effect. However, stimulation with TGF-β1 for five days greatly increases confluence, which may itself influence subsequent chondrogenic differentiation. To better isolate the specific effect of pre-conditioning, we therefore limited the pre-conditioning period to two days in the present study. However, it remains to be determined whether an alternative pre-conditioning time window and/or different TGF-β1 concentration would alter these outcomes.

So far, results from pellet cultures show a small increase in chondrogenic gene expression and histological markers, but also an unwanted increase in COL10A1 expression following TGF-β1 pre-conditioning. Despite modest effects on the expression of some markers, TGF-β1 preconditioning did not measurably reduce inter-donor variability in pellet culture. While pellet culture is widely regarded as the gold standard for in vitro chondrogenesis, it poorly mimics the native cellular environment. In vivo, TGF-β is secreted as a latent complex together with its latency-associated peptide (LAP), and the critical step for initiating downstream signalling is the dissociation of the LAP and thus the activation of TGF-β, which occurs via proteolytic cleavage, changes in heat or pH, or through the application of mechanical force [18, 23]. Human synovial fluid contains around 2 ng/mL latent and less than 0.05 ng/mL active TGF-β, whereas the concentration of active TGF-β used in pellet culture is typically around 10 ng/mL [23]. We have previously shown that chondrogenic differentiation can also be induced without the addition of exogenous TGF-β when cells are embedded in polyurethane-fibrin scaffolds which are exposed to mechanical loads on a daily basis [24]. We therefore extended our study to assess the effect of TGF-β1 pre-conditioning in a mechanical chondrogenesis model. MSCs from the same donors used in pellet culture were expanded with or without pre-conditioning, seeded into polyurethane-fibrin scaffolds, and subjected to mechanical loading in CP medium (CP-L) for one hour per day over 28 days. For comparisons, we also kept free swelling controls in CP medium (CP-FS) and in CG medium (CG-FS). For the CG-FS group, no statistically significant differences were observed between pre-conditioned and non-conditioned cells. Nevertheless, the trends mirrored those seen in CG-FS pellets: COL2A1 expression was increased, COL10A1 was also elevated, the COL2A1/COL1A1 ratio showed a slight increase, and ACAN expression was higher. No differences were detected in sGAG content in the medium or within the scaffolds. Histologically, pre-conditioned cells showed more intense Safranin-O staining compared to the non-conditioned cells. When chondrogenesis was induced by mechanical stimulation without the addition of exogeneous TGF-β1 (CP-L), pre-conditioning had no noticeable effect on gene expression or on sGAG release into the medium and retained within the scaffolds. Histological sections were technically challenging to obtain due to the material properties of the scaffolds and were therefore difficult to interpret. Overall, staining signals were generally faint, difference between groups were small, and no statistically significant effect of pre-conditioning was observed. As we showed for the pellet culture model, TGF-β1 preconditioning did not reduce inter-donor variability in a scaffold system, regardless of whether differentiation was induced by TGF-β1 treatment or by mechanical stimulation.

The differential effect of pre-conditioning observed between mechanically and TGF-β1-induced differentiation in the scaffold model leads us to speculate that other signalling pathways may interact with TGF-β signalling under mechanical load. Potential candidate pathways include integrin‑mediated signalling, such as pathways associated with mechanosensitive ion channels, including TRPV4 and Piezo1 [25]; mechano-transduction pathways mediated by YAP/TAZ [26]; as well as nitric oxide–dependent signalling, which has been implicated in a similar mechanical loading model [27]. Further studies will be required to investigate the potential involvement of these pathways.

This study has several limitations. The pilot experiments were conducted using only three FBS batches, and larger studies including a greater number of serum batches would be required to draw more generalizable conclusions. A second limitation of this study is that all donors responded to chondrogenic stimulation, which may have masked the potential effects of pre-conditioning. This may be attributable to the comparatively high levels of TGF-β1 present in the FBS used for monolayer expansion compared to previous studies, which may have unintentionally pre-conditioned the control cells. Experiments using serum-free expansion medium or TGF‑β-depleted FBS supplemented with defined concentrations of TGF‑β1 would be required to clarify the direct effects of pre-conditioning on MSC chondrogenic potential, independent of the exogenous TGF-β amount present in the serum [12]. Moreover, detailed analysis of downstream components of TGF‑β signalling, such as Western blot analysis of phosphorylated SMAD proteins, would help to further elucidate pathway activation. Another limitation of the present study is the relatively small sample size, which restricts statistical power and warrants cautious interpretation of donor‑to‑donor variability. Finally, we employed a single mechanical loading system using a PU-fibrin scaffold, and we cannot exclude the possibility that pre-conditioning may prove beneficial for mechanically induced chondrogenesis in alternative loading systems or scaffold configurations.

Conclusions

The greatest hurdle for the clinical translation of MSC-based cartilage therapies is the variable response of MSCs to chondrogenic stimulation. MSC fate can already be influenced during monolayer expansion, and our data demonstrate that the choice of serum significantly affects the chondrogenic potential of MSCs. Establishing standardized expansion conditions and using well-defined serum is therefore critical to minimize variability at this early stage and improve the reproducibility of MSC-based cartilage repair strategies. We can conclude that, at least under the conditions herein tested, TGF-β1 preconditioning does not measurably reduce inter-donor variability as we originally hypothesized. While pre-conditioning modestly enhanced the expression of certain chondrogenic markers at the gene expression level in pellet culture, it also increased the expression of undesirable hypertrophic markers and did not visibly reduce donor-to-donor variability. Moreover, these transcriptional changes were not accompanied by marked improvements at the matrix level. We further extended this concept to a more clinically relevant, mechanically induced chondrogenesis model. However, in this context, pre-conditioning with TGF-β1 did not produce a clear benefit, suggesting that additional signalling pathways may play a role in mechanically induced MSC chondrogenesis.
