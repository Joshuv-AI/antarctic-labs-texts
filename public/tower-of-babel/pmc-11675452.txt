Axiomatic Approach to Measures of Total Correlations
Authors: Gabriel L. Moraes, Renato M. Angelo, Ana C. S. Costa
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Correlations play a pivotal role in various fields of science, particularly in quantum mechanics, yet their proper quantification remains a subject of debate. In this work, we aimed to discuss the challenge of defining a reliable measure of total correlations. We first outlined the essential properties that an effective correlation measure should satisfy and reviewed existing measures, including quantum mutual information, the p-norm of the correlation matrix, and the recently defined quantum Pearson correlation coefficient. Additionally, we introduced new measures based on Rényi and Tsallis relative entropies, as well as the Kullback–Leibler divergence. Our analysis revealed that while quantum mutual information, the p-norm, and the Pearson measure exhibit equivalence for two-qubit systems, they all suffer from an ordering problem. Despite criticisms regarding its reliability, we argued that QMI remains a valid measure of total correlations.

1. Introduction

Whenever a bipartite quantum system is described by a product state ρ12=ρ1⊗ρ2, the system is said to be uncorrelated. Physically, this means that the probability distributions associated with part 1 in no way depend on those of part 2. When such full factorization does not apply, the system is said to possess a feature called correlation. With applications in several fields [1], this feature is essential in quantum foundations [2,3,4,5], quantum information science [6,7,8], and quantum thermodynamics [9,10,11,12,13,14]. It is well established by now that correlations can be distinguished as classical and quantum [15], with the latter implying advantages in certain physical tasks [16,17,18,19,20].

One way to quantify the total correlations encoded in a quantum state is through quantum mutual information (QMI). As discussed in Ref. [21], the expression “quantum mutual information” dates back to the work of Cerf and Adami [22]. In contrast, its mathematical formulation, as it is known today, recalls the quantity named “coupling information” in the work of Stratonovich [23]. One of its first uses was by Zurek while studying information in quantum measurements [24]. Furthermore, it is also called the “correlation index” by Barnett and Phoenix [25,26]. In the context of multipartite states, it has recently been shown that at least n−1 families of quantum mutual information exist for a system of n≥2 parties [27]. Furthermore, any positive linear combination of these families will generate another valid family.

Although QMI is widely used as a measure of total correlations, other measures can be found in the literature, such as the norm of the correlation matrix [28] and the proposal based on the Pearson coefficient [29]. However, these measures might not always agree on the strength of the correlation, bringing up a behavior among them known as the ordering problem. Regarding other types of correlations, such as entanglement, known measures may not consistently order states in terms of their level of correlation [30]. In other words, different measures may not agree which one of two given quantum states is more correlated. Because QMI also exhibits this characteristic when compared with the norm of the correlation matrix, the authors of Ref. [28] suggest that QMI may not be a reliable measure of total correlations. Of course, one might wonder why the ordering problem is not attributed to the norm of the correlation matrix instead.

This ambiguity persists because the concept of total correlations lacks a clear characterization in terms of a minimal set of axioms that a measure should satisfy. The aim of this article was to address the question: how do we define a bona fide measure of total correlations? To accomplish this task, we introduced a list of properties we deemed necessary for proper measurement in Section 2. In Section 3, we presented some proposals of correlation measures, and, in Section 4, we analyzed them for general two-qubit systems. Section 5 outlined discussions and future perspectives. Finally, we reaffirmed, based on our axiomatic approach, that QMI remains the most faithful measure of total correlations so far.

2. Axioms

What properties a measure of total correlation should have remains an open question. Some works already hinted at some of them [27,31,32]. In what follows, using reasonability only, we postulate some properties that we deem to be non-negotiable for a faithful quantifier of total correlations. For future convenience, we introduced the factorizing map Ω, which transforms any N-partite quantum state ρ acting on the Hilbert space H=H1⊗H2⊗⋯⊗HN into its fully uncorrelated counterpart ρ1⊗ρ2⊗⋯⊗ρN. Symbolically, (1)Ω(ρ):=⨂i=1NTr〈i〉ρ=⨂i=1Nρi≡Ωρ, where the operation Tr〈i〉 means that every part, except the i-th one, should be traced out. The properties that ought to be satisfied by every measure C(ρ) of the total correlations encoded in a specific multi-partition of the full Hilbert space are as follows.

(i) Non-negativity: C(ρ)≥0, with equality holding if and only if ρ=Ωρ.

(ii) Local unitary invariance: CULOCρULOC†=C(ρ), where ULOC=⨂i=1NUi and Ui are unitary transformations acting on the single Hilbert space Hi.

(iii) Monotonicity under local completely positive trace preserving (CPTP) maps: C(η)≤C(ρ), where η=ε1∘ε2∘⋯∘εN(ρ), and εi is a CPTP map acting on part i only. In particular, C(Triρ)≤C(ρ).

(iv) Continuity: C(ρ)−C(ρϵ)≥ϵC(ρ) for ρϵ:=(1−ϵ)ρ+ϵΩρ and 0≤ϵ≤1. Also, C(ρ1−ε)≤εC(ρ), where 0≤ε≤1.

(v) Additivity: C(ρX⊗ρY)=C(ρX)+C(ρY), with ρX and ρY multipartite states corresponding to the composite parts X={1,2,…,k} and Y={k+1,k+2,…,N}, respectively.

Condition (i) implies a positive semidefinite scale for the correlations measure, with zero representing the lack of correlations. Conditions (ii) and (iii) are often required for correlation measures [21,33]. The former incorporates the fact that correlations are global properties that should not change upon unitary operations. The latter implements the concept that local CPTP maps do not increase correlations (in particular, discarding a part removes correlations). Condition (iv) requires that if one slightly degrades a correlated state ρ, particularly towards reaching its uncorrelated counterpart Ωρ, then correlations will be destroyed, but only to a small extent. Similarly, if one slightly perturbs an uncorrelated state Ωρ, particularly towards reaching its correlated counterpart ρ, then correlations will be generated, but only to a small extent. Since C(Ωρ)=0, it follows that condition (iv) also implies the convexity of C with respect to ρϵ. This is not to say, though, that C must be convex in general, for it is clear that (1−ϵ)ρ1⊗ρ2+ϵσ1⊗σ2≠ω1⊗ω2, meaning that mixing can increase correlations. Condition (v) is justified as follows. Consider that the multipart X={1,2}≡12 of a system has no physical connection whatsoever with multipart Y={3,4}≡34, i.e., X and Y are actually statistically independent systems. It is natural that a quantifier detects only the remaining internal correlations within the multiparts. In particular, it must hold that C(ρ12⊗ρ3)=C(ρ12).

Some remarks are now opportune. First, the five axioms proposed above are not intended to exhaust all the reasonable conditions that must be satisfied by a faithful measure of total correlations. We expected, however, that they form a set that is restrictive enough to select among candidate measures. Second, it was not our concern to describe how a multipart X={1,2,⋯,k} is correlated with another multipart Y={k+1,k+2,⋯,N}, although this is a valid problem. To accomplish this task, one could, for instance, redefine the factorizing map to yield Ω(ρ)=ρX⊗ρY. Third, the additivity condition is relevant only in scenarios involving different multiparts. In the simplest bipartite case (N=2), it makes no sense. Note that properties (i)–(iii) are similar to the axioms proposed by Henderson and Vedral in [31], while axioms (iv)–(v) represent new additions. As already mentioned above, we understand that these two conditions contribute to the construction of reliable measures: condition (iv) ensures smooth functions, while condition (v) guarantees that uncorrelated states do not affect the degree of total correlations for a specific multi-partition of the total system.

3. Total Correlation Measures

Quantifying correlations is an important and equally difficult task, as we learned from the entanglement literature [34,35,36]. In principle, one might propose infinitely more functionals that are well-behaved mathematically but fail to satisfy a given set of reasonable axioms, as the one proposed above. The aim of this section was to make this point and, ultimately, identify faithful measures of total correlations.

3.1. Quantum Mutual Information

The first candidate measure can be none other than the most traditional one, namely, QMI. Considering an N-partite state ρ and its uncorrelated counterpart defined in Equation (1), QMI can be expressed as (2)I(ρ)=D(ρ||Ωρ) where D(ϱ||σ):=Tr[ϱ(logϱ−logσ)] is the Umegaki divergence between the quantum states ϱ and σ [37]. Also known in the literature as von Neumann relative entropy, D is the quantum extension of the Kullback–Leibler divergence [38]. The Umegaki divergence satisfies several interesting properties, such as positive definiteness, being null only if its entries are equal, invariance under the action of local unitaries, monotonicity under CPTP maps (also known as contractivity), joint convexity, and additivity [37]. For the purposes of the analyses conducted in the present work, we refer the reader to the useful summary of properties reported in Table I of Ref. [39].

Defined in terms of the Umegaki divergence, I(ρ) directly satisfies all the axioms on our list, which can be demonstrated in a straightforward manner. To be sure, we noted that condition C(Triρ)≤C(ρ) was proven by recognizing that partial tracing is a CPTP map, while condition (iv) derives from joint convexity. For practical purposes, it is interesting to note that QMI can be written in terms of the von Neumann entropy, S(ρ)=−Tr(ρlogρ), as (3)I(ρ)=∑i=1NS(ρi)−S(ρ).

3.2. Rényi Divergence

Our second candidate measure of total correlations is (4)Iα(ρ)=Dα(ρ||Ωρ), where Dα(ϱ||σ):=11−αlog[Tr(ϱασ1−α)] is the Rényi divergence between the quantum states ϱ and σ, for α∈(0,1)∪(1,+∞) [40]. The one-parameter measure (4) is a clear generalization of (2), since Dα→1(ϱ||σ)=D(ϱ||σ). Some variants of the Rényi divergence are known (see Ref. [39] and references therein) that can also be candidate measures. However, they will not be analyzed here because our intention was to be illustrative only. All the properties we mentioned that are satisfied by the Umegaki divergence remain intact for all values of α, except monotonicity under the action of CPTP maps, which is valid only for α∈(0,1)∪(1,2] [41]. It then follows that, as long as one restricts α to the aforementioned domains, Iα(ρ) will also be a faithful measure of total correlations.

3.3. Tsallis Relative Entropy

Another generalization of the measure (2) is (5)Iq(ρ)=Dq(ρ||Ωρ), where Dq(ϱ||σ):=Trϱq(lnqϱ−lnqσ) is the Tsallis relative entropy of the quantum states ϱ and σ [42,43], with q∈(0,1)∪(1,+∞) and lnq(x):=(x1−q−1)/(1−q). It is well known that Dq→1(ϱ||σ)=D(ϱ||σ). Once again, the logic behind the measure is to quantify how distinguishable ρ is from its uncorrelated counterpart Ωρ. Although the Tsallis relative entropy is regarded as an extension of von Neumann relative entropy, it does not preserve all the properties listed in Section 2. From the results reported in Refs. [43,44], one can validate properties (i) and (ii) for the entire domain of q, and properties (iii) and (iv) for q∈(0,1)∪(1,2], but additivity is violated. Recall, however, that this does not constitute a problem for N=2, meaning that Iq(ρ) is a faithful measure for bipartite states.

3.4. Norm of Correlation Matrix

So far, we only considered entropy-based measures, comparing ρ with Ωρ. In this section, we analyzed the adequacy of a norm-based measure. Specifically, drawing inspiration from Ref. [28], we considered the “geometric quantifier” (6)Gp(ρ)=||ρ−Ωρ||p, where ||O||p:=TrO†Op/21/p is the Schatten p-norm of an operator O, for any real number p≥1, and ρ−Ωρ is the correlation matrix. From the semipositive definiteness of any norm, it follows that condition (i) is promptly satisfied. Property (ii) is guaranteed by the well-known fact that the Schatten p-norm is unitary invariant [45]. Condition (iii) is satisfied but only under a special condition. In Ref. [28], the authors used the 2-norm to define the correlation matrix. However, for this specific value, monotonicity is satisfied only for unital CPTP maps [45], which is ensured by the Pinching inequality. Here, however, we were interested in monotonicity being satisfied for any CPTP map, and the contractivity of the norms holds only for p=1. Because all norms are convex (by the triangle inequality), Gp(ρ) automatically satisfies condition (iv). More specifically, since Ωρϵ=Ωρ, one has Gp(ρϵ)=(1−ϵ)Gp(ρ). To show that additivity is not respected by Gp, let us consider, for simplicity, the state ρ12⊗ρ3. Plugging this state and its uncorrelated counterpart ρ1⊗ρ2⊗ρ3 into Equation (6) returns Gp(ρ12⊗ρ3)=Gp(ρ12)||ρ3||p. It is then clear that the measure under scrutiny has an important flaw: the correlations encoded in ρ12⊗ρ3 are given not only by the correlations in ρ12 but also by the norm of the completely irrelevant state ρ3. Therefore, although the measure proposed in Ref. [28] is fine for bipartite states, it does not pass the axiomatic test we introduced in Section 2 for N>2. One might suspect that normalizing the Formula (6) with ||Ωρ||p would fix the problem, but this is not true. Doing so would instead affect the other properties.

3.5. Pearson Correlation Coefficient

For the classical case, it has been shown that the Pearson correlation coefficient (PCC)—a measure based on the first and second-moment statistics of distributions—can satisfactorily capture the correlation between two random variables [46,47]. Recently, an extension of the Pearson coefficient was introduced in the quantum domain to formulate a measure of total correlations for bipartite states [29]. Here, we introduced a generalization of the concept for the N-partite case. Let Wi be a discrete-spectrum observable acting on a Hilbert space Hi. Using the notation W={W1,W2,⋯,WN}, we consider the following covariance function: (7)CρW:=Tr⨂i=1NWiρ−Ωρ=〈W1⊗W2⊗⋯⊗WN〉ρ−〈W1〉ρ1〈W2〉ρ2⋯〈WN〉ρN, Let ΔWi,ρ=〈Wi2〉ρ−〈Wi〉ρ21/2 be the uncertainty of Wi, where 〈Win〉ρ=TrWinρ for n∈N>0. The N-partite generalization of PCC then reads as follows:(8)RW(ρ):=CρW⨂iΔWi,ρ. The normalization guarantees that RW(ρ) is dimensionless. In order to capture all existing correlations in the quantum state with the smallest number of measurements, the authors in Ref. [29] considered the maximum set of complementary observables in the Hilbert space of each subsystem. To implement this procedure, we had to replace each operator Wi in the construction above with Wik, where k∈{1,2,⋯,v} and v is the cardinality of the maximum set of pairwise complementary observables. Two maximally incompatible observables X and Y are called complementary, denoted as X♮Y, and have mutually unbiased bases (MUB), i.e., |〈xi|yj〉|2=1/d∀i,j∈{0,1,⋯,d−1}.) Wik♮Wik′ acting on Hi. Accordingly, in what follows, the set W is updated to Wk={W1k,W2k,⋯,WNk}. Thus, the total correlations of an N-partite state ρ can be calculated using the PCC measure as follows:(9)R(ρ):=maxWk∑k=1v|RWk(ρ)|, where the maximization is taken over all possible sets Wk.

The measure (9) satisfies axioms (i) and (ii) by construction: the former via the modulus and the latter via the maximization. It also satisfies monotonicity under CPTP maps [48,49]. Through direct calculations, it is proven that R(ρϵ)=(1−ϵ)R(ρ), thus showing that axiom (iv) is satisfied. Finally, it is not difficult to show that the additivity axiom is violated for N>2.

3.6. Kullback–Leibler Divergence

As in the previous section, we investigated a measure starting with correlations associated with local measurements. Let Zi=∑zziZzi be a discrete-spectrum observable acting on a Hilbert space Hi, where Zzi are projection operators satisfying ZziZz′i=δzz′Zzi and zi are eigenvalues of Zi. The key idea here is to quantify how distinguishable the joint probability distribution (10)p(z|ρ)≡p(z1,z2,⋯,zn|ρ)=Tr⨂i=1NZziρ is from p(z|Ωρ). Once again, we can resort to using an entropy-based tool [here, the Kullback–Leibler (KL) divergence and all its known properties] to measure the total correlations as follows:(11)DKL(ρ)=max{Zzi}∑zp(z|ρ)logp(z|ρ)p(z|Ωρ).

Axiom (i) is satisfied because KL divergence is positive semidefinite, being null iff its entries are equal. To verify the validity of axiom (ii), we first note that (12)Tr⨂iZziULOCρULOC†=Tr⨂iZ˜ziρ, with Z˜zi=Ui†ZziUi. Because the maximization in Equation (11) is supposed to run across all projection operators, the Z˜zi are also considered. It follows that DKLULOCρULOC†=DKL(ρ), as desired. Axiom (iii) is satisfied given that the KL divergence is monotonic under the action of CPTP maps [50]. Axiom (iv) directly follows from the convexity of the KL divergence and straightforward calculations yields DKL(ρX⊗ρY)=DKL(ρX)+DKL(ρY), thus validating axiom (v).

Note that the idea of investigating correlations through the analysis of the effect of local measurements is widely used in the literature, for example, in the definition of symmetric quantum discord [51].

4. Analysis of the Total Correlation Measures for Two-Qubit Systems

In this section, we aimed to analyze the presented measures of total correlations for systems of two qubits. To do so, we compared all of them with QMI. The latter is chosen as the reference given its importance in the literature and because it satisfies all required properties of a well-behaved measure. For comparison, we generated 106 random quantum states and confronted the measures as shown in Figure 1. In these comparisons, we expected equivalence between the measures; that is, for the states where QMI is at a maximum (minimum), the corresponding proposal in the comparison should also be at a maximum (minimum).

In Figure 1, one can see that Gp(ρAB) (where we chose the specific norms 1 and 2), and R(ρAB) are equivalent to I(ρ). This indicates that they can be considered good measures of total correlations when considering two-qubit systems. However, Iα=2(ρAB), Iq=2(ρAB), and DKL(ρAB) agree about the minimum while diverging about the quantum state that maximizes the measure. Although the plots display only specific values of p, α, and q, the same behavior was observed for other values of these parameters, provided that the relevant properties are satisfied. Specifically, we restrict α and q to the range (0,2]∖{1}. Finally, for Gp, we tested the value p=1, for which condition (iii) is satisfied, and p=2, as originally investigated in [28].

Regarding the properties that have been enunciated as necessary for a measure of total correlations, except for Tsallis relative entropy-based measures (5) and the p-norm of the correlation matrix (6), which fail to satisfy condition (iii), the other proposed measures satisfy all of them. This result somehow diverges from the one presented in [28], where the authors argue in favor of the norm of the correlation matrix as a reliable measure of total correlations. It is important to notice that their criticism is solely based on the problem of ordering between measures, something that occurs for several measures of quantum resources. 5. Final Remarks

The importance of correlations in science, and particularly in quantum mechanics, need not be emphasized. Still, the proper quantification of these objects is repeatedly questioned, revealing that it is essential to establish axioms for correlation measures, especially for total correlations. The objective of this work was to take some steps toward deepening the issue.

This work provided a brief overview of the properties we consider essential for an effective correlation measure. Following this discussion, we presented several total correlation measures from the literature, including the QMI [Equation (2)], the p-norm of the correlation matrix [Equation (6)], and the recently defined PCC measure [Equation (9)]. Additionally, we introduced new measures based on the Rényi [Equation (4)] and Tsallis [Equation (5)] relative entropies, as well as the Kullback–Leibler divergence [Equation (11)].

While analyzing the properties satisfied by each measure, we found that additivity is not fulfilled by the p-norm of the correlation matrix and the Tsallis-based measure. This aspect raises the question of how essential this property is for a correlation measure. However, if one chooses to disregard this property, the measure’s sensitivity to uncorrelated systems may be affected, although its equivalence with the QMI remains unchanged. In this context, among the measures considered, we demonstrated that for systems of two qubits, only the QMI, the p-norm of the correlation matrix, and the PCC measure are equivalent to each other. Nevertheless, they all exhibit an ordering problem. All in all, our work suggests that, despite some claims to the contrary [28], QMI remains an arguably reliable measure of total correlations.

For future work, it would be interesting to investigate monogamy relations for the introduced measures, showing how total correlations spread in this kind of system for each of the considered measures.
