Ultra-processed Foods and Athletes: Current Insights on Classification, Use, Awareness, and Impact—A Narrative Review
Authors: Sara F Forbes, Louise M Burke, Evangeline Mantzioris, Adrienne K Forsyth
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThere is growing concern about the impact of food processing on human health and planetary health, with greater dietary exposure to ultra-processed foods being associated with a higher risk of adverse health outcomes. While athletes are encouraged to follow a food first approach, there are situations, where meeting performance goals is not achievable with everyday foods and drinks. In such cases, sports foods, protein-fortified foods, or discretionary foods, which due to their formulation are typically ultra-processed, may be recommended to help achieve specific goals. This review aimed to summarise the existing research on patterns of sports food and ultra-processed food use by athletes. A narrative review methodology was employed. Relevant literature was identified via searches in Google Scholar, Scopus, Web of Science and peak sporting body websites. There is little research on athletes and planetary health. Consumer awareness and knowledge of ultra-processed foods is an emerging area of research; no published studies in athletes were identified. The frequency of processed foods consumption by athletes has been explored in four small studies, with results suggesting frequent use of ultra-processed foods, though classification inconsistencies limit application. No studies specifically asked athletes about their reasons for consuming ultra-processed foods. Convenience and performance were common considerations for athletes when choosing general and sports foods. Benefits of ultra-processed food consumption include microbiological safety, convenience and ease of use. Current literature offers limited insight into how planetary health considerations influence athletes’ food choices, and athletes’ knowledge, awareness and consumption of ultra-processed foods. Further research is needed to understand the prevalence and patterns of use of sports foods and ultra-processed foods, and how dietitians/nutritionists can help athletes optimise their nutritional strategies which support performance and promote planetary health.Keywords: Ultra-processed food, Sports food, Athlete, Planetary health

Introduction

Since the ancient Olympic Games, a range of foods and/or drinks has been consumed by athletes with the goal of enhancing their performance. Historical records indicate that foods such as figs, bread, meat and wine were consumed by athletes in the ancient Olympic Games [1–3]. During the early 1900s there were anecdotal reports of athletes consuming brandy and champagne alongside rice and other foods during the Tour de France. Gatorade, the first formulated carbohydrate drink (“sports drink”) for athletes was developed in the mid-1960s [4] initiating a sports food industry that has grown exponentially over the last 60 years due to opportunities such as developments in food technology and production and funds investment [5]. The Australia New Zealand Food Standards Code includes a specific category for products that are specially formulated to provide a convenient form of nutritional support around exercise sessions [6]. Such sports foods, typically in the form of a drink, gel, confectionary and/or bar are considered to be processed foods and regulated under Standard 2.9.4 Formulated supplementary sports foods [6].

The Australian Institute of Sport (AIS) Sports Supplement Framework provides guidance on the use of sports foods and supplements within its ABCD classification system [7]. In this system, sports foods as well as supplement ingredients are ranked into four groups according to the scientific evidence and the following three key considerations: is it safe; is it effective; and is it permitted for use in sport? Sports foods are collated in a sub-category of Group A of the Framework, noting that there is strong evidence for their safe and effective use in certain situations in sport [7]. A separate food category, protein-fortified foods, is also of interest from a sport perspective because of its use by athletes to achieve exercise-associated protein intake targets [8]. While many do not fit within Standard 2.9.4 [6] and/or the ABCD classification system of the AIS Sports Supplement Framework [7], products such as high protein yoghurt, protein-fortified cereals and nut bars may contain added protein isolates (from animal or plant sources). For this reason, they have been the subject of an investigation [8] of their risk of contamination with substances included on the World Anti-Doping Agency’s Prohibited List, an issue of consideration in the use of all supplements and sports foods by high performance athletes [9]. Importantly, this investigation found that protein-fortified foods that are commercially manufactured within Australia present no additional risk of containing World Anti-Doping Agency prohibited substances than other processed foods with mixed ingredients [8].

Sports foods are not typically included in the “food first” approach preferred in sports nutrition. This philosophy, defined by Close et al. [10] notes that “where practically possible nutrient provision should come from standard food and drinks rather than from isolated food components, dietary supplements or sports foods”. Indeed, due to their highly processed nature there is a range of reasons to exclude sports foods. However, for athletes, any such exclusion must be balanced against the need to meet sports nutrition goals while also considering broader health outcomes. Over the past 10 years, there has been growing concern about the impact of food processing on both human health and planetary health. An umbrella review by Lane and colleagues [11] in 2024 highlighted the higher risk of adverse health outcomes associated with greater dietary exposure to ultra-processed foods (UPFs) in human populations across the lifespan, regardless of health status. In particular, the authors concluded the evidence related to adverse cardiometabolic and mental health was convincing and highly suggestive, which is of concern for an athlete’s health and their performance. Subsequently, in a systematic review of 104 prospective studies, 92 demonstrated an association between an ultra-processed dietary pattern and increased risk of chronic disease outcomes in the general adult population [12]. In addition to health issues, Anastasiou et al. [13] highlighted that UPFs are responsible for significant diet-related environmental impacts. Therefore, limiting intakes of highly processed foods represents positive action to achieve both human health and environmental sustainability [14].

Sports dietitians and other nutrition professionals working in sports nutrition are well-placed to shift their own practices as well as to influence those of athletes, coaches and performance support staff. They offer the potential to co-design and action transformational planetary health solutions which also consider performance goals [15]. These activities require an understanding of the prevalence of consumption of sports foods and other UPFs by athletes, and the rationale for their use. Such information would provide insight into their contribution to athletes’ nutrition and health status, the barriers and enablers to their use, and opportunities to change patterns of use while maintaining performance goals. To date, we are not aware of any publications that have systematically addressed the literature in this area. Therefore, the aim of this review is to summarise the existing research on patterns of sports food and UPF use by athletes. The following questions are considered: (1) what are UPFs?; (2) what are the benefits and disadvantages of the use of UPFs?; (3) what is the awareness, knowledge and understanding of UPFs in the general population and by athletes?; (4) how and why do athletes use UPFs and sports foods; and (5) what is the impact of UPF consumption in athletes on health, performance and planetary health?

Methods

A narrative review methodology was employed to synthesise the existing literature and to identify any research gaps. Relevant literature was identified via searches in Google Scholar, Scopus, Web of Science and peak sporting body websites. The search focused on peer-reviewed articles, reviews, and relevant reports published in the English language. Key terms included “ultra-process*”, “food”, “knowledge”, “awareness”, “consume*”, “athlete*”, “planetary health”, and “sustainabil*”. The search covered all records in databases searched from inception to December 2025. Articles were selected according to the relevance to the topic of the review. This narrative review was prepared according to the Academy of Nutrition and Dietetics Narrative Review checklist [16].

What Are Ultra-processed Foods?

The definition of the term “ultra-processed foods” has evolved since its inception in 2009 [17]. Currently, the most widely used definition is from the NOVA classification system developed by Monteiro and colleagues [18, 19]. This classification system recognises four categories of edible substances based on the nature, extent and purpose of their processing: (1) unprocessed and minimally processed foods; (2) processed culinary ingredients; (3) processed foods; and (4) ultra-processed foods. Table 2 provides an overview of the NOVA classification system, noting the composition, typical consumption/use patterns, and examples of foods within each category. It is critical to highlight, at least in the current version of the classification system, that foods and beverages are only assessed on the degree of processing and the additives added to the food or beverage rather than a more comprehensive assessment of nutrition, health or performance value and/or impact on planetary health/environmental sustainability. This limitation may be particularly relevant for athletes and other population groups with special nutrition requirements, where foods choices must consider not only health outcomes but also performance, recovery and training demands in contrast to the needs of the general population. Although it has been proposed that the concept of UPF could inform dietary guidelines beyond nutrient-based food classifications, there is ongoing debate about its suitability to do so [20–27]. Another food classification system, Siga, addresses several of the criticisms directed at the NOVA system; however, there are also gaps in the suitability of this system [26]. For instance, Siga’s categorisation framework allows for more precise identification of UPFs and provides stronger connections between processing levels, nutrient profiles, and potential health outcomes. The Siga categories are A0 unprocessed foods; A1 minimally processed foods; A2 culinary ingredients; B1 nutritionally balanced processed foods; B2 high salt, sugar and/or fat level processed foods; C0.1 nutritionally balanced UPFs; C0.2 high salt, sugar and/or fat level UPFs; C1 UPFs with more than one marker of ultra-processing.

Many examples of categorisation anomalies or ambiguities are used to show the limitations of the current NOVA classification system. For example, plain yoghurt is considered to be “minimally processed”, while some fruit yoghurts are categorised as “processed”, and flavoured yoghurt is “ultra-processed”. Meanwhile, homemade potato fries cooked with oil and salt, or Doritos® original salted flavoured corn chips (both considered to be processed food) are differentiated, respectively, from packaged potato fries cooked at home or Doritos® cheese supreme corn chips (UPF). Food Standards Australia New Zealand, who set the standards for safe food in Australia, currently do not require food manufacturers to label packaged foods and beverages with information about processing technique(s). This is also the case in other countries, making it challenging to determine into which category a packaged food or beverage falls. Certainly, it has been noted that the use of the NOVA categorisation system in the research setting sometimes causes discordance, even among food and nutrition experts and when ingredient lists are available [28, 29]. Comparisons between researcher-generated lists will also likely reveal discordance as these lists reflect a specific timepoint and setting. Product reformulation by manufacturers may alter how a product is categorised in the future. Therefore, these lists are of limited relevance for use by the general population unless the foods they contain and their categorisation are first reviewed.

Future activities may produce a refined and enhanced alternative/version of the NOVA classification. Indeed, work by the Task Force on Food Processing for Nutrition, Diet and Health developed the formulation and processing food index, which considers both the levels of food product formulation and processing [30]. However, in the meantime, and in the absence of labelling, Monteiro et al. [19] provide the following guidance to determine if a food is ultra-processed or not:

The ingredient list of a food or beverage product contains at least one item characteristic of the UPF group and/or

The ingredient list of a food or beverage product contains at least one item characteristic of the UPF group and/or

The items include a food substance not used or rarely used in a domestic kitchen or contains one or more of the classes of additives (e.g., hydrolysed proteins, fructose, maltodextrin, flavour enhancers, thickeners, emulsifiers, and gelling agents) whose function is to make the final produce palatable or more appealing.

Benefits and disadvantages of consuming ultra processed foods

Broadly, these aspects cover the functional role of UPFs in performance contexts and situations, where UPFs may be necessary or advantageous while acknowledging the need to balance performance benefits with overall dietary quality considerations, potential long-term health and planetary health outcomes. Research has typically focused on the longer-term impacts of UPF consumption in the general population, with evidence predominantly from observational studies, rather than the immediate or short-term impacts. Here, it is also noted that such research has focused predominantly on the general population, with little specifically targeting athletes. Furthermore, the analyses conducted by researchers have tended to be on UPF consumption as a whole rather than consumption of sub-groups of UPFs. The authors of a multinational cohort study found that certain sub-groups of UPFs were associated with a higher risk of multimorbidity of cancer and cardiometabolic diseases [31]. The most notable associations were for the animal-based products, and artificially and sugar-sweetened beverages subgroups, whereas the ultra-processed breads and cereals subgroup was inversely associated with risk, however, with borderline certainty. Other sub-groups such as plant-based alternatives, savoury snacks, sweets and desserts, ready to eat foods showed no association with risk of multimorbidity. In a systematic review there was strong evidence that consumption of ready-to-eat cereal, particularly whole grain cereals, is associated with reduced cardiovascular disease and mortality [32]. Friden et al. [33] reported that although most studies included on UPF breads and cereals failed to show associations with the risk of type 2 diabetes in their systematic review, the associations across other UPF types varied in both strength and direction. To date, no studies have specifically investigated sports food consumption and health outcomes in athletes or the general population. There is emerging evidence suggesting certain types of sports food may have higher environmental impacts. For example, an Australian study found that ultra-processed water-based beverages (e.g., sports drinks and fruit drinks) have higher environmental impacts than bottled water [38].

Awareness, Understanding and Recognition of UPFs in the General Population and by Athletes

Although the rationale for the beneficial use of sports foods (Table 1) and UPFs (Table 2) by athletes has been identified, explicit discussion regarding the classification of these products as UPFs is an emerging area of interest. Research examining general consumer awareness and understanding, as well as identifying of UPFs is a relatively new research theme, and to date no studies have explored these in athlete populations or investigated how consumers classify sports foods. Consequently, our current understanding is limited to studies conducted in the general population which are discussed below. These studies relied primarily on self-reported questionnaire data, and study populations may not be representative of the broader population. In addition, differences in questionnaire design, question wording, and the categorisation of foods across countries may introduce measurement bias and limit comparability between studies. As these studies employed a cross-sectional design, they provide only a snapshot of awareness and understanding of UPFs and, therefore, cannot determine whether levels of knowledge and awareness in the general population have changed over time, since the studies were conducted.

Awareness and Understanding

The eight studies summarised in Table 4 investigated consumers’ (n = 7), and nutrition professionals’ (n = 1) awareness and/or understanding of the term “ultra-processed foods” with mixed results observed. Even when familiarity with the term “ultra-processed food” in the adult population groups surveyed appears to be high, this differs with the degree of understanding. Studies of nutrition professionals are of particular interest, given their role in education and counselling. Despite over 50% of surveyed dietitians being unfamiliar with NOVA, 90% reported being familiar with the advice to limit the intake of UPFs.

Recognition of UPFs

The findings are also varied when respondents were asked to list or classify UPF foods or classify foods according to the NOVA classification system. Uruguayan workers, excluding those with a medicine or nutrition background, were asked to list UPFs, with 84% of foods listed corresponding to the UPF category [57]. In a Brazilian study, 92% of participants correctly identified soft drink as UPF, but loaf bread and potato chips with salt were only recognised by 56% and 62%, respectively [61]. Similarly, only 58% of participants correctly identified packaged bread as a UPF, along with 57% and 51% recognition of packaged breakfast cereals and ice cream [60]. There were mixed results for classification of non UPF products, including fruit in syrup (63%), pasteurised yoghurt (80%), salted nuts (79%), and smoked meat (51%) [60]. In another study, dietitians were asked to identify which grains foods are classified as ultra-processed and for the majority of these foods including wheat bran cereal, muesli, and rye bread less than 25% of dietitians accurately classified them [29]. In addition, it appears that some food categories/products are correctly classified more often.

Further insight can be gained from study by Pedro-Botet et al. [59], discussed earlier in this section, about which products had the highest correct or incorrect classifications. Chips, crab sticks, fish canned in olive oil, goat cheese, guacamole, and microwave noodles had the highest percentage of correct classifications, whereas fish canned in sunflower oil, jam without added sugar, muesli without added sugar, fruit yogurt, peaches in syrup, Catalan sausage, and packaged meatballs had the highest percentage of incorrect classifications. In the British study, Robinson et al. [60] also asked participants to rate their confidence in identifying whether a food is a UPF or not, with 39% selecting yes, they were confident, 31% no and 30% unsure. On average in this study 2.7/5 of UPFs and 3.5/5 of non-UPFs shown were correctly identified, with only 13% of participants correctly categorising all UPFs, indicating that confidence in identifying UPFs does not necessarily match with the ability to do so.

Athletes, UPFs and Sports Foods

Athletes may consume UPFs as part of their general diet, as well as to address the specific needs of their sporting activities, particularly around exercise sessions. Insights into the rationale for intake of UPFs is also important in confirming intake data and forming the basis for behaviour change if needed. Given that sports foods are certainly UPFs, there is interest in understanding how these contribute to total dietary intake and whether opportunities to substitute everyday foods in their place can reduce total intake of UPFs. Present knowledge on these themes is now presented.

General Understanding of Determinants of Food Choice in Athletes

A systematic scoping review by Pelly et al. [64] on the determinants of food choice in athletes found that performance and health were relevant to athlete food choice, with other determinants such as experience, cultural background, and competition season having a varying level of impact. They concluded that more research was needed in different cohorts of athletes, particularly females. Two recent studies provide further insights on factors affecting food choices [65, 66]. In a cross-sectional study involving 1145 athletes (62% female, 67% competing at local level or higher, 87% living in Ireland) who completed the Athlete Food Choice Questionnaire, the most influential factor in food choice was “sensory appeal” followed by “food and health awareness”, and “performance” [65]. For the latter two, this was particularly seen in those competing at high levels. Conversely “food values and beliefs” ranked lowest on average in influencing the decision making of athletes. The most influential individual food choice statements, by mean rank score, were “the taste of the food” (28.90), “the flavor of the food” (27.06), and the “nutritional content of the food” (26.10) [65]. Meanwhile, the three lowest ranked statements were “my religious food beliefs” (3.77), “how angry I feel” (8.33), and “what my friends are eating “(10.12). Foo et al. [66] interviewed 10 professional English Premier League players to determine their perceptions of the nutrition culture in the professional soccer environment. The researchers found that the soccer players’ nutrition practices and food preferences were influenced by several factors including personal upbringing, i.e., familial, ethnic and religious backgrounds, and the club environment [66]. Dietary practices of the soccer players were also influenced by managers/coaches, teammates and online influences, and economic resources impact on nutrition provision.

Further insight can be gained about the influences on an athlete’s food choices from the perspective of sports dietitians. Convenience, taste preference and advice from dietitians were noted to be the main influences of athletes’ food choices in phone interviews of 42 American Sports Registered Dietitian Nutritionist [67]. The Sports Registered Dietitian Nutritionists also believed that other influencers of performance, health and nutrition advice were coaches/teammates and that athletes were also influenced by “what they see in the media”. A limitation of this study is that Registered Dietitian Nutritionists provided general information about their clients and may have not included important behaviours/influencers during the interview. The potential mechanisms of teammate influence on the eating attitudes and behaviours of athletes were investigated in a systematic review, with teammate support and pressures being explored [68]. Teammate support included supportive friendships, promotion of healthy eating, vigilance against disordered eating, while for teammate pressures, there were conflicting teammate friendships, critical comments and appearance conversations, maladaptive team norms and competitive comparisons [68].

The highest rated factors influencing food choices during competition of 101 experienced ultra-endurance athletes (73% male) who had completed at least one event (running events of at least 42km, cycling events of at least 100km) were “does not cause GI discomfort”, “provides me with energy” and “nutritious” [69]. Convenience and performance-related factors were also considered important with “can be carried easily”, “can be consumed easily”, “does not compromise ability to train or compete”, “helps me cope with high training and competition demands” being highly rated. Convenience was a key factor reported in two other studies, with Australian athletes reporting convenience both in terms of being easily transported as well as able being suitable to carry during a race [70]. Meanwhile, German endurance athletes reported that although everyday foods were preferred, there were some situations in which sports foods were more convenient or easier/time saving to consume [71]. Improved performance, endurance, muscle strength and recovery was also rated highly as a factor by respondents in the Australian study, whereas cost and taste were the most frequently reported reasons for not consuming these products [70]. Further insight can be gained by considering the factors which were selected the least often by German endurance athletes: price, sensory appeal, social influences, because their nutritionist or coach told them to choose that carbohydrate source [71].

Research on planetary health, environmental sustainability and/or climate considerations in food consumption of athletes is limited, with most questionnaires focusing on performance and health. In a Swedish study with a mixed-method approach, nine recreational CrossFit athletes completed 7-day food diaries and then a follow-up interview to help gain an understanding of their food consumption practices and the possible role of climate consideration [72]. Many of the participants reported wanting to avoid ultra-processed and semi-processed foods. This same view did not extend to whey protein concentrate, which participants did not consider to be problematic either from its processing profile or its contribution to sustainability goals. The authors concluded that the primary purpose of food consumption for recreational CrossFit athletes is to maintain and improve their performance [72]. Sustainability characteristics were also found by Carey et al. [65] to have minimal influence on athletes’ food choices with the statement “if the food is sustainably produced” being placed in the lowest quartile of influencing characteristics.

Use of Sports Foods and UPFs by Athletes

There are several methods available to assess UPF and/or sports food intake including self-reported questionnaires, food frequency questionnaires (FFQs), dietary recalls and food records/diaries. However, when tools only assess specific categories of food rather than the entire diet, we are not able to determine how these foods contribute to total energy intake or how they fit within the overall diet pattern. Another limitation is the different approaches researchers use to classify the foods/sports foods. Seven studies were identified with athlete populations and all used self-reported questionnaires or FFQs to determine intake of UPF and/or sports food. A summary of the findings is below.

An exploratory survey of the use and attitudes towards sports foods by Australian athletes and exercisers found that nearly all participants reported consuming these products within the past 12 months [70]. In another study, where German speaking triathletes, cyclists and runners completed a questionnaire on carbohydrate intake during training, nearly 70% of respondents reported that they used a combination of commercial sport nutrition products and everyday foods [71]. Triathletes were more likely than runners to report using a combination, and males were more likely to report consuming this combination than females [71]. It was not clear in this study what was categorised as everyday foods.

Amongst male varsity badminton players in Pakistan, 53 participants (95%) reported intake of highly processed foods at a consumption level that was assessed as low, two participants (3.6%) as medium and one participant (1.5%) with a high consumption level [73]. More non-athlete students in this study reported intake in the high (8.9%) and medium (64.3%) consumption level groups compared to the athlete group. Here, it is noted that the study tool, sQ-HPF was based on 14 food groups originally selected for a food frequency questionnaire in the Spanish Prevention with Mediterranean Diet trial, used without modification [74]. The justification for this decision and the inherent bias is unknown.

The frequency with which athletes consume processed foods has been investigated in four European studies. According to a FFQ included in the Nutrition and Running high Mileage (NURMI) Study, male endurance runners reported a significantly higher consumption of processed foods and processed meats compared to females, with more than 5% difference between the groups [75]. Consumption was determined by assessing how often and how much of the dietary items were consumed. The dietary items were grouped into defined food clusters. The use of a FFQ limited the ability to quantify total energy and macro- and micronutrient intakes. In a study of Romanian male soccer players, 27% reported never consuming processed foods, 42% once weekly, 27% 2–3 times weekly and 5% daily [76]. The relatively low prevalence of consumption may reflect limited food examples in the survey question “how often do you consume processed foods (chips, fast food etc.)?” potentially leading participants to exclude other processed items (e.g., flavoured yogurt) from their responses.

In a Spanish study where reported dietary intake of recreational cyclists and triathletes was assessed, females reported a lower intake of UPFs, and higher intake of unprocessed foods compared to males [77]. In contrast, another study by the same authors reported no significant difference in reported intake of UPFs between male and female elite cyclists [78]. Muros et al. [77] also found triathletes had a higher reported intake of unprocessed foods than cyclists. However, this result may only be limited to a comparison of the specific UPFs identified in this study (soft drinks, sweet or savoury packaged snacks, reconstituted meat products, and pre-prepared frozen dishes) as it does not appear that other UPFs such as flavoured milk or store-bought bread were included in the analysis. This was the only study out of the four where estimated energy intake was presented. However, the contribution of different types of foods (i.e., dairy, fruits, sugar and sweets, unprocessed foods) was not reported, nor intake of UPFs as a percentage of total energy intake which limits comparison.

Research in this area is sparse and combined with the use of different questionnaires to collect dietary data as well as differing approaches to classification of processed foods, makes comparing results challenging. Only two of the studies [77, 78] provided detail on reported intake of UPFs; however, this did not appear to encompass all possible UPF products. Furthermore, the reported studies do not contain sufficient detail to determine intake of UPFs as a percentage of total energy intake, which is important to assess the potential impact of UPFs on athlete health as well as make comparisons across populations and studies. Inferences from other population groups are limited, as data from 21 countries shows substantial variability in the percent of total energy intake from UPF, ranging as high as 50% in the United States of America and the United Kingdom, to as low as 10% in Italy [79].

Substitution of Everyday Food and Drink Alternatives to Sports Foods to Reduce UPF Intake

Dietitians and nutritionists often employ a “food first” approach when working with athletes. However, this approach can include recommendations for highly processed foods and drinks within the definition of “everyday” foods to achieve an athlete’s nutritional or performance goals. In situations where meeting these goals is not achievable with everyday foods and drinks, sports foods, protein fortified foods and/or occasional/discretionary foods may be recommended (see Table 1 for scenarios). As previously discussed, the majority of these foods are also classified as ultra-processed under the NOVA food classification system [19].

Indeed, in an Australian study, where products in the FoodSwitch app were reviewed, 81% of the 13 456 discretionary foods aligned with the UPF classification, while 40% of the 14 615 core foods were classified as ultra-processed [80]. Supplementary foods were excluded from this analysis, and as this app only contains packaged foods and beverages, unpackaged core or discretionary foods are not counted here. In the sub-category analysis, confectionary foods had the highest alignment with the UPF classification (96%), then foods for specific dietary use (i.e., specific medical purposes) [80]. Foods from two of these sub-categories (muesli bars and lollies) were listed by Australian athletes as everyday food alternatives for sports food [70].

In a systematic review of 15 studies where food sources (candy bar, banana, raisins and potato) were compared to carbohydrate supplements it was reported that there were little/no differences in running or cycling performance/capacity between athletes [81]. Likewise, researchers in Spain demonstrated that the nutrition needs of ultra endurance athletes could be met with sustainable, organic, locally, seasonally, and unprocessed and minimally processed food without using packaging or plastic waste during an 18-h challenge [82]. The practicality of doing this outside of a controlled setting such as a laboratory for all athletes needs further investigation. Even so, there will likely be times when an UPF is the most practical, convenient and/or safe option to meet an athlete’s performance needs.

Sports nutrition resources such as Sports Dietitians Australia fact sheets [83, 84] and the sports foods infographics available on the AIS supplements website [85] provide guidance to athletes about everyday foods and drinks that could be used as replacements for sports foods. While these substitutions may have practical outcomes in terms of expense, the effect on intake of UPF has not previously been considered. To address this, we classified the “food-first swaps” for each sports food category identified in the AIS Sports Supplement Framework resources according to the NOVA groups (Table 5). Where foods were presented in combination, i.e., banana and muesli bar they were disaggregated and assessed as single food items. Some food items appear in multiple categories, for example, yoghurt, as depending on ingredients added and processing undertaken could be minimally processed, processed or ultra-processed. Each sports food category includes several food items which align with the different NOVA classification categories, with the exception of the processed culinary ingredients. There are certainly more potential swaps than those presented in the infographics, and additional combinations could be considered. For instance, while honey is mentioned when paired with another food item, it could also be a standalone substitute for a sports gel. For athletes, decision making around appropriate food and drink choices may be complicated by the coexistence of several food classification systems and navigating general population and athlete specific nutrition guidelines. Furthermore, the practicality of implementing these food first swaps across different environments, during training and competition, and across different sports will likely also influence decision making and warrants further investigation. Nevertheless, this exercise demonstrated that a “food first approach” to the use of sports foods does not necessarily guarantee an improvement in dietary quality.

Impact of UPF and Sports Food Use by Athletes

There are no published research studies indicating that UPF consumption creates specific problems for athletes such as adverse sleep-related or health outcomes, increased obesity risk, or changes to the gut microbiome similar to those reported in the general population (Table 3). Despite this, Australian athletes and exercisers appear concerned about the potential health effects of UPFs, with half of survey respondents reporting such concerns [70]. Evidence from the general population, therefore, raises concerns for both current and former athletes, especially when considered alongside the higher prevalence of specific health risks reported in athlete populations.

Although, regular physical activity/exercise and a healthy diet can prevent, or delay chronic diseases [86], athletes are not immune to health risks. Former athletes appear to have better cardiometabolic indicators and leaner body composition than non-athlete controls [87], and lower cardiovascular disease (CVD) incidence in male athletes compared with the general population [88] yet sport-specific differences exist. Retired field-based athletes had a comparable CVD risk profile with the general population [89] and power sport athletes have no significant difference in CVD mortality to the general population [88]. Mental health outcomes on the other hand vary and there is evidence of sport-specific differences. In a 2024 meta-analysis, the timepoint prevalence of depression and anxiety in former athletes was over twice that of the general population [90]. Sport specific differences are also evident with American football players and jockeys having the highest prevalence of depression, and a threefold higher prevalence of anxiety observed in former cricket players. In contrast the prevalence of anxiety and depression in rugby players were not significantly different from the general population [90].

While quantification of the specific environmental impact of UPF use among athletes is not available, evidence from the broader literature suggests that athletes may have a higher environmental food-related footprint for several reasons. First, athletes generally require higher total dietary energy intake than the general population, and a systematic review of Australian data found a positive correlation between total dietary energy intake and environmental impacts scores [91]. Second, athletes often require higher protein intakes, and protein-rich foods represent a significant contributor to environmental impacts in Australia [91]. Reducing protein intake or swapping to solely plant-based proteins may not be feasible for athletes and could lead to unintended negative consequences for health and performance. Data on Turkish elite athletes indicates they have higher greenhouse gas emissions and water footprint values compared to the general population [92]. Both environmental impact indicators were strongly associated with red meat and animal protein consumption, with reported consumption of these by the athletes in the study higher also than the general population. Acar et al. [92] also examined results across athlete categories with male athletes and strength/power athletes having significantly higher dietary environmental impacts than female and team sports athletes. One limitation of this study was the inability to estimate the athletes’ total energy intake, thereby limiting meaningful comparison. Third, given that diets rich in UPFs are associated with high greenhouse gas emissions and energy use [37], greater dietary reliance on UPFs to help meet athletes’ higher energy and/or protein requirements may further increase the environmental impact.

While there is strong evidence for the use of sports foods in specific situations in sport [7], there is only one study to date that has looked at the effect of high UPF consumption in an athlete’s diet on fitness/performance measures [93]. There were no differences between low versus high consumption in terms of anthropometric characteristics and cardiovascular fitness. However, a confounder was that athletes did not appear to be eating optimal amounts of carbohydrate (average 4.1 g/kg/day) needed for performance [94] and fat contribution (41.4% of total energy intake) was higher than for general nutrition recommendations [94]. The authors were not able to determine if the energy intake of the athletes was adequate. Research is needed to understand the impact of UPF use by athletes on fitness/performance measures.

Future Research

The existing literature provides limited insight into considerations of planetary health and environmental sustainability by athletes in their food choices including sports foods, as well as athletes’ knowledge, awareness and consumption of UPFs. Figure 1 provides an overview of the key research themes identified to address these gaps. Future research should also explore whether differences exist according to athlete characteristics, such as sex and competition level. Meyer and Reguant-Closa [95] highlighted the importance of introducing athletes and their support staff to the link between food choices, health and sustainability. Sports dietitians/nutritionists play a key role in helping athletes optimise nutritional strategies that support performance while promoting sustainable dietary practices. Building on this, Meyer et al. [96] emphasised the specific role of sports dietitians/nutritionists in guiding protein source selection, including their quantity, quality and timing in an athlete’s diet. Therefore, it is essential to provide clear guidance for dietitians, coaches and high-performance staff who support athletes regarding the use of UPFs.

Conclusion

The existing literature provides limited insight into considerations of planetary health by athletes in their food choices. Research on athletes’ knowledge, awareness and consumption of UPFs remains limited, yet there are aspects related to consumption which may be of special interest for athletes. Further research is needed to understand the prevalence and patterns of use of sports foods and UPFs, and how dietitians/nutritionists can help athletes optimise nutrition strategies that support performance, personal health and promote planetary health.
