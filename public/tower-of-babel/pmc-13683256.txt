Integrated transcriptomics reveals a TNFRSF-metabolism axis and a context-dependent BCMA signaling state in myeloma progression
Authors: Wei-Han Huang, Mohammed Thangameeran Shaik Ismail, Chih-Wen Peng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMultiple myeloma (MM) evolves from monoclonal gammopathy of undetermined significance (MGUS) and smoldering multiple myeloma (SMM) with extensive metabolic reprogramming and immune remodeling. We investigated whether coordinated activation of the tumor necrosis factor receptor superfamily (TNFRSF) aligns with metabolic state and prognosis across this continuum. We integrated bone-marrow single-cell RNA sequencing from healthy donors, MGUS, SMM, and MM with bulk RNA sequencing from the Multiple Myeloma Research Foundation (MMRF) CoMMpass cohort. At single-cell resolution, per-cell metabolic activity scores increased with disease stage and were highest in malignant plasma cells. TNF/TNFRSF signaling was strongest in MM, and expression of TNFRSF17 (B-cell maturation antigen, BCMA) in plasma cells correlated with metabolic activity (Spearman ρ = 0.56 in MM). In the clinical cohort, a pooled TNFRSF score stratified overall survival (log-rank p < 0.0001), with individual associations for TNFRSF17 and TNFRSF1A/1B. Joint stratification by TNFRSF activation and metabolic activity revealed the poorest outcomes in tumors with low TNFRSF activation but high metabolic activity, indicating that metabolic state contributes to adverse risk in a receptor-context–dependent manner, while TNFRSF captured additional risk not explained by metabolism alone. When focusing specifically on BCMA, the prognostic ordering across BCMA x metabolism strata was not identical to the pooled TNFRSF model, underscoring that metabolic risk is modulated by the receptor state rather than acting as a universal driver. These findings define an integrated TNFRSF-metabolism axis and a context-dependent BCMA state in advanced MM, clinically relevant but adjustment-sensitive survival associations that warrant further validation and combination strategies that explicitly consider metabolic status.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s00277-026-07192-w.Keywords: Multiple myeloma, TNF receptor superfamily, BCMA, Metabolic reprogramming, Single-cell transcriptomics

Introduction

Multiple myeloma (MM) evolves from monoclonal gammopathy of undetermined significance (MGUS) and smoldering multiple myeloma (SMM) with extensive metabolic reprogramming and immune-niche remodeling that support survival and therapy resistance [1–4]. The marrow microenvironment becomes immunosuppressive, with increased regulatory T cells, myeloid-derived suppressor cells, tumor-associated macrophages, and dysfunctional dendritic cells, with impaired antigen presentation and diminished T-cell effector function [5–7]. Metabolic plasticity (glutaminolysis, NAD+ salvage, oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS), glycolysis, lipophagy) intensifies from MGUS through MM and relates to immune infiltration [8–16].

The tumor necrosis factor receptor superfamily (TNFRSF) comprises key receptors such as TNFRSF17 (BCMA), TNFRSF13B (TACI), and TNFRSF1A/B, which orchestrate immune response, apoptosis, and cytokine signaling [17, 18]. TNFRSF signaling also shapes cellular metabolism: in T cells (e.g., TNFRSF9/4-1BB and TNFRSF4/OX40 enhance glycolysis and sustain function) and in B/plasma cells (TNFRSF13B/TACI, TNFRSF17/BCMA, TNFRSF13C/BAFF-R interface with mTOR and NF-κB) [19–22].

Monocyte-driven inflammation may amplify BAFF/APRIL and secondarily tune TNFRSF activity on plasma cells [23]. Yet how TNFRSF programs align with metabolic states across MGUS-SMM-MM and how this impacts response to BCMA-derived therapies remains unclear. To address this gap, we integrated MM progression scRNA-seq (GSE271107) with AUCell-based metabolic scoring, CellChat ligand-receptor inference, and MMRF CoMMpass bulk RNA-seq survival modeling, linking TNFRSF expression to metabolic phenotypes, intercellular signaling, and outcome across MGUS-SMM-MM continuum.

Materials and methods

Data acquisition and preprocessing (scRNA-seq)

Single-cell RNA-seq data from healthy HD, MGUS, SMM and MM bone marrow (GEO GSE271107) were processed in R (v4.5.0) using Seurat (v5.3.0). The analyzed cohort comprised 19 samples in total: HD (n = 5), MGUS (n = 6), SMM (n = 4), and MM (n = 4). Raw 10x Genomics .h5 files were loaded, merged, and quality-filtered; mitochondrial content was calculated using PercentageFeatureSet (pattern = “^MT-”) and cells with < 200 or > 6,000 detected genes or > 15% mitochondrial RNA was excluded. Data were normalized using NormalizeData, 2,000 variable features were identified using the VST method, and the data were scaled prior to PCA. A shared nearest-neighbor graph was constructed using FindNeighbors (dims = 1:20, k.param = 20), and clustering was performed using FindClusters at resolution = 0.5 UMAP visualization was generated from the first 20 principal components using the uwot backend (n.neighbors = 30, min.dist = 0.3, metric = “cosine”, seed.use = 42). Counts were normalized, variable features selected (VST), and data scaled. Dimensionality reduction used PCA and UMAP (top 20 PCs). Per-sample cell yields and disease-group totals are provided in Supplementary Table S1. The final clustering resolution was selected based on cluster interpretability and the balance between stable major population separation and avoidance of over-fragmentation of closely related subpopulations.

Cell type annotation and integration

Graph-based clustering (Seurat FindNeighbors/FindClusters on the top PCs) defined communities, which were visualized with UMAP. Cell identities were assigned after unsupervised clustering by identifying cluster-enriched marker genes using FindAllMarkers, followed by manual annotation based on canonical lineage markers and the overall marker-expression patterns of each cluster. Annotation was cross-checked against published human bone marrow single-cell reference atlases and curated marker resources, including The Human Cell Atlas bone marrow reference [24], human bone marrow single-cell assessment study [25], human bone marrow single-cell reference atlas [26], and CellMarker 2.0 [27]. Monocyte and dendritic-cell subset assignments were additionally supported by published single-cell definitions of human blood monocyte and dendritic-cell populations [28]. Representative cluster-enriched marker genes supporting cell-type annotations are provided in Supplementary Table S2.

Metabolic activity scoring

Single-cell metabolic activity was quantified using AUCell from a single pooled metabolism-related gene set rather than by scoring each pathway separately. The input gene set was assembled from MSigDB curated pathway gene sets retrieved through the msigdbr R package, after filtering pathway names using metabolism-related keywords, including “metabolism,” “glycolysis,” “gluconeogenesis,” “lipid,” “fatty acid,” “carbohydrate,” “amino acid,” “nucleotide,” “energy,” “oxidative,” “oxidation,” “biosynthesis,” “catabolism,” and “biosynthetic.” Genes from the matched pathways were then collapsed into a non-redundant list, yielding, 4,885 unique genes before restriction to genes present in the scRNA-seq dataset. AUCell ranking were constructed at the single-cell level, and AUC values were calculated using aucMaxRank = ceiling (0.05 x number of ranked genes). The resulting Metabolic_Score was retained as a continuous per-cell score, and no hard AUCell threshold was applied. The full source pathway list, pathway-to-gene mapping, and final metabolic gene list used for AUCell scoring are provided in Supplementary Table S3.

TNFRSF expression analysis

Expression dynamics of the TNFRSF family were profiled across disease states in the integrated single-cell dataset. A prespecified panel of 21 TNFRSF genes was extracted from the integrated object (Supplementary Table S4). Expression patterns were visualized across cell types and disease using Seurat-based dot plots together with violin/box plots generated in ggplot2, and average expression summaries were displayed as heatmaps using pheatmap. Where stage trends were performed, disease stage was modeled as an ordinal variable (HD = 1, MGUS = 2, SMM = 3, MM = 4).

Cell-Cell Communication (CellChat)

Intercellular signaling was inferred with CellChat (v2.2.0) [29]. For CellChat analysis, cells were grouped using a condition-level aggregation strategy. Specifically, separate CellChat objects were constructed from each disease category (HD, MGUS, SMM, and MM), and communication inference was performed independently within each condition-specific object. Thus, cells from all samples belonging to a given condition were pooled prior to CellChat inference, and the resulting signaling networks were compared across condition-level communication models rather than by first performing sample-level CellChat analyses followed by integration. This strategy was used to increase the robustness of ligand-receptor inference for shared cell populations across disease states and to reduce sparsity associated with sample-level analyses.

CellChat communication probabilities were inferred using the standard computeCommunProb framework, which estimates intercellular communication based on ligand and receptor expression together with the curated CellChatDB prior, including multimeric ligand-receptor complexes and cofactors such as agonists, antagonists, and co-receptors. CellChat was run in label-based mode using the annotated cell-type identities. The relevant inference parameters were type.mean = “trimean”, trim = 0.1, raw.use = TRUE, population.size = FALSE, nboot = 100, seed.use = 1, Kh = 0.5, n = 1, and contact.dependent = FALSE. The analysis used CellChatDB v1, including interaction categories such as Secreted Signaling, ECM-Receptor, Cell-Cell Contact, and Non-protein Signaling. At the ligand-receptor level, inferred communication probabilities are stored in object@net$prob, whereas pathway-level communication probabilities are stored in object@netP$prob.

Total TNFRSF interaction strength was calculated as the sum of the relevant communication probabilities across selected TNFRSF/TNF-family ligand-receptor interactions and sender-receiver cell-type pairs within each condition-specific object. TNF-specific strength was calculated as the sum of pathway-level communication probabilities for the TNF signaling pathway across sender-receiver cell-type pairs. CellChat incorporates curated ligand-receptor complex structure and cofactor information from CellChatDB, but does not explicitly fit pair-specific biochemical binding affinity constants as separate model parameters. To provide orthogonal support for the inferred monocyte to CD8 TNF axis, donor-level summaries were additionally computed across the 19 samples for TNF expression in monocyte subsets, TNFRSF1A/TNFRSF1B expression in CD8 T cells, and a CD8 TNF-response signature (Fig. S1A-E).

Bulk RNA-seq and TNFRSF signatures (CoMMpass)

Bulk RNA-seq and clinical data from MMRF CoMMpass (IA18) were obtained for baseline characterization and survival modeling. A clinical baseline summary table for the analyzed CoMMpass cohort, including demographic variables, ISS stage, ECOG performance status, treatment-class variables, treatment-line burden-related variables, and covariate missingness, is provided in Supplementary Table S5. In the clinical flat file used for baseline characterization, 1,143 patients were available; median age was 63 years (IQR 56–70), and ISS stage distribution was Stage I 35.0%, Stage II 35.1%, and Stage III 27.3%, with ISS stage missing in 30/1,143 patients (2.6%). Bulk RNA-seq and clinical data from MMRF CoMMpass (IA18) cohort were harmonized to HUGO Gene Nomenclature Committee (HGNC)-approved gene symbols. Only TNFRSF genes with reliable HGNC mapping and available expression values in the CoMMpass expression matrix were retained for pooled-score construction (n = 18; Supplementary Table S6).

The initial merged CoMMpass objected contained 906 sample-level rows. To generate a harmonized patient-level cohort for downstream analyses, one representative sample per participant was retained using a predefined rule: earliest available visit, with bone marrow preferred when multiple samples were available. This yielded a representative patient-level cohort of N = 791. Analyses involving patient-level expression, pooled TNFRSF score, metabolic index, and APRIL-BCMA program score that did not require survival follow-up were performed on this harmonized cohort. Survival analyses were further restricted to participants with positive overall survival time (OS_time > 0), yielding N = 788. Survival time (oscdy) and status (censos) were merged by Participant_ID. For each retained gene, patients were dichotomized into High versus Low at the cohort median expression. Kaplan-Meier curves (survival v3.5-8; survminer v0.4.9) with log-rank tests evaluated associations.

A pooled TNFRSF score was computed per patient by binarizing each retained genes (High = 1, Low = 0) and summing, across the retained TNFRSF members. Genes that were not reliably mapped or not available in the dataset were not imputed and were excluded from score construction. No alternative gene substitutions were introduced when applying the scoring framework across datasets; only directly matched genes retained after harmonization were used. As sensitivity analyses, we additionally evaluated quartile-based stratification of the pooled TNFRSF score and an alternative penalized Cox-derived TNFRSF score. Because the more conservative lambda.1se solution selected no genes, the penalized model was based on the lambda.min solution and is presented as an exploratory sensitivity analysis rather than a finalized prognostic classifier. For the bulk metabolic summary, raw count data were normalized using edgeR calcNormFactors() and transformed to logCPM using edgeR cpm() with log = TRUE and prior.count = 1. Expression values were then restricted to KEGG metabolic genes present in the expression matrix (n = 1,380 in the present dataset). For each retained gene, expression was standardized across samples by row-wise Z-scoring, and the final Metabolic_Index for each sample was defined as the column-wise mean of these gene-wise Z-scores. For downstream Cox analyses, Metabolic_z was used as the standardized version of this per-sample index.

APRIL-BCMA associated program score

The APRIL-BCMA associated program score was derived from the APRIL/BCMA-responsive genes reported in the previous study [23]. Specifically, the score was calculated from the following genes: CCND2, MYC, MCL1, BIRC3, CD44, ICAM1, CCL3, VEGFA, IL10, CD274, TGFB1, PECAM1, and CXCL8. These corresponding to the APRIL/BCMA-associated genes reported in [23], with standard gene symbols used in our present study. For score calculation, normalized expression values were extracted from the genes present in the expression matrix, each gene was standardized across samples as a Z-score, and the final APRIL-BCMA score for each sample was defined as the mean of these gene-wise Z-scores.

Multivariable survival modeling

Multivariable Cox proportional hazards analyses were performed in the MMRF CoMMpass cohort to evaluate whether the TNFRSF score and metabolic index retained prognostic significance after clinical adjustment. In the primary models, covariates included age, ISS stage, and the treatment-line burden (maxline), using complete-case data (n = 771). In additional sensitivity models, gender and ECOG performance status were further included, using complete-case data (n = 576). Hazard ratios are reported per 1-SD increase for continuous predictors. Proportional hazards assumptions were evaluated using standard model diagnostics; evidence of non-proportionality was driven mainly by the maxline covariate. Full multivariable model outputs, complete-case count, and the diagnostic results are provided in the Supplementary Table S5.

Pathway-level correlation analysis

To provide indirect mechanistic context for TNFRSF-metabolic coupling, additional pathway-level scores were calculated in plasma cells for NF-κB, mTOR, glycolysis, OXPHOS, hypoxia, and related programs, and correlated with both the plasma-cell TNFRSF core score and Metabolic_Score using Spearman correlation with Benjamini-Hochberg adjustment (Supplementary Table S7 & Fig, S2A-B).

Visualization and statistics

Analyses were performed in R (v4.5.0) with Seurat/ggplot2/pheatmap. Group comparisons used ANOVA or Wilcoxon tests as appropriate; multiple testing was controlled by Benjamini–Hochberg FDR unless stated otherwise. Figures show effect estimates with sample sizes; full model outputs are in the Supplement. To reduce pseudoreplication risk, key scRNA-seq comparisons were reanalyzed using donor-level aggregation (per-sample summaries/pseudo-bulk-style analysis) and complementary mixed-effects models, rather than relying exclusively on cell-level hypothesis testing. For figure annotations, exact adjusted p-values were displayed where possible; values below numerical display precision were reported as p < 2.2 × 10e-16. For heatmap visualization, logCPM expression values were standardized as row-wise Z-scores across patients using t(scale(t(mat))); NA values after scaling were set to 0, and Z-scores were truncated to the range of − 2 to 2 for display. Patients were ordered by pooled TNFRSF score, while genes were hierarchically clustered.

Results

Single-cell profiling and cell type identification across myeloma progression

We analyzed bone-marrow scRNA-seq (GSE271107) from 19 samples spanning healthy donors (HD, n = 5), MGUS (n = 6), SMM (n = 4), and MM (n = 4). After quality control, 143,748 cells were retained across the cohort (HD, 29,482; MGUS, 41,174; SMM, 37,080; MM, 36,012), with per-sample cell yields provided in Supplementary Table S1. After import into Seurat, stringent QC excluded cells with < 200 or > 6,000 detected genes or > 15% mitochondrial RNA (Fig. 1A–B). Normalization and dimensionality reduction yielded clear separation of major immune and stromal populations. UMAPs stratified by sample showed minimal batch effects, supporting integration across conditions (Fig. 1C). Canonical markers annotated plasma cells, T cells, monocytes, and other lineages; condition-wise UMAPs showed compositional shifts, notably progressive plasma-cell expansion in SMM/MM (Fig. 1D–G).

Stage-dependent metabolic reprogramming in plasma cells and the marrow microenvironment

Single-cell AUCell scoring revealed stage-linked remodeling of metabolic activity across the bone-marrow ecosystem. In HD, metabolic scores were uniformly low across CD4⁺/CD8⁺ T cells, NK cells, monocytes, plasma cells, and epithelial-like cells; with progression, scores increased and clusters dispersed, most prominently in SMM/MM CD8⁺ T cells, immature myeloid cells, and plasma cells (Fig. 2A, B; Fig. S2A-B). Cell type–wise quantification identified stage-associated differences in metabolic scores; however, additional sample-level reanalysis using per-sample summaries, Kruskal-Wallis/Wilcoxon testing, Cohen’s d, median differences, log2 fold changes or median, and a mixed-effects model accounting for sample-to-sample variability showed that many of these shifts were modest and heterogeneous across cell types rather than uniformly strong (Fig. S2C). For the principal stage-comparison analyses, donor-aware reanalysis using per-sample aggregated values and mixed-effects modeling across 19 donors (HD, n = 5; MGUS, n = 6; SMM, n = 4; MM, n = 4) was performed to confirm the major trends observed in the single-cell visualizations.

(A) UMAP showing metabolic activity in the health donor (HD) with cells colored by metabolic score across the integrated cellular landscape. Pre-B cells remained largely unchanged, whereas pDCs showed only a small shift despite nominal statistical significance. These patterns suggest that metabolic remodeling extends beyond malignant plasma cells to diverse immune/stromal compartments, motivating tests of parallel TNFRSF signaling changes. Expanded condition-wise metabolic distributions across major annotated cell types, together with additional cell-type-specific TNFRSF analyses, are provided in the Fig. S2C to support this broader compartment-level interpretation.

TNFRSF genes across stages and compartments

Differential profiling of TNFRSF family genes highlighted TNFRSF17 (BCMA) and TNFRSF13B (TACI) for further study given their roles in plasma-cell biology. Across all cells, the top hits by Kruskal–Wallis rank were TNFRSF13B, TNFRSF17, and TNFRSF21 (Fig. 3A). In plasma cells, TNFRSF17 showed elevated expression in advanced disease states, including MM (Fig. 3B), consistent with its APRIL/BAFF responsiveness and therapeutic relevance, whereas TNFRSF13B was low in plasma cells despite significance at the cohort level. Direct sample-level comparisons of TNFRSF17 expression using per-sample summaries, Wilcoxon testing, Cohen’s d, median differences, and a mixed-effects model within plasma-cell compartments, together with sensitivity analyses using alternative plasma-cell definitions, did not support a significant increase between SMM and MM, indicating substantial inter-sample heterogeneity across stages (Supplementary Table 8). Given APRIL’s role as a BCMA ligand, this pattern may reflect intrinsic malignant changes and microenvironmental cues (e.g., monocyte-associated inflammation) [23, 30, 31]. Cell-type-specific TNFRSF-metabolic coupling across the marrow microenvironment

To test coupling between TNFRSF signaling and metabolism, we correlated AUCell scores with TNFRSF17 and TNFRSF13B expression within plasma cells. TNFRSF17 rose across HD to MM and correlated most strongly with metabolic activity in MM (ρ = 0.56), while TNFRSF13B correlations increased more modestly (HD ρ = 0.23 to MM ρ = 0.35; Fig. 4A–F). Extensions to other receptors showed receptor-specific patterns: TNFRSF1B correlations diminished with stage (HD ρ = 0.31 to MM ρ = 0.08), TNFRSF1A remained modest and stable (ρ ≈ 0.18–0.23), and TNFRSF14 increased (HD ρ = 0.39 to MM ρ = 0.41; Fig. S3). Correlation analysis between TNFRSF expression and Metabolic_Score showed that TNFRSF-metabolic coupling was most pronounced in the plasma-cell compartment but was not confined to it. In plasma cells, TNFRSF17 and TNFRSF13B showed robust positive correlations with metabolic activity (ρ = 0.52 and ρ = 0.40, respectively), with additional associations for TNFRSF14 (ρ = 0.45), TNFRSF1B (ρ = 0.20), and TNFRSF1A (ρ = 0.15). In CD8 + T cells (Cytotoxic/Activated), the associations were more modest but remained statistically significant for TNFRSF1A (ρ = 0.05), TNFRSF1B (ρ = 0.09), and TNFRSF14 (ρ = 0.08), whereas TNFRSF17 and TNFRSF13B were not significantly associated Metabolic_Score. In Monocytes (Non-classical/Inflammatory), the pattern was more heterogeneous: TNFRSF1A showed a weak positive correlation (ρ = 0.09), TNFRSF1B showed a weak negative correlation (ρ = −0.13), and TNFRSF14 showed no significant association (ρ = −0.02), while TNFRSF17 and TNFRSF13B again did not show significant correlation (Fig. S4A-B). Together, these findings indicate that TNFRSF-metabolic coupling is cell-type-specific in both magnitude and direction, with the strongest positive observation in the plasma-cell compartments.

TNFRSF–metabolic pathway links differ by compartment

A network integrating TNFRSF genes with metabolic programs (Fig. 5A) connected TNFRSF1A/1B, TNFRSF13B, TNFRSF14, TNFRSF17, TNFRSF25, and TNFRSF10A–D to oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS), glycolysis/gluconeogenesis, amino-acid metabolism, and mitochondrial/ribosomal modules. Pathway-level correlation analysis further supported receptor-specific coupling patterns, including association of TNFRSF14/TNFRSF17 with oxidative phosphorylation/ribosomal programs, TNFRSF10A-D with nucleotide repair and stress-related pathways, and TNFRSF13B with glycolytic/mitochondrial programs (Fig. S5A). In MMRF CoMMpass bulk RNA-seq, compartments resolved analysis separated CD138⁺ (plasma/myeloma) from CD138⁻ (microenvironment) fractions and showed TNFRSF13B/TNFRSF17 were more strongly linked to glycolytic/OXPHOS-associated metabolic genes in the CD138⁺ compartment, whereas TNFRSF1A/1B predominated in the CD138⁻ compartment (Fig. 5B). PCA likewise supported transcriptional separation between these compartments (Fig. S5B), consistent with distinct signaling-metabolic landscape across tumor and microenvironmental fractions. To further assess whether TNFRSF-associated plasma-cell states overlap with canonical inflammatory and metabolic programs, we computed a plasma-cell TNFRSF core score and correlated it with pathway scores of NF-κB, mTOR, glycolysis, OXPHOS, hypoxia. The TNFRSF core score showed positive associations with NF-κB (ρ = 0.21), mTOR (ρ = 0.30), glycolysis (ρ = 0.48), OXPHOS (ρ = 0.47), and hypoxia (ρ = 0.38), all with BH-adjusted significance. In parallel, Metabolic_Score was also positively associated with these same pathway programs, including NF-κB (ρ = 0.30), mTOR (ρ = 0.33), glycolysis (ρ = 0.62), OXPHOS (ρ = 0.64), and hypoxia (ρ = 0.52) (Fig. S5C). These analyses indicate that the TNFRSF-associated plasma-cell state overlaps with established inflammatory and metabolic pathway programs, while remaining agnostic with respect to the direct causal ordering.

TNF signaling trajectory maps monocyte-centered remodeling

CellChat modeling of TNF/TNFRSF communication across stages showed that total TNFRSF interaction strength was highest in MM and generally elevated relative to earlier stages (MM 3.1554 > MGUS 2.7094 > HD 2.3393 > SMM 2.1304; Fig. 6A). TNF-specific interaction strength likewise peaked in MM (0.3175) whereas earlier stages showed smaller and non-monotonic difference (HD 0.1694 > SMM 0.1577 > MGUS 0.1462; Fig. 6B). TNF signaling role analysis further showed a shift from the more diffuse signaling pattern observed in MGUS to more concentrated monocyte-associated signaling architecture in MM, with strengthened outgoing and incoming contributions involving non-classical/inflammatory monocytes (Fig. 6C & D). The intermediate SMM TNF signaling role profile is show in Fig. S1F, and the detailed TNF-mediated cell-cell communication circle plots of MGUS, SMM, and MM are provided in Fig. S1G-I, supporting a progressive transition toward the MM stage.

Because these CellChat models were constructed at the condition level rather than the individual-sample level, the inferred communication strengths should be interpreted as condition-level signaling structure and not as a patient-level quantities that can be directly linked to per-patient metabolic or survival measures. To provide orthogonal donor-level support for the inferred monocyte to CD8 TNF axis, we additionally examined sample-level expression of pathway components across the 19 donors. TNF expression differed across conditions in both classical/inflammatory monocytes (Kruskal-Wallis p = 3.02e-02) and non-classical/inflammatory monocytes (p = 3.78e-02), and CD8 T-cell TNFRSF1B mean expression also showed condition-wise differences (p = 3.11e-02), with a concordant borderline trend in the percentage of TNFRSF1B-positive CD8 cells (p = 5.88e-02). By contrast, TNFRSF1A and the CD8 TNF-response signature showed weaker condition-wise separation and are therefore presented as supportive contextual analyses in the Fig S1A-E. Because CellChat enumerates TNF signaling mainly through TNFRSF1A/1B, indirect routes may also enhance APRIL production and thereby augment BCMA signaling in plasma cells [23].

Integrated TNFRSF activation and metabolism are associated with survival stratification

Building on the staged amplification of monocyte-driven TNF/TNFRSF signaling (Fig. 6), we next asked whether integrated TNFRSF activation marks metabolic rewiring and carries prognostic value in patient cohorts. We derived a pooled TNFRSF score from retained TNFRSF genes with reliable mapping and available expression in the CoMMpass dataset and evaluated overall survival (OS) alone and in combination with a transcriptomic metabolic activity index (score distribution and its relationship to survival status/time are shown in Fig. S6A-B). Using quartile-based stratification survival separation was attenuated and no longer significant (log-rank p = 0.24; Fig. S7A), indicating that the prognostic effect is not strongly threshold-robust across four discrete groups.

A pooled TNFRSF score (binarized gene set, summed per patient) separated overall survival (OS) with strong group differences (log-rank p < 0.0001; Fig. 7A; Supplementary Table S8), consistent with clearer single-gene survival separation for TNFRSF1A/1B and TNFRSF17. In contrast, TNFRSF13B and TNFRSF14 did not show comparable prognostic separation in the bulk cohort (Fig. S8A-E). TNFRSF-high patients showed higher metabolic activity (Fig. 7B). Joint stratification by TNFRSF × metabolism (quadrants) produced an asymmetric risk gradient (Fig. 7C–D): Low–High (low TNFRSF, high metabolism) had the shortest median OS (~ 660 days), High–Low and Low–Low were longer (~ 1823 and 1776 days), and High–High was intermediate-poor (~ 1260 days). These data support a model in which metabolic activation associates with adverse outcomes within the pooled TNFRSF framework, but the impact of metabolism is not uniform across receptor contexts. A patient-ordered heatmap (Fig. 7E) showed coordinated upregulation of multiple TNFRSFs toward the high-score tail, with enriched events and shorter OS–indicating a polygenic adverse signal rather than a single-gene effect.

To assess whether these survival associations were independent of standard clinical covariates, we performed multivariable Cox proportional hazards analysis in the CoMMpass cohort. In the primary model adjusted for age, ISS stage, and maxline (n = 771 complete cases), neither the TNFRSF score nor the metabolic index retained independent statistical significance, whereas established clinical covariates remained significant predictors. Specifically, the TNFRSF score showed HR = 1.07 (95% CI 0.94–1.21, p = 0.288), and the metabolic index showed HR = 1.11 (95% CI 0.96–1.28, p = 0.175). Sensitivity models further adjusted for gender and ECOG performance status (n = 576 complete cases) yielded similar conclusions. These findings indicate that the TNFRSF/metabolic variables show prognostic signal in unadjusted analyses, but do not demonstrate robust independent significance after clinical adjustment in the current modeling framework. However, an alternative penalized Cox-derived TNFRSF score remained independently associated with overall survival in both the primary and sensitivity multivariable models, supporting partial robustness of the prognostic signal to score-weighting strategy. These exploratory results are presented in the Fig. S7B-D and are interpreted cautiously given the lambda.min-based model selection.

BCMA–metabolism coupling and APRIL–BCMA program activity

Focusing on the actionable node BCMA (TNFRSF17), OS stratification by BCMA × metabolism quadrants remained significant (p = 0.00017; Fig. 8A–B). Notably, the ordering of risk across BCMA x metabolism quadrants was not identical to the pooled TNFRSF x metabolism model (Fig. 7C–D), indicating that metabolic state interacts with the specific receptor context (pooled TNFRSF versus BCMA-focused) rather than acting as a universal prognostic determinant. In particular, the BCMA High-High quadrant did not exhibit the poorest outcome in this cohort, suggesting that high metabolic activity does not confer the same prognostic effect across all receptor contexts and the BCMA abundance may mark a biologically distinct state relative to the broader pooled TNFRSF model. BCMA expression correlated inversely with an APRIL–BCMA target program (ρ = −0.24, p = 1.4 × 10⁻¹¹), suggesting decoupling between receptor abundance and downstream activation (Fig. 8C). Each 1-SD increase in the APRIL–BCMA program showed a borderline higher hazard (HR ≈ 1.10, 95% CI ~ 1.00–1.20; p ≈ 0.057). Ordering patients by the pooled TNFRSF score recapitulated the gradient linking TNFRSF co-expression, metabolism, and outcomes (Fig. 8D).

Discussion

Our integrated single-cell analysis across the MGUS-SMM-MM spectrum identifies a monocyte-driven TNF-TNFRSF signaling axis in reshaping the metabolic landscape of the bone marrow microenvironment. We confirm metabolic escalation in MM plasma cells and delineate its clinical relevance by coupling receptor activity to outcome: a pooled TNFRSF score (Fig. 7A) robustly stratifies overall survival in unadjusted analyses, track with higher metabolic activity (Fig. 7B), and shows that outcomes are poorest in the Low-High quadrant (high metabolism/low TNFRSF), whereas High-High is intermediate (Fig. 7C-D), indicating that metabolic state contributes materially to adverse risk within the pooled TNFRSF framework while the direction and magnitude of this effect depends on receptor context. However, in multivariable Cox models adjusted for standard clinical covariates, the TNFRSF score and metabolic index did not retain independent statistical significance, indicating that these features are best interpreted as disease-associated biological correlates with prognostic signal in unadjusted analyses rather than as definitely independent prognostic markers.

Additional sensitivity analyses further showed that quartile-based stratification attenuated the survival signal, whereas an exploratory penalized Cox-derived TNFRSF score remained independently associated with overall survival after clinical adjustment. Together, these results indicate that the prognostic signal is sensitive to the scoring framework and should be interpreted cautiously rather than as a fixed threshold-based classifier.

Our findings are consistent with an upstream regulatory link to the APRIL-BCMA axis described by Tai et al. [23]. Specifically, BCMA (TNFRSF17) integrates with metabolic fitness to further stratify risk: quadrants separate significantly (Fig. 8A), and the BCMA-metabolism plane reproduces distinct median OS quadrants (Fig. 8B). Importantly, the quadrant associated with poorest outcome in the pooled TNFRSF x metabolism model (Fig. 7C–D) is not required to match the poorest-outcome quadrant in the BCMA x metabolism model (Fig. 8A–B), because these analyses interrogate different receptor contexts (polygenic TNFRSF activation versus a single actionable receptor). The absence of a worst-outcome pattern in the BCMA High-High group suggests that the prognostic contribution of metabolic state is not monotonic across receptor settings and that high BCMA expression may reflect a biologically distinct state rather than simply amplifying metabolic risk. This indicates that metabolic risk is receptor-context dependent and may be shaped by additional TNFRSF nodes (e.g., TNFRSF1A/1B) and upstream inflammatory inputs. Consistent with pathway wiring, BCMA expression shows an inverse association with an APRIL-BCMA target program (Fig. 8C), while a 1-SD increase in APRIL-BCMA program activity trends toward elevated hazard (HR ≈ 1.10, 95% CI 1.00–1.20; p ≈ 0.057; Fig. 8C). Together with our pooled TNFRSF classifier, these data indicate that inflammatory receptor signaling and metabolic state mark biologically and clinically relevant disease states, although their survival associations were not independently retained after multivariable clinical adjustment. Treatment-regiment-specific analyses were not performed here and will be important for future work, particularly in the context of BCMA-directed therapies.

At the systems level, TNFRSF nodes interlock with OXPHOS, glycolysis, and ribosomal programs (Fig. 5A-B), and compartment-resolved analyses in CoMMpass suggest that TNFRSF17/TNFRSF13B couple most strongly to bioenergetic modules in CD138 + tumor cells, whereas TNFRSF1A/1B associations are enriched in the CD138- microenvironment (Fig. 5C-D). In plasma cells, additional pathway-overlap analyses further showed that TNFRSF-associated states were positively aligned with NF-κB, mTOR, glycolysis, OXPHOS, and hypoxia-related programs.

At the single-cell level, this relationship was strongest in plasma cells but remained detectable in selected non-plasma compartments, with modest positive associations in CD8 + T cells and more heterogeneous patterns in non-classical/inflammatory monocytes, indicating that TNFRSF-metabolic coupling is compartment-dependent rather than plasma-cell-exclusive. Together, these findings are consistent with overlap between TNFRSF-associated states, and canonical inflammatory/metabolic circuitry, including mTOR/NF-κB/HIF1α-linked programs [32–35], but do not by themselves establish direct causal regulation, providing a rationale for co-targeting inflammatory signaling and metabolism.

These findings have direct implications for BCMA-targeted immunotherapies currently in clinical use. Because risk across the BCMA x metabolism plane is non-monotonic in this cohort (Fig. 8A-B), these findings should be interpreted cautiously, particularly because treatment-regiment-specific analyses were not performed in the present study. In this setting, the Low-receptor/High-metabolism phenotype may represent a biologic state in which reduced target abundance coexists with a metabolically adverse microenvironment. This interpretation is consistent with prior work showing that APRIL-BCMA signaling promotes myeloma growth and downstream AKT/MAPK/NF-κB signaling, and with studies showing that BCMA shedding lower surface antigen density and increases soluble BCMA, both of which may limit BCMA-directed immunotherapy efficacy [23, 36, 37]. In that context, antigen-density-restoring approaches are particularly relevant: γ-secretase inhibition has been shown preclinically to increase surface BCMA and improve BCMA CAR-T recognition, and a phase 1 study further showed that combining a γ-secretase inhibitor with BCMA CAR-T therapy was feasible and increased target antigen density in patients [36, 38]. Our data also raise the possibility that metabolism-direction combinations could be explored for the Low-High subgroup, but this should presently be regarded as exploratory rather than established. Emerging studies indicates that CAR-T efficacy is influenced by cellular metabolic fitness and that metabolic rewiring may improve persistence and function; in MM, resistance to T-cell-redirecting therapies has likewise been linked to impaired T-cell fitness, Treg-associated suppression and exhaustion-related phenotypes [4, 39, 40]. Accordingly, we now view metabolic co-targeting as a rationale for future BCMA CAR-T or bispecific-antibody combination studies, rather than as a clinically established recommendation.

A central novel insight of our study is the stage-specific remodeling of TNF-mediated intercellular communication and its downstream effects on the microenvironment. CellChat analysis charts a transition from diffuse TNF priming in MGUS to a focused, high-amplitude, monocyte-centered hub that peaks in MM (Fig. 6A-H), consistent with reported enrichment of inflammatory monocytes in precursor disease and evolving antigen-presentation defects that reshape local immunity. Functionally, TNF traffic shifts toward and is maximal for CD8 + cytotoxic/activated T cells in MM (Fig. 6E-H) potentially contributing to chronic activation and checkpoint upregulation that culminate in exhaustion [41–43]. Because these communication models were inferred from condition-level pooled data, they should be interpreted as stage-associated signaling architectures rather than patient-level predictors of metabolic phenotype or outcome.

Integrating our outcome-based markers, we propose a temporal model wherein monocyte TNF initiates broad priming (MGUS), narrows onto effector circuits (SMM), consolidates a TNF-TNFRSF/BCMA-metabolism circuit that aligns with metabolically adverse strata (MM; Figs. 7 and 8). In the pooled TNFRDF TNFRSF model, the Low-High quadrant showed the poorest outcome, whereas in the BCMA-focused model the prognostic ordering differed and the High-High group was not the worst-outcome stratum in this cohort. These patterns reinforce that the prognostic contribution of metabolic state depends on the receptor context being interrogated (pooled TNFRSF versus BCMA-specific) rather than following a single universal risk gradient. This framework reconciles inflammatory amplification with immune dysfunction in MM and suggest rational partners of BCMA-derived therapies, including agents that attenuate monocyte TNF, normalize metabolic flux, or relieve checkpoint-mediated exhaustion [44, 45]. Together with prior descriptions of an inflammatory stromal landscape, our data support monocyte as an upstream broadcast hub linking cytokine tone to receptor activation and metabolic remodeling at the condition level [46].

Clinically, the TNFRSF-metabolism classifier (Fig. 7) and the BCMA-metabolism/APRIL-BCMA program readouts (Fig. 8) support exploratory stratification concepts rather than immediate treatment-selection tools. In particular, the APRIL-BCMA program score may be useful because it captures pathway activity downstream of receptor abundance and may therefore complement BCMA expression alone. At present, however, clinically explored BCMA-response biomarkers are centered mainly on soluble BCMA, tumor burden, and BCMA antigen density [37, 47]. We therefore interpret the APRIL-BCMA program score as a candidate companion biomarker that should be tested prospectively in BCMA CAR-T and bispecific-antibody cohorts to determine whether it adds predictive value beyond soluble BCMA, surface BCMA density and standard clinical variables. Conceptually, the data map a transition from immune equilibrium to escape with TNF-centered-metabolic circuit anchoring the MM niche. Limitations include the correlative nature of several key associations, including the TNFRSF-metabolism axis and APRIL-BCMA-related analyses, as well as under-representation of specific immune subsets; functional perturbation studies and metabolomic validation will be essential to resolve causal ordering and biological mechanism. An important limitation of the present study is that the single-cell component is based on one public discovery cohort; therefore, validation in an independent, stage-comparable myeloma scRNA-seq dataset will be an important next step.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our study provides a comprehensive single-cell dissection of how an inflammatory monocyte-TNF axis interfaces with TNFRSF signaling and is associated with metabolic reprogramming across MM development. By linking receptor activity, metabolic state, intercellular communication, and survival (Figs. 5, 6, 7 and 8), we define a TNFRSF-metabolism axis and a BCMA-related signaling framework whose clinical relevance depends on metabolic and receptor context. These features are biologically coherent and potentially translationally informative, but they require further prospective validation before use as independent prognostic or treatment-stratification tools.
