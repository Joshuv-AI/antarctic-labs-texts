Deconstructing Subset Construction
John W. Nicol, Markus Frohme

Abstract
We present a novel perspective on the NFA canonization problem, which introduces intermediate minimization steps to reduce the exploration space on-the-fly. Essential to our approach are so-called equivalence registries which manage information about equivalent states and allow for incorporating further optimization techniques such as convexity closures or simulation to boost performance. Due to the generality of our approach, these concepts can be embedded in classic subset construction or [ 7 ] ’s approach. We evaluate our approach on a set of real-world examples from automatic sequences and observe that we are able to improve especially worst-case scenarios. We implement our approach in an open-source library for users to experiment with.

Reducing While Determinizing

Introduction

Finite state machines are foundational models of computation in computer science, where their simplicity and expressive power make them of both theoretical and practical interest.
Among the most prominent types are deterministic finite automata (DFAs) and non-deterministic finite automata (NFAs), which are extensively studied for their applications in various domains.
DFAs are often preferred over NFAs due to their efficiency in operations such as language equivalence and inclusion, universality, complementation, and other closure properties
[
24
,
22
]
,
trace inclusion
[
19
]
, and string matching
[
2
,
19
]
.

\Citeauthor

RabinS59
[
33
]
have shown that DFAs and NFAs are equally expressive by transforming NFAs into language-equivalent DFAs via
subset
(or
powerset
)
construction
.
The resulting DFAs may be of exponential size compared to the original NFAs and may not be minimal.
The latter can be easily addressed via an additional minimization step
[
23
]
which is often required if the resulting DFAs need to be processed further.
A minimal DFA is called
canonical
since a minimal DFA is unique up to isomorphism
[
24
]
.
Constructing the canonical DFA for a given NFA is called the
canonization problem

[
44
,
19
]
.

The exponential blowup of canonization can be a challenge in practice.
However, many NFAs exhibit a notable
compression ratio
, i.e., the ratio between the size of the determinized (via subset construction) DFA and its minimized version.
Real-world cases report compression ratios ranging from
30
(
[
14
]
), to
2,500
(
[
43
]
), to
5
million (
[
36
,
38
]
, and addenda for
[
32
]
).
In such situations, materializing the full DFA is often the bottleneck of the canonization process.

For certain (NFA-related) problems there exists extensive research on limiting the impact of state blowup, e.g., by using
antichains
for testing universality and language inclusion
[
47
,
13
]
or
bisimulation up to congruence
for testing language equivalence
[
5
]
.
See
[
10
,
17
]
for surveys on the current state of this work.
For the canonization problem, however, the construction of the full DFA is necessary.

In this paper, we present OTF – an
on-the-fly
approach for canonizing NFAs.
Instead of treating determinization and minimization as distinct phases of the canonization process, OTF interleaves these concepts by repeatedly minimizing the (partially) constructed DFA of subset construction.
Insights about equivalent states of the tentative DFA are then fed back to subset construction in order to reduce the state space requiring exploration.
This form of communication is formalized via
equivalence registries
with which the process can be parameterized, giving OTF a framework character.
We present two exemplary implementations of equivalence registries.
In our evaluation, we show that OTF is able to improve the performance of canonization for complex real-world NFAs, while remaining competitive for simple systems.
We implement these concepts in a public tool

The idea of exploiting equivalencies to reduce state space appears in various automaton-related fields of research such as

[

and WS1S formulae
[
15

However, these approaches typically exploit some domain-specific knowledge of their use-cases.
In contrast, OTF processes generic NFAs, allowing for a flexible application.
Additionally, by processing NFAs directly, OTF is compatible with other directions of NFA minimization including (bi-) simulation reduction, transition pruning, and transition saturation

A noteworthy alternative to classical NFA canonization is

[

which first determinizes (e.g., via subset construction) the reversed NFA, then reverses the constructed DFA, and finally determinizes it again.
This method canonizes the original NFA without requiring an additional minimization step and can outperform classic subset construction in some cases.
Since it relies only on determinization, it is compatible with OTF and is included in our evaluation.
Finally,

considers the potential of compression ratio by generalizing subset construction although its performance is not compelling.

2.2
Simulation and Bisimulation

For reasoning about the behavioral relationships between states of an FSA, there exist various notions of equivalence.
A popular one is (bi-) simulation
[
30
]
.

Definition 2
(Simulation)

Let
A
be an FSA.
A binary relation
R\subseteq S\times S
is called a
simulation relation
iff
{(x,y)\in R}
implies

•

x\in F\Leftrightarrow y\in F
and

•

\forall i\in\Sigma\colon(x,i,x^{\prime})\in\delta\Rightarrow(\exists y^{\prime}\in S\colon(y,i,y^{\prime})\in\delta\land(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\in R)
.

Definition 3
(Bisimulation)

Let
A
be an FSA.
A binary relation
R\subseteq S\times S
is called a
bisimulation relation
iff
R
and
R^{-1}
are simulation relations for
A
.

Note that bisimulation is stricter than simulation equivalence, as it requires the inverse of the
same
relation to also be a simulation.

The coarsest simulation and bisimulation relations, known as
similarity
and
bisimilarity
, can be computed in
\mathcal{O}(|\delta|\cdot|S|)
time
[
34
]
and
\mathcal{O}(|\delta|\cdot\log|S|)
time
[
46
]
, respectively.
It is well known that simulation implies finite trace (i.e., language) inclusion
[
11
,
19
,
18
]
and bisimulation implies finite trace (i.e., language) equivalence
[
18
]
.
Thus, bisimulation or simulation equivalences can be used to quotient NFAs, which is often done to reduce the size of NFAs before determinizing.
Note that this step is only relevant for non-deterministic systems since for DFAs, minimization and bisimulation quotienting coincide
[
5
]
.

2.3
Semi-lattice, Convexity, Antichain

Let
X
denote a finite set.
A
semi-lattice

{(X,\circ)}
is an algebraic structure that consists of
X
and a binary operation
{\circ:X\times X\rightarrow X}
which is associative, commutative, and idempotent.
Let
(X,\circ)
be a semi-lattice and
{M\subseteq X}
.
We call
(M,\circ)
a (semi-)
sub-lattice
of
(X,\circ)
iff
(M,\circ)
is a well-defined semi-lattice itself.
We call a semi-lattice
(M,\circ)

convex
iff
{\{y\in X\mid x,z\in M,x\preceq y\preceq z\}\subseteq M}
.
Here,
\prec
denotes the preorder related to the respective (join/meet) operator
\circ
.
Let
{L=(M,\circ)}
be a semi-lattice.
The
convexity closure
of
L
is obtained by adding all missing elements to
L
in order to make it convex.

In this paper, we mainly consider variations of the well-known powerset lattice
{(2^{X},\cup,\cap)}
.
In particular,
{(2^{X},\cup)}
is a join-semi-lattice.
Furthermore, let
X=\{1,\ldots,5\},A=\{1,2\},B=\{3,4\}
.
Then
L_{1}={(\{A,B,A\cup B\},\cup)}
is a
(semi-)
sub-lattice of
(2^{X},\cup)
and the convexity closure of
L_{1}
is given by

(\{1,2\},\{3,4\},\{1,2,3\},\{1,2,4\},\{2,3,4\},\{1,2,3,4\}\},\cup).

Testing whether an element is contained in a convex (join-) semi-lattice
L
can be efficiently implemented by testing whether it is covered from above by
L
’s greatest element and covered from below by any of
L
’s minimal elements.
Let
L=(X,\cup)
denote a (join-) semi-lattice.
The
greatest element
of
L
is given by
{\bigcup L=\bigcup_{x\in X}x}
.
An element
{x\in X}
is called
minimal
iff
{\nexists y\in X\colon y\subset x}
.
Here,
\subset
is the related preorder of the join operator (
\cup
) without equality.
For
L_{1}
, the greatest element is
\{1,2,3,4\}
and the minimal elements are
\{1,2\}
and
\{3,4\}
.
The element
\{1,2,3\}
is contained in the convexity closure of
L_{1}
because
{\{1,2\}\subseteq\{1,2,3\}}
and
{\{1,2,3\}\subseteq\{1,2,3,4\}}
.
It is worth noting that the minimal elements of
L
form an antichain, i.e., a set of pairwise incomparable (in the sense of
\prec

3
The OTF Framework

This section presents how we integrate the OTF concept into the determinization process.
Intuitively, our approach is similar to classic subset construction with two major modifications.

First, the NFA state-space exploration may be repeatedly interrupted by a
threshold
predicate.
During this interrupt, the partially constructed DFA is minimized by an efficient algorithm such as
[
23
]
.
On one hand, this reduces the size of the intermediate DFA model.
On the other, the information about equivalent DFA states may be used to optimize the remaining exploration of the NFA state-space (see below).

Second, the mapping between NFA metastates and DFA states is handled by an
equivalence registry
supporting the following operations:

•

GET
(Q)
returns a metastate identifier with the same language as metastate
Q
, or undefined if this behavior has not been previously observed.

•

PUT
(Q,x)
associates the behavior of metastate
Q
with identifier
x
.

•

UNIFY
(x_{1},x_{2})
marks metastates
x_{1}
and
x_{2}
as (language) equivalent.

Looking up identifiers
modulo language-equivalence
has a huge potential to reduce the metastate space that needs to be explored, thus improving determinization performance.

We design OTF as a
framework
by allowing the threshold and equivalence registry to be parameters to this process.
In practice, this allows users to construct highly customizable canonization processes specifically tailored to their use-cases.

3.2
Termination and Correctness

With a constant
false
predicate and an identity-based registry that only retrieves (GET) or stores (PUT) identifiers,
Algorithm

1
coincides with classic subset construction.
Thus, we omit general discussions about termination and correctness, as they follow directly from the original algorithm.
Instead, we focus on how our modifications preserve these properties.

The first modification relaxes GET to return representatives of language-equivalent metastates instead of previously associated ones.
Once classic subset construction terminates, these metastates correspond to equivalent DFA states, which can be permuted or merged without affecting the language of the DFA.
Allowing these transformations earlier (during lookup) only shortcuts these steps while preserving language equivalence.

The second modification introduces intermediate minimizations, where unexplored states are assigned unique blocks in the initial partition, ensuring they remain distinct until their behavior is fully determined.
Consequently, minimization never merges states with incomplete information.
Only explored states with identical acceptance and identical successors are merged, meaning the final DFA remains equivalent to its non-minimized counterpart.
Thus, intermediate minimizations do not alter the DFA’s language, maintaining parity with classic subset construction.

4
Equivalence Registries

The performance of OTF largely depends on the chosen equivalence registry.
Intelligently looking up and effectively unifying language-equivalent metastates can drastically mitigate the blow-up of the exploration space, a key limitation of classic subset construction.
We continue with presenting two implementations that performed well in our evaluation.

4.1
CCL – Convexity Closure Lattice

The CCL registry uses the concept of convexity closure of lattices.
It is based on an observation of
[
5
]

[
5
]
that for metastates
A,B
of an NFA:

L(A\cup B)=L(A)\cup L(B).

Exploiting this property further, we can conclude for
{L(A)=L(B)}
that

L(A\cup B)=L(A)\cup L(B)=L(A)=L(B).

This relationship is not limited to disjoint metastates.
Consider the situation where
{L(A)=L(A\cup B)}
and
{A\subseteq A\cup B}
.
This means that no state in
{B\setminus A}
impacts the language of either
L(A)
or
L(A\cup B)
.
In particular, for every
X
such that
{A\subseteq X\subseteq A\cup B}
, we have

L(A)=L(X)=L(A\cup B).

The same reasoning applies to
{L(B)=L(A\cup B)}
, placing us in a scenario similar to
Section

2.3
: detecting equivalence between two metastates allows one to construct a (join-semi-) lattice
L
with minimal elements
A,B
and greatest element
{A\cup B}
.

L
itself forms a sub-lattice for the powerset lattice of the global metastate space.
Due to these properties, language equivalence holds for all elements in the convexity closure of
L
.
Thus, recognizing equivalence between two metastates
A
,
B
provides insight into many (potentially unobserved) metastates.

The registry manages a distinct lattice for each DFA state, covering its language-equivalent metastates.
Initially, when a new metastate is registered (PUT), a singleton lattice is stored.
When unifying two states (UNIFY), their associated lattices
L_{1},L_{2}
must be merged.
If both are singletons, the process is straightforward.
However, if either has undergone previous merges, we recursively apply convexity closure: since all states covered by
L_{1},L_{2}
are language-equivalent, we select their greatest elements as representatives and apply the same steps as before.
The greatest element of the new lattice
L^{\prime}
is the union of the greatest elements of
L_{1},L_{2}
.
Since union is the join operator,
L^{\prime}
covers all elements covered by
L_{1},L_{2}
from above.
The minimal elements of
L^{\prime}
are constructed from the union of minimal elements
M_{1},M_{2}
of
L_{1},L_{2}
, respectively.
Here, elements in
M_{1},M_{2}
may be in a subset relation which would violate minimality in
L^{\prime}
.
To tackle this issue, we simply filter out these elements which requires at most
\mathcal{O}(|M_{1}||M_{2}|)
subset comparisons.

For GET operations, recall from
Section

2.3
that the convexity closure of a lattice can be efficiently stored using its antichain of minimal elements and greatest element.
Thus, the registry first scans for eligible lattices whose greatest element is a superset of
S
.
Then, for each candidate, the respective antichain is searched for elements that are a subset of
S
.
If such a candidate is found, the associated representative is returned.

This description focuses on the functional view of the registry, though many implementation optimizations exist.
For example, singleton lattices can be retrieved via a faster hash-based data structure rather than the general lookup logic.
Also, subset and superset checks can be accelerated using inverse indices and bit-vector operations: see
[
27
]
for an involved discussion of options.
We omit specifics for clarity, instead referring to our publicly available implementation.

4.2
CCLS – CCL with Similarity

The core mechanism of the CCL registry is the intermediate minimization that establishes equivalencies between NFA metastates via UNIFY calls.
Thus, the registry can typically reduce the search space only after minimization.
Using a similar idea as
[
1
,
11
,
19
]
, the CCLS registry refines the CCL approach by introducing preprocessing to compute the similarity relation on the original NFA, then exploiting state-level relations to accelerate metastate equivalence detection, potentially even before UNIFY is invoked.

Let
a,b
be NFA states.
If
a

simulates

b
, then
{L(b)\subseteq L(a)}
which implies
{L(\{a,b\})=L(\{a\})}
.
In metastate terms, we can replace
\{a,b\}
with
\{a\}
(pruning) and
\{a\}
with
\{a,b\}
(saturating).
This principle extends to supersets as well, e.g.,
\{a,b,c\}
can be pruned to
\{a,c\}
and
\{a,c\}
can be saturated to
\{a,b,c\}
.

The CCLS registry leverages this in two ways.
GET
(S)
first prunes
S
into
S_{p}
using similarity information and then finds a lattice covering
S_{p}
, as in the CCL registry.
PUT
(S,x)
first prunes and saturates
S
into
S_{p}
and
S_{s}
: if
{S_{p}\neq S_{s}}
, instead of storing a singleton lattice like CCL, it creates a
“complex”
lattice with
S_{p}
as a single minimal element and
S_{s}
as the greatest element.
Thus, each lattice may cover a larger metastate space
“for free”
, further reducing the search space.

The effectiveness of the CCLS registry depends on the use case.
Computing the simulation preorder can be prohibitively expensive for large or dense NFAs, so the benefit of precomputing simulation relations must outweigh the cost.
On an implementation level, we preprocess the NFA’s initial and final states following techniques from
[
11
]
.
Additionally, since the CCLS registry requires similarity information at construction time, we compute it before running OTF.
As a result, we can further quotient the system via simulation equivalence.
This also allows us to avoid challenges such as cyclic subset inclusions and tie-breaking during metastate selection
[
19
]
.

Evaluation

This section compares the performance of OTF with classic NFA canonization algorithms.
For the evaluation, we focus on practical examples from the field of automatic sequences.
In particular, we analyze various case-studies of the tool Walnut
[

We evaluated the following algorithms and OTF configurations:

OTF

describes the OTF-based canonization process using the CCL equivalence registry presented in

describes the canonization process via classic subset construction.

describes a variation of SC with pruning and saturation.

describes a variation of BRZ which uses SC-S for the first determinization phase.

As a result, we use plain SC for the second phase in this configuration.

BRZ-OTF-S

All configurations use Hopcroft’s algorithm

5.3
Evaluation

The benchmarks were executed on an AMD EPYC 7763 CPU (2.45-3.5GHz) running a JDK 17.0.14 with 1.8TB assigned memory.
Note that the memory was mainly needed to run multiple benchmarks in parallel and does not indicate the actual memory requirements of our approach.
In fact, we have often seen a much lower memory consumption compared to the alternatives.
For each configuration, we consider the total runtime of the canonization process which includes the time for pre-processing steps such as computing (bi-) similarity.
The full data is available in the benchmark repository.

Figure

1
presents a survival plot of the results.
The x-axis shows the time required for canonization in base 10 where we aborted a process after a timeout of one hour.
The y-axis shows the number of successfully canonized systems out of the 42 systems in total.
All
-S
-based configurations are indicated by dashed lines (sim = true).

Figure 1:
Survival plot of the Walnut examples.

The results allow for analyses in different directions.
When comparing BRZ-based configurations with non-BRZ-based ones, we see that especially for smaller / faster systems, BRZ-based configurations outperform their competitors as more systems are canonized.
However, it seems that these approaches hit a
“wall”
after which only very little progress can be observed (see after
10
and
100
seconds).
Here, only the OTF-based variation of BRZ is able to break this pattern and eventually catch up with the total number of canonized systems.
In contrast, the non-BRZ-based configurations show a more continuous progression.

Regarding the impact of simulation, we see that the BRZ-based configurations are largely unaffected as their performance remains relatively similar.
Only for systems near the timeout threshold, we can see that omitting simulation information may actually improve performance.
The full data shows that the computation of the simulation relation only requires a few seconds so the time is lost in the CCLS implementation.
It seems that the overhead of pruning and saturating the queried metastates is greater than the saved exploration time of the reversed automata.
In contrast, the non-BRZ-based approaches seem to benefit from including the simulation information.
The plain subset construction experiences a small performance boost for fast(er) systems but eventually meets up with its non-simulated version so that no continuing trend is observable.
Only OTF is able to significantly reduce canonization time and maintain its advantage over the other configurations.
In particular, extrapolating the results for large systems from
[
41
]
, OTF-variant algorithms performed over
100
times better.

Regarding systems that timed out, they seem to have a comparatively low compression ratio which results in high DFA state counts.
Initially, we ran the benchmarks without any timeout and noticed that the process would run out of memory, affecting further data collection.
In particular, the benchmark includes systems such as
tribpseudo3
(
“Open Problem 30”
in
[
28
]
) which has not been successfully canonized yet.

In summary, we can see that OTF does introduce overhead which is especially notable for systems whose canonization process is fast to begin with.
However, the longer this process needs (
>100
seconds) the more promising the results of OTF become.
Framing these observations in terms of absolute time consumption, we argue that OTF helps especially when dealing with complex systems.
In contrast, the overhead for smaller / faster systems (
<10
seconds) is often negligible in practice.

6
Conclusion and Future Work

In this paper, we present OTF – a framework for NFA canonization.
OTF combines on-the-fly minimization with dynamic equivalence registries for which we present two possible implementations.
Our performance evaluation shows that for NFAs with
“structure”
, the discussed OTF configurations outperform classic subset construction and
[
7
]
’s algorithm for complex systems while being competitive for the remaining ones.
Looking forward, there exist several directions for extension.

One obvious direction deals with exploring further implementations of equivalence registries.
Full set-rewriting as with HKC
[
5
]
can capture additional relations between equivalence classes, thus reducing exploration space even more.
However, this was slower in our testing (see
[
16
]
for more information).
This appeared to be due to high-cardinality equivalences, which are both slow and unlikely to match a given metastate.
There is the possibility of a hybridized HKC approach that is limited to smaller cardinalities, or alternatively, a hierarchical approach.
These ideas touch on the vast Formal Concept Analysis literature
[
4
]
.

Furthermore, states and transitions can be represented as a binary decision diagram
[
10
,
45
]
, potentially combining with a semi-symbolic approach
[
47
]
.
This may be helpful in cases where systems have large alphabets
[
16
]
.

Finally, we note an equivalence between the steps of subset construction and a sequence of mutated NFAs, either built directly from the original NFA states, or from a more efficient
cover
including new DFA metastates.
The minimization steps of OTF could be applied to a mutated NFA, potentially finding more equivalences, but requiring a more involved minimization algorithm
[
46
]
.
A mutated NFA also allows for dynamically switching determinization strategies, which can break out of local minima, i.e., particularly difficult determinization sections.

0.A.1
Iterated Language Projection of Random Systems

Language projection
is a fundamental operation in formal language theory.
Given a language
{L\subseteq\Sigma^{*}}
over an alphabet
\Sigma
and an onto function
{\pi:\Sigma\to\Gamma\cup\{\tau\}}
where
\tau
denotes a special symbol for
“quiescence”
, the projected language
{\pi(L)}
is obtained by applying
\pi
to each symbol of every word in
L
.
In particular, symbols mapped to
\tau
are effectively
“hidden”
or
“deleted”
which can cause transitions in the corresponding automaton to merge.
When applied to a DFA, this operation usually introduces non-determinism and transforms the given DFA into an NFA.

A key application of this concept arises in
Presburger quantifier elimination

[
8
,
20
]
.
While language projection imposes some structure already, real-world scenarios typically involve multiple or
iterated
projection steps in which several intermediate automata are combined via production constructions.
To analyze the performance of OTF in these situations, this benchmark looks at the canonization of repeatedly language projected products of random DFAs.

Systems

Each benchmark run is parameterized by a random seed
{r\in\{0,\ldots,99\}}
, an alphabet size
{s\in\{8,16,32\}}
, and a compression factor
{\mathit{cf}\in\{1.3,1.7\}}
.
First, for a given seed, two random DFAs with four states and
s
input symbols are generated.
These DFAs are then projected onto an NFA with
\frac{s}{cf}
input symbols such that the preimage partition classes of the projection are equally sized.
Then the symmetric difference of the two NFA language is constructed via an intermediate determinization step and a product automaton construction using the logical exclusive-or as state acceptance.
Finally, the resulting DFA is projected again using the similar projection steps.

While four states for the initial NFAs seem relatively low, even small changes to this number result in either trivial or practically infeasible systems.
In contrast, controlling the final sizes via the number of input symbols as well as the compression factor allows for a much more fine-grained control over the resulting systems.

Configurations

We use the same configurations as described in
Section

5.2
.

Evaluation

Figure

2
shows two survival plots of the benchmark results, comparing the elapsed runtime against the number of successfully canonized systems.
The plots are split according to the compression factor but otherwise aggregate all data over the random seeds and alphabet sizes.
Each benchmark was configured to timeout after ten minutes.

Figure 2:
Survival plot of the iterated language projection examples.

Beginning with the results for a compression factor of
1.3
(upper half), the plot only contains data for OTF and subset construction.
All BRZ-based configurations ran into memory problems which prevented a reliable extraction of timing results.
We observed that the issues often occurred during construction of the reversed automata when determining successor states.
This suggests to us that the constructed automata are very
“join-heavy”
, i.e., reversing the adjacency structure results in big sets of successor states, which puts BRZ at a disadvantage by design.

For the available data, two interesting properties can be observed.
The configurations involving simulation outperform the ones that do not and subset construction outperforms OTF.
Analyzing these effects simultaneously highlights a central property: OTF mainly benefits from equivalences detected during minimization.
The fact that subset construction (which does not perform any minimizations) outperforms OTF indicates that the intermediate minimizations are not able to significantly reduce the search space but instead, the management of equivalence registries introduces additional overhead.
At the same time, the systems do compress as indicated by the speedup obtained when including simulation information.
Still, the data shows that OTF (through the CCLS registry) is able to leverage additional knowledge such as similarity and remains relatively competitive with subset construction, even in this disadvantageous use-case.

For systems with a more aggressive compression factor of
1.7
(lower half), we can see that the BRZ-based configurations are able to terminate again.
In fact, they even outperform the non-BRZ-based configurations by a small margin up until the very last few systems, which aligns with the trends observed in
Figure

1
.
Another interesting point is that the impact of the simulation information seems to have an inverted effect on the BRZ-based configurations as the
“non-sim”
configurations outperform the
“sim”
-based ones.
The performance of BRZ and BRZ-OTF is nearly identical because due to the structure (size) of the systems, only very few (often zero) intermediate minimizations are performed.

In summary, this benchmark gives an indication of how different system structures can positively or negatively impact canonization performance, which is another interesting direction for future research.
We see OTF as an enabler for incorporating several useful heuristics through its equivalence registries which allow one to adapt to various environments and external factors.
