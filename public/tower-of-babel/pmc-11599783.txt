Lack of WDFY4 leads to impaired immune response and poor cancer prognosis
Authors: Hanxuan Bao, Chen Zhang, Xue Peng, Jingnan Jia, Yi Yang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Abstract WDFY4 plays an essential role in the immune system by regulating B‐cell growth and development and participating in antigen processing during cross‐presentation. WDFY4 is closely related to asthma and systemic lupus erythematosus; however, its role in cancer remains unclear. The purpose of this study is to use bioinformatics to determine whether abnormal expression of WDFY4 is a risk factor for cancer and to preliminarily analyze the ways in which WDFY4 affects cancer through experiments. R language packages and bioinformatic database were used to mine the potential carcinogenic effect of WDFY4 and analyze the differential WDFY4 expression in cancer, gene mutations, different tumor prognoses, immune cell infiltration, tumor microenvironment, and DNA methylation correlation. H1975 and A549 cell lines were infected with lentiviruses to overexpress WDFY4, and the effect of WDFY4 on the activity, proliferation, apoptosis, and cell cycle of lung cancer cells was analyzed. WDFY4 was differentially expressed in human tumors in unpaired and paired samples. The differential expression of WDFY4 in unpaired and paired or protein samples from the Clinical Proteome Tumor Analysis Consortium of eight cancers was consistent. WDFY4 methylation was downregulated in 17 cancer types and caused prognostic differences in different directions in some cancers. WDFY4 overexpression significantly inhibited the activity and proliferation of lung cancer cell lines, promoted apoptosis, and caused cell cycle arrest. Differential WDFY4 expression in cancers leads to differences in the prognosis of various cancers. WDFY4 can be an independent prognostic factor for glioma, KIRC, and LUSC.

INTRODUCTION

Cancer is the leading cause of death worldwide and is a significant barrier to increasing life expectancy. 1 The burden of cancer morbidity and mortality is rapidly increasing globally, bringing physical, mental, and other suffering to people. Currently, cancer treatment strategies are constantly being improved, and various treatment methods such as surgery, chemotherapy, radiotherapy, targeted therapy, and immunotherapy have achieved positive results. However, owing to drug resistance and side effects, the prognosis and survival rate of patients with cancer require further improvement. 2 Therefore, identifying effective therapeutic targets and novel sensitive tumor biomarkers is essential for improving the efficiency of cancer diagnosis and clinical efficacy. 3

WD repeat and FYVE domain‐containing 4 (WDFY4) is a protein‐coding gene and one of the nine mammalian BEACH domain‐containing proteins (BDCPs) that often contain Pleckstrin homology‐like domains and WD repeats. 4 The RNA of WDFY4 is primarily expressed in the blood and immune organs, and its protein exists in the major organs, including the lungs, kidneys, and brain (Figure S1A). WDFY4 protein is primarily localized in the early endosome and endoplasmic reticulum (Figure S1B). BDCP functions as a protein scaffold that regulates vesicle fission and fusion events in cells and is closely related to the immune system, with some of its functions have been implicated in human autoimmune diseases 4 , 5 , 6 (Figure S1C). WDFY4 is involved in the progression of various diseases through the immune system, including, but not limited to, participation in the cross‐presentation of cell‐associated antigens, 7 B‐cell maintenance 8 and promoting type II helper T (Th2) cell differentiation and Th2 cytokine production. 9

However, the role of WDFY4 in cancer development remains unclear. Therefore, this study aimed to elucidate the role of WDFY4 in cancer from a bioinformatics and experimental perspective.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Data preprocessing and differential expression analysis

RNA sequencing data of patients with cancer were downloaded from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) database (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov) as transcripts per million (TPM) and log2(TPM + 1) for gene‐level results. Owing to the lack of sufficient normal samples as controls in some cancer types, we supplemented 7568 normal samples from the XENA database (https://xenabrowser.net/) to analyze the differential expression of WDFY4 mRNA in cancerous and normal tissues. The protein expression data of 10 cancers were obtained from the Clinical Proteome Tumor Analysis Consortium (CPTAC) and International Cancer Proteome Consortium datasets to analyze the protein differences between cancer tissues and normal samples. The WDFY4 alteration type and frequency were evaluated using the TCGA pan‐cancer dataset collected by cBioPortal 10 , 11 and various cancer cell lines from the Cancer Cell Line Encyclopedia (CCLE).

Diagnostic and prognostic analyses

The pathological stage of the tumor was selected from the clinical information of cancer samples provided by the TCGA database, and its association with WDFY4 expression was analyzed. The potential value of WDFY4 in cancer diagnosis was detected using a receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis using data from the TCGA database. The area under the curve (AUC) is accurate at 0.7–0.9, and AUC > 0.9 has a high diagnostic value. Proportional hazards hypothesis test and fitted survival regression were performed using the survival package (version 3.2–10) in R, and the results were visualized using the Survminer (version 0.4.9) and ggplot2 packages (version 3.3.6) for overall survival (OS), disease‐free survival, and progression‐free interval (PFI). Pathological stage, age, gender, race, and other crucial clinical factors were incorporated into the univariate and multivariate regression models to analyze the potential value of WDFY4 in cancer prognosis.

Immune infiltration analysis

Using the RGSVA software package (version 1.34.0), the ssGSEA algorithm was used to measure the correlation between WDFY4 expression and 24 different immune cell markers. The results are shown as lollipop plots. STROMALScore, ImmunoScore, and ESTIMATEScore algorithms were used to quantify the relationship between WDFY4 expression and six types of tumor‐infiltrating immune cells: CD4+ T cells, CD8+ T cells, B cells, neutrophils, dendritic cells (DCs), and macrophages. Associations were estimated using the R package (version 1.0.13). The TIMER 2.0 database (http://timer.cistrome.org/) was used to further explore the relationship between WDFY4 and immune infiltration, tumor purity, and correlation analysis of the corresponding biomarkers. We also investigated the relationship between WDFY4 expression and immune subtypes in different human cancers. Timer, QUANTISEQ, CIBERSORT‐ABS, XCELL, EPIC, and MCPCOUNTER algorithms were used to evaluate the relationship between immune cell infiltration and specific immune cells. The TISIDB database, which contains multiple tumor immune infiltration data cohorts, was used to explore the relationship between WDFY4 and lymphocytes, immune stimulators, immunosuppressants, major histocompatibility complex (MHC) molecules, chemokines, and chemokine receptors. Finally, the TISIDB and TISCH2 databases were used to evaluate the relationship between WDFY4 expression and the tumor microenvironment (TME) in various cancers.

Functional enrichment analysis

Functional and pathway enrichment analysis of H2B‐encoded proteins was performed using the STRING database covering over 24 million proteins from 5090 organisms with a minimum interaction score of ≥0.7 (high confidence) required to construct a protein–protein interaction (PPI) network. Using the ClusterProfiler (version 3.14.3) software package, the genomes divided into WDFY4 high‐ and low‐expression groups were combined with logFC values for Gene Ontology (GO), Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes, and gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA). We calculated the z‐scores using the GOplot package (version 1.0.2). MSigDB ensemble data were used for GSEA analysis regarding c2.cp.v7.symbols.gmt. A nominal false discovery rate of <0.25 and a p‐value of <.05 were considered significant enrichment.

WDFY4 methylation analysis

The UALCAN database (http://timer.cistrome.org/) was used to study the differences in gene methylation in different TCGA cohorts. The MethSurv database was used to perform a survival analysis of methylated WDFY4, using COX regression analysis and the likelihood ratio test.

Interaction of WDFY4 with chemicals

The Comparative Toxicogenomics Database (CTD; http://www.ctdbase.org/) is a digital resource that facilitates the study of new molecular mechanisms by which chemicals affect human health. 12 We used this database to query the interacting chemical substances of WDFY4 and explored the genes highly similar to those of WDFY4 regarding common interacting chemical substances.

Cell culture

The A549 (RRID:CVCL_4V07) and H1975 (RRID:CVCL_B0JT) cell lines were obtained from the Cell Resource Center, Peking Union Medical College (PCRC). A549 and H1975 cells frozen in liquid nitrogen were quickly placed in a 37°C water bath and thawed by shaking. The thawed cell mixture was centrifuged at 1000 rpm for 5 min. The supernatant was carefully discarded, and 1 mL of complete culture medium (Dulbecco's modified Eagle medium +10% fetal bovine serum +1% penicillin and streptomycin) was added to mix the pellet. The pellet was carefully added to a 10‐cm Petri dish containing 10 mL of culture medium. The cell culture was cultivated in a 37°C cell culture incubator overnight to allow cell adhesion to the wall. The medium was replaced with fresh medium the next day, and the cells were used for subsequent experiments. Cell culture requires changing the medium every other day, and cells were passaged for 3 days. The cells in the exponential proliferation phase were passaged at a ratio of 1:3.

Lentiviral infection

Beijing Hesheng Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China) performed vector construction and lentiviral packaging. Cells were observed under an inverted microscope (Sunny Optical Technology(group)Co., Ltd., Ningbo, China). The cells were in good condition and had a moderate density. The cells were digested with 0.25% trypsin‐ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid to prepare a single‐cell suspension. The suspension was centrifuged at 1000 rpm for 5 min, the supernatant was removed, the cells resuspended in a culture medium, and the cells were counted using Automated cell counter (IM1200, Countstar, Shanghai, China). The cell suspension (2 mL) was prepared in a six‐well plate. The culture plates were incubated for 24 h (37°C, 5% CO2). When the cells reached 80% confluence, the overexpressing lentivirus and control virus of the WDFY4 gene were added to interfere with the cells (multiplicity of infection = 40). The cells were collected 12 h after infection and used for downstream experiments.

Cell Counting Kit‐8 (CCK‐8) method for detecting cell proliferation

The experimental groups included a control, negative control (NC), and overexpression (OE). The transfected A549 and H1975 cells were cultured in an incubator for 24, 48, 72, or 96 h. Subsequently, 10 μL of CCK‐8 solution was added to each well. The plates were incubated for 2 h (37°C, 5% CO2), and the absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader.

5‐Ethynyl‐2′‐deoxyuridine (EdU) method to detect cell proliferation

The final 10 μM EdU concentration (1×) was prepared by adding 1 mL of 2× EdU working solution (20 μM) preheated at 37°C. The cells were then incubated for 2 h. The culture medium was removed, 1 mL of fixative (4% paraformaldehyde; P0099) was added, and the cells were fixed at 22°C (room temperature) for 15 min. The fixative was removed, and the cells were washed thrice with 1 mL of washing solution per well for 3–5 min. The washing solution was removed, and 1 mL of phosphate‐buffered saline (PBS) containing 0.3% Triton X‐100 was added to each well and incubated at room temperature for 10–15 min. The permeabilization solution was removed, and the cells were washed one to two times with 1 mL of washing solution in each well for 3–5 min. After aspirating the washing solution, 1 mL of 1X Hoechst 33342 solution was added to each well and incubated at room temperature in the dark for 10 min. The 1X Hoechst 33342 solution was then aspirated, and the cells were washed thrice with washing solution for 3–5 min. Subsequently, fluorescence was detected. Hoechst 33342 exhibits blue fluorescence with a maximum excitation wavelength of 346 nm and a maximum emission wavelength of 460 nm.

Apoptosis and cell cycle determination using flow cytometry

Cells were digested with trypsin, and a single‐cell suspension was prepared to detect the cell cycle. The suspension was centrifuged at 1000 × g for 5 min to pellet the cells, and the supernatant was discarded. The cells were rinsed once with 1 mL of pre‐cooled PBS and centrifuged at 1000 × g for 5 min to collect the cells. The cell pellet was gently mixed with 1 mL pre‐cooled 70% ethanol and fixed at 4°C overnight. The cells were pelleted by centrifuging at 1000 × g for 5 min and resuspended in 1 mL of pre‐chilled PBS. Subsequently, 10 μL propidium iodide (PI) stock solution (40301‐B) and 10 μL RNaseA (40301‐A) solution were added to 0.5 mL staining buffer (40301‐C) and mixed well. PI staining solution (0.5 mL) was added to each sample and mixed gently. After incubation in the dark for 30 min, the cells were filtered through a 37‐nm mesh, detected using a flow cytometer (cytoFLEX, Beckman Coulter, America) at an excitation wavelength of 488 nm and analyzed for cell DNA content and light scattering analysis.

Apoptosis was detected by washing the cells once with PBS and then with 1× binding buffer. The cells (1–5 × 106 cells) were then suspended in 1× binding buffer. Fluorescent dye‐conjugated Annexin V (5 μL) was added to 100 μL cell suspension. The mixture was then incubated for 15 min. The cells were washed once with 1× binding buffer, resuspended in 200 μL of the same buffer, and 5 μL of PI staining solution was added. The suspension was tested by flow cytometry within 4 h. The results were analyzed using the flow analysis software CytExpert (version 2.4.0.28; Beckman Coulter, Brea, CA, USA).

Analysis of correlation between WDFY4 gene expression and drug sensitivity

IC50 of 265 small molecules and their corresponding mRNA gene expression were collected in 860 cell lines from Genomics of Drug Sensitivity in Cancer (GDSC) and in 1001 cell lines from Genomics of Therapeutic Response Portal (CTRP) Expression of mRNA genes corresponding to IC50 of 481 small molecules. Pearson correlation analysis was used to obtain the correlation between gene mRNA expression and drug IC50. p‐values are adjusted by FDR.

Statistical analysis

WDFY4 expression data were assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk normality test, and the samples did not fit a normal distribution. Differential gene expression data were analyzed using the Wilcoxon rank‐sum test. Survival analysis was performed using log‐rank regression and univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses to calculate the hazard ratio and P‐values. Kaplan–Meier analysis was used to study the survival time of patients stratified according to high or low WDFY4 expression levels. p‐values were adjusted for multiple comparisons. p < .05 was set as the significance threshold for all statistical analyses.

RESULTS

Pan‐cancer expression and alteration landscape of WDFY4

WDFY4 was differentially expressed in 11 common human tumors in unpaired samples (Figure 1A). In breast invasive carcinoma, cholangiocarcinoma (CHOL), kidney renal clear cell carcinoma (KIRC), kidney renal papillary cell carcinoma (KIRP), and stomach adenocarcinoma (STAD) datasets, WDFY4 expression was significantly higher in cancer tissues than in adjacent normal tissues (p < .05). In bladder urothelial carcinoma (BLCA), colon adenocarcinoma (COAD), lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD), lung squamous cell carcinoma (LUSC), pancreatic adenocarcinoma (PAAD), and rectal adenocarcinoma (READ) datasets, WDFY4 expression was significantly lower in cancerous tissues than in adjacent normal tissues. WDFY4 was differentially expressed in nine tumor types in paired samples (Figure 1B); the mRNA of WDFY4 in the uterine corpus endometrial carcinoma (UCEC) dataset was differentially expressed in cancerous and adjacent normal tissues, whereas this difference was not observed in unpaired samples. In addition, CHOL, PAAD, and READ did not show differential WDFY4 expression in the paired samples. Using normal samples from the XENA database, we additionally compared the differential expression of WDFY4 mRNA in cancer tissues and normal tissues in Figure S2A. Further analysis and comparison of WDFY4 protein expression according to the CPTAC database showed that WDFY4 protein expression in breast, colon, and lung cancers (including LUAD and LUSC, Figure S2B,C) and liver cancer tumor tissues was significantly lower than that in normal tissues; however, WDFY4 protein expression was higher in clear cell renal cell carcinoma, PAAD, and glioblastoma (GBM) than in normal tissues (Figure 1C). In summary, multiple analysis methods for analyzing the mRNA data of the TCGA database showed that WDFY4 was downregulated in BLCA, LUAD, and LUSC patients, and upregulated in BRCA, KIRC, KIRP, and STAD patients.

Using the cBioPortal database, we analyzed the mutation status of WDFY4 in the TCGA database and the mutation rate of WDFY4 in over a thousand cell lines from the CCLE database. Data from human cancer samples in the TCGA dataset showed that the mutation rate of WDFY4 was higher in non‐hematological tumors, especially in UCEC, where the mutation rate reached 15% (Figure 2A). Missense mutations were the most common alteration in WDFY4 (Figure 2B). In cancer cell line data from the CCLE database, WDFY4 had a higher mutation rate in hematological tumor‐related cell lines, especially in Burkitt lymphoma, which exceeded 80% (Figure 2C). Figure 2D shows the common alteration sites, with amplification and missense mutations being the most common alterations in WDFY4. Figure S2D also shows the three‐dimensional structure of this protein, with red representing the mutation positions.

Diagnostic value of WDFY4 across Cancer types

To study the relationship between WDFY4 expression and the clinicopathological features of various cancers, we selected cancers with consistent differential WDFY4 expression in paired and unpaired samples or consistent protein expression trends, including BLCA, COAD, glioma, KIRC, KIRP, LUAD, LUSC, and STAD. ROC curves were used to evaluate the diagnostic accuracy of WDFY4. The ROC analysis AUC of this model had high diagnostic accuracy for four cancers: BLCA, COAD, KIRC, and LUAD, and the highest diagnostic accuracy for LUSC, with an AUC of 0.933 (Figure 3). The correlation between WDFY4 expression and cancer stages I, II, III, and IV was evaluated. In patients with LUAD and KIRP, WDFY4 expression gradually decreased with cancer progression; In addition, the expression of WDFY4 in patients with stage 3 glioma was higher than that in patients with stage 2 glioma; the expression of WDFY4 in patients with stage 3 STAD was higher than that in patients with stage 1 STAD. However, this was not observed in other cancers (Figure 4).

Pan‐cancer analysis of the multifaceted prognostic value of WDFY4

Using a pan‐cancer dataset, we estimated the relationship between WDFY4 expression and patient prognosis. Survival measures included OS, disease‐specific survival (DSS), and PFI. Log‐rank regression analysis of the results of the above eight cancers showed that WDRY4 expression in four cancers, BLCA, glioma, KIRC, and LUAD, was significantly correlated with prognostic survival (Figure 5A). Kaplan–Meier survival curves indicated that downregulated WDFY4 expression was significantly associated with good OS in KIRC and LUAD; however, it was associated with poor overall survival in BLCA and glioma (Figure 5B). Univariate and multivariate regression analyses supported low WDFY4 expression as a risk factor for overall survival in patients with KIRC and LUAD, and its impact was similar to that of pathological stage and age (Tables S1 and S2). However, univariate and multivariate regression analyses showed that WDFY4 was not a protective or risk factor for BLCA and glioma (Tables S3 and S4).

Based on the results of univariate and multivariate regression analyses, we established a prognostic nomogram and a standard prognostic curve to corroborate each other and predict the 1‐, 3‐, and 5‐year prognostic survival of patients with KIRC and LUAD according to various clinical factors (Figure 5C–F).

Relationship between WDFY4 expression level and tumor immune cell infiltration

Next, we studied the effect of WDFY4 on the immune infiltration of 24 types of immune cells in various cancers, and r > .7 was regarded as a strong correlation. WDFY4 positively correlated with macrophages, T cells, and B cells in BLCA, KIRP, LUSC, and STAD (Figure 6A). Similar trends were observed using the TIMER, QUANTISEQ, CIBERSORT‐ABS, XCELL, EPIC, and MPCOUNTER algorithms to evaluate the relationship between WDFY4 and immune cell infiltration in multiple cancer types (Figure 6B). Myeloid DCs were closely associated with WDFY4 in various cancers. Correlations between WDFY4 and lymphocytes, immunostimulators, immunosuppressants, MHC molecules, chemokines, and chemokine receptor biomarkers in various tumor types were validated using data from the TISIDB database (Figure S3). We then globally assessed the relationship between WDFY4 expression and immune subtypes that characterize the TME. Except for C5 (immunologically quiet), WDFY4 expression was closely associated with all immune subtypes. However, in gliomas, WDFY4 was closely associated with C5 (Figure 6C).

Functional enrichment analysis of WDFY4

STRING data were used to explore the functions and pathways of WDFY4. A PPI network was constructed using 52 proteins closely related to WDFY4 (Figure 9A). Some of these results are visualized in Figure 9B, and the complete results are shown in Table S5. Enrichment analysis showed that WDFY4 was involved in histamine secretion by mast cells (GO: 0002553), regulation of neutrophil degranulation (GO: 0043313), the B‐cell receptor signaling pathway (GO: 0050853), forkhead box protein O‐mediated oxidative stress, metabolism, neuronal gene transcription (HSA‐9615017), production of molecular mediators involved in the inflammatory response (GO: 0002532), and differentiation of DCs (GO: 0097028).

The logFC values were combined to perform enrichment analysis on WDFY4, taking KIRC and LUAD as examples. We observed >300 functions or pathways in KIRC, including complement activation, classical pathway (GO: 0006958), B‐cell receptor signaling pathway (GO: 0050853), immunoglobulin production (GO: 0002377), immunoglobulin complex (GO: 0019814), T‐cell receptor complex (GO: 0042101), immunoglobulin complex, circulating (GO: 0042571), antigen binding (GO: 0003823), immunoglobulin receptor binding (GO: 0034987), immune receptor activity (GO: 0140375), complement and coagulation cascades (hsa04610), Staphylococcus aureus infection (hsa05150), and neuroactive ligand–receptor interaction (hsa04080) (Figure 9C). GSEA analysis revealed the most likely pathways involved in WDFY4 in KIRC, namely FCERI‐mediated nuclear factor kappa B activation and the role of LAT2/NTAL/LAB on calcium mobilization (Figure 9D). In LUAD, 45 functions or pathways were observed, including the detection of chemical stimulus involved in sensory perception (GO: 0050907), formation of quadruple SL/U4/U5/U6 small nuclear ribonucleoprotein (snRNP) (GO: 0000353), mRNA trans‐splicing via spliceosome (GO: 0000365), Cajal body (GO: 0015030), spliceosomal snRNP complex (GO: 0097525), snRNP complex (GO: 0030532), olfactory receptor activity (GO: 0004984), bitter taste receptor activity (GO: 0033038), taste receptor activity (GO: 0008527), olfactory transduction (hsa04740), taste transduction (hsa04742), and spliceosome (hsa03040) (Figure 9E). GSEA analysis revealed that the pathways most likely involving WDFY4 in LUAD included the translation and metabolism of amino acids and derivatives (Figure 9F).

WDFY4 methylation in cancers

Methylation levels of WDFY4 in different cancers were assessed using the UALCAN database (Figure 10). WDFY4 shows differential methylation and is hypermethylated in 17 cancer types. The Methsurv database revealed that differential methylation of WDFY4 causes different prognoses in patients with cancer, including pediatric low‐grade gliomas, Uveal Melanoma (UVM), GBM, acute myeloid leukemia (LAML), soft tissue sarcoma (SARC), KIRC, STAD, and BLCA (Table 2). Hypermethylated WDFY4 was a risk factor for GBM, LAML, and SARC and a protective factor in the remaining five cancers.

In vitro studies of WDFY4 function

Next, we studied how WDFY4 affects lung cancer in vitro. We constructed a lentivirus and infected the A549 and H1975 cell lines to overexpress WDFY4. The cell activity of the OE‐WDFY4 group did not change significantly at 24 h in A549 and H1975 cells; however, their cell activity decreased significantly at 48 and 72 h (Figure 11A). Simultaneously, cell proliferation experiments also showed that the vitality of cell proliferation in the WDFY4 group was considerably lower than in the control group (Figure 11B). WDFY4 overexpression significantly increased the number of apoptotic A549 and H1975 cells, indicating that WDFY4 overexpression promoted apoptosis (Figure 11C). In addition, in A549 cells, WDFY4 overexpression had no significant effect on the cell cycle. However, in H1975 cells, WDFY4 overexpression significantly reduced the proportion of cells in the G0/G1 phase and increased the number of cells in the G2/M phase, indicating that the cell cycle was inhibited (Figure 11D). To validate this finding, we further analyzed the gene expressions of CDK1, CDK2, CCNA2, and CCNB1 in H1975 cells following WDFY4 overexpression and observed a significant decrease. Further details of the cell experiments are in Figure S4.

DISCUSSION

To date, oncology has been a research hotspot in the medical field. Many cancer‐related databases have been created, making it convenient for researchers to conduct in‐depth research on cancer incidence and treatment strategies. TCGA is one of the largest cancer genome databases available for patients with cancer. Researchers have explored biomarkers suitable for broad‐spectrum cancer diagnosis through differential gene expression analysis. However, the role of WDFY4 in cancers was unclear. In this study, WDFY4 was upregulated or downregulated in various cancer types, and we confirmed this differential result at the gene and protein levels. We selected eight cancers with the most typical differential WDFY4 expression and consistent and statistically significant gene and protein expression differences, including BLCA, COAD, glioma, KIRC, KIRP, LUAD, LUSC, and STAD. Significant differences in expression between cancerous and normal tissues were elucidated, and their early detection value, regulatory pathways, related genes, and chemicals were discussed.

In the pan‐cancer analysis of WDFY4 gene expression, we found that, in breast cancer and pancreatic cancer, there is an opposite trend between gene expression and protein level. As we all know, proteins go through the process of transcription, translation, and post‐translational modification. The level of mRNA is only one part of the transcription process in the reaction.

This study found that WDFY4 had different differential expression trends and prognostic factors in different cancers. The role of WDFY4 in malignant tumors is complex and may play a role in many aspects of immunology. WDFY4 interacts with various immune cells in the immune system. For example, WDFY4 blocks the cross‐presentation of DCs to CD8+ T cells and induces CD8+ T‐cell apoptosis, resulting in impaired CD8+ T‐cell responses and uncontrolled tumor growth. 7 Moreover, the lack of WDFY4 in T cells can enhance reactive oxygen species‐induced apoptosis and inhibit T‐cell proliferation through the p53 and ERK signaling pathways, decreasing CD8+ T cells and impaired antitumor responses. 13 Additionally, the loss of WDFY4 severely affects B‐cell differentiation and development, decreasing the total number of B cells. 8 , 14 These studies elucidate why low WDFY4 expression results in poor prognosis in KIRC, LUAD, and LUSC. However, high WDFY4 expression is a prognostic risk factor in glioma and BLCA, and studies are still insufficient to elucidate its specific mechanism.

Recently, a non‐canonical form of autophagy, LC3‐associated phagocytosis (LAP), has been identified. Autophagy is characterized by LC3 lipidation. 15 However, unlike autophagy, LAP proceeds independently of a pre‐initiation complex containing ULK1 and FIP200, whose central protein complex contains many factors, including PIK3C3. 16 Therefore, atypical autophagy may be an important phenotype in which WDFY4 affects B‐cell development. Lipidation of LC3 in WDFY4‐knockdown B cells was apparent, and the level of the atypical autophagy‐related molecule PI3KC3 was altered. Furthermore, WDFY4 knockdown increased the activity of the B‐cell Golgi apparatus, a corresponding organelle of atypical autophagy. 17 Therefore, WDFY4 may play an essential role in cancer progression via atypical autophagy. These studies suggest that WDFY4 is primarily distributed in B cells and DCs in NSCLC. Our study suggests that reducing WDFY4 expression is a means of immune escape in lung cancer.

DNA methylation is an important mechanism of DNA epigenetic modification that regulates gene expression without changing the DNA sequence. 18 DNA methylation usually inhibits gene expression by changing the chromatin structure, DNA stability, and DNA conformation 19 and plays a crucial regulatory role in cell differentiation, proliferation, and aging. 20 The link between DNA methylation and cancer has been discovered recently. Hypermethylation of the enhancer and promoter regions often leads to the silencing or inactivation of tumor suppressor genes in cancer cells, 18 , 19 such as genes in NSCLC, including p16, RASSF1A, and MGMT. 21 The present study showed that the DNA methylation of WDFY4 was downregulated in 17 cancers, inconsistent with the altered WDFY4 expression. In addition, WDFY4 methylation affected the prognosis of eight cancers. Therefore, the regulatory relationship between DNA methylation and WDFY4 expression warrants further investigation.

Our in vitro data indicate that overexpression of WDFY4 inhibits the activity and proliferation of H1975 and A549 cell lines while promoting cell apoptosis. Furthermore, overexpression of WDFY4 induces G2/M phase arrest in the H1975 cell line, a phenomenon not observed in the A549 cell line. It is well established that the cell cycle is regulated by cyclins and cyclin‐dependent kinase (CDK) family genes, prompting us to examine the expression levels of CDK1, CDK2, CCNA2, and CCNB1 in the H1975 cell line. We found that WDFY4 overexpression results in decreased expression of these genes. Hence, we speculate that WDFY4 overexpression may cause G2/M phase arrest in the H1975 lung adenocarcinoma cell line by downregulating CDK1, CDK2, CCNA2, and CCNB1 expression. The specific mechanisms underlying this effect warrant further investigation.

However, this study has some limitations. First, owing to the lack of commercial WDFY4 protein antibodies, we could not verify the various phenotypes at the protein level. Second, evidence regarding the role of WDFY4 in tumor immunity is lacking. Combining previous and current studies, we speculate that, in lung cancer, WDFY4 may have some interaction with B cells and T cells, or play a role in antigen presentation with cDC cells. Therefore, our next study will focus on the role of WDFY4 in the tumor immune microenvironment.

CONCLUSIONS

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to show that WDFY4 has extensive expression differences between tumor and normal tissues through pan‐cancer analysis, revealing a correlation between WDFY4 expression and clinical prognosis. A strong positive correlation between tumor invasion and stromal scores was observed. WDFY4 could be an independent prognostic factor for BLCA, glioma, LUAD, and KIRC. WDFY4 strongly correlates positively with B and T cells in BLCA, KIRP, LUSC, and STAD. In addition, WDFY4 was primarily distributed in the B cells and DCs of NSCLC immune cells. Therefore, we believe that a reduction in WDFY4 expression leads to an impaired immune response in NSCLC, one of the reasons for lung cancer onset and progression. We also studied WDFY4 in terms of gene mutation, enrichment analysis, methylation level, and chemical substance interactions, clarifying the mechanism of WDFY4 in tumorigenesis and development and the development of drugs targeting WDFY4. Finally, lentiviral WDFY4 overexpression significantly inhibited the activity and proliferation of lung cancer cell lines, promoted their apoptosis, and stopped their cell cycle in the G2/M phase. Our results will be the cornerstone of WDFY4 research on cancer and immunity, as WDFY4 can be used as a novel biomarker for cancer. In the future, the role of WDFY4 in tumor immunity will be investigated along with the various WDFY4 phenotypes at the protein level.
