Environmental exposure and respiratory health in industrial gulf coast communities: a mixed-method study
Authors: Ayodeji Iyanda, Chiung-Fang Chang, Jennifer Erdely, Genny Carrillo, Tianjun Lu, Christopher Jones, Hilton Kelley, Daikwon Han, Xiao Huang, Yongmei Lu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIndustrialized communities along the U.S. Gulf Coast face persistent respiratory health inequities that are shaped by cumulative environmental exposures and social vulnerability. This study examined how residential proximity to multiple pollution sources interacts with sociodemographic and behavioral factors to influence respiratory outcomes in two industrial cities in Southeast Texas.MethodsA cross-sectional mixed methods design integrated survey data from 2,965 adult residents with 13 qualitative interviews. Multivariable logistic regression models estimated associations between self-reported residential proximity to pollution sources and physician-diagnosed asthma, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), and other lung diseases, and included interaction terms to assess effect modification by age, sex, race/ethnicity, and smoking status.ResultsRespiratory conditions were common: 38.3% reported asthma, 11.1% COPD, and 6.0% other lung diseases. Proximity to pesticide-related industries was associated with substantially higher odds of asthma (OR = 3.11, 95% CI: 2.11–4.59) and other lung diseases (OR = 3.14, 95% CI: 1.71–5.75). Living near waste management facilities associated with substantial higher odds of asthma (OR = 2.62, 95% CI: 1.83–3.75) and COPD (OR = 3.05, 95% CI: 1.95–4.76). Self-reported proximity to industrial factories was strongly associated with asthma (OR = 3.11, 95% CI: 2.35–4.11) and COPD (OR = 1.80, 95% CI: 1.14–2.82).Interaction models revealed subgroup-specific patterns of association. Smoking modified the association between self-reported traffic exposure and asthma with higher odds observed among active smokers reporting high traffic exposure (Active smoker × high traffic: OR = 1.88; p < 0.05). Among adults aged 50–59, residential proximity to refineries was associated with substantially higher odds of COPD (OR = 10.57; p < 0.001). Gender also modified the association between pesticide-related exposure to lung disease, with women exhibiting lower relative odds compared with men (interaction OR = 0.34; p < 0.05). Longer residential duration was associated with higher odds of lung disease, while residential stability showed outcome-specific and divergent associations across respiratory conditions.Qualitative interviews highlighted chronic exposure to industrial emissions, inadequate risk communication, limited mobility, and barriers to healthcare, reinforcing and contextualizing the quantitative associations.ConclusionsRespiratory health risks in Gulf Coast fence-line communities are shaped by intersecting environmental exposures, behavioral factors, and social conditions. Elevated risks associated with proximity to pesticide-related industries, waste facilities, and industrial factories, combined with differential vulnerabilities across age, gender, and smoking status, underscore the cumulative nature of environmental health burdens. Addressing these inequities requires community-engaged, place-based interventions and policies that simultaneously target industrial emissions, risk communication, and broader structural determinants of health.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-026-27306-3.

Introduction

Communities along the U.S. Gulf Coast experience persistent health inequities arising from the convergence of industrial land use, social disadvantage, and environmental exposure. Decades of research have demonstrated that residential proximity to petrochemical facilities, refineries, high-traffic corridors, and other hazardous land uses is associated with elevated risks of respiratory disease, cardiovascular conditions, cancer, adverse birth outcomes, and mental health stressors [1, 2]. These health burdens are not randomly distributed; rather, they disproportionately affect low-income communities and communities of color, reflecting historical zoning practices, structural racism, and uneven regulatory enforcement [3–5].

The Social Determinants of Health (SDOH) framework provides a critical foundation for understanding these inequities by situating health outcomes within the broader social, economic, and environmental contexts [6, 7]. Factors such as income, employment, housing quality, access to healthcare, transportation, and neighborhood infrastructure interact with environmental exposures to produce cumulative health exposures across the life course [8, 9]. In heavily industrialized regions, these determinants are often compounded by chronic exposure to air pollution and other environmental hazards, reinforcing cycles of vulnerability and poor respiratory health outcomes.

Environmental Justice Theory further emphasizes that environmental exposure is inseparable from power, governance, and historical inequality [10, 11]. Foundational environmental justice scholarship has shown that hazardous facilities are more likely to be sited in communities with limited political capital, resulting in disproportionate exposure to pollution and reduced access to environmental protections [3–5, 12–16]. Subsequent studies highlight how these inequities persist through regulatory gaps, limited public participation in environmental decision-making, and insufficient transparency from both industry and government institutions [14, 17–20]. As a result, environmental injustice operates not only through direct physical exposure but also through chronic stress, uncertainty, and diminished trust in institutions—factors that have been linked to adverse respiratory and mental health outcomes.

Gulf Coast communities are further shaped by climate-related stressors, including more frequent and intense hurricanes, flooding, extreme heat, and infrastructure disruption. These climate hazards amplify existing social and environmental vulnerabilities, particularly in communities with limited adaptive capacity and under-resourced public health systems [21, 22]. Research increasingly shows that the cumulative effects of industrial exposure, social disadvantage, and climate stressors contribute to both acute and chronic health conditions, underscoring the need for integrative approaches that examine environmental, social, and behavioral processes simultaneously [23].

While quantitative studies have documented associations between environmental exposures and respiratory health, there remains a need for research designs that also capture how residents perceive, experience, and navigate environmental exposures in everyday life [24]. Community Resilience Theory emphasizes the role of social networks, collective efficacy, and local knowledge in shaping community responses to environmental and health challenges [25]. At the same time, scholars caution against framing resilience as a substitute for structural change, noting that community coping strategies often emerge in response to systemic neglect rather than equitable governance [26–28].

Within this context, Community-Based Participatory Research (CBPR) has emerged as a critical methodological and ethical approach for studying environmental health inequities. CBPR emphasizes shared decision-making, co-production of knowledge, and the integration of community priorities throughout the research process [29]. Empirical evidence demonstrates that CBPR enhances the relevance, validity, and translational impact of environmental health research, particularly in communities that have historically been marginalized or excluded from research and policy processes [30, 31].

Guided by these theoretical frameworks, the present study employs a cross-sectional mixed methods design to examine environmental exposures, social determinants of health, and respiratory health outcomes in two industrialized Gulf Coast cities in Southeast Texas. By integrating population-level survey data with in-depth qualitative interviews and grounding the research in CBPR principles, the study aims to generate both empirical evidence and contextual understanding of how environmental conditions and social vulnerability intersect to shape respiratory health in industrially burdened communities by partnering with local community-based and environmental justice orgnaizations.

The aims of this study are twofold. First, using cross-sectional survey data, the study examines associations between self-reported residential proximity to multiple sources of environmental pollution—including high-traffic roadways, refineries, pesticide-related industries, waste management facilities, and other manufacturing operations—and self-reported respiratory health outcomes, specifically asthma, COPD, and other lung diseases, while accounting for key sociodemographic and behavioral factors. Second, through in-depth qualitative interviews, the study explores how residents and community stakeholders perceive environmental conditions, describe health-related exposures, and navigate the social and structural contexts within which exposure and health are experienced in everyday life. Together, these complementary approaches address the following research questions: (1) How is residential proximity to major sources of pollution associated with respiratory health outcomes in industrialized Gulf Coast communities? (2) How do social determinants of health intersect with environmental exposure in relation to respiratory health outcomes? and (3) How do community members interpret, experience, and respond to environmental and health challenges within these settings?

Methods

This study was conducted in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) Statement [32] and adhered to the Sex and Gender Equity in Research (SAGER) guidelines [33].

Study design

This study employed a cross-sectional mixed-methods design integrating quantitative survey data and qualitative in-depth interviews to examine environmental exposures, social determinants of health (SDOH), and respiratory health outcomes in two industrialized Gulf Coast cities in Southeast Texas. A mixed-methods approach was selected to capture both population-level patterns and in-depth contextual insights regarding how residents experience and respond to environmental conditions in their daily lives [34]. The design aligns with principles of CBPR by centering community priorities, leveraging community partnerships for recruitment, and producing findings intended to inform local public health action [31]. A convergent mixed methods design was adopted for this study [35]. The quantitative and qualitative components were conducted concurrently and analyzed separately, with integration and triangulation occurring during interpretation to provide complementary perspectives on environmental health risk and lived experience (Fig. 1).

Study area and setting

The study was conducted in Beaumont and Port Arthur, Texas, two neighboring cities located along the upper Texas Gulf Coast that have long served as major hubs for petrochemical refining, energy production, shipping, and industrial manufacturing. Both cities are situated within Jefferson County and form part of a broader industrial corridor that includes refineries, chemical plants, bulk storage facilities, rail yards, ports, and extensive highway infrastructure. These land-use patterns place residential neighborhoods in close proximity to multiple sources of air pollution, including stationary industrial emissions and mobile sources associated with freight transport and commuter traffic. Beaumont and Port Arthur are also historically significant environmental justice communities. Decades of industrial siting, zoning practices, and uneven regulatory enforcement have contributed to disproportionate environmental burdens among low-income residents and communities of color [36]. As observed by Bruno and Jepson [37], many neighborhoods are “fenceline” communities, where homes, schools, and churches are located adjacent to or downwind from industrial facilities. Residents frequently experience chronic exposure to industrial odors, flaring events, accidental releases, and routine emissions, as well as cumulative stressors related to flooding, extreme heat, and infrastructure vulnerability. These environmental conditions intersect with social determinants of health such as limited access to healthcare, housing insecurity, constrained residential mobility, and employment patterns tied to the industrial economy.

Quantitative data for this study were collected across 11 ZIP codes spanning Beaumont and Port Arthur, capturing spatial variation in residential proximity to refineries, manufacturing facilities, waste management sites, pesticide-related industries, and high-traffic transportation corridors. The selected ZIP codes include neighborhoods with long-standing residential stability as well as areas experiencing gradual demographic and economic change, allowing for examination of cumulative exposure processes alongside mobility and life-course dynamics. The geographic scope was intentionally defined to balance analytic rigor with community relevance, aligning with CBPR principles and local partner priorities. Together, these settings provide a critical context for examining how overlapping industrial, social, and environmental conditions are associated with respiratory health risks in Gulf Coast communities.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Eligibility for participation in the quantitative survey was restricted to adult residents aged 18 years or older who reported current or prior residence within the 11 target ZIP codes in Beaumont and Port Arthur, Texas. These ZIP codes were selected a priori to capture neighborhoods with varying proximity to industrial facilities, transportation corridors, and other potential sources of environmental exposure.

Participants were required to provide informed consent and to complete survey items assessing environmental exposure and respiratory health outcomes. Individuals were excluded if they reported residence outside the defined study area, were younger than 18 years of age, or did not provide sufficient information on key exposure or outcome variables required for analysis. Participation was voluntary, and no incentives were tied to eligibility criteria.

Data cleaning and final analytic sample

Following data collection, we applied a prespecified data-cleaning protocol prior to analysis. First, we restricted the dataset to respondents residing within the 11 target ZIP codes in Beaumont and Port Arthur; records with ZIP codes outside this catchment area or without verifiable Texas Gulf Coast addresses were excluded. Second, we removed ineligible respondents younger than 18 years of age.

Third, duplicate or multiple survey submissions were identified and removed using a combination of timestamps, repeated contact fields (when provided, e.g., cell number), device or browser identifiers, and IP or subnet checks. When duplicate entries were detected, the earliest complete response was retained. Fourth, we excluded responses that failed internal consistency or attention checks, including logically incompatible answers, implausible values, patterned straight-lining on scaled items, or nonsensical open-ended responses. Surveys flagged as potentially fraudulent (e.g., bot-like completion times far below the 5th percentile) were also removed.

Finally, partial surveys that did not meet the predefined completion threshold for core exposure, outcome, and covariate variables were excluded. After applying these inclusion, exclusion, and data-quality criteria, the final analytic sample consisted of 2,965 unique, eligible respondents.

Quantitative component

Survey design and administration

The quantitative component consisted of an online cross-sectional survey administered through Qualtrics and disseminated across multiple channels—including Facebook, community researchers, and partner organization networks—to maximize reach within the target communities between November 2024 and April 2025 (See supplementary material). Participation was voluntary, responses were confidential, and the survey was conducted as an IRB-reviewed study; all participants provided online informed consent before accessing survey questions. The survey instrument was designed to capture residents’ experiences with environmental conditions and health in their daily lives and included domains assessing perceptions of air quality, environmental conditions, and climate impacts; self-reported health and well-being; and socioeconomic and other characteristics related to social determinants of health such as access to health care, housing conditions, transportation access, community resources, and social support.

The survey was intentionally designed to target adult residents (> 18 years) living in environmentally vulnerable areas and to document shared patterns across communities rather than isolated individual experiences. This approach responds to the recognition that community concerns are often underrepresented in technical or highly aggregated datasets, despite environmental conditions being a persistent feature of daily life in Southeast Texas.

Quantitative measures

Self-reported residential proximity

Primary environmental exposure variables were derived from participants’ responses to survey items assessing residential proximity to potential sources of environmental pollution. Participants were asked whether they had ever lived near high-traffic roadways or busy roads, refineries, industrial or manufacturing facilities, waste management sites such as landfills or incinerators, or agricultural areas characterized by heavy pesticide use. Environmental exposure variables reflect self-reported residential proximity at any point in participants’ residential history within the study area, rather than current residence alone.

Proximity was intentionally defined as perceived residential proximity, capturing residents’ lived experience of environmental exposure rather than objective distance-based thresholds or modeled exposure estimates. This approach is consistent with community-based and environmental justice research conducted in industrialized settings, where emissions are often episodic, hyperlocal, and inadequately characterized by regulatory monitoring, and where residents’ experiential knowledge provides critical insight into cumulative exposure conditions.

Each exposure item included dichotomous response options (Yes = 1, No = 2) and was operationalized as a binary indicator reflecting whether participants reported having lived near the specified pollution source. These measures do not capture the precise timing or duration of exposure relative to disease diagnosis; therefore, findings are interpreted as population-level associations rather than evidence of temporal or causal relationships. To partially account for cumulative exposure processes, models additionally adjusted for residential duration (years lived in the community) and residential stability.

Respiratory health outcomes

Respiratory health outcomes were assessed using self-reported, physician-diagnosed conditions. Participants were asked whether a healthcare professional had ever diagnosed them with asthma, COPD, or any other lung disease, with responses coded as Yes = 1 and No = 2. Each outcome was analyzed as a binary variable. Participants who reported “other lung disease” provided examples including interstitial lung disease, eosinophilic pneumonia, chronic bronchitis, fluid in the lungs, and pneumonia.

Self-reported physician diagnoses are commonly used in population-based studies of respiratory and environmental health outcomes, particularly when clinical verification is not feasible [1, 38, 39]. By explicitly referencing diagnoses made by healthcare professionals, this approach reduces the likelihood of misclassifying undiagnosed symptoms as disease. Nonetheless, self-reported diagnoses may be influenced by recall error, health literacy, or access to healthcare.

These considerations are especially relevant in environmental justice communities, where structural barriers such as limited access to healthcare, delayed diagnosis, and differential diagnostic opportunity may contribute to underreporting of respiratory conditions. As a result, any misclassification of outcomes is likely to be non-differential with respect to environmental exposure and would bias estimated associations toward the null. To partially account for factors related to diagnostic access and reporting, all models adjusted for key sociodemographic and household characteristics, including age, employment status, ethnicity, residential stability, and household composition.

Sociodemographic variables

The study included key sociodemographic and behavioral covariates identified in prior environmental health and epidemiologic research as relevant to respiratory outcomes and exposure risk. Covariates included smoking status defined whether participants smoke actively (1), occasionally (2), or do not smoke (3, reference category). Age was assessed categorically (1 = 18–29 years, 2 = 30–39, 3 = 40–49, 4 = 50–59, 5 = 60 years +). Sex originally captured four categories: 1 = male, 2 = female, 3 = nonbinary, 4 = other and was collapsed due to small response of this identified as nonbinary/third gender. The race variable was originally coded into six categories (1 = Asian, 2 = Black/African American, 3 = White/Caucasian, 4 = Native American, 5 = Pacific Islander, 6 = Other). Because the study was conducted in two predominantly Black communities, race was recoded into a binary variable: Black (1) and non-Black (0).

Ethnicity was coded as yes (1) for Hispanic/Latinx and no (0) for other. Employment status during the past two weeks was assessed using the following categories: (1) employed full-time (≈ 40 h/week), (2) employed part-time, (3) employed in two or more part-time jobs, (4) not employed, and (5) others. For analysis, this variable was dichotomized into employed (1) versus not employed (0). In addition, we asked respondents how long they have been living in the community, and it was reported as a continuous variable (local_yr). Household composition was captured using two binary items assessing whether participants had children in the home and whether anyone aged 65 years or older resided in the household (responses: yes = 1, no = 0). Outdoor activity was assessed by asking participants how often they engaged in outdoor physical activities, with response options: daily (1), several times per week (2), once per week (3), rarely (4), and never (5). For analytic purposes, this variable was dichotomized into regular outdoor activity (daily or several times per week = 1) versus infrequent outdoor activity (once per week or less = 0). These variables were included as covariates in all multivariable models to control for confounding and to isolate the association between environmental exposure and respiratory health outcomes.

Quantitative data analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize participant characteristics, exposure patterns, and respiratory health outcomes. We estimated multivariable logistic regression models to examine associations between self-reported residential proximity to pollution sources and each respiratory outcome—asthma, COPD, and other lung diseases. Separate models were specified for each outcome and adjusted for the full set of sociodemographic, behavioral, and household covariates described above. To assess and control for potential confounding, all multivariable logistic regression models included a priori sociodemographic, behavioral, household, and residential covariates identified from prior environmental health and epidemiologic literature. Key confounders—including smoking status, age group, sex, race/ethnicity, employment status, household composition, residential duration, and outdoor activity—were entered simultaneously into the models to estimate adjusted associations between environmental exposure and respiratory outcomes. This approach allowed us to evaluate the independent effects of environmental exposures while accounting for individual-level risk factors and potential co-exposures.

To assess potential effect modification, interaction terms between key individual characteristics (age group, sex, race/ethnicity, and smoking status) and environmental exposure variables were incorporated into selected models. These interaction analyses were used to evaluate whether the strength of associations between pollution exposure and respiratory outcomes varied across population subgroups. Results are presented as odds ratios (ORs) with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Multicollinearity was assessed using variance inflation factors (VIFs) from an equivalent linear regression model, and all VIF values were low (mean = 1.21; range: 1.03–1.84), indicating no evidence of problematic multicollinearity among predictors, including age and residential duration. Consistent with the cross-sectional design, the analyses emphasize population-level associations rather than causal inference. All statistical analyses were conducted in Stata version 19 [40].

In addition, we estimate the prevalence of respiratory health outcomes by zip codes using cross-tabulation method and we used geographic information systems (GIS) to map geographic distribution. To visualize spatial co-location with potential sources of environmental exposure, we overlay TRI point feature and transportation line feature on the health prevalence map.

Sensitivity analysis

To evaluate the robustness of our findings, we constructed a composite respiratory morbidity variable (resp_cb), coded as 1 if any of the following diagnoses were present—asthma (asma_s), COPD (copd_s), or other lung disease (lung_s)—and 0 if all three were absent. This combined outcome was used to test whether associations observed for individual conditions persisted when respiratory morbidity was considered more broadly, to enhance statistical power where individual outcomes were infrequent, and to reflect shared environmental risk pathways across respiratory conditions. Primary models were re-estimated substituting resp_cb for each individual outcome, using the same covariate set and model specifications as in the main analyses.

Qualitative component

Participant recruitment and sample

The qualitative component consisted of in-depth, semi-structured interviews with adult residents and community stakeholders recruited using a purposive sampling strategy consistent with CBPR principles. Participants were identified through community partner networks, referrals, and local outreach in Beaumont and Port Arthur to ensure inclusion of individuals with lived experience of environmental exposure, respiratory health concerns, and community engagement. The final qualitative sample included 13 participants representing a range of community roles, including educators, public health workers, veterans, and leaders of community-based organizations. The sample size was intentionally modest and not intended to be representative of the broader population. Given the long history of research in the Beaumont–Port Arthur region and documented community research fatigue, the study prioritized a smaller, information-rich sample to minimize participant burden while still achieving thematic saturation. Saturation was assessed iteratively during data collection, and no substantively new themes emerged in later interviews, indicating adequate depth and coverage of key study domains.

Interviews were conducted between December 2024 and April 2025 by a trained interviewer (JE). To ensure participant comfort, accessibility, and safety, interviews were held in public or open locations chosen collaboratively with participants (e.g., community spaces, cafés, or other neutral venues). Interviews followed a semi-structured guide designed to explore perceptions of environmental conditions, health and well-being, access to resources, and community-level challenges and strengths. The average interview duration was approximately 60 min, allowing sufficient time for participants to elaborate on experiences and perspectives. All interviews were audio-recorded with participant consent and later transcribed verbatim for analysis. Identifying information was removed during transcription to protect confidentiality.

Qualitative data analysis

Interviews were audio-recorded with participant consent, transcribed verbatim, and de-identified prior to analysis. Qualitative data were analyzed using a thematic analysis approach. Coding combined deductive domains informed by the study’s conceptual frameworks—environmental justice, social determinants of health, and community resilience—with inductive themes that emerged from participants’ narratives. Transcripts were reviewed iteratively to identify patterns and recurring concepts related to environmental exposure, respiratory health, social conditions, and community response. Analytic rigor was supported through repeated transcript review and reflexive memoing to document analytic decisions and emerging interpretations.

Integration of mixed methods

This study employed a convergent mixed-methods design in which quantitative and qualitative components were conducted concurrently, analyzed separately, and integrated during interpretation. Integration occurred at multiple stages of the research process. During study design, survey instruments and interview guides were informed by shared conceptual frameworks, including environmental justice, social determinants of health, and community resilience. Quantitative analyses identified population-level associations between self-reported residential proximity to environmental pollution sources and self-reported respiratory health outcomes, as well as subgroup-specific vulnerabilities through interaction models. Qualitative interviews were used to contextualize these statistical patterns by eliciting residents’ and community stakeholders’ perspectives on environmental conditions, exposure experiences, healthcare access, and everyday strategies for coping with industrial and social stressors.

Integration occurred primarily at the interpretive stage, where qualitative findings were used to explain, elaborate on, and provide context for observed quantitative associations. For example, statistical evidence of elevated respiratory risk near specific industrial sources was interpreted alongside interview narratives describing chronic emissions, limited residential mobility, and inadequate risk communication. Qualitative data were not used to validate causal relationships, but rather to illuminate mechanisms, lived experiences, and structural conditions underlying the quantitative results. This triangulation strengthened the explanatory depth of the findings while preserving the distinct analytic roles of each method.

ZIP-code–level patterns reveal marked spatial heterogeneity in respiratory health outcomes (Fig. 3; Supplementary Table S1). As shown in Fig. 3, the prevalence of asthma, COPD, and other lung diseases varied considerably across the 11 ZIP codes in Beaumont and Port Arthur. Areas with higher respiratory disease prevalence tended to overlap with neighborhoods characterized by dense industrial land use, proximity to TRI facilities, and major transportation corridors, as illustrated by the overlay of industrial sites and road networks. These spatial patterns suggest that respiratory health risks are shaped by cumulative exposure to multiple environmental sources in conjunction with underlying social vulnerability, rather than by any single point source. ZIP-code–level variation likely reflects differences in long-term residential exposure, land-use history, and neighborhood-level social conditions, underscoring the importance of place-based approaches to understanding respiratory health inequities in industrialized Gulf Coast communities.

Asthma prevalence exceeded 40% in several ZIP codes (77655, 77642, 77705, and 77707), while COPD prevalence was highest in ZIP codes 77642 and 77708. Other lung diseases were less prevalent overall but showed notable clustering in ZIP codes 77713, 77708, and 77642, suggesting localized cumulative exposure or vulnerability.

Self-reported environmental exposures were widespread across the research sample. Nearly one-quarter of respondents reported living near high-traffic roadways (24%), and approximately one-third reported proximity to industrial or manufacturing facilities (31%). Smaller but notable proportions reported residential proximity to refineries (11%), pesticide-related industries (8%), and waste management facilities (7%), reflecting pervasive exposure to multiple industrial pollution sources.

The sample was predominantly male (63%) and relatively young, with 92% of participants aged 18–49 years and the largest age group between 30 and 39 years (44%). Approximately one-third identified as Black/African American (33%), and 14% identified as Hispanic/Latinx. More than half of respondents reported current smoking (53%). Most participants were employed (93%) and lived in households with children (79%), while 36% reported the presence of an elderly household member. Residential stability was high, with 87% reporting no residential moves in the past decade and an average residential duration of 15 years (SD = 10.04), suggesting prolonged exposure to local environmental conditions.

Asthma

In the interaction-adjusted model, asthma risk remained strongly patterned by both self-reported environmental exposures and individual characteristics. Self-reported proximity to pesticide-related industries (OR = 3.11; p < 0.001), waste management facilities (OR = 2.62; p < 0.001), and industrial factories (OR = 3.11; p < 0.001) was independently associated with higher odds of asthma. Self-reported proximity to high-traffic roadways was not associated with asthma overall; however, there was a significant interaction with smoking status. The interaction between active smokers and self-reported living near high-traffic roadways was associated with asthma (OR = 1.88; p < 0.05), indicating that smoking amplifies traffic-related respiratory risk.

Age demonstrated a strong, monotonic association with asthma, with adults aged 50–59 and ≥ 60 years experiencing markedly elevated odds. Notably, the interaction between refinery proximity and age revealed effect modification: among adults aged 50–59 years, living near refineries was associated with significantly lower odds of asthma (OR = 0.10; p < 0.001), suggesting potential survivor effects, exposure-related residential mobility, or diagnostic differences across age groups.

Self-reported smoking status remained a strong predictor, with both active and occasional smokers exhibiting higher odds of asthma (Table 2). Household composition also mattered in this context. For example households with young children were more likely to report higher prevalence of asthma (OR = 2.18; p < 0.001), whereas the odds of reporting higher prevalence of asthma was lower in household with an elderly persons (OR = 0.47; p < 0.001) and engaging in regular outdoor activity (OR = 0.76; p < 0.05). Longer residential duration was modestly associated with lower odds of asthma, whereas residential stability (non-movers) was associated with higher asthma odds, a pattern consistent with cumulative exposure processes among long-term residents.

Qualitative interviews reinforce the quantitative findings, with residents describing persistent industrial emissions and normalization of asthma—particularly among children—in fenceline neighborhoods. Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease

For COPD, self-reported proximity to waste management facilities (OR = 3.05, p < 0.001) and industrial factories (OR = 1.80; p < 0.05 ) emerged as significant environmental predictors. Smoking was the dominant behavioral risk factor, with active smokers exhibiting more than twice the odds of COPD (OR = 2.17; p < 0.001) and occasional smokers higher odds of reporting COPD (OR = 1.63; p < 0.05).

Effect modification by age was observed for self-reported refinery proximity. Among adults aged 50–59 years, residential proximity to refineries was associated with substantially higher odds of COPD (OR = 10.57, p < 0.001). No statistically significant interactions were observed between self-reported traffic exposure and smoking.

Black/African American participants had higher odds of COPD overall (OR = 1.42; p < 0.05), although the interaction between self-reported waste proximity and race was not statistically significant. Hispanic participants exhibited substantially lower odds of COPD relative to non-Hispanic participants (OR = 0.33; p < 0.001). Furthermore, living with an elderly household member was associated with higher odds of COPD (OR = 2.37; p < 0.001), whereas self-reported outdoor activity (OR = 0.63; p < 0.001 p < 0.01) and residential stability (OR = 0.52; p < 0.001) were protective. Qualitative interviews underscored narratives of progressive respiratory decline linked to long-term industrial exposure and aging. Interviewees shared that their elderly mothers were diagnosed with COPD.[Mom right after Harvey found out she had COPD. It was about March. Other lung diseases

For other lung diseases, self-reported proximity to pesticide-related industries was strongly associated with higher odd ration of reporting COPD (OR = 3.14; p < 0.001). Smoking demonstrated the largest effects across all models, with active smokers experiencing more than threefold higher odds (OR = 3.30; p < 0.001) and while those who smoke occasionally had about three times the odds of reporting lung diseases (OR = 2.78; p < 0.001). Sex significantly modified the association between pesticide exposure and lung disease. While female participants had higher odds of lung disease overall (OR = 2.53; p < 0.001), women living near pesticide-related industries experienced significantly lower odds compared with men (interaction OR = 0.34; p < 0.001), which may be explained by gendered differences in exposure pathways, occupational roles, or health-seeking behaviors.

Age-related interactions with self-reported proximity to refinery were also observed. Among adults aged ≥ 60 years, living near refineries was associated with higher odds of lung disease (OR = 16.10; p < 0.05), suggesting a potential longtime exposure. Qualitative findings echoed these quantitative patterns, particularly among older adults and women who described worsening respiratory symptoms over time, limited access to specialty care, and challenges managing chronic conditions in the context of long-term industrial exposure. When asked for specific lung diseases, interviewees reported that lung cancer “has been real popular.”

Longer residential duration was associated with higher lung disease odds (OR = 1.04; p < 0.001), while residential stability was strongly protective (OR = 0.18; p < 0.001). Hispanic participants again exhibited lower odds of reporting lung diseases (OR = 0.21; p < 0.001) compared with their non-Hispanic participants.

Sensitivity analysis

To assess robustness and improve statistical power, we re-estimated the primary models using a composite respiratory morbidity outcome (resp_cb), defined as the presence of asthma, COPD, or other lung disease. Associations observed in the main analyses were largely preserved, with smoking and residential proximity to pesticide-related industries, waste management facilities, and industrial factories remaining significantly associated with higher odds of respiratory morbidity, while proximity to high-traffic roadways was not (Supplementary Table S2). Age showed a strong positive gradient, whereas longer residential duration and regular outdoor physical activity were inversely associated with respiratory morbidity. The consistency of these findings supports shared environmental risk pathways across respiratory conditions and confirms the robustness of the individual outcome results presented in Table 2.

Integration of quantitative and qualitative findings

Across all outcomes, interaction models revealed that respiratory health risks in industrialized Gulf Coast communities are not uniform but are shaped by the intersection of environmental exposure with smoking behavior, age, and gender. Proximity to pesticide-related industries and waste management facilities consistently emerged as high-risk exposures, while effect modification highlighted vulnerable subpopulations—particularly older adults, smokers, and long-term residents.

Qualitative narratives reinforce how these statistical interactions manifest in everyday life through cumulative exposure, constrained mobility, and uneven access to healthcare and environmental protections, reinforcing the central role of structural and contextual factors in producing respiratory health disparities.

One interviewee described a persistent lack of communication from local industries whenever leaks or hazardous releases occur. According to the participant, this failure to notify residents is perceived as contributing to heightened respiratory health concerns within the community—particularly for children and older adults, who are viewed as especially vulnerable. Their account underscores the importance of strengthening risk-communication practices and ensuring that residents receive timely, transparent information during environmental incidents.[I want to say, was it November or December? Discussion

This study provides mixed-methods evidence that residential proximity to industrial pollution sources was associated with adverse respiratory health outcomes in Gulf Coast communities. Because this study is cross-sectional, the observed associations should be interpreted cautiously. The findings reflect population-level relationships between residential proximity to environmental pollution sources and respiratory health outcomes and do not establish temporal ordering or causality. By integrating population-level survey data with in-depth qualitative interviews and grounding the analysis in environmental justice, social determinants of health, and CBPR frameworks, the findings move beyond documenting disparities to elucidating how industrial land use, social stratification, and differential vulnerability interact to produce adverse respiratory outcomes. Importantly, the inclusion of interaction models demonstrates that the health impacts of environmental proximity are not uniform across populations but are modified by age, sex, race/ethnicity, and smoking behavior—highlighting the relational and stratified nature of environmental risk.

Industrial proximity and respiratory Health

Consistent with decades of environmental justice scholarship, residential proximity to industrial pollution sources, particularly pesticide-related industries and waste management facilities, was strongly and consistently associated with asthma, COPD, and other lung diseases. These associations persisted after adjustment for smoking, demographic characteristics, and household composition, underscoring that environmental exposures exert independent effects on respiratory health. The observed effect sizes, including two- to fourfold differences in odds for certain outcomes, align with patterns reported in prior studies of respiratory morbidity in fence-line communities near petrochemical and waste-related facilities, without implying causality [2, 38].

The interaction models further extend this literature by demonstrating that the strength of these associations varies systematically across social groups. For example, proximity to waste management and pesticide-related facilities was more strongly associated with asthma and lung disease among older adults and smokers, suggesting synergistic effects between environmental exposure and biological susceptibility. These findings reinforce environmental justice theory by illustrating that disproportionate burden arises not only from unequal exposure but also from unequal vulnerability shaped by age, health status, and social position.

Qualitative findings deepen this interpretation by illustrating how residents experience industrial proximity not as discrete exposure events but as chronic, embodied conditions of daily life. Participants described persistent odors, visible emissions, and recurring respiratory irritation, reinforcing the notion that cumulative, low-level exposures—often inadequately captured by regulatory monitoring—contribute meaningfully to respiratory disease burden. These narratives echo critiques within environmental justice literature that formal risk assessments frequently underestimate community-level exposure by failing to account for cumulative and synergistic hazards [15, 41, 42].

Social determinants of health as modifiers of environmental risk

The findings support the SDOH framework by showing that environmental exposures interact with household composition, employment, residential stability, and behavioral factors to influence respiratory outcomes. The main effect models show an incremental effect among age groups where older population are more than ten times odds of reporting asthma. Interaction analyses indicate that the presence of children in the household amplified the association between industrial proximity and asthma, highlighting children as a particularly vulnerable subgroup within industrialized environments. This quantitative pattern is reinforced by qualitative accounts in which caregivers described frequent childhood respiratory symptoms and limited capacity to shield children from ambient pollution.

Similarly, interaction effects involving elderly household members suggest heightened vulnerability to Asthma, COPD and other lung diseases in the context of industrial exposure. These findings underscore intergenerational dimensions of environmental health risk and align with life-course perspectives emphasizing cumulative exposure and age-related susceptibility. Qualitative interviews further reveal how inadequate housing quality, limited healthcare access, and transportation barriers constrain residents’ ability to mitigate exposure or manage chronic conditions effectively. Participants describe the proximity of industrial sites to households, the ubiquitousness of foul-smelling odors, and the frequency of announced/acknowledged and unannounced/unacknowledged pollution events as persistent factors in health outcomes.

Cumulative exposure, residential stability, and life-course risk

Residential duration and stability emerged as important—but outcome-specific—predictors of respiratory health. Longer residential duration was associated with higher odds of other lung diseases, consistent with cumulative exposure effects emphasized in life-course epidemiology. Interaction models suggest that long-term residence near multiple pollution sources compounds risk, particularly among older adults and those with limited mobility.

In contrast, residential stability was associated with higher odds of asthma but lower odds of COPD and lung disease, indicating complex mobility–health dynamics. One interpretation, supported by qualitative data, is that individuals with more severe or progressive disease may relocate when resources allow, while those with fewer resources remain exposed over long periods [43]. Interview participants frequently linked long-term residence and family employment histories in industrial sectors to worsening health over time, reinforcing the concept of embodied environmental risk.

These findings align with emerging atmospheric health literature emphasizing cumulative exposure metrics, residential duration, and interaction effects as critical components of respiratory risk assessment [39, 44].

Race/ethnicity, gender, and uneven burdens of risk

The consistently elevated odds of respiratory outcomes among Hispanic participants—particularly for lung disease—highlight the intersection of environmental exposure with racialized and ethnicized vulnerability. Interaction analyses suggest that Hispanic identity modified the association between industrial proximity and lung disease, pointing to compounded risks potentially linked to language barriers, occupational exposures, healthcare access, or residential location within industrial zones. Qualitative accounts of delayed diagnoses and limited access to specialty care among Hispanic residents lend credibility to this interpretation and underscore the need for more disaggregated analyses in future research.

Gender differences observed in lung disease outcomes were also reflected in interaction effects, suggesting that exposure pathways and health impacts may differ by sex. Interview narratives from women describing chronic symptoms, caregiving responsibilities, and limited opportunities to relocate support calls within environmental health research to more explicitly examine gendered dimensions of exposure, vulnerability, and care work.

Risk communication, stress, and non-physical pathways of harm

Beyond physical exposure, qualitative findings highlight psychosocial pathways through which environmental conditions affect health. Participants emphasized inadequate risk communication, limited access to real-time air quality information, and distrust in institutional responses during industrial incidents. These conditions contribute to chronic stress, anxiety, and uncertainty—factors increasingly recognized as contributors to respiratory morbidity. Importantly, interaction findings indicate that stress-related pathways are associated with differential exposure–health patterns among socially vulnerable groups, reinforcing environmental justice perspectives that health inequities reflect not only unequal exposure but also unequal access to information, participation, and institutional accountability [17, 23].

Community knowledge, agency, and the value of CBPR

Despite systemic challenges, residents demonstrated substantial environmental knowledge and collective agency. Participants described documenting pollution events, mobilizing social networks, and advocating for monitoring and accountability. These practices align with principles of community resilience but also underscore that resilience often emerges in response to institutional failure rather than equitable governance [28]. The CBPR-oriented design strengthened the study by ensuring that interaction findings and statistical patterns were interpreted in light of lived experience. Integrating qualitative insights with quantitative interaction models illustrates how CBPR can bridge scientific evidence and community knowledge, producing findings that are both analytically rigorous and socially actionable.

Implications for policy and public health

From an atmospheric science perspective, the interaction results underscore the limitations of relying solely on average exposure estimates or main-effect models. Health risks are shaped by who is exposed, under what social conditions, and over what duration. Incorporating effect modification and community-derived insights is therefore essential for accurately characterizing exposure–health relationships in industrialized regions.

Policy implications include the need for stronger land-use regulation, cumulative impact assessment, improved risk communication, and community-engaged monitoring. Public health interventions must move beyond individual behavior change to address upstream structural determinants—including housing quality, healthcare access, occupational exposure, and environmental governance—to meaningfully reduce respiratory health inequities in Gulf Coast industrial communities.

Limitations and future directions

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, the cross-sectional design limits causal inference, and both environmental exposure measures and respiratory health outcomes were self-reported. Health outcomes were based on self-reported physician diagnoses and may be influenced by recall, health literacy, or access to care. Although models adjusted for key confounders including smoking, age, and household characteristics, residual confounding by unmeasured factors (e.g., occupational exposures or healthcare access) cannot be ruled out. In environmental justice communities, structural barriers such as limited healthcare access and delayed diagnosis may contribute to underreporting respiratory disease; however, such misclassification would likely be non-differential with respect to environmental exposure and would bias associations toward the null.

Second, the study sample is not intended to be representative of the general population. Recruitment relied on online surveys disseminated through social media, community researchers, and partner organization networks, consistent with CBPR approach. These strategies may introduce selection bias, as individuals with greater concern about environmental conditions, poorer health, or existing respiratory disease may have been more likely to participate. The relatively high proportion of male participants likely reflects local demographic and occupational patterns in industrial Gulf Coast communities, where petrochemical and industrial employment—often male-dominated—remains prevalent. Similarly, elevated smoking and asthma prevalence may reflect both true underlying community burden and self-selection into the study. Accordingly, these prevalence estimates should be interpreted descriptively rather than as population-representative, while the primary analytic focus remains on within-sample associations between environmental exposure and respiratory health outcomes.

Third, exposure was assessed using self-reported residential proximity to pollution sources rather than objective distance-based or modeled exposure measures. While this approach captures residents’ lived experience of environmental exposure, it does not account for spatial and temporal variability in pollutant dispersion driven by meteorology, emission intensity, or duration of exposure. Additionally, ZIP codes were used as the primary spatial unit of analysis, which may introduce spatial aggregation bias and obscure finer-scale exposure gradients– the modifiable areal unit problem (MAUP) [45].

Finally, the analysis focused primarily on residential exposure pathways. Occupational exposures—particularly relevant in Gulf Coast communities with substantial industrial employment—were not explicitly captured and may contribute to cumulative respiratory risk. Future research should incorporate longitudinal designs, finer-scale geocoded exposure data, occupational histories, and clinical validation of health outcomes to more fully characterize cumulative environmental health risks in industrialized fenceline communities.

Conclusions

This mixed-methods study provides robust evidence that respiratory health disparities in industrialized Gulf Coast communities are shaped by the intersection of environmental exposures, social determinants of health, and cumulative residential vulnerability. Using population-level survey data complemented by in-depth qualitative interviews, the study demonstrates that residential proximity to pesticide-related industries, waste management facilities, and industrial factories is consistently associated with higher odds of asthma, COPD, and other lung diseases, with risks varying across age, gender, smoking status, and residential histories.

The integration of quantitative associations with residents’ lived experiences highlights that environmental exposure in these communities is not episodic or isolated, but chronic and cumulative. Qualitative narratives contextualize statistical patterns by illustrating how persistent industrial emissions, limited residential mobility, inadequate risk communication, and barriers to healthcare shape everyday respiratory health risks. Together, these findings underscore that environmental health inequities are produced not only through unequal exposure, but also through unequal vulnerability and structural conditions.

From a public health and policy perspective, the results emphasize the need for place-based and community-engaged interventions that move beyond single-pollutant or facility-specific approaches. Strengthening land-use regulation, improving cumulative impact assessment, enhancing risk communication, and expanding access to healthcare are critical steps toward reducing respiratory health burdens in fenceline communities. Importantly, the study demonstrates the value of combining epidemiologic analysis with community knowledge through CBPR-informed mixed-methods research, offering an approach that is both scientifically rigorous and socially responsive.

Addressing respiratory health inequities in industrial Gulf Coast communities will require sustained, coordinated efforts that integrate environmental governance, public health practice, and community participation. By foregrounding cumulative exposure and lived experience, this study contributes evidence to inform more equitable environmental and health policy decisions in industrialized regions.
