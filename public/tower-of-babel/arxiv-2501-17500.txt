A Kernelized Operator Approach to Nonlinear Data-Enabled Predictive Control
Thomas de Jong, Siep Weiland, Mircea Lazar

Abstract
This paper considers the design of nonlinear data-enabled predictive control (DeePC) using kernel functions. Compared with existing methods that use kernels to parameterize multi-step predictors for nonlinear DeePC, we adopt a novel, operator-based approach. More specifically, we employ a universal product kernel parameterization of nonlinear systems operators as a prediction mechanism for nonlinear DeePC. We show that by using a product reproducing kernel Hilbert space (RKHS) to learn the system trajectories, big data sets can be handled effectively to construct the corresponding product Gram matrix. Moreover, we show that the structure of the adopted product RKHS representation allows for a computationally efficient DeePC formulation. Compared to existing methods, our approach achieves substantially faster computation times for the same data size. This allows for the use of much larger data sets and enhanced control performance.



I
INTRODUCTION

Willems’ fundamental lemma underpins data-driven control by enabling the prediction of system behavior directly from data, without requiring an explicit model
[
1
]
. This concept forms the foundation of data-enabled predictive control (DeePC), which uses historical data for predicting future trajectories
[
2
]
. For linear time-invariant (LTI) systems and noise-free data, equivalences between Model Predictive Control (MPC), Subspace Predictive Control (SPC) and DeePC have been established
[
3
]
. Extending DeePC to nonlinear systems has been an area of active research. For example, in
[
4
]
, DeePC was generalized to nonlinear systems using general basis functions, while in
[
5
]
, the Koopman framework was employed to parameterize the DeePC predictor. The Koopman approach provides a way to represent nonlinear dynamics by mapping system states to a higher-dimensional space by means of observable functions, where a linear operator governs their evolution. Identifying suitable basis functions or Koopman observable functions can be done using for example neural networks as was done in
[
6
]
to construct a basis, or as done in
[
5
,
7
]
for learning Koopman observable functions.

Alternatively, function estimation using regularized kernel methods offers a tractable solution for learning dynamical systems due to the well-posedness of function classes in RKHSs
[
8
,
9
]
. This has led to increased interest in kernel-based approaches for data-driven control
[
10
,
11
,
12
]
. Several kernel-based approaches have also emerged in the field of data-driven predictive control. In
[
13
]
a multi-step kernel-based predictor was identified and used within an MPC framework; therein, tight bounds on the kernelized multi-step predictors’ approximation error were derived. More recently,
[
14
]
presented a kernelized DeePC formulation for nonlinear systems, which corresponds to solving a system of equations similar to Willem’s fundamental lemma. Therein, methods for robustification and efficient online implementation based on kernels that are linear in future control inputs were presented. In
[
4
]
it was shown that the special structure of basis or kernel functions that are linear in the future control inputs corresponds in fact to a DeePC formulation of linear-in-control input Koopman MPC
[
15
]
. The above kernelized predictors for data-driven predictive control require in general usage of kernel functions defined on the stacked space of initial conditions and future control input sequences. Generating long persistently exciting input sequences that sample effectively the corresponding stacked space can be challenging for nonlinear systems, however. Persistency of excitation conditions that lead to exact kernelized representations for certain classes of nonlinear systems were recently presented in
[
16
]
, along with kernelized fundamental lemmas for linear, Hammerstein and flat nonlinear systems.

Alternatively, a discrete-time dynamical system can be regarded as an operator defined on a Hilbert space
[
17
]
that maps sequences of inputs to sequences of outputs. More recently,
[
18
]
defined the domain of the operator as a
product
Hilbert space. The corresponding operator maps input sequences and states to output sequences. Therein, it was proven that for positive definite kernels
[
19
]
the resulting product RKHS is dense in the space of nonlinear systems operators and that its unique minimizer is a universal approximator. The developed results in
[
18
]
yield an alternative formulation of Willems’ fundamental lemma and a new insight in persistency of excitation for nonlinear systems. Specifically,
[
18
]
points out that a that a persistently exciting input sequence should be paired with a linearly independent/distinct set of points in the space of initial conditions. This allows for generating larger data sets of trajectories by using a short, persistently exciting input sequence that is applied to several distinct initial conditions. Hence, it would be very interesting to employ the product RKHS operator parameterization for designing nonlinear DeePC algorithms.

To this end, in this paper, building on the framework from
[
18
]
, we develop a kernelized operator approach for data-driven control of nonlinear systems through the DeePC framework. By modeling discrete-time nonlinear systems as operators on a Hilbert space, our method generalizes beyond linear systems and incorporates specific classes of nonlinear systems not considered by previous methods that rely directly on the fundamental lemma such as
[
16
,
20
]
. A main challenge in extending the kernelized operator framework of
[
18
]
for data-driven control is the dependence of the predictor on the Gram matrix, whose dimension scales with both input and state data. We demonstrate that the product kernel structure allows separation of the input and state data, which reduces the dimension of the DeePC equality constraints significantly. Additionally, we introduce a systematic data-generation method, providing a structured approach for creating datasets required to learn the operator. Our product kernel formulation offers computational advantages over previous kernel-based DeePC approaches, such as
[
14
]
.

Preliminaries

We consider nonlinear MIMO systems in state space form with inputs
u\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
, states
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}

y\in\mathbb{R}^{p}

\displaystyle x_{k+1}

\displaystyle=f(x_{k},u_{k}),\quad k\in\mathbb{N},

(1)

\displaystyle y_{k}

\displaystyle=h(x_{k}).

The functions

. We denote by

For simplicity of exposition we assume that the state is measured, but the derived methods extend directly to a surrogate state consisting of stacked past inputs and outputs.

Definition II.1

A function
k:\mathcal{Z}\times\mathcal{Z}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, where
\mathcal{Z}
denotes the data space, is called a

if it satisfies the following properties: (
i
) it is symmetric, i.e.,
k(z_{1},z_{2})=k(z_{2},z_{1})
for all
(z_{1},z_{2})\in\mathcal{Z}\times\mathcal{Z}
; and (
ii
) it is positive semi-definite, i.e., for any

and any

the matrix

\displaystyle K:=\begin{pmatrix}k(z_{1},z_{1})&\dots&k(z_{1},z_{T})\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
k(z_{T},z_{1})&\dots&k(z_{T},z_{T})\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{T\times T},

(2)

is positive semi-definite. The matrix

is called the Gram matrix. Moreover, a kernel function
k
is called a
universal kernel
(or a positive definite kernel) if its corresponding Gram matrix is positive definite for any

with distinct points and any

.

Definition II.2

Given a kernel function
k:\mathcal{Z}\times\mathcal{Z}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}(k,\mathcal{Z})
is a reproducing kernel Hilbert space of functions from
\mathcal{Z}
to
\mathbb{R}
for
k
if (
i
) for every
z\in\mathcal{Z}
, the function
k(z,\cdot)\in\mathcal{H}(k,\mathcal{X})
and (
ii
) the reproducing property holds, i.e.,
f(z)=\langle f,k(z,\cdot)\rangle_{\mathcal{H}(k,\mathcal{Z})}
for every
f\in\mathcal{H}(k,\mathcal{Z})
and
z\in\mathcal{Z}
, where
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\mathcal{H}(k,\mathcal{Z})}
denotes the inner product associated with
\mathcal{H}(k,\mathcal{Z})
. For a positive definite kernel
k
we have that for all
T>0
it holds that
\text{span}\{k(\cdot,z_{1}),k(\cdot,z_{2}),\dots,k(\cdot,z_{T})\}\subseteq\mathcal{H}(k,\mathcal{Z})
.

In what follows we will make use of the following notation for the vector-kernel corresponding to the standard basis, i.e.,
\text{span}\{k(\cdot,z_{1}),k(\cdot,z_{2}),\dots,k(\cdot,z_{T})\}:

\mathbf{k}(z):=\operatorname{col}(k(z,z_{1}),k(z,z_{2}),\dots,k(z,z_{T}))\in\mathbb{R}^{T},\forall z\in\mathcal{Z}.

The entries of the Gram matrix or the kernel functions are related to a feature map as follows,

K_{ij}=k(z_{i},z_{j})=\langle\phi(z_{i}),\phi(z_{j})\rangle_{\mathcal{H}(k,\mathcal{Z})},

where

is a nonlinear kernel function and

is the feature map. It is noteworthy to mention that the above equation does not explicitly require the computation of the feature map
\phi(z_{j})
, but requires only the characterization of the dot product in the feature space defined using a kernel function
k
. Two important classes of universal kernels
[
19
]
, which are also radial basis functions that comply with the conditions of
[
21
]
are Gaussian kernels, i.e.,

k(z_{1},z_{1})=e^{-\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}\|z_{1}-z_{2}\|_{2}^{2}}

(3)

and Hardy reverse multiquadratics kernels, i.e.,

k(z_{1},z_{1})=\left(1+\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}\|z_{1}-z_{2}\|_{2}^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}

(4)

where
\sigma
is a positive real number.

To establish the problem setup for some finite
N\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq 1}
we define relevant Hilbert spaces of square summable sequences
\mathcal{U}:=l_{2}(\{0,1,\dots,N-1\},\mathbb{R}^{m})
,
\mathcal{Y}:=l_{2}(\{1,\dots,N\},\mathbb{R}^{p})
and
\mathcal{X}:=l_{2}(\{0\},\mathbb{R}^{n})
, where the notation was inspired by
[
22
]
. Next consider a set of input trajectories

with
\mathbf{u}_{i}\in\mathcal{U}
, a set of initial states

and a corresponding set of output trajectories

. To this end let

and

be kernel functions, respectively. Let
K_{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{T_{u}\times T_{u}}
,

denote the corresponding Gram matrices and for any

,
\mathbf{u}\in\mathcal{U}
and
\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{Y}
let
\mathbf{k}_{u}(\mathbf{u})
,

denote the corresponding vector-kernels, i.e.,

\displaystyle\mathbf{k}_{u}(\mathbf{u}):=\begin{bmatrix}k_{u}(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{u}_{1})\\

k_{u}(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{u}_{T_{u}})\end{bmatrix},\quad\mathbf{k}_{x}(x):=\begin{bmatrix}k_{x}(x,x_{1})\\

]

with corresponding Gram matrix and vector-kernel, i.e.,

III
Kernelized Operator Data-Enabled Predictive Control

In this section we present the new formulation of nonlinear DeePC using product kernel-based operators.

III-A

Kernelized Operator DeePC problem formulation

We would like to use the data sets
\mathbf{U}
,
\mathbf{Y}
and
\mathbf{X}_{0}
to learn the underlying operator
\mathbf{y}=G(\mathbf{u})(x)
that maps pairs of points in
\mathcal{U}
and
\mathcal{X}
into points in
\mathcal{Y}
, i.e.,

G(\mathbf{u})(x)=\Theta^{*}\mathbf{k}_{\otimes}(\mathbf{u},x),

where
\Theta^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{Np\times T_{x}T_{u}}
is unknown. The operator learning problem can be formulated as

\displaystyle\Theta^{*}=\arg\min_{\Theta}\|\mathbf{Y}-\Theta K_{\otimes}\|_{F}^{2},

(9)

where
\|M\|_{F}
denotes the Frobenius norm. Note that the minimizer to (
9
) is given by
\Theta^{*}=YK^{-1}_{\otimes}
if
K_{\otimes}
is invertible. Let
\mathbf{k}^{i}_{j}=\mathbf{k}_{\otimes}(\mathbf{u}_{j},x_{i})
then
K_{\otimes}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{k}^{1}_{1}&\dots&\mathbf{k}^{1}_{T_{u}}&\dots&\mathbf{k}^{T_{x}}_{1}&\dots&\mathbf{k}^{T_{x}}_{T_{u}}\end{bmatrix}
. Note that the initial conditions and input sequences used to construct the columns of
K_{\otimes}
are related to the output trajectories corresponding to the columns of
\mathbf{Y}
. To formulate the prototype kernelized operator based DeePC (KerODeePC) problem, at time
k\in\mathbb{N}
we define:

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}_{[0,N-1]}(k)

\displaystyle:=\operatorname{col}(u_{0|k},\dots,u_{N-1|k}),

\displaystyle\mathbf{y}_{[1,N]}(k)

\displaystyle:=\operatorname{col}(y_{1|k},\dots,y_{N|k}),

where for a variable
a
the notation
a_{i|k}
is the predicted value of
a_{k+i}
at time instant
k
.

Remark III.1

Due to the properties of the Kronecker product it holds that
K_{\otimes}^{-1}=K_{u}^{-1}\otimes K_{x}^{-1}
. This significantly reduces the computational complexity of inverting
K_{\otimes}
. I.e.
K_{\otimes}
is guaranteed to be invertible if and only if
K_{u}
and
K_{x}
are invertible.

Next, we present the KerODeePC formulation.

Problem III.2

(KerODeePC Problem)

\displaystyle\min_{\Xi}

\displaystyle\quad l_{N}(y_{N|k})+\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}l(y_{i|k},u_{i|k})+\lambda l_{g}(\mathbf{g}_{k}),

subject to constraints:

\displaystyle K_{\otimes}\mathbf{g}_{k}=\mathbf{k}_{\otimes}(\mathbf{u}_{[0,N-1]}(k),x_{k}),

(10a)

\displaystyle Y\mathbf{g}_{k}=\mathbf{y}_{[1,N]}(k),

(10b)

\displaystyle(\mathbf{y}_{[1,N]}(k),\mathbf{u}_{[0,N-1]}(k))\in\mathbb{Y}^{N}\times\mathbb{U}^{N},

(10c)

where
\Xi=\{\mathbf{u}_{[0,N-1]}(k),\mathbf{y}_{[1,N]}(k),\mathbf{g}_{k}\}
. For suitable references

l_{N}(y_{N|k}):=(y_{N|k}-r^{y}_{N|k})^{\top}P(y_{N|k}-r^{y}_{N|k})

l(y_{i|k},u_{i|k}):=(y_{i|k}-r^{y}_{i|k})^{\top}Q(y_{i|k}-r^{y}_{i|k})+(u_{i|k}-r^{u}_{i|k})^{\top}R(u_{i|k}-r^{u}_{i|k})

,
Q\succ 0
,

and

.

Note that the gram matrix
K_{\otimes}
and the data matrix

are constructed from the offline collected data matrices
\mathbf{U}
,
\mathbf{Y}
and
\mathbf{X}_{0}

IV
Computationally Efficient KerODeePC

The dimension of
K_{\otimes}\in\mathbb{R}^{T_{x}T_{u}\times T_{x}T_{u}}
becomes very large for large
T_{u}
and
T_{x}
. As a result, implementation of Problem
III.2
becomes challenging. To address this, next, we exploit the structure of the product kernel function
\mathbf{k}_{\otimes}(\mathbf{u},x)
to rewrite Problem
III.2
such that the implementation becomes much more efficient, as follows.

Lemma IV.1

Let
\mathbf{k}_{x}(x)\neq\mathbf{0}
for all
x\in\mathcal{X}
, and suppose
K_{x}
and
K_{u}
are full-rank matrices. Then, the system of equations (
7
) can be equivalently re-written as the reduced system of equations:

\displaystyle\Omega(x)\mathbf{g}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{k}_{u}(\mathbf{u}),

(11a)

\displaystyle\mathbf{Y}\mathbf{g}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{y},

(11b)

where
\Omega(x):=\left(I_{T_{u}}\otimes\frac{\mathbf{k}^{\top}_{x}(x)}{\|\mathbf{k}^{\top}_{x}(x)\|_{2}^{2}}\right)K_{\otimes}\in\mathbb{R}^{T_{u}\times T_{u}T_{x}}
.

Proof:

Due to the properties of the Kronecker product we can rewrite (
7a
) as follows:

\displaystyle K_{\otimes}\mathbf{g}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{k}_{\otimes}(\mathbf{u},x)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{k}_{u}(\mathbf{u})\otimes\mathbf{k}_{x}(x)

\displaystyle=\left(I_{T_{u}}\otimes\mathbf{k}_{x}(\mathbf{x})\right)\left(\mathbf{k}_{u}(\mathbf{u})\otimes I_{1}\right).

Here we used the property that for two matrices
A\in\mathbb{R}^{m_{1}\times n_{1}}
and
B\in\mathbb{R}^{m_{2}\times n_{2}}
it holds that
A\otimes B=(I_{m_{1}}\otimes B)(A\otimes I_{n_{2}})
. Since
I_{1}=1
this gives the following expression:

\displaystyle K_{\otimes}\mathbf{g}=\left(I_{T_{u}}\otimes\mathbf{k}_{x}(\mathbf{x})\right)\mathbf{k}_{u}(\mathbf{u}).

(12)

Moreover, for the Kronecker product it holds that
(A\otimes B)^{{\dagger}}=A^{{\dagger}}\otimes B^{{\dagger}}
and
{\displaystyle\operatorname{rank}(A\otimes B)=\operatorname{rank}A\,\operatorname{rank}B}
. Since
\operatorname{rank}I_{T_{u}}=T_{u}
it holds that
\left(I_{T_{u}}\otimes\mathbf{k}_{x}(x)\right)
is full column rank if
\mathbf{k}_{x}(x)\neq\mathbf{0}
. This implies that the pseudo-inverse exists and is given by:

\displaystyle\left(I_{T_{u}}\otimes\mathbf{k}_{x}(\mathbf{x})\right)^{{\dagger}}=\left(I_{T_{u}}\otimes\frac{\mathbf{k}^{\top}_{x}(x)}{\|\mathbf{k}_{x}(x)\|_{2}^{2}}\right).

Multiplying both sides of (
12
) from the left by this expression gives (
11a
), which completes the proof.
∎

By using the formulation in (
11
) as a prediction model for Problem
III.2
, the number of equality constraints reduces significantly from
T_{x}T_{u}
to
T_{u}
after substituting the initial condition
x
. However, the decision vector
\mathbf{g}
remains in
\mathbb{R}^{T_{x}T_{u}}
, which continues to pose implementation challenges. In
[
14
]
, an iterative approach was introduced, solving
\mathbf{g}
through a QP and
\mathbf{u}
through a nonlinear optimization. Here, we propose a one-shot solution to efficiently handle the full KerODeePC problem directly, based on an equivalent predictor representation inspired by
[
4
]
. To this end, we will use the following notion of model equivalence.

Definition IV.2

For the system (
1
) consider two models
\mathbf{y}^{M_{1}}=G_{M_{1}}(\mathbf{u},x)
and
\mathbf{y}^{M_{2}}=G_{M_{2}}(\mathbf{u},x)
. These models are called
equivalent
if for every
x\in\mathcal{X}
and every
\mathbf{u}\in\mathcal{U}
it holds that

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{y}^{M_{1}}-\mathbf{y}\|=\|\mathbf{y}^{M_{2}}-\mathbf{y}\|,

(13)

where
\mathbf{y}
is the true system (
1
) output obtained form initializing (
1
) at
x
and applying the sequences
\mathbf{u}
recursively.

Notice that one way to establish model equivalence is to show that
\mathbf{y}^{M_{1}}=\mathbf{y}^{M_{2}}
for all
x\in\mathcal{X}
and every
\mathbf{u}\in\mathcal{U}
.

Lemma IV.3

Consider the system of equations (
7
) and the reduced system of equations (
11
) defined using the same set of data

\{\mathbf{U},\mathbf{Y},\mathbf{X}_{0}\}
generated using system (
1
). Suppose
K_{\otimes}\succ 0
. Then, by Definition
IV.2
, the prediction models (
7
) and (
11
) are equivalent models for system (
1
) if and only if
\mathbf{Y}\hat{\mathbf{g}}=\mathbf{0}
for all
\hat{\mathbf{g}}\in\mathcal{N}\left(\Omega(x)\right)
, for all
x\in\mathcal{X}
, where
\mathcal{N}(\cdot)
denotes the null space.

Proof:

Let
\mathbf{y}^{\Omega}
denote the solution for
\mathbf{y}
in (
11
) generated by the data sets
\{\mathbf{U},\mathbf{Y},\mathbf{X}_{0}\}
. This solution is not unique, but given by the set

\displaystyle\mathbf{y}^{\Omega}\in\left\{\mathbf{Y}\left(\Omega^{{\dagger}}(x)\mathbf{k}_{u}(\mathbf{u})+\hat{\mathbf{g}}\right):\hat{\mathbf{g}}\in\mathcal{N}(\Omega(x))\right\}.

Above we used the fact that the set of all
\mathbf{g}
that satisfy (
11a
) can be explicitly characterized as

\mathbf{g}\in\{\Omega^{{\dagger}}(x)\mathbf{k}_{u}(\mathbf{u})+\hat{\mathbf{g}}:\hat{\mathbf{g}}\in\mathcal{N}(\Omega(x))\}.

Above,
\Omega^{{\dagger}}(x)
, which always exists if
K_{\otimes}
is full rank, is analytically given by

Ω^†(x) = K^-1_⊗ (I_T_u ⊗
k
_x(x_k)).

This is true because by assumption
K_{\otimes}\succ 0
, which implies that
K_{\otimes}^{-1}=K_{\otimes}^{{\dagger}}
. Let
\mathbf{y}^{\otimes}
denote the solution for
\mathbf{y}
in (
7
) generated using the same data sets, then for all
x\in\mathcal{X}
and for all
\mathbf{u}\in\mathcal{U}
the solution is
uniquely
given by:

\displaystyle\mathbf{y}^{\otimes}:=

\displaystyle\mathbf{Y}K^{-1}_{\otimes}\mathbf{k}_{u}(\mathbf{u})\otimes\mathbf{k}_{x}(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbf{Y}K^{-1}_{\otimes}(I_{T_{u}}\otimes\mathbf{k}_{x}(x))\mathbf{k}_{u}(\mathbf{u})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbf{Y}\Omega^{{\dagger}}(x)\mathbf{k}_{u}(\mathbf{u}).

From this it can be concluded that
\mathbf{y}^{\Omega}=\mathbf{y}^{\otimes}
if and only if
\mathbf{Y}\hat{\mathbf{g}}=\mathbf{0}
for all
\hat{\mathbf{g}}\in\mathcal{N}(\Omega(x))
.
∎

From Lemma
IV.3
it follows that the set of solutions for equations (
11a
) and (
11b
) are equivalent to the set of solutions for:

\displaystyle\Omega(x)\hat{\mathbf{g}}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{0},

(14a)

\displaystyle\mathbf{Y}\left(\Omega^{{\dagger}}(x)\mathbf{k}_{u}(\mathbf{u})+\hat{\mathbf{g}}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{y}.

(14b)

Finally, to reduce the dimension of the vector of variables
\hat{\mathbf{g}}\in\mathbb{R}^{T_{x}T_{u}}
, we adopt a projection on the output space, i.e., we aim to substitute
\hat{\mathbf{g}}
with
\tilde{\mathbf{g}}\in\mathbb{R}^{Np}
, which are related by
\hat{\mathbf{g}}=Y^{{\dagger}}\tilde{\mathbf{g}}
. This yields the following computationally efficient KerODeePC problem formulation.

Problem IV.4

(Efficient KerODeePC Problem)

\displaystyle\min_{\Xi}

\displaystyle\quad l_{N}(y_{N|k})+\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}l(y_{i|k},u_{i|k})+\lambda l_{g}(\tilde{\mathbf{g}}_{k}),

(15a)

subject to constraints:

\displaystyle\Omega(x_{k})\mathbf{Y}^{{\dagger}}\tilde{\mathbf{g}}_{k}=\mathbf{0},

(15b)

\displaystyle\mathbf{Y}\Omega^{{\dagger}}(x_{k})\mathbf{k}_{u}(\mathbf{u}_{[0,N-1]}(k)))+\tilde{\mathbf{g}}_{k}=\mathbf{y}_{[1,N]}(k),

(15c)

\displaystyle(\mathbf{y}_{[1,N]}(k),\mathbf{u}_{[0,N-1]}(k))\in\mathbb{Y}^{N}\times\mathbb{U}^{N},

(15d)

In Problem
IV.4
the free variables are now mapped into the predicted output space of dimension
pN
, which is
typically much smaller than
T_{x}T_{u}
. The price to pay is less free variables for optimizing the bias/variance trade–off and it is necessary that the output data matrix
\mathbf{Y}
has full row-rank. Let
\mathbf{y}^{\Omega_{e}}_{[1,N]}(k)
denote the solution to Problem
IV.4
and let
\mathbf{y}^{\otimes}_{[1,N]}(k)
denote the solutions to Problem
III.2
at time instant
k
. In the formulation of Problem
IV.4
, when
\lambda\rightarrow\infty
, we have that
\tilde{\mathbf{g}}_{k}\rightarrow 0
and thus
\hat{\mathbf{g}}_{k}\rightarrow 0
by Lemma
IV.1
this implies that
\mathbf{y}^{\Omega_{e}}_{[1,N]}(k)\rightarrow\mathbf{y}^{\otimes}_{[1,N]}(k)
.

VI
Conclusions

In this work, we presented a novel kernel based operator approach for nonlinear data-enabled predictive control using the product kernel operator learning framework introduced in
[
18
]
. The resulting KerODeePC formulation was rendered computationally efficient, particularly for large datasets, by exploiting the product kernel structure. Through numerical examples, we demonstrated that the required computation time per sampling instance was reduced by a factor 30 compared with existing methods when the exact same data set is used. Due to this computational advantage the proposed method allows for much larger data sets yielding superior performance.

Future work will deal with researching methods for reducing the product kernel dimension without loosing learning accuracy, e.g., by exploiting kernel principal component analysis or sparse feature selection.
