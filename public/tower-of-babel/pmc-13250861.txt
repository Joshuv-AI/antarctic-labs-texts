Microbiome dynamics and early microbial signatures predict bone regeneration in marsupialized jaw lesions: a longitudinal 16S sequencing cohort study
Authors: Bin Zeng, Mailikanmuhan Maitikuerban, Linling Chen, Di-kan Wang, Jingyuan Li, Le Yang, Xiao-mei Lao, Si-en Zhang, Gui-qing Liao, Yu-jie Liang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground Marsupialization is a conservative treatment for odontogenic jaw lesions but is hampered by protracted, unpredictable bone regeneration, lacking early biomarkers. Furthermore, whether these intraosseous lesions harbour a unique microbiome remains unknown. We aimed to characterize microbial dynamics during treatment and develop a prognostic model to address clinical unpredictability.MethodsA prospective longitudinal study was conducted with multi-site sampling (saliva, tissue, luminal fluid and swabs) across three phases: primary surgery, 3-month follow-up and secondary surgery. Bacterial 16S rRNA gene sequencing was subsequently performed. Microbial dynamics were analyzed using diversity analysis and source tracking. A prognostic model was constructed using a hybrid feature selection strategy to predict 12-month bone regeneration using 3-month post-operative swabs.ResultsBaseline analysis revealed that odontogenic jaw lesions harbour a unique, biologically selected microbiome enriched in Fusobacterium and Clostridia, which are distinct from saliva. Marsupialization induced a profound microbial remodelling, transforming this closed niche into an open, stochastic ecosystem dominated by salivary commensals. Long-term analysis indicated a secondary succession, where primary pathogens were replaced by a Bacteroidia-enriched community. Crucially, after a rigorous feature selection process, a streamlined microbial signature accurately predicted 12-month bone regeneration (AUC = 0.937). Poor bone regeneration was associated with the persistence of the primary Clostridia core, whereas favourable regeneration was linked to a shift towards Bacteroidia and Proteobacteria.ConclusionMarsupialization drives the lesion microbiome from a pathogenic niche toward a stochastic, commensal-like state. This succession trajectory is associated with clinical outcome. Our prognostic model offers a novel precision tool to optimize lesion management.Keywords: Odontogenic jaw lesions, marsupialization, 16S rDNA sequencing, microbiome, prognostic model, bone regeneration, longitudinal study

Introduction

Odontogenic jaw lesions, particularly aggressive entities such as odontogenic keratocyst and ameloblastoma, present a clinical and biological paradox [1]. Though they are always histopathologically classified as benign, their capacity for relentless, compressive or invasive growth poses a substantial threat to the structural integrity of the jawbones. This threat is amplified by their insidious, often asymptomatic, nature, which leads to significant diagnostic delays and extensive, sometimes irreversible, bone destruction. The management of these extensive lesions thus confronts clinicians with a significant dilemma. On the one hand, radical resection offers a definitive solution with lower recurrence rates but often at the devastating cost of critical anatomical structures, leading to severe functional and aesthetic morbidity. On the other hand, marsupialization stands as a conservative, function-preserving alternative, converting a major surgical challenge into a manageable secondary procedure [2–5]. However, the clinical utility of this conservative approach is significantly hampered by two principal drawbacks: a protracted recovery period, often spanning a year or more, and the profound uncertainty of the bone regeneration outcome [5]. This unpredictability creates a critical gap in clinical decision-making, as there are currently no reliable early biomarkers to forecast prognostic outcomes, stratify patients for risk, or personalize treatment strategies.

The microbiome, an indispensable part of the human body, has its role in health and disease increasingly well-defined [6]. In recent years, the presence of microorganisms has also been discovered in several unexpected tissue regions (such as the brain, blood and retina), where they also influence health [7–9]. In oncology, the research focus has expanded beyond a few well-established pathogenic microbes (e.g. H. pylori) to now encompass the complex microbial communities within the tumour microenvironment [10]. These communities do not always exert their effects through infection but rather by modulating local immunity, metabolism and inflammatory responses to promote or inhibit tumour biological behaviours [11–13]. The oral microbiome, as the second-largest microbial community in the human body, has been extensively documented for its significant role in conditions such as obesity, Alzheimer's disease, depression and various cancers [8,14]. In light of this, we are compelled to ask: are these enclosed odontogenic lesions within the jawbones a ‘sterile’ environment, as traditionally believed, or do they harbour a unique microbial community? To date, however, this question has not been investigated.

Marsupialization fundamentally alters the lesion's microenvironment – transforming it from a closed intraosseous space into an open cavity communicating with the oral cavity [2]. For clinicians and researchers, it is theoretically and intuitively obvious that this drastic environmental shift would markedly alter the microbial community. However, concrete evidence is lacking on the specific manner in which the microbial community changes, and whether this dynamic ecological succession can reflect or influence the process of bone recovery.

In this study, we hypothesized that odontogenic jaw lesions harbour a unique microbial community and that the post-marsupialization microbial succession is associated with the patient's bone regeneration prognosis. To validate this hypothesis, we designed and implemented a study combining multi-site sampling (saliva, tissue, swabs and luminal fluid) and longitudinal tracking (pre-operative, intra-treatment follow-up and secondary surgery). Through 16S rRNA gene sequencing, we aimed to systematically resolve the baseline microbial signatures within odontogenic jaw lesions and their dynamic changes throughout the treatment course. More importantly, we attempted to utilize post-marsupialization cavity swab collected at the 3-month follow-up to construct a model capable of predicting the bone regeneration outcome at 12 months post-operation. Our work not only unveils the microbial landscape within the microenvironment of odontogenic jaw lesions for further fundamental research but also delivers a predictive tool to help clinicians identify high-risk patients in advance and achieve individualized treatment decisions.

Materials and methods

Study design

We conducted this longitudinal study at the Hospital of Stomatology, Sun Yat-sen University in China. Eligible patients were clinically diagnosed with jaw lesions and subsequently received histological confirmation of odontogenic origin (including ameloblastoma and odontogenic keratocyst). All patients were treated with marsupialization. The study protocol and all amendments were approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee of the Hospital of Stomatology, Sun Yat-sen University (Grant Number: ERC-[2016]-41) in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all patients prior to enrollment for prospective specimen collection. The reporting of this study adheres to the Strengthening the Organization and Reporting of Microbiome Studies (STORMS) checklist (Checklist DOI: 10.5281/zenodo.18015567) [15].

Data collection

Clinicopathological data were systematically collected, including demographic information (age, gender), lifestyle factors (smoking status, alcohol consumption), medical history (systemic diseases), and lesion characteristics (site, presence of pain, paresthesia, radiographic features and pathological diagnosis). Inpatient test results, such as bacterial cultures and drug sensitivity tests, were also recorded. Specifically, panoramic radiographs were obtained to assess the initial extent of the lesion at baseline (pre-marsupialization) and to monitor bone regeneration and lesion shrinkage at 3-, 6- and 12-months post-marsupialization. Data preprocessing, including verification and conversion of categorical variables into dummy variables, was conducted prior to statistical analysis.

Radiographic assessment of bone healing and prognostic grouping

Bone regeneration was quantitatively evaluated using panoramic radiographs taken at baseline and post-operation follow-up. To ensure objectivity and reproducibility, a standardized, semi-automated analysis was performed using a custom R script.

Raters and blinding

The crucial step of manual delineation of regions of interest and execution of the analysis script was independently performed by three clinicians. To prevent bias, all three raters were blinded to the microbiome data and clinical outcomes throughout the entire assessment process. The final quantitative indices for each patient were calculated as the average of the three raters' measurements.

Image processing and index calculation

The analysis workflow first involved image standardization. Geometric scaling and positional alignment were conducted using stable anatomical landmarks to correct for differences in magnification and positioning between radiographs. Subsequently, pixel intensity was normalized based on the darkest and brightest reference points in each image to account for exposure variations. After standardization, three indices were calculated: (1) Area Recovery Rate, the percentage of the original defect area filled with new bone; (2) Density Increase, the change in mean normalized pixel intensity; and (3) a Texture Index.

Our rationale for the Texture Index was to capture the development of bone-like structural complexity. It was mathematically defined as the change in the standard deviation (SD) of normalized pixel intensity values within the regions of interest. A higher SD reflects greater pixel heterogeneity, which is characteristic of mature bone with trabeculae and marrow spaces. As a supplementary validation, we also performed a Grey-Level Co-occurrence Matrix (GLCM) analysis, deriving a ‘GLCM Texture Score’ from Contrast and Entropy parameters.

Reliability, validation and grouping

The reliability of our primary assessment method was rigorously validated. The inter-rater reliability among the three clinicians for the final composite Z-score was outstanding, as determined by the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC (A,1) = 0.971; 95% CI: 0.936–0.989). Furthermore, to ensure clinical relevance, we performed an external validation. A senior surgeon, blinded to all other data, independently rated the regeneration outcomes on a 5-point scale. Our quantitative Z-scores showed a strong and highly significant correlation with the expert's clinical ratings (Spearman's ρ = 0.866, P < 0.0001).

Finally, a composite Z-score, derived from the primary metrics, was used to stratify patients into ‘Good’ and ‘Poor’ recovery groups for subsequent prognostic modelling.

Specimen collections

A multi-phase sampling strategy was implemented to track the microbial succession:

Baseline samples (Phase I): Unstimulated whole saliva and intralesional specimens were collected prior to or during the primary marsupialization surgery. Patients were instructed to rinse their mouth with sterile saline and rest for 30 min before the collection of 5 mL of saliva. During the operation, intralesional luminal fluid was aspirated via needle biopsy or irrigated with sterile saline in cases with insufficient fluid volume. Subsequently, a preoperative lesional swab was obtained by carefully scraping the inner surface of the remaining tissue surface. Finally, the primary lesion tissue was resected and collected. In total, 26 patients were enrolled at baseline, mostly yielding paired preoperative saliva, tissue, luminal fluid and lesional swab samples for sequencing.

Follow-up samples (Phase II): Post-marsupialization cavity swabs were collected at the first follow-up visit (approximately 3 months post-operation). The samples were carefully obtained from the inner luminal surface of the marsupialized bone cavity through the fenestration window, with strict care taken to avoid contact with the surrounding oral mucosa or saliva to ensure site specificity. Specifically, the sampling site was isolated with sterile gauze, and a saliva ejector was used to maintain a dry field. Under clear and direct vision, using new sterile instruments, a sterile swab was carefully introduced through the fenestration window, avoiding contact with the surrounding oral mucosa, to firmly scrape the internal luminal surface. A total of 16 swab samples were successfully retrieved and sequenced at this time point.

Secondary surgery samples (Phase III): For patients undergoing secondary enucleation (typically 12 months post-operation), paired saliva and residual tissue surface were collected using the same protocol as in Phase I. A total of 6 residual tissue samples were successfully retrieved and sequenced at this time point.

To prevent contamination, new sterile instruments were used for each sampling step. All specimens were histologically confirmed by a pathologist and immediately stored at −80 °C until further analysis [16].

DNA extraction and 16S rRNA gene sequencing

Total bacterial genomic DNA was extracted from all specimens using the FastDNA SPIN Kit (MP Biomedicals, USA) according to the manufacturer's instructions. The V3–V4 hypervariable regions of the bacterial 16S rRNA gene were amplified using the primer pair 338F (5'-ACTCCTACGGGAGGCAGCAG-3') and 806R (5’-GGACTACHVGGGTWTCTAAT-3’). The PCR products underwent quality control via gel electrophoresis, and the qualified amplicons were sequenced on the Illumina platform at The Beijing Genomics Institute (BGI, China).

Sequence data processing and taxonomy assignment

The raw paired-end reads were merged using vsearch (v2.40). To ensure high-quality assembly, merged sequences were subjected to stringent quality filtering with a maximum expected error rate of 0.01. Unique sequences with low abundance (<10 reads) were discarded to minimize sequencing noise. Subsequently, the UNOISE3 algorithm was employed to generate Amplicon Sequence Variants (ASVs) at single-nucleotide resolution. Chimeric sequences were detected and removed by cross-referencing with the SILVA database (v132).

Taxonomic classification was assigned to representative ASVs using the SINTAX algorithm against the SILVA (v132) and RDP (v18) databases. Non-bacterial sequences (e.g. chloroplasts, mitochondria) were filtered out. For diversity analyses, the ASV table was rarefied to the minimum library size to account for sequencing depth differences. In contrast, for differential abundance analyses (e.g. LEfSe, MaAsLin2), the non-rarefied (raw count) table was utilized with method-specific normalization [17,18].

Microbial diversity and functional profiling

Diversity and composition

Alpha diversity (Shannon, Richness and Chao1 indices) was calculated based on the rarefied ASV table, with group differences assessed via the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. Beta diversity was evaluated using Bray-Curtis dissimilarity, as well as weighted and unweighted UniFrac distances, and visualized via Principal Coordinates Analysis (PCoA). Differences in overall community structure were tested using Permutational Multivariate Analysis of Variance (PERMANOVA). To characterize the taxonomic composition, the relative abundance of microbial taxa was calculated at different taxonomic levels and visualized using stacked bar charts.

Co-occurrence network analysis

To decipher complex microbial interactions, networks were constructed based on the Phase I tissue samples. We employed the SparCC algorithm to construct the correlation matrix, addressing the compositionality bias of microbiome data [19]. Valid edges were defined as strong correlations with a magnitude > 0.6 and a statistically significant P-value < 0.05. The network topology was visualized using R with a self-script [20]. Uniquely, network nodes were annotated based on their differential abundance status in the Phase III tissue to visualize the evolutionary fate of pathogenic hubs.

Functional prediction and assembly

Functional potential was inferred using PICRUSt2 to predict KEGG pathway abundances, FAPROTAX to annotate biogeochemical cycling functions, and BugBase to predict organism-level phenotypes (e.g. biofilm formation and oxidative stress tolerance) [21–23]. To decipher the ecological dynamics specifically following marsupialization (Phase II), community assembly mechanisms were dissected using the FEAST (Fast Expectation-Maximization for Microbial Source Tracking) algorithm [24]. Preoperative saliva and preoperative lesional swab samples were designated as the ‘source’ and post-marsupialization cavity swabs as the ‘sink’ to quantify the proportional contribution of saliva to the marsupialized open cavity. Additionally, the Beta Nearest Taxon Index (beta-NTI) was calculated for these longitudinal swab samples to distinguish whether the post-operative community assembly was governed by deterministic selection processes or stochastic drift [25].

Statistical analysis and biomarker discovery

All statistical analyses were performed using R (v4.5.0). Statistical significance was defined as a two-tailed P-value < 0.05 or a False Discovery Rate (FDR)-corrected q-value < 0.05. For continuous variables, group differences were assessed using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test or the Kruskal‒Wallis test. To identify robust microbial biomarkers associated with clinical features, we employed a complementary multivariate strategy. LEfSe (Linear Discriminant Analysis Effect Size) was utilized to discover biomarkers with high effect sizes that differentiate between groups. Furthermore, to perform rigorous association analysis while controlling for potential clinical confounders, MaAsLin2 (Microbiome Multivariable Associations with Linear Models) was applied to identify microbial features significantly associated with sample metadata [17,18].

Prognostic model construction

To identify core microbial markers associated with bone regeneration and predict outcomes, we constructed a ‘hybrid feature matrix’ by integrating microbial features (spanning ASV to family levels) identified as significant in the aforementioned differential analyses. We employed Partial Least Squares Discriminant Analysis (PLS-DA) coupled with Leave-One-Out Cross-Validation (LOOCV) to address the challenges of high dimensionality and multicollinearity in the dataset.

A hierarchical feature selection strategy was implemented to ensure model robustness. First, univariate analysis (Wilcoxon rank-sum test) was performed to preliminarily screen features associated with prognostic outcomes under varying significance thresholds to generate candidate subsets. Second, these filtered subsets were subjected to multivariate PLS-DA modelling for dimensionality reduction and classification. Model performance was evaluated iteratively via LOOCV, and the predictive accuracy was quantified using the Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic Curve (AUC).

To rigorously assess model stability and significance, especially given the small sample size, two additional validation procedures were performed. First, a non-parametric bootstrapping analysis (2,000 replicates) was conducted on both the full 62-feature model and the optimized 7-feature model. This procedure involved repeated sampling with replacement from the original dataset to generate 95% confidence intervals for the AUC, providing a robust estimate of model stability. Second, a permutation test (2,000 iterations) was employed to evaluate whether the model's performance was statistically significant or due to chance. In this test, the prognostic outcome labels (‘Good’ vs. ‘Poor’ were randomly shuffled, and the entire modelling pipeline – including the univariate feature selection step – was reapplied to each permuted dataset. This generated a null distribution of AUCs, against which the AUC of the original model was compared to calculate an empirical P-value.

Results

The unique microbial landscape of odontogenic jaw lesions

To characterize the baseline microbial landscape of odontogenic jaw lesions, we enrolled 26 patients and performed 16S rRNA gene sequencing on multi-site specimens, including saliva, jaw tumour/cyst tissue, luminal fluid and lesion surface swabs, collected during the primary surgery (Figure 1A, Supplementary Table 1). First, we assessed the microbial diversity. In alpha diversity analyses, a significant reduction in microbial richness (Chao1 and Richness indices) was observed in both tissue and luminal fluid samples relative to saliva (Figures 1B, S1A). In contrast, the Shannon index, which accounts for both richness and evenness, was significantly elevated in the tissue samples (Figure 1C). Rarefaction curves confirmed that the sequencing depth was sufficient to capture the microbial diversity across all sample types (Figure 1D). Next, we evaluated the overall community structure using constrained principal coordinates analysis (cPCoA) based on Bray-Curtis dissimilarity. This analysis demonstrated a clear and significant separation (P = 0.001) between saliva and the three intralesional samples (tissue, luminal fluid and swab), which themselves showed considerable overlap (Figures 1E, S1B). Taxonomic profiling further elucidated the unique composition of the intralesional microbiome. At the phylum level, while all the groups were dominated by Bacteroidetes, Firmicutes and Proteobacteria, the tissue samples were uniquely enriched in Fusobacteria, and the luminal fluid samples showed a higher abundance of Actinobacteria (Figure 1F). At the genus level, a distinct compositional shift was observed: tissue samples were characterized by a significant enrichment of pathobiont genera such as Fusobacterium and Porphyromonas, coupled with a depletion of the commensal genus Streptococcus. In contrast, saliva was dominated by genera such as Haemophilus, Prevotella and Veillonella (Figure 1G). Taken together, this paradoxical diversity pattern (reduced richness but increased evenness), combined with the clear community segregation from saliva and the specific enrichment of pathobiont genera such as Fusobacterium, strongly suggests that the tissue microenvironment acts as a selective ecological niche, actively shaping a distinct microbial community rather than being a passive recipient of oral bacteria.

To identify robust microbial biomarkers distinguishing these sample types, we employed both LEfSe and MaAsLin2 analyses. LEfSe identified a suite of taxa specifically enriched in each microenvironment. For instance, the Haemophilus and Neisseria lineages were hallmarks of saliva, whereas the tissue samples were significantly characterized by taxa from the class Clostridia, including the genus Parvimonas and members of Family XI and Family XIII (Figures 1H, S1C). Complementarily, the MaAsLin2 multivariable analysis comparing tissue versus saliva confirmed the significant depletion of Streptococcus in tissue, while highlighting the enrichment of a broad array of strictly anaerobic bacteria, such as Sphaerochaeta, Odoribacter and Pseudoramibacter (Figure 1I).

Finally, we inferred the functional potential of these distinct microbial communities. Principal coordinates analysis based on KEGG pathways using PICRUSt2 revealed a significant and pronounced structural difference across the four sample types (R² = 0.201, P = 0.001, Figure 1J, 1K). Specifically, saliva and tissue samples formed two distinct and tightly clustered groups, indicating that they represent stable and specific functional niches with low intra-group variation. In contrast, luminal fluid and swab samples exhibited significantly higher dispersion, encompassing the variability of both saliva and tissue clusters (Figure 1K). A detailed comparison showed that the tissue microbiome was significantly enriched in pathways related to bacterial motility (flagellar assembly, bacterial chemotaxis and bacterial motility proteins), and xenobiotic degradation (e.g. chlorocyclohexane, atrazine and benzoate degradation). Conversely, pathways for glycan degradation were more abundant in the saliva microbiome (Figures 1J, S1D).

In summary, these results collectively demonstrate that the intraosseous lesion fosters a unique microbial community that is fundamentally distinct from the oral cavity. This tissue-specific niche is characterized by a paradoxical diversity pattern (reduced richness but increased evenness), a taxonomic shift towards pathobionts (e.g. Fusobacterium), and a functional adaptation favouring bacterial motility and xenobiotic metabolism. Having established that the intralesional microbiome is not a random assembly but rather a selectively shaped community, we next sought to investigate how this closed ecosystem responds to the drastic environmental perturbation induced by marsupialization.

Marsupialization drives a stochastic, saliva-like shift in the cavity swab microbiome

To elucidate the impact of marsupialization – which transforms the closed intraosseous niche into an open marsupialized cavity – on microbial succession, we compared the microbiome of post-marsupialization cavity swabs, collected at ~3-months follow-up (ASswab, n = 16, Supplementary Table 2), with preoperative saliva (F1saliva) and lesional swabs (F1swab).

First, we examined the shifts in microbial diversity and structure. Alpha diversity indices showed no significant differences among the three groups, suggesting that the surgical intervention maintained a comparable level of diversity (Figures 2A, S2A, S2B). However, beta diversity analysis based on Constrained Principal Coordinate Analysis (CPCoA) revealed a significant shift in community structure (P = 0.001). The post-operative swabs formed a distinct cluster that was clearly separated from both the preoperative lesional and saliva baselines, indicating the establishment of a novel, transitional microbial state (Figure 2B). Taxonomic profiling unveiled the specific pattern of this succession. At the genus level, the post-operative swab microbial community was marked by a marked increase in the relative abundance of typical oral commensals, particularly Streptococcus and Neisseria, which are facultative anaerobes or aerobes. This shift marked a departure from the baseline lesional profile, where these taxa were less dominant, and also differed from the baseline saliva, which maintained higher proportions of Haemophilus, Veillonella and Prevotella (Figure 2C). The reduction in the proportion of ‘Other’ (less abundant) genera in the post-operative swabs further suggests that these common oral colonizers effectively outcompeted the lesion-specific taxa found at baseline. At the phylum level, while the major phyla remained stable, subtle shifts, such as variations in Firmicutes and Fusobacteria abundance, were observed (Figure S2C).

To quantify the origin of this post-operative cavity community, we employed FEAST source tracking analysis. The results demonstrated that a dominant fraction of the post-operative swab microbiome was traceable to salivary input, which is consistent with an influx of oral bacteria into the exposed bone cavity (Figure 2D). We further investigated whether the source composition was correlated with bone regeneration outcomes. However, stratifying patients into ‘Good’ and ‘Poor’ recovery groups revealed no distinct differences in the source contributions (Figure S2D). Furthermore, correlation analysis between source proportions and specific radiographic healing indices (area, density and texture) indicated only weak associations (e.g. the proportion of preoperative swab source vs. texture contrast, r = 0.43). These data suggest that the source composition alone is likely not the primary determinant of healing prognosis (Figure S2E).

Finally, to decipher the ecological mechanisms governing this community assembly, we applied a null model analysis based on the Beta Nearest Taxon Index (beta-NTI). Notably, the majority of post-operative swabs (91.7%) exhibited beta-NTI values within the range of stochastic processes (|beta-NTI| < 2) (Figure 2F). This indicates that the community assembly in the post-operative marsupialized cavity is predominantly driven by stochastic dispersal, rather than deterministic environmental selection observed in the closed and pathological microenvironment.

In summary, these findings demonstrate that marsupialization induces a profound ecological succession. The opening of the bone cavity facilitates a stochastic influx of salivary bacteria, reshaping the community from a niche-specific microbiome into an oral-like assemblage dominated by commensals such as Streptococcus. This transition from a deterministic state to a stochastic, dispersal-driven state raises the question of whether specific microbial interactions within this newly formed community, rather than their origin, influence the subsequent bone regeneration process.

Community restructuring and functional attenuation in the residual tissue microbiome

To investigate whether the prolonged exposure to the oral environment and the subsequent bone remodelling process fundamentally alter the tissue-resident microbiome, we compared the residual tissue samples collected during secondary surgery (F2tissue, n = 6, ~12 months post-operation) with the baseline primary tissue (F1tissue) and saliva.

First, we assessed the long-term changes in microbial diversity and community structure. Alpha diversity analysis revealed that the residual tissue possessed the highest Shannon index, significantly surpassing both primary lesion tissue and saliva, despite maintaining a comparable richness (Chao1) comparable to the baseline (Figure 3A, 3B, 3C). This increase in community evenness indicates a more complex and balanced ecosystem with reduced dominance of specific taxa in the post-marsupialization tissue. In terms of beta diversity, PCoA showed that the residual tissue did not fully revert to a saliva-like state. Instead, it retained a distribution that largely overlapped with the primary lesion tissue, while remaining distinct from the saliva cluster (Figure 3D). Furthermore, taxonomic profiling at the genus level captured the initial signs of this restructuring, most notably the reduction of Fusobacterium abundance in the residual tissue (Figures 3E, S3B). Collectively, these diversity metrics indicate that while the tissue niche remains distinct from the oral cavity, the internal community structure has undergone a fundamental reorganization.

Next, to pinpoint the specific taxa driving this restructuring, we employed LEfSe and MaAsLin2 analyses. These analyses confirmed that the residual tissue is not merely a subset of the baseline but also harbours a unique microbial signature. While the primary lesion tissue was enriched in taxa such as Clostridia and Burkholderiales, the residual tissue was characterized by a significant succession towards a distinct suite of strict anaerobes and ‘late colonizers.’ Specifically, taxa including Filifactor, Parvimonas, Odoribacter, Dialister and Treponema were identified as the specific biomarkers of the residual tissue (Figures 3F, 3G, S3C). This confirms that the ecological succession involves a robust taxonomic shift, replacing the primary pathobionts with a secondary anaerobic consortium, providing a distinct feature set for subsequent modelling.

We then inferred the functional consequences of this taxonomic shift using FAPROTAX and PICRUSt2. FAPROTAX analysis showed a functional transition from specific biodegradation pathways (e.g. aromatic compound degradation) in the primary lesion tissue to generic fermentation pathways in the residual tissue (Figures 3H, S3D). More importantly, PICRUSt2 revealed a broad downregulation of multiple disease-associated and host-interaction pathways in the residual tissue (Figure 3I). For instance, gene sets annotated to p53 signalling, colorectal cancer, and viral myocarditis – which often reflect microbial capabilities to modulate host cell cycle and immune responses – were significantly depleted compared to the baseline. This suggests a transition from a hyperactive, pathogenic state to a functionally quiescent profile, reflecting an attenuated potential to interfere with host tissue homoeostasis in the healed environment.

Finally, to visualize the ecological stability and interaction patterns of this remodelled community, we performed a co-occurrence network analysis based on the primary tissue microbiome and mapped the evolutionary fate of each node in the residual tissue. The network topology revealed a robust core structure orchestrated by prominent hubs such as Fusobacterium, Streptococcus, and Veillonella (grey nodes). Strikingly, despite the surgical intervention and long-term healing, these central keystone taxa remained stable, maintaining their dominance and connectivity in both the primary lesion and residual tissue. However, dynamic shifts were observed in specific sub-modules of the network. A distinct cluster of nodes, centred around Saccharibacteria (ASV_22) (red node), was significantly depleted in the residual tissue, indicating the collapse of this specific baseline-associated module. In contrast, another module comprising Odoribacter, Prevotella, and Oribacterium (blue nodes) significantly expanded, marking the emergence of a secondary anaerobic guild in the healed tissue. This suggests that the ‘healed’ tissue microbiome retains a stable pathogenic core, while the ecological succession is primarily driven by the turnover of peripheral modules.

In summary, these longitudinal comparisons demonstrate that marsupialization induces a complex ecological restructuring rather than a simple reversion to health. While the tissue niche retains a stable core of pathobionts (e.g. Fusobacterium), it undergoes a significant peripheral succession, characterized by the depletion of primary indicators (e.g. Saccharibacteria) and the enrichment of secondary anaerobes (e.g. Odoribacter), accompanied by a functional attenuation of disease-associated pathways. Crucially, the identification of these dynamically shifting taxa – which differentiate the distinct stages of tissue healing (baseline vs. residual vs. saliva) – provides a rational pool of candidate biomarkers.

Early post-operative microbial signatures predict long-term bone regeneration outcomes

To translate our ecological findings into clinical utility, we aimed to construct a predictive model capable of forecasting long-term bone regeneration outcomes using microbial data collected from the early post-operative window (3-month cavity swabs).

First, we established a robust candidate feature pool by integrating differential taxa identified in our longitudinal analyses. Specifically, we targeted the core pathogenic signatures of the primary lesion. To this end, we extracted ASVs that were significantly enriched in the primary lesion tissue from two distinct comparisons: (1) vs. saliva (representing tissue-specific pathogens) and (2) vs. residual tissue (representing persistent pathobionts). These ASV-level markers, identified by MaAsLin2, were then synthesized with broader taxonomic features identified by LEfSe. To minimize redundancy, we filtered the LEfSe results to retain only the most specific taxonomic levels and merged them with the MaAsLin2 ASVs, excluding overlapping ASV features. This yielded a final panel of 62 non-redundant microbial features (30 taxa and 32 ASVs), all representative of the primary lesion's microbial footprint (Supplementary Table 3).

Next, we evaluated the prognostic performance of these features in stratifying patients based on 12-month bone regeneration rates. We compared models constructed with different feature subsets filtered by significance thresholds. ROC analysis revealed that while the full 62-feature model achieved an AUC of 0.778, the optimized 7-feature model (filtered by p < 0.2) demonstrated superior predictive power with an outstanding AUC of 0.937 (Figure 4A).

We then validated the discriminatory power of these models by calculating the predicted risk scores. The 62-feature model showed a visible separation trend between ‘Good’ and ‘Poor’ recovery groups but failed to reach statistical significance (P = 0.072), likely due to noise from non-informative features (Figure 4B). In contrast, the optimized 7-feature model yielded a highly significant separation (P = 0.0043), effectively distinguishing patients at risk of poor bone regeneration (Figure 4C). This classification potential was visually corroborated by the PLS-DA score plot based on the 62-feature set, which displayed a recognizable clustering pattern distinguishing the two outcome groups (Figure 4D).

To rigorously assess the stability and statistical significance of these models, we performed bootstrapping and permutation tests. The bootstrapping analysis (2,000 replicates) confirmed the superior stability of the optimized 7-feature model, which yielded an excellent 95% confidence interval (CI) for its AUC of [0.81, 1.00]. In contrast, the full model was highly unstable, with a wide 95% CI of [0.48, 1.00], its lower bound falling below the line of chance (Figure 4E). Furthermore, permutation testing (2,000 iterations) validated that the performance of our optimized model was statistically significant (P = 0.0315), indicating a true biological signal. Conversely, the performance of the full model was not significant (P = 0.1055), suggesting that it was likely driven by noise (Figure 4F, 4G). These analyses confirmed that the 7-feature model was the most robust and valid choice for further investigation.

Finally, to biologically interpret the model, we mapped the taxonomic flow and enrichment patterns of the predictive features. The Sankey diagram (Figure 4H) and Lollipop chart (Figure 4I) revealed distinct microbial signatures driving the healing trajectories. The ‘Poor Recovery’ signature was ecologically defined by a persistent anaerobic consortium. Specifically, the class Clostridia (within Firmicutes) appeared to be the dominant driver, with 8 genera enriched in the poor outcome group. This unfavourable profile was further compounded by specific genera from Negativicutes, Spirochaetes, and Fusobacteriia. Notably, certain lineages within Proteobacteria (from Gamma-, Delta- and Beta-classes) also contributed to this signature. Conversely, the ‘Good Recovery’ signature was characterized by a distinct community structure. Unexpectedly, this group was enriched in Bacteroidia (7 genera) and Sphingobacteriia, suggesting a protective or commensal role for these Bacteroidetes lineages in the open cavity. Furthermore, the favourable profile was strongly driven by a diverse array of Proteobacteria, particularly Alphaproteobacteria and Betaproteobacteria, along with Actinobacteria.

In summary, our model indicates that delayed bone regeneration is linked to the persistence of a deep-anaerobic, Clostridia-enriched consortium, whereas favourable regeneration is heralded by a community shift towards Bacteroidia and specific Proteobacteria. This distinct taxonomic divergence validates the utility of these early microbial markers as potent predictors for clinical prognosis.

Discussion

In this study, we present the first comprehensive, longitudinal characterization of the microbiome associated with odontogenic jaw lesions and its ecological trajectory following marsupialization. By integrating multi-site sampling (saliva, luminal fluid, tissue and swab) with follow-up, we have delineated the ecological trajectory of this unique microbiome: from its baseline establishment in the enclosed lesion to its stochastic restructuring in the post-marsupialization open cavity, and finally to its secondary succession in the residual tissue. Finally, we translated these ecological insights into a clinical prognostic model. Through a rigorous feature selection process, we identified a core signature of seven microbial features from early post-operative swabs. This streamlined panel was used to construct a non-invasive tool that predicted long-term bone regeneration outcomes with high accuracy (AUC = 0.937).

Our baseline analysis provides definitive evidence that odontogenic jaw lesions harbour a distinct, biologically selected microbiome, rather than merely reflecting the passive accumulation of oral bacteria. Recent advances in high-throughput sequencing have challenged traditional dogmas regarding ‘sterile’ anatomical sites, revealing the presence and pathogenic roles of microbes in compartments previously thought to be germ-free – such as Candida albicans in the brain influencing Alzheimer’s disease, or gut bacteria translocating to the retina to induce pathology [8,9]. In this context, our study provides the evidence that the intraosseous jaw lesions, despite appearing physically isolated, harbour a distinct and active microbial community. Through rigorous multi-site comparisons, we confirmed that these intralesional microbes are not artifacts of salivary contamination or passive leakage during surgery but rather constitute a microbial community with unique ecological characteristics [16,26,27]. Specifically, compared to saliva, the tissue microenvironment exhibited a distinct diversity pattern and was selectively enriched in core pathobionts such as Fusobacterium, Clostridia and Parvimonas. This clear community segregation strongly suggests that the specific physicochemical microenvironment within the jawbone acts as a potent ecological filter, actively shaping and maintaining this unique pathogenic niche [28–30].

Marsupialization fundamentally alters this enclosed environment by creating a patent stoma, facilitating continuous interaction with the oral cavity [2–5]. Clinically, previous literature and our own studies have confirmed that this procedure yields substantial bone regeneration and minimizes the need for osteotomy with low recurrence rates, and across various jaw lesions [2,3,5]. However, this study is the first to describe this phenomenon from a microbial ecology perspective. We observed that in the early post-operative phase, the cavity swab microbiome undergoes a stochastic shift, becoming increasingly homogenized with the salivary environment. Source tracking analysis confirmed that the contribution of the primary lesion sources diminished significantly, replaced by a massive influx of salivary bacteria [31,32]. This provides biological validation for the clinical efficacy of marsupialization: the surgical opening breaks the ‘vicious cycle’ of the enclosed, pathogen-rich anaerobic niche, enforcing a shift towards a more commensal-dominated, open ecosystem. Importantly, while the cavity is open to salivary influx, our analyses confirm that the predictive microbial signatures we identified (e.g. Clostridia) are distinct from dominant salivary taxa, underscoring that our findings represent true ecological signals related to healing rather than mere contamination artifacts.

A key insight from our longitudinal analysis is that long-term healing does not imply a complete reversion to a microorganism-free state. Instead, the ‘healed’ residual tissue undergoes a specific secondary succession. While the primary pathobionts (Fusobacterium, Clostridia) are depleted, they are replaced by a ‘secondary anaerobic consortium’ dominated by Bacteroidia (e.g. Odoribacter, Prevotella). Crucially, linking this tissue succession to our predictive modelling reveals a profound ecological logic governing bone regeneration. Our model identified that patients with ‘Poor Recovery’ retained a microbiome signature strikingly similar to that of the primary lesion, characterized by the persistence of Clostridia and other deep-pathogens. This suggests that ‘healing failure’ is essentially an ‘ecological stagnation,’ where the primary pathogenic niche fails to be disrupted. Conversely, the ‘Good Recovery’ signature mirrored the secondary succession observed in the residual healed tissue (enriched in Bacteroidia) and the saliva-like shift (enriched in Proteobacteria and Actinobacteria). This creates a hypothesis that successful bone regeneration is ecologically defined by the transition from a ​​​​‘​​​​​​Clostridia-enriched pathogenic state’ to a remodelled state. The enrichment of Bacteroidia in the good outcome group is particularly intriguing; it implies that in the context of an open cavity, these secondary anaerobes may function as benign place-holders or commensals that prevent the recolonization of more virulent taxa. However, it is important to note that our reliance on 16S rRNA gene sequencing limits our ability to fully verify this mechanism. While 16S sequencing is cost-effective for clinical screening, it lacks the resolution to identify specific strains or measure actual gene expression. Future studies utilizing metagenomics or metatranscriptomics are therefore warranted to decode the specific metabolic pathways and virulence factors driving this critical ‘​​​​​​Clostridia vs. Bacteroidia’ divergence.

Finally, the ultimate goal of our exploration was clinical application. The prediction of bone regeneration after marsupialization has traditionally relied on slow radiographic changes. Our study introduces a novel paradigm: microbial forecasting. This finding aligns with a growing trend in medicine to identify non-invasive biomarkers for monitoring disease activity and predicting therapeutic responses. This concept has been successfully applied in diverse clinical contexts. For instance, longitudinal studies in periodontology have demonstrated that successful non-surgical therapy induces a functional shift in the subgingival microbiome, which correlates with clinical improvement [33–35]. In oncology, baseline gut microbiome features have been shown to predict the tumour response to chemotherapy in colorectal cancer patients, and dynamic changes in the salivary microbiota can even track the success of interventions such as smoking cessation [36]. Similarly, our work extends this principle to a surgical setting, demonstrating that early microbial signatures from non-invasive swabs can forecast long-term surgical outcomes months in advance. By employing a hybrid feature selection strategy (combining ASV-level specificity from MaAsLin2 with taxon-level stability from LEfSe), we curated a panel of 62 markers. While the full model showed some predictive capacity, our analysis revealed that an optimized model (7 features, filtered by P-value < 0.2) achieved superior and more robust predictive performance, effectively stratifying patient risk as early as 3 months post-operation. The practicality of this model lies in its use of non-invasive swabs and cost-effective 16S sequencing. Given that ‘Poor Recovery’ is linked to specific anaerobic persistence, this tool not only predicts outcome but also suggests actionable interventions: patients identified as ‘high risk’ could benefit from intensified local debridement, antimicrobial therapy, or modified irrigation protocols to disrupt the persistent anaerobic niche. However, the sample size for the prognostic model (n = 16 for ASswab) was relatively small. To address this limitation, we performed extensive validation. While our initial LOOCV supported the model, its robustness was more definitively established through bootstrapping (yielding a stable 95% CI for the AUC) and permutation testing (confirming statistical significance). Despite this strong internal validation, larger multi-centre cohorts are imperative to externally validate its predictive accuracy.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study maps the complete microbial dynamics of odontogenic jaw lesions under surgical intervention. We demonstrate that marsupialization drives the ecosystem from a distinct, pathogen-rich dysbiosis toward a stochastic, commensal-like state. However, the trajectory of this shift determines the clinical fate: microbial succession towards a Bacteroidia/Proteobacteria profile supports bone regeneration, while the persistence of the primary Clostridia core hinders it. Our 16S-based predictive model successfully captures these subtle ecological signals, offering a promising, non-invasive precision medicine tool to optimize the management of these common jaw pathologies.
