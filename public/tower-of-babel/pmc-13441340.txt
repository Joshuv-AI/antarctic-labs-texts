Development and Initial Validation of the Response to Suicidal Ideation Inventory (RSII)
Authors: Si Ning Yeo, Jeremy G. Stewart
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectivesYoung adults (18–29 years old) often report difficulties coping during suicide crises. However, the responses that young adults employ to manage suicidal ideation (SI) are underexplored. This study developed a new measure to quantify how young adults with a history of suicidal thoughts and behaviors (STBs) respond to their SI, and conducted an initial evaluation of the measure's psychometric properties.MethodsUsing inductive and deductive approaches, we developed a preliminary item set (n = 79) for the Response to Suicide Ideation Inventory (RSII). Thereafter, 491 participants (M age = 22.0, SDage = 3.3, 18–29 years old), completed the RSII, as well as questionnaires assessing the RSII's content validity, general coping, emotion dysregulation, history of STBs, reasons for living, and future expectations of engaging in STBs.ResultsResults from an exploratory factor analysis indicated that a 43‐item, seven‐factor solution was an appropriate fit to the data. The RSII and its subscales showed acceptable reliability, as well as preliminary content validity. The RSII's subscales also demonstrated medium‐sized positive correlations with measures of general coping, and weak associations with emotion dysregulation, suicide resilience, and expectations of future STBs.ConclusionsOur results point to some important directions for refining the RSII. Exploring the range and types of responses young adults engage in to manage their SI may improve our understanding of, and ability to predict, fluctuations in SI severity.

Introduction

Suicide is one of the leading causes of death among young adults, defined in our study as individuals aged 18–29, worldwide (World Health Organization 2025). While STBs occur across the lifespan, they often first onset during adolescence (Voss et al. 2019), and the rate of suicide deaths increases significantly between early adolescence and young adulthood (Cha et al. 2018; Nock et al. 2008). Importantly, far more young adults experience SI than plan or attempt suicide (O'Connor et al. 2018). Thus, studies that catalog and examine the thoughts and behaviors that occur in response to SI can enhance our understanding of how some young adults transition from ideation to attempt. Nonetheless, there have been few efforts to operationalize and measure responses to SI.

Coping with SI adaptively is crucial because doing so may prevent its future occurrence or may reduce the severity and consequences if SI reoccurs (Bryan et al. 2018). Indeed, in many first‐line interventions, individuals presenting with acute SI are assisted in identifying coping strategies for navigating future suicide crises (e.g., Stanley and Brown 2012; Tyndal et al. 2022). However, young adults with a history of SI express low‐to‐moderate confidence in their ability to apply coping strategies during crises (Czyz et al. 2016) and are reluctant to access professional or medical resources in such times (De Luca et al. 2020; Michelmore and Hindley 2012). The unique challenges of adolescence and young adulthood may partly explain the increased rate of SI in this developmental period, and associated difficulties in managing these cognitions.

In late adolescence and early adulthood, individuals navigate changing social roles and more intimate relationships, while being tasked with forming a clearer sense of identity and pursuing higher education and/or employment (Shanahan 2000). These demands, along with age‐related changes in preference for and access to different coping strategies (e.g., greater preference for avoidance to manage sadness in young adulthood, Amirkhan and Auyeung 2007; Zimmermann and Iwanski 2014; increased access to alcohol and other substances; DiBello et al. 2018), may partially drive how young adults respond to SI, both over time and across contexts; however, the literature on these processes is in its infancy.

Prior studies have identified some common responses to SI (e.g., distraction [Rainbow et al. 2024], seeking informal support [Michelmore and Hindley 2012]), but the full range of responses has not been formally cataloged. Further, there are no tools that specifically measure the response to SI. Frequently adopted coping scales like the Coping Orientation to Problems Experienced (COPE) inventory (Carver et al. 1989) assess coping in response to specific stressors. Other scales used by suicidologists measure concepts adjacent to suicide‐related coping or assess a limited set of coping strategies. For instance, the Suicide‐Related Coping Scale (Stanley et al. 2017) measures one's perceived self‐efficacy for coping during a suicide crisis, while the Suicide Thought Control Questionnaire (Tucker et al. 2017) measures the use of cognitive strategies (i.e., thought control) to manage SI. These and other similar scales are not useful for measuring the full repertoire of actions young adults may take upon experiencing SI. A measure that comprehensively assesses reactions to SI may help us better understand the variability in experiences of SI across individuals (and within individuals across contexts). Ultimately, such a measure would improve insight into the transition from suicidal thinking to suicidal action.

Conceptualizing Response to SI

Since coping is a primary target in clinical work with individuals experiencing STBs (Nuij et al. 2021), we use the general coping literature as a starting point to conceptualize response to SI. Coping includes myriad cognitive and behavioral responses that an individual may employ to mitigate the effects of internal and external stressors (Pearlin and Schooler 1978). Here, we draw on existing coping categories (e.g., problem solving, support‐seeking; see Skinner et al. 2003 for overview; Supporting Material Table 1.1) to conceptualize and measure responses that might occur at the onset of SI. At the same time, some important dimensions reflecting common responses to SI may be distinct from general coping. For instance, some information‐seeking responses (e.g., looking up suicide methods online) may be more appropriately categorized as suicide planning behaviors than others (e.g., searching websites for treatment information or peer support). Thus, applying the general coping framework to SI “out of the box” would likely miss important nuances. Specifically, SI onsets and progresses within a context of highly personalized dynamic factors (Sewall and Wright 2021). Coping might be one functional response category that is used by some when SI occurs. However, considering individual differences linked to SI is necessary to capture the diversity of responses to SI beyond general coping.

How young adults relate to their SI is a key individual difference that may be relevant to coping. While SI typically evokes distress (Al‐Dajani and Uliaszek 2021), some young adults experience their SI as non‐aversive or even comforting (Crane et al. 2014; Kleiman et al. 2018). The strategies young adults employ to navigate SI episodes are likely shaped by how they interpret their SI (e.g., as distressing, comforting, or both) and by what they hope to achieve through their responses. For instance, many young adults engage in non‐suicidal self‐injury (NSSI) to manage SI (Saraff and Pepper 2014). Functional analyses of NSSI show that these behaviors have several possible intrapersonal (e.g., emotional relief) and interpersonal functions (e.g., communicating distress to others; Taylor et al. 2018), which may contribute to a reduction in distress. In contrast, individuals who hold more accepting attitudes towards suicide and are comforted by their SI may be more inclined to use thought reframing strategies to foster acceptance of their SI. Given that most people have complex feelings about dying by suicide (Lakeman and FitzGerald 2008; Søndergaard et al. 2023), the way people navigate SI when it occurs is likely to involve a broad range of responses (e.g., Interian et al. 2021).

Besides one's views and how they relate to their SI, the characteristics and intensity of their SI may also influence response repertoire. The experience of SI often entails both the desire to live and the desire to die, resulting in a state of ambivalence (Kovacs and Beck 1977). Consequently, individuals may employ a combination of life‐preserving and risky strategies, reflecting the relative balance in these opposing desires. Ideation‐to‐action theories further propose that experiences that are painful, frightening, and/or life‐threatening may reduce the natural fear of death that people typically possess (Klonsky et al. 2018; Kirshenbaum et al. 2024). Thus, more intense SI may be linked to greater fearlessness about death (see Hart et al. 2024; Stewart et al. 2023), risky behavior engagement (e.g., Smith and Wells 2023; Stewart et al. 2017), and cognitive and behavioral states (e.g., Wenzel and Beck 2008) that are conceivably life‐threatening. Furthermore, more severe SI may also be linked to using fewer life‐preserving responses following SI, which may in turn increase their propensity to use more lethal responses in subsequent SI episodes.

Taken together, literature relevant to understanding responses to SI suggests that these are multifaceted, may serve different functions, and likely transact with key intraindividual and broader contextual variables. Presently, there is no definitive and comprehensive understanding of the types, ranges, and combinations of behaviors and cognitions that occur in response to SI across individuals. Developing a standardized and empirically validated measure of response to SI will support further research in this area and guide the formulation of more precise treatment and prevention of worsening STBs. Accordingly, the current project aimed to (1) develop the Response to Suicide Ideation Inventory (RSII), a comprehensive measure of the response to SI, and (2) report its preliminary psychometric properties in a large sample of young adults.

Methods

Developing the RSII

We followed best‐practice recommendations for developing and validating scales (e.g., Boateng et al. 2018). Development of the RSII was based on our conceptual definition of response to SI: “behaviors and/or cognitions that occur following SI onset.”

We generated instructions, candidate items, and a response scale for the initial draft of the RSII guided by the literature on general coping and drawing from coping strategies and cognitive responses reported by individuals with lived experiences of STBs on online communities/forums and suicide prevention organizations. Thereafter, we requested input from suicide scientists regarding the instructions, the face and content validity of the items, and the overall grammatical structure and readability. The RSII was amended following the feedback, and the final version included 79 items. Further details of our item creation procedures and the initial and final versions of the RSII are described in the Supporting Material (pp. 1‐22).

Participants

Eligible participants were 18 to 29 years old, had experienced SI within the previous 3 months, were English literate, resided in North America, had access to a computer or laptop and the internet, and had normal or corrected vision.

Five hundred and ninety‐eight people participated in the study. Data from participants were removed prior to analysis if (1) they requested to withdraw their data from the study (n = 2), (2) they completed the study twice (n = 4), (3) they did not complete the RSII (n = 28), (4) they had an inconsistent response style (n = 3), and (5) if they failed at least one of three attention check questions (n = 70). A final 491 participants were included in the data analysis.

Sociodemographic characteristics of the sample are presented in Table 1. The mean age of participants was 22.0 (SD = 3.3). The sample predominantly identified as White (59.7%), heterosexual (43.8%), women (72.5%), and as having some college education or a completed degree (75.1%). Participants reported having suicidal thoughts for an average of 26.2 days (SD = 26.8), in the 3 months prior to their study participation.

Procedures

All procedures were approved and overseen by our institutional ethics board. Participants were recruited through advertisements placed on social media and classified sites, organizational listservs, and the university's research participant pool1. Interested individuals completed an online screening survey to confirm their eligibility. The following question in the screening survey determined whether participants met the eligibility requirement of experiencing SI within the previous 3 months: “Have you had suicidal thoughts in the past 3 months? Suicidal thoughts can resemble any of the following: 'I wish I could disappear or not exist'; 'I wish I were never born'; 'My life is not worth living'; 'I wish I would go to sleep and never wake up again'; 'I wish I were dead'; 'Maybe I should kill myself'; 'I should kill myself'; or 'I am going to kill myself.'” Participants who responded “yes” to this question and met other eligibility criteria were invited to participate.

The study team performed steps recommended by Storozuk et al. (2020) to identify bots and spam respondents. The screening survey had open‐ended and attention check questions. We also verified the IP address and geolocation linked to each response and screened email addresses for legitimacy. We excluded respondents who did not meet the inclusion criteria and/or who were identified as bots or spam from enrollment.

All study procedures were completed remotely. Participants first reviewed the study information and provided consent to participate before completing a battery of surveys. Participants were remunerated with either 0.5 course credit or a $10 e‐gift card.

Measures

Demographics

The demographic questionnaire assessed age, country of residence, gender identity, sexual orientation, relationship status, ethnicity, religious affiliation, education level, and history of mental health diagnosis.

Self‐Injurious Thoughts and Behaviours Interview‐Revised (SITBI‐R; Fox et al. 2020)

The SITBI‐R assessed the history, presence, frequency, and characteristics of STBs. The SITBI‐R is reliable and valid when administered as an online self‐report assessment (Fox et al. 2020). We administered sections related to STBs of interest (i.e., NSSI, SI, suicide planning, suicide preparation, and suicide attempts). We adjusted the SITBI‐R to include items that queried the frequency of STBs of interest in the 3 months prior to study participation (e.g., “How many times in the past 3 months have you purposely hurt yourself without wanting to die?”). Future expectations of engaging in STBs of interest were assessed with the item, “What is the likelihood that you will engage in [STB of interest] in the future?”, rated from 0 (Not at all) to 4 (Extremely).

Response to Suicidal Ideation Inventory (RSII; See Supporting Material pp. 23–26)

The RSII measures the frequency individuals have engaged in a list of 79 possible behavioral (e.g., “I distracted myself by scrolling through social media”) and cognitive (e.g., “I went through in my head the steps I would take to end my life”) responses to SI. The time frame of reference was the 3‐month period prior to study participation. Participants rated each item on a scale from 1 (I did not do this at all) to 6 (I did this every time). A “Not Applicable” (N.A.) option was also provided and recoded as 1 for analyses rather than treated as missing data. Following factor analysis, we computed subscale scores by summing the items of each subscale. A higher total RSII score indicates more frequent use of responses, and a higher subscale score indicates more frequent use of responses within the subscale.

RSII Feedback Measure

The study team designed this measure to solicit general participant experience in completing the RSII. Three questions assessed the readability of the RSII's instructions, response scale, and items (i.e., Question 1: “It is easy to understand the instructions of this measure”; Question 2: “I had difficulty understanding the items in the measure”), as well as conceptual congruence of the items (i.e., Question 3: “The list of thoughts and behaviors is representative of what I did and thought of when I had suicidal thoughts”). Participants indicated their agreement on a scale from 1 (Strongly Disagree) to 7 (Strongly Agree).

Brief Cope (Carver 1997)

The Brief COPE is a 28‐item inventory that measures behavioral strategies for coping with stress. The 14 subscales include active coping (e.g., “I have been taking action to try to make the situation better”), distraction (e.g., “I have been turning to work or other activities to take my mind off things”), and self‐blame (e.g., “I have been criticizing myself”). Items were rated on a 4‐point scale from 1 (I have not been doing this at all) to 4 (I have been doing this a lot). The Brief COPE demonstrates good convergent and discriminant validity, with subscales showing adequate internal consistency (Rodrigues et al. 2022). Although the measure is not typically used to generate a single coping index, we computed the total score by summing all items to examine its alignment with the total RSII score. Higher total scores indicate more frequent use of coping strategies. We calculated subscale scores by summing the items within each subscale, following Carver (1997) scoring instructions, with higher scores reflecting more frequent use of responses in that subscale. The Brief COPE showed acceptable reliability for the overall scale (α = 0.83). Reliability for the subscales ranged from 0.48 to 0.96 (Supporting Material Table 4.1).

Difficulties in Emotion Regulation Scale‐ Short Form (DERS‐SF; Kaufman et al. 2015)

The DERS‐SF is an 18‐item self‐report measure of emotion dysregulation comprising six subscales: Strategies (e.g., “When I'm upset, it takes me a long time to feel better”), Non‐Acceptance (e.g., “When I'm upset, I feel guilty for feeling that way”), Impulse (e.g., “When I'm upset, I become out of control”), Goals (e.g., “When I'm upset, I have difficulty concentrating”), Awareness (e.g., “I pay attention to how I feel”), and Clarity (e.g., “I am confused about how I feel”). Participants rated items on a 5‐point scale from 1 (Almost Never) to 5 (Almost Always). The DERS‐SF has excellent psychometric properties, measuring the same dimensions of emotion regulation deficits as the original DERS (Gratz and Roemer 2004). We summed responses to generate a total DERS‐SF score and computed scores for each of the six subscales. Higher scores reflect greater emotion regulation difficulties and less effective strategies. The DERS‐SF showed acceptable reliability for the total scale (α = 0.89) and across its subscales (0.76 to 0.90; Supporting Material Table 4.2).

Reasons for Living‐ Short Form (RFL; Linehan et al. 1983)

The RFL is a 48‐item self‐report measure assessing the importance of different reasons for living (e.g., “I am afraid of the unknown”). The measure demonstrated validity in the initial examination by the authors and in subsequent studies (e.g., Osman et al. 1992; Osman et al. 1993). Items were rated on a 6‐point scale from 1 (Not At All Important) to 6 (Extremely Important). A higher total score reflects stronger reasons for living. We derived the RFL total score in accordance with the instructions provided by Linehan et al. (1983) and used the total score for subsequent analysis. The RFL showed acceptable reliability for the total scale score (α = 0.95).

Data Analysis

Data analyses were conducted in RStudio (Posit Team 2025) and IBM SPSS Statistics 28 following recommendations for conducting Exploratory Factor Analysis (EFA; e.g., Güvendir & Özkan [2022], Fabrigar et al. [1999]). Given the lack of understanding of responses to SI and the underlying latent structure, we determined that EFA, rather than Principal Component Analysis, was more appropriate for the purposes of our study. We conducted preliminary missing data analysis and considered the range, skewness, and kurtosis of response distributions of each RSII item. We assessed the suitability of the data for EFA by examining the Kaiser‐Meyer‐Olkin (KMO) measure of sampling adequacy (Kaiser and Rice 1974) and Bartlett's Test of Sphericity (Bartlett 1954). We next conducted Horn's parallel analysis, generated a scree plot, and conducted Principal Axis Factoring (PAF) with Direct Oblimin rotation to determine the most appropriate factor model. Model selection was based on the results of the parallel analysis, visual inspection of the scree plot, and comparisons of PAF‐derived factor models in conjunction with existing theories and understanding of SI and its correlates. We used recommended fit indices to evaluate factor models from the PAF analysis (i.e., RMSEA < 0.05, RMSR < 0.08, TLI > 0.9; see Finch 2020). In line with established recommendations, items with factor loadings that were less than |0.32| were removed (Comrey and Lee 1992; Tabachnick and Fidell 2001). Cross‐loading items with a difference of less than 0.20 between their primary and alternative factor loadings, and with alternative loadings that were above |0.30| were removed as well (Howard 2016).

We next generated reliability statistics, as well as inter‐factor and inter‐item correlations. Inter‐factor correlations below 0.7 were considered ideal, as a correlation greater than 0.7 would indicate that there was a high shared variance among the factors, which may indicate poor discriminant validity (Vijayamohanan and Rjumohan 2020). Mean inter‐item correlation between 0.2 and 0.4 was considered acceptable (Piedmont 2014).

We examined the convergent, discriminant, and concurrent validity of the RSII using correlation analyses between the RSII, the brief COPE, the DERS‐SF, the SITBI‐R, and the RFL. Where item distributions were non‐normal, we conducted Spearman correlations. Where response variables consisted of count data, we conducted negative binomial regressions. All other associations were quantified with Pearson correlations. Usability and content validity estimates of the RSII were derived by examining the mean and standard deviation of ratings on the RSII feedback measure and the frequency that participants endorsed Agree to Strongly Agree (i.e., 5 and above on the 7‐point scale).

Results

Preliminary Analyses

Four participants had missing responses in their RSII. Little's Missing Completely at Random test (Little 1988) indicated that results were not missing completely at random, χ2(300, N = 491) = 334.39, p = 0.084. We dealt with these missing data using pairwise deletion.

We assessed the mean, standard deviation, skewness, and kurtosis of the RSII items to determine item quality (Supporting Material Table 2). Nineteen items were removed due to a high proportion of participants endorsing the extreme end (i.e., I did not do this at all/Not Applicable) on the response scale, resulting in pronounced non‐normality ( | skewness | > 2, |kurtosis | > 7; see Curran et al. 1996). The KMO value for the dataset was 0.85, indicating that the strength of the relations among the examined variables was high. The Bartlett's test of Sphericity indicated that items on the RSII were significantly correlated, χ2 (3081) = 15360.62, p < 0.001, demonstrating that a data reduction technique was appropriate for the data. Thus, the data were suitable for EFA (Figure 1).

The seven‐, eight‐, and 10‐ factor models had acceptable fit indices, apart from the TLI values, which were below 0.84 across the three models. Although the models had less than ideal TLI values, other fit indices and the interpretability of the factor solutions suggested acceptable fit and supported retaining the models. In comparing the three models, the 10‐factor model had the best fit, but its improvement over the seven‐ and eight‐ factor models was not large enough to warrant the reduction in parsimony (Preacher et al. 2013). Thus, we assessed the seven‐factor and eight‐factor models.

In the seven‐factor model, five items (i.e., items 21, 29, 32, 43, and 46) did not load on any factor. These items were focused on restricting food intake, playing video games, restricting access to means of suicide, spending time with pets, and going to a crowded place. Nine items (i.e., items 30, 36, 40, 56, 70, 74, 76, 77, and 79) loaded on two factors. These items did not meet the cross‐loading cut‐off criterion for inclusion (i.e., a difference of more than 0.20 between the primary and alternative factor loadings; loading less than |0.30| on alternative factors). The remaining 48 items had communality values ranging from 0.21 to 0.65 and factor loadings ranging from |0.32| to |0.79 |. The factors accounted for 36% of the total variance. Overall, the entire RSII scale showed acceptable reliability (α = 0.87, ω = 0.84).

In the eight‐factor model, four items (i.e., items 21, 32, 43, and 46) did not load on any factor, while six items (i.e., items 36, 56, 70, 74, 76 and 77) had loadings on two factors. The remaining 52 items had communality values ranging from 0.15 to 0.67 and factor loadings ranging from |0.32| to |0.80|. The factors accounted for 38% of the total variance. The items in this factor solution showed acceptable reliability (α = 0.87, ω = 0.85). Overall, the item loadings in this model were like the item loadings in the seven‐factor model. While there were some improvements in item loadings as compared to the seven‐factor model (i.e., fewer cross‐loading items and fewer non‐loading items), the additional factor only had two items (i.e., items 19 and 20). A factor with fewer than three items is generally considered unstable (Costello and Osborne 2009). Further, both items, which describe consuming food to achieve a desired state, were subsumed under Factor 6 in the seven‐factor model. We concluded that a seven‐factor model was sufficient to explain the underlying structure of the response to SI.

We removed the non‐loading and cross‐loading items from the seven‐factor model and re‐ran the analysis. There were no non‐loading and cross‐loading items after two rounds of item removal and analyses. Factor loadings and statistics for the final seven‐factor model are presented in Table 3. The seven factors were labeled “Considering Consequences of Suicide”, “Suicide Planning”, “Pleasant Activities”, “Risky Behaviors”, “Seeking Emotional Support”, “Behavioral Distraction”, and “Cognitive Distraction”. The resulting 43 items had communality values ranging from 0.21 to 0.68 and factor loadings ranging from |0.33| to |0.80 |. The factors accounted for 39% of the total variance. The overall RSII scale showed high reliability (α = 0.84, ω = 0.83). Factor loadings, item communalities, variance proportion, and reliability of factors for the 43 items are presented in Table 3.

Convergent and Divergent Validity

We examined the convergent, discriminant, and concurrent validity of the RSII using correlation analyses between the RSII, the brief COPE, the DERS‐SF, the SITBI‐R, and the RFL. The RSII total scores demonstrated a moderate positive correlation with the Brief COPE total scores, r (473) = 0.57, p < 0.001. To assess convergent validity at the subscale level, we conducted correlations between the RSII and Brief COPE subscales and the DERS‐SF subscales.

The Risky Behaviors subscale of the RSII was moderately and positively correlated with the Substance Use subscale on the Brief COPE, r (479) = 0.51, p < 0.001. Similarly, the Seeking Emotional Support subscale of the RSII had a moderate positive correlation with the Emotional Support subscale of the Brief COPE r (479) = 0.54, p < 0.001. Correlations between other pairs of conceptually similar subscales of the two scales were weak (r = 0.17−0.22; Supporting Material Table 4.1).

The Cognitive Distraction subscale of the RSII had a positive correlation with the Non‐Acceptance subscale of the DERS‐SF that was in the weak‐to‐moderate range, r (472) = 0.36, p < 0.001. All other significant associations among the RSII subscale and DERS‐SF subscales were weak (r = 0.09−0.33; Supporting Material Table 4.2).

In support of divergent validity, the RSII total scores demonstrated weak correlations with the DERS‐SF total scores, RFL total scores (DERS‐SF: r (472) = 0.24, p < 0.001; RFL: r (464) = 0.30, p < 0.001).

Concurrent Validity

The frequency of suicide planning and suicide preparation in the past 3 months (as measured by the SITBI‐R) had significant positive associations with the RSII Suicide planning subscale (SITBI‐R Suicide planning: Model χ2(1) = 432.53, p < 0.001; b = 0.11, SE = 0.01, 95% CI = 0.01, 0.12, χ2(1) = 334.59, p < 0.001; IRR = 1.12, 95% CI = 1.10, 1.13; SITBI‐R Suicide Preparation: Model χ2(1) = 565.69, p < 0.001; b = 0.16, SE = 0.01, 95% CI = 0.15, 0.18, χ2(1) = 311.15, p < 0.001; IRR = 1.17, 95% CI = 1.16, 1.20). These effects support the RSII's concurrent validity.

No other questionnaires administered in the study measured constructs that conceptually overlapped with the RSII subscales. Accordingly, we examined correlations between the RSII subscales and future expectations of engaging in NSSI, SI, suicide planning, and suicide attempt (as measured by the SITBI‐R), and the RFL total score to explore if they were in expected directions. We aimed to provide further preliminary support for the RSII's concurrent validity (Supporting Material Table 4.3). Future expectations of engaging in NSSI, SI, suicide planning, and suicide attempt had small to moderate positive correlations with the Suicide Planning subscale. The total RFL score had a moderate positive correlation with the Pleasant Activities subscale. The total RFL score also had weak positive correlations with the Considering Consequences of Suicide, Seeking Emotional Support, Behavioral Distraction, and Cognitive Distraction subscales, and a weak negative correlation with the Suicide Planning subscale.

Readability and Content Validity

On the RSII feedback measure, most participants (Question 1: 91.4%, Question 2: 88.3%) agreed that the RSII items and instructions were easy to understand (i.e., they endorsed 5 and above on the 7‐point scale; Question 1: M = 6.3, SD = 1.2; Question 2: M = 6.1, SD = 1.4). Further, in support of content validity, 72.6% agreed that the items were representative of the thoughts and cognitions they engaged in when they experience SI (i.e., endorsed 5 and above on the 7‐point scale; Question 3: M = 5.2, SD = 1.5).

Discussion

Despite its relevance to the transition from suicidal thoughts to behaviors, the response to SI remains understudied. This study developed a novel measure, the RSII, to capture responses to SI and provided a preliminary evaluation of its psychometric properties. The resultant measure consists of 43 items with a seven‐factor structure that was a good fit to the data. The RSII also demonstrated some convergence with general coping and divergence from emotion dysregulation, expectations of future suicidal behaviors, and suicide resilience. The concurrent validity of the RSII was evidenced by significant associations between scores on some of its factors and measures of severity of STBs, suicide resilience, and expectations of future engagement in STBs. The RSII is the first measure of its kind to assess the diverse ways in which young adults respond to SI and to examine distinct dimensions of these responses, addressing a critical gap in suicide research.

Our measure development approach provided insight into potential responses to SI that were not conceptualized as such within the extant literature (e.g., mental rehearsal of suicide, suicide preparation). In support of the content and ecological validity of the RSII, none of the questionnaire items were not endorsed by participants, suggesting that the RSII resonated with participant experiences. Most participants (72.6%) also indicated strong agreement that the items were representative of their response to SI in the RSII feedback measure.

Factor analytic results were consistent with broad categories of response to SI identified in prior research. Four factors‐ Pleasant Activities, Seeking Emotional Support, Behavioral Distraction, and Cognitive Distraction‐ overlapped conceptually with the coping categories of self‐reliance, support‐seeking, and escape (Skinner et al. 2003). Likewise, the items that loaded on Considering Consequences of Suicide described contemplating the implications of one's suicide on social and community ties. People with lived experience of STBs often describe thinking about close others when considering suicide and often note their responsibility towards their close ties as a reason for living (Rasmussen et al. 2025; Scarth et al. 2021). Thus, items in these five RSII factors are related to oft‐reported experiences of increasing feelings of hope and connection with others to overcome SI (Søndergaard et al. 2023), which ultimately reinforces the desire to live (Bakhiyi et al. 2016).

The remaining two factors‐ Suicide Planning and Risky Behaviors‐ align with studies showing that some people respond to SI with behaviors and cognitions that may increase suicide risk. Indeed, escalation from the onset of an SI episode to planning, preparatory actions, and suicidal behavior can occur within minutes or hours for some (Millner et al. 2017). Studies examining males with a history of suicide attempts have found that alcohol and drug use are commonly employed as (ineffective) coping strategies in the context of SI, and these behaviors precede suicide attempts (Cleary 2017; Richardson et al. 2021). Such responses may be phenotypic expressions of underlying feelings of defeat and entrapment‐ conceptualized as a sense of failed struggle and perceived entrapment in an unbearably painful situation, respectively (Gilbert and Allan 1998). Defeat and entrapment are cognitive‐affective states that are hypothesized to be crucial drivers of the onset and escalation of STBs (O'connor and Kirtley 2018; Souza et al. 2024). During experiences of SI, these feelings may motivate individuals to engage in suicide planning and risky behaviors, thereby facilitating the exploration of dying as an escape from their circumstances.

Overall, the RSII's seven factors align with our conceptualization that the ambivalence that typically accompanies SI may produce a range of risky and life‐preserving behaviors and cognitions. Correspondingly, young adults in our study reported engaging in both life‐preserving and risky responses across SI episodes, and all RSII subscales were positively correlated. Critically, while some people report more stable, persistent and severe SI, others experience SI that is less frequent, more variable, and/or less intense within and across days (Kivelä et al. 2025; Kleiman et al. 2018). Thus, one's response to their SI may influence their subsequent SI intensity and the underlying conflicting desires for life and death. For instance, a study of adult outpatients found that, compared to individuals who have never attempted suicide, those with a history of suicide attempts reported a stronger suicidal desire and a more variable wish to live and wish to die over time (Ernst et al. 2024). Therefore, the RSII may be a useful valuable tool for capturing nuanced ways in which young adults navigate SI and how it may relate to short‐term fluctuations in their SI severity.

The overall RSII and its factors were generally internally consistent. Specifically, five out of the seven factors showed acceptable alpha values while two factors (Seeking Emotional Support and Behavioral Distraction) had alphas that were slightly below conventional cutoffs for acceptable reliability. The Seeking Emotional Support factor had items that were similar in content and had acceptable inter‐item correlations. However, it consisted of only four items. Similarly, the Behavioral Distraction factor had five items with inter‐item correlations that were within the acceptable range. In this case, the items covered a broad range of behavioral domains (i.e., problematic food intake, viewing multimedia entertainment). Existing methodological guidelines stipulate that five or more strongly loading items ( > 0.50) are indicative of a robust factor (Costello and Osborne 2009). Further steps are needed to increase the number of items in select RSII factors, re‐evaluate the RSII items in other young adult samples, and confirm the underlying dimensions of the RSII through Confirmatory Factor Analysis.

The RSII subscales were generally unrelated to their corresponding Brief COPE subscales despite having conceptually similar items. These results evince that, apart from some similarities in avoidance and emotional support‐seeking behaviors during SI and general stress, behaviors and cognitions that young adults use in response to SI may be distinct from their responses to negative or stressful contexts. SI is fundamentally characterized by existential deliberations about life and death (Teismann et al. 2024). This internal debate over whether to live or die accompanies the suicide process, from ideation to attempt (Galasiński and Ziółkowska 2024). Therefore, reactions to SI are likely to involve cognitions and behaviors rooted in one's struggle between wishing to live and wishing to die. These reactions likely extend beyond the efforts to cope with the negative affect that SI sometimes evokes, and in addition, or alternatively, reflect survival instincts and related constructs (e.g., controlling suicidal thoughts and urges; Marchionatti et al. 2024).

This study has important limitations in our conceptualization of responses to SI, sample characteristics, study procedures, and results that suggest novel directions for future research. Despite our attempt to be comprehensive in our conceptual approach to developing the RSII, we acknowledge that the measure does not provide insight into whether participants view their responses as helpful or adaptive, or potentially harmful and/or contributing to a worsening of their SI. This is a potential drawback of the measure for certain applications (e.g., limited utility as a standalone risk management tool). The characteristics of the sample (i.e., predominantly white, female, and college‐educated), together with modest representation of key demographic subgroups (e.g., transgender and gender‐nonconforming individuals), meant that we could not assess factor invariance. Future studies should examine the factor structure and psychometric properties of the RSII in racial, ethnic, gender, and sexual minorities. Further, our study focuses on individuals aged 18–29. This sampling decision was made to remove barriers related to requiring parental consent for adolescents under 18, as such requirements may reduce self‐disclosure and affect data quality (Smith et al. 2022). The period spanning early adolescence to young adulthood is associated with elevated rates of STBs (e.g., Cha et al. 2018). Consequently, our findings are limited to young adults and cannot be generalized to adolescents under the age of 18. Given developmental differences in general coping strategies (e.g., Amirkhan and Auyeung 2007) and characteristics of STBs (e.g., attempt methods; Brdar et al. 2025; Lee et al. 2019), future work that attends to distinct age ranges (e.g., late adolescence [15–17 years], emerging adulthood [18–25 years], young adulthood [25–29 years]) may help clarify how responses to SI differ across the lifespan. In our study procedures, participants were instructed to recall their response to their SI in the 3 months prior to their study participation. This time window for recall may have led some participants to under‐ or overestimate the frequency of engagement in each response (Beegle et al. 2012). Future research using ecological momentary assessments can provide more accurate insights into the in‐the‐moment reactions to SI. Finally, since this is the first study to directly measure the response to SI as a construct, the evaluation of its psychometric properties was somewhat constrained by the dearth of similar measures. Further, our factor solutions had TLI values that were below the cutoff (< 0.90), which may indicate underfactoring (e.g., Lee and Cham 2024). However, we note that the effectiveness of incremental fit indices cutoffs can be sensitive to data characteristics (e.g., continuous indicators, number of indicators, factor intercorrelations; Clark and Bowles 2018; Lee and Cham 2024), and we employed a multi‐indicator approach following best‐practice recommendations to retain the seven‐factor model. Additional research efforts, such as testing the RSII in other young adult samples and determining if the RSII can differentiate individuals with varying histories of STBs (e.g., those who have made suicide plans vs. those who have never done so) will offer additional evidence of its validity.

In sum, our study developed and provided initial evidence for the RSII as a tool for examining the various dimensions of the response to SI, filling a critical empirical gap in suicidology. Replicating the results of the factor analysis in other samples of youths is vital. Importantly, the equifinal nature of SI and other STBs underscores the need for more personalized approaches to understanding how young adults experience and respond to their SI. In this context, the RSII can facilitate the examination of within‐individual response patterns during SI episodes, enabling the identification of areas of risk and resilience within each young adult. For instance, this approach can augment existing safety planning procedures by providing clinicians and clients with a comprehensive overview of the responses clients engage in during SI episodes, while facilitating discussions on prioritizing protective responses and minimizing risky responses during subsequent suicide crises.
