Abstract
. The study of the dynamics of a continuous observable and non-controllable three-dimensional symmetric piecewise linear system with three zones can be reduced to the study of the existence of limit cycles for the piecewise differential equation x ′ = a ​ x + ( b − a ) ​ sat ( x ) + μ ​ sin ⁡ t x^{\prime}=ax+(b-a)\mathop{\rm sat}(x)+\mu\sin t , where sat \operatorname{sat} stands for the normalized saturation function. This paper proves that the number of limit cycles of the equation is finite independently of a , b , μ a,b,\mu . Moreover, it is proven that the maximum number of limit cycles is exactly one or three for certain values of the parameters. Using Melnikov theory for a certain deformation of the equation, it is proven that for small values of the perturbation parameter, there are exactly three, five or one limit cycles, depending on the values of μ \mu . This strengthens the conjecture that the equation has at most five limit cycles.

Introduction and main results

Within the framework of temporal evolution systems and the study of nonlinear oscillators, non-smooth differential systems naturally emerge, and, as a particular case, piecewise linear dynamic systems. These latter systems, beyond being mathematically attractive structures in themselves, allow the modeling of different electronic
[
14
,
15
,
21
]
or mechanical devices
[
37
,
38
]
and are capable of capturing the nonlinear dynamics of the differentiable systems
[
2
,
11
,
13
,
40
]
. Furthermore, when continuity conditions are not required, they present new phenomena that seem well-suited to explain behaviors intrinsic to various physical mechanisms
[
1
,
19
,
27
]
and even neurological mechanisms
[
35
]
.

The study of the dynamics of non-smooth systems begins with identifying the regions where the system behaves in a differentiable manner and describing the boundary zones between them. For
n-
dimensional systems, the boundary separations are often parallel hyperplanes, and the inherent problem of the dynamical behavior of the system shifts to understanding how points in a boundary separation transform, through the flow of the system, into points of an adjacent zone (or even the same zone). The map that transforms points from one boundary zone to another is typically called a transition map, and when the starting and ending zones are the same, it is usually referred to as a Poincaré half-map. The global behavior of the system is understood by knowing the conduct of these maps, their composition, and the behavior in the boundary separations. When the vector field of the system is continuous, or its flow does not present jumps in each boundary zone, then the behavior in them does not exhibit confusion. However, otherwise, it is common to resort to Filippov’s convention
[
20
]
to establish the dynamical behavior in the boundary zones, where sliding or escaping regions frequently appear.

The simplest piecewise linear system is the one constructed in two dimensions with two linearity regions separated by a straight line. When the continuity is imposed on the vector field that defines it, or when there is no sliding region on the separation line, the dynamical behavior is almost completely understood thanks to the novel integral characterization of the Poincaré half-maps recently discovered
[
9
]
. For instance, it is already known that the system has at most one limit cycle, that is, a unique isolated periodic orbit
[
7
,
8
]
, and it is conjectured that it has at most one non-trivial isolated invariant curve. However, when the presence of the sliding region on the separating line is allowed, the dynamics are not understood, and until very recently, it was unknown whether the system could have an infinite number of limit cycles. It is now established that the maximum number of limit cycles is finite, and an explicit upper bound for this number is provided in
[
12
]
.

For the
n
-dimensional case, the study of a parametrized family of autonomous piecewise linear systems begins with their formulation in an appropriate canonical form

17

16

Specifically, it focuses on the analysis of the periodic behavior of a continuous symmetric observable and non-controllable three-dimensional symmetric piecewise linear system with three zones of linearity.

Proposition 3.2
.

Equation (
1.7
) is a global center if and only if
a=b=0
.

Proof.

Note that by Remark
2.4
, we can assume, without loss of generality, that

. If equation (
1.7
) is a global center, then
u(t,0,x)
is periodic for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
. So, considering the vector field defined by equation (
1.7
) on the line

, one can see that
u(t,0,x)<-1
for every

and
x\ll-1

Therefore using (
2.3
) we obtain

x=u(2\pi,0,x)=e^{2\pi a}x+\int_{0}^{2\pi}(a-b+\mu\sin(s))e^{a(2\pi-s)}\,ds;

for all

and so

. Conversely, when
a=b=0
, equation (
1.7
) is

, which presents clearly a global center.
∎

Proposition 3.3
.

Suppose
a\neq 0
and
b=0
, then equation (
1.7
) either has a center and no limit cycles, if
0\leqslant|\mu|<1
, or it has exactly one limit cycle, if
|\mu|\geqslant 1
.

Proof.

When
b=0
, by Proposition
2.2
, the derivative of displacement function
d
is
d^{\prime}(x)=e^{a(2\pi-m(A_{in}(x)))}-1
. Therefore, since
m(A_{in}(x))\leq 2\pi
, for

we have that
ad^{\prime}(x)\geqslant 0
and that it is zero only for those

such that
|u(t,0,x)|\leq 1
, for every

. Now, according to (

), if

for every

, then

, and we conclude that there exists an open interval

such that

for every

and

if and only if

. Moreover, in this case,

for every

.

In summary, equation (
1.7
) has a center with no limit cycles if

and has at most one limit cycle otherwise. By Proposition
2.3
we obtain the desired result.
∎

Proposition 3.4
.

If
ab\geq 0
and
b\neq 0
, then (
1.7
) has exactly one limit cycle, the symmetric periodic solution
u^{s}(t)
given in Proposition
2.3
. Moreover, the limit cycle is hyperbolic, globally asymptotically stable when

and unstable when

.

Proof.

Recall that, by Proposition
2.2
,
d^{\prime}(x)=e^{2\pi a+(b-a)m(A_{in}(x))}-1
.

If
a=0
, then
d^{\prime}(x)=e^{b\,m(A_{in}(x))}-1
. So, since

, we have

and, for

, it is zero only for those

such that
|u(t,0,x)|\geq 1
, for every

. Therefore, by (
2.3
) or (
2.5
) as appropriate, we conclude that
d(x)=-2\pi b\operatorname{sgn}(x)
for those

such that
|u(t,0,x)|\geqslant 1
for

. Thus, when
b\neq 0
, it is easy to see that equation (
1.7
) has at most one limit cycle
u(t,0,\bar{x})
and it is verified
bd^{\prime}(\bar{x})>0
. Thus, from Proposition
2.3
, the limit cycle unique and, moreover, it is hyperbolic, globally asymptotically stable when

and unstable when

.

If

, then

does not vanish because

So, we conclude that

has a constant sign. Therefore, equation (

∎

4.
Finiteness of the number of limit cycles

Let us show that equation (
1.7
) has finitely many limit cycles. Indeed, we will prove that there exists a constant
L^{\ast}\in\mathbb{N}
, which does not depend on
a,b
and
\mu
, such that equation (
1.7
) has no more than
L^{\ast}
limit cycles.

Obviously, the one-region case of (
1.7
) has at most one limit cycle, since it corresponds to a linear differential equation. The general linear piecewise equation with two zones,
x^{\prime}=f(t,x)
, where
f
is linear and piecewise with respect to
x
and
2\pi
-periodic with respect to
t
, is known to can have any number of limit cycles, as shown in
[
6
,
18
]
. However, there are certain families for which finiteness of limit cycles occurs (see
[
23
,
5
]
). In particular, it is known that the two-region equations of the form
x^{\prime}=g(x)+\mu\sin t
, where
g
is a continuous piecewise linear function with two zones of linearity, have at most two limit cycles (see
[
17
]
).

Therefore, we only need to prove that equation (
1.7
) has finitely many isolated periodic solutions passing through all three zones. To avoid dealing with singular periodic solutions, we consider equation (
1.7
) perturbed by
\lambda
, that is, the differential equation

(4.1)

x^{\prime}=f(x)+\mu\sin(t)+\lambda,

where
f(x)
is defined as in (
1.8
). As the corresponding displacement function depends monotonously on
\lambda
, if
d(x,\lambda)
denotes the displacement function fixing
a,b,\mu
, and varying
\lambda
, then
d(x,\lambda)=0
defines implicitly a function
\lambda(x)
, that is, for every
x\in\mathbb{R}
there is a unique value
\lambda(x)
such that
d(x,\lambda(x))=0
. Moreover, as
x\to d(x,\lambda)
is differentiable in
\mathbb{R}
and analytic except for the finite values corresponding to solutions with a tangent to the lines
x=\pm 1

(see e.g.
[
6
]
), the same holds for
\lambda(x)
.
Let
\lambda(x_{0})=\lambda^{\prime}(x_{0})=0
and assume that
x_{0}
is a maximum (minimum point) of
\lambda(x)
. Then, there exists a neighborhood of
(x_{0},0)
such that for every
\lambda<0
(
\lambda>0
),
d(x,\lambda)=0
has two non-singular solutions. If
x_{0}
is neither a maximum nor a minimum, then for each
\lambda\neq 0
,
d(x,\lambda)=0
has one non-singular solution.
Therefore, it is possible to state the following result.

Lemma 4.1
.

The number of limit cycles of equation (
1.7
) is less than or equal to the number of non-singular periodic solutions of equation (
4.1
).

Before stating our finiteness theorem, we introduce a useful technical result for its proof.

Lemma 4.2
.

Consider
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
and denote by
u(t,\tau,x,\lambda)
the maximal solution of the differential equation (
4.1
) determined by the initial condition
u(\tau,\tau,x,\lambda)=x
. If
u(t,0,x,\lambda)
is a periodic solution of equation (
4.1
) that crosses both
x=1
and
x=-1
, then there exist
t_{1}<t_{2}<t_{3}<t_{4}<t_{1}+2\pi
,
t_{1}\in[0,2\pi)
, such that

(4.2)

\begin{split}e^{-bt_{1}}(g(t_{1},b)+\lambda)+e^{-bt_{2}}(g(t_{2}+\pi,b)-\lambda)=0;\\
e^{-at_{2}}(g(t_{2}+\pi,a)-\lambda)-e^{-at_{3}}(g(t_{3}+\pi,a)-\lambda)=0;\\
e^{-bt_{3}}(g(t_{3}+\pi,b)-\lambda)+e^{-bt_{4}}(g(t_{4},b)+\lambda)=0;\\
e^{-at_{1}}(g(t_{1},a)+\lambda)-e^{-a(t_{4}-2\pi)}(g(t_{4},a)+\lambda)=0,\end{split}

where
g(t,s):=\Big(\mu s\big(s\sin(t)+\cos(t)\big)+b(s^{2}+1)\Big)/(s^{2}+1)
.

Proof.

If
u(t,0,x,\lambda)
is a periodic solution of equation (
4.1
) that crosses both
x=1
and
x=-1
, then there exist
t_{1}<t_{2}<t_{3}<t_{4}<t_{1}+2\pi
,
t_{1}\in[0,2\pi)
, such that

|u(t,t_{1},1,\lambda)|<1
for
t\in(t_{1},t_{2})\cup(t_{3},t_{4})
,
u(t,t_{1},1,\lambda)<-1
for
t\in(t_{2},t_{3})
,
u(t,t_{1},1,\lambda)>1
for
t\in(t_{4},t_{1}+2\pi)
, and

(4.3)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rcr}u(t_{2},t_{1},1,\lambda)&=&-1\\
u(t_{3},t_{2},-1,\lambda)&=&-1\\
u(t_{4},t_{3},-1,\lambda)&=&1\\
u(t_{1}+2\pi,t_{4},1,\lambda)&=&1\end{array}\right.

Now, using properly the expression of the solution (
2.2
) for the linear equation (
2.1
) in each linearity zone, one can easily check that system (
4.3
) is equivalent to system (
4.2
).
∎

Theorem 4.3
.

There exists a constant
L^{\ast}\in\mathbb{N}
, which does not depend on
a,b
and
\mu
, such that equation (
1.7
) has no more than
L^{\ast}
limit cycles.

Proof.

Note, firstly, that by means of Theorem
3.1
, equation (
1.7
) has at most one limit cycle when
ab\geqslant 0
. Therefore, by virtue of Lemma
4.1
, to prove the finiteness of the number of limit cycles for equation (
1.7
), we must prove the finiteness of the number of non–singular periodic solutions of equation (
4.1
) for the case
ab<0
. Note that for
\mu=0
, equation (
4.1
) does not have non-constant periodic solutions.

Taking into account that a linear differential equation in the form (
2.1
) has at most one non-singular periodic solution, equation (
4.1
) has at most three one-zonal non-singular periodic solutions (that is, each of them lives in a zone of linearity). From Theorem 4.19 of
[
17
]
, equation (
4.1
) has at most four two-zonal non-singular periodic solutions (each of them only visits two zones of linearity). Therefore, to prove the finiteness of the number of non-singular periodic solutions for equation (
4.1
) for the case
ab<0
, we must prove the finiteness of the number non-singular periodic solutions of equation (
4.1
) that cross both
x=1
and
x=-1
.

From Lemma
4.2
, each periodic solution of (
4.1
) that crosses both
x=1
and
x=-1
corresponds to a solution of system (
4.2
) which is a system of four equations in four real unknowns
t_{1},\ldots,t_{4}
, where the equations are polynomials of degree
2
in the following sixteen real variables
y_{i1}=e^{-at_{i}},y_{i2}=e^{-bt_{i}},u_{i}=\sin(t_{i})
and
v_{i}=\cos(t_{i}),\ i\in\{1,2,3,4\}
. So, by Khovanskiĭ’s Theorem (see, e.g.,
[
26
, p. 14]
), we conclude that the number of non-singular (in particular, isolated) real solutions of (

) is upper bounded independently of
\lambda,a,b
and
\mu
and the conclusion follows.
∎

5.
Number of limit cycles for extreme values of
\mu

According to Theorem
3.1
, possible bifurcations can only occur if
ab
is strictly negative. Thus, assuming
ab<0
, we show that there are exactly three limit cycles for
|\mu|
close to zero (Theorem
5.4
), and exactly one non-singular limit cycle for
|\mu|
sufficiently large (Theorem

).

Let us start with a useful lemma.

Lemma 5.1
.

Consider equation (
2.1
) with
p\neq 0
. The only periodic solution of equation (
2.1
) is

(5.1)

v(t)=-\frac{q}{p}-\frac{\mu(\cos(t)+p\sin(t))}{p^{2}+1}.

Moreover, this periodic solution is non-singular (that is, hyperbolic), globally asymptotically stable when
p<0
, and unstable when
p>0
.

Proof.

From expression (
2.2
), one can deduce that the Poincaré map for equation (
2.1
) is given by the linear map

\tilde{P}(x):=v(2\pi,0,x)=\frac{\left(e^{2\pi p}-1\right)\left(p^{2}+1\right)q+e^{2\pi p}p\left(\mu+p^{2}x+x\right)-\mu p}{p^{3}+p}

which, for
p\neq 0
, has a unique fixed point
\bar{x}=-q/p-\mu\left(p^{2}+1\right)^{-1}
. This fixed point provides the unique solution periodic
v(t):=v(t,0,\bar{x})
given in (
5.1
). Since the derivative of
P
is
\tilde{P}^{\prime}(x)=e^{2\pi p}
, the conclusion of the hyperbolicity and stability of the periodic solution

is direct.
∎

Due to the previous lemma, we can determine when equation (
1.7
) has a periodic solution in the regions
x\geq 1
and
x\leq-1
.

Proposition 5.2
.

Consider equation (
1.7
) with
ab<0
. The following statements are equivalent.

(1)

There exists a periodic solution
u^{+}
of equation (
1.7
) such that
u^{+}(t)\geq 1
for all

.

(2)

There exists a periodic solution
u^{-}
of equation (
1.7
) such that
u^{-}(t)\leq-1
for all

.

(3)

|\mu|\leqslant-b\sqrt{a^{2}+1}/a
.

In these cases, the periodic solutions are the only ones in the corresponding zone of linearity; specifically,

(5.2)

u^{+}(t)=\frac{a-b}{a}-\frac{\mu(\cos(t)+a\sin(t))}{a^{2}+1}\quad\text{and}\quad u^{-}(t)=-u^{+}(t+\pi).

In addition, these periodic solutions are hyperbolic, asymptotically stable when
b>0
, and unstable when
b<0
.

Proof.

Since the equivalence of items (1) and (2) follows by symmetry, it suffices to prove the equivalence of items (1) and (3).

If equation (
1.7
) possesses a periodic solution
u^{+}
such that
u^{+}(t)\geqslant 1
for all
t\in\mathbb{R}
, then the solution

is a periodic solution of the linear differential equation

. Thus, by Lemma

, the expression for the periodic solution

is provided in (

and

in the periodic solution

of (

).

A straightforward computation indicates that the global minimum of the function

given in (

H:=\frac{a-b}{a}-\frac{|\mu|}{\sqrt{1+a^{2}}}.

Therefore, the function
u^{+}
is a periodic solution of (
1.7
) if and only if

, which is equivalent to the inequality of item (3). The expression for the periodic solution
u^{-}
follows by symmetry, and, since

, the conclusion for the hyperbolicity and stability of the periodic solutions is a direct consequence of Lemma
5.1
.
∎

Next, we study when limit cycles of equation (
1.7
) exist in the zone
|x|\leqslant 1
. The proof of the next result is similar to the proof of Proposition
5.2
and is omitted.

Proposition 5.3
.

Consider equation (
1.7
) with
b\neq 0
. There exists a isolated periodic solution

of equation (

) such that
|u(t)|\leqslant 1

which is the symmetric periodic solution

given in Proposition

. In addition, this periodic solution is hyperbolic, asymptotically stable when

and unstable when

.

Summarizing, we can state our result for the existence of three limit cycles for equation (

Theorem 5.4
.

Suppose that
ab<0
. Equation (
1.7
) has exactly three limit cycles, each of them located in a zone of linearity, if and only if

|\mu|\leq\min\left\{\frac{-b\sqrt{a^{2}+1}}{a},\sqrt{b^{2}+1}\right\}.

and

are asymptotically stable when

and unstable when

, and the limit cycle contained in the region

To conclude this section, we prove the uniqueness of limit cycles for
|\mu|
sufficiently large, where the size depends on parameters

.

Theorem 5.5
.

Suppose that
ab<0
.
There exists a value
\bar{\mu}=\bar{\mu}(a,b)>0
such that if

, then equation (

) has only one limit cycle

. Moreover, this limit cycle is symmetric, hyperbolic, globally asymptotically stable when

Proof.

First, note that periodic solutions of equation (

. For

transforms equation (

(5.3)

X^{\prime}=g(X)+\operatorname{sgn}(\mu)\sin t

6.

Define

In this section, we compute the first order Melnikov function or averaging function for the deformation (

Theorem 6.1
.

Let
a
and

be fixed such that

(6.1)

\mu_{1}:=\frac{c}{\sin(c)}\,,\qquad\mu_{2}:=\frac{1}{\cos(c/2)}\,,\qquad\mbox{with}\qquad c:=\frac{\pi b}{b-a}\,.

Then
0<\mu_{1}<\mu_{2}
and, for each

(1)

if

(2)

if

(3)

6.1.
Melnikov function: general properties.

Let us write

for the solution of equation (
1.9

u(0,x,\mu,\epsilon)=x
.

For
\epsilon=0
, equation (

u(t,x,\mu,0)=\mu(1-\cos t)+x,

for every

.

The change of variable

transforms the differential equation (

x^{\prime}=\varepsilon f(\mu(1-\cos t)+x),

and, therefore, the study of the periodic solutions of equation (

) for

sufficiently small can be carried out by means of the averaging theory (or Menilkov theory) recently developed for non–smooth systems in works as
[
10
,
28
,
29
,
30
,
34
]
, and also applied to Abel equation by Neto
[
33
]
. Thus, for instance, from Theorem A in
[

]
, one can deduce that the zeros of the first-order averaging function

with non–zero Brouwer degree provide limit cycles of equation (
1.9
) for
|\varepsilon|
sufficiently small. Notice that the derivative with respect to
\varepsilon
of the solution
u(t,x,\mu,\varepsilon)
for

and
\varepsilon=0
coincides with the averaging function
\mathcal{M}
defined in (
