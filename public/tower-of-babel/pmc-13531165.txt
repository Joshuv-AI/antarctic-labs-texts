Mutual Information–based Prognostic Biomarker Discovery in Cancer Genomics: Conceptual Framework and Representative Applications of MI-POG
Authors: KAZUNORI AKIMOTO, SHOMA TAMORI, KEIKO SATO
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Mutual information (MI)-based approaches have increasingly been applied to cancer genomics; however, their use for genome-wide prognostic biomarker discovery remains relatively underexplored. The present article summarizes the conceptual workflow of Mutual Information-based Prognostic Omics Gene (MI-POG) based on previously published applications in breast cancer, lower-grade glioma, and other cancer datasets. The framework consists of clinical endpoint discretization, genome-wide MI-based screening, candidate ranking, and downstream validation using conventional survival-analysis approaches. Previous MI-POG applications identified solute carrier family 20 member 1 (SLC20A1) as a prognostic biomarker in hormone receptor-positive breast cancer. Elevated SLC20A1 expression was associated with unfavorable survival outcomes and was independently validated in the Molecular Taxonomy of Breast Cancer International Consortium (METABRIC) cohort. Methodological analyses demonstrated how survival endpoints can be integrated into an information-theoretic framework through fixed-time outcome discretization, enabling model-independent assessment of molecular–clinical dependencies. Applications across multiple cancer datasets suggested the potential applicability of the framework across biologically distinct tumor types, although further validation will be required to establish its robustness and generalizability. In conclusion, MI-POG can be formalized as an information-theoretic framework for genome-wide identification of prognostic biomarkers by quantifying molecular–clinical dependencies using mutual information. Representative applications from previously published studies suggest that MI-POG may complement conventional survival-analysis approaches and provide a useful strategy for biomarker discovery, although additional benchmarking and prospective validation will be required.

Introduction

Cancer genomics has reshaped modern oncology by enabling molecular classification, prognostic stratification, and therapy selection based on tumor-specific genomic characteristics rather than histopathological features alone (1-3). Large-scale initiatives such as The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) and other comprehensive sequencing efforts have delineated the mutational and transcriptomic landscapes of diverse malignancies (4). As high-throughput technologies generate increasingly complex multi-omics datasets, the central challenge in precision oncology has shifted from data acquisition to extracting clinically meaningful information from high-dimensional molecular data (4, 5). Identifying robust and biologically interpretable prognostic biomarkers from such datasets therefore remains a major objective in cancer research. From a biological perspective, such prognostic signals ultimately reflect cancer’s core capabilities, such as sustained proliferation, survival, invasion, and therapy resistance, conceptualized as the “hallmarks of cancer” framework (6). Breast cancer exemplifies the clinical and molecular heterogeneity revealed by cancer genomics (7, 8). It remains the most frequently diagnosed malignancy among women worldwide and a leading cause of cancer-related mortality (9). Clinical classification based on estrogen receptor (ER), progesterone receptor, and HER2 expression stratifies tumors into hormone receptor–positive, HER2-positive, and triple-negative subtypes (10, 11), while transcriptomic profiling further subdivides breast cancer into intrinsic molecular subtypes including Luminal A, Luminal B, HER2-enriched, Basal-like, Claudin-low, and Normal-like subtypes (12-15).

Among these, hormone receptor–positive (HR+) tumors account for approximately 70-80% of breast cancers (9, 10). Although HR-positive breast cancer generally shows favorable short-term outcomes with endocrine therapy, late recurrence occurring 10-20 years after diagnosis remains a persistent clinical challenge (16-18). Existing multigene assays, such as Oncotype DX, MammaPrint, and PAM50, improve early recurrence risk stratification but are primarily optimized for predicting recurrence within the first 5-10 years after diagnosis (19-23). Consequently, reliable genomic markers capable of predicting very late recurrence remain limited. Prognostic biomarker discovery in cancer genomics has traditionally relied on statistical approaches such as correlation analysis, log-rank testing, and Cox proportional hazards regression modeling (24-26), together with diagnostic tests for evaluating the proportional hazards assumption (27). More recently, high-dimensional prognostic modeling strategies, including penalized Cox regression, stability-selection frameworks, random survival forests, and deep-learning survival models, have been introduced to improve risk prediction in complex genomic datasets (28-32). While these approaches have substantially advanced prognostic modeling, they remain primarily oriented toward predictive model construction and risk stratification. Furthermore, challenges remain in identifying biologically interpretable molecular features that exhibit robust associations with clinical outcomes, particularly when such relationships are nonlinear, context-dependent, or embedded within high-dimensional genomic data (5, 26, 28-30, 33, 34).

Information-theoretic approaches offer a complementary analytical strategy for exploring complex biological datasets. Mutual information (MI), originally formulated within information theory by Shannon (35-37), quantifies the total statistical dependency between variables without assuming linear relationships or predefined model structures. MI has been widely applied in computational biology for tasks such as gene regulatory network inference, feature selection, and nonlinear association detection (38-46). Despite these developments, the systematic application of MI-based approaches to direct genome-wide discovery of prognostic biomarkers linked to clinical outcomes remains comparatively underexplored. In this context, genome-wide Mutual Information-based Prognostic Omics Gene (MI-POG)–based screening of TCGA breast cancer transcriptomic data identified solute carrier family 20 member 1 (SLC20A1) as a prognostic biomarker in hormone receptor–positive breast cancer (47). Subsequent validation studies in independent cohorts, including Molecular Taxonomy of Breast Cancer International Consortium (METABRIC), together with functional analyses in cancer cell models, supported the clinical and biological relevance of SLC20A1 in tumor progression, therapeutic response, and late recurrence (48-50). These findings provide empirical evidence that mutual information–based genome-wide screening can uncover clinically meaningful molecular–clinical dependencies that may not be readily detectable through conventional statistical frameworks. This article proposes the MI-POG framework as an application-oriented strategy for genome-wide identification of gene–clinical outcome dependencies in cancer genomics. The concept is supported by empirical findings from MI-based screening that identified SLC20A1 as a clinically relevant prognostic biomarker.

Despite increasing interest in information-theoretic approaches, the application of mutual information to genome-wide prognostic biomarker discovery in cancer genomics remains relatively underexplored. In the present article, we synthesize findings from our previously published studies and propose MI-POG as a unified information-theoretic framework for genome-wide prognostic biomarker discovery. Rather than reporting a new primary analysis, this article aims to formalize the conceptual basis, analytical workflow, and representative applications of MI-POG, and to position the framework within the broader landscape of mutual information–based methodologies in cancer genomics. The biological findings discussed herein, including SLC20A1 in breast cancer, CD58 in lower-grade glioma, and GGT6 in melanoma, are presented as illustrative examples derived from previously published studies.

Framework Description of MI-POG

The following section describes the conceptual workflow of MI-POG and summarizes methodological elements that were implemented in previously published applications.

Data sources. The MI-POG framework was originally developed and applied to publicly available cancer transcriptomic datasets, including breast cancer and lower-grade glioma cohorts (47, 51). In breast cancer studies, gene expression and clinical outcome data were obtained from TCGA (4) and validated using the METABRIC cohort (8), as described in the original MI-POG studies (47-50). In lower-grade glioma studies, publicly available transcriptomic and clinical datasets were analyzed as previously described (51). Genome-wide gene expression profiles and corresponding clinical outcome data were obtained from publicly accessible repositories.

Clinical outcome definition and survival endpoint discretization. To enable MI-based dependency analysis, time-to-event clinical outcomes were transformed into fixed-time categorical variables. In the original breast cancer application, patients were classified into predefined survival categories according to clinical outcome status after diagnosis (47). Similarly, in lower-grade glioma analyses, patients were categorized according to survival status at a predefined time point (51). Patients lacking sufficient follow-up information to assign outcome status were excluded from MI calculations. This transformation enabled integration of survival-related clinical information into an information-theoretic framework (35, 36).

Mutual information-based screening. Mutual information was used to quantify statistical dependency between gene expression variables and clinical outcome categories. In the original breast cancer MI-POG application, mutual information was estimated using discretized gene-expression values and categorical clinical outcome variables (47). Gene expression status was classified using predefined thresholds based on normalized expression values. For each gene, MI was calculated between gene expression status and clinical outcome categories across all eligible patients.

The mutual information value for gene was defined as:

MI(X;Yi)=∑x∈X∑y∈Yip(x,y)log2p(x,y)p(x)p(y)

where the probability distributions p(x), p(y) and p(x, y) were estimated from the observed frequencies of the categories across patients, X represents clinical outcome categories and represents gene-expression categories for gene (35, 36). Genes exhibiting higher MI (X;) were interpreted as showing stronger molecular–clinical dependency and were prioritized as candidate prognostic biomarkers, as described in the original breast cancer and lower-grade glioma applications (47, 51).

Candidate gene ranking and prioritization. Genes were ranked according to their MI values in descending order, and candidate prognostic genes were selected from the highest-ranking genes showing strong dependency with clinical outcomes (47, 51). Mutual information was interpreted primarily as an information-theoretical measure of dependency strength for exploratory ranking purposes, and genes were prioritized according to their relative information content rather than formal statistical significance.

Statistical validation. Candidate genes identified through MI-based screening were subsequently evaluated using conventional survival-analysis approaches. Kaplan–Meier survival analysis, log-rank testing, and Cox proportional hazards regression models were used to assess prognostic significance and estimate effect sizes. In breast cancer applications, multivariable Cox regression analyses adjusting for established clinical prognostic factors, including age, tumor stage, and therapeutic variables such as chemotherapy, radiotherapy, and endocrine therapy, were performed to determine whether MI-POG–identified biomarkers provided prognostic information independent of conventional clinicopathological parameters (47-50). Similarly, in glioma applications, multivariable Cox regression models adjusting for age, gender, IDH1 mutation status, and tumor grade were performed (51).

Independent cohort validation. To evaluate reproducibility, candidate biomarkers identified in discovery datasets were examined in independent patient cohorts. For example, prognostic associations identified in TCGA breast cancer datasets were subsequently evaluated in the METABRIC cohort (48-50). Consistent associations across independent cohorts were considered supportive evidence for biomarker robustness.

Biological interpretation and functional assessment. Genes identified through MI-POG screening were subsequently interpreted using biological knowledge, literature evidence, pathway analysis, and where available, experimental validation (48, 52, 53). The identification of SLC20A1 in hormone receptor-positive breast cancer and CD58 in lower-grade glioma served as proof-of-concept examples demonstrating the translational utility of the MI-POG framework.

Mutual Information in Cancer Genomics: Conceptual Foundations and Existing Methodologies

In cancer genomics, identifying statistical dependencies between molecular features and clinical outcomes is essential for biomarker discovery. Information theory provides a mathematical framework for quantifying uncertainty and statistical dependence between variables. Originally formulated by Shannon in 1948 (35), information theory has become a fundamental conceptual basis for a wide range of computational approaches in cancer genomics and machine learning (36, 37). Among these measures, MI quantifies the degree to which knowledge of one variable reduces uncertainty about another and therefore measures the statistical dependency between variables (35, 37).

In the context of cancer genomics, MI can therefore be used to measure how strongly molecular features such as gene expression levels inform clinical outcomes including survival, recurrence, or treatment response. Unlike correlation-based statistics, MI captures both linear and nonlinear dependencies without assuming a predefined functional relationship between variables (36, 37). This property is particularly relevant in oncology, where complex regulatory networks, tumor–microenvironment interactions, and heterogeneous evolutionary trajectories generate context-dependent molecular–clinical associations. As high-throughput technologies increasingly generate large-scale multi-omics datasets, model-free dependency measures such as MI provide an attractive analytical strategy for exploring relationships that may not be captured by conventional parametric models. Over the past two decades, multiple MI-based computational frameworks have been developed and applied to cancer genomics. Early applications focused primarily on gene regulatory network inference. For example, the Algorithm for the Reconstruction of Accurate Cellular Networks (ARACNe) framework employs mutual information combined with the Data Processing Inequality (DPI) to identify direct gene–gene regulatory interactions while eliminating indirect associations (39). This approach enabled the identification of master regulators in malignancies such as B-cell lymphoma and glioblastoma (40, 41). In parallel, mutual information has been widely applied to feature selection and nonlinear association detection in high-dimensional biological datasets. The minimal Redundancy Maximal Relevance (mRMR) framework utilizes mutual information to select features that maximize relevance to a target variable while minimizing redundancy among selected variables (33). Similarly, the Maximal Information-based Nonparametric Exploration (MINE) framework introduced the maximal information coefficient (MIC), enabling detection of diverse nonlinear associations in large-scale datasets (34). Related mutual-information–based strategies have also been proposed to stabilize multigene prognostic signatures in breast cancer datasets (54). Subsequent methodological developments further extended MI-based analysis through conditional dependency modeling. Partial Mutual Information (PMI) was introduced to quantify dependencies between variables while accounting for potential confounding regulators, thereby reducing spurious correlations arising from shared upstream signals (42). These approaches demonstrated that information-theoretic measures can effectively identify informative relationships in complex biological data that may remain undetected using conventional correlation-based approaches. This capability has motivated the application of mutual-information–based analytical frameworks to cancer biomarker discovery (55).

More recent studies have further expanded the application of MI-based methods beyond transcriptomic data. Algorithms such as MIAMI and consensus mutual information (CoMI) have been developed to analyze multiplex imaging and cross-dataset transcriptomic data, illustrating the flexibility of MI-based frameworks across diverse cancer datasets (46, 56). In addition, mutual-information-based radiomic feature selection has been applied to imaging-derived datasets to improve diagnostic classification and biomarker discovery in breast cancer (57). More recently, mutual information–based approaches have been extended to single-cell transcriptomic analysis, further broadening the applicability of information-theoretic frameworks in cancer genomics. The scMINER framework applies mutual information–based network inference to single-cell RNA-seq data to reconstruct transcriptional regulatory networks and identify hidden driver genes from heterogeneous cellular populations (58). Collectively, these developments demonstrate that information-theoretic approaches have become important tools in cancer genomics, enabling analysis of complex dependency structures across molecular and phenotypic data. These approaches are primarily designed for prognostic model construction and outcome prediction (28-32). Mutual information offers a complementary analytical strategy. Rather than constructing predictive models, MI quantifies the total statistical dependency between variables without imposing assumptions regarding linearity, proportional hazards, or distributional form (35-37). These methods estimate effect sizes and hazard ratios under explicit statistical assumptions, including proportional hazards and linear relationships between predictors and outcomes. While these approaches remain foundational in translational oncology, violations of such assumptions may limit their ability to capture nonlinear molecular–clinical dependencies in high-dimensional genomic datasets (26, 28, 59). MI-POG differs conceptually from penalized regression and machine-learning survival models in that its primary objective is feature discovery rather than predictive model construction. Within this broader methodological landscape, MI-POG–based genome-wide screening provides an opportunity to systematically explore molecular–clinical dependencies across the transcriptome. However, the application of mutual information specifically to genome-wide prognostic biomarker discovery linked to survival outcomes has remained relatively limited. Most existing MI-based algorithms focus primarily on gene–gene regulatory relationships or feature selection tasks, rather than directly quantifying gene–clinical outcome dependencies across the transcriptome. To overcome this limitation, MI-POG was developed as an application-oriented extension of information-theoretic methodologies designed to quantify gene–clinical outcome dependency across large-scale cancer genomics datasets. Conceptual framework of MI-POG. Despite the growing use of mutual information in cancer genomics, its systematic application to genome-wide discovery of gene–clinical outcome dependencies has remained limited. To bridge this methodological gap, we propose the MI-POG as a structured strategy for prognostic biomarker discovery in cancer genomics. Building upon the information-theoretic concepts outlined above, MI-POG applies genome-wide MI-based screening to quantify statistical dependencies between molecular features and clinical outcomes. Unlike many existing mutual-information–based algorithms that focus on gene–gene network inference or redundancy-aware feature selection, MI-POG is specifically designed to extract molecular features associated with clinical outcomes, thereby enabling systematic discovery of prognostic biomarkers from high-dimensional omics datasets. Conceptually, MI-POG operates as a model-independent screening layer that precedes conventional survival analysis. A schematic illustration of this analytical concept is shown in Figure 1. Within the broader ecosystem of mutual-information–based computational methodologies, MI-POG should be understood as an application-oriented extension rather than a fundamentally new information-theoretic measure. Established MI algorithms such as ARACNe, mRMR, and MINE were primarily developed for network reconstruction, feature selection, or nonlinear association detection. In contrast, MI-POG specifically targets genome-wide identification of gene–clinical outcome dependencies, positioning mutual information as a screening strategy for prognostic biomarker discovery. Methodological workflow of the MI-POG framework. The MI-POG framework is an information-theoretic workflow for genome-wide discovery of prognostic biomarkers. Gene-expression and clinical outcome data are first integrated, and time-to-event outcomes are transformed into fixed-time binary variables using a predefined landmark-based discretization approach. Patients with indeterminate outcome status at the selected landmark time, including those censored before the landmark or lacking sufficient follow-up information, are excluded from mutual information analysis. Mutual information is then calculated between each gene and the clinical outcome to quantify gene–outcome dependency in a model-independent manner. Genes are ranked according to their mutual information values and prioritized as candidate prognostic biomarkers. Candidate genes are subsequently evaluated using Kaplan–Meier survival analysis and Cox proportional hazards regression in independent cohorts. Finally, biological interpretation and functional validation are performed to assess the potential clinical and biological relevance of the identified biomarkers.

Clinical outcome transformation for MI analysis. To enable MI-based analysis, time-to-event clinical outcomes are transformed into fixed-time binary variables (47, 51). In MI-POG, survival endpoints such as overall survival (OS) or recurrence-free survival (RFS) are converted using a predefined landmark time (e.g., 5 or 10 years). Patients are classified according to their survival status at the selected landmark time. Individuals who have experienced the event of interest (death or recurrence) before the landmark time are labeled as event-positive, whereas those confirmed to be event-free beyond the landmark time are labeled as event-negative. Patients with indeterminate survival status at the landmark time, such as those lost to follow-up before the landmark or without sufficient follow-up information to determine status at the landmark, are excluded because a unique binary outcome cannot be assigned under this fixed-time binary transformation. This transformation enables direct application of MI-based dependency analysis to clinical outcome variables (35-37, 47, 51). MI requires discrete outcome categories for quantification of gene–outcome dependency (35-37). Therefore, conversion of survival endpoints into binary variables provides a practical strategy for integrating time-to-event clinical data with information-theoretic screening approaches (35-37, 47, 51). A consistent landmark time is applied across discovery and validation cohorts to ensure comparability of MI-based dependency scores across studies (47, 51). Although this fixed-time transformation enables MI-based dependency analysis, it inevitably reduces the amount of survival information available for analysis (26). Specifically, the exact timing of events occurring before the landmark time is not retained, and patients experiencing events at different times prior to the landmark are treated equivalently (26). Furthermore, the binary representation does not explicitly model censoring processes or hazard functions (25, 26). Consequently, MI-POG should be viewed as a feature-screening framework designed to identify candidate molecular–clinical dependencies, whereas detailed prognostic evaluation remains dependent on subsequent survival-analysis methods such as Kaplan–Meier estimation and Cox proportional hazards regression (24-26, 47, 51).

MI-based screening and candidate prioritization. In MI-POG, MI is used to quantify the statistical dependency between individual gene expression variables and discretized clinical outcome variables defined using the fixed-time binary transformation described above (47, 51). MI provides a model-free measure of association that does not assume linearity or a specific functional form between variables (35-37). For each gene, MI is computed between its expression values and the binary clinical outcome variable. In the original breast cancer MI-POG application, mutual information was estimated using discretized gene-expression values and binary clinical outcome variables (47). Although alternative estimators, including kernel-density and k-nearest-neighbor methods, have been proposed for MI estimation (36, 60), the original MI-POG studies employed a discretization-based approach. Gene expression values are preprocessed using standard normalization procedures prior to MI estimation. The resulting MI values represent the strength of dependency between each gene and the clinical endpoint (36, 47, 51). Genes exhibiting strong molecular–clinical dependency are prioritized as candidate prognostic markers and subsequently evaluated using established statistical approaches, including Kaplan–Meier analysis and Cox proportional hazards regression (24-26, 47, 51). This two-stage strategy allows nonlinear dependencies to be detected during the initial screening step while maintaining compatibility with conventional clinical survival modeling for downstream validation (24-26, 35-37, 47, 51). The analytical workflow of MI-POG therefore consists of three principal steps. First, genome-wide transcriptomic datasets are analyzed to quantify MI between individual genes and clinical outcome variables (47, 51). Second, candidate genes are ranked according to their MI values and subjected to statistical validation using independent patient cohorts (47-51). Finally, biological and functional investigations are performed to determine whether the identified genes contribute mechanistically to tumor progression, therapeutic response, or disease recurrence (48-50, 52). Through this workflow, MI-POG integrates information-theoretic screening with conventional translational cancer research pipelines (35-37, 47-51, 52).

Proof-of-concept applications of MI-POG. The feasibility of this approach has been illustrated in previously published MI-POG–based screening of cancer transcriptomic datasets. In hormone receptor–positive breast cancer, analysis of the TCGA breast cancer dataset identified candidate prognostic genes based on MI–derived molecular–clinical dependency scores (47), which were subsequently validated in independent patient cohorts including METABRIC (48-50). These findings provided an initial demonstration that MI-POG–based genome-wide screening can identify clinically meaningful prognostic markers from high-dimensional genomic datasets. Importantly, these findings suggest that the MI-POG framework may be broadly applicable across different malignancies, although the specific prognostic genes identified are likely to vary according to tumor biology. For example, direct MI-based screening in lower-grade glioma identified a distinct set of survival-associated genes capable of stratifying patient prognosis across independent datasets (51). Thus, MI-POG may provide a general framework for prognostic biomarker discovery while capturing tumor-specific molecular–clinical dependencies. These observations illustrate the conceptual role of MI-POG as a framework for systematic discovery of tumor-specific prognostic biomarkers from large-scale molecular datasets.

The identification of SLC20A1 as a prognostic gene in breast cancer provides a representative proof-of-concept example of how MI-based genome-wide screening can generate clinically relevant biomarkers and support the MI-POG framework. Among the candidate genes identified through MI-POG–based screening, SLC20A1, which encodes a sodium-dependent phosphate transporter (61, 62), emerged as a gene showing strong statistical dependency with survival outcomes. Subsequent analyses demonstrated that elevated SLC20A1 expression was significantly associated with poorer clinical outcomes in breast cancer cohorts (47). These findings suggested that MI-based genome-wide screening can identify prognostic signals embedded within high-dimensional transcriptomic datasets that may not be readily detected using conventional statistical approaches alone. Importantly, the prognostic relevance of SLC20A1 was subsequently evaluated in independent patient cohorts. Validation analyses using the METABRIC breast cancer dataset confirmed that increased SLC20A1 expression was associated with unfavorable prognosis, demonstrating the reproducibility of the MI-based screening results across independent transcriptomic datasets (48-50). These cross-cohort validations provided supportive evidence that MI-POG–driven feature extraction may identify clinically meaningful molecular markers with prognostic relevance.

Notably, further analyses suggested that the clinical significance of SLC20A1 may be particularly relevant in the context of late recurrence in hormone receptor–positive breast cancer, where tumor relapse can occur many years after initial treatment. Unlike many prognostic markers primarily associated with early disease progression, elevated SLC20A1 expression appeared to be linked with increased risk of long-term recurrence, indicating that this gene may capture molecular processes related to persistent tumor cell survival and delayed disease progression (48-50). Taken together, the identification and validation of SLC20A1 illustrate how information-theoretic genome-wide screening can reveal clinically relevant molecular–clinical dependencies within large cancer transcriptomic datasets.

Importantly, SLC20A1 should not be interpreted as a universal biomarker across all malignancies; rather, it represents a tumor-specific prognostic signal identified through MI-based dependency analysis. As such, this example demonstrates how MI-POG can facilitate systematic discovery of cancer-type–specific prognostic genes, integrating information-theoretic feature screening with conventional survival modeling and downstream biological investigation. In addition, SLC20A1, originally identified through MI-POG–based screening in breast cancer, has independently been reported to be associated with adverse prognosis in pancreatic and prostate cancers (52, 63). Importantly, the clinical relevance of SLC20A1 is supported beyond its original identification through MI-POG–based screening in breast cancer. High SLC20A1 expression has also been associated with unfavorable prognosis in pancreatic cancer by independent studies through subsequent investigations using analytical approaches distinct from MI-POG (64, 65). These observations support the broader biological and clinical significance of SLC20A1 across multiple malignancies. Although these findings do not represent a systematic implementation of MI-POG in those tumor types, they provide supportive evidence that genes identified through the MI-POG framework may reflect broader biological processes relevant to tumor progression across malignancies.

Expanding Biomarker Discovery and Tumor-specific Applications Using the MI-POG Framework

Single-gene markers are unlikely to fully capture the molecular complexity underlying cancer progression and recurrence. Within MI-POG, genome-wide MI screening can be extended beyond individual genes to identify multigene prognostic signatures that collectively reflect molecular–clinical dependency structures within tumor datasets. Because MI-POG leverages mutual information to quantify total statistical dependency without assuming linearity or proportional hazards, this framework may capture nonlinear interactions and combinatorial molecular effects that are not readily detected by conventional regression-based survival models. In practice, candidate gene sets derived from MI-POG–based screening require downstream biological interpretation. Functional annotation, pathway enrichment analysis, and network-based approaches can help identify biological processes associated with the extracted gene signatures. Integration of MI-POG–based feature screening with established survival modeling frameworks, including Kaplan–Meier analysis and Cox proportional hazards regression, may further improve clinical interpretability, provided that appropriate discretization strategies, bias correction procedures, and cross-cohort validation are implemented (26, 36, 59, 60). Beyond transcriptomic datasets, the MI-POG strategy may also be extended to multimodal cancer data, including radiomic features, proteogenomic measurements, and spatially resolved molecular platforms. These emerging data modalities offer opportunities to characterize tumor heterogeneity and microenvironmental interactions that cannot be captured through gene expression analysis alone. Although systematic implementation of MI-POG–based prognostic screening across these data types remains limited, continued integration of the MI-POG framework with multi-omics cancer datasets may facilitate broader discovery of clinically relevant biomarkers across diverse tumor contexts.

Methodological Considerations for MI-POG–based Prognostic Screening

Despite these strengths, the application of MI to high-dimensional cancer genomics datasets entails several important methodological considerations. Accurate estimation of MI requires appropriate selection of estimation strategies, including discretization-based approaches, kernel density estimation, or k-nearest neighbor methods (36, 37). The choice of estimator and discretization scheme can substantially influence the calculated dependency between molecular variables and clinical outcomes, particularly when the underlying distributions are heterogeneous or nonlinear. Another important issue arises from finite-sample bias, which may occur when the number of molecular features substantially exceeds the number of available patient samples. In such settings, MI estimates may be inflated or unstable due to sparse sampling of high-dimensional feature space (60). To mitigate these effects, bias-correction procedures such as permutation testing, entropy-based adjustments, and appropriate feature filtering strategies are often applied to improve the robustness of dependency estimation (36, 60). Although these procedures were not part of the original MI-POG workflow, permutation-based significance assessment and false-discovery-rate (FDR) correction may serve as useful complementary approaches in future applications for evaluating the robustness of MI-based rankings against random associations, particularly in high-dimensional genomic datasets involving large-scale genome-wide screening (60, 66). Finally, as with regression-based survival modeling, independent cohort validation and transparent reporting of analytical pipelines will be essential to ensure the reproducibility and clinical interpretability of MI-POG–derived biomarkers. Although mutual information is theoretically model-independent, its practical implementation in cancer genomics requires methodological rigor comparable to that required for conventional statistical modeling frameworks. Importantly, MI-POG should not be regarded as a direct replacement for modern prognostic modeling approaches such as penalized Cox regression, stability-selection pipelines, random survival forests, or deep-learning survival models (28-32). These methods are primarily optimized for risk prediction and prognostic model construction, whereas MI-POG is intended as a model-independent feature-discovery framework (28-32). Consequently, MI-POG may be viewed as an upstream screening layer that can complement downstream statistical and machine-learning approaches by identifying candidate molecular features prior to predictive modeling and validation (28-32, 47, 51).

Study limitations. Although MI provides a flexible and model-independent framework for identifying molecular–clinical dependencies, several limitations should be considered when applying MI-based approaches to cancer genomics. First, reliable estimation of MI in high-dimensional datasets remains challenging. In cancer genomics, the number of molecular features frequently exceeds the number of available patient samples, which can lead to unstable or inflated dependency estimates. Second, reproducibility across datasets remains an important challenge. Unlike conventional survival modeling, standardized analytical pipelines for MI-POG–based prognostic screening have yet to be widely established. Differences in preprocessing, normalization, discretization, and estimator implementation may lead to inconsistent results across studies. Development of harmonized computational workflows, similar to those established for genomic classifiers, will likely be necessary to ensure reproducibility and facilitate cross-cohort comparisons. Third, clinical translation of MI-derived biomarkers remains limited. Most currently reported MI-POG–derived prognostic findings, including those involving SLC20A1, are based primarily on retrospective analyses of publicly available transcriptomic datasets. Prospective multi-institutional validation will therefore be required to establish clinical utility. Furthermore, translation into routine oncology practice will require evaluation of cost-effectiveness, assay standardization, and regulatory considerations. Collectively, these limitations highlight the importance of rigorous validation, standardized analytical pipelines, and multidisciplinary collaboration for advancing MI-POG–based biomarker discovery toward clinical application.

Future perspectives. Improving prediction of late recurrence in hormone receptor–positive breast cancer remains a major unmet challenge in clinical oncology. Despite substantial advances in genomic profiling and molecular risk stratification, accurately identifying patients at risk of long-term relapse remains difficult. Previously published retrospective studies, including the identification of SLC20A1 in hormone receptor–positive breast cancer, CD58 in lower-grade glioma, and GGT6 in melanoma through MI-POG–based genome-wide screening, suggest the potential of MI-POG as a complementary strategy for tumor-specific prognostic biomarker discovery across biologically distinct tumor types (47-51, 67). By quantifying molecular–clinical dependencies without assuming predefined statistical models, MI-POG–based screening may help identify prognostic signals that are not readily detected by conventional regression-based approaches, although direct comparative benchmarking studies remain limited. In this context, MI-POG–derived biomarkers may complement existing multigene assays designed primarily for early recurrence prediction (19, 22, 23). Future methodological development will likely extend MI-POG beyond single-gene discovery toward multigene and network- and pathway-level prognostic signatures. Integration of MI-POG–based feature screening with pathway analysis, network-level modeling, and proteogenomic data may help delineate regulatory networks underlying endocrine resistance, tumor dormancy, and minimal residual disease. Rather than identifying universal biomarkers shared across malignancies, such approaches aim to systematically extract tumor-type– and subtype-specific molecular signatures that reflect the biological architecture of individual cancers. Similarly, recent investigations have identified prognostic associations involving PKCζ-centered molecular programs across multiple breast cancer subtypes. In luminal B breast cancer, combined expression patterns such as PKCζ/ALDH1A3 and PKCζ/CTNNBIP1 were associated with poor clinical outcomes, particularly in endocrine therapy-treated patients (68, 69). More recently, PKCζ/ALDH1A3 co-expression was associated with reduced therapeutic efficacy and unfavorable prognosis in basal-like breast cancer treated with chemotherapy (70). Although these studies were not direct applications of MI-POG, they illustrate how large-scale genomic analyses can reveal subtype-specific molecular programs linked to therapeutic resistance and clinically relevant outcome stratification. Beyond invasive breast cancer, biomarker discovery remains an important challenge in premalignant lesions such as ductal carcinoma in situ (DCIS), where accurate prediction of progression risk is required to avoid overtreatment (71). Extending information-theoretic biomarker discovery strategies to such early-stage lesions may help improve risk stratification and clinical decision-making. Information-theoretic approaches such as MI-POG may provide a complementary strategy for identifying molecular features associated with progression risk and biological heterogeneity in these early-stage lesions. Future benchmarking studies comparing MI-POG with penalized Cox regression, stability-selection frameworks, random survival forests, and deep-learning survival models will be important for clarifying the relative strengths and limitations of information-theoretic feature screening. Such studies may also help define optimal strategies for integrating MI-based screening with modern predictive modeling pipelines. From a translational perspective, several challenges must be addressed before biomarkers identified through the MI-POG framework can be implemented in clinical practice. Standardized computational pipelines for MI-POG–based feature screening and MI estimation will be required to ensure reproducibility across institutions. Prospective multi-institutional validation studies will also be necessary to establish clinical utility and generalizability. The development, validation, and clinical implementation of MI-POG–derived biomarkers will require rigorous evaluation frameworks analogous to those proposed for genomic classifiers in translational oncology (59). Integration with existing genomic assays and cost-effectiveness assessments will be important to determine whether MI-POG–derived biomarkers provide added value in routine oncology practice, as highlighted in previous evaluations of multigene expression assays (72). Beyond breast cancer, systematic application of MI-POG to additional malignancies and emerging multimodal datasets, including radiomic, spatial transcriptomic, and proteomic platforms, may further clarify its broader applicability. Because mutual information quantifies statistical dependency without imposing disease-specific parametric assumptions, MI-POG may also be adaptable to other complex diseases characterized by high-dimensional molecular interactions. However, such expansion will require careful methodological validation and disease-specific interpretation. Continued collaboration among computational scientists, cancer biologists, and clinical oncologists will therefore be essential to translate discoveries from the MI-POG framework into clinically meaningful advances. Future integration of MI-POG with artificial intelligence and machine-learning frameworks may further enhance automated feature discovery, multi-omics data integration, and clinically interpretable decision-support systems, thereby facilitating the translation of information-theoretic biomarker discovery into precision oncology.

Conclusion

In this article, we synthesized evidence from previously published studies and formalized MI-POG as an information-theoretic framework for genome-wide prognostic biomarker discovery. Information-theoretic approaches provide a powerful means of exploring complex molecular–clinical relationships within high-dimensional cancer datasets. The identification and validation of SLC20A1 demonstrate how MI-POG–based screening can complement conventional survival analysis, existing multigene prognostic assays, and modern machine-learning–based prognostic modeling by serving as an information-theoretic feature-discovery layer. Importantly, MI-POG is not intended to replace predictive modeling frameworks. Rather, it provides a model-independent approach for prioritizing candidate molecular features that can subsequently be evaluated using statistical, machine-learning, and biological validation strategies. In this regard, MI-POG may contribute to the discovery of clinically relevant biomarkers that might otherwise remain undetected using conventional feature-selection approaches alone. Continued methodological standardization, benchmarking against emerging prognostic modeling frameworks, cross-cohort validation, and integration with multi-omics datasets will be important for further evaluation of robustness and generalizability of MI-POG–based biomarker discovery. These developments may help establish information-theoretic screening as a complementary component of future translational cancer research and precision oncology pipelines.
