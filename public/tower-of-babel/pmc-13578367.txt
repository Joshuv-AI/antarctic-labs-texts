Transcriptional control of asparagine utilization regulates mycobacterial pH-driven adaptation at the host–pathogen crossroads
Authors: Bhanwar Bamniya, Kajal, Khushboo Mehta, Dibyendu Sarkar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Mycobacterium tuberculosis, an intracellular pathogen, survives within the membrane-bound vacuole, the phagosome, with acidic pH and limited access to nutrients. To survive and replicate within the human host, M. tuberculosis must adapt and fulfil its nutritional requirements. To investigate how the intracellular bacillus scavenges nutrients from its host, we studied mycobacterial acquisition of host-derived amino acids, the preferred nitrogen sources. We discovered that PhoP, a key determinant of mycobacterial adaptation to phagosomal acidification, controls expression of AnsP1 and AnsP2 to facilitate acquisition of host aspartate and asparagine, respectively. Thus, macrophage-infected WT-H37Rv showed a significantly higher level of intra-bacterial Asn compared to the phoPR-KO mutant, and a complemented mutant could restore Asn levels to those of WT-H37Rv. Under acidic conditions, elevated DNA binding of PhoP within the promoters leads to direct activation of ansP1 and ansP2. Consistently, phoPR-KO is unable to utilize Asn under acidic conditions, and overexpression of ansP1 or ansP2 in the mutant restored the intracellular growth defect of the mutant. These findings uncover the regulatory network allowing utilization of organic nitrogen sources by the pathogen during infection.

Human tuberculosis is caused by the bacillus Mycobacterium tuberculosis, which thrives inside the host macrophages and evades eradication by the human immune system. Like other bacterial diseases, tuberculosis continues to become increasingly drug-resistant, with strains resistant to two frontline drugs (MDR) and an additional resistance to an injectable antibiotic and a quinolone (XDR), claiming lives of one million patients with TB out of 10 million falling ill every year (WHO, 2025). Thus, the consensus opinion is that a better understanding of the interaction of the pathogen and its human host, particularly uncovering microbial mechanisms involved in acquisition and metabolism of host-derived nutrients by the pathogen during its complex life cycle, may help identify new targets for novel antimicrobials (1, 2, 3). As an intracellular pathogen, M. tuberculosis resides and multiplies inside a macrophage phagosome, which fuses poorly with host lysosomes (4, 5, 6, 7). However, a hallmark of virulence strategy by the pathogen is its unique ability to block phagosome maturation and avoid lysosomal degradation. While the molecular mechanism could be the result of a complex set of multiple events, our failure to identify transformative antitubercular therapy is largely attributable to our inadequate understanding of bacterial acquisition of host-derived nutrients by the pathogen.

The mycobacterial phagosome represents an environment comprising different niches with acidic pH, hypoxia, scarcity of nutrients, and presence of reactive oxygen and nitrogen species (8, 9). A number of high-throughput studies showing induction of acid-inducible genes and genes involved in utilization of alternative carbon sources, for example, host derived fatty acids and cholesterol (10, 11, 12), suggest that such an environment with multiple environmental cues translate into a significant remodeling of mycobacterial transcriptional landscape soon after phagocytosis. Previously, we had shown that M. tuberculosis has evolved mechanisms integrating hypoxia and metabolism of nitrogen, the alternate electron acceptor under limiting oxygen availability (13). While this pathway promotes intracellular survival and growth of the bacilli in the phagosomal hypoxic environment, nitrogen remains an essential component of amino acids, nucleotides, and many biomolecules like organic co-factors. In keeping with this, there has been compelling evidence on the critical requirement of amino acids during M. tuberculosis infection (14, 15). To identify host-relevant nitrogen metabolism in M. tuberculosis, two previous reports demonstrated that host-derived amino acids L-aspartate (Asp) and L-asparagine are critical nitrogen sources during infection. An elegant extension of these studies more recently explored intricate details of the nitrogen metabolic network of M. tuberculosis (16). These results demonstrate (a) a more efficient nitrogen utilization from proteinogenic amino acids compared to ammonium, (b) comparable rate of utilization of preferred amino acids as nitrogen sources and (c) lack of stringent homeostatic control mechanism of nitrogen utilization using amino acids. While amino acids remain superior nitrogen sources compared to ammonium based on doubling time and final biomass generated, the four amino acids Glu, Gln, Asp, and Asn, at all concentrations tested, were found to be more effective than NH4Cl (16). Notably, of these amino acids Asn as a nitrogen source undergoes conversion to ammonium, and is known to be exploited by M. tuberculosis to assimilate nitrogen and resist acid stress during infection (17). What remains unknown is the transcriptional regulatory network, which couple utilization of Asn as the effective nitrogen source to resist acidic conditions of growth.

M. tuberculosis lacks homologues of nearly all known bacterial transcription factors involved in nitrogen metabolism (18). Thus, transcription regulation of nitrogen metabolism in M. tuberculosis is poorly understood. Given the fact that Asn, as one of the most preferred amino acids (18), provides mycobacterial resistance to low pH conditions of growth (17) in one hand, and mycobacterial adaptation to the phagosomal low pH conditions requires functioning of the phoPR system on the other hand, we sought to investigate whether phoP locus is linked to Asn utilization of mycobacteria. Thus, using amino acids as the sole source of nitrogen, we studied bacterial physiology, transcriptional regulation and intracellular survival to examine the effect of nitrogen utilization by M. tuberculosis metabolic network. Our results demonstrate that virulence regulator PhoP functions as a DNA-binding transcription factor to impact the expression of the Asp transporter, AnsP1, and the Asn transporter, AnsP2, respectively. Along the line, acidic pH-dependent elevated DNA binding by PhoP within the promoters of Asn/Asp transporters contributes to restructure the transcriptional landscape, impacting mycobacterial tolerance to acidic pH conditions and intracellular survival within the host. In keeping with these results, mycobacterial growth inside phagosomes is supported by release of ammonia and consequent pH buffering. These results uncover a novel mechanism of how phagosomal adaptation of mycobacteria (19, 20) integrates nitrogen metabolism and acidic conditions of growth to promote intracellular survival of the pathogen.

Results

Asn is an effective nitrogen source of M. tuberculosis growth under acidic conditions

A recent study elegantly demonstrated that M. tuberculosis has a very flexible metabolic network with Asn and Gln as the most preferred nitrogen sources (16). To examine the effect of nitrogen source on mycobacterial growth, we attempted to characterize in vitro growth phenotypes of the M. tuberculosis wild-type (WT-H37Rv) using synthetic Middlebrook 7H9 (m7H9) liquid medium containing either Asn or Gln as the sole source of nitrogen (Fig. 1, A and B). For growth under acidic conditions, cells at OD600 of 0.4 to 0.6 were pelleted, washed twice with media (pH 4.5) buffered with 100 mM MES, and finally resuspended in media (pH 4.5) containing indicated nitrogen sources (21). M. tuberculosis senses the phagosomal environment and mounts effective adaptive responses to support intracellular growth of the bacilli. This is considered a hallmark feature of the pathogen as macrophage colonization remains key to establish and maintain infection. Notably, pH gradient remains one of the defining features of the endosomal–lysosomal sorting pathway. Pathogenic mycobacteria that arrest phagosome maturation in macrophages reside in a phagosome with a pH of ∼ 6.4 (22, 23). In contrast, in activated macrophages mycobacteria are trafficked to an acidic vacuole of pH 5.5 (24). However, bacteria moving to lysosome encounter a more highly acidic environment with a pH below 5.0. Thus, the mechanism of defining the intracellular location of M. tuberculosis strictly depends on the mycobacterial ability to sense phagosomal pH. In this study, growth experiments under an acidic environment were performed with WT-H37Rv at pH 4.5. However, as a control, pH 6.6 was the preferred normal condition for growth. Growth kinetics of WT-H37Rv in the presence of either Asn or Gln as the sole nitrogen sources were largely similar both under normal (pH 6.6) and acidic (pH 4.5) conditions (Fig. 1, A and B, respectively). In both cases, WT-H37Rv showed reduced growth under acidic pH compared to the normal conditions. However, Asp as the nitrogen source, under identical experimental conditions, was least effective for WT-H37Rv grown under acidic pH (Fig. S1A). A comparative growth analysis of WT-H37Rv under acidic pH using Asn, Gln or Asp as the sole nitrogen source, respectively, uncovers that Asn most effectively supports mycobacterial growth under acidic pH (Fig. S1B). In CFU enumeration, under acidic pH and in the presence of either of the three amino acids, the number of viable colonies remained comparable at the onset of the experiment (‘0’ day sample) (Fig. 1C). However, on the seventh day sample, we observed a significantly higher viable counts of mycobacterial cells grown in the presence of Asn compared to Gln or Asp as the sole nitrogen source. From these results, we conclude that Asn remains the most effective nitrogen source to support mycobacterial growth under acidic conditions. Having shown that Asn supports mycobacterial growth under acidic pH, we wanted to investigate expression of genes related to Asn utilization. For acid stress, we grew WT-H37Rv in m7H9 under acidic conditions (pH 4.5) till OD600 of 0.4 to 0.6. Cells were pelleted, washed twice with media (pH 4.5), and resuspended in media of pH 4.5. The cells were further grown for 24 h at 37 °C. Next, we assessed relative abundance of mRNAs related to Asn utilization (Fig. 1D). We observed low pH-inducible expression of aspartate transporter ansP1, asparagine transporter ansP2, and asparaginase ansA, respectively. pks2, encoding low-pH inducible polyketide synthase was used as a positive control, whereas sigA and 16S rRNA genes served as endogenous controls. These observations are consistent with previously reported results suggesting importance of Asn as a critically important nitrogen source (16), and most effective in providing mycobacterial resistance to acidic stress (25).

PhoP facilitates Asn utilization of M. tuberculosis

PhoP has been implicated in mycobacterial adaptation to low pH conditions inside the macrophage phagosome and is significantly activated under acidic pH both in vitro and ex vivo (19, 20, 26, 27). Since Asn was effectively utilized by mycobacteria under acidic conditions of growth, we next explored role of the phoP locus in mycobacterial Asn utilization. We adopted a previously-reported CRISPR interference-based approach (28) to generate a phoP knock-down (phoP-KD) construct as described earlier (29). To examine in vitro growth phenotype, phoP-KD mutant was grown under normal conditions (pH 6.6) in synthetic m7H9 containing Asn as the sole nitrogen source (Fig. 2A). While phoP-KD mutant showed a significantly reduced growth relative to WT-H37Rv, under identical conditions a control strain (prrB-KD) showed a similar growth profile as that of WT-H37Rv. In sharp contrast, both knock-down mutants grew as effectively as the WT-bacilli with Gln (Fig. 2B) or ammonium (Fig. S2A) as the sole nitrogen source. Note that phoP-KD mutant showed a significantly reduced expression of phoP (≈12%); however, expression level of the sensor kinase prrB (as a positive control) was comparable in WT-H37Rv and phoP-KD mutant (Fig. S2B). To further probe the role of phoP locus on Asn utilization, we next grew WT-H37Rv, phoPR-KO and complemented phoPR-KO mutant (Compl.) in synthetic m7H9 supplemented with Asn or Gln as the sole source of nitrogen (Fig. 2, C and D). Our results demonstrate that phoPR-KO is significantly growth defective in synthetic m7H9 media containing Asn as the sole nitrogen source (Fig. 2C). However, under identical conditions, the complemented mutant expressing a copy of the phoP gene showed a largely similar growth to that of WT-H37Rv. In contrast, WT bacteria, the phoPR-KO mutant and the complemented mutant strain displayed a comparable growth profile in m7H9 supplemented with either Gln (Fig. 2D) or ammonium (Fig. S2D) as the sole source of nitrogen. Notably, CFU enumeration of mycobacterial strains grown in synthetic m7H9 supplemented with Asn (Fig. 2E) or Gln (Fig. 2F) as the sole nitrogen source displayed consistent results. Together, these results uncover the role of the phoP locus in mycobacterial utilization of Asn. pH homeostasis is linked to mycobacterial Asn utilization via the phoP locus

Previous studies implicate a major role of the response regulator PhoP in phagosomal pH-dependent adaptation of mycobacteria (19, 20, 27). While low pH remains the activating signal of phoR–phoP system (26), results reported here suggest that PhoP facilitates Asn utilization of mycobacteria. To probe whether intra-bacterial pH homeostasis is linked to Asn utilization, we transformed WT-H37Rv and the phoPR-KO strain with a GFP-based pH-sensitive probe (30), and grew the strains both under acidic (pH 5.5) and normal (pH 6.6) conditions using Asn or ammonium as the sole source of nitrogen, as described in the methods. To assess role of PhoP in Asn utilization, we preferred pH 5.5 during in vitro growth experiments since even in rich media phoPR-KO strain shows a considerably reduced growth at pH 4.5 compared to WT-H37Rv (20). This pH-sensitive fluorescent reporter plasmid, which was used to determine intra-mycobacterial pH (30), is accurate and sensitive and was utilized to demonstrate that phagosomal acidic pH induces reductive stress in M. tuberculosis residing within macrophages (31). Based on a standard curve plotting intra-bacterial pH as a function of ratio of fluorescence intensities (395 nm/475 nm) from bacterial cells grown under varying pH conditions (30), we could accurately determine intra-bacterial pH. Our results demonstrate that WT bacteria, under acidic conditions of growth (pH 5.5), could maintain an intra-bacterial pH of ∼6.60 (Fig. 3A). However, under identical conditions, phoPR-KO failed to maintain intra-bacterial pH. As a control, we did not observe a significant difference in intra-bacterial pH between WT-H37Rv and the phoPR-KO mutant grown in the presence of ammonium as the nitrogen source. Note that an identical comparison of intra-bacterial pH of WT-H37Rv and phoPR-KO mutant, grown under normal conditions (pH 6.6), also suggests that phoPR-KO fails to maintain intra-bacterial pH in the presence of Asn, but not ammonium as the sole nitrogen source (Fig. S3A). In an identical experimental set up, we next compared intra-bacterial Asn level of WT-H37Rv and phoPR-KO mutant (Fig. 3B). Our results demonstrate a significantly lower Asn level of the phoPR-KO mutant relative to WT bacteria. However, stable expression of a copy of phoP gene in the mutant restored Asn level to the WT bacilli. These results suggest that intra-bacterial pH homeostasis of mycobacteria is linked to Asn acquisition via the phoP locus. In agreement with these results, under identical conditions we observed a significantly lower level of intra-bacterial ammonia in phoPR-KO mutant relative to the WT bacilli and the complemented mutant (Compl.) (Fig. S3B).

To investigate mycobacterial Asn acquisition from macrophages in vivo, we next infected THP-1 macrophages with WT-H37Rv and phoPR-KO mutant of M. tuberculosis. 48- hour post infection, bacteria were isolated from infected macrophages, macrophage debris was removed by extensive washing and intra-mycobacterial Asn levels were measured from comparable counts of bacterial cells. Under identical conditions, macrophage-derived WT-H37Rv showed a significantly higher level of intra-bacterial Asn compared to the phoPR-KO mutant (Fig. 3C). A complemented mutant stably expressing a copy of the phoP gene (Compl.) could restore intra-bacterial Asn levels to those of WT-H37Rv. It should be noted that using infected THP-1 macrophages, we consistently observed a significantly lower intra-bacterial ammonia level for macrophage-derived phoPR-KO strain relative to WT-H37Rv and a complemented strain (Fig. S3C). From these results, we conclude that in vivo Asn acquisition of M. tuberculosis requires the phoP locus. In this regard, we cite a previous study examining transcriptional adaptation of M. tuberculosis within macrophages (32). In this work, a comparison of varying postinfection time points uncovered significant changes in M. tuberculosis gene expression between 4 and 24 h, for both the resting and IFN-activated macrophages. The expression profiles of M. tuberculosis 24 and 48 h after infection were very similar, suggesting that 24 h after infection the transcriptome of M. tuberculosis remains stable for the next 24 h. These findings justify 3 h and 48 h, as early uptake and extended time points, respectively.

We next compared expression of ansP1 and ansP2 in WT-H37Rv and phoR-KO mutant, grown under normal (pH 6.6) and acidic (pH 4.5) conditions of growth (Fig. S3, D and E), respectively. Although under normal conditions of bacterial growth ansP1 and ansP2 showed comparable expression in WT-H37Rv and the mutant, clearly expression of both the transporters underwent significant reduction in the mutant relative to WT-H37Rv under acidic conditions of growth. These results are consistent with the model suggesting that PhoR senses acidic pH, and activates PhoP, leading to P ∼ PhoP- dependent activation of ansP1 and ansP2.

With the uncovering of a role of phoP locus in mycobacterial Asn acquisition, we next compared relative expression of representative Asn metabolism genes in phoPR-KO mutant and WT-H37Rv, grown under normal (pH 6.6) conditions of growth (Fig. 4A). We observed a significant downregulation of ansP1 and ansP2 expression in phoPR-KO mutant relative to WT-H37Rv. In contrast, expression of ansA, asnT and glnA1 remains unaffected in the phoPR-KO mutant relative to WT bacilli. To assess whether transcription regulation of ansP1 and ansP2 is attributable to recruitment of PhoP, we examined in vivo DNA binding of the regulator within the target promoters of WT-H37Rv, grown under normal (pH 6.6) and acidic (pH 4.5) conditions of growth (Fig. 4B). We utilized chromatin immunoprecipitation assays (ChIP) using anti-PhoP antibody, coupled with quantitative PCR measurements as described in the methods. Our results highlight a significantly elevated recruitment of PhoP within the upstream regulatory regions of ansP1 (ansP1up) and ansP2 (ansP2up) for WT-H37Rv grown under acidic (pH 4.5) relative to normal (pH 6.6) conditions. These results are consistent with (a) the importance of acidic pH-inducible PhoR-dependent activation of PhoP leading to high-affinity DNA binding and transcription activation (33, 34), and (b) the functioning of PhoP as a regulator of ansP1 and ansP2 expression. Direct transcriptional control of mycobacterial Asp and Asn transporters

With the results showing a considerable effect of PhoP on transcriptional control of ansP1 and ansP2, we wished to investigate if PhoP functions as a direct regulator of M. tuberculosis ansP1 and ansP2 genes in vivo. To this end, we constructed transcriptional fusions to lacZ by cloning PCR-amplified fragments of ansP1 and ansP2 upstream regulatory regions at the ScaI site of pSM128, an integrative promoter probe vector for mycobacteria (35). Mycobacterium smegmatis harboring the transcriptional fusions, ansP1up-lacZ or ansP2up-lacZ (see methods for details) were co-transformed with pME1mL1-phoP, an inducible expression system expressing M. tuberculosis PhoP from the Pmyc1tetO promoter under TetR repressor as described previously (36). Co-transformed cells were grown in 7H9 medium containing appropriate antibiotics with or without anhydrotetracycline (ATc; 50 ng/ml) to induce expression of PhoP. Notably, ansP1up-lacZ fusion was significantly activated with induction of PhoP at the 24- h time point, as the β-galactosidase levels obtained in presence of ATc was ≈2.1 ± 0.1-fold higher relative to the activity in the absence of ATc (Fig. 5A). Similarly, cells carrying ansP2up-lacZ fusion showed a ≈ 3.6 ± 0.4-fold higher β-galactosidase activity when PhoP expression was induced compared to the uninduced sample (Fig. 5B). Note that for both cases in the absence of PhoP expression (pME1mL1 was used as an empty vector control), we failed to observe a significant difference in promoter activity in the absence or presence of ATc. As a control, under identical conditions, we used a strain harboring the reporter construct ansAup-lacZ, comprising −379 to +108 of the ansA upstream regulatory region relative to its ORF start site. We observed that even in the absence of inducing PhoP expression (in the absence of ATc), presence of PhoP expression plasmid alone significantly elevates β-galactosidase activity from the ansA-lacZ fusion relative to the empty vector control (see Fig. 5C). Insets compare expression of PhoP in M. smegmatis in the absence or presence of ATc. We suggest that this observation is attributable to PhoP-independent leaky expression of ansA promoter since we were unable to detect PhoP expression in the absence of the inducer (ATc). As an additional control, we also performed reporter assays using a non PhoP controlled promoter (sucCup-lacZ)-lacZ fusion (Fig. S4). We used a strain harboring the reporter construct sucCAup-lacZ, comprising −270 to +62 of the sucC upstream regulatory region relative to its ORF start site. Despite an ATc-dependent induction of PhoP expression as above (see Fig. 5), we observed a comparably low promoter activity in both the uninduced and induced samples, suggesting that under identical conditions examined PhoP does not influence non-specific promoter activity. Our results show that the fold change of β-galactosidase activity at 24 h in the presence and absence of inducing PhoP expression was 1. 1(±0.12)- and 1.05(±0.02)-fold (compare green versus blue columns) from the ansP1upmut-lacZ and ansP2upmut-lacZ, respectively (left panels of Fig. 5, F and G). The insets compare expression of PhoP in the absence and presence of ATc. These results are in striking contrast with PhoP-dependent 2.1(±0.1)- and 3.6(±0.4)- fold activation of ansP1up and ansP2up expression, respectively, under identical conditions examined (middle panels, Fig. 5, F and G). Thus, we conclude that PhoP binding at the newly-identified sequence motif within the corresponding regulatory regions is necessary and sufficient for transcription activation of ansP1, and ansP2, respectively. To examine the effect of phosphorylation of PhoP on activation of ansP1up and ansP2up, we utilized identical fusion constructs along with inducing expression of PhoPD71N, a mutant PhoP defective for phosphorylation at Asp-71 (34). Although PhoPD71N displayed an ATc-dependent induction of expression comparable to that of wild-type PhoP (insets to right panels, Fig. 5, F and G), it showed 1.2 ± 0.07 and 0.8 ± 0.14-fold variation in promoter activation from ansP1up and ansP2up, respectively (right panels, Fig. 5, F and G). Thus, we conclude that PhoP- dependent activation of ansP1, and ansP2 is attributable to phosphorylation of PhoP. These results are consistent with low pH-dependent elevated DNA binding of P ∼ PhoP during mycobacterial growth under acidic conditions (relative to normal conditions), known to activate functioning of the regulator via phosphorylation (26, 34).

Discussion

Identifying mycobacterial acquisition of essential elements hold a key step toward understanding of host-pathogen interactions in TB. Previous studies suggest that Asp and Asn are key nitrogen sources used by M. tuberculosis during infection (17, 40, 41). Further, it has been shown that Asn is an effective nitrogen source of the pathogen via transport through the amino acid permease AnsP2, and subsequent hydrolysis by AnsA, the asparaginase. These results are of significant physiological relevance given the fact that human plasma contains 50 to 60 μM Asn (42), and its concentration is much higher in white blood cells (43, 44). In addition, Asn has been shown to accumulate in mycobacterial vacuoles inside infected macrophages (17), suggesting in vivo abundance of the amino acid to the intracellular pathogen during infection. However, the regulatory network of the pathogen that mediates effective utilization of Asn remains unknown.

The fact that Asn, unlike Gln or ammonium, effectively supports in vitro growth of WT bacilli under low pH conditions of growth (Figs. 1 and S1) is remarkably consistent with previous work by Neyrolles and coworkers showing Asn -dependent mycobacterial resistance to acidic pH (17). In this study, the authors showed that M. tuberculosis lacking ansA displays greatly reduced growth at pH 5.5 as compared to WT-H37Rv and complemented strains. In fact, accompanying a severe growth defect of the ansA-KO mutant under acidic pH, the authors also noted a markedly diminished Asn-dependent ammonia secretion and pH buffering. These results underscore that pH buffering and growth under acidic conditions are intrinsically linked metabolic events and are essentially dependent on AnsA to sustain M. tuberculosis growth in the presence of Asn as the sole provider of nitrogen source. Along the line, growth of the ansP2-KO mutant in the presence of Asn was greatly reduced at pH 5.5 as compared to the WT-H37Rv and complemented strains, suggesting that in an acidic environment Asn catabolism requires AnsP2 and strictly depends on AnsA to support M. tuberculosis growth in the presence of Asn. These results connect Asn acquisition with the virulence regulator PhoP, a major player in phagosomal acidic pH dependent adaptive program of mycobacteria (19, 20, 45). Subsequent gene expression studies uncover PhoP-dependent regulation of Asp transporter (AnsP1) and Asn transporter (AnsP2), respectively (Figs. 3 and 4), suggesting functioning of PhoP as a regulator of Asn metabolism. To probe the in vivo significance, we compared intra-bacterial pH of WT-H37Rv and a mutant lacking phoPR, grown under acidic pH (pH 5.5) and in the presence of Asn as the sole nitrogen source. Interestingly, phoPR-KO mutant, unlike WT-H37Rv failed to maintain intra-bacterial pH (Fig. 3A) and displayed a significantly reduced intra-bacterial Asn level (Fig. 3B). These results are consistent with PhoP-dependent Asn uptake contributing to mycobacterial pH homeostasis. PhoP-controlled activation of ansP1 and ansP2 followed a direct regulatory mechanism as shown by specific in vivo recruitment of the regulator within the upstream regulatory regions of ansP1 and ansP2 (Fig. 4B) coupled with reporter assays showing promoter activation in M. smegmatis (Fig. 5).

M. tuberculosis PhoP is a global regulator that impacts expression of ∼2% of the bacterial genome. In keeping with this, phoPR-KO mutant of M. tuberculosis shows significant growth attenuation in both macrophages and mice (46, 47, 48, 49). More recent studies uncover that PhoP controls numerous aspects of M. tuberculosis physiology, including ESAT-6 secretion, specific T-cell recognition during virulence regulation and pH sensing during intracellular adaptation (19, 20, 50, 51). In this study, we showed that phoP locus plays a major role in mycobacterial utilization of Asn as the nitrogen source and thereby contributes to mycobacterial adaptation to low pH. Having shown that activation of both aspartate and asparagine transporters is under direct regulation of PhoP, using cellular infection models, we sought to investigate whether independent over-expression of ansP1 and ansP2 impacts growth defect of phoPR-KO mutant. The most fundamental insight is derived from partial growth restoration of the phoPR-KO mutant (Fig. 6), suggesting striking physiological significance of Asn utilization as an effective resistance mechanism to low pH, impacting intracellular survival of the bacilli.

A previous study showed that ansP2-KO mutant was partially impaired in nitrogen incorporation from Asn in vitro and was not attenuated in vivo (17). Thus, AnsP1, an AsnP2 paralogue was considered to complement AnsP2 functionality (52). However, ansP1-KO grew comparably well as that of WT-H37Rv with Asn as the sole nitrogen source and transported Asn in vitro as effectively as that of WT-H37Rv (40). These results suggest that ansP1 and ansP2 appear to complement each other, at least in part, and only obliteration of both genes simultaneously would result in a clean phenotype. These results explain and fit well with (a) overexpression of either ansP1 or ansP2 in a phoPR-KO facilitating mycobacterial utilization of Asn both in vitro and within host cells, and (b) growth attenuation of phoPR-KO, lacking the key regulator which activates expression of both ansP1 and ansP2. It should be noted that functioning of PhoP is critically required for M. tuberculosis virulence (46, 49) as it impacts numerous aspects of bacterial physiology including complex lipid biosynthesis (47, 48, 53), early response to hypoxia (51), regulation of ESAT-6 secretion (50, 51, 54, 55), and pH sensing during intracellular adaptation (19, 20, 26). Using genome-wide probing, SELEX studies and ChIP-seq analysis clearly identified the consensus PhoP binding site (38, 56). Results reported here showing mutagenesis of promoters coupled with ex vivo reporter assays using transcriptional fusion of WT and mutant promoters (carrying changes at the PhoP binding sites) establish that the newly-identified direct repeat motif alone is likely attributable to PhoP-DNA interactions at the regulatory sites, and recruitment of P ∼ PhoP at these sites is essential for PhoP-dependent regulation of ansP1, and ansP2 (Fig. 5). In keeping with these results, the PhoP-DNA co-crystal structure demonstrates tandem head-to-head binding of the proteins to the adjacent repeat units where a single protomer recognizes each unit (57, 58, 59). Interestingly, our results on newly identified PhoP-binding sites within the ansP1 and ansP2 promoters are in broad agreement with the presence of two 7-bp repeat units separated by a 4-bp spacer sequence (Fig. 5, D and E). However, the nucleotide sequences of the repeat sites are significantly different from each other, suggesting that similar sequence modules with variable nucleotide sequences display differential affinity for the same transcription factor (PhoP) and account for functional diversity to accommodate variable physiological functions.

The fact that phoP-KD mutant shows a stronger growth defect in utilizing Asn as the nitrogen source relative to the phoPR-KO mutant (Fig. 2) possibly suggests that expression of some other important M. tuberculosis gene(s) also undergoes non-specific (off target) inhibition in the knockdown construct. While we cannot rule out this possibility, an alternate suggestion speculates that the phoPR-KO mutant utilizes substitute nitrogen source carried over from the rich medium, thereby displaying a lesser effect in the presence of Asn as the sole nitrogen source. Nevertheless, the above results expand upon our previous work on sequence-specific promoter recognition by P ∼ PhoP to demonstrate transcriptional control of ansP1 and ansP2, genes with related metabolic function involving Asp and Asn acquisition of mycobacteria. Although our results suggest that under normal conditions, PhoP regulates ansP1/ansP2 to support utilization of Asn as a nitrogen source (Fig. 5), acidic conditions (pH 5.5) of growth clearly demonstrate in vivo role of the phoP locus on ansP1/ansP2 expression (Fig. 4A), consistent with significantly higher recruitment of PhoP within the corresponding promoters under acidic conditions relative to normal conditions of growth (Fig. 4B). In agreement with these results, phoR locus under acidic conditions, but not under normal conditions, significantly impacted ansP1/ansP2 expression (compare Figs. S3, D and F). Given the fact that acidic condition of growth promotes PhoR-dependent phosphorylation of PhoP (26) and phosphorylation defective PhoPD71N fails to activate ansP1/ansP2 expression (Fig. 5), together we surmised that the physiological significance of PhoP-dependent ansP1/ansP2 regulation is associated with in vivo phosphorylation of PhoP. Collectively, these results, as shown schematically (Fig. 8), uncover that (a) direct interactions between the P ∼ PhoP and the newly identified PhoP binding site(s) are essential for activation of target genes ansP1 and ansP2, and (b) phosphorylation of a single residue of PhoP contributes to regulation of Asn acquisition and maintenance of pH homeostasis, a novel result of unusual significance at the host–pathogen crossroads. In conclusion, these results provide yet another example of close connectivity shaped through evolution to accommodate physiology and virulence regulation of microbial pathogens together. It also underscores the need to further extend exploration of such connections and identify novel strategies to combat infectious diseases.Figure 8Model showing a schematic representation of how Asn acquisition contributes to mycobacterial survival under acidic pH. Experimental procedures

Bacterial strains and culture conditions

E. coli TOP10 and DH5α strains, used for cloning, were grown on LB agar medium and/or LB broth. M. tuberculosis H37Rv and M. smegmatis mc2155 were cultured in Middlebrook 7H9 broth supplemented with 10% OADC (oleic acid, albumin, dextrose, and catalase) and 0.05% Tween-80 or on Middlebrook 7H11 medium supplemented with 10% OADC. phoPR disruption mutant of M. tuberculosis H37Rv (referred to as the phoPR-KO) was a kind gift of Dr Issar Smith. This strain was constructed by inserting a kanamycin-resistant cassette from pUC-K4 into a unique EcoRV site within the coding region of phoP, and disruption was confirmed by Southern blot analysis of chromosomal DNA (47). Next, purified plasmid DNAs were electroporated into wild-type M. tuberculosis strain by standard protocol (60). phoPR expression was complemented by cloning a 3.6 kb DNA fragment of M. tuberculosis phoPR including 200 bp phoP promoter region, a hygromycin resistance cassette, attP site, and the gene encoding phage L5 integrase into pSM607, as detailed previously (47). The plasmid was used to transform phoPR mutant to integrate at the L5 attB site. phoR-deleted M. tuberculosis H37Rv (referred to as the phoR-KO) was constructed by ‘mycobacterial recombineering’ as described previously (26). Growth, transformation of wild-type (WT), and mutant strains, and selection of transformants were performed in the presence of specific antibiotics at the following concentrations: hygromycin (50 μg/ml), kanamycin (20 μg/ml) and streptomycin (20 μg/ml).

For nitrogen-defined conditions, synthetic m7H9 medium supplemented with 10% ADS (albumin-dextrose-sodium chloride) lacking glutamic acid was used, ammonium sulphate and ferric ammonium citrate replaced with sodium sulphate and ferric citrate, respectively. Mycobacterial strains were grown in the synthetic m7H9 medium supplemented with specific amino acids at a final concentration of 1 g/L as the sole nitrogen sources. In a previous study, based on 14C-Asn uptake assay the authors showed that M. tuberculosis grown in 7H9 with 5 mM Asn could take up an intra-bacterial Asn of 20 μM (41). It should be noted that in this study pH 5.5 was used as the acidic conditions of growth to investigate acid stress. Further, based on the detailed in vitro analysis of M. tuberculosis growth at a range of Asn concentrations (16), we chose to perform the growth experiments in the presence of 1 g/L (equivalent to 7.57 mM) Asn. M. tuberculosis H37Rv cultures were first pre-adapted to synthetic m7H9 containing the respective nitrogen source for 3 days, the pre-adapted cultures inoculated into fresh synthetic 7H9, containing the same nitrogen source and adjusted to the specific pH at an OD600 of 0.5. For M. tuberculosis growth under specific acidic pH, cells at OD600 of 0.4 to 0.6 were pelleted, washed twice with specific media buffered with 100 mM anhydrous MES [2-(N-Morpholino) ethane sulfonic acid] (Sigma-Aldrich)] and 2N HCl for pH 6.6, 5.5 and 4.5, respectively, as described previously (21) and finally resuspended in media of indicated pH. For in vitro growth studies, following inoculation, OD600 was recorded at different time pints over a period of 10 days. For results on growth curve and colony forming units (CFUs) reported in Figure 2, the strains were inoculated in fresh 7H9-ADS medium at an OD600 of ∼0.05. Cultures at the onset (0 days) and after 7 days of growth were serially diluted, plated at various dilutions on 7H11-OADS agar plates, and grown for 21 days to enumerate CFU. In each case, data from three independent investigations were used to generate growth curves.

Construction of M. tuberculosis knock-down mutants

We used a previously reported CRISPRi system (28) to construct knock-down mutants (phoP-KD and prrB-KD), as described (26, 29). This method effectively inhibits expression of target genes via inducible expression of dCas9 along with gene specific guide RNAs (sgRNA). To compare expression of target genes, total RNA from the respective knock-down constructs were utilized in RT-qPCR experiments relative to corresponding uninduced strains as shown in Fig. S2.

Determination of intrabacterial pH and Asn level

To measure intra-bacterial pH, we have transformed WT-H37Rv and the phoPR-KO strain with a GFP-based pH sensitive probe (30), and grew the strains both under acidic (pH 5.5) and normal (pH 6.6) conditions in synthetic m7H9 supplemented with Asn or ammonium as the sole source of nitrogen, as described in the methods. Cultures in early log phase were adjusted to an OD600 of 0.5. Next, 1-ml aliquots in triplicate were incubated at 37°C and concentrated 5-fold to improve GFP signal intensity. Finally, 100 μl of cell aliquots were used in a fluorescent plate reader with excitation wavelengths set at 395 nm and 475 nm, and emission recorded at 510 nm, respectively. Briefly, pH-GFP fluorescence ratios obtained at dual excitation wavelengths were correlated with buffers of known pH. The fluorescence ratio (F395/F475) was plotted against pH, fitted using a nonlinear sigmoidal regression model to generate a calibration curve as described previously (30) and intra-bacterial pH values were determined by interpolation from the resulting standard curve. Note that the fluorescence ratio was measured at a range of bacterial growth and was found to remain unchanged, confirming the density-independent nature of the ratiometric pH-GFP reporter system (30, 61).

Intra-bacterial Asn levels were measured by growing WT and phoPR-KO mutant to an initial OD600 of 0.1. Next, the cultures were further incubated at 37 °C for an additional 3 days, and a comparable number of WT-H37Rv and mutant cells lysed in a buffer containing 50 mM Tris (pH 7.5), 1% Triton X-100, 150 mM NaCl, 1 mM PMSF, 1 mM EDTA and 5% glycerol, and Lysing Matrix B (100 μm silica beads) in a bead beater (MPBio) at a speed setting of 6.0 for 1 min, with cooling intervals between cycles to prevent overheating. After repeating the procedure for three cycles, the lysates were clarified at 1300 rpm for 10 min, and supernatants were collected. 20 μl of each sample was taken into wells of a 96-well flat-bottom plate in duplicate, and intrabacterial Asn levels were determined using the Megazyme reagents and protocol according to the manufacturer’s recommendation.

Bacterial RNA extraction

Total RNA was isolated from bacterial cultures grown under specific conditions as described earlier (26, 29). In brief, 25 ml of bacterial culture (OD600 = 0.6–0.8) was mixed with equal volume of a 5 M guanidinium thiocyanate solution containing 1% β-mercaptoethanol and 0.5% Tween-80. The cells were pelleted by centrifugation, resuspended in 1 ml of Trizol (Ambion), and then subjected to lysis with Lysing Matrix B (100 μm silica beads, MPBio). Lysis was performed using a FastPrep-24 bead beater (MP Bio) at a speed setting of 6.0 for 30 s, repeating the process for three cycles with cooling intervals to prevent overheating. The resulting lysates were centrifuged at 13,000 rpm for 10 min, and the RNA was extracted from the supernatant using the Direct-Zol RNA isolation kit (ZYMO). Genomic DNA contamination was removed by treatment with DNase I (Promega). RNA quality was assessed using an UV-VIS spectrophotometer (Shimadzu Corporation), and the integrity of 23S and 16S rRNA was further confirmed by gel electrophoresis. RNA concentrations were determined with a Qubit fluorometer (Invitrogen).

Quantitative real-time PCR analysis

Transcriptional profiling was performed using RNA extracted from M. tuberculosis cultures grown under specific growth conditions. cDNA synthesis and PCR amplification were performed with the Superscript III Platinum SYBR Green One-Step RT-qPCR Kit (Invitrogen), following previously reported protocol (62). To determine fold changes in gene expression, multiple independent RNA preparations were analyzed by ΔΔCT method (63) using M. tuberculosis rpoB or 16S rDNA as internal controls. The primer sequences used for the RT-qPCR analysis are provided in Table S1.

Cloning

To over-express mycobacterial ansP1 and ansA, we amplified both the ORFs from M. tuberculosis genomic DNA using primer pairs FPansP1/RPansP1 and FPansA/RPansA, respectively, and cloned them between BamHI and HindIII sites of the mycobacterial expression vector p19Kpro (37). Similarly, ansP2 was amplified using FPansP1/RPansP2 primer pair and cloned between HindIII and ClaI sites of p19Kpro. Recombinant His6-tagged PhoP was cloned between NdeI and HindIII sites of pET-28b using the primer pair phoPstart/phoPstop. To express M. tuberculosis PhoP in mycobacteria, the ORF with an N-terminal Flag-tag was cloned and expressed from p19Kpro (37) as described previously (55). To express M. tuberculosis PhoP in M. smegmatis, wild-type or mutant phoP was cloned in pME1mL1 (36) and expressed from Pmyc1tetO promoter under TetR repressor, as described elsewhere (34). Each construct was checked by DNA sequencing. Tables S2 and S3 list the oligonucleotide primers and plasmids, respectively, used for cloning reported in this work.

Macrophage infection assays

Human THP1-monocytes were procured from ATCC (ATCC TIB-202) and were cultured in RPMI 1640 media (Gibco) supplemented with 10% FBS at 37 °C under 5% CO2. Monocytes were differentiated for 24 h with 30 ng/ml of phorbol 12-myristate 13-acetate (PMA, Sigma) as described previously (61) and subsequent experiments were carried out after 48 h of recovery in PMA-free RPMI medium. Infection of THP-1 macrophages with M. tuberculosis strains was carried out at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 1:10, according to previously established protocols (38). 48 h post-infection cells were stained with Lyso-Tracker Deep-Red (Invitrogen; 150 nM) and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (Sigma) as described previously (26, 29). For LAMP1 staining, after fixation, the cells were incubated with anti-LAMP1 rabbit polyclonal antibody (Abcam) at room temperature for 2 h. The antibody dilution was made in staining solution containing 0.25% saponin (Sigma) and 1% bovine serum albumin. This step was followed by three consecutive washes, and further incubation in secondary Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (Invitrogen) for 45 min at room temperature as described elsewhere (61). M. tuberculosis H37Rv strains were stained using phenolic auramine solution and the nucleus was counterstained with Hoechst (Sigma) for 15 min at room temperature. After extensive washing with 1X PBS, coverslips were mounted using Slowfade Diamond Antifade mountant (Invitrogen). Confocal images were acquired in a Nikon A1R Confocal laser Scanning Microscope using a 60× /1.0 NA oil immersion objective. NIS element software was used to acquire the images, which were analyzed through Fiji software. Laser and detector settings were optimized based on macrophages infected with WT-H37Rv to ensure consistency, and images processed digitally with IMARIS software (version 9.20), with standard intensity thresholds. The percentage of bacterial co-localization was calculated by analyzing 50 infected macrophages from 10 different fields across three independent replicate experiments.

To determine intracellular survival by assessing CFU, THP1-monocytes (1 × 106) were plated in a 6-well plate and incubated in a CO2 incubator at 37 °C for 24 h. Next, cells were infected with the indicated mycobacterial strains at an MOI of 1:10 for 3 h and treated with 100 μg/ml gentamicin (GIBCO) for 30 min (to remove extracellular bacilli). After 48 h, cells were lysed using 0.06% SDS, serially diluted in PBS, and plated on Middlebrook 7H11(BD) plates supplemented with 10% OADC. The plates were further incubated at 37 °C, and bacterial colonies were counted after ∼3 weeks. CFU was enumerated and plotted using GraphPad PRISM v11.0.0. To determine intra-bacterial Asn level of macrophage -derived M. tuberculosis strains, 48- h post infection, macrophages were lysed with 0.06% SDS in 1X PBS and centrifuged at 4000 rpm for 10 min. The pellet was washed twice with 1X PBS buffer to ensure complete removal of macrophage debris. The bacterial pellet was then resuspended in 0.2 ml of 1X PBS and comparable number of bacterial cells from each strain was lysed with IP buffer (50 mM Tris (pH 7.5), 150 mM NaCl, 1 mM EDTA, 1% Triton X- 100, 1 mM PMSF and 5% glycerol) containing protease inhibitor cocktail. The resultant lysates were centrifuged at 13,000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C. The supernatants were then used to measure intra-bacterial Asn level as described above.

Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP)- qPCR

ChIP assays were performed with actively growing M. tuberculosis cultures as described previously (26, 62) using anti-FLAG/anti-PhoP antibody and protein A/G agarose beads (Pierce). For qPCR measurements, immunoprecipitated DNA was diluted 10-fold and combined with a reaction mixture with SYBR Green mix, appropriate primers (Table S1) targeting specific promoter regions, and Platinum Taq DNA polymerase (1 Unit, Invitrogen). Amplification was done using a real-time PCR detection system (Applied Biosystems). Control primers specific to 16S rDNA or rpoB genes were included to validate specific enrichment from IP samples. To assess recruitment efficiency, mock IP samples (without antibody) were used as negative controls, and data were normalized to PCR signal from the control reactions. Melting curve analysis confirmed amplification of a single product in all reactions. Replicate qPCR measurements were performed using DNA from multiple independent bacterial cultures. PhoP binding to target promoters was evaluated by analyzing 1 μl of IP or mock IP DNA, combined with SYBR green mix (Invitrogen) and promoter-specific primers.

Construction of lacZ fusions

To construct the promoter -lacZ fusions, upstream of ansP1 (encompassing −370 to +95 of ansP1), ansP2 (encompassing −384 to +88 of ansP2), ansA (encompassing −379 to +108 of ansA), and sucC (encompassing −270 to +62 of sucC), relative to corresponding ORF start sites were PCR amplified using WT-H37Rv genomic DNA as template and FPansP1up/RPansP1up, FPansP2up/RPansP2up, FPansAup/RPansAup, and FPsucCup/RPsucCup as primer pairs, respectively (Table S2). The PCR-amplified fragments were cloned upstream of lacZ in the promoter-less integrative plasmid pSM128 (64), a kind gift from Prof. Issar Smith, Public Health Research Institute, UMDNJ. These plasmids were introduced in M. smegmatis by electroporation and co-transformants with pME1mL1/pME1mL1-phoP were selected on 7H10 plates containing hygromycin (hyg) and streptomycin (strep). To assess the role of PhoP binding sites, sequences of the repeat units within ansP1up, and ansP2up were mutated using overlap extension method by interchanging the As with Cs, the Gs with Ts, and vice versa as shown in Figure 4, D and E. All the lacZ transcriptional fusions were verified by DNA sequencing.

Regulation of mycobacterial promoter activity in M. Smegmatis

Inducible expression of M. tuberculosis PhoP (WT/mutant) in M. smegmatis from pME1mL1, and measurement of WT/mutant promoter activities from co-transformed strains, as indicated in the figure, were carried out as described previously (34). A420 values of the supernatant were determined, and units of β-galactosidase calculated as follows: 1000 x A420 per mg protein per minute in Miller Units. The protein concentration in the cell lysates was measured, and M. smegmatis co-transformed with pSM128 and pME1ml1 served as the control. PhoP expression from crude lysates of M. smegmatis were detected by Western blot analysis using anti-PhoP antibody.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using Microsoft Excel or GraphPad PRISM v11.0.0, and statistical significance was determined either by Student’s t test or one-way ANOVA, respectively. The results were plotted as the average with error bars representing the standard error of mean (SEM) derived from three biological replicates, unless stated otherwise. p values less than 0.05 were considered significant.
