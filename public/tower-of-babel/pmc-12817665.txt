Prevalence and risk factors for parasitic infections among internally displaced persons in the North-Central Geopolitical Zone of Nigeria
Authors: Raphael Terlumun Ikpe, Luke Ekundayo Edungbola, Amase Nymangee
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundInternally displaced Persons (IDPs) are exceptionally vulnerable to parasitic infections due to the poor sanitary and socioeconomic conditions of IDPs camps and IDPs host communities, where most IDPs live.ObjectivesThis study assessed the prevalence, intensity and risk factors for parasitic infections among IDPs in Benue and Nasarawa States, Nigeria.MethodsA total of 403 participants were randomly selected for the study. Stool, urine and blood samples were collected from each participant. Qualitative data were collected via interview-based questionnaires. Parasites from collected samples were identified via microscopy. The data were analysed using SPSS, and P values less than 0.05 were considered significant in all analyses.ResultsOf the 403 participants, 309 (76.7%) were infected with at least one parasitic infection as follows: Plasmodium falciparum (40.5%), Schistosoma haematobium (23.0%), Hookworms (22.6%), Ascaris lumbricoides (21.1%), Entamoeba histolytica (20.8%), Giardia lamblia (9.4%) and Schistosoma mansoni (6.0%). The mean intensities of P. falciparum and S. haematobium were 1344 ± 311.3 parasites/µL of blood and 15.4 ± 2.8 eggs/10 mL of urine, respectively. The mean intensities of A. lumbricoides, hookworm, E. histolytica, Giardia lamblia and S. mansoni infections were 40.8 (epg), 29.0 (epg), 22.0 (cpg), 9.0 (cpg), and 3.3 (epg), respectively. Risk factors for parasitic infections were identified as water contact activities (aORs: 2.8; 95% CI: 2.2–3.6), non-use of long-lasting treated networks (aORs: 3.3; 95% CI: 2.1–5.7), and walking barefoot (aORs of 2.9; 95% CI: 2.1–3.8). The knowledge, attitudes and practices (KAPs) of the IDPs in relation to parasitic infections were poor.ConclusionsParasitic infections were highly prevalent among the IDPs in North Central Nigeria; however, the intensity of infections was low. Risk factors for parasitic infection were present in the IDP camp and host communities. Moreover, the IDPs had poor KAPs for parasitic infections. Sustained health education and deworming campaigns, and the provision and maintenance of basic Water, Sanitation and Hygiene (WASH) facilities are recommended.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-025-12343-x.

Introduction

Internally displaced persons (IDPs) are highly vulnerable populations whose forced movement and temporary living arrangements often create environmental and socioeconomic conditions that facilitate the transmission of parasitic infections. Internal displacement leads to overcrowded settlements that are often characterized by inadequate water, sanitation and hygiene (WASH), limited access to health care, and increased exposure to vectors and contaminated water or soil. The interplay of these host‒parasite and environmental interactions increase the transmission and impact of intestinal parasites, schistosomes, soil-transmitted helminths (STHs), and vector-borne parasites such as Plasmodium spp [1]. In summary, parasitic infections are closely associated with the triad of ignorance, poverty and neglect [2]. Moreover, population movements due to internal displacement could promote the transmission and spread of parasitic diseases to areas that were previously nonendemic. Displacement, therefore, is a significant determinant of health globally [3].

By the end of 2024, the number of IDPs globally reached an all-time high (83.4 million) of which 38.8 million (47%) were from Sub–Saharan Africa. Nigeria is has the highest number of IDPs in Africa (3,709,000) after Sudan (11,560,000) and DCR (6.890,000) [4]. It has been estimated that approximately 70% of all IDPs are children, women and the elderly [5].

Parasitic infections are endemic across Nigeria [6]. This implies that displaced populations in North Central Nigeria are exposed to parasites that are already highly prevalent in the environment, increasing the risk of transmission and chronic morbidity.

Documenting the prevalence, intensity and specific risk factors for parasitic infections in local IDPs context will provide baseline information needed for informed policy decisions and local, national and international interventions. Given the recurrent displacement episodes and the limited data on the burden of parasitic diseases in North Central Nigeria, this study aimed to determine the prevalence, intensity, and risk factors of parasitic infections among IDPs in North Central Nigeria.

Materials and methods

Study areas

The study areas included seven selected IDPs camps across four local government areas (LGAs) in Benue and Nasarawa States in the North Central Geopolitical Zone of Nigeria Five IDPs camps were selected from 2 LGAs in Benue State and from 2 host communities in 2 LGAs in Nasarawa State (Fig. 1).

Study design and sampling

The study was a cross-sectional study. The samples were collected over a period of 6 months (January to June 2022). IDP camps and host communities were selected by purposive sampling (due to insecurity not all IDPs can be assessed). Participants for the study were selected by stratified random sampling technique. From each IDPs camp or host community, all subjects who agreed to participate in the study were grouped into strata across various demographic characteristics (e.g. age, gender). From each stratum, participants were randomly selected by balloting. The number of participants selected from each stratum was proportional to the total stratum size in the overall population.

Sample size

An expected prevalence of 50% was used to calculate the sample size since there was no prior information on the prevalence of parasitic infections in North Central Nigeria. A total of 403 participants were recruited for the study.

Ethical issues/considerations

The study was conducted with the approval of the Ethics Committee of the Faculty of Basic Clinical Sciences, University of Ilorin, Ilorin (UERC/ASN/2021/2293) and adhered to the ethical guidelines outlined in the 1975 Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from each participant. Consent was obtained from parents of the participants who were less than 18 years old, based on the ethical guidelines, which ensures that any personal data or identifiable information will be appropriately anonymized to protect individual privacy.

Specimen collection

Urine, blood and stool samples were collected for microscopy. Samples were packed at room temperature and transported to the laboratory for examination.

Urine

Each of the selected participants was given prelabelled disposable cups to be filled with all the urine they could void between 11.00 and 15.00 h. Using a 10 mL disposable syringe, 10 mL of homogenously mixed urine was collected from each of the collected urine samples, dispensed into a corresponding labelled universal sample bottle and immediately preserved in 10% formalin.

Blood

Five millilitres of venous blood was obtained from each participant and dispensed into EDTA sample bottles.

Stool

Each participant was provided with a clean labelled plastic universal bottle with a screw cap. They were instructed to pass through the stool and half-fill the bottle and then to cover the bottle tightly with the screw cap. The stool samples were collected and preserved immediately in 10% formalin.

Data collection via questionnaires

A structured, pretested and standardized questionnaire was administered via interview, in which trained interpreters were used to obtain sociodemographic information from each participant.

Microscopy

Samples were examined microscopically 24 h after collection. Each sample slide was examined separately by two persons to ensure quality control.

Stool samples were examined using wet mount and sedimentation techniques for ova, cysts and oocyst of intestinal parasites. The urine samples were prepared using filtration method and examined microscopically for the detection of S. haematobium ova. Blood samples prepared into thick and thin films and each slide was examined microscopically for hemoparasites.

Examination of stool samples

Direct smear method was used to detect parasites from each sample. A spatula was used to mix a small amount of the specimen with saline and iodine to make a thin smear and covered with a cover slip. Saline preparations were made for larvae, cysts and oocysts while iodine preparations were for the used to detect the nuclei of cysts. Thereafter, they were examined using x10 and x40 objective of a compound light microscope to detect the presence of intestinal parasites.

Additionally, the formol ether sedimentation technique was used to increase the sensitivity of the microscopy. About 1 g of stool was emulsified in 10% formalin, the mixture was then filtered through a sieve into a centrifuge tube to remove large debris. About 2mL of ether was added to dissolve fat and further clear debris. The tube was tightly stoppered, shaken gently, and then centrifuged for 2 min at 2500 revolutions per minute (rpm). Thereafter, the supernatant was discarded, and the sediment was used to prepare a smear on a clean slide. The smear was stained with iodine and examined microscopically at X10 and X40 objective for intestinal parasite ova and cyst.

Egg count was done three times consecutively for each specimen using a tally hand counter and the average count was recorded. Intensity was reported as egg per gram (epg) or cyst per gram (cpg) of stool.

Examination of urine samples

Each of the 10mL urine specimen was homogenously mixed before being filtered using the urine filtration method. A fine Millipore wire mesh was used as a filter. The mesh was folded conically, placed in a funnel while the urine filtered through it. The eggs filtered out on the mesh were washed with water using a wash bottle, into a cross-hatched petri dish for the examination of the parasitic eggs, urinary cast, crystals and red blood cells (Pugh, 1978). A compound microscope was used to detect Schistosome eggs at X10 objective. The number of eggs present in each sample was counted using a tally hand counter. Egg count was done three times consecutively for each specimen and the average count was recorded. The intensity of infection was expressed as the number of eggs/10mL of urine.

Examination of blood samples

Both thick and thin film smears were prepared and stained with Giemsa stain. Each slide was then examined microscopically under immersion oil at X100 objective. Plasmodium species were detected based on the characteristic morphology and staining. Statistical analysis

The statistical analysis IBM SPSS software package (Statistical Package for Social Sciences, version 20 for Windows IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA)) was used to analyse the data. Continuous variables are presented as the means, and categorical variables are presented as numbers and percentages. Categorical data were analysed for comparison via chi-square tests. A multivariate logistic analysis was used to identify risk factors for parasitic infections. P value less than 0.05 was considered significant in all analyses.

Results

Demographic characteristics of the IDPs in Nasarawa and Benue States

A total of 403 IDPs respondents participated in the study. Among the 403 IDPs, 250 (62.0%) were selected from 5 IDPs camps in Benue State and 153 (38.0%) were selected from 2 host communities in Nasarawa State.

Of the 403 respondents, 53.8% were females, and 46.2% were males. The age of the IDPs ranged from 5 to 70 years. The mean age was 21.2 ± 16.7 years. A total of 100 (24.8%) females of child-bearing age (18–45 years) participated in the study; 33 (33.0%) were pregnant. The majority (52.6%) of the IDPs had been displaced for 3–4 years and 11 (2.7%) of the IDPs had been displaced for more than 6 years (Table 1).

Prevalence of parasitic infections among the IDPs in Nasarawa and Benue States

Among the 403 respondents, 309 (76.7%) were infected with at least one parasitic infection in Nasarawa and Benue States. A total of 250 respondents were examined in Benue State, of whom 182 (72.8%) were infected with at least one parasitic infection. Among the 153 IDPs examined in Nasarawa State, 127 (83.0%) were infected with at least one parasite.

Among the 217 females examined, 51 (81.2%) were infected, whereas 158 (72.8%) of the 186 males examined were infected with at least one parasite (P < 0.05). The respondents between the ages of 8 and 19 years had the highest prevalence of infection. while the lowest prevalence of infection was recorded among the oldest age group (≥ 20 years). The difference in overall prevalence by age group was statistically significant (P < 0.05) (Table 2).

Intensity of parasitic infections

The overall mean intensity of P. falciparum infection was 1344 ± 311.3 parasites/µL. The difference in P. falciparum intensity by sex and age group was statistically significant, and the difference in the intensity of A. lumbricoides, hookworm and E. histolytica infection was statistically significant according to sex (P < 0.005).

The overall S. haematobium intensity was 15.4 ± 2.8 eggs/10 mL of urine. The intensity of S. haematobium was highest in the 11–13 years age group (19.8 ± 6.0). The oldest age group had the lowest S. haematobium intensity (1.5 ± 0.5). The difference in S. haematobium intensity by age group was statistically significant. The male and female IDPs had similar intensities (10.2 eggs/10 mL of urine and 10.3 eggs/10 mL of urine, respectively). (Tables 5 and 6).

Knowledge attitudes and practices of IDPs on parasitic infections

The knowledge of parasites and parasitic infections was graded on the basis of the number of correct answers provided by the IDPs in the questionnaire administered (Good-3/3; 2/3-Fair; ≤1/3-Poor). There was poor knowledge of parasites, sites of infection on the body, transmission and prevention among the IDPs.

Among the 403 IDPs, 218 (54.1%) had poor knowledge of the transmission of parasitic infections. Few (0.3%) had good knowledge of parasite transmission. A total of 363 (90.1%) of the 403 IDPs had poor knowledge of parasitic infection prevention.

A majority (66.0%) of the IDPs had boreholes as their source of drinking water. Most (77.2%) defecated via the toilet, and 43.4% always used mosquito nets. Only 79 (19.6%) of the IDPs had water contact activities. In terms of hygiene, 57.1% of the participants always washed fruits and vegetables before consumption. A total of 41.7% walked barefooted, and 49.9% were dewormed less than a year ago (Table 8).

Discussion

The high prevalence of parasitic infections (76.6%) recorded in this study could be linked to the poor sanitary conditions of the IDPs camps and host communities, and poor personal hygiene, inadequate potable water supply and ignorance of parasitic infections which were the plight of most of the IDPs. Additionally, the nutritional status of IDPs is generally poor, consequently impairing their immunity to parasitic infections. The prevalence of parasitic infections reported in this study is similar to other findings on the status of parasitic infections among IDPs in Nigeria and Africa. In 2019 [5], reported a 79.5% prevalence of parasitic infections among IDPs in Nasarawa State. In 2022 [7], reported a prevalence of 89.6% in Benin, Edo State, southern Nigeria. In Borno State, Northeast Nigeria, a 62.0% prevalence of urinary schistosomiasis was reported by [8]. In another study among IDPs in Borno State, Nigeria, the prevalence of schistosomiasis (27.0%) was reported by [9]. Elsewhere in Africa, a 61.0% prevalence of malaria was reported in Sudan [10]. The obvious disparity in the prevalence of parasitic infections across the different geographical locations could be due to variations in the time or season during which the studies were conducted, the methodology used, the duration of the study, the targeted populations and the type and effectiveness of the parasitic disease intervention programs deployed in the different geographical areas.

Plasmodium falciparum

Malaria remains the most significant parasitic disease globally (and among vulnerable groups), with the highest morbidity and morbidity among parasitic infections. This is largely attributable to ignorance and nonavailability of drugs, a lack of vaccines, the resistance of anopheline mosquitoes to insecticides and the resistance of Plasmodium to chemotherapy [11].

Other complicating factors that could be responsible for the high prevalence of malaria reported in this study include striking mosquito behavior (endophilic, anthropophilic and endophagic), poor housing (tents, houses with no mosquito nets), ignorance and weakened immunity of the IDPs due to poor nutrition and psychological stress.

The high prevalence (40.5%) of malaria infection among the IDPs in this study was similar to the 41.7% reported in Cameroun by [12], the 61.1% reported among IDPs in Sudan [10] and the 58% reported among IDPs in Congo [13]. A lower prevalence of 17% has been reported in the Democratic Republic of the Congo and 11.5% in Myanmar [14, 15].

Notably, the IDPs slept in tents and in unused classrooms, with the majority not using Long Lasting Treated Nets (LLTN) at all or not using the LLTN properly. These predisposing factors could explain the high prevalence of malaria among the IDPs reported in this study.

Strikingly, in this study, the prevalence of P. falciparum infection was similar regardless of pregnancy status, sex, and location of the IDP. Other studies also reported that there was no significant difference in P. falciparum infection [13, 16, 17]. These findings suggest that the susceptibility of IDPs to P. falciparum malaria is not sex dependent, as both sexes are equally exposed to the same environmental conditions and mosquito bites. In contrast [15] and [10], reported a higher prevalence of malaria among male IDPs in Myanmar and Sudan, respectively, whereas [13] reported a higher prevalence among female IDPs in the Democratic Republic of the Congo. This variation could be associated with the levels of exposure to mosquito bites by the males and females in the study areas on the basis of sociocultural factors.

There was also a significantly greater prevalence of malaria among teenagers than among children and adults in this study. This finding contrasts with those of previous studies [17, 18], where a higher prevalence was reported among preschool-aged and school-aged children. This report, however, is in accordance with reports by [13] in the Democratic Republic of the Congo [15, 16], who reported higher prevalence rates among teenagers in Myanmar and Nigeria, respectively. The higher prevalence among the teenagers suggested that there was no community-acquired immunity among the teenage IDPs [19], and the teenagers were also more exposed to mosquito bites, probably because of more time outdoors.

The variations in malaria incidence by age group could be attributed to disparities in the levels of exposure to infection and levels of compliance with the use of LLTNs. IDPs who stayed in displacement for more than 6 years were significantly more likely to be infected than those who were in displacement for fewer years. This could be a result of active transmission of P. falciparum in the camps and probably a poorer attitude toward the use of LLTNs due to their complacency.

Despite control programs focused on LLTN distribution, displacement, particularly due to violent conflicts, increases the risk of malaria [14]. There is evidence that malaria is more prevalent among IDPs than among nondisplaced populations. For example, the prevalence of malaria was reported to be 4.5 times higher among IDPs than among nondisplaced individuals in Colombia [20]. On the other hand, the relatively stationary status of the IDPs could be an advantage for concerted and effective intervention strategies, as reported at the China–Myanmar border; malaria prevalence was significantly lower among IDPs than among neighbouring villages for effective interventions [15].

Schistosomiasis haematobium

Both urinary and intestinal schistosomiasis were prevalent among IDPs. However, the prevalence (23.03%) of schistosomiasis haematobium was higher than the prevalence of schistosomiasis mansoni (5.96%). According to [21], the Bulinus snail (S. haematobium intermediate host) was more widespread and abundant than the Biomphalaria snail (S. mansoni intermediate host) in Nigeria. The prevalence of schistosomiasis haematobium (23.03%) recorded in this study was higher than the national average prevalence of 9.5% reported previously [22]. Similarly, a prevalence of 21.9% has been reported among riverine communities in Borno State [23]. Higher prevalences of 65.7% and 69% were reported in the Jidawa and Zobiya communities, respectively, in Jigawa State, Nigeria [24].

The variation in the prevalence of schistosomiasis could be due to the focal nature of schistosomiasis prevalence, socio cultural factors (e.g. washing clothes and kitchen utensils, and fetching water from the dams and streams are strictly the women and girls’ responsibility in some communities) and variations in the methodological approach of the various studies, the duration of the studies and differences in the study populations.

In this study, the Kadarko host community in Nasarawa State and the Ayem IDP camp in Benue State had the highest prevalence rates of schistosomiasis haematobium (41.9%) and schistosomiasis mansoni (14.29%), respectively. This is probably due to the proximity of both locations to streams, which are the major sources of water for domestic and recreational activities by the IDPs and the host community. The prevalence of schistosomiasis haematobium was also highest among adolescents (14–16 years) and lowest among adults (> 20 years). This could be because schistosomiasis is a chronic disease, and clinical manifestations are more likely to peak in the adolescent stage. This age group is also more active and more involved in swimming; thus, these individuals are more exposed to reinfections. Other studies have reported a relatively high prevalence of schistosomiasis haematobium among teenagers [25–27]. However, this finding contrasts with reports by [28], who reported a higher prevalence in children younger than 10 years.

The higher prevalence among the males observed in this study may be because males tend to be more engaged in water contact activities such as swimming, irrigation farming and fishing than females are. Other reports [29, 30] also revealed a higher prevalence among males. However, this is contrary to reports by [17], where females were more infected than males were. These variations may be due to differences in the methodology used and the timing of the studies.

Intestinal parasitic infections

The soil-transmitted nematodes, protozoans (G. lamblia and E. histolytica) and the trematode S. mansoni are some of the most prevalent intestinal parasites in Nigeria. There was no significant difference in the prevalence of all intestinal parasitic infections by age or sex. This could be a result of equal exposure and ignorance of the IDPs to intestinal parasite infections. Walking barefoot was common among both genders and all age groups. Most of the adults were farmers, and soil contact was common. The sanitary conditions, personal hygiene levels and socioeconomic status of the participants were the same. Thus, they were equally exposed to intestinal parasites.

In general, the prevalence of intestinal parasites (21.8%) reported in this study was significantly lower than the prevalence of 89.6% reported among IDPs in Benin City [7]. These variations could be due to differences in the time/season in which the studies were conducted, the WASH conditions in the study areas, interventions prior to the study the methods used, the target population, the age group and the diagnostic methods used.

Coinfections

Coinfections of intestinal parasites, S. haematobium and P. falciparum were observed among the IDPs, although the prevalences were low. Coinfections of parasitic infections have been reported in different parts of Africa [31]. Coinfections of parasitic infections are a common phenomenon as a result of similar environmental conditions (poor sanitation, human activities and behaviors (poor personal hygiene) that favour the transmission of all parasitic infections [32, 33]. The prevalence of P. falciparum among children coinfected with intestinal parasites could be linked to suppressed immunity and increased susceptibility to malaria due to intestinal parasitic infections [34, 35]. The implication of coinfections is multifaceted. For instance, coinfections pose significant challenges for control, effective treatment and cost of treatment. Drug selection and priority treatment are key in promoting effectiveness and preventing complications while treating coinfections. Furthermore, Mass Drug Administration (MDA) could be an effective intervention against STH infections alone, whereas coinfections with schistosomiasis, malaria and amoebia require more than one intervention. In addition, the severity of morbidity and complications due to coinfections is greater than that of single infections.

Intensity of parasitic infections

The greater P. falciparum intensity among the males than among the females aligns with other reports in northwestern and southwestern Nigeria [36, 37], where P. falciparum intensity was significantly greater in males and females. The female IDPs had less P. falciparum intensity because of their higher level of compliance with the use of LLTNs, as observed during the study.

In line with the findings of this study, other studies reported a higher prevalence of malaria among the younger age group [38, 39]. Since Immunological memory increases with age adults are less susceptible to malaria than younger adults are. This possibly accounts for the greater intensity recorded among the younger age groups.

According to reports, a majority of people infected with S. haematobium have light to moderate infection [40]. In this report, the overall intensity of S. haematobium (15.4 ± 2.8) was light according to the WHO classification [41]. The intensity of infections plays a key role in schistosomiasis transmission dynamics. The implication of the light intensity of schistosomiasis infections is that the transmission potential of the parasite is low because, from the final host (man) to the intermediate host (snail), there are high chances of destruction of the fragile schistosome ova in the environment. Thus, a low intensity suggests a low transmission potential. However, the morbidity due to infection remains significant considering the chronic nature of schistosomiasis infection.

The mean intensity of S. haematobium infection was similar among males (10.2 ± 9.7 eggs/10 mL of urine) and females (10.3 ± 11.3 eggs/10 mL of urine). This is contrary to previous reports [40, 42], where intensity varied with gender. The similar intensity of S. haematobium infection among males and females in this study is probably because both genders are not exposed to water contact activities, as most of the IDP camp had fewer water-contact activities. In fact, most of the infections were likely acquired in their indigenous communities before displacement.

The highest intensity of S. haematobium infection (19.8 ± 6.0 eggs/10 mL of urine) was among the 11–13-year age group, whereas the oldest age group, ≥ 20 years, had the lowest intensity of infection (1.5 ± 0.5 eggs/10 mL). This is likely because the older age group has developed partial immunity as a result of long-term exposure to infections. Moreover, children are more likely to engage in longer water contact activities like swimming in fresh water bodies than the older age groups. This could increase their likelihood of infection, re-infection and multiple infection hence higher intensity of infections.

The classification was based on the WHO classification [43]. All the intestinal parasitic infections encountered were of light intensity. This finding contrasts with other reports in southwestern Ethiopia [44] and in Kogi State, Nigeria [45], where moderate and heavy intensities of intestinal parasites were reported. However, in the same studies where moderate and heavy intensities were reported, low intensities were also recorded [44–46]. The intensity of infections varies with the duration of infection, reinfection, nutritional status and environmental factors. Thus, the intensity of infections may vary even among individuals from the same sample population. The recent use of mass Drug Administration (MDA) and diagnostic techniques could also be factors that contribute to variations in intestinal parasite intensity. In this study, approximately half (49.9%) of the IDPs had been dewormed less than 7 months prior to the study, and sampling was limited to cross-sectional sampling. These findings could explain the low intensity of intestinal parasites recorded in this study. Moreover, due to infrastructural and logistical limitations, stool samples could not be examined immediately after collection, as such, protozoan trophozoites could not be detected, therefore, the intensities of G. lamblia and E. histolytica could be under reported. However, given the chronic nature of parasitic diseases even low parasite intensities still pose significant health and nutritional impact.

Risk factors for parasitic infections

Parasitic diseases transmission begins with contact with a parasite vector or infective stage of the parasite. Thus, any behavior or practice which enhances contact with vectors or infective stages of parasites are potentially risk factors for parasitic infections.

In this study, none use of LLTN was identified as a risk factor for P. falciparum infection. This is obviously due to increased exposure to female anopheline mosquito bites among those who do not use LLTN. Walking barefoot, is a major risk factor for hook worm infections because of the likelihood of skin penetration with infective larvae stages of hookworm if an individual walks on contaminated soil. This explains why IDPs who walked barefoot were significantly more likely to be infected with hookworm. Water contact activities were identified as a risk factor for schistosomiasis infections among the IDPs. Those who engaged in swimming, rice farming, washing or fetching water from the dams were significantly more likely to be infected, particularly by S.haematobium and S. mansoni. This is also linked to the increased possibility of skin penetration by infective cercaria stages of schistosomes if the fresh water body had been contaminated.

KAPs of the IDPs in parasitic infections

The poor knowledge, attitudes and practices related to parasitic infections reported in this study are similar to those reported in previous studies in other parts of Nigeria, Yemen and Malaysia [47–49]. In contrast [50], reported that most of the pupils in Ebonyi, Southeast Nigeria, had significantly high knowledge of the transmission and prevention of parasitic infections. KAPs towards parasitic infection are important drivers of parasite transmission. For instance, most of the IDPs did not know that walking barefoot is a risk factor for hookworm infection or swimming in fresh water bodies exposes them to schistosomes infection. If they knew the implications of practices such as open defecation, urinating in the stream while washing, or swimming, walking barefoot, incorrect or non-use of LLTN the IDPs would not engage in such practices. With improved awareness, the attitudes and practices of the IDPs towards parasitic diseases will be better, consequently, parasite transmission and infections will be significantly reduced in the IDPs camps and host communities.

Conclusions

Parasitic infections were highly prevalent among the IDPs in north central Nigeria. Although the intensity of infections is light according to WHO standards, the parasitic infections burden remains epidemiologically significant. Frequent water contact activities like swimming in fresh water bodies like dams and irrigation farming, non-use of LLTN and walking barefooted were identified as risk factors for parasitic infections. Most of the IDPs had poor KAPs regarding parasitic infections. Government and development partners should integrate IDPs into national parasitic disease control programs through coordinated, multisectoral strategies. Sustained health education, mass deworming campaigns, vector control strategies, provision and maintenance of basic WASH facilities for IDPs camps and host communities is recommended. Periodic evaluation of these interventions programs is crucial for continuous improvements and better outcomes.

Limitations of the study

Protozoan trophozoites were not be detected via microscopy since they are destroyed upon preservation of stool samples in 10% formalin. Thus, the prevalence of G. lamblia and E. histolytica reported based on detection of cyst may be underestimates. Additionally, this is a cross-sectional study in which samples were collected at a single point. This could also limit the estimated intensities of infections.
