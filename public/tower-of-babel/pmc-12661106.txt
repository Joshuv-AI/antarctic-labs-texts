Delayed presentation of splenic pseudoaneurysm and its management: A case report
Authors: Madhur Uniyal, Priya Ahuja, Divakar Goyal
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSplenic pseudoaneurysm following splenic trauma is a rare but serious complication with a high risk of rupture. Percutaneous embolization has emerged as the preferred treatment modality in hemodynamically stable patients. However, the exact timing of pseudoaneurysm appearance and the management of multiple pseudoaneurysms remain unclear and lack standardized protocols.MethodsWe present a case of a 35-year-old female with a Grade 4 splenic injury (according to the AAST grading system) who did not have any pseudoaneurysm on her initial contrast-enhanced CT. However, on post-injury day 5, ultrasound (USG) revealed multiple splenic pseudoaneurysms, which were further confirmed by CT angiography. The patient underwent percutaneous Gelfoam embolization to manage these various pseudoaneurysms.ResultsThe patient underwent successful Gelfoam embolization, effectively controlling the multiple pseudoaneurysms in the spleen with no immediate complications. Post-procedural recovery was uneventful, and the patient was discharged after appropriate monitoring.ConclusionA well-established protocol is necessary to promptly diagnose splenic pseudoaneurysms after solid organ injury. Follow-up imaging, such as USG and CT angiography, should be performed promptly. To reduce interobserver variations, it is recommended that the same radiologist perform the follow-up ultrasound for consistency in the interpretation and detection of complications.Keywords: Splenic injury, Pseudoaneurysms, Management

Introduction

Splenic artery pseudoaneurysm is among the most common visceral artery pseudoaneurysms, with a reported incidence of 2.5 % [1]. The incidence of rupture in these cases has been reported to be as high as 37 % [2]. Despite its relatively high incidence, the exact timing for developing pseudoaneurysms remains poorly defined, though they have been reported to grow within the first week of injury [3].

A significant challenge in managing splenic artery pseudoaneurysms is the lack of well-established protocols for patient selection, timing of follow-up, and the type of radiological investigation to use. Delayed detection of vascular injuries, sometimes manifesting as late as one-week post-injury, can be detrimental to the patient's outcome and complicate management strategies [4].

Recent advances have led to a shift in management paradigms toward splenic-preserving techniques, such as endovascular and percutaneous embolization, which have become preferred treatment methods. In light of this, we present a case of multiple splenic pseudoaneurysms detected 5 days post-injury, successfully managed by endovascular embolization.

Case study

A 35-year-old female was brought to the Trauma Emergency Department 12 h after falling from a tractor. She was alert and conscious upon presentation but reported left upper quadrant pain, difficulty breathing, and mild chest discomfort. There were no signs of neurological deficits or loss of consciousness during the incident.

Initial examination and investigations

On examination, the patient was hemodynamically stable, with stable vital signs. A FAST (Focused Assessment with Sonography for Trauma) scan revealed free fluid in the splenorenal space, suggesting intra-abdominal injury. Laboratory values, including haematocrit, base deficit, and lactate levels, were within normal limits, showing no signs of acute haemorrhagic shock. The patient was diagnosed with Grade 4 splenic injury after the initial scans. Despite the severity of the injuries, the patient remained stable, with no further deterioration of clinical or laboratory parameters. Given her hemodynamic stability, the decision was made to manage the patient non-operatively.

Nonoperative management

The patient was closely monitored in the Intensive Care Unit (ICU) for 48 h following admission. Serial lactate, base deficit, and haematocrit measurements were taken, all remaining stable. No signs of ongoing haemorrhage or clinical deterioration were noted, allowing for continued nonoperative management.

Follow-up imaging and complications

On post-injury day 5, as per institutional protocol, the patient underwent a follow-up CT imaging of the abdomen. Which confirm the presence of multiple pseudoaneurysms in the splenic artery (Fig. 1, Fig. 2).

Intervention

Given the risk of rupture and further haemorrhage from the pseudoaneurysms, the decision was made to perform selective angioembolization (Fig. 3). The procedure was successfully carried out using Gelfoam embolization, effectively controlling the bleeding and preventing further complications. The patient tolerated the procedure well, with no immediate post-procedural complications.

Outcome and discharge

The patient remained hemodynamically stable after successful angioembolization. Her laboratory values continued to remain within normal limits, and no further complications developed. The patient was discharged on post-injury day 10 with appropriate follow-up instructions and recommendations for continued observation for delayed complications.

Discussion

The spleen is among the most frequently injured organs in blunt abdominal trauma, and injury to the spleen may result in severe complications, including haemorrhagic shock.

In the past, there was increase in the rate of splenectomies due to ruptured spleen in the 1950s. Nevertheless, the management strategy for splenic injuries has evolved from surgical treatment to nonoperative management (NOM) [5].

Even though the success of NOM is high, its effectiveness shows a decrease with increasing severity of the injury, mainly as graded by the American Association for the Surgery of Trauma (AAST) classification system, from Grade 1 to Grade 5 [6].

Nevertheless, with the advent of newer modalities, such as splenic artery embolization (SAE), preserving the spleen in trauma is much easier now. According to Gaarder et al., the splenic salvage rate has been significantly improved, from 57 % to 75 %, by employing SAE [7]. The selective embolization of the splenic artery is used in this procedure to achieve control over the haemorrhage, thus sparing a splenectomy.

As per a review by Olthof et al. the indications for SAE are: [8].

Although these signs are usually detectable in the first few days after trauma, the precise time of pseudoaneurysm development is not well established. Pseudoaneurysms and other vascular injuries diagnosed late, after discharge of the patient, can be life-threatening.

Weinberg et al. in their study have mentioned in their study that 80 % of patients with splenic trauma pseudoaneurysms were not identified on initial scan but after 3 days when scans were repeated [9]. Another study by Wallen et al. demonstrated that 67 % of splenic PSAs were detected in delayed fashion [10]. These studies and others have provided evidence to support routine surveillance imaging for high-grade splenic injury. This was evidenced in our case, where a pseudoaneurysm was found five days after the first trauma on a follow-up ultrasound scan.

In addition, transcatheter embolization has explicitly been effective in dealing with large or multiple pseudoaneurysms, as observed in our case. After the embolization has been completed, it is essential to ensure that there is a clearly established follow-up schedule to check for the patient's recovery and to observe for any expected complications.

In our case, follow-up with an ultrasound scan was done one-week post-procedure and then another month later to assess healing and identify any evidence of further complications.

In a recent study by Radding et al. the authors have advocated the role High-resolution CT imaging to detect pseudoaneurysms and prophylactic angioembolisation for Grade 4 and Grade 5 splenic injuries but yet not mentioned the benefits the same in the success of NOM [11].

Nevertheless, no study in the literature to our best of knowledge has come with a well-defined protocol for the management of pseudoaneurysm as well timing of the repeat imaging to identify such vascular lesions.

Conclusion

In conclusion, while SAE has revolutionized the management of traumatic splenic injuries, the establishment of a standardized protocol for the early identification and ongoing monitoring of vascular complications is essential. This case report serves as a call to action for clinicians and institutions to consider the development of such protocols, ensuring that patients who undergo SAE receive the highest standard of care throughout their recovery journey.
