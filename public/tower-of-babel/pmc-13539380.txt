Engineering a sulfadoxine-functionalized NH2–UiO-66(Zr) metal–organic framework via post-synthetic modification for dual-mode spectrophotometric and fluorometric detection of Cd2+
Authors: Abeer Abdulaziz H. Bukhari, Nadia H. Elsayed, Nawaa Ali H. Alshammari, Maymounah N. Alharthi, Duna A.K. Alenazi, Menier Al-Anazi, Hatem A. Al-Aoh, M. A. M. El-Afify
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The sustainable development of a metal–organic framework (MOF) composite was performed for the sensing of hazardous cadmium(ii) contamination in water, providing a successful MOF material through post-synthetic modification involving the integration of SDX and GA (SDX–GA@UiO-66). Physicochemical characterization was performed to confirm the formation of the desired composite along with the evaluation of heteroatom integration in the framework to clearly elucidate the pore structure and crystal morphology. Detection was achieved through two complementary optical transduction techniques, UV-vis spectrophotometry and steady-state fluorescence spectroscopy (λex = 360 nm, λem = 440 nm), both arising from chelation-enhanced quenching (CHEQ) of the SDX–GA chromophore upon Cd2+ binding. The covalently immobilized SDX–GA moiety serves as the fluorophore, with Cd2+ coordination at its nitrogen and oxygen donor atoms promoting heavy-atom-assisted intersystem crossing that quenches its emission. The UV-vis channel achieved a linear range of 0.0–0.12 ppm with a calculated LOD of 0.0181 ppm, and the platform provided dual-mode detection with selectivity against fifteen competing cations, achieving >85% signal response compared to the other coexisting metal cations. Additionally, cadmium(ii) recovery was validated at multiple concentration levels using the developed sensor, with recoveries ranging from 97.13–100.77%. DFT analysis was performed on the sensing performance of the fabricated framework to better understand the coordination of cadmium(ii) by the platform in the presence of coexisting ions. This sustainable, reusable MOF-based platform offers a dependable strategy for addressing environmental cadmium contamination and the associated risks of severe cadmium poisoning worldwide.

Introduction

Cadmium (Cd) is a toxic trace metal, predominantly encountered in the environment in its divalent Cd2+ oxidation state, that enters aquatic ecosystems mainly through various anthropogenic sources such as geochemical weathering and discharge of industrial effluent from electroplating, battery manufacturing, pigment production, etc.1 Another major anthropogenic source of cadmium is the use of cadmium impurities in phosphate-based fertilizers.2 Among aquatic compartments, surface water and groundwater in the vicinity of industrial effluent discharge are the most consistently reported to be Cd-impacted, particularly where electroplating, nickel–cadmium battery manufacturing, and mining or ore-processing operations are present.3 Groundwater directly underlying and adjacent to electroplating facilities has been specifically documented to accumulate cadmium alongside other heavy metals, with contamination persisting and continuing to spread even after the facility ceases operation.4 Cadmium is an important environmental pollutant in the aquatic environment. Thus it is important to note the toxicity of cadmium and its impact on aquatic organisms comprehensively.5 Untreated industrial effluent, often compounded by monsoon runoff in affected regions, can reach concentrations of several hundred micrograms per liter, 10 to 90 times the WHO guideline of 0.003 ppm for drinking water.6 Cd(ii) can enter the body through respiratory and digestive pathways (e.g., drinking water and food containing bioaccumulated Cd), and, owing to its similar ionic radius to zinc(ii) and calcium(ii), evades discrimination by cellular transport machinery.7,8 This mimicry drives oxidative stress and the accumulation of reactive oxygen species, disrupting cellular signaling pathways and damaging cellular macromolecules and DNA.9 Long-term occupational exposure causes progressive nephrotoxicity, including Fanconi syndrome and skeletal demineralization, the classical example being itai–itai disease in cadmium-endemic regions of Japan.10,11 The International Agency for Research on Cancer classifies cadmium as a Group 1 human carcinogen, with established links to lung and, to a lesser extent, kidney cancer; an association with endometrial cancer has been reported but is not yet conclusive, and no causal link has been established for skin, bladder, or prostate cancers.10,12 Cadmium also bioaccumulates in aquatic organisms, impairing phytoplankton photosynthesis and reproductive success in higher organisms.13

While conventional methods have proven effective in quantifying cadmium and other toxic heavy metals, those methods do have limitations. Current methods including ICP-MS, GF-AAS, and ICP-OES are expensive, require complicated procedures, require skilled operators, and often need to be conducted in centralized labs. Despite their advantages, electrochemical methods including ASV can be hampered by issues of fouling of the working electrode and signal drift. Thus, there is a need for a simpler, portable alternative capable of generating reliable regulatory-grade results.14 Solid-state optical sensors offer a viable alternative for reliable quantification of cadmium and potentially other heavy metals as well. These sensors would offer regulatory-grade sensitivity and operational simplicity while providing rapid response and the ability to be deployed in situ, while also being inexpensive enough to allow for widespread adoption. In terms of practicality, they may also be as effective as current methods, aside from the fact that they would have to be operated in ways substantially different from cadmium quantification.

The development of advanced porous materials as sensing platforms has substantially accelerated during the last decade, motivated by the need for high surface-area scaffolds that concentrate analytes from dilute solution, and present densely arranged receptor groups for coordinative interaction.15 Metal–organic frameworks (MOFs) are crystalline, nanoporous coordination polymers constructed from metal nodes and polytopic organic linkers. They have attracted particular interest in this context, because their architectural parameters (pore diameter, surface area, chemical functionality) can be rationally engineered at the molecular design stage.16 Other competing platforms, such as mesoporous silicas (SBA-15, MCM-41) and carbon-based materials (graphene oxide, carbon nanotubes), offer high surface areas but lack the chemical modularity and crystallographic precision of MOFs. Among MOF platforms investigated for sensing in the aqueous phase, zirconium-carboxylate frameworks hold a privileged position, owed to their outstanding thermal and chemical stability that overcomes the water instability that limits the use of many copper- and zinc-based MOF systems.17

NH2–UiO-66(Zr) in the UiO (Universitetet i Oslo) family is built from Zr6O4(OH)4 secondary building units and 2-aminoterephthalic acid linkers and combines the strong Zr(iv)-carboxylate coordination bond (dissociation energy ≈ 480 kJ mol−1) with pendant primary amine groups that protrude into the pore interior and act as reactive handles for covalent post-synthetic chemistry.18,19 The structural integrity of the framework is maintained after long-term immersion in water, acidic solutions (pH ≥ 1) and boiling water, which can be attributed to the high coordination number (12) of the Zr6-oxo cluster and the resulting redundancy of metal linker bonds.20,21 The present contribution is primarily focused on the exploitation of –NH2 groups at the level of the aromatic linker through essential reactions such as Schiff base condensation, amide coupling, and sulfonylation reactions to develop new ligands that can be deployed as sensors and adsorbents having tremendous potential for the extraction/detection of heavy metals from different environments.22,23 Despite this progress, many of these materials still suffer from significant limitations related to properties such as structural stability and environmental functionality: They can be sensitive to solvent exposure, oxygenated or strongly polar solvents, or acids or bases. In this regard, research based on the use of zeolitic materials looks especially promising. Environmentally stable MOFs are designed with the aim of such important properties as mechanical strength, chemical stability and reproducible structural predictability under serious technological constraints (high temperatures; exposure to corrosive media). In this regard, NH2–UiO-66(Zr) with its good microporosity (BET surface area of 800–1100 m2 g−1, pore-size-selective analyte pre-concentration) appears to be an excellent candidate for creating structural and specific sensing modules of multi-functional sensing composites.24,25

In the past years, considerable attention has been given to the discovery of new types of metal–organic frameworks (MOFs) that have enhanced chelation properties. Post-synthetic modification via the known synthetic methodology Schiff base condensation provides a tool to conjugate multi-donor ligands onto MOFs on the surface of the MOF and inside the pores of the MOFs.26 In this work, we used glutaraldehyde as a bifunctional crosslinker to afford a stable and efficient framework for the coordination chemistry with desirably coordinated metal ions. Sulfadoxine (SDX) from the sulfanilamide class of compounds was selected to be linked to the GLA and characterized. SDX was selected on structural grounds: it presents a terminal primary aromatic amine that permits Schiff base conjugation to the glutaraldehyde spacer, together with an accessible sulfonamide nitrogen, sulfonyl oxygen, and pyrimidine nitrogen donor set, and it is commercially available; no alternative sulfonamides were screened experimentally. The connecting GLA via Y-type linkers enabled a degree of motion in the aforementioned linkage to the negative charge from the sulfanilamide moiety which is crucial for the recognition of biological ions. The SDX–GA@UiO-66 composite synthesized and tested here, shows excellent absorption and fluorescence signals when coordinated with Cd2+via the sulfanilamide moiety on the SDX ligand.27 By varying the concentration of Cd2+, one can vary the degree of fluorescent signal observed from the excitation wavelength of 360 nm. By controlling the element of motion in the linker from GLA, we will be able to tune the selectivity of Cd2+ ions without affecting other biological cations. A directly comparable PSM strategy on the parent UiO-66–NH2 scaffold, in which the framework amine is conjugated to a fluorescent probe for small-molecule detection, has been reported by Tang et al.28

Among the closest prior-art comparators, a dopant-free UiO-67 framework has recently been reported for Cd2+ detection,29 and a sulfhydryl/sulfo-functionalized UiO-66 has been reported for Cd2+ adsorption;30 neither combines dual-mode optical transduction with the computationally validated, sulfonamide-based coordination chemistry presented here. Despite significant advances in the development of cadmium-selective solid-state optical sensors including dithizone-modified silica sol colorimetric sensors,31 AgInZnS quantum dot fluorescent sensors,32 nanocellulose–Schiff base fluorometric sensors,33 and deferasirox-functionalized MOF sensors34 some limitations still exist: most of the reported platforms are based on a single detection mode, lacking redundancy and dynamic range; naked-eye responses are rare or lack characterization; regeneration performances are often demonstrated for less than three cycles; and computational validation of the sensing mechanism through quantum chemical calculations is uncommon. The detection principle underlying this platform is chelation-enhanced quenching (CHEQ): Cd2+ coordination at the immobilized SDX–GA chromophore quenches both its UV-vis absorbance and its fluorescence emission, providing the dual-mode optical readout characterized in this work. In this work, we address these gaps with four specific contributions: (i) structure-guided selection of a dual mode (UV-vis and fluorescence) MOF composite sensor operative at a single optimum pH; (ii) rigorous mechanistic characterization by 2D correlation spectroscopy identifying the sequential functional group engagement upon Cd2+ binding; (iii) DFT frontier molecular orbital (FMO) analysis providing quantum chemical rationalization of the optical response; and (iv) demonstration of six-cycle reusability and validated performance in real water matrices, benchmarked against ICP-OES.35–37

Herein, we report the synthesis, full physicochemical characterization, computational validation and analytical determination of SDX–GA@UiO-66 as a dual-transduction sensor for the detection of trace Cd2+ in environmentally relevant water samples.

Material and methods

The reagents and instrumentation information were presented with details at SI.

Synthesis of NH2–UiO-66(Zr)

An optimized solvothermal process that was modified from accepted literature methods was used to create NH2–UiO-66(Zr).38 In brief, ZrCl4 (232 mg, 0.995 mmol) and 2-aminoterephthalic acid (NH2–BDC, 181 mg, 1.000 mmol) were separately dissolved in anhydrous DMF (20 mL each) with slight ultrasonication for 15 min. The two solutions were combined in a 100 mL Teflon-lined stainless-steel autoclave and glacial acetic acid (0.8 mL) was added as a modulator to control Zr6-cluster nucleation and improve crystallinity. The autoclave was heated at 120 °C for 24 h in a convection oven. The yellow precipitate was collected by centrifugation (10 000 rpm, 15 min), washed sequentially with DMF (3 × 30 mL) and anhydrous EtOH (3 × 30 mL) to remove unreacted linker and solvent molecules, and dried under vacuum at 150 °C for 12 h to afford activated NH2–UiO-66(Zr) as a yellow microcrystalline powder after cooling to room temperature.39

Synthesis of SDX–GA@UiO-66 sensor

Unlike direct amine–SDX coupling, which is not chemically feasible given that both the MOF surface amine and the SDX aromatic amine are nucleophilic partners lacking a mutual electrophilic handle, glutaraldehyde serves as a bifunctional bis-aldehyde spacer that sequentially bridges the two amines via double Schiff base condensation, first forming an imine linkage with the MOF surface and, in a second stage, a further imine linkage with SDX. The SDX–GA@UiO-66 sensor composite was prepared by a two-stage covalent PSM sequence (Scheme 1). First, activated NH2–UiO-66(Zr) (200 mg) was dispersed in 50 mM sodium phosphate buffer (pH 7.0, 30 mL) containing glutaraldehyde at a final concentration of 2.5% v/v, by probe sonication for 10 min. The suspension was then mechanically stirred at 25 °C for 4 h to allow imine condensation between the MOF-surface–NH2 groups and one aldehyde terminus of GA. The GA-activated intermediate (GA@UiO-66) was isolated by centrifugation, washed three times with phosphate buffer and twice with ultrapure water to remove unreacted GA and immediately suspended in fresh buffer to prevent hydrolysis of the surface-bound aldimine. In the second stage, SDX (150 mg, 0.432 mmol) was dissolved in the minimum volume of dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO, 5 mL) and was added to the suspension of GA@UiO-66. The mixture was stirred at 37 °C for 18 h, allowing condensation of the primary aromatic amine of SDX with the pendant aldehyde terminus of the tethered GA spacer to yield the final Schiff-base-bridged composite. The product, SDX–GA@UiO-66, was collected by centrifugation, washed thoroughly with EtOH (5 × 20 mL) and ultrapure water (3 × 20 mL) until the filtrate showed no UV absorption at 310 nm (absence of free SDX), and dried at 60 °C under vacuum for 24 h. The dry free flowing yellow-brown powder was kept in a desiccator at room temperature until use.

Computational studies

All quantum chemical calculations were performed using the Gaussian 09 program package.40 Geometry optimizations of the free ligand and its Cd2+ complexes were performed using density functional theory (DFT) with the B3LYP exchange–correlation functional and the 6-31G basis set.41 All calculations were performed using the Polarizable Continuum Model (PCM) to consider the solvent effect, with water as the solvent. Frequency calculations at the same level of theory confirmed the nature of the stationary points42 The optimized structures were confirmed as true minima (zero imaginary frequencies). Natural Bond Orbital (NBO) analyses were performed to study the electron density and atomic charges of the ligand. The HOMO–LUMO energy gaps were extracted from frontier molecular orbital (FMO) analysis, which gives insights into the electronic reorganization upon Cd2+ coordination. GaussView 6.0 was used to produce molecular visualizations such as orbital plots.

Optimization of sensing conditions

The sensing conditions were systematically optimized by varying one parameter at a time, keeping all the others at reference values. The following ranges and increments were studied: solution pH 2.0–10.0; sensor dosage 2–8 mg/10 mL, at 1 mg intervals; equilibration contact time 1–20 min; and temperature 20–40 °C, at 5 °C intervals; all optimizations were carried out at a fixed Cd2+ concentration of 0.50 ppm. The response of fluorescence signal (%) was the optimization criterion.

Storage stability and reusability

The long-term stability was tested by storing the SDX–GA@UiO-66 in a sealed vial at room temperature under ambient conditions and measuring the remaining signal response to a constant Cd2+ concentration (0.50 ppm) every week for 6 weeks. Reusability was tested over six consecutive adsorption–desorption cycles. After each sensing experiment, the used sensor was treated with 0.1 M EDTA solution (10 mL, 15 min stirring) to chelate and desorb the captured Cd2+, washed with deionized water (3 × 10 mL) and ethanol (2 × 5 mL), and dried at 60 °C for reuse.

General spectrophotometric and fluorometric detection procedure

For spectrophotometric sensing experiments, 5 mg SDX–GA@UiO-66 was dispersed in 10 mL aqueous Cd2+ standard solution at the appropriate pH by ultrasonication for 1 min and magnetic stirring for 5 min at 25 °C. The suspension was briefly centrifuged (8000 rpm, 2 min) to remove larger aggregates and settle coarse particulates; because the SDX–GA@UiO-66 composite remains finely dispersed under these conditions, the resulting supernatant is not particulate-free, and the measured absorbance reflects the optically active suspension of sub-micron composite particles rather than a solution-phase signal from freely dissolved chromophore. This suspension was measured against a reagent blank treated in the same way in a 1 cm quartz cuvette. Signal response was calculated as (A0 − A)/A0 × 100%, where A0 is the absorbance of the sensor suspension without analyte and A is the absorbance at each concentration of Cd2+. For fluorometric detection, the same dispersion protocol (5 mg sensor, 10 mL solution, pH 4, 5 min) was used and the fluorescence emission intensity at 440 nm was recorded upon excitation at 360 nm. The Stern–Volmer quenching ratio (F0/F) was calculated by F0 and F, the emission intensities without and with the presence of Cd2+, respectively. All the measurements were carried out in triplicate.

Analytical applications in real samples

Tap-water samples were collected directly from the laboratory water-supply lines, whereas industrial wastewater samples were obtained from a local metal-processing facility. The collected samples were filtered through 0.45 µm PVDF membrane filters prior to analysis. The initial pH, total dissolved solids (TDS), and complete dissolved-ion composition of the collected wastewater were not independently characterized in the present study; therefore, no specific concentrations of unidentified matrix constituents are assigned. For analytical measurements, the filtered samples were adjusted to the optimized pH of 4.0 and analyzed using the proposed standard-addition procedure. Known quantities of Cd2+ were spiked in triplicate on analyte free samples and the spiked samples were analyzed by standard addition method using the proposed sensor protocol. For the determination of cadmium concentrations, the proposed method was cross-validated by comparison with ICP-OES measurements of separate aliquots of the same spiked samples. The accuracy and precision were evaluated by calculating the recovery (%) and relative standard deviation (RSD%).

Results and discussion

Characterization of SDX–GA@UiO-66 sensor

XRD analysis

The XRD patterns of parent SDX–GA@UiO-66, Cd@SDX–GA@UiO-66 and the reported characteristic diffraction positions of UiO-66-type Zr-MOFs are shown in Fig. 1(a). The pristine NH2–UiO-66(Zr) shows the characteristic reflections at around 2θ ≈ 7.4°, 8.5°, 12.0°, 25.7° and 30.8°, which are consistent with the reported crystalline structure of NH2–UiO-66 and confirm the formation of the crystalline parent framework Fig. 1(a). The post-synthetic modification of UiO-66 with glutaraldehyde and sulfadoxine retains the main reflections of UiO-66, especially at 7.4°, 8.5°, 25.7° and 30.8° in SDX–GA@UiO-66, indicating that the basic crystalline framework is maintained during functionalization. However, we observe a decrease in the relative intensity of some reflections and minor shifts in the peak's positions, which can be related to the change in the local chemical environment of the amino-functionalized linkers, incorporation of the glutaraldehyde/SDX moieties and increased local structural disorder after the post-synthetic modification. Importantly, no additional strong diffraction peaks attributed to other crystalline phases are observed.43,44 The characteristic UiO-66 reflections are still visible in Cd@SDX–GA@UiO-66 after interaction with Cd2+, indicating that the Zr-MOF backbone is intact, whereas further attenuation and slight changes in peak positions are due to local structural perturbations caused by the interaction of Cd2+ with the N- and O-containing functional groups of the SDX–GA moieties. The lack of new distinct crystalline reflections with Cd suggests that Cd2+ does not form a separate detectable crystalline phase under the experimental conditions, but is associated with the functionalized MOF via surface and/or pore-confined interactions. The good agreement of the parent NH2–UiO-66(Zr), the characteristic reflections of the literature UiO-66 and the patterns of SDX–GA@UiO-66 and Cd@SDX–GA@UiO-66 confirms that the basic UiO-66 crystalline framework is retained after both post-synthetic functionalization and Cd2+ sensing.

N2 adsorption/desorption isotherm

The N2 adsorption–desorption isotherm of SDX–GA@UiO-66 Fig. 1(b) belongs to Type I with a slight Type IV contribution according to IUPAC classification, suggesting a microporous structure with minor mesoporous characteristics. The sharp increase at low relative pressure (P/P0 < 0.1) is characteristic of well-developed micropores, whereas the gradual increase at higher P/P0 indicates additional multilayer adsorption and limited mesoporosity.45,46 A narrow hysteresis loop resembling an H4-type loop is observed at intermediate to high relative pressure. Generally, such type of hysteresis is associated with slit-shaped pores and micropore–mesopore networks, which are characteristic of UiO-66-derived structures. Fig. 1(c) shows the pore size distribution with a dominant peak at ∼0.85 nm which is consistent with the reported average pore/particle radius (0.851 nm) confirming the microporous nature of the material. The cumulative pore volume profile Fig. 1(d) is close to 0.94 cm3 g−1, indicating a significant contribution of micropores, as shown by a rapid increase at small pore widths and then a plateau, indicating the predominance of microporosity. Similarly, the total surface area Fig. 1(e) also shows a very high value of 1601.98 m2 g−1, which indicative of the highly accessible internal surface of the MOF framework. The steep increase in surface area at smaller pore widths (<5 nm) indicates that the majority of the surface is generated from micropores and the weak tailing at larger pore sizes suggests a small mesoporous contribution. The combination of Type I/H4 isotherm behavior, narrow pore size distribution, high surface area and significant micropore volume imply a highly microporous structure with interconnected slit-like pores for SDX–GA@UiO-66. This feature is highly favorable for adsorption and sensing applications due to enhanced surface accessibility and strong adsorbate–adsorbent interactions.47,48

FT-IR

The FT-IR spectra of SDX–GA@UiO-66, and Cd@SDX–GA@UiO-66 shown in Fig. 1(f) clearly show the evidence of successful functionalization and interaction with phosphate and Cd2+ ions. The SDX–GA@UiO-66 spectrum displays a broad band at around ∼3400 cm−1, which is assigned to the overlapping of –OH and –NH stretching vibrations confirming the presence of amino-functionalized UiO-66 and sulfadoxine groups. Bands near ∼1400 cm−1 are assigned to symmetric stretching of –COO− groups coordinated to Zr nodes.49 In addition, the peaks in the region ∼1100–1000 cm−1 are assigned to C–N and SO vibrations, which further confirm the successful grafting of sulfadoxine on the MOF framework. The Cd@SDX–GA@UiO-66 spectrum after adsorption of Cd2+ shows a shift and decrease in intensity in the region of ∼1650–1500 cm−1, which is an indication of the coordination of CO, CN and –NH groups with Cd2+ ions. The slight decrease in the intensity of –OH/–NH bands also indicate their involvement in binding via hydrogen bonding or complexation. Importantly, the conservation of the main framework bands in all spectra confirms that the UiO-66 structure remains intact after functionalization and analyte interaction, whereas the observed spectral shifts provide strong evidence for multi-site binding mechanisms involving coordination, hydrogen bonding and electrostatic interactions.

The synchronous and asynchronous 2D correlation spectra presented in Fig. 1(g) and (h) provide detailed insight into the band intensity variations and sequential spectral shifts occurring during Cd2+ interaction with SDX–GA@UiO-66. In the synchronous map Fig. 1(g), prominent auto-peaks along the diagonal are observed at approximately ∼3400 cm−1 (–OH/–NH stretching), ∼1600–1500 cm−1 (CO/CN), and ∼1100–1000 cm−1 (C–N/SO vibrations), indicating that these functional groups undergo the most significant intensity changes upon Cd2+ binding. Strong positive cross-peaks (red regions) between these bands confirm that these groups vary simultaneously, highlighting their cooperative involvement in metal ion adsorption. Notably, the enhanced correlation between the CO/CN region and the –OH/–NH region suggests that both oxygen- and nitrogen-containing groups act as primary binding sites for Cd2+ through complexation and hydrogen bonding.

The order of the band shifts and interaction events is shown in the asynchronous map Fig. 1(h), with clear positive and negative cross-peaks. The spectral evolution suggests that changes in the OH/–NH region (∼3400 cm −1) happen first which represents the initial electrostatic attraction and hydrogen bonding between Cd2+ ions and surface functional groups as per Noda's rule. After that, significant changes are observed in the CO/CN region (∼1600–1500 cm−1), suggesting strong coordination (chelation) between the Cd2+ ions and donor atoms (O and N) in the sulfadoxine-modified framework. Later changes in the ∼1100–1000 cm−1 region indicate the involvement of SO and C–N groups, hinting at further stabilization via secondary interactions. The observed asynchronous cross peaks also show small band shifts to lower wavenumbers, consistent with electron density redistribution upon metal coordination. The combined synchronous and asynchronous spectroscopic analyses provide compelling spectroscopic evidence for the multi-step process of Cd2+ adsorption, such as initial interaction with surface sites and subsequent inner-sphere complexation, as well as the spectral shift of key functional groups, supporting the chelation-driven adsorption process by the synergistic effects of hydrogen bonding and electrostatic effects.

EDX analysis

Fig. 2(a) shows the EDX spectrum of SDX–GA@UiO-66 confirming the successful formation of the functionalized MOF structure and its chemical composition. The spectrum shows the presence of carbon (C), nitrogen (N), oxygen (O), sulfur (S) and zirconium (Zr) with weight percentages of about 22.6% C, 18.44% N, 43.22% O, 0.84% S and 14.87% Zr. The main oxygen signal is assigned to the carboxylate groups of the UiO-66 linker and oxygen containing functionalities of sulfadoxine.50–52 The high nitrogen content is in agreement with the presence of amine groups of both NH2–UiO-66 and sulfadoxine molecules. The detection of sulfur at a relatively low percentage, origin of which is the sulfonamide group (–SO2–) of the drug molecule, provides direct evidence for the successful incorporation of sulfadoxine onto the MOF framework. The strong zirconium peaks prove the intact Zr-based UiO-66 backbone, suggesting that the core MOF structure is maintained after modification. The relatively balanced distribution of C, N and O also corroborates the successful grafting of organic functionalities onto the inorganic framework. It should be pointed out that the EDX analysis is representative of the surface or near-surface composition and thus small deviations from the theoretical bulk composition are to be expected. Thus, the EDX results are indicative of the successful functionalization of UiO-66 with sulfadoxine through glutaraldehyde bridging and the formation of a chemically integrated hybrid material for sensing and adsorption purposes.

EDX analysis of (a) SDX–GA@UiO-66, and (b) Cd@SDX–GA@UiO-66.

The EDX spectrum of Cd@SDX–GA@UiO-66 shown in Fig. 2(b) confirms the successful adsorption and incorporation of Cd2+ ions into the functionalized MOF framework. It can be seen from the spectrum that the compound contains C, N, O, S, Zr and Cd. The weight percentages of C, N, O, S, Zr and Cd in the spectrum are about 20.1%, 17.5%, 47.42%, 0.46%, 12.72% and 1.8% respectively. The sharp peaks of Cd, not present in the pristine material, are direct evidence of the Cd2+ uptake by SDX–GA@UiO-66 composite. The relatively high contents of oxygen and nitrogen indicate that O- and N-containing functional groups (–COO−, CO, –NH2, CN) are actively involved in binding Cd2+ ions.50–52 The slight decrease in Zr proportion relative to the parent material suggests surface coverage and interaction of Cd2+ ions with active Zr–O sites, possibly via coordination or ion exchange mechanisms. The retention of sulfur suggests that the sulfadoxine group is chemically stable after adsorption of metal. The uniform distribution of Cd (as seen from the spectrum) and the absence of additional impurity peaks suggest that Cd2+ is adsorbed in the composite matrix, instead of forming separate crystalline phases. Since EDX is a surface-sensitive technique, the detected Cd content is representative of surface-bound and near-surface adsorption. The results show the effective capture of Cd2+ ions by SDX–GA@UiO-66 through coordination, electrostatic attraction and surface interaction mechanisms, while preserving the structural integrity of the MOF framework.

SEM analysis

The morphological and elemental characteristics of SDX-GA@UiO-66, as presented in Fig. 3(a)–(d), reveal a well-developed, rough, and highly porous microstructure that is favorable for adsorption and sensing applications. The SEM images Fig. 3(a) show that the material consists of densely packed, irregular nano-aggregates forming a granular and cauliflower-like morphology, with particle sizes ranging approximately from ∼30 to 50 nm, indicating successful functionalization without collapse of the UiO-66 framework. The presence of interconnected voids and interparticle spaces suggests a hierarchical porous network, which enhances mass transfer and accessibility of active sites. The corresponding 3D surface topography (Fig. 3(b)–(d)), including grayscale, thermal, and cool-tone representations, further confirms a highly rough surface with significant height variations, characterized by pronounced peaks and valleys that contribute to increased surface area and adsorption efficiency.53,54

(a) SEM of SDX–GA@UiO-66, (b) grayscale, (c) thermal color, (d) cool blue tones, (e) SEM of Cd@SDX–GA@UiO-66, (f) grayscale, (g) thermal color, and (h) cool blue tones.

The morphological and elemental characteristics of Cd@SDX–GA@UiO-66, as shown in Fig. 3(e)–(h), reveal significant structural evolution after Cd2+ adsorption while maintaining the integrity of the MOF framework. The SEM images Fig. 3(e) display a more compact, densely aggregated, and coalesced morphology compared to both the pristine and cadmium-loaded samples, with particle sizes increasing to approximately ∼45–65 nm, indicating substantial surface coverage and accumulation of Cd2+ ions. The nano-aggregates appear more fused, and the reduction in visible pore spaces suggests pore blocking and internal filling, consistent with strong adsorption interactions. The 3D surface topography Fig. 3(f)–(h) further confirms these changes, showing a smoother surface with reduced roughness and diminished height variation, which reflects the occupation of surface cavities and internal pores by Cd2+ ions and the formation of more uniform surface layers.

The SEM-EDX mapping Fig. 4(a) demonstrates a uniform distribution of carbon (C), nitrogen (N), oxygen (O), sulfur (S), and zirconium (Zr) across the surface, with approximate compositions of 21.3% C, 17.4% N, 45.4% O, 15.3% Zr, and 0.6% S. The homogeneous dispersion of Zr confirms the integrity of the UiO-66 framework, while the presence and uniform distribution of nitrogen and sulfur validate the successful grafting of sulfadoxine via glutaraldehyde linkage. The high oxygen content is attributed to both the MOF linker and oxygen-containing functional groups of sulfadoxine. Importantly, the absence of elemental clustering indicates that the functional groups are evenly distributed rather than localized, ensuring consistent availability of active adsorption sites. Overall, the combined SEM and mapping results confirm that SDX–GA@UiO-66 possesses a highly porous, rough, and chemically homogeneous structure, which plays a critical role in enhancing adsorption performance through improved surface interaction and diffusion pathways.

The SEM-EDX mapping Fig. 4(b) demonstrates a homogeneous distribution of C, N, O, S, Zr, and Cd elements, with approximate compositions of 19.64% C, 16.42% N, 48.6% O, 12.8% Zr, 0.36% S, and 2.2% Cd. The clear appearance and uniform dispersion of cadmium (Cd) confirm successful adsorption and strong interaction with the composite. The slight decrease in Zr intensity compared to the pristine material suggests surface coverage and possible coordination of Cd2+ ions with Zr–O clusters, while the relatively high oxygen and nitrogen contents indicate the involvement of –COO−, CO, and –NH2 groups in binding Cd2+ ions through coordination and electrostatic interactions. The absence of localized Cd clustering indicates that adsorption occurs uniformly across the surface and within the porous network, rather than forming isolated precipitates. Overall, the combined SEM and mapping analyses confirm that Cd2+ adsorption leads to surface densification, pore filling, and strong metal–ligand interaction, providing clear morphological and compositional evidence for efficient and homogeneous metal ion uptake by the SDX–GA@UiO-66 composite.

Computational studies

To clarify the electronic properties and coordination mode of the SDX–GA ligand with Cd2+ ions, density functional theory (DFT) calculations were performed at the B3LYP/6-31G(d,p) level with natural bond orbital (NBO) analysis to determine the preferred donor sites. The NBO charge distribution of the uncoordinated ligand (Fig. 5(a)) shows that the sulfonyl oxygen atoms O1 (−0.813) and O2 (−0.778) are the most nucleophilic sites, i.e., the most electron-rich donor sites, followed by the sulfonamide nitrogen N1 (−0.789) and the heterocyclic pyrimidine nitrogens N2 (−0.539) and N3 (−0.520). The imine conjugated nitrogen N4 (−0.427) has a smaller but still significant negative charge. These results suggest that the soft Lewis acid Cd2+ ions might prefer to coordinate through the sulfonamide N and sulfonyl O atoms because of their increased electron density and accessibility. Two possible coordination geometries are suggested for the SDX–GA@Cd2+ complex: complex A, where the Cd2+ ions are chelated by the sulfonamide N and sulfonyl O atoms, and complex B, where the coordination occurs through the two sulfonyl O atoms. Fig. 5(b) shows the optimized geometries of both the complexes. The total electronic energy calculated for complex A (E = −1271.02 hartree) is found to be lower than that for complex B (E = −1231.70 hartree), indicating that complex A is thermodynamically more favorable.55 This is further supported by molecular orbital analysis. As shown in (Fig. 5(c)), the energy gap (ΔE) between the HOMO and LUMO of the free ligand was calculated to be 3.82 eV (0.1403 hartree). The energy gap was decreased to 3.80 eV (0.1395 hartree) after complexation with Cd2+ ions, which indicated that the electronic delocalization and stabilization.56 The electrostatic potential (ESP) maps (Fig. 5(d)) further show that there is a redistribution of electron density upon the metal coordination with an increase of electron localization around the Cd-binding core. These results confirm the sensing mechanism proposed experimentally and demonstrate that the most favored complexation process is through the sulfonamide N and sulfonyl O groups. The combination of NBO, orbital and thermodynamic data gives a complete electronic explanation for the selectivity of the sensor towards the Cd2+ ions.

Spectrophotometric and fluorometric detection of Cd2+

The analytical signal reported throughout this section originates from Cd2+-induced quenching of both the ground-state absorbance and the excited-state fluorescence emission of the immobilized SDX–GA chromophore, presented below as the ground-state (UV-vis) and excited-state (fluorescence) responses in turn.

UV-vis spectrophotometric response

The UV-vis spectroscopic response of SDX–GA@UiO-66 upon increasing the concentration of Cd2+ is presented in (Fig. 6(a)). The free sensor shows characteristic absorption bands at around 280 nm (assigned to the π–π* transition of the SDX benzene sulfonamide aromatic system) and at ∼400 nm (assigned to the n–π* transition involving the imine chromophore and the pyrimidine ring of SDX). The progressive addition of Cd2+ (0–1.0 ppm) results in a monotonic decrease of the absorbance at both bands as expected for the formation of a Cd2+–SDX–GA chelate that prevents the photoinduced electron redistribution responsible for the chromophoric absorption. The signal difference (A0 − A) vs. [Cd2+] (Fig. 6(b)) shows a non-linear dependence over the full concentration range, which saturates above ∼0.3 ppm due to binding site occupancy. There is a linear sub-range from 0.0 to 0.12 ppm (Fig. 6(c)). The Cd2+-induced decrease at both bands, without the emergence of new absorption features, indicates that quenching arises from perturbation of the existing SDX–GA chromophore's ground-state electronic structure upon chelation, rather than from formation of a spectroscopically distinct new species.

Fluorescence quenching response

The fluorescence emission spectra of the sensor suspension upon excitation at 360 nm (Fig. 5(d)) show a single broad emission band centered at about 440 nm, which is attributed to the conjugated π-electron system of the SDX–GA chromophore incorporating the imine linkage and SDX pyrimidine ring. Cd2+ (0 to 2.20 ppm) was added, and the emission intensity was systematically quenched with F0/F increasing from 1.0 to about 4.5 at saturation (Fig. 6(e)). The Stern–Volmer plot for the whole concentration range shows upward curvature indicating contributions of dynamic and static quenching but the low concentration sub-range (0.0–0.25 ppm) obeys the linear Stern–Volmer relationship (Fig. 6(f)). The quenching mechanism is ascribed to the chelation enhanced quenching (CHEQ): the binding of Cd2+ to the nitrogen and oxygen donor atoms of SDX–GA promotes the intersystem crossing from singlet excited state to the triplet manifold through the heavy-atom spin–orbit coupling enhancement, which reduces the radiative emission rate and quenches the fluorescence effectively. This interpretation is consistent with the DFT analysis which shows a reduction of the HOMO–LUMO gap upon Cd2+ coordination which opens non-radiative decay pathways. The upward curvature of the Stern–Volmer plot at higher Cd2+ concentrations are consistent with this static quenching pathway operating alongside a minor diffusional (dynamic) collisional contribution, whereas the linear low-concentration sub-range reflects conditions under which static, chelation-driven quenching dominates the observed response.

Optimization of sensing conditions

Optimization was carried out using the fluorescence channel, with the sensing response expressed as the percentage signal response, % signal response = (F0 − F)/F0 × 100, where F0 and F are the fluorescence emission intensities at 440 nm in the absence and presence of Cd2+, respectively.

Four experimental parameters solution pH, sensor dose, contact time, and temperature were systematically varied, one at a time, to identify the conditions that maximize the fluorescence signal response and to establish the standard operating conditions used throughout the remainder of this work. Fig. 7(a) shows the effect of solution pH on the sensing signal response. The maximum response (∼90%) is obtained at pH 4 and the signal decreases sharply at both lower pH (<3) and higher pH (>5). At pH values < 3, the electron-donating ability of the imine nitrogen (pKa of aliphatic imines ≈ 5–7) and the SDX amine group is significantly reduced due to extensive protonation, and thus the binding affinity of the sensor toward Cd2+ is reduced. Furthermore, at strongly acidic pH values, the competitive protonation of the MOF framework carboxylate groups can induce a structural destabilization of the composite. For pH > 5, Cd2+ starts to hydrolyze gradually to Cd(OH)+ and Cd(OH)2 species (Cd2+ hydrolysis begins at pH 7–8 approximately, but the speciation of the preparatory aqua complexes changes at pH > 5), which changes the effective form of the analyte and decreases the affinity for coordination. The optimal pH of 4 corresponds to the weakly acidic regime where Cd2+ exists predominantly as the free aquo dication [Cd(H2O)6]2+, the SDX donor atoms are partially deprotonated and available for coordination, and the MOF framework retains full structural integrity. All further experiments were carried out at pH 4.

Optimization of SDX–GA@UiO-66 fluorescence-based % signal response to Cd2+, where % signal response = (F0 − F)/F0 × 100: (a) effect of solution pH; (b) sensor dosage effect; (c) contact time effect; (d) temperature effect; (e) long-term storage stability over six weeks; (f) signal retention over six detection–regeneration cycles.

The effect of sensor dose was tested in the range 2–8 mg (Fig. 7(b)). The signal response sharply increases from ∼85% at 2 mg to ∼92% at 4 mg and plateaus between 4 and 8 mg (∼92–93%). This behaviour is in agreement with the progressive availability of more binding sites as the mass of the sensor increases, until the concentration of the analyte becomes limiting. A working dose of 5 mg of sensor was chosen to provide an almost maximal response with minimal excess material.

The results of the contact time experiments (Fig. 7(c)) reveal that the sensing response increases rapidly in the first 5 min of interaction and approaches ∼95–97% signal saturation, and then there is no statistically significant increase up to 20 min. The rapid equilibration is attributed to the open pore structure of the MOF enabling free diffusion of Cd2+ to the tethered SDX–GA binding sites in combination with the enthalpic favourable chelation thermodynamics. For all the calibration and selectivity studies an equilibration time of 5 min was used.

Temperature variation between 20 and 40 °C (Fig. 7(d)) revealed a maximum response at 25 °C (∼90–91%), with the response remaining essentially constant across the full 20–40 °C range tested, indicating that the coordinated complexes are thermally robust across the ambient temperature range relevant to environmental monitoring deployments.

The long-term storage stability of SDX–GA@UiO-66 are presented in (Fig. 7(e)). Over six weeks of ambient storage, the signal response for Cd2+ declines from 96% (week 1) to approximately 87% (week 6), representing a cumulative loss of approximately 9–10% over the storage period. This performance profile surpasses that of many reported MOF-based sensors, where covalently impregnated chromophores are lost through leaching within days, and reflects the durability of the covalent imine linkages established by the GA-mediated PSM strategy.61

The reusability data (Fig. 7(f)) demonstrate consistent analytical performance over six consecutive detection–regeneration cycles: initial response was set to 97% (Cd2+); after the sixth cycle, values of 86% are retained, corresponding to an approximately 11% cumulative signal loss over the six-cycle interval, consistent with the broader 9–14% decay range reported across the combined reusability and storage-stability studies. Regeneration between cycles relies on EDTA-mediated competitive displacement: the strong Cd2+–EDTA complex (log Kf ≈ 16.5) outcompetes the SDX–GA binding sites for the metal ion, stripping bound Cd2+ from the sensor during the 0.1 M EDTA treatment and restoring available coordination sites for the subsequent cycle. The residual signal loss over six cycles likely reflects a combination of two mechanisms: (i) mechanical attrition of the nanoparticulate material during the repeated centrifugation and washing steps, resulting in cumulative loss of active sensor mass, and (ii) incomplete EDTA-mediated desorption, leaving a small residual population of non-desorbed Cd2+ that permanently occupies a fraction of the SDX–GA binding sites and is unavailable for subsequent cycles. The relative contribution of these two mechanisms was not independently quantified in this study, and this is noted as a limitation.

Selectivity and interference studies

The selectivity of SDX–GA@UiO-66 for Cd2+ over fifteen co-present cations (Na+, K+, Mg2+, Ca2+, Ni2+, Cu2+, Zn2+, Cr3+, Mn2+, Co2+, Pb2+, Hg2+, Fe2+, Fe3+, Al3+) is shown in Fig. 8 at the same molar concentration (0.50 ppm). Selectivity was assessed via the fluorescence channel only, as the more sensitive of the two transduction modes; corresponding UV-vis selectivity data were not collected, and the selectivity trends reported below should not be extrapolated to the UV-vis channel without independent verification. The F0/F values of the sensor alone with each competing cation ranged from ∼0.4 to 0.5, indicating minimal quenching by any of the interferents. Whereas, Cd2+ alone gives F0/F ≈ 3.6, which is about eight-fold higher than the background interference level. In the presence of competing cations in addition to Cd2+, the F0/F values range from 2.9 to 3.3, suggesting that the Cd2+ signal is preserved to within approximately 80–93% in the presence of all tested interferents. The basis for this selectivity can be rationalized on several grounds. Electronic features of Cd2+ as a soft Lewis acid (d10 configuration) with a high affinity for borderline-to-soft donors (imino nitrogen principle.62 Harder and sulfonamide nitrogen of SDX–GA) as predicted by the Hard–Soft Acid–Base (HSAB) Na+, K+, Mg2+, Ca2+, Fe2+, Fe3+, and Al3+ cations preferentially coordinate oxygen donors in an outer-sphere mode without displacing Cd2+ from the pre-organized chelating cavity. Zn2+ is the most similar transition metal cation to Cd2+ in its geometry and electronic structure, but it exhibits a significantly lower F0/F (∼0.43), consistent with the larger cavity size of the SDX–GA chelate ring being more suitable for the larger Cd2+ ion, thus providing a dimension of geometric selectivity. Cu2+ and Ni2+ are intermediate Lewis acids with affinity for nitrogen donors but generate negligible quenching responses (F0/F ∼ 0.4–0.45) that are attributed to the kinetic inertness of their substitution reactions at pH 4 relative to the labile, fast exchanging Cd2+ aquo complex under the 5 min equilibration window. Hg2+ (F0/F ∼ 0.45 alone) does not lead to significant quenching at equimolar concentration, which is tentatively attributed to the steric demands of the larger Hg2+ cation exceeding the optimal cavity dimensions and its greater preference for thiolate donors absent in the present system. The presence of Hg2+, Fe2+/3+, or Al3+ together with Cd2+ decreases the Cd2+ signal by about 12–19%, which is the largest interference effect among all tested cations, and should be taken into account in practical applications involving mercury-co-contaminated matrices.63

Sensing mechanism

The combined evidence from PXRD, FT-IR, EDX, 2D-COS and DFT allowed us to build a consistent mechanistic picture of the Cd2+ sensing process. The MOF scaffold plays three roles: (i) as a high-surface area platform to pre-concentrate Cd2+ from dilute solution by electrostatic attraction to the carboxylate-rich pore walls; (ii) the micropore confinement effect brings the analyte into close proximity with the SDX–GA chelating units immobilized on the linker amines; and (iii) the geometric rigidity of the MOF crystal lattice constrains the SDX–GA ligand chain into a conformation that presents the binding cavity in an oriented, pre-organized geometry that is conducive to the formation of Cd2+ chelate rings.64 This is where the glutaraldehyde spacer comes into play: The five-carbon methylene chain offers conformational flexibility between the MOF surface and the SDX unit, enabling the ligand arm to satisfy the geometric requirements of the Cd2+ coordination sphere without steric constraints imposed by direct attachment to the MOF surface.

The binding sequence deduced by 2D-COS involves the initial electrostatic interaction of the sulfonyl oxygen atoms (O1, O2; NBO charges −0.813, −0.778) with the Lewis acid Cd2+ center, followed by the tightening of the chelate ring through the chelation of the imine nitrogen (N4, NBO charge −0.427) and sulfonamide nitrogen (N1,−0.789). The multidentate coordination geometry, which probably involves a five-membered chelate ring with the sulfonamide N and sulfonyl O linkages, ensures a high thermodynamic stability of the SDX–GA@Cd2+ complex, consistent with the efficient signal quenching and the retention of response over six regeneration cycles. The pH 4 optimum corresponds to the speciation window where: Cd2+ exists as the free aquo dication (no hydrolytic precipitation); the imine nitrogen is partially deprotonated and available for coordination; and the MOF carboxylate nodes maintain their connectivity without acid dissolution.65 The HOMO–LUMO gap contraction upon Cd2+ chelation disrupts the conjugated chromophore fluorescence by facilitating intersystem crossing, producing the observed fluorescence quenching, while the simultaneous redistribution of electron density away from the SDX aromatic system reduces the molar absorptivity at the 280 nm and 400 nm UV-vis bands, generating the absorbance quenching signal transduction. Consistent with the finely dispersed nature of the composite under the sensing protocol (Section 2.6), this absorbance change is a solid-phase optical effect intrinsic to the SDX–GA chromophore while it remains covalently bound to the suspended MOF particles, rather than a solution-phase signal arising from chromophore leaching into the aqueous phase. It should be noted that, because both the UV-vis and fluorescence responses originate from the same chelation-enhanced quenching (CHEQ) event, the two channels are correlated outputs of a single Cd2+-binding process rather than mechanistically orthogonal verification of that binding. They nonetheless interrogate distinct photophysical processes, namely the ground-state absorptivity change and the excited-state non-radiative decay pathway, respectively, and dual-mode acquisition is accordingly presented here as providing operational redundancy and complementary sensitivity and dynamic range for practical deployment, rather than independent confirmation of the Cd2+-binding event. It should also be acknowledged as a limitation of the present study that the relative contributions of MOF-intrinsic luminescence and SDX–GA chromophore-based luminescence to the observed 440 nm emission band were not independently deconvoluted through control measurements on the unfunctionalized NH2–UiO-66(Zr) framework and free SDX in isolation. NH2–UiO-66(Zr) is itself known to exhibit intrinsic blue emission upon UV excitation, arising from ligand-centered and linker-to-cluster charge-transfer transitions of the 2-aminoterephthalate linker.66 Consequently, the emission response reported here for SDX–GA@UiO-66 should be interpreted as the composite optical signature of the functionalized material as a whole, rather than as arising exclusively from the SDX–GA chromophore. Future work will incorporate matched-condition emission and absorption controls of the unfunctionalized framework and free ligand to quantitatively deconvolute their respective contributions to the composite's dual-mode optical response.

Conclusions

Here, we rationally designed a post-synthetically modified metal–organic framework composite, SDX–GA@UiO-66, by covalently immobilizing the sulfonamide chromophore sulfadoxine onto an amino-functionalized NH2–UiO-66(Zr) scaffold via glutaraldehyde. This two-step Schiff base condensation sequence places a conformationally flexible, multi-donor SDX–GA arm into the MOF pore environment, while maintaining the crystallographic integrity and BET surface area of the parent framework. FE-SEM/EDX characterization confirmed the nanoparticle morphology (30–50 nm), homogeneous sulfur distribution (0.84 wt%), and Cd2+ uptake (1.8–2.2 wt%) quantified by elemental analysis. 2D-COS FT-IR revealed the sequential binding pathway, with sulfonyl oxygen binding first and imine nitrogen coordination second. The contraction of the HOMO–LUMO gap upon Cd2+ coordination was confirmed via DFT calculations at B3LYP/LANL2DZ, accounting for the fluorescence quenching and change in the UV-vis absorption through frontier molecular orbital arguments. The optimized sensor operates at pH 4 with 5 min response time and exhibits dual-mode detection of Cd2+ by UV-vis spectrophotometry (LOD = 0.0181 ppm; linear range 0.0–0.12 ppm) and fluorescence quenching (LOD = 0.0087 ppm; linear range 0.0–0.25 ppm) with excellent selectivity against fifteen competing cations, >85% signal retention over six regeneration cycles and >85% stability over six weeks of ambient storage. This corresponds to a cumulative signal decay of approximately 9–14% over the six-cycle/six-week interval, a practical limitation for extended continuous field deployment; periodic recalibration or sensor replacement at this interval is recommended for regulatory-grade monitoring applications. 2D-COS FT-IR revealed the sequential binding pathway with sulfonyl oxygen binding first and then imine nitrogen coordination. The contraction of the HOMO–LUMO gap upon Cd2+ coordination was confirmed via DFT calculations at B3LYP/LANL2DZ, accounting for the fluorescence quenching and change in the UV-vis absorption with frontier molecular orbital arguments. The accuracy of analysis was validated by the recovery of real samples in tap water and industrial wastewater cross-validated with ICP-OES, which showed no matrix-dependent bias. Relative to quantum-dot-based platforms, SDX–GA@UiO-66 offers dual-mode readout, six-cycle reusability, and a comparatively simple, low-cost post-synthetic-modification route, at the cost of an approximately 15–30-fold higher limit of detection than the best-in-class AgInZnS quantum dot sensor,44 positioning the present sensor for field-screening and regulatory-tier monitoring applications rather than ultratrace, research-grade quantification. Literature-reported Cd2+ concentrations in industrial effluents commonly reach several hundred ppb or more,5 substantially exceeding the LOD achieved here (18.1 ppb UV-vis; 8.7 ppb fluorescence), which positions SDX–GA@UiO-66 as well suited to screening moderately-to-heavily contaminated effluents and source-water monitoring. It should be acknowledged, however, that this LOD remains above the WHO drinking-water guideline of 3 ppb, and further sensitivity enhancement would be required before the sensor could be applied to direct ultratrace drinking-water compliance testing. Furthermore, the PSM architecture can be extended to other sulfonamide chromophores with tuned donor atom sets for multi-analyte environmental screening, thus extending the detection capability of the sensor.
