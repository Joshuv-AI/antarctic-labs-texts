Time-series analysis of vitiligo-related online search behavior in response to ambient air pollutants
Authors: Shijie Zhu, Xixia Dai, Ling Jiang, Chuhan Fu, Jing Chen, Yuting Hu, Yidan Li, Keyi Zhang, Qinghai Zeng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAmbient air pollutants are hazardous materials posing significant risks to global public health. While their impacts on respiratory and cardiovascular systems are well-established, their role in triggering specific autoimmune skin diseases remains under-explored. This study investigates the temporally resolved public health response to air pollutant exposure, using the autoimmune condition vitiligo as a sensitive health endpoint. We aimed to evaluate the differential public health responses to six major air pollutants by analyzing their lagged effects on vitiligo-related online search behavior, using the Baidu Search Index (BSI) as a novel proxy for public concern and health-seeking activity. In this ecological time-series study, we applied a Distributed Lag Non-linear Model (DLNM) to daily data on BSI, air pollutants, and meteorological factors in Changsha, China (2019–2021). The findings reveal distinct, pollutant-specific temporal patterns of public response, suggesting different toxicological pathways. Exposure to industrial pollutants like sulfur dioxide (SO2) prompted an immediate, acute increase in search behavior. In contrast, traffic-related pollutants such as carbon monoxide (CO) were associated with a more persistent, long-term response. These associations remained robust in two-pollutant models. Short-term exposure to hazardous air pollutants is associated with significant changes in public health-seeking behavior. The study highlights the value of infodemiology as a near real-time surveillance tool for monitoring population-level responses to environmental hazards and provides new evidence on the differential health impacts of specific pollutants on autoimmune skin conditions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-31422-2.Subject terms: Diseases, Environmental sciences, Health care, Risk factors

Introduction

Air pollution is a critical environmental health risk linked to numerous diseases1,2. Despite control efforts, exposure to pollutants like PM2.5 remains prevalent3. Globally, studies have confirmed associations between pollutants and systemic conditions such as diabetes, dyslipidemia, hypertension, Parkinson’s disease and adverse outcomes in interstitial pneumonia4–7. On the mechanistic front, research has found that air pollutants can infiltrate the bloodstream via the respiratory system, inducing systemic inflammation, oxidative stress, and endothelial dysfunction, thereby promoting the development of cardiovascular diseases8. As the body’s primary barrier, the skin is highly susceptible to air pollutants. Research has strongly associated pollutant exposure with skin aging, inflammation, and diseases like atopic dermatitis, eczema, and psoriasis9–12. For example, studies suggest air pollution can be a significant environmental trigger for atopic dermatitis and eczema13,14.Vitiligo is a chronic autoimmune skin disease, characterized by depigmentation, that affects 0.5–2% of the global population15,16. Its pathogenesis is known to involve genetic, environmental, and autoimmune factors17,18. However, the potential impact of air pollution on vitiligo remains largely unexplored in epidemiological studies.

The public increasingly uses the internet to seek health information about disease prevention, diagnosis, and treatment19, making online search data a valuable resource for public health surveillance. The Baidu Search Index (BSI), from the Chinese search engine Baidu, quantifies user search trends and has been widely used in environmental health research20. The volume of searches for a specific disease may indirectly reflect public concern and, potentially, its incidence rate.Recent methodological advances in spatiotemporal modeling and exposure assessment have improved our capacity to link environmental data with health outcomes21–23.This study employs a Distributed Lag Non-linear Model (DLNM), a statistical method designed to assess complex and delayed exposure-response relationships, it’s particularly suited for analyzing the impact of environmental factors on health outcomes24–26.We used DLNMs to analyze BSI, air pollution, and meteorological data from Changsha city. Our objective is to investigate the lagged association between ambient air pollutants and public interest in vitiligo, aiming to identify potential environmental triggers and provide a data-driven basis for public health strategies.

Materials and methods

Study location

The study was set in Changsha (27°51′–28°40′N; 111°53′–114°15′E), the capital of Hunan Province, China (Fig. 1a, b) ,with a population of approximately 10.62 million as of 2024. The city has a subtropical monsoon climate27.

Study locations. (a) Location of Hunan Province in China. (b) Location of Changsha in Hunan Province. (c) Locations of Changsha’s 10 ambient air monitoring stations.

Air pollution and meteorological data

Data Collection Daily data from January 1, 2019, to December 31, 2021, were obtained for ambient air pollutants (PM2.5, PM10, NO2, SO2, CO, and O3) from 10 monitoring stations in Changsha(Fig. 1c), sourced from the China National Environmental Monitoring Centre (CNEMC). We calculated the 24-hour average for PM2.5, PM10, NO2, SO2, and CO, and the maximum 8-hour average for O3 (O3_8h) to represent daily exposure levels. Daily meteorological data (air temperature, atmospheric pressure, relative humidity) were sourced from the National Meteorological Information Center. Missing data were handled using multiple imputation.

Baidu search index

We used the mobile BSI to quantify daily online information-seeking behavior for “vitiligo”. The BSI is a weighted index of search frequencies on Baidu, China’s largest search engine20. The mobile index was chosen as it better reflects the public’s immediate and spontaneous search behavior, which is suitable for exploring short-term environmental health impacts.All BSI data were extracted specifically for Changsha city using the Baidu Index platform’s geographic filtering function (https://index.baidu.com). We selected “Changsha city” as the geographic region when retrieving the data to ensure spatial correspondence between the air pollution measurements (from 10 monitoring stations distributed across the city) and the health information-seeking behavior data. This geographic matching at the city level is methodologically appropriate for ecological time-series analysis, as it ensures that both exposure and outcome data are collected at the same spatiotemporal scale.

Statistical analysis

A Distributed Lag Non-linear Model (DLNM) with a quasi-Poisson regression was used to analyze the lagged effects of each air pollutant on the vitiligo BSI. The model was specified as: log[E(Yt)]=βXt + ns(time, df = 7/year) + DOWt + ns(RH, df = 3) + ns(Temp, df = 3) + BP + α,Here, E(Yt) is the BSI on day t; Xt is the cross-basis matrix for the pollutant; β is the log-relative risk; and the model controlled for long-term trends, seasonality, day of the week (DOW), relative humidity (RH), temperature (Temp), and atmospheric pressure (BP). A cross-basis function with a fourth-degree polynomial was applied to a lag period of 0–60 days. Single-pollutant models were constructed, and cumulative effects for various lag periods were calculated.All analyses were performed using R (version 4.4.2). Statistical modeling used the “dlnm” and “splines” packages, with missing data imputed via the “mice” package. Data processing and visualization were performed using the “dplyr,” “tidyr,” and “ggplot2” packages. Maps (Figure 1) were created using the “sf” and “ggplot2” packages based on standard maps from Tianditu (www.tianditu.gov.cn, review No. GS (2024) 0650), without modifying administrative boundaries. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Sensitivity analysis

To ensure robustness, we developed two-pollutant models to assess confounding effects. Multicollinearity was evaluated using Spearman correlation (threshold:∣r∣>0.8) and Variance Inflation Factor (VIF, threshold: > 10).

Results

Data description

From 2019 to 2021 in Changsha, the mean daily concentrations were: PM2.5 (43.8 µg/m3), PM10 (53.3 µg/m3), NO2 (29.6 µg/m3), SO2 (6.9 µg/m3), CO (0.8 mg/m3), and O3 (90.1 µg/m3). The mean temperature was 17.8 °C, relative humidity 78%, and atmospheric pressure 100.1 kPa, with an average daily BSI of 172.3 (Table 1).

Correlation analysis (Supplementary Fig. 1) showed positive associations among most pollutants, with the exception of O3. The strongest inter-pollutant correlations were observed for PM2.5-PM10 (r = 0.79), PM2.5-CO (r = 0.71), and PM10-NO2(r = 0.69), all remaining below 0.8. Association between air pollutants and vitiligo BSI

Single-Pollutant models

After adjusting for confounders, all pollutants except PM10 showed significant lagged effects on the vitiligo BSI. The duration of these effects varied: CO was the most persistent (lag 5–60), while SO2 was the most acute and shortest (lag 0–5). Lag effects for other pollutants were observed for PM2.5 (lag 6–58), NO2 (lag 14–57), and O3 (lag 13–27) (Fig. 2, Supplementary Fig. 3).

Percentage changes in daily mobile search volume from Baidu for vitiligo per 10-µg/m3 increase (CO: per 0.1-mg/m3 increase) in air pollutant concentrations from lag 0 (current day) to lag 60 (previous 60 days) in Changsha, China, from 1 January 2019 to 31 December 2021.

Regarding effect magnitude, SO2 had the strongest immediate impact (lag 0), with a 10 µg/m3 increase associated with a 2.03% (95% CI: 0.54–3.54) rise in BSI. For CO, effects are reported per 0.1 mg/m3 increment, reflecting the actual exposure variability observed in Changsha (mean: 0.8 mg/m3, IQR: 0.7–0.9 mg/m3).CO’s peak was at lag 31–37, where a 0.1 mg/m3increase led to a 0.74% (95% CI: 0.39–1.09) rise. Following this, the peaks for PM2.5 (lag 28–33), NO2 (lag 25–31), and O3 (lag 21–27) corresponded to BSI increases of 0.39% (95% CI: 0.16–0.62), 0.37% (95% CI: 0.06–0.67), and 0.09% (95% CI: 0.01–0.17) respectively, for every 10 µg/m3 increase. (Fig. 2, Supplementary Table 1).

Analysis of cumulative effects over 60 days showed that PM2.5, CO, and NO2 had significant and progressively increasing impacts, while PM10 and O3 did not. The effect of SO2 was significant only in the short term (peaking at lag 0–11). At lag 0–60, the cumulative effects for PM2.5 and CO were substantial and significant, with CO showing the largest overall impact (Fig. 3, Supplementary Table 2).

Two-pollutant models

Building upon the single-pollutant models, we developed two-pollutant models to evaluate the independent effects of additional pollutants on the vitiligo BSI while controlling for one pollutant. To illustrate their lagged effects in these models (Table 2), we selected the lag day with the maximum effect for SO2 (lag 0) and a common lag day where effects were generally observed for all other pollutants (lag 14). The strengths and significance patterns of individual pollutants varied when another pollutant was included in the model. Overall, CO and SO2 consistently exhibited significant effects after adjusting for other pollutants, indicating a relatively robust association with vitiligo-related search behavior.

Discussion

This study uses single and two-pollutant models, analyzed the temporal association between various ambient air pollutants and the vitiligo BSI in Changsha from 2019 to 2021, offering a novel perspective on the potential role of environmental factors in this condition.

Numerous studies indicate that atmospheric pollutants like particulate matter (PM), polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs), and volatile organic compounds (VOCs) can impair skin structure and function, leading to various skin diseases. Primary proposed pathogenic mechanisms include oxidative stress, skin barrier damage, skin microbiome dysbiosis, and cutaneous inflammation28–30. Furthermore, research demonstrates that natural antioxidants can protect skin from airborne particulate matter by mitigating oxidative stress31. Although the precise mechanisms linking air pollutants to vitiligo are unclear, several plausible pathways have been proposed. For instance, pollutant exposure can induce the production of Reactive Oxygen Species (ROS), which in turn activates the inflammasome; notably, mutations in the NLRP1 inflammasome are linked to the pathogenesis of vitiligo32. Consistent with this, a study in the highly industrialized city of Baroda found a higher local incidence of vitiligo, and that patients with the condition exhibited significantly lower levels of antioxidant enzymes33. We therefore speculate that air pollutants may elevate the risk of vitiligo through oxidative stress-mediated pathways.

Our single-pollutant models revealed that various pollutants had significant, yet distinct, effects on the vitiligo BSI. Notably, SO2, a primary indicator of industrial emissions34, demonstrated a strong and immediate effect, suggesting an underlying mechanism of acute biological injury. We postulate this mechanism is mediated by oxidative stress, as SO2 is an established oxidant capable of elevating ROS generation in dermal and respiratory cells, thereby inducing a significant oxidative stress response35. Moreover, oxidative damage is a recognized pathological mechanism driving melanocyte dysfunction and apoptosis in vitiligo36. Therefore, we propose that acute exposure to high concentrations of SO2 could rapidly escalate cutaneous oxidative stress and intracellular ROS, in turn damaging melanocytes or activating inflammatory pathways to initiate or exacerbate vitiligo. Such a rapid mechanism aligns well with the immediate effects observed in our data.

In contrast, pollutants characteristic of vehicle exhaust such as CO and NO237,38, which tend to accumulate gradually, were primarily associated with lagged and cumulative effects. While the precise immunomodulatory mechanisms warrant further investigation, the sustained, long-term impact of CO suggests a slower, more insidious pathogenic process. We propose chronic hypoxia as a plausible pathway. It has been demonstrated that long-term, low-dose exposure to CO can mimic the effects of a high-altitude environment by blocking oxygen-binding sites on hemoglobin molecules39. This is particularly relevant for melanocytes, as these highly metabolically active cells rely critically on mitochondrial homeostasis and energy metabolism for their survival and function. Indeed, a review by Xuan et al.40highlights mitochondrial dysfunction as a key event that drives melanocyte apoptosis under oxidative stress, creating a destructive “vicious cycle”. We therefore postulate that the persistent environmental pressure from pollutants like CO, acting via this chronic, low-level hypoxic state, disrupts essential mitochondrial functions and energy metabolism in melanocytes, resulting in their gradual impairment. This proposed mechanism aligns well with the sustained lagged effects observed in our data.

Notably, PM10 did not show a significant effect, which is consistent with the findings of some studies. For example, research by Wang et al. in Shanghai also found that PM2.5, rather than PM10, was associated with outpatient visits for pediatric respiratory diseases41. This is likely because PM2.5 has a smaller particle size and a larger specific surface area42, allowing it to penetrate deeper into the pulmonary alveoli and blood circulation, and it possesses a stronger capacity than PM10 to adsorb toxic substances such as heavy metals and PAHs43–45, making it more likely to induce systemic or deep-seated biological effects.

Concurrently, O3 did not exhibit a significant cumulative effect. This is probably because ozone is a secondary pollutant, formed via photochemical reactions from precursors like NOx and VOCs emitted from sources such as energy production and vehicle exhaust46. Its formation is highly susceptible to sunlight and temperature47. Temperature can modulate the rates of these photochemical reactions, preventing a stable, cumulative relationship from forming between ozone concentration and precursor emissions, and ozone concentration is affected by strong spatiotemporal variability; its continuous accumulation is frequently interrupted by diurnal variations (e.g., weakening afternoon sunlight) or shifts in weather systems (e.g., precipitation, high winds)48–50. This makes a stable build-up difficult, leading to statistically insignificant cumulative effects in long-term analyses.

The results from the two-pollutant models provide a deeper understanding of the independent effects of pollutants and underscore the complexity involved in assessing inter-pollutant effect evaluations. In these models, after controlling for the influence of a co-pollutant, the independent effects of individual pollutants on the vitiligo BSI exhibited complex patterns of variation. For instance, SO2’s significant short-term effect (lag 0) was not only robust but was amplified after controlling for co-pollutants like PM2.5, with its effect estimate increasing from 2.03% to 2.55% in the SO2-PM2.5 model. This suggests a distinct, independent immediate impact of SO2 on health-related search behavior.

Conversely, the effect of CO was more complex. In the single-pollutant model, its effect became significant at lag 5. However, after adjusting for PM2.5, the onset was delayed to lag 10 and its overall strength was diminished (e.g., effect estimate decreased from 0.5% to 0.39%). This is likely due to confounding from shared emission sources (e.g., traffic, industry), as high CO and PM2.5 exposure often co-occur, causing their effects to overlap in single-pollutant analyses51–53.

This study reveals a critical link between air quality and public health attention, particularly for skin diseases in urban areas like Changsha. Our findings suggest that public health strategies should prioritize mitigating key pollutants like SO2, CO, and PM2.5 and issue timely health advisories during high-pollution periods. Clinically, these results support the need for targeted preventive measures, such as enhanced skin protection with antioxidants, for individuals concerned about vitiligo. By integrating environmental surveillance with chronic disease monitoring, this research provides a scientific basis for developing more refined urban public health policies.

This study has several limitations. First, the BSI is a proxy for public attention, reflects public concern and information-seeking behavior, it is an indirect measure that may not perfectly correlate with actual disease incidence or clinical encounters. Search volumes can be temporarily elevated by media events, public awareness campaigns, or celebrity disclosures about vitiligo. Second, the findings are from a single city and may lack spatial generalizability to other regions with different environmental and demographic characteristics.Our findings are based on data from Changsha, a city with a subtropical monsoon climate and distinct pollutant profiles that may differ from those in northern industrial cities or coastal regions. The traffic flow patterns, industrial emission characteristics, and healthcare referral systems in Changsha may not be representative of other Chinese cities. Therefore, caution should be exercised when extrapolating these results to other geographic contexts. Multi-city studies encompassing diverse climatic zones and urban characteristics would help establish the generalizability of our findings and identify potential regional variations in pollutant-health associations. Third, as with all observational ecological studies, our findings demonstrate statistical associations rather than definitive causal relationships. Despite controlling for key confounders, unmeasured variables such as socioeconomic status, lifestyle factors, or indoor air quality could introduce residual confounding. Experimental studies or individual-level prospective cohort studies would be needed to establish causation definitively.Fourth, complex interactions among co-pollutants may not be fully accounted for.While our two-pollutant models provide evidence for independent pollutant effects, they do not fully capture potential synergistic or antagonistic interactions in pollutant mixtures. Future studies could employ advanced mixture modeling approaches such as Bayesian Kernel Machine Regression (BKMR) or quantile g-computation to better characterize joint effects and potential interactions.Future research integrating mixture methods with mechanistic understanding of skin barrier function and immune responses could provide deeper insights into how pollutant combinations affect vitiligo pathogenesis. Finally, our study established a statistical association without exploring the underlying biological mechanisms, and the 60-day lag period may not capture more extended chronic effects. Future multi-city research incorporating clinical and experimental data is needed to validate these findings and elucidate causal pathways.

Conclusions

This study reveals distinct temporal associations between ambient air pollutants and public interest in vitiligo, highlighting a prominent short-term effect from SO2 and a long-lasting influence from CO. These findings suggest that the acute impact of SO2 and the sustained environmental pressure from CO trigger public concern on different timescales. At a societal level, this shift in public attention serves as a crucial signal for exploring the link between pollutants and vitiligo pathogenesis.

The study’s novelty lies in using social perception data to capture the immediate public response to environmental changes. This big data approach offers a new perspective for environmental health risk communication and provides a scientific basis for developing targeted environmental policies, clinical preventive measures, and public health interventions. Future research should integrate clinical and experimental data to validate these associations and elucidate the underlying biological mechanisms linking specific pollutants to vitiligo pathogenesis.
