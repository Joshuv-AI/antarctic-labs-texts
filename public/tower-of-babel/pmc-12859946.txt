Cost-effectiveness of primary care-based risk assessment and hereditary cancer genetic testing
Authors: Beth Devine, Sanne E Aalbers, HuiHsuan Chan, Shangqing Jiang, Emerson J Dusic, Sarah Knerr, Heather M Harris, Catharine Wang, Barbara M Norquist, Laurie A Riemann, Jeannine M Brant, Brian H Shirts, Elizabeth M Swisher
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGuidelines recommend identifying individuals with a family or personal history of cancer, offering genetic testing, with the goal of managing disease risk. Yet, risk assessment (“screening”) and genetic testing remain underutilized in the primary care setting and the value of an optimal strategy for engaging individuals is unknown. Our goal is to estimate the incremental cost, incremental effectiveness, and incremental cost-effectiveness ratio (ICER) between two population-based engagement strategies in proportions of individuals risk assessed and tested, by providing these services in a primary care setting.MethodsData were obtained from the EDGE (Early Detection of GEnetic risk)-clinic-randomized controlled trial that evaluated two engagement strategies - an in-clinic point of care (POC) strategy and a direct participant engagement (DPE) strategy that involved (e)mailing invitations after a visit. At risk participants were offered complementary genetic testing and counseling. Using these data, we constructed a decision-analytic cohort model and compared the POC to DPE strategy. We modeled testing all clinic participants over a two-year timeframe and present results from both health-system and limited societal perspectives. Outcomes were the proportion of participants risk-assessed and tested, the costs for each strategy, and the ICERs.ResultsFrom the health-system perspective, costs for approaching 100,000 participants were $641,278 (POC) and $702,653 (DPE). The POC strategy led to 14,490 (46%) of participants completing risk assessment and the DPE strategy to 6,385 (7%) participants, thus POC dominated DPE for risk assessment completion (68% of simulations). The POC strategy led to fewer individuals completing testing than the DPE strategy [780 (73%) vs. 1,184 (77%)], revealing an ICER of $152 (health-system) and $136 (limited societal) perspectives in favor of DPE (52% and 58% of simulations). With substantial uncertainty, results suggest that the DPE strategy may provide good value for money spent on testing at a willingness-to-pay test-kit cost of $250.Conclusions and relevanceThat the incremental cost-effectiveness of risk assessment versus testing contrasts, suggests that engagement approaches play an important role in shaping outcomes of population-based risk assessments. A hybrid approach (DPE, followed by POC for those who do not respond) may be optimally cost-effective.Trial registrationNCT04746794; Date: February 4, 2021.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12875-025-03137-w.Keywords: Pragmatic clinical trial, Hereditary cancer risk assessment/Screening, Genetic testing, Primary care, Cost-effectiveness, Decision modeling

Background

Pathogenic variants (PVs) in high and moderate penetrance genes increase risk for a range of cancers. Targeted risk-reducing interventions (e.g. breast MRI) are known to lower morbidity and mortality in individuals with cancer-causing PVs [1–3]. Clinical guidelines detail personal and family history profiles associated with an increased likelihood of hereditary cancer and stress the importance of proactively identifying these individuals and offering them comprehensive genetic testing [4–9].

Our earlier work demonstrated the value of implementing a family history questionnaire to identify individuals referred for genetic counseling and considered for genetic testing. The questionnaire identified 21 of 26 (81%) individuals recommended for genetic referral compared to 13 of 26 (50%) potentially identified through medical records [10]. A more recent study implemented a family history questionnaire delivered in 19 primary care clinics across four health systems. Of the 1,829 participants who completed risk assessment, 15.2% were referred to genetic counseling. Providers in this study reported that risk assessment did not impede clinical workflow and recommended wider implementation [11].

Despite these results, risk assessment coupled with an offer of genetic testing is not routinely conducted in primary care [12–16]. Barriers to implementation occur at participant[12], provider [17, 18], health-system [19, 20], and policy levels [21], suggesting that better strategies to deliver recommended services are needed [12]. The EDGE (Early Detection of GEnetic risk) clinic-randomized controlled trial (RCT) was designed to generate this evidence. EDGE compared two primary care, population-based engagement strategies for identifying participants eligible for cancer genetic testing by risk assessing individuals with a risk assessment tool designed by the investigator team. Those who met pre-specified eligibility criteria for being at risk were then offered genetic testing [22]. The present study uses cost-effectiveness analyses (CEAs) to estimate the comparative value of the two engagement strategies: point of care (POC) or direct participant engagement DPE).

Methods

Overview of the EDGE RCT

The design and results of the EDGE RCT has been published [22, 23]. Briefly, clinics randomized to POC invited participants to complete risk assessment on a tablet computer as they arrived in person for a routinely scheduled primary care appointment. Clinics randomized to DPE contacted participants via email or paper mail one to three months after a primary care appointment; and provided a link to the risk assessment survey to facilitate asynchronous completion. All participants 25 years of age or older, comfortable reading and writing in English, and meeting the appointment criteria above were eligible for outreach.

Clinics enrolled in the EDGE Trial were recruited from two healthcare networks in the Northwest Participant and Clinical Interactions Network (NW PCI Network) [24]. The NW PCI Network is a collaboration of regional clinical and translational research centers affiliated with medical centers, healthcare systems and universities in the five-state WWAMI region (Washington, Wyoming, Alaska, Montana and Idaho). The NW PCI Network is led by the University of Washington’s Institute of Translational Health Sciences, one over 60 Clinical and Translational Science Award sites funded by the National Institutes of Health. The two health systems in the network best positioned to participate in the EDGE RCT were the Billings Clinic serving Montana, Wyoming, and the Dakotas, and Multicare, serving Washington State. Due to the Covid pandemic, Multicare switched to telehealth visits during the data collection timeframe (April 1, 2021 through March 31, 2022). To preserve data collection as accurately as possible, reduce model complexity, and facilitate fair comparisons, this CEA is limited to clinics within the Billings Clinic network. Billings Clinic offers primary care- and specialty services, inpatient care settings and a network of federally qualified health centers. Genetic services are available on a limited basis. Clinic-level randomization strategy to either point of care (POC; N = 3) or direct participant engagement (DPE; N = 3) provided acceptable balance with respect to participant and clinic characteristics.

Participants eligible for genetic testing received a prompt at completion of the risk assessment and were invited to provide contact information to study staff. Staff followed-up by contacting each participant, answering questions and gathering information required to have a test kit sent from Color Health® to the participant’s address [25]. Participants were required to provide an email address and to set up an online account to activate their saliva test kit and to receive results. Those with pathogenic or likely pathogenic (P/LP) variants were offered counseling. Both the test kit and counseling were complementary during the study. For tested participants found to have P/LP variants, a gene-specific risk management plan was placed in the electronic health record for review by the clinician and participant. The University of Washington Human Subjects (Participants) Division (Institutional Review Board) deemed the RCT constituted minimal risk and approved it as study number STUDY00009476. Therefore, informed consent was waived for participants. However, participants were given the option to decline the risk assessment and participation in any subsequent RCT activities. The RCT adhered to the requirements of the Declaration of Helsinki. The RCT results manuscript followed the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) 2010 reporting guideline [26]. This economic evaluation manuscript adheres to the CHEERS 2 (Consolidated Health Economic Evaluation Reporting Standards 2022, Additional File 1) best practices checklist for economic analyses [27].

Decision model structure

We employed a decision tree framework and created a cohort state transition model in Microsoft Excel® (Redmond, WA) to estimate the incremental cost, incremental effectiveness, and incremental cost-effectiveness ratio (ICER) between the two strategies. The comparative value was assessed by calculating the difference in costs between the two strategies, divided by the difference in each outcome (risk assessment/testing) respectively, to arrive at the ICER.

Our reference perspective is that of a health-system developing and launching (baseline phase) and maintaining (maintenance phase) a population-based risk assessment program for hereditary cancers. We also estimated the value from a limited societal perspective by including participant time costs for completing risk assessment and, for participants with P/LP variants, their follow-up time to understand results. We used an annual model cycle, a 2-year time horizon to capture all eligible clinic participants during a wellness visit, a 3% discount rate in year 2, and present results in 2023 $US [28].

Model input parameters

Data to inform model input parameters were collected alongside the 12-month RCT. (Additional File 2)

Transition probabilities

Probabilities in the decision model reflect the proportions of participants followed at each step in the RCT. Ranges reflect clinic-specific variability in the proportions of participants who proceeded to each following transition.

Time use and costs

Time use and costs excluded study-related activities. For clinic providers and staff, we estimated the median number of hours spent developing, launching, and maintaining the risk assessment and genetic testing protocol using bespoke surveys we created for this purpose. Each survey was comprised of role-, and phase-specific questions created with input from academic and clinic-based investigators. Ranges represent minimum/maximum hours. We converted these estimates to costs by multiplying them using 2023 region-specific mean wage statistics provided by the US National Bureau of Labor Statistics [29]. (Additional File 3)

For participants, we captured the median number of hours spent completing the risk-assessment survey using real-time electronic audit logs. We also embedded four targeted questions into participant interview guides to capture information about the amount of time individuals with P/LP results spent reviewing results on the Color® website, and talking to a genetic counselor, their provider, and family members about their results. We converted all participant time estimates to mean costs using methods recommended for costing leisure time in the field of health economics [30]. (Additional File 4)

Supply, equipment, test kit costs

Supply costs included (e)mailing an invitation to those in the DPE arm, inviting them to complete the risk assessment survey by clicking on a customizable URL. The link was used to track which participants completed the risk assessment. Test results were mailed to participants with P/LP variants and variants of unknown significance (VUS). Equipment costs were included for the purchase of tablet computers and accessories used in the POC strategy. We captured the cost of each test kit mailed by and returned to Color® for analysis, as well as those not returned. We used audit logs to capture the cost of all mailings, purchase invoices to capture the cost of tablet computers and accessories, and test kits ordered and returned. Ranges were calculating by adding and subtracting 20% from each dollar value. (Additional File 5)

All costs were apportioned to the proportion of participants who completed each step (approached, risk assessed, eligible, tested) and then summed to arrive at total costs.

Outcomes

The two clinical outcome measures were the proportions of participants completing risk assessment and testing, both of which were obtained from the EDGE enrollment numbers.

Model assumptions

Estimates from the National Health Interview Survey suggest that approximately 80% of registered participants complete a primary care wellness visit annually [31]. We considered the proportion in our study to represent the 80% and extended our model over two years to capture the remaining 20%, such that the entire clinic population would be offered risk assessment once in a 2-year timeframe. Second, we assumed one-time testing is sufficient to identify risks and did not model changes in personal or family history or newly discovered PVs over time. Third, we assumed that readying each clinic for implementation took five months and considered the sixth month as a launch timeframe, giving clinics time to improve the roll-out of the risk assessment service, and included these data as the baseline phase. We considered time spent conducting risk assessment, testing, and conducting follow-up as the maintenance phase. Fourth, we assumed that all of a clinic’s provider/clinic staff would be impacted by the population-based program. Thus, we multiplied the median time spent by those who completed our surveys, by the numbers of provider and clinic staff serving in each role in each clinic.

Statistical analysis

Reference cases and sensitivity analyses

We modeled a health-system of 100,000 participants, calculating the reference (base) case from two perspectives. In the health-system perspective, we included direct costs incurred by providers and clinic staff implementing the program and providing care, cost of mailings, tablet computers, and test kits. In the limited societal perspective, we added cost of leisure time participants spent completing risk assessment, testing, and in follow-up activities. For each perspective and each strategy, we calculated the ICER. Deterministically, we conducted one-way sensitivity analyses (OWSAs), varying each input parameter over a pre-specified range based on the RCT data, holding other input parameters constant, reporting these in tornado diagrams. We conducted a probabilistic sensitivity analysis (PSA) where we randomly varied all input parameters simultaneously, based on pre-specified distributions. To perform the PSA, we used 1,000 iterations and report results as a scatterplot. We estimated the probability of an engagement strategy being cost-effective over a range of willingness to pay (WTP) values per test kit (0 to $1,000) and use these to illustrate cost-effectiveness acceptability curves (CEAC). We separately conducted two companion analyses using national wages in place of regional health-system wages [32].

Scenario analysis

We conducted a separate scenario analysis (using reference case numbers) of those risk assessed by isolating the proportion and costs for those who completed the risk assessment but did not proceed to testing.

Outcomes and cost-effectiveness analysis

Reference case

The POC strategy led to 14,490 participants (46%) while the DPE strategy led to 6,385 (7%) participants completing risk assessments. As the POC strategy cost less and led to a greater proportion of participants completing the risk assessment, the POC strategy dominated the DPE strategy with respect to risk assessment. However, the POC strategy led to 780 (73%) participants undergoing genetic testing while the DPE strategy led to 1,184 (77%) participants completing testing. Thus, the DPE strategy was more costly, but resulted in more participants tested, for an ICER of $152 per participant tested. Model results from the limited societal perspective were similar. One-way sensitivity analyses (OWSA)

Risk assessment

In the sensitivity analyses, the DPE strategy was dominated by the POC strategy as it was both more expensive and less effective. Therefore, OWSA results for the DPE strategy were not interpreted, as they hold no meaningful relevance.

Testing

The OWSAs for testing (Figs. 2a and b) present the most influential parameters. The list is led by the proportion of participants approached in the POC arm, with corresponding ICERs ranging from $93 to $69,967. As the proportion of participants approached increased, the proportion tested in the POC strategy rose, reducing the difference in participants tested between the two strategies, leading to a higher ICER. At the original high input value for this parameter (0.484), the POC arm exceeded the DPE arm in participants tested, rendering the DPE strategy both more expensive and less effective (dominated). To ensure interpretable results, we capped the proportion of participants approached in the POC arm at 0.479, yielding the highest ICER value ($69,967) for this parameter.

One-way sensitivity analyses. a health-system perspective tested; b limited societal perspective tested

Similarly, the proportion of tests ordered in the POC arm influenced the ICER, with higher test orders increasing ICERs as the proportion tested in the POC arm approached and exceeded the DPE arm. To maintain interpretability, we capped the proportion of tests ordered in the POC arm at 0.34 (vs. 0.38). Maintenance costs for providers in year 1 in POC arm were the third most influential driver. As these costs increased in the POC strategy, the cost difference between strategies narrowed, lowering the ICER, while decreases in costs raised the ICER.

For the DPE strategy, the proportion of participants approached, and provider maintenance costs were key drivers. Higher participant outreach in the DPE strategy widened outcome differences and lowered the ICER, while increased provider costs raised the ICER.

Discussion

The EDGE RCT is the first to compare the effectiveness of two engagement strategies for conducting population-based risk assessment and offering genetic testing in primary care. Our accompanying cohort decision model provides the first estimates of the comparative value of these two strategies. Model results suggest the POC strategy dominates the DPE strategy for risk assessment completion. However, once the cost of a test kit reaches approximately $200 for a test kit, the DPE testing strategy provides good value for dollars spent, with an ICER of $152 from the health-system perspective. Model results from the limited societal perspective were similar. The model using national wages confirms model robustness.

Other published cost-effectiveness models that compare population-based testing strategies to clinical or family histories strategies also indicate preferences for population-based strategies. Guo modeled the cost-utility of population-based versus family history-based multi-gene testing for breast cancer in 30- to 35-year-old women, and found that a population-based strategy was cost-effective in 100% of the simulations at a WTP threshold of $100,000 per quality-adjusted life year (QALY) gained [33]. Guzauskas´ and Manchanda´s models found similar results [34, 35]. In their Lynch syndrome model, Guzauskas´ group also found that population-based testing in 30-year-olds was cost-effective in 71% of simulations at a WTP threshold of $150,000/QALY gained [36]. Our model is unique in that it describes the comparative value of two participant engagement strategies on near-term outcomes of proportions of participants completing risk assessment and genetic testing. The literature applying utilities to risk assessment and testing in those at risk for hereditary cancers is scant. We identified only one study that reported utilities for testing for breast and ovarian cancer. These utilities were for individuals with positive test results and ranged from 0.83 to 1.00, with a duration of time lasting one to four years [37]. This is an area that deserves study, as we postulate personal preferences underlie risk assessment and testing rates, as evidenced in our study.

Although our risk assessment and test results seem contrary to one another, we believe they are plausible. The lower cost of conducting risk assessment in the POC strategy reflects the fewer proportion of participants approached (32% vs. 96%), as the POC strategy was more labor intensive in that it required in-person contact and its effectiveness was limited by staffing available to approach participants in real time. Although a greater proportion of those approached in the POC strategy were willing to complete risk assessment (46% vs. 7%), fewer were eligible to complete testing (33% vs. 43%), relative to DPE. Furthermore, those eligible in the POC strategy were less likely than DPE participants to follow through by ordering a genetic testing kit once home (23% vs. 55%) and returning the test kit (73% vs. 77%). In contrast, although the DPE strategy invited more participants to complete risk assessment (approached), fewer were willing to do so. We posit that DPE participants who completed risk assessment were more likely to have a personal or family history of cancer, which may have motivated them to follow through with testing [38]. The proportion that tested positive in the DPE strategy was twice that in the POC strategy – 12% vs. 6%.

Our scenario analysis that isolates the ICER for those who were approached and completed risk assessment supports this reasoning. In this analysis, the POC strategy cost more to facilitate a higher proportion of those completing a risk assessment – at $6 per participant risk assessed. Although $6 suggests that risk assessment is, in itself a worthwhile program, without knowing what proportion of the 68% (100% −32%) enrolled in the POC strategy would have completed risk assessment had they been approached and been eligible for testing, or agreed to testing, is unknown. We reason that this risk assessment ICER does not reflect the true cost of offering risk assessment to everyone. These results highlight that differing risk assessment strategies may reach individuals with varying underlying motivations and supports the primary analysis that comprises the combined risk assessment and testing program.

Per-participant clinic staff time costs were greater than providers´ through the baseline phase, as staff were primarily responsible for setting up the program. Per-participant provider time was greatest during the maintenance phase of the study, because, although only a small proportion of tested participants had a PV, provider time is expensive; this drove strategy cost effectiveness. Per participant time costs to complete risk assessment were higher in the POC strategy, while participant time costs for follow-up of positive test results reflect the greater proportion of participants identified with PVs in the DPE strategy, although neither drove cost-effectiveness. (Table)

Neither test kits not returned or returned to Color® (analyzed) significantly impacted model results. The latter included the opportunity to engage Color’s® health care professionals in care management, potentially lowering costs to the health-system. However, insurance carriers do not consistently reimburse for these tests. The EDGE RCT removed this barrier by covering test kit and counseling costs. We used $250 in the model with the knowledge that the out-of-pocket costs for a test kit for hereditary cancer testing is between $0-$250 [39, 40]. Genetic testing costs have dropped over the past decade, testing for multiple genes is more efficient than testing a few. Yet, lack of training in genomic testing and lack of evidence for clinical utility introduces reluctance on the part of providers to order them and payers to pay for them [41].

As we enrolled English-speaking participants over the age of 25 years, the generalizability of our results may be limited to populations similar to those in our enrolled clinics. Importantly, genetic risk for cancers varies across ancestries [42]. This diversity arises from differences in germline genetics and tumor biology. Race and ethnicity are inadequate substitutes for estimating underlying cancer risk. Moreover, variability can be influenced by environmental exposures, life-style factors, insurance status, and access to healthcare; these are often underpinned by socioeconomic characteristics [42]. “Furthermore, those under 25 years (adolescents and young adults) may be less inclined to attend clinic. However, of those who did enroll in the EDGE RCT, when compared to the 25 to 44 year olds, the odds of completing testing was lower in those who were ≥ 85 years, lower in those who were 65 to 84 years, and lower those who were 45 to 64 years [23]. Females were more likely to complete testing than males. The adjusted odds of completing testing were higher in those who had ≥ 2 first degree relatives with canccer. Those with a personal history of cancer were less likely to complete testing than those without a cancer history. Each of these may influence individual motivations to risk assess and test. Our results are context-specific and caution is warranted when considering generalizing to other settings and populations.”

As a pragmatic RCT, EDGE could be evaluated using the PRECIS-2 tool (PRagmatic Explanatory Continuum Indicator Summary), designed to aid trialists as they consider where their trial lay on the pragmatic/explanatory continuum in ten domains: eligibility, recruitment, setting, organization, flexible delivery, flexible adherence, follow-up, primary outcome, and primary analysis [43]. The EDGE study was challenging to execute in many of these domains, which resulted in the uncertainty reflected in our model. The major modeling limitation was the imprecision created due to our reliance on the use of REDCap surveys to capture time estimates, despite our careful attempts to craft these in a meaningful way. Study resource limitations prevented us from conducting a formal time-motion study in the clinics. Finally, we did not capture time spent by participants to whom VUS results were mailed and acknowledge that understanding these results may be time-consuming.

That we were able to complete the RCT and illuminate the complexities involved by conducting this companion CEA is this study’s greatest strength. These findings lead to three potential follow-on evaluations. First, they prompt the question of which latent participant characteristics motivated risk assessment versus testing; a future study from a behavioral economic perspective might inform this. Second, dissecting the importance of each step in the implementation process may be useful. Lastly, a hybrid approach, that is, sending posted mail or emails to all participants and following up at the POC of care for those who do not respond could be explored as a winning strategy.

Conclusions

The most salient finding from the EDGE cost-effectiveness analysis is that in-person contact prompted a greater proportion of participants to complete risk assessment, to the point of being the dominant strategy, while the posted mail/email approach prompted a greater proportion to test, and at a cost that is lower than the cost of a test kit. These results suggest that personal motivation may be setting-specific (in-clinic vs. mailing). The analysis sheds light on the importance of tailoring implementation strategies to maximize yield for hereditary cancer risk assessment and follow-on genetic testing in the primary care setting. Which approach employed, or a combination thereof, will depend on the needs of the population cared for, and by the resources available within each unique health-system. Regardless, we have demonstrated the cost-effectiveness of risk assessment and testing participants for hereditary cancers in primary care.
