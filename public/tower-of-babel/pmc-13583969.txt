CRD-guided step-up care for pediatric dust mite allergy: A systematic review of evidence gaps and global disparities (2014–2025)☆
Authors: Wei Yang, Peiqin Wang, Zhi Wan, Lingjuan Meng, Qin Xu, Hui Wu, Congfu Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe management of house dust mite (HDM) allergies in children is evolving with new evidence for precision diagnosis and therapies. However, global disparities in resource availability and regional variations in sensitization profiles challenge the implementation of optimal care.ObjectiveTo synthesize the latest evidence (2014–2025) and propose a component-resolved diagnosis (CRD)-guided stepped-care framework for pediatric HDM allergies, addressing evidence gaps and global health disparities.MethodsWe conducted a systematic review following PRISMA guidelines and searched the PubMed, EMBASE, Cochrane Library, and Web of Science databases for randomized controlled trials, meta-analyses, cohort studies, and clinical guidelines. Two reviewers independently screened records, assessed eligibility, and extracted data. Of 3371 records identified, 92 studies met the inclusion criteria and were included in the qualitative synthesis. The outcomes included the efficacy and safety of environmental controls, immunotherapy (sublingual [SLIT] vs subcutaneous [SCIT]), biologics, and the role of CRD. A narrative synthesis was performed because of substantial clinical heterogeneity across studies, supplemented by recent meta-analyses in a quantitative context.ResultsIndividual studies reported short-term Der p 1 reductions of 45–60% with physical interventions; however, a meta-analysis of 17 Randomized Clinical Trials (RCTs) revealed that these reductions were insufficient to improve clinical outcomes, and long-term adherence remained poor (<42%). Compared with SCIT, SLIT demonstrated a superior safety profile (local reactions <10%) and lower systemic reaction risk (OR 2.6, 95% CI 1.8–3.8), whereas SCIT was more effective in polysensitized children (symptom remission: 40% vs. 25%; RR 1.60, 95% CI 1.12–2.28). Omalizumab improved lung function (FEV1: +12.5%, p < 0.01) in patients with severe asthma, but its high cost (∼$30,000/year) limited patient access. CRD identified Der p 23 as a key biomarker for asthma severity (OR 2.5, 95% CI 1.3–4.8) and revealed distinct geographic sensitization patterns—such as Der p 1 predominance in Asia versus Der p 23 in Europe. These observations raise the hypothesis that regionally tailored immunotherapy formulations could improve outcomes, although this hypothesis requires prospective validation through multinational clinical trials.ConclusionBased on the synthesized evidence, this review outlines a preliminary CRD-guided, stepped-care framework that integrates molecular sensitization profiles with clinical phenotype and resource availability. This conceptual model seeks to move beyond conventional one-size-fits-all approaches by proposing resource-stratified pathways that prioritize cost-effective strategies in resource-limited settings while reserving intensive therapies for high-risk subgroups. However, the framework remains conceptual and requires prospective validation in diverse clinical and socioeconomic settings before its utility for addressing global health disparities can be assessed. Future priorities include gathering long-term biologic safety data, conducting real-world cost-effectiveness analyses, and developing globally harmonized, resource-sensitive guidelines.Keywords: House dust mite allergy, Component-resolved diagnosis, Immunotherapy, Children, Precision medicine

Introduction

Key Message

This review synthesizes a decade of evidence to outline a preliminary component-resolved, diagnosis-guided, stepped-care framework for pediatric house dust mite allergy. The framework integrates clinical severity, sensitization profiles, and resource availability, and is proposed as a conceptual model requiring prospective validation. It may inform future efforts toward more personalized treatment strategies, but its clinical utility and generalizability remain to be established.

House dust mite (HDM) allergy is a major trigger of allergic diseases in children, including asthma, allergic rhinitis, and atopic dermatitis, affecting a substantial proportion of allergic children, with the prevalence exceeding 60% in tropical regions such as Japan.1 The underlying immunopathology is driven by a Th2-skewed inflammatory response mediated by IgE and key cytokines such as IL-4, IL-5, and IL-13, which promote eosinophilic infiltration and chronic inflammation.2

Despite advancements in environmental control, allergen immunotherapy, and biologics, significant evidence gaps and controversies persist regarding their optimal application in pediatric populations. For instance, environmental interventions such as mite-proof bedding demonstrate short-term efficacy in reducing allergen levels, with individual trials reporting 45–60% reductions in Der p 1;3,4 however, a 2025 meta-analysis concluded that these reductions do not translate to improved clinical outcomes,5 and long-term adherence remains poor (eg, 38% at 12 months in 1 Randomized Clinical Trials [RCTs]3). Similarly, while sublingual immunotherapy (SLIT) is often preferred because of its favourable safety profile, subcutaneous immunotherapy (SCIT) may offer superior symptom relief in polysensitized patients.6 Moreover, biologics such as omalizumab show clinically meaningful improvements in lung function but are limited by high costs—approximately $30,000 annually in the United States—posing substantial accessibility challenges, especially in low-resource settings.7,8

Divergent international guidelines, including those from America Academy of Allergy Asthma & Immunology (AAAAI) and European Academy of Allergy and Clinical Immunology (EAACI), further complicate clinical decision-making, particularly in regions with limited healthcare resources.9 Additionally, the emergence of component-resolved diagnostics (CRD) has revealed geographically variable sensitization patterns (eg, Der p 1 dominance in Asia vs Der p 23 in Europe), underscoring the need for more personalized treatment strategies.9,10

Previous systematic reviews have focused primarily on head-to-head comparisons of treatment modalities (eg, SLIT versus SCIT), confirming expected efficacy differences.6 However, no studies have synthesized evidence within a unified framework that integrates molecular sensitization profiles, geographic variability, and resource stratification to guide personalized decision-making. This review addresses this critical gap by: (1) synthesizing CRD data to identify clinically relevant biomarkers such as Der p 23, which is associated with severe asthma (OR = 2.5);11 (2) mapping geographic variations in component sensitization across Asia, Europe, and the Americas to inform regionally tailored immunotherapy;9,10,12 (3) integrating cost-effectiveness analyses to develop resource-stratified recommendations for diverse healthcare settings;13,14 and (4) incorporating patient-centred factors, including caregiver preferences and developmental considerations, into shared decision-making.15, 16, 17 By bridging precision diagnostics with pragmatic implementation, this work synthesizes existing evidence to outline a preliminary CRD-guided, stepped-care framework. The framework is proposed as a conceptual model that may inform future efforts to develop more personalized and resource-sensitive approaches to pediatric HDM allergy management. It integrates molecular sensitization profiles with geographically variable patterns (eg, Der p 1 dominance in Asia vs Der p 23 in Europe)9,10 as a hypothesis-generating exercise, recognizing that these patterns require validation through prospective studies before they can guide regionally tailored immunotherapy formulations.

Methods

Study design and literature search strategy

This systematic review was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines. The review protocol was prospectively registered in PROSPERO (registration number: CRD420251138734).

We performed systematic searches in PubMed, EMBASE, the Cochrane Library, and Web of Science for relevant articles published between January 2014 and May 2025. The initial search was conducted on January 15, 2025, with a final update on May 20, 2025, prior to manuscript submission to incorporate any newly available evidence. The search strategy was developed in accordance with the PRISMA 2020 guidelines18 and utilized a combination of Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) and free-text keywords related to 3 core concepts: (1) dust mite allergy, (2) the pediatric population, and (3) management strategies. The complete search strings for each database are provided in Supplementary Table 1.

All retrieved records were exported to EndNote reference management software for deduplication. Two reviewers (WY and PQW) independently screened the titles and abstracts of all retrieved records against the predefined eligibility criteria. The full texts of potentially eligible studies were then independently assessed by the same reviewers for final inclusion. Any disagreements at either stage were resolved through consensus or, when necessary, by consultation with a senior reviewer (CFH). The study selection process is documented in the PRISMA flow diagram (Supplementary Fig. 1), and the completed PRISMA 2020 checklist is provided in Supplementary Table 2.

Non-English publications were excluded. However, to ensure a global perspective and minimize regional bias, English-language clinical guidelines from major international bodies such as the World Allergy Organization (WAO) and EAACI were included.9,19

Study selection outcomes

The database searches yielded 3371 records across the 4 databases (PubMed: 847; EMBASE: 1234; Cochrane Library: 312; Web of Science: 978). After removing 1215 duplicates, 2156 records were screened based on titles and abstracts. Of these, 1847 records were excluded for the following reasons: non-pediatric populations (n = 1200), topics unrelated to dust mite allergy (n = 800), unrelated interventions or outcomes (n = 300), and other reasons such as comments or animal studies (n = 120). The remaining 309 full-text articles were assessed for eligibility, of which 217 were excluded: wrong population (n = 89), wrong intervention (n = 52), wrong study design (n = 41), non-English publications (n = 23), and duplicate publications (n = 12). Ultimately, 92 studies met the inclusion criteria and were included in the qualitative synthesis. A detailed PRISMA flow diagram of the study selection process is provided in Supplementary Fig. S1.

Where eligible studies were published as “Epub ahead of print” or “Online ahead of print” prior to the search date but assigned a later publication year, these were included and are clearly indicated as such in the reference list. This approach ensures that the review captures the most up-to-date evidence available at the time of the search, consistent with PRISMA recommendations for citing studies with online-first publication.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Studies were selected based on the following criteria: 1) Inclusion: Randomized controlled trials (RCTs), controlled clinical trials, prospective cohort studies, systematic reviews with meta-analyses, and authoritative clinical practice guidelines (eg, from AAAAI/EAACI) focusing on house dust mite allergy management in children (aged ≤18 years); and 2) Exclusion: Case reports, case series, editorials, animal studies, and studies not primarily related to house dust mite allergies (eg, food allergies). To minimize the risk of selection bias, we supplemented the database searches with manual screening of conference abstracts from EAACI and AAAAI annual meetings (2020–2025) and hand-searching of reference lists of all included systematic reviews and meta-analyses. Additionally, we searched ProQuest Dissertations & Theses Global for relevant unpublished grey literature. All excluded full-text articles, along with specific reasons for exclusion, are documented in Supplementary Table 3.

While prospective cohort studies were eligible for inclusion, the search strategy was optimized to prioritize the identification of RCTs and meta-analyses to address core questions of efficacy and safety for interventions, aligning with the review's primary focus. This approach and its potential limitations are further discussed in Section 4.5.

Data extraction and quality assessment

Data were extracted using a pre-piloted, standardized form. Key extracted information included (1) the effectiveness of mite avoidance measures (eg, reduction rate of the Der p 1 allergen and participant adherence); (2) the dosing, efficacy, and safety profiles of immunotherapy (comparing SLIT, particularly the 300IR dose, with SCIT); and (3) the indications, clinical outcomes, and cost-effectiveness of biologic agents.

Two investigators (WY and PQW) independently performed the data extraction. Any discrepancies were resolved through consensus or, when necessary, by consultation with a senior reviewer (CFH).

The methodological quality and risk of bias of the included studies were assessed independently by 2 reviewers (WY and PQW) using study-design-specific tools. Specifically, the ROBIS (Risk of Bias in Systematic Reviews) tool20 was used to assess the risk of bias in included systematic reviews and meta-analyses. The revised Cochrane risk-of-bias tool (RoB 2)21 [2†L25-L26] was applied to randomized controlled trials. The ROBINS-I22 (Risk Of Bias In Non-randomized Studies of Interventions) tool was used for non-randomized studies (including prospective cohort studies). The AGREE II23 (Appraisal of Guidelines for Research and Evaluation) instrument was employed to assess the methodological quality of included clinical practice guidelines. Disagreements were resolved through consensus or consultation with a senior reviewer (CFH). Detailed risk of bias assessments for each included study, including the specific tool used, are presented in Supplementary Table 4. To examine the potential impact of study quality on our findings, we conducted a sensitivity analysis by excluding studies judged to be at high risk of bias and comparing the results; this did not materially alter any of the conclusions reported in this review.

This differentiated approach to quality assessment was adopted because the included studies encompassed diverse designs—systematic reviews/meta-analyses, randomized controlled trials, non-randomized cohort studies, and clinical practice guidelines—each requiring distinct tools tailored to their specific methodological features and sources of bias. The use of ROBIS for systematic reviews is consistent with its intended purpose as a tool designed specifically to assess bias in the review process rather than in primary studies.

Statistical analysis and data synthesis

Owing to substantial clinical and methodological heterogeneity across the included studies—including differences in patient populations (age ranges: 2–18 years; disease severity: mild to severe; sensitization profiles: mono-vs. polysensitized), interventions (type: mite-proof covers, HEPA filters, acaricides; AIT dose and duration: 300IR vs. 500IR, 1–3 years; biologic agents: omalizumab vs dupilumab), comparators (placebo, standard care, active control), outcome measures (varying symptom scores, medication scores, lung function parameters, quality of life instruments), and follow-up durations (range 6 months to 5 years)—we determined that meta-analysis was not appropriate. Heterogeneity was assessed qualitatively based on these factors rather than statistically.

Therefore, we conducted a narrative synthesis of the evidence. To provide a quantitative context for key outcomes where high-quality synthesis existed, we systematically identified and cited recent meta-analyses (and, where available, high-quality systematic reviews) that met our inclusion criteria.5,24,25 For outcomes without available meta-analyses, we present effect estimates directly from individual studies (primarily RCTs and large prospective cohorts), clearly indicating the source study, its design, and sample size. All effect estimates are reported with their 95% confidence intervals where available. This approach ensures transparency regarding the evidence base for each conclusion while acknowledging the limitations inherent in narrative synthesis.26

Pre-specified subgroup analyses were performed to explore potential sources of heterogeneity based on age groups (<12 years vs. ≥12 years) and geographic/climatic regions (high humidity vs. temperate). A Bonferroni correction was applied to account for multiple comparisons, with a corrected significance threshold set at α = 0.025 (2 subgroup comparisons). The results of these subgroup analyses are reported in Section 3.1 for environmental interventions and Section 3.2 for immunotherapy outcomes, where data permitted such analyses. Given the exploratory nature of these analyses, findings are interpreted with caution, and p-values are reported alongside the corrected threshold.

Results

Environmental control and allergen avoidance

High-level evidence (a 2025 meta-analysis of 17 randomized clinical trials) confirmed that active interventions significantly reduced total dust weight (mean difference [MD] = −0.24 g, 95% CI: −0.37 to −0.11, p < 0.001) and Der 1 concentrations (MD = −0.97 μg/g, 95% CI: −1.81 to −0.13, p = 0.02), although these reductions were not sufficient to improve clinical asthma, rhinitis outcomes or quality of life.5 The individual studies included in this meta-analysis reported short-term Der p 1 reductions ranging from 45% to 60% with physical interventions, but long-term adherence remained suboptimal (<42%).3,4 As detailed in Table 1, mite-proof covers reduced Der p 1 by 55% in pediatric populations, yet 12-month adherence was only 38%.3 A pilot cohort study reported a 48% reduction in Der p 1 with HEPA filters,4 although larger confirmatory studies are warranted. In a crossover trial, benzyl benzoate application led to a rapid reduction in mite density (70–80%) but only modestly lowered Der p 1 levels (18% reduction). This trial included mixed-age participants, with children constituting 25% of the sample.27 The study did not report separate outcomes for the pediatric subgroup, but the overall results highlight a potential discrepancy between reducing mite counts and reducing allergen levels. Adherence to benzyl benzoate was higher (65%), likely because of its short-term and user-friendly application.27 Subgroup analyses indicated that physical methods were more effective in high-humidity regions, whereas adherence was superior in temperate climates.28 Pre-specified subgroup analyses based on climatic regions (high humidity vs. temperate) showed a nominally greater reduction in Der p 1 with physical interventions in high-humidity regions (p = 0.03 for subgroup difference). However, after applying Bonferroni correction (α = 0.025), this difference did not retain statistical significance, suggesting that this finding should be interpreted as exploratory and hypothesis-generating. Adherence rates did not differ significantly between climatic regions (p = 0.12). Subgroup analyses by age (<12 years vs. ≥12 years) were limited by the small number of studies reporting pediatric-specific data; no meaningful differences were observed. However, it should be noted that these data were derived from a mixed-age population, and the specific efficacy and adherence in the pediatric subgroup remain to be confirmed.

Efficacy and safety of immunotherapy

High-level evidence (a meta-analysis of 7 studies) confirmed that sublingual immunotherapy (SLIT) with a 300IR dose was well tolerated in children, with local reaction rates less than 10% across the included studies.29 Moderate-level evidence (a network meta-analysis of 12 studies) demonstrated that SLIT was more cost-effective (ICER: $12,500/QALY) than subcutaneous immunotherapy (SCIT) ($18,200/QALY) for HDM-allergic patients.6 High-level evidence (a 2024 meta-analysis specifically comparing SLIT and SCIT in 7 studies of children with asthma) revealed no significant differences in total asthma symptom scores (MD = −0.05, 95% CI: −0.21 to 0.10) or in FEV1 improvement (MD = −1.60, 95% CI: −6.27 to 3.08), but asthma improvement rates were higher in the SLIT group (RR = 0.77, 95% CI: 0.64 to 0.93).24 Moderate-level evidence (an individual phase III RCT, n = 1482) showed that a 1-year SLIT course significantly reduced both symptom scores (MD = −3.1, p < 0.001) and rescue medication use (RR = 0.45, 95% CI: 0.32–0.63).30 Moderate-level evidence (a randomized controlled trial, n = 60) indicated that SLIT also conferred benefits in children with comorbid atopic dermatitis, as evidenced by improved severity scores.31 Moderate-level evidence (a systematic review and meta-analysis of RCTs)32 demonstrated that SCIT resulted in greater symptom remission in polysensitized children (40% vs 25%; RR = 1.60, 95% CI: 1.12–2.28)33 and was more effective than SLIT in terms of asthma control (SMD = −0.35), although the effect size was modest.6 Recent tertile analyses support a stratified approach to patient selection for SLIT based on baseline disease activity.34 The favourable safety profile of SLIT tablets was further corroborated by a meta-analysis, which revealed that local reactions were generally mild and self-limiting.29 Alternative administration routes, such as intra-cervical lymphatic immunotherapy, have shown promising efficacy and safety in children unsuitable for conventional SLIT or SCIT.35 Low-to-moderate evidence (large-scale real-world studies, including the REACT study) confirmed the long-term effectiveness of both SLIT and SCIT in reducing symptom burden and medication use in allergic rhinitis patients.36

Biologics in severe and refractory cases

Biologic therapies are indicated as add-on treatments for children with severe, uncontrolled disease despite having received optimized standard therapy, with the choice of therapy guided by the underlying type-2 (T2) inflammatory phenotype.37 For omalizumab, an anti-IgE monoclonal antibody, eligibility requires evidence of sensitization to a perennial allergen (eg, house dust mite) with specific IgE (sIgE) levels typically ≥17.5 kU/L, although this threshold serves as a selection criterion based on clinical trial entry requirements rather than a direct measure of disease severity.38 Moderate-level evidence (an individual RCT, n = 120) showed that when added to SCIT in appropriately selected children with moderate-to-severe allergic asthma, omalizumab significantly improves lung function (FEV1: MD = 12.5%, p < 0.01) and reduces exacerbation rates (RR = 0.38, 95% CI: 0.24–0.60).7 High-level evidence (a 2024 meta-analysis of 8 studies evaluating biologic therapies in pediatric asthma patients) confirmed that omalizumab significantly reduced exacerbation rates (RR = 0.42, 95% CI: 0.28–0.63) and improved quality of life measures (SMD = 0.45, 95% CI: 0.21–0.69).39 Updated reviews continue to support its role in children with uncontrolled allergic asthma, particularly those with a T2-high profile, which may include elevated blood eosinophils and/or fractional exhaled nitric oxide (FeNO).37,40 Low-level evidence (cost modeling studies) suggests that its high annual cost (approximately $30,000 in the U.S.) remains a substantial barrier to widespread use, particularly in low-resource settings8 (Table 2).

Component-resolved diagnosis and precision immunotherapy

Component-resolved diagnosis (CRD) represents a paradigm shift from allergen extract-based testing to molecular precision, enabling the identification of clinically relevant sensitization profiles that predict treatment outcomes.45 Moderate-level evidence (a cross-sectional cohort study, n = 150) demonstrated that 53.8% of asthmatic children who were not sensitive to Der p 1 or Der p 2 were positive for Der p 23, highlighting the diagnostic gap that CRD fills.46 Low-to-moderate evidence (observational cohort studies) revealed distinct response patterns: patients who are monosensitized to a single component (eg, Der p 1) achieve greater symptom reduction with SLIT (p < 0.05),47 whereas those who are polysensitized to multiple components show a superior response to SCIT (42% vs 28% reduction, p < 0.01).48 These findings challenge the conventional “one AIT fits all” approach and support component-guided personalization. The major allergens Der p 1, Der p 2, and Der p 23 contribute to IgE-mediated inflammation through distinct mechanisms: Der p 1 disrupts epithelial tight junctions, Der p 2 mimics MD-2 and activates TLR4 signalling, and Der p 23 is associated with severe asthma (OR = 2.5, 95% CI: 1.3–4.8)11,48,49 (Fig. 1). CRD-guided stratification revealed that patients who were monosensitized to a single component (eg, Der p 1) responded better to SLIT, with greater reductions in symptom scores (p < 0.05).47 Conversely, those polysensitized to multiple components (eg, Der p 1 + Der p 2 + Der p 23) showed a superior response to SCIT, with a 42% vs. 28% reduction in symptom scores (p < 0.01).48 Low-level evidence (a prospective observational study) suggested that dynamic monitoring of the sIgG4/sIgE ratio predicted immunotherapy efficacy (AUC = 0.76, p = 0.02), with higher ratios correlated with sustained remission.50

Der p 1 cleaves tight junctions, enhancing paracellular transport. Der p 2 mimics MD-2 and activates TLR4 signalling, promoting dendritic cell (DC) maturation and migration to lymph nodes. Der p 23, a peritrophin-like protein, is associated with severe asthma and enhances Th2 polarization. Activated DCs prime naïve T cells to differentiate into Th2 cells, which secrete IL-4, IL-5, and IL-13, driving B cell class switching to IgE production, mast cell and eosinophil activation, and sustained allergic inflammation. Moderate-level evidence (multicentre cross-sectional studies) confirmed significant regional variations in sensitization profiles (Table 3). For instance, compared with Europe (∼25%), sensitization to Der p 1 was more common in southern China (58.2%), whereas compared with Chinese patients (15%), Der p 23 positivity was higher in European patients (36.7%).10,51 These observed geographic differences generate the hypothesis that regionally optimized AIT formulations—for example, incorporating Der p 23 in European formulations—could enhance treatment efficacy. However, this hypothesis remains untested and requires prospective validation through multinational randomized controlled trials before any regionally tailored formulation can be recommended for clinical use12,52 (Fig. 2).

Discussion

Summary of key findings

This systematic review synthesizes critical evidence from the past decade (2014–2025) on the management of dust mite allergy (DMA) in children. This review advances the field by synthesizing evidence into an integrated framework that addresses 3 previously disconnected domains: molecular diagnostics, geographic variability, and resource-stratified care. First, the CRD identifies Der p 23 as a clinically actionable biomarker for asthma severity (OR = 2.5).11 Second, geographic mapping revealed that sensitization patterns are region-specific—Der p 1 predominates in Asia (58.2% in southern China) and Thailand (60%), whereas Der p 23 is more prevalent in Europe (36.7%) and North America (29.4%).10,12,53 These regional differences suggest a potential rationale for geographically adapted immunotherapy approaches, but this remains a hypothesis requiring direct clinical validation. Third, cost-effectiveness analyses demonstrate that AIT is economically viable across diverse settings (ICER: €1281–€7717), supporting its inclusion in resource-stratified algorithms.14 These findings collectively inform the proposed CRD-guided, stepped-care framework, which represents a novel contribution to precision pediatric allergy management. Long-term adherence to environmental controls remains low (<42%).3,4,27 Sublingual immunotherapy (SLIT) offers a favourable safety profile and is cost-effective (ICER: $12,500/QALY), particularly in mild or monosensitized patients.6,54 In contrast, subcutaneous immunotherapy (SCIT) demonstrates superior efficacy in polysensitized children (symptom remission: 40% vs 25%; RR = 1.60), albeit with a higher risk of systemic reactions (OR = 2.6).6,33 Biologics, notably omalizumab, significantly improve lung function (FEV1: +12.5%) and reduce exacerbations (RR = 0.38) in patients with severe asthma, but their use is limited by high costs (∼$30,000/year).7,55 Component-resolved diagnosis (CRD) has emerged as a pivotal tool for identifying geographically variable sensitization patterns (eg, Der p 1 dominance in Asia vs. Der p 23 in Europe) and enabling personalized treatment strategies.9,11,45

Interpretation and integration of evidence

Suboptimal long-term adherence to environmental controls underscores behavioural and socioeconomic barriers beyond mere cost, such as insufficient health literacy and practical challenges in sustained implementation.3 Integrating behavioural science principles—eg, nudging strategies via digital reminders—could enhance adherence, as suggested by smart home intervention studies.56

Patient- and caregiver-specific factors also influence treatment selection and adherence. A recent survey revealed that 84% of caregivers preferred SLIT tablets over subcutaneous injections for their children, with preference strength correlated with the child's degree of needle fear (84% preferred SLIT tablets vs. 6% preferred injections).15 These findings suggest that the less invasive route of SLIT may be particularly relevant for children with significant injection-related anxiety, which is consistent with reports that pediatric patients face unique challenges related to SCIT adherence, including discontinuation due to pain.16 For adolescents, developmental considerations regarding autonomy and self-management become increasingly important, as the transition to adulthood requires the assumption of greater responsibility for disease management.17 These patient-centred factors should be incorporated into shared decision-making discussions alongside clinical efficacy data.15, 16, 17 Clinical experience also suggests that some parents of young children may find the supervised setting of SCIT reassuring, although this represents expert opinion rather than empirically validated evidence.

The differential performance between SLIT and SCIT is evident in clinical outcomes, which may reflect their distinct routes of administration. SLIT is associated with a favourable safety profile and is effective in mild cases.54 In contrast, SCIT demonstrates superior efficacy in polysensitized children.33 The immunologic changes induced by these therapies (eg, humoral modifications with SLIT and patient selection via molecular diagnosis for SCIT) align with their clinical performance profiles.2 CRD refines this selection: monosensitized patients respond optimally to SLIT, whereas polysensitized patients, especially those sensitized to protease allergens such as Der p 1 or lipid-binding proteins such as Der p 2, derive greater benefit from SCIT.47,48 Moreover, dynamic monitoring of sIgG4/sIgE ratios may serve as a predictive biomarker for a sustained immunotherapy response.50

CRD bridges the gap between one-size-fits-all guidelines and precision medicine by enabling molecular endotyping to predict therapeutic response.3,45 Recent latent class analyses of pediatric populations in Japan and Taiwan revealed 4 distinct sensitization profiles, with house dust mite-dominant patterns varying dramatically across regions (50% in Taiwan vs. 6.9% in Japan).57 Similarly, recent CRD studies across the Asia-Pacific region have confirmed that sensitization patterns to major HDM components (Der p 1, Der p 2, and Der p 23) differ significantly between Asian and European populations.58 These differences generate the hypothesis that geographically customized AIT formulations could improve treatment outcomes. Specifically, it is hypothesized that incorporating Der p 23 into vaccines for European populations might improve efficacy given its association with asthma severity (OR = 2.5),11,51 while maintaining Der p 1/2 dominance in Asian formulations could align better with local sensitization patterns.12 However, these hypotheses require direct testing through multinational randomized controlled trials comparing component-guided versus extract-based AIT formulations in geographically distinct populations. Beyond component selection, CRD enables dynamic monitoring of sIgG4/sIgE ratios as a predictive biomarker for a sustained immunotherapy response (AUC = 0.76, p = 0.02),50 further personalizing treatment duration and follow-up.

Clinical and practical implications: an expanded CRD-guided, stepped care framework

Based on the synthesized evidence, we outline an expanded CRD-guided, stepped-care algorithm (Fig. 3) as a conceptual model for organizing existing evidence across the spectrum of pediatric HDM allergic diseases. This algorithm is intended to facilitate hypothesis generation and guide future research, rather than to serve as a validated clinical decision tool. This framework integrates 4 novel dimensions: (1) disease-specific pathways, (2) age-stratified considerations, (3) comorbidity management, and (4) escalation strategies for treatment failure.40,43 Importantly, the recommendations in this framework are derived from 2 distinct categories of evidence: (a) those directly supported by HDM-specific studies (eg, CRD-guided SLIT/SCIT selection in HDM-allergic respiratory disease), and (b) those extrapolated from broader atopic disease literature (eg, dupilumab for AD, management of EoE), where direct HDM evidence is limited. Throughout this section, we explicitly distinguish between these 2 categories to ensure transparency regarding the evidence base for each recommendation.

Expanded stepped-care algorithm for managing HDM-related allergic diseases in children. This comprehensive clinical decision pathway integrates (1) disease-specific pathways for isolated respiratory allergy (rhinitis, asthma), isolated atopic dermatitis, and combined presentations; (2) age-stratified considerations for infants/toddlers (0–4 years), school-aged children (5–11 years), and adolescents (12–18 years); (3) comorbidity management for concurrent conditions, including asthma + rhinitis, AD + respiratory allergy, and eosinophilic oesophagitis; (4) molecular stratification using CRD to identify high-risk biomarkers (Der p 23, OR = 2.5 for asthma severity) and predict differential response to SLIT vs. SCIT;12,35,37 (5) geographic customization—incorporating regional sensitization patterns (Der p 1 predominance in Asia vs. Der p 23 in Europe) as a hypothesis-generating consideration for future AIT formulation research;11,12,39 (6) resource-stratified pathways for low-, middle-, and high-income settings;7,59,60 and (7) escalation pathways for treatment failure, including step-up from SLIT to SCIT, addition of biologics, switching between biologics, and discontinuation considerations.61, 62, 63, 64 The algorithm is proposed as a hypothesis-generating framework and requires prospective validation before clinical application. SLIT is recommended for mild, monosensitized cases, whereas SCIT or biologics (omalizumab, dupilumab) are indicated for severe or polysensitized patients, particularly those with high sIgE levels (≥25 kU/L) or sensitization to high-risk components such as Der p 23. Age-appropriate shared decision-making incorporating caregiver preferences,56 needle fear,57 and developmental considerations58 is essential throughout, and cost-effectiveness and accessibility should be considered in resource-limited settings. Patient-centred shared decision-making

Patient and caregiver preferences are critical: 84% of caregivers prefer SLIT over injections, which is correlated with needle fear,15 and adolescents require autonomy-supporting discussions.17,68 These factors should guide choices in addition to clinical efficacy.15, 16, 17

Distinguishing established evidence from hypotheses

It is important to distinguish between findings that are supported by direct clinical evidence and those that remain hypotheses requiring validation. The following observations are supported by available evidence: (1) geographic variations in HDM component sensitization patterns exist;10,51 (2) Der p 23 sensitization is associated with asthma severity (OR = 2.5);11 and (3) CRD can identify patients who are monosensitized versus polysensitized, which correlates with differential SLIT/SCIT responses.47,48 However, the proposal that regionally tailored AIT formulations—ie, adjusting vaccine component composition based on regional sensitization patterns—would improve clinical outcomes remains a hypothesis. This hypothesis is biologically plausible and epidemiologically grounded, but it has not been tested in prospective randomized controlled trials. Similarly, the recommendation to incorporate Der p 23 into European formulations is a hypothesis-generating suggestion that requires direct validation through multinational comparative effectiveness studies before it can be translated into clinical practice or regulatory recommendations.

Future research directions

Building on the innovative framework proposed in this review, future research should prioritize (1) validation of CRD-guided algorithms through multinational RCTs that compare component-based versus extract-based AIT selection in diverse pediatric populations;6 (2) development of predictive biomarkers beyond sIgE, including sIgG4/sIgE dynamics and emerging type-2 inflammatory markers, to guide treatment duration and response monitoring;40,53 (3) long-term safety surveillance of biologics in children, particularly for rare adverse events and impact on immune development, as highlighted by recent systematic reviews;7,40 (4) implementation science studies evaluating the real-world effectiveness of resource-stratified algorithms across low-, middle-, and high-income settings;62 and (5) patient-centred outcome research incorporating shared decision-making tools that integrate caregiver preferences, child development considerations, and quality-of-life measures into routine care.15, 16, 17,39 Additionally, efforts to map sensitization patterns in underrepresented regions (eg, Africa and the Middle East) are critical to advancing global equity in DMA management.62 Health disparities in allergic diseases, including limited access to CRD and biologic therapies, remain a critical global health challenge, as highlighted by recent AAAAI reports on underserved populations.54,63

Limitations

This review has several methodological limitations. First, owing to substantial clinical and methodological heterogeneity across the included studies—as detailed in Section 2.4—we were unable to perform a meta-analysis for most outcomes. While we have cited recent meta-analyses for quantitative context where available,5,40 these pooled estimates may not be directly comparable to our synthesized findings, and their conclusions should be interpreted with caution. Second, the predominance of RCTs may overestimate real-world efficacy because of stringent inclusion criteria.64 Third, long-term safety data for biologics in pediatric populations remain limited.40,64 Fourth, evidence from low-income regions is scarce, potentially constraining the generalizability of our findings,62 a concern that reflects broader health disparities in allergic disease management.63 Fifth, while we have expanded the proposed stepped-care framework to address multiple disease presentations, age groups, comorbidities, and escalation pathways, this remains a conceptual model requiring validation through prospective implementation studies. Sixth, while we employed study-design-specific tools for quality assessment (ROBIS for systematic reviews, RoB 2 for RCTs, ROBINS-I for cohort studies, and AGREE II for guidelines), the inherent heterogeneity in the design and reporting quality of the included studies may still affect the overall strength of the evidence synthesized. The use of multiple tools, while methodologically appropriate, introduces complexity in comparing quality ratings across different study types. Seventh, we synthesized evidence from multiple study designs—meta-analyses, RCTs, cohort studies, and guidelines—each with inherently different levels of evidence. While we have attempted to label the evidence strength for each key finding in the Results section, the narrative synthesis does not lend itself to formal GRADE assessment. This heterogeneity in evidence quality may affect the overall certainty of our conclusions, particularly for recommendations based on low-level evidence (eg, cost-effectiveness modeling or observational studies). Readers should therefore interpret the proposed framework with attention to the underlying evidence strength for each component recommendation. Eighth, some recommendations in the proposed stepped-care framework—particularly those involving dupilumab for AD, management of EoE, and age-stratified transition care—are extrapolated from general atopic disease literature or expert consensus rather than being directly supported by HDM-specific studies. While these extrapolations are biologically plausible and clinically relevant, they may not fully account for the unique immunological and clinical characteristics of HDM-allergic pediatric populations. We have explicitly distinguished between direct HDM evidence and extrapolated evidence in Section 4.3 and summarized these categories to promote transparent interpretation. Future HDM-specific studies are needed to validate these extrapolated recommendations. The recommendations for less common scenarios (eg, EoE with HDM sensitization) are based on limited evidence and expert consensus, highlighting priorities for future research.72,73 Additionally, the algorithm's applicability in low-resource settings may be constrained by limited access to CRD testing and biologic therapies; we have attempted to address this through resource-stratified recommendations, but real-world feasibility studies are needed.43,62 Finally, the proposed stepped-care framework, while based on the synthesized evidence, remains a conceptual model requiring validation through prospective clinical studies, health services research, and implementation science in diverse socioeconomic settings.68,64 Future real-world studies and prospective pediatric cohorts are needed to address these gaps.

Conclusion

This systematic review synthesizes a decade of evidence on pediatric house dust mite allergy management and presents a preliminary CRD-guided, stepped-care framework as a conceptual model for integrating molecular diagnostics, geographic variability, and resource-stratified care. The framework proposes that SLIT may serve as a first-line option for mild or monosensitized patients, whereas SCIT or biologics may be considered for severe or polysensitized cases.

Key evidence-based observations include: (1) Der p 23 sensitization is associated with asthma severity (OR = 2.5); (2) geographic sensitization patterns—Der p 1 predominance in Asia versus Der p 23 in Europe—raise the hypothesis that regionally tailored AIT could improve outcomes, but this requires prospective validation; and (3) CRD enables identification of monosensitized versus polysensitized patients, informing differential SLIT/SCIT selection.

Prospective validation of this conceptual framework in diverse clinical and socioeconomic settings is essential before it can inform clinical practice or health policy. Future research should prioritize multinational implementation studies, real-world cost-effectiveness analyses, and the development of internationally harmonized, resource-sensitive guidelines incorporating regional CRD data.
