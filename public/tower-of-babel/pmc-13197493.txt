Thermal radiation and viscous dissipation impact on 2D MHD Williamson ternary hybrid nanofluid flow over a stretching surface
Authors: Ali Rehman, Mustafa Inc, Abdullah Aziz Saad, Siti Sabariah Abas, K. Sudarmozhi, Najiyah Safwa Khashi’ie
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
In this research, the impact of thermal effects, viscous energy loss, and magnetic-field interaction on a Williamson ternary hybrid nanofluid (Ag, SWCNT, MWCNT) that simulates blood flow (in 2D) over an elastically moving surface is assessed. An analytical technique is developed to provide a framework for enhancing convective heat transfer and reducing streamwise resistance in high-energy systems. Obtaining semi-analytical solutions to the governing nonlinear partial differential equations within the BVPh 1.0 and BVPh 2.0 packages for Mathematica involves transforming them into ordinary differential equations via special similarity variables, applying the homotopy analysis approximating method, and achieving residuals below 10−5 in fewer than 20 steps. The analysis reveals that the resultant average heat transfer (Nu) is over 21% due to the surface cooling heat flux, the thickening of the opaque thermal layer from thermal effects, the extension of the Eckert number, the nanofluid volumetric concentration, and the magnetic Williamson number (slowing rates). And accurately including these ternary hybrid nanofluids in biomedical wearables, blood cooling, polymer extrusion cooling, and highly oriented micro- and electronic heat exchangers for efficient simultaneous temperature and shear stress, is revealing.

Introduction

In engineering and physical science fields, basic flows in two-dimensional (2D) MHD models are crucial. Velocity components change in increments across stretching or moving surfaces. For conducting fluids, applying a magnetic field and utilizing Lorentz forces are techniques that control and suppress turbulence, regulate flow rate, and enhance heat transfer. The Williamson model describes the shear-thinning non-Newtonian behavior of a fluid; therefore, MHD models become significant in accurately describing industrial and biological flows. With THNFs, consisting of a base fluid and three types of nanoparticles, friction is further reduced, and thermal conductivity is enhanced compared to mono- or binary nanofluids. As a result, the 2D MHD Williamson THNF is a complex yet pertinent model for nonlinear interactions in advanced thermal systems.

The branch of physics that studies the behavior of electrically conducting fluids is Magnetohydrodynamics (MHD). In MHD, Lorentz forces change the flow, stabilizing and diminishing turbulence. MHD principles are harnessed in electromagnetic pumps, plasma containment, rapid cooling in metallurgy, flow control in biomedicine, and magnetic targeting of drugs. The Lorentz force acts as an electromagnetic brake and can be used with blood and blood-suspended conductive nanofluids to control flow and optimize heat transfer. The MHD, when applied to heat transfer, enables engineering and biomedical systems with precise control of boundary-layer thickness and temperature. On this basis, variations in MHD flows and HNFs have been examined by several researchers, to whom the hybrid MHD flow literature still refers, unfortunately. The bulk of the literature, and therefore the current attempts to describe the gap, are based on single-phase nanofluids, rudimentary radiation models, or disregard viscous dissipation, non-Newtonian behavior, and their interactions. The following sections outline the most significant contributions to the field and highlight the most critical remaining gaps. Initial studies in nanofluid bioconvection, with an emphasis on gyrotactic microorganisms, have demonstrated increased fluid stability and improved heat transfer efficiency. Mabood et al. [1] studied the HNF flow of Cu–Al2O3–H2O with nonlinear thermal radiation and melting heat transfer. While the work presented was thorough, the assumption of constant nanoparticle properties significantly diminishes the study’s real-world applicability under dynamic heating conditions. Khan et al. [2] built on this work by studying the MHD flow of nanofluids with gyrotactic microorganisms through a vertical plate. However, the lack of consideration of non-Newtonian flow made the results less broadly applicable. Acharya et al. [3] also investigated the effect of solar radiation on nanofluid bioconvection. Although the study presented reasonable findings, the simplistic radiation models adopted limited the results’ real-world applicability. Shaw et al. [4] investigated the effect of a magnetic field with viscous dissipation in porous media while neglecting hybrid nanoparticles, thus underestimating the enhancement in conductivity. In the lack of radiation and shear-thinning viscosity, Aziz et al. [5] analyzed free convection in porous media with gyrotactic organisms.

While the importance of gyrotactic microorganisms and bioconvection in MHD nanofluids is evident in the literature, there is also a lack of ternary hybrids, shear-thinning viscosity, and coupled radiation–dissipation. This necessitates the development of more generalized models. This, in turn, justifies the incorporation of studies focused on activation energy and chemical reactions with complex nanofluid systems. The effects of chemical reactions and activation energy in MHD nanofluids have been studied. Khan et al. [6] studied binary chemical reactions in 3D cross-nanofluid flows with radiative transfer, but they ignored the effects of the magnetic field. Hayat et al. [7] studied the Arrhenius activation energy in 3D nanofluid flow over a Darcy-Forchheimer-rotating basin, framing their analysis around a single rotating nanoparticle. Dawar et al. [8] presented a Williamson nanofluid model with activation energy over a nonlinear stretching plate, thereby expanding the non-Newtonian modeling approach, but did not consider viscous dissipation or nanoparticle interactions. Hayat et al. [9] studied third-grade nanofluids and activation energy; however, the theoretical model was so idealized that its practical application was highly limited. Tayebi et al. [10] investigated HNFs in wavy cylinders for natural convection, but did not incorporate a non-Newtonian model.

Nanofluids are specifically engineered thermal fluids that contain nanoparticles (1–100 nm) suspended in a primary fluid, such as water, oil, or blood, to increase their effective thermal conductivity and heat capacity. Pure fluids have poor thermal performance, but integrating nanoparticles of metals (such as Ag, Cu) or oxides (such as Al2O3, TiO2) improves heat transfer. Their high heat-transfer capacity in small-volume systems makes them ideal for micro-channel cooling, solar heat USB collectors, and other biomedical applications. Nevertheless, their efficiency is often hindered by sedimentation and agglomeration, which is why hybrid and ternary hybrid nanofluids have been constructed. These studies demonstrate the growing effort to incorporate reaction kinetics and nonlinear boundary effects into the flow. Still, the order of the nanoparticle systems, the lack of combined radiation with dissipation effects, and the flow systems in the studies motivate the proposal of research in unsteady flows with more realistic time-dependent systems. The study of unsteady flows has gained attention for its relevance in both industry and biology. Hayat et al. [11] investigated couple-stress fluid flows with chemical reactions, but their study was incomplete regarding enhanced thermal transport because they did not incorporate hybridized nanoparticles. Hayat et al. [12] studied unsteady stretching flows with simultaneous heat and mass transfer, yet did not include activation energy and viscous dissipation. Ishaq et al. [13] considered unsteady permeable stretching surfaces with a prescribed wall temperature, although the constant-viscosity hypothesis introduced significant limitations. Sharidan et al. [14] focused on similarity solutions for boundary-layer unsteady flows; however, the use of linear stretching laws significantly reduced the potential for generalization. Ganga et al. [15] introduced a more realistic perspective by incorporating viscous dissipation, ohmic heating, and heat generation/absorption in MHD nanofluid flow; however, the model still did not account for ternary nanoparticle interactions.

Overall, these studies have made significant contributions to the understanding of time-dependent flows. However, the use of single-phase or binary nanofluids, as well as the absence of Williamson shear-thinning, suggests the need to incorporate models of elasticity, radiation, and mixed convection. Elastic and radiative contexts have also been investigated for heat transfer on stretching surfaces. Nandeppanavar et al. [16] studied Walter’s liquid model with elastic deformation, but did not account for radiation. Hayat et al. [17] analyzed inclined stretching cylinders with radiation and mixed convection but did not consider HNFs. Asghar et al. [18] analyzed Joule heating and slip in HNFs across shrinking sheets; however, the boundary constraints in their work limited the broader applicability. Nadeem et al. [19] analyzed HNF flow over a curved surface, but did not consider the non-Newtonian case. Time-dependent viscosity flow with radiation was studied by Hussain et al. [20]; however, no consideration was given to hybrid nanoparticles. Pattnaik et al. [21] performed EMHD sensitivity analyses in ferrite–water hybrids; however, the assumption of unstable flow severely limited practical applicability.

Ternary hybrid nanofluids (THNFs) are an extension of the hybrid nanofluids. They consist of the same three types of nanofluids. The purpose of these three nanofluid types is to optimize their synergistic interactions. The current research disperses silver (Ag), single-walled carbon nanotubes (SWCNT), and multi-walled carbon nanotubes (MWCNT) into the base fluid, blood, to form a THNF with high conductivity and rheological stability. The silver provides high thermal conductivity, and the CNTs (i.e., SWCNTs and MWCNTs) enhance the structure’s stability, elasticity, and thermal energy diffusion. Overall, nanofluids are better at heat transfer than mono- or binary nanofluids. The THNFs are perfect for implementing thermal management in polymer extrusion cooling, magnetic hyperthermia, and micro heat exchangers. Although these studies have enriched the modelling of radiation, Joule heating, and mixed convection, the major limitation of all of them is that, except for Nadeem, they did not incorporate ternary hybridization with Williamson rheology, which is critical for the combined use of synergistic rheological and realistic rheological aspects coupled with realistic flow friction. The research work of Makhdum and Alghoul, as well as that of Baithalu et al. [22], studied models of CNT-enabled nanofluids and then extended previous work on blood flow over spinning discs. Baithalu models for blood flow over spinning discs, while Baithalu and Makhdum incorporate Darcy-Forchheimer drag. Ratha et al. [23] studied the Yamada-Ota and Hamilton-Crosser models for nanofluid flow, focusing on the influence of particle shape while neglecting magnetic effects. Vanitha et al. [24] studied the Marangoni-driven flow with radiation in a porous sheet, while ignoring the impact of ternary nanoparticles. Ontela et al. [25] improved nanofluid hybrid optimization and heat transfer by utilizing sensitivity analysis, which had previously been limited to binary formulations. Mabood and Alghoul also studied Cu–Al2O3–H2O HNF flows with irreversibility analysis, but did not incorporate the analysis of rheological non-Newtonian effects.

Mehta and Senthilvadivu [26] used an artificial neural network architecture to numerically analyze the heat and mass transfer characteristics of an MHD Carreau ternary hybrid nanofluid flow over a vertical cylinder, demonstrating the capability of neural networks to predict complex thermal phenomena. This study, however, is limited to numerical computing and, without experimental or analytical comparison, greatly diminishes the proposed model’s potential for general application. Choudhary et al. [27] analyzed thermally stratified MHD Carreau ternary hybrid nanofluid flow over an inclined vertical cylinder under radiation, stratifying the study by inclined angle and nanofluid heat transfer enhancement. However, the analysis did not consider nanoparticle agglomeration, and the effect of nanoparticle stability on practical heat transfer performance may be significant. Choudhary et al. [28] numerically investigated MHD heat transfer within a Casson hybrid nanofluid flow past an inclined moving plate with radiation, with a focus on the phenomenon of thermal enhancement, constituted within the rheology of a non-Newtonian fluid, with the strengthening of similar fields. The study provides extensive knowledge of the phenomena. However, the assumption of constant thermophysical properties used in the study may be a significant simplification for practical applications, especially for industrial or biomedical applications.

Choudhary et al. [29] gathered considerable data on MHD Williamson hybrid nanofluid flow and its Soret and Dufour effects. They documented the mechanisms of mass and heat transport. Their developments, however, did not address any mechanical/viscous relaxations or reactions, making their work less relevant to chemistries and applications in high-shear scenarios. Choudhary et al. [30] presented a computational study of the classical bioconvective MHD flow of a Casson hybrid nanofluid around a slender needle. They introduced improvements to account for the Brownian and thermophoretic effects. This work did not introduce variations in microbial motility or provide experimental validation, and remains of limited applicability to engineering biosystems. Choudhary et al. [31] compared the thermal effects of ternary nanofluids based on kerosene oil on a bidirectionally stretching sheet. They discovered that a hybrid CNT-based nanofluid showed a significant, commercially important enhancement in heat transfer rate. They did not, however, consider the cost of the nanoparticles, their long-term stability, and the sedimentation, aspects of considerable importance in a practical energy system.

Chandel and Sharma [32] did a thorough job analyzing the interaction of flow nanofluids with stagnation points, including their work on the interaction with an inclined Mag field and an exponential heating field, and its stagnation point. They attempted to frame their work in the context of research on advanced computer and aerospace systems. Still, the results were framed within a laminar regime, making the applicability to high Reynolds numbers void. Sharma and Sood [33] considered the effects of an inclined magnetic field on the flow of Williamson nanofluid over an exponentially stretching surface situated in a Darcy-Forchheimer porous medium. They showed the case of magnetic control of velocity and heat transfer. However, the model does not account for thermal radiation and nanoparticle shape, which could be relevant at higher temperatures. Thakur et al. [34] studied the case of mixed convection of a tri-hybrid nanofluid over a stretching sheet of variable thickness with Newtonian heating. They presented a case of thermal transport enhancement due to tri-nanoparticle interactions. The work, however, assumes uniform dispersion of the nanoparticles and therefore ignores the clustering effects.

In Prasad et al. [35], the combined effect of thermal radiation and viscous dissipation on the flow of a ferrofluid over a non-flat stretching sheet in a porous medium was studied, thereby emphasizing the energy dissipation mechanism. However, the non-Newtonian case is not considered in the study, which limits the work to simpler fluid models. Sharma et al. [36] studied the flow of a Williamson-micropolar ternary nanofluid over a porous rotatory surface, conducted numerically, and concluded the importance of micro-rotation on momentum and heat transfer. However, the mass transfer analysis was not included, which limits the analysis of transport phenomena to some extent. Sharma and Chandel [37] conducted a comparative study of different flows involving non-Newtonian fluids over an exponentially stretching sheet, which serves as a convective boundary, providing a thorough understanding of the rheological effects. However, the absence of magnetic and radiative effects is also a drawback of the study, which are fundamental for MHD-concerned heating systems.

Several industrial and biological fluids, such as blood, lubricants, and some polymers, exhibit non-Newtonian behavior that Newtonian parameters cannot adequately represent. The Williamson fluid model captures shear-thinning viscosity and some elastic response and closely corresponds to the behavior of blood and polymer melts. The inclusion of Williamson rheology makes it possible to assess the interaction of elastic response and viscosity reduction with shear and the fluid’s magnetic and radiative effects in MHD-augmented hybrid nanoflows. This interaction becomes particularly essential in the construction of biomedical apparatus, polymer manufacturing, and advanced thermal cooling systems. There, the systems are subjected to intricate rheology, and their behavior is maintained at elevated thermal performance.

Sharma and Sood [38] studied the effects of radiation and slip on MHD nanofluid flow over an exponentially stretching surface and found that boundary slip is more important at the micro level. However, even though it is a preprint, the absence of peer-reviewed and experimental validation is a limitation. In [39], Basha studied the Soret and Dufour effects in the flow of a 3D MHD Jeffrey nanofluid, investigating the transport of heat and mass and providing the mass transport in the system with the coupled mechanism of mass flow transport. However, the steady-state condition assumption, which restricts the study to systems in a transient thermal state, is a considerable drawback. In [40], Prasad et al. studied the 3D mixed convection flow of an Oldroyd-B nanofluid over a slippery stretching surface, highlighting the effects of viscoelasticity on heat transfer. However, a significant drawback of this study is the lack of consideration of thermal radiation. Nanoparticle perturbations may be responsible for higher heat transfer rates, thereby significantly influencing heat transfer.

Basha [41] acknowledged the importance of incorporating variable fluid properties to improve the accuracy of thermal modeling in 3D MHD Maxwell nanofluid flow with chemical reactions. However, the variable properties formulation is complex, increasing the computational effort required and reducing its tractability. Basha [42] examined the effects of electromagnetic forces on the thermal control of convectively heated Riga plates with Soret–Dufour and radiation effects. Although this is a significant contribution, the research is strictly numerical, and there is no evidence that the study could be validated through an experimental approach. Basha and Ramanjini [43] compared the thermal effects of Al2O3 and Cu nanoparticles in MHD flow over an inclined plate, with Cu nanoparticles exhibiting greater thermal enhancement and thus being more favorable from a thermal perspective. However, the study lacks stability and oxidation resistance of the nanoparticles, which are essential for long-term application.

Upon reviewing the literature, it is evident that while significant innovations have been made in the field of hybrid and non-Newtonian nanofluid flows, fundamental gaps remain. For example, constant thermophysical properties, simplified boundary conditions (BCs), and the combined effects of radiation, viscous dissipation, and ternary hybridization are frequently overlooked in existing scholarship. Williamson THNF models that incorporate realistic, nonlinear conditions are scarce. This study makes a significant contribution by providing a mathematical model of the 2D MHD flow of a Williamson THNF over a stretching surface that accounts for thermal radiation and viscous dissipation. For the first time, the governing nonlinear equations of this complex thermofluidic flow system have been solved exactly analytically using the BVPh 1.0 and BVPh 2.0 tools in Mathematica. This work accounts for the intricate dynamics of hybrid nanoparticle flow with non-Newtonian characteristics and joint thermal and flow processes, thereby presenting a model of significant practical relevance to the field of thermal engineering.

Research shows significant gaps in modeling THNF flows that incorporate non-Newtonian Williamson rheology and account for thermal radiation, viscous dissipation, and MHD. Most research works deal with single-phase or binary nanofluids, use simplified radiation models, assume constant thermophysical properties, or do not account for meaningful interactions such as Lorentz forces and shear-thinning viscosity; thus, their work is not relevant to real applications in thermal systems (see Refs. [44–48]). No research to date has thoroughly examined the coupling of Ag-SWCNT-MWCNT THNF in blood with Williamson fluid properties, dissipation, radiation, magnetic fields, and MHD in the 2D boundary-layer flow. This research is the first to develop and fully solve a mathematical model of 2D MHD Williamson THNF flow over a stretching surface, solving the problem analytically using, for the first time, the thermal package BVPh 1.0 and BVPh 2.0, with residuals converging to below 10−5 in 20 iterations. Here, the synergy is quantified; for example, a 15% decrease in velocity is attributed to magnetic fields, and a 21% increase in the Nusselt number is attributed to THNF. Since the problem has significant applications in biomedical cooling, polymer supersession, and thermal control of microelectronics, the results should contribute considerably to the design of such systems.

The thermo-physical properties of single-walled carbon nanotube, multi-walled carbon nanotube, silver and blood

Determination of application and nanoparticle selection

The focus of this study is on thermal management for biomedical devices (magnetic drug delivery, blood-compatible cooling), polymer extrusion processes, microelectronics heat exchangers, and energy storage systems, where increased thermal conductivity and controlled rheology enhance heat dissipation, and flow resistance is regulated by magnetic fields. Study authors selected Ag (silver) for its unmatched thermal conductivity (429 W/m K) and SWCNT (single-walled carbon nanotubes) for its unparalleled strength and conductivity (density of ~ 6600 kg/m3 and high aspect ratio). In contrast, for MWCNT (multi-walled), it is chosen due to its economical reinforcement and the stability it brings to blood-based fluids, thus forming a synergistic ternary hybrid nanofluid (THNF) with 21% increase in Nusselt number than the nanofluids where the MWCNTs were the only active nanoparticles. These selected engineered nanoparticles enable biomedical applications such as Ag-MHD heating for targeted hyperthermia, blood substitutes with CNT elasticity for reinforcement, and high-conductivity combinations for cooling electronics.

Importance of blood as baseline fluid for study

For this study, blood is used as a biochemically realistic base fluid, providing a foundation for applicability to magnetic drug targeting, the design of artificial organs, and blood-compatible thermal therapies, in which THNFs simulate enhanced blood flow and heat transfer under MHD control. The blood’s non-Newtonian and shear-thinning rheology, as described by the Williamson parameter, is a natural fit for the focus of this study. Furthermore, while blood has low thermal conductivity (0.492 W/m K), improvements from the Ag-SWCNT-MWCNT nanoparticles are transferable to and necessary for hyperthermic cancer treatment and the cooling of cardiovascular devices. The blood’s physiological electrical conductivity and its response to a transverse magnetic field (reducing velocity by about 15% due to the Lorentz force) mimic the actual magnetic drug delivery system, in which nanoparticles are controlled and aggregated to targeted tumors. To this end, water as a blood substitute would ignore the biomedical importance. Blood ultimately connects theoretical THNF modeling to bioheat transfer applications, such as blood pump heat exchangers and stent cooling.

Methodology

The series solution is a practical approach to tackling nonlinear problems commonly encountered in science and engineering. A convenient and contemporary method for resolving coupled nonlinear equations is the HAM. The goal of the latest HAM-based software tools, BVPh 1.0 and BVPh 2.0, is to enhance mathematical model convergence. BVPh 2.0 has the quickest convergence. The first solution is obtained using the subsequent method. This methodology uses similarity transformations to convert the governing NLPDEs for momentum and energy transport into NLODEs, and then into boundary layers. The non-Newtonian shear-thinning effects are modeled using the Williamson differential model, while the magnetic field and thermal radiation are incorporated into the flow equations. The complex nonlinear ordinary differential equations are solved by the semi-analytical HAM using the BVPh 1.0 and BVPh 2.0 Mathematica packages. The solution converges by minimizing the residual error, yielding error norms below 10 − 5 within 20 iterations; thus, the solution is converged, reliable, and computationally efficient.

To begin, the two-dimensional steady, incompressible, and MHD flow and energy equations are derived for a Williamson ternary hybrid nanofluid resembling blood that contains Ag, SWCNT, and MWCNT nanoparticles over a stretching surface in a transverse magnetic field, including thermal radiation and viscous dissipation in the energy equation. Mass and energy equations are formulated in the Cartesian coordinate system as boundary-layer flow equations, expressed as mass, momentum, and energy equations, with appropriate no-slip and wall boundary conditions and free-stream ambient conditions on the surface. No slip and constant temperature are suitable conditions for the wall. No-slip and constant-temperature boundary conditions are imposed. Then, the transformed equations are (re-formulated) into a set of nonlinear ordinary differential equations where the similarity variable for the boundary layer problem is posed in such a way as to be reduced to a one-dimensional ordinary differential equation. These nonlinear ordinary differential equations are solved semi-analytically using the homotopy analysis method and the BVPh 1.0 and BVPh 2.0 packages in Mathematica, in which the approximations, linear, and convergence-control parameters are appropriately chosen to build a rapidly convergent parameter system that controls the restrictive convergence of the solution for the construction of the series.

Convergence is tracked by reducing the residual error norms from the transformed equations. Results and discussion

The impact of the MHD parameter on the Williamson THNF velocity profiles is illustrated in Fig. 2. When a static magnetic field is applied to the fluid mixture, it interacts with the conducting nanofluid, generating Lorentz forces that oppose the nanofluid’s motion. This magnetic field creates resistance, dissipating fluid kinetic energy. Thus, a higher magnetic field intensity slows the fluid. Controlled fluid deceleration has medical applications, such as magnetic drug targeting, where low fluid velocity enables precise drug localization in tissues. It also has applications in the detection of electronic devices, where the flow rate of magnetic field coolant is adjusted during cooling to enhance heat removal and control flow to electronic devices. The parameters were chosen based on the design of the experiment, with consideration given to the physic-chemical properties of blood, THNF experimental stability limits, as well as the engineering applications. This ensured accurate physics and numerical convergence (with residuals less than 10−5 in fewer than 20 BVPh iterations).

Velocity profile with different MHD parameter

The velocity field images in Fig. 3 are a result of changing fluid material properties based on the concentration of the volume fraction of the fluid with embedded solid particles. Increasing the volume fraction of solid particles in the fluid increases the liquid’s overall viscosity due to increased particle-fluid and particle-particle interactions. This results in an increase in flow resistance and a decrease in axial velocity. However, the increase in the surface area of solid particles enhances conductive heat transfer, facilitating heat absorption. This is particularly applicable in microfluidic heat exchangers, where the required flow rate for efficient thermal management is low, necessitating significant resistance to flow. This is commonly used in biomedical diagnostics and lab-on-chip technologies.

The effects of MHD forces on the momentum transport of Williamson’s ternary hybrid nanofluid are key to what flow features are demonstrated. When a transverse magnetic field is imposed, the blood-based conducting nanofluid experiences a frictional magnetic drag, known as the Lorentz force. This force, which opposes the motion of the fluid, adds a drag resistance to the fluid, which, along with the conversion of kinetic energy to heat due to Joule heating, results in a conversion of part of the kinetic energy flowing along the fluid stream into thermal energy. The Lorentz force alters the flow of blood along the stream. The Lorentz force alters the distribution of the blood flow within the stream. This alters the friction along the stream. This blood flow alteration causes a reduction. The increased magnetic parameter leads to a more pronounced velocity reduction, which explains the observed velocity profile suppression.

The integration of the Lorentz force and the non-Newtonian shear-thinning behavior of the Williamson model produces opposing effects: the magnetic field flowing against the Lorentz force sheath and motion is damped, while shear-thinning viscosity facilitates the motion of the flow with an easier pathway at greater shear rates. The equilibrium of these flow disruptions leads to the dissipation of internal thermal energy within the boundary layer. The flow suppression/velocity reduction due to MHD is a positive aspect in biomedical and industrial flow control applications, such as magnetic drug targeting, cooling of extruded polymers, and controlled electromagnetic dampers, as it significantly improves control safety and efficiency. The graphical data in the field suggest an increase in magnetic field strength, which, in turn, results in a 15% reduction in flow velocity.

Figure 4 depicts how the Williamson fluid parameter affects velocity, considering the non-Newtonian shear-thinning and elastic characteristics of the fluid. The parameter’s increase correlates with an increase in elastic stresses and internal friction. Flow velocity is diminished, even with shear-thinning fluids whose viscosity decreases in high shear rates. This rheological behavior is observed in industrial polymer extrusion, where the fluid’s elastic and viscous properties are maintained to achieve controlled flow. This is crucial in defect-free polymer extrusion, which provides homogeneous material properties. The parameter’s elasticity control aids in the processing of complex fluids such as paints and cosmeceuticals. It is also applicable in the processing of biological fluids, such as suspensions.

Temperature profile with different thermal radiation parameter

Figure 5 illustrates the impact of thermal radiation on temperature profiles. The increase in the fluid’s radiation parameter is associated with an increase in internal energy due to absorption of additional radiative heat. This creates thicker thermal boundary layers. The increase in heat transfer and the radiation heat is critical to the optimization of solar thermal collectors. In aerospace engineering, the controlled cavitation of radiative heat shields is crucial in the development of thermal protection systems for spacecraft, especially during atmospheric re-entry.

Figure 6 illustrates the influence of the Eckert number on the temperature of the fluid. This phenomenon occurs as a result of the viscous dissipation of the fluid’s kinetic energy and heat. As the Eckert number increases, the temperature rise increases, and the temperature difference (temperature gradient) decreases, particularly at the fluid’s surface. The self-heating of a fluid, especially one with high viscosity, can lead to thermal degradation or thermal runaway, making its control especially crucial in chemical reactors and lubrication systems. This understanding enables engineers to devise optimal techniques and materials for operating within the desired temperature ranges in high-viscosity or high-shear flow systems. As described in Fig. 7, in the presence of an MHD magnet, the parameter ‘M’ causes the fluid’s temperature to rise even further as kinetic energy is converted to heat and flow is decelerated. This accumulated heat causes the thermal boundary layer to thicken, increasing the thermal resistance to flow. Peak localized heating in the described regime is a desirable phenomenon in plasma devices and magnetic refrigeration systems, where inefficient heating must be mitigated to maintain a sufficiently cooled state.

The streamline patterns depicted in Fig. 10 illustrate the flow trajectories and boundary-layer structures influenced by the synergistic effects of magnetic, viscous, and nanoparticle forces. Changes in the curvature of the streamlines provide insight into the distribution of shear and the degree of flow uniformity, factors that are fundamental to optimizing mixing in chemical reactors and avoiding dead zones that may lead to clotting in biomedical devices. The characterization of such flow phenomena enables accurate engineering of systems in which fluid-flow control is essential for optimal operation and for mitigating hazards. The drop in velocity as the magnetic field strength increases can be explained by the Lorentz force, which acts as an electromagnetic drag on the electrically conducting THNF. This force transforms the fluid’s kinetic energy into drag energy that retards its motion. At the same time, the mechanical energy dissipated due to internal friction is converted into heat through viscous dissipation, raising the fluid temperature, and the thermal boundary layer thickens. The addition of multiwalled and single-walled carbon nanotubes, along with silver nanoparticles, forms highly conductive pathways that mitigate flow resistance due to increased fluid viscosity, thereby increasing the rate of heat transfer and thermal conductivity. The Williamson fluid parameter characterizes shear-thinning rheology, and a viscous fluid will experience a reduction in resistance with an increasing shear rate. However, the elastic rheological phenomena will introduce internal resistance and affect the overall velocity. Absorption of thermal radiation increases the fluid temperature and contributes to thermal dispersion, while the fluid’s rheological properties dissipate heat. Combining these electromagnetic and rheological interactions provides a means to finely adjust the fluid’s flow and heat-transfer properties, which is critical in thermal and biomedical engineering for manipulating complex fluids. Conclusions

In this semi-analytical study, the combined effects of thermal radiation and viscous dissipation on a 2D MHD Williamson THNF flow over a stretching surface are investigated. Ag, MWCNT, and SWCNT nanoparticles combine to form a tri-HNF using blood as the base fluid. According to the present research, the combined effects of the magnetic field and non-Newtonian Williamson rheology dramatically change the velocity and temperature distributions in the boundary layer. The findings show that in engineering systems like polymer extrusion, cooling technologies, and energy devices, optimizing thermal transport requires careful adjustment of the radiation and dissipation parameters. The thermal behavior of complex non-Newtonian HNFs under radiative and magneto-hydrodynamic conditions is better understood due to this work.

Limitations of the current work

The current study assumes steady 2D boundary-layer flow under generalized conditions, thereby simplifying the model and omitting several complexities found in practice. Modeling a single-phase Theouin fluid neglects the effects of observed particle Brownian motion, agglomeration, and sedimentation, which could lead to overstated predictions of stability at higher volume fractions. The blood is represented by the Williamson model, which captures shear-thinning behavior. Brownian diffusion of the nanoparticles is a significant factor in underpredicting the heat transfer compared to a complete two-phase model. Validation is based solely on BVPh convergence (residual < 10− 5), and while it is likely the strongest predictor, experimental validation with actual Ag-SWCNT-MWCNT-blood suspensions is essential to demonstrate the model’s practical utility. Future efforts ought to extend to numerical models based on flow multiphysics coupling, e.g., variable properties, 3D, unsteady.

Future work

The extension of the present models to three-dimensional, time-dependent flows will include the effects of nanoparticle Brownian motion, thermophoresis, and shape-dependent drag via two-phase models, enabling realistic simulations of THNF behavior. Benchmarks are predicted heat transfer and stability limits, which will be quantified in future experiments using control, Ag-SWCNT-MWCNT-blood suspensions in MHD fields. Broader impacts on the field of active membranes and the processing of reactive polymers are predicted from the integration of thermally variable porous stretched sheets, suction/injection, and reaction kinetics (activation energy). The identification of suitable parameter ranges using thermodynamic irreversibility, multi-objective optimization, and ANN could guide simulations for biomedical hyperthermia and microelectronics cooling, applying the findings to multi-objective problems. Higher relevance to the design of blood pumps and targeted therapies is expected in the hybrid non-Newtonian models (Casson-Williamson) and gyrotactic bioconvection.

Practical implications

The design recommendations provided to biomedical engineers address modifications to magnetic drug delivery systems. It is possible to target the drug-delivering nanoparticles into tumors precisely because there is a 15% reduction in velocity relative to the control under MHD. For hyperthermia to be efficiently utilized, ternary hybrid nanofluids show a 21% improvement, achieved through an enhancement in the Nusselt number, with no overheating. Defects can be minimized by controlled boundary-layer thickening when polymer manufacturers optimize extrusion cooling by balancing the effects of Williamson rheology and radiation. Microelectronics cooling is also positively impacted. There is superior heat dissipation, moderated by magnetic flow, which is essential for heat control in small devices. Other compact systems can also benefit from the blood-mimicking THNFs in flexible heat exchangers. There is also an improvement in the energy storage systems through the management of the viscous dissipation. The high-Prandtl biofluids that are at risk of thermal runaway are brought under control. The quantified synergies provide a pathway to improve thermal efficiency in the various fields mentioned.
