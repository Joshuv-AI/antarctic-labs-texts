Abstract
. Differential privacy (DP) is considered as the gold standard for data privacy. While the problem of answering simple queries and functions under DP guarantees has been thoroughly addressed in recent years, the problem of releasing multidimensional data under DP remains challenging. In this paper, we focus on this problem, in particular on how to construct privacy-preserving views using a domain decomposition approach. The main idea is to recursively split the domain into sub-domains until a convergence condition is met. The resulting sub-domains are perturbed and then published in order to be used to answer arbitrary queries. Existing methods that have addressed this problem using domain decomposition face two main challenges: (i) efficient privacy budget management over a variable and undefined decomposition depth h h ; and (ii) defining an optimal data-dependent splitting strategy that minimizes the error in the sub-domains while ensuring the smallest possible decomposition. To address these challenges, we present RIPOST , a multidimensional data decomposition algorithm that bypasses the constraint of predefined depth h h and applies a data-aware splitting strategy to optimize the quality of the decomposition results. The core of RIPOST is a two-phase strategy that separates non-empty sub-domains at an early stage from empty sub-domains by exploiting the properties of multidimensional datasets, and then decomposes the resulting sub-domains with minimal inaccuracies using the mean function. Moreover, RIPOST introduces a privacy budget distribution that allows decomposition without requiring prior computation of the depth h h . Through extensive experiments, we demonstrated that RIPOST outperforms state-of-the-art methods in terms of data utility and accuracy on a variety of datasets and test cases 1 1 1 https://github.com/AlaEddineLaouir/RIPOST.git .

2.
Related Works

The problem of data publication has become a major research topic in recent years. The naive solution would be to inject noise into each cell of the
(
Dwork 2006
;
Ding et al. 2011
)
tensor, but this would cause too much perturbation, making the published data inaccurate.
To provide a better alternative, many methods have been developed to publish and query high-dimensional data under DP. We present here the state-of-the-art on this problem.

Partitioning approaches.

Rather than perturbing data points,
(
Zhang et al. 2014
;
Li et al. 2014
)
proposed algorithms that partition data into bins. In
(
Li et al. 2014
)
, they focused on data partitioning based on minimal
aggregation error
, and then applied the
Laplace mechanism
to add noise to the mean value of each bin. Their solution is only effective in low-dimensional/small-domain data, due to the complexity of finding the best partitioning.
Other solutions have proposed a recursive domain decomposition algorithm that uses the quadtree strategy to divide the tensor into smaller blocks
(
Zhang et al. 2016
;
Srivastava et al. [n.d.]
)
.
Partitioning continues until a convergence condition is satisfied. After convergence, blocks will only keep a representative value of the cells, such as the mean (similar to
(
Li et al. 2014
)
), and perturb this value to ensure DP.
One of the main challenges with partitioning is defining a limit or depth, denoted as
h
, to manage the budget distribution. Some solutions
(
Cormode et al. 2012
;
Shaham et al. 2023
;
Kreačić et al. 2023
)
use heuristics to determine an appropriate value for
h
, while
(
Zhang et al. 2016
)
bypasses this constraint altogether, as it can hinder performance and introduce inaccuracies. We will address this challenge more in details in Section
4
.

HDPView
(
Kato et al. 2022
)
can be considered an improvement over
(
Zhang et al. 2016
)
. Instead of using quadtree partitioning, HDPView introduces a data-dependent partitioning strategy, which enhances the quality of the resulting DP view by reducing both the
Aggregation Error (AE)
and DP noise. However, this data-dependent strategy is still constrained by the depth parameter
h
(after which it becomes totally random). While the convergence of
(
Kato et al. 2022
)
is independent of
h
, the partitioning strategy itself is limited by
h
, which reintroduces the issue in part of their solution.

Workload-based approaches.

Some solutions aim to release only a part of the data delimited by a predefined set of queries, also called
Workload
. In
(
Li et al. 2015
)
, they introduced a matrix mechanism (MM) that creates queries and outputs optimized for a given workload. The work in
(
McKenna et al. 2018
)
presents an extension to
(
Li et al. 2015
)
by adapting and optimizing the matrix mechanism to high-dimensional data (HDMM).
In addition, HDMM improves
(
Li et al. 2014
)
, which also falls into this category of solutions.
The main issues with these techniques are: (1) workload dependency, and (2) matrix operations can be computationally costly.
PrivateSQL
(
Kotsogiannis et al. 2019
)
provides a framework that calculates the sensitivity of relational data based on its schema and relationships. It then creates a multidimensional view of these data using methods such as
(
Li et al. 2014
;
Li et al. 2015
)
.
Another solution in this class is
(
Laouir and Imine 2024
)
, which uses random sampling to create a synopsis of the original data and complements it with a dedicated perturbation table to inject noise into each query. This approach is considered a data publishing solution because it perturbs the data upfront, and no further budget is required for individual queries. However, it differs from the previously introduced solutions in that the generated data remains on the server to handle query perturbation, making it a hybrid between data publishing and online query answering. As a result,
(
Laouir and Imine 2024
)
is relevant to our work but not directly comparable.

Private generative models.

Another direction of research in this area is the use of generative models to create synthetic, private versions of the data that can then be published.
In
(
Zhang et al. 2017
)
, the authors trained a Bayesian network under DP guarantees, while others
(
Xu et al. 2017
;
Zhang et al. 2021
;
Qardaji et al. 2014
)
used marginal tables to learn the distribution of the original data.
Given the impressive performance of deep generative models in many research areas,
(
Fan et al. 2020
;
Acs et al. 2018
;
Harder et al. 2021
;
Jordon et al. 2019
;
Takagi et al. 2021
)
proposed models that preserve DP while synthesizing data.
These models, such as
(
Fan et al. 2020
)
(which uses a Generative Adversarial Network, GAN), outperform
(
Zhang et al. 2017
)
only on some classification tasks, but
(
Zhang et al. 2017
)
outperforms GAN-based methods on private tabular data.
The limitations of these approaches are, in general, as follows: (1) The models are not specifically tailored to tensor data structures and OLAP tasks, which is a major drawback compared to partitioning and workload-dependent approaches, and (2) Generative models are very complex and resource-intensive for large-scale data. In scenarios where data is frequently updated, this computational cost becomes very significant.

In our work, we focus on
workload-independent partitioning-based approaches
.
More specifically, we address the
limitations and inefficiencies
of existing methods such as
(
Kato et al. 2022
;
Zhang et al. 2016
;
Cormode et al. 2012
)
which we will present in more detail in Section
4
.
We propose a new partitioning algorithm
RIPOST
, which creates a better quality view in terms of
accuracy
while ensuring
data privacy and utility
.

Differential Privacy (DP).
Differential Privacy is a privacy model that provides formal guarantees of
indistinguishability
, ensuring that query results do not reveal significant information about the presence or absence of any particular individual. Consequently, it hides information about which of the
neighbouring tensors

(
Dwork et al. 2014a
)
was used to answer the query.

Definition 3.0 (Neighbouring Tensors).

Two tensors
T
and
T^{\prime}
are
neighbouring
if we can obtain one of them by incrementing the value of a cell in the other by 1. We denote this distance as
Dist(T,T^{\prime})=1
.

Let
M
be a randomized data publishing algorithm.
M
is

, when it is insensitive to the presence or absence of any individual in
T
.

Definition 3.0 (
\epsilon
-Differential privacy
(
Dwork et al. 2014b
)
).

An algorithm
M
satisfies
\epsilon

(or
\epsilon

) if for any neighbouring tensors
T

T^{\prime}
, and all possible sets of outputs

:

\frac{\mbox{Pr}\left[M\left(T\right)\in S\right]}{\mbox{Pr}\left[M\left(T^{\prime}\right)\in S\right]}\leq e^{\epsilon}

The parameter
\epsilon
is called the
privacy budget
.

It is well known that DP is used to answer specific queries on databases. Let
f
be a query on a tensor
\mathcal{T}
whose its answer
f(\mathcal{T})
returns a number. The
sensitivity
of
f
is the amount by which the output of
f
changes for all neighbouring tensors.

Definition 3.0 (Sensitivity
(
Dwork et al. 2014b
)
).

The sensitivity of a query
f
for
any two neighboring tensors
\mathcal{T}
,
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}
is:

\Delta_{f}=\max_{\mathcal{T},\mathcal{T}^{\prime}}\left\|f(\mathcal{T})-f(\mathcal{T}^{\prime})\right\|_{1}

where
\left\|.\right\|_{1}
is the
L_{1}
norm.

For instance, if
f
is a count range query then
\Delta_{f}
is
1
.

To achieve DP for a query
f
, we need to add random noise to its answer
f(\mathcal{T})
. To inject adequate noise, some fundamental mechanisms have been developed for DP. One of them is the
Laplace Mechanism
.

Definition 3.0 (Laplace Mechanism
(
Dwork et al. 2014b
)
).

The
Laplace mechanism
adds noise to

as:

f(\mathcal{T})+Lap\left(\frac{\Delta_{f}}{\epsilon}\right)

where
\Delta_{f}
is the
sensitivity
of
f
, and
Lap(\alpha)
denotes sampling from the Laplace distribution with center
0
and scale
\alpha
.

For instance, if
f
is a count range query then the noise is sampled from the distribution

.

Unlike the Laplace Mechanism (based on noisy numerical answers), the
Exponential mechanism
allows for selecting the “best” element (according to a scoring function) in a set while preserving DP
(
Dwork et al. 2014b
)
.

Definition 3.0 (Exponential Mechanism
(
Dwork et al. 2014b
)
).

Given a set of values
V
extracted from a tensor
\mathcal{T}
and a real scoring function
u
to select values in
V
. The

randomly samples

from

\exp\left(\frac{\epsilon\times u(\mathcal{T},v)}{2\times\Delta_{u}}\right)

where
\Delta_{u}
is the sensitivity of

.

The Exponential Mechanism satisfies DP by approximately maximizing the score of the value it returns, while sometimes returning a value from the set that does not have the highest score.

Combining several DP mechanisms is possible, and the privacy accounting is managed using the sequential and the parallel composition properties of DP. Let
M_{1},...,M_{n}
be mechanisms satisfying
\epsilon_{1},...,\epsilon_{n}
-DP.

Theorem 3.6 (Sequential Composition
(

)
).

An algorithm sequentially applying

\left(\sum_{j=1}^{n}\epsilon_{j}\right)
-DP.

Theorem 3.7 (Parallel Composition
(
Dwork et al. 2014b
)
).

An algorithm applying
M_{1},\ldots,M_{n}
to
n

in parallel satisfies (
max^{n}_{j=1}\epsilon_{j}
)-DP.

4.
Problem Statement

Given a multidimensional tensor
T
, the spatial decomposition problem involves dividing the domain and cells of
T
into disjoint sub-blocks (sub-domains) such that the cells within each block
b
can be approximated by their mean
\bar{x}
with minimal error. This error, referred to as
Aggregation Error
(
AE
), measures how much the cells deviate from being perfectly uniform
AE=\sum_{c\in b}|\bar{x}-c|
, where
c
is a cell in
b
. Range queries are estimated on these sub-blocks based on their intersections with the blocks. Consequently, minimizing
AE
is essential for maximizing utility.

In order to create such sub-domains (blocks), a decomposition algorithm consists of two main components:

•

A convergence condition (
CC
), which determines whether further partitioning of a domain is necessary.

•

A splitting strategy (
SS
), which defines how a given domain (sub-domain) is divided into smaller sub-domains.

As a result, the decomposition process produces a tree-like structure that serves as an index, enabling faster access to blocks within a given domain.

(without DP) is shown, where:

•

CC
is defined based on the

of the current sub-domain:
AE\leq\theta
, where in this example,
\theta=10
.

•

SS
splits a domain into two sub-domains,
\{\text{left},\text{right}\}
, and chooses the splitting point that minimizes
\{AE(\text{left})+AE(\text{right})\}
.

The illustrative example in Figure

, starts with one dimensional tensor with domaine
[0,10]
. Using
SS
, the process continue to split the domain and cells until the
CC
is satisfied. Notice that the mean is added only for the leaf nodes in Figure

.
For approximating queries, only the leaves are considered. For example, for a query
Q=\{\texttt{Service}\in[7,10]\}
, we need to compute its intersection with each leaf node
ln
in the list of tree leaves
TL
(
ln\in TL
). The approximation is therefor performed as follows:

A(Q)=\sum_{ln\in TL}\left(ln_{\text{dom}}\cap Q_{\text{dom}}\right)\times ln_{\text{mean}}

For this example query, the resulting approximation is
A(Q)=4.8
.

The problem becomes more complex when the decomposition is made
differentially private (DP)
, as each block must be noised, introducing an additional error termed
Perturbation Error
(
PE
). These two errors are inversely related: increasing the number of blocks reduces
AE
but increases
PE
, and vice versa. Therefore, an optimal decomposition strikes a balance between
AE
and
PE
.
The first challenge in creating a DP decomposition algorithm is determining how many times the convergence condition (
CC
) should be tested. Since
CC
is data-dependent, each test consumes a portion of the privacy budget. Moreover, because each branch operates on a disjoint portion of the domain, only the maximum depth of the tree must be predefined to limit and appropriately distribute the budget.

Defining this maximum depth
h
independently of the underlying data
(
Cormode et al. 2012
;
Inan et al. 2010
)
can simplify budget management but does not necessarily guarantee an acceptable decomposition with respect to
AE
and
PE
. On the other hand, using a heuristic
(
Shaham et al. 2023
;
Kreačić et al. 2023
)
before decomposition to determine an appropriate
h
based on the data is not practical for two reasons: (1) the result of the heuristic must be DP, as it relies on the data, and (2) the computational complexity of finding optimal
h
may be comparable to performing the actual decomposition itself, thus they usually approximate
h
vaguely.

The first approach to bypass the dependency on
h
is PrivTree
(
Zhang et al. 2016
)
, which proposed a
Biased Convergence Condition (BCC)
. This condition allows repeated testing while ensuring that the total privacy budget consumption remains fixed and independent of
h
. To better clarify the
BCC
, consider a
CC
defined as
M(t)\leq\theta
, where
M
is any arbitrary metric,
t
is a sub-block, and
\theta
is a threshold. To ensure a DP-safe
CC
,
Laplace
noise with a budget
\epsilon
is added to
M(t)
, resulting in
M^{\prime}(t)
, which is used for testing. Their contribution relies on a key observation regarding budget consumption, particularly when:

•

M(t)\gg\theta
for all intermediate nodes in a path except the leaf, and

•

For any parent/child pair
t_{p}
and
t_{c}
in a path,
M(t_{p})-M(t_{c})\geq c
.

Under these conditions, the actual budget consumption along the path is less than
h/\epsilon
(without releasing the actual value of
M^{\prime}(t)
). They proved that if these two conditions are met, the total budget is constant and equal to
\epsilon
.

On real datasets, the issue arises with the second condition, as it cannot be guaranteed for all possible datasets. To address this, they introduced the
BCC
, defined as follows:

•

\hat{M(t)}=\max\{\theta-\delta,M(t)-\text{depth}(t)\times\delta\}
, where
\delta
is the
bias
term, defined independently of the data. This guarantees that the second condition always holds:
\hat{M(t_{p})}-\hat{M(t_{c})}\geq\delta
. Alternatively,
\hat{M(t)}=\theta-\delta
, which is data-independent and does not require privacy budget.

•

M^{\prime}(t)=\hat{M(t)}+\text{Lap}(\lambda)
, where
\lambda\geq\Phi\cdot\frac{1}{\epsilon}
, where of
\Phi
is based on some parameters (see
(
Zhang et al. 2016
)
for more details). This ensures that the total budget of
CC
is
\epsilon
, regardless of how many times it is repeated.

This proposition indeed eliminates the need to specify a fixed depth at the beginning, but it has a significant disadvantage. The
BCC
incorporates a deterministic, incrementing perturbation (
-\text{depth}(t)\times\delta
) to the metric
M
used in
CC
, in addition to the standard
Laplace noise
. This deterministic perturbation, referred to as the
bias term
in
(
Zhang et al. 2016
)
, is defined independently of the data. Consequently, it may lead to what we call
Early Convergence
, where the decomposition halts before achieving an acceptable balance between
AE
and
PE
.

The second challenge lies in the quality of the splitting strategy (

) used in the decomposition algorithm. The
SS
can be defined as an data independent process; for example, splitting the domain into
2^{|D|}

(

With our proposed solution

, we aim to address both challenges while overcoming the limitations of previous approaches. Leveraging the fact that multidimensional tensors are typically sparse,
RIPOST
operates in two phases. In the first phase, it separates the empty regions from the nonempty regions of the tensor. In the second phase, the nonempty regions are further decomposed based on
AE
.
Since
AE

allows us to minimize the
AE
in significantly fewer steps. The tree example shown in Figure

reinforces this logic. As seen in the results in Figure

is based on

Furthermore,
RIPOST
ensures that both

and

in each phase are independent of the depth
h
and do not require a predefined or maximum
h
as input. In Figure
3
plot 3, we see the performances of
RIPOST
compared to the others. It is able to reduce the
Early Convergence
by a big margin to only
29\%
while generating more refined blocks.

5.
Proposed Algorithm

In this section, we present our method
RIPOST
, which constructs a DP view by privately decomposing a tensor into a set of blocks, and the user can query this view without any privacy risk while ensuring the utility of the query results.

5.1.
Overview of
RIPOST

Our proposed solution addresses all the key challenges and problems highlighted in Section
4
. Thus,
RIPOST
iteratively splits the original tensor into two sub-blocks and stops when the cells in a block have minimal

in each block.

During the first phase, the algorithm uses the

5.2.
Privacy Budget Distribution

In order to ensure that our algorithm
RIPOST
satisfies
\epsilon
-DP, we need to ensure not only that randomness is introduced, but also that the total budget remains below
\epsilon
as required.
As mentioned in Section
4
, controlling the budget in a decomposition often results in a limitation on depth, which introduces significant challenges and tradeoffs.
To avoid such a constraint, we need to define a function that distributes a given budget
\epsilon
for any arbitrary depth
h
, such that:

(1)

\epsilon\geq\sum_{i=1}^{h}{\epsilon_{i}}\text{, where }\epsilon_{i}>0

Since each
\epsilon_{i}<\epsilon
, we can define
\epsilon_{i}
as:
\epsilon_{i}=\epsilon\times\omega_{i}
, where
\omega_{i}\in]0,1[
. Thus, we can redefine the constraint in Equation
1
as:

(2)

\begin{array}[]{ll}\epsilon\geq\sum_{i=1}^{h}{\epsilon\times\omega_{i}}\text{ , where }\epsilon_{i}=\epsilon\times\omega_{i}\\
\\
1\geq\sum_{i=1}^{h}{\omega_{i}}\end{array}

According to Equation
2
, we need to define a weight for each level and ensure that the sum of weights does not exceed
1
. To define such a distribution, we can opt for the following convergent series
(
Zeidler et al. 2004
)
:

(3)

\begin{array}[]{ll}\omega_{i}=\frac{1}{i(i+1)}\text{ with positive integers }i\\
\\
\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\omega_{i}=1\end{array}

The issue in this case is that most of the allocation will be given to the very first few levels of the decomposition (i.e. bigger weights at the start) because such series decays quickly. Thus, it may lead to injecting too much noise early in the decomposition and cause the
Early Convergence
discussed in Section
4
due to to the budget being consumed quickly (big
\omega_{i}
early on).

Simply considering a slower series may not be sufficient. So to slow down the weights distribution, we considered the sum of multiple instances of the series term by term. For instance, given
S=1/i(i+1)
, then we define
S^{2}=1/i(i+1)+1/i(i+1)=2/i(i+1)
. Here,
S^{2}
decays slower than
S
, but it no longer satisfies the condition in Equation
2
and the sum of weights is no longer equal to 1. However, we notice that the first term of
S^{2}
is
S^{2}_{1}=2/(1+1)=1
, so the remaining terms have the sum equal to 1:
\sum_{i=2}^{n}{2/i(i+1)}=1
. Therefore, we can offset the start of the series (in the case of
S^{2}
the
offset
is
1
, we refer to it as
os
) to obtain a new series that: (1) decays slower and (2) satisfies Equation
2
.

So in the case of
S=1/i(i+1)
, we can use a series
S^{k}=k/i(i+1)
with a given
offset

os
to define the weight
\omega_{i}
as:

(4)

\omega_{i}=S^{k}_{(i+os)}

Thus, we define a function
get\_weight(.)
that will be used by
Secure\_cc
and
Secure\_ss
functions (used in Algorithm
1
) to obtain the proper budget for an operation based on the block’s depth at each phase of Algorithm
1
.

Setting the appropriate
offset

os
depends on the choice of
S
. In this work, we consider
S=\frac{1}{i(i+1)}
, though other series can also be used. To determine
os
for this definition of
S
, we need to ensure that the following condition holds:

Given
S
, we know that:

\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{i(i+1)}\leq 1-\frac{1}{n+1},

which implies:

\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{k}{i(i+1)}\leq k\left(1-\frac{1}{n+1}\right)\leq k

Thus, we define
os
such as:

\sum_{i=1}^{os-1}\frac{k}{i(i+1)}+\sum_{i=os}^{n}\frac{k}{i(i+1)}\leq k

And in such a way :

\sum_{i=1}^{os-1}\frac{k}{i(i+1)}\geq k-1\text{ and }\sum_{i=os}^{n}\frac{k}{i(i+1)}\leq 1

The first sum
\sum_{i=1}^{os-1}\frac{k}{i(i+1)}
represents the sum of terms that we will ignore, and the second sum
\sum_{i=os}^{n}\frac{k}{i(i+1)}
represents the sum of terms that we want to use in Equation
4
. Based on this, we get:

\sum_{i=1}^{os-1}\frac{k}{i(i+1)}\geq k-1\implies k\left(1-\frac{1}{(os-1)+1}\right)\geq k-1

which leads to the condition, for any given
S^{k}
, the
offset

os
must satisfy:

os\geq k

to ensure that the sum of the weights remains within the bound
\leq 1
.

To show the difference in decay between
S
and its variants
S^{k}
, Figure
4
illustrates the
\omega_{i}
values obtained at each depth using different series.

Figure 4.
\omega_{i}
for each level of depth
h
using
S^{k}

Other alternatives, such as uniform budget distribution, are not possible since
h
is not known in advance. Additionally, the use of a growing series might be more interesting than a decaying series
(
Cormode et al. 2012
)
in some cases, but it also requires a predefined depth
h
. Without a predefined
h
, it would be very difficult to create such a series while ensuring that the constraint in Equation
2
holds.

5.3.3.
Sensitivity Min, Sum and AE

In order to apply the

and

used in

and

respectively, we need to compute the sensitivity of each metric used.

Theorem 5.1 (Sensitivity of the Min, Sum and AE).

For any neighbouring tensors
T
and
T^{\prime}
:
\Delta_{Min}=1
,
\Delta_{Sum}=1
and
\Delta_{AE}=2
.

Proof:

In the case of the
Sum
(and the
Min
)
: It is straightforward,
\Delta_{Sum}=1
(
\Delta_{Min}=1
), since given two neighboring tensors
T
and
T^{\prime}
, the
Sum
(
count
of empty or nonempty cells) will change by
1
because of a cell having
+1
or
-1
.

In the case of
AE
: Consider
T
and
T^{\prime}
. In
T^{\prime}
, suppose the cell
c_{l}
got
+1
, we compute the
AE
as follows:

(5)

\begin{array}[]{ll}AE(T)=\sum_{c\in\Omega_{D}}{|\bar{x}-c|}\text{ and,}&\bar{x}=\frac{Sum(T)}{|\Omega_{D}|}\\
AE(T^{\prime})=\sum_{c\in\Omega_{D},c\neq c_{l}}{|\bar{x^{\prime}}-c|+|\bar{x^{\prime}}-c_{l}|}\text{ and,}&\bar{x^{\prime}}=\frac{Sum(T)+1}{|\Omega_{D}|}\end{array}

Based on Equation
5
,
AE(T^{\prime})\leq\sum_{c\in\Omega_{D}}{|\bar{x}-c|}+2\times\frac{n-1}{n}
which implies that
\Delta_{AE}=|AE(T)-AE(T^{\prime})|\leq 2\times\frac{n-1}{n}\leq 2
.

5.4.
Privacy budget

To compute the privacy budget consumption of our decomposition algorithm, and since each branch manipulates a distinct portion of tensor
T
(as mention in Section
4
), we need only to track the consumption of only one path from the root
T
to a leaf node. Then based on the parallel composition, we can deduce the total consumption.

Each leaf node published by our algorithm must be produced by a branch that extends from the root and goes through two phases of decompositions as described in Algorithm
1
. Based on this, let’s assume that a branch went
k
iterations in our first decomposition phase and
p
iterations in the second phase. In the last step, the algorithm perturbs the leaves before publishing them. Given that all these iterations are applied consecutively to the same portion of the tensor, the total privacy budget is computed using the sequential composition property of DP, accumulating the budget consumed in each iteration.

So, to ensure that the budget consumption by the algorithm is in bound of the total budget
\epsilon
given as input to Algorithm
1
, means proving that for any given leaf block
B_{l}
this equation holds:

(6)

\frac{Pr[B_{l}|T]}{Pr[B_{l}|T^{\prime}]}\leq e^{\epsilon}

We note
B_{l}^{i}
the
i
’th predecessor of
B_{l}
starting from the root and belonging to the path from root to
B_{l}
. According to our Algorithm
1
, for the
Secure\_cc
during the first the phase we have the following budget consumption :

\left|\prod_{i=1}^{k-1}{\frac{Pr[Sum(B^{i}_{l})+Lap(\lambda_{i})>\theta_{!}]}{Pr[Sum(B^{\prime i}_{l})+Lap(\lambda_{i})>\theta_{1}]}}\right|\times\frac{Pr[Sum(B^{k}_{l})+Lap(\lambda_{i})\leq\theta_{1}]}{Pr[Sum(B^{\prime k}_{l})+Lap(\lambda_{i})\leq\theta_{1}]}

And for the
Secure\_ss
during the first phase:

\prod_{i=1}^{k}{\frac{Pr[point_{j}|B^{i}_{l},Min]}{Pr[point_{j}|B^{\prime i}_{l},Min]}}

Where
Pr[point_{j}|B^{i}_{l},metric]
is the probability
point_{j}
being selected as a cutting point given a block
B_{l}^{i}
and a scoring function using
metric
. For the second phase we have a similar budget consumption. For the
Secure\_cc
in the second phase:

\left|\prod_{i=k}^{p-1}{\frac{Pr[AE(B^{i}_{l})+Lap(\lambda_{i})>\theta_{2}]}{Pr[AE(B^{\prime i}_{l})+Lap(\lambda_{i})>\theta_{2}]}}\right|\times\frac{Pr[AE(B^{p}_{l})+Lap(\lambda_{i})\leq\theta_{2}]}{Pr[AE(B^{\prime p}_{l})+Lap(\lambda_{i})\leq\theta_{2}]}

And for the
Secure\_ss
:

\prod_{i=k}^{p}{\frac{Pr[point_{i}|B^{i}_{l},AE]}{Pr[point_{i}|B^{\prime i}_{l},AE]}}

Finally, Algorithm
1
perturbs the mean of the leaf:

\frac{Pr[mean(B_{l})+Lap(\lambda)=S]}{Pr[mean(B^{\prime}_{l})+Lap(\lambda)=S]}

Since each operation (
Secure\_cc
and
Secure\_ss
) is allocated a dedicated budget for each phase of Algorithm
1
lines 1-4, and according to the budget distribution we proposed in Section
5.2
and the definitions in
3.4
,
3.5
:
Secure\_cc
will not exceed
\epsilon^{1}_{cc}
and
\epsilon^{2}_{cc}
in both phases. Similarly,
Secure\_ss
will not exceed
\epsilon^{1}_{ss}
and
\epsilon^{2}_{ss}
in both phases. So based on the sequential composition, we have:

\frac{Pr[B_{l}|T]}{Pr[B_{l}|T^{\prime}]}\leq e^{\epsilon^{1}_{cc}}\times e^{\epsilon^{1}_{ss}}\times e^{\epsilon^{2}_{cc}}\times e^{\epsilon^{2}_{ss}}\times e^{\epsilon_{p}}\leq e^{\epsilon}

This result shows that our algorithm doesn’t exceed the privacy budget given as an input, independently of the depth
h
of any branch during the decomposition. Based on the
Parallel Composition
property of DP, this conclusion can be generalized to all paths of the decomposition.

In our evaluation, we used the RMSE (Root-Mean-Square Error) to measure the error between the query answer from the original dataset and those from the perturbed view.
The RMSE is defined as:
\text{RMSE}=\sqrt{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{Q\in W}(Q(T)-Q(T^{\prime}))^{2}}

where
W
is the workload, and
T
,
T^{\prime}
are the original and private views of the tensor, respectively (regardless of the method used to create
T^{\prime}
).
To compare the results across different algorithms, we calculated the relative RMSE, denoted as
R-RMSE
, by dividing the RMSE obtained by each approach on a workload
W
by the RMSE obtained using our approach on the same dataset.

Thus, if
\text{R-RMSE}>1
, it means our approach outperformed the other, and
R-RMSE
indicates the magnitude of the difference. Conversely, if
\text{R-RMSE}<1
, it means our approach underperformed compared to the other, and
R-RMSE
indicates the fraction that the RMSE of the other approach represents relative to ours.

Privacy budget

:
Here, we measured the performance of

obtained while changing the total privacy budget allocation
\epsilon
. A smaller value of

From Figure

Parameter
\alpha
:
According to Algorithm
1
,
RIPOST
uses this parameter to distribute the budget
\epsilon
between leaf perturbation
\epsilon_{p}
and the decomposition (
\epsilon_{d}
). A smaller
\alpha
means more budget is allocated to leaf perturbation and less to the decomposition, and vice-versa.
From Figure
15
(second plot), we observe that as
\alpha
increases, more blocks are generated, which is expected. However, the RMSE also increases due to the higher perturbation error (PE). This happens because: (i) less budget is allocated to leaf perturbation, resulting in more noise, and (ii) the number of blocks increases.
Based on the results in Figure
15
(second plot), the best performance of
RIPOST
is found when
\alpha
is between 0.2 and 0.4, achieving a balance between decomposition accuracy and perturbation error.

Parameter
\gamma
:
According to Algorithm
1
,
RIPOST
uses this parameter to distribute the budget
\epsilon_{d}
between the first phase of the decomposition
\epsilon_{1}
and the second phase
\epsilon_{2}
. A smaller
\gamma
means less budget is allocated to phase 1 and more to phase 2, and vice-versa.

From Figure
15
(third plot), we observe that as
\gamma
increases, more blocks are generated, and there is a decrease in RMSE. This highlights the importance of the first phase of the decomposition in
RIPOST
, which aims to separate the non-empty cells from the empty ones. This phase is one of the most distinctive features of
RIPOST
compared to other existing decomposition algorithms, and these results confirm the observations made in Section
4
.

Parameter

:
According to Algorithm
1
,
RIPOST
uses this parameter to allocate the decomposition budget for each phase,
\epsilon_{1}
(or
\epsilon_{2}
), between

,
\epsilon_{cc}^{1}
(or

), and

\epsilon_{ss}^{1}

7.
Discussion

In this section, we put forward some discussion points that can be considered as possible extensions and improvements that can be added to our proposed solution. First, according to the budget distribution strategy introduced in Section
5.2
, both
Secure_{cc}
and
Secure_{ss}
will never consume the full budget allocated to them. This is due to the fact that the decomposition eventually converges; even if the depth
h
varies from tensor to tensor, it can never be infinite. A simple optimization would be to take the unconsumed budget during the decomposition and add it to
\epsilon_{p}
to reduce the noise added to the leaves, thus reducing the perturbation error (PE). On the same track of improving budget consumption, an improvement could be to apply
Secure_{cc}
normally once and skip it (or give it budget
0
) for a certain number of steps
k
, and afterwards apply it normally once again, and so on. This will allow us to chop the budget for
Secure_{cc}
in both phases by
k
and add the saved budget to
\epsilon_{p}
to reduce the PE.

Figure 16.
Results of extended
RIPOST

In Figure
16
, we present the results of a small experiment conducted on a 2D tensor created using the Adult dataset. On the x-axis, we display the number of steps
k
between consecutive applications of the
Secure_{cc}
. From the results, we observe the following: (i) as
k
increases, the number of blocks generated also increases, which leads to a reduction in the aggregation error (AE), and (ii) the RMSE decreases, highlighting the advantage of reallocating the budget gained to reduce the perturbation error (PE). Another interesting direction for improving both decomposition computation cost and privacy consumption is to focus on enhancing the algorithm for
Secure_{ss}
. Specifically, in Algorithm
3
, line 3,
RIPOST
iterates over all possible cutting points in the domain. A potential improvement could involve considering a smaller search space, such as a random sample of the domain. By selecting this sample randomly, we could enforce privacy and reduce budget consumption
(
Ebadi et al. 2016
)
. However, such enhancements require careful consideration, as random sampling may lead to suboptimal partitioning. For this reason, we prefer to address this in a dedicated future project. The final point we would like to mention is the potential use of the generated tree to improve both query processing time and accuracy, as highlighted in
(
Cormode et al. 2012
;
Zhang et al. 2016
)
. In the current version of
RIPOST
, the tree can be leveraged to enhance processing time. However,
RIPOST
does not yet incorporate the tree to improve accuracy. Given the results obtained in Section
6
, it would be interesting to explore whether we can extend
RIPOST
to also address accuracy in future work.

8.
Conclusion

In this work, we addressed the problem of releasing a privacy-preserving view of multidimensional data using a domain decomposition-based method. We introduced our solution,
RIPOST
, which overcomes the design and theoretical limitations of previously proposed solutions in the literature, specifically regarding data-aware splitting and the independence from the depth
h
of the decomposition. Through extensive experiments, we demonstrated that
RIPOST
outperforms existing methods in terms of the utility of the generated view. The positive results obtained in this work encourage us to explore various extensions and improvements in future work, particularly focusing on tighter budget management and reducing computational costs.
