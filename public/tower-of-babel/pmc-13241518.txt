Tesla-scale magnetic field measurement based on Sideband-overlap Zeeman spectroscopy using a functionalized MEMS vapor cell
Authors: Ju Guo, Yintao Ma, Dejiang Lu, Mingzhi Yu, Yanbin Wang, Ping Yang, Qijing Lin, Libo Zhao, Yao Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Accurate measurement of strong magnetic fields in the Tesla range remains a persistent challenge due to calibration drift, nonlinearity, and spatial gradient sensitivity. Here, we present a compact magnetometry approach based on sideband-overlap Zeeman spectroscopy, enabled by a functionalized MEMS cesium vapor cell. The vapor cell features a dual-chamber glass–Si–glass structure with integrated microheaters on the optical window. Magnetic field scanning from zero to Tesla-scale fields enables extraction of the frequency offset between reference and Zeeman-shifted spectra. This frequency shift is directly converted into magnetic field strength using Zeeman spectroscopy in the hyperfine Paschen–Back regime. The method resolves sixteen Zeeman transitions (eight \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{\rm{\sigma }}}^{+}$$\end{document}σ+ and eight \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{\rm{\sigma }}}^{-}$$\end{document}σ−), allowing measurement of a field strength of 0.6694921 T, with a single-shot resolution of 6.4 μT and a cross-transition repeatability of σB = 8.9 μT (corresponding to 13 ppm at 0.6695 T). In a millimeter-scale sensing volume, the system achieves sub-10 μT repeatability without requiring a uniform bias field. These results indicate that the microfabricated high-field magnetometer can serve as a compact and practical approach for Tesla-range field measurement and pave the way for chip-scale quantum devices.

Introduction

Precise measurement of strong magnetic fields in the Tesla (T) range is essential for superconducting magnet calibration1, high-field nuclear magnetic resonance2, quantum spin devices3, and fusion diagnostics4–7. However, conventional solid-state sensors such as Hall probes and magnetoresistive devices commonly exhibit limited linearity, thermal drift, and calibration instability as the magnetic field increases toward the Tesla-scale regime, complicating absolute and traceable field metrology8–10. Fluxgates are generally not intended for Tesla-class static fields, and SQUID-based systems require cryogenic infrastructure and are generally unsuitable for compact deployment in Tesla environments11–14. These limitations hinder compact, absolute, and traceable field measurements in Tesla-scale environments.

Atomic spectroscopy offers a fundamentally different pathway by linking magnetic field strength to atomic transition frequencies, thereby enabling absolute and traceable measurements15–17. In alkali atoms, Zeeman splitting of hyperfine levels produces frequency shifts in the optical absorption spectrum18. Under high magnetic fields, atoms enter the hyperfine Paschen–Back (HPB) regime19–21, where nuclear and electronic spins become decoupled. This results in symmetric and near-linear Zeeman shifts, allowing field extraction directly from the frequency spacing between resolved Zeeman branches22.

Recent advances have demonstrated high-field Zeeman spectroscopy using micron-thick or MEMS alkali vapor cells23,24. For example, Sargsyan et al.25 employed nanocell-based selective reflection to probe the HPB regime in cesium, and related nanocell magnetometry has demonstrated wide-range operation with reduced vapor thickness and improved spatial localization26. Han et al.27 demonstrated sub-100 μT-level resolution in a micron-scale cesium cell using beat-frequency measurements. Optical spectroscopy in micro-sized vapor samples has further extended HPB-regime measurements to very high fields (tens of tesla) with ppm-level accuracy in small sensing volumes21. In application-driven settings, optically engineered cesium magnetometers have demonstrated multi-Tesla operation with robust field tracking and favorable noise performance, particularly in MRI systems that utilize homogeneous superconducting coils28. Meanwhile, from a device engineering perspective, MEMS alkali vapor cells have become an enabling platform for miniaturized and manufacturable atomic sensors29–31. Building on this platform, chip-scale hot-atom devices have continued to advance in device design, packaging, and system integration32–34. However, most MEMS vapor cell studies to date have focused on low-field magnetometry, where spin-relaxation control, magnetic shielding, and compensation coils are employed to optimize sensitivity14,35. MEMS vapor cells have also enabled compact magnetic field imaging techniques, such as free-induction decay (FID) imaging36. Nevertheless, for Tesla-class fields, MEMS-compatible implementations with simplified frequency referencing and self-referenced readout remain underexplored. Many strong-field spectroscopy approaches still rely on complex alignment, multiple stabilized lasers, wide laser tuning, or RF-assisted architectures, which limit system integration and portability.

Here, we present an all-optical and self-referenced method for Tesla-range Zeeman spectroscopy enabled by a functionalized MEMS cesium vapor cell. A single DFB laser and an electro-optic modulator (EOM) generate optical sidebands, which are tuned to match Zeeman-shifted D1 transitions in the HPB regime. This configuration provides a self-referenced frequency axis within a compact MEMS sensor head, eliminating the need for frequency locking or multiple stabilized lasers. Simultaneous acquisition of zero-field and high-field spectra allows the magnetic field to be extracted through Lorentzian fitting without RF excitation. The spectroscopy is realized in a dual-chamber glass–Si–glass MEMS cesium vapor cell with integrated non-magnetic microheaters for stable operation and repeatable spectroscopic response. In the HPB regime, the system resolves sixteen Zeeman transitions (eight \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{\rm{\sigma }}}^{+}$$\end{document}σ+ and eight \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{\rm{\sigma }}}^{-}$$\end{document}σ− branches), enabling multi-branch validation of the extracted field. Using sideband-overlap Zeeman spectroscopy (SOZS), we demonstrate measurement of 0.6694921 T with a single-shot resolution of 6.4 μT and a cross-transition repeatability of σB = 8.9 μT (corresponding to 13 ppm). The effective sensing volume is confined to a millimeter-scale interaction region, achieving sub-10 μT repeatability without the need for a homogeneous bias field. In this implementation, the strong magnetic field is generated by a compact permanent-magnet assembly, enabling localized Tesla-scale field metrology. The presented platform thus offers a compact, absolute, and self-referenced solution for characterizing permanent magnet fields within miniaturized volumes.

Materials and Methods

Fabrication process of MEMS vapor cells

The cesium vapor cell was fabricated with a dual-chamber glass–silicon–glass sandwich structure, consisting of a cesium reservoir and an optical interaction chamber connected via a narrow microchannel for controlled vapor transmission. To ensure thermal stability and suppress spectral drift, resistive heating coils were symmetrically integrated onto the outer surfaces of the top and bottom glass substrates to form integrated vapor cells.

I. A 5 mm-thick, high-resistivity silicon wafer was CNC-machined to form a reservoir, a 5 mm-diameter optical cavity, and a connecting microchannel.

II. After solvent cleaning and thermal baking to remove surface contaminants, the wafer was anodically bonded to a borosilicate glass wafer at 300 °C under 700 V for 30 min to form the initial cavity.

III. A commercial cesium dispenser was manually inserted into the reservoir.

IV. The assembly was transferred to a vacuum chamber (10⁻⁶ mbar), and a second glass wafer was anodically bonded under the same conditions to complete hermetic sealing.

V. A 150 nm-thick NiCr film acting as the heater was sputtered onto the top glass surface.

VI. The same process was applied to the bottom glass surface to create a thermally symmetric configuration.

VII. The bonded wafer stack was diced into individual vapor cell chips using a water-cooled precision saw.

VIII. The cesium dispenser was activated by near-infrared laser irradiation ( ~ 10 s) to release cesium vapor.

The single functionalized MEMS vapor cell is presented in Fig. 1b. The overall dimensions were 8 × 5 × 12 mm³, with a 5 mm optical path defined by circular apertures in both glass windows.

The symmetric NiCr heaters ensured uniform temperature control across the 5 mm interaction area, with steady-state fluctuations maintained within ± 0.1 K and lateral non-uniformity below 0.3 K. The thermal controller achieved setpoint stabilization within tens of seconds with minimal overshoot. All heating materials and interconnects were non-magnetic, arranged in a balanced layout to eliminate stray magnetic fields during high-field spectroscopy up to 0.6695 T.

Miniaturized 3D-Printed Probe

The MEMS cesium vapor cell was integrated into a custom-designed, 3D-printed probe that housed the optical beam path, polarization optics, and a photodetector (PD) within a compact and modular structure. The entire probe assembly was mounted on a motorized three-axis translation stage, allowing precise spatial positioning and fine optical alignment of the vapor cell within the magnetic field region.

As illustrated in Fig. 2b, the probe was positioned between two axially aligned cylindrical permanent magnets that produced a static magnetic field. The MEMS vapor cell was placed at the geometric center of the field region, where the magnetic flux density reached approximately 0.6695 T. The field strength was independently verified using a calibrated Hall-effect sensor with a measurement accuracy better than 0.1%.

The integrated non-magnetic NiCr microheaters maintained the vapor cell at a highly stable operating temperature of 50.0 ± 0.01 °C, ensuring consistent cesium vapor pressure and minimizing frequency drift caused by thermal fluctuations. The combination of a rigid 3D-printed structural frame, a stable permanent-magnet configuration, and high-resolution motorized translation enabled highly repeatable Zeeman spectroscopy measurements, achieving sub-100 kHz spectral precision corresponding to a magnetic field resolution of 10 μT.

The magnetic field experienced by the MEMS cesium vapor cell was quantitatively measured using a first-order sideband-overlap technique, based on phase modulation induced by an electro-optic modulator (EOM). The modulation frequency fEOM was incrementally swept from 3580 to 3585 MHz in 0.5 MHz steps around the first-order sideband-overlap condition. For each frequency setting, the SAS1 (0 T) and SAS2 (0.6695 T) spectra were simultaneously acquired. The measurement uncertainty is primarily limited by systematic rather than statistical effects. In addition, in the hyperfine Paschen–Back regime, accurate identification of the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{\rm{\sigma }}}^{+}/{{\rm{\sigma }}}^{-}$$\end{document}σ+/σ− transitions and the corresponding nuclear spin sublevels (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${m}_{I}$$\end{document}mI) is essential, as incorrect assignment can introduce systematic bias in the extracted magnetic field. Secondary sources of error include laser-frequency drift, power broadening, and collision-induced spectral shifts.

Several mitigation strategies were implemented to reduce both systematic and random errors. Synchronous acquisition of SAS1 and SAS2 spectra ensured a shared frequency axis, eliminating distortions from nonlinear laser scanning. Active temperature control stabilized thermal conditions and vapor density, minimizing long-term drift. Differential photodetection suppressed common-mode optical and electronic noise, enhancing the precision of line-center extraction.

Collectively, these strategies significantly reduce both systematic bias and random fluctuations. Compared to compact Hall sensors or fluxgate magnetometers, the demonstrated atomic-based technique achieves sub-10 μT repeatability in Tesla-scale magnetic fields, measured over millimeter-scale interaction volumes, without the need for homogeneous bias fields or RF excitation.

These characteristics make the system particularly well-suited for in situ mapping of local magnetic fields near permanent magnets or other strong-field sources, where high spatial resolution and atomic-scale accuracy are critical.

Discussion

The experimental results obtained in this work demonstrate good consistency with the theoretical predictions of Zeeman splitting in the high-field Paschen–Back regime. By integrating a MEMS cesium vapor cell with a sideband-overlap readout technique, we demonstrate direct all-optical measurement of Tesla-scale magnetic fields with microtesla-level resolution, in a compact architecture compatible with chip-scale integration.

The dominant sources of uncertainty are systematic in nature. Magnetic field gradients across the 5 mm optical interaction region, together with slight mechanical misalignments of the sensor probe, contribute to residual deviations in extracted field values. These gradients primarily lead to resonance broadening, as atoms experience a distribution of local magnetic fields across the interaction volume. The center frequency extracted from Lorentzian fitting tracks the mean field, provided the sampled distribution is not strongly asymmetric. However, small variations in probe positioning between runs can slightly shift the sampled field distribution, introducing a residual bias in the extracted center. To account for this effect, we use the dispersion of the inverted field values across the 16 resolved Zeeman transitions as an empirical upper bound on the gradient-induced bias under our measurement conditions.

Thermal fluctuations, power broadening, and laser frequency drift also affect the stability of line-center extraction. These effects were mitigated by actively stabilizing the vapor cell temperature, implementing differential photodetection, and synchronizing the acquisition of zero-field and high-field spectra on a shared frequency axis. This combination of strategies allows for reproducible extraction of Zeeman line positions across multiple spectral branches.

Compared with conventional miniature magnetic sensors, the presented method provides intrinsically frequency-traceable readout and localized measurements in the strong, non-uniform fields produced by permanent magnets. The measurement volume is confined to a few cubic millimeters, and no homogeneous bias field or RF excitation is required. This makes the technique particularly attractive for applications involving strong, localized magnetic fields, including permanent magnet characterization and integrated field mapping.

Spatially resolved field mapping is particularly relevant near permanent magnets, where Tesla-scale fields are typically accompanied by strong spatial gradients. In the present implementation, the spatial resolution is primarily set by the effective sensing volume, which is defined by the optical beam diameter together with the interaction length of the MEMS vapor cell. Two-dimensional maps can therefore be obtained by raster scanning the probe using the existing motorized three-axis translation stage, with the step size chosen according to the desired sampling density. Higher spatial resolution can be pursued by reducing the beam diameter and by adopting shorter optical-path MEMS cell designs. Looking forward, multi-cell or array configurations may enable parallel acquisition, and combining measurements with known magnet geometry and numerical simulation would facilitate reconstruction of three-dimensional field distributions. Further miniaturization will focus on integrating the reference optical path and related components into the main system or within the probe head to reduce overall footprint and improve robustness.

Further improvement is expected through polarization-resolved detection, enhanced mechanical referencing, and active compensation of magnetic field gradients. Integration with wafer-level optical components and microelectronic elements may further support system miniaturization, bringing this approach closer to practical deployment in embedded quantum sensors.

Conclusions

We have demonstrated a Tesla-range magnetic metrology technique for localized permanent-magnet fields using an integrated probe based on a functionalized MEMS Cs vapor cell and first-order EOM sideband-overlap spectroscopy. The glass–Si–glass vapor cell, equipped with non-magnetic serpentine microheaters, maintained thermal stability at 50 °C and enabled high-SNR, Doppler-free absorption spectra under both zero-field and Tesla-scale magnetic field conditions. By employing synchronous dual-arm scans, quadratic-background Lorentzian fitting, and a high-field Zeeman model, a local magnetic field of 0.6694921 T was measured with a single-shot resolution of 6.4 μT and a cross-transition repeatability of σB = 8.9 μT (corresponding to 13 ppm) based on sixteen resolved Zeeman transitions. These experimental results closely matched theoretical predictions over the 0.6–0.7 T range.

The results establish MEMS-based sideband-overlap Zeeman spectroscopy as a robust, compact, and self-referenced technique for Tesla-range magnetic-field diagnostics and calibration in constrained geometries. Looking forward, future integration of polarization- or angle-resolved vector readout, wafer-scale micro-EOMs, advanced thermal and optical leveling techniques, and active field-gradient compensation is anticipated to improve the resolution beyond 5 μT, while retaining low power consumption and full chip-scale integrability. Additionally, embedding the reference arm and associated optics directly into the main sensing module or probe head represents a promising direction for system-level miniaturization and enhanced robustness.
