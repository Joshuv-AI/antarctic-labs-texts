Influence of age on eggshell quality in ducks: An analysis based on calcium metabolism, gut microbiota, and uterine transcriptome
Authors: Xihuai Xiong, Hanxue Sun, Zhengyu Hu, Yong Tian, Tao Zeng, Lizhi Lu, Fangxiong Shi, Yansen Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Our current project aims to elucidate the impact of advancing date on eggshell quality in Shanma ducks and its underlying mechanisms. A total of 60 healthy Shanma ducks from the youth group (160 days old) and the elderly group (560 days old) were selected to evaluate eggshell physicochemical properties, serum calcium and phosphorus metabolism, gut microbiota, and uterine tissue transcriptomic expressions. Results demonstrated that the elderly group exhibited significantly reduced eggshell thickness and strength, with ultrastructural analysis revealing uneven calcium deposition and a thinner effective layer compared to the youth group. Elderly ducks showed decreased serum levels of 25-hydroxyvitamin D₃, alongside elevated parathyroid hormone and bone remodeling markers (TRAP-5b, BAP), suggesting disrupted calcium metabolism. Histological observation identified duodenal villus atrophy, humeral matrix erosion, and uterine interstitial edema in elderly ducks. Gut microbiome analysis indicated reduced microbial diversity and simplified community structure in elderly ducks, with Helicobacter and Lactobacillus becoming dominant genera, and significant downregulation of mineral absorption-related functional pathways. Uterine transcriptomics identified 607 differentially expressed genes, significantly enriched in pathways such as calcium signaling and steroid hormone biosynthesis. Correlation network analysis further revealed that eggshell strength was closely associated with bone resorption markers, Helicobacter abundance, and the expression of multiple calcium metabolism-related genes (e.g., KCNS2, GABRA1, FOXF1, ATP1B1). Notably, the positive correlation between eggshell strength and SMAD9 observed in the youth group was absent in the elderly group. In conclusion, the age-related decline in eggshell quality results from the combined effects of calcium metabolism imbalance, enhanced bone remodeling, gut microbial dysbiosis, and uterine functional disruption, providing multi-omics insights into the decline of avian reproductive performance with age.

Introduction

Eggshell strength, a critical property determining the economic value of eggs, is well known to decline gradually with advancing age in poultry (Wang et al., 2021a). It directly influences the breakage rate during transport, storage safety, and the hatchability of fertilized eggs (De Ketelaere et al., 2023). Global annual egg production is estimated at 76 million tons (Omotoso et al., 2021); however, post-production losses due to inadequate eggshell strength reach 8%–10% (Wang et al., 2021b). Economic losses from cracked and broken eggs during the late laying period are even more substantial, amounting to 12%–20% (Travel A et al., 2011). These issues adversely affect farmers' profits, impede the implementation of breeding programs, and elevate food safety risks (Chousalkar et al., 2010). Therefore, improving eggshell quality in the late laying phase is of paramount importance. This imperative has motivated extensive investigation into the impact of increasing age on eggshell strength. In commercial laying ducks, a significant decline in eggshell breaking strength is observed from the peak laying phase (31 weeks of age) to the late phase (65 weeks of age). This deterioration is accompanied by a reduction in mammillary layer density, a decrease in effective thickness, and impaired calcium metabolism (Zhang et al., 2022). Physiological studies in laying hens have revealed a parallel mechanism, where the efficiency of duodenal calcium absorption drops from 408 to 329 pmol/mg tissue/min between 37 and 58 weeks of age. This decline occurs concurrently with reductions in eggshell thickness and femur ash content (Al-batshan et al., 1994).

Eggshell strength is a complex trait orchestrated by multiple physiological processes, and the expression of its genetic potential is constrained by various factors. Calcium metabolism serves as the central regulatory hub, encompassing intestinal absorption, blood transport, bone mobilization, and uterine deposition. The complexity of this system becomes increasingly pronounced with advancing age. Calcium is primarily absorbed in the duodenum, mediated by the calcium-binding protein in epithelial cells (Bar and Hurwitz, 1987). After entering the bloodstream, calcium is deposited into the medullary bone during the day and is subsequently mobilized at night for mineralization into the eggshell (Kim et al., 2012).

Research has revealed that the expression of S100β, an essential calcium-binding protein in the avian duodenum, markedly declines during the late growth phase (post-55 days). This reduction impairs intestinal integrity, ultimately leading to an age-dependent decrease in nutrient absorption (Han et al., 2025). In a study of 79-week-old laying hens, the group with lower eggshell strength exhibited significantly reduced femoral bone density and bone calcium content, along with downregulated expression of osteogenesis-related genes such as COL12A1 and FMOD. This reveals that an imbalance in bone metabolism is a key mechanism affecting eggshell strength (Fu et al., 2024). Further studies in laying hens indicated that the expression of genes essential for biomineralization decreases with age. At the same time, the expression of inflammatory cytokines such as IL-1β in the uterus (shell gland) is upregulated (Feng et al., 2020). Therefore, in poultry, especially in older birds, eggshell strength is influenced by multiple physiological processes, including intestinal calcium absorption, skeletal calcium mobilization, and uterine calcium deposition.

The Shanma duck is an excellent egg-laying breed originating from Fujian Province (Zhu et al., 2024). As a representative indigenous genetic resource of domestic ducks in China, it exhibits remarkable advantages such as small body size, early sexual maturity, and high egg production (Lin et al., 2016). This breed typically begins laying eggs at 100–110 days of age, with the egg-laying rate reaching over 95% at 180 days of age. The peak production period can be sustained until approximately 400 days of age, after which laying performance gradually declines (Zhu et al., 2017). In previous studies, we investigated the variability of eggshell quality during the peak laying period (Sun et al., 2025). However, the decline in eggshell quality and disturbances in calcium metabolism during the late laying phase also warrant attention (Gao et al., 2024). Therefore, this study uses Shanma ducks at 160 days of age (transitioning from the early laying stage to the peak production period) and 560 days of age (late laying stage with declining production performance) as animal models, integrating multi-dimensional data including eggshell ultrastructure, serum calcium metabolism parameters, gut microbiome sequencing, and uterine transcriptome sequencing. The aim is to systematically elucidate the intrinsic mechanisms by which advancing age affects eggshell quality, thereby providing a theoretical foundation for the healthy breeding of laying ducks and genetic improvement of eggshell quality.

Materials and methods

Experimental animals and grouping

A total of 60 healthy laying Shanma ducks (youth group: 160 days old, n = 30; elderly group: 560 days old, n = 30) were used in this study. All ducks were provided by Guiliu Agricultural Animal Husbandry Co., Ltd. (Zhoukou county, Zhejiang Province, China) and housed under uniform environmental conditions. After a 4-week acclimatization period, 120 eggs were collected over two consecutive days for egg quality assessment. Preliminary phenotypic analysis revealed a significant difference in eggshell strength between the two groups (see Supplementary Table S1 for details). Based on these results, a subset of 20 ducks (10 individuals from each group, representing the median eggshell strength within their respective groups) was selected for subsequent in-depth analysis. This subset underwent systematic evaluation of phenotypic differences, serum biochemical parameters, cecal microbiota (16S rRNA sequencing), and uterine transcriptome profiling. For transcriptome analysis, a total of 12 uterine tissue samples (6 from the youth group and 6 from the elderly group) were subjected to RNA sequencing.

Housing and feeding conditions

The ducks were individually housed in a three-tier cage system (cage dimensions: 27 cm width × 40 cm depth × 40 cm height) under controlled environmental conditions. The temperature in the experimental facility was maintained at 20–25 °C, with relative humidity set at 65%–75%. A daily lighting regimen of 16 h of light (04:00–20:00) was provided. All animals received a nutritionally complete feed that complies with Chinese national standards (GB/T 41189-2021). The detailed nutritional composition of the diet is provided in Supplementary Table S2.

Animal ethics statement

No medications were administered to the animals, and euthanasia was performed at the end of the study. Animal care and use procedures followed the guidelines approved by the Animal Welfare Ethics Committee of the Zhejiang Academy of Agricultural Sciences (Approval No: 2024ZAASLA027).

Sample collection

From the 60 ducks initially enrolled in this study, a subset of 20 ducks were selected and divided into a youth group (YG, n = 10) and an elderly group (EG, n = 10) based on age. According to the established experimental protocol, standardized biological sample collection was conducted for both groups, which included: collecting duck eggs for two consecutive days prior to the experiment, totaling 40 eggs; collecting blood samples via jugular venipuncture before euthanasia using 10 mL blood collection tubes, with serum immediately separated after collection (centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 15 min) and stored at -80 °C; and aseptically obtaining the following tissues and samples after euthanasia: uterine tissue (molecular biology samples flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen, histology samples fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde at 4 °C for 24 h), shell gland tissue (flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen), duodenal tissue (fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin), duodenal content (placed in sterile EP tubes and flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen), and the right humerus (fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde). All frozen samples were transferred to -80 °C ultra-low temperature freezers within 2 h of collection for long-term storage.

Eggshell quality and serum biochemical indices

Multiple parameters were monitored in both groups, including egg weight (EW), egg shape index (ESX), shell thickness (ST), speckle score, eggshell strength (ES), and eggshell microstructure. A total of 40 eggs (20 per group) were measured. The transverse and longitudinal diameters of the eggs were measured using a vernier caliper (accuracy 0.01 mm, Shanghai Jiuquan Hardware Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China), and the ESX was calculated. The average thickness of the blunt end, sharp end, and equatorial region of the eggshell was measured using an eggshell thickness gauge (Robotmation, ECHOMETER 1061, Japan). Eggshell strength (ES) was determined using an eggshell strength tester (FGV-10XY, ORKA Food Technology Ltd., Israel). Speckle levels on four surfaces (A Side, B Side, C Side, D Side) of the eggshell were scored with an eggshell speckle detector (24ME07, Beijing Tianxiang Feiyu Technology Co., Ltd., Beijing, China). EW (g) was recorded using an electronic balance (range 4 kg, accuracy 0.01 g, Shanghai Ante Weighing Equipment Co., Ltd.). Finally, processed eggshell samples were observed and imaged using a scanning electron microscope (SU8100, HITACHI, Japan) to examine the microstructure (including effective layer thickness, mammillary layer thickness, total thickness, etc.).

Serum samples were thawed at 4 °C to determine the concentrations of calcium, phosphorus, calcium-regulating hormones, and bone remodeling-related enzymes in both groups. Calcium (Ca, kit HY-N0021) and phosphorus (P, kit HY-N0022) levels in serum were measured by colorimetric methods. Serum estradiol (E₂) was assessed using kit HY-C0007, parathyroid hormone (PTH) with kit HY-A0013, 25-hydroxyvitamin D₃ (25-OH-VD₃) with kit HY-NE100, calcitonin (CT) with kit HY-J0002, bone-specific alkaline phosphatase (BAP) with kit HY-J0005, and tartrate-resistant acid phosphatase 5b (TRAP-5b) with kit HY-NE138. All kits were supplied by the Beijing Huaying Biotechnology Institute (Beijing, China). All assays were performed in strict accordance with the manufacturer's instructions to ensure accuracy and reliability of the results.

Histological observation of biological tissues (HE, TUNEL and Goldner Staining)

Tissue samples of the duodenum and shell gland were fixed overnight, embedded in paraffin blocks, and sectioned using a routine microtome. Hematoxylin and eosin (HE) staining was performed for histopathological observation. Another section of the shell gland was used to detect cell apoptosis with a fluorescein TUNEL assay kit (C1088, Beyotime, Shanghai, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, tissue sections were deparaffinized and rehydrated. Protease K solution was then applied for antigen retrieval. After permeabilization, the TUNEL reaction solution was added, and nuclei were stained with DAPI solution. Finally, microscopic examination and image acquisition were carried out using a fluorescence microscope (Nikon Eclipse C1, Tokyo, Japan). Blue indicates nuclei, and green indicates apoptosis-positive cells.

Goldner’s trichrome staining was performed as previously described (Farr et al., 2017). Paraformaldehyde-fixed humerus samples (one sample per replicate) were decalcified, dehydrated, and embedded in paraffin. The paraffin blocks were cut into 4 μm thick sections using a microtome. After deparaffinization, Goldner’s trichrome staining was conducted using a commercial kit (G1064, Servicebio Technology Co., Ltd., Wuhan, China) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Bone images were scanned using a scanning electron microscope (SU8100, HITACHI, Japan).

Gut microbiome analysis

To ensure the accuracy and reliability of sequencing data, strict quality control was implemented at each step from DNA samples to data generation, including sample inspection, PCR, purification, library construction, and sequencing. Total genomic DNA was extracted from duodenal content samples using the CTAB/SDS method. DNA concentration and purity were assessed by 1% agarose gel electrophoresis, and the DNA was diluted to 1 ng/μL with sterile water. The V3–V4 region of the bacterial 16S rRNA gene was amplified using the primers 341F 5′-CCTACGGGNGGCWGCAG-3′ and 805R 5′-GACTACHVGGGTATCTAATCC-3′, together with sample-specific barcode sequences. PCR amplification was performed using Phusion® High-Fidelity PCR Master Mix. PCR products were examined by 2% agarose gel electrophoresis, pooled in equimolar amounts, and purified using the Qiagen Gel Extraction Kit. Sequencing libraries were prepared using the NEBNext® Ultra™ II DNA Library Prep Kit (Cat. No. E7645) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Library quality was evaluated using the Qubit 2.0 Fluorometer and Agilent Bioanalyzer 2100 system. Qualified libraries were sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform to generate 250-bp paired-end reads.

After obtaining the raw data, we conducted systematic bioinformatic analyses: First, software such as fastp (Chen et al., 2018) and FLASH was used for quality control and assembly of raw reads, and chimeric sequences were removed using vsearch to obtain high-quality effective tags. These tags were then clustered into operational taxonomic units (OTUs) at 97% similarity threshold. Taxonomic annotation was carried out using QIIME2 software (Rai et al., 2021) and the Silva 138 database to clarify the microbial community composition from phylum to genus level in each sample. To assess microbial diversity, multiple alpha diversity indices including Chao1 and Shannon were calculated, and rarefaction curves were plotted to evaluate sequencing depth. Beta diversity analysis was performed based on Weighted and Unweighted Unifrac distances using PCoA and NMDS, with statistical methods such as ANOSIM and ADONIS applied to test the significance of community structure differences between groups. To identify differential taxa between groups, Wilcoxon rank-sum tests were performed at different taxonomic levels based on relative abundance data. At the genus level, taxa showing significant intergroup differences (P < 0.05) were selected for visualization in the heatmap. In parallel, LEfSe analysis (LDA score > 2) was used to identify group-enriched microbial biomarkers across taxonomic levels. Finally, the Tax4Fun2 (Wemheuer et al., 2020) tool was used to predict functional profiles of the communities based on 16S rRNA gene sequences, and abundance differences in KEGG metabolic pathways were compared among different groups.

Shell gland transcriptome analysis

Total RNA was extracted using the TRIzol method. RNA concentration and purity were measured with a NanoDrop™ 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), and RNA integrity was assessed using the Agilent 4200 TapeStation system (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA). Only samples with an RNA Integrity Number (RIN) ≥ 7.0 were retained for subsequent library construction. Libraries were prepared using the Illumina TruSeq™ RNA Sample Preparation Kit and sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq™ 6000 platform, generating 2 × 150 bp paired-end reads. Raw sequencing data were quality-controlled and filtered using cutadapt (v4.3) (Kechin et al., 2017). Clean reads were aligned to the mallard reference genome (ASM874695v1) using STAR (v2.7.10b) (Dobin et al., 2013). Gene expression levels were quantified with featureCounts (v2.0.4) (Liao et al., 2014) and normalized using TPM. Differential expression analysis was performed using edgeR (v3.40.2) (Robinson et al., 2010), with differentially expressed genes (DEGs) identified based on an FDR < 0.05 and |log₂ fold change| ≥ 1. Gene Ontology (GO) functional enrichment analysis was conducted using topGO (v2.50.0), and KEGG pathway enrichment analysis was performed using clusterProfiler (v4.6.2). Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) was carried out with clusterProfiler to assess overall expression trends between the two groups based on KEGG and GO gene sets.

Statistical analysis

All quantitative data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Differences between the YG and EG were assessed using independent samples t-tests unless otherwise specified. Statistical approaches for microbiome diversity, differential taxa, transcriptomic differential expression, and functional enrichment analyses are described in the corresponding sections above. All statistical analyses were performed in the R environment, and figures were mainly generated using the ggplot2 package. A P-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant, and a P-value of < 0.01 was considered highly significant.

Comparison of tissue sections in ducks of different ages

Representative images of HE-stained duodenal sections are shown in Fig. 2J. Compared with the YG, the EG showed significantly reduced duodenal villus length and crypt depth (P < 0.05, Fig. 2H). Figs. 2K-N present representative images of humerus sections stained with Goldner's trichrome stain. In both groups, the outer edge of the periosteal bone was clear and smooth. However, in the EG, the inner bone edge and the boundary with adjacent trabeculae were indistinct. Furthermore, more irregular erosion and demineralized areas were observed in the cortical bone regions of the EG compared to the YG, although no significant difference in cortical bone thickness was found between the two groups (P > 0.05, Fig. 2O).

Fig. 2P shows representative images of TUNEL assay and HE staining of uterine tissue. The TUNEL assay indicated no statistically significant difference in the level of apoptosis between the two groups (P > 0.05, Fig. 2I). HE staining revealed structurally intact uterine epithelium with tightly arranged cells in both groups. Notably, tissues from the EG exhibited significant interstitial edema, along with deeper hematoxylin staining of the nuclei. This observation suggests the presence of potential inflammatory pathological changes in the uterine tissue of the EG.

Bacterial analysis in ducks of different ages

Sequencing of 20 duodenal samples yielded a total of 994.18 million base pairs (bp) of raw data. After stringent quality filtering and removal of chimeric sequences, the average number of processed reads per sample was 97,141 (Supplementary Table S3). Rarefaction analysis (dilution curve in Fig. 3A) confirmed sequencing saturation across samples. Application of diversity indices (Ace, Chao1, Goods coverage, Shannon, Simpson, and Observed_features, Fig. 3E) revealed significant differences in the intestinal microbial community structure between the YG and the EG. Specifically, the YG showed significantly higher values in multiple indices reflecting species richness, including Chao1 (P < 0.01), Ace (P < 0.01), and Observed_features (P < 0.01). Additionally, the YG exhibited significantly higher values in comprehensive community diversity indices, Shannon (P < 0.001) and Simpson (P < 0.01). In contrast, the EG demonstrated higher sequencing coverage (Goods coverage, P < 0.01), suggesting a relatively simpler microbial community structure. Through taxonomic analysis, we identified the 30 most abundant and significantly different bacterial phyla and genera in the duck duodenal microbiota. Community structure analysis showed that Figs. 3B and 3C display the top 30 species in terms of abundance at the phylum and genus levels, respectively, while Figs. 3G and 3H present the top 30 most significantly different species at these taxonomic levels. Furthermore, based on SIMPER analysis (Fig. 3D), we identified the bacterial genera contributing most to the inter-group differences.

Fig. 3B shows that the composition at the phylum level was consistent between the EG and YG, but differences were observed at the genus level (Fig. 3C). At the phylum level, the most abundant phylum in the EG was Firmicutes (50.84%), followed by Campilobacterota (38.15%), Actinobacteriota (4.71%), and Proteobacteria (2.93%). The YG was also dominated by Firmicutes (59.48%), followed by Campilobacterota (16.24%), Actinobacteriota (9.88%), and Proteobacteria (6.65%). The relative abundance of Proteobacteria was significantly lower in the EG compared to the YG (P < 0.05). Additionally, the relative abundances of Cyanobacteria, Nitrospirota, Bdellovibrionota, and Chloroflexi were also significantly lower in the EG than in the YG (P < 0.05) (Fig. 3G).

At the genus level, the YG had the highest relative abundance of Bacillus (21.18%), followed by Streptococcus (13.23%), Helicobacter (12.37%), and Lactobacillus (10.43%). In contrast, the EG was dominated by Helicobacter (38.15%) and Lactobacillus (33.19%), followed by Enterococcus (9.15%) and Rothia (3.78%) (Fig. 3C). SIMPER analysis indicated (Fig. 3D) that the differences in microbial community structure between the two groups were primarily driven by Helicobacter (contribution 18.74%) and Lactobacillus (contribution 16.48%), followed by Bacillus (10.04%), Enterococcus (5.52%), and Streptococcus (5.38%). Further analysis revealed that 30 genera were significantly more abundant in the YG than in the EG (P < 0.05, Fig. 3H), including Bifidobacterium, Collinsella, Clostridium_sensu_stricto_1, Pediococcus, Weissella, Leuconostoc, as well as Lachnospiraceae_NK4A136_group and Rikenellaceae_RC9_gut_group, among others. Notably, although Bacillus and Streptococcus were high-abundance genera shared by both groups, their proportions were significantly higher in the YG. The raw genus-level taxonomic abundance data for each sample are provided in Supplementary Table S4. In summary, alpha diversity analysis indicated that the YG harbored higher microbial richness and diversity, whereas the EG exhibited a simpler community structure. Consistently, Fig. 3K showed that the majority of significantly differential genera had lower relative abundances in the EG than in the YG.

PCA revealed distinct structural differences in the intestinal microbial composition between EG and YG (Fig. 3F). LEfSe analysis further identified microbial biomarkers significantly different between the YG and EG across various taxonomic levels (LDA Score > 2.0). The results showed that the vast majority of biomarkers were enriched in the YG (Figs. 3I, J), involving multiple phyla including Firmicutes, Actinobacteriota, Proteobacteria, and Bacteroidota. The YG-specific biomarkers were distributed across multiple taxonomic levels and, at the genus level, mainly included Bacillus, Streptococcus, Clostridium_sensu_stricto_1, Bifidobacterium, and Collinsella. In contrast, the number of biomarkers enriched in the EG was very limited and was mainly concentrated in Lactobacillus under the order Lactobacillales within Firmicutes.

Fig. 3L displays the 30 KEGG functional pathways with the largest differences between the two groups. The results showed that multiple metabolic functions of the intestinal microbiota in EG were altered compared to the YG. Several key pathways, including Mineral absorption, Flavonoid biosynthesis, Insulin secretion, and the Estrogen signaling pathway, were significantly downregulated in the EG (P < 0.05). Meanwhile, the PI3K-Akt signaling pathway, associated with cell proliferation and survival, was one of the few upregulated pathways. As these functional profiles were predicted using Tax4Fun2 based on 16S rRNA gene sequencing data, they should be interpreted as predicted functional results.

Transcriptomic analysis in ducks of different ages

This study utilized the Illumina platform with PE150 sequencing mode to conduct high-throughput sequencing of 11 cDNA libraries, systematically analyzing transcriptomic differences in uterine tissues between the EG (6 samples, E1-E6) and the YG (5 samples, Y1-Y3, Y5-Y6; Y4 was excluded due to failing quality control, Supplementary Figures 1A) of Shanma ducks. The mapping rate remained stable between 94.58% and 95.44% (reference genome ASM874695v1, GCA_008746955.1). Gene expression distribution met standard quality control metrics (Supplementary Figures 1B, C). Based on the strictly filtered high-quality transcriptomic data, a total of 607 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified, comprising 250 upregulated and 357 downregulated genes (Fig. 4C). Analysis of key genes significantly associated with eggshell strength revealed that genes related to cell proliferation and signaling (FOXM1, KCNS2, GABRA1, SPON2) were consistently upregulated in the EG, while core genes involved in ion transport and tissue homeostasis (ATP1B1, FOXF1, SMAD9) were significantly downregulated. Functional enrichment analysis revealed that in KEGG pathways, the DEGs were significantly enriched in Cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction, cAMP signaling pathway, cGMP-PKG signaling pathway, Cell adhesion molecules, Purine metabolism, and Neuroactive ligand-receptor interaction, among others (Fig. 4D, Table S6). GO enrichment analysis results showed that for Biological Process (BP), terms were primarily enriched in homeostatic process, signal transduction, regulation of cell communication, hormone transport, and cell-cell adhesion. Cellular Component (CC) was significantly enriched in structures such as synaptic membrane, extracellular matrix, plasma membrane region, and secretory vesicle. Molecular Function (MF) was notably enriched in terms including calcium ion binding, extracellular matrix structural constituent, signaling receptor activity, and cytokine activity (Figs. 4F-H, Table S7).

GSEA further indicated significant alterations in core biological pathways within the eggshell gland transcriptome of EG: metabolic and signaling pathways such as Steroid hormone biosynthesis and the Calcium signaling pathway were significantly upregulated, whereas fundamental physiological processes like Ribosome and MAPK signaling pathway were enriched in the YG. Additionally, immune-related pathways such as Asthma and Allograft rejection were aberrantly activated in the EG (Fig. 4E).

Transcriptomic sequencing analysis revealed that youth ducks exhibited greater activity in protein synthesis, immune homeostasis, and cellular signal transduction, whereas elderly ducks displayed characteristics of hormonal metabolic disruption, dysregulated calcium signaling, and immune activation.

Correlation network between eggshell strength and multi-omics indicators

To systematically elucidate the key regulatory factors affecting eggshell strength, we integrated serum biochemical parameters, gut microbiota data, and uterine transcriptomic data to construct a correlation network between eggshell strength and multi-omics indicators (Fig. 4I). Analysis revealed that in the EG, eggshell strength showed a significant negative correlation with serum 25-OH-VD₃ levels (r = –0.68, P < 0.05), while it was positively correlated with the bone resorption marker TRAP-5b (r = 0.70, P < 0.05). Gut microbiota analysis further indicated a strong negative correlation between eggshell strength and the relative abundance of the genus Helicobacter (r = –0.90, P < 0.01). Uterine transcriptomic data revealed that eggshell strength was closely associated with the expression of several key genes: the expression of the potassium channel gene KCNS2 (r = –0.80, P < 0.01) and the neurotransmitter receptor gene GABRA1 (r = –0.78, P < 0.01) showed significant negative correlations with eggshell strength. Conversely, the transcription factor FOXF1 (r = 0.67, P < 0.05), involved in the calcium signaling pathway, and ATP1B1 (r = 0.65, P < 0.05), which maintains ion homeostasis, showed significant positive correlations with eggshell strength. Notably, in the YG, eggshell strength exhibited a strong positive correlation with the expression of SMAD9, a key transcription factor in the BMP signaling pathway (r = 0.94, P < 0.01), whereas this strong association was completely absent in the EG.

Discussion

Age-related deterioration of eggshell quality has been widely reported in poultry, but the specific structural and physiological changes may vary among species, breeds, and laying stages (Benavides-Reyes et al., 2021; Li et al., 2025). In the present study, elderly Shanma ducks showed reduced eggshell breaking strength and decreased effective layer thickness, whereas egg weight and egg shape index were not significantly altered. This result is generally consistent with the study of Zhang et al. (2022), who reported that commercial laying ducks at the late laying stage had weaker eggshells, reduced shell thickness, and altered shell gland gene expression compared with ducks at the peak laying stage. Similar reductions in shell quality with increasing age have also been observed in laying hens (Elhamouly et al., 2023). These findings suggest that the decline in eggshell mechanical performance during late production is a common phenomenon in poultry, although the underlying structural features may differ among experimental populations.

The eggshell ultrastructure results provide a basis for comparing Shanma ducks with previous studies in hens. In some laying hen studies, age-related shell deterioration has been associated with abnormalities in mammillary layer organization, reduced palisade layer thickness, and changes in shell mineral components (Park and Sohn, 2018). In the present study, however, the mammillary layer thickness was not significantly different between YG and EG, whereas the effective layer was reduced. This difference may indicate that the structural effect of aging in Shanma ducks is more evident in the growth and consolidation of the mineralized layer than in the initial formation of mammillary structures. However, because shell strength is influenced by multiple ultrastructural traits, including crystal orientation, mammillary cone density, effective thickness, and organic matrix distribution, this observation should be interpreted cautiously and verified in larger duck populations.

The relationship between shell ultrastructure and mechanical strength has been emphasized in studies of avian eggshell biomineralization. The avian eggshell is a composite biomaterial formed by calcium carbonate crystals and a minor but functionally important organic matrix. Matrix proteins can influence calcite crystal morphology, orientation, and organization, thereby contributing to shell mechanical properties (Nys et al., 2004). More recent reviews further indicate that eggshell mineralization depends on coordinated regulation of ion supply, uterine secretion, and matrix protein deposition (Gautron et al., 2021). In this context, the reduced effective layer thickness observed in elderly Shanma ducks is consistent with the general view that shell strength depends not only on total mineral deposition but also on the organization of mineralized layers. This interpretation is also supported by proteomic evidence from aged laying hens, where changes in eggshell matrix proteins were associated with age-related differences in eggshell quality and ultrastructure (Fu et al., 2025).

Calcium metabolism is central to eggshell formation, and previous studies have shown that age can affect intestinal calcium absorption, skeletal calcium mobilization, and shell gland mineral deposition. In laying hens, Al-batshan et al. (1994) reported that duodenal calcium uptake, femur ash, and eggshell quality declined with age, indicating that intestinal and skeletal factors are closely linked to shell quality. The present study showed lower serum 25-OH-VD3 and higher PTH, TRAP-5b, and BAP levels in elderly ducks. These changes are generally consistent with the concept that vitamin D metabolism and bone turnover are involved in maintaining calcium supply during eggshell formation. However, serum calcium and phosphorus levels did not differ significantly between age groups. This finding is not contradictory, because circulating calcium is tightly regulated and may remain relatively stable even when calcium absorption, bone remodeling, or uterine deposition is altered.

The bone-related results also correspond partly with studies in aged hens, but some differences should be noted. Fu et al. (2024) reported that decreased eggshell strength in aged laying hens was associated with impaired uterine calcium transport and changes in bone quality, emphasizing the interaction between skeletal metabolism and shell gland function. In the present study, elevated bone remodeling markers and histological signs of humeral erosion were observed in elderly ducks. These findings suggest that skeletal calcium metabolism was altered with age, but the present data do not allow a direct causal conclusion regarding whether bone changes were a cause or a consequence of reduced shell quality. Instead, they indicate that bone status should be considered together with intestinal absorption and uterine transport when evaluating eggshell quality in aged ducks.

Vitamin D status may be another relevant point of comparison. Sinclair-Black et al. (2023) summarized that calcium and phosphorus homeostasis in laying hens involves the coordinated actions of intestinal absorption, renal regulation, medullary bone, and shell gland mineralization. Nutritional studies have also shown that dietary calcium and 25-OH-VD3 can influence laying performance, serum biochemical indices, and mineral digestibility in late-laying hens (Lim and Ryu, 2022). Compared with these intervention studies, the present study is observational and does not test dietary regulation directly. Nevertheless, the lower 25-OH-VD3 level in elderly ducks is consistent with the broader literature indicating that vitamin D-related pathways are important for calcium utilization and eggshell formation during late production.

The intestinal histological changes observed in elderly ducks add another dimension to the discussion of calcium supply. Reduced villus length and crypt depth may indicate decreased absorptive surface area or altered epithelial renewal. Similar associations between intestinal morphology, nutrient absorption, and eggshell quality have been reported in studies evaluating dietary or probiotic interventions in laying hens. For example, (Wang et al., 2021a) found that Bacillus subtilis supplementation and calcium level affected eggshell quality, intestinal morphology, and calbindin-D28k expression in late-laying hens. Compared with that intervention study, the present work does not demonstrate that intestinal morphology directly caused shell quality decline, but it supports the view that intestinal condition is relevant when assessing eggshell quality in aged poultry.

The gut microbiota results are also broadly consistent with the emerging view that intestinal microbial communities are associated with egg quality and host metabolism. (Dai et al., 2022) reviewed the relationship between intestinal microbiota, egg quality, and egg safety in laying hens, emphasizing that microbiota may influence nutrient utilization, gut barrier function, and host immune status. In the present study, EG showed lower microbial richness and diversity, together with a simplified microbial structure. This pattern may indicate a less stable intestinal microbial ecosystem in aged (Deng et al., 2025). At the genus level, the enrichment of Helicobacter and Lactobacillus in EG, together with the higher abundance of Bacillus, Streptococcus, Bifidobacterium, and Clostridium_sensu_stricto_1 in YG, suggests that aging may be accompanied by a shift in dominant intestinal taxa. Such microbial changes may be related to differences in intestinal nutrient utilization, mucosal homeostasis, and mineral absorption capacity, thereby providing a potential microbial context for the age-related decline in eggshell quality.

The association between microbial composition and eggshell quality is also supported by studies involving microbial or environmental modulation. For example, probiotic supplementation has been shown to affect laying performance, egg quality, immune function, and intestinal microbiota composition in laying hens (Xu et al., 2023). In aged hens, a 28-h ahemeral light cycle was reported to improve eggshell quality in parallel with changes in gut microbiota and metabolic rhythms (Xu et al., 2025). These findings suggest that intestinal microbial communities may be involved in host physiological processes related to eggshell formation. In the present study, the predicted reduction of mineral absorption-related microbial pathways in elderly ducks further indicates that age-associated microbial shifts may be linked to changes in mineral utilization. Together with the observed differences in microbial diversity and dominant taxa, these results provide a microbial perspective for understanding the decline in eggshell quality during the late laying stage.

At the uterine level, the present transcriptomic results are consistent with previous evidence that the shell gland undergoes molecular changes during aging. (Feng et al., 2020) reported that aged laying hens exhibited changes in uterine gene expression associated with eggshell ultrastructural differences, including pathways related to inflammation, ion transport, and extracellular matrix regulation. In the present study, differentially expressed genes were enriched in calcium signaling, steroid hormone biosynthesis, cytokine-related pathways, and extracellular matrix-related functions. These similarities indicate that the aged uterus may show changes in both mineral transport and tissue homeostasis across poultry species. However, differences between ducks and hens should also be considered, as reproductive rhythm, shell formation duration, and breed-specific genetic background may influence shell gland transcriptomic profiles.

The involvement of uterine ion transport is supported by several previous studies. RNA sequencing-based analysis of the laying hen uterus has shown that eggshell calcification involves molecular processes related to secretion, transport, and biomineralization of calcium carbonate (Sah et al., 2018). More recently, (Yang et al., 2025) reported that hens laying high- and low-strength eggs differed in uterine calcium transport-related proteins and metabolites during shell calcification. In the present study, ATP1B1 and FOXF1 were downregulated in elderly ducks and were positively correlated with eggshell strength. These genes may be related to ion homeostasis and tissue regulation, respectively, but the present data remain correlative. Therefore, these genes may represent potential molecular links between uterine ion homeostasis and eggshell strength in Shanma ducks.

The enrichment of calcium signaling and steroid hormone biosynthesis pathways in elderly ducks should be discussed with caution. Pathway enrichment may reflect altered cellular status, compensatory responses, or changes in tissue composition, and it does not necessarily indicate improved mineralization capacity. Similar caution has been raised in transcriptomic studies of shell gland function, where activation of stress- or immune-related pathways may coexist with poorer eggshell ultrastructure (Feng et al., 2020). The uterine interstitial edema observed in elderly ducks is consistent with the possibility of altered tissue status, but further histological, protein-level, and functional experiments are needed to clarify whether these transcriptomic changes are adaptive or detrimental.

The age-dependent relationship between SMAD9 and eggshell strength is a notable observation. SMAD9 is a downstream component of BMP signaling and has been reported to participate in avian reproductive regulation. (Yu et al., 2019) suggested that SMAD9 may be involved in goose follicular selection through regulation of luteinizing hormone receptor expression. Another study identified SMAD9 as a candidate gene involved in goose follicular initiation (Xu et al., 2015). In the present study, SMAD9 was strongly correlated with eggshell strength in YG, whereas this relationship was absent in elderly ducks. This finding suggests that the association between BMP-related signaling and eggshell traits may change with age.

Overall, the present study agrees with earlier research showing that age-related eggshell quality decline is associated with shell ultrastructure, calcium metabolism, intestinal function, skeletal remodeling, and uterine molecular regulation. Its main contribution is the integration of these factors in Shanma ducks, a Chinese indigenous laying duck breed. Compared with studies focusing on a single tissue or pathway, the combined analysis of eggshell structure, serum biochemical indices, tissue morphology, gut microbiota, and uterine transcriptome provides a broader description of age-associated changes during late laying. At the same time, this integrative design also requires careful interpretation, because associations across multiple biological layers do not establish causal relationships.

Conclusion

By integrating multi-omics data encompassing eggshell ultrastructure, calcium metabolism indices, gut microbiota profiles, and uterine transcriptomes, this study systematically elucidates the cascade regulatory mechanism underlying age-dependent decline in eggshell quality in Shanma ducks. We demonstrate that elderly ducks exhibit significantly reduced gut microbiota diversity, wherein Helicobacter overproliferation triggers intestinal structural damage and functional impairment, ultimately diminishing calcium absorption capacity. Concurrently, decreased serum 25-OH-VD₃ levels and compensatory PTH elevation drive high-turnover bone remodeling, resulting in bone matrix erosion. At the uterine level, downregulation of key calcium transport genes (FOXF1, ATP1B1) coupled with dysfunctional BMP-SMAD9 signaling collectively impair transmembrane calcium transport and biomineralization.

This comprehensive regulatory network from gut microbiome to eggshell phenotype reveals the multidimensional mechanisms driving eggshell quality deterioration during late-laying stages. Critically, it identifies multi-tiered intervention targets for improving eggshell quality in elderly egg-laying ducks. Future research should focus on precision nutrition strategies and genetic improvement programs targeting three pivotal nodes: gut microbiota modulation, calcium homeostasis restoration, and uterine functional preservation. Such approaches will enhance economic efficiency and food safety in late-laying production systems, while offering novel insights for research on aging regulation of poultry reproductive performance.
