Evaluation of laser Doppler vibrometer’s performance by a multimode laser and changeable reference arm length, equipped with an auto-focus system
Authors: Farshid Seydipour, Fatemeh Rezaei
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
In the present study, the theoretical framework underlying interferometric measurement using multimode lasers—forming the basis of laser vibrometry—is comprehensively described. A numerical simulation of the Doppler signal for the Laser Doppler Vibrometer (LDV) is conducted, and the technique for extracting vibration velocity via zero-crossing counting is detailed and subsequently verified through experimental assessment. In addition, two critical optical challenges in this field, namely the determination of the optimal object distances and laser focusing, are addressed. To overcome these issues, a novel set-up is proposed that incorporates systematic adjustments of the reference arm length along with active auto-focusing, facilitated by a laser rangefinder. Moreover, to validate the performance of the proposed device and facilitate comparison with conventional contact accelerometers, several frequency response function (FRF) tests were conducted under different conditions. The test cases included laser measurements when the accelerometer was not mounted on the plate, measurements obtained directly from the accelerometer, laser measurements when the beam was directed onto the accelerometer, and laser measurements when the beam was directed onto the plate. Comparative analysis of the data acquired via the accelerometer and the LDV confirmed the accuracy and reliability of the LDV set-up. Moreover, the measurements revealed that mass loading from the accelerometer caused detectable shifts in the natural frequencies of the 1-mm-thick galvanized plate—shifts that typically require a correction step often treated as modal analysis noise in the literature. This underscores the advantage of LDV’s non-contact nature in preserving the structure’s true vibrational characteristics of the sample and eliminates the need for such corrective procedures.

Introduction

The Laser Doppler Vibrometer (LDV), recognized as a powerful tool in vibration measurement, offers numerous advantages over traditional methods. Unlike accelerometers, the LDV employs a non-contact technique that eliminates factors such as mass loading effects, allowing for precise measurements without altering the dynamic properties of the test object. These devices have extensive applications in fields such as aerospace1–3, automotive3–8, architecture9,10, and rail11–13 for vibration testing and velocity measurement. Moreover, it is utilized in biology to measure eardrum14–18 and heart displacements19–21; in acoustics for characterizing loudspeakers22–24 and microphones25, as well as musical instruments such as violins26 and guitars27; in the military for mine detection28; in security for remote surveillance29–31 and in calibration for calibrating instruments such as accelerometers32,33. LDV operates by directing a laser beam onto a vibrating object and analyzing the reflected beam. According to the Doppler effect, the frequency of the reflected beam changes in proportion to the velocity of the moving object. By interfering this reflected beam with a reference beam and processing the resulting interference wave, the vibration characteristics of the object, such as velocity and displacement, can be accurately determined.

Laser-Doppler techniques emerged shortly after the first laser demonstration in 196034, leading to the development of Doppler anemometry in the 1960s35. LDV was introduced for vibrational analysis in the late 1960s36,37, followed by advancements in fiber-based and in-plane vibrometers during the 1980s38–40. By the 1990s, scanning LDV systems became commercially available for vibration analysis31,41,42, though multi-point LDV was needed for transient vibrations43–47. Recently, multi-point LDV capable of measuring multiple dimensions has been developed48, expanding applications across various fields31,49. Despite the numerous advantages of LDV, including its precision, measurement speed, frequency range, non-contact nature, and remote capability, certain challenges must be addressed for optimal measurement. These include the laser spot diameter on the target and the distance between the object and the device, both of which require optimization. For instance, Qu et al.29 introduced an active autofocus system using a camera alongside an LDV device, employing triangulation-based distance measurement50 to achieve fast focusing and an optimal laser spot diameter. However, one drawback of the triangulation method is its limited maximum measurable distance. In addition, Gatzwiller et al.51 discussed challenges associated with LDV devices utilizing multimode lasers and proposes a solution where either the test object or the device is manually repositioned to achieve the optimal measurement distance. The root of this problem lies in the coherence length of multimode lasers, which is significantly shorter than that of single-mode lasers. Given LDV’s widespread applications, manual repositioning is not a comprehensive or practical solution, as it can be cumbersome, time-consuming, or even infeasible in certain scenarios. Nevertheless, many commercial LDV systems currently available on the market, such as those produced by Polytec and Ometron, employ multimode lasers, primarily due to their relatively lower cost. Manufacturers of these devices typically recommend manual adjustment of the distance between the device and the object. However, this manual approach becomes particularly time-consuming and challenging in large-scale applications, such as vibration testing of automobile bodies, buildings, or bridges, where multiple points at varying distances must be scanned. In such cases, the vibrometer may need to be repositioned several times.

In this study, a novel configuration is proposed in which the optical path difference between the reference and measurement arms is automatically adjusted according to the object distance. This is achieved by varying the reference arm length using a motorized mechanism, thereby ensuring the optimal path difference without requiring manual repositioning of either the object or the device. The proposed setup has been constructed as a compact, integrated system. To validate its performance, vibration and modal analysis tests were conducted on a galvanized plate in a vibration laboratory, and the results were compared with those obtained from a contact accelerometer. The strong agreement between the two sets of results confirms the accuracy of the proposed device and its potential applicability in industrial vibration measurement. The structure of this paper is as follows: first, a review of the mathematical principles underlying LDV is presented, with emphasis on laser spectral width, spectral profile, and mode structure. A simulation of the Doppler signal is then performed, from which the vibration velocity of the object is extracted. Two critical challenges in LDV, namely laser focusing and the optimal object-to-device distance, are discussed, and the proposed setup is introduced as a solution to these issues. Furthermore, vibration velocity is experimentally measured using the proposed configuration, and the device’s performance is ultimately compared with that of a contact accelerometer in the frequency domain, both to verify its accuracy in vibration measurement and to highlight the inherent advantages of laser vibrometers over contact-based accelerometers.

Materials and methods

LDV experimental set-up

In laser Doppler vibrometers (LDVs), the incident laser beam on a moving object experiences a phase shift upon reflection, a phenomenon referred to as the Doppler effect. Since this phase shift is proportional to the object’s velocity and vibration, it is possible to derive the object’s velocity by applying a series of processing steps to the reflected beam. However, since the lasers employed in LDVs operate at frequencies around terahertz, directly detecting their phase shift with a photodetector alone is highly challenging. Optical interferometers such as Michelson and Mach–Zehnder are employed to address this. In these interferometers, the general process involves splitting the output beam from a coherent laser source with frequency \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\nu$$\end{document} into two beams: a reference beam and a measurement beam. The measurement beam, after being reflected from the object, interferes with the reference beam at the detector. By analyzing the beat frequency generated by this interference, the phase variations of the reflected beam can be extracted.

In this study, a Michelson interferometer was employed for measuring vibration velocity due to its simple set-up. Figure 1 illustrates a typical schematic of a Michelson interferometer configuration, where the incident laser beam is divided into reference and measurement beams by a beam splitter. Typically, this beam splitter is a polarizing beam splitter (PBS), which, when combined with quarter-wave plates (QWPs) in the reference and measurement arms, prevents back-reflection into the laser and directs the signal toward the detector. Placing wave plates in front of the mirror and the object ensures that the polarization of the incident light rotates by 90 degrees after reflection and passing through the plates again. As a result, the interfering beams at the detector possess orthogonal polarizations. To facilitate interference, a polarizer set at angle of about 45 degrees to the polarization of the two interfering beams is positioned in front of the detector.Fig. 1Optical set-up of the Michelson interferometer.

In this research, a 1 mW multimode helium–neon laser with a wavelength of 632.8 nm and a mode spacing of 730 MHz was employed in a Michelson interferometry set-up for vibrometry of a galvanized sheet measuring 20 × 20 cm with a thickness of 1 mm. Various methods exist for extracting velocity, which can be classified into analog and digital approaches. In analog methods, electronic hardware components such as frequency-to-voltage converters, phase-locked loops, and fringe counters are utilized. In digital methods, the signal is initially converted into digital data using an analog-to-digital converter (ADC). This data is then sent to a computer or processing board, where algorithms are employed to extract velocity. Among the digital velocity extraction methods, the inverse tangent method and the fringe-counting method are notable41,52. The inverse tangent method can be utilized in both homodyne and heterodyne set-ups, while the fringe-counting method is applicable to the configuration employed in this study. In this method, velocity or displacement is determined by counting the zero crossings of the raw signal.

Multi-mode lasers in LDV experiment

In LDV systems, the multi-mode helium–neon lasers are commonly used. However, the coherence length of these lasers (typically less than 20 cm) is significantly shorter than that of single-mode lasers, which can extend to several hundred meters. Generally, multi-mode lasers are preferred in commercial systems due to their lower cost and higher power output. The primary challenge arises from the difference in the optical path lengths traveled by the reference and measurement beams, which should not be large enough to reduce the interference signal strength significantly. This paper investigates the theory and mathematics of interferometry using both single-mode and multi-mode lasers, considering the laser’s frequency bandwidth, and presents numerical simulations of the interference signal in the vibration condition of the moving object, along with the method for extracting vibration velocity from it. To address this challenge, a Michelson interferometer-based set-up is proposed, where the reference mirror is adjustable over the length of the laser cavity, and a laser rangefinder is employed. The method operates as follows: the distance to the object is first measured using the rangefinder, and according to this distance, the reference mirror is displaced by a specific amount, ensuring the maximum signal is achieved at that particular distance. Additionally, with the use of the laser rangefinder and an optical system with an adjustable lens, the set-up is equipped with an active autofocus system that can be employed to enhance the received signal magnitude. To validate the device’s performance and compare it with contact accelerometers, multiple stages of FRF testing were conducted under varying conditions. The test cases included laser measurements when the accelerometer was not mounted on the plate, measurements obtained directly from the accelerometer, laser measurements when the beam was directed onto the accelerometer, and laser measurements when the beam was directed onto the plate.

The solution

In this paper, an automatic solution is proposed to address the above-mentioned problem. Instead of changing the distance between the object and the device (which is the measuring arm length), the reference arm length is modified. The schematic of the setup is shown in Fig. 8. One of the differences of this setup compared to the standard Michelson configuration used for LDV is that, with the help of motor1, the moving mirror is capable of shifting along a rail, allowing for the adjustment of the reference arm length. The purpose of the fixed mirror is to reduce the size of the device by splitting the reference beam.Fig. 8Schematic of the experimental set-up used in this study.

Schematic of the experimental set-up used in this study.

The operation proceeds as follows: the distance to the object is measured by a Laser Range Finder (LRF), and depending on the measured distance, the moving mirror is adjusted to change the optical path difference so that it becomes an even integer multiple of the laser cavity length. This adjustment can be made up to the length of the laser cavity. A Dichroic Mirror (DM) is used in this setup to separate the laser wavelength from the range finder.

Auto-focus

Autofocus automatically adjusts the focal distance of the laser beam to ensure the optimal signal is obtained. This feature is especially important when working with dynamic and complex measurement environments, where manual focusing can be time-consuming and requires a skilled operator to achieve the best result. The larger the spot size on the object, the less Doppler signal is received by the optical system. If the reflection detected by the device is below a certain threshold, it can lead to drop-outs in the vibration velocity signal. It is undoubtedly preferable for the focus to be performed automatically rather than manually. To perform a fast and accurate autofocus, the distance to the object needs to be known, which is made possible by using a laser range finder in the setup of this paper. Thus, only the lens distances need to be adjusted.

Laser focusing is one of the initial steps in optical preparation for laser vibrometry. In the proposed setup, this process is performed using an active autofocus system assisted by a laser rangefinder. The procedure starts by measuring the object’s distance from the system using the rangefinder. Then, Motor 2 adjusts a movable lens to achieve an initial approximate focus on the object. To further refine the focus, a scanning process is conducted around this initial focal position. The scanning range is determined by the accuracy of the rangefinder measurement. Conclusion

In this study, the theoretical framework of laser-based interferometry using a multi-mode laser, which employed laser Doppler vibrometry (LDV), was presented. A numerical simulation of the Doppler signal for LDV had been conducted. The methodology for extracting vibration velocity using the zero-crossing counting technique was outlined, and this procedure was also performed practically and experimentally. Two significant optical challenges in LDV systems, optimal object distance and laser focusing, were addressed. A novel set-up was proposed to mitigate these issues by dynamically adjusting the reference arm length and utilizing an active autofocus system integrated with a laser rangefinder. A key innovation of this study lies in the design of the optical setup, which dynamically adjusts the optical path difference between the reference and measurement arms based on the distance of the vibrating object—measured using a laser rangefinder, to achieve maximum interference signal strength. This adjustment is performed entirely automatically by modifying the length of the reference arm via a stepper motor. Experimental data obtained from a conventional LDV setup confirm that Doppler signal strength exhibits not only an inverse relationship with distance but also a periodic variation, governed by the laser cavity length, an effect consistent with the theoretical framework presented in this work. In contrast, this periodic behavior is not observed in measurements taken using the proposed setup. As a result, the new configuration demonstrates superior performance, particularly at critical object-to-sensor distances, by significantly reducing noise in the velocity signal. To verify the performance of the proposed device, FRF tests were carried out under different conditions using both the device and a contact accelerometer, and the results were compared. Measurements were conducted using the laser without the accelerometer mounted on the plate, using the accelerometer alone, and with the laser directed onto both the accelerometer and the plate. A comparison of accelerometer and laser data, when the laser was directed onto the accelerometer, revealed strong agreement in the FRF results and in the identified natural frequencies from both devices, confirming the correct operation of the proposed system. Furthermore, comparison of accelerometer data with laser measurements prior to mounting the accelerometer on the specimen revealed that the added mass of the accelerometer significantly altered the natural frequencies. This observation is fully consistent with the expected behavior of LDV as a non-contact vibration measurement technique, which does not alter the dynamic properties of the specimen. Overall, the vibration tests confirm both the accuracy and the practical applicability of the proposed configuration for vibration measurement.
