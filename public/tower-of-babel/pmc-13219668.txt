Point-of-Care Nucleic Acid Amplification Tests for Respiratory Syncytial Virus Detection in Children and Adults: A Systematic Literature Review
Authors: Kashmira Date, Kyle Fowler, Mary Lynn Baniecki, Katherine K Perez, Sima S Toussi, Elaine Thomas, Katherine T Schneider, Ornella Ruiz, Suzie Seabroke, Konstantina Chatzikonstantinidou, Laura E Sarabia, Bradford Gessner, Elizabeth Begier
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionRapid point-of-care (PoC) nucleic acid amplification tests (NAATs), including reverse-transcriptase polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR), provide timely detection of respiratory syncytial virus (RSV) closer to patient care. We conducted a systematic review to summarize the characteristics and performance of PoC NAATs for RSV in comparison to standard laboratory RT-PCR diagnostics.MethodsWe systematically searched Embase, MEDLINE, and gray literature (2018–2024) for studies evaluating PoC RT-PCR and other NAATs for RSV, without language restrictions. Eligible studies were identified, and data on test characteristics and performance were extracted (PROSPERO-CRD42024611164).ResultsOf 3939 articles identified, 74 studies were included, evaluating 39 distinct PoC assays, comprising 18 RT-PCR-based platforms and 21 other NAAT methods (e.g., loop-mediated isothermal amplification). Only 8% of studies were conducted exclusively in adults and 18% in children, while 37% included both age groups; age was not specified in the rest. Most studies originated from the Western Pacific region (n = 29, 39%), Europe (n = 22, 30%), and the Americas (n = 19, 26%). Compared to laboratory-based RT-PCR, PoC NAATs had high specificity (median 100% in all age groups) but variable sensitivity. Median sensitivities were 96% (range 73–100%) in children, 91% (63–100%) in adults, and 99% (67–100%) in mixed-age groups.ConclusionsPoC RSV NAATs offer high specificity, with a modest sensitivity reduction compared with laboratory RT-PCR (notably in adult populations). Wider availability of reliable, affordable PoC testing could facilitate rapid and accurate RSV diagnosis, improve disease surveillance and patient management, and guide public health interventions such as vaccination.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s40121-026-01356-x.Keywords: Diagnostic tests, Nucleic acid amplification techniques, Point-of-care systems, Respiratory syncytial viruses, Respiratory tract infections

Key Summary Points

Point-of-care (PoC) assays could improve testing frequency and thus study efficiency when evaluating new respiratory syncytial virus (RSV) therapeutics and preventative measures such as vaccines.Many PoC molecular tests for RSV detection are currently available for both adults and children.The turnaround time from sample to results of PoC tests ranges between 13 and 150 min in most studies.PoC RSV nucleic acid amplification tests offer high specificity, with a modest sensitivity reduction compared with laboratory reverse-transcriptase polymerase chain reaction (notably in adult populations).

Introduction

Respiratory syncytial virus (RSV) is a leading cause of severe lower respiratory tract disease worldwide [1]. However, RSV testing has historically been underutilized, resulting in underestimation of the true disease burden. In the absence of specific antiviral therapy, clinicians often do not prioritize confirmation of RSV infection [2]. Routine diagnostic testing for RSV has been uncommon [3], although its use is now increasing with growing awareness of RSV’s impact [4].

Conventional methods to identify RSV include viral culture, rapid antigen detection tests (RADT), immunofluorescence tests, and molecular techniques such as reverse-transcriptase polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR) [5, 6]. RT-PCR is the most sensitive and serves as the reference standard for confirming RSV infection [7–9]. Yet laboratory RT-PCR is time-consuming, requires specialized facilities and expertise, and involves multiple steps for sample collection, transportation to a laboratory, and processing of samples [8, 10], often causing delays in results. In contrast, point-of-care (PoC) testing platforms can be used near the patient, delivering rapid results, potentially improving clinical decision-making [10, 11]. Until recently, most PoC RSV tests were antigen-based. These RADTs provide moderate sensitivity in children (81%, 95% confidence interval [CI] 78–84) but poor sensitivity in adults (29% [95% CI 11–48]) [9]. Newer PoC assays employing nucleic acid amplification tests (NAATs), including portable RT-PCR and isothermal amplification methods, have demonstrated improved sensitivity in both pediatric and adult populations [12–15], outperforming RADTs [16].

Accurate and accessible RSV diagnostics are essential for accurate disease burden estimations, guiding patient care, and improving public health responses [17–22]. RSV vaccines and monoclonal antibodies have recently been approved and introduced [17, 23–25], and investigational antivirals are under development [26–28]. These underscore the need for timely identification of RSV infections to inform treatment decisions, surveillance, and assessments of intervention effectiveness and impact. To address this, we conducted a systematic literature review of PoC RT-PCR and other NAAT-based tests for RSV. Our goal was to identify currently available PoC molecular diagnostics and summarize their operational characteristics and performance in detecting RSV, to inform clinical practice and future research in the era of emerging RSV therapeutics and prevention strategies.

Methods

Study Design

We followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Review and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines [29]. The protocol was registered in PROSPERO (CRD42024611164) [30]. We included primary studies of RSV PoC diagnostics meeting these criteria: (1) the study evaluated a PoC test (“index test”) against a reference standard applied to the same individuals (traditional RT-PCR for adults; RT-PCR or other diagnostic test for children); (2) at least two specimen types or diagnostic methods were compared; and (3) data were reported for all age groups or specific age subsets. We classified a device as PoC if the turnaround time (TAT) was ≤ 1 h, except for certain cartridge-based systems (Panther Fusion®, Hologic; FilmArray®, BioFire; Simplexa™, DiaSorin; Aries®, Luminex) commonly used near-patient, despite longer runs. No language or geographic restrictions were applied. We defined children as < 18 years, adults as ≥ 18 years, and mixed-age as studies including both or unspecified ages. The eligibility criteria are listed in Table S1.

Search Strategy

We searched MEDLINE (via PubMed) and Embase for relevant publications from 2018 through 2024. To capture gray literature, we also searched conference abstract archives and preprint servers (in Embase, 2023–2024). In addition, we manually checked reference lists of relevant systematic reviews (results of snowball search included in Table S2) and the European Medicines Agency (EMA) and US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) websites [31, 32] for newly reported RSV PoC tests. The full search strategy is provided in Table S3.

Study Selection and Data Extraction

Two reviewers independently screened all titles and abstracts against the inclusion criteria. Potentially eligible reports then underwent full-text review. From each included study, one reviewer extracted data using a standardized form (developed in DistillerSR [33]), and a second reviewer independently verified a 30% sample for accuracy (Table S4). We recorded study characteristics (setting, design, sample size, population) and test performance data (e.g., number of RSV detections by each method).

Risk of Bias Assessment

One reviewer assessed the risk of bias (RoB) for each study using the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) critical appraisal checklists [34], with a second reviewer independently verifying 30% of assessments. We rated each study’s risk as low, moderate, or high, emphasizing key indicators of methodological rigor (Table S5A).

Data Analysis

We summarized study characteristics with descriptive statistics. For each PoC assay, we compiled the range of performance measures across studies. If a study did not explicitly report sensitivity or specificity but provided raw positive/negative results, we calculated these metrics and 95% CI (exact Clopper–Pearson method) [35]. Notably, some studies used “positive percent agreement” and “negative percent agreement” in lieu of sensitivity and specificity; we treated these equivalently. We also calculated summary measures (mean, median, standard deviation, interquartile range) for key parameters such as TAT and test performance stratified by population type (pediatric, adult, mixed). All analyses were conducted using Microsoft Excel and R v4.4.1.

Ethical Approval

This article is based on previously conducted studies and does not contain any new studies with human participants or animals performed by any of the authors.

Assay Characteristics

A total of 39 distinct PoC NAAT assays were evaluated across the 74 studies. These included 18 RT-PCR platforms and 21 assays using isothermal or other molecular methods (e.g., seven transcription-mediated amplification, three loop-mediated isothermal amplification [LAMP], one CRISPR-based; ten, other NAATs, Table S9). The most frequently studied platforms were the Cepheid Xpert® Xpress tests (Flu/RSV and SARS-CoV-2/Flu/RSV; 20 studies), Roche cobas® Liat® (11 studies), Hologic Panther Fusion (10 studies), BioFire FilmArray respiratory panels (7 studies), and DiaSorin Simplexa (5 studies). Together, these five platforms accounted for approximately 60% of the included studies. All were used in both pediatric and adult populations. Other PoC tests evaluated included the Alere i® (currently marketed as Abbott ID NOW™), Luminex Aries, and various in-house NAATs (Table 2).

Assay complexity was recorded as per the reported FDA Clinical Laboratory Improvement Amendments (CLIA) categories. Waived, low-complexity tests require minimal knowledge and training, use prepackaged/premeasured materials, and require minimal interpretation. High-complexity tests require specialized training and handling of reagents/specimens [36]. The Xpert Xpress Flu/RSV and SARS-CoV-2/Flu/RSV assays, as well as the cobas Liat platform, were most frequently reported with TATs of ≤ 1 h and CLIA waived. In contrast, the Simplexa RSV/FLU A/B assay exhibited TATs ranging from 70 to 120 min and is classified as a moderate-complexity test. The Panther Fusion and FilmArray Respiratory and Pneumonia Panels, which can detect multiple respiratory pathogens, demonstrated longer TATs of up to 156 min and are categorized as high- or moderate-complexity assays.

Diagnostic Performance by Age Group

Fourteen studies focused on pediatric populations (Table S10), 9 on adults (Table S11), and 54 on mixed-age groups (Table S12); some mixed-age studies did not report age-specific results.

Overall, pediatric studies reported a median sensitivity of 96% (range 73–100%), mixed-population studies 99% (67–100%), and adult studies 91% (63–100%). Specificity of PoC NAATs for RSV was uniformly high when compared with laboratory RT-PCR, with median specificity 100% in children, adults, and mixed populations. Among the seven most-studied PoC assays, sensitivity varied by age group and test platform, while specificity showed similar patterns, consistently remaining ≥ 97.5% in children (Table 2). Several multi-pathogen tests exhibited lower sensitivity in adults than in children. For example, the Cepheid Xpert Xpress Flu/RSV assay had a median sensitivity of approximately 84% in adults vs. approximately 98% in children, and the BioFire FilmArray Respiratory Panel similarly approximately 72% vs. approximately 94%. In contrast, other assays such as the cobas Liat, Panther Fusion, and Alere i (currently ID NOW) maintained high sensitivity (approx. 95–100%) across all age groups. Table 2 summarizes the performance of key PoC tests by age group.

Performance by Specimen Type

Several studies compared PoC test performance across different specimen types (e.g., nasopharyngeal swabs [NPS] vs. nasopharyngeal aspirates [NPA] vs. nasal swabs [NS] vs. bronchoalveolar lavage [BAL]). Overall, RSV NAAT performance was similar for upper respiratory specimens, with some exceptions (Table 3). One pediatric study of a LAMP-based PoC test (Simprova) found lower sensitivity on NPA samples (approx. 84%) than on NPS/NS swabs (approx. 93%), while specificity remained 100% for all sample types [37]. Another evaluation of the Alere i (currently ID NOW) test in children showed high sensitivity (approx. 98.5%) for both direct NPS and viral transport medium (VTM) NPS, with specificity of approximately 97–98% for both; in adults, the same test yielded 100% sensitivity and specificity on NPS (with and without VTM) [38]. Among mixed-age studies, a comparison of the BioFire Pneumonia Panel using paired samples reported 100% RSV sensitivity on both sputum and BAL, with specificity of approximately 99–100% [39]. A UK study of the Xpert Xpress Flu/RSV assay found that NS gave higher sensitivity (93%) than throat swabs (72%), while NPA were also highly sensitive (96%); specificity exceeded 95% for all sample types [40]. Similarly, a US study reported the Xpert Xpress assay achieved approximately 98% sensitivity with both NS and NPS, with > 98% specificity for each [41]. Cost and Operational Impact

Thirteen studies reported data on testing costs or downstream healthcare costs related to RSV PoC testing (five in pediatric settings). Overall, these analyses suggest that implementing rapid PoC testing can reduce healthcare utilization costs by shortening unnecessary isolation or hospital stays. For example, a UK pediatric hospital study found that using the cobas Liat RSV/Flu PoC test to guide isolation reduced average isolation time by 0.47 days per patient, translating to an estimated £57,000 savings per 1000 patients compared to standard laboratory testing [42]. In an adult study from the Netherlands, each PoC test (cobas Liat) cost about €90, but its use reduced average hospital costs per patient from €5243 (no PoC testing) to €4904 (with PoC for in-hospital cohorting only) or €4206 (PoC affecting both cohorting and earlier discharge), largely by shortening length of stay and avoiding redundant isolation [43]. Reported reagent costs for PoC NAATs varied by setting. Two pediatric studies evaluating LAMP-based PoC assays reported low per-test costs (US $2–4) for consumables. Other pediatric studies of experimental NAAT devices reported costs around $2.6 (excluding RNA extraction) to $4.3 (including all reagents) per test. In contrast, cartridge-based commercial tests in high-income settings were costlier: the Cepheid Xpert Xpress RSV/Flu assay was reported at approximately $31–68 per test in several studies (and £37 in one UK study) [44]. Discussion

This systematic review provides a comprehensive assessment of PoC NAATs for RSV across pediatric, adult, and mixed-age populations. Overall, we found that the specificity of PoC assays was consistently high (often approx. 100%) across all platforms and age groups, whereas sensitivity varied notably by test and population. In pediatric settings, most PoC NAATs approached the sensitivity of laboratory RT-PCR, but in adults, some assays yielded lower sensitivity.

The reduced sensitivity in adults likely reflects differences in RSV infection dynamics between age groups. Adults typically have lower nasopharyngeal viral loads and often present later in the illness course, making virus detection more challenging [45]. Other factors, such as drier nasal mucosa and suboptimal sampling, can further diminish test yield in adults [45, 46]. In contrast, children with RSV infection usually shed higher concentrations of virus for longer durations [47, 48], and their smaller airways and acute symptoms facilitate virus capture from a single swab [5]. These biological and practical differences explain why certain PoC tests performed better in children than in adults; laboratory RT-PCR (especially singleplex assays) remains the most sensitive diagnostic for adult RSV [7]. Notably, some PoC NAAT platforms in our review maintained excellent sensitivity even in adults, indicating that assay design and target selection can partly overcome these challenges. For example, several multiplex PCR panels had a more pronounced drop in adult sensitivity (e.g., Cepheid Xpert Xpress Flu/RSV), whereas the Roche cobas Liat and Hologic Panther Fusion systems both reported > 95% sensitivity in adult populations. This variability may reflect the challenge of detecting multiple pathogens when the RSV viral load is low.

Furthermore, the use of alternative or multiple specimen types can improve NAAT performance. One study in our review found that adding an oropharyngeal swab to the standard nasopharyngeal sample significantly increased RSV sensitivity in adults [49]. Likewise, PoC assays capable of using lower respiratory specimens (like sputum or BAL) achieved a sensitivity near 100% in studies of severe infections. These findings suggest that optimizing sampling (e.g., combined nasal/throat swabs for older patients or appropriate suction samples for young children) may improve PoC test sensitivity, especially in adults.

In children, the high accuracy of PoC NAATs means they can reliably guide decisions such as patient cohorting, hospital admission, or antiviral research enrollment. Rapid confirmation of RSV in a child can prevent unnecessary antibiotic use and help target isolation resources during seasonal surges. In adults, PoC NAATs still offer crucial time savings that can inform early decisions on cohorting and downstream testing. However, clinicians should be mindful of the slightly higher false-negative rate in adults. If an adult patient tests negative on a PoC NAAT but clinical suspicion for RSV is strong (e.g., typical symptoms in an outbreak setting), a confirmatory laboratory PCR or repeat testing is advisable. This nuanced use of PoC results can maximize their benefit while mitigating the risk of missed diagnoses.

The TAT of PoC tests in our review ranged from approximately 15 min (for some isothermal NAATs such as Alere i) up to approximately 2.5 h (for complex multiplex platforms such as BioFire FilmArray). Faster assays often correlate with simpler technology and CLIA-waived status, whereas longer TATs are seen with high-complexity multi-pathogen devices. An ideal PoC test balances speed with sensitivity. In practice, even a 1–2 h result is a great improvement over send-out PCR, but ultra-rapid assays can further enhance PoC utility in settings such as emergency departments. We did not identify truly hand-held molecular tests in this review (all platforms still require a bench-top analyzer). In late 2025, the FDA cleared the first over-the-counter 4-in-1 home test that detects RSV, influenza A/B, and COVID-19 [50]. This lateral-flow antigen kit (Flowflex® Plus, ACON Laboratories) represents a breakthrough as the first RSV test available for home use, even including children as young as 6 months. While antigen-based home tests generally have lower sensitivity than NAATs (the FDA advises confirming negative results from this kit with a molecular test), their availability signals a trend toward more accessible and comprehensive respiratory diagnostics. However, it remains crucial that highly sensitive PoC NAATs continue to be utilized in clinical settings, especially for infants, elderly, and immunocompromised patients.

Key strengths of our review include a broad, inclusive search strategy (covering multiple databases and gray literature without regional or language restrictions) and the use of robust methodologies (dual independent screening, standardized data extraction, and quality appraisal). These steps reduce bias and enhance the generalizability of our conclusions. We synthesized evidence from diverse settings and age groups, which allowed us to directly compare PoC test performance in children vs. adults—a unique contribution of this work.

Nevertheless, we acknowledge several limitations. Many included studies were relatively small, limiting the precision of performance estimates, particularly in adult subgroups. Additionally, 65% of studies were assessed as having moderate RoB (and none as high risk), typically due to non-random participant selection or lack of blinding of index tests. These methodological issues could inflate diagnostic accuracy results, so the true field performance of some assays might be slightly lower than reported. Another challenge was the lack of a uniform definition of PoC across studies—we included a few platforms (e.g., Panther Fusion) that have slightly longer TATs or higher complexity but were used in near-patient workflows. The inclusion of such tests reflects real-world practice but complicates straightforward comparisons. Most studies were conducted in hospital or laboratory settings; data on PoC NAAT use in primary care clinics, pharmacies, or home environments were scarce. Thus, the generalizability of our findings to community settings is limited and should be explored further. We also observed heterogeneity in specimen collection and reference standards, for example, some pediatric studies used combined viral testing as a reference instead of a single RT-PCR, and sampling methods varied (NS vs. NPS). This heterogeneity may contribute to the variability in sensitivity observed. Furthermore, cost and economic outcomes were under-reported. Only a handful of studies examined the impact of PoC testing on healthcare costs or length of stay, so we could not draw strong conclusions about cost-effectiveness. Finally, we found few direct comparisons between PoC NAATs and older rapid antigen tests within the same study (especially in pediatric populations). While separate studies confirm that NAATs are more sensitive than RADTs in children [9, 16], head-to-head trials would better quantify the benefits of upgrading to molecular tests in various clinical scenarios.

Our review highlights important areas for future research and development. First, improving RSV PoC testing in adults should be a priority. This could involve refining assay chemistry for low viral load detection or investigating whether obtaining multiple sample types (e.g., combined nasal and throat swabs) can consistently improve sensitivity in adults. Second, more studies are needed in outpatient and resource-limited settings to understand how PoC NAATs perform outside of well-resourced hospitals. Such studies should also evaluate operational factors, such as ease of use by non-laboratory personnel and impact on patient flow, which were beyond the scope of most papers we reviewed. Third, the field should continue to explore multiplex respiratory panels at the PoC. Clinicians increasingly face patients with undifferentiated respiratory symptoms (especially during the winter viral season), and a single test that covers RSV, influenza, COVID-19, and other common viruses is highly attractive. New multiplex PoC platforms are emerging, including not only antigen kits (as noted above) but also cartridge-based NAATs that can detect several pathogens in one run. Rigorous evaluation of these assays in pediatrics and adults will be needed to ensure they deliver on their promise of comprehensive, rapid diagnosis. The recent FDA-authorized 4-in-1 home test [50] exemplifies the push toward such multi-analyte diagnostics; similar innovation on the molecular side (e.g., CRISPR-based NAATs or smartphone-integrated devices) may further enhance sensitivity and accessibility. Finally, as RSV preventive measures expand (e.g., new monoclonal antibodies and vaccines for infants, maternal immunization, and older adult vaccines [17, 23–25]), timely and accurate diagnostics become even more critical. Future studies should assess how PoC testing can best support these interventions, for example, identifying breakthrough infections in vaccinated individuals or guiding the use of emerging antivirals [26–28].

Conclusion

PoC NAATs for RSV present a valuable tool to accelerate diagnosis and patient management. Our review shows that these tests generally offer excellent specificity and good overall sensitivity, with particularly strong performance in pediatric populations. In adults, PoC NAATs still provide rapid results but may miss some infections, so a negative result should be interpreted in context. Improving the sensitivity of PoC RSV tests for adults will further enhance their clinical utility. In the meantime, healthcare providers should use adult PoC results judiciously, confirming negatives when necessary. Expanding access to reliable PoC RSV testing, in both clinical settings and at home, stands to improve surveillance and early care for RSV, which is especially important given newly available RSV vaccines/preventive measures and forthcoming treatments. Continued efforts to develop and validate high-performance, user-friendly diagnostic tools will support better patient outcomes and inform public health decisions.
