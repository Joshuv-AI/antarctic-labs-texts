The Strong Singular Value Property for Matrices Journal: Linear Algebra and Its Applications
Caleb Cheung, Bryan Shader

Abstract
A new property, the strong singular value property, is introduced, developed, and utilized to study the problem of which lists of nonnegative real numbers occur as the singular values of a matrix with a prescribed zero-nonzero pattern.

The

Given an

The outline of the remainder of the paper is as follows.
In Section 2, we study the ISVP-

for a variety of lists and patterns. In particular, we show that the ISVP-

need not be well-behaved with respect to inserting new nonzero positions into a pattern. Section 3 introduces the Strong Singular Value Property (SSVP for short) for matrices and gives various constructions of matrices

Inverse Singular Value Problems

We begin with a few instances where much can be said about the ISVP-
P
. First, we consider the case that the singular values are all equal.

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
P
be an
m\times n
pattern with
m\leq n
.
Then there is a matrix with pattern

with all singular values equal to

if and only if

.

Proof.

As the rank of a matrix equals the number of nonzero singular values of the matrix, if there is a matrix with pattern
P
having all singular values
0
, then
P=O
.
Conversely, if
P=O
, then
P
itself has all singular values equal to
0
.
∎

A matrix

provided

AA^{\top}=I
.
Patterns of row-orthonormal matrices have been studied in

4
,

15
]
. The diagonal
matrix whose diagonal entries are

Theorem 2.2
.

Let
P
be an
m\times n
pattern with
m\leq n
.
Then there exists a matrix with pattern
P
having
all singular values equal and nonzero if and only if
there is a row-orthonormal matrix with pattern

.
Moreover, in this case, each list of
m
positive real numbers is
the list of singular values of a matrix with pattern

Proof.

First suppose there is

with all singular values equal to the nonzero value
\ell
.
This implies that
AA^{\top}
is a symmetric matrix with all eigenvalues equal to
\ell^{2}
, which in turn implies

. Hence
\frac{1}{\sqrt{\ell}}A
is a row-orthonormal matrix with pattern

.
Conversely, if

is

.

If

is a list of

\sigma_{1},\ldots,\sigma_{m}

∎

By

Theorem 2.3
.

Let

(a)

(b)

m\times(n-k)

(c)

First assume (a). Since

Next assume (b).

[1]\mbox{ and }{1/\sqrt{2}}\left[\begin{array}[]{rr}1&1\\

Theorem 2.5
.

Let
P
be an
n\times(n+1)
pattern whose underlying bigraph is a path on
2n+1
vertices. Then a list
\Sigma
of
n
nonnegative real numbers forms the singular values of a matrix with pattern
P
if and only if the elements are distinct and nonzero.

Proof.

Without loss of generality
P
is the
n\times(n+1)
pattern with nonzeros in positions

(1,1)
,
(1,2)
,
(2,2)
,
(2,3)
, …,
(n-1,n)
,
(n,n)
and
(n,n+1)
, and zeros elsewhere.

Let
B
be a matrix with pattern
P
. Then
BB^{\top}
is
a symmetric matrix whose graph is the path
1
–
2
–
\cdots
–
n
.
In particular, this implies that each eigenvalue of
B{B^{\top}}
is simple.
Hence, each singular value of
B
is simple. Additionally, as
B[\{1,\ldots,n\},\{2,\ldots,n+1\}]
is upper triangular with nonzeros on its diagonal, the rows of
B
are linearly independent.
Therefore the inverse singular value problem for
P
has a solution only if the prescribed singular values
are distinct and nonzero.

Now let
\sigma_{1}>\sigma_{2}>\cdots>\sigma_{n+1}=0
be
n+1
real numbers.
Then by Theorem 4.2.1 of
[
8
]
, there exists a nonnegative matrix

M=[m_{ij}]
whose graph is the path on
n+1
vertices and whose eigenvalues are
\sigma_{j}^{2}
for
j=1,2\ldots,n+1
.
Define the
n\times(n+1)
matrix
B=[b_{ij}]

by

b_{ii}=\sqrt{\frac{\det M[\{1,\ldots,i\}]}{\det M[\{1,\ldots,i-1\}]}}

for
i=1,\ldots,n
(here we take the determinant of a
0\times 0
matrix to be
1
),

b_{i,i+1}=m_{i,i+1}/b_{i,i}
for
i=1,2,\ldots n
, and
b_{i,j}=0
for all other
i
and
j
. Note the pattern of
B
is
P
.

We claim that
B^{\top}B=M
.
If
|j-i|\geq 2
, then the
(i,j)
-entries of
B^{\top}B
and
M

are both zero.
Next note that for
i=1,\ldots,n
, the
(i+1,i)
-entry of
B^{\top}B
equals

b_{i,i+1}b_{i,i}=m_{i,i+1}/b_{i,i}\cdot b_{i,i}=m_{i,i+1},

as desired.
Also note for
i=1,\ldots,n
the
(i,i)
-entry of
B^{\top}B
equals

\displaystyle b_{i,i}^{2}+b_{i-1,i}^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\det M[\{1,\ldots,i\}]}{\det M[\{1,\ldots,i-1\}]}+\frac{m_{i,i-1}^{2}\det M[\{1,\ldots,i-2\}]}{\det M[\{1,\ldots,i-1\}]}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\det M[\{1,\ldots,i-1\}]}(m_{i,i}\det M[\{1,\ldots,i-1\}]

\displaystyle-m_{i,i-1}^{2}\det M[\{1,\ldots,i-2\}]+m_{i,i-1}^{2}\det M[\{1,\ldots,i-2\}])

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle m_{i,i}.

Here we take
b_{0,1}
to be
0
, and the second equality follows from
Laplace expansion of the determinant of
M[\{1,\ldots,i\}]
along its

-th row.

Finally note that the
(n+1,n+1)
-entry of

equals

\displaystyle b_{n,n+1}^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle m_{n+1,n}^{2}/b_{n,n}^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{m_{n+1,n}^{2}\det M[\{1,\ldots,n-1\}]}{\det M[\{1,\ldots,n\}]}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{m_{n+1,n+1}\det M[\{1,\ldots,n\}]}{\det M[\{1,\ldots,n\}]}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle m_{n+1,n+1}.

The second to last equality follows from the fact that

0=\det M=m_{n+1,n+1}\det M[\{1,\ldots,n\}]-m_{n+1,n}^{2}\det M[\{1,\ldots,n-1\}].

Hence
B^{\top}B\ =M
, and
B^{\top}
has singular values
\sigma_{i}
for

. It follows that

has singular values
\sigma_{i}

for
i=1,2,\ldots,n
.
∎

3
The Strong Singular Value Property

2.4
shows that the ISVP-
P
can be poorly behaved with respect to superpatterns.
We now describe a condition on a matrix
A
that guarantees the existence of a matrix
with the same singular values as
A
for each superpattern of
A
. We use
A\circ B

to denote the Schur (that is, entry-wise product) of two matrices
A
and
B
of the same size.

The
m\times n
matrix
A
has the
Strong Singular Value Property
(the
SSVP
, for short) provided the zero matrix is the only
m\times n
matrix
X
such that

(a)

XA^{\top}
is symmetric,

(b)

A^{\top}X
is symmetric, and

(c)

A\circ X=O
.

In this section we give examples of matrices having the SSVP, establish fundamental properties of SSVP matrices, characterize SSVP matrices of
certain types and show how the problem of recognizing whether a matrix has the SSVP
can be reduced to the problem of recognizing whether the columns of an associated
matrix are linearly independent. In the next section we describe how the SSVP can be used
in the ISVP-
P
. We first explore the SSVP with some examples.

Example 3.1
.

Each nowhere zero matrix has the SSVP.

This is clear, since for a nowhere zero matrix
A
,
A\circ X=O
implies
X=O
.∎

The next example shows that the definition of the SSVP requires
both statements (a) and (b) in the definition.

Example 3.2
.

Consider

A=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}1&0\\
0&2\end{array}\right].

Each matrix
X
such that
A\circ X=O

has the form

X=\left[\begin{array}[]{ccc}0&a\\
b&0\end{array}\right].

Direct computation shows that
XA^{\top}
is symmetric
if and only if
b=2a
. Similarly,
A^{\top}X
is symmetric
if and only if
a=2b
. It follows that
A
has the SSVP.
Moreover, both equations
b=2a
and
a=2b
are required to conclude

X=O
.
∎

Example 3.3
.

No
m\times n
matrix with
m\leq n
and a row of zeros has the SSVP.

Let
A
be such a matrix with
m\leq n
whose
i
-th row is zero, and let
\bf e_{i}
be the column vector with a
1
in position
i
and zeros elsewhere.
As
m-1<n
, there is a nonzero vector

\mathbf{z}
such that
A{\mathbf{z}}={\mathbf{0}}
. Let
X={\mathbf{e}_{i}}{\mathbf{z}}^{\top}
. Then
XA^{\top}={\mathbf{e}_{i}}(A{\mathbf{z}})^{\top}=O
,

A^{\top}X=A^{\top}{\mathbf{e}_{i}}{\mathbf{z}}^{\top}={\mathbf{0}}{\mathbf{z}}^{\top}=O
,
A\circ X=O
and
X\neq O
. Thus,
A
does not have the SSVP.
∎

Proposition 3.4
.

Let
\mathbf{y}^{\top}
be a
1\times n
matrix with
n\geq 1
.
Then
\mathbf{y}^{\top}
has the SSVP if and only if
\mathbf{y}\neq{\bf 0}
.

Proof.

By
3.3
, if
\mathbf{y}^{\top}
has the SSVP, then
\mathbf{y}
is nonzero.

For the converse, assume that
\mathbf{y}\neq\mathbf{0}
,
and note that if
\mathbf{x}
is a vector
such that
\mathbf{y}\mathbf{x}^{\top}
is symmetric, then
\mathbf{x}
is a multiple of
\mathbf{y}
,
hence
\mathbf{y}\circ\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{0}
, implies
\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{0}
.
Thus
\mathbf{y}
has the SSVP.
∎

The following shows that having the SSVP is preserved under any combination of
transposition, permuting rows, permuting columns, negating some set of rows, and
negating some set of columns.

Theorem 3.5
.

Let
A
be an
m\times n
matrix.
Then for each of
\rm(a)
–
\rm(e)
below,

A
has the SSVP if and only if
B
does.

(a)

B=A^{\top}
.

(b)

B=PA
for some permutation matrix
P
.

(c)

B=AQ
for some permutation matrix
Q
.

(d)

B
is obtained from
A
by negating some of its rows.

(e)

B
is obtained from
A
by negating some of its columns.

Proof.

We note in each case the if statement follows from the only if statement. For example,
if we have established that if
A
has the SSVP then
A^{\top}
does; the statement
if
A^{\top}
has the SSVP, then
A
has the SSVP comes from applying the first statement
to
A^{\top}
and using
(A^{\top})^{\top}=A
.

Assume that
A
has the SSVP.

Proof of (a).
Let
B=A^{\top}
, and suppose
B^{\top}Y
is symmetric,
YB^{\top}
is symmetric, and
B\circ Y=O
for some matrix
Y
. Consider
X=Y^{\top}
. Since
B^{\top}Y
is symmetric,
AX^{\top}
(and hence
XA^{\top}
) is symmetric.
Similarly, since
YB^{\top}
is symmetric,
X^{\top}A

(and hence
A^{\top}X
) is symmetric. Finally,
B\circ Y=O
implies
A^{\top}\circ X^{\top}=O
, which is true only if
A\circ X=O
. Since
A
has the SSVP,
X=O
. Hence
Y=O
, and
we conclude that
B
has the SSVP.

Proof of (b).
Let
P
be a permutation matrix and
B=PA
, and suppose
B^{\top}Y
is symmetric,
YB^{\top}
is symmetric, and
B\circ Y=O
for some matrix
Y
. Consider

. Since
B^{\top}Y
is symmetric

,

is symmetric. Similarly, since
YB^{\top}
is symmetric,
PXA^{\top}P^{\top}
is symmetric. This says that
XA^{\top}
is permutationally similarly to a symmetric matrix, and hence

is symmetric.

Finally, since

, we know that
(PA)\circ Y=O
. Pre-multiplying by

gives

. Since
A\circ X=O

,

. Thus

, and

has the SSVP.

Proof of
(c).
Let

, where

is a permutation matrix. By (a),
A^{\top}
has the SSVP.
By (b),
Q^{\top}A^{\top}
has the SSVP. Applying (a) again, we see that

has the SSVP.

Proof of
(d).
Let
B
be a matrix obtained by negating a subset of rows of
A
. Then

B=CA
for some diagonal matrix each of whose diagonal entries lie in
\{-1,1\}
.
Note

. Suppose that

are symmetric and

for some matrix

. Consider

.
Since

,

A^{\top}X
is symmetric.
Since

,

XA^{\top}
is orthogonally similar to a symmetric matrix. Thus,

is symmetric.
Finally, since

(CA)\circ Y=O
. Pre-multiplying by

. As

,

. Since

. Hence

Proof of
(e).
Let
B
be a matrix obtained from

by negating a subset of its columns.
Then

for some diagonal matrix whose diagonal entries lie in
\{-1,1\}
.
Note that

.
By (a),
A^{\top}
has the SSVP. By (b),

has the SSVP.
Finally, by (a) again,

has the SSVP.

The following shows that having columns of
\mathbf{0}
do not typically affect whether or not a matrix has the SSVP.

Theorem 3.6
.

Let
A
be a matrix of the form,

A=\left[\begin{array}[]{c|c}B&O\end{array}\right],

where
B
is a nonzero
m\times n
matrix and
O
is an
m\times\ell
zero matrix with

. Then
A
has the SSVP if and only if the rows of
B
are linearly independent and
B
has the SSVP.

Proof.

First suppose that
B
does not have the SSVP. Then there exists a nonzero
m\times n
matrix

X
such that
B\circ X=O
, and both
B^{\top}X
and
XB^{\top}
are symmetric.
Evidently, the matrix

\widehat{X}=\left[\begin{array}[]{c|c}X&O\end{array}\right]

certifies that
A
does not have the SSVP. Next suppose that the rows of
B
are linearly dependent. Then there exists an
m\times\ell
nonzero matrix
Y
such that
B^{\top}Y=O
. The matrix

\widehat{X}=\left[\begin{array}[]{c|c}O&Y\end{array}\right]

certifies that
A
does not have the SSVP. Therefore, we have shown that if
A
has the SSVP, then
B
has the SSVP and the rows of

B
are linearly independent.

Conversely, assume that
B
has the SSVP and its rows are linearly independent. Let
X
be an
m\times(n+\ell)
matrix such that
A^{\top}X
and
XA^{\top}
are symmetric and that

4
Implications of the SSVP

In this section we state four fundamental results implied by the SSVP,
and show how they can be applied to study inverse singular value problems. The proofs of these results are quite technical in nature, and are left to

We note that these results are analogs of those for other strong properties
such as the strong Arnold property

,
the strong spectral property
[

,
and the strong inner product property

[

As we noted in

allows a matrix with the same singular values

Theorem 4.1

(
Superpattern Theorem).

Let

have the SSVP and
P
be a superpattern of the pattern of
A
. Then there exists a matrix
with pattern
P
and singular values
\Sigma(A)
.

One application of the the Superpattern Theorem is the following which generalizes the results
in
[

,

]
for eigenvalue setting.

Corollary 4.2

(

Let
\Sigma
be a set of

distinct positive real numbers
and

be an

pattern.
Then there exists an
m\times n
matrix

with pattern

and singular values

if and only if

has term-rank

Proof.

Suppose such a matrix

exists. Then 0 is not a singular value of

Hence the rank of

is
m
.
Thus

has term-rank

.

Next suppose that

has term-rank

. Without loss of generality

By

\left[\begin{array}[]{r|r}D&O\end{array}\right],

now implies the existence of the desired matrix

∎

The Superpattern Theorem allows one to perturb the entries of a matrix with the SSVP to a matrix with a desired superpattern with the same set of singular values. In a similar way, we can perturb the singular values of a matrix while preserving its pattern.

Theorem 4.3

(

Let

pattern

and singular values

.

If
A
is an
m\times n
matrix with distinct singular values

of

is
(m_{1},\dots,m_{k})
. The Bifurcation Theorem allows one to study the set of multiplicity lists allowed by a pattern. The following example gives a nontrivial example of a pattern for which the Bifurcation Theorem allows us to show the existence of matrices with multiplicity lists and the same pattern tha are not accessible by elementary means.

Let

6
Theoretical Underpinnings

In this section, we establish the theoretical underpinnings of
the strong singular value property. The results, like the
analogous ones for other strong properties that have been established and studied, utilize the Inverse Function Theorem (IVT) (see
Theorem 3.3.2 of
[
10
]
). Throughout, we use

\|\cdot\|
for the standard Euclidean norm on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

We begin with some linear algebraic results.
Recall we view
\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
as an inner product space with the inner product
\langle M,N\rangle=\mbox{tr}(M^{\top}N)
. We use
S^{\perp}
to denote the
orthogonal complement
of a subset
S
of
\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
with respect to this inner product. Thus
\mbox{Sym}(n)^{\perp}=\mbox{Skew}(n)
, and
\mbox{Skew}(n)^{\perp}=\mbox{Sym}(n)
.

It is known that if
K
is a skew-symmetric matrix,
then
e^{K}=I+K+K^{2}/2!+\cdots

is an orthogonal matrix.
Given an
m\times n
symmetric matrix
A
with pattern
P
,
we will need to know the
orthogonal complements of the
spaces

\{KA:K\in\mbox{Skew}(m)\}
,

\{AL:K\in\mbox{Skew}(m)\}
,
and
\mbox{Span}(\mathcal{Z}(P))
.

Lemma 6.1
.

Let
A\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
with pattern
P=[p_{ij}]
.
Then

(a)

\{KA:K\in\mbox{\rm Skew}(m)\}^{\perp}=\{X\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}:XA^{\top}\mbox{ is symmetric}\}.

(b)

\{AL:L\in\mbox{\rm Skew}(n)\}^{\perp}=\{X\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}:A^{\top}X\mbox{ is symmetric}\}
.

(c)

\mbox{\rm Span}(\mathcal{Z}(P))^{\perp}=\{X\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}:A\circ X=O\}
.

(d)

A
has the SSVP if and only if

\{KA:K\in\mbox{\rm Skew}(m)\}+\{AL:L\in\mbox{\rm Skew}(n)\}+\mbox{\rm Span}(\mathcal{Z}(P))=\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}.

(e)

A
has the SSVP if and only if
\mbox{\rm Tan}_{A}^{\Sigma}=\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
.

Proof.

Note that
X\in\{KA:K\in\mbox{Skew}(m)\}^{\perp}
if and only if
\mbox{tr}(X^{\top}KA)=0

for all
K\in\mbox{Skew}(m)
. As

\mbox{tr}(X^{\top}KA)=\mbox{tr}(AX^{\top}K)=\mbox{tr}((XA^{\top})^{\top}K)=\langle XA^{\top},K\rangle,

we see that
X\in\{KA:K\in\mbox{Skew}(m)\}^{\perp}
if and only if
XA^{\top}

is symmetric. Hence (a) holds. Statement (b) follows by a similar argument. Statement (c) follows from noting that
X=[x_{ij}]\in\mathcal{Z}(P)^{\perp}
if and only if
x_{ij}=0
whenever

p_{ij}\neq 0
. By the definition of SSVP,
A
has the SSVP if and only if

\{X:A^{\top}X\mbox{ is symmetric}\}\cap\{X:XA^{\top}\mbox{ is symmetric}\}\cap\{AL:\mbox{Span}(\mathcal{Z}(P))^{\perp}=\{O\}.

(14)

Statement (d) follows from (a)-(c) and taking orthogonal complements of both sides of (
14
).
Statement (e) follows from (d) and the definition of
\mbox{Tan}_{A}^{\Sigma}
.
∎

We next discuss needed concepts and results about multivariable real functions.
Let
\mathcal{U}
be an open subset of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. The
function

f\colon\mathcal{U}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{m}
is
differentiable
at the
point
\mathbf{u}\in\mathcal{U}
provided there exists a linear transformation

D_{\mathbf{u}}f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{m}

such that

\lim_{\mathbf{x}\rightarrow\mathbf{u}}\frac{\|f(\mathbf{x})-f(\mathbf{u})-D_{\mathbf{u}}f(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{u})\|}{\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{u}\|}=0.

It is known that if
D_{\mathbf{u}}f
exists, then it is unique.
In this case,
D_{\mathbf{u}}f
is called the
total derivative of
f
at
\mathbf{u}
. The map
\mathcal{L}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{m}
by
\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{x})=f(a)+D_{\mathbf{u}}f(x-a)
is called the
linearization of
f
at
\mathbf{u}
.

The function
f
determines functions

f_{i}\colon\mathcal{U}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}

(i=1,2,\ldots,m)

with

f(\mathbf{x})=(f_{1}(\mathbf{x}),\ldots,f_{m}(\mathbf{x}))

for all
\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\in\mathcal{U}
.
It is known that if
\frac{\partial{f_{i}}}{\partial x_{j}}
exists and is continuous for each
i
and
j
and each
\mathbf{x}
in
\mathcal{U}
, then

D_{\mathbf{u}}f
exists, and is given by

D_{\mathbf{u}}f(\mathbf{x})=\mbox{Jac}_{f}\;{\mathbf{x}},

where
\mbox{Jac}_{f}
is the
m\times n
matrix whose
(i,j)
-entry equals

\frac{\partial{f_{i}}}{\partial x_{j}}(\mathbf{u})

for
i=1,\ldots,m
; and
j=1,\ldots,n
.
The matrix
\mbox{Jac}_{f}
is known as the
Jacobian matrix of
f
at
\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{u}
.
The relationship between
D_{\mathbf{u}}
and
\mbox{Jac}_{f}
and basic facts about linear algebra give the following facts.

Observation 6.2
.

The following are equivalent:

(a)

D_{\mathbf{u}}f
is an onto map.

(b)

The column space of
\mbox{Jac}_{f}
is
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

(c)

The rows of
\mbox{Jac}_{f}
are linearly dependent.

We are now ready to state the Inverse Function Theorem which relates the
local invertibility of a function to the invertibility of its derivative (see
[
10
]
).

Theorem 6.3

(
Inverse function theorem (IVT)).

Let
\mathcal{U}
be an open set of
\mathbb{R}^{m}
,
\mathbf{u}\in\mathcal{U}

and
f\colon\mathcal{U}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{m}
be a smooth function on
\mathcal{U}
. If the Jacobian matrix of
f
at
\mathbf{u}
is invertible
(equivalently,
D_{\mathbf{u}}f
is an onto function), then there exists an open neighborhood
\mathcal{V}\subseteq\mathcal{U}

of
\mathbf{u}
such that the restriction
f|{\mathcal{V}}
is injective;
f(\mathcal{V})

is open, and the inverse of
f|\mathcal{V}
is differentiable.

The following consequence of the IVT is convenient for our purposes.

Corollary 6.4
.

Let
\mathcal{U}
be an open set of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
\mathbf{u}\in\mathcal{U}
and

f\colon\mathcal{U}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{m}
be a smooth function on
\mathcal{U}
.
If the Jacobian matrix of
f
at
\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{u}

has rank
m
(equivalently,
D_{\mathbf{u}}f
is onto), then there exists an open neighborhood

\mathcal{V}\subseteq\mathcal{U}
of
\mathbf{u}
and an open neighborhood
\mathcal{W}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m}
of
f(\mathbf{u})

such that
\mathcal{W}
is contained in
f(\mathcal{V})
.

Proof.

Assume
\mathbf{u}\in\mathcal{U}
and the Jacobian of

f
at
\mathbf{u}
has rank
m
.
Then
n\geq m
and there exist
m
variables,
which we may assume to be
x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{m}
,
such that
\mbox{Jac}_{f}[\{1,\ldots,m\},\{1,\ldots,m\}]
is invertible.
The subset
\mathcal{R}=\mathbb{R}^{m\times\{[u_{m_{1}},\ldots,u_{n}]^{\top}\}}
is homeomorphic
to
\mathbb{R}^{m}
, and we can naturally restrict
f
to
\mathcal{R}
.
We focus on the restriction of
f
to
\mathcal{R}
.
Its Jacobian matrix is an invertible matrix.

By
Theorem

6.3
, there is an open neighborhood
\hat{\mathcal{V}}
of

containing

and an open neighborhood
\mathcal{W}
such that

.
Taking
\mathcal{V}
to be any open set of
\mathcal{U}
containing
\hat{\mathcal{V}}
completes the proof.
∎

Proof of the Superpattern Theorem.

Let
A
be an
m\times n
matrix with the SSVP and let
P
be a superpattern of the pattern of
A
. Our goal is to find a matrix with pattern
P
and the same singular values as
A
.

Define
f\colon\mbox{\rm Skew}(m)\times\mbox{\rm Skew}(n)\times\mbox{Span}(\mathcal{Z}(P))\to\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
via

\displaystyle(K,L,B)\mapsto e^{K}Ae^{L}+B.

We claim that

D_{(O,O,O)}f
is the linear transformation given by

(K,L,B)\mapsto KA+AL+B.

Note

,

by

;

;
and

by

.
Since

,
D_{O}g(K)=K
.
Similarly,
D_{O}g(L)=L
. Finally,
D_{O}i(B)=B
.
Hence by the addition and product rules,

D_{(O,O,O)}f(K,L,B)=KA+AL+B.

Since
A
has the SSVP,

6.1
implies that
D_{(O,O,O)}f
is onto.

Since
f
is continuous at
(O,O,O)
, there
exists an open neighborhood

\mathcal{U}\subseteq\rm Skew(m)\times\rm Skew(n)\times\mbox{Span}(\mathcal{Z}(P))
of

such that for each
(K,L,B)\in\mathcal{U}
,
A-B
has the same pattern as

. By
Corollary

6.4
, there is a open neighborhood
\mathcal{V}\subseteq\mathcal{U}
of
(O,O,O)
and an open neighborhood
\mathcal{W}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
of

such that

\mathcal{W}\subseteq f(\mathcal{V}).

(15)

Thus, there exists a matrix
\hat{A}\in\mathcal{W}
such that
\hat{A}

has pattern
P
.
By (
15
), there exists
(K,L,B)\in\mathcal{V}
such that

. Now,

\displaystyle\hat{A}

\displaystyle=e^{K}Ae^{L}+B

\displaystyle\hat{A}-B

\displaystyle=e^{k}Ae^{L}.

By the choice of
\mathcal{U}
,

has the same pattern as

, the pattern of

.
Since

∎

Our goal is to show that if

with pattern

Let

\mathcal{U}
