Bounding Radial Variation of positive harmonic Functions on Lipschitz Domains
Jakob Fromherz, Paul F.X. Müller, Katharina Riegler

Abstract
Let D ⊂ ℝ d \displaystyle D\subset\mathbb{R}^{d} be a Lipschitz domain (bounded or unbounded) and let u \displaystyle u be a bounded positive harmonic function on D \displaystyle D . By a point of bounded radial variation, or a Bourgain point, we will refer to a point x \displaystyle x on ∂ D \displaystyle\partial D , such that the integral ∫ 0 1 ‖ ∇ u ​ ( x + t ​ v ) ‖ ​ 𝑑 t \displaystyle\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\|\nabla u(x+tv)\|\mathrm{d}t is finite for some vector v \displaystyle v satisfying ( x , x + v ] ) ⊂ D \displaystyle(x,x+v])\subset D . Let 𝒱 u ​ ( ∂ D ) \displaystyle\mathcal{V}^{u}(\partial D) denote the set of all Bourgain points on ∂ D \displaystyle\partial D . This paper presents techniques going back to Havin and Mozolyako [ 11 ] that determine a large set of Bourgain points on ∂ D \displaystyle\partial D . To be more precise, any ball B \displaystyle B with center on ∂ D \displaystyle\partial D contains a Bourgain point. As a consequence, the Hausdorff dimension of 𝒱 u ​ ( ∂ D ) ∩ B ⁡ ( x , r ) \displaystyle\mathcal{V}^{u}(\partial D)\cap B(x,r) is greater than ( d − 1 ) / 2 \displaystyle(d-1)/2 , for arbitrary x ∈ ∂ D \displaystyle x\in\partial D and r > 0 \displaystyle r>0 . The intention of this paper is to provide and document an updated and refined version of [ 13 ] . AMS Subject Classification 2010: 31B25, 31B10, 31B05, 31A20 Keywords: Radial Variation, Boundary Behaviour, Positive Harmonic Functions, Potential Theory

Introduction

In 1993, J. Bourgain published two papers
[
3
]
,
[
2
]
in which he proved, that for any nonnegative harmonic on the unit disk
\displaystyle\mathbb{D}:=\{z\in\mathbb{C}:|z|\leq 1\}
, the quantity

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathrm{var}(u,\theta):=\int_{0}^{1}|\nabla u(\rho e^{\mathrm{i}\theta})|\mathrm{d}\rho

is finite on a subset of
\displaystyle\partial\mathbb{D}
with Hausdorff dimension 1. A compact overview of the two papers by Bourgain and their implications on minimal surfaces in
\displaystyle\mathbb{R}^{2}
is featured in the Master’s thesis of Katharina Riegler
[
15
]
.

Later, in 2016, Havin and Mozolyako considerably expanded the techniques developed by Bourgain in
[
11
]
and proved analogous results for positive harmonic functions
\displaystyle u
on domains
\displaystyle D\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
\displaystyle d\geq 3
with
\displaystyle C^{2}
boundary. To be more precise, it was shown that on a subset of the boundary
\displaystyle\partial D
that has Hausdorff dimension
\displaystyle d-1
, the variation of
\displaystyle u
along the inward normal vector is finite i.e. the set

\displaystyle\displaystyle\left\{x\in\partial D:\int_{0}^{1}\|\nabla u(x+\rho r(x)\vec{N}(x))\|\mathrm{d}\rho<\infty\right\}

has full Hausdorff dimension.

In the introduction of the aforementioned paper, the authors comment on the possibility of further weakening regularity assumptions on the boundary of the domain and still producing analogous results for Lipschitz domains using their techniques. This remark was further expanded on by Müller and Riegler in 2020
[
13
]
, providing the full proof and necessary modifications of how the paper by Havin and Mozolyako extends to Lipschitz domains. Using the results in
[
13
]
, they were able to lift a theorem on boundedness of radial variation of Bloch functions on the unit disk to arbitrary dimensions in
[
12
]
. The combination of both papers forms the PhD-thesis of Katharina Riegler
[
16
]
and serves as comprehensive source for what follows.

The following treatise is concerned with presenting and sharpening the results in
[
13
]
: For a bounded positive harmonic function
\displaystyle u
on a Lipschitz domain
\displaystyle D
(bounded or unbounded), we are interested in characterizing the set

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathcal{V}^{u}(\partial D):=\left\{x\in\partial D:\exists v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\text{ s.t. }(x,x+v]\subset D\text{ and }\int_{0}^{1}\|\nabla u(x+tv)\|\mathrm{d}t<\infty\right\},

(1)

in particular, estimating its Hausdorff dimension. In accordance to previous papers (
[
11
]
,
[
13
]
) dealing with boundedness of radial variation, we shall call it the set of
Bourgain
points.

We will first solve the problem on very special Lipschitz domains, namely on
near half spaces
. Then through locally identifying a Lipschitz domain with a corresponding near half space, we will carry over the results to arbitrary Lipschitz domains

Section
2
will introduce some preliminaries, while Section
3
is dedicated to proving important variational estimates on near half spaces. In particular, it is shown, that in any surface ball on the boundary of a near half space, there exists a Bourgain point (Theorem
3.10
). Section
4
will use results from previous sections to estimate the Hausdorff dimension of the set of all Bourgain points. This was not done in
[
13
]
and the resulting bound on the Hausdorff dimension is weaker than that proven for domains with
\displaystyle C^{2}
boundaries in
[
11
]
. However, an improvement is very likely. As previously announced, Section
5
contains the necessary steps to extend Hausdorff dimension estimates of the set of all Bourgain points to general Lipschitz domains.

2.1
Notation

Let
\displaystyle n\in\mathbb{N}
, then for the set of subsets of
\displaystyle\{1,\dots,n\}
of cardinality
\displaystyle k\in\{0,\dots,n\}
we write
\displaystyle\left\{\binom{n}{k}\right\}
, i.e.

\left\{\binom{n}{k}\right\}:=\left\{M\subseteq\{1,\dots,n\}:|A|=k\right\}.

Furthermore, we denote the dyadic numbers on
\displaystyle[0,1]
by
\displaystyle\mathcal{D}:=\left\{\frac{k}{2^{n}}:n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\quad k\in\{0,\dots,2^{n}\}\right\}
. For a compact interval
\displaystyle[a,b]
we denote with
\displaystyle\mathcal{D}([a,b])
the dyadic numbers in
\displaystyle[0,1]
, transformed to
\displaystyle[a,b]
i.e.

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathcal{D}\left([a,b]\right):=\left\{a+\frac{(b-a)k}{2^{n}}:n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\quad k\in\{0,\dots,2^{n}\}\right\}.

By
\displaystyle B(x,r)
we will denote the open ball around
\displaystyle x
with radius
\displaystyle r>0
. Sometimes, if we want to specify the dimension
\displaystyle d\in\mathbb{N}
in which we consider the ball, we add the
\displaystyle d
as superscript, i.e.
\displaystyle B^{d}(x,r)=\{y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\|x-y\|<r\}
.

Let
\displaystyle X
be any locally compact Hausdorff space, then we denote by

\displaystyle\displaystyle C_{0}(X):=\left\{f:X\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}:f\text{ is continuous and }\forall\epsilon>0\exists K\subset X\text{ compact}:\left|f_{|X\setminus K}\right|<\epsilon\right\}

the normed space of continuous functions on
\displaystyle X
, that vanish at infinity, equipped with the norm
\displaystyle\left\|f\right\|_{C_{0}(X)}:=\sup_{z\in X}|f(z)|
. Riesz’s representation theorem
[
17
]
states, that the dual of
\displaystyle C_{0}(X)
, is the space of regular signed Borel measures with finite total variation, i.e:
\displaystyle C_{0}(X)\cong\mathcal{M}(X)
where

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathcal{M}(X):=\left\{\mu:\mu\text{ regular, signed measure with }|\mu(X)|<\infty\right\}.

We continue to define
near half spaces
. Those are special Lipschitz domains which allow us to derive variational estimates of positive harmonic functions thereon.

Definition 2.1

(Near Half Space)
.

A Lipschitz domain
\displaystyle\mathcal{O}
is called a near half space, if there exists a Lipschitz function
\displaystyle\Phi:\mathbb{R}^{d-1}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}
with the extra condition

\exists r\in(0,1):\quad\Phi\arrowvert_{\mathbb{R}^{d-1}\setminus B^{d-1}(0,r)}=0,

such that
\displaystyle\mathcal{O}
is given by the epigraph of
\displaystyle\Phi
, i.e.

\mathcal{O}=\left\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:x\in\mathbb{R}^{d-1},y\in\mathbb{R}\quad\text{and}\quad y>\Phi(x)\right\}.

The boundary of
\displaystyle\mathcal{O}
is then given by the graph of
\displaystyle\Phi
, i.e.

S:=\partial\mathcal{O}=\left\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:x\in\mathbb{R}^{d-1},y\in\mathbb{R}\quad\text{and}\quad y=\Phi(x)\right\}.

Remark 1
.

(Convention)

Throughout this paper, the constant
\displaystyle c_{S}
denotes a positive constant that depends only on the Lipschitz boundary of our domain
\displaystyle\mathcal{O}
. It will be used very flexibly, in one line of inequalities it might take on different values but remains dependent only on
\displaystyle S
for example
\displaystyle c_{S}\cdot\exp(c_{S})=c_{S}
and
\displaystyle c_{S}\leq 3\cdot c_{S}=c_{S}
.
For
\displaystyle x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
we will use the notational convention
\displaystyle x_{y}:=x+y\vec{e_{d}}
, where
\displaystyle\vec{e_{d}}
denotes the
\displaystyle d
-th unit vector of the standard basis and
\displaystyle y\in\mathbb{R}
. With our definition of the half-space
\displaystyle\mathcal{O}
we immediately obtain
\displaystyle x_{y}\in\mathcal{O}
for all
\displaystyle x\in S
if
\displaystyle y>0
. Analogously, sets are shifted, i.e. for
\displaystyle A\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
we define
\displaystyle A_{y}:=y\vec{e_{d}}+A
. Now let
\displaystyle A,B\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\displaystyle y\in\mathbb{R}
such that
\displaystyle A_{y}\subseteq B
. For a function
\displaystyle\varphi
defined on
\displaystyle B
we define the function
\displaystyle\varphi_{y}
on
\displaystyle A
by
\displaystyle\varphi_{y}(x):=\varphi(x_{y})
.

2.2
Harnack Inequality and Harnack chains

An important ingredient necessary to analyze positive harmonic functions is Harnack’s inequality:

Theorem 2.2

(Harnack inequality
[
6
]
)
.

Let
\displaystyle x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
\displaystyle R>0
and
\displaystyle u:B(x_{0},R)\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}
be non-negative harmonic function. Then for every
\displaystyle x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
\displaystyle\|x-x_{0}\|=r<R
we have the double inequality

\displaystyle\displaystyle\frac{\left(1-\frac{r}{R}\right)}{\left(1+\frac{r}{R}\right)^{d-1}}u(x_{0})\leq u(x)\leq\frac{\left(1+\frac{r}{R}\right)}{\left(1-\frac{r}{R}\right)^{d-1}}u(x_{0}).

Corollary 2.2.1
.

Let
\displaystyle\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a domain and
\displaystyle u:\Omega\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}
be positive harmonic. Then for
\displaystyle x\in\Omega
we can bound the gradient of
\displaystyle u
in the following way:

\displaystyle\displaystyle\|\nabla u(x)\|\leq d^{3/2}\frac{u(x)}{d(x,\partial\Omega)}.

Proof.

Let
\displaystyle x\in\Omega
and denote
\displaystyle R=\textrm{dist}(x,\partial\Omega)
. Then for any
\displaystyle h\in(0,R)
we have by Harnack’s inequality

\displaystyle\displaystyle\frac{u(x+h\vec{e_{i}})-u(x)}{h}

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq\left(\frac{\frac{1}{h}+\frac{1}{R}}{\left(1-\frac{h}{R}\right)^{d-1}}-\frac{1}{h}\right)u(x)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\left(\frac{\frac{1}{h}\left(1-\left(1-\frac{h}{R}\right)^{d-1}\right)+\frac{1}{R}}{\left(1-\frac{h}{R}\right)^{d-1}}\right)u(x).

Taking the limit
\displaystyle h\rightarrow 0
we obtain

\displaystyle\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{i}}u(x)\leq\frac{d}{R}u(x),

since the constant terms in
\displaystyle p=1-\left(1-\frac{h}{R}\right)^{d-1}
cancel and hence
\displaystyle p/h\rightarrow\frac{d-1}{R}
as
\displaystyle h\rightarrow 0
. A similar calculation also shows
\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{i}}u(x)\geq-\frac{d}{R}u(x)
and thus
\displaystyle\left|\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{i}}u(x)\right|\leq\frac{d}{R}u(x)
. This implies the claimed inequality.
∎

This next Lemma will enable us to properly exploit Harnack’s inequality for positive harmonic functions on near half spaces by providing a simple formula estimating the distance of points in
\displaystyle\mathcal{O}
to the boundary
\displaystyle S
.

Lemma 2.3

(
[
13
]
)
.

Let
\displaystyle\mathcal{O}
be a near-half space with boundary
\displaystyle S
. Then there exists
\displaystyle c_{S}\leq 1
such that for all
\displaystyle x\in S
and every
\displaystyle y>0
we have
\displaystyle y\geq d(x_{y},S)\geq c_{S}y
.

Proof.

The first inequality is trivial. For
\displaystyle z=(z_{1},\dots,z_{d})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
we denote
\displaystyle\bar{z}=(z_{1},\dots,z_{d-1})\in\mathbb{R}^{d-1}
so in particular we have
\displaystyle z=(\bar{z},\Phi(\bar{z}))
for any
\displaystyle z\in S
. Choosing an arbitrary
\displaystyle z\in S
, we obtain

\displaystyle\displaystyle d(x_{y},z)^{2}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=d((\bar{x},\Phi(\bar{x})+y),(\bar{z},\Phi(\bar{z})))^{2}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=d(\bar{x},\bar{z})^{2}+(y+\Phi(\bar{x})-\Phi(\bar{z}))^{2}

\displaystyle\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{L^{2}}(\Phi(\bar{x})-\Phi(\bar{z}))^{2}+y^{2}+2y(\Phi(\bar{x})-\Phi(\bar{z}))+(\Phi(\bar{x})-\Phi(\bar{z}))^{2}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\left(\frac{\sqrt{L^{2}+1}}{L}(\Phi(\bar{x})-\Phi(\bar{z}))+\frac{L}{\sqrt{L^{2}+1}}y\right)^{2}+y^{2}-\frac{L^{2}}{L^{2}+1}y^{2}

\displaystyle\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{L^{2}+1}y^{2}.

Therefore we have

\inf_{z\in S}d(x_{y},z)\geq\frac{1}{\sqrt{L^{2}+1}}y.

∎

Equipped with Harnack’s inequality and the distance formula from Lemma
2.3
, we formulate a lemma on Harnack chains, a sequence of overlapping balls inside
\displaystyle\mathcal{O}
, that connects 2 points in
\displaystyle\mathcal{O}
.

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
\displaystyle u
be a positive harmonic function on a near half space
\displaystyle\mathcal{O}
with
\displaystyle\partial\mathcal{O}=S
and
\displaystyle 0<y_{1}\leq y_{2}
. Then there exists
\displaystyle\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}>0
, that solely depend on the Lipschitz boundary of
\displaystyle\mathcal{O}
and
\displaystyle C_{1},C_{2}>0
, depending on the the dimension
\displaystyle d
, such that for all
\displaystyle x\in S
we have

\displaystyle\displaystyle C_{1}\left(\frac{y_{1}}{y_{2}}\right)^{\alpha_{1}}\leq\frac{u_{y_{2}}(x)}{u_{y_{1}}(x)}\leq C_{2}\left(\frac{y_{2}}{y_{1}}\right)^{\alpha_{2}}.

Proof.

Let
\displaystyle c_{S}=c
be the constant from Lemma (
2.3
), in particular we have that for all
\displaystyle y>0
,
\displaystyle u
is positive harmonic on
\displaystyle B(x_{y},\delta cy)
since
\displaystyle B(x_{y},\delta cy)\subset\mathcal{O}
for any
\displaystyle 0<\delta<1
. Furthermore, depending on
\displaystyle y_{1},y_{2}
, there exists
\displaystyle N=N(y_{1},y_{2})
such that

\displaystyle\displaystyle\left(1-\frac{c}{2}\right)^{N}y_{2}\leq y_{1}\leq\left(1-\frac{c}{2}\right)^{N-1}y_{2}.

(2)

Denoting
\displaystyle a_{n}:=\left(1-\frac{c}{2}\right)^{n}y_{2}
, we have
\displaystyle x_{y_{1}}\in B(x_{a_{N-1}},a_{N-1}-a_{N})
. An easy calculation shows
\displaystyle a_{n-1}-a_{n}=\frac{c}{2}a_{n-1}
. Applying the Harnack inequality to

B(x_{a_{n-1}},a_{n-1}-a_{n})=B\left(x_{a_{n-1}},\frac{c}{2}a_{n-1}\right)\subsetneq B\left(x_{a_{n-1}},\frac{3}{4}ca_{n-1}\right)

for every
\displaystyle n
yields

\displaystyle\displaystyle\frac{u(x_{a_{n}})}{u(x_{a_{n-1}})}\geq\frac{1-\frac{\frac{c}{2}a_{n-1}}{\frac{3c}{4}a_{n-1}}}{\left(1+\frac{\frac{c}{2}a_{n-1}}{\frac{3c}{4}a_{n-1}}\right)^{d-1}}=\frac{1}{3}\left(\frac{5}{3}\right)^{1-d}=:C_{d}^{(1)}

and analogously

\displaystyle\displaystyle\frac{u(x_{a_{n}})}{u(x_{a_{n-1}})}\leq\frac{1+\frac{\frac{c}{2}a_{n-1}}{\frac{3c}{4}a_{n-1}}}{\left(1-\frac{\frac{c}{2}a_{n-1}}{\frac{3c}{4}a_{n-1}}\right)^{d-1}}=\frac{5}{3}\left(\frac{1}{3}\right)^{1-d}=:C_{d}^{(2)}.

Furthermore, since
\displaystyle x_{y_{1}}\in B\left(x_{a_{N-1}},\frac{c}{2}a_{N-1}\right)
and thus
\displaystyle y_{1}\geq a_{N}
, we can once more use the Harnack inequality to estimate

\displaystyle\displaystyle C_{d}^{(1)}\leq\frac{1-\frac{a_{N-1}-y_{1}}{\frac{3c}{4}a_{n-1}}}{\left(1+\frac{a_{N-1}-y_{1}}{\frac{3c}{4}a_{n-1}}\right)^{d-1}}\leq\frac{u(x_{y_{1}})}{u(x_{a_{N-1}})}\leq\frac{1+\frac{a_{N-1}-y_{1}}{\frac{3c}{4}a_{n-1}}}{\left(1-\frac{a_{N-1}-y_{1}}{\frac{3c}{4}a_{n-1}}\right)^{d-1}}\leq C_{d}^{(2)}.

Writing

\displaystyle\displaystyle\frac{u(x_{y_{2}})}{u(x_{y_{1}})}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\prod_{i=1}^{N-1}\frac{u(x_{a_{i-1}})}{u(x_{a_{i}})}\cdot\frac{u(x_{a_{N-1}})}{u(x_{y_{1}})},

we can finally bound

\displaystyle\displaystyle\left(C_{d}^{(2)}\right)^{-N}\leq\frac{u(x_{y_{2}})}{u(x_{y_{1}})}\leq\left(C_{d}^{(1)}\right)^{-N}.

Taking the logarithm in (
2
) we obtain

\displaystyle\displaystyle\frac{\ln\left(\frac{y_{1}}{y_{2}}\right)}{\ln\left(1-\frac{c}{2}\right)}\leq N\leq\frac{\ln\left(\frac{y_{1}}{y_{2}}\right)}{\ln\left(1-\frac{c}{2}\right)}+1.

Since
\displaystyle C_{d}^{(1)}<1
, a simple calculation shows

\displaystyle\displaystyle\left(C_{d}^{(1)}\right)^{-N}\leq\left(C_{d}^{(1)}\right)^{-1}\left(\frac{y_{2}}{y_{1}}\right)^{\alpha_{1}}\quad\text{where}\quad\alpha_{1}=\frac{\ln\left(C_{d}^{(1)}\right)}{\ln\left(1-\frac{c}{2}\right)}>0.

Moreover, since
\displaystyle C_{d}^{(2)}>1
, we can derive

\displaystyle\displaystyle\left(C_{d}^{(2)}\right)^{-N}\geq\left(C_{d}^{(2)}\right)^{-1}\left(\frac{y_{1}}{y_{2}}\right)^{\alpha_{2}}\quad\text{where}\quad\alpha_{2}=-\frac{\ln\left(C_{d}^{(2)}\right)}{\ln\left(1-\frac{c}{2}\right)}>0.

∎

2.3
The Dirichlet problem and Harmonic Measure

One of the key tools in our analysis of harmonic functions on the domains above a Lipschitz graph will be the harmonic measure, a measure defined on the boundary
\displaystyle S
, such that integration of bounded and continuous functions on the boundary yields the unique harmonic extension, i.e. the extension is harmonic inside the domain and converges back to the boundary values. We take a step back and formulate the problem and aims for general domains
\displaystyle D\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

In the following we will consider differential operators
\displaystyle L:C^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\longrightarrow C(\mathbb{R}^{d})
of the following form

\displaystyle\displaystyle L=\sum_{i,j=1}^{d}a_{ij}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x_{i}\partial x_{j}}+\sum_{i=1}^{d}b_{i}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{i}},

(3)

where
\displaystyle a_{ij}
and
\displaystyle b_{i}
are continuous functions. We call
\displaystyle L

semi-elliptic
if the eigenvalues of
\displaystyle(a_{ij})_{i,j=1}^{d}
are non-negative,
elliptic
if they are positive and
uniformly elliptic
if the eigenvalues are positiv and bounded away from
\displaystyle 0
.
We now state the problem of interest.

Problem 1
.

(Dirichlet problem)
Given a domain
\displaystyle D\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, a semi-elliptic differential operator
\displaystyle L
as in (
3
) on
\displaystyle D
and some bounded measurable function
\displaystyle\phi
defined on
\displaystyle\partial D
, find a function
\displaystyle u\in C^{2}(D)
such that the following two properties hold

(i)

\displaystyle Lu=0
in
\displaystyle D
,

(ii)

\displaystyle\lim_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\rightarrow y\\
x\in D\end{subarray}}u(x)=\phi(y)
for all
\displaystyle y\in\partial D
.

A unique solution to Problem
1
, will be called
harmonic extension
. However, we are not only interested in existence and uniqueness of a solution to problem
1
, we also want the unique solution to be representable as integration of the boundary values
\displaystyle\phi:\partial D\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
against a measure, i.e. there exists a a family of measures
\displaystyle\{\mu^{x}\}_{x\in D}
on the Borel subsets of
\displaystyle\partial D
, such that the solution
\displaystyle u\in C^{2}(D)
to Problem
1
is given by

\displaystyle\displaystyle u(x)=\int_{\partial D}\phi(\xi)\mathrm{d}\mu^{x}(\xi).

(4)

For
\displaystyle L=\Delta
on bounded domains
\displaystyle D
and continuous boundary values (i.e.
\displaystyle\phi\in C(\partial D)
in definition
1
), recall that the Perron operator
\displaystyle H_{D}:C(\partial D)\rightarrow C^{2}(D)\cap C(\bar{D})
realizes the desired harmonic extension
[
7
]
. The family of measure that we look for is constructed using Riesz’s representation theorem: For
\displaystyle x\in D
define the functional
\displaystyle L_{x}:C(\partial D)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
where
\displaystyle\phi\longmapsto(H_{D}\phi)(x)
. The maximum principle assures boundedness of
\displaystyle L_{x}
moreover,
\displaystyle L_{x}
is positive and
\displaystyle L_{x}(1)=1
, thus by Riesz’s representation theorem,
\displaystyle L_{x}
is uniquely representable as a probability measure
\displaystyle\mu^{x}
in the sense of (
4
). The probability measure
\displaystyle\mu^{x}
is then called the
harmonic measure
of
\displaystyle D
w.r.t. the pole
\displaystyle x\in D
.

Rather than extending this classical approach of defining the harmonic measure for unbounded domains, which is done in
[
7
]
, we will use the following probabilistic line of arguments found in
[
14
]
and
[
1
]
. We begin by defining Brownian motion.

Let
\displaystyle(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
be a probability space and
\displaystyle\mathcal{B}
the
\displaystyle\sigma
algebra generated by the open subsets of
\displaystyle[0,\infty)
. A stochastic process
\displaystyle X(t,\omega):=X_{t}(\omega):[0,\infty)\times\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
, is a measurable mapping w.r.t to
\displaystyle\mathcal{B}\otimes\mathcal{F}
, where
\displaystyle\mathcal{B}=\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})
denotes the
\displaystyle\sigma
-algebra generated by the open subsets of the real line.

Definition 2.5
.

(Brownian Motion)
A stochastic process
\displaystyle B_{t}
is a one-dimensional Brownian Motion if it satisfies the following conditions

(i)

\displaystyle B_{0}=0
a.s.,

(ii)

\displaystyle\forall s\leq t:B_{t}-B_{s}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,s-t)
, i.e.
\displaystyle B_{s}-B_{t}
is normally distributed with mean
\displaystyle 0
and variance
\displaystyle s-t
,

(iii)

\displaystyle\forall s<t:B_{s}-B_{t}
is independent of
\displaystyle\sigma(\{B_{r}:r\in[0,t]\})
,

(iv)

The paths
\displaystyle t\mapsto B_{t}
are continuous a.e.

If
\displaystyle B_{t}^{(1)},\dots B_{t}^{(d)}
are independent one-dimensional Brownian Motions, then

\displaystyle\displaystyle B_{t}:=\left(B_{t}^{(1)},\dots,B_{t}^{(d)}\right)

defines a
\displaystyle d
-dimensional Brownian Motion.

There are numerous proofs of existence of such a stochastic process, for some constructions refer to
[
1
]
and
[
14
]
. However, the most intuitive and direct approach works on a probability space
\displaystyle(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
on which there exists a countable family of independent Gaussian random variables with mean
\displaystyle 0
and variance
\displaystyle 1
and uses the Haar functions
[
1
, 11]
.

Definition 2.6
.

(Stopping time)
Let
\displaystyle\{\mathcal{F}_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
be a filtration of
\displaystyle\mathcal{F}
. A mapping
\displaystyle\tau:\Omega\longrightarrow[0,\infty)
that satisfies
\displaystyle\{\tau<t\}\in\mathcal{F}_{t}
for all
\displaystyle t\geq 0
, is called a stopping time.

Now let
\displaystyle D\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a domain and define for any
\displaystyle x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}

\displaystyle\displaystyle\tau_{D}^{x}:=\inf\left\{t>0:x+B_{t}\notin D\right\},

(5)

then
\displaystyle\tau_{D}^{x}

\displaystyle\omega\in\Omega

Definition 2.7

(Harmonic measure)
.

For
\displaystyle x\in D\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}

\displaystyle\displaystyle\omega_{D}^{x}(A):=\mathbb{P}\left(x+B_{\tau_{D}^{x}}\in A\right)\quad\text{for all}\quad A\in\mathcal{B}(\partial D).

(6)

The harmonic measure can thus also be interpreted as the hitting distribution of Brownian motion started inside the domain. If we now consider any ball
\displaystyle B(y,r)

\displaystyle D
and define the function

\displaystyle\displaystyle\varphi(x)=\mathbb{E}(f(x+B_{\tau_{D}^{x}}))\quad x\in D

\displaystyle f

\displaystyle\partial D

\displaystyle\varphi
satisfies the following mean-value property:

\displaystyle\displaystyle\varphi(x)=\int_{\partial B(y,r)}\varphi(\xi)\mathrm{d}\omega_{B(y,r)}^{x}(\xi)\quad\text{for all }x\in B(y,r).

(8)

This formula is a consequence of the strong Markov property of Brownian motion, for exact proofs, refer to
[
14
, 116
\psq
]
. Since the harmonic measure
\displaystyle\omega_{B(y,r)}^{x}
on the ball can be computed to be the normalized surface measure on
\displaystyle\partial B(y,r)

[
1
, 14]

\displaystyle x=y
in (

\displaystyle\varphi
is harmonic. Functions of the form given in (

1

\displaystyle f=\chi_{A}
for
\displaystyle A\in\mathcal{B}(\partial D)

\displaystyle x\in D

\displaystyle\displaystyle\omega_{D}^{x}(A)=\mathbb{E}\left(\chi_{A}\left(x+B_{\tau_{D}^{x}}\right)\right).

Observe that if
\displaystyle D
is the epigraph of a Lipschitz function, then by Harnack’s inequality and a Harnack chain argument, for any
\displaystyle x_{0}\in D
, there exist positive constants
\displaystyle c_{1},c_{2}

\displaystyle S,x,x_{0}
but not on
\displaystyle A

\displaystyle\displaystyle c_{1}\omega_{D}^{x_{0}}(A)\leq\omega_{D}^{x}(A)\leq c_{2}\omega_{D}^{x_{0}}(A)

Hence if

is measurable and not a null set of
\displaystyle\omega_{D}^{x}

\displaystyle\omega_{D}^{x_{0}}

\displaystyle A

\displaystyle x\longmapsto\omega^{x}_{D}(A)

We now specify the domain

2.4
Harmonic Measure and Hausdorff Measure: Dahlberg’s Theorem

In 1977, B.E.J. Dahlberg
[
4
]
established an important connection between the harmonic measure and Hausdorff measure. To be more precise, he proved that the harmonic measure
\displaystyle\omega^{x_{0}}
on the boundary
\displaystyle\partial D
of a Lipschitz domain
\displaystyle D\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, w.r.t to a pole
\displaystyle x_{0}\in D
, is mutually absolutely continuous to the
\displaystyle d-1
-dimensional Hausdorff measure,
\displaystyle\mathcal{H}^{d-1}
, on
\displaystyle\partial D
. Moreover, the Radon-Nikodym derivative of
\displaystyle\omega^{x_{0}}
w.r.t to
\displaystyle\mathbb{H}^{d-1}
even is locally in
\displaystyle L^{2+\varepsilon}(\partial D)
, for some
\displaystyle\varepsilon>0
. Since the surface measure on
\displaystyle\partial D
is just a multiple of
\displaystyle\mathcal{H}^{d-1}
restricted to the boundary of a Lipschitz domain, we have the same statements for the surface measure
\displaystyle s
on
\displaystyle\partial D
, as stated in
[
1
]
.

Theorem 2.9

(Dahlberg
[
4
]
)
.

Let
\displaystyle D\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be either a bounded Lipschitz domain or the epigraph of a Lipschitz function and
\displaystyle x_{0}\in D
. Then the following statements hold:

(i)

\displaystyle\omega^{z_{0}}
and
\displaystyle s
are mutually absolutely continuous.

(ii)

If
\displaystyle d
is the Radon-Nikodym derivative of
\displaystyle\omega^{z_{0}}
with respect to
\displaystyle s
, then there exists
\displaystyle\varepsilon>0
, such that
\displaystyle d\in L^{2+\varepsilon}_{\text{loc}}(S)
.

This powerful theorem enables us to transfer knowledge about the geometry of Lipschitz domains to properties of their harmonic measure.´

2.5
The Martin kernel for epigraphs of Lipschitz domains

We will now observe that epigraphs of Lipschitz functions have ”Poisson” kernels.
[
1
, 192
\psq
]

Theorem 2.10

(Martin Kernel
[
1
]
)
.

Let
\displaystyle D\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be the epigraph of a Lipschitz function. Then for any
\displaystyle x,x_{0}\in D
, the harmonic measures
\displaystyle\omega^{x}
is absolutely continuous to
\displaystyle\omega^{x_{0}}
. Furthermore there exists a positive and continuous function
\displaystyle k^{x_{0}}:D\times\partial D\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}^{+}
satisfying

\displaystyle\displaystyle\omega^{x}(A)=\int_{A}k^{x_{0}}(x,\xi)\mathrm{d}\omega^{x_{0}}(\xi)\quad\text{for any}\quad A\in\mathcal{B}(\partial D)

and

\displaystyle\displaystyle x\longmapsto k^{x_{0}}(x,\xi)\quad\text{is harmonic for all}\quad\xi\in\partial D.

We call the function
\displaystyle k^{x_{0}}
the Martin kernel of
\displaystyle D
at
\displaystyle x_{0}
.
In particular, the Radon-Nikodym derivative of
\displaystyle\omega^{x}
with respect to
\displaystyle\omega^{x_{0}}
is given by
\displaystyle k^{x_{0}}(x,\cdot)
.

The fact that
\displaystyle\omega_{D}^{x}
is absolutely continuous w.r.t.
\displaystyle\omega_{D}^{x_{0}}
is a consequence of Harnack’s inequality, i.e. since for any measurable
\displaystyle A\subset D
, that is no
\displaystyle\omega^{x}_{D}
-nullset, the mapping
\displaystyle x\mapsto\omega_{D}^{x}(A)
is a positive harmonic function, thus there exists a constant
\displaystyle c
, independent of
\displaystyle A
, such that
\displaystyle\omega^{x}_{D}(A)\leq c\omega_{D}^{x_{0}}(A)
. With the same reasoning and the roles reversed, we even obtain, that
\displaystyle\omega_{D}^{x}
and
\displaystyle\omega_{D}^{x_{0}}
are equivalent. Their Radon-Nikodym derivative is thus positive
\displaystyle\omega_{D}^{x}
a.e. and
\displaystyle\omega_{D}^{x_{0}}
a.e.. Now given that
\displaystyle k^{x_{0}}
is continuous, it is positive everywhere on
\displaystyle D
.

Combining the Martin kernel with Theorem
2.8
, an immediate consequence is the next lemma.

Lemma 2.11
.

Let
\displaystyle D
be the epigraph of a Lipschitz domain and
\displaystyle x_{0},x\in D
, then for all continuous and bounded
\displaystyle f:\partial D\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}
we have

\displaystyle\displaystyle\int_{\partial D}f(\xi)\mathrm{d}\omega^{x}(\xi)=\int_{\partial D}k^{x_{0}}(x,\xi)f(\xi)\mathrm{d}\omega^{x_{0}}(\xi),

i.e. integration of
\displaystyle f
against the Martin kernel, retrieves the harmonic extension of
\displaystyle f
to
\displaystyle D
. In particular, if
\displaystyle u
is harmonic in
\displaystyle D
and continuous up to the boundary, then

u(x)=\int_{\partial D}k^{x_{0}}(x,\xi)u(\xi)\mathrm{d}\omega^{x_{0}}(\xi).

What Lemma
2.11
states, is that we can solve the Dirichlet problem on epigraphs of Lipschitz functions with continuous boundary values, using only
\displaystyle\omega^{x_{0}}
at a fixed
\displaystyle x_{0}
and the corresponding Martin kernel
\displaystyle k^{x_{0}}
. This will be exploited heavily throughout.

The Martin kernel on Lipschitz domains has been well studied by Hunt and Wheeden in

The first inequality of its kind, found by Hunt and Wheeden, characterizes the decay of the Martin kernel on bounded Lipschitz domains and is stated below.

Lemma 2.12

(Hunt Wheeden
[

)

Let
\displaystyle D
be a bounded Lipschitz domain,
\displaystyle z_{0}\in D

\displaystyle q_{0}\in\partial D

\displaystyle\|p-q_{0}\|=a

\displaystyle R_{0}:=\Delta_{0}

\displaystyle\displaystyle\|k(p,q)\|_{L^{\infty}(R_{j})}\leq c\frac{c_{j}}{\omega^{z_{0}}(\Delta_{j})},

(9)

where additionally
\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{N}c_{j}\leq c^{\prime}<\infty

\displaystyle\partial D

Remark 2

In fact, the inequality above, even holds for for NTA domains and the sequence of constants

Lemma 2.13

(Decay of Martin Kernel on Near Half Spaces)
.

Let
\displaystyle D
denote an epigraph of a Lipschitz function,
\displaystyle z_{0}\in D

\displaystyle q_{0}\in\partial D

\displaystyle\|p-q_{0}\|=y>0

\displaystyle\Delta_{j}:=B(q_{0},2^{j}y)\cap S

\displaystyle\displaystyle\|k(p,q)\|_{L^{\infty}(R_{j})}\leq\begin{cases}\frac{c2^{-\alpha j}}{\omega^{z_{0}}(\Delta_{j})},\quad&j\in\{0,\dots,i-1\}\\

\displaystyle z_{0}

\displaystyle\displaystyle\forall j\in\mathbb{N}_{0}:\quad\|k(p,q)\|_{L^{\infty}(R_{j})}\leq\frac{c2^{-\alpha j}}{\omega^{z_{0}}(\Delta_{j})}.

If

3.1
Kernels

The term

will refer to any function on
\displaystyle S\times S
that defines a meaningful integral operator in the following way: Let
\displaystyle q:S\times S\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}
be a kernel and set

M_{q}:=\left\{f:S\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}:\int_{S}q(x,\xi)f(\xi)\mathrm{d}\omega^{z_{0}}(\xi)\quad\text{exists in }\mathbb{R}\right\}.

Then the integral operator

is defined as

(Qf)(x):=\int_{S}q(x,\xi)f(\xi)\mathrm{d}\omega^{z_{0}}(\xi).

Furthermore we will make use of the convention, that kernels are denoted as lowercase letters and that the integral operator induced by the kernel is denoted by the respective uppercase letter. The concatenation of two kernels

is defined as

(p\circ q)(x,\xi):=\int_{S}p(x,\zeta)q(\zeta,\xi)\mathrm{d}\omega^{z_{0}}(\zeta),

if well-defined.

Definition 3.1
.

(Martin Kernel and Harmonic Extension Operator)
The operator

(K_{y}u)(x)=\int_{S}k(x_{y},\xi)u(\xi)\mathrm{d}\omega^{z_{0}}(\xi)

is well-defined since integration of a function
\displaystyle u
given on
\displaystyle S
with respect to the measure
\displaystyle k_{y}\cdot\mathrm{d}\omega^{z_{0}}

\displaystyle u
to
\displaystyle\mathcal{O}
at the point
\displaystyle x_{y}

Lemma 3.2

(Properties of kernel
\displaystyle k_{y}

[
13
]
)
.

Let
\displaystyle k
be the Martin kernel for the domain
\displaystyle\mathcal{O}
with boundary
\displaystyle\partial\mathcal{O}=S
and
\displaystyle y>0
. Then
\displaystyle k_{y}
has the following properties

1.

Let
\displaystyle u
be a harmonic function on
\displaystyle\mathcal{O}

\displaystyle\displaystyle K_{y_{2}}(u_{y_{1}}|_{S})(x)=u_{y_{1}+y_{2}}(x).

2.

(Semi-group property) For

\displaystyle\displaystyle k_{y_{1}}\circ k_{y_{2}}=k_{y_{1}+y_{2}}.

3.

(Quotient bound) For
\displaystyle 0<y_{1}\leq y_{2}

\displaystyle\frac{k_{y_{2}}}{k_{y_{1}}}

\displaystyle\displaystyle\frac{k_{y_{2}}}{k_{y_{1}}}\leq c\left(\frac{y_{2}}{y_{1}}\right)^{\alpha}.

is a constant only depending on the boundary
\displaystyle S
and
\displaystyle c=c_{d}

\displaystyle d

4.

\displaystyle K_{y}(1)=1
.

5.

Let
\displaystyle y\geq z>0
and
\displaystyle\tau\geq 0

\displaystyle\displaystyle|k_{y+\tau}-k_{y}|\leq c_{S}\left(\left(y+\tau\right)^{\alpha}-y^{\alpha}\right)\frac{k_{z}}{z^{\alpha}}.

\displaystyle\displaystyle\int_{a}^{b}\left|k_{y+\tau}-k_{y}\right|\mathrm{d}y\leq c_{S}\tau(b-a)(b+\tau)^{\alpha-1}\frac{k_{z}}{z^{\alpha}}.

We prove properties 1 to 5

1.

Since
\displaystyle k_{y_{2}}(\zeta,\xi)

Follows directly from Lemma (

We begin by observing that for

:

\displaystyle\displaystyle\begin{aligned} |\pi_{A}|&\leq k_{\gamma(A)}\prod_{s\in A}\left(c_{S}\varepsilon\varrho(\Delta)^{\alpha-1}\frac{|j_{s}|}{m(\Delta)}\right)\\
&\leq k_{\gamma(A)}\frac{1}{K^{q}}\left(c_{S}\lambda(1+\beta)^{\alpha-1}\frac{\varepsilon|\Delta|}{m(\Delta)}\right)^{q}\\
&\leq k_{m(\Delta)}c_{S}\frac{\left(2M(\Delta)\right)^{\alpha}}{m(\Delta)^{\alpha}}\frac{q^{\alpha}}{K^{q}}\left(c_{S,\beta}\lambda\frac{\varepsilon|\Delta|}{m(\Delta)}\right)^{q}\\
&\leq k_{m(\Delta)}\underbrace{c_{S}(2(1+\beta))^{\alpha}(c_{S,\beta})^{2}}_{:=c_{S,\beta}}\frac{\varepsilon^{2}|\Delta|^{2}}{m(\Delta)^{2}}\frac{q^{\alpha}}{K^{q}}\lambda^{2}\left(c_{S,\beta}\lambda\frac{\varepsilon|\Delta|}{m(\Delta)}\right)^{q-2}\end{aligned}

(15)

Since
\displaystyle\binom{K}{q}\leq\frac{K^{q}}{q!}
, we obtain the following bound:

\displaystyle\displaystyle|\rho_{\mu}|

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq c_{S,\beta}k_{m(\Delta)}\frac{\varepsilon^{2}|\Delta|^{2}}{m(\Delta)^{2}}\sum_{q=2}^{K}\frac{q^{\alpha}}{q!}\lambda^{2}\left(c_{S,\beta}\lambda\varepsilon\frac{|\Delta|}{m(\Delta)}\right)^{q-2}.

With the inequality

\displaystyle\displaystyle\sum_{q=2}^{K}\frac{q^{\alpha}}{q!}\lambda^{2}\left(c_{S,\beta}\lambda\varepsilon\frac{|\Delta|}{m(\Delta)}\right)^{q-2}\leq\sum_{q=2}^{\infty}\frac{q^{\alpha}}{q!}\lambda^{2}(\varepsilon c_{S,\lambda,\beta})^{q-2}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\varepsilon<1}}{{\leq}}c_{\{S,\lambda,\beta\}},

we arrive at

\displaystyle\displaystyle|\rho_{\mu}|\leq c_{\{S,\lambda,\beta\}}\frac{\varepsilon^{2}|\Delta|^{2}}{m(\Delta)^{2}}k_{m(\Delta)}.

∎

Lemma 3.18

(
[
13
]
)
.

For
\displaystyle\lambda
-regular partitions
\displaystyle\mu=\left\{j_{1},\cdots,j_{K}\right\}
of
\displaystyle\Delta\in\mathrm{segm}^{+}
satisfying
\displaystyle|\Delta|\leq\beta m(\Delta)
for some
\displaystyle\beta>0
, we have the following bound

\displaystyle\displaystyle|\Pi^{\mu}-\tilde{\omega}_{\Delta}|

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq c_{\{S,\lambda,\beta\}}\left(\varrho(\Delta)^{\alpha-1}+\varrho(\Delta)^{2\alpha-1}+1\right)\frac{\varepsilon|\Delta|^{2}}{m(\Delta)^{2}}k_{m(\Delta)}

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq c_{\{S,\lambda,\beta\}}\frac{\varepsilon|\Delta|^{2}}{m(\Delta)^{2}}k_{m(\Delta)}.

Proof.

Combining Lemmata
3.15
and
3.17
, we immediately obtain the desired inequality

\displaystyle\displaystyle|\Pi^{\mu}-\tilde{\omega}_{\Delta}|

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq\varepsilon\sum_{s=1}^{K}|b_{j_{s}}-v_{s}|+|\rho_{\mu}|

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq\left(c_{S}\left(\varrho(\Delta)^{\alpha-1}+\varrho(\Delta)^{2\alpha-1}\right)+c_{\{S,\lambda,\beta\}}\right)\frac{\varepsilon|\Delta|^{2}}{m(\Delta)^{2}}k_{m(\Delta)},

since
\displaystyle\varepsilon<1
. The second inequality stated in the Lemma follows from the simple observation that
\displaystyle\varrho(\Delta)\leq 1+\beta
.
∎

Lemma 3.19

(
[
13
]
)
.

For
\displaystyle\lambda
-regular partitions
\displaystyle\mu=\left\{j_{1},\cdots,j_{K}\right\}
of
\displaystyle\Delta\in\mathrm{segm}^{+}
satisfying
\displaystyle|\Delta|\leq\beta m(\Delta)
for some
\displaystyle\beta>0
and
\displaystyle 0<m(\Delta)<1

\displaystyle|\Pi^{\mu}|

\displaystyle\displaystyle|\Pi^{\mu}|\leq k_{|\Delta|}+c_{\{S,\lambda,\beta\}}\frac{\varepsilon|\Delta|}{m(\Delta)}k_{m(\Delta)}\leq k_{|\Delta|}+c_{\{S,\lambda,\beta\}}\varepsilon k_{m(\Delta)}.

Proof.

Considering the definition of
\displaystyle\tilde{\omega}_{\Delta}=k_{|\Delta|}-\varepsilon b_{\Delta}
we obtain the bound

\displaystyle\displaystyle|\tilde{\omega}_{\Delta}|

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq k_{|\Delta|}+\varepsilon c_{S}\frac{\varrho(\Delta)^{\alpha-1}}{m(\Delta)}|\Delta|k_{m(\Delta)}

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq k_{|\Delta|}+\varepsilon c_{S}\frac{(1+\beta)^{\alpha-1}}{m(\Delta)}|\Delta|k_{m(\Delta)}.

Furthermore Lemma
3.18
yields the bound

\displaystyle\displaystyle|\Pi^{\mu}-\tilde{\omega}_{\Delta}|

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq c_{\{S,\lambda,\beta\}}\frac{\varepsilon|\Delta|^{2}}{m(\Delta)^{2}}k_{m(\Delta)}\leq c_{\{S,\lambda,\beta\}}\frac{\varepsilon\beta|\Delta|}{m(\Delta)}k_{m(\Delta)}.

Combining these two estimate with the estimate, we immediately obtain the desired bound.
∎

The bounds on
\displaystyle\Pi^{\mu}
, finally give reassurance that in fact the iterated kernel is again nice in a sense that it is majorized by the Martin kernel. In particular, it is again continuous and integrable w.r.t. to the measure
\displaystyle\kappa\otimes\omega^{z_{0}}
, for an arbitrary bounded measure
\displaystyle\kappa
on
\displaystyle S
.

Now we present one more minor technical detail, which seems trivial but is critical in the next lemma, thus it will be separately stated.

Lemma 3.20
.

Let
\displaystyle\mu
be any partition of
\displaystyle\Delta\in\mathrm{segm}^{+}
,
\displaystyle\nu\subseteq\mu
a subpartition of
\displaystyle\mu
and
\displaystyle\beta>0,\lambda\geq 1
. Then the following holds:

1.

If
\displaystyle|\Delta|\leq\beta m(\Delta)
then
\displaystyle|\mathcal{U}(\nu)|\leq\beta m(\mathcal{U}(\nu)).

2.

If
\displaystyle\mu
is a
\displaystyle\lambda
-regular partition of
\displaystyle\Delta
, then
\displaystyle\nu
is a
\displaystyle\lambda
-regular partition of
\displaystyle\mathcal{U}(\nu)
.

Proof.

1.

Obviously we have
\displaystyle m(\Delta)\leq m(\mathcal{U}(\nu))
. With this we obtain,

\displaystyle\displaystyle|\mathcal{U}(\nu)|=\sum_{j\in\nu}|j|\leq|\Delta|\leq\beta m(\Delta)\leq\beta m(\mathcal{U}(\nu)).

2.

Since
\displaystyle\nu\subseteq\mu
we immediately have the inequality

\displaystyle\displaystyle\sup_{\Delta\in\nu}|\Delta|\leq\sup_{\Delta\in\mu}|\Delta|\leq\lambda\inf_{\Delta\in\mu}|\Delta|\leq\lambda\inf_{\Delta\in\nu}|\Delta|.

∎

The main message of the above lemma is that
\displaystyle\lambda
-regularity is preserved for subpartitions, a circumstance that will be exploited in the following Lemma, in which we prove the convergence of the kernels
\displaystyle\Pi^{\mu}
.

Lemma 3.21

(
[
13
]
)
.

Let
\displaystyle\lambda\geq 1
and
\displaystyle\sigma,\tau
be
\displaystyle\lambda
-regular partitions of
\displaystyle\Delta\in\mathrm{segm}^{+}
such that
\displaystyle\sigma\succ\tau
and
\displaystyle|\Delta|\leq\beta m(\Delta)
,
\displaystyle\beta>0
. Then we have

\displaystyle\displaystyle|\Pi^{\tau}-\Pi^{\sigma}|\leq\frac{c_{\{S,\lambda,\beta\}}}{m(\Delta)}\sup_{j\in\tau}|j|k_{m(\Delta)}.

Proof.

Suppose
\displaystyle|\tau|=K
and write
\displaystyle\tau=\{\Delta_{1},\dots,\Delta_{K}\}
with
\displaystyle m(\Delta_{1})<\dots<m(\Delta_{K})
. Define
\displaystyle\sigma_{k}
as the partition of
\displaystyle\Delta_{k}
by
\displaystyle\sigma
, thus
\displaystyle\sigma_{k}:=\{j\in\sigma|j\subseteq\Delta_{k}\}
. Obviously
\displaystyle\sigma=\bigcup_{1\leq k\leq K}\sigma_{k}.

Now fix
\displaystyle i\in\{2,\dots,K\}
and define
\displaystyle\sigma_{i}^{-}:=\bigcup_{0\leq k<i}\sigma_{k}
as the the partition of the interval
\displaystyle\left(m(\Delta_{1}),m(\Delta_{i})\right)
by
\displaystyle\sigma
. Analogously define
\displaystyle\tau_{i}^{+}=\bigcup_{i<k\leq K}\{\Delta_{k}\}
as the partition of the interval
\displaystyle\left(m(\Delta_{i+1}),M(\Delta_{K})\right)
by
\displaystyle\tau
. Additionally, we set
\displaystyle\sigma_{1}^{-}=\emptyset=\tau_{K+1}^{+}
, Finally we denote
\displaystyle\tau(i)=\sigma_{i}^{-}\cup\{\Delta_{i}\}\cup\tau_{i}^{+}
and
\displaystyle\tau(K+1)=\sigma
. Writing
\displaystyle\Pi^{\tau(i)}:=\Pi_{i}
, this yields by telescoping

\displaystyle\displaystyle\Pi^{\tau}-\Pi^{\sigma}=\sum_{i=1}^{K}\left(\Pi_{i}-\Pi_{i+1}\right).

(16)

For
\displaystyle 1\leq i\leq K
we now have

\displaystyle\displaystyle\Pi_{i}-\Pi_{i+1}=\Pi^{\tau_{i}^{+}}\circ\left(\tilde{\omega}_{\Delta_{i}}-\Pi^{\sigma_{i}}\right)\circ\Pi^{\sigma_{i}^{-}}.

(17)

In equation (
17
) we have rewritten the difference of two subsequent refinements from left to right as concatenation of kernels depending on the partitions:

\displaystyle\displaystyle\tau_{i}^{+}\quad

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathrm{of}\quad\Delta_{i}^{+}:=\mathcal{U}(\tau_{i}^{+})

\displaystyle\displaystyle\sigma_{i}\quad

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathrm{of}\quad\Delta_{i}

\displaystyle\displaystyle\sigma_{i}^{-}\quad

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathrm{of}\quad\Delta_{i}^{-}:=\mathcal{U}(\sigma_{i}^{-})

Since
\displaystyle|\Delta|\leq\beta m(\Delta)
, Lemma
3.20
guarantees, that for each partition above, the measure of its union is bounded by the smallest number in the union with constant
\displaystyle\beta
. Furthermore, again by Lemma
3.20
, since
\displaystyle\tau
and
\displaystyle\sigma
are
\displaystyle\lambda
-regular, the partitions above all are again
\displaystyle\lambda
-regular. Hence Lemmata
3.18
and
3.19
apply and we have bounds for every summand in (
16
). By the triangle inequality and
\displaystyle\varepsilon<1
, we get after lengthy computations:

\displaystyle\displaystyle|\Pi_{i}-\Pi_{i+1}|

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq\left(k_{|\Delta_{i}^{+}|}+c_{\{S,\lambda,\beta\}}k_{m(\Delta_{i}^{+})}\right)\circ\left(c_{\{S,\lambda,\beta\}}\frac{|\Delta_{i}|^{2}}{m(\Delta_{i})^{2}}k_{m(\Delta_{i})}\right)\circ\left(k_{|\Delta_{i}^{-}|}+c_{\{S,\lambda,\beta\}}k_{m(\Delta_{i}^{-})}\right)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=c_{\{S,\lambda,\beta\}}\frac{|\Delta_{i}|^{2}}{m(\Delta_{i})^{2}}\left(k_{|\Delta_{i}^{+}|+m(\Delta_{i})}+c_{\{S,\lambda,\beta\}}k_{m(\Delta_{i}^{+})+m(\Delta_{i})}\right)\circ\left(k_{|\Delta_{i}^{-}|}+c_{\{S,\lambda,\beta\}}k_{m(\Delta_{i}^{-})}\right)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq c_{\{S,\lambda,\beta\}}\frac{|\Delta_{i}|^{2}}{m(\Delta_{i})^{2}}\left(k_{|\Delta_{i}^{+}|+m(\Delta_{i})+|\Delta_{i}^{-}|}+c_{\{S,\lambda,\beta\}}k_{m(\Delta_{i}^{+})+m(\Delta_{i})+|\Delta_{i}^{-}|}\right.

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mkern 175.0mu\left.+c_{\{S,\lambda,\beta\}}k_{|\Delta_{i}^{+}|+m(\Delta_{i})+m(\Delta_{i}^{-})}+c_{\{S,\lambda,\beta\}}k_{m(\Delta_{i}^{+})+m(\Delta_{i})+m(\Delta_{i}^{-})}\right).

A rough estimation yields that the heights

\displaystyle\displaystyle|\Delta_{i}^{+}|+m(\Delta_{i})+|\Delta_{i}^{-}|

\displaystyle\displaystyle m(\Delta_{i}^{+})+m(\Delta_{i})+|\Delta_{i}^{-}|

\displaystyle\displaystyle|\Delta_{i}^{+}|+m(\Delta_{i})+m(\Delta_{i}^{-})

\displaystyle\displaystyle m(\Delta_{i}^{+})+m(\Delta_{i})+m(\Delta_{i}^{-})

are all in the interval
\displaystyle[m(\Delta),3M(\Delta)]
. Thus we proceed using the quotient bound for the Martin kernel in Lemma
3.2
and obtain

\displaystyle\displaystyle|\Pi_{i}-\Pi_{i+1}|

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq c_{\{S,\lambda,\beta\}}\frac{|\Delta_{i}|^{2}}{m(\Delta_{i})^{2}}\cdot c_{S}\left(\frac{3M(\Delta)}{m(\Delta)}\right)^{\alpha}k_{m(\Delta)}

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq c_{\{S,\lambda,\beta\}}\frac{|\Delta_{i}|^{2}}{m(\Delta_{i})^{2}}k_{m(\Delta)},

since
\displaystyle\varrho(\Delta)\leq 1+\beta
..
Calculating the sum in (
16
), we finally obtain the estimate

\displaystyle\displaystyle|\Pi^{\tau}-\Pi^{\sigma}|

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{K}c_{\{S,\lambda,\beta\}}\frac{|\Delta_{i}|^{2}}{m(\Delta_{i})^{2}}k_{m(\Delta)}

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq\frac{c_{\{S,\lambda,\beta\}}}{m(\Delta)^{2}}\sup_{j\in\tau}|j|\sum_{i=1}^{K}|\Delta_{i}|k_{m(\Delta)}

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq\frac{\beta c_{\{S,\lambda,\beta\}}}{m(\Delta)}\sup_{j\in\tau}|j|k_{m(\Delta)},

as
\displaystyle|\Delta|\leq\beta m(\Delta)
.
∎

3.5.1
Weak
\displaystyle\lambda
-regularity

We compare the condition of
\displaystyle\lambda
-regularity given in (
13
) with the following condition:

Let
\displaystyle\tau
be a partition of
\displaystyle\Delta\in\mathrm{segm}^{+}
and
\displaystyle\lambda\geq 1
. We call
\displaystyle\tau
a weakly
\displaystyle\lambda
-regular partition of
\displaystyle\Delta
if

\displaystyle\displaystyle\sup_{j\in\tau}|j|\leq\lambda\frac{\Delta}{|\tau|}.

(18)

The name of this condition is justified, since (
13
) implies (
14
) and thus (
18
).
We make this distinction because in the paper by Havin and Mozolyako
[
11
]
, merely condition (
18
) was imposed on the refining partitions in the proof of Lemma
3.21
. This is sufficient for
\displaystyle 1
-regular partitions, since
\displaystyle 1
-regularity implies that all elements in the partition have equal measure and consequently every subpartition of a
\displaystyle 1
-regular partition is again
\displaystyle 1
-regular. For
\displaystyle\lambda>1
, we will however, demonstrate that this condition leaves a logical gap in the proof of Lemma
3.21
, by showing that in general weak
\displaystyle\lambda
-regularity is not preserved for subpartitions if
\displaystyle\lambda>1
.

As announced, we will shortly digress from the topics above to attend to the following general Problem: Suppose we are given an arbitrary partition
\displaystyle\tau
of
\displaystyle\Delta
, that is weakly
\displaystyle\lambda
-regular. The natural question that arises is whether any subpartition
\displaystyle\tau_{1}\subset\tau
is again a weakly
\displaystyle\lambda
-regular partition of
\displaystyle\mathcal{U}(\tau_{1})
. This statement can be promptly refuted with an easy counterexample.
Consider the intervals

\displaystyle\displaystyle\Delta_{1}=\left[0,\frac{1}{16}\right)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\Delta_{2}=\left[\frac{1}{16},\frac{7}{16}\right)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\Delta_{3}=\left[\frac{7}{16},\frac{1}{2}\right)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\Delta_{4}=\left[\frac{1}{2},1\right].

Now
\displaystyle\tau:=\left\{\Delta_{i}|i\in\{1,\dots,4\}\right\}
is a weakly
\displaystyle 2
-regular partition of
\displaystyle\left[0,1\right]
, since

\frac{1}{2}=|\Delta_{4}|=\max_{\Delta\in\tau}|\Delta|\leq 2\frac{|\left[0,1\right]|}{|\tau|}=\frac{1}{2}.

However the partition
\displaystyle\{\Delta_{1},\Delta_{2},\Delta_{3}\}
of
\displaystyle\left[0,\frac{1}{2}\right]
is no longer weakly
\displaystyle 2
-regular as

\frac{3}{8}=|\Delta_{2}|=\max\{|\Delta_{1}|,|\Delta_{2}|,|\Delta_{3}|\}>2\frac{|\left(0,\frac{1}{2}\right)|}{3}=\frac{1}{3}.

The next question that arises, is whether it is possible to derive a bound on the constant of regularity of an arbitrary subpartition. The following attempt delivers the sought after estimates but only under some restricting assumptions on the subpartition.

Theorem 3.22
.

Let
\displaystyle\Delta
be an interval with
\displaystyle|\Delta|<\infty
and
\displaystyle\tau
be a weakly
\displaystyle\lambda
-regular partition of
\displaystyle\Delta
. Furthermore let
\displaystyle\mu\subset\tau
such that
\displaystyle\lambda|\mu|<|\tau|
. Then the partition
\displaystyle\tau_{1}:=\tau\setminus\mu
of the set
\displaystyle\mathcal{U}(\tau_{1})
is weakly regular with constant
\displaystyle\frac{\lambda|\tau_{1}|}{|\tau|-\lambda|\mu|}
, so in particular we have

\sup_{j\in\tau_{1}}|j|\leq\frac{\lambda|\tau_{1}|}{|\tau|-\lambda|\mu|}\frac{|\mathcal{U}(\tau_{1})|}{|\tau_{1}|}=\frac{\lambda}{|\tau|-\lambda|\mu|}|\mathcal{U}(\tau_{1})|.

Proof.

Since
\displaystyle\tau
is weakly
\displaystyle\lambda
-regular and
\displaystyle\sup_{j\in\mu}|j|\leq\sup_{j\in\tau}|j|
, we have the estimates

\displaystyle\displaystyle\lambda\frac{|\mathcal{U}(\tau_{1})|}{|\tau_{1}|}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\frac{\lambda}{|\tau_{1}|}\left(|\mathcal{U}(\tau)|-|\mathcal{U}(\mu)|\right)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{|\tau_{1}|}\left(|\tau|\sup_{j\in\tau}|j|-\lambda\sum_{j\in\mu}|j|\right)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\geq\frac{|\tau|-\lambda|\mu|}{|\tau_{1}|}\sup_{j\in\tau}|j|.

Rearranging yields the desired inequality.
∎

Remark 5
.

In the setting of Theorem
3.22
, the estimate above gives us a useful bound on the constant of regularity of the partition
\displaystyle\tau_{1}
that results after taking away a sufficiently small subset
\displaystyle\mu
of the original partition
\displaystyle\tau
. The condition
\displaystyle\lambda|\mu|<|\tau|
can be reformulated as

\lambda|\mu|<|\tau|\Longleftrightarrow\lambda\left(|\tau|-|\tau_{1}|\right)<|\tau|\Longleftrightarrow|\tau_{1}|>\frac{\lambda-1}{\lambda}|\tau|.

Thus, depending on the constant of weak regularity of
\displaystyle\tau
we obtain information about the constant of weak regularity of subsets
\displaystyle\tau_{1}
with cardinality strictly larger than
\displaystyle\frac{\lambda-1}{\lambda}|\tau|
.

For
\displaystyle\lambda=1
we can obtain a bound since
\displaystyle|\tau_{1}|\geq 1>0
, however in this case we anyways have that
\displaystyle\tau_{1}
is weakly
\displaystyle 1
regular since weak
\displaystyle 1
-regularity of
\displaystyle\tau
is equivalent to all elements of
\displaystyle\tau
having the same length.

If
\displaystyle\lambda
is large, we obtain no useful information from Theorem
3.22
. Now consider the case that
\displaystyle\lambda=1+\varepsilon
with small
\displaystyle\varepsilon>0
. Then we can bound subpartitions
\displaystyle\tau_{1}
that have at least
\displaystyle\frac{\varepsilon}{1+\varepsilon}|\tau|
elements however, no useful bound can be derived for subpartitions with smaller cardinality. This begs the question, whether it is even possible to a bound the constant of weak regularity for arbitrarily small subpartitions with respect to
\displaystyle\tau
. This problem is negatively answered in the following Theorem.

Theorem 3.23
.

Let
\displaystyle\Delta
with
\displaystyle|\Delta|<\infty
,
\displaystyle A\in\mathbb{N}
and
\displaystyle\lambda>1
. Then there exists a weakly
\displaystyle\lambda
-regular partition
\displaystyle\tau
of
\displaystyle\Delta
and disjoint subpartitions
\displaystyle\tau_{1},\tau_{2}\subset\tau
, i.e.
\displaystyle\tau_{1}\cap\tau_{2}=\emptyset
, such that
\displaystyle\tau=\tau_{1}\cup\tau_{2}
and

\displaystyle\displaystyle\sup_{j\in\tau_{1}}|j|\geq A\cdot\frac{|\mathcal{U}(\tau_{1})|}{|\tau_{1}|}.

(19)

Proof.

We first construct a partition without taking into account the interval we obtain when taking the union over the partition and then specify the construction to any given bounded interval.

In the first step we construct
\displaystyle\tau_{1}
. Let
\displaystyle\omega>0
and define intervals
\displaystyle\Delta_{0},\dots,\Delta_{A}
such that

M(\Delta_{i})=m(\Delta_{i+1})\quad\text{for all }i\in\{0,\dots,A-1\},

\displaystyle|\Delta_{0}|=\omega
and
\displaystyle|\Delta_{i}|=\frac{\omega}{A^{2}}
for
\displaystyle i\in\{1,\dots,A\}
. Obviously
\displaystyle|\Delta_{0}|\geq|\Delta_{i}|
holds. We set
\displaystyle\tau_{1}=\{\Delta_{0},\dots\Delta_{A}\}
and verify equation (
19
):

\displaystyle\displaystyle A\cdot\frac{|\mathcal{U}(\tau_{1})|}{|\tau_{1}|}=\frac{A}{A+1}\left(\omega+A\cdot\frac{\omega}{A^{2}}\right)=\omega=\sup_{j\in\tau_{1}}|j|.

Choose
\displaystyle\varepsilon>0
with
\displaystyle\frac{1+\varepsilon}{\lambda}<1
and define
\displaystyle N\in\mathbb{N}
,
\displaystyle N>A+1
large enough such that the inequality

\displaystyle\displaystyle(N-A-1)(1+\varepsilon)\geq\lambda\left(\frac{N}{\lambda}-1-\frac{1}{A}\right)

(20)

is satisfied. This choice is possible since the quotient

q_{n}:=\frac{n-A-1}{\frac{n}{\lambda}-1-\frac{1}{A}}

converges to
\displaystyle\lambda
, so in particular
\displaystyle q_{n}\geq\frac{\lambda}{1+\varepsilon}
for large enough
\displaystyle n
. We proceed the construction by defining intervals
\displaystyle\Delta_{A+1},\dots,\Delta_{N}
satisfying

M(\Delta_{i})=m(\Delta_{i+1})\quad\text{for all }i\in\{A,\dots,N-1\},

and
\displaystyle|\Delta_{i}|=\frac{1+\varepsilon}{\lambda}\omega
for all
\displaystyle i\in\{A+1,\dots,N\}
. By our choice of
\displaystyle\varepsilon
we have
\displaystyle|\Delta_{i}|<\omega
, so denoting
\displaystyle\tau:=\{\Delta_{0},\dots,\Delta_{N}\}
we obtain
\displaystyle\sup_{j\in\tau}|j|=\omega
. This construction yields a weakly
\displaystyle\lambda
-regular partition
\displaystyle\tau
since

\displaystyle\displaystyle\lambda\frac{|\mathcal{U}(\tau)|}{|\tau|}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\frac{\lambda}{N}\left(\sum_{j\in\tau_{1}}|j|+\sum_{j\in\tau\setminus\tau_{1}}|j|\right)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\frac{\lambda}{N}\left(\omega+\frac{\omega}{A}+(N-A-1)\frac{1+\varepsilon}{\lambda}\omega\right)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:lim_cond}}}{{\geq}}\frac{\lambda\omega}{N}\left(1+\frac{1}{A}+\left(\frac{N}{\lambda}-1-\frac{1}{A}\right)\right)=\omega.

Now let
\displaystyle\Delta
be a bounded interval. The computations above show that
\displaystyle|\mathcal{U}(\tau)|
is a non-zero multiple of
\displaystyle\omega
so we can determine
\displaystyle\omega
such that
\displaystyle|\mathcal{U}(\tau)|=|\Delta|
. If we choose
\displaystyle m(\Delta_{0})=m(\Delta)
, the construction above yields a desired partition
\displaystyle\tau
of
\displaystyle\Delta
.
∎

This result essentially tells us, that for any weakly
\displaystyle\lambda
-regular partition, the weak regularity of an arbitrary subpartition cannot be bounded. Therefore the process of piecewise refinement of a coarse partition to a fine partition, that the proof of Lemma
3.21
heavily relies on, is not applicable to arbitrary weakly
\displaystyle\lambda
-regular partitions. This can be seen as follows. Let
\displaystyle\lambda>1
be arbitrary and assume that the constant of regularity of any subpartition of a weakly
\displaystyle\lambda
-regular partition can be bounded by some
\displaystyle C
. Now we take the weakly
\displaystyle\lambda
-regular partition
\displaystyle\tau
constructed in Theorem
3.23
such that the subpartition
\displaystyle\tau_{1}
is not
\displaystyle A
-regular, where
\displaystyle A>C
. Construct the partition
\displaystyle\mu
by halving all intervals in
\displaystyle\tau
i.e.

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mu:=\left\{\left[m(\Delta),m(\Delta)+\frac{|\Delta|}{2}\right],\left[m(\Delta)+\frac{|\Delta|}{2},M(\Delta)\right]:\Delta\in\tau\right\}.

Analogously, construct
\displaystyle\mu_{1}
as the collection of all halved intervals in
\displaystyle\tau_{1}
. Now observe that
\displaystyle\mu
is again weakly
\displaystyle\lambda
-regular and similarily
\displaystyle\mu_{1}
is again not weakly
\displaystyle A
-regular, since

\displaystyle\displaystyle\sup_{j\in\tau}|j|=2\sup_{j\in\mu}|j|\quad\text{and}\quad|\tau|=2|\mu|

as well as

\displaystyle\displaystyle\sup_{j\in\tau_{1}}|j|=2\sup_{j\in\mu_{1}}|j|\quad\text{and}\quad|\tau_{1}|=2|\mu_{1}|.

Obviously
\displaystyle\mu
is a refinement of
\displaystyle\tau
and
\displaystyle\mu_{1}
a refinement of
\displaystyle\tau_{1}
.

3.6
The kernel
\displaystyle\omega_{\Delta}
and its properties

Finally we have arrived at a point where we can define the kernel
\displaystyle\omega_{\Delta}
and guarantee its existence.

Theorem 3.24

(
[
13
]
)
.

Fix
\displaystyle\lambda\geq 1
,
\displaystyle\Delta\in\mathrm{segm}^{+}
and let
\displaystyle(\tau_{n}(\Delta))_{n}=(\tau_{n})_{n}
be a sequence of
\displaystyle\lambda
-regular partitions such that for all
\displaystyle n\in\mathbb{N}
we have
\displaystyle\tau_{n+1}\succ\tau_{n}
and

\displaystyle\displaystyle\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\sup_{j\in\tau_{n}}|j|=0.

(21)

Then, for any compact
\displaystyle\mathcal{K}\subset S
, the sequence of continuous kernels
\displaystyle\left(\Pi^{\tau_{n}}\right)_{n}\subset C(S\times S)
is uniformly convergent and independent of the approximating sequence of partitions and their degree of regularity. Furthermore there exists a function
\displaystyle\omega_{\Delta}\in C(S\times S)
such that

\displaystyle\displaystyle\omega_{\Delta}:=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\Pi^{\tau_{n}}\quad\text{on }\mathcal{K}\times S

(22)

for any compact subset
\displaystyle\mathcal{K}
of
\displaystyle S
.

Proof.

Fix any compact
\displaystyle\mathcal{K}\subset S
. By Lemma
3.21
we have

\displaystyle\displaystyle\forall m\geq n:\quad\left|\Pi^{\tau_{m}}-\Pi^{\tau_{n}}\right|\leq C\sup_{j\in\tau_{n}}|j|k_{m(\Delta)}

(23)

everywhere on
\displaystyle S\times S
. Lemma
2.13.1
implies
\displaystyle\left\|k_{m(\Delta)}\right\|_{C(\mathcal{K}\times S)}<\infty
, hence
\displaystyle(\Pi^{\tau_{n}})_{n}:\mathcal{K}\times S\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}

3.6.1
Positivity of
\displaystyle\omega_{\Delta}
and consequences

Lemma 3.27

(
[
13
]
)
.

There exists
\displaystyle\varepsilon(S)>0
such that for any
\displaystyle\varepsilon\in(0,\varepsilon(S))
, the kernel
\displaystyle\omega_{\Delta}
is positive if
\displaystyle|\Delta|\geq m(\Delta)
.

Proof.

We begin by showing that for any
\displaystyle\Delta\in\mathrm{segm}^{+}
satisfying
\displaystyle m(\Delta)\leq|\Delta|\leq 3m(\Delta)
and any
\displaystyle\lambda
-regular partition
\displaystyle\mu=\{j_{1},\dots,j_{K}\}
of
\displaystyle\Delta
, the kernel
\displaystyle\Pi^{\mu}
is positive and pointwise bounded away from
\displaystyle 0
. As can be seen in the discussion following Definition
3.14
we are able to write:

\displaystyle\displaystyle\Pi^{\mu}=k_{|\Delta|}-\varepsilon\sum_{s=1}^{K}w_{s}+\rho_{\mu},

where
\displaystyle\rho_{\mu}
is as in (
12
) and
\displaystyle w_{s}=k_{M(\Delta)-M(j_{s})}\circ b_{j_{s}}\circ k_{m(j_{s})-m(\Delta)}
. Due to the bound on
\displaystyle|b_{y}|
in Lemma
3.12
, and
\displaystyle\varrho(\Delta)\leq 4
, we have

\displaystyle\displaystyle|w_{s}|

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq k_{M(\Delta)-M(j_{s})}\circ|b_{j_{s}}|\circ k_{m(j_{s})-m(\Delta)}

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq c_{S}\frac{\varrho(j_{s})^{\alpha-1}}{m(j_{s})}|j_{s}|k_{M(\Delta)-M(j_{s})+m(j_{s})+m(j_{s})-m(\Delta)}

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq c_{S}\frac{4^{\alpha-1}}{m(\Delta)}|j_{s}|k_{|\Delta|-|j_{s}|+m(j_{s})}=\frac{c_{S}}{m(\Delta)}|j_{s}|k_{|\Delta|-|j_{s}|+m(j_{s})}.

Since
\displaystyle|\Delta|-|j_{s}|+m(j_{s})\geq m(j_{s})\geq m(\Delta)

and
\displaystyle\frac{|\Delta|-|j_{s}|+m(j_{s})}{m(\Delta)}\leq\frac{|\Delta|+M(\Delta)}{m(\Delta)}\leq 7
, we can use the quotient estimate in Lemma
3.2
to obtain

\displaystyle\displaystyle|w_{s}|\leq\frac{c_{S}}{m(\Delta)}|j_{s}|\left(\frac{|\Delta|-|j_{s}|+m(j_{s})}{m(\Delta)}\right)^{\alpha}k_{m(\Delta)}\leq c_{S}\frac{|j_{s}|}{m(\Delta)}k_{m(\Delta)}.

Summing up yields the bound

\displaystyle\displaystyle\sum_{s=1}^{K}|w_{s}|\leq c_{S}\frac{|\Delta|}{m(\Delta)}k_{m(\Delta)}\leq c_{S}3^{\alpha}k_{m(\Delta)}=c_{S}k_{m(\Delta)}.

In Lemma
3.17
we derived a bound on
\displaystyle|\rho_{\mu}|
, in the current setting
\displaystyle\beta=3
, thus

\displaystyle\displaystyle|\rho_{\mu}|\leq c_{S,\lambda}\frac{\varepsilon^{2}|\Delta|^{2}}{m(\Delta)^{2}}k_{m(\Delta)}\leq c_{S,\lambda}3^{\alpha}\varepsilon^{2}k_{m(\Delta)}.

The last step comprises finding a lower bound for
\displaystyle k_{|\Delta|}
of the form:
\displaystyle k_{|\Delta|}\geq Ck_{m(\Delta)}
, for some constant
\displaystyle C>0
. Since
\displaystyle m(\Delta)\leq|\Delta|
we use Lemma
2.4
and obtain

\displaystyle\displaystyle k_{|\Delta|}\geq c\left(\frac{m(\Delta)}{|\Delta|}\right)^{\alpha}k_{m(\Delta)}\geq\frac{c}{3^{\alpha}}k_{m(\Delta)}=c_{S}k_{m(\Delta)}.

In total we estimate

\displaystyle\displaystyle\Pi^{\mu}\geq c_{S}k_{m(\Delta)}-\varepsilon c_{S}k_{m(\Delta)}-\varepsilon^{2}c_{S,\lambda}k_{m(\Delta)}>0

for fixed
\displaystyle\lambda
and
\displaystyle\varepsilon>0
small enough, in particular there exists
\displaystyle\varepsilon(S)>0
, such that for all
\displaystyle\varepsilon<\varepsilon(S)
, the kernel
\displaystyle\omega_{\Delta}
is positive. Taking the limit on the left side, the inequality is preserved and thus
\displaystyle\omega_{\Delta}>0
if
\displaystyle m(\Delta)\leq|\Delta|\leq 3m(\Delta)
.
Now we apply this statement to prove that
\displaystyle\omega_{\Delta}>0
for
\displaystyle|\Delta|\geq m(\Delta)
. Choose
\displaystyle n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
such that
\displaystyle 2^{n+1}m(\Delta)\leq M(\Delta)\leq 2^{n+2}m(\Delta)
and partition
\displaystyle\Delta
in the following way:

\displaystyle\displaystyle\Delta=\bigcup_{k=0}^{n-1}\left[2^{k}m(\Delta),2^{k+1}m(\Delta)\right]\cup\left[2^{n}m(\Delta),M(\Delta)\right].

Since
\displaystyle 2^{k+1}m(\Delta)-2^{k}m(\Delta)=2^{k}m(\Delta)

\displaystyle 2^{n}m(\Delta)\leq M(\Delta)-2^{n}m(\Delta)\leq 3\cdot 2^{n}m(\Delta)

\displaystyle|B_{J}\psi|

\displaystyle\displaystyle\begin{aligned} |(B_{J}\psi)(x)|\leq\int_{J}K_{t}\left(|C_{t}\psi|\right)(x)\mathrm{d}t&\leq\frac{c_{S}}{y}\int_{J}\left(K_{2t}\psi\right)(x)\mathrm{d}t\\
&=\frac{c_{S}}{y}\int_{J}v_{2t}(x)\mathrm{d}t\leq c_{S}\frac{\psi(x)}{y}|J|\end{aligned}

(29)

since
\displaystyle v_{2t}(x)\leq c\left(\frac{2t+y}{y}\right)^{\alpha}v(x)\leq c3^{\alpha}v(x)=c_{S}\psi(x)
by Lemma (
2.4
). Now we turn to the last summand. To appropriately estimate
\displaystyle R_{J}\psi
, we assume the extra condition
\displaystyle|J|\leq m(J)
, enabling us to apply the bound (3) from Lemma

\displaystyle\beta=1
. With that we obtain

\displaystyle\displaystyle\begin{aligned} |(R_{J}\psi)(x)|&\leq\int_{S}|\omega_{J}(x,\xi)-\tilde{\omega}_{J}(x,\xi)|\psi(\xi)\mathrm{d}\omega^{z_{0}}(\xi)\\
&\leq\varepsilon c_{S}\frac{|J|^{2}}{m(J)^{2}}\int_{S}k_{m(J)}(x,\xi)\psi(\xi)\mathrm{d}\omega^{z_{0}}(\xi)\\
&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\varepsilon<1}}{{\leq}}c_{S}\frac{|J|^{2}}{m(J)^{2}}v\left(x_{m(J)}\right)\\
&\leq\frac{c_{S}2^{\alpha}}{m(\Delta)^{2}}|J|^{2}\psi(x)\end{aligned}

(30)

since by Lemma
2.4

\displaystyle v_{m(J)}(x)\leq c\left(\frac{m(J)+y}{y}\right)^{\alpha}v(x)\leq c2^{\alpha}\psi(x)
. Combining inequalities (
28
), (
29
) and (
30

\displaystyle\displaystyle|(\Omega_{J}\psi)(x)-\psi(x)|

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq\left(c_{S}+\frac{c_{S}|J|y}{m(\Delta)^{2}}\right)\frac{|J|}{y}\psi(x)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=:c_{S}\left(1+\frac{|J|y}{m(\Delta)^{2}}\right)\frac{|J|}{y}\psi(x),

so after reformulating we end up with

\displaystyle\displaystyle\left(1-c_{S}\left(1+\frac{|J|y}{m(\Delta)^{2}}\right)\frac{|J|}{y}\right)\psi\leq\Omega_{J}\psi\leq\left(1+c_{S}\left(1+\frac{|J|y}{m(\Delta)^{2}}\right)\frac{|J|}{y}\right)\psi

(31)

everywhere on
\displaystyle S
. Having established this inequality we partition
\displaystyle\Delta
into small intervals and apply it to each element of the partition. Choose
\displaystyle K:=K(\Delta,y)\in\mathbb{N}
such that the three conditions

\displaystyle\displaystyle\frac{|\Delta|}{K}\leq m(\Delta)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\frac{|\Delta|y}{Km(\Delta)^{2}}\leq 1

\displaystyle\displaystyle 2c_{S}\frac{|\Delta|}{Ky}\leq\frac{1}{2}

\displaystyle\Delta

\displaystyle K

\displaystyle\frac{|\Delta|}{K}

\displaystyle\Delta=\bigcup_{1\leq k\leq K}J_{k}

\displaystyle m(J_{k})<m(J_{k+1})

\displaystyle|J_{k}|=\frac{|\Delta|}{K}

\displaystyle c_{S}\left(1+\frac{|J_{k}|y}{m(\Delta)^{2}}\right)\leq 2c_{S}
and
\displaystyle|J_{k}|\leq m(J_{k})
, by the choice of
\displaystyle K
. Remark (
7

\displaystyle J\in\mathrm{segm}^{+}

\displaystyle\displaystyle\Omega_{\Delta}\psi

\displaystyle\displaystyle=(\Omega_{J_{K}}\Omega_{J_{K-1}}\cdots\Omega_{J_{1}})\psi

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq\left(1+\frac{2c_{S}|\Delta|}{Ky}\right)^{K}\psi\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(*)}}{{\leq}}\exp\left(\frac{2c_{S}|\Delta|}{y}\right)\psi\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(**)}}{{\leq}}\left(1+e^{2c_{s}}\frac{|\Delta|}{y}\right)\psi,

(32)

where we used
\displaystyle\ln(1+\zeta)\leq\zeta

\displaystyle\zeta>-1
in

\displaystyle\displaystyle\left(1+\frac{z}{K}\right)^{K}\leq e^{z}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\ln\left(1+\frac{z}{K}\right)\leq\frac{z}{K}.

For
\displaystyle(**)
observe that
\displaystyle\xi=|\Delta|/y\leq 1

\displaystyle\displaystyle´e^{c\xi}-1=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{(c\xi)^{k}}{k!}=\xi\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{c^{k}\xi^{k-1}}{k!}\leq e^{c}\xi.

Completely analogously we show the other inequality

\displaystyle\displaystyle\Omega_{\Delta}\psi

\displaystyle\displaystyle=(\Omega_{J_{K}}\Omega_{J_{K-1}}\cdots\Omega_{J_{1}})\psi

\displaystyle\displaystyle\geq\left(1-\frac{2c_{S}|\Delta|}{Ky}\right)^{K}\psi\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\star)}}{{\geq}}\exp\left(-\frac{4c_{S}|\Delta|}{y}\right)\psi\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\star\star)}}{{\geq}}\left(1-\sinh(4c_{s})\frac{|\Delta|}{y}\right)\psi.

(33)

\displaystyle\ln(1-\zeta)\geq-\frac{\zeta}{1-\zeta}
for
\displaystyle\zeta<1
i.e. with

\displaystyle\displaystyle K\ln\left(1-\frac{z}{K}\right)\geq-\frac{z}{1-z/K}\Longleftrightarrow\left(1-\frac{z}{K}\right)^{K}\geq\exp\left(-\frac{z}{1-z/K}\right).

Since by assumption
\displaystyle z/K<1/2

\displaystyle\left(1-\frac{z}{K}\right)^{K}\geq e^{-2z}
.
In
\displaystyle(\star\star)
we again have
\displaystyle\xi=|\Delta|/y\leq 1
, moreover, for
\displaystyle c=4c_{S}\geq 0

\displaystyle\displaystyle\frac{e^{-c\xi}-1}{z}=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{k}c^{k}\xi^{k-1}}{k!}\geq-\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{c^{2n+1}\xi^{2n}}{(2n+1)!}\geq-\sinh(c)

holds.
∎

Lemma 3.29

(

)

There exist constants

Let
\displaystyle\rho>0
, as a first step we derive bounds for
\displaystyle\omega_{[\rho,2\rho]}
. Write

\displaystyle\displaystyle\omega_{[\rho,2\rho]}=k_{\rho}-\varepsilon b_{[\rho,2\rho]}+r_{[\rho,2\rho]}

and apply available bounds for

\displaystyle\displaystyle\omega_{[\rho,2\rho]}

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq k_{\rho}+c_{S}\varepsilon 2^{\alpha-1}k_{\rho}+c_{S}\varepsilon k_{\rho}\leq(1+c_{S}\varepsilon)k_{\rho}

\displaystyle K=K(y)\in\mathbb{N},K>0

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq k_{|\Delta|}+\varepsilon\left|b_{\Delta}\right|+\left|r_{\Delta}\right|

3.6.3
Differential equation

The following theorem will arguably be one of the most important ingredient in the arguments leading up to proving the main theorem for near half spaces
3.10
.

Theorem 3.30

(Differential equation
[
13
]
)
.

Let
\displaystyle\varphi
be a positive harmonic function on
\displaystyle\mathcal{O}
that is bounded on
\displaystyle\mathcal{O}_{y}
for any
\displaystyle y>0
and vanishes at infinity on all positive shifts of
\displaystyle S
, i.e.

\displaystyle\displaystyle\forall y>0:\quad\lim_{\begin{subarray}{c}\|z\|\rightarrow\infty\\
z\in S\end{subarray}}\varphi(z_{y})=0.

For every
\displaystyle x\in S
, define the real-valued function

\displaystyle\displaystyle f^{x}:(0,1]

\displaystyle\displaystyle\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}

\displaystyle\displaystyle y\longmapsto(\Omega_{y}(\varphi_{y}))(x).

Then for any
\displaystyle y_{0}\in(0,1)
,
\displaystyle f^{x}
is Lipschitz on
\displaystyle[y_{0},1]
and we have

\displaystyle\displaystyle f^{x}(y_{2})-f^{x}(y_{1})=\int_{y_{1}}^{y_{2}}\varepsilon(\Omega_{y}(B_{y}\varphi_{y}))(x)\mathrm{d}y

(41)

for all
\displaystyle y_{1},y_{2}\in(y_{0},1]
.

Proof.

We begin by demonstrating the Lipschitz property of
\displaystyle f^{x}
. By Rademacher’s Theorem
\displaystyle f^{x}
is then a.e. differentiable and the principle theorem of integration holds, i.e.

\displaystyle\displaystyle f^{x}(y_{2})-f^{x}(y_{1})=\int_{y_{1}}^{y_{2}}(f^{x})^{\prime}(y)\mathrm{d}y

where
\displaystyle(f^{x})^{\prime}
denotes the a.e. derivative of
\displaystyle f^{x}
. Then we will compute the left derivative of
\displaystyle(f^{x})^{\prime}_{-}(y)=(\Omega_{y}(B_{y}\varphi_{y}))(x)
which exists everywhere on
\displaystyle(0,1]
and is continuous. Since the a.e. derivative
\displaystyle(f^{x})^{\prime}
and
\displaystyle(f^{x})^{\prime}_{-}
coincide except on a set of measure
\displaystyle 0
, we have established the desired equality (
41
).

Fix
\displaystyle x\in S
,
\displaystyle y_{0}\in(0,1]
and for
\displaystyle y\in[y_{0},1]
,
\displaystyle h>0
satisfying
\displaystyle y>y-h\geq y_{0}
, define
\displaystyle\Delta:=[y-h,y]
. We obtain by definition of
\displaystyle f^{x}
and remark (
7
):

\displaystyle\displaystyle\begin{aligned} f^{x}(y)-&f^{x}(y-h)=\\
&(\Omega_{y}(\varphi_{y}-\Omega_{\Delta}\varphi_{y}+\varphi_{y}-\varphi_{y-h}))(x)+((\Omega_{y-h}-\Omega_{y})(\varphi_{y}-\varphi_{y-h}))(x).\end{aligned}

(42)

Setting
\displaystyle K:=\sup_{y_{0}\leq y\leq 1}\|\varphi_{y}\|_{C(S)}
, we observe the following. Since
\displaystyle\Delta=[y-h,y]\subset(0,y]
, the
\displaystyle\Phi
-property in Lemma
3.28
implies

\displaystyle\displaystyle\forall z\in S:\quad|\varphi_{y}(z)-(\Omega_{\Delta}\varphi_{y})(z)|\leq c_{S}\frac{h}{y}\varphi_{y}(z)\leq\frac{c_{S}K}{y_{0}}h.

Furthermore, by the mean value theorem we obtain for every
\displaystyle z\in S
a
\displaystyle\theta=\theta(z)\in\Delta^{\circ}
, such that for any
\displaystyle z\in S
, we have

\displaystyle\displaystyle|\varphi_{y}(z)-\varphi_{y-h}(z)|=\left|\langle\nabla\varphi(z_{\theta}),\vec{e_{d}}\rangle\right|h\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(*)}}{{\leq}}c_{d}\frac{\varphi(z_{\theta})}{\theta}h\leq c_{d}\frac{K}{y_{0}}h,

where we used Harnack’s inequality in
\displaystyle(*)
, and
\displaystyle c_{d}
only depends on the dimension
\displaystyle d
. By Corollary
3.27.2
we also obtain the bound

\displaystyle\displaystyle\left\|\Omega_{y}(\varphi_{y}-\Omega_{\Delta}\varphi_{y}\right.

\displaystyle\displaystyle\left.+\varphi_{y}-\varphi_{y-h})\right\|_{C(S)}

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq C\left(\|\varphi_{y}-\Omega_{\Delta}\varphi_{y}\|_{C(S)}+\|\varphi_{y}-\varphi_{y-h}\|_{C(S)}\right)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq C\frac{K(c_{d}+c_{S})}{y_{0}}h,

where
\displaystyle C
is given in Remark
6
and notably only depends on
\displaystyle S
and on
\displaystyle\varepsilon
. For the second term in equation (
42
), we apply the operator inequality again to obtain

\displaystyle\displaystyle\left\|(\Omega_{y-h}-\Omega_{y})(\varphi_{y}-\varphi_{y-h})\right\|_{C(S)}\leq 2C\frac{c_{d}K}{y_{0}}h.

Combining estimates, we now obtain

\displaystyle\displaystyle|f^{x}(y)-f^{x}(y-h)|\leq CK\frac{3c_{d}+c_{S}}{y_{0}}h.

Hence
\displaystyle f^{x}
is Lipschitz on
\displaystyle[y_{0},1]
.

We now turn to computing the left derivative of
\displaystyle f^{x}
. Using the same idea as in (
42
), we have for any
\displaystyle x\in S
,
\displaystyle y\in(0,1)
and
\displaystyle h<y
,

\displaystyle\displaystyle\frac{f^{x}(y-h)-f^{x}(y)}{-h}=\Omega_{y}\left(\frac{\varphi_{y}-\Omega_{\Delta}\varphi_{y}+\varphi_{y}-\varphi_{y-h}}{h}\right)(x)+(\Omega_{y-h}-\Omega_{y})\left(\frac{\varphi_{y}-\varphi_{y-h}}{h}\right)(x)

and thus

\displaystyle\displaystyle\left|\frac{f^{x}(y-h)-f^{x}(y)}{-h}-\varepsilon\Omega_{y}(B_{y}\varphi_{y})(x)\right|

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq\left|\Omega_{y}\left(\frac{\varphi_{y}-\Omega_{\Delta}\varphi_{y}+\varphi_{y}-\varphi_{y-h}}{h}-\varepsilon B_{y}\varphi_{y}\right)(x)\right|

\displaystyle\displaystyle+\left|(\Omega_{y-h}-\Omega_{y})\left(\frac{\varphi_{y}-\varphi_{y-h}}{h}\right)(x)\right|.

In a first step we treat the last term and show

\displaystyle\displaystyle\lim_{h\downarrow 0}(\Omega_{y-h}-\Omega_{y})\left(\frac{\varphi_{y}-\varphi_{y-h}}{h}\right)=0.

(43)

We rewrite (and drop the notational dependence on
\displaystyle x\in S
)

\displaystyle\displaystyle(\Omega_{y-h}-\Omega_{y})\left(\frac{\varphi_{y}-\varphi_{y-h}}{h}\right)=(\Omega_{y}\Omega_{\Delta}-\Omega_{y})\left(\frac{\partial\varphi_{y}}{\partial\vec{e_{d}}}+\frac{\varphi_{y}-\varphi_{y-h}}{h}-\frac{\partial\varphi_{y}}{\partial\vec{e_{d}}}\right).

By Harnack we have for all
\displaystyle z\in S
:

\displaystyle\displaystyle\left|\frac{\partial\varphi}{\partial\vec{e_{d}}}(z_{y})\right|\leq c\frac{\varphi_{y}(z)}{y}

and thus
\displaystyle\lim_{|z|\rightarrow\infty,z\in S}\frac{\partial\varphi}{\partial\vec{e_{d}}}(z_{y})=0
, i.e.
\displaystyle\frac{\partial\varphi_{y}}{\partial\vec{e_{d}}}\in C_{0}(S)
. For small enough
\displaystyle h>0
(in particular such that
\displaystyle|\Delta|\leq m(\Delta)
), we can apply Remark
9
to obtain

\displaystyle\displaystyle(\Omega_{y}\Omega_{\Delta}-\Omega_{y})\left(\frac{\partial\varphi_{y}}{\partial\vec{e_{d}}}\right)(x)=\int_{S}\omega_{y}(x,z)\left(\Omega_{\Delta}\frac{\partial\varphi_{y}}{\partial\vec{e_{d}}}(z)-\frac{\partial\varphi_{y}}{\partial\vec{e_{d}}}(z)\right)\mathrm{d}\omega^{z_{0}}(z)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle h\rightarrow 0}}{{\longrightarrow}}0,

since by Remark
12
,
\displaystyle c_{S}\omega_{y}(x,z)\|\psi\|_{C(S)}
is an integrable majorant.

We now estimate the norm of

\displaystyle\displaystyle\frac{\varphi_{y}-\varphi_{y-h}}{h}-\frac{\partial\varphi_{y}}{\partial\vec{e_{d}}}.

Boundedness of
\displaystyle\varphi
on
\displaystyle\mathcal{O}_{y_{0}}
allows us to uniformly bound all second derivatives on
\displaystyle\overline{\mathcal{O}_{3y/4}}=:\Omega
. Consider
\displaystyle z\in\Omega
and let
\displaystyle r>0
be such that
\displaystyle B(z,r)\subseteq\mathcal{O}_{y/2}
. Applying Remark
11
to
\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial_{x_{j}}}\varphi
on
\displaystyle B(z,r/2)
, we obtain

\displaystyle\displaystyle\left\|\nabla\frac{\partial}{\partial_{x_{j}}}\varphi(z)\right\|\leq\frac{2n}{r}\sup_{\zeta\in\partial B(z,r/2)}\left|\frac{\partial}{\partial_{x_{j}}}\varphi(\zeta)\right|=\frac{2n}{r}\left|\frac{\partial}{\partial_{x_{j}}}\varphi(\xi)\right|

for some
\displaystyle\xi\in\partial B(z,r/2)
. We apply Remark
11
again, this time to
\displaystyle\varphi
on
\displaystyle B(\xi,r/2)\subseteq B(x,r)
and obtain

\displaystyle\displaystyle\|\nabla\varphi(\xi)\|\leq\frac{2n}{r}\sup_{\zeta\in\partial B(\xi,r/2)}\varphi(\zeta)\leq\frac{2n}{r}\sup_{\zeta\in\mathcal{O}_{y/2}}\varphi(\zeta),

so in total we have

\displaystyle\displaystyle\left|\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x_{i}\partial x_{j}}\varphi(z)\right|\leq\left(\frac{2n}{r}\right)^{2}\sup_{\zeta\in\mathcal{O}_{y/2}}\varphi(\zeta)\leq\left(\frac{8n}{c_{S}y}\right)^{2}\sup_{\zeta\in\mathcal{O}_{y/2}}\varphi(\zeta),

since
\displaystyle r>0
was arbitrary,

Remark 11

Let
\displaystyle f

3.7.1

Remark 12

The classical weak maximum principle states
[
6
]
:

Let
\displaystyle\Omega
be a bounded domain and
\displaystyle L
an elliptic differential operator of the form

L=\sum_{i,j=1}^{d}a_{ij}D_{ij}+\sum_{i=1}^{d}b_{i}D_{i}.

Note that there is no contribution of the function
\displaystyle f
in
\displaystyle Lf
. Functions
\displaystyle\varphi\in C^{2}(\Omega)\cap C(\bar{\Omega})
satisfying
\displaystyle L\varphi=0
on
\displaystyle\Omega
follow the (weak) maximum principle

\displaystyle\displaystyle\sup_{\Omega}|\varphi|=\sup_{\partial\Omega}|\varphi|.

We will now extend the classical maximum principle to unbounded domains
\displaystyle\Omega
and solution
\displaystyle\varphi
to
\displaystyle L\varphi=0
on
\displaystyle\Omega
, that vanish at infinity, i.e.
\displaystyle\varphi\in C^{2}(\Omega)\cap C(\bar{\Omega})\cap C_{0}(\Omega)
. Denote
\displaystyle M=\sup_{\Omega}|\varphi|
. Suppose
\displaystyle x\in\partial\Omega
, and choose
\displaystyle R>0
large enough, such that on
\displaystyle\Omega\setminus B
, we have
\displaystyle|\varphi|<M/2
and
\displaystyle\Omega\cap B
is connected, where
\displaystyle B=B(x,R)
. Now on the domain
\displaystyle\Omega\cap B
, the weak maximum principle holds i.e.

\displaystyle\displaystyle\sup_{\Omega\cap B}|\varphi|=\sup_{\partial\left(\Omega\cap B\right)}|\varphi|=M,

since
\displaystyle|\varphi|<M/2
on
\displaystyle\Omega\setminus B
. Due to the same reasoning, we have
\displaystyle\sup_{\Omega}|\varphi|=\sup_{\Omega\cap B}|\varphi|
and
\displaystyle\sup_{\partial\Omega}|\varphi|=\sup_{\partial\left(\Omega\setminus B\right)}|\varphi|
and thus

\displaystyle\displaystyle\sup_{\Omega}|\varphi|=\sup_{\partial\Omega}|\varphi|.

This concludes all preparations needed for proving the main statement of
[
13
]

\displaystyle S

4

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}

In this last section we will show that the Hausdorff dimension of

\displaystyle(d-1)\frac{1+\eta}{2+\eta}

\displaystyle S

\displaystyle\displaystyle\forall x\in S\quad\forall r>0:\quad\dim\left(\mathcal{V}\cap B(x,r)\right)=d-1

\displaystyle\dim(\mathcal{V}(S))=d-1

]

]

\displaystyle C^{2}

,

2.

\displaystyle\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus D\cap\mathcal{C}=\left\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{d-1}\times\mathbb{R}:\|x\|<r\text{ and }-h<y<\phi(x)\right\}
,

3.

\displaystyle\partial D\cap\mathcal{C}=\phi|_{B^{d-1}(r)}
,

where
\displaystyle\phi
denotes the local parametrization of
\displaystyle\partial D
at
\displaystyle p
. We extend the Lipschitz function
\displaystyle\phi
on
\displaystyle B^{d-1}(r)
to a Lipschitz function
\displaystyle\Phi
on
\displaystyle\mathbb{R}^{d-1}
such that
\displaystyle\Phi\equiv 0
outside
\displaystyle B^{d-1}(2r)
and define the near half space

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathcal{O}=\mathcal{O}_{\rho}:=\left\{(x,y):x\in\mathbb{R}^{d-1}\text{ and }y>\Phi(x)\right\},

i.e.
\displaystyle\mathcal{O}
is the epigraph of the Lipschitz function
\displaystyle\Phi
. Notice that
\displaystyle S:=\partial\mathcal{O}=\Phi(\mathbb{R}^{d-1})
and
\displaystyle S\cap\mathcal{C}\subset S\cap\partial D
. By
\displaystyle\lambda\mathcal{C}
we will denote the set
\displaystyle\left\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{d-1}\times\mathbb{R}:\|x\|<\lambda r\text{ and }|y|<h\right\}
.

In the following we will show that for every
\displaystyle\rho\in\partial D
and its associated cylinder
\displaystyle\mathcal{C}
, we find a positive harmonic function
\displaystyle w
on
\displaystyle\mathcal{O}
, satisfying the assumptions of the previous sections, such that the equality

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathcal{V}^{u}(\partial D)\cap\frac{1}{4}\mathcal{C}\supseteq\mathcal{V}^{w}(\partial\mathcal{O})\cap\frac{1}{4}\mathcal{C}

(54)

holds. (Recall (
1
)).

We fix a
\displaystyle\rho\in\partial D
. Recall
\displaystyle\rho=0
after rotation and translation The domain
\displaystyle U:=D\cap\mathcal{C}
is contained in the intersection of
\displaystyle D
and
\displaystyle\mathcal{O}
and clearly is again a Lipschitz domain that is bounded. In particular, the function
\displaystyle u
is positive harmonic and bounded on
\displaystyle U
and therefore, by the crucial theorem of Hunt and Wheeden
[
8
, Section 2]
,
\displaystyle u
admits the following integral representation:

\displaystyle\displaystyle\forall p\in U:\quad u(p)=\int_{\partial U}k_{p_{0}}^{U}(p,q)f(q)\mathrm{d}\omega^{p_{0}}_{U}(q),

where
\displaystyle f\in L^{1}(\partial U,\omega^{p_{0}}_{U})
,
\displaystyle p_{0}\in U
is some fixed point,
\displaystyle\omega_{U}^{p_{0}}
denotes the harmonic measure in
\displaystyle U
with pole at
\displaystyle p_{0}
and
\displaystyle k_{U}^{p_{0}}(p,\cdot)
is the Martin kernel, i.e. the Radon-Nikodym derivative of
\displaystyle\omega_{U}^{p}
w.r.t.
\displaystyle\omega_{U}^{p_{0}}
. Additionally note, as the nontangential limit of
\displaystyle u
,
\displaystyle f
inherits the same upper and lower bounds as the positive harmonic function
\displaystyle u
, i.e.
\displaystyle 0\leq f\leq C
. Now let
\displaystyle\Sigma:=\phi(B^{d-1}(r/2))\subset\partial U\cap\partial D\cap\mathcal{C}
and define the function
\displaystyle w
on the near half space
\displaystyle\mathcal{O}
, as follows:

\displaystyle\displaystyle\forall p\in\mathcal{O}:\quad w(p)=\int_{\Sigma}k^{\mathcal{O}}_{p_{0}}(p,q)f(q)\mathrm{d}\omega^{p_{0}}_{U}(q).

Notice, that
\displaystyle w
is positive harmonic on
\displaystyle\mathcal{O}
. Furthermore,
\displaystyle w
is bounded on
\displaystyle\mathcal{O}
and vanishes at infinity on
\displaystyle S_{y}
, for
\displaystyle y>0
. This can be seen as follows:

We first proof boundedness. Let
\displaystyle p\in\mathcal{O}
be arbitrary, then
\displaystyle p=x_{y}
for some
\displaystyle x\in S
and
\displaystyle y>0
. By Lemma
2.13
we have the inequality

\displaystyle\displaystyle\int_{\Sigma}k^{\mathcal{O}}_{p_{0}}(x_{y},q)f(q)\mathrm{d}\omega^{p_{0}}_{U}(q)\leq\sum_{j\in\mathbb{N}}\frac{c2^{-\alpha j}}{\omega^{\mathcal{O}}_{p_{0}}(\Delta_{j})}\int_{\Sigma\cap R_{j}}f(q)\mathrm{d}\omega_{U}^{p_{0}}(q),

where
\displaystyle\Delta_{j}=B(x,2^{j}y)\cap S
and
\displaystyle R_{j}=\Delta_{j}\setminus\Delta_{j-1}
. By Remark
13
we can estimate
\displaystyle\omega^{p_{0}}_{\mathcal{O}}(\Delta_{j})\geq\omega^{p_{0}}_{\mathcal{O}}(\Delta_{j}\cap\partial U)\geq\omega^{p_{0}}_{U}(\Delta_{j}\cap\partial U)
. Since
\displaystyle 0\leq f\leq C
, we obtain the uniform bound

\displaystyle\displaystyle\int_{\Sigma}k^{\mathcal{O}}_{p_{0}}(x_{y},q)f(q)\mathrm{d}\omega^{p_{0}}_{U}(q)\leq cC\sum_{j\in\mathbb{N}}2^{-\alpha j}<\infty.

The second property to check is
\displaystyle\lim_{\begin{subarray}{c}\|x\|\rightarrow\infty\\
x\in S\end{subarray}}w(x_{y})=0
for any
\displaystyle y>0
. This is again a direct consequence of Lemma
2.13
. Fix
\displaystyle y>0
, then for
\displaystyle x\in S
satisfying
\displaystyle d(x,\Sigma)\in(2^{j-1}y,2^{j}y)
, we have
\displaystyle\Sigma\subset B(x,2^{j}y)\setminus B(x,2^{j-1}y)
and thus by Lemma
2.13

\displaystyle\displaystyle w(x_{y})=\int_{\Sigma}k^{\mathcal{O}}_{p_{0}}(x_{y},q)f(q)\mathrm{d}\omega^{p_{0}}_{U}(q)\leq C\frac{c2^{-\alpha j}}{\omega^{p_{0}}_{U}(\Sigma)}.

In total,
\displaystyle w
satisfies all the assumptions from the previous sections, in particular, we have information about
\displaystyle\mathcal{V}^{w}(\partial U)\cap\frac{1}{4}\mathcal{C}
. Also, since
\displaystyle w
is bounded, we can again apply the aforementioned theorem in Section 2 of
[
8
]
by Hunt and Wheeden, yielding the representation

\displaystyle\displaystyle\forall p\in U:\quad w(p)=\int_{\partial U}k^{U}_{p_{0}}(p,q)g(q)\mathrm{d}\omega^{p_{0}}_{U}(q).

where again
\displaystyle g\in L^{1}(\partial U)
(and bounded). Moreover, by a very similar argument as in
[
8
, Section 2]
, we obtain
\displaystyle f=g
on
\displaystyle\Sigma
and thus

\displaystyle\displaystyle u(p)-w(p)=\int_{\partial U\setminus\Sigma}k^{U}_{p_{0}}(p,q)(f(q)-g(q))\mathrm{d}\omega^{p_{0}}_{U}(q)

everywhere on
\displaystyle U
. We will from now on drop the sub- and superscripts
\displaystyle U
and
\displaystyle p_{0}
in our notation, i.e.
\displaystyle k:=k_{p_{0}}^{U}
and
\displaystyle\omega:=\omega^{p_{0}}_{U}
. Differentiating and applying the bound on gradients of positive harmonic functions due to Harnack, we obtain

\displaystyle\displaystyle\|\nabla(u-w)(p)\|\leq\int_{\partial U\setminus\Sigma}\frac{k(p,q)}{d(p,\partial U)}|f(q)-g(q)|\mathrm{d}\omega(q)

(55)

for
\displaystyle p\in U
. Equality (
54
) will be proven if we can show

\displaystyle\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\left\|\nabla u(p^{*}+yv)\right\|\mathrm{d}y<\infty\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\int_{0}^{1}\left\|\nabla w(p^{*}+yv)\right\|\mathrm{d}y<\infty

(56)

for any
\displaystyle p^{*}\in\Sigma/2
, where
\displaystyle v=h/2\vec{e_{d}}
and
\displaystyle\Sigma/2=\phi(B^{d-1}(r/4))
. To prove (
56
) we plug into (
55
) and estimate

\displaystyle\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\|\nabla(u-w)(p^{*}+yv)\|\mathrm{d}y\leq c_{S}\int_{\partial U\setminus\Sigma}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{k(p^{*}+yv,q)}{y}\mathrm{d}y|f(q)-g(q)|\mathrm{d}\omega(q).

Denote
\displaystyle c=d(\Sigma/2,\partial U\setminus\Sigma)
and rewrite

\displaystyle\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\frac{k(p^{*}+yv,q)}{y}\mathrm{d}y=\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\sum_{k=0}^{N}\int_{2^{-k-1}}^{2^{-k}}\frac{k(p^{*}+yv,q)}{y}\mathrm{d}y

(57)

Let
\displaystyle p^{*}\in\Sigma/2
and
\displaystyle q\in\partial U\setminus\Sigma
and w.l.o.g
\displaystyle\|v\|=1
. Fix
\displaystyle k\in\mathbb{N}
and
\displaystyle y\in[2^{-k-1},2^{-k}]
. Now choose
\displaystyle j=j_{k}
such that

\displaystyle\displaystyle\frac{c}{4}\leq 2^{j-k-2}\leq 2^{j-1}y\leq\|p^{*}-q\|\leq 2^{j}y\leq 2^{j-k}.

The first inequality holds since otherwise
\displaystyle 2^{j-k}<c\leq\|p^{*}-q\|
. This implies that in the notation of Lemma
2.12

\displaystyle\displaystyle q\in R_{j-1}^{k}\cup R_{j}^{k},

where
\displaystyle R^{k}_{l}:=\left\{q\in\partial U:2^{l-k-1}\leq\|p^{*}-q\|\leq 2^{l-k}\right\}
. Thus, by Lemma
2.12

\displaystyle\displaystyle k(p^{*}+vy,q)\leq c^{\prime}\max\left\{\frac{c_{j_{k}}}{\omega(B(2^{j_{k}}y,p^{*}))},\frac{c_{j_{k}+1}}{\omega(B(2^{j_{k}+1}y,p^{*}))}\right\}\leq\tilde{c}\frac{2^{-\alpha j_{k}}}{\omega(B(c/4,p^{*}))},

where
\displaystyle\tilde{c}
only depends on
\displaystyle U
and
\displaystyle p_{0}
and
\displaystyle\alpha>0
only depends on
\displaystyle U
. Inserting into (
57
) we obtain

\displaystyle\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\frac{k(p^{*}+yv,q)}{y}\mathrm{d}y\leq\frac{\tilde{c}}{\omega(B(c/4,p^{*}))}\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\sum_{k=0}^{N}2^{-\alpha j_{k}}<\infty

and thus

\displaystyle\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\|\nabla(u-w)(p^{*}+yv)\|\mathrm{d}y<\infty,

implying (
56
).

Remark 13
.

In the above situation we want to show

\displaystyle\displaystyle\omega^{p_{0}}_{\mathcal{O}}(\Delta_{j})\geq\omega_{U}^{p_{0}}(\Delta_{j}\cap\partial U).

(58)

W.l.o.g., assume
\displaystyle\Delta_{j}\cap\partial U\neq\emptyset
. We will prove the desired inequality in a more general setting.

Let
\displaystyle\mathcal{O}
be any epigraph of a Lipschitz function,
\displaystyle U\subset\mathcal{O}
be a bounded Lipschitz domain and
\displaystyle p\in U
. Let
\displaystyle M\subset\partial\mathcal{O}
denote an open set and suppose
\displaystyle R:=M\cap\partial U\neq\emptyset
. We want to show
\displaystyle\omega^{p}_{\mathcal{O}}(R)\geq\omega_{U}^{p}(R)
as this obviously implies (
58
).

For fixed
\displaystyle\epsilon>0
, find a continuous function
\displaystyle\varphi:\partial\mathcal{O}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}
satisfying
\displaystyle\varphi|_{R}\equiv 1
and
\displaystyle\varphi\equiv 0
on
\displaystyle\partial\mathcal{O}\setminus R_{\epsilon}

where
\displaystyle R_{\epsilon}:=\{q\in\partial\mathcal{O}:d(q,R)<\epsilon\}
. Consider the harmonic extension of
\displaystyle\varphi
to
\displaystyle\mathcal{O}
;

\displaystyle\displaystyle\Phi(p):=\int_{\partial\mathcal{O}}\varphi(q)\mathrm{d}\omega_{\mathcal{O}}^{p}(q),\quad p\in\mathcal{O}.

Then, obviously
\displaystyle\Phi(p)\leq\omega_{\mathcal{O}}^{p}(R_{\epsilon})
. Now define the function
\displaystyle\tilde{\varphi}:\partial U\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}
satisfying

\displaystyle\displaystyle\tilde{\varphi}(q)=\begin{cases}\varphi(q),\quad q\in\partial\mathcal{O}\cap\partial U\\
\Phi(q),\quad q\in\partial U\setminus\partial\mathcal{O}\end{cases}.

\displaystyle\tilde{\varphi}
is bounded and continuous hence by uniqueness, its harmonic extension to
\displaystyle U
retrieves
\displaystyle\Phi
on
\displaystyle U
, yielding

\displaystyle\displaystyle\forall p\in U:\quad\Phi(p)=\int_{\partial U}\tilde{\varphi}(q)\mathrm{d}\omega_{U}^{p}(q).

The last integral is bounded from below by
\displaystyle\omega^{p}_{U}(R)
and since
\displaystyle\epsilon>0
was arbitrary, we obtain
\displaystyle\omega_{\mathcal{O}}^{p}(R)\geq\omega^{p}_{U}(R)
.
