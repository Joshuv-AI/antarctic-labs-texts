Response to the Letter regarding the EBIOM-D-23-04056: “CPAP may promote an endothelial inflammatory milieu in sleep apnoea after coronary revascularization”
Authors: Yuksel Peker, Yeliz Celik, Afrouz Behboudi, Susan Redline, Daniel J Gottlieb, Sanja Jelic
Published: 2024

We thank Mendes et al. on their interest in our work showing that greater levels of continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP) are associated with an unfavorable trajectory of markers of lung epithelial and endothelial injury in patients with obstructive sleep apnoea (OSA) after coronary revascularization, consistent with two prior studies showing increases in angiopoietin-2 following CPAP initiation.1, 2, 3 These findings suggest a plausible explanation for the failure of randomized controlled trials to demonstrate a reduced risk of cardiovascular and cerebrovascular disease (CVD) events with CPAP, which is the unequivocal finding of the cited meta-analysis of the RICCADSA, ISAACC, and SAVE trials that found an overall hazard ratio for the impact of CPAP on major adverse cardiovascular and cerebrovascular outcomes of 1.01 (95% CI, 0.87–1.17).4 This intention-to-treat analysis is a gold standard for interpreting the results of randomized controlled trials and post hoc analyses comparing adherent patients to non-adherent patients or to the full control group are likely to be highly misleading owing to confounding by factors associated with adherence (the “healthy adherer bias”), as we have addressed in depth elsewhere.5 Our proposed model is that, while CPAP decreases OSA-related hypoxemia and thereby reduces cardiovascular risk, CPAP also increases systemic inflammation through excessive lung inflation, opposing its beneficial effects.1 The balance of these effects will determine whether an individual patient derives benefit or harm from CPAP. The model predicts that patients with a higher baseline severity of OSA-related CVD risk will be more likely to derive benefit from CPAP, while those with a lower baseline CVD risk will be more likely to suffer harm from CPAP. The results of two post hoc analyses of randomized trials provide empiric support for this model. One stratified participants in the ISAACC trial into high and low CVD risk groups based on the extent of event-related hypoxemia.6 The other stratified participants in the RICCADSA trial into high and low CVD risk groups based on the heart rate response to events.7 In both analyses, CPAP was associated with lower risk of recurrent CVD events in those at high baseline risk. In contrast, CPAP was associated with greater risk of recurrent CVD events in those at low baseline risk.6,7 The model also predicts that higher CPAP levels, which will cause greater lung inflation, will be more likely to cause harm. This is supported by our findings that among patient randomized to CPAP, angiopoietin-2 level at 12 months was correlated with median CPAP pressure, and that pressures above the median pressure were associated with a higher rate of CVD events.1 This suggests that a more conservative CPAP level, which may also help to optimize adherence, may be most useful for treatment of OSA, and this is an important question for further study.

The effectiveness of CPAP therapy was assessed by residual AHI, which was in the normal range. While mask type and unintentional leak may affect adherence with therapy, they are not measures of adequacy of therapy. While the median CPAP utilization of 3.5 h per night (24.5 h/week) may be suboptimal, it exceeds the common clinical standard of 4 h per night, 5 night per week (i.e., 20 h/week) used to define adequate adherence.

Primary outcomes and models testing the association of biomarker levels with mortality and with cardiovascular morbidity were adjusted for diabetes and hypertension (in addition to age, sex, body mass index [BMI], and OSA severity), and showed that the change in angiopoietin-2 levels from baseline to 12 months remained significantly different between CPAP and usual care (p = 0.039), and that the increase in level of angiopoietin-2 from baseline to 12 months was associated with greater mortality in patients with OSA after coronary revascularization.

Mendes et al. correctly note that fragmented sleep and short sleep duration promote vascular inflammation and dysfunction. In the absence of intermittent hypoxia of OSA, the mechanisms underlying these changes involve altered bone marrow milieu and impaired endothelial antioxidant responses.8,9 These mechanisms were not assessed in our study owing to the lack of available biological specimens permitting such assessments. While we cannot draw conclusions regarding the effects of sleep duration on outcomes reported in this study, randomization makes it unlikely that there were large systematic differences between treatment groups. Whether an adverse impact of CPAP on sleep quality, as has been documented in some groups of OSA patients,10 might contribute to an adverse effect of CPAP observed in our study is an important question that could be investigated in future studies.
