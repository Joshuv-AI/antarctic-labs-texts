Fungicidal Efficacy of Leaf Extracts From Khaya senegalensis (Desr.) A. Juss. Irrigated With Agricultural Drainage Water and Treated With Some Fertilizer Types
Authors: Esraa A. M. Mohamed, Ahmed M. El‐Baha, Ahmed A. A. El‑Settawy, Nader D. Shetta, Abeer A. Mohamed, Mohamed Z. M. Salem
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThis study evaluated the bio‐based chemical compounds and antifungal potential of leaf ethanolic extracts from 20‐month‐old Khaya senegalensis (Desr.) A. Juss. seedlings. Plants were irrigated with three water types: 100% Nile water (NW), 50% NW + 50% agricultural drainage water (ADW), and 100% ADW, and fertilized with NPK, seaweed extract (SE), microbial top strains (TS), or their combinations. The extracts were tested against plant pathogenic fungi, Botrytis fabae and Alternaria alternata. Rutin, gallic acid, and naringenin were the primary bioactive compounds. Under 100% NW, maximum concentrations reached 29.79, 6.87, and 13.039 mg/g extract, respectively. While 100% ADW caused severe abiotic stress and threshold failure in unfertilized plants, adding SE and TS biostimulants to the 50% ADW blend protected the leaf chemical profile (PC1 = 97.5%, PC2 = 1.5%). The highest fungal inhibition percentage (FIP) against B. fabae reached 54.8% using 50% NW + 50% ADW combined with NPK + TS. Against A. alternata, the maximum FIP was 85.6% at 3% with 100% ADW + NPK + TS. Minimum inhibitory concentrations were 0.75%–5% for B. fabae and 0.0625%–0.25% for A. alternata. Thus, K. senegalensis extracts offer a sustainable biopesticide resource for managing plant pathogens under marginal water irrigation.

Introduction

Converting plant biomass into bio‐based chemical compounds is a necessary approach to protect the environment, as these chemicals are considered environmentally safe for human and animal health. These chemicals also exhibit good toxicity against some pathogenic organisms, such as bacteria, fungi, and insects [1, 2, 3, 4, 5]. In this regard, the mahogany family (Meliaceae) comprises several timber tree species that are rich sources of bioactive compounds. These trees have been identified as potential plant systems possessing a vast array of biologically active compounds that are chemically diverse and structurally complex [6].

African Mahogany, or Khaya senegalensis (Desr.) A. Juss. (Meliaceae Family), is a massive deciduous tree native to the sub‐Saharan savannah that extends from Senegal to Uganda [7, 8, 9]. The tree stands between 15 and 30 m tall, and its rounded, evergreen crown of glossy, dark green leaves makes it easy to identify. African mahogany is an extremely valuable tree with significant economic value due to its superior‐grade timber [10, 11, 12]. It produces a bear‐woody fruit capsule [13]. It is a fast‐growing, semi‐deciduous to evergreen tree [14]. The bark is dark grey with small, thin, reddish‐tinged scales; the slash is dark pink to bright crimson and exudes a red sap [14]. The leaves are alternate, pinnately compound (13–33 cm long), bearing 3–7 pairs of oblong to elliptical leaflets (4–12 × 2–5 cm) [7, 14, 15, 16]. The flowers are small, cream‐colored, tetramerous, and borne in lax axillary panicles up to 17 cm long [8]. The fruit is a woody, almost spherical capsule (4–6 cm in diameter) that opens by 4 valves from the apex to release numerous brown, winged seeds [14]. It is one of the most economically important trees and is highly valued for its high‐quality timber [17, 18]. It has been used as traditional medicine against several diseases, such as pain killers, inflammation, malaria, anemia, diabetes, and gastrointestinal disease [17, 19, 20, 21]. Many secondary metabolites were investigated in leaf and bark extracts, including alkaloids, reducing sugars, flavonoids, phenolics, sterols, triterpenes, glycosides, and tannins [20, 22, 23, 24, 25, 26, 27, 28]. The richness of K. senegalensis extracts in secondary metabolites could be the origin of its numerous biological, pharmacological, and therapeutic activities [9, 29].

Available databases confirmed the antibacterial potential of the stem bark and leaves of K. senegalensis on bacterial strains, as well as its antioxidant properties [30, 31]. A few studies have examined the antifungal activity of its leaf extracts, despite the abundant information on the biological activities of K. senegalensis, particularly its antibacterial and antioxidant properties [30, 32, 33, 34, 35, 36]. Extracts from leaf and stem bark, containing saponins, flavonoids, alkaloids, and tannins, with good antibacterial activity against Escherichia coli, Shigella spp., and Salmonella spp., were studied by Abdallah [28]. It has been shown that the better‐known principle, “calicedrin,” found in the stem bark is composed of a combination of triterpenes with a furan ring and lactone or epoxide activity. Additionally, it has been claimed that the bark includes quinone, β‐sitosterol and its B‐D‐glucoside, tannins, saponins, and polysaccharides in addition to 2,6‐dimethoxy‐p‐benzo‐tannins [37, 38, 39, 40, 41]. K. senegalensis extracts have been shown to produce a variety of limonoids [42, 43, 44, 45].

Furthermore, it has been claimed that this plant contains phenolic substances such as procyanidins, rutin, and catechin [38]. Extracts from the leaves, bark, and roots of K. senegalensis have been shown to possess antimicrobial and antioxidant properties [22, 29, 33, 38, 46]. Bark extract displayed inhibitory effects against P. aeruginosa and S. pyogenes, but a strong inhibitory effect was observed against K. pneumoniae and A. baylyi [47]. Extracts of K. senegalensis have antibacterial properties that cause Gram‐negative bacteria to break down by weakening the stability of their cytoplasmic membrane [25].

Faba bean production is negatively affected by various foliar pathogens, including Peronospora viciae (bean downy mildew), Cercospora zonata (leaf spot), Ascochyta fabae (Ascochyta blight), and chocolate spot, caused by Botrytis fabae [48, 49]. Moreover, the chocolate spot is known to drastically reduce production in important bean‐producing locations worldwide [50, 51]. The tomato, or Solanum lycopersicum L., is extremely vulnerable to several fungal diseases, which can severely reduce tomato yield. These include Fusarium wilt (Fusarium oxysporum f. sp. lycopersici), collar rot (Sclerotium rolfsii), late blight (Phytophthora infestans), early blight (Alternaria solani), and damping off (Pythium sp.) [52, 53]. One of the diseases that most severely lowers agricultural production is Alternaria alternata, the pathogen that causes Alternaria blight. It damages and destroys crops all around the world [54, 55].

The best way to reduce fruit fungus decay is to use synthetic fungicides, which are the basis of fungal disease control strategies. Nowadays, increasing concerns about environmental issues and food safety have prompted the creation of innovative, environmentally friendly methods of disease prevention and control [56, 57]. Therefore, this study aimed to examine the variations in phytochemical components that were obtained from the leaf biomass of K. senegalensis irrigated using agricultural drainage water (ADW). In this regard, natural extracts from timber trees work well as bioactive chemicals to act as bioactive agents against plant pathogens. This study focused on two typical plant pathogenic fungi, namely, Alternaria alternata and B. fabae.

Materials and Methods

Plant Used and Quantification of Extracts

Samples from fresh leaves of K. senegalensis seedlings were collected from the greenhouse in Nubaria city (west of Alexandria), Beheira governorate (N3008°55'' E30°08'42''), at the end of July 2023, then carefully sealed in waterproof bags to preserve their fresh weight before being sent to the laboratory. For each sample, two weights were recorded: the green weight to measure the weight again, and the air‐dry weight in a lab setting at 22 ± 5°C for 14 days, for extraction.

Preparation of Leaf Extracts

Leaves of K. senegalensis collected from plants treated with various treatments, as shown in Table 1, were washed with tap water and then air‐dried at room temperature until completely dry. The dried leaves (from 24 treatments) were ground to a fine powder using a small laboratory mill. About 15 g of fine‐ground leaves of K. senegalensis from each treatment was extracted by soaking in 100 mL of 70% ethanol for three days [42, 58]. After the extraction process was finished, the leaf ethanolic extracts (LEEs) were filtered through a cotton plug and then through Whatman filter paper No. 1 [25]. The filtered LEEs were concentrated by evaporating the ethanol solvent to obtain the K. senegalensis LEEs, which were poured into Petri dishes to complete the drying and concentration [59]. The LEEs were collected and kept dry at 4°C for further analysis and bioassays.

Chemical Composition of the Irrigation Water Sources

The chemical composition and heavy metal concentrations of three irrigation water sources assessed in an agricultural experiment are described in Tables 2 and 3. The main objective is to ascertain whether the water sources present risks to crops and soil structures due to salinity, sodicity, or toxicity [60]. NW is superior for the majority of crops, according to Electrical Conductivity (EC), which gauges the concentration of dissolved salts (Table 2). Leaching techniques are necessary because WDW is minimal. All except extremely salt‐tolerant crops experience osmotic drought stress due to SDW's high salinity (EC > 3.0) [61]. The Sodium Adsorption Ratio (SAR) assesses the possibility that sodium would damage the soil matrix. As SDW becomes closer to a high‐risk value (SAR = 8), it may eventually lead to waterlogging, poor soil aeration, and clay dispersion [62]. The most prevalent ions in all sources are sodium (Na+) and chloride (Cl−). In sensitive fruit or leaf tissue, high concentrations of these ions might result in particular toxicities [60].

Table 3 evaluates trace heavy metals, and these values can be compared against common Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO) agricultural standards to determine environmental safety [63]. Summer drainage water contained 0.299 mg/L of Cr, which is comfortably below the maximum standard level of 0.50 mg/L that is typically set for continuous long‐term irrigation. Both drainage sources had Pb levels (0.046 mg/L in SDW and 0.031 mg/L in WDW), which are substantially below the typical hazardous threshold of 5.0 mg/L for soils. However, caution should be exercised when applying Pb to bioaccumulative leaf crops. Cu was found in trace concentrations that were comfortably above the 0.20 mg/L standard limit (0.018 mg/L in SDW and 0.0052 mg/L in WDW). There was no evidence of Cd or Zn toxicity in any of the assessed drainage types.

HPLC Conditions for Analysis of Phenolic and Flavonoid Compounds

The phenolic and flavonoid compounds in the LEEs were analyzed by an HPLC apparatus (Agilent 1260 series, Zorbax Eclipse Plus C8 column with 4.6 mm × 250 mm i.d., 5 µm). The mobile phase consisted of water (A) and 0.05% trifluoroacetic acid in acetonitrile (B) at a flow rate of 0.9 mL/min. The mobile phase was programmed consecutively in a linear gradient as follows: 0 min (82% A); 0–1 min (82% A); 1–11 min (75% A); 11–18 min (60% A); 18–22 min (82% A); 22–24 min (82% A). The multi‐wavelength detector was monitored at 280 nm. The injection volume was 5 µL for each of the sample solutions. The column temperature was maintained at 40°C [64].

Standard HPLC‐grade phenolic and flavonoid compounds include gallic acid, chlorogenic acid, catechin, methyl gallate, caffeic acid, syringic acid, pyrocatechol, rutin, ellagic acid, p‐coumaric acid, vanillin, ferulic acid, naringenin, rosmarinic acid, daidzein, quercetin, cinnamic acid, kaempferol, and hesperetin. The HPLC chromatograms of the injected standard compounds are shown in Figure S1.

Chemosystematic Significance via Multivariate Analysis

The chemosystematic significance of phytochemical profiling of K. senegalensis under variable irrigation regimes and biostimulant treatments was established via multivariate assessment, comprising principal component analysis (PCA). PCA was used to study how different water options (100% Nile Water [NW], 50% NW + 50% ADW, and 100% ADW) and biofertilizers affect the leaf chemicals of K. senegalensis seedlings.

Isolation and Morphological Characterization of Fungal Pathogens

The disease known as Alternaria blight of tomato leaves and Chocolate spot of Faba Bean seeds was identified by the isolation of affected plants exhibiting similar symptoms. Samples were collected from Behera Governorate, Egypt. The fungal isolates were isolated and cultured at the Department of Seed Pathology, Plant Pathology Research Institute, Agricultural Research Center, Egypt. To meet the organisms' developmental needs, the fungal pathogen was isolated from tissues using potato dextrose agar (PDA) and kept in the dark for 1 week at 26 ± 1°C [51].

Based on their physical characteristics, including their mycelia and spores, and an analysis of the structure of their conidia examined under a light microscope, pure cultures of Botrytis and Alternaria were isolated [51, 65].

Molecular Characterization of Genomic DNA From Fungal Isolates

Two pure isolates of the species Alternaria and Botrytis were cultured in vitro on potato dextrose agar, and their genomic DNA was extracted using the micropreparation technique [66]. The ITS region of these isolates was amplified via PCR using universal primers ITS1 (5′‐TCCGTAGGTGAACCTGCGG‐3′) and ITS4 (5′‐TCCTCCGCTTATTGATATGC‐3′); the conserved ribosomal internal transcribed spacer (ITS) region was used to identify fungal cultures [67], two isolates' amplified ITS1‐5.8s and ITS2 regions (500–700 bp) of selected Botrytis and Alternaria isolates were sent for sequencing (Macrogen, Scientific Services Company, Korea) [68]. The sequences were compared to those in GenBank (http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov) using a BLAST search on the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI); the sequences obtained in the present study were submitted to GenBank.

Antifungal Activity of Wood Treated With K. senegalensis Leaf Extracts

The LEEs of K. senegalensis were prepared in concentrations of 1%, 2%, and 3% by dissolving the respective amount in dimethyl sulfoxide (10% DMSO) and used to treat pure cultures of two pathogenic fungi, Botrytis and Alternaria isolates. Fungal isolates were kept on Potato Dextrose Agar (PDA) medium for one week of fresh culture. The prepared Teak wood samples (Tectona grandis L.f.) at a dimension of 2 × 2 × 0.7 cm were autoclaved for 20 min at 121°C, then left to cool and used instead of filter disc papers. Three replicates were used for each concentration for each fungus, and the same for positive Dithane M‐45 (Mancozeb) as a fungicide at the recommended dosage of 2 g/L and negative (10% DMSO) controls. Each wood sample received 200 µL of the prepared concentration from the LEEs. A single fungal disk of 7 mm diam. was taken using a cork borer and placed in the center of the Petri dishes; then the treated wood samples were inserted and incubated at 26 ± 1°C for 7 days. The measurements were taken when the mycelial fungus growth filled the Petri dish in the control treatment (negative). The minimum inhibitory concentrations (MICs) were determined by serial dilution of all treatments, ranging from 0.0625% to 5% [3, 69]. The fungal inhibition percentage (FIP) was calculated as follows: FIP = [(AC × AT)/AC] × 100, where AC and AT represent the average diameters of the fungal colonies of the control and treatment, respectively [59].

Statistical Analysis

Data on antifungal activity (inhibition percentage of fungal linear growth) as affected by the LEEs from treated plants and the tested concentrations (2%, 4%, and 6%), compared with positive and negative controls, were statistically analyzed using two‐way analysis of variance (ANOVA) and processed with Statistical Analysis Software (SAS) system [70]. The differences among the means of treatments were recorded using Fisher's Least Significant Difference (LSD) at a 0.05 level of probability.

The extracts from K. senegalensis fertilized with NPK + TS + seaweed contained rutin with the highest amount (21.83 mg/g extract), followed by naringenin (13.04 mg/g extract) and gallic acid (2.99 mg/g extract).

Principal Component Analysis (PCA) of Phytochemical Profiles

Figure 1 displays the phytochemical characterization and Principal Component Analysis (PCA) of K. senegalensis leaves under water quality regimes and biostimulant treatments. A PCA was conducted using 19 measured phenolic and flavonoid chemicals to assess the overall effects of various irrigation regimes (100% NW, 50% NW + 50% ADW, and 100% ADW) and biostimulant treatments on the secondary metabolism of K. senegalensis leaves. 99.0% of the total dataset variation was correctly captured by the first two principal components, with PC1 accounting for 97.5% and PC2 for 1.5%. Three separate treatment clusters were identified by the resulting score plot, which was mostly based on mitigation status and water quality.

The PCA of the phytochemical profiles of K. senegalensis under irrigation regimes and biostimulant treatments.

Gallic acid, chlorogenic acid, catechin, methyl gallate, caffeic acid, and syringic acid were found in a tight cluster in the upper‐left quadrant of the control treatment (100% NW). Under uncompromised 100% NW circumstances, these treatments showed consistent scores that represented the baseline phytochemical profile. The treatments with a 50% mix of ADW (ferulic acid, naringenin, quercetin, cinnamic acid, and kaempferol) mapped next to or directly overlapped with the 100% NW control group from the mitigated moderate‐stress cluster (50% NW + 50% ADW). This closeness shows that, in spite of moderate irrigation stress, the concomitant biostimulant and fertilizer treatments effectively preserved a leaf chemical matrix comparable to that of the control plants.

Pyrocatechol, p‐coumaric acid, vanillin, rosmarinic acid, daidzein, and hesperetin showed a clear rightward shift along the PC1 axis toward higher values for the severe abiotic stress dispersal (100% ADW). This movement suggests that 100% ADW irrigation has significantly changed secondary metabolic pathways. On the other hand, rutin was identified as an extreme, isolated outlier that separated the highest‐stress treatment group from all other mitigated variants and was located at the plot's lowest left boundary (PC1 = −1.050; PC2 ≈ −1.050).

A pure culture of B. fabae isolate was obtained from faba bean seed displayed typical symptoms of chocolate spots, showing a few discrete chocolate‐colored spots appearing on green seeds, with additional large, grey necrotic patches appearing, as well as a pure culture of A. alternata isolate displayed symptoms of Alternaria blight from infected tomato leaves, showing lesions on leaves appear as black or dark, coffee‐colored spots with a series of concentric rings surrounded by a yellowish halo. They were obtained from crop fields of Beheira Governorate. The fungal isolates were identified based on cultural and morphological characteristics by light microscopic examination (Figure 2).

Molecular Identification Based on rDNA‐ITS Sequences

The fungal isolates were purified, and both strands were sequenced through the rDNA‐ITS region. The two isolates produced a PCR product of approximately 500–700 bp, and the ITS sequences were submitted to GenBank. The sequence data, alongside the BLAST tool search, proved the identity to be A. alternata (accession no. PP737871) and B. fabae (accession no. PP758476). The DNA sequence obtained for each fungal isolate showed 99.5% homology with the A. alternata sequences and B. fabae available in GenBank. The level of similarity reported here confirms the morphological identification of the isolates.

Discussion

The bio‐based chemical compounds derived from the extracts of K. senegalensis plants irrigated with three irrigation regimes combined with several fertilization formulations were identified by the chromatographic tool with an HPLC analysis. According to the results of the HPLC analysis, rutin, naringenin, gallic acid, chlorogenic acid, and catechin were the most abundant polyphenolic compounds detected in the extracts from K. senegalensis. Furthermore, components including syringic acid, ellagic acid, rosmarinic acid, and kaempferol were also reported.

The HPLC analysis of the extracts has revealed the existence of flavonoid compounds. Previous work revealed that the extracts showed notable flavonoid content (20.59–104.43 mg rutin equivalent/g), and the stem bark extracts displayed the highest total phenolics (87.69–46.28 mg gallic acid equivalent/g), phenolic acid (62.96–107.22 mg CE/g), and flavanol (3.60–135.40 mg CAE/g) contents [9]. The presence of chlorogenic acids in the K. senegalensis extract has been previously reported in the seed extract [71]. Methanol leaf extracts of K. senegalensis contained different coumaric acid derivatives [9].

Numerous bioactive compounds were detected in K. senegalensis leaves, including phenols (catechol), fatty acids (n‐hexadecanoic acid, 9,12,15‐octadecatrienoic acid, and oleic acid), and reducing sugar 4‐O‐methyl mannose (66.47%) [22]. Catechin, rutin, and quercetin rhamnoside are among the distinguishable phenolics found in the LE of K. senegalensis, reportedly [38]. The bark and leaf extracts of K. senegalensis contained some phenolic acids, such as protocatechuic acid glucoside, gallic acid, chlorogenic acid, neochlorogenic acid, cryptochlorogenic acid, p‐coumaric acid glucoside, p‐coumaroylquinic acid, caffeoylshikimic acid, feruloylquinic acid, and kelampayoside A [9]. The leaf and bark extract of K. senegalensis included several flavonoid components, such as (epi)catechin, apigenin hexoside, kaempferol rhamnoside, derivatives of quercetin, quercetin hexoside, quercetin rhamnoside, luteolin rhamnoside, rutin, and catechin [9]. From K. senegalensis leaf extract, five components have been identified and confirmed to be α‐amyrin, β‐amyrin, β‐sitesterol, quercetin, and rutin [72]. Quercetin glycosides, luteolin and isorhamnetin glycosides, and rutin were isolated from leaves of K. senegalensis [72].

The symptoms of Alternaria blight caused by A. alternata on the leaves were tiny, asymmetrical, or round dots that ranged in size from 2 to 4 mm. Additionally, there were noticeable patches that ranged in color from light brown to dark brown. The spots had distinctive concentric zonation inside them, and in extreme cases, the spots got bigger as the leaves completely dried and became blighted [73, 74]. The various morphological structures, viz., mycelium and conidia of A. alternata, were studied. Initially, the fungus produced profuse mycelial growth on PDA. The mycelium was hyaline, which turned gray. The finding about mycelium color is in conformity with present investigations of Atole [75], who noticed mycelium of Alternaria spp. as grayish to deep olive grey in color in infected chilli and watermelon. The symptoms of chocolate spot, caused by B. fabae on the Faba bean seeds, were caused by necrosis of the mesophyll [48, 49]. The conformation of conidiophores and conidia of Botrytis revealed by light microscopy reflects conidial size. The cultural and morphological investigations in this study were in agreement with Lee et al. [76]. Based on DNA sequences through the conserved ribosomal internal transcribed spacer (ITS) region, isolates of Alternaria and Botrytis spp. identified in this study (PP737871 and PP758476) had 96% to 99% sequence identity with a reference strain that was identified and obtained from GenBank, respectively. This is consistent with previous works [77, 78].

In the present work, potential antifungal activity, especially against A. alternata, was observed when the extracts were applied to teak wood samples. These data justify the correlation, reported by several authors, between the antimicrobial activity of K. senegalensis and these bioactive compounds [18, 27, 28, 42]. The potential antibacterial activity of the extract against Shigella and E. coli was demonstrated [28]. The plant flowers' defatted acetone extract was found to be efficient against Candida albicans, S. typhimurium, E. coli, and P. aeruginosa [79]. The ethanol and aqueous leaf and stem bark of K. senegalensis were effective against S. aureus, P. aeruginosa, E. coli, and S. pneumoniae [23]. Leaf aqueous extracts inhibited the mycelial growth of Rhizopus spp. and Mucor spp. [80].

Several studies have highlighted the biological attributes and bioavailability of phenolic bioactive compounds in medicinal and food plants [81, 82, 83, 84, 85]. From the fruit extract of K. senegalensis, methyl 6‐hydroxyangolensate, 2‐acetoxyseneganolide A, and seneganolide A showed promising antifungal activity against Botrytis cinerea [42]. The antifungal activity of K. senegalensis was confirmed, which revealed inhibition of the growth of Aspergillus flavus, A. niger, and Fusarium moniliforme [86].

Plants contain phenolic compounds, which have a variety of biological activities and health advantages, such as antibacterial, anticancer, anti‐inflammatory, and antioxidant effects [87, 88]. The establishment of environmentally friendly wood preservatives may benefit from the phenolic compounds' antimicrobial properties. The phenolic compounds that were evaluated exhibited varying degrees of activity against either A. flavus or Penicillium expansum [89].

When applied to wood samples at a concentration of 3%, phenolic and flavonoid compounds, such as kaempferol, myricetin, rutin, quercetin, rosmarinic acid, catechol, p‐hydroxybenzoic acid, and benzoic acid, that were discovered in another plant extract (Haplophyllum tuberculatum), inhibited the growth of Fusarium culmorum, Penicillium chrysogenum, and Rhizoctonia solani [90]. The wood specimens of Pinus sylvestris, Fagus orientalis, and Populus tremula treated with mimosa and quebracho extracts demonstrated potential antifungal activity against Trametes versicolor, Pleurotus ostreatus, Fomitopsis palustris, and Gloeophyllum trabeum; however, the bark extract of Pinus brutia showed no activity [91].

Extracts from Eucalyptus camaldulensis (leaves), Costus speciosus (rhizomes), and Pinus rigida (heartwood) to inhibit the growth of A. alternata, F. subglutinans, C. globosum, A. niger, and T. viride when applied to three different wood species (P. sylvestris, P. rigida, and F. sylvatica) showed the strongest antifungal activity [92]. Coniophora puteana and Serpula lacrymans were shown to be susceptible to potential antifungal activity in beech wood samples treated with a 25% concentration of Thymus serpyllum and Satureja montana extracts. Cinnamomum cassia bark extract did not demonstrate any discernible antifungal activity [93]. When larch wood extract was impregnated into poplar wood, it demonstrated strong antifungal activity against Gloeophyllum trabeum and Trametes versicolor. The extract caused the breakdown of cell membranes and reduced the enzyme activity of the wood decay fungus [94].

Finally, the leaf extract of K. senegalensis contained many phenolic and flavonoid compounds that were identified by HPLC. The highest amount at all was for rutin (29.8 mg/g extract) displayed in the extract of leaves unfertilized plants irrigated with 100% NW, followed by 27.28 mg/g extract as detected in the extract of leaves of plants irrigated with 50% NW + 50% ADW, which fertilized with NPK, and 20.92 mg/g extract, which was found in the extract of leaves from plants irrigated with 100% ADW and fertilized with NPK + TS.

The extract from plants irrigated with 100% NW, which fertilized with NPK + TS + seaweed, contained the highest amount of naringenin (13.03 mg/g extract), followed by the plants irrigated with 100% ADW, which fertilized with seaweed (11.03 mg/g extract), and (10.19 mg/g extract) was detected in the plants irrigated with 50% NW + 50% ADW, which fertilized with NPK.

It has been found that the extract of leaves of plants irrigated with 100% NW, which was fertilized with SE, induced the highest amount of gallic acid (6.87 mg/g extract), followed by those irrigated with 50% NW + 50% ADW, which was fertilized with NPK + TS + SE (6.51 mg/g extract), and 5.15 mg/g extract in plants irrigated with 100% ADW were fertilized with seaweed.

The plant's secondary metabolic pathways exhibit a highly sensitive and adaptable response to irrigation water quality and fertilization regimes, as demonstrated by the definitive separation of K. senegalensis leaf samples in the PCA space. A stable baseline is established by the close clustering of the 100% NW treatments (gallic acid, chlorogenic acid, catechin, methyl gallate, caffeic acid, and syringic acid), suggesting that the synthesis of phenolics and flavonoids remains strictly homeostatic under ideal water conditions.

A significant systemic elevation of defensive secondary metabolites is indicated by the marked rightward shift along the PC1 axis for the 100% ADW treatments (pyrocatechol, p‐coumaric acid, vanillin, rosmarinic acid, daidzein, and hesperetin). Heavy metal ions, residual agrochemicals, and high salinity are common in ADW. K. senegalensis changes its carbon allocation toward the phenylpropanoid pathway to combat the ensuing intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS) and oxidative stress. Strong antioxidant substances, including gallic acid, rutin, quercetin, and chlorogenic acid, which are crucial free‐radical scavengers and osmoprotectants that stop cellular damage, accumulate as a result.

Importantly, the extraordinary effectiveness of the applied biostimulants is demonstrated by the overlapping or adjacent placement of the 50% NW + 50% ADW treatments (ferulic acid, naringenin, quercetin, cinnamic acid, and kaempferol) with the pure control group. SE and microbial strains (TS), either by themselves or in combination with NPK, serve as a metabolic buffer when plants are irrigated with moderate‐stress drainage water. While biofertilizers containing beneficial Bacillus and Trichoderma strains improve root nutrient uptake and increase systemic tolerance, SEs provide easily accessible trace elements, signaling molecules, and betaines. By protecting the plant from metabolic shock and enabling it to maintain a balanced leaf chemistry that nearly resembles the healthy control group, this dual action successfully reduces the abiotic stress of the drainage water.

On the other hand, rutin's extreme isolation in the lower‐left quadrant indicates a condition of severe metabolic distress. Standard physiological adaptations break down under pure 100% ADW irrigation without any biostimulant or fertilizer intervention due to the lack of root‐zone support. This distinct location on the PCA plot indicates a significant threshold failure, which is defined by a totally changed and isolated phytochemical profile as a result of extreme, unrelieved oxidative pressure.

These findings show that specific biostimulants can successfully lessen the stress brought on by cultivating with recycled ADW.

Our research and the literature suggest that extracts from K. senegalensis leaves have potential antifungal activity against pathogenic fungi that cause diseases in economically important plants, attributed to the presence of phytochemicals, especially rutin, gallic acid, and naringenin, the main compounds.

Rutin targets several structural and metabolic processes in the fungal cell at once to produce its antifungal effects. This natural flavonoid employs a multifaceted mechanism that reduces the capacity of fungi to acquire drug resistance, in contrast to target‐specific pharmaceutical antifungals [95]. By disrupting fungal cell structures, blocking essential enzymes, and stopping the production of biofilms, rutin demonstrates strong antifungal efficacy [96]. Rutin targets the lipid components of the fungal cell, destroying its physical barrier. The cytoplasmic membrane is weakened by rutin's lack of stabilizing sterols. As a result, essential intracellular components like potassium ions (K+), alkaline phosphatase (AKP), and crucial cellular proteins immediately seep out [97]. Rutin inhibits normal cellular replication, stops Candida yeast budding, and slows down tissue‐penetrating hyphal elongation by attacking the fungal cell wall, changing normal hyphal shape, and flattening the cell structures [96, 99]. Fungal homeostasis is disturbed, and energy production is limited when rutin binds to essential metabolic enzymes. It directly prevents the synthesis of proteins and nucleic acids (DNA/RNA), which stops the fungus from growing or mending its damaged structures [95, 98, 99].

By concurrently targeting the fungal cell membrane, triggering programmed cell death (apoptosis), and downregulating virulence factors, gallic acid (3,4,5‐trihydroxybenzoic acid) demonstrates its antifungal effectiveness. Similar to rutin, this naturally occurring plant polyphenol has a multi‐target mechanism that makes it extremely powerful against a variety of diseases, such as agricultural molds like Fusarium graminearum, Trichophyton rubrum, Candida albicans, and Candida auris [100, 101]. The primary target of gallic acid is the integrity of the fungal plasma membrane [100]. Ergosterol, the essential sterol that maintains the stability of fungal membranes, can be directly bound by gallic acid [102]. Gallic acid actively sets off a chain reaction that compels the fungal cell to self‐destruct. It disrupts the fungus's energy production by changing the transmembrane potential of the mitochondria. Fungal cells exposed to gallic acid undergo a structural change in which phosphatidylserine flips to the outer membrane layer, a well‐known indicator of cell death [100]. Gallic acid causes intracellular ROS to build up quickly and severely. The cellular fatty acids are attacked by this internal oxidative surge. It accelerates cell lysis by breaking down the remaining healthy lipid structures. To withstand medications, fungal colonies form a biofilm, a protective matrix. This matrix is disrupted by gallic acid, which breaks down its biomass and exposes the susceptible cells beneath to host immunity or traditional antifungals [103, 104].

One well‐known citrus flavanone, naringenin, inhibits fungal energy metabolism, induces mitochondria‐mediated apoptosis, and damages cell membranes to provide its antifungal effects. Because of its multi‐target action, it is very efficient against both resistant agricultural fungi like Diaporthe citri and human diseases like Candida albicans [105, 106]. A key enzyme in the metabolism of glucose, fungal glucokinase (GCK), has an active pocket that naringenin binds to with great affinity [106]. Naringenin totally stops glycolysis and subsequent sugar metabolism by physically preventing glucose from attaching to GCK. This deprives the fungus of the energy needed for tissue colonization and basic survival [106]. Localized naringenin exposure paradoxically causes the fungus to overproduce extremely harmful intracellular ROS, particularly superoxide (O2 −) and hydroxyl radicals (OH−), rather than acting as an antioxidant [105]. Concurrently, the substance obstructs the fungal mitochondrial respiratory chain, exacerbating the depletion of ATP (energy) [107]. Naringenin dramatically downregulates the genes that produce the sticky, protective extracellular matrix of fungal biofilms, acting as an antagonist against cell‐to‐cell communication (quorum sensing) signaling molecules. This makes free yeast cells completely susceptible to removal by preventing them from adhering to host tissues or medical equipment [107].

Reusing agricultural wastewater to irrigate woody tree plantations is a workable solution to the problem of water scarcity in arid regions. Therefore, as long as the proper species are selected and management strategies are put in place, sustainable agriculture can make use of saline ADW and other low‐quality water. Irrigating crops with ADW, which contains salts and organic and mineral pollutants, alters leaf extract composition by increasing oxidative stress markers and salt levels, while modifying antioxidant and crude extract yields depending on dilution and treatment [58, 108]. Several studies show that salinity, water quality, and soil characteristics are the main factors influencing how ADW affects the growth of woody tree seedlings. In contrast, freshwater provided a more suitable growth environment with lower salt and pollutant concentrations, enhancing the efficiency of vital processes such as photosynthesis and protein synthesis while reducing oxidative damage.

Freshwater‐watered faba bean plants performed better than those irrigated with ADW in the majority of growth and productivity indicators, which led to greater oxidation indices [108, 109]. The ADW used causes osmotic and physiological stress for growing faba beans since it contains high levels of electrical conductivity, sodium, and potentially heavy metals in addition to pathogens [110].

When compared to other treatments, mango seedlings sprayed with seaweed or garlic extract and irrigated with ADW showed notable increases in growth, photosynthetic pigments, and leaf nutritional content. Additionally, irrigation with ADW markedly elevated proline and MDA, suggesting oxidative stress [111]. Another study revealed that under salt and waterlogging stress, the buildup of ions in the leaves and roots of five different types of woody plants varies. While trees with high salt tolerance can either exclude harmful ions (ion exclusion) or store them in vacuoles (vacuolar compartmentalization), sensitive species exhibit clear signs of toxicity, such as leaf scorch, leaf drop, and tip dieback [112]. Increasing the salinity of irrigation water changed the dynamics of root growth in olive seedlings, with root density and length declining as salt concentrations rose. As an adaptation response to salt stress, salinity has changed the distribution of biomass between aerial and sub‐aerial sections, increasing the root‐to‐shoot ratio [113]. In another investigation, it was discovered that salt stress altered the growth parameters, biomass, and photosynthetic traits of Terminalia arjuna seedlings. Ion accumulation in plant tissues and a reduction in height, stem diameter, and leaf area were the outcomes of increased salt content [114].

Among the most important secondary metabolites that aid plants in overcoming abiotic stresses, especially salt stress brought on by ADW, are phenolic compounds and flavonoids. It was shown that in order to protect themselves from oxidative stress, plants under salt stress create more phenolic compounds. Free radicals (ROS) like superoxide and hydrogen peroxide are produced by saline ions and can damage proteins, nucleic acids, and cell membranes [115]. The levels of flavonoids and total phenolics in the leaves and roots of four varieties of olives were markedly elevated by salt stress. According to the study, flavonols and flavonoid classes significantly contributed to salt stress, indicating their significance in the reaction to salt stress [116]. A thorough investigation found that secondary metabolite production pathways, especially those involving phenolics, triterpenoids, and flavonoids, are triggered by abiotic stresses including drought and salinity. It has been demonstrated that salt stress increases the gene expression of important enzymes in the phenylpropanoid pathway, such as 4‐coumaroyl‐CoA ligase (4CL), cinnamate 4‐hydroxylase (C4H), and phenylalanine ammonia‐lyase (PAL), which are thought to be the primary control points in the biosynthesis of phenolics and flavonoids [117]. The genetic regulation of secondary metabolic pathways responds to salt stress by activating stress‐related transcription factors such as WRKY, MYB, and bHLH factors, which control the expression of genes involved in flavonoid and phenolic production. These components are necessary to link the secondary metabolic response to stress signals [118]. When Dalbergia odorifera tissues were subjected to salt stress and waterlogging, which simulate the effects of poorly drained agricultural wastewater irrigation, the quantities of total phenolic compounds and total flavonoids increased considerably. This increase was associated with the activation of antioxidant defense systems because these compounds scavenge free radicals and protect cellular membranes from oxidation. As a result, changes in the metabolome, such as an increase in amino acids and osmolytes such as proline and glycine betaine, accompany these changes in secondary metabolism [119].

To survive in high‐salinity environments, the Kandelia candel tree, a woody mangrove tree, developed large amounts of secondary compounds such as phenolics, flavonoids, and anthocyanins. The variations between the salt‐responsive genes in this tree and those in salt‐sensitive herbaceous plants show the evolutionary adaptations exclusive to salt‐tolerant woody plants [120]. Abiotic stresses cause a significant rise in secondary chemical production in woody plants. Plant tissues exhibit increased concentrations of flavonoids, terpenoids, and volatile secondary metabolites in response to drought and salt stress. This increase is a protective adaptation process that enables the plant to survive under harsh conditions rather than a negative reaction [121].

Conclusions

African mahogany, or K. senegalensis, is significant for medicine and has several uses. Phytochemical screening revealed that the ethanolic extracts of the leaves contained a wide range of bio‐based chemical constituents. Gallic acid, chlorogenic acid, catechin, methyl gallate, caffeic acid, syringic acid, rutin, ferulic acid, naringenin, and kaempferol were the most prevalent phenolic and flavonoid compounds, and they were present in varying concentrations as the K. senegalensis plants were subjected to various treatments. This work shows that PCA is a useful method for mapping the metabolic adaptations of K. senegalensis under various agricultural and irrigation conditions. The addition of SE and TS biostimulants to the 50% ADW blend preserved the leaf chemical profile and kept it near the healthy control, but 100% ADW induced severe abiotic stress and threshold failure in unfertilized plants, which compels the plant to hyper‐accumulate defensive phenolics and flavonoids. The plant is able to preserve a leaf chemical composition that is comparable to the freshwater controls for this protective effect. These results show that recycled drainage water can be used in sustainable forestry as long as the trees are given the proper bio‐organic root‐zone treatments. It has frequently been demonstrated that the higher the concentration of the extract, the higher the efficiency against the pathogenic fungi examined. The chemical composition analysis may have revealed important bioactive chemicals that contributed to the noticeable biofungicide effect.
