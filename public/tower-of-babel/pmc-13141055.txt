Reflections on dental disease prevention in sub-elite competitive endurance sports: a mixed-methods analysis
Authors: Annabel Simpson, Rebecca O’Hanlon, Chris Easton, Fiona Henriquez, Mia C Burleigh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCompetitive athletes consistently demonstrate high rates of oral disease. This mixed-methods study investigated and compared the knowledge, beliefs and attitudes of athletes, coaches and dental healthcare professionals (DHPs) regarding oral disease prevention in a cohort of predominantly endurance athletes to inform future targeted health promotion. An online questionnaire was used to collect quantitative and qualitative data from sub-elite competitive endurance athletes, coaches, and DHPs, which were analysed using a mixed-methods approach. Responses from 67 athletes, 33 coaches, and 33 DHPs were analysed. Median knowledge scores were poor for athletes and coaches (36.4%) and DHPs (45.5%), with 0% as the lowest possible score and 100% the highest. Few coaches considered dental disease prevention part of their role, although most acknowledged responsibility for broader athlete health. DHPs applied evidence-based preventive principles to all patients but exhibited limited awareness of sport-specific risk factors and reported barriers in treating athletes, including time constraints and limited knowledge of the competitive sports environment. Overall, knowledge of dental disease prevention in competitive sport was low across all groups. Targeted educational and behavioural interventions could help reduce disease burden and its impact on athlete performance and well-being.

Introduction

Dental health is a fundamental element of overall well-being, influencing not only physical health but also daily functioning, confidence and overall quality of life.1 Plaque-induced dental diseases result from disruption in the homeostasis of the oral microbiome and environment, triggered by host behaviours such as poor oral hygiene and frequent carbohydrate intake.2 Although prevalent, these conditions are largely preventable when proactive behavioural changes are supported by evidence-based preventive interventions delivered by dental healthcare professionals (DHPs).3 Behavioural change theories, including the COM-B model and the Theory of Planned Behaviour, suggest that health behaviours are shaped by capability, opportunity, motivation and social influences.4 Applying these frameworks may help explain why athletes engage in behaviours that increase oral disease risk and inform the development of more effective preventive strategies.

One emerging area of interest is the relationship between lifestyle habits and oral health, with research suggesting that physical activity is associated with self-reported oral health in the general population.5 Moreover, links have been established between sports performance and oral health.4 6 Despite this, epidemiological studies of elite athletes consistently demonstrate a high prevalence of disease compared with the general population, with a corresponding impact on quality of life (recently reviewed by Schulze and Busse7). Emerging evidence suggests that similar patterns may also be present among sub-elite athletes.8 Contributing factors likely include reduced attendance at routine dental check-ups due to frequent travel for competition and training,9 physiological effects of intensive training such as systemic dehydration, reduced salivary flow and immunosuppression,10 as well as reliance on carbohydrate supplementation in sports with elevated energy demands.

Endurance sports, including long-distance running, cycling and triathlon, involve regular prolonged moderate- to high-intensity activity. Athletes rely on carbohydrate intake to support daily training and any efforts lasting over 75 min, helping to maintain blood glucose, delay fatigue and optimise performance.11 Even athletes in shorter events, such as the 800 m, regularly train for over an hour, often with sessions exceeding 75 min, making carbohydrate supplementation important for performance and recovery.

Current evidence suggests that athletes are receptive to preventive behaviour changes when these follow targeted interventions from a sports dentist.12 In the UK, however, most dental care is provided by DHPs without a special interest in sports dentistry, and just over half of elite-level athletes recall receiving dietary advice from their dentist.13 Instead, athletes report receiving advice from other members of their support team, including their coach.1417 Although epidemiological studies have documented the high prevalence of dental disease among athletes, most have been quantitative, providing limited insight into why athletes engage in behaviours that may compromise oral health. This study used a mixed-methods approach to explore and compare the knowledge, beliefs and attitudes of athletes, coaches and non-specialist DHPs regarding the prevention of dental disease in a cohort of predominantly endurance athletes training for competitive sports.

Methods

Data collection

A self-administered questionnaire was developed and hosted on the QuestionPro online platform to address the study aims. The first section gathered demographic information. The second included 11 scenario-based multiple-choice questions, rated on a five-point Likert scale, with an additional open ‘other’ option to assess participant knowledge. The final section consisted of modified Likert scale questions, checkboxes and open-ended questions to gather data regarding the respondents’ perceived role in maintaining athletes’ oral health, their reasons for holding these beliefs and whether they were aware of any barriers preventing them from promoting athlete dental health. The questionnaire logic was designed to ensure that each participant group completed only the sections relevant to their role and experiences. The full questionnaire is provided as online supplemental file 1.

Three cohorts were identified through purposive sampling, and the survey was distributed via email to professional groups and athletics clubs, with a request that these organisations forward the questionnaire to their members. Athletes were eligible if they were aged >18 years and participated in organised training aimed at improving competitive performance. Coaches were included if they were involved in setting training plans for athletes to improve competitive performance. DHPs were included if they were involved in the provision of preventative care and registered with the General Dental Council. The study was approved by the School of Science and Sport Ethics Committee, University of the West of Scotland. Public engagement was incorporated during the piloting stage, during which all three participant groups provided feedback. The full process of questionnaire development and piloting is outlined in online supplemental table 1 and 2.

Equity, diversity and inclusion statement

The author group was predominantly female and included early-, mid- and senior-career researchers from multiple disciplines. The study included both female and male participants; however, socioeconomic status, race/ethnicity and cultural identity were not used as selection criteria.

Quantitative analysis

Data were cleaned in Excel (V.2402) and exported to GraphPad Prism (V.10.1.1) for statistical analysis. Knowledge of dental disease prevention in competitive sports was assessed by calculating each respondent’s percentage score based on the total number of correct answers. Correct responses were determined by the research team using established guidance18 19 and are listed in online supplemental table 4.

Data normality was assessed using Shapiro-Wilk tests and boxplots. Athlete and coach knowledge scores were compared using the Mann-Whitney test. Frequency distributions were calculated for categorical and Likert-scale data. Associations between role (DHP or coach) and responses were examined using cross-tabulations and Fisher’s exact tests. Statistical significance was set at p≤0.05.

Qualitative analysis

Qualitative data were gathered through open-ended questions embedded throughout the questionnaire and were analysed using reflexive thematic analysis, following Braun and Clarke’s six-step model.20 Recognising that the researcher’s dual background as a dentist and competitive endurance athlete may have influenced interpretation, an artfully interpretive approach was applied.21 The dataset was read in full multiple times, and descriptive codes were generated through iterative identification of phrases with similar meanings. Related codes were then grouped and developed into overarching themes by the researcher.

Results and discussion

The responses of 67 athletes, 33 coaches and 33 DHPs were included in the analysis (see online supplemental table 3a-c for demographic characteristics). While two DHPs were aware of resources aimed at preventing dental disease in competitive sports, no athletes or coaches were aware of any such resources. Completion rates were as follows: 88% for athletes, 80.5% for coaches and 94.3% for DHPs.

The total number of correct answers from each respondent was calculated to determine overall percentage scores in the knowledge section of the questionnaire. The questions and their correct responses are shown in online supplemental table 4a-c. The median scores were 36.4% (IQR 27.3%–45.6%) for athletes and 36.4% (IQR 27.3%–45.6%) for coaches (figure 1A). No significant difference was detected between groups (p=0.74, U=1094). The median score for DHPs was 45.5% (IQR 36.4%–54.6%; figure 1B).

Athlete responses

Theme 1: missing sleep and dropping out—dental disease impacts athletes on and off the field of play

Consistent training is essential for achieving the physiological adaptations that underpin competitive performance, meaning any disruptions to an athlete’s usual routine may impact performance. In this context, it is notable that 15% (10) of respondents reported that dental disease had prevented them from carrying out their usual activity. This is comparable to the 6%–18% of athletes who have previously reported negative impacts of oral health on performance.7 For example, A46 had been left, ‘Unable to run due to an infection’, and A32 spoke of having, ‘…toothache once which was horrendous and prevented me from training until it was fixed’. A42 attributed poor race performance to dental disease as, ‘Toothache has given me a few consecutive bad night’s sleep in the week before a race and I dropped out…‘. 88% of elite-level athletes felt that awareness of the potential impacts of dental health on performance would motivate adherence to dental recommendations.12 The results of this study suggest performance benefits of effective dental prevention at the sub-elite level.

Theme 2: diet and dental advice from multiple sources (not the dentist)

Diet is a major determinant of oral health, with increased consumption of specific substances increasing the risk of dental disease.2 The athletes relied on multiple sources to inform their dietary choices, including coaches, other athletes, family, sports nutritionists and social media.

Coaches were discussed as valuable sources of advice, mirroring findings of previous studies.1416 A26 would, ‘…get recipes from my coach’, while A53 has, ‘…chats with coach about fuelling during marathon training blocks and races’. This highlights opportunities for well-informed coaches to encourage food-first nutrition and provide practical tips to mitigate the effects of carbohydrate supplementation.22 23

Several respondents consulted nutritionists for advice, including A30, who would seek advice within, ‘My training group—one of them is a nutritionist so provides us with what supplements, etc to take to help with certain things as well as posting healthy meals on his Instagram’ and A31, who indicated they worked formally alongside a nutritionist stating, ‘…in my club we work with a nutritionist and she gives us advice on what is good to eat and what foods are good for performance’.

Finally, several obtained advice from either peers or elite-level athletes. A30 noted that in the club setting they, ‘…share advice in the group about what gels etc are good and what tastes nice’, while A11 relied on advice from, ‘My brother, who is a division I athlete in the US’ and, ‘Cookbooks from professional runners’ and A12 discussed following advice from, ‘Elite athletes on social media’. There was scepticism regarding the usefulness of information provided by individuals who did not have experience in elite-level sports, and A64 said they would, ‘…discount it as most running influencers are not very competitive’.

This attitude towards social media influencers can be contrasted with the athletes’ responses regarding their dental health behaviours. When asked, 16% (11) of respondents indicated they would consider changing their oral health behaviours due to pressures from outside sources. Of note, three respondents mentioned influencers made them consider using tooth-whitening products, and three stated that they had used a tooth-whitening product as a direct result of something they had seen on social media. In contrast, only one respondent stated they would change their oral health behaviours following advice from their dentist.

Thematic analysis of coach responses

Theme 1: the role of the coach in the provision of diet advice

The coaches indicated nuances within the nutrition advice they provided, focusing on maintaining good general health and event-specific nutrition to maximise performance.11

Subtheme 1: diet advice for general health

Many believed their primary role in providing dietary advice was to ensure general health and well-being. C1 felt that they had a responsibility to, ‘Encourage general self-care of athletes…’ while C13 would, ‘…recommend a normal healthy diet that is balanced’. Ensuring sufficient food for energy expenditure was also a priority, summarised by C26 who said athletes, ‘Can’t start a session depleted as quality and quantity will be negatively impacted. Like trying to drive a car with no petrol’.

Coaches did not feel all carbohydrate sources were of equal merit. Several, including C29, advocated for the consumption of complex carbohydrates, encouraging ‘…good (complex) carbohydrates in the form of pasta/bread/rice/bananas etc in advance of and after training sessions’. These foods help maintain glycogen stores, supporting performance,24 with lower risk to oral health when compared with cariogenic and erosive carbohydrate supplements. The coaches primarily advocated for the use of water for rehydration. C25 said they, ‘…don’t currently recommend energy drinks…water all the way!…’ and C12 provided advice on, ‘what drinks they (athletes) bring to training, (water only, no energy drink or flavoured drinks)’. This agrees with expert guidance, which suggests limiting the consumption of carbohydrate supplementation where possible, in favour of a food-first approach to nutrition and the use of water for hydration.22 23

Additionally, C2 discussed their recommendation of ‘after sessions milkshakes’, to aid in recovery after a hard exercise bout. While the use of chocolate milk as an alternative recovery aid to other sports supplements is well documented,25 the impact of its higher sugar content on oral health is such that it is unlikely to be recommended by DHPs. Although the benefits of milkshakes as a recovery drink are less clear,26 cow’s milk demonstrates oral health benefits and may contribute to enamel remineralisation and inhibit cariogenic bacteria.27 These findings suggest that coaches could encourage athletes to consume milk post-training to support both recovery and oral health.

Subtheme 2: diet advice for sports performance

Several coaches discussed tailoring nutrition to individual needs and training load to support performance,11 described as ‘…a very individual 'thing’ for each athlete…’ by C27. Alongside several other respondents, and in agreement with expert guidance, C14 recommended carbohydrate supplementation in specific scenarios, ‘For those competing at half-marathon and beyond (including hill racing) or in heats, semis, etc. I suggest use of gels and sports drinks during long runs and competition.’, while C20 stated the exact quantities of carbohydrate needed to fuel different training intensities. These coaches acknowledged that some athletes may need to consume sports supplements, which carry possible oral health risks, and suggested both coaches and athletes may benefit from advice on how to minimise these risks.

Some felt athlete performance level impacted their ability to provide diet advice, an attitude shared by many DHPs. C16, ‘…would not provide any diet advice for my highest performing athletes as I have no qualifications to do so.’. Several others would direct athletes elsewhere due to their lack of formal qualifications. C24 said ‘I believe only basic general advice on diet should be advised unless the coach has some specific qualification/training in diet/nutrition and oral health.’ while C2 had ‘No formal qualifications in nutrition or diet, so therefore would signpost athletes to good sources of information or nutritionist…’. These responses align with previous studies showing that coaches have some understanding of nutrition guidelines but also significant knowledge gaps.28 Furthermore, the limited awareness among coaches regarding educational resources for dental disease prevention suggests they would benefit from targeted information through workshops and courses.

Theme 2: trusted sources of information provide the best advice

The coaches reported relying on several trusted sources to inform their practice, many of which overlapped with those cited by athletes and DHPs. Sports nutritionists were mentioned most often. The advice given by C20 was, ‘…Discussed at length with sports nutritionist’ and C10 stated, ‘We (their athletics club) have a nutritionist who guides us on what is appropriate at different stages of their training throughout the year’. These responses highlight the broader athlete support network involved in providing dietary advice, suggesting that targeted training for sports nutritionists could also help minimise any potential negative impacts of an athlete-specific diet on oral health.

Nearly 80% of the coaches had personal experience participating in competitive sports. Unlike the DHPs, who often relied on their own experiences to shape the advice they provided, only C14 mentioned that their own ‘…experience of the use of carbohydrate drinks and gels’ influenced their guidance. There was also evidence that coaches drew on expertise gained outside of sport, with C26 informed by their, ‘…background in physiology and public health’ and C14 referring to courses they had attended on the prevention of eating disorders and RED-S. Incorporating information on dental disease prevention within competitive sports into educational events for coaches could encourage them to tailor sports nutrition advice in ways that protect their athletes’ oral health.

Theme 3: the perceived role (or lack thereof) of the coach in dental health

While less than a third of coach respondents felt they had a role in preventing dental disease in athletes, some did acknowledge a degree of responsibility. C32 felt they had a ‘Major role as with other health issues. We want our athletes to be able to train and compete to their best. This involves being medically and dentally fit…’ and C30 stated they would ‘consider providing basic advice regarding diet and oral health.’. In contrast, C22 confessed, ‘I must confess I've never thought about my role in relation to dental health.’, while C13 admitted they ‘had not considered it before’. Encouragingly, no coach expressed opposition to taking on a role in athlete-specific dental disease prevention.

This range of responses highlights an opportunity to better engage coaches in supporting oral health. Many coaches currently do not see themselves as equipped to effectively support athlete oral health. Despite this, several already include recommendations to reduce unnecessary exposure to cariogenic substances as part of their basic ‘general’ health advice. Directing coaches towards accessible existing educational materials19 2931 could strengthen this approach. While the advice provided by coaches would never replace tailored preventive care offered by DHPs, integrating coaches into a broader support network, alongside nutritionists and other trained team members, may promote healthier oral habits among athletes.

Thematic analysis of DHP responses

Theme 1: patient education is paramount to achieving good oral health

DHP32 stated, ‘Patient education is paramount to achieving good oral health’, while DHP15 said their role came, ‘…down to educating the individuals if they demonstrate a lack of knowledge or errors in oral hygiene’. Providing oral health advice is essential to help patients understand dental disease prevention.32 However, after delivering this advice, the DHPs indicated that the ultimate responsibility lies with the patient. DHP5 stated that disease prevention was the ‘…patients’ responsibility with ownership of care’, and DHP2 said they must, ‘…allow them to make their own decisions’.

DHP13 indicated some athletes did not heed advice, speaking of, ‘Patients refusing to take on board information, for example, those who do ultra-marathons and who don’t have the weight to pack a toothbrush and toothpaste for that time so have a week of gels, and no fluoride’. Previous studies have identified poor adherence as a common source of frustration among DHPs.33 Although the performance level of the athlete described is unknown, this behaviour contrasts with earlier research showing that athletes are generally receptive to behavioural changes that support oral health.12 Emphasising the impact of dental disease on performance may be an effective way to motivate all athletes to prioritise their oral health during competition periods.

While fewer DHPs reported providing diet advice for general health, many still recognised it as part of their role. For example, DHP22 stated that their, ‘Role is to deliver general diet advice re health and oral hygiene’, while DHP27 emphasised the importance of diet for overall health, explaining that they, ‘will often mention the importance of a balanced diet’ to patients involved in sports. Interestingly, from their experience, dental disease ‘often correlates with other general health issues such as lack of periods’ in these patients. This suggests an awareness of possible links between Relative Energy Deficiency in Sport (RED-S) and oral health. Dentists may also detect other oral indicators of under-fuelling in athletes, including signs of haematinic deficiencies34 and eating disorders,35 indicating this is an area for further exploration.

Theme 2: if athletes are at increased risk of dental disease

Previous studies suggest an increased risk of dental disease among elite-level athletes7 and the DHPs indicated they would use several mitigation strategies to support these individuals.

Subtheme 1: diet advice will mitigate this

The frequent consumption of carbohydrate supplementation could be implicated in an increased risk of dental disease in athletes.36 DHP25 expressed apprehension over the ‘high sugar content of gels and sports drinks’, while DHP10 felt they must ‘highlight links between hydration drinks/carb gels to dental diseases’. There was acknowledgement from several DHPs that carbohydrate supplementation might sometimes be unavoidable but that athletes should aim to minimise its usage. DHP26 suggested athletes find a, ‘Balance between the need for energy and the need to reduce frequency and amount of sugar’, while DHP31 said, ‘I understand it is difficult however as they are wishing to get maximum performance’. Ensuring athletes and DHPs are aware of inter-exercise mitigation strategies could help reduce disease risk where feasible.

Subtheme 2: oral hygiene instruction will mitigate this

DHPs should advise patients on oral hygiene, including the use of fluoride dentifrices and proper techniques for toothbrushing and interdental cleaning.19 37 38 DHP21 advised, ‘…making sure the timing of OH measures is appropriate so as not to increase damage’ and DHP9 felt they had a responsibility, ‘To provide OHI with regards to toothbrushing with a fluoride toothpaste if they are consuming high amounts of fluids containing sugars, regular dental check-ups to screen for dental disease and provide preventative advice’. Some athletes and coaches indicated uncertainty over the use of fluoride in the competitive sports environment. Encouraging fluoride use and advocating for its safety for all patient groups may be a critical step in preventing dental disease among athlete populations.

Subtheme 3: enhanced prevention will mitigate this

Expert guidance recommends the use of high-concentration fluoride toothpaste, of at least 2800 ppm, when carbohydrate supplementation is used regularly and suggests fluoride mouthwash may also be appropriate.23 DHP13 said that they, ‘provide enhanced prevention if necessary - e.g higher dose fluoride toothpaste’ and DHP10 would, ‘…if necessary, increase fluoride exposure’. However, DHP12 would, ‘caveat that not all athletes are high caries risk…’. This perspective may have influenced responses to other parts of the questionnaire, possibly making some DHPs reluctant to assume an increased risk of dental disease in a hypothetical athlete patient compared with the general population.

Theme 3: I face barriers when I treat athletes

Previous studies highlight common barriers in the provision of preventive dental care to the general population. These include a lack of time, funding shortages, the perception that prevention is not an efficient use of clinical time, and poor patient adherence.39 Similar barriers were discussed by the respondents of this study.

Accurate assessment of the lifestyle and dietary factors that influence dental disease risk and the delivery of tailored advice requires significant time. DHP23, 24, 29 and 31 all stated, ‘time’ was a barrier to their provision of preventative care. This concern was particularly pronounced among those working under NHS contracts, with DHP17 stating, ‘…NHS time constraints do have an impact on our ability to deliver services’ and by DHP18 who had concerns over, ‘NHS zero time with patient’.

As athletes comprise only a small proportion of patients, providing sport-specific preventive care was generally not a priority for respondents. DHP6 admitted it was, ‘Not routinely within my history to even ask if they’re an athlete’ while DHP2 noted, ‘…there are not a huge number of professional athletes and for most dentists it is a low priority’. Several respondents suggested that athletes themselves may need to prompt such discussions. DHP15 said, ‘…I’m not going to be asking questions like “are you a professional or aspiring professional athlete” as part of a new patient examination…’ and DHP23 said, ‘Time with patients is very limited. This information (what carbohydrate supplementation is used) would need to be from the patients’. Athletes have previously been recommended to inform their dental team of participation in contact sports to ensure appropriate mouthguard guidance, an approach that could also be extended to sport-specific dietary considerations.40

Previous research indicates that a perceived lack of knowledge can undermine DHPs’ confidence in delivering care. DHP3 expressed interest in, ‘…learning more about the supplements’ and DHP9 wished to know, ‘…a bit more about what athletes require to perform and train and what these supplements contain’. Increasing awareness of the composition and use of carbohydrate supplements could help dental teams discourage unnecessary use and provide practical strategies to mitigate their impact. However, several DHPs felt uncomfortable contradicting other members of the athlete support team, with DHP10 stating, ‘If someone is on a coach-led diet - I wouldn’t feel confident changing the diet for sports performance…’. Like the coaches, the athlete’s performance level also influenced DHPs’ comfort in delivering advice. DHP25 stated, ‘There is a huge difference in giving advice to elite sportspeople and joggers competing in the local park run. I would feel entirely comfortable advising the latter, but the former may well have professional coaches and all the other facilities including dieticians working with them to maximise their sporting abilities. I would be much less comfortable advising an elite athlete as their perspective on dental health vs performance within their sporting activity will be entirely different from a member of the public who has sport as a hobby.’. While other professionals provide dietary guidance, dental health is not their primary focus,41 making the preventative advice from the dental team an important and unique contribution for athletes at all levels.

Theme 4: trusted sources of information provide the best advice

The DHPs indicated they relied on specific information sources to direct their actions. Of note, they appeared to place great emphasis on how they used their previous personal experiences to inform their practice.

Some indicated that previous participation in competitive sports provided them with useful insights that could be used to guide athletes. DHP14 had, ‘Some personal experience of endurance cycling events’ and DHP4 had, ‘…knowledge of athletes’ diet through personal experience…’ and would, ‘…use that to help with my advice I offered’. Experience treating athletes also appeared to enhance DHPs’ confidence managing similar patients in the future. DHP2 said, ‘…most of my knowledge comes from having patients that are professional athletes and trying to help them’. Exposure to certain sports enabled some DHPs to identify sport-specific dental risk factors. DHP31, who had previously treated swimmers, noted they were ‘likely to be increased caries risk due to high consumption of sugary drinks’ while DHP29 observed footballers’, ‘…diets were high in sugar and oh poor’, in line with previous studies into elite42 and sub-elite players.8 Conversely, DHPs without experience of competitive sports may be less equipped to deliver targeted preventive care to athlete populations, with negative repercussions for any in their care.

DHP23 stated their treatment of athletes was informed by CPD they had previously attended, stating, ‘Courses have shown high tooth wear and caries so fluoride toothpaste and regular recall is important’. While DHPs have a professional obligation to undertake CPD, the demands of clinical practice mean that training must be prioritised pragmatically.43 For some, the relatively small number of athlete patients makes targeted CPD a low priority, as explained by DHP2, who said, ‘I think courses would be useful but there are not a huge number of professional athletes and for most dentists it is a low priority’. In such cases, athletes who proactively disclose their sport-specific behaviours, such as carbohydrate use or training load, may help ensure they receive appropriate, tailored preventive advice.

Strengths and limitations

The mixed-methods design enabled both quantitative and qualitative analysis, highlighting knowledge gaps and exploring the reasoning behind respondents’ views. Though some social desirability bias was likely unavoidable,44 the anonymous online questionnaire offered a convenient means of data collection and may have encouraged more honest answers from respondents. However, during piloting, respondents were eager to elaborate beyond the questionnaire format, suggesting that focus groups or interviews might have yielded richer qualitative insights, although with a greater demand for time and resources.

While previous studies have focused on dentists with a specific interest in competitive sports, the mix of private and NHS DHPs who participated in this study more closely reflects the professionals that UK-based sub-elite athletes are likely to encounter. However, the sample may still over-represent individuals with a stronger interest in sports and a willingness to provide targeted preventative care. Although DHP recruitment included social media outreach, the questionnaire was distributed via email to staff at a dental hospital, who may not be representative of all UK-based DHPs. Similarly, athletes who perceived their dental health as affecting performance and coaches who valued their role in athlete well-being may have been more inclined to respond. Most athlete and coach respondents participated in individual endurance disciplines, reflecting the researcher’s network as a competitive endurance runner. While recent evidence suggests that athletes in individual sports may have poorer oral health than those in team sports,45 future research should also include team-sport athletes, whose supplementation practices and training demands may differ. Finally, all three groups in this study lacked ethnic diversity, which may have influenced perspectives on oral health priorities and the perceived importance of targeted preventative care.

Sports performance and research implications

Multiple athletes reported experiencing discomfort and disruptions to their usual activities due to dental disease. Over time, these issues can lead to a lifelong burden of dental treatment, reduced quality of life and an elevated risk of systemic conditions such as diabetes and cardiovascular disease. This highlights the need for coordinated action from agencies responsible for athlete well-being.

An IOC consensus statement advocates simple interventions to prevent non-communicable diseases, focusing on individual behavioural change supported by engagement from the athlete’s wider network.46 A similar approach has been suggested for reducing dental disease risk in athletes.22 However, securing commitment from all stakeholders remains a challenge. While DHPs can draw on evidence-based guidance19 and adapt it for patients, responsibility for fostering engagement and upskilling the wider athlete network is unclear. Notably, only two respondents, both DHPs, were aware of existing educational resources designed to reduce dental disease risk in competitive sport, indicating a significant gap in awareness and dissemination. Although the expertise of dental professionals in preventive care is irreplaceable, oral health education could be delivered through the same channels that many sports governing bodies use to provide guidance on general health, performance nutrition or RED-S prevention.47 Such platforms offer opportunities to integrate concise, targeted oral health advice, including incorporating ‘food first’ nutrition messages and advice on rinsing with water to reduce acidic and cariogenic exposure into workshops on carbohydrate supplementation for endurance performance.23 They could also encourage athletes to share dietary behaviours with their dentist, improving the likelihood of receiving appropriate, targeted care.

In addition, patient education alone will not sustain behaviour change48 and effective oral health promotion should instead be grounded in behaviour change theory, identifying motivators specific to individual athletes. One such intervention has already shown success in competitive sport,12 suggesting that understanding the unique motivators within the sporting environment could improve adherence and reduce dental disease risk.

Coaches, who have regular and direct contact with athletes, may be well placed to deliver preventive advice. Given that both athletes and coaches frequently consult sports nutritionists, this group may also represent an important target for promoting oral health. Future research should consider involving coaches and sports nutritionists as key stakeholders in the design and delivery of targeted behaviour change interventions.

Conclusions

Athletes, coaches and DHPs showed limited knowledge of dental disease prevention in competitive sport, with few aware of existing sport-specific resources. Coaches’ advice focused on athlete well-being and performance, while DHPs applied preventive principles regardless of training status and faced barriers when treating competitive athletes. Targeted educational and behavioural interventions could reduce the burden of dental disease and its effects on performance and well-being.
