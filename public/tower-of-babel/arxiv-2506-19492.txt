Is Long-to-Short a Free Lunch? Investigating Inconsistency and Reasoning Efficiency in LRMs
Shu Yang, Junchao Wu, Xuansheng Wu, Derek Wong, Ninhao Liu, Di Wang

Abstract
Large Reasoning Models (LRMs) have achieved remarkable performance on complex tasks by engaging in extended reasoning before producing final answers, yet this strength introduces the risk of overthinking, where excessive token generation occurs even for simple tasks. While recent work in efficient reasoning seeks to reduce reasoning length while preserving accuracy, it remains unclear whether such optimization is truly a free lunch. Drawing on the intuition that compressing reasoning may reduce the robustness of model responses and lead models to omit key reasoning steps, we investigate whether efficient reasoning strategies introduce behavioral inconsistencies. To systematically assess this, we introduce ICbench , a benchmark designed to measure inconsistency in LRMs across three dimensions: inconsistency across task settings (ITS), inconsistency between training objectives and learned behavior (TR-LB), and inconsistency between internal reasoning and self-explanations (IR-SE). Applying ICbench to a range of open-source LRMs, we find that while larger models generally exhibit greater consistency than smaller ones, they all display widespread “scheming” behaviors, including self-disagreement, post-hoc rationalization, and the withholding of reasoning cues. Crucially, our results demonstrate that efficient reasoning strategies such as NoThinking and Simple Token-Budget consistently increase all three defined types of inconsistency. These findings suggest that although efficient reasoning enhances token-level efficiency, further investigation is imperative to ascertain whether it concurrently introduces the risk of models evading effective supervision.

Introduction

Large Reasoning Models (LRMs) have significantly pushed up the boundary of performance in solving complex problems by allowing extended “thinking” before giving the final answers
[
10
,
27
,
33
]
. During this “thinking” stage, LRMs iteratively explore multiple strategies, reflect on their own reasoning, and perform self-correction. However, this process can also lead to the “overthinking phenomenon,” where the model generates an excessive number of tokens, even for a trivially simple question such as
2 plus 3

[
8
]
. To solve this problem, considerable efforts have been devoted to
efficient reasoning
, which seeks to optimize reasoning length while preserving the final performance in solving problems, offering practical benefits such as reduced computational costs for real-world applications
[
31
]
.

However,
is long-to-short reasoning truly a free lunch?
While such methods maintain accuracy and increase the token efficiency of reasoning, we must ask: what might be
overlooked
in the process?
Drawing inspiration from
Baker et al. 2025b
, which suggested that reducing inference costs by penalizing long chains-of-thoughts (CoTs) may force models to compress reasoning into internal activations while concealing decision-driving factors, we consider the potential side effects of such efficiency.
This compression potentially encourages the behavioral inconsistency of LRMs, such as “say one thing and do another”
[
36
]
and “don’t say what they think”
[
34
,
3
]
. Building on this intuition, our work explores whether efficient reasoning can induce such inconsistencies, rather than merely focusing on accuracy as in prior work. A key challenge to answering this question lies in how to define and measure the inconsistency of LRMs. Prior works on CoT faithfulness
[
34
,
9
,
3
]
, fake alignment
[
35
]
, and LLM specification gaming
[
11
]
have pointed to different forms of inconsistency, but these have typically been discussed in isolation, with ambiguous and overlapping definitions.

Related work and background

Inconsistency in large language models.

Previous work has extensively discussed the inconsistency of LLMs from various perspectives. However, the concept itself remains ambiguously defined and conceptually entangled. Reward hacking and tampering
[
1
,
13
]
critically challenge LLM alignment, exposing a core inconsistency between the objectives we intend the model to learn and what it actually internalizes
[
30
,
28
]
. Models may discover and exploit shortcuts such as skipping the unit test to achieve higher rewards during training
[
5
]
. If given sufficient permission, they could even manipulate the environment’s reward mechanisms directly or learn to scheme to hide their true capabilities and objectives and escape from monitoring
[
11
]
.
Moreover,
Greenblatt et al. 2024
,
Xu et al. 2025
have shown that LLMs can produce inconsistent answers to the same question when presented under different system or task settings. For instance, a model may refuse to respond to a harmful request in a paid-tier user context but provide a harmful response in a free-tier user context
[
16
]
. Similar inconsistencies arise across evaluation formats, for example, when the same question is posed in a multiple-choice format versus a Question-Answering format, or even when the wording of the question is slightly altered. As demonstrated in
Arcuschin et al. 2025b
, a model might answer “yes” to both “Was Subhash Ghai’s Taal released later than Velu Prabakaran’s Puratchikkaaran?” and “Was Velu Prabakaran’s Puratchikkaaran released later than Subhash Ghai’s Taal?”, further underscoring the fragility of consistency in LLM behavior. Moreover, as a large reasoning model which will have a thinking stage before giving the output, CoT or self-explanation faithfulness and consistency are getting more and more important; will the LRM give out the true things they are thinking or just give out a Post-Hoc Rationalization
[
34
,
3
]
.

As LRMs increasingly become promising approaches for solving complex tasks, concerns around the faithfulness and consistency of their chain-of-thought or self-explanations have become more prominent. A central question is whether these models truly reflect their internal reasoning processes or merely generate plausible post-hoc rationalizations to justify their outputs
[
34
,
3
]
.
In this paper, we begin by providing a precise definition of behavioral inconsistency in LLMs, with a particular focus on LRMs (§
3.1
). We then propose
ICbench
, a structured evaluation framework for systematically assessing inconsistency across a range of tasks and settings (§
3.2
).

Efficient reasoning.

Although recent O-1 or R-1 style LRMs
[
27
,
10
,
32
,
33
]
have demonstrated strong capabilities in self-reflection and solution searching, pushing the boundaries of performance across various tasks, they still struggle with the so-called “overthinking phenomenon”
[
8
,
31
]
: they may produce verbose reasoning or redundant intermediate steps even for a very simple question, leading to lengthy output responses, resulting in substantial computational overhead and increased reasoning time. To address this issue, numerous studies have explored methods for optimizing the reasoning trajectory and improving token efficiency while preserving accuracy.
Some approaches introduce length-based rewards during the reinforcement learning phase to incentivize models to balance the trade-off between accuracy and efficiency. In such settings, shorter yet accurate answers are expected to receive higher rewards generally
[
4
,
32
,
21
]
. Other methods apply supervised fine-tuning (SFT) using variable-length reasoning datasets, including techniques such as CoT compression and reconstruction with tailored strategies—for instance, retaining the earliest solution that first arrives at the correct answer
[
31
]
, sampling the shortest correct answers
[
25
]
, or leveraging state-of-the-art LLMs as compressors to generate more concise reasoning paths
[
18
]
. Meanwhile, recent work has shown that models can be routed into two distinct inference modes: quick answer generation or step-by-step reasoning
[
33
]
. Notably, training-free methods have also been shown to be effective in this context.
Ma et al. 2025
demonstrate that models can maintain high accuracy even when prompted to skip the reasoning stage and directly generate the final answer. Similarly,
Lee et al. 2025
show that models are capable of compressing their own CoT to stay within a token budget, suggesting that accuracy and efficiency can seemingly be preserved without trade-offs. In this work, we revisit this apparent “free lunch” through the perspective of behavioral inconsistency, asking: does the pursuit of efficiency compromise the consistency of models?

Defining and measuring inconsistency in large reasoning models

In this section, we first clarify the definition of inconsistency in LRMs (§
3.1
), and then introduce our benchmark,
ICbench
, designed to systematically measure inconsistency in LRMs (§
3.2
).

3.1
Characterizing and defining inconsistency

Various terms have been used to describe different forms of inconsistency, as discussed in §
2
. In this work, we primarily focus on behavioral inconsistency as manifested during model inference, rather than inconsistencies introduced during training, such as reward hacking
[
30
]
. Before conducting a comprehensive evaluation, we first present a systematic definition of three key types of behavioral inconsistency. These categories are informed by and aligned with the most frequently discussed inconsistency phenomena in prior work (e.g.,
[
34
,
3
,
16
]
).

Inconsistency across task settings
(ITS)
.
refers to the phenomenon where a model produces different answers to the same question when presented in different settings. For instance,
Xu et al. 2025
observed that LLMs exhibit divergent behavior between word-based questions (i.e., What’s your opinion) and deed-based questions (i.e., Which action will you take), characterized as
“saying one thing and doing another.”
Similarly, models behave differently when answering the same question in open-ended formats versus multiple-choice settings
[
35
,
2
]
. Another form of
ITS
arises in refusal behavior: the same toxic prompt may be accepted or rejected depending on whether the model is in a Deployment vs. Development phase, as illustrated by the left example in Figure
1
, or whether it is accessed via a free-user or paid-user
[
35
]
. These inconsistencies have been widely discussed in the context of jailbreaking, particularly under methods employing “competing objectives”
[
20
,
16
]
.

Inconsistency between our training objective and learned model behavior
(TR-LB)
.
arises when a model’s outputs are influenced by triggers like hints or biasing features
[
34
]
embedded in prompts, leading it to adopt behaviors such as shortcutting or sycophancy to try to get a higher reward from the evaluator
[
11
,
6
]
. For instance, a model can learn to modify the
compute_reward.py
script in the environment
[
11
]
or employ hint in the context window
[
9
]
. These behaviors often manifest as scheming, where the model manipulates its outputs to superficially align with user expectations
[
24
]
. As shown in the middle panel of Figure
1
, the model changes its previously correct answer to an incorrect one when exposed to a misleading hint injected into the prompt.

Inconsistency between internal reasoning and output self-explanations
(IR-SE).
means the model will not always tell the truth about what motivates it to get their final decision, even if the self-explanation appears plausible, characterized as models “don’t always say what they think” or as producing unfaithful self-explanations
[
9
,
29
]
. Furthermore, as shown in the right panel of Figure
1
, LRM may internally admit (e.g., between
<think>
and
</think>
tags) that they merely guessed the answer, but still output a seemingly well-reasoned explanation for choosing option C, which aligns with the finding defined as “Implicit Post-Hoc Rationalization” by
Arcuschin et al. 2025a
. Efficient reasoning by encouraging the model to skip the thinking stage or compress the CoT may intuitively lead the model to hide its actual guesswork and instead produce post-hoc rationalizations. To further investigate this possibility, we present a comprehensive benchmark for evaluating such inconsistencies in the §
3.2
.

3.2
ICbench
: A Framework for measuring inconsistency of LRMs

Drawing inspiration from previous research on jailbreaking and other adversarial attack methodologies
[
20
,
11
,
12
]
, we develop adversarial inputs with triggers, manipulate environmental conditions, and utilize diverse task settings to systematically elicit and measure inconsistencies in models.

Given a query
x\in\mathcal{D}
, we define the response of an LRM
\mathcal{M}
to an original prompt (a prompt prefixed solely with “
Please answer the following question
” and without additional triggers or cues) as

\mathcal{M}(x)=\text{Concat}\left(\hbox{\pagecolor{lightMint}\text{$t^{\text{r}}$}},\ \hbox{\pagecolor{lightBrown}\text{$t^{\text{a}}$}},\ \hbox{\pagecolor{lightLavender}\text{$c$}},\ \hbox{\pagecolor{lightPeach}\text{$a$}}\right),

where
t
denotes the model’s internal reasoning trace, typically enclosed between special tokens
<think>
and
</think>
. This
t
can be further split into reflective reasoning steps
t^{\text{r}}
and a conclusion
t^{\text{a}}
.
c
corresponds to the externalized chain-of-thought following
</think>
and preceding the final output. Finally,
a
represents the model’s conclusive answer or action.

In the following, we formalize the metrics to measure the inconsistency regarding
ITS
,
TR-LB
, and
IR-SE
respectively:

Adversarial task settings for measuring
ITS
.

To measure
ITS
, we evaluate whether a model’s final answer remains consistent under adversarial task settings, such as
Deployment
versus
Development
.
We construct pairs of task configurations, where each pair consists of a baseline setting
\mathcal{T}_{\text{base}}
and a corrupted setting
\mathcal{T}_{\text{corr}}
as illustrated in Table
1
.
These configurations are designed to induce behavioral changes in the model by altering system-level prompts (e.g., switching to an “act as in the development mode") or by modifying the task format (e.g., converting an open-ended question-answering to multiple choice).

For the given
\mathcal{M}
and a query
x
, we can define the occurrence of
ITS
as the final answer changes under
\mathcal{T}_{\text{base}}
and
\mathcal{T}_{\text{corr}}
:

a_{\text{base}}\neq a_{\text{corr}},

where
\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{T}_{\text{base}}(x))=\text{Concat}(t_{\text{base}},c_{\text{base}},a_{\text{base}})\text{ and }\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{T}_{\text{corr}}(x))=\text{Concat}(t_{\text{corr}},c_{\text{corr}},a_{\text{corr}}).

Crucially, this inequality does not refer to token-level exact match, but instead reflects a shift in the model’s final answer. The criteria used for this judgment are detailed in Appendix
B
.

To quantify this, we define the
\text{ITS}_{\text{score}}
as the expected
ITS
over a dataset
\mathcal{D_{T}}
:
\text{ITS}_{\text{score}}=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D_{T}}}\left[\mathbb{I}\left(a_{\text{base}}\neq a_{\text{corr}}\right)\right],

where
\mathbb{I}
is the indicator function.

Contextual cues for measuring
TR-LB
.
To measure
TR-LB
, we examine whether the model’s final answer is influenced by contextual cues that elicit sycophancy and shortcut-taking behavior. Specifically, as shown in Table
1
, we define a set of counterfactual cues
\mathcal{C}
designed to trigger such behaviors in model
\mathcal{M}
. Inspired by recent studies on LLM sycophancy and strategic misalignment
[
11
,
15
,
24
,
14
]
, we categorize these cues into two types:

•

Sycophancy cues (
\mathcal{C}_{c}
), where the model is implicitly nudged to conform to user-provided opinions or preferences, even when they are incorrect.
3
3

3

For example, the prompt may include a persona that provides a misleading hint like “I’m pretty sure the answer is C,” which may influence the model to choose C despite its internal reasoning.

•

Shortcut-inducing cues (
\mathcal{C}_{s}
), where the prompt includes spurious patterns or permissions that suggest exploitable shortcuts.
4
4

4

For instance, all previous examples in the context window may choose option A, subtly biasing the model to follow suit regardless of its reasoning.

Given a model
\mathcal{M}
and a query
x
, we define the occurrence of
TR-LB
as the case where the final answer under contextual cues, denoted
a_{\mathcal{C}}
, differs from the original answer
a
:

a_{\mathcal{C}}\neq a,

where the model’s response under these cues is
\mathcal{M}({\mathcal{C}(x)})=\text{Concat}(t_{\mathcal{C}},c_{\mathcal{C}},a_{\mathcal{C}})
. The
\text{TR-LB}_{\text{score}}
quantifies this inconsistency over a dataset
\mathcal{D_{C}}
:

\text{TR-LB}_{\text{score}}=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D_{C}}}\left[\mathbb{I}\left(a_{\mathcal{C}}\neq a\right)\right].

Normalized inconsistency score (NIS).
To ensure that observed inconsistencies are not merely due to randomness (e.g., sampling variability), we need to further optimize the
\text{ITS}_{\text{score}}
and
\text{TR-LB}_{\text{score}}
by normalization. For each query
x\in\mathcal{D}
, we generate
N
samples under each setting, denoted by
\{a_{*}^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^{N}
. The
intrinsic inconsistency
is then estimated by computing the proportion of pairwise disagreements among these answers of
\mathcal{T_{\text{base}}}
or the original setting:

p_{\text{intrinsic}}(x)=\frac{\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq N}\mathbb{I}(a_{*}^{(i)}\neq a_{*}^{(j)})}{\max\left\{\frac{N(N-1)}{2},\,\mathcal{S}\right\}}.

Here,
\mathcal{S}
denotes the maximum number of sample pairs we set to control computational cost. If
\mathcal{S}<\frac{N(N-1)}{2}
, only a subset of sample pairs is randomly selected.

To estimate the
raw inconsistency
under perturbation, we define the perturbed setting as the same query
x
evaluated with an augmented prompt (i.e., injected with counterfactual cues
\mathcal{C}
for
TR-LB
or alternate task settings
\mathcal{T}_{\text{corr}}
for
ITS
). The model responses in this setting are denoted as
{a^{(i)}_{\circ}}_{i=1}^{N}
. We then compute the disagreement between each perturbed sample and its corresponding unperturbed counterpart:

p(x)=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\mathbb{I}(a^{(i)}_{\circ}\neq a^{(j)}_{*})}{\max\left\{N^{2},\,\mathcal{S}\right\}}.

The calibrated inconsistency probability is defined as the excess inconsistency remaining after subtracting the intrinsic inconsistency:

p_{\text{calibrated}}(x)=\max\left(0,\,p(x)-p_{\text{intrinsic}}(x)\right).

Finally, we define the Normalized Inconsistency Score (NIS) by aggregating the per-example calibrated scores and normalizing by the residual inconsistency not explained by intrinsic variability, following a similar formulation as in
Chen et al. 2024b
:

\text{ITS}_{\text{NIS}}\text{ or }\text{TR-LB}_{\text{NIS}}=\frac{\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D}}\left[p_{\text{calibrated}}(x)\right]}{1-\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D}}\left[p_{\text{intrinsic}}(x)\right]}.

Measuring
IR-SE
.
We define
IR-SE
to capture the misalignment or “scheming” behavior within the model’s reasoning process, building on inconsistent cases previously identified in
ITS
and
TR-LB
. Inspired by work on unfaithful self-explanations in LLMs
[
34
,
23
,
9
]
, we introduce three failure modes in which a model’s final answer or explanation contradicts its own internal reasoning. ( examples of each case are illustrated in Figure
1
) : (1)
Self-disagreement
(
\text{IR-SE}_{\text{self}}
): The model internally arrives at an answer
t^{a}
during its reasoning phase but outputs a different final answer
a
, formalized as
\mathbb{I}(t^{a}\neq a)
. (2)
Post-hoc rationalization
(
\text{IR-SE}_{\text{posthoc}}
). Although
\mathbb{I}(t^{a}=a)
, the model generates a reasoning chain
c
that is inconsistent with the internal trace or scratchpad
t
, which can be formulated as
\mathbb{I}(t^{r}\neq c\text{ and }t^{a}=a)
. (3)
Withholding influencing factors
(
\text{IR-SE}_{\text{withhold}}
). The model fails to verbalize key contextual signals or cues (
\mathcal{T}
or
\mathcal{C}
) that significantly impacted the answer inconsistency, formulated as
\mathbb{I}(\text{verbalize}(\text{Contac}(t_{*},c_{*}),\mathcal{T}or~\mathcal{C}))=0
, where
t_{*}
and
c_{*}
refers to the reasoning trace and output by the model in any given evaluation condition.

Each of these dimensions captures a unique way in which the model’s internal reasoning may fail to faithfully support its decision. To quantify these failure modes, we compute their occurrence rates over the subset of inconsistent samples

\mathcal{D}_{\text{incons}}
as follows:

\text{IR-SE}_{\text{self\_score}}=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D}_{\text{incons}}}\left[\mathbb{I}(t_{a}\neq a)\right]
,

\text{IR-SE}_{\text{posthoc\_score}}=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D}_{\text{incons}}}\left[\mathbb{I}(t^{r}\neq c\text{ and }t^{a}=a)\right]
,

\text{IR-SE}_{\text{withhold\_score}}=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D}_{\text{incons}}}\left[\mathbb{I}(\text{verbalize}(\text{Contac}(t_{*},c_{*}),\mathcal{T}or~\mathcal{C}))=0\right]

.

Benchmarking LRMs in
ICbench

Consistency emerges with scale in distilled LRMs.

Table

compares

across eight open-source LRMs from two model families. We observe a clear inverse relationship: as model parameters increase, inconsistency generally decreases, although all these models are fine-tuned with the same datasets distilled from the corresponding teacher model (DeepSeek-R1 and Qwen3-235B). This suggests that student base models with greater capacity may be more adept at faithfully reproducing the nuanced decision-making of the teacher, thereby preserving more consistency. These findings align with prior work showing that stronger student models can push the boundaries of performance
[
7
]
, and we further underscore consistency as a key benefit of scaling in stronger students. However, as shown in Table
2
, when tackling more complex problems such as those in OlympiadBench, the Qwen3-4B model anomalously exhibits a higher
TR-LB
compared to other models. This suggests that, when faced with particularly challenging tasks, medium-scale models like Qwen3-4B may be more inclined to leverage contextual cues to revise or adapt their initial judgments. One possible explanation is they have better recognition of their own uncertainty and are using additional information than 0.5B or 1.7B models.

Inconsistency between speak and deed can be more stubborn.
As illustrated in Table
2
, nearly all evaluated models exhibit notable inconsistency on the WDCT task, which involves opinion-based or ethical judgments. Unlike tasks predominantly reliant on reasoning, inconsistency in the WDCT task does not display a strong monotonic correlation with model size. This suggests that addressing the “say one thing and do another” phenomenon
[

]
can be a more intricate challenge, one not necessarily resolved merely by increasing the parameters.

LRMs can be scheming.
As shown in Figure

\text{IR-SE}_{\text{self\_score}}

[

]
preserving computational intermediates. Interestingly, even when the final answer
a
aligns with the internal reasoning answer
t^{a}
, the model can display post-hoc rationalization. Specifically, it may express uncertainty or indicate guessing during the internal reasoning, but this uncertainty is

in the output response, which appears confident and well-justified as we exemplified in Table

, and poses a significant challenge for reliably monitoring misalignment in large reasoning models
[

. We give more discussion about the inconsistency across different adversarial task settings and contextual cues in Appendix

[

: if models are constrained in their response length, even if only prompted to self-compress without specific training, they prefer to curtail their internal reasoning trace, which may not be visible to humans rather than the details of their final output; thereby, it becomes increasingly difficult to effectively monitor potential deceptive behaviors during training and inference. This observation underscores the need for further investigation into whether efficient reasoning , while pursuing high accuracy and efficiency, may inadvertently introduce the risk of models evading effective supervision. We include additional experiments with 1.5B and 14B models, as well as analyses of corrupted examples, in Appendix
D.2
and Appendix

to further support our findings.

5
Conclusion

As LRMs increasingly showcase strong capabilities in solving complex problems, efficient reasoning has become a significant area of research interest. In this paper, we investigated a critical concern: whether long-to-short strategies can compromise the behavioral consistency of LRMs. To systematically explore this, we first constructed
ICbench
, a dedicated benchmark for evaluating the inconsistency of LRMs. Our findings revealed that training-free efficient reasoning approaches like
NoThinking
and
Simple Token-Budget
can substantially harm LRMs’ consistency. This observation underscores an urgent need for further investigation into whether the pursuit of efficient reasoning, while aiming for high accuracy and shorter responses, can inadvertently introduce risks that complicate or impede effective model supervision and monitoring.

Appendix E
Limitations

To better explore the “no-free-lunch” phenomenon within efficient reasoning, our study preferentially focused on training-free methods. This choice was motivated by their practical advantages, namely their independence from external computational resources and additional datasets, coupled with their demonstrated effectiveness in compressing Chain-of-Thought. However, a comprehensive evaluation across the broader spectrum of long-to-short reasoning methods, such as those mentioned in §
2
, was beyond the scope of the current work. Such an extensive investigation would be crucial for a more complete understanding of how different efficiency techniques impact model consistency, and we leave this as a direction for future study.
