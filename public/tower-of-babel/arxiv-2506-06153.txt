Personalized Large Language Models Can Increase the Belief Accuracy of Social Networks
Adiba Proma, Neeley Pate, Sean Kelty, Gourab Ghoshal, James N. Druckman, Ehsan Hoque

Abstract
Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly involved in shaping public understanding on contested issues. This has led to substantial discussion about the potential of LLMs to reinforce or correct misperceptions. While existing literature documents the impact of LLMs on individuals’ beliefs, limited work explores how LLMs affect social networks. We address this gap with a pre-registered experiment (N = 1265) around the 2024 U.S. presidential election, where we empirically explore the impact of personalized LLMs on belief accuracy in the context of social networks. The LLMs are constructed to be personalized, offering messages tailored to individuals’ profiles, and to have guardrails for accurate information retrieval. We find that the presence of a personalized LLM leads individuals to update their beliefs towards the truth. More importantly, individuals with a personalized LLM in their social network not only choose to follow it (as a way of indicating they would like to obtain information from it in subsequent interactions), but also construct subsequent social networks to include other individuals with beliefs similar to the LLM – in this case, more accurate beliefs. Therefore, our results show that LLMs have the capacity to influence individual beliefs and the social networks in which people exist, and highlight the potential of LLMs to act as corrective agents in online environments. Our findings can inform future strategies for responsible AI-mediated communication.

Here, we study the impact of personalized LLMs in the context of a social network. The LLMs are constructed to provide accurate information, in line with work on misperception corrections

Specifically, we expect that, relative to individuals in social networks without a personalized LLM, those in networks with access to a personalized LLM response will be more likely to move toward belief accuracy (hypothesis 1). This follows since the LLM will provide persuasive messaging, tailored to individual preferences. Additionally, we predict that those in social networks with an LLM are likely to include the LLM in their subsequent social network at rates greater than chance (hypothesis 2a). Prior research suggests that individuals tend to seek factual truth
[

Finally, this same logic (i.e., the truth-seeking nature of individuals) leads us to hypothesize that, relative to individuals in social networks without an LLM (i.e., the control), those with access to an LLM will form subsequent social networks that include significantly more members whose beliefs tend towards accuracy (hypothesis 2b). The belief updating a la hypothesis 1 combined with the desire to associate with others similar to themselves contributes to this prediction. This prediction suggests that adding an LLM to a network not only stimulates belief accuracy but it also shapes subsequent social networks such that they include more accurate beliefs overall.

Here, we evaluate our hypotheses in a dynamic, socially embedded, empirical setting (Figure

Specifically, we were interested in whether the presence of the LLM caused participants to have more accurate beliefs, and if it impacted how participants constructed their social networks.

Personalized LLM Responses Shift Beliefs Toward Factual Accuracy

The LLM intervention influenced the structure of participants’ social networks. Beyond the bot itself, participants tended to follow human peers whose views were more closely aligned with the bot’s accurate stance, as predicted by hypothesis 2b. While both groups engage in truth-seeking behaviors

To quantify this tendency, we computed a “follow signal” for each participant: the mean factual accuracy score (calibrated by truth direction) of the individuals they chose to follow at each round, averaged over all three rounds. The score was normalized such that responses closer to the truth yielded lower values, and the most truthful individual—either the bot, if followed, or the lowest-scoring peer—was excluded to enable fair comparisons (see SM Section

Given the adjusted belief scale ranges from 0 (most accurate option) to 4 (least accurate option), our results suggest that participants in the treatment notably shift towards more truthful network compositions. Moreover, we find that networks became more assortative between stages 2 and 3 (see SM Section

Importantly, participants were not unaware of the bot’s presence. In the post-surveys, 51% of treatment participants reported interacting with a bot, compared to 22% in the control group (Figure

Discussion

We show that personalized LLM interventions can support belief updates toward ground truth in the context of political statements. The fact that participants followed the bot at high rates and sought out others aligned with its position indicates that targeted AI agents can influence both individual cognition and network-level structure. Individuals tend to seek truth
[
27
]
, and by embedding a truthful agent within the social context, our intervention amplified this baseline tendency. We find no difference in the efficacy of the intervention across multiple factors, including the statement veracity, economic relevance, topic salience, individual demographic traits, and time of data collection. This suggests that the intervention is robust.

To our knowledge, this is the first study to empirically demonstrate that a personalized LLM embedded in a social network can reshape the surrounding social topology in a direction aligned with factual accuracy. Participants in the treatment condition were more likely to connect with others whose views were closer to the ground truth, compared to those in the control group. These findings point to the dual function of such agents: they serve as direct informants and as catalysts for reconfiguring information flows in a network. In contemporary online ecosystems, where individuals are routinely exposed to conflicting signals, personalized LLMs may offer a scalable way to redirect attention toward reliable sources and re-anchor belief trajectories.

Notably, the observed belief shifts occurred even without a back-and-forth dialogue and even when participants were not explicitly aware that they were interacting with a bot (note that they were made aware that a bot might be present and had provided consent to interacting with one). These features make our experimental setup more reflective of actual digital environments, where users often engage with brief messages and may only have partial awareness of whether content is AI-generated.

At the same time, the mechanisms that enable this influence also carry clear risks. Prior studies have shown that users often accept LLM responses, even when they are factually incorrect or deliberately misleading
[
30
,
31
]
. Our results underscore how even modest personalization and contextual awareness can enhance credibility. Thus, replacing our guardrails with maligned structures makes it plausible that a similarly structured but malicious bot could amplify misinformation rather than mitigate it. As LLM-driven systems become more integrated into public discourse, designing effective guardrails such as transparent sourcing, regular audits, and limits on persuasive framing will be essential. Our findings help define what such systems can do and where oversight must be targeted. The results make clear that LLMs have enormous potential in not only altering beliefs but also altering the very social fabric in which people interact.

As moderating content on large-scale social media platforms becomes more difficult to complete directly, personalized LLMs could be utilized as a potential method. With user permission, individuals could receive persuasive, factual responses to potential sources of misinformed beliefs in any information domain. To ensure responsible use, not only should guardrails as we describe here be considered, but also be extended to further protect users and incorporate auditing, such as receiving consent and not collecting any personally identifiable information. Such personalized content should focus on ensuring transparency, fairness, and accountability in how the system operates – positioning them as assistive technologies for navigating today’s complex information environment.

This work can shape future research within the space of personalized LLMs and factors of belief. For example, conducting a similar study within other information environments would help reinforce the generalization of these findings. Furthermore, investigating the effects of such an intervention in real-world platforms would shed light on its efficacy in a setting with more complex information streams. While considerable research addresses how LLMs affect beliefs, our results accentuate that the impact may go further by altering the social networks within which we exchange information. Thus, it is imperative to continue to study LLM interactions within social settings consisting of both human and AI interactions, and to protect the end users throughout the process.

Materials and Methods

We are interested in how personalized information from an LLM bot (relative to the absence of a personalized bot) impacts beliefs and subsequent social network construction. The basic structure of the study placed individuals in a social network and then had them: 1) report their belief about the veracity of a statement and explain their rationale with brief text, 2) learn about the veracity judgments of others in the social network, 3) have the opportunity to update their belief and explain it again, and, 4) choose whom from the network they would like to follow. We made two additional design decisions. First, we followed prior political science work on opinion formation by including four entities in a social network
[
32
,
33
]
. Participants in each network took part concurrently (and were recruited to do so). There was a median of 16 participants per study batch, within the range 10-28 participants. This variation is due in some part to the timing in the day of the experiment and participant availability. Framework limitations also play a role, such as 10 participants being required to participate in order for the experiment to work properly, and a maximum of 24 people can be supported in the treatment because of time constraints on bot response generation. Second, we created a dynamic setting where individuals took part in three rounds (with each proceeding as described below). A choice in one round influenced later rounds. Specifically, when a participant chose someone to follow in an earlier round, that actor became more likely to appear in the participant’s subsequent round(s) of the social networks. This increased experimental realism, which refers to the extent to which participants take the study seriously
[
34
]
. Making consequential choices presumably increases engagement.

For reference, see Figures
S1
,
S2
,
S3
to view the interface for all parts of the experiment, which are described below. Note that while these figures depict real responses from participants and a bot, this does not reflect a real snapshot of one round of a given experiment; the figures are purely for framework description. While the interfaces remain consistent across all rounds, the behavior in round 1 differs slightly from rounds 2 and 3.

The precise sequence for the
first round
was as follows.

Stage 1: Response stage (First round)

Participants are provided with a prompt statement on one of the following topics: immigration, inflation, fuel and oil, or overpopulation. Some of these statements were true while others were false. More details on how these statements were selected are provided in section
2.1.2
of Supplemental Materials. Participants reported how much they believed the statement to be true on a 5-point scale (with higher scores indicating stronger belief that the statement is true). They additionally provided reasoning for their rating in a maximum of 1,000 characters. We refer to each evaluation and its accompanying explanation as a “response entry.” All participants in a given network receive the same statement.

Stage 2: Revision stage (First round)

In the revision stage, participants received the response entries of a few others in the network. Participants re-rated the same prompt again on a 5-point scale, and also selected from a pre-determined list of reasons the rationale behind their selection (see Supplement Section
3.8
for options). The participant’s initial rating is displayed to ensure that any change in their rating is intentional. However, even if they do not change their response, participants still had to click the scale manually.

This stage includes our experimental manipulation. In the control, participants were shown the entries of three other participants in the study. In the treatment, participants viewed the entries of two other participants as well as that of the personalized bot. The personalized LLM bot always provided the correct value, with a response entry tailored to the participant’s preference.

Stage 3: Selection stage (First round)

In the selection stage, participants were presented with six response entries. Participants learned these entries came from those they previously followed, and/or those ‘recommended’ by the system. Participants saw the three peers from stage 2 (including the bot for the treatment condition), with the other three being randomly recommended. This setup ensures that participants get access to diverse responses. The only difference in the selection stage between control and the treatment is that in the treatment, one of the response entries is that of the personalized bot.

Participants must then like or dislike each response entry (to prompt thoughtful engagement with the content) and select (i.e., “followed”) three peers whose answers they would like to subsequently see in the next round. They could stick to the peers assigned at stage 2 or pick new ones (or do a mix of both). Through this stage, we can evaluate how participants restructured their networks, including whether they chose to follow the personalized bot and incorporate the bot into their networks. We can also quantify the changes in the participants’ networks given the presence of the bot.

Rounds 2 and 3
were similar, with a few changes to account for increasing experimental realism. Participants have the same stage 1 but with a new prompt on the same topic (i.e., a new statement on the same issue). Stages 2 and 3 for rounds 2 and 3) differed, however, as follows.

Stage 2: Revision stage

Similar to round 1, participants saw three others. For the control, there was one randomly chosen person that they had followed in the prior round and two others they had not followed (randomly chosen). For the treatment, the participants saw the personalized bot, a followed human participant (chosen at random), and a non-followed human participant (chosen at random). As mentioned, we ensured participants saw at least one person they had followed to provide them with partial autonomy over their networks (and thus, their social influence). We included non-followed individuals to minimize the possibility of extreme homophily.

Stage 3: Selection stage

In stage 3, participants saw the peers they were shown in stage 2 (including the bot for the treatment condition), those they followed in the previous round, and the rest were randomly recommended. This ensures that the setup maintains the “social media” structure, where once someone follows a person, they do not get unfollowed unless the user manually unfollows them.

Evaluation Metrics

To generate all shift plots (i.e., plots showing shift towards or away from the truth), we first encoded the initial and updated scores as a value from [0, 4], where 0 represents “Strongly Disbelieve” and 4 represents “Strongly Believe,” since these were the labels provided to participants during the study. To account for the distinction between true and false statements, the difference between the two values for statements with the ground truth of “true” are calculated as initial - updated, and statements with the ground truth “false” are calculated as updated - initial. These calculations ensure that a shift in the intended direction is encoded as a negative change, while a shift towards misinformed beliefs is encoded as a positive change. For each person, this difference was averaged over all rounds, and then plotted using a kernel density plot. For determining distribution differences, all calculations use the t-score to calculate the confidence interval and p-value. When confidence intervals between findings of interest overlap, Levene’s test is utilized to determine whether the variance between the two samples are equivalent, then the appropriate t-test is conducted based on those results to determine significance. Levene’s test is considered to be a robust test
[
37
]
utilized in prior works
[
38
]
. We use t-tests because the standard deviation of the underlying population is not known, and initial tests indicate the distribution was not normal.

For calculating the follow signal, we consider the subset of individuals within a given round that were followed by each participant. For each person’s followed subset, we drop the most truthful person when 3 people were followed (which is mostly the case, except for a few edge cases). We then average the scores of the followed participants, again normalized to true/false statements by considering the initial score in the false case and 4 - initial score in the true case for all followed players. This ensures a score of 0 is the most truthful score. After computing the average follow signal for each round, we then find the mean across all rounds; this represents the average follow signal across the whole experiment for a given individual (see Equation
S1
). This is again plotted using a kernel density plot and tested for significance in a similar procedure as described for shift evaluations.

\delta_{followsignal}=\frac{\sum^{3}_{i=1}\frac{(\sum^{n}_{j=1}f_{j})-f_{min}}{n-1}}{3}

(S1)

When computing proportions, we take the number of participants who fall into a specified category and divide it by the sum of all answers given. To compare proportions, we utilize a two proportion z test, where we compare the proportion of the given category across two conditions.

2
Designing the Experiment and the Personalized LLM Bot

Below describes our specific processes for determining elements of experiment design and the personalized LLM bot.

2.1
Experimental Design

Beyond the experimental platform design, other aspects of the design had to be considered: the news sources the LLM bot should be able to access, the political statements individuals would see during the experiment, and the selection of our time-frames for hosting these studies. Each of these is discussed in detail below.

2.1.1
Selection of the news sources

The news sources an individual chooses to trust can be affected by a variety of factors, and as a result, our goal was to select a wide variety of news sources that would encapsulate the potentially diverse opinions/preferences of individuals. Several resources were consulted and compared to create a comprehensive, representative list of trustworthy sources. One of the most important factors for trust in news sources is political affiliation. According to a 2020 report from Pew Research Center
[
39
]
, a large proportion of U.S. adults trust sources such as CNN, CBS, and Fox News. Similarly, a Cooperative Election Study (CES) Common Content 2022 study conducted “across a nationally representative sample of 60,000 American adults” which showed that Americans engage with several news networks, including CNN, MSNBC, and Fox News
[
40
]
. Next, we considered AllSides Media Bias Chart and “The Chart” created by Ad Fontes in 2018
[
41
]
to determine the political leaning and the factuality of the sources. The factuality and leaning of these sources were cross-referenced with those provided by ground.news
[
42
]
, a website that also provides media bias and factuality ratings aggregated according to the news reported by a news source. Through this process, we shortlist potential news sources, ensuring a relatively equal representation of left, right, and central sources. Next, we checked whether their API is publicly available through GNews and if enough articles can be extracted from that source to build our RAG. Our final set of 10 news sources were Washington Examiner (right), New York Post (right), Fox News (right), AP News (center), The Economist (center), BBC (center), New York Times (left), CNN (left), Washington Post (left), and MSNBC (left). Note that the news preference dataset was built considering these 10 news sources, and the articles from these news sources were used to build our RAG. The number of articles extracted from each source is provided in Table
S4
.

2.1.2
Political Statement Generation

We selected four specific topics — overpopulation, inflation, immigration, and fuel and oil — of varying saliency levels, to ensure generalizability of our experimental setup. The topics were selected based on reported saliency of issues during the period of the 2024 US presidential election, balancing with two high salience (inflation and immigration) and two low salience (overpopulation and fuel and oil) topics
[
43
,
44
]
. We curate a series of political statements for each of the topics. These statements must be bipartisan and with objective ground truth. The statements themselves can be true or false. To ensure a variety of statements, we generate them in two different ways. First, we conduct a series of in-person focus group studies, where participants come up with their own politics-related statements, and find their corresponding verifying information online. This provided insights into the personal aspects of misinformation (i.e., what makes statements believable for individuals). To create the statements used for this study, we relied on credible information to prove or disprove our statements from government published statistics, Politifact, FactCheck.org and Gallup. The final selected statements are provided in Tables
S1
,
S2
, and
S3
.

2.1.3
Selection of our time-frames

We conducted our study before and after elections. The duration for before elections was October 1 - October 31, 2024 and after the elections, it was November 15 - December 15, 2024. There are prior work suggesting that there is some change in beliefs pre and post elections
[
45
]
. However, we did not have prior predictions about it moderating the treatment effects. Our results show that the differences in context do not, in fact, affect the effectiveness of the intervention. More details about these results are presented in Section
3.6
.

2.2
Personalized LLM Bot

Our goal was to first design an LLM bot that can incorporate an individual’s preferred news sources and rhetorical styling to create compelling arguments. The personalized bot design follows a four-step pipeline to generate the responses using ChatGPT as the base model. At first, we predict users’ news source preferences and their preferred rhetorical style from ethos, pathos, and logos using K-Means clustering and collaborative filtering. The preferred news source predictions are then passed to the RAG model. The RAG contains news articles from 2023 to 2024 from the 10 sources previously mentioned in section
2.1.1
. The model finds related articles, considering the predicted news source preference, and then summarizes those articles to 3-4 sentences. The exact prompts used are provided below. This summarized information, along with the predicted rhetorical style, is then passed to another LLM, which rephrases the summarized information to incorporate the rhetorical style provided. We provide tables that capture a variety of LLM bot final outputs, along with their initial input statement (Tables
S5
,
S6
,
S7
,
S8
). For anonymity, the demographics and personality input into the system are not given. The table shows the different variations in facts, sources, and styles the LLM utilized.

3.9
The connection between changing rating and following the bot

To understand if there is a connection between individual rating and following the bot, we take a more granular look at the treatment group. For each round for each participant, we consider if they followed the bot or not, and calculate their belief change (with a more negative rating meaning moving closer to the truth). Our results show that the mean change in rating was almost twice as high when they followed the bot compared to when they did not follow the bot. Mean change was -0.473 (CI: -0.526, -0.419) for when they followed the bot, and mean was -0.210 (CI: -0.261, -0.159), when they did not follow the bot. This is consistent with the possibility that the individuals altered their beliefs due to the bot, and then chose to follow the bot. We cannot, however, directly test that dynamic since we cannot isolate the precise actor in the network that influenced a respondent’s beliefs.

3.10
Network-Level Following Trends

Given the main paper focuses on effects at the individual level, we also look at network effects. First, we examine the assortativity of the network, or how often individuals connect with others of similar beliefs. We find that individuals move towards homophily. This analysis is done by comparing stage 2 and stage 3, since individuals have a choice in stage 3 but not in stage 2 (all stage 2 and stage 3 differences significant at p <0.001). We highlight these findings to show that individuals in both conditions are not updating their preferences at random.

Since individuals in both the treatment and control are not updating their following at random, we then investigate how often individuals follow the bot per round, compared with how often individuals follow the “most truthful” option in control. When there is potentially more than one “most truthful” option (for example, two people have the same value), we select one from the set at random and check whether the individual followed them. This is meant to be comparable to the bot follow in the treatment, since the bot can be considered as only one “user”. Here we see similar trends in following these individuals between the treatment and the control (p = 0.118), suggesting that individuals in both the treatment and the control tend to pursue others with more truthful beliefs.

These findings in tandem show that the individuals within the experiment are utilizing the follow mechanic as intended, and individuals pursue truth within their networks. The plots are in Figures
S24
,
S22
and
S23
. We argue this pursuit of truth can be leveraged for supporting LLM interventions such as ours in the future.

3.11
Persuasiveness of the LLM Bot

All previous work discussed in this manuscript points to the implication that our LLM intervention is, in fact, persuasive. Prior works have emphasized that LLMs can embody persuasive traits
[
15
,
16
,
47
]
. That being said, we are interested in understanding why our LLM intervention is persuasive. In order to identify what aspects of the intervention make it persuasive, we analyzed message properties from Stage 1 for both the participants and the LLM intervention, comparing the shifts. We find that personal references within participant responses and displaying a mix of partisan cues through source references (compared to one partisan cue) were significant.

Figure
S25
shows the results of the control and treatment shifts, partitioned on whether or not the participant utilized personal pronouns within their Stage 1 written response. Personal pronouns include “I”, “we”, “our”, “us”, and variants of those including contractions. “us” was explicitly checked for whether or not it was referring to “US” (United States) since many of our statements are about the US at large. We find no notable difference in the control conditions (p = 0.179), while the treatment condition shows a larger shift when individuals reference personal pronouns (p <0.00001). Given this is only significant in the treatment, this suggests that the bot has more of an influence on individuals who feel the topic is personally relevant, which aligns with prior persuasion work
[
48
,
49
]
.

Figure
S26
shows the results of the treatment shifts, partitioned on whether the LLM intervention pulled in sources of a single political leaning compared against when it showed sources of different political leanings. The results show that individuals shifted more towards the truth when a variety of source leanings is provided (p <0.05), though this happens somewhat infrequently throughout the experiments (n = 241 where individuals saw a mix of political leanings in sources in a given round, n = 962 where individuals saw one political leaning cue in a given round). This suggests that pulling in cross-partisan agreement can boost persuasiveness by mitigating partisan motivated reasoning, as shown in prior works
[
22
,
50
]
.
