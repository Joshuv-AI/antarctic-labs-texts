Revealing the cuproptosis-lncRNA nexus in gastric cancer: A multicohort observational study
Authors: Xin Zhou, Jin Liu, Xiaping Wang, Yiqing Zhang, Lan Zhang, Zicong Jiao, Xuan Gao, Li Liu, Mingxia Zhu, Jing He, Tongshan Wang, Qing Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
To establish a cuproptosis-related lncRNA signature and evaluate its prognostic value and potential immunological relevance in gastric cancer (GC). Transcriptomic data from the cancer genome atlas and gene expression omnibus were analyzed using Pearson correlation and least absolute shrinkage and selection operator Cox regression to construct the prognostic model. Model performance was assessed by Kaplan–Meier analysis, time-dependent receiver operating characteristic curves, and decision curve analysis. An mRNA-based RS score was further validated in Gene Expression Omnibus datasets and the IMvigor210 cohort. Expression of 2 key long noncoding RNAs (AC245041.1 and LINC01614) was confirmed by qRT-PCR in an independent GC cohort. A 25-lncRNA cuproptosis-related signature effectively stratified patients into high- and low-risk groups, with the high-risk group showing significantly poorer survival. The model outperformed conventional clinical variables. Functional enrichment suggested involvement of immune regulation and mitochondrial pathways related to cuproptosis, and the low-risk group displayed a more immunologically active profile. The RS score showed consistent predictive performance across external cohorts, and qRT-PCR validated the prognostic relevance of AC245041.1 and LINC01614. This cuproptosis-related lncRNA signature demonstrates potential as a prognostic indicator and may provide insights into immunotherapy relevance, offering a promising direction for personalized GC management.

1. Introduction

Gastric cancer (GC) continues to pose a major global health burden, ranking fifth in cancer-related mortality worldwide.[1] Owing to its insidious onset, ~80% of GC patients are diagnosed at advanced stages, leading to poor clinical outcomes. While immunotherapy has revolutionized the treatment landscape for metastatic GC, its effectiveness is limited, with only a subset of patients responding to immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs).[2,3] The 5-year overall survival (OS) rate for advanced-stage GC remains as low as 5%,[1] underscoring the pressing demand for reliable prognostic predictors and novel therapeutic strategies.

GC progression is driven by a multifaceted interaction of genetic, epigenetic, and environmental influences. Among these, long noncoding RNAs (lncRNAs) play crucial roles in histone modifications, DNA methylation, chromatin remodeling, and transcriptional regulation.[4] Several lncRNAs have been implicated in GC pathogenesis. For instance, UCA1 enhances the proliferation and migration of GC cells by downregulating p21 and SPRY1, while also enhancing immune evasion by preventing miRNA-mediated PD-L1 suppression.[5] Given their functional versatility, lncRNAs have emerged as promising diagnostic and prognostic biomarkers for GC.[6-8]

Cuproptosis is a recently identified form of copper-dependent programmed cell death.[9] Unlike other forms of cell death, cuproptosis is triggered by mitochondrial respiration, wherein excess copper binds to lipoylated components of the tricarboxylic acid cycle, leading to proteotoxic stress and subsequent cell death.[9] Targeting cuproptosis has shown therapeutic potential, as evidenced by a phase III clinical trial, where the copper ionophore elesclomol demonstrated antitumor efficacy in patients with melanoma.[10]

Recent studies have further elucidated the role of cuproptosis in GC and other cancers.[11] Huang et al revealed that the atypical methyltransferase METTL16 enhances FDX1 accumulation by mediating N6-methyladenosine (m6A) modification of FDX1 mRNA, ultimately inducing cuproptosis in GC cells.[12,13] Furthermore, Li et al developed a novel prognostic signature comprising 7 DE cuproptosis-related immune genes to predict long-term survival in GC patients.[14] This highlights the intricate regulatory network governing copper-induced cell death and suggests potential therapeutic avenues. However, despite preliminary explorations into the interplay among cuproptosis, lncRNAs, and GC, existing studies have typically been restricted to a narrow subset of lncRNAs, lacked comprehensive bioinformatics analyses, or failed to include necessary experimental validation.[15-21] Consequently, the prognostic relevance of cuproptosis-associated lncRNAs in GC is still not fully understood.

Therefore, our study integrates large-scale transcriptomic datasets from the cancer genome atlas (TCGA) and gene expression omnibus (GEO) to construct a robust prognostic model, systematically characterizing the cuproptosis-lncRNA landscape in GC. Furthermore, our validation of 2 major cuproptosis-related lncRNAs in an independent GC cohort further emphasizes the therapeutic potential of targeting the cuproptosis-lncRNA axis.

2. Materials and methods

2.1. Data collection and initial processing

This STROBE-compliant observational study was designed with a predefined primary and secondary objective. The primary objective was to develop a cuproptosis-related lncRNA prognostic signature for GC. Secondary objectives were to assess its association with immune characteristics, validate an RS score across independent cohorts, and confirm the expression of key lncRNAs using qRT-PCR. RNA transcriptome data in the fragments per kilobase per million format, with corresponding clinical information for GC patients, were retrieved from TCGA database on April 5, 2022. A total of 407 samples (375 GC tissues and 32 normal tissues) with full clinical information, including age, gender, T stage, N stage, M stage (according to the AJCC 8th) and survival data were included to construct the risk score model. Subsequently, 3 suitable datasets: GSE57303 (n = 70), GSE62254 (n = 300), and GSE84437 (n = 433), containing mRNA microarray profiles and prognostic data were obtained from the GEO database to serve as validation cohorts. Additionally, an independent dataset, GSE15459 (n = 192), which lack clinical information, was chosen as an external validation. The clinical characteristics of all included patients were summarized in Table 1. In addition, we included the IMvigor210 cohort-patients with advanced urothelial carcinoma undergoing atezolizumab (anti-PD-L1) therapy, accompanied by full clinical data in our analysis.[22]

2.2. Identification of cuproptosis-induced cell death related LncRNAs

Ten cuproptosis-related genes, namely FDX1, LIAS, LIPT1, DLD, DLAT, PDHA1, PDHB, MTF1, GLS, and CDKN2A were collected according to Tsvetkov’s study.[9] Differentially expressed (DE) cuproptosis-related genes and lncRNAs between normal and tumor tissues were analyzed and the pearson correlation analysis was employed to define the correlation between related genes and corresponding lncRNAs. The correlation coefficient |R2| > 0.1 and P < .05 as well as mean expression value >1 were used as the screening criteria to identify DE cuproptosis-related lncRNAs.

2.3. Construction of the cuproptosis-related LncRNA risk score model

First, univariate Cox regression was performed to specify prognostic lncRNAs related to OS in the TCGA cohort (P < .05). Next, the least absolute shrinkage and selection operator Cox regression analysis was performed using the “glmnet” R package to develop the optimal prognostic signature.

2.4. Prognosis analysis

Univariate Cox regression was employed to assess the prognostic value of the risk score model for OS, disease-specific survival (DSS), and progression-free interval (PFI). Kaplan–Meier (K–M) curves and the forest plots were performed to assess the differences in survival times. A nomogram was developed by incorporating multiple prognostic factors to predict patients’ OS rates. Furthermore, we evaluated the Net-Benefits of different predictive factors using the “rmda” algorithm. To observe the distribution of the identified lncRNAs, principal component analysis and t-distributed stochastic neighbor embedding were employed using the “prcomp” function of the “stats” R package and the “Rtsne” R package respectively.

2.5. Therapeutic value analysis

To assess the risk model’s ability to predict chemotherapy outcomes, we determined the half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) for some drugs (including cisplatin, docetaxel, paclitaxel, doxorubicin, metformin and rapamycin) in different groups using the “pRRophetic” package. Next, using data from the cancer immunome atlas,[23] the relationship between different groups and immunophenoscore (IPS) was also calculated and visualized via “ggpubr” R package.

2.6. Immunogenomic landscape analysis

The mutation profiles of the low- and high-risk groups were calculated through the “maftools’ package in R. Tumor mutational burden (TMB) and microsatellite instability (MSI) of GC patients in the 2 groups were analyzed by ‘limma’ package. To assess the immune cell infiltration of different risk score groups, “CIBERSORT” algorithm was used and visualized by “pheatmap.” Analyses regarding the correlation between immune function scores, m6A regulators and different risk score groups were achieved by “limma” package.

2.7. Functional enrichment analysis

To investigate pathway enrichment, we utilized the gene set enrichment analysis softwarefor gene ontology (GO) and kyoto encyclopedia of genes and genomes (KEGG) analyses.[24-26] Analyses were carried out with the R packages “org.Hs.e.g.db,” “clusterProfiler,” “enrichplot,” “DOSE/limma.” Gene sets with |NES|>1, NOM P < .05, and FDR q < 0.25 were considered as enrichment significant.

2.8. Construction of the RS score model

We further constructed a scoring system of RS score as a surrogate of cuproptosis-related lncRNA model in GEO cohorts.[27] The procedures were as follows: the genes with higher expression in the high-risk subset was defined as gene signature A, while those in the low-risk subset termed as gene signature B. Enrichment score of gene signature A/B in each sample was calculated using the “GSVA” package. The RS score was then derived by taking the difference between the enrichment scores of gene signature B and gene signature A.

2.9. Experimental validation in tissue samples

Tissue samples from 80 formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded(FFPE) gastric adenocarcinoma cases were collected at the First Affiliated Hospital of Nanjing Medical University between January 2012 and January 2020, with written informed consent obtained from all patients. Clinical characteristics are summarized in Table 1. After standard treatment, patients were regularly followed for a median of 41.5 months (range, 4.8–73.2 months), with the last follow-up on January 10, 2020. Two cuproptosis-related lncRNAs, AC245041.1 and LINC01614, were selected for validation, as they exhibited the smallest P-values among all the identified lncRNAs. RNA extraction, quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) of the 2 selected lncRNAs were performed as previously described.[28,29] Briefly, RNA was isolated from FFPE tissues using the RecoverAll™ Total Nucleic Acid Isolation Kit (Ambion, Austin, TX, USA). qRT-PCR was conducted with SYBR Green on a LightCycler 480 (Roche, Germany), with each sample run in triplicate. Primer sequences are listed in Table S1, Supplemental Digital Content. GAPDH was used as the reference gene, and relative expression levels were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Nanjing Medical University (Approval No. 2017-SRFA-104) and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki (1975) and its latest revision (2024).

2.10. Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were carried out on the R platform (v4.1.0). The Wilcoxon test was used to assess differences between the 2 groups, while the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test was applied to compare clinical phenotypes across groups. Correlation coefficients among cuproptosis-related lncRNAs, genes, risk score, RS score were computed via Spearman and distance correlation analyses. To determine independent prognostic factors for GC patients, both univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses were applied. Survival differences were compared using Kaplan–Meier curves and the log-rank test. The C-index and the bootstrap method with 1000 resampling were selected to assess the prediction accuracy of the risk score model, and the clinical validity of the model was confirmed through Decision curve analysis (DCA) analysis. Restricted mean survival time curves were generated to estimate the average survival time within a defined follow-up period. All tests were 2-tailed with significance at P ≤ .05.

3. Results

3.1. Identification of cuproptosis-related DE-lncRNAs

The workflow of this study was demonstrated in Figure S1, Supplemental Digital Content. To identify the cuproptosis-related DE-lncRNAs among GC patients, we first analyzed the expression of 10 cuproptosis regulators in the TCGA cohort. As depicted in Figure 1A, 9 of the 10 cuproptosis-related genes exhibited significantly higher expression in GC tissues compared to normal tissues. A total of 10,614 lncRNAs were screened by analyzing the RNA-seq data of GC patients, among which 5774 lncRNAs were identified as DE lncRNAs (shown in Fig. 1B). Additionally, 649 lncRNAs correlated with the 9 cuproptosis-related DEGs were also DE between GC and normal tissues and were defined as cuproptosis-related DE-lncRNAs (listed in Table S2, Supplemental Digital Content).

3.2. Generation of a cuproptosis-related LncRNA risk score model

The prognostic relevance of cuproptosis-related DE-lncRNAs in GC was determined by identifying OS-associated lncRNAs in the TCGA cohort using univariate Cox regression and least absolute shrinkage and selection operator, resulting in 25 cuproptosis-related lncRNAs (Fig. 1C–E). Among which, there were 10 risk factors (AC245041.2, AL138756.1, AC245041.1, AC090204.1, AP005233.2, AP001033.2, TGFB2-AS1, AC124067.2, LINC02532, and LINC01614) and 15 protective molecules (AC011451.1, MIR3142HG, LINC01614, AC092368.3, JPX, AL590666.2, PINK1-AS, AP003392.1, PVT1, TNFRSF10A-AS1, LINC00857, AC093752.2, AL355574.1, AC124319.1, and AC006547.1). All the identified cuproptosis-related lncRNAs showed significant correlation with at least one cuproptosis regulator (Fig. 1F).

GC patients were divided into low- and high-risk groups based on cutoff value, with their risk scores shown in Figure 2A. As presented in Figure 2B, a greater number of deceased patients were observed in the high-risk group. The expression levels of the 25 cuproptosis-related lncRNAs were visualized as a heatmap in Figure 2C. K–M curves illustrated that high-risk patients had significantly poorer OS (Fig. 2D), with similar trends observed for DSS and PFI (Supplemental Fig. S2, Supplemental Digital Content). Univariate and multivariate Cox analyses confirmed that age, stage, and risk score independently predicted OS in GC patients (Table S3, Supplemental Digital Content, Fig. 2E, F).

Multiple validation methods well further applied to verify the prognostic efficiency and reliability of the risk score model. We firstly benchmarked our risk score model against 5 recently published signatures to assess prognostic accuracy in GC.[15-19] As shown in Figure S3A, B, Supplemental Digital Content, our model achieved the highest area under the curves across all time points and the top C-index, confirming its superior discriminative ability and prognostic accuracy. The restricted mean survival time curve further indicated stable long-term predictive ability (Fig. S3C, Supplemental Digital Content). To enhance predictive accuracy, a nomogram integrating the risk score with clinical variables was constructed. Higher total points corresponded to poorer 1-, 3-, and 5-year OS (Fig. 3A). The combined model achieved the highest prognostic accuracy based on the C-index (Fig. 3B) and was further validated by bootstrap resampling (1000 iterations; Fig. 3C). DCA confirmed that the nomogram provided the greatest clinical net benefit, outperforming both the risk-score and clinical models (Fig. 3D).

3.3. Functional characteristics of the risk score model

To illustrate the comprehensive correlations between the risk score and clinicopathological features, a heatmap was generated (Fig. S4A, Supplemental Digital Content). The high-risk group predominantly consisted of male GC patients and was strongly linked to advanced tumor grade and TNM stage (Fig. S4B–D, Supplemental Digital Content). Principal component analysis and t-distributed stochastic neighbor embedding analyses showed no significant expression differences between the 2 groups (Fig. S4E, F, Supplemental Digital Content).

3.4. Tumor immune landscape analyses

We next investigated the relationship between the risk score and antitumor immunity. The waterfall plot revealed a higher mutation frequency in the low-risk group (Fig. 4A, B), which also showed significantly elevated TMB levels (Fig. 4C). A negative correlation was observed between TMB and risk score (R = –0.20, P < .001; Fig. 4D). Consistently, patients with MSI-H status were predominantly classified into the low-risk group (Fig. 4E, F). The high- and low-risk groups exhibited distinct immune infiltration patterns, with the high-risk group showing increased CD8+ T cells, neutrophils, M2 macrophages, and CAFs (Fig. 5A). Immune function scores, including APC co-inhibition, checkpoint, HLA, and parainflammation, were markedly higher in the high-risk group (Fig. 5B). Expression of CD86, TNFSF18, and PDCD1LG2 correlated positively with the risk score, whereas PDCD1 and ICOSLG correlated negatively (Fig. 5C). GO and KEGG enrichment analyses revealed significant enrichment of divalent inorganic cation homeostasis, negative regulation of hydrolase activity, ECM-receptor interaction, and Toll-like receptor signaling, indicating altered copper-ion metabolism, oxidative stress, and mitochondrial dysfunction, thus linking the model closely to cuproptosis-related mechanisms (Fig. 5D, E).

3.5. Therapeutic value analyses

We further assessed the effect of risk score on drug response. The IC50 values of 6 common chemotherapeutic drugs (cisplatin, docetaxel, paclitaxel doxorubicin, metformin and rapamycin) in low- and high-risk groups were predicted. However, no statistical difference was observed (Fig. S5A–F, Supplemental Digital Content). Subsequently, we assessed the immune response between the 2 groups based on the IPS score model. Results showed that the low-risk group had higher IPS scores in both IPS-CTLA-NEG-PD1-NEG and IPS-CTLA-POS-PD1-NEG groups (Fig. S5G–J, Supplemental Digital Content).

3.6. RS score model and functional analysis

Since the cuproptosis-related lncRNAs in the risk score model could not be translated into the mRNA transcriptome data, an alternative scoring system: RS score was established to enable cross-dataset validation. Two gene signatures were defined: signature A (CYTL1, LHFPL2, GPX3, DAB2, VSIG4) with higher expression in the high-risk group, and signature B (GDPGP1, PABPN1, METTL2B, TSSK6, RIBC1, SAC3D1, DFFA, PPAN, TAF8, ALG1L2, COX20, TUBA8, VHL, PRR5) with higher expression in the low-risk group. The RS score was calculated as the enrichment score of signature B minus that of signature A. Significant differences in the enrichment scores of the RS score and gene signatures A and B were observed between the 2 groups (Fig. S6A, Supplemental Digital Content). A remarkably positive correlation was observed between RS score and risk score (Fig. S6B, Supplemental Digital Content, R = 0.64, P < .001), which verified our hypothesis that RS score could serve as an alternative to risk score.

Next, we examined the value of RS score model in predicting patients’ outcomes in TCGA cohort. The distribution of RS scores, survival status and the expression of the 19 genes in the 2 gene signatures were presented in Figure S6C–E, Supplemental Digital Content. The K–M curves indicated that patients in the L-RS group experienced a marked survival benefit (Fig. S6F, Supplemental Digital Content). Same trends were observed in DSS and PFI analyses (Fig. S7, Supplemental Digital Content), suggesting that the newly developed scoring model could effectively predict survival. In addition, a sankey diagram was constructed to visualize patient transitions between risk score groups, RS score groups and their living status (Fig. S6G, Supplemental Digital Content).

3.7. Additional validation of the RS score model

Next, the prognostic performance of the RS score model was validated in the GSE57303, GSE62254, and GSE84437 merged cohort. Prognostic analysis demonstrated that patients in the H-RS group had worse OS than the L-RS group (Fig. S8A, Supplemental Digital Content). In addition, we revealed that younger, female and advanced-stage patients were significantly associated with higher RS score (Fig. S8B–D, Supplemental Digital Content). The predictive efficacy for OS was also proved in an external GSE15459 cohort (Fig. S8E, Supplemental Digital Content). To further explore the immunological behavior of the identified model, we fixed attention on the immunotherapeutic cohort of IMvigor210. The K–M curve revealed that patients in the H-RS group was significantly connected with shorter OS (Fig. S8F, Supplemental Digital Content). Furthermore, patients with lower RS scores exhibited better immune response (Fig. S8G, H, Supplemental Digital Content).

3.8. Experimental validation with GC cohort

To further validate our findings, we performed an independent verification using our own cohort. Among the 25 identified cuproptosis-related lncRNAs, AC245041.1 and LINC01614 exhibited the smallest P-values (Fig. 2C), prompting their selection for subsequent analyses. Patients were stratified into high- and low-expression cohorts based on the cutoff value of AC245041.1 and LINC01614. The results indicated that AC245041.1 expression showed no significant association with clinical parameters, including age, gender, TNM stage, and tumor grade (Table S4, Supplemental Digital Content). In contrast, LINC01614 expression was significantly correlated with advanced TNM stage (Table S5, Supplemental Digital Content). K–M survival analysis for OS demonstrated that elevated expression of AC245041.1 was significantly associated with poorer OS (P = .002, Fig. 6A). Similarly, increased expression levels of LINC01614 correlated significantly with adverse OS outcomes (P < .001, Fig. 6B). Univariate Cox regression analysis further confirmed that AC245041.1 (P = .002), LINC01614 (P = .003), and tumor stage (P = .044) were significant prognostic factors for OS (Fig. 6C). However, no independent prognostic factors for OS were identified (Fig. 6D).

4. Discussion

Cuproptosis is a newly identified form of copper-mediated cell death. A recent study revealed that copper toxicity involves the dissociation of certain mitochondrial metabolic enzymes, thus initiating a brand-new way of cell death.[9] Increasing evidence demonstrated that epigenetic effectors such as lncRNAs took on an indispensable role in tumor progression and drug resistance, leading to their emergence as new biomarkers and therapeutic targets in cancer diagnosis and treatment. Here in our study, we did a comprehensively study of prognostic value and potential molecular mechanism of cuproptosis-related lncRNAs in GC patients.

In this study, we constructed a risk score model containing 25 copper-related lncRNAs, including 10 risk factors and 15 protective molecules. Among which, AP003392.1, AC245041.2 REPIN1-AS1, TGFB2-AS1, AC124067.2, and AL355574.1 have been previous reported as prognostic biomarkers for GC patients through bioinformatic analysis, while their exact role in GC remains unknown.[30-34] Dai et al found that LINC02532 expression was significantly up-regulated in GC patients and elevated level of LINC02532 was associated with higher TNM stage, poorer differentiation grade and shorter OS.[35] In addition, LINC01614 was identified as an oncogenic lncRNA and could promote cell proliferation, migration, invasion, and epithelial-mesenchymal transition in GC.[36] The above studies were consistent with our findings that LINC02532 and LINC01614 act as risk factors for GC. Nonetheless, there exist some controversial points. Previous studies have identified JPX, TNFRSF10A-AS1, PINK1-AS, PVT1, and LINC00857 as oncogenic lncRNAs involved in GC progression and poor prognosis.[37-40] However, our findings classified these lncRNAs as low-risk factors, potentially due to tissue heterogeneity, variations in patient cohorts, or methodological differences. This discrepancy underscores the complexity of lncRNA-mediated regulatory mechanisms in GC and highlights the need for further comprehensive investigations to elucidate their precise biological functions and clinical relevance.

Our prognostic analyses demonstrated that patients in the high-risk group had worse OS, DSS and PFI than those in the low-risk group. The receiver operating characteristic and DCA analyses indicated that the risk model possessed a good predictive performance than other clinical factors. Due to lack of more lncRNA datasets with general clinical information, we further built a RS score model as a well surrogate of cuproptosis-related lncRNA model with mRNA transcriptome data. Just alike the results of risk score clustering, the new RS scoring system could successfully distinguish GC patients with different clinical outcomes, immunological and therapeutic responses.

Predictive biomarkers such as MSI and TMB are crucial for identifying patients who may benefit from ICIs.[41,42] High TMB or MSI-H status has been associated with favorable immunotherapy responses. In our study, the low-risk group exhibited higher TMB, a greater frequency of MSI-H, and elevated IPS scores in the IPS-CTLA-POS–PD1-NEG subgroup. In the IMvigor210 immunotherapy cohort, patients with lower RS scores showed more favorable immune responses; although this cohort is not GC-specific, these findings indirectly indicate that the scoring system may capture immune-related features associated with ICI responsiveness. Moreover, GO and KEGG analyses revealed enrichment of immune-related pathways, suggesting molecular mechanisms linking the model to immune activation. The effectiveness of immunotherapy depends largely on the tumor microenvironment (TME).[43] The high-risk group showed increased infiltration of CD8+ T cells, neutrophils, M2 macrophages, and CAFs. While CD8+ T cells mediate antitumor immunity,[44] tumor-associated neutrophils, M2 macrophages, and CAFs promote tumor growth and immune suppression.[45-47] We previously established a TRS signature based on TAN-related genes that accurately predicted prognosis and treatment response.[28] In the current study, excessive infiltration of pro-tumorcells and higher scores for APC co-inhibition, CCR, and parainflammation in the high-risk group indicate diminished antitumor immunity. These factors may collectively contribute to poorer outcomes and suggest that chemotherapy might be more beneficial than immunotherapy for high-risk patients, providing a rationale for personalized treatment strategies in GC.

Several studies have previously explored lncRNA-based prognostic models in GC, including those by Xu et al,[15] Yang et al,[16] Li et al,[17] Bao et al,[18] Chen et al,[19] He et al[20] and Wang et al.[21] However, their findings differ from ours due to key methodological differences, particularly in the selection of cuproptosis-related genes. Tsvetkov et al identified only ten genes as critical mediators of copper-induced cell death. Compared with previous models established by the above researches, which incorporated 13 to 22 genes, including several not essential for cuproptosis (e.g., DBT, GSCH, DLST), our signature was derived through a stricter selection of cuproptosis-related genes, conferring greater biological specificity and interpretability in GC. Our study strictly adheres to the ten core genes identified by Tsvetkov et al,[9] ensuring a more precise biological foundation for our prognostic model. Moreover, Wang et al constructed a similar prognostic signature for GC patients comprised of 5 specific genes (AC129926.1, AP002954.1, AC023511.1, LINC01537, and TMEM75).[48] However, that study relied exclusively on TCGA data and lacked external validation. In contrast, our study further validated the risk score model using independent GEO cohorts and an in-house GC cohort, significantly enhancing its robustness and clinical applicability. Collectively, these methodological refinements suggest that our model demonstrates superior accuracy and reliability as a prognostic tool for GC patients.

Several limitations should be considered in our study. First, because the cuproptosis-related lncRNAs in the risk score model could not be translated into the mRNA transcriptome data, we built a RS score as a well surrogate to validate the therapeutic and prognostic performance of the risk score model. As a result, the verification was not direct. Second, our study mainly relied on sequencing and microarray data from multiple public databases, which could potentially result in systematic bias. Finally, the precise mechanisms between cuproptosis-related lncRNAs and cuproptosis as well as TME remained unexplored. Hence, future mechanistic researches at the cellular and molecular levels are needed.

In conclusion, our study underscores the clinical and biological significance of cuproptosis-related lncRNAs in GC. The proposed risk-score model outperformed conventional clinicopathological factors in prognostic prediction and distinguished subgroups with distinct tumor behavior, genomic stability, and immune profiles. Low-risk patients exhibited stronger immune activation and potential sensitivity to ICIs, suggesting possible prognostic and immunotherapy relevance. Collectively, this work provides a step forward toward the realization of precision medicine in GC by integrating molecular signatures with clinical management.
