A bilingual evaluation of chatbot performance in bruxism-related information: accuracy and readability across two models
Authors: Merve Cennet Altuntaş, Gülbahar Erdinç Akyol
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundArtificial intelligence (AI) chatbots have increasing applications in healthcare; however, their accuracy and readability across different languages remain unclear. Therefore, this study aimed to compare the performance of ChatGPT-5 and DeepSeek-V3 on bruxism-related questions in English and Turkish.MethodsResponses generated by ChatGPT-5 and DeepSeek-V3 to 20 questions were evaluated in Turkish and English, yielding four chatbot–language conditions. Accuracy was independently scored by two prosthodontists using a Modified Global Quality Score, and inter-rater reliability was assessed using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC). Readability was defined as the ease of reading and was assessed using the Flesch Reading Ease (FRE) scale for English texts and the Ateşman readability formula for Turkish texts. Descriptive statistics were calculated, differences in accuracy across conditions were analyzed using the Friedman test, and the relationship between accuracy and readability was examined using Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient.ResultsAccuracy scores of responses generated by ChatGPT-5 and DeepSeek-V3 were generally high across both Turkish and English. No statistically significant differences in accuracy were observed among the four model–language combinations based on the Friedman test (χ² (3) = 3.204, p=0.361). Inter-rater reliability analysis demonstrated moderate agreement for single measures and good agreement for average measures between the two evaluators (ICC = 0.623 and 0.767, respectively). Comparison of Atesman readability scores and FRE-based English readability analyses revealed no statistically significant differences between the two chatbots, as confirmed by Mann–Whitney U tests (Turkish: p = 0.646; English: p = 0.745).ConclusionChatGPT-5 and DeepSeek-V3 demonstrated comparable accuracy in bruxism-related responses across Turkish and English. However, high readability levels limit patient accessibility, and no association was found between accuracy and readability, indicating that simplification is necessary for effective patient education.

Introduction

Large language models (LLMs) constitute an advanced form of artificial intelligence that simulates human linguistic processing and significantly transforms the way information is accessed in digital environments [1]. Unlike conventional search engines, chatbots obviate the necessity for users to manually search, assess, and navigate through multiple web sources. LLM-powered chatbots offer a conversational interface that interprets natural language queries and delivers synthesized, context-aware responses [2]. LLMs aggregate and synthesize available data into structured textual outputs, thereby enhancing accessibility, optimizing time efficiency, and presenting an ostensibly reliable source of information [3, 4].

Among these systems, ChatGPT, a transformer-based language model, has gained significant attention for its ability to produce human-like text [5]. Since its initial release in November 2022, subsequent versions have demonstrated notable improvements in performance and applicability, with GPT-5 representing one of the most recent iterations [6].

More recently, DeepSeek, introduced in January 2025, has emerged as a competitive alternative in the LLM landscape, offering open-access capabilities and rapidly evolving performance [7]. Despite limited resources, DeepSeek has demonstrated comparable performance to leading models in mathematical and scientific reasoning tasks, while its transparent and collaborative development framework allows for continuous refinement [8, 9].

Given the increasing availability of multiple LLMs with differing architectures, accessibility models, and training approaches, comparing their performance has become essential. ChatGPT and DeepSeek exhibit broadly similar capabilities in tasks such as clinical data interpretation and scientific text generation, yet differ in transparency, accessibility, and potential regional or contextual limitations [10, 11]. These differences may influence the quality, consistency, and reliability of the information provided to users, particularly in healthcare-related contexts.

One such context is bruxism, a condition characterized by repetitive jaw muscle activity, including teeth grinding, clenching, or mandibular thrusting [12]. Due to its relatively high prevalence, individuals increasingly seek information about bruxism through online platforms, including AI-based chatbots. While these tools offer rapid and accessible responses, concerns remain regarding the accuracy and reliability of health-related information, especially in non-curated digital environments where misinformation may also be prevalent [13–17].

Recent studies evaluating LLM-generated responses in the context of bruxism have shown that, although these models can provide generally informative answers, their accuracy, consistency, and clinical reliability may vary depending on the topic and complexity of the question [18, 19]. However, existing research has largely focused on single-model evaluations or limited linguistic contexts, leaving important gaps in comparative and multilingual assessments.

In addition, accumulating evidence suggests that LLM performance may differ across languages, with reduced accuracy and consistency reported in non-English outputs compared to English responses [20]. Given that LLMs are increasingly used by diverse populations, evaluating their cross-linguistic performance is critical for ensuring equitable access to reliable health information [21].

Furthermore, although LLMs are increasingly applied in various dental disciplines, their ability to provide accurate and clinically meaningful responses in specific domains remains uncertain. Importantly, these systems are not specifically designed to deliver medical advice and may generate responses that appear coherent but contain inaccuracies or unsupported claims [8, 22–25] .

Therefore, a structured evaluation of different LLMs across both clinical content and language contexts is necessary. In particular, comparing models with distinct development philosophies—such as ChatGPT and DeepSeek—may provide valuable insights into their relative strengths and limitations in delivering patient-oriented information.

The aim of this study was to evaluate the accuracy and readability of responses generated by ChatGPT-5 and DeepSeek-V3 to bruxism-related questions in English and Turkish. The research hypothesis was that there would be no difference in accuracy and readability between the responses produced by the two models.

Questions included for answer generation by ChatGPT and DeepSeek

The 20 questions were developed based on a literature-informed and expert-driven approach. To ensure comprehensive content coverage, the questions were designed to represent varying levels of complexity, ranging from basic definitions to advanced clinical scenarios [18, 19, 26].

To minimize potential bias related to personalization and prior interactions, a newly created account was used, and each query was initiated in a separate “new chat” session. This approach is consistent with previous studies aiming to reduce the influence of algorithmic memory, personalization effects, and context carryover in large language model outputs [27, 28]. Additionally, browser data, including search history and cookies, were cleared prior to data collection.

“Queries were conducted using the official web interfaces of DeepSeek-V3 (https://chat.deepseek.com/) and ChatGPT-5 (https://chat.openai.com/). To ensure the reproducibility of the study, both models were accessed on January 12, 2026, between 13:00 and 18:00, utilizing the latest stable versions available at the time of access. No restrictions were imposed on the length or content of the responses. All questions were entered using a standardized prompt format without additional instructions or role assignment (e.g., no ‘act as a dental expert’ directive), ensuring that responses reflected the default behavior of each model without prompt engineering.

Each question listed in Table 1 was posed once to each language model under standardized conditions, and responses were recorded as single-instance outputs. The questions were administered in both English and Turkish in the same fixed order without interruption. All responses were compiled into an electronic spreadsheet (Excel version 16; Microsoft Corp., Redmond, WA, USA).“All chatbot responses were evaluated using a blinded assessment procedure. A third independent researcher anonymized the outputs by removing platform-specific identifiers and labeling them as “Chatbot 1” and “Chatbot 2,” thereby preventing reviewers from identifying the source and minimizing potential bias.

Accuracy was defined as the extent to which the information provided was consistent with established scientific knowledge and evidence-based clinical guidelines in bruxism and temporomandibular disorder management [29–31]. Two prosthodontists (G.E.A. and M.C.A.) independently assessed response accuracy using the five-point Modified Global Quality Score (GQS) [32]. The scoring criteria were as follows: score 1, poor quality with missing information and no practical value; score 2, generally poor quality with limited usefulness; score 3, moderate quality with partially adequate information; score 4, good quality with most relevant information; and score 5, excellent quality with comprehensive and highly useful information.

Each reviewer evaluated all responses across the four model–language conditions independently. In cases of disagreement, the evaluations were first reviewed independently and subsequently discussed to reach consensus. If consensus could not be achieved, a third senior prosthodontist was consulted for the final decision.

Inter-rater reliability was assessed using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC), applying a two-way random-effects model with absolute agreement. Considering the ordinal nature of the GQS data and the repeated-measures design, non-parametric statistical methods were used. Following confirmation of acceptable inter-rater agreement, the mean of the two reviewers’ scores was used for further statistical analyses.

Readability in this study was defined as the ease with which a text can be understood by readers. For English responses, readability was assessed using the Flesch Reading Ease (FRE) scale, which assigns a score between 0 and 100. Higher scores indicate easier readability, whereas lower scores reflect increased complexity. The FRE score was calculated using the standard formula: \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$206.835-1.015\times\left(\text{total words}/\text{total sentences}\right)-84.6\times\left(\text{total syllables}/\text{total words}\right)$$\end{document} [33, 34].

For Turkish responses, readability was evaluated using the Ateşman readability formula, which is specifically adapted for Turkish language structure. The Ateşman index was calculated using the following formula: \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$198.825-40.175\times\left(\text{total syllables}/\text{total words}\right)-2.610\times\left(\text{total words}/\text{total sentences}\right)$$\end{document} [35].

To ensure accuracy, the results obtained from the online readability tool were manually verified using the corresponding formulas. Readability scores in both languages were further categorized into educational levels based on established conversion tables.

Descriptive statistics, including mean, standard deviation, minimum, and maximum values, were calculated for accuracy scores across all four model–language conditions. Differences in accuracy were analyzed using the Friedman test. Comparisons of readability scores (FRE and Ateşman) between the two chatbots were performed using the Mann–Whitney U test. The relationship between accuracy and readability was assessed using Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. All analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics for Mac (version 25.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

Results

The intraclass correlation analysis demonstrated moderate inter-rater agreement. The ICC for single measures was 0.623 (95% CI: 0.467–0.741), indicating moderate reliability according to established interpretation guidelines. The ICC for average measures was 0.767, indicating good reliability and supporting the use of mean scores for further analysis.

Descriptive statistics showed that the highest mean (M = 4.30) accuracy scores were observed in the ChatGPT–English and DeepSeek–Turkish conditions, whereas the lowest mean accuracy (M = 4.03) was found in the ChatGPT–Turkish condition. Overall, accuracy scores were high across all conditions, with relatively low variability (Table 2).

The item-level analysis revealed variability in the accuracy scores of individual bruxism-related questions. In Turkish questions, ChatGPT showed lower accuracy scores in items 14, 15, 16, and 17, as well as items 6 and 13. In DeepSeek, lower performance was observed in items 1, 6, 8, and 11. For English questions, ChatGPT demonstrated relatively lower accuracy in items 11 and 16, whereas DeepSeek showed lower performance in items 2, 4, and 10.

The Mann–Whitney U test was used to compare Atesman and FRE readability scores between ChatGPT-5 and DeepSeek-V3. Comparison of Atesman readability scores revealed no statistically significant difference in readability (U = 183.000, Z = -0.460, p = 0.646). Similarly, comparison of FRE readability scores also showed no statistically significant difference between the two chatbots (U = 188.000, Z = -0.325, p = 0.745).

Spearman correlation analysis revealed a very weak positive relationship between Turkish readability scores (Atesman) and Turkish accuracy scores (r = 0.075). However, this association was not statistically significant (p = 0.646), indicating that readability was not related to accuracy in Turkish chatbot responses. Spearman correlation analysis revealed a very weak positive relationship between English readability scores (Flesch Reading Ease) and English accuracy scores (r = 0.107). However, this association was not statistically significant (p = 0.513), indicating that readability was not related to accuracy in English chatbot responses. A moderate, positive, and statistically significant correlation was found between the readability scores of the Turkish and English responses (Spearman’s rho = 0.636, p < 0.001). The Turkish readability scores (Atesman) and the English readability scores (Flesch Reading Ease) increased and decreased in parallel, indicating consistency in readability across languages (Fig. 1). Discussion

The primary findings of this study indicate that both ChatGPT and DeepSeek-V3 provide clinically accurate information regarding bruxism, with no statistically significant differences in performance between English and Turkish languages. However, a critical observation was the ‘very difficult’ readability scores, particularly in English, which underscores a significant gap between technical accuracy and patient accessibility. Furthermore, our bilingual analysis demonstrated that linguistic complexity remains consistent across both languages (rho = 0.636), suggesting that the models maintain their informative structure regardless of the language used.

Item-level analysis revealed variability in the accuracy scores of individual bruxism-related questions, indicating that model performance differed according to question complexity and clinical nature. Lower-performing items were predominantly associated with complex clinical content, including treatment planning, pharmacological management, differential diagnosis, multidisciplinary approaches, and long-term outcomes. In contrast, definition-based and fundamental knowledge questions were consistently answered with higher accuracy by both models. For instance, questions regarding the specific dosage of pharmacological agents for sleep bruxism or the differential diagnosis between awake bruxism and orofacial dystonia yielded lower accuracy scores. This suggests that while LLMs are proficient in providing general health information, they struggle with the nuanced clinical reasoning required for specialized prosthodontic practice. For example, incorrect pharmacological recommendations may lead to inappropriate medication use, while errors in differential diagnosis could result in misidentification of conditions such as orofacial dystonia, which require fundamentally different management approaches.

In particular, questions requiring behavioral and psychological treatment considerations, botulinum toxin applications, and hereditary aspects of bruxism demonstrated greater variability in performance. These findings suggest that LLMs perform more consistently in structured, fact-based queries, whereas their performance may be limited in scenarios requiring advanced clinical reasoning and integrated decision-making. Clinically, this limitation may reduce the reliability of chatbot-generated guidance in complex bruxism management, where individualized and multidisciplinary approaches are essential.

The present study demonstrated a moderate-to-strong positive correlation between Turkish and English readability scores (rho = 0.636, p < 0.001), suggesting that texts identified as complex in one language tend to exhibit similar complexity in the other. Methodologically, this indicates a degree of consistency across languages when evaluating identical content. However, readability formulas are inherently language-dependent and influenced by structural differences such as syntax, syllable structure, and word length. Therefore, readability scores should not be considered directly interchangeable. Clinically, this finding underscores the importance of evaluating patient education materials separately for each language in multilingual populations. One of the key contributions of this study is its bilingual design, demonstrating that both linguistic complexity and accuracy patterns remain consistent across languages, with important implications for multilingual patient populations.

Wen et al., evaluating orthodontic health literacy tasks in Chinese and English, found comparable accuracy and consistency between ChatGPT-4o and DeepSeek, although ChatGPT-4o’s faster response time made it more suitable for time-sensitive clinical applications [36]. Gonzalez et al. reported higher accuracy scores for ChatGPT responses in English compared with Spanish [16]. Similarly, studies comparing English with other languages, such as Chinese, have demonstrated superior performance in English, likely due to standardized terminology and the predominance of English-language training data [37]. However, in the present study, no statistically significant difference was observed between Turkish and English responses for either model. These findings suggest that language did not significantly influence response accuracy in this context. Nevertheless, given that LLMs are predominantly trained on English data, disparities in information quality across languages remain a concern and may contribute to unequal access to accurate health information [37, 38].

The findings of the present study are consistent with recent literature evaluating large language models in temporomandibular disorder (TMD) contexts. For instance, a recent comparative study reported that ChatGPT-4 achieved the highest proportion of objectively accurate responses (78.3%), outperforming other models such as Gemini and Copilot, although inconsistencies remained in specific domains [19]. Similarly, another study evaluating the performance of ChatGPT-4o, Gemini Advanced-2.0, and DeepSeek-V3 in TMD diagnosis and management demonstrated moderate accuracy levels across all models, with ChatGPT-4o showing relatively superior performance, particularly in management-related tasks [39]. These findings align with the present study, which also revealed variability in performance across models. In contrast, studies focusing on patient-oriented outcomes have emphasized that, beyond accuracy, readability and comprehensibility are also critical. For example, chatbot-generated information may be reasonably accurate yet limited in patient readability and clarity [40]. Additionally, review studies have highlighted the potential of ChatGPT in temporomandibular joint disorders while emphasizing the need for careful validation prior to clinical integration [41]. Compared with these studies, the present research contributes to the literature by incorporating a bilingual evaluation design and directly comparing model performance across language conditions, providing additional insight into the potential influence of language on response quality—an aspect that has been relatively underexplored in previous TMD-focused LLM studies.

Health literacy and readability are critical for clinical applicability. Although both models provided generally accurate information, readability levels were very low. In the present study, mean FRE scores for English texts (ChatGPT-5: 29.45; DeepSeek-V3: 29.51) indicated a level requiring advanced education, suggesting limited accessibility for the general population. This highlights a clear gap between technical accuracy and patient comprehension. Clinically, such high linguistic complexity (FRE < 30) may lead to misinterpretation or reduced patient compliance without simplification or professional guidance. For Turkish texts, Atesman scores ranged from easy to difficult, whereas English texts were consistently classified as fairly difficult to very difficult, corresponding to high school to college-level readability.

Jabos et al. reported that responses for third molar extraction patients were highly complex, with mean FRE scores of 52, exceeding recommended levels [33]. Similarly, Guven et al. reported a mean FRE score of 49.25 for ChatGPT-3.5, indicating difficult readability requiring college-level education [42]. In the present study, both ChatGPT and DeepSeek demonstrated comparable accuracy performance, with DeepSeek–Turkish and ChatGPT–English achieving the highest mean accuracy scores. Therefore, no clear superiority was observed between the models in terms of accuracy within the evaluated scenarios.

Inter-rater reliability for single measures indicated moderate agreement, likely due to the subjective nature of Likert-based and global quality assessments, even when conducted by experienced clinicians. This finding is consistent with previous studies reporting moderate inter-rater agreement in evaluating online and AI-generated health information [43, 44] The literature employs various indices to evaluate the correctness of AI chatbot responses; however, no standardized approach has been established. Some studies utilize the DISCERN scale, although when not all questions are suitable for evaluation, its first section is often modified [42, 45, 46]. The Global Quality Scale (GQS), originally developed in 2007 by Bernard et al. to assess medical information in video-based content, has also been adapted for evaluating online and AI-generated health information, including chatbot responses and digital patient education tools [47, 48]. Nevertheless, comparing ChatGPT’s performance across studies remains challenging due to methodological differences and variability across dental specialties [17, 32].

It should be noted that LLMs exhibit inherent stochasticity, leading to variability in outputs even with identical prompts. Previous studies have shown that this intra-model variability can affect both semantic consistency and performance stability, highlighting the importance of repeatability assessment [49]. In the present study, each question was asked only once to reflect real-world usage; however, this may limit the evaluation of response stability.

This study has several limitations. The analysis was limited to two chatbot models and their specific versions, restricting generalizability. Accuracy was assessed using expert-based GQS scoring due to the lack of standardized evaluation tools. Although responses were anonymized, the involvement of the same researchers in both question development and evaluation may have limited blinding. The use of only two evaluators may also affect generalizability. Additionally, only Turkish and English responses to a limited number of bruxism-related questions were analyzed, which may not represent broader clinical contexts. Finally, the analytical approach did not model language and chatbot type as independent variables or assess potential interaction effects.

Conclusion

Both ChatGPT-5 and DeepSeek-V3 provided comparable accuracy in bruxism-related responses, with no significant differences between Turkish and English. However, high readability levels limit patient accessibility, and no association was found between accuracy and readability. These findings highlight a gap between technical correctness and clinical usability, suggesting that chatbot outputs require simplification before being used in patient education, regardless of language.
