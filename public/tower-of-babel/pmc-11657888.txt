Mitochondria-targeted antioxidant mitoquinone mitigates vitrification-induced damage in mouse ovarian tissue by maintaining mitochondrial homeostasis via the p38 MAPK pathway
Authors: Tianqi Du, Han Su, Dan Cao, Qingxia Meng, Ming Zhang, Zhenxing Liu, Hong Li
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveOvarian tissue cryopreservation has become a promising alternative for fertility preservation in cancer patients, allowing ovarian tissue to be stored for future autotransplantation. Oxidative stress damage occurring during the cryopreservation process may impact tissue quality and function. This study aims to investigate the protective effects and potential mechanisms of Mitoquinone (MitoQ), a mitochondria-targeted derivative of the antioxidant ubiquinone, during the vitrification of ovarian tissue in mice.MethodsKGN cells were treated with various concentrations (0.1, 1, 10, and 50 μM) of MitoQ to determine the optimal concentration. Female ICR mice were divided into three groups: control, conventional vitrification, and MitoQ-supplemented vitrification. Ovarian samples were cryopreserved, thawed, and assessed for tissue morphology using Hematoxylin and Eosin (H&E) staining, and mitochondrial changes using immunofluorescence, transmission electron microscopy, and Western blot analysis. RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) was employed to explore potential protective mechanisms. Autotransplantation experiments were conducted, and the long-term effects of MitoQ on ovarian function were evaluated by counting follicle numbers through H&E staining and measuring serum estradiol and AMH levels using ELISA.ResultsMitoQ at 1 μM was found to be the optimal concentration for maintaining follicular morphology after vitrification. It effectively reduced mitochondrial oxidative damage, preserved mitochondrial morphology, and regulated the expression of mitochondrial dynamics proteins (Drp1 and Mfn2). RNA-seq and Western blot analyses revealed that MitoQ inhibited the p38 MAPK pathway, thereby reducing apoptosis. Additionally, autotransplantation experiments showed that MitoQ treatment significantly increased follicle counts, estradiol (E2), and anti-Müllerian hormone (AMH) levels compared to conventional vitrification.ConclusionsMitoQ effectively mitigates vitrification-induced oxidative damage, maintains mitochondrial homeostasis, and preserves both follicular reserve and endocrine function. These findings suggest that MitoQ is a valuable adjunct in ovarian tissue cryopreservation and could significantly improve fertility preservation outcomes for cancer patients.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40001-024-02181-z.Keywords: Mitoquinone, Ovarian tissue cryopreservation, Oxidative stress, Mitochondrial homeostasis, Fertility preservation, p38 MAPK pathway

Introduction

In recent years, cancer incidence has continued to increase annually; however, advancements in diagnostic and therapeutic techniques have reduced overall cancer mortality rates [1, 2]. The number of young and prepubertal cancer survivors is also steadily rising. Nevertheless, radiotherapy and chemotherapy can lead to premature ovarian failure, resulting in loss of reproductive function and infertility [3–5]. Consequently, fertility preservation for cancer patients is becoming an increasingly important field with a growing demand for effective solutions. Ovarian tissue cryopreservation has emerged as the most flexible and effective fertility preservation option for prepubescent girls and cancer patients who cannot undergo ovarian stimulation or delay treatment [6, 7]. The technique has yielded over 200 live babies, demonstrating its promise [8]. Since the American Society of Reproductive Medicine removed the experimental label from this technology in December 2019, it has gained widespread acceptance [9]. Despite these hopeful results, several important stages in ovarian tissue cryopreservation, such as ex vivo ischemia, cryoprotectant toxicity, and osmotic stress, may lead to oxidative stress [10–12]. “Oxidative stress” refers to an imbalance between the production of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and the antioxidant system [13]. This leads to the oxidation of proteins, lipids, and nucleic acids, compromising cryopreservation efficiency [14]. Furthermore, cryopreservation alters mitochondrial membrane potential, disrupts mitochondrial distribution, and results in functional impairments [15, 16]. Ca2+ overload induced by permeating cryoprotectants and increased ROS could cause mPTP prolonged openings triggering further ROS generation, rupture of mitochondrial membranes, Cyt C release and eventually leading to cell death [15]. Mitochondrial homeostasis, which includes mitochondrial biogenesis and dynamics, is essential for cell function and is regulated by mitochondrial fission, fusion, and autophagy [17–19]. Furthermore, mitochondria accumulate oxidative damage quicker than other cellular components, resulting in apoptosis [20].

Redox approaches have been recognized as a potential solution to these problems. Numerous studies have shown the importance of antioxidants in avoiding cryopreservation-induced ovarian tissue damage [21–23]. Melatonin, for example, reduces oxidative stress and apoptosis in cryopreserved ovarian tissue by activating antioxidant enzymes and modulating the Nrf2/HO-1 signaling pathway [12]. Trolox, a vitamin E analogue, increases antioxidant activity and stabilizes cell membranes in cryopreserved ovarian stromal tissues [24]. Natural antioxidants such as anethole and robinin enhance stromal cell and preantral follicle development by lowering ROS levels and maintaining mitochondrial function [25]. However, traditional antioxidants face challenges in crossing the mitochondrial membrane and reaching the mitochondrial interior [26]. As a result, this study emphasizes on Mitoquinone (MitoQ), a mitochondrial-targeted antioxidant, as a cryoprotective additive for ovarian tissue.

MitoQ is a ten-carbon aliphatic chain that covalently bonds a triphenylphosphonium cation (TPP+) to the quinone component of coenzyme Q. This structure allows MitoQ to cross the mitochondrial membrane and concentrate within the mitochondrial matrix owing to the mitochondrial membrane potential. Its reactive oxygen species (ROS) scavenging activity is hundreds of times higher than that of traditional antioxidants [27, 28]. Previous studies have shown that MitoQ significantly mitigates mitochondrial damage and exerts strong antioxidant benefits in a variety of disorders [29–31]. The addition of MitoQ to the preservation solution for donor hearts awaiting transplantation effectively prevents oxidative damage to the graft and reduces ischemia–reperfusion (IR) injury by inhibiting early inflammatory responses [29]. Hou reported that intraperitoneal injection of MitoQ significantly improved lung tissue injury by preventing Nrf2/Drp1-mediated mitochondrial fission, thereby suppressing apoptosis and barrier disruption [31]. Preliminary investigations suggest MitoQ can minimize oxidative damage and improve cell survival and developmental potential in cryopreservation of oocytes and sperm, though the underlying mechanisms are unknown [32–34]. Mitochondria are considered the most sensitive organelles in oocytes [35]. Studies have shown that altered mitochondrial distribution patterns and ultrastructural damage are observed in oocytes from cryopreserved human and mouse ovarian tissues [36, 37]. The addition of MitoQ to vitrification solutions can improve mitochondrial membrane potential and cell survival rates in oocytes. However, there have been no reports on the application of MitoQ in ovarian tissue cryopreservation, and its probable mechanisms are unknown.

The purpose of this study is to validate MitoQ’s protective effects against vitrification-induced damage in ovarian tissue using in vivo and in vitro models. The emphasis is on its ability to maintain mitochondrial homeostasis and prevent oxidative damage. Additionally, RNA-seq analysis is used to explore the potential protective mechanisms of MitoQ.

Methods

Preparation of mitoquinone solution and cell viability assay

A stock solution of Mitoquinone (MitoQ, Cat#HY-100116A, MCE) was made by dissolving MitoQ in DMSO (Cat#D2650, Sigma) to obtain a concentration of 100 mM. For cell treatment, the stock solution was diluted with DMEM (Cat# C11995500CP, Gibco) which contained 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Cat#10270-106, Gibco) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin (Cat#B540733, Sangon). This resulted in working concentrations of MitoQ at 0.1, 1, 10, and 50 μM. The final concentration of DMSO in all solutions was maintained below 0.1%. KGN cells were placed in 96-well plates at a concentration of 5000 cells per well and cultured overnight for cell attachment. The cells were treated with MitoQ (0.1, 1, 10 and 50 μM) for a duration of 48 h. After treatment, 10 μL concentration of CCK-8 solution (Cat# C0038, Beyotime) was added to each well. Thereafter, the plate was kept in a CO2 incubator with humidification at 37 °C for an additional four hours, and the absorbance was subsequently measured via a microplate reader (450 nm). Cell viability is expressed as percentage of control group (untreated cells).

Animals and experimental grouping

With the approval of the Ethics Committee of Nanjing Medical University Affiliated Suzhou Hospital, 10-week-old ICR female mice (SPF grade, weight 30 ± 5 g) were obtained. For the cryopreservation experiment, 48 ICR mice were randomly assigned to one of three groups: the fresh control group (Ctr), in which ovarian tissue was not subjected to cryopreservation or thawing (n = 16); the conventional vitrification group (V), where ovarian tissue underwent standard vitrification and thawing (n = 16); and the MitoQ-supplemented vitrification group (VQ), where ovarian tissue was vitrified and thawed with the addition of 1 μM MitoQ to the cryoprotectant solutions (n = 16). Sixteen ovarian tissues per group were obtained from 16 individual animals, providing independent samples for the study. In the transplantation experiment, a total of 60 ICR mice were randomly divided into four groups for autotransplantation: Sham-T, Ctr-T, V-T, and VQ-T groups, with 15 mice in each group. The specific treatment protocols for each group are detailed in “Ovarian transplantation surgery” section.

Ovarian cryopreservation and thawing procedure

The ovarian tissue vitrification protocol was based on the method described by Kagawa et al., using DMSO, EG, and sucrose as cryoprotectants [38]. Cryopreservation: ovaries were incubated in a series of cryoprotectant, starting with ES solution, (7.5% DMSO + 7.5% EG + RPMI-1640) for 25 min at room temperature and VS solution (20% DMSO + 20% EG + 0.5 M sucrose + RPMI-1640) for 15 min. After that, the ovaries were put on a cryopreservation carrier, moved into cryovials, and kept in liquid nitrogen at − 196 °C. Thawing: 1 week later, ovaries were thawed by sequential incubation in 37 °C TS solution (1 M sucrose + 10% FBS + RPMI-1640) for 1 min, DS solution (0.5 M sucrose + 10% FBS + RPMI-1640) at room temperature for 5 min, and TS solution (10% FBS + RPMI-1640) at room temperature for 5 min, repeated twice. The V group underwent cryopreservation and thawing as described above. The VQ group was treated similarly, but with solutions containing 1 μM MitoQ.

Ovarian transplantation surgery

All mice in the autotransplantation experimental groups received general anesthesia via intraperitoneal injection of tribromoethanol (Cat#M2920, Nanjing AIBI Bio-Tech Co.), followed by aseptic dorsal incisions for surgical intervention. Sham surgery group (Sham-T, n = 15): underwent a dorsal incision and abdominal cavity entry without ovary removal. Fresh control autotransplantation group (Ctr-T, n = 15): ovaries were removed and transferred to the contralateral fallopian tube-ovary unit. Conventional vitrification autotransplantation group (V-T, n = 15): ovaries were excised, vitrified for 1 week, thawed, and autotransplanted as in the Ctr-T group. MitoQ-supplemented vitrification autotransplantation group (VQ-T, n = 15): ovaries were excised, vitrified and thawed in solutions supplemented with 1 μM MitoQ, and then autotransplanted as in the Ctr-T group. Four weeks post-transplantation, ovaries and serum were collected from the mice under general anesthesia administered via intraperitoneal injection of tribromoethanol.

H&E staining and criteria for normal follicle morphology

Ovarian tissues from each group were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (Cat# BL539A, Biosharp), paraffin-embedded, and sectioned at 4 μm thickness for H&E staining (Cat# C0105S, Beyotime). Stained samples were observed and photographed under a microscope (Olympus, Japan) at magnifications of 40×, 200×, and 400×. The morphology of normal follicles is characterized by intact basal membranes, equally dispersed granulosa cells, and intact nuclei with distinct nucleoli. The abnormal follicle morphology is characterized by disorganized granulosa cells, detached or shrunken basal membranes, and cytoplasmic vacuoles. The percentage of follicles with normal morphology was calculated.

Immunofluorescence

The deparaffinized and rehydrated paraffin sections were then treated with citrate buffer for antigen retrieval. After blocking in 5% BSA, the sections were permeabilized with Triton-X100 (Cat# T8787, Sigma) for 40 min. After rinsing, sections were then incubated with primary antibodies at 4 °C for overnight: 8-OHdG (1:100, Cat# sc-66036, Santa Cruz) and AMH (1:100, Cat#:14461-1-AP, Proteintech). Secondary antibodies Alexa Fluor 488 donkey anti-mouse IgG (1:2000, Cat#A32766, Invitrogen) and Alexa Fluor 594 goat anti-rabbit IgG (1:2000, Cat#A11012, Invitrogen) were applied for 1 h at 37 °C in the dark. Then sections were counterstained with DAPI (Cat# C1002, Beyotime) for 10 min, The staining intensity was examined by imaging random fields under a microscope at 200× magnification.

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM)

Ovary tissue samples (1 mm3) were initially fixed in 2.5% glutaraldehyde for 6 h, followed by three rinses in 0.1 M PBS. The samples were then post-fixed with 1% osmium tetroxide for 1 h, dehydrated through a graded acetone series, and embedded in Epon 812 resin. Ultrathin sections (80–100 nm) were cut and stained with uranyl acetate and lead citrate. Mitochondrial morphology was evaluated in randomly selected granulosa cell fields using a transmission electron microscope.

Western blot

Protein (30 μg) from ovarian tissues was separated on 12% SDS-PAGE and transferred onto PVDF membranes. Following washing, membranes were blocked at room temperature for 2 h and then incubated with the following antibodies overnight at 4 °C: Drp1 (1:500, Cat#: sc-271583, Santa Cruz Biotechnology), Mfn2(1:500, Cat#: sc-515647, Santa Cruz Biotechnology), Caspase3 (1:500, Cat#: 66470-2-1g, Proteintech), CytC (1:500, Cat#: sc-13156, Santa Cruz Biotechnology). After TBST washes, membranes were incubated with goat anti-rabbit (1:5000; Cat#A0208, Beyotime) or goat anti-mouse (1:5000; Cat#A0216, Beyotime) immunoglobulins for 1 h. After ECL (Cat# E422-01, Vazyme) detection, bands were imaged with a Bio-Rad imager. β-Actin protein was used as an internal reference, the gray value of the bands was quantified using Image J software. The protein gray value of the Ctr group was normalized and set to 1, with protein levels in the V and VQ groups expressed as multiples of the Ctr group. GAPDH antibody was used as a loading control and the gray value of the bands was quantified using Image J software.

RNA-seq

The Trizol technique was utilized to extract RNA from ovarian tissue samples obtained from each of the three groups. Genedenovo Biotechnology Co., Ltd constructed the sequencing libraries and sequenced on Illumina platforms, whereafter data were normalized to TPM values followed by differential expression analysis using DESeq2. Based on P < 0.05 and |Fold Change| ≥ 1.5, significant differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified. Gene expression patterns were investigated by hierarchical clustering, and the cluster Profiler R package was utilized to perform GO word and KEGG pathway enrichment studies.

Serum hormone measurement by ELISA

Four weeks post-transplantation, serum samples were collected from mice confirmed to be in the estrus stage through vaginal cytology analysis. Serum was separated through centrifugation at 3000 rpm and stored at − 80 °C. ELISA kits were used to measure serum E2 (Cat#ML29034-3, Meilian) and AMH (Cat#YJ037597, Meilian) levels according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were conducted with at least three biological replicates, and data are presented as mean ± standard deviation. Data normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. For normally distributed data, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Dunnett’s post hoc test was used, while non-normally distributed data were analyzed using the Kruskal–Wallis rank-sum test. GO and KEGG enrichment analyses in RNA-seq were performed using the hypergeometric test and Benjamini–Hochberg (BH) correction. Statistical analyses were conducted in SPSS 20.0, and visualizations were generated with GraphPad Prism v.9. A P-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Optimal MitoQ concentration preserves follicular morphology post-vitrification

To identify the optimal concentration of MitoQ that minimizes vitrification-induced damage to follicular morphology, we conducted a CCK-8 experiment in KGN cells. The results showed no significant change in cell viability with 0.1 μM and 1 μM MitoQ compared to the control group (Ctr). However, higher concentrations of 10 μM and 50 μM MitoQ resulted in a significant reduction in cell viability (P < 0.001) (Fig. 1A). Based on these findings, 1 μM was selected as the optimal concentration for subsequent experiments. To further evaluate the effect of this optimal concentration on follicular morphology post-vitrification, H&E staining was performed.

The vitrification group (V) showed a significantly lower percentage of morphologically normal primordial follicles and primary follicles compared to the fresh control group (Ctr) (56.99 ± 32.18% vs. 94.05 ± 10.88%, P < 0.01 and 41.26 ± 35.68% vs. 88.02 ± 15.13%, P < 0.05, respectively). In contrast, the MitoQ-supplemented vitrification group (VQ) had a significantly higher proportion of morphologically normal primordial follicles and primary follicles than the V group (78.42 ± 23.47% vs. 56.99 ± 32.18%, P < 0.01 and 69.34 ± 20.18% vs. 41.26 ± 35.68%, P < 0.05, respectively), although they were still lower than the Ctr group (78.42 ± 23.47% vs. 94.05 ± 10.88%, P < 0.05 and 69.34 ± 20.18% vs. 88.02 ± 15.13%, P < 0.05, respectively) (Fig. 1B, C, D).These results indicate that 1 μM MitoQ effectively preserves morphology of primordial follicles and primary follicles following vitrification, reducing the damage observed with higher MitoQ concentrations or in untreated vitrification controls. While no significant improvement was observed in the proportion of morphologically normal secondary and antral follicles (see Supplementary Table 1). Thus, 1 μM MitoQ was determined to be the optimal concentration for enhancing follicle preservation during vitrification.

MitoQ ameliorates mitochondrial oxidative damage and morphological changes post-vitrification

To investigate the effect of MitoQ on mitochondrial oxidative damage and morphological changes following vitrification, several analyses were conducted. Immunofluorescence staining revealed co-localization of 8-OHdG and AMH was observed in the cytoplasm of ovarian granulosa cells. Since mitochondria are the only organelles with DNA in the cytoplasm, this suggests that oxidative damage primarily affects mitochondrial DNA (Fig. 2A). The V group showed significantly higher 8-OHdG staining compared to the Ctr group, indicating increased oxidative damage. The VQ group exhibited lower 8-OHdG staining than the V group, but still higher than the Ctr group. This suggests that while MitoQ reduces oxidative damage, it does not fully restore it to normal levels. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) analysis revealed that the V group had swollen mitochondria with shortened, fused, and dispersed cristae. In contrast, the mitochondria in the VQ group retained a nearly normal elongated shape with well-organized cristae (Fig. 2B). Western blot analysis further supported these findings, revealing significantly elevated levels of dynamin-related protein 1 (Drp1) in the V group compared to the control (P < 0.01), with a marked reduction in the VQ group (P < 0.01). Conversely, the V group had significantly reduced levels of mitofusin 2 (Mfn2) compared to the control (P < 0.001), with partial recovery in the VQ group (P < 0.01) (Fig. 2C–E).

These results indicate that MitoQ offers protection against mitochondrial oxidative damage induced by vitrification. The reduction in 8-OHdG staining and preservation of mitochondrial morphology in the MitoQ-supplemented group highlight its efficacy in reducing mitochondrial damage and maintaining mitochondrial function post-vitrification.

RNA-seq analysis and validation

To further elucidate the protective mechanisms of MitoQ during ovarian tissue cryopreservation, RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) was performed on samples from three groups, each with three replicates. Principal component analysis (PCA) identified V1 as an outlier and excluded it from the analysis, resulting in 12,156 genes having a TPM > 5 in at least one sample (Ctr: 12,058 genes; V: 12,084 genes; VQ: 12,035 genes). Applying a threshold of |Fold Change| > 1.5 and P < 0.05, the comparison between the V and Ctr groups revealed 643 upregulated and 753 downregulated genes (Fig. 3A). Comparison between VQ and V indicated 696 upregulated and 665 downregulated genes (Fig. 3B). Intersection analysis identified 378 genes that were significantly upregulated in the V group and downregulated in the VQ group (Fig. 3C). KEGG pathway analysis indicated that these genes were predominantly enriched in MAPK, ferroptosis, PI3K, ErbB, and TNF signaling pathways, with MAPK having the lowest P-value and the highest gene count. Notably, Map2k3 was significantly elevated in the V group, as confirmed by RT-qPCR (***P < 0.001) (Supplementary Fig. 1). Western blot analysis of phosphorylated P38 protein (p-P38) showed significant upregulation in the V group compared to the Ctr group (P < 0.05, n = 3), with levels in the VQ group reduced to those comparable with the Ctr group (P < 0.05, n = 3) (Fig. 3F, G). Gene Ontology (GO) functional analysis revealed enrichment in categories related to the negative regulation of cell proliferation, apoptosis processes, oxidative stress response, extracellular matrix organization, and protein kinase binding (Fig. 3E). Western blot analysis of apoptosis markers CytC and cleaved-caspase3 showed significant elevation in the V group compared to controls (P < 0.05, n = 3), with levels significantly reduced in the VQ group (P < 0.05, n = 3), similar to controls (Fig. 3H, I).

These findings demonstrate that MitoQ treatment alters gene expression profiles, particularly in pathways related to oxidative stress and apoptosis. The data underscore MitoQ’s protective role by modulating key genes and pathways involved in mitochondrial stress and apoptosis, with validation through RT-qPCR and Western blot analysis confirming its effects.

Autologous transplantation experiment validated MitoQ’s role in maintaining mitochondrial stability and inhibiting p38 MAPK pathway activation

To assess the impact of MitoQ on mitochondrial stability and p38 MAPK pathway activation following autologous transplantation, we analyzed the ovarian tissues from various groups (Ctr-T, V-T, VQ-T) after transplantation. The results showed a reduction in ovary size across all groups, with the most significant decrease observed in the V-T group (Fig. 4A). Western blot analysis showed significantly higher levels of phosphorylated P38 (p-P38) protein in the V-T group compared to the Ctr-T group, but these levels were significantly reduced in the VQ-T group compared to the V-T group (P < 0.05) (Fig. 4B, C). Analysis of mitochondrial dynamics-related proteins showed that Drp1 levels were significantly elevated in both V-T and VQ-T groups compared to Ctr-T, but these levels were notably lower in the VQ-T group compared to V-T (P < 0.05) (Fig. 4D). Conversely, Mfn2 levels were significantly reduced in the V-T and VQ-T groups compared to Ctr-T, with higher levels observed in the VQ-T group compared to V-T (P < 0.05) (Fig. 4E).

The autologous transplantation experiment validates the role of MitoQ in maintaining mitochondrial stability and reducing p38 MAPK pathway activation. MitoQ treatment led to reduced mitochondrial damage and better preservation of ovarian tissue structure, suggesting its efficacy in protecting against the adverse effects of vitrification and transplantation.

MitoQ supports follicle count maintenance and endocrine function restoration post cryopreservation and transplantation

To evaluate the efficacy of MitoQ in preserving follicle counts and restoring endocrine function following cryopreservation and transplantation, we performed H&E staining and follicle counting on the largest cross-sections of transplanted ovaries. The number of follicles at each stage was counted (see Supplementary Table 2), and statistical analysis was performed on the total number of all viable follicles, including primordial, primary, secondary, and antral follicles. The results indicated a significant reduction in follicle numbers among the Ctr-T, V-T, and VQ-T groups compared to the Sham group (P < 0.001). Although both V-T and VQ-T groups showed lower follicle counts compared to the Ctr-T group, the VQ-T group had a significantly higher number of follicles than the V-T group (P < 0.001) (Fig. 5A, B). Additionally, ELISA analysis of serum samples during the estrus phase revealed that levels of anti-Müllerian hormone (AMH) and estradiol (E2) were significantly lower in all transplanted groups compared to the Sham group (P < 0.001). While E2 levels in the Ctr-T group showed a trend toward lower levels than the Sham group, this difference was not statistically significant (P > 0.05). Both AMH and E2 levels were significantly lower in the V-T and VQ-T groups compared to the Ctr-T group, but were notably higher in the VQ-T group compared to the V-T group (P < 0.001) (Fig. 5C, D).

These findings confirm that MitoQ preserves follicle counts and aids in restoring endocrine function after cryopreservation and transplantation. The increased follicle numbers and higher hormone levels in the MitoQ-treated group compared to untreated controls underscore its protective role in maintaining ovarian function during these processes.

Discussion

This study elucidates the critical role of MitoQ in maintaining mitochondrial homeostasis, reducing oxidative damage, and retaining the normal morphology of follicles after ovarian tissue vitrification. The RNA-seq findings suggest that MitoQ reduces apoptosis through suppressing the p38 MAPK pathway activation. Autologous transplantation experiments further validated that MitoQ maintains mitochondrial stability and minimizes ovarian tissue damage by inhibiting p38 MAPK pathway activation, thereby effectively preserving endocrine function after transplantation.

Our findings are consistent with previous studies that have shown that the freeze–thaw process leads to increased oxidative damage and abnormal follicle morphology [12, 39, 40]. Studies utilizing antioxidants, such as melatonin and N-acetylcysteine, in the cryopreservation of ovarian tissue and oocytes have shown that redox-based approaches play a significant role in mitigating oxidative stress and apoptosis induced by cryopreservation, aligning with our findings on the protective effects of MitoQ [23, 41, 42]. Specifically, melatonin has been shown to reduce oxidative stress and apoptosis via the Nrf2/HO-1 signaling pathway. These results further underscore the value of mitochondria-targeted antioxidants in protecting ovarian tissue.

In this study, the colocalization of 8-OHdG with AMH indicates that oxidative damage primarily affects the mitochondria in granulosa cells. This discovery aligns with prior studies that have demonstrated that vitrification can lead to alterations in mitochondrial membrane potential, aberrant distribution of mitochondria, and impaired mitochondrial function [15, 16]. Hence, it is crucial to apply exogenous antioxidants as a supplement for protecting mitochondria. MitoQ, being a mitochondria-targeted antioxidant, has the ability to efficiently penetrate cells, accumulate within mitochondria, and transform into the active antioxidant ubiquinol, thus contributing to antioxidation [43]. Studies have shown that MitoQ can ameliorate mitochondrial degeneration caused by ROS in oocytes and granulosa cells, thereby preventing apoptosis [44, 45]. Specifically, MitoQ protects germ cells from ROS-induced damage through three primary pathways: a mitochondrial-dependent mechanism, oxeiptosis, and a metabolic shift for cellular protection and regulation [44].

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) observations revealed that MitoQ significantly reversed mitochondrial swelling and cristae disruption. Post-thaw, mitochondrial dysfunction and dysregulation of mitochondrial dynamics-related proteins were associated with apoptosis. These disturbances persisted even after autologous transplantation. The biological activity of cells is closely related to mitochondrial homeostasis, encompassing processes such as metabolism, apoptosis, and necrosis [15]. Maintaining mitochondrial homeostasis is vital for the process of female reproduction. Elevated levels of Drp1 expression leads to excessive mitochondrial fission, which is associated with granulosa cell apoptosis and follicle growth arrest [46]. Conversely, reduced levels of Mfn2 expression impair mitochondrial function and promotes apoptosis of granulosa cells, hence influencing the outcomes of in vitro fertilization (IVF) [47]. Mfn2 plays a crucial role in oocyte quality, meiosis, spindle morphology, chromosome segregation, and mitochondrial function, and is indispensable for blastocyst formation and early embryonic development [48–50]. In this study, both cryopreservation and autologous transplantation were observed to upregulate Drp1 and downregulate Mfn2, whereas MitoQ prevented these changes, reducing mitochondrial fission and promoting fusion, thereby maintaining mitochondrial homeostasis and preserving ovarian function.

Since mitochondrial dysfunction may involve various metabolic pathways and oxidative stress damage [51], the use of antioxidants alone may not be sufficient to fully reverse all types of damage. Thus, exploring combination therapies with other interventions, such as mitochondrial biogenesis stimulators, may further enhance the effects of MitoQ. MitoQ may exert protective effects on mitochondrial structure and function not only through its direct antioxidative action but also by promoting mitochondrial autophagy. It has been reported that MitoQ can promote PINK1/Parkin-mediated mitophagy and restore mitochondrial dysfunction [52, 53]. Therefore, in the context of ovarian tissue cryopreservation, it is essential to evaluate mitochondrial autophagy levels following MitoQ treatment to further elucidate the potential mechanisms by which MitoQ improves mitochondrial function.

RNA-seq analysis revealed that MitoQ could inhibit the activation of the p38 MAPK pathway during ovarian tissue vitrification. This finding aligns with Gu’s RNA-seq findings in human ovarian cortex vitrification, emphasizing the consistency across different species and strengthening the potential significance of the p38 MAPK pathway in the vitrification of ovarian tissue [54]. Reactive oxygen species (ROS), acting as signaling molecules, have the ability to activate the MAPK pathway [55, 56]. However, the accumulation of ROS may be prevented by antioxidants, which in turn inhibits the activation of MAPK [57]. The MAPK pathway is strongly associated with apoptosis triggered by osmotic stress, ischemia, and ROS [58]. GO functional analysis in our study also indicated the activation of the apoptotic process. Phosphorylation of MAPK affects the potential and permeability of the mitochondrial membrane, as well as the opening of the permeability transition pore. This leads to the release of CytC from mitochondria to the cytoplasm, where it binds with Apaf1, triggering a caspase cascade and ultimately resulting in apoptosis [59–61].

A widely used method to assess fertility preservation, aiming to restore ovarian reserve and endocrine function, involves evaluating the quantity and morphology of primordial follicles. Estradiol (E2), the predominant type of estrogen, is a vital endocrine hormone in animals that plays a crucial role in regulating reproductive processes [62, 63]. The initial stage of estrogen synthesis entails the transportation of cholesterol to the mitochondria, the primary site for steroid hormone production [64, 65]. Research indicates that malfunctioning mitochondria in ovarian granulosa cells may result in decreased production of E2 [66]. Anti-Müllerian hormone (AMH) serves as an indicator of ovarian reserve, and its levels are directly related to the number of follicles after autologous transplantation [67]. Our findings demonstrate that the use of MitoQ leads to improvements in estradiol and AMH levels, resulting in a significant increase in follicular reserve and ovarian endocrine function compared with conventional vitrification techniques.

In this study, 1 μM MitoQ was selected as an additive in the vitrification cryoprotectant for mouse ovarian tissue. At this concentration, MitoQ demonstrated protective effects by exerting antioxidant activity and maintaining mitochondrial homeostasis, with no evident toxicity observed. However, due to pharmacokinetic differences between humans and mice, as well as variations in ovarian size, further experimental and clinical studies are required prior to clinical application. Through the use of various in vitro and in vivo models (such as human ovarian cells and different animal models), it is essential to determine a safe and effective dosage range of MitoQ for human use to ensure optimal protective effects without increasing toxicity. Additionally, long-term animal studies, including assessments of oocyte quality and offspring health, are necessary to further verify the reproductive safety of MitoQ. Such data would be instrumental in enhancing the prediction and management of potential side effects.

There were certain limitations to the present research. (1) We did not examine the responses of different ovarian cell types, such as oocytes and stromal cells, to vitrification-induced damage, although these cells are essential for maintaining overall ovarian health. Future studies will utilize spatial transcriptomics analysis to investigate the responses of oocytes and granulosa cells at various follicular stages, as well as stromal cells, to vitrification-induced damage and to explore signaling interactions among these cells. (2) This study did not assess angiogenesis or ischemia–reperfusion injury in transplanted ovarian tissue, which is closely related to the restoration of ovarian function post-transplantation. Future research will focus on evaluating angiogenesis after transplantation by analyzing microvascular density and assessing angiogenic markers such as VEGF and CD31. Additionally, an ischemia–reperfusion injury model will be established in mice by clamping the ovarian artery to further investigate reperfusion injury. (3) Although we observed the protective effects of MitoQ on mitochondrial structure and function, we did not directly measure key antioxidant enzymes (such as SOD, GPx, and CAT) or ROS levels, nor did we capture their dynamic changes during the freeze–thaw process. This limitation restricts our understanding of the extent of oxidative stress induced by cryopreservation. (4) The long-term effects of MitoQ on tissue repair were not addressed. Future studies will prioritize assessing oocyte quality, pregnancy rates, offspring health, and the safety of different MitoQ concentrations for clinical application.

In conclusion, MitoQ exerts a protective effect on ovarian tissue during vitrification by maintaining mitochondrial homeostasis, reducing oxidative damage, and preserving follicle morphology. The results indicate that the addition of MitoQ in ovarian tissue cryopreservation methods could improve the effectiveness of fertility preservation in cancer patients.

Conclusion

This work is the initial investigation into the application of MitoQ as an additive in the vitrification process of ovarian tissue, demonstrating its potent capacity to mitigate oxidative damage. Combining both in vivo and in vitro models, we have shown that MitoQ efficiently regulates the p38 MAPK pathway, maintains the mitochondrial homeostasis, decreases apoptosis, and greatly improves the number of follicles and endocrine function after autotransplantation.

The results emphasize the significant potential of MitoQ as a supplementation in the process of freezing ovarian tissue.

However, this study did not investigate the differential responses of various cell types (such as oocytes, granulosa cells, and stromal cells) to cryodamage signaling pathways or the intercellular communication involved in these processes. Furthermore, aspects such as angiogenesis and reperfusion injury after transplantation were not considered. And these will be the main focus of future research.

In summary, our research provides novel insights into the protective mechanisms of MitoQ in the vitrification of ovarian tissue. These findings provide a theoretical basis for using MitoQ as a new pharmaceutical addition in the cryopreservation of human ovarian tissue. This might potentially improve fertility preservation outcomes in cancer patients.
