Body size, functional form and environmental correlates of maximum longevity in marine‐occurring fishes
Authors: Longyu Li, Zenghui Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMaximum longevity varies widely among marine‐occurring fishes, but the form of its relationship with body size and the contribution of broad environmental gradients remain uncertain. We analysed 757 species, using maximum length as the primary size metric and completed mass only in sensitivity analyses. A quadratic length model improved fit over a linear comparator and captured broad attenuation in the size–longevity association. Maximum depth was positively associated with log10 maximum longevity after accounting for this curvature (estimate per 1000 m = 0.0692, 95% CI 0.0312 to 0.1072, p < 0.001). Latitude estimates varied among range‐based metrics and model specifications, and the latitude term performed less consistently when entire families and orders were withheld. None of the group or continuous‐interaction contrasts met the defined FDR threshold of q < 0.05. FishTree matched 605 species but excluded every elasmobranch in the canonical dataset; branch‐length phylogenetic generalized least squares (PGLS) therefore provides a matched‐subset sensitivity rather than full phylogenetic coverage. Linear contrasts from 5000 species‐level bootstrap resamples and nonlinear quantile sensitivities did not indicate broad upper‐tail steepening. Teleostei‐only, broad‐Class‐adjusted and Family‐clustered analyses retained a positive maximum‐depth coefficient, whereas latitude was less stable under clustered uncertainty. Body size and maximum depth thus provide the most consistent macroecological associations with maximum longevity. Latitude remains exploratory, and the data do not identify causal mechanisms.

INTRODUCTION

Maximum longevity integrates growth, maturation and mortality schedules over an organism's lifespan. Body size is often among its strongest comparative correlates because size covaries with growth, energetic demand, predation exposure and demographic turnover. Yet broad comparisons also show substantial variation in ageing and survival among lineages (Brown et al., 2004; Charnov, 1993; Jones et al., 2014; Stearns, 1992). A comparative size‐longevity analysis must therefore estimate the form of the relationship instead of assuming that one exponent applies across the full size range.

Marine‐occurring fishes provide a stringent test because they span several orders of magnitude in size and include unusually long‐lived taxa such as the Greenland shark Somniosus microcephalus and Pacific rockfishes Sebastes spp. (Kolora et al., 2021; Nielsen et al., 2016). Fish life histories link size, growth and mortality, but body size cannot by itself represent thermal exposure, oxygen availability, ecological mortality or sampling history (Andersen et al., 2016; Charnov & Gillooly, 2004; Gislason et al., 2010; Stark, 2022). Maximum‐age records also depend on ageing method and validation quality, especially among long‐lived species (Campana, 2001).

Maximum depth and latitude summarize broad environmental gradients. Greater depth can coincide with lower temperature, reduced light and different resource or predation regimes, whereas latitude covaries with temperature, seasonality and biogeographic history (Black et al., 2021; Drazen & Haedrich, 2012; Munch & Salinas, 2009; Pauly, 1980; Priede & Froese, 2013; Rubalcaba et al., 2020). These variables do not identify the operative pathway. Extrinsic mortality and ageing may be related in several ways, and species‐level trait databases do not resolve local mortality schedules (Dańko et al., 2018; de Vries et al., 2023). We therefore evaluate depth and latitude as macroecological correlates.

Ordinary least squares (OLS) describes conditional‐mean associations, whereas quantile regression tests whether fitted slopes differ across the observed response distribution (Cade & Noon, 2003; Koenker, 2005; Koenker & Bassett, 1978). Visually prominent scatterplot edges require formal contrasts before they can be interpreted as distributional structure (Scharf et al., 1998). Phylogenetic generalized least squares (PGLS) offers a complementary matched‐subset analysis by modelling residual covariance under a specified tree and branch‐length structure (Felsenstein, 1985; Freckleton et al., 2002; Symonds & Blomberg, 2014).

We asked five questions. Is the size‐longevity relationship adequately represented by a linear model, or does a low‐complexity nonlinear form fit better? Do maximum depth and a range‐based latitude proxy add to the retained size relationship? Do diagnostic group slopes or continuous interactions indicate variation in the centred length coefficient? Are broad associations consistent in the FishTree‐matched subset and across taxonomic sensitivities? Do linear and nonlinear quantile analyses indicate steeper upper‐tail relationships? Length‐based analyses were primary because their provenance was more direct; mass‐based models were sensitivity evidence.

METHODS

Data assembly, filtering and trait provenance

We assembled the canonical dataset from FishBase release 25.04 queries archived through rfishbase (Boettiger et al., 2012; Froese & Pauly, 2026). The FishBase web resource and species‐name query were accessed on 10 June 2026; signed stock endpoints and maximum‐length‐type metadata were queried or refreshed on 13 July 2026. The executable filtering sequence began with 36,132 species records, retained 18,666 with Saltwater = 1, 16,942 with positive maximum length, 930 with positive maximum longevity, 833 with maximum depth and 757 with both latitude endpoints; identifier and recovered‐name checks left the same 757‐species canonical dataset. Thus, 17,466, 1724, 16,012, 97 and 76 species were excluded at the successive substantive filters. The archived species table already contained one row per SpecCode. Repeated stock records were deterministically summarized within SpecCode before the one‐to‐one join; signed northern and southern limits were the maximum and minimum reconstructed endpoints, respectively, and fail‐fast uniqueness checks stopped processing if any SpecCode remained duplicated. Complete‐case filtering was applied to longevity, length, depth and latitude, and these primary fields were not imputed. Harmonized species‐level metadata on ageing methods and validation status were not consistently available, so no species were excluded on this basis; the associated uncertainty is addressed in Discussion 4.5. Longevity was recorded in years, length in centimetres, mass in grams, depth in metres and latitude in degrees; longevity, length and mass were log10‐transformed for modelling. Further details on dataset assembly, variable definitions and provenance are provided in Section S1, Figure S1 and Table S1.

Maximum length was the primary size variable. FishBase identified total length (TL) for 595 species, standard length for 119, fork length for 35 and other or unspecified length types for eight. Length types were not converted because species‐specific conversion factors were unavailable. This heterogeneity may introduce measurement error, so a TL‐only sensitivity analysis was used to assess its influence. The complete length‐type inventory and TL‐only sensitivity are reported in Table S3.

Completed mass was used only in sensitivity analyses. Direct body‐mass records were available for 424 species. For 261 species, mass was reconstructed using W=aLb where W is mass in grams, L is length in centimetres and a and b are arithmetic means of the available FishBase length–weight coefficients within SpecCode. No genus‐level coefficients were substituted. A further 72 completed mass values used the archived fallback W=0.01L3 These 72 entries were classified as unresolved in provenance because their values could not be linked to a direct mass record or species‐level coefficient records. This fallback, together with the strong collinearity between length and mass, precluded interpretation of mass as an independent primary predictor. Completed‐mass provenance is summarized in Table S2.

Environmental predictors, latitude reconstruction, phylogeny and data support

Maximum depth was analysed continuously per 1000 m. Signed latitude endpoints were reconstructed from the NorthSouthN and NorthSouthS hemisphere fields in the FishBase stocks table: the northern limit was the largest signed endpoint and the southern limit the smallest. Absolute range‐midpoint latitude was calculated as the absolute value of the midpoint between the signed northern and southern range limits: northern limit+southern limit2 It was analysed per 10°. Latitudinal range width and maximum absolute endpoint were retained for sensitivities; 301 species ranges crossed the equator. Alternative models used the legacy mean absolute endpoint proxy, the maximum absolute endpoint, the signed midpoint after excluding cross‐equatorial species and midpoint together with range width. The latitude variable is a range‐based proxy rather than an occurrence‐weighted geographical or thermal exposure measure.

Continuous depth and latitude were used for primary environmental inference. Categories described support and summarized diagnostic slopes: epipelagic‐associated (<200 m), mesopelagic‐associated (200–<1000 m) and bathypelagic‐associated (≥1000 m) for depth, and low (<23.5°), intermediate (23.5–66.5°) and high (>66.5°) absolute range‐midpoint latitude. Latitude groups contained 314, 438 and five species, respectively, so the high group was descriptive. Pairwise predictor‐overlap plots, group‐wise length distributions, depth‐by‐latitude cell counts and length‐bin occupancy were examined before grouped comparisons (Zuur et al., 2010). Common‐support subsets excluded the high group, restricted analyses to the intermediate group, retained the observed shared length range among depth groups, or retained the intersection of their 5th–95th percentile length ranges. Predictor overlap and length‐bin occupancy are reported in Section S5 and Table S18.

Scientific names were queried using the fishtree R package against the FishTree ray‐finned fish phylogenetic resource; 605 of 757 species matched and 152 unmatched species were excluded from phylogenetic models without grafting (Chang et al., 2019; Rabosky et al., 2018). The returned 605‐tip chronogram required only a numerical terminal‐branch correction within 7 × 10−6 branch‐length units to satisfy ultrametric tolerance; topology was unchanged, and the corrected tree was used for PGLS. Full diagnostics are provided in Table S5. The FishTree‐matched subset was dominated by ray‐finned fishes and excluded all elasmobranch species in the canonical dataset: it contained 595 Teleostei and 10 Chondrostei, whereas all 75 Elasmobranchii, three Petromyzonti and one Coelacanthi were unmatched. Only three of the five high range‐midpoint species matched. FishTree matching, taxonomic coverage and chronogram diagnostics are detailed in Section S2 and Table S4.

Primary models and functional‐form assessment

Let y denote log10 maximum longevity, x log10 maximum length, xc = x − mean(x), xc 2 the squared centred length term, depth1000 = maximum depth/1000 and latitude10 = absolute range‐midpoint latitude/10. We compared y ~ xc with y ~ xc + xc 2 and fitted y ~ s(xc, k = 4) as a low‐complexity generalized additive model (GAM; Wood, 2017). An ML‐fitted GAM provided an Akaike information criterion (AIC) sensitivity on the same observations and Gaussian likelihood, whereas a REML‐fitted GAM was used only as a flexible shape sensitivity and was not ranked against the linear models by AIC. In the final reanalysis, functional form was evaluated using a model‐evaluation framework that considered AIC improvement, the nested‐model test, reduction in systematic residual trend and stability of the curvature term within central common support. The quadratic was retained because its AIC was at least 10 units lower than that of the linear model, the nested F test had p < 0.01, the RMS of the fitted residual trend fell by at least 50% and the quadratic coefficient retained its direction in central common support. The xc coefficient is the local slope near mean log10 length. The quadratic is a parsimonious approximation to broad attenuation, not an exact second‐order biological law.

The primary environmental OLS model was y ~ x_c + x_c2 + depth_1000 + latitude_10. Depth‐only, alternative‐latitude, TL‐only, mass and taxonomic‐composition models were supplementary sensitivities. Group models retained common curvature and added x_c‐by‐group interactions. Continuous‐interaction models added x_c‐by‐depth and x_c‐by‐latitude terms using raw depth and log10(maximum depth + 1 m) in parallel. Common‐support subsets were refitted with the primary additive model. Benjamini‐Hochberg FDR adjustment was applied within the defined depth‐contrast, latitude‐contrast, continuous‐interaction and quantile‐contrast families (Benjamini & Hochberg, 1995).

For linear, quadratic and GAM fits, residual RMSE and the RMS of a loess‐smoothed residual trend were computed separately. The same residual‐pattern function used fitted values, model residuals, span 0.75 and degree 2 for every form; only the RMS of the fitted residual trend informed the curvature diagnostic. Residual‐versus‐fitted plots, Q–Q plots, Cook's distances and a Breusch–Pagan‐style auxiliary regression of squared residuals on fitted values were also examined. Observations with Cook's distance greater than 4/n were flagged for influence assessment, but this diagnostic threshold did not trigger automatic exclusion; all primary analyses retained the complete canonical dataset unless otherwise stated. Predictor overlap and collinearity were assessed before interpretation (Dormann et al., 2013). The fitted quadratic derivative reached zero near 487 cm, but only 15 species occurred above that point and it lay outside the central common‐support window. This location was not treated as a biological threshold, the descending extreme tail was not extrapolated beyond the observed range and its shape was considered poorly resolved. Collinearity diagnostics are provided in Table S17 and Figure S4.

Phylogenetic, quantile and validation analyses

PGLS models used the same centred quadratic form and environmental display scales as OLS, with residual covariance derived from the numerically corrected FishTree chronogram using ape and caper (Orme et al., 2025; Paradis & Schliep, 2019). Pagel's λ was estimated by maximum likelihood for each primary model. Grouped PGLS models fixed λ at the primary environmental estimate for their contrasts; a supplementary analysis estimated λ independently for each grouped model. PGLS is therefore a matched‐subset phylogenetic‐covariance sensitivity, not a correction representing all 757 canonical species.

Linear quantile regressions were fitted separately for length and mass at τ = 0.10, 0.25, 0.50, 0.75, 0.90 and 0.95 using quantreg::rq. Species‐level bootstrap contrasts used B = 5000 resamples with replacement and seed 20,260,610; OLS and quantile slopes were refitted in every resample. Percentile 95% intervals were calculated for β 0.90 − β 0.50, β₀.₉₅ − β 0.50, β 0.90 − β OLS and β₀.₉₅ − β OLS. Two‐sided bootstrap p‐values were calculated as p=2×minPrΔ≤0PrΔ≥0 where Δ denotes the resampled contrast; values were capped at 1. Quadratic quantile models at τ = 0.50, 0.90 and 0.95 used B = 1000 species bootstraps and seed 20,260,713; predicted curves and local slope differences at the 25th, 50th and 75th percentiles of log10 length were summarized with percentile intervals. These non‐phylogenetic analyses describe distributional variation in the canonical dataset.

Internal five‐fold diagnostics compared a nested sequence of quadratic length alone, quadratic length plus maximum depth and quadratic length plus maximum depth and the latitude proxy. For species‐random folds, unique SpecCode values were sorted in ascending order, permuted after set. seed(20260713) and allocated cyclically to five folds of 151 or 152 species. The archived map records scientific name, random order, fold, seed and assignment rule, and no species crossed folds. Family‐ and order‐blocked diagnostics kept each taxonomic group in one fold. Groups were ordered by decreasing species count; ties were randomized with seeds 20,260,713 and 20,260,813, respectively, and a greedy algorithm assigned each intact group to the currently smallest fold. No taxonomic label was missing. Whole‐order sizes produced necessarily unequal folds of 106–224 species. RMSE, MAE and predictive R2 are internal transportability diagnostics, not external measures of predictive accuracy.

The pooled held‐out coefficient of determination was calculated as follows: Rpred2=1−∑iyi−y^i2∑iyi−ȳ2 Here, y i is the held‐out observed response, ŷ i is its held‐out prediction and ȳ is the mean of all pooled held‐out observations. Main‐text predictive R 2 values were calculated from the pooled held‐out predictions; fold‐level values and their standard deviations are reported in the Supporting Information. A negative predictive R 2 indicates performance below the pooled mean‐only comparator.

Taxonomic‐composition sensitivities refitted the final OLS model in Teleostei, added broad Class fixed effects to the canonical model, and estimated Family‐clustered CR2 standard errors with Satterthwaite degrees of freedom using clubSandwich. A 75‐species elasmobranch‐only model and predictor ranges were reported as supplementary support checks. Additional sensitivities compared the two classes represented in FishTree and the 605 matched species. These models do not replace the canonical OLS analysis or broaden the PGLS sample.

Software, reproducibility and reporting

Analyses were run in R 4.6.0 (R Core Team, 2026). Direct package versions included quantreg 6.1, ape 5.8‐1, caper 1.0.4, fishtree 0.3.4, rfishbase 5.0.3, mgcv 1.9‐4, clubSandwich 0.7.0, sandwich 3.1‐2 and ggplot2 4.0.3. Fixed seeds were 20,260,610 for linear quantile bootstraps, 20,260,713 for nonlinear quantile bootstraps and species/family fold ties and 20,260,813 for order‐fold ties. The reproducibility package contains canonical data, cached source queries, returned and corrected chronograms, scripts, fold maps, model outputs, figures, tables and session information.

Check mode validates required files and independently specified expected dimensions; missing sources, invalid statuses or mismatched values terminate execution. A negative control deliberately perturbs the expected canonical row count and must return FAIL. Full rebuild mode reruns the analysis and figure pipeline, performs dimension checks before and after analysis and records stdout, stderr and session information. Figures and tables are generated from machine‐readable outputs. Data and code are available at https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.21769695. Estimates and confidence intervals are displayed to four decimals in main tables, p‐values to three decimals or <0.001 and FDR q‐values to three decimals.

RESULTS

Dataset coverage and analysis sets

The canonical dataset contained 757 complete‐case species, of which 605 matched FishTree and 152 remained unmatched. Total length accounted for 595 maximum‐length records. Mass provenance comprised 424 raw values, 261 length‐weight derivations and 72 values with unresolved provenance. Depth groups contained 373, 273 and 111 species, whereas latitude groups contained 314 low, 438 intermediate and five high range‐midpoint species. FishTree coverage was taxonomically uneven. The matched subset contained 595 Teleostei and 10 Chondrostei, but none of the 75 Elasmobranchii, three Petromyzonti, or one Coelacanthi. These coverage differences bound the interpretation of PGLS and grouped comparisons.

Nonlinear body size‐longevity association

The size–longevity relationship was positive but not adequately summarized by one linear coefficient. The quadratic model had AIC = 497.486, a decrease of 43.345 units relative to the linear model (AIC = 540.831); adjusted R 2 increased from 0.317 to 0.356, and the RMS of the fitted residual trend decreased from 0.0971 to 0.0378. Its centred linear coefficient was 0.5812 (95% CI 0.5223 to 0.6402, p < 0.001) and the quadratic term was −0.3233 (95% CI −0.4163 to −0.2303, p < 0.001). The central common‐support estimate of the quadratic term remained negative (−0.9581, 95% CI −1.3016 to −0.6146, p < 0.001).

As a flexible shape sensitivity, the ML‐fitted GAM had AIC = 492.601 and an RMS of the fitted residual trend of 0.0375, while the REML fit described a similar broad attenuation. Neither fit was treated as a separate biological model. The quadratic derivative reached zero near 487 cm, beyond the central common‐support upper limit; 15 species lay above that location and five exceeded the Cook's distance diagnostic threshold. The quadratic therefore summarizes broad attenuation, whereas the descending extreme tail is poorly resolved and is not interpreted as evidence that longevity declines among the largest fishes (Figure 2; Table S6). Complete functional‐form and selected OLS outputs are provided in Section S3 and Table S7.

Environmental associations after accounting for curvature

Maximum depth showed the most consistent environmental association beyond size. In the primary OLS model, the estimate per 1000 m was 0.0692 (95% CI 0.0312 to 0.1072, p < 0.001), with adjusted R 2 = 0.379. On the original longevity scale, this estimate corresponds to approximately 17% higher expected maximum longevity per additional 1000 m of maximum depth, holding body size and the latitude proxy constant. The positive direction was retained in the central common‐support subset (0.1007, 95% CI 0.0575 to 0.1439, p < 0.001) and the intermediate‐latitude subset (0.0696, 95% CI 0.0265 to 0.1127, p = 0.002).

The absolute signed range‐midpoint latitude estimate was 0.0200 per 10° (95% CI 0.0078 to 0.0323, p = 0.001), but alternative range summaries were less consistent. The legacy mean absolute endpoint and maximum absolute endpoint intervals included zero, as did the stricter central common‐support estimate (0.0101, 95% CI −0.0034 to 0.0237, p = 0.143). Latitude was therefore retained as an exploratory association based on a range‐midpoint proxy, not as an environmental association comparable in stability to maximum depth (Table 2; Tables S20 and S21). Complete quadratic environmental‐model outputs are reported in Table S10.

Diagnostic group interactions and taxonomic sensitivity

None of the common‐curvature group contrasts met the defined FDR threshold of q < 0.05. OLS depth contrasts had q‐values of 0.335–0.498, and the low‐minus‐intermediate latitude contrast was −0.1134 (95% CI −0.2369 to 0.0101, p = 0.072, q = 0.216). The high group contained five species and its contrasts were neither estimated nor interpreted. Continuous interactions followed the same hierarchy: two terms had unadjusted p‐values below 0.05 under log‐depth scaling, but none met the defined FDR threshold; all q‐values were at least 0.095 (Figures 3 and 4; Table 3). Complete OLS group‐slope contrasts are reported in Section S4 and Table S11. Continuous‐interaction sensitivities are reported in Table S19.

DISCUSSION

Body size and functional form

Body size remained the most consistent correlate of observed maximum longevity, in line with broad life‐history and metabolic expectations. Larger fishes often grow more slowly, mature later and experience lower mass‐specific metabolic rates, while increased size can reduce exposure to some sources of predation and demographic turnover (Brown et al., 2004; Charnov, 1993; Charnov & Gillooly, 2004; Stearns, 1992). These links make body size a useful comparative baseline, but no single allometric exponent is likely to describe every lineage or every part of the size range. Ageing trajectories vary widely across vertebrates, and fishes show substantial departures from simple size‐based expectations even among broadly comparable taxa (Jones et al., 2014; Stark, 2022).

The positive size–longevity association was not linear across the observed size range. The quadratic model indicated broad curvature: longevity increased strongly through much of the observed size range, but the incremental association weakened among larger species. The flexible GAM sensitivity showed a similar broad shape but did not resolve the extreme tail. The traits accompanying greater size—growth rate, maturation schedule, reproductive allocation, and mortality exposure—do not change in fixed proportion across all fishes. A curved relationship is biologically plausible and is consistent with the reduction in systematic residual structure relative to the linear model. The quadratic provides a compact approximation to broad attenuation; it does not establish a universal polynomial law or a biological downturn among the largest fishes.

That uncertainty is especially relevant for the largest and longest‐lived fishes. Only 15 species occurred beyond the fitted zero‐derivative location; several were influential observations, and this part of the curve lay outside the central common‐support window. The location is not interpreted as a biological threshold, and the descending tail is not evidence that maximum longevity declines among the largest fishes. Exceptional cases such as the Greenland shark Somniosus microcephalus and long‐lived Pacific rockfishes Sebastes spp. show how lineage‐specific growth, maturation and ageing strategies can generate longevity far beyond a simple size prediction (Kolora et al., 2021; Nielsen et al., 2016). The data support broad attenuation at large body sizes more strongly than any exact turning point or decline.

Mass‐based sensitivities produced the same general positive size association, which increases confidence that the result is not unique to the selected length measure. Their inferential value remains secondary because completed mass was partly reconstructed and strongly collinear with length. The analysis therefore does not support a single scaling exponent across all fishes. Body size remains a central comparative axis, but its association with maximum longevity changes across the observed size range, while residual variation may reflect lineage‐specific energetic trade‐offs, ecological conditions, extrinsic mortality and differences in sampling effort (Stark, 2022; Stark et al., 2018, 2020). Parallel mass‐based group sensitivities are shown in Figure S3.

Maximum depth beyond body size

Maximum depth was the environmental variable that showed the greatest consistency across the analytical framework. Its positive coefficient was retained in the full model, central common‐support subsets, Teleostei‐only and broad‐Class‐adjusted models and under Family‐clustered uncertainty. The FishTree‐matched PGLS model produced the same direction, while a supplementary analysis of elasmobranchs indicated that the pattern was not restricted to ray‐finned fishes. The positive depth association was robust to these alternative specifications and taxonomic sensitivity analyses. This consistency does not identify a causal process or rule out residual confounding.

The result is broadly consistent with the slow life histories documented in many deep‐living fishes. Deep‐sea taxa frequently combine slow growth, delayed maturation, low natural mortality and extended lifespan, although these traits vary continuously rather than forming a single deep‐sea syndrome (Drazen & Haedrich, 2012; Priede & Froese, 2013). Cooler environments may reduce metabolic turnover and alter growth–mortality relationships, while low resource availability can favour conservative energy allocation and prolonged development (Black et al., 2021; Gislason et al., 2010; Pauly, 1980). Oxygen availability may further modify the temperature and body‐size dependence of metabolism in aquatic ectotherms (Rubalcaba et al., 2020). These processes are not mutually exclusive, and their relative importance probably differs among lineages and habitats.

Variation in extrinsic mortality offers an additional route linking depth to longevity. Predation regimes, physical disturbance and fishing exposure can all change with depth. Life‐history theory predicts that sustained differences in external mortality may shape growth, maturation, and investment in somatic maintenance, although the direction of this relationship depends on ecological context and cannot be inferred from a single range variable (Dańko et al., 2018; de Vries et al., 2023). Some deep‐associated species may experience lower exploitation or predation, whereas others remain exposed during shallower life stages or through deep‐water fisheries. Maximum depth alone cannot distinguish these histories.

Depth entered the models mainly as an additive term. Once the common curvature of the size–longevity relationship was retained, neither grouped nor continuous interaction models showed a stable depth‐related change in the centred length coefficient. Species reaching greater depths therefore tended to have higher expected longevity across the observed size range, with little evidence that depth systematically altered the strength of the size association itself. This distinction revises the stronger environmental‐modulation interpretation considered earlier in the study. Maximum depth appears to summarize a broad ecological and life‐history context that adds information beyond body size, while the underlying pathways remain unresolved.

Why group‐specific slopes are weak

No stable group‐specific scaling differences were detected. Separate fitted lines can appear to diverge when environmental groups occupy different parts of a curved relationship. In the present dataset, larger species were not distributed evenly among depth and latitude categories, and the high range‐midpoint group contained only five species. A linear fit within each category would combine genuine between‐group differences with the local slope of the shared nonlinear relationship. Retaining a common quadratic term removed much of this ambiguity. The remaining differences in the centred length coefficient were small relative to their uncertainty, and none of the contrasts met the defined FDR threshold of q < 0.05.

The grouped analysis also shows what is lost when continuous gradients are divided into broad classes. Categories are useful for displaying coverage and identifying poorly represented combinations, but their boundaries are analytical conventions. Species close to a depth or latitude threshold may be ecologically similar while being assigned to different groups. At the same time, sparse cells can exert disproportionate influence on an estimated line. The overlap plots, occupancy counts and common‐support analyses were therefore central to interpretation, not simply supplementary diagnostics. They showed that all major depth groups contained species across broad length intervals, but depth and latitude were not fully crossed and the extreme latitude category lacked enough observations for formal inference. Restricting the analysis to supported regions retained the additive depth association but did not reveal consistent slope differences.

The quantile results point in the same direction. Upper edges in ecological scatterplots can look biologically meaningful even when they reflect unequal sample density, measurement error or the changing variance of the response (Cade & Noon, 2003; Scharf et al., 1998). Here, the estimated slopes at τ = 0.90 and τ = 0.95 were not steeper than the median or OLS slopes, and the nonlinear local‐slope contrast intervals also included zero. The data consequently do not support a distinct upper boundary that becomes more size‐dependent under particular environmental conditions.

Together, the interaction and quantile results support a narrower interpretation in which environmental context is associated with differences in expected longevity around a shared, curved size relationship. The grouped fits remain useful descriptions of the observed sample, but they should not be treated as separate ecological regimes. More decisive tests of slope variation will require denser sampling across the joint size–depth–latitude space, especially among very large species and at the highest range‐midpoint latitudes.

Latitude and phylogenetic uncertainty

Latitude produced a less coherent pattern than maximum depth. The absolute signed range midpoint was positively associated with longevity in the primary OLS model and in the FishTree‐matched PGLS analysis, but that result depended on how geographic range was summarized. Coefficients based on the legacy endpoint mean and maximum absolute endpoint were uncertain; the strict common‐support interval included zero, and the latitude term performed less consistently when entire families or orders were withheld. Its interval also included zero under Family‐clustered uncertainty. These differences indicate that the significant primary coefficient captures information specific to one range‐based representation; it does not provide a stable estimate of a general latitude effect.

A species' range midpoint is only a coarse approximation to environmental exposure. Nearly 40% of the species in the dataset crossed the equator, and two species with similar midpoint values can occupy very different combinations of hemispheres, seasonal regimes and latitudinal range widths. Ocean currents, regional productivity and the vertical thermal structure of the water column can also decouple latitude from temperature. Earlier evidence for latitudinal variation in lifespan has often concerned within‐species populations or more direct geographical comparisons (Munch & Salinas, 2009). The present analysis instead compares range limits across distantly related species. The two settings address different biological questions, and the current proxy cannot distinguish thermal exposure from biogeographic history or range geometry.

Phylogenetic modelling does not remove this measurement limitation. The positive PGLS coefficient describes covariance within the 605‐species FishTree subset, which was dominated by Teleostei and excluded every elasmobranch in the canonical dataset. Similar grouped contrasts under fixed and independently estimated Pagel's λ show that those particular results were not sensitive to one λ setting. They do not compensate for missing lineages or an imperfect environmental proxy. Comparative methods can control covariance among represented species, but their inferential reach remains tied to tree coverage, trait measurement and the composition of the matched sample (Felsenstein, 1985; Freckleton et al., 2002; Symonds & Blomberg, 2014). Latitude should therefore remain an exploratory result. A stronger test will require occurrence‐weighted geographical and thermal exposure, broader phylogenetic coverage and explicit separation of within‐lineage from among‐lineage variation.

Limitations and future work

Some uncertainty enters before any model is fitted. Maximum longevity is a record statistic whose value increases with sampling effort and depends on ageing method, validation quality and the availability of exceptionally old individuals. These issues are especially consequential for long‐lived fishes, in which small ageing biases can translate into large errors in estimated lifespan (Campana, 2001). Maximum length is also heterogeneous because the database combines total, standard and fork length, while completed mass includes reconstructed values. The TL‐only and mass sensitivities help to assess these choices, but they cannot remove measurement error from the original records.

Selection and ascertainment may introduce a second layer of uncertainty. The complete‐case dataset necessarily favours species for which longevity, length, depth and latitude have all been documented. Well‐studied, large‐bodied, commercially important or unusually long‐lived taxa may therefore be overrepresented. Maximum longevity, maximum length and maximum recorded depth are all extreme values, and each can increase as research effort accumulates. Shared ascertainment could strengthen their observed associations even when the underlying biological relationships are weaker. The current data contain no consistent species‐level measure of study effort with which to model this process directly.

The environmental variables create a third limitation. Neither variable resolves ontogenetic habitat shifts, seasonal movement, oxygen exposure, productivity, fishing mortality, or local predation. Future work should link validated population‐level ages to occurrence‐weighted depth and temperature histories, exploitation records and local mortality schedules. Hierarchical models could then separate variation among populations, species, and lineages, while measurement‐error models could propagate uncertainty in age, body size and environmental exposure. Such data would move the analysis from broad macroecological pattern towards direct tests of demographic and physiological hypotheses.

CONCLUSION

Maximum longevity in marine‐occurring fishes was positively but nonlinearly related to body size, with a parsimonious quadratic model capturing broad attenuation across the observed range. Maximum depth showed the most consistent positive environmental association beyond size. Latitude varied among range‐based metrics, common‐support restrictions, clustered uncertainty and taxonomically blocked diagnostics, so it remains exploratory. Group‐specific slope contrasts and continuous interactions did not indicate robust environmental variation in the centred length coefficient after FDR adjustment, and quantile analyses did not indicate broad upper‐tail steepening. These results describe bounded macroecological associations, not tested causal mechanisms.
