Inverse nonlinear fast Fourier transform on SU(2) with applications to quantum signal processing
Hongkang Ni, Rahul Sarkar, Lexing Ying, Lin Lin

Abstract
. The nonlinear Fourier transform (NLFT) extends the classical Fourier transform by replacing addition with matrix multiplication. While the NLFT on SU ⁡ ( 1 , 1 ) \mathrm{SU}(1,1) has been widely studied, its SU ⁡ ( 2 ) \mathrm{SU}(2) variant has only recently attracted attention due to emerging applications in quantum signal processing (QSP) and quantum singular value transformation (QSVT). In this paper, we investigate the inverse NLFT on SU ⁡ ( 2 ) \mathrm{SU}(2) and establish the numerical stability of the layer stripping algorithm for the first time under suitable conditions. Furthermore, we develop a fast and numerically stable algorithm, called inverse nonlinear fast Fourier transform, for performing inverse NLFT with near-linear complexity. This algorithm is applicable to computing phase factors for both QSP and the generalized QSP (GQSP).

1.
Introduction

The Fourier transform is a fundamental tool in mathematics and is used ubiquitously in scientific and engineering computations. In comparison, the nonlinear Fourier transform (NLFT) is far less well-known despite having a long and rich history. Its origin can be traced back to Schur’s 1917 study of the properties of bounded holomorphic functions on the unit disk, now known as Schur functions
[
Sch18
]
. Over the following century, NLFT has been rediscovered in seemingly unrelated contexts under different names, including scattering theory
[
BC85
,
WS98
,
DK04
,
Hit01
]
, integrable systems
[
Tan72
,
FG94
]
, orthogonal polynomials
[
Sze39
,
Cas75
,
Den02
,
Dei00
]
, Jacobi matrices
[
Sim04
,
KS03
,
DK04
,
VY02
]
, logarithmic integrals
[
Koo98
]
, and stationary Gaussian processes
[
DM08
]
, to name a few. Briefly speaking, NLFT replaces the addition operation in the linear Fourier transform with matrix multiplication. It maps a complex sequence
\boldsymbol{\gamma}=(\gamma_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}
to a one-parameter family of matrices
\overbrace{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}(z)
, where
z
is on the unit circle
\mathbb{T}
. Moreover,
\overbrace{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}(z)
can be expressed as a product of
z
-dependent matrices, where each matrix is in a Lie group and is parameterized by an entry
\gamma_{k}
. In the case of Schur functions and the aforementioned applications, this Lie group is

\mathrm{SU}(1,1):=\Set{\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
\overline{b}&\overline{a}\end{pmatrix}:\left|a\right|^{2}-\left|b\right|^{2}=1,\quad a,b\in\mathbb{C}},

where
\overline{a}
denotes the complex conjugation of
a
. The transformation from
\boldsymbol{\gamma}
to
\overbrace{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}
is called the forward NLFT, and the mapping from
\overbrace{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}
back to
\boldsymbol{\gamma}
is called the inverse NLFT. We refer readers to
[
TT12
]
for further background.

Compared to the
\mathrm{SU}(1,1)
case, the NLFT on the Lie group

\mathrm{SU}(2):=\Set{\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
-\overline{b}&\overline{a}\end{pmatrix}:\left|a\right|^{2}+\left|b\right|^{2}=1,\quad a,b\in\mathbb{C}}

has been studied much later. This was first systematically explored in the thesis of Tsai
[
Tsa05
]
, which derives analytic results in the
\mathrm{SU}(2)
setting that parallel those of
\mathrm{SU}(1,1)
. However, there are important differences between these two cases, particularly in terms of the domain, range, and injectivity of the NLFT map (for example, compare
[
Tsa05
, Theorem 2.3]
and
[
TT12
, Theorem 1]
for the relevant results for compactly supported sequences). The NLFT on
\mathrm{SU}(2)
has applications in the study of solitons from certain nonlinear Schrödinger equations (see e.g.,
[
FRT07
]
,
[
Tsa05
, Chapter 5]
), but its most significant application has emerged only in the past few years from the quantum computing community, independent of the existing NLFT literature. This development is known as quantum signal processing (QSP), first introduced by Low and Chuang
[
LC17
]
. QSP is defined in terms of a product of parameterized matrices in
\mathrm{SU}(2)
, and the parameters are known as phase factors. QSP was subsequently extended by Gilyén
et al.

[
GSLW19
]
into quantum singular value transformation (QSVT), which has since been recognized as a seminal development in quantum algorithms, providing a unifying framework for many existing and new quantum algorithms
[
MRTC21
]
.
The problem of determining phase factors was initially considered computationally challenging
[
CMN+18
]
, and there have been significant algorithmic advancements in recent years on this topic
[
CDG+20
,
DMWL21
,
Yin22
,

,

,

,

,

,

.

The connection between QSP and the
\mathrm{SU}(2)
NLFT was recently established in
[
AMT24
]
, which showed that determining the phase factors in QSP is equivalent to solving a variant of the inverse NLFT, a task that we refer to as the incomplete inverse NLFT (
1
). This connection, along with its generalization, is discussed in
Section

3
. Given an incomplete inverse NLFT, one first completes it to obtain a standard inverse NLFT problem and solves the latter. In this paper, we focus exclusively on the
\mathrm{SU}(2)
version of the NLFT; any reference to the NLFT or its inverse hereon will refer to this case, unless stated otherwise.

Specifically, in this paper, with all formal definitions appearing later in
Section

2
, we consider a sequence
\boldsymbol{\gamma}:\mathbb{Z}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
compactly supported on
[m,n]\subseteq\mathbb{Z}
, whose NLFT is
\overbrace{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}(z)=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}a(z)&b(z)\\
-b^{*}(z)&a^{*}(z)\end{smallmatrix}\right)=\prod_{k=m}^{n}\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+|\gamma_{k}|^{2}}}\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&\gamma_{k}z^{k}\\
-\overline{\gamma_{k}}z^{-k}&1\end{smallmatrix}\right)

with
a^{*}(z):=\overline{a(\overline{z}^{-1})}
. Then
a(z)
and
b(z)
are Laurent polynomials satisfying
aa^{*}+bb^{*}=1
and
0<a^{*}(0)<\infty
, and all such pairs
(a,b)
are elements of the set
\mathcal{S}
, which is the image of the NLFT map. Our focus will be on the development of stable and efficient algorithms for solving the inverse NLFT problem:

Problem 1

(Inverse NLFT)
.

Given Laurent polynomials
a
and
b
such that
(a,b)\in\mathcal{S}
, determine a compactly supported sequence
\boldsymbol{\gamma}
such that
\overbrace{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}a&b\\
-b^{\ast}&a^{\ast}\end{smallmatrix}\right)

A key challenge in solving the inverse NLFT problem is its numerical stability.
A numerically stable algorithm should be able to compute the nonzero entries of
\boldsymbol{\gamma}
supported on an interval of size
n
to within an error
\epsilon
, using floating-point arithmetic with only
\operatorname{polylog}(n/\epsilon)
bits of precision.
One widely used approach for inverse NLFT is the layer stripping algorithm. However, its numerical stability is not guaranteed due to the potential accumulation of round-off errors. The algorithm is inherently sequential, and small inaccuracies introduced in earlier steps can propagate and amplify exponentially as the computation proceeds.
The recently developed Riemann-Hilbert factorization method
[
ALM+24
]
addresses this issue by replacing the sequential process with a parallel evaluation of the entries of
\boldsymbol{\gamma}
. This method yields the first provably numerically stable algorithm for inverse NLFT under certain conditions and for solving the associated QSP phase factor problem.
Nevertheless, it remains an open question whether numerical stability is intrinsic to the inverse NLFT problem itself or is due to specific algorithms, such as layer stripping. Another important issue is computational efficiency. All known algorithms currently scale polynomially with
n
, and it is still unknown whether there exists a numerically stable algorithm that scales linearly in
n
, for arbitrary input Laurent polynomials
a(z)
and
b(z)
.

1.2.
Related works

The previously known layer stripping algorithm
[
Tsa05
]
for solving the inverse NLFT problem lacked a guarantee of numerical stability, prompting the development of two new approaches. These are the Riemann-Hilbert factorization method
[
ALM+24
]
and its optimized variant, the Half-Cholesky algorithm
[
NY24
]
, which have runtime complexities of
\mathcal{O}(n^{4})
and
\mathcal{O}(n^{2})
, respectively, for a compactly supported sequence of length
n
.
The Riemann-Hilbert method computes each coefficient of the inverse NLFT sequence independently by solving a separate linear system; as a result, the numerical errors in computing each coefficient are independent, a property that contributes to its numerical stability. However, solving a separate linear system for each coefficient introduces considerable redundancy. The Half-Cholesky method eliminates this inefficiency by leveraging the displacement structure of the problem, while crucially maintaining numerical stability.

The central topic of this paper is to investigate stable and efficient algorithms for inverse NLFT without Riemann-Hilbert factorization. We show that the original layer stripping algorithm is also numerically stable, achieving the same
\mathcal{O}(n^{2})
runtime as the Half-Cholesky method, while offering a smaller pre-constant. Beyond that, the inverse nonlinear fast Fourier transform that we introduce improves the runtime to
\mathcal{O}(n\log^{2}n)
, while keeping the space complexity
\mathcal{O}(n)
unchanged. This fast algorithm is directly adapted from a similar algorithm underlying the superfast Toeplitz solver
[
AG89
]
in the
{\rm SU}(1,1)
case. Interestingly, all four algorithms share the same conditions under which numerical stability is guaranteed, and their key features are tabulated in
Table

1
.

The displacement low-rank structure plays a crucial role in structured matrix theory and has a surprising connection to inverse NLFT algorithms.
Extensive research has been conducted on fast triangular decomposition methods for such matrices
[
KS95
,
Pol10
,
Ste97
,
GKO95
]
, as well as on other efficient techniques for solving related linear systems
[
DFS93
,
AG88
,
XXG12
,
PZ00
,
CGS+08
,
CN96
,
XXCB14
]
. Stability concerns have been raised for these fast algorithms, and several methods have been proposed to mitigate these issues
[
AG89
,
VBHK01
,
Ste03
,
GKO95
]
. In particular, stability results have been established in the special case where the matrix is a positive definite Toeplitz-like matrix
[
CS96
,
SVD97
]
. The triangular decomposition of displacement low-rank matrices is closely related to the layer stripping process in NLFT. We leverage this connection to establish stability results for both the layer stripping method and the inverse nonlinear FFT algorithm (see
Section

5
).

Finding QSP phase factors for a target polynomial is an important application of inverse NLFT algorithms. We briefly review the relevant literature here and refer the reader to
[
NY24
, Table 1]
for a more detailed comparison of the complexity and stability of existing methods. Early algorithms can be grouped into two broad categories:
direct methods
and
iterative methods
.
The direct methods first find a complementary polynomial, which is analogous to the incomplete inverse NLFT task mentioned above, and then perform layer stripping to retrieve phase factors.
[
GSLW19
,
Haa19
]
determine the complementary polynomial via root-finding, which is numerically unstable and requires extended precision arithmetic to compute the roots accurately. Various improvements to the direct method have been carried out in
[

]
, but the numerical stability of these approaches has not yet been proved. For the GQSP problem, a similar complementary polynomial step is handled using an optimization-based approach
[

]
, but without guaranteed convergence.
In contrast, the iterative methods
[
DMWL21
,
DLNW24a
,
DLNW24b
]
find the QSP phase factors by solving an optimization problem or a nonlinear system of equations, and are numerically stable. Despite the complex energy landscape
[
WDL22
]
, the efficiency of these methods can be proved for a subset of target functions
[
WDL22
,
DLNW24a
]
. To our knowledge, current theoretical tools cannot guarantee the convergence of such iterative methods for all functions admissible under the QSP framework. Recently developed methods based on NLFT techniques
[
AMT24
,
ALM+24
,
NY24
]
can also be classified as direct methods. In these approaches, the complementary polynomial is obtained using the Weiss algorithm introduced in
[
ALM+24
]
, and the phase factors are subsequently recovered via the inverse NLFT algorithms summarized in

\mathcal{S}=\{(a,b):a,b\text{ are Laurent polynomials},\;aa^{\ast}+bb^{\ast}=1,\;0<a^{\ast}(0)<\infty\}.

One of the important aspects of
Lemma

2.1
is that the lowest and highest degrees of
b
immediately indicate the support of the sequence
\boldsymbol{\gamma}
. It also puts a constraint on the highest and lowest degrees of
a
. Note that the conditions specifying the image of NLFT in
Lemma

2.2
, namely (i)
aa^{\ast}+bb^{\ast}=1
, and (ii)
0<a^{\ast}(0)<\infty
, also automatically impose these constraints on the highest and lowest degrees of
a
and
b
. Moreover, the degree conditions on
a
in
Lemma

2.1
imply that
a^{\ast}
is a polynomial of lowest degree
0
and highest degree
n-m
. Condition (i) then implies that
|a(z)|,|b(z)|\leq 1
for all
z\in\mathbb{T}
.

2.2.
Complementary polynomials

Let us define the projection maps onto the first and second factors of
\mathcal{S}
, and denote the resulting projections as
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{B}
respectively:

(8)

\pi_{1}:\mathcal{S}\ni(a,b)\mapsto a\in\mathcal{A},\;\;\pi_{2}:\mathcal{S}\ni(a,b)\mapsto b\in\mathcal{B}.

The questions of membership in the sets
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{B}
have an answer based on the well-known Fejér-Riesz theorem for Laurent polynomials
[
DR10
]
: a Laurent polynomial
f
that is real on
\mathbb{T}
satisfies
f=f^{\ast}
, and if
f\geq 0
on
\mathbb{T}
then it can be factorized as
f=gg^{\ast}
for some Laurent polynomial
g
. Thus
b\in\mathcal{B}
if and only of
1-bb^{\ast}\geq 0
on
\mathbb{T}
. Similarly,
a\in\mathcal{A}
if and only if
1-aa^{\ast}\geq 0
on
\mathbb{T}
and
a^{*}(0)>0
.

For
a,b
such that
(a,b)\in\mathcal{S}
in
Lemma

2.2
, we say that
a
and
b
are
complementary polynomials
. Note that if
(a,b)\in\mathcal{S}
, then it implies that
(a,z^{k}b)\in\mathcal{S}
and
(z^{k}a,b)\not\in\mathcal{S}
, for every
k\in\mathbb{N}
. Thus, a natural question to ask is how much freedom we have in choosing
a
or
b
, given only one of them. This question has a clear answer, which we state in
Appendix

A
.
A precise characterization of the preimage over each point in
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{B}
is given in
Lemmas

A.3
and
A.2
. An implication of these two lemmas is worth noticing, which we use later in
Section

5.2
: if
(a,b)\in\mathcal{S}
and
a^{\ast}
has degree
n
, then there exists a unique
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}^{\ast}
such that
(\lambda z^{-n}a^{\ast},b)\in\mathcal{S}
.
Lemma

A.2
should be compared to
[
Tsa05
, Lemma 2.4]
, which essentially follows from
Lemma

A.2
. In fact, the roles of
a
and
b
can be interchanged to some extent, as stated in
Lemma

A.3
.

A polynomial
f
is called an
outer polynomial
if and only if it has no zeros in
\mathbb{D}
. A more general definition of outer functions beyond polynomials is given in
Appendix

B
. The discussion of outer polynomials arises in NLFT in the context of complementary polynomials. If
b\in\mathcal{B}
, then a corollary of
Lemma

A.2
is that there exists a unique
a\in\mathcal{A}
such that
(a,b)\in\mathcal{S}
and
a^{\ast}
is outer, a result that also appears previously in
[
Tsa05
, Theorem 4]
and
[
ALM+24
]
. The Weiss algorithm introduced in
[
ALM+24
]
provides an efficient and stable numerical procedure for computing the outer complementary polynomial
a^{*}
given
b
. The outerness condition plays a crucial role in ensuring the stability of inverse NLFT algorithms, as further discussed in
Section

5
.

2.3.
Inverse NLFT

The bijective property of NLFT, as given by
Lemma

2.2
, makes it possible to define the problem of computing the
inverse nonlinear Fourier transform
of a compactly supported sequence
\boldsymbol{\gamma}
. This leads to the statement of
1
that we have already introduced previously in
Section

1
. The solution
\boldsymbol{\gamma}
to
1
is called the inverse NLFT of
(a,b)
. By
Lemma

2.1
, determining the support of
\boldsymbol{\gamma}
is trivial—it follows directly from the lowest and highest degrees of
b
; let these degrees be
m
and
n
respectively. It is convenient to change
1
to one where the left endpoint of the support interval of
\boldsymbol{\gamma}
is
0
. For this, we use the fact from
[
AMT24
, Theorem 2]
, that if
\boldsymbol{\gamma},\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{\prime}\in\ell_{0}(\mathbb{Z},\mathbb{C})
such that
\gamma_{k-1}=\gamma^{\prime}_{k}
for all
k\in\mathbb{Z}
, then their NLFTs
\overbrace{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}=(a,b)
and
\overbrace{\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{\prime}}=(a^{\prime},b^{\prime})
satisfy

(9)

a=a^{\prime}\text{, and }zb=b^{\prime}.

Using this fact repeatedly, we can change
1
as follows: instead of determining the inverse NLFT of
(a,b)
, let us determine the inverse NLFT of
(a^{\prime},b^{\prime})
, where
a^{\prime}=a
and
b^{\prime}=z^{-m}b
. Note that properties (i) and (ii) in
1
are also satisfied by the pair
(a^{\prime},b^{\prime})
. Thus, without loss of generality, we will assume in the rest of the paper that
b
is a polynomial when talking about the inverse NLFT of
(a,b)
in
1
. Our focus in this paper will be on two algorithms for computing the inverse NLFT in a numerically stable manner: (a) the previously known layer stripping algorithm
[
Tsa05
]
, and (b) a new fast algorithm that we adapt from the
\mathrm{SU}(1,1)
case
[
AG89
]
. We discuss both of them in
Sections

4.1
and
4.2
.

Our stability analysis will rely on the condition that
a^{*}
has no roots in
\overline{\mathbb{D}}
, which is a slightly stronger condition than
a^{\ast}
being outer, introduced in
Section

2.2
. This condition is related to the function class
\mathbf{S}_{\eta}
in
[
ALM+24
,
NY24
]
. For a parameter
\eta\in(0,1)
, we define the decreasing family of subsets
\mathcal{S}_{\eta}\subseteq\mathcal{S}
as

(10)

\mathcal{S}_{\eta}:=\left\{(a,b)\in\mathcal{S}:b\text{ is a polynomial, }0<\sup_{z\in\mathbb{T}}|b(z)|\leq 1-\eta\right\}.

When
\sup_{z\in\mathbb{T}}|b(z)|
approaches
1
, the inverse NLFT problem becomes more and more ill-conditioned, as we will discover later in
Section

5
where
\eta^{-1}
appears in the condition number bounds. If
(a,b)\in\mathcal{S}_{\eta}
and
a^{\ast}
is outer, then it must be that
a^{\ast}
has no zeros in
\overline{\mathbb{D}}
(as
|a(z)|^{2}+|b(z)|^{2}=1
for all
z\in\mathbb{T}
). We record this fact as a lemma below, where the forward implication is true by compactness of
\mathbb{T}
:

Lemma 2.3

(No zeros in
\overline{\mathbb{D}}
property)
.

Suppose
(a,b)\in\mathcal{S}
and
a^{\ast}
is outer. Then
a^{\ast}
has no zeros in
\overline{\mathbb{D}}
if and only if
(a,b)\in\mathcal{S}_{\eta}
, for some
\eta\in(0,1)
.

Our stability analysis in
Section

5
will rely precisely on this above assumption that the inverse NLFT of
(a,b)\in\mathcal{S}
being computed satisfies the condition that
a^{\ast}
has no zeros in
\overline{\mathbb{D}}
.

Both NLFT and inverse NLFT are continuous maps, as proven in
[
Tsa05
]
, assuming various topologies on the domain and codomain of the two maps. However, the function spaces involved there are infinite-dimensional. In the compactly supported setting, it is desirable to have simpler continuity estimates as we always work with finite-dimensional spaces, and such estimates are established in
Section

6
. In particular, we show that the NLFT map is Lipschitz continuous, while the inverse NLFT map is merely locally Lipschitz continuous and not uniformly continuous, where the topologies on both the domain and codomain are inherited from the Euclidean metric.

3.
Connections between NLFT, QSP, and GQSP

The connection between symmetric QSP and NLFT, both for finitely many and infinitely many phase factors, was established in previous works
[
AMT24
,
ALM+24
]
. In this section, we extend this correspondence to the case of QSP involving asymmetric phase factors
[
GSLW19
]
, as well as its generalization, GQSP
[
MW24
]
. We use

Z:=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&-1\end{pmatrix},\ X:=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix},\ \text{and}\ H:=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\
1&-1\end{pmatrix}

to denote the
2\times 2
Pauli-
Z
, Pauli-
X
, and the Hadamard matrices respectively.

The connection between NLFT and QSP in all existing works depends on a close analog of the inverse NLFT problem (
1
), where we instead have incomplete NLFT data — specifically, this means that we are only given one of the Laurent polynomials
a
or
b
such that
(a,b)\in\mathcal{S}
, while the other remains unspecified. In recent NLFT literature, such as
[
AMT24
,
ALM+24
]
, it is the second Laurent polynomial
b
that is always specified, while the first one needs to be determined. We formalize this task below, and it will be our goal in this section to reduce both the QSP and GQSP phase factor finding tasks to it:

Task 1

(Incomplete inverse NLFT)
.

Given a Laurent polynomial
b\in\mathcal{B}
, determine a complementary Laurent polynomial
a\in\mathcal{A}
and a compactly supported sequence
\boldsymbol{\gamma}
such that
\overbrace{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}=(a,b)
.

As mentioned in
Section

2.2
,
1
can be converted to
1
with an outer
a^{*}
by applying the Weiss algorithm. This places it squarely within the scope of the algorithmic and stability results developed later in this paper. As in the case of the inverse NLFT problem in
Section

2.3
, in this section
we will assume without loss of generality that
b\in\mathcal{B}
is actually a polynomial with minimum degree zero, in the context of
1
. The general case easily reduces to this special case, just like in the case of inverse NLFT (
1
).

3.1.
Quantum signal processing

We define the family of
\mathrm{SU}(2)
valued matrices
W(x):=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}x&i\sqrt{1-x^{2}}\\
i\sqrt{1-x^{2}}&x\end{smallmatrix}\right)
, for all
x\in[-1,1]
. In (finite) quantum signal processing
[
GSLW19
,
LYC16
]
, we start with a sequence
\Psi:=\left\{\psi_{k}\in\left[-\frac{\pi}{2},\frac{\pi}{2}\right]:k=0,1,\dots,n\right\}
, and then consider the
\mathrm{SU}(2)
valued map
U_{n}:\left[-\frac{\pi}{2},\frac{\pi}{2}\right]^{n+1}\times[-1,1]
defined as follows:

(11)

U_{n}(\Psi,x)=\begin{pmatrix}u_{n}(\Psi,x)&i\,v_{n}(\Psi,x)\\
i\,\overline{v_{n}}(\Psi,x)&\overline{u_{n}}(\Psi,x)\end{pmatrix}:=e^{i\psi_{0}Z}\prod_{k=1}^{n}\left(W(x)e^{i\psi_{k}Z}\right).

The sequence
\Psi
is called the
phase factor sequence
of the QSP protocol
d
d

d

The reader should note that there are a few different conventions available in the literature for defining the QSP protocol. Our definition coincides with the
Chebyshev QSP
protocol. A good summary of the relationships between our definition and other QSP protocols, like
Laurent QSP
or
Analytic QSP
, can be found in
[
Lan25
]
.
, the matrix
W(x)
is called the
signal unitary
, and the
Z
-rotation matrices
\{e^{i\psi_{k}Z}\}_{k=0}^{n}
are called
control unitaries
. The task of finding the QSP phase factors is an inverse process of finding a
\Psi
given partial knowledge of
U_{n}(\Psi,x)
— specifically, it refers to the following task:

Task 2

(Determine QSP phase factors)
.

Given an even or odd real target polynomial
f(x)\in\mathbb{R}[x]
of degree
n
, satisfying
\|f\|_{\infty}:=\max\limits_{x\in[-1,1]}|f(x)|\leq 1
, find a sequence
\Psi
such that
\mathrm{Im}(u_{n}(\Psi,x))=f(x)
.

3.2.
Generalized quantum signal processing

In the standard QSP protocol introduced above in
Eq.

11
, each of the control unitaries
e^{i\psi_{k}Z}
only has one degree of freedom. In a slightly different setting, the paper
[
MW24
]
proposed a more flexible quantum algorithm called
generalized quantum signal processing
, which uses a
2
-parameter family of control unitaries within the analytic QSP protocol. We will follow the same setup with a slightly different choice for the parameterized unitaries as follows:

(12)

R(\psi,\phi):=\begin{pmatrix}\cos\psi&e^{i\phi}\sin\psi\\
-e^{-i\phi}\sin\psi&\cos\psi\end{pmatrix},\;\;\;\psi,\phi\in[-\pi,\pi],

and with this choice, the complete form of the GQSP protocol is given on the right-hand side of
Eq.

13
. The advantage of GQSP over QSP is that it lifts the parity restrictions on the target polynomial
[
MW24
, Theorem 3]
. Just like in the case of QSP, we may now also state the analogous phase factor finding task for GQSP:

Task 3

(Determine GQSP phase factors)
.

Given a target polynomial
Q(z)\in\mathbb{C}[z]
of degree
n
, satisfying
\max\limits_{z\in\mathbb{T}}|Q(z)|\leq 1
, find sequences
\{\phi_{k}\}_{k=0}^{n}
and
\{\psi_{k}\}_{k=0}^{n}
such that

(13)

\begin{pmatrix}\cdot&Q(z)\\
\cdot&\cdot\end{pmatrix}=R(\psi_{0},\phi_{0})\prod_{k=1}^{n}\left(\begin{pmatrix}z&\\
&1\end{pmatrix}R(\psi_{k},\phi_{k})\right).

The original GQSP problem in
[
MW24
]
placed the target polynomial in the upper left corner rather than the upper right corner like us, but this does not matter since we can multiply a matrix
\left(\begin{smallmatrix}0&1\\
-1&0\end{smallmatrix}\right)\in\text{SU(2)}
at the end from the right. Moreover our parameterization of
R(\psi,\phi)
in
Eq.

12
actually gives an element in
\mathrm{SU}(2)
, unlike the choice in
[
MW24
]
. The sequences
\{\phi_{k}\}_{k=0}^{n}
and
\{\psi_{k}\}_{k=0}^{n}
in
Eq.

13
are called the
GQSP phase factor sequences
.

3.3.
Correspondence between QSP, GQSP, and NLFT

To establish the correspondence between the QSP protocol discussed above and the incomplete inverse NLFT problem, we will need the following result whose part (b) is exactly analogous to
[
AMT24
, Lemma 1]
, and we include it here to elucidate the point that asymmetric QSP does not pose any special difficulty, as compared to the symmetric case. On the other hand, part (a) of the lemma has the nice feature that the sequence
\boldsymbol{\gamma}
determined from
\Psi
remains real when
\Psi
is real, and it is what we will use later in
Theorem

3.2
.

Lemma 3.1
.

For
x\in[-1,1]
, let
\theta\in[0,\pi]
be such that
\cos\theta=x
. For
n\geq 0
, let
U_{n}
be defined as in Eq. (
11
), and additionally assume that
\psi_{k}\in(-\frac{\pi}{2},\frac{\pi}{2})
for all
k=0,\dots,n
.

(a)

If we define the sequence
\boldsymbol{\gamma}:\mathbb{Z}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
as
\gamma_{k}:=\tan\psi_{k}
, for
k=0,\dots,n
, and zero otherwise, then for all
x\in[-1,1]
we have

(14)

\begin{pmatrix}1&\\
&i\end{pmatrix}HU_{n}(\Psi,\cos\theta)H\begin{pmatrix}1&\\
&-i\end{pmatrix}=\overbrace{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}(e^{2i\theta})\begin{pmatrix}e^{in\theta}&0\\
0&e^{-in\theta}\end{pmatrix}.

(b)

If we define the sequence
\boldsymbol{\gamma}:\mathbb{Z}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
as
\gamma_{k}:=i\tan\psi_{k}
, for
k=0,\dots,n
, and zero otherwise, then for all
x\in[-1,1]
we have

(15)

HU_{n}(\Psi,\cos\theta)H=\overbrace{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}(e^{2i\theta})\begin{pmatrix}e^{in\theta}&0\\
0&e^{-in\theta}\end{pmatrix}.

Proof.

This proof works for both parts (a) and (b). For part (a) we denote
J:=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&0\\
0&i\end{smallmatrix}\right)
, so that
J^{\ast}=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&0\\
0&-i\end{smallmatrix}\right)=J^{-1}
, while in part (b),
J
is simply the identity matrix.

Let us define
\widetilde{W}(x):=HW(x)H
, and note that since
\cos\theta=x
, we may obtain

(16)

\widetilde{W}(x)=\begin{pmatrix}e^{i\theta}&0\\
0&e^{-i\theta}\end{pmatrix},\;\;JHW(x)HJ^{\ast}=J\widetilde{W}(x)J^{\ast}=\widetilde{W}(x).

Also recall that
HZH=X
, which together with the definition of
\gamma_{k}
, leads to

(17)

JHe^{i\psi_{k}Z}HJ^{\ast}=Je^{i\psi_{k}X}J^{\ast}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+|\gamma_{k}|^{2}}}\begin{pmatrix}1&\gamma_{k}\\
-\overline{\gamma_{k}}&1\end{pmatrix},\;\;k=0,\dots,n.

Next, the left-hand sides of
Eqs.

14
and
15
equate to
JHU_{n}(\Psi,\cos\theta)HJ^{\ast}
, which we can also express in the following ordered product form using
Eqs.

16
and
17
:

(18)

JHU_{n}(\Psi,\cos\theta)HJ^{\ast}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+|\gamma_{0}|^{2}}}\begin{pmatrix}1&\gamma_{0}\\
-\overline{\gamma_{0}}&1\end{pmatrix}\prod_{k=1}^{n}\left[\widetilde{W}(x)\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+|\gamma_{k}|^{2}}}\begin{pmatrix}1&\gamma_{k}\\
-\overline{\gamma_{k}}&1\end{pmatrix}\right].

Finally, notice the following identity, for any
t\in\mathbb{R}

(19)

\begin{pmatrix}e^{i\theta t}&0\\

which allows us to simplify the right-hand side of

\begin{split}JHU_{n}(\Psi,\cos\theta)HJ^{\ast}&=\left(\prod_{k=0}^{n}\left[\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+|\gamma_{k}|^{2}}}\begin{pmatrix}1&\gamma_{k}e^{2ik\theta}\\

0&e^{-in\theta}\end{pmatrix}.\end{split}

This finishes the proof.
∎

\gamma_{n}
is the component at index
r^{\prime}
. The NLFT of
\boldsymbol{\gamma}
will be denoted
\overbrace{[\gamma_{m},\dots,\gamma_{n}]}
. For instance, with this notation, we have

(23)

\overbrace{[\gamma_{m},\ldots,\gamma_{n}]}=\overbrace{[\gamma_{m},\ldots,\gamma_{n},0,0,\ldots]}=\prod_{j=0}^{n-m}\left[\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+|\gamma_{j+m}|^{2}}}\begin{pmatrix}1&\gamma_{j+m}z^{j}\\
-\overline{\gamma_{j+m}}z^{-j}&1\end{pmatrix}\right].

Now we assume that we are given
(a,b)\in\mathcal{S}
, as in
1
, and that the lowest and highest degrees of
b
are
0
and
n-1
, respectively. Recall from
Lemma

2.1
that the inverse NLFT of
(a,b)
is an element of
\ell(0,n-1)
. Thus our task is to find the sequence
[\gamma_{0},\ldots,\gamma_{n-1}]
satisfying

(24)

\begin{pmatrix}a(z)&b(z)\\
-b^{*}(z)&a^{*}(z)\end{pmatrix}=\overbrace{[\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1},\ldots,\gamma_{n-1}]}.

The basic strategy for determining this sequence is to strip off the unitary matrices one at a time from the left, thereby reducing the problem size by one each time, and then apply the method recursively to the smaller sequence until the entire sequence is read off, which justifies the name “layer stripping”.

The problem of determining the first component becomes finding
\gamma_{0}\in\mathbb{C}
such that

(25)

\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+|\gamma_{0}|^{2}}}\begin{pmatrix}1&-\gamma_{0}\\
\overline{\gamma_{0}}&1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}a(z)&b(z)\\
-b^{*}(z)&a^{*}(z)\end{pmatrix}=\overbrace{[0,\gamma_{1},\ldots,\gamma_{n-1}]}.

If we let

(26)

\begin{pmatrix}a_{1}(z)&b_{1}(z)\\
-b_{1}^{*}(z)&a_{1}^{*}(z)\end{pmatrix}=\overbrace{[\gamma_{1},\ldots,\gamma_{n-1}]},

then from
Eq.

9
we can rewrite
Eq.

25
as

(27)

\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+|\gamma_{0}|^{2}}}\begin{pmatrix}a(z)+\gamma_{0}b^{\ast}(z)&b(z)-\gamma_{0}a^{*}(z)\\
\overline{\gamma_{0}}a(z)-b^{*}(z)&\overline{\gamma_{0}}b(z)+a^{*}(z)\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}a_{1}(z)&zb_{1}(z)\\
-z^{-1}b_{1}^{*}(z)&a_{1}^{*}(z)\end{pmatrix}.

Comparing the upper right element, it is clear that the only way to make
\frac{b(z)-\gamma_{0}a^{*}(z)}{z}
still be a polynomial is to let
\gamma_{0}=\frac{b(0)}{a^{*}(0)}
, which is well defined as
a^{\ast}(0)>0
by
Lemma

2.1
. After determining
\gamma_{0}
, we can calculate
a_{1}(z)
and
b_{1}(z)
from
Eq.

27
. The remaining problem is to retrieve the rest of the sequence
\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2},\ldots,\gamma_{n-1}
satisfying
Eq.

26
. This is equivalent to solving
Eq.

24
but with reduced size, and we may iteratively apply the same procedure to recover the remaining coefficients
\gamma_{k}
one by one.

From
Eq.

27
, we can write the recursion formula as

(28)

\gamma_{k}=\frac{b_{k}(0)}{a_{k}^{*}(0)},\quad a_{k+1}^{*}(z)=\frac{a_{k}^{*}(z)+\overline{\gamma_{k}}b_{k}(z)}{\sqrt{1+|\gamma_{k}|^{2}}},\quad b_{k+1}(z)=\frac{b_{k}(z)-\gamma_{k}a_{k}^{*}(z)}{z\sqrt{1+|\gamma_{k}|^{2}}}.

If we temporarily lift the restriction that
(a,b)\in\mathcal{S}
, then
Eq.

28
can also be applied iteratively to an arbitrary pair of functions
(a_{0}^{*},b_{0})
, as long as
a_{0}^{\ast}
and
b_{0}
satisfy two conditions: (i)
a_{0}^{\ast}
and
b_{0}
are both holomorphic at
0
, and (ii)
a_{0}^{\ast}(0)>0
. This procedure then yields a sequence
\boldsymbol{\gamma}=(\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1},\ldots)
, and sequences of functions
\{a_{k}^{\ast}:k=0,1,\dots\}
and
\{b_{k}:k=0,1,\dots\}
, all of which also satisfy the same conditions as
a_{0}^{\ast}
and
b_{0}
:

(29)

a_{k}^{\ast},b_{k}\text{ are holomorphic at }0,\;\;\;a_{k}^{\ast}(0)>0,\;\;k\in\mathbb{N}.

Furthermore, from
Eq.

28
one checks that if
b_{k^{\prime}}=0
, then
b_{k}=0
and
a_{k}^{\ast}=a_{k^{\prime}}
for all
k\geq k^{\prime}
; thus we may say that the procedure
terminates
at iteration
k^{\prime}
, if
k^{\prime}
is the smallest value for which
b_{k^{\prime}}=0
. If
(a,b)\in\mathcal{S}
, this procedure terminates at iteration
k^{\prime}
(here
k^{\prime}
is precisely the size of the support of
\boldsymbol{\gamma}
) with
a_{k^{\prime}}=1
, and the output sequence
\boldsymbol{\gamma}
is the inverse NLFT of
(a,b)
. But when
(a,b)\not\in\mathcal{S}
, two main differences arise:

(i)

The iteration may not terminate; that is,
\boldsymbol{\gamma}
may contain infinitely many nonzero elements.

(ii)

The NLFT
\overbrace{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}(z)
of this sequence, when it exists, may not coincide with the matrix
\left(\begin{smallmatrix}a_{0}(z)&b_{0}(z)\\
-b_{0}^{\ast}(z)&a_{0}^{\ast}(z)\end{smallmatrix}\right)
.

Therefore, we may still call
\boldsymbol{\gamma}
as the
layer stripping sequence
of
(a_{0}^{*},b_{0})
, while
\overbrace{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}=(a_{0},b_{0})
if and only if
(a_{0},b_{0})\in\mathcal{S}
.
Nonetheless, this observation proves useful in developing the inverse nonlinear FFT algorithm discussed in
Section

4.2
.

We also remark that if
c(z)
is holomorphic at
z=0
with
c(0)>0
, and we apply the layer stripping procedure
Eq.

28
to the scaled pair
(\tilde{a}_{0}^{*}(z),\tilde{b}_{0}(z)):=\left(\frac{a_{0}^{*}(z)}{c(z)},\frac{b_{0}(z)}{c(z)}\right)
, then by induction we have

(30)

\tilde{\gamma}_{k}=\gamma_{k},\quad\tilde{a}_{k+1}^{*}(z)=\frac{a_{k+1}^{*}(z)}{c(z)},\quad\tilde{b}_{k+1}(z)=\frac{b_{k+1}(z)}{c(z)},\quad k=0,1,\dots.

In other words, scaling
(a_{0}^{*},b_{0})
by such a function
c(z)
does not alter the layer stripping sequence. In particular, the pair
\left(1,\frac{b_{0}(z)}{a_{0}^{*}(z)}\right)
yields the same layer stripping sequence as
(a_{0}^{*}(z),b_{0}(z))
. This offers another explanation for why the Half-Cholesky algorithm in
[
NY24
]
works.

Next, we discuss the matrix form of the layer stripping algorithm, which facilitates its numerical implementation. We define

(31)

(a_{k}(z),b_{k}(z)):=\overbrace{[\gamma_{k},\ldots,\gamma_{n-1}]},\ k=0,1,\ldots,n-1,

and work on the coefficients of
b_{k}(z):=\sum_{j=0}^{n-1-k}b_{j,k}z^{j}
and
a_{k}^{*}(z):=\sum_{j=0}^{n-1-k}a_{j,k}z^{j}
. Note that we are using the coefficients of
a_{k}^{*}(z)
instead of
a_{k}(z)
to avoid negative powers of
z
. We also point out that
(a_{0},b_{0})=(a,b)
. Define the column vectors
\mathbf{a}_{k}:=(a_{j,k})_{0\leq j\leq n-k-1}
,
\mathbf{b}_{k}:=(b_{j,k})_{0\leq j\leq n-k-1}
, and the
(n-k)\times 2
matrix

(32)

G_{k}:=(\mathbf{a}_{k},\mathbf{b}_{k}).

Throughout this subsection, we will also refer to
a_{n-k,k}=0
for
k\leq n-1
and
a_{0,n}=1
, whose choices will be evident from the discussion below.
The
k^{\text{th}}
step of layer stripping can be described as outputting
\gamma_{k}=\frac{b_{0,k}}{a_{0,k}}
, and also obtaining the coefficients of the polynomials
a_{k+1}^{*}(z)
and
zb_{k+1}(z)
when
k<n-1
, by performing the following Givens rotation

(33)

\begin{pmatrix}a_{0,k+1}&0\\
a_{1,k+1}&b_{0,k+1}\\
\vdots&\vdots\\
a_{n-k-2,k+1}&b_{n-k-3,k+1}\\
a_{n-k-1,k+1}&b_{n-k-2,k+1}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}{a}_{0,k}&{b}_{0,k}\\
{a}_{1,k}&{b}_{1,k}\\
\vdots&\vdots\\
{a}_{n-k-2,k}&{b}_{n-k-2,k}\\
{a}_{n-k-1,k}&{b}_{n-k-1,k}\end{pmatrix}\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+|\gamma_{k}|^{2}}}\begin{pmatrix}1&-\gamma_{k}\\
\overline{\gamma_{k}}&1\end{pmatrix},

where the matrix
\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+|\gamma_{k}|^{2}}}\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&-\gamma_{k}\\
\overline{\gamma_{k}}&1\end{smallmatrix}\right)\in\mathrm{SU}(2)
. When
k<n-1
, the element
a_{n-k-1,k+1}=0
, since the
a^{\ast}_{k+1}(z)
given by
(a_{k+1}(z),b_{k+1}(z))=\overbrace{[\gamma_{k+1},\ldots,\gamma_{n-1}]}
is a polynomial of degree
n-k-2
. We then obtain

(34)

G_{k+1}=(\mathbf{a}_{k+1},\mathbf{b}_{k+1})=\begin{pmatrix}a_{0,k+1}&b_{0,k+1}\\
a_{1,k+1}&b_{1,k+1}\\
\vdots&\vdots\\
a_{n-k-2,k+1}&b_{n-k-2,k+1}\end{pmatrix}

by realigning the columns of the above matrix, corresponding to stripping the extra factor of
z
in
zb_{k+1}(z)
.

Some easy properties of the layer stripping algorithm are recorded in the following lemma for later use in
Section

6
:

Lemma 4.1

(Layer stripping properties)
.

Define
\Theta(t):=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+|t|^{2}}}\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&-t\\
\overline{t}&1\end{smallmatrix}\right)\in\mathrm{SU}(2)
, for
t\in\mathbb{C}
. With this notation, the layer stripping algorithm satisfies

(a)

a_{0,k}=a_{0,k-1}\sqrt{1+|\gamma_{k-1}|^{2}}
, for every
k\geq 1
. This implies
a_{0,0}\leq a_{0,1}\leq\dots\leq a_{0,n-1}
.

(b)

\left\lVert G_{k}\Theta(\gamma_{k})\right\rVert_{2}=\left\lVert G_{k}\right\rVert_{2}
, and
\left\lVert G_{k}\Theta(\gamma_{k})\right\rVert_{F}=\left\lVert G_{k}\right\rVert_{F}=\left\lVert G_{0}\right\rVert_{F}=1
, for every
k\geq 0
.

(c)

\left\lVert G_{k+1}\right\rVert_{1}=\left\lVert G_{k}\Theta(\gamma_{k})\right\rVert_{1}
, for every
0\leq k\leq n-2
.

(d)

|a_{j,k}|,|b_{j,k}|\leq 1
, for every
0\leq k\leq n-1
, and
0\leq j\leq n-k-1
.

(e)

|\gamma_{k}|\leq\left(a_{0,0}\prod_{j=0}^{k-1}\sqrt{1+|\gamma_{j}|^{2}}\right)^{-1}
, for every
0\leq k\leq n-1
.

Proof.

(a)

We may read off from the first row of
Eq.

33
that
a_{0,k}=a_{0,k-1}\sqrt{1+|\gamma_{k-1}|^{2}}
, for every
k\geq 1
.

(b)

This follows as
\Theta(\gamma_{k})\in\mathrm{SU}(2)
, because the matrix 2-norm and the Frobenius norm are invariant under right multiplication by unitary matrices.
\left\lVert G_{0}\right\rVert^{2}_{F}=1
because
|a^{\ast}(z)|^{2}+|b(z)|^{2}=1
by
Lemma

2.1
(see discussion near
Eq.

135
).

(c)

Note that for the matrix
G_{k}\Theta(\gamma_{k})
, the first entry of the second column and the last entry of the first column are guaranteed to be zero, as we have argued before this lemma. After that, we obtain the matrix
G_{k+1}
by shifting the first column of the matrix
G_{k}\Theta(\gamma_{k})
by one row and then deleting the first row (see
Eq.

34
). Thus, the
1
-norms of both columns of
G_{k}\Theta(\gamma_{k})
and
G_{k+1}
are the same, and the conclusion follows.

(d)

Follows from part (b).

(e)

Follows since
|\gamma_{k}|=\left|\frac{b_{0,k}}{a_{0,k}}\right|
, and then using parts (a) and (d).

∎

As discussed earlier, the layer stripping procedure can be applied to general function pairs. This becomes even clearer from the matrix perspective, where the algorithm is iteratively performing the “rotation and shift” operations, which can be initialized from any
n\times 2
matrix
G_{0}
as long as
a_{0,0}>0
, and produce a layer stripping sequence. The difference is that the
a_{n-k-1,k+1}=0
may no longer hold in
Eq.

33
, but we still drop this element after the shift.

Complexity
. From the matrix form of the layer stripping algorithm, it is clear that the time complexity of the algorithm is
\mathcal{O}(n^{2})
, because at every iteration
k=0,\dots,n-1
, the rotation operation acts on
\mathcal{O}(n)
length vectors, and each shift operation is
\mathcal{O}(1)
. This also shows that the space complexity of the algorithm is
\mathcal{O}(n)
as both the rotation and shift operations can be performed in place.

4.2.
Fast algorithm for inverse NLFT

The layer stripping algorithm involves substantial redundant computation. This section aims to develop a more efficient alternative by carefully analyzing and restructuring the existing method. We refer to this new algorithm as the
inverse nonlinear fast Fourier transform
(inverse nonlinear FFT) algorithm, as it shares the same divide-and-conquer structure as the standard FFT. However, unlike the standard FFT, where the forward and inverse algorithms are nearly identical, the inverse nonlinear FFT differs substantially from its forward counterpart (see
[
NY24
, Section 3.1]
), despite the fact that they both rely on fast polynomial multiplication.

Our method is inspired by the superfast Toeplitz system solver introduced in
[
AG89
]
. It is known that the
\mathrm{SU}(1,1)
NLFT is closely related to Toeplitz solvers, and we observe that a similar idea can be adapted to the
\mathrm{SU}(2)
inverse NLFT problem considered in this work.

Suppose
m=\left\lceil\frac{n}{2}\right\rceil
, and we divide the task of computing
\boldsymbol{\gamma}
into two parts:
\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{\mathrm{up}}=(\gamma_{0},\ldots,\gamma_{m-1})^{T}
and
\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{\mathrm{down}}=(\gamma_{m},\ldots,\gamma_{n-1})^{T}
. It can be observed that computing
\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{\mathrm{up}}
depends only on the first
m
rows of
\mathbf{a}_{0}
and
\mathbf{b}_{0}
. This is because the output of the first
m
steps of the layer stripping process,
Eq.

33
and
Eq.

34
, does not involve the last
n-m
rows of the matrices
G_{0},\ldots,G_{m-1}
.

On the other hand, the computation of
\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{\mathrm{down}}
only relies on
G_{m}=(\mathbf{a}_{m},\mathbf{b}_{m})
. Therefore, if we have an efficient way to compute
\mathbf{a}_{m}
and
\mathbf{b}_{m}
, we can avoid computing
a_{j,k}
and
b_{j,k}
for
0\leq j\leq m-1
and
m-j\leq k\leq n-1-j
, which correspond to the last
n-m
rows of the matrices
G_{0},\ldots,G_{m-1}
.

Next, we explain how to efficiently calculate
(\mathbf{a}_{m},\mathbf{b}_{m})
given
(\mathbf{a}_{0},\mathbf{b}_{0})
and
\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{\mathrm{up}}
. Define polynomials
\eta_{k\to m}(z)
and
\xi_{k\to m}(z)
by

(35)

\overbrace{[\gamma_{k},\ldots,\gamma_{m-1}]}=\begin{pmatrix}\eta_{k\to m}^{*}(z)&\xi_{k\to m}(z)\\
-\xi_{k\to m}^{*}(z)&\eta_{k\to m}(z)\end{pmatrix},

and we will abbreviate
\eta_{0\to m}
and
\xi_{0\to m}
as
\eta_{m}
and
\xi_{m}
. With these notations, the identity

(36)

\overbrace{[\gamma_{0},\ldots,\gamma_{m-1}]}\;\overbrace{[0,\ldots,0,\gamma_{m},\ldots,\gamma_{n-1}]}=\overbrace{[\gamma_{0},\ldots,\gamma_{n-1}]}

can be written as

(37)

\begin{pmatrix}\eta_{m}^{*}(z)&\xi_{m}(z)\\
-\xi_{m}^{*}(z)&\eta_{m}(z)\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}a_{m}(z)&z^{m}b_{m}(z)\\
-z^{-m}b_{m}^{*}(z)&a_{m}^{*}(z)\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}a_{0}(z)&b_{0}(z)\\
-b_{0}^{*}(z)&a_{0}^{*}(z)\end{pmatrix}.

We can invert the first matrix to obtain

(38)

\begin{pmatrix}z^{m}b_{m}(z)\\
a_{m}^{*}(z)\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\eta_{m}(z)&-\xi_{m}(z)\\
\xi_{m}^{*}(z)&\eta_{m}^{*}(z)\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}b_{0}(z)\\
a_{0}^{*}(z)\end{pmatrix}.

This involves only a few polynomial multiplications and can therefore be implemented efficiently using the fast Fourier transform (FFT). The only remaining task is to compute
\eta_{m}(z)
and
\xi_{m}(z)
from
[\gamma_{0},\ldots,\gamma_{m-1}]
efficiently. This can be accomplished using a divide-and-conquer approach as described in
[
NY24
, Section 3.1]
. To be self-contained, we present the method here, but as a recursive process.
Assume
l=\left\lceil\frac{m}{2}\right\rceil
, and that the recursive steps have already yielded

\overbrace{[\gamma_{0},\ldots,\gamma_{l-1}]}\quad\text{and}\quad\overbrace{[\gamma_{l},\ldots,\gamma_{m-1}]},

and then we may compute
\overbrace{[\gamma_{0},\ldots,\gamma_{m-1}]}
using
Eq.

36
, where the polynomial-valued matrix multiplications can also be efficiently carried out using FFT. Once this is done,
\eta_{m}(z)
and
\xi_{m}(z)
can be extracted directly from the resulting matrix
\overbrace{[\gamma_{0},\ldots,\gamma_{m-1}]}
.

In the calculation procedure, we define polynomials
\xi_{n}^{\sharp}:=z^{n}\xi_{n}^{*}
, and
\eta_{n}^{\sharp}:=z^{n}\eta_{n}^{*}
,
\xi_{m\to n}^{\sharp}=z^{n-m}\xi_{m\to n}^{*}
, and likewise
\eta_{m\to n}^{\sharp}=z^{n-m}\eta_{m\to n}^{*}
. While this change does not essentially affect the underlying computation, working with
\xi_{n}^{\sharp},\eta_{n}^{\sharp}
rather than the Laurent polynomials
\xi_{n}^{*},\eta_{n}^{*}
eliminates the negative powers, and also makes polynomial multiplication directly correspond to the convolution of the coefficient vectors without any shifting.

The complete algorithm is presented in
Algorithm

1
. Note that the output also includes the coefficients of
\xi_{n}
and
\eta_{n}
, which are needed in the recursive steps. We remark that in Step 2, the relevant coefficients
\{(a_{j,0},b_{j,0})\}_{j=0}^{m-1}
does not correspond to a function pair in
\mathcal{S}
. However, a crucial fact is that
Eq.

38
also holds for the recurrence relations given by
Eq.

28
, even when
(a_{0},b_{0})\not\in\mathcal{S}
. Thus, as discussed in the second last paragraph of
Section

4.1
, there is no ambiguity in referring to its layer stripping sequence as
\{\gamma_{k}\}_{k=0}^{m-1}
and proceeding with the recursive call.

Algorithm 1

Inverse Nonlinear Fast Fourier Transform

Input:
Sequences
\{a_{0,0},\ldots,a_{n-1,0}\}
, and
\{b_{0,0},\ldots,b_{n-1,0}\}
.

Output:
Sequences
\{\gamma_{0},\ldots,\gamma_{n-1}\}
, and the coefficients of
\xi_{n}(z)
and
\eta_{n}(z)
.

Step 1:
If
n=1
, exit algorithm with output
\gamma_{0}=\frac{b_{0,0}}{a_{0,0}}
,
\xi_{1}=\frac{\gamma_{0}}{\sqrt{1+|\gamma_{0}|^{2}}}
, and
\eta_{1}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+|\gamma_{0}|^{2}}}
.

Step 2:
If
n\geq 2
, let
m=\lceil\frac{n}{2}\rceil
, then use the first
m
coefficients of
a_{0}^{*}(z)
and
b_{0}(z)
to calculate
\{\gamma_{k}\}_{k=0}^{m-1}
, and polynomials
\xi_{m}
, and
\eta_{m}
, which is a recursive call of size
\frac{n}{2}
.

Step 3:
Use the first
n
coefficients of
a_{0}^{*}(z)
and
b_{0}(z)
, together with
\xi_{m}
and
\eta_{m}
, to calculate the first
n-m
coefficients of
a_{m}^{*}(z)
and
b_{m}(z)
through

(39)

\begin{pmatrix}a_{m}^{*}(z)\\
b_{m}(z)\end{pmatrix}=\frac{1}{z^{m}}\begin{pmatrix}\eta_{m}^{\sharp}(z)&\xi_{m}^{\sharp}(z)\\
-\xi_{m}(z)&\eta_{m}(z)\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}a_{0}^{*}(z)\\
b_{0}(z)\end{pmatrix}.

The
\frac{1}{z^{m}}
term is used to cancel the common
z^{m}
factors appearing in both
a_{m}^{*}
and
b_{m}
.

Step 4:
Use the first
n-m
coefficients of
a_{m}^{*}(z)
and
b_{m}(z)
to calculate
\{\gamma_{k}\}_{k=m}^{n-1}
, and the polynomials
\xi_{m\to n}
and
\eta_{m\to n}
. This is another recursive call of size
\frac{n}{2}
, with input being the coefficients of
a_{m}^{*}
and
b_{m}
.

Step 5:
Calculate
\xi_{n}
and
\eta_{n}
through

(40)

\begin{pmatrix}\eta_{n}^{\sharp}&\xi_{n}\\
-\xi_{n}^{\sharp}&\eta_{n}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\eta_{m}^{\sharp}&\xi_{m}\\
-\xi_{m}^{\sharp}&\eta_{m}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\eta_{m\to n}^{\sharp}&\xi_{m\to n}\\
-\xi_{m\to n}^{\sharp}&\eta_{m\to n}\end{pmatrix}.

Finally, output
\{\gamma_{k}\}_{k=0}^{n-1}
and polynomials
\xi_{n}
,
\eta_{n}
.

Complexity
. The inverse nonlinear FFT algorithm contains two recursive calls of size
\frac{n}{2}
(Steps 2 and 4), and several polynomial multiplications (Steps 3 and 5). These polynomial multiplications are of
\mathcal{O}\left(n\log n\right)
time complexity when FFT is used to calculate the convolution of their coefficients. Therefore, the total time complexity is

(41)

n\log n+2\left(\frac{n}{2}\log\frac{n}{2}\right)+4\left(\frac{n}{4}\log\frac{n}{4}\right)+\cdots=\mathcal{O}\left(n\log^{2}n\right).

For the space complexity of the inverse nonlinear FFT algorithm, notice that at any stage of execution of the entire algorithm, there can be at most one active recursive call of size
n/2^{k}
, where
0\leq k\leq\left\lceil\log_{2}n\right\rceil
. This is because in
Algorithm

1
, Step 2 must finish before Step 4 can start, and when Step 4 is executing, the memory used during the execution of the recursive call in Step 2 can be freed. During the execution of a recursive call of size
n/2^{k}
, all operations, including the polynomial multiplications, require a storage space of size bounded by
Cn/2^{k}
, for some constant
C
independent of
n
and
k
. Thus, the maximum space required to run
Algorithm

1
is bounded by

(42)

C(n+n/2+n/4+\dots)\leq 2Cn,

which shows that the space complexity of the algorithm is
\mathcal{O}(n)
.

5.
Numerically stable inverse NLFT

This section discusses the numerical stability of the two inverse NLFT algorithms. In
Section

5.1
, we briefly review the notions of forward and backward numerical stability. Next, in
Section

5.2
, we use examples to demonstrate that the outerness condition of
a^{*}
is crucial for numerical stability. We then establish the relationship between NLFT and the Gaussian elimination process for a matrix with displacement structure in
Section

5.3
, which plays an important role in the subsequent stability proofs. Finally, we present the stability proofs of the layer stripping algorithm and the inverse nonlinear FFT algorithm in
Section

5.4
and
Section

5.5
, respectively.

5.1.
Background on floating point arithmetic and error analysis

We briefly review some fundamental concepts from numerical error analysis; for comprehensive discussions, we refer readers to
[
Hig02
]
. To analyze the numerical stability of our algorithm, we adopt the standard model of floating-point arithmetic. Specifically, for any basic arithmetic operation
\circ
, the computed result
\mathrm{fl}(a\circ b)
satisfies

\mathrm{fl}(a\circ b)=(a\circ b)(1+\delta),\quad|\delta|\leq\epsilon_{\mathrm{ma}},

where
\epsilon_{\mathrm{ma}}
denotes the machine precision.

Consider an algorithm whose exact output is
x
, and suppose the target precision is
\epsilon
. We say the algorithm has a bit requirement
r
if, when using floating point arithmetic with
\epsilon_{\mathrm{ma}}=2^{-r}
, the computed output
\hat{x}
satisfies
\frac{\|x-\hat{x}\|}{\left\lVert x\right\rVert}\leq\epsilon
. In practice, we prefer algorithms that operate reliably under fixed precision regardless of the problem size, such as
r=53
for IEEE double precision. However, in the worst case, numerical error may accumulate, making this goal theoretically unattainable. We say an algorithm is
numerically stable
if the bit requirement is
r=\mathcal{O}(\operatorname{polylog}(n,1/\epsilon))
, where
n
denotes the problem size. In practice, such algorithms tend to perform robustly using standard double precision. On the other hand, an algorithm is numerically unstable if the bit requirement of an algorithm is at least
r=\Omega(\poly(n))
, in which case the error would accumulate and blow up rapidly for moderate values of
n
under standard double precision arithmetic in practice.

Numerical stability is typically assessed via forward and backward error analysis. The forward error measures the deviation of the computed solution
\hat{x}
from the exact solution
x
, while the backward error reflects the smallest perturbation to the input that would make
\hat{x}
an exact solution. For example, when solving a linear system
Ax=b
, assuming
A,b
are non-zero, we have:

(43)

Forward (relative) error

\displaystyle:=\frac{\|\hat{x}-x\|}{\|x\|},

(44)

Backward (relative) error

\displaystyle:=\min_{\Delta A}\left\{\frac{\|\Delta A\|}{\|A\|}:(A+\Delta A)\hat{x}=b\right\},

for some choice of norms (usually the
2
-norm). While forward error bounds are often the ultimate goal, backward error bounds are generally easier to derive. In the case of linear systems, forward and backward errors are linked by the condition number
\kappa(A):=\|A\|\|A^{-1}\|
, with the forward error bounded by the product of the condition number and the backward error
[
Hig02
, Theorem 7.2]
. We provide the formal version of this sensitive analysis statement in the following lemma.

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
Ax=b
and
(A+\Delta A)y=b+\Delta b
, and assume
\kappa(A)\frac{\left\lVert\Delta A\right\rVert}{\left\lVert A\right\rVert}<\frac{1}{2}
and
b\neq 0
. Then

\frac{\|x-y\|}{\|x\|}\leq 2\kappa(A)\left(\frac{\left\lVert\Delta b\right\rVert}{\|b\|}+\frac{\left\lVert\Delta A\right\rVert}{\left\lVert A\right\rVert}\right).

Proof.

In
[
Hig02
, Theorem 7.2]
, we may choose
\epsilon=1
,
E=\Delta A
,
f=\Delta b
and obtain

(45)

\frac{\|x-y\|}{\|x\|}\leq\frac{1}{1-\kappa(A)\frac{\left\lVert\Delta A\right\rVert}{\left\lVert A\right\rVert}}\left(\frac{\kappa(A)\|\Delta b\|}{\left\lVert A\right\rVert\|x\|}+\left\|A^{-1}\right\|\|\Delta A\|\right)\leq 2\left(\kappa(A)\frac{\|\Delta b\|}{\left\lVert b\right\rVert}+\kappa(A)\frac{\left\lVert\Delta A\right\rVert}{\left\lVert A\right\rVert}\right).

∎

5.3.
Inverse NLFT via Gauss elimination

Fast algorithms for solving linear systems with structured coefficient matrices have been extensively studied. At first glance, these methods may appear unrelated to the layer stripping procedure. However, an algorithm known as Schur’s algorithm, which is used for inverting matrices with displacement structure, is closely connected to the layer stripping method discussed here. In the following sections, we explore this surprising connection and demonstrate how it facilitates the stability analysis of layer stripping and the inverse nonlinear FFT algorithm.

We begin by defining a matrix
K:=T(\mathbf{a}_{0})T(\mathbf{a}_{0})^{*}+T(\mathbf{b}_{0})T(\mathbf{b}_{0})^{*}
, where
T(\mathbf{a}_{0})
is the lower triangular Toeplitz matrix

(46)

T(\mathbf{a}_{0}):=\begin{pmatrix}a_{0,0}&&&&\\
a_{1,0}&a_{0,0}&&&\\
a_{2,0}&a_{1,0}&a_{0,0}&&\\
\vdots&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&\\
a_{n-1,0}&\cdots&a_{2,0}&a_{1,0}&a_{0,0}\end{pmatrix},

with first column
\mathbf{a}_{0}=(a_{0,0},a_{1,0},\ldots,a_{n-1,0})^{T}
. It follows from
[
KKM79
, Lemma 2]
that
K
satisfies the identity

(47)

K-Z_{n}KZ_{n}^{*}=G_{0}G_{0}^{*}=\mathbf{a}_{0}\mathbf{a}_{0}^{*}+\mathbf{b}_{0}\mathbf{b}_{0}^{*},\quad Z_{n}:=\begin{pmatrix}0&&&\\
1&0&&\\
&\ddots&\ddots&\\
&&1&0\end{pmatrix}_{n\times n},

where
Z_{n}
is the lower shift matrix. The relationship
Eq.

47
is called the
displacement low-rank structure
of
K
, or the displacement structure for short. The
displacement rank
is defined as the rank of the right-hand side matrix
G_{0}G_{0}^{\ast}
, which is at most two. For a displacement low-rank matrix, the generalized Schur algorithm
[
KS95
]
can perform the
LDL^{*}
factorization of it in
\mathcal{O}(n^{2})
time, where
L
is a lower-triangular matrix with ones on the diagonal and
D
is a diagonal matrix (see
[
GVL13
, Section 4.2.6]
for a discussion on the
LDL^{\ast}
factorization). Moreover, the
LDL^{\ast}
factorization of
K
is unique as
K
is non-singular, since
T(\mathbf{a}_{0})
is also non-singular in
Eq.

46
(recall that
a_{0,0}>0
from
Lemma

4.1
(a)). We will briefly review the algorithm for our specific
K
and find that it essentially performs layer stripping for the sequences
\mathbf{a}_{0}
and
\mathbf{b}_{0}
.

When we determine
\gamma_{0}=\frac{b_{0,0}}{a_{0,0}}
and multiply a rotation matrix to the right of
(\mathbf{a}_{0},\mathbf{b}_{0})
as in
Eq.

33
, we obtain an
n\times 2
matrix

(48)

\begin{pmatrix}a_{0,1}&0\\
\mathbf{u}&\mathbf{b}_{1}\end{pmatrix}:=\begin{pmatrix}a_{0,1}&0\\
a_{1,1}&b_{0,1}\\
\vdots&\vdots\\
a_{n-1,1}&b_{n-2,1}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{a}_{0}&\mathbf{b}_{0}\end{pmatrix}\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+|\gamma_{0}|^{2}}}\begin{pmatrix}1&-\gamma_{0}\\
\overline{\gamma_{0}}&1\end{pmatrix},

where the column vector
\mathbf{u}=[a_{1,1},a_{2,1},\ldots,a_{n-1,1}]^{T}
. Plugging this into
Eq.

47
, we get

(49)

K-Z_{n}KZ_{n}^{*}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{a}_{0}&\mathbf{b}_{0}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{a}_{0}^{*}\\
\mathbf{b}_{0}^{*}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}a_{0,1}&0\\
\mathbf{u}&\mathbf{b}_{1}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\overline{a_{0,1}}&\mathbf{u}^{*}\\
0&\mathbf{b}_{1}^{*}\end{pmatrix},

From this, we may recover the first column of
K
as
\begin{pmatrix}\left|a_{0,1}\right|^{2}\\
\overline{a_{0,1}}\mathbf{u}\end{pmatrix}
, and therefore the first step of the
LDL^{*}
decomposition of
K
is

(50)

K=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
\mathbf{u}/a_{0,1}&I\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\left|a_{0,1}\right|^{2}&0\\
0&K_{1}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1&\mathbf{u}^{*}/\overline{a_{0,1}}\\
0&I\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\left|a_{0,1}\right|^{2}&a_{0,1}\mathbf{u}^{*}\\
\overline{a_{0,1}}\mathbf{u}&\mathbf{u}\mathbf{u}^{*}+K_{1}\end{pmatrix},

where
K_{1}
is the first Schur complement of
K
. If we substitute this expression into
Eq.

49
, we obtain

\begin{pmatrix}\left|a_{0,1}\right|^{2}&a_{0,1}\mathbf{u}^{*}\\
\overline{a_{0,1}}\mathbf{u}&\mathbf{u}\mathbf{u}^{*}+K_{1}\end{pmatrix}-\begin{pmatrix}0&\\
\mathbf{e}_{0}&Z_{n-1}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\left|a_{0,1}\right|^{2}&a_{0,1}\mathbf{u}^{*}\\
\overline{a_{0,1}}\mathbf{u}&\mathbf{u}\mathbf{u}^{*}+K_{1}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}0&\mathbf{e}_{0}^{*}\\
&Z_{n-1}^{*}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\left|a_{0,1}\right|^{2}&a_{0,1}\mathbf{u}^{*}\\
\overline{a_{0,1}}\mathbf{u}&\mathbf{u}\mathbf{u}^{*}+\mathbf{b}_{1}\mathbf{b}_{1}^{*}\end{pmatrix},

where
\mathbf{e}_{0}
is the unit vector with the first element
1
. By comparing the lower-right block, we deduce that
K_{1}
satisfies

(51)

K_{1}-Z_{n-1}K_{1}Z_{n-1}^{*}=(a_{0,1}\mathbf{e}_{0}+Z_{n-1}\mathbf{u})(a_{0,1}\mathbf{e}_{0}+Z_{n-1}\mathbf{u})^{*}+\mathbf{b}_{1}\mathbf{b}_{1}^{*}=\mathbf{a}_{1}\mathbf{a}_{1}^{*}+\mathbf{b}_{1}\mathbf{b}_{1}^{*},

where
a_{0,1}\mathbf{e}_{0}+Z_{n-1}\mathbf{u}=\mathbf{a}_{1}
is obtained from
Eq.

48
. This equation is in the same form as
Eq.

47
; therefore, we can perform this elimination step iteratively as in
Eq.

50
. Finally, we can express
K=LDL^{*}
, where

(52)

L=\begin{pmatrix}1&&&&\\
\frac{a_{1,1}}{a_{0,1}}&1&&&\\
\frac{a_{2,1}}{a_{0,1}}&\frac{a_{1,2}}{a_{0,2}}&1&&\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\\
\frac{a_{n-1,1}}{a_{0,1}}&\frac{a_{n-2,2}}{a_{0,2}}&\frac{a_{n-3,3}}{a_{0,3}}&\cdots&1\end{pmatrix},\ D=\begin{pmatrix}\left|a_{0,1}\right|^{2}&&&&\\
&\left|a_{0,2}\right|^{2}&&&\\
&&\left|a_{0,3}\right|^{2}&&\\
&&&\ddots&\\
&&&&\left|a_{0,n}\right|^{2}\end{pmatrix}.

The absolute value notation in
D
emphasizes its positive definiteness, although by construction we already have
a_{0,k}>0
for all
k
.

The significance of relating layer stripping with the displacement structure and the Cholesky factorization lies in establishing the following lemma, which plays a central role in the stability proofs presented in the subsequent subsections.

Lemma 5.2
.

Let
L
be as defined in
Eq.

52
. Then the NLFT coefficient vector
\boldsymbol{\gamma}
satisfies

(53)

L\boldsymbol{\gamma}=\frac{1}{a_{00}}\mathbf{b}_{0}.

Proof.

We prove the lemma by induction. When
n=1
, the lemma holds since
L=1
and
\gamma_{0}=\frac{b_{0,0}}{a_{0,0}}
.

Assuming the lemma holds for the
n-1
case, let us consider the lemma for
n
. For convenience, we introduce some notations

(54)

\mathbf{a}_{+,1}:=\begin{pmatrix}a_{1,1}\\
\vdots\\
a_{n-1,1}\end{pmatrix},\ \mathbf{b}_{+,0}:=\begin{pmatrix}b_{1,0}\\
\vdots\\
b_{n-1,0}\end{pmatrix},\ \boldsymbol{\gamma}_{+}:=\begin{pmatrix}\gamma_{1}\\
\vdots\\
\gamma_{n-1}\end{pmatrix},\ \Theta(\gamma_{k}):=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+|\gamma_{k}|^{2}}}\begin{pmatrix}1&-\gamma_{k}\\
\overline{\gamma_{k}}&1\end{pmatrix}.

We denote
L_{1}\in\mathbb{C}^{(n-1)\times(n-1)}
be the matrix containing the last
n-1
rows and columns of
L
. After calculating
\gamma_{0}
, the remaining procedure is reduced to the layer stripping process starting from
(\mathbf{a}_{1},\mathbf{b}_{1})
. First, by the induction hypothesis, we have

(55)

L_{1}\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{+}=\frac{1}{a_{0,1}}\mathbf{b}_{1}.

Next, from
Eq.

48
we know that for
1\leq k\leq n-1
,

(56)

(a_{k,1},b_{k-1,1})\Theta(-\gamma_{0})=(a_{k,0},b_{k,0}).

The second component of this formula gives

(57)

\gamma_{0}a_{k,1}+b_{k-1,1}=\sqrt{1+\left|\gamma_{0}\right|^{2}}b_{k,0}=\frac{a_{0,1}}{a_{0,0}}b_{k,0},

where the last equality derives from
\quad a_{0,1}=\sqrt{1+\left|\gamma_{0}\right|^{2}}a_{0,0},
which is also due to
Eq.

48
. We may collect the equations for
1\leq k\leq n-1
and write them in vector form to obtain

\frac{\gamma_{0}}{a_{0,1}}\mathbf{a}_{+,1}+\frac{1}{a_{0,1}}\mathbf{b}_{1}=\frac{1}{a_{0,0}}\mathbf{b}_{+,0}.

Together with
Eq.

55
, we then have

(58)

\frac{\gamma_{0}}{a_{0,1}}\mathbf{a}_{+,1}+L_{1}\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{+}=\frac{1}{a_{0,0}}\mathbf{b}_{+,0},

which can be written in the block matrix form

\begin{pmatrix}1&\\
\frac{\mathbf{a}_{+,1}}{a_{0,1}}&L_{1}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\gamma_{0}\\
\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{+}\end{pmatrix}=\frac{1}{a_{0,0}}\begin{pmatrix}{b_{0,0}}\\
{\mathbf{b}_{+,0}}\end{pmatrix}.

This proves
L\boldsymbol{\gamma}=\frac{1}{a_{00}}\mathbf{b}_{0}
and completes the induction step.
∎

5.4.
Proof of stability for the layer stripping algorithm

Lemma

5.2
suggests an approach to prove the stability of the layer stripping algorithm through backward error estimation. In contrast, prior work
[
Haa19
]
utilized forward error analysis to show that the error in the layer stripping algorithm can be controlled when
\mathcal{O}(n)
bits are used. However, this result is unsatisfactory as it does not establish the algorithm’s stability under the usual constraint of using only
\mathcal{O}(\polylog(n))
bits. In this work, we present a proof of the algorithm’s stability under the condition that
a_{0}^{*}(z)
has no zeros in
\overline{\mathbb{D}}
.

The following
Lemma

5.3
compares the output of the layer stripping process with the solution of the linear system in
Eq.

53
. We show that the computed solution
\hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}
is the exact solution to a slightly perturbed system, which is in the spirit of a backward stability result. Then, in
Lemma

5.4
, we establish the well-conditionedness of
Eq.

53
. Finally, these results are combined into the overall stability statement in
Theorem

5.5
.

We use the notation
\hat{\cdot}
to indicate quantities computed with round-off errors in floating-point arithmetic during the layer stripping procedure. We remark that discussing
\hat{L}
directly is ambiguous, since
L
is not explicitly formed during the algorithm. However, we introduce the matrices

(59)

U=\begin{pmatrix}a_{0,1}&&&\\
a_{1,1}&a_{0,2}&&\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\\
a_{n-1,1}&a_{n-2,2}&\cdots&a_{0,n}\end{pmatrix},\ H=\begin{pmatrix}a_{0,1}&&&\\
&a_{0,2}&&\\
&&\ddots&\\

and

. We use
\hat{U}
and

to denote the computed counterparts of

Lemma 5.3
.

Let

n
coefficients of the polynomial
a_{0}^{*}(z)v(z)
. As a consequence of Parseval’s identity, we can estimate

(69)

\left\lVert T(\mathbf{a}_{0})\mathbf{v}\right\rVert\leq\left\lVert a_{0}^{*}(z)v(z)\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T})}\leq\left\lVert a_{0}^{*}(z)\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T})}\left\lVert v(z)\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T})}=\left\lVert a_{0}^{*}(z)\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T})}\left\lVert\mathbf{v}\right\rVert.

Therefore, we obtain
\left\lVert T(\mathbf{a}_{0})\right\rVert\leq\left\lVert a_{0}^{*}(z)\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T})}\leq 1
, and also have
\left\lVert T(\mathbf{b}_{0})\right\rVert\leq\left\lVert b_{0}(z)\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T})}\leq 1-\eta
for the same reason. Then we may conclude

\lambda_{\max}(K)=\left\lVert K\right\rVert\leq\left\lVert T(\mathbf{a}_{0})\right\rVert^{2}+\left\lVert T(\mathbf{b}_{0})\right\rVert^{2}\leq 2-2\eta+\eta^{2}\leq 2-\eta.

For the lower bound, we notice that the power series
a_{0}^{*}(z)^{-1}=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}w_{j}z^{j}
converges for
z\in\mathbb{T}
since
a_{0}^{*}(z)
never vanishes in the closed unit disc due to
Lemma

2.3
. We may check that
T([w_{0},\ldots,w_{n-1}]^{T})
is the inverse matrix of
T(\mathbf{a}_{0})
. Using the same argument as above, we can also bound

\left\lVert T(\mathbf{a}_{0})^{-1}\mathbf{v}\right\rVert\leq\left\lVert a_{0}^{*}(z)^{-1}v(z)\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T})}\leq\left\lVert a_{0}^{*}(z)^{-1}\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T})}\left\lVert v(z)\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T})}=\left\lVert a_{0}^{*}(z)^{-1}\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T})}\left\lVert\mathbf{v}\right\rVert.

From the relationship
\left|a_{0}^{*}(z)\right|^{2}+\left|b_{0}(z)\right|^{2}=1
on the unit circle
\mathbb{T}
, we obtain

\min_{z\in\mathbb{T}}|a_{0}^{*}(z)|\geq\sqrt{1-(1-\eta)^{2}}=\sqrt{\eta(2-\eta)},

which means

\lambda_{\min}(K)\geq\lambda_{\min}(T(\mathbf{a}_{0})T(\mathbf{a}_{0})^{*})=\min_{\mathbf{v}\neq 0}\frac{\left\lVert\mathbf{v}\right\rVert^{2}}{\left\lVert T(\mathbf{a}_{0})^{-1}\mathbf{v}\right\rVert^{2}}\geq\frac{1}{\left\lVert a_{0}^{*}(z)^{-1}\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T})}^{2}}=\min_{z\in\mathbb{T}}|a_{0}^{*}(z)|^{2}\geq\eta(2-\eta).

Regarding the norm of
L
and
L^{-1}
, we have the lower bound
\left\lVert L\right\rVert\geq 1
and
\left\lVert L^{-1}\right\rVert\geq 1
since the diagonal elements of them are all
1
. Now recall that
H=\sqrt{D}
and
U=LH
, which implies
K=UU^{*}
. Then we may get bounds on the singular values of
U
,

\sqrt{\eta(2-\eta)}\leq\sqrt{\lambda_{\min}(K)}=\sigma_{\min}(U)\leq\sigma_{\max}(U)=\sqrt{\lambda_{\max}(K)}\leq\sqrt{2-\eta}.

Since
H=\mathrm{diag}\{a_{0,1},\ldots,a_{0,n}\}
is the diagonal part of
U
, we have

\left\lVert H\right\rVert=\max_{k}\{a_{0,k}\}\leq\sigma_{\max}(U)\leq\sqrt{2-\eta}.

As
U
is lower diagonal,
H^{-1}
is also the diagonal part of
U^{-1}
, so similarly

\left\lVert H^{-1}\right\rVert=\max_{k}\{a_{0,k}^{-1}\}\leq\sigma_{\max}(U^{-1})\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{\eta(2-\eta)}}.

Therefore, we have the estimates

\left\lVert L\right\rVert=\left\lVert UH^{-1}\right\rVert\leq\left\lVert U\right\rVert\left\lVert H^{-1}\right\rVert\leq\sqrt{2-\eta}\cdot\frac{1}{\sqrt{\eta(2-\eta)}}=\eta^{-1/2},

\left\lVert L^{-1}\right\rVert=\left\lVert HU^{-1}\right\rVert\leq\left\lVert H\right\rVert\left\lVert U^{-1}\right\rVert\leq\sqrt{2-\eta}\cdot\frac{1}{\sqrt{\eta(2-\eta)}}=\eta^{-1/2},

\left\lVert(DL^{*})^{-1}\right\rVert=\left\lVert(HU^{*})^{-1}\right\rVert\leq\left\lVert U^{-1}\right\rVert\left\lVert H^{-1}\right\rVert\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{\eta(2-\eta)}}\cdot\frac{1}{\sqrt{\eta(2-\eta)}}=\frac{1}{\eta(2-\eta)}.

The bound also holds for
L_{\mathcal{K}}
since the norm of a submatrix is bounded by the norms of the original matrix. The bounds for
(L_{\mathcal{K}})^{-1}
and
((DL^{*})_{\mathcal{K}})^{-1}
hold since
(L_{\mathcal{K}})^{-1}=(L^{-1})_{\mathcal{K}}
and
((DL^{*})_{\mathcal{K}})^{-1}=((DL^{*})^{-1})_{\mathcal{K}}
as triangular matrices.
∎

Theorem 5.5
.

Assume
(a_{0},b_{0})\in\mathcal{S}_{\eta}
, and
a_{0}^{*}(z)
is outer. Suppose further that
n^{3}\eta^{-5/2}\epsilon_{\mathrm{ma}}=\mathcal{O}(1)
. Then, for the layer stripping algorithm, the relative error satisfies

(70)

\frac{\left\lVert\hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}-\boldsymbol{\gamma}\right\rVert}{\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\gamma}\right\rVert}=\mathcal{O}(n^{3}\eta^{-5/2}\epsilon_{\mathrm{ma}}).

In particular, to achieve a target precision
\epsilon
, it suffices to use
\mathcal{O}(\log(n\eta^{-1}\epsilon^{-1}))
bits of precision when implementing the layer stripping algorithm in floating-point arithmetic.

Proof.

As
K=L(DL^{*})=(UH^{-1})(HU^{*})
, we know that
(\hat{U}\hat{H}^{-1})(\hat{H}\hat{U}^{*})
is an approximation of the LU factorization of
K
. From the proof of
[
NY24
, Theorem 3]
, we have an error bound

(71)

\left\lVert(\hat{U}\hat{H}^{-1})(\hat{H}\hat{U}^{*})-K\right\rVert_{F}=\left\lVert\hat{U}\hat{U}^{*}-K\right\rVert_{F}=\mathcal{O}(n^{3})\epsilon_{\mathrm{ma}}\left\lVert G_{0}\right\rVert_{F}^{2}=\mathcal{O}(n^{3}\epsilon_{\mathrm{ma}}),

as
\left\lVert G_{0}\right\rVert_{F}^{2}=1
from
Lemma

4.1
(b).
Therefore, by the bounds from
Lemma

5.4
, we obtain

\left\lVert L^{-1}\right\rVert\left\lVert(DL^{*})^{-1}\right\rVert\left\lVert(\hat{U}\hat{H}^{-1})(\hat{H}\hat{U}^{*})-K\right\rVert_{F}\leq\eta^{-1/2}\frac{1}{\eta(2-\eta)}\mathcal{O}(n^{3}\epsilon_{\mathrm{ma}})=\mathcal{O}(n^{3}\eta^{-3/2}\epsilon_{\mathrm{ma}}).

To guarantee this quantity is no more than
\frac{1}{2}
, we need the assumption that
\mathcal{O}(n^{3}\eta^{-3/2}\epsilon_{\mathrm{ma}})
is less than some constant threshold.
By the sensitivity analysis of LU factorization
[
Hig02
, Theorem 9.15]
we deduce that

(72)

\displaystyle\frac{\left\lVert\hat{U}\hat{H}^{-1}-L\right\rVert}{\left\lVert L\right\rVert}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\left\lVert L^{-1}\right\rVert\left\lVert(DL^{*})^{-1}\right\rVert\left\lVert(\hat{U}\hat{H}^{-1})(\hat{H}\hat{U}^{*})-K\right\rVert_{F}}{1-\left\lVert L^{-1}\right\rVert\left\lVert(DL^{*})^{-1}\right\rVert\left\lVert(\hat{U}\hat{H}^{-1})(\hat{H}\hat{U}^{*})-K\right\rVert_{F}}\frac{\left\lVert K\right\rVert_{2}}{\left\lVert K\right\rVert_{F}}

\displaystyle\leq 2\cdot\mathcal{O}(n^{3}\eta^{-3/2}\epsilon_{\mathrm{ma}})\cdot 1=\mathcal{O}(n^{3}\eta^{-3/2}\epsilon_{\mathrm{ma}}).

Then we may convert the elementwise bound in
Lemma

5.3
into a 2-norm bound

(73)

\left\lVert E\right\rVert\leq\left\lVert E\right\rVert_{F}\leq\mathcal{O}(n\epsilon_{\mathrm{ma}})\left\lVert\hat{U}\hat{H}^{-1}\right\rVert_{F}\leq\mathcal{O}(n^{2}\epsilon_{\mathrm{ma}})\left\lVert\hat{U}\hat{H}^{-1}\right\rVert\leq\mathcal{O}(n^{2}\epsilon_{\mathrm{ma}})\left\lVert L\right\rVert,

where we used
\left\lVert\hat{U}\hat{H}^{-1}\right\rVert=\mathcal{O}(1)\left\lVert L\right\rVert
implied from
Eq.

72
. Denote
\widetilde{L}:=\hat{U}\hat{H}^{-1}+E
in
Lemma

5.3
, then we have

(74)

\widetilde{L}\hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}=\frac{1}{a_{0,0}}\mathbf{b}_{0}=L\boldsymbol{\gamma}

and

(75)

\frac{\left\lVert\widetilde{L}-L\right\rVert}{\left\lVert L\right\rVert}\leq\frac{\left\lVert E\right\rVert+\left\lVert\hat{U}\hat{H}^{-1}-L\right\rVert}{\left\lVert L\right\rVert}\leq\mathcal{O}(n^{2}\epsilon_{\mathrm{ma}})+\mathcal{O}(n^{3}\eta^{-3/2}\epsilon_{\mathrm{ma}})=\mathcal{O}(n^{3}\eta^{-3/2}\epsilon_{\mathrm{ma}}).

Here we need the assumption
n^{3}\eta^{-5/2}\epsilon_{\mathrm{ma}}=\mathcal{O}(1)
again to guarantee
\frac{\left\lVert\widetilde{L}-L\right\rVert}{\left\lVert L\right\rVert}\leq\frac{1}{2}
, and get the forward error estimate by the sensitivity of the linear system stated in
Lemma

5.1
.

(76)

\frac{\left\lVert\hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}-\boldsymbol{\gamma}\right\rVert}{\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\gamma}\right\rVert}\leq 2\left\lVert L\right\rVert\left\lVert L^{-1}\right\rVert\left(0+\frac{\left\lVert\widetilde{L}-L\right\rVert}{\left\lVert L\right\rVert}\right)=\mathcal{O}(n^{3}\eta^{-5/2}\epsilon_{\mathrm{ma}}),

where we used the estimates for
\left\lVert L\right\rVert
and
\left\lVert L^{-1}\right\rVert
from
Lemma

5.4
in the last step.
∎

Lemma 5.10
.

5.7

(128)

Define an operator
\mathcal{A}
on the space of matrices
\mathbb{C}^{m\times m}
as

\mathcal{A}:X\mapsto X-ZXZ^{*},

and the operator norm associated with the matrix Frobenius norm as

\left\lVert\mathcal{A}\right\rVert_{\mathrm{op}(F)}:=\max_{\left\lVert X\right\rVert_{F}=1}\left\lVert\mathcal{A}(X)\right\rVert_{F}.

If we flatten the matrix
X
as a vector of length
m^{2}
, then this operator has a matrix form
\mathcal{A}=I-Z\otimes Z
. The Frobenius norm of

is the same as the 2 norm of the flattened vector, so the induced operator norm of
\mathcal{A}^{-1}

\left\lVert\mathcal{A}^{-1}\right\rVert_{\mathrm{op}(F)}=\left\lVert(I-Z\otimes Z)^{-1}\right\rVert_{2}=\left\lVert\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}Z^{j}\otimes Z^{j}\right\rVert_{2}\leq m

\mathcal{A}

By definition, we have

and
\widetilde{K}_{\mathrm{down}}=\mathcal{A}^{-1}(\hat{G}_{m}\hat{G}_{m}^{*})
, so the following estimate holds.

\displaystyle\left\lVert K_{\mathrm{down}}-\widetilde{K}_{\mathrm{down}}\right\rVert_{F}

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert\mathcal{A}^{-1}\right\rVert_{\mathrm{op}(F)}\left\lVert G_{m}G_{m}^{*}-\hat{G}_{m}\hat{G}_{m}^{*}\right\rVert_{F}

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert\mathcal{A}^{-1}\right\rVert_{\mathrm{op}(F)}\left(\left\lVert G_{m}\right\rVert+\left\lVert\hat{G}_{m}\right\rVert_{F}\right)\left\lVert G_{m}-\hat{G}_{m}\right\rVert_{F}

\displaystyle\leq 2m\left\lVert G_{m}-\hat{G}_{m}\right\rVert_{F}.

As
L_{\mathrm{down}}\cdot(D_{\mathrm{down}}L_{\mathrm{down}}^{*})
is the LU factorization of
K_{\mathrm{down}}
, we have the error estimation
[
Hig02
, Theorem 9.15]

(129)

\displaystyle\left\lVert\widetilde{L}_{\mathrm{down}}-L_{\mathrm{down}}\right\rVert_{F}

\displaystyle\leq C_{1}\left\lVert L_{\mathrm{down}}\right\rVert\left\lVert L_{\mathrm{down}}^{-1}\right\rVert\left\lVert(D_{\mathrm{down}}L_{\mathrm{down}}^{*})^{-1}\right\rVert\frac{\left\lVert K_{\mathrm{down}}\right\rVert}{\left\lVert K_{\mathrm{down}}\right\rVert_{F}}\left\lVert\widetilde{K}_{\mathrm{down}}-K_{\mathrm{down}}\right\rVert_{F}

\displaystyle\leq C_{1}\left\lVert L_{\mathrm{down}}\right\rVert\eta^{-\frac{1}{2}}\frac{1}{\eta(2-\eta)}\cdot 1\cdot\left\lVert\widetilde{K}_{\mathrm{down}}-K_{\mathrm{down}}\right\rVert_{F}

\displaystyle\leq 2C_{1}m\eta^{-\frac{3}{2}}\left\lVert L_{\mathrm{down}}\right\rVert\left\lVert G_{m}-\hat{G}_{m}\right\rVert_{F}.

Here we used the estimation of
L_{\mathrm{down}}^{-1},(D_{\mathrm{down}}L_{\mathrm{down}}^{*})^{-1}
from
Lemma

5.4
, since they are the submatrices of the triangular matrices
L^{-1}
and
(DL^{*})^{-1}
, respectively. Finally, we use
Eq.

113
to conclude this lemma.
∎

6.
Locally Lipschitz estimates for inverse NLFT

In this section, we derive some continuity estimates for NLFT and inverse NLFT for the compactly supported case in the
\mathrm{SU}(2)
setting, which establishes continuity of the maps avoiding the use of infinite dimensional function spaces (cf.
[
Tsa05
, Theorem 2.5, Lemma 3.10]
). In particular, we will show that the NLFT map is Lipschitz continuous, and the inverse NLFT map, while it fails to be uniformly continuous, is locally Lipschitz continuous. The nature of the locally Lipschitz estimate then allows us to identify conditions under which the inverse NLFT map is Lipschitz. In all cases, the domains and codomains of the maps will be endowed with the standard Euclidean topology.

We will suppose
\boldsymbol{\gamma},\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{\prime}\in\ell(0,n-1)
with
\overbrace{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}:=(a,b)
and
\overbrace{\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{\prime}}:=(a^{\prime},b^{\prime})
, and we will also assume throughout that
n\geq 2
. Let us collect the coefficients of the Laurent polynomials
a,a^{\prime},b,b^{\prime}
into vectors
\mathbf{a},\mathbf{a}^{\prime},\mathbf{b},\mathbf{b}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
(refer to
Lemma

2.1
why they are
n
dimensional vectors). Thus, for example, we have
\mathbf{a}:=(a_{0},\dots,a_{n-1})
and
\mathbf{b}:=(b_{0},\dots,b_{n-1})
, where
a=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}a_{k}z^{-k}
and
b=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}b_{k}z^{k}
. Note that this choice deviates from the definitions of
a_{k}
and
b_{k}
in
Section

4
, but this streamlines the presentation in this section. Then the NLFT map
\boldsymbol{\gamma}\mapsto(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b})
can be regarded as a map from
\mathbb{C}^{\ast}\times\mathbb{C}^{n-2}\times\mathbb{C}^{\ast}

to
\mathbb{C}^{n}\times\mathbb{C}^{n}
. In the next lemma, we establish Lipschitz estimates for the NLFT map, where one merely thinks of the image of the NLFT map as elements of
\mathbb{C}^{n}\times\mathbb{C}^{n}
.

Lemma 6.1
.

Let
p,q,r\geq 1
. Then the following estimate holds:

(130)

n^{-\frac{1}{p}}\left\lVert\mathbf{a}-\mathbf{a}^{\prime}\right\rVert_{p}+n^{-\frac{1}{q}}\left\lVert\mathbf{b}-\mathbf{b}^{\prime}\right\rVert_{q}\leq 6n^{\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{r}}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\gamma}-\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{\prime}\right\rVert_{r}.

Thus the NLFT map
\mathbb{C}^{\ast}\times\mathbb{C}^{n-1}\times\mathbb{C}^{\ast}\ni\boldsymbol{\gamma}\mapsto(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b})\in\mathbb{C}^{n}\times\mathbb{C}^{n}
is Lipschitz continuous.

Proof.

Let
\mathtt{T}(z),\mathtt{T}^{\prime}(z)
be the
\mathrm{SU}(2)
matrices corresponding to
(a(z),b(z))
and
(a^{\prime}(z),b^{\prime}(z))
respectively, for
z\in\mathbb{T}
. From
[
Tsa05
, Theorem 2.5]
, we have
\left\lVert\mathtt{T}(z)-\mathtt{T}^{\prime}(z)\right\rVert_{2}\leq 3\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\gamma}-\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{\prime}\right\rVert_{1}
. We also have
\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\gamma}-\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{\prime}\right\rVert_{1}\leq n^{1-\frac{1}{r}}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\gamma}-\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{\prime}\right\rVert_{r}
, by the power mean inequality. This establishes the result

(131)

\sup_{z\in\mathbb{T}}\left\lVert\mathtt{T}(z)-\mathtt{T}^{\prime}(z)\right\rVert_{2}\leq 3n^{1-\frac{1}{r}}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\gamma}-\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{\prime}\right\rVert_{r}.

So we only need to prove the direction

(132)

\sup_{z\in\mathbb{T}}\left\lVert\mathtt{T}(z)-\mathtt{T}^{\prime}(z)\right\rVert_{2}\geq\frac{1}{2}\left(n^{\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{p}}\left\lVert\mathbf{a}-\mathbf{a}^{\prime}\right\rVert_{p}+n^{\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{q}}\left\lVert\mathbf{b}-\mathbf{b}^{\prime}\right\rVert_{q}\right).

For this, we recall that for every
z\in\mathbb{T}
, we have
\left\lVert\mathtt{T}(z)-\mathtt{T}^{\prime}(z)\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\geq\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert\mathtt{T}(z)-\mathtt{T}^{\prime}(z)\right\rVert_{F}^{2}
. Next, noting that for any
z\in\mathbb{T}
and a Laurent polynomial
s
, we have
|\overline{s(z)}|=|s^{\ast}(z)|
, we may deduce that
\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert\mathtt{T}(z)-\mathtt{T}^{\prime}(z)\right\rVert_{F}^{2}=|a(z)-a^{\prime}(z)|^{2}+|b(z)-b^{\prime}(z)|^{2}
. Putting everything together, we have

(133)

\begin{split}&\sup_{z\in\mathbb{T}}\left\lVert\mathtt{T}(z)-\mathtt{T}^{\prime}(z)\right\rVert_{2}\geq\sup_{z\in\mathbb{T}}\left(|a(z)-a^{\prime}(z)|^{2}+|b(z)-b^{\prime}(z)|^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\\
&\geq\frac{1}{2}\sup_{z\in\mathbb{T}}|a(z)-a^{\prime}(z)|+\frac{1}{2}\sup_{z\in\mathbb{T}}|b(z)-b^{\prime}(z)|\geq\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert a-a^{\prime}\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T})}+\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert b-b^{\prime}\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T})}\\
&=\frac{1}{2}\left(\left\lVert\mathbf{a}-\mathbf{a}^{\prime}\right\rVert_{2}+\left\lVert\mathbf{b}-\mathbf{b}^{\prime}\right\rVert_{2}\right)\geq\frac{1}{2}\left(n^{\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{p}}\left\lVert\mathbf{a}-\mathbf{a}^{\prime}\right\rVert_{p}+n^{\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{q}}\left\lVert\mathbf{b}-\mathbf{b}^{\prime}\right\rVert_{q}\right),\end{split}

where the last inequality is again due to the power mean inequality.
∎

We next simplify the characterization of the image of the NLFT map, as given in
Lemmas

2.1
and
2.2
, to the particular setting being discussed here for
\boldsymbol{\gamma}\in\ell(0,n-1)
. The image is a subset of
\mathbb{C}^{n}\times\mathbb{C}^{n}
, and it is given by

(134)

\mathtt{S}_{n}:=\{(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b})\in\mathbb{C}^{n}\times\mathbb{C}^{n}:\mathbb{R}\ni a_{0}>0,\;\text{xcor}(\mathbf{a},\overline{\mathbf{a}})+\text{xcor}(\mathbf{b},\overline{\mathbf{b}})=[\underbrace{0,\dots,0}_{n-1},1,\underbrace{0,\dots,0}_{n-1}]\;\},

where the
\text{xcor}(\cdot,\cdot)
function takes as input two length
n
vectors and outputs their aperiodic crosscorrelation vector of length
2n-1
(see
[
PM21
, Section 2.6]
for definition of crosscorrelation of finite length sequences). The condition
a_{0}>0
is the consequence of requiring that
a(\infty)>0
, while the second condition is equivalent to requiring
aa^{\ast}+bb^{\ast}=1
(see
Lemma

2.2
). If we impose the standard Euclidean topology on
\mathtt{S}_{n}
, then
a_{0}>0
implies that it is not complete as a metric space. In fact, this is not the only obstruction to completeness — we also know that
a_{n-1}
,
b_{0}
and
b_{n-1}
are all non-zero by
Lemma

2.1
, and so each of these is an obstruction to completeness as well. However,
\mathtt{S}_{n}
is a bounded subset of
\mathbb{C}^{n}\times\mathbb{C}^{n}
, and this follows from the second condition in
Eq.

134
which yields

(135)

\left\lVert\mathbf{a}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}+\left\lVert\mathbf{b}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}=1,\;\;\implies|a_{i}|\leq 1,\;|b_{i}|\leq 1,\;\forall i=0,\dots,n-1.

Notice that the condition
aa^{\ast}+bb^{\ast}=1
in
Lemma

2.2
implies that for all
z\in\mathbb{T}
, we have
|a^{\ast}(z)|^{2}+|b(z)|^{2}=1
, and so we may conclude that both
a^{\ast}
and
b
are Schur functions
[
Sch18
]
, which also leads to the same boundedness conclusion, namely
|a_{i}|\leq 1
and
|b_{i}|\leq 1
, by the property of Schur functions.

The inverse NLFT is a bijectve map from
\mathtt{S}_{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}^{\ast}\times\mathbb{C}^{n-2}\times\mathbb{C}^{\ast}
. Unlike the NLFT, we will next give an example showing that the inverse NLFT is not uniformly continuous when one equips
\mathtt{S}_{n}
with the Euclidean topology. In fact, such a statement already appears in the non-compactly supported setting in
[
Tsa05
, Page 31]
; so the contribution of this example is to show that the additional assumption of compact support does not change this fact. For example, we consider the following: define the sequence
\{(\mathbf{a}^{(k)},\mathbf{b}^{(k)})\in\mathtt{S}_{n}:k=2,3,\dots\}
such that

(136)

\mathbf{a}^{(k)}:=\left(\frac{1}{k},0,\dots,0,\sqrt{\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{k^{2}}}\right),\;\mathbf{b}^{(k)}:=\left(\sqrt{\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{k^{2}}},0,\dots,0,-\frac{1}{k}\right).

Let
\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{(k)}
be the inverse NLFT of
(\mathbf{a}^{(k)},\mathbf{b}^{(k)})
, i.e.
\overbrace{\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{(k)}}=(\mathbf{a}^{(k)},\mathbf{b}^{(k)})
. In this case the first component of
\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{(k)}
equals
(k^{2}/2-1)^{\frac{1}{2}}
by the layer stripping procedure (see
Section

4.1
). Thus, we may conclude that for all
k\geq 2
, we have

(137)

\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{(k+1)}-\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{(k)}\right\rVert_{2}\geq\sqrt{(k+1)^{2}/2-1}-\sqrt{k^{2}/2-1}\geq\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}.

On the other hand, we may also calculate for
k\geq 2
,

(138)

\begin{split}&\left\lVert\mathbf{a}^{(k+1)}-\mathbf{a}^{(k)}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}+\left\lVert\mathbf{b}^{(k+1)}-\mathbf{b}^{(k)}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}=2\left(\frac{1}{k^{2}+k}\right)^{2}+2\left(\sqrt{\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{(k+1)^{2}}}-\sqrt{\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{k^{2}}}\right)^{2}\\
&\leq\frac{2}{k^{4}}+2\left(\sqrt{\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{(k+1)^{2}}}-\sqrt{\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{k^{2}}}\right)^{2}\leq\frac{2}{k^{4}}+\frac{8}{k^{6}}\leq\frac{10}{k^{4}},\end{split}

which goes to zero as
k\rightarrow\infty
. This completes the argument for the nonexistence of uniform continuity of inverse NLFT, even for compactly supported sequences.

We now proceed to establish a locally Lipschitz estimate for the inverse NLFT map with respect to the Euclidean topology on both the domain and the codomain, thereby avoiding the use of non-Euclidean metrics and quasi-metrics used in the continuity proof in the infinite-dimensional case
[
Tsa05
, Lemma 3.10]
. For example, in that proof, on the codomain of inverse NLFT, Tsai uses the symmetric quasi-metric
d(\boldsymbol{\gamma},\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{\prime}):=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\log(1+|\gamma_{k}-\gamma^{\prime}_{k}|^{2})
, for
\boldsymbol{\gamma},\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{C}^{\ast}\times\mathbb{C}^{n-2}\times\mathbb{C}^{\ast}
. To establish the locally Lipschitz estimate, we will need two helper lemmas,
Lemmas

C.1
and
C.2
, proved in
Appendix

C
. Note that the inverse NLFT map is smooth; hence it must be the case that the map is locally Lipschitz, and so the main contribution of the following lemma is to provide an explicit form of the estimate, which also allows us to derive a Lipschitz continuity condition in
6.3
. In the proof below, we utilize several well-known results relating different matrix norms, which can be found in any standard numerical linear algebra textbook (cf.
[
GVL13
]
).

Lemma 6.2

(Locally Lipschitz estimate)
.

Let
(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}),(\mathbf{a}^{\prime},\mathbf{b}^{\prime})\in\mathtt{S}_{n}
, and suppose
\left\lVert\mathbf{a}-\mathbf{a}^{\prime}\right\rVert_{\infty}\leq\epsilon
,
\left\lVert\mathbf{b}-\mathbf{b}^{\prime}\right\rVert_{\infty}\leq\epsilon
, for some
\epsilon>0
. Let
\boldsymbol{\gamma},\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{\prime}
be such that
\overbrace{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}=(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b})
and
\overbrace{\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{\prime}}=(\mathbf{a}^{\prime},\mathbf{b}^{\prime})
. Then we have the estimate

(139)

\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\gamma}-\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{\prime}\right\rVert_{1}<\epsilon(3n)^{n}(1+1/\delta)^{2n},

where
\delta:=\min\{a_{0},a^{\prime}_{0}\}
. Thus, the inverse NLFT is a locally Lipschitz bijective map from
\mathtt{S}_{n}
to
\mathbb{C}^{\ast}\times\mathbb{C}^{n-2}\times\mathbb{C}^{\ast}
, with respect to the Euclidean topology on both spaces.

Proof.

In this proof, we will use the notation of
Section

4.1
. Let the inverse NLFT of
(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b})
and
(\mathbf{a}^{\prime},\mathbf{b}^{\prime})
be
\boldsymbol{\gamma}:=(\gamma_{0},\dots,\gamma_{n-1})
and
\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{\prime}:=\{\gamma^{\prime}_{0},\dots,\gamma^{\prime}_{n-1}\}
respectively. For
0\leq k\leq n-1
, at the start of the
k^{\text{th}}
step of the layer stripping algorithm for determining
\gamma_{k}
, let the
n-k
dimensional vectors be
\mathbf{a}_{k}:=(a_{j,k})_{0\leq j\leq n-k-1}
and
\mathbf{b}_{k}:=(b_{j,k})_{0\leq j\leq n-k-1}
, as defined in
Eq.

32
. We initialize
(\mathbf{a}_{0},\mathbf{b}_{0}):=(\overline{\mathbf{a}},\mathbf{b})
, and then iteratively obtain
(\mathbf{a}_{k+1},\mathbf{b}_{k+1})
from
(\mathbf{a}_{k},\mathbf{b}_{k})
using
Eqs.

33
and
34
. Analogously, for every
0\leq k\leq n-1
, we define the
n-k
dimensional vectors
\mathbf{a}^{\prime}_{k},\mathbf{b}^{\prime}_{k}
corresponding to the layer stripping process for obtaining
\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{\prime}
, with the initialization
(\mathbf{a}^{\prime}_{0},\mathbf{b}^{\prime}_{0}):=(\overline{\mathbf{a}^{\prime}},\mathbf{b}^{\prime})
. Let us also define the
(n-k)\times 2
matrices
G_{k}:=(\mathbf{a}_{k},\mathbf{b}_{k})
and
G^{\prime}_{k}:=(\mathbf{a}^{\prime}_{k},\mathbf{b}^{\prime}_{k})
. For a complex number
\gamma
, we will define the
\mathrm{SU}(2)
matrix
\Theta(\gamma):=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+|\gamma|^{2}}}\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&-\gamma\\
\overline{\gamma}&1\end{smallmatrix}\right)
.

We recall from
Lemma

4.1
(a) that
a_{0,0}\leq a_{0,1}\leq\dots\leq a_{0,n-1}
, and
a^{\prime}_{0,0}\leq a^{\prime}_{0,1}\leq\dots\leq a^{\prime}_{0,n-1}
. Let
\delta:=\min\{a_{0,0},a^{\prime}_{0,0}\}>0
. Note that
Lemma

4.1
(d) implies
\left\lVert\mathbf{b}_{k}\right\rVert_{\infty},\left\lVert\mathbf{b}^{\prime}_{k}\right\rVert_{\infty}\leq 1
, for every
k
. At step
k
of layer stripping, let us assume that we have
\left\lVert\mathbf{a}_{k}-\mathbf{a}^{\prime}_{k}\right\rVert_{\infty}\leq\epsilon_{k}
and
\left\lVert\mathbf{b}_{k}-\mathbf{b}^{\prime}_{k}\right\rVert_{\infty}\leq\epsilon_{k}
, for some
\epsilon_{k}>0
to be specified later. We may then additionally deduce that for every
k
, we have
\left\lVert G_{k}-G^{\prime}_{k}\right\rVert_{1}\leq(n-k)\epsilon_{k}
, and
\left\lVert G^{\prime}_{k}\right\rVert_{1}\leq\sqrt{n-k}\left\lVert G^{\prime}_{k}\right\rVert_{F}=\sqrt{n-k}
, using the fact
\left\lVert G^{\prime}_{k}\right\rVert_{F}=1
by
Lemma

4.1
(b). We will use these observations below.

Now at step
k
of layer stripping, we first have
\gamma_{k}=\frac{b_{0,k}}{a_{0,k}}
,
\gamma^{\prime}_{k}=\frac{b^{\prime}_{0,k}}{a^{\prime}_{0,k}}
, and thus by
Lemma

C.1
we get
|\gamma_{k}-\gamma^{\prime}_{k}|\leq\delta^{-1}(1+1/\delta)\epsilon_{k}
. Next, we estimate

(140)

\begin{split}&\left\lVert G_{k}\Theta(\gamma_{k})-G^{\prime}_{k}\Theta(\gamma^{\prime}_{k})\right\rVert_{1}\leq\left\lVert G_{k}\Theta(\gamma_{k})-G^{\prime}_{k}\Theta(\gamma_{k})\right\rVert_{1}+\left\lVert G^{\prime}_{k}\Theta(\gamma_{k})-G^{\prime}_{k}\Theta(\gamma^{\prime}_{k})\right\rVert_{1}\\
&\leq\left\lVert G_{k}-G^{\prime}_{k}\right\rVert_{1}\left\lVert\Theta(\gamma_{k})\right\rVert_{1}+\left\lVert G^{\prime}_{k}\right\rVert_{1}\left\lVert\Theta(\gamma_{k})-\Theta(\gamma^{\prime}_{k})\right\rVert_{1}<\sqrt{2}\left\lVert G_{k}-G^{\prime}_{k}\right\rVert_{1}+3\left\lVert G^{\prime}_{k}\right\rVert_{1}|\gamma_{k}-\gamma^{\prime}_{k}|\\
&\leq\sqrt{2}(n-k)\epsilon_{k}+3\delta^{-1}(1+1/\delta)\epsilon_{k}\sqrt{n-k}<3n(1+1/\delta+1/\delta^{2})\epsilon_{k},\end{split}

where in the second line we have used
\left\lVert\Theta(\gamma_{k})\right\rVert_{1}\leq\sqrt{2}\left\lVert\Theta(\gamma_{k})\right\rVert_{2}=\sqrt{2}
and
Lemma

C.2
, and the third line follows from the observations in the last paragraph. Finally, recall that
\left\lVert G_{k+1}-G^{\prime}_{k+1}\right\rVert_{1}=\left\lVert G_{k}\Theta(\gamma_{k})-G^{\prime}_{k}\Theta(\gamma^{\prime}_{k})\right\rVert_{1}
by
Lemma

4.1
(c), and thus
Eq.

140
allows us to conclude

(141)

\max\{\left\lVert\mathbf{a}_{k+1}-\mathbf{a}^{\prime}_{k+1}\right\rVert_{\infty},\left\lVert\mathbf{b}_{k+1}-\mathbf{b}^{\prime}_{k+1}\right\rVert_{\infty}\}\leq\left\lVert G_{k+1}-G^{\prime}_{k+1}\right\rVert_{1}<3n(1+1/\delta+1/\delta^{2})\epsilon_{k}.

We may now set
\epsilon_{0}:=\epsilon
, and then recursively set
\epsilon_{k+1}:=3n(1+1/\delta+1/\delta^{2})\epsilon_{k}
, for
0\leq k\leq n-2
. This allows us to estimate

(142)

\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\gamma}-\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{\prime}\right\rVert_{1}=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}|\gamma_{k}-\gamma^{\prime}_{k}|\leq\delta^{-1}(1+1/\delta)\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\epsilon_{k}<\epsilon(3n)^{n}(1+1/\delta)^{2n}.

This estimate implies that the inverse NLFT map is locally Lipschitz.
∎

An immediate consequence of
Lemma

6.2
is the following corollary that gives rise to a Lipschitz estimate on a subset of
\mathtt{S}_{n}
:

Corollary 6.3
.

Let
\mathtt{S}_{n,\delta}:=\{(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b})\in\mathtt{S}_{n}:a_{0}>\delta\}
, for some
\delta>0
. Then the inverse NLFT is a Lipschitz map on
\mathtt{S}_{n,\delta}
, with the Lipschitz constant depending on
n
and
\delta
.

A few remarks about the estimate in
Lemma

6.2
are in order. In comparison with the proof of
[
Tsa05
, Lemma 3.10]
where continuity of inverse NLFT is established without proving an explicit locally Lipschitz estimate, here we have an explicit estimate in
Eq.

139
. However, in our case, we need
n
to be fixed. Clearly, our proof technique cannot lead to a locally Lipschitz estimate for the non-compactly supported setting, as we cannot make the estimate independent of
n
. In fact, it is unclear whether in the non-compactly supported setting of
[
Tsa05
, Lemma 3.10]
, such a locally Lipschitz estimate could hold or not.

One should also compare
Eq.

139
with similar estimates that already exist in the literature. Two such recent results can be found in
[
AMT24
,
ALM+24
]
. The first of these results
[
AMT24
, Theorem 5]
establishes a Lipschitz estimate on the inverse NLFT when one restricts the domain of inverse NLFT to the set
\mathtt{S}_{n}^{\beta}:=\{(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b})\in\mathtt{S}_{n}:\overbrace{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}=(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}),\;\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\gamma}\right\rVert_{1}\leq\beta=0.36\}
. In this case, one obtains the Lipschitz estimate
\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\gamma}-\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{\prime}\right\rVert_{1}\leq 2\left\lVert\mathbf{b}-\mathbf{b}^{\prime}\right\rVert_{1}
directly by applying the stated theorem. In contrast, our Lipschitz estimate in
6.3
does not assume such an explicit bound on the
\ell_{1}
norm of
\boldsymbol{\gamma}
. Moreover, given
(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b})\in\mathtt{S}_{n}
, the condition we have in
6.3
to test the membership of
(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b})
in
\mathtt{S}_{n,\delta}
is straightforward, while checking the membership in the set
\mathtt{S}_{n}^{\beta}
is nontrivial and involves computing the corresponding
\boldsymbol{\gamma}
.

Comparison of
6.3
with the second result
[
ALM+24
, Theorem 12]
is also interesting. Specializing to the compactly supported setting of this paper, that result establishes a Lipschitz-type estimate on the inverse NLFT map by restricting the domain of the inverse NLFT to
\mathbf{B}_{\delta}
defined as

(143)

\mathbf{B}_{\delta}:=\left\{(a,b)\in\mathcal{S}:a^{\ast}\text{ is outer},\;\int_{\mathbb{T}}\log|a(z)|>-\delta\right\},\;\delta>0,

with the estimate taking the following form for a constant
C_{\delta}
that depends only on
\delta
:

(144)

\max_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}|\gamma_{k}-\gamma^{\prime}_{k}|\leq C_{\delta}\left\lVert\frac{b}{a}-\frac{b^{\prime}}{a^{\prime}}\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T})},\;\;(a,b),(a^{\prime},b^{\prime})\in\mathbf{B}_{\delta},\;\overbrace{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}=(a,b),\;\overbrace{\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{\prime}}=(a^{\prime},b^{\prime}).

If we apply the condition in
Eq.

149
, we observe that the second condition defining
\mathbf{B}_{\delta}
in
Eq.

143
, assuming that
a^{\ast}
is outer, simplifies to

(145)

\int_{\mathbb{T}}\log|a^{\ast}(z)|=\int_{\mathbb{T}}\log|a(z)|>-\delta\iff a^{\ast}(0)>e^{-\delta}.

This is the same condition that we assume in our definition of
\mathtt{S}_{n,\delta}
, except that we do not additionally assume that
a^{\ast}
is an outer polynomial. Thus, our Lipschitz estimate in
6.3
is more general than
[
ALM+24
, Theorem 12]
with respect to this outerness condition on
a^{\ast}
, in the compactly supported setting. However, in doing so, the price that we pay is that our Lipschitz constant ends up depending also on
n
, the size of the support of
\boldsymbol{\gamma}
. However, it is expected that our Lipschitz constant can be improved under additional assumptions, such as an outerness assumption on
a^{\ast}
.

Preimages of the projection maps
\pi_{1}

A.1.
Polynomial solutions to
aa^{\ast}=\text{constant}

For the statement of
Lemma

A.1
, we will need to define a counting function. Let
R=\{\alpha_{j}\in\mathbb{C}^{\ast}:j=0,\dots,k-1\}
be a multiset. We define an equivalence relation on

:
\alpha_{j}\sim\alpha_{k}
if and only if

or
\alpha_{j}=\alpha_{k}
, and denote the quotient set of equivalence classes as

(note that

y\in R/\sim
, we define the function
\sharp:R/\sim\;\;\rightarrow\mathbb{N}

(146)

\sharp(y):=\begin{cases}1&\text{if}\;\mathbb{T}\cap y\neq\emptyset\\
1+\frac{|y|}{2}&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

and then define the counting function

(147)

\mathcal{N}(R):=\prod_{y\in R/\sim}\sharp(y).

Lemma A.1
.

Let
a,b
be polynomials satisfying the conditions (i)
a(0)\neq 0
,
b(0)\neq 0
, and (ii)
a(z)a^{\ast}(z)=b(z)b^{\ast}(z)
for infinitely many
z\in\mathbb{C}^{\ast}
. Then
a
and
b
have the same degree
m
. Let
C_{a}
and
C_{b}
be the coefficients of the highest degree terms of
a
and
b
, respectively. Suppose the zeros of
a
and
b
are given by the multisets
\mathcal{R}_{a}:=\{\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{m}\}
and
\mathcal{R}_{b}:=\{\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{m}\}
respectively, and let
\mathcal{R}_{ab}
be the multiset of common zeros of
a
and
b
. Define the multisets
\overline{\mathcal{R}}_{a}:=\mathcal{R}_{a}\setminus\mathcal{R}_{ab}
and
\overline{\mathcal{R}}_{b}:=\mathcal{R}_{b}\setminus\mathcal{R}_{ab}
. Then

(a)

The multisets
\{\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{m},1/\overline{\alpha_{1}},\dots,1/\overline{\alpha_{m}}\}
and
\{\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{m},1/\overline{\beta_{1}},\dots,1/\overline{\beta_{m}}\}
are equal.

(b)

\mathcal{R}_{a}
,
\mathcal{R}_{b}
and
\mathcal{R}_{ab}
have the same multiplicities for each zero in
\mathbb{T}
, while
\overline{\mathcal{R}}_{a}
and
\overline{\mathcal{R}}_{b}
do not contain any element in
\mathbb{T}
.

(c)

If
y\in\overline{\mathcal{R}}_{a}
(resp.
\overline{\mathcal{R}}_{b}
) with multiplicity
k
, then it implies
y\not\in\overline{\mathcal{R}}_{b}
(resp.
\overline{\mathcal{R}}_{a}
),
1/\overline{y}\not\in\overline{\mathcal{R}}_{a}
(resp.
\overline{\mathcal{R}}_{b}
), and
1/\overline{y}\in\overline{\mathcal{R}}_{b}
(resp.
\overline{\mathcal{R}}_{a}
) with multiplicity
k
.

(d)

|\overline{\mathcal{R}}_{a}|=|\overline{\mathcal{R}}_{b}|
and
\prod_{y\in\overline{\mathcal{R}}_{a}}y=\prod_{y\in\overline{\mathcal{R}}_{b}}1/\overline{y}
.

(e)

C_{b}=\lambda C_{a}\left|\prod_{y\in\overline{\mathcal{R}}_{a}}\right|
for some
\lambda\in\mathbb{T}
.

(f)

Suppose we impose the additional requirement that
\mathbb{R}\ni b(0)>0
(resp.
<0
). Then the number of polynomials
b
satisfying (i), (ii) for a fixed polynomial
a
, is given by
\mathcal{N}(R)
, where
R:=\{\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{m},1/\overline{\alpha_{1}},\dots,1/\overline{\alpha_{m}}\}
and
\mathcal{N}(R)
is defined in
Eq.

147
.

Proof.

Suppose
a(z):=C_{a}\prod_{j=1}^{m}(z-\alpha_{j})
and
b(z):=C_{b}\prod_{j=1}^{n}(z-\beta_{j})
, for some
C_{a},C_{b}\in\mathbb{C}^{\ast}
. Condition (i) ensures that none of the
\alpha_{j}
or
\beta_{j}
is zero. This implies that we have

(148)

\begin{split}a(z)a^{\ast}(z)&=|C_{a}|^{2}z^{-m}\prod_{j=1}^{m}(z-\alpha_{j})(1-\overline{\alpha_{j}}z),\;b(z)b^{\ast}(z)=|C_{b}|^{2}z^{-n}\prod_{j=1}^{n}(z-\beta_{j})(1-\overline{\beta_{j}}z),\\
\end{split}

defined for all
z\in\mathbb{C}^{\ast}
. By condition (ii), the two polynomials
|C_{a}|^{2}z^{n}\prod_{j=1}^{m}(z-\alpha_{j})(1-\overline{\alpha_{j}}z)
and
|C_{b}|^{2}z^{m}\prod_{j=1}^{n}(z-\beta_{j})(1-\overline{\beta_{j}}z)
agree on an infinite set, and hence they must be the same. In particular, these two polynomials must have the same set of zeros and identical multiplicities for each zero. This implies
m=n
, since none of the
\alpha_{j}
or
\beta_{j}
are zero, and establishes equality of the multisets
\{\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{m},1/\overline{\alpha_{1}},\dots,1/\overline{\alpha_{m}}\}
and
\{\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{m},1/\overline{\beta_{1}},\dots,1/\overline{\beta_{m}}\}
, thus proving the equality of degrees of
a
and
b
, and part (a).

Parts (b) and (c) immediately follow from part (a), while part (d) follows from part (c).

For part (e), we note that
|C_{a}|^{2}\prod_{j=1}^{m}\overline{\alpha_{j}}=|C_{b}|^{2}\prod_{j=1}^{m}\overline{\beta_{j}}
by equality of the polynomials above. After taking complex conjugate on both sides, this implies
|C_{a}|^{2}\left(\prod_{y\in\mathcal{R}_{ab}}y\right)\left(\prod_{y\in\overline{\mathcal{R}}_{a}}y\right)=|C_{b}|^{2}\left(\prod_{y\in\mathcal{R}_{ab}}y\right)\left(\prod_{y\in\overline{\mathcal{R}}_{b}}y\right)
, and now the conclusion follows as a consequence of part (d).

For part (f), we first note that the number of ways to choose the multiset
\mathcal{R}_{b}
such that part (a) holds is given exactly by
\mathcal{N}(R)
. Now, once
\mathcal{R}_{b}
is chosen, the condition
b(0)>0
(resp.
<0
) implies that there is a unique choice of
\lambda
in part (e) satisfying all the assumptions, finishing the proof.
∎

Appendix B
Outer functions

In
Section

2.2
we introduced the concept of outer polynomials, which we defined to be polynomials with no zeros in the unit disk
\mathbb{D}
. Here, we give a definition of outer functions that is also applicable more generally to holomorphic functions on
\mathbb{D}
that are not polynomials. There are several equivalent characterizations, and we will state one of the simplest ones based on
[
Rud87
, Chapter 17]
. Let us denote the set of bounded holomorphic functions on the unit disk by
H^{\infty}(\mathbb{D})
. If
f\in H^{\infty}(\mathbb{D})
and
f(0)\neq 0
, then we say that
f
is an
outer function
if and only if

(149)

\log|f(0)|=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\log|\hat{f}(e^{i\theta})|\;d\theta,

where
\hat{f}(e^{i\theta})
denotes the radial limit
\lim_{r\rightarrow 1^{-}}f(re^{i\theta})
. Note that by
[
Rud87
, Theorem 17.17]
,
\hat{f}
exists almost everywhere on
\mathbb{T}
and the integral on the right-hand side of
Eq.

149
is finite, and thus the condition makes sense under our assumptions. We may check that if a polynomial
f
has no zeros in
\mathbb{D}
then it satisfies
Eq.

149
with
\hat{f}=f
on
\mathbb{T}
, while if
f
has zeros in
\mathbb{D}
(still with
f(0)\neq 0
), then one must have
\log|f(0)|<\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\log|f(e^{i\theta})|\;d\theta
, both being consequences of Jensen’s formula
[
Rud87
, Theorem 15.18]
. For polynomials, these facts can also be derived avoiding Jensen’s formula entirely, because of the much simpler known result in complex integration theory
[
SS03
, Chapter 3, Exercise 11]
:

(150)

\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\log|1-\omega e^{i\theta}|\;d\theta=\begin{cases}0&\text{ if }|\omega|\leq 1,\\
\log|\omega|&\text{ if }|\omega|>1.\end{cases}

Lemma C.1
.

Let
a,b,a^{\prime},b^{\prime}\in\mathbb{C}
, with
|a-a^{\prime}|\leq\epsilon
and
|b-b^{\prime}|\leq\epsilon
. Suppose there exist
\delta,\delta^{\prime}
such that
0<\delta\leq\min\{|a|,|a^{\prime}|\}
and
\max\{|b|,|b^{\prime}|\}\leq\delta^{\prime}
. Then we have

(151)

\left|\frac{b}{a}-\frac{b^{\prime}}{a^{\prime}}\right|\leq\epsilon\left(1+\max\{|b/a|,|b^{\prime}/a^{\prime}|\}\right)/\min\{|a|,|a^{\prime}|\}\leq\epsilon\delta^{-1}(1+\delta^{\prime}/\delta).

Proof.

We have

(152)

\begin{split}&|b/a-b^{\prime}/a^{\prime}|\leq|(b-b^{\prime})/a|+|b^{\prime}|\;|1/a-1/a^{\prime}|\leq\epsilon\left(1+|b^{\prime}|/|a^{\prime}|\right)/|a|\\
&\leq\epsilon\left(1+\max\{|b/a|,|b^{\prime}/a^{\prime}|\}\right)/\min\{|a|,|a^{\prime}|\}\leq\epsilon\delta^{-1}(1+\delta^{\prime}/\delta).\end{split}

∎

Lemma C.2
.

Suppose
\gamma_{k}\in\mathbb{C}
and we define
\Theta(\gamma_{k}):=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+|\gamma_{k}|^{2}}}\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&-\gamma_{k}\\
\overline{\gamma_{k}}&1\end{smallmatrix}\right)
for
k=1,2
. Then the map
\mathbb{C}\ni\gamma\rightarrow\Theta(\gamma)\in\mathrm{SU}(2)
is Lipschitz continuous satisfying
\left\lVert\Theta(\gamma_{1})-\Theta(\gamma_{2})\right\rVert_{2}\leq\sqrt{10}\;|\gamma_{1}-\gamma_{2}|
. Additionally, we also have
\left\lVert\Theta(\gamma_{1})-\Theta(\gamma_{2})\right\rVert_{1}<3\;|\gamma_{1}-\gamma_{2}|
.

Proof.

Without loss of generality, we may assume that
0\leq|\gamma_{1}|\leq|\gamma_{2}|
. Note that by the mean value theorem, for some
\lambda\in[|\gamma_{1}|,|\gamma_{2}|]
, we have

(153)

\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+|\gamma_{1}|^{2}}}-\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+|\gamma_{2}|^{2}}}=(|\gamma_{2}|-|\gamma_{1}|)\lambda(1+\lambda^{2})^{-\frac{3}{2}}<|\gamma_{1}-\gamma_{2}|.

This, in turn, allows us to estimate

(154)

\begin{split}&\left|\frac{\gamma_{1}}{\sqrt{1+|\gamma_{1}|^{2}}}-\frac{\gamma_{2}}{\sqrt{1+|\gamma_{2}|^{2}}}\right|=\left|\frac{\gamma_{1}-\gamma_{2}}{\sqrt{1+|\gamma_{2}|^{2}}}+\frac{\gamma_{1}}{\sqrt{1+|\gamma_{1}|^{2}}}-\frac{\gamma_{1}}{\sqrt{1+|\gamma_{2}|^{2}}}\right|\\
&\leq\frac{|\gamma_{1}-\gamma_{2}|}{\sqrt{1+|\gamma_{2}|^{2}}}+|\gamma_{1}|\;|\gamma_{1}-\gamma_{2}|\;\frac{\lambda}{(1+\lambda^{2})^{\frac{3}{2}}}\leq\frac{|\gamma_{1}-\gamma_{2}|}{(1+\lambda^{2})^{\frac{1}{2}}}+|\gamma_{1}-\gamma_{2}|\;\frac{\lambda^{2}}{(1+\lambda^{2})^{\frac{3}{2}}}\\
&\leq 2\;|\gamma_{1}-\gamma_{2}|\;(1+|\lambda|^{2})^{-\frac{1}{2}}<2\,|\gamma_{1}-\gamma_{2}|.\end{split}

This gives the bound
\left\lVert\Theta(\gamma_{1})-\Theta(\gamma_{2})\right\rVert_{1}<3|\gamma_{1}-\gamma_{2}|
. Additionally, we have
\left\lVert\Theta(\gamma_{1})-\Theta(\gamma_{2})\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\leq\left\lVert\Theta(\gamma_{1})-\Theta(\gamma_{2})\right\rVert_{F}^{2}<10\;|\gamma_{1}-\gamma_{2}|^{2}
.
∎
