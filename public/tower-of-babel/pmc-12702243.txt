Glycolysis maintains effector function of lung Th17 TRM cells in precision cut lung slices
Authors: Haoran Yang, Vaishnavi R. Kumaran, Kejing Song, T. Parks Remcho, Robert D.E. Clark, Sharad Thakur, Shiping Lu, Mark A.A. Harrison, Naoki Iwanaga, Maria de la Luz Garcia-Hernandez, Javier Rangel-Moreno, Kevin J. Zwezdaryk, Janet E. McCombs, Elizabeth B. Norton, Jay K. Kolls
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryVaccination with Klebsiella pneumoniae OmpX and the Th17 adjuvant LTA1 elicits lung CD4+ tissue-resident memory (TRM) Th17 cells that provide serotype-independent protection against K. pneumoniae, but vaccine efficacy declines as TRM numbers wane. To define the mechanisms sustaining these cells, we developed an ovalbumin (OVA) model with tetramer enrichment to track antigen-specific CD4+ TRM cells over time. Serial single-cell RNA sequencing on days 30, 90, and 184 after LTA1/OVA immunization revealed persistent glycolytic signatures across all time points, while ornithine decarboxylase 1 (Odc1), although required for Th17 differentiation in vitro, was dispensable for TRM maintenance in vivo. Inhibition of glycolysis with 2-deoxy-D-glucose impaired IL-17A production both in vivo and in precision-cut lung slice (PCLS) cultures which preserve functional TRM cells ex vivo. These findings identify glycolysis as a key metabolic pathway sustaining lung CD4+ Th17 TRM effector function and highlight PCLS as an ex vivo model.

Introduction

Current vaccine technology against pneumonia-causing encapsulated bacteria is focused on generating anti-capsular polysaccharide IgG antibodies.1,2 A limitation of this approach is a lack of serotype coverage as well as serotype replacement.3 Some pathogens such as Streptococcus pneumoniae can also reduce capsule expression in the lung, which may explain the greater efficacy in preventing meningitis more than pneumonia with current pneumococcal vaccines. We and others4,5,6 have shown that lung Th17 tissue-resident memory (TRM) cells can be elicited by Klebsiella pneumoniae and these cells can provide serotype-independent immunity against other capsular serotypes of the organism. More recently, we have shown that a subunit approach using the vaccine-adjuvant E. coli heat-labile toxin A1 domain (LTA1)7,8,9 with a recombinant outer membrane protein OmpX confers similar protection.10 However, protection was reduced over time, which coincided with CD4+ TRM cell contraction.10 To further develop vaccines that target CD4+ TRM cells, understanding TRM survival and their metabolic requirements is essential.

In this study, we used unbiased longitudinal single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) to study the expansion and contraction of lung CD4+ Th17 TRM cells. To accomplish this, we used the model antigen, ovalbumin (OVA), with LTA1 as the adjuvant. Importantly, single-cell analysis showed OVA+CD4+ T cells clustered with OmpX+CD4+ T cells post-vaccination with respective antigen and LTA1, demonstrating they have a similar gene expression after initial vaccination. We found that Ornithine decarboxylase (Odc1) was highly expressed on CD4+ TRM cells compared to naive CD4+ T cells. Although ODC1 was required for in vitro Th17 differentiation, it was not essential for the generation of LTA1-induced lung CD4+ Th17 TRM cells. Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) of long-lived Th17 TRM cells showed that the glycolysis pathway was enriched in LTA1-elicited CD4+ TRM cells over time, indicating a different metabolic state from in vitro-differentiated Th17 cells. Seahorse assays also confirmed these LTA1-induced CD4+ TRM cells were more dependent on glycolysis than oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS). CD4+ TRM cells were also more sensitive to 2-deoxy-D-glucose (2-DG) than oligomycin, showing these cells relied more on glycolysis, ex vivo, as well as in a novel model using precision cut lung slice (PCLS) tissue.

Results

Ovalbumin vaccination adjuvanted with LTA1 elicits tissue-resident lung CD4+ Th17 cells, which resembles LTA1/OmpX-elicited cells

Our previous work has shown that K. pneumoniae-derived OmpX adjuvanted with LTA1 can elicit IL-17A-producing CD4+ TRM cells in the lung to control Klebsiella infection, independent of bacterial serotype. However, the efficacy of the vaccine decreased over time along with reduced CD4+ TRM cell numbers in the lung.10 OmpX lacks an immunodominant peptide and thus to follow the kinetics of these cells, we induced OVA-specific lung Th17 TRM cells with LTA1/OVA mucosal immunization, as previously described.10 Mice were immunized intratracheally with 10 μg of OVA and 10 μg of LTA1 on day 0 and day 21; followed by intratracheal challenge with 10 μg of OVA one day before euthanasia to model an in vivo recall response (Figure 1A). OVA-specific TRM cells were enriched by the tetramer-pulldown method on days 30, 90, or 184 after initial vaccination. The purity of OVA+ cells increased from 25% to 90% after enrichment (Figures 1B and S1A). Splenic CD4+ T cells were isolated from naive mice as a control since there were too few CD4+ T cell from a naive mouse lung (Figures S2A–S2C). Enriched cells were used for scRNA-seq, single-cell T cell receptor sequencing (scTCR-seq) and Isoplexis single-cell proteomics for OVA+CD4+ T cells collected on day 90 post-vaccination. We found the TRM cells produced IL-17A at the protein level by single-cell proteomics. Moreover, TRM cells from challenged mice produced more IL-17A compared to unchallenged mice (Figure 1C). Furthermore, the uniform manifold approximation and projections (UMAPs) of scRNA-seq data showed that OVA+ TRM cells (after two rounds of immunization) closely resembled our prior OmpX+ lung TRM cells that were enriched using CD4+ magnetic beads on day 28 (Figure 1D). Both groups CD4+ T cells highly expressed Il17a (Figure 1E). We compared the transcriptional profiles of CD4+OVA+ and OmpX+ cells and found that 6,071 of 6,402 genes (94.8%) showed no significant differential expression, indicating a high degree of similarity between the two populations (Table S1). At matched sequencing saturation (∼99.9%), scTCR-seq showed that LTA1/OVA-induced antigen-specific CD4 T cells were highly oligoclonal compared to the CD4+ T cells enriched from naive spleen (Figures S3A and S3B), which was similar to our previous report on LTA1/OmpX-induced OmpX+CD4+ T cells. We also compared our OVA+ cells with in vitro-differentiated Th17 cells from a public dataset including non-pathogenic (npTh17) and pathogenic Th17 cells (pTh17).11 The UMAP showed that OVA+ and OmpX+ CD4+ T cells were separated from in vitro-differentiated Th17 cells, indicating intrinsic differences between these Th17-producing populations indicating intrinsic differences of these Th17-producing cells (Figure 1D). The heatmap also revealed that OVA+CD4+ T cells exhibited highly similar gene expression profiles to OmpX+ TRM cells, whereas both populations were transcriptionally distinct from npTh17 and pTh17 cells (Figure 1E). By pathway analysis, we found that the day 30 OVA+ cells were enriched in the glycolysis pathway compared to npTh17 and pTh17 cells (Figure 1F). To test this, naive CD4+ T cells were differentiated into Th17 cells in vitro and subsequently treated with 2-DG, a glycolysis inhibitor, to examine the relevance of glycolysis on IL-17A production. We found that the IL-17A production was inhibited by 2-DG (Figures S3C and S3D). Since OXPHOS is another major metabolic pathway for CD4 T cells, we also tested if OXPHOS plays a role in the in vitro-differentiated Th17 cells. Odc1 is highly expressed on LTA1-elicited lung OVA+CD4+ T cells

ODC1 participates in Th17 differentiation and function.12,13,14 However, there is a lack of data on the function of ODC1 in generating IL-17A-producing TRM cells. By comparing scRNA-seq data from OVA+ and OmpX+CD4+ T cells, we found that Odc1 was highly expressed compared to naive splenic CD4+ T cells.10 Odc1 expression was maintained in OVA+ lung TRM cells after in vivo antigen recall on days 30, 90, and 184 (Figure 2A). Interestingly there were no differences in Odc1 expression in OVA+ lung TRM cells at these different time points, suggesting that Odc1 is not enriched in long-lived lung CD4+ TRM cells. These data suggested that Odc1 might preferentially contribute to CD4+ TRM cell generation rather than in maintenance. To study the role of Odc1 on CD4+ TRM cells, we isolated OVA+CD4+ T cells by tetramer, followed by ex vivo treatment with difluoromethylornithine (DFMO), an Odc1 inhibitor, which induces rapid depletion of intracellular putrescine and spermidine both in vitro and in vivo.15 Interestingly, 200 μM DFMO treatment had no effect on CD3/C28-stimulated-IL-17A production detected by flow cytometry in established Th17 TRM cells (Figures 2B, 2C, and S1B). DFMO treatment resulted in a reduced frequency of IFN-γ+ and IL-17A+IFN-γ+ CD4+ T cells (Figures 2B and 2C). However, the differentiation of in vitro Th17 cells16 was inhibited by 200 μM DFMO, consistent with prior literature12,13,14 (Figure S4A). This result suggested that although Odc1 is enriched in CD4+ lung TRM cells, once established, Odc1 is not required for IL-17A expression. Similarly, we found that addition of DFMO did not inhibit IL-17A production by in vitro-differentiated Th17 cells (Figures S4B and S4C). To investigate the role of Odc1 in the in vivo generation of CD4+ TRM cells, we immunized Odc1fl/fl Cd4-Cre mice with LTA1/OmpX. Flow cytometric analysis revealed that Odc1fl/fl Cd4-Cre+ mice (conditional knockout, KO) exhibited a modest reduced number of IL-17A-producing CD4+ T cells (Figures 2D–2F and S1B). We also depleted ODC1 pharmacologically by treating mice with DFMO in the drinking water, using a protocol that inhibited Th17 cells in a model of experimental autoimmune encephalitis.13 Briefly, mice were provided with 0.5% DFMO in the drinking water or untreated water, starting 3 days prior to initial immunization. Mice were immunized with LTA1/OmpX, and DFMO administration was maintained through the boost until euthanasia. To assess lung CD4+ TRM function, we challenged the mice with 104 colony-forming units (CFUs) of the Klebsiella K1 strain on day 28 after prime immunization. Interestingly, we did not observe a significant effect of DFMO on vaccine efficacy (Figure S5A). Both groups of immunized mice showed similar vaccine efficacy with a substantial reduction in lung CFUs compared to non-immunized mice. Using flow cytometry, we observed no differences in the number of IL-17A+CD4+ T cells and IFNγ+IL-17A+CD4+T cells in the lung between the immunized mice with or without DFMO treatment (Figures S5B and S5C). However, we did observe greater numbers of IFN-γ+CD4+ T cells in DFMO-treated mice (Figures S5B and S5C). Long-lived lung CD4+ Th17 cells are enriched in glycolysis over time

Similar to what we observed with OmpX+ lung TRM cells,10 OVA+ lung TRM cells also contracted over time (Figure 3A). There were more than one million OVA+ cells in the lung per mouse 30 days after prime immunization. However, the cell number was reduced to 1x105 cells per mouse at day 90 and day 184 post-immunization. Using scRNA-seq of tetramer positive cells over time, we conducted GSEA comparing the OVA+CD4+ T cells across different time points. In this analysis we found that the MYC target pathways (version 1 and 2), glycolysis pathway, fatty acid metabolism pathway, and the OXPHOS pathway were enriched in antigen-specific CD4+ T cells collected at day 90 after immunization, compared to the cell collected at day 30 post-immunization (Figures 3B and 3C; Table S2). The glycolysis pathway was also enriched in antigen-specific CD4 T cells collected at day 184 after OVA immunization (data not shown). The IL-17A+CD4+OVA+ cell subset also exhibited enrichment of glycolysis pathway at day 90 compared with day 30 (Figure 3D). This was unexpected because previous studies have shown that OXPHOS is critical for the efficient metabolism and survival of long-lived CD8+ TRM cells.17,18 The utilization of different metabolic pathways might explain the differences in the contraction of CD8+ and CD4+ T cells. Lung Th17 TRM requires glycolysis for effector function

To validate our GSEA result, we conducted seahorse assays of lung CD4+ TRM cells and naive CD4+ T cells sorted on a Miltenyi Tyto to assess the role of glycolysis versus OXPHOS. Consistent with our findings, lung CD4+ TRM cells were more glycolytic compared to naive CD4+ T cells even prior to anti-CD3/CD28 activation (Figure 4A). Naive splenic CD4+ T cells showed an increased glycolysis level upon activation. However, lung CD4+ TRM cells did not show this phenomenon. Incubation of LTA1-elicited lung CD4+ TRM cells with 2-DG but not oligomycin inhibited IL-17A production from anti-CD3/CD28 stimulated lung TRM cells ex vivo (Figure 4B). Treatment with 2-DG or oligomycin showed a trend toward reduced IFN-γ production (Figure 4B). This was confirmed by flow cytometry with fewer IL-17A+cells, IFN-γ+ cells, and IL-17A+IFN-γ+ cells in the 2-DG condition, whereas oligomycin had no significant effect (Figures 4C, 4D, and S1C). The CD3+CD4+TCRβ+ T cell numbers were comparable across all groups (Figures S6A and S6B). Metabolic features of TRM cells are maintained in PCLS

To understand the metabolic requirements for TRM cells in a tissue context, we first administered the fluorescent glucose analog 2-NBDG intratracheally to determine if lung Th17 TRM cells actively uptake this molecule from the airway. Indeed, we observed significant labeling of lung TRM cells (Figures 5A and S1D). Secondly, we reasoned that since LTA1-elicited TRM cells occupy a sub-mucosal niche along the airways,10 TRM cells would be maintained in PCLSs.20 To test this hypothesis, we inflated lungs from naive or LTA1-immunized mice with gelatin given that it is more water soluble than low melting agarose21,22,23 (Figure 5B). We then performed multicolor immunofluorescence staining on PCLS with antibodies specific for CD3, CD69, and IL-17. We found abundant numbers of CD3+CD69+IL-17+ cells in the PCLS of vaccinated mice, which is consistent with our prior RNAscope data10 (Figure 5C). To determine if lung TRM Th17 cells could be activated in PCLS tissue, we added CD3/CD28 beads and assessed the induction of Il17a in lung tissue. Indeed, the addition of CD3/CD28 beads induced a substantial increase in Il17a expression compared to the media alone (Figure 5D). Strikingly, the addition of K1 K. pneumoniae induced an even more substantial increase in Il17a (Figure 5E), and this increase was blocked by 2-DG, consistent with a requirement for glycolysis in IL-17-producing cells. Discussion

TRM cells are a critical population to rapidly respond to infection, providing a first line of defense in the mucosal and barrier sites. Lung CD4+ TRM cells are effective in providing protection against several pathogens including K. pneumoniae, Mycobacterium tuberculosis, and Bordetella pertussis, among others.10,24,25

Studies demonstrated that metabolism is critical for memory T cell generation, function, development, and survival.26 Different memory T cell subsets share a common metabolic signature that they have a higher reliance on mitochondrial fatty acid oxidation (FAO)-dependent OXPHOS, with a reduced glycolytic metabolism.27,28,29,30,31 Effector memory T (TEM) cells rely less on OXPHOS than central memory T (TCM) cells and TRM cells, whereas TCM and TRM cells have a similar OXPHOS dependency.18 However, most of the studies to date on TRM cell metabolism have focused on CD8+ TRM cells. For example, skin CD8+ TRM cells exhibit a greater reliance on FAO compared to other circulating memory cells and naive cells, which predominantly rely on higher rates of glycolysis.32,33 However, the metabolism profile of CD4+ TRM cells remains elusive. There are a few studies about CD4+ Th17 cells demonstrating that homeostatic Th17 cells mainly rely on OXPHOS, and proinflammatory Th17 cells rely both on glycolysis and OXPHOS.13,34,35,36,37 Pyruvate dehydrogenase (PDH) catalyzes the conversion of pyruvate to acetyl-coenzyme A, which connects glycolysis with the mitochondrial tricarboxylic acid cycle.38 PDH has been shown to direct a citrate pool controlling glycolysis, glutaminolysis, and lipid, which is critical in Th17 function.39 ODC1, a rate-limiting enzyme of polyamine synthesis, also plays a role in Th17 differentiation and function, indicating polyamine metabolism is also important for Th17 cells.12,13,14

In our previous work, we developed a vaccine consisting of OmpX and LTA1, which can elicit long-lived CD4+ TRM cells in the lung submucosa of the conducting airways and provide protection against K. pneumoniae and phylogenetically related Serratia marcescens.10 These cells contracted in lung tissue over time and the number of OmpX-specific Th17 cells was strongly associated with the ability of the host to control growth of hypervirulent K1 K. pneumoniae in the lung over time.10 Due to tetramer availability, we developed a LTA1/OVA model to study the LTA1-induced lung CD4+ TRM cells. This model elicits OVA+CD4+ T cells in the lung, which resemble OmpX+CD4+ T cells by comparing the UMAP of their scRNA-seq data. By doing a time-course study, we found the OVA+CD4+ T cells contracted over time as well.

scRNA-seq revealed that OVA+CD4+ T cells highly expressed Odc1, which has been reported to be required for in vitro Th17 differentiation and function.12,13,14 Moreover, global inhibition of ODC1 by DFMO or deletion of Sat1 (which encodes spermidine/spermine N1-acetyltransferase) in CD4+ T cells in mice delayed the onset and severity of experimental autoimmune encephalitis.13 These studies indicated that polyamine metabolism was critical for Th17 cell differentiation. But the function of Odc1 on Th17 TRM cells was unclear. By using the small-molecule ODC1-inhibitor DFMO, we found DFMO did not affect LTA1-induced Th17 TRM cell function and maintenance. Furthermore, Odc1 expression was increased at day 30 in lung TRM Th17 cells (compared to naive CD4+ T cells) but not enriched in CD4+ lung TRM cells that survived up to day 90 or day 184, which indicated Odc1 might play a more important role in TRM cell generation. To study the role of Odc1 in TRM generation, we immunized Odc1fl/fl Cd4-Cre mice with LTA1/OmpX. Relative to Cre- mice, Cre+ mice showed fewer lung IL-17A-producing cells in the lung. We also depleted ODC1 pharmacologically by putting DFMO in the drinking water before we immunized C57/B6 mice and kept it in the water until euthanasia. However, mice that were fed DMFO developed similar lung Th17 cells as controls. This difference between autoimmune pathology and tissue-resident immunity raises the possibility that TRM cells, despite sharing a Th17 lineage, may have distinct metabolic and functional requirements compared to circulating or pathogenic Th17 cells.

Our longitudinal scRNAseq data showed that lung Th17 TRM cells were enriched in genes in the glycolysis pathway. This was confirmed ex vivo using seahorse assays. Moreover, 2-DG but not oligomycin can inhibit IL-17A production ex vivo. These data are consistent with prior data that T cells can shift from OXPHOS to glycolysis to fulfill their proliferation and effector function upon activation.40 It has been reported that stimulated ex vivo pulmonary-resident CD4+ T cells did not shift to glycolysis, which is different from circulating CD4+ T cells.41 Similar to this finding, we found that the glycolysis level is very close between stimulated and unstimulated CD4+ TRM cells by seahorse assay. In contrast, splenic CD4+ T cells switched to glycolysis upon activation. Also, our CD4+ TRM cells maintained a high glycolysis level, even enriched for later time points, in contrast to what has been reported for CD8+ TRM cells.18

Studies on the comparative longevity of lung CD4+ and CD8+ TRM are limited, and it is unknown what an expected upper bound is for the persistence of CD4+ TRM cells. However, identifying that glycolytic pathways are enriched in and critical for function of long-term lung CD4+ Th17 TRM raises the possibility that pharmacological manipulation at the time of vaccination could improve the duration of T cell memory. Indeed, metabolic modulators such as rapamycin have been explored for this purpose.42,43 However, as much of this work has been performed in the systemic context and on CD8+ T cells, further studies are needed to identify pathways regulating the fate of CD4+ TRMs, which are likely to vary in metabolic requirements in a tissue-specific manner.

We were concerned that TRM cell behavior could be modified by ex vivo culture and thus we thought studying them in an organotypic culture would be valuable. Organoids has been used for studying TRM cells.44,45 Organoids, typically derived from stem cells, though model the differentiation and function of epithelial cell types, often fail to fully preserve the native architecture and cellular heterogeneity of the original tissue. This limitation arises primarily from the absence of multiple compartments, such as specialized immune cells, that are present in the native tissue.46,47 Recent study uses tissue-resident and autologous immune compartment starting from readily available adult human tissue sample to generate intestinal immuno-organoids.44 The study of TRM cells in this context requires the addition of TRM cells into the system as well as taking a few weeks to generate intestine organoids.44 In contrast, PCLSs are obtained from actual lung tissue, generated in a few hours and maintaining the nature of lung. To this end, we reasoned that Th17 TRM cells, given their submucosal position in the airway, would be preserved in PCLS. There are multiple methods of infiltration of the lung for the PCLS model including the use of hydrogels, gelatin, or low melting point agarose.22,48,49 In our experiments we focused our efforts on the use of gelatin infiltration because during the incubation, excess gelatin would melt out of the slices given the lowered melting point.50,51 By doing so, we minimize the potential challenges associated with using agarose such as difficulties in RNA extraction.52 Although, we have not yet tested the low melting point agarose in this context. We found that in addition to being preserved in PCLS, we were able to stimulate Th17 TRM cells with CD3/CD28 beads as well as K1 K. pneumoniae in the PCLS model. Moreover, we were able to successfully adopt the PCLS model to conduct metabolism experiments. Given that 50 PCLS tissues can be generated from a single mouse lung, this technique may be helpful in efforts to achieve reduction, refinement, and replacement of mice in TRM experiments. PCLS can be maintained at least for 14 days and thus could be a useful tool to study TRM cells over time.20 Moreover, PCLS tissues can be maintained in optical transwells that may facilitate imaging studies.

Limitations of the study

This study utilized both LTA1/OVA and LTA1/OmpX models, which involve different antigens. As a result, the properties of antigen-specific Th17 TRM cells may not be directly comparable. Future studies should address this potential variability. We used naive splenic CD4+ T cells as the comparator for lung OVA+CD4+ T cells because naive CD4+ T cells are scarce in the lung parenchyma and are largely blood derived. However, splenic memory CD4+ T cells could be a more appropriate comparator.

While 2-DG has been widely used to inhibit glycolysis, it is associated with significant off-target effects and toxicity. Alternative strategies to target glucose metabolism in TRM cells include the use of selective GLUT1 inhibitors (such as BAY-876), PDH kinase (PDHK1) inhibitors (e.g., dichloroacetate), and lactate dehydrogenase inhibitors (e.g., FX11).53,54,55 These approaches offer more targeted disruption of glucose metabolism pathways and may provide better specificity with reduced systemic toxicity. Future studies will explore these alternative metabolic interventions to further elucidate the role of glycolysis in the maintenance and function of lung CD4+ TRM.

Although the DFMO in drinking-water model was based on the prior literature we did not independently verify the concentration in vivo. Target engagement could be assessed by polyamine ELISA and perhaps a more accurate delivery of DFMO could be achieved by oral gavage taking into consideration the mouse weight. Additionally although 2-NBDG labeling was used to assess glucose uptake in our study, we acknowledge its limitations as a precise measure of glycolytic activity.56 To address this, future studies will incorporate additional approaches such as evaluating glycolytic protein expression (e.g., GLUT1, hexokinase, and lactate dehydrogenase) and applying single-cell metabolic profiling methods such as SCENITH to validate and extend our findings.56 Lastly, in our PCLS experiments, reduced Il17a expression after glycolytic inhibition raised the question of whether this effect reflected a loss of Th17 cell numbers or a functional defect in cytokine production. Future studies employing flow cytometry and immunofluorescence will be important to further delineate functional dynamics in the lung TRM compartment.

Experimental model and study participant details

Experimental animals

Male mice (6 to 12 weeks old) were used for in vivo studies. Odc1 fl/fl xCd4-cre mice were obtained from Ruoning Wang’s lab at Nationwide Children's Hospital. C57BL/6J mice were bred in-house or purchased from the Jackson Laboratory. All mice were housed and bred at the Tulane University Department of Comparative Medicine Facility. Animals were housed in a pathogen-free environment. All experiments were performed using sex- and age-matched controls and approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Tulane University.

Bacterial strains

K. pneumoniae–396 (K1 strain) was prepared as previous reported.10 Briefly, K. pneumoniae–396 (K1 strain) was grown in 30 ml of tryptic soy broth (Difco) overnight at 37°C with shaking at 233 rpm. Cultures were then diluted at 1:100 and grown in the same conditions for 2.5 hours to achieve early logarithmic phase. The bacterial load of K. pneumoniae was determined by measuring the optical density at 600 nm. Bacteria were pelleted and washed twice in cold phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and then resuspended in PBS to the desired concentration. Mice were infected with 104 CFU intratracheally and euthanized at 24 hours post infection. The lungs and spleens were homogenized and diluted in PBS, plated on TSB agar plates for estimating the colony forming units (CFU). 10 μg of OVA were intratracheally administered to mice for the antigenic challenge.

Method details

Antigen and adjuvant preparation for immunization

Recombinant OmpX was generated from K. pneumoniae–43816 and purified by using E. coli expression systems.10 The adjuvant LTA1 was also purified from E. coli expression systems as previously described.8 To study the LTA1-induced CD4+ lung TRM cells, we performed LTA1/OVA or LTA1/OmpX immunization with isoflurane-anesthetized mice using the oropharyngeal aspiration–tongue pull technique and boosted with the same vaccine 3 weeks later. 1 g OmpX or 10ug OVA (vac-pova, InvivoGen) adjuvanted with 10ug LTA1 were used per mouse. C57BL/6J mice (male, 6-12 weeks old) were used for this experiment.

Single cell suspension preparation

Spleens were pressed through 70 μm cell strainer (Fisher) and centrifuged at 300 x g for 5 min to prepare single cell suspensions. ACK lysis buffer (Gibco) was added to the cell pellet for 4 min, washed and finally resuspended in complete IMDM medium. Lungs were minced manually with dissection scissors and digested in 4 mL IMDM (Gibco) with 2 mg/mL collagenase (Sigma-Aldrich) and 80 U/mL DNase1 (Sigma-Aldrich) at 37°C for 1 hour. Digested tissue was strained through a 70 μm cell strainer (Fisher). ACK lysis buffer (Gibco) was added to the cell pellet for 4 min and then washed and resuspended in complete IMDM medium.

Intracellular cytokine staining and flow cytometry

Single-cell suspensions from mouse lungs and spleens were stimulated for 5 hours with phorbol 12-myristate 13-acetate (50 ng/ml; Sigma-Aldrich) and ionomycin (750 ng/ml; Sigma-Aldrich), together with GolgiStop (1 mg/ml; BD Biosciences). Cells were then stained with antibodies specific for cell surface markers, followed by permeabilization/fixation with Cytofix/Cytoperm (BD Biosciences) and stained with antibodies against intracellular proteins. Cells were analyzed using a Cytek Aurora spectral flow cytometer. Antibodies used for blocking and staining are as follows: Rat Anti-Mouse CD16/CD32 Fc Block (clone 2.4G2, BD Biosciences), PE-Cy7 Rat Anti-Mouse CD4 (clone RM4-5, BD Biosciences), APC rat anti-mouse CD3ε (clone 17A2, Biolegend), PE-Cy5 hamster anti-mouse TCRβ (clone H57-597, BD Biosciences), FITC rat anti-mouse IL-17A (clone TC11-18H10.1, Biolegend), Brilliant Violet 421 rat anti-mouse IFN-γ (clone XMG1.2, Biolegend).

Inhibitors

Inhibitors are added at the time of indicated as follows: 200 μM DFMO (S4582, Selleckchem), 25 mM 2-DG (S4701, Selleckchem) or 1 μM oligomycin (S1478, Selleckchem). For Intracellular cytokine staining, inhibitors or vehicle control were maintained in the PMA and Ionomycin stimulation step.

DFMO treatment in the drinking water

Mice were immunized by LTA1/OmpX as described before. Mice were treated with 0.5% DFMO in drinking water from day -3 to day 29. DFMO was replenished every third day. Mice were challenged on day 28 with 104 CFU Klebsiella K1 strain, and bacterial burden (CFU) in lungs was determined at 24 hours after infection.

Tetramer pull-down enrichment

V(D)J Target Enriched and 5’ Gene Expression Sequencing

Cell numbers and viabilities were validated by Cellometer Automated Cell Counter (Nexcelom Bioscience, Lawrence, MA, USA) prior to single cell RNAseq library preparation. For 10x single cell V(D)J sequencing assay, 5000 live cells per sample were targeted by using 10x Chromium Next GEM Single Cell 5’ to generate GEMs by combining barcoded Single Cell 5’ Gel Beads using chip K. Following GEM-RT, RT cleanup, full-length cDNA amplification and QC, T-cell receptor enriched libraries and 5’ gene expression libraries containing standard Illumina P5 and P7 paired-end constructs were generated. Library quality controls were performed by using Agilent High Sensitive DNA kit with Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer and quantified by Qubit 2.0 fluorometer. Pooled libraries at a final concentration of 750 pM were sequenced with paired end dual index configuration by Illumina NextSeq 2000. Cell Ranger v7.1.0 (10x Genomics) was used to process raw sequencing data and to generate barcodes, features, and matrix files for each sample using the mouse (mm10) 2020-A reference. The Seurat (version 5.3.0) in R (version 4.4.1) was used for quality control and downstream analysis. Raw 10x matrices were imported into Seurat (v5.3.0; SeuratObject v5.1.0). Genes detected in fewer than 3 cells were removed. Cells with nFeature_RNA < 200, nFeature_RNA > 6,000, or mitochondrial content > 15% were excluded. Samples were normalized individually using SCTransform, and 3,000 integration features were selected (SelectIntegrationFeatures). Objects were prepared with PrepSCTIntegration, per-sample PCA was computed, and integration anchors were identified with FindIntegrationAnchors (reduction = “rpca”, dims = 1–50, normalization.method = “SCT”). Datasets were integrated with IntegrateData (normalization.method = “SCT”, dims = 1–50). PCA and UMAP were run on the integrated assay (RunPCA; RunUMAP on PCs 1–50), followed by graph-based clustering (FindNeighbors/FindClusters, resolution = 0.5). Integration was used for visualization and batch correction.

Seurat ‘subset’ function was used to extract the certain population. Differential gene expression was performed on the non-integrated assay using FindMarkers with the likelihood-ratio test (LR) and latent variables to control for nCount_RNA and percent.mt. Reported p values were Bonferroni-adjusted (Seurat p_val_adj). AverageExpression was used to compute group means; heatmaps display row-wise z-scores.

GSEA was performed with fgsea (version 1.32.0). Ranked gene lists were constructed from DE tables using the LR statistic. Species-matched gene sets were retrieved via msigdbr, and fgsea was run with 10,000 permutations; results are reported as normalized enrichment score (NES) and FDR (Benjamini–Hochberg). Enrichment plots were generated with fgsea/enrichplot utilities and annotated with group labels, NES, and FDR. For Figure 1F, pathways were analyzed with iPathwayGuide (Advaita Bioinformatics) using all detected genes (and their statistics) as the background. Differential genes were defined by adjusted P ≤ 0.05 and log2FC ≥ 1.2. Pathways were considered significant at Bonferroni-adjusted P ≤ 0.05. scTCR-seq data were processed with Cell Ranger V(D)J v6.0.1 (10x Genomics) against the mouse GRCm38 V(D)J reference v5.0.0. Clonotypes were visualized in Loupe V(D)J Browser v3.0.0 (10x Genomics). Sequencing saturation was quantified with the ‘saturation’ pipeline using V(D)J contig annotations.Data are deposited in GEO under accession number GSE280858: https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/query/acc.cgi?acc=GSE280858.

Seahorse XF glycolysis stress test

Seahorse XF Glycolysis Stress Test kit (103020-100, Agilent) was used for this assay following manufacture’s protocol. Briefly, ECAR (in mpH min−1) was measured using a Seahorse XF96 analyzer (Agilent Technologies) according to the company’s recommendations. XF sensor cartridge was hydrated in XF calibrant at 37°C in a non-CO2 incubator overnight. Seahorse XFp cell culture plate was coated with Cell-Tak Cell and Tissue Adhesive (354240, Corning) in a 37°C non-CO2 incubator for 1 hour. The plate was washed with 37°C sterile water twice, and air dry for further use. Cells were resuspended in Seahorse base medium (Cat#103334-100, Agilent Technologies) supplemented with glutamines (Cat#103579-100, Agilent Technologies) at a final concentration of 2 μM. 2x10ˆ5 cells/ well were resuspended in the medium mentioned above and seeded in the coated plate. The plate was centrifuged for 5 min at 450rpm without brake. Plate was rotated and centrifuged again. Then plate was transferred to 37°C non-CO2 incubator for 30-60 mins. After Seahorse XFp Analyzer was calibrated with hydrated cartridge, the XFp culture plate was transferred into the analyzer and ECAR was measured. ECAR were measured in basal conditions and in response to 10 mM glucose, 1 μM oligomycin and 50 mM 2-DG. The measured ECAR was analyzed using the Seahorse Wave software (Agilent Technologies) and normalized by cell number.

Generation of PCLS

Animals were anesthetized using a CO2 chamber and exsanguinated by severing the inferior vena cava. After euthanasia, the lungs were perfused by injecting 5 mL of warm 1x phosphate-buffered saline (70011069, ThermoFisher Scientific) slowly into the right ventricle of the heart using a 23G × ¾ (0.6 × 19 mm) PrecisionGlide needle (305143, BD) to flush out residual blood. The trachea was cannulated and 900 μL of 15% gelatin (G9382, Sigma) dissolved in 1 x PBS was slowly infiltrated into the lungs via the cannula connected to a 3 mL syringe. Following the infiltration a 0.2 mL bolus of air was injected to ensure the gelatin is uniformly expanded throughout the lungs. After infiltration, the mice were placed into 4 degree for 10 minutes to ensure complete setting of gelatin prior to excision of lungs. Lungs were then carefully excised and separated into lobes. Each lung was then externally embedded in 15% gelatin in a peel away embedding mold (50-727-404, Fisher) and placed on ice for 10 minutes to solidify completely. The separate lobes were uniformly cut into 250 μm thick slices using a Leica VT1200s Fully Automatic Vibrating Blade Microtome at a speed of 0.6 mm/s. Each slice was then cored using a 4mm diameter biopsy punch (69031-55, Integra) and collected in chilled 1× PBS before being transferred into 24-well culture plates. The freshly cut slices were placed into sterile submerged well (229124, CELLTREAT) tissue culture plates with 1× DMEM High Glucose containing 1× P/S and 10% FBS and incubated in 5% CO2 at 37°C with media changed every 30 minutes for an hour. Following the initial hour, the media was changed every hour for 2 hours and left overnight to allow recovery from slicing. Afterwards slices were treated with 25 mM 2-DG, 1 μM oligomycin or 200 μM DFMO or vehicle controls solubilized in 1X DMEM High Glucose containing 10% FBS. After 24 hours of treatment, the slices were collected for histology, RNA extraction and RT-qPCR.

RNA isolation and RT-qPCR quantification

PCLS cultures were harvested in buffer RLT Plus (1053393, Qiagen) and homogenized using a PRO Scientific Bio-Gen PRO200 Homogenizer (01–4607). This was followed by total RNA isolation with the RNAeasy plus mini kit (74106, Qiagen) following the manufacturer’s protocol. Subsequently, cDNA was synthesized from 150 ng of total RNA per 20 μL reaction using the iScript cDNA Synthesis Kit (1708841, Bio-Rad). The expression of IL-17A was quantified by RT-qPCR using TaqMan Gene expression assays mix (Mm00439618_m1, ThermoFisher Scientific). The gene expression was normalized using the mouse Gapdh gene expression assay (Mm99999915_g, ThermoFisher Scientific).

Multicolor immunofluorescence staining

Primary antibodies

The following antibodies were utilized for immunostaining: goat anti-CD3ε (clone M-20, Cat# sc-1127, RRID: AB_631128, Santa Cruz Biotechnology) at a 1:100 dilution, FITC-hamster anti-mouse CD69 (Cat# 119-15149, RayBiotech), rabbit anti-IL-17A (Cat # RQ4021, nsj Bioreagents) antibodies were used at a 1:50 dilution.

Secondary antibodies

Alexa Fluor 568-conjugated donkey anti-goat IgG (A-11057, RRID: AB_2534104, Thermo Fisher Scientific) dilution to detect CD3ε, FITC-conjugated anti-Syrian hamster IgG (307-096-003, RRID:AB_2339583, Jackson ImmunoResearch Laboratories) to amplify the signal of FITC-CD69 and Alexa Fluor 647 donkey anti-rabbit IgG (711-606-152, RRID:AB_2340625, Jackson ImmunoResearch Laboratories) to detect IL-17A were used at a 1:200 dilution.

Staining protocol

Briefly, 5 μm formalin-fixed paraffin sections were incubated at 60°C overnight for deparaffinization. The tissue sections were quickly transferred to xylene and gradually hydrated by sequential transfer to absolute alcohol, 96% alcohol, 70% alcohol, and finally water. Subsequently, the sections were immersed in Antigen Unmasking Solution (Vector Laboratories) and boiled for 2 h. Nonspecific binding was blocked with 5% normal donkey serum in PBS containing 0.5% Triton X-100 for 40 min at RT in a humidified chamber. Primary antibodies at appropriate concentrations were then added to these sections and incubated overnight, in a humid chamber and at room temperature (RT). Sections were washed with PBS and incubated with fluorescently-labeled secondary antibodies at RT for 2 hours. Finally, the slides were rinsed for 1 h in PBS and mounted with Vectashield antifade mounting medium with DAPI (H-1200-10, Vector Laboratories, Burlingame, CA, USA). Images were acquired with a Zeiss Axioplan 2 microscope connected to a Hamamatsu camera.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed with Prism (GraphPad) software. Student’s t-test was used for comparison between 2 groups. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Turkey’s post hoc analysis was used for comparing 3 or more groups. We performed a log transformation on the data and performed ANOVA on transformed data for analyses involving bacterial burdens. p values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. p values are annotated as follows: ∗, p ≤ 0.05; ∗∗, p ≤ 0.01; ∗∗∗, p ≤ 0.001; and ∗∗∗∗, p ≤ 0.0001. All of the statistical details of experiments can be found in the figure legends of the manuscript.
