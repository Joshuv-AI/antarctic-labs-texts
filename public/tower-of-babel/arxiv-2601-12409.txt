Topological quantum color code model on infinite lattice
Sheng Tan

Abstract
The color code model is a crucial instance of a Calderbank–Shor–Steane (CSS)-type topological quantum error-correcting code, which notably supports transversal implementation of the full Clifford group. Its robustness against local noise is rooted in the structure of its topological excitations. From the perspective of quantum phases of matter, it is essential to understand these excitations in the thermodynamic limit. In this work, we analyze the color code model on an infinite lattice within the quasi-local C ∗ C^{*} -algebra framework, using a cone-localized Doplicher-Haag-Roberts (DHR) analysis. We classify its irreducible anyon superselection sectors and construct explicit string operators that generate anyonic excitations from the ground state. We further examine the fusion and braiding properties of these excitations. Our results show that the topological order of the color code is described by 𝖱𝖾𝗉 ⁡ ( D ⁡ ( ℤ 2 × ℤ 2 ) ) ≃ 𝖱𝖾𝗉 ⁡ ( D ⁡ ( ℤ 2 ) ) ⊠ 𝖱𝖾𝗉 ⁡ ( D ⁡ ( ℤ 2 ) ) \mathsf{Rep}(D(\mathbb{Z}_{2}\times\mathbb{Z}_{2}))\simeq\mathsf{Rep}(D(\mathbb{Z}_{2}))\boxtimes\mathsf{Rep}(D(\mathbb{Z}_{2})) , which is equivalent to a double layer of the toric code and consistent with established analyses on finite lattices.



1
Introduction

The interplay between topological quantum matter and topological quantum information processing—encompassing topological quantum error correction and topological quantum computation—has attracted significant attention over the past several decades
[
16
,
31
]
. Two-dimensional topological lattice models, such as the quantum double model and its various generalizations
[
31
,
9
,
19
,
20
,
25
,
11
,
36
,
2
]
and string-net models and their generalizations
[
35
,
32
,
17
]
, have found fruitful applications in both condensed matter physics and quantum information and computation theory.

The color code is one of the simplest and most elegant leading topological quantum codes for implementing quantum error correction and enabling universal topological quantum computation
[
5
,
33
,
34
]
.
The toric code model (the
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
quantum double model, also called the surface code) cannot implement the full Clifford group of gates transversally; lattice surgery is required to realize some Clifford gates, which increases circuit depth and overall overhead.
In contrast, the 2d color code supports the full Clifford group of transversal gates, making it easier to achieve universality
[
5
,
33
]
.
Its ability to implement fault-tolerant logical gates depends strongly on the topological quantum phases of the underlying Hamiltonian model
[
28
,
29
]
.
Therefore, developing a deep understanding of the topological order of the color code model is a crucial problem.

The color code model is defined on a specific type of lattice, which satisfies the following conditions:
(1) it is trivalent, meaning exactly three edges meet at each vertex;
(2) its faces are colored with three distinct colors such that no two adjacent faces share the same color.
We label the faces as “red”, “green”, and “blue”. An edge connecting two red faces is called a red edge, and similarly for the other colors. Consequently, a face coloring naturally induces a corresponding edge coloring.
In this work, we focus on the trivalent lattice with square faces, denoted by
\Sigma
; see Figure
1
(right) for an illustration.
The sets of vertices, edges, and faces of
\Sigma
are denoted by
V(\Sigma)
,
E(\Sigma)
, and
F(\Sigma)
, respectively.

Figure 1
:
Two equivalent colored trivalent lattices used in color code model. Each vertex (represented by a black dot) hosts a physical qubit. The lattice is composed of hexagon (left) or square (right) faces, each face colored red, green, or blue, in such a way that no two adjacent faces share the same color. Each vertex is trivalent, meaning exactly three edges meet at each vertex.

The Hamiltonian for the color code model is defined as the negative sum of two types of stabilizer operators, with each operator acting on the six vertices of a face on the lattice.
Specifically, for each face
f\in F(\Sigma)
, define

K_{f}:=\bigotimes_{v\in\partial f}\sigma_{v}^{\mathtt{x}},\quad J_{f}:=\bigotimes_{v\in\partial f}\sigma_{v}^{\mathtt{z}},

where
v\in\partial f
means
v
is one of vertices of the face
f
, and
we use the Pauli matrices
\sigma^{\mathtt{x}}
,
\sigma^{\mathtt{y}}
,
\sigma^{\mathtt{z}}
:

\sigma^{\mathtt{x}}=\begin{bmatrix}0&1\\
1&0\end{bmatrix},\quad\sigma^{\mathtt{y}}=\begin{bmatrix}0&-i\\
i&0\end{bmatrix},\quad\sigma^{\mathtt{z}}=\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\
0&-1\end{bmatrix}.

Note that the Pauli matrices satisfy the following relations:

\sigma^{i}\sigma^{j}=\delta_{ij}\,\mathds{1}+i\,\varepsilon_{ijk}\,\sigma^{k},

where
i=1,2,3
correspond to
\mathtt{x},\mathtt{y},\mathtt{z}
, respectively, and
\varepsilon_{ijk}
denotes the Levi-Civita symbol.
It follows immediately that any two Pauli matrices either commute or anti-commute with each other.

It is straightforward to verify that

[K_{f},J_{g}]=0,\qquad(K_{f})^{2}=(J_{f})^{2}=\mathds{1},\qquad(K_{f})^{\dagger}=K_{f},\quad(J_{f})^{\dagger}=J_{f},

for any
f,g\in F(\Sigma)
. Indeed, any two faces share an even number of common vertices, ensuring commutativity through the relation
\sigma^{\mathtt{x}}\sigma^{\mathtt{z}}=-\sigma^{\mathtt{z}}\sigma^{\mathtt{x}}
. The remaining identities follow directly from
(\sigma^{i})^{2}=\mathds{1}
and
(\sigma^{i})^{\dagger}=\sigma^{i}
for
i={\mathtt{x}},{\mathtt{z}}
.
Let
\Lambda\subset V(\Sigma)
be a finite subset. The associated local Hamiltonian is defined as

H_{\Lambda}=-\sum_{f:f\subset\Lambda}K_{f}-\sum_{f:f\subset\Lambda}J_{f}.

The sums run over all faces whose vertices lie entirely within
\Lambda
. When
\Sigma
is a finite lattice, we obtain the color code Hamiltonian
H_{\Sigma}
.
For a lattice
\Sigma_{g}
on a two-dimensional surface of genus
g
, the ground-state degeneracy of
H_{\Sigma_{g}}
is

\operatorname{GSD}=2^{4g}=2^{2(2-\chi)},

where
\chi=2-2g
is the Euler characteristic of the surface.
Since the ground-state degeneracy depends only on the genus—a topological invariant—it is robust to local perturbations, reflecting the topological nature of the color code. The ground state space serves as the code space, safeguarding logical quantum information against noise-induced errors.

The aim of this work is to carry out a systematic investigation of the color code model in the thermodynamic limit, namely on an infinite planar lattice
\Sigma
.
Notice that in this setting, the summation appearing in the Hamiltonian may fail to converge and should therefore be understood only as a formal expression.
A suitable framework for treating such systems is the quasi-local algebra
[
13
,
14
]
, an approximately finite-dimensional (AF)
C^{*}
-algebra that captures the underlying locality structure.
Recently, there has been substantial progress along this direction for various models; see, for example,
[
37
,
45
,
4
,
10
,
3
]
.
In this work, we develop the corresponding theory for the color code model on an infinite lattice.
For finite-lattice color codes, prior studies have firmly established that topological excitations are categorically characterized by
\mathsf{Rep}(D(\mathbb{Z}_{2}\times\mathbb{Z}_{2}))
, the representation category of the quantum double
D(\mathbb{Z}_{2}\times\mathbb{Z}_{2})
.
Extending this result to the infinite-lattice setting, we construct the superselection sector theory for the thermodynamic-limit color code and prove that its category of anyon sectors is isomorphic to
\mathsf{Rep}(D(\mathbb{Z}_{2}\times\mathbb{Z}_{2}))
. We also show that the ground state of color code model satisfies the Haag duality.

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows.
Section
2
establishes the existence and purity of the ground state.
Section
3
develops the superselection sector theory for topological excitations of the color code, together with their fusion rules and braiding; we then show that these excitations form a theory equivalent to the category
\mathsf{Rep}(D(\mathbb{Z}_{2}\times\mathbb{Z}_{2}))
.
Section
4
establishes Haag duality for the color code.
For completeness, the appendix provides details on the representation theory of the quantum double
D(\mathbb{Z}_{2}\times\mathbb{Z}_{2})
.

2
Ground state of color code model

In this section, we establish the existence and essential properties of the ground state within the
C^{*}
-algebraic framework for quantum spin systems
[
6
,
7
]
. We begin by reviewing necessary tools from the theory of operator algebras and constructing the quasi-local algebra associated with the infinite colored lattice. We then prove that the color code Hamiltonian admits a unique translationally invariant ground state, which is a pure state satisfying the ground state condition. This result provides the foundational vacuum sector upon which the theory of topological excitations, developed in the subsequent sections, will be built.

A state on a
C^{*}
-algebra
\mathcal{A}
is a positive linear functional
\omega:\mathcal{A}\to\mathbb{C}
of norm one.
If
\mathcal{A}
is unital with unit element
\mathds{1}
, the norm one condition reads
\omega(\mathds{1})=1
.
The quasi-local algebra we consider here is unital.
The positive condition means that
\omega(a^{*}a)\geq 0
for all
a\in\mathcal{A}
.
The following provides a fundamental tool for evaluating a state on a general element.
The proof follows immediately by virtue of the Cauchy-Schwartz inequality for states, cf.
[
1
]
.

Lemma 2.1

(
[
1
, Sec. 2.1.1]
)
.

Let
\omega
be a state on a unital
C^{*}
-algebra
\mathcal{A}
. Let
a\in\mathcal{A}
be a self-adjoint element such that
a\leq\mathds{1}
and
\omega(a)=1
. Then for any
b\in\mathcal{A}
, one has

\omega(b)=\omega(ab)=\omega(ba).

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be a
C^{*}
-algebra, and define the
stabilizer subalgebra

\mathcal{A}_{\rm stab}
as the subalgebra generated by a set of commuting involutory elements
\{g_{i}\}_{i\in I}
,

\mathcal{A}_{\rm stab}=\big\langle g_{i}\in\mathcal{A}\,:\,g_{i}g_{j}=g_{j}g_{i},\,g_{i}^{2}=\mathds{1},\forall\,i,j\in I\big\rangle.

Then there is a state on
\mathcal{A}_{\rm stab}
such that

\omega(g_{i})=1,\quad\forall\,i\in I.

Proof.

Consider the index set configuration space over the stabilizer algebra’s generator index set
I
defined by

\mathcal{X}=\{f:I\to\{\pm 1\}\},

which is equivalently the product space
\{\pm 1\}^{I}
.
Equipping
\{\pm 1\}
with the discrete topology, the product space
\mathcal{X}
becomes a compact Hausdorff space under the product topology, by Tychonoff’s theorem.
Then we obtain a commutative
C^{*}
algebra
C(\mathcal{X})
consisting of complex-valued continuous functions on
\mathcal{X}
(the multiplication is multiplication of functions and
*
-operation is complex conjugation).

The evaluation map
\tilde{g}_{i}:\mathcal{X}\to\{\pm 1\}\subset\mathbb{C}
is defined as

\tilde{g}_{i}(f):=f(i).

It is clear that
\tilde{g}_{i}^{*}(f)=\overline{f(i)}=f(i)=\tilde{g}_{i}(f)
; thus,
\tilde{g}_{i}^{*}=\tilde{g}_{i}
. Also,
\tilde{g}_{i}^{2}(f)=\tilde{g}_{i}(f)\tilde{g}_{i}(f)=f(i)^{2}=1
, and therefore
\tilde{g}_{i}^{2}=1
.
Consider the subalgebra
\tilde{\mathcal{A}}\subset C(\mathcal{X})
generated by those
\tilde{g}_{i}
, then we have the isomorphism

\mathcal{A}_{\rm stab}\cong\tilde{\mathcal{A}}.

We now define a functional
\omega_{0}:\tilde{\mathcal{A}}\to\mathbb{C}
by

\omega_{0}(\tilde{h})=\tilde{h}(1_{I}),

where
1_{I}\in\mathcal{X}
is the configuration that maps all indices to
1
.

It is straightforward to see that
\omega_{0}(1)=1
, since
1\in C(\mathcal{X})
denotes the constant function taking the value
1
on all inputs.
For any positive element
\tilde{k}^{*}\tilde{k}
, we have

\omega_{0}(\tilde{k}^{*}\tilde{k})=\overline{\tilde{k}(1_{I})}\,\tilde{k}(1_{I})\geq 0.

Hence,
\omega_{0}
defines a state on
\tilde{\mathcal{A}}
.
On the generators
\tilde{g}_{i}
, we have

\omega_{0}(\tilde{g}_{i})=\tilde{g}_{i}(1_{I})=1.

Using the isomorphism
\mathcal{A}_{\rm stab}\cong\tilde{\mathcal{A}}
, the claim follows.
∎

2.1
Quasi-local algebra

We now construct the quasi-local algebra
\mathcal{A}
associated with a colored trivalent lattice
\Sigma
embedded in the plane, which is equal to the inductive limit of a local net of finite algebras.
In a color code model, a qubit is placed on each vertex of the lattice.
Recall that
V(\Sigma)
denotes the set of vertices of
\Sigma
.
For each site
v\in V(\Sigma)
, the local state space is
\mathcal{H}_{\{v\}}=\mathbb{C}[\mathbb{Z}_{2}]\simeq\mathbb{C}^{2}
, where the observables are
\mathcal{A}(\{v\})=\mathbf{B}(\mathbb{C}^{2})=M_{2}(\mathbb{C})
. Here
M_{2}(\mathbb{C})
is the matrix algebra of complex
2\times 2
matrices.

For a finite subset
\Lambda\subset V(\Sigma)
,
\mathcal{A}(\Lambda)
is defined to be the algebra consisting of observables living on the vertices of
\Lambda
, i.e.,
\mathcal{A}(\Lambda)=\otimes_{v\in\Lambda}M_{2}(\mathbb{C})
. This algebra acts on the local Hilbert space supporting on
\Lambda
:
\mathcal{H}_{\Lambda}=\otimes_{v\in\Lambda}\mathbb{C}^{2}
. For an inclusion
\Lambda_{1}\subset\Lambda_{2}
of finite subsets, there is a natural inclusion of algebras

\mathcal{A}(\Lambda_{1})\hookrightarrow\mathcal{A}(\Lambda_{2}),\quad A\mapsto A\otimes\mathds{1}_{\Lambda_{2}\setminus\Lambda_{1}},

(2.1)

where
\mathds{1}_{\Lambda_{2}\setminus\Lambda_{1}}=\otimes_{v\in{\Lambda_{2}\setminus\Lambda_{1}}}\mathds{1}_{\{v\}}
.
Here the identification
\mathcal{H}_{\Lambda_{2}}=\mathcal{H}_{\Lambda_{1}}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{\Lambda_{2}\setminus\Lambda_{1}}
induces the algebra isomorphism
\mathcal{A}({\Lambda_{2}})\simeq\mathcal{A}({\Lambda_{1}})\otimes\mathcal{A}({\Lambda_{2}\setminus\Lambda_{1}})
.
The algebra of local observables is given by

\mathcal{A}_{\rm loc}=\bigcup_{\Lambda\subset_{f}V(\Sigma)}\mathcal{A}(\Lambda),

where
\Lambda\subset_{f}V(\Sigma)
means the union is taken over all finite subsets
\Lambda
of
V(\Sigma)
under the natural inclusion (
2.1
).
This algebra is equipped with a norm
\|\cdot\|
induced from the norm from the matrix algebra
M_{2}(\mathbb{C})
.
Hence the quasi-local algebra
\mathcal{A}
is defined to be the norm completion of
\mathcal{A}_{\rm loc}
:

\mathcal{A}=\overline{\mathcal{A}_{\rm loc}}^{\|\cdot\|}=\overline{\bigcup_{\Lambda\subset_{f}V(\Sigma)}\mathcal{A}(\Lambda)}^{\|\cdot\|}.

For an arbitrary subset
\Lambda\subset V(\Sigma)
, we can perform an analogous construction to obtain the algebra localized in
\Lambda
:

\mathcal{A}(\Lambda)=\overline{\bigcup_{\Lambda^{\prime}\subset_{f}\Lambda}\mathcal{A}(\Lambda^{\prime})}^{\|\cdot\|},

where
\Lambda^{\prime}\subset_{f}\Lambda
means the union is taken over all finite subsets
\Lambda^{\prime}
of
\Lambda
. If
A\in\mathcal{A}(\Lambda)
, we say that
A
is localized in
\Lambda
; the support
\operatorname{supp}(A)
of
A
is the smallest
\Lambda\subset V(\Sigma)
in which
A
is localized.

Any state on a
C^{*}
algebra gives rise to a GNS representation. Pure state corresponds to an irreducible representation.
We will use the following to determine if two GNS representations are equivalent.

Proposition 2.3

(
[
40
, Prop. 3.2.8]
)
.

The GNS representations of two pure states
\omega
,
\omega^{\prime}
on the quasi-local algebra
\mathcal{A}
are equivalent if and only if for any
\varepsilon>0
, there is a finite subset
S_{\varepsilon}\subset V(\Sigma)
such that
\|\omega(A)-\omega^{\prime}(A)\|<\varepsilon\|A\|
for all
A\in\mathcal{A}(S)
with
S
any finite subset of
S_{\varepsilon}^{c}:=V(\Sigma)\setminus S_{\varepsilon}
.

2.2
Ground states

By
[
7
, Thm. 6.2.4]
, we can define a derivation
\delta
with domain
D(\delta)=\mathcal{A}_{\rm loc}
as follows. Suppose
A\in\mathcal{A}_{\rm loc}
is localized in
\Lambda
. Then we have

\delta(A)=i[H_{\Lambda},A].

This derivation generates a dynamics
\alpha
on the quasi-local algebra
\mathcal{A}
, i.e., a one-parameter group of automorphisms
t\mapsto\alpha_{t}\in\operatorname{Aut}(\mathcal{A})
. On the finite subset
\Lambda
, the action
\alpha_{t}^{\Lambda}:=\alpha_{t}|_{\mathcal{A}(\Lambda)}
on
\mathcal{A}(\Lambda)
reads

\alpha_{t}^{\Lambda}(A)=e^{itH_{\Lambda}}Ae^{-itH_{\Lambda}},\quad A\in\mathcal{A}(\Lambda).

Definition 2.4
.

A ground state for the system
(\mathcal{A},\alpha)
is a state
\omega
on the quasi-local algebra
\mathcal{A}
satisfying the condition

-i\omega(A^{\dagger}\delta(A))\geq 0,\quad\text{for~all}~A\in\mathcal{A}_{\rm loc}.

Theorem 2.5
.

The color code model on the plane has a unique translationally invariant state
\omega_{0}
, which is a pure state.

Proof.

The proof follows an approach analogous to the toric code case.
The foundational idea was initially presented in
[
1
, Sec. 2.2]
; a complete technical proof for the toric code appears in
[
40
, Sec. 3.5]
.

Let
\mathcal{A}_{\rm stab}
be the unital
*
-subalgebra of
\mathcal{A}
generated by all stabilizer operators
\{K_{f},J_{f}\}_{f}
, which is Abelian since any two operators in the generating set commute. By Lemma
2.2
, there is a state
\omega
on
\mathcal{A}_{\rm stab}
such that

\omega(K_{f})=\omega(J_{f})=1,

(2.2)

for all
f\in F(\Sigma)
.
Note that
K_{f}\leq\mathds{1}
,
J_{f}\leq\mathds{1}
.
Hence by Lemma
2.1
, the condition (
2.2
) determines
\omega
on
\mathcal{A}_{\rm stab}
completely.

By Hahn-Banach extension theorem, there is an extension
\omega_{0}:\mathcal{A}\to\mathbb{C}
that extends
\omega
.
Now we will show that
\omega_{0}
is a ground state in the sense of Definition
2.4
. Let
A\in\mathcal{A}(\Lambda)
be a local operator.
Then we have

\delta(A)=-i\sum_{f\cap\Lambda\neq\emptyset}[K_{f},A]-i\sum_{f\cap\Lambda\neq\emptyset}[J_{f},A],

where the sum runs over all faces whose set of vertices has a nonempty intersection with
\Lambda
.
Therefore we have

\displaystyle-i\omega_{0}(A^{\dagger}\delta(A))

\displaystyle=\sum_{f\cap\Lambda\neq\emptyset}(-\omega_{0}(A^{\dagger}K_{f}A)+\omega_{0}(A^{\dagger}AK_{f}))

\displaystyle+\sum_{f\cap\Lambda\neq\emptyset}(-\omega_{0}(A^{\dagger}J_{f}A)+\omega_{0}(A^{\dagger}AJ_{f})).

By Lemma
2.1
,
\omega_{0}(A^{\dagger}AK_{f})=\omega_{0}(A^{\dagger}A)=\omega_{0}(A^{\dagger}AJ_{f})
. Also note that

-\omega_{0}(A^{\dagger}K_{f}A)+\omega_{0}(A^{\dagger}A)=\omega_{0}(A^{\dagger}(\mathds{1}-K_{f})A)\geq 0,

as
A^{\dagger}(\mathds{1}-K_{f})A
is positive. Similar observation holds for
J_{f}
.
It follows that
-i\omega_{0}(A^{\dagger}\delta(A))\geq 0
for all
A\in\mathcal{A}_{\rm loc}
. By definition, this means
\omega_{0}
is a ground state.

Next, we show that such an extension is uniquely determined by the condition (
2.2
) for all faces
f\in F(\Sigma)
.
By linearity, it suffices to compute the values of
\omega_{0}
on monomials in the Pauli matrices.
Let
A
be such an operator, with support
\Lambda
.
If
A
anti-commutes with some
K_{f}
or
J_{f}
, then
\omega_{0}(A)=0
, as Lemma
2.1
implies
\omega_{0}(A)=\omega_{0}(K_{f}AK_{f})=-\omega_{0}(A)
.
Now suppose
\omega_{0}(A)\neq 0
.
Thus
A
cannot anti-commute with any
K_{f}
and
J_{f}
.
Consider the northernmost, and then easternmost, vertex
v\in\Lambda
.
Then
v
cannot be the vertex
v_{3}
in the following picture:

3
Topological excitations of color code

In this section, we develop the superselection sector theory for the topological excitations of the quantum color code within the Doplicher-Haag-Roberts (DHR) framework. Our goal is to construct and classify the irreducible anyon sectors, derive their associated fusion rules and braiding statistics, and demonstrate that the resulting braided tensor category is equivalent to
\mathsf{Rep}(D(\mathbb{Z}_{2}\times\mathbb{Z}_{2}))
. This provides a rigorous characterization of the topological order of color code model in the thermodynamic limit, consistent with known results for finite lattices. We begin by recalling the known modular tensor category structure, then explicitly construct the superselection sectors via string operators, and finally establish their fusion and braiding properties.

3.1
Modular tensor category of topological excitations in the color code

The topological excitations in color code models are point-like quasiparticles that cannot be created or annihilated by local operators.
It is known that, on a finite lattice, the modular tensor category
\mathsf{CC}_{\mathbb{Z}_{2}}

\mathsf{CC}_{\mathbb{Z}_{2}}\simeq\mathsf{TC}_{\mathbb{Z}_{2}}\boxtimes\mathsf{TC}_{\mathbb{Z}_{2}}\simeq\mathsf{Rep}(D(\mathbb{Z}_{2}\times\mathbb{Z}_{2})),

where
D(\mathbb{Z}_{2}\times\mathbb{Z}_{2})
is the Drinfeld quantum double of
\mathbb{Z}_{2}\times\mathbb{Z}_{2}
and “
\boxtimes

This correspondence is useful for understanding the topological order of the color code model and plays a crucial role in quantum error correction.

3.2
String operators

Figure 2
:
An illustration of three strings:
\gamma^{\mathtt{r}}
(red),
\gamma^{\mathtt{g}}
(green), and
\gamma^{\mathtt{b}}
(blue), arranged from left to right. The blue string
\gamma^{\mathtt{b}}
on the right is a closed string.

To concretely generate and manipulate the anyonic excitations, we give a precise definition of colored strings on the color code lattice
\Sigma
and construct the associated string operators.
These are local operators supported on one-dimensional paths on the lattice, whose endpoints create pairs of topological charges.

Given a coloring of the faces
F(\Sigma)
, we can assign a coloring of the edges
E(\Sigma)
in the following way: an edge is colored by
c
if it connects two faces colored by
c
.
Roughly speaking, a finite string is a path that traverses consecutive edges and faces, all of which share the same color.
The rigorous definition is given as follows.

Definition 3.1

(String)
.

A null string is a string with no support.
A single vertex is considered to be a string.
Let
c\in\{\mathtt{r},\mathtt{g},\mathtt{b}\}
.
A finite string
\gamma^{c}
of type
c
is a path composed of finitely many consecutive
c
-colored faces and edges, which starts and terminates on faces.
Formally, it is a sequence

\gamma^{c}=(f_{0},v_{0},v_{1},f_{1},v_{2},v_{3},\cdots,f_{n},v_{2n},v_{2n+1},f_{n+1}),

where faces
\{f_{i}\}_{i=0}^{n+1}
and edges
\{e_{2i,2i+1}\}_{i=0}^{n}
are colored by
c
, such that
v_{0}\in\partial f_{0}
,
v_{2i-1},v_{2i}\in\partial f_{i}
for
1\leq i\leq n
,
v_{2n+1}\in\partial f_{n+1}
, and the edge
e_{2j,2j+1}
connects the faces
f_{2j}
and
f_{2j+1}
, for
0\leq j\leq n
.
We denote by
\partial_{0}\gamma^{c}=f_{0}
the starting face of
\gamma^{c}
, and by
\partial_{1}\gamma^{c}=f_{n+1}
the terminating face of
\gamma^{c}
.
If
\partial_{0}\gamma^{c}=\partial_{1}\gamma^{c}
, then
\gamma^{c}
is called a closed string.
The support of
\gamma^{c}
is the set of vertices
\{v_{0},v_{1},\cdots,v_{2n+1}\}
.

As an illustration, Figure
2
provides examples of three strings:
\gamma^{\mathtt{r}}
,
\gamma^{\mathtt{g}}
, and
\gamma^{\mathtt{b}}
, with their supports are labeled by bullets. The green string, for instance, is given by

\gamma^{\mathtt{g}}=(f_{0},v_{0},v_{1},f_{1},v_{2},v_{3},f_{2},v_{4},v_{5},f_{3},v_{6},v_{7},f_{4}),

where
f_{0}
and
f_{4}
are the starting and terminating faces, respectively, and the support is the green bullets.
The blue string
\gamma^{\mathtt{b}}
is a closed string.

A string operator is an operator in
\mathcal{A}_{\rm loc}
supporting on a string.

Definition 3.2

(String operator)
.

An identity operator is associated to a null string.
If
\gamma=\{v\}
is a string of a single vertex, define its string operators by
S_{\gamma}^{\mathtt{x}}=\sigma_{v}^{\mathtt{x}}
,
S_{\gamma}^{\mathtt{z}}=\sigma_{v}^{\mathtt{z}}
, and
S_{\gamma}^{\mathtt{y}}=S_{\gamma}^{\mathtt{x}}S_{\gamma}^{\mathtt{z}}
.
Let
c\in\{\mathtt{r},\mathtt{g},\mathtt{b}\}
. Let
\gamma
be a
c
-colored finite string.
The string operators are defined by

S_{\gamma}^{c\mathtt{x}}=\bigotimes_{v_{j}\in\gamma}\sigma_{v_{j}}^{\mathtt{x}},\quad S_{\gamma}^{c\mathtt{z}}=\bigotimes_{v_{j}\in\gamma}\sigma_{v_{j}}^{z},\;S_{\gamma}^{c\mathtt{y}}=S_{\gamma}^{c\mathtt{x}}S_{\gamma}^{c\mathtt{z}}.

The string operator
S_{\gamma}^{ck}
is said to be of type
ck
.

For example, the string operators associated to the green string
\gamma=\gamma^{\mathtt{g}}
in Figure
2
are given by

S_{\gamma}^{\mathtt{g}\mathtt{x}}=\bigotimes_{j=0}^{7}\sigma_{v_{j}}^{\mathtt{x}},\quad S_{\gamma}^{\mathtt{g}\mathtt{y}}=\bigotimes_{j=0}^{7}\sigma_{v_{j}}^{\mathtt{x}}\sigma_{v_{j}}^{\mathtt{z}},\quad S_{\gamma}^{\mathtt{g}\mathtt{z}}=\bigotimes_{j=0}^{7}\sigma_{v_{j}}^{\mathtt{z}}.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
\gamma_{1}
and
\gamma_{2}
be two different strings of the same type
c\in\{\mathtt{r},\mathtt{g},\mathtt{b}\}
connecting the same starting and terminating faces. Then for all
k\in\{\mathtt{x},\mathtt{y},\mathtt{z}\}
,
S_{\gamma_{1}}^{ck}
can be deformed to
S_{\gamma_{2}}^{ck}
by multiplying suitable
K_{f}
and
J_{f}
.

Proof.

Without loss of generality, we may suppose
c=\mathtt{r}
and
k=\mathtt{x}
.
Let
f_{1},\cdots,f_{N}
be the faces inside the region bounded by
\gamma_{1}
and
\gamma_{2}
that are colored either by
\mathtt{g}
or
\mathtt{b}
. Then one can verify that
S_{\gamma_{1}}^{\mathtt{r}\mathtt{x}}A=S_{\gamma_{2}}^{\mathtt{r}\mathtt{x}}
, where
A=\otimes_{j=1}^{N}K_{f_{j}}
.
For
k=\mathtt{y}
, one needs to engage
K
and
J
stabilizers in the definition of
A
.
∎

The string operator
S_{\gamma}^{ck}
associated with an open string
\gamma
creates excitations at its starting and terminating faces
\partial_{0}\gamma
,
\partial_{1}\gamma
.
This occurs because at each ending face, there is exactly one
c
-colored face
\partial_{i}\gamma
that shares only a single vertex with
\gamma
; the string operator anti-commutes with the corresponding stabilizer (
K_{f}
or
J_{f}
) at this face.
In contrast, all other faces share an even number of vertices with
\gamma
and thus commute with the string operator.
An excitation created by
S_{\gamma}^{ck}
is labeled by
\pi^{ck}
. All nine of these excitations are bosons. There are six fermions obtained by fusing two bosons that differ in both
c
and
k
labels, cf.
[
28
]
.
If
\gamma
is a closed string, then
S_{\gamma}^{ck}
is a product of stabilizer operators by the proof of the above lemma.
Since
K_{f}\Omega=J_{f}\Omega=\Omega
for all
f
, the same holds for the closed string operator, where we identify an operator in
\mathcal{A}
with its image in
\pi_{0}(\mathcal{A})
.

3.3
Superselection sectors

We adopt the method of
[
37
]
-originally used for the toric code-to study excitations in the color code. The idea is to create a pair of excitations using a string operator and then move one of them to infinity. We will construct a braided tensor category such that it is braided equivalent to the representation category of the quantum double
D(\mathbb{Z}_{2}\times\mathbb{Z}_{2})
, which characterizes the excitations of the color code model. The objects are composed by the so-called localized and transportable endomorphisms.

We will mainly use cone region in this paper, but the shape is not so essential in most of the cases.
On the plane
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, a cone with an apex at the origin can be expressed by

\Lambda=\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\,|\,x\cdot a>\|x\|\cos\theta\right\},

for some
\theta\in(0,\pi)
(the half-opening angle) and unit vector
a\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
(the axis).
The other cones can be constructed by translation of cones at the origin.
A cone in the vertex set
V(\Sigma)
is by convention the intersection of
V(\Sigma)
with a cone
\Lambda
in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
.
By abuse of notation, we also denote this cone by
\Lambda
.
For example, a cone
\Lambda
in
V(\Sigma)
is depicted in Figure
3
, where
\Lambda
consists of the black bullet points.
We denote by
\Lambda^{c}
the complement of a cone
\Lambda
in
V(\Sigma)
.

Figure 3
:
A cone with apex
a
. The black bullet vertices are considered to be inside the cone.

Let
(\pi_{0},\mathcal{H},\Omega)
be a GNS representation of the unique ground state
\omega_{0}
in Theorem
2.5
.

Definition 3.4
.

1. A
*
-homomorphism
\pi:\mathcal{A}\to\mathbf{B}(\mathcal{H})
is said to be localized in a cone
\Lambda
if it acts as
\pi_{0}
outside
\Lambda
; that is,
\pi(A)=\pi_{0}(A)
for all
A\in\mathcal{A}(\Lambda^{c})
.

2. A
*
-homomorphism
\pi:\mathcal{A}\to\mathbf{B}(\mathcal{H})
localized in a cone
\Lambda
is said to be transportable if for any cone
\Lambda^{\prime}
, there is a
*
-homomorphism
\pi^{\prime}:\mathcal{A}\to\mathbf{B}(\mathcal{H})
localized in
\Lambda^{\prime}
that is unitarily equivalent to
\pi
, namely, there is a unitary
U
such that
\pi^{\prime}(A)=U^{\dagger}\pi(A)U
for all
A\in\mathcal{A}
.

Definition 3.5
.

A representation
\pi
of
\mathcal{A}
is said to satisfy the superselection criterion if for any cone
\Lambda
, it holds that

\pi|_{\mathcal{A}(\Lambda^{c})}\simeq\pi_{0}|_{\mathcal{A}(\Lambda^{c})},

where
\simeq
means unitary equivalence. Such a representation is called a superselection sector.

Let
\Lambda
be a cone. We consider a half-infinite string
\gamma
which is contained in
\Lambda
, meaning the support of
\gamma
is contained in
\Lambda
. Denote by
\gamma_{n}
the finite string formed by first
n
faces and all intermediate vertices of
\gamma
. For a finite operator
A\in\mathcal{A}_{\rm loc}
,
c\in\{\mathtt{r},\mathtt{g},\mathtt{b}\}
, and
k\in\{\mathtt{x},\mathtt{y},\mathtt{z}\}
, define

\rho_{\gamma}^{ck}(A):=\lim_{n\to\infty}(S_{\gamma_{n}}^{ck})^{\dagger}AS_{\gamma_{n}}^{ck},

where the convergence is taken in norm topology.
This is well-defined, as the support of
A
is finite, there exists an
N>0
such that
\gamma\setminus\gamma_{n}
does not intersect with the support of
A
for
n\geq N
, which results in
(S_{\gamma_{n}}^{ck})^{\dagger}AS_{\gamma_{n}}^{ck}=(S_{\gamma_{N}}^{ck})^{\dagger}AS_{\gamma_{N}}^{ck}
.
Extending by continuity, we obtain an endomorphism
\rho_{\gamma}^{ck}:\mathcal{A}\to\mathcal{A}
(cf.
[
37
]
).

Proposition 3.6
.

Let
\Lambda
be a cone,
c\in\{\mathtt{r},\mathtt{g},\mathtt{b}\}
, and
k\in\{\mathtt{x},\mathtt{y},\mathtt{z}\}
.
Let
\gamma
be a half-infinite string of type
c
in
\Lambda
. Then
\pi_{0}\mathchoice{\mathrel{\raisebox{0.86108pt}{$\scriptstyle\circ$}}}{\mathrel{\raisebox{0.86108pt}{$\scriptstyle\circ$}}}{\mathrel{\raisebox{0.43057pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\circ$}}}{\mathrel{\raisebox{0.43057pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\circ$}}}\rho_{\gamma}^{ck}:\mathcal{A}\to\mathbf{B}(\mathcal{H})
is localized in
\Lambda
, and is transportable.
Moreover, the state
\omega_{f}^{ck}:=\omega_{0}\mathchoice{\mathrel{\raisebox{0.86108pt}{$\scriptstyle\circ$}}}{\mathrel{\raisebox{0.86108pt}{$\scriptstyle\circ$}}}{\mathrel{\raisebox{0.43057pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\circ$}}}{\mathrel{\raisebox{0.43057pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\circ$}}}\rho_{\gamma}^{ck}
only depends on the starting face
f=\partial_{0}\gamma
, but not on the string
\gamma
.

Proof.

The localization follows since if
A\in\mathcal{A}(\Lambda^{c})
, then
[A,S_{\gamma_{n}}^{ck}]=0
for all
n
, resulting in
\rho_{\gamma}^{ck}(A)=A
and
\pi_{0}\mathchoice{\mathrel{\raisebox{0.86108pt}{$\scriptstyle\circ$}}}{\mathrel{\raisebox{0.86108pt}{$\scriptstyle\circ$}}}{\mathrel{\raisebox{0.43057pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\circ$}}}{\mathrel{\raisebox{0.43057pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\circ$}}}\rho_{\gamma}^{ck}(A)=\pi_{0}(A)
.
To show the transportability, let
\Lambda^{\prime}
be an arbitrary cone.
Choose a
c
-colored face
f^{\prime}
in
\Lambda^{\prime}
and a half-infinite string
\gamma^{\prime}
of type
c
in
\Lambda^{\prime}
starting from
f^{\prime}
.
Using a similar argument as in
[
37
, Lem. 4.1]
(see Proposition
3.11
), one can show that there is a unitary
U
which is an intertwiner from
\rho_{\gamma}^{ck}
to
\rho_{\gamma^{\prime}}^{ck}
; that is,
U\rho_{\gamma}^{ck}(A)=\rho_{\gamma^{\prime}}^{ck}(A)U
for all
A\in\mathcal{A}
.
In fact,
U
can be taken as the limit of the unitaries
U_{n}:=S_{\gamma_{n}}^{ck}S_{\tilde{\gamma}_{n}}^{ck}S_{\gamma^{\prime}_{n}}^{ck}
under the weak topology, where
\tilde{\gamma}_{n}
is any string of type
c
from
\partial_{1}\gamma_{n}
to
\partial_{1}\gamma^{\prime}_{n}
such that
\operatorname{dist}(\tilde{\gamma}_{n},f)
and
\operatorname{dist}(\tilde{\gamma}_{n},f^{\prime})
approaches to infinity as
n\to\infty
.
Therefore, it is direct to see that
\pi_{0}\mathchoice{\mathrel{\raisebox{0.86108pt}{$\scriptstyle\circ$}}}{\mathrel{\raisebox{0.86108pt}{$\scriptstyle\circ$}}}{\mathrel{\raisebox{0.43057pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\circ$}}}{\mathrel{\raisebox{0.43057pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\circ$}}}\rho_{\gamma}^{ck}
is equivalent to
\pi_{0}\mathchoice{\mathrel{\raisebox{0.86108pt}{$\scriptstyle\circ$}}}{\mathrel{\raisebox{0.86108pt}{$\scriptstyle\circ$}}}{\mathrel{\raisebox{0.43057pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\circ$}}}{\mathrel{\raisebox{0.43057pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\circ$}}}\rho_{\gamma^{\prime}}^{ck}
which is localized in
\Lambda^{\prime}
.

It remains to show the last statement. This follows from Lemma
3.3
as shown in the following.
Without lose of generality, we may suppose
c=\mathtt{r}
and
k=\mathtt{x}
. Let
\gamma^{1}
and
\gamma^{2}
be two half-infinite strings of type
\mathtt{r}
in
\Lambda
, both starting at
f
.
Let
A\in\mathcal{A}_{\rm loc}
.
Then there is an
n_{0}>0
such that
(\gamma^{1}\setminus\gamma^{1}_{n})\cap\operatorname{supp}(A)=(\gamma^{2}\setminus\gamma^{2}_{n})\cap\operatorname{supp}(A)=\emptyset
for all
n\geq n_{0}
.
Let
\gamma_{n}
be a string of type
\mathtt{x}
connecting the terminating faces of
\gamma^{1}_{n}
and
\gamma_{n}^{2}
.
Then by Lemma
3.3
, there is an operator
X=\otimes_{j=1}^{N}K_{f_{j}}
such that
S_{\gamma^{2}_{n}}^{\mathtt{r}\mathtt{x}}=S_{\gamma^{1}_{n}\gamma_{n}}^{\mathtt{r}\mathtt{x}}X
, where
f_{1},\cdots,f_{N}
are faces inside the region bounded by
\gamma^{1}_{n}
,
\gamma_{n}^{2}
and
\gamma_{n}
that are colored either by
\mathtt{g}
or
\mathtt{b}
. It follows that for
n\geq n_{0}
,

\omega_{0}((S_{\gamma^{2}_{n}}^{\mathtt{r}\mathtt{x}})^{\dagger}AS_{\gamma^{2}_{n}}^{\mathtt{r}\mathtt{x}})=\omega_{0}(X^{\dagger}(S_{\gamma^{1}_{n}\gamma_{n}}^{\mathtt{r}\mathtt{x}})^{\dagger}AS_{\gamma^{1}_{n}\gamma_{n}}^{\mathtt{r}\mathtt{x}}X)=\omega_{0}((S_{\gamma^{1}_{n}}^{\mathtt{r}\mathtt{x}})^{\dagger}AS_{\gamma^{1}_{n}}^{\mathtt{r}\mathtt{x}}),

where the last equality comes from Lemma
2.1
and
[A,S_{\gamma_{n}}]=0
.
This shows that
\omega_{0}\mathchoice{\mathrel{\raisebox{0.86108pt}{$\scriptstyle\circ$}}}{\mathrel{\raisebox{0.86108pt}{$\scriptstyle\circ$}}}{\mathrel{\raisebox{0.43057pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\circ$}}}{\mathrel{\raisebox{0.43057pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\circ$}}}\rho_{\gamma^{1}}^{\mathtt{r}\mathtt{x}}(A)=\omega_{0}\mathchoice{\mathrel{\raisebox{0.86108pt}{$\scriptstyle\circ$}}}{\mathrel{\raisebox{0.86108pt}{$\scriptstyle\circ$}}}{\mathrel{\raisebox{0.43057pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\circ$}}}{\mathrel{\raisebox{0.43057pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\circ$}}}\rho_{\gamma^{2}}^{\mathtt{r}\mathtt{x}}(A)
for
A\in\mathcal{A}_{\rm loc}
.
The general statements follows from continuity and the fact that
\mathcal{A}_{\rm loc}
is dense in
\mathcal{A}
.
∎

Let
\pi^{ck}_{f}
be a GNS representation of the state
\omega_{f}^{ck}
. The following shows that
\pi_{f}^{ck}
satisfies the superselection criterion with respect to
\pi_{0}
.

Theorem 3.7
.

Let
\Lambda
be a cone. The following hold:

1.

\pi^{ck}_{f}|_{\mathcal{A}(\Lambda^{c})}\simeq\pi_{0}|_{\mathcal{A}(\Lambda^{c})}
for any
c
and
k
.

2.

\pi^{ck}_{f}\simeq\pi_{f^{\prime}}^{ck}
for
c
-colored
f\neq f^{\prime}
.

3.

\pi_{f}^{ck}\simeq\pi^{c^{\prime}k^{\prime}}_{f^{\prime}}
if and only if
ck=c^{\prime}k^{\prime}
.

Proof.

1. Let
\gamma
be a string in
\Lambda
starting from
f
.
Then by localization,
\pi_{0}\mathchoice{\mathrel{\raisebox{0.86108pt}{$\scriptstyle\circ$}}}{\mathrel{\raisebox{0.86108pt}{$\scriptstyle\circ$}}}{\mathrel{\raisebox{0.43057pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\circ$}}}{\mathrel{\raisebox{0.43057pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\circ$}}}\rho_{\gamma}^{ck}(A)=\pi_{0}(A)
for all
A\in\mathcal{A}(\Lambda^{c})
.
Note that
(\pi_{0}\mathchoice{\mathrel{\raisebox{0.86108pt}{$\scriptstyle\circ$}}}{\mathrel{\raisebox{0.86108pt}{$\scriptstyle\circ$}}}{\mathrel{\raisebox{0.43057pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\circ$}}}{\mathrel{\raisebox{0.43057pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\circ$}}}\rho_{\gamma}^{ck},\mathcal{H},\Omega)
is also a GNS representation for
\omega_{f}^{ck}
; hence the assertion follows by the uniqueness of GNS representation.

2. Let
\gamma
be a half-infinite string of type
c
in
\Lambda
, with starting face
f
.
Choose a string
\gamma^{\prime}
of the same type in
\Lambda
that connects
f
and
f^{\prime}
.
Then
\gamma\gamma^{\prime}
is a half-infinite string of type
c
in
\Lambda
starting at

.
It follows that
\pi_{f^{\prime}}^{ck}
(resp.
\pi_{f}^{ck}
) is the GNS representation of the state
\omega_{f^{\prime}}^{ck}=\omega_{0}\mathchoice{\mathrel{\raisebox{0.86108pt}{$\scriptstyle\circ$}}}{\mathrel{\raisebox{0.86108pt}{$\scriptstyle\circ$}}}{\mathrel{\raisebox{0.43057pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\circ$}}}{\mathrel{\raisebox{0.43057pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\circ$}}}\rho_{\gamma\gamma^{\prime}}^{ck}=\omega_{0}(\operatorname{Ad}(S_{\gamma^{\prime}}^{ck})\mathchoice{\mathrel{\raisebox{0.86108pt}{$\scriptstyle\circ$}}}{\mathrel{\raisebox{0.86108pt}{$\scriptstyle\circ$}}}{\mathrel{\raisebox{0.43057pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\circ$}}}{\mathrel{\raisebox{0.43057pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\circ$}}}\rho_{\gamma}^{ck})
(resp.
\omega_{f}^{ck}=\omega_{0}\mathchoice{\mathrel{\raisebox{0.86108pt}{$\scriptstyle\circ$}}}{\mathrel{\raisebox{0.86108pt}{$\scriptstyle\circ$}}}{\mathrel{\raisebox{0.43057pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\circ$}}}{\mathrel{\raisebox{0.43057pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\circ$}}}\rho_{\gamma}^{ck}
).
The homomorphism
\operatorname{Ad}(S_{\gamma^{\prime}}^{ck}):\mathcal{A}\to\mathcal{A}

A\mapsto(S_{\gamma^{\prime}}^{ck})^{\dagger}AS_{\gamma^{\prime}}^{ck}
is an inner automorphism. Hence their corresponding GNS representations are equivalent, which proves the assertion.
This shows that
\pi_{f}^{ck}
is independent of the starting face
f
.

3. Suppose that
ck\neq c^{\prime}k^{\prime}
.
Because
\omega_{0}
is a pure state, its GNS representation
\pi_{0}
is irreducible; hence so is the GNS representation
\pi_{f}^{ck}
of
\omega_{f}^{ck}
, being obtained by composing
\omega_{0}
with an automorphism of the quasi-local algebra
\mathcal{A}
.
The same argument applies to
\omega_{f^{\prime}}^{c^{\prime}k^{\prime}}
.
We will show that the pure states
\omega_{f}^{ck}
and
\omega_{f^{\prime}}^{c^{\prime}k^{\prime}}
violate the criterion in Proposition
2.3
; hence their corresponding GNS representations are not equivalent.
Choose
\varepsilon_{0}=1
.
Let
S
be any finite subset of
V(\Sigma)
; without lose of generality, we may suppose that
S
contains the starting face of the half-infinite string that defines the automorphism
\rho_{\gamma}^{ck}
.
By the second statement of the theorem, we may suppose that
f^{\prime}
is not contained in
S
.
Let
\tilde{\gamma}
be a closed and non-self-intersecting string of type not equal to
c
, such that

is contained in the region bounded by
\tilde{\gamma}
, and
f^{\prime}
is not in this region (otherwise we can move

farther inside
\Lambda
).
Then for

or

\omega_{f^{\prime}}^{c^{\prime}k^{\prime}}(S_{\tilde{\gamma}}^{c^{\prime\prime}k^{\prime\prime}})=\lim_{n}\omega_{0}(S_{\gamma^{\prime}_{n}}^{c^{\prime}k^{\prime}}S_{\tilde{\gamma}}^{c^{\prime\prime}k^{\prime\prime}}S_{\gamma^{\prime}_{n}}^{c^{\prime}k^{\prime}})=\lim_{n}\omega_{0}(S_{\tilde{\gamma}}^{c^{\prime\prime}k^{\prime\prime}}S_{\gamma^{\prime}_{n}}^{c^{\prime}k^{\prime}}S_{\gamma^{\prime}_{n}}^{c^{\prime}k^{\prime}})=\omega_{0}(S_{\tilde{\gamma}}^{c^{\prime\prime}k^{\prime\prime}})=1

3.4
Fusion rules

In subsequent sections, we will show that the topological excitations are characterized by the category of superselection sectors whose morphisms are intertwiners.
By definition, an intertwiner from
(\pi_{1},\mathcal{H}_{1})
to
(\pi_{2},\mathcal{H}_{2})
is a operator
U
such that
U\pi_{1}(A)=\pi_{2}(A)U
for all
A\in\mathcal{A}
.
If
U
is unitary, it is also called a charge transportor.
Note that the quasi-local algebra
\mathcal{A}
is simple (cf.
[
40
]
); hence
\mathcal{A}
can be identified with its image
\pi_{0}(\mathcal{A})
in
\mathbf{B}(\mathcal{H})
, which facilitates the discussion.
Because of this reason, the superselection sector
\pi_{0}\mathchoice{\mathrel{\raisebox{0.86108pt}{$\scriptstyle\circ$}}}{\mathrel{\raisebox{0.86108pt}{$\scriptstyle\circ$}}}{\mathrel{\raisebox{0.43057pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\circ$}}}{\mathrel{\raisebox{0.43057pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\circ$}}}\rho_{\gamma}
corresponding to half-infinite strings
\gamma
discussed in the previous section are momentarily identified with endomorphisms of
\mathcal{A}
.
Therefore, it is equivalent to studying the intertwiners between endomorphisms of
\mathcal{A}
.

Proposition 3.11

(
[
37
, Lem. 4.1, Thm. 4.1]
)
.

Let
\gamma
and
\gamma^{\prime}
be two half-infinite strings of type
c
in a cone
\Lambda
, starting at the faces
f
and
f^{\prime}
respectively. Then there is a unitary intertwiner
U
from
\rho_{\gamma}^{ck}
to
\rho_{\gamma^{\prime}}^{ck}
such that
U\Omega=S_{\tilde{\gamma}}^{ck}\Omega
, where
\tilde{\gamma}
is any finite string of type
c
from
f
to
f^{\prime}
.
Moreover,
U
can be taken as the limit
U=\operatorname{\textit{w}\text{-}lim}_{n}U_{n}
under the weak operator topology, where
U_{n}=S_{\gamma_{n}}^{ck}S^{ck}_{\tilde{\gamma}_{n}}S^{ck}_{\gamma^{\prime}_{n}}
with
\tilde{\gamma}_{n}
is any string connecting the terminating faces of
\gamma_{n}
and
\gamma^{\prime}_{n}
and satisfying
\operatorname{dist}(f,\tilde{\gamma}_{n})
and
\operatorname{dist}(f^{\prime},\tilde{\gamma}_{n})\to\infty
as
n\to\infty
.
Therefore, every intertwiner
V
from
\rho_{\gamma}^{ck}
to
\rho_{\gamma^{\prime}}^{ck}
belongs to
\mathcal{A}(\Lambda)^{\prime\prime}
.

Proof.

The argument mimics the one in
[
37
, Lem. 4.1]
. We repeat it for clarity.
First of all, the existence of
U
is guaranteed by the second statement of Theorem
3.7
, and the uniqueness comes from Schur’s Lemma.

Let us show the second assertion. For simplicity, we omit the superscript
ck
. We may suppose that
\gamma
and
\gamma^{\prime}
have the same starting face; if not, replace
\gamma^{\prime}
with the string
\gamma^{\prime}\tilde{\gamma}
. Then the string
\gamma_{n}\tilde{\gamma}_{n}\gamma^{\prime}_{n}
is a closed string. The corresponding string operator is denoted as
U_{n}:=S_{\gamma_{n}}S_{\tilde{\gamma}_{n}}S_{\gamma^{\prime}_{n}}
, which is a product of
K_{f}
and
J_{f}
, and therefore stabilizers the cyclic vector
\Omega
, i.e.,
U_{n}\Omega=\Omega
.
We want to show that
\langle x,U_{n}y\rangle\to\langle x,Uy\rangle
as
n\to\infty
for all
x,y\in\mathcal{H}
.
But
\rho_{\gamma}(\mathcal{A}_{\rm loc})\Omega
is dense in
\mathcal{H}
, it suffices to consider
x=\rho_{\gamma}(A)\Omega
and
y=\rho_{\gamma}(B)\Omega
with
A,B\in\mathcal{A}_{\rm loc}
.
Choose
N\geq 1
such that for all
n\geq N
, the support of
B
does not intersect with
\tilde{\gamma}_{n}
,
\gamma_{n}\setminus\gamma_{N}
and
\gamma^{\prime}_{n}\setminus\gamma^{\prime}_{N}
.
Then by locality, one has

\displaystyle U_{n}\rho_{\gamma}(B)

\displaystyle=S_{\gamma_{N}}S_{\gamma_{n}\setminus\gamma_{N}}S_{\tilde{\gamma}_{n}}S_{\gamma^{\prime}_{N}}S_{\gamma^{\prime}_{n}\setminus\gamma^{\prime}_{N}}S_{\gamma_{N}}BS_{\gamma_{N}}

\displaystyle=S_{\gamma_{N}}S_{\gamma_{N}}S_{\gamma^{\prime}_{N}}BS_{\gamma^{\prime}_{N}}S_{\gamma^{\prime}_{N}}S_{\gamma_{n}\setminus\gamma_{N}}S_{\tilde{\gamma}_{n}}S_{\gamma^{\prime}_{n}\setminus\gamma^{\prime}_{N}}S_{\gamma_{N}}

\displaystyle=S_{\gamma_{N}^{\prime}}BS_{\gamma_{N}^{\prime}}S_{\gamma_{n}}S_{\tilde{\gamma}_{n}}S_{\gamma_{n}^{\prime}}=\rho_{\gamma^{\prime}}(B)U_{n}.

Therefore, we have

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\langle\rho_{\gamma}(A)\Omega,U_{n}\rho_{\gamma}(B)\Omega\rangle

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\to\infty}\langle\rho_{\gamma}(A)\Omega,\rho_{\gamma^{\prime}}(B)U_{n}\Omega\rangle=\langle\rho_{\gamma}(A)\Omega,\rho_{\gamma^{\prime}}(B)\Omega\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\rho_{\gamma}(A)\Omega,\rho_{\gamma^{\prime}}(B)U\Omega\rangle=\langle\rho_{\gamma}(A)\Omega,U\rho_{\gamma}(B)\Omega\rangle,

as desired.

Finally, the second assertion implies that
U\in\mathcal{A}(\Lambda)^{\prime\prime}
. As, by Schur’s Lemma,
V
is a scalar multiple of
U
, the last statement follows.
∎

Now we are ready to define the tensor product of localized endomorphisms.
Let
\rho
and
\rho^{\prime}
be two endomorphisms of
\mathcal{A}
, localized in cones
\Lambda
and
\Lambda^{\prime}
respectively.
Define the tensor product
\rho\otimes\rho^{\prime}:\mathcal{A}\to\mathcal{A}
by

(\rho\otimes\rho^{\prime})(A)=\rho(\rho^{\prime}(A)),\quad\text{for}~A\in\mathcal{A}.

Note that
\rho\otimes\rho^{\prime}
is localized in a bigger cone
\tilde{\Lambda}
that contains both
\Lambda
and
\Lambda^{\prime}
.
This defines a tensor functor on the level of objects.
To define the tensor product on the level of morphisms, however, one needs to be careful, since the intertwiners are no longer in the quasi-algebra
\mathcal{A}
anymore; instead, it is in
\mathcal{A}(\tilde{\Lambda})^{\prime\prime}
by Proposition
3.11
. (Note that we have identified
\mathcal{A}
with
\pi_{0}(\mathcal{A})
.)
Usually, the construction can be remedied by the Haag duality, which states that for any cone
\Lambda
there holds

\pi_{0}(\mathcal{A}(\Lambda))^{\prime\prime}=\pi_{0}(\mathcal{A}(\Lambda^{c}))^{\prime}.

It is established that Haag duality holds for quantum double models of Abelian groups
[
39
,
15
]
.
Recently, Ogata, Pérez-García, and Ruiz-de-Alarcón
[
41
]
claimed that two-dimensional tensor network states based on biconnected
C^{*}
-weak Hopf algebras satisfy Haag duality, which includes as particular cases Kitaev quantum double and Levin-Wen string-net models.
See Section
4
for a proof for the color code model.
Nevertheless, it is still possible to proceed without Haag duality, following the approach for the toric code
[
37
]
.

Fix a cone
\Lambda_{0}
. Consider an auxiliary algebra

\mathcal{A}^{au}=\overline{\bigcup_{x\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}}\pi_{0}(\mathcal{A}(\Lambda_{0}+x))^{\prime\prime}}^{\|\cdot\|},

where the union is taken with respect to the partial order “
x\leq y\Leftrightarrow(\Lambda+x)\subset(\Lambda+y)
”, and the overline means the norm completion.
Clearly,
\mathcal{A}\subset\mathcal{A}^{au}
. The following is crucial for defining the tensor product of intertwiners.

Proposition 3.12
.

Let
\pi:\mathcal{A}\to\mathbf{B}(\mathcal{H})
be a superselection sector of
\pi_{0}
. Then
\pi
extends uniquely to
\mathcal{A}^{au}
. Moreover, if
\pi
corresponds to some half-infinite string, the extension
\bar{\pi}
satisfies
\bar{\pi}(\mathcal{A}^{au})\subset\mathcal{A}^{au}
.

Proof.

Consider
\pi_{0}(\mathcal{A}(\Lambda_{0}+x))^{\prime\prime}
. Pick a cone
\Lambda
such that
\Lambda\cap(\Lambda_{0}+x)=\emptyset
. As
\pi
is a superselection sector, there exists a unitary
U\in\mathbf{B}(\mathcal{H})
such that
\pi(A)=U^{\dagger}\pi_{0}(A)U
for any
A\in\mathcal{A}(\Lambda^{c})
. In particular, this holds for all
A\in\mathcal{A}(\Lambda_{0}+x)
. Since multiplication is continuous with respect to the weak operator topology, there is a unique extension
\bar{\pi}
of
\pi
to
\mathcal{A}(\Lambda_{0}+x)^{\prime\prime}
given by
\bar{\pi}(B)=U^{\dagger}\pi_{0}(B)U
for any
B\in\pi_{0}(\mathcal{A}(\Lambda^{c}))^{\prime\prime}
, which is also weakly continuous. Thus the unique extension is established. Now suppose that
\pi=\pi_{0}\mathchoice{\mathrel{\raisebox{0.86108pt}{$\scriptstyle\circ$}}}{\mathrel{\raisebox{0.86108pt}{$\scriptstyle\circ$}}}{\mathrel{\raisebox{0.43057pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\circ$}}}{\mathrel{\raisebox{0.43057pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\circ$}}}\rho_{\gamma}
corresponding to some half-infinite string. Note that
\rho_{\gamma}(\mathcal{A}(\Lambda))\subset\mathcal{A}(\Lambda)
for every cone contains
\gamma
(hence for all cones since
\pi
is transportable). For every
A\in\pi_{0}(\mathcal{A}(\Lambda_{0}+x))^{\prime\prime}
, choose a sequence
A_{n}\in\mathcal{A}(\Lambda_{0}+x)
with
w\text{-}\lim_{n}\pi_{0}(A_{n})=A
; then by weak continuity,

\bar{\pi}(A)=\underset{n\to\infty}{w\mathrm{\text{-}lim}}\,\bar{\pi}(\pi_{0}(A_{n}))=\underset{n\to\infty}{w\mathrm{\text{-}lim}}\,\pi_{0}(\rho_{\gamma}(A_{n}))\in\pi_{0}(\mathcal{A}(\Lambda_{0}+x))^{\prime\prime}.

Thus
\bar{\pi}(\pi_{0}(\mathcal{A}(\Lambda_{0}+x))^{\prime\prime})\subset\pi_{0}(\mathcal{A}(\Lambda_{0}+x))^{\prime\prime}
, and the proof is finished.
∎

Now it is ready to define the tensor product of intertwiners.

\rho_{i},\rho^{\prime}_{i}
(

S^{\prime}

\rho_{2}
, and
\rho^{\prime}_{1}

It has been shown that

Let
\bar{\rho}_{i}
be the unique extension of
\rho_{i}
(

) on
\mathcal{A}^{au}
obtained in the proposition above.
By continuity,
S
is also an intertwiner from
\bar{\rho}_{1}
to
\bar{\rho}_{2}

For technical reasons, we assume that
\Lambda_{0}
is oriented upwards and contains the origin

V(\Sigma)

\Lambda_{0}

\Lambda_{0}=u+\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}~|~x\cdot a>\|x\|\cos\theta\}

associated with

\mathcal{A}
.
In this part, we identify
\mathcal{A}
with its image
\pi_{0}(\mathcal{A})

\mathcal{A}^{au}

4
Haag duality

Haag duality is a fundamental structural property in algebraic quantum field theory, asserting that the commutant of the algebra of observables localized in a given spacetime region coincides precisely with the algebra associated with its causal complement. This property has been established for the ground states of a variety of topological quantum lattice models. In this section, we establish Haag duality for the ground state of the quantum color code model.

4.1
Cone algebras as infinite factors

As usual, let
\omega_{0}
be the ground state, and
(\pi_{0},\Omega,\mathcal{H})
be its GNS representation. Following
[
37
]
, we show that the von Neumann algebra
\mathcal{R}_{\Lambda}:=\pi_{0}(\mathcal{A}(\Lambda))^{\prime\prime}
is an infinite factor for any cone
\Lambda
in this section.

Recall that for two von Neumann subalgebras
R_{1}
and
R_{2}
of
\mathbf{B}(\mathcal{H})
,
R_{1}\vee R_{2}
is the smallest von Neumann subalgebra of
\mathbf{B}(\mathcal{H})
that contains both
R_{1}
and
R_{2}
.
The following is analogous to
[
37
, Lem. 5.1]
.

Lemma 4.1
.

For any cone
\Lambda
, we have that
\mathcal{R}_{\Lambda}\vee\mathcal{R}_{\Lambda^{c}}=\mathbf{B}(\mathcal{H})
. Moreover,
\mathcal{R}_{\Lambda}
is a factor.

Proof.

It follows from the purity of
\omega_{0}
that
\pi_{0}(\mathcal{A})^{\prime}=\mathbb{C}\mathds{1}
, which implies

\mathbf{B}(\mathcal{H})=\pi_{0}(\mathcal{A})^{\prime\prime}=(\pi_{0}(\mathcal{A}(\Lambda))\cup\pi_{0}(\mathcal{A}(\Lambda^{c})))^{\prime\prime}=\pi_{0}(\mathcal{A}(\Lambda))^{\prime\prime}\vee\pi_{0}(\mathcal{A}(\Lambda^{c}))^{\prime\prime},

as claimed.
To show
\mathcal{R}_{\Lambda}
is a factor, consider its center
\mathcal{Z}(\mathcal{R}_{\Lambda})=\mathcal{R}_{\Lambda}\cap\mathcal{R}_{\Lambda}^{\prime}
.
It follows from the fact
\mathcal{R}_{\Lambda^{c}}\subset\mathcal{R}_{\Lambda}^{\prime}
that

\mathcal{Z}(\mathcal{R}_{\Lambda})^{\prime}=(\mathcal{R}_{\Lambda}\cap\mathcal{R}_{\Lambda}^{\prime})^{\prime}=\mathcal{R}_{\Lambda}\vee\mathcal{R}_{\Lambda}^{\prime}\supset\mathcal{R}_{\Lambda}\vee\mathcal{R}_{\Lambda^{c}}=\mathbf{B}(\mathcal{H}),

which shows
\mathcal{Z}(\mathcal{R}_{\Lambda})=\mathbb{C}\mathds{1}
. Thus
\mathcal{R}_{\Lambda}
is a factor by definition.
∎

Lemma 4.2
.

The state
\omega_{0}
is not tracial.

Proof.

Let
\gamma_{0}
be a closed
\mathtt{r}
-colored string. Choose a
\mathtt{g}
-colored face
f_{0}
inside the region bounded by
\gamma_{0}
, and a
\mathtt{g}
-colored face
f_{1}
outside this region. Let
\gamma_{1}
and
\gamma_{2}
be two
\mathtt{g}
-colored strings satisfying the following conditions: (1)
\partial_{0}\gamma_{1}=\partial_{1}\gamma_{2}=f_{0}
, and
\partial_{1}\gamma_{1}=\partial_{0}\gamma_{2}=f_{1}
; (2) each
\gamma_{i}
intersects

at exactly one vertex. Then
\gamma_{1}\gamma_{2}
is a
\mathtt{g}
-colored closed string. Therefore, the string operator
S_{\gamma_{1}}^{\mathtt{gz}}S_{\gamma_{2}}^{\mathtt{gz}}=S_{\gamma_{2}}^{\mathtt{gz}}S_{\gamma_{1}}^{\mathtt{gz}}
is a product of

On the other hand, if

were tracial, one obtains

which is impossible. Thus the assertion follows.
∎

Proposition 4.3

For any cone
\Lambda

Proof.

This follows the proof of
[

[
30

Suppose that

Then the tracial state

4.2
Proof of Haag duality for color code model

In the context of topological quantum lattice models, Haag duality asserts a maximal complementarity between the observable algebras of a region and its causal (spacelike) complement. More precisely, for a cone region
\Lambda
on the infinite lattice, Haag duality requires that the von Neumann algebra generated by local observables in
\Lambda
be exactly the commutant of the algebra generated by observables in the complement
\Lambda^{c}
within the ground state representation:

\pi_{0}(\mathcal{A}(\Lambda))^{\prime\prime}=\pi_{0}(\mathcal{A}(\Lambda^{c}))^{\prime}.

From a physical perspective, Haag duality encodes the idea that all degrees of freedom in a region are fully correlated with those in its complement, with no hidden independent parts, which is a reflection of the entanglement structure and the absence of local excitations in a pure topological phase.

In the program of reconstructing anyon statistics via localized endomorphisms (the DHR approach), Haag duality plays a pivotal role. It ensures that the intertwiners between superselection sectors localized in disjoint regions are nontrivial and allows for a clean definition of the tensor product of sectors and their braiding. For the color code, establishing Haag duality is essential to rigorously justify the categorical constructions in sections
3.4
and
3.5
, namely the definition of the tensor product of intertwiners and the subsequent analysis of braiding. While it is known from recent general results
[
41
]
that Haag duality holds for two-dimensional tensor network states based on biconnected
C^{*}
-weak Hopf algebras—encompassing Kitaev quantum double and Levin-Wen string-net models—a direct proof tailored to the color code provides explicit insight into its stabilizer and string-operator structure.

In what follows, we adapt the strategy developed for the toric code
[
39
]
to the color code, leveraging its stabilizer formalism and string-operator structure. Subsequently, we fix a cone
\Lambda
.
The inclusion
\pi_{0}(\mathcal{A}(\Lambda))^{\prime\prime}\subseteq\pi_{0}(\mathcal{A}(\Lambda^{c}))^{\prime}
follows directly from locality.
It suffices to establish the reverse inclusion.
As before, we denote
\mathcal{R}_{\Lambda}:=\pi_{0}(\mathcal{A}(\Lambda))^{\prime\prime}
and
\mathcal{R}_{\Lambda^{c}}:=\pi_{0}(\mathcal{A}(\Lambda^{c}))^{\prime\prime}
.
With these notations, the above inclusion reads
\mathcal{R}_{\Lambda}\subseteq\mathcal{R}_{\Lambda^{c}}^{\prime}
. Consider the set of finite string operators localized inside
\Lambda
:

\mathcal{S}_{\Lambda}=\{S_{\gamma}^{ck}~|~\gamma~\text{is~a~finite~string~of~type}~ck~\text{contained~in}~\Lambda\}\subset\mathcal{R}_{\Lambda}.

Define
\mathcal{S}_{\Lambda^{c}}
in a similar way.
Recall that
(\pi_{0},\mathcal{H},\Omega)
is a GNS triple of the ground state
\omega_{0}
.
Note that we also consider a single vertex as a string, whose string operators are given by Pauli matrices
\sigma_{v}^{\mathtt{x}},\sigma_{v}^{\mathtt{y}},\sigma_{v}^{\mathtt{z}}
.
These operators together with identity matrix
\mathds{1}
(considered as a string operator associated to a null string) generate the local algebra
\mathcal{A}(\{v\})
.
Hence, by considering longer strings, one sees that
\mathcal{S}_{\Lambda}
and
\mathcal{S}_{\Lambda^{c}}
generate
\mathcal{A}_{\rm loc}
.
As
\mathcal{A}_{\rm loc}
is dense in
\mathcal{A}
, and
\Omega
is a cyclic, it follows that the vector space

\mathcal{H}^{\prime}=\operatorname{span}\{S_{1}\cdots S_{n}S_{1}^{\prime}\cdots S_{m}^{\prime}\Omega~|~S_{1},\cdots,S_{n}\in\mathcal{S}_{\Lambda},S_{1}^{\prime},\cdots,S_{m}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}_{\Lambda^{c}}\}

is dense in
\mathcal{H}
(
[
39
, Lem. 3.3]
).
Consider the vector space

\mathcal{H}_{\Lambda}=\overline{\operatorname{span}\{S_{1}\cdots S_{n}\Omega~|~S_{1},\cdots,S_{n}\in\mathcal{S}_{\Lambda}\}}\subset\mathcal{H},

where overline means taking closure inside
\mathcal{H}
.
Let
P_{\Lambda}
be the projection onto
\mathcal{H}_{\Lambda}
. Thus
P_{\Lambda}\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{H}_{\Lambda}
.
It is immediate that
\mathcal{H}_{\Lambda}
is an invariant subspace of
\pi_{0}(\mathcal{A}(\Lambda))
by how it is defined.
In summary, we have the following.

Lemma 4.5
.

The vector space
\mathcal{H}^{\prime}
is dense in
\mathcal{H}
.
The subspace
\mathcal{H}_{\Lambda}
is invariant for
\pi_{0}(\mathcal{A}(\Lambda))
, i.e.,
\pi_{0}(\mathcal{A}(\Lambda))\mathcal{H}_{\Lambda}\subseteq\mathcal{H}_{\Lambda}
.

Lemma 4.6
.

Operators in
\mathcal{R}_{\Lambda^{c}}^{\prime}
are uniquely determined by their restrictions on
\mathcal{H}_{\Lambda}
. That is, for operators
A,B\in\mathcal{R}_{\Lambda^{c}}^{\prime}
,
A|_{\mathcal{H}_{\Lambda}}=B|_{\mathcal{H}_{\Lambda}}
implies
A=B
.

Proof.

The argument is similar to
[
39
, Lem. 3.5]
.
It suffices to show their actions on elements of the form
S^{\prime}S\Omega
are the same, where
S
(resp.
S^{\prime}
) is a product of string operators in
\mathcal{S}_{\Lambda}
(resp.
\mathcal{S}_{\Lambda^{c}}
), since the space
\mathcal{H}^{\prime}
spanned by these elements is dense in
\mathcal{H}
.
As
S^{\prime}\in\mathcal{R}_{\Lambda^{c}}
,
A
and
B
commute with
S^{\prime}
. Therefore,
AS^{\prime}S\Omega=S^{\prime}AS\Omega=S^{\prime}BS\Omega=BS^{\prime}S\Omega
.
∎

Remark 4.1
.

The same proof also shows that operators in
\mathcal{R}_{\Lambda}
are uniquely determined by their restrictions on
\mathcal{H}_{\Lambda}
.

Since Haag duality is supposed to be true,
\mathcal{R}_{\Lambda^{c}}^{\prime}
must share the same defining property as
\mathcal{R}_{\Lambda}
. In particular,
\mathcal{R}_{\Lambda^{c}}^{\prime}
is expected to preserve the subspace
\mathcal{H}_{\Lambda}
.
For a cone
\Lambda
, we say that a face
f
is a boundary face if it contains vertices both in
\Lambda
and in
\Lambda^{c}
.
Hence,
K_{f}
or
J_{f}\in\mathcal{A}(\Lambda)
means that all vertices of
f
lie in
\Lambda
.

Lemma 4.7
.

The subspace
\mathcal{H}_{\Lambda}
is invariant under the action of
\mathcal{R}_{\Lambda^{c}}^{\prime}
.

Proof.

The argument follows that of
[
39
, Lem. 3.6]
with appropriate modification.
Let
A\in\mathcal{R}_{\Lambda^{c}}^{\prime}
. We want to show
A\eta\in\mathcal{H}_{\Lambda}
for
\eta\in\mathcal{H}_{\Lambda}
.
It is enough to show this holds for
\eta=S\Omega
, where
S
is a product of finite string operators in
\mathcal{S}_{\Lambda}
.
For this purpose, we will show that
\langle\xi,A\eta\rangle=0
for any
\xi\in\mathcal{H}_{\Lambda}^{\perp}
.
By density, it is sufficient to take
\xi=\tilde{S}S^{\prime}\Omega
with
\tilde{S}
being a product of finite string operators in
\mathcal{S}_{\Lambda}
, and
S^{\prime}
a product of finite string operators
S_{1}^{\prime},\cdots,S_{m}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}_{\Lambda^{c}}
.
Note that each stabilizer operator
K_{f}
or
J_{f}
must commute or anti-commute with
S^{\prime}
.

First, if there were a face
f
inside
\Lambda^{c}
such that
K_{f}
or
J_{f}
anti-commutes with
S^{\prime}
, then (taking
K_{f}
as an example) one has

\displaystyle\langle\xi,A\eta\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\tilde{S}S^{\prime}\Omega,ASK_{f}\Omega\rangle=\langle\tilde{S}S^{\prime}\Omega,K_{f}AS\Omega\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle K_{f}\tilde{S}S^{\prime}\Omega,AS\Omega\rangle=-\langle\tilde{S}S^{\prime}K_{f}\Omega,AS\Omega\rangle=-\langle\xi,A\eta\rangle,

implying
\langle\xi,A\eta\rangle=0
. Here the second equality is from
\mathcal{S}_{\Lambda}\subset\mathcal{R}_{\Lambda}\subset\mathcal{R}_{\Lambda^{c}}^{\prime}
.
Second, suppose that no such stabilizer operator anti-commutes with
S^{\prime}
.
Let
\rho_{i}^{\prime}
be the string defining
S_{i}^{\prime}
, which is colored by
c_{i}
.
We may assume that the starting faces
\partial_{0}\rho_{i}^{\prime}
are pairwise distinct for different
i
, and likewise for the terminating faces
\partial_{1}\rho_{i}^{\prime}
.
Since each
S_{i}^{\prime}
commutates with any stabilizer operator in
\Lambda^{c}
, the ending faces
\partial_{0}\rho_{i}^{\prime}
and
\partial_{1}\rho_{i}^{\prime}
cannot be inside
\Lambda^{c}
; they must be on the boundary.
In this case, there is a finite
c_{i}
-colored string
\rho_{i}
inside
\Lambda
whose starting and terminating faces are
\partial_{1}\rho_{i}^{\prime}
and
\partial_{0}\rho_{i}^{\prime}
respectively, and it follows that
\rho_{i}\rho_{i}^{\prime}
is a closed string.
For each
i
, let
S_{i}
be the string operator on
\rho_{i}
defined by the same Pauli matrix as
S_{i}^{\prime}
.
Then,
S_{i}S_{i}^{\prime}\Omega=\Omega
.
This shows that
\xi=\tilde{S}S_{1}^{\prime}\cdots S_{m}^{\prime}\Omega=\pm\tilde{S}S_{1}\cdots S_{m}\Omega\in\mathcal{H}_{\Lambda}
.
This establishes the statement.
∎

Corollary 4.8
.

P_{\Lambda}\in\mathcal{R}_{\Lambda}^{\prime}
, and
P_{\Lambda}\in\mathcal{R}_{\Lambda^{c}}
.

Proof.

Let
A\in\mathcal{R}_{\Lambda^{c}}^{\prime}
, and let
\eta\in\mathcal{H}
be arbitrary.
Then
P_{\Lambda}\eta\in\mathcal{H}_{\Lambda}
. Thus
AP_{\Lambda}\eta\in\mathcal{H}_{\Lambda}
by Lemma
4.7
. Thus
P_{\Lambda}AP_{\Lambda}\eta=AP_{\Lambda}\eta
, which implies
P_{\Lambda}AP_{\Lambda}=AP_{\Lambda}
since
\eta
is arbitrary. Since
A^{\dagger}
is also in
\mathcal{R}_{\Lambda^{c}}^{\prime}
,
P_{\Lambda}A^{\dagger}P_{\Lambda}=A^{\dagger}P_{\Lambda}
. Taking
\dagger
shows
AP_{\Lambda}=P_{\Lambda}AP_{\Lambda}=(P_{\Lambda}A^{\dagger}P_{\Lambda})^{\dagger}=(A^{\dagger}P_{\Lambda})^{\dagger}=P_{\Lambda}A
. Hence,
P_{\Lambda}\in(\mathcal{R}_{\Lambda^{c}}^{\prime})^{\prime}=\mathcal{R}_{\Lambda^{c}}\subseteq\mathcal{R}_{\Lambda}^{\prime}
.
∎

Consider the
*
-algebras

\mathcal{A}_{\Lambda}:=\mathcal{R}_{\Lambda}P_{\Lambda}\subset\mathbf{B}(\mathcal{H}_{\Lambda}),\quad\mathcal{B}_{\Lambda}=P_{\Lambda}\mathcal{R}_{\Lambda^{c}}P_{\Lambda}\subset\mathbf{B}(\mathcal{H}_{\Lambda}).

By a standard result of operator theory, both
\mathcal{A}_{\Lambda}
and
\mathcal{B}_{\Lambda}
are von Neumann algebras.
Let
\mathcal{A}_{\Lambda,sa}
(resp.
\mathcal{B}_{\Lambda,sa}
) be the real vector space formed by self-adjoint elements of
\mathcal{A}_{\Lambda}
(resp.
\mathcal{B}_{\Lambda}
).
The following is an analogy to
[
39
, Lem. 3.8]
and plays an essential role in establishing the Haag duality.

Lemma 4.9
.

The space
\mathcal{A}_{\Lambda,sa}\Omega+i\mathcal{B}_{\Lambda,sa}\Omega
is dense in
\mathcal{H}_{\Lambda}
.

Proof.

The proof is adapted from that of
[
39
, Lem. 3.8]
and is included here for completeness.
Since both
\mathcal{A}_{\Lambda,sa}
and
\mathcal{B}_{\Lambda,sa}
are real vector spaces, it is sufficient to show that for any
S=S_{1}\cdots S_{n}
with each
S_{i}\in\mathcal{S}_{\Lambda}
, we have
S\Omega
and
iS\Omega
belong to
\mathcal{A}_{\Lambda,sa}\Omega+i\mathcal{B}_{\Lambda,sa}\Omega
.
As each
S_{i}
is self-adjoint, and two
S_{i}
and
S_{j}
either commute or anti-commutate,
S
satisfies either
S^{\dagger}=S
or
S^{\dagger}=-S
.
If
S=S^{\dagger}
, then
S\in\mathcal{A}_{\Lambda,\text{sa}}
and hence
S\Omega\in\mathcal{A}_{\Lambda,\text{sa}}\Omega
; it remains to show that
iS\Omega\in\mathcal{A}_{\Lambda,\text{sa}}\Omega+i\mathcal{B}_{\Lambda,\text{sa}}\Omega
.
If
S^{\dagger}=-S
, then
iS
is self-adjoint, so
iS\in\mathcal{A}_{\Lambda,\text{sa}}
and
iS\Omega\in\mathcal{A}_{\Lambda,\text{sa}}\Omega
; it remains to show that
S\Omega\in\mathcal{A}_{\Lambda,\text{sa}}\Omega+i\mathcal{B}_{\Lambda,\text{sa}}\Omega
.

Note that each stabilizer operator is either commuting or anti-commuting with
S
.
First, suppose that there exists one stabilizer operator in
\mathcal{R}_{\Lambda}
, say
K_{f}
, anti-commutes with
S
.
If
S^{\dagger}=S
, on the one hand, we have shown that
S\Omega\in\mathcal{A}_{\Lambda,sa}\Omega
; on the other hand,
iK_{f}S
is self-adjoint, hence
iS\Omega=iSK_{f}\Omega=-(iK_{f}S)\Omega\in\mathcal{A}_{\Lambda,sa}\Omega
.
Similarly, if
S^{\dagger}=-S
, then
iS\Omega\in\mathcal{A}_{\Lambda,sa}\Omega
by the above argument, and since
K_{f}S
is self-adjoint,
S\Omega=SK_{f}\Omega=-(K_{f}S)\Omega\in\mathcal{A}_{\Lambda,sa}\Omega
.

Second, suppose that all stabilizer operators in
\mathcal{R}_{\Lambda}
commute with
S
.
Let
\rho_{i}
be the defining string of
S_{i}
, which is of type
c_{i}k_{i}
.
We may assume that the starting faces
\partial_{0}\rho_{i}
are pairwise distinct for different
i
, and likewise for the terminating faces
\partial_{1}\rho_{i}
.
Since each
S_{i}
commutes with stabilizer operators in
\mathcal{R}_{\Lambda}
, its ending faces
\partial_{0}\rho_{i}
and
\partial_{1}\rho_{i}
must be boundary faces of
\Lambda
.
Choose a finite string
\rho_{i}^{\prime}
of type
c_{i}k_{i}
inside
\Lambda^{c}
, such that it starts at
\partial_{1}\rho_{i}
and ends at
\partial_{0}\rho_{i}
. Let
S_{i}^{\prime}
be the string operator of
\rho_{i}
and
S^{\prime}=S_{1}^{\prime}\cdots S_{n}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{R}_{\Lambda^{c}}
. It satisfies
S^{\prime}\Omega=\pm S\Omega
since
SS^{\prime}
is a product of closed string operators.
Let us show that
S^{\dagger}=S
if and only if
S^{\prime\dagger}=S^{\prime}
.
It is enough to show that
[S_{i},S_{j}]=0
if and only if
[S_{i}^{\prime},S_{j}^{\prime}]=0
for
i\neq j
.
The claim is clear if
\rho_{i}
and
\rho_{j}
have the same color, since each
\rho_{i}^{\prime}
has the same color as
\rho_{i}
, and the supports of strings with the same color do not intersect.
Suppose
c_{i}\neq c_{j}
. Note that two closed strings of different colors intersect an even number of times.
This is because each time one string crosses the other, it must cross back to return to its original side, resulting in a pair of intersection points.
Hence,
\rho_{i}\rho_{i}^{\prime}
and
\rho_{j}\rho_{j}^{\prime}
intersect an even number of times. This implies that
\rho_{i}
and
\rho_{j}
intersect an even number of times if and only if
\rho_{i}^{\prime}
and
\rho_{j}^{\prime}
intersect an even number of times, which proves the claim. Similarly,
S^{\dagger}=-S
if and only if
S^{\prime\dagger}=-S^{\prime}
.
We now return to the proof.
If
S^{\dagger}=S
, then
S\Omega\in\mathcal{A}_{\Lambda,sa}\Omega+i\mathcal{B}_{\Lambda,sa}\Omega
is already shown.
Since
S^{\prime\dagger}=S^{\prime}
,
P_{\Lambda}S^{\prime}P_{\Lambda}\in\mathcal{B}_{\Lambda,sa}
. We thus have

iS\Omega=iP_{\Lambda}S\Omega=\pm iP_{\Lambda}S^{\prime}\Omega=\pm iP_{\Lambda}S^{\prime}P_{\Lambda}\Omega\in i\mathcal{B}_{\Lambda,sa}\Omega.

If
S^{\dagger}=-S
, then
iS\Omega\in\mathcal{A}_{\Lambda,sa}\Omega+i\mathcal{B}_{\Lambda,sa}\Omega
is already shown.
Since
S^{\prime\dagger}=-S^{\prime}
,
iP_{\Lambda}S^{\prime}P_{\Lambda}\in\mathcal{B}_{\Lambda,sa}
. We thus have

S\Omega=P_{\Lambda}S\Omega=\pm P_{\Lambda}S^{\prime}\Omega=\mp i(iP_{\Lambda}S^{\prime}P_{\Lambda})\Omega\in i\mathcal{B}_{\Lambda,sa}\Omega.

In summary, we have shown that for any
S
given by a finite product of operators from
\mathcal{S}_{\Lambda}
, both
S\Omega
and
iS\Omega
lie in
\mathcal{A}_{\Lambda,sa}\Omega+i\mathcal{B}_{\Lambda,sa}\Omega
. This establishes the desired result.
∎

Now we are ready to prove the Haag duality for color code model.

Theorem 4.10
.

The Haag duality holds for the ground state representation of color code model. That is, for any cone
\Lambda
, we have
\pi_{0}(\mathcal{A}(\Lambda))^{\prime\prime}=\pi_{0}(\mathcal{A}(\Lambda^{c}))^{\prime}
.

Proof.

It is sufficient to show
\mathcal{R}_{\Lambda^{c}}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathcal{R}_{\Lambda}
.
First, it follows from
[
43
, Thm. 2]
that Lemma
4.9
implies
\mathcal{A}_{\Lambda}=\mathcal{B}_{\Lambda}^{\prime}
in
\mathbf{B}(\mathcal{H}_{\Lambda})
.
Second, by
[
44
, Prop. II 3.10]
, we have
\mathcal{B}_{\Lambda}^{\prime}=\mathcal{R}_{\Lambda^{c}}^{\prime}P_{\Lambda}
.
Thus
\mathcal{R}_{\Lambda}P_{\Lambda}=\mathcal{R}_{\Lambda^{c}}^{\prime}P_{\Lambda}
.
For an arbitrary
A\in\mathcal{R}_{\Lambda^{c}}^{\prime}
, there is an
\hat{A}\in\mathcal{R}_{\Lambda}
such that
AP_{\Lambda}=\hat{A}P_{\Lambda}
. This means that
A|_{\mathcal{H}_{\Lambda}}=\hat{A}|_{\mathcal{H}_{\Lambda}}
, which results in
A=\hat{A}\in\mathcal{R}_{\Lambda}
, by Lemma
4.6
. Hence, the Haag duality for color code model is established.
∎

5
Conclusion and outlooks

In this work, we rigorously define the anyon superselection sectors of the color code model and show that the resulting anyon superselection category is equivalent to
\mathsf{Rep}(D(\mathbb{Z}_{2}\times\mathbb{Z}_{2}))
, in agreement with analyses on finite lattices.
We also prove Haag duality for the color code model.
These results have potential applications in the study of topological quantum phases as well as in quantum error correction.

The following are several interesting directions for future research:

(1) Studying the finite-group-valued color code on an infinite lattice remains an interesting and largely open problem.
Extending the color code model to general finite groups
[
8
]
, or even to (weak) Hopf algebra input, is still unresolved.
Such generalizations are expected to have close connections to Kitaev’s quantum double models
[
31
,
9
,
19
,
20
,
18
,
23
,
24
,
4
,
10
]
and to string-net models with (multi)fusion category input
[
35
,
17
,
21
,
22
,
3
]
.
A systematic investigation along these lines, for both finite and infinite lattices, will be crucial for developing a comprehensive understanding of generalized color codes.

(2) Another intriguing direction concerns topological boundary conditions and bulk-to-boundary anyon condensation
[
45
,
26
,
42
]
.
The six possible topological boundary conditions for the color code are classified in
[
28
]
; developing an infinite-lattice boundary theory will be essential for understanding the stability of the code and the mechanisms of anyon condensation.
Our framework can be naturally extended to incorporate boundaries.

(3) The color code model also admits higher-dimensional generalizations
[
33
]
.
Using tools from algebraic quantum field theory to study these higher-dimensional models would be particularly valuable for obtaining a deeper understanding of their quantum phases.

(4) It is also natural to construct the Kubo-Martin-Schwinger (KMS) states for the color code, in analogy with the toric code model
[
1
]
.
Such an analysis will be important for understanding the stability of the color code as a topological quantum memory.

These problems will be left for future research.
