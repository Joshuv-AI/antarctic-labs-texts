Medication-related osteonecrosis of the jaw in patient with McCune-Albright syndrome: A rare case report
Authors: Cintia Mussi Milani, Guilherme Vargas Marques, Rafaela Sávio Melzer, Liliane Roskamp, Natanael Henrique Ribeiro Mattos
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
McCune-Albright Syndrome (MAS) is a rare disorder characterized by the combination of two or more classic features: Fibrous dysplasia of bone, café-au-lait skin macules and hyperfunctioning endocrinopathies. Bisphosphonates are commonly administered in these patients to mitigate bone lesions and relieve pain; despite the benefits of this medication, its use places patients at risk for medication-related osteonecrosis of the jaws (MRONJ) making it essential to obtain a thorough medical history prior to any oral surgical procedure. The aim of this study was to present a rare case of MRONJ in a 29-year-old female patient with MAS, who had been receiving intravenous zoledronate for seven years and was submitted to the upper right impacted third molar removal. MRONJ remains a challenging condition with no standardized treatment protocol. In the present case, bone sequestrum removal combined with Platelet-Rich Fibrin and buccal fat pad grafting, along with pentoxifylline and tocopherol therapy, yielded positive outcomes.

INTRODUCTION

McCune-Albright Syndrome (MAS) is a rare disorder caused by post-zygotic activating mutations in the GNAS gene, leading to fibrous dysplasia, café-au-lait macules, and endocrinopathies.[12] Impaired skeletal stem cell differentiation results in bone deformities, fractures, and pain.[23] Pharmacologic management commonly includes bisphosphonates (BPs) or denosumab (DMB) to reduce bone lesions and pain, though benefits may be temporary and associated with adverse effects such as medication-related osteonecrosis of the jaw (MRONJ).[1] MRONJ, often triggered by tooth extraction, involves persistent exposed bone in patients receiving antiresorptive therapy.[456] This report describes a case of stage 3 MRONJ in a woman with MAS previously treated with zoledronic acid.

CASE REPORT

A 29-year-old female patient presented to the Dental Clinic with a main complain of ‘liquids draining through the nose and exposed bone in the mouth’. During anamnesis, she reported being diagnosed with MAS at the age of 5 years and 6 months, due to precocious menarche and increased volume in the right supraorbital region. Tomographic images of the cranioencephalic region, right forearm, and femur revealed fibrous dysplasia, confirming the syndrome diagnosis by her doctors [Figures 1–3]. For managing the bone pain, she had been on zoledronic acid therapy for 7 years, discontinuing its use 2 years before. She reported that during the medication period, she underwent an upper right third molar extraction and, since then, had been experiencing exposed bone and nasal fluid leakage. She also revealed undergoing four unsuccessful surgeries to resolve the issue.

Extraoral examination revealed a firm, asymptomatic swelling in the right supraorbital region, attributed to fibrous dysplasia (FD) [Figure 4]. Intraoral examination revealed exposed bone in the missing upper right second and third molars, with purulent drainage [Figure 5]. The upper right first molar did not present caries or restorations; however, it yielded no positive response to the pulp vitality test. The second upper pre-molar was vital, and the first pre-molar was absent. These teeth presented no mobility or sensitivity to palpation or horizontal and vertical percussion. The patient mentioned that the second molar and the first premolar were removed more than 10 years before due to carious lesions.

(a) Frontal extraoral view showing asymmetry in the right supraorbital region. (b) Close-up highlighting the swelling in the supraorbital area

Initial intraoral view showing exposed bone and pus drainage in the posterior right maxillary alveolar ridge

A cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT) of the right posterior maxilla revealed a hyperdense area surrounded by a hypodense line in the area, characteristic of bone sequestrum. Additionally, there was disruption of the inferior cortical border of the maxillary sinus and mucosal thickening [Figure 6]. Based on history, clinical and imaging findings, a diagnosis of stage 3 MRONJ was established. Pentoxifylline (400 mg every 12 hours) and tocopherol (1000 IU once daily) were prescribed (PENTO therapy), as well as amoxicillin–clavulanic acid (875/125 mg every 12 hours for 10 days).

(a) Panoramic view of the Cone-Beam computed tomography showing a hyperdense image in the right posterior maxilla, indicating bone sequestrum. (b) Sagittal section revealing an oroantral communication and thickening of the maxillary sinus mucosal lining

After obtaining informed consent, treatment was initiated with the endodontic therapy of the maxillary first molar. Seven days later, under local anaesthesia, the bone sequestrum was removed [Figure 7], and the cavity was lined with platelet-rich fibrin (PRF);the buccal fat pad was then mobilized to close the oroantral communication [Figure 8]. The patient continued the use of amoxicillin with clavulanic acid for two days and the PENTO for ninety days; paracetamol with codeine (30 mg) was prescribed every six hours as needed for pain control.

(a) Intraoperative view showing the exposed buccal fat pad and the PRF membrane positioned for grafting. (b) Final intraoperative view with the buccal fat pad in position. PRF = Platelet-rich fibrin

Histopathological examination of the bone sequestrum revealed non-vital bone tissue associated with acute inflammation, with no evidence of dysplasia or malignancy. A 30-day postoperative evaluation showed closure of the oroantral communication and complete mucosal healing [Figure 9]. A 2-year radiographic follow-up demonstrated new bone formation in the alveolar ridge corresponding to the regions of teeth 17 and 18 [Figure 10].

DISCUSSION

MAS is a rare and complex disease that results in multiple complications for patients, including bone pain, skeletal deformities with potential pathological fractures, impaired mobility, endocrine disorders, increased risk of malignancy, and reduced quality of life.[237] Due to its broad clinical spectrum and limited differential diagnoses, the disease is typically diagnosed between 11 and 20 years of age, primarily through a combination of clinical and radiographic findings, without the need for biopsy.[7] In the present case, the patient reported menarche at 5.5 years old and an edema in the right supraorbital region. The diagnosis was confirmed by the doctors through imaging and clinical history. However, delayed diagnosis affects over one-third of patients, compromising their prognosis.[8] Referral to specialized tertiary centres is recommended to ensure comprehensive care, pain control, and improved quality of life.[3]

The main therapeutic options for skeletal lesions in MAS include BPs and DMB, aiming to control disease activity and alleviating bone pain.[1] Despite their benefits, these drugs carry a significant risk MRONJ, as observed in our case, where the patient was treated with monthly zoledronic acid infusions for seven years.

Antiresorptive agents like BPs and DMB enhance bone density but decrease maxillary vascularity, predisposing patients to MRONJ,[9] especially following trauma such as the tooth extraction performed in our patient. Risk factors for MRONJ include treatment duration and administration route, dentoalveolar surgery, concurrent oral diseases, smoking, corticosteroid therapy, and systemic or anatomical conditions.[5] Dentoalveolar surgery is the most common predisposing factor.[456] It is recommended to complete necessary dental procedures before initiating antiresorptive therapy and to delay treatment until full bone healing has occurred.[5]

Elective dentoalveolar surgeries such as extractions and implants should be avoided during antiresorptive therapy.[45] If surgery is unavoidable, patients should be monitored closely every 6 to 8 weeks until mucosal healing is complete.[4] Antimicrobial mouth rinses and systemic antibiotics (e.g. penicillin or metronidazole) are advised pre- and post-operatively, with alternatives like erythromycin, clindamycin, or ciprofloxacin for penicillin-allergic patients.[10] Minimally traumatic surgery, smooth bone margins, and tension-free sutures help promote healing.[10]

Dentists play a crucial role in MRONJ diagnosis, prevention, and management. Diagnosis requires the presence of all three criteria outlined previously.[5] A complete dental evaluation should be performed prior to antiresorptive therapy, followed by periodic monitoring during and after treatment.[10]

Treatment aims to control pain, infection, and promote bone healing. Stages 0 to 3 of MRONJ reflect increasing severity. Stage 0 includes patients without exposed bone but presenting nonspecific symptoms such as jaw pain, sinus discomfort, or altered sensation, that may be accompanied by radiographic changes. Stage 1 is characterized by exposed or probable necrotic bone in asymptomatic patients without signs of infection. In stage 2, the exposed or probable bone is associated with infection, inflammation, and pain, with radiographic alterations typically localized to the alveolar region. Stage 3, the one presented by our patient, is characterized by necrotic bone extending beyond the alveolar bone, often with infection, pathological fracture, extraoral fistula, osteolisis extending to the inferior border of the mandible or to the maxillary sinus floor.[4510]

Patients with defined sequestra or poor oral hygiene who do not respond to conservative treatment may benefit from surgical intervention.[5] In progressive or advanced disease, marginal or segmental bone resection is indicated to increase the likelihood of resolution.[4]

The pentoxifylline and tocopherol (PENTO) protocol has shown potential as a non-surgical or adjunctive treatment for MRONJ.[69] Pentoxifylline improves ischemic conditions in necrotic bone, while tocopherol supports angiogenesis.[9] PENTO is generally well tolerated, though side effects like nausea may require dose adjustments or change in prescription for cilostazol.[611] It was not observed in our patient, who tolerated the use of PENTO well. In the current literature, different PENTO protocols are described and there is still no standardized approach for the use of PENTO. Pentoxifylline 400 mg and tocopherol 400 IU every 8 hours, starting 15 days before tooth extraction and continued for another 30 days, was used with encouraging results.[6] Authors also reported a protocol of pentoxifylline 600 mg twice a day and tocopherol 800 IU once daily, starting two months before surgery and continuing for 6 months after surgery, also demonstrating positive outcomes.[9] Due to variations in treatment duration and the ideal dosages reported in the literature, further studies on the mechanism of action of the PENTO protocol in the management of MRONJ are needed to determine the optimal dosage and treatment duration.[11]

Therapy with autologous blood concentrate, known as PRF, is widely used in the treatment of MRONJ.[1213] Its membranes stimulate healing potential and exhibit antimicrobial properties that help control postoperative pain and infection, reduce complication rates, and support the healing process, making it an important adjuvant therapy to surgical treatment.[1214]

Adequate vascularization is critical in MRONJ treatment. The buccal fat pad has a rich blood supply and is easily accessible, making it an effective flap for closing sinus floor defects in MRONJ cases. Its use in conjunction with the removal of bone sequestrum in cases of MRONJ has already been successfully demonstrated,[15] making it a valuable adjunctive therapeutic option to surgical treatment, as observed in the present case.

CONCLUSION

Antiresorptive agents, such as zoledronic acid, are among the pharmacological therapies used in MAS to manage skeletal lesions and bone pain. The well-documented relationship between dentoalveolar trauma and MRONJ in intravenous bisphosphonate users underscores the importance of thorough patient history-taking by dental surgeons prior to any surgical procedure, as well as patient education and risk minimization strategies when surgery is unavoidable. MRONJ remains a challenging condition with no standardized treatment protocol. In this case, bone sequestrum removal combined with PRF and buccal fat pad grafting, along with PENTO therapy, yielded positive outcomes.

Key messages

Patients with McCune-Albright Syndrome receiving long-term antiresorptive therapy are at increased risk of MRONJ, especially following dental trauma.

Comprehensive dental evaluation, patient education, and preventive strategies are essential before any oral surgery.

Surgical sequestrum removal with platelet-rich fibrin (PRF), buccal fat pad grafting, and pentoxifylline and tocopherol therapy can achieve favourable outcomes.
