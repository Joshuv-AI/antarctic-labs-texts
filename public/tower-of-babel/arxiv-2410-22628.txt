Force-current structure in Markovian open quantum systems and its applications: geometric housekeeping-excess decomposition and thermodynamic trade-off relations
Kohei Yoshimura, Yoh Maekawa, Ryuna Nagayama, Sosuke Ito

Abstract
Thermodynamic force and irreversible current are the foundational concepts of classical nonequilibrium thermodynamics. Entropy production rate is provided by their product in classical systems, ranging from mesoscopic to macroscopic systems. However, there is no complete quantum extension of such a structure that respects quantum mechanics. In this paper, we propose anti-Hermitian operators that represent currents and forces accompanied by a gradient structure in open quantum systems described by the quantum master equation. We prove that the entropy production rate is given by the product of the force and current operators, which extends the canonical expression of the entropy production rate in the classical systems. The framework constitutes a comprehensive analogy with the nonequilibrium thermodynamics of discrete classical systems. We also show that the structure leads to the extensions of some results in stochastic thermodynamics: the geometric housekeeping-excess decomposition of entropy production and thermodynamic trade-off relations such as the thermodynamic uncertainty relation and the dissipation-time uncertainty relation. In discussing the trade-off relations, we will introduce a measure of fluctuation, which we term the quantum diffusivity.

II
Preliminary

II.1
Open quantum systems

We consider an open quantum system described by a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
.
Let
\mathrm{opr}(\mathcal{H})
denote the linear operators on
\mathcal{H}
, and
\mathrm{herm}(\mathcal{H})
and
\mathrm{anti}(\mathcal{H})
the subsets of Hermitian and anti-Hermitian operators in
\mathrm{opr}(\mathcal{H})
.
The density operator
\rho\in\mathrm{herm}(\mathcal{H})
(
\rho>0
,
\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\nolimits(\rho)=1
) is assumed to obey the quantum master equation (also known as the Gorini–Kossakowski–Sudarshan–Lindblad equation)
[
19
,
20
]

\displaystyle\frac{d\rho}{dt}=-i[H,\rho]+\mathcal{D}(\rho),

(2)

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}(\rho)\coloneqq\sum_{k\in K_{\mathrm{all}}}\gamma_{k}\left(L_{k}\rho L_{k}^{\dagger}-\frac{1}{2}\{L_{k}^{\dagger}L_{k},\rho\}\right).

(3)

Here,
H\in\mathrm{herm}(\mathcal{H})
is the Hamiltonian, the reduced Planck constant
\hbar
is set to one,
K_{\mathrm{all}}
is the set of jumps, and
\{\cdot,\cdot\}
is the anti-commutator.
Each
k\in K_{\mathrm{all}}
has a positive rate
\gamma_{k}>0
and a jump operator
L_{k}\in\mathrm{opr}(\mathcal{H})
, which represents state changes.
We assume that every
k\in K_{\mathrm{all}}
has a unique counterpart
-k\in K_{\mathrm{all}}
such that
L_{-k}=L_{k}^{\dagger}
.
Let
K
be the set of indexes that only includes either of each pair.
On the other hand, we do not assume the uniqueness of the steady state of the quantum master equation, and the system can have nontrivial conservative quantities.
For later convenience, we define for each
k\in K

\begin{split}\mathcal{D}_{k}(\rho)&\coloneqq\gamma_{k}\left(L_{k}\rho L_{k}^{\dagger}-\frac{1}{2}\{L_{k}^{\dagger}L_{k},\rho\}\right)\\
&\phantom{\coloneqq}+\gamma_{-k}\left(L_{k}^{\dagger}\rho L_{k}-\frac{1}{2}\{L_{k}L_{k}^{\dagger},\rho\}\right).\end{split}

(4)

Thus, we have
\mathcal{D}(\rho)=\sum_{k\in K}\mathcal{D}_{k}(\rho)
.

Thermodynamics is introduced to the model by imposing the local detailed balance condition
\ln(\gamma_{k}/\gamma_{-k})=s_{k}
for each pair
(k,-k)
, where
s_{k}
is the entropy flux into the environment (
s_{-k}=-s_{k}
)
[
17
]
.
The entropy production rate (EPR) of the total system (system + environment) is given as
[
3
]

\displaystyle\dot{\Sigma}(\rho)=-k_{\mathrm{B}}\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\nolimits\big(\mathcal{D}(\rho)\ln\rho\big)+\sum_{k\in K_{\mathrm{all}}}s_{k}\gamma_{k}\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\nolimits(L_{k}^{\dagger}L_{k}\rho).

(5)

The first term gives the von Neumann entropy change
-(d/dt)k_{\mathrm{B}}\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\nolimits(\rho\ln\rho)
, and the second provides the entropy change in the environment.
As shown later, the EPR is always non-negative owing to the local detailed balance condition.
Hereafter, we set the Boltzmann constant
k_{\mathrm{B}}
to one for simplicity.

Especially when process
k
is mediated by a thermal bath at inverse temperature
\beta_{k}
, we may assume the thermodynamic consistency conditions
[
17
]

\displaystyle s_{k}=\beta_{k}\omega_{k},\quad[L_{k},H]=\omega_{k}L_{k},

(6)

where
\omega_{k}
is the energy gap corresponding to
k
and
\beta_{k}\omega_{k}
provides the entropy change
s_{k}
in the bath.
In this case, the second term in the right-hand side of Eq. (
5
) is given by
-\sum_{k}\beta_{k}\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\nolimits(H\mathcal{D}_{k}(\rho))
and
-\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\nolimits(H\mathcal{D}_{k}(\rho))
is interpreted as the heat flux into bath
k

[
3
]
.
In addition, if
\beta_{k}=\beta
for every
k
with a single parameter
\beta
holds, the system can have an equilibrium state
\rho^{\mathrm{eq}}=e^{-\beta H}/Z
with
Z=\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\nolimits(e^{-\beta H})
, where the EPR vanishes.
We call systems satisfying the conditions in Eq. (
6
) with
\beta_{k}=\beta

detailed balanced
.

Throughout the paper, we always assume the Hamiltonian, the jump operators, and the rates are time-independent; namely, we only consider autonomous systems. However, this does not restrict the generality of the results, and they can easily be extended to non-autonomous systems because we only deal with quantities defined at each time, and time derivatives are not generally involved.

We should emphasize the fact that
\gamma_{k}
and
L_{k}
in Eq. (
3
) are not uniquely separable because the quantum master equation is invariant under the transformation
(\gamma_{k},L_{k})\to(\gamma_{k}/|c_{k}|^{2},c_{k}L_{k})
with any
c_{k}\in\mathbb{C}
such that
c_{k}\neq 0
and
c_{-k}=c_{k}^{*}
, where the star indicates the complex conjugate.
For example, when considering a two-level system,
L_{k}
can be chosen as
\sigma_{-}
or
\sigma_{-}/2
.
Although this indefiniteness can be inherited by operators that we introduce later, it will be seen that it does not cause any crucial issues.

II.2
Classical expression of the quantum master equation

The quantum master equation can be reformed into a classical master equation
[
18
]
.
We assume the spectral decomposition
\rho=\sum_{i}p_{i}|i\rangle\langle i|
.
Then, the diagonal elements obey the classical master equation

\displaystyle\frac{dp_{i}}{dt}=\sum_{j,k\in K_{\mathrm{all}}}R_{ij}^{k}p_{j},

(7)

where
R_{ij}^{k}=\gamma_{k}|\langle i|L_{k}|j\rangle|^{2}
for
i\neq j
and
R_{ii}^{k}=-\sum_{j\neq i}R_{ij}^{k}
.
This equation means the equivalence between the dynamics of diagonal elements and that of an occupation probability in the continuous-time Markov chain (Markov jump process) with transition rates
\{R_{ij}^{k}\}
. Label
k
now indicates the “route” used in the transition from
j
to
i
.

Further, “currents” and “forces” are defined by
J_{ij}^{k}=R_{ji}^{k}p_{i}-R_{ij}^{-k}p_{j}
,
F_{ij}^{k}=\ln(R_{ji}^{k}p_{i}/(R_{ij}^{-k}p_{j}))
.
As a consequence, we can prove

\displaystyle\dot{\Sigma}(\rho)=\sum_{i,j}\sum_{k\in K}J_{ij}^{k}F_{ij}^{k},

(8)

which is the standard form known in classical systems.

These classical expressions have been utilized in exploring quantum thermodynamics (e.g.,
[
18
,
48
,
47
]
); however, we can find some issues in this method.
First, the transition rates change in time, even if
\gamma_{k}
and
L_{k}
do not because the eigenbasis
|i\rangle
is time-dependent.
Second, the current and force have nothing to do with any operator expression, so the connection to quantum mechanics is lost.
The first point can be attributed to the Hamiltonian dynamics, which should not be directly associated with the dissipation because it is supposed to govern the reversible dynamics.
In fact, our definition will never refer to the Hamiltonian.
The second problem is also resolved in our formulation; we will introduce operators expressing currents and forces.
We will provide a method to treat quantum thermodynamics as a quantum theory without relying on any basis.
In addition to these problems, another one exists in the conventional definition of force and current, as discussed in the next section.

II.3
Brief review of classical systems

Before considering the quantum extension of the force-current structure, reviewing some preliminary on classical systems would be better.
More details and examples can be found in Ref.
[
31
]
.

Generally, nonequilibrium thermodynamics of classical discrete systems such as Markov jump processes (MJPs) and chemical reaction networks (CRNs) can be treated simultaneously in a single framework.
We consider a distribution
\bm{x}=(x_{i})
, corresponding to the occupation probability in MJPs or the concentration distribution in CRNs. Label
i
indicates microscopic states or chemical species then.
The distribution changes as processes occur, such as state transitions or chemical reactions. The processes are labeled by
e\in E
, where
E
is the set of processes.
The influence of process
e
is encoded in an integer matrix called the stoichiometric matrix
{\nabla}^{\mathrm{T}}
whose
(i,e)
-element provides how
x_{i}
increases/decreases in process
e
(superscript
\mathrm{T}
means transposition).
For each
e
, we define current
J_{e}
as the process’s occurrence rate, which is probability current in MJPs and reaction rate in CRNs.
Finally, the equation of motion (master equation/reaction rate equation) reads

\displaystyle\frac{d\bm{x}}{dt}=\nabla^{\mathrm{T}}\bm{J}(\bm{x}),

(9)

where
\bm{J}
is the vector of
J_{e}
’s and
\bm{J}(\bm{x})
indicates the dependence of
\bm{J}
on
\bm{x}
.

We can equip the dynamics with a thermodynamic structure by assuming that the currents are split into forward and backward fluxes
J_{e}^{+}>0
and
J_{e}^{-}>0
, as
J_{e}=J_{e}^{+}-J_{e}^{-}
and the local detailed balance
F_{e}=\ln(J_{e}^{+}/J_{e}^{-})
. Here,
F_{e}
is the thermodynamic force on process
e
and provides the EPR
\dot{\Sigma}=\bm{J}^{\mathrm{T}}\bm{F}=\sum_{e\in E}J_{e}F_{e}
, where the EPR is in advance defined by the system’s entropy change and environmental entropy increase
[
1
,
4
]
.
Let us examine these quantities in a little more depth.

First, we can characterize the detailed balance in terms of the thermodynamic force.
We define a force
\bm{F}
to be conservative if it is provided as
-\nabla\bm{\phi}
with a potential vector
\bm{\phi}
, which has the same dimension as
\bm{x}
.
Then, if we assume the so-called mass-action form of
\bm{J}^{\pm}=(J_{e}^{\pm})
, the following two statements can be proved equivalent: 1)
\bm{F}(\bm{x})
is conservative 2) Eq. (
9
) has an equilibrium solution
\bm{x}^{\mathrm{eq}}
, where the detailed balance
\bm{J}(\bm{x}^{\mathrm{eq}})=\bm{0}
holds
[
54
]
.
The mass action form of fluxes is widely accepted in chemical thermodynamics
[
55
]
and holds automatically in MJPs (for details, see Appendix
A
).
Because the gradient operator usually has nontrivial null space, potential
\bm{\phi}(\bm{x})
such that
\bm{F}(\bm{x})=-\nabla\bm{\phi}(\bm{x})
is not unique. Nonetheless, we can provide one of them by
\bm{\phi}(\bm{x})=\ln\bm{x}-\ln\bm{x}^{\mathrm{eq}}
with an equilibrium solution
\bm{x}^{\mathrm{eq}}
(where the log of a vector, e.g.
\ln\bm{x}
, denotes the vector of logs, like
(\ln x_{i})
).
Therefore, asking whether or not the force is conservative is equivalent to asking whether or not the system is detailed balanced in a broad class of systems.

Second, the EPR has an additional geometric interpretation, whose utility will be shown in later sections.
We define a diagonal matrix
\mathsf{M}=(M_{e}\delta_{ee^{\prime}})
by
M_{e}=(J_{e}^{+}-J_{e}^{-})/\ln(J_{e}^{+}/J_{e}^{-})
(
\delta_{ee^{\prime}}
is the Kronecker delta), which connects the force and current as
\bm{J}=\mathsf{M}\bm{F}
.
The diagonal elements of
\mathsf{M}
are the so-called logarithmic means between
\bm{J}^{+}
and
\bm{J}^{-}

[
56
]
. Since the log mean is always positive as long as the two numbers are positive,
\mathsf{M}
is a positive definite matrix.
Therefore, the EPR can be seen as the squared norm of
\bm{F}
with
\mathsf{M}
being the metric,
\dot{\Sigma}=\bm{F}^{\mathrm{T}}\mathsf{M}\bm{F}
.
Consequently, we can decompose the EPR into the housekeeping and excess parts in general by projecting the force onto the conservative subspace orthogonally regarding this metric (if interested, see also
[
29
,
30
,
37
,
32
,
33
,
35
]
).

Even though the classical representation of the quantum master equation in the previous section fits in this framework from the dynamical point of view, it has a crucial problem regarding thermodynamics.
Now, we could regard the eigenvalues of
\rho
as the distribution. The processes are labeled by
e=(i,j,k)
and currents read
J_{e}=R_{ij}^{k}p_{j}-R_{ji}^{-k}p_{i}
.
Since
e=(i,j,k)
corresponds to a jump from
j
to
i
,
[\nabla^{\mathrm{T}}]_{ie}=1
,
[\nabla^{\mathrm{T}}]_{je}=-1
and
0
otherwise.
However, when reflecting on the thermodynamic force, we encounter an issue; in the light of the classical case, we expect that the force is given by a potential as
F_{e}=-\sum_{i}[\nabla]_{ei}\phi_{i}
if there is an equilibrium state
\rho^{\mathrm{eq}}
, and that the potential determines the equilibrium state.
However,
\rho
and
\rho^{\mathrm{eq}}
do not generally commute, so how the potential vector and the equilibrium state are connected is unclear.
Then, we wonder if there is a potential
operator
that defines conservative forces and has a natural connection to
\rho^{\mathrm{eq}}
.
In the following, we answer this question affirmatively.

III
Reformulating quantum thermodynamics

III.1
Current operator and force operator

First of all, we introduce an auxiliary Hilbert space
\mathfrak{h}\coloneqq\mathbb{C}^{2|K|}
and operators
\mathbb{L}\in\mathrm{herm}(\mathfrak{h}\otimes\mathcal{H})
and
\Gamma\in\mathrm{herm}(\mathfrak{h})
as

\displaystyle\mathbb{L}\coloneqq\bigoplus_{k\in K}\begin{pmatrix}0&L_{k}^{\dagger}\\
L_{k}&0\end{pmatrix},\quad\Gamma\coloneqq\bigoplus_{k\in K}\begin{pmatrix}\gamma_{k}/2&0\\
0&\gamma_{-k}/2\end{pmatrix},

(10)

where
\otimes
means the tensor product and
\oplus
the direct sum of operators
(the direct sum can be seen as a diagonal alignment of the blocks of operators/real numbers).
These operators are supposed to separately represent the transition structure and the transition rates in the dynamics.
Hereafter, we do not distinguish the number zero and the zero operators and write them as
0
.

The introduction of an auxiliary space is essential; in classical systems, a jump can be labeled by a pair of states
i,j
without introducing a special label for jumps. On the other hand, in quantum dynamics, a jump can involve more than two (eigen)states. In our formulation, an auxiliary space is necessary to treat quantum jumps apart from specific states, and it enables us to discuss connections between dynamics and thermodynamics more efficiently.
As will be discussed later, the characterization of conservative systems in terms of thermodynamic forces and the definition of the geometric housekeeping-excess decomposition are only possible with the introduction of an auxiliary space.

Next, regarding
\Gamma\otimes\rho
as the fluxes (
\bm{J}^{\pm}
) in the classical case, we introduce the current and the force operator as follows:

\displaystyle\mathbb{J}(\rho)

\displaystyle\coloneqq[\mathbb{L},\Gamma\otimes\rho],

(11)

\displaystyle\mathbb{F}(\rho)

\displaystyle\coloneqq[\mathbb{L},\ln(\Gamma\otimes\rho)].

(12)

They are anti-Hermitian operators on
\mathfrak{h}\otimes\mathcal{H}
.
We can express them alternatively as

\begin{split}&\mathbb{J}(\rho)=\bigoplus_{k\in K}\begin{pmatrix}0&J_{-k}\\
J_{k}&0\end{pmatrix}\\
&\text{with}\quad J_{k}=\frac{1}{2}(\gamma_{k}L_{k}\rho-\gamma_{-k}\rho L_{k})\end{split}

(13)

and

\begin{split}&\mathbb{F}(\rho)=\bigoplus_{k\in K}\begin{pmatrix}0&F_{-k}\\
F_{k}&0\end{pmatrix}\\
&\text{with}\quad F_{k}=\ln\left(\frac{\gamma_{k}}{\gamma_{-k}}\right)L_{k}+[L_{k},\ln\rho].\end{split}

(14)

Note that, in particular, the thermodynamic consistency conditions (
6
) with the local detailed balance condition allow us to rewrite
F_{k}
into

\displaystyle F_{k}=[L_{k},\ln\rho+\beta_{k}H].

(15)

The reason we regard these operators as currents and forces is that they satisfy the dynamical and thermodynamic relationships that classical currents and forces fulfill, as will be shown in the following. A few of them are listed in Table
1
. Some correspondence to the classical notion is also suggested in Example
V.1
.
We comment here that an expression similar to Eq. (
13
) is provided recently in Ref.
[
57
]
.

The current and force operators are connected as

\displaystyle\mathbb{J}(\rho)=\mathcal{M}_{\Gamma\otimes\rho}(\mathbb{F}(\rho))

(16)

with a linear super-operator
\mathcal{M}_{\Gamma\otimes\rho}
, which is defined as

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{B}(A)\coloneqq\int_{0}^{1}B^{s}AB^{1-s}ds

(17)

for an operator
A
and a positive operator
B
.
Given the spectral decomposition
B=\sum_{n}b_{n}|n\rangle\langle n|
, we find the explicit formula
[
58
]

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{B}(A)=\sum_{n,m}\frac{b_{n}-b_{m}}{\ln(b_{n}/b_{m})}\langle n|A|m\rangle|n\rangle\langle m|.

(18)

That is, the force and current operators are associated by a quantum generalization of the logarithmic mean.
Despite its nontrivial look, Eq. (
16
) is proved easily; first notice the identity
-(d/ds)\big(e^{sQ}Ae^{(1-s)Q}\big)=e^{sQ}[A,Q]e^{(1-s)Q}
. Inserting
Q=\ln B
and integrating both sides over
s\in[0,1]
, we get

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{B}([A,\ln B])=-BA+AB=[A,B].

Setting
A=\mathbb{L}
and
B=\Gamma\otimes\rho
finally leads to Eq. (
16
).

In the following, the inner product given by
\mathcal{M}_{\Gamma\otimes\rho}
,

\displaystyle\langle\mathbb{F},\mathbb{F}^{\prime}\rangle_{\Gamma\otimes\rho}\coloneqq\langle\mathbb{F},\mathcal{M}_{\Gamma\otimes\rho}(\mathbb{F}^{\prime})\rangle,

(19)

and the induced norm

\displaystyle\lVert\mathbb{F}\rVert_{\Gamma\otimes\rho}\coloneqq\sqrt{\langle\mathbb{F},\mathbb{F}\rangle_{\Gamma\otimes\rho}}

(20)

will play a central role. Hereafter, the plain inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
represents the Hilbert–Schmidt inner product
\langle A,B\rangle=\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\nolimits(A^{\dagger}B)
for the appropriate operator space.
To see that
\mathcal{M}_{\Gamma\otimes\rho}
actually induces an inner product, we show some crucial properties of
\mathcal{M}_{B}
for an arbitrary positive operator
B
.

Let
A
and
C
also be arbitrary (possibly not positive) operators on the same Hilbert space as the one on which
B
acts.
First,
\mathcal{M}_{B}
is linear, which is evident from the definition. Second, it is a symmetric super-operator regarding the Hilbert–Schmidt inner product because

\displaystyle\langle\mathcal{M}_{B}(A),C\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{n,m}\frac{b_{n}-b_{m}}{\ln(b_{n}/b_{m})}\langle m|A^{\dagger}|n\rangle\langle n|C|m\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle A,\mathcal{M}_{B}(C)\rangle.

Third, it is positive definite because, for an arbitrary operator,

\displaystyle\langle\mathcal{M}_{B}(A),A\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{n,m}\frac{b_{n}-b_{m}}{\ln(b_{n}/b_{m})}|\langle n|A|m\rangle|^{2}\geq 0,

and this quantity is zero only if
A
is a zero operator.
As a result, the induced inner product
\langle A,C\rangle_{B}\coloneqq\langle A,\mathcal{M}_{B}(C)\rangle
satisfies the axioms of inner product, and we can define the norm
\lVert A\rVert_{B}\coloneqq\sqrt{\langle A,A\rangle_{B}}
.

In addition, we note that the inverse
\mathcal{M}_{B}^{-1}
can be defined by inverting the log-mean factor in Eq. (

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{B}^{-1}(A)\coloneqq\sum_{n,m}\frac{\ln(b_{n}/b_{m})}{b_{n}-b_{m}}\langle n|A|m\rangle|n\rangle\langle m|.

(21)

Since it shares all three properties with

, it also leads to an inner product.

Now that the three properties of

, linearity, symmetry, and positive definiteness, are proved, we can connect Eq. (

[

]
.
Let us consider physical currents, e.g., electric currents or heat currents,

(

\displaystyle j_{\alpha}(\rho)=\langle\mathbb{A}_{\alpha},\mathbb{J}(\rho)\rangle

(22)

with anti-Hermitian operators
\mathbb{A}_{\alpha}\in\mathrm{anti}(\mathfrak{h}\otimes\mathcal{H})
.
We assume that the force operator is given in the dual form

\displaystyle\mathbb{F}(\rho)=\sum_{\alpha}f_{\alpha}(\rho)\mathbb{A}_{\alpha}

(23)

by physical forces
f_{\alpha}(\rho)\in\mathbb{R}
, such as voltage or temperature gradient.
We further define a matrix
L(\rho)
by

\displaystyle L_{\alpha\beta}(\rho)\coloneqq\langle\mathbb{A}_{\alpha},\mathbb{A}_{\beta}\rangle_{\Gamma\otimes\rho}=\langle\mathbb{A}_{\alpha},\mathcal{M}_{\Gamma\otimes\rho}(\mathbb{A}_{\beta})\rangle.

(24)

If
\rho=\rho^{\mathrm{eq}}=e^{-\beta H}/Z
, this definition resembles the Green–Kubo formula
[

,
60
]
as

\displaystyle L_{\alpha\beta}(\rho^{\mathrm{eq}})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{Z}\int_{0}^{1}\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\nolimits[\mathbb{A}_{\alpha}(\Gamma^{s}\otimes e^{-s\beta H})\mathbb{A}_{\beta}(\Gamma^{1-s}\otimes e^{-(1-s)\beta H})]ds.

(25)

Then, Eq. (
16
) leads to the relation

\displaystyle j_{\alpha}(\rho)=\sum_{\beta}L_{\alpha\beta}(\rho)f_{\beta}(\rho)

(26)

because
\sum_{\beta}L_{\alpha\beta}(\rho)f_{\beta}(\rho)=\sum_{\beta}\langle\mathbb{A}_{\alpha},\mathcal{M}_{\Gamma\otimes\rho}(f_{\beta}(\rho)\mathbb{A}_{\beta})\rangle=\langle\mathbb{A}_{\alpha},\mathcal{M}_{\Gamma\otimes\rho}(\mathbb{F}(\rho))\rangle
and
\mathbb{J}(\rho)=\mathcal{M}_{\Gamma\otimes\rho}(\mathbb{F}(\rho))
.
Let us discuss that Eq. (
26
) is a nonequilibrium extension of the Onsager reciprocal relation.
When
\rho
is close to equilibrium
\rho^{\mathrm{eq}}
,
j_{\alpha}(\rho)
and
f_{\beta}(\rho)
will be of the order of the difference between
\rho
and
\rho^{\mathrm{eq}}
, which we write
\varepsilon
.
Then,
L(\rho)
can be replaced by
L(\rho^{\mathrm{eq}})
because
[L_{\alpha\beta}(\rho)-L_{\alpha\beta}(\rho^{\mathrm{eq}})]f_{\beta}(\rho)=O(\varepsilon^{2})
.
Thus, near the equilibrium state
\rho^{\mathrm{eq}}
, to leading order, we obtain

\displaystyle j_{\alpha}(\rho)=\sum_{\beta}L_{\alpha\beta}(\rho^{\mathrm{eq}})f_{\beta}(\rho).

(27)

Moreover, since
\mathbb{A}_{\alpha}
are anti-Hermitian, the symmetry of the inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\Gamma\otimes\rho}
proves the reciprocal relation

\displaystyle L_{\alpha\beta}(\rho)=L_{\beta\alpha}(\rho).

(28)

Therefore, Eq. (
27
) is the Onsager reciprocal relation
[
12
,
13
]
and Eq. (
26
) is seen as its nonequilibrium extension
[
61
]
.
If
\mathbb{A}_{\alpha}
are linearly independent,

becomes positive definite because for any
\bm{\xi}=(\xi_{\alpha})
, we have

\displaystyle\bm{\xi}^{\mathrm{T}}L(\rho)\bm{\xi}=\Big\lVert\sum_{\alpha}\xi_{\alpha}\mathbb{A}_{\alpha}\Big\rVert_{\Gamma\otimes\rho}^{2},

(29)

and it is positive unless
\bm{\xi}=0
.
In general, we have the following expression of the EPR

\displaystyle\dot{\Sigma}(\rho)=\sum_{\alpha,\beta}f_{\alpha}(\rho)L_{\alpha\beta}(\rho)f_{\beta}(\rho).

(30)

We finally mention that Eq. (
23
) is a crucial assumption and should be proved generally or verified in concrete cases.

III.2
Connection to dynamics

Next, we explain how the operators are related to the dynamics.
First, we define the gradient super-operator
\nabla_{\mathbb{L}}:\mathrm{opr}(\mathcal{H})\to\mathrm{opr}(\mathfrak{h}\otimes\mathcal{H})
by

\displaystyle\nabla_{\mathbb{L}}A\coloneqq[(I_{\mathfrak{h}}\otimes A),\mathbb{L}]=\bigoplus_{k\in K}\begin{pmatrix}0&[A,L_{k}^{\dagger}]\\
[A,L_{k}]&0\end{pmatrix},

(31)

where

is the identity matrix of
\mathfrak{h}
.
Specifically, it maps
\mathrm{herm}(\mathcal{H})
into
\mathrm{anti}(\mathfrak{h}\otimes\mathcal{H})

\nabla_{\mathbb{L}}(AB)=\nabla_{\mathbb{L}}A(I_{\mathfrak{h}}\otimes B)+(I_{\mathfrak{h}}\otimes A)\nabla_{\mathbb{L}}B

The divergence super-operator

, i.e., the adjoint of

, is provided as

\displaystyle\nabla_{\mathbb{L}}^{*}\mathbb{B}=\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\nolimits_{\mathfrak{h}}[\mathbb{B},\mathbb{L}].

(32)

It can be seen as follows: for

and
\mathbb{B}\in\mathrm{opr}(\mathfrak{h}\otimes\mathcal{H})
, we have
\langle\nabla_{\mathbb{L}}A,\mathbb{B}\rangle=\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\nolimits([\mathbb{L},(I_{\mathfrak{h}}\otimes A^{\dagger})]\mathbb{B})=\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\nolimits((I_{\mathfrak{h}}\otimes A^{\dagger})[\mathbb{B},\mathbb{L}])=\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\nolimits_{\mathcal{H}}(A^{\dagger}\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\nolimits_{\mathfrak{h}}[\mathbb{B},\mathbb{L}])=\langle A,\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\nolimits_{\mathfrak{h}}[\mathbb{B},\mathbb{L}]\rangle
, where
\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\nolimits_{\mathcal{H}}
and
\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\nolimits_{\mathfrak{h}}
are the partial traces over
\mathcal{H}
and
\mathfrak{h}
. When
\mathbb{B}
is in the form

\displaystyle\mathbb{B}=\bigoplus_{k\in K}\begin{pmatrix}0&B_{-k}\\
B_{k}&0\end{pmatrix},

(33)

the divergence reads

\displaystyle\nabla_{\mathbb{L}}^{*}\mathbb{B}=\sum_{k\in K}\big([B_{k},L_{k}^{\dagger}]+[B_{-k},L_{k}]\big).

(34)

With the gradient super-operator, we can define conservative forces; define a force operator

to be

with a potential operator

Moreover, the divergence super-operator allows us to rewrite the dissipator in a continuity-equation form

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}(\rho)=\nabla_{\mathbb{L}}^{*}\mathbb{J}(\rho).

(35)

This expression was proved for detailed-balanced systems in the form

Keeping the relation

When the system is detailed balanced, the system has an equilibrium solution

The adiabatic-nonadiabatic decomposition is defined as follows

IV.1.2
Housekeeping EPR

We first define the housekeeping EPR in a geometric manner.
We assume that the Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
has a finite dimension for technical reasons, but we expect that it can be generalized to infinite-dimensional cases with appropriate functional analytic treatment.
In the geometric decomposition, the housekeeping EPR is defined as the squared “distance” between the actual force and the subspace of conservative forces (see Fig.
2
).
Now, we have the squared-norm expression of EPR
\dot{\Sigma}(\rho)=\lVert\mathbb{F}(\rho)\rVert_{\Gamma\otimes\rho}^{2}
and the definition of conservative force, i.e., the form
-\nabla_{\mathbb{L}}\phi
.
Therefore, we can define the housekeeping EPR as

\displaystyle\dot{\Sigma}^{\mathrm{hk}}(\rho)\coloneqq\min_{\mathbb{F}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}}\lVert\mathbb{F}(\rho)-\mathbb{F}^{\prime}\rVert_{\Gamma\otimes\rho}^{2},

(50)

where
\mathcal{C}\coloneqq\{-\nabla_{\mathbb{L}}\phi\mid\phi\in\mathrm{herm}(\mathcal{H})\}\subset\mathcal{F}
is the subspace of conservative forces and
\mathcal{F}=\mathrm{anti}(\mathfrak{h}\otimes\mathcal{H})
denotes the space of forces.

The housekeeping EPR vanishes when the system is conservative, i.e., detailed balanced.
On the other hand, when
\mathbb{F}(\rho)
contains a non-conservative contribution, it has a positive value.
Therefore, the housekeeping EPR quantifies how the external driving that makes the system non-conservative causes dissipation.

Figure 2:
Schematics of the geometric housekeeping-excess decomposition.
We have two subspaces in the entire space of thermodynamic forces:
\mathcal{C}
, the space of conservative forces
\{-\nabla_{\mathbb{L}}\phi\mid\phi\in\mathrm{herm}(\mathcal{H})\}
, and the orthogonal complement
\mathcal{C}^{\perp}
with respect to the inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\Gamma\otimes\rho}
. If two forces
\mathbb{F}^{\prime}
and
\mathbb{F}^{\prime\prime}
satisfy
\mathbb{F}^{\prime}-\mathbb{F}^{\prime\prime}\in\mathcal{C}^{\perp}
, then they provide the same dissipator as
\nabla_{\mathbb{L}}^{*}\mathbb{J}^{\prime}=\nabla_{\mathbb{L}}^{*}\mathbb{J}^{\prime\prime}
, where
\mathbb{J}^{\prime}=\mathcal{M}_{\Gamma\otimes\rho}(\mathbb{F}^{\prime})
and
\mathbb{J}^{\prime\prime}=\mathcal{M}_{\Gamma\otimes\rho}(\mathbb{F}^{\prime\prime})
.
In the decomposition, the actual force
\mathbb{F}(\rho)
is projected onto the conservative subspace
\mathcal{C}
orthogonally.
Given the projected force be
\mathbb{F}_{\mathrm{c}}(\rho)
,
the distance between
\mathbb{F}(\rho)
and
\mathcal{C}
is obtained as the norm of
\mathbb{F}_{\mathrm{nc}}(\rho)=\mathbb{F}(\rho)-\mathbb{F}_{\mathrm{c}}(\rho)
.
The squared norm of
\mathbb{F}_{\mathrm{nc}}(\rho)
is interpreted as the housekeeping EPR, while that of
\mathbb{F}_{\mathrm{c}}(\rho)
is the excess EPR.
As suggested in the schematics, the projected force
\mathbb{F}_{\mathrm{c}}(\rho)
is unique, and its norm can be identified with the distance between the zero force
0
and the affine subspace
\mathbb{F}(\rho)+\mathcal{C}^{\perp}
.

To understand those facts in more depth, we consider the properties of the minimizer.
We write the minimizer of Eq. (
50
) as
\mathbb{F}_{\mathrm{c}}(\rho)=-\nabla_{\mathbb{L}}\phi_{*}(\rho)
.
The minimizer
\mathbb{F}_{\mathrm{c}}(\rho)
is the conservative force closest to the actual force in terms of the metric determined by
\Gamma\otimes\rho
.
From standard facts in linear algebra, we have the following facts
[
67
]
(see also Fig.
2
):

1.

\mathbb{F}_{\mathrm{c}}(\rho)
is unique,

2.

\langle\mathbb{F}(\rho)-\mathbb{F}_{\mathrm{c}}(\rho),\mathbb{F}_{\mathrm{c}}(\rho)\rangle_{\Gamma\otimes\rho}=0
,

3.

\mathbb{F}(\rho)-\mathbb{F}_{\mathrm{c}}(\rho)\in\mathcal{C}^{\perp}
,

where
\mathcal{C}^{\perp}
is the orthogonal complement of
\mathcal{C}
, given as

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}^{\perp}=\{\mathbb{F}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{F}\mid\nabla_{\mathbb{L}}^{*}\mathcal{M}_{\Gamma\otimes\rho}(\mathbb{F}^{\prime})=0\}.

(51)

If we define the non-conservative force and the conservative and non-conservative currents as
\mathbb{F}_{\mathrm{nc}}(\rho)=\mathbb{F}(\rho)-\mathbb{F}_{\mathrm{c}}(\rho)
,
\mathbb{J}_{\mathrm{c}}(\rho)=\mathcal{M}_{\Gamma\otimes\rho}(\mathbb{F}_{\mathrm{c}}(\rho))
and
\mathbb{J}_{\mathrm{nc}}(\rho)=\mathcal{M}_{\Gamma\otimes\rho}(\mathbb{F}_{\mathrm{nc}}(\rho))
, we have
\mathbb{J}(\rho)=\mathbb{J}_{\mathrm{c}}(\rho)+\mathbb{J}_{\mathrm{nc}}(\rho)
and

\displaystyle\nabla_{\mathbb{L}}^{*}\mathbb{J}(\rho)=\nabla_{\mathbb{L}}^{*}\mathbb{J}_{\mathrm{c}}(\rho),\quad\nabla_{\mathbb{L}}^{*}\mathbb{J}_{\mathrm{nc}}(\rho)=0.

(52)

Therefore, by the definition of the housekeeping EPR, we can divide the total current
\mathbb{J}(\rho)
into the “futile” part
\mathbb{J}_{\mathrm{nc}}(\rho)
that does not contribute to the dynamics and the essential one
\mathbb{J}_{\mathrm{c}}(\rho)
.
The non-conservative motion is only used to keep the system out of equilibrium and does not affect state change.

Since
\dot{\Sigma}^{\mathrm{hk}}=\lVert\mathbb{F}_{\mathrm{nc}}(\rho)\rVert_{\Gamma\otimes\rho}^{2}
, we have the expression

\displaystyle\dot{\Sigma}^{\mathrm{hk}}(\rho)=\langle\mathbb{J}_{\mathrm{nc}}(\rho),\mathbb{F}_{\mathrm{nc}}(\rho)\rangle,

(53)

where the inner product is the Hilbert–Schmidt one.
This expression represents the fact that the housekeeping dissipation stems from the “futile” motion in the dynamics.
The formula has been obtained for steady-state dissipation in Schnakenberg’s seminal work
[
14
]
and extended to the housekeeping dissipation in classical systems in and out of steady states in Ref.
[
31
]
.
Equation (
53
) can be regarded as a quantum extension of those results.

IV.1.3
Excess EPR

Next, we define the excess EPR by
\dot{\Sigma}^{\mathrm{ex}}\coloneqq\dot{\Sigma}-\dot{\Sigma}^{\mathrm{hk}}
.
It has other expressions

\displaystyle\dot{\Sigma}^{\mathrm{ex}}=\lVert\mathbb{F}_{\mathrm{c}}(\rho)\rVert_{\Gamma\otimes\rho}^{2}=\min_{\mathbb{F}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{F}(\rho)+\mathcal{C}^{\perp}}\lVert\mathbb{F}^{\prime}\rVert_{\Gamma\otimes\rho}^{2},

(54)

where
\mathbb{F}(\rho)+\mathcal{C}^{\perp}=\{\mathbb{F}^{\prime}\mid\exists\mathbb{F}^{\prime\prime}\in\mathcal{C}^{\perp}\;\mathrm{s.t.}\;\mathbb{F}^{\prime}=\mathbb{F}(\rho)+\mathbb{F}^{\prime\prime}\}
.
These equations are derived from the standard facts in linear algebra

4.

\lVert\mathbb{F}(\rho)\rVert_{\Gamma\otimes\rho}^{2}=\lVert\mathbb{F}_{\mathrm{nc}}(\rho)\rVert_{\Gamma\otimes\rho}^{2}+\lVert\mathbb{F}_{\mathrm{c}}(\rho)\rVert_{\Gamma\otimes\rho}^{2}

5.

\displaystyle\lVert\mathbb{F}_{\mathrm{c}}(\rho)\rVert_{\Gamma\otimes\rho}^{2}=\min_{\mathbb{F}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{F}(\rho)+\mathcal{C}^{\perp}}\lVert\mathbb{F}^{\prime}\rVert_{\Gamma\otimes\rho}^{2}
.

The implication of the condition
\mathbb{F}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{F}(\rho)+\mathcal{C}^{\perp}
becomes clearer if we focus on currents rather than forces; we can rewrite Eq. (
54
) as follows:

\begin{split}&\dot{\Sigma}^{\mathrm{ex}}(\rho)=\min_{\mathbb{J}^{\prime}}\langle\mathbb{J}^{\prime},\mathcal{M}_{\Gamma\otimes\rho}^{-1}(\mathbb{J}^{\prime})\rangle\\
&\mathrm{s.t.}\quad\nabla_{\mathbb{L}}^{*}\mathbb{J}^{\prime}=\nabla_{\mathbb{L}}^{*}\mathbb{J}(\rho)\equiv\mathcal{D}(\rho).\end{split}

(55)

Therefore, the excess EPR can be understood as the minimum dissipation to induce the given dissipative dynamics
\mathcal{D}(\rho)
.

We remark on a connection to the optimal transport theory
[
68
]
.
We can derive the classical version of Eqs. (
54
) and
(
55
)

[
31
,
34
]
, which directly provides a discrete generalization of the 2-Wasserstein distance
[
69
,
70
]
through the Benamou–Brenier formula
[
71
]
.
While further consideration is required on how to treat the unitary term
-i[H,\rho]
in the dynamics, the expressions in Eqs. (
54
) and (
55
) will likely serve as an essential stepping stone in considering optimal transport theory in quantum mechanics.

IV.1.4
Maximization formula

The housekeeping and excess EPRs can be formulated by maximization:

\displaystyle\dot{\Sigma}^{\mathrm{hk}}(\rho)

\displaystyle=\max_{\mathbb{F}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}^{\perp}}\frac{|\langle\mathbb{F}^{\prime},\mathbb{F}(\rho)\rangle_{\Gamma\otimes\rho}|^{2}}{\lVert\mathbb{F}^{\prime}\rVert_{\Gamma\otimes\rho}^{2}},

(56)

\displaystyle\dot{\Sigma}^{\mathrm{ex}}(\rho)

\displaystyle=\max_{\mathbb{F}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}}\frac{|\langle\mathbb{F}^{\prime},\mathbb{F}(\rho)\rangle_{\Gamma\otimes\rho}|^{2}}{\lVert\mathbb{F}^{\prime}\rVert_{\Gamma\otimes\rho}^{2}}.

(57)

Note that the difference between the two equations is in the range of maximization.
These are proved at once as follows: we let
\alpha
stand for
\mathrm{c}
or
\mathrm{nc}
. In the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality

\displaystyle\lVert\mathbb{F}^{\prime}\rVert_{\Gamma\otimes\rho}^{2}\lVert\mathbb{F}_{\alpha}(\rho)\rVert_{\Gamma\otimes\rho}^{2}\geq|\langle\mathbb{F}^{\prime},\mathbb{F}_{\alpha}(\rho)\rangle_{\Gamma\otimes\rho}|^{2},

(58)

we have

\displaystyle\langle\mathbb{F}^{\prime},\mathbb{F}_{\alpha}(\rho)\rangle_{\Gamma\otimes\rho}=\langle\mathbb{F}^{\prime},\mathbb{F}(\rho)\rangle_{\Gamma\otimes\rho}

(59)

for
\mathbb{F}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}
if
\alpha=\mathrm{c}
, and for
\mathbb{F}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}^{\perp}
if
\alpha=\mathrm{nc}
.
With the corresponding subspace denoted by
\mathcal{C}_{\alpha}
, rearranging Eq. (
58
) leads to

\displaystyle\lVert\mathbb{F}_{\alpha}(\rho)\rVert_{\Gamma\otimes\rho}^{2}\geq\frac{|\langle\mathbb{F}^{\prime},\mathbb{F}(\rho)\rangle_{\Gamma\otimes\rho}|^{2}}{\lVert\mathbb{F}^{\prime}\rVert_{\Gamma\otimes\rho}^{2}}

(60)

for any
\mathbb{F}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}_{\alpha}
. The equality can be achieved by setting
\mathbb{F}^{\prime}=\mathbb{F}_{\alpha}(\rho)\in\mathcal{C}_{\alpha}
.

IV.1.5
Invariance

We show the invariance of the decomposition under the transformation
(\gamma_{k},L_{k})\to(\gamma_{k}/|c_{k}|^{2},c_{k}L_{k})
discussed in Sec.
II.1
.
We prove this statement in two steps: first, we show that the potential(s)
\phi
that provides the conservative force is independent of the transformation. Next, we show that the excess EPR is invariant, which implies the invariance of the decomposition.

First, we note that the conservative force
\mathbb{F}_{\mathrm{c}}(\rho)
is the unique element that satisfies the conditions
\mathbb{F}_{\mathrm{c}}(\rho)\in\mathcal{C}
and
\mathbb{F}(\rho)-\mathbb{F}_{\mathrm{c}}\in\mathcal{C}^{\perp}
simultaneously (c.f. Fig.
2
).
Thus, any
\phi
that solves the equation

\displaystyle-\nabla_{\mathbb{L}}^{*}\mathcal{M}_{\Gamma\otimes\rho}(\nabla_{\mathbb{L}}\phi)=\mathcal{D}(\rho)

(61)

can give the conservative force as
\mathbb{F}_{\mathrm{c}}(\rho)=-\nabla_{\mathbb{L}}\phi
because then
-\nabla_{\mathbb{L}}\phi
satisfies both the conditions. Note that
\mathcal{D}(\rho)
appeared from
\nabla_{\mathbb{L}}^{*}\mathcal{M}_{\Gamma\otimes\rho}(\mathbb{F}(\rho))
and is invariant under the transformation.

Importantly, the left-hand side of Eq. (
61
) is also invariant regardless of
\phi
;
For any
X\in\mathrm{opr}(\mathcal{H})
, we can show

\displaystyle\nabla_{\mathbb{L}}^{*}\mathcal{M}_{\Gamma\otimes\rho}(\nabla_{\mathbb{L}}X)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\in K}\bigg(\int_{0}^{1}ds\frac{\gamma_{k}^{s}\gamma_{-k}^{1-s}}{2}\big[\rho^{s}[X,L_{k}^{\dagger}]\rho^{1-s},L_{k}\big]

\displaystyle\phantom{=\sum_{k\in K}\Big(}+\int_{0}^{1}ds\frac{\gamma_{k}^{1-s}\gamma_{-k}^{s}}{2}\big[\rho^{s}[X,L_{k}]\rho^{1-s},L_{k}^{\dagger}\big]\bigg),

(62)

and this is invariant under
(\gamma_{k},L_{k})\to(\gamma_{k}/|c_{k}|^{2},c_{k}L_{k})
with
c_{-k}
being the complex conjugate of
c_{k}
because the pair of
L_{k}
and
L_{k}^{\dagger}
yields
|c_{k}|^{2}
, while both
\gamma_{k}^{s}\gamma_{-k}^{1-s}
and
\gamma_{k}^{1-s}\gamma_{-k}^{s}
result in the canceling factor
1/|c_{k}|^{2}
(the derivation of Eq. (
62
) is given in Appendix
B
).
Therefore, we can define the
Laplacian

\mathcal{L}
as

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(X)\coloneqq\nabla_{\mathbb{L}}^{*}\mathcal{M}_{\Gamma\otimes\rho}(\nabla_{\mathbb{L}}X)

(63)

and it becomes invariant.

Consequently, Eq. (
61
) reads

\displaystyle-\mathcal{L}(\phi)=\mathcal{D}(\rho)

(64)

and it is clear that the potentials that can give the conservative force are insensitive to the transformation. This means that
\dot{\Sigma}^{\mathrm{ex}}(\rho)=\lVert\mathbb{F}_{\mathrm{c}}(\rho)\rVert_{\Gamma\otimes\rho}^{2}
is also invariant under the transformation because

\displaystyle\lVert\mathbb{F}_{\mathrm{c}}(\rho)\rVert_{\Gamma\otimes\rho}^{2}=\lVert-\nabla_{\mathbb{L}}\phi\rVert_{\Gamma\otimes\rho}^{2}=\langle\phi,\mathcal{L}(\phi)\rangle

(65)

for any
\phi
that solves
-\mathcal{L}(\phi)=\mathcal{D}(\rho)
.
Therefore, the excess and housekeeping EPRs are well-defined regardless of the indefiniteness of
\gamma_{k}
and
L_{k}
.

We note that Eq. (
62
) further implies that the Laplacian is separable and the decomposed parts are also invariant:

\begin{split}&\mathcal{L}(X)=\sum_{k\in K}\mathcal{L}_{k}(X)\\
&\text{with}\quad\mathcal{L}_{k}(X)\coloneqq\nabla_{\mathbb{L}_{k}}^{*}\mathcal{M}_{\Gamma_{k}\otimes\rho}(\nabla_{\mathbb{L}_{k}}X).\end{split}

(66)

IV.1.6
Unseparability

We discuss that the decomposed EPRs are
not
separable, unlike the total EPR; if we do the minimization (
50
) for each
k
, we will get a force of the form
\bigoplus_{k\in K}(-\nabla_{\mathbb{L}_{k}}\phi_{k})
, which is not a
globally
conservative force but a
fully-locally
conservative force.
In fact, this fully local conservativeness is automatically satisfied if the dissipations are mediated by thermal baths; then, the expression in Eq. (
15
) implies
\mathbb{F}_{k}(\rho)=-\nabla_{\mathbb{L}_{k}}\phi_{k}(\rho)
, where
\phi_{k}(\rho)=\beta_{k}H+\ln\rho
.
On the other hand, global conservativeness, namely, expression
\mathbb{F}(\rho)=-\nabla_{\mathbb{L}}\phi
by a single potential operator
\phi
, is available only when the system is free from external driving so that it relaxes to the equilibrium.
Therefore, in the decomposition, it is crucial that the minimization is implemented simultaneously to quantify the external effect on dissipation.

Formally, we may define partial elements because the conservative force and current keep the direct-sum structure as
\mathbb{F}_{\mathrm{c}}(\rho)=\bigoplus_{k\in K}(-\nabla_{\mathbb{L}_{k}}\phi_{*}(\rho))
and
\mathbb{J}_{\mathrm{c}}(\rho)=\bigoplus_{k\in K}\mathcal{M}_{\Gamma_{k}\otimes\rho}(-\nabla_{\mathbb{L}_{k}}\phi_{*}(\rho))
.
However, in addition to the point that conservativeness is crucial only globally, we should be aware that the first equation in Eq. (
52
),
\nabla_{\mathbb{L}}\mathbb{J}_{\mathrm{c}}(\rho)=\mathcal{D}(\rho)
, does not mean that each current component
\mathcal{M}_{\Gamma_{k}\otimes\rho}(\nabla_{\mathbb{L}_{k}}\phi_{*}(\rho))
gives
\mathcal{D}_{k}(\rho)
when operated by
\nabla_{\mathbb{L}_{k}}^{*}
; therefore, the fragments of the housekeeping and excess EPRs would not be associated with conservativeness or minimum dissipation in the global sense.
We note that, however, it could be interesting to consider
partially-local
conservativeness if we have a reasonable partition of
K
into a few parts, as in the bipartite systems considered in information thermodynamics
[
72
]
.

IV.2
Thermodynamic trade-off relations

Next, we discuss trade-off relations derived from the geometric structure.
Let us begin with a few general results.
The first one is a lower bound on the excess EPR derived from the maximization representation of the excess EPR (
57
). For any
X\in\mathrm{herm}(\mathcal{H})
, the maximization implies

\displaystyle\dot{\Sigma}^{\mathrm{ex}}(\rho)\geq\frac{|\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\nolimits(X\mathcal{D}(\rho))|^{2}}{\lVert\nabla_{\mathbb{L}}X\rVert_{\Gamma\otimes\rho}^{2}},

(67)

where the numerator is derived from

\displaystyle\langle\nabla_{\mathbb{L}}X,\mathbb{F}(\rho)\rangle_{\Gamma\otimes\rho}

\displaystyle=\langle X,\nabla_{\mathbb{L}}^{*}\mathcal{M}_{\Gamma\otimes\rho}(\mathbb{F}(\rho))\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle X,\mathcal{D}(\rho)\rangle=\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\nolimits(X\mathcal{D}(\rho)).

Since the excess EPR
\dot{\Sigma}^{\mathrm{ex}}(\rho)
is smaller than or equal to the total EPR
\dot{\Sigma}(\rho)
, a weaker inequality is obtained by replacing
\dot{\Sigma}^{\mathrm{ex}}(\rho)
with
\dot{\Sigma}(\rho)
in Eq. (
67
).
When
X
is time-independent, the time derivative of the expectation value
\langle X\rangle=\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\nolimits(X\rho)
is given by

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\langle X\rangle=-i\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\nolimits(X[H,\rho])+\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\nolimits(X\mathcal{D}(\rho)).

(68)

If
X
further commutes with
\rho
or
H
, we obtain
\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\nolimits(X[H,\rho])=0
; then, Eq. (
67
) reads

\displaystyle\dot{\Sigma}^{\mathrm{ex}}(\rho)\geq\frac{|\frac{d}{dt}\langle X\rangle|^{2}}{\lVert\nabla_{\mathbb{L}}X\rVert_{\Gamma\otimes\rho}^{2}}.

(69)

The second one is a lower bound on the partial EPRs:

\displaystyle\dot{\Sigma}_{k}(\rho)\geq\frac{|\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\nolimits(X\mathcal{D}_{k}(\rho))|^{2}}{\lVert\nabla_{\mathbb{L}_{k}}X\rVert_{\Gamma_{k}\otimes\rho}^{2}}.

(70)

It is proved by applying the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality as

\displaystyle\dot{\Sigma}_{k}(\rho)=\lVert\mathbb{F}_{k}(\rho)\rVert_{\Gamma_{k}\otimes\rho}^{2}\geq\frac{|\langle\nabla_{\mathbb{L}_{k}}X,\mathbb{F}_{k}(\rho)\rangle_{\Gamma_{k}\otimes\rho}|^{2}}{\lVert\nabla_{\mathbb{L}_{k}}X\rVert_{\Gamma_{k}\otimes\rho}^{2}}

(71)

and rewriting the numerator.

IV.2.1
Quantum diffusivity

In the two bounds in Eqs. (
67
) and (
70
), the denominators can be upper bound as

\displaystyle\lVert\nabla_{\mathbb{L}_{k}}X\rVert_{\Gamma_{k}\otimes\rho}^{2}\leq\mathscr{D}_{X}^{k},\quad\lVert\nabla_{\mathbb{L}}X\rVert_{\Gamma\otimes\rho}^{2}\leq\mathscr{D}_{X}

(72)

with

\displaystyle\mathscr{D}_{X}^{k}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\frac{1}{2}\Big[\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\nolimits\big(X^{2}\mathcal{D}_{k}(\rho)\big)-\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\nolimits\big(X\mathcal{D}_{k}(\{X,\rho\})\big)\Big]

(73)

\displaystyle\mathscr{D}_{X}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\sum_{k\in K}\mathscr{D}_{X}^{k}

(74)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\Big[\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\nolimits\big(X^{2}\mathcal{D}(\rho)\big)-\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\nolimits\big(X\mathcal{D}(\{X,\rho\})\big)\Big].

(75)

As discussed later,
\mathscr{D}_{X}
can be regarded as a measure of fluctuation of observable
X
; so we call
\mathscr{D}_{X}
the
quantum diffusivity
of
X
.
Technically, because of the separable structure, we have
\lVert\nabla_{\mathbb{L}}X\rVert_{\Gamma\otimes\rho}^{2}=\sum_{k\in K}\lVert\nabla_{\mathbb{L}_{k}}X\rVert_{\Gamma_{k}\otimes\rho}^{2}
; thus, the second inequality in Eq. (
72
) follows from the first one.
Yet, since the proof is complicated, we skip it to Appendix
C
.
Instead, we prove here that
\mathscr{D}_{X}
coincides with the classical definition of diffusivity when
X
commutes with
\rho
.

When
[X,\rho]=0
, they are diagonalized simultaneously as
\rho=\sum_{i}p_{i}|i\rangle\langle i|
and
X=\sum_{i}x_{i}|i\rangle\langle i|
.
Then, we can show the formula

\displaystyle\mathscr{D}_{X}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j,k\in K_{\mathrm{all}}}(x_{i}-x_{j})^{2}R_{ij}^{k}p_{j}

(76)

(recall
R_{ij}^{k}=\gamma_{k}|\langle i|L_{k}|j\rangle|^{2}
).
The meaning of this quantity becomes clear if we consider a classical variable
\hat{x}
that takes value
x_{i}
at probability
p_{i}
whose dynamics is described as an MJP with rates
\{R_{ij}^{k}\}
.
Then, we obtain the relation

\displaystyle\lim_{dt\to 0}\frac{\mathrm{Var}(\Delta\hat{x})}{2dt}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j,k\in K_{\mathrm{all}}}(x_{i}-x_{j})^{2}R_{ij}^{k}p_{j},

(77)

where
\mathrm{Var}
indicates the variance and
\Delta\hat{x}=\hat{x}(t+dt)-\hat{x}(t)
.
Equation (
77
) is proved as follows: in an infinitesimal time interval
dt
, change in state
j\to i(\neq j)
occurs at probability
R_{ij}p_{j}dt
(
R_{ij}=\sum_{k}R_{ij}^{k}
), so
\hat{x}
’s change
\Delta\hat{x}
has moments
\sum_{i,j}(x_{i}-x_{j})^{n}R_{ij}p_{j}dt
. The equation immediately follows by considering the first and second moments.
To show Eq. (
76
), we first note that for any
A=\sum_{i}a_{i}|i\rangle\langle i|
,

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\nolimits\big(|i\rangle\langle i|\mathcal{D}(A)\big)=\sum_{j,k\in K_{\mathrm{all}}}(R_{ij}^{k}a_{j}-R_{ji}^{k}a_{i}).

(78)

Applying this equality to each term in Eq. (
75
) leads to

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\nolimits(X^{2}\mathcal{D}(\rho))

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j,k\in K_{\mathrm{all}}}x_{i}^{2}(R_{ij}^{k}p_{j}-R_{ji}^{k}p_{i}),

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\nolimits(X\mathcal{D}(\{X,\rho\}))

\displaystyle=2\sum_{i,j,k\in K_{\mathrm{all}}}x_{i}(R_{ij}^{k}p_{j}x_{j}-R_{ji}^{k}p_{i}x_{i}).

Therefore, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathscr{D}_{X}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j,k\in K_{\mathrm{all}}}R_{ij}^{k}p_{j}(x_{i}^{2}-x_{j}^{2}-2x_{i}x_{j}+2x_{j}^{2})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j,k\in K_{\mathrm{all}}}(x_{i}-x_{j})^{2}R_{ij}^{k}p_{j}.

(79)

We have seen that
\mathscr{D}_{X}
coincides with the classical diffusivity when
X
commutes with
\rho
.
In addition,
\mathscr{D}_{X}
is defined for any observable
X
and always nonnegative, which is evident if we admit Eq. (
72
).
Thus, we can regard
\mathscr{D}_{X}
as a quantum extension of diffusivity.
This should be important because the quantum extension of the variance of change (i.e., the mean square displacement) is not obvious as
X
at time
t
and
t+dt
do not necessarily commute.
We discuss analytical results on the quantum diffusivity with the damped harmonic oscillator in Sec.
V.3
.

Finally, we remark that with the adjoint super-operators
\mathcal{D}_{k}^{*}
and
\mathcal{D}^{*}
, the quantum diffusivity is rewritten as

\displaystyle\mathscr{D}_{X}^{k}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\nolimits\Big[\rho\big(\mathcal{D}_{k}^{*}(X^{2})-\{\mathcal{D}_{k}^{*}(X),X\}\big)\Big],

(80)

\displaystyle\mathscr{D}_{X}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\nolimits\Big[\rho\big(\mathcal{D}^{*}(X^{2})-\{\mathcal{D}^{*}(X),X\}\big)\Big].

(81)

Here, the adjoint super-operators are given as

\begin{split}\mathcal{D}_{k}^{*}(X)&=\gamma_{k}\left(L_{k}^{\dagger}XL_{k}-\frac{1}{2}\{L_{k}^{\dagger}L_{k},X\}\right)\\
&\phantom{=}+\gamma_{-k}\left(L_{k}XL_{k}^{\dagger}-\frac{1}{2}\{L_{k}L_{k}^{\dagger},X\}\right)\end{split}

(82)

and
\mathcal{D}^{*}=\sum_{k\in K}\mathcal{D}_{k}^{*}
.

IV.2.2
Thermodynamic uncertainty relation

Defining the quantum diffusivity, we obtain the inequalities

\displaystyle\dot{\Sigma}_{k}(\rho)\frac{\mathscr{D}_{X}^{k}}{|\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\nolimits(X\mathcal{D}_{k}(\rho))|^{2}}

\displaystyle\geq 1,

(83)

\displaystyle\dot{\Sigma}^{\mathrm{ex}}(\rho)\frac{\mathscr{D}_{X}}{|\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\nolimits(X\mathcal{D}(\rho))|^{2}}

\displaystyle\geq 1,

(84)

which can be seen as a quantum generalization of the thermodynamic uncertainty relation (TUR)
[
9
,
45
]
;
the inequalities show universal trade-off relations between dissipation and the short-time fluctuation relative to the changing rate.
As shown soon later, these inequalities are generalizations of known quantum trade-off relations
[
18
,
51
]
.

The first inequality further leads to

\displaystyle\dot{\Sigma}(\rho)\mathscr{D}_{X}\geq\left(\sum_{k\in K}\big|\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\nolimits(X\mathcal{D}_{k}(\rho))\big|\right)^{2}.

(85)

From the triangle inequality, we see that
\sum_{k\in K}\big|\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\nolimits(X\mathcal{D}_{k}(\rho))|
is larger than
|\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\nolimits(X\mathcal{D}(\rho))|
, which appears in Eq. (
84
);
thus, if
\dot{\Sigma}(\rho)=\dot{\Sigma}^{\mathrm{ex}}(\rho)
, hence the system is conservative, the inequality in Eq. (
85
) is stronger than that of Eq. (
84
), where
\dot{\Sigma}^{\mathrm{ex}}(\rho)
reads
\dot{\Sigma}(\rho)
.
If the system is not conservative (
\dot{\Sigma}^{\mathrm{hk}}(\rho)\neq 0
), there is no such strict order since
\dot{\Sigma}^{\mathrm{ex}}(\rho)
is smaller than
\dot{\Sigma}(\rho)
.
Equation (
85
) is shown as

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{k\in K}\big|\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\nolimits(X\mathcal{D}_{k}(\rho))\big|\right)^{2}\leq\left(\sum_{k\in K}\sqrt{\dot{\Sigma}_{k}(\rho)\mathscr{D}_{X}^{k}}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k\in K}\dot{\Sigma}_{k}(\rho)\times\sum_{k\in K}\mathscr{D}_{X}^{k}=\dot{\Sigma}(\rho)\mathscr{D}_{X},

where we used the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality in the second line.

Let us see that the TURs generalize previously known results:
First, we show Eq. (
85
) leads to a key inequality in Ref.
[
51
]
. Take
X=H
and assume
\beta_{k}=\beta
for all
k
. Then,

\displaystyle Q_{k}(\rho)\coloneqq|\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\nolimits(H\mathcal{D}_{k}(\rho))|

(86)

provides the magnitude of the heat flow between the system and bath
k
.
Using the formula (
81
) and the commutation relation
[L_{k},H]=\omega_{k}L_{k}
, we can derive the relation

\displaystyle\mathscr{D}_{H}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k\in K_{\mathrm{all}}}\gamma_{k}\omega_{k}^{2}\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\nolimits(L_{k}^{\dagger}L_{k}\rho)

(87)

(for details, see Appendix
D
).
By equation (
85
), we obtain the inequality

\displaystyle\frac{Q_{\mathrm{tot}}(\rho)^{2}}{\dot{\Sigma}(\rho)}\leq\mathscr{D}_{H},

(88)

where
Q_{\mathrm{tot}}(\rho)=\sum_{k\in K}Q_{k}(\rho)
.
This is the central inequality in the proof of the trade-off relation provided as Eq. (4) in Ref.
[
51
]
.
In Sec.
V.2
, we further demonstrate that the bound can be tightened (if we regard Eq. (
88
) as a lower bound on the EPR) by changing the observable we focus on from the Hamiltonian.

Second, we discuss that Eq. (
84
) tightens the main inequality provided in Ref.
[
18
]
.
Let
\rho
be eigendecomposed as
\sum_{i}p_{i}|i\rangle\langle i|
. We define
X
by
X=\sum_{i}x_{i}|i\rangle\langle i|
with

\displaystyle x_{i}\coloneqq\frac{1}{2}\frac{dp_{i}/dt}{|dp_{i}/dt|}

(89)

if
|dp_{i}/dt|\neq 0
; otherwise, we set
x_{i}=0
.
Then, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\nolimits(X\mathcal{D}(\rho))

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i}\left|\frac{dp_{i}}{dt}\right|=\lVert\mathcal{D}(\rho)\rVert_{\mathrm{tr}},

(90)

\displaystyle\mathscr{D}_{X}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j(\neq i),k\in K_{\mathrm{all}}}R_{ij}^{k}p_{j}=:A(\rho),

(91)

where
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{\mathrm{tr}}
indicates the trace distance and
A(\rho)
is the so-called dynamical activity
[
73
]
.
The inequality in the second line comes from
(x_{i}-x_{j})^{2}\leq(|x_{i}|+|x_{j}|)^{2}\leq 1
.
Therefore, equation (
84
) leads to

\displaystyle\lVert\mathcal{D}(\rho)\rVert_{\mathrm{tr}}\leq\sqrt{\frac{1}{2}\dot{\Sigma}^{\mathrm{ex}}(\rho)A(\rho)},

(92)

which generalizes a part of the main result presented as Eq. (39) in Ref.
[
18
]
.
In the original result,
\dot{\Sigma}(\rho)
is used instead of
\dot{\Sigma}^{\mathrm{ex}}(\rho)
, so the bound is tightened by using the excess EPR.

IV.2.3
Time-dissipation uncertainty relation

Another insight can be extracted from the inequalities if we define time
\tau_{X}
by

\displaystyle\tau_{X}\coloneqq\frac{2\mathscr{D}_{X}}{|\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\nolimits(X\mathcal{D}(\rho))|^{2}}.

(93)

If
X
is time-independent and commutes with
H
or
\rho
, then
\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\nolimits(X\mathcal{D}(\rho))=d\langle X\rangle/dt
and the expectation value of
X
approximately changes
\tau_{X}\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\nolimits(X\mathcal{D}(\rho))
in the interval
\tau_{X}
.
On the other hand, the “standard deviation” of the change would approximately be given by
\sqrt{2\mathscr{D}_{X}\tau_{X}}
once we admit
\mathscr{D}_{X}
is a quantum extension of diffusivity. By definition of
\tau_{X}
, we have

\displaystyle\tau_{X}\big|\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\nolimits(X\mathcal{D}(\rho))\big|=\sqrt{2\mathscr{D}_{X}\tau_{X}}.

(94)

Therefore,
\tau_{X}
provides a typical time scale for
\langle X\rangle
to change as much as its fluctuation.

A similar notion was introduced in Ref.
[
53
]
as

\displaystyle\tilde{\tau}_{X}\coloneqq\frac{\sigma_{X}}{|\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\nolimits(X[H,\rho])|}

(95)

to obtain the time-energy uncertainty relation (also known as the quantum speed limit (QSL)
[
74
]
)

\displaystyle\tilde{\tau}_{X}\cdot\sigma_{H}\geq\frac{1}{2},

(96)

where
\sigma_{X}
and
\sigma_{H}
indicate the standard deviation,
\sigma_{X}=\sqrt{\langle X^{2}\rangle-\langle X\rangle^{2}}
and
\sigma_{H}=\sqrt{\langle H^{2}\rangle-\langle H\rangle^{2}}
.
If there are no dissipators, the denominator
|\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\nolimits(X[H,\rho])|
becomes
|d\langle X\rangle/dt|
and
\tilde{\tau}_{X}
provides the time it takes
\langle X\rangle
to change as much as the standard deviation, similarly to
\tau_{X}
.

As we have the time-energy uncertainty relation for
\tilde{\tau}_{X}
, we can get an inequality for
\tau_{X}
,

\displaystyle\tau_{X}\cdot\dot{\Sigma}^{\mathrm{ex}}(\rho)\geq 2,

(97)

which is derived by combining the definition of
\tau_{X}
and the inequality in Eq. (
84
).
Since this inequality holds for any
X\in\mathrm{herm}(\mathcal{H})
, we can further obtain the symbolic form

\displaystyle\tau_{*}\cdot\dot{\Sigma}^{\mathrm{ex}}(\rho)\geq 2

(98)

by defining the minimum time
\tau_{*}\coloneq\min_{X\in\mathrm{herm}(\mathcal{H})}\tau_{X}
.
Those inequalities represent the universal trade-off relation between the typical time of the observable or the system itself and the excess component of the dissipation; namely, it shows the fact that changing faster requires more dissipation.
It is reasonable that the excess component appears because it is related to the minimum dissipation, as discussed in Sec.
IV.1
.
We may regard the inequalities as a variety of the time-dissipation uncertainty relation
[
52
]
.

While time-energy uncertainty relation (QSL) (
96
) focuses on the unitary term in the dynamics (cf. Eq. (
68
)), we have derived another uncertainty relation (QSL) (
97
) by focusing on the dissipative contribution.
Yet, it should be noted that neither of the denominators in the definitions of
\tau_{X}
and
\tilde{\tau}_{X}
unconditionally provide the time derivative. Thus, interpreting them as the time required to overcome the fluctuation is not always valid.
Nonetheless, they can complement each other since the interpretations are exclusively reasonable;
d\langle X\rangle/dt\simeq\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\nolimits(X\mathcal{D}(\rho))
holds if
-i\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\nolimits(X[H,\rho])
is negligible, and vice versa.

Examples

V.1
Two-level system attached to two heat baths

Figure 3:
Summary of the results in the first example. (a) We simulate the dissipative dynamics of a two-level system coupled to two heat baths at different temperatures. (b) We begin with a pure state
(|g\rangle+|e\rangle)/\sqrt{2}
added a small noise to avoid divergence of
\ln\rho
. The coherence
\rho_{ge}
vanished while the classical population converges to the stationary distribution. We do the simulation with parameters
\omega=1
,
\beta_{h}=0.58
,
\beta_{\ell}=1
, and
\gamma_{+}=\gamma_{-}=0.1
. (c) We plot the EPRs. The excess and housekeeping EPRs are computed by solving Eq. (
64
).

As the system relaxes, the total and housekeeping EPRs converge to a single nonzero value, and the excess EPR becomes zero. The approximation formula reproduces the excess EPR well with a small error (shown in the inset).

First, let us illustrate the general framework and the decomposition through a minimal model of the nonequilibrium quantum system.
Consider a two-level system that is equipped with Hamiltonian
H=\omega|e\rangle\langle e|
and attached to two heat baths at inverse temperatures
\beta_{h}
and
\beta_{\ell}
(
\beta_{h}<\beta_{\ell}
).
The two ways of dissipation induced by the baths are represented by jump operators
L_{h}=L_{\ell}=|g\rangle\langle e|
.
The rates obey the local detailed balance

\displaystyle\ln\frac{\gamma_{h}}{\gamma_{-h}}=\beta_{h}\omega,\quad\ln\frac{\gamma_{\ell}}{\gamma_{-\ell}}=\beta_{\ell}\omega.

(99)

The system possesses the steady state

\displaystyle\rho^{\mathrm{ss}}=\frac{\Gamma_{+}}{\Gamma_{+}+\Gamma_{-}}|g\rangle\langle g|+\frac{\Gamma_{-}}{\Gamma_{+}+\Gamma_{-}}|e\rangle\langle e|

(100)

where
\Gamma_{\pm}=\gamma_{\pm h}+\gamma_{\pm\ell}
.
The situation is summarized in Fig.
3
(a).

Now, the components of the force and current operators, defined in Eqs. (
11
) and (
12
), read

\displaystyle J_{k}

\displaystyle=\frac{\gamma_{k}\rho_{ee}-\gamma_{-k}\rho_{gg}}{2}L_{k}+\frac{\rho_{eg}}{2}(\gamma_{k}|g\rangle\langle g|-\gamma_{-k}|e\rangle\langle e|),

(101)

\displaystyle F_{k}

\displaystyle=\beta_{k}\omega L_{k}+\big[L_{k},\ln\rho\big].

(102)

for
k=h,\ell
. Here,
\rho_{ij}
denotes the matrix elements of
\rho
in basis
\{|g\rangle,|e\rangle\}
.
We see
J_{k}
has a classical component proportional to
\gamma_{k}\rho_{ee}-\gamma_{-k}\rho_{gg}
and a non-classical part depending on
\rho_{eg}
.
Since
\beta_{k}
depends on
k
,
F_{k}
cannot be expressed as
[\phi,L_{k}]
(thus, the system is non-conservative due to the temperature gap).

For this simple case, we can analytically obtain the potential operator
\phi\in\mathrm{herm}(\mathcal{H})
such that
\mathbb{F}_{\mathrm{c}}(\rho)=-\nabla_{\mathbb{L}}\phi
.
Because
\nabla_{\mathbb{L}}
is insensible to the identity operator (
\nabla_{\mathbb{L}}I_{\mathcal{H}}=0
), we can assume the form

\displaystyle\phi=\begin{pmatrix}0&\psi\\
\psi^{*}&\Delta\end{pmatrix},

(103)

where
\Delta
is a real number corresponding to the potential difference between energy eigenstates, while
\psi
are non-classical terms.
As discussed in Sec.
IV.1.5
, such a
\phi
is obtained by solving Eq. (
64
).
The excess and housekeeping EPRs can be computed by using the potential as
\dot{\Sigma}^{\mathrm{hk}}=\lVert\mathbb{F}(\rho)-(-\nabla_{\mathbb{L}}\phi)\rVert_{\rho}^{2}
and
\dot{\Sigma}^{\mathrm{ex}}=\lVert-\nabla_{\mathbb{L}}\phi\rVert_{\rho}^{2}
.
For the present model, moreover, it is possible to solve the equation analytically.
However, the exact form of the solution, given in Appendix
E
, is so complicated that it is little tractable.
Instead, here we consider some extreme cases.

First, when the system has no coherence,
\rho_{ge}=\rho_{eg}=0
, we recover the classical result provided in Ref.
[
31
]
(the sign is flipped since the “positive” direction of the jump is opposite),

\displaystyle\Delta=\Delta_{\mathrm{cl}}(\rho)\coloneqq\frac{J_{\mathrm{cl}}(\rho)}{L_{\mathrm{cl}}(\rho)},\quad\psi=0,

(104)

where

\displaystyle J_{\mathrm{cl}}(\rho)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=h,\ell}(\gamma_{k}\rho_{ee}-\gamma_{-k}\rho_{gg}),

(105)

\displaystyle L_{\mathrm{cl}}(\rho)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=h,\ell}\frac{\gamma_{k}\rho_{ee}-\gamma_{-k}\rho_{gg}}{\ln(\gamma_{k}\rho_{ee}/(\gamma_{-k}\rho_{gg}))}.

(106)

In addition, if
\beta_{h}=\beta_{\ell}=\beta
, the potential difference will be
f_{ee}-f_{gg}
with
f=\ln\rho-\ln\rho^{\mathrm{ss}}
; this is because the isothermality leads to

\displaystyle\ln\frac{\gamma_{k}\rho_{ee}}{\gamma_{-k}\rho_{gg}}=\beta\omega+\ln\frac{\rho_{ee}}{\rho_{gg}}

and
\rho^{\mathrm{ss}}_{gg}/\rho^{\mathrm{ss}}_{ee}=\Gamma_{+}/\Gamma_{-}=e^{\beta\omega}
. Thus, we get

\displaystyle\frac{J_{\mathrm{cl}}(\rho)}{L_{\mathrm{cl}}(\rho)}=\beta\omega+\ln\frac{\rho_{ee}}{\rho_{gg}}=\ln\frac{\rho_{ee}}{\rho_{ee}^{\mathrm{ss}}}-\ln\frac{\rho_{gg}}{\rho_{gg}^{\mathrm{ss}}}.

(107)

Second, when
\rho_{ge}
is small and
\rho
is close to steady state
\rho^{\mathrm{ss}}
, we obtain the approximation formulas

\displaystyle\tilde{\Delta}

\displaystyle=\Delta_{\mathrm{cl}}(\rho)+\frac{2A_{\mathrm{cl}}(u)}{\delta\rho}\left(\frac{1}{L_{\mathrm{cl}}(u)}-\frac{1}{L_{\mathrm{cl}}(\rho)}\right)|\rho_{ge}|^{2},

(108)

\displaystyle\tilde{\psi}

\displaystyle=\frac{A_{\mathrm{cl}}(u)}{L_{\mathrm{cl}}(u)}\rho_{ge},

(109)

where
u=I_{\mathcal{H}}/2
is the maximally mixed state,
\delta\rho=\rho_{gg}-\rho_{ee}
is the population difference, and
A_{\mathrm{cl}}(\rho)=\sum_{k=h,\ell}(\gamma_{k}\rho_{ee}+\gamma_{-k}\rho_{gg})
is the classical dynamical activity.
With the approximated potential
\tilde{\phi}
given by
\tilde{\Delta}
and
\tilde{\psi}
, we can compute the approximated value
\dot{\Sigma}^{\mathrm{ex,app}}(\rho)
by

\displaystyle\dot{\Sigma}^{\mathrm{ex,app}}(\rho)=\lVert-\nabla_{\mathbb{L}}\tilde{\phi}\rVert_{\Gamma\otimes\rho}^{2}.

(110)

In Fig.
3
(b) and (c), we show numerical results of the two-level system demonstrating the accuracy of the approximation.
The numerical results here and in the next example are obtained with the Python quantum toolbox, QuTiP
[
75
]
.
In the time evolution, the system approaches the nonequilibrium steady state where heat flows from the hot bath to the cold bath.
At the same time, the total EPR approaches the value of the housekeeping EPR, and the decomposition splits the net dissipation into the two contributions.
The housekeeping EPR is almost at a constant value corresponding to the stationary EPR, whereas the excess EPR is vanishing. We can see that the excess EPR is well approximated by
\dot{\Sigma}^{\mathrm{ex,app}}(\rho)
even when
\rho
is not so close to the steady state.

V.2
Relaxation of a superradiant system

Figure 4:
(a) We consider a model of superradiance, where two two-level systems are simultaneously influenced by a heat bath. We simulate the time evolution with parameters
\omega=1
,
\beta=1
,
\gamma_{+}=\Gamma(n_{\mathrm{th}}+1)
and
\gamma_{-}=\Gamma n_{\mathrm{th}}
, where
\Gamma=0.1
and
n_{\mathrm{th}}=1/(e^{\beta\omega}-1)
. (b) We can observe two stages of relaxation; in the earlier region (before
t\approx 3
), the TUR bound given by
\xi=|ge\rangle\langle eg|+|eg\rangle\langle ge|
is tighter than that given by
H
, and the inverse holds in the later regime.

Next, we check how the TUR works by analyzing the model of superradiance used in Ref.
[
51
]
.
As depicted in Fig.
4
(a), the system consists of two two-level systems with Hamiltonian

\displaystyle H=\omega\big(|e\rangle\langle e|\otimes I+I\otimes|e\rangle\langle e|\big),

(111)

which has degeneracy;
|g\rangle|e\rangle
and
|e\rangle|g\rangle
both have the eigenvalue
\omega
.
We also have a single collective dissipation (
|K|=1
) represented by

\displaystyle L_{+}

\displaystyle=|g\rangle\langle e|\otimes I+I\otimes|g\rangle\langle e|=L_{-}^{\dagger}.

(112)

With the system coupled to a single bath at inverse temperature
\beta
, the local detailed balance
\gamma_{+}/\gamma_{-}=\exp(\beta\omega)
is assumed.
Considering this model, the authors of Ref.
[
51
]
exemplified that the coherence between the degenerate states enables the reduction of the dissipation beyond the classical limit.

We note that the steady state is not unique; the system has a conserved quantity in addition to the trace,

\displaystyle Q=\frac{1}{2}\big(|ge\rangle\langle ge|+|eg\rangle\langle eg|-|ge\rangle\langle eg|-|eg\rangle\langle ge|\big),

(113)

where
|ge\rangle=|g\rangle|e\rangle
and
|eg\rangle=|e\rangle|g\rangle
.
Namely, if initially
\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\nolimits(Q\rho_{t=0})=q
, we have
\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\nolimits(Q\rho_{t})=q
for any
t
.
This is because
[H,Q]=0
and
L_{\pm}Q=QL_{\pm}=0
, thus
(d/dt)\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\nolimits(Q\rho_{t})=0
.
As a result, we can expect that the system relaxes to the so-called generalized Gibbs ensemble
[
76
]

\displaystyle\rho_{\beta,\lambda}=\frac{e^{-\beta H-\lambda Q}}{\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\nolimits(e^{-\beta H-\lambda Q})},

(114)

rather than the thermal equilibrium state
e^{-\beta H}/\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\nolimits(e^{-\beta H})
.
Here,
\beta
is the bath inverse temperature, and
\lambda
is a parameter determined by the initial condition.
In Appendix
F
, we show that (i)
\rho_{\beta,\lambda}
is a steady state of the quantum master equation and (ii)
\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\nolimits(Q\rho_{\beta,\lambda})=q
is solved uniquely by

\displaystyle\lambda(\beta,q)=-\ln\left[\frac{q}{1-q}(e^{\beta\omega}+e^{-\beta\omega}+1)\right].

(115)

Although these facts do not directly mean that
\rho_{t}
starting from
\rho_{0}
such that
\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\nolimits(Q\rho_{0})=q
converges to
\rho_{\beta,\lambda(\beta,q)}
,
we obtain numerical evidence of the convergence to
\rho_{\beta,\lambda}
in the Appendix.

In illustrating the TUR, we take an initial state that has significant coherence between
|ge\rangle
and
|eg\rangle
as

\displaystyle\rho_{0}=\frac{e^{-\beta H}}{\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\nolimits(e^{-\beta H})}+\alpha\frac{e^{-\beta\omega}}{\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\nolimits(e^{-\beta H})}\xi

(116)

with
\xi=|ge\rangle\langle eg|+|eg\rangle\langle ge|
.
To keep the positivity of
\rho_{0}
,
\alpha
must satisfy
-1<\alpha<1
.
We choose
\alpha=-0.9
and plot the time course of the EPR and the TUR bounds (
83
) for
X=H
and
X=\xi
in Fig.
4
(b) with parameter values described in the caption.

In Fig.
4
(b), EPR’s time evolution shows that there are two regimes of relaxation.
We can estimate that the earlier stage is the decay of coherence, and the latter is the usual thermalization.
In fact, in the earlier step, the TUR bound for
X=\xi
is much tighter than that for
X=H
, while
H
’s bound gets closer to the EPR in the later step.
Therefore, in the first stage, conflict with the “fluctuation” of
\xi
is more crucial for the EPR, and later, the TUR associated with the energy will be more important.
We expect that our general TUR bounds can generally provide limitations suited to the situation by properly choosing the observable.

V.3
Damped harmonic oscillator

Finally, we examine the quantum diffusivity in the damped harmonic oscillator.
The system is described by Hamiltonian
H=\hbar\omega(a^{\dagger}a+1/2)
, jump operators
L_{+}=a=L_{-}^{\dagger}
and decay rates
\gamma_{+}=\Gamma(n_{\mathrm{th}}+1)
and
\gamma_{-}=\Gamma n_{\mathrm{th}}
, where
a
is the annihilation operator, which satisfies
[a,a^{\dagger}]=1
, and
n_{\mathrm{th}}=1/(e^{\beta\hbar\omega}-1)

[
2
]
.
Here, we recover the reduced Planck constant to clarify the dimension later.
Even though the Hilbert space is not finite-dimensional, our theory can be applied to this system.

We can compute the quantum diffusivity with the formula (
81
), for example.
Let us consider the position operator

\displaystyle Q=\sqrt{\frac{\hbar}{2m\omega}}(a+a^{\dagger}),

(117)

where
m
is the mass of the particle.
Because of the equalities

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}^{*}(Q^{2})

\displaystyle=-\frac{\hbar\Gamma}{2m\omega}(a^{2}+(a^{\dagger})^{2}+2a^{\dagger}a-2n_{\mathrm{th}}),

(118)

\displaystyle\{\mathcal{D}^{*}(Q),Q\}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\hbar\Gamma}{2m\omega}(a+a^{\dagger})^{2},

(119)

the quantum diffusivity is given as

\displaystyle\mathscr{D}_{Q}=\frac{\hbar\Gamma}{m\omega}\Big(n_{\mathrm{th}}+\frac{1}{2}\Big)

(120)

regardless of
\rho
(the derivation of Eqs. (
118
) and (
119
) are given in Appendix
G
).
It is easy to see that
\mathscr{D}_{Q}
has the same dimension of
(\text{Length})^{2}/\text{Time}
as the classical diffusion coefficient.
Moreover,
\mathscr{D}_{Q}
has a direct connection to the equilibrium fluctuation of
Q

\displaystyle(\sigma_{Q}^{\mathrm{eq}})^{2}

\displaystyle=\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\nolimits\big(Q^{2}\rho^{\mathrm{eq}}\big)-\big(\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\nolimits\big(Q\rho^{\mathrm{eq}}\big)\big)^{2}

(121)

\displaystyle=\frac{\hbar}{m\omega}\Big(n_{\mathrm{th}}+\frac{1}{2}\Big).

(122)

Therefore, we obtain the equality

\displaystyle\mathscr{D}_{Q}=\Gamma(\sigma_{Q}^{\mathrm{eq}})^{2}.

(123)

Note that whereas the expression of
(\sigma_{Q}^{\mathrm{eq}})^{2}
is only valid in equilibrium, the quantum diffusivity is always provided in the form of Eq. (
120
).
This indicates that the quantum diffusivity is a more intrinsic measure of fluctuation.
Still, we should be aware that
\mathscr{D}_{X}
generally depends on the state
\rho
, and Eq. (
123
) could be a specific result in this simple model; this is similar to the classical case, where the constant diffusivity is an idealization that is invalid in complex systems.

We note that the quantum diffusivity may be seen as a generalization of the quantum diffusion coefficient investigated in the study of the damped quantum harmonic oscillators from the dynamical point of view
[
77
]
.
Although the detailed comparison is a subject for future research, we emphasize that our diffusivity can be defined for any Hermitian operators in arbitrary open systems if they are described by the quantum master equation, not restricted to the coordinate and momentum of the harmonic oscillator.

VI
Conclusion

We have studied the thermodynamics of the open quantum systems described by the quantum master equation to find the force-current structure, which ubiquitously exists in classical nonequilibrium systems.
The force and current are provided as anti-Hermitian operators, accompanied by a consistent gradient structure characterized by the gradient super-operator.
The developed framework constitutes a comprehensive analogy with the classical counterpart without resorting to any method that bluntly uses the information of eigenbases.

Not only does it have mathematical elegance, but the force-current structure also provides several physical insights.
Its geometric structure lets us decompose the entropy production rate (EPR) into the housekeeping and excess parts.
The geometric decomposition overcomes the problem
of the conventional approach of decomposition, which requires a special steady state.
Moreover, the geometric form of EPRs naturally leads to fundamental restrictions residing in the dynamics, such as the thermodynamic uncertainty relation (TUR) and the time-dissipation trade-off.
Our inequalities generalize some previous results and enable us to discuss trade-offs in a generic manner.

In deriving the TURs, we have found a quantity that we term the quantum diffusivity.
We have discussed that it can be a quantum measure of thermal fluctuation.
Revealing its more detailed theoretical properties should be an important future task; for example, it may be possible to connect the diffusivity to the variance of a short-time displacement.

We would like to end this paper with some more comments on future directions: In this paper, we focused on the Euclidean geometry of forces. On the other hand, in classical systems, we can develop another type of theory that has more to do with optimization and the variational principle by using a non-Euclidean geometry called information geometry
[
32
,
33
]
.
It would be an interesting avenue to consider the connection between thermodynamics and information geometry.

In addition, we expect the thermodynamic uncertainty relations (TURs) (
83
) and (
84
) can be practically useful in examining infinite-dimensional systems.
As discussed in Sec.
V.3
, our theory does not require the finite dimension of the Hilbert space.
However, the EPR is hard to compute in such systems because it is generally challenging to calculate
\ln\rho

[
78
]
.
Although we should carefully confirm whether the bound can be tight or not, the TURs may be utilized to estimate the EPR in infinite-dimensional systems (as is actually done in classical systems
[
50
,
79
]
).

Appendix A
Mass action kinetics

Consider reactions

\displaystyle\sum_{i}\nu_{ei}^{\mathrm{R}}X_{i}\rightleftharpoons\sum_{i}\nu_{ei}^{\mathrm{P}}X_{i}

(124)

labeled by
e
. Here,
\nu_{ei}^{\mathrm{R}}
and
\nu_{ei}^{\mathrm{P}}
are non-negative integers representing the stoichiometry of reactants and products.
Then, the stoichiometric matrix is given by

\displaystyle\nabla_{ei}=\nu_{ei}^{\mathrm{P}}-\nu_{ei}^{\mathrm{R}}.

(125)

Mass action kinetics is defined by

\displaystyle J_{e}^{+}=k_{e}^{+}\prod_{i}x_{i}^{\nu_{ei}^{\mathrm{R}}},\quad J_{e}^{-}=k_{e}^{-}\prod_{i}x_{i}^{\nu_{ei}^{\mathrm{P}}},

(126)

where
k_{e}^{\pm}
are constants.
If it is assumed, the force reads

\displaystyle F_{e}=\ln\frac{k_{e}^{+}}{k_{e}^{-}}-\sum_{i}\nabla_{ei}\ln x_{i}.

(127)

While
F
is not generally written as an image of
\nabla
, if

\displaystyle\ln\frac{k_{e}^{+}}{k_{e}^{-}}=\sum_{i}\nabla_{ei}\psi_{i}

(128)

holds,
F=-\nabla\phi
with
\phi=\ln x-\psi
and
F
vanishes at
\pi
proportional to
e^{\psi}
.

Markov jump processes are an example of this framework.
A state change from
i
to
j
can be regarded as a reaction with
\nu_{le}^{\mathrm{R}}=\delta_{il}

Appendix C
Derivation of Eq. (
72
)

We first prove the equality

\displaystyle\mathscr{D}_{X}^{k}=\langle\nabla_{\mathbb{L}_{k}}X,\mathcal{A}_{\Gamma_{k}\otimes\rho}(\nabla_{\mathbb{L}_{k}}X)\rangle

(137)

for any
X\in\mathrm{herm}(\mathcal{H})
, where
\mathcal{A}_{B}(C)\coloneqq(BC+CB)/2
is the quantum generalization of the multiplication of the arithmetic mean.
Without loss of generality, we assume
|K|=1
and do not write
k
hereafter.
The right-hand side of Eq. (
137
) is transformed as

\displaystyle\langle\nabla_{\mathbb{L}}X,\mathcal{A}_{\Gamma\otimes\rho}(\nabla_{\mathbb{L}}X)\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\nolimits\big((\nabla_{\mathbb{L}}X)^{\dagger}(\Gamma\otimes\rho)(\nabla_{\mathbb{L}}X)\big)

\displaystyle\phantom{=}+\frac{1}{2}\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\nolimits\big((\nabla_{\mathbb{L}}X)^{\dagger}(\nabla_{\mathbb{L}}X)(\Gamma\otimes\rho)\big)

(138)

\displaystyle=\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\nolimits((\nabla_{\mathbb{L}}X)^{\dagger}(\nabla_{\mathbb{L}}X)\Gamma\otimes\rho),

(139)

where we used
(\nabla_{\mathbb{L}}X)^{\dagger}=-\nabla_{\mathbb{L}}X
in the last line.
We also have

\displaystyle(\nabla_{\mathbb{L}}X)^{\dagger}(\nabla_{\mathbb{L}}X)

\displaystyle=-\begin{pmatrix}0&[L^{\dagger},X]\\
[L,X]&0\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}0&[L^{\dagger},X]\\
[L,X]&0\end{pmatrix}

(140)

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}[L^{\dagger},X][X,L]&0\\
0&[X,L][L^{\dagger},X]\end{pmatrix}.

(141)

Thus,

\displaystyle\langle\nabla_{\mathbb{L}}X,\mathcal{A}_{\Gamma\otimes\rho}(\nabla_{\mathbb{L}}X)\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{\gamma_{+}}{2}\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\nolimits_{\mathcal{H}}\big([L^{\dagger},X][X,L]\rho\big)+\frac{\gamma_{-}}{2}\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\nolimits_{\mathcal{H}}\big([X,L][L^{\dagger},X]\rho\big).

(142)

Each term can be expanded as

\displaystyle[L^{\dagger},X][X,L]

\displaystyle=L^{\dagger}X^{2}L-\frac{1}{2}\{L^{\dagger}L,X^{2}\}+\frac{1}{2}\{L^{\dagger}L,X^{2}\}

\displaystyle\phantom{=}-L^{\dagger}XLX-XL^{\dagger}XL+XL^{\dagger}LX

(143)

\displaystyle=L^{\dagger}X^{2}L-\frac{1}{2}\{L^{\dagger}L,X^{2}\}

\displaystyle\phantom{=}-X\Big(-\frac{1}{2}XL^{\dagger}L+L^{\dagger}XL-\frac{1}{2}L^{\dagger}LX\Big)

\displaystyle\phantom{=}-\Big(-\frac{1}{2}L^{\dagger}LX+L^{\dagger}XL-\frac{1}{2}XL^{\dagger}L\Big)X

(144)

\displaystyle=L^{\dagger}X^{2}L-\frac{1}{2}\{L^{\dagger}L,X^{2}\}

\displaystyle\phantom{=}-\Big\{X,L^{\dagger}XL-\frac{1}{2}\{L^{\dagger}L,X\}\Big\}

(145)

and similarly,

\displaystyle[X,L][L^{\dagger},X]

\displaystyle=LX^{2}L^{\dagger}-\frac{1}{2}\{LL^{\dagger},X^{2}\}

\displaystyle\phantom{=}-\Big\{X,LXL^{\dagger}-\frac{1}{2}\{LL^{\dagger},X\}\Big\}.

(146)

Therefore, plugging them into Eq. (
142
), we obtain

\displaystyle\langle\nabla_{\mathbb{L}}X,\mathcal{A}_{\Gamma\otimes\rho}(\nabla_{\mathbb{L}}X)\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\nolimits_{\mathcal{H}}\big((\mathcal{D}^{*}(X^{2})-\{\mathcal{D}^{*}(X),X\})\rho\big),

(147)

which shows Eq. (
137
) even if
|K|>1
.

Next, we show the general inequality for positive Hermitian operator
B
and arbitrary operator
C

\displaystyle\langle C,\mathcal{M}_{B}(C)\rangle\leq\langle C,\mathcal{A}_{B}(C)\rangle.

(148)

Choosing
B=\Gamma_{k}\otimes\rho
and
C=\nabla_{\mathbb{L}_{k}}X
leads to the inequalities in Eq. (
72
).
Equation (
148
) is proved with the eigendecomposition
B=\sum_{i}b_{i}|i\rangle\langle i|
;
then, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{B}(C)=\sum_{i}\frac{b_{i}+b_{j}}{2}\langle i|C|j\rangle|i\rangle\langle j|

(149)

because

\displaystyle\langle i|\frac{BC+CB}{2}|j\rangle=\frac{b_{i}\langle i|C|j\rangle+\langle i|C|j\rangle b_{j}}{2}.

(150)

From the inequality between log mean and arithmetic mean

\displaystyle\frac{x-y}{\ln(x/y)}\leq\frac{x+y}{2},

(151)

Eq. (
148
) follows as

\displaystyle\langle C,\mathcal{M}_{B}(C)\rangle=\sum_{i,j}\frac{b_{i}-b_{j}}{\ln(b_{i}/b_{j})}|\langle i|C|j\rangle|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i,j}\frac{b_{i}+b_{j}}{2}|\langle i|C|j\rangle|^{2}=\langle C,\mathcal{A}_{B}(C)\rangle.

(152)

In case, we leave proof of Eq. (
151
); using the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality, we see

\displaystyle(x-y)^{2}=\Big(\int_{y}^{x}\sqrt{s}\frac{1}{\sqrt{s}}ds\Big)^{2}

(153)

\displaystyle\leq\int_{y}^{x}sds\int_{y}^{x}\frac{1}{s}ds

(154)

\displaystyle=\frac{x^{2}-y^{2}}{2}\ln\Big(\frac{x}{y}\Big).

(155)

We obtain Eq. (
151
) by dividing both sides by
(x-y)\ln(x/y)
, which is always positive because
x-y
and
\ln(x/y)
have the same sign.

Appendix D
Derivation of Eq. (

From the linearity, it is sufficient to show

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\nolimits\Big[\rho\big(\tilde{\mathcal{D}}_{k}(H^{2})-\{\tilde{\mathcal{D}}_{k}(H),H\}\big)\Big]=\gamma_{k}\omega_{k}^{2}\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\nolimits(L_{k}^{\dagger}L_{k}\rho),

(156)

where

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathcal{D}}_{k}(X)=\gamma_{k}\Big(L_{k}^{\dagger}XL_{k}-\frac{1}{2}\{L_{k}^{\dagger}L_{k},X\}\Big).

(157)

We find

\displaystyle L_{k}^{\dagger}HL_{k}-\frac{1}{2}\{L_{k}^{\dagger}L_{k},H\}

\displaystyle=L_{k}^{\dagger}HL_{k}-\frac{1}{2}L_{k}^{\dagger}L_{k}H-\frac{1}{2}HL_{k}^{\dagger}L_{k}

(158)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}L_{k}^{\dagger}[L_{k},H]+\frac{1}{2}[L_{k}^{\dagger},H]L_{k}

(159)

\displaystyle=-\omega_{k}L_{k}^{\dagger}L_{k}

(160)

and

\displaystyle L_{k}^{\dagger}H^{2}L_{k}-\frac{1}{2}\{L_{k}^{\dagger}L_{k},H^{2}\}

\displaystyle=L_{k}^{\dagger}H^{2}L_{k}-\frac{1}{2}L_{k}^{\dagger}L_{k}H^{2}-\frac{1}{2}H^{2}L_{k}^{\dagger}L_{k}

(161)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}L_{k}^{\dagger}[L_{k},H^{2}]+\frac{1}{2}[L_{k}^{\dagger},H^{2}]L_{k}

(162)

\displaystyle=-\frac{\omega_{k}}{2}L_{k}^{\dagger}L_{k}H-\frac{\omega_{k}}{2}L_{k}^{\dagger}HL_{k}

\displaystyle\phantom{=}-\frac{\omega_{k}}{2}L_{k}^{\dagger}HL_{k}-\frac{\omega_{k}}{2}HL_{k}^{\dagger}L_{k}

(163)

\displaystyle=-\frac{\omega_{k}}{2}\{L_{k}^{\dagger}L_{k},H\}-\omega_{k}L_{k}^{\dagger}HL_{k},

(164)

where we used
[L_{k}^{\dagger},H]=-[L_{k},H]^{\dagger}=-\omega_{k}L_{k}^{\dagger}

\displaystyle[L_{k},H^{2}]=[L_{k},H]H+H[L_{k},H]=\omega_{k}\{L_{k},H\}.

(165)

Thus, we obtain

\displaystyle\gamma_{k}^{-1}\big(\tilde{\mathcal{D}}_{k}(H^{2})-\{\tilde{\mathcal{D}}_{k}(H),H\}\big)

\displaystyle=-\frac{\omega_{k}}{2}\{L_{k}^{\dagger}L_{k},H\}-\omega_{k}L_{k}^{\dagger}HL_{k}+\omega_{k}\{L_{k}^{\dagger}L_{k},H\}

(166)

\displaystyle=\frac{\omega_{k}}{2}(L_{k}^{\dagger}L_{k}H+HL_{k}^{\dagger}L_{k}-2L_{k}^{\dagger}HL_{k})

(167)

\displaystyle=\frac{\omega_{k}}{2}(L_{k}^{\dagger}[L_{k},H]+[H,L_{k}^{\dagger}]L_{k})

(168)

\displaystyle=\omega_{k}^{2}L_{k}^{\dagger}L_{k},

(169)

which shows Eq. (
156
).
