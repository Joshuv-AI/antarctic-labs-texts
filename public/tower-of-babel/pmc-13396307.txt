Predictive Factors for Non-response to First Use of Polyvalent Intravenous Immunoglobulin in Generalised Myasthenia Gravis
Authors: Matthias Dion, Antoine Gavoille, Valérie Chamouard, Antoine Pegat, Françoise Bouhour
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionMyasthenia gravis (MG) is an autoimmune neurological disorder affecting the neuromuscular junction. Rapid clinical deterioration during a myasthenic crisis (MC) or severe exacerbation may be life-threatening due to respiratory or bulbar involvement. Plasma exchange (PLEX) and intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIg) are two rescue therapies that show comparable efficacy in this setting. However, the characteristics of patients who respond poorly to IVIg remain insufficiently described. The aim of this study was to identify predictive factors of non-response to IVIg administered during a first MC or exacerbation.MethodsThis was a single-centre retrospective cohort study carried out at a French referral centre for neuromuscular diseases between 2017 and 2023. Adult patients with generalised MG experiencing a first MC or exacerbation and treated for the first time with IVIg were included in the study. Data on clinical, laboratory, electrophysiological and therapeutic variables were collected. Treatment response was defined as the maximum gain in Garches clinical score following IVIg, adjusted for baseline score.ResultsA total of 93 were included in the study. The median age of the cohort was 68 (interquartile range [IQR] 49–76) years, and the median delay from diagnosis was 0.7 years. The median baseline Garches score was 66/100 (IQR 55–75) points, which improved to 85/100 (IQR 75–94) points after treatment. Bulbar involvement was significantly associated with a greater response to IVIg.ConclusionsIn this large and heterogeneous cohort, IVIg demonstrated consistent efficacy without identification of significant predictors of non-response. These findings support IVIg as a reliable and evidence-based first-line therapy for patients with MG experiencing exacerbation or MC.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s40120-026-00941-z.Keywords: Myasthenia gravis, Polyvalent intravenous immunoglobulins, Myasthenic crisis, Myasthenic exacerbation, Garches clinical score, Predictive factors

Introduction

Myasthenia gravis (MG) is an autoimmune disorder of the neuromuscular junction targeting postsynaptic components, leading to impaired neuromuscular transmission [1]. The disease is characterised by fluctuating skeletal muscle weakness affecting ocular, bulbar, axial and limb muscles [2, 3]. Clinical manifestations significantly impair daily functioning and may become life-threatening during exacerbations or a myasthenic crisis (MC), often precipitated by triggering factors [3, 4]. MCs occur most frequently within the first 3 years following diagnosis and are defined by rapid clinical deterioration over 2 to 5 days, potentially progressing to respiratory failure or severe bulbar involvement requiring intensive care management. In approximately 60% of MC cases, the patient requires admission to the intensive care unit (ICU) [4]. In this context, rescue therapies, including plasma exchange (PLEX) and intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIg), provide rapid clinical improvement [5]. Both treatments have demonstrated comparable efficacy and are widely used in current practice [6]. IVIg exerts multifactorial mechanisms, including neutralisation of pathogenic autoantibodies, inhibition of complement activation, downregulation of proinflammatory cytokines and chemokines, modulation of dendritic cell function and saturation of neonatal Fc receptors leading to accelerated degradation of pathogenic immunoglobulin G (IgG) [7, 8]. Unlike PLEX, IVIg can be administered via peripheral venous access in conventional care settings, facilitating rapid use in routine clinical practice. IVIg is also administered during severe exacerbations in patients insufficiently controlled with background therapy and in those with contraindications to corticosteroids, or early in the disease course to achieve rapid improvement while awaiting the delayed effects of immunosuppressive therapy. In cases of IVIg failure, rescue PLEX may be required [9].

With the emergence of novel therapeutic agents such as neonatal Fc receptor (FcRn) inhibitors and complement inhibitors, [10–12] and in the context of global IVIg shortages, identifying predictive factors of IVIg non-response has become increasingly important in order to optimise treatment selection. The authors of previous studies have suggested that response to IVIg improves in patients who are acetylcholine receptor antibody-positive (AChR+) [13, 14], in those with greater disease severity at baseline [13–16] and in patients with marked electrophysiological abnormalities [14, 15]. Conversely, patients with muscle-specific kinase antibody-positive (MuSK+) MG has been associated with poorer IVIg response [14, 17, 18].

The aim of the present study was to identify predictive factors of non-response to first-line IVIg in patients with generalised MG experiencing their first MC or exacerbation.

Methods

Study Design and Patients

This retrospective single-centre cohort study was conducted at the Hospices Civils de Lyon Groupement Hospitalier Est (HCL GHE) between February 2017 and July 2023.

We included adults (aged ≥ 18 years) with generalised autoimmune MG who experienced a first MC [4, 19] or exacerbation [20] and received first-line IVIg (1 or 2 g/kg). All patients were naïve to IVIg and PLEX therapy. Patients requiring subsequent PLEX due to IVIg failure were retained in the analysis, as they represented a refractory population. Patients presenting pure ocular MG were excluded.

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and applicable national regulations on data protection. The study protocol was reviewed by the institutional ethics committee (Comité d’éthique, HCL number: 23-5460), which issued a favorable opinion. Patients were informed of the use of their anonymised data and did not express any opposition to the use of their data for research purposes.

Data Collection

Clinical, laboratory, electrophysiological and therapeutic data were retrospectively extracted from medical records. Laboratory parameters included C-reactive protein (CRP), neutrophil and lymphocyte counts, serum IgG levels at admission, antibody status (acetylcholine receptor [AChR], MuSK or double seronegative) and corresponding antibody titres. Electrophysiological data obtained from low-frequency repetitive nerve stimulation (LF-RNS) included the presence of a significant decrement (> 10%), the maximal decrement amplitude and the mean amplitude of pathological decrements. Thymectomy status, thymic histopathology and the interval between thymectomy and the exacerbation or MC were recorded. Data regarding prior and ongoing treatments, including corticosteroids, other immunosuppressive agents, and symptomatic therapies, were also collected.

Clinical status was retrospectively assessed at admission (day 0), discharge and at the 1-month follow-up visit. Muscle involvement was categorised as ocular, bulbar, axial, upper limb, or lower limb. The need for non-invasive or invasive ventilation (NIV/IV) and for nasogastric tube (NGT) placement was also documented.

Clinical severity and treatment response were evaluated using the Garches clinical score, a scale that is routinely employed in France for MG follow-up. This scale assesses muscle weakness in ocular (20 points), bulbar (30 points), axial (20 points), upper limb (15 points) and lower limb (15 points) domains, yielding a total score ranging from 0 (maximal weakness) to 100 (no weakness) [21].

The Myasthenia Gravis Foundation of America (MGFA) classification was also retrospectively determined for all patients, ranging from class I (isolated ocular weakness) to class V (requirement for mechanical ventilation).

Treatment response was defined as the maximal gain in Garches score following IVIg, calculated as the highest post-treatment score (at discharge or 1 month follow-up) minus the baseline score at admission. A clinically significant improvement was defined as an increase of at least 10 points in the Garches score. In patients who required add-on PLEX, response was evaluated relative to the Garches score immediately prior to PLEX initiation.

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables were expressed as the median and interquartile range (IQR), and categorical variables as counts and percentage (%). Associations between all variables and IVIg response were assessed using linear regression models of Garches score gain, adjusted for baseline score. p-values < 0.05 were considered to be statistically significant. Analyses were performed using R software, version 4.0.3 ® Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Results

Study Population

A total of 93 patients (55.9% male, 44.1% female; median age 68 [range 19–91] years) were included in the study. Median time from diagnosis to first IVIg was 0.7 (IQR 0.25–2.30) years. Of the 93 patients, 78 (83.9%) were anti-AchR+, seven (7.5%) were anti-MuSK+ and eight (8.6%) were double seronegative. The median Garches score at admission was 66.0 out of 100 (IQR 55.0–75.0), and 74.2% presented a MGFA score ≥ III.

Triggering factors were identified in 23 patients (24.7%), with the most prevalent being infectious diseases (7 patients, 7.5%) and use of contraindicated medications (6 patients, 6.5%). A minority of patients received a background treatment: 20 (21.5%) patients received corticosteroids, and 12 (12.9%) patients received other immunosuppressants. Prior thymectomy was identified in seven (7.5%) patients, with median time between surgery and first IVIg of 22 (IQR 5.0–184.0) months (Table 1).

Response to IVIg Treatment

Median Garches score after treatment was 85.0 out of 100 (IQR 75.0–99.0) points, corresponding to a median gain of 15.0 (IQR 8.0–30.0) points. Clinical improvement occurred in 87.1% of patients, stability in 6.5% and worsening in 6.4% (Fig. 1).

Median Garches score was 66.0 (IQR 55.0–75.0) points at day 0 and 85.0 (IQR 75.0–95.0) points after IVIg initiation. Garches score improved in 81 (87.1%) patients, was stable in six (6.5%) patients and worsened in six (6.4%) patients. Median Garches gain was 15.0 (range − 6.0 to 74.0) points

A bulbar involvement was associated with a statistically significant greater response to IVIg, with mean difference of + 8.47 Garches score points (95% confidence interval [CI] 2.65–14.29, p = 0.005). No other clinical parameters influenced the response to IVIg.

Non-significant trends toward poorer response were observed in patients with an history of immunosuppressive treatment (mean gain of − 6.86 points, 95% CI − 13.92 to 0.20 points, p = 0.057), with prior thymectomy (mean gain of − 7.46 points, 95% CI − 16.35 to 1.43,, p = 0.099), with infectious trigger (mean gain of − 8.15 points, 95% CI − 17.02 to 0.71, p = 0.071) and requirement for NGT (mean gain of − 9.05 points, 95% CI − 19.74 to 1.64, p = 0.096).

Antibody status, IVIg dose, electrophysiological parameters, laboratory findings and associated thymic pathology did not significantly influence treatment response (see Electronic Supplementary Material [ESM] Appendix A; ESM Table 1).

Patients Requiring Add-On PLEX

Five patients required rescue PLEX after IVIg failure. All subsequently improved. This sub-population was exclusively composed of patients with AchR+ with recent disease onset (median time between MG onset and first IVIg of 1.0 [IQR 0.5–2.0] month compared to 4.0 [IQR 1.00–17.5] months in patients without PLEX). These patients had no prior immunosuppressive therapy and presented with greater baseline severity, with a median initial Garches score of 59.0 out of 100 (IQR 59.0–61.0) points compared to 68.0 out of 100 (IQR 55.0–77.2) points in patients without PLEX; the MGFA score was ≥ 3 for all five patients. The add-on PLEX population needed more NGT (2 out 5 patients; 40%) compared to the rest of the cohort (3 out of 88 patients; 3.4%). No consistent distinguishing predictive factor was identified in this sub-population (see ESM Appendix A; ESM Table 2).

Discussion

This study investigated predictive factors associated with response to first-line IVIg in a large cohort of patients with generalised MG presenting with a first exacerbation or MC. Identifying predictors of non-response could help clinicians consider alternative first-line strategies, such as plasma exchange, FcRn inhibitors or complement inhibitors, in selected patients [22, 23].

In this cohort, no independent predictor of IVIg non-response was identified. Only bulbar involvement was significantly associated with a greater response to IVIg. Several factors showed a non-significant trend toward poorer response, including prior use of immunosuppressive therapy (excluding corticosteroids), infectious trigger, previous thymectomy and need for NGT. Overall, IVIg was associated with a favourable clinical response in a population with heterogeneous disease characteristics.

The cohort accurately reflects the epidemiology of generalised MG, with approximately 80% patients with AchR+ and 7% patients with MuSK+ [24]. The median delay of 8 months between diagnosis and first IVIg suggests relatively recent disease onset, consistent with the low proportion of patients receiving background immunosuppressive therapy at the time of exacerbation or MC. Clinical severity was moderate overall: half of the patients were classified as MGFA III, bulbar involvement was present in 75% of patients and only one patient required mechanical ventilation, suggesting that most cases corresponded to exacerbations rather than full MC.

The observed trend toward poorer IVIg response in patients previously treated with non-steroidal immunosuppressants may reflect a subgroup with more refractory disease. This trend was not observed in patients treated with corticosteroids alone, which are widely recommended as first-line therapy and usually prescribed in standard rather than refractory cases.

A similar trend was observed in patients with prior thymectomy. Although thymectomy is known to reduce disease activity over time, the number of such patients in our cohort was small, and the delay between surgery and IVIg administration does not support a perioperative trigger effect. In current practice, IVIg is often administered before thymectomy in uncontrolled MG to prevent postoperative exacerbation [25], which may explain the limited number of patients who have been thymectomised receiving first-line IVIg in our study.

Exacerbations triggered by infection also showed a tendency toward poorer response. Infections, particularly respiratory infections, are well-recognised triggers of MC; however, their impact on IVIg responsiveness has not previously been reported. Although PLEX may represent an alternative in severe cases, its use can be challenging in patients with systemic infection, haemodynamic instability and vascular access concerns and in those limited by access to ICUs.

Patients requiring NGT also showed a trend toward poorer response, likely reflecting greater baseline severity, as these patients were frequently hospitalised in ICUs and sometimes required rescue PLEX following IVIg failure. Nevertheless, greater baseline clinical severity and very early disease onset (< 3 months) were not significantly associated with IVIg non-response in our supplementary analysis.

Interestingly, disease serotype (AchR+ , MuSK+ or double-seronegative) did not influence IVIg response. This contrasts with previous reports suggesting lower IVIg efficacy in MuSK+ MG, considered to be related to the predominance of IgG4 antibodies, which do not activate complement—one of the proposed mechanisms of IVIg action. However, the small number of patients with MuSK+ in our cohort limits definitive conclusions.

Electrophysiological parameters did not predict response to IVIg in our cohort. Although more pronounced abnormalities on repetitive nerve stimulation might theoretically reflect higher disease activity and therefore greater potential for improvement, electrophysiological–clinical dissociation is well recognised in MG [26, 27]. The authors of some previous studies have reported that the intensity of single fiber electromyography (SF-EMG) abnormalities was related to a better response to IVIg, [14, 15], but we did not study this possible association in the present study.

Bulbar involvement was the only significant predictor of better response to IVIg. However, this finding should be interpreted cautiously, as bulbar symptoms carry greater weight in the Garches clinical score (accounting for 30% of the total score), potentially amplifying measurable improvement in this domain.

PLEX may serve as a valuable alternative in patients with rapidly progressive MG, particularly when deterioration occurs within a short time frame (< 48 h) and baseline severity is high (MGFA IVb–V). PLEX often achieves a faster clinical response than IVIg, typically within a few days [28], making it especially relevant in these rapidly evolving scenarios. However, using PLEX after IVIg risks removing previously infused immunoglobulins and reducing efficacy. Although its use can be limited by vascular access, general context and ICU availability, early consideration of PLEX in this highly selected subset may provide the most efficient and timely therapeutic intervention for severe, rapidly progressing cases, and would allow the unnecessary use of IVIg to be avoided, in the context of recurrent supply shortages.

This study has a number of limitations, primarily related to its retrospective design and lack of standardised prospective evaluation. Although Garches and MGFA scores were available at baseline, post-treatment and the 1-month follow-up for all patients, missing data and small subgroup sizes may have limited statistical power. An additional limitation of this study relates to the potential confounding effect of concomitant corticosteroid therapy, either newly introduced or escalated following the first exacerbation or MC, and could have contributed to the observed improvement in Garches scores. Due to the retrospective design of the study, precise adjustment for these concomitant therapies was not feasible, and their potential influence on treatment response cannot be excluded. However, neither the presence of corticosteroids at the time of exacerbation (D0) nor the corticosteroid dose at the 1 month follow-up was associated with a significant impact on treatment response.

Conclusion

IVIg appears to be an effective first-line treatment for MG exacerbation and MC across a broad spectrum of clinical and biological profiles. No significant predictor of non-response was identified. While certain features showed a trend toward poorer response, these findings require confirmation in larger, prospective studies. Clinicians may nevertheless exercise caution in patients with immunosuppressive therapy, infectious triggers, prior thymectomy or severe bulbar dysfunction requiring NGT, in whom early consideration of alternative or rescue therapies may be appropriate.
