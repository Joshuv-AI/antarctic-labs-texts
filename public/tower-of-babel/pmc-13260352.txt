Focused ultrasound-induced cavitation in a human brain microphysiological system produces injury signaling biomarkers consistent with blast trauma
Authors: Peter Hsi, Tyler Gerhardson, Jennifer L Walker, Ernest Kim, Olivia T Spagnola, Brett Isenberg, Anthony Quinnert, David L Kaplan, Vishal Tandon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBlast traumatic brain injury (bTBI) is a significant clinical challenge particularly in military populations, yet the biological mechanisms linking cavitation-induced injuries in brain tissue remain poorly understood and effective treatments are limited. Intracranial cavitation may arise from blast wave exposure or other sources of mechanical and acoustic energy, and has been proposed to contribute to brain injury, but the cellular response to cavitation has been difficult to examine in controlled, physiologically relevant experimental systems. To address this limitation, we present a human brain microphysiological system to investigate cavitation-induced injury in a multicellular neural tissue environment. The platform consists of three-dimensional engineered brain tissue constructs containing human neurons, astrocytes, and microglia, and uses focused ultrasound to generate localized cavitation within the tissue. Cavitation injury produces dose- and time-dependent cytotoxic and excitotoxic cellular responses, including the release of clinically relevant neurotrauma biomarkers consistent with patterns reported in experimental and clinical TBI. Protein and functional analyses further reveal disruption of mechanotransduction-associated signaling, cytoskeletal integrity, and network activity. Together, these results demonstrate a scalable human multicellular brain tissue platform for mechanistic studies and evaluation of candidate therapeutics or protective strategies relevant to bTBI and other conditions where intracranial cavitation is implicated.Subject terms: Biological techniques, Cell biology, Engineering, Neurology, Neuroscience

Introduction

Traumatic brain injury (TBI) is a leading cause of morbidity and long-term neurological dysfunction in both civilian and military populations, affecting 50–60 million people each year1. Blast-induced TBI (bTBI) is a subset of TBI that impacts those in close proximity to explosions during combat or training2. Distinct from blunt force and impact induced TBI3–5, bTBI is characterized by exposure to shockwaves emitted from explosions, which impinge on the head and transmit mechanical forces into the brain6. The forces are believed to initiate cellular and tissue-level injury processes that manifest in cognitive impairment, mood changes, and other neurological symptoms that can contribute to conditions such as post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD)7.

Despite awareness around the health impacts of blast exposures8, establishing the link between the physics of blast wave impingement and biological injury is challenging, in part due to limitations such as variability in existing experimental models and designs9,10. While open-field detonations and shock tubes are used to model blast waves, studies often use surrogate head and brain models that may not recapitulate the key cellular signatures of bTBI11. For example, head geometry has been shown to be an important factor in mechanical loading within the brain during blast, but common animal models used in bTBI studies have substantially different geometry and require scaling laws, adding a layer of abstraction to the experimental findings12,13. Additionally, abiotic human head phantoms used to investigate mechanical loading of the brain are unable to provide associated post-injury biological outcomes14. Traditional in vitro models can be designed to add the appropriate level of physiological fidelity but may simplify the loading conditions or lack critical features such as three dimensionality or relevant cell types15. Therefore, human microphysiological tissue models that link injury mechanisms with multicellular biological responses in a physiologically accurate context represent a powerful experimental approach for studying bTBI.

There is substantial evidence that tissue strain due to mechanical loading is the primary mechanism of cell damage in bTBI16. Several mechanisms have been proposed to explain how blast waves generate this strain, including inertial loading, spallation, and pressure-driven implosion17. Among these, cavitation generated within the brain during blast exposure has been highlighted as a potential driver of injury due to its strain-amplifying characteristics17,18. Computational models and phantom studies suggest that cavitation within the brain may result from multiple interacting factors, including the direct transmission of the incident blast wave18, reflections at interfaces between tissues of differing acoustic impedances17, and localized skull flexure that alters intracranial pressure distribution14,19. Cavitation within the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) can significantly increase the strain in the cortex, particularly in the tissue adjacent to the CSF20, and studies using high-speed imaging have captured the formation and collapse of cavitation bubbles in the CSF of a head surrogate21. The collapse of the bubbles can generate liquid jets that disrupt blood-brain-barrier (BBB) tight junctions22, and shock waves23 that permeabilize cell membranes, leading to loss of internal contents and ionic imbalances24. It is proposed that these effects from cavitation can damage the brain tissue and contribute to the onset and progression of bTBI symptoms.

Isolating mechanisms associated with mechanical loading in bTBI enables direct study of how blast-related forces contribute to cellular damage in the brain. Although damage profiles of cavitation on brain tissue have been explored using animal models, tissue surrogates, and computational simulations, there is limited data for cavitation-induced damage in viable human brain tissue models25–28. Human-derived in vitro brain models recapitulate key aspects of the neural microenvironment and provide experimentally accessible platforms for studying injury-induced cellular signaling. Brain organoids, engineered 3D neural tissues, and microfluidic models better capture human-specific neuroanatomical, cellular, and molecular features than traditional rodent or 2D models29. These platforms allow for controlled manipulation of cell type composition and the microenvironment, enabling mechanistic studies of neural development, disease, and injury30. While some of these systems have been adapted to model blunt-TBI mechanical compression or shear injury15, and blast exposure using blast chambers31 or pressure-wave systems32, the specific multicellular responses to cavitation have not yet been investigated. Beyond blast exposure, cavitation-induced brain injury may be relevant to a range of other contexts, including directed energy exposure, space-associated intracranial pressure changes33, and focused ultrasound therapeutics25,34, where understanding the biological response of neural tissue to cavitation is critical. A controlled, human-relevant in vitro platform for studying cavitation-induced neural injury would therefore have broad utility across these applications.

Here, we present an engineered human brain tissue platform that enables investigation of neural cellular injury by applying targeted focused ultrasound (FUS) induced cavitation. The model consists of a triculture of human neurons, astrocytes and microglia cultured in a silk fibroin-collagen scaffold that recapitulates brain-mimetic mechanical properties35, and has previously been used to study TBI in the context of blunt force trauma36, blast overpressure31, and secreted extracellular vesicle biomarkers37. To generate controlled cavitation within the tissues, we developed a FUS transducer designed to emit high-amplitude, short-duration ultrasound pulses capable of producing cavitation through mechanisms well-established in the field of histotripsy38,39. The FUS transducer integrates with a custom-built platform containing an automated positioning system to deliver reproducible and spatially targeted cavitation exposure to the brain model. We evaluated dose- and time-response to cavitation injury, including secreted injury and inflammatory signals, structural and morphological damage, disruption of mechanotransduction and cytoskeletal integrity, neurotrauma biomarker secretion, and alterations in calcium signaling. Collectively, these results establish a novel human multicellular brain microphysiological system for characterizing intercellular signaling responses to cavitation-induced injury, with relevance to bTBI and other conditions involving mechanical or acoustic forces in the brain tissue.

Results

Automated focused ultrasound platform enabled controlled cavitation in engineered human brain tissues

A custom focused ultrasound (FUS) platform was developed to generate and monitor controlled cavitation within 3D human neural tissue constructs (Fig. 1A). The system integrates a FUS transducer (Supplementary Fig. 1A, B) with a motorized three-axis positioning stage, a water bath for acoustic coupling, and a custom culture well plate and holder that allow precise alignment of individual tissue constructs above the transducer aperture. To verify cavitation generation and establish detection thresholds, the FUS transducer was operated both as an acoustic source and as a passive cavitation detector (Fig. 1B)40. Cavitation produced characteristic acoustic emissions, which were monitored by recording the received signal from the same transducer used for excitation. By isolating the receive window to correspond to the round-trip time of sound propagation to the focal region and back, acoustic emissions associated with cavitation could be distinguished from the drive signal. The plotted receive signal (Fig. 1B) represents acoustic emissions from cavitation events captured in passive detection mode, and is therefore lower in magnitude than the drive signal. In parallel, optical visualization of cavitation was performed by centering the transducer’s focus within the field of view of a high-speed camera coupled to a stereomicroscope. As the driving voltage increased incrementally, acoustic signatures of cavitation were detected at 500 V and the camera detected cavitation signals at 700 V. Imaging confirmed a lateral cavitation zone of approximately 500 µm × 500 µm (Fig. 1B), and approximately 1000 µm in the axial direction as visualized in the side-view image of the bubble cloud (Supplementary Fig. 1D).

To enable scalable and reproducible injury delivery across multiple tissue samples, a hybrid positioning workflow was implemented. A manual rail-based system allowed well-to-well translation of the culture plate with magnetic alignment to ensure consistent placement over the transducer. Within each well, the motorized three-axis positioning system moved the FUS transducer to raster-scan the focal region and register the position using alignment features in the plate holder (Supplementary Fig. 2). This approach allowed for spatial distribution of cavitation events within the tissue while maintaining consistent dosing across samples. Targeted cavitation was visualized in water (Supplementary Fig. 1D) and with red blood cell phantoms (Supplementary Fig. 3), which confirmed spatial localization of injury. Following cavitation validation, subsequent cell experiments were set up by positioning the brain tissue constructs (Fig. 1C) containing a triculture of neurons, astrocytes, and microglia in silk-collagen scaffolds within the culture well plate (Fig. 1D).

Cavitation-induced cytotoxicity, excitotoxicity, and pro-inflammatory response

To assess early cytotoxic and excitotoxic responses, LDH and extracellular glutamate were quantified in the culture media at 4, 24, and 72 h post-injury (Fig. 2A, B). Both LDH and glutamate showed cavitation dose-dependent increases, with significant elevations at all time points for the moderate and high dose groups, but no changes at low dose. These results indicated that a moderate-to-high cavitation dose induced sustained cytotoxicity and excitotoxic stress, whereas a lower dose did not produce detectable injury. Inflammatory cytokine IL-6 and IL-8 concentrations were also measured at 4, 24, 72 h post-injury (Fig. 2C, D). In contrast, inflammatory responses were more limited. IL-6 increased at 4 h in the highest dose, and at 24 h in both moderate and high groups compared to sham before decreasing by 72 h. IL-8 did not show significant changes across any groups or timepoints.

Cavitation damaged structural networks and altered early mechanotransduction and cytoskeletal injury signaling

IF staining for TUJ1 (neurons), GFAP (astrocytes), Iba1 (microglia), and DAPI (nuclei) was performed to evaluate cellular morphology across increasing cavitation doses. Representative images at 24 h post injury are shown (Fig. 3A). In sham controls, TUJ1-positive neurons formed continuous, well-organized networks. With increasing cavitation dose, networks appeared progressively disrupted and fragmented, indicating structural neuronal damage. GFAP-positive astrocytes exhibited more punctate and shrunken morphologies under higher cavitation doses, suggesting cytoskeletal breakdown or reactive remodeling. Similarly, Iba1-positive microglia showed decreased signal density and more fragmented appearance with increasing injury dose, which may reflect microglial retraction or loss. To quantify structural damage to neuronal networks following cavitation, TUJ1 IF was acquired across entire scaffolds. Maximum intensity projections were generated, and a binary mask was created to calculate TUJ1-positive coverage as a metric of neuronal network integrity (Fig. 3B). Analysis revealed a significant reduction in TUJ1-positive coverage in injured scaffolds compared to sham. All time points showed a significant reduction in TUJ1 at moderate and high cavitation doses, and low doses decreased TUJ1 at 24 h (Fig. 3C), indicating that FUS cavitation disrupted neuronal network integrity.

To investigate the impact of cavitation injury on mechanotransduction signaling and cytoskeletal structure, Western blot analysis (Fig. 3D) was performed on scaffold lysates at 4, 24, and 72 h post-injury. We hypothesized that cavitation causes direct mechanical damage to cells or disrupts the surrounding extracellular matrix, leading to altered mechanotransduction-associated signaling pathways. We measured focal adhesion kinase (FAK), a central mechanosensitive signaling protein kinase that mediates integrin signaling and links extracellular signals with intracellular survival pathways41. Total FAK significantly decreased in response to high-dose cavitation at all timepoints. Significant decreases in FAK were not observed at the low and moderate doses (Fig. 3E), suggesting insufficient disruption of focal adhesion dynamics at these timepoints. To evaluate cytoskeletal integrity, we analyzed ɑII-spectrin, a major structural protein in neurons that forms networks with actin to maintain cellular function and stability42. Spectrin breakdown products (SBDPs) are formed by cleavage from different proteases and have been used as biomarkers of TBI43, with cleavage by calpain producing a 150 kDa fragment (SBDP150), and cleavage by caspase creating a 120 kDa fragment (SBDP120)44. We observed a significant increase in SBDP150 at moderate and high dose cavitation at 4 and 24 h, indicating acute activation of calpain-mediated spectrin breakdown. After 72 h, there were no significant changes in SBDP150 (Fig. 3F). In contrast, no significant changes in SBDP120 in any treatments or timepoints were observed suggesting minimal activation of caspase-mediated spectrin cleavage under these conditions (Fig. 3G).

Cavitation produced dose and time-dependent secretion of neurotrauma biomarkers

To characterize FUS-induced cavitation injury against other TBI studies, we measured the presence of clinically relevant neurotrauma biomarkers, including S100B, GFAP, UCHL1, NSE, and NF-H (Fig. 4A-E). Unlike the previous assays which quantified up to 72 h post-injury, the neurotrauma biomarkers were tracked out to 1 and 2 weeks enabling us to capture acute and subacute biomarker injury responses. This longer time window is important for translational relevance, as clinical patient studies often monitor biomarker levels for days to weeks post-injury45, making it possible to more directly compare our model with reported data. Four of the biomarkers (S100B, GFAP, UCHL1, and NF-H) showed a stronger dose-dependent response at early time points (4–24 h) and declined by 1-2 weeks, consistent with acute injury signaling. An exception was NSE, which showed more delayed and sustained response at the highest and moderate cavitation dose. In contrast, low-dose cavitation did not produce significant changes in any biomarker at any time point, suggesting a threshold is required to trigger measurable neurotrauma responses. For S100B and NF-H, the highest cavitation dose produced sharp early peaks at 4 h (3.3-fold and 3.7-fold over sham, respectively), followed by a steady decline. Moderate dose conditions generated more gradual increases that peaked at later time points and persisted longer, suggesting variations in S100B and NF-H astrocytic and axonal injury dynamics, respectively in response to cavitation dose. In contrast, GFAP and UCHL1 showed a more consistent temporal pattern across doses both rising gradually and peaking after 4 h. GFAP showed the broadest time window of elevation with increases detectable by 4 h and sustained through 72 h, indicating sustained astrocytic damage and activation. UCHL1 increased more steeply, reaching the highest absolute fold change by 24 h in the highest dose relative to sham (5.9-fold), indicating neuronal damage. When comparing across biomarkers, the magnitude of peak responses varied, with UCHL1 (5.9-fold) having shown the greatest increase, followed by NF-H (3.7-fold), S100B (3.3-fold), GFAP (2.1-fold), and NSE (1.5-fold), respectively, suggesting differences in sensitivity and injury response to cavitation dose.

Cavitation impaired functional calcium signaling

Calcium signaling was measured 24 h after injury to assess functional activity in the 3D brain tissue scaffolds (Fig. 5A, B). The total spike rate per individual scaffold (spikes·min−1·scaffold−1) and the fraction of active sub-ROIs (regions with at least 1 spike) showed a clear downward trend with a significant decrease in the highest cavitation dose-treated samples compared to sham (Fig. 5C, D). When analysis was restricted to active sub-ROIs only, dose-dependent trends were no longer observed for the spike rate, amplitude, or width (Fig. 5E-G). These results indicated that high-dose cavitation primarily reduced overall signaling by decreasing the proportion of active regions, but the characteristics of the measured signaling events were preserved. The diminished signaling likely reflected a combination of reduced cell viability and excitability.

Discussion

Intracranial cavitation generated upon blast wave exposure has been proposed as a driver of neural insult in bTBI18, but characterization of the biological response from viable, human brain tissue to cavitation injury is a missing component of bTBI research. Our platform addresses this gap by leveraging FUS to apply highly configurable cavitation injury to physiologically relevant human 3D brain tissue constructs, which enables studying the injury response of cavitation exposed human derived tissue. Collectively, our findings showed that human brain tissue constructs exposed to cavitation produced evidence of cytotoxic, excitotoxic, and inflammatory stress, network disruption, impaired mechanotransduction signaling and cytoskeletal integrity, increased secretion of neurotrauma biomarkers such as GFAP and UCHL1, and reduced functional signaling, responses that are consistent with indicators reported in TBI and bTBI studies46–52.

A critical question with any surrogate TBI model is its relevance to human TBI. While side-by-side model-human comparison studies are impractical due to ethical issues, commonly reported clinical diagnostic metrics can offer insight into a model’s relevance. One such metric is serum biomarkers that are measured and reported during TBI diagnosis43,53. In 2021, the i-STAT TBI test was FDA-approved to measure serum levels of GFAP and UCHL1 as a diagnostic for TBI54. Both biomarkers have been shown to produce distinct temporal responses after TBI with GFAP concentrations increasing around 4 h and remaining elevated until 72 h before decaying and UCHL1 peaking between 4 and 12 h before gradually declining55. These trends are consistent with the results of our tissue model, where GFAP increased steadily over 72 h, and UCHL1 increased rapidly before declining at 24 h. Similarly, both S100B56 and NF-H57 peaked early followed by decay, while NSE showed sustained elevation even after 72 h58, showing our model reproduces reported biomarker dynamics. While many of these reports are not isolated to bTBI but to TBI more broadly, studies focused specifically on bTBI show similar trends59–61. Beyond biochemical markers, bridging experimental models with human bTBI pathology can be strengthened using additional clinically relevant readouts such as MRI or postmortem brain-slice analysis. For example, our tissue model showed clear destruction of the neural organizational structure as measured by loss of signal in both IF and calcium imaging. Clinically, white matter abnormalities following bTBI have been reported using diffusion tensor imaging (DTI)62, which is further supported by analysis of human post-mortem brain tissues, whereas astroglial scarring at gray-white matter junctions and perivascular spaces63, as well as altered aquaporin-4 expression on astrocytic endfeet at the gray-white matter junctions64 suggest alterations in tissue architecture and network disruption. Diffuse axonal injury (DAI), characterized by axonal varicosities, beading, and swelling is another hallmark of bTBI that has been observed in patients had a primary blast exposure and is mechanistically distinct from the focal axonal disruption association with contusion TBI65. Axonal varicosity formation has previously been quantified in the same 3D scaffold system following blast overpressure exposure, confirming the system’s capacity to recapitulate DAI-associated morphological changes31. Prospective imaging at lower cavitation doses with preserved cell viability and reduced cellular debris would enable cleaner morphological analysis in our system, and is an important future direction for characterizing DAI features across injury modalities.

A key finding from our study was a clear dependence of the injury response magnitude on cavitation dose. While the presence or amount of cavitation has not been directly monitored during in vivo blast exposures, animal bTBI studies mapped to humans using scaling laws have shown a similar dependence between injury severity and blast overpressure amplitude66. This dose-dependent behavior reflects a broader trend seen across TBI research, that the magnitude of the mechanical insult scales with the resulting biological injury. Additionally, studies of military personnel have shown a positive correlation between blood UCHL1 and GFAP levels and increased blast exposure59,60, which align with trends between these biomarkers and cavitation dose observed in our model. Rather than directly modeling characteristics of blast waves such as overpressure or cumulative blast exposure, our tissue model controls the spatial distribution and amount of cavitation injury applied across the engineered tissue, which have been mapped to blast wave characteristics using computational modeling and phantom studies21,67. Such studies suggest that the correlation between blast overpressure and severity of bTBI is mechanistically due to the increased amount of cavitation generated within the brain associated with greater overpressures. To comprehensively span the cavitation dose parameter space, we chose to apply doses across 3-orders of magnitude. While mapping this dose range to blast overpressure is an important future effort, the range captured clearly distinguishable changes in the biological response. However, the temporal pattern of cytotoxic, excitotoxic, and inflammatory markers across the doses applied illustrates the complexity of dose-dependent injury responses. At the highest cavitation dose, IL-6 was elevated early but not sustained through 72 h, suggesting that extensive cavitation-induced cell death may progressively reduce the viable microglial population responsible for prolonged cytokine secretion. At a moderate dose, the delayed IL-6 elevation at 24 h is more consistent with an active inflammatory response mounted by surviving cells. The sustained elevation of LDH and glutamate beyond 4 h post-injury further suggests that cytotoxicity and excitotoxic stress reflect a combination of both cellular structural disruption from membrane lysis and ongoing secondary injury processes. Future studies employing glutamate receptor antagonists and microglial-specific viability markers will be important for delineating the relative contributions of these mechanisms.

Directly isolating cavitation as an injury mechanism and controlling its application within a human neural tissue model offers a distinct advantage for in vitro studies. Although shock tubes and blast chambers advance the understanding of blast physics and bubble dynamics68, they do not allow cavitation to be isolated as a controlled insult within living neural tissue models. Traditional in vitro bTBI models may also simplify or exclude key anatomical structures, such as the skull69, that have been shown to influence pressure transmission and injury patterns14,17. Such simplifications can alter the resulting biological outcomes. For instance, the 3D brain model used in this study was previously integrated with a simplified skull model and tested in a blast chamber with overpressures of 10.4 and 18.6 psi31. While the injury response showed similar dose and time trends for LDH, glutamate and NF-H, there were key differences in other metrics. Most notably, the blast wave exposed model showed no change to GFAP, which has been shown to be an important biomarker expressed in blast exposed military personnel59,61. Additionally, S100B peaked later at 48 h, which deviates from our model’s peak between 4 and 24 h and clinical TBI data that also reports peaks earlier than 24 h56. Integration of our 3D brain model with controlled cortical impact (CCI) injury methods offers an additional point to benchmark the results of our cavitation injury model. CCI-exposed brain models showed comparable loss of neuronal network density after injury, and an increase in inflammatory markers such as IL-636. However, the compression applied by CCI was distinct from the tensile and shear forces associated with cavitation. While the CCI study did not measure all metrics reported in this study, differences in brain mechanics and injury profiles associated with compressive and tensile loading have been reported70,71, and it is postulated that these differences explain key distinctions between blast and blunt force trauma3–5,72.

While biomarkers served as a useful tool to benchmark our tissue model against other models and clinical outcomes, we also sought to understand the cellular signaling pathways through which tissues responded to the forces of cavitation. One pathway we examined was mechanotransduction, the process by which cells sense and respond to physical stress through their connections with the extracellular matrix (ECM)49. In our model, cavitation led to a reduction in total FAK expression along with an increase in calpain-mediated spectrin breakdown product (SBDP150), suggesting the potential disruption of cell-matrix anchoring and cytoskeletal integrity. FAK serves as a central mediator of integrin-based signaling that regulates neuronal morphology, survival, and mechanosensitive pathways, so its decreased expression may reflect destabilization of these adhesion complexes41,73. Because focal adhesions physically couple the ECM to the actin–spectrin cytoskeleton42, the observed increase in αII-spectrin breakdown product further implicates calpain activation and cytoskeletal proteolysis44,74,75. This pattern aligns with previous reports that calpain, rather than caspase, dominates in early cytoskeletal degradation following TBI75. Although additional studies are needed to assess FAK phosphorylation dynamics and downstream pathway activation, these findings point to a potential mechanistic link between cavitation-induced mechanical stress, and the initiation of cellular injury cascades. Notably, the observed increase in SBDP150 is most likely of predominantly neuronal origin, given that αII-spectrin is abundantly expressed in the neuronal cytoskeleton74. While the bulk lysate approach used in this study cannot resolve cell-type-specific contributions to FAK and spectrin signals, the intercellular interactions between neurons, astrocytes, and microglia likely shape the overall injury response. Astrocyte-mediated neuroprotection may reduce cavitation-induced neuronal damage, which could in turn attenuate neuroinflammation and microglial reactivity. Future studies using monoculture and defined coculture systems will be important for dissecting these cell-type-specific contributions to cavitation-induced mechanotransduction dysregulation.

The integration of controlled cavitation with our human 3D brain tissue models offers a powerful platform for studying cellular injury associated with bTBI. To increase the fidelity and applicability of this platform, there are several critical areas warranting further development. Head surrogate studies have correlated increased blast overpressure with the number of cavitation events21,67, but additional work is needed to directly relate cavitation dose applied in our system to blast overpressure. The dynamics of cavitation generated by our FUS transducer which use high amplitude (MPa), short cycles (µs) should be evaluated against cavitation generated under blast scenarios which may be lower amplitude (kPa), longer cycles (ms)10. Additionally, cavitation is thought to preferentially initiate within the brain at fluid–tissue interfaces, such as perivascular spaces21. Incorporating vascular components, such as brain microvascular endothelial cells or engineered microfluidic BBB channels76, will enable studies of perivascular injury and BBB disruption. Calcium imaging provided insight into network-level function following cavitation, but the reduced activity likely reflected a combined loss of tissue viability and network connectivity from both neurons and astrocytes77. Incorporating neuron-specific calcium reporters with viability assays will distinguish functional impairment from reductions caused by loss of viability, and including spatially mapped calcium imaging with defined injury zone coordinates will enable direct interrogation of local versus long-range network disruption, injury signal propagation, and network-level consequences of cavitation injury. Furthermore, the 6-week culture timepoint reflects a mature neural tissue state most relevant to adult TBI populations78,79. Age is a strong predictor of adverse TBI outcome, and injury severity, neuroinflammatory responses, and recovery trajectories differ across developmental stages80. Future studies could leverage the long-term stability of this scaffold system81 to interrogate age-dependent vulnerability to cavitation injury by modulating culture duration prior to injury induction. The cavitation injury platform developed here may also find utility beyond blast applications, as controlled cavitation delivery to human neural tissue is relevant to a range of fields, including directed energy27, therapeutic focused ultrasound25,34 and other contexts where mechanical or acoustic forces interact with brain tissue. Finally, blast exposure can also cause trauma in peripheral organs such as the lungs82, so coupling the brain model to an established lung model83,84 in a multi-organ microphysiological system with circulating immune cells85, can provide insight into organ crosstalk and systemic inflammation.

In summary, we developed and characterized a FUS cavitation platform integrated with a human-derived 3D brain tissue scaffold to investigate cavitation-related injury, and identified cellular responses consistent with features reported in bTBI. By systematically tuning cavitation exposure, we observed dose- and time-dependent injury response, including neurotrauma biomarker release, cytoskeletal degradation, and neuronal network disruption. Protein expression analysis revealed loss of FAK and calpain-mediated spectrin cleavage, linking cavitation to impaired mechanotransduction and cytoskeletal breakdown. Functional calcium imaging demonstrated reduced network activity post-injury, consistent with cavitation-induced cell fragmentation and loss of viability, proportional to the cavitation dose applied. Overall, this platform provides a controllable, human-relevant approach to study cavitation-induced injury, and supports future studies of multicellular signaling and therapeutic development across a range of clinical and research contexts where intracranial cavitation is a proposed injury mechanism.

Methods

Cavitation injury platform

A custom setup to spatially distribute cavitation injury throughout the brain tissue cultures was developed (Fig. 1A). The stage comprised two positioning systems that facilitated rapid sample-to-sample switching and the application of a preprogrammed cavitation injury dose for a given sample. A manual well-to-well positioning system placed the target sample above the FUS transducer using a set of rails with a magnetic position alignment mechanism that translated the well plate in 1-well increments along the column and row dimensions of the plate. The plate was integrated into the rail positioning system using a custom stainless steel plate holder. A motorized transducer positioning system raster-scanned the cavitation injury zone through the tissue culture with prespecified conditions using a 3-axis motorized positioning system that consisted of a translational stage (Thorlabs, MT3, Newton, NJ), stepper motors (Thorlabs, ZST225B), controllers (Thorlabs, KST201) and custom transducer mount. A deionized (DI) water bath was used as ultrasound coupling between the FUS transducer and tissue culture well plate. The workflow for an injury experiment consisted of: (1) translating the well plate to position a well of interest over the transducer aperture, (2) registering the transducer (Supplementary Fig. 2) to the well to achieve targeted injury within the well and tissue culture, (3) applying a prespecified cavitation dose throughout the tissue culture sample.

FUS driving parameters

The FUS transducer was driven with a high voltage pulser that comprised several commercially available components (Supplementary Fig. 1A-C). Two channels of a function generator (Tektronix, AFG3022C) operated in burst mode were assigned distinct settings. Channel 1 was set to produce an 8-cycle, 4.3 MHz sinusoidal pulse with an amplitude of 850 mV. Channel 2 was set to produce a 5 µs period, 1-cycle square wave with an amplitude of 5 V and offset of 2.5 V. Burst periods on both channels were set to 1 s, 100 ms or 20 ms, corresponding to pulse-repetition-frequencies (PRFs) of 1 Hz, 10 Hz and 50 Hz, respectively. Channel 1 of the function generator was connected to the RF input of a 5-kW gated RF amplifier (Ritec Inc., 5 kW GA-2500). Channel 2 of the function generator was connected to the gate input of the amplifier, which triggered amplification of the signal into the RF input. The RF output of the amp was routed to a step-down impedance matching transformer (APC International, LTD, 90-4496) that was set to an impedance of 16 ohms. The transducer was connected to the output of the impedance-matching transformer. Voltage across and current into the transducer were monitored using a high voltage probe (BK Precision, PR2000B) and current probe (Tektronix, 011-0106-00), respectively, connected to an oscilloscope (Tektronix, DPO 2004B). During cavitation injury experiments, the gain of the amplifier was set to 8, which produced a peak-to-peak voltage of 1.25 kVpp across the transducer (Supplementary Fig. 1E). The transducer geometry was designed to ensure acoustic delivery through the well plate bottom without obstruction from the well sidewalls, and the operating frequency was chosen to achieve sufficient focal gain to overcome attenuation through the plastic well plate bottom and reliably generate cavitation within the tissue-containing wells. Cavitation at these parameters were confirmed by visualization of the cavitation cloud in water (Supplementary Fig. 1D), and with fluorescent viability staining of astrocytes embedded in collagen (Supplementary Fig. 1F).

Injury application to brain tissue cultures

Following registration of the transducer to a well (SI “Methods”), custom-written control software implemented in MATLAB delivered a prespecified number of cavitation pulses per unit area (dose) throughout the brain tissue culture within that well. The cavitation dose was applied by raster-scanning the transducer focus through a gridded plane of points within the tissue culture and applying cavitation pulses at each point. The dose metric, pulses/mm2, was chosen to reflect the cumulative cavitation exposure across the tissue rather than specific waveform characteristics, consistent with studies demonstrating blast overpressure correlates with the amount of intracranial cavitation generated21,67. To maintain a total exposure time of less than 5 min per sample, the PRF was adjusted for each dose such that 3 pulses/mm2 used 1 Hz, 30 pulses/mm2 used 10 Hz, and 300 pulses/mm2 used 50 Hz. The transducer was also operated in a passive mode to monitor the presence of cavitation.

Silk scaffold fabrication

Silk sponge scaffolds used for establishing the 3D brain model were fabricated as previously described35,86. Silk fibroin was purified and concentrated to 6% (w/v) in DI water. Salt-leaching with 425–500 µm sized NaCl particles was used to create porous silk sponges, which were cut with biopsy punches to create scaffolds that were 6 mm in diameter with a central cut-out hole of 2 mm in diameter, and trimmed to a height of 1.5 mm. Scaffolds were sterilized by autoclave, followed by coating with 10 µg/mL poly-L-ornithine (Sigma-Aldrich, P4957-50ML, St. Louis, MO) at 37 °C overnight, and 5 µg/mL laminin (Sigma-Aldrich, 11243217001) at 37 °C overnight prior to cell seeding.

Human induced neural stem cells (hiNSCs)

HiNSCs previously derived from human neonatal foreskin fibroblasts78 were seeded and expanded on an inactivated feeder layer of mouse embryonic fibroblasts (ATCC, SCRC-1008) and maintained in KnockOut DMEM (Thermo Scientific, 10829018) containing 20% CTS KnockOut SR XenoFree Medium (Thermo Scientific, 12618013), 1% GlutaMax (Thermo Scientific, 3505006), 1% antibiotic-antimycotic, 0.1% 2-mercaptoethanol (Thermo Scientific, 21985023), and 20 ng/mL human basic fibroblast growth factor (Thermo Scientific, PHG0024). Media was changed daily, and hiNSCs were harvested with TrypLE Select (Thermo Scientific, 12563-011) for scaffold seeding.

Human primary astrocytes

Human primary astrocytes (ScienCell Research Laboratories, 1800, Carlsbad, CA) were cultured on plates coated with 20 µg/mL poly-L-lysine solution (Sigma, P4832) and maintained in Astrocyte Media (ScienCell Research Laboratories, 1801) supplemented with 2% fetal bovine serum, 1% penicillin/streptomycin solution, and 1% astrocyte growth supplement (ScienCell Research Laboratories, 1852). Media was changed every 3 days, and astrocytes were harvested with Trypsin-EDTA (Thermo Scientific, 25200056) for scaffold seeding.

Human microglia

Human microglia cells, HMC3 (ATCC, CRL-3304, Manassas, VA), were cultured in EMEM (ATCC, 30-2003) supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum (Thermo Scientific, A5256801) and 1% antibiotic–antimycotic. Media was changed every 3 days, and HMC3s were harvested with Trypsin-EDTA for scaffold seeding.

Brain tissue cultures

Brain tissue constructs were created as previously described36. The physical and biochemical properties of silk scaffolds, including compressive modulus and neuronal compatibility, have been characterized in prior studies, where the scaffold stiffness was within the range of brain parenchymal tissue35. Each scaffold was seeded with a triculture of 2 × 106 hiNSCs, 0.5 × 106 astrocytes, and 0.1 × 106 microglia in 40 µL of Neurobasal Medium (Thermo Scientific, 12348017) supplemented with 2% B27 (Thermo Scientific, 17504044), 1% glutamax, 1% anti-anti, and 1% astrocyte growth supplement (NB complete media). Scaffolds were incubated at 37 °C for 30 min, and an additional 150 µL of media was added. After overnight incubation, scaffolds were transferred to a new 96-well plate and embedded in 100 µL of 3 mg/mL type I rat-tail collagen (R&D Systems, 3447-020-01, Minneapolis, MN), pH 7.0–7.2. The next day, collagen-embedded scaffolds were transferred to 48-well plates containing 1 mL of NB complete media. Scaffolds were maintained for 6 weeks prior to injury to allow for maturation, with media changes performed twice weekly.

Workflow for tissue injury experiments

Six-week-old brain models were transferred into the center of individual wells of the custom plate and laid flat. Air bubbles were removed to ensure full contact with the bottom surface. Then 100 µL of NB complete media was added to each well to maintain hydration and to establish a continuous fluid interface between the scaffold and the well surface. The plate containing the brain tissue cultures was then transferred to the cavitation injury platform to apply injury (Fig. 1A). Sham controls were placed in the plate for equal durations but were not exposed to cavitation. After injury, supernatants and scaffolds were collected for analysis at 4, 24, 72 h, and for neurotrauma biomarker analysis, additional collections were performed at 1 week (wk), and 2 weeks post-injury for analysis.

LDH assay

Lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) was measured following the manufacturer’s protocol (Thermo Scientific, C20300). Supernatant was collected from each sample at 4, 24, and 72 h post-injury for all treatment groups and mixed with LDH reaction mixture for 30 min at room temperature protected from light. Stop solution was added to halt the reaction and the 490 nm absorbance was measured on a Synergy H1 microplate reader (BioTek, Winooski, VT).

Glutamate assay

Glutamate was measured following the manufacturer’s protocol (Abcam, ab83389, Cambridge, UK). Supernatant was collected from each sample at 4, 24, and 72 h post-injury for all treatment groups and mixed with Glutamate assay reaction mixture for 30 min at 37 °C protected from light. The absorbance at 450 nm was measured on a Synergy H1 microplate reader (BioTek).

Immunofluorescence (IF) imaging and analysis

Scaffolds were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 1 h, followed by three, 10-min PBS washes. Scaffolds were then stained with 0.1% Sudan Black B (Sigma-Aldrich, 199664-25 g) in 70% ethanol for 20 min followed by three, 10-min PBS washes. Scaffolds were then permeabilized and blocked for 1 h in PBS containing 0.2% Triton X-100 and 4% goat serum. Primary antibodies for TUJ1, GFAP, and Iba1 (SI Table 1) were diluted in blocking buffer and incubated overnight at 4 °C. The next day, scaffolds were washed with 0.1% Tween-20 in PBS (PBST) for three 10-min washes. Secondary antibodies (SI Table 1) were diluted in blocking buffer and added to the scaffolds for 1 h. Scaffolds were washed with PBST for three 10-min washes. 4’,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) was added to the scaffolds for 20 min. IF was performed using a Zeiss LSM780 confocal microscope (Zeiss). Tile-scan (6800 µm × 6800 µm) and z-stack (500 µm) images were collected for each scaffold. To quantify TUJ1-positive neuronal network coverage, maximum intensity projections were generated from each z-stack. Although this approach does not preserve volumetric information, it provides a consistent readout of total neuronal coverage across the scaffold surface. In FIJI (version 1.54p), a circular region of interest (ROI) was created to encompass the entire scaffold. TUJ1 signal was thresholded at an intensity value of 20 to generate a binary mask, and the “Analyze Particles” function was used to calculate the TUJ1-positive area. Percent coverage was determined by dividing the TUJ1-positive area by the total ROI area. For normalization, values were expressed as a percentage relative to time-matched sham controls.

Inflammation and Brain Injury Panel Luminex

Interleukin-6 (IL-6) and interleukin-8 (IL-8) were measured by using RnD Systems human Luminex discovery assay (R&D Systems, LXSAHM) following the manufacturer’s protocol. For the brain injury biomarkers, S100 calcium-binding protein B (S100B), glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP), ubiquitin carboxyl-terminal hydrolase L1 (UCHL1), neuron-specific enolase (NSE), and neurofilament heavy (NF-H), were measured using ProcartaPlex Human Brain Injury Panel, 5plex (Thermo Fisher, EPX050-15838-901) following the manufacturer’s protocol. Supernatants were collected from scaffolds at 4, 24, and 72 h post-injury for all treatment groups. For IL-6 and IL-8 assays, n = 16 scaffolds were analyzed across four independent experiments. For the neurotrauma biomarker panel, n = 20 scaffolds were analyzed across five independent experiments at 4, 24, and 72 h, and n = 8 scaffolds across two independent experiments at 1 week and 2 weeks. Data acquisition was performed on a Luminex FLEXMAP 3D instrument (Thermo Scientific). Standard curves were generated for each analyte, and biomarker concentrations were calculated and expressed as fold change relative to time-matched sham controls. Sham normalization was performed to account for batch-to-batch variability in absolute biomarker concentrations inherent to complex 3D tissue systems, including differences in scaffold seeding density and cell passage number across independent experiments. This approach preserved dose-dependent trends while enabling comparisons across experimental replicates without requiring cell counting.

Western blots

Cells were lysed with 1X RIPA buffer (Sigma-Aldrich, 20-188) containing protease and phosphatase inhibitor (Thermo Scientific, A32961) and sonicated at 20% amplitude for 20 pulses (1 s on and 1 s off). The lysate was centrifuged at 16,000 × g for 20 min at 4 °C, and the debris was discarded. Protein concentration was quantified by Bradford Assay (Thermo Scientific, 23200), 30 µg of protein was loaded per sample on a 4–12% Bolt Bis-Tris gel (Thermo Scientific, NW04125BOX) and separated at 120 V for 60 min, then transferred to PVDF membranes (Thermo Scientific, IB242002) using the iBlot2 Transfer system (Thermo Scientific) at 23 V for 3 min. Membranes were blocked for 1 h in 5% BSA (Jackson Immuno Research Labs, 001-000-161, West Grove, PA) in TBST and incubated in primary antibodies (SI Table 1) diluted in blocking solution at 4 °C overnight. On the following day, membranes were washed three times for 10 min each and incubated in HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (SI Table 1) for 1 h in blocking solution. Membranes were washed and incubated for 5 min in SuperSignal West Pico Plus substrate (Thermo Scientific 34577). Protein was visualized using a Bio-Rad ChemiDoc MP (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA). Images were converted to 8-bit grayscale and bands were selected using equal-sized rectangular ROIs in FIJI. Densitometry was performed using the “Analyze, Gels, Plot Lanes” function and the area under each peak was measured to quantify band intensity. Band intensities were normalized to beta-actin to account for loading variability, and beta-actin band intensities were confirmed to be consistent across all dose groups and timepoints by densitometry.

Calcium imaging

After 24 h post-injury, scaffolds were stained with 5 µm Calbryte 520 AM (AAT Bioquest 20650, Pleasanton, CA) in artificial cerebral spinal fluid solution (140 mM NaCl, 2.8 mM KCl, 2 mM CaCl2, 2 mM MgCl2, HEPES 10 mM in DI H20) containing 0.02% Pluronic F-127 in DMSO (AAT Bioquest 20052), 2.5 mM Probenecid (Thermo Scientific, P36400), and 1X PowerLoad (Thermo Scientific, P10020). Scaffolds were imaged on a Zeiss Axio Observer Epifluorescence Microscope (Zeiss) at 10x magnification. For each scaffold, three separate ROIs (665.6 µm × 665.6 µm each) located at the interface between the silk scaffold and the central collagen region were imaged for 3 min each at 40 Hz (Supplementary Fig. 4A). A total of eight independent scaffolds per condition across two experiments were imaged. Time series data were analyzed in FIJI, and bleach correction with exponential fit was applied. Each recorded ROI was subdivided into a 16 × 16 grid, yielding 256 distinct sub-ROIs (41.6 µm × 41.6 µm each). For each sub-ROI, mean fluorescence intensity was extracted and analyzed in MATLAB (version 2022a). Calcium transients (spikes) were identified using the findpeaks function to detect individual signaling events (Supplementary Fig. 4B). Fluorescence intensity traces for each sub-ROI were normalized to baseline fluorescence (ΔF/F0), where F0 was defined as the 10th percentile of fluorescence intensity over the recording period to account for baseline drift. From the normalized ΔF/F0 traces, the following parameters were calculated: Total spike rate (spikes · min−1 · scaffold−1): the total number of detected calcium transients summed across all 256 sub-ROIs in a field, divided by the recording duration in minutes. Percentage of active sub-ROIs (%): the fraction of sub-ROIs exhibiting at least one detected transient, expressed as a percentage of the total 256 sub-ROIs. Spike rate within active sub-ROIs (spikes · min−1 · ROI−1): the mean transient rate calculated only from sub-ROIs containing ≥ 1 spike, representing activity normalized to active regions. Mean spike amplitude (ΔF/F0): the average peak amplitude of all detected calcium transients within active sub-ROIs. Mean spike width (s): the average full width at half-maximum (FWHM) duration of detected transients within active sub-ROIs, reflecting the temporal profile of calcium events.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism (version 10.5.0). All data are presented as mean ± SEM, with each point representing one independent scaffold. Statistical comparisons across treatment groups and time points were made using two-way ANOVA, and Dunnett’s post-hoc test was applied to compare each treatment group to its time-matched sham control, with correction for multiple comparisons. Statistical significance was defined as *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, ****p < 0.0001.
