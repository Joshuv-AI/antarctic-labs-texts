Mechanisms underlying the response of different resistant quinoa cultivars to Spodoptera exigua feeding
Authors: Junna Liu, Ping Zhang, Hanxue Li, Yutao Bai, Xuqin Wang, Lingyuan Zhang, Guofei Jiang, Shunhe Cheng, Peng Qin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSpodoptera exigua is the main pest that harms quinoa. Its larvae feed on leaves, flowers, and grains, seriously affecting the quality and yield of quinoa. However, faced with the feeding of S. exigua, some quinoa cultivars have the characteristics of avoiding or reducing damage and having compensatory ability after being harmed. This study aims to dissect the defensive responses of quinoa cultivars with differing resistance levels to feeding by S. exigua.MethodsWe compared defensive metabolites and transcriptomes among high-resistant (HR), medium-resistant (MR) and highly-susceptible (HS) quinoa genotypes, and verified gene function via heterologous transformation.ResultsThe contents of linoleic acid, α-linolenic acid, 12-OPDA, JA and JA-Ile were significantly elevated in MR and HR genotypes. Transgenic Arabidopsis restricted larval feeding, retarded growth and lowered survival of S. exigua. The expression of CqMYC2L1 varied greatly among resistance groups, indicating a potential correlation between JA pathway metabolites. Subcellular localization verified that the CqMYC2L1 protein localizes to the nucleus, and dual-luciferase (LUC/REN) assays confirmed its transcriptional repressive activity. Heterologous overexpression of CqMYC2L1 in Arabidopsis significantly elevated the levels of JA, JA-Ile, 12-OPDA, total alkaloids, phenolic acids and lipids, and CqMYC2L1 (4.15-fold) showed significant induction to varying degrees. Exogenous JA rapidly activated the transcription and protein abundance of CqMYC2L1, consistent with JA accumulation, and Western blot validated the stable expression of CqMYC2L1 protein.ConclusionsCqMYC2L1 is associated with JA-mediated defensive metabolism, which enhances the accumulation of alkaloids, phenolic acids and lipids to deter S. exigua.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12870-026-09542-2.Keywords: Quinoa, Spodoptera exigua, Defensive substances, Resistance gene, Functional verification

Introduction

The evolution of plant defense mechanisms against insect herbivory and the offensive capacity of herbivorous insects has been synergistic and ongoing. Plants have evolved specialized defense mechanisms to improve their survival and adaptability [1–4]. Plants deploy physiological, biochemical and morphological strategies against herbivore attack. Upon insect feeding, plants activate defensive signaling cascades, reprogram transcript abundance, and accumulate specialized metabolites. These defense compounds can affect the behavior, morphology, and physiology of insects, effectively resisting attacks and ultimately enhancing plant adaptability [5–8]. Plants and insects have evolved over a long period of time using two mechanisms to resist insect harm: constitutive defense and induced defense [9, 10]. Plants quickly activate defense signals after being injured by herbivorous insects, thereby activating various induced defense responses in the plant. Through changes in physiological, biochemical, and morphological characteristics, they develop resistance to pests, and the plant defense enzyme system and its activity undergo changes [11–14]. Tomatoes determine whether to activate induced defense responses based on the strength of their constitutive defense, thereby achieving optimal protective effects [15].

Chenopodium quinoa is an annual dicotyledonous herbaceous plant belonging to the genus Chenopodium within the Chenopodioideae subfamily, it is typically cross-pollinated and exhibits allopolyploidy. Quinoa is phylogenetically related to spinach (Spinacia oleracea L.) and sugar beet (Beta vulgaris L.), which are also important members of the Amaranthaceae family. Originating from the Titicaca Lake region in South America, quinoa has a cultivation history of about 5000 ~ 7000 years, In the 1970 s, quinoa was selected by NASA as a staple food in space and became known as the “super grain” [16–18]. Quinoa has garnered significant attention for its comprehensive nutritional value, high functional benefits, strong stability, and ecological adaptability. The Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO) of the United Nations has recognized it as a non genetically modified nutritional food and the only crop that can meet all the nutritional needs of humans with single crop [19]. Common pests of quinoa are aphids, Lygus, and stem boring flies, which can cause up to 100% yield loss [20, 21]. Although S. exigua infests a wide range of crops, larval preference and damage intensity differ markedly among host plants. Secondary metabolites constitute the key factor governing feeding selection by S. exigua [22, 23].

The defense response mode of insect feeding overlaps with the mechanical damage response mode. Following insect herbivory and mechanical wounding, Different resistant quinoa exhibited distinct patterns in the accumulation of secondary metabolites as well as the profiles and activities of defensive enzymes. These responses also differed from those triggered by mechanical wounding alone [24–26]. Salivary secretions from chewing insects activate intricate wound-signaling cascades and initiate host plant defenses. Plants rapidly produce jasmonic acid (JA), and leaf-induced defenses are governed by JA-Ile, which orchestrates resistance responses to herbivorous insects [27–36]. However, JA and JA Ile have not been directly validated for their defense against quinoa. JA is an important phytohormone that mediates the plant's insect resistance and defense responses. MYC2 serves as the master regulator of the JA signaling pathway. Upon JA signal perception, MYC2 transcription factors govern the transcript abundance of JA-responsive genes and modulate insect resistance as well as the biosynthesis of secondary metabolites [33, 37–40].

MYC2 regulates the expression of downstream target genes, regulates secondary metabolic pathways in response to the JA pathway, and has a relatively conserved function across species [41, 42]. Similar to quinoa, other Amaranthaceae species activate the JA signaling pathway and downstream transcription factors upon insect attack. This process elevates the transcript abundance of defensive proteins including protease inhibitors and polyphenol oxidase, disrupting digestive physiology in herbivores [43]. Plants defective in JA biosynthesis or JA perception show greatly reduced accumulation of defensive secondary metabolites, leading to weakened resistance against herbivorous insects. This indicates that JA controls the biosynthesis of pest-induced defensive metabolites [44]. AtMYB44 regulates JA mediated expression of defense genes and plays a crucial role in Arabidopsis resistance resist the phloem-feeding polyphagous green peach aphid (Myzus persicae Sulzer) and the diamondback moth (Plutella xylostella Linnaeus) [45, 46]. Overexpression of CmMYB15 enhances resistance to aphids by regulating lignin biosynthesis in chrysanthemums [47]. AtMYB102 is locally expressed at the feeding sites of Pieris rapae in damaged leaves, thereby activating the expression of defense-related genes and cell wall modification genes [48].

Against the growing demand for diverse, nutrient-dense, safe and healthy food in recent years, quinoa cultivation frequently suffers infestation by the chewing herbivore S. exigua, resulting in substantial yield losses [24]. Currently, there are few studies on the molecular mechanism of quinoa resistance to S. exigua. This study selected high resistant cultivar (HR), medium resistant cultivar (MR), and highly susceptible cultivar (HS) from a Lijiang red quinoa population with small differences in agronomic traits and basically consistent genetic backgrounds as materials for subsequent research. Through morphological indicators, physicochemical indicators, wide target metabolomics analysis, volatile metabolomics analysis, hormone analysis, transcriptome analysis, gene family analysis, subcellular localization, gene function verification, and WB detection, the response of different resistance types of quinoa to S. exigua feeding was systematically elucidated, and resistance related genes were identified.

Materials and methods

Plant materials and sampling

In this study, three quinoa cultivars with nearly identical agronomic traits and consistent genetic backgrounds were individually screened from the segregating population of Lijiang red quinoa. These materials include high resistant quinoa “Dianli-1415”(HR), medium resistant cultivar “Dianli-1910”(MR), and highly susceptible cultivar “Dianli-598”(HS), which were selected from 200 quinoa germplasm cultivars with varying resistance independently developed by Yunnan Agricultural University. Divide the plots into groups randomly, with each plot covering 20 m2(10 m × 2 m) and three replicates. Spodoptera exigua (Keyun Biotech) were reared (relative temperature: 23 ± 2 °C, relative humidity: 44 ± 6%, light/dark photoperiod: 16:8 h), these conditions simulate the growth conditions of S. exigua in its natural environment, ensuring the stability and health status of the tested insect source. Before the quinoa was inoculated with the insect, the S. exigua were all fasted for about 8 h. Cephalic shell width of S. exigua larvae can be used as the optimal indicator for age classification [49].

For the 200 independently selected high-generation cultivars, 30 plants were chosen for each cultivar, 10 s-instar S. exigua larvae were inoculated onto each plant, and then the plants were covered with gauze. The survival rate of larvae and the degree of plant damage were recorded 7 d after inoculation (Tables 1, and 2) (Figure S1). Different resistant quinoa and same-aged S. exigua were placed in containers to further screen the materials (Figure S2). Based on the resistance classification of quinoa to S. exigua (Tables 1, and 2), high resistant quinoa “Dianli-1415”(HR), medium resistant quinoa “Dianli-1910”(MR), and highly susceptible quinoa “Dianli-598”(HS) were finally determined.

Sampling time and method: During the flowering stage (60 d) of quinoa, three independent plants with consistent growth were selected (3 biological replicates), ensuring that the plant leaves were free from any damage. Samples were taken from the fourth and fifth leaves from the top of the plant, with the feeding site remaining consistent. Different resistant quinoa plants were marked in a completely random manner, and third/fourth instar nymphs were used for biological determination and biochemical research. Mechanical damage treatments used a 0.5 cm diameter perforator to punch holes in leaves at flowering stage, with the damage area mimicking wounds from S. exigua feeding, and the number of treatment slices per plant was consistent with feeding by S. exigua.

Morphological data collection

Leaves of quinoa were sampled at the flowering stage (60 d), and plant height of quinoa was measured with a tape measure; diameter of the main stem was measured using a vernier caliper (1183-150A); the diameter of the main stem of quinoa was measured using a Vernier caliper (Model 1183-150A); measure the leaf area of quinoa using a crop leaf morphometer (TPYX-A) and measure the relative chlorophyll content using a chlorophyll meter (TYS-B, Zhejiang, China, https://www.tpyn.net) (Table S1).

Physiological measurement indicators and methods for quinoa leaves and S. exigua

The activity differences of different enzymes in quinoa leaves were determined using ACC oxidase (ACO), superoxide dismutase (SOD), terpene synthase (TPS), lipoxygenase (LOX), total flavonoids (TF), as well as PAL, PPO, and POD ELISA detection kits (Jiangsu Jingmei Biotechnology Co., Ltd; http://www.jsjmsw.com). The differences in metabolite content of quinoa leaves were determined using a total alkaloid assay kit and a total amino acid (T-AA), total phenol, and tannin assay kit (http://www.njjcbio.com) (Table S2). SOD, LOX, PPO, POD, TPS, PAL and CAT were quantified on a fresh weight basis of quinoa leaf samples. Only ACO activity was determined according to the volume of reaction mixture.

The reaction of S. exigua to different leaf treatments was determined using insect multifunctional oxidases (MFOs), phenoloxidase (PO), POD, carboxylesterase (CES), SOD, and glutathione S-transferase (GST) ELISA detection kits.

Metabolite detection, qualitative and quantitative metabolite analysis

Metabolites were detected and further analyzed by Wuhan Metware Biotechnology Co., Ltd.(Wuhan, China). Metabolites were detected using ultra performance liquid chromatography tandem mass spectrometry (UPLC–MS/MS) (Supplementary material) detection platform and a self-built database MWDB (Metware Database). Equal-volume mixed QC samples were prepared to evaluate instrument stability. The overlapping status of total ion chromatograms (TIC) of QC samples was monitored, and only metabolites with RSD < 30% in QC samples were kept to ensure experimental repeatability. Screen differential accumulated metabolites (DAMs) with variable importance in projection (VIP) ≥ 1, fold change (FC) ≥ 2, and FC ≤ 0.5. Annotation analysis was performed using the KEGG database (http://www.kegg.jp/kegg/compound/) to further identify differentially accumulated metabolites.

Determination of volatile compounds

Leaves of quinoa plants with different resistance types were analyzed online using Gas Chromatography-Mass Spectrometry (GC–MS) [50]. Volatile metabolites with VIP ≥ 1, |log2FC|≥ 1 and p < 0.05 were considered as DAMs for group discrimination.

Transcriptome sequencing and data analysis

The cDNA libraries from quinoa leaves were sequenced on the Illumina sequencing platform by Metware Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, China). Raw reads were filtered to remove adapters and low-quality sequences to generate clean reads, with strict quality thresholds: Q20 > 97% and Q30 > 92%. The reference genome used in this study is GCF_001683475.1_ASM168347v1_genomic.fna. Fragments Per Kilobase of transcript per Million fragments mapped (FPKM) was used as an indicator for measuring transcript abundance. Differentially expressed genes were identified by applying the criteria |log2FC|≥ 1 and FDR < 0.05. To verify the accuracy of RNA sequencing data, RT-qPCR (Real-time quantitative PCR) reactions were performed using PerfectStart® Green qPCR SuperMix (Beijing, China). TUB-6 was used as an internal reference gene, and the relative transcript abundance was calculated using the 2−∆∆CT method [51]. All primers information used for RT-qPCR were detailed in Table S3.

Correlation analysis between metabolite profiling and transcriptome

By establishing hierarchical intermolecular data relationships and integrating functional analysis with metabolic pathway enrichment analysis, we systematically and comprehensively decipher the functions and regulatory mechanisms of biomolecules. This approach identifies key metabolic pathways, genes, and metabolites, providing a foundation for subsequent in-depth experimental validation and application.

Weighted gene coexpression network analysis (WGCNA)

WGCNA was used to evaluate the relationship between key DAMs and DEGs in quinoa under different resistant quinoa of S. exigua and to identify key regulatory genes. To further integrate genes and DAMs data, module-trait relationships were analyzed using Metware Cloud (https://cloud.metware.cn), and Cytoscape software (v3.8.0) was used to visualize the Chord diagram for interactions with coefficients ≥ 0.80 and P < 0.05 [52].

Subcellular localization

Transform the constructed pBWA(V)HS-730184-GFP (CqMYC2L1), and pBWA(V)HS-732543-GFP (CqMYC2L2) into Agrobacterium tumefaciens GV3101. Meanwhile, we constructed the marker gene sequences of the corresponding organelles onto the pBWA(V)HS-RFP vector, co-transform tobacco leaves by Agrobacterium tumefaciens immersion method. Instantly transform tobacco leaves under the control of the 35S promoter and observe GFP fluorescence signal.

Transcription factor activity validation analysis

Using pGreenII 62sk-GAL4-730,184 (CqMYC2L1) as the vector, pGreenII 0800-GAL4-TATA + pGreenII 62sk-GAL4 as the negative control, and pGreenII 0800-GAL4-TATA + pGreenII 62sk-GAL4-VP16 as the positive control, the experiment was conducted. With renilla luciferase (REN) as the internal reference, the value obtained by measuring firefly luciferase (LUC) was divided by the value obtained by measuring REN, and the experimental result was Luc/Ren.

Target gene cloning

Weigh quinoa leaves, grind them in liquid nitrogen, and extract total RNA using TransZol Up Enhanced RNA Extraction Kit (Beijing, China). Perform reverse transcription to synthesize the first strand of cDNA using TransScript® One-Step gDNA Removal and cDNA Synthesis SuperMix (Beijing, China). Design 4 pairs of specific primers for amplification using Primer 6.0 software, and obtain the full-length gene sequence (Table 3). The reaction conditions are 94 ℃ pre denaturation for 3 min; denaturation at 94 ℃ for 30 s, annealing at 51 ℃ for 30 s, and extension at 72 ℃ for 1 min, for 30 cycles; extend at 72 ℃ for 10 min.

Construction of an overexpression vector

The amplified products were analyzed by 1% agarose gel electrophoresis, and the recovered and purified target fragment was ligated into the pBWA(V)BS vector. Following transformation and plasmid extraction, the resulting plasmid was submitted to Wuhan Boyuan Biotechnology Co., Ltd. for sequencing. Based on the obtained cDNA sequence of the target gene, combined with the homologous recombination site on the binary expression vector pBWA (V) BS, primers with upstream homologous arm (AACACGGGGGACTGCAAC) and downstream homologous arm (CCTGAGGGCCGCTGTACA) were designed to amplify the target gene sequence. The product was then transformed into Escherichia coli competent cells (DH5α), cultured overnight, and monoclonal colonies were selected. Selected colonies were sent to Boyuan Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, China) for sequencing, the positive recombinants were named pBWA(V)BS-730184 (CqMYC2L1) and pBWA(V)BS-732543 (CqMYC2L2).

Genetic transformation of target genes in Arabidopsis thaliana

Positive Arabidopsis thaliana (Columbia) plants were screened using Basta (15 mg/L), and Arabidopsis genomic DNA was extracted using CTAB method for PCR detection until T2 generation positive Arabidopsis seeds were obtained. When Arabidopsis blooms (18 d), place two 2nd instar S. exigua on the leaves of wild-type and transgenic Arabidopsis (third or fourth leaf from the top) and feed freely for 15 min. Simultaneously, mechanical damage was artificially induced on quinoa plants of different resistance types (using a punch with a diameter of 0.5 cm to punch holes on the blades) for 15 min. Using S. exigua feeding for 0 min as a control and 15 min as a treatment, leaf samples (3 biological replicates) were collected and stored at − 80 ℃ in a refrigerator.

Results

Analysis of morphological and physicochemical characteristics of different resistant quinoa cultivars under S. exigua feeding

Compared to HR, MR, and HS were subjected to morphological (Table S1) and physiological indicators (defensive enzyme activity and secondary metabolites in different resistant quinoa) under the feeding of S. exigua. From the morphological indicators, it was found that the plant height of HR and MR was lower than that of HS, while HS had the smallest stem diameter and leaf area; the leaf color and relative chlorophyll content of HR and MR were higher than that of HS (Table 3, Fig. S3). From the physicochemical indexes, it was found that there were significant differences between HR, MR, and HS themselves and mechanical damage.

In terms of SOD, LOX, ACO, PPO, POD, TPS, PAL and CAT activities, the enzyme activities of leaves fed for 15 min by S. exigua > enzyme activities of leaves with anthropogenic mechanical damage > intact leaves. And in terms of different quinoa enzyme activities, HR≧MR > HS (Fig. 1A-B); the accumulation of secondary metabolites of HR and MR were significantly higher than HS (Fig. 1C-E). To determine the differences in JA, ABA, SA and JA-Ile content in quinoa at different levels it was found that HR had the highest levels of JA and JA-Ile, while HS, which had been fed on by S. exigua, had the lowest levels (Fig. 1F).

Differences in response of S. exigua feeding on quinoa with different resistance

The resistance response of plants to insects is related to insect species, age, feeding methods, feeding sites, and the degree of damage to plants. The changes of insects include physical changes, weight changes, and growth metabolism. Three 4th S. exigua fed on different resistant quinoa for 60 min, and it was seen that S. exigua fed less on HR and MR and more on HS, with most of its body appearing green (Fig. 2A-C). Comparing the body weight of S. exigua before and after feeding with different resistant quinoa, it was found that feeding with HR, MR, and HS increased by 0.09%, 0.1%, and 0.19%, respectively. Among them, HR (before: 0.0051 g, after: 0.0060 g), MR (before: 0.0052 g, after: 0.0062 g), and HS (before: 0.0054 g, after: 0.0073 g).

Differential analysis of metabolites related to different resistance quinoa

To systematically identify all metabolites involved in the direct defense against S.exigua across different resistant quinoa, the metabolites of different resistant quinoa were analyzed. Based on principal component analysis (PCA, Fig. 3A), coefficient of variation (CV, Fig. 3B), and pearson's correlation coefficient r (PCC, Fig. 3C) indicated that the experimental data was stable, and hierarchical cluster analysis (HCA; Fig. 3D) was conducted on the accumulation patterns of metabolites among different samples, and the concentrations of most detected metabolites showed significant changes under different treatments, confirming that the measurement of physiological indicators were consistent with the PCA results. A total of 1865 metabolites were detected, including 605 DAMs (Table S4-S5). Through the Venn diagram (Fig. 3E), 25 common DAMs and specific differential metabolites were identified HRvs.MR (53), HRvs.HS (143), MRvs.HS (80) among groups, which can be seen that there were fewer specific to HRvs.MR, and more specific differential metabolites between resistant and susceptible cultivars. Among the top 20 metabolites in different resistant quinoa, both HRvs.HS and MRvs.HS metabolite changes were dominated by alkaloids, phenolic acids and flavonoids (Figure S5). To investigate the relative content changes of metabolites, Kmeans clustering analysis was conducted and it was found that all DAMs had 10 different trends (Fig. 3F, Table S6), it has been confirmed that the accumulation and quantity of metabolites in HR and MR are higher than those in HS, mainly due to alkaloids, phenolic acids, amino acids and their derivatives, and flavonoids. The number of KEGG annotations for three groups of significantly different metabolites was divided into 220, 392, and 347 for HRvs.MR, HRvs.HS, and MRvs.HS, with 55, 49, and 64 pathways annotated, respectively (Table S7). In addition to primary and secondary metabolism, significantly enriched pathways were 2-Oxocarboxylic acid metabolism; linoleic acid metabolism; alpha linolenic acid metabolism; phenylalanine metabolism; tryptophan metabolism(Fig. 3G1-G3). Using KEGG annotation data for differential metabolites, five significantly enriched KEGG metabolic pathways were selected. All differential metabolites within these pathways underwent clustering analysis to investigate the variation patterns of substance content in potentially important metabolic pathways across different groups. The differential metabolite clustering heatmap of KEGG pathways clearly shows that metabolite accumulation levels in HR and MR were significantly higher than in HS (Fig. 3H1-H3).

Transcript abundance differences among quinoa cultivars with different resistance

Transcript abundance profiling of quinoa cultivars with different resistance was performed to screen hub genes and dissect molecular resistance against S. exigua. PCC | r |≥ 0.88 indicates strong correlation of the sample (Fig. 5A). 1094(574 up − and 520 down − regulated), 5324 (3505 up − and 1819 down − regulated), 4251 (3037 up − and 1214 down − regulated) genes were identified in HRvs.MR, HRvs.HS, and MRvs.HS respectively(Fig. 5B, Table S10-S12). Cluster analysis of transcript abundance showed that HR and MR had similar high expression of resistance genes, while HS had the opposite expression (Fig. 5C). To investigate the expression level of genes under different treatment conditions, K-means clustering was used to analyze 2 different expression trends (Subclass 1, 2466 DEGs and Subclass 2, 3901 DEGs) (Fig. 5D). Subclass1 clustering genes obviously showed high expression of constitutive and induced resistance in quinoa. The Venn diagram of differentially expressed genes identified 214 shared DEGs. The numbers of unique DEGs were 208 in HR vs. MR, 1438 in HR vs. HS, and 633 in MR vs. HS. The number of differentially expressed genes was markedly higher in comparisons between resistant cultivars and the susceptible cultivar (Fig. 5E). Plant hormone signal transduction, alpha linolenic acid metabolism, and phenylpropanoid biosynthesis were the most abundant common pathway gene in different resistant quinoa (Fig. 5F1-3). These findings were consistent with the results of K-means clustering analysis and further confirm that defense pathways were more pronounced in resistant cultivars than in susceptible ones. Collectively, CqMYC2L1 and CqMYC2L2 are potentially involved in JA-mediated plant hormone signal transduction. Matching the differential genes to the KOG database and plotting the bar graph (Fig. 5G), it is obvious that these defense functions contain significantly different numbers of differential genes in different resistant quinoa.

Identification of Arabidopsis transgenic plants

PCR amplification was utilized to detect transgenic Arabidopsis positive seedlings, and the results showed that the genes CqMYC2L1 and CqMYC2L2 had been integrated into the genome of transgenic Arabidopsis positive seedlings (Figure S9). When the transgenic Arabidopsis was flowering, two 2nd-instar S. exigua were placed on wild-type Arabidopsis and transgenic Arabidopsis leaves to feed randomly for 15 min (Figure S10A). The leaf feeding area of S.exigua larvae was significantly lower in CqMYC2L1 and CqMYC2L2 overexpressing Arabidopsis lines compared with wild-type (WT) and control transgenic lines (LOC110693510 and LOC110687209) (Figure S10B-C). After 15 min of feeding, the average leaf damaged area of WT and control lines was substantially larger, while almost no obvious feeding holes were observed on CqMYC2L1 and CqMYC2L2 overexpression leaves (Figure S10D-E). Furthermore, after continuous feeding treatment, the S. exigua larvae fed on WT and control Arabidopsis leaves exhibited higher body weight gain and better growth vigor. In contrast, larvae feeding on CqMYC2L1 and CqMYC2L2 transgenic leaves showed obviously inhibited growth, slower weight increase, and reduced activity. Correspondingly, the larval survival rate was significantly decreased on CqMYC2L1 and CqMYC2L2 overexpression plants under continuous insect infestation.

These newly supplemented quantitative results confirm that the heterologous overexpression of CqMYC2L1 and CqMYC2L2 significantly enhances plant resistance against S. exigua by restricting larval feeding, suppressing larval growth, and reducing larval survival.

To further determine the functionalities of CqMYC2L1 and CqMYC2L2 genes, targeted analysis was conducted on undamaged leaves, mechanically damaged leaves, and leaves fed by S.exigua of wild-type Arabidopsis and two transgenic Arabidopsis (Fig. 10A). We propose that overexpressing CqMYC2L1 and CqMYC2L2 elevates JA production in Arabidopsis to strengthen resistance to S. exigua.

Discussion

Quinoa is renowned worldwide for its high nutritional value and plasticity to adapt to harsh conditions [53]. The characteristics of quinoa that some quinoa cultivars can avoid or mitigate the damage and the ability to compensate after damage in the case of more severe insect damage. Although S. exigua poses a threat to various crops, the degree of preference and harm varies among different crops, and the presence of secondary metabolites is a key factor determining whether larvae prefer to feed on them [54]. After being attacked by herbivorous insects, plants undergo a series of adaptive responses in terms of physiology, biochemistry, and morphology, which are manifested as changes in nutrients, secondary metabolites, toxic substances, and defense proteins, thus adversely affecting growth, development, and reproduction of conspecific or heterospecific insects [55, 56]. The growth-defense tradeoff principle governs how plants adjust their growth and defense strategies in response to external factors, thereby influencing interactions among plants, herbivorous insects, and their natural enemies [57, 58]. In this study, we utilized broad-targeted metabolomics, volatile metabolomics, phytohormones, and transcriptomics to analyze the response of different resistant quinoa to feeding by S. exigua and identify resistance-related genes.

Plants induced by adverse factors such as insect feeding, mechanical damage, adversity stress, and chemical stimulation to form multiple defense mechanisms to protect themselves [59, 60]. Research has shown that the changes in the types and contents of secondary metabolites and defense enzymes are different in plants after mechanical damage and pest induction [61]. Pest stress induces changes in the activity of plant defense enzymes, generating defense related substances and responding to pest stress, and defense enzymes of resistant cultivars respond faster than those of susceptible cultivars, with higher levels of enzyme activity and the more rapid the increase in activity [9, 62]. The defense response mode of S. exigua and the mechanical damage response mode overlap, and changes in the types and contents of secondary metabolites and defense enzymes in quinoa are different after mechanical damage and pest induction. Plant responsiveness to biotic stress requires the redistribution of metabolic resources and nutrients, which comes at the cost of energy expenditure and impaired plant development. Changes in the types and contents of secondary metabolites and defense enzymes were different in different quinoa after induction by mechanical damage and feeding by S. exigua. Measurements of defensive enzyme (SOD, LOX, ACO, PPO, POD, TPS, PAL, CAT) activities and secondary metabolite (total flavonoids, tannins, total alkaloids, total phenols, total amino acids) contents in quinoa with differential resistance revealed that S. exigua feeding induced dramatic shifts in defensive compounds relative to intact leaves. High- and medium-resistant quinoa cultivars possessed significantly greater enzyme activity and secondary metabolite abundance than highly susceptible materials. When different quinoa were fed by S. exigua for 15 min, the defense response was most obvious, and the changes in enzyme activity and secondary metabolite content induced by mechanical damage were similar to those of S. exigua fed for 5 min. There are significant differences in the activity of detoxifying enzymes (PO, CES, GST, MFO, POD, SOD) in the body of S. exigua after feeding on different quinoa, and the strength of the activity was consistent with its sensitivity. S. exigua preferred feeding on susceptible (HS) quinoa rather than resistant (HR/MR) cultivars. Larval weight gain rate was highest on HS (0.19%) and lowest on HR (0.09%). Resistant quinoa accumulated more defensive metabolites and JA/JA-Ile, exerting toxic and antifeedant effects. These metabolites induced strong larval detoxification responses, causing metabolic burdens and inhibiting pest growth and feeding, while HS failed to trigger effective anti-pest defense.

Metabolomic and transcriptomic analyses of different resistant quinoa showed that resistant (healthy) quinoa had a significant competitive advantage when competing with susceptible (damaged) quinoa. Within a few minutes after insect attack, plants begin to synthesize the hormone JA, and the induced defense of leaves is regulated by JA plant hormones and protected by JA induced defense, which coordinates the defense response against insect herbivores [31–33, 63], whereas chewing insects can cause damage to plant tissues and activate the JA signaling pathway in plants [64, 65]. Plants exhibit various defense characteristics against herbivores, which are constitutively expressed or induced defenses against insects at the time of attack, and are only displayed or enhanced after herbivore attack [66]. In this study, the accumulation of JA in the two resistant cultivars was significantly higher than that in the susceptible cultivars, while the changes in the feeding dynamics of quinoa against S. exigua showed a significant enrichment of plant hormone signal transduction and alpha linolenic acid metabolism, which verified the activation of JA signaling in response to S. exigua defense, which is in agreement with the previous study. MYC2 is a key transcription factor regulates JA signaling genes with conserved functions in plants [67, 68]. MYC2 is a positive regulator of the JA signaling pathway, while transcription factor JAZ can negatively regulate the JA signaling pathway by inhibiting MYC2 [36, 69–72]. Similar to AtMYC2, OsMYC2 enhances the entire JA biosynthesis pathway in plants through JA recognition to activate JA signals [73, 74], while plants with impaired or perceptual defects in JA biosynthesis were severely affected by the accumulation of defensive secondary metabolites, and severely weakened the plant resistance to herbivorous insects [75–80], indicating that JAs modulates the synthesis of pest-induced secondary metabolites.

MYC2 is a major regulatory factor in the JA signaling pathway, which regulates the transcription and expression of downstream target genes through transcriptional regulation; MYC2 regulates secondary metabolic pathways in response to the JA pathway and has relatively conserved functions across species [81]. In this study, we identified the transcription factor MYC2 gene as a regulatory gene for quinoa's resistance to S. exigua and a major regulator in the JA signaling pathway of different resistant quinoa through the combined transcriptomic and metabolomic analyses of different resistant quinoa and S. exigua feeding dynamics. Gene family identification combined with bioinformatics methods and transcriptome data analysis preliminarily determined that MYC2 may be a homologous gene with CqMYC2L1 and CqMYC2L2, performing similar functions and participating in the response to JA pathway; JA has a high accumulation in HR and MR, and is significantly higher than that in HS. The transcriptome data was reintegrated and analyzed, and CqMYC2L1 and CqMYC2L2 were expressed differently and down-regulated in different resistant quinoa; the transcription and expression of the CqMYC2L1 and CqMYC2L2 genes may be the major regulators in the JA signaling pathway. MYC2 protein was demonstrated to be a nuclear localized protein in tobacco cells and Arabidopsis plant cells with transient expression [82]. By constructing the GFP fusion expression vector pBWA(V)HS-GFP, subcellular localization identified that the proteins encoded by CqMYC2L1 and CqMYC2L2 were both located in the nucleus, proving that CqMYC2L1 and CqMYC2L2 are expressed and function in the nucleus, which is also their basic characteristic as transcription factors. The dual luciferase assay demonstrated that CqMYC2L1 has transcriptional activity and the ability to regulate gene transcription.

JA is highly present under stress with MYC2 targeted transcription factors directly regulating the transcription of late defense genes. In this study, CqMYC2L1 and CqMYC2L2 were significantly expressed in high resistant quinoa to resist the feeding of S. exigua. The higher resistance to Manduca sexta folivory in Ah24 overexpressing transgenic tobacco plants [83]. By heterologously expressing CqMYC2L1 and CqMYC2L2 from quinoa in Arabidopsis, transgenic Arabidopsis showed significant induction of CqMYC2L1 (4.15-fold) and CqMYC2L2 (4.13-fold) compared to wild-type Arabidopsis, consistent with the trend of JAs (JA, 12-OPDA, JA-Ile) accumulation, indicating that CqMYC2L1 and CqMYC2L2 were successfully inserted into the Arabidopsis genome and expressed normally. Compared with wild-type Arabidopsis, transgenic Arabidopsis showed significant accumulation of total alkaloids CqMYC2L1 (2.08-fold) and CqMYC2L2 (1.97-fold), total phenolic acids CqMYC2L1 (1.69-fold) and CqMYC2L2 (1.60-fold), and lipids CqMYC2L1 (1.21-fold) and CqMYC2L2 (1.19-fold) to varying degrees. Previous transcriptome data showed that CqMYC2L1 and CqMYC2L2 were closely related to alkaloids, phenolic acids, and lipid substances. Exogenous application of JA rapidly induces the expression and activity of CqMYC2L1 and CqMYC2L2, and the expression level of CqMYC2L1 encoded proteins was verified to be an effective expression target protein by WB. CqMYC2L1 diverge in transcription regulation and induced expression; CqMYC2L1 represses transcription to form JA negative feedback, balancing defense cost and sustaining stable anti-insect resistance. Consistent multi-omics, dual-luciferase, heterologous transformation and WB data all support this regulatory model. These results indicate that CqMYC2L1 in quinoa regulates JAs mediated defense response, while combining alkaloids, phenolic acids, and lipids to resist feeding by S. exigua. While attempting to establish a genetic transformation system for quinoa to achieve gene editing, based on the fact that most plant viruses can cause local and systemic infections of quinoa [84], developing a gene function verification system based on VIGS (Virus Induced Gene Silencing) may also become a hot topic in molecular genetic research of quinoa.

Conclusions

This study utilizes three different resistant quinoa as materials, through physiological indicators, metabolomics, hormone profiling, and transcriptome analysis indicate that the defense enzyme activity and secondary metabolite content in resistant quinoa were significantly higher than those in highly sensitive quinoa. JAs, total alkaloids, total phenolic acids, and lipids were key differential metabolites, while CqMYC2L1 was key regulatory genes for resistance to S. exigua feeding in resistant cultivars. The roles of metabolites and core genes were validated in transgenic Arabidopsis through overexpression of CqMYC2L1. These results effectively demonstrate that CqMYC2L1 regulates the defense response mediated by JAs, while combining alkaloids, phenolic acids, and lipids to resist the feeding of S. exigua. Omics and transgenic data indicate a close correlation between JA and CqMYC2L1. As a transcriptional repressor acting downstream of JA signaling, whether CqMYC2L1 directly regulates JA biosynthetic genes requires further experimental validation. Development of a VIGS-based functional verification system for resistance genes will become a key research subject in quinoa molecular genetics. The findings of this study offer new clues to facilitate the breeding of insect-resistant quinoa cultivars, and subsequent field trials will be necessary to confirm their practical value in actual breeding practice.
