Ursolic acid alleviates high starch induced hepatic glucose and lipid metabolism disorders in largemouth bass (Micropterus salmoides) via the CERK/PI3K/AKT signaling pathway
Authors: Xiwen Zhang, Ming Wang, Shuang Wu, Qi Luo, Shuning Jin, Xiang Li, Yue Yin, Yongfang Wang, Yurong Zhao, Hongquan Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Disorders of hepatic glucose and lipid metabolism are a significant factor reducing the economic value of largemouth bass. Ursolic acid (UA) has been shown to regulate such metabolic disorders. This study aimed to investigate the core mechanism of UA in hepatic glucose and lipid metabolism in largemouth bass fed a high starch (HS) diet. A total of 800 healthy largemouth bass with an initial average body weight of 11.00 ± 0.12 g were selected and randomly allocated into four treatment groups in a 2 × 2 factorial design: control (CON) group, UA group, HS group, and HS + UA group. Each group had five replicates with 40 fish per replicate. Fish in the CON group received a basal diet (7.38% starch); the UA group received the basal diet supplemented with UA at 500 mg/kg; the HS group received the HS diet (13.26% starch); and the HS + UA group received the HS diet supplemented with UA at 500 mg/kg. The experiment lasted for 8 weeks. To further validate the direct regulatory effect of UA on the CERK/PI3K/AKT signaling pathway in hepatocytes, this study established an in vitro primary hepatocyte culture model containing five groups: CON, high-glucose (HG), HG + UA, HG + NVP231 (CERK inhibitor), and HG + UA + NVP231. Results showed that the HS diet led to reduced final weight, weight gain ratio and body crude protein content, increased body ether extract content and hepatic collagen area (P < 0.05), while UA intervention reversed these adverse changes in bass (P < 0.05). Transcriptomic sequencing identified cerk and b4galnt1b as key genes regulating hepatic glucose and lipid metabolism, enriched in the glycosphingolipid biosynthesis-ganglio series pathway. Compared with the CON group, hepatic b4galnt1b expression was decreased, while cerk expression was increased in the HS group, and these expression trends were reversed by UA treatment (P < 0.05). Furthermore, UA increased hepatic p-PI3K and p-AKT1 protein levels, and reduced serum triacylglycerol and cholesterol contents (P < 0.05), while improved serum insulin contents, and hepatic phosphofructokinase (PFK) activity (P < 0.05). It also down-regulated hepatic il-1β and tnf-α expression and up-regulated il-10 expression (P < 0.05). In vitro experiments showed that 25 mmol/L glucose induction enhanced cell viability but increased triacylglycerol content and phosphoenolpyruvate carboxykinase activity while decreasing PFK activity (P < 0.05). Treatment with 1 μmol/L UA further improved cell viability, ameliorated these metabolic indicators, and enhanced p-AKT1, AKT1, and p-PI3K protein expression (P < 0.05). The CERK inhibitor NVP231 was applied, the expression of p-PI3K, p-AKT1, and AKT1 was suppressed (P < 0.05), and UA could no longer activate this pathway under HG conditions. In summary, the UA alleviates HS induced hepatic glucose and lipid metabolism disorders in largemouth bass main via the CERK/PI3K/AKT signaling pathway.

Introduction

Largemouth bass (Micropterus salmoides), is a freshwater carnivorous fish native to North America. Due to its outstanding qualities, it has rapidly become one of the backbone species freshwater aquaculture (Liu et al., 2025). It is highly popular among consumers for its delicious taste, firm texture, and low intramuscular bone content, leading to high market acceptance (Liu et al., 2025). It not only serves as an important source of income for farmers but also meets consumer demand for high-quality protein. Despite the large scale of the largemouth bass industry, farming faces a series of challenges, many of which are closely related to liver health (Zhong et al., 2022). As a carnivorous fish, it has high dietary protein requirements and a limited ability to utilize carbohydrates. Usually, when the intake of starch exceeds 10%, it can cause disorders in hepatic glucose and lipid metabolism in bass, leading to hepatic steatosis and a series of related problems (Li et al., 2020). From a molecular mechanism perspective, a high carbohydrate load first leads to postprandial persistent hyperglycemia, which stimulates excessive insulin secretion and ultimately induces insulin resistance. In the state of insulin resistance, the capacity for glucose uptake and glycogen synthesis mediated by the PI3K/AKT (The full names of the abbreviations of all genes and proteins can be found in supplementary file abbreviations and full names of genes and proteins) signaling pathway is diminished (Reytor-González et al., 2025). Excess glucose that is not effectively utilized strongly drives the de novo lipogenesis pathway in the liver by activating transcription factors such as carbohydrate response element binding protein and sterol regulatory element binding protein 1c. This leads to the upregulation of key enzyme genes, including fasn and acc1α, resulting in substantial triacylglycerol production. Concurrently, insulin resistance also suppresses the activity of PPARα and the expression of its downstream target genes, cpt-1a, thereby diminishing fatty acid β-oxidation. This dual hit of increased synthesis and decreased degradation is the core molecular event leading to the abnormal accumulation of lipids in hepatocytes, known as hepatic steatosis (Rui, 2014). Sustained lipid overload and metabolic stress further activate inflammatory signaling pathways within the liver, such as the nuclear factor kappa-B (NF-κB) pathway. This results in the upregulation of pro-inflammatory cytokines like tnf-α and il-1β, and the downregulation of anti-inflammatory cytokines such as il-10, thereby inducing liver inflammation. Chronic metabolic dysregulation and the inflammatory microenvironment can activate the caspase cascade via the mitochondrial pathway (e.g., BAX/BCL-2 imbalance) or death receptor pathways. This ultimately leads to hepatocyte apoptosis, exacerbates liver injury, and may even progress to liver fibrosis (Cheng et al., 2025).

For cost control and feed processing reasons, artificial compound feeds for largemouth bass typically contain a certain proportion (20%-30%) of carbohydrates (e.g., starch) as a cheap energy source and binder. After consuming this high-carbohydrate feed, largemouth bass experience a rapid rise in blood glucose levels, stimulating the pancreas to secrete large amounts of insulin (Li et al., 2020). However, due to its metabolic system being ill-adapted to handling large sugar loads, cells become less sensitive to insulin, leading to insulin resistance (Liao et al., 2024). This means glucose cannot be effectively taken up and utilized by peripheral tissues like muscle and fat. To lower blood glucose, the liver is forced to become the primary site for processing the excess glucose. Glucose is converted into liver glycogen for storage, but the liver's glycogen storage capacity is limited. Glucose exceeding this capacity is largely converted into triacylglycerol (TG) in the liver via the de novo lipogenesis pathway (Rui, 2014). Concurrently, insulin resistance inhibits fatty acid β-oxidation (breakdown for energy), leading to a situation where fatty acid synthesis far exceeds breakdown. The large amount of synthesized triglycerides cannot be transported out of the liver in time as very low-density lipoproteins, leading to their abnormal accumulation within hepatocytes, resulting in hepatic steatosis and even fibrosis (Rui, 2014; Zhang et al., 2024). Furthermore, the liver is a vital immune organ. Fatty liver disease leads to a significant reduction in non-specific immune functions. With the immune system suppressed, the fish's resistance plummets, making them highly susceptible to secondary bacterial (e.g., Nocardia and Aeromonas) and viral infections (Cheng et al., 2025). This creates a typical syndrome of liver disease-body ulceration-mortality, causing large-scale deaths and representing one of the primary sources of economic loss in farming.

Ursolic acid (UA) is a natural pentacyclic triterpenoid compound widely found in apple peels, bearberry leaves, and other plants (Sandhu et al., 2023). Research has shown that UA improves insulin sensitivity by activating the PI3K/AKT insulin signaling pathway, promoting glucose uptake (He et al., 2014; Wang et al., 2021). It also activates the AMPK pathway, inhibiting key fatty acid synthesis enzymes and promoting fatty acid β-oxidation (Cheng et al., 2020). Additionally, the UA enhances the activity of antioxidant enzymes via the NRF2 pathway and suppresses the NF-κB pathway to alleviate inflammatory responses (Wang et al., 2023). Therefore, this study hypothesize that through these multi-target actions, dietary UA supplementation can effectively improve hepatic glucose and lipid metabolic homeostasis in largemouth bass, alleviate hepatic steatosis and inflammation, and thereby mitigate nutritional fatty liver disease, enhancing the fish's health status and farming performance. Preliminary research identified 500 mg/kg as a suitable dietary level of UA for largemouth bass under normal feeding conditions, demonstrating benefits for growth performance, body indices, and gut microbiota structure (Wang et al., 2024). In this study, to investigate the role of UA in a high starch (HS) model of largemouth bass, a fatty liver model was induced by supplementing the basal diet with HS (13.26%). This starch concentration was determined based on previous research reported to effectively induce glucose-lipid metabolism disorders in largemouth bass (Chen et al., 2023). In addition, 500 mg/kg of UA was supplemented as a treatment intervention, using a 2 × 2 factorial experimental design. Utilizing transcriptomics technology and largemouth bass primary hepatocyte culture techniques, this study aims to elucidate the molecular mechanisms by which UA regulates glucose and lipid metabolism in bass. The research is intended to provide scientific guidance and a theoretical foundation for solving the industry problems faced by largemouth bass farming enterprises.

Materials and methods

Animal ethics statement

All experimental procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Ethics Committee of Hunan Agricultural University, Changsha, China (approval number: 2024 - 00125, 5 December 2024), and fish welfare was rigorously maintained.

Animal experiment

The UA (purity ≥ 60%) was supplied by Hunan Jinhan Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd., Changsha, Hunan, China and 800 healthy largemouth bass were provided by Zhengda Aquatic Products Co., Ltd., Wuhan, Hubei, China. Prior to the formal experiment, the fish were acclimated for two weeks while being fed a basal diet. Thereafter, 800 healthy fish of uniform size (initial body weight 11.00 ± 0.12 g) were randomly selected and assigned to four groups under a completely randomized 2 × 2 factorial design, with five replicates per group. Fish from each group were stocked in cages (1.5 m × 1.5 m × 1.0 m), with 40 fish per cage. The experimental groups were as follows: control (CON) group, UA group, HS group, and HS + UA group. Fish in the CON group received a basal diet (7.38% starch); the UA group received the basal diet supplemented with UA at 500 mg/kg; the HS group received the HS diet (13.26% starch); and the HS + UA group received the HS diet supplemented with UA at 500 mg/kg. The fish were fed their respective diets for 8 weeks with feed formulations tailored to the nutritional requirements of largemouth bass. The ingredients and nutrient levels of the diet is presented in Table 1. During the trial, the daily feeding ration was adjusted according to weather conditions and the feeding behavior of the fish, at a rate ranging from 3% to 5% of the total fish body weight. Feeding was conducted twice daily at 06:30 and 16:30. Daily feeding records and mortality were documented. Sample collection

After completing the 8-week aquaculture trial, fish were fasted for 24 h. The fish in each cage were weighed and counted to obtain growth performance data. Prior to sampling, fish were anesthetized with 100 mg/L MS-222 (Yuan et al., 2024). After rapid collection from the tail vein, blood samples were allowed to clot at 4 °C for 2 h before centrifugation at 4 °C and 860 × g for 10 min. The serum was separated and transferred into 1.5-mL sterile enzyme-free tubes and stored at −80 °C for biochemical measurements. Following dissection, liver tissue near the common bile duct was rapidly extracted and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for histological analysis. Additional liver tissues were placed in RNA-free tubes, rapidly frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at −80 °C for biochemical measurements, transcriptome sequencing, real-time quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR) analysis, and protein level determination. The foregut tissue was also collected and stored at −80 °C for gene expression analysis and biochemical measurements. Five bass of approximately average body weight were randomly selected from each group for body composition analysis.

Composition measurement

Moisture content was determined using a forced-air drying oven (DHG-9203A, Shanghai Yiheng Biological Instrument Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China) in accordance with GB/T 6435-2014 (China National Standard, 2014), crude protein was determined by the Kjeldahl method GB/T 6432-2018 (China National Standard, 2018) using a semi-automatic Kjeldahl nitrogen analyzer (K9840, Hanon Future Technology Group Co., Ltd., Jinan, Shandong, China), crude fat was determined according to GB/T 6433-2006 (China National Standard, 2006), and crude ash was determined according to GB/T 6438-2007 (China National Standard, 2007). The organic matter content can be obtained by subtracting the crude ash from the raw weight. The starch content in diets was calculated based on the Chinese Feed Composition and Nutritional Value Table (China Feed Database, 2020). The gross energy of diets was determined using a fully automatic calorimeter (SDACM3100, Hunan Sundy Science and Technology Co., Ltd., Changsha, Hunan, China) with reference to GB/T 45104-2024 (China National Standard, 2024).

Growth performance and body composition

Growth performance indices included initial weight (IW), final weight (FW), weight gain ratio (WGR), specific growth ratio (SGR), feed conversion ratio (FCR), survival ratio (SR), and feed intake ratio (FI). Calculations for FI followed the methods reported by Wang et al. (2024) and Huang et al. (2025). All indices were calculated according to the formulas below:WGR(%)=[FW(g)–IW(g)]/IW(g)×100;SGR(%/d)=[FW(g)–IW(g)]/Numberofdaysinrearing×100;FCR=Totalfeedintake(g)/[FW(g)–IW(g)];SR(%)=Finalnumberofanimals/Initialnumberofanimals×100;IF(%/d)=100×Feedconsumed/[(IW(g)+FW(g)/2]/Days.

Additionally, the main indicators measured in whole-body composition analysis of the bass included crude protein, ether extract, and crude ash, and the determination methods were the same as those described in section 2.4.

Biochemical analysis of serum and liver

The concentrations of serum cholesterol (TC; A11111), TG (A11011), insulin (H20311), and glucagon (H18312) were measured using a microplate reader (Infinite M PLEX, T Tecan Austria GmbH, Grödig, Austria). The activities of hepatic phosphoenolpyruvate carboxykinase (PEPCK; A13111) and phosphofructokinase (PFK; A12911), as well as intestinal amylase (AMS; C01611), were also determined. All assay kits were provided by the Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute in China, Nanjing, Jiangsu, China and the procedures were carried out in strict accordance with the manufacturer's instructions.

Liver histology analysis

Liver tissue samples from largemouth bass were fixed in a pre-prepared 4% paraformaldehyde solution. After 48 h of fixation, the specimens were trimmed and then dehydrated through a graded ethanol series, cleared, and embedded in paraffin using an embedding station. Tissue sections were prepared at a thickness of 5 μm using a microtome (HistoCore BIOCUT, Leica Biosystems Nussloch GmbH, Nussloch, Germany). The sections were stained using hematoxylin and eosin (H&E), periodic acid-Schiff (PAS), Oil Red O, and Masson's trichrome methods before being mounted. Following preparation, the sections were observed and photographed under a microscope (Eclipse E100, Nikon Corporation, Tokyo, Japan) at 100 × or 400 × magnification. Statistical analysis was performed using Image-Pro Plus 6.0 software (Media Cybernetics, Rockville, MD, USA) to measure the area positive for Oil Red O staining and the relative area of collagen fibers within the liver sections. All staining reagent kits were supplied by the Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute in China, Nanjing, Jiangsu, China, and all staining procedures were performed strictly in accordance with the manufacturer's protocols.

Transcriptome sequencing

Bass liver tissues (n = 3 per group) were homogenized in liquid nitrogen, and total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent (15596026, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), followed by DNase I digestion to remove genomic DNA contamination. RNA integrity was confirmed by 1% agarose gel electrophoresis, while purity and concentration were assessed using a NanoPhotometer spectrophotometer (N-568-S, Implen GmbH, Munich, Germany), a Qubit 2.0 Fluorometer (Q32866, Life Technologies Corporation, Carlsbad, CA, USA) and an Agilent 2100 bioanalyzer (G2939A, Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA). Poly(A) + mRNA was enriched with oligo (dT) magnetic beads and randomly fragmented. First- and second-strands of cDNA were synthesized from the fragmented mRNA. The double-stranded cDNA underwent end repair, A-tailing, and adapter ligation. cDNA fragments of approximately 370 to 420 bp were selected for PCR amplification to construct the sequencing library. The constructed libraries were quantified and quality-controlled using the Qubit 2.0 (Q32866, Life Technologies Corporation, Carlsbad, CA, USA) Agilent 2100 bioanalyzer (G2939A, Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA), and RT-qPCR (LightCycler480II, Roche Diagnostics GmbH, Rotkreuz, Zug, Switzerland). Qualified libraries were sequenced on the Illumina HiSeq 4000 platform (HiSeq 4000 Sequencing System, Illumina, Inc., San Diego, CA, USA) to generate 150 bp paired-end reads. The clean reads from each sample were aligned to the largemouth bass reference genome using HISAT2. Gene expression levels were quantified, and differential expression analysis was performed using DESeq2. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were defined as those with a P-value < 0.05 and an absolute log2(fold change) > 1. Finally, the Gene Ontology (GO) functional and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analyses of the DEGs were conducted using ClusterProfiler.

Culture of primary hepatocytes from largemouth bass

Healthy largemouth bass were selected. After being fasted for 24 h, the fish were immersed in a 0.01% potassium permanganate solution for 30 min, and the body surface was disinfected with 75% ethanol. Following decapitation by cutting the gill arches and exsanguination, the fish were placed in a laminar flow cabinet. Under sterile conditions, the liver tissue was excised using forceps and surgical scissors and transferred to a Petri dish. The tissue was washed five times with pre-cooled phosphate buffered saline (PBS) buffer containing 200 IU/mL penicillin-streptomycin-amphotericin B (a triple antibiotic solution). The tissue was then minced into fragments of approximately 1 mm3 and transferred to T25 culture flasks. Sufficient 0.25% trypsin was added to cover the tissue fragments (approximately at a 1:3 volume ratio of tissue to trypsin). Digestion was carried out in a 28 °C incubator for 21 min, with gentle shaking every 5 min, and the process was monitored under a microscope (Axio Vert A1, Carl Zeiss AG, Oberkochen, Germany). The digestion was terminated by adding 1 to 2 mL of M199 complete medium (supplemented with 200 IU/mL of the triple antibiotic solution and 20% fetal bovine serum). The cell suspension was filtered through a sterilized 70 μm cell strainer into 50 mL centrifuge tubes and centrifuged at 100 × g for 10 min, after which the supernatant was discarded. The pellet was resuspended in PBS, mixed with a red blood cell lysis buffer at a 1:3 volume ratio, and centrifuged at 100 × g for 5 min. After discarding the supernatant, the pellet was washed twice with PBS by repeating the centrifugation step. Finally, the cell pellet was resuspended in M199 complete medium, and hepatocyte viability was assessed using the trypan blue exclusion method. Cell suspensions with a viability exceeding 90% were seeded into new T25 culture flasks for cultivation. The growth of primary hepatocytes was monitored daily, and the culture medium was replaced based on the observed cell density and conditions. For the formal experiments, stably passaged primary liver cells with normal growth characteristics were used.

Determining the suitable concentration for primary hepatocyte culture

A stock solution of D-glucose was prepared by dissolving 0.09 g of powder in 10 mL of M199 culture medium. After complete dissolution, the solution was sterilized by filtration through a 0.22-μm microporous filter and then diluted with M199 medium to obtain the required working concentrations of 0, 10, 20, 25, and 30 mmol/L. These high-glucose solutions were stored at 4 °C. Ursolic acid, with a molecular weight of 456.7 g/mol, was used to prepare a 10 mmol/L stock solution by dissolving 45.67 mg of powder in 10 mL of dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO). The stock solution was sterilized by filtration through a 0.22 μm filter, aliquoted into 1.5 mL microcentrifuge tubes, and stored at −20 °C. Working solutions (0, 1, 5, 10, and 20 μmol/L) were prepared by dilution at the time of use. NVP231, a CERK inhibitor with a molecular weight of 431.55 g/mol (Pastukhov et al., 2014), was prepared as a 5 mmol/L stock solution in DMSO according to the manufacturer's instructions. This stock solution was stored at −20 °C and later diluted to the required working concentrations (0.1, 0.5, 0.8, 1.0, and 2.0 μmol/L). Primary hepatocytes, obtained using the method described above, were seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 4 × 105 cells per well in a volume of 100 μL. For each concentration of glucose, UA and NVP231, at least six replicate wells were prepared. The plates were then incubated for 24 h. Cell viability was assessed using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8, G02111) colorimetric assay by Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China. Briefly, 10 μL of CCK-8 solution was added to each well, followed by a 2-h incubation. The absorbance was measured at a wavelength of 450 nm. The TG (A11011) content, as well as the enzymatic activities of PEPCK (A13111) and PFK (A12911), were measured using commercial assay kits purchased from the Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute in China, Nanjing, Jiangsu, China.

Hepatocyte hyperglycemic model construction

Primary liver cells (4 × 105 cells per well) were plated in 24-well plates with four replicates. Each well received 1 mL L-15/M199 complete medium for 24-h cultivation. The experiment was divided into: CON group, cells cultured in M199 medium for 24 h; HG group, cells exposed to 25 mmol/mL glucose for 24 h; HG + UA group, cells pre-treated with 1.0 μmol/mL UA for 4 h prior to 20-h stimulation with 25 mmol/mL glucose; HG + NVP231 group, cells pre-treated with 1.0 μmol/mL NVP231 for 3 h prior to 21-h stimulation with 25 mmol/mL glucose; HG + UA + NVP231 group, cells sequentially pre-treated with 1.0 μmol/mL UA (4 h) and 1.0 μmol/mL NVP231 (3 h), followed by 17-h stimulation with 25 mmol/mL glucose.

RT-qPCR

The expression of genes related to lipid metabolism and glucose metabolism in liver, gut, and cell were detected by RT-qPCR. Total RNA was extracted from the tissues using the RNA extraction kit (SteadyPure, Accurate Biotechnology (Hunan) Co., Ltd., Changsha, Hunan, China). The reverse transcription kit (Evo M-MLVA, Accurate Biotechnology (Hunan) Co., Ltd., Changsha, Hunan, China) was utilized to obtain cDNA, and the quantitative kit (AG11701, Accurate Biotechnology (Hunan) Co., Ltd., Changsha, Hunan, China) was used to detect the relative expression of genes (Zhang et al., 2025). Primers were synthesized in Beijing Qingke Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Beijing, China and their sequences can be found in Table 2. The reaction program was: pre-denaturation at 95 °C for 30 s, followed by 40 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 10 s and annealing at 60 °C for 30 s. The reaction system is shown in Table 3. Western blot

Western blot analysis was performed as described by Pu et al. (2022). Briefly, total protein was extracted from liver tissue and quantified using a BCA assay (A04542, Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute in China, Nanjing, Jiangsu, China). After denaturation, equal amounts of protein were separated by sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis and transferred to a polyvinylidene difluoride membrane. The membrane was blocked and then incubated with the primary antibodies at 4 °C overnight, followed by incubation with a horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibody at room temperature for 2 h. Protein bands were visualized using a chemiluminescence detection system (ChemiDoc, Bio-Rad Laboratories, Inc., Hercules, CA, USA) and quantified using AIWBwellTM software (Servicebio, Wuhan, Hubei, China). The following primary antibodies were used: β-actin (1:10,000; bs-0061R), p-PI3K (1:1000; bs-3332R), PI3K (1:1000; bs-0128R), p-AKT1 (1:1000; bs-0876R), and AKT1 (1:1000; bs-0115R), all from Beijing Biosynthesis Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Beijing, China. The secondary antibody was goat anti-rabbit immunoglobulin G-HRP (1:25,000; bs-0295G-HRP, Beijing Biosynthesis Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Beijing, China).

Statistical analysis

Excel 2023 was used to sort out the data, SPSS 26.0 software (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) was used for normal distribution test, and two-way analysis of variance of general linear model was used for data analysis. The model included two main effects of HS and UA and their interaction. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) and Duncan 's multi-range test were used for comparison between groups. The data graph is visualized using the GraphPad Prism 8 software (GraphPad Software, LLC., Boston, MA, USA). Transcriptomic data analysis was performed on the Biomarker Cloud Platform (www.biomarker.com.cn).

Data analysis was conducted using the statistical model for two-way ANOVA in SPSS 26.0. The calculation formula was as follows:Y=β0+β1A+β2B+β3(A×B)+ϵ,

among two-way ANOVA, Y represents the response variable; β0 represents the intercept term; β1, β2, and β3 represent the coefficients for the main effects of 2-level factor HS, 2-level factor UA, and their interaction (HS × UA), respectively; and ϵ represents the error term, which is assumed to follow ϵ ∼ N (0, σ2).

Data analysis was conducted using the statistical model for one-way ANOVA is expressed as follows:Yij=μ+αi+εij,where Yij represents the observed value of the dependent variable; μ represents the overall mean, αi represents the i-th treatment effect; εij represents the random error.

Analysis of liver histomorphology

The H&E staining showed normal hepatic histology in the CON and UA groups, characterized by centrally located nuclei. In contrast, the HS group exhibited pronounced nuclear displacement and vacuolation, which were alleviated by UA treatment (Fig. 1). A HS diet induced both nuclear displacement and vacuolation in hepatocytes. Treatment with UA reduced nuclear displacement and prevented vacuolation (Fig. 1A). Masson staining showed nuclear displacement in the livers of fish on the basal diet, but no tissue fibrosis. Dietary UA reduced nuclear displacement and also presented no hepatic fibrosis. The HS diet group exhibited nuclear displacement along with extensive hepatic fibrosis. The UA treatment decreased nuclear displacement and prevented fibrosis (Fig. 1B). Oil Red O staining indicated normal hepatic lipid accumulation in bass fed the standard control diet or the UA diet. The HS diet led to substantial lipid accumulation. In a HS diet, UA treatment reduced this lipid buildup (Fig. 1C). The PAS staining demonstrated increased glycogen content in the UA diet compared to the normal diet group, whereas it was reduced in the HS group (P < 0.05; Table 5 and Fig. 1D). Quantitative analysis of Oil Red O and Masson staining revealed that dietary UA reduced the areas of collagen deposition and oil accumulation. The UA treatment decreased both collagen area and oil area (P < 0.05; Table 5). Transcriptome sequencing analysis of differential genes in the liver

The DEGs were identified from expressed genes (fragments per kilobase per million mapped reads; FPKM > 1) based on a threshold of |log2 (fold change)| > 1 and an adjusted P-value < 0.05. The results showed that, compared to the CON group, the livers of bass in the HS group contained 346 DEGs, comprising 197 down-regulated and 149 up-regulated genes (Fig. S1). Compared to the HS group, the HS + UA group exhibited 342 DEGs, with 165 down-regulated and 177 up-regulated genes (Fig. S1). As shown in Table S1, the top 30 up-regulated genes in the liver of bass in the HS group compared to the CON group included alk, cerk, ephx1, lnx1, and samd11, while the top 30 down-regulated genes included abat, b4galnt1b, fads6, fahd2a, and fndc4a (Table S2). According to Table S3, the top 30 up-regulated genes in the HS group compared to the HS + UA group included aacs, b4galnt1b, cacna2d2a, cmtr1, and glb1, while the top 30 down-regulated genes included cerk, cspg4, fam163ab, flrt3, and fryb (Table S4).

GO enrichment analysis of liver transcriptomics

The GO enrichment analysis of the DEGs is summarized in Fig. S2. In the comparison between the CON and HS groups, the DEGs were significantly enriched in several terms. For biological process, these included nuclear chromosome segregation, lipid transport, and DNA integration. The cellular component terms were primarily protein complexes, virion components, the viral capsid, and the viral envelope. The molecular functions were dominated by structural molecule activity, ubiquitin-protein ligase activity, and serine-type endopeptidase activity. In the contrast between the HS and HS + UA groups, the DEGs were enriched in distinct functions. The biological processes included DNA metabolic process, DNA replication, immune response, and antigen processing and presentation. The cellular components featured membrane protein complexes and the plasma membrane region. The molecular functions included structural constituents of the extracellular matrix, fatty acyl-CoA binding, and acyl-CoA binding.

KEGG enrichment analysis of liver transcriptomics

The KEGG enrichment analysis of DEGs between CON group and HS group revealed significantly enriched pathways included steroid biosynthesis, biosynthesis of unsaturated fatty acids, fatty acid elongation, arginine biosynthesis, basic transcription factors, and glycosphingolipid biosynthesis ganglion series (Fig. S3). When comparing HS group with HS + UA group, the analysis identified significantly enriched pathways including glycosphingolipid biosynthesis ganglion series, DNA replication, glycosaminoglycan degradation, arginine biosynthesis, non-homologous end joining, thiamine metabolism, and galactose metabolism.

UA improved glucose metabolism in bass

Analysis of glucose metabolism-related indicators revealed that UA enhanced hepatic PFK activity and increased serum insulin concentration relative to the CON group (P < 0.05; Table 6). It also up-regulated the mRNA expression of intestinal glut2, glut4, and sglt1(P < 0.05; Fig. 3A), while increasing gut AMS activity (P = 0.002; Table 6). In contrast, the UA reduced hepatic PEPCK activity (P = 0.032; Table 6). Compared with the CON group, the HS group exhibited elevated hepatic PFK activity and serum glucagon levels in bass (P < 0.05; Table 6). Meanwhile, compared with the CON group, the HS group showed decreased serum insulin concentration (P < 0.001; Table 6), and increased mRNA expression of intestinal glut2 (P < 0.001; Fig. 3A). Discussion

This study employed both in vivo animal experiments and in vitro cell models to investigate the role and molecular mechanisms of UA in alleviating HS-diet-induced glycolipid metabolic disorders in largemouth bass. This study finding indicates that a HS diet (13.26%) reduced the growth rate of the fish. Chen et al. (2023) also reported a significant growth reduction in bass fed a HS diet (13.56%) for eight weeks, which is consistent with the results. It is noteworthy that glycolipid metabolism disorders, accompanied by inflammation, were observed in bass after only two weeks on the HS diet. However, Chen et al. (2023) reported that after eight weeks, the fish developed a self-repair mechanism, gradually restoring glycolipid metabolic balance and resolving inflammation, with liver histology showing no significant differences from the normal-diet group. This finding contrasts with the results. In this study, after eight weeks on the HS diet, histological analysis (H&E, Oil Red O, PAS, and Masson staining) clearly demonstrated hepatocyte vacuolization, abnormal lipid droplet accumulation, collagen fiber deposition, and decreased glycogen content. The bass in this experiment did not adapt to the adverse effects of the HS diet. The discrepancy with the study by Chen et al. (2023) may stem from differing rearing environments. Chen et al. (2023) maintained the bass under optimal growth conditions, whereas this experimental conditions were closer to practical aquaculture scenarios. Therefore, this study concludes that under practical production conditions, an 8-week HS diet induces glycolipid metabolism disorders and inflammation, consequently reducing the growth rate of largemouth bass. However, under optimal conditions, the possibility of self-repair and damage reversal cannot be ruled out, and the underlying mechanisms warrant further investigation. Furthermore, the UA supplementation was found to reverse the liver damage induced by the HS diet. Histological results showed reduced lipid accumulation, collagen deposition, and vacuolization, along with increased glycogen content, indicating that UA can regulate hepatic glycolipid metabolism in bass. Notably, while the study histology clearly shows pathological changes, employing transmission electron microscopy in future investigations would provide ultrastructural evidence, allowing for the observation of changes in organelles such as mitochondria (e.g., cristae integrity), endoplasmic reticulum (e.g., stress), and the number and morphology of lipid droplets, thereby revealing the subcellular sites of UA's protective action, offering a more comprehensive understanding of how UA preserves hepatic cellular homeostasis.

Furthermore, although the HS diet affected growth, no significant changes in FI were observed among the groups. This finding carries significant implications, as it suggests that the growth inhibition induced by the HS diet is not due to anorexia or reduced FI in the fish. The fish maintained similar levels of feed consumption throughout the experiment; therefore, the differences in growth performance are more likely attributable to the impact of the dietary composition itself on the organism's metabolism (Villasante et al., 2022). The disruption of glucose and lipid metabolism induced by the HS diet, particularly hepatic steatosis, inflammation, and insulin resistance, may lead to a reduction in net energy and nutrients available for growth. More energy is likely diverted to cope with metabolic stress and repair tissue damage, ultimately manifesting as stunted growth and reduced feed utilization efficiency (Rui, 2014; Villasante et al., 2022). The supplementation of UA ameliorated the growth inhibition and metabolic disorders caused by the HS diet without altering FI. This further confirms that UA exerts its growth-promoting and hepatoprotective effects by modulating the organism's internal metabolic homeostasis, rather than by stimulating appetite.

Additionally, a HS diet significantly altered the body composition of bass, manifesting as a significant decrease in body CP content and an increase in EE content. This phenomenon may be attributed to the physiological characteristics of carnivorous fish, which have a limited capacity to utilize carbohydrates. In carnivorous fish such as bass, excessive intake of starch (available carbohydrates) leads to persistent postprandial hyperglycemia. Due to the low efficiency of glucose utilization in fish (especially carnivorous species), excess glucose cannot be promptly oxidized for energy or stored as glycogen. Instead, it is converted into acetyl-CoA through glycolysis, subsequently entering the lipid synthesis pathway and resulting in increased body fat deposition. Concurrently, a HS diet may alter the balance between protein deposition and breakdown. Although a protein-sparing effect theoretically exists when non-protein energy (from starch) is excessively high, in carnivorous fish, an excessive carbohydrate load is often accompanied by liver damage or metabolic stress. This may instead inhibit protein synthesis or promote amino acid catabolism, ultimately leading to a relative decrease in body protein content. Under a normal dietary background, the addition of UA alone did not cause significant changes in any indicators. This suggests that under nutritionally balanced conditions without metabolic stress, exogenously added UA may not participate in key metabolic regulatory pathways, or the body may neutralize its effects through homeostatic mechanisms (Yu et al., 2024).

However, the UA supplementation in the HS context exhibited a significant mitigating effect. Compared to the HS group, the UA-treated group showed increased body CP content and decreased body EE content. This phenomenon reveals the potential metabolic regulatory role of UA under high carbohydrate load. The underlying mechanism may involve UA acting as a non-protein nitrogen source, which could modulate nitrogen balance to some extent, promoting nitrogen reutilization and thereby aiding body protein deposition. Alternatively, UA might activate specific signaling pathways (e.g., the AMPK pathway), promoting fatty acid oxidation and inhibiting fat synthesis, thus alleviating the excessive fat deposition induced by HS (Cheng et al., 2020). The main effect analysis further quantified the roles of the two factors: the overall effect of HS was to decrease protein and increase fat, whereas the overall effect of UA was to significantly reduce fat content. This result emphasizes the core role of UA in regulating fat metabolism. Importantly, this study observed a significant interaction effect for the crude lipid indicator. This interaction indicates that the fat-lowering effect of UA is not constant but depends on the dietary starch level. That is, the UA has little effect on body fat in normal-starch diets but can effectively inhibit fat accumulation under HS-induced excessive fat deposition. This interaction suggests that UA may function by intervening in metabolic pathways specific to a high-glucose environment, such as inhibiting the expression of sterol regulatory element-binding protein, thereby reducing de novo lipogenesis. In a normal glycemic state, this pathway is not over activated, and therefore, the effect of UA is not pronounced.

To further elucidate the core mechanism by which UA improves glycolipid metabolism in largemouth bass, this study employed transcriptomic sequencing to analyze hepatic gene expression. The key DEGs identified were cerk and b4galnt1b. Validation via RT-qPCR confirmed the accuracy of gene expression: compared to the CON group, the HS group showed a significant upregulation of cerk gene expression and a significant downregulation of b4galnt1b gene expression. Compared to the HS group, the HS + UA group exhibited a significant down-regulation of cerk and a significant up-regulation of b4galnt1b, which is consistent with the transcriptomic sequencing results. Dong et al. (2024) found that cerk expression is reactively up-regulated in acute liver injury, protecting hepatocytes and resisting oxidative damage. The specific mechanism involves up-regulation of cerk gene expression catalyzing the production of ceramide-1-phosphate (C1P). The C1P binds to the Kelch domain of Kelch-like ECH-associated protein 1 (KEAP1), promoting the dissociation of the KEAP1-NRF2 complex, leading to the translocation of NRF2 into the nucleus, where it exerts its antioxidant effects. Xia et al. (2022) discovered that inhibiting cerk expression can promote hepatic glycogen synthesis and improve insulin resistance. Therefore, whether UA's inhibition of high-starch-induced cerk gene expression in largemouth bass produces positive effects warrants further investigation. Furthermore, Dai et al. (2017) reported that deletion of the b4galnt1b gene can lead to body spasms in organisms, involving the glycosphingolipid biosynthesis and sphingolipid metabolism pathways. The b4galnt1b encodes beta-1,4-N-acetylgalactosaminyltransferase 1b, an enzyme involved in the synthesis of gangliosides (Schnaar, 2016). Gangliosides are essential sphingolipid components of the cell membrane and play a role in regulating various cellular functions, including insulin signaling and glucose transport (Monkhouse and Deane, 2024). Although their precise role in glycolipid metabolism is not yet fully understood, given that the glycosphingolipid biosynthesis pathway in which they participate is closely connected to the sphingolipid metabolism pathway involving CERK, changes in the expression of b4galnt1b may reflect alterations in the remodeling of cell membrane components and the signaling microenvironment, thereby indirectly affecting glycolipid metabolic homeostasis (Monkhouse and Deane, 2024; Schnaar, 2016). Consequently, this study further conducted KEGG and GO enrichment analysis on DEGs in the liver of largemouth bass. The results showed that compared to the CON group, the most significantly enriched pathways in the HS group were steroid biosynthesis, unsaturated fatty acid biosynthesis, fatty acid elongation, arginine biosynthesis, basal transcription factors, and glycosphingolipid biosynthesis-ganglio series. Compared to the HS group, the most significantly enriched pathways in the HS + UA group were glycosphingolipid biosynthesis-ganglio series, DNA replication, glycosaminoglycan degradation, arginine biosynthesis, non-homologous end joining, thiamine metabolism, and galactose metabolism. Comparative analysis revealed that glycosphingolipid biosynthesis - ganglio series was the most significantly enriched pathway common to both comparisons. Merrill (2011) reported that sphingolipids share a common structural feature: a backbone composed of a long-chain or sphingoid base, most of which are amide-linked to a long-chain or very-long-chain fatty acid to form ceramide (Cer). The Cer can be further derivatized by adding head groups to form more complex sphingolipids, such as sphingomyelin, glucosylceramide, galactosylceramide, and more complex glycosphingolipids. Dong et al. (2024) highlighted that CERK is the only known lipid kinase in mammalian cells that catalyzes the phosphorylation of CER to C1P. The CER is a substrate of this enzymatic reaction and plays a central role in sphingolipid metabolism and signal transduction pathways, regulating many fundamental cellular responses (Takahashi et al., 2019). The C1P, the product of this reaction, is also a bioactive lipid closely associated with cell function and fate. Therefore, this study speculates that CERK may play a significant regulatory role in the glycosphingolipid biosynthesis-ganglio series signaling pathway in largemouth bass.

Existing studies have revealed that CERK can act as an upstream regulator of the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway (Schwalm et al., 2020). Study have shown that UA can activate the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway (He et al., 2014). Once activated, this pathway can regulate systemic glucose and lipid metabolism and improve insulin resistance (He et al., 2014). Therefore, this research analyzed the expression of proteins related to the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway in the liver and found that UA up-regulates the expression levels of PI3K and AKT1 proteins and promotes their phosphorylation in the livers of bass fed a HS diet. However, the UA had no significant effect on PI3K and AKT1 protein levels in bass under a normal diet. This suggests that UA may regulate HS diet induced glycolipid metabolism disorders in bass via the phosphorylation of the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway. To further verify whether glycolipid metabolism in bass was improved, this study found that UA increased serum insulin and glucagon concentrations in bass and up-regulated the gene expression of intestinal glucose transporters (glut2, glut4, and sglt1) and the activity of the key glycolytic enzyme PFK. Simultaneously, it inhibited the activity of PEPCK, a rate-limiting enzyme in gluconeogenesis. This indicates that UA promotes glucose absorption and hepatic glycolysis while suppressing gluconeogenesis, thereby enhancing liver glycogen storage. This is consistent with the quantitative histological analysis showing a significant increase in glycogen content. To build a more comprehensive picture of glucose metabolic flux, future studies could also investigate the effects of UA on other key enzymes, such as glucokinase, which initiates glycolysis, pyruvate kinase, which controls the final step of glycolysis, and glucose 6-phosphatase and fructose-1,6-bisphosphatase, which, along with PEPCK, govern gluconeogenesis. Analyzing metabolites like pyruvic acid and lactic acid, along with the activity of lactate dehydrogenase, would further illuminate the fate of glucose carbon and the balance between aerobic and anaerobic metabolism.

In addition, the increase in serum insulin and glucagon concentrations by UA suggests a potential improvement in lipid metabolism in bass (Lafferty et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2021). Further analysis revealed that UA inhibits hepatic lipid synthesis by down-regulating the expression of genes such as acc1α and fasn. Concurrently, it strongly promotes fatty acid β-oxidation by up-regulating pparα and its target gene cpt-1a, thereby reducing liver TG content and restoring lipid metabolic balance. Additionally, this study found that a HS load not only induced metabolic disorders but also triggered a significant hepatic inflammatory response and apoptosis, characterized by the up-regulation of pro-inflammatory factors (il-1β and tnf-α) and pro-apoptotic genes (bax and caspase 3). Intervention with UA effectively reversed this trend and significantly increased the level of the anti-inflammatory factor il-10. This indicates that the benefits of UA extend beyond mere metabolic regulation. By inhibiting classical inflammatory pathways such as NF-κB and apoptotic signals, UA improved the hepatic immune microenvironment and enhanced the overall disease resistance of the fish. This provides a molecular basis for explaining the alleviation of the liver disease - body surface ulceration-death syndrome observed in production settings. Although this study focused on the PI3K/AKT pathway, the observed improvement in insulin sensitivity and glucose metabolism suggests a potential crosstalk with the insulin signaling pathway and its key components, such as the insulin receptor and insulin receptor substrate. Future work could explore whether UA's effects involve modulating the expression or phosphorylation status of insulin receptor and insulin receptor substrate proteins.

To further verify whether UA regulates glucose and lipid metabolism in bass liver through the CERK/PI3K/AKT signaling pathway, primary hepatocytes were isolated and cultured to test this hypothesis. This study found that glucose concentrations ranging from 0 to 25 mmol/L had no significant effect on the proliferation rate of primary hepatocytes, whereas 30 mmol/L significantly reduced it. Therefore, a high-glucose model was established using 25 mmol/L for subsequent experiments. Previous research has demonstrated that UA increases the mitotic index and the number of proliferating cell nuclear antigen-positive hepatocytes in mice, promoting hepatocyte proliferation and regeneration, thereby alleviating liver injury (Liu et al., 1998; Žaloudková et al., 2020). Consistent with the above, the cell viability of primary hepatocytes was significantly enhanced by 1 and 5 μmol/L UA, confirming that UA promotes hepatocyte proliferation. The NVP231, a targeted inhibitor of CERK, has been reported to have no significant effect on the viability of human breast cancer cells at 1 μmol/L while markedly suppressing CERK expression (Schwalm et al., 2020). Similarly, in this study, concentrations of NVP231 up to 1.0 μmol/L did not affect hepatocyte proliferation, but 2.0 μmol/L significantly inhibited it. Accordingly, a concentration of 1.0 μmol/L was selected for subsequent experiments.

Given that Schwalm et al. (2020) demonstrated NVP231 inhibits PI3K/AKT protein expression, and considering the PI3K/AKT pathway is a classic insulin downstream pathway involved in glucose transport, this study investigated its role in this study model. This study found that under high-glucose conditions, UA intervention suppressed the gluconeogenic key enzyme PEPCK and enhanced the glycolytic key enzyme PFK. Conversely, inhibition of CERK promoted gluconeogenesis, potentially reducing glycogen content. Critically, NVP231 intervention blocked UA's ability to ameliorate these glucose metabolic disorders. The RT-qPCR and Western blot analysis revealed that UA up-regulated the gene and phosphorylated protein expression of PI3K and AKT1 in the high-glucose model. Inhibition of CERK, however, reduced the phosphorylation of AKT1. No significant difference in PI3K and AKT1 expression was observed between the HG + UA and HG + UA + NVP231 groups. Dong et al. (2024) reported that CERK expression increases with the severity of acute liver injury and normalizes during recovery. Similarly, Presa et al. (2019) found elevated CERK transcript levels in high-fat-diet-fed mice, which were restored by the antioxidant vitamin E. These findings align with this study in vivo results in largemouth bass, where a HS diet induced metabolic disturbances and significantly elevated cerk expression, which was then normalized by UA intervention. This pattern suggests CERK may be subject to feedback regulation in vivo, being up-regulated to protect hepatocytes under metabolic stress. In the in vitro high-glucose model, UA increased cerk expression and activated the PI3K/AKT pathway. It concurrently up-regulated genes related to fatty acid β-oxidation (cpt-1α and pparα) and down-regulated genes involved in fatty acid synthesis (fasn and acc1a). This coordinated action suppressed TG accumulation and excessive gluconeogenesis, thereby alleviating hepatocyte damage. In contrast, the HG + UA + NVP231 group, with reduced cerk expression, exhibited disordered glucose and lipid metabolism and aggravated lipid deposition. This indicates that CERK inhibition abolished UA's lipid-lowering and insulin-sensitizing effects. In conclusion, the CERK is integral to UA's activation of the PI3K/AKT pathway to ameliorate glucose and lipid metabolic disorders in largemouth bass. Although b4galnt1b, as another core differential gene, has not been demonstrated in this study to directly participate in the CERK/PI3K/AKT axis, its role in the glycosphingolipid synthesis pathway suggests that UA may influence the sphingolipid composition of the cell membrane by regulating this gene, thereby creating a suitable membrane microenvironment for the action of cerk expression or the activation of PI3K/AKT signaling. Future studies will be necessary to further elucidate the precise role of b4galnt1b in this process and its potential interaction with cerk. These findings provide a new perspective on UA's mechanism of action and suggest that sphingolipid metabolism is a key research direction for regulating hepatic glucose and lipid metabolism in bass.

Conclusions

Supplementation of largemouth bass feed with 500 mg/kg UA improved growth performance and glucose and lipid metabolism. High-starch feeding reduced growth performance, disrupted glucose and lipid metabolism, and was accompanied by hepatic inflammation and apoptosis. The HS diet supplemented with 500 mg/kg UA ameliorated these disturbances in glucose and lipid metabolism, alleviated liver inflammation and apoptosis, and enhanced growth performance. Notably, these phenotypic changes were achieved through UA mediated activation of the CERK/PI3K/AKT signaling pathway.
