Logarithmic de Rham stacks and non-abelian Hodge theory
Michael Barz

Abstract
. In this article, we introduce the logarithmic de Rham stack of a pair ( X , D ) , (X,D), for X X a smooth variety over a field k k of positive characteristic p , p, and D D a strict normal crossings divisor on X . X. Using this stack, we prove a new version of logarithmic Cartier descent, and a new logarithmic non-abelian Hodge theorem for curves, both stated using a certain logarithmic Frobenius twist. Our logarithmic non-abelian Hodge theorem implies an earlier logarithmic non-abelian Hodge theorem of de Cataldo-Zhang [ 15 ] .

1.
Introduction

Fix a prime
p.
For
k
a field of characteristic
p,
and
X
a smooth variety over
k
with a strict normal crossings divisor
D,
we will introduce a stack
X^{\dR}(\log D),
which we dub the
logarithmic de Rham stack
of
(X,D).
The definition is straightforward; setting
\Theta=\mathbb{A}^{1}/\mathbb{G}_{m},
if
D
has
n
components, then we can encode the divisor
D
as a certain smooth morphism
c_{D}:X\to\Theta^{n}
(this will be explained in
Section 5
).

Definition 1.1
.

The
logarithmic de Rham stack

X^{\dR}(\log D)
of
(X,D)
is the relative de Rham stack
(X/\Theta^{n})^{\dR}
of
X
over
\Theta^{n}.
The notion of a de Rham stack relative to a general base will be made precise in
Section 2
.

de Rham stacks in characteristic 0 were first defined by Simpson
[
39
]
, as a way to ‘geometrize’ algebraic de Rham cohomology. That is, for
Y
a complex algebraic variety, Simpson defined a certain stack
f:Y^{\operatorname{Simpson-dR}}\to\Spec(\mathbb{C})
having the following two properties:

(1)

there is an equivalence of categories
\Qcoh(Y^{\operatorname{Simpson-dR}})\simeq\Conn(Y),
for
\Conn(Y)
the category of quasicoherent sheaves on
Y
endowed with a flat connection,

(2)

for each
q,
there are isomorphisms
R^{q}f_{*}\mathcal{O}_{Y^{\operatorname{Simpson-dR}}}\simeq H^{q}_{\dR}(Y,\mathbb{C})
between the coherent cohomology of
Y^{\operatorname{Simpson-dR}}
and the algebraic de Rham cohomology of
Y.

The utility of Simpson’s construction is that now algebraic de Rham cohomology behaves as a coherent cohomology theory. Working over
Y^{\operatorname{Simpson-dR}}
also allows one to better understand phenomena in non-abelian Hodge theory.

Since then, Drinfeld
[
17
]
and Bhatt–Lurie
[
9
]
have made mixed and positive characteristic analogues of Simpson’s de Rham stack. Recently, Bhatt–Kanaev–Mathew–Vologodsky–Zhang
[
8
]
have introduced a ‘sheared’ variant of this construction of Drinfeld and Bhatt–Lurie, which has slightly better properties. Our logarithmic de Rham stack is essentially a logarithmic variant of these constructions, which allows one to geometrize
logarithmic
de Rham cohomology, in the following sense.

Remark 1.2
.

Analogously to Simpson’s de Rham stack, we will prove in
Section 5
that

\Qcoh(X^{\dR}(\log D))\simeq\Conn^{\psi_{p}\text{ nilp}}(X,D),

where by
\Conn(X,D)
we mean the category of pairs
(\mathcal{E},\nabla:\mathcal{E}\to\mathcal{E}\otimes\Omega^{1}_{X}(\log D)),
where
\mathcal{E}\in\Qcoh(X)
and
\nabla
is a flat connection whose
p
-curvature is nilpotent. It is inconvenient for some applications to need to restrict to connections with
p
-curvature zero; to eliminate this problem, we will also define a
sheared
variant of
X^{\dR}(\log D),
which we define by
X^{\widehat{\dR}}(\log D).
The theory of sheared de Rham stacks will be reviewed in
Section 2
, but the key difference is that

\Qcoh(X^{\widehat{\dR}}(\log D))\simeq\Conn(X,D),

where now we have the category of
all
flat connections
\nabla:\mathcal{E}\to\mathcal{E}\otimes\Omega^{1}_{X}(\log D),
with no restriction on
p
-curvature.

Remark 1.3
.

The idea of making logarithmic constructions by working over
\mathbb{A}^{1}/\mathbb{G}_{m}
is due to Olsson; see Olsson’s
[
35
]
and
[
36
]
for some examples especially relevant to our applications.

Many connections which arise in practice have logarithmic poles; thus, for any theorem about the category
\Conn(X)
(meaning flat connections on
X
with
no
poles), it is often essential to have a corresponding theorem about
\Conn(X,D).
Our logarithmic de Rham stack will allow one to easily transmute the proof of a theorem about
\Conn(X)
to a proof of a theorem about
\Conn(X,D).
The point is that, since the logarithmic de Rham stack is defined in almost exactly the same way as the usual de Rham stack
(X/k)^{\dR},
most arguments involving the de Rham stack will not care about if the base is
\Spec(k)
or if it is
(\mathbb{A}^{1}/\mathbb{G}_{m})^{n}.

To persuade the reader of this point, we show how two interesting theorems – Cartier descent, and Groechenig’s
[
21
]
non-abelian Hodge theorem for curves – can be proven in the logarithmic setting in nearly exactly the same way as they are proven in the non-logarithmic setting. Besides just suggesting proofs, in both cases the logarithmic de Rham stack also
suggests what the statement
should be.

We now overview these two applications.

1.1.
Logarithmic Cartier descent

Definition 1.4
.

We write
X^{\prime}
for the Frobenius twist of
X
relative to
k.

In characteristic 0, one can reconstruct a linear ODE from its local system of solutions. Cartier found an analogue to this in positive characteristic; the starting point is that, instead of a local system, solutions to a linear ODE on in characteristic
p
naturally live over the Frobenius twist (this is more or less the observation that
x^{p}
has derivative 0 in characteristic
p
), allowing us to define a functor

\Sol:\Conn(X)\to\Qcoh(X^{\prime}),

sending a vector bundle with flat connection
(\mathcal{V},\nabla)
on
X
to its quasicoherent sheaf of solutions on the Frobenius twist
X^{\prime}.

Remark 1.5
.

Geometrically, this fact that solutions live on
X^{\prime}
is related to the fact that
(X/k)^{\dR}
admits a natural morphsim to
X^{\prime}.
In
Section 3
, we will similarly construct a certain natural morphism

X^{\dR}(\log D)\to(X/\Theta^{n})^{(1)},

where by
(X/\Theta^{n})^{(1)}
we mean the Frobenius twist of
X
relative to
\Theta^{n}.

Remark 1.6
.

This relative Frobenius twist
(X/\Theta^{n})^{(1)}
appearing in
Remark 1.5
is a certain root stack. More precisely,
(X/\Theta^{n})^{(1)}
is the
(p,p,...,p)
-multiroot stack of
X^{\prime}
along each component of
D^{\prime}.
In
Section 5
, we will review multiroot stacks.

The analogue of the Riemann-Hilbert correspondence, reconstructing an ODE from its local system of solutions, then becomes the following.

Theorem 1.7

(Cartier descent, Theorem 5.1 of
[
23
]
)
.

The functor

\Sol:\Conn(X)\to\Qcoh(X^{\prime})

restricts to an equivalence of categories

\Conn(X)^{\psi_{p}=0}\hookrightarrow\Conn(X)\xrightarrow{\Sol}\Qcoh(X^{\prime})

gives an equivalence of categories between
\Qcoh(X^{\prime})
and the category of flat connections on
X
with
p
-curvature 0.

As suggested by
Remark 1.5
, using the logarithmic de Rham stack suggests that, in the logarithmic setting, solutions to an ODE naturally live on this stacky Frobenius twist
(X/\Theta^{n})^{(1)}.
We now give an example illustrating this in a concrete situation.

Example 1.8
.

Let
X=\mathbb{A}^{1}=\Spec k[x].
For each
a\in\{0,1,...,p-1\},
we can define a connection

\nabla_{a}:\mathcal{O}_{X}\to\Omega_{X}^{1},\nabla_{a}=d-\frac{a}{x}dx.

In this case,
X^{\prime}=\Spec k[x^{p}],
and the solutions to
\nabla_{a}
are the
k[x^{p}]
-module
\Sol_{a}:=x^{a}\cdot k[x^{p}].
In particular, as
k[x^{p}]
-modules, the
\Sol_{a}
are all isomorphic.

This prevents a naive version of Cartier descent (
Theorem 1.7
) from holding in the logarithmic setting, as the connections
\nabla_{a}
are all distinct – and all have
p
-curvature zero – yet have isomorphic sheaves of solutions.

However, if we view the solutions as sheaves on the Frobenius twist
(X/\Theta)^{(1)},
then the situation improves. A quasicoherent sheaf on
(X/\Theta)^{(1)}
consists of a
k[x^{p}]
-module, together with a
p
-step filtration obeying certain properties (see ). In this situation, the
p
-step filtration on
\Sol_{a}
is given by

F^{p}=x^{a+p}\cdot k[x^{p}]\subseteq\cdots F^{a+1}=x^{a+p}\cdot k[x^{p}]\subseteq F^{a}=x^{a}\cdot k[x^{p}]\subseteq\cdots\subseteq F^{0}=x^{a}\cdot k[x^{p}].

In other words,
F^{i}
consists of all solutions to
\nabla_{a}
which are divisible by
x^{i}.
Note that these filtrations allow us to distinguish the modules
\Sol_{a}
; and so, while a naive version of Cartier descent fails in the logarithmic situation, we might hope that if we replace
X^{\prime}
by
(X/\Theta^{n})^{(1)}
in
Theorem 1.7
, we can salvage Cartier descent.

The phenomenon of
Example 1.8
generalizes, and we will prove in
Section 3
that one always has the following form of logarithmic Cartier descent.

Theorem 1.9

(Logarithmic Cartier descent)
.

There is a morphism
\nu:X^{\dR}(\log D)\to(X/\Theta^{n})^{(1)}
(which we will explicitly construct in
Section 3
) such that

\nu^{*}:\Qcoh((X/\Theta^{n})^{(1)})\to\Qcoh(X^{\dR}(\log D))

is fully faithful, and has essential image those logarithmic connections of
p
-curvature zero.

Remark 1.10
.

Implicit in the statement of
Theorem 1.9
is that there is a notion of
p
-curvature of logarithmic connections; we will define a notion of
p
-curvature for any relative de Rham stack in
Section 3
.

Remark 1.11
.

In practice, one can formally manipulate quasicoherent sheaves on
(X/\Theta^{n})^{(1)}
without needing to think too hard about the implicit filtrations (called
parabolic structures
). We hope this is apparent in our proof of logarithmic Cartier descent, which is completely formal, and never needs to explicitly track parabolic structures. Our proof of logarithmic non-abelian Hodge theory also never needs to explicitly use parabolic structures, except for when we deduce de Cataldo-Zhang’s
[
15
]
non-abelian Hodge theorem from ours.

Remark 1.12
.

The stack
(X/\Theta^{n})^{(1)}
appears also in the work of Esnault-Groechenig
[
20
]
(see their Lemma 3.16). Esnault-Groechenig’s
[
20
]
was a large inspiration for this project, as the author was trying to understand the constructions of that paper in terms of de Rham stacks.

Remark 1.13
.

While the stack
(X/\Theta^{n})^{(1)}
does not appear explicitly in Hablicsek’s
[
22
]
, Hablicsek does essentially work with quasicoherent sheaves on this stack (in the guise of parabolic bundles – see Borne
[
13
]
for the relationship). Encountering Hablicsek’s work gave the author confidence that de Rham stacks relative to
\Theta^{n}
should behave analogously to de Rham stacks over
\Spec(k).

Remark 1.14
.

Lorenzon
[
29
]
has an alternative version of logarithmic Cartier descent, phrased in the language of
indexed
Azumaya algebras. At the present moment, the author is unaware of a direct relationship between Lorenzon’s indexed Azumaya algebras and the parabolic structures which show up in our logarithmic Cartier descent.

1.2.
Logarithmic non-abelian Hodge theory

In characteristic 0, Simpson and others established the
non-abelian Hodge theorem
, which relates a moduli space of
vector bundles with connection
on a Riemann surface to the moduli space of
Higgs fields
on that same surface. Inspired by this characteristic 0 story, Groechenig
[
21
]
found a wonderful characteristic
p
variant.

In the characteristic 0 story, the curves are defined over
\mathbb{C},
yet the comparison of moduli spaces is only
real analytic
. Similarly, Groechenig’s
[
21
]
non-abelian Hodge theorem involves a Frobenius twist. Using the Frobenius twist
(X/\Theta^{n})^{(1)}
suggested by the logarithmic de Rham stack also allows us to prove a logarithmic non-abelian Hodge theorem for curves using almost verbatim the same argument that Groechenig
[
21
]
used in the non-logarithmic setting. More precisely, when
\dim X=1,
we have the following.

Let
\mathcal{M}_{\Dol}:=\mathcal{M}_{\Dol}((X/\Theta^{n})^{(1)},r)
denote the moduli stack of rank
r
Higgs fields on
(X/\Theta^{n})^{(1)}.
This moduli stack admits a natural Hitchin morphism

h:\mathcal{M}_{\Dol}((X/\Theta^{n})^{(1)},r)\to\mathcal{A}^{(1)}:=\bigoplus_{i=1}^{r}H^{0}((X/\Theta^{n})^{(1)},\omega_{(X/\Theta^{n})^{(1)}}^{\otimes i}).

Similarly, let
\mathcal{M}_{\dR}:=\mathcal{M}_{\dR}(X,D,r)
denote the moduli stack of pairs
(\mathcal{E},\nabla),
where
\mathcal{E}
is a rank
r
vector bundle on
X
and
\nabla:\mathcal{E}\to\mathcal{E}\otimes\Omega_{X}^{1}(\log D)
is a connection. In characteristic
p,
there is a natural
linear
invariant of a connection, called the
p
-curvature
. The
p
-curvature will be a certain
linear
map
\psi_{p}:\mathcal{E}\to\mathcal{E}\otimes F^{*}_{X}\Omega_{X}^{1}(\log D).
Though naturally the characteristic polynomial of such a map has coefficients in
\mathcal{A}:=\bigoplus_{i=1}^{r}H^{0}(X,F^{*}_{X}(\Omega_{X}^{1}(\log D)^{\otimes i})),
Laszlo-Pauly
[
25
]
(though see also Zhang
[
14
]
) proved the wonderful result that the
p
-curvature of a connection always has a charateristic polynomial whose coefficients lie in a subspace of
\mathcal{A}
isomorphic to
\mathcal{A}^{(1)}.
In this way, Laszlo-Pauly
[
25
]
allow us to define a de Rham Hitchin morphism

h_{\dR}:\mathcal{M}_{\dR}(X,D,r)\to\mathcal{A}^{(1)}.

Remark 1.15
.

We give a definition of
p
-curvature in terms of de Rham stacks, which allows us to give a new construction of
h_{\dR},
by viewing
\psi_{p}
as a morphism of quasicoherent sheaves on
X^{\widehat{\dR}}(\log D).
See
Section 4.2
for more information.

Theorem 1.16

(Our
Theorem 6.29
)
.

There is a group scheme
\mathcal{P}
over
\mathcal{A}^{(1)}
acting naturally on
\mathcal{M}_{\Dol},
and a
\mathcal{P}
-torsor
\mathcal{S}
such that

\mathcal{S}\times^{\mathcal{P}}\mathcal{M}_{\Dol}\simeq\mathcal{M}_{\dR}

as
\mathcal{A}^{(1)}
-stacks.

Remark 1.17
.

In particular, the fibers of
h:\mathcal{M}_{\Dol}\to\mathcal{A}^{(1)}
and
h_{\dR}:\mathcal{M}_{\dR}\to\mathcal{A}^{(1)}
over any point of
\mathcal{A}^{(1)}
are (non-canonically!) isomorphic.

de Cataldo-Zhang
[
15
]
have a different logarithmic non-abelian Hodge theorem; we are able to deduce theirs from ours. The key differences between our theorems is that

(1)

de Cataldo-Zhang do not use this stacky Frobenius twist
(X/\Theta^{n})^{(1)}
in defining their Dolbeault side, and instead only use
X^{\prime},

(2)

de Cataldo-Zhang have a slightly different Hitchin base, remembering not just the spectral data of
p
-curvature, but also the spectral data of the
residue
of a connection.

One may understand our difference in approaches as follows. Naively, one might hope to compare
\mathcal{M}_{\dR}(X,D,r)
and
\mathcal{M}_{\Dol}(X^{\prime},D^{\prime},r).
Unfortunately, the fiber of
\mathcal{M}_{\dR}(X,D,r)
over a point
a\in\mathcal{A}^{(1)}
is in general much larger than the fiber of
\mathcal{M}_{\Dol}(X^{\prime},D^{\prime},r)
over
a.
To fix this, our
Theorem 1.16
makes the Dolbeault side bigger by replacing
X^{\prime}
with the stacky Frobenius twist
(X/\Theta^{n})^{(1)}.
However, one could instead fix this by making the fibers on the de Rham side smaller; de Cataldo-Zhang
[
15
]
accomplish this by enlarging the Hitchin base.

To deduce de Cataldo-Zhang’s non-abelian Hodge theorem from ours, we track carefully how the residue of a connection is related to parabolic structures (as a parabolic structure is the difference between a sheaf on
X^{\prime}
and a sheaf on
(X/\Theta^{n})^{(1)}
).

1.2.1.
Relation to work of Li–Sun

Li–Sun
[
26
]
explore a version of logarithmic non-abelian Hodge theory for curves in positive characteristic using parahoric structures, more closely analogous to the characteristic 0 story. The author is presently unaware of any direct relationship between our
Theorem 1.16
and their results. While quasicoherent sheaves on root stacks (such as
(X/\Theta^{n})^{(1)}
) are often called sheaves with parabolic structure, it seems the parahoric structures used are of a very different nature than the parabolic structures considered here. Indeed, their parahoric structures depend heavily on the group used, whereas our parabolic structures are always just
p
-step filtrations.

1.2.2.
Relation to work of Ogus–Vologodsky and Schepler

Ogus–Vologodsky
[
34
]
prove a different sort of logarthmic non-abelian Hodge theorem; Schepler
[
38
]
found a logarithmic variant of their work. The key differences between our work and Schepler is that

(1)

we give a logarithmic version of the non-abelian Hodge theorem of Groechenig
[
21
]
, which gives a correspondence between
all
Higgs fields and
all
connections, but only in dimension 1;

(2)

Schepler
[
38
]
gives a logarithmic version of Ogus–Vologodsky’s
[
34
]
non-abelian Hodge theorem, which gives a correspondence in
all
dimensions, but only for Higgs fields and connections obeying a certain nilpotence condition.

Gleb Terentiuk is currently writing an article on Ogus–Vologodsky’s work, but re-done in the language of de Rham stacks; it seems very likely to the author of this work that Terentiuk’s results will also give a strengthening Schepler’s
[
38
]
result.

1.3.
Weight filtrations

The logarithmic de Rham stack
X^{\widehat{\dR}}(\log D)
naturally lives over
(\mathbb{A}^{1}/\mathbb{G}_{m})^{n},
since it is defined as the relative de Rham stack of a morphism over
(\mathbb{A}^{1}/\mathbb{G}_{m})^{n}.
Thus, the cohomology of any quasicoherent sheaf on
X^{\widehat{\dR}}(\log D)
is naturally equipped with a
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
-indexed filtration. In a forthcoming work, the author will focus more on these aspects of the logarithmic de Rham stack (which are interesting even in characteristic 0).

For now, we pose the following questions. Recently, Annala-Pstragowski
[
2
]
constructed filtrations on logarithmic de Rham cohomology in positive characteristic; they then proved that these filtrations actually depend only on the open part
U:=X\setminus D,
and not on the specific choice of compactification.

Question 1.18
.

Can one geometrize the weight filtration of Annala-Pstragowski?

Remark 1.19
.

The logarithmic de Rham stack
X^{\dR}(\log D)
does
not
depend on just
X\setminus D,
so in searching for a geometrization of this weight filtration of Annala-Pstragowski, it would be nice to find some modification of the logarithmic de Rham stack which
does
only depend on
X\setminus D.

In this vein, it is also interesting to ask the following question.

Question 1.20
.

Let
U
be a smooth variety over
k.
Is there some stack
U^{\dR,\reg}
, depending only on
U,
such that
\Qcoh(U^{\dR,\reg})\simeq\Conn(U)^{\reg},
the category of connections on
U
with regular singularities at infinity?

Remark 1.21
.

As a smooth curve has a unique compactification, logarithmic de Rham stacks provide a positive answer to
Question 1.20
for
\dim U=1.

The relative de Rham stack and its sheared variant

In this
purely expository
section, we recall a few results on de Rham stacks and sheared de Rham stacks. This is done mostly because the results we need about sheared de Rham stacks are not yet in the literature, but nothing in this section should be viewed as original; all arguments we make in this section essentially appear in Bhatt’s notes
[
7
]
(which themselves are based on the related works
[
18
]
of Drinfeld and
[
9
]
of Bhatt–Lurie). We hope this section can be useful to a reader less familiar with the theory of de Rham stacks.

Warning 2.1
.

To reiterate, no results in this section are original; all come from Bhatt–Mathew–Vologodsky–Zhang’s forthcoming work
[
10
]
, Drinfeld’s
[
19
]
and
[
18
]
, and Bhatt–Lurie’s
[
9
]
. We recall these results in part because
[

2.1.
The ring stacks
\mathbb{G}_{a}^{\dR}
and
\mathbb{G}_{a}^{\widehat{\dR}}

Remark 2.2

(Motivational remark)
.

In characteristic 0, Simpson
[
39
]
defines the de Rham stack of a scheme
X\in\Sch_{\mathbb{C}}
by

(2.3)

X^{\operatorname{Simpson-dR}}(A):=X(A_{\red}).

Simpson makes this definition for the following reason:
X^{\operatorname{Simpson-dR}}
is a stack whose coherent cohomology is the algebraic de Rham cohomology of
X,
and whose quasicoherent sheaves are precisely quasicoherent
\mathcal{D}_{X/\mathbb{C}}
-modules. This allows one to reason about de Rham cohomology and
\mathcal{D}
-modules using the machinery of algebraic geometry.
In characteristic
p,
we will use a very similar formula, but instead of replacing a ring
A
by its quotienet
A_{\red}=A/\nil(A)
, we will quotient by elements admitting a system of divided powers; this is related to the difference between the infinitesimal and crystalline sites. One can think of Simpson’s de Rham stack as geometrizing the infinitesimal site, the characteristic
p
de Rham stack as geometrizing the crystalline site, and the sheared de Rham stack as geometrizing the
nilpotent
crystalline site.

Unfortunately, while being nilpotent is a
property
of an element, a divided power structure is
data
. This means that, even to handle de Rham stacks of schemes, one needs to use (1-truncated) animated rings (also called
ring groupoids
).

Analogously to Simpson’s formula
(2.3)
, the de Rham and sheared de Rham stacks are defined by
transmutation
of certain ring stacks. That is, for
X
an
\mathbb{F}_{p}
-stack, we will see that

X^{\dR}(A)=X(\mathbb{G}_{a}^{\dR}(A)),X^{\widehat{\dR}}(A)=X(\mathbb{G}_{a}^{\widehat{\dR}}(A)),

for certain ring stacks
\mathbb{G}_{a}^{\dR}
and
\mathbb{G}_{a}^{\widehat{\dR}}.
The purpose of this section is to define these ring stacks.

2.1.1.
Reminder on quasi-ideals

Drinfeld developed a very convenient way to model 1-truncated ring stacks, called
quasi-ideals
; see section 3.1 of
[
17
]
, or the short article
[
18
]
, for the full story, but for the reader’s convenience we will recall a few of Drinfeld’s results.

Definition 2.4
.

A
quasi-ideal
in a ring
R
is a pair
(I,d)
where
I
is an
R
-module and
d:I\to R
is an
R
-linear map obeying
d(x)\cdot y=d(y)\cdot x
for all
x,y\in I.

A
sheaf of quasi-ideals
on a sheaf of rings
\mathcal{R}
(for us,
\mathcal{R}
will be a sheaf of rings on
\Sch_{\mathbb{F}_{p}}
with the fpqc topology, but one can make this definition on any Grothendieck site) is a sheaf of
\mathcal{R}
-modules
\mathcal{I}
together with an
\mathcal{R}
-linear map
d:\mathcal{I}\to\mathcal{R}
such that, for every test object
T,
the map
d(T):\mathcal{I}(T)\to\mathcal{R}(T)
is a quasi-ideal on the ring
R(T).

For any quasi-ideal
d:I\to R,
Drinfeld constructs a 1-truncated animated
R
-algebra which he calls

\Cone(I\to R).

See section 3 of
[
18
]
for the construction; one should think of it as a suitably derived version of
R/I
.

Remark 2.5
.

The underlying 1-truncated animated
R
-module of
\Cone(I\to R)
has a very succint definition:

\Cone(I\to R):=\tau_{\leq 1}\cofib(I\to R),

where we take the cofiber in the category of
R
-modules; see
[
18
]
’s remark just before section 3.4.8.

In an
\infty
-categorical context, the cofiber
\cofib(I\to R)
is a little more natural, and the truncation can make the cone seem a little confusing to work with. We will explain that, for our examples of interest, the truncation is not truly needed.

Drinfeld’s construction is functorial in the quasi-ideal, and hence if
d:\mathcal{I}\to\mathcal{R}
is a sheaf of quasi-ideals on
\Sch_{\mathbb{F}_{p}}
(with the flat topoplogy), then one can define a functor

T\mapsto\Cone(\mathcal{I}(T)\to\mathcal{R}(T))

from
\Sch_{\mathbb{F}_{p}}
to the category of animated rings. The fpqc
sheafification
of this functor is denoted by
\Cone(\mathcal{I}\to\mathcal{R}),
and is an animated ring stack on
\Sch_{\mathbb{F}_{p}}.
As
\Cone(\mathcal{I}(T)\to\mathcal{R}(T))
is an
\mathcal{R}(T)
-algebra, we can also treat
\Cone(\mathcal{I}\to\mathcal{R})
as a sheaf of
\mathcal{R}
-algebras.

Remark 2.6

(Sheafified cones are still 1-truncated)
.

Sheafification is a left exact functor, and so by proposition 5.5.6.16 of Lurie’s
[
31
]
, it takes 1-truncated objects to 1-truncated objects; thus
\cone(\mathcal{I}\to\mathcal{R})
will still be a sheaf of 1-truncated animated rings.

One might be worried about set-theoretic subtleties implicit in fpqc sheafification; fortunately, we can give an explicit construction of the sheafification as a quotient stack.

We will start by explaining how to define
\Cone(\mathcal{I}\to\mathcal{R})
as an fpqc stack of
\mathcal{R}
-modules.

Lemma 2.7
.

Let
[\mathcal{R}/\mathcal{I}]
denote the quotient stack; explicitly, we can define
[\mathcal{R}/\mathcal{I}]
as the stack of groupoids defined by setting
[\mathcal{R}/\mathcal{I}](T)
to be the groupoid of pairs
(\mathcal{F},\varphi:\mathcal{F}\to\mathcal{R}|_{T})
, where
\mathcal{F}
is an fpqc
\mathcal{I}|_{T}
-torsor on
T
and
\varphi
is
\mathcal{I}|_{T}
-equivariant. In particular,
[\mathcal{R}/\mathcal{I}]
is 1-truncated.

Then
[\mathcal{R}/\mathcal{I}]
is an fpqc sheafification of the presheaf of anima defined by

T\mapsto\Cone(\mathcal{I}(T)\to\mathcal{R}(T)).

Proof.

By
Remark 2.5
, the object
\Cone(\mathcal{I}(T)\to\mathcal{R}(T)),
considered as an anima, is nothing but the 1-truncation of
\cofib(\mathcal{I}(T)\to\mathcal{R}(T)).
The sheafification of
T\mapsto\cofib(\mathcal{I}(T)\to\mathcal{R}(T))
is by definition the sheaf
\cofib(\mathcal{I}\to\mathcal{R}),
and so the sheafification of

T\mapsto\tau_{\leq 1}(\cofib(\mathcal{I}(T)\to\mathcal{R}(T)))

is
\tau_{\leq 1}\cofib(\mathcal{I}\to\mathcal{R})\simeq[\mathcal{R}/\mathcal{I}],
as desired. This last identification with the quotient stack is because the cofiber, computed in the category of
presheaves
, has functor of points

\cofib(\mathcal{I}(T)\to\mathcal{R}(T)),

whose 1-truncation is just the groupoid whose objects are elements of the ring
\mathcal{R}(T),
and where morphisms
r\to r^{\prime}
are just the data of some
i\in\mathcal{I}(T)
so that
r^{\prime}=r+i.
Sheafifying this presheaf of groupoids produces the usual quotient stack.
∎

However, we defined
\Cone(\mathcal{I}\to\mathcal{R})
to be a sheaf of animated
rings
, not a sheaf of anima. Fortunately, the fpqc sheafification of rings we use exists because ring stacks are equivalent to ring objects in the category of stacks, and so we can sheafify the ring structure morphisms (addition, multiplication, etc.) on the presheaf

T\mapsto\Cone(\mathcal{I}(T)\to\mathcal{R}(T))

to get a ring structure on
[\mathcal{R}/\mathcal{I}].

2.1.2.
The quasi-ideals
\mathbb{G}_{a}^{\#}
and
\mathbb{G}_{a}^{\#,\wedge}

As explained in
Remark 2.2
, to define the de Rham stack in positive characteristic, we will want to keep track of divided power structures on elements of
\mathbb{F}_{p}
-algebras. We now introduce a sheaf which does this.

Definition 2.8

(Systems of divided powers)
.

Let
\mathbb{G}_{a}=\Spec\mathbb{F}_{p}[t],\mathbb{G}_{a}^{\#}=\Spec\mathbb{F}_{p}[t,t^{2}/2!,t^{3}/3!,...].
We will subsequently write
\mathbb{F}_{p}\left\langle t\right\rangle
to denote
\mathbb{F}_{p}[t,t^{2}/2!,...].

Let
A
be an
\mathbb{F}_{p}
-algebra. A
system of divided powers
for
a\in A
is a sequence of
\gamma_{n}\in A
so that
\gamma_{0}=1,\gamma_{1}=a,
and

\gamma_{n}\cdot\gamma_{m}=\binom{n+m}{n}\cdot\gamma_{n+m}

for all
n,m\geq 0.
It’s easy to check that divided power structures on
a\in A
are in natural bijection with
\mathbb{F}_{p}
-algebra homomorphisms
\mathbb{F}_{p}\left\langle t\right\rangle\to A
sending
t
to
a.
Thus
\mathbb{G}_{a}^{\#}(A)
is the set of pairs
(a,\gamma_{\bullet}
where
a\in A
and
\gamma_{\bullet}
is a system of divided powers for
a.

For the sheared de Rham stack, we will need a variant of
\mathbb{G}_{a}^{\#}
keeping track of
nilpotent
divided powers. One should think of the difference between the following definition and the previous one as being the difference between the crystalline site and the nilpotent crystalline site.

Definition 2.9

(Systems of nilpotent divided powers)
.

Set
\mathbb{G}_{a}^{\#,\wedge}:=\Spf\mathbb{F}_{p}\left\langle t\right\rangle^{\wedge},
where we let
\mathbb{F}_{p}\left\langle t\right\rangle^{\wedge}
denote the topological ring of all formal power series of the form

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a_{n}\cdot\frac{t^{n}}{n!}

with
a_{n}\in\mathbb{F}_{p},
and the topology is given by the metric

d(f(t),g(t))=p^{-\nu(f(t)-g(t))},

where
\nu(f(t))
is defined to be the smallest
n
so that the
t^{n}/n!
coefficient in
f(t)
is nonzero.

Concretely,
\mathbb{G}_{a}^{\#,\wedge}(A)
can be identified with the set of all pairs
(a,\gamma_{\bullet})
of
a\in A
and
\gamma_{\bullet}
a system of divided powers for
a\in A
such that
\gamma_{N}=0
for all
N\gg 0.

Remark 2.10
.

One can make analogous definitions in mixed characteristic, but we will not need those notions in this paper.

The morphisms

\mathbb{G}_{a}^{\#}\to\mathbb{G}_{a}

and

\mathbb{G}_{a}^{\#,\wedge}\to\mathbb{G}_{a}

(corresponding to forgetting the divided power structure) are both quasi-ideals in the sense of Drinfeld.

Definition 2.11
.

We define

\mathbb{G}_{a}^{\dR}:=\Cone(\mathbb{G}_{a}^{\#}\to\mathbb{G}_{a}),

\mathbb{G}_{a}^{\widehat{\dR}}:=\Cone(\mathbb{G}_{a}^{\#,\wedge}\to\mathbb{G}_{a}),

where we use
\Cone
in the sense of
Section 2.1.1
. In particular,
\mathbb{G}_{a}^{\dR}
and
\mathbb{G}_{a}^{\widehat{\dR}}
are fpqc stacks of 1-truncated animated rings on
\Sch_{\mathbb{F}_{p}}.

The definition of
\Cone
in
Section 2.1.1
was a little subtle because it involved sheafification. We now explore how one can make these two ring stacks a bit more concrete.

2.1.3.
Witt vectors and
\mathbb{G}_{a}^{\#}

We start by explaining some simplifications one can do to
\mathbb{G}_{a}^{\dR}
, stemming from the relationship between
\mathbb{G}_{a}^{\#}
and Witt vectors. These results all come from
[
9
]
and
[
17
]
, and our presentation follows section 2.6 of Bhatt’s
[
7
]
.

Remark 2.12
.

We omit most of the proofs in this section because the proofs in the sheared case are entirely analogous, and we feel it is more valuable to prove the sheared versions since these non-sheared results are already very well-documented in the literature.

Definition 2.13
.

We define
W
the ring scheme of Witt vectors, sending an
\mathbb{F}_{p}
-algebra
A
to the ring
W(A)
of
p
-typical Witt vectors.

Lemma 2.14
.

Let
R:W\to\mathbb{G}_{a}
be the 0th ghost map; in this way, we can view
\mathbb{G}_{a}
(and hence
\mathbb{G}_{a}^{\#}
) as a sheaf of
W
-modules. Let
F:W\to W
be the Frobenius on Witt vectors. There is a short exact sequence of sheaves of
W
-modules

0\to\mathbb{G}_{a}^{\#}\to W\xrightarrow{F}F_{*}W\to 0.

In other words,
F
is faithfully flat and there is a
W
-linear equivalence
\mathbb{G}_{a}^{\#}\simeq W[F].

Proof.

See
[
17
]
lemma 3.2.6, or
[
7
]
lemma 2.6.1. The argument is not very involved, though:
F
being faithfully flat is clear since
W\simeq\prod_{i\geq 0}\mathbb{A}^{1}_{\mathbb{F}_{p}}
as an
\mathbb{F}_{p}
-scheme. To check that the kernel of Frobenius can be identified with
\mathbb{G}_{a}^{\#},
one does a simple computation to check that
(a_{0},a_{1},...)\in W(A)
lies in the kernel of Frobenius if and only if the
a_{n}
are, up to units, a system of divided powers on
a_{0}
(more precisely,
a_{n}
will be a divided power
a_{0}^{p^{n}}/(p^{n})!,
up to units).
∎

Corollary 2.15
.

For
A
any
\mathbb{F}_{p}
-algebra, the complex
R\Gamma_{\operatorname{flat}}(\Spec(A),\mathbb{G}_{a}^{\#})
is concentrated in degrees 0 and 1. Moreover, for
A
semiperfect, this complex is quasi-isomorphic to
\mathbb{G}_{a}^{\#}(A)[0].

Remark 2.16
.

As pointed out by Bhatt (see footnote 22 in
[
7
]
, at the bottom of page 28), this shows that the subtleties implicit in fpqc cohomology of
\mathbb{G}_{a}^{\#}
do not matter: the notion of
H^{0}
and
H^{1}
are always meaningful (global sections and torsors), and the higher cohomologies, which a priori might depend on a cutoff cardinal, always vanish.

Proof.

By
Lemma 2.14
, it suffices to prove that
R\Gamma_{\operatorname{flat}}(\Spec(A),W)
is concentrated in degree 0 (even the semiperfectness claim follows from this, since for
A
semiperfect the Frobenius
W(A)\to W(A)
is surjective).

Write
W=\prod_{i\geq 0}\mathbb{G}_{a}
(which is true as an equality of abelian sheaves). Then

\displaystyle R\Gamma(\Spec(A),W)

\displaystyle\simeq R\Gamma\left(\Spec(A),\mathop{\mathchoice{\vtop{\halign{#\cr\hfil$\displaystyle\operator@font lim$\hfil\cr\kern 1.5pt\cr\leftarrow\textstyle\cr\kern 1.0pt\cr\cr}}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr\hfil$\textstyle\operator@font lim$\hfil\cr\kern 1.5pt\cr\leftarrow\textstyle\cr\kern 1.0pt\cr\cr}}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr\hfil$\scriptstyle\operator@font lim$\hfil\cr\kern 1.5pt\cr\leftarrow\textstyle\cr\kern 1.0pt\cr\cr}}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr\hfil$\scriptscriptstyle\operator@font lim$\hfil\cr\kern 1.5pt\cr\leftarrow\textstyle\cr\kern 1.0pt\cr\cr}}}}\displaylimits_{n}\prod_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{G}_{a}\right)

\displaystyle\simeq R\mathop{\mathchoice{\vtop{\halign{#\cr\hfil$\displaystyle\operator@font lim$\hfil\cr\kern 1.5pt\cr\leftarrow\textstyle\cr\kern 1.0pt\cr\cr}}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr\hfil$\textstyle\operator@font lim$\hfil\cr\kern 1.5pt\cr\leftarrow\textstyle\cr\kern 1.0pt\cr\cr}}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr\hfil$\scriptstyle\operator@font lim$\hfil\cr\kern 1.5pt\cr\leftarrow\textstyle\cr\kern 1.0pt\cr\cr}}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr\hfil$\scriptscriptstyle\operator@font lim$\hfil\cr\kern 1.5pt\cr\leftarrow\textstyle\cr\kern 1.0pt\cr\cr}}}}\displaylimits_{n}R\Gamma(\Spec(A),(\mathbb{G}_{a})^{\oplus n})

\displaystyle\simeq R\mathop{\mathchoice{\vtop{\halign{#\cr\hfil$\displaystyle\operator@font lim$\hfil\cr\kern 1.5pt\cr\leftarrow\textstyle\cr\kern 1.0pt\cr\cr}}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr\hfil$\textstyle\operator@font lim$\hfil\cr\kern 1.5pt\cr\leftarrow\textstyle\cr\kern 1.0pt\cr\cr}}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr\hfil$\scriptstyle\operator@font lim$\hfil\cr\kern 1.5pt\cr\leftarrow\textstyle\cr\kern 1.0pt\cr\cr}}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr\hfil$\scriptscriptstyle\operator@font lim$\hfil\cr\kern 1.5pt\cr\leftarrow\textstyle\cr\kern 1.0pt\cr\cr}}}}\displaylimits_{n}A^{\oplus n}

\displaystyle\simeq W(A),

since the transition maps
W_{n}(A)\to W_{n-1}(A)
of truncated Witt vectors are clearly surjective, and so the Mittag-Leffler condition ensures we have no
R^{1}\lim.
We conclude.
∎

Corollary 2.17
.

For any
\mathbb{F}_{p}
-algebra
A,
there is a (functorial in
A
) equivalence

\mathbb{G}_{a}^{\dR}(A)\simeq R\Gamma_{\operatorname{flat}}(\Spec(A),\mathbb{G}_{a}^{\dR}).

When
A
is semiperfect, we even have

(2.18)

\mathbb{G}_{a}^{\dR}(A)\simeq\cofib(\mathbb{G}_{a}^{\#}(A)\to A).

Proof.

By
Lemma 2.7
, we have
\mathbb{G}_{a}^{\dR}(A)\simeq[\mathbb{G}_{a}/\mathbb{G}_{a}^{\#}](A),
where the right hand side is the 1-truncated quotient stack. But because of
Corollary 2.15
, this 1-truncated quotient stack is actually equal to
\cofib(\mathbb{G}_{a}^{\#}\to\mathbb{G}_{a})
(the cofiber taken in the category of fpqc sheaves; since it is equal to the quotient stack
[\mathbb{G}_{a}/\mathbb{G}_{a}^{\#}]
by
Corollary 2.15
, the set theoretic issues implicit in its definition are irrelevant). Thus

\mathbb{G}_{a}^{\dR}(A)\simeq[\mathbb{G}_{a}/\mathbb{G}_{a}^{\#}](A)\simeq R\Gamma_{\operatorname{flat}}(\Spec(A),\mathbb{G}_{a}^{\dR}).

The last equality is because

\cofib(\mathbb{G}_{a}^{\#}\to\mathbb{G}_{a})(A)=R\Gamma_{\operatorname{flat}}(\Spec(A),\mathbb{G}_{a}^{\dR}),

essentially by definition.

Moreover, because
\mathbb{G}_{a}^{\dR}\simeq\cofib(\mathbb{G}_{a}^{\#}\to\mathbb{G}_{a}),
we have an exact triangle

\mathbb{G}_{a}^{\#}\to\mathbb{G}_{a}\to\mathbb{G}_{a}^{\dR}

of sheaves of
\mathbb{G}_{a}
-modules. Taking flat cohomology and using
Corollary 2.15
, for
A
semiperfect we get an exact triangle

\mathbb{G}_{a}^{\#}(A)\to A\to R\Gamma_{\operatorname{flat}}(\Spec(A),\mathbb{G}_{a}^{\dR}),

of
A
-modules, which implies
(2.18)
.
∎

As the semiperfect
\mathbb{F}_{p}
-algebras form a basis for the flat topology (see
[
11
]
proposition 4.31 for an even more general statement), it often suffices to check properties of
\mathbb{G}_{a}^{\dR}
just on semiperfect
\mathbb{F}_{p}
-algebras; thus the formula
(2.18)
allows us to prove properties of
\mathbb{G}_{a}^{\dR}
without needing to worry about the sheafification used in its definition.

We end with one more useful fact about
\mathbb{G}_{a}^{\dR},
expressing it in terms of Witt vectors. This allows us to compute its homotopy groups, which will be useful later.

Proposition 2.19
.

View
\mathbb{G}_{a}^{\dR}
as a
W
-module via the composition
W\xrightarrow{R}\mathbb{G}_{a}\to\mathbb{G}_{a}^{\dR},
where recall
R:W\to\mathbb{G}_{a}
is the 0th ghost component. There is a natural equivalence of
W
-modules

\mathbb{G}_{a}^{\dR}\simeq\cone(F_{*}W\xrightarrow{p}F_{*}W).

Proof.

This is proven in exactly the same way as the sheared version of this statement, which is our
Proposition 2.28
. Alternatively, see Corollary 2.6.8 of
[
7
]
.
∎

Corollary 2.20
.

As fpqc sheaves of
W
-modules, we have

\pi_{0}(\mathbb{G}_{a}^{\dR})=F_{*}\mathbb{G}_{a},\pi_{1}(\mathbb{G}_{a}^{\dR})=F_{*}\mathbb{G}_{a}^{\#}.

Warning 2.21
.

In contrast to the other results of this section, in this corollary it is
crucial
that we are working over
\mathbb{F}_{p}.
This is because at some point in the proof we will use that
FV=VF,
which of course only holds when we take Witt vectors of rings of characteristic
p.

Proof.

As with
Proposition 2.19
, below we will prove the sheared version of this, which has exactly the same proof; we remark that
[
7
]
includes this as Corollary 2.6.11.
∎

It is incredibly desirable to have sheared variants of
(2.18)
and
Corollary 2.20
. To this end, we introduce the
sheared
Witt vectors.

2.1.4.
Sheared Witt vectors and
\mathbb{G}_{a}^{\#,\wedge}

Our first goal is to give a variant of
Lemma 2.14
involving
\mathbb{G}_{a}^{\#,\wedge}.
As
\mathbb{G}_{a}^{\#,\wedge}
classifies divided powers which are eventually zero, it is natural to introduce the following ideal of
W.

Definition 2.22
.

For any
\mathbb{F}_{p}
-algebra
A,
we define
W^{\prime}(A)\subseteq W(A)
the ideal consisting of all sequences
(a_{0},a_{1},a_{2},...)\in W(A)
such that
a_{n}=0
for all
n\gg 0.

Lemma 2.23

(Drinfeld)
.

The equivalence
W[F]\simeq\mathbb{G}_{a}^{\#}
induces to an equivalence
W^{\prime}[F]\simeq\mathbb{G}_{a}^{\#,\wedge}.

Proof.

See Drinfeld
[
19
]
, lemma 3.2.5 for full details, but we give a sketch. When we constructed the equivalence
W[F]\simeq\mathbb{G}_{a}^{\#}
in
Lemma 2.14
, we stated that
(a_{0},a_{1},...)\in W
belongs to
W[F]
if and only if the
a_{n}
form a system of divided powers (up to minor technicalities). A system of divided powers belongs to
\mathbb{G}_{a}^{\#,\wedge}
if and only if the powers are eventually zero, we see that this equivalence identifies
\mathbb{G}_{a}^{\#,\wedge}
with
W^{\prime}[F],
as desired.
∎

Unfortunately, it is not yet immediate to prove an analogue of
Corollary 2.15
for
\mathbb{G}_{a}^{\#,\wedge}.
The trouble is that it’s not clear that, for
A
semiperfect, the map
W^{\prime}(A)\to W^{\prime}(A)
is surjective. So we introduce another ideal, for which this surjectivity is apparent.

Definition 2.24
.

We define
\widehat{W}(A)\subseteq W^{\prime}(A)
the ideal consisting of all sequences
(a_{0},a_{1},...)\in W(A)
such that
a_{n}=0
for all
n\gg 0,
and so that each
a_{i}
is nilpotent.

Lemma 2.25

(Drinfeld)
.

The canonical inclusion
\widehat{W}[F]\hookrightarrow W^{\prime}[F]
is an equivalence, and so
Lemma 2.23
gives
\widehat{W}[F]\simeq\mathbb{G}_{a}^{\#,\wedge}.

Proof.

By
Lemma 2.23
, the Witt components of any element of
W^{\prime}[F]
admit divided powers; but any element of a characteristic
p
ring admitting divided powers is automatically nilpotent, and so the inclusion
\widehat{W}[F]\hookrightarrow W^{\prime}[F]
is an equality, as desired.
∎

Corollary 2.26
.

For
A
any
semiperfect

\mathbb{F}_{p}
-algebra, the complex
R\Gamma_{\operatorname{flat}}(\Spec(A),\mathbb{G}_{a}^{\#,\wedge})
is quasi-isomorphic to
\mathbb{G}_{a}^{\#,\wedge}(A)[0].

Proof.

Observe that
\widehat{W}
is stable under
F
(this is easiest to see using ghost components). We claim first that if
A
is semiperfect, then
F:\widehat{W}(A)\to\widehat{W}(A)
is surjective; this is easy to check by hand, since given
a=(a_{0},a_{1},...,a_{n},0,0,...)\in\widehat{W}(A),
by semiperfectness of
A
we can find
b_{i}\in A
with
b_{i}^{p}=a_{i}
, and then
b=(b_{0},b_{1},...,b_{n},0,0,...)\in\widehat{W}(A)
has
F(b)=a.

By
Lemma 2.25
, it thus suffices to prove that
R\Gamma_{\operatorname{flat}}(\Spec(A),W^{\prime})
is concentrated in degree 0 to get our claim, since we have a short exact sequence

(2.27)

0\to\mathbb{G}_{a}^{\#,\wedge}\to\widehat{W}\xrightarrow{F}\widehat{W}\to 0

of fpqc sheaves, with
F:\widehat{W}(A)\to\widehat{W}(A)
surjective for
A.

Thus to get our result, it suffices to prove that
\Spec(A)
is
\widehat{W}
-acyclic in the fpqc topology. As an abelian sheaf, we may write
\widehat{W}=\mathop{\mathchoice{\vtop{\halign{#\cr\hfil$\displaystyle\operator@font lim$\hfil\cr\kern 1.5pt\cr\rightarrow\textstyle\cr\kern 1.0pt\cr\cr}}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr\hfil$\textstyle\operator@font lim$\hfil\cr\kern 1.5pt\cr\rightarrow\textstyle\cr\kern 1.0pt\cr\cr}}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr\hfil$\scriptstyle\operator@font lim$\hfil\cr\kern 1.5pt\cr\rightarrow\textstyle\cr\kern 1.0pt\cr\cr}}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr\hfil$\scriptscriptstyle\operator@font lim$\hfil\cr\kern 1.5pt\cr\rightarrow\textstyle\cr\kern 1.0pt\cr\cr}}}}\displaylimits_{n}\prod_{i=0}^{n}\widehat{\mathbb{G}_{a}},
since every element of
\widehat{W}(A)
can be represented as a finite tuple of nilpotents.

In remark 2.2.18 of
[
7
]
, Bhatt uses the short exact sequence

0\to\widehat{\mathbb{G}_{a}}\to\mathbb{G}_{a}\to\mathbb{G}_{a,\perf}\to 0

of fppf sheaves (where
\mathbb{G}_{a,\perf}(R):=R_{\perf}
is the
colimit
perfection) to prove that

R\Gamma_{\operatorname{fppf}}(\Spec(A),\widehat{\mathbb{G}_{a}})=\cofib(A\to A_{\perf})[-1].

For
A
semiperfect, the map
A\to A_{\perf}
is surjective, and so the above cohomology is just
\widehat{\mathbb{G}_{a}}(A).
Note that
\mathbb{G}_{a}
is a quasicoherent sheaf and hence has no higher cohomology;
\mathbb{G}_{a,\perf},
being a filtered colimit of quasicoherent sheaves (namely copies of
\mathbb{G}_{a}
), is still quasicoherent and hence the higher fpqc cohomology of
\mathbb{G}_{a,\perf}
on
\Spec(A)
vanishes; thus we deduce
R\Gamma_{\operatorname{fpqc}}(\Spec(A),\widehat{\mathbb{G}_{a}})=\cofib(A\to A_{\perf})[-1]
as well.

Therefore

\displaystyle R\Gamma_{\operatorname{fpqc}}(\Spec(A),\widehat{W})

\displaystyle\simeq R\Gamma_{\operatorname{fpqc}}\left(\Spec(A),\mathop{\mathchoice{\vtop{\halign{#\cr\hfil$\displaystyle\operator@font lim$\hfil\cr\kern 1.5pt\cr\rightarrow\textstyle\cr\kern 1.0pt\cr\cr}}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr\hfil$\textstyle\operator@font lim$\hfil\cr\kern 1.5pt\cr\rightarrow\textstyle\cr\kern 1.0pt\cr\cr}}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr\hfil$\scriptstyle\operator@font lim$\hfil\cr\kern 1.5pt\cr\rightarrow\textstyle\cr\kern 1.0pt\cr\cr}}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr\hfil$\scriptscriptstyle\operator@font lim$\hfil\cr\kern 1.5pt\cr\rightarrow\textstyle\cr\kern 1.0pt\cr\cr}}}}\displaylimits_{n}\prod_{i=0}^{n}\widehat{\mathbb{G}_{a}}\right)

Corollary 2.26

. We next introduce the

The idea is to introduce an fpqc sheaf of rings

Set

where we take the quotient in the category of fpqc sheaves. Drinfeld then defines

{}^{s}W:=\mathop{\mathchoice{\vtop{\halign{#\cr\hfil$\displaystyle\operator@font lim$\hfil\cr\kern 1.5pt\cr\leftarrow\textstyle\cr\kern 1.0pt\cr\cr}}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr\hfil$\textstyle\operator@font lim$\hfil\cr\kern 1.5pt\cr\leftarrow\textstyle\cr\kern 1.0pt\cr\cr}}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr\hfil$\scriptstyle\operator@font lim$\hfil\cr\kern 1.5pt\cr\leftarrow\textstyle\cr\kern 1.0pt\cr\cr}}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr\hfil$\scriptscriptstyle\operator@font lim$\hfil\cr\kern 1.5pt\cr\leftarrow\textstyle\cr\kern 1.0pt\cr\cr}}}}\displaylimits_{n}Q_{n},

Composing with the map

sending a Witt vector to its 0th ghost component, we get a natural map

This definition has a number of nice properties. Firstly,

which have exactly the same properties as the usual Frobenius and Verschiebung on

[
19
]
for extensive discussion of these points, particularly Drinfeld’s sections 1.4 and 3.2. In particular, Drinfeld proves

(1)

(see 3.2.4 of
[
19
]
),

(2)

(see
[
10
]
),

(3)

is faithfully flat, and
{}^{s}W[F]=\widehat{W}[F]
(Lemma 3.2.5 of
[
19
]
)

(4)

\tilde{V}:F_{*}{}^{s}W\to{}^{s}W

(Lemma 3.2.5 of
[
19
]
),

(5)

[
19
]

Proposition 2.28
.

There is a natural quasi-isomorphism of animated

\mathbb{G}_{a}^{\widehat{\dR}}\simeq\Cone(F_{*}{}^{s}W\xrightarrow{p}F_{*}{}^{s}W)=:F_{*}{}^{s}W/p.

Proof.

We will use the properties of

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathbb{G}_{a}^{\#,\wedge}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathbb{G}_{a}^{\#,\wedge}\oplus F_{*}{}^{s}W}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces F_{*}{}^{s}W}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathbb{G}_{a}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{}^{s}W}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces F_{*}{}^{s}W.}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces(\can,V)}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces p}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces R}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces F}

Here, the map

Each column is a quasi-ideal (because

), and the two squarse are morphisms of pairs of a ring with a quasi-ideal. Thus the left square induces a map

Similarly, the right square induces a map

Composing these two equivalences, we conclude.

Corollary 2.30
.

As sheaves of
{}^{s}W
-modules, we have

\pi_{0}(\mathbb{G}_{a}^{\widehat{\dR}})=F_{*}\mathbb{G}_{a},\pi_{1}(\mathbb{G}_{a}^{\widehat{\dR}})=F_{*}\mathbb{G}_{a}^{\#,\wedge}.

Proof.

By
Proposition 2.28
, we have
\mathbb{G}_{a}^{\widehat{\dR}}\simeq F_{*}{}^{s}W/p.

We may write

Thus

\pi_{0}(F_{*}{}^{s}W/p)=F_{*}{}^{s}W/F_{*}(V{}^{s}W))\simeq F_{*}({}^{s}W/V{}^{s}W).

But the short exact sequence (3.13) of Drinfeld’s

and so

as desired.

For

we have

\pi_{1}(F_{*}{}^{s}W/p)=F_{*}\ker(p:{}^{s}W\to{}^{s}W).

∎

2.2.
Defining de Rham stacks

We can now define the de Rham stack.

Definition 2.31
.

Let

be an
\mathbb{F}_{p}
-stack. We define

and

by transmutation from the ring stacks

-quasi-ideals

Definition 2.33
.

For

-stacks, we define

-stacks

Remark 2.34
.

In principle, one can also define these stacks by transmutation relative to the base

but the theory of transmutation over an arbitrary base seems quite delicate, and so we have chosen not to develop it as this more direct definition suffices for our applications. The author thanks Bhargav Bhatt for observing that this transmutation-free definition would lead to a more efficient presentation.

We can make a relative version of the morphisms

.

Construction 2.1
.

Fix

a morphism of
\mathbb{F}_{p}
-stacks. We define

the morphism of

-stacks induced by the commutative diagram
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X^{\dR}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces S}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces S^{\dR}.}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\pi_{X}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces f}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces f^{\dR}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\pi_{S}}

2.2.1.

Proposition 2.36

2.2.2.

Before giving our result, we recall a basic result from derived deformation theory.

2.4.1.
The algebra
\mathcal{D}_{X/S}

We start by defining a (non-commutative!) quasicoherent
\mathcal{O}_{X}
-algebra
\mathcal{D}_{X/S}.
As

is smooth and representable, its cotangent complex is just the vector bundle
\Omega^{1}_{X/S}.
In particular, we can define the tangent bundle
\mathcal{T}_{X/S}:=\underline{\Hom}(\Omega_{X/S}^{1},\mathcal{O}_{X}).

This
\mathcal{T}_{X/S}
has the structure of a Lie algebroid; that is, there is a natural action

\mathcal{T}_{X/S}\oplus\mathcal{O}_{X}\to\mathcal{O}_{X}

of
\mathcal{T}_{X/S}
on functions (defined by sending
(\partial,f)
to
\partial(df),
viewing
\partial
as a map
\Omega_{X/S}^{1}\to\mathcal{O}_{X}
), and a natural Lie bracket

\mathcal{T}_{X/S}\oplus\mathcal{T}_{X/S}\to\mathcal{T}_{X/S}

defined by
[\partial,\partial^{\prime}](\omega)=\partial(\partial^{\prime}(\omega))-\partial^{\prime}(\partial(\omega)).

Note that the action
\mathcal{T}_{X/S}\oplus\mathcal{O}_{X}\to\mathcal{O}_{X}
is not
\mathcal{O}_{X}
-bilinear, and likewise the Lie bracket is not
\mathcal{O}_{X}
-bilinear; instead both obey suitable Leibniz rules.

We can then define
\mathcal{D}_{X/S}
to be the universal enveloping algebra of this Lie algebroid.

Definition 2.48
.

We let
\mathcal{D}_{X/S}
be the quasicoherent
\mathcal{O}_{X}
-algebra defined such that, for any morphism
\varphi:\Spec(A)\to X,
the pullback
\varphi^{*}(\mathcal{D}_{X/S})
is the associative
A
-algebra generated by the free
A
-module
\varphi^{*}(\mathcal{T}_{X/S}),
subject to the relations that

\partial\cdot\partial^{\prime}-\partial^{\prime}\cdot\partial=[\partial,\partial^{\prime}],

\partial\cdot a-a\cdot\partial=\partial(a),

where in the first relation we mean to take the bracket of
\partial,\partial^{\prime}
(the Lie bracket on
\mathcal{T}_{X/S}
pulls back to a pairing on
\varphi^{*}(\mathcal{T}_{X/S})
) and in the second relation by
\partial(a)
we mean the element of
A
obtained by acting
\partial
on
a.

2.4.4.
The Azumaya algebra property

The fact that
(X/S)^{\dR}
is a gerbe over
X^{(1)}
is closely related to an observation of Bezrukavnikov
[
5
]
, that in the classical situation
F_{X/S,*}\mathcal{D}_{X/S}
is always an Azumaya algebra over its center, which can be identified with
\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{V}(\Omega^{1}_{X^{(1)}/S}}.

Unfortunately, Bezrukavnikov’s amazing Azumaya algebra property is not always true for stacks.

Example 2.55
.

Consider the canonical morphism
\pt\to B\mathbb{G}_{m}.
Using the Cartesian diagram
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathbb{G}_{m}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\pt}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\pt}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces B\mathbb{G}_{m},}

and that
\mathcal{D}_{\mathbb{G}_{m}/\pt}=k[t^{\pm 1},\partial_{t}],
we deduce via descent that
\mathcal{D}_{\pt/B\mathbb{G}_{m}}=k[t\partial_{t}].

In particular,
k[t\partial_{t}]
is a
commutative
algebra, and so
F_{\pt/B\mathbb{G}_{m},*}(k[t\partial_{t}])
will be a certain commutative algebra on

, and in particular have center much too large for the claim about the center being the cotangent bundle to hold.

Remark 2.56
.

This phenomenon is possible essentially because
\mathcal{D}_{X/S}
is defined via a certain Lie algebroid, and Lie algebroids do not pullback well (because of the anchor map). This prevents us from checking this Azumaya algebra property by a descent argument, and so the fact that the Azumaya algebra holds for
\mathbb{G}_{m}\to\pt
does not imply anything about the case of
\pt\to B\mathbb{G}_{m}.

Remark 2.57
.

Another way of understanding this failure is to note that the usual proof of the Azumaya property essentially goes by choosing etale coordinates, and then checking using those etale coordinates. But unfortunately,
\pt
is too small and so we can’t reduce to an easier to check case.

As alluded to in the proof of

2.5.1.
Technical point: representability of

We start with a useful lemma about the relative Frobenius.

Lemma 2.58
.

Let
f:X\to S
be a smooth, representable morphism of
\mathbb{F}_{p}
-stacks, such that
S
admits a flat cover by a scheme.

Then the relative Frobenius
F_{X/S}:X\to X^{(1)}
is representable and qcqs; in fact, it is even finite. Moreover,
F_{X/S}
is faithfully flat.

Proof.

First, we prove representability
by algebraic spaces
. As
S
has an fppf cover by a scheme, we can base change and assume
S
is a scheme – it suffices to check representability after such a base change because representability by algberaic spaces can be checked fppf locally on the base; see tag 04ZP of the Stacks Project
[
3
]
.

And in the situation where
S
is a scheme, representability of
X
allows us to deduce that
X
is a scheme, and so
F_{X/S}:X\to X^{(1)}
is just a morphism of schemes, meaning of course it is representable by algebraic spaces. For smooth morphisms, the relative Frobenius is known to be finite and faithfully flat (see tag 0FW2 of the Stacks Project
[
3
]
); as these properties can also be checked after fppf base change, we deduce that
F_{X/S}
is representable by algebraic spacse, finite, and faithfully flat.

To conclude, we just need to prove that the relative Frobenius is representable by schemes. Take
T
a scheme, and
T\to X^{(1)}
a morphism. Then
X\times_{X^{(1)}}T\to T
is a finite morphism of algebraic spaces; by tag 03XX in the Stacks Project
[
3
]
, it follows that
X\times_{X^{(1)}}T
is automatically a scheme; in particular,
F_{X/S}
is representable by schemes, as desired.
∎

Remark 2.59
.

The purpose of
Lemma 2.58
is that, now that we know
F_{X/S}
is representable and qcqs, the (derived) pushforward

of fppf sheaves on

to fppf sheaves on

will send quasicoherent sheaves to quasicoherent sheaves.

This might seem pointless to check, since if

doesn’t send quasicoherents to quasicoherents we could just take the right adjoint to

However, because we are going to be pushforward things like connections

which are not
\mathcal{O}_{X}
-linear, it is vital to know that this is just the usual pushforward of sheaves.

\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\varphi}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\nabla^{\prime}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\nabla}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\varphi\otimes\id_{\Omega_{X/S}^{1}}}

commutes.

Writing
F_{X/S}^{*}\mathcal{F}=F_{X/S}^{-1}\mathcal{F}\otimes_{F_{X/S}^{-1}\mathcal{O}_{X^{(1)}}}\mathcal{O}_{X},
we have

\nabla^{\prime}(s\otimes f)=s^{\prime}\otimes df.

By adjunction, a morphism
\varphi:F_{X/S}^{*}\mathcal{F}\to\mathcal{E}
is equivalent to giving a morphism
\psi:\mathcal{F}\to F_{X/S,*}\mathcal{E},
and
\varphi,\psi
are related by the formula

\varphi(s\otimes f)=f\psi(s).

Thus asking that
\varphi
is compatible with the connections is equivalent to demanding that

f\nabla(\psi(s))+\psi(s)\otimes df=\psi(s)\otimes df

for all
f\in\mathcal{O}_{X},s\in\mathcal{F}.
Of course, this is equivalent to demanding that
\nabla(\psi(s))=0
for all
s,
or equivalently that
\psi
factors through
\Sol(\mathcal{E},\nabla).

∎

Remark 2.61
.

As

Remark 3.5

3.2.
Relative Cartier descent

Fix
f:X\to S
a smooth, representable morphism of algebraic
\mathbb{F}_{p}
-stacks. Assume
S
admits an fppf cover
\mathcal{U}\to S,
where
\mathcal{U}
is a scheme.

In this section, we will prove a relative version of Cartier descent, finally allowing us to characterize the essential image of
\nu_{X/S,\wedge}^{*}
in terms of
p
-curvature.

Construction 3.1
.

Let
(\mathcal{E},\nabla)\in\mathcal{D}_{\qc}((X/S)^{\widehat{\dR}}).
Then the
(\nu^{*},\Sol)
adjunction gives us a counit map

\epsilon:\nu^{*}\Sol(\mathcal{E},\nabla)\to(\mathcal{E},\nabla).

Applying
\pi^{*},
we get a map

(3.7)

F_{X/S}^{*}\Sol(\mathcal{E},\nabla)\to\mathcal{E}.

Theorem 3.8

(Relative Cartier descent)
.

The functor

\nu_{X/S,\wedge}^{*}:\mathcal{D}_{\qc}(X^{(1)})\to\mathcal{D}_{\qc}((X/S)^{\widehat{\dR}})

is fully faithful. Moreover, for an object
(\mathcal{E},\nabla)\in\mathcal{D}_{\qc}((X/S)^{\widehat{\dR}}),
the following are equivalent:

(1)

(\mathcal{E},\nabla)
lies in the essential image of
\nu_{X/S,\wedge}^{*},

(2)

the map
F_{X/S}^{*}\Sol(\mathcal{E},\nabla)\to\mathcal{E}
constructed in
Construction 3.1
is an equivalence,

(3)

the
p
-curvature morphism
\psi_{p}(\mathcal{E},\nabla):\mathcal{E}\to\mathcal{E}\otimes\nu_{X/S}^{*}\Omega_{X^{(1)}/S}^{1}
is the zero morphism.

Proof.

Proposition 2.60
already tells us about the fully faithfulness, so we just need to identify its essential image.

For an object
(\mathcal{E},\nabla)\in\mathcal{D}_{\qc}((X/S)^{\widehat{\dR}}),
we have that
(\mathcal{E},\nabla)
belongs to the essential image of
\nu_{X/S,\wedge}^{*}
if and only if the co-unit

\epsilon:\nu_{X/S,\wedge}^{*}(\Sol_{X/S}(\mathcal{E},\nabla))\to(\mathcal{E},\nabla)

is an equivalence.

As
\pi:X\to(X/S)^{\widehat{\dR}}
is a flat cover, by flat descent we have that
\epsilon
is an equivalence if and only if
\pi^{*}(\epsilon)
is an equivalence. But
\pi^{*}(\epsilon)
is exactly the morphism
(3.7)
, and so conditions 1 and 2 are equivalent.

We now show condition 1 implies condition 3; that is, we check every
\nu^{*}\mathcal{F}
has
p
-curvature zero. Recall that
p
-curvature is defined using the Cartesian diagram

(3.9)

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces B\mathbb{V}(F_{X}^{*}\mathcal{T}_{X/S})}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces(X/S)^{\widehat{\dR}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X^{(1)}.}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces F^{\prime}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\nu^{\prime}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\nu}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces F}

It follows from the commutativity of
(3.9)
that

(3.10)

F^{{}^{\prime},*}(\nu^{*}\mathcal{F})=\nu^{{}^{\prime},*}(F^{*}\mathcal{F}).

Using Cartier duality, we can describe objects of
\mathcal{D}_{\qc}(B\mathbb{V}(F_{X}^{*}\mathcal{T}_{X/S}))\simeq\mathcal{D}_{\qc}(\mathbb{V}(F_{X}^{*}\Omega_{X/S}^{1}))
as pairs
(\mathcal{E}^{\prime},\theta:\mathcal{E}^{\prime}\to\mathcal{E}^{\prime}\otimes F_{X}^{*}\Omega_{X/S}^{1})
where
\theta\wedge\theta=0.

In this description, we have

F^{{}^{\prime},*}(\nu^{*}\mathcal{F})=(F^{*}\mathcal{F},\pi^{*}(\psi_{p}(\nu^{*}\mathcal{F})))

(by definition of the
p
-curvature morphism) and

\nu^{{}^{\prime},*}(F^{*}\mathcal{F})=(F^{*}\mathcal{F},0).

Thus
(3.10)
tells us
\pi^{*}(\psi_{p}(\nu^{*}\mathcal{F}))=0,
which since
\pi
is a flat cover implies
\psi_{p}(\nu^{*}\mathcal{F})=0.
In particular, condition 1 implies condition 3.

Finally, we prove condition 3 implies condition 2; this is the hardest part of the argument. Assume condition 3 holds; we will show
\epsilon
is an equivalence. As being an equivalence can be checked on cohomology sheaves, it suffices to prove this assuming that
(\mathcal{E},\nabla)\in\mathcal{D}_{\qc}((X/S)^{\widehat{\dR}})^{\heartsuit}.

Moreover, as being an equivalence can be checked after flat descent, we can assume
S=\Spec k
is an affine scheme and
X=\mathbb{A}^{d}_{k}.
In this case, classical Cartier descent (see Theorem 5.1 of
[
23
]
) shows that our
\psi_{p}=0
assumption implies condition 2 holds, as desired.
∎

Hitchin-type morphisms

We now turn our attention towards non-abelian Hodge theory. To state our result, we will need some Hitchin morphisms; we now give some general setup in which one can define Hitchin morphisms, since we will use it twice below.

Generalities

Fix

Write

Notation 4.1

of

The characteristic polynomial of an

Definition 4.3

Construction 4.1

(Characteristic polynomials)

4.1.4.
The BNR correspondence

We now prove a variant of the Beauville–Narasimhan–Ramanan (BNR) correspondence for stacky curves, which we will use in our study of logarithmic non-abelian Hodge theory. Beauville–Narasimhan–Ramanan
[
4
]
proved the first version of the BNR correspondence for

[

]
later generalized their work to arbitrary special curves. Our treatment follows Schaub
[

]
.

So, we now will now specialize to the case where

is a multi-root stack over a smooth connected curve
C.
We let
\rho:X\to C
be the map to the course moduli space.

We now make a definition of torsion-free rank 1 sheaf which applies to spectral curves of such an

Definition 4.10
.

Let
\tilde{X}
be a multi-root stack over a curve
\tilde{C},
with
\tilde{\rho}:\tilde{X}\to\tilde{C}
the corresponding coarse moduli. We say that
\mathcal{F}\in\Qcoh(\tilde{X})
is

if
\mathcal{G}:=\tilde{\rho}_{*}\mathcal{F}
is a coherent sheaf on
\tilde{C}
which is torsion-free of rank 1 in the sense of Schaub
[
37
]
; we recall that to Schaub,
\mathcal{G}
is

(1)

torsion-free
if the map
\mathcal{G}\to\mathcal{G}\otimes Q(\mathcal{O}_{\tilde{C}})
(for
Q(\mathcal{O}_{\tilde{C}})
the total ring of fractions of
\mathcal{O}_{\tilde{C}}
) is injective, and

(2)

rank 1
if for every generic point
\eta_{i}
of
\tilde{C},
we have
\ell(\mathcal{G}_{\eta_{i}})=\ell(\mathcal{O}_{\tilde{C},\eta_{i}}),
where
\ell(-)
is is used to indicate the length of an
\mathcal{O}_{\tilde{C},\eta_{i}}
-module.

Example 4.11
.

If
\tilde{C}=\Spec k[x,t^{2}],
then there are two torsion-free coherent sheaves of rank 1:

(1)

the sheaf
\mathcal{O}_{\tilde{C}}
itself;

(2)

the coherent sheaf associated to the
k[x,t^{2}]
-module
M:=k[x]\oplus k[x],
where
t
acts as
t\cdot(f(x),g(x))=(0,f(x)).

It’s clear that
\mathcal{O}_{\tilde{C}}
is torsion-free of rank 1. For the second example, the torsion-freeness is clear, and the rank 1 condition is because
\tilde{C}
has a unique generic point
\eta
corresponding to the minimal prime
(t),
and

\ell(\mathcal{O}_{\tilde{C},\eta})=\ell((k[x,t]/(t^{2}))_{(t)})=2.

As

is a 2-dimensional

-vector space, we deduce it has length 2 as well, and hence we get our rank 1 condition.

Proposition 4.12

(The BNR correspondence)
.

Assume
X
is a multi-root stack over a smooth connected curve
C
.

Let
T
be a
k
-scheme, and fix a
T
-point point

. Let

denote the spectral curve corresponding to

Set
\mathcal{G}(T)
the groupoid of quasicoherent sheaves on

Define a map

(4.13)

of groupoids by sending
(\mathcal{E},\theta)
to the corresponding coherent sheaf on

Then the map
(4.13)
is a fully faithful. Moreover, for
\pi:Y_{a}\hookrightarrow\mathbb{V}(\omega_{T})\to X_{T}
the canonical map, the essential image of
(4.13)
consists exactly of those
\mathcal{F}\in\mathcal{G}(T)
which are torsion free of rank 1.

Proof.

The full faithfulness is immediate from the fact that the category of effective descent data for
\mathbb{V}(\omega_{T})\to X_{T}
emebds fully faithfully into the category of quasicoherent sheaves on
\mathbb{V}(\omega_{T}),
and so it suffices to check the claim about the essential image.

Now, we characterize the essential image. Suppose
\mathcal{F}\in\mathcal{G}(T)
is torsion free of rank 1. Then, as
\pi
is finite flat of rank
n,
we deduce that
\mathcal{E}:=\pi_{*}\mathcal{F}
is a rank
n
vector bundle
\mathcal{E}
on
X_{T},
using that
X_{T}
is just a multi-root stack over the corresponding spectral curve for

and applying Schaub
[
37
]
Lemma 2.2 (in the same way Schaub
[
37
]
applies it to deduce his proposition 2.1).

Moreover,
\mathcal{E}
has a natural Higgs field structure: just let

be the morphism obtained by remembering that

In particular,

4.2.
On the characteristic polynomial of

We now explain how to treat the characteristic polynomial of

Remark 4.15
.

Using de Rham stacks give us some advantage here; indeed, in the usual story of characteristic polynomials of
p
-curvature (see Laszlo-Pauly
[
25
]
, and also Zhang
[
14
]
), one views the
p
-curvature as a
F^{*}\Omega^{1}_{X/S}
-twisted linear endomorphism on
X

p
-curvature is correct, and indeed one has to prove a theorem constraining the possible characteristic polynomials of
p
-curvature to get the Hitchin bases to align. In our de Rham stack approach, this constraint just comes from the fact that the

-curvature actually lives on

Fix
f:X\to S
smooth, representable of relative dimension 1. Set
\omega:=\nu_{X/S,\wedge}^{*}\Omega^{1}_{X^{(1)}/S}.
By
Proposition 2.60
, the unit

\left(\Omega^{1}_{X^{(1)}/S}\right)^{\otimes i}\to\Sol(\omega^{\otimes i})

of the
(\nu^{*},\Sol)
adjunction is an equivalence for every
i\geq 0
. Taking global sections, we deduce that

H^{0}(X^{(1)},\omega_{X^{(1)}/S}^{\otimes i})\simeq H^{0}(X^{(1)},\Sol(\omega^{\otimes i})).

But by adjunction,

\displaystyle H^{0}(X^{(1)},\Sol(\omega^{\otimes i}))

\displaystyle=\Hom(\mathcal{O}_{X^{(1)}},\Sol(\omega^{\otimes i}))

\displaystyle=\Hom((\mathcal{O}_{X},d),\omega^{\otimes i})

\displaystyle=H^{0}((X/S)^{\widehat{\dR}},\omega^{\otimes i}).

Therefore

(4.16)

H^{0}((X/S)^{\widehat{\dR}},\omega^{\otimes i})=H^{0}(X^{(1)},(\Omega^{1}_{X^{(1)}/S})^{\otimes i}).

Thus the Hitchin base for
\omega
on
(X/S)^{\widehat{\dR}}
is exactly the same as the Hitchin base for
\Omega^{1}_{X^{(1)}/S}
on
X^{(1)}.

Theorem 5.4

(Olsson)

Let

k

with

Moreover,
\Omega^{1}_{D_{i}/B\mathbb{G}_{m}}=\varphi_{i}^{*}\Omega^{1}_{X}(\log D),

Proof.

We include this statement and proof for the reader’s convenience, but the ideas are all due to Olsson
[
35
]
,
[
36
]

To start we explain why

is representable; for this it suffices to prove each

is representable.

The fiber product
\mathbb{A}^{1}\times_{\mathbb{A}^{1}/\mathbb{G}_{m}}X
is just the frame bundle of the line bundle

and in particular is a scheme. As representability by

can be checked etale locally (see
[
3
]
, tag 04ZP), we deduce that

is representable by algebraic spaces. Checking an algebraic space is a scheme can be done Zariski locally, so we can now assume

is affine. Then

is an affine morphism of stacks (because affineness can be checked after a flat cover, and the pullback

is just the frame bundle of the line bundle

which will be affine whenever

is affine). It follows that, when
X
is affine,
c_{D}
is affine; hence
c_{D}
is representable by algebraic spaces and affine, implying it is representable by schemes.

Thus

is a smooth, representable morphism. Now, we compute its relative Kahler differentials. To start, we recall that there is a natural map

of sheaves on

We claim that this

is actually a derivation relative to

and that the induced map

\Omega_{X/\Theta^{n}}\to\Omega^{1}_{X/k}(\log D)

is an isomorphism of quasicoherent sheaves on

Both the property of being a derivation, and the property of a morphism being an equivalence, can be checked locally. Thus we can assume

and

is the map classifying the divisor

in

In this case, we have a Cartesian diagram
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathbb{A}^{n+m}\times\Spec k[t_{1}^{\pm 1},...,t_{n}^{\pm 1}]}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathbb{A}^{n+m}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathbb{A}^{n}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\Theta^{n},}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces c_{D}^{\prime}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces c_{D}}

and

generated by the symbols

In particular,

(The Azumaya property for

Let

for

Take

The logarithmic Frobenius twist

is the

along the divisor

Then

with the two generators having commutator

Note that

and

are both central elements of
\mathcal{D}_{X}(\log D).

The pushforward

thought of as a quasicoherent sheaf on

with parabolic structure (in the sense of Borne
[

]
), is just the

-algebra

with parabolic structure

The logarithmic cotangent bundle of

is just the trivial bundle. Writing

we can then upgrade

to an algebra on

by having

as multiplication by the central element

In this way, we view

as a quasicoherent algebra on

As the map

is faithfully flat, the induced map

of

is a matrix algebra. As a sheaf on

can be modelled as the parabolic sheaf

where

acts as
(x\partial)^{p}-x\partial.

View

as a sheaf on

via the parabolic bundle

\cdots\subseteq F^{1}=k[x,x\partial]\cdot x\subseteq F^{0}=k[x,x\partial]\subseteq F^{-1}=k[x,x\partial]\cdot x^{-1}\subseteq\cdots,

where again we have

The structure sheaf of

can be identified as the subbundle

of

We denote this subbundle by

Consider now the sheaf of endomorphisms

as a

-module. There is a natural map of parabolic bundles

The diagram
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces G}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\Spec(k)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\Spec(k)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces BG}

is Cartesian even in the world of derived stacks; thus applying

we find that
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces G^{\dR}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\Spec(k)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\Spec(k)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces(BG)^{\dR}}

is Cartesian (since formation of de Rham stacks commutes with derived limits).

In characteristic 0, finite type group schemes are smooth; in positive characteristic, finite type group schemes are quasisyntomic. Thus
G\to\Spec(k)
is a quasisynotomic surjection; hence
\Spec(k)\to BG
is a quasisyntomic surjection. As formation of
\dR
preserves quasisynotomic covers, we deduce that
\Spec(k)\to(BG)^{\dR}
is still a quasisyntomic cover. Hence we have a surjection
\pt\to(BG)^{\dR}
whose fiber is

; this is exactly the data needed to identify
B(G^{\dR})\simeq(BG)^{\dR},
so we conclude. The same argument works for
\widehat{\dR}.

∎

Corollary 5.19
.

We have

\mathbb{G}_{m}^{\dR}=\cone(\mathbb{G}_{m}^{\#}\to\mathbb{G}_{m}),

\mathbb{G}_{m}^{\widehat{\dR}}=\cone(\mathbb{G}_{m}^{\#,\wedge}\to\mathbb{G}_{m}),

B\mathbb{G}_{m}^{\dR}=\cone(B\mathbb{G}_{m}^{\#}\to B\mathbb{G}_{m}),

B\mathbb{G}_{m}^{\widehat{\dR}}=\cone(B\mathbb{G}_{m}^{\#,\wedge}\to B\mathbb{G}_{m}).

Moreover, under these canonical identifications, the morphisms

B\mathbb{G}_{m}\to B\mathbb{G}_{m}^{\dR}\to B\mathbb{G}_{m}^{\widehat{\dR}}

become the canonical maps

B\mathbb{G}_{m}\to\cone(B\mathbb{G}_{m}^{\#}\to B\mathbb{G}_{m})\to\cone(B\mathbb{G}_{m}^{\#,\wedge}\to B\mathbb{G}_{m}).

Proof.

The first two claims follow immediately from the definition of (sheared) de Rham stacks; the other claims then follow from
Lemma 5.16
.
∎

Corollary 5.20
.

We have

(\pt/B\mathbb{G}_{m})^{\dR}\simeq B\mathbb{G}_{m}^{\#},

and

(\pt/B\mathbb{G}_{m})^{\widehat{\dR}}\simeq B\mathbb{G}_{m}^{\#,\wedge}.

Proof.

By definition,
(\pt/B\mathbb{G}_{m})^{\dR}
is defined as the fiber product
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces(\pt/B\mathbb{G}_{m})^{\dR}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\pt^{\dR}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces B\mathbb{G}_{m}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces B\mathbb{G}_{m}^{\dR}.}

As

and

is just the canonical map

∎

Remark 5.23
.

It follows from

of a vector space and a residue.

We also spell out the
p
-curvature for
\pt\to B\mathbb{G}_{m}.
The relative Frobenius twist of
\pt
over
B\mathbb{G}_{m}
is
B\mu_{p}.
As
\mathbb{L}_{B\mu_{p}/B\mathbb{G}_{m}}\simeq\mathcal{O}_{B\mu_{p}}
(computed for instance by pulling back along
\pt\to B\mathbb{G}_{m}
to get
\mathbb{G}_{m}/\mu_{p}\simeq\mathbb{G}_{m}
), we find that
(\pt/B\mathbb{G}_{m})^{\widehat{\dR}}\to B\mu_{p}
is a
\mathbb{G}_{a}^{\#,\wedge}
-gerbe. This is exactly what we’d expect:
(\pt/B\mathbb{G}_{m})^{\widehat{\dR}}=B\mathbb{G}_{m}^{\#,\wedge},
and the map

B\mathbb{G}_{m}^{\#,\wedge}\to B\mu_{p}

is induced by the group homomorphism
\mathbb{G}_{m}^{\#,\wedge}\to\mu_{p},
which is surjective with kernel
\mathbb{G}_{a}^{\#,\wedge}
according to the following lemma.

Lemma 5.24
.

There is a short exact sequence of commutative affine group schemes

(5.25)

0\to\mathbb{G}_{a}^{\#,\wedge}\to\mathbb{G}_{m}^{\#,\wedge}\to\mu_{p}\to 0.

Moreover,
\mathbb{G}_{m}^{\#,\wedge}\to\mu_{p}
admits a section
at the level of schemes
.

Warning 5.26
.

The sequence
(5.25)
is not split as a short exact sequence of group schemes! We only construct a scheme-theoretic section; it will not respect the group laws. In fact, no section can be a group homomorphism, because the Cartier dual to
(5.25)
is the Artin-Schreier sequence

0\to\mathbb{Z}/p\to\mathbb{G}_{a}\to\mathbb{G}_{a}\to 0,

which famously is not split.

Remark 5.27
.

Sanath Devalapurkar, in a private communication to the author, explained that the non-sheared version of
(5.25)
splits group theoretically, and hence
(\pt/B\mathbb{G}_{m})^{\dR}\to B\mu_{p}
is the trivial gerbe. We will not need this in this paper, so we do not include it.

Proof.

For an
\mathbb{F}_{p}
-algebra
A,
we have

\mu_{p}(A)=\{a\in A\mid a^{p}=1\},

\mathbb{G}_{m}^{\#,\wedge}(A)=\{(a,\gamma_{\bullet})\mid a\in A^{\times},\gamma_{\bullet}\text{ is a system of nilpotent divided powers for $1-a$}\},

\mathbb{G}_{a}^{\#,\wedge}(A)=\{(a,\gamma_{\bullet})\mid a\in A,\gamma_{\bullet}\text{ is a system of nilpotent divided powers for $a$}\}.

For surjectivity, just note that if
a^{p}=1,
then
1-a
admits the system of nilpotent divided powers

\gamma_{n}(1-a):=\begin{cases}\frac{(1-a)^{n}}{n!}&n<p,\\
0&n\geq p.\end{cases}

In fact, this defines a function

s(A):\mu_{p}(A)\to\mathbb{G}_{m}^{\#,\wedge}(A)

which is clearly functorial in
A,
and hence gives us a scheme-theoretic section
s:\mu_{p}\to\mathbb{G}_{m}^{\#,\wedge}
of
\mathbb{G}_{m}^{\#,\wedge}\to\mu_{p}.

The kernel of
\mathbb{G}_{m}^{\#,\wedge}\to\mu_{p}
is then given by all the systems of divided powers for
0\in A.

These are equivalent to giving elements
\gamma_{n}\in A
so that

\gamma_{0}=1,\gamma_{1}=0,

\gamma_{n}\cdot\gamma_{m}=\frac{(n+m)!}{n!m!}\gamma_{n+m}.

It’s easy to see these conditions imply
\gamma_{n}=0
for all
n<p.
Hence if
p\nmid n,
the second condition let’s us write

\frac{n!}{i!(n-i)!}\gamma_{n}=\gamma_{n-i}\cdot\gamma_{i}=0

for
i
the remainder of
n
when divided by
p.
Note that
n!/(i!(n-i)!)
is an integer not divisible by
p
, and hence
\gamma_{n}=0
as well.

However, the
\gamma_{pn}
have no reason to be zero. They are just elements of
A
obeying

\gamma_{p\cdot 0}=1,

\gamma_{pn}\cdot\gamma_{pm}=\frac{(pn+pm)!}{(pn)!(pm)!}\gamma_{p(n+m)}=\frac{(n+m)!}{n!m!}\gamma_{p(n+m)}.

The last equality is from Lucas’ theorem.

Hence giving the
\gamma_{n}
are equivalent to giving some
\gamma_{p}\in A
together with any PD structure on
\gamma_{p}.
This is exactly the data of an element of
\mathbb{G}_{a}^{\#}(A),
and so we conclude.
∎

The Artin-Schreier sequence appearing in
Warning 5.26
allows us to compute
p
-curvature in terms of residue.

Lemma 5.28
.

Let
(V,\res)
be a quasicoherent sheaf on
(\pt/B\mathbb{G}_{m})^{\widehat{\dR}}.
Then the
p
-curvature morphism

\psi_{p}:V\to V\otimes F^{*}\Omega_{B\mu_{p}/B\mathbb{G}_{m}}=V

is just the endomorphism

\psi_{p}=\res^{p}-\res.

Proof.

Recall that
p
-curvature is defined in terms of the Cartesian diagram
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces(\pt/B\mathbb{G}_{m})^{\widehat{\dR}}\times_{B\mu_{p}}\pt}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces(\pt/B\mathbb{G}_{m})^{\widehat{\dR}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\pt}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces B\mu_{p}.}

As
(\pt/B\mathbb{G}_{m})^{\widehat{\dR}}=B\mathbb{G}_{m}^{\#,\wedge},
we find from
Lemma 5.24
that the fiber product is just
B\mathbb{G}_{a}^{\#,\wedge},
with the map
B\mathbb{G}_{a}^{\#,\wedge}\to B\mathbb{G}_{m}^{\#,\wedge}
in the above Cartesian diagram being the one induced by the inclusion
\mathbb{G}_{a}^{\#,\wedge}\to\mathbb{G}_{m}^{\#,\wedge}.

As in

, after applying Cartier duality this inclusion becomes the Artin-Schreier cover; as

and residue are both defined in terms of Cartier duality, we deduce that

is the image of

as desired.
∎

5.4.2.

Theorem 5.29
.

Let

, and

a reduced effective divisor in

with

Let

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces(\mathbb{A}^{1}/\mathbb{G}_{m})^{n}}

.

The map

∎

5.4.3.
Residue and Cartier descent

Take some
\mathcal{V}\in\Qcoh(X^{(1)}).
It’s pullback
\nu^{*}\mathcal{V}\in\Qcoh(X^{\dR}(\log D))
is some vector bundle with logarithmic connection; and in particular, along each component
D_{i}
of
D,
we can form the residue of
\nu^{*}\mathcal{V}
along
D_{i}.

Is there an easy way to read off this residue directly from
\mathcal{V}
? The answer is, fortunately, yes; it will turn out, as we shall see, that the parabolic structure of
\mathcal{V}
encodes the residue of
\nu^{*}\mathcal{V}.

Each inclusion
D_{i}\hookrightarrow X
gives rise to a commutative diagram
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces(\pt/B\mathbb{G}_{m})^{\dR}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X^{\dR}(\log D)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces B\mu_{p}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X^{(1)}}

of relative de Rham stacks. As the residue is obtained by pulling back along
(\pt/B\mathbb{G}_{m})^{\dR}\to X^{\dR}(\log D),
using this commutative diagram we see that the residue of
\nu^{*}\mathcal{V}
can be computed by first pulling back
\mathcal{V}
to
B\mu_{p}.

Proposition 5.31
.

Let
\mathcal{V}\in\Qcoh(X^{(1)}).
Then
\nu^{*}\mathcal{V}
has residue zero along each
D_{i}
if and only if
\mathcal{V}
has trivial parabolic structure (that is,
\mathcal{V}
lies in the essential image of
\pi^{*}:\Qcoh(X^{\prime})\to\Qcoh(X^{(1)})
).

Proof.

This is proven essentially by staring at the commutative diagram
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X^{\dR}(\log D)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X^{\dR}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X^{(1)}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X^{\prime}.}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\pi^{\prime}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\nu}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\nu^{\prime}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\pi}

Indeed,
\nu^{*}\mathcal{V}
has zero residue along each
D_{i}
if and only if it is a usual connection (that is, doesn’t have any logarithmic poles) if and only if it lies in the essential image of
(\pi^{\prime})^{*}:\Qcoh(X^{\dR})\to\Qcoh(X^{\dR}(\log D)).

Thus, the commuativity of our diagram immediately implies that if
\mathcal{V}
has trivial parabolic structure, then
\nu^{*}\mathcal{V}
has residue zero along each
D_{i}.
And conversely, if
\nu^{*}\mathcal{V}
has residue zero, then we can write
\nu^{*}\mathcal{V}=(\pi^{\prime})^{*}(\mathcal{E},\nabla)
; but
\nabla
will have
p
-curvature zero since
\nu^{*}\mathcal{V}
does, and so by Cartier descent we deduce
(\mathcal{E},\nabla)=(\nu^{\prime})^{*}(\mathcal{V}^{\prime})
for some
\mathcal{V}^{\prime}.
Thus

\nu^{*}\mathcal{V}\simeq\nu^{*}(\pi^{*}\mathcal{V}^{\prime}),

which by fully faithfulness of
\nu^{*}
implies
\mathcal{V}\simeq\pi^{*}\mathcal{V}^{\prime},
as desired.
∎

6.
Logarithmic non-abelian Hodge theory

We now prove our logarithmic non-abelian Hodge theorem for curves in positive characteristic. We begin by fixing our setup.

Fix
k
an
algebraically closed
field of positive characteristic
p,
and
X
a smooth proper curve over
k.
Take
D
a reduced effective divisor, with components
D_{1},...,D_{n}\in X(k)
(so these
D_{i}
are just distinct points).

We set

and let

be the morphism classifying

(see

).

We write

for the Frobenius twist of

relative to

, and

for the Frobenius twist of

Recall from
Proposition 5.9
that

is the
(p,p,...,p)
-multiroot stack of

along the points

We also fix a positive integer

6.1.
Stating the etale local equivalence

The non-abelian Hodge theorem concerns two moduli stacks: a moduli stack of vector bundles with connection, and a moduli stack of Higgs fields. We now introduce both.

6.1.1.
The de Rham side

In our situation,
\nu_{X/S,\wedge}^{*}\Omega_{X^{(1)}/S}^{1}
is a line bundle on
(X/S)^{\widehat{\dR}}.
Applying
Section 4.1
to the
k
-stack
(X/S)^{\widehat{\dR}}
and the line bundle
\nu_{X/S,\wedge}^{*}\Omega_{X^{(1)}/S}^{1}
, we get a Hitchin morphism

(6.1)

h:\mathcal{M}((X/S)^{\widehat{\dR}},r,\nu_{X/S,\wedge}^{*}\Omega_{X^{(1)}/S}^{1})\to\mathcal{H}((X/S)^{\widehat{\dR}},r,\nu_{X/S,\wedge}^{*}\Omega_{X^{(1)}/S}^{1}).

Thanks to
(4.16)
, the Hitchin base of
(6.1)
is actually the same as the Hitchin base
\mathcal{H}(X^{(1)},r,\Omega_{X^{(1)}/S}^{1}).
We will now, following Groechenig, label this Hitchin base as

\mathcal{A}^{(1)}:=\mathcal{H}(X^{(1)},r,\Omega_{X^{(1)}/S}^{1}).

The moduli stack
\mathcal{M}((X/S)^{\widehat{\dR}},r,\nu_{X/S,\wedge}^{*}\Omega_{X^{(1)}/S}^{1})
is still a little big, though. We define
\mathcal{M}_{\dR}(X/S)
the moduli stack of rank
r
vector bundles on
(X/S)^{\widehat{\dR}}.
By
Section 3.1.2
, taking

-curvature of a vector bundle with flat connection gives us a natural morphism of stacks

\psi_{p}:\mathcal{M}_{\dR}(X/S)\to\mathcal{M}((X/S)^{\widehat{\dR}},r,\nu_{X/S,\wedge}^{*}\Omega_{X^{(1)}/S}^{1})

We define

h_{\dR}:\mathcal{M}_{\dR}(X/S)\to\mathcal{A}^{(1)}

to be the composition

Remark 6.2
.

A

-point of
\mathcal{M}_{\dR}(X/S)
is, by definition, a rank
r
vector bundle on

(X/S)^{\widehat{\dR}}\times_{k}T.

We can rewrite this in a convenient way. As

is a field,

is automatically flat; hence

is a flat

-scheme, and so the Cartesian diagram
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X_{T}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces S_{T}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces S}

is Cartesian even in the derived sense; as formation of de Rham stacks commutes with derived limits, we deduce that
(X_{T})^{\widehat{\dR}}\simeq X^{\widehat{\dR}}\times_{S^{\widehat{\dR}}}(S_{T})^{\widehat{\dR}}.
Thus

\displaystyle(X_{T}/S_{T})^{\widehat{\dR}}

\displaystyle:=(X_{T})^{\widehat{\dR}}\times_{(S_{T})^{\widehat{\dR}}}S_{T}

\displaystyle\simeq(X^{\widehat{\dR}}\times_{S^{\widehat{\dR}}}(S_{T})^{\widehat{\dR}})\times_{(S_{T})^{\widehat{\dR}}}S_{T}

\displaystyle\simeq X^{\widehat{\dR}}\times_{S^{\widehat{\dR}}}(S\times_{k}T)

\displaystyle\simeq(X^{\widehat{\dR}}\times_{S^{\widehat{\dR}}}S)\times_{k}T

\displaystyle\simeq(X/S)^{\widehat{\dR}}\times_{k}T.

Thus

-points of
\mathcal{M}_{\dR}(X/S)
are just the rank

vector bundles on
(X_{T}/S_{T})^{\widehat{\dR}}.

6.1.2.
The Dolbeault side

The Dolbeault side is, fortunately, much quicker to define: we put

\mathcal{M}_{\Dol}(X^{(1)}):=\mathcal{M}(X^{(1)},r,\Omega_{X^{(1)}/S}^{1}).

Applying
Section 4.1
in this situation gives us a Hitchin morphism

h_{\Dol}:\mathcal{M}_{\Dol}(X^{(1)})\to\mathcal{A}^{(1)}.

6.1.3.
The equivalence statement

We can now state the first form of our non-abelian Hodge theorem.

Theorem 6.3

(Logarithmic non-abelian Hodge theory)
.

There is an etale cover
\mathcal{U}\to\mathcal{A}^{(1)}
such that, after base changing to
\mathcal{U},
there is an isomorphism of
\mathcal{U}
-stacks

\mathcal{M}_{\dR}(X/S)\times_{\mathcal{A}^{(1)}}\mathcal{U}\simeq\mathcal{M}_{\Dol}(X^{(1)})\times_{\mathcal{A}^{(1)}}\mathcal{U}.

Actually,
Theorem 6.3
will follow from a slightly more precise statement; but to make this more precise comparison, we first need to introduce the

of the Azumaya algebra
F_{X/S,*}\mathcal{D}_{X/S}.
In fact, most of the proof of

is really just getting in to a position where the objects of the precise statement are defined; once they are, proving the more precise statement is simple.

Remark 6.4
.

Our proof of
Theorem 6.3
is basically identical to Groechenig’s
[
21
]
proof in the non-logarithmic setting. We view this as an advantage to our approach, as it reveals that, when phrased appropriately, Groechenig’s proof also covers logarithmic non-abelian Hodge theory; more generally, it seems that most arguments involving de Rham stacks should work in the relative case without much additional input, allowing one to use Olsson’s ideas to get logarithmic versions of results.

Put

Let

Lemma 6.19

Let
\mathcal{E}

Let

Forgetting the parabolic structures on
\mathcal{E}

is a sheaf of
\mathcal{O}_{X}
-modules; moreover, by compatibility of relative Frobenius with open immersions, we deduce that

To upgrade this to a statement on

we will argue that for any torsionfree coherent

there is a unique way of giving

And indeed, consider a point

Choose a uniformizer

A parabolic structure for

Lemma 6.25

is, by definition, a line bundle
\mathcal{L}
on
C^{(1)}_{a}
; and a
T
-point of
\mathcal{M}_{\Dol}(X^{(1)})
is, by the BNR correspondence
Proposition 4.12
, equivalent to a torsion free rank 1 coherent sheaf
\mathcal{F}
on
C^{(1)}_{a}.
The tensor product
\mathcal{L}\otimes\mathcal{F}
is still a torsion free rank 1 coherent sheaf on
C^{(1)}_{a},
and hence by
Proposition 4.12
is a
T
-point of
\mathcal{M}_{\Dol}(X^{(1)}).
In this way, we get an action of
\mathcal{P}
on
\mathcal{M}_{\Dol}(X^{(1)}).

We will now seek to prove the following more precise variant of
Theorem 6.3
.

Theorem 6.29
.

There is an equivalence of
\mathcal{A}^{(1)}
-stacks

\mathcal{S}\times^{\mathcal{P}}\mathcal{M}_{\Dol}(X^{(1)})\simeq\mathcal{M}_{\dR}(X).

Before proving
Theorem 6.29
, we explain why it implies
Theorem 6.3
.

Proof of
Theorem 6.3
.

By
Theorem 6.27
,
\mathcal{S}
is an etale
\mathcal{P}
-torsor. Hence after some etale cover
U\to\mathcal{A}^{(1)}
we have
\mathcal{S}_{U}\simeq\mathcal{P}_{U},
and so after pulling back to
U,

Theorem 6.29
becomes

\mathcal{M}_{\Dol}(X^{(1)})\times_{\mathcal{A}^{(1)}}U\simeq\mathcal{S}_{U}\times^{\mathcal{P}_{U}}(\mathcal{M}_{\Dol}(X^{(1)})\times_{\mathcal{A}^{(1)}}U)\simeq\mathcal{M}_{\dR}(X)\times_{\mathcal{A}^{(1)}}U,

as desired.
∎

To prove
Theorem 6.3
, we will follow the strategy of Groechenig
[
21
]
in the non-logarithmic case. Thus we first prove a
\mathcal{P}
-equivariant analogue of
Theorem 6.3
, and then take a quotient.

Lemma 6.30

(Logarithmic version of Groechenig
[
21
]
Theorem 3.29)
.

There is an isomorphism

(6.31)

\varphi:\mathcal{S}\times_{\mathcal{A}^{(1)}}\mathcal{M}_{\Dol}\simeq\mathcal{S}\times_{\mathcal{A}^{(1)}}\mathcal{M}_{\dR}

of
\mathcal{A}^{(1)}
-stacks.

Proof.

Let
T
be a test scheme, equipped with a map
a:T\to\mathcal{A}^{(1)}
. The
T
-points of
\mathcal{S}\times_{\mathcal{A}^{(1)}}\mathcal{M}_{\Dol}
form the groupoid of triples
(\mathcal{V},\varphi:\End(\mathcal{V})\simeq\mathcal{D}_{a},\mathcal{L}),
where
(\mathcal{V},\varphi)
is a splitting of the Azumaya algebra
\mathcal{D}_{a}
on the spectral curve
C^{(1)}_{a},
and
\mathcal{L}
is a rank 1 torsion free coherent sheaf on
C^{(1)}_{a}.

By
Proposition 6.20
, the groupoid of
T
-points of
\mathcal{S}\times_{\mathcal{A}^{(1)}}\mathcal{M}_{\dR}
is the groupoid of triples
(\mathcal{V},\varphi:\End(\mathcal{V})\simeq\mathcal{D}_{a},\mathcal{E}),
where
(\mathcal{V},\varphi)
is a splitting of
\mathcal{D}_{a}
and
\mathcal{E}
is a torsion free, rank
p
coherent sheaf on
C^{(1)}_{a}
together with a
\mathcal{D}_{a}
-module structure.

Define

\varphi(T)(\mathcal{V},\varphi,\mathcal{L})=(\mathcal{V},\varphi,\mathcal{L}\otimes\mathcal{V}).

Note that
\mathcal{L}\otimes\mathcal{V}
is torsionfree of rank
p,
and has a natural action of
\End(\mathcal{V}),
which thanks to
\varphi
furnishes a
\mathcal{D}_{a}
-module structure on
\mathcal{E}\otimes\mathcal{V}.
This is how we view
(\mathcal{V},\varphi,\mathcal{L}\otimes\mathcal{V})
as a
T
-point of
\mathcal{S}\times_{\mathcal{A}^{(1)}}\mathcal{M}_{\dR}.

To see essential surjectivity of
\varphi(T),
take some a
T
-point
(\mathcal{V},\varphi,\mathcal{E})
on the de Rham side. Then
\mathcal{L}:=\underline{\Hom}_{\mathcal{D}_{a}}(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{E})
will be torsion free of rank 1, and the evaluation map
\mathcal{L}\otimes\mathcal{V}\to\mathcal{E}
is an euqivalence. The full faithfulness of
\varphi(T)
is similar, and so we conclude.
∎

6.3.1.
The proof of
Theorem 6.29

To conclude our proof of
Theorem 6.29
, we will essentially quotient the equivalence
(6.31)
by
\mathcal{P}.
This strategy is slightly different than Groechenig’s
[
21
]
original approach, and is essentially identical to the non-logarithmic argument made by de Cataldo–Groechenig–Zhang
[
16
]
in their Section 4.2.

First, observe that
\mathcal{P}
has natural actions on both sides of
(6.31)
: multiplication by
\mathcal{T}\in\mathcal{P}(T)
can be defined to

(1)

send
(\mathcal{V},\varphi,\mathcal{L})
to
(\mathcal{V}\otimes\mathcal{T},\varphi,\mathcal{L}\otimes\mathcal{T}^{-1}),

(2)

send
(\mathcal{V},\varphi,\mathcal{E})
to
(\mathcal{E}\otimes\mathcal{T},\varphi,\mathcal{E}).

With these actions, the isomorphism
\varphi
of
Lemma 6.30
is
\mathcal{P}
-equivariant. Hence, quotienting by
\mathcal{P},
it induces an isomorphism of stacks

\mathcal{S}\times^{\mathcal{P}}\mathcal{M}_{\Dol}\simeq(\mathcal{S}/\mathcal{P})\times_{\mathcal{A}^{(1)}}\mathcal{M}_{\dR}.

As
\mathcal{S}
is a
\mathcal{P}
-torsor, the quotient
\mathcal{S}/\mathcal{P}\simeq\mathcal{A}^{(1)},
and hence we deduce
Theorem 6.29
.

6.4.
de Cataldo–Zhang’s Hitchin base

In the rest of this section, we will explain how to use our logarithmic non-abelian Hodge theorem to prove de Cataldo–Zhang’s
[
15
]
logarithmic non-abelian Hodge theorem.

To start, we of course must
state
their theorem. This requires us to introduce a
new
Hitchin base. In the logarithmic setting, besides
p
-curvature, the de Rham side has another linear invariant: the
residue
of a connection.

By
Theorem 5.29
, each
P_{i}\in D
gives us a map

(\pt/B\mathbb{G}_{m})^{\widehat{\dR}}\xrightarrow{P_{i}^{\widehat{\dR}}}X^{\widehat{\dR}}(\log D),

pullback along which realizes residue. Applying
Construction 4.2
to these morphisms, we produce a map

\mathcal{A}^{(1)}\to\prod_{P_{i}\in D}\mathbb{A}^{r},

where we view
\mathbb{A}^{r}
as the Hitchin base on the point. There are similarly natural maps

h_{i}:\mathcal{M}_{\dR}(X)\to\mathbb{A}^{r}

for each
P_{i}\in D,
sending a connection to the characteristic polynomial of its residue at
P_{i}.
By
Lemma 5.28
, the characteristic polynomial of the residue at
P_{i},
and the characteristic polynomial of
\psi_{p}
at
P_{i},
are tightly related. This inspired de Cataldo-Zhang
[
15
]
to introduce the following modification of the Hitchin base.

Definition 6.32

(de Cataldo–Zhang’s
[
15
]
Hitchin base)
.

Following de Cataldo–Zhang
[
15
]
, we let
\mathcal{A}^{\DCZ}
denote the fiber product
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{A}^{\DCZ}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{A}^{(1)}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\prod_{P_{i}\in D}\mathbb{A}^{r}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\prod_{P_{i}\in D}\mathbb{A}^{r},}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\AS}

As mentioned above,

The stack of splittings

Identifying

Let

To deduce

6.5.
Residues and parabolic structures

Our strategy for showing
Theorem 6.38
will be to prove that
\mathcal{P}^{\DCZ}\hookrightarrow\mathcal{P}
can alternatively be characterized as the subgroupoid of line bundles which fix the residue of a connection. Once we have this characterization, deducing
Theorem 6.38
from our
Theorem 6.29
will be formal.

Fix a
T
-point
a:T\to\mathcal{A}^{(1)}
of the Hitchin base, for
T
a
k
-scheme. Let
\nabla:\mathcal{E}\to\mathcal{E}\otimes\Omega^{1}_{X_{T}/S_{T}}
be a logarithmic connection on a rank
r
vector bundle
\mathcal{E}
on
X_{T},
whose
p
-curvature
\psi_{p}
has characteristic polynomial
a.
The de Rham BNR correspondence associates to this
\nabla
a torsion free rank
p
coherent sheaf
\mathcal{F}
on
C^{(1)}_{a},
together with a
\mathcal{D}_{a}
-module structure.

Proposition 6.39
.

Let
\mathcal{L}\in\mathcal{P}(a)
be a line bundle on
C^{(1)}_{a}.
The following are equivalent:

(1)

\mathcal{L}
has trivial parabolic structure;

(2)

for
any
such connection
(\mathcal{E},\nabla),

\mathcal{F}
and
\mathcal{L}\otimes\mathcal{F}
correspond to
\mathcal{D}_{X_{T}/S_{T}}
-modules with the same residue at every point;

(3)

for
one
connection
(\mathcal{E},\nabla)
as above,
\mathcal{F}
and
\mathcal{L}\otimes\mathcal{F}
correspond to
\mathcal{D}_{X_{T}/S_{T}}
-modules with the same residue at every point.

Proof.

It’s easier to prove this if we think about how
\mathcal{L}
acts on
(\mathcal{E},\nabla)
directly, instead of juggling these intermediaries
\mathcal{F}.
For this, just note that a line bundle
\mathcal{L}
on
C^{(1)}_{a}
will pushforward to a rank
r
vector bundle
\mathcal{V}
on
X^{(1)}
under the finite flat degree
r
map
C^{(1)}_{a}\to X^{(1)}.
Then
\mathcal{L}\otimes\mathcal{F}
is the de Rham BNR partner of
\mathcal{E}\otimes F_{X/S}^{*}\mathcal{V},
with its natural tensor product connection.

By
Proposition 5.31
, the residue of the natural connection on
F_{X/S}^{*}\mathcal{V}
is zero if and only if
\mathcal{V}
has trivial parabolic structure. In a tensor product of connections, the residue is
\res\otimes\id+\id\otimes\res^{\prime},
and thus we deduce immediately that our three conditions are equivalent.
∎

6.5.1.
Deducing de Cataldo–Zhang’s theorem

Proof of
Theorem 6.38
.

Fix some
\mathcal{E}\in\mathcal{S}_{a}.

For any
(\mathcal{E}^{\prime},\mathcal{L}^{\prime})\in(\mathcal{S}\times^{\mathcal{P}}\mathcal{M}_{\Dol})_{a},
by transitivity of
\mathcal{P}
on
\mathcal{S},
we can find some
\mathcal{L}
so that
(\mathcal{E}^{\prime},\mathcal{L}^{\prime})\simeq(\mathcal{E},\mathcal{L})
in the groupoid
(\mathcal{S}^{\times{\mathcal{P}}}\mathcal{M}_{\Dol})(a).
By
Proposition 6.39
, because
\mathcal{E}
and
\mathcal{E}\otimes\mathcal{L}
have the same spectral data, we deduce
\mathcal{L}
has trivial parabolic structure.

Thus the map

(6.40)

(\mathcal{S}\times^{\tilde{\mathcal{P}^{\DCZ}}}\tilde{\mathcal{M}_{\Dol}^{\DCZ}})(a)\to(\mathcal{S}\times^{\mathcal{P}}\mathcal{M}_{\Dol})(a)

is essentially surjective, since we can represent every object of the target groupoid via a pair
(\mathcal{E},\mathcal{L})
where
\mathcal{L}
has trivial parabolic structure.

It is also fully faithful, because if
(\mathcal{E},\mathcal{L}),(\mathcal{E}^{\prime},\mathcal{L}^{\prime})
are two objects of the source category (so,
\mathcal{L},\mathcal{L}^{\prime}
both have trivial parabolic structure), then morphisms

(\mathcal{E},\mathcal{L})\to(\mathcal{E}^{\prime},\mathcal{L}^{\prime})

are equivalent to giving a triple consisitng of

(1)

a line bundle
\mathcal{V}
on
C^{(1)}_{a},

(2)

an isomorphism
\mathcal{E}\otimes\mathcal{V}\simeq\mathcal{E}^{\prime}
of
\mathcal{D}_{a}
-modules, and

(3)

an isomorphism
\mathcal{V}^{\vee}\otimes\mathcal{L}\simeq\mathcal{L}^{\prime}.

In the source category, morphisms are the same but the line bundle
\mathcal{V}
is required to have trivial parabolic structure. But again by
Proposition 6.39
, an ismomorphism
\mathcal{E}\otimes\mathcal{V}\simeq\mathcal{E}^{\prime}
existing implies
\mathcal{V}
has trivial parabolic structure.
∎
