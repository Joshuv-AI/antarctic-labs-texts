Distribution and antibiotic resistance profiles of uropathogens at Madan Bhandari Hospital, Nepal: Focus on multidrug and extensively drug-resistant strains
Authors: Rahi Bikram Thapa, Prakash Chandra Karkee, Sabin Shrestha, Neha Yadav, Rewati Pokharel, Usha Bhattarai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Multidrug-resistant and extensively drug-resistant uropathogens are highly prevalent in Eastern Nepal.•E. coli and Klebsiella spp. are the main resistant strains.•Meropenem, doxycycline, and nitrofurantoin show strong efficacy.•Secondary hospital study informs region-specific urinary tract infection treatment.Keywords: Antimicrobial resistance, Urinary tract infections, E. coli, Antibiotic resistance patterns, Nepal

Introduction

Urinary tract infections (UTIs) are one of the most common types of bacterial infections globally [1]. These infections occur when bacteria grow in the normally sterile urinary tract system, which includes the kidneys, bladder, urethra, and ureters [2]. UTIs are responsible for a significant portion of outpatient visits and hospital admissions worldwide, affecting people of all ages [3]. Previous literature has revealed that UTIs affect more than 150 million individuals annually, resulting in an annual health care expenditure of 6 billion US dollar, which imposes a significant burden on health care systems, particularly in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) [4,5]. For instance, approximately 13-37% of Nepalese adults were estimated to seek hospital services for UTIs in Nepal [6]. Escherichia coli (E. coli) is the most prevalent pathogen, responsible for approximately 80-90% of community-acquired infections globally, and UTIs are frequently caused by a variety of other Gram-negative and Gram-positive micro-organisms [7]. UTIs are frequently associated with other Gram-negative micro-organisms, such as Klebsiella pneumoniae, Proteus mirabilis, and Gram-positive Staphylococcus saprophyticus [8].

The management of UTIs has become more challenging because of the rising threat of antimicrobial resistance (AMR). AMR, a major barrier to treating infections like UTIs, leads to higher mortality, morbidity, longer hospital stays, and increased health care costs [8]. This poses significant health complications and socioeconomic burdens on society. The indiscriminate and overuse of antibiotics has contributed significantly to the rise of multidrug-resistant (MDR) and extensively drug-resistant (XDR) strains of uropathogens [6,9]. This phenomenon complicates treatment, often requiring second-line antibiotics that are not only more expensive but also have more side effects [5,6]. The World Health Organization has identified AMR as one of the most critical threats to global health, leading to increased morbidity, mortality, and health care costs [10].

In Nepal, AMR is a growing concern, particularly in the treatment of UTIs. Studies have shown that bacterial pathogens in Nepal exhibit high resistance rates to commonly used antibiotics, including beta-lactams, fluoroquinolones, and aminoglycosides [6,11]. A 2021 study found that 84.3% of uropathogens were resistant to at least one antibiotic, with 53.6% classified as MDR [11]. Moreover, a recent study conducted on a pediatric tertiary care hospital in Nepal found a high prevalence of MDR (36.5%), XDR strains (22.6%), and Extended-Spectrum Beta-Lactamase (ESBL) producers (14.4%) uropathogens [12]. The overuse of broad-spectrum antibiotics, unauthorized sales, inadequate diagnostic facilities, poor patient education, and non-compliance, often because of poverty and inappropriate self-medication, are factors contributing to AMR, which are further exacerbated by inadequate health care infrastructure, poor infection control, and the lack of effective policy implementation [13,14].

Nepal recently promulgated the National Antimicrobial Treatment Guidelines 2023 to standardize UTI management and mitigate AMR [15]. These guidelines designate preferred agents, including nitrofurantoin (contraindicated in renal impairment [glomerular filtration rate <60 mL/min] and third-trimester pregnancy), cotrimoxazole, fluoroquinolones (e.g. ciprofloxacin), aminoglycosides (amikacin, gentamicin), and beta-lactam/beta-lactamase inhibitors (ampicillin-sulbactam, amoxicillin-clavulanate, piperacillin-tazobactam) [15]. Alternative regimens incorporate cephalosporins (e.g. cefuroxime), carbapenems (imipenem, meropenem), or amoxicillin for specific indications like asymptomatic bacteriuria [15]. However, the referenced international frameworks underpinning these recommendations appear to inadequately incorporate local or regional Nepalese AMR epidemiology, likely reflecting the absence of comprehensive national and local antibiograms. This critical gap risks perpetuating the empiric use of broad-spectrum agents against which partial resistance already exists, potentially exacerbating AMR through injudicious antibiotic selection. Although the government of Nepal has already endorsed and approved the National Action Plan for AMR Nepal 2024-2028 to combat AMR and given instructions to implement it at the provincial and local levels, we still do not know about the AMR pattern of many hospitals in the eastern region [16]. The National Public Health Laboratory (NPHL) serves as the National Coordinating Center for AMR surveillance in our country and receives AMR surveillance data from 26 sites, but it is able to gather AMR surveillance data from only three hospitals (Mechi, Koshi, and BPKIHS) of Koshi province [17].

Although few studies have been conducted in the central part of Nepal [9,11,12,18], to our knowledge, only one study has been conducted in a tertiary care hospital (i.e. Koshi hospital) of Eastern Nepal [6]. However, this study has focused only on inpatients. Unlike a previous study from Koshi hospital, our study captures data from emergency, outpatients, and inpatients about uropathogens and their resistance patterns. Additionally, this is the first study conducted in Madan Bhandari Hospital and Trauma Center to explore AMR patterns in uropathogens from various departments. This study aims to address the gap in knowledge by conducting a retrospective analysis of the prevalence, spectrum, and resistance patterns of uropathogens isolated from patients with UTIs at a secondary care hospital in Eastern Nepal. This study analyzed urine culture data across emergency, outpatient, and inpatient settings over a ten-month period to comprehensively characterize the local AMR landscape. Findings provide an empirical basis for understanding regional resistance trends, informing clinical decision-making, and guiding targeted antimicrobial stewardship initiatives. Ultimately, these data inform the development of optimized, local treatment regimens for UTIs and underscore the necessity for sustained AMR surveillance and tailored management strategies in this region.

Methods

Study design, period, and setting

This retrospective study was conducted at Madan Bhandari Hospital and Trauma Center (MBHTC), a secondary health care facility located in Urlabari, Morang, Nepal. The study period spanned from June 15, 2024, to April 13, 2025. Data for this investigation were obtained from the hospital’s microbiology laboratory section, and one of the researchers was directly involved in testing and analysis of samples. MBHTC is a 50-bed hospital under the provincial government, located in Urlabari Municipality, and serves as a key health care institution in the eastern region, providing comprehensive diagnostic and treatment services and serving a diverse population from different districts, making it an ideal setting for examining UTIs and the AMR patterns of uropathogens [19].

Study population and eligibility criteria

The study population consisted of patients who had urine cultures performed at MBHTC between June 15, 2024, and April 13, 2025.

Inclusion criteria included both male and female patients of all age groups whose urine culture was ordered by their attending physician for suspected UTI. All patients from the emergency, outpatient, and inpatient departments were included.

Samples that were not collected in sterile containers as per protocol, contaminated, had billing issues, repetitive isolates from the same patients with the same culture sensitivity profile, or samples with no uropathogens growth were excluded from the study.

Data collection and processing

For data collection, the research team visited the MBHTC laboratory. Data were retrieved from the hospital’s electronic medical records system (MEDAS Software V3.2) for urine cultures performed between June 15, 2024, and April 13, 2025. Those reports that had bacterial growth, identified bacteria, and performed antibiotic sensitivity testing were filtered out. Duplicate reports were removed and exported. Key variables extracted included patient demographics, sample collection dates, uropathogen identification, and antimicrobial susceptibility profiles.

Laboratory processing and testing

Urine specimen collection and processing

Urine specimens were collected as per routine procedure using the midstream clean-catch method, following proper genital cleaning with soap and water, and avoiding antiseptics. For special cases, urine was collected via indwelling catheters or suprapubic aspiration, performed by trained clinicians. Specimens were transported promptly to the laboratory. Upon arrival, specimens were processed by microscopy and cultured on Blood Agar and MacConkey Agar for bacterial isolation. The procedures adhered to the protocols set forth in the NPHL’s AMR Bacteriology Standard Operating Procedure (SOP): Version 2.0 (2024) [20]. This document, authorized by the government, serves as the official SOP to improve the reliability and reproducibility of laboratory test results. It standardizes the bacteriological processes involved in the isolation, identification, antibiotic susceptibility testing (AST), and reporting of bacterial isolates.

Bacterial isolation and identification

For bacterial isolation, urine specimens were plated onto Blood Agar and MacConkey Agar or cystine lactose electrolyte-deficient plates using a calibrated loop. Plates were incubated aerobically at 35-37°C for 18-24 hours. After incubation, colonies were examined for growth and morphology. Gram staining was performed to differentiate bacterial species, followed by biochemical testing to confirm identification. The identification process followed standard microbiological procedures as mentioned in AMR Bacteriology SOP: Version 2.0 (2024), including tests for oxidase, catalase, indole, and urease production, and sugar fermentation patterns, and then subjected to AMR profiling [20].

AST

AST of bacterial isolates from urine specimens was performed using the modified Kirby-Bauer disk diffusion method on Mueller-Hinton agar, as per AMR Bacteriology SOP: Version 2.0 (2024) [20]. Each isolate was tested against a panel of antibiotics appropriate for the identified uropathogens; however, there was variability in testing, as additional antibiotics were often included based on the treating physician’s request, despite the need for a standardized panel. The antibiotics tested included a basic set for common uropathogens such as Escherichia coli, Klebsiella pneumoniae, and Acinetobacter spp. and an extended set for MDR organisms. HiMedia Laboratories (India) antibiotic disks were used throughout the study. The following antibiotics and disk concentrations were tested: amoxicillin (25 µg), ampicillin (10 µg), ampicillin-sulbactam (10/10 µg), piperacillin-tazobactam (100/10 µg), ceftriaxone (30 µg), cefixime (5 µg), ceftazidime (30 µg), cefepime (30 µg), ceftriaxone-sulbactam (30/15 µg), cefotaxime + clavulanic acid (30/10 µg), cefoperazone-sulbactam (75/30 µg), meropenem (10 µg), imipenem (10 µg), ciprofloxacin (5 µg), ofloxacin (5 µg), azithromycin (15 µg), gentamicin (10 µg for Staphylococcus aureus), doxycycline (30 µg), tigecycline (15 µg), co-trimoxazole (trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole, 1.25/23.75 µg), nitrofurantoin (300 µg), and colistin (10 µg). Pathogen-specific panels followed the tiered approach detailed in Supplementary File 1.

The zone of inhibition was measured and interpreted according to Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute breakpoints to determine the susceptibility, intermediate resistance, or resistance of the bacteria to each antibiotic as mentioned in Annexe 2 of AMR Bacteriology SOP: Version 2.0 (2024) [20]. The exact zone diameter breakpoints for each antibiotic-organism combination are provided in Supplementary File 2 (Table for interpretation of zone diameter).

Identification of MDR, XDR, and pan-drug-resistant (PDR) isolates

The identification of MDR, XDR, and PDR isolates in this study was performed using the criteria proposed by the European Centre for Disease Prevention and Control (CDC) and the CDC [21].

MDR was defined as non-susceptible to at least one agent from three or more different antimicrobial categories, where non-susceptible includes both intermediate resistance and resistance isolates. XDR was categorized when a bacterium was non-susceptible to at least one antimicrobial agent from all but two categories, meaning it remains susceptible to only one or two categories. PDR isolates were those non-susceptible to all available antimicrobial agents tested, signifying non-susceptibility to all clinically relevant antimicrobial categories [21].

To categorize isolates, we tested the resistance of the bacteria against a range of antimicrobial agents representing various classes, following standard protocols [21]. A bacterium was considered MDR if it showed resistance to at least one antibiotic from three or more groups. XDR was assigned when resistance was observed across most antimicrobial categories, with susceptibility retained in only one or two classes. Finally, PDR was defined by resistance to all available agents across all tested categories, confirming the complete absence of susceptibility to any clinically useful antibiotics. The antimicrobial categories used to define MDR, XDR, and PDR in this study follow the European CDC/CDC expert proposal [21] and are detailed in Supplementary File 3.

Quality control for AST was conducted as per the Quality Assurance in Microbiology section of AMR Bacteriology SOP: Version 2.0 (2024) to ensure the accuracy and reliability of results [20]. The laboratory adhered to internal quality control procedures by testing known control strains of Escherichia coli American Type Culture Collection 25922 and Staphylococcus aureus American Type Culture Collection 25923 on every testing day to validate the performance of antimicrobial agents. External quality assurance was ensured through participation in the NPHL proficiency testing program, which included quarterly dispatch of test panels for AST and identification. The results from surveillance site laboratories were reviewed, and feedback was provided to maintain consistency in testing. Any discrepancies in results were addressed through further testing and validation to uphold the standard of care.

Data analysis

Data were entered and cleaned in Microsoft Excel (Version 16.96.1) and analyzed using SPSS software, version 27. Descriptive statistics, including frequencies and percentages, were used for categorical variables.

Results

Over a 10-month period, 2748 urine samples were submitted for culture. Fourteen samples were excluded per predefined criteria, yielding 2734 samples for analysis. Among these, 209 (7.64%) demonstrated positive urine cultures, with monomicrobial growth identified in all cases.

Most patients were female (79.9%), with a significant portion aged ≥60 years (35.4%). Most were outpatients (84.2%), and most patients came from the Morang district (98%). Regarding resistance, 22.96% of isolates were MDR, 7.65% were XDR, and 0.96% were PDR, as shown in Table 1.

The AMR analysis at Madhan Bhandari Trauma Center revealed critical trends across key antibiotics. Ampicillin/sulbactam resistance was notably high, particularly in Klebsiella spp. (69.23%) and S. aureus (80.00%), whereas E. coli exhibited 59.43% resistance. Cefixime demonstrated severe resistance across all species, with E. coli at 72.84%, Klebsiella spp. at 59.46%, and universal resistance in S. aureus (100.00%, 5/5). Nitrofurantoin showed divergent efficacy, maintaining low resistance in E. coli (17.12%) but higher rates in Klebsiella spp. (57.89%) and S. aureus (41.67%). Co-trimoxazole resistance peaked in S. aureus (85.71%), compared to 41.96% in E. coli and 29.03% in Klebsiella spp. Ciprofloxacin resistance was consistently observed (E. coli: 43.48%; Klebsiella spp.: 46.15%). Alarmingly, imipenem approached universal resistance in Gram-negative isolates (E. coli: 100%; Klebsiella spp.: 90.91%).

Regarding therapeutic efficacy, meropenem achieved 100% sensitivity against S. aureus and 77.01% against E. coli, but only 45.65% against Klebsiella spp. Doxycycline maintained strong activity across all species (S. aureus: 100%; E. coli: 78.57%; Klebsiella spp.: 63.64%). Piperacillin-tazobactam was fully effective against S. aureus (100%), with moderate sensitivity in E. coli (63.25%) and lower efficacy in Klebsiella spp. (41.27%), as depicted in Table 3. Discussion

In this study, we examined the spectrum of uropathogens and their AMR patterns in patients at MBHTC in Eastern Nepal. Our findings reveal a significant prevalence of MDR and XDR uropathogens, underscoring the growing threat of AMR in the region.

The growth percentage of pathogenic bacteria in our study (7.64%) was found to be lower than the national data published by NPHL (13.6%), suggesting a lower incidence of uropathogenic infection in this region than at the national level [17]. The distribution of uropathogens in this study revealed E. coli as the most commonly isolated pathogen (58.37%), followed by Klebsiella species (31.10%). These findings align with previous studies from both Nepal and global settings [3,[6], [7], [8], [9],12,22], where E. coli consistently emerges as the predominant cause of UTIs. Similarly, Klebsiella species, along with Gram-positive S. aureus, were frequently identified in other studies from South Asia and Africa [5,8,23], supporting the generalizability of these findings across regions.

This study revealed a substantial burden of MDR pathogens, with 22.96% of isolates identified as MDR, mainly involving E. coli and Klebsiella species. These findings are consistent with studies from Nepal and Ethiopia, where MDR strains are major contributors to drug-resistant UTIs [6,9,12,23], The presence of MDR pathogens complicates empirical and first-line treatment, particularly in hospital settings where resistant strains are more common. Notably, 7.65% of isolates were XDR, predominantly Klebsiella species (10.77%), reflecting concerning trends across South Asia and highlighting a significant public health threat. The emergence of XDR pathogens emphasizes the need for alternative therapies and strengthened infection control measures. Factors driving the high rates of MDR and XDR uropathogens might be —such as the overuse of broad-spectrum antibiotics, self-medication, poor infection control, and limited diagnostic access—mirror those reported in other low- and middle-income countries, including Vietnam and Ethiopia, where over-the-counter antibiotic sales and poor treatment adherence have been linked to rising resistance rates [7,24]. Although few cases of PDR (0.96%) were identified in this study, this is a pressing issue, having devastating effects on the health and well-being of patients, as they are difficult to treat.

The resistance patterns observed in this study highlight a concerning trend of high AMR among Gram-negative uropathogens, particularly E. coli and Klebsiella species. E. coli isolates demonstrated extremely high resistance to ampicillin/sulbactam (59.43%), consistent with findings from Nepal, Ethiopia, and Vietnam, where beta-lactams have shown poor efficacy [2,6,7]. Similarly, significant resistance to cephalosporins—ceftriaxone (52.17%) and cefixime (72.84%)—was observed, mirroring reports from Ethiopia and reinforcing concerns over the declining effectiveness of first-line antibiotics, including the cephalosporin group [23]. Likewise, imipenem is found to be almost absolute resistant in most of the isolates. Nevertheless, meropenem retained high susceptibility rates (77.01%), in line with studies from Vietnam and Ethiopia [7,23], suggesting they remain viable options for severe infections, although their use must be carefully managed to prevent the emergence of resistance as they belong to the “Watch” category of AWaRe (Access, Watch, and Reserve) classification system developed by the WO) [25]. Klebsiella species also exhibited high resistance rates, exceeding 40% for most tested antibiotics, except for cefepime, doxycycline, and meropenem, aligning with evidence of Klebsiella’s role in hospital-acquired, MDR infections [26]. The Gram-positive pathogen Staphylococcus aureus showed 100% resistance to ampicillin and cefixime, consistent with global trends [27], but maintained high susceptibility to gentamicin (100%) and doxycycline (78.57%), underscoring the continued utility of these agents against resistant Gram-positive uropathogens [8]. Moreover, nitrofurantoin shows a good level of sensitivity to E. coli, signifying its important role in the treatment of UTIs caused by this pathogen [18]. National antimicrobial treatment guidelines 2023 have also recognized its value in treating UTIs and recommended it as the first line of treatment for acute, uncomplicated cystitis [15].

This study reveals a critical misalignment between Nepal’s 2023 UTI treatment guidelines [15] and the convergent AMR patterns documented in Eastern Nepal and national surveillance data [17]. Although guidelines advocate nitrofurantoin, cotrimoxazole, and ciprofloxacin as first-line cystitis therapies [15], both datasets demonstrate these agents are compromised: nitrofurantoin, while effective against E. coli (81.08% local/84% national susceptibility [17]), fails against Klebsiella spp. (57.9% local/65.3% national resistance); Cotrimoxazole resistance exceeds 40% in E. coli (41.96% local/46.1% national); and ciprofloxacin resistance surpasses 50% in key uropathogens (E. coli: 43.5% local/55.5% national; Klebsiella: 46.2% local/60.5% national). Similarly, guideline-recommended [15] cephalosporins (e.g. cefixime) and ampicillin-sulbactam exhibit >60% resistance in E. coli/Klebsiella locally and nationally, exacerbated by ESBL prevalence (60.2% nationally) [17]—however, remain endorsed for empiric use. Conversely, agents like piperacillin-tazobactam (100% local/93% national susceptibility for S. aureus; 63.3% local/74.3% national for E. coli UTIs) and meropenem (77.0% local/89.7% national for E. coli) are underprioritized, whereas oral options like doxycycline (78.6-100% local susceptibility) and fosfomycin (absent from guidelines) are overlooked despite their potential. The parallel rise in MDR uropathogens (49-52% in E. coli/Klebsiella nationally since 2021) [17] and demographic vulnerabilities (e.g. 62% MDR E. coli in female UTIs) underscore that current guidelines—unchanged despite Nepal’s robust AMR surveillance—propagate ineffective therapy and resistance amplification. Urgent stewardship reforms must prioritize: restricting high-resistance drugs (fluoroquinolones, cephalosporins); adopting region-specific empiric regimens (e.g. piperacillin-tazobactam for pyelonephritis; nitrofurantoin only for culture-confirmed E. coli cystitis); and mandating urine culture before treatment, given >45% MDR prevalence nationally [17].

The findings of this study underscore the urgent need for updated empirical treatment guidelines for UTIs in Eastern Nepal, informed by regional antibiograms. Given the high resistance rates to first-line antibiotics such as ampicillin, amoxicillin, and cephalosporins, these agents should no longer be recommended for empirical therapy. Carbapenems like meropenem, which demonstrated high efficacy against resistant strains, may serve as alternatives for severe infections; however, their use must be judicious to prevent the emergence of carbapenem resistance [28]. For uncomplicated lower UTIs caused by E. coli, nitrofurantoin remains a highly effective first-line option, given its low resistance rate and minimal cross-resistance with other antibiotic classes [29,30]. Piperacillin/tazobactam and doxycycline also showed promising activity against Gram-positive uropathogens, particularly S. aureus, supporting their role in targeted therapies. Strengthening antimicrobial stewardship programs, improving rapid diagnostic capabilities, enforcing robust infection control measures, and promoting public education on appropriate antibiotic use are essential strategies to combat the rising threat of AMR in Nepal.

Strengths and limitations

This study has several limitations. The retrospective design prevented the inclusion of critical clinical data such as urine macroscopic and microscopic details, complicated vs uncomplicated UTIs, antibiotic use history, and comorbidities, which could have provided further insights into risk factors for AMR. Additionally, the laboratory does not distinguish between ESBL and methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus isolates, limiting a more detailed analysis of these resistant strains. Also, because of the relatively small number of isolates and antibiotics tested for some pathogens, based on physician request, the percentage values should be interpreted with caution. Finally, as a unicentric study, the findings may not be fully generalizable to other regions. Similarly, the hospital laboratory does not have molecular diagnostic facilities; therefore, we could not perform molecular characterization (e.g. resistance gene detection, sequencing, or phylogenetic analysis) of the isolates. Such analyses would have provided deeper epidemiological insights, including whether resistant and non-resistant isolates belong to different dominant lineages.

Despite these limitations, the study provides valuable insights. The large sample size analyzed for culture and sensitivity testing (2734 patients) and adherence to strict validated and government-approved protocols ensures valid, reliable, and consistent results. The detailed classification of isolates as per internationally accepted guidelines into MDR, XDR, and PDR categories offers comparable data to international studies, helping to inform treatment decisions. The extensive testing of antimicrobials enhances the study’s comprehensiveness, and the data from multiple hospital wards (emergency, inpatients, outpatients) provide a broad overview of the resistance patterns in a hospital setting.

Conclusion

This study documents an alarming prevalence of MDR uropathogens in Eastern Nepal, with E. coli and Klebsiella spp. exhibiting extensive resistance to first-line antibiotics (ampicillin, cephalosporins). Although meropenem and doxycycline retain efficacy against most Gram-negative isolates, piperacillin/tazobactam and doxycycline show utility against Gram-positive pathogens, Nepal’s national UTI guidelines remain fundamentally misaligned with these regional resistance patterns and the country’s own surveillance data. Critically, the guidelines persist in recommending less effective agents like cephalosporins, fluoroquinolones, and ampicillin-sulbactam—despite documented high resistance—while underprioritizing locally effective alternatives. This disconnect threatens patient outcomes and accelerates AMR. Urgent reform must prioritize region-specific AMR surveillance as the non-negotiable foundation for treatment protocols. Without this pivot, national guidelines will continue to undermine stewardship efforts and thwart the achievement of therapeutic outcomes across Nepal.
