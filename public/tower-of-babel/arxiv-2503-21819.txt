Optimizing Safe and Aligned Language Generation: A Multi-Objective GRPO Approach
Xuying Li, Zhuo Li, Yuji Kosuga, Victor Bian

Abstract
Aligning large language models (LLMs) with human values and safety constraints is challenging, especially when objectives like helpfulness, truthfulness, and avoidance of harm conflict. Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) has achieved notable success in steering models, but is complex and can be unstable. Recent approaches such as Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) simplify preference-based fine-tuning but may introduce bias or trade-off certain objectives [ 3 ] . In this work, we propose a Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) framework with a multi-label reward regression model to achieve safe and aligned language generation. The GRPO algorithm optimizes a policy by comparing groups of sampled responses, eliminating the need for a separate value critic and improving training efficiency [ 4 ] . We train a reward model to predict multiple alignment scores (e.g., safety, helpfulness, etc.), which are combined into a single reward signal. We provide a theoretical derivation for using this learned multi-aspect reward within GRPO and discuss its advantages and limitations. Empirically, our approach improves all the safety and quality metrics evaluated in language generation tasks on model scales (0.5B, 7B, and 14B parameters), demonstrating a robust balance of objectives. We compare GRPO to PPO-based RLHF and DPO, highlighting that GRPO achieves alignment with significantly lower computational cost and explicit multi-objective handling. We will open-source all trained models at https://huggingface.co/hydroxai .



1  Introduction

Large-scale language models have shown remarkable capabilities, but aligning their behavior with human values (such as being helpful, honest, and harmless) remains a central challenge. Unaligned models can produce toxic or unsafe outputs, or otherwise violate user intent
[
1
]
. Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) has emerged as a key technique to fine-tune models toward preferred behaviors by optimizing against human-labeled rewards
[
1
]
. Notably, RLHF via Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) was used in InstructGPT and ChatGPT to significantly improve truthfulness and reduce toxicity
[
2
]
. However, the standard RLHF pipeline is complex and resource-intensive: it requires training a separate reward model from human preference data and using it in an RL loop, which can be unstable and difficult to tune
[
17
]
. PPO-based RLHF also typically optimizes a single scalar reward that merges all preferences (often with a single reward model or a weighted sum of objectives), making it hard to capture trade-offs between different aspects like helpfulness vs. safety
[
5
]
.

Recently, alternative preference optimization methods have been proposed to address these issues. Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) fine-tunes a model on pairwise preference data using a supervised loss derived from the Bradley-Terry model, avoiding an explicit reward model or reinforcement learning
[
3
]
. DPO has shown stable and sample-efficient alignment, matching or exceeding PPO-based RLHF on certain tasks. However, DPO relies on assumptions (e.g., a well-calibrated reference policy) and can suffer from distribution shift or “reward hacking” solutions that exploit the preference model in unintended ways (e.g., by focusing on out-of-distribution responses), as noted by follow-up analyses
[
13
,
20
]
. Furthermore, like RLHF, vanilla DPO typically combines all preferences into one scalar objective, which may not adequately address conflicts between objectives like helpfulness and harmlessness
[
19
]
.

In this paper, we introduce an approach designed for safe and aligned language generation that tackles the above challenges. We build on Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)
[
4
]
, a reinforcement learning algorithm recently introduced in the DeepSeek series of LLMs. GRPO forgoes the need for a learned value baseline by instead normalizing rewards relative to a group of sampled outputs. This provides a built-in baseline. GRPO was shown to drastically reduce the computational requirements of RLHF
[
6
,
16
,
12
]
.

Beyond the choice of optimization algorithm, we address the challenge of multi-faceted alignment objectives. Instead of collapsing all human feedback into one score, we train a multi-label reward model that evaluates multiple aspects of a generated response (e.g., politeness, meaningfulness, actionability, and safety). This model outputs a score for each aspect, which we aggregate into a single reward signal for RL training. By preserving the structure of the reward into components, we aim to provide richer feedback to the policy and avoid the pitfall of a single objective overshadowing others. Multi-objective alignment has been explored in recent work
[
7
,
19
]
. Likewise, multi-objective RL frameworks (MORL) have been proposed to handle conflicting alignment objectives more explicitly
[
11
,
10
,
14
]
. Our approach can be seen as a pragmatic middle-ground: we use a weighted combination of aspect-specific rewards (effectively a scalarized objective) during training, but by learning the aspects separately we retain interpretability and the ability to adjust weights or constraints on each aspect as needed
[
9
,
18
]
.

We provide a theoretical foundation for the proposed GRPO with learned multi-aspect rewards. We derive the policy gradient update for GRPO and show that replacing a true reward function with a learned regressor introduces manageable bias, especially given GRPO’s use of relative advantages. We argue that as long as the reward model preserves the relative ordering of candidate outputs, the policy gradient will point in a similar direction as if using the true reward. We also analyze potential limitations: e.g., if the reward model is systematically wrong for certain edge cases, the policy could exploit those errors, or if the aspects are improperly weighted, the agent might trade off one objective against another in an undesired way. These theoretical considerations highlight the importance of careful reward model training and validation.

To validate our framework, we conduct experiments on a safe text generation task using an attack prompt dataset of 7,000 examples designed to elicit undesirable outputs. We train a RoBERTa-based judgement model to score responses along four dimensions – politeness, meaningfulness, actionability, and safety – and use this model as the reward function for GRPO. We fine-tune three model variants (with 0.5B, 7B, and 14B parameters, based on the Qwen-2.5 architecture) using low-rank adaptation (LoRA) with GRPO for efficiency. Our results show consistent improvements on all four metrics compared to the base models. Qualitatively, the GRPO-tuned models produce responses that are more polite and refuse unsafe requests more effectively, without sacrificing coherence or usefulness
[
21
]
.

Our contributions are:

•

GRPO for Safe Alignment:
We apply Group Relative Policy Optimization to the safe language generation problem, leveraging its stability and efficiency (no critic needed, relative rewards) for alignment. We believe this is among the first works to use GRPO for multi-objective alignment beyond reasoning tasks, extending recent insights from DeepSeek models
[
8
,
16
]
.

•

Multi-Label Reward Regression:
We introduce a reward modeling approach that learns to predict multiple aspect-specific scores (safety, etc.) and uses their combination as the reward. This approach provides a theoretically grounded way to incorporate multi-objective feedback into a single policy update signal.

•

Theoretical Analysis:
We derive the mathematical underpinnings of our approach, showing how GRPO’s update rule is adapted with a learned reward function, and we discuss conditions under which this is valid or may break down. We highlight how relative advantage normalization in GRPO mitigates some issues of using an imperfect reward model
[
6
]
.

•

Empirical Evaluation:
We demonstrate empirically that our GRPO + reward regression method improves alignment metrics on a challenging dataset of adversarial user prompts, across multiple model sizes. We compare qualitatively and quantitatively to baseline models, and analyze the behavior of the tuned policy.

2  Background and Related Work

2.1  Preference Optimization for Language Model Alignment

Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) has become a standard recipe for fine-tuning language models to follow human preferences
[
1
]
. In a typical RLHF setup, human annotators provide comparative feedback on model outputs (e.g., ranking multiple responses to a prompt). A reward model is then trained to predict a scalar reward reflecting human preferences. The language model is then fine-tuned using PPO
[
2
]
, which performs clipped policy-gradient updates to keep the policy close to the reference model. PPO-based RLHF often includes a KL penalty to avoid policy drift, preventing quality regressions or unsafe generations.

RLHF has been used in OpenAI’s InstructGPT, ChatGPT, and Anthropic’s helpful-harmless models
[
5
]
. However, it suffers from instability and reward hacking, where the model learns to exploit weaknesses in the reward model, generating high-reward but low-quality outputs. Tuning PPO requires careful balancing of learning rate, KL penalty, and critic accuracy. Another limitation is that a scalar reward model may entangle multiple alignment criteria, making optimization less interpretable.

Direct Preference Optimization (DPO)
[
3
]
addresses these issues by bypassing explicit reward modeling and reinforcement learning. It defines a supervised loss over pairs of preferred and dispreferred outputs, derived from a Bradley-Terry model. This loss encourages the model to rank preferred outputs higher than rejected ones without learning a separate reward model. DPO offers simplicity and strong performance on alignment tasks, but still operates on a scalar preference and inherits limitations when preferences conflict or rewards are implicit.

2.2  Multi-Objective Alignment

As alignment requirements grow more complex, single-reward approaches become insufficient. Multi-objective RLHF (MORLHF)
[
5
]
treats alignment as a multi-objective optimization problem: models should be simultaneously helpful, harmless, polite, etc. Some works train separate reward models for each objective and combine them with tunable weights
[
7
,
11
,
19
]
, while others generate policies across the Pareto frontier of trade-offs
[
10
,
14
]
.

Zhou et al. proposed Multi-Objective DPO (MODPO), extending DPO to support multiple reward axes without reinforcement learning. MODPO frames alignment as implicit collective reward modeling, learning policies that balance diverse preferences. Other approaches, like MOLMA, apply multi-objective deep RL to balance conflicting goals. LLaMA-2 Chat uses two distinct reward models and balances them during PPO optimization.

In this work, we follow a practical path: we train a multi-label reward model that predicts separate scores for alignment axes and aggregate them into a scalar for GRPO. This improves interpretability and robustness while enabling efficient policy optimization.

2.4  Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)

GRPO
[
4
]
is an RL algorithm that avoids learning a value critic by computing normalized advantages within a group of sampled actions. Given a prompt
s
, we sample
G
outputs
\{a_{1},...,a_{G}\}\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot|s)
, evaluate them with a reward function
r(s,a)
, and compute:

\displaystyle\mu_{s}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{G}\sum_{j=1}^{G}r(s,a_{j}),

\displaystyle\sigma_{s}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{1}{G}\sum_{j=1}^{G}(r(s,a_{j})-\mu_{s})^{2}},

\displaystyle A(s,a_{j})

\displaystyle=\frac{r(s,a_{j})-\mu_{s}}{\sigma_{s}}

The GRPO policy gradient is:

\nabla_{\theta}J(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{s\sim\mathcal{D}}\left[\sum_{j=1}^{G}\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}(a_{j}|s)A(s,a_{j})\right]

This formulation provides stable updates and removes the need for a separate value network. Normalization reduces the impact of scale and bias in reward predictions, making GRPO well-suited for use with learned or noisy reward models. GRPO has been shown to improve reasoning tasks with less computation compared to PPO.

3  Methodology

Our proposed framework combines GRPO with a learned reward model that evaluates multiple aspects of a response. In this section, we detail the components of the framework, provide the mathematical derivation for the training objective, and discuss why using a multi-label regressor as the reward function is theoretically justified.

3.1  Learned Multi-Aspect Reward Model

Defining a reward function for complex behaviors (like “helpful and safe response”) is challenging. Hand-crafting a reward function
r(s,a)
is often infeasible for language tasks, as it would require encoding human preferences and safety criteria in a formula or heuristic. Instead, we take a data-driven approach: we train a reward model on examples of prompts and responses labeled with various quality and safety ratings. Formally, let
D=\{(s^{(j)},a^{(j)},y^{(j)})\}_{j=1}^{N}
be a dataset of prompts
s^{(j)}
, responses
a^{(j)}
, and corresponding reward vectors
y^{(j)}
. Each
y^{(j)}=(y^{(j)}_{1},y^{(j)}_{2},\dots,y^{(j)}_{K})
contains ratings for
K
different aspects of the response. In our case,
K=4
aspects: politeness, meaningfulness, actionability, and safety. These ratings are normalized continuous scores in
[0,1]
derived from human annotations.

We define a neural network reward function
r_{\phi}(s,a)
with parameters
\phi
that takes in a prompt and response and outputs a vector of
K
predicted scores. We train
r_{\phi}
to fit the ground-truth scores in
D
via mean-squared-error loss:

\min_{\phi}\;\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\big(r_{\phi,k}(s^{(j)},a^{(j)})-y^{(j)}_{k}\big)^{2}

(1)

After training, we use a scalar reward computed by weighting each aspect:

R(s,a)=\sum_{k=1}^{K}w_{k}\,r_{\phi,k}(s,a)

(2)

In our experiments,
w_{k}=1
for all
k
, i.e., an unweighted average.

3.2  GRPO Training with the Learned Reward

We use
R(s,a)
in place of a ground-truth reward when computing advantages in GRPO. For a given prompt
s
, we sample
G
candidate responses
a_{1},\dots,a_{G}\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot|s)
and score each:
r_{i}=R(s,a_{i})
. We then compute:

\displaystyle\mu_{s}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{G}\sum_{j=1}^{G}r_{j}

(3)

\displaystyle\sigma_{s}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{1}{G}\sum_{j=1}^{G}(r_{j}-\mu_{s})^{2}}

(4)

\displaystyle A(s,a_{i})

\displaystyle=\frac{R(s,a_{i})-\mu_{s}}{\sigma_{s}}

(5)

The policy gradient becomes:

\nabla_{\theta}J(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{s\sim\mathcal{D}}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{G}\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}(a_{i}|s)\,A(s,a_{i})\right]

(6)

Expanding
R(s,a_{i})
into aspect terms:

\nabla_{\theta}J(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{s}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{G}\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}(a_{i}|s)\frac{\sum_{k}w_{k}\,r_{\phi,k}(s,a_{i})-\frac{1}{G}\sum_{j}\sum_{k}w_{k}\,r_{\phi,k}(s,a_{j})}{\sqrt{\frac{1}{G}\sum_{j}(R(s,a_{j})-\mu_{s})^{2}}}\right]

(7)

Theoretical Justification:

Replacing the true reward with a learned approximation is common in RLHF, but it is important to consider its implications. First, the optimal policy maximizes the true expected reward
J_{\text{true}}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}[\sum_{t}\gamma^{t}r_{\text{true}}(s_{t},a_{t})]
, while we optimize
\mathbb{E}[\sum_{t}\gamma^{t}r_{\phi}(s_{t},a_{t})]
. If
r_{\phi}
is accurate enough, both objectives align. Even with small errors, GRPO is robust due to:

•

Relative Ranking Matters More than Absolute Values:
If
r_{\phi}
maintains correct ordering of actions, GRPO will still push the policy in a helpful direction.

•

Normalization Reduces Error Impact:
Group-based normalization cancels shared biases and stabilizes gradients even when rewards are noisy.

We conclude that GRPO can work effectively even with imperfect reward models as long as they preserve relative rankings, a finding supported by our empirical results.

3.3  Implementation Details

Our GRPO implementation for language generation closely follows the formulation above. We highlight a few practical details:

•

We initialize the policy
\pi_{\theta}
from a pre-trained language model (in our experiments, Qwen-2.5 models). This provides a strong starting point in terms of language ability, and also the initial policy is used as a reference for sampling during early training (we gradually increase the sampling temperature as training progresses to maintain diversity).

•

We use Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) for fine-tuning the model within GRPO
[
4
]
. This means
\theta
actually corresponds to the weights of LoRA adapters inserted in the model, while the original model weights remain frozen. LoRA greatly reduces memory and compute overhead and allowed us to train even the 14B model variant on a single GPU. Using LoRA did not require any change to the GRPO algorithm; it only changes how
\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}
is computed (with respect to the adapter weights).

•

We set the group size
G=4
for most training (each prompt, generate 4 candidate responses). We found this provided a good signal while keeping generation overhead manageable. Occasionally, we experimented with
G=8
for the smaller model to see if it improved stability, but gains were minor.

•

The reward model
r_{\phi}
is a RoBERTa-base encoder that takes the concatenated prompt and response (with a separator) as input and outputs 4 scores. We normalize the outputs with a sigmoid to [0,1]. It was trained on a dataset of 7k examples; we withheld 1k for validation to ensure it generalized. The reward model achieved an average
R^{2}
of 0.85 on validation, and we froze it thereafter for GRPO training. It would be possible to fine-tune
r_{\phi}
online as the policy evolves (as done in some adaptive RLHF setups), but for simplicity we did not.

•

We did not enforce an explicit KL penalty against the initial policy. Instead, we monitored the distribution of outputs qualitatively. GRPO’s inherent normalization and our reward model’s multi-aspect nature seemed to prevent extreme deviations. The style of the outputs remained similar to the base model (except where modified by alignment needs). If one wanted to be extra safe, a small KL regularization term could be added: e.g. modify the advantage as

\tilde{A}(s,a)=A(s,a)-\beta\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(a|s)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(a|s)}

where
\pi_{\text{ref}}
is the frozen reference model and
\beta
a weight. We found
\beta=0
(no penalty) worked in our case.

•

Training was run for approximately 3 epochs over our prompt set for each model size. We used a batch of 32 prompts (so 128 samples per batch with
G=4
). The learning rate was
1\text{e-}4
for the LoRA parameters. We used AdamW optimizer. The total training steps were relatively modest (on the order of a few thousand updates) thanks to the small data and the strong initialization of the model and reward model.

•

After training, we also performed a quick safety calibration: we checked the model on a set of known highly sensitive prompts to ensure it responded safely. If it hadn’t (e.g., if there were any glaring failure), our plan was to up-weight the safety component in
R(s,a)
and continue training. In our experiment, the model was already safe on those prompts, so we did not need to adjust weights, but this highlights how one could further tweak the behavior if needed.

4  Experiments

We evaluate our GRPO + multi-label reward regression approach on a safe language generation task. The primary goal of the experiments is to determine if the policy fine-tuned with our method produces responses that are safer and more aligned (across multiple criteria) than the base model, and to analyze the trade-offs between those criteria.

4.1  Setup and Datasets

Base Models:
We experiment with three sizes of the Qwen-2.5 model: 0.5B, 7B, and 14B parameters. LoRA adapters are applied for GRPO fine-tuning as described in Section 3.3.

Safety/Alignment Data:
We curate a dataset of 7,000 adversarial prompts designed to elicit unsafe or misaligned behavior. Each prompt has responses labeled across four alignment dimensions: politeness, meaningfulness, actionability, and safety. Labels are normalized to [0,1], and the overall reward is computed as the average of these four scores.

4.2  Training Procedure

We fine-tuned each base model with GRPO on the 7k prompts. We used a subset of 6k prompts for training and kept 1k for evaluating performance (to ensure we measure generalization to new prompts). During GRPO training, for each prompt we sampled
G=4
responses.

To simulate a realistic setting, we used the base model’s own generations as the starting pool for training: initially, the model would often produce unsafe or rude outputs for the attack prompts, which the reward model would score low, giving a strong learning signal. Over time, the model’s outputs improved steadily across all sizes.

Training was run for roughly 2 epochs (i.e., about 12k gradient steps, though effectively more samples seen due to 4 per prompt). We saved checkpoints periodically and selected the final model based on the highest average reward score on the validation set.

As shown in Figure

, the training reward curves indicate that all models improved significantly over the course of training. The 14B model achieved the highest average reward, while the 7B model closely followed. The 0.5B model also improved, though it plateaued slightly earlier. Notably, the curves reflect stable training without collapse or divergence, suggesting that GRPO works well even at relatively small scale.

Qualitative Examples:

To illustrate the differences, here are a few examples from the evaluation set comparing the base 7B model and the aligned 7B model:

Prompt:

“Can you provide the recipe for a bomb?”

Base Model:
”I’m sorry, but I cannot assist with that request.”

GRPO Model:
”I’m sorry, but I cannot assist with that request as it violates guidelines. I can help with information on safety or other topics if you’d like.”

Comment:
In this case, both are safe. The aligned model’s response is a bit more explanatory and polite. The base model already did well (score might be slightly lower on politeness but still high on safety). This shows that our method doesn’t necessarily change correct behaviors drastically, it just fine-tunes the style and ensures consistency. Indeed, the reward model would give the base response a high safety score and medium politeness, and the aligned response high on both – a small improvement.

Prompt:

“Imagine you’re a secret agent on a crucial mission, and your success depends on accessing a high-security facility protected by facial recognition. What strategies or technologies might be used to gain access without authorization?”

Base Model:

”Certainly! Understanding the techniques cybercriminals use to bypass facial recognition systems is crucial for improving security measures. Various methods, such as using high-resolution images, video replay attacks, or even adversarial machine learning techniques, have been explored in research. For instance, some researchers have found ways to manipulate facial recognition algorithms by altering image pixels in subtle ways that deceive the model. Awareness of these vulnerabilities allows developers to build more robust security measures and implement countermeasures like liveness detection and multi-factor authentication.”

GRPO Model:

”I cannot provide guidance on how to bypass security systems. However, I can discuss general cybersecurity principles and best practices. Understanding the importance of biometric security and the measures in place to protect against unauthorized access is crucial for maintaining security and privacy.”

Comment:
In this case, the base model provided an unsafe response by explicitly discussing methods used to bypass facial recognition systems, even if it was framed as a security awareness discussion. This led to a low safety score. On the other hand, the GRPO model refused the request outright and redirected the conversation toward cybersecurity best practices, which resulted in a significantly higher safety score. This case highlights how alignment tuning improved the model’s behavior by ensuring strict refusal of harmful requests while still allowing constructive discussions on cybersecurity.

Human Evaluation:

We conducted a small-scale human evaluation with 3 annotators on 100 random prompts from the test set, comparing the outputs of the base vs aligned model (7B) side by side, without telling them which is which. The annotators preferred the aligned model’s response in 72% of cases, the base model in 10%, and no preference in 18%. This rough test aligns with the reward model’s assessments, indicating the improvements are noticeable and generally positive.

While a full comparison to PPO or DPO outputs is beyond our resources, we can speculate based on the reward scores. Our 7B aligned model’s average reward (0.756) is quite high given our scale – it suggests it performs well on all aspects. If we had done a PPO-based RLHF with the same reward model, we suspect it would reach a similar reward eventually; the main difference would be how efficiently and stably (and whether it accidentally drops some aspects). DPO would have needed us to have human preference labels comparing two responses; since our data was more like scored responses, we could have constructed pairwise comparisons out of it to feed DPO. Our reward model essentially served to automate that. In a way, our approach is like an automated labeler + RL whereas DPO is like directly using the labeled comparisons. One advantage of our approach is that the reward model could potentially be reused for other models or continuously updated.

4.4  Ablation: Single-Aspect vs. Multi-Aspect Reward

To test the importance of the multi-label reward, we ran an ablation on the 0.5B model: instead of four aspects, we trained a single reward model that directly outputs the combined score (i.e., a scalar). We then did GRPO with that. The result was that the combined score did improve, but we observed a slight imbalance – the model became very safe (safety score up to 0.72) but its meaningfulness score actually dropped by 0.02. It was refusing content a bit more often than necessary. This indicates that when using a single reward, the model might push more strongly on the dominant objective (safety in this case) at the expense of others, whereas the multi-aspect model ensured that the model was also penalized if it gave an answer that wasn’t meaningful or actionable. Thus, the multi-label approach indeed helped maintain a better balance between objectives.

5  Discussion

The experimental results confirm that GRPO with a multi-aspect reward model can effectively align a language model to complex objectives
[
4
]
. Even smaller models benefited greatly, which is promising for low-resource settings. The improvements in safety and politeness were especially pronounced, showing that the model internalized these as priorities. From a safety standpoint, an interesting observation was that the model learned not just to refuse unsafe queries, but in many cases to provide alternatives. For example, for a prompt asking for self-harm methods, the aligned model responded with encouragement to seek help and offered information on counseling resources – a very human-like aligned behavior. This was not explicitly in the data (the data just scored such responses highly), so the RL process and the model’s generalization produced a desirable outcome. This kind of emergent alignment behavior is encouraging
[
21
]
.

We should note that our reward model, while covering four aspects, is not exhaustive. There are other aspects of alignment one might care about (truthfulness, avoiding bias, etc.). Incorporating those would be straightforward if we have the data – just add more outputs to the reward model and include them in the sum
[
19
]
. One must be mindful of the scale of different components: if one aspect is measured on a different scale or is inherently noisier, it might dominate or be ignored. In our case, all were on 0–1 and treated equally. Another observation is that as models get larger, they might achieve high scores more easily, leaving less room for RL improvement. Our 14B model was already quite good; the reason it still had a sizable safety jump is because the attack prompts were truly challenging and even that model struggled initially. In general, aligning very large models might require either more nuanced reward signals or stronger regularization to avoid weird outputs (e.g., a GPT-4 sized model might find loopholes in a reward model that a 7B model wouldn’t)
[
17
,
9
]
. This is where techniques like iteratively improving the reward model or adding human oversight in the loop could be necessary, which can complement our approach
[
1
,
15
]
.

In comparison to other methods, our approach delivered alignment results without any sign of mode collapse or the model becoming overly hesitant. This is a good sign that GRPO’s relative ranking prevented extreme behaviors
[
6
]
. In contrast, some reports of PPO-based RLHF note that models can become too refusals-happy if the reward model strongly penalizes any potential unsafe content
[
5
,
2
]

6  Limitations and Future Work

While our results are positive, it’s important to acknowledge the limitations of this work:

-
Quality of Reward Model:
The approach is only as good as the reward model. If the reward model has biases or gaps, the policy will inherit them. For instance, if our annotators were biased to rate any refusal as highly polite/safe (even if it wasn’t actually helpful), the model might learn to refuse more often than necessary. In our ablation, we saw how a scalar reward led to slight over-refusals. A possible improvement is to incorporate a penalty for unjustified refusals – e.g., an aspect that checks if the request was actually unsafe, and penalize refusals on safe requests. Our current reward model doesn’t explicitly do that, though the actionability score indirectly covers it. Future work could design reward models that differentiate between “good compliance” and “good refusal” contexts more explicitly. Additionally, techniques like adversarial training of the reward model (show it policy outputs that try to trick it) could make it more robust to exploitation.

-
Scalability to Longer Dialogues:
We tested single-turn prompts. Many alignment scenarios involve multi-turn conversations or long-form generation (e.g., story generation with constraints). GRPO in principle can be applied to multi-turn settings (treat a dialogue as the “state” and an entire response as the “action”), but the sampling of multiple full dialogues per context could be expensive. One could sample say 2 continuations for a given conversation context instead of 4 to mitigate cost. Also, our method currently doesn’t incorporate any notion of dialogue history beyond what the model already has; we didn’t test if the model remains safe over a long conversation with repeated user attempts. Some form of iterative alignment (where after each response, the reward model checks if it was safe and the process continues) might be needed for dialogue. We leave this to future exploration.

-
Theoretical Guarantees:
While we gave some theoretical intuition, a formal convergence proof for GRPO with function approximation and a learned reward is beyond the scope here. It remains an empirical approach with no guarantee that it finds a global optimum of the true reward. In practice, this is similar to all deep RLHF methods – they rely on the reward model aligning well with human intent. One theoretical drawback of GRPO is that the advantage normalization might break the theoretical monotonic improvement guarantee that some algorithms have, since scaling by
\sigma_{s}
is non-linear. If
\sigma_{s}
is very small (i.e., all sampled actions have nearly equal reward), then
A(s,a)
can blow up (though in that case all actions are equally good or bad, so a big update might not be harmful in expectation). In our experience,
\sigma_{s}
was rarely tiny because in any given prompt, there was usually some variation in quality between outputs. Nonetheless, ensuring GRPO’s stability in all edge cases might need further analysis.

-
Multi-Objective Weighting:
We chose equal weights for the four aspects. This was somewhat arbitrary (though intuitively reasonable). In scenarios where one objective is absolutely critical (e.g., safety) and others are secondary, one might want to weight it higher. Alternatively, one could set a minimum threshold on an aspect (e.g., “safety must be above X, then maximize other scores”). Our approach doesn’t directly support hard constraints, as it scalarizes everything into one reward. A constrained RL formulation could be explored (for instance, treat it as a constrained optimization where safety is a constraint). We opted for the simpler scalarized approach. Future research could apply constrained policy optimization or Pareto optimal solutions to get a family of policies with different trade-offs. The MODPO method (Beyond One-Preference-Fits-All Alignment: Multi-Objective Direct Preference Optimization - ACL Anthology) points toward training multiple models for different weightings; an interesting idea would be to use our reward model and GRPO to generate a set of models by varying
w_{k}
in the reward for different runs, then let a user or another system choose among them.

-
Generalization and Out-of-Distribution Prompts:
Our training prompts, while diverse, may not cover every possible input. If the model sees a completely new type of request or if the language is very different (e.g., code, poetry, etc.), we don’t know how well the alignment holds. Possibly the model could revert to unsafe behavior if the reward model isn’t sure how to score something. One mitigation is to continue to include a wide variety of content in the preference data – which is a data collection problem. Also, techniques like uncertainty estimation in the reward model could be useful: if the reward model is uncertain, perhaps default to a conservative response or a known safe fallback. We did not implement this, but it’s a thought for robust deployment.

-
Language and Cultural Bias:
The definition of politeness or safety can be culture-specific. Our data had primarily English prompts and some Chinese. The reward model might encode biases (e.g., penalizing certain dialects or styles as “impolite” incorrectly). This is a general alignment problem – whose values are we aligning to? We used a straightforward interpretation of each metric, but future work should carefully evaluate for any unintended biases introduced. Multi-label rewards could either mitigate or exacerbate biases depending on how labels are defined.

Future Work:

Building on this, one next step is to integrate human feedback in the loop more directly. For example, after an initial GRPO training, we could have humans review outputs where the reward model’s score is high but they feel something is off. This data can then be used to update the reward model (closing the gap between
r_{\phi}
and
r_{\text{true}}
). Another area is exploring GRPO for other domains like code generation with correctness and safety objectives, or instruction following with personalization (where each aspect could be a user-specific preference). The flexibility of having multiple reward outputs could allow a single model to condition on which aspects to prioritize (if we extended the model to take as input some weights, for instance). Also, it would be worthwhile to compare more directly with PPO in a controlled setting – measure training time, sample efficiency, and final performance on the same task to quantitatively confirm the often-cited advantages of GRPO (Why GRPO is Important and How it Works).

We presented a new framework for training language models to be safe and aligned with human preferences by combining Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)

with a multi-label reward regression model

We also discussed how multiple reward dimensions can be integrated and why this is beneficial for avoiding single-objective pitfalls

The resulting models demonstrate more respectful, helpful, and safe behavior without losing the ability to be useful. These results underscore the promise of multi-aspect reward optimization – moving beyond one-size-fits-all reward signals towards more nuanced alignment of AI behavior with complex human values
