Low complexity convergence rate bounds for push-sum algorithms with homogeneous correlation structure
Balázs Gerencsér, Miklós Kornyik

Abstract
The objective of this work is to establish an upper bound for the almost sure convergence rate for a class of push-sum algorithms. The current work extends the methods and results of the authors on a similar low-complexity bound on push-sum algorithms with some particular synchronous message passing schemes and complements the general approach of Gerencsér and Gerencsér from 2022 providing an exact, but often less accessible description. Furthermore, a parametric analysis is presented on the “weight” of the messages, which is found to be convex with an explicit expression for the gradient. This allows the fine-tuning of the algorithm used for improved efficiency. Numerical results confirm the speedup in evaluating the computable bounds without deteriorating their performance, for a graph on 120 vertices the runtime drops by more than 4 orders of magnitude.

The importance of the fundamental building block studied has been well understood for some while, the goal of distributed averaging of certain initial values along a network, while only relying on local communication and low computational overhead

Related work.
An essential question to assess the usability and efficiency of such algorithms is understanding their asymptotic behavior, the convergence of the processes and the rate at which it takes place.
In the original work
[
14
]
, exponential convergence has been shown for the reference push-sum scheme. At that stage, it has been a qualitative observation, without going after the exact convergence rate.

For a more quantitative insight, an important step was proving a remarkable upper bound for the almost sure (a.s.) rate of convergence along an infinite subset of the timeline
[
13
]
. Another direction has been proposed
[
22
]
to capture the finite time behavior, capable of stating explicit bounds, although far from being optimal.
Via a much more general and abstract treatment, the asymptotic
tight bound for the rate of a.s. convergence
has been identified
[
7

through the gap of the Lyapunov exponents of the (random) update matrix series. Although this sheds light on the rate, the practical concern is that this Lyapunov gap is known to be

[

Following up on the results above, it was possible to learn and combine from
[
13
]

[
7

[

computable quantity
. The bounds are numerically appealing, however for a graph on
N
vertices, matrices of size

are encountered, thus
increasing in complexity
quickly as
N

In view of this bottleneck, a bound with low computational complexity has been developed
[
9
]
, still for getting guarantees on the a.s. exponential convergence rate. This needed the restriction of focus to networks and communication schemes with high symmetries. The gain on numerical complexity is a speedup factor of at least
N

Contributions.
On one hand we extend the easily accessible convergence rate estimates of
[
9
]
for a broader class of messaging protocols. While still requiring a substantially symmetric setup, this is only in terms of the network and the correlation structure of the messaging, which is sufficient to deduce rate guarantees that are simple but perform well.
Furthermore, we analyze the effect of the proportion of transmission, which is often set in some arbitrary way. It turns out that the rate bounds are convex in the proportion, and can be efficiently optimized (supported by structural understanding), leading to fine-tuning the push-sum algorithm in a quasi-optimal way. A range of numerical experiments illustrate the analytical results above.

Model description

Let
(A(t))_{t\geq 1}
be a sequence of i.i.d. (non-negative) column-stochastic matrices of size
N\times N
and
x(0)\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
be the initial data. The initial auxiliary weight variable is initialized as
w(0)=\mathbf{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
. Push-sum algorithms, orginally introduced in
[
14
]
, are characterized by the following time-dependent equations:

\displaystyle x(t)

\displaystyle=A(t)x(t-1),

\displaystyle w(t)

\displaystyle=A(t)w(t-1).

The estimate of the average of the initial values at coordinate
i
is given by
z_{i}(t)=x_{i}(t)/w_{i}(t)
.
Under some mild conditions this is known to converge almost surely to average consensus, i.e.
z_{i}(t)\to\bar{x}:=N^{-1}\sum_{j}x_{j}(0)
as
t\to\infty
for all
i
, shown already for a range of cases in
[
14
]
.
Concerning the efficiency of this scheme, there is a tight asymptotic bound on the rate of almost sure convergence in
[
7
]
. However, due to the computational difficulties of retrieving it, more accessible approximate solutions and upper bounds are quantities of high interest.

Let us write
A(t)
as

A(t)=I+C(t)-D(t)

where
C(t)
consists of the message passing terms with
c_{ij}\geq 0
,
\sum_{i}c_{ij}\leq 1
, and
c_{ii}=0

\forall i,j
, while
D(t)
denotes the corrective diagonal matrix so that
A(t)
is column-stochastic. Note that these conditions imply
a_{ij}(t)\geq 0
for all
i,j
. We remark that the term
D(t)-C(t)
is also known as the Laplacian matrix from a graph theoretical perspective.

Let us prescribe the following relations concerning the elements of
C
.

\displaystyle\mathbf{1}^{\top}\mathbb{E}C

\displaystyle=q\sqrt{u}\mathbf{1}^{\top}

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[c_{ji}c_{lk}]

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\alpha q^{2}p_{ji}p_{lk}&i\neq k,\\
\beta q^{2}p_{ji}p_{li}&i=k,j\neq l,\\
(qr)^{2}p_{ji}&i=k,j=l.\end{cases}

(1)

Here
P=(p_{ij})
is a column-stochastic matrix of non-negative elements and
u,q,r\in[0,1]
.
One can think of
P
as the collection of normalized recipient distribution in each column and of
q
as the average proportion that is sent per message.

For the current analysis, some assumptions have to be imposed on the transfer matrices
(A(t))_{t\geq 1}
, or equivalently on
(C(t))_{t\geq 1}
as follows.

Assumption 1
.

The following are required for the communication events:

1.

(C(t))_{t\geq 1}
forms an i.i.d. series of non-negative matrices;

2.

C(1)
satisfies the moment conditions given in (
2
);

3.

C(1)
is allowable, i.e. no row of
I+C(1)-D(1)
can be fully 0;

4.

C(1)
does not have extremely small positive elements, more precisely for
m=\min\{C_{ij}(1)\mid C_{ij}(1)>0,i,j\in[N]\}
we have
\mathbb{E}\log^{-}m>-\infty
.

Furthermore, we need connectivity and a strong symmetry on the network where the averaging takes place:

Assumption 2
.

The following are required for the network descriptor
P
:

1.

Connections are symmetric, i.e.
P=P^{\top}
;

2.

The graph formed by the positive elements of
P
is connected and
q\sqrt{u}<1
to provide loops;

3.

The network is transitive, i.e. for any
i,j
vertices there exists a permutation matrix
\Pi
so that
\Pi^{-1}P\Pi=P
and the permutation maps
i
to
j
.

To understand the motivation of
2
let consider the following. Let us write the
(j,i)
element of
C
as

\displaystyle c_{ji}

\displaystyle=Q_{i}U_{i}\frac{\chi_{ji}}{M_{i}}

where
Q_{i}
denotes the proportion sent by vertex
i
,
U_{i}\sim Bern(u)
denotes the indicator of
i
sending a message at all,
\chi_{ji}
is the indicator of an interaction
i\to j
, while
M_{i}=\sum_{j}\chi_{ji}
is basically the number of recipients. Note that in case vertex
i
send messages to
M_{i}=m
recipients, then the expected message rate per recipient will be
\tilde{q}/m
. We will see in this case that
q=\tilde{q}\sqrt{u}
. We make the following assumptions:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[U_{i}]

\displaystyle=u,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[Q_{i}|U_{i}]

\displaystyle=\tilde{q}U_{i},

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[c_{ji}|U_{i}]

\displaystyle=\tilde{q}p_{ji}U_{i},

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{\chi_{ji}}{M_{i}}|U_{i}\right]

\displaystyle=p_{ji}U_{i},

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[Q_{i}^{2}|U_{i}]

\displaystyle=(\tilde{q}r)^{2}U_{i},

where
\sum_{j}p_{ji}=1
for all
i
, i.e. the matrix
(p_{ji})
is column-stochastic. In the current context let us further assume that for any
i\neq k
the quantities
Q_{i},Q_{k}
and
(\chi_{ji})_{j},(\chi_{lk})_{l}
are independent given
U_{i},U_{k}
. Note that the condition
M_{i}>0
is equivalent with
U_{i}=1
, hence
\sigma(U_{i})\subset\sigma(M_{i})
, i.e. if a random variable
X
is
M_{i}
-measurable then it is also
U_{i}
-measurable.
Let us now calculate the following quantities.

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[c_{ji}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[qU_{i}\mathbb{E}[\chi_{ji}/M_{i}|U_{i}]]=E[\tilde{q}U_{i}p_{ji}]=\tilde{q}up_{ji}.

For
i\neq k
we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[c_{ji}c_{lk}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[\tilde{q}^{2}U_{i}U_{k}\mathbb{E}[\chi_{ji}/M_{i}|U_{i}]\mathbb{E}[\chi_{lk}/M_{k}|U_{k}]]=\tilde{q}^{2}p_{ji}p_{lk}\mathbb{E}[U_{i}U_{k}].

Denote by
\alpha u:=\mathbb{E}[U_{i}U_{k}]
, and obtain the trivial upper bound

\alpha u:=\mathbb{E}[U_{i}U_{k}]\leq\mathbb{E}[U_{i}]=u\Rightarrow 0\leq\alpha\leq 1.

In case
i=k
and
j\neq l
the calculation reads

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[c_{ji}c_{li}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[\mathbb{E}[Q_{i}^{2}U_{i}\chi_{ji}/M_{i}\chi_{li}/M_{i}|U_{i}]]=\mathbb{E}[Q_{i}^{2}U_{i}\mathbb{E}[\chi_{ji}\chi_{li}/M_{i}^{2}|U_{i}]]=:\beta u\tilde{q}^{2}p_{ji}p_{li}\Rightarrow\beta\geq 0,

while for
i=k
and
j=l
, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[c_{ji}^{2}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[Q_{i}^{2}U_{i}\chi_{ji}/M_{i}^{2}]=\mathbb{E}[Q_{i}/M_{i}\mathbb{E}[Q_{i}U_{i}\chi_{ji}/M_{i}|M_{i}]]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[Q_{i}/M_{i}\mathbb{E}[c_{ji}|M_{i}]]=qp_{ji}\mathbb{E}[Q_{i}/M_{i}U_{i}]=:(r\tilde{q})^{2}p_{ji}u.

After summarizing the above we arrive at

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[c_{ji}c_{lk}]=\begin{cases}\alpha\tilde{q}^{2}up_{ji}p_{lk}&i\neq k,\\
\beta\tilde{q}^{2}up_{ji}p_{li}&i=k,j\neq l,\\
(\tilde{q}r)^{2}up_{ji}&i=k,j=l,\end{cases}

which coincides with (
2
) by setting
q:=\tilde{q}\sqrt{u}
.
Note that in this case

\mathbb{E}[c_{ji}]=q\sqrt{u}p_{ji}.

The next examples represent the diversity of the model class.

Example 1
.

Consider a
d
-regular transitive graph
G=(V,E)
and define the following dynamics: in each step a uniformly random vertex sends a message to a uniformly random neighbor with some globally fixed messaging rate
\tilde{q}\in(0,1)
. In this case
\alpha=\beta=0
,
r=1
and
q=\tilde{q}\sqrt{u}
,
u=1/N
and
p_{ij}=d^{-1}\mathbb{I}((j,i)\in E)
.

Example 2
.

Let
G
be a
d
-regular transitive graph and let us consider the following messaging protocol: in each step a randomly chosen vertex sends a message to a single random neighbor with probability
\rho
, or it broadcasts, i.e. sends a message to all of its neighbors, with probability
1-\rho
, and the total weight of the messages is given by
\tilde{q}
. The proportion sent can be specified, but in the simplest setting the total weight of the message(s) is equal in each case. This gives us the following:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[c_{ji}]

\displaystyle=\frac{\tilde{q}}{N}\big(\rho d+1-\rho\big)p_{ji},

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[c_{ji}c_{lk}]

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}0&i\neq k,\\
\tilde{q}^{2}/N\ (1-\rho)p_{ji}p_{kl}&i=k,j\neq l,\\
\tilde{q}^{2}/N\ \big(\rho d+(1-\rho)/d\big)p_{ji}&i=k,j=l,\end{cases}

and so
u=1/N
,
q=\tilde{q}\sqrt{u}
,
\beta=(1-\rho)
,
r^{2}=\rho d+(1-\rho)/d
,
u=1/N
and
p_{ji}=\chi_{ji}/d
, where
\chi_{ji}
is the indicator of the activity of edge
(i,j)
.

Example 3
.

Given a
d
-regular transitive graph
G=(V,E)
, let
\chi_{ij},(j,i)\in E
denote i.i.d. random variables with Bernoulli(
\rho
) (
\rho\in(0,1)
) distribution and let

c_{ji}=\tilde{q}\frac{\chi_{ji}}{\max\left(1,\sum\limits_{k:(i,k)\in E}\chi_{ki}\right)}.

where
\tilde{q}\in(0,1)
is the global fixed message rate for each node
j
.

The previous example is used to showcase our algorithm in Section
5
.

Remark 1
.

Although our results will not deal with them, we note that a natural extension to the scheme above is to specify node- and vertex-dependent coefficients as follows:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[c_{ji}]

\displaystyle=q_{i}\sqrt{u_{i}}p_{ji}\quad i\neq j,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[c_{ji}c_{lk}]

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\alpha_{ijkl}q_{i}q_{k}p_{ji}p_{lk}&i\neq k,\\
\beta_{ijk}q_{i}^{2}p_{ji}p_{li}&i=k,j\neq l,\\
(q_{i}r_{i})^{2}p_{ji}&i=k,j=l.\end{cases}

(2)

Similar conditions are required for
P=(p_{ji})
and
q_{i},u_{i},r_{i}\in[0,1]
for all
i
.

Let us outline further examples for this more general class. Note that Example

fits in the stricter scheme in case

are constant.

Example 4
.

Each vertex sends a message independently to one of its neighbors, i.e.
\mathbb{E}[c_{ji}c_{lk}]=\mathbb{E}[c_{ji}]\mathbb{E}[c_{lk}]=u_{i}q_{i}p_{ji}u_{k}q_{k}p_{lk}.

Example 5
.

Given is a graph
G=(V,E)
, suppose there is a probability distribution
\nu

2^{V}
. In each step a

(p_{ij})_{i}

. The messages sent are independent conditioned on their senders. The overall proportions

may be inhomogeneous.

3
Low-complexity rate bounds

We will now state our first main result concerning the upper bound on the almost sure convergence rate of family of models described by (
2
).

Theorem 1
.

Let Assumptions
1
and
2
be in force for a homogeneous dynamics on
N
vertices.
Assume furthermore that
\forall i:p_{ii}=0
and either
\forall i,j:p_{ij}\in\{0,c\}
for some global
c\in(0,1)
or
\beta=0
. Let
1=\lambda_{1}\geq\lambda_{2}\geq\ldots\geq\lambda_{N}
denote the real eigenvalues of
P
. Then for the rate of almost sure convergence we have

\limsup_{t}\frac{1}{t}\max_{i}\log\bigg|\frac{x_{i}(t)}{w_{i}(t)}-\bar{x}\bigg|\leq\frac{1}{2}\log\xi_{1}=:\frac{1}{2}\gamma

(3)

where
\xi_{1}
denotes the largest root of the polynomial

f(\xi)=\prod_{i>1}(\xi-\Delta_{i})\bigg(1-\frac{q^{2}}{N}\sum_{j>1}\frac{b_{j}}{\xi-\Delta_{j}}\bigg).

The constants
\Delta_{j},b_{j}
satisfy

\displaystyle\Delta_{j}

\displaystyle=1-2q\sqrt{u}(1-\lambda_{j})+\alpha q^{2}(1-\lambda_{j})^{2},

\displaystyle b_{j}

\displaystyle=(1-\lambda_{j})\bigg((\beta-\alpha)(1-\lambda_{j})-\beta c+2r^{2}\bigg).

As we can see, to obtain the bound above, simply the spectrum of a matrix of size
N\times N
and a single root of a degree
N
polynomial is required. This has theoretical worst case complexity of
O(N^{3})

[
4
]
. The numerical precision and practical computational cost is further discussed in Section
5
.

This theorem is a generalization of Theorem 2 in
[
9
]
and uses very similar methods, thus we will only focus on the novel elements.
Denoting
H(t)=A(t)A(t-1)\ldots A(1)
, the primary bound towards (
3
) is obtained as

\displaystyle\max_{i}\left|\frac{x_{i}(t)}{w_{i}(t)}-\bar{x}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{c}{\min_{i}w_{i}(t)}||x(0)||_{2}||H(t)(I-J)||_{2}

(4)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{c}{\min_{i}w_{i}(t)}||x(0)||_{2}||H(t)(I-J)||_{F},

with some constant
c>0
thanks to the equivalence of
l^{2}
and Frobenius norms.
The first term on
w_{i}(t)
is subexponential according to Lemma 10 of
[
6
]
, relying on Assumptions
1
,
2
. Note that in the cited lemma primitivity is needed, i.e.
H(t)
eventually becoming elementwise positive, which is now provided by the connectivity assumption in terms of
P
and requiring
q\sqrt{u}<1
, which ensures a positive diagonal of
\mathbb{E}A(1)
.

The crucial last term is then handled using the map
\Phi(X)=\mathbb{E}[A(1)^{\top}XA(1)]
and its adjoint
\Phi^{*}(X)=\mathbb{E}[A(1)XA(1)^{\top}]
as

\|x(t)-\overline{x}\mathbf{1}\|_{2}^{2}\leq\|(\Phi^{*})^{t}(I-J))\|^{2}_{F}\|x(0)\|_{2}^{2}\leq\|\Phi^{*}\|^{t}_{\mathcal{x}(0)}\|x(0)\|^{2}_{2},

(5)

where
\mathcal{X}_{0}=\{X\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}:X=X^{\top},XJ=0\}
.
Further details on the properties of
\Phi
and the connections above are spelled out in Appendix
A
.
To proceed, we present some simple structural observations on
\Phi^{*}
.

Lemma 1
.

Let
X
be an arbitrary
N\times N
matrix with constant diagonal and assume that
P
is symmetric. Consider the general model (
1
) with
\alpha_{ijkl}=\alpha
,
q_{i}=q
,
r_{i}=r
, and
\beta_{ijk}=0
, or, in case
\beta_{ijk}=\beta\neq 0
, assume that
P
consists of the elements
0,c
. Then
\Phi^{*}(X)
is a degree 2 matrix polynomial of the variables
X,P,\omega(X)I
, where
\omega(X)
denotes the common diagonal element of
X
.

The proof can be found in the Appendix B.

Corollary 1
.

Suppose (
2
) is in force,
and
P
is symmetric and transitive.
Then
X
having constant diagonal
\omega
and
XP=PX
imply
P\Phi^{*}(X)=\Phi^{*}(X)P
.

For symmetric matrices the condition
XP=PX
, i.e.

and
P
commuting, implies that
P
and

have shared eigenvectors and there is a natural correspondence between their eigenvalues. We say that eigenvalues

Proposition 1
.

Let
X_{0}=I-J
and
X_{t+1}=\Phi^{*}(X_{t})
and let Assumptions

,
2
be in force.
Let us order the eigenvalues

(and the corresponding eigenvectors

.

The proof is a series of quite straightforward but rather cumbersome calculations, its outline can be found in Appendix

We can write

but with different

\lim_{t}\frac{1}{t}\max_{j}\log\mu_{t,j}\leq(\log\xi_{1})/2,

\xi_{1}
denotes the largest root of the characteristic polynomial

\prod_{i>1}(\xi-\Delta_{i})\bigg(1-\frac{q^{2}}{N}\sum_{j>1}\frac{b_{j}}{\xi-\Delta_{j}}\bigg).

(6)

Given that the claim does not depend on the actual

4
Optimizing transmission intensity for rates

In what follows we analyze the dependence on
q
of the bound obtained in Theorem
1
, which will be helpful for algorithmic tuning. Even though optimizing an upper bound on the rate does not apriori provide the optimal rate, we will see this is an easily accessible quantity with good performance, see also Section
5
for numerical demonstration.

Establishing the convexity of
q\to\xi_{1}(q)
is interesting on it own,
and opens a wide range of possibilities for optimization.

Theorem 2
.

Assume the same conditions as in Theorem
1
. Then
the function
[0,1]\ni q\mapsto\xi_{1}(q)
is convex.

Proof.

By definition
\xi_{1}(q)
is the largest solution to
f(\xi,q)=0
in
\xi
. The latter can be written as

\prod_{j>1}(\xi-\Delta_{j})\bigg(1-\frac{q^{2}}{N}\sum_{i>1}\frac{b_{i}}{\xi-\Delta_{i}}\bigg)=0.

(7)

According to Proposition
4
detailed in Appendix
C
we know that
\xi_{1}(q)>\Delta_{j_{0}}
, the largest entry of

\displaystyle=\frac{(2(\xi-\Delta)-2q\Delta^{\prime}+2q\Delta^{\prime}+q^{2}\Delta^{\prime\prime})(\xi-\Delta)^{2}+(2q(\xi-\Delta)+q^{2}\Delta^{\prime})2(\xi-\Delta)\Delta^{\prime}}{(\xi-\Delta)^{4}}

\displaystyle=\frac{2(\xi-\Delta)^{2}+q^{2}\Delta^{\prime\prime}(\xi-\Delta)+4q(\xi-\Delta)\Delta^{\prime}+2q^{2}(\Delta^{\prime})^{2}}{(\xi-\Delta)^{3}}

and so

\displaystyle\det h^{\prime\prime}=\det\begin{bmatrix}\partial_{\xi}^{2}h&\partial_{q}\partial_{\xi}h\\
\partial_{\xi}\partial_{q}h&\partial_{q}^{2}h\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=\partial_{\xi}^{2}h\partial_{q}^{2}h-(\partial_{q}\partial_{\xi}h)^{2}

\displaystyle=\big(4q^{2}(\xi-\Delta)^{2}+2q^{4}(\xi-\Delta)\Delta^{\prime\prime}+8q^{3}(\xi-\Delta)\Delta^{\prime}+4q^{4}(\Delta^{\prime})^{2}\big)/(\xi-\Delta)^{6}

\displaystyle\quad-4\big(q^{2}(\xi-\Delta)^{2}+2q^{3}(\xi-\Delta)\Delta^{\prime}+q^{4}(\Delta^{\prime})^{2}\big)/(\xi-\Delta)^{6}

\displaystyle=\frac{2q^{4}(\xi-\Delta)\Delta^{\prime\prime}}{(\xi-\Delta)^{6}}=2q^{4}\Delta^{\prime\prime}(\xi-\Delta)^{-5}.

∎
