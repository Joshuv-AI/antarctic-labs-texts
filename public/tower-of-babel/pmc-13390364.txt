Distinct neural substrates of disease severity and cognitive impairment in COPD: a multimodal MRI study
Authors: Wei Wang, Yang Xue, Gang Wang, Ming Zhao, Yi Xiao, Wencai Huang, Yang Yang, Zhongyu Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe neural substrates distinguishing Chronic Obstructive Pulmonary Disease (COPD) patients with mild cognitive impairment (MCI) from those with preserved cognition are not fully understood. This study aims to investigate the distinct neural substrates associated with disease severity and cognitive impairment in COPD using multimodal MRI.MethodsThirty-three COPD patients with MCI (COPD-MCI), 30 cognitively normal patients (COPD-nMCI), and 34 healthy controls underwent structural and functional MRI. Voxel-Based Morphometry (VBM) and seed-based Functional Connectivity (FC) analyses identified gray matter volume (GMV) and network alterations. Diagnostic utility was evaluated using ROC analysis.ResultsCommon to all COPD patients, GMV atrophy and reduced FC in the sensorimotor network (left postcentral gyrus/inferior parietal lobule) were observed, correlating with disease duration (r = -0.523) and distinguished patients from healthy controls (cross-validated AUC = 0.746). Notably, the MCI subgroup was characterized by focal atrophy in the left thalamus and functional decoupling within the visual association network. Visual network FC served as a robust discriminator between the two COPD subgroups (cross-validated AUC = 0.833) and correlated with MoCA scores (r = 0.585). Additionally, reduced fronto-striatal connectivity was observed in the MCI group, while its integrity correlated with lung function in the nMCI group (r = 0.666).ConclusionsOur findings suggest distinct substrates underlying clinical heterogeneity in COPD. Sensorimotor alterations likely reflect a generalized disease trait of chronic respiratory burden, whereas the integrity of higher-order associative networks, involving the thalamus, visual, and fronto-striatal circuits, acts as a critical checkpoint against cognitive decline. These findings offer novel biomarkers for the early detection of COPD-related cognitive impairment.

Introduction

Chronic Obstructive Pulmonary Disease (COPD) represents a substantial global health burden, characterized by persistent airflow limitation and chronic airway inflammation. Current epidemiological models estimate that COPD affects approximately 10–12% of the global adult population, with its prevalence and mortality increasing significantly with age [1, 2]. While traditionally categorized as a respiratory condition, COPD is now widely acknowledged as a complex multi-organ syndrome. Among its extrapulmonary manifestations, cognitive impairment is particularly debilitating, with prevalence rates ranging from 10% to over 60% depending on the cohort and diagnostic criteria employed [3].

While the epidemiological association is strong, the biological pathways remain complex. The potential mechanisms linking COPD to cognitive decline are multifactorial [4–6]. Chronic hypoxia and hypercapnia may induce direct neuronal toxicity, while systemic inflammation—mediated by cytokines such as IL-6 and TNF-α—can compromise the blood-brain barrier and promote neuroinflammation [7]. Furthermore, oxidative stress and vascular endothelial dysfunction may accelerate cerebral small vessel disease [8]. Despite these established pathophysiological pathways, a critical heterogeneity exists in clinical presentations: a subset of COPD patients develops rapid cognitive deterioration (MCI or dementia), while others with comparable lung function impairment maintain preserved cognition (COPD-nMCI) [9]. This clinical divergence suggests that lung function parameters alone are insufficient to predict cognitive outcomes.

Given that brain structural and functional changes often precede overt clinical symptoms, unraveling the specific neural substrates that differentiate these two phenotypes is critical. Previous neuroimaging studies have identified generalized cerebral atrophy, altered functional connectivity (FC) [10, 11] and extensive white matter damage [12] in COPD patients. However, the majority of these studies have treated COPD as a homogeneous entity, potentially obscuring specific neuroimaging signatures associated with the onset of MCI. Furthermore, single-modality studies often fail to capture the interplay between structural damage and functional network reorganization.

To address these gaps, the present study employed a combined structural (Voxel-Based Morphometry, VBM) and functional (resting-state FC) MRI approach. We stratified stable COPD patients into MCI and non-MCI subgroups to test the hypothesis of “dissociable neural involvement”. Specifically, we hypothesized that: (1) Alterations in sensorimotor and parietal regions would be a generalized feature of COPD pathology, potentially reflecting the chronic sensation of dyspnea; whereas (2) Aberrant patterns in high-order cognitive hubs (e.g., thalamus, prefrontal cortex) and visual association networks would be specific to the MCI subgroup, serving as potential biomarkers associated with early cognitive decline.

Materials and methods

Participants and study design

This cross-sectional study was conducted between October 2018 and December 2019 at the outpatient and Department of Respiratory Medicine, Jinan Military General Hospital. A total of 83 patients with stable COPD were initially enrolled.

Inclusion Criteria: (1) Diagnosis of COPD according to the Global Initiative for Chronic Obstructive Lung Disease (GOLD) criteria [13] (post-bronchodilator FEV1/FVC < 0.70, 30% ≤ FEV1 ≤ 80% predicted); (2) Age > 40 years; (3) Clinical stability, defined as the absence of acute exacerbations, hospital admissions, or changes in medication within the preceding 6 months. Exclusion Criteria: (1) Dependency on long-term home oxygen therapy (to rule out severe continuous hypoxia confounding); (2) History of other neurological (e.g., stroke, tumor, epilepsy), psychiatric, or severe cardiovascular diseases; (3) Alcohol or substance abuse; (4) Contraindications to MRI scanning (e.g., metallic implants, claustrophobia).

An additional 40 age-, gender-, and education-matched healthy volunteers were recruited as healthy controls (HCs). The protocol was approved by the ethics committee of The Changzheng hospital, and written informed consent were obtained from all participants after a full written and verbal explanation of this study. Our work was carried out in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Clinical and cognitive assessment

All participants underwent a comprehensive clinical evaluation, including pulmonary function tests (PFT) utilizing a spirometer (Jaeger, Germany). The Medical Research Council (MRC) Dyspnea Scale was used to quantify breathlessness [14]. The severity of airflow limitation was stratified according to the 2025 Global Initiative for Chronic Obstructive Lung Disease (GOLD) strategy [15]. Based on post-bronchodilator FEV1% predicted, patients were classified into four stages: GOLD 1 (mild, FEV1 ≥ 80%), GOLD 2 (moderate, 50% ≤FEV1 < 80%), GOLD 3 (severe, 30% ≤ FEV1 < 50%), and GOLD 4 (very severe, FEV1 < 30%). Patients with very severe airflow limitation (GOLD 4) were excluded due to their inability to tolerate the MRI scanning protocol.

Cognitive function was assessed using the Beijing version of the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) [16], a sensitive screening tool for MCI. Based on the recommended cutoff score, COPD patients were stratified into two groups: the COPD-MCI group (MoCA score < 26) and the COPD-nMCI group (MoCA score ≥ 26). To minimize the confounding effect of educational background, a correction factor was applied: one point was added to the total score for participants with ≤ 12 years of formal education.

The study protocol adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Ethics Committee of Changzheng Hospital, Affiliated to Naval Military Medical University. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

MRI data acquisition

Magnetic resonance imaging was performed using a GE 3.0T MR750 scanner (General Electric, Milwaukee, USA) equipped with an eight-channel head coil. Foam padding and earplugs were used to minimize head motion and scanner noise. The routine head sequences (T1-weighted imaging, T2-weighted imaging, T2-FLAIR imaging and diffusion-weighted imaging) of all participants were firstly acquired, and checked by two experienced neuroradiologists to rule out anatomic abnormalities.

Structural MRI: High-resolution T1-weighted anatomical images were acquired using a 3D Brain Volume (BRAVO) sequence with the following parameters: TR = 8.2 ms, TE = 3.2 ms, flip angle = 12°, field of view (FOV) = 256 × 256 mm², matrix = 256 × 256, slice thickness = 1 mm, and 120 axial slices.Functional MRI: Resting-state BOLD images were acquired using a gradient-echo planar imaging (EPI) sequence: TR = 2000 ms, TE = 35 ms, flip angle = 90°, FOV = 240 × 240 mm², matrix = 64 × 64, slice thickness = 4 mm, gap = 0 mm, and 200 volumes. Participants were instructed to keep their eyes closed, relax, and avoid engaging in structured thought processes or falling asleep.

Data preprocessing

Data preprocessing was performed using the Computational Anatomy Toolbox 12 (CAT12, r1355; http://www.neuro.uni-jena.de/cat/) and Data Processing & Analysis for Brain Imaging (DPABI, V4.2_190919; http://rfmri.org/dpabi) [17] software on the MATLAB (R2016b) platform.

T1-weighted images were bias-corrected and segmented into gray matter (GM), white matter (WM), and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF). The DARTEL (Diffeomorphic Anatomical Registration Through Exponentiated Lie Algebra) algorithm was used to normalize the GM tissue segments to the Montreal Neurological Institute (MNI) space. GM images were modulated to preserve the total amount of GM and smoothed with an 8-mm full-width at half-maximum (FWHM) Gaussian kernel.

Functional images underwent removal of the first 10 time points, slice timing correction, and head motion correction. Participants with head motion > 2.0 mm in translation or > 2.0° in rotation were excluded. The images were spatially normalized to the MNI space (resampled to 3 × 3 × 3 mm³), smoothed with an 8-mm FWHM kernel, detrended, and band-pass filtered (0.01–0.08 Hz). Nuisance covariates (Friston 24-parameter head motion model, WM signal, CSF signal, and global signal) were regressed out. Global signal regression was specifically included to stringently control for global physiological artifacts (e.g., prominent respiration and cardiac fluctuations), which is particularly crucial in a COPD cohort known for altered respiratory mechanics.

After quality control, 26 participants were excluded due to poor image quality or excessive head motion. The final analysis included 33 COPD-MCI patients, 30 COPD-nMCI patients, and 34 HCs.

Statistical analysis

The clinical and demographic data were analyzed by using SPSS20.0. Group differences were assessed using one-way Analysis of Variance (ANOVA) for continuous variables and Chi-square tests for categorical variables. All statistical thresholds were set at p < 0.05. The Bonferroni method was used for post-hoc comparative analysis.

Voxel-wise GMV differences among the three groups were analyzed using Analysis of Covariance (ANCOVA) in SPM12, with age, gender, education level, and Total Intracranial Volume (TIV) as covariates. A False Discovery Rate (FDR) correction was applied with a threshold of p < 0.05.

Brain regions showing significant GMV atrophy in the ANCOVA were defined as Regions of Interest (ROIs). Seed-based FC analysis was performed by calculating Pearson’s correlation coefficients between the mean time series of each seed and all other voxels in the brain. Fisher’s r-to-z transformation was applied to improve normality. Group differences in FC maps were assessed using ANCOVA followed by post-hoc t-tests. Multiple comparisons were corrected using Gaussian Random Field (GRF) theory (voxel-level p < 0.005, cluster-level p < 0.05). To quantify the magnitude of group differences, effect sizes were calculated for all significant findings. Partial correlation analyses (controlling for age, gender, and education) were conducted to examine relationships between neuroimaging indices and clinical variables (PFTs, disease duration, MoCA). To evaluate the diagnostic potential of the identified neuroimaging markers while mitigating the risk of overfitting inherent to small sample sizes, we employed a Leave-One-Out Cross-Validation (LOOCV) strategy. The diagnostic performance was quantified using the Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic Curve (AUC) based on the cross-validated predicted probabilities. Furthermore, robust 95% confidence intervals (CI) for the AUCs were estimated using 1,000 bootstrap iterations.

Results

Clinical demographic data and clinical characteristics

There were no significant differences in age, gender, BMI, or education level among the three groups. However, significant differences were observed in pulmonary function parameters, MoCA scores, and subdomain scores among the groups (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$p$$\end{document} < 0.001). Specifically, the COPD-MCI group exhibited the lowest scores in cognitive domains compared to the COPD-nMCI and HC groups. The two COPD subgroups were well-matched in terms of demographic characteristics and clinical severity. Notably, the majority of the patients were classified as GOLD stage 2 or 3. Baseline resting SpO2, exacerbation history, and pharmacological treatments (including LAMA, LABA/LAMA, and triple therapies) showed no significant differences between the two COPD subgroups (all \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:p$$\end{document} > 0.05). In post-hoc comparisons relative to the HCs, the COPD-MCI group showed widespread GMV atrophy in the bilateral LING, left MTG, left thalamus, right SMG, left PoCG/IPL, and right DLPFC. The COPD-nMCI group exhibited reduced GMV only in the left PoCG/IPL compared to HCs. FC comparisons

Using the regions with significant GMV atrophy as seeds, ANCOVA revealed altered functional connectivity (FC) patterns among the three groups, primarily involving the visual network (bilateral LING and right LING/fusiform gyrus), the sensorimotor network (left PoCG/IPL and bilateral precuneus [PCUN], left SMG), and the fronto-striatal circuit (right DLPFC and left caudate nucleus [CAU]) (Fig. 2A).

Exploratory ROC analysis based on the LOOCV procedure demonstrated robust diagnostic capability for the identified neural signatures. For distinguishing COPD patients from HCs, the FC value between the left SMG and left PoCG/IPL yielded a cross-validated AUC of 0.746 (95% CI: 0.639–0.850, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:p$$\end{document} < 0.001). Discussion

To our knowledge, this study is one of the first multimodal MRI investigations to systematically dissociate the neuroimaging signatures of general COPD pathology from those specific to the presence of MCI. Our study reveals a divergent pattern of brain structural and functional anomalies: while sensorimotor network alterations emerge as a generalized consequence of chronic respiratory disease, specific structural atrophy in the left thalamus and functional decoupling within the visual association networks characterize the MCI stage. Together, these findings provide a robust neurobiological framework explaining the heterogeneity of COPD, illustrating why some patients maintain cognitive preservation while others experience decline.

A key finding of this study is the specific atrophy of the left thalamus in the COPD-MCI group related to COPD-nMCI. Although cortical atrophy was widespread, the reduction in thalamic volume best distinguished the two patient subgroups. Recent evidence highlights the thalamus not as a passive sensory relay but as a cognitive gateway, a structure crucial for maintaining cortical mental representations and supporting executive control [19, 20]. Mechanistically, the thalamus possesses a high metabolic rate and a dense microvascular supply, rendering it theoretically vulnerable to the chronic hypoxic-ischemic insults and systemic inflammation characteristic of COPD [21]. We propose that in the nMCI stage, the brain likely employs compensatory recruitment to preserve thalamic function. However, as the hypoxic load accumulates, this subcortical hub may eventually exhibit structural vulnerability. The atrophy of the thalamus could potentially disrupt the synchronization of thalamo-cortical circuits, potentially marking a critical threshold associated with the clinical onset of MCI. This aligns with vascular cognitive impairment models, where subcortical integrity is a key predictor of processing speed [22, 23].

Perhaps the most novel functional finding is the specific disconnection within the visual association network, located between the bilateral LING and right LING/fusiform gyrus, in the COPD-MCI group. This FC metric showed a high diagnostic accuracy (LOOCV AUC = 0.833) and correlated strongly with MoCA scores. Traditionally, these regions are associated with visual processing; however, emerging research indicates that the integrity of sensory cortices, including visual areas, is fundamental to global cognition [24]. Theoretically, this can be interpreted through the Sensory Deprivation Hypothesis, which postulates that when primary sensory inputs are degraded, whether through structural atrophy or functional decoupling, the brain must reallocate excessive cognitive resources to process basic perceptual information, thereby depleting the neural reserve available for higher-order tasks [25]. Our results suggest that in COPD-MCI, the posterior brain networks fail to efficiently integrate visual information. This bottom-up processing failure likely contributes to the broader cognitive deficits observed, supporting the growing consensus that sensory network dysfunction is an early, sensitive biomarker of prodromal dementia [11].

In stark contrast to the MCI-specific changes, reduced FC between the left SMG and the PoCG/IPL was observed in all COPD patients, regardless of cognitive status. Furthermore, this connectivity negatively correlated with disease duration and positively with FEV1/FVC, tracking the physiological severity of the lung disease. The IPL and SMG are integral components of the sensorimotor network, repeatedly implicated in the multidimensional perception of dyspnea and respiratory interoception [26]. We interpret these widespread parietal alterations as a functional maladaptation to the relentless sensation of breathlessness, a generalized background noise of COPD pathology. This distinction is clinically important. Neural changes in the parietal lobes of COPD patients should be interpreted cautiously, as they likely reflect the sensory burden of the respiratory disease itself rather than a specific mechanism of cognitive decline [27].

Additionally, the COPD-MCI group also exhibited reduced FC between the right DLPFC and the CAU. This fronto-striatal circuit is heavily reliant on dopaminergic signaling, which is known to be impaired by chronic pro-inflammatory cytokines (e.g., TNF-α, IL-6) crossing the blood-brain barrier [28]. Chronic systemic inflammation can trigger microglial activation, leading to synaptic loss in these vulnerable circuits [29]. The correlation between this circuit’s integrity and lung function (FEV1%) in the nMCI group implies a resilience threshold, suggesting that patients with better-preserved pulmonary function may maintain sufficient fronto-striatal connectivity to compensate for other neural deficits, thereby delaying the cognitive manifestation of the disease [30]. Consequently, the breakdown of this frontal resilience, likely triggered when pulmonary damage surpasses a critical threshold, combined with the collapse of the thalamic-visual integrity, may contribute to the clinical manifestation of MCI.

Several limitations warrant discussion. First, regarding neuroimaging methodology, the seed-based FC analysis, while maximizing sensitivity to atrophy-related dysfunction, is inherently hypothesis-driven. This approach captures structure-function coupled alterations but may overlook broader network reorganization in structurally preserved regions that data-driven methods (e.g., ICA) could reveal. Furthermore, despite rigorous motion correction (Friston-24 regression and censoring), residual head motion and non-neuronal respiratory fluctuations remain potential confounds, particularly given the altered respiratory mechanics in COPD [31, 32]. Additionally, while Global Signal Regression was applied to robustly mitigate these extensive respiratory artifacts, we acknowledge that GSR remains debated and may introduce artificial negative correlations or attenuate widespread neural signals. Future studies utilizing multi-echo fMRI or physiological recordings are recommended to improve artifact removal. Second, regarding clinical interpretation, the proposed biological mechanisms are inferential. As we did not quantify serum cytokines or utilize molecular imaging, attributing the observed neural deficits to specific inflammatory or hypoxic pathways remains speculative and requires validation via multi-modal biomarkers. Additionally, the reliance on MoCA—a screening tool lacking domain-specific granularity—limits our ability to characterize specific cognitive subtypes. The absence of polysomnography to definitively rule out obstructive sleep apnea, which shares hypoxic mechanisms with COPD, is also a potential confounder. Finally, regarding study design and diagnostic performance, the cross-sectional nature precludes causal inference regarding the temporal sequence of brain-lung interactions. While we implemented Leave-One-Out Cross-Validation to mitigate overfitting in the ROC analysis, it is important to emphasize that the features used for the classifier were initially identified as statistically significant across the entire dataset. This intrinsic circularity inherently inflates the diagnostic accuracy estimates. Thus, the reported diagnostic accuracy should be strictly interpreted as proof-of-concept evidence rather than a true out-of-sample predictive performance, necessitating external validation in large-scale, longitudinal, and fully independent cohorts.”

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study provides compelling evidence for distinct neural substrates associated with disease severity and cognitive status in COPD. We propose that sensorimotor network disruptions represent a generalized disease trait reflecting the chronic physiological burden of respiratory distress. In contrast, the integrity of higher-order associative and executive networks, specifically characterized by thalamic atrophy, visual network decoupling, and the exhaustion of fronto-striatal reserve, appears to be a critical factor associated with cognitive outcomes. These findings not only deepen our understanding of the “lung-brain axis” but also highlight the potential of monitoring these specific non-motor circuits as robust biomarkers for the early detection of COPD-related cognitive impairment.
