Long-RP Tachycardia in a Professional Cyclist
Authors: Sara Coacci, Andrea Antonelli, Guido Guglielmo Michele De Ambroggi, Sara Foresti, Hussam Ali, Silvia Magnani, Pierpaolo Lupo, Riccardo Cappato, Francesco Bandera
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLong RP tachycardias share similar clinical presentations and challenging electrocardiographic features which frequently lead to diagnostic uncertainty. When unrecognized or misdiagnosed, the incessant behavior may lead to tachycardiomyopathy.Case SummaryA 28-year-old asymptomatic professional cyclist presented after his Apple Watch repeatedly reported sustained elevated resting heart rate. Physical examination, laboratory results, imaging, and echocardiography were normal. Electrocardiogram showed narrow QRS tachycardia with negative P waves in inferior leads. Electrophysiological study excluded accessory pathways, localizing an ectopic atrial substrate at the posteroseptal tricuspid annulus. Radiofrequency ablation successfully eliminated the focal atrial tachycardia, restoring sinus rhythm. The Apple Watch confirmed stable heart rate normalization postprocedure and at follow-up.DiscussionWe emphasize the role of wearable devices in early arrhythmia detection and the diagnostic challenges posed by long RP tachycardias.Take-Home MessageSmartwatches can fasten arrhythmias detection and management in asymptomatic athletes, and electrophysiological study remains essential for definitive supraventricular arrhythmias diagnosis.

History of Presentation

A 28-year-old male professional cyclist presented to our emergency department (ED) after his Apple Watch repeatedly alerted him of a sustained increase in resting heart rate over the preceding month. His usual average heart rate of 50 beats/min had risen to >100 beats/min at rest. He denied palpitations, dizziness, chest pain, dyspnea, or exercise intolerance. On arrival, he was asymptomatic. Vital signs were stable. Physical examination, laboratory tests, chest radiograph, and echocardiography were unremarkable. Past Medical History

The patient’s prior medical history was unrevealing. He reported no cardiovascular risk factors and no family history of coronary artery disease, sudden cardiac death, or arrhythmias. He took no medications and denied stimulants use. He had undergone previous baseline ECG and exercise stress testing as part of routine annual athletic screening, which were always normal.

Differential Diagnosis

Given his young age, athletic baseline bradycardia, and persistent tachycardia alerts, initial differential diagnoses included atrioventricular nodal re-entrant tachycardia (AVNRT), orthodromic atrioventricular re-entrant tachycardia (AVRT) with concealed accessory pathway (AP), focal atrial tachycardia (FAT), permanent junctional reciprocating tachycardia (PJRT), enhanced automaticity or frequent supraventricular ectopy, and tachycardia secondary to noncardiac causes (eg, thyrotoxicosis, anemia, infection), but this was quickly excluded.

Investigations

The ECG at presentation revealed narrow QRS complex tachycardia with incessant pattern. P waves were negative in inferior leads and positive in V1, with a RP interval longer than the PR interval. No signs of ventricular pre-excitation were visible. Laboratory tests including complete blood count, electrolytes, thyroid function, liver and kidney function, and inflammatory markers were normal. Chest radiograph showed a normal cardiac silhouette, with no evidence of pulmonary edema or consolidation, and no mediastinal abnormalities. Rest echocardiography showed normal biventricular structure and function and continent valves. In the ED, vagal maneuvers, including carotid sinus massage, were ineffective in terminating the arrhythmia. Likewise, administration of 100 mg intravenous flecainide, followed by a 2.5-mg metoprolol bolus, failed to suppress the tachycardia. Thus, the patient was hospitalized for electrophysiological study (EPS).

Management

At the time of EPS, the patient was in sinus rhythm with frequent supraventricular ectopic beats, also occurring in short runs. Key findings included normal baseline conduction intervals (PQ: 150 milliseconds; QRS: 95 milliseconds; AH: 75 milliseconds; HV: 40 milliseconds), frequent supraventricular ectopy with negative P waves in inferior leads, and positive P waves in V1. Retrograde conduction was concentric and decremental. No APs were identified during para-Hisian pacing and differential ventricular pacing. No reproducible re-entrant tachycardia, and no inducibility of PJRT, AVNRT, or AVRT was obtained. Conventional mapping of the ectopic atrial beats showed earliest activation (−25 milliseconds before the extrasystolic P-wave onset) at the posteroseptal tricuspid annulus (Figure 3). Pacing maneuvers were not undertaken because the tachycardia was unsustained and noninducible throughout the procedure despite isoprotenerol infusion. Adenosine challenge during ventricular pacing was not performed because its ability to differentiate atrioventricular nodal from AP conduction is limited in nodal-like pathways such as PJRT, which are also adenosine-sensitive. Targeted nonirrigated radiofrequency ablation (20–25 W) at the posteroseptal tricuspid annulus successfully eliminated the FAT (Figure 4A). Outcome and Follow-Up

Postablation, the patient's resting heart rate immediately returned to his baseline 50 beats/min. Apple Watch tracings captured normalization of rhythm within hours (Figure 5). No pericardial effusion was observed at postprocedural echocardiography. He remained asymptomatic at discharge and at first follow-up visit, where smartwatch data confirmed a stable, persistently reduced resting heart rate trend. ECG Holter monitoring confirmed absence of ectopy or tachyarrhythmia recurrence. Discussion

Modern heart rhythm monitoring systems, such as Apple Watches, are increasingly relevant in the early detection of arrhythmias. These devices provide continuous tracking of cardiac activity and can alert users to abnormal heart rate patterns, as demonstrated in this case, where the smartwatch was the sole trigger for clinical evaluation. In asymptomatic individuals, particularly athletes such as cyclists with baseline bradycardia that may mask arrhythmias, such devices can prompt earlier EPS assessment.1,2 Early detection of incessant or recurrent arrhythmias enables timely intervention, preventing progression to cardiac dysfunction, arrhythmia deterioration, and tachycardia-induced cardiomyopathy, especially in patients with minimal or no symptoms.2,3

In the present case, the patient was largely asymptomatic at rest and during exercise, which may be explained by sinus rate acceleration during exertion competing with the FAT. Notably, timely heart rate alerts from the Apple Watch prompted earlier ED presentation, speeding up the following diagnostic and therapeutic work-up. Although smartwatches are increasingly helpful to assist the diagnosis of atrial fibrillation or other supraventricular tachycardias,4 similar cases of FAT have not been reported, making this case, to our knowledge, the first of its kind.

FAT can electrocardiographically mimic PJRT, or Coumel tachycardia, a rare form of orthodromic reciprocating tachycardia characterized by a slowly conducting, concealed, and usually posteroseptal AP.5 PJRT accounts for <1% of supraventricular tachycardias in children and young adults, and its incessant nature may lead to delayed diagnosis and, in some cases, tachycardia-induced cardiomyopathy.6,7 By contrast, FAT represents a more frequent cause of long RP supraventricular tachycardia in young athletes, arising from a discrete atrial focus commonly located along the crista terminalis, pulmonary veins, or tricuspid annulus.8 Although typically nonincessant, repetitive or sustained FAT may similarly predispose to tachycardiomyopathy.9 Differentiating FAT from PJRT can be challenging due to electrocardiographic features resemblance and anatomic origin overlap.8,9

Classically, FAT features nonsinus P waves with a variable RP interval, reflecting its origin from a discrete atrial focus rather than a re-entrant circuit.9 PJRT is characterized by a long RP interval, inverted P waves in inferior leads, and a tendency toward incessant behavior.9 Atypical AVNRT may also present with a long RP interval due to delayed retrograde atrial activation, which may further complicate differentiation from FAT or PJRT. Typical AVNRT demonstrates a short RP interval with retrograde P waves often appearing as a pseudo r' in V1 or a pseudo-S pattern in inferior leads.9 Orthodromic AVRT generally presents with a short RP interval and AP-dependent reentry, with the earliest atrial activation at annular insertions.9

In the present case, although PJRT and atypical AVNRT were initially considered, some electrocardiographic findings did not support either diagnoses. Despite the tachycardia being incessant from admission to EPS, the patient was in sinus rhythm and noninducible at the time of the study, possibly due to conscious sedation. Furthermore, on baseline ECG, each tachycardia termination was followed by immediate reinitiation with an inverted P-wave and no preceding sinus beat, making PJRT unlikely. Finally, during EPS, no APs nor re-entrant circuits were induced, whereas conventional mapping localized a focal atrial/ectopic origin at the posteroseptal tricuspid annulus. Notably, this region is a common anatomic substrate for PJRT; however, several electrophysiological findings—including lack of retrograde AP conduction and inability to induce re-entrant tachycardia—favored a diagnosis of FAT or ectopic beats originating from the posteroseptal cavotricuspid annulus.10 This overlap of ECG-mimicking features and anatomic substrate posed a significant challenge for both clinicians and electrophysiologists, highlighting the risk of misdiagnosis of long RP tachycardias, particularly in asymptomatic patients. This underscores the crucial role of EPS in accurately characterizing arrhythmic substrates and establishing a definitive diagnosis.10 Once the arrhythmia's location is identified, catheter ablation represents the gold standard treatment, offering high success rates and low complication risk when guided by modern mapping techniques.3,6 Given the potentially incessant nature of long RP tachycardias, pharmacologic therapy such as beta-blockers or class IC antiarrhythmic drugs, may provide temporary rate control but is often insufficient for long-term suppression, reinforcing ablation as the definitive therapeutic option.1,3,7

Conclusions

Smartwatch-derived alerts may significantly shorten diagnostic delays and allow timely intervention before structural heart remodeling occurs. Nonetheless, differentiation of long RP tachycardias solely based on ECG remains challenging, highlighting the central role of EPS for definitive diagnosis.
