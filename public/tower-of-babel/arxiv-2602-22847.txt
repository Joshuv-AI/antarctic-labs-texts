Decentralized Ranking Aggregation: Gossip Algorithms for Borda and Copeland Consensus
Anna van Elst, Kerrian Le Caillec 12, Igor Colin 1, Stephan Clémençon 1

Abstract
The concept of ranking aggregation plays a central role in preference analysis, and numerous algorithms for calculating median rankings, often originating in social choice theory, have been documented in the literature, offering theoretical guarantees in a centralized setting, i.e. , when all the ranking data to be aggregated can be brought together in a single computing unit. For many technologies ( e.g. peer-to-peer networks, IoT, multi-agent systems), extending the ability to calculate consensus rankings with guarantees in a decentralized setting, i.e. , when preference data is initially distributed across a communicating network, remains a major methodological challenge. Indeed, in recent years, the literature on decentralized computation has mainly focused on computing or optimizing statistics such as arithmetic means using gossip algorithms. The purpose of this article is precisely to study how to achieve reliable consensus on collective rankings using classical rules ( e.g. Borda, Copeland) in a decentralized setting, thereby raising new questions, robustness to corrupted nodes, and scalability through reduced communication costs in particular. The approach proposed and analyzed here relies on random gossip communication, allowing autonomous agents to compute global ranking consensus using only local interactions, without coordination or central authority. We provide rigorous convergence guarantees, including explicit rate bounds, for the Borda and Copeland consensus methods. Beyond these rules, we also provide a decentralized implementation of consensus according to the median rank rule and local Kemenization. Extensive empirical evaluations on various network topologies and real and synthetic ranking datasets demonstrate that our algorithms converge quickly and reliably to the correct ranking aggregation. This work paves the way for principled collective decision-making in fully decentralized systems.

2.1
Ranking Aggregation/Consensus

Given a collection
\{{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{1},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{n}\}
of
n\geq 1
permutations in
\mathfrak{S}_{m}
, the objective of ranking aggregation is to compute a consensus permutation
{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{*}\in\mathfrak{S}_{m}
that best summarizes it. This notion of central ranking can be interpreted in different ways. It can correspond to the mode of a probability distribution on
\mathfrak{S}_{m}
(typically the Mallows distribution
[
23
]
), considering the rankings
{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{k}
’s as independent realizations of it. Alternatively, consensus can be considered as a barycentric ranking according to the metric approach, which is very popular in practice. Equipping
\mathfrak{S}_{m}
with a (pseudo-) metric
d({\boldsymbol{\sigma}},{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}^{\prime})
, ranking medians are then defined as the (not necessarily unique) solutions of the (discrete) optimization problem

\min_{{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}\in\mathfrak{S}_{m}}\sum_{v=1}^{n}d\left({\boldsymbol{\sigma}},{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{v}\right).

(1)

Among the many possible choices (see
e.g.

[
13
]
), a very popular metric is the Kendall
\tau
distance, defined as the number of discording pairs: for any
{\boldsymbol{\sigma}},{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}^{\prime}\in\mathfrak{S}_{m}
as

d_{\tau}\left({\boldsymbol{\sigma}},{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}^{\prime}\right)=\sum_{i<j}\mathbb{I}\left\{({\sigma}(i)-{\sigma}(j))\left({\sigma}^{\prime}(i)-{\sigma}^{\prime}(j)\right)<0\right\}.

In this case, solutions of (
1
) are then referred to as
Kemeny medians

[
20
]
and, remarkably, the two approaches, probabilistic and metric, coincide insofar as Kendall
\tau
medians correspond to maximum likelihood estimators under the assumption that the
{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{v}
’s are independent realizations of a Mallows distribution, see
[
29
]
. From a social choice perspective, it satisfies the Condorcet criterion: any candidate
i\in\llbracket m\rrbracket
who would win against all others in head-to-head majority votes is guaranteed to be ranked first,
cf

[
7
]
. The Kemeny consensus further satisfies the extended Condorcet property, which provides robustness to spam inputs, refer to
[
14
]
. However, these desirable characteristics come at a computational cost, since computing a Kemeny consensus is NP-hard in general, see
e.g.

[
19
]
. Several other distances on
\mathfrak{S}_{m}
define well-known ranking aggregation methods. Spearman’s rank correlation distance is defined as
d_{2}({\boldsymbol{\sigma}},{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}^{\prime})=\sum_{i}({\sigma}(i)-{\sigma}^{\prime}(i))^{2}
, while Spearman’s footrule distance is given by
d_{1}({\boldsymbol{\sigma}},{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}^{\prime})=\sum_{i}|{\sigma}(i)-{\sigma}^{\prime}(i)|
. Minimizing the sum of Spearman’s rank correlations leads to the classical Borda method, see
[
5
]
: for each candidate
i\in\llbracket m\rrbracket
, compute the average rank across all
n
votes:
s^{B}_{i}=(1/n)\sum_{v=1}^{n}{\sigma}_{v}(i)
. The Borda consensus
{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}^{B}_{*}\in\mathfrak{S}_{m}
, ranks candidates in ascending order of their average rank scores
\bar{{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}}:=(s^{B}_{1},\ldots,s^{B}_{m})^{\top}
. When the Borda scores are distinct, this consensus is thus the unique minimizer of the sum of Spearman rank correlations
[
14
]
. Spearman’s footrule, in turn, gives rise to the median aggregation rule
[
15
]
: if the coordinate-wise median ranks across voters form a valid permutation, then this permutation minimizes the total footrule distance
[
15
]
. However, unlike the Borda method, median ranks may not define a permutation in practice. Another widely used approach is the Copeland method, which directly satisfies the Condorcet criterion
[
12
]
, described as follows. For each pair of distinct candidates
(i,j)
, let
\hat{p}_{ij}=(1/n)\sum_{v=1}^{n}\mathbb{I}\!\left\{{\sigma}_{v}(i)<{\sigma}_{v}(j)\right\}
denote the fraction of voters who prefer candidate
i
over
j
. The Copeland score for candidate
i
is then
\sum_{j\neq i}\mathbb{I}\left\{\hat{p}_{ij}\leq 1/2\right\}-\mathbb{I}\left\{\hat{p}_{ij}>1/2\right\}
counting the number of items it beats in pairwise duels minus the number of items it loses against. The Copeland consensus
{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}^{C}_{*}
ranks candidates in descending order of their Copeland scores. Under weak stochastic transitivity

\Rightarrow

. Under strong stochastic transitivity

Local Kemenization.
Beyond global optimality, local Kemeny optimality
[
14
]
offers a computationally tractable alternative that satisfies the extended Condorcet property—ensuring that Condorcet winners are ranked highly while Condorcet losers are placed near the bottom. Every Kemeny optimal solution is locally Kemeny optimal, but the converse does not hold, making local optimality an attractive relaxation that approximates the Kemeny consensus. Any initial ranking can be transformed into a locally Kemeny optimal solution via the following iterative procedure, known as local Kemenization
[
14
]
: a) initialize with an aggregation from a tractable method, such as Borda or Copeland; b) For each pair of adjacent candidates
(i,j)
in the current ranking, check whether a majority of voters prefer
j
over
i
(
i.e.
,
\hat{p}_{ij}<1/2
). If so, swap
i
and
j
; c) Repeat step b until no further swaps occur. This procedure converges in polynomial time and inherits robustness properties against
spam
or noisy inputs.

Partial Rankings.
The aggregation methods above extend naturally to partial rankings, where each voter ranks only their top
k<m
items. One approach imputes the average rank
(k+1+m)/2
to all unranked items
[
18
]
. Alternatively, one can normalize ranked items as
{\sigma}(i)_{\text{scaled}}=({\sigma}(i)-1)/k
and assign a normalized rank of 1 to unranked items
[
18
]
. Borda scores are then computed from these completed or normalized rankings in the usual way. For Copeland’s method, we use weak pairwise preferences
p_{ij}=\mathbb{I}\left\{\sigma(i)<\sigma(j)\right\}+(1/2)\mathbb{I}\left\{\sigma(i)=\sigma(j)\right\}
, and Copeland scores are computed as usual. While local Kemenization is computationally efficient in a centralized setting, its implementation in a fully decentralized network requires specific communication protocols, which we detail in Section
3.3
.

2.2
Decentralized Estimation - Gossip Algorithms

We begin by reviewing the randomized gossip framework for distributed averaging, then formalize our problem setting.

Randomized Gossip Algorithms.
Gossip-based protocols provide a natural framework for distributed computation, enabling nodes indexed by
v\in\llbracket n\rrbracket
in a network defined by a set of edges
E\subset\llbracket n\rrbracket^{2}
to iteratively converge towards global quantities through purely local interactions. In the classical randomized gossip algorithm for averaging
[
6
]
, each node
v\in\llbracket n\rrbracket
maintains a local estimate
x_{v}(t)
initialized to some value
x_{v}(0)
. At each iteration
t\geq 1
, an edge
e=(u,v)\in E
is sampled with probability
p_{e}
, and the two incident nodes replace their estimates with their pairwise average:
x_{u}(t+1)=x_{v}(t+1)=(x_{u}(t)+x_{v}(t))/2
. All other nodes retain their current values unchanged. Under mild assumptions on the graph and positivity of the edge probabilities, this process converges almost surely to the global average
\bar{x}=(1/n)\sum_{v=1}^{n}x_{v}(0)
. The rate of convergence is governed by the second-largest eigenvalue of the expected gossip matrix and depends on the graph topology and the edge sampling distribution
\mathbf{P}:=(p_{e})_{e\in E}
. We refer the reader to
[
6
,
26
]
for a comprehensive treatment of the convergence analysis.

Setup and Problem Formulation.
We consider a distributed system comprising
n\geq 2
voters (or agents), each of whom expresses a preference ranking over
m\geq 1
items/candidates indexed by
i\in\llbracket m\rrbracket
. Each voter
k\in\llbracket n\rrbracket
provides a preference ordering of the candidates which can be represented by a permutation
\boldsymbol{\pi}_{k}\in\mathfrak{S}_{m}
. The voters interact through a communication network represented by a
connected
and
non-bipartite
graph
\mathcal{G}=(V,E)
with vertex set
V=\llbracket n\rrbracket
. Communication occurs randomly between pairs of agents: at each step, an edge
e\in E
is selected with a certain probability
p_{e}>0
, and the two connected nodes exchange information. The related probability distribution on
E
is denoted by
\mathbf{P}:=(p_{e})_{e\in E}
. The system operates under an
asynchronous gossip
model, wherein each agent’s local clock follows an independent homogeneous Poisson process with rate
1
. As shown in prior work, this is equivalent to having a single global Poisson clock of rate
n
, with a random edge activated at each tick, see
[
6
]
. Specifically, at each iteration, node
u
wakes up with probability
1/n
and contacts a neighbor uniformly at random (with probability
1/d_{u}
), resulting in a probability of activation for edge
e
given by

p_{e}=\frac{1}{n}\left(\frac{1}{d_{u}}+\frac{1}{d_{v}}\right),\text{ with }e=(u,v)\in E,

where
d_{u}
and
d_{v}
denote the degrees of nodes
u
and
v
, respectively. Our objective is to design a gossip-based algorithm that accurately estimates the several ranking consensus using only local pairwise communication, without access to a central coordinator.

Algorithms and Convergence Analysis

We define the dataset
S_{n}=\{\boldsymbol{\pi}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{\pi}_{n}\}
as the collection of observed rankings submitted by
n
voters. For each pair of distinct candidates
(i,j)
, we define the pairwise scores
\hat{p}_{ij}=(1/n)\sum_{v=1}^{n}\mathbb{I}\!\left\{\pi_{v}(i)<\pi_{v}(j)\right\}
, representing the fraction of voters who prefer candidate
i
to candidate
j
. In what follows, we develop decentralized methods for computing both Borda, Copeland, and Footrule ranking aggregations, as well as, Local Kemenization.

3.1
Decentralized Borda Consensus

In this section, we present our algorithm for decentralized Borda consensus. Recall from Section 2 that for any item
i\in\llbracket m\rrbracket
, the Borda score is defined as
s^{B}_{i}=(1/n)\sum_{v=1}^{n}\pi_{v}(i)
, and the Borda consensus
\boldsymbol{\pi}^{B}_{*}\in\mathfrak{S}_{m}
is the ranking induced by sorting items according to these scores. For simplicity, we assume that all Borda scores are distinct,
i.e.
,
s_{i}^{B}\neq s_{j}^{B}
for
i\neq j
, which ensures a unique solution. Our algorithm proceeds in two stages: (1) gossip-based averaging of the local rankings to compute the Borda scores, and (2) local sorting to recover the Borda consensus. These stages are decoupled—sorting can be performed whenever scores are updated, or deferred until the final iteration
T
to reduce computational overhead. The complete procedure is formalized in Algorithm
1
.

Each agent
v\in\llbracket n\rrbracket
initializes its score vector to its initial ranking, setting
\mathbf{x}_{v}{(0)}=\boldsymbol{\pi}_{v}

.

Let
I

and summing over all voters yields

\displaystyle\sum_{v=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\bigl[d_{\tau}(\boldsymbol{\pi}_{*}^{B},\hat{\boldsymbol{\pi}}_{v}(t))\bigr]

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i<j}\frac{1}{|s_{i}{-}s_{j}|^{2}}\sum_{v=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\left[2\varepsilon_{v,i}(t)^{2}{+}2\varepsilon_{v,j}(t)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq 2\sum_{i<j}\frac{\mathbb{E}\|\mathbf{S}_{i}{(t)}{-}s_{i}\mathbf{1}_{n}\|^{2}{+}\mathbb{E}\|\mathbf{S}_{j}{(t)}{-}s_{j}\mathbf{1}_{n}\|^{2}}{|s_{i}-s_{j}|^{2}}.

Since
|s_{i}-s_{j}|\geq\Delta^{B}
and applying Eq. (
2
) to the final inequality finishes the proof of the first bound:
C_{1}(1-c/2)^{t}\leq C_{1}e^{-ct/2}
.

The bound with rate
c_{2}
follows from Markov’s inequality applied to
\mathbb{P}\bigl\{\varepsilon_{v,i}(t)+\varepsilon_{v,j}(t)\geq|s_{i}-s_{j}|\bigr\}
:

\displaystyle\sum_{v=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\bigl[d_{\tau}(\boldsymbol{\pi}_{*}^{B},\hat{\boldsymbol{\pi}}_{v}(t))\bigr]

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i<j}\frac{1}{|s_{i}-s_{j}|}\sum_{v=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\left[\varepsilon_{v,i}(t)+\varepsilon_{v,j}(t)\right]

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i<j}\frac{\mathbb{E}\|\mathbf{S}_{i}{(t)}{-}s_{i}\mathbf{1}_{n}\|_{1}{+}\mathbb{E}\|\mathbf{S}_{j}{(t)}{-}s_{j}\mathbf{1}_{n}\|_{1}}{|s_{i}-s_{j}|},

where
\|\cdot\|_{1}
denotes the usual
\ell_{1}
-norm. Since
|s_{i}-s_{j}|\geq\Delta^{B}
for all
i\neq j
and
\|\cdot\|_{1}\leq\sqrt{n}\|\cdot\|
, we get

\displaystyle\sum_{v=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\bigl[d_{\tau}(\boldsymbol{\pi}_{*}^{B},\hat{\boldsymbol{\pi}}_{v}(t))\bigr]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\sqrt{n}(m-1)}{\Delta^{B}}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mathbb{E}\|\mathbf{S}_{i}(t)-s_{i}\mathbf{1}_{n}\|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\sqrt{nm}(m-1)}{\Delta^{B}}\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mathbb{E}\left\|\mathbf{S}_{i}(t)-s_{i}\mathbf{1}_{n}\right\|^{2}},

where the last inequality follows from Jensen’s inequality (concavity of
\sqrt{\cdot}
) and Cauchy-Schwarz inequality. Applying Eq. (
2
) to the final inequality completes the proof of the second bound.

\square

Setup.
We generate synthetic ranking data according to the Mallows model
[

]

and a dispersion parameter

\mathbb{P}(\pi)=\exp(-\phi\,{d_{\tau}(\pi,\pi_{0})})/Z(\phi)
to each permutation

Z(\phi)
is the normalizing constant. In the limiting case
\phi=0
, the model assigns all probability mass to the central permutation
\pi_{0}
, while
\phi=1
reduces to the uniform distribution over
\mathfrak{S}_{m}
. We set the number of agents to
n=151
, the number of items to
m=8
, and the dispersion parameter to
\phi=0.5
, with a central permutation
\pi_{0}
drawn uniformly at random from
\mathfrak{S}_{m}
and fixed across all trials. In each trial, the rankings are sampled independently from the Mallows distribution with parameters
(\pi_{0},\phi)
. Communication graphs are generated using three standard random graph models: complete graphs, Watts–Strogatz graphs, and random geometric graphs. For geometric graphs we set the connectivity radius
r=\sqrt{(\log(n)+16)/(n\pi)}
, and for Watts–Strogatz graphs we set
k=\max(\lfloor n/500\rfloor,5)
. All generated graphs are verified to have a connectivity constant

N=100
independent trials, with each trial running for
2,000

5
Conclusion and Perspectives

We have introduced a family of gossip-based algorithms for decentralized ranking aggregation, addressing a fundamental challenge in distributed systems where agents must collectively aggregate preferences without centralized coordination. Our theoretical analysis establishes exponential convergence rates of
\mathcal{O}(e^{-ct/2})
for both Borda and Copeland consensus, providing strong guarantees for practical deployment. Moreover, we extended this framework to accommodate Footrule consensus, and further provided a fully decentralized implementation of Local Kemenization. Our extensive empirical evaluation across diverse network topologies and ranking datasets validates the theoretical findings and reveal the fast convergence of our methods.

The robustness analysis further underscores that while Borda consensus is sensitive to outliers, Copeland and Kemeny-based methods offer superior resilience to data contamination. Notably, the decentralized Local Kemenization procedure proves to be a lightweight yet efficient approach for refining initial estimates into Condorcet-consistent rankings, even in the presence of adversarial noise.

Appendix A
Convergence of Gossip-Based Averaging

For completeness, we recall standard properties of gossip matrices and provide the classical convergence proof for gossip-based averaging (as stated in Lemma
1
).

Lemma 3
.

Assume the graph
\mathcal{G}=(V,E)
is connected and non-bipartite. Let
t>0
. If at iteration
t
, edge
e=(i,j)
is selected with probability
p_{e}>0
, then the transition matrix is given by
\mathbf{W}_{2}(t)=\mathbf{I}_{n}-\mathbf{L}_{e}/2
where the elementary Laplacian is defined as
\mathbf{L}_{e}=\left(\mathbf{e}_{i}-\mathbf{e}_{j}\right)\left(\mathbf{e}_{i}-\mathbf{e}_{j}\right)^{\top}
.
The following properties hold:

(a)

The matrices are symmetric and doubly stochastic, meaning that
\mathbf{W}_{2}(t)\mathbf{1}_{n}=\mathbf{1}_{n}
and
\mathbf{1}_{n}^{\top}\mathbf{W}_{2}(t)=\mathbf{1}_{n}^{\top}
.
Moreover, we have
\mathbf{W}_{2}(t)^{2}=\mathbf{W}_{2}(t)
.

(b)

Since
\sum_{e\in E}p_{e}=1
,
\mathbf{W}_{2}=\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{W}_{2}(t)]=\mathbf{I}_{n}-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{e\in E}p_{e}\mathbf{L}_{e}.

The matrix
\mathbf{W}_{2}
is also doubly stochastic and it follows that
\mathbf{1}_{n}
is an eigenvector with eigenvalue 1.

(c)

The matrix
\tilde{\mathbf{W}}_{2}\triangleq\mathbf{W}_{2}-{\mathbf{1}_{n}\mathbf{1}_{n}^{\top}}/n
satisfies, by construction,
\tilde{\mathbf{W}}\mathbf{1}_{n}=0
, and it can be shown that
\|\tilde{\mathbf{W}}_{\alpha}\|_{\operatorname{op}}\leq\lambda_{2}(2)
, where
\lambda_{2}(2)
is the second largest eigenvalue of
\mathbf{W}_{\alpha}
