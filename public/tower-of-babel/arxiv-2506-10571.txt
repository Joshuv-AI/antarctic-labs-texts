Unitary Scrambling and Collapse: A Quantum Diffusion Framework for Generative Modeling
Yihua Li, Jiayi Chen, Tamanna S. Kumavat, Kyriakos Flouris

Abstract
Quantum computing, with its promise of exponential speedups, is rapidly emerging as a powerful paradigm for advancing artificial intelligence. We propose QSC-Diffusion, the first fully quantum diffusion-based framework for image generation. Our method integrates classical Gaussian noise with quantum scrambling in the forward process, and employs parameterized quantum circuits with measurement-induced collapse for reverse denoising—enabling end-to-end sampling without reliance on classical neural architectures or preprocessing modules. To address optimization challenges in deep quantum models, we introduce a hybrid loss that balances fidelity and diversity, coupled with a divide-and-conquer training strategy to mitigate barren plateaus. Remarkably, QSC-Diffusion achieves competitive FID scores across multiple datasets while using orders of magnitude fewer parameters, outperforming even some quantum-classical hybrid baselines in efficiency. These results highlight the potential of quantum-native generative modeling and mark a foundational step toward scalable quantum machine learning. Our implementation is available at https://github.com/Li-Yihua/QSC-Diffusion

Introduction

Generative modeling has become a core pillar of machine learning, with wide-ranging impact across research and industry
ray2023chatgpt
;
daras2022discovering
;
isola2017image
;
paganini2018calogan
. It has evolved through several key paradigms, including VAEs
kingma2013auto
, EBMs
du2019implicit
, and GANs
ho2020denoising
, each with distinct trade-offs between expressiveness and tractability. Recently, diffusion models have gained prominence for their stable training and strong performance, becoming the state-of-the-art in many generative tasks.

Despite their success, conventional generative models remain face significant challenges, including high computational demands and the necessity for extensive parameter tuning
dhariwal2021diffusion
;
ho2020denoising
;
rombach2022high
;
flouris_canonical_2023
. Recent advances in quantum computing offer promising solutions to these challenges by representing neural networks as systems of interacting quantum spins, corresponding to a tunable Ising model
biamonte2017quantum
;
schuld2014quest
. These architectures leverage quantum coherence to reduce the sampling complexity of gradient estimation and enable more scalable training through quantum data access mechanisms such as quantum random access memory (qRAM) or oracle subroutines
giovannetti2008quantum
;
wiebe2014quantum
. This yields potential quadratic speedups over classical methods, Paving the way for more efficient training of large-scale generative models
rebentrost2014quantum
.

Beyond computational benefits, quantum models can learn to generate quantum states, allowing the network to act as a form of quantum associative memory
schuld2014quantum
. This capability reflects their enhanced expressive power: an
N
qubit system is described by a
2^{N}
dimensional state vector, enabling exponential encoding capacity that far exceeds the limitations of classical bits
nielsen2002quantum
. Moreover, by incorporating tunable transverse interactions, Ising-based quantum systems become universal for quantum computation
farhi2001quantum
;
lucas2014ising
. This allows them to implement transformations that can express rich high-order correlations, potentially capturing data structures that remain inaccessible to classical neural networks without deeper layers or significantly more trainable parameters. Therefore, these quantum models not only reduce architectural complexity but also enhance pattern recognition in regimes where classical models struggle
schuld2015introduction
.

In this paper, we introduce the Quantum Scrambling and Collapse Diffusion Model (QSC-Diffusion), implemented entirely within a quantum computing paradigm. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first diffusion-based image generation framework to achieve faithful synthesis without relying on any classical architectural components (e.g., convolutional backbones) or heuristic augmentations (e.g., patching or hybrid encoders). Our approach retains the generative strengths of classical DDPMs while leveraging the computational benefits of quantum systems. The framework consists of two core components: (1) a quantum forward scrambling process, which gradually disperses structured information via Gaussian noise combined with unitary scrambling; and (2) a quantum reverse denoising process, implemented via parameterized quantum circuits (PQCs)
abbas2021power
that leverage quantum interference and entanglement to achieve strong representational capacity, recovers structured image distributions from noisy, delocalized ones via measurement-induced collapse
benedetti2019parameterized
.

To enable efficient training, we adopt a divide-and-conquer strategy that interpolates between the data and noise distributions via intermediate quantum states
meng2022minimal
, allowing the reverse process to be decomposed into low-depth subproblems and mitigating barren plateaus
mcclean2018barren
.

Finally, our contributions are threefold:
(1) we propose QSC-Diffusion, the first fully quantum image generative model that integrates unitary scrambling and measurement-induced collapse, achieving diffusion-based generation without any classical components; (2) we formulate a forward quantum scrambling process that combines Gaussian noise with quantum scrambling, effectively dispersing structured information across the quantum state space; and (3) we adopt a hybrid loss function that integrates L1 reconstruction loss and KL divergence, encouraging pixel-level fidelity and latent space regularization to enhance both generation quality and diversity.

We evaluate QSC-Diffusion against hybrid quantum GANs (QGANs) and hybrid quantum diffusion models (QDDMs) on MNIST and Fashion-MNIST, using the Fréchet Inception Distance (FID) as the evaluation metric. QSC-Diffusion achieves superior or comparable performance while using magnitude fewer parameters. These results underscore the viability of fully quantum generative models as a scalable and promising foundation for future developments in quantum machine learning.

2
Related work

Multiple prior works have explored combining classical DDPMs with quantum algorithms. For instance, the work in
de2024towards
introduced a hybrid quantum-classical diffusion model, which employs classical forward noising and a quantum denoising U-Net enhanced with ResNet
he2016deep
and Attention blocks. While effective on MNIST, the reliance on strong classical components (e.g., auto-encoders
flouris2025explicitdataefficientencodinggradient
, ResNet, Attention) obscures the individual contribution of quantum computation. In contrast, our work proposes a fully quantum diffusion framework for image generation that synthesizes high-quality images with strong parameter efficiency, requiring significantly fewer trainable parameters.

The work in
zhang2024generative
proposed a Quantum Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Model (QuDDPM), which employs two quantum circuits to generate samples from an unknown quantum state distribution. In the forward process, a sequence of random unitary gates is applied independently to each timestep, effectively scrambling the input into noise
anand2022quantumdiffusion
. The backward denoising is performed via a PQC, which reconstructs the data from noise. QuDDPM outperformed both quantum GANs and quantum direct transport models in prior evaluations. While this work demonstrates the feasibility of quantum-based noising, its application has thus far been limited to quantum state generation. When applied to image synthesis tasks, QuDDPM applies unitary transformations independently at each time step, causing rapid dispersion of image information and leading to model collapse
mcclean2018barren
;
cerezo2021cost
. To address this, we propose a quantum scrambling forward process that mitigates information loss and yields strong empirical results, further validating the use of quantum noising for image generation. Moreover, we further extend this line of research by systematically evaluating multiple quantum noising strategies and demonstrating improved stability and generative performance in image synthesis.

Beyond QuDDPM, a broader set of quantum-native generative models has been proposed, including Quantum Born Machines
benedetti2019generative
, Quantum Boltzmann Machines
amin2018quantum
, Quantum GANs
rehm2023full
;
chakrabarti2019quantum
;
mossi_matrix_2025
, and Quantum Diffusion Models
romero2021variational
;
kade2023quantum
;
zhang2024generative
. However, most existing models are constrained to low-dimensional or domain-specific data (e.g. quantum states, particle physics) and depend on classical preprocessing techniques such as patch embedding, PCA, or autoencoders. These classical components hinder fully quantum, end-to-end generation. In contrast, QSC-Diffusion introduces the first fully quantum diffusion-based image generation framework that eliminates all classical machine learning architectural dependencies.

3
Methodology

We propose QSC-Diffusion, a fully quantum generative model composed of two core components. First, a sequence of quantum scrambling operators disperses structured information across the quantum state space by combining Gaussian noise with unitary transformations. Second, PQCs perform the reverse denoising process, inferring structured distributions from noisy, delocalized quantum states via measurement-induced collapse.

The remainder of this section presents the forward quantum scrambling process (Section
3.1
), the reverse quantum denoising procedure for image synthesis (Section
3.2
), and the overall training strategy (Section
3.3
).

3.1
Forward quantum scrambling process

Given an image
\mathbf{x}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
, we define a quantum scrambling operator
\mathcal{D}
such that
\mathbf{x}_{t}=\mathcal{D}(\mathbf{x}_{0},t)
, which approximates the marginal posterior distribution
q(\mathbf{x}_{1:T}\mid\mathbf{x}_{0})
. Here,
\mathbf{x}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{x}_{T}
are latent variables of the same dimensionality as the input
\mathbf{x}_{0}\sim q(\mathbf{x}_{0})
.

As shown in Figure
1
,
\mathcal{D}
consists of two components: (1) classical Gaussian noise
\epsilon\in[0,1]
, and (2) a sequence of fixed, approximately Haar-random unitaries
\{\mathcal{U}_{t,\theta_{t}}\}_{t=1}^{T}
, where each
\theta_{t}
is sampled once and held fixed. These operations gradually disperse localized probability distribution, eroding spatial structure over the diffusion trajectory.

A visual comparison of different forward strategies is provided in Figure
2
a. To mitigate entropy homogenization—where information becomes uniformly delocalized and undermines reversibility—we inject Gaussian noise before scrambling to preserve entropy (Details provided in Appendix
D
).

At each step
t
, Gaussian noise is injected into
\mathbf{x}_{t}
following a variance schedule
\{\beta_{t}\}_{t=1}^{T}
, resulting in a noised latent
\mathbf{z}_{t}
:

\mathbf{z}_{t}=\sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_{t}}\,\mathbf{x}_{t}+\sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_{t}}\,\boldsymbol{\epsilon},\quad\boldsymbol{\epsilon}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\mathbf{I}),

(1)

where
\alpha_{t}:=1-\lambda_{s}\beta_{t}
and
\bar{\alpha}_{t}:=\prod_{s=1}^{t}\alpha_{s}
. The scaling factor
\lambda_{s}\in[0,1]
modulates classical noise: moderate levels mitigate entropy collapse, while excessive noise degrades PQC denoising performance.

The resulting noised latent
\mathbf{z}_{t}
is then amplitude-encoded into an
n
-qubit quantum state:

\left|\psi^{(t)}_{\text{embed}}\right\rangle=\sum_{j=0}^{2^{n}-1}z_{t}^{(j)}\left|j\right\rangle
. This embedded state evolves under a scrambling unitary
\mathcal{U}_{t,\theta_{t}}
, where the perturbation strength is modulated via the noise schedule:
\theta_{t}=\sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_{t}}\cdot\varepsilon_{t}
to avoid abrupt information loss:

\left|\psi^{(t+1)}\right\rangle=\mathcal{U}_{t,\theta_{t}}\left|\psi^{(t)}_{\text{embed}}\right\rangle

(2)

To extract classical information, we perform a partial measurement in the computational basis, yielding a probability distribution
\mathbf{p}_{t+1}
. Compared to expectation-based readouts, this approach provides richer gradients for optimization. The output is then normalized to produce the next-step latent:

\mathbf{x}_{t+1}=\frac{\mathbf{p}_{t+1}}{\max_{j}\mathbf{p}_{t+1}(j)}

(3)

3.2
Reverse quantum denoising process

The reverse process begins with the latent variable
\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{T}
, initialized as
\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{T}=\mathbf{x}_{T}
, and progressively reduces noise through iterative measurement steps, eventually producing
\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{0}
that approximates the original data distribution.

At each denoising step
t
, we apply a
n
-qubit PQC
\tilde{\mathcal{U}}_{t,\theta}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{t})
, augmented with
n_{A}
ancillary qubits initialized in the state
\left|0\right\rangle^{\otimes n_{A}}
. Each circuit consists of an amplitude embedding layer followed by a stack of trainable unitary blocks. Mathematically, the unitary denoising
\tilde{\mathcal{U}}_{t,\theta}(\mathbf{\tilde{x}_{t}})
can be written as an L-repetition of learning layers
\tilde{\mathcal{U}}^{\ell_{t}}_{t,\theta}(\mathbf{\tilde{x}_{t}})
:

\tilde{\mathcal{U}}_{t,\theta}^{\ell_{t}}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{t})=\tilde{U}_{t,\theta_{L}}^{L}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{t})\cdots\tilde{U}_{t,\theta_{1}}^{1}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{t})

(4)

where
\ell_{t}=\ell_{0}+t
determines the depth of the circuit. Since diffusion models accumulate error over time, adversely affecting sample quality and diversity
nichol2021improved
;
watson2022learning
. we progressively increase the depth of the PQC to allow later denoising blocks to better correct residual noise and improve the fidelity of the generation
chung2022come
.

We evaluated three circuit variants for a single denoising block,
\tilde{\mathcal{U}}_{t,\theta}^{\ell}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{t})
, as detailed in the Appendix
B
. Given a latent input
\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{t}
, the combined unitary evolution and measurement produce the updated latent
\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{t-1}
:

\tilde{\mathbf{p}}_{t-1}(j)=\left\langle\tilde{\psi}^{(t-1)}_{\theta}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{t})\middle|\left(\left|j\right\rangle\left\langle j\right|\otimes\left|0\right\rangle\left\langle 0\right|^{\otimes n_{A}}\right)\middle|\tilde{\psi}^{(t-1)}_{\theta}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{t})\right\rangle,\quad\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{t-1}=\frac{\tilde{\mathbf{p}}_{t-1}}{\max_{j^{\prime}}\tilde{\mathbf{p}}_{t-1}(j^{\prime})}

(5)

where
j\in\{0,\dots,2^{n+n_{A}}-1\}
indexes the computational basis of the joint Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
with
\dim(\mathcal{H})=2^{n+n_{A}}
. The quantum state
\left|\tilde{\psi}^{(t-1)}_{\theta}\right\rangle
thus resides in a system with both latent and ancillary degrees of freedom.

During training, all circuits
\tilde{\mathcal{U}}_{t,\theta}
are optimized to map scrambled latent distributions back to clean samples. Once trained, the unitaries remain fixed, and the reverse process becomes a deterministic sampling procedure informed by the learned structure of the data.

At each iteration, the forward process is executed up to step

to generate the noisy latent
\mathbf{x}_{k}
, while the reverse process is performed from step
T
down to
k+1
to reconstruct
\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{k}
. The parameters of the denoising circuit
\tilde{\mathcal{U}}_{k+1,\theta}
are updated by minimizing the discrepancy between
\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{k}
and
\mathbf{x}_{k}
. This reverse-time training strategy enables each PQC block to learn to invert the forward scrambling process at its corresponding timestep.

3.3
Training strategy

In classical DDPMs, the Gaussian nature of the diffusion process enables efficient training by maximizing an evidence lower bound (ELBO) on the log-likelihood, which can be computed analytically
sohl2015deep
;
ho2020denoising
;
suppl_prl2024
. In contrast, such analytical simplification is not expected to hold in QuDDPM, as classical simulation of quantum systems is inherently inefficient. Instead, the training of the QuDDPM relies on the capability of quantum measurements to extract information about the ensemble of quantum states for the efficient evaluation of a loss function.

The training of our
T
-step SQC-diffusion model proceeds in reverse order, starting from the final denoising block
\tilde{\mathcal{U}}_{T,\theta}
and iteratively updating earlier blocks down to
\tilde{\mathcal{U}}_{1,\theta}
. As illustrated in Figure
3
, at training step
(T+1-k)
, the forward process is applied up to
\mathcal{D}_{k}
to produce the noisy latent
\mathbf{x}_{k}
. Simultaneously, the reverse process is executed from
\tilde{\mathcal{U}}_{T,\theta}
down to
\tilde{\mathcal{U}}_{k+1,\theta}
to generate the intermediate latent
\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{k}
.

The parameters of
\tilde{\mathcal{U}}_{k+1,\theta}
are then updated to minimize the discrepancy between
\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{k}
and
\mathbf{x}_{k}
, effectively encouraging the denoising circuit to invert the quantum noising process. This iterative, time-reversed training strategy ensures that each denoising block learns to reconstruct the latent at its corresponding timestep, enabling accurate sampling during inference.

To promote stable convergence during training, we adopt a hybrid loss function that combines KL divergence and L1 distance. At denoising step
k
, the loss is computed between the generated latent
\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{k}
and the forward-scrambled latent
\mathbf{x}_{k}
, and is defined as a function of the unitary operations
\{\tilde{\mathcal{U}}_{c,\theta}\}_{k+1}^{T}
and the forward operators
\{\mathcal{D}_{c}\}_{1}^{k-1}
:

\mathcal{L}\left(\{\tilde{\mathcal{U}}_{c,\theta}\}_{k+1}^{T},\{\mathcal{D}_{c}\}_{1}^{k-1},\mathbf{x}_{0},\mathbf{x}_{T}\right)={D}\left(\mathbf{x}_{k}\left[\{\mathcal{D}_{c}\}_{1}^{k-1},\mathbf{x}_{0}\right],\,\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{k}\left[\{\tilde{\mathcal{U}}_{c,\theta}\}_{k+1}^{T},\mathbf{x}_{T}\right]\right),

(6)

where the distance function is defined as:

{D}(\mathbf{x}_{k},\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{k})=\lambda_{\mathrm{KL}}\,D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\mathbf{x}_{k}\,||\,\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{k})+\lambda_{\ell_{1}}\,\left\|\mathbf{x}_{k}-\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{k}\right\|_{1}.

(7)

with
\lambda_{\mathrm{KL}}
and
\lambda_{\ell_{1}}
controlling the trade-off between distributional alignment and reconstruction accuracy.

In preliminary ablation studies, we observed that omitting the KL divergence term produced visually high-quality outputs but led to structural collapse across samples, significantly reducing diversity (Figure

These results highlight a fundamental trade-off between fidelity and diversity: the L1 term promotes pixel-level accuracy and structural realism, while the KL divergence term plays a critical role in preserving distributional richness by regularizing the approximate posterior toward the forward diffusion prior. When combined, they help the model retain both sample sharpness and distributional diversity—essential for stable and expressive quantum generation.

4.1
Comparison to the Baseline

We benchmark QSC-Diffusion against two hybrid quantum baselines. QDDPM (QVUNet)
de2024towards
integrates PQCs into a U-Net architecture with ResNet and Attention modules, while QGAN (PQWGAN)
tsang2023hybrid
employs a patch-based quantum generator with a classical discriminator.

To assess generative quality at a fine-grained level, we report class-wise FID scores on MNIST and Fashion-MNIST (Tables
1
and
2
). By inheriting the theoretical foundations of diffusion models, QSC-Diffusion retains the generative strengths of classical DDPMs. As a result, it exhibits more stable training and better generalization compared to PQWGAN, which suffers from common GAN pathologies such as mode collapse. This is reflected in the consistent FID scores of QSC-Diffusion across different datasets and categories.

While QVUNet achieves the lowest FID scores overall, largely due to its high-capacity ResNet and attention-based architecture, QSC-Diffusion remains competitive across all classes despite using nearly 80× fewer parameters. This highlights a favorable trade-off between compactness and generative quality.

Importantly, QSC-Diffusion achieves competitive results without relying on any classical deep learning components (e.g., ResNet, attention blocks), operating entirely with quantum computational primitives. This highlights not only its strong parameter efficiency but also the practical feasibility of fully quantum generative modeling. These findings underscore the broader potential of quantum machine learning as a foundation for next-generation generative frameworks.

4.2
Ablation on Forward Scrambling Strategies

This ablation study compares QSC-Diffusion against three forward diffusion variants: a classical Gaussian process (CDP), Independent Unitary Scramiling Process (IUSP), and Gradual Unitary Scramiling Process (GUSP). Figure
2
a visualizes how image structure evolves over timesteps during the forward process for each strategy. For fair comparison, all methods adopt the same cosine-based noise schedule with fixed
\lambda_{s}=0.1
.

While a greater diffusion depth
T
improves theoretical alignment with continuous-time dynamics
sohl2015deep
, its empirical effects depend on the choice of perturbation strategy. As shown in Figure
5
, IUSP shows flat performance regardless of
T
, indicating that uncorrelated, stepwise scrambling fails to exploit additional diffusion steps. GUSP initially benefits from increased
T
, but performance degrades beyond
T=8
, likely due to entropy homogenization, where excessive perturbation disperses information beyond recoverability.

By contrast, QSC-Diffusion effectively combines classical noise injection with structured quantum scrambling, enabling consistent gains with increasing
T
and stable performance beyond
T=8
.

These results highlight the role of controlled quantum perturbation in maintaining expressivity without compromising stability. Notably, compared to traditional Gaussian diffusion, QSC-Diffusion achieves reasonably high-fidelity generation using significantly fewer diffusion steps. This reduces computational overhead and mitigates the accumulation of measurement noise, a key limitation in quantum settings. Such efficiency is particularly promising for near-term deployment on quantum hardware and points to the broader potential of resource-aware quantum generative modeling.

4.3
Robustness to statistical noise

Up to this point, SQC-Diffusion has been trained and evaluated under the idealized assumption of infinite measurement shots. However, in real-world quantum hardware, finite-shot sampling introduces statistical noise that may affect output quality. In this section, we empirically evaluate the model’s robustness under such practical constraints.

Let
\widetilde{\mathcal{I}}_{\infty}
denote the image generated analytically (i.e., with infinite shots). To assess robustness, we fix the pre-trained SQC-Diffusion parameters and reconstruct images
\widetilde{\mathcal{I}}_{\text{shots}}
using sampled noise
\epsilon\in[0,1]
under varying shot counts
N_{\text{shots}}=2^{k}
, for
k=5,\dots,14
. Figure
5
shows generated samples from MNIST and Fashion-MNIST across different shot counts. Visually, the images become nearly indistinguishable from the infinite-shot baseline once
N_{\text{shots}}\geq 1024
.

As shown in Figure
6
, the mean pixel-wise error decreases exponentially with shot count, while the FID score stabilizes at
N_{\text{shots}}\geq 1024
, closely matching the infinite-shot baseline. This suggests that numerical deviations from finite-shot sampling are largely imperceptible to perceptual metrics.

These findings confirm that SQC-diffusion remains effective even under low-shot conditions. The model’s robustness to statistical noise not only enhances its practicality for near-term quantum hardware, but also supports the use of finite sampling budgets without compromising image fidelity.

5
Discussion and limitations

Our results demonstrate the viability of SQC-Diffusion as a fully quantum, diffusion-based framework for image generation. It demonstrates strong parameter efficiency, achieving competitive performance with significantly fewer parameters than hybrid baselines, and maintains consistent behavior across different noise schedules, perturbation strategies, and finite-shot settings.These properties highlight its potential for deployment on near-term quantum hardware.

Nevertheless, several limitations remain. First, all experiments are conducted on quantum simulators, actual deployment on NISQ hardware may introduce additional noise sources from decoherence and gate infidelity. Nevertheless, this also highlights a key advantage: native parallelism can significantly accelerate sampling and evolution, making SQC-Diffusion well-suited for real-device execution. Second, although QSC-Diffusion exhibits strong parameter efficiency, its scalability to higher-resolution data (e.g.,
32\times 32
images or beyond) may be constrained by the available qubit count and circuit depth. Third, while we employ a progressive training strategy to mitigate optimization challenges, issues such as barren plateaus and the expressivity–trainability trade-off persist for deeper circuits. Lastly, the model exhibits reduced performance when trained on more diverse image classes simultaneously (e.g., all digits 0–9), indicating limitations in modeling complex multimodal distributions under constrained quantum resources.

Future work will explore architectural extensions such as disentangled latent spaces, hybrid classical–quantum encoders, and task-specific adaptations (e.g., conditional generation, image-to-image translation). Additionally, experimental evaluation on real quantum hardware will be critical for validating practical impact of SQC-Diffusion.

6
Conclusion

We introduced QSC-Diffusion, the first diffusion-based image generation framework that combines classical Gaussian noise with quantum scrambling in the forward process and uses parameterized quantum circuits for denoising via measurement-induced collapse. By temporally structuring quantum perturbations, the model maintains expressivity while ensuring training stability. Moreover, QSC-Diffusion achieves competitive FID scores across multiple datasets while significantly outperforming quantum-classical hybrid baselines in parameter efficiency. Ablation studies further validate its robustness to different noise schedules, scrambling strategies, and finite-shot sampling. QSC-Diffusion provides a compact yet capable approach to image generation, demonstrating robustness and efficiency with limited latent parameters. We regard this work as a foundational milestone—demonstrating not only the technical feasibility but also the long-term potential of quantum-native generative modeling, particularly as quantum hardware continues to evolve as a catalyst for future advances in artificial intelligence.

Appendix B
Parameterized Quantum Circuit (PQC)

We evaluated three parameterized quantum circuit (PQC) architectures to implement the unitary maps
\mathcal{U}^{\ell_{t}}_{t,\theta}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{t})
used in the reverse denoising process (see Section 3.2). Figure
7
illustrates the architectures of all PQCs tested during this phase.

At each reverse diffusion step
t
, the latent vector
\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{t}
is encoded into a quantum state via amplitude embedding. This embedded state is then processed through a sequence of denoising blocks, where each block corresponds to a unitary
\mathcal{U}^{\ell_{t}}_{t,\theta}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{t})
with learnable parameters
\theta
. The PQC architecture dynamically adapts to the diffusion timestep
t
by adjusting both its circuit depth
\ell_{t}
and entanglement structure to effectively model the time-dependent denoising transformation.

Each denoising block typically consists of multiple layers, including:

•

Parameterized single-qubit rotations such as
R_{y}(\cdot)
,
R_{z}(\cdot)
, or the general rotation gate
Rot(\theta,\phi,\lambda)
,

•

Entangling gates like CNOT, CZ, or SU(4)-type operations,

•

Optional input re-uploading, where the latent vector
\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{t}
is re-encoded across layers to enhance the circuit’s expressivity.

After quantum state evolution through the denoising blocks, classical samples are obtained by measurement of the final quantum state.

Appendix D
Forward Scrambling Strategy Details

C.1 Classical Forward Diffusion Process

In classical denoising diffusion probabilistic models (DDPM)
[
7
]
, the forward process—referred to as the Classical Forward Diffusion Process (CDP)—gradually corrupts data
\mathbf{x}_{0}
by adding Gaussian noise over
T
discrete time steps. This process defines a Markov chain:

q(\mathbf{x}_{t}\mid\mathbf{x}_{t-1})=\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x}_{t};\sqrt{1-\beta_{t}}\,\mathbf{x}_{t-1},\beta_{t}\mathbf{I}),\quad t=1,\dots,T,

where
\beta_{t}\in(0,1)
denotes a fixed variance schedule. The full forward process marginal at any time
t
is given by:

q(\mathbf{x}_{t}\mid\mathbf{x}_{0})=\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x}_{t};\sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_{t}}\,\mathbf{x}_{0},(1-\bar{\alpha}_{t})\mathbf{I}),

with
\alpha_{t}=1-\beta_{t}
, and
\bar{\alpha}_{t}=\prod_{s=1}^{t}\alpha_{s}
.

This process ensures that as
t\to T
, the sample
\mathbf{x}_{T}
approaches an isotropic Gaussian distribution, effectively destroying all structure in the original data. This corrupted sample
\mathbf{x}_{T}
is then used in the reverse process to reconstruct or generate samples resembling the original data distribution.

C.2 Independent Unitary Quantum Scrambling Process

The Independent Unitary Quantum Scrambling Process(IUCP) enable the forward diffusion of latent states toward noise, which applies independent quantum scrambling circuits to approximate randomization at each step.

Given a sample image
\mathbf{x}_{0}
, its latent representation is amplitude-encoded into an
n
-qubit quantum state:

\left|\psi^{(0)}_{\text{embed}}\right\rangle=\sum_{j=0}^{2^{n}-1}x_{0}^{(j)}\left|j\right\rangle.

For each quantum state
\left|\psi^{(0)}_{\text{embed}}\right\rangle
, the forward scrambling process samples a series of
T
random unitary gates
\{\mathcal{U}_{t,\theta_{t}}\}_{t=1}^{T}
. These gates evolve the state over
k
steps as follows:

\left|\psi^{(k)}\right\rangle=\prod_{\ell=1}^{k}\mathcal{U}_{\ell,\theta_{\ell}}\left|\psi^{(0)}_{embed}\right\rangle,\quad k\in[0,T]

(8)

where each rotation angle
\theta_{\ell}
is independently sampled from a uniform distribution,
\theta_{\ell}\sim U(0,1)
. As
k
increases from
0
to
T
, the evolved quantum states form an increasingly randomized ensemble of pure states, effectively scrambling the original input.

C.3  Gradual Unitary Quantum Scrambling Process

The Gradual Unitary Quantum Scrambling Process (GUCP) modifies IUCP by progressively increasing the strength of perturbations over time, rather than applying fully randomized unitaries at each step. This design aims to mitigate excessive noise in early steps, which can degrade structural information and impair the learnability of the reverse process.

Following the same state initialization and gate structure as in IUCP, GUCP modulates each unitary gate
\mathcal{U}_{t,\theta_{t}}
with a time-dependent rotation angle
\theta_{t}
governed by a classical noise schedule. Specifically, we use:

\theta_{t}=\sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_{t}}\cdot\varepsilon_{t},\quad\varepsilon_{t}\sim{U}(0,1),

where
\bar{\alpha}_{t}=\prod_{s=1}^{t}\alpha_{s}
follows the DDPM cosine schedule. The factor
\theta_{t}
controls the intensity of perturbation at each step, allowing gradual scrambling of the quantum state across time steps.
