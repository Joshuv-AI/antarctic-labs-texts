Polynomial-Time Algorithms for Black-Box Distributive Expanded Groups
Mikhail Anokhin

Abstract
Let Ω \Omega be a finite set of finitary operation symbols. An Ω \Omega -expanded group is a group (written additively and called the additive group of the Ω \Omega -expanded group) with an Ω \Omega -algebra structure. We use the black-box model of computation in Ω \Omega -expanded groups. In this model, elements of a finite Ω \Omega -expanded group H H are represented (not necessarily uniquely) by bit strings of the same length, say, n n . Given representations of elements of H H , equality testing and the fundamental operations of H H are performed by an oracle. Assume that H H is distributive, i.e., all its fundamental operations associated with nonnullary operation symbols in Ω \Omega are distributive over addition. Suppose s = ( s 1 , … , s m ) s=(s_{1},\dots,s_{m}) is a generating system of H H . In this paper, we present probabilistic polynomial-time black-box Ω \Omega -expanded group algorithms for the following problems: (i) given ( 1 n , s ) (1^{n},s) , construct a generating system of the additive group of H H , (ii) given ( 1 n , s , ( t 1 , … , t k ) ) (1^{n},s,(t_{1},\dots,t_{k})) with t 1 , … , t k ∈ H t_{1},\dots,t_{k}\in H , find a generating system of the additive group of the ideal in H H generated by { t 1 , … , t k } \{t_{1},\dots,t_{k}\} , and (iii) given ( 1 n , s ) (1^{n},s) , decide whether H ∈ 𝔙 H\in\mathfrak{V} , where 𝔙 \mathfrak{V} is an arbitrary finitely based variety of distributive Ω \Omega -expanded groups with nilpotent additive groups. The error probability of these algorithms is exponentially small in n n . In particular, this can be applied to groups, rings, R R -modules, and R R -algebras, where R R is a fixed finitely generated commutative associative ring with 1 1 . Rings and R R -algebras may be here with or without 1 1 , where 1 1 is considered as a nullary fundamental operation.

Introduction

In
[
7
]
, Babai and Szemerédi introduced a model of
computation in finite groups, called the black-box group
model. In that model, elements of a finite group
G
are
represented (not necessarily uniquely) by bit strings of the
same length. Given representations of elements of
G
,
equality testing and the group operations in
G
are
performed by an oracle. When an algorithm performs a
computation in
G
, it works with the above-mentioned
representations of elements of
G
and has access to an
arbitrary oracle performing the above tasks. Also, this
algorithm takes as input a tuple
(r_{1},\dots,r_{m})
, where

r_{i}
is a representation of
s_{i}\in G
for every

i\in\{1,\dots,m\}
; it is assumed that the system

(s_{1},\dots,s_{m})
generates the group
G
.

We note that in
[
7
]
, testing equality of elements of

G
(more precisely, the equivalent task of testing equality
to
1
in
G
) is understood in a special sense. However, we
understand testing equality of elements of
G
in the
standard sense, i.e., given representations of arbitrary
elements
g_{1},g_{2}\in G
, decide whether
g_{1}=g_{2}
.

Let
\Sigma
be a fixed finite set of finitary operation
symbols. Then the black-box group model can be naturally
generalized to
\Sigma
-algebras (see
Subsection
2.4
). Namely, we define a
black-box
\Sigma
-algebra as a pair
(H,\rho)
, where
H

is a finite
\Sigma
-algebra and
\rho
is a function from a
subset of
\{0,1\}^{n}
onto
H
for some
n\in\mathbb{N}
. (Throughout
this paper,
\mathbb{N}=\{0,1,\dotsc\}
.) Suppose
\mathsf{H}=(H,\rho)

is a black-box
\Sigma
-algebra. Any algebraic statement
concerning
\mathsf{H}
should be understood as concerning
H
.
If
H\neq\emptyset
, then there exists a unique
n\in\mathbb{N}
such
that
\rho
is defined on a subset of
\{0,1\}^{n}
; this
nonnegative integer
n
is called the encoding length
of
\mathsf{H}
(
cf.

[
4
]
). Otherwise any
n\in\mathbb{N}
can be
taken as an encoding length of
\mathsf{H}
. For every
h\in H
,
an arbitrary preimage of
h
under
\rho
is considered as a
representation of
h
for computational purposes. For
brevity, we often identify elements of
H
with their
representations. Loosely speaking, a black-box

\Sigma
-algebra algorithm is an algorithm that works with
such representations and has access to an arbitrary oracle
performing equality testing and the fundamental operations
during a computation in an arbitrary black-box

\Sigma
-algebra.

1.1
Previous work

There are many works concerning various versions of the
black-box model of computation in algebraic structures. Most
of those works deal with black-box groups. For surveys on
the theory of computation in black-box groups, we refer the
reader to
[
4
]
and
[
12
, Subsections 2.2–2.4]
;
see also
[
20
, Chapter 2]
. Black-box prime fields
were studied in
[
10
]
. Some polynomial-time algorithms
for associative black-box rings were constructed
in
[
3
]
and
[
9
]
. (In this paper, unless
otherwise stated, rings and
R
-algebras are not necessarily
associative, commutative, or with
1
. Here,
R
is a
commutative associative ring with
1
. We do not identify
rings and
\mathbb{Z}
-algebras because they are
\Sigma
-algebras
for different sets
\Sigma
.)

Note that black-box
\Sigma
-algebra quantum algorithms are
also considered in the literature. See,
e.g.
,
[
21
,
14
]
for groups,
[
3
,
9
]
for associative rings,
and
[
2
]
. However, in this paper we restrict
ourselves to classical algorithms.

We briefly state some results related to the results of this
paper. Suppose
\mathsf{G}=(G,\alpha)
is a black-box group and

n
is the encoding length of
\mathsf{G}
. Babai et
al.
[
5
, Theorems 1.5, 2.5, and 3.7]
proposed
probabilistic polynomial-time black-box group algorithms for
the following problems:

[label=()]

1.

Given a generating system

(s_{1},\dots,s_{m})
of
G
, construct a generating system with

O(n)
elements for
G
.

2.

Given a generating system

(s_{1},\dots,s_{O(n)})
for
G
and a tuple

(t_{1},\dots,t_{O(n)})
of elements of
G
, find a generating
system with
O(n)
elements for the normal closure
N
of

\{t_{1},\dots,t_{O(n)}\}
in
G
.

See also
[
20
, Subsections 2.3.2, 2.3.3, and 2.4.2]
.
Note that if the input is correct, then the algorithm for
problem
1
(resp.,

2
) always outputs a system of
elements of
G
(resp.,
N
). Therefore, for any constant

\epsilon
satisfying
0<\epsilon<1
, we can assume that the
success probability of these algorithms is at
least
\epsilon
. This can be proved in a standard way by
running the algorithm multiple times and concatenating the
output systems.

Arvind, Das, and Mukhopadhyay
[
3
, Theorem 1 and its
proof]
constructed a probabilistic polynomial-time
black-box ring algorithm
A
such that the following holds.
Suppose
\mathsf{R}=(R,\gamma)
is a black-box associative ring
and
s\in R^{*}
is a generating system of
R
. (For any set

Y
,
Y^{*}
denotes the set of all tuples of elements
in
Y
.) Then
A
on input
s
outputs a generating system
of the additive group of
R
with probability at
least
1/6
. The algorithm
A
on input
s
always outputs a
system of elements of
R
. Hence for any polynomially
bounded function
\eta\colon\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}=\{r\in\mathbb{R}\,|\,r\geq 0\}
,
the success probability of
A
can be amplified to at least

1-2^{-\eta(n)}
, where
n
is the encoding length of
\mathsf{R}
. This can be done in a standard way by running the
algorithm
A
multiple times and concatenating the output
systems. In this result,
\Sigma
consists of symbols for
addition, additive inverse, zero, and multiplication.

Let
\mathfrak{C}
and
\mathfrak{U}
be abstract (i.e., closed under
isomorphism) classes of
\Sigma
-algebras. We say that
\mathfrak{C}
is
G-decidable
in
\mathfrak{U}
if there exists a
probabilistic polynomial-time black-box
\Sigma
-algebra
algorithm
A
such that the following holds. Suppose
\mathsf{H}=(H,\rho)
is a black-box
\Sigma
-algebra with
H\in\mathfrak{U}
,
n
is an encoding length of
\mathsf{H}
(which is unique if

H\neq\emptyset
and arbitrary otherwise), and
s\in H^{*}
is
a generating system of
H
. Then, given
(1^{n},s)
(where

1^{n}
is the unary representation of
n
),
A
correctly
decides whether
H\in\mathfrak{C}
with probability at least
2/3
.
In Subsection
2.4
, we explain why we
include
1^{n}
in the input to the algorithm
A
and list
some cases where this is not necessary. The letter G in the
term “G-decidable” stands for generating system. We note
that the following conditions are equivalent:

•

The class
\mathfrak{C}
is G-decidable in
\mathfrak{U}
.

•

There exist a polynomially bounded function

\xi\colon\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
and a probabilistic polynomial-time
black-box
\Sigma
-algebra algorithm that decides
\mathfrak{C}
in

\mathfrak{U}
as in the above definition of G-decidability, but
with success probability at least
1/2+1/\xi(n)
.

•

For any polynomially bounded function

\eta\colon\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
, there exists a probabilistic
polynomial-time black-box
\Sigma
-algebra algorithm that
decides
\mathfrak{C}
in
\mathfrak{U}
as in the above definition of
G-decidability, but with success probability at least

1-2^{-\eta(n)}
.

This equivalence can be proved in a standard way by running
the decision algorithm multiple times, taking the majority
of the outputs, and using the Chernoff bound. In particular,
we obtain an equivalent definition of G-decidability if we
replace the lower bound
2/3
in the above definition by an
arbitrary constant
\delta
such that
1/2<\delta<1
.

If we require the algorithm
A
in the above definition of
G-decidability to be deterministic, then we obtain a
definition of
deterministic G-decidability
; of
course, this deterministic algorithm always gives the
correct answer. These types of decidability (in a slightly
modified form) were defined in
[
1
]
(see
Definition 3.1 in that paper).

Let
k\in\mathbb{N}
. Then
\mathfrak{N}_{k}
denotes the variety of all
nilpotent groups of class at most
k
. Also,
\mathfrak{A}
is the
variety of all abelian groups, i.e.,
\mathfrak{A}=\mathfrak{N}_{1}
. If

\mathfrak{V}
and
\mathfrak{W}
are varieties of groups, then their
product
\mathfrak{V}\mathfrak{W}
consists of all groups
G
containing a
normal subgroup
N\in\mathfrak{V}
such that
G/N\in\mathfrak{W}
. It is
well known that multiplication of varieties of groups is
associative. Furthermore, the
k
th power
\mathfrak{A}^{k}
of
\mathfrak{A}
is the variety of all solvable groups of derived length
at most
k
. See also
[
17
, 14.64, 14.61, 21.11, 21.51, and
21.52]
. In particular,
\mathfrak{A}^{0}=\mathfrak{N}_{0}
is the
variety of trivial groups.

Babai et al.
[
5
, Corollary 1.6]
proved that the
classes of all nilpotent and all solvable groups are
G-decidable in the class of all groups. Furthermore, that
corollary implies G-decidability of
\mathfrak{N}_{k}
and
\mathfrak{A}^{k}

in the class of all groups, where
k
is an arbitrary
nonnegative integer. On the other hand, the classes of all
cyclic and all simple groups are not G-decidable even in the
class of all elementary abelian
p
-groups, where the prime

p
depends on the group. This follows from the reasoning of
Babai in
[
8
, Section 9]
.

In our opinion, the most interesting case of the problem of
deciding
\mathfrak{C}
in
\mathfrak{U}
is the case where
\mathfrak{C}
is
defined within
\mathfrak{U}
by a finite set of closed first-order
formulas. This is justified by the fact that in this case,

\mathfrak{C}
satisfies the above definition of deterministic
G-decidability in
\mathfrak{U}
with the following modification:
The algorithm
A
on input
(1^{n},(s_{1},\dots,s_{m}))
runs in
time polynomial in
2^{n}
and
m
. See
[
1
, Lemma 3.1
(together with its proof) and Remark 3.4]
.

The simplest closed first-order formulas are identities,
which define varieties. Most of the results in
[
1
]

concern the problem of deciding whether a given black-box

\Sigma
-algebra belongs to a fixed finitely based variety
of
\Sigma
-algebras. Although in
[
1
]
it is assumed
that each element of a black-box
\Sigma
-algebra is
represented by a unique bit string (and hence equality
testing can be performed without access to an oracle), many
of the results in that paper are valid without this
assumption. We state two of these results here. First, every
variety of nilpotent groups (resp., Lie rings or Lie

R
-algebras) is deterministically G-decidable in the class
of all groups (resp., Lie rings or Lie
R
-algebras). Here,

R
is an arbitrary finitely generated commutative
associative ring with
1
. This result follows
from
[
1
, Corollary 5.1]
because for classes of
groups, rings, and
R
-algebras, strong G-decidability in
the sense of
[
1
]
implies deterministic
G-decidability in the above sense. Second, suppose
\mathfrak{V}_{1},\dots,\mathfrak{V}_{k}
are varieties of groups such that for all

i\in\{1,\dots,k\}
, we have
\mathfrak{V}_{i}\subseteq\mathfrak{A}\mathfrak{N}_{c_{i}}\cap\mathfrak{N}_{d_{i}}\mathfrak{A}
, where
c_{i}
and
d_{i}
are some
nonnegative integers. Then
\mathfrak{V}_{1}\dots\mathfrak{V}_{k}
is
G-decidable in the class of all groups
(see
[
1
, Proposition 5.2]
).

1.2
Our contributions

Suppose
\Omega
is a fixed finite set of finitary operation
symbols. An
\Omega
-expanded group is a group, written
additively but not necessarily abelian, with an

\Omega
-algebra structure. This group (without the

\Omega
-algebra structure) is called the additive group of
the
\Omega
-expanded group. We assume that the symbols for
addition (
+
), additive inverse (
-
), and zero (
0
) do
not belong to
\Omega
. See also
Definition
2.2
. Furthermore, an

\Omega
-expanded group
H
is said to be distributive if
all the fundamental operations of
H
associated with
nonnullary operation symbols in
\Omega
are distributive
over addition. See also Definition
2.2
.

Let
c
be a constant greater than
1
. We construct a
probabilistic polynomial-time black-box
\Omega
-expanded
group algorithm
B
such that the following holds. Suppose

\mathsf{H}=(H,\rho)
is a black-box distributive

\Omega
-expanded group,
n
is the encoding length of
\mathsf{H}
, and
s\in H^{*}
is a generating system of
H
. Then
B

on input
(1^{n},s)
outputs a generating system of the
additive group of
H
with probability at least
1-n/c^{n}
.
See Theorem
3
for a precise
statement; the algorithm
B
is described just before this
theorem. This result, referred to as
Result 1
,
generalizes the result of Arvind, Das, and Mukhopadhyay
stated in Subsection
1.1
. The algorithm
B
is
based on the same ideas as the closure algorithm described
just before Lemma 2.3.8 in
[
20
]
. We use random
subsums (see Subsection
2.3
) to construct
generating systems with
O(n)
elements for certain
subgroups of the additive group of
H
. Note that the
algorithm of Arvind, Das, and Mukhopadhyay mentioned in
Subsection
1.1
is based on different ideas.

Result 1 has at least two interesting applications. First,
we obtain a probabilistic polynomial-time black-box

\Omega
-expanded group algorithm
C
such that the
following holds. Let
\mathsf{H}=(H,\rho)
,
n
, and
s
be as in
Result 1 and let
t\in H^{*}
. Then
C
on input
(1^{n},s,t)

outputs with probability at least
1-2n/c^{n}
a generating
system of the additive group of the ideal in
H
generated
by
t
. See Theorem
3
for a
precise statement; the algorithm
C
is described in the
proof of this theorem. This result generalizes the result of
Babai et al. on the existence of a probabilistic
polynomial-time black-box group algorithm for
problem
2
(see
Subsection
1.1
). Using the algorithm of Babai
et al. for problem
1
(see
Subsection
1.1
), we can reduce the number of
elements in a generating system for the additive group of
the above-mentioned ideal to
O(n)
in polynomial time. In
fact, the algorithm
C
on the above input just runs the
algorithm
B
twice with different parameters.

Note that the algorithm
B
(resp.,
C
) always outputs a
system of elements of
H
(resp., the ideal in
H
generated
by
t
) provided that the input is correct. Therefore, for
any polynomially bounded function
\eta\colon\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
, the
success probability of these algorithms can be amplified to
at least
1-2^{-\eta(n)}
. As above, this can be done in a
standard way by running the algorithm multiple times and
concatenating the output systems.

Second, Result 1 together with the result of
[
1
]

stated in Lemma
3
yields the
following. Suppose
\mathfrak{V}
is a finitely based variety of
distributive
\Omega
-expanded groups such that the additive
group of any
\Omega
-expanded group in
\mathfrak{V}
is
nilpotent. Then
\mathfrak{V}
is G-decidable in the class of all
distributive
\Omega
-expanded groups. See
Theorem
3
for a precise statement. In our
opinion, this result is the main motivation for proving
Result 1.

In particular, all our results stated in this subsection
hold when the distributive
\Omega
-expanded groups are
ordinary groups, rings,
R
-modules, or
R
-algebras, where

R
is a fixed finitely generated commutative associative
ring with
1
. Rings and
R
-algebras may be here with or
without
1
, where
1
is considered as a nullary
fundamental operation. See also Remark
3
.

1.3
Open questions and organization of the paper

Denote by
\mathfrak{G}
the class of all groups. Here are two open
questions for future research:

•

Does there exist a finitely based variety of groups
that is not G-decidable in
\mathfrak{G}
? Note that finitely based
varieties of semigroups that are not G-decidable in the
class of all semigroups do exist
(see
[
1
, Section 7]
). Also, there exists a
quasivariety that is defined within
\mathfrak{N}_{2}
by a single
quasi-identity and is not G-decidable in
\mathfrak{N}_{2}

(see
[
1
, Subsection 1.3]
).

•

Let
d
be an integer such that
d\geq 4
. Is the
Burnside variety
\mathfrak{B}_{d}
G-decidable in
\mathfrak{G}
? For any

k\in\mathbb{N}\setminus\{0\}
, the
Burnside variety

\mathfrak{B}_{k}
is defined within
\mathfrak{G}
by the identity
\forall\,x\ (x^{k}=1)
(see
[
17
, 14.63]
). Note that
\mathfrak{B}_{1}

consists only of trivial groups,
\mathfrak{B}_{2}\subseteq\mathfrak{A}
,
and
\mathfrak{B}_{3}\subseteq\mathfrak{N}_{3}
. In fact, the inclusion

[

]
; see also
[

, 12.3.5 and item (iv)

. Hence, by the first of two results stated

, we

,

, and

.

2.1
Universal algebra

For a detailed introduction to universal algebra, the reader
is referred to standard books,
e.g.
,
[
11
,
22
]
.

Throughout the paper,
\Sigma
denotes a set of finitary
operation symbols. Each
\sigma\in\Sigma
is assigned a
nonnegative integer called the
arity
of
\sigma
and
denoted by
\ar\sigma
. A
\Sigma
-algebra
is a set

H
called the
carrier
(or the
underlying set
)
together with a family
(\widehat{\sigma}\colon H^{\ar\sigma}\to H\,|\,\sigma\in\Sigma)
of operations on
H

called the
fundamental operations
. We usually denote
a
\Sigma
-algebra and its carrier by the same symbol when
there is no confusion.

Let
H
be a
\Sigma
-algebra. Then the fundamental
operation of
H
associated with a symbol
\sigma\in\Sigma

will be denoted by
\sigma^{H}
or simply by
\sigma
.
Furthermore, suppose
\Psi
is a subset of
\Sigma
. Then
the
\Psi
-algebra obtained from
H
by omitting the
fundamental operations associated with symbols in

\Sigma\setminus\Psi
is said to be the
\Psi
-reduct

of
H
(or the
reduct
of
H
to
\Psi
). We denote
the
\Psi
-reduct of
H
by
H|_{\Psi}
. Also, let
S
be a
set or system of elements of
H
. Then
\langle S\rangle_{\Psi}
denotes
the subalgebra of
H|_{\Psi}
generated by
S
. For simplicity
of notation, if
\langle S\rangle_{\Psi}=H|_{\Psi}
(in other words,
S

generates
H|_{\Psi}
), then we will write
\langle S\rangle_{\Psi}=H
.

Denote by
\Sigma_{0}
the set of all nullary operation
symbols in
\Sigma
. Whenever
\sigma\in\Sigma_{0}
, it is
common to write
\sigma
instead of
\sigma()
. We note that
if
\Sigma_{0}=\emptyset
, then a
\Sigma
-algebra may be
empty, as in the books
[
11
,
22
]
. This is not
standard in universal algebra, but is convenient, at least
in our opinion. However, for this paper it does not really
matter much whether we allow empty
\Sigma
-algebras. This
is because our main results are concerned with distributive
expanded groups and any expanded group is nonempty.

Suppose
X=\{x_{1},x_{2},\dotsc\}
is a countably infinite set
of symbols called variables. We assume that
x_{1},x_{2},\dotsc

are distinct and are not in
\Sigma
. Denote by

\Tm_{\infty}(\Sigma)
the set of all
\Sigma
-terms over
X
.
This set is defined as the smallest set
T
such that

X\cup\Sigma_{0}\subseteq T
and if

\sigma\in\Sigma\setminus\Sigma_{0}
and

v_{1},\dots,v_{\ar\sigma}\in T
, then the formal expression

\sigma(v_{1},\dots,v_{\ar\sigma})
is in
T
. Of course,

\Tm_{\infty}(\Sigma)
is a
\Sigma
-algebra under the natural
operations.

An
identity
(or a
law
) over
\Sigma
is a
closed first-order formula of the form

\forall\,x_{1},\dots,x_{m}\ (v=w)
, where
m\in\mathbb{N}
and

v,w\in\langle\{x_{1},\dots,x_{m}\}\rangle_{\Sigma}
. We usually omit the
phrase “over
\Sigma
.” We will write identities simply as

v=w
, where
v,w\in\Tm_{\infty}(\Sigma)
, assuming that all
variables are universally quantified. A class
\mathfrak{V}
of

\Sigma
-algebras is said to be a
variety
if it can
be defined (within the class of all
\Sigma
-algebras) by a
set of identities. By the Birkhoff variety theorem (see,

e.g.
,
[
11
, Chapter IV, Theorem 3.1]

or
[
22
, Subsection 3.2.3, Theorem 21]
), a class of

\Sigma
-algebras is a variety if and only if it is closed
under taking subalgebras, homomorphic images, and direct
products.

In this paper,
\mathfrak{V}
denotes a variety of

\Sigma
-algebras. Suppose
\mathfrak{W}
is a variety of

\Sigma
-algebras such that
\mathfrak{V}\subseteq\mathfrak{W}
. Then
\mathfrak{V}
is called
finitely based within
\mathfrak{W}
if
\mathfrak{V}

can be defined within
\mathfrak{W}
by a finite set of identities.
Also,
\mathfrak{V}
is said to be
finitely based
if it is
finitely based within the variety of all
\Sigma
-algebras.
It is evident that if
\mathfrak{W}
is finitely based, then
\mathfrak{V}
is finitely based if and only if
\mathfrak{V}
is finitely
based within
\mathfrak{W}
.

2.2
Expanded groups

Throughout the paper,
\Omega
denotes a set of finitary
operation symbols. Similarly to
\Sigma
, the arity of any

\omega\in\Omega
is denoted by
\ar\omega
and

\Omega_{0}=\{\omega\in\Omega\,|\,\ar\omega=0\}
.

{defi}

[
\Omega
-expanded group and its additive
group]

Let
\Gamma
be the set of operation symbols consisting of
the standard symbols for addition (
+
), additive inverse
(
-
), and zero (
0
). (In particular, the arities of
+
,

-
, and
0
are
2
,
1
, and
0
, respectively.) We assume
that
\Gamma\cap\Omega=\emptyset
and

\Sigma=\Gamma\cup\Omega
. Then a
\Sigma
-algebra
H
is
called an
\Omega
-expanded group
(or simply

expanded group
) if
H|_{\Gamma}
is a group under
+
with
inverse operation
-
and identity element
0
. This group
is said to be the
additive group
of
H
; we emphasize
that it is not necessarily abelian.

When it comes to
\Omega
-expanded groups, we assume that

\Sigma=\Gamma\cup\Omega
, where
\Gamma
is as in
Definition
2.2
(in particular,

\Gamma\cap\Omega=\emptyset
). Suppose
\Sigma
is such a
set and
a
and
b
are elements of a
\Sigma
-algebra.
Then, of course, we write
a+b
and
-a
instead of
+(a,b)

and
-(a)
, respectively. We also use
a-b
as shorthand for

a+(-b)
.

The notion of
\Omega
-expanded group generalizes the notion
of
\Omega
-group introduced by Higgins
in
[
13
, Section 2]
. Namely, an
\Omega
-expanded
group is an
\Omega
-group
if and only if

\Omega_{0}=\emptyset
and
\omega(0,\dots,0)=0
for all

\omega\in\Omega
. Note that
\Omega
-groups are also known
as groups with multiple operators (see,

e.g.
,
[
11
, Chapter II, Section 2]
). We emphasize
that rings with
1
can be considered as
\Omega
-expanded
groups, but not as
\Omega
-groups if
1
is required to be
a nullary fundamental operation. The same holds for

R
-algebras with
1
, where
R
is a commutative
associative ring with
1
.

{defi}

[distributive
\Omega
-expanded group]

An
\Omega
-expanded group
H
is called
distributive

if for any
\omega\in\Omega\setminus\Omega_{0}
, the
fundamental operation
\omega^{H}
is distributive over
+^{H}
,
i.e.,

\displaystyle\omega(h_{1},\dots,h_{i-1},a+b,h_{i+1},\dots,h_{\ar\omega})

\displaystyle\qquad=\omega(h_{1},\dots,h_{i-1},a,h_{i+1},\dots,h_{\ar\omega})+\omega(h_{1},\dots,h_{i-1},b,h_{i+1},\dots,h_{\ar\omega})

for all
i\in\{1,\dots,\ar\omega\}
and

a,b,h_{1},\dots,h_{i-1},h_{i+1},\dots,h_{\ar\omega}\in H
.

It is easy to see that if
\Omega_{0}=\emptyset
, then
distributive
\Omega
-expanded groups are the same as
distributive
\Omega
-groups. Distributive
\Omega
-groups
were defined by Higgins in
[
13
, Section 4]
.

Clearly, the classes of all
\Omega
-expanded groups and of
all distributive
\Omega
-expanded groups are varieties of

\Sigma
-algebras. The variety of all
\Omega
-expanded
groups is finitely based. If
\Omega\setminus\Omega_{0}
is
finite, then the variety of all distributive

\Omega
-expanded groups is also finitely based.

Of course, groups are exactly
\emptyset
-expanded groups,
which are always distributive. It is easy to see that the
class of all rings (resp., rings with
1
) is a finitely
based variety of distributive
\{\cdot\}
-expanded groups
(resp., distributive
\{\cdot,1\}
-expanded groups). The
symbols
\cdot
and
1
have their standard meanings here.

Let
H
be an
\Omega
-expanded group. Then any subalgebra
of
H
as a
\Sigma
-algebra is called an

\Omega
-subgroup
of
H
. This term is commonly used
for subalgebras of
\Omega
-groups; we extend it to
subalgebras of
\Omega
-expanded groups.

{defi}

[ideal of an
\Omega
-expanded group]
A set
A\subseteq H
is said to be an
ideal
of
H
if

•

A
is a normal subgroup of
H|_{\Gamma}
and

•

for any
\omega\in\Omega\setminus\Omega_{0}
,

i\in\{1,\dots,\ar\omega\}
,
h_{1},\dots,h_{\ar\omega}\in H
,
and
a\in A
, we have

\omega(h_{1},\dots,h_{i-1},a+h_{i},h_{i+1},\dots,h_{\ar\omega})-\omega(h_{1},\dots,h_{i-1},h_{i},h_{i+1},\dots,h_{\ar\omega})\in A.

The
additive group
of an ideal in
H
is this ideal
considered as a subgroup of
H|_{\Gamma}
. For any ideal
A
of

H
, let
\theta_{A}
be the equivalence relation on
H
whose
equivalence classes are the cosets of
A
in
H|_{\Gamma}
. Then
it is easy to see that
A\mapsto\theta_{A}
is a one-to-one
correspondence between the set of all ideals of
H
and the
set of all congruences on
H
.

{rem}

Assume that the
\Omega
-expanded group
H
is distributive.
Suppose
A
is a subset of
H
. Then it is evident that
A

is an ideal of
H
if and only if
A
is a normal subgroup
of
H|_{\Gamma}
and for all
\omega\in\Omega\setminus\Omega_{0}

and
i\in\{1,\dots,\ar\omega\}
, we have

\omega(H,\dots,H,A,H,\dots,H)\subseteq A
, where
A
occurs
in the
i
th position.

See
[
13
, Section 3, Theorem 4A, and its corollary]

for a definition and characterization of ideals of

\Omega
-groups.

{rem}

Assume that
H
is distributive. Then for any set

S\subseteq H
and any
\omega\in\Omega
, we have

\omega(\langle S\rangle_{\Gamma}^{\ar\omega})\subseteq\langle\omega(S^{\ar\omega})\rangle_{\Gamma}
. Indeed,
if
\ar\omega=0
, then this is trivial. Otherwise this
inclusion follows from the distributivity of
\omega^{H}

over
+^{H}
.

Define the function
\tau
from the set of all subsets of

H
to itself by

\tau(S)=\left\langle S\cup\bigcup_{\omega\in\Omega}\omega(S^{\ar\omega})\right\rangle_{\Gamma}\quad\text{for any set }S\subseteq H.

(1)

The
k
-fold composition of
\tau
with itself is denoted by

\tau^{k}
(
k\in\mathbb{N}
). In particular,
\tau^{0}
is the identity
function on the set of all subsets of
H
. It is evident
that
S\subseteq\tau(S)
for all
S\subseteq H
. Also, if

S\subseteq T\subseteq H
, then
\tau(S)\subseteq\tau(T)
.

{lem}

Assume that
H
is distributive. Then
\tau(S)=\tau(\langle S\rangle_{\Gamma})

for every set
S\subseteq H
.

Proof.

Let
S
be a subset of
H
. Since
S\subseteq\langle S\rangle_{\Gamma}
, we have

\tau(S)\subseteq\tau(\langle S\rangle_{\Gamma})
. Furthermore, by
Remark
2.2
,
\omega(\langle S\rangle_{\Gamma}^{\ar\omega})\subseteq\langle\omega(S^{\ar\omega})\rangle_{\Gamma}
for all

\omega\in\Omega
. Hence,

\langle S\rangle_{\Gamma}\cup\bigcup_{\omega\in\Omega}\omega(\langle S\rangle_{\Gamma}^{\ar\omega})\subseteq\langle S\rangle_{\Gamma}\cup\bigcup_{\omega\in\Omega}\langle\omega(S^{\ar\omega})\rangle_{\Gamma}\subseteq\left\langle S\cup\bigcup_{\omega\in\Omega}\omega(S^{\ar\omega})\right\rangle_{\Gamma}=\tau(S).

This implies that

\tau(\langle S\rangle_{\Gamma})=\left\langle\langle S\rangle_{\Gamma}\cup\bigcup_{\omega\in\Omega}\omega(\langle S\rangle_{\Gamma}^{\ar\omega})\right\rangle_{\Gamma}\subseteq\tau(S)

because
\tau(S)
is a subgroup of
H|_{\Gamma}
. Thus,

\tau(S)=\tau(\langle S\rangle_{\Gamma})
.
∎

{lem}

For any set
S\subseteq H
, we have

\bigcup_{i=0}^{\infty}\tau^{i}(S)=\langle S\rangle_{\Sigma}
.

Proof.

Suppose
S
is a subset of
H
and

U=\bigcup_{i=0}^{\infty}\tau^{i}(S)
. It is easy to see that if

T\subseteq\langle S\rangle_{\Sigma}
, then
\tau(T)\subseteq\langle S\rangle_{\Sigma}
. This implies by induction on
i
that

\tau^{i}(S)\subseteq\langle S\rangle_{\Sigma}
for all
i\in\mathbb{N}
.
Therefore, we have
U\subseteq\langle S\rangle_{\Sigma}
.

Let
\sigma\in\Sigma
and
h_{1},\dots,h_{\ar\sigma}\in U
.
Since
\tau^{0}(S)\subseteq\tau^{1}(S)\subseteq\dotsb
, we can
choose a positive integer
k
such that

h_{1},\dots,h_{\ar\sigma}\in\tau^{k}(S)
. Then

\sigma(h_{1},\dots,h_{\ar\sigma})\in\tau^{k}(S)\subseteq U
if

\sigma\in\Gamma
(because
\tau^{k}(S)
is a subgroup of

H|_{\Gamma}
) and

\sigma(h_{1},\dots,h_{\ar\sigma})\in\tau^{k+1}(S)\subseteq U

if
\sigma\in\Omega
. This shows that
U
is an

\Omega
-subgroup of
H
. Also,
S=\tau^{0}(S)\subseteq U
.
Hence we have
\langle S\rangle_{\Sigma}\subseteq U
. Thus,
U=\langle S\rangle_{\Sigma}
, as required.
∎

{rem}

Suppose
R
is a commutative associative ring with
1

generated by a system
s=(s_{1},\dots,s_{m})
as a ring with

1
, where
m\in\mathbb{N}
. In this remark, we assume that
\Omega

consists of
m
unary operation symbols

\omega_{1},\dots,\omega_{m}
. We show how to represent

R
-modules as distributive
\Omega
-expanded groups. All

R
-modules are assumed to be unital. Moreover, the class of
all
R
-modules represented in such a way is a finitely
based variety of distributive
\Omega
-expanded groups.
Similarly, the same can be done for
R
-algebras and

R
-algebras with
1
, where
1
is considered as a nullary
fundamental operation.

Let
B
be an
R
-module. Consider
B
as a distributive

\Omega
-expanded group in which
\omega_{i}(b)=s_{i}b
for all

i\in\{1,\dots,m\}
and
b\in B
. The additive group of
B

as an
\Omega
-expanded group (i.e.,
B|_{\Gamma}
) is the same
as that of
B
as an
R
-module. It is evident that

\omega_{1}^{B},\dots,\omega_{m}^{B}
are commuting endomorphisms
of
B|_{\Gamma}
.

Suppose
y_{1},\dots,y_{m}
are independent commuting variables
and let
y=(y_{1},\dots,y_{m})
. As usual, denote by
\mathbb{Z}[y]
the
polynomial ring in variables
y_{1},\dots,y_{m}
over
\mathbb{Z}
. Put

Q=\{p\in\mathbb{Z}[y]\,|\,p(s)=0\}
. Then
Q
is an ideal of
\mathbb{Z}[y]

and
p+Q\mapsto p(s)
, where
p\in\mathbb{Z}[y]
, is an isomorphism
of
\mathbb{Z}[y]/Q
onto
R
. Of course,

q(\omega_{1}^{B},\dots,\omega_{m}^{B})(b)=q(s)b=0
for any
q\in Q

and
b\in B
, where
\omega_{1}^{B},\dots,\omega_{m}^{B}
are
considered as commuting elements of the endomorphism ring
of
B|_{\Gamma}
. The Hilbert basis theorem implies the existence
of a finite set
T\subseteq Q
such that
Q=\sum_{t\in T}\mathbb{Z}[y]t
. Choose such a set
T
. Thus, we see that
B

(considered as an
\Omega
-expanded group) satisfies the
following identities:

[label=()]

1.

(x_{1}+x_{2})+x_{3}=x_{1}+(x_{2}+x_{3})
,

x_{1}+x_{2}=x_{2}+x_{1}
,
x_{1}+0=x_{1}
,
x_{1}-x_{1}=0
;

2.

\omega_{i}(x_{1}+x_{2})=\omega_{i}(x_{1})+\omega_{i}(x_{2})
for
all
i\in\{1,\dots,m\}
;

3.

\omega_{j}(\omega_{i}(x_{1}))=\omega_{i}(\omega_{j}(x_{1}))
for
all
i\in\{1,\dots,m\}
and
j\in\{i+1,\dots,m\}
;

4.

t(\omega_{1},\dots,\omega_{m})(x_{1})=0
for all
t\in T
.

In identities
4
, we use terms of the form

p(\omega_{1},\dots,\omega_{m})(v)
, where
p\in\mathbb{Z}[y]
and

v\in\Tm_{\infty}(\Sigma)
. To avoid ambiguity, we formally
define these terms.

Put
I=\{(i_{1},\dots,i_{d})\,|\,d,i_{1},\dots,i_{d}\in\mathbb{N},\,1\leq i_{1}\leq\dots\leq i_{d}\leq m\}
. Suppose

v\in\Tm_{\infty}(\Sigma)
. Then for every

j=(i_{1},\dots,i_{d})\in I
, we define

\omega_{j}(v)=\omega_{i_{d}}(\dots(\omega_{i_{1}}(v))\dots)
.
Furthermore, choose a total order (
e.g.
, lexicographic)
on
I
. For any nonempty finite multisubset

J=\{j_{1},\dots,j_{k}\}
of
I
, where
j_{1}\leq\dots\leq j_{k}
,
let

\omega_{J}(v)=(\dots(\omega_{j_{1}}(v)+\omega_{j_{2}}(v))+\dotsb)+\omega_{j_{k}}(v).

(Note that addition in
\Tm_{\infty}(\Sigma)
is neither
commutative nor associative.)

Suppose
p\in\mathbb{Z}[y]
. Represent
p
as

\left(\sum_{(i_{1},\dots,i_{d})\in J_{+}}y_{i_{1}}\dots y_{i_{d}}\right)-\left(\sum_{(i_{1},\dots,i_{d})\in J_{-}}y_{i_{1}}\dots y_{i_{d}}\right),

where
J_{+}
and
J_{-}
are disjoint finite multisubsets of

I
; these multisubsets are uniquely determined by
p
. Then
we define

p(\omega_{1},\dots,\omega_{m})(v)=\begin{cases}\omega_{J_{+}}(v)-\omega_{J_{-}}(v)&\text{if
}J_{+}\neq\emptyset\text{ and }J_{-}\neq\emptyset,\\
\omega_{J_{+}}(v)&\text{if }J_{+}\neq\emptyset\text{ and
}J_{-}=\emptyset,\\
-\omega_{J_{-}}(v)&\text{if
}J_{+}=\emptyset\text{ and }J_{-}\neq\emptyset,\\
0&\text{if }J_{+}=J_{-}=\emptyset.\end{cases}

Conversely, let
H
be a
\Sigma
-algebra satisfying
identities
1
–
4
, where

\Sigma
is as in Definition
2.2
.
Then
H
is a distributive
\Omega
-expanded group with
abelian additive group. Moreover, it is easy to see that
H

is an
R
-module with additive group
H|_{\Gamma}
and action
defined by
p(s)h=p(\omega_{1}^{H},\dots,\omega_{m}^{H})(h)
, where

p\in\mathbb{Z}[y]
and
h\in H
. This action is well defined
because
\omega_{1}^{H},\dots,\omega_{m}^{H}
are commuting elements
of the endomorphism ring of
H|_{\Gamma}
and

q(\omega_{1}^{H},\dots,\omega_{m}^{H})(h)=0
for all
q\in Q
and

h\in H
. The latter follows from
identities
4
.

2.3
Groups

For a detailed introduction to group theory, we refer the
reader to standard textbooks,
e.g.
,
[
18
,
19
]
.

Suppose
G
is a group. A set of subgroups of the group
G

is said to be a

if it is nonempty and totally
ordered by inclusion. If
C
is a finite chain of subgroups
of
G
, then
\lvert C\rvert-1
is called the

of

.

{rem}

It is well known that if
G
is finite, then any chain of
subgroups of

has length at most
\log_{2}\lvert G\rvert
. This
follows from the fact that if
K
is a proper subgroup of a
finite group

, then
\lvert L\rvert\geq 2\lvert K\rvert
.

Let
g=(g_{1},\dots,g_{m})
, where
m\in\mathbb{N}
and

g_{1},\dots,g_{m}\in G
. Then a
random subproduct
of

is defined as
g_{1}^{\mathrm{b}_{1}}\dots g_{m}^{\mathrm{b}_{m}}
, where
\mathrm{b}_{1},\dots,\mathrm{b}_{m}
are chosen independently from the uniform
distribution on
\{0,1\}
. When
G
is written additively, we use
the term “random subsum” instead. Namely, a
random
subsum
of
g
is
\mathrm{b}_{1}g_{1}+\dots+\mathrm{b}_{m}g_{m}
, where
\mathrm{b}_{1},\dots,\mathrm{b}_{m}
are as above. Random subproducts were
introduced by Babai, Luks, and Seress in the preliminary
version of
[
6
]
(see Subsection 6.2 in the
preliminary version and Definition 5.12 in the journal
version of that work). Some applications of random
subproducts can be found in
[
5
, Section 2]
. See
also
[
20
, Section 2.3]
and
[
12
, Section 2]
.

The inequality in the next lemma is equivalent to
inequality (2.5) in
[
20
]
(see the proof of
Lemma 2.3.4 in that book). The original result is due to
Babai et al.
[

, Theorem 2.5 and its proof]
.

{lem}

Assume that the group
G
is finite and that any chain of
subgroups of
G
has length at most

. Suppose

is a generating system of

. Also, let

and let

be

\Pr[\{r_{1},\dots,r_{kl}\}\text{ does not generate }G]\leq e^{-(1-2/k)^{2}kl/4}.

2.4
Black-box universal algebra model

In this subsection, we assume that
\Sigma
is an arbitrary
fixed finite set of finitary operation symbols. This allows
us to avoid representation issues.

{defi}

[black-box
\Sigma
-algebra]
Suppose
H
is a finite
\Sigma
-algebra and
\rho
is a
function from a subset of
\{0,1\}^{n}
for some
n\in\mathbb{N}
onto
H
.
Then the pair
(H,\rho)
is called a
black-box

\Sigma
-algebra
.

Let
\mathsf{H}=(H,\rho)
be a black-box
\Sigma
-algebra. Any
algebraic statement concerning
\mathsf{H}
should be understood
as concerning
H
. For example, if
\mathfrak{C}
is an abstract
class of
\Sigma
-algebras, then
\mathsf{H}\in\mathfrak{C}
means that

H\in\mathfrak{C}
. Suppose
E
is the domain of
\rho
. If

H\neq\emptyset
, then
E\neq\emptyset
and there exists a
unique

such that

; this nonnegative
integer

is said to be the
encoding length
of
\mathsf{H}
. If
H=\emptyset
, then

for
all
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Therefore, in this case, any
n\in\mathbb{N}
can be
taken as an encoding length of
\mathsf{H}
. It is evident that
if
n
is an encoding length of
\mathsf{H}
, then
\lvert H\rvert\leq 2^{n}
.

For every
h\in H
, we denote by
[h]_{\rho}
an arbitrary
preimage of
h
under
\rho
. Any of these preimages will be
used as a representation of

for computational purposes.
A similar notation was used by Boneh and Lipton
in
[
10
]
.

{defi}

[
\Sigma
-oracle]

An oracle

is called a
\Sigma
-oracle
for

[label=()]

1.

, where
h_{1},h_{2}\in H
, the oracle

decides whether
h_{1}=h_{2}
(

, returns

if

and

otherwise).

2.

Given any query of the
form
(\sigma,[h_{1}]_{\rho},\dots,[h_{\ar\sigma}]_{\rho})
,
where
\sigma\in\Sigma
and
h_{1},\dots,h_{\ar\sigma}\in H
,
the oracle
O
returns

[\sigma(h_{1},\dots,h_{\ar\sigma})]_{\rho}
.

On other queries, the behavior of the oracle

may be
arbitrary.

To avoid confusion, when it comes to black-box

\Sigma
-algebra model, we assume that the symbol

is not
in
\Sigma
.

{defi}

[black-box
\Sigma
-algebra algorithm]

is said to be a

\Sigma

\Sigma
-algebra,

•

A
has access to a
\Sigma

\Sigma
-algebra and

•

each query made by

When it comes to black-box
\Sigma
-algebra algorithms and

, we write

. In particular, a tuple

(h_{1},\dots,h_{m})
, where
