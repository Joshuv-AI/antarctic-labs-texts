Integrated network toxicology and molecular simulations uncover the molecular mechanism and core targets of DNBaP-induced lung cancer
Authors: Peng He, Zhongting Lu, Yunfei zhao, Tao Li, Jing Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This study aims to comprehensively apply network toxicology and molecular dynamics simulation techniques to investigate the molecular mechanisms of DNBaP-induced lung cancer. We first predicted the toxicological characteristics of DNBaP related to carcinogenicity. Subsequently, by integrating multiple databases, 71 potential cross-targets of DNBaP-induced lung cancer were screened. GO analysis and KEGG pathway enrichment analysis of these targets verified their functions in biological processes and signaling pathways, further confirming their association with lung cancer. Using these potential cross-targets, a STRING interaction network was constructed, and five core genes were identified through Cytoscape software. Molecular docking was performed to predict the binding affinity between DNBaP and these core genes. Finally, molecular dynamics simulations were conducted to further validate the stability of the complexes and infer how the biological functions of the protein structures are affected after complex formation. This study systematically reveals the molecular mechanism of DNBaP-induced lung cancer: it disrupts intracellular homeostasis by interfering with core genes (CDKN2A, EGFR, ESR1, TNF, TP53) and activating key signaling pathways such as PI3K-Akt and MAPK. The research demonstrates the effectiveness and feasibility of integrating network toxicology, molecular docking, and molecular dynamics simulation techniques in assessing the toxic mechanisms of environmental pollutants. It provides an important theoretical basis and scientific methodology for understanding the health risks of DNBaP, screening new targets for lung cancer intervention, and developing preventive strategies. Introduction

With the acceleration of industrialization and urbanization, the exposure to chemical toxins caused by combustion emissions and industrial pollutants has become a major public health issue [1]. Among them, nitro polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs) pollutants mainly exist in the emissions from combustion sources such as diesel exhaust, industrial waste gas, and coal combustion [2]. Studies have confirmed that they have significant carcinogenicity and respiratory toxicity. Long-term exposure to the environment containing nitro PAHs pollutants can significantly increase the risk of malignant tumors of the respiratory system [3]. Although dinitrobenzo[a]pyrene (DNBaP) is a typical nitro-polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbon pollutant, some studies suggest that DNBaP exposure may be associated with the risk of lung cancer (LC) [4]. , the specific mechanisms by which DNBaP may lead to the occurrence and progression of LC still need to be fully elucidated.

Lung cancer is a highly aggressive and prevalent disease worldwide, with an estimated 2.2 million new cases and 1.8 million deaths in 2020. Globally, it is the leading cause of cancer-related deaths in men and the second leading cause in women [5]. Most lung cancer patients are diagnosed at an advanced stage, with a very low survival rate. The existing treatment methods have limited efficacy for advanced patients [6]. LC increases the socioeconomic burden, reduces the quality of life of patients, and has a serious impact on the physical and mental health of patients [7]. This severe situation of disease prevention and control urgently requires us to seek breakthroughs at the level of the action mechanism of environmental carcinogens.

This study takes DNBaP as the research object to explore its potential molecular mechanism of action. To this end, this study intends to adopt a systematic toxicology research strategy, integrate multidisciplinary technical means such as network toxicology prediction, molecular docking verification, and molecular dynamics simulation, and systematically reveal the key molecular targets of DNBaP-induced LC. These findings not only analyze the molecular effects of DNBaP involved in the pathological process of LC but also provide potential therapeutic targets for targeted intervention in the carcinogenic effects of DNBaP, and are expected to open up a new path for reducing the risk of LC caused by DNBaP exposure.

Research methods

Construction of the initial network database for DNBaP toxicity analysis

The ADMETlab 3.0 (https://admetlab3.scbdd.com/)and ProTox-II, https://tox.charite.de/protox3/models were used to predict and evaluate the toxicity of DNBaP [8, 9], and its standard chemical structure and Simplified Molecular Input Line Entry Specification (SMILES) expression were obtained through the best match (Compound CID: 129843460) from the PubChem database (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/) [10].The database resources of SwissTargetPrediction(http://www.swisstargetprediction.ch/),ChEMBL(https://www.ebi.ac.uk/chembl/) and STITCH(http://stitch.embl.de/) were integrated and duplicates were removed to construct a compound - target interaction database [11–13].

Construction of the disease - related target network

LC - related genes were screened through the GeneCards database(https://www.genecards.org/), with a correlation score >10 set as the screening threshold [14].Combined with the OMIM(https://omim.org/), TTD(https://db.idrblab.net/ttd/) databases and domestic and foreign literature [15, 16], the disease - related targets were accurately located. These targets were jointly screened with the targets of DNBaP, and finally 71 potential LC - related targets were determined, and the intersection results were presented in a Venn diagram.

Construction of the Protein - Protein interaction network and screening of core targets

Using the STRING database (https://cn.string-db.org/) and focusing on Homo sapiens with an interaction score exceeding 0.4, a protein - protein interaction network was built from 71 shared targets. The top five core targets were selected using Cytoscape 3.9.0 software by integrating multiple indicators such as degree value, so as to evaluate the significance of these targets in cellular functions and disease mechanisms [17].

Functional and pathway enrichment analysis of target proteins

Functional annotation and pathway enrichment analysis of potential targets were conducted using the GO and KEGG databases [18]. Interpretation of target functions occurred through three dimensions in GO analysis, namely biological process, cellular component, and molecular function. Considering the KEGG database’s strengths in metabolic and signaling pathway coverage, standardized annotation, and toxin ecology research, this database was chosen for pathway enrichment analysis.

Molecular Docking verification of DNBaP and core targets

The 5 core targets were standardized in UniProt(https://www.uniprot.org/) before retrieving their three - dimensional structures from the PDB (https://www.rcsb.org/) database [19, 20]. Following pre - processing via PyMOL 3.1.0, PDBQT files were created with AutoDockTools-1.5.7, and active pockets were identified. Docking Studies were executed using AutoDock Vina 1.1.2 [21]. The conformation exhibiting minimal binding energy alongside maximal clustering frequency was selected as the optimal configuration. Binding sites between DNBaP and core targets were elucidated through visual analysis conducted by Discovery Studio Client v24.1.0.23298 and PyMOL 3.1.0, assessing the stability of the complex along with structural and functional impacts on the protein due to interactions.

Molecular dynamics simulation

This study utilized the optimal binding conformation screened by AutoDock Vina 1.1.2 as the initial model to investigate the interaction mechanisms between DNBaP and lung cancer-related targets, as well as their effects on the structure and function of target proteins. The CHARMM36 force field was employed for protein parameters [22], while ligand topology was generated using sobtop_1.0 with GAFF force field parameters. Using gromacs 2023.2 software, the protein-ligand complex was placed in a cubic box with periodic boundary conditions and solvated with TIP3P water molecules [23]. Ions were added to neutralize the system charge and eliminate net electrostatic interactions, achieving an electrically neutral system. The steepest descent and conjugate gradient methods were subsequently applied to gradually optimize atomic positions and minimize the total energy of the system, thereby eliminating initial structural conflicts. Core conformations of the protein/ligand were constrained to preserve structural integrity and prevent deformation during equilibration. To ensure “environmental authenticity” for subsequent production simulations, NVT and NPT pre-equilibrations were performed. Finally, a 100 ns molecular dynamics simulation was conducted on the complex [24].

To directly evaluate the stability of the DNBaP - protein complex, we used two different free energy landscape diagrams (FELs) constructed by RMSD, Rg and principal components (PC1/PC2) of PCA respectively. Through multi - dimensional complementary analysis, we aimed to comprehensively reveal the interaction mechanism between toxic molecules and biological targets (such as proteins and nucleic acids). At the same time, we constructed a covariance diagram through the principal components (PC1/PC2) of PCA. By analyzing the motion correlation between atoms, we could intuitively reveal the synergistic dynamic behavior of the system and its association with functions or interactions.

Research results

Preliminary toxicity assessment of DNBaP and target screening

In this study, the ProTox and ADMETlab3 platforms were used to conduct a toxicity simulation analysis of DNBaP. The results showed that its active toxicological endpoints were closely related to carcinogenicity and respiratory toxicity. Its 2D and 3D chemical structures were obtained through PubChem (Fig. 1A). By integrating the SwissTargetPrediction, STITCH, and ChEMBL databases, a total of 654 DNBaP exposure-related targets were obtained; combined with the GeneCards, OMIM, and TTD databases, 727 LC-related targets were screened out. Through systematic cross-comparison, 71 overlapping targets were finally determined, and these targets were identified as the key targets for revealing the molecular mechanism of DNBaP-induced LC (Fig. 1B).

Construction of PPI Network, target function and pathway enrichment analysis

Using the STRING database, a PPI network comprising 71 potential targets was established (Fig. 1C). Core targets were identified via Cytoscape based on multiple metrics such as degree value (Table 1), resulting in CDKN2A(1a5e), EGFR(1m14), ESR1(3ert), TNF(1a8m), and TP53(2g3r) (Fig. 1D). GeneCards analysis showed an upregulation trend of these targets in lung cancer. Notably, EGFR exhibited the highest correlation score of 176.04, reflecting its potential critical role in lung cancer progression; TP53 and CDKN2A scored 64.36 and 44.19 respectively, emphasizing the significance of tumor suppressor genes in lung cancer development; ESR1 achieved a score of 27.85, suggesting that the regulation of hormone levels and related receptors may be involved in the occurrence and development of lung cancer. Despite TNF’s lower score of 13.89, molecular dynamics simulations indicated that it can form a stable complex with DNBaP, implying potential regulation of lung cancer through specific mechanisms (Table 3).

Following ranking based on the false discovery rate, visualizing the top 10 enriched terms per category indicated that pathways linked to insulin, such as insulin receptor signaling, and growth factors, including VEGF and HGF receptors, showed substantial enrichment. This implies these pathways might serve as critical regulatory mechanisms. Functions related to cell adhesion and chromosomal regions could connect to processes like cell proliferation and DNA repair. The presence of kinase activity and receptor functions provided further evidence for pathway activation (Fig. 2C and D).

Molecular Docking analysis of DNBaP and LC core targets

Molecular docking analysis explored how DNBaP binds to key LC proteins as shown in Fig. 3. Docking Studies performed on CDKN2A, EGFR, ESR1, TNF and TP53, revealing all targets had binding energies below − 7 kcal/mol as per Table 2, signifying robust interactions. This implies DNBaP exposure could substantially impact the molecular mechanisms of LC. PyMOL processed visualizations confirming intermolecular interactions and binding sites. Two-dimensional interaction studies indicated DNBaP engaged nearby amino acids via van der Waals forces, with hydrophobic residues like Met, Ala, Val, and Leu within the protein’s active site playing a crucial role in stability. CDKN2A, ESR1, and TP53 exhibited ionic bonds, demonstrating strong electrostatic compatibility, which may govern binding affinity and specificity. Variations in these intermolecular forces uncovered DNBaP’s structure-activity relationship when binding to diverse targets, offering structural biology support for its multi-target synergistic carcinogenic mechanism. Molecular dynamics simulation of core targets

Molecular dynamics simulations were performed to confirm the binding affinity of DNBaP with five core targets, incorporating DNBaP and these targets. Conformational stability of proteins and ligands can be assessed by root mean square deviation, reflecting atomic positional changes relative to initial states. Greater stability corresponds to lesser positional change. Simulation system stability was analyzed using RMSD values. In Fig. 4, all complexes involving DNBaP, such as DNBaP-CDKN2A, achieved equilibrium within 90 ns, with RMSD fluctuations limited to around 0.1 nm, demonstrating stable interactions between DNBaP and target proteins post-simulation. It is worth noting that although the RMSD value of the DNBaP-EGFR complex after simulation was relatively high, its value rose rapidly and then remained stable. To investigate this situation, we used VMD for visualization processing and found that there was no deviation or structural change in the binding site of DNBaP-EGFR, but the amino acid sequence segment 979–996 of EGFR was folded, resulting in a change in the spatial conformation, which may be the cause of the relatively high RMSD value. The flexibility of amino acid residues in the protein is reflected by root mean square fluctuation. In Fig. 5, apart from the c-terminal and n-terminal regions, the RMSF values for residues in the DNBaP-EGFR, DNBaP-ESR1, DNBaP-TNF, and DNBaP-TP53 complexes remained relatively low, primarily ranging from 0.1 to 0.5 nm. Conversely, the DNBaP-CDKN2A complex exhibited a higher RMSF value compared to the other four complexes, suggesting increased flexibility. Solvent accessible surface area serves as an essential parameter for assessing protein surface exposure. This simulation analyzed SASA interactions between target proteins and DNBaP, as illustrated in Fig. 6. Findings revealed stable fluctuations in SASA values for the DNBaP-TNF and DNBaP-TP53 complexes, implying no significant expansion or contraction upon DNBaP binding to TNF and TP53. Radius of gyration quantifies overall structural changes and indicates protein compactness; substantial Rg alterations signify system expansion. As depicted in Fig. 7, Rg values decreased following DNBaP binding to CDKN2A and EGFR, demonstrating more compact conformations resulting from these interactions. Furthermore, Rg values for the DNBaP-ESR1, DNBaP-TNF, and DNBaP-TP53 complexes stayed largely consistent during the 100 ns simulation, indicating minimal structural modifications throughout this phase. Additionally, backbone hydrogen bond analysis confirmed stable hydrogen bonds within DNBaP, as shown in Fig. 8.

Through the free energy landscape diagram in Fig. 9, we found that all complexes exhibited a single minimum energy cluster, indicating that after DNBaP bound to different target proteins, it tended to stabilize a dominant conformational state and that the binding to the target proteins was kinetically significantly stable. This result, together with indicators such as RMSD and Rg, supported the conclusion that DNBaP formed a stable complex with the core targets. In addition, the transition state energies (high free energy regions) of different proteins were significantly different, suggesting that DNBaP had different effects on the conformational transition ability of different targets. Finally, for the covariance diagram constructed by the principal components (PC1/PC2) of PCA (Fig. 10), the brighter the color (such as red and yellow), the greater the covariance between atoms and the stronger the movement correlation. The positions of the highlights in each diagram were different, reflecting that when DNBaP interacted with each protein, the key atomic regions of the coordinated movement were different. And the distribution of highlights in some diagrams was diverse, suggesting the specific structural domains or interaction sites of different proteins when interacting with DNBaP. These diagrams revealed the differences in the coordination of atomic movements when DNBaP interacted with different proteins, which was helpful for locating the key action regions and explaining the kinetic characteristics of the intermolecular interactions.

Discussion

Lung cancer, as a complex disease, arises from multi-factor interactions and multi-pathway regulations. Protein-protein interaction network analysis identified five critical targets, including CDKN2A, EGFR, ESR1, TNF, and TP53, which significantly influence tumor formation and gene mutation. Pathway enrichment via KEGG pinpointed key signaling mechanisms tied to LC pathology. Notably, the PI3K-Akt pathway exhibited significant enrichment, highlighting its essential role in controlling cell survival and proliferation, closely linked to malignant behaviors of lung cancer cells [25].

Tumor protein p53 and cyclin-dependent kinase inhibitor 2 A serve as tumor suppressor genes. The CDKN2A gene generates p16INK4a and p14ARF via distinct transcriptional variants, regulating cell cycle arrest and DNA damage repair through the CDK4/6-pRb and MDM2-p53 pathways. TP53 encodes a nuclear phosphoprotein that induces apoptosis or halts the cell cycle under stress conditions, playing a critical role in maintaining genomic integrity and controlling the cell cycle [26]. Functional synergy exists between these two genes. Dual inactivation of TP53/CDKN2A by DNBaP disrupts the transcriptome and DNA methylome, causing cells to fail in responding appropriately to DNA damage, leading to uncontrolled proliferation and tumor initiation [27].

As a transmembrane tyrosine kinase receptor and member of the protein kinase superfamily, epidermal growth factor receptor participates significantly in tumorigenesis, cell proliferation, apoptosis resistance, and chemoresistance [28]. EGFR modulates the MAPK/ERK, PI3K/AKT, and RAS/RAF pathways via ligand-dependent activation, exerting profound effects on cellular behavior [29–31].

In the field of breast cancer research, the association between the estrogen receptor encoded by ESR1 and the drug resistance of hormone receptor-positive metastatic breast cancer has received much attention, and related studies have been extensive [32–34]. This study found ESR1 mutations related to LC, and the endocrine resistance pathway was among the top in the KEGG pathway. The results are consistent with drug resistance, providing a new intervention target for the endocrine treatment of LC. As a versatile cytokine, TNF exhibits intricate functions in immune regulation, inflammation, and tumor development [35]. Activation of NF-κB by environmental and intrinsic factors through inflammatory cytokine gene expression like TNF-α triggers survival and pro-inflammatory genes [36], creating advantageous conditions for tumor cell apoptosis, growth, and dissemination [37].

This study not only systematically analyzed the molecular mechanism by which DNBaP induces LC but also fully demonstrated the unique advantages of network toxicology in the field of toxicity research on environmental pollutants. Compared with traditional toxicology research methods relying on animal models, network toxicology, with the help of bioinformatics, genomics, and big data analysis technologies, can quickly construct a molecular association network between pollutants and diseases, breaking through the limitations of traditional methods. Due to the differences in physiological and genetic backgrounds among species, animal models are difficult to accurately simulate the real response of humans to chemical substances, resulting in deviations in predicting human health risks. At the same time, the high cost and long cycle characteristics of animal experiments greatly limit the range of chemical substances that can be studied, resulting in a lack of sufficient evaluation of many emerging pollutants. Network toxicology, by constructing biological molecular networks, can identify the common toxic mechanisms of different chemical substances, accurately locate key toxic targets, significantly improve the efficiency and comprehensiveness of toxicology research, and help discover the core regulatory nodes affecting human health.

In this study, the molecular dynamics simulation technology played an indispensable role. It can simulate the interaction process between chemical substances and biological targets at the molecular and atomic levels, providing an intuitive basis for in-depth analysis of the action mechanism and dynamic behavior. The organic combination of network toxicology and molecular dynamics simulation technology has greatly improved the depth of toxicology screening and the accuracy of prediction, opening up a new path for the rapid evaluation of the toxicity of many unknown fuels and environmental pollutants.

Although the integrated application of network toxicology and molecular dynamics simulation demonstrates remarkable efficiency, this study still has limitations. All data were derived from in silico simulations, and the conclusions remain speculative. Therefore, targeted experimental validation is essential to confirm the reliability of the results. For instance, after observing DNBaP-induced carcinogenesis in normal lung cells, it would be necessary to verify whether the mutated genes align with those identified in this study and whether inhibiting these genes effectively controls lung cancer progression. Additionally, integrating extensive longitudinal epidemiological data, conducting systematic investigations using animal models, and clarifying core targets and signaling mechanisms are crucial to establishing a solid scientific foundation for developing prevention and control strategies against DNBaP-induced lung cancer. This integrated research approach not only deepens the understanding of health risks associated with fuels and pollutants but also provides precise scientific guidance for addressing emerging environmental health challenges.

Conclusion

This study comprehensively applied network toxicology and molecular docking technologies to systematically analyze the potential toxic mechanism of the fuel environmental pollutant DNBaP. Through bioinformatics analysis, 71 potential targets closely associated with LC were successfully identified, and based on the protein-protein interaction (PPI) network, five major core targets, namely CDKN2A, EGFR, ESR1, TNF, and TP53, were screened out, confirming their key regulatory roles in the process of DNBaP-induced LC.

Through GO and KEGG pathway enrichment analysis, the research uncovered the action mechanisms of the mentioned targets within biological processes, cellular components, molecular functions, and signaling pathways associated with lung cancer development. The results showed that signaling pathways such as PI3K-Akt, MAPK, and RAS were significantly enriched, suggesting that DNBaP may disrupt the homeostasis of cellular signal transduction through multiple signaling pathways, thereby promoting the occurrence of lung cancer. Molecular docking and dynamics simulation results offered direct proof of these mechanisms, as the docking study validated DNBaP’s binding capacity to core targets while the dynamics simulation confirmed the stability and affinity of their interaction, showing that DNBaP induces lung cancer by targeting key proteins.

This research clarified the molecular mechanism of DNBaP-induced LC for the first time. It confirmed the rationality and feasibility of integrating network toxicology, molecular docking, and molecular dynamics simulation in evaluating environmental pollutant toxicity. These findings offer an innovative approach to assessing health risks related to fuels and pollutants while establishing a robust theoretical basis for developing LC prevention strategies. This contributes significantly to advancing environmental toxicology and precision medicine as interdisciplinary domains.

Limitations.

Although this study adopted a systematic and multi-dimensional research approach, several limitations should be considered when interpreting the results. First, all analyses in this study were based on in silico predictions and simulations. While network toxicology, molecular docking, and molecular dynamics simulations are powerful tools for hypothesis generation and mechanism elucidation, the conclusions remain speculative without experimental validation. Due to the lack of in vitro or in vivo data—such as gene expression verification, functional assays, or animal model studies—the molecular mechanisms proposed in this study, as well as the roles of core targets (CDKN2A, EGFR, ESR1, TNF, TP53) in DNBaP-induced lung carcinogenesis, require further confirmation.

Second, the target screening process in this study relied entirely on the integrity and coverage of public databases. Potential biases or incomplete annotations in these databases may have led to the omission of relevant targets or pathways. Finally, although molecular dynamics simulations provided valuable information, their results were limited by the selected force fields and the 100-nanosecond (ns) simulation duration. This timeframe may be insufficient to capture all relevant conformational changes or rare binding events. In addition, the current model did not account for the complex heterogeneity of lung cancer subtypes or the influence of the tumor microenvironment, which may restrict the generalizability of the study’s conclusions across different clinical scenarios. Future research should integrate experimental validation, metabolic profiling, and subtype-specific analyses to further consolidate the findings of this study.
