On the set of atoms and strong atoms in additive monoids of cyclic semidomains
Jiya Dani, Anna Deng, Marly Gotti, Bryan Li, Arav Paladiya, Joseph Vulakh, Jason Zeng

Abstract
. Let M M be a cancellative and commutative monoid. A non-invertible element of M M is called an atom (or irreducible element) if it cannot be factored into two non-invertible elements, while an atom a a of M M is called strong if a n a^{n} has a unique factorization in M M for every n ∈ ℕ n\in\mathbb{N} . The monoid M M is atomic if every non-invertible element factors into finitely many atoms (repetitions allowed). For an algebraic number α \alpha , we let M α M_{\alpha} denote the additive monoid of the subsemiring ℕ 0 ​ [ α ] \mathbb{N}_{0}[\alpha] of ℂ \mathbb{C} . The atomic structure of M α M_{\alpha} reflects intricate interactions between algebraic number theory and additive semigroup theory. For m , n ∈ ℕ 0 ∪ { ∞ } m,n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\cup\{\infty\} (with m ≤ n m\leq n ), the pair ( m , n ) (m,n) is called realizable if there exists an algebraic number α ∈ ℂ \alpha\in\mathbb{C} such that M α M_{\alpha} has m m strong atoms and n n atoms. Our primary goal is to identify classes of realizable pairs with the long-term goal of providing a complete description of the full set of realizable pairs.

1.
Introduction

The study of factorizations in algebraic structures such as commutative monoids, rings, and semirings has become a vibrant area of modern algebra, intersecting with combinatorics
[
1
]
, number theory
[
17
]
, and geometry
[
18
]
. A particularly rich framework for these investigations is provided by additive monoids arising from commutative semidomains
[
4
]
, especially the so-called simple extensions
\mathbb{N}_{0}[\alpha]
of the semidomain
\mathbb{N}_{0}
for all
\alpha\in\mathbb{A}
, where
\mathbb{A}
denotes the set of all algebraic numbers (see
[
2
,
8
]
and references therein). For each
\alpha\in\mathbb{A}
, we denote by
M_{\alpha}
the underlying additive monoid of the semidomain
\mathbb{N}_{0}[\alpha]
. The atomic structure of
M_{\alpha}
is tightly connected to the arithmetic and algebraic properties of the minimal polynomial of
\alpha
, denoted
m_{\alpha}(x)
throughout this paper.

A nonzero element of
M_{\alpha}
is called an
atom
if it cannot be expressed in
M_{\alpha}
as a sum of two nonzero elements. We let
\mathscr{A}(M_{\alpha})
denote the set of all atoms of
M_{\alpha}
. The monoid
M_{\alpha}
is said to be
atomic
if every nonzero element can be written as a sum of finitely many atoms (repetitions allowed). It was shown in
[
8
, Theorem 4.2]
that
M_{\alpha}
is atomic if and only if
1
is an atom; in this case,
\mathscr{A}(M_{\alpha})=\{1,\alpha,\dots,\alpha^{n}\}
for some
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
when
M_{\alpha}
is finitely generated while
\mathscr{A}(M_{\alpha})=\{\alpha^{n}:n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\}
when
M_{\alpha}
is not finitely generated.

An atom
a
in
M_{\alpha}
is called a
strong atom
if, for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
, the only decomposition of
na
as a finite sum of atoms in
M_{\alpha}
is the obvious one, which consists of
n
copies of
a
. The set of all strong atoms of
M_{\alpha}
is denoted by
\mathscr{S}(M_{\alpha})
. It follows from the definition of a strong atom that
\mathscr{S}(M_{\alpha})\subseteq\mathscr{A}(M_{\alpha})
.

A nonzero
p\in M_{\alpha}
is defined to be a
prime
if for all
b,c\in M_{\alpha}
, the condition
b+c\in p+M_{\alpha}
implies that either
b\in p+M_{\alpha}
or
c\in p+M_{\alpha}
. The set of all primes of
M_{\alpha}
is denoted by
\mathscr{P}(M_{\alpha})
. Clearly, every prime in
M_{\alpha}
is a strong atom. It was established in
[
3
]
(CrowdMath 2022) that, for each positive algebraic number
\alpha\in\mathbb{A}
, the monoid
M_{\alpha}
contains prime elements if and only if it is a unique factorization monoid (UFM). In such a case, one has

\mathscr{P}(M_{\alpha})=\mathscr{S}(M_{\alpha})=\mathscr{A}(M_{\alpha})=\{\alpha^{n}:n\in\llbracket 0,d-1\rrbracket\},

where
d
is the degree of the minimal polynomial of
\alpha
. However, as we will see in Example
3.1
and Theorem
3.6
, the monoid
M_{\alpha}
may contain strong atoms even when it is not a UFM.

More generally, the notions of atoms, strong atoms, and primes can be defined for any commutative cancellative monoid
M
analogously to the way we have just defined these notions in
M_{\alpha}
. For such a monoid
M
, we obtain the natural inclusions

(1.1)

\mathscr{P}(M)\subseteq\mathscr{S}(M)\subseteq\mathscr{A}(M),

where
\mathscr{P}(M)
,
\mathscr{S}(M)
, and
\mathscr{A}(M)
denote the sets of primes, strong atoms, and atoms of
M
, respectively. For an integral domain
R
, we let
R^{*}
denote its multiplicative monoid (the multiplicative monoid consisting of all nonzero elements of
R
). In the recent paper
[
12
]
, Fadinger et al. study the chain of inclusions (
1.1
) in the setting of multiplicative monoids of integral domains, providing explicit examples of atomic integral domains that exhibit each of the eight possible combinations regarding the existence or absence of primes, strong atoms that are not atoms, and atoms.

The first systematic study of the atomic and factorization-theoretic structure of the family of monoids
M_{\alpha}
was carried out by Correa-Morris and Gotti in
[
8
]
. Motivated by their investigation, we undertake a detailed analysis of the sets of atoms and the sets of strong atoms in the monoids
M_{\alpha}
with the aim of determining the possible pairs

(|\mathscr{S}(M_{\alpha}))|,|\mathscr{A}(M_{\alpha})|)

as
\alpha
varies over
\mathbb{A}
. Since we have already observed that
M_{\alpha}
is a UFM precisely when it contains primes, we focus our attention on the sets
\mathscr{A}(M_{\alpha})
and
\mathscr{S}(M_{\alpha})
and disregard
\mathscr{P}(M_{\alpha})
in our analysis. We say that a pair
(m,n)\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{2}
is
realizable
if it belongs to the set

\big\{(|\mathscr{S}(M_{\alpha})|,|\mathscr{A}(M_{\alpha})|):\alpha\in\mathbb{A}\big\}.

In this paper, we try to understand which pairs of
\mathbb{N}_{0}^{2}
are realizable. Towards this end, we introduce and analyze the function
f\colon\mathbb{A}\to(\mathbb{N}_{0}\cup\{\infty\})^{2}
defined as follows:

f(\alpha):=(|\mathscr{S}(M_{\alpha})|,|\mathscr{A}(M_{\alpha})|)

for all
\alpha\in\mathbb{A}
. The function
f
describes the number of strong atoms and atoms of the associated monoid
M_{\alpha}
. We identify various realizable and non-realizable pairs, provide constructive methods to produce algebraic numbers
\alpha
for which
f(\alpha)
assumes prescribed values, and examine how transformations of minimal polynomials (such as the substitution
x\mapsto x^{k}
) affect atomic and factorization-theoretic properties. Our results shed new light on the algebraic and combinatorial behavior of the monoids
M_{\alpha}
and establish connections to classical results, including Descartes’ Rule of Signs and Curtiss’ Theorem.

This paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
, we introduce the notation, terminology, and known results that we shall use throughout the paper. Then in Section
4
, we determine the pairs of
\mathbb{N}_{0}^{2}
that are realizable by the algebraic numbers of degree
2
, namely, those algebraic numbers whose minimal polynomials have degree
2
. Finally, in Section
5
, we first prove that for every
(k,c)\in\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{N}_{0}
, the pair
(4k+c,5k+c)
is realizable, and we then prove that the pair
(n,n+1)
is realizable if and only if
n\geq 4
. This last result also illustrates that not every pair
(m,n)\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{2}
with
m\leq n
is a realizable pair.

2.
Background

2.1.
General Notation

Throughout this paper, we let
\mathbb{Z}
,
\mathbb{Q}
,
\mathbb{R}
,
\mathbb{A}
, and
\mathbb{C}
stand, as usual, for the set integers, rational numbers, real numbers, algebraic complex numbers, and complex numbers, respectively. We let
\mathbb{P}
,
\mathbb{N}
, and
\mathbb{N}_{0}
denote the set of standard primes, positive integers, and nonnegative integers, respectively. For any
r,s\in\mathbb{R}
, we let
\llbracket r,s\rrbracket
denote the set of integers between
r
and
s
, that is,

\llbracket r,s\rrbracket:=\{n\in\mathbb{Z}:r\leq n\leq s\}.

Observe that
\llbracket r,s\rrbracket
is empty for any
r,s\in\mathbb{R}
with
r\geq s
. For a subset
S
of the real line, we set
S_{\geq r}:=\{s\in S:s\geq r\}
and
S_{>r}:=\{s\in S:s>r\}
.

2.2.
Commutative Monoids

Although a monoid is conventionally defined to be a semigroup with an identity element, in the scope of this paper we will reserve the term
monoid
to refer to a semigroup that has an identity element and is cancellative and commutative. Monoids here are written additively unless we explicitly state otherwise.

Let
M
be a monoid. The set
\mathscr{U}(M)
consisting of all invertible elements of the monoids
M
is an abelian group that is often referred to as the
group of units
of
M
. When the group
\mathscr{U}(M)
is trivial, the monoid
M
is called
reduced
. The monoids we study in this paper are reduced. The quotient monoid
M/\mathscr{U}(M)
is called the
reduced monoid
of
M
and is denoted by
M_{\text{red}}
: the monoid
M_{\text{red}}
is clearly reduced, and
M\simeq M_{\text{red}}
if and only if
M
is already a reduced monoid.

An element
a\in M\!\setminus\!\mathscr{U}(M)
is called an
atom
if whenever
a=u+v
for some
u,v\in M
, then either
u\in\mathscr{U}(M)
or
v\in\mathscr{U}(M)
. The set consisting of all the atoms of
M
is denoted by
\mathscr{A}(M)
. Following Coykendall, Dobbs, and Mullins
[
9
]
, we say that
M
is
antimatter
provided that
\mathscr{A}(M)
is the empty set. An element of
M
is said to be
atomic
if it is invertible or can be written as a sum of finitely many atoms in
M
(repetitions allowed). Following Cohn
[
7
]
, we say that the monoid
M
is
atomic
if every element of
M
is atomic.

For the rest of this section, assume that
M
is an atomic monoid. An atom
a\in\mathscr{A}(M)
is called a
strong atom
provided that, for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
, the element
na
can be written as a sum of finitely many atoms in only one way, the obvious one. We let
\mathscr{S}(M)
denote the set of strong atoms of
M
. A non-invertible element
p\in M
is called a
prime
if for any
b,c\in M
the fact that
b+c\in p+M
implies that
b\in p+M
or
c\in p+M
. We let
\mathscr{P}(M)
denote the set consisting of all primes of
M
. It is well known and easy to prove that

\mathscr{P}(M)\subseteq\mathscr{S}(M)\subseteq\mathscr{A}(M).

Let
\mathsf{Z}(M)
denote the free commutative monoid on the set of atoms
\mathscr{A}(M_{\text{red}})
, and let
\pi\colon\mathsf{Z}(M)\to M_{\text{red}}
denote the only monoid homomorphism that fixes every element of the set
\mathscr{A}(M_{\text{red}})
. The elements of
\mathsf{Z}(M)
are called
factorizations
. Given a factorization
z:=a_{1}\cdots a_{\ell}\in\mathsf{Z}(M)
for atoms
a_{1},\dots,a_{\ell}\in\mathscr{A}(M_{\text{red}})
, we say that the
length
of
z
is
\ell
and we often denote the length of
z
by
|z|
. For each
b\in M
, we set

\mathsf{Z}_{M}(b):=\pi^{-1}(b+\mathscr{U}(M))\quad\text{and}\quad\mathsf{L}_{M}(b):=\{|z|:z\in\mathsf{Z}(b)\}.

When there seems to be no risk of ambiguity, we write
\mathsf{Z}(b)
and
\mathsf{L}(b)
instead of
\mathsf{Z}_{M}(b)
and
\mathsf{L}_{M}(b)
, respectively. The monoid
M
is called a
unique factorization monoid
(resp.,
finite factorization monoid
) if
|\mathsf{Z}(b)|=1
(resp.,
1\leq|\mathsf{Z}(b)|<\infty
) for every
b\in M
. We will write UFM (resp., FFM) instead of unique factorization monoid (resp., finite factorization monoid). Every UFM is clearly an FFM. We say that
M
is a
bounded factorization monoid
(BFM) if
1\leq|\mathsf{L}(b)|<\infty
for all
b\in M
. It follows directly from the corresponding definitions that every FFM is a BFM.

2.3.
Polynomial Rings and Semirings

Let
R
be an integral domain. Let
f(x)
be a nonzero polynomial in
R[x]
and write
f(x)=\sum_{n=0}^{d}c_{n}x^{n}
for some coefficients
c_{0},\dots,c_{d}\in R
such that
c_{d}\neq 0
. Then the
support
of
f(x)
is the set

\text{supp}\,f(x):=\{k\in\llbracket 0,d\rrbracket:c_{k}\neq 0\},

while
\deg f(x):=d
and
\text{ord}\,f(x):=\min\text{supp}\,f(x)
are called the
degree
and
order
of
f(x)
, respectively. Descartes’ Rule of Signs will be helpful later: it states that the number of variations of sign of a nonzero polynomial
f(x)\in\mathbb{R}[x]
has the same parity as and is at least the number of positive roots of
f(x)
(counting multiplicity). The following theorem is another tool we will use later.

Theorem 2.1
.

[
10
, Section 5]

For each nonzero polynomial
f(x)\in\mathbb{R}[x]
, there exists a nonzero polynomial
\mu(x)\in\mathbb{R}[x]
such that the number of variations of sign of
\mu(x)f(x)
equals the number of positive roots of
f(x)
, counting multiplicity.

Now assume that
f(x)=\sum_{n=0}^{d}c_{n}x^{n}\in\mathbb{Q}[x]
is a nonzero polynomial with
\deg f(x)=d
. Then there exists a unique
r\in\mathbb{Q}_{>0}
such that
rf(x)
is a polynomial in
\mathbb{Z}[x]
with content
1
(i.e., the greatest common divisor of the set of coefficients of
rf(x)
is
1
). Then there exists a unique pair of polynomials
(p(x),q(x))\in\mathbb{N}_{0}[x]
such that
rf(x)=p(x)-q(x)
, called the minimal pair of
f(x)
. Let
\alpha
be a nonzero algebraic number. We often denote the minimal polynomial of
\alpha
by
m_{\alpha}(x)\in\mathbb{Q}[x]
. We refer to the minimal pair of
m_{\alpha}(x)
also as the
minimal pair
of
\alpha
. Recall that the
degree
of
\alpha
is
\deg m_{\alpha}(x)
while the
conjugates
of
\alpha
are the roots of
m_{\alpha}(x)
.

2.4.
The Additive Monoid of the Semidomain
\mathbb{N}_{0}[\alpha]

For any nonempty set
S
and binary operations ‘
+
’ and ‘
\cdot
’ on
S
, we say that the triple
(S,+,\cdot)
is a
semidomain
if the following conditions hold:

•

(S,+)
is a monoid,

•

(S\setminus\{0\},\cdot)
is a monoid, and

•

the distributive law holds:
r(s+t)=rs+rt
for all
r,s,t\in S
.

Let
(S,+,\cdot)
be a semidomain, which we denote simply by
S
. The identity element
0
(resp.,
1
) of
(S,+)
(resp.,
(S\setminus\{0\},\cdot)
) is called the
zero element
(resp.,
identity element
) of
S
. A submonoid
S^{\prime}
of
S
is called a
sub-semidomain
of
S
provided that
1\in S^{\prime}
and
S^{\prime}
is closed under multiplication. It is well known that any semidomain can be naturally extended to an integral domain (i.e., a commutative ring with identity and without nonzero zero-divisors). As for polynomial domains, we let
S[x]
denote the semidomain consisting of all polynomials over
S
. Then, for any
\tau\in\mathbb{C}
, it follows that

\mathbb{N}_{0}[\tau]:=\big\{p(\tau):p(x)\in\mathbb{N}_{0}[x]\big\}.

is a semidomain, which we call the
cyclic semidomain
generated by
\tau
. When
\tau
is transcendental,
\mathbb{N}_{0}[\tau]
is isomorphic to the polynomial semiring
\mathbb{N}_{0}[x]
and so its additive monoid is isomorphic to the free commutative monoid
\bigoplus_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}\mathbb{N}_{0}
. As every free commutative monoid is a UFM, the additive monoids of cyclic semidomains generated by transcendental numbers are rather trivial from the viewpoints of atomicity and factorizations. Thus, without loss of generality, we can restrict our attention to cyclic semidomains
\mathbb{N}_{0}[\alpha]
parameterized by algebraic numbers
\alpha\in\mathbb{A}
. As the underlying additive monoids of the semidomains
\mathbb{N}_{0}[\alpha]
(for any
\alpha\in\mathbb{A}
) are the central algebraic objects we study in this paper, in order to distinguish the former from the later, it is convenient to introduce the following notation.

Notation.
For any
\alpha\in\mathbb{A}
, we let
M_{\alpha}
denote the underlying additive monoid of the cyclic semidomain
\mathbb{N}_{0}[\alpha]
.

For both self-containment and future reference, we conclude this section mentioning some known results on the atomicity and factorization of the monoids
M_{\alpha}
(for any
\alpha\in\mathbb{A}
). We start with a characterization of atomicity.

Theorem 2.2
.

[
8
, Theorem 4.2]

For
\alpha\in\mathbb{A}
, the additive monoid
M_{\alpha}
is atomic if and only if
1\in\mathscr{A}(M_{\alpha})
, in which case, there exists
\sigma\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\cup\{\infty\}
such that

(2.1)

\mathscr{A}(M_{\alpha})=\{\alpha^{n}:n\in[0,\sigma)\cap\mathbb{N}_{0}\}.

Moreover,
\sigma=\min\{n\in\mathbb{N}:\alpha^{n}\in\{\alpha^{j}:j\in\llbracket 0,n-1\rrbracket\}\}
if
M_{\alpha}
is finitely generated (and so atomic).

Here we have a sufficient condition for the atomicity of the studied monoids.

Proposition 2.3
.

[
3
, Proposition 4.5]

Let
\alpha
be a positive algebraic number with minimal polynomial polynomial
m_{\alpha}(X)
. The monoid
M_{\alpha}
is atomic with infinitely many atoms if
m_{\alpha}(x)
has a positive root different from
\alpha
.

We conclude with a characterization of the monoids
M_{\alpha}
that are UFMs.

Theorem 2.4
.

[
8
, Theorem 5.4]

Let
\alpha
be an algebraic number with degree
d
, minimal polynomial
m_{\alpha}(x)\in\mathbb{Q}[x]
, and minimal pair
(p(x),q(x))\in\mathbb{N}_{0}[x]^{2}
. Then the following conditions are equivalent.

(a)

M_{\alpha}
is a UFM.

(b)

\deg m_{\alpha}(x)=|\mathscr{A}(M_{\alpha})|
.

(c)

p(x)=x^{d}
for some
d\in\mathbb{N}
.

Moreover, if
M_{\alpha}
is a UFM, then it is finitely generated.

3.
Realization of the Sizes of the Sets of Atoms and Strong Atoms

3.1.
Atoms and Strong Atoms

Fix an algebraic number
\alpha
. It was proved in
[
3
]
(CrowdMath 2022) that we can take
m\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\cup\{\infty\}
such that the set of strong atoms of
M_{\alpha}
is

(3.1)

\mathscr{S}(M_{\alpha})=\big\{\alpha^{k}:k\in[0,m)\cap\mathbb{N}_{0}\big\}.

Thus, when
M_{\alpha}
has infinitely many strong atoms,
\mathscr{S}(M_{\alpha})=\mathscr{A}(M_{\alpha})
. This is not necessarily the case when
M_{\alpha}
has only finitely many strong atoms. The following simple example (also worked out in CrowdMath 2022) shows a monoid
M_{\alpha}
with infinitely many atoms and only one strong atom.

Example 3.1
.

Consider the polynomial
m(x):=x^{2}-3x+1\in\mathbb{Z}[x]
. Observe that
m(x)
is an irreducible polynomial over
\mathbb{Q}
such that
m(0)=1
and
m(1)=-1
. Thus,
m(x)
has two positive roots: one root
\alpha\in(0,1)
and another root
\beta>1
. By
Proposition

2.3
,
M_{\alpha}
is atomic with infinitely many atoms, so
\mathscr{A}(M_{\alpha})=\{\alpha^{n}:n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\}
. Since
3\alpha=1+\alpha^{2}
, it follows that
\alpha
is not a strong atom, and so the inclusion
\alpha M_{\alpha}\subseteq M_{\alpha}
ensures that
\alpha^{n}
cannot be a strong atom for any
n\geq 1
. On the other hand, we claim that
1
is a strong atom. For some
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
c_{1},\dots,c_{N}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, set
g(x):=\big(\sum_{i=1}^{N}c_{i}x^{i}\big)-n
, and observe that
g(x)
has only one variation of sign, so by Descartes’ Rule of Signs,
g(x)
has one positive real root. However, if
g(\alpha)=0
such that
n\cdot 1=\sum_{i=1}^{N}c_{i}\alpha^{i}
, we would need
g(x)
to be divisible by
m(x)
and have at least two distinct positive real roots. Thus,
\mathscr{S}(M_{\alpha})=\{1\}
.

As our primary purpose is to determine the pairs
(m,n)\in(\mathbb{N}_{0}^{2}\cup\{\infty\})^{2}
for which there exists
\alpha\in\mathbb{A}
such that the monoid
M_{\alpha}
has exactly
m
strong atoms and
n
atoms, it is convenient to formally introduce the following function:

(3.2)

f\colon\mathbb{A}\to\big\{(m,n)\in(\mathbb{N}_{0}\cup\{\infty\})^{2}:m\leq n\big\},

defined as

f(\alpha):=\big(|\mathscr{S}(M_{\alpha})|,|\mathscr{A}(M_{\alpha})|\big)

for all
\alpha\in\mathbb{A}
. We are mostly interested here in determining the image of the function
f
. Thus, let us introduce the following terminology.

Definition 3.2
.

We call the function
f
in (
3.2
) the
realization function
and, for any
m,n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\cup\{\infty\}
, we call the pair
(m,n)
a
realizable pair
if
(m,n)\in f(\mathbb{A})
.

Observe that in light of Example
3.1
, there exists
\alpha\in\mathbb{A}
such that
f(\alpha)=(1,\infty)
. As a result of the main theorem of the next section, we obtain infinitely many
\alpha\in\mathbb{A}
such that
f(\alpha)=(0,\infty)
.

The following lemma will be helpful later in characterizing the number of strong atoms of the monoid
M_{\alpha}
.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
\alpha
be an algebraic number with minimal polynomial
m_{\alpha}(x)
. If
M_{\alpha}
is finitely generated and
1\leq|\mathscr{S}(M_{\alpha})|<|\mathscr{A}(M_{\alpha})|
, then there exists a polynomial
f(x)\in\mathbb{Q}[x]
of degree
|\mathscr{S}(M_{\alpha})|-\deg m_{\alpha}(x)
so that
f(x)m_{\alpha}(x)
has negative leading coefficient and all other coefficients nonnegative.

Proof.

Set
m:=|\mathscr{S}(M_{\alpha})|
and
n:=|\mathscr{A}(M_{\alpha})|
. It suffices to show that the equality
\alpha^{m}=\sum_{i=0}^{m-1}q_{i}\alpha^{i}
holds for some
q_{0},\dots,q_{m-1}\in\mathbb{Q}_{\geq 0}
. Indeed, if
\ell
is a common multiple of the denominators
\mathsf{d}(q_{0}),\dots,\mathsf{d}(q_{m-1})
, then the polynomial

\ell x^{m}-\sum_{i=0}^{m-1}\ell q_{i}x^{i}\in\mathbb{Z}[x]

will have
\alpha
as a root and, therefore, be divisible by
m_{\alpha}(x)
. Observe that because
m\leq n-1
, the atom
\alpha^{n-1}
is not a strong atom, so we can write
\alpha^{n-1}
as
\sum_{i=0}^{n-2}q_{n-1,i}\alpha^{i}
, where each
q_{n-1,i}\in\mathbb{Q}_{\geq 0}
.

Now we prove by induction that for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
such that
k\in\llbracket m,n-1\rrbracket
, we can write
\alpha^{k}
as a linear combination of the elements
\alpha^{i}
for
i\in\llbracket 0,k-1\rrbracket
. We have proved the base case of
k=n-1
, so fix
m\leq k<n-1
and assume that for all
j
where
k<j<n
, we have
\alpha^{j}
equal to
\sum_{i=0}^{j-1}q_{j,i}\alpha^{i}
, where
q_{j,i}\in\mathbb{Q}_{\geq 0}
. Denote this summation with
z_{j}
. Because
\alpha
is not a root of any polynomial in
\mathbb{Q}[x]
with degree
m\geq 1
, at least two coefficients
q_{j,i}
are nonzero for each
j
. Now let
\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}c_{i}\alpha^{i}
be a nontrivial factorization of
c\alpha^{k}
for some large enough
c\in\mathbb{N}
, which must exist because
\alpha^{k}
is not a strong atom. Going from the
\alpha^{n-1}
term to the
\alpha^{k+1}
term, we can successively rewrite the
\alpha^{i}
in each term as
z_{i}
for
k<i<n
from our assumption. We then obtain a new representation
c\alpha^{k}=\sum_{i=0}^{k}q_{k,i}\alpha^{i}
, where, since each
z_{i}
has at least two nonzero coefficients, at least two
q_{k,i}
are nonzero. We can then assume that
q_{k,k}=0
by subtracting an appropriate rational multiple of
\alpha^{k}
from both sides. This completes the induction step. Hence we can write
\alpha^{m}=\sum_{i=0}^{m-1}q_{i}\alpha^{i}
, as desired.
∎

As direct consequences of the previous proposition, we obtain the following corollaries, which will be useful later.

3.2.
Binomial Minimal Polynomials

It was proved in
[
16
, Theorem 6.2]
that, for each
q\in\mathbb{Q}_{>0}\setminus\mathbb{N}^{\pm 1}
, the monoid
M_{q}
is atomic with
|\mathscr{A}(M_{q})|=\infty
. This result was generalized in
[
2
, Proposition 3.9]
for any positive irreducible
n
-th root of any
q\in\mathbb{Q}_{>0}\setminus\mathbb{N}^{\pm 1}
(for
q\in\mathbb{Q}_{>0}
, an
irreducible root
of
q
is an
n
-th root
\alpha:=\sqrt[n]{q}
of
q
such that
\alpha,\dots,\alpha^{n-1}
are all irrationals). It is not difficult to argue that the polynomial
x^{n}-q
is irreducible in
\mathbb{Q}[x]
for any pair
(n,q)\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 2}\times\mathbb{Q}_{>0}
such that
\sqrt[n]{q}
is an irreducible
n
-th root of
q
(see
[
22
, page 297]
). Let us establish a similar result for the set of strong atoms.

Theorem 3.6
.

For
q\in\mathbb{Q}_{>0}\setminus\mathbb{N}^{\pm 1}
, let
\alpha
be a positive irreducible
n
-th root
q
. Then the monoid
M_{\alpha}
is atomic with
\mathscr{A}(M_{\alpha})=\{\alpha^{n}:n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\}
and
\mathscr{S}(M_{\alpha})=\emptyset
.

Proof.

As shown in
[
22
, page 297]
, if
q=\frac{a}{b}
for relatively prime
a,b\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 2}
, the polynomial
x^{n}-\frac{a}{b}
is irreducible in
\mathbb{Q}[x]
. Thus, the polynomial
bx^{n}-a
is irreducible in
\mathbb{Z}[x]
. From
[
2
, Proposition 3.9]
, the set of atoms is
\mathscr{A}(M_{\alpha})=\{\alpha^{n}:n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\}
, and so
M_{\alpha}
is an atomic monoid. Now observe that, for each
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, the equality
a\cdot\alpha^{k}=b\cdot\alpha^{n+k}
holds, and so the atom
\alpha^{k}\in M_{\alpha}
cannot be a strong atom of
M_{\alpha}
. As a consequence, we conclude that the set of strong atoms of
M_{\alpha}
is empty.
∎

Corollary 3.7
.

There exists
\alpha\in\mathbb{A}
such that
f(\alpha)=(0,\infty)
.

In the following example we exhibit a class of monoids
M_{\alpha}
with infinitely many strong atoms, which implies that the pair
(\infty,\infty)
is realizable.

Proposition 3.8
.

Let
\alpha
be a positive algebraic number with exactly three positive conjugates (including
\alpha
itself). Then
f(\alpha)=(\infty,\infty)
.

Proof.

Define
m(x)
as the minimal polynomial of
\alpha
. Since
m(x)
has
3
positive roots,
M_{\alpha}
is atomic with infinitely many atoms by
Proposition

2.3
. Assume, for the sake of contradiction, that some
\alpha^{\ell}
is not a strong atom in
M_{\alpha}
. Then
\alpha
must be a root of some polynomial
q(x)=\sum_{i=0}^{N}c_{i}x^{i}
, where
c_{\ell}\leq-1
, and every other
c_{i}
is nonnegative. Observe that
q(x)
has at most
2
changes in sign, so it has at most two positive real roots by Descartes’ Rule of Signs. However, this contradicts that
q(x)
is a multiple of
m(x)
, which has three positive real roots. Thus, every power of
\alpha
is a strong atom, and so
f(\alpha)=(\infty,\infty)
.
∎

4.
Degree-2 Minimal Polynomials

Let
\mathbb{A}_{2}
be the subset of
\mathbb{C}
consisting of all algebraic numbers with minimal polynomials of degree
2
. In this section, we determine
f(\mathbb{A}_{2})
by considering cases regarding the form of minimal polynomials of degree
2
.

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
\alpha
be an algebraic number of degree
2
, and let
m(x)
be the only primitive polynomial that is a scalar multiple of the minimal polynomial of
\alpha
. Then the following statements hold.

(1)

If
m(x)\in\mathbb{N}_{0}[x]
, then
f(\alpha)=(0,0)
.

(2)

If
m(x):=ax^{2}+bx-c\in\mathbb{Z}[x]
for some
a,b,c\in\mathbb{N}
with
\sqrt{b^{2}+4ac}\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\mathbb{Q}
, then
f(\alpha)\in\{(0,0),(0,\infty)\}
.

(3)

If
m(x):=ax^{2}-bx+c\in\mathbb{Z}[x]
for some
a,b,c\in\mathbb{N}
with
\sqrt{b^{2}-4ac}\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\mathbb{Q}
, then
f(\alpha)=(1,\infty)
.

(4)

If
m(x):=ax^{2}-bx-c\in\mathbb{Z}[x]
for some
a,b,c\in\mathbb{N}
with
\sqrt{b^{2}+4ac}\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\mathbb{Q}
, then
f(\alpha)\in\{(2,2),(2,\infty)\}
.

Proof.

(1) If
m(x)\in\mathbb{N}_{0}[x]
, then
\mathbb{N}_{0}[\alpha]=\mathbb{Z}^{2}\times\mathbb{Z}/a
,
where
a
is the leading coefficient of
m(x)
.
In this case,
M_{\alpha}
is an abelian group, and thus an antimatter monoid. As a consequence,
\mathscr{A}(M_{\alpha})
and
\mathscr{S}(M_{\alpha})
are both empty sets, which means that
f(\alpha)=(0,0)
.

(2) If
c=1
, then
1=ax^{2}+bx
and so
1\in M_{\alpha}
is not an atom. Consequently, no elements of the generating set are atoms. In this case,
M_{\alpha}
is antimatter, so
f(\alpha)=(0,0)
. Now assume that
c>1
. Then
c\cdot 1
can be written as
a\cdot\alpha^{2}+b\cdot\alpha
, and so the inclusion
1M_{\alpha}\subseteq M_{\alpha}
ensures that
\alpha^{n}
cannot be a strong atom for any
n\geq 0
. Hence
M_{\alpha}
contains no strong atoms. Let us argue now that the equality
\mathscr{A}(M_{\alpha})=\{\alpha^{n}:n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\}
holds.

Suppose, for the sake of contradiction, that
M_{\alpha}
has finitely many atoms, and then set
n:=|\mathscr{A}(M_{\alpha})|
. Therefore we can write
\alpha^{n}=\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}p_{i}\alpha^{i}
for some coefficients
p_{0},\dots,p_{n-1}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
. This assumption implies that
m(x)
divides
x^{n}-\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}p_{i}x^{i}
in
\mathbb{Z}[x]
, which in turn implies that
a=1
. Let
x^{n-2}+\sum_{i=0}^{n-3}q_{i}x^{i}
for
q_{0},\dots,q_{n-3}\in\mathbb{Z}
be the quotient of the polynomial division such that

x^{n}-\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}p_{i}x^{i}=(x^{2}+bx-c)\left(x^{n-2}+\sum_{i=0}^{n-3}q_{i}x^{i}\right).

We can assume, without loss of generality, that
q_{0}\neq 0
as, otherwise, we can divide out a factor of
x
. Since
p_{0}\geq 0
, we must have
cq_{0}=p_{0}
, meaning that
q_{0}
is positive. Since
p_{1}\geq 0
, we have
cq_{1}-bq_{0}>0
, which also implies that
q_{1}
is positive. Furthermore, we have the following relations:

\displaystyle p_{2}=cq_{2}-bq_{1}-q_{0}

\displaystyle\geq 0

\displaystyle p_{3}=cq_{3}-bq_{2}-q_{1}

\displaystyle\geq 0

\displaystyle p_{n-3}=cq_{n-3}-bq_{n-4}-q_{n-5}

\displaystyle\geq 0.

If
q_{i}
and
q_{i+1}
are positive, and we have the relation
cq_{i+2}-bq_{i+1}-q_{i}\geq 0
, then
q_{i+2}
must be positive. Hence, by induction we have
q_{n-3}>0
. However, we also have the relation
p_{n-1}=-b-q_{n-3}\geq 0
, which implies
q_{n-3}<0
. This gives a contradiction. Therefore
f(\alpha)=(0,\infty)
in this case.

(3) If
b=1
, then it is impossible for
\sqrt{b^{2}-4ac}
to be real. Now assume that
b>1
. Then the roots of
m(x)
are
\frac{1}{2a}(b\pm\sqrt{b^{2}-4ac})
which are both positive. By
Proposition

2.3
,
M_{\alpha}
is atomic with infinitely many atoms. Because
b\cdot\alpha=a\cdot\alpha^{2}+c
,
\alpha
is not a strong atom, and the inclusion
\alpha M_{\alpha}\subseteq M_{\alpha}
ensures that
\alpha^{n}
cannot be a strong atom for any
n\geq 1
. On the other hand, we claim that
1
is a strong atom. Suppose that this is not the case, and take
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
c_{1},\dots,c_{N}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
such that
n\cdot 1=\sum_{i=1}^{N}c_{i}\alpha^{i}
. Now set

g(x):=\bigg(\sum_{i=1}^{N}c_{i}x^{i}\bigg)-n,

and observe that
g(x)
has at least two distinct positive real roots because it is divisible by
m(x)
, which has two distinct positive real roots. However, this contradicts the statement of the Descartes’ Rule of Signs as
g(x)
has only one variation of sign. Hence
\mathscr{S}(M_{\alpha})=\{1\}
, so
f(\alpha)=(1,\infty)
.

(4) Since
a\cdot\alpha^{2}=b\cdot\alpha+c
, the inclusion
\alpha^{2}M_{\alpha}\subseteq M_{\alpha}
ensures that
\alpha^{i}
cannot be a strong atom for any
i\geq 2
. We claim that
\mathscr{S}(M_{\alpha})=\{1,\alpha\}
. Suppose that
\alpha
is not a strong atom. Then
m(x)
divides a polynomial
f(x)
of the form
c_{0}-c_{1}x+\sum_{i=2}^{n}c_{i}x^{i}
for some
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
c_{i}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
.
Let
\sum_{i=0}^{N-2}d_{i}x^{i}
for
d_{0}
, …,
d_{N-2}\in\mathbb{Z}

be the quotient of the polynomial division such that

c_{0}-c_{1}x+\sum_{i=2}^{n}c_{i}x^{i}=(ax^{2}-bx-c)\sum_{i=0}^{n-2}d_{i}x^{i}.

Since
-cd_{0}=c_{0}>0
, we must have
d_{0}<0
.
Also,
-c_{1}=-bd_{0}-cd_{1}
,
and since
-c_{1}<0
and
-bd_{0},c>0
, we must have
d_{1}>0
.
We also have

\displaystyle c_{2}=-cd_{2}-bd_{1}+ad_{0}

\displaystyle\geq 0

\displaystyle c_{2}=-cd_{3}-bd_{2}+ad_{1}

\displaystyle\geq 0

\displaystyle c_{n-2}=-cd_{n-2}-bd_{n-3}+ad_{n-4}

\displaystyle\geq 0.

We claim that for each
i\in\llbracket 0,n-3\rrbracket
, at least one of
d_{i}
and
d_{i+1}
is negative.
The statement is true for
i=0
, so we proceed by induction. Assume the statement is true for some
i<n-3
. If
d_{i+1}<0
, then the statement automatically is true for
i+1
. Otherwise, we have
d_{i}<0
and
d_{i+1}\geq 0
, in which case
c_{i+2}=-cd_{i+2}-bd_{i+1}+ad_{i}\geq 0

and
-bd_{i+1}\leq 0
,
ad_{i}<0
, which forces
d_{i+2}<0
, completing the induction.
Now
d_{n-2}
must be positive, as
c_{n}=ad_{n-2}
. Thus,
d_{n-3}
is negative,
so
c_{n-1}=ad_{n-3}-bd_{n-2}>0
gives a contradiction. Therefore
\alpha
is a strong atom as desired, and so
1
is as well, proving that
|\mathscr{S}(M_{\alpha})|=2
.

We now determine the possible values of
|\mathscr{A}(S)|
.
If
a=1,
then
\alpha^{2}=b\cdot\alpha+c
holds, so
\alpha^{2}
is not an atom, and the inclusion
\alpha^{2}M_{\alpha}\subseteq M_{\alpha}
ensures that
\alpha^{n}
cannot be an atom for any
n\geq 2
. The set of atoms is thus
\{1,\alpha\}
, as otherwise
m(x)
would not be minimal.

Now assume
a>1
. We claim that
\mathscr{A}(M_{\alpha})=\{\alpha^{n}:n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\}
. To prove this assume, for the sake of contradiction, that
\alpha^{n}
is not an atom. By Gauss’s Lemma, if
m(x)
divides a polynomial of the form
x^{n}-\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}c_{i}x^{i}
for some
c_{0},\dots,c_{N-1}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, then
a=1
, which is a contradiction. Hence
f(\alpha)\in\{(2,2),(2,\infty)\}
, concluding the proof.
∎

5.
Further Realizable Pairs

In this section, we determine other pairs that belong to the image of our main function
f
. Let us start with the following proposition.

Proposition 5.1
.

Let
\alpha
be an algebraic number with minimal polynomial
m(x)\in\mathbb{Q}[x]
such that
m(x^{k})
is irreducible in
\mathbb{Q}[x]
for some
k\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq 2}
. If
\beta
is a root of
m(x^{k})
, then
f(\beta)=kf(\alpha)
.

Proof.

Fix
k\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq 2}
such that the polynomial
m(x^{k})
is irreducible in
\mathbb{Q}[x]
, and let
\beta
be a root of
m(x^{k})
. As
m(x^{k})
is monic and irreducible, it must be the minimal polynomial of
\beta
. We are done once we establish the following two equalities:

|\mathscr{A}(M_{\beta})|=k|\mathscr{A}(M_{\alpha})|\quad\text{ and }\quad|\mathscr{S}(M_{\beta})|=k|\mathscr{S}(M_{\alpha})|.

To argue the first of these two equalities, take
g(x)\in\mathbb{Z}[x]
to be the monic polynomial with minimal degree such that its non-leading coefficients are negative and
m(x)q(x)=g(x)
for some
q(x)\in\mathbb{Z}[x]
. Then
\text{deg }g(x)=|\mathscr{A}(M_{\alpha})|
. Since
m(x^{k})q(x^{k})=g(x^{k})
also holds, and
g(x^{k})
satisfies the condition that its leading coefficient is positive while the rest of its coefficients are negative, we have that

\text{deg }g(x^{k})=k|\mathscr{A}(M_{\alpha})|\geq|\mathscr{A}(M_{\beta})|.

In order to show that equality holds, we will show that
g(x^{k})
has minimal degree among polynomials with this property.

Suppose, for the sake of a contradiction, that the degree of
g(x^{k})
is not minimal among such polynomials, and let
h(x)\in\mathbb{Z}[x]
be such a polynomial of minimal degree. Then, we can write
m(x^{k})b(x)=h(x)
for some
b(x)\in\mathbb{Z}[x]
such that
\deg b(x)<\deg q(x^{k})
. Let us argue the following claim.

Claim.

k
is a common divisor of
\text{supp }b(x)
.

Proof of Claim.
Suppose that
\text{supp }b(x)
had two distinct elements mod
k
. For each
e\in\llbracket 0,k-1\rrbracket
, we let
h_{e}(x)
denote the sum of the terms of
h(x)
with degree
e
mod
k
. Since
\text{supp }b(x)
has two distinct elements modulo
k
, we see that
h_{a}(x),h_{b}(x)\neq 0
for some distinct
a,b
. Since
\text{supp }m(x^{k})
only consists of multiples of
k
, the polynomial
m(x^{k})
divides both
h_{a}(x)
and
h_{b}(x)
. We know that
h(x)
only has one positive coefficient, meaning one of
h_{a}(x)
and
h_{b}(x)
must have all of its coefficients being negative. However,
m(x^{k})
has a positive real root, which gives a contradiction by Descartes’ Rule of Signs. Therefore all the positive integers in
\text{supp }b(x)
are equal modulo
k
. Since
0\in\text{supp }b(x)
for
h(x)
to be minimal, we see that
\text{supp }b(x)\subset k\mathbb{N}_{0}
. The claim is then established.

In light of the proved claim, we can write
b(x)=b_{1}(x^{k})
and
h(x)=h_{1}(x^{k})
where
b_{1}(x),h_{1}(x)\in\mathbb{Z}[x]
. Therefore
m(x^{k})b_{1}(x^{k})=h_{1}(x^{k})
, and so
m(x)b_{1}(x)=h_{1}(x)
. This, along with the inequality
\deg h(x)<\deg g(x^{k})
, implies that
\deg h_{1}(x)<\deg g(x)
. However, this last inequality contradicts the minimality of
g(x)
. As a consequence,
|\mathscr{A}(M_{\beta})|=\deg g(x^{k})=k\deg g(x)=k|\mathscr{A}(M_{\alpha})|
, as desired.

Establishing the equality
|\mathscr{S}(M_{\beta})|=k|\mathscr{S}(M_{\alpha})|
is quite similar. First, let
g(x)
be a polynomial in
\mathbb{Z}[x]
with exactly one negative coefficient such that the degree
d
of the term with the negative coefficient is minimal, and
m(x)q(x)=g(x)
for some
q(x)\in\mathbb{Z}[x]
. Then, for the sake of contradiction, we let
h(x)
be a polynomial satisfying the same condition for
m(x^{k})
, where the degree of the negative term is less than
k\deg g(x)
, and write
m(x^{k})b(x)=h(x)
for some polynomial
b(x)\in\mathbb{Z}[x]
. Since
h(x)
only has one negative coefficient, we can follow as we did previously to obtain that all the terms of
\text{supp }b(x)
are divisible by
k
. Then we can write
b(x)=b_{1}(x^{k})
and
h(x)=h_{1}(x^{k})
, giving
m(x)b_{1}(x)=h_{1}(x)
. However,
h_{1}(x)
has its negative coefficient term with degree less than
d
, which contradicts the minimality of
g(x)
. Hence the equality
|\mathscr{S}(M_{\beta})|=k|\mathscr{S}(M_{\alpha})|
also holds, which concludes our proof.
∎

Observe that the hypothesis in the statement of Proposition
5.1
is guaranteed to hold if the minimal polynomial
m(x)
satisfies Eisenstein’s criterion, whence we obtain the following corollary.

Corollary 5.2
.

Let
\alpha
be an algebraic number with minimal polynomial
m(x)\in\mathbb{Z}[x]
satisfying the hypothesis of Eisenstein’s criterion. Then, for every
k\in\mathbb{N}
, the polynomial
m(x^{k})
is irreducible in
\mathbb{Q}[x]
and
f(\beta)=kf(\alpha)
for any root
\beta
of
m(x^{k})
.

As an immediate consequence of Proposition
5.1
, we also obtain the following realization result.

Corollary 5.3
.

If
f(\alpha)=(m,n)
, then the pair
(cm,cn)
is realizable for every
c\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
such that
m_{\alpha}(x^{c})
is irreducible.

Next, we prove the following realization result.

Theorem 5.4
.

The pair
(4k+c,5k+c)
is realizable for all
(k,c)\in\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{N}_{0}
.

Proof.

For the case when
c=0
, we claim that for the positive root
\alpha
of
m(x)=x^{3}-8x^{2}+4x-2
, we have
f(\alpha)=(4,5)
. Since
m(x)
satisfies Eisenstein’s criterion, it will follow from Corollary
5.2
that the pair
(4k,5k)
is realizable for any
k\in\mathbb{N}
. To see that
|\mathscr{A}(M_{\alpha})|=5
, note that

(x^{2}+2x+1)m(x)=x^{5}-6x^{4}-11x^{3}-2x^{2}-2,

so
|\mathscr{A}(M_{\alpha})|\leq 5
. By Corollary
3.5
,
|\mathscr{A}(M_{\alpha})|\geq 3
, and clearly
|\mathscr{A}(M_{\alpha})|\neq 3
as
m(x)
has multiple negative coefficients. It remains to show that
|\mathscr{A}(M_{\alpha})|\neq 4
.

Suppose, for the sake of a contradiction, that there are
4
atoms. Then there must exist some linear polynomial
f(x)=bx+a\in\mathbb{Z}[x]
such that
f(x)m(x)
has a leading coefficient
1
and all other coefficients non-positive. Multiplying, we get that

f(x)m(x)=bx^{4}+(a-8b)x^{3}+(4b-8a)x^{2}+(4a-2b)x-2a.

Note that we must have
b=1
, and also
4b-8a\leq 0
and
4a-2b\leq 0
imply that
b-2a=0
. Thus, clearly
a
is not an integer, so we reach a contradiction, proving that indeed
|\mathscr{A}(M_{\alpha})|=5
. To show that
|\mathscr{S}(M_{\alpha})|=4
, note that due to Corollary
3.5
, we have
|\mathscr{S}(M_{\alpha})|\geq 3
, and clearly there are more than three strong atoms as
m(x)
has multiple negative coefficients. Note that
(2x+1)m(x)=2x^{4}-15x^{3}-2
, which satisfies the conditions of Corollary
3.4
, so
|\mathscr{S}(M_{\alpha})|=4
.

We now consider the case when
c>0
. Fix
(k,c)\in\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{N}
, and let us show that the
(4k+c,5k+c)
belongs to the image of
f
. To do so, first observe that the polynomial

m(x)=x^{3k+c}-8x^{2k+c}+4x^{k+c}-2x^{c}-2

is irreducible in
\mathbb{Z}[x]
by Eisenstein’s criterion at the ideal
2\mathbb{Z}
and, therefore, in
\mathbb{Q}[x]
by virtue of Gauss’s lemma. In addition,
m(x)
has a positive root
\alpha
as
m(0)<0
. We proceed to show that

Similarly, for the atoms, suppose for the sake of a contradiction that
\alpha^{5k+c-1}

\displaystyle m(x)q_{d}(x)

\displaystyle=-2a_{d}x^{d}-2a_{d}x^{d+c}-2b_{d}x^{d+k}+(4a_{d}-2b_{d})x^{k+c+d}

\displaystyle+(4b_{d}-8a_{d})x^{2k+c+d}+(a_{d}-8b_{d})x^{3k+c+d}+b_{d}x^{4k+c+d}.

Proposition 5.5
