ABSTRACT
Despite considerable progress in prevention, treatment, and eradication efforts, malaria continues to impose a significant public health and socioeconomic burden for many countries around the globe. With the effectiveness of current treatment options partially or totally compromised due to resistance, the validation of new targets and the development of new drugs that can reach the demanding standards required for the treatment of this disease represent an urgent and considerable challenge. Herein, we report the evaluation of approximately 48 200-compound library selected from the GSK collection against PfGCN5. From the 6 initial hits with pIC50 > 5, two compounds were progressed for further in vitro profiling and optimization. Computational and medicinal chemistry efforts delivered significantly improved compounds 21h and 41d with pIC50 within the submicromolar range (pIC50 6.6 and 7.0, respectively), but unfortunately, the lead compounds did not show potent in vitro antiparasitic activity. Although further studies are still necessary, our initial data suggest that targeting PfGCN5 to develop new antimalarial compounds might be challenging.

Introduction

Malaria remains a significant global health challenge. Despite considerable progress in prevention, treatment, and control efforts, this disease continues to impose a heavy burden on public health and socioeconomic development, particularly in regions where the parasites responsible for the disease are endemic. Five species of the Plasmodium genus are known to be responsible for human infections, P. falciparum, P. vivax, P. ovale, P. malariae, and P. knowlesi. Among them, P. falciparum causes the most severe cases of the disease, especially in children under the age of five, who are among the most vulnerable groups. Despite P. vivax being less virulent, it still poses an important threat. Its milder symptoms favor propagation, while undetectable hypnozoites and relapses complicate control and eradication efforts.

Historically, the main treatment for malaria relied heavily on traditional drugs, but their effectiveness has now been completely or partially compromised because of resistance. Artemisinin-based combination therapies (ACTs), unlike other drugs, remain relatively effective against uncomplicated malaria infections. However, the emergence and spread of drug resistance to various partner components (lumefantrine, artemether, etc.) have started to challenge the efficacy of these treatments in many regions, highlighting the need for alternative approaches. The emergence of insecticide resistance affecting vector control and drug-resistant strains of Plasmodium, together with limitations in existing treatment options, accentuates the urgent need for innovative therapeutic strategies.

Over the past decades, partnerships between public, private, academic, and philanthropic sectors , have been directed toward the discovery and development of new antimalarial treatments and the identification of novel targets. , The characterization and validation of new targets, the elevated costs and low success rate in the progression of new drugs, together with the advances in computational techniques have driven a shift from more traditional high-throughput phenotypic screening toward target-based approaches. This approach facilitates target selection and validation, hit optimization, and progression among other aspects. Consortia as MalDA (Malaria Drug Accelerator) have coordinated efforts to avoid duplicities, prioritize progressable targets, and accelerate the discovery and development of novel molecules able to meet the demanding target product profile required for new antimalarial drugs. These endeavors have yielded valuable insights into the biology of the parasite and the mechanisms underlying its survival and pathogenesis and have identified new molecular targets essential for the discovery and rational design of next-generation antimalarial drugs, such as cytoplasmic isoleucine–tRNA ligase (cIRS) and farnesyl pyrophosphate–geranylgeranyl diphosphate synthase (FPPS–GGPPS).

One of the new targets that has been explored is the histone acetyltransferase (HAT), GCN5 (General Control Nonrepressed Protein 5). The complex lifecycle of apicomplexan parasites requires an intricate regulation of transcription as they interact with the host and mosquito vector. HAT enzymes are evolutionarily conserved large multimeric protein complexes such as the 20-subunits SAGA (Spt-Ada-Gcn5 acetyltransferase) complex, which bears GCN5 as part of the HAT catalytic core (GCN5, ADA2, ADA3, and Sgf29). Interestingly, the HAT module in parasites such as P. falciparum might be significantly divergent from the HAT in humans or yeasts SAGA complexes. Rather than the conserved Sgf29 Tudor reader found in many eukaryotic SAGA complexes, P. falciparum‘s HAT complex contains an unconventional reader with four PHD-finger domains.

GCN5 plays a crucial role in regulating gene expression in Plasmodium parasites by catalyzing the acetylation of histone protein 3 at lysines 9 and 14 (H3K9Ac and H3K14Ac). This modification modulates the chromatin structure and accessibility, thereby triggering the expression of targeted genes. Recent studies have highlighted the importance of GCN5 in the transcriptional regulation of genes involved in Plasmodium development, antigenic variation, erythrocyte invasion, and virulence. , GCN5 was experimentally identified through piggyBac transposon-saturation mutagenesis as being likely essential in P. falciparum as well as in its murine orthologue. Disruption of GCN5 activity has been shown to impair parasite growth and development, suggesting that inhibitors targeting this enzyme could exhibit potent asexual blood-stage antimalarial activity. Moreover, the conservation of GCN5 across different Plasmodium species and its expression across all stages of the lifecycle further supports its potential as a broad-spectrum antimalarial target making it attractive for drug discovery. In addition, presumably selective pharmacological inhibition of the human bromodomains PCAF/GCN5 has been shown to be insufficient to disrupt their function, suggesting that a PfGCN5 inhibitor might have a wider selectivity window, making it a promising target in terms of safety. Despite all of this, further research is still needed to identify and characterize new molecules with potent activity against PfGCN5 that translates into submicromolar parasite growth inhibition.

Several compounds are known to modulate PfGCN5′s activity (Figure ). Cui et al. (2008) reported that Anacardic acid (AA) reduces acetylation of histone 3 at K9 and K14 in a dose–response manner and inhibits recombinant PfGCN5 with a pIC50 of 4.6. This molecule also inhibits the growth of chloroquine-sensitive and -resistant parasite strains with a pIC50 of approximately 4.5. More recently, Chen et al. (2016) developed a microfluid-based drug-screening system and identified known HAT inhibitors such as Butyrolactone 3 (MB-3, pIC50 3.9), CPTH-2 (pIC50 3.9), and Curcumin (pIC50 5.5) as inhibitors of PfGCN5. In the same year, Moustakim and coworkers developed the first nanomolar, selective PCAF/GCN5 bromodomain inhibitor L-45 (or L-Moses) and presented a cocrystal structure of PfGCN5 with the inhibitor. Other compounds such as the natural benzoquinone Embelin (Srivastava et al. 2014) have been reported as specific inhibitors for the PCAF/GCN5 HAT family, decreasing H3K9Ac levels in the parasite and inhibiting parasitic growth at low micromolar range (pIC50 5.0) which points to a possible PfGCN5-mediated inhibition. In 2017, the same laboratory computationally identified multiple potential PfGCN5 inhibitors and experimentally evaluated C14 in vitro and in vivo as an inhibitor of PfGCN5 demonstrating fairly potent antimalarial activity. However, C14 reduced H3K9 acetylation only at 1 μM, while its whole-cell potency against parasites is ∼0.2 μM, raising the possibility that the observed antiplasmodial activity may involve targets other than PfGCN5.

With the possible exception of C14, none of these molecules are expected to act as specific inhibitors of PfGCN5 over its human orthologue; rather, most are broad-spectrum HAT inhibitors or human-targeted probes that happen to cross-react with the parasite enzyme. Anacardic acid, MB-3, curcumin, CPTH-2, and embelin all inhibit human HATs (p300, PCAF, or GCN5), and L-45, although selective for the human PCAF/GCN5 bromodomain, also engages PfGCN5 due to the structural homology between the human and parasite bromodomains. Only C14 was explicitly designed to exploit active-site differences between PfGCN5 and hGCN5, and while computational studies predicted species-selective inhibition, experimental evidence confirming PfGCN5 selectivity at the target level is still lacking.

In this work, we provide an overview of our recent efforts to discover and develop novel PfGCN5 bromodomain inhibitors. We discuss the structure–activity relationship of our compounds and the correlation between enzymatic versus asexual blood-stage (ABS) whole-cell activity as well as the potential implications for the development of inhibitors of this epigenetic modulator for the treatment of malaria.

Results and Discussion

We first sought PfGCN5 inhibitors by screening the TCOLF diversity set of compounds using a FRET-based assay that measures the binding between the recombinantly expressed GCN5 bromodomain (2 nM) and an acetylated histone peptide (histone 4 a.a 1–20, K12ac). Compounds that inhibit the binding between protein and peptide should prevent or reduce the FRET signal detection. The affinity between the acetylated peptide and the GCN5 bromodomain was determined using the FRET system (KD = 225 nM). L-45 was used as a tool compound to validate the assay, with a pIC50 of 7 on average. The assay DMSO tolerance was established as being 0.3%, which is not surprising, since DMSO is an acetyl mimetic known for binding to bromodomains. A mock screen was also run as a validation step before the screening of compounds, with plates showing a typical Z′ of 0.9 and an S/B of 30.

This compound set (TCOLF diversity set) was initially designed and computationally refined from the GSK collection. The GSK 4 M compound library was virtually filtered using different physicochemical property filters (MW < 500 and cPFI < 9), removing compounds that exhibit assay interference, undesirable mechanisms of action (nuisance compounds), promiscuity (high inhibitory frequency index, IFI), or were restricted for other specific reasons. The resulting compounds were clustered with a path-based fingerprint using Tanimoto similarity in several steps between 0.75 and 0.6. Finally, 200 K compounds were found to represent clusters of more than 2 compounds. Among them, a diversity set of 42K compounds was selected by random selection to build a manageable diverse compound library.

Given the availability of structural information for PfGCN5, an additional set of 6.2K compounds was built ad hoc using the high-throughput virtual screening tool (HTVS) and PfGCN5 X-ray structure (PDB 5TPX) in complex with the known PfGCN5 inhibitor L-45.

The TCOLF diversity set and PfGCN5 ad hoc set were tested in duplicate at a single concentration (2.5 μM of compound). Compounds showing inhibition >25% in at least one copy were progressed for dose–response evaluation. This resulted in 372 compounds tested in dose–response delivering 6 early hits with an initial pIC50 close to or higher than 5 against PfGCN5. Further evaluation was carried out to select hits for progression. Initial physicochemical properties (ChromLogD, CAD solubility, and PFI), host cellular toxicity (HepG2), selectivity compared to human PCAF, and antimalarial activity against asexual blood-stage (ABS) parasites were evaluated (Table ). The 6 initial hits were resynthesized and retested delivering similar pIC50 to the HTS results, except compound 1, whose activity was reduced by more than 1 log. None of the evaluated compounds showed cellular toxicity or ABS antimalarial activity against the P. falciparum 3D7 strain, while the ABS assay control Mefloquine displayed a pIC50 of 7.7 ± 0.2, which, at this stage, could be due to low activity against the target. All compounds were evaluated using an orthogonal SPR assay, and selectivity was determined against human PCAF (p300/CBP-associated factor) (Table ).

1Hit Structures and Initial Activity Profile

Hit Structures and Initial Activity Profile

Resynthesized compounds 1 and 2 displayed similar potencies in the FRET assay, although only compound 2 showed activity in the SPR. Compounds 2 and 5 showed low affinity for HsPCAF (pK d < 4.5), lacked cytotoxicity in HepG2 cells, and possessed favorable physicochemical profiles. Compound 3, with PfGCN5 pIC50 of 6.3 (IC50 0.5 μM), was also explored but none of the examined analogues demonstrated better activity (data not shown). Among the rest of the hits, compound 6 exhibited good initial potency by SPR (PfGCN5 pK d 5.7) but, as expected, it has a higher binding affinity with the human enzyme (HsPCAF pK d 6.5) since it was previously developed at GSK as inhibitor for human PCAF.

Due to its high potency and at least 10-fold selectivity over HsPCAF, we initially focused our efforts on compound 2, aiming to increase potency against PfGCN5, improve solubility, and decrease chromLogD while maintaining a low cellular toxicity. In addition, a similar fragment compound, SKT1174, has been previously described as a low-affinity BPTF (Bromodomain PHD Finger Transcription Factor) inhibitor (SPR, K d 280 ± 70 μM) and has been cocrystallized with the protein. This small molecule and compound 2 share a cyclopropylcarbonyl-substituted tetrahydroquinoline moiety, a motif commonly found in other bromodomain inhibitors. BPTF and PfGCN5 are predicted to be structurally related (Figure A) and they present a highly homologous binding pocket (59% sequence identity among residues within 6 Å from the ligand). Furthermore, both proteins have been recently reported to be involved in the acetylation of the nonconserved histone variant H2A.Z.

3D and 2D representation of (A) Superposition of PfGCN5 and BPTF with cocrystallized fragment SKT1174. (B) Molecular docking and predicted binding mode for compound 2 with PfGCN5. (C) PfGCN5 cocrystal structure with L-45.

Initial molecular modeling studies point to a binding mode in which the cyclopropylcarbonyl tetrahydroquinoline binds to the PfGCN5 pocket acting as an acetyl lysine mimic, a feature that is typically observed in other bromodomain inhibitors. Compound 2 shares the same interactions with ASN1436 and TYR1393 through a water bridge presented in the fragment SKT1174 cocrystal structure (Figure B). Moreover, L-Moses (or L-45) establishes similar interactions with PfGCN5 (Figure C). Therefore, given this predicted possible binding mode, this part of the molecule was initially left unchanged. Instead, we centered our efforts on modifying the thiazole substitution aiming to understand the role of this substituent and improve potency. Aminothiazole substitution was initially evaluated (Table ).

2SAR1: Aminothiophene Substitution

SAR1: Aminothiophene Substitution

Different amides, secondary and tertiary amines, and sulfonamides were tested. Initial methylation of the amide (11a) led to a clear loss of activity (PfGCN5 pIC50 < 4.6). Introduction of branching (11b) aiming to reduce clearance led to weak antiplasmodial activity (PfGCN5 pIC50, pIC50 4.6). Encouragingly, the use of cyclic amines such as morpholine (11c) showed a slight increase in potency against GCN5 (pIC50 = 6.4) but no further increase in antimalarial activity. Comparing derivatives 11b and 11c with 12b and 12c, it was clear that thiazole substitution in position C6 of the tetrahydroquinoline exhibited equal or better activity than its corresponding C7-substituted derivatives. Hence, we focused our attention on the preparation of C6-substituted aminothiazole derivatives.

The use of aromatic unsubstituted (11d) and substituted amines (11e and 11g) and amides (11f) caused a large drop-in activity. Primary amine 11h, aliphatic amines, and amides retained or slightly impaired activity (11i-11m); in addition, these derivatives showed lower solubility and higher chromLogD and cytotoxicity. Aliphatic sulfonamide derivatives 11n and 11o had a positive impact on physicochemical properties improving cytotoxicity, chromLogD, and solubility. Besides, compound 11n delivered similar potency compared with the corresponding amide 11m (pIC50 6.3 and 6.2, respectively), keeping a low cytotoxicity (pIC50 4.1), chromLogD (3.2), and significantly improving solubility (≥178 μg/mL). Pyridine (11s) and methylpyridine derivative (11t) showed a significant loss of activity (pIC50 < 4.6 and 4.7, respectively) while unexpectedly, contrary to the behavior observed for the initial hit, compound 11r recovered activity when the thiazole amide is methylated (pIC50 5.8) and a positive effect is also observed by SPR. Introduction of a pyrazine (11v) rings slightly decreased potency against the target while substitution of the ring (11u) dropped more than 1 log in pIC50 (5.7 and 4.6, respectively). Other smaller heterocycles such as furan 11w showed similar potency than the hit while methylisoxazole 11x dropped 1 log.

Interestingly, the introduction of covalent binders such as acrylamide (11p) and chloroacetamide (11q) showed a positive increase on the activity for both biochemical (pIC50 7.1 and 6.9, respectively) and biophysical measurements (pK d 5.5 and 6, respectively). Both derivatives also showed a significant improvement in ABS activity with pIC50 values of 5.2 and 5.7, respectively, but in both cases, the compounds exhibited significant cytotoxicity in HepG2 with pIC50 values of >5. These results point to a covalent approach as a possible strategy for improving target inhibition. Next, we replaced the thiazole ring with different aromatic, aliphatic, and heteroaromatic five- and six-membered rings (Table ).

3SAR2: Thiophene Scaffold Hopping

SAR2: Thiophene Scaffold Hopping

Replacement of the thiazole for a 6-membered ring (21a-21f) maintained potency against PfGCN5. The phenyl ring derivative 21a showed a pIC50 of 6.2 in the biochemical assay and low activity in the ABS assay against P. falciparum 3D7 with a pIC50 of 4.5 (IC50 32 μM). Substituted aminopyridines with the nitrogen atom and amide in different positions retained similar activity against both protein and parasite (21b-21d). Compound 21c, although still low, showed the most potent antimalarial activity with a pIC50 of 4.6 (IC50 25 μM) and improved lipophilicity (3.5) and CAD solubility (≥123 μg/mL) together with no cellular toxicity. Also, worth noting, the introduction of a methyl group in position 5 of the pyridine to break planarity was not well tolerated resulting in a 1 log reduction in potency (21f pIC50 5.2 vs 21d pIC50 6.4).

This detrimental effect upon methylation was also observed for thiazole (21j) with lower potency (pIC50 4.9), lower solubility (3 μg/mL), and increased lipophilicity (5.2). Likewise, the introduction of a thiadiazole ring (21g) resulted in a loss of potency. In contrast, the thiophene analogue (21i) improved activity against the protein and displayed a weak antiplasmodial activity (PfGCN5 pIC50 6.5 and ABS 3D7 pIC50 4.5) but significant increase in cellular toxicity (HepG2 pIC50 5.0). Introduction of N-methylpyrazole (21k), imidazole (13) or pyrazole (21h) led to an increase in potency with pIC50 of 6.1, 6.3, and 6.6, respectively, but no ABS activity. Finally, the use of aliphatic heterocycles such as pyrrolidine (34) led to a decrease in potency compared with the initial hit. From the different rings evaluated, compounds 21h and 21i showed the best results with 21h, which incorporates a pyrazole ring, being the most potent analogue with PfGCN5 pIC50 of 6.6 (275 nM), lower chromLogD (2.9), good solubility (≥117 μg/mL), and low cytotoxicity (pCI50 HepG2 4.3).

Subsequently, after evaluating the effect of the position and substitution of the aminothiazole, we addressed the effect of the tetrahydroquinoline structure on the activity (Table ). Again, a clear influence of the aminothiazole position on the activity was observed with different tetrahydroquinoline-modified scaffolds. Methylation of the tetrahydroquinoline (15b) retained some activity (pIC50 5.4), while a similar drop in pIC50 was observed when O (26b) or S (27b) atoms were introduced in the aliphatic ring (pIC50 5.5 and 5.8, respectively). Finally, ring contraction (18) or expansion (28a) and changes on the cyclopropyl position (17) negatively affect potency, rendering the compounds inactive.

4SAR3: Tetrahydroquinoline Modification

SAR3: Tetrahydroquinoline Modification

Next, the effect of the cyclopropyl substituent was evaluated (Table ). In this part of the molecule, replacing the amide group with a sulfonamide (14) negatively impacted activity, rendering the compound inactive. Similarly, replacing cyclopropyl with a methyl was not tolerated (16a).

5SAR4: Cyclopropyl Amide Modifications

SAR4: Cyclopropyl Amide Modifications

Due to the presence of reactive amino acids (CYS1430 and CYS3003) in the PfGCN5 active site, we decided to explore a covalent approach to facilitate target engagement. Introduction of a chloroacetamide (22) or acrylamide (23) warhead showed moderate potency against P. falciparum. Thus, chloroacetamide derivative 22 (pIC50 5.0 in 3D7) was the most potent inhibitor of parasite growth. However, mass spectrometry analysis indicated no binding to the protein for chloroacetylamide and acrylamide derivatives. The introduction of an epoxide (25) was not tolerated. These analogues with covalent warheads demonstrated differentiated cytotoxicities in HepG2. Chloroacetamide derivative (22) displayed significant toxicity (pIC50 5.4), while acrylamide and epoxide derivatives (23 and 25) were less toxic (pIC50 4.5 and 4.2, respectively).

The SAR exploration carried out for compound 2 was extensive, including newly designed compounds reported here and more than 100 analogues retaining the substituted tetrahydroquinoline scaffold that were available at GSK and were tested against PfGCN5. As a final attempt to increase the potency of the initial hit 2, the synthesis of a bivalent compound was pursued (Figure ). The synthesis of symmetrical and pseudo-symmetrical bivalent inhibitors has been successfully applied in the design of BET bromodomain inhibitors, delivering bivalent compounds with a significant increase of potency compared with the initial monovalent inhibitor. From 11h, the bivalent derivative 11y was initially synthesized and evaluated to explore this alternative approach. In our case, although we were pleased to observe that our bivalent inhibitor shows a significant increase in ABS activity compared with the initial hit 2 (pIC50 of 5.6 and <4.3, respectively), this increase in phenotypic potency was not driven by an increase in binding affinity for PfGCN5 compared with the hit.

Compound 5, identified from the HTS, exhibited lower potency (pIC50 of 5.3) compared to hit 2 but higher solubility (≥182 μg/mL) and lower chromLogD (2.71). Compound 5 contained a substituted N-methylpicolinamide group which represents a classic acetyl lysine mimic commonly found in other bromodomain inhibitors. As shown for the previously explored hit 2 and L-Moses (Figure A and B), compound 5 shares the same interaction among the TYR1442, CYS1432, and TYR1393 (water bridge) with the N-methylpicolinamide moiety (Figure ). So, this part of the molecule was initially left untouched.

Molecular docking and predicted binding mode for compound 6 with PfGCN5.

In the first approach, we aimed to reduce the flexibility of the substituted piperidine. However, reducing the length and flexibility of the aliphatic linker (41a) led to a slight decrease in activity (from 5.3 to 5.0) (Table ). Replacing the aliphatic piperidine with a more rigid phenyl ring improved the inhibitory activity delivering 41b with a pIC50 of 6.5 against PfGCN5. Incorporating aromatic heterocycles including well-known antimalarial scaffolds such as quinoline with extended linkers had a positive impact on compound activity, although this increase in potency came at the cost of increased lipophilicity and PFI. Compound 41c with a phenyl ring substitution of the N-methylpicolinamide and a methyl quinoline separated by a spacer retained similar activity to 41b (6.5 and 6.3, respectively). Interestingly, in this case, a shorter linker and restricting flexibility (41d) led to a significant improvement in activity delivering the most potent derivative against PfGCN5 with a pIC50 of 7.0. Biophysical evaluation delivered similar potency for both analogues (pK d 5.6 and 5.5). Methylation of the piperazine heterocycle (41e) recovered significant inhibitory activity with a pIC50 of 6.2 while the replacement of the aromatic ring for other heterocycles such as pyrazole (41f) led to a loss of activity (pIC50 of 5.5). Finally, replacement of the piperazine in 41c moiety with a piperidine (41h) improved potency, achieving a pIC50 of 6.7, while also enhancing both lipophilicity (4.1 vs 5.7) and solubility (78 vs 24 μg/mL). This resulted in a significant improvement in binding, increasing the pK d value from 5.6 to 6.0. Encouragingly, derivatives 41e and 41h with 2-methylpiperazine and piperidine moieties, respectively, showed activity against the parasite (pIC50 5.1 and 5.9, respectively).

6Activity of Hit 5 and Analogues

Activity of Hit 5 and Analogues

However, it is important to highlight that while the presence of the N-methylpicolinamide moiety seems to be crucial for the inhibitory activity against the target, it is not essential for the activity against the parasite. For instance, compounds 41g with a primary amide and 41k with an ethyl ester group are inactive against the target (pIC50 < 4.6) but retained significant activity against the parasite (pIC50 5.5 and 6.3, respectively). Similar behavior is observed for compounds 41i and 41j, where the replacement of the aromatic ring in 41h with a thiazole or the pyridine moiety with a dihydroimidazo­[1,5-a]­quinoline retains ABS activity (pIC50 of 5.7 and 6.4, respectively) but with a significant drop in potency against the target protein (pIC50 of 5.4 and 5.0).

These results suggest that PfGCN5 inhibition may not be the primary driver of ABS antimalarial activity in this series. While inhibition of β-hematin metabolism is a well-known property of quinoline-containing compounds, the mechanism underlying this weak whole-cell activitypotentially independent of PfGCN5remains unclear and would require investigation beyond the scope of this manuscript.

It is possible that a similar behavior could account for the ABS activity for compounds 41e, 41f, 41g, 41h, 41i, 41j, and 41k.

7Permeability Data in MDCK-MDR1 Cells in the Presence of a P-Glycoprotein Inhibitor

Permeability Data in MDCK-MDR1 Cells in the Presence of a P-Glycoprotein Inhibitor

To rationalize the disconnect between the biochemical potency against PfGCN5 and the potency against the parasite, we measured other physicochemical properties that could influence the target engagement in the parasite. We measured the permeability of the compounds in MDR1-transfected MDCK2 cells to assess their ability to cross cellular membranes and evaluate P-glycoprotein efflux transport.

As shown in Table , compounds 41e and 41h, despite exhibiting significantly lower solubility and permeability compared to the initial hit (6), displayed some antimalarial activity against P. falciparum with a pIC50 > 5. For hit 2, replacing the amide with a sulfonamide (11n) increases the solubility (30 to ≥178 μg/mL, respectively) but results in lower permeability (157 and 60.5 nm/min). Scaffold hopping to an aminopyrazole (21h) again led to an improvement in solubility (≥117 μg/mL) retaining good permeability (160 nm/min). Also, except for the initial hit 2, these two analogues (11n and 21h) show clear changes in the efflux rate when assessed in MDCK2-MDR1 assays with Elacridar (P-glycoprotein inhibitor) (5.1 and 6.8) demonstrating to be substrates of the transporter. Thiophene derivative 21i, although it is not a P-glycoprotein substrate, shows a drop in permeability (70.5 nm/min) and low solubility (23 μg/mL). Finally, compounds 21b and 21c, which have similar potencies in both the target and parasite, exhibit a clear influence of the propylamide position on their behavior as P-glycoprotein substrates, solubility, permeability, and toxicity which improves when the amide is in position C4 of the pyridine ring (21c). For this family of compounds, the combination of low solubility and low permeability might account for the low activity observed in ABS assays.

Conclusions

In summary, the screening of a subset of 48,200 compounds from the GSK collection led to the identification of novel, tractable inhibitors of PfGCN5 bromodomain. Despite the improvements in their properties after a SAR survey, the lead compounds did not show the expected parasite growth inhibition. Based on prior observations across comparable programs, we typically see an approximately 1.0–1.5 log shift between biochemical pIC50 and whole-cell pIC50, so compounds with HTRF pIC50 ≥ 6 would be expected to show measurable whole-cell activity (pIC50 > 4.5); however, most compounds do not meet this threshold, indicating a disconnect between biochemical potency and cellular efficacy. Moreover, Jingyi Tang et al. recently reported that PfGCN5ΔBRD parasites could not survive beyond 20 generations, but even though PfGCN5:HA protein was undetectable by 72 h after rapamycin treatment, a significant decrease in parasite growth was not observed until 88 h.

Furthermore, tool compounds previously demonstrated to possess strong antimalarial activity have been found to interact with PfGCN5; however, this antimalarial effect may not be exclusively attributed to PfGCN5 inhibition. The mere presence of compound binding and apparent gene essentiality does not establish causality. Therefore, assertions regarding bromodomain inhibition of PfGCN5 as the mechanism for parasite growth suppression should be made with caution, until further validation is obtained. To address this, we plan to perform resistance selection with the most potent compounds, followed by whole-genome sequencing (WGS) to identify resistance-associated changes and assess on-target engagement.

PfGCN5 was prioritized based on reported genetic essentiality and the availability of tool compounds. In hindsight, the target rationale could have been further supported before fully committing medicinal chemistry resources. Complementary validationsuch as conditional knockdown experiments to confirm essentiality, quantify the extent of knockdown required to induce parasite death, and determine time-to-killwould have clarified the therapeutic potential and liabilities.

This data package suggests that targeting the PfGCN5 bromodomain to develop antimalarial compounds might not be as effective as expected and raises the question of whether solely targeting the bromodomain region on PfGCN5 effectively translates into parasite death. Nonetheless, BDR inhibitors might be slow-acting compounds with potency increasing when evaluated over longer time points. This delayed effect could potentially align with the development of chemoprophylactic drugs that are intended to prevent infection over extended durations. A different strategy to knock down the protein, such as targeted protein degradation, might also be considered in order to increase antiplasmodial potency.

Importantly, the inability of PfGCN5:ΔBRD parasites to form infective stage V gametocytes, together with inhibition of sporozoite development, indicates that PfGCN5 BRD inhibitors could also serve as transmission-blocking agents. Alternatively, PfGCN5 upregulation during stress conditions has been associated with the generation of artemisinin resistance in P. falciparum, and a decrease of resistance levels has been reported in the presence of weak PfGCN5 inhibitors. Although current data indicate that the development of clinical candidates for this protein might be challenging, inhibition of this target could still be exploited as a resensitizing strategy for artemisinin-resistant strain lines.

Experimental Section

General Methods

Chemicals and solvents were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich, Combi-Blocks, and Enamine and were used as received. Oxygen- and moisture-sensitive reactions were carried out under an inert atmosphere of nitrogen. Dry solvents were purchased in Sure Seal bottles (DMF and DCM stored over molecular sieves). Flash column chromatography was performed using prepacked silica gel iLOK and SiliCycle cartridges of different sizes using a Biotage Isolera CombiFlash. 1H NMR and 13C NMR spectra were recorded on a Bruker Avance NEO 400 spectrometer (1H at 400.1 MHz, 13C at 101.8 MHz). Chemical shifts (δ) are expressed in ppm registered using the residual solvent as the internal reference (DMSO–D6, CD3OD, or CDCl3) in all cases. Signal splitting patterns are described as singlet (s), doublet (d), triplet (t), quartet (q), quintuplet (quin), sextuplet (sxt), multiplet (m), broad (br s), or a combination thereof. Coupling constants (J) are quoted to the nearest 0.1 Hz. Reactions were followed using precoated TLC aluminum plates (Kieselgel 60 F254, 0.2 mm, 40 × 80 mm, ALUGRAM SIL) or low-resolution mass spectrometry recorded on an Agilent Technologies 1290 Infinity Series HPLC connected to an Agilent Technologies 6120 quadrupole LCMS, connected to an Agilent diode array detector, where the column used was a Waters XBridge column (4.6 mm × 50 mm, 3.5 μm particle size) with a gradient of 5–90% using 0.05 MM NH4HCO3 in water (solvent A) and acetonitrile (solvent B) as a mobile phase. Preparative Agilent HPLC chromatographic separations were performed using a Waters X-Bridge C18 column (1 injection, 50 mm × 150 mm, 5 μm particle size, flow 100 mL/min, G7161B pumps, prep autosampler G7157A), connected to a G7115A UV/vis detector alongside a 1260 DAD WR, using 0.05 MM NH4HCO3 in water (solvent A) and acetonitrile (solvent B) as a mobile phase. The fractions containing product were collected, solvents evaporated in vacuo, subjected to Genevaćs EZ-2 Elite centrifugal evaporator, and then dried in the oven under 40 °C overnight to give the appropriate purified sample.

All final compounds showed chemical purity of ≥95%, except compound 12c (purity = 93%), as determined from the UV chromatogram (210–500 nm) obtained by UPLC analysis, conducted on an Acquity UPLC BEH (MS: Waters SQD; injection volume 1 μL, 1.7 μM, C18 3 × 50 mm column at 50 °C) with a gradient of 5–100% acetonitrile/water +0.1% formic acid, a flow rate of 0.8 mL/min, and a time of 2 min. Microwave-assisted chemistry was performed using a Biotage Initiator+. The synthesis of the intermediates and characterization data for the final compounds are included in the Supporting Information.

HTRF Assay

Purified PfGCN5BRD conjugated with GST and a histone 4 acetylated and biotinylated peptide (Alta Bioscience, cat. no. HS26) were diluted in binding buffer (part of Cisbio EPIgeneous Binding Domain Kit B, cat. no. 62BDBPEH). GST-Eu3+ cryptate antibody and streptavidin-XL665 conjugate were diluted in detection buffer (all components of Cisbio EPIgeneous Binding Domain Kit B, cat. no. 62BDBPEH). Four μL of PfGCN5, 4 μL of peptide, 5 μL of antibody, 5 μL of SA-XL665 solution, and 2 μL of binding buffer were added to wells of white 384-well low-volume plates (Greiner, cat, no. 784904) yielding final concentrations of 2 nM for PfGCN5, 200 nM for histone peptide, 0.125X for Eu antibody, and 25 nM for SA-XL665. Plates were prestamped with compounds using an acoustic dispenser. The final DMSO concentration was kept at 0.25%. The system was incubated for 2 h and 30 min at room temperature, and TR-FRET measurements were acquired on a PHERAstar plate reader (BMG): 337 nm excitation, 620 nm (Eu) and 665 nm (XL665) emissions, delay 60 μs, integration 400 μs. The TR-FRET ratio was taken as the 665/620 nm intensity ratio, multiplied by a factor of 104. Three independent experiments were run for dose–response plates (11 points, dilution 1/3) and two independent experiments were run for single-shot experiments. Data were analyzed using ActivityBase XE and SIGNALS VitroVivo (SVV) Analysis Software. Quality control statistical analysis was performed using Z′ as the referenced parameter.

RCtrl1 and RCtrl2 were calculated as the average of control 1 (DMSO) and control 2 (L-45). For all the experiments, the Z′ range was 0.7–0.9 and Signal/Background around 30. The percentage of inhibition was calculated based on the response of each compound (x) and normalized with the average of controls.

In the single-concentration assays, robust cutoffs at the robust mean+3xSD of control 1 were used to consider compounds as active. For this project, active compounds that exhibited a percentage inhibition of >25% were selected as hits.

A control compound (GSK4027) was injected as a 6-point, 3-fold dilution concentration series (10 μM top) at the beginning and end of the experiment, in each channel.

Ad Hoc Library Preparation and Virtual Screening

Ligands were prepared using Maestro LigPrep at pH= 7.4 ± 0.5, retaining specific chirality and including all stereocenters when the absolute configuration was not known. The PfGCN5 X-ray structure (PDB 5TPX) was prepared using the default option in the Maestro Protein Preparation Wizard. The protein grid was generated without any constraints using the cocrystallized ligand as a reference. A conserved water molecule (HOH 1607) near Tyr1393 was kept in the binding pocket of L-45 for the HTVS docking.

A 56K available library was docked at the HTVS level, and results were filtered according to the best pose (with a small RMSE with respect to the L-45 pose) and with a docking score of less than −5.5 kcal/mol, which was 3 kcal/mol lower than the docking score of the reference ligand (−8.3 kcal/mol). In this range, 6.2K compounds were selected for the screening.
