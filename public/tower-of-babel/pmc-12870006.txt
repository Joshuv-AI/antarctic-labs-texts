Quantifying the effects of the Correa pathway from Helicobacter pylori infection to gastric cancer: causal inference found in 6.8 million Koreans
Authors: Hyun Jin Oh, Chung Ho Kim, Hyun-Wook Park, Bomi Park
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe Correa pathway describes the sequential progression of gastric cancer through Helicobacter pylori (H. pylori) infection, chronic gastritis, atrophic gastritis, intestinal metaplasia, adenoma, and gastric cancer. Clarifying the causal relationships along this pathway can provide robust evidence for targeted gastric cancer preventive strategies.MethodsThis study was conducted using data from the Korean National Health Insurance Service, including 6,863,103 individuals who participated in the National Cancer Screening Program for gastric cancer in 2018, with a 2-year follow-up. We used doubly robust targeted maximum likelihood estimation (TMLE) to quantify the total effect of H. pylori eradication on incident gastric cancer and applied causal mediation analysis (CMA) to evaluate indirect pathways through atrophic gastritis/intestinal metaplasia and adenoma. Analyses were adjusted for age, sex, income, smoking, alcohol use, and family history of gastric cancer.ResultsTMLE revealed that H. pylori infection significantly increased gastric cancer risk (relative risk [RR] = 6.40; 95% confidence interval [CI]: 6.05–6.77), as well as the risk of atrophic gastritis/intestinal metaplasia (RR = 1.41; 95% CI: 1.35–1.43) and adenoma (RR = 5.81; 95% CI: 5.68–5.94). atrophic gastritis/intestinal metaplasia substantially elevated the risk of adenoma (RR = 1.72; 95% CI: 1.67–1.77) and gastric cancer (RR = 1.33; 95% CI: 1.28–1.44). CMA showed that 3% of the H. pylori effect on gastric cancer was mediated through atrophic gastritis/intestinal metaplasia (odds ratio [OR] = 1.03, 95% CI: 1.02–1.04), and 36% was mediated via adenoma (OR = 1.97, 95% CI: 1.94–2.01). Among individuals with atrophic gastritis/intestinal metaplasia, adenoma accounted for 44% of the subsequent gastric cancer risk (OR = 1.13, 95% CI: 1.03–1.34).ConclusionsOur findings demonstrate that H. pylori infection is the most important causal determinant of gastric cancer and adenoma, indicating that prevention of H. pylori is central to gastric cancer prevention.

Background

Gastric cancer (GC) remains a significant global health concern. In 2022, an estimated 968,784 new cases of stomach cancer were diagnosed worldwide, making it the fifth most common cancer [1]. It also accounted for approximately 659,853 deaths, making it the fourth leading cause of cancer-related mortality [1]. East Asian countries, including South Korea, report the highest incidence and mortality rates. According to the latest Korean National Cancer Registry data (2022), approximately 26,662 new cases were recorded, highlighting a substantial national burden [2]. Despite advancements in diagnostic and therapeutic strategies, prognosis remains poor in advanced stages, underscoring the importance of early detection [3, 4]. Therefore, implementing effective screening and prevention programs is essential to reducing GC incidence and mortality [5].

The Correa pathway is a widely accepted model describing the histological progression of intestinal-type GC: chronic gastritis, atrophic gastritis (AG), intestinal metaplasia (IM), dysplasia, and invasive carcinoma [6, 7]. Helicobacter pylori (H. pylori) infection is a well-established initiator of chronic gastric inflammation, promoting these premalignant stages [8, 9]. Numerous sero-epidemiological studies have robustly linked H. pylori to an increased GC risk [8, 9]. AG and IM, frequently resulting from chronic H. pylori-induced gastritis, are known to significantly elevate cancer risk [6]. However, several uncertainties remain. The molecular mechanisms by which H. pylori drives carcinogenesis are not fully elucidated. Moreover, while each stage in the Correa pathway is recognized as a precursor, the precise causal contributions and the extent to which each intermediate lesion mediates the progression from H. pylori infection to invasive cancer require further elucidation [10].

Several epidemiological studies have investigated the associations between H. pylori infection, AG, IM, adenoma, and GC. Both case–control and cohort studies have consistently demonstrated strong correlations between these premalignant lesions and GC risk [11–13]. For instance, an extensive Japanese cohort study reported a significantly increased risk of GC among individuals with AG or IM identified via endoscopy [13]. Similarly, European observational studies have emphasized AG or IM as precancerous conditions warranting close surveillance [12]. However, most of these studies have relied on associative analyses rather than formal causal inference methods. These limitations include potential residual confounding, absence of rigorous quantitative mediation analysis, and methodological constraints in accurately estimating causal effects [11]. To address these limitations, contemporary causal inference techniques are necessary but have seldom been applied in this context.

Therefore, this study aimed to quantitatively evaluate the causal effects of H. pylori infection on GC development and to elucidate the mediating roles of AG, IM, and gastric adenoma. By clarifying the causal pathways and mediation proportions within the Correa pathway, our findings aim to provide robust evidence to support targeted prevention strategies in GC control.

Methods

Study design and population

This study used data from South Korea’s National Health Insurance Service (NHIS), which covers approximately 97% of the population [14]. A retrospective cohort study was conducted among individuals aged 40–74 years who underwent GC screening through the National Cancer Screening Program (NCSP) in 2018. Because endoscopic findings of AG/IM were available in the national screening database only from 2018, the analytic cohort was restricted to individuals undergoing index screening endoscopy in 2018 or later. Exclusion criteria included a prior history of any cancer (ICD-10 codes C00–C97) before the baseline screening, GC diagnosed within one year after the baseline screening to minimize reverse causation and exclude latent cases [15], death within two years of screening, and missing data on key confounders. After applying these criteria, the final cohort included 6,863,103 participants.

Exposure and mediators

The exposure of interest was H. pylori infection, operationalized by identifying individuals who were prescribed eradication therapy in 2018 using prescription claims data from the NHIS. Potential mediators included AG, IM, and gastric adenoma, which were identified through the 2018 NCSP database. The presence of each condition was determined based on standardized records that reflect clinicians’ assessments of endoscopic findings, supported by biopsy results.

Outcome

The primary outcome was GC diagnosed within 24 months after the index endoscopy. This 24-month interval was treated as a follow-up window, consistent with the biennial screening interval of the Korean National Cancer Screening Program. Incident GC was identified from NHIS claims using ICD-10 code C16 as a primary or secondary diagnosis in combination with cancer-specific Rare and Intractable Disease (RID) exemption codes (V193/V194), which indicate official registration for confirmed cancer. This combined definition has been validated against the Korea Central Cancer Registry (KCCR), demonstrating high accuracy (sensitivity 96.0% and positive predictive value 94.1%) for identifying GC cases in Korean administrative data [16].

Covariates

Covariates were selected based on prior literature and causal assumptions depicted in a Directed Acyclic Graph (DAG) developed for this study (Fig. 1) [17]. These variables were considered potential confounders of the relationship between H. pylori infection and subsequent GC risk. Information on covariates was obtained from the 2018 NHIS database, specifically from the eligibility file (demographics and socioeconomic status), general health screening questionnaire (e.g., smoking and alcohol use), and NCSP questionnaire (e.g., family history of GC). Socioeconomic status was measured using national health insurance premium contribution quartiles, which are commonly used as a proxy for household income in Korean population-based studies. The final covariates included age (categorized: 40–44, 45–49, 50–54, 55–59, 60–64, 65–69, 70–74 years), sex (male, female), socioeconomic status (quartiles), smoking status (never, former, current), alcohol consumption (none, ≤ 3 times/week, ≥ 4 times/week), and family history of GC. H.pylori bacterial genotypes were not included in the minimally sufficient adjustment set. Because CagA and vacA genotypes arise only after H. pylori infection, they represent strain-specific virulence characteristics of the exposure rather than independent pre-exposure confounders [18]. Adjusting for such post-exposure variables may block part of the biological pathway from infection to AG/IM, adenoma, and GC, leading to over-adjustment. Statistical analyses

Targeted Maximum Likelihood Estimation (TMLE) was applied to estimate the causal effect of H. pylori infection on GC incidence, using baseline data from 2018 as time zero [19]. TMLE is a doubly robust, semi-parametric method that minimizes bias from confounding and model misspecification by combining outcome regression with inverse probability weighting [20]. To flexibly model both the outcome and treatment assignment mechanisms, we applied Super Learner, an ensemble machine learning algorithm that combines multiple candidate learners through cross-validation. Our Super Learner library included generalized linear models and XGBoost, with fivefold cross-validation used to optimize predictive performance [21].

Causal Mediation Analysis (CMA) was conducted to investigate mediating pathways. Specifically, we assessed 1) whether AG or IM mediated the association between H. pylori infection and GC, 2) whether adenoma mediated the association between H. pylori infection and GC, and 3) whether adenoma mediated the association between AG or IM and GC. All mediation analyses were adjusted for the same covariates used in the TMLE analysis based on the constructed DAG. We assumed that there was no unmeasured confounding between the exposure and mediator, or between the mediator and outcome, and that no mediator–outcome confounders were themselves affected by the exposure. Mediation effects were quantified using natural direct and indirect effects. Natural direct effects represent the influence of H. pylori infection independent of mediators, while indirect effects capture pathways mediated by AG, IM, or adenoma [22].

All statistical analyses were conducted in R software (version 4.3.1). TMLE was implemented using the tmle package, and CMA was performed using the medflex package. Statistical significance was defined as a two-sided P-value < 0.05.

Discussion

Our findings substantiate the Correa pathway by demonstrating that H. pylori infection significantly increases GC risk via defined histopathological stages, including AG, IM, and gastric adenoma. Using CMA, we quantified the extent to which each stage contributed to GC development and found that these preneoplastic and dysplastic changes collectively explain a meaningful portion of the effect of H. pylori infection. Among these, adenoma accounted for the largest proportion of mediation, while AG and IM also showed significant associations with subsequent adenoma or cancer development. Our findings demonstrate that H. pylori infection is the dominant risk factor for GC development in our study population. Using CMA, we quantified the extent to which AG, IM, and gastric adenoma contributed to GC development. Among these mediating pathways, adenoma accounted for the largest proportion, while AG and IM showed smaller but statistically significant associations with subsequent adenoma or cancer development. Taken together, these results indicate that while gastric carcinogenesis may proceed through stepwise histopathological stages, H. pylori infection itself remains the primary driver of GC risk.

By employing advanced causal inference methods, TMLE and CMA, our study provides robust causal estimates that overcome limitations inherent in traditional observational analyses. Randomized controlled trials (RCTs) evaluating the full Correa sequence are virtually infeasible due to the long latency of gastric carcinogenesis and ethical challenges in withholding treatment from high-risk individuals. As such, observational studies using rigorous analytical methods remain the most feasible and ethical approach to understanding the causal processes linking H. pylori infection to GC. Our use of TMLE and CMA enables precise quantification of both direct and indirect effects, minimizing bias from confounding and measurement error [23, 24]. This analytical clarity reinforces the biological plausibility of the Correa pathway and informs more refined strategies for surveillance and prevention.

Mechanistically, H. pylori infection induces chronic inflammation, oxidative stress, and DNA damage, triggering histological changes that contribute to GC development [8, 10, 25]. AG and IM are preneoplastic conditions marked by the replacement of gastric epithelium with intestinal-type cells, while adenomas are advanced dysplastic lesions with substantial malignant potential [26]. Our findings support this pathogenic sequence by identifying adenoma as a particularly significant mediator in the H. pylori–GC pathway. The presence of adenoma accounted for 36% of the total effect of H. pylori on GC and mediated 44% of the effect of AG/IM on GC, underscoring its pivotal role in malignant transformation. These results highlight the importance of actively detecting and managing adenomatous lesions, in addition to H. pylori infection or early-stage AG/IM, particularly in high-risk populations. Effective GC prevention may require integrated approaches combining early detection of adenoma with eradication of H. pylori and mitigation of chronic inflammation.

The traditional model suggests sequential progression through AG and IM as key precancerous stages [6, 7]. The Correa hypothesis posits that these conditions are more likely to lead to the intestinal type of GC, highlighting the importance of their proper surveillance and management [6, 7]. Our findings align with this model: individuals with AG or IM had a significantly elevated risk of developing adenoma or GC within two years of screening endoscopy. This result supports previous evidence suggesting that the progression from IM to neoplastic lesions can occur relatively quickly. For instance, the 2020 American Gastroenterological Association (AGA) clinical guideline estimated an annual progression risk from IM to GC of 0.124% [27], and a European cohort study reported that progression from IM to adenoma or GC occurred within a median of 11–32 months—particularly shorter in cases of incomplete IM [28]. These data suggest that a uniform surveillance interval of two years, as currently applied in Korea’s NCSP, may not be optimal for individuals with AG or IM. Risk-stratified surveillance intervals should be considered for more effective prevention in this high-risk subgroup.

However, despite the strong association between AG or IM and GC, our mediation analysis revealed that these conditions accounted for only a small portion—approximately 3%—of the total effect of H. pylori infection on GC. This limited mediating effect suggests that while AG and IM are important indicators of gastric carcinogenesis, additional or alternative pathways may contribute to malignant transformation. Previous studies have indicated that targeting AG or IM alone through pharmacological or lifestyle interventions has yielded only modest reductions in GC incidence [11, 12]. Emerging evidence supports the existence of H. pylori-induced carcinogenic mechanisms that bypass these intermediate lesions, including direct effects of chronic inflammation, oxidative stress, and genetic or epigenetic alterations [29, 30]. In this nationwide cohort, only a modest proportion of the association between H. pylori infection and GC was mediated through clinically detected AG or IM. Even acknowledging that our approach likely underestimates the total mediation, these findings indicate that, in current real-world practice, a substantial share of GCs may arise through pathways not fully captured by the classical Correa sequence. Thus, our data do not support the view that the Correa pathway is uniformly predominant in this population; instead, they highlight the probable importance of Correa-independent or as-yet-uncharacterized mechanisms. Given that our study population was restricted to individuals aged 40–74 years, these patterns may be more pronounced in this relatively younger cohort compared with GC populations that include older ages.”

From a prevention standpoint, these results imply that population-based strategies focusing solely on surveillance for AG/IM may have limited impact on overall GC incidence in contemporary Korea. Broader approaches—such as intensified H. pylori eradication, identification of molecular or genetic markers of non-Correa pathways, and risk-stratified endoscopic surveillance—are likely necessary to address the substantial burden of cancers arising outside the classical Correa sequence.

A major strength of our study lies in the application of rigorous causal inference methods—TMLE and CMA—to a large-scale, nationally representative cohort. These methods allow for doubly robust estimation and minimize bias due to confounding and model misspecification, which are inherent challenges in traditional observational studies. By utilizing the comprehensive NHIS database, we were able to include a large number of individuals with diverse clinical and demographic backgrounds, enhancing both the internal and external validity of our findings. Furthermore, the integration of national endoscopy screening data with longitudinal claims enabled a temporally ordered analysis of H. pylori infection, intermediate lesions, and GC, supporting causal interpretations of complex disease pathways.

Despite its strengths, our study has several limitations. First, information on the severity and extent of AG and IM. Although AG and IM were identified using standardized endoscopic diagnoses, supported by biopsy findings, diagnosis based primarily on white-light endoscopy has limited accuracy and substantial interobserver variability, which may vary across screening centers. As a result, misclassification across a broad spectrum of AG and IM severity may have biased the estimated mediating effects toward the null, potentially leading to an underestimation of the contribution of AG and IM to gastric carcinogenesis in our analyses. Second, in the CMA, we assumed that there was no unmeasured confounding between the exposure and mediator, or between the mediator and outcome, and that the mediator–outcome confounders were unlikely to be affected by the exposure. This latter assumption is supported by prior evidence indicating that age, sex, socioeconomic status, smoking, and alcohol use function primarily as upstream determinants rather than exposure-induced variables [10, 31]. However, we cannot completely exclude the possibility of residual exposure-related confounding. Third, we excluded individuals who died within two years after baseline screening. This may have introduced selection bias, as those at highest risk of early death and GC may have been systematically excluded, potentially biasing our estimates toward the null. Moreover, this exclusion precluded proper consideration of competing risks, such as death prior to cancer diagnosis, which may have provided a more realistic depiction of risk trajectories. Fourth, participants with missing data on key confounders such as smoking and alcohol consumption were excluded from the analysis, potentially introducing additional selection bias. The use of multiple imputation or other missing data techniques may be useful to explore in future studies to assess the robustness of findings. Fifth, H. pylori infection was inferred from records of eradication therapy rather than confirmed by diagnostic testing. This proxy definition may have misclassified untreated infected individuals as uninfected and could not account for treatment failure or unsuccessful eradication, leading to non-differential misclassification and a potential bias toward the null. In addition, because gastric inflammation and mucosal injury may gradually regress following successful eradication, the risk estimates derived from treated individuals may underestimate the true magnitude of risk associated with persistent active infection. Therefore, our findings likely represent a conservative estimate of the carcinogenic effect of ongoing H. pylori infection. In addition, H. pylori eradication therapy may have been under-ascertained in the claims data, because only reimbursed prescriptions are captured; eradication regimens prescribed as non-reimbursed (out-of-pocket) treatments were not available, which could have further contributed to exposure misclassification. Sixth, we lacked bacteriological data on H. pylori virulence genotypes. However, in Korea and East Asia, H. pylori strains show very high homogeneity, with near-universal CagA positivity (East-Asian EPIYA-D type) and predominant vacA s1/i1/m1 genotypes [32, 33]. Because these virulence markers vary little within this population and are post-exposure attributes of the infection, they are unlikely to introduce meaningful unmeasured confounding in our analysis. Lastly, information on histologic subtype and tumor location was not consistently available; therefore, our results reflect overall GC risk rather than subtype- or site-specific associations.

Conclusions

Using a large, nationally representative database from a population with high H. pylori prevalence, we found that clinically diagnosed AG/IM account for only a limited fraction of the H. pylori–associated risk of GC. Importantly, this finding indicates that a substantial proportion of GCs may arise from H. pylori–exposed stomachs even in the absence of clinically recognized intermediate lesions, underscoring that H. pylori infection itself is a dominant determinant of GC risk. These results indicate that, although the Correa pathway has long been regarded as the predominant route to GC, pathways beyond the classical sequence may play an important role in gastric carcinogenesis in Korea, at least among individuals aged 40–74 years. Although early detection and management of AG or IM remain relevant for individuals who follow this pathway, our results underscore that effective population-level prevention of GC and adenoma should primarily focus on preventing H. pylori infection itself.
