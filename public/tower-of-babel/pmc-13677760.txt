Exploring the potential benefits of Shirodhara as an add-on therapy for alleviating post-COVID anxiety and depression: A case study
Authors: Manoj Shamkuwar, Anandaraman PV, Dinesh Kataria, Santosh Bhatted, Bhavna Bhatia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
In this case study, we present a 31-year-old female, a homemaker, who developed persistent symptoms of anxiousness, fatigue, weakness, irritation, anger issues, and difficulty concentrating for nearly 12 months following recovery from COVID-19. She was diagnosed by a consulting psychiatrist with post-COVID anxiety with depression, and had been on anxiolytic and antidepressant medications for the past ten months. She underwent Shirodhara (dripping of medicated oil on the forehead) using Balashwagandhadi taila for 14 days as an add-on therapy, while continuing her conventional medications. Assessments at baseline, 8th day, 15th day, and 30th day showed progressive improvements: Hamilton Anxiety Rating Scale (HAM-A) scores reduced from 42 to 10, Hamilton Depression Rating Scale (HDRS) from 23 to 6, Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9) from 24 to 1, and Flanagan Quality of Life Scale (QOLS) improved from 59 to 79 by the 30th day. Serum cortisol was 3.09 μg/dL at baseline, below the normal range, and returned to the normal physiological range (11.76 μg/dL) on the 15th day, reflecting improved stress adaptation. C-Reactive Protein remained stable, while Systemic Immune-Inflammation Index showed a transient, non-pathological rise within normal limits. Also, based on the evaluation and recommendation of the psychiatrist, the dosage of conventional medications was reduced: Propranolol was tapered from 20 mg twice daily to once daily, Clonazepam 0.5 mg at bedtime was shifted to as-needed use, and Omeprazole was discontinued. This case highlights the potential role of Shirodhara as an adjunctive therapy in post-COVID anxiety, along with depression. These findings require validation through larger studies with long-term follow-up.

Introduction

The emergence of SARS-CoV-2 in late 2019 led to a global health crisis, with the WHO declaring it a pandemic in March 2020 [1]. While much attention has been given to acute respiratory and cardiovascular complications, post-COVID-19 symptoms, including neuropsychiatric manifestations, remain a growing concern. Studies suggest that recovered patients experience varied symptoms, including fatigue, headache, sleep disturbances, myalgia, etc. [[2], [3], [4]] Psychiatric symptoms such as Post-Traumatic Stress Disorder (PTSD), anxiety, and depression have been observed in previous coronavirus outbreaks and are now increasingly reported in COVID-19 survivors as well [[5], [6], [7]]. Persistent symptoms may result from a combination of biological and psychological factors, including prolonged viral presence in brain tissue, immune disturbances, hypocortisolemia, and chronic stress responses resembling chronic fatigue syndrome [[8], [9], [10]].

Managing post-COVID neuropsychiatric symptoms requires a multidisciplinary approach. Current interventions include pharmacological treatments such as Selective serotonin receptor inhibitors (SSRI), tricyclic and tetracyclic antidepressants, serotonin and nor-epinephrine receptor inhibitors (SNRI), monoamine oxidase (MAO) inhibitors, alongside psychotherapy techniques like Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT), mindfulness-based therapy, interpersonal therapy etc. [[11], [12], [13]] However, traditional antidepressants often have side effects, leading to low adherence. Prolonged use of conventional antidepressants is linked to tremors, sexual challenges, weight gain, etc. along with low adherence due to dependency-related concerns [[13], [14], [15]].

There is scattered information on mental disorders and treatments in Classical Ayurveda texts. The diseases which affects the psyche, can be classified as Rajasika (disorders of psyche caused by increase in Rajo guna in the individual such as excessive anger, jealousy, etc.) and Tamasika (disorders of psyche caused by increase in Tama guna in the individual such as grief, slothfulness, excessive sleepiness, etc.) [16].

According to Ayurveda, it can be closely related to disorders of Manovaha srotasa (channels or pathways that carry and regulate mental processes) like - Mano-avasada (depression), Vishada (depression), and Chittodvega (agitation/anxiety). According to the National Ayurveda Morbidity Code (NAMC), Manovaha srotovikara is classified under the code EM.

Shirodhara (dripping of medicated oil or other suitable liquid on the forehead) is a unique non-invasive Panchakarma therapy which promotes Mana-sthairya (stability of mind) [17]. It is known to be effective in treating insomnia, anxiety, stress, depression, etc. [[18], [19], [20], [21], [22], [23]] This case study aims to assess the effect of Shirodhara as an add-on therapy along with conventional medication in managing post-COVID anxiety with depression.

Case report

Patient information

The patient was a 31-year-old female, a homemaker, who had no prior history of psychiatric illness. She contracted COVID-19 in December 2021–January 2022 and, after an initial moderate illness managed at home with isolation, she recovered physically within 1–2 weeks. However, her psychological symptoms started soon after recovery and were continuous in nature, persisting for nearly 12 months. These included anxiousness, fatigue, weakness, irritation, anger issues, and loss of concentration. In April 2022, she sought psychiatric consultation at Lady Hardinge Medical College (LHMC), Delhi, where she was diagnosed with post-COVID anxiety and depression based on her history and DSM-5 criteria. She had been on anxiolytic and antidepressant medications for the past ten months, but continued to experience persistent symptoms. While there was partial relief, her symptoms of anxiety and depression remained significant, leading her to seek further management at the Panchakarma Outpatient Department (OPD) of Ayurvedic and Unani Tibbia College and Hospital (AUTCH), New Delhi in February 2023. The patient's informed written consent was obtained before the examination and procedure.

Clinical findings

General examination and personal history

The patient's overall health was satisfactory. There was no deviation in vital signs. The patient led a sedentary lifestyle and had a Body Mass Index (BMI) of 22.1 Kg/m2. There was no previous history of substance abuse. Family history - Her mother suffered from anxiety as well. The biochemical parameters, like Complete Blood Count (CBC), liver, and kidney function tests, were within the standard limit.

Nadi (pulse) was Vata-Pitta dominant; Mala (bowel) was Samyaka (normal), with occasional constipation; Mutra (urination) was Samyaka; Jihva (tongue) was Sama (coated tongue indicating the presence of Ama [metabolic toxins]); Shabda (speech) was low-pitched and unsteady; Drika (vision) and Akruti (body build/appearance) were Prakrita (normal); and Sparsha (skin/tactile examination) revealed mild dryness of the skin. Additionally, Kshudha (appetite) was normal, while Nidra (sleep) was Khandita (interrupted) and Alpa (reduced). The patient's Prakriti (constitutional type) was assessed as Vataja-Pittaja (predominantly Vata-Pitta) with Tamasika-Rajasika Manasika Prakriti (mental constitution dominated by Tama and Raja). Samhanana (body compactness) was Madhyama (moderate), whereas Satva (mental strength) and Vyayama Shakti (physical endurance) were Avara (low).

The identified Nidana (aetiological factors) included mental stress, grief, and the psychological after-effects of COVID-19. The Rupa (clinical features) included persistent anxiousness, irritability, restlessness, disturbed sleep, and poor concentration, which were consistent with the clinical presentation of Chittodvega (anxiety). In addition, low mood, reduced interest in activities, low-pitched speech, and diminished emotional expression were indicative of associated Mano-avasada (depressive state). Collectively, these findings suggested Manovaha-srotodushti (vitiation of the Manovaha Srotasa, i.e., the channels responsible for the flow of thoughts, emotions, and mental processes).

Based on the patient's history and overall clinical assessment, differential diagnoses such as Anavasthita-chitta (mental instability), Kaphaja Unmada (Kapha-predominant psychotic disorder), Chittodvega (anxiety), and Mano-avasada/Vishada (depressive state) were considered. Considering the predominance of anxiety-related features with associated depressive manifestations, the final Ayurvedic diagnosis was established as Chittodvega (anxiety) associated with Mano-avasada (depressive state), arising from Tridoshaja (involvement of all three Doshas) imbalance with predominance of Vata Dosha (principle governing movement and neural regulation) and Tama Guna (mental attribute of inertia, dullness, and ignorance) affecting the Mana (mind).

Therapeutic intervention and assessment

Therapeutic intervention

The patient was admitted to the Panchakarma inpatient department (IPD) for 15 days, during which Shirodhara therapy was administered and the patient continued taking conventional medications as prescribed by the Psychiatrist at LHMC, including Paroxetine 25 mg once daily, Propranolol 20 mg twice daily, Omeprazole 20 mg once daily, and Clonazepam 0.5 mg at bedtime.

Shirodhara was performed using Balashwagandhadi taila, a polyherbal formulation that comprises Bala (Sida cordifolia) and Ashwagandha (Withania somnifera) as primary herbal ingredients and Tila taila (oil of Sesamum indicum) as the base. It has been prescribed for Sarva Jwara (all types of fever), Unmada (insanity), Kshaya (emaciation), Kasa (cough), and Vividha Vataroga (diseases caused by Vata dosha aggravation) [24]. Since anxiety along with depression (Manovaha-srotovikara) is due to Vata-predominant Tridoshaja (three regulatory functional factors of the body) imbalance, therefore, Balashwagandhadi taila was chosen for Shirodhara therapy [25]. It was purchased from a Good Manufacturing Practices (GMP) certified company with batch number 216284, manufacturing date was 5/09/2022.

After completion of the 14-day Shirodhara intervention, the patient was advised with Brahmi vati 250 mg twice daily, in addition to the prescribed conventional oral medication, and was subsequently followed up on an outpatient department (OPD) basis.

Shirodhara was planned in the following way:-

Purvakarma (Preparatory procedures)

The patient was examined and determined to be a candidate for Shirodhara due to anxiety, mild depression, headache, and disturbed sleep.

Preparation of the patient

After the patient had completed her natural urges, such as bowel and bladder evacuation, she was given a head and neck-shoulder massage. A thick cotton cord was tied around the head (just above the eyebrows and ears) to prevent the oil from getting into the eyes. Cotton pads were used to cover the eyes. Cotton buds were used to close the ears. The patient was instructed to lie supine on the Droni (A wooden table designed to collect and drain medicated oils/liquids during therapies). A towel was rolled up and placed beneath the neck.

Preparation of drug

On induction, the balashwagandhadi oil was warmed to 40.0°C ± 0.2°C and kept at that temperature throughout the procedure (The patient confirmed whether the temperature was sukhoshna (lukewarm) or not).

Pradhana karma (Main therapeutic procedures)

Lukewarm Balashwagandhadi oil was poured into the Dharapatra (a bronze dripping apparatus, with a varti inserted into the nozzle). Varti (cotton thread) was hanged down so that the tip of the Varti from the patient's forehead was 8 cm and Dharapatra was 20 cm above the patient's forehead.

The oil was then allowed to flow through Varti on the forehead in a continuous stream. During the procedure, the vessel was oscillated, causing oil to drip all over the patient's forehead.

Intermittent scalp massage was performed.

The oil that had drained from the head was collected, reheated, and then used for Dhara (stream). Throughout the procedure, the Dhara was kept continuous, and the temperature was kept constant. Shirodhara was performed for 45 minutes per day for 14 days. The procedure took place between 9:00 a.m. to 10:00 a.m.

Pashchata karma (post-therapy procedures)

The cord and earplugs were both removed. The excess oil from the scalp was squeezed out before Talam (applying Rasnadi churna to the crown of the head). The patient was advised to take a bath with lukewarm water after half an hour and to get some rest. The patient was advised to follow a light diet.

Patient assessment

Prior to treatment (at baseline), the subjective parameters were assessed using HAM-A, HAM-D, PHQ-9, and QOLS; the second assessment was completed on the eighth day, the third assessment on the fifteenth day, and the fourth assessment on the thirtieth day. At the baseline, Sr. cortisol and other biochemical parameters were measured, and thereafter on the 15th day, i.e. after the 14th Shirodhara therapy.

The Systemic Immune-Inflammation Index (SII) increased from 245.82 to 408.72, though it remained within the normal physiological range (<500 in healthy individuals). SII, while valuable as a general marker of systemic inflammation and immune response, is not disease-specific and may be influenced by various factors such as infection, physical or psychological stress, or ongoing inflammation. Therefore, this change should be interpreted in the broader context of the patient's overall recovery rather than as a direct indicator of disease activity. Given the concurrent clinical improvements and normalization of other parameters, the increment within the normal range likely reflects a transient, non-pathological immune readjustment during convalescence.

Additionally, liver function tests (LFT), kidney function tests (KFT), and complete blood count (CBC) remained within normal limits, confirming the safety of the procedure. No adverse effects were reported during or after the therapy.

Reduction in conventional medication use

Following Shirodhara as an add-on therapy, the Psychiatry consultant at LHMC reduced the dose and frequency of conventional medications. Paroxetine was continued at 25 mg once daily; Propranolol was tapered from 20 mg twice daily to 20 mg once daily; Clonazepam 0.5 mg at bedtime was changed to as-needed/if required use; and Omeprazole was discontinued.

Latest follow-up

During telephonic follow-up in June 2026, the patient reported sustained improvement in anxiety, mood, sleep quality, and daily functioning compared with her pre-treatment status. She further reported that, under psychiatric supervision, all conventional medications had been gradually discontinued, and she has remained medication-free for the past year without recurrence of significant symptoms.

Discussion

This case report describes a 31-year-old woman presenting with persistent symptoms of anxiety and depression for over one year following COVID-19 infection. Based on clinical history and DSM-5 criteria, a diagnosis of Post-COVID Anxiety with Depressive features was established. Baseline symptom severity was quantified using standardized clinician-rated and self-reported scales, including HAM-A, HAM-D, PHQ-9, and QOLS. It is noteworthy that these instruments are severity rating scales with overlapping symptom domains and are primarily intended for monitoring disease severity and therapeutic response. Despite regular use of conventional anxiolytics and antidepressants for eight months, the patient reported limited relief. Shirodhara therapy was then introduced as a complementary intervention with the intent to enhance ongoing management.

Globally, there is growing interest in integrative psychiatry, where traditional therapies are being incorporated into standard mental health protocols. Shirodhara, due to its calming and neurohormonal effects, has emerged as a promising adjunct within this model. As a non-pharmacological, external therapy, it may complement conventional medications by improving sleep, mood, and anxiety holistically [19,20,[24], [25], [26]]. Its integration could help reduce drug dosage or minimize side effects, supporting long-term tolerability and better treatment adherence, particularly in individuals sensitive to pharmacological interventions.

Analysis of the subjective parameters revealed that the most significant improvements were observed in core domains of anxiety and depression. On the HAM-A scale, domains such as anxious mood, tension, insomnia, autonomic symptoms, and somatic symptoms (both muscular and cardiovascular) showed marked reduction, indicating improvement in both psychological and somatic aspects of anxiety. In HAM-D, notable improvements were observed in depressed mood, psychic anxiety, insomnia, and somatic symptoms. Quality of Life Scale (QOLS) analysis further demonstrated consistent enhancement across domains of physical and material well-being, relationships with other people, social activities, and fulfillment, indicating not only symptomatic relief but also functional improvement.

In this case, the patient's serum cortisol level was 3.09 μg/dL at baseline, which is below the normal morning reference range of 4.3–22.0 μg/dL (measured between 7 and 9 AM using the chemiluminescence method), indicating hypocortisolemia likely due to HPA axis suppression, as observed in chronic stress, long-COVID, and depression disorders [27]. After 14 days of Shirodhara, administered as an adjunct to conventional pharmacotherapy, the cortisol level rose to 11.76 μg/dL, entering the normal range. This normalization suggests recovery of HPA axis homeostasis and improved physiological adaptation to stress.

The biochemical improvement was paralleled by clinical changes, as shown by significant reductions in HAM-A, HAM-D, and PHQ-9 scores, along with an increase in QOLS score between baseline and day 15, sustained through day 30. Together, these findings indicate that the integrative approach contributed to both neuroendocrine recovery and psychological well-being.

Shirodhara may exert its therapeutic action through gentle vibratory and thermal stimulation to the forehead and scalp, influencing the autonomic nervous system and higher brain centers. Studies have shown that it promotes alpha and theta brain wave activity, similar to meditative states, and enhances natural killer cell activity, demonstrating its psycho-neuro-immunological effects [19,21,28]. From a modern biomedical perspective, post-COVID anxiety and depression are thought to arise due to HPA axis dysregulation, hypocortisolemia, and neuroinflammation linked to cytokine storms and viral interaction with Angiotensin-converting enzyme 2 (ACE2) receptors in the hypothalamus and adrenal glands [10,29,30]. Within this mechanistic framework, the normalization of serum cortisol in the present case serves as an objective biochemical correlate of improved neuroendocrine regulation, complementing the classical Ayurvedic explanation of Shirodhara's therapeutic action discussed below.

According to Ayurveda, Chittodvega (anxiety) associated with Mano-avasada (depression) is understood as a Tridoshaja (involving all three Doshas) disorder with predominance of aggravated Vata Dosha (the bio-regulatory principle governing movement and neural functions), particularly Prana Vata (the subtype regulating higher mental and cognitive functions), along with disturbance of Sadhaka Pitta (the subtype governing intellect, emotions, and higher mental functions) and predominance of Raja and Tama (psychological attributes responsible for mental agitation and inertia), ultimately leading to Manovaha Srotodushti (vitiation of the channels governing mental functions) [31]. In the present case, persistent anxiousness, restlessness, disturbed sleep, poor concentration, and emotional instability reflected this underlying Ayurvedic pathogenesis. Accordingly, the therapeutic effect of Shirodhara with Balashwagandhadi Taila can be understood because, as a classical Murdhni Taila (therapeutic oil application over the head) procedure, it pacifies aggravated Vata and Pitta Doshas (bio-regulatory principles governing movement and metabolism), stabilizes the Mana (mind), promotes Indriya Prasada (clarity and integrity of the senses), facilitates Suswapna (sound sleep), and restores the normal functioning of the Manovaha Srotasa (channels governing mental functions) [17,[32], [33], [34]].

Thus, the therapeutic outcomes observed in this patient demonstrate concordance between the classical Ayurvedic actions of Shirodhara and contemporary clinical assessment. The improvement observed across the validated HAM-A, HAM-D, PHQ-9, and QOLS assessments reflects restoration of mental stability as described in the classical texts, while normalization of serum cortisol serves as an additional objective correlate supporting improved regulation of the stress response rather than constituting the primary explanation for the therapeutic effect.

Conventional antidepressants and anxiolytics primarily act through monoamine modulation—affecting serotonin, norepinephrine, and dopamine pathways [35]. In contrast, Shirodhara appears to influence autonomic regulation, cortisol levels, and brainwave activity (α and θ waves), thereby offering a multimodal effect on the stress response [19,21,23,28]. This makes it a promising complementary therapy, particularly in chronic or treatment-resistant cases or cases not responding to medication alone.

This case does not advocate replacing pharmacotherapy with Ayurvedic treatments but emphasizes a complementary and integrative approach. The observed clinical improvement and normalization of serum cortisol were likely achieved through the combined effect of modern pharmacological treatment and traditional Ayurvedic therapy. This supports the concept of Shirodhara as an effective adjunct that may enhance the efficacy of standard care in treating post-COVID anxiety and depression conditions.

Further controlled studies with larger sample sizes are warranted to confirm these observations and better define the mechanisms by which Shirodhara complements conventional treatments.

Conclusion

Shirodhara improved the serum biomarker, i.e., Serum Cortisol, which increased to normal range after Shirodhara. Subjective symptoms improved significantly as depicted by the scoring of the assessment scales used. Somatic anxiety (physical symptoms associated with anxiety) and psychic anxiety (mental agitation and psychological distress) both improved significantly. In addition, with an increased sense of well-being, the dose and frequency of allopathic medications were reduced by the Psychiatry consultant at LHMC as well.

Patient perspective

After three days of Shirodhara, the patient reported a sound and regular sleep pattern. The patient's anxiety level had significantly decreased. Sadness, hopelessness, helplessness, and the frequency of headache also significantly reduced. Furthermore, the patient began to show an interest/pleasure in doing things. The patient began to feel more energized as well. Concentration also improved. The patient felt relaxed, sleepy, and happy after the Shirodhara therapy.

Informed consent

The patient provided written informed consent for this case study.
