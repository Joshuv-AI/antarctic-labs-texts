Radiologic Progression of Early-Stage Ankle Osteoarthritis: A Mean 12-Year Follow-up Observational Study
Authors: Myoung Yeol Shin, Jae Hyeon Seo, Young Kwan Moon, Jin Kyung Kim, Young Rak Choi, Ho Seong Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe natural history of early-stage ankle osteoarthritis (OA) remains poorly defined, and the long-term outcomes of nonoperative management are unclear. This study investigated long-term Takakura stage progression and associated risk factors in early-stage ankle OA managed nonoperatively.MethodsIn this study, 57 patients (89 ankles) with Takakura stage I-IIIA ankle OA who were followed up for a mean of 12.1 years were retrospectively reviewed. At the first and final visits, radiographic parameters including Takakura stage, mechanical axis deviation (MAD), hindfoot alignment angle (HAA), hindfoot moment arm (HMA), tibial articular surface angle (TAS), tibial lateral surface angle (TLS), and talar tilt angle (TT) were measured. Clinical outcomes were assessed using the Foot Function Index (FFI) and visual analog scale (VAS). Risk factors for stage progression were evaluated using multivariable logistic regression.ResultsOf the 89 ankles, 50 (56.2%) showed no Takakura stage progression, 35 (39.3%) progressed by 1 stage, and 4 (4.5%) progressed by 2 stages. Among ankles classified as stage IIIA at baseline, progression to stage IIIB occurred more frequently in those with hindfoot varus deformity (p = 0.023). In the progression group, HMA, HAA, and TT increased (all p < 0.001), whereas TAS and TLS decreased (p < 0.001 and p = 0.045, respectively). In multivariable analysis, HAA (odds ratio [OR], 2.49; p = 0.076), HMA (OR, 2.09; p = 0.085), and TT (OR, 1.38; p = 0.064) showed statistical trends toward association with progression, whereas TAS, TLS, MAD, initial stage, sex, age, and body mass index (BMI) were not significant predictors. The VAS score improved in the no-progression group (p = 0.008) and worsened in the progression group (p = 0.006), while changes in FFI were not significant.ConclusionsIn this 12.1-year follow-up observational study of early-stage ankle OA, no radiographic progression was observed in 56.2% of ankles, 1-stage progression in 39.3%, and 2-stage progression in 4.5%. Progression from stage IIIA to stage IIIB occurred more frequently in ankles with hindfoot varus. Lower limb alignment and clinical factors (sex, age, and BMI) were not significantly associated with OA progression.

Among the treatment options for ankle osteoarthritis (OA), surgical interventions such as total ankle arthroplasty (TAA) or arthrodesis are primarily considered in cases of end-stage ankle OA.12) However, treatment guidelines for early and intermediate-stage ankle OA remain controversial. In early-stage OA, ligament-balancing and corrective osteotomy are commonly performed to achieve realignment.34) Nevertheless, the optimal surgical technique and the extent of postoperative correction are being debated, and whether these procedures effectively halt OA progression remains unclear. Therefore, an understanding of the natural progression of ankle OA is important in determining treatment strategies.

In a prospective short-term cohort study, only 4% of patients with ankle OA exhibited radiographic progression over a mean follow-up of 3.5 years.5) Additionally, a retrospective cohort study reported radiographic progression in 48.5% of 97 ankles over 4.9 years.6) However, because these studies were limited to short-term follow-ups (< 5 years), their findings do not adequately reflect long-term OA progression. Another study examined the risk factors for OA progression in varus-aligned ankles and reported 57% disease progression over a 99-month follow-up, identifying talar tilt angle (TT) as a key predictor.7) However, it included only patients with preexisting varus deformity of the hindfoot; as such, its findings may not be generalizable to patients with early-stage OA and normal alignment. Hence, further research should assess disease progression in patients with early ankle OA through long-term studies.

This study aimed to analyze the long-term natural course of ankle OA progression in patients without surgical intervention with an average follow-up of 12.1 years (minimum of 8 years). We also evaluated whether radiographic parameters and patient-related factors were associated with disease progression. No universally accepted definition of early-stage ankle arthritis is available, but it can be inferred by referencing existing radiographic-based arthritis classification systems. The Kellgren-Lawrence classification8) can be applied to OA of any joint, but it is considered crude. Conversely, the Takakura classification9) is widely used as an ankle OA-specific classification.

Generally, stages IIIB and IV are considered indications for total ankle replacement or ankle arthrodesis, whereas stages I, II, and IIIA are not indications for joint-sacrificing surgery. However, the appropriate treatment options for these early stages are still being debated. Therefore, in this study, early ankle OA was defined as Takakura stage I-IIIA, and the natural history of these stages was investigated.

METHODS

This retrospective longitudinal observational study was conducted with the approval of the Research Ethics Review Committee of Asan Medical Center (IRB No. S2023-1477-0003) and a waiver for obtaining patient consent.

Study Design and Patient Selection

Patients who met the following inclusion criteria were included: those who initially visited the hospital from September 2006 to December 2012 and were diagnosed with early ankle OA (Takakura stage I, II, IIIA) on the weight-bearing anteroposterior (AP) radiographs. Among 370 ankles of these patients, those with the following conditions were excluded: (1) history of ankle fractures, (2) intra-articular infection, (3) previous bone surgery around the ankle, (4) neuropathic disease such as Charcot arthritis, and (5) ankle OA with a severe flat foot. One hundred sixty-nine patients with early-stage ankle OA were included initially. Of the 169 patients, 97 patients were excluded during the follow-up period because they either died (8 patients) or were followed up for less than 8 years (89 patients). Furthermore, 15 patients were excluded because they underwent surgical intervention either at our hospital (6 patients) or at another hospital (9 patients) during the follow-up period. As a result, a total of 89 ankles of 57 patients were enrolled. The mean follow-up period was 12.1 years (range, 8–18 years) (Fig. 1).

Outpatient Treatment

At the initial outpatient clinic visit, the patients received a standard conservative treatment. They were advised to take cyclooxygenase-2-selective nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (COX-2 NSAIDs) intermittently during periods of pain, although these NSAIDs were rarely used. Footwear modifications or insoles were also recommended to alleviate their symptoms. In selective rare cases, intra-articular corticosteroid injection was administered intermittently to the anteromedial side of the ankle joint. Immobilizers such as splints and braces were not recommended.

Walking on uneven surfaces, such as hiking trails, was not advised, but no other activity restrictions were imposed, allowing patients to continue participating in low-impact exercises such as swimming, cycling, golf, and jogging. Single heel-raising exercises were encouraged to prevent muscle weakness. During follow-up, 6 patients experienced persistent pain and opted for surgical intervention. They underwent joint-preserving periarticular osteotomy and were subsequently excluded from this study.

Radiographic Assessment

Radiographic assessment included standardized weight-bearing AP and lateral ankle views, hindfoot radiographs obtained at the first visit and the final follow-up, and whole-leg radiographs obtained for leg alignment evaluation. Ankle OA stage was evaluated using the Takakura classification system,9) and progression was defined by changes in the stage between the initial and final radiographs. Hindfoot valgus or varus was also assessed on the hindfoot alignment view. In patients with stage II or IIIA, the TT angle was measured on weight-bearing AP radiographs.

On the weight-bearing ankle AP view, the tibial articular surface angle (TAS)10) was defined as the angle between the tibial axis determined by connecting the tibial centers at 8 cm and 13 cm proximal to the plafond and a line parallel to the tibial plafond. The TT angle was defined as the angle between the tibial plafond and the talar dome (Fig. 2A). On the lateral view, the tibial lateral surface angle (TLS) was measured as the angle between the tibial axis and the distal tibial articular surface (Fig. 2B).

On the hindfoot alignment view, the hindfoot alignment angle (HAA)1112) was defined as the angle between the tibial axis and a line drawn from the most inferior point of the calcaneus to its intersection with the ankle joint line. The hindfoot moment arm (HMA)1314) was defined as the perpendicular distance from the most inferior calcaneal point to the tibial axis and recorded as positive when this point was medial to the axis (Fig. 2C). Hindfoot deformity was classified according to the criteria of Choi et al.12): neutral (–4° ≤ HAA ≤ 4°), varus (HAA > 4°), and valgus (HAA < –4°).

On the whole-leg weight-bearing x-ray, the mechanical axis (Mikulicz line15)) was defined as a line drawn from the femoral head center to the ankle center. The distance from the knee center to this line was defined as the mechanical axis deviation (MAD) (Fig. 2D): neutral (–6 mm ≤ MAD ≤ 6 mm), varus (MAD > 6 mm), and valgus (MAD < –6 mm).

Clinical Assessment

The Foot Function Index (FFI)16) and visual analog scale (VAS)17) were measured at the initial and last visit. The VAS measure of pain is a 10-point scale, where 10 indicates the worst pain. The FFI score ranges from 0 to 230, where a higher score indicates more pain and disability. The association of patient-related factors such as age, sex, and body mass index (BMI) with ankle OA progression was also analyzed.

Statistical Analysis

Ankle OA progression according to the presence of hindfoot deformity was compared using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test (for cases with 2- or 3-stage progression). Radiographic parameters (Takakura stage, HAA, HMA, TAS, TLS, and TT) were compared between the first visit and the last follow-up. Data normality was assessed using the F test. If normally distributed, comparisons were made with the paired t-test; otherwise, the Wilcoxon signed-rank test was applied.

Multivariable logistic regression analysis was performed to identify radiographic parameters (HAA, HMA, TAS, TLS, TT, MAD, and initial stage) and clinical factors (sex, age, and BMI) associated with ankle OA progression. Differences in VAS and FFI scores between the first and last follow-up were evaluated using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test. For interobserver reliability, the Takakura classification and ankle/hindfoot parameters were independently assessed by 2 experienced orthopedic foot and ankle specialists (MYS and JKK). The standardized weight-bearing AP and lateral ankle views and hindfoot and whole-leg radiographs were evaluated by each observer twice, with a 4-week interval between assessments. Inter- and intraobserver reliabilities were expressed using the intraclass correlation coefficient and classified as follows: > 0.90, excellent; 0.75–0.90, good; 0.50–0.75, moderate; and < 0.50, poor. Data were statistically analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 29 (IBM Corp.), and a 2-sided significance level was set at p < 0.05.

RESULTS

Demographic Data of the Patients

A total of 57 patients (men: 26, women: 31) with 89 affected ankle joints were included in the study. The average age at the initial visit was 58.3 years (range, 33–76 years), and the mean BMI was 27.6 kg/m2 (range, 20.8–34.6 kg/m2). In accordance with the World Health Organization BMI classification, 47.4% of patients (27 / 57) were categorized as overweight (BMI 25.0–29.9 kg/m2), 26.3% (15 / 57) were obese (BMI ≥ 30.0 kg/m2), and only 26.3% (15 / 57) had a normal weight (BMI 18.5–24.9 kg/m2). The average follow-up period was 12.1 years (range, 8–18 years). At the initial visit, the distribution of their age was as follows: 1 patient was aged ≤ 39 years, 7 were 40–49 years, 27 were 50–59 years, 17 were 60–69 years, and 5 were ≥ 70 years (Table 1).

Changes in Takakura Stage

The initial Takakura stage classification of the 89 affected ankle joints was as follows: 5 ankles (5.6%) in stage I, 37 ankles (41.6%) in stage II, and 47 ankles (52.8%) in stage IIIA. At the last follow-up, the Takakura stage distribution was as follows: 5 ankles (5.6%) in stage I, 18 ankles (20.2%) in stage II, 45 ankles (50.6%) in stage IIIA, 18 ankles (20.2%) in stage IIIB, and 3 ankles (3.4%) in stage IV (Fig. 3).

Of the 89 ankles, 50 (56.2%) showed no change, while OA progression was observed in 39 ankles (43.8%) (Table 2). All 5 ankles (100%) with stage I remained unchanged, showing no radiographic stage progression. Of the 37 ankles with stage II, 18 (48.6%) remained unchanged, 18 (48.6%) progressed to stage IIIA, and 1 (2.7%) progressed to stage IIIB. Of the 47 ankles with stage IIIA, 27 (57.4%) had no change, 17 (36.2%) progressed to stage IIIB, and 3 (6.4%) progressed to stage IV (Fig. 3).

Of the 89 ankles, 29 (32.6%) had varus heel on the initial hindfoot radiograph (stage I: 1 / 5 cases; stage II: 7 / 37 cases; and stage IIIA: 21 / 47 cases). One varus heel with stage I remained unchanged, showing no stage progression. Among 7 varus heels with stage II, 3 ankles (42.9%) showed no change, 3 ankles (42.9%) progressed to stage IIIA, and 1 ankle (14.3%) progressed to stage IIIB. Among the 21 varus heels with stage IIIA, 9 ankles (42.9%) showed no change, 11 ankles (52.4%) progressed to stage IIIB, and 1 ankle (4.8%) progressed to stage IV (Fig. 4, Table 2).

The results of the comparison of ankle OA progression between patients with and without varus hindfoot deformity are summarized in Table 2. Among the ankles without hindfoot deformity, 37 of 60 (61.7%) showed no progression, whereas only 13 of 29 (44.8%) in the varus hindfoot group remained unchanged. Overall, no radiographic progression was observed in most ankles regardless of the presence of hindfoot varus deformity. The groups with and without hindfoot varus deformity did not significantly differ in terms of no change (p = 0.173), 1-stage progression (excluding IIIA to IIIB, p = 0.254), or 2-stage progression (p = 0.213). However, progression from stage IIIA to stage IIIB was significantly more frequent in the varus hindfoot group than in the other group (p = 0.023).

Analysis of the Relationship between Radiographic Parameters, Clinical Factors, and OA

The radiographic changes during follow-up are summarized in Table 2. The Takakura stage significantly progressed over time (p = 0.002). In the no-progression group, HMA increased slightly (p < 0.001); HAA also increased (p = 0.035). By comparison, TAS (p = 0.256), TLS (p = 0.201), and TT (p = 0.130) did not significantly change. Conversely, HMA (p < 0.001), HAA (p < 0.001), and TT (p < 0.001) significantly increased in the progression group, whereas TAS (p < 0.001) and TLS (p = 0.045) significantly decreased. These findings indicated that progressive structural changes in the ankle articular surface, accompanied by an increased TT, developed over time in ankles with radiographic progression.

Multivariable logistic regression analysis was performed to identify the radiographic and clinical factors associated with ankle OA progression (Table 3). HAA (odds ratio [OR], 2.49; 95% CI, 0.93–7.25; p = 0.076), HMA (OR, 2.09; 95% CI, 0.94–3.26; p = 0.085), and TT (OR, 1.38; 95% CI, 0.92–1.89; p = 0.064) showed statistical trends toward association with progression; however, these associations did not reach statistical significance. Conversely, TAS, TLS, MAD, initial stage on the first visit radiograph, and clinical factors, including sex, age, and BMI, were not significantly associated with OA progression.

Clinical Outcomes

In the no-progression group, the mean VAS score decreased from 4.11 (range, 1–8) at the initial visit to 3.12 (range, 1–5) at the final follow-up (p = 0.008). The mean FFI increased from 61.34 (range, 21–149) to 68.21 (range, 25–145); however, this finding showed no statistically significant difference (p = 0.231). In the progression group, the mean VAS score increased from 4.22 (range, 1–8) to 4.96 (range, 2–8) (p = 0.006). The mean FFI increased from 64.56 (range, 28–118) to 89.02 (range, 45–175), but this increase was not statistically significant (p = 0.165).

DISCUSSION

In this study, we aimed to determine the long-term radiographic progression rate of early-stage ankle OA and identify the potential risk factors and predictors of OA progression. During the mean follow-up period of 12.1 years, 50 of 89 ankles (56.2%) had no change in Takakura stage. Notably, even among the ankles classified as Takakura stage IIIA at the first visit, 27 of 47 ankles (57.4%) did not show radiographic progression (Table 2, Fig. 3). One-stage progression was observed in 35 of 89 ankles (39.3%); furthermore, 17 of 47 ankles (36.2%) with initial stage IIIA progressed to stage IIIB. In particular, the presence of varus hindfoot deformity was significantly associated with progression from stage IIIA to IIIB (p = 0.023) (Table 2, Fig. 4). In the no-progression group, the mean VAS score decreased from 4.1 to 3.1 (p = 0.008), whereas the FFI score did not significantly change (p = 0.231). In the progression group, the mean VAS score increased from 4.2 to 5.0 (p = 0.006), and the FFI score showed a non-significant trend toward a worsening function (p = 0.165).

The analysis of radiographic parameters demonstrated different longitudinal patterns between the progression and no-progression groups. In the no-progression group, TAS, TLS, and TT did not substantially change during follow-up. Conversely, in the progression group, TAS (p < 0.001) and TLS (p = 0.045) decreased significantly, whereas TT increased significantly (p < 0.001), suggesting a progressive change in the tibiotalar articular surface over time (Table 2). HMA and HAA increased significantly from the initial radiographic assessment in the no-progression group (HMA, p < 0.001; HAA, p = 0.035) and the progression group (HMA, p < 0.001; HAA, p < 0.001); the magnitude of increase in the progression group was greater than that in the no-progression group. These findings suggest that HMA and HAA are dynamic parameters that change over time rather than fixed deformities present at the first visit.

Hindfoot varus deformity has been regarded as both a cause and consequence of ankle OA progression.1819) Although we hypothesized that the presence of hindfoot varus deformity at the first visit would be associated with a higher likelihood of ankle OA progression, hindfoot alignment parameters (HAA and HMA) did not reach statistical significance as initial predictors of subsequent Takakura stage progression (HAA, p = 0.076; HMA, p = 0.085) (Table 3). However, among ankles classified as Takakura stage IIIA at the first visit, those with hindfoot varus deformity were significantly more likely to progress to stage IIIB (p = 0.023) (Table 2), suggesting that hindfoot varus may contribute specifically to progression from stage IIIA to stage IIIB. Representative longitudinal radiographs illustrating both progression and non-progression during the natural course of early-stage ankle OA are shown in Fig. 5. These findings are consistent with previous reports202122) indicating that coronal plane malalignment increases medial ankle loading and may accelerate cartilage degeneration. However, the increase in HAA and HMA in the progression group may not be explained solely by articular wear and increasing TT, as hindfoot varus can occur even with minimal TT due to medial talar translation.2324) Thus, the observed hindfoot varus may reflect tilt-related incongruity, medial translation, or a combination of both mechanisms.

In multivariable logistic regression analysis, HAA (OR, 2.49; 95% CI, 0.93–7.25; p = 0.076), HMA (OR, 2.09; 95% CI, 0.94–3.26; p = 0.085), and TT (OR, 1.38; 95% CI, 0.92–1.89; p = 0.064) showed statistical trends toward association with ankle OA stage progression, although they did not reach statistical significance. MAD (OR, 1.02; 95% CI, 0.91–1.55; p = 0.331) was not associated with progression (Table 3). These findings suggest that HAA, HMA, and TT may still have potential clinical relevance as alignment markers of ankle OA progression and warrant further evaluation in larger cohorts. They likely function as dynamic parameters that change secondarily during disease progression rather than as primary etiologic factors. Progressive articular surface changes, increasing TT, and cartilage wear may lead to sequential alterations in hindfoot alignment over time, limiting the ability of any single parameter measured at the first visit to serve as an independent predictor.

Previous studies2526) emphasized the role of subtalar joint compensation in varus ankle OA progression. Tanaka et al.9) reported that the subtalar joint can provide valgus compensatory alignment to counteract varus ankle OA; however, the loss of this compensatory capacity may allow progression to stage IIIB. Hayashi et al.27) similarly demonstrated that as tibiotalar varus deformity progresses, the subtalar joint tilts into valgus to reduce medial stress; however, once the compensatory mechanism fails, the subtalar joint itself tilts into varus, consequently increasing the medial overload and causing potential progression to end-stage OA. Collectively, these studies have suggested that the loss of subtalar compensation represents a critical turning point in ankle OA progression. A recent 3-dimensional analysis28) using the Go-ICP algorithm further supports this concept by showing that maintaining the coronal plane compensatory function in advanced stages is difficult; once this threshold is exceeded, tibiotalar varus is transmitted to the subtalar joint, leading to hindfoot malalignment. In addition, previous studies2324) have suggested that coronal malalignment in ankle OA may arise not only from varus TT but also from medial translation of the talus. This indicates that subtalar compensation may respond to both angular deformity and translational malalignment in the early stage. Therefore, our findings support a model in which the subtalar joint partially compensates for coronal malalignment in the early stage, whereas loss of this compensatory capacity leads to progressive hindfoot varus, increasing HAA, HMA, and TT, and subsequent ankle OA progression.

Various risk factors for ankle OA progression have been reported in the literature. Jaleel et al.5) reported that smoking, prior ankle trauma, concomitant joint disease, and higher BMI are risk factors for ankle OA progression at 2–5 years of follow-up. Kurokawa et al.7) identified TT angle (cutoff, 2.0°) and age (cutoff, 67 years) as risk factors for the progression of varus ankle OA. Murray et al.29) showed that female sex is associated with OA progression. However, in the present study, patient characteristics (sex, age, and BMI) and initial radiographic parameters (TAS, TLS, MAD and initial stage) measured at the first visit were not significant predictors of ankle OA progression.

This study has several limitations. First, because it was not prospective and a substantial number of patients were lost to follow-up, selection bias may have been introduced, potentially leading to underestimation of the true progression rate. Second, follow-up evaluations were not performed at regular intervals, limiting our ability to fully capture cumulative radiographic and clinical changes over time. Third, potential confounders, such as occupation, comorbidities, and physical activity level, could not be fully controlled. Fourth, talar translation was not directly quantified; therefore, it was not possible to determine whether the observed hindfoot varus progression was predominantly tilt-driven, translation-driven, or mixed. Future studies should incorporate more detailed, stratified analyses of lower-limb alignment, including the knee, ankle, hindfoot, and forefoot, as well as direct translation-related measurements or weight-bearing computed tomography to better clarify the prognostic significance of parameters that showed statistical trends toward association in this study (HAA, HMA, and TT).

In conclusion, this long-term retrospective study demonstrated that over an average follow-up of 12.1 years, 50 of 89 ankles (56.2%) showed no radiographic progression, 35 ankles (39.3%) showed 1-stage progression, and 4 ankles (4.5%) showed 2-stage progression. Progression from stage IIIA to stage IIIB occurred more frequently in ankles with hindfoot varus deformity than in those without this condition. In the stage-progression group, TAS and TLS decreased, whereas TT increased over time. HAA and HMA also increased, with a greater magnitude of change than that in the no-progression group, consistent with progressive varus malalignment. In multivariable analysis, HAA, HMA, and TT showed statistical trends toward association with stage progression, whereas other factors were not significant predictors of ankle OA progression. Overall, TT and hindfoot alignment may serve as clinically relevant markers of ankle OA progression, possibly reflecting reduced subtalar compensatory capacity. They may also help provide a basis for individualized follow-up and treatment strategies.
