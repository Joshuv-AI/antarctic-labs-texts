Examining the mediating effect of real and cyber self-control on cyberbullying and health risk behaviors among secondary vocational students in China: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Jinyang Wang, Hailian Zhang, Di Tian, Yaqin An, Yu Wang, Yunhe Zheng
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCyberbullying and health risk behaviors are pervasive issues for secondary vocational students that not only detrimentally impact their academic achievement but also pose a significant threat to overall health. The purpose of this study was to understand the current situation of cyberbullying and health risk behaviors among secondary vocational students in China and to explore the relationships among cyberbullying, self-control, self-control over internet usage, and health risk behaviors through a mediation model.MethodsThis was a cross-sectional study. From March to April 2023, a self-administered questionnaire was completed by a cluster sample of 1184 students from grades 1 to 3 at three secondary vocational schools in Jilin Province. The researchers utilized a sociodemographic questionnaire, the Cyberbullying and Cybervictimization Scale (CAV), the Adolescent Health Related Risky Behavior Inventory (AHRBI), the Self-Control Scale (SCS), and the Internet Usage Self-Control Scale (IUSCS-CS) to assess the prevalence and impact of cyberbullying and health risk behaviors. Descriptive statistics, spearman correlation analyses. Finally, the Process plug-in V2.16.3 was used to analyze a mediation model for the data.ResultsAmong secondary vocational students, 839 individuals (70.86%) experienced cyberbullying and 1036 individuals (87.50%) had one or more health risk behaviors. A chi-square test showed that the differences in cyberbullying incidence were statistically significant (P < 0.05) for being children, type of accommodation, residence, ethnicity, purpose of using the internet, and family composition. Statistically significant differences in health risk behaviors were observed based on gender, type of accommodation, residence, ethnicity, academic achievement, classmate relationships, purpose of using the internet, and family composition (P < 0.05). Health risk behaviors and cyberbullying were correlated (r = 0.60), health risk behaviors and self-control and internet usage self-control were negatively correlated (r = -0.42, -0.50). Mediation analysis indicated that self-control (0.02, 0.08) and internet usage self-control (0.08, 0.17) partially mediated the association between cyberbullying and health risk behaviors.ConclusionsSecondary vocational students exhibit a high prevalence of cyberbullying and health risk behaviors. The study confirmed that self-control and internet usage self-control has an important role in mediating cybeibullying and health risk behaviors. Adolescent health promoters should develop targeted strategies to enhance the self-control of secondary vocational school students to mitigate their engagement in health risk behaviors.

Introduction

Health risk behaviors have become a global public health issue that affects the physical and mental health of adolescents worldwide [1, 2]. Adolescence, particularly between the ages of 12 and 17, is a critical period characterized by an increased risk of mortality and engagement in social problem behaviors, including alcohol consumption, smoking, suicidal tendencies, gambling, and substance abuse [3–5]. These behaviors can lead to severe health issues and have implications that can persist into adulthood [6]. The health risk behaviors of adolescents in both online and offline settings have become more prominent due to the close connection between social development and internet information services [7]. The rapid increase in media use has raised concerns about its potential effects on adolescents' health risk behaviors [8].

In China, secondary vocational schools are are the same age as high school students where students acquire professional knowledge and skills. Compared to their counterparts in high schools, secondary vocational school students exhibit poor academic performance and demonstrate a tendency toward low motivation, excessive self-consciousness, weak teamwork skills, poor self-management ability, and greater engagement in health risk behaviors [9–11]. However, the majority of campus surveys focus on teenagers in primary, junior high, and regular high schools as well as universities, with relatively few efforts made to collect data in secondary vocational schools.

In 2021, the number of underage internet users in China surged to 191 million, representing a penetration rate of 96.8%; secondary vocational students exhibited the highest rate of internet usage [12]. Adolescents enjoy the interaction, integration, and convenience of the internet, but they face the pressing issue of cyberbullying. In China, 34.8% of adolescents have reported being involved in cyberbullying, while 56.9% of adolescents in China have been victims of cyberbullying; the co-occurrence of bullying in both offline and online settings has exacerbated health problems among this population [13]. Prior research has demonstrated that cyberbullying is associated with harm to physical health, increased risk of depression and suicidal behavior [14]. Currently, there is no globally unified definition of cyberbullying, nor is there a standardized assessment tool [15]. Furthermore, the reported prevalence varies across different countries. Cyberbullying is characterized by the repeated use of digital technology with the intent to intimidate, provoke, or humiliate others [16]. Cyberbullying can result in negative consequences for adolescents, including learning difficulties [17], social problems [18], psychological distress [19], tendencies toward self-harm, and suicidal behaviors [20]. In contrast to Western countries, little is known about the effects of health risk behaviors and cyberbullying in China. Understanding cyberbullying and health risk behaviors in this demographic is essential for public health. Hypothesis 1 suggests a positive association between cyberbullying and health risk behaviors.

The Problem Behavior Theory (PBT), proposed by Jessor in 1977, posits that behavior results from the interaction between an individual and their environment. This theory emphasizes three major systems: the personality system, the perceived environment system, and the behavioral system [21]. The media, with its significant influence, shapes public perception and acts as a catalyst during crucial events [22]. The experience of cyberbullying was used as a contextual factor as new media is an important part of social culture and has a negative impact on secondary school production. In the personal control architecture, both self-control and network self-control have been validated in correcting problematic behaviors [23].

Self-control refers to an individual’s ability to alter his or her dominant cognitive, emotional, and behavioral reactions to follow social norms and pursue long-term goals [24]. Self-control is an inherent part of the development of physical and mental health in adolescents [25, 26]. Prior research has demonstrated that low self-control is positively associated with criminal offenses, aggressive or bullying behavior, substance abuse, early sexual debut and risky sex, as well as health risk behaviors such as drinking and smoking [27, 28]. Additionally, some studies have indicated that self-control acts as a mediating factor in the relationship between social behavior and outcomes. For example, self-control serves as a protective factor against bedtime procrastination, depression and anxiety [29]. Therefore, self-control has a significant impact on the occurrence of health risk behaviors. Hypothesis 2 proposes that self-control plays a mediating role between cyberbullying and health risk behaviors.

With the emergence of the 5G era, the research scope of self-control related to internet usage has expanded. Researchers have devised new tools to assess self-control among adolescents in online settings [30]. Researcher Ouyang defines internet usage self-control as the regulation of cognition, emotions, and behaviors by individuals during online activities to promote healthy and effective engagement with digital resources [30]. Empirical studies have provided evidence that self-control in internet usage is linked to reduced severity of problematic internet use (PIU) and may act as a moderator in social relationships with parents and peers [31]. Additionally, insufficient self-control related to internet usage is among the most significant risk factors for internet addiction, bullying, and aggressive behavior [32, 33]. Given this information and the long-term consequences of low self-control in adolescents, it is imperative to focus on enhancing their self-control. Hypothesis 3 proposes that internet usage self-control plays a mediating role between cyberbullying and health risk behaviors.

Based on the above mentioned existing literatures, cyberbullying, self-control, internet usage self-control and health risk behaviors are related. However, the mediating role of self-control and internet usage self-control between cyberbullying and health risk behaviors is unclear. The main objective of the current study is to investigate the prevalence of cyberbullying and health risk behaviors among a sample of 1184 secondary vocational students. Importantly, We aim to investigate the mediating role of self-control and internet usage self-control within the real-to-cyber context. Methods

Design and participants

This descriptive correlational study employed a cross-sectional survey method. These schools were randomly chosen from 8 districts of Jilin Province, China. We conducted a cluster sampling survey in Yanji and Panshi, with populations of 680,000 and 520,000, respectively. Yanji hosts two secondary vocational schools, while Panshi has only one. The study population consisted of all first through third grade students who participated in the self-report survey and met the inclusion criteria: The participants completed the printed questionnaires. Parents and students filled out a consent form. The research was approved by the ethical committees of Yanbian University, China. Each student was given a notebook at the end of the survey, including those who did not participate.

Measurements

The sociodemographic data of the participants provided information on gender, children status, type of accommodation, residence, grade, ethnicity, academic achievement, classmate relationships, purpose of using the Internet, and family composition.

The Cyberbullying and Cybervictimization Scale (CAV), which includes two forms, cyberbullying (CB) and cybervictimization (CV), was developed by Shapka in 2017 to assess the extent of cyberbullying and cybervictimization among adolescents [34]. Both forms consist of 12 items that describe different forms of CB or CV. Each item is scored on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from "never happens" (0) to "happens every day" (4). The total score of the scale ranges from 0 to 48, where higher scores reflect greater levels of cyberbullying and victimization. The CAV was rigorously developed and demonstrated good validity/reliability in a Chinese population [35]. Cronbach’s alpha was 0.90 in this study.

The Adolescent Health Related Risky Behavior Inventory (AHRBI) was developed and validated by Wang et al. in 2012 [36]. It consists of a 38-item scale that contains six dimensions: (a) aggression and violence (AV), (b) rule breaking (RB), (c) unprotected sex (US), (d) suicide and self-injury (SS), (e) smoking and drinking (SD), and (f) health-compromising behaviors (HCB). Because the internal consistency of health compromising behavior was 0.22, this dimension was deleted. Participants used a 5-point scale (0 = never, 4 = always) to rate the items. A higher score indicated a greater occurrence of health risk behaviors. Cronbach's alpha for this study was 0.91.

The Self-Control Scale (SCS) was used to evaluate the self-control ability of adolescents in China. The Chinese version of the SCS was culturally adjusted by Tan et al. [37] based on the short version of the self-control scale published by Tangney et al. in 2004. The participants provided ratings for each item using a 5-point scale ranging from 1 (never) to 5 (always). The item scores were summed, with higher scores indicating greater self-control. The reliability of the SCS scale was measured using Cronbach's alpha coefficient, which was found to be 0.87.

The Internet Usage Self-Control Scale (IUSCS-CS) is a 34-item (item scores 1–5) and 3-dimensional (cognitive, emotional, and behavioral self-control) self-report scale designed to measure internet usage with regard to self-control [30]. In the confirmatory factor analysis, the scale had sound fit indices. The higher the score, the more self-control the respondent demonstrated over the network. Cronbach’s alpha was 0.88 in this study.

Procedure

Data were collected by trained investigators in classroom settings from March to April 2023. Prior to the survey, to ensure data authenticity, students anonymously completed self-report questionnaires in the classrooms without teacher supervision. Participation was voluntary, with no negative consequences for students who chose to refuse or withdraw from the survey. During the survey, only students who did not refuse were permitted to participate. Due to the sensitive nature of the questionnaire items, to protect privacy, all respondents were asked to maintain a distance of one meter while answering. The survey took place during self-study periods, with students allocating 20 min to complete the questionnaires. Upon completion of the survey, the investigators collected the questionnaires.

Statistical analysis

This study utilized SPSS26.0 for statistical analysis. For continuous variables such as CAV, AHRBI, SCS, and IUSCS-CS, which were not normally distributed, the median (lower quartile, upper quartile) was used. The chi-square test was used to compare sociodemographic variables and cyberbullying or health risk behavior groups. Spearman correlation analysis was performed to evaluate the correlations between cyberbullying, health risk behaviors, self-control, and internet usage self-control. To verify the mediation model, the process plug-in V2.16.3 was utilized, and the 95% confidence interval (CI) for the mediation effect was estimated by extracting 5000 samples from the original samples. The mediation effect was considered significant if its CI was 95% and if the bias-corrected lower and upper limits for indirect effects did not contain zero [38]. In addition, a two-sided P value < 0.05 was regarded as statistically significant.

Mediation analysis of self-control and internet usage self-control

According to the correlation analysis results and research hypotheses, this study conducted an intermediary effect test. The relationship between self-control and internet usage self-control mediated cyberbullying and health risk behaviors was confirmed. Cyberbullying affected self-control (B = -0.545, P < 0.001), with an explained variation of 24.0%, and cyberbullying affected internet usage self-control (B = -1.111, P < 0.001), with an explained variation of 32.0%. Discussion

The findings of this study suggest that the prevalence of cyberbullying among secondary vocational students is suboptimal. The overall prevalence of cyberbullying was 70.86% in this study. Among the students, 9.29% reported that they had engaged in cyberbullying, 15.55% reported that they had experienced cybervictimization, and 46.02% reported that they had experienced cyberbullying-victimization. The prevalence of cybervictimization was significantly greater than that of cyberbullying, which is consistent with the findings of a study by Zhou [13]. Studies conducted in China have reported varying prevalence rates of cyberbullying among adolescents ranging from 8.9% to 70% depending on the definition and measurement criteria used [39]. It is important to consider that mentally immature teenagers may not fully recognize or comprehend their involvement in or victimization by cyberbullying. When assessing the presence of cyberbullying, investigators should consider various factors, such as cyber bystanders, to help identify the occurrence of cyberbullying [40, 41]. It is crucial to develop a comprehensive understanding of adolescent cyberbullying, promptly monitor the behavioral changes of secondary vocational school students, and provide effective support and assistance.

The analysis of differences between cyberbullying groups indicates that factors such as being children, type of accommodation, residence, ethnicity, internet use, and family composition may play a role. Girls are more likely to be cybervictims and perpetrators than boys are, possibly due to spending more time online and differences in personality and thinking style, which can affect their use of social media and interactions with strangers [42]. The prevalence of cyberbullying is more significant among people of ethnic groups other than the Han population. Additionally, the higher risk of experiencing cybervictimization among secondary vocational students residing in urban areas can be attributed to variations in internet penetration and information proliferation across different regions with distinct cultural backgrounds [43]. In addition to nuclear families, other family structures among secondary vocational school students are associated with higher rates of cyberbullying. The prevalence of cyberbullying is more significant among students whose parents have lower levels of education, suggesting that parental and familial factors play a role in the development of adolescent problem behaviors [44]. Previous studies have shown that a disharmonious family atmosphere can lead to an increased risk of experiencing online victimization [45]. Therefore, cyberbullying is not only an individual problem but is also closely intertwined with familial, educational, and other personal environments.

The study found that the prevalence of health risk behaviors was 80.75% in boys, which was relatively higher than the prevalence in girls and was consistent with the findings of Lars [46]. The incidence of violation of discipline and aggressive violent behaviors among secondary vocational students was high, which is related to immature physical and mental development and a lack of self-management ability [13]. Moreover, some adolescents hold the misconception that behaviors such as smoking and drinking are socially and peer acceptable [47]. Suicide and self-harm are the main causes of adolescent death due to psychological problems caused by cumulative health risk behaviors, but there is still no standardized method for accurately predicting the occurrence of suicidal behavior [48]. The proportion of secondary vocational students who reported unprotected sexual behavior was low. This is related to the early comprehensive development of sexual health education in China [49]. At the same time, we are concerned that a Brazilian study found that unsafe sex among high school students was linked to recent binge drinking and illicit drug use [50]. Schools and families need to be aware of the problem of alcohol abuse, as drinking among high school students is more common in China.

The analysis of differences among groups with respect to health risk behaviors indicates that factors such as gender, type of accommodation, residence, ethnicity, academic achievement, classmate relationships, internet use, and family composition may contribute to these differences. Gender plays a crucial role, boys are more likely to engage in excessive drinking and smoking, while girls tend to focus more on weight loss and diet control [51, 52]. However, regardless of gender, prioritizing health behaviors and taking proactive steps to address them is essential. To promote the healthy development and integration of secondary vocational students into society, it is crucial to develop targeted intervention strategies for different ethnicities and regions [53]. Additionally, actively advocating for healthy families and fostering a harmonious family atmosphere can contribute to instilling a positive understanding of healthy behavior in teenagers through their parents and families [54, 55]. Secondary vocational students are prone to increased entertainment consumption and decreased focus on learning and obtaining information. Consequently, it is important for families and schools to implement reasonable supervision and management of mobile phone usage among these students while also identifying and formulating early intervention strategies. These measures aim to effectively control the occurrence of health risk behaviors among secondary vocational students [56, 57].

The study’s findings revealed that cyberbullying is positively correlated with health risk behaviors. In other words, secondary vocational students with more cyberbullying experience were found to engage in more health risk behaviors. This finding supports Hypothesis 1 and is consistent with Sitthi's research [58]. Adolescents tend to use bullying as a way to resolve conflicts or to gain "respect" through bullying, but cyberbullying can lead to psychological problems and health risk behaviors such as self-harm and suicide [59, 60]. Adolescents exhibit varied responses to cyberbullying. The most prevalent effects of cyberbullying are emotional and psychological problems, which can also adversely affect academic performance and social interaction [61–63]. A meta-analysis revealed that being targeted by cyberbullying was linked to suicidal thoughts more strongly than being targeted by traditional bullying [64]. Therefore, policy-makers should pay attention to changes in adolescents' daily behavior and psychological responses, screen for cyberbullying and health risk behaviors as early as possible, and standardize internet use and health risk behavior education.

Our results support Hypothesis 2 and show that self-control plays an intermediary role between cyberbullying and health risk behaviors. Self-control has been consistently identified as a crucial mediating factor in predicting various problem behaviors, particularly aggression resulting from a lack of self-control [65]. In a study of Korean youth, low self-control was an important predictor of cyberbullying [66]. Low self-control was also associated with an increased likelihood of engaging in health risk behaviors, a finding congruent with Roberta's research [27]. The dual-system model can explain the occurrence of health risk behaviors among adolescents, which are attributed to an imbalance between socioemotional and cognitive control systems [67]. Adolescents are prone to taking risks and seeking stimulation. Some intervention projects have shown that enhancing self-control significantly improves adolescents’ behavioral problems, underscoring the importance of self-control as a key objective in behavioral interventions [68]. A high level of self-control, as a cognitive control mechanism, enables adolescents to exercise better inhibitory control, plan, and monitor their actions, thereby preventing involvement in negative risk-taking behaviors [69].

Cyberbullying resulting from internet addiction is increasingly prevalent, and individuals who exhibit low self-control in internet use are more susceptible to becoming both repeat victims and perpetrators [25]. Secondary vocational students have a high daily internet usage rate. Due to inadequate preparation for college entrance exams and low self-control, adolescents may spend excessive amounts of time on the internet, thereby exacerbating their risk of developing internet addiction [70]. The excessive use of the internet by adolescents has received widespread attention due to its negative effects on health [71, 72]. Previous research has shown a correlation between poor self-control in internet usage and engagement in health risk behaviors. Individuals who use the internet daily and skip breakfast due to insufficient sleep are more likely to demonstrate health risk behaviors such as smoking, drinking, and drug use [71, 73]. Therefore, it can be concluded that higher levels of self-control and better control over internet usage among secondary vocational students can mitigate the incidence of cyberbullying and health risk behaviors, which supports Hypothesis 3.

Implications

This study showed that both real and cyber self-control significantly mediated the relationship between cyberbullying and health risk behaviors among secondary vocational students, offering new insights into health behavior interventions for improving their well-being. We recommend that policy-makers prioritize the cultivation of strong self-control among adolescents. This entails acquiring skills in self-management and emotional regulation and maintaining composure when confronted with cyberbullying and health risk behaviors. In pursuit of this goal, active engagement in psychological training programs can enhance self-control abilities. Furthermore, employing the internet appropriately is crucial for preventing victimization from cyberbullying and avoiding potential harm. Most importantly, seeking assistance and support is paramount. If adolescents experience cyberbullying or engage in health risk behaviors, they should promptly seek aid from family, friends, or professional institutions to actively address the issue. This approach can effectively resolve this issue and prevent additional harm.

Limitations

There might be some possible limitations in this study. First, the cross-sectional design of this study precludes causal inferences between cyberbullying and health risk behaviors. Second, reliance on self-reported data may limit our ability to fully understand the extent of cyberbullying and health risk behaviors. Finally, the use of a small sample from only three schools may restrict the generalizability of the findings. In the future, it is necessary to further understand the differences between secondary vocational students and other groups, conduct longitudinal assessments, and promote the sustainable development of adolescent health.

Conclusions

This study investigated the current situation of cyberbullying and health risk behaviors. Additionally, this study discussed the role of self-control and internet use self-control in cyberbullying and health risk behaviors through an intermediary model. The results indicate a high prevalence of cyberbullying and health risk behaviors among secondary vocational students. Our findings further demonstrate that self-control and internet usage self-control act as mediators between cyberbullying and health risk behaviors. These findings suggest that policy-makers and prevention program designers should take into account the mediating effects of self-control and internet usage self-control on the relationship between cyberbullying and health risk behaviors. Additionally, the results provide new evidence and research directions for understanding the association between cyberbullying and health risk behaviors. Finally, adolescent health promoters can use this information to contribute to the development of public policies that promote the well-being and health of adolescents worldwide.
