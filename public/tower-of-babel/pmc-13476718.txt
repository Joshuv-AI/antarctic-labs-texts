Acquisition-inspired motion artifact modeling and degradation-consistent diffusion restoration in photoacoustic microscopy
Authors: Heqing Wang, Shuyan Zhang, Jingtan Li, Xinyi Lim, Naidi Sun, Bin Hu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Motion artifacts are a major source of degradation in photoacoustic microscopy (PAM), where sequential raster scanning converts target-probe motion into structured coordinate mismatch, causing line misregistration, vessel discontinuity, truncation, and local deformation. We propose an acquisition-inspired framework for PAM motion artifact modeling and degradation-guided diffusion restoration. Motion corruption is formulated as raster-scan coordinate mismatch and decomposed into inter-line misregistration and intra-line sampling distortion. Based on this formulation, we construct a controllable simulator that generates paired clean-corrupted PAM images with explicit degradation priors. A conditional diffusion restoration model then uses motion-related priors for guidance and enforces degradation consistency by applying the forward inter-line and intra-line degradation operators during training. To support real-data inference, a degradation prior estimator predicts row-wise displacement and artifact-region information from corrupted images. Experiments on synthetic mouse brain PAM data show superior restoration performance, while selected real examples provide preliminary qualitative observations on non-synthetic motion-corrupted acquisitions.

Introduction

Motion artifacts are a common source of image degradation in medical imaging. They can arise from patient movement, respiration, heartbeat, involuntary tissue motion, or relative displacement between the imaging system and the biological target. Depending on the acquisition mechanism, motion may appear as ghosting, blurring, streaking, spatial misregistration, or local structural distortion, and can compromise both visual interpretation and quantitative analysis. Motion artifact correction has therefore been widely investigated in established imaging modalities such as magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and computed tomography (CT) [1], [2]. However, the appearance and physical origin of motion artifacts are highly modality-dependent. Effective correction methods must therefore take into account how motion is encoded into the acquired image.

Photoacoustic microscopy (PAM) is a high-resolution biomedical imaging modality that combines optical absorption contrast with ultrasonic detection, making it particularly suitable for visualizing microvascular structures and hemodynamic information in vivo [3], [4], [5], [6]. In typical PAM systems, image formation is achieved through sequential raster scanning: the optical-acoustic focus is scanned rapidly along the fast axis and progressively advanced along the slow axis to form a two-dimensional image or volumetric dataset [7]. Although high-speed PAM systems based on improved light sources, scanning mechanisms, and compact scanning hardware have been actively developed to improve imaging throughput [8], [9], [10], [11], the trade-off among imaging speed, field of view, and spatial resolution remains a practical constraint. As a result, in vivo PAM acquisitions are still susceptible to motion during scanning, especially in small-animal vascular imaging where respiration, heartbeat, or body movement may occur during the acquisition of a single image.

Unlike conventional noise or blur, PAM motion artifacts are closely tied to sequential raster acquisition in point-scanning or fast-scanning PAM systems. Since different image rows and pixels are acquired at different time points during fast-axis and slow-axis scanning, relative motion between the sample and the imaging probe is projected into the reconstructed image as structured coordinate mismatch. Such artifacts often appear as line-to-line displacement, vessel discontinuity, local truncation, asymmetric protrusion, or spatially nonuniform deformation. Representative examples of real in vivo PAM motion artifacts across different organs and imaging scales are shown in Fig. 1. Across brain, kidney, ear, and hindlimb vessel imaging, these artifacts appear as line-to-line misregistration, vessel discontinuity, local truncation, protrusion-like distortion, and mixed structural deformation. In this work, we interpret these recurring patterns under an acquisition-inspired framework and decompose them into inter-line misregistration, intra-line sampling distortion, and mixed inter-line/intra-line patterns in which both effects coexist. Such distortions are particularly harmful in vascular PAM because thin vessels, bifurcations, and local vessel trajectories are important for morphological interpretation. Several studies have investigated motion correction or scan alignment in photoacoustic imaging. Schwarz et al. demonstrated motion correction in raster-scan PAM by compensating motion-induced distortions in vivo [12]. Zhao et al. proposed a dedicated motion correction framework for optical-resolution PAM and showed its importance for preserving vascular structures under in vivo conditions [13]. Chen et al. introduced an early CNN-based motion-correction algorithm for optical-resolution PAM, demonstrating that data-driven restoration can suppress motion artifacts beyond hand-crafted correction rules [14]. More recent studies have further explored learning-based alignment of bidirectional raster scanning [15], unsupervised real-time registration for fast-scanning optical-resolution PAM [16], and orthogonal cross-correlation for high-rate motion correction [17]. These works establish the importance of PAM motion correction and provide valuable physical or data-driven strategies. Nevertheless, many existing methods still focus on registration, alignment, or direct image-domain correction, while the explicit relationship between raster acquisition, motion-induced coordinate mismatch, and restoration learning remains insufficiently explored.

In parallel, deep learning has become increasingly important in medical image restoration and reconstruction. CNN-, GAN-, and Transformer-based networks have been widely explored for denoising, artifact reduction, super-resolution, and sparse-data reconstruction in medical imaging. In photoacoustic imaging and PAM, learning-based methods have also been investigated for sparse-view reconstruction, image enhancement, limited-view correction, and artifact reduction [18], [19]. These approaches demonstrate the ability of data-driven models to recover high-frequency structures from degraded observations. However, many existing restoration networks still treat degradation mainly as an image-domain mapping problem, and may not explicitly incorporate how motion is encoded by the sequential PAM acquisition process.

Diffusion models have recently become an important class of generative priors for image restoration and inverse problems. Foundational denoising diffusion and score-based models formulate image generation as an iterative denoising or reverse-time stochastic process, providing a flexible way to model complex image distributions [20], [21]. Beyond unconditional generation, diffusion priors have been incorporated into inverse-problem solvers through measurement consistency, posterior sampling, or manifold constraints, as represented by methods such as diffusion posterior sampling and manifold-constrained diffusion [22], [23]. More recent generative formulations, including image-to-image Schrödinger bridge, flow matching, and rectified flow, further extend iterative generative restoration by learning transport or bridge processes between degraded and target distributions [24], [25], [26].

In biomedical imaging, diffusion models have been increasingly explored for reconstruction and restoration tasks, including MRI and CT reconstruction, sparse-view or undersampled imaging, denoising, and image enhancement [27]. Compared with deterministic CNN- or Transformer-based restoration networks and adversarial GAN-based image translation, diffusion models provide an iterative generative restoration process, which is useful for ill-posed structural recovery. This property is particularly relevant to PAM vascular imaging, where motion artifacts may cause vessel discontinuity, local truncation, duplicated vessel-like responses, and spatial misregistration that cannot always be corrected by a single direct image-to-image mapping. In photoacoustic imaging and PAM, diffusion-based approaches have begun to appear for undersampled reconstruction and sparse-sampling recovery [28], [29], [30]. However, these studies mainly address incomplete sampling or reconstruction acceleration, rather than motion artifacts caused by sequential raster scanning. In contrast, raster-scan PAM motion artifacts arise from acquisition-time coordinate mismatch, where different rows and fast-axis samples are acquired at different time points. Although diffusion models can provide learned image priors for progressively recovering plausible vascular morphology, a generic diffusion prior alone does not explicitly encode how raster-scan motion produces row-wise displacement and intra-line sampling distortion. This motivates a restoration framework in which diffusion is not used as a generic image prior alone, but is conditioned on motion-related degradation priors derived from the PAM acquisition process.

Motivated by this limitation, we propose an acquisition-inspired framework for PAM motion artifact modeling and degradation-guided diffusion restoration. Instead of treating motion corruption as an arbitrary image-to-image degradation, we formulate it as coordinate mismatch induced by sequential raster scanning. For clarity, we decompose this acquisition-induced mismatch into two components, which we refer to as inter-line misregistration and intra-line sampling distortion. The inter-line component describes row-wise displacement across neighboring scan lines, while the intra-line component describes localized perturbation of the effective fast-axis sampling trajectory. This decomposition is not intended to fully reproduce the biomechanical complexity of in vivo motion; rather, it provides a controllable and physically interpretable way to generate structured PAM motion artifacts that resemble common raster-scan corruption patterns.

Based on this acquisition-inspired degradation model, we develop a degradation-guided diffusion restoration framework. The framework estimates row-wise displacement and local artifact-region priors from the corrupted PAM image and uses them as conditional cues for diffusion-based restoration. A degradation-consistency term is further introduced during training by re-applying the modeled forward artifact formation process to the restored output and comparing it with the observed corrupted image. We use this term as a model-based regularizer to encourage consistency with the modeled raster-scan corruption process, while avoiding an assumption of physically unique recovery. The main contributions of this study are summarized as follows:1.We propose an acquisition-inspired motion artifact modeling strategy for PAM, which formulates motion corruption as coordinate mismatch during sequential raster scanning and decomposes it into inter-line misregistration and intra-line sampling distortion.2.We develop a degradation-guided diffusion restoration framework that incorporates motion-related degradation priors and degradation-consistency supervision, supporting restoration that explicitly uses motion-related cues from the PAM acquisition process.3.We introduce a degradation prior estimation strategy that predicts row-wise displacement and local artifact-region priors directly from corrupted images, allowing the restoration model to use estimated degradation information during inference without simulator-provided annotations.

Method

Acquisition-inspired motion artifact simulation

Coordinate mismatch formulation

Motion artifacts in PAM are visually manifested as vascular discontinuity, local truncation, asymmetric protrusion, spatial misregistration, and other mixed structural distortions. Directly modeling these image-level vascular appearances is difficult, because the observed morphologies are diverse, partly overlapping, and not easily parameterized in a unified manner. More importantly, such appearance-driven modeling does not explicitly reflect how motion is encoded during raster-scan acquisition. Therefore, rather than describing motion corruption directly in terms of vessel shapes, we formulate PAM motion artifacts as acquisition-domain coordinate mismatch during sequential raster scanning.

In raster-scanning PAM, each scan line is acquired by rapidly scanning the optical-acoustic focus along the fast axis, while successive scan lines are obtained as the slow axis progressively advances the scan. As a result, different pixels are sampled at different time points [13], [31]. Let x(r,u) denote the clean PAM image, where x∈ℝH×W, r∈{1,…,H} is the slow-axis row index andu∈{1,…,W} is the nominal fast-axis coordinate. The corrupted image is denoted as y(r,u)∈ℝH×W. Under motion corruption, the effective sampling position deviates from the nominal trajectory, and the observed image can be written as:(1)y(r,u)=x(r,u˜(r,u))where u˜(r,u)is the effective coordinate actually sampled during acquisition.

To explicitly describe this acquisition-domain mismatch, we decompose the effective coordinate as:(2)u˜(r,u)=u+Δ(r)+δ(r,u)where Δ(r) denotes row-wise inter-line displacement and δ(r,u) denotes localized intra-line trajectory perturbation. Accordingly, PAM motion artifacts are decomposed into two acquisition-driven components: inter-line misregistration and intra-line sampling distortion. The former mainly affects spatial continuity across neighboring scan lines, whereas the latter perturbs the sampling trajectory within an individual scan line and therefore produces localized structural corruption. This decomposition provides a controllable acquisition-domain representation of raster-scan motion artifacts, rather than a direct appearance-level model of vascular breakage or protrusion.

Inter-line misregistration modeling

We model inter-line misregistration as blockwise displacement across consecutive rows. Specifically, for an affected row interval [r0,r1], a nearly constant lateral shift is imposed:(3)Δ(r)≈ba,r∈[r0,r1]where ba is the displacement amplitude. Here, Δ(r) represents the fast-axis displacement applied to the r-th scan line, and the complete row-wise displacement field is denoted as Δ={Δ(r)}r=1H. In addition, a short relaxation tail may follow the displaced band, with return, residual-offset, or overshoot behavior. To represent different temporal behaviors of relative motion during slow-axis scanning, the blockwise displacement can be followed by a short relaxation segment. As illustrated in Fig. 2, we consider four representative inter-line patterns. Fig. 2(a) shows transient displacement, where a short group of rows is shifted and then returns to the nominal position. Fig. 2(b) shows sustained displacement, where the shifted state persists across a longer row interval. Fig. 2(c) shows gradual recovery, where the displacement progressively returns to the nominal trajectory. Fig. 2(d) shows overshoot-like recovery, where the trajectory crosses the nominal position during relaxation.

This formulation covers several representative line-wise artifact patterns, including short-span local misregistration, long-span blockwise displacement, gradual return to the nominal position, and overshoot-like recovery behavior. From a practical perspective, such patterns may correspond to sustained relative displacement between the probe and the biological target during slow-axis progression. For example, a long displaced band may arise when the subject struggles or temporarily remains shifted at a new position for multiple scan lines, whereas shorter bands may reflect milder respiratory motion or brief body movement.

In the simulator, the corresponding inter-line operation is denoted as Finter, which applies the row-wise displacement field Δ to the clean image and produces an intermediate image with inter-line misregistration:(4)xinter=Finter(x;Δ).

This intermediate image is then used as the input to the intra-line sampling distortion stage described in the following subsection.

Intra-line sampling distortion modeling

We model intra-line sampling distortion through localized perturbation of the fast-axis trajectory within a single scan line. This component is introduced because raster-scanned PAM does not acquire an entire row instantaneously, and short-duration relative motion during fast-axis scanning may locally change the effective sampling trajectory within the affected row. For an affected row r, the effective in-row sampling path is written as:(5)pr(u)=u+δ(r,u)and the row is resampled according to pr(u). During resampling, out-of-bound coordinates are mirrored back into the image domain to avoid introducing artificial black padding at the image boundary. Here, δ(r,u) denotes the local fast-axis trajectory perturbation at row r and coordinate u, and the complete intra-line perturbation field is denoted as δ={δ(r,u)}r=1,u=1H,W.

The perturbation δ(r,u) is generated from a small set of local trajectory primitives, including offset-like perturbations, abrupt jumps, jumps with partial recovery, and mixed offset-and-jump events. These primitives provide controllable trajectory-level perturbations, while their image-domain appearances depend on the timing and direction of the effective sampling trajectory relative to local vascular structures.

In the simulator, the corresponding intra-line operation is denoted as Fintra, which applies the intra-line perturbation field δ to the intermediate image xinter obtained after inter-line misregistration:(6)y=Fintra(xinter;δ)

This operation implements the within-row fast-axis sampling distortion in the coordinate-mismatch formulation, and produces the final corrupted image used for paired synthetic training. Although these perturbation primitives are simple, they are sufficient to reproduce the dominant local structural artifacts repeatedly observed in real PAM images.

For offset-like perturbations, the artifact depends on when the forward shift occurs relative to the vessel region. If the scan has already entered the vessel before the trajectory shifts forward, only part of the vessel interval is skipped, leading to localized vessel discontinuity, as shown in Fig. 3(b). In contrast, if the trajectory shifts forward before reaching the vessel, the scan may miss the vessel region entirely; when the trajectory returns, the vessel location has already been passed, resulting in complete vessel breakage, as shown in Fig. 3(c). For jump-like perturbations, the effective trajectory may abruptly enter or leave the vessel region. Jumping onto the vessel region too early can create an additional response before the true vessel position, whereas jumping away after entering the vessel can interrupt the true vessel signal and produce duplicated sampling after the correct location. These timing- and direction-dependent effects lead to complete discontinuity, advanced or delayed duplicated responses, or mixed breakage and extra vascular responses, as shown in Figs. 3(d)–3(f). For jumps with partial recovery, the trajectory deviates briefly and then returns toward the nominal path, producing hollowed, weakened, or partially duplicated vessel appearances, as shown in Fig. 3(g) and Fig. 3(h). Finally, combined offset-and-jump perturbations accumulate multiple local sampling errors within the same scan line and therefore generate more complex artifact morphologies, as shown in Fig. 3(i) and Fig. 3(j).

Complete artifact synthesis and patch construction

Based on the above formulation, the complete synthetic artifact generation process is implemented in two stages. Starting from a clean patch x, we first apply an inter-line displacement operator Finter(x) to obtain an intermediate image with row-wise misregistration:(7)xinter=Finter(x;Δ)where Δ={Δ(r)}r=1H is the row-wise inter-line displacement field. We then apply an intra-line resampling operatorFintra(⋅) to generate the final corrupted image:(8)y=Fintra(xinter;δ)=Fintra(Finter(x;Δ);δ)where δ={δ(r,u)}r=1,u=1H,W is the intra-line trajectory perturbation field. This ordering reflects our interpretation that coarse line-wise displacement and fine within-line trajectory perturbation jointly shape the final motion artifact. Thus, Eq. (7) implements the row-wise component Δ(r) in the coordinate-mismatch formulation, whereas Eq. (8) implements the within-row fast-axis perturbation δ(r,u).

In addition to the paired clean and corrupted patches, the simulator also provides degradation descriptors used in the subsequent restoration framework, including the row-wise displacement field Δ, the intra-line perturbation field δ, and the artifact-region mask m. The mask m∈{0,1}H×W is derived from the motion-affected regions induced by the inter-line and intra-line perturbations, and is therefore a direct output of the artifact synthesis process rather than an independent manual annotation.

To illustrate the magnification-dependent effect of the same physical motion, Fig. 4 shows artifact synthesis under three spatial magnifications. Higher magnification increases the pixel-level displacement caused by a similar target-probe motion and can therefore make the resulting local structural distortion more apparent. In the simulator, this effect is approximated by scaling the displacement amplitudes with magnification, using factors of 1.0, 1.25, and 1.5 for 1.0 × , 1.5 × , and 2.0 × views, respectively.Fig. 4Magnification-dependent artifact synthesis. Importantly, the simulator does not attempt to reproduce the full biomechanical complexity of in vivo motion. Instead, it focuses on the dominant way in which relative motion is projected into PAM images through sequential raster acquisition. Degradation prior estimation

The acquisition-inspired simulator described above provides explicit simulator-generated degradation annotations for each corrupted image, including the row-wise inter-line displacement field Δ, the intra-line trajectory perturbation field δ, and the artifact-region mask m. During training on synthetic data, these annotations are directly available from the simulator. However, in real PAM acquisitions, the underlying motion process is unknown and no simulator-provided degradation annotations are available. Therefore, as shown in Fig. 5, we introduce a degradation prior estimator to predict motion-related degradation priors directly from the corrupted PAM image. The predicted priors are then used as conditional cues for the subsequent restoration model. Specifically, the estimator predicts a row-wise displacement prior Δˆ, an artifact-region mask mˆ, and an auxiliary dense intra-line perturbation map δˆmap. Among these outputs, only Δˆ and mˆ are used as restoration conditions during inference, whereas δˆmap is used only as an auxiliary supervision target during estimator training.Fig. 5Architecture of the degradation prior estimator. The estimator adopts a lightweight three-stage U-Net-like encoder-decoder architecture [32], shown as the light purple box in Fig. 5. Given an artifact-corrupted image y∈ℝH×W, the encoder progressively extracts multi-scale features through three convolutional stages with downsampling. A bottleneck block further aggregates low-resolution contextual information, and the decoder restores the spatial resolution through three upsampling stages. Skip connections are used between corresponding encoder and decoder stages to preserve fine spatial details. The final decoder feature is denoted as d1∈ℝH×W×C, where C is the number of feature channels. This feature serves as the shared representation for both spatial prior prediction and row-wise displacement estimation.

As shown in the light blue box in Fig. 5, two spatial prediction heads are built on the shared decoder feature. The first head predicts the artifact-region mask through a 1×1 convolution:(9)mˆ=fm(d1)where mˆ∈ℝH×W denotes the predicted artifact-region mask logits. After sigmoid activation, mˆ provides a soft localization map for motion-affected regions. The second head predicts a dense intra-line perturbation map, which describes local fast-axis sampling perturbations. Unlike the mask head, this branch uses a small convolutional head composed of two 3×3 convolutional layers with nonlinear activation, producing a single-channel perturbation map:(10)δˆmap=fδ(d1)where δˆmap∈ℝH×W represents the predicted dense intra-line perturbation map. The mask provides a compact localization cue for corrupted regions, while the dense perturbation map provides denser supervision for the spatial pattern of intra-line distortion. Because reliable intra-line perturbation labels are unavailable in real PAM acquisitions, δˆmap is not used as an explicit restoration condition during real-image inference.

In parallel, the row-wise prediction module, shown as the light yellow box in Fig. 5, is used to estimate the inter-line displacement and row activity. Since inter-line misregistration is defined at the row level, the two-dimensional decoder feature d1 is compressed along the image width. Specifically, mean pooling and max pooling are applied over the width dimension and then concatenated:(11)z=[MeanPoolW(d1),MaxPoolW(d1)]where z∈ℝH×2C is the row feature sequence. Each row feature summarizes the local image evidence and degradation cues of one scan line, while the channel dimension retains the semantic and structural information extracted by the encoder-decoder.

The row feature sequence is processed by four lightweight one-dimensional heads. The regression head directly predicts a continuous row-wise displacement estimate. The soft-bin classification head predicts a probability distribution over discrete displacement bins for each row. Let {ck}k=1K denote the displacement bin centers and pr,k denote the predicted probability of the k-th bin at row r. The classification-derived coarse displacement is computed by soft expectation:(12)Δˆcls(r)=∑k=1Kpr,kck

This classification branch provides a more stable coarse estimate of the row displacement, especially when the displacement magnitude falls into a limited range of plausible motion amplitudes. The regression branch provides complementary continuous prediction, and the residual refinement head predicts a small correction term for fine adjustment. The final inter-line displacement estimate is obtained by weighted fusion:(13)Δˆ(r)=0.75Δˆcls(r)+0.25Δˆreg(r)+Δˆres(r)where Δˆreg(r) is the regression output, Δˆres(r) is the residual correction. In our implementation, the classification-derived displacement and continuous regression output are fused with weights of 0.75 and 0.25, respectively, and the residual branch provides a final correction.

The fourth row-wise head predicts row activity, indicating whether each scan line is affected by inter-line motion. This branch is used as an auxiliary target to stabilize row-wise displacement estimation. The estimator is trained with a multi-task objective using the simulator-generated degradation descriptors, including the row-wise displacement field Δ, the artifact-region mask m, and the intra-line perturbation field δ. The objective jointly supervises row-wise displacement estimation, artifact-region localization, row activity, and auxiliary intra-line perturbation prediction, with detailed loss definitions and weights provided in Supplementary Section S2. During restoration inference, the estimator takes only the corrupted PAM image as input, and only the predicted row-wise displacement Δˆ and artifact-region mask mˆ are passed to the restoration model. The dense intra-line perturbation map and row-activity output are used only for estimator training and are not used as explicit restoration conditions. It should be noted that the predicted priors on real PAM images are estimated degradation cues rather than measured motion trajectories or anatomical ground truth. Therefore, when applied to real unpaired PAM acquisitions, the predicted displacement and mask are interpreted as prior-localization information for guiding restoration, not as quantitative proof that the underlying true vascular morphology has been recovered.

Degradation-consistent diffusion restoration

Based on the acquisition-inspired artifact model described above, we formulate PAM motion artifact correction as a degradation-aware image restoration problem. Given a corrupted PAM image y∈ℝH×W, the goal is to recover a structurally faithful clean image while incorporating degradation cues related to the raster-scan motion process. Rather than treating restoration as a generic image-to-image mapping, we explicitly incorporate motion-related degradation priors into the restoration network and further constrain the restored image through a degradation-consistency mechanism. In this work, degradation consistency is used as a model-based regularizer during training, rather than as evidence that the true physical motion trajectory or the unique ground-truth vascular morphology can be identified from an unpaired corrupted image.

During synthetic training, simulator-provided degradation descriptors are available, including the row-wise inter-line displacement field Δ, the intra-line perturbation field δ, and the artifact-region mask m. These descriptors are used for supervision, condition construction, local mask generation, and degradation-consistency regularization in the synthetic setting. During real-image inference, the simulator-provided descriptors are unavailable. Therefore, the restoration model uses only the predicted row-wise displacement Δˆ and predicted artifact-region mask mˆ estimated from the corrupted image by the degradation prior estimator. The auxiliary dense intra-line perturbation map predicted in 2.2 is used only for prior-estimator training and is not used as an explicit restoration condition during inference.

As illustrated in Fig. 6, the proposed framework consists of three main components: a shift-guided v-prediction diffusion backbone [33], a confidence-gated local refiner, and a degradation-consistency branch used only during training. The diffusion backbone constructs a shift-conditioned input from the corrupted image y and the row-wise displacement prior Δˆ, and produces an initial clean estimate xˆ0. The local refiner further improves artifact-prone regions using xˆ0, y, the normalized shift map, and a local distortion mask derived from the predicted artifact-region prior. The refiner is designed to focus on motion-sensitive regions while suppressing unnecessary modification outside the local mask. During synthetic training, the degradation-consistency branch re-applies the modeled forward degradation process to xˆ0 using Finter and Fintra, and constrains the re-degraded image yˆ to remain compatible with the observed corrupted image y. During synthetic training, this branch uses simulator-provided Δ, δ, and m to construct the re-degraded image, but it is not applied during real-image inference because reliable intra-line perturbation trajectories are not available for real unpaired PAM images. In real-image inference, the restoration model instead relies only on the predicted Δˆ and mˆ for conditioning and local refinement. Shift-guided v-prediction diffusion backbone

As shown in Fig. 6(a), the corrupted image and the row-wise inter-line displacement prior are first converted into a shift-conditioned input, which is then fed into the v-prediction diffusion backbone in Fig. 6(b). The first part of the proposed framework is a conditional diffusion restoration backbone that explicitly incorporates motion-related geometric priors. Conditional diffusion models have been widely used for image-to-image translation and restoration, where the degraded observation or auxiliary condition guides the reverse denoising process [23], [34], [35].

For a corrupted PAM image y∈ℝH×W, we construct a shift-conditioned input by combining the corrupted image with a normalized shift map derived from the row-wise inter-line displacement prior. During synthetic training, this prior can be provided by the simulator as Δ, whereas during real-image inference, it is replaced by the predicted prior Δˆ from the degradation prior estimator. Given Δˆ∈ℝH, the row-wise displacement is expanded along the fast-axis direction and normalized by a displacement scale Δscale to obtain a shift map s∈[−1,1]H×W. Equivalently, s(r,u) is obtained by assigning the normalized row displacement to all fast-axis coordinates in the same row. The diffusion condition is then defined as:(14)cond=[y,s]∈ℝH×W×2where s denotes the normalized shift map obtained by row-wise expansion of the inter-line displacement prior. This conditioning strategy allows the restoration network to access explicit degradation-related spatial cues, rather than relying only on implicit feature learning from the corrupted image itself. The artifact-region mask is not concatenated into this diffusion backbone, but is used later by the confidence-gated local refiner to localize artifact-prone regions.

During training, the clean image x0∈ℝH×W is diffused to an intermediate noisy state xt according to the forward diffusion process:(15)xt=αtx0+1−αtϵwhere t is the diffusion timestep, ϵ∼N(0,I) is Gaussian noise, and αt denotes the cumulative noise schedule. The noisy sample xt, together with timestep t and condition cond, is then fed into a conditional U-Net backbone. Instead of directly predicting the clean image or the additive noise, the network adopts a v-prediction parameterization. The backbone therefore predicts the latent velocity as:(16)vˆt=fθ(xt,t,cond)where fθ denotes the conditional diffusion network and vˆt is the predicted v-parameterized latent velocity. The corresponding v-prediction target is defined as:(17)vt=αtϵ−1−αtx0

The diffusion backbone is optimized by minimizing the mean-squared error between the predicted and target latent velocities:(18)ℒdiff=‖vˆt−vt‖22

After predicting vˆt, the initial clean estimate is recovered as:(19)xˆ0=αtxt−1−αtvˆt

The predicted xˆ0 serves as the initial clean estimate before local refinement. During training, xˆ0 is further passed to the confidence-gated local refiner and the degradation-consistency branch. During real-image inference, xˆ0 is obtained through reverse diffusion sampling under the fixed shift-conditioned input cond=[y,s], and is then passed to the local refiner to produce the final restored image.

The use of shift-guided conditioning is particularly important for PAM motion artifact correction. Unlike generic image degradation, PAM motion corruption is closely related to coordinate mismatch induced during sequential raster acquisition. By explicitly injecting the shift map into the diffusion backbone, the network is encouraged to restore vascular structures under degradation-aware geometric guidance. This design does not assume that the predicted shift prior uniquely determines the true clean morphology, but provides motion-related spatial information that helps the diffusion backbone focus on artifact-consistent restoration rather than unconstrained image rewriting.

Confidence-gated local refiner

Although the diffusion backbone can provide a globally plausible restored image, severe local distortions are often more difficult to correct completely. In PAM images, these hard regions typically correspond to localized discontinuity, truncation-like defects, protrusions, locally adhered vessels, or mixed distortions around vessels. To further improve recovery in such challenging regions while avoiding unnecessary modification of already well-restored or non-artifact regions, we introduce a confidence-gated local refiner, as shown in Fig. 6(c).

The refiner takes as input the initial clean estimate xˆ0, the corrupted PAM image y, the normalized shift map s, and a local distortion mask mloc. Here, xˆ0,y,s,mloc∈ℝH×W. During synthetic training, mloc is derived from the simulator-provided artifact-region mask m, whereas during real-image inference, it is derived from the predicted artifact-region mask mˆ. These inputs are concatenated as:(20)qref=[xˆ0,y,s,mloc]∈ℝH×W×4and processed by a lightweight convolutional module. The module predicts two outputs: a residual correction map rref∈ℝH×W and a confidence gate g∈[0,1]H×W. The residual correction represents the candidate local structural adjustment, whereas the confidence gate controls the spatial strength of this adjustment. The final refined output is written as:(21)xˆref=xˆ0+g⊙mloc⊙rrefwhere ⊙ denotes element-wise multiplication. In this formulation, mloc restricts the correction to distortion-prone local regions, while g adaptively controls how strongly the residual correction is applied within these regions.

By restricting the residual correction to artifact-prone regions and modulating it with a confidence gate, the refiner reduces unnecessary modification of intact vascular structures. This localized design complements the diffusion backbone by improving structural fidelity in motion-sensitive regions while preserving the global restoration quality of the initial diffusion output.

Degradation-consistency branch

As shown in Fig. 6(d), a degradation-consistency branch is introduced only during synthetic training. The key idea is that the restored image should not only look structurally plausible as a clean PAM image, but should also remain compatible with the forward artifact formation process described in 2.1. This branch is used as a model-based regularizer to constrain the restoration space, rather than as evidence that the restored image is physically unique or anatomically guaranteed.

Starting from the initial clean estimate xˆ0, we first apply the inter-line degradation operator using the simulator-provided row-wise displacement field Δ, and then apply the intra-line degradation operator using the simulator-provided intra-line perturbation field δ. The re-degraded artifact image is therefore written as:(22)yˆ=Fintra(Finter(xˆ0;Δ);δ)where Finter represents the inter-line misregistration process and Fintra represents the intra-line sampling distortion process. Here, yˆ∈ℝH×W denotes the re-degraded image, Δ={Δ(r)}r=1H is the simulator-provided row-wise displacement field, and δ={δ(r,u)}r=1,u=1H,W is the simulator-provided intra-line perturbation field. These descriptors are available only for synthetic training samples generated by the simulator. During real-image inference, this branch is disabled and no intra-line ground-truth perturbation field is required.

The re-degraded image yˆ is then constrained to match the observed corrupted image y through a weighted degradation-consistency loss:(23)Ldeg=∑r,uw(r,u)ρ(yˆ(r,u)−y(r,u))∑r,uw(r,u)+εwhere ρ(⋅) denotes the Smooth L1 penalty, and w∈ℝH×W is a spatial weighting map that places larger emphasis on artifact-prone regions. In our implementation, the weight map is derived from the simulator-provided artifact-region mask m as:(24)w(r,u)=wout+(1−wout)m(r,u)where wout=0.05 is a small background weight that keeps non-artifact regions weakly involved while assigning larger weights to artifact-prone regions.

The degradation-consistency branch differs from a conventional image-domain reconstruction loss because it does not directly enforce similarity between the restored image and the clean target alone. Instead, it adds a forward-compatibility constraint: when the modeled inter-line and intra-line degradation operators are re-applied to the restored image, the resulting image should remain consistent with the observed corrupted input. Therefore, this branch encourages compatibility with the acquisition-inspired degradation model while avoiding the stronger claim that degradation consistency alone can verify the true vascular morphology in real unpaired PAM images.

Edge-aware structural preservation and overall objective

In addition to degradation consistency and local refinement, preserving vascular boundary sharpness and fine morphology is also essential for PAM motion artifact restoration. Motion corruption often disrupts vessel continuity, weakens local edge contrast, and distorts thin branches. Therefore, relying only on image-domain restoration and degradation consistency may still lead to over-smoothed predictions in structurally sensitive regions. To further preserve vascular contours and local structural transitions, we introduce an edge-aware structural preservation loss.

To preserve boundary information, we compute horizontal and vertical Sobel gradients of the refined output xˆref and the clean target x0. The edge-aware loss is defined as:(25)ℒedge=‖∇hxˆref−∇hx0‖1+‖∇vxˆref−∇vx0‖1where ∇h and ∇v denote horizontal and vertical Sobel gradient operators, respectively. This term encourages the restored image to preserve vessel boundaries and fine structural transitions that are critical for vascular interpretation.

For the local refinement module, we further impose refinement-related supervision on the distortion-prone region. Given the refined output xˆref, the local refinement loss is computed within the local distortion mask mloc:(26)Lref=∑r,umloc(r,u)ρ(xˆref(r,u)−x0(r,u))∑r,umloc(r,u)+εwhere mloc∈[0,1]H×W is the local distortion mask, ρ(⋅) denotes the Smooth L1 penalty, and ε is a small constant for numerical stability. This masked supervision encourages the refiner to improve artifact-sensitive regions while suppressing unnecessary modification outside the mask.

In addition, the gate prediction is regularized to control the spatial strength of local refinement. The target gate is derived from the local error between the initial diffusion estimate and the clean target:(27)g*(r,u)=clip(|xˆ0(r,u)−x0(r,u)|τg,0,1)where τg=0.15 is a scaling threshold for converting local prediction error into gate supervision. The gate loss is then computed within the local distortion mask:(28)Lgate=∑r,umloc(r,u)ρ(g(r,u)−g*(r,u))∑r,umloc(r,u)+εwhere g∈[0,1]H×W is the predicted confidence gate. This loss encourages stronger refinement where the initial diffusion estimate has larger local errors and weaker refinement in already well-restored regions.

The full training objective is formulated as:(29)ℒtotal=λdiffℒdiff+λdegℒdeg+λedgeℒedge+λrefℒref+λgateℒgate

Here, ℒdiff is the v-prediction diffusion loss defined in Eq. (18), and ℒdeg is the weighted degradation-consistency loss defined in Eq. (23). The diffusion loss has a weight of λdiff=1.0, and the remaining weights are fixed across all experiments as λdeg=0.10, λedge=0.10, λref=0.20, and λgate=0.05. Together, these objectives enable the proposed framework to achieve globally plausible restoration, compatibility with the modeled degradation process, vascular edge preservation, and localized confidence-gated refinement for PAM motion artifact correction.

In summary, the proposed framework combines acquisition-inspired artifact modeling with degradation-consistent diffusion restoration for PAM motion artifact correction. The shift-guided diffusion backbone provides globally plausible restoration, the confidence-gated local refiner improves artifact-sensitive local regions, and the degradation-consistency branch encourages compatibility with the forward artifact formation process as a training-time regularizer. In the next section, we evaluate the proposed method on simulated and real PAM datasets and compare it with representative restoration baselines.

Experiments

Experimental setup

Data source, dataset split, and patch construction

The data used in this study were derived from a previously acquired in vivo optical-resolution photoacoustic microscopy (OR-PAM) mouse brain dataset. The source dataset contained full-brain vascular images acquired from 11 male CD−1 mice under a consistent PAM imaging setting, with an effective scanning area of approximately 5.5 mm × 4.2 mm. The original animal experiments and imaging protocol have been described in a previous study [36]. No new animal experiments were performed in the present work. Instead, this study conducted secondary analysis and task-specific preprocessing of the previously acquired structural PAM images.

To avoid subject-level data leakage, the dataset was split according to mouse identity before patch extraction, geometric augmentation, and motion artifact simulation. Images from 9 mice were used for training, and images from the remaining 2 mice were used for validation. All patches, augmented variants, and simulated motion-corrupted samples derived from the same mouse were kept within the same subset. This subject-wise split prevents highly similar vascular structures from the same animal from appearing in both training and validation sets.

After image acquisition, effective vascular regions were cropped and non-informative skull regions were removed. The full-sampled brain vascular images were used as clean source images for motion artifact simulation and restoration experiments. Unlike the previous sparse-reconstruction study, which used 256 × 256 image patches, the present work extracted 128 × 128 patches to better match the local spatial extent of motion artifacts and the memory requirements of the diffusion-based restoration framework. Overlapping patches were extracted using a sliding-window strategy with a maximum overlap of 50% between adjacent patches. Each clean patch was further augmented by rotations of 0°, 90°, 180°, and 270° before synthetic motion corruption was applied. This procedure produced 11,484 clean training patches and 2552 clean validation patches, which were then used to generate the same number of paired motion-corrupted samples.

Normalization and motion artifact simulation

For each clean patch, synthetic motion corruption was generated using the acquisition-inspired artifact simulator described in 2.1. The simulator produced a paired clean-corrupted sample and the corresponding degradation annotations, including the inter-line displacement field, intra-line trajectory perturbation field, intra-line displacement map, and artifact-region mask. These annotations were used as supervisory or conditioning signals for shift-guided conditioning, local refinement, region-weighted degradation-consistency training, and artifact-region evaluation.

Patch intensities were clipped at the 99.9th percentile to reduce the influence of isolated high-intensity pixels and then normalized using statistics estimated from the training subset only. During training, each clean-corrupted pair was mapped to the range [−1, 1]. For quantitative evaluation, the restored outputs were converted back to the raw intensity domain before computing image-fidelity and region-wise metrics. Since diffusion-based restoration was sensitive to the training intensity range in our preliminary experiments, random gain, gamma, and additive bias perturbations were applied consistently to each clean-corrupted training pair to improve robustness to brightness variation while preserving the paired restoration target and vascular topology.

Patch-wise inference and full-image reassembly

For full-field PAM images larger than the network input size, restoration was performed in a patch-wise manner. Each image was first divided into overlapping patches with the same spatial size as that used during training. The proposed restoration model was then applied independently to each patch, using the predicted degradation prior estimated from the corresponding corrupted patch. To reduce boundary discontinuities between adjacent restored patches, we used an overlap-and-blending strategy during full-image reassembly. Specifically, each restored patch was multiplied by a smooth spatial weighting window that assigns higher weights to the patch center and lower weights near the patch boundaries. The final restored full-field image was obtained by weighted averaging all overlapping patch predictions:(30)Iˆ(x,y)=∑kwk(x,y)Iˆk(x,y)∑kwk(x,y)+ϵwhere Iˆk denotes the restored prediction from the k-th patch, wkis the corresponding spatial blending weight, and ϵ is a small constant to avoid division by zero. After reassembly, the expected patch-boundary regions and overlapping areas were inspected to check whether patch-wise restoration introduced visible grid-like artifacts or boundary discontinuities. This overlap-and-blending strategy allows large PAM images to be processed while mitigating visible seams at patch boundaries.

Compared methods and training details

For comparison, we evaluated the proposed method against several representative image restoration baselines, including a plain CNN baseline, U-Net [32], Pix2Pix [37], Restormer [38], Diffusion Posterior Sampling (DPS) [23], and Rectified Flow [26]. The CNN baseline was included not only as a lightweight convolutional restoration model, but also because, to the best of our knowledge, it is the closest previously published deep-learning method specifically related to PAM motion correction under an image-restoration setting. U-Net represented a standard encoder-decoder architecture for biomedical image restoration, Pix2Pix/cGAN served as a representative adversarial image-to-image translation framework, and Restormer was included as a strong modern deterministic restoration baseline based on transformer-style feature modeling. DPS was included as a representative diffusion-based inverse-problem solver with posterior sampling, while Rectified Flow represented a recent flow-based generative framework for learning efficient transport between corrupted and clean image distributions. All compared methods were trained using the same dataset split and optimized under a unified experimental protocol for fair comparison.

The proposed framework was implemented in Python 3.8.10 using PyTorch 2.0.0. Unless otherwise stated, training was performed using the Adam optimizer with a default batch size of 16. For memory-intensive baselines, the batch size was slightly reduced when necessary. All experiments were conducted under Ubuntu 20.04 on a workstation equipped with an Intel Xeon(R) Platinum 8474 C CPU and an NVIDIA RTX 4090D GPU. For the proposed diffusion-based restoration model, the backbone adopted the v-prediction parameterization and used 30 sampling timesteps during inference. All models were trained for 120 epochs, and validation was performed every five epochs. The best checkpoint for each method was selected according to the validation PSNR in the raw intensity domain.

In the experiments, the evaluation protocol was organized in three stages. First, the role of degradation prior estimation was validated using the base restoration model without predicted-prior fine-tuning, by comparing zero-prior, predicted-prior, and simulator-provided oracle-prior guidance. Second, after confirming the effectiveness of predicted priors, the restoration model was further fine-tuned for 20 additional epochs under the predicted-prior setting, and this fine-tuned model was used as the final proposed method in the comparison with baseline methods. Third, ablation experiments were conducted under the same final evaluation setting to assess the contribution of each component in the proposed framework.

Evaluation metrics

To evaluate restoration performance, we adopted two widely used image-quality metrics, namely peak signal-to-noise ratio (PSNR) and structural similarity index measure (SSIM) [39]. PSNR measures pixel-wise reconstruction fidelity, while SSIM evaluates perceptual structural similarity between the restored image and the clean target image x. Specifically, PSNR is defined as:(31)PSNR=20log10ImaxMSE,MSE=1N∑i=1N(xˆi−xi)2where N denotes the total number of image pixels. SSIM is computed as(32)SSIM(x,xˆ)=(2μxμxˆ+c1)(2σxxˆ+c2)(μx2+μxˆ2+c1)(σx2+σxˆ2+c2)where μx and μxˆ are local means, σx and σxˆ are local variances, and σxxˆ denotes the local covariance between the restored and target images.

In addition to full-image evaluation, we further assessed restoration quality within artifact regions, because PAM motion artifacts are often highly localized and may severely disrupt thin vessels even when the global image quality remains relatively stable. Let Ω denote the artifact-region mask produced by the simulator. The region-aware PSNR is defined as:(33)PSNRΩ=20log10Imax∑iΩi(xˆi−xi)2∑iΩiwhere Imax denotes the maximum intensity value in the evaluation intensity range. This metric emphasizes reconstruction accuracy inside artifact-sensitive local regions. Correspondingly, region-aware SSIM was computed on the local image area enclosed by the artifact mask, so that structural recovery in distortion-prone regions could be assessed more directly.

To evaluate whether restoration was achieved without undesired modification of unaffected structures, we also computed PSNR and SSIM in the non-artifact region, defined as the complement of Ω. Therefore, the quantitative evaluation included full-image, artifact-region, and non-artifact-region PSNR/SSIM.

Because PAM images contain vessel-like structures whose continuity and topology are important for downstream interpretation, we further included topology- and hallucination-aware metrics. After applying the same vessel-mask extraction procedure to the restored and reference images, Dice coefficient [40] and centerline Dice (clDice) [41] were used to measure vessel-region overlap and skeleton-level agreement, respectively. We also reported skeleton precision/recall, connected-component error, false insertion rate, and false deletion rate to assess vascular topology preservation and possible hallucination-like vessel insertion or deletion. These metrics were computed for the full image and separately within artifact and non-artifact regions when applicable. Detailed mathematical definitions are provided in Supplementary Section S3.

To qualitatively examine the behavior of the synthetic-trained framework on non-synthetic acquisitions, we applied the trained model to real in vivo PAM images from mouse brain and kidney vascular imaging. Unlike the synthetic validation set, real acquisitions do not provide paired artifact-free ground-truth images, nor do they include simulator-generated degradation priors. Therefore, full-reference metrics such as PSNR and SSIM are not reported in this section. Instead, we present visual results as qualitative illustrations of how the model behaves on real motion-corrupted images.

Because paired artifact-free references were not available for real in vivo PAM acquisitions, the real-image experiment was conducted as a retrospective qualitative assessment. From available previously acquired PAM images, we selected representative mouse brain and kidney examples that showed visually identifiable motion-related corruption, including row-wise misalignment, local vessel discontinuity, and vessel adhesion-like deformation. Discussion

Motion artifacts in PAM differ fundamentally from conventional degradations such as additive noise or blur. Additive noise mainly appears as local intensity perturbations, whereas blur primarily reduces edge sharpness and high-frequency detail. In contrast, the motion artifacts considered in this work introduce directional, spatially localized, and structurally disruptive geometric inconsistencies, including row-wise displacement, vessel discontinuity, local bending, truncation, and mixed deformation of vessel-like structures. Therefore, the main challenge is not merely to improve local contrast or recover texture, but to restore motion-induced spatial misalignment while preserving vascular continuity and geometric consistency. For mouse brain vascular imaging, these properties are particularly important because even small local disruptions may affect the interpretation of thin vessels, branch connectivity, and regional vascular morphology. This task characteristic motivates us to formulate PAM motion artifact correction beyond standard denoising or deblurring.

The difference in problem setting also affects the choice of comparison methods. Several prior PAM-specific motion-correction studies were not included in the direct quantitative comparison because their input assumptions and correction targets differ substantially from the present work. For example, Zhao et al. [13] focused on motion tracking and correction between adjacent B-scan images within 3D OR-PAM acquisitions, rather than single-image post-restoration from already reconstructed motion-corrupted PAM images. Kim et al. [15] addressed bidirectional raster-scan alignment caused by scanner-path mismatch, while Hong et al. [16] developed an unsupervised registration framework for correcting B-scan distortion and misalignment among adjacent or repetitive fast-scanning images. Zou et al. [17] further performed motion correction at the photoacoustic signal level using orthogonal cross-correlation, which differs from our image-domain restoration setting. Because these methods require different data organization, acquisition assumptions, adjacent or repeated scans, or signal-level inputs, they are not directly comparable to the present single-image restoration framework under a strictly matched pixel-wise evaluation protocol. Therefore, the direct comparison in this study was designed around methods that can operate in the same single-image restoration setting, including one PAM-related CNN baseline and representative generic image restoration baselines trained and evaluated under a unified protocol. Accordingly, the proposed framework should be viewed as complementary to acquisition-level or registration-based motion correction methods, rather than as a replacement for methods that use repeated scans, B-scan sequences, or signal-level measurements.

The design of the degradation simulator is also important to the proposed framework. Our preliminary experiments suggested that modeling motion corruption using only simple whole-line displacement was not sufficient to capture the range of artifact patterns encountered in PAM restoration. Although row-wise shift can describe an important class of motion-induced misregistration, biological motion during sequential raster scanning may also produce nonuniform displacement within the same scan line. As a result, the observed artifacts are not always well approximated by rigid line translation alone, and may instead appear as localized truncation, vessel discontinuity, asymmetric protrusion, or mixed structural deformation. For this reason, we modeled the degradation process using both inter-line misregistration and intra-line sampling distortion, which provides a more flexible and acquisition-consistent description of PAM motion artifacts.

At the same time, the proposed simulator is designed as an acquisition-inspired approximation rather than a complete physical model of in vivo motion. Its purpose is not to reproduce the full biomechanical or physiological complexity of living tissue motion, but to capture several dominant ways in which relative target-probe motion is projected into PAM images through sequential raster scanning, including inter-line misregistration, localized intra-line distortion, and their common combinations. Importantly, the simulator defines a shared synthetic training and evaluation environment for all compared methods rather than a method-specific advantage. All baseline models and the proposed method were trained and evaluated using the same synthetic data generation protocol. Therefore, the superior performance of the proposed framework over CNN, U-Net, Pix2Pix, Restormer, DPS, Rectified Flow and the basic diffusion baseline cannot be attributed simply to the use of a synthetic degradation simulator. Instead, it reflects the benefit of explicitly incorporating degradation priors and degradation-consistent restoration under matched synthetic conditions. The degradation-consistency term should be interpreted as a model-based regularizer that encourages compatibility with the modeled raster-scan corruption process, rather than as proof that the recovered vascular morphology is physically unique. Nevertheless, because the proposed framework makes more explicit use of simulator-related degradation cues, its transferability to real acquisitions may still depend on how well the simulated degradations capture the major characteristics of real PAM motion artifacts.

The evaluation protocol should also reflect the localized nature of the artifact. Since motion corruption often affects only part of the image or a subset of rows, whole-image PSNR and SSIM can be dominated by unaffected regions and may therefore underestimate differences in restoration quality within corrupted areas. For this reason, we report both whole-image metrics and artifact-region metrics. Whole-image metrics evaluate global fidelity, whereas artifact-region metrics more directly assess restoration performance in motion-affected regions. In addition, topology-aware metrics were included to examine whether the restored outputs preserve vessel-like continuity and avoid excessive false insertion or deletion of vascular structures. This evaluation strategy helps distinguish whether a model mainly preserves already intact regions or actually corrects motion-corrupted structures. In our experiments, the proposed method shows a larger advantage in artifact-region and topology-related metrics than in global metrics, which is consistent with the task-specific goal of restoring localized structural disruption rather than only improving overall visual quality.

The comparison with Rectified Flow also provides useful insight into the task specificity of PAM motion-artifact restoration. In this study, Rectified Flow was included as a generic corrupted-to-clean transport baseline. However, it does not explicitly invert the acquisition-specific motion process of raster-scanned PAM, nor does it use the row-wise displacement and artifact-region priors that are central to the proposed framework. In addition, the artifacts considered here are local, sparse, and structurally misaligned, rather than a smooth global distribution shift between corrupted and clean images. Under this setting, a generic transport formulation may tend to learn an averaged or under-corrected mapping, especially in regions where the corruption occupies only a small part of the image but causes substantial local geometric disruption. This may explain why Rectified Flow does not perform as well as the proposed degradation-prior-guided diffusion framework in the present task.

The controlled-prior experiments further indicate that simply adding degradation-prior channels to an existing restoration model does not necessarily guarantee improved performance. For example, Restormer is already a strong deterministic restoration model and can exploit image context through global feature modeling. When additional prior channels are directly concatenated to its input, imperfect prior localization or noisy mask prediction may slightly disturb its learned global representation, leading to a small performance decrease in some settings. This should not be interpreted as evidence that degradation priors are unhelpful. Rather, it suggests that the benefit of degradation priors depends on whether the restoration architecture is designed to use them effectively. The proposed diffusion framework is built around shift-guided conditioning, confidence-gated local refinement, and degradation-consistency regularization, and is therefore better aligned with the structure of the estimated priors than a generic deterministic baseline with directly appended prior channels.

For real images, paired artifact-free references are unavailable, and the real-image results in this study should therefore be interpreted as qualitative and preliminary observations rather than definitive real-world validation. The supplementary comparisons show that other models can also alleviate visible real artifacts to some extent, which suggests that the simulated degradations may capture part of the dominant real artifact patterns. Within this limited qualitative setting, the proposed framework tends to produce visually more continuous and less distorted vessel-like outputs in selected representative examples. These observations provide preliminary support for the feasibility of applying the proposed strategy to selected real motion-corrupted PAM images, but they should not be interpreted as conclusive proof of real-data restoration accuracy, anatomical correctness, or cross-organ generalization. More rigorous real-data validation will require dedicated benchmarks, such as repeated acquisitions, relatively stable reference scans, controlled-motion experiments, or registration-supported surrogate references.

The proposed method still has several limitations. First, real mouse brain and kidney PAM images do not provide paired artifact-free ground-truth images. As a result, experiments on real acquisitions can only be evaluated qualitatively, rather than using full-reference metrics as in the synthetic setting. Future work may construct more reliable real-world benchmarks through repeated acquisitions, stable sample imaging, controlled-motion imaging, or multi-frame registration. Second, the restoration performance still depends on the accuracy of degradation prior estimation. Although the predicted priors substantially improve restoration quality, a clear gap remains between predicted priors and simulator-provided ground-truth priors. Inaccurate shift estimation or incomplete artifact-region detection may provide misleading conditions to the restoration model. Future research could investigate uncertainty-aware prior estimation [42], joint optimization of the prior estimator and restoration model, and more robust training strategies tailored to the error characteristics of predicted priors.

Third, the current simulator is designed to represent motion artifacts that are still reasonably recoverable, rather than arbitrarily severe corruption. Some real PAM acquisitions may exhibit much more complex deformation, such as extended vessel twisting, large-scale structural distortion, or severe non-motion-like corruption. In these cases, reacquisition may be preferable to restoration. We did not attempt to explicitly simulate all such severe patterns, because overly aggressive deformation modeling may bias the restoration network toward incorrect geometric regularization. For example, the model may become more likely to undesirably straighten naturally curved vessels or alter normal vascular morphology. Accordingly, the present framework is better interpreted as a method for correcting moderate motion artifacts than as a universal solution for all severely corrupted PAM acquisitions. In practice, the predicted degradation severity score can be used as a conservative warning indicator for potentially non-restorable cases. Because paired real references are unavailable, we do not define it as a calibrated hard rejection threshold. Nevertheless, cases with high predicted severity should be interpreted with caution, and reacquisition may be preferred when severe corruption is indicated.

Finally, the inference efficiency of diffusion models remains an important issue for practical deployment, especially because deployable biomedical AI systems often require lightweight and efficient model designs [43]. Although the proposed method improves restoration quality, its fixed-step diffusion sampling process is computationally heavier than deterministic CNN- or Transformer-based baselines. We explored an adaptive row-wise sampling strategy, in which different image rows were assigned different numbers of diffusion sampling steps according to the estimated artifact severity, with the goal of reducing overall inference time. However, this strategy did not consistently preserve restoration quality in preliminary experiments. One possible reason is that inconsistent sampling schedules across rows may disrupt the global coherence of the diffusion process and introduce new structural discontinuities. Therefore, the final method uses a fixed sampling schedule during inference. Future work may further investigate more efficient restoration frameworks, such as diffusion distillation, few-step sampling [44], lightweight conditional networks, or more stable region-adaptive sampling strategies that preserve global consistency.

Conclusion

In this work, we proposed a degradation-prior-guided conditional diffusion framework for PAM motion artifact restoration. By modeling motion corruption as structured acquisition-induced degradation, we introduced explicit row-wise displacement and artifact-region priors and incorporated them into a conditional diffusion restoration model. A degradation prior estimator was further developed to predict these priors directly from corrupted images, enabling inference without simulator-provided degradation labels.

Experiments on synthetic mouse brain PAM data demonstrate that the proposed method outperforms representative baselines in whole-image, artifact-region, and topology-aware metrics, while ablation studies support the contribution of prior-guided restoration. Qualitative real mouse brain and kidney examples further illustrate the behavior of the synthetic-trained model on selected non-synthetic motion-corrupted acquisitions, but should not be interpreted as definitive evidence of real-data restoration accuracy or cross-organ generalization. Overall, this study suggests that embedding acquisition-aware degradation priors into restoration models may provide a useful and interpretable route for correcting structured PAM motion artifacts.
