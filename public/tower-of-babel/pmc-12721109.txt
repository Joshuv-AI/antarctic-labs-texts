Shunt-related internal carotid artery dissection during carotid endarterectomy: illustrative cases
Authors: Ikuo Yamamoto, Tadashi Higuchi, Koshiro Isayama, Fumihiro Matano, Yohei Nounaka, Minoru Ideguchi, Ryuta Nakae, Akio Morita, Yasuo Murai
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBACKGROUNDIntraoperative shunt use during carotid endarterectomy (CEA), although critical for cerebral protection, may cause internal carotid artery (ICA) dissection in approximately 0.5% of cases. Shunt-induced dissections represent a distinct pathophysiological mechanism, differing from surgical technique–related dissection, and typically occur distal to the arteriotomy site due to trauma from shunt tips or balloon inflation.OBSERVATIONSThe authors present 2 cases of ICA dissection occurring during CEA with shunt use, both successfully managed with emergency endovascular stent insertion. In both case, complete resolution was achieved, with modified Rankin Scale scores of 0. Case 1 involved a 70-year-old male with 95% right carotid stenosis who developed dissection extending 6 cm distal to the arteriotomy. Dual-stent carotid artery stent placement resulted in complete resolution. Case 2 involved an 80-year-old female who developed extensive 55-mm left internal carotid dissection, immediately treated with overlapping dual-stent placement. A literature review of 13 cases demonstrated favorable outcomes with recent endovascular management approaches.LESSONSShunt-related dissection is a preventable complication requiring specific technical precautions, including ensuring symmetric balloon expansion, using a gentle insertion technique, and minimizing longitudinal movement. Carotid endarterectomy (CEA) has been established as an effective treatment for carotid artery stenosis through numerous large-scale clinical studies and is now widely performed as a standard surgical technique. Perioperative complications occur in 1.5%–9.0% of cases,1,2 with vascular dissection distal to the CEA site reported in approximately 0.5% of procedures.1,2

Postoperative vascular dissection following CEA is typically attributed to distal intimal disruption during the procedure. However, particularly for dissections beginning at sites distant from the endarterectomy endpoint, shunt insertion used to preserve cerebral blood flow during surgery has been implicated as a potential causative factor.3–14 Specifically, balloon expansion for hemorrhage control during shunt placement has been reported to cause vascular injury, including arterial rupture and dissection.

This report presents 2 cases of internal carotid artery (ICA) dissection attributed to intraoperative shunt use during CEA, successfully treated with urgent endovascular stent placement. We also present a literature review of shunt-related vascular dissection following CEA and discuss preventive strategies and optimal treatment approaches.

Illustrative Cases

Case 1

A male in his 70s with a medical history of hypertension, dyslipidemia, and hyperuricemia was referred for evaluation of suspected severe right ICA stenosis discovered during routine health screening neck ultrasonography. Physical examination revealed a right cervical bruit. Cerebral angiography confirmed 95% stenosis of the right common carotid artery (CCA) by the North American Symptomatic Carotid Endarterectomy Trial (NASCET) criteria (Fig. 1A). CT demonstrated atherosclerotic calcification, necessitating right CEA using an internal shunt (Pruitt-Inahara carotid shunt, LeMaitre Vascular). During surgery, after removal of heavily calcified intima and shunt withdrawal, indocyanine green (ICG) video angiography revealed a mild delay in blood flow in the ICA, but the surgeon did not notice this (Video 1). The patient had been on clopidogrel therapy prior to CEA.

B: Postoperative lateral right CCA angiogram revealing dissection in the distal ICA (arrow), located 60 mm distal to the arteriotomy site. Postoperative MRI on day 1 showed no ischemic lesions on diffusion-weighted imaging; however, head and neck MR angiography (MRA) demonstrated dissection in the right ICA. 123I-iodoamphetamine single-photon emission CT demonstrated no lateralizing differences in cerebral blood flow. Cerebral angiography confirmed cervical carotid dissection, and right carotid artery stent (CAS) placement was performed the same day for embolic complication prevention, given the extensive dissection.

Under general anesthesia with systemic heparinization maintaining an activated clotting time (ACT) > 250 seconds, digital subtraction angiography (DSA) revealed dissection extending distally to the petrous portion, with the entry point located 60 mm distal to the CEA arteriotomy site (Fig. 1B). After confirming access to the true lumen, we deployed a wall stent (6 mm × 22 mm) under proximal flow arrest. Although this case occurred after CEA surgery, since more than 24 hours had elapsed at the time of stent placement, aspirin was administered after the first stent treatment. Follow-up cerebral angiography 1 week later showed persistent dissection at the entry site, necessitating repeat right CAS placement with a PRECISE stent (7 mm × 40 mm; Cordis) providing greater radial force (Fig. 1C). The patient experienced no new infarction or hyperperfusion syndrome postoperatively (Fig. 1D). Although hospitalization was prolonged due to treatment of statin-induced hepatic dysfunction, the patient was discharged home independently approximately 4 weeks after CEA with a modified Rankin Scale (mRS) score of 0. The patient remained recurrence free on continued clopidogrel therapy for 105 months. After stent treatment, aspirin was added and continued for 3 months. Subsequently, this was changed to clopidogrel monotherapy.

Case 2

A female in her 80s with a medical history of hypertension, dyslipidemia, mild aortic regurgitation, glaucoma, and cataract developed transient right amaurosis fugax. Carotid ultrasonography revealed bilateral carotid stenosis. Cerebral angiography confirmed 67% right and 80% left carotid stenosis by NASCET criteria (Fig. 2A). Following successful right CEA, the patient was readmitted approximately 6 months later for treatment of left internal carotid stenosis. CT demonstrated calcification in the left internal carotid stenotic segment, necessitating CEA using an internal shunt (Pruitt-Inahara carotid shunt).

A: Preoperative MR angiogram showing severe stenosis at the left carotid bifurcation (arrow), with normal appearance of the distal ICA. B: Immediate postoperative MR angiogram showing poor visualization of the left ICA (arrow), raising suspicion for dissection. C: Lateral left ICA angiogram demonstrating a 55-mm dissection (arrow) in the distal left ICA. D: Post–stent placement lateral cerebral angiogram showing resolution of the dissection cavity (arrow) with restored normal flow.

During surgery, after removal of heavily calcified intima and shunt withdrawal, ICG video angiography demonstrated reduced flow velocity in the ICA (Video 1). Vessel inspection revealed no visible thrombus or obvious dissection; however, because distal pathology was suspected, rapid hemostasis and closure were performed with immediate postoperative evaluation planned. During surgery, heparin was administered intravenously to maintain an ACT > 250. Without reversing the heparin, the surgical wound was closed, general anesthesia was continued, and the patient was transferred to the MRI suite.

Immediate postoperative MRI showed no high signal on diffusion-weighted imaging, but poor visualization of the left ICA was noted (Fig. 2B), raising suspicion for carotid dissection. Cerebral angiography revealed a 15-mm dissection extending 55 mm distal to the carotid bifurcation (Fig. 2C). Owing to the extensive lesion length, same-day dual-stent CAS placement was performed.

Using a standard endovascular technique via a right femoral approach, we confirmed the lesion with 3D-DSA in the ICA cervical portion distal to the CEA site. Complete lesion coverage was achieved with an overlapping wall stent (6 mm × 22 mm) and PRECISE stent (6 mm × 30 mm). Postprocedural angiography demonstrated resolution of stenotic findings (Fig. 2D).

The patient experienced no hyperperfusion syndrome, and wound healing was excellent. She was discharged home independently with an mRS score of 0 approximately 2 weeks after CEA and remained recurrence free on continued aspirin and prasugrel therapy for 3 months. Since the patient was diagnosed with atrial fibrillation around the same time, the cardiology department determined that edoxaban and aspirin were necessary, and this regimen has continued for 3 years.

Literature Review

We conducted a comprehensive literature search using the keywords “carotid endarterectomy,” “dissection,” and “shunt” in the PubMed database for English-language papers, yielding 52 articles. After excluding articles unrelated to CEA intraoperative complications or cases in which CEA was performed as treatment for internal carotid dissection, 7 relevant articles remained.3,4,6,8,12–14 Additional references identified a total of 13 cases of carotid dissection potentially attributable to CEA shunt use,3,4,6,8,10–14 including our 2 cases (Tables 1 and 2).

Among all 13 cases, the mean age was 70.5 years (range 55–80 years), with 11 males and 2 females. Dissection laterality was left-sided in 9 cases, right-sided in 2 cases, and unspecified in 2 cases. Documented shunt types included the Argyle 6-inch straight shunt (1 case),3 Pruitt-Inahara carotid shunt (4 cases),12,13 and Furui’s double-balloon internal shunt system (3 cases).10,11,14 A shunt was not used or the type was not specified in the original publications for the remaining cases. Continuity of dissection with the arteriotomy site was confirmed in only 1 case;10 the remaining 12 were attributed to balloon inflation during shunt insertion or trauma from the shunt tip.3,4,6,811–14

Among the 13 cases with vascular dissection, 7 underwent stent placement (reported in 2017–2025),4,10,11,14 while 5 did not receive stents (reported in 1985–2015).3,6,8,12,13 Seven cases (54%) became symptomatic,3,8,10–14 and among symptomatic cases with documented onset timing, the interval ranged from 1 hour to 13 days (mean 5.8 days, median 6 days) (Table 2). Final outcomes were favorable in all cases except one reported in 1986 that developed acute cerebral infarction 1 hour post-CEA, resulting in residual aphasia and severe right upper extremity paralysis.8 All cases with asymptomatic dissection discovery and early stent treatment had excellent outcomes.

Discussion

Observations

We successfully treated 2 cases of acute endovascular stent placement for distal ICA dissection following CEA attributed to intraoperative shunt use, with both patients experiencing no neurological sequelae. The key observation is that both dissections occurred well beyond the endarterectomy endpoint (60 mm and 55 mm distally), strongly implicating shunt insertion rather than surgical technique as the cause.

The literature review revealed that this complication, while rare, has been increasingly recognized since the 1980s.3,8 The shift from conservative management to endovascular treatment around 2010 coincides with improved outcomes, suggesting that early stent treatment on dissection discovery provides superior results compared with simple observation.10 Additionally, balloon-type shunts were commonly used in 5 cases in the literature,10,–,14 suggesting device-specific risk factors. The Pruitt-Inahara carotid shunt used in our cases (Fig. 3) occasionally demonstrates nonconcentric balloon expansion, potentially causing shunt tip adherence to the vessel wall.

Photograph of the Pruitt-Inahara carotid shunt (LeMaitre Vascular) used in both cases, showing potential for nonconcentric balloon expansion (arrow).

Case 2 demonstrated that careful intraoperative ICG angiography can facilitate early recognition of dissection. In case 1, abnormal findings on postoperative day 1 imaging led to early intervention. When using shunts during CEA, high clinical suspicion for shunt-induced dissection is essential when intraoperative or early postoperative flow abnormalities are detected.

Lessons

The most important lesson from this report is that shunt-related carotid dissection represents a preventable complication requiring specific technical precautions. Prevention strategies include ensuring concentric balloon inflation during shunt preparation, as asymmetric inflation generates localized shear stress and compression at vessel wall–balloon sites, potentially triggering dissection. Gentle insertion technique, minimal insertion length, and minimizing longitudinal shunt movement are equally crucial. A study by Wilkinson et al.15 examining the impact of shunt morphology on cerebral blood flow reported that the Pruitt balloon-type shunt failed to maintain preoperative middle cerebral artery velocity in 66% of cases, while the Javid clamp-type shunt had a higher incidence of emboli at the time of declamping. Furthermore, they15 pointed out that a structure with proximal and distal balloon fixation, similar to the Pruitt type, would be preferable for reducing intimal damage. They15 also suggested that it should be resistant to kinking and as short as practically feasible. The clamp type has also been reported16 to have the advantages of being inexpensive, easy to operate, and able to be rapidly inserted.2 We have experienced dissection with the balloon type and believe that careful attention must be paid to eccentric balloon expansion, balloon overinflation, excessive deep insertion, and catheter migration during surgery.

When dissection occurs despite these preventive measures, early endovascular stent treatment may provide superior outcomes compared with conservative management. Our cases and literature review demonstrate that early intervention effectively prevents thromboembolic complications while improving functional outcomes. These technical successes support the paradigm shift from observation to active intervention.

Surgeons performing CEA must recognize that this complication arises from shunt-specific mechanisms. The pathophysiology differs from conventional endarterectomy-related direct dissection, involving distal balloon trauma or shunt tip injury. Therefore, when intraoperative flow abnormalities are detected, immediate postoperative imaging evaluation should be performed to enable early diagnosis and intervention.

Our cases demonstrated favorable long-term outcomes with no recurrence during the 105-month and 30-month follow-up periods. These excellent long-term results support the efficacy of prompt endovascular treatment and provide evidence for recommending stent treatment rather than observation when this complication is identified.
