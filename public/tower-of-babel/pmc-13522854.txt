Conditioned Pain Modulation and Pleasant Pain Relief as Complementary Processes Reflecting Individual Differences in Pain Coping: A Clustering Approach
Authors: Matthieu Vincenot, Maxime Roberge, Charles‐Édouard Giguère, Stéphane Potvin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundPain perception is the result of different modulatory mechanisms. Inhibition, via descending brainstem mechanisms, is key to pain reduction and is typically elicited by the cold pressor test (CPT). Recently, a compensatory hedonic response has been observed after CPT interruption, thought to facilitate return to homeostasis. Although both are triggered by the CPT, how these mechanisms vary across individuals remains unknown.MethodsPain‐free individuals (n = 200) participated in a quantitative sensory testing protocol assessing inhibition (via conditioned pain modulation (CPM)) and hedonic (via pleasant pain relief (PPR)) responses. CPM paradigm involved a tonic heat stimulation, administered before and after a CPT at 10°C. CPM magnitude was calculated as the change in pain ratings following the conditioning stimulus. PPR magnitude was quantified using participants' subjective perception of pain relief immediately following cessation of the CPT. A k‐means clustering analysis based on four principal variables was performed to identify distinct response sub‐groups.ResultsFour clusters provided an optimal solution and explained 58% of the total variance. Cluster 1 demonstrated high CPM and moderate PPR response. In contrast, Cluster 2 demonstrated weak CPM and strong PPR. Cluster 3 appears to be similar to Cluster 2. Finally, Cluster 4 was characterized by both weak CPM and weak PPR associated with low pain perception during the CPT.ConclusionThis study supports that two distinct phenomena pre‐exist, one leading to inhibition and the other to compensation. Although distinct, there appear to be complementary, with the PPR being stronger in individuals with weaker CPM responses.Significance StatementThis study highlights the recruitment of a potential hedonic compensatory mechanism when inhibitory resources are insufficient to cope with pain.

Introduction

Pain perception is a non‐linear phenomenon mediated by different mechanisms that may modulate its subjective experience (Marchand 2020; Vincenot et al. 2024), some of which are known to reduce pain perception. This is notably the case with descending inhibitory mechanisms (Bannister et al. 2021; Ramaswamy and Wodehouse 2021; Sirucek et al. 2023). In humans, under experimental conditions, a conditioned pain modulation (CPM) paradigm may provide an indirect behavioural index of descending pain inhibition (Tousignant‐Laflamme et al. 2008; Youssef et al. 2016). Briefly, a CPM protocol involves a painful test stimulus applied before and after a conditioning stimulus, often triggered by a cold‐pressor test.

Beyond the pain inhibition phenomenon, another mechanism seems to occur to cope with discomfort caused by nociceptive experiences, which has recently gained traction. The pleasant pain relief (PPR) is a mechanism explained by the opponent process theory (Leknes et al. 2008), which postulates that an initial emotional/motivational direction will be followed by an after‐reaction in the opposite direction. Thus, after the interruption of a negatively valenced reaction, a hedonic overshoot will occur, acting as a homeostatic correction to the preceding negative state. In the pain context, it has been observed that the offset of nociceptive stimulation is followed by the experience of PPR, and is more pronounced in paradigms with a cold‐pressor test compared to those with a thermode (Bitar et al. 2018, 2020; Henri et al. 2022; Leknes et al. 2008; Leknes and Tracey 2008).

CPM and PPR seem to recruit distinct mechanisms of action. CPM involves descending pathways originating mainly from brainstem structures (Harrison et al. 2022; Li et al. 2025; Mills et al. 2021; Wake et al. 2026). Although the neurobiological mechanisms underlying PPR remain understudied, early evidence suggests that this compensatory mechanism recruits core regions of the mesolimbic reward system (Leknes and Tracey 2008; Taylor et al. 2016). Given their distinct neurobiological mechanisms, it seems plausible that CPM and PPR may manifest differently across different subgroups of individuals.

Our laboratory has previously demonstrated that although PPR and CPM effects may be both elicited by a cold‐pressor test, their magnitudes are not significantly correlated (Bitar et al. 2018; Henri et al. 2022). This lack of association suggests that they may reflect independent processes of pain regulation/coping. However, it remains unclear whether this negligible correlation is linked to distinct sub‐groups of pain response used to investigate whether latent and distinct pain response profiles are revealed in CPM protocols (Preud'homme et al. 2021; Sarker 2021). Of these, only one study on CPM identified subgroups with varying inhibitory responses beyond differences in conditioning pain intensity. However, no clustering study has examined the inter‐individual variability of both CPM and PPR.

This study aimed to identify subgroups based on CPM and PPR responses using a clustering approach to better understand how they are expressed across individuals. Given the weak association between CPM and PPR observed in healthy individuals, we hypothesized that the highest and lowest levels of both processes would not co‐occur within the same clusters.

Material and Methods

Participants

Experiments were conducted at the Centre de Recherche from the Institut Universitaire en Santé Mentale à Montréal (Montreal, Canada). This study adhered to the Helsinki Declaration. Ethics approval was granted by the institutional review board of the Centre intégré universitaire de santé et de services sociaux de l'Est‐de‐l'Île‐de‐Montréal. A non‐probabilistic convenience sampling strategy was used for recruitment. This cross‐sectional study involved 200 healthy participants aged between 18 and 35 years. The study consisted of a single quantitative sensory testing (QST) session which lasted about 120 min. Exclusion criteria (auto‐declarative) were as follows: (1) any psychiatric disorder present in the DSM‐5 (including substance use disorder), (2) usage of any medication affecting the central nervous system, (3) neurological disorders, (4) any unstable medical condition or (5) any chronic pain condition. A financial compensation of $50 CAD was provided to each participant upon completion of the testing session.

Questionnaires

All participants completed a sociodemographic questionnaire. Psychological functioning was assessed using the Beck Depression Inventory (BDI‐II) (Beck et al. 1996; Collet and Cottraux 1986), the Snaith‐Hamilton Pleasure Scale (SHPS) (Loas et al. 1997; Snaith et al. 1995), and the State–Trait Anxiety Inventory—Forms Y1 and Y2 (STAI‐Y1/Y2) (Gauthier and Bouchard 1993; Spielberger et al. 1983), measuring depressive traits, anhedonia, and anxiety traits, respectively.

Dynamic Quantitative Sensory Testing Procedures

The experimental protocol was divided into two procedures: first, the CPM paradigm (Tousignant‐Laflamme et al. 2008; Yarnitsky et al. 2015), followed by the PPR paradigm (Henri et al. 2022). Briefly, the CPM procedure involved a painful tonic test stimulus administered before and after a conditioning stimulus delivered by a cold pressor test. After a 30‐min break, the PPR paradigm was applied, involving a cold pressor test followed by the assessment of subjective PPR. An overview of the procedure is displayed in Figure 1. CPM Paradigm

Pain Perception Recording

To record pain perception, a computerized visual analog scale (CoVAS), which consists of a slider running along a 100 mm horizontal track, was used. Participants were asked to rate their pain perception by moving the slider between the left boundary (“No Pain”) which represented a score of 0 and the right boundary (“Maximum Pain Tolerance”) which represented a score of 100.

Pretests

To familiarize the participants with the experimental pain paradigm, a thermode (TSA‐II, Medoc, Advanced Medical Systems, Ramat Yishai, Israel) was placed in the participants' left palm and they were asked to tell the administrator when the stimulation started to become painful (heat pain threshold—HPT) and when their maximal pain tolerance (heat pain tolerance—HPTol) was achieved. This procedure is commonly used to help people understand the nature of heat pain stimulation and minimize the element of surprise during experimental trials. To determine stimulation parameters, the thermode was then placed on the participant's left forearm. Participants were asked to move the slider of the CoVAS from its initial position to the right as soon as they perceived the first sensation of pain and continuously adjust the slider to reflect the level of their pain perception until their maximal tolerance was reached. For both phases, thermodes were set at a baseline temperature of 32°C at the start of trials before increasing at a rate of 0.3°C per second. We also determined a target temperature (T50), representing 50% of the individual participants' maximal pain tolerance. The T50 was estimated through two consecutive trials. To avoid any participants' sensitization or habituation, thermode placement was shifted such that a different area of the forearm was tested during each trial.

Test Stimulus

The test stimulus consisted in a heat pain stimulation applied on the participant's left forearm at a predetermined, individually tailored temperature (T50). To avoid expectations, participants were told that the thermode temperature could randomly vary (increase, remain stable, or decrease) during the stimulation. In fact, after a constant rise (0.3 °C/s) from baseline (32 °C) to the T50, the temperature remained constant throughout the 120 s of the test stimulus. Participants were asked to continuously report their pain level, using the CoVAS. This test stimulus was re‐administered under the same conditions after the conditioning stimulus.

Conditioning Stimulus

The conditioning stimulus consisted of a cold pressor test (CPT), wherein the participants immersed their right arm in a fixed cold‐water bath system (Julabo F33‐HL heating/refrigerating circulators) at 10°C up to the elbow for 120 s. During the CPT, participants were asked to verbally report their pain intensity from the moment that their arm was immersed and every 30 s until the 120 s were completed, for a total of 5 measurements. Pain intensity scores varied on a scale from 0 (no pain) to 100 (maximal pain that can be tolerated). The CPM amplitude was determined as an absolute difference by subtracting the mean pain levels induced by the thermal stimuli before and after the conditioning stimulus. The mean post‐immersion pain score was subtracted from the mean pre‐immersion pain score, such that a positive score was interpreted as indicating hypoalgesia (CPM effect).

PPR Paradigm

After a 30‐min break, the participants underwent another CPT using the same parameters as for the CPM paradigm (immersion of the forearm for 120 s in a bath of water at 10°C). They then reported their level of pain relief or, more precisely, the degree of relief they felt when the stimulation stopped. This relief was quantified using a numerical scale ranging from 0 (no sense of pleasant relief) to 100 (the greatest sense of pleasant relief). To understand this phenomenon, one could imagine the sensation experienced when walking into a heated room after being outside in freezing temperatures. This approach has proved successful in our previous PPR studies from our team (Bitar et al. 2018; Henri et al. 2022). The PPR was measured immediately after the arm was removed from the water‐bath, then every 10 s for the first 30 s, followed by every 30 s for the next 4 min, for a total of 11 measurements. The PPR amplitude was estimated by averaging all the PPR scores monitored after the CPT. A higher PPR score indicated a higher level of pleasure relief experienced after the offset of the CPT.

Statistical Analysis

A G*Power analysis (effect size, d = 0.25, α = 0.05) indicated that a sample size of 200 participants would provide sufficient statistical power (1–β = 0.85) to detect differences among cluster variables (Cohen 1988; Kang 2021; Serdar et al. 2021).

The cluster analysis involved 4 variables: (1) the CPM score (CPM); (2) the mean PPR score (PPR); (3) the temperature used in the test stimuli (T50); and (4) the intensity of pain perceived during the cold‐pressor test (CPT). However, as explained previously, two CPTs were carried out that were respectively used to trigger CPM and PPR subsequently, 30 min later. Given that the two CPTs were strongly correlated (r = 0.811, p < 0.01), it was not recommended to include both variables in the clustering model. Therefore, we opted to average the pain intensity across the two CPTs to represent the CPT‐Average variable. For control purposes, we also carried out the same analyses, considering the first (CPT‐1 model) and second (CPT‐2 model) CPTs independently in the model. The results of these separate CPT models were highly similar to those achieved with the CPT‐Average model. These results are presented in Supporting Information only.

A preprocessing transformation was performed to standardize the variables of interest into Z scores, as recommended for performing distance‐based clustering analyses (Wongoutong 2024). To determine the optimal number of clusters, we applied a mixed method from the NbClust package. This package includes a total of 30 clustering validation indices, calculated independently (Charrad et al. 2014), each providing an estimate of the optimal number of clusters. The synthesis of these indices involves aggregating their estimates to identify the solution with the largest consensus.

To perform the k‐means clustering, the cluster and factoextra packages were used. K‐means is an iterative partitioning method that minimizes the within‐class sum of squares for a predefined number of clusters (Gao et al. 2023). The algorithm is initialized by randomly selecting a first cluster centroid, after which each observation is assigned to the cluster with the nearest centroid based on Euclidean distance. Cluster centroids are then updated as additional participants are assigned to each cluster (Gao et al. 2023). This assignment–update procedure is repeated iteratively until convergence, i.e., when cluster assignments or centroids no longer change (Charrad et al. 2014). The k‐means approach was chosen because our data appears to have a compact, approximately spherical form, a structure for which k‐means is particularly well suited (Gao et al. 2023). That said, given the potential impact of choosing a different approach, we also carried out the same analyses using a hierarchical approach. These data will be presented as Supporting Information only.

As complementary analyses, we conducted a one‐way analysis of variance with Tukey's post hoc test to examine potential differences between clusters in terms of sociodemographic and psychological variables, and psychophysical scores. Analyses were performed via RStudio and SPSS.

Clustering Analysis

Regarding the optimal number of clusters, the majority of the NbClust indices converged across models (CPT‐Average, CPT‐1 and CPT‐2) toward solutions with 4 clusters (see Figure S1), also confirmed by the hierarchical model. For the CPT‐Average model, the 4‐cluster solution accounted for 58% of the total variance. This solution was therefore selected for further analysis.

After determining the optimal number of clusters, we compared participants from the 4 identified clusters across our four variables of interest (CPM, PPR, T50 and CPT‐Average) (see Table 2). Our results showed significant differences between the clusters for each of these four variables.

Cluster 1 accounted for 18% of the sample. Participants in this cluster demonstrated high endogenous pain inhibition and moderate PPR response. Members of this subgroup showed the strongest CPM effect across all four clusters, with an average 35‐point reduction in pain ratings following the CPT. In contrast, PPR scores were only moderate relative to the other clusters. Pain intensity during the CPT was relatively high and comparable to the CPT experiences by participants in Clusters 2 and 3. In contrast, Cluster 2 (which represented 30% of the sample), demonstrated weak pain inhibition and strong compensatory PPR response. Although individuals reported pain intensity during the CPT comparable to that observed in Clusters 1 and 3, they exhibited (with Cluster 3) the highest PPR scores among all clusters alongside a limited CPM effect. Cluster 3 (18% of the sample) appeared to be quite similar to Cluster 2 in that individuals in this cluster also exhibited a strong PPR response. However, although CPM responses showed substantial inter‐individual variability within this cluster, their distribution was shifted toward lower CPM values compared to other clusters with a marked tendency toward negative responses (57% of participants in Cluster 3 have a negative CPM score compare to 0%, 10% and 32% in Clusters 1, 2 and 4 respectively), indicative of paradoxical hyperalgesia following the CPT (Figure S2). A key feature of this cluster is that the T50 was substantially the lower than in other clusters. Finally, Cluster 4 (including 34% of the sample) was characterized by both minor pain inhibition and the weakest compensatory PPR response. In addition, participants in this subgroup also reported the lowest pain intensity during the CPT across all clusters. The model generated using the hierarchical approach identified equivalent sub‐groups (see Table S3).

Finally, as shown in Table 3, no significant difference (all p > 0.25) in terms of sex, age or psychological characteristics was found between the clusters.

Discussion

The objective of the study was to understand the relation between CPM and pleasant pain relief using a clustering model. Four sub‐groups have been highlighted, two of which appear to represent opposite ends of the spectrum: on the one hand, strong pain inhibition and low PPR compensation; on the other, low pain inhibition and strong PPR compensation. Sub‐analyses indicate that none of the socio‐demographic and psychological characteristics differed between clusters.

This study highlights significant inter‐individual variability in both CPM and PPR responses. While CPM variability is well‐documented (Henri et al. 2022; Petersen et al. 2025), growing evidence indicates that PPR also varies widely across individuals (Bitar et al. 2018; Leknes et al. 2008, 2013). These differences likely reflect distinct underlying physiological phenotypes, reinforcing the relevance of subgroup‐based analyses and justifying the four clusters identified here.

Cluster 1 was characterized by a relative strong pain inhibition, with an average pain reduction of 35 points. Indeed, recent normative data from the healthy population show that, for a protocol similar to ours, a reduction in pain perception post‐CPT of around 35 points is observed in the top 5% of the effectiveness responses (Vincenot et al. 2025). Conversely, when compared with the average responses from the other clusters, the PPR response remains very modest in that cluster (around 30). Although no study has previously established normative values for the PPR, data from two previous publications by our laboratory had shown that the average PPR score was around 40, with a peak PPR ranging from 63 (Henri et al. 2022) to 69 (Bitar et al. 2018). This sub‐group suggests that strong pain inhibitory capacity was not accompanied by a particularly elevated hedonic compensatory response in Cluster 1. This is consistent with previous data published by our laboratory (Henri et al. 2022), which showed that CPM and PPR were not correlated although they are both triggered by the same stimulus (e.g., CPT). Interestingly, Cluster 2 showed an inverted response, with a strong PPR response (around 63 points) contrasting with a moderate CPM response (around 14 points). This sub‐group highlights a response association where a high PPR co‐occurs with limited endogenous inhibitory efficiency. Meanwhile, Cluster 3 was comprised of individuals with highly ineffective CPM responses, as they exhibit paradoxical hyperalgesia following the CPT. Furthermore, similar to Cluster 2, Cluster 3 exhibits high levels of PPR, which may indicate the necessity of engaging compensatory mechanisms whilst inhibitory pain mechanisms are most impaired. Compared to other clusters, Cluster 3 exhibited a significantly lower stimulation temperature (T50), indicating greater heat pain sensitivity as well as a high level of pain experienced during the CPT (as also seen in Clusters 1 and 2). Taken together, these observations suggest that individuals in Cluster 3 might present a vulnerable sub‐group, a hypothesis that warrants future longitudinal investigation regarding chronic pain risk.

Taken together, Clusters 1, 2 and 3 lead us to believe that two distinct systems for coping with pain exist: on one side, a descending inhibitory mechanism that tries to limit the ascending nociceptive pathway (Heinricher et al. 2009); on the other, a co‐occurring compensatory mechanism involving the activation of the reward system, which might act to counteract the sensation of pain and restore homeostasis. The present results emphasize the idea that these systems may function in a compensatory manner across individuals. Following a painful stimulus, when an individual possesses sufficient inhibitory resources, a putative PPR activity would not be required. However, in the absence of sufficiently effective inhibitory mechanisms, the PPR system would be engaged to cope with the painful sensation to maximize the return to homeostasis. However, because these sub‐groups were measured at a single timepoint, they should be interpreted as cross‐sectional snapshots rather than stable traits, especially given the moderate test–retest reliability of CPM (Brady et al. 2023; Imai et al. 2016; Kennedy et al. 2016; Rana et al. 2025; Taylor et al. 2026; Vaegter et al. 2018; Vincenot et al. 2024).

Finally, Cluster 4 is somewhat different in that individuals in this group exhibited both an inhibitory and a compensatory response of low intensity; however, it should be noted that this subgroup reported a particularly low level of pain intensity during the conditioning stimulus (CPT) compared with the other clusters. It is therefore quite likely that the pain intensity experienced during the CPT was not sufficient to trigger these adaptive mechanisms. The relevant guidelines (Yarnitsky et al. 2015) on CPM emphasize that the conditioning stimulus should elicit a pain intensity of at least 40 on a 0–100 pain scale. CPT is the most common form of stimulation used in counter‐irritation protocols (Nuwailati et al. 2022) and is often considered to be particularly painful. That said, there appears to be inter‐individual variability in the pain intensity evoked by the CPT. This cluster accounts for 34% of our sample. This observation is consistent with a previous study from our team, which identified a subgroup of individuals (around 30%) with lower sensitivity to the CPT (Bitar et al. 2018). Future directions could focus on optimizing protocols specifically for these less sensitive subgroups.

Few studies have examined how sex, age and psychological characteristics affect PPR. In a similar sample (Henri et al. 2022), sub‐clinical depressive, anxiety, and anhedonia symptoms showed no significant association with PPR responses. Regarding CPM, it has long been emphasized that biological sex (Popescu et al. 2010) and age (Hackett et al. 2020; van der Meulen et al. 2024), as well as psychological characteristics, are factors associated with the inhibitory pain response. More recent scientific literature (Boggero et al. 2024; He et al. 2024; Ibancos‐Losada et al. 2020; Nahman‐Averbuch et al. 2016; Potvin et al. 2024; Vincenot et al. 2025) tends to suggest a more marginal involvement on the subject. It should be noted, however, that our participants exhibited very low levels of depression, anxiety or anhedonia (due to our exclusion criteria), making it more difficult to identify any potential correlations. Other parameters which were not considered in this study may differ between the clusters or contribute to cluster assignment.

While descending inhibitory mechanisms have long been linked to chronic pain, recent evidence suggests their overall contribution may be less prominent than previously thought (Fernandes et al. 2019; Nuwailati et al. 2022; Ramaswamy and Wodehouse 2021). Instead, weaker CPM mechanisms appear restricted to a specific subgroup of individuals, contributing to their pronociceptive phenotype (Potvin and Marchand 2016). This highlights the potential involvement of other pathways. While most research focuses on pain perception during stimulation, the post‐stimulation response remains understudied (Miclescu et al. 2021; Navratilova et al. 2015; Schreiber et al. 2017). Consequently, investigating other processes, such as a potentially compensatory phenomenon that may involve the reward system (Navratilova et al. 2015), represents a promising avenue.

Strengths and Limitations

Several limitations must be acknowledged. First, whether other experimental pain procedures would yield different results remains unknown. The age and healthy state of the participants may limit the generalisability of the findings to chronic pain populations who tend to be older. To meet statistical requirements, we averaged data from the two CPTs; however, additional analyses using each CPT independently yielded equivalent results. Moreover, while k‐means was the most suitable approach for our data, clustering can be method‐dependent. Nevertheless, a hierarchical approach produced fully consistent clusters, highlighting the replicability of these subgroups. Finally, the fixed sequence order (CPM followed by PPR) may have introduced an order effect. Several strengths should be highlighted. First, the sample size and data structure are well‐suited for k‐means analysis, improving distance estimates and enhancing cluster validity (Atif et al. 2024; Dalmaijer et al. 2022). Second, our CPM protocol follows expert guidelines and is extensively validated (Bitar et al. 2018, 2020; Henri et al. 2022; Potvin et al. 2024; Yarnitsky et al. 2015). Finally, the PPR protocol utilized a CPT that induces a robust response, a well‐established and highly replicable procedure in the literature (Bitar et al. 2018; Henri et al. 2022).

Conclusion

It is recognized that individuals with chronic pain exhibit distinct pain perception and modulation responses compared to healthy controls, and that experimental pain paradigms capture only part of the complexity of clinical pain. It would therefore be of interest to extend the current findings to clinical populations to evaluate the predictive value of inhibition and compensation mechanisms in chronic pain development and maintenance. One might hypothesise that chronic pain patients will be likely to belong to a subgroup characterized by a combination of deficits in inhibition and pleasant pain relief compensation. Building on our findings showing that the CPM and PPR mechanisms may represent partially distinct sub‐groups within individuals, future work should investigate whether they can help identify individuals at risk of chronic pain and whether clustering approaches can reveal meaningful patient subtypes. Finally, given the potential involvement of brain reward systems in PPR (Navratilova et al. 2015), it would be relevant to examine whether CPM and PPR mechanisms differ in chronic pain populations with and without comorbid conditions such as depression or substance use disorders.
