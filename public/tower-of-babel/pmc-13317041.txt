Characterizing HIV-related patient portal preferences among men who have sex with men in the United States
Authors: Kevon-Mark Jackman, Tom Carpino, Maria Trent, Jade Burns, Travis Sanchez, Stefan Baral
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGay, bisexual, and other men who have sex with men (MSM) remain disproportionately affected by sexually transmitted infections (STIs), including HIV, in the United States. Patient-facing digital tools, such as patient portals, can enhance STI prevention and care if designed to align with user needs and preferences. Thus, this study examined interest in specific portal features across various segments of MSM communities.MethodsData were drawn from the 2022–2023 cycle of the American Men’s Internet Survey. A randomized subset of participants was asked about interest in four portal features: (A) easier interpretation of lab results, (B) behavioral guidance after positive STI results, (C) ability to order HIV/STI home test kits, and (D) education on undetectable HIV viral load and transmission. To measure associations of sociodemographic, behavioral, and psychosocial variables with these multivariate feature interest outcomes we estimated adjusted prevalence ratios (aPRs) using multivariable Poisson regression models with robust error variance.ResultsThe final analytic sample included 3,495 MSM (median age = 44 years; 70% White; 13% self-reported as living with HIV). Interest was highest for easier lab interpretation (64%), followed by behavioral guidance (43%), home test kit ordering (36%), and education on undetectable viral load (36%). Adjusted prevalence ratios for endorsing the integration of Undetectable=Untransmittable (U = U) education into portals was 24% (95%CI = 1.07, 1.45) and 37% (95%CI = 1.18, 1.60) higher among Black and Hispanic/Latino participants compared to White participants. Reporting anticipated healthcare stigma (aPR = 1.53; 95%CI = 1.33, 1.76) and socio-sexual networking app use (aPR = 1.17; 95%CI = 1.03, 1.32) were associated with greater interest in home test kits. People living with HIV (PLHIV) expressed lower interest in home test kits (aPR = 0.64; 95%CI = 0.53, 0.77) but higher interest in U = U education (aPR = 1.72; 95%CI = 1.96). Similarly, reporting suicidal thoughts was associated with a 19% higher aPR for interest in the inclusion of U = U education.ConclusionsMSM broadly support patient portal features that enhance understanding of health information, provide actionable guidance, and facilitate testing and education. The variability in preferences by HIV status, age, race/ethnicity, stigma experiences, and mental health underscore the need for tailored design and implementation of digital health tools among MSM communities.Keywords: Digital health, Sexually transmitted infections, HIV, patient portals, Men who have sex with men

Introduction

Today, gay, bisexual, and other men who have sex with men (MSM) remain disproportionately affected by sexually transmitted infections (STIs), including HIV, in the United States (US) [1, 2]. Persistent disparities underscore the need for prevention strategies that address the needs of diverse, well-educated, web-connected MSM individuals and communities [3]. Patient-facing digital healthcare tools particularly those delivered through patient portals—represent a promising and rapidly expanding approach to improving STI prevention and care outcomes [4].

Effective digital health tools and interventions require intentional design that aligns with users’ needs, beliefs, values, and preferences; these items are central to strategies focused on supporting healthy decision making and psychographic segmentation methods for personalizing patient education so the information is most effective [5, 6]. In this study, we examine preferences for personalizing patient portal design to increase healthcare engagement and facilitate STI prevention across multiple segments of MSM in the US.

Interpretability of patient-facing information in patient portals

The 21st Century Cures Act has driven the evolution of patient-facing digital health tools; patient portals have expanded from simple access to personal health records into comprehensive, cross-platform systems that support patient-centered care [7]. For patients, the interpretability of patient-facing content in portals is important to making health information actionable. However, limited digital health literacy present as barriers to healthcare engagement with patient portals [8]. In a qualitative study investigating patient portal user experiences among young people living with HIV (PLHIV), a participant identifying as MSM expressed, “I struggle figuring out how to read my test results.” More importantly, there is a lack of research investigating the broader scope of digital health literacy needs among various segments of MSM communities [9].

Patient portals as platforms for patient-centric sexual health engagement

Patient portals also play a role in addressing STI related stigma and supporting sexual health communications [9]. Sharing validated health records indicating undetectable viral loads with sexual and romantic partners is empowering to some PLHIV and serves to combat pervasive HIV stigma [9]. When asked about using the patient portal to show a sexual partner his viral load, the above mentioned participant responded, “That would be perfect! For them to be like […] “Oh, I think you lying.” I got proof. I think that’s perfect. I got the proof right here. I don’t got to sugar coat it. I don’t got to lie to you. You can read it all for yourself [9].” Some MSM communities consider incorporating health education into patient portals as a means to improve the quality of sexual health service delivery [10, 11]. However, less is known about how MSM, both living with and without HIV, perceive the use of patient portals to communicate transmission risk and guide sexual decision-making in the context of undetectable viral loads.

Understanding these perceptions is particularly important given the role of HIV knowledge in shaping sexual behavior among MSM. MSM who know that people living with HIV that have an undetectable viral load cannot transmit HIV, are more comfortable engaging in condomless sex [12]. Equipping patient portals with educational resources may extend to providing patient-centric behavioral guidance on mitigating STI acquisition risk, for example, when using HIV preexposure prophylaxis (PrEP), after testing positive for STIs, and when HIV viral loads are undetectable. Since PrEP adherence effectively protects against HIV acquisition, some MSM who use PrEP may be motivated to increase physical sexual satisfaction by removing physical barriers to sexual pleasure, such as those created by condom use [13, 14]. However, HIV PrEP does not protect against the acquisition of other STIs; use of PrEP has been associated with an increased incidence of STIs [15–18]. Condomless sex likely contributes to the finding that nearly half of incident primary and secondary syphilis cases occur among MSM living with HIV [2].

Public health and clinician guidance encouraging STI testing is an important core to STI prevention for MSM. Home-based STI testing is increasingly popular and acceptable and serves as an important mechanism for healthcare linkage and continuity of care during PrEP use [19, 20] Regular home delivery of tests have demonstrated to increase access and utilization of STI testing services [21]. Understanding how various segments of MSM value providing in-portal linkages to home-based STI testing services and behavioral guidance in contexts of having an STI remains underexplored.

Study purpose

To address gaps in the literature and understand community needs, this study examines the prevalence of perceptions regarding four ways patient portals could better support STI prevention among men who have sex with men (MSM): (1) improving the interpretability of patient-facing health information; (2) education about what to do or not do if testing positive for STIs; (3) facilitating linkage to home STI testing kits; and (4) explaining what an undetectable HIV viral load means for risk of transmitting through sex. We further evaluate preferences across diverse segments of MSM communities, including by race/ethnicity, age, recent bacterial STI diagnosis, suicidal ideation, use of sociosexual dating apps, HIV status, and sexual behavior–related stigma.

Methods

Study overview

Data used were collected as part of the American Men’s Internet Survey (AMIS); an annual series of cross-sectional internet surveys conducted to assess sexual wellness among MSM in the U.S. [22]. Briefly, a non-representative sample of MSM were recruited via email and via advertisements posted on websites and within other socio-sexual networking apps that facilitate connection between individuals for a range of purposes, from friendship and social networking to dating and sexual encounters) between August 2022 and January 2023. Participants were eligible for the study if 15 years or older, identified as cisgender male, lived in the U.S. or dependent area, and reported at least one lifetime sex act (oral or anal) with a male partner or gay/bisexual identity if aged 15 to 17 years. Participants were provided informed consent. incentives were not provided for study participation. All study procedures were conducted in compliance with the Helsinki Declaration and with the written approval of the Emory University Institutional Review Board.

Outcome variables

A randomized subset of AMIS participants (N = 4,348) was asked about their perceptions and utilization of patient portals, described as, something that allows patients to securely view their personal health records online. Participants indicated (yes/no) what features would be helpful to include in patient portals. Four patient portal features were included in the analysis as multivariate outcomes and presented in the survey as: [A] “Make it easier to understand my laboratory test results”; [B] “Educate people about what to do or not do if they have certain STIs”; [C] “Help me to order home test kits for HIV and other STIs”; and [D] “Explain what an undetectable HIV viral load means for risk of transmitting through sex.” Patient portal features were identified from prior studies exploring perceptions about using the patient portal for sexual health [10, 11, 23].

Covariates: exploratory segments of MSM communities

Bacterial STI diagnosis was defined as whether participants reported a chlamydia, gonorrhea, or syphilis diagnosis in the 12 months prior to the study (yes/no). HIV diagnosis was defined as whether participants reported ever receiving a positive HIV test result. Suicidal thoughts was assessed using an item that asks participants “how often have you been bothered by thoughts that you would be better off dead, or of hurting yourself.” Responses included, (a) not at all, (b) several days, (c) more than half the days, and (d) nearly everyday. Suicidal thoughts was categorized as a binary variable comparing participants who reported any thoughts of being better off dead compared to those who reported “not at all”. Use of socio-sexual networking apps was defined as participants who reported using mobile apps to meet or socialize with gay men in 12 months prior to study (yes/no). (reference this scale)

Three sexual behavior stigma scales assessed anticipated healthcare provider stigma (5-items; score range = 0–5), general social stigma (5-items; score range = 0–5), and stigma from family and friends (3-items; score range 0–3) [24]. Stigma scales were dichotomized as endorsed (yes; score greater than or equal to 1) versus not endorsed (no; score of zero).

Analysis

Univariate and bivariate analysis were conducted for descriptive statistics. Bivariate analysis described prevalence of the four multivariate outcomes by age category, race/ethnicity, HIV or STI diagnosis, recent suicidal thoughts, use of socio-sexual networking apps, and sexual behavior stigma. Variables with a p<.10 in chi-square analysis were retained in multivariable Poisson regression models for each multivariate outcome [25].

Multivariable Poisson regression analyses with robust error variance were conducted to measure associations with each of the four features multivariate outcomes. All models adjusted for age, categorized as quartiles of the study sample’s age distribution, and race/ethnicity, categorized as, (a) White, non-Hispanic, (b) Hispanic, (c) Black, non-Hispanic, and (d) any other race/ethnicity. Significance was determined at p<.05.

Results

Among the N = 4,348 AMIS participants presented with patient portal questions, 853 were excluded due to missing data; including 745 missing data on socio-sexual networking apps use and 96 missing bacterial STI testing data. The final analytic sample included 3,495 participants (Table 1) with a median age of 44 years (interquartile range = 33 to 57). 70% of participants were White (2,435/3,495); 16% (572/3,495) reported bacterial STI diagnosis in 12 months prior to study. Among people self-reported to be living with HIV (13%; 433/3,495), 32% (137/433) reported a recent bacteria STI diagnosis (data not shown in tables).

Easier interpretations of lab results

64% (2,242/3,495) of participants indicated that a patient portal feature to make it easier to interpret laboratory results would be helpful (Table 2). Proportions of Hispanic/Latino participants (68%; 234/345) and Black, non-Hispanic participants (67%; 252/376) desiring easier interpretations of lab results were greater than the sample mean. Among those living with HIV, 68% (293/433) expressed interest compared to 64% of participants not living with HIV (1,949/3,062; p=.10). In multivariable Poisson regression models, Hispanic/Latino participants showed significantly greater interest compared to White participants (aPR = 1.09; 95%CI = 1.00, 1.18). Interest in this feature did not significantly vary by age, unlike other features (Table 3).

Behavioral guidance for positive results

43% (1,505/3,495) of participants endorsed that a patient portal feature which provides behavioral guidance for patients with positive STI test results would be helpful (Table 2). Among those diagnosed with a bacterial STI, 47% (269/572) expressed interest in this feature compared to 42% (1,236/2,923) of participants without a recent STI diagnosis (p=.04). Participants who reported sexual behavior stigma from family or friends, (46%; 468/1,026) and those reporting social stigma (558/1,222) also showed elevated interest. In regression analysis, interest in behavioral guidance following positive results was significantly higher among younger participants; participants aged 15 to 33 years exhibited the highest adjusted prevalence ratios (Table 3).

Linkage to order HIV/STI home test kits

36% (1,255/3,495) of participants supported the inclusion of a patient portal feature that provides its users with linkages to order HIV/STI home test kits (Table 2). This interest was higher among participants with bacterial STI diagnosis [(41%; 232/572) vs. (35% 1,023/2,923); p=.01], those using socio-sexual networking apps [(39%; 1,089/2,805) vs. (24%; 166/690); p<.001] and those reporting anticipated healthcare stigma [(41%;451/1,109) vs. (34% 804/2,386); p<.001] compared to their respective counterparts. Only 23% (100/433) of participants living with HIV endorsed this feature. In adjusted models, people living with HIV (PLHIV) were significantly less likely to express interest (aPR = 0.64; 95%CI = 0.53, 0.77), where those using socio-sexual apps (aPR = 1.53; 95%CI = 1.33, 1.76) and reporting anticipated healthcare stigma (aPR = 1.17;95%CI = 1.03,1.32) were significantly more likely to express interest (Table 3). Adjusted prevalence ratios for interest in the home test kit feature were significantly lower in participants age 45 to 58 years (aPR = 0.78; 95%CI = 0.69, 0.88) and 59 years or older (aPR = 0.72; 95%CI = 0.62, 0.83) compared to participants aged 15 to 33 years; however there were no significant differences with participants aged 34 to 44 years.

Education on undetectable HIV viral load and transmission

17% (598/3,495) of participants indicated that the inclusion of educational content on undetectable HIV viral load and transmission would be a helpful portal feature (Table 2). Among the exposure variables, participants living with HIV (45%; 195/433) and those with recent suicidal thoughts (35%; 227/654) had the highest proportions of interest in this feature compared to their respective counterparts. Multivariable models showed significantly higher interest among Hispanic/Latino participants (aPR = 1.37; 95%CI = 1.18, 1.60) and Black participants (aPR = 1.24; 95% CI = 1.07, 1.45) compared to White participants (Table 3). People living with HIV (aPR = 1.72; 95%CI = 1.52, 1.96), reporting suicidal thoughts (aPR = 1.19; 95%CI = 1.05, 1.34), and using socio-sexual networking apps (aPR = 1.24; 95%CI = 1.07, 1.44) also showed significantly higher interest. Adjusted prevalence ratios for interest in education on undetectable HIV viral load and transmission significantly increased as the age category decreased, with participants aged 15 to 33 years having the greatest adjusted prevalence ratio.

Discussion

This study investigated the perceived utility of including specific features within patient portals among a large, diverse sample of MSM, with the goal of informing digital strategies to reduce the public health burden of HIV and other STIs. Overall, participants most strongly endorsed features that enhanced the accessibility and interpretability of patient-facing health information. This finding aligns with prior research spotlighting the critical need to address health literacy as persistent barriers to healthcare engagement, particularly among populations affected by disparities in digital access, educational attainment, and stigma-related mistrust of healthcare systems [8, 26, 27]. Limited health literacy may further exacerbate socioecological factors that proliferate racial/ethnic disparities in the STI burden. More specifically, the disproportionate burden of STI among Black and Hispanic MSM are attributed to broader socioecological determinants such as network dynamics, access to culturally affirming care, and structural racism [28, 29]. Studies using artificial intelligence and large language model tools to improve the readability of health information for laypeople show early promise for addressing health literacy barriers [30–32]. Patient portals that integrate such innovations represent scalable solutions that, when tailored appropriately, may help to mitigate some of the aforementioned disparities.

Notably, PLHIV expressed lower interest in home-based STI testing features, but showed strong endorsement of educational content related to undetectable viral load and HIV transmission (i.e., undetectable=untransmittable or U = U). HIV-related stigma may contribute to discomfort or disinterest in options that may precipitate connotations of HIV self-disclosure, particularly when compounded by privacy concerns [33]. Conversely, the ability for PLHIV who are undetectable to use patient portals to demonstrate to their partners that they pose no transmission risk can help reduce HIV-related stigma and provide a more acceptable and empowering component to self-disclosure [9]. The elevated interest in U = U education among PLHIV may also intersect with mental health vulnerability, as we observed higher endorsement of these features among participants reporting suicidal thoughts. This is notable given that suicide deaths are 100-fold higher in people living with HIV than in the general population [34].

Mental health plays a role in STI vulnerability among MSM. Prior studies demonstrate that incident STIs are associated with the intersection of sexual behavior stigma and suicidal thoughts [35]. Suicidal thoughts are often associated with hopelessness, pessimism, negative self-perceptions, and poor problem-solving skills that may hinder condom use.35–37 Digital health interventions to benefit the mental health of MSM may be coupled with patient portal-based interventions to support communicating with partners about U = U and to improve self-efficacy for facilitating HIV self-disclosure [38, 39]. The preferences expressed by participants living with HIV suggest a need for well specified adaptive customization of digital health content, ensuring that patient portals do more than deliver lab results. Patient portals should also support ongoing education, particularly around U = U messaging, to reduce internalized stigma, promote health agency, and improve both mental and physical wellbeing and reduce anticipated HIV stigma [40, 41].

HIV self-disclosure has implications for condom use decisions; [42] this is particularly important given the STI risk among PLHIV. Nearly one-third of PLHIV in our sample also reported a past-year STI diagnosis, underscoring the importance of integrated STI prevention resources in HIV care. This aligns with a previous study on self-collected home-based testing, where participants living with HIV had a higher prevalence of syphilis as well as a higher prevalence of chlamydia and gonorrhea compared with those who were not living with HIV [43]. As digital health platforms increasingly engage younger users, the opportunity to integrate mental health screenings, motivational messaging, or connections to peer support may further increase engagement and reduce barriers to sexual healthcare.

Findings are also consistent with literature indicating that anticipated stigma related to sexual behavior and healthcare interactions may drive support for discreet home-based testing options more broadly among MSM, conversely to MSM with HIV [44–46] These findings are further consistent with literature on disclosure-related stress and the role of digital tools in facilitating communication of sensitive health information [47]. In particular, we found that MSM experiencing sexual behavior stigma from family or friends were more likely to value features that allowed for digital sharing of test results, suggesting that patient portals may serve a disclosure function when direct provider or lab communication feels unsafe. In contexts of disclosing test results, it may be particularly important that MSM using socio-sexual networking apps be able to leverage guidance on when positive test results are received or disclosed, including HIV. While socio-sexual networking apps have been leveraged for promoting sexual health, such as HIV status displays, PrEP promotion, and taking HIV medications, its use have also been associated with a greater likelihood of testing positive for chlamydia and gonorrhea among MSM [48, 49]. In addition to patient portals, these platforms may therefore serve as efficient channels to promote linkages to ordering home-based STI testing as a prevention strategy [50].

Limitations

There are limitations to this study. Principally, the strength of associations provided by reported prevalence ratios may be challenging to interpret within and between models given that they are based on varying prevalences of exposures and outcomes. Nevertheless, our models were able to detect significant variations in patient portal feature interest by exposure variables. We are additionally limited in the ability to determine relationships of various combinations of intersecting person-level characteristics (i.e., exposure variables) to patient portal feature interest. As patient portals and other digital health tools integrate desired sexual health features, latent variable analyses may be useful in segmenting various user-type profiles.

Conclusion

Our findings indicate that while MSM generally support enhanced digital features—particularly those focused on education, disclosure, and testing—many still need clearer and more accessible ways to navigate and interpret their health information. Designing patient portals and other digital health tools with attention to HIV status, mental health, and sociodemographic differences can promote agency rather than widen existing gaps. One-size-fits-all approaches risk overlooking the needs of individuals most affected by stigma, syndemics, and healthcare inequities.

Future research should assess the real-world effectiveness of patient portal features, including their acceptability, usage patterns, and long-term health outcomes. Embedding syndemic-informed frameworks and structural interventions into digital health design may help advance sexual health equity. As digital tools become integral to care delivery, optimizing person-centered patient portals is critical to building responsive and inclusive public health infrastructure. Community-engaged co-design should remain central to ensure these tools are both usable and trusted.
