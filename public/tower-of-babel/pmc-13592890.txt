Association of Conicity Index and Abdominal Volume Index with Handgrip Strength and Quality of Life in Hemodialysis Patients: Evidence from a Cross-Sectional Study
Authors: Fatemeh Navab, Farnaz Shahdadian, Sahar Foshati, Nafiseh Sharif, Gholamreza Askari, Mohammad Bagherniya, Firouzeh Moeinzadeh, Mohammad Hossein Rouhani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Little is known about the association of anthropometric indicators of visceral obesity with handgrip strength (HGS) and quality of life (QOL). We aimed to investigate the association of conicity index (CI) and abdominal volume index (AVI) with HGS and QOL in hemodialysis patients.Methods:The present multicenter cross-sectional study was performed on 326 maintenance hemodialysis patients. Anthropometric measurements were used for CI and AVI. The Kidney Disease Quality of Life Short Form and Smedley type hand dynamometer were used to assess QOL and HGS, respectively.Results:A higher level of CI was inversely associated with HGS (B: -25.62, P= 0.01) and QOL (B: -54.89, P= 0.03) only in female and not in male (P > 0.05). An increase in AVI was positively associated with HGS in male (B: 0.50, P= 0.01), while the significant association was not found between AVI and HGS as well as QOL in female in the fully adjusted model (P > 0.05).Conclusions:Our study evidenced an inverse association of CI with HGS and QOL in female. However, a positive association was found between AVI and HGS only in male. To confirm these findings, more studies are warranted.

Introduction

Quality of life (QOL) is considered as an increasingly important feature in health evaluations of hemodialysis (HD) patients.[12] Although hemodialysis plays a crucial role in these patients’ survival, it is unable to mimic all aspects of kidney functions.[3] Therefore, a variety of metabolic dysfunctions negatively affect their QOL.[45] Evidence proposed that both physical and psychosocial aspects of QOL in HD patients were not as good as in the general population.[6]

It has been found that low handgrip strength (HGS), a proxy for general muscle strength, is one of the most powerful predictors of adverse outcomes, including poor QOL, prolonged hospital stays, impaired functions, morbidity, and mortality.[7] According to previous studies, HGS is a predictor of not only the physical aspects but also the physiological ones.[8] Decreased muscle strength is known to negatively impact performance of daily activities, cognition, and subsequently the overall QOL.[89]

There are a variety of factors that can contribute to reduced grip strength in HD patients, including excessive circulating urea, high inflammation levels, increased oxidative stress, and endocrine complications.[910] In addition, unhealthful weight gain, obesity-related comorbidities, and metabolic disorders are also linked with less HGS.[1112]

Chronic HD is alongside with alterations in body composition; some patients suffer from excessive fat tissue loss, in addition to diminish lean body mass, which is associated with adverse outcomes.[13] Meanwhile, others gain fat tissues, and there are controversies regarding the effects of fat accumulation on QOL and HGS. The results of a 6-year cohort study on HD patients revealed an inverse association between body fat percentage and QOL.[14] Also, according to a recent study, having a high muscle mass combined with low adiposity content was associated with positive effects on both physical and physiological aspects of QOL.[15] In contrast, a recent cohort study indicated a direct relationship between adipose tissue and muscle functions with QOL.[16] In addition, there was direct association between body mass index (BMI) with QOL[1718] and HGS[19], which created “the obesity paradox” phenomenon.[1718] Nevertheless, a larger waist circumference (WC)[19] as well as a higher abdominal obesity was associated with a weaker HGS.[20]

Despite the widespread use of BMI in measurement of obesity in both clinical practices and researches, it is unable to differentiate between fat and muscle mass contents.[2122] Further, it cannot pinpoint the distribution of fat or muscle across the body.[23]

Anthropometric indicators of visceral obesity should therefore be simple, easy to perform, inexpensive, and valid in order to prove beneficial. In this regard, conicity index (CI) and abdominal volume index (AVI) have been suggested as good alternative measurements for visceral fat.[24] Since results regarding the association of adipose tissue with QOL and HGS are inconsistency, and there were no studies regarding the relationship between fat tissue with QOL and HGS in HD patients, we aimed to investigate the association between AVI and CI with QOL and HGS in HD patients.

Materials and Methods

Study population and protocol

In this multicenter cross-sectional study, 326 maintenance HD patients from the five governmental and charity hemodialysis centers in Isfahan, Iran were recruited. Hemodialysis patients of the mentioned centers were included in the study according to the inclusion criteria and with the nonprobability (available) sampling method.

We included patients if they met the following criteria: 1) being at least 18 years old, 2) undergoing maintenance HD for a minimum of 3 months, and 3) having the ability and interest to take part in our study. Subjects were excluded if: 1) their daily energy intake exceeded 4200 kcal/d or fell below 800 kcal/d, 2) they had enteral or parenteral nutrition, 3) they had recorded history of myocardial infarction or any psychological disorders, and 4) they were smokers. In order to be sure about participants’ conception of the study’s importance, methods, goals, and timeline, a brief explanation was provided to each of them. Then, informed consent forms were completed by all volunteers prior to participate in our study. This study was approved by The Research Ethics Committee of Isfahan University of Medical Sciences, Isfahan, Iran (IR.MUI.PHANUT.REC.1403.138).

Sample size calculation

Sample size was estimated using the following formula:

N = [(Z1–α/2)2 × SD2]/d2.

By considering a power of 80% and an alpha error of 0.1 and taking a low response rate into account, the sample size was calculated to be at least 300 subjects.

Assessment of quality of life

We used the Kidney Disease Quality of Life Short Form (KDQOL-SF 1/3) to assess QOL in HD patients.[25] The questionnaire consists of kidney disease-related domains such as symptoms and problems (12 items), the impacts of renal disease on daily living (8 items), the burdens of renal disease (4 items), occupational status (2 items), cognitive functions (3 items), social contact qualities (3 items), sexual functions (2 items), sleep qualities and quantities (4 items), social supports (2 items), dialysis staff encouragements (2 items), and patient satisfactions (1 item).[2526] It was organized into eight parts and two summary scores including the physiological and the physical component summary scores. This questionnaire covered physical functions, physical roles, bodily pains, general health perceptions, emotional wellbeing, psychological roles, social functions, and energy/fatigue-related questions. Each answer is then scored on a scale from 0 to 100, which higher values indicate better QOL.[27] The reliability and validity of this questionnaire have been previously examined and found to be acceptable in Iranian HD patients.[27]

Assessment of muscle strength

Handgrip test was performed in order to determine patients’ muscle strength using a Smedley springs handgrip dynamometer (model TS-HG100 MP1, made in Iran). It was measured after the end of a dialysis session from the dominant hand, unless there was contraindication. Patients were instructed to squeeze the HGS dynamometer with maximum force in a seated position, while shoulders were adducted neutrally, arms were unsupported, and elbows were bent at 90°. The measurement was conducted three times with an interval of 1 min. The mean of three trials was defined as muscle strength. According to Merkies et al.,[28] age and sex-specific thresholds were used to identify low muscle strength for each individual.

Anthropometric measurements

Dry weight was determined by a gradual change in post-dialysis weight at which neither hypovolemia nor hypervolemia occurred.[29] After the dialysis session, dry weight was measured in light clothing without shoes. A calibrated digital floor scale with 0.1 kg accuracy was used for this purpose. Subjects’ height was determined using anon-stretchable tape with 0.1 cm precision. They were applied to stand in a situation where their shoulders and barefoot touched the wall. Body mass and composition were examined using an 8-electrode body composition analyzer—MC-780 by TANITA (BIA Technology Multifrequency—5 kHz/50 kHz/250 kHz). The analyzer has the EU certificate CE0122. It is certified for medical use and meets NAWI CLASS III standards for medical measurements.[30] After a dialysis session, patients stood on this analyzer to measure body compositions including total body water, BMI, BFP, FM, and FFM. In order to determine waist circumference (WC), patients were instructed to straightly stand up and relax their abdomens with a gentle breathing. It was measured at the midpoint between the lowest rib and iliac crest. Hip circumference (HC) was also measured at the maximum circumference over the buttocks. Assessment of other variables

Data regarding patients’ age, sex, place of habitation, marital status, occupational status, educational status, family income, smoking history, menopausal status, and alcohol intake were collected from patient interviews and medical records. Medical records were also used to gather information including medications, dialysis vintage, dialysis frequency, dialysis duration, comorbidities, and etiology of renal failure.

Statistical analysis

The Kolmogorov–Smirnov test was applied to evaluate the normality of quantitative variables. The quantitative variables were illustrated as mean ± SD and qualitative variables as frequency (percentage). To compare the quantitative variables across tertiles of CI and AVI, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used, while for categorical variables, Chi-square test was used. The correlation of CI and AVI with anthropometric and renal characteristics, HGS, and QOL of participants was evaluated by Pearson correlation coefficient. To determine the linear association of CI and AVI with HGS and QOL, a crude and a multivariable-adjusted linear regression model was applied. In model I, adjustment was made for age. In model II, additional adjustments were done for marital status. In model III, weeks of dialysis, dialysis duration, dialysis sessions, Kt-V, and URR were added to previous adjustments. The SPSS software version 20 (IBM, Chicago, IL) was used to perform analysis, and Pvalue < 0.05 (two-sided) was considered statistically significant.

Results

Demographic and renal characteristics of participants across tertiles of the CI and AVI are illustrated in Table 1. The mean age of subjects was higher in the upper tertile of CI and AVI compared to the first tertile. In addition, subjects in the highest tertile of CI and AVI had a higher body weight, BMI, waist circumference, and fat. The HGS in the highest tertile of CI was lower than the first tertile, while HGS was not significantly different among the tertiles of AVI. By increasing CI and AVI, the QOL decreased. Moreover, after stratified based on sex, HGS was not significantly different among the tertiles of both CI and AVI in male.

Discussion

Our study indicated an inverse association of CI with HGS and QOL in female. However, a significant relationship between CI and HGS as well as QOL was not shown in male. In spite of the reverse association of AVI with QOL in female in a crude model, a significant association was not observed in the fully adjusted model. In addition, a one-unit increase in AVI was directly associated with increased HGS in male.

Nonetheless, gender’s role in QOL has remained a challenge yet.[6] As a result of the higher prevalence of viral hepatitis in men undergoing HD, they suffer more from lower QOL than females.[33] Opposed to that, studies revealed poorer QOL among females due to the higher prevalence of psychological disorders,[34] lower exercise tolerance, greater arm fat mass, less muscle mass, and less handgrip strength (HGS).[35] Further, a sex-related difference is also found in abdominal fat deposition patterns. There is a greater accumulation of subcutaneous fat in women, compared to visceral fat in men. The sex-specific rate of muscle strength[36] and subsequently the overall quality of life[37] might also be affected by such differences.

One of the most noteworthy observations of our study was that high lean tissue content has a positive relationship with AVI and consequently with quality of life. Similarly, Kalantar-Zadeh et al.[13] found that greater tissue fat content was negatively associated with QOL scores of HD patients.

In our research, BMI demonstrated a significant correlation with AVI. Additionally, a recent study examining the connection between body composition, QOL, and depression symptoms in nonobese individuals found that body composition was associated with QOL and depression symptoms. In females, higher overall adiposity and an abdominal distribution of adipose tissue were linked to lower QOL. In males, larger LTM, including greater lower extremity musculature measured by thigh circumference, was associated with higher QOL.[38]

The significance of body composition assessments in routine clinical practice is confirmed by these findings, which suggest that BMI has limited value, particularly in HD patients. Martinson et al.’s[39] longitudinal study examined the connection between musculature and QOL in 74 HD patients using MRI to evaluate body composition. The outcomes revealed that patients with larger mid-thigh muscle areas had better scores in both mental and physical QOL. In order to gain a deeper understanding of how musculature affects QOL, we introduced another factor into our analysis, muscle strength. Recent research has highlighted the significance of dynapenia, a condition characterized by reduced muscle strength without a corresponding decrease in muscle mass. Souweine et al.[40] discovered that individuals with dynapenia were at a higher risk of mortality, even if they were younger and had fewer comorbidities than those with sarcopenia.

To the best of our knowledge, no studies have compared the loss of function in different muscles related to end-stage kidney disease (ESKD), nor have they explored the association between the strength of different muscle groups and the QOL in HD patients. It is well established that numerous chronic diseases, as well as aging, can elevate the depletion of muscle mass and strength.[4142]

A study was carried out on patients aged 18 years or older undergoing hemodialysis, which suggested that low muscle strength was not linked to health-related QOL or mortality when using the WHOQOL-Bref instrument.[43] Consequently, future research should investigate any potential discrepancies in muscle loss related to ESKD and its correlation with QOL, as well as its potential clinical significance. Additionally, evaluating sarcopenia based on thigh muscle strength instead of handgrip strength ought to be taken into consideration.

Our study’s findings indirectly emphasize the significance of physical activity interventions for HD patients.[44] This notion is supported by meta-analyses of exercise interventions in this population, which demonstrate that both interdialytic and at-home exercise routines enhance the mental and physical QOL of HD patients.[4546] Ultimately, interventions that aim to increase muscle strength and lean tissue content should be encouraged, given their protective impact on QOL.

There were several limitations in our study. First, the study was cross-sectional and we cannot definitively establish causality in the relationship between exposures and outcomes. Second, unknown and residual confounders should also be taken into account. Last, the small sample size limited our approach.

Conclusions

Our study evidenced an inverse association of CI with HGS and QOL in female. However, a positive association was found between AVI and HGS only in male. To confirm these findings, more studies are warranted.
