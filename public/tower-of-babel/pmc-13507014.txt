Cerebellar and Subcortical Connectivity Associated with Arthrogenic Muscle Inhibition After Acute ACL Rupture in Males: A Clinical and fMRI Study
Authors: Bertrand Sonnery-Cottet, Patrice Peran, Dustin R. Grooms, Jacques Luaute, Thais Dutra Vieira, Marc Antoine Demaret, Nicolas de Neve, Alessandro Carrozzo, Etienne Cavaignac
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundArthrogenic muscle inhibition (AMI) is a neurally mediated impairment of quadriceps activation following knee injury. Peripheral and spinal mechanisms are well described, but supraspinal contributions to AMI in the acute phase after injury remain underexplored.ObjectiveTo investigate sensorimotor brain network connectivity changes associated with clinically classified AMI with task-based and resting-state functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) in male patients with acute anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) rupture.MethodsIn this prospective comparative cohort study, 40 male patients (mean age 24.1 ± 2.9 years) with acute ACL injuries underwent resting-state and a motor imagery task-based functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI). Patients were classified into AMI (n = 20) and non-AMI (n = 20) groups based on clinical assessment using the SANTI classification system.ResultsThe average time from injury to scanning was 22.7 ± 7 days. Clinically, the AMI group presented with qualitative vastus medialis obliquus inhibition and more frequent extension deficit. Compared with the non-AMI group, the AMI group demonstrated significantly greater functional connectivity of the right cerebellar Crus 1 with several sensory and cognitive brain regions. Exploratory (not corrected for multiple comparisons) task-based analyses revealed greater activation in the right caudate nucleus and right thalamus during motor imagery of the involved limb in the AMI group.ConclusionMale patients with acute ACL rupture and clinically classified AMI using the SANTI classification presented more frequently with knee extension deficits and consistent vastus medialis obliquus inhibition than those without AMI. In these patients, AMI was associated with altered cerebellar connectivity and, on exploratory analysis, greater subcortical activation during motor imagery. These findings support a central neural contribution to AMI and identify cerebellar–subcortical pathways as candidate targets for future mechanistic and rehabilitation studies.Trial registration NCT03950024 (ClinicalTrials.gov) registered on May 9th, 2019.

Introduction

Anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) rupture is increasingly recognized as neurophysiological as well as mechanical injury [1, 2]. One of the most clinically important consequences of ACL injury neuroplasticity is arthrogenic muscle inhibition (AMI), a neurally mediated impairment of quadriceps activation that manifests as reduced knee extension strength, often accompanied by reflex hamstring hyperactivity [3–5]. AMI contributes to quadriceps atrophy, dynamic instability, and increased osteoarthritis risk, with persistent deficits despite interventions like cryotherapy and strengthening exercises [4, 6–15]. While peripheral and spinal mechanisms are well documented contributors to AMI, supraspinal contributions remain less understood due to limited quantification of AMI in the prior neuroimaging studies.

The brain activity and connectivity differences in those experiencing AMI are particularly relevant in the acute phase after ACL rupture, when pain, joint effusion, altered proprioceptive input, and protective motor behavior are most prominent. This complex and multifactorial disruption of afferent signaling from the injured knee has been shown to acutely impact corticospinal excitability, but more widespread brain alterations specific to AMI are unknown [16]. Prior cross-sectional studies well after the acute phase and return to activity demonstrate extensive differences in those with ACL injury/reconstruction history across the sensory and motor cortices, sensory–visual integration, cognitive control of movement regions and the cerebellum [1, 2, 17–20]. However, no study to our knowledge has evaluated whole-brain activity or connectivity in the early stage of ACL injury recovery specific to AMI to better inform the potential therapeutic target development to mitigate AMI. Thus, the aim of this study was to explore the influence of clinically classified AMI using the SANTI classification on brain functional connectivity after acute ACL rupture in male patients.

Methods

Study Design

This prospective comparative cohort study included patients presenting to Toulouse University Hospital with an acute ACL rupture, defined as injury occurring within 4 weeks before inclusion. Participants were recruited sequentially between October 2019 and June 2024 and were evaluated by a knee specialist (Sport Doctor/Orthopedic Surgeon). Patients were then allocated to an AMI or non-AMI group according to clinical assessment based on the SANTI classification system, which identifies AMI from quadriceps inhibition and hamstring co-contraction [21]. All patients underwent diagnostic knee MRI at inclusion. MRI was reviewed in all cases and no patient presented a displaced meniscal tear, loose body, or other structural cause of mechanical extension block. All participants subsequently underwent brain functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI), including resting-state and motor imagery sequences, at the Toulouse Neuroimaging Center (ToNIC, Inserm/UPS).

Sample Size and Patient Characteristics

As no prior fMRI studies had specifically investigated AMI after acute ACL rupture at the time the study began, the sample size was based on commonly accepted standards for exploratory neuroimaging studies. In such studies, 15–20 participants per group are often considered sufficient to detect moderate-to-large effects while maintaining feasibility in clinically constrained populations [22].

Forty patients (20 per group), were included and met the inclusion criteria of: right-handed, male, aged 20–30 years, right-sided acute ACL rupture and an Edinburgh Handedness Inventory score of at least 8/10 to reduce heterogeneity and variability that may influence brain activity or connectivity patterns and reduce power loss to covariates [23]. All participants had completed high school education. Exclusion criteria were contraindications to MRI, previous ipsilateral ACL surgery, ACL revision injury, previous contralateral ligament reconstruction, multiligament knee injury, and any musculoskeletal or neurological disorder within the previous 5 years. ACL rupture was diagnosed clinically by a positive Lachman and pivot-shift examination and confirmed on MRI by complete ligament discontinuity. The study was approved by the regional ethics committee (CPP2019-05-037a/2019-A00479-48/19.03.29.62256), and all participants provided written informed consent.

Clinical Assessment and AMI Classification

Clinical assessment was performed by experienced knee specialists (EC, MAD, NDN).

Patients with acute ACL rupture were classified as having AMI or non-AMI using the SANTI classification system, a clinically based method that identifies AMI through quadriceps inhibition and associated hamstring co-contraction. The SANTI system [3] stratifies AMI into four clinically observable grades based on quadriceps inhibition and hamstring co-contraction, with physiological validity confirmed by EMG evidence showing distinct neuromuscular activation patterns for each grade [24]. Its reliability was established in a prospective study of 140 patients, reporting excellent inter- and intra-rater agreement (ICC = 0.92–0.99) [25].

In addition to AMI classification, the following variables were recorded at inclusion: pain at rest using a Visual Analog Scale (VAS, 0–10), joint effusion by patellar tap test (none, mild, moderate, or major), passive extension deficit measured with a goniometer and considered present when greater than 5°, and vastus medialis obliquus (VMO) inhibition assessed by palpation during isometric quadriceps contraction and considered present when no visible or palpable contraction was detected. Pain outside the knee was also screened using a full-body VAS, with no participant reporting pain outside the knee. These clinical secondary variables were analyzed descriptively and presented to provide context on the patient cohort.

MRI Acquisition

MRI was performed on a 3-T scanner (Philips Achieva, Inserm/UPS UMR1214 ToNIC Technical Platform, Toulouse, France). Acquisition included a 3D T1 anatomical sequence, resting-state fMRI, and task-based fMRI. Resting-state and task-related sequences were acquired using gradient echo-planar imaging (EPI) with the following parameters: TE = 30 ms, TR = 1.3 s, parallel imaging, 44 axial slices, a voxel size of 3×3x3 mm3. During resting-state acquisition, participants were instructed to fixate on a cross, remain relaxed, and avoid movement for the scan duration of 10.8 min. For task-based fMRI, participants were instructed to imagine flexing either the left knee or the right knee according to visual cues presented on a screen (5 blocks of 32.5 s each for each condition).

fMRI Data Processing

Task-fMRI data were analyzed using SPM12 in Matlab. Images were realigned, co-registered, normalized to Montreal Neurological Institute space, and smoothed with an 8-mm full-width at half-maximum Gaussian kernel. BOLD responses were modeled with a canonical hemodynamic response function, and motion parameters were included as regressors. Resting-state data were processed using SPM and the CONN toolbox. Preprocessing included realignment, normalization, smoothing, denoising with aCompCor, and band-pass filtering (0.009–0.08 Hz). This band corresponds to the default temporal filter of the CONN pipeline used in our laboratory; as a precaution, the data were re-analyzed using the conventional 0.01–0.1 Hz band, which yielded comparable MVPA results. Potential outlier scans were identified with ART using a framewise displacement threshold >0.9 mm or global BOLD signal change >5 standard deviations. To further reduce motion-related variance, 12 motion regressors were included (3 translations, 3 rotations, and their first-order derivatives). Mean framewise displacement did not differ between groups (AMI: 0.13 ± 0.03 mm; non-AMI: 0.13 ± 0.04 mm), and the number of excluded scans was also similar (AMI: 5.4 ± 8.1; non-AMI: 4.9 ± 6.4).

fMRI Statistical Analyses

For task-based fMRI, first-level contrasts were generated for imagined left knee flexion versus rest, imagined right knee flexion versus rest, and imagined left versus imagined right knee flexion. At the second level, group comparisons were performed using two-sample t-tests between AMI and non-AMI participants. Findings surviving family-wise error (FWE) correction at p < 0.05 were considered significant; results at voxel-level p < 0.001 uncorrected were considered exploratory. For resting-state fMRI, we performed whole-brain data-driven multivariate pattern analysis (MVPA) as described in previous works [26, 27] implemented in the connectome-MVPA CONN toolbox [28]. The aim of this method is to derive seeds based on the data, then to perform a post hoc analysis on the seeds to analyze brain connectivity patterns. The MVPA method computes FC patterns for each voxel by performing a PCA on the connectivity patterns between each single voxel and all the other voxels. Using this approach, we compared the two groups and identified regions surviving cluster-level false discovery rate (FDR) correction at p < 0.05. These regions were then entered into post hoc seed-to-voxel analyses. Seed-to-voxel analyses had a threshold at voxel-level p < 0.001 uncorrected with cluster-level FDR correction at p < 0.05. Because the sample was intentionally restricted by sex, handedness, education, and injury side to reduce heterogeneity, these variables were not included in the primary models; however, a confirmatory analysis including age and education level as covariates did not materially change the resting-state findings. A further sensitivity analysis including resting-state pain (VAS) as a covariate likewise did not materially change the resting-state findings; because approximately half of the VAS values were zero, this covariate was interpreted with caution.

Clinical and Patient Data Statistical Analyses

Clinical and demographic variables were compared between groups using independent t-tests or Mann–Whitney U tests for continuous data, depending on distribution, and Chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests for categorical data. Results are presented as mean ± standard deviation or number and percentage, with 95% confidence intervals where appropriate.

SANTI-Classified AMI Characteristics

The present cohort was grouped dichotomously for the primary analyses, but the clinical pattern in the AMI group reflected a relatively pronounced phenotype, as all AMI participants demonstrated VMO inhibition and most (95%) also had marked extension deficit with hamstrings contracture, consistent with the clinical classification (Table 1).

fMRI Findings

MVPA identified the right cerebellar Crus 1 as a seed region showing significant between-group differences. Compared with the non-AMI group, the AMI group demonstrated greater resting-state connectivity between this seed and several sensory and cognitive regions (Table 3; Fig. 1). Discussion

The primary finding of this study was that clinically classified AMI using the SANTI classification after acute ACL rupture was associated with greater resting-state functional connectivity between the right cerebellar Crus 1 and sensory and cognitive brain regions. The exploratory motor imagery analyses revealed greater right caudate nucleus and right thalamus activation in the AMI group, though effects did not survive multiple comparison correction. Together, these data support a model in which AMI after ACL rupture is not solely a peripheral or spinal phenomenon, but involves altered cerebellar–cortical and subcortical circuitry.

AMI-Associated Brain Connectivity

To our knowledge, this study is the first to demonstrate that clinically classified AMI using the SANTI classification may be associated with unique cerebral reorganization in the acute phase after ACL rupture. Prior ACL neuroimaging work has shown altered activity in sensory, motor, visual, and cerebellar systems, but has not isolated the contribution of AMI, nor evaluated patients in the acute phase within ~20 days of injury [1, 29]. Unlike previous studies focusing on brain activity differences related to muscle strength [30], patient outcomes [31], visual-cognition [32], postural control [33], or injury-risk biomechanics [34], our data suggest that AMI also uniquely contributes to injury-associated neuroplasticity [35]. The clinical profile of the AMI group is also informative. Pain at rest and effusion were not statistically different between groups, whereas extension deficit and IKDC scores were worse in the AMI group. This clinical presentation suggests that the observed neural differences may not originate primarily from pain or effusion as commonly attributed [36]. Although prior experimental pain and effusion studies demonstrated reductions in central activation ratio [37], in the acute phase after injury the clinical presentation of AMI may depend more on supraspinal response to injury, as effusion and pain were similar regardless of presence of clinically classified AMI using the SANTI classification. The elevated connectivity may thus represent an attempted adaptive response to overcome the inhibition by attempting to resolve disrupted sensory processing and perceived function from the injury [38–42].

However, the lower perceived function assessed via IKDC scores in the AMI group indicate that the potential compensatory nature of the connectivity is limited, and could reflect an interaction between perceived disability, AMI, and differential cerebellar connectivity. While our cross-sectional data cannot indicate which one drives the other, whether the perception of disability (IKDC) increases cerebellar–cortical connectivity or the injury disrupted afference changes cerebellar connectivity related to motor behavior, that in turn manifests as reduced patient-reported function. We would conjecture it is the unique neurophysiological state of the patient that results in elevated AMI after injury that in turn degrades IKDC score and recovery.

The anatomical specificity of the right cerebellar Crus 1 and associated connectivity points to higher-order cerebellar–cortical network involvement beyond simple motor execution only. If the effect was centered more on lobules V–VI or VIII that may have suggested isolated sensory lower limb representation or motor control alterations are driving the connectivity group differences [40, 41]. However, the Crus 1 seed instead showed greater connectivity with frontal, temporal, parietal, postcentral/supramarginal, and occipital regions (Table 3), a pattern that maps more closely onto cognitive-associative, sensory integration, movement planning, and sensory prediction functions than onto isolated quadriceps motor execution [43–46]. This distinction supports a model in which AMI reflects altered integration of degraded knee afference with higher-order movement representations. Within this model, increased Crus 1 connectivity may represent a compensatory attempt to stabilize sensory expectations and motor plans after ACL injury, or it may represent maladaptive over-reliance on associative control networks. This interpretation is consistent with the Neuroplastic Outcomes from Impaired Sensory Expectations (NOISE) hypothesis, which proposes that ACL dysfunction may degrade sensory expectations and increase reliance on compensatory neural strategies to maintain knee stability [38]. Interestingly, while all patients experienced the same injury, the variability in AMI presentation suggests that patients neurologically compensate for the disrupted afference input via different means with different associated clinically classified AMI presentation [5, 44].

It should be noted that the exclusive right lateralization of the cerebellar finding (right Crus 1) is consistent with the right-sided ACL given the ipsilateral cerebellar representation of the injured right limb and crossed cortical–cerebellar organization [40, 47–50]. A non-exclusive alternative hypothesis is related to the dominance effect and handedness, as the right cerebellar hemisphere is preferentially connected to the left cerebral hemisphere. The absence of any left cerebellar finding should not be interpreted as evidence that the contralateral cerebellum is uninvolved in AMI; given the exploratory design and the modest sample size, this null result may equally reflect limited statistical power.

From a functional neuro-anatomical perspective, the key role of Crus 1 is consistent with the known connectivity patterns with the cerebral associative pre-frontal cortex rather than primary sensory-motor cortices [51]. Functional imaging studies have shown that Crus 1, particularly expanded in humans, is involved in higher-order executive processes and emotional integration [40], which is in line with the top-down AMI mechanism previously hypothesized. However, as we did not capture spinal or peripheral specific neural excitability or activation, we cannot determine the degree of peripheral influence on the connectivity associated with AMI. However, the specific involvement of the right Crus 1 is compatible with the right-sided ACL patients given the spino-cerebro-cerebellar organization of the cerebellum indicating the potential for spinal contributions to the connectivity differences to be likely.

Imaged Knee Movement Activity

The network of areas activated during the knee flexion imagery task aligns with previous findings in healthy individuals and those with neurological disorders [52, 53]. The bilateral activation with relatively higher frontal than primary sensorimotor activity is consistent with motor imagery tasks, particularly for the less lateralized lower extremity [54, 55]. The exploratory increase in right caudate and thalamic activation in the AMI group during right knee motor imagery may suggest greater engagement of subcortical circuits beyond the primary motor cortex, potentially related to sensorimotor integration through basal ganglia–thalamocortical pathways (Fig. 2) [1, 2, 56]. Although the resting-state and task-based findings are reported separately, they can be integrated into a tentative neurocircuitry model. The two results implicate partially distinct nodes: the resting-state signal centers on the right Crus 1 and its cortical association targets, whereas the exploratory task-based signal centers on the right caudate and thalamus, which did not themselves emerge among the resting-state clusters. Rather than being contradictory, this dissociation is consistent with a two-component account in which increased intrinsic Crus 1-cortical connectivity reflects a comparatively stable, trait-like reorganization detectable at rest, while caudate-thalamic activation reflects a state-dependent response recruited specifically when the injured limb is challenged by motor imagery. Both components converge on cerebello-thalamo-cortical and basal ganglia-thalamo-cortical loops that are well placed to integrate the elevated peripheral afferent inflow generated by the acute injury, supporting the interpretation of AMI as a distributed subcortical phenomenon rather than a purely cortical or spinal one. These task-based observations remain uncorrected and hypothesis-generating and require confirmation in adequately powered samples.

Clinical Validity of the SANTI Classification System

A central aspect of this study is the use of the SANTI clinical classification system to qualitatively identify AMI, a pragmatic alternative to laboratory-based methods like the interpolated twitch technique or central activation ratio, which are limited by cost, complexity, and impracticality for routine clinical use [36]. This approach was well suited to an acute clinical cohort and enabled the present in vivo neuroimaging comparison. At the same time, the study should not be interpreted as establishing clinical palpation as a full substitute for instrumented AMI assessment. Rather, the findings suggest that a clinically classified AMI phenotype can map onto distinct neural organization. The current sample also represents a pronounced AMI phenotype, with every AMI group patient exhibiting observable VMO inhibition and 95% demonstrating extension deficit. In this acute cohort the loss of terminal extension was functional rather than mechanical: structural causes of extension block were excluded on MRI in all patients.

It is important to emphasize that the extension deficit observed in the AMI group should not be interpreted as a mechanical restriction of knee extension. All patients underwent MRI before inclusion, excluding intra-articular lesions capable of producing a true mechanical block. Within the SANTI classification, extension deficit reflects hamstring contracture associated with quadriceps inhibition and therefore represents a clinical manifestation of AMI rather than a separate pathological process. Consequently, the higher prevalence of extension deficit in the AMI group was expected and supports the clinical validity of the classification rather than providing an alternative explanation for the observed brain connectivity differences. This clustering of clinical findings supports the face validity of the SANTI-based grouping in the present cohort while also indicating that future work should determine whether different SANTI grades correspond to distinct neural signatures, whether those signatures track recovery over time and whether they corroborate more sophisticated electromyographic measures of AMI.

Clinical Implications

These findings refine the neurophysiological model of AMI and suggest that interventions directed only at peripheral afference or isolated voluntary quadriceps contraction may be incomplete for some patients [4, 59]. If altered cerebellar–cortical association region connectivity reflects disrupted sensory prediction and higher-order sensorimotor integration, rehabilitation may benefit from approaches that more directly challenge movement prediction-error updating, sensory reweighting, and coordinated quadriceps-hamstring control. Potential examples include error-augmentation or perturbation-based tasks that exaggerate knee-position or loading errors to drive neural recalibration [60, 61], graded sensory-reweighting tasks that manipulate visual and somatosensory reliability [62, 63], and biofeedback-assisted quadriceps-hamstring retraining to restore VMO activation while reducing protective hamstring co-contraction [64, 65]. The exploratory caudate and thalamic findings further suggest that motor imagery or action-observation practice with performance feedback, neurofeedback, or reinforcement-learning structure may be useful for engaging basal ganglia–thalamocortical circuits during the acute phase when knee loading is limited [66, 67]. These suggestions may enhance standard of care by directly challenging sensory or motor predictions, sensory reweighting and integration, and coordinated quadriceps-hamstring neuromotor control, though require further study.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the sample size was modest, which is common in exploratory neuroimaging research but may have reduced statistical power, particularly for task-based analyses. Accordingly, the motor imagery findings should be considered preliminary, especially because they did not survive correction for multiple comparisons. Second, the inclusion of only right-handed male participants with right-sided ACL rupture reduced biological and neurofunctional heterogeneity but also limits external validity. These findings therefore cannot be directly generalized to female patients, left-sided injuries, bilateral involvement, or broader ACL-injured populations. Third, the study focused on the acute phase after injury, with imaging performed at a mean of approximately 22 days, and therefore does not inform whether the observed neural changes persist, resolve, or evolve during rehabilitation. Fourth, AMI was identified clinically using the SANTI classification rather than with instrumented measures such as interpolated twitch or central activation ratio, although this classification is pragmatic and supported by prior reliability and electrophysiological data [24, 25]. Finally, although motion was carefully controlled and did not differ between groups, residual confounding inherent to fMRI studies cannot be excluded. Longitudinal studies combining clinical, electrophysiological, and neuroimaging measures are now needed to determine whether these cerebellar and subcortical signatures track AMI severity and treatment response.

Several further limitations relate to the imagery task and to the clinical phenotype. First, motor-imagery ability and task compliance were not assessed with a validated instrument or confirmed by structured post-scan debriefing; the task-related activation pattern observed here is, however, consistent with the patterns reported in previously published mental-simulation and motor-imagery studies [52], which argues against gross non-compliance. Second, pain during the motor-imagery task and during knee movement was not recorded, which is a limitation given the influence of nociception on cerebellar and thalamic activity; resting pain was nonetheless low and comparable between groups (median [IQR] 1.0 [0.0; 3.0] in the AMI group vs 0.0 [0.0; 2.5] in the non-AMI group, with a between-group difference below the minimal clinically important difference of 1 point [68, 69]), and a post hoc analysis including resting-state VAS as a covariate did not materially change the resting-state findings, although with approximately half of the values equal to zero this adjustment should be interpreted with caution. Third, because the extension deficit forms part of the AMI phenotype rather than acting independently of it, the present design cannot isolate the neural correlates of quadriceps inhibition from those of the broader clinical phenotype of which the extension deficit is a component.

Conclusions

Male patients with acute ACL rupture and clinically classified AMI using the SANTI classification presented more frequently with knee extension deficits and consistent vastus medialis obliquus inhibition than those without AMI. In these patients, AMI was associated with altered cerebellar connectivity and, on exploratory analysis, greater subcortical activation during motor imagery. These findings support a central neural contribution to AMI and identify cerebellar–subcortical pathways as candidate targets for future mechanistic and rehabilitation studies.
