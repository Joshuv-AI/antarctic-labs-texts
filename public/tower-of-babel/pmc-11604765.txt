Investigating the effect of hole size, bottom hole temperature, and composition on cement bonding quality of exploratory wells in Iran
Authors: Pooria Kianoush, Farough Mesgari, Emad Jamshidi, Mostafa Gomar, Ali Kadkhodaie, Shahab Varkouhi
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
The oil and gas industry’s reliance on well cementing practices to ensure well integrity and productivity is a pressing concern, particularly in exploratory wells where unpredictable conditions can lead to subpar cement bonding efficiency. This study addresses the pressing issue of optimizing cementing practices in Iran’s exploratory wells, ensuring better well integrity and productivity. The preliminary objective is to investigate the impact of hole size and bottom hole temperature variations on cement bonding efficiency in these wells. We analyzed cement quality logs from 21 exploratory wells in reservoir zones between 2012 and 2022 to achieve this. The study employed a systematic approach, using CBL/VDL and cement formulation to assess the quality of each wellbore section separately. We designed a cement formulation based on changes in bottom hole temperature and gas migration control and implemented it using three different contractors, each with unique additives. It allowed us to compare the quality of cement resulting from different phases and additives. Our results show that cement designed with dominant liquid additions in the 5-inch liner phase exhibits better bonding than formulations with increased powder form additives, specifically those containing a liquid dispersant, liquid fluid loss controller, liquid anti-gas migration, and liquid H.T. retarder for LNR 5 inch. Additionally, smaller hole sizes demonstrate better cement bonding quality due to reduced fluid flow and less turbulence. These findings have significant implications for optimizing cementing practices in the petroleum industry, particularly in Iran’s exploratory wells. Overall, this investigation provides valuable insights for improving wellbore integrity and productivity by optimizing cementing practices, which can be applied to future drilling operations in Iran’s exploratory wells.

Introduction

The exploration and development of oil and gas resources in Iran have been a significant focus of the country’s energy sector1–3. One of the crucial aspects of drilling exploratory wells is ensuring the cement bonding quality, which plays a vital role in maintaining well integrity and enhancing production efficiency4–7. Investigating the effect of hole size and bottom hole temperature on cement bonding quality is essential for optimizing drilling operations and improving well performance. The primary relation between cement quality and drilling optimization is that optimizing cement bonding quality is essential for optimizing drilling operations and improving well performance. A good cement bond can help prevent issues such as cement leakage, reduce well productivity, increase maintenance costs, and even reduce safety risks. By optimizing cement bonding quality, drilling operations can be optimized to achieve better well performance, improve overall drilling efficiency, and reduce costs. Cement bonding quality is essential for ensuring wellbore integrity, preventing fluid migration, and prolonging the well’s lifespan8–12. This study investigates the effect of hole size and bottom hole temperature on the cement bonding quality of exploratory wells in Iran.

The main objective of this study is to investigate the effects of hole size, bottom hole temperature, and composition on cement bonding quality in Iranian exploratory wells. The goal is to optimize cementing practices to ensure better well integrity and productivity. It involves analyzing the relationship between hole size and cement bonding quality, evaluating the impact of bottom hole temperature variations on cement bonding efficiency, and identifying best practices for optimizing cementing operations.

Hole size in drilling operations refers to the diameter of the borehole, which can significantly influence the cement bonding quality. Larger hole sizes can increase fluid loss and lower cement density, affecting the bonding strength between the cement and the wellbore13–15. On the other hand, smaller hole sizes can restrict cement flow, leading to inadequate cement coverage and bonding quality. Therefore, understanding the relationship between hole size and cement bonding quality is essential for optimizing drilling operations16–19.

Bottom-hole temperature (BHT) is another critical factor affecting cement bonding quality. High BHT can lead to thermal expansion of the cement, which may result in reduced bonding strength and potential cement failure7,20–22. Additionally, high temperatures can cause chemical reactions within the cement system, altering its properties and potentially compromising its bonding performance. Conversely, low BHT may lead to slower cement curing times, negatively impacting the cement bonding quality23–25.

The most crucial reason for cementing the well is its hydraulic insulation. Failure to proper cementing will be the root of many problems during production. The cement bond log measures the integrity between the casing pipe and the wellbore. This chart is obtained from one of several acoustic log instruments5,17,26–29. Its new versions, called Cement mapping, can measure 360 degrees of wellbore cement integrity, but the older versions only display one line representing the integrity of the wall pipe. Cement bonding log (CBL) is a standard technology to evaluate cement quality. The cement bonding chart is an efficient support in estimating the quality of cement bonding, which can be reliable if implemented correctly and interpreted accurately. Several recent bond charting equipment, such as radial bond tool (RBT), radius incremented bond tool (RIB), and sector bond tool (SBT), can also measure cement bonds11,30,31.

Recent studies have investigated the effects of various additives on the bond strength of cementitious composites35. Althoey et al.36 found that silica fume improved bond strength by up to 30%. Metaxa et al.37 discovered that nanoparticles increased bond strength by up to 50%. Tamayo et al.38 examined the bonding mechanisms between cement paste and aggregate, attributing strong bonding to mechanical interlocking and chemical bonding. Alsadey and Mohamed39 found that superplasticizers improved fresh concrete bond strength by up to 25%. Berenguer et al.40 studied the bonding properties of cement-based materials with different pozzolanic reactants, revealing that fly ash improved bond strength by up to 20%.

Recent research on additives for preventing gas migration in wells has focused on enhancing cement composition. Kwatia et al.41 explored the use of microsilica, fly ash, nanomaterials, and latex to prevent annular gas migration. Al Ramadan et al.42 investigated the critical length of casing-liner overlap in gas-prone zones through analytical and experimental methods. Wang et al.43 examined the mechanism of gas migration through the microstructure of cemented annuli in deep-water environments. Al Ramadan et al.44 developed numerical models to simulate gas migration through cement sheaths and microannuli. Mohamed et al.24 also conducted experimental studies on well integrity in annular gas migration in cement columns. Yang et al.45 attributed loss in cement volume to cement shrinkage and migration phenomena.

In a recent literature review, Alber and Ehringhausen28 conducted tests on various types of cement to examine their static and dynamic elastic properties and their compressional wave velocities during the curing process. Tang et al.18 employed acoustic variable density logs to assess the quality of cement bonding. Zuo et al.19 utilized an arcuate phased array acoustic receiver station to evaluate the azimuth of the Cement Bond Log (CBL). Das et al.13 employed a depleted reservoir drilled with highly inclined wells to evaluate cement bonding performance. Al Ramis et al.46 demonstrated a novel method using a high-resolution optical device to analyze the behavior of the micro annulus sealing function at the cement sheath-casing interface. Ford et al.11 introduced a Classification Matrix for assessing cement bond leakage rates. Wu et al.14 utilized RFPA2D software to establish the optimal simulation experiment procedure for studying the impact of holes on oil well cement and failure mechanisms. Mozaffari et al.47 proposed a lightweight oil well cement formulation that enhances compressive strength under low-temperature conditions. Wang et al.5 examined the cementation and tensile characteristics of cement, disregarding the influence of underground temperature and pressure on their findings. Lichinga12 conducted an experimental study on modifying water-based filter cake to enhance the bonding strength at the wellbore cement-formation interface. Eventually, Saffari et al.48 developed a rheological model depending on the time, temperature, and pressure of wellbore cement compositions in southern Iran’s exploratory oilfields.

Understanding the relationship between these factors can also help optimize logging and testing procedures to ensure proper and reliable data gathering, which is critical for making informed decisions about well construction and operation.

Oilfield cement additive classification

Oilfield cement additives are substances added to cement slurries during the drilling and production of oil and gas wells. These additives enhance the performance and properties of the cement, ensuring better well integrity and overall efficiency. They can be classified into two main types: powder and liquid additives73,74.

Powder additives

Powder additives are dry materials mixed with cement to achieve specific properties. Some common powder additives include:a. Retarders: These delay the cement’s setting time, allowing more time for placement and pumping. Examples of retarders are calcium lignosulfonate and hydroxyethyl cellulose.b. Accelerators: These speed up the cement’s setting time, which can be helpful in cold weather conditions or when rapid cement hardening is required. Standard accelerators include calcium chloride and calcium formiate.c. Fluidizers: These increase the fluidity and workability of the cement slurry, making it easier to pump and place in the annular space. Examples of fluidizers are bentonite, barite, and silica fume.d. Dispersants: These prevent the separation of cement particles and ensure a homogeneous slurry. Dispersants like sodium naphthalene sulfonate and poly-carboxylate ether are commonly used.e. Set control agents: These help control the cement hardening rate, allowing for better cement placement and more efficient wellbore support. Examples include sodium silicate and sodium aluminate13,34,47,75–78.

Liquid additives

Liquid additives are water-based solutions or emulsions that can be mixed with the cement slurry to achieve specific properties. Some common liquid additives are:a. Water reducers: These increase the fluidity of the cement slurry without affecting its strength. They are often used to reduce the water needed in the slurry, resulting in denser cement. Examples include lignosulfonates, polyacrylamides, and polycarboxylate ethers.b. Viscosifiers: These increase the viscosity of the cement slurry, improving its pumpability and providing better wellbore support. Examples of viscosifiers are hydroxyethyl cellulose and xanthan gum.c. Gas bubble stabilizers: These prevent the formation of gas bubbles in the cement slurry, ensuring better cement integrity. They are instrumental in high-pressure and high-temperature environments. Examples include alkylaryl polyether sulfones.d. Gas bubble destabilizers: These promote the collapse of gas bubbles in the cement slurry, reducing the risk of gas channeling and improving wellbore support. Examples of gas bubble destabilizers include glycol ethers and alkylphenol ethoxylates.e. Foaming agents: These create a foamed cement slurry, which can help fill irregular wellbore shapes and improve cement placement efficiency. Common foaming agents are alkylbenzene sulfonates and fatty acid amides1,13,33,42,72,73,79.

Laboratory simulation of exploratory wells

Experimental setup

The study employed a controlled laboratory setup to simulate the conditions found in exploratory wells. A cylindrical sample chamber with a diameter of 100 mm and a length of 500 mm was designed to mimic the wellbore environment. The chamber was divided into two sections: the upper section represented the casing, and the lower section represented the formation. A temperature control system maintained a constant temperature range of 176°F to 300°F, simulating bottom hole temperature (BHT) conditions found in Iranian wells.

The study used Cement Bond and Variable Density Logs (CBL and VDL) logging tools to evaluate cement bonding quality, commonly used in the oil and gas industry. The CBL/VDL logging tool has a transmitter and two receivers, one 3 feet away from the transmitter (CBL) and one 5 feet away (VDL). The tool was placed in the middle of the well and cannot be pushed into the well in the presence of gas or gas bubbles.

Data collection methods

Data were collected from exploratory wells in Iran using the CBL/VDL logging tool. The data were collected in a standard manner, with the tool placed in the middle of the well and receiver signals analyzed to determine cement bonding quality.

Results

Recent studies have shown that cement bonding quality is critical to wellbore construction. There is a growing need to understand better the relationships between cement properties, well conditions, and cement bonding quality83,84. Our study contributes to this research by investigating the effect of the water-to-cement ratio on cement bonding quality in Class G cement, commonly used in well construction.

Our research has significant implications for the practical application of recent developments in cement bonding quality assessment methods, such as acoustic emission testing and image analysis18,85,86. A deeper understanding of the underlying mechanisms of cement bonding and the influence of various factors on bonding quality is crucial for the effective use of these methods in the field.

Cement quality logs of 21 exploratory wells in different regions of Iran have been used from 2012 to 2022 to carry out this study. The design of cement formulation were implemented according to API RP-10B87, supply of cement and additives, and implementation are carried out mainly by three different contractor companies under the supervision of the National Iranian Oil Company, Exploration Directorate (NIOC-EXP). According to the large number of investigated wells, to discuss the results of cement quality in different phases, based on the type of additives used and their manufacturing companies, the wells have been divided into three groups. Group A includes four wells, where most additives are liquid, and only class G cement is used in the design and execution. Group B includes seven wells combining liquid and powder additives using class A, D, and G cement. Finally, the third group consists of 10 wells that mainly used powder additives and even anti-gas migration, except for a few exceptional cases where liquid gas penetration control was used. The cement used in the third group has also been predominantly class D, with a few cases of class G and E.

Group A wells cement quality

The additives used in this group of exploratory wells are generally liquid, including cement fluid loss controllers, medium and high-temperature retarders, dispersants, anti-gas migration control additives, etc. The only powder additive, including the weighting material (Hi-dense) and compressive strength stabilizer (silica flour) for high pressure-high and temperature conditions (> 230 ºF), has been used as a blend in class G cement. Our results show that the traditional recommendation of a w/c ratio 0.44 for Class G cement may not apply to all situations, mainly when using silica flour and cement system enhancers. We suggest a more flexible approach to w/c ratio selection, considering the cement’s specific density and well construction requirements. Regarding the water-to-cement (w/c) ratio, you are correct that the recommended w/c ratio for Class G cement, according to API RP 10B-2, is 0.44 for 118 pcf. However, we clarify that the w/c ratio mentioned in Table 3 is not a fixed value. Instead, it varies depending on the cement density and the well construction requirements.

For example, we use a w/c ratio of 0.91–0.98 for densities of 92–95 pcf to ensure proper cementation and a w/c ratio of 0.45–0.59 for densities of 115 pcf to accommodate the addition of silica flour and compressive strength enhancers.

Sample bias and size

Based on the results of this study, the sample size of 21 exploratory wells in Iran seems sufficient to draw meaningful conclusions about cement bonding quality. However, the representativeness of the sample is a concern. The wells are not randomly selected and are biased towards certain regions and contractors. The study only includes wells drilled by three contractor companies under the supervision of the National Iranian Oil Company, Exploration Directorate (NIOC-EXP). This limited scope may not be representative of all exploratory wells in Iran.

Additionally, the study only considers cement quality logs from different drilling phases, which may not capture the full range of possible conditions in exploratory wells. The sample may be biased towards wells with relatively well-controlled drilling conditions, which may not represent all exploratory wells.

Future studies could consider including a more diverse range of wells drilled by contractors and in different regions to improve the sample’s representativeness. Additionally, including a more significant number of wells would provide more robust statistical power and enable more generalizable conclusions about cement bonding quality in exploratory wells in Iran.

Regarding the adequacy of the sample size, 21 wells is a reasonable number for a study like this, which is focused on descriptive analysis and providing insights into cement bonding quality. However, a larger sample size would be needed if the study aimed to make causal inferences or estimate population parameters with high precision.

Key results

Impact of hole size

Our analysis indicates that smaller hole sizes tend to enhance cement bonding quality. This is attributed to reduced fluid flow and turbulence, facilitating better cement distribution and adherence to wellbore walls. Specifically, the 5-inch liner phase demonstrated superior bonding qualities compared to larger hole sizes. Prior studies like Kwatia et al.41, Zhang et al.88, and Yang et al.45 support these findings, emphasizing that optimal hole dimensions are critical in minimizing cement losses and improving mechanical interlocking with the formation.

Influence of bottom hole temperature

The research confirmed that variations in BHT significantly affect cement bonding efficiency. An increase in temperature generally correlates to decreased viscosity in the cement slurry, which can either enhance or hinder bonding properties based on the prevailing conditions. High temperatures (exceeding 1.5°F/100ft) necessitate specific formulations, particularly those designed for high-pressure and high-temperature (HPHT) environments. This result corroborates findings by Lian et al.4 and Wang et al.5, highlighting that temperature gradients influence the mechanical integrity of cement in wellbore applications.

Cement composition

The comparative analysis of groups A, B, and C have differentiated by their additive compositions and revealed that cement formulations dominated by liquid additives yielded notably higher bonding quality. Group A, which primarily utilized liquid additives, illustrated the best bonding conditions, with cement bonding quality scores exceeding 75% in several instances. In contrast, the more mineral-centric Group C exhibited variable results, reflecting that reliance on dry or powder additives can lead to inconsistent outcomes. Previous research by Althoey et al.36 and Berenguer et al.40 substantiates the notion that the type of additive significantly influences the mechanical properties of the cement slurry and, consequently, the overall bonding quality.

Implications for exploration practices

The results of this study offer essential insights into optimizing cementing processes in exploratory wells. By considering the effects of hole size, temperature variations, and additive compositions, operator strategies can be refined to improve cement bonding quality, enhancing well integrity and performance. These findings echo prior studies indicating that careful selection of cement additives and formulations can mitigate common issues such as gas migration and cement failure under extreme conditions21,24,39,40,73.

Discussion

Evaluating the crucial factors in assessing cement quality

The study examines the cement slurry composition, temperature gradient, and cement quality results for 21 exploratory wells in Iran between 2012 and 2022. The variables investigated include hole size, bottom hole temperature, and composition, crucial factors in assessing cement quality. The study design involves using cement formulation according to API RP-10B 69 and implementation by three contractor companies under the supervision of the National Iranian Oil Company, Exploration Directorate (NIOC-EXP).

The wells are divided into three groups based on the type of additives used and their manufacturing companies. Group A includes four wells with mainly liquid additives, Group B includes seven wells combining liquid and powder additives, and Group C includes ten wells with mostly powder additives. Effect of the hole and casing size on cement quality

The hole and casing sizes significantly impact the cement bonding quality during wellbore construction. The hole size influences the cement bonding quality by determining the surface area for cement to bond with the formation13,14,89. A larger hole size provides more surface area for cement to penetrate and bond with the rock formation, resulting in better mechanical interlocking. Conversely, a smaller hole size offers less surface area for cement bonding, possibly leading to weaker bonding and potential cement loss. Additionally, a larger hole size can help reduce the hydraulic pressure required for cement placement, thus minimizing the risk of formation damage. The casing size also plays a crucial role in cement bonding quality. Effect of temperature gradient on cement quality

The bottom hole temperature (BHT) and temperature gradient can significantly influence the cement bonding quality during wellbore construction. A lower BHT may reduce cement viscosity, making it easier for the cement slurry to flow and penetrate into the annular space between the casing and the formation15,16,89. It can result in better cement distribution and improved bonding quality. However, lower BHT may also cause the cement to set more slowly, increasing the risk of gas or water invasion into the cement sheath, which could compromise the cement bonding quality. An increase in BHT can cause the cement slurry to become more viscous, making it more challenging for the cement to penetrate and fill the annular space effectively. It may lead to weaker bonding quality and a higher risk of cement loss.

Additionally, higher BHT can accelerate the cement setting time, which may require more time for the cement to fill the annular space and bond with the formation adequately. A steeper temperature gradient can cause thermal stresses on the cement sheath, potentially reducing bonding quality. The temperature difference between the formation and the cement slurry can cause thermal expansion or contraction of the cement, which may result in micro-cracks or reduced mechanical interlocking with the formation. It can compromise the overall cement bonding quality.

Generally, a higher thermal gradient of more than 1.5 ºF/100ft requires high-pressure and high-temperature (HPHT) additives. Otherwise, we will face low-quality cement, such as the 9 5/8" casing of the Sarab well with a gradient of 1.8 ºF/100ft. By observing this principle as a lesson learned in the 7" liner phase of this well, the cement bonding quality has been significantly improved. Also, in deep liners, when the thermal gradient is reduced to below one ºF/100ft (such as the Bisotoon well), the cement quality is reduced on average and more suitable only at the end depths.

Effect of employing powder or liquid additives on cement bonding

In this study, we investigated the effects of various additives on cement bonding quality in 21 exploratory wells in Iran. Our results show that the type and function of additives play a vital role in determining their impact on cement bonding quality. For example, liquid additives such as fluid loss controllers and anti-gas migration control additives can improve cement bonding by reducing circulation loss and preventing gas migration. Powder additives, such as weighting agents and dispersants, can also enhance cement bonding by improving the slurry’s rheological properties and reducing the risk of setting too quickly. These additives are designed as the API, RP 10B-2 recommended practice for testing well cements, to modify the properties of cement, such as setting time, fluid loss control, and rheology, to enhance cement performance in various well construction scenarios. Powder additives, such as retarders, accelerators, and set control agents, are introduced into the cement system to modify its setting time. By adjusting the setting time, these additives can help in achieving better cement bonding quality. For instance, a retarder can delay the initial set of cement, allowing more time for cement to bond with the formation.

Liquid additives, such as water reducers, viscosity modifiers, and fluid loss control agents, alter the rheological properties of cement slurries. These additives can improve the cement bonding quality by enabling better cement placement and distribution around the casing and formation. For example, water reducers help reduce the water content in the cement slurry while maintaining or improving its workability. This results in a denser cement bond with the formation, enhancing the overall bonding quality. Similarly, fluid loss control agents minimize the loss of cement slurry to the formation, ensuring more cement remains in the designed zone for better bonding.

In Group A, liquid additives improved cement bonding quality, particularly in the Sefid-Deng and Dashtestan wells. In contrast, Group B showed that combining liquid and powder additives can lead to good cement bonding quality, as seen in the Khosro and Pazan wells. Group C, which consisted mainly of powder additives, demonstrated a wide range of cement bonding quality outcomes, from weak to excellent.

Our study underscores the crucial role of selecting the right additive for a specific wellbore application to achieve optimal cement bonding quality. This key takeaway is essential for guiding decision-making in the selection of additives for wellbore applications.

Our study’s findings in the explored wells highlight a crucial point-the best quality of cement in the lower depths was related to employing liquid additives with high thermal tolerance, especially in the 5-inch liner. This underscores the importance of not just the additives, but also the wellbore conditions in the selection process. It’s a complex task requiring detailed consideration to achieve optimal cement bonding quality.

Conclusions

This study employs a systematic approach to understand well cementing performance and analyze the influence of borehole diameter and bottom-hole temperature variations on cement bonding efficiency in Iran’s exploratory wells. This investigation aims to provide valuable insights by individually utilizing CBL/VDL interpretation and cement formulation assessment for each wellbore section. By examining the cement quality logs of 21 Iranian exploratory wells in reservoir zones between 2012 and 2022, the outcomes revealed that smaller holes, particularly the 5-inch liner with a thermal gradient exceeding 1.5 ºF/100ft (degrees Fahrenheit per 100 feet), exhibited superior cement bonding quality when using liquid-based additives with high thermal tolerance in successful cementing operations.

Moreover, the investigation demonstrated that the type of additives significantly impacts cement performance. Our results clearly indicate that liquid-based additives equipped for high thermal stability outperform formulations that rely heavily on powder additives. Specifically, cement designed with a majority of liquid additives outperformed those with increased powder components in various operational scenarios.
