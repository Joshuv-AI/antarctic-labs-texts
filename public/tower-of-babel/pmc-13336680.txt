Asymmetric U-shaped association between first-trimester maternal free thyroxine levels and the risk of preeclampsia: a large-scale cohort study
Authors: Sha Chen, Xueya Zhao, Lina Xia, Yu Meng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjective:This study aimed to define the nonlinear relationship between maternal free thyroxine (FT4) levels in early pregnancy and the risk of preeclampsia.Methods:This study used a large-scale retrospective cohort of 46 592 women with singleton pregnancies. Thyroid function parameters and preeclampsia outcomes were collected. A restricted cubic spline model analyzed the nonlinear relationship between FT4 levels and preeclampsia risk. Odds ratios were calculated using multivariable logistic regression, adjusting for confounders. Preeclampsia risk was also assessed based on thyroid disease classifications. Sensitivity analyses validated the robustness of the results.Results:The study found a U-shaped relationship between FT4 levels in early pregnancy and preeclampsia risk. The risk was lowest at 15 pmol/l, with a significant increase as FT4 deviated from this value, especially at lower FT4 levels. A physiological optimal range of 13.9–17.3 pmol/l was identified. Compared to women in this range, low FT4 levels (< 11.7 pmol/l) had a significantly higher risk of preeclampsia (adjusted odds ratio, aOR = 2.23). Even women with FT4 at the lower end of the normal range (11.7–13.9 pmol/l) had increased risk (aOR = 1.26), indicating a latent high-risk group. In traditional thyroid classification, only hypothyroxinemia was a significant independent risk factor. Sensitivity analyses confirmed result stability.Conclusion:This study reveals that low FT4 level is an independent risk factor for preeclampsia, with a dose-dependent effect. These findings support targeted prevention strategies and provide a new approach for precise preeclampsia prevention.Keywords: cohort study, first trimester, free thyroxine, preeclampsia, thyroid-stimulating hormone

INTRODUCTION

Preeclampsia is a major pregnancy-specific cause of maternal and perinatal morbidity and mortality worldwide [1,2]. Although its pathophysiology is not fully understood, it is closely linked to placental dysfunction and maternal endothelial impairment. Pregnancy induces significant physiological changes in maternal thyroid hormone levels to meet metabolic demands, and thyroid hormones play a critical role in placental development, trophoblast invasion, and vascular remodeling [3]. However, evidence on the association between thyroid dysfunction and preeclampsia risk remains limited and inconsistent. Some studies report no significant difference in free thyroxine (FT4) levels between preeclamptic and healthy pregnancies [4], while others find significantly lower FT4 in preeclamptic patients [5,6]. These discrepancies may stem from variations in study design, sample size, and analytical methods. To address these gaps, this study uses a large retrospective cohort to systematically examine the relationship between first-trimester FT4 and thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) levels and preeclampsia risk, aiming to provide new evidence for early prediction and prevention.

METHODS

Study participants

This retrospective cohort study was conducted at a tertiary specialized women's hospital in Shanghai. Data were extracted from the electronic medical record system, which included singleton pregnant women who received systematic prenatal care and delivered at the hospital between January 2013 and December 2016. Eligible participants were selected based on predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria for final analysis. The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of the International Peace Maternity and Child Health Hospital (Approval No. GKLW 2019-16). As the research involved secondary analysis of anonymized routine clinical data, the requirement for informed consent was waived. All procedures adhered to institutional and ethical guidelines. Inclusion criteria required participants to meet all of the following: singleton pregnancy; Chinese nationality; residence in an iodine-sufficient region; completion of thyroid function testing in early pregnancy (11–13 weeks of gestation); delivery at the study hospital with complete delivery records; and postpartum follow-up at the hospital extending beyond 12 weeks. Exclusion criteria included any of the following: preexisting hypertension; history of thyroid disorders prior to pregnancy; use of medications affecting thyroid function before or during pregnancy (e.g., levothyroxine or antithyroid drugs); other major systemic conditions with potential significant impact on thyroid function or blood pressure; multiple gestation; non-Chinese nationality; or delivery at another facility or lack of delivery information.

Data collection and laboratory analysis

Fasting blood samples were obtained from the median cubital vein of all participants during the first trimester (11–13 weeks of gestation). Following collection, samples were centrifuged within 6 h to isolate serum. Quantitative determination of serum TSH, FT4, and thyroid peroxidase antibody (TPOAb) levels was performed on the Architect i2000 immunoassay analyzer (Abbott, Chicago, USA) in strict accordance with the manufacturer's instructions. TPOAb positivity was defined as a concentration ≥5.61 IU/ml. Demographic and clinical baseline information such as maternal age, parity, and educational level was gathered via structured interviews conducted at the initial prenatal visit. Height and weight were measured directly during the visit to derive prepregnancy body mass index (BMI). Neonatal sex was retrieved from delivery documentation. Utilizing the comprehensively assembled dataset from the electronic medical record system, this study was designed to investigate the potential relationship between first-trimester thyroid markers and subsequent development of preeclampsia.

Definition and classification criteria

The following definitions and classification criteria were used in this study based on medical guidelines and the reported literature: normal reference ranges of TSH and FT4: the Endocrine Society and the American Thyroid Association recommend using locally established reference ranges, as general population values are not applicable to pregnant women [7]. In this study, we determined trimester-specific reference ranges during the first trimester (11–13 weeks) by calculating the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles for TSH and FT4. The resulting normal ranges for the first trimester were 0.03–3.64 mIU/l for TSH and 11.7–19.6 pmol/l for FT4. Thyroid function categories: based on the normal reference ranges of TSH and FT4, thyroid function was classified as euthyroid (FT4 11.7–19.6 pmol/l and TSH 0.03–3.64 mIU/l), hyperthyroidism (FT4 > 19.6 pmol/l and/or TSH < 0.03 mIU/l), subclinical hyperthyroidism (FT4 11.7–19.6 pmol/l and TSH < 0.03 mIU/l), hypothyroidism (FT4 < 11.7 pmol/l and TSH > 3.64 mIU/l), subclinical hypothyroidism (FT4 11.7–19.6 pmol/l and TSH > 3.64 mIU/l), and hypothyroxinemia (FT4 < 11.7 pmol/l with TSH 0.03–3.64 mIU/l). Preeclampsia: according to ACOG guidelines [8], preeclampsia is defined as systolic blood pressure ≥140 mmHg or diastolic blood pressure ≥90 mmHg on two occasions at least 4 h apart after 20 weeks of gestation in a previously normotensive woman, along with either 24-h urinary protein excretion ≥300 mg or a protein/creatinine ratio ≥0.3. Obesity definition: body mass index (BMI) was calculated as weight in kilograms divided by height in meters squared (kg/m2). Obesity was defined as a BMI ≥28 kg/m2, according to the criteria of the Working Group on Obesity in China (WGOC) and the International Life Sciences Institute Focal Point in China [9,10].

Statistical analyses

All statistical analyses were conducted using R software (version 4.5.0). Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median [interquartile range, IQR], depending on distribution; categorical variables are expressed as counts or percentages. The relationship between early pregnancy maternal FT4/TSH levels and preeclampsia risk was assessed using threshold effect analysis, followed by restricted cubic splines (RCS) combined with logistic regression to explore nonlinear associations. Multivariable models adjusted for confounders, including maternal age, prepregnancy BMI, education, parity, neonatal sex, in vitro fertilization (IVF), and TPOAb status. Threshold and segmented regression analyses identified FT4 levels linked to the lowest preeclampsia risk and defined clinical cutoffs. Based on these cutoffs, participants were grouped into five categories: absolute low, relatively low within the normal range, absolute normal, relatively high within the normal range, and absolute high. Logistic regression models assessed preeclampsia risk across FT4 groups. The relationship between thyroid function categories (euthyroid, clinical/subclinical hyperthyroidism or hypothyroidism, and hypothyroxinemia) and preeclampsia was also evaluated. Sensitivity analyses excluded specific subgroups (including participants who were obese, elder, multiparous, TPOAb positive, or conceived via IVF) to confirm result robustness. Statistical significance was set at two-sided P < 0.05.

RESULTS

Population characteristics

For this retrospective analysis, a total of 50 895 delivering women were identified from a tertiary specialized hospital database for the period from January 2013 to December 2016. After applying the exclusion criteria, which comprised twin pregnancies (n = 1276), second-trimester stillbirths or miscarriages (n = 68), preexisting thyroid disease (n = 1555), use of thyroid medications (n = 1315), and preexisting hypertension (n = 89), the final study population included 46 592 singleton pregnancies. The flowchart of participant selection is shown in Fig. 1. This final cohort was further categorized into women with preeclampsia (n = 1039) and those without preeclampsia (n = 45 553). The incidence of preeclampsia in the final cohort was 2.2%. The baseline characteristics of the study participants are summarized in Table S1, Supplemental Digital Content. Formal statistical comparisons between the groups were not performed, as any potential confounding was adjusted in subsequent multivariable analyses.

Threshold effect analysis

We initially used two different models to assess the relationship between FT4 levels and the risk of preeclampsia. Model 1 was a standard linear regression model, assuming a continuous linear relationship between FT4 levels and preeclampsia risk. The results showed that FT4 was not significantly associated with preeclampsia risk in Model 1 [odds ratio (OR) 0.97, 95% confidence interval (CI) 0.93–1.01, P = 0.088]. To further explore a potential threshold effect of FT4, we applied a two-piecewise linear regression model (Model 2). Unlike Model 1, Model 2 allows the relationship between FT4 and preeclampsia risk to change at a specific point, known as the inflection point. Using this model, we identified the inflection point at 15.0 pmol/l. When the analysis was restricted to FT4 values below 15.0 pmol/l, each 1 pmol/l increase in FT4 was associated with a 16% reduction in preeclampsia risk (OR = 0.84, 95% CI 0.78–0.91, P < 0.001). This OR reflects only the change in risk within the low FT4 range and should not be interpreted as a comparison between FT4 < 15 pmol/l and FT4 ≥ 15 pmol/l. Within this range, lower FT4 levels correspond to higher preeclampsia risk. Importantly, this result corresponds to the left arm of the U-shaped association observed in the subsequent RCS analysis. Conversely, when the analysis was restricted to FT4 values at or above 15.0 pmol/l, the association became positive (OR = 1.08, 95% CI 1.00–1.15, P = 0.036), indicating that within this higher FT4 range, higher FT4 levels were associated with an increased risk of preeclampsia. This OR also reflects only the change in risk within the higher FT4 range and corresponds to the right arm of the U-shaped association. Furthermore, a likelihood ratio test showed that the two-piecewise regression model fit the data significantly better than the standard linear regression model (P < 0.001). A summary of the threshold effect analysis is provided in Table S2, Supplemental Digital Content. These findings provide the rationale for the subsequent RCS analysis.

Nonlinear associations between free thyroxine/thyroid-stimulating hormone and preeclampsia

Building on the threshold effect analysis, we applied RCS combined with multivariable logistic regression to explore the nonlinear association between maternal FT4/TSH levels and preeclampsia risk. Multivariable models were adjusted for potential confounders, including maternal age, prepregnancy BMI, education, parity, neonatal sex, IVF, and TPOAb status. RCS regression showed a U-shaped nonlinear association between early pregnancy FT4 levels and preeclampsia risk (P for overall < 0.001, P for nonlinearity <0.001), as shown in Fig. 2. The inflection point at 15.0 pmol/l identified in the threshold effect analysis corresponds to the nadir of this U-shaped curve. Consistent with the segmented regression results, the left arm of the curve (FT4 < 15 pmol/l) shows a decreasing risk of preeclampsia with increasing FT4 levels, whereas the right arm (FT4 ≥ 15 pmol/l) shows an increasing risk of preeclampsia with increasing FT4 levels. In contrast, TSH levels did not significantly correlate with preeclampsia risk in the RCS analysis (P for overall = 0.392, P for nonlinearity = 0.268).

Determination of optimal free thyroxine thresholds for the lowest risk of preeclampsia

After identifying a significant nonlinear association between maternal FT4 levels and the risk of preeclampsia, we further examined the FT4 concentration range associated with the lowest risk. Following common practice in noninferiority research and evidence from prior systematic evaluations [11,12], we defined a relative increase in risk of no more than 20% as clinically acceptable. Therefore, an odds ratio of 1.20 was selected as the threshold for clinical relevance. Using the above adjusted segmented regression equation (with an inflection point at 15.0 pmol/l), we calculated the FT4 values corresponding to an odds ratio of 1.20. On the left side of the inflection point (FT4 < 15 pmol/l), corresponding to the left arm of the RCS curve, the risk rises to OR = 1.20 at a lower threshold of 13.9 pmol/l. On the right side (FT4 ≥ 15 pmol/l), corresponding to the right arm of the RCS curve, the risk rises to OR = 1.20 at an upper threshold of 17.3 pmol/l. When FT4 levels were below 13.9 pmol/l or above 17.3 pmol/l, the risk of preeclampsia increased to a clinically meaningful degree. Therefore, the FT4 range of 13.9 to 17.3 pmol/l was defined as the optimal interval associated with the lowest risk of preeclampsia.

Risk of preeclampsia across different maternal free thyroxine levels

The normal range for FT4 in early pregnancy, based on the 2.5th to 97.5th percentiles of our cohort, was 11.7–19.6 pmol/l. To further investigate the association between FT4 levels and the risk of preeclampsia, we combined the population-based reference range (11.7–19.6 pmol/l) with the optimal FT4 interval (13.9–17.3 pmol/l) identified from the segmented regression. Based on these four thresholds, participants were classified into five groups: absolute low FT4 (<11.7 pmol/l), relatively low normal FT4 (11.7–13.9 pmol/l), absolute normal FT4 (13.9–17.3 pmol/l), relatively high normal FT4 (17.3–19.6 pmol/l), and absolute high FT4 (>19.6 pmol/l). Univariate and multivariable logistic regression models were used to assess the association between FT4 categories and the risk of preeclampsia. The multivariable analyses were adjusted for maternal age, body mass index, educational level, parity, in vitro fertilization, neonatal sex, and TPOAb status. The results are shown in Table 1. In the univariate analysis, compared with the absolute normal FT4 group, the risk of preeclampsia was significantly higher in the relatively low normal FT4 group (OR 1.25, 95% CI 1.09–1.43, P = 0.002), the absolute high FT4 group (OR 1.52, 95% CI 1.07–2.16, P = 0.019), and the absolute low FT4 group (OR 2.32, 95% CI 1.72–3.13, P < 0.001). After adjustment for potential confounders, the risk gradient became more pronounced. Women in the absolute low FT4 group (<11.7 pmol/l) had more than a two-fold increase in the risk of preeclampsia (aOR 2.23, 95% CI 1.62–3.09, P < 0.001). Importantly, women in the relatively low normal FT4 group (11.7–13.9 pmol/l), although still within the traditional normal reference range, continued to show a significantly elevated risk (aOR 1.26, 95% CI 1.07–1.48, P = 0.005). In contrast, the increased risk associated with the absolute high FT4 groups was reduced after adjustment and was no longer statistically significant.

Association between thyroid disease categories and the risk of preeclampsia

After establishing a refined risk stratification based on FT4 levels, we further assessed the traditional thyroid disease classification framework commonly used in clinical practice to evaluate its effectiveness in identifying high-risk individuals for preeclampsia. Univariate and multivariate logistic regression models were used to evaluate the association between different thyroid disease categories and the risk of preeclampsia. Participants were classified into six groups: euthyroid, hyperthyroidism, subclinical hyperthyroidism, hypothyroidism, subclinical hypothyroidism, and hypothyroxinemia. The results are shown in Table 2. Among women with normal thyroid function, the preeclampsia incidence was 2.14%. Although univariate analysis suggested a potential link between hyperthyroidism and preeclampsia (OR = 1.59), this was not significant after adjustment (adjusted OR = 1.59, P = 0.155). In contrast, women in the low FT4 group had a significantly higher preeclampsia incidence of 4.82%, with a univariate OR of 2.32 (95% CI: 1.73–3.11, P < 0.001) and an adjusted OR of 2.18 (95% CI: 1.59–2.99, P < 0.001), confirming a strong association between low FT4 and increased preeclampsia risk. Other categories, including clinical and subclinical hyperthyroidism, and clinical and subclinical hypothyroidism, showed no significant association with preeclampsia risk.

Sensitivity analyses

To ensure the reliability of our findings and assess potential influences from study assumptions or population characteristics, we conducted sensitivity analyses by excluding specific subgroups, including obese women, those of advanced maternal age, multiparous, TPOAb positive, or those who conceived via IVF. The results are summarized in Table 3. Sensitivity analyses compared relatively low normal FT4 (11.7–13.9 pmol/l) and absolute low FT4 (<11.7 pmol/l) with the absolute normal group (13.9–17.3pmol/l). The positive associations between both low FT4 groups and preeclampsia remained consistent across all subgroups. Specifically, after excluding women who underwent IVF, were obese (BMI ≥ 28 kg/m2), of advanced maternal age (≥35 years), multiparous, or TPOAb positive, adjusted odds ratios showed minimal change and remained statistically significant (all P < 0.05). These findings confirm that the association between low FT4 and preeclampsia is robust across various sensitivity analyses.

DISSCUSSION

This study identified a nonlinear asymmetric U-shaped relationship between maternal FT4 levels in early pregnancy and preeclampsia risk. Both low and high FT4 levels may increase susceptibility through distinct physiological mechanisms, consistent with the results reported by Li et al.[13]. Based on this pattern, we identified a physiological optimal range that surpasses the conventional statistical reference range. The findings highlight three major insights. First, the study defined the dynamic pattern linking FT4 to preeclampsia risk. On the left arm of the U-shaped curve (FT4 < 15 pmol/l), lower FT4 levels were associated with a higher risk of preeclampsia, whereas on the right arm (FT4 ≥ 15 pmol/l), higher FT4 levels were associated with an elevated risk. This nonmonotonic pattern helps resolve long-standing inconsistencies in this field. Many previous studies analyzed FT4 as a linear variable and therefore failed to detect its bidirectional effects, allowing the high risk associated with low FT4 and the potential risk associated with high FT4 to cancel each other out in linear models, which obscured the true association [5]. Second, the study showed that the distribution of risk was clearly asymmetric, with the risk increasing more sharply at lower FT4 levels than at higher levels, further emphasizing the critical role of low FT4 in preeclampsia risk. While elevated FT4 levels (above 15 pmol/l) were also associated with an increased risk of preeclampsia, the risk increase was less pronounced than for low FT4 levels (below 15 pmol/l). Specifically, a decline in FT4 below the threshold of 15 pmol/l resulted in a more substantial and consistent rise in preeclampsia risk, suggesting that low FT4 is a stronger and more significant predictor of preeclampsia compared to higher FT4 levels. The risk of preeclampsia was lowest within the physiological optimal range and increased as FT4 levels moved away from this range, with the increase concentrated mainly on the low-FT4 side. This directional gradient strongly suggests that the association is not random but is supported by underlying biological mechanisms. Finally, the study confirmed that low FT4 is one of the strongest and most consistent independent risk factors for preeclampsia. Notably, even FT4 levels in the lower part of the traditional normal range were associated with a significantly increased risk. These low-normal women are often overlooked in routine prenatal care and may represent a large latent high-risk group. Taken together, the study underscores the importance of individualized assessment and management of thyroid function in early pregnancy. Maintaining FT4 within an appropriate physiological range may be critical for preventing preeclampsia. Future multicenter studies with large sample sizes are needed to validate these findings and to determine an optimal reference range for FT4 during pregnancy to guide clinical practice.

The underlying mechanisms reflect the critical role of thyroid hormones in vascular endothelial function, placental perfusion, and placental development [14,15]. Low FT4 primarily drives risk through placental hypoperfusion and metabolic disturbances. Inadequate FT4 reduces nitric oxide (NO) bioavailability, impairing vasodilation and placental blood flow, leading to ischemia and hypoxia [15,16]. Simultaneously, low FT4 exacerbates oxidative stress and promotes the release of inflammatory cytokines, causing endothelial injury [16,17]. Additionally, low FT4 induces lipid metabolic disturbances, with elevated total cholesterol, LDL, and apoB, creating an atherogenic lipid profile that restricts fetal oxygen and nutrient supply, creating a vicious cycle [18]. Conversely, some researchers suggest that excess FT4 increases basal metabolism, promotes protein and lipid breakdown, depletes maternal energy reserves, and causes oxidative damage. It can also affect placental development by altering trophoblast function. Additionally, excessive thyroid hormones can elevate heart rate, cardiac output, and vascular resistance, leading to gestational hypertension [19,20]. Medici et al. also support these theories, suggesting that high FT4 levels during early pregnancy are linked to an increased risk of hypertensive disorders [21]. However, In our categorical analysis after multivariable adjustment, we did not find a significant association between high FT4 levels and an increased risk of preeclampsia. Possible reasons for this discrepancy include differences in study design, adjusted factors, sample size, and gestational age at thyroid function testing, all of which may have influenced the observed results. Our findings are consistent with those of Petca et al., who suggest that hypothyroidism, rather than hyperthyroidism, is the primary thyroid disorder associated with the development of preeclampsia [22]. In summary, low and high FT4 act through different pathological mechanisms in the development of preeclampsia. These dual mechanisms explain the U-shaped risk curve observed in this study and emphasize the importance of maintaining FT4 within a precise physiological window for preeclampsia prevention.

In this study, traditional thyroid disease classification analysis revealed a key finding: only hypothyroxinemia was independently associated with the risk of preeclampsia. This result has profound implications. Traditional classification systems are primarily based on the feedback regulation of the hypothalamic-pituitary-thyroid axis and rely on TSH levels for diagnosis. However, our findings suggest that FT4 has a more direct and significant relationship with preeclampsia risk than TSH, as FT4 is the active hormone that directly acts on peripheral tissues. Therefore, hypothyroxinemia may be considered an independent risk phenotype closely related to placental dysfunction. A large cohort study showed that early pregnancy FT4 levels are closely associated with placental vascular resistance, while the effects of TSH are more transient and limited [23]. Velasco et al. found that maternal FT4 influences cord FT4 levels, thereby regulating fetal growth, while maternal and cord TSH showed no significant correlation with birth weight [24]. A meta-analysis by Fitzgerald et al. revealed that FT4 had a stronger association with clinical outcomes, with about 50% of analyses showing significant associations, compared to only about 23% for TSH [25]. Furthermore, studies have shown that in intrauterine growth restriction (IUGR) fetuses, FT4 levels are significantly lower, while TSH levels show no significant changes. The increased expression of TRα and TRβ proteins in IUGR placentas further suggests that changes in FT4 levels more directly reflect placental function [26]. These findings highlight the importance of shifting focus from TSH-based disease diagnosis to directly assessing FT4 levels when evaluating preeclampsia risk.

The strengths and limitations

This study has several key strengths. First, the large-scale retrospective cohort design, with thyroid function tests conducted prior to preeclampsia onset, minimizes reverse causality bias. The dose-response relationship between FT4 levels and preeclampsia risk aligns with Hill's causal inference criteria – temporality, dose-response, and consistency [27], providing strong evidence for FT4's role in preeclampsia. Second, population-specific reference ranges were calculated, enhancing internal validity and clinical relevance [28]. Third, unlike previous studies using linear models, we employed restricted cubic spline models, revealing a nonmonotonic U-shaped relationship and offering a clinically meaningful “physiological optimal range.” Fourth, rigorous adjustment for confounders ensured reliable association estimates. Sensitivity and subgroup analyses confirmed the robustness of the low FT4-related risk. Importantly, the study identified a latent high-risk group within the “normal-low range” of FT4, which has practical implications for prenatal screening and risk management. However, the study has limitations. First, while known confounders were adjusted for, residual confounding may still exist. Second, data from a single center limits generalizability, and further validation across diverse populations is needed. Additionally, the low prevalence of hypothyroidism in pregnancy led to a small sample size, potentially causing a Type II error. Lastly, while individual iodine data were unavailable, Shanghai's universal salt iodization program likely mitigated iodine deficiency's impact on thyroid function [29].
