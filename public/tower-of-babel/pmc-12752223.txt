Exploring the predictors of postoperative complications in hand surgery: a cross-sectional analysis
Authors: Yuanyuan Zeng, Qian Xie, Jian Xie, Weifeng Shangguan, Guoping Zou
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPostoperative complications in hand surgery can be influenced by factors such as nutritional and metabolic factors. Understanding these associations is critical for improving surgical outcomes. The aim of this study was to investigate the impact of glycemic, renal, and thyroid markers, smoking, anthropometric measures, vitamin D, and albumin levels on postoperative complications in patients undergoing hand surgeries.MethodsThis cross-sectional study included patients who underwent hand surgery from 2018 to 2023. Demographic data, preoperative fasting blood sugar (FBS), glycated hemoglobin (A1C), thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH), blood urea nitrogen (BUN)/Creatinine ratio, smoking status, body mass index (BMI), vitamin D and albumin levels, and postoperative complications were recorded. Multivariate logistic regression was used to identify predictors of complications.ResultsMultivariate analysis on 690 participants identified age (OR = 1.02), vitamin D sufficiency (OR = 0.57), smoking (OR = 1.81), albumin levels (OR= 0.35), FBS (OR = 1.03), and A1C (OR = 1.08) as significant predictors of complications. No significant association was observed between gender, BMI, BUN/Cr ratio, TSH levels, and complications.ConclusionAge, vitamin D sufficiency, smoking status, glycemic control, and albumin levels significantly influence postoperative complication risks. Optimizing these factors preoperatively may improve outcomes. Future studies should confirm these findings and explore targeted interventions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12893-025-03361-2.

Background

Hand surgery is a vital field within orthopedic and reconstructive medicine, addressing a wide variety of conditions affecting the hand, wrist, and upper extremities. From trauma-induced injuries to congenital deformities and degenerative diseases, hand surgery aims to restore function, relieve pain, and enhance the quality of life for patients [1]. However, despite the remarkable advancements in surgical techniques and post-operative care, the risk of acute complications remains a significant concern. These complications, which include delayed wound healing, infections, hematoma formation, nerve injuries, and neuropathic pain, can severely hinder the recovery process, leading to prolonged rehabilitation and diminished functional outcomes [2, 3]. Understanding the underlying predictors of these complications is essential for improving patient outcomes and optimizing post-surgical recovery.

The occurrence of postoperative complications in hand surgery is influenced by various factors, including patient demographics, comorbid conditions, inflammatory responses, and metabolic profiles [2–4]. For instance, age has been recognized as a key determinant, with older patients typically exhibiting slower recovery rates and higher complication risks due to decreased tissue regeneration [5]. Similarly, pre-existing conditions, such as diabetes, obesity, and nutritional deficiencies, have been shown to impair wound healing, increase susceptibility to infections, and disrupt the ability of immune system to combat postoperative complications [6–8]. In addition, vitamin D deficiency has emerged as a critical factor influencing surgical outcomes, as it plays an essential role in immune modulation, bone health, and tissue repair. Deficient levels of vitamin D have been linked to slower wound healing, increased infection rates, and a greater incidence of complications following surgery [9, 10]. Furthermore, smoking continues to be one of the most important modifiable risk factors for postoperative complications. It has been shown to impair circulation, reduce tissue oxygenation, and compromise immune function, all of which contribute to delayed wound healing, higher infection rates, and an overall increased risk of postoperative complications [11–13].

Studies examining the risk factors determining postoperative complications in hand surgery are limited, and each has only investigated a few of the potential risk factors [2, 3, 12]. This study aims to explore the relationship between various demographic, nutritional, and metabolic factors and the incidence of acute postoperative complications in patients undergoing hand surgery. By identifying the key predictors of complications, this research will provide valuable insights into improving the management of hand surgery patients, optimizing surgical techniques, and developing targeted interventions to reduce the risk of adverse outcomes.

Methods

Study design and participants

This retrospective cross-sectional study was conducted at the Longyan First Affiliated Hospital to explore the predictors of acute postoperative complications in patients undergoing hand surgery, including soft tissue surgeries, bone/joint surgeries, and deformity correction surgeries. The study period ranged from January 2018 to December 2023.

The study included adult patients (≥ 18 years) who underwent elective or emergent hand surgeries, with a follow-up period of at least three months. Exclusion criteria included patients under 18 years of age, individuals receiving multiple concurrent surgical procedures, those undergoing surgeries above the elbow, patients lacking postoperative follow-up at our institution, those with systemic infections or active inflammatory conditions, individuals with malignancies, patients with severe renal or hepatic impairment, and those who did not complete the required 3-month follow-up period.

Sample size

Based on the sample size calculation formula for studies with binary outcomes [14] and assuming a positive association between obesity and the likelihood of postoperative complications in hand surgery, with expected odds ratio (OR) of 1.41, and an estimated 0.55 exposure rate to obesity in the control group as reported in a study by Bryant et al. [3], the minimum required sample size for the case group will be 687 individuals. This is based on a 95% confidence interval, 80% power, α = 0.05, β = 0.1, and r = 0.2. We recruited an additional 80 participants to account for potential exclusions due to patients meeting the exclusion criteria or having incomplete medical records, ensuring that we reached a final sample size of 687 participants. Ultimately, 690 participants were included in the statistical analysis.

Data collection

Biochemical data were obtained from routine preoperative assessments recorded in medical records, including serum albumin, fasting blood sugar (FBS), glycated hemoglobin (A1c), thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH), and blood urea nitrogen (BUN)/Creatinine ratio. All tests were performed within seven days before surgery under standardized fasting conditions as part of the institutional preoperative protocol. Serum vitamin D levels, categorized as deficient (< 20 ng/mL), insufficient (20–30 ng/mL), or sufficient (>30 ng/mL), were measured using liquid chromatography with mass spectrometry (LC/MS) in the laboratory center of Longyan First Affiliated Hospital. Delayed wound healing was defined as healing requiring more than 7 days. While there is no universally accepted cutoff for delayed wound healing after hand surgery, the seven-day threshold was chosen based on typical clinical expectations for uncomplicated recovery in minor hand procedures, as reported in previous surgical literature [15]. Infection was identified by erythema, swelling, or purulent discharge at the surgical site requiring antibiotics or additional surgery; hematoma formation referred to a localized blood collection visible clinically or via imaging; ischemia was indicated by signs of compromised blood flow necessitating intervention; nerve injury was defined as sensory or motor dysfunction confirmed by clinical examination; neuropathic pain was pain persisting beyond the expected recovery period; impaired mobility referred to reduced range of motion affecting daily activities; thromboembolic events included deep vein thrombosis or pulmonary embolism confirmed by imaging; scar hypertrophy was defined as abnormal scar thickening; tendon rupture involved partial or complete failure of repaired tendons; joint stiffness was persistent restriction of joint motion; complex regional pain syndrome (CRPS) was defined based on Budapest criteria [16]; hardware failure referred to mechanical dysfunction of implants like plates or screws; synovial fistula was persistent drainage from a joint; scar tenderness was defined as pain upon palpation of the scar; and transient or excessive wound edema was persistent swelling beyond the normal postoperative course. All postoperative complications including infections were confirmed by the treating physician using objective clinical criteria (erythema, swelling, purulent discharge, or need for antibiotics/surgical intervention) during follow-up visits, minimizing reliance on patient self-report.

To standardize the assessment of severity, all complications were graded according to the Clavien–Dindo classification system [17]. In this system, Grade I represents deviations from the normal postoperative course not requiring pharmacological, surgical, or radiological intervention. Grade II includes complications requiring pharmacological treatment, Grade III represents complications requiring surgical, endoscopic, or radiological intervention (subdivided into IIIa without and IIIb with general anesthesia), Grade IV refers to life-threatening complications requiring ICU management, and Grade V indicates death.

Surgeon and procedure characteristics

The operating seniority of surgeon (consultant/attending vs. resident under supervision) and exact procedure type were recorded. Procedures were later grouped for sensitivity analyses into soft-tissue versus bone/joint/deformity categories. Rare procedures (< 1% of total cases) were identified for exclusion in secondary analyses.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using STATA version 15 (StataCorp, College Station, TX). Descriptive statistics were presented as means ± standard deviations (SD) for normally distributed continuous variables and medians (interquartile ranges, IQR) for non-normally distributed continuous variables. Categorical variables were reported as frequencies (percentages). For non-normally distributed continuous variables, a logarithmic transformation was applied before entering the data into the logistic regression analysis. A multivariate logistic regression was performed to identify independent predictors of postoperative complications. Variables considered as potential predictors included age, gender, BMI, vitamin D status, smoking status, albumin levels, FBS, A1c, BUN/creatinine ratio, and TSH levels. Variables were selected based on a backward elimination procedure with a p-value threshold for entry set at 0.1 to select the most significant predictors for the final model. To account for potential intra-surgeon correlation, logistic regression models were run with cluster-robust standard errors at the surgeon level. Sensitivity analyses were performed by (i) collapsing procedures into soft-tissue versus bone/joint/deformity categories, (ii) excluding rare procedures (< 1% of cases), and (iii) surgery urgency (emergency vs. elective). Odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated to assess the strength and precision of the associations between the predictors and postoperative complications. To assess the goodness-of-fit of the logistic regression model, the Hosmer and Lemeshow test was performed. A p-value greater than 0.05 from this test indicates a good fit of the model to the data. Two outcomes were considered including the occurrence of any complication (Clavien–Dindo Grade I or higher) and the occurrence of major complications (Clavien–Dindo Grade III or higher). A significance level of p < 0.05 was considered for all statistical tests.

Results

Characteristics of the study population

Table 1 outlined the baseline characteristics of study population. The study cohort (n = 690) had a mean age of 49.8 ± 13.5 years, with 68.7% male participants. The mean weight and BMI reflected a range typical of overweight individuals. Vitamin D deficiency was prevalent, with nearly half of the participants categorized as deficient (serum levels < 20 ng/mL). The mean serum BUN/Creatinine ratio indicated generally normal renal function. Glycemic markers suggested that many participants were likely within a pre-diabetic or diabetic range. Thyroid function, indicated by TSH levels, was within the typical range. The majority of the participants were non-smokers, and the surgical procedures were diverse, encompassing interventions like carpal tunnel release, trigger finger surgery, and fracture fixation. A total of 12 surgeons performed the procedures included in this study, with a median case volume of 45 cases per surgeon. Regarding case urgency, 605 procedures were elective, while 85 procedures were performed on an emergency basis.

Predictors of postoperative complications

Multivariable logistic regression with cluster-robust standard errors at the surgeon level followed by a backward elimination method identified several predictors of all postoperative complications (Clavien–Dindo ≥ I) (Tables 3 and 4). The results of the adjusted logistic regression model revealed that increasing age (with a coefficient of 0.02 and p-value = 0.005) was significantly associated with a higher likelihood of postoperative complications. Additionally, vitamin D status (sufficient vs. deficient) showed a protective effect, with a 43% reduction in the likelihood of complications (OR = 0.57, p-value = 0.002). Smoking was associated with an increased likelihood of complications (OR = 1.81, p-value = 0.011). Albumin levels (with a negative coefficient of −1.04 and p-value < 0.001) were significantly associated with a reduced risk of complications. Furthermore, FBS levels (with a coefficient of 0.03 and p-value < 0.001) and A1C levels (with a coefficient of 0.07 and p-value = 0.001) were negatively associated with postoperative recovery, as increases in these variables were linked to higher odds of complications. No significant association was found between the BUN/Cr ratio and TSH levels and postoperative complications (p-value > 0.05). These findings suggest that factors including age, vitamin D status, smoking, albumin levels, FBS, and A1C play a significant role in predicting the occurrence of postoperative complications.

Sensitivity analysis

Results remained consistent after collapsing procedures into soft-tissue versus bone/joint/deformity, excluding rare procedures (< 1% of cases), and adjusting for emergency vs. elective. Effect estimates for key predictors (age, vitamin D, smoking, albumin, FBS, and A1C) were largely unchanged (Supplementary Table S1), indicating that the associations were robust to variability in procedure type and surgeon experience.

Discussion

This study aimed to explore the predictors of acute postoperative complications in patients undergoing hand surgery, with a particular focus on nutritional and metabolic factors. The results of our analysis highlight the significant roles of age, smoking status, serum albumin levels, vitamin D status, and metabolic parameters such as FBS and A1C levels in predicting postoperative complications. However, BMI, BUN/creatinine ratio, TSH levels, and gender did not exhibit significant associations with postoperative complications. Our findings underscore the importance of optimizing modifiable risk factors, including glycemic control, nutritional and vitamin D status, and smoking cessation, as part of preoperative preparation for hand surgery. Routine preoperative screening and correction of these abnormalities may help reduce postoperative complications and improve functional outcomes. Although only preoperative values were available, HbA1c reflects long-term glycemic control over preceding months, and serum vitamin D and albumin levels represent relatively stable markers of metabolic and nutritional status. Hence, these parameters serve as meaningful proxies of chronic risk exposure.

In our study, a positive correlation between age and the likelihood of complications was observed. This finding is consistent with a large body of literature that demonstrates an increased incidence of postoperative complications in older patients, particularly those undergoing elective surgeries like hand surgery [18]. The aging process involves several physiological changes, including reduced tissue regeneration capacity, slower immune responses, and a decline in overall metabolic function, all of which can hinder recovery after surgery [19]. In hand surgery, these age-related changes may contribute to delayed wound healing, a higher risk of infection, and an increased likelihood of joint stiffness and other musculoskeletal complications [20, 21]. Moreover, older patients are more likely to have comorbid conditions such as diabetes, cardiovascular disease, and osteoporosis [22], which further increase their risk for postoperative complications. Therefore, age should be considered an important factor in preoperative assessments and may warrant more careful perioperative management in older patients.

Smoking emerged as a significant risk factor for postoperative complications, with smokers exhibiting a higher likelihood of complications compared to non-smokers. This result is consistent with previous studies that have demonstrated a clear association between smoking and poor surgical outcomes [3, 12]. Smoking impairs the ability of body to heal wounds by reducing blood flow, decreasing oxygen delivery to tissues, and suppressing immune function [23]. Nicotine constricts blood vessels, limiting the supply of nutrients and oxygen required for tissue repair [24]. Furthermore, the toxins in cigarette smoke increase inflammation, which can impair the normal healing process after surgery [25]. In the context of hand surgery, smoking can contribute to poor wound healing, infection, and other complications such as tendon rupture or scar formation [26, 27]. Given these well-established risks, smoking cessation should be strongly encouraged in patients undergoing hand surgery.

Serum albumin levels were found to be a significant predictor of postoperative complications, with lower albumin levels being associated with an increased risk of adverse outcomes. Albumin, a major plasma protein, is crucial for maintaining osmotic pressure, supporting cellular repair processes, and modulating inflammatory responses [28, 29]. Low albumin levels are commonly associated with malnutrition, chronic disease, and inflammation, all of which can impede wound healing and increase the susceptibility to infections following surgery [30]. Patients with low albumin levels may also be at higher risk for complications such as hematoma formation [31]. Previous studies have shown that hypoalbuminemia is a strong predictor of poor postoperative outcomes, including increased rates of infection, delayed wound healing, and longer hospital stays [32, 33]. Therefore, assessing albumin levels preoperatively could help identify patients at higher risk for complications, allowing for more targeted nutritional and medical interventions.

In contrast to Lee et al., who found no association between baseline vitamin D levels and complications [34], our findings suggest that sufficient vitamin D levels are associated with a reduced risk of postoperative complications. Lee et al. observed that while baseline vitamin D levels did not influence grip strength recovery in injured hands, they were significantly correlated with grip strength in uninjured hands, highlighting role of vitamin D in overall musculoskeletal health rather than recovery alone [34]. Additionally, vitamin D supplementation, younger age, and greater wrist range of motion were independent predictors of better grip strength recovery [34]. This difference might arise from variations in study design, populations, or types of injuries examined. For example, Lee et al. focused on distal radius fractures and grip strength [34], whereas our study emphasized a wide range of postoperative outcomes, which might be more sensitive to vitamin D status. Similarly, Lim et al. found that vitamin D was associated with reduced rate of complications following a hip fracture surgery [35]. Vitamin D plays a crucial role in modulating immune and inflammatory responses, potentially reducing postoperative complications by limiting excessive inflammatory activity. It enhances innate immunity while downregulating pro-inflammatory cytokines, which are implicated in poor healing and increased susceptibility to infections [36–39]. Additionally, vitamin D supports bone metabolism and tissue repair by promoting calcium homeostasis and osteoblast activity.

Metabolic parameters such as FBS and A1C were significantly associated with postoperative complications. Both FBS and A1C levels were found to increase the risk of postoperative complications, which is consistent with the growing body of evidence linking poor glycemic control with adverse surgical outcomes, particularly in hand surgery [3, 40, 41]. Elevated blood sugar levels can impair immune function, delay wound healing, and increase the risk of infection and inflammation [42]. In patients undergoing hand surgery, hyperglycemia may also contribute to complications such as tendon rupture, delayed bone healing, and increased scarring [43, 44]. Therefore, optimizing blood sugar control prior to surgery is essential in reducing the risk of postoperative complications.

Our findings did not reveal a significant association between BMI and postoperative complications, which aligns with the observation by London et al. that the overall complication rates were similar between obese and non-obese patients (8.5% vs. 9.0%) [2]. However, their study identified bony procedures as having the greatest risk of complications in both groups (15%). Importantly, London et al. noted a dose-dependent effect of BMI in obese patients, with increasing BMI heightening the risk of complications, particularly in those with a BMI >45 [9]. This discrepancy might suggest that while BMI may not universally predict complications, its influence could be more pronounced in specific surgical procedures, such as bony surgeries. The absence of a significant association in our study may reflect differences in surgical types or BMI distributions across the cohorts. Bryant et al. demonstrated that the combination of diabetes, tobacco use, and obesity substantially increased the risk of complications. Their study reported that the diabetic-smoker-obese group had the highest predicted rate of complications (42.02%), followed by diabetic-smoker (38.69%) and diabetic-obese (11.37%) groups. Notably, obesity alone (11.37%) and the healthy control group (10.05%) had the lowest predicted complication rates [3]. This highlights the importance of overlapping comorbidities, such as diabetes and smoking, as independent predictors of complications, with obesity acting as an additive risk factor.

Although kidney function (as indicated by the BUN/creatinine ratio) and thyroid function (TSH levels) are known to affect healing and immune responses [45], these factors did not emerge as significant predictors in our study. This might be due to that our sample did not include patients with severe renal or thyroid dysfunction, or because the impact of these factors on acute postoperative complications is less pronounced compared to other nutritional and metabolic factors. Gender, despite being a known factor in some postoperative complications, also did not show a significant relationship with complications in this study. This may be due to the relatively balanced gender distribution in our sample or the fact that gender-related differences in surgical outcomes are more pronounced in certain types of surgeries or complications than in others.

While this study provides valuable insights into the risk factors associated with postoperative complications, there are some limitations to consider. First, the study design is observational, which limits the ability to draw causal inferences. Second, the cohort consisted primarily of adult patients undergoing hand and orthopedic surgeries at a single center, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to other surgical populations or settings. Further research with diverse surgical populations and randomized controlled trials to evaluate the effectiveness of interventions targeting these risk factors is needed. Third, factors such as postoperative care, adherence to rehabilitation protocols, and other psychosocial factors were not included in this analysis but may significantly influence surgical outcomes. Incorporating these variables in future studies could provide a more comprehensive understanding of the factors contributing to postoperative complications. Fourth, complications were not stratified by timing, and early and late postoperative events may be influenced differently by clinical factors. While the Clavien–Dindo classification standardizes severity assessment, future studies should perform time-to-event analyses to evaluate whether specific risk factors have differential effects on early versus late complications. Fifth, as this was a retrospective study, variability in sample timing and the absence of postoperative biomarker measurements could not be fully controlled. However, all preoperative tests were performed under fasting conditions within one week before surgery, minimizing diurnal bias.

A key strength of this study is the comprehensive evaluation of a broad range of patient-related and metabolic factors, including vitamin D status, serum albumin, fasting blood glucose, HbA1c, and smoking, in a relatively large cohort of patients undergoing hand surgery. Another strength is the use of the Clavien–Dindo classification to standardize the assessment and grading of postoperative complications, which allows for objective evaluation of complication severity. Additionally, the study distinguishes between any postoperative complication and major complications requiring surgical or ICU intervention, providing clinically meaningful risk stratification. By integrating metabolic, biochemical, and lifestyle predictors with standardized complication severity, the study extends current knowledge and offers a more precise framework for identifying patients at higher risk of adverse outcomes following hand surgery.

Conclusion

In this study, several factors were independently associated with acute postoperative complications in hand surgery, including increasing age, smoking status, low albumin levels, vitamin D deficiency, and elevated FBS and A1C levels. When the outcome was restricted to major complications (Clavien–Dindo ≥ III), the same predictors remained significant, with smoking and low albumin showing particularly strong associations with severe complications. These findings suggest that preoperative assessment of nutritional and metabolic status may help identify patients at higher risk of complications, although causal effects cannot be established from this observational study. Given the observational design, causal relationships cannot be established. Prospective studies are warranted to evaluate whether targeted correction of these modifiable factors can reduce postoperative complications and improve patient outcomes.
