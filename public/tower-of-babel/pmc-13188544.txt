Impact of oral frailty on postoperative delirium in older patients undergoing off-pump coronary artery bypass grafting: a prospective observational study
Authors: Shizhao Wang, Peiying Huang, Fuzhen Zhang, Sufang Jiang, Lichao Di, Sichen Cui, Rongtian Kang, Lining Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPostoperative delirium (POD) is a frequent and serious complication after cardiac surgery. Although oral frailty has been associated with cognitive decline and systemic inflammation, its role in POD after cardiac surgery remains unclear. This study aimed to investigate the association between preoperative oral frailty and POD in older patients undergoing off-pump coronary artery bypass grafting (OPCABG).MethodsIn this prospective observational study, 289 consecutive patients aged ≥ 60 years who underwent OPCABG between July 2024 and October 2025 were enrolled. Preoperative oral function was assessed using the Oral Frailty Index-8 (OFI-8), with oral frailty defined as a score ≥ 4. POD was evaluated on postoperative days 1–7 using the Confusion Assessment Method (CAM) and CAM-ICU. Multivariable logistic regression was performed to identify independent predictors. The discriminative performance of the multivariable model incorporating oral frailty was assessed using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis, and mediation analysis was used to explore the indirect role of preoperative cognitive function.ResultsPOD occurred in 116 patients (40.1%). After adjustment for confounders, advanced age ( OR 1.075, 95% CI 1.014–1.739, p = 0.015), oral frailty (OR 2.103, 95% CI 1.204–3.673, p = 0.009), diabetes mellitus (OR 1.797, 95% CI 1.052–3.017, p = 0.032), and preoperative MoCA score (OR 0.931, 95% CI 0.867–0.999, p = 0.041) were independently associated with POD. The multivariable model incorporating oral frailty demonstrated moderate discrimination (AUC 0.711, 95% CI 0.650–0.771) and good calibration (Hosmer–Lemeshow p = 0.843), with a sensitivity of 75.9% and specificity of 58.4%. Mediation analysis indicated that preoperative cognitive function accounted for only 1% of the indirect effect of oral frailty on POD ( p = 0.176).ConclusionsPreoperative oral frailty is independently associated with POD in older patients undergoing OPCABG. Although its discriminative ability is moderate, oral frailty screening may serve as a feasible and cost-effective adjunctive tool for perioperative risk stratification and targeted delirium-prevention strategies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12871-026-03811-1.Keywords: Oral frailty, Off-pump coronary artery bypass grafting, Postoperative delirium

Introduction

Postoperative delirium (POD) is a common form of postoperative cognitive dysfunction, characterized by an acute onset and fluctuating symptoms, primarily manifesting as disturbances in attention, cognition, and consciousness. POD is strongly associated with poor postoperative recovery, prolonged hospital stays, an increased risk of dementia, and higher mortality rates [1, 2]. Consequently, the identification of patients at high risk for POD is essential for its prevention, management, and ultimately for improving postoperative outcomes.

Coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG) is a widely performed cardiac surgery for the treatment of severe coronary artery disease and myocardial ischemia. Although advancements in medical technology have significantly improved the safety of CABG, the incidence of major complications remains substantial, with a reported complication rate of 41% [3]. Frailty, defined as a state of reduced physiological reserve and diminished resistance to stressors, is prevalent in 10% to 60% of patients with cardiovascular disease, particularly among older adults [4, 5]. Notably, frailty in CABG patients is significantly associated with poorer preoperative functional independence, cognitive function, and depressive symptoms, as well as an increased incidence of postoperative complications [6–8]. Studies have shown that frail patients undergoing cardiac surgery have a significantly higher incidence of POD compared to non-frail patients [9].

In recent years, oral health and function in older adults have been recognized as important predictors of frailty and loss of independence [10, 11]. The Japanese Association of Oral Health introduced the concept of “oral frailty” to describe age-related changes in oral health, such as tooth loss, deteriorating oral hygiene, and declining oral function, accompanied by diminished attention to oral health and a reduction in physical and mental reserves [12]. Oral frailty has been closely linked to cognitive dysfunction [13, 14] and may be associated with brain dysfunction through pathways involving chronic inflammation, malnutrition, and neurodegeneration. Previous studies have also indicated an association between oral frailty and POD in elderly patients undergoing non-cardiac surgery [15].

However, research examining the relationship between oral frailty and POD after cardiac surgery remains limited, particularly in patients undergoing off-pump coronary artery bypass grafting (OPCABG), with evidence still insufficient. This study aims to investigate the association between oral frailty and POD in elderly patients undergoing OPCABG, with the goal of providing new theoretical insights for perioperative risk stratification and targeted intervention strategies.

Methods

Study design and ethics

This prospective observational cohort study was conducted at the Second Hospital of Hebei Medical University (Shijiazhuang, China) between July 2024 and October 2025. The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee of the Second Hospital of Hebei Medical University (approval number: 2024-R522) and prospectively registered in the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (ChiCTR2400091280). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants or their legal representatives. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and reported following the STROBE guidelines.

Participants

Patients aged ≥ 60 years scheduled to undergo off-pump coronary artery bypass grafting (OPCABG) were consecutively screened.

Exclusion criteria were: (1) Preoperative diagnosis of dementia, Parkinson’s disease, or major psychiatric disorders. (2) Refusal to participate or withdrawal of consent. (3) Missing preoperative cognitive assessment or incomplete postoperative delirium evaluation. (4) Intraoperative conversion from OPCABG to conventional on-pump CABG. (5) Incomplete clinical data.

Eligible patients were enrolled consecutively, and perioperative data were collected prospectively.

Anesthetic and perioperative management

All procedures were performed under combined intravenous and inhalational anesthesia. Sufentanil was used for analgesia, propofol and sevoflurane for maintenance of anesthesia, and rocuronium for neuromuscular blockade. Depth of anesthesia was monitored using bispectral index (BIS), maintained between 40 and 60. Normothermia was preserved using active warming measures. Anticholinergic medications were avoided or used conservatively. At the end of surgery, patients remained intubated and were transferred to the cardiac intensive care unit (ICU) for standardized postoperative management.

Assessment of oral frailty

Preoperative oral function was assessed using the Oral Frailty Index-8 (OFI-8) [16]. Assessments were conducted by trained and calibrated examiners who underwent standardized training before study initiation. Calibration procedures were performed to ensure inter-examiner reliability. The OFI-8 consists of eight items evaluating chewing ability, swallowing difficulty, denture use, oral dryness, social participation, ability to eat hard foods, tooth-brushing frequency, and dental clinic visits. Scoring followed the standard protocol described in the original validation study. The complete questionnaire and scoring method are provided in Supplementary Material 1. Consistent with the original validation study and subsequent epidemiological investigations, an OFI-8 score ≥ 4 was used to define oral frailty [17]. Although partial conceptual overlap exists between oral frailty and broader constructs such as malnutrition or general frailty, the OFI-8 specifically captures oral functional domains that are not routinely assessed in conventional frailty instruments.

Assessment of postoperative delirium and sedation management

Postoperative delirium was assessed using the Confusion Assessment Method for the Intensive Care Unit (CAM-ICU) and the standard Confusion Assessment Method (CAM) after extubation or transfer to the ward [18]. Level of consciousness was evaluated using the Richmond Agitation–Sedation Scale (RASS) [19]. Patients with a RASS score of − 4 or − 5 were considered comatose and were not assessed for delirium. Patients with RASS scores between − 3 and + 4 were eligible for CAM-ICU evaluation. Sedation in the ICU followed a standardized institutional protocol consisting of propofol in combination with dexmedetomidine, which was applied uniformly to all patients, with a target RASS score between − 2 and 0 unless deeper sedation was clinically indicated. RASS scores were recorded at least every 4 h.

All assessors received standardized training before study initiation and were blinded to preoperative oral frailty status. The first delirium assessment was performed approximately 24 h after surgery to minimize the influence of residual anesthetic effects. From postoperative day 1 to day 7, assessments were conducted twice daily (08:00–10:00 and 18:00–20:00). Additional unscheduled assessments were triggered by behavioral or cognitive changes reported by nurses or family members. Evaluations combined structured CAM/CAM-ICU assessment with review of medical records and collateral information to ensure comprehensive detection of delirium.

Data collection

Comprehensive perioperative data were collected prospectively. Demographic and baseline clinical variables included age, gender, education, smoking status and alcohol consumption, comorbidities (hypertension, diabetes mellitus, cerebrovascular disease), preoperative Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) score, OFI-8 score, and number of remaining teeth and dentures. Preoperative laboratory data included hemoglobin, neutrophil and lymphocyte counts, and serum albumin levels. Intraoperative variables included total duration of surgery and anesthesia, as well as the use of autologous blood transfusion. Postoperative data encompassed ICU length of stay, duration of endotracheal intubation, postoperative hospital stay, and the incidence of POD during postoperative days 1–7.

Sample size calculation

Sample size estimation followed the rule of at least 10 outcome events per predictor variable for multivariable logistic regression analysis. Based on an anticipated postoperative delirium incidence of approximately 40% and inclusion of up to 10 predictors, a minimum of 100 outcome events were required. Therefore, the estimated total sample size was at least 250 patients. To account for potential attrition, we aimed to enroll approximately 290 patients. Ultimately, 289 eligible patients were included in the final analysis.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were tested for normality using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. Normally distributed variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), and non-normally distributed variables as median (interquartile range). Categorical variables were expressed as counts and percentages. Between-group comparisons were performed using Student’s t-test or the Mann–Whitney U test for continuous variables and Pearson’s chi-square test for categorical variables. Univariable logistic regression was conducted to explore associations between candidate variables and POD. Variables with p < 0.20 in univariable analysis and considered appropriate for preoperative risk stratification were entered into the multivariable logistic regression model to identify independent predictors. Odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were reported. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. The discriminative performance of the multivariable model incorporating oral frailty was evaluated using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis. Model calibration was assessed using the Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test. Sensitivity and specificity were calculated based on the optimal Youden index. Mediation analysis was performed to assess whether preoperative cognitive function (MoCA score) mediated the association between oral frailty and POD. All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

Results

Patient characteristics

Between July 2024 and October 2025, 458 patients underwent OPCABG, of whom 373 were aged ≥ 60 years. After applying exclusion criteria, 84 patients were excluded: 33 lacked preoperative cognitive assessment, 16 lacked postoperative delirium evaluation, 29 had incomplete clinical data, and 6 were converted to on-pump CABG. A total of 289 patients were included in the final analysis. Patients were divided into a POD group (n = 116) and a non-POD group (n = 173). Preoperative oral frailty was present in 36 patients in the non-POD group and 44 patients in the POD group (Fig. 1). Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics are summarized in Table 1.

Association between oral frailty and POD

Univariable and multivariable logistic regression analyses are presented in Table 2. Variables with p < 0.20 in univariable analysis (age, diabetes mellitus, education level, oral frailty, preoperative MoCA score, and lymphocyte count) were entered into the multivariable model. The results showed that age (OR 1.075, 95% CI 1.014–1.739, p = 0.015), oral frailty (OR 2.103, 95% CI 1.204–3.673, p = 0.009), diabetes mellitus (OR 1.797, 95% CI 1.052–3.017, p = 0.032), and preoperative MoCA score (OR 0.931, 95% CI 0.867–0.999, p = 0.041) were independently associated with POD (p < 0.05). After adjustment for confounding factors, oral frailty remained a significant predictor of POD (OR 2.103, 95% CI 1.204–3.673).

Predictive model for POD

A multivariable logistic regression model was constructed to evaluate the predictive value of oral frailty. ROC curve analysis demonstrated an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.711 (95% CI 0.650–0.771, p < 0.001), with an optimal Youden index of 0.349. The corresponding sensitivity and specificity were 75.9% and 58.4%, respectively. Model calibration assessed by the Hosmer–Lemeshow test indicated good fit (χ² = 4.155, p = 0.843) (Fig. 2).

Mediation analysis

Mediation analysis revealed that preoperative MoCA score accounted for only 1% (95% CI 0.00–3.00%; p = 0.176) of the indirect effect of oral frailty on POD, representing 4.48% of the total effect. No significant interaction between oral frailty and MoCA score was observed. Results from two sensitivity analyses were consistent with the primary mediation analysis (Table 3).

Discussion

In this prospective cohort study, preoperative oral frailty was independently associated with postoperative delirium (POD) in older patients undergoing OPCABG. The overall POD incidence was 40.1%, consistent with previously reported rates in cardiac surgical populations [20–22]. Patients with oral frailty experienced significantly higher POD incidence than those without (55.0% vs. 34.4%), and this association remained significant after adjustment for established risk factors. These findings suggest that impaired oral functional status represents an underrecognized vulnerability domain in high-risk cardiac surgical patients.

Comparison with existing literature

POD remains a common and clinically significant complication following cardiac surgery, with reported incidence ranging from 30% to 60% depending on diagnostic methodology and patient characteristics [1, 20, 21]. Established risk factors include advanced age, baseline cognitive impairment, frailty, metabolic disorders, and systemic inflammation [23, 24].

Consistent with prior evidence, older age, lower preoperative MoCA score, and diabetes mellitus were independently associated with POD in our cohort. Each additional year of age increased POD risk by 7.5%, reinforcing age as a dominant vulnerability factor [1, 24]. Preoperative cognitive impairment and diabetes have similarly been linked to delirium through mechanisms involving neurovascular dysfunction, metabolic dysregulation, and inflammatory activation [25–27].

Frailty has been repeatedly associated with adverse outcomes after CABG [4–8], and modified frailty indices predict POD in large cardiac surgical cohorts [9]. However, oral frailty—a domain-specific construct reflecting decline in oral functional capacity—has been insufficiently investigated in cardiac surgery populations. Extending previous findings in non-cardiac surgery [15], our study demonstrates that oral frailty independently predicts POD after OPCABG, even after adjustment for conventional frailty-related and cognitive variables.

Clinical performance and practical implications

The multivariable model incorporating oral frailty demonstrated moderate discrimination (AUC 0.711) with good calibration. Although sensitivity was modest (75.9%), specificity was relatively high (58.4%), indicating that oral frailty assessment should not be used as a standalone diagnostic test but rather as a complementary risk stratification tool. In high-risk cardiac surgical populations, even incremental improvements in preoperative risk stratification may support more individualized perioperative management.

Given the simplicity, low cost, and non-invasive nature of the OFI-8 instrument [16], incorporation of oral frailty screening into routine preoperative assessment appears feasible. Identification of patients with oral frailty may enable targeted perioperative strategies, including multimodal delirium-prevention bundles, nutritional optimization, enhanced oral hygiene management, metabolic control, and early mobilization. In this context, oral frailty screening may serve as a practical adjunct to conventional frailty assessment in aging cardiac surgical populations.

Potential biological mechanisms

Several plausible mechanisms may underlie the observed association; however, these interpretations remain speculative because no perioperative inflammatory biomarkers were measured in this study. First, impaired mastication reduces chewing efficiency and dietary diversity, potentially increasing the risk of protein–energy malnutrition and sarcopenia. Beyond nutritional effects, mastication provides afferent sensory stimulation to hippocampal and prefrontal cortical regions, which may influence cognitive reserve [28]. Reduced masticatory activity may therefore lower the resilience of neural networks under surgical stress, increasing susceptibility to delirium [29]. Second, oral frailty is often accompanied by periodontal disease and oral dysbiosis. Chronic periodontal inflammation has been associated with elevated systemic inflammatory markers, including C-reactive protein (CRP), which are linked to frailty and cognitive impairment [30]. Circulating inflammatory mediators may disrupt blood–brain barrier integrity and promote neuroinflammatory cascades implicated in delirium pathophysiology [31]. Experimental evidence suggests that periodontal pathogens such as Porphyromonas gingivalis may promote cerebrovascular amyloid deposition via receptor for advanced glycation end products (RAGE) signaling [32]. In addition, the oral–gut–brain axis has emerged as a potential pathway linking microbial dysbiosis to neurodegeneration and cognitive dysfunction [33]. Third, xerostomia and swallowing dysfunction may impair dietary intake and accelerate systemic frailty. Frailty itself is a well-established risk factor for adverse postoperative outcomes, including delirium. Importantly, mediation analysis indicated that baseline cognitive function accounted for only a small proportion of the total association, suggesting that the relationship between oral frailty and POD may involve inflammatory and metabolic pathways beyond preexisting cognitive impairment.

Conceptual considerations

Oral frailty partially overlaps with broader constructs such as malnutrition, sarcopenia, and general frailty. However, it represents a domain-specific decline in oral functional capacity and oral health-related behaviors [34]. The OFI-8 captures early deterioration in chewing, swallowing, oral dryness, and dental care practices—domains not routinely assessed in standard frailty indices. Although questionnaire-based and partly subjective, multidimensional geriatric screening tools are widely used to identify early vulnerability before overt physiological decline becomes evident [35]. Oral frailty should therefore be interpreted as a complementary risk domain rather than a surrogate marker of global frailty. Emerging evidence suggests that oral hypofunction may independently predict physical frailty progression and adverse outcomes, supporting its potential additive value in perioperative risk assessment.

Strengths and limitations

This study has several strengths, including its prospective design, standardized delirium assessment using CAM and CAM-ICU, blinded outcome evaluators, and multivariable adjustment for established cardiac surgical risk factors. Nevertheless, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, this was a single-center study conducted in a specific healthcare setting, which may limit external validity. Oral frailty patterns may vary across populations due to differences in dietary habits, cultural practices, and access to dental care, potentially affecting generalizability. Second, as an observational study, causal inference cannot be established, and residual confounding may persist despite statistical adjustment. Although major preoperative risk factors were included in the multivariable model, unmeasured variables such as cultural and regional factors, detailed nutritional indices, and other geriatric domains were not available. Third, no perioperative inflammatory biomarkers (e.g., C-reactive protein, interleukins, or other systemic inflammatory markers) were measured or analyzed in this study. This limits our ability to directly evaluate the role of systemic inflammation in the association between oral frailty and postoperative delirium. Therefore, the proposed inflammatory mechanisms should be interpreted with caution. Fourth, although delirium assessments were performed twice daily using validated tools and supplemented by nurse-triggered evaluations, transient or rapidly fluctuating episodes may have been missed. In addition, deeply sedated patients were not assessable during certain periods, which may have resulted in underestimation of delirium incidence. Although ICU sedation followed a standardized protocol consisting of propofol in combination with dexmedetomidine and was applied uniformly to all patients, detailed cumulative dose and duration data were not fully available. Therefore, residual confounding related to sedation exposure cannot be completely excluded. Finally, the OFI-8 is a questionnaire-based instrument and partly subjective. Although administered by trained and calibrated examiners, measurement bias cannot be completely excluded.

Future multicenter studies incorporating biomarker assessment, detailed perioperative data, and longitudinal follow-up are warranted to further clarify causal pathways and validate the clinical utility of oral frailty screening in cardiac surgical populations.

Conclusion

Preoperative oral frailty was independently associated with postoperative delirium in older patients undergoing off-pump coronary artery bypass grafting. Incorporating oral frailty screening into routine preoperative risk assessment may help identify vulnerable individuals who could benefit from multidisciplinary strategies aimed at optimizing oral and systemic health before surgery.
