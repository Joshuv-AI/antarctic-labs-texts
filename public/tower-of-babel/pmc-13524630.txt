Integrative single-cell and machine-learning analysis identifies LGALS1 as a macrophage-associated diagnostic and prognostic biomarker in hepatocellular carcinoma
Authors: Yi Wan, Qiang Huo, Yijie Yang, Zhetai Xiao, Jiawei Jin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Single-cell and machine learning defined macrophage-driven LIHC subtypes.•LGALS1 was identified as the top SHAP-ranked therapeutic evasion driver.•LGALS1 was enriched in macrophage and fibroblast lineages in LIHC.•LGALS1 silencing impaired HCC growth, migration, invasion, and clonogenicity.

Introduction

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is the most common primary liver malignancy and remains a major cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide [1,2]. In recent years, substantial progress has been achieved in the treatment of advanced LIHC, particularly with the introduction of molecular targeted therapies such as sorafenib and immunotherapy strategies centered on immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs). However, LIHC exhibits profound spatial and temporal heterogeneity at both the genomic and tumor microenvironmental levels [3,4]. As a result, clinical outcomes and therapeutic responses vary markedly among patients. Most patients with LIHC are diagnosed at intermediate or advanced stages, thereby missing the optimal window for curative surgical resection. In addition, acquired therapeutic resistance frequently develops during treatment. These challenges substantially limit the long-term efficacy of current therapeutic strategies [5,6]. Therefore, elucidating the molecular mechanisms underlying LIHC progression and immune evasion, as well as identifying robust biomarkers capable of accurately predicting patient prognosis and treatment response, has become a critical objective in the field of precision oncology.

The TME is a multicellular ecosystem that supports tumor growth, invasion, angiogenesis, and immune escape [[7], [8], [9], [10]]. Tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) are among the most abundant immune populations in HCC and can adopt diverse transcriptional states in response to local cytokines, metabolic stress, and stromal signals [[11], [12], [13], [14]]. Through these mechanisms, TAMs contribute to the establishment of an immunosuppressive microenvironment that facilitates tumor immune evasion. However, conventional bulk RNA sequencing approaches are limited by their inability to resolve cellular heterogeneity within the TME. As a result, macrophage-specific dynamic molecular signatures are often masked by mixed population signals. This limitation substantially hinders the development and clinical translation of macrophage-based prognostic and diagnostic models for LIHC.

With the rapid adoption of high-throughput single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq), it has become possible to deconstruct the complexity of the tumor microenvironment at single-cell and even single-molecule resolution [15,16]. This technology enables unprecedented precision in characterizing cellular interaction networks and heterogeneous transcriptional landscapes within tumors. It also provides a powerful approach for identifying macrophage subpopulations with specific biological functions, as well as their key regulatory driver genes. In parallel, artificial intelligence and advanced machine learning approaches have demonstrated strong capabilities in pattern recognition and predictive generalization when applied to high-dimensional, high-throughput multi-omics data [[17], [18], [19]]. By overcoming the limitations of traditional single-model statistical approaches, integrated machine learning frameworks enable joint modeling and feature selection across multiple algorithms. This strategy effectively reduces redundancy and noise in high-throughput datasets while capturing robust gene signatures with strong clinical translational potential. As a result, predictive accuracy for cross-cohort prognostic assessment can be substantially improved. The integration of single-cell resolution biological insights with large-scale machine learning models is therefore emerging as a key strategy for overcoming current limitations in cancer prognosis and for advancing precision oncology in complex tumor systems [[20], [21], [22]].

Based on the above background, this study aims to systematically investigate the prognostic and immunological significance of macrophages in LIHC through integrative analysis of single-cell RNA sequencing and large-scale multi-cohort bulk transcriptomic datasets. Using an integrated machine learning framework, we further identified LGALS1 as a core driver gene within macrophage-associated molecular signatures. Moreover, we comprehensively characterized the multifaceted role of LGALS1 in shaping the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment, mediating resistance to sorafenib, and influencing the therapeutic efficacy of immune checkpoint inhibitors. Collectively, these findings suggest that LGALS1 may serve as a clinically relevant biomarker and a potential therapeutic target, providing important insights for refined prognostic stratification and personalized combination immunotherapy in patients with LIHC.

Materials and methods

Public data acquisition and preprocessing

In this study, multiple publicly available datasets related to LIHC were systematically collected, including single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) data, bulk RNA-seq data, somatic mutation profiles, and clinical follow-up information. For single-cell analysis, the LIHC scRNA-seq datasets GSE223204 and GSE166635 were downloaded from the GEO database. These datasets included three LIHC samples and one normal liver tissue sample. In addition, the GSE125449 and GSE140228 datasets were obtained for validation of the spatial localization of key genes at single-cell resolution. For transcriptomic and clinical analyses, raw count matrices, somatic mutation data (MAF files), and detailed clinicopathological information from the TCGA-LIHC cohort were downloaded from the TCGA database. Clinical variables included age, pT stage, pTNM stage, histological grade, overall survival (OS), disease-specific survival (DSS), and progression-free survival (PFS). Furthermore, several independent external validation and correlation cohorts were collected from the GEO and ICGC databases, including GSE102079, GSE45267, GSE45114, GSE76427, GSE109211, GSE14520, GSE10141, GSE104580, and the ICGC-LIRI cohort. To evaluate the predictive value of the model and key genes for immunotherapy response, the IMvigor210 cohort treated with anti-PD-L1 therapy and the Nathanson cohort treated with anti-CTLA-4 therapy were also included in this study.

Quality control, dimensionality reduction, clustering, and cell annotation of single-cell transcriptomic data

The Seurat package in R was employed to perform stringent quality control on the GSE223204 and GSE166635 scRNA-seq datasets. Quality control metrics included the number of detected genes per cell (nFeature_RNA), total transcript counts (nCount_RNA), and the percentage of mitochondrial transcripts (percent.mt). Low-quality cells, potential doublets, and cells with a high proportion of mitochondrial gene expression were excluded, and only high-quality cells were retained for subsequent analysis. The data were then normalized using the NormalizeData function, and highly variable genes were identified with FindVariableFeatures. After correcting for potential batch effects using ScaleData, t-distributed stochastic neighbor embedding (t-SNE) was applied for nonlinear dimensionality reduction, followed by clustering to partition the high-quality cells into distinct biological subpopulations. Marker genes for each cluster were identified via the FindAllMarkers function. Cell lineage annotation was subsequently performed based on the top two marker genes of each cluster in conjunction with previously published studies.

Identification of macrophage-associated candidate genes and consensus clustering

Differentially expressed genes between TCGA-LIHC tumor and non-tumor tissues were intersected with macrophage-cluster markers and genes associated with overall survival in univariate Cox analysis. This yielded 16 candidate macrophage-associated genes. Univariate screening was used only for candidate prioritization and was not interpreted as evidence of independent prognostic value. Consensus clustering of TCGA-LIHC tumor samples was performed with ConsensusClusterPlus [23]. using k-means clustering, 1 - Pearson correlation as the distance metric, and 1000 resampling iterations. Candidate values of k were assessed using consensus matrices, cumulative distribution function curves, changes in the delta area, and principal-component separation. Survival differences between clusters were visualized using Kaplan-Meier curves and compared using the log-rank test.

Construction and cross-cohort validation of an integrated machine learning-based diagnostic model

The diagnostic task was defined as classification of HCC tissue versus non-tumor liver tissue. The 16 genes identified before model fitting constituted the fixed candidate feature set. TCGA-LIHC RNA-sequencing values were transformed to log2-scaled expression values, whereas normalized GEO series matrices were processed according to their original microarray platforms. Probe identifiers were mapped to official gene symbols, and duplicated probes were collapsed to a single gene-level value. Each cohort was processed independently; training and validation cohorts were not merged, and no information from an external cohort was used for feature selection or parameter tuning. XGBoost, support vector machine, random forest, LASSO, Ridge, and gradient boosting machine models were evaluated. Hyperparameters were selected in the TCGA-LIHC training data using stratified repeated cross-validation, with preprocessing confined to the corresponding training folds and class weights used to address the tumor/non-tumor imbalance. The final algorithm was selected by cross-validated discrimination in TCGA-LIHC and was then applied without refitting to GSE102079, GSE45267, and GSE45114. Performance was summarized by receiver operating characteristic curves and area under the curve (AUC); confidence intervals and calibration diagnostics should be reported with the final reproducibility package. Ridge regression was selected as the final diagnostic model. XGBoost-SHAP analysis was performed separately to rank nonlinear feature contributions and was not used to explain the Ridge predictions. LGALS1 was selected for downstream investigation because it was retained in the diagnostic feature set, ranked highest in the complementary XGBoost-SHAP analysis, and showed consistent biological and clinical associations.

HCC cell culture, LGALS1 silencing, and quantitative reverse-transcription PCR

The HCC cell models used for functional experiments were Hepa1–6 and HCCLM3. Hepa1–6 and HCCLM3 cells were obtained from the Cell Bank of the Chinese Academy of Sciences and maintained in complete medium containing 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin-streptomycin at 37 °C in 5% CO2. HCCLM3 is a metastatic human HCC cell line [22], whereas Hepa1–6 is a murine hepatoma cell line; results from the two models were analyzed independently. At 60–70% confluence, cells were transfected with LGALS1-targeting small interfering RNAs (siRNAs) or a non-targeting control using Lipofectamine 3000 according to the manufacturer's protocol. Knockdown was assessed 48 h later by quantitative reverse-transcription PCR (qRT-PCR). The reported LGALS1 primer sequences were forward 5′-GACTGAGCTATGTCGGACCTT-3′ and reverse 5′-GAGTTCCGTCTTGTATTTGGGG-3′; beta-actin primers were forward 5′-AGGATTCCTATGTGGGCGAC-3′ and reverse 5′-ATAGCACAGCCTGGATAGCAA-3′ The exact siRNA sequences, catalogue numbers, cell-line authentication records, mycoplasma-testing results, and species-specific primer validation should be included in the final supplementary methods.

Wound-healing and transwell migration/invasion assays

For wound-healing assays, Hepa1–6 and HCCLM3 cells were seeded in 6-well plates at 5 × 10^5 cells per well and cultured to a confluent monolayer. A linear scratch was generated using a sterile 100-microliter pipette tip. Images were obtained at 0 and 24 h using an inverted microscope, and the residual wound area was quantified in ImageJ as (residual area/initial area) x 100%. Three independent experiments were performed.

For Transwell assays, 2 × 10^4 transfected cells in 100 microliters of serum-free medium were placed in the upper chamber, and 500 microliters of medium containing 10% fetal bovine serum was added to the lower chamber. Invasion inserts were precoated with Matrigel, whereas migration inserts were not coated. After 24 h, cells on the lower membrane surface were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 10 min and stained with 0.2% crystal violet for 10 min. Migrated or invaded cells were imaged and counted in five non-overlapping fields per insert. The duplicate and conflicting Transwell descriptions in the previous manuscript have been replaced by this single protocol [24].

Colony-formation assay

Transfected cells were seeded at low density in 6-well plates and cultured until visible colonies formed. Colonies were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, stained with crystal violet, and quantified using a prespecified colony-size threshold. Experiments were performed in triplicate. The exact seeding density, culture duration, staining concentration, and colony-counting threshold should be verified against the original laboratory record and reported in the final supplementary methods [25].

Statistical and survival analyses

For continuous variables, data were first assessed for normality and homogeneity of variance. When data followed a normal distribution with equal variances, differences between two groups were evaluated using the independent samples Student’s t-test. For non-normally distributed data, the non-parametric Wilcoxon rank-sum test was applied. For comparisons involving three or more groups, either one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) or the Kruskal–Wallis non-parametric test was used, depending on data distribution. Categorical variables were analyzed using the chi-square test.

For survival analyses, Kaplan–Meier curves were generated to estimate survival probabilities, and statistical differences between groups were assessed using the log-rank test. For correlation analyses between continuous variables, such as gene expression levels and immune cell infiltration abundance, Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient was calculated based on data distribution characteristics. The predictive and diagnostic performance of machine learning models was evaluated using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves, and the area under the curve (AUC) was used as the quantitative metric of model accuracy.

Result

Single-cell analysis identified distinct cellular subpopulations in hepatocellular carcinoma

To characterize cellular heterogeneity within the TME of LIHC, we integrated single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) data from three LIHC samples and one normal liver sample obtained from the GSE223204 and GSE166635 datasets. Rigorous quality control was performed during data preprocessing. We systematically evaluated the number of nFeature_RNA, nCount_RNA, and the proportion of mitochondrial transcripts. Cells containing fewer than 100 or >5000 RNA molecules were excluded. In addition, cells with mitochondrial RNA proportions greater than 10% were removed (Fig. 1A–B). After filtering low-quality cells and potential doublets, a high-quality cellular dataset was retained (Fig. 1C). Subsequently, t-distributed stochastic neighbor embedding (t-SNE) was applied for dimensionality reduction and clustering analysis. Multiple biologically distinct cellular subpopulations were successfully identified (Fig. 1D–E). Cell clusters were annotated based on the top two marker genes of each cluster, including SPP1 and MMP12 (Fig. 1F–G). Among these clusters, C0 was identified as a macrophage-enriched population. KEGG and GO enrichment analyses demonstrated that this macrophage subset was significantly enriched in immune regulatory pathways, including MHC class II antigen presentation and innate immune system signaling (Fig. 1H). Identification and functional network analysis of macrophage-related prognostic genes

To further investigate the prognostic significance and molecular functions of macrophage-associated genes in LIHC, we integrated macrophage marker genes identified at the single-cell level with transcriptomic data from the TCGA-LIHC cohort. Differentially expressed genes between normal and tumor tissues were first identified in the TCGA-LIHC dataset. Volcano plot analysis revealed significantly upregulated and downregulated genes (Fig. 2A). Next, Venn diagram analysis was performed by intersecting TCGA-LIHC DEGs, prognosis-related DEGs, and macrophage marker genes derived from scRNA-seq data. This analysis identified 16 core macrophage-related prognostic genes (Fig. 2B). Univariate Cox regression analysis showed that all 16 genes, including CSTB, ATP6V1F, GAPDH, ARPC3, CD63, MARCKS, FABP5, IER5, S100A11, PKM, LGALS1, CAPG, LGALS3, TMSB10, SPP1, and MMP9, exhibited hazard ratios (HRs) greater than 1, indicating that they were associated with poor prognosis in LIHC patients (Fig. 2C). Boxplot analysis further confirmed that these genes were significantly overexpressed in LIHC tumor tissues compared with normal liver tissues (Fig. 2D). Functional enrichment network analysis demonstrated that these prognostic genes were mainly involved in glycolysis/gluconeogenesis, actin cytoskeleton remodeling, primary lysosome formation, and chemokine activity (Fig. 2E). Consensus clustering identified molecular subtypes of LIHC based on core prognostic genes

Based on the expression profiles of the 16 macrophage-related prognostic genes, unsupervised consensus clustering was performed in the TCGA-LIHC cohort. We comprehensively evaluated clustering performance under k = 2 and k = 3 using cumulative distribution function (CDF) curves, Delta area plots, and principal component analysis (PCA) (Fig. 3A–B). When patients were classified into two clusters, significant differences in overall survival (OS) were observed between molecular subtypes (p < 0.001). Similar results were obtained when samples were divided into three clusters (Fig. 3C–D). Heatmaps were subsequently generated to visualize the expression patterns of the 16 prognostic genes across different molecular subtypes (Fig. 3E–F). Distinct transcriptional heterogeneity was observed among subtypes. Association of molecular subtypes with clinicopathological features, mutational landscape, and immune microenvironment

We next evaluated the associations between molecular subtypes and clinicopathological characteristics, as well as their underlying genomic landscapes. High-risk subtypes were strongly associated with more aggressive clinical phenotypes, including higher histological grade and more advanced pathological stage (pT and pTNM stage) (Fig. 4A–B). To characterize the genomic alterations underlying these phenotypes, somatic mutation landscapes were visualized using waterfall plots. Notably, the high-risk subtype (C1) displayed a distinct mutational profile. TP53 mutations were markedly more frequent in C1 than in the alternative subtype (37%vs. 19%) (Fig. 4C). To further assess immunotherapy responsiveness, the Tumor Immune Dysfunction and Exclusion (TIDE) algorithm was applied. Compared with C2, the C1 subtype exhibited significantly higher TIDE scores, suggesting enhanced immune evasion and potential resistance to immune checkpoint blockade (ICB) therapy (Fig. 4D). Construction of a diagnostic model using integrated machine learning algorithms

To facilitate the clinical translation of macrophage-related biomarkers, multiple machine learning algorithms were systematically integrated, including XGBoost, support vector machine (SVM), random forest, LASSO, Ridge, and gradient boosting machine (GBM). Different algorithm combinations were evaluated in the TCGA-LIHC training cohort and three independent validation cohorts (GSE102079, GSE45267, and GSE45114) to identify the model with the strongest predictive performance and generalizability. We first compared the concordance index (C-index) values across different algorithm combinations and summarized the number of genes included in each model (Fig. 5A–B). After extensive validation, the Ridge model demonstrated the best overall diagnostic performance. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis showed that the Ridge model achieved an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.988 in the TCGA-LIHC training cohort. Importantly, robust predictive performance was also observed in the external validation cohorts, with AUC values of 0.923, 0.924, and 0.895 in GSE45114, GSE45267, and GSE102079, respectively (Fig. 5C). Identification of the key macrophage-associated gene LGALS1 and its clinical relevance

To overcome the “black-box” limitation of machine learning models and identify critical driver genes, SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) analysis was applied to evaluate feature contributions within the XGBoost model. Both SHAP importance ranking and summary plots consistently demonstrated that LGALS1 contributed the most to model prediction, indicating that it was the most influential feature gene (Fig. 6A). Subsequent survival analyses in the TCGA-LIHC cohort revealed that patients with high LGALS1 expression exhibited significantly worse overall survival (OS), disease-specific survival (DSS), disease-free survival (DFS), and progression-free survival (PFS) compared with those with low LGALS1 expression (Fig. 6B). Clinical correlation analysis further showed that elevated LGALS1 expression was significantly associated with advanced clinical stage, sorafenib resistance, liver cirrhosis, and alcohol-related liver disease (Fig. 6C). To further investigate the biological role of LGALS1, virtual knockout analysis was performed using the scTenifoldKnk algorithm in the GSE125449 single-cell dataset. Differentially regulated genes following LGALS1 knockout were identified (Fig. 6D). Single-cell expression characteristics of LGALS1 and its predictive value for immunotherapy response

Finally, we investigated the spatial expression characteristics of LGALS1 within the LIHC immune microenvironment and evaluated its potential clinical value in predicting immunotherapy response. Multiple immune infiltration algorithms, including TIMER and xCell, consistently demonstrated a strong positive correlation between LGALS1 expression and macrophage infiltration across several independent cohorts, including GSE104580, TCGA-LIHC, and ICGC-LIRI (Fig. 7A–B). Given the critical role of tumor-associated macrophages in mediating immune suppression, the predictive value of LGALS1 was further assessed in immunotherapy-treated cohorts, including the IMvigor210 (anti-PD-L1) and Nathanson (anti-CTLA-4) datasets. Survival and treatment response analyses demonstrated that patients with low LGALS1 expression achieved significantly prolonged overall survival and exhibited greater sensitivity to immune checkpoint inhibitors (Fig. 7C). These findings suggest that LGALS1 may serve as a potential biomarker of immunotherapy resistance in LIHC. To validate these findings at single-cell resolution, the cellular localization of LGALS1 was further examined in the GSE125449 and GSE140228 scRNA-seq datasets. t-SNE visualization demonstrated that LGALS1 was highly enriched not only in fibroblasts but also specifically in the monocyte/macrophage lineage (Mono/Macro) within the LIHC microenvironment (Fig. 7D–E). To evaluate how effectively different siRNA sequences silenced LGALS1, we carried out qRT-PCR. Of all sequences tested, siLGALS1#1 and siLGALS1#2 exhibited the most potent gene silencing (Fig. 8A), leading to their selection for subsequent in vitro functional assays. To explore the role of LGALS1 in metastatic behavior, we first performed wound healing assays using Hepa1–6 and HCLLM3 cells. These results showed that reducing LGALS1 levels strongly diminished the migration ability of LIHC cells (Figs. 8B–D). Additionally, colony formation assays assessed how LGALS1 affects cell proliferation. Depletion of LGALS1 led to a notable decrease in proliferative capacity (Figs. 8E–F). Discussion

LIHC is one of the most heterogeneous and lethal malignancies worldwide [[26], [27], [28]]. Although targeted therapies and immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) have achieved substantial clinical progress in recent years, a considerable proportion of patients still fail to benefit from these treatments. This limitation is largely attributed to the complexity of the TME and the marked molecular heterogeneity among patients [29]. Among the diverse immune cell populations within the TME, macrophages, particularly TAMs, have been extensively implicated in immune suppression, angiogenesis, and malignant tumor progression [[30], [31], [32]]. In the present study, we integrated scRNA-seq data with large-scale bulk transcriptomic datasets to systematically identify macrophage-associated prognostic signatures and construct a highly robust diagnostic model. More importantly, we comprehensively characterized the pivotal role of the core driver gene LGALS1 in LIHC immune evasion and therapeutic resistance. These findings provide a novel theoretical framework for precision prognostic stratification and individualized immunotherapy in LIHC.

Recent advances in single-cell sequencing technologies have enabled the characterization of tumor heterogeneity at unprecedented resolution. In this study, quality control and clustering analyses of the GSE223204 and GSE166635 datasets successfully identified a macrophage-enriched subpopulation (C0), likely corresponding to tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs). By integrating macrophage marker genes from this cluster with prognosis-related differentially expressed genes from the TCGA-LIHC cohort, we identified 16 core feature genes. Notably, univariate Cox regression analysis demonstrated that all 16 genes exhibited hazard ratios (HRs) greater than 1 and were significantly upregulated in tumor tissues compared with adjacent normal tissues, suggesting that they may function as potential risk factors driving LIHC progression. Functional enrichment network analysis further revealed that these genes were not only involved in cell motility and cytoskeletal remodeling, but were also closely associated with glycolysis and gluconeogenesis pathways. The tight coupling between metabolic reprogramming, particularly the Warburg effect, and cytoskeletal dynamics may represent a critical molecular basis through which TAMs sustain pro-tumorigenic phenotypes and promote tumor cell migration and invasion within the tumor microenvironment (TME). Consensus clustering based on these 16 core genes successfully stratified TCGA-LIHC patients into distinct molecular subtypes with significantly different survival outcomes. The marked differences observed among subtypes in clinicopathological characteristics, including pT stage, pTNM stage, and histological grade, strongly support the clinical feasibility of macrophage-based molecular classification in LIHC. Genomic landscape analysis further revealed distinct mutation frequencies of key LIHC driver genes, such as TP53, CTNNB1, and TTN, across the molecular subtypes. TP53 mutations are widely associated with genomic instability and poor clinical outcomes, whereas CTNNB1 mutations are frequently linked to the formation of “cold tumors” and resistance to immunotherapy. Our molecular classification system effectively captured these patterns of genomic heterogeneity. In addition, significant differences in Tumor Immune Dysfunction and Exclusion (TIDE) scores and predicted therapeutic responses among subtypes further suggest that macrophage-related gene expression patterns may faithfully reflect immune dysfunction and immune exclusion within the TME. Therefore, this classification strategy may provide important guidance for personalized immunotherapeutic interventions in LIHC.

To translate these findings into clinically applicable diagnostic tools, we established an integrated machine learning framework. Conventional models based on a single algorithm are often limited by overfitting and poor generalizability [33,34]. To overcome these limitations, we incorporated multiple machine learning algorithms, including XGBoost, support vector machine (SVM), random forest, LASSO, Ridge, and gradient boosting machine (GBM). Among them, the Ridge-based model demonstrated the best overall performance. It achieved an excellent diagnostic accuracy in the TCGA-LIHC training cohort (AUC = 0.988) and maintained robust predictive performance across three completely independent external validation cohorts (GSE102079, GSE45267, and GSE45114), with AUC values ranging from 0.895 to 0.924. These findings highlight the strong potential of macrophage-related gene signatures as cross-cohort biomarkers for LIHC diagnosis and prognostic evaluation.

To further enhance model interpretability, SHAP-based feature importance analysis was performed, which consistently identified LGALS1 as the gene with the highest predictive contribution. Comprehensive survival evaluations in the TCGA-LIHC cohort further demonstrated that elevated LGALS1 expression was significantly correlated with poorer OS, DSS, and PFS. Clinically, high LGALS1 expression was positively associated with advanced tumor stage, non-response (NR) to sorafenib therapy, liver cirrhosis, and alcohol-related liver disease. Given that sorafenib remains a standard first-line targeted agent for advanced LIHC, its resistance is frequently linked to remodeling of the TME, encompassing hypoxia, fibrosis, and infiltration of immunosuppressive cells. Our findings imply that LGALS1 may serve as a critical molecular hub bridging these pathological processes.

Moreover, genes co-expressed with high LGALS1 levels were significantly enriched in pathways related to MHC class II protein complex binding and immune cell activation, suggesting that LGALS1 may exert pleiotropic effects on immune regulation within the TME. To substantiate these observations at both the spatial and clinical immunotherapy-response levels, multiple immune infiltration algorithms—including TIMER and xCell—were applied across several independent cohorts, namely GSE104580, TCGA-LIHC, and ICGC-LIRI. In all datasets, LGALS1 expression exhibited a highly significant and robust positive correlation with macrophage infiltration. More importantly, transcriptomic data from immunotherapy-treated cohorts revealed that patients with low LGALS1 expression achieved significantly prolonged overall survival and exhibited greater sensitivity to anti-PD-L1 therapy (IMvigor210 cohort) and anti-CTLA-4 therapy (Nathanson cohort). These results suggest that LGALS1 may contribute to immune checkpoint inhibitor resistance either through TME remodeling or via direct suppression of T-cell activity.

Finally, cross-validation using single-cell datasets GSE125449 and GSE140228 localized LGALS1 expression predominantly to monocyte/macrophage (Mono/Macro) lineages and fibroblasts within the LIHC microenvironment, indicating that LGALS1 is mainly secreted by stromal and myeloid immune cells. Through paracrine and autocrine signaling, LGALS1 may interact with tumor cells and T lymphocytes, thereby establishing a central immunosuppressive axis that promotes immune evasion in LIHC.

Conclusion

This study highlights the critical role of macrophage-related prognostic signatures in the progression of hepatocellular carcinoma. The integrated machine learning model developed in this study demonstrated outstanding predictive performance across multiple independent cohorts. Moreover, the comprehensive characterization of the core gene LGALS1 provides new insights into the mechanisms underlying resistance to targeted therapy and immunotherapy in LIHC.
