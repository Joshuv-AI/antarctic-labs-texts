1.
Introduction

It has been known for at least thirty years that convex functions have the
C^{2}
-Lusin property
, meaning that if
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
is convex, then for every
\varepsilon>0
there is a function
g\in C^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
such that
|\{f\neq g\}|<\varepsilon
, see
[
1
,
5
,
7
]
. Here
|A|
denotes the Lebesgue measure of
A
. In that case we say that
g
is a
Lusin approximation of
f
.

However, in general the function
g
cannot be convex. For example for
f:\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
,
f(x,y)=|x|
, it is easy to show that if
g:\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
is convex and
|\{f\neq g\}|<\infty
, then
f=g
everywhere. In general we have

Theorem 1.1

(
[
2
,
4
]
)
.

Let
U\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be open and convex, and let
f:U\to\mathbb{R}
be a convex function, such that
f\not\in C^{1,1}_{\rm loc}(U)
. Then, the following statements are equivalent:

(1)

For every
\varepsilon>0
there exists a convex function
g\in C^{1,1}_{\rm loc}(U)
such that

|\{x\in U:f(x)\neq g(x)\}|<\varepsilon.

(2)

The graph of
f
does not contain any line isometric to
\mathbb{R}
.

Moreover, if the graph of
f
contains no lines, we can find
g
satisfying
g\geq f
.

Here
C^{1,1}_{\rm loc}
stands for the class of functions with locally Lipschitz continuous gradient.

However, the Lusin approximation by convex
C^{1,1}_{{\mathrm{loc}}}
functions is much easier than the Lusin approximation by convex
C^{2}
functions and the approximation by
C^{2}
convex functions has only been proved under an additional condition that the function
f
is locally strongly convex.

Recall that a function
f:U\to\mathbb{R}
is
strongly convex
, where
U\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
is open and convex, if there exists
\eta>0
such that
f(x)-\frac{\eta}{2}|x|^{2}
is convex. In this case, we say that
f
is
\eta
-strongly convex
. Moreover,
f
is
locally strongly convex
whenever for every
x\in U
there exists
r>0
such that the restriction of
f
to
B(x,r)\subset U
is strongly convex. Note that locally strongly convex functions cannot contain a line on the graph and thence they can always be approximated in the Lusin sense by convex
C^{1,1}_{\rm loc}
functions. However, we have a much stronger result in that case:

Theorem 1.2

(
[
3
]
)
.

Let
U\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
be open and convex, and let
f:U\to\mathbb{R}
be locally strongly convex. Then for every
\varepsilon_{o}>0
and for every continuous function
\varepsilon:U\to(0,1]
there is a locally strongly convex function

g\in C^{2}(U)
, such that

|\{x\in U:\,f(x)\neq g(x)\}|<\varepsilon_{o}\quad\text{and}\quad|f(x)-g(x)|<\varepsilon(x)\text{ for all }x\in U.

Also, if
f
is
\eta
-strongly convex on
U
, then for every
\widetilde{\eta}\in(0,\eta)
there exists such a function
g
which is
\widetilde{\eta}
-strongly convex on
U
.

The main difficulty in Theorems
1.1
and
1.2
stems from the fact that they are true in an arbitrary dimension. While the dimension
n=1
is much easier to deal with, it seems it has not been fully investigated yet. The main result of the paper resolves the problem of Lusin approximation of convex functions when
n=1
.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
f:(a,b)\to\mathbb{R}
be convex,
-\infty\leq a<b\leq\infty
. Then for every
\varepsilon_{o}>0
and for every continuous function
\varepsilon:(a,b)\to(0,\infty)
there is a convex function
g\in C^{2}(a,b)
such that

|\{x\in(a,b):\,f(x)\neq g(x)\}|<\varepsilon_{o}\quad\text{and}\quad|f(x)-g(x)|<\varepsilon(x)\text{ for all }x\in(a,b).

The uniform approximation described in the theorem allows one to have increasing accuracy when approaching the boundary. In particular, if
f
is continuous on
[a,b]
, we can find
g\in C^{2}(a,b)\cap C([a,b])
with
g(a)=f(a)
and
g(b)=f(b)
.

The problem whether every convex function in dimension
n\geq 2
, whose graph does not contain a line, can be approximated in the Lusin sense by
C^{2}
convex functions is open. However, we conjecture:

Conjecture 1.4
.

If
n\geq 2
, then there is a convex function
f:B^{n}(0,1)\to\mathbb{R}
and
m>0
, such that if
g\in C^{2}(B^{n}(0,1))
is convex, then

|\{f\neq g\}|\geq m
.

Let us explain now the main idea of the proof of Theorem
1.3
along with the main difficulty. Given a convex function
f:(a,b)\to\mathbb{R}
, we want to find a convex function
g\in C^{2}(a,b)
, such that
|\{f\neq g\}|<\varepsilon
. Theorem
1.1
allows us to assume that
f\in C^{1,1}_{\rm loc}
and it is well known that a
C^{1,1}_{\rm loc}
function coincides with a
C^{2}
function outside a set of arbitrarily small measure. Thus, we can find
u\in C^{2}(a,b)
such that
u=f
on a closed set
E
with
|(a,b)\setminus E|<\varepsilon
. However, the function
u
need not be convex and we want to correct it outside
E
to make it
C^{2}
and convex. The set
(a,b)\setminus E
is the union of countably many intervals
(a_{i},b_{i})
. If
u
is convex on
[a_{i},b_{i}]
, we do not change it on
[a_{i},b_{i}]
. If
u
is not convex on
[a_{i},b_{i}]
, we want to replace it with a convex
C^{2}
function on
[a_{i},b_{i}]
while keeping values of
u
,
u^{\prime}
and
u^{\prime\prime}
at the endpoints.

However, it is not always possible. If for example,

f(x)=\begin{cases}x^{2}&\text{if }x\leq 0,\\
0&\text{if }0\leq x\leq 1,\\
(x-1)^{2}&\text{if }x\geq 1,\end{cases}

then
f
is convex, of class
C^{1,1}
, but not of class
C^{2}
. Clearly, there is
u\in C^{2}(\mathbb{R})
, such that

u=f
on
\mathbb{R}\setminus(0,1)
, but there is no convex
C^{2}(\mathbb{R})
function that coincides with
f
on
\mathbb{R}\setminus(0,1)
. Indeed, it is easy to see that the only convex function that coincides with
f
on
\mathbb{R}\setminus(0,1)
is
f
itself. This is because the value of
f(1)
lies on the tangent line at
x=0
.

Therefore, when we select the closed set
E\subset(a,b)
where
u=f
, we have to make sure that the intervals
(a_{i},b_{i})
have the property that the value of
f(b_{i})
lines above the tangent line at
a_{i}
and that we have good estimates on how far
f(b_{i})
is from the tangent line at
a_{i}
, so that when we replace
u
on infinitely many intervals
(a_{i},b_{i})
with explicitly constructed convex functions of class
C^{2}([a_{i},b_{i}])
, the resulting function will be of class
C^{2}(a,b)
.

2.
Proof of Theorem
1.3

2.1.
Proof of a special case

In this section we prove a weaker result (Lemma
2.1
). The general case is treated in Section
2.2
.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
f:(a,b)\to\mathbb{R}
be convex,
a,b\in\mathbb{R}
. Then for every
\varepsilon>0
there is a convex function
g\in C^{2}(a,b)
such that

|\{x\in(a,b):\,f(x)\neq g(x)\}|<\varepsilon.

Note that we assume here that the interval
(a,b)
is bounded, while in Theorem
1.3
we have any open interval.

Proof.

According to Theorem
1.1
, we can assume that
f\in C^{1,1}_{\rm loc}(a,b)
. Clearly,
f
is twice differentiable a.e.

Fix small
\varepsilon>0
.
It follows from Whitney’s theorem
[
9
, Theorem 4]
that
f\in C^{1,1}_{\rm loc}(a,b)
coincides with a
C^{2}
function
u\in C^{2}(a,b)
outside a set of measure less that
\varepsilon/3
. However, the result does not guarantee convexity of
u
.

Let
E_{1}\subset(a,b)
,
|(a,b)\setminus E_{1}|<\varepsilon/3
, be a compact set such that
u=f
,
u^{\prime}=f^{\prime}
,
u^{\prime\prime}=f^{\prime\prime}
on
E_{1}
.

Let
h:=f^{\prime\prime}
. According to the Lebesgue differentiation theorem, almost every
x\in(a,b)
is a Lebesgue point of
h
and at such a point we have (see
[
8
, p. 11]
)

h_{r^{\pm}}(x)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle r\to 0}}{{\longrightarrow}}h(x),\quad\text{where}\quad h_{r^{+}}(x):=\frac{1}{r}\int_{x}^{x+r}h(y)\,dy,\quad h_{r^{-}}(x):=\frac{1}{r}\int_{x-r}^{x}h(y)\,dy.

(1)

Egorov’s and Lusin’s theorems yield a compact set
E_{2}\subset(a,b)
,
|(a,b)\setminus E_{2}|<\varepsilon/3
, such that
h|_{E_{2}}
is continuous and

h_{1/i^{\pm}}\rightrightarrows h\quad\text{uniformly on}\quad E_{2}\text{ as }i\to\infty

(2)

This implies that

h_{r^{\pm}}\rightrightarrows h\quad\text{uniformly on}\quad E_{2}\text{ as }r\to 0.

(3)

Indeed,
h
is bounded in an open neighborhood of
E_{2}
, say
0\leq h\leq M
. For each
r\in(0,1)
, let
i\in\mathbb{N}
be such that
1/i\leq r<1/(i-1)
; it suffices to observe that for sufficiently small
r
,

|h_{r^{\pm}}-h_{1/i^{\pm}}|\leq 2M/i\to 0\quad\text{as }i\to\infty.

This and (
2
) imply (
3
).

Fix
\delta>0
such that

|\{x\in(a,b):\,h(x)\in(0,\delta)\}|<\varepsilon/3,

and let

E_{3}\subset\{x\in(a,b):\,h(x)\not\in(0,\delta)\},\quad|(a,b)\setminus E_{3}|<\varepsilon/3,

be a compact set. Clearly,
h(x)=0
or
h(x)\geq\delta
for all
x\in E_{3}
. Finally define

E:=E_{1}\cap E_{2}\cap E_{3},\quad\text{so}\quad|(a,b)\setminus E|<\varepsilon.

By removing countably many points from
E
, we can assume that there are no isolated points in
E
.

The function
u\in C^{2}(a,b)
coincides with
f
on
E
and hence it approximates
f
in the Lusin sense, i.e.
|\{u\neq f\}|<\varepsilon
. However,
u
is not necessarily convex. We will
construct a convex function
g\in C^{2}(a,b)
such that
f=g
on
E
by applying an infinite sequence of modifications to the function
u
while keeping its values on the set
E
unchanged.

The set
(a,b)\setminus E=\bigcup_{i}(a_{i},b_{i})
is the union of countably many open intervals.
Since the set
E
has no isolated points, any two intervals
(a_{i},b_{i})
and
(a_{j},b_{j})
,
i\neq j
, have positive distance one from another.

Consider first the endpoint intervals
(a_{i},b_{i})=(a,b_{i})
and
(a_{i},b_{i})=(a_{i},b)
.
Denote the endpoint intervals by
(a,\alpha):=(a,b_{i})
and
(\beta,b):=(a_{i},b)
, so
E\subset I:=[\alpha,\beta]
.

We replace
u
in
(a_{i},b_{i}]=(a,\alpha]
with any convex function
u_{i}\in C^{2}((a,\alpha])
such that
u_{i}(\alpha)=u(\alpha)
,
u_{i}^{\prime}(\alpha)=u^{\prime}(\alpha)
, and
u_{i}^{\prime\prime}(\alpha)=u^{\prime\prime}(\alpha)
. We also replace
u
on
[a_{i},b_{i})=[\beta,b)
with
u_{i}\in C^{2}([\beta,b))
in a similar way.
The resulting function will still be denoted by
u
.

Assume now that an interval
(a_{i},b_{i})
is not an endpoint interval, i.e.,
a_{i}>a
and
b_{i}<b
.
Clearly,
[a_{i},b_{i}]\subset I=[\alpha,\beta]
.
If

h(a_{i})>0\qquad\text{or}\qquad h(b_{i})>0,

(4)

then there is a convex function
u_{i}\in C^{2}([a_{i},b_{i}])
such that

\displaystyle u_{i}(a_{i})=u(a_{i}),\ u_{i}^{\prime}(a_{i})=u^{\prime}(a_{i}),\ u_{i}^{\prime\prime}(a_{i})=u^{\prime\prime}(a_{i}),

(5)

\displaystyle u_{i}(b_{i})=u(b_{i}),\ u_{i}^{\prime}(b_{i})=u^{\prime}(b_{i}),\ u_{i}^{\prime\prime}(b_{i})=u^{\prime\prime}(b_{i}).

Indeed, if for example
h(a_{i})>0
, then
f^{\prime\prime}=h>0
on a subset of
(a_{i},b_{i})
of positive measure, because (
1
) is satisfied at
x=a_{i}
. Therefore,

u(b_{i})=f(b_{i})>f(a_{i})+f^{\prime}(a_{i})(b_{i}-a_{i})=u(a_{i})+u^{\prime}(a_{i})(b_{i}-a_{i}),

i.e., the value of
u(b_{i})
is above the tangent line at
a_{i}
and this allows us to define
u_{i}
explicitly by prescribing (piecewise linear)
u_{i}^{\prime\prime}\geq 0
on
[a_{i},b_{i}]
. For more details, see the discussion surrounding formula (
12
).

We fix such a function
u_{i}
for each interval
[a_{i},b_{i}]
satisfying (
4
).

First, consider intervals
(a_{i},b_{i})
, such that

h(a_{i})>0\quad\text{and}\quad h(b_{i})>0.

(6)

If
u
is convex on
[a_{i},b_{i}]
, we do not alter
u
on
[a_{i},b_{i}]
. Thus, assume that

h(a_{i})>0,\quad h(b_{i})>0,\quad u\text{ is not convex on }[a_{i},b_{i}].

(7)

Uniform continuity of
u^{\prime\prime}
on
I=[\alpha,\beta]
implies that
I
contains at most finitely many intervals
[a_{i},b_{i}]
satisfying (
7
) (they are pairwise disjoint, because
E
has no isolated points). Indeed, there is
\tau>0
, such that

|x-y|<\tau,\,\,\,\,x,y\in I\quad\Longrightarrow\quad|u^{\prime\prime}(x)-u^{\prime\prime}(y)|<\delta.

If
u
is not convex on
[a_{i},b_{i}]
, then there is
x\in(a_{i},b_{i})
with
u^{\prime\prime}(x)<0
. Hence

|u^{\prime\prime}(a_{i})-u^{\prime\prime}(x)|>u^{\prime\prime}(a_{i})=h(a_{i})\geq\delta\quad\text{implies that}\quad|a_{i}-b_{i}|>|a_{i}-x|\geq\tau.

Clearly, we can have only a finite number of pairwise disjoint intervals of length at least
\tau
inside
I
.
On intervals satisfying (
7
) we redefine
u
as follows:

u(x):=u_{i}(x)\quad\text{if }x\in[a_{i},b_{i}]\text{ and }\eqref{eq8}\text{ is satisfied.}

This completes the redefinition of
u
on all intervals satisfying (
6
).
Note that although we redefined the function
u
on intervals
[a_{i},b_{i}]
, we still denote the new function by
u
.

Now, let us consider intervals satisfying

\big(h(a_{i})=0\text{ and }h(b_{i})>0\big)\qquad\text{or}\qquad\big(h(a_{i})>0\text{ and }h(b_{i})=0\big).

(8)

Again, the interval
I=[\alpha,\beta]
contains at most finitely many intervals
[a_{i},b_{i}]\subset I
satisfying (
8
).
Indeed, since
h
is uniformly continuous on
E
, there is a constant
\tau>0
, such that

|x-y|<\tau,\ x,y\in E\quad\Longrightarrow\quad|h(x)-h(y)|<\delta.

Since (
8
) implies that
|h(a_{i})-h(b_{i})|\geq\delta
, it follows that
|a_{i}-b_{i}|\geq\tau
,
and we can only have a finite number of such intervals in
I
.
As in the previous case, on such intervals we replace
u
with

u(x):=u_{i}(x)\quad\text{if }x\in[a_{i},b_{i}]\text{ and }\eqref{eq6}\text{ is satisfied.}

(9)

We corrected
u
on all intervals
[a_{i},b_{i}]
satisfying (
6
) or (
8
), that is, on all intervals satisfying (
4
), on which
u
was not convex already. Since we altered
u
on a finite family of pairwise disjoint intervals while keeping the values of
u
,
u^{\prime}
and
u^{\prime\prime}
at the endpoints, the resulting new function (still denoted by
u
) is of class
C^{2}(a,b)
and

u^{\prime\prime}(x)\geq 0\text{ whenever }x\not\in(a_{i},b_{i})\text{ for some }i\text{ such that }h(a_{i})=h(b_{i})=0.

(10)

The remaining intervals on which we need to modify the function
u
are such that

h(a_{i})=h(b_{i})=0.

(11)

Denote by
\mathcal{I}
the set of indices
i
satisfying (
11
).

We will replace
u
on the intervals
[a_{i},b_{i}]
,

, with suitable

convex functions
{u_{i}\in C^{2}([a_{i},b_{i}])}
satisfying (

In the previous modifications of

. However, this time the family of intervals
[a_{i},b_{i}]
,

, may be infinite and that the resulting modified function is still of class
C^{2}(a,b)
will require some work.

Since
u
,
u^{\prime}
and
u^{\prime\prime}
agree with
f
,
f^{\prime}
and
f^{\prime\prime}
at the points
a_{i}
and
b_{i}
, we are looking for a
C^{2}
convex function
u_{i}
such that
u_{i}
,
u^{\prime}_{i}
and
u^{\prime\prime}_{i}
agree with
f
,
f^{\prime}
and
f^{\prime\prime}
at the points
a_{i}
and
b_{i}
(in particular,
u_{i}^{\prime\prime}(a_{i})=u_{i}^{\prime\prime}(b_{i})=0)
.

The simplest case is when
h=0
at almost every point of
(a_{i},b_{i})
. Then the function
f
is linear on
[a_{i},b_{i}]
and hence
u^{\prime}(a_{i})=u^{\prime}(b_{i})
. In that case we define
u_{i}
to be linear on
[a_{i},b_{i}]
.

Therefore we may assume that

. Upon translation, we can assume that

, where

Since

and

, Taylor’s formula with the integral remainder yields

f^{\prime}(c_{i})=f^{\prime}(0)+\int_{0}^{c_{i}}h(t)\,dt,\quad f(c_{i})=f(0)+f^{\prime}(0)c_{i}+\int_{0}^{c_{i}}h(t)(c_{i}-t)\,dt.

and

is defined as the second anti-derivative of

u_{i}(x):=f(0)+f^{\prime}(0)x+\int_{0}^{x}\int_{0}^{\tau}h_{i}(t)\,dt\,d\tau\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm(Fubini)}}}{{=}}f(0)+f^{\prime}(0)x+\int_{0}^{x}h_{i}(t)(x-t)\,dt,

(13)

,

and

Thus we need to construct a function

Clearly
\int_{0}^{c_{i}}h_{i}(t)\,dt
equals the area of the triangle, which is
P_{i}
. If
h_{i}
is regarded as a mass density, the center of mass is located at
\tau_{i}
(by symmetry) and hence

\frac{\int_{0}^{c_{i}}th_{i}(t)\,dt}{\int_{0}^{c_{i}}h_{i}(t)\,dt}=\tau_{i},\quad\int_{0}^{c_{i}}th_{i}(t)\,dt=P_{i}\tau_{i}.

Therefore if
i\in\mathcal{I}
and
\int_{a_{i}}^{b_{i}}h(t)\,dt>0
, we define convex
u_{i}\in C^{2}([0,c_{i}])
and after translation, convex
u_{i}\in C^{2}([a_{i},b_{i}])
using (
13
), (
14
) and (

).

Until now we did not make any distinction between the two cases when
\mathcal{I}
is a finite and an infinite set. The case of finite
\mathcal{I}
is easy: the procedure described above consists of a finite number of corrections on disjoint intervals
[a_{i},b_{i}]
; the
C^{2}
-differentiability of the resulting function is immediate. The case of infinite
\mathcal{I}
requires an additional argument.

The next result provides a crucial estimate for the functions
u_{i}^{\prime\prime}
,
i\in\mathcal{I}
. Let

\varepsilon_{i}:=\max\Big\{\sup_{x\in(0,c_{i}]}\frac{1}{x}\int_{0}^{x}h(t)\,dt,\,\,\sup_{x\in(0,c_{i}]}\frac{1}{x}\int_{c_{i}-x}^{c_{i}}h(t)\,dt\Big\}\,.

(16)

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
i\in\mathcal{I}
. Then
0\leq u^{\prime\prime}_{i}\leq 4\varepsilon_{i}
on
[a_{i},b_{i}]
.

Proof.

If
\int_{a_{i}}^{b_{i}}h(t)\,dt=0
,
u_{i}
is linear and hence
u_{i}^{\prime\prime}=0
. Thus we may assume that the integral is positive. Since
u_{i}^{\prime\prime}=h_{i}
and
0\leq h_{i}\leq H_{i}
, it suffices to prove that
H_{i}\leq 4\varepsilon_{i}
.
First, assume that
\tau_{i}\in(0,c_{i}/2]
. For
x\in(0,c_{i})
we have

P_{i}=\int_{0}^{c_{i}}h(t)\,dt\leq x\cdot\frac{1}{x}\int_{0}^{x}h(t)\,dt+\frac{1}{x}\int_{x}^{c_{i}}th(t)\,dt\leq x\varepsilon_{i}+\frac{1}{x}P_{i}\tau_{i}.

(17)

Since
P_{i}\leq c_{i}\varepsilon_{i}
, we have
x:=P_{i}/(2\varepsilon_{i})\leq c_{i}/2
and hence we can apply it to (
17
) which readily yields
H_{i}=P_{i}/\tau_{i}\leq 4\varepsilon_{i}
.

Now, assume that
\tau_{i}\in[c_{i}/2,c_{i})
. For
x\in(0,c_{i})
we have

P_{i}\leq\frac{1}{c_{i}-x}\int_{0}^{x}(c_{i}-t)h(t)\,dt+(c_{i}-x)\cdot\frac{1}{c_{i}-x}\int_{x}^{c_{i}}h(t)\,dt\leq\frac{(c_{i}-\tau_{i})P_{i}}{c_{i}-x}+(c_{i}-x)\varepsilon_{i},

and taking
x=c_{i}-P_{i}/(2\varepsilon_{i})\in[c_{i}/2,c_{i})
yields
H_{i}=P_{i}/(c_{i}-\tau_{i})\leq 4\varepsilon_{i}
.
∎

Corollary 2.3
.

Assume the set
\mathcal{I}
is infinite. Then we have

\lim_{\mathcal{I}\ni i\to\infty}\sup_{[a_{i},b_{i}]}|u_{i}^{\prime\prime}|=\lim_{\mathcal{I}\ni i\to\infty}\sup_{[a_{i},b_{i}]}|u^{\prime\prime}|=0,

and hence

\lim_{\mathcal{I}\ni i\to\infty}\sup_{[a_{i},b_{i}]}\big(|u_{i}-u|+|(u_{i}-u)^{\prime}|+|(u_{i}-u)^{\prime\prime}|\big)=0.

(19)

Proof.

Note that
\lim_{\mathcal{I}\ni i\to\infty}(b_{i}-a_{i})=0
. Hence

\lim_{\mathcal{I}\ni i\to\infty}\sup_{[a_{i},b_{i}]}|u^{\prime\prime}|=0
follows from the uniform continuity of

on

and from the fact that
u^{\prime\prime}(a_{i})=u^{\prime\prime}(b_{i})=0
.
Also,
\lim_{\mathcal{I}\ni i\to\infty}\varepsilon_{i}=0
. Indeed, convergence of
\varepsilon_{i}
to zero follows from the uniform convergence (

), the definition (

) of
\varepsilon_{i}
and the fact that
h(a_{i})=h(b_{i})=0

\lim_{\mathcal{I}\ni i\to\infty}\sup_{[a_{i},b_{i}]}|u_{i}^{\prime\prime}|=0
is a consequence of Lemma
2.2
. This proves (

Clearly, (

\lim_{\mathcal{I}\ni i\to\infty}\sup_{[a_{i},b_{i}]}|(u_{i}-u)^{\prime\prime}|=0
. Since
u_{i}(a_{i})-u(a_{i})=0
and
u_{i}^{\prime}(a_{i})-u^{\prime}(a_{i})=0
, (
19
) follows upon integration.
∎

Now, we are ready to make the final modification of the function

We define

Proof.

This is obvious when

Proof of Theorem
1.3
.

Let
\varepsilon_{o}>0
and continuous
\varepsilon:(a,b)\to(0,\infty)
be given. We write

(a,b)=\bigcup_{i=-\infty}^{\infty}[\alpha_{i},\alpha_{i+1}],\qquad\alpha_{i}<\alpha_{i+1}.

Using Lemma
2.1
, we can approximate
f
on

in the Lusin sense by a convex function

so that the measure

|\{x\in(\alpha_{i},\alpha_{i+1}):\,f(x)\neq g_{i}(x)\}|

(21)

is as small as we wish. Then, Lemma
2.5
allows us to glue

to a convex function
g\in C^{2}(a,b)
.
It is important that

and

agrees with
f
and

at the endpoints of the intervals

. This will guarantee that

on

followed by
f
on the interval between

and

(for every

– this is needed if we want to apply Lemma
2.5
.

Making sure that the measure (
21
) is sufficiently small and that

|(\alpha_{i},\alpha_{i+1})\setminus I_{i}|
is sufficiently small, we can guarantee that

and that

|\{x\in[\alpha_{i-1},\alpha_{i+2}]:f(x)\neq g(x)\}|<\min\Big\{\frac{\inf_{t\in[\alpha_{i-1},\alpha_{i+2}]}\varepsilon(t)}{4L_{i}},\alpha_{i}-\alpha_{i-1},\alpha_{i+2}-\alpha_{i+1}\Big\},

(22)
