Unified Statistical Theory of Heat Conduction in Nonuniform Media
Yi Zeng, Jianjun Dong

Abstract
We present a theory of heat conduction that encompasses and extends the classical paradigm defined by Fourier’s law and the Kapitza interfacial conductance model. Derived from the Zwanzig projection operator formalism, our approach introduces a spatiotemporal kernel function of heat conduction, ℤ ↔ ​ ( r → , R → , t ) \overleftrightarrow{\mathbb{Z}}(\vec{r},\vec{R},t) , which governs the spatiotemporal response of the local heat flux in nonuniform media to surrounding temperature gradients while incorporating temporal memory effects. Our statistical analysis shows that memory, nonlocality, and interfacial temperature discontinuities emerge naturally from the microscopic coupling between two classes of irrelevant degrees of freedom: flux-mode and nonlocal-mode variables. Although these irrelevant variables are not directly observable at the macroscopic level, their integrated influence on the heat flux is fully encoded in the kernel function. In the diffusive limit, the kernel recovers and generalizes Fourier’s law by replacing the thermal conductivity with an infinite hierarchy of position-dependent higher-order tensors, κ ↔ ( n ) ​ ( r → ) \overleftrightarrow{\kappa}^{(n)}(\vec{r}) , that capture nanoscale nonlocality. We also outline atomistic strategies for computing the spatiotemporal kernel in various classes of material media, enabling predictive modeling of heat transport in structurally complex and compositionally inhomogeneous systems. These include nanostructured, anisotropic, and interfacial materials where classical local models break down. Finally, the kernel function formalism is extensible to electronic, spin, or magnetic systems, providing a broadly applicable platform for next-generation transport theory at ultrafast and nanoscale regimes.



I
Introduction

Heat conduction in materials has traditionally been described by Fourier’s law, which postulates that the heat flux,
\vec{j}(\vec{r},t)
, arises instantaneously and linearly in response to local temperature gradients,
\vec{\nabla}_{r}T(\vec{r},t)
. This assumption leads to the diffusive evolution of temperature fields in both space and time, governed by the following set of foundational equations
Fourier 1888
;
Carslaw and Jaeger 1959
:

\displaystyle\rho c\,\frac{\partial T(\vec{r},t)}{\partial t}+\vec{\nabla}_{r}\cdot\vec{j}(\vec{r},t)=0,

(1a)

\displaystyle\vec{j}(\vec{r},t)=-\overleftrightarrow{\kappa}_{\text{bulk}}(\vec{r})\cdot\vec{\nabla}_{r}T(\vec{r},t),

(1b)

\displaystyle\overleftrightarrow{\kappa}_{\text{bulk}}=\frac{V}{k_{B}T_{0}^{2}}\langle\vec{j}_{\text{bulk}}(t)\otimes\vec{j}_{\text{bulk}}(0)\rangle_{\text{eq}},

(1c)

where
\rho
is the mass density and
c
is the specific heat capacity per unit mass. Equation (
1c
), known as the Green–Kubo relation
Green 1954
;
Dong et al. 2001
, expresses the bulk thermal conductivity tensor
\overleftrightarrow{\kappa}_{\text{bulk}}
in terms of the time-autocorrelation of the fluctuating bulk heat flux
\vec{j}_{\text{bulk}}
, averaged over an equilibrium ensemble at temperature
T_{0}
. The prefactor involves the Boltzmann constant
k_{\text{B}}
and the system volume
V
, which together normalize the microscopic fluctuations to yield a macroscopic transport coefficient.

Real materials often exhibit spatial variations in structure, composition, and transport properties. Traditional modeling approaches address these inhomogeneities either by assigning continuously varying thermal conductivity fields or by using simplified interface models. A common interface model is the Kapitza conductance framework
Kapitza 1941
, which introduces an effective interfacial thermal conductance,
G_{K}
, by relating the temperature discontinuity
\Delta T
across an abrupt interface to the local heat flux via
j=-G_{K}\Delta T
.

While highly effective for macroscopic and near-equilibrium conditions, classical heat conduction models based on Fourier thermal conductivity (
\kappa
) and Kapitza interfacial thermal conductance (
G_{\text{K}}
) break down in nanoscale materials and ultrafast thermal regimes. A growing body of experimental evidence has revealed pronounced non-Fourier behavior, including wave-like heat propagation (second sound)
Ackerman et al. 1966
;
McNelly et al. 1970
;
Jackson et al. 1970
;
Pohl and Irniger 1976
;
Narayanamurti and Dynes 1972
;
Danil’Chenko et al. 1979
;
Huberman et al. 2019
;
Ding et al. 2022
;
Jeong et al. 2021
;
Beardo et al. 2021a
, quasi-ballistic transport
Maznev et al. 2011
;
Minnich et al. 2011
;
Johnson et al. 2013
;
Johnson et al. 2015
;
Hua et al. 2019
;
Beardo et al. 2021b
, and signatures of phonon Poiseuille flow
Machida et al. 2018
;
Markov et al. 2018
;
Martelli et al. 2018
;
Machida et al. 2020
;
Huang et al. 2023
;
Huang et al. 2024
. These observations highlight the essential roles of temporal memory and spatial nonlocality, fundamentally incompatible with purely diffusive descriptions.

In response to these observations, a variety of generalized transport models have been developed to extend the classical framework. Notably, the Maxwell–Cattaneo–Vernotte (MCV)
Maxwell 2003
;
Cattaneo 1948
;
Vernotte 1958
;
Chester 1963
and Guyer–Krumhansl (GK)
Guyer and Krumhansl 1966a
;
Guyer and Krumhansl 1966b
;
Joseph and Preziosi 1989
equations incorporate finite relaxation times for transient heat flux and higher-order spatial gradients of inhomogeneous flux, respectively, to account for memory and nonlocal effects. While these models formally extend Fourier’s law into the ultrafast and nanoscale regimes, they typically rely on the phenomenological introduction of additional transport parameters, such as flux relaxation time
\tau
, nonlocal length scale
l
, or phonon viscosity
Simoncelli et al. 2020
, to complement classical quantities like thermal conductivity and interfacial conductance.

However, due to the absence of quantitative definitions grounded in microscopic theory, these non-Fourier parameters are often treated as fitting variables rather than as rigorously derived properties. As a result, their predictive capacity remains limited, and empirical calibration is generally required for each specific material system or device geometry.

Microscopically, the phonon Boltzmann transport equation (BTE) provides a quantitative framework for modeling heat conduction from first principles. Numerical solutions of the linearized BTE
Hua and Lindsay 2020
;
Chiloyan et al. 2021
;
Malviya and Ravichandran 2025
have revealed pronounced deviations from Fourier behavior, particularly under cryogenic conditions and in low-dimensional or nanostructured materials. These deviations are often further exacerbated in heterogeneous systems, where spatial variations in structure and composition enhance gradients in local temperature and heat flux fields. Despite its theoretical rigor, BTE-based modeling becomes computationally prohibitive for strongly inhomogeneous phonon systems and may obscure the physical mechanisms underlying non-Fourier thermal transport. As a result, a conceptual and practical divide remains between atomistic simulations and continuum-scale modeling and measurements.

In this work, we perform a first-principles statistical analysis of phonon dynamics to rigorously establish the connection between microscopic degrees of freedom and macroscopic heat transport behavior. Building on the projection-operator formalism
Zwanzig 1960
;
Zwanzig 1961
;
Zwanzig 2001
, we adopt Zwanzig’s framework for irreversible processes, which systematically separates slow thermodynamic variables, such as temperature, from fast
irrelevant
microscopic modes. This separation naturally introduces temporal memory and spatial nonlocality through the coupled dynamics of the
irrelevant
degrees of freedom, components traditionally treated as stochastic noise, but shown here to play a central role in governing heat conduction in nonuniform media.

Our earlier formulation
Crawford et al. 2024
addressed transient, local heat conduction in homogeneous media using a time-domain memory kernel function,
\overleftrightarrow{Z}(t)
. In the present work, we extend this framework to incorporate both spatial nonlocality and material inhomogeneity. Rather than relying on phenomenological extensions of classical transport models, we derive the governing equations directly from microscopic dynamics in terms of a spatiotemporal kernel function,
\overleftrightarrow{\mathbb{Z}}(\vec{r},\vec{R},t)
, which provides a rigorous statistical foundation for capturing temporal memory, spatial nonlocality, and interfacial heat conduction in heterogeneous media across a broad spectrum of transport regimes.

Crucially, the resulting unified theory captures a wide range of non-Fourier phenomena, including second sound and Poiseuille flow, within a single statistical formalism. In appropriate limits, it recovers established models such as Fourier’s law, the empirical MCV equation, and the hydrodynamic GK equation. Moreover, the transport descriptors introduced here can be computed from atomistic simulations, enabling predictive modeling of heat transport in complex, nanoscale, and compositionally nonuniform systems.

II
Statistical Theory of Phonon-Mediated Heat Conduction

II.1
Zwanzig Projection

The nonequilibrium statistical framework adopted in this study, originally proposed by Zwanzig
Zwanzig 1960
;
Zwanzig 1961
, provides a systematic projection-operator approach for modeling irreversible processes. This formalism partitions the full set of microscopic state variables into two distinct categories: (i) a small subset of slowly evolving, coarse-grained
relevant
variables that govern macroscopic transport, and (ii) a large ensemble of rapidly fluctuating
irrelevant
variables that encode microscopic dissipation and thermal noise. Within this framework, the evolution of relevant variables is statistically influenced by the dynamics of irrelevant ones, leading to non-Markovian, memory-dependent behavior as an emergent feature of nonequilibrium statistical mechanics
Zwanzig 2001
. A summary of the Zwanzig projection formalism, using notation consistent with this work, is provided in Appendix
A.1
.

In the context of heat conduction, the temperature field
T(\vec{r},t)
is identified as the primary relevant variable governing macroscopic energy transport. While temperature is rigorously defined in thermodynamic equilibrium, its extension to nonequilibrium systems relies on the assumption of local thermal equilibrium. Experimentally,
T(\vec{r},t)
is typically inferred from spatially and temporally resolved observables—such as thermal expansion, Raman shifts, or optical reflectance—which are calibrated under equilibrium conditions and interpreted as proxies for temperature. Consequently, the validity of
T(\vec{r},t)
as a well-defined thermodynamic field becomes increasingly uncertain in ultrafast or nanoscale regimes, where the assumption of local equilibrium may fail to capture the full complexity of nonequilibrium states. The limitations and implications of this assumption are discussed further in Appendix
A.2
.

The macroscopic heat flux
\vec{j}(\vec{r},t)
is another central quantity in thermal transport, yet it cannot be directly measured. Instead, it is typically inferred from the evolution of the temperature field via the energy conservation relation in Eq. (
1a
). As a result, theories of heat conduction treat
T(\vec{r},t)
as the sole independent relevant variable, while
\vec{j}(\vec{r},t)
is regarded as a dependent macroscopic quantity that emerges from the underlying microscopic dynamics. A complementary transport equation is therefore required to describe how the transient heat flux field
\vec{j}(\vec{r},t)
responds to nonequilibrium conditions in the material medium.

Our statistical analysis begins by considering heat conduction in an infinitely large, homogeneous vibrating lattice, which can be accurately described using the phonon gas model. In this model, thermal excitations are characterized by the occupation numbers of vibrational modes. For convenience, each phonon mode is indexed by a composite label
\alpha=(\vec{q},b)
, where
\vec{q}
denotes the wavevector and
b
indicates the phonon branch. The set of local occupation numbers
\{n_{\alpha}(\vec{r},t)\}
defines the microscopic state of the system, from which macroscopic quantities such as local energy density and heat flux can be constructed as:

\displaystyle e(\vec{r},t)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{V}\sum_{\alpha}n_{\alpha}(\vec{r},t)\hbar\omega_{\alpha},

(2a)

\displaystyle\vec{j}(\vec{r},t)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{V}\sum_{\alpha}n_{\alpha}(\vec{r},t)\hbar\omega_{\alpha}\vec{v}_{\alpha},

(2b)

where
\omega_{\alpha}
and
\vec{v}_{\alpha}
are the angular frequency and group velocity of phonon mode
\alpha
, respectively, and
V
is the system volume.

To analyze microscopic dynamics of a phonon gas, we consider the deviation of phonon occupation numbers from equilibrium:
\delta n_{\alpha}(\vec{r},t)=n_{\alpha}(\vec{r},t)-n^{\mathrm{eq}}_{\alpha}(T_{0})
, where
n^{\mathrm{eq}}_{\alpha}(T_{0})
denotes the equilibrium occupation number of mode
\alpha
at the global equilibrium temperature
T_{0}
. In the linear response regime, the evolution of these deviations is governed by the linearized phonon scattering matrix
\mathbb{L}
, whose elements
L_{\alpha\beta}
are defined in the phonon occupation number representation:

\left.\frac{dn_{\alpha}(\vec{r},t)}{dt}\right|_{\text{relax}}\approx-\sum_{\beta}L_{\alpha\beta}\cdot\frac{\sinh\left(\frac{\hbar\omega_{\beta}}{2k_{\mathrm{B}}T_{0}}\right)}{\sinh\left(\frac{\hbar\omega_{\alpha}}{2k_{\mathrm{B}}T_{0}}\right)}\cdot\left[n_{\beta}(\vec{r},t)-n^{\mathrm{eq}}_{\beta}(T_{0})\right].

(3)

The linearized scattering operator
\mathbb{L}
is a symmetric, positive semi-definite matrix that encodes phonon-mode-specific relaxation rates and inter-phonon-mode couplings, forming the foundation for the eigenmode decomposition central to our theoretical framework. Owing to the symmetry of phonon occupation numbers under wavevector inversion (
\vec{q}\leftrightarrow-\vec{q}
), the eigenmodes of
\mathbb{L}
can be classified by their parity. Odd-parity modes, which are antisymmetric under
\vec{q}
inversion, contribute directly to the net heat flux, whereas even-parity modes represent
\vec{q}
-space symmetric deviations that do not carry net energy current. The complete orthonormal set of eigenvectors includes a single zero-eigenvalue Bose–Einstein mode, along with a hierarchy of even-parity modes
\nu_{\iota}
and odd-parity modes
\mu_{\lambda}
:

\displaystyle\sum_{\alpha}L_{\alpha\beta}c^{1/2}_{\beta}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0,\quad\text{(Bose–Einstein mode)}

(4a)

\displaystyle\sum_{\alpha}L_{\alpha\beta}\nu_{\iota\beta}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{\iota}\nu_{\iota\alpha},\quad\text{(even parity)}

(4b)

\displaystyle\sum_{\alpha}L_{\alpha\beta}\mu_{\lambda\beta}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\gamma_{\lambda}\mu_{\lambda\alpha}.\quad\text{(odd parity)}

(4c)

These eigenmodes serve as a physically meaningful, orthonormal basis to describe the nonequilibrium phonon distribution. The odd-parity modes govern heat flux, while the even-parity modes encode residual deviations from local equilibrium. This basis allows a compact representation of microscopic dynamics and connects directly to macroscopic observables. The parity of each eigenmode is defined by its symmetry under wavevector inversion in reciprocal space. The eigenvectors
\vec{w}
(Bose–Einstein mode),
\{\vec{\nu}_{\iota}\}
(even parity), and
\{\vec{\mu}_{\lambda}\}
(odd parity) form a complete orthonormal basis spanning the space of phonon occupation number fluctuations. The indices
\iota=1,\dots,M_{\mathrm{E}}
and
\lambda=1,\dots,M_{\mathrm{O}}
denote the even and odd parity modes, respectively. Including the zero-eigenvalue Bose–Einstein mode, the completeness condition reads
1+M_{\mathrm{E}}+M_{\mathrm{O}}=N_{\mathrm{mode}}
.

Within the Zwanzig formalism, the Bose–Einstein mode with zero eigenvalue corresponds to the local energy density, with its normalized eigenvector given by
w_{\alpha}=\left(c_{\alpha}/C\right)^{1/2}
, where
C=\sum_{\alpha}c_{\alpha}
denotes the total heat capacity of the phonon gas. The mode amplitude of this Bose–Einstein mode is proportional to the temperature field
T(\vec{r},t)
, which serves as the sole independent relevant variable governing macroscopic heat transport. The eigenvalues
\varepsilon_{\iota}>0
and
\gamma_{\lambda}\geq 0
characterize the relaxation rates of the irrelevant variables associated with the even- and odd-parity modes, respectively.

Using this orthonormal basis, the nonequilibirum deviation of the phonon occupation number from equilibrium can be expressed as:

\begin{split}n_{\alpha}(\vec{r},t)=n^{\mathrm{eq}}_{\alpha}(T_{0})+\frac{\left(\frac{c_{\alpha}}{k_{\mathrm{B}}}\right)^{1/2}\frac{T(\vec{r},t)-T_{0}}{T_{0}}+\sum_{\iota=1}^{M_{\mathrm{E}}}\nu_{\iota\alpha}\theta_{\iota}(\vec{r},t)+\sum_{\lambda=1}^{M_{\mathrm{O}}}\mu_{\lambda\alpha}\eta_{\lambda}(\vec{r},t)}{2\sinh\left(\frac{\hbar\omega_{\alpha}}{2k_{\mathrm{B}}T_{0}}\right)}.\end{split}

(5)

Conversely, the Zwanzig variables of phonon heat conduction,
T(\vec{r},t)
,
\theta_{\iota}(\vec{r},t)
, and
\eta_{\lambda}(\vec{r},t)
, can be projected from
n_{\alpha}(\vec{r},t)
using:

\displaystyle T(\vec{r},t)=

\displaystyle T_{0}+\frac{1}{C}\sum_{\alpha=1}^{N_{\mathrm{mode}}}\left[n_{\alpha}(\vec{r},t)-n^{\mathrm{eq}}_{\alpha}(T_{0})\right]\cdot\hbar\omega_{\alpha},

(6a)

\displaystyle\theta_{\iota}(\vec{r},t)=

\displaystyle\sum_{\alpha=1}^{N_{\mathrm{mode}}}2\sinh\left(\frac{\hbar\omega_{\alpha}}{2k_{\mathrm{B}}T_{0}}\right)\cdot\left[n_{\alpha}(\vec{r},t)-n^{\mathrm{eq}}_{\alpha}(T_{0})\right]\cdot\nu_{\iota\alpha},

(6b)

\displaystyle\eta_{\lambda}(\vec{r},t)=

\displaystyle\sum_{\alpha=1}^{N_{\mathrm{mode}}}2\sinh\left(\frac{\hbar\omega_{\alpha}}{2k_{\mathrm{B}}T_{0}}\right)\cdot\left[n_{\alpha}(\vec{r},t)-n^{\mathrm{eq}}_{\alpha}(T_{0})\right]\cdot\mu_{\lambda\alpha}.

(6c)

The variables
\eta_{\lambda}
and
\theta_{\iota}
represent projections of the phonon distribution onto the odd- and even-parity eigenmodes of the scattering operator, respectively.

The odd-parity variables
\eta_{\lambda}
encode the microscopic fluctuations associated with net energy flow and are responsible for carrying the macroscopic heat flux. Specifically, the total heat flux can be reconstructed as a linear superposition of these flux-mode amplitudes:

\vec{j}(\vec{r},t)=\frac{1}{V}\sum_{\lambda=1}^{M_{\text{O}}}\vec{j}_{\lambda}(\vec{r},t)=\frac{1}{V}\sum_{\lambda=1}^{M_{\text{O}}}\vec{\Lambda}(\lambda)\cdot\eta_{\lambda}(\vec{r},t),

(7)

where the vector
\vec{\Lambda}(\lambda)
is defined as

\vec{\Lambda}(\lambda)=\sum_{\alpha=1}^{N_{\mathrm{mode}}}\frac{\mu_{\lambda\alpha}\hbar\omega_{\alpha}\vec{v}_{\alpha}}{2\sinh(\hbar\omega_{\alpha}/2k_{\text{B}}T_{0})}=k_{\text{B}}^{1/2}T_{0}\sum_{\alpha=1}^{N_{\mathrm{mode}}}c_{\alpha}^{1/2}\mu_{\lambda\alpha}\vec{v}_{\alpha},

(8)

and quantifies the contribution of the odd-parity mode
\eta_{\lambda}
to the corresponding modal heat flux
\vec{j}_{\lambda}
.

The even-parity eigenmodes
\theta_{\iota}
are orthogonal to both the equilibrium Bose–Einstein mode and the flux-carrying odd-parity modes. While they do not directly contribute to the macroscopic heat flux
\vec{j}(\vec{r},t)
, they play a crucial indirect role by mediating the coupling between different flux modes in space and time. These variables encode residual deviations from local equilibrium and govern how microscopic memory and spatial correlations influence the evolution of the heat flux. Their rapid relaxation, characterized by eigenvalues
\varepsilon_{\iota}>0
, modulates the dynamic response of
\eta_{\lambda}
and enables nonlocal effects to emerge systematically in the reduced-order transport equations. Together,
\theta_{\iota}
and
\eta_{\lambda}
define the irreversibly relaxing subspace that underpins a unified theory of heat conduction beyond the classical paradigm.

II.2
Zwanzig Dynamics

Within the Zwanzig projection formalism, we construct the mode-coupled dynamics by expressing the projected variables in terms of the eigenmodes of the scattering operator
\mathbb{L}
and derive a hierarchy of transport equations that underpins our theory. This formulation begins with the evolution equation for the mode-resolved phonon occupation numbers
n_{\alpha}(\vec{r},t)
, governed by the linearized Boltzmann transport equation (td-phBTE):

\frac{\partial n_{\alpha}(\vec{r},t)}{\partial t}+\vec{v}_{\alpha}(\vec{r})\cdot\vec{\nabla}_{r}n_{\alpha}(\vec{r},t)=-\sum_{\beta=1}^{N_{\mathrm{mode}}}L_{\alpha\beta}(\vec{r})\frac{\sinh(\hbar\omega_{\beta}(\vec{r})/2k_{\text{B}}T_{0})}{\sinh(\hbar\omega_{\alpha}(\vec{r})/2k_{\text{B}}T_{0})}\left(n_{\beta}(\vec{r},t)-n^{\mathrm{eq}}_{\beta}(T_{0})\right).

(9)

As detailed in Appendix
B
, Eq. (
9
) can be reformulated as a coupled system of equations involving three sets of variables:

•

the independent
relevant
variable
T(\vec{r},t)
,

•

the flux-carrying odd-parity
irrelevant
variables
\eta_{\lambda}(\vec{r},t)
, and

•

the nonlocal even-parity
irrelevant
variables
\theta_{\iota}(\vec{r},t)
.

To demonstrate how the mode-coupled transport equations specialize under different physical conditions, we consider two limiting cases: perfectly homogeneous phonon gases and spatially varying inhomogeneous systems.

II.2.1
Homogeneous Phonon Gases

We begin by considering an infinitely large and defect-free crystal in which phonon–phonon scattering arises solely from lattice anharmonicity. As a result, all phonon properties, including mode frequency
\omega
, group velocity
\vec{v}
, and the phonon scattering matrix
\mathbb{L}
, are spatially homogeneous and independent of position
\vec{r}
. Under these conditions, the coupled system of equations takes the following form:

\displaystyle C\cdot\frac{\partial T(\vec{r},t)}{\partial t}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\sum_{\lambda=1}^{M_{\text{O}}}\vec{\Lambda}(\lambda)\cdot\vec{\nabla}_{r}\eta_{\lambda}(\vec{r},t),

(10a)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\eta_{\lambda}(\vec{r},t)}{\partial t}+\gamma_{\lambda}\eta_{\lambda}(\vec{r},t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{k_{\text{B}}T_{0}^{2}}\vec{\Lambda}(\lambda)\cdot\vec{\nabla}_{r}T(\vec{r},t)-\sum_{\iota=1}^{M_{\text{E}}}\vec{\Pi}(\lambda,\iota)\cdot\vec{\nabla}_{r}\theta_{\iota}(\vec{r},t),

(10b)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\theta_{\iota}(\vec{r},t)}{\partial t}+\varepsilon_{\iota}\theta_{\iota}(\vec{r},t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\sum_{\lambda=1}^{M_{\text{O}}}\vec{\Pi}(\lambda,\iota)\cdot\vec{\nabla}_{r}\eta_{\lambda}(\vec{r},t).

(10c)

Here, Eq. (
10a
) corresponds directly to the energy conservation relation given in Eq. (
1a
), using the definition of the heat flux in terms of the flux-mode variables as introduced in Eq. (
7
). The gradient terms in Eqs. (
10b
) and (
10c
) couple irrelevant variables of opposite parity, with the coupling coefficient between the
\lambda
-th flux mode and the
\iota
-th nonlocal mode defined as:

\vec{\Pi}(\lambda,\iota)\equiv\sum_{\alpha=1}^{N_{\mathrm{mode}}}\mu_{\lambda\alpha}\nu_{\iota\alpha}\vec{v}_{\alpha}.

(11)

where
\mu_{\lambda\alpha}
and
\nu_{\iota\alpha}
are the components of the eigenvectors of the operator
\mathcal{L}
corresponding to the flux and nonlocal modes, respectively.

Although structurally similar to the mode-resolved phonon transport equations developed by Hua and Lindsay
Hua and Lindsay 2020
, our formulation is derived entirely within the Zwanzig projection framework, yielding a more transparent physical interpretation. Unlike reciprocal-space approaches that rely on Fourier transformation, we construct our solution directly in real space and time, preserving spatial inhomogeneity and enabling hierarchical solution strategies based on modal relaxation times and spatial derivatives. As detailed in Appendix
B
, the flux-mode and nonlocal-mode variables,
\eta_{\lambda}
and
\theta_{\iota}

\displaystyle\eta_{\lambda}(\vec{r},t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\int_{0}^{\infty}dt_{1}\frac{e^{-\gamma_{\lambda}t_{1}}}{k_{\text{B}}T_{0}^{2}}\vec{\Lambda}(\lambda)\cdot\vec{\nabla}_{r}T(\vec{r},t-t_{1})

(12a)

\displaystyle-\int_{0}^{\infty}dt_{1}\,e^{-\gamma_{\lambda}t_{1}}\sum_{\iota=1}^{M_{\text{E}}}\vec{\Pi}(\lambda,\iota)\cdot\vec{\nabla}_{r}\theta_{\iota}(\vec{r},t-t_{1}),

\displaystyle\theta_{\iota}(\vec{r},t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\int_{0}^{\infty}dt_{1}\,e^{-\varepsilon_{\iota}t_{1}}\sum_{\lambda=1}^{M_{\text{O}}}\vec{\Pi}(\lambda,\iota)\cdot\vec{\nabla}_{r}\eta_{\lambda}(\vec{r},t-t_{1}).

(12b)

These expressions highlight the central structure of the Zwanzig-based theory, in which the temperature field acts as the sole independent relevant variable, while all nonequilibrium flux and nonlocal responses emerge from the time-retarded dynamics of rapidly relaxing modal variables. The convolution structure of Eqs. (
12

To quantify the influence of nonlocal modes,

\begin{split}\eta_{\lambda}(\vec{r},t)&=-\int_{0}^{\infty}dt_{1}\frac{e^{-\gamma_{\lambda}t_{1}}}{k_{\text{B}}T^{2}_{0}}\vec{\Lambda}(\lambda)\cdot\vec{\nabla}_{r}T(\vec{r},t-t_{1})\\

where flux-mode coupling function takes the tensor form:

II.2.2
Inhomogeneous Phonon Gases

Real materials consist of finite-sized lattices with surfaces and may exhibit spatial inhomogeneities in atomic structure and composition. When these variations occur gradually in space, without sharp discontinuities, the conventional phonon gas model can be extended to an inhomogeneous phonon framework in which material properties are position-dependent. This scenario is naturally treated by introducing spatially varying harmonic phonon spectra and phonon scattering matrices into the Zwanzig projection formalism, enabling a direct generalization of the homogeneous theory.

For an inhomogeneous phonon gas, we define a position-dependent phonon dispersion and phonon scattering matrix,
\mathcal{L}(\vec{r})

\eta_{\lambda}(\vec{r},t)
and
\theta_{\iota}(\vec{r},t)

\vec{\Lambda}(\vec{r};\lambda)
, and nonlocal coupling tensors,
\overleftrightarrow{\Pi}(\vec{r};\lambda,\iota)
, based on the local phonon spectra and the eigenmodes of the position-dependent scattering matrix
\mathcal{L}(\vec{r})
, following the formulation introduced in Equations (
8
) and (
11
).

. A key result of this extended formulation is that the iterative solution for the flux-mode variables,

&+\int_{0}^{\infty}dt_{1}e^{-\gamma_{\lambda}(\vec{r})t_{1}}\sum_{\lambda^{\prime}=1}^{M_{\text{O}}}\int_{0}^{\infty}dt_{2}\\
&\quad\biggr[\sum_{I,J=1}^{3}\Phi_{IJ}(\vec{r},t_{2};\lambda,\lambda^{\prime})\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x_{I}\partial x_{J}}\eta_{\lambda^{\prime}}(\vec{r},t-t_{1}-t_{2})\\
&+\sum_{I=1}^{3}\psi_{I}(\vec{r},t_{2};\lambda,\lambda^{\prime})\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{I}}\eta_{\lambda^{\prime}}(\vec{r},t-t_{1}-t_{2})\biggr],\end{split}

(15)

where the first
\vec{r}
-dependent flux-to-flux coupling function,
\overleftrightarrow{\Phi}
, is defined as

\overleftrightarrow{\Phi}(\vec{r},t;\lambda,\lambda^{\prime})=\sum_{\iota=1}^{M_{\text{E}}}\vec{\Pi}(\vec{r};\lambda,\iota)\otimes\vec{\Pi}(\vec{r};\lambda^{\prime},\iota)~e^{-{\varepsilon}_{\iota}(\vec{r})t},

(16)

and corresponds to the spatially varying extension of Eq. (
14
). The second
\vec{r}
-dependent nonlocal flux-to-flux coupling function,
\vec{\psi}
, is given by

\vec{\psi}(\vec{r},t;\lambda,\lambda^{\prime})=\sum_{\iota=1}^{M_{\text{E}}}\sum_{J=1}^{3}\Pi_{J}(\vec{r};\lambda,\iota)\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{J}}\biggr(\vec{\Pi}(\vec{r};\lambda^{\prime},\iota)~e^{-{\varepsilon}_{\iota}(\vec{r})t}\biggr),

(17)

and captures additional spatial coupling arising from material heterogeneity in the mode-coupling parameters. Together, these results provide a consistent and extensible foundation for describing nonlocal, transient heat transport across both ideal and complex material systems. They motivate the introduction of a more compact and general representation in terms of space- and time-resolved conduction kernels, developed next.

III
Reduced Models for Distinct Heat Conduction Regimes

Having developed a general expression for phonon-mediated heat flux that accounts for memory effects, spatial nonlocality, and material inhomogeneity in Eq. (
15
), we now explore its limiting cases. These include the phonon hydrodynamic regime, the local (transient) regime, the classical diffusive limit, and interfacial conduction. In each case, the full spatiotemporal theory can be systematically reduced to yield simpler yet physically accurate transport models. These reduced forms balance theoretical fidelity with practical feasibility, enabling analytical insight and computational tractability.

III.1
Hydrodynamic Regime

The phonon hydrodynamic regime represents an intermediate transport regime between the ballistic and diffusive limits, where momentum-conserving phonon–phonon scattering dominates over resistive processes. These interactions lead to collective phonon behavior and enable phenomena such as second sound and Poiseuille heat flow. A variety of approximate models, most notably the GK equation, have been developed to describe this regime and to account for a broad range of non-Fourier heat conduction phenomena, including heat wave propagation at cryogenic temperatures and ultrafast time-scales, hydrodynamic heat flux profiles at nanoscales, and anomalous diffusion in nanomaterials.

Within our Zwanzig-based formalism, the hydrodynamic regime emerges naturally when a small subset of odd-parity flux modes exhibits exceptionally slow relaxation. These long-lived modes dominate transient transport and must be retained as macroscopically relevant variables. Their dynamics are coupled to a small number of even-parity (nonlocal) modes, which we treat in a quasi-steady approximation due to their rapid decay.

For clarity, we focus on a crystal with cubic symmetry. In this case, we retain only the three lowest-order flux modes and three corresponding nonlocal modes, while all higher-order degrees of freedom are truncated. The retained flux vectors and coupling tensors are defined as
\vec{\Lambda}_{1}=(\Lambda,0,0)
,
\vec{\Lambda}_{2}=(0,\Lambda,0)
,
\vec{\Lambda}_{3}=(0,0,\Lambda)
, and
\Pi_{K}(\lambda,\iota)=\Pi\cdot\delta_{\lambda,K}\cdot\delta_{\iota,K}
. Under these assumptions, the mode-coupled dynamics simplify to:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\eta_{\lambda}(\vec{r},t)}{\partial t}+\gamma\eta_{\lambda}(\vec{r},t)

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle-\frac{\Lambda}{k_{\text{B}}T_{0}^{2}}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{\lambda}}T(\vec{r},t)-\Pi\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{\lambda}}\theta_{\lambda}(\vec{r},t),\quad\lambda=1,2,3

(18a)

\displaystyle\eta_{\lambda}(\vec{r},t)

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle 0,\quad\lambda\geq 4

(18b)

\displaystyle\theta_{\iota}(\vec{r},t)

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle-\varepsilon^{-1}\Pi\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{\iota}}\eta_{\iota}(\vec{r},t),\quad\iota=1,2,3

(18c)

\displaystyle\theta_{\iota}(\vec{r},t)

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle 0,\quad\iota\geq 4.

(18d)

Combining these relations yields a constitutive law formally equivalent to the GK equation:

\vec{j}(\vec{r},t)=-\kappa\,\vec{\nabla}_{r}T(\vec{r},t)-\tau\,\frac{\partial\vec{j}(\vec{r},t)}{\partial t}+l^{2}\nabla^{2}\vec{j}(\vec{r},t),

(19)

where
\kappa=\frac{\Lambda^{2}}{Vk_{B}T_{0}^{2}\gamma}
is the thermal conductivity,
\tau=\gamma^{-1}
is the relaxation time, and
l=\frac{\Pi}{\sqrt{\gamma\varepsilon}}
is the characteristic nonlocal length scale.

The phonon hydrodynamic GK equation treats memory and nonlocality as two distinct non-Fourier effects, with the relaxation time
\tau
quantifying memory (temporal) effects and the characteristic length scale
l
capturing spatial nonlocality. In the limit of negligible nonlocal effects (
l\rightarrow 0
), the GK equation reduces to its temporally nonlocal counterpart, the MCV equation:

\vec{j}(\vec{r},t)=-\kappa\,\vec{\nabla}_{r}T(\vec{r},t)-\tau\,\frac{\partial\vec{j}(\vec{r},t)}{\partial t},

(20)

while in the quasi-steady limit (
\partial\vec{j}/\partial t\approx 0
), it reduces to a nonlocal diffusion equation:

\vec{j}(\vec{r},t)=-\kappa\,\vec{\nabla}_{r}T(\vec{r},t)+l^{2}\nabla^{2}\vec{j}(\vec{r},t),

(21)

highlighting the role of spatially extended interactions in modifying Fourier-like behavior.

Although widely used, hydrodynamic models like the GK equation rest on idealized assumptions. They assume nearly crystalline purity and dominance of momentum-conserving scattering, restricting their applicability to select materials and temperature regimes. In contrast, the present formalism provides a unified foundation that recovers the GK equation as a limiting case and clarifies its microscopic basis in terms of mode-resolved phonon dynamics.

III.2
Local Regime

In many bulk or near-homogeneous materials, the coupling between flux and nonlocal modes is weak. When
\vec{\Pi}(\vec{r};\lambda,\iota)\rightarrow 0
, the general mode-coupled dynamics for
irrelevant
degrees of freedom simplifies to:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\eta_{\lambda}(\vec{r},t)}{\partial t}+\gamma_{\lambda}(\vec{r})\eta_{\lambda}(\vec{r},t)

\displaystyle\approx-\frac{1}{k_{\text{B}}T_{0}^{2}}\vec{\Lambda}(\vec{r};\lambda)\cdot\vec{\nabla}_{r}T(\vec{r},t),

(22a)

\displaystyle\theta_{\iota}(\vec{r},t)

\displaystyle\approx 0.

(22b)

In this flux-nonlocal decoupled regime, the influence of the nonlocal-mode variables
\theta_{\iota}(\vec{r},t)
on the
relevant
temperature field
T(\vec{r},t)
is negligible. This follows directly from the absence of coupling between the
irrelevant
flux modes
\eta_{\lambda}
and
irrelevant
nonlocal modes
\theta_{\iota}
.

We refer to this limit as the regime of
local
heat conduction, characteristic of bulk or nearly homogeneous materials. Our prior work
Crawford et al. 2024
analyzed this regime for an infinite, homogeneous phonon gas, capturing both ultrafast transients and the steady-state limit. Here, we extend that formulation to finite and inhomogeneous systems by introducing a position-resolved Zwanzig conduction function,
\overleftrightarrow{Z}(\vec{r},t)
, defined as:

\overleftrightarrow{Z}(\vec{r},t)=\frac{1}{Vk_{\text{B}}T_{0}^{2}}\sum_{\lambda=1}^{M_{\text{O}}}\vec{\Lambda}(\vec{r};\lambda)\otimes\vec{\Lambda}(\vec{r};\lambda)e^{-\gamma_{\lambda}(\vec{r})t}.

(23)

In this regime, the macroscopic heat flux
\vec{j}(\vec{r},t)
is approximated as

\vec{j}(\vec{r},t)\approx\vec{j}^{\text{local}}(\vec{r},t)=-\int_{0}^{\infty}dt^{\prime}\,\overleftrightarrow{Z}(\vec{r},t^{\prime})\cdot\vec{\nabla}_{r}T(\vec{r},t-t^{\prime}).

(24)

The time dependence of
\overleftrightarrow{Z}(\vec{r},t)
captures intrinsic memory effects associated with ultrafast thermal transport, providing a microscopic foundation for modeling second sound phenomena
Crawford et al. 2024
. A simplified representation of this kernel function is to approximate it as a single-exponential decay in the time domain:

\overleftrightarrow{Z}(\vec{r},t)\approx\overleftrightarrow{Z}(\vec{r},0)\,e^{-t/\tau(\vec{r})},

(25)

which leads to the MCV equation form of the constitutive relation:

\vec{j}(\vec{r},t)=-\overleftrightarrow{\kappa}(\vec{r})\cdot\vec{\nabla}_{r}T(\vec{r},t)-\tau(\vec{r})\,\frac{\partial\vec{j}(\vec{r},t)}{\partial t},

(26)

where the local thermal conductivity tensor is given by
\overleftrightarrow{\kappa}(\vec{r})=\tau(\vec{r})\,\overleftrightarrow{Z}(\vec{r},0)
.

In the steady-state limit, Eq. (
24
) reduces to the conventional Fourier’s law, as expressed in Eq. (
1b
), where the bulk thermal conductivity tensor
\overleftrightarrow{\kappa}(\vec{r})
is given by:

\overleftrightarrow{\kappa}(\vec{r})=\int_{0}^{\infty}dt\,\overleftrightarrow{Z}(\vec{r},t)=\frac{1}{Vk_{\text{B}}T_{0}^{2}}\sum_{\lambda=1}^{M_{\text{O}}}\vec{\Lambda}(\vec{r};\lambda)\otimes\vec{\Lambda}(\vec{r};\lambda)\gamma_{\lambda}^{-1}(\vec{r}).

(27)

The commonly observed size dependence of effective thermal conductivity in nanomaterials can be understood as a consequence of spatial variation in the Zwanzig conduction function
\overleftrightarrow{Z}(\vec{r},t)
, which arises from the position dependence of the phonon spectra and scattering matrix
\mathcal{L}(\vec{r})
. In such systems, structural inhomogeneity, boundary conditions, or geometric confinement may induce spatial variation in the local thermal conductivity tensor
\overleftrightarrow{\kappa}(\vec{r})
, encoded through
\gamma_{\lambda}(\vec{r})
and
\vec{\Lambda}(\vec{r};\lambda)
. A particularly notable example is Poiseuille heat flow
Sussmann and Thellung 1963
, characterized by a parabolic flux profile and commonly attributed to nonlocal thermal transport. However, as suggested by Eqs. (
1b
) and (
27
), such behavior can also emerge from spatially varying local transport properties alone. This underscores the need to distinguish intrinsic spatial nonlocality from nonlocal effects that arise due to spatial inhomogeneity, particularly in nanoscale and nonuniform materials.

III.3
Diffusive Limit

The classical theory of heat conduction applies in the diffusive limit, under the assumptions of bulk and quasi-homogeneous materials operating at quasi-steady-state conditions. In this regime, heat transport is well described by Fourier’s law, Eq. (
1b
), in which the bulk thermal conductivity tensor
\overleftrightarrow{\kappa}
encapsulates all internal heat dissipation mechanisms. However, at an abrupt material interface, the continuum framework becomes insufficient and must be augmented by an interfacial boundary condition that captures cross-interface heat transfer. A widely used macroscopic formalism introduces the Kapitza (thermal boundary) conductance,
G_{\mathrm{K}}
, through the relation
j_{\mathrm{interface}}=-G_{\mathrm{K}}\Delta T
, where
\Delta T
is the finite temperature discontinuity across the interface.

In the remainder of this subsection, we examine heat conduction in the diffusion limit under steady-state conditions, while incorporating more realistic features such as spatial inhomogeneity and nanoscale nonlocal effects. Starting from the general transient Zwanzig equations, we derive a reduced steady-state formulation for the coupled-mode dynamics in inhomogeneous systems:

\begin{cases}\eta_{\lambda}(\vec{r})\approx-\frac{\gamma_{\lambda}^{-1}(\vec{r})}{k_{\text{B}}T_{0}^{2}}\vec{\Lambda}(\vec{r};\lambda)\cdot\vec{\nabla}_{r}T(\vec{r})-\gamma_{\lambda}^{-1}(\vec{r})\sum_{\iota=1}^{M_{\text{E}}}\vec{\Pi}(\vec{r};\lambda,\iota)\cdot\vec{\nabla}_{r}\theta_{\iota}(\vec{r}),\\
\quad\\
\theta_{\iota}(\vec{r})\approx-\varepsilon_{\iota}^{-1}(\vec{r})\sum_{\lambda=1}^{M_{\text{O}}}\vec{\Pi}(\vec{r};\lambda,\iota)\cdot\vec{\nabla}_{r}\eta_{\lambda}(\vec{r}),\end{cases}

(28)

which can be equivalently rewritten in terms of
\eta_{\lambda}(\vec{r})
only:

\begin{split}\eta_{\lambda}(\vec{r})\approx&-\frac{\gamma_{\lambda}^{-1}(\vec{r})}{k_{\text{B}}T_{0}^{2}}\vec{\Lambda}(\vec{r};\lambda)\cdot\vec{\nabla}_{r}T(\vec{r})\\
&+\gamma_{\lambda}^{-1}(\vec{r})\sum_{\lambda^{\prime}=1}^{M_{\text{O}}}\sum_{\iota=1}^{M_{\text{E}}}\vec{\Pi}(\vec{r};\lambda,\iota)\cdot\vec{\nabla}_{r}\biggr[\varepsilon_{\iota}^{-1}(\vec{r})\vec{\Pi}(\vec{r};\lambda^{\prime},\iota)\cdot\vec{\nabla}_{r}\eta_{\lambda^{\prime}}(\vec{r})\biggr].\end{split}

(29)

III.3.1
Hierarchy of Nonlocality

Equation (
28
) reveals an intrinsic hierarchical structure in the mode-resolved dynamics of
\eta_{\lambda}(\vec{r},t)
, which admits an iterative expansion. A detailed mathematical derivation of this expansion is provided in Appendix
B.4
.

The zeroth-order solution,
\eta^{(0)}_{\lambda}(\vec{r})
, is given by

\eta^{(0)}_{\lambda}(\vec{r})=-\frac{\gamma_{\lambda}^{-1}(\vec{r})}{k_{B}T_{0}^{2}}\vec{\Lambda}(\vec{r};\lambda)\cdot\vec{\nabla}_{r}T(\vec{r}),

(30)

and corresponds to the local conduction regime. It recovers the conventional form of Fourier’s law given in Eq. (
1b
), with the thermal conductivity
\kappa
defined as in Eq. (
27
).

Correspondingly, all higher-order correction terms,
\Delta\eta^{(1)}_{\lambda},\Delta\eta^{(2)}_{\lambda},\dots
, represent nonlocal contributions to the heat flux that are absent in the classical Fourier description. These corrections can be systematically computed from the microscopic inputs, including the harmonic phonon spectra and the local phonon scattering properties. Specifically, nonlocality is governed by mode-specific relaxation rates,
\gamma_{\lambda}
and
\varepsilon_{\iota}
, the mode–flux coupling vectors,
\vec{\Lambda}
, and the flux–nonlocal mode coupling tensors,
\vec{\Pi}
. As an example, substituting the zeroth-order solution from Eq. (
30
) into the second term on the right-hand side of Eq. (
29
) yields the first-order correction
\Delta\eta^{(1)}_{\lambda}
:

\begin{split}\Delta\eta^{(1)}_{\lambda}(\vec{r})=-\frac{\gamma_{\lambda}^{-1}(\vec{r})}{k_{B}T^{2}_{0}}\biggr[&\delta\vec{c}^{(1)}(\vec{r};\lambda)\cdot\vec{\nabla}_{r}T(\vec{r})+\sum_{J_{1}=1}^{3}\delta\vec{c}^{(1)}(\vec{r};\lambda,J_{1})\cdot\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{J_{1}}}\vec{\nabla}_{r}T(\vec{r})\\
&+\sum_{J_{1},J_{2}=1}^{3}\delta\vec{c}^{(1)}(\vec{r};\lambda,J_{1},J_{2})\cdot\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x_{J_{1}}\partial x_{J_{2}}}\vec{\nabla}_{r}T(\vec{r})\biggr],\end{split}

(31)

where

\displaystyle\delta\vec{c}^{(1)}(\vec{r};\lambda)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\lambda^{\prime}=1}^{M_{\text{O}}}\sum_{\iota=1}^{M_{\text{E}}}\big(\vec{\Pi}(\vec{r};\lambda,\iota)\cdot\vec{\nabla}_{r}\big)\big[\varepsilon_{\iota}^{-1}(\vec{r})\gamma_{\lambda^{\prime}}^{-1}(\vec{r})\big(\vec{\Pi}(\vec{r};\lambda^{\prime},\iota)\cdot\vec{\nabla}_{r}\big)\vec{\Lambda}(\vec{r};\lambda^{\prime})\big],

(32a)

\displaystyle\delta\vec{c}^{(1)}(\vec{r};\lambda,J_{1})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\lambda^{\prime}=1}^{M_{\text{O}}}\sum_{\iota=1}^{M_{\text{E}}}\big[\big(\vec{\Pi}(\vec{r};\lambda,\iota)\cdot\vec{\nabla}_{r}\big)~\Pi_{J_{1}}(\vec{r};\lambda^{\prime},\iota)\varepsilon_{\iota}^{-1}(\vec{r})\gamma_{\lambda^{\prime}}^{-1}(\vec{r})\vec{\Lambda}(\vec{r};\lambda^{\prime})\big]

(32b)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\big[\big(\vec{\Pi}(\vec{r};\lambda,\iota)\cdot\vec{\nabla}_{r}\big)\Pi_{J_{1}}(\vec{r};\lambda^{\prime},\iota)\big]\varepsilon_{\iota}^{-1}(\vec{r})\gamma_{\lambda^{\prime}}^{-1}(\vec{r})\vec{\Lambda}(\vec{r};\lambda^{\prime}),

\displaystyle\delta\vec{c}^{(1)}(\vec{r};\lambda,J_{1},J_{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\lambda^{\prime}=1}^{M_{\text{O}}}\sum_{\iota=1}^{M_{\text{E}}}\Pi_{J_{1}}(\vec{r};\lambda,\iota)\Pi_{J_{2}}(\vec{r};\lambda^{\prime},\iota)\varepsilon_{\iota}^{-1}(\vec{r})\gamma_{\lambda^{\prime}}^{-1}(\vec{r})\vec{\Lambda}(\vec{r};\lambda^{\prime}).

(32c)

Specifically, the first-order correction term
\Delta\eta^{(1)}_{\lambda}
in Eq. (
31
) reveals that nonlocal heat conduction arises from flux responses to higher-order spatial derivatives of the local temperature gradient field,
\vec{\nabla}_{r}T(\vec{r})
. The leading-order nonlocal corrections involve second derivatives of the form
\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x_{J_{1}}\partial x_{J_{2}}}\vec{\nabla}_{r}T(\vec{r})
, whose corresponding coefficients
\delta\vec{c}^{(1)}(\vec{r};\lambda,J_{1},J_{2})
, defined in Eq. (
32c
), depend on weighted products of the flux–nonlocal mode coupling tensors
\vec{\Pi}
and the inverse relaxation rates. These terms reflect spatial coupling between flux-carrying and nonlocal modes, and their magnitude becomes appreciable when both types of modes are long-lived and strongly coupled. Interestingly, the lower-order coefficients
\delta\vec{c}^{(1)}(\vec{r};\lambda)
and
\delta\vec{c}^{(1)}(\vec{r};\lambda,J_{1})
, given in Eqs. (
32a
) and (
32b
), respectively, vanish in homogeneous media. This observation highlights that nonlocality and spatial inhomogeneity are not independent phenomena but instead arise from a common microscopic mechanism—the coupling between slowly relaxing flux and nonlocal modes.

Similarly, all higher-order correction terms
\Delta\eta^{(n)}_{\lambda}
can be expressed as a series involving spatial derivatives of the temperature field up to order
2n
, i.e., terms of the form
\frac{\partial^{(2n)}}{\partial x_{J_{1}}\partial x_{J_{2}}\dots\partial x_{J_{2n}}}\vec{\nabla}_{r}T(\vec{r})
. Consequently, the steady-state heat flux can be written as:

\vec{j}(\vec{r})=-\sum_{n_{1}=0}^{\infty}\sum_{n_{2}=0}^{\infty}\sum_{n_{3}=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n_{1}!n_{2}!n_{3}!}\overleftrightarrow{\kappa}^{(n_{1}+n_{2}+n_{3})}(\vec{r};n_{1},n_{2},n_{3})\cdot\frac{\partial^{n_{1}+n_{2}+n_{3}}}{\partial^{n_{1}}x\partial^{n_{2}}y\partial^{n_{3}}z}\vec{\nabla}_{r}T(\vec{r}),

(33)

where
\overleftrightarrow{\kappa}^{(n_{1}+n_{2}+n_{3})}(\vec{r};n_{1},n_{2},n_{3})
are referred to in this work as generalized higher-order thermal conductivity tensors.

Within this formalism, all orders of these generalized conductivity tensors for a phonon gas can, in principle, be computed from microscopic phonon properties, given knowledge of the position-dependent phonon dispersion relations and phonon scattering matrix. However, in practical settings, it is often unnecessary to retain the complete series. For many materials and geometries, a finite truncation captures the essential physics while significantly reducing computational cost. The appropriate truncation order depends on the strength of intrinsic nonlocality, the required numerical accuracy, and the spatial resolution of interest in simulations or experiments.

We further demonstrate that in homogeneous media, the generalized thermal conductivity tensors become spatially invariant, i.e., independent of
\vec{r}
, and all odd-order tensors vanish due to symmetry. For isotropic media, all
n
-th order generalized thermal conductivity tensors
\kappa^{n}(n_{1},n_{2},n_{3})
. Accordingly, the steady-state heat flux reduces to:

\begin{split}&\vec{j}_{\text{iso}}(\vec{r})=-{\kappa}_{\text{iso}}^{(0)}\vec{\nabla}_{r}T(\vec{r})-\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\left(\frac{1}{(2n)!}\kappa_{\text{iso}}^{(2n)}\nabla^{2n}_{r}\vec{\nabla}_{r}T(\vec{r})\right)\\
&=-{\kappa}_{\text{iso}}^{(0)}\vec{\nabla}_{r}T(\vec{r})+\frac{\kappa_{\text{iso}}^{(2)}}{2\kappa_{\text{iso}}^{(0)}}\nabla^{2}_{r}\left(-\kappa_{\text{iso}}^{(0)}\vec{\nabla}_{r}T(\vec{r})-\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{2}{(2n+2)!}\frac{\kappa_{\text{iso}}^{(2n+2)}\kappa_{\text{iso}}^{(0)}}{\kappa_{\text{iso}}^{(2)}}\nabla_{r}^{2n}\vec{\nabla}_{r}T(\vec{r})\right)\\
&\approx-{\kappa}_{\text{iso}}^{(0)}\vec{\nabla}_{r}T(\vec{r})+l^{2}\nabla^{2}_{r}\vec{j}_{\text{iso}}(\vec{r}),\end{split}

(34)

where the characteristic length
l
is defined as
l=\sqrt{\kappa_{\text{iso}}^{(2)}/(2\kappa_{\text{iso}}^{(0)})}
. The difference between the two infinite series, namely
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\left(\frac{1}{(2n)!}\kappa_{\text{iso}}^{(2n)}\nabla^{2n}_{r}\vec{\nabla}_{r}T(\vec{r})\right)
and
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\left(\frac{2}{2(n+1)!}\frac{\kappa_{\text{iso}}^{(2n+2)}\kappa_{\text{iso}}^{(0)}}{\kappa_{\text{iso}}^{(2)}}\nabla_{r}^{2n}\vec{\nabla}_{r}T(\vec{r})\right)
, is assumed to be negligible compared to the leading term
{\kappa}^{(0)}\vec{\nabla}_{r}T(\vec{r})
, justifying the approximation in the last line.

A comparison between Eqs. (
21
) and (
34
) reveals that two distinct representations of nonlocal heat conduction, namely the hydrodynamic form based on the
\nabla^{2}_{r}\vec{j}
correction and the generalized formulation involving higher-order thermal conductivity tensors
\overleftrightarrow{\kappa^{(n)}}
, are semi-quantitatively consistent. However, the hydrodynamic representation (Eqs. (
21
) and (
34
)) should be regarded as an approximation to the full tensorial expansion (Eq. (
33
)).

The concept of an infinite hierarchy of generalized thermal conductivity tensors, as defined in Eq. (
33
), aligns closely with the original notion of nonlocal thermal conductivity pioneered by Mahan and Claro
Mahan and Claro 1988
. However, their formulation was restricted to homogeneous phonon systems and focused primarily on the lowest-order correction term,
\overleftrightarrow{\kappa}^{(2)}
, which characterizes nonlocal heat flux contributions arising from the second-order spatial derivatives of the temperature gradient field,
\vec{\nabla}_{r}T(\vec{r},t)
. Additionally, their work proposed that the magnitude of nonlocal effects scales with the phonon mean free path, which is a heuristic assumption that has been widely adopted in subsequent discussions of nonlocal transport, often without rigorous justification.

In contrast, our analysis is grounded in a first-principles statistical framework that avoids phenomenological assumptions. In particular, Eq. (
31
) reveals that the generalized conductivity tensors
\overleftrightarrow{\kappa}^{(n)}
are not simply functions of phonon mean free paths, but depend intricately on microscopic transport properties. Specifically, these tensors are directly proportional to the fundamental flux–nonlocal-mode coupling coefficients,
\vec{\Pi}
, and inversely proportional to the relaxation rates
\gamma^{-1}
and
\varepsilon^{-1}
.

The general formalism expressed in Eq. (
33
) further indicates that this framework, based on an infinite set of generalized thermal conductivity tensors, is applicable to a wide range of systems beyond phonon-mediated transport, thereby providing a unified approach for characterizing spatially nonlocal thermal phenomena across diverse material platforms.

III.3.2
Kernel Function Representation

While Eq. (
33
) provides a general and complete mathematical description of heat conduction in the diffusive limit by using macroscopically measurable generalized transport coefficients, its infinite series form is often cumbersome to implement numerically. To address this, we reformulate the theory using a more compact but mathematically equivalent representation, using a spatial kernel function formalism to reformulate Eq. (
33
) as

\vec{j}(\vec{r})=-\int_{\mathbb{V}}d^{3}\vec{R}\overleftrightarrow{Z}_{D}(\vec{r},\vec{R})\cdot\vec{\nabla}_{r}T(\vec{R}),

(35)

where
\overleftrightarrow{Z}_{D}(\vec{r},\vec{R})
, typically peak at
\vec{r}
and decays as
\vec{R}
moves away from
\vec{r}
. The generalized higher order thermal conductivity tensors in Eq. (
33
) are simply the spatial-moments of the kernel function,
\overleftrightarrow{Z}_{D}(\vec{r},\vec{R})
:

\overleftrightarrow{\kappa}^{(n_{1}+n_{2}+n_{3})}(\vec{r};n_{1},n_{2},n_{3})=-\int_{\mathbb{V}}d^{3}\vec{R}\overleftrightarrow{Z}_{D}(\vec{r},\vec{R}){(\delta x)}^{n_{1}}{(\delta y)}^{n_{2}}{(\delta z)}^{n_{3}}.

(36)

Further details are provided in Appendix
B.5
. The kernel function formalism offers a compact alternative to the generalized tensorial hierarchy by eliminating the need for explicit truncation of higher-order terms. Rather than computing the full series of generalized thermal conductivity tensors, the key physics can be captured by approximating the spatial heat conduction kernel in the diffusive limit,
\overleftrightarrow{Z}_{D}(\vec{r},\vec{R})
, using simplified functional forms with a small number of physically meaningful parameters. These kernels encapsulate the complex microscopic behavior of phonon transport and enable efficient modeling of spatially nonlocal effects. As such, the formalism establishes a new paradigm for both numerical evaluation of transport properties and interpretation of experimental observations in nanoscale regimes.

As an illustrate example, we consider a simple model of a homogeneous and isotropic kernel function
\overleftrightarrow{Z}_{D}(\vec{r},\vec{R})
as:

Z_{D}(\vec{r},\vec{R})=\frac{\kappa}{{(\sqrt{2\pi}a)}^{3}}e^{-\frac{|\vec{r}-\vec{R}|^{2}}{2a^{2}}},

(37)

where
\kappa
denotes the conventional lattice thermal conductivity, and
a
represents a characteristic length scale of the medium. Higher-order generalized thermal conductivity coefficients can be computed using Eq. (
36
). For instance, in isotropic media, the second-order components satisfy
\kappa^{(2)}(2,0,0)=\kappa^{(2)}(0,2,0)=\kappa^{(2)}(0,0,2)=\kappa a^{2}
. According to Eq. (
34
), the characteristic length
a
used in Eq. (
37
) corresponds to
\sqrt{2}\,l
, where
l
is the nonlocal length appearing in the hydrodynamic GK equation.

Spatial kernel functions have appeared previously in phenomenological models of nonlocal heat conduction, notably in the works of Sobolev
Sobolev 1994
and Allen
Alvarez 2018
. In particular, Allen introduced a conceptual example to interpret the nonlinear temperature profiles ubiquitously observed in nonequilibrium molecular dynamics (MD) simulations, especially near localized heat sources and sinks, using hypothesized spatial conduction kernels. However, most prior studies treated these kernel functions as empirical fitting tools for experimental or simulation data, without establishing a clear connection to microscopic theory. Notably, earlier kernel-based models often echoed the notion introduced by Mahan and Claro
Mahan and Claro 1988
, suggesting that the characteristic length scale of spatial kernels correlates qualitatively with the phonon mean free path. In many cases, the kernel functions were applied to homogeneous media under the assumption of translational invariance, such that
\overleftrightarrow{Z}_{D}(\vec{r},\vec{R})
depends only on the relative displacement
\vec{R}-\vec{r}
. This allowed the use of Fourier transforms to represent the kernels in reciprocal
\vec{k}
-space, further simplifying the analysis under the assumption of spatial homogeneity.

Grounded in microscopic statistical analysis, our analysis demonstrates the consistency of three representations of spatial nonlocality in the diffusive limit: (i) an approximate form involving second-order spatial derivatives of the heat flux,
\nabla^{2}\vec{j}
, as expressed in Eq. (
21
); (ii) a systematic series expansion in terms of generalized higher-order thermal conductivity tensors,
\overleftrightarrow{\kappa}^{(n)}
, as shown in Eq. (
33
); and (iii) an integral formulation using a spatial heat conduction kernel,
\overleftrightarrow{Z}_{D}(\vec{r},\vec{R})
, given in Eq. (
35
). While the first representation provides a simplified hydrodynamic approximation, the latter two offer mathematically rigorous and broadly applicable formulations that accurately capture spatial nonlocality, thereby establishing a robust framework for modeling nanoscale and spatially heterogeneous thermal transport. Importantly, our analysis provides a microscopic foundation for numerically evaluating the full series of generalized thermal conductivity tensors and links them to the spatial kernel function through well-defined mathematical moment relations.

III.3.3
Interfacial Heat Conduction

An important feature of the spatial kernel function formalism presented in Eq. (
35

(38)

IV
Unified Theory of Heat Conduction in Nonuniform Media

IV.1
General Transport Equation

As detailed in Appendix
B.5
, statistical analysis of inhomogeneous phonon gases reveals that temporal memory and spatial nonlocality in the macroscopic heat flux,
\vec{j}(\vec{r},t)
, can be rigorously described by a generalized constitutive law:

\begin{split}\vec{j}(\vec{r},t)=-&\sum_{n_{1}=0}^{\infty}\sum_{n_{2}=0}^{\infty}\sum_{n_{3}=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{(n_{1})!(n_{2})!(n_{3})!}\int_{0}^{\infty}dt^{\prime}\overleftrightarrow{Z}^{(n_{1}+n_{2}+n_{3})}(\vec{r},t^{\prime};n_{1},n_{2},n_{3})\\
&\cdot\frac{\partial^{(n_{1}+n_{2}+n_{3})}}{\partial^{n_{1}}x\partial^{n_{2}}y\partial^{n_{3}}z}\vec{\nabla}_{r}T(\vec{r},t-t^{\prime}).\end{split}

(42)

Here
\overleftrightarrow{Z}^{(n)}(\vec{r},t^{\prime};n_{1},n_{2},n_{3})
are single-time convolution kernels encapsulating higher-order memory and nonlocal effects. Derivation details are provided in Appendix
B.5
.

We propose that Eq. (
42
) holds for arbitrarily nonuniform media. Furthermore, it can be reformulated in terms of a compact spatiotemporal kernel function,
\overleftrightarrow{\mathbb{Z}}(\vec{r},\vec{R},t)
, defined over all point pairs
\vec{r}
and
\vec{R}
within a domain
\mathbb{V}
of a heterogeneous material. This kernel governs the full space- and time-resolved response of the local heat flux to surrounding thermal gradients, systematically accounting for spatial inhomogeneity, temporal memory, and nonlocal interactions within a unified theoretical framework. The resulting transport equation takes the form:

\vec{j}(\vec{r},t)=-\int_{\mathbb{V}}d^{3}\vec{R}\int_{0}^{\infty}dt^{\prime}\,\overleftrightarrow{\mathbb{Z}}(\vec{r},\vec{R},t^{\prime})\cdot\vec{\nabla}_{r}T(\vec{R},t-t^{\prime}),

(43)

where the spatiotemporal heat conduction kernel function,
\overleftrightarrow{\mathbb{Z}}(\vec{r},\vec{R},t)
, replaces the conventional thermal conductivity, serves as the fundamental transport descriptor for heat conduction in nonuniform media, and the kernels
\overleftrightarrow{Z}^{(n_{1}+n_{2}+n_{3})}(\vec{r},t^{\prime};n_{1},n_{2},n_{3})
in Eq. (
42
) are spatial moments of
\overleftrightarrow{\mathbb{Z}}(\vec{r},\vec{R},t)
:

\overleftrightarrow{Z}^{(n_{1}+n_{2}+n_{3})}(\vec{r},t;n_{1},n_{2},n_{3})=-\int_{\mathbb{V}}d^{3}\vec{R}\overleftrightarrow{\mathbb{Z}}(\vec{r},\vec{R},t){(\delta x)}^{n_{1}}{(\delta y)}^{n_{2}}{(\delta z)}^{n_{3}},

(44)

where
\delta x=X-x
,
\delta y=Y-y
, and
\delta z=Z-z
denote the components of
\vec{R}-\vec{r}
.

Equation (
43
) generalizes earlier formulations, such as Eq. (34) in Ref.
Hua and Lindsay 2020
by Hua and Lindsay, which apply only to infinite, homogeneous systems. Their derivation assumes translational invariance and utilizes Fourier transforms to express the kernel as a function of the relative displacement
\vec{R}-\vec{r}
. However, this assumption no longer holds in spatially inhomogeneous media, where phonon spectra and scattering rates vary with position. In contrast, our formulation introduces a spatiotemporal kernel
\overleftrightarrow{\mathbb{Z}}(\vec{r},\vec{R},t)
that depends explicitly on both spatial coordinates, allowing for a rigorous description of heat conduction in finite systems, across material interfaces, and within anisotropic domains. As such, Eq. (
43
) elevates the kernel function to a fundamental role in modeling thermal transport in heterogeneous and temporally evolving media.

Mathematically, the spatiotemporal kernel functions can be transformed into the frequency domain via a Fourier transformation in time,
t
, yielding the general heat transport equation in the
\omega
-domain:

\vec{\tilde{j}}(\vec{r},\omega)=-\int_{\mathbb{V}}d^{3}\vec{R}\overleftrightarrow{\mathbb{\tilde{Z}}}(\vec{r},\vec{R},\omega)\cdot\vec{\nabla}_{r}\tilde{T}(\vec{R},\omega),

(45)

where

\displaystyle\vec{\tilde{j}}(\vec{r},\omega)=\int_{0}^{\infty}dt\,\vec{j}(\vec{r},t)e^{i\omega t}

(46a)

\displaystyle\vec{\nabla}_{r}\tilde{T}(\vec{r},\omega)=\int_{0}^{\infty}dt\,\vec{\nabla}_{r}\tilde{T}(\vec{r},t)e^{i\omega t}

(46b)

\displaystyle\overleftrightarrow{\tilde{\mathbb{Z}}}(\vec{r},\vec{R},\omega)=\int_{0}^{\infty}dt\,\overleftrightarrow{\mathbb{Z}}(\vec{r},\vec{R},t)e^{i\omega t}.

(46c)

Unlike optical or electronic transport, where external driving fields are often directly frequency-controlled, thermal transport is typically governed by time-dependent temperature evolution. As a result, the time-domain formulation of heat conduction remains more physically intuitive and closely aligned with experimental observables. In prior work
Crawford et al. 2024
, we demonstrated that the temporal conduction kernel
\overleftrightarrow{Z}(t)
can often be approximated by a finite sum of exponential modes, each corresponding to a distinct phonon dissipation channel characterized by its relaxation rate. This structure facilitates both analytical treatment and numerical fitting of memory effects in transient heat flow.

The spatial heat conduction kernel at the diffusive limit,
\overleftrightarrow{Z}_{D}(\vec{r},\vec{R})
, emerges as the steady-state limit of the full spatiotemporal kernel function,
\overleftrightarrow{\mathbb{Z}}(\vec{r},\vec{R},t)
, and can be formally obtained by time integration under steady-state conditions:

\overleftrightarrow{Z}_{D}(\vec{r},\vec{R})=\int_{0}^{\infty}dt\,\overleftrightarrow{\mathbb{Z}}(\vec{r},\vec{R},t).

(47)

Similarly, the
\vec{r}
-resolved local conduction kernel,
\overleftrightarrow{Z}(\vec{r},t)
, can be derived from the spatiotemporal conduction kernel
\overleftrightarrow{\mathbb{Z}}(\vec{r},\vec{R},t)
under the assumption that the spatial decay length of
\overleftrightarrow{\mathbb{Z}}
is much smaller than the characteristic length scale over which the temperature gradient
\vec{\nabla}_{r}T(\vec{r},t)
varies:

\overleftrightarrow{Z}(\vec{r},t)=\int_{\mathbb{V}}d^{3}\vec{R}~\overleftrightarrow{\mathbb{Z}}(\vec{r},\vec{R},t).

(48)

Classical Fourier’s law emerges in the local and diffusive limit, wherein the conventional Fourier thermal conductivity tensor
\overleftrightarrow{\kappa}(\vec{r})
is recovered as the zeroth-order, steady-state limit of the spatiotemporal kernel function:

\overleftrightarrow{\kappa}(\vec{r})=\int_{\mathbb{V}}d^{3}\vec{R}\int_{0}^{\infty}dt\,\overleftrightarrow{\mathbb{Z}}(\vec{r},\vec{R},t).

(49)

Beyond this local limit, generalized thermal conductivity tensors, which are defined in Eq. (
33
) to characterize spatially nonlocal effects at the diffusive regime are given as:

\overleftrightarrow{\kappa}^{(n_{1}+n_{2}+n_{3})}(\vec{r};n_{1},n_{2},n_{3})=\int_{\mathbb{V}}d^{3}\vec{R}\int_{0}^{\infty}dt\,\overleftrightarrow{\mathbb{Z}}(\vec{r},\vec{R},t)\,(\delta x)^{n_{1}}(\delta y)^{n_{2}}(\delta z)^{n_{3}}.

(50)

IV.2
Generalized Green-Kubo Formalism

The classical Green–Kubo relation expresses bulk thermal conductivity as the time integral of the autocorrelation function (ACF) of the spatially averaged heat flux. In our framework, this concept is rigorously generalized to heterogeneous media by identifying the space- and time-resolved conduction kernel
\overleftrightarrow{\mathbb{Z}}(\vec{r},\vec{R},t)
as the fundamental transport descriptor. The bulk Green–Kubo formula takes the form:

\overleftrightarrow{\kappa}_{\text{bulk}}=\frac{V}{k_{B}T_{0}^{2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}dt\,\langle\vec{j}_{\text{bulk}}(0)\otimes\vec{j}_{\text{bulk}}(t)\rangle_{\text{eq}},

(51)

where
\vec{j}\text{bulk}(t)
is the spatially averaged heat flux:

\vec{j}_{\text{bulk}}(t)=\frac{1}{V}\int_{\mathbb{V}}d^{3}\vec{r}\,\vec{j}(\vec{r},t).

(52)

This leads to a double integral over spatially resolved heat flux correlations:

\langle\vec{j}_{\text{bulk}}(t)\otimes\vec{j}_{\text{bulk}}(0)\rangle_{\text{eq}}=\frac{1}{V^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{V}}d^{3}\vec{r}\int_{\mathbb{V}}d^{3}\vec{R}\,\langle\vec{j}(\vec{r},0)\otimes\vec{j}(\vec{R},t)\rangle_{\text{eq}}.

(53)

Comparison between Eq. (
49
) and Eqs. (
51
)–(
53
) suggests that the space- and time-resolved heat conduction kernel function,
\overleftrightarrow{\mathbb{Z}}(\vec{r},\vec{R},t)
, which plays a central role in quantifying transient and nonlocal heat conduction in heterogeneous media, can be evaluated via numerical calculation of the space-resolved heat flux ACF:

\overleftrightarrow{\mathbb{Z}}(\vec{r},\vec{R},t)=\frac{1}{k_{B}T_{0}^{2}}\langle\vec{j}(\vec{r},0)\otimes\vec{j}(\vec{R},t)\rangle_{\text{eq}},

(54)

where
\vec{r}
-dependence encodes the inhomogeneity in the nonuniform media as this kernel becomes translationally invariant for
\vec{r}
in the homogeneous limit. More generally, Eq. (
54
) provides a first-principles pathway to extract the kernel from molecular dynamics (MD) simulations via evaluation of equilibrium heat flux correlations.

In summary, this unified theory systematically generalizes Zwanzig’s nonequilibrium statistical framework, rigorously incorporating temporal memory and spatial nonlocal effects into heat conduction. Starting from the homogeneous, local equilibrium limit, we progressively introduced hierarchical expansions to capture the emergence of memory and nonlocality, culminating in a full extension to spatially heterogeneous systems with both smooth and abrupt variations in material properties. This comprehensive framework offers a microscopically grounded yet computationally tractable methodology for modeling non-Fourier heat conduction across diverse material systems. The theory is encapsulated by the following set of three equations:

\displaystyle\rho C~\frac{\partial}{\partial t}T(\vec{r},t)+\vec{\nabla}_{r}\cdot\vec{j}(\vec{r},t)=0,

(55a)

\displaystyle\vec{j}(\vec{r},t)=-\int_{\mathbb{V}}d^{3}\vec{R}\int_{0}^{\infty}dt^{\prime}\,\overleftrightarrow{\mathbb{Z}}(\vec{r},\vec{R},t^{\prime})\cdot\vec{\nabla}_{r}T(\vec{R},t-t^{\prime}),

(55b)

\displaystyle\overleftrightarrow{\mathbb{Z}}(\vec{r},\vec{R},t)=\frac{1}{k_{B}T_{0}^{2}}\langle\vec{j}(\vec{R},t)\otimes\vec{j}(\vec{r},0)\rangle_{\text{eq}}.

(55c)

Beyond its theoretical role in transport equation, the kernel function also provides a direct link between nonequilibrium statistical theory and atomistic simulation. Traditional Green–Kubo methods use MD to estimate an effective thermal conductivity by time-averaging the autocorrelation of the total heat flux. While effective in the diffusive regime, this scalar reduction discards the spatial and temporal structure critical to understanding nanoscale and ultrafast transport. In contrast, the kernel formalism developed here enables extraction of the full space- and time-resolved conduction kernel,
\overleftrightarrow{\mathbb{Z}}(\vec{r},\vec{R},t)
, from MD simulations via spatially resolved equilibrium flux–flux correlations. This kernel captures how local heat flux responds to both delayed and distributed temperature gradients, offering a rigorous foundation for quantifying memory and nonlocality.

Rather than reducing simulation output to a single number, our framework transforms MD into a source of rich, position- and time-dependent transport information. This enables first-principles benchmarking of complex systems such as interfaces, nanostructures, and nonuniform media, where classical conductivity models fail. In this sense, the theory provides not only a unifying conceptual foundation, but also a practical platform for simulation–theory integration in the study of next-generation heat transport phenomena.

V
Conclusion

We have presented a unified statistical theory of heat conduction that rigorously accounts for temporal memory and spatial nonlocality. By integrating the Zwanzig projection operator formalism with the phonon Boltzmann transport equation, we demonstrate that these non-Fourier effects originate from the statistical influence of
irrelevant
microscopic degrees of freedom on the
relevant
thermodynamic variable, the local temperature field,
T(\vec{r},t)
. These irrelevant variables can be categorized into two distinct classes: (i) flux-mode variables, which contribute directly to the macroscopic heat flux
\vec{j}(\vec{r},t)
, and (ii) nonlocal-mode variables, which describe higher-order deviations from local thermal equilibrium. Each mode is characterized by a finite relaxation rate, which sets the intrinsic memory timescale.

The macroscopic heat flux includes both a local, gradient-driven term and a nonlocal contribution that reflects spatial and temporal correlations among the underlying modes. This structure leads to a hierarchical, temporally nonlocal expansion, which we reformulate as a compact spatiotemporal kernel,
\overleftrightarrow{\mathbb{Z}}(\vec{r},\vec{R},t)
. This kernel provides a general, microscopically grounded transport descriptor for complex and heterogeneous media, extending the classical framework of heat conduction.

Our framework also enables new computational strategies for modeling heat conduction kernels in complex materials at the atomistic scale. Using first-principles or atomistic calculations of phonon spectra and scattering matrices as microscopic inputs, one can compute a finite set of spatial moments of the kernel function, such as
\overleftrightarrow{Z}^{(n_{1}+n_{2}+n_{3})}(\vec{r},t;n_{1},n_{2},n_{3})
in Eq. (
42
) or
\overleftrightarrow{\kappa}^{(n_{1}+n_{2}+n_{3})}(\vec{r};n_{1},n_{2},n_{3})
in Eq. (
33
), to approximately reconstruct the full spatiotemporal kernel
\overleftrightarrow{\mathbb{Z}}(\vec{r},\vec{R},t)
or its time-independent counterpart
\overleftrightarrow{Z}_{D}(\vec{r},\vec{R})
. Alternatively, the kernel can be extracted directly from time-correlation functions of the space-resolved local heat flux density,
\vec{j}(\vec{r},t)
, obtained via molecular dynamics simulations or Fokker-Planck equation. Although evaluating the full kernel through time-correlation approaches is computationally intensive, recent advances in machine-learned interatomic potentials
Bartók et al. 2018
provide a promising path toward predictive modeling of heat transport in nanostructured and compositionally complex materials.

Finally, the kernel formalism is general and can be applied to other irreversible transport processes governed by Onsager reciprocity
Onsager 1931a
;
Onsager 1931b
. By generalizing classical transport coefficients to spatiotemporal kernel functions, our theory offers a unified platform for analyzing coupled transport phenomena in nonequilibrium systems.

Appendix A
Projection Operator Formalism: Background and Notation

This appendix summarizes the Zwanzig projection operator method, with emphasis on the aspects most relevant to the theory developed in Sec.
II.1
.

A.1
Zwanzig formulism

Let
f(\Gamma,t)
be the full probability distribution in the complete phase space
\Gamma
, which includes all microscopic degrees of freedom. The time evolution of
f(\Gamma,t)
follows the Liouville equation:

\frac{\partial f(\Gamma,t)}{\partial t}=-i\hat{L}(\Gamma)f(\Gamma,t),

(56)

where
\hat{L}
is the Liouville operator. The formal solution is
f(\Gamma,t)=e^{-it\hat{L}}f(\Gamma,0)
for a given initial condition.

Zwanzig’s method separates the dynamics into slow, experimentally accessible
relevant
variables and fast, fluctuating
irrelevant
variables. The projection operator
\hat{P}
maps the full distribution onto the relevant subspace:

f_{R}(\Gamma_{R},t)=\hat{P}f(\Gamma,t),

(57)

where
\Gamma_{R}
denotes the reduced phase space of relevant variables. The remainder,

\Delta f(t)=f(t)-f_{R}(t)=(\hat{1}-\hat{P})f(t),

(58)

captures the influence of irrelevant variables. Its time evolution is

\Delta f(t)=e^{-it(\hat{1}-\hat{P})\hat{L}}\Delta f(0)-i\int_{0}^{t}dt_{1}\,e^{-it_{1}(\hat{1}-\hat{P})\hat{L}}(\hat{1}-\hat{P})\hat{L}f_{R}(t-t_{1}).

(59)

Substituting Eqs. (
57
) and (
59
) into the full Liouville equation in Eq. (
56
) yields the projected Liouville equation:

\frac{\partial f_{R}(t)}{\partial t}=-i\hat{P}\hat{L}e^{-it(\hat{1}-\hat{P})\hat{L}}\Delta f(0)-i\hat{P}\hat{L}f_{R}(t)-\int_{0}^{t}dt_{1}\,\hat{P}\hat{L}e^{-it_{1}(\hat{1}-\hat{P})\hat{L}}(\hat{1}-\hat{P})\hat{L}f_{R}(t-t_{1}).

(60)

The second and third terms on the right encode the influence of irrelevant variables, with the final term introducing a memory kernel that couples
f_{R}
to its full time history and results in non-Markovian dynamics.

While explicit forms for
\hat{L}
and
\hat{P}
are often unavailable, the hierarchical, time-convolution structure of Eq. (
60
) enables systematic approximations at varying levels of resolution. This flexibility is valuable in heat transport modeling, where the set of relevant variables and the degree of coupling to irrelevant variables can be tailored for accuracy and computational efficiency.

A.2
Relevant Variables at Local Thermal Equilibrium

A key step is the identification of relevant variables that describe irreversible processes. In heat transport, the local energy density
e(\vec{r},t)
and the local heat flux
\vec{j}(\vec{r},t)
are fundamental and satisfy:

\frac{\partial}{\partial t}e(\vec{r},t)+\vec{\nabla}_{r}\cdot\vec{j}(\vec{r},t)=0.

(61)

However, these fields are not directly accessible in experiment. Instead, the local temperature
T(\vec{r},t)
is inferred from observables such as thermal expansion, Raman shifts, or reflectivity, under the assumption of local thermal equilibrium:

T(\vec{r},t)=T_{0}+\frac{e(\vec{r},t)-e^{\text{eq}}(T_{0})}{\rho C},

(62)

where
T_{0}
is a reference equilibrium temperature.

This assumption is justified when deviations from global equilibrium are small and each control volume contains many energy carriers, so
T(\vec{r},t)
is well defined. However, the validity of
T(\vec{r},t)
is fundamentally limited by the spatial and temporal resolution of experiments. At ultrafast or nanoscale regimes, these limits become especially important.

Even when
T(\vec{r},t)
is a valid thermodynamic variable, microscopic deviations from local equilibrium may exist. These arise from nonequilibrium fluctuations not fully described by
T(\vec{r},t)
alone. The theory developed in this work systematically separates slow, macroscopic variables from fast microscopic ones, retaining
T(\vec{r},t)
as the sole relevant variable and capturing microscopic effects through a hierarchy of projected auxiliary variables. These evolve on shorter timescales and encode the memory and nonlocality that extend beyond classical heat conduction.

A.3
Local Homogeneous Approximation

Many real materials exhibit spatial variations in structures and compositions. When the spatial resolution of measured fields
T(\vec{r},t)
and
\vec{j}(\vec{r},t)
is coarser than the relevant inhomogeneity length scales, a mean-field or coarse-grained approximation can be adopted. In this case, the medium is treated as piecewise within a representative local volume
\Omega
:

\begin{cases}\tilde{T}(\vec{r},t)=\frac{1}{\Omega}\int_{\Omega}d^{3}\vec{r^{\prime}}\,T(\vec{r}+\vec{r^{\prime}},t),\\
\vec{\tilde{j}}(\vec{r},t)=\frac{1}{\Omega}\int_{\Omega}d^{3}\vec{r^{\prime}}\,\vec{j}(\vec{r}+\vec{r^{\prime}},t).\end{cases}

(63)

To go beyond this local homogeneity approximation, especially where material inhomogeneities become significant, the theory must explicitly account for spatial variation. In this work, we distinguish between (i) continuously varying media, where the temperature gradient is always defined and differential descriptions are valid, and (ii) systems with sharp material interfaces, where temperature gradients become discontinuous and transport must be described by finite jumps,
\Delta T
, across the interface.

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\tilde{\eta}_{\lambda}(\vec{r},t)}{\partial t}=-\frac{e^{\gamma_{\lambda}t}}{k_{\text{B}}T^{2}_{0}}\vec{\Lambda}(\lambda)\cdot\vec{\nabla}_{r}T(\vec{r},t)-e^{\gamma_{\lambda}t}\sum_{\iota=1}^{{M}_{\text{E}}}e^{-\varepsilon_{\iota}t}\vec{{\Pi}}(\lambda,\iota)\cdot\vec{\nabla}_{r}{\tilde{\theta}}_{\iota}(\vec{r},t),

(73a)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\tilde{\theta}_{\iota}(\vec{r},t)}{\partial t}=-e^{\varepsilon_{\iota}t}\sum_{\lambda^{\prime}=1}^{{M}_{\text{O}}}e^{-\gamma_{\lambda^{\prime}}t}\vec{{\Pi}}(\lambda^{\prime},\iota)\cdot\vec{\nabla}_{r}{\tilde{\eta}}_{\lambda^{\prime}}(\vec{r},t).

(73b)

The solutions to Eqs. (
73a
) and (
73b
) can be formally expressed as time-convolution integrals:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\tilde{\eta}_{\lambda}(\vec{r},t)=&-\int_{-\infty}^{t}dt_{1}\ \frac{e^{\gamma_{\lambda}t_{1}}}{k_{\text{B}}T^{2}_{0}}\vec{\Lambda}(\lambda)\cdot\vec{\nabla}_{r}T(\vec{r},t_{1})\\
&-\sum_{\iota=1}^{M_{\text{E}}}\int_{-\infty}^{t}dt_{1}e^{(\gamma_{\lambda}-\varepsilon_{\iota})t_{1}}\vec{\Pi}(\lambda,\iota)\cdot\vec{\nabla}_{r}\tilde{\theta}_{\iota}(\vec{r},t_{1}),\\
\end{split}

(74a)

\displaystyle\tilde{\theta}_{\iota}(\vec{r},t)=

\displaystyle-\sum_{\lambda^{\prime}=1}^{M_{\text{O}}}\int_{-\infty}^{t}dt_{1}e^{(\varepsilon_{\iota}-\gamma_{\lambda^{\prime}})t_{1}}\vec{\Pi}(\lambda^{\prime},\iota)\cdot\vec{\nabla}_{r}\tilde{\eta}_{\lambda^{\prime}}(\vec{r},t_{1}).

(74b)

Substituting back using Eqs. (
71a
) and (
71b
), we obtain the expressions presented in Eqs (
12a
) and (
12b
).

B.3
Position-Dependent Formalism

For spatially inhomogeneous materials, the Zwanzig variables acquire spatial dependence. The coupled equations read

\displaystyle C\cdot\frac{\partial T(\vec{r},t)}{\partial t}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\sum_{\lambda=1}^{M_{\text{O}}}\vec{\Lambda}(\vec{r};\lambda)\cdot\vec{\nabla}_{r}\eta_{\lambda}(\vec{r},t),

(75a)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\eta_{\lambda}(\vec{r},t)}{\partial t}+\gamma_{\lambda}(\vec{r})\eta_{\lambda}(\vec{r},t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{k_{\text{B}}T_{0}^{2}}\vec{\Lambda}(\vec{r};\lambda)\cdot\vec{\nabla}_{r}T(\vec{r},t)-\sum_{\iota=1}^{M_{\text{E}}}\vec{\Pi}(\vec{r};\lambda,\iota)\cdot\vec{\nabla}_{r}\theta_{\iota}(\vec{r},t),

(75b)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\theta_{\iota}(\vec{r},t)}{\partial t}+\varepsilon_{\iota}(\vec{r})\theta_{\iota}(\vec{r},t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\sum_{\lambda=1}^{M_{\text{O}}}\vec{\Pi}(\vec{r};\lambda,\iota)\cdot\vec{\nabla}_{r}\eta_{\lambda}(\vec{r},t),

(75c)

where
\gamma_{\lambda}(\vec{r})
and
\varepsilon_{\iota}(\vec{r})
denote the
\vec{r}
-dependent odd- and even-parity eigenvalues, respectively, of the local phonon scattering matrix
\mathcal{L}(\vec{r})
. The corresponding coefficients
\vec{\Lambda}(\vec{r};\lambda)
and
\vec{\Pi}(\vec{r};\lambda,\iota)
are derived from the eigenvectors associated with the
\lambda
-th odd-parity and
\iota
-th even-parity eigenmodes of
\mathcal{L}(\vec{r})
. The solution to Eqs. (
75
) can be expressed as:

\displaystyle\eta_{\lambda}(\vec{r},t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\int_{0}^{\infty}dt_{1}\frac{e^{-\gamma_{\lambda}(\vec{r})t_{1}}}{k_{\text{B}}T_{0}^{2}}\vec{\Lambda}(\vec{r};\lambda)\cdot\vec{\nabla}_{r}T(\vec{r},t-t_{1})

(76a)

\displaystyle-\int_{0}^{\infty}dt_{1}\,e^{-\gamma_{\lambda}(\vec{r})t_{1}}\sum_{\iota=1}^{M_{\text{E}}}\vec{\Pi}(\vec{r};\lambda,\iota)\cdot\vec{\nabla}_{r}\theta_{\iota}(\vec{r},t-t_{1}),

\displaystyle\theta_{\iota}(\vec{r},t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\int_{0}^{\infty}dt_{1}\,e^{-\varepsilon_{\iota}(\vec{r})t_{1}}\sum_{\lambda=1}^{M_{\text{O}}}\vec{\Pi}(\vec{r};\lambda,\iota)\cdot\vec{\nabla}_{r}\eta_{\lambda}(\vec{r},t-t_{1}).

(76b)

Applying the spatial gradient operator
\vec{\nabla}_{r}
to both sides of Eq. (
76b
) yields:

\begin{split}\vec{\nabla}_{r}\theta_{\iota}(\vec{r},t)=&-\int_{0}^{\infty}dt_{1}\sum_{\lambda=1}^{M_{\text{O}}}\sum_{I=1}^{3}\,e^{-\varepsilon_{\iota}(\vec{r})t_{1}}\Pi_{I}(\vec{r};\lambda,\iota)\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{I}}\vec{\nabla}_{r}\eta_{\lambda}(\vec{r},t-t_{1})\\
-&\int_{0}^{\infty}dt_{1}\sum_{\lambda=1}^{M_{\text{O}}}\sum_{I=1}^{3}\,\vec{\nabla}_{r}\biggr(e^{-\varepsilon_{\iota}(\vec{r})t_{1}}\Pi_{I}(\vec{r};\lambda,\iota)\biggr)\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{I}}\eta_{\lambda}(\vec{r},t-t_{1}).\end{split}

(77)

Substitution of Eq. (
77
) into Eq. (
76a
) yields the generalized position-dependent flux-mode equation, Eq. (
15
), presented in the main text.

B.4
Iterative Expansion of Modal Solutions

The iterative expansion of Eq. (
15
) allows systematic inclusion of memory and spatial nonlocality. For example, the flux-mode variable
\eta_{\lambda}(\vec{r},t)
can be written as:

\eta_{\lambda}=\eta^{(0)}_{\lambda}+\Delta\eta^{(1)}_{\lambda}+\Delta\eta^{(2)}_{\lambda}+\dots~,

(78)

where the zeroth-order term
\eta^{(0)}_{\lambda}
is

\eta^{(0)}_{\lambda}(\vec{r},t)=-\frac{1}{k_{B}T^{2}_{0}}\int_{0}^{\infty}dt_{1}e^{-\gamma_{\lambda}(\vec{r})t_{1}}\biggr(\vec{\Lambda}(\vec{r};\lambda)\cdot\vec{\nabla}_{r}T(\vec{r},t-t_{1})\biggr),

(79)

and the first-order correction
\Delta\eta^{(1)}_{\lambda}
is

\begin{split}\Delta\eta^{(1)}_{\lambda}(\vec{r},t)=&-\frac{1}{k_{B}T_{0}^{2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}dt_{1}\int_{0}^{\infty}dt_{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}dt_{3}e^{-\gamma_{\lambda}(\vec{r})t_{1}}\sum_{\lambda^{\prime}=1}^{M_{\text{O}}}\\
\biggr(&\sum_{I=1}^{3}\Psi_{I}(\vec{r},t_{2};\lambda,\lambda^{\prime})\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{I}}[e^{-\gamma_{\lambda}^{\prime}(\vec{r})t_{3}}\vec{\Lambda}(\vec{r};\lambda^{\prime})\cdot\vec{\nabla}_{r}T(\vec{r},t-t_{1}-t_{2}-t_{3})]\\
+&\sum_{I,J=1}^{3}\phi_{IJ}(t_{2};\lambda,\lambda^{\prime})\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x_{I}\partial x_{J}}[e^{-\gamma_{\lambda}^{\prime}(\vec{r})t_{3}}\vec{\Lambda}(\vec{r};\lambda^{\prime})\cdot\vec{\nabla}_{r}T(\vec{r},t-t_{1}-t_{2}-t_{3})]\biggr).\end{split}

(80)

Next, we introduce the total lag ,
t^{\prime}
, through the variable change:

t^{\prime}=t_{1}+t_{2}+t_{3},

(81)

and keep
t_{2}
and
t_{3}
as inner time variables. The mapping
(t_{1},t_{2},t_{3})\rightarrow(t^{\prime},t_{2},t_{3})
has unit Jacobian, i.e.
dt_{1}dt_{2}dt_{3}=dt^{\prime}dt_{1}dt_{2}
. Domain mapping of
t_{1}\geq 0
,
t_{2}\geq 0
, and
t_{3}\geq 0
gives:

\begin{cases}t^{\prime}\geq 0,\\
0\leq t_{2}\leq t^{\prime},\\
0\leq t_{3}\leq t^{\prime}-t_{2}.\end{cases}

(82)

Hence,

\begin{split}\Delta\eta^{(1)}_{\lambda}(\vec{r},t)=&-\int_{0}^{\infty}dt^{\prime}\frac{e^{-\gamma_{\lambda}(\vec{r})t^{\prime}}}{k_{B}T_{0}^{2}}\int_{0}^{t^{\prime}}dt_{2}\int_{0}^{t^{\prime}-t_{2}}dt_{3}e^{\gamma_{\lambda}(\vec{r})(t_{2}+t_{3})}\sum_{\lambda^{\prime}=1}^{M_{\text{O}}}\\
\biggr(&\sum_{I=1}^{3}\Psi_{I}(\vec{r},t_{2};\lambda,\lambda^{\prime})\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{I}}[e^{-\gamma_{\lambda}^{\prime}(\vec{r})t_{3}}\vec{\Lambda}(\vec{r};\lambda^{\prime})\cdot\vec{\nabla}_{r}T(\vec{r},t-t^{\prime})]\\
+&\sum_{I,J=1}^{3}\phi_{IJ}(t_{2};\lambda,\lambda^{\prime})\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x_{I}\partial x_{J}}[e^{-\gamma_{\lambda}^{\prime}(\vec{r})t_{3}}\vec{\Lambda}(\vec{r};\lambda^{\prime})\cdot\vec{\nabla}_{r}T(\vec{r},t-t^{\prime})]\biggr)\\
=&-\int_{0}^{\infty}dt^{\prime}\frac{e^{-\gamma_{\lambda}(\vec{r})t^{\prime}}}{k_{B}T_{0}^{2}}\biggr(\vec{A}_{0}^{(1)}(\vec{r},t^{\prime};\lambda)\cdot\vec{\nabla}_{r}T(\vec{r},t-t^{\prime})+\sum_{I=1}^{3}\vec{A}_{1}^{(1)}(\vec{r},t^{\prime};\lambda,I)\cdot\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{I}}\vec{\nabla}_{r}T(\vec{r},t-t^{\prime})\\
&+\sum_{I,J=1}^{3}\vec{A}_{2}^{(1)}(\vec{r},t^{\prime};\lambda,I,J)\cdot\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x_{I}\partial x_{J}}\vec{\nabla}_{r}T(\vec{r},t-t^{\prime})\biggr),\end{split}

(83)

where

\begin{split}&\vec{A}_{0}^{(1)}(\vec{r},t^{\prime};\lambda)=\int_{0}^{t^{\prime}}dt_{2}\int_{0}^{t^{\prime}-t_{2}}dt_{3}e^{\gamma_{\lambda}(\vec{r})(t_{2}+t_{3})}\sum_{\lambda^{\prime}=1}^{M_{\text{O}}}\biggr(\\
&\sum_{I=1}^{3}\{\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{I}}[\Psi_{I}(\vec{r},t_{2};\lambda)e^{-\gamma_{\lambda^{\prime}}(\vec{r})t_{3}}\vec{\Lambda}(\vec{r};\lambda^{\prime})]-\frac{\partial\Psi_{I}(\vec{r},t_{2};\lambda,\lambda^{\prime})}{\partial x_{I}}e^{-\gamma_{\lambda^{\prime}}(\vec{r})t_{3}}\vec{\Lambda}(\vec{r};\lambda^{\prime})\}+\\
&\sum_{I,J=1}^{3}\{\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x_{I}\partial x_{J}}[\phi_{IJ}(\vec{r},t_{2};\lambda)e^{-\gamma_{\lambda^{\prime}}(\vec{r})t_{3}}\vec{\Lambda}(\vec{r};\lambda^{\prime})]-\frac{\partial^{2}\phi_{IJ}(\vec{r},t_{2};\lambda,\lambda^{\prime})}{\partial x_{I}\partial x_{j}}e^{-\gamma_{\lambda^{\prime}}(\vec{r})t_{3}}\vec{\Lambda}(\vec{r};\lambda^{\prime})\}\biggr)\end{split}

(84)

\vec{A}_{1}^{(1)}(\vec{r},t^{\prime};\lambda,I)=\int_{0}^{t^{\prime}}dt_{2}\int_{0}^{t^{\prime}-t_{2}}dt_{3}e^{\gamma_{\lambda}(\vec{r})(t_{2}+t_{3})}\sum_{\lambda^{\prime}=1}^{M_{\text{O}}}\Psi_{I}(\vec{r},t_{2};\lambda,\lambda^{\prime})e^{-\gamma_{\lambda^{\prime}}(\vec{r})t_{3}}\vec{\Lambda}(\vec{r};\lambda^{\prime}),

(85)

\vec{A}_{2}^{(1)}(\vec{r},t^{\prime};\lambda,I,J)=\int_{0}^{t^{\prime}}dt_{2}\int_{0}^{t^{\prime}-t_{2}}dt_{3}e^{\gamma_{\lambda}(\vec{r})(t_{2}+t_{3})}\sum_{\lambda^{\prime}=1}^{M_{\text{O}}}\phi_{IJ}(\vec{r},t_{2};\lambda,\lambda^{\prime})e^{-\gamma_{\lambda^{\prime}}(\vec{r})t_{3}}\vec{\Lambda}(\vec{r};\lambda^{\prime}),

(86)

Therefore, the first-order correction
\Delta\eta^{(1)}_{\lambda}(\vec{r},t)
depends on a time convolution of the temperature gradient field
\vec{\nabla}_{r}T(\vec{r},t)
, its first-order spatial derivatives
\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{I}}\vec{\nabla}_{r}T(\vec{r},t)
, and its second-order spatial derivatives
\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x_{I}\partial x_{J}}\vec{\nabla}_{r}T(\vec{r},t)
.

Although Eq. (
83
) originally involves three nested time integrals, this structure can be interpreted as a single causal convolution, whose kernel is given by the ordinary time-domain convolution of the corresponding nested integral kernels shown in Eqs. (
84
), (
85
), and (
86
).

Similarly, the second-order correction
\Delta\eta^{(2)}_{\lambda}(\vec{r},t)
can be expressed in a similar form:

\begin{split}&\Delta\eta^{(2)}_{\lambda}(\vec{r},t)=-\int_{0}^{\infty}dt^{\prime}\frac{e^{-\gamma_{\lambda}(\vec{r})t^{\prime}}}{k_{B}T_{0}^{2}}\biggr(\vec{A}_{0}^{(2)}(\vec{r},t^{\prime};\lambda)\cdot\vec{\nabla}_{r}T(\vec{r},t-t^{\prime})+\sum_{I=1}^{3}\vec{A}_{1}^{(2)}(\vec{r},t^{\prime};\lambda,I)\cdot\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{I}}\vec{\nabla}_{r}T(\vec{r},t-t^{\prime})\\
&+\sum_{I,J=1}^{3}\vec{A}_{2}^{(2)}(\vec{r},t^{\prime};\lambda,I,J)\cdot\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x_{I}\partial x_{J}}\vec{\nabla}_{r}T(\vec{r},t-t^{\prime})\\
&+\sum_{I,J,K=1}^{3}\vec{A}_{3}^{(2)}(\vec{r},t^{\prime};\lambda,I,J,K)\cdot\frac{\partial^{3}}{\partial x_{I}\partial x_{J}\partial x_{K}}\vec{\nabla}_{r}T(\vec{r},t-t^{\prime})\biggr),\end{split}

(87)

Consequently, macroscopic heat flux
\vec{j}(\vec{r},t)
is obtained by summing over the microscopic flux-modes. The iterative expansion for
\eta_{\lambda}
leads to a generalized constitutive law:

\begin{split}\vec{j}(\vec{r},t)=-&\sum_{n_{1}=0}^{\infty}\sum_{n_{2}=0}^{\infty}\sum_{n_{3}=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{(n_{1})!(n_{2})!(n_{3})!}\int_{0}^{\infty}dt^{\prime}\overleftrightarrow{Z}^{(n_{1}+n_{2}+n_{3})}(\vec{r},t^{\prime};n_{1},n_{2},n_{3})\\
&\cdot\frac{\partial^{(n_{1}+n_{2}+n_{3})}}{\partial^{n_{1}}x\partial^{n_{2}}y\partial^{n_{3}}z}\vec{\nabla}_{r}T(\vec{r},t-t^{\prime}).\end{split}

(88)

Here
\overleftrightarrow{Z}^{(n)}(\vec{r},t^{\prime};n_{1},n_{2},n_{3})
are single-time convolution kernels encapsulating higher-order memory and nonlocal effects.

This compact representation highlights the hierarchical nonlocal and memory-dependent structure of the transport response encoded in the correction term. The lowest order term (
n_{1}=n_{2}=n_{3}=0
), yields the local, transient heat flux,

\vec{j}^{\text{local}}(\vec{r},t)=-\int_{0}^{\infty}dt^{\prime}\,\overleftrightarrow{Z}^{(0)}(\vec{r},t^{\prime};0,0,0)\cdot\vec{\nabla}_{r}T(\vec{r},t-t^{\prime}).

(89)

All higher-order terms (
n=n_{1}+n_{2}+n_{3}\geq 1
,) represent nonlocal corrections.

B.5
Conduction Kernel Formalism

To capture all orders, an infinite set of time-domain kernels is required. For practical computation, a more compact representation is achieved by introducing a spatiotemporal kernel
\overleftrightarrow{\mathbb{Z}}(\vec{r},\vec{R},t)
:

\vec{j}(\vec{r},t)=-\int_{\mathbb{V}}d^{3}\vec{R}\int_{0}^{\infty}dt^{\prime}\,\overleftrightarrow{\mathbb{Z}}(\vec{r},\vec{R},t^{\prime})\cdot\vec{\nabla}_{r}T(\vec{R},t-t^{\prime}).

(90)

Here, we adopt the Taylor expansion series:

\vec{\nabla}_{r}T(\vec{R},t)=\vec{\nabla}_{r}T(\vec{r}+\delta\vec{r},t)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n!}(\delta\vec{r}\cdot\vec{\nabla}_{r})^{n}\vec{\nabla}_{r}T(\vec{r},t)

(91)

The kernels
\overleftrightarrow{Z}^{(n)}
are related to
\overleftrightarrow{\mathbb{Z}}
by spatial moments,

\overleftrightarrow{Z}^{(n_{1}+n_{2}+n_{3})}(\vec{r},t;n_{1},n_{2},n_{3})=\int_{\mathbb{V}}d^{3}\vec{R}\,\overleftrightarrow{\mathbb{Z}}(\vec{r},\vec{R},t)\,{(\delta x)}^{n_{1}}{(\delta y)}^{n_{2}}{(\delta z)}^{n_{3}}.

(92)

If all spatial moments are known, the kernel can be reconstructed. In practice, only a finite set is accessible, so the kernel is approximated to the degree allowed by available data. In the steady-state limit, this framework yields an explicit, space-domain conduction kernel,

\vec{j}(\vec{r})=-\int_{\mathbb{V}}d^{3}\vec{R}\overleftrightarrow{Z}_{D}(\vec{r},\vec{R})\cdot\vec{\nabla}_{r}T(\vec{R}),

(93)

where

\overleftrightarrow{Z}_{D}(\vec{r},\vec{R})=-\int_{0}^{\infty}dt\overleftrightarrow{\mathbb{Z}}(\vec{r},\vec{R},t).

(94)
