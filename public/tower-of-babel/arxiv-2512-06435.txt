Canonical Tail Dependence for Soft Extremal Clustering
of Multichannel Brain Signals

Abstract




We develop a novel characterization of extremal dependence between two cortical regions of the brain when its signals display extremely large amplitudes. We show that connectivity in the tails of the distribution reveals unique features of extreme events (e.g., seizures) that can help to identify their occurrence. Numerous studies have established that connectivity-based features are effective for discriminating brain states. Here, we demonstrate the advantage of the proposed approach: that tail connectivity provides additional discriminatory power, enabling more accurate identification of extreme-related events and improved seizure risk management. Common approaches in tail dependence modeling use pairwise summary measures or parametric models. However, these approaches do not identify channels that drive the maximal tail dependence between two groups of signals—an information that is useful when analyzing electroencephalography of epileptic patients where specific channels are responsible for seizure occurrences. A familiar approach in traditional signal processing is canonical correlation, which we extend to the tails to develop a visualization of extremal channel-contributions. Through the tail pairwise dependence matrix (TPDM), we develop a computationally-efficient estimator for our canonical tail dependence measure. Our method is then used for accurate frequency-based soft clustering of neonates, distinguishing those with seizures from those without.

Keywords:
feature extraction; multivariate analysis; multivariate regular variation; representation learning; spectral analysis.

1
Introduction

Early detection of seizures is crucial, especially at early ages where seizures pose a high risk of disrupting brain development. When unprovoked seizures occur in neonates, they often lead to epileptic encephalopathy
(
38
)
,
hence explaining why close monitoring in neonatal intensive care units (NICUs) is necessary. The primary modality for seizure management in an NICU is the electroencephalogram (EEG)
(
54
)
. However, even with medical-grade EEG technology, distinguishing neonates with seizures from those without (i.e., with normal ultrasound recordings) remains challenging, as the patterns and symptoms of seizures can appear deceptively similar
(
60
)
. While our study is not directly aimed at detecting seizures, we develop a data-driven framework that extracts
unique features
from the tails of the signal distribution which has the ability to isolate, even without expert knowledge, neonates with seizures. Our contributions thus pave the way for efficient representation learning of extremes, which can be useful in a wide range of scientific applications.

Many brain-state analyses involve extraction of features through dependence matrices
(
63
;
14
;
30
)
. In fact, a number of studies demonstrated associations between physiological signals (through brain functional connectivity), and behavior and cognition
(
19
;
8
;
57
)
.
In addition to the spectral density matrix
(
58
, see Ch. 4 of)
, canonical coherence—introduced by
4
as the time-series analogue of canonical correlation
(
21
)
—provides a multivariate measure of synchrony between two groups of signals at specific frequencies. Extending this framework to frequency bands (defined in Section
2.2
) in the presence of outlying observations, the Kendall’s tau based canonical coherence
(
62
)
provides a rank-based estimator for the canonical coherence of filtered signals, enabling discrimination of brain states that is robust to outliers.
However, there are cases when connectivity in the bulk of the data differs from connectivity at the tails. As demonstrated in
61
, there are key features of extreme events that are only highlighted when focus is placed on the joint tails of brain signals.
This is true for brain connectivity, where many extreme neurological events, such as seizures, are a result of concurrent or interrelated extreme firing of neurons
(
40
)
.
This complexity has led to recent growing interest in multivariate risk analysis in neuroscience applications
(
16
;
47
;
61
;
36
)
, which addresses questions such as “What is the likelihood that two brain signals exhibit extreme amplitudes simultaneously?"

The extremal dependence measure (EDM), introduced in
50
and studied by
29
, encodes tail dependence via the framework of multivariate regular variation
(
48
)
.

5
extended the EDM beyond the bivariate setting by introducing the tail pairwise dependence matrix (TPDM), which, analogous to a covariance matrix, encodes extremal dependence for random vectors. The TPDM has established the basis for a number of multivariate analyses in extreme value theory, such as high-dimensional graphical or causal analysis
(
27
;
13
;
25
)
and clustering
(
52
;
12
)
.
In this paper, we utilize the TPDM to propose a novel measure of canonical tail dependence (CTD) to discriminate the joint-tail regional structure of neonates with extreme brain conditions (e.g., seizures) from those patients with normal brain conditions (e.g., seizure-free). We define CTD as the maximized EDM value between linear combinations of signals from two brain regions. Using these optimized linear combinations, we can further investigate patterns associated with different brain conditions based on the tail connectivity of the signals.

A number of studies consider features extracted from multivariate extremes for hard clustering based on, e.g., max-linear models, hidden regular variation, max-stable processes, and conditional limit laws
(
24
;
37
;
17
;
42
)
.
However, extending these hard clustering approaches to multi-subject EEG data is non-trivial. EEG signals display complex and non-stationary cross-dependencies
(
47
;
41
)
, high noise levels, and significant inter-subject variability
(
33
)
. Thus, relying on a single ‘hard’ cluster in combination with standard feature-extraction techniques frequently fails to represent a subject’s behavior accurately
(
55
)
.
This leads us to develop a novel mechanism for feature extraction from the joint-tails of brain signals and propose an accurate soft clustering framework for multivariate extremes. Our method provides a membership degree to each subject based on their extremal brain connectivity structure.

One well-known soft clustering technique is fuzzy clustering, grounded in Zadeh’s fuzzy set theory
(
65
)
, which treats partitions between units as continuous transitions rather than hard boundaries. The most widely-used approach is the Fuzzy C-Means (FCM) algorithm
(
2
)
which has gained popularity in applications that include image
(
64
;
23
)
and signal processing
(
39
;
67
;
66
)
.
There are recent developments in soft clustering of multivariate signals that are quantile-based
(
31
)
, and
34
showed that canonical coherence-based fuzzy clustering is superior to the state-of-the-art fuzzy clustering, namely, traditional FCM, variable-based principal component analysis (PCA) clustering
(
20
)
, quantile cross-spectral density based fuzzy clustering
(
31
)
, wavelet-based clustering
(
11
)
, and fuzzy clustering using PCA
(
33
)
.
Hence, we apply canonical correlation based soft clustering on seizure data of neonates and presented the result in Section
2
. From our preliminary analyses, traditional canonical correlation based soft clustering do not adequately separate seizure vs non-seizure patients.
This is because these clustering methods focus on the bulk of the distribution and are not tailored to extreme events.
While
10
proposed a fuzzy clustering of extremes, this is only for univariate time series, and not multivariate brain signals.

In summary, our paper provides two key innovations: (1) a novel tail dependence measure (the CTD) between random vectors, which facilitates representation learning of extremal connectivity structure; and (2) an unsupervised soft clustering method for multivariate extremes, applied to identification of neonates with seizures.
Our goal is to provide explainable features that reveal key information contained in the tail of the joint distribution.
Thus, one of the major advantages of our method is its interpretability; our method offers a pathway to relate soft clusters and tail dependence of brain signals. Through the CTD, we can visualize the unifying characteristics of each cluster, facilitating accurate differentiation between neonates with and without seizures. Although applied to brain signals, our method is applicable in other fields, such as finance and environmental science.

2.2
Spectral Domain Features

Analyzing brain signals during high-frequency oscillations offers information on bursts of its electrical activity, e.g., seizures
(
16
;
44
)
.
We thus extract features from the EEGs that are attributed to the standard frequency bands: delta, for
\Omega=(0-4]
Hz; theta, for
\Omega=(4-8]
Hz; alpha, for
\Omega=(8-12]
Hz; beta, for
\Omega=(12-30]
Hz; gamma, for
\Omega=(30-50]
Hz.

Our approach is to divide the EEG recording into
B
disjoint blocks where each block contains
A
number of time points.
For block
b
, denote the local discrete Fourier transform (DFT) of the
j
-th channel at the fundamental frequency
\omega_{a}:=a/A
,
a=0,1,\dots,A-1
, as

d_{j,b}(\omega_{a})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{A}}\sum_{t=A(b-1)+1}^{A(b-1)+A}E_{j,t}\exp(-\text{i}2\pi\omega_{a}t),\ \ \text{for }j=1,\dots,D,

where
-\text{i}=\sqrt{-1}
and
\boldsymbol{E}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{D}
denotes the EEG signals observed at time
t
,
t=1,2,\dots,AB
, that is ‘locally stationary’: the time series is approximately stationary on each of the
B
time blocks.
Then, we compute the local periodogram (at block
b
and frequency
\omega_{a}
),

Z_{j,b}(\omega_{a})=|d_{j,b}(\omega_{a})|^{2}=\frac{1}{A}\bigg|\sum_{t=A(b-1)+1}^{A(b-1)+A}E_{j,t}\exp(-\text{i}2\pi\omega_{a}t)\bigg|^{2}.

The local periodogram
Z_{j,b}(\omega_{a})
measures the contribution of the amplitude of the oscillation at frequency
\omega_{a}
to the total variance.
Consistent with standard analyses of brain signals
(
59
)
, we aggregate the local periodogram over the standard frequency bands. Hence, the local
\Omega
-periodogram for channel
j=1,\dots D
, at time-block
b
, is

Z_{j,b}(\Omega)=\frac{1}{|\Omega|}\sum_{\{a:\mbox{SR}\times\omega_{a}\in\Omega\}}Z_{j,b}(\omega_{a}),

(1)

where SR is the sampling rate.
In our application, the sampling rate is SR = 256Hz and the local time window is two seconds; hence,
A=256\times 2=512
.

Canonical correlation analysis (CCA) finds the maximum linear association among all possible linear combinations of the columns of two matrices. Let
\boldsymbol{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{P}
and
\boldsymbol{Y}\in\mathbb{R}^{Q}
. The
CCA finds the vectors
\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{P}
and
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{Q}
that maximize the correlation between
\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{0}^{\top}\boldsymbol{X}
and
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0}^{\top}\boldsymbol{Y}
. These vectors are called “canonical directions" or “canonical variates"; and the corresponding correlation is the “canonical correlation". Precisely, the canonical correlation is defined by
21
as

\rho=\max_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{0},\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0}}\text{Cor}(\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{0}^{\top}\boldsymbol{X},\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0}^{\top}\boldsymbol{Y})^{2}\in(0,1),

subject to the constraint
\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{0}^{\top}\text{Cov}(\boldsymbol{X},\boldsymbol{X})\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{0}=\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0}^{\top}\text{Cov}(\boldsymbol{Y},\boldsymbol{Y})\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0}=1
for identifiability.

\boldsymbol{Z}_{b}^{(n)}=(\boldsymbol{X}^{(n)\top}_{b},\boldsymbol{Y}^{(n)\top}_{b})^{\top}

Our method provides a principled way of extracting connectivity-based features from the tails of the distribution to offer an interpretable and accurate way of clustering brain signals.

3.1
Background: Multivariate Regular Variation

A well-established representation of joint tail behavior is multivariate regular variation
(
48
)
. We follow
6
in its definition.

Definition 3.1
.

(
6
, Multivariate Regular Variation,)

A
P
-dimensional random vector,
\boldsymbol{X}
, is ‘regularly varying’ of order
\alpha>0
, i.e.,
{\boldsymbol{X}\in\text{RV}^{P}(\alpha)}
, if there exists a normalizing sequence
w(u)\rightarrow\infty
such that

w(u)\mathbb{P}(u^{-1}\boldsymbol{X}\in C)\overset{\mathrm{v}}{\rightarrow}\nu_{\boldsymbol{X}}(C),\;\;\;u\rightarrow\infty,

where
\nu_{\boldsymbol{X}}(\cdot)\in[-\infty,\infty]^{P}\backslash\{\boldsymbol{0}_{P}\}:=\overline{\mathbb{R}}^{P}
is a limiting measure on the space of (non-trivial) Radon measures for Borel sets
C\in[-\infty,\infty]^{P}\backslash\{\boldsymbol{0}_{P}\},
and
\overset{\mathrm{v}}{\rightarrow}
denotes ‘vague’ convergence of measures. The limit measure is
(-\alpha)-
homogeneous, i.e.,
\nu_{\boldsymbol{X}}(zC)=z^{-\alpha}\nu_{\boldsymbol{X}}(C)
, and the normalizing sequence satisfies
\lim_{u\rightarrow\infty}w(uz)/w(u)=z^{\alpha}
, for
z>0
.

Following
5
, we set
\alpha=2
. This is without loss of generality (with respect to marginal transformations) as we can always standardize the data to have
\text{RV}^{1}(2)
margins
(
26
, see, e.g.,)
.
The homogeneity of the limit measure,
\nu_{\boldsymbol{X}}(\cdot)
, makes a radial-angular representation particularly well-suited for describing multivariate regular variation. Let
(R,\boldsymbol{\psi}):=(\|\boldsymbol{x}\|,\boldsymbol{x}/\|\boldsymbol{x}\|)
be the vector of radial and angular components where
\|\cdot\|
is the L
2
norm. For large
r>0
, define the set

C(r,\Psi):=\{\boldsymbol{x}\in\overline{\mathbb{R}}^{P}:R>r,\boldsymbol{\psi}\in\Psi\},

(2)

where
\Psi\subset\mathbb{S}^{P-1}
, with
\mathbb{S}^{P-1}:=\{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{P}:\|\boldsymbol{x}\|=1\}
the unit
(P-1)
-sphere. We then define the
angular measure
as

H_{\boldsymbol{X}}(\Psi):=\nu_{\boldsymbol{X}}(C(1,\Psi))
, which allows us to quantify the relative contribution of components of
\boldsymbol{X}
to multivariate extreme events
(
6
)
. In other words,
H_{\boldsymbol{X}}
can quantify the strength of extremal association between variables through
\boldsymbol{\psi}

(see
48
, for details)
.

50
provide a summary measure of pairwise extremal association by defining the extremal dependence measure (EDM) of a vector
(X_{1},X_{2})^{\top}\in\text{RV}^{2}(2)
as

\sigma(X_{1},X_{2})=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}\psi_{1}\psi_{2}\text{d}H_{(X_{1},X_{2})}(\boldsymbol{\psi})=2\lim_{r\rightarrow\infty}\mathbb{E}(\psi_{1}\psi_{2}|R>r)\in[0,1],

where the strength of extremal dependence in
(X_{1},X_{2})^{\top}
increases with
\sigma
.

5
proposed the tail pairwise dependence matrix (TPDM) which extends the EDM to a
P\times P
matrix. We denote the TPDM by
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}\in\mathbb{R}^{P\times P}
and its elements are given by the following formula:

{\Gamma}_{jk}:=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{P-1}}\psi_{j}\psi_{k}\text{d}H_{\boldsymbol{X}}(\boldsymbol{\psi})=P\lim_{r\rightarrow\infty}\mathbb{E}(\psi_{j}\psi_{k}|R>r)

for
j,k=1,\dots,P
, where now the radius
R
and the angles
\psi_{j},\psi_{k}
are computed based on the full random vector
\boldsymbol{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{P}
. Furthermore, note that (a) the TPDM evaluates the integral over the entire set
\mathbb{S}^{P-1}
, in contrast to the set
\mathbb{S}^{1}
used in the EDM, and (b) the TPDM is a positive semi-definite and symmetric matrix.

3.2
Canonical Tail Dependence

Consider now a
D
-dimensional regularly varying vector
\boldsymbol{Z}\in\text{RV}^{D}(2)
. Suppose that
\boldsymbol{Z}
is composed of two distinct subsets, so that
\boldsymbol{Z}=(\boldsymbol{X}^{\top},\boldsymbol{Y}^{\top})^{\top}
, where
{\boldsymbol{X}\in\text{RV}^{P}(2)}
represents features from one brain region, and

\boldsymbol{Y}\in\text{RV}^{Q}(2)
represents features from a second brain region, and
{D=(P+Q)}
with
P,Q\geq 2
. We summarize extremes in each brain region using vectors
\boldsymbol{\gamma}\in\mathbb{R}^{P}
and
\boldsymbol{\beta}\in\mathbb{R}^{Q}
. Let
\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{\top}\boldsymbol{X}\in\mathbb{R}
and
\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\top}\boldsymbol{Y}\in\mathbb{R}
be the projections of
\boldsymbol{X}
and
\boldsymbol{Y}
onto the line defined by vectors
\boldsymbol{\gamma}
and
\boldsymbol{\beta}
for brain regions one and two, respectively. The canonical tail-dependence is the squared maximal EDM between the projections of the two brain regions.

Definition 3.2
.

Let
\boldsymbol{X}\in\text{RV}^{P}(2)
,
\boldsymbol{Y}\in\text{RV}^{Q}(2)
,
\boldsymbol{\gamma}\in\mathbb{R}^{P}
, and
\boldsymbol{\beta}\in\mathbb{R}^{Q}

\displaystyle\tau

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\max_{\boldsymbol{\gamma},\boldsymbol{\beta}}\left\{\sigma\left(\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{\top}\boldsymbol{X},\ \ \boldsymbol{\beta}^{\top}\boldsymbol{Y}\right)\right\}^{2}\in[0,1].

(3)

The canonical tail dependence,
\tau
,
generalizes the approach in
53
, which regresses multiple extreme covariates on a single dependent variable. With the CTD, our goal is to identify linear combinations of two sets of variables that maximize tail dependence.
Direct numerical maximization of this objective function, however, incurs a substantial computational cost, particularly for large
P
and

To drastically simplify computations, we derive an analytical solution to Equation (
3

.

\boldsymbol{Z}=(\boldsymbol{X}^{\top},\boldsymbol{Y}^{\top})^{\top}

\boldsymbol{Y}\in\text{RV}^{Q}(2)

\boldsymbol{X}

\boldsymbol{Y}

\boldsymbol{Y}

\boldsymbol{\Gamma}=\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{XX}&\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{XY}\\

\displaystyle\tau=\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}\boldsymbol{\gamma},\boldsymbol{\beta}:\\

Define

\displaystyle\nu_{\boldsymbol{Z}}(C(1,\Psi_{\zeta}))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\zeta^{-1}(\Psi_{\zeta})}\int_{1/\|\zeta(\boldsymbol{\psi})\|}^{\infty}\alpha r^{-\alpha-1}\text{d}r\text{d}H_{\boldsymbol{Z}}(\boldsymbol{\psi})

\displaystyle\int_{\zeta^{-1}(\Psi_{\zeta})}\|\zeta(\boldsymbol{\psi})\|^{\alpha}\text{d}H_{\boldsymbol{Z}}(\boldsymbol{\psi})=:H_{\boldsymbol{Z}_{\zeta}}(\boldsymbol{\psi}_{\zeta}).

We partition the set

and
\boldsymbol{Y}

\boldsymbol{\psi}:=(\boldsymbol{\psi}_{X}^{\top},\boldsymbol{\psi}_{Y}^{\top})^{\top}

\displaystyle\sigma\left(\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{\top}\boldsymbol{X},\ \ \boldsymbol{\beta}^{\top}\boldsymbol{Y}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}\psi_{1,\zeta}\psi_{2,\zeta}\ \text{d}H_{\boldsymbol{Z}_{\zeta}}(\boldsymbol{\psi}_{\zeta})

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{S}^{D-1}}\frac{\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\psi}_{X}}{\|\zeta(\boldsymbol{\psi})\|}\frac{\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\psi}_{Y}}{\|\zeta(\boldsymbol{\psi})\|}{\|\zeta(\boldsymbol{\psi})\|}^{2}\text{d}H_{\boldsymbol{Z}}(\boldsymbol{\psi})

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{S}^{D-1}}(\gamma_{1}\psi_{1}+\dots+\gamma_{P}\psi_{P})(\beta_{1}\psi_{P+1}+\dots+\beta_{Q}\psi_{D})\text{d}H_{\boldsymbol{Z}}(\boldsymbol{\psi})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\gamma_{1}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{D-1}}\psi_{1}\psi_{P+1}\text{d}H_{\boldsymbol{Z}}(\boldsymbol{\psi})\beta_{1}+\dots+\gamma_{P}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{D-1}}\psi_{P}\psi_{D}\text{d}H_{\boldsymbol{Z}}(\boldsymbol{\psi})\beta_{Q}

\displaystyle\gamma_{1}\Gamma_{1,P+1}\beta_{1}+\dots+\gamma_{P}\Gamma_{P,D}\beta_{Q}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{XY}\boldsymbol{\beta},

where the second line follows from Equation (
4
) and the transformation
\boldsymbol{\psi}_{\zeta}:=\zeta(\boldsymbol{\psi})/\|\zeta(\boldsymbol{\psi})\|
. Note that the surface Jacobian of this transformation is equal to one. ∎

Proposition
3.1
gives a straightforward estimator of the CTD. After estimating the TPDM,
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}
, we solve the optimization problem in Equation (

) through Proposition
3.1
, i.e., the optimization of CTD reduces to an eigen-decomposition problem similar to Theorem 10.2.1 in

.
We then employ this optimization problem to characterize a novel connectivity structure in the joint extremes of two vectors, which we call “canonical tail variates” (defined below).

Definition 3.3
.

Let
\boldsymbol{X}\in\text{RV}^{P}(2)
,
\boldsymbol{Y}\in\text{RV}^{Q}(2)
,
\boldsymbol{\gamma}\in\mathbb{R}^{P}
, and
\boldsymbol{\beta}\in\mathbb{R}^{Q}
. Further, let
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{XX}\in\mathbb{R}^{P\times P}
and
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{YY}\in\mathbb{R}^{Q\times Q}
be the TPDMs of
\boldsymbol{X}
and
\boldsymbol{Y}
, respectively. The “canonical tail-variates”, denoted by
\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{*},\boldsymbol{\beta}^{*}
, are defined as

\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{*},\boldsymbol{\beta}^{*}=\argmax_{\boldsymbol{\gamma},\boldsymbol{\beta}}\left\{\sigma\left(\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{\top}\boldsymbol{X},\ \ \boldsymbol{\beta}^{\top}\boldsymbol{Y}\right)\right\}^{2},\\

(5)

subject to the constraint
\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{XX}\boldsymbol{\gamma}=\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{YY}\boldsymbol{\beta}=1
for identifiability.
Furthermore, the “extremal scores” are defined as
\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{*\top}\boldsymbol{X}
and
\boldsymbol{\beta}^{*\top}\boldsymbol{Y}
.

From Definition
3.3
and Proposition
3.1
, it follows that the canonical tail-variates can be computed as

\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{*},\boldsymbol{\beta}^{*}=\argmax_{\begin{subarray}{c}\boldsymbol{\gamma},\boldsymbol{\beta}:\\
\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{XX}\boldsymbol{\gamma}=\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{YY}\boldsymbol{\beta}=1\end{subarray}}\{\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{XY}\boldsymbol{\beta}\}^{2}.

Theorem 10.2.1 in
35
further implies that
{\tau}
is the first eigenvalue of

\boldsymbol{G}_{1}={\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}_{XX}^{-1}{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}_{XY}{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}_{YY}^{-1}{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}_{YX}\ \ \text{or, equivalently, of}\ \ \boldsymbol{G}_{2}={\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}_{YY}^{-1}{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}_{YX}{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}_{XX}^{-1}{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}_{XY}.

(6)

Moreover, the eigenvectors that correspond to the largest eigenvalues of
\boldsymbol{G}_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{P\times P}
and
\boldsymbol{G}_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{Q\times Q}
are defined, respectively, as

\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{1}:=\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{XX}^{1/2}\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{P}\ \ \text{and}\ \ \boldsymbol{\lambda}_{2}:=\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{YY}^{1/2}\boldsymbol{\beta}^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{Q},

(7)

which we refer to as the “tail-topology”.
The absolute value of our tail-topology reflects the contribution of each component in attaining maximum tail dependence between two random vectors. In applications to brain signals, the tail-topology captures the weights assigned to each channel that drive the two brain regions to produce high amplitudes simultaneously.

3.3
Fuzzy Clustering of Multivariate Extremes

We now develop a fuzzy clustering algorithm based on CTD. One novel feature of our method is that it uses the first eigenvectors (that correspond to the largest eigenvalues),
\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{1}
and
\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{2}
, of the canonical tail-dependence (as defined in Equation (
7
)) to represent the topology of multivariate extremes.
Suppose we have
N
subjects, each with
D
-variate data
\boldsymbol{Z}_{b}^{(n)}\in\text{RV}^{D}(2)
, for
n=1,\dots,N
, and time block
b=1,\dots,B
.
We now provide a step-by-step procedure for Fuzzy Clustering of Multivariate Extremes via the CTD.

Step 1:

Estimate the CTD.

We follow
5
for estimating the TPDM. For each subject data
\{\boldsymbol{Z}^{(n)}_{b}\}_{b=1}^{B}
,
n=1,\dots,N
, estimate the elements of the TPDM, denoted by
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}^{(n)}\in\mathbb{R}^{D\times D}
, by

\widehat{\Gamma}^{(n)}_{jk}=\frac{D}{c}\sum_{b=1}^{B}\frac{Z^{(n)}_{j,b}Z^{(n)}_{k,b}}{\{\|\boldsymbol{Z}^{(n)}_{b}\|\}^{2}}\mathbb{I}{\{\|\boldsymbol{Z}^{(n)}_{b}\|>r_{n}\}},

(8)

for
j,k=1,\dots,D
, where
\mathbb{I}{\{\cdot\}}
is the indicator function,

c=\sum_{b=1}^{B}\mathbb{I}{\{\|\boldsymbol{Z}^{(n)}_{b}\|>r_{n}\}}
is the number of radial exceedances above the threshold
r_{n}>0
and
\|\cdot\|
is the L
2
norm. Hereafter, we take
r_{n}
as the empirical
q
-th quantile, i.e.,

r_{n}:=\hat{F}^{-1}_{\|\boldsymbol{Z}^{(n)}_{b}\|}(q)

where
\hat{F}_{\|\boldsymbol{Z}^{(n)}_{b}\|}(q)
is the empirical distribution function of
\{\|\boldsymbol{Z}^{(n)}_{b}\|\}_{b=1}^{B}
and
q\in(0,1)
is a probability level close to one. We advocate conducting a sensitivity analysis to choose the value of
r_{n},n=1,\dots,N
.
Using
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}^{(n)}
, we obtain estimates of the tail topology,
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{1}^{(n)}
and
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{2}^{(n)}
, through the eigen-decomposition in Equation (
6
).

Step 2:

Stack the tail-connectivity features.

Here, we take the absolute tail-topologies.
32
showed that taking the absolute values of eigenvectors stabilizes clustering assignment and avoids ambiguity brought by the signs of the vectors.
Denote the set of absolute CTD eigenvectors of
\{\boldsymbol{Z}_{b}^{(n)}\}_{b=1}^{B}
to be

{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{n}=\left(\lvert\widehat{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{1}^{(n)\top}\rvert,\lvert\widehat{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{2}^{(n)\top}\rvert\right)^{\top}\in[0,\infty)^{D}},

for
{n=1,\dots,N}
.
Hence, for
N
subjects, we have
{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}=\{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{N}\}\in[0,\infty)^{D\times N}}
as the collection of tail-topology features.

Step 3:

Fuzzy clustering and computation of the membership matrix.

We then perform a fuzzy
C
-means clustering (FCM) based on
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}
.
FCM involves solving for a set of
S\in\mathbb{N}
cluster centers

\overline{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}=\left\{\overline{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}_{1},\dots,\overline{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}_{S}\right\}\in[0,\infty)^{S}
,
and the fuzzy coefficient matrix, denoted as
\boldsymbol{U}\in[0,1]^{N\times S}
.
The elements of the fuzzy coefficient matrix,
U_{ns}
, quantify the membership degree for subject
n
to cluster
s\in\{1,\dots,S\}
. The FCM method finds
\overline{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}
and
\boldsymbol{U}
by solving the minimization problem:

\min_{\overline{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}},\boldsymbol{U}}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\sum_{s=1}^{S}(U_{ns})^{m}\left\|\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{n}-\overline{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}_{s}\right\|^{2},\ \ \text{subject to }\ \sum_{s=1}^{S}U_{ns}=1,\ U_{ns}\geq 0,\forall n=1,\dots,N,

where
m\in(1,3)
is the fuzziness parameter that governs the extent of cluster overlap in the partition.
This optimization problem is solved via an iterative procedure, i.e., we update the current values of
\boldsymbol{U}
using

U_{ns}=\left(\sum_{s^{\prime}\in\{1,\dots,S\}\backslash s}\left(\frac{\left\|\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{n}-\overline{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}_{s}\right\|^{2}}{\left\|\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{n}-\overline{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}_{(s^{\prime})}\right\|^{2}}\right)^{\frac{1}{m-1}}\right)^{-1},

where
s^{\prime}\neq s
. For the centers, we update them according to

\overline{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}_{s}=\frac{W(m)\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{n}}{W(m)},\ \ \text{where }W(m)=\sum_{n=1}^{N}(U_{ns})^{m}.

Step 4:

Label assignment.
Using the estimated membership matrix
\boldsymbol{U}
, assign a label to each subject
n=1,\dots,N
by selecting a cut-off. In this paper, if
U_{ns}>0.7
, we assign subject
n
to cluster
s
. If
{U_{ns}<0.7}
for all
s=1,\dots,S
, then the subject is declared as a fuzzy-subject, i.e., the subject has tail characteristics that are compatible with more than one cluster.

For data-driven selection of
S
, we could exploit a number of cluster validity indices, e.g., the fuzzy silhouette index from
46
. In our application, we set
S=2
, in order to identify groups of neonates who have had seizure. We use experts’ annotations as the true cluster labels and create a confusion matrix,
\boldsymbol{M}\in\{0,\dots,N\}^{2\times 2}
, to measure accuracy. The rows of
\boldsymbol{M}
are the experts’ labels and the columns are the estimated cluster labels. We calculate the accuracy as

\text{Accuracy}=\frac{\max\{M_{11}+M_{22},M_{12}+M_{21}\}}{N}\in[0.5,1].

(9)

We perform the clustering for different values of
m
in the interval
(1,3)
and select the
m
that gives the highest accuracy.

6
Conclusion

In this paper, we have developed a novel method that addresses the problem of clustering extreme events—which is prevalent in many areas including
climate and finance, as well as biology and neuroscience. However, this task is non-trivial, especially when data are jointly heavy-tailed. In this paper, we extract unique features lurking at the tails of the distribution which enhance discrimination of extreme events, and paves the way for representation learning of rare events. In particular, we develop a novel way of characterizing multivariate tail-connectivity based on multivariate regular-variation
(
48
)
.
Our proposed canonical tail dependence (CTD) measure is the first of its kind to offer tail-topology of connectivity between two random vectors. The CTD finds the combination of variables that provide the maximal tail-dependence between groups—we solve for unique weights attributed to each component that achieves the maximal tail-dependence of two linear combinations. These weights form the tail-topology that facilitates visualization and interpretation of clusters, highlighting a major advantage of our method.

We provide an efficient way of estimating the CTD through the tail pairwise dependence matrix
(
5
)
.
We embed the CTD estimates into the fuzzy C-means algorithm, thereby developing a novel
soft clustering of multivariate extremes
. The application to neonatal EEGs revealed interesting tail-topology of epileptic and non-epileptic neonates. At the gamma band, channels in non-epileptic patients contribute equally to the maximal tail-dependence (or CTD), while few specific channels in epileptic patients are likely to be the key drivers of the CTD.

Our method is currently limited to stationary but jointly heavy-tailed signals. Moreover, the CTD considers linear combinations of regularly varying variables. Our future work will focus on weakening the stationarity assumption by using wavelets
(
15
;
51
)
as a basis, instead of Fourier coefficients. Additionally, one may also extend our approach to the case of asymptotic independence of signals, i.e., hidden regular variation
(
49
)
.
Another interesting direction is to develop a data-driven approach to select the optimal groups that maximize clustering accuracy, such as fuzzy co-clustering
(
28
)
. Furthermore, future studies may consider the maximal extremal dependence between non-linear combinations of vectors. This could utilize deep learning which has already seen effective use in capturing complex tail-dependencies
(
7
;
56
;
22
, see e.g.,)
, and has already seen use in canonical correlation analyses
(
1
)
.
