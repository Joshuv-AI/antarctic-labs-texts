Denture use is associated with a higher risk of cholelithiasis: a prospective cohort study
Authors: Pengfei Li, Jinyu Zhao, Ningning Mi, Wangping He, Zhengping An, Chenyang Wei, Sheng Chen, Xiaodi Wang, Long Gao, Yanyan Lin, Ping Yue, Jinqiu Yuan, Wenbo Meng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesTo investigate whether denture use is associated with an increased risk of cholelithiasis and to examine the combined effects of denture use and genetic susceptibility on cholelithiasis risk.MethodsThis prospective cohort study included 446,743 participants free of cholelithiasis at baseline from the UK Biobank. Denture use was assessed via touchscreen questionnaire at baseline. Incident cholelithiasis was identified through hospital records. Cox proportional hazards models were used to estimate hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Polygenic risk scores (PRS) for cholelithiasis were constructed to assess genetic susceptibility. Mediation analysis was performed to quantify the contributions of metabolic and dietary pathways. Joint analyses with oral symptoms were conducted to disentangle denture use from concurrent oral diseases. Two-sample Mendelian randomization (MR) was used to evaluate potential causality.ResultsOver a median follow-up of 13.69 years, 16,715 incident cholelithiasis cases were documented. Denture use was associated with a 29% higher risk of cholelithiasis after full adjustment (HR = 1.29, 95% CI: 1.24–1.34). The risk was highest among denture users with high genetic risk (HR = 1.57, 95% CI: 1.43–1.71). No multiplicative or additive interaction was observed between denture use and genetic risk. In joint analyses, denture use alone was associated with substantially higher risks than oral symptoms alone, with a significant dose–response gradient across ordered categories (P for trend < 0.001). Mediation analysis showed that BMI mediated 12.96% of the total association, while dietary characteristics mediated less than 1%. MR analysis supported a potential causal relationship (IVW OR = 1.97, 95% CI: 1.05–3.70). Results remained robust across multiple sensitivity analyses.ConclusionsDenture use was independently associated with a higher risk of cholelithiasis, regardless of genetic susceptibility. The association persisted after accounting for oral symptoms. These findings identify denture use as a novel factor that may contribute to cholelithiasis risk and suggest a potential role of oral health surveillance in gallstone prevention.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12903-026-08648-1.

Introduction

Cholelithiasis is a common gastrointestinal disorder that raises the demand for emergency surgeries and hospital admissions, creating a considerable socio-economic burden [1–3]. It affects approximately 6% of the global population, and its incidence is rising [1]. Gallstone formation involves the interplay of genetic susceptibility, hepatic cholesterol hypersecretion, and bile acid metabolism dysregulation [3]. Cholesterol supersaturation in bile represents the critical step in the pathogenesis of cholesterol gallstones, which account for the majority of cases [3, 4]. Identifying new modifiable risk factors for cholelithiasis is essential for primary prevention.

As the global population ages, more people are losing teeth, leading to increased use of removable dentures. The United Nations predicted a significant rise in the denture-wearing population by 2050 [5]. As a key predictor of oral health–related quality of life [6, 7], dentures restore chewing ability and enhance appearance, but issues such as material composition, cleaning difficulties, and long-term use may cause biofilm buildup and overgrowth of pathogens like Candida albicans, causing denture stomatitis and disruption of oral microbiota [8]. Emerging research on the “oral-gut” axis suggests that oral microorganisms can affect distant organs by migrating to the gut, altering the intestinal microbiota, and contributing to systemic inflammation [9–11]. Many oral bacteria can survive gastrointestinal transit when protected within salivary aggregates or food particles, ultimately colonizing the intestine [11]. Studies have documented distinct oral microbiota profiles in denture users, with Streptococcus and Neisseria being more dominant among denture users compared to non-denture users [12], and Candida species being more prevalent on denture surfaces [13]. Additionally, compared to natural teeth, dentures, especially removable ones, may reduce chewing efficiency, causing users to prefer softer, low-fiber foods [14, 15], which could affect lipid metabolism and bile composition [4]. Since cholelithiasis is also associated with imbalances in the gut microbiota, bile acid metabolism, and dietary habits [4, 16, 17], we hypothesize that denture use may increase the risk of cholelithiasis.

While a few studies have explored the link between denture use and cardiovascular diseases [18, 19], the role in digestive system diseases, especially cholelithiasis, is very limited. Most studies on oral health have examined periodontal disease or tooth loss [20, 21], overlooking dentures as a distinct exposure. Notably, earlier studies linked tooth loss to gallstone disease [22, 23], but whether dentures modify this risk has not been investigated.

Genetic susceptibility also plays a key role in gallstone formation [24], with genome-wide association studies (GWAS) identifying relevant genetic loci [25, 26]. These loci form the basis for polygenic risk scores (PRS), which assess genetic risk for cholelithiasis. However, PRS predictions vary depending on environmental exposure, suggesting that environmental factors for cholelithiasis may have an amplified effect in populations with high genetic predisposition [26–28]. So far, no studies have explored whether the link between denture use and cholelithiasis risk varies by genetic susceptibility quantified by PRS.

To address these knowledge gaps, we utilized the UK Biobank database to investigate the relationships among denture use, genetic susceptibility, and cholelithiasis risk. We also conducted mediation analysis to quantify the contributions of metabolic and dietary pathways, joint analyses with oral symptoms to separate denture use from concurrent oral diseases, and Mendelian randomization to evaluate potential causality.

Methods

Study population

This prospective cohort study utilized data from the UK Biobank, which recruited over 500,000 individuals aged 40–69 from 22 centers across England, Wales, and Scotland [29]. Participants provided demographic, lifestyle, medical history, and medication data through touchscreen questionnaires, interviews, and physical measurements during the baseline assessment. Further details regarding the UK Biobank have been published elsewhere [30]. The UK Biobank obtained ethics approval from the National Research Ethics Committee (REC ID: 16/NW/0274).

In the present study, we included 502,250 participants with available data. After excluding individuals with missing data and those unwilling to respond to the oral health questionnaire (n = 6,470), as well as those with a history of cancer, cholelithiasis, or cholecystectomy at baseline (n = 47,978), along with those with missing lifestyle data (n = 1,059), a total of 446,743 participants were included in the primary analysis. Of these, 339,737 White participants with complete genetic data were involved in the gene–environment interaction analysis (Supplementary Figure S2). No duplicate participants were identified, as UK Biobank assigns a unique identifier to each participant.

Assessment of main exposure

All oral and dental symptoms were self-reported at the baseline assessment. In this study, we used a touchscreen question related to mouth/teeth problems from the UK Biobank [30–32], with data field 6149, covering 495,780 participants. The touchscreen question was asked, “Do you have any of the following? (You can select more than one answer)”. The choices included “Mouth ulcers”, “Painful gums”, “Bleeding gums”, “Loose teeth”, “Toothache”, “Dentures”, and “None of the above”. The main exposure in this study was defined as selecting “Dentures” (Supplementary Table S1). Based on these oral symptoms, periodontitis was defined as the presence of at least one of bleeding gums, painful gums, or loose teeth, consistent with previous studies using UK Biobank self-reported oral health data [20, 21]. Any oral complaint was defined as the presence of at least one of the five symptoms.

Assessment of outcomes

The primary outcome was incident cholelithiasis, confirmed through hospital records linked to the Health and Social Care Information Centre (England and Wales) and the National Health Service Central Register (Scotland). The diagnoses of cholelithiasis were coded according to the International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision (ICD-10), recorded as K80.

Construction of polygenic risk score for cholelithiasis

Summary statistics for cholelithiasis were obtained from FinnGen Release 9 (https://r9.risteys.finngen.fi/endpoints/K11_CHOLELITH), which included 37,041 cases and 330,903 controls of European ancestry defined by the ICD-10 code K80. Detailed quality control procedures for the GWAS data are provided in the Supplementary Methods. Following the approach described by Mi et al. [26], we used a pruning and thresholding method to derive a series of candidate polygenic risk scores (PRS) across a grid of significance thresholds (p = 1, 0.5, 5 × 10⁻2, 5 × 10⁻3, 5 × 10⁻4, 5 × 10⁻5, 5 × 10⁻⁶, 5 × 10⁻⁷, and 5 × 10⁻⁸) and linkage disequilibrium clumping r2 thresholds (0.1, 0.01, and 0.001) within a 1000 kb window. In short, the number of effect alleles (0, 1, or 2) for each individual was multiplied by the effect size of SNPs associated with cholelithiasis, and then summed to derive PRS for all individuals in the UK Biobank. The goodness of fit of each candidate PRS was evaluated using pseudo R2 (Nagelkerke’s) and the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) in logistic regression models. Among these candidates, the combination of p < 5 × 10⁻6 and r2 < 0.001 demonstrated the highest discriminative capability and was therefore selected for all subsequent analyses. This optimal PRS comprised 13 independent single-nucleotide polymorphisms (Supplementary Table S2). PRS distribution plots, pseudo R2 comparisons, and AUC evaluations are provided in Supplementary Figures S5–S9. For analysis, genetic risk was categorized as low (quintile 1), intermediate (quintiles 2–4), and high (quintile 5), as described previously [26–28].

Assessment of covariates

At enrollment, information was collected through a touchscreen questionnaire and face-to-face interviews, including socio-demographic factors (age, gender, ethnicity), anthropometric measurements (height and weight), lifestyle factors (smoking status, alcohol intake, physical activity, and diet), and medical history (type 2 diabetes, hypertension, hypertriglyceridemia, hypercholesterolemia, nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAID) use, multivitamin and mineral supplement intake). Physical activity was assessed using the validated short-form International Physical Activity Questionnaire [33]. Dietary characteristics were evaluated according to American Heart Association guidelines [34] (Supplementary Table S1). The Index of Multiple Deprivation (IMD), reflecting socioeconomic status, was directly obtained from the UK Biobank. Body Mass Index (BMI) was calculated as weight divided by height squared (kg/m2).

Self-reported overall health status and longstanding illness were initially considered as potential covariates. However, overall health status exhibited moderate multicollinearity with other model covariates (variance inflation factor [VIF] > 5; Supplementary Table S4), and both variables are subjective measures likely to lie on the causal pathway or act as proxies for conditions already adjusted for. Therefore, they were excluded from the final models to avoid overadjustment bias and model instability.

Statistical analysis

Baseline characteristics were presented as means (standard deviation, SD) for continuous variables and counts (percentages) for categorical variables. The follow-up duration in person-years was calculated from the assessment date for each participant until the first diagnosis of cholelithiasis, death, or the last follow-up date (May 31, 2022), whichever occurred first.

Cox proportional hazards models

To guide covariate selection and clarify the assumed causal structure, we constructed a directed acyclic graph (DAG; Supplementary Figure S1) distinguishing confounders from potential mediators. Based on this framework, we constructed three nested Cox proportional hazards models to evaluate the association between denture use and cholelithiasis risk. Model 1 adjusted for demographic factors: age, gender, ethnicity, and IMD. Model 2 additionally adjusted for lifestyle factors: smoking status, alcohol intake, and physical activity. Model 3 (total effect model) further adjusted for medical history and medication use: hypertension, NSAID use, multivitamin use, and mineral supplement intake. Potential mediators—BMI, type 2 diabetes, triglycerides, cholesterol, and dietary characteristics—were not included in Model 3 to avoid overadjustment bias. Model fit indices (AIC, BIC, LogLik) and likelihood ratio tests for nested models are provided in Supplementary Table S3. The proportional hazards assumption was verified using Schoenfeld residuals, with no violations detected.

Mediation analysis

To quantify the extent to which the association between denture use and cholelithiasis was mediated by metabolic and dietary factors, we performed formal causal mediation analysis using the mediation package in R. Each potential mediator (BMI, type 2 diabetes, triglycerides, cholesterol, and dietary characteristics) was evaluated in a separate model adjusted for all Model 3 covariates. Average causal mediation effects (ACME), average direct effects (ADE), and the proportion mediated were estimated using a representative 20% random subsample (n = 88,869) due to computational constraints. The subsample was representative of the full cohort in terms of all baseline characteristics (Supplementary Table S10).

Gene–environment interaction analysis

To assess whether the association between denture use and cholelithiasis varied by genetic susceptibility, participants were cross-classified by denture use (yes, no) and PRS category (low, intermediate, high). Multiplicative interaction was formally tested using the likelihood ratio test comparing models with and without the product term (denture use × PRS). Additive interaction was also evaluated using measures of relative excess risk due to interaction (RERI), attributable proportion (AP), and synergy index (SI) (Supplementary Table S6).

Subgroup and joint analyses

We assessed potential effect modification by demographic factors (age and gender), lifestyle factors (smoking status, alcohol intake, and physical activity), and medical history (hypertension, NSAID use, multivitamin use, and mineral supplement intake) using stratified analyses. Multiplicative interaction was tested using likelihood ratio tests. For subgroup variables comprising more than two groups (age and physical activity), interaction P values were evaluated against a Bonferroni-corrected threshold of P < 0.025 (0.05/2). All subgroup analyses were considered exploratory and hypothesis-generating.

We further examined whether the association between denture use and cholelithiasis differed by the presence of individual oral symptoms. Subgroup analyses were conducted by stratifying participants according to the presence of each oral symptom (bleeding gums, painful gums, loose teeth, toothache, and mouth ulcers), and P for interaction was derived from likelihood ratio tests.

To disentangle the effect of denture use from the underlying oral diseases, we jointly classified participants by denture use and the presence of oral symptoms (bleeding gums, painful gums, loose teeth, toothache, mouth ulcers, as well as periodontitis and any oral complaint, as defined above). Participants were categorized into four groups: neither denture nor oral disease, oral disease only, denture only, or both. P for trend was calculated by modeling ordinal categories (0 = neither, 1 = oral symptom only, 2 = denture only, 3 = both) as a continuous variable in the Cox model.

Sensitivity analyses

To evaluate the robustness of the findings, several sensitivity analyses were conducted. First, we excluded cholelithiasis cases occurring within the first 2 or 4 years of follow-up to mitigate potential reverse causation. Second, we performed propensity score matching (PSM) using 1:2 nearest neighbor matching without replacement (caliper = 0.2) to estimate the average treatment effect on the treated (ATT). Matching was based on 11 baseline covariates: age, gender, ethnicity, IMD, smoking status, alcohol intake, physical activity, hypertension, NSAID use, multivitamin use, and mineral supplement intake. Covariate balance before and after matching was assessed using standardized mean differences (SMD), with SMD < 0.1 indicating acceptable balance (Table 1 and Supplementary Figure S4). Third, we restricted the exposure to participants reporting denture use without any other oral symptoms and compared them with those reporting no oral problems. Fourth, we applied the Fine–Gray competing risk model treating death as a competing event in a representative 20% random subsample (n = 89,348; Supplementary Table S11). Fifth, for female participants, we additionally adjusted for menopausal status, hormone replacement therapy (HRT) use, and oral contraceptive use in a sensitivity analysis. Finally, we calculated the E-value to quantify the minimum strength of association that an unmeasured confounder would need to have with both exposure and outcome to fully explain away the observed Mendelian randomization analysis

Two-sample Mendelian randomization (MR) was performed to evaluate the potential causal effect between denture use and cholelithiasis. Genetic instruments for denture use were obtained from a GWAS of UK Biobank participants of European ancestry (IEU OpenGWAS ID: ukb-b-12930; N = 461,113; 77,732 cases/383,381 controls). Summary statistics for cholelithiasis were obtained from FinnGen Release 9 (37,041 cases/330,903 controls). Instrumental SNPs were selected at genome-wide significance (p = 5 × 10⁻⁸) and clumped for independence (r2 = 0.1, 1000 kb window). Three palindromic and ambiguous SNPs (rs2349034, rs6925047, rs7367897) were excluded, leaving 61 SNPs for the primary analyses. The inverse variance weighted (IVW) method was used as the primary analysis. Sensitivity analyses included weighted median, MR-Egger, weighted mode, and simple mode methods. Instrument strength was assessed using the F-statistic, with values > 10 indicating no weak instrument bias. Heterogeneity was evaluated using Cochran's Q test, and horizontal pleiotropy was assessed using the MR-Egger intercept test. Detailed MR results are provided in Supplementary Table S9 and Supplementary Figure S10.

All analyses were conducted using R version 4.5.3. A two-tailed P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant unless otherwise specified.

Reporting guidelines

This study was conducted and reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines for cohort studies. The completed STROBE checklist is provided as a supplementary file.

Results

Baseline characteristics of participants

This study included 446,743 participants, of whom 71,468 (16.0%) reported denture use at baseline. Table 1 presents the baseline characteristics before and after propensity score matching. Before matching, denture users were more likely to be older, male, White, current smokers, and less likely to be current drinkers. They also had a higher IMD, a greater prevalence of hypertension, and higher BMI, triglycerides, and type 2 diabetes prevalence. After 1:2 propensity score matching on 11 baseline covariates, all standardized mean differences were below 0.1, indicating good balance between groups (N = 135,620 non-denture users; N = 71,442 denture users).

Denture use and the risk of cholelithiasis

In the crude model, denture use was associated with a 42% increased risk of cholelithiasis (HR = 1.42, 95% CI: 1.37–1.47). Mediation analysis

Formal mediation analysis showed that BMI, type 2 diabetes, triglycerides, cholesterol, and dietary characteristics each partially mediated the association between denture use and cholelithiasis (Table 3). To disentangle the effect of denture use from the underlying oral diseases, we jointly classified participants by both denture use and oral symptom status. The results revealed a consistent pattern across all seven oral symptom definitions: participants with oral symptoms alone showed only modestly elevated risks, whereas those with dentures alone had substantially higher risks. Specifically, the hazard ratios for oral symptoms alone ranged from 1.08 to 1.22, while the hazard ratios for dentures alone ranged from 1.30 to 1.34. P for trend across ordered categories (0 = neither, 1 = oral symptom only, 2 = denture only, 3 = both) was < 0.001 for all individual symptoms and composite definitions (periodontitis and any oral complaint).

Sensitivity analyses

The main findings were consistent across all sensitivity analyses (Supplementary Table S8). After excluding events within the first 2 or 4 years of follow-up, the HRs were 1.28 (95% CI: 1.23–1.34) and 1.28 (95% CI: 1.22–1.33), respectively. In the PSM cohort, the HR was 1.28 (95% CI: 1.23–1.34). When restricting the exposure to participants reporting denture use without any other oral symptoms, the association remained robust (HR = 1.33, 95% CI: 1.27–1.39). The Fine–Gray competing risk model treating death as a competing event yielded an HR of 1.30 (95% CI: 1.19–1.42). Among female participants, the HR was 1.34 both without and with additional adjustment for menopausal status, HRT use, and oral contraceptive use. The E-value for the main association was 1.90 (point estimate) and 1.79 (lower CI), suggesting that substantial unmeasured confounding would be required to fully explain away the observed association.

Discussion

This large prospective cohort study examined the associations between denture use, genetic predisposition, and cholelithiasis risk. Our results indicate that denture use is associated with an increased risk of cholelithiasis, independent of demographic factors, lifestyle behaviors, medical history, and medication use. By incorporating a PRS to assess genetic susceptibility for cholelithiasis, we found that the risk was highest among denture users with high genetic risk. Joint analyses with oral symptoms further demonstrated that the risk associated with denture use persisted even in the absence of concurrent oral disease. Mendelian randomization analysis provided supportive evidence for a potential causal relationship.

To our knowledge, this is the first prospective cohort study to investigate the association between denture use and cholelithiasis. While dentures are widely used, particularly in aging populations, their relationship with digestive system diseases has received little attention. Previous research on oral health and systemic diseases has primarily focused on periodontal disease [20, 21], overlooking denture use as a distinct exposure. Notably, two earlier studies have linked tooth loss to gallstone disease. Bhandari et al. reported that missing teeth were an independent predictive factor for ultrasound-confirmed gallstone disease in a US population (adjusted OR: 1.7, 95% CI: 1.1–2.8) [22]. More recently, Yu et al. found that a higher number of missing teeth was associated with an increased prevalence of gallstones (OR: 1.93, 95% CI: 1.14–3.26), with blood glucose partly mediating this association (mediation proportion: 4.91%) [23]. However, neither study evaluated whether the missing teeth had been replaced with dentures, a distinction that may have important biological implications given the distinct microbial colonization patterns associated with denture surfaces. Our findings extend this literature by demonstrating that the association between denture use and cholelithiasis is independent of the underlying oral diseases that led to tooth replacement.

Our joint analysis of denture use and oral symptoms was designed to examine whether the observed association is attributable to denture use itself or to the underlying oral diseases that led to tooth replacement. Across seven oral symptom definitions, participants with oral symptoms alone showed only modestly elevated risks, whereas those with dentures alone had substantially higher risks. The dose–response gradient across ordered categories (P for trend < 0.001) indicated that cholelithiasis risk increased monotonically with greater oral health burden, and that denture use was the primary driver of this escalation. The combined presence of dentures and oral symptoms did not further increase the risk beyond that associated with dentures alone. These findings do not support the hypothesis that denture use is simply a marker of severe oral diseases.

Our study suggests two potential mechanisms through which denture use may increase cholelithiasis risk. First, dentures may disrupt oral microbiota, leading to dysbiosis that affects the gut microbiota via the "oral-gut" axis, potentially disrupting bile acid metabolism and contributing to gallstone formation. Oral bacteria can influence intestinal flora through the bloodstream, immune cells, and direct transmission to the intestines [11]. Recent research indicates that gut microbiota can promote cholesterol gallstone formation by modulating bile acid composition and biliary cholesterol secretion [16, 17, 35]. Studies have also confirmed changes in the oral flora of denture users, with Streptococcus and Neisseria being more dominant in denture users compared to non-users [12, 13]. Second, dentures may reduce chewing efficiency, leading users to favor softer, low-fiber foods [14, 15], which could affect lipid metabolism and bile composition. Jin et al. showed that diets rich in fruits, vegetables, and whole grains can lower cholelithiasis risk [4].

We further explored the relative contributions of these pathways through formal mediation analysis. Dietary characteristics mediated less than 1% of the total association, and BMI was the strongest mediator at 12.96%, followed by type 2 diabetes and blood lipids. These modest mediation proportions indicate that metabolic and dietary factors only partially explain the association, and that the contribution of diet may be even smaller than expected. However, our dietary assessment relied on a brief food frequency questionnaire categorized as a binary variable, which may not fully capture the specific dietary dimensions relevant to cholesterol gallstone formation, particularly fiber intake and fat composition. Therefore, the role of diet cannot be definitively excluded based on our data alone. Taken together, these findings are consistent with the hypothesis that the oral-gut axis may play a more prominent role, although this remains speculative in the absence of direct microbiome data. Further research incorporating detailed dietary assessment and microbiota sequencing is needed to clarify the relative importance of these pathways.

In constructing our analytical models, we applied a systematic approach to covariate selection and model building. A directed acyclic graph was used to distinguish confounders from potential mediators, and three nested Cox models were constructed with sequential adjustment for demographic, lifestyle, medical history, and medication use. Model fit was assessed using AIC, BIC, and likelihood ratio tests, which confirmed progressive improvement with each step of adjustment (Supplementary Table S3). This structured approach enhances the transparency and interpretability of our effect estimates.

Subgroup analyses of demographic, lifestyle, medical history, and medication use showed that the association between denture use and cholelithiasis was broadly consistent across strata, with HRs ranging from 1.23 to 1.40. For subgroup variables comprising more than two groups (age and physical activity), interaction P values were evaluated against a Bonferroni-corrected threshold of P < 0.025 and did not reach statistical significance; for binary variables, nominal P values are reported. All subgroup analyses should be considered exploratory. In additional analyses stratified by the presence of individual oral symptoms, the association was generally consistent. A nominally significant interaction was observed for loose teeth, where the association was attenuated in participants with loose teeth. This may reflect that shared risk factors for tooth loss and gallstone formation, such as chronic inflammation or metabolic disturbances, play a dominant role when dentition is severely compromised, making the additional contribution of dentures less apparent.

PRS has been shown to effectively stratify cholelithiasis risk in previous studies [25, 26, 36]. We did not observe a multiplicative or additive interaction between denture use and genetic susceptibility, suggesting that the two factors operate through largely independent pathways. Denture users with high genetic risk had the highest absolute risk, underscoring the importance of considering both factors in risk assessment. From a genetic perspective, gallstone-associated variants are primarily involved in hepatic cholesterol secretion and bile acid metabolism. For instance, variants in the ABCG5/8 gene locus, which encodes cholesterol transporters responsible for biliary cholesterol excretion, represent the most well-established genetic risk factor for gallstone disease [37]. In addition, GCKR and CYP7A1 variants affect hepatic lipid synthesis and bile acid production, respectively, further contributing to gallstone susceptibility [38]. Denture use, in contrast, likely exerts its effects through the oral–gut axis and related metabolic disturbances, a pathway distinct from these established genetic mechanisms. The convergence of these independent pathways may explain why individuals with both risk factors face the highest risk, even in the absence of a statistical interaction. These findings underscore the clinical importance of considering both behavioral and genetic factors in risk assessment.

The present study has several strengths, including its prospective design, large sample size, long follow-up period, and comprehensive adjustment for confounders guided by a directed acyclic graph. The use of multiple complementary analytical approaches enhanced the robustness of the findings. These included propensity score matching, Fine-Gray competing risk modeling, formal mediation analysis, joint analysis of denture use and oral symptoms, and Mendelian randomization. The E-value analysis indicated that substantial unmeasured confounding would be required to fully explain away the observed association.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the observational design cannot definitively establish causality, although the consistency across sensitivity analyses and the supportive Mendelian randomization findings provide some reassurance against residual confounding and reverse causation. Second, denture use was assessed through self-report at a single baseline time point, which may introduce misclassification and does not capture changes in denture status during follow-up. Third, detailed information on denture characteristics—including type, material, and duration of wear—was not available. Although prior UK population surveys suggest that dentures are the most commonly removable prostheses [39], this classification imprecision may have attenuated the specificity of the exposure assessment. Fourth, we lacked data on the number of remaining natural teeth, which limited our ability to fully distinguish the effect of denture use from that of the underlying tooth loss. Fifth, denture users and non-users may have differed systematically in health behaviors, dietary patterns, healthcare utilization, and access to dental care, all of which could introduce residual confounding. In addition, specific oral hygiene behaviors and antibiotic use data were unavailable and therefore could not be adjusted for. The E-value for the main association (point estimate: 1.90; lower CI: 1.79) indicates that substantial unmeasured confounding would be required to fully explain away the observed association. Sixth, cholelithiasis was ascertained through hospital records, which may preferentially capture symptomatic cases. Because denture users in this cohort tended to be older and had a greater burden of comorbidities, they may have undergone more frequent healthcare contacts and abdominal imaging, increasing the likelihood of incidental gallstone detection and potentially inflating the observed hazard ratios. Seventh, the UK Biobank is subject to healthy volunteer bias, as participants are generally healthier than the general population, which may limit the generalizability of our findings [40]. Eighth, the PRS was derived from European ancestry populations, restricting the applicability of the gene–environment interaction findings to other ethnic groups, and its genomic coverage remains limited compared with genome-wide approaches. Ninth, the dietary variable was based on a brief food frequency questionnaire dichotomized into healthy and unhealthy categories, which may not adequately capture the specific dietary components relevant to cholesterol gallstone formation. Finally, the proposed oral-gut axis mechanism remains speculative in the absence of direct microbiome or inflammatory biomarker data. Future studies incorporating microbiota sequencing, repeated dietary assessments, and detailed dental examinations are needed to clarify the biological pathways linking denture use to cholelithiasis.

Conclusions

This large prospective cohort study found that denture use was independently associated with a higher risk of cholelithiasis. The association persisted across multiple sensitivity analyses and was independent of demographic, lifestyle, medical history, and medication use. Joint analysis with oral symptoms demonstrated that denture use, rather than the underlying oral diseases, was the dominant risk contributor. The risk was further elevated among individuals with high genetic susceptibility. Mendelian randomization analysis provided supportive evidence for a potential causal relationship. These findings identify denture use as a novel factor that may contribute to cholelithiasis risk, and suggest that oral health surveillance may have a role in gallstone prevention strategies. Further research is needed to validate these findings in diverse populations and to elucidate the underlying biological mechanisms.
