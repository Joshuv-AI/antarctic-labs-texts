Letter to the Editor: Evaluating the Clinical Readiness of General-Purpose Large Language Models in Evidence Synthesis
Authors: Muhammad Kamran Khan
Published: 2026

We read with considerable interest the recent validation study by Sakamoto et al.1), which evaluates the performance of general-purpose large language models (LLMs) in medical reasoning and orthopedic literature screening. The authors indicate impressive advances, with top-tier models achieving >94% accuracy on MedQA-USMLE and near-expert agreement (κ≈0.88) in meta-analysis screening tasks. Although these findings suggest meaningful progress in LLM capabilities, several methodologic considerations temper their clinical applicability. First, the reliance on a single meta-analysis dataset (n=384, inclusion rate ~6–7%) limits generalizability, particularly given the well-documented sensitivity of screening performance to dataset imbalance and task formulation. High kappa values in such contexts may partially reflect dominance of the majority class rather than true discriminative capability. Moreover, the absence of prompt optimization and the use of default model parameters, although methodologically consistent, may underestimate or inconsistently represent real-world implementation scenarios, in which tailored prompting significantly influences performance. The potential for benchmark contamination in MedQA-based evaluation further complicates interpretation of reasoning versus memorization, a concern previously highlighted in LLM assessments2,3).

From a clinical and methodologic perspective, the study raises important implications for integrating LLMs into evidence synthesis workflows, particularly given the well-documented sensitivity of screening performance to dataset imbalance and task formulation4,5). The finding that model performance varies by task despite high knowledge-based accuracy underscores that clinical reasoning extends beyond factual recall to nuanced interpretation of study design and eligibility criteria, as reflected in the observed error patterns (e.g., cohort misclassification and study design misidentification). Notably, the use of structured output with confidence scoring is a valuable contribution, suggesting a pathway toward hybrid screening models; however, the observed variability in calibration across model families highlights a critical barrier to standardization. Agreement with human evaluators, although encouraging, should not be conflated with correctness, particularly when inter-expert agreement itself is imperfect. Therefore, although LLMs show promise in reducing reviewer burden, their role should remain assistive rather than substitutive, pending validation across diverse clinical domains, multilingual datasets, and full systematic review pipelines. Future research should prioritize prospective validation, standardized benchmarking frameworks, and rigorous evaluation of safety, reproducibility, and bias before widespread clinical adoption in evidence-based practice.
