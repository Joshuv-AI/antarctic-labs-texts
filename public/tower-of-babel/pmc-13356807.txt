Oral cancer large observational screening study in Hungary
Authors: Edit Marosi, István Kenessey, Éva Székelyhidi, Katalin Nagy, Ferenc Oberna, Zoltán Takácsi-Nagy, András Wéber, Csilla Egri Árváné, Renáta Tamás Bertókné, Orsolya Surján, Péter Nagy, Cecília Müller
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIncidence and mortality of oral cancer slightly decreased in recent years in Hungary; however, age-standardized estimated incidence and mortality for both sexes are still among the highest in Europe. The objective of the large observational study was to conduct oral cancer screening in Hungary using mobile screening units to examine 5000 individuals living in converging municipalities with low population density and under challenging socio-economic conditions. The study also aimed to develop related patient pathways, and to educate the population on the prevention of oral malignancies. The population screened for oral cancers using Visual Oral Examination (VOE) between 21 June 2021 and 31 March 2023 included individuals of 18 years of age or older, living in converging municipalities and in other geographical locations across Hungary. In 2024, a retrospective cross-sectional study was carried out to analyze the results. During the studied time period, a total of 8266 people attended the oral cancer screening study from converging municipalities (n = 4275; 51.7%), and from other geographical locations (n = 3991; 48.3%). Oral mucosal lesions were detected in 1725 individuals, of whom only 66 people (3.8%)—13 men and 53 women—visited a specialist, and 6 were diagnosed with malignant neoplasms. Mobile screening units can be an effective tool of secondary prevention for screening high-risk groups in rural, socio-economically challenging settings. However, low compliance of screen positive individuals to attend follow-up visits poses a threat to the effectiveness of the screening programs and it needs to be addressed by optimizing patient pathways.

Introduction

The incidence and mortality of lip and oral cavity cancers have been on the rise worldwide. According to the estimates of GLOBOCAN, between 2002 and 2022, the number of new cases increased from approximately 274 000 to 389 800, while the number of deaths emerged from 127 000 to 188 000 worldwide [1]. Global patterns show that the burden of lip and oral cavity cancers is greatest in Southeast-Central Asia and Oceania.

Within the European region, the age-standardized incidence and mortality rates are the highest in Central and Eastern Europe [2]. Over 90% of oral cancers are squamous cell carcinoma [3]. The 2022 data of GLOBOCAN indicate that Hungary ranks 8th and 10th in the world, and 2nd and 3rd in Europe for age-standardized estimated incidence and mortality of oral cancer for both sexes, respectively [1]. According to the Hungarian National Cancer Registry, cancers of the lip, oral cavity, and pharynx are the eighth most frequent type of cancers in both males and females, with men experiencing 2.5-fold higher incidence than women. In recent years, a slight decline has been observed in both incidence (from 3378 new cases registered in 2008 to 2958 new cases in 2019) and mortality (from 1651 deaths in 2008 to 1311 deaths in 2019) of oral and pharyngeal cancers in men and women combined. Between 2008 and 2019, the number of newly registered cases of lip and oral cancer decreased from 1441 to 1251, while mortality declined from 706 to 531. In contrast to the slightly decreasing incidence and mortality of oral cancers over the last decade, Hungary still ranks among the highest in Europe [4].

In addition to the leading risk factors for oral cancer—tobacco and alcohol consumption—socio-economic parameters, poor oral hygiene, human papillomavirus (HPV) infection and ultraviolet radiation (UVR) exposure also play significant roles [5–11].

While organizations such as the World Health Organization-International Agency for Research on Cancer, the US Preventive Services Task Force, and the UK National Screening Committee do not recommend population-based screening programs for oral cancer, they do advocate for targeted screening strategies focused specifically on high-risk groups [12–14].

As the severity of this disease in Hungary requires urgent intervention, an observational study was initiated with the goal to screen 5000 people in low-density, socio-economically challenged rural areas using mobile units. The study aimed to develop patient pathways and educate underserved communities on preventing oral malignancies. The screening in the so-called converging municipalities was part of the governmental initiative “Hungarian Mobile Health Screening Program” under the health policy program related to the National Public Health Strategy [15]. The initiative was aligned with the EFOP-1.8.1-VEKOP-15-2016-00001 “Complex public health screening” project. The aim of the “Hungarian Mobile Health Screening Program” initiative was to provide quick and easy access to various screenings for residents living in rural, socio-economically challenged communities in converging municipalities, including small villages to promote prevention and health awareness, while ensuring equitable access to screening. Alongside cervical and colorectal cancer screenings, organized public health screenings included general health check-ups, cardiovascular risk assessment and specialist and pulmonology examinations, respiratory function test, bone density measurement, carotid ultrasound, and stroke prevention.

From 2021—after consultations between the National Centre for Public Health and Pharmacy, the National Institute of Oncology, and the University of Szeged—oral cancer screening was offered on the mobile screening units [16].

Methods

Study sample

The population screened in Hungary for oral cancers using Visual Oral Examination (VOE) in mobile screening units between 21 June 2021 and 31 March 2023 were 18 years of age or older. The population resided both in municipalities designated by legislation as so-called converging municipalities and in other municipalities without the aforementioned classification [17, 18]. Supplementary Fig. S1 depicts the location of converging municipalities as designated by legislation. In both study groups, dental hygiene students or graduates from the University of Szeged were selected and trained to conduct these examinations for cost-effectiveness [19, 20]. Methods followed national legislation and dental screening guidelines [21].

Each mobile screening unit was equipped with a fully functional oral screening station, including adjustable dermatological chairs, external lighting, and Wi-Fi connection (Supplementary Figs S2 and S3) to register patient information and variables into the National eHealth Infrastructure system (EESZT) [16]. During the oral cancer screening, besides VOE used as a screening method, a survey was created for dental hygienists performing the examinations (Ethical Permit from the Medical Research Council’s Research Ethic Committee, ETT TUKEB IV/2395-3/2022/EKU). Data collected include the results of the health findings recorded during the VOE, such as oral hygiene habits and visits to the dentist. In addition, a risk assessment questionnaire was administered covering smoking and alcohol consumption habits, screening participation, other lifestyle factors, and health literacy, among others [16]. Items of a standardized tool, the World Health Organization’s Well-Being Questionnaire (WBI-5) were also utilized [22].

Measures

Data collected had mandatory patient information, including geographical location (city, district, county) address, study group (converging municipality or other municipality), date of screening, name, date of birth, unique social insurance identification number (‘TAJ’), and sex.

Additional measures, such as level of education and items of the risk assessment survey were collected voluntarily. The mandatory information and the voluntary risk assessment survey were recorded by two administrators. The latter survey was only introduced into the EESZT system on 1 June 2022, resulting in data loss for information captured beforehand.

Given that the main purpose of the study was to screen the population and often more than 100 people signed up for the screening at a given location, due to time constraints and lack of resources, the voluntary questions were not always administered, therefore data were missing about education, lifestyle, and risks.

Socio-demographics

Socio-demographic predictors examined included age in years, sex, education, and marital status. In this study, sex refers to biological attributes such as chromosomes, hormone levels, and reproductive/sexual anatomy, typically categorized as male, or female. Level of education was assessed based on the individual’s highest level of formal education attained. There were originally four categories for education reflecting the national education system: (i) 8th grade (elementary school) or less, (ii) secondary education without a secondary school diploma (vocational training) (iii) secondary education with a secondary school diploma (vocational secondary school, secondary school), and (iv) higher education (university, college). During the analysis, answers 2, 3 were combined into one category, secondary education. Missing data were categorized as not available (N/A). Education was used in the analysis as a socio-demographic predictor. Marital status was collected as a self-reported categorical variable and included the following categories: single, married/cohabitation, divorced, and widowed. Missing data were categorized as not available (N/A).

Risk-assessment metrics—smoking, alcohol consumption, other behavioral and lifestyle predictors

The risk assessment questionnaire had 43 items, including some demographical questions that are detailed under the socio-demographics sub-section. Other predictors, included smoking habits, alcohol consumption, eating habits (fruits and vegetables, snacks, sweets), consumption of energy drinks and other sugary soft drinks among others. All variables were self-reported. Current smoking habits (daily, occasionally, never, quit less than a year ago, quit over a year ago) were categorized into three variables (i) currently/previously smoked, (ii) non-smoker, (iii) not available (N/A) for missing data. Additional variables related to tobacco use included the average number of cigarettes smoked per day. This was measured using a 5‑category response scale: (0) 0 cigarettes; (1) 1–10 cigarettes; (2) 11–20 cigarettes; and subsequent categories increasing in increments of 10 cigarettes per day. Alcohol consumption was assessed through several items capturing the frequency of beer, wine, wine coolers, and distilled spirits consumed during the past 30 days. Responses were recorded using a 7-category scale: (0) 0 times; (1) 1–2 times; (2) 3–5 times; (3) 6–9 times; (4) 10–19 times; (5) 20–39 times; and (6) more than 40 times.

Vegetable and fruit consumption were categorized as daily versus not daily, with missing responses coded as N/A. Variables on the consumption of sugary soft drinks, energy drinks, sweets and snack were measured with the following scale: (1) 4 times or more in a week; (2) maximum 3 times in a week; (3) occasionally; (4) never; and (5) not available (N/A) for missing data.

Oral cancer screening examination metrics

The methods entailed VOE and the face, lymph nodes, lips, oral cavity, dentition, tongue, gums, tonsils, floor of the mouth and oropharyngeal region. Among others, face, lips, lymph nodes, tongue, gums, tonsils, floor of the mouth, fillings, cavities, missing dentition, tooth mobility, were examined. Oral hygiene habits were recorded with a (i) yes and (ii) no answers regarding the use of manual toothbrush, mechanical toothbrush, interdental cleaner, dental floss, mouthwash, toothpaste tablet. Regarding the use of toothpaste, the variables recorded were (i) yes; (ii) no; (iii) not available (N/A).

Primary outcomes

Once potential malignancies were discovered, individuals were referred to the specialist care of a dentist. Using each person’s “TAJ” number, the results of the examinations were entered into the National eHealth Infrastructure (EESZT) system. Diagnosed suspicious malignancies were validated using the database of the National Health Insurance Fund of Hungary (NEAK). These records were also crosschecked with the Hungarian National Cancer Registry, which collects all new cancer cases in Hungary [23]. This ensured that only newly identified, histologically confirmed malignant cases were reported. Variables recorded included positive signs during screening (referred individuals) and whether the individuals attended specialist care with the answer (i) yes; and (ii) no for measures.

The outcomes measured include the number of newly diagnosed oral cancer cases (C00-14 codes according to the 10th Revision of the International Classification of Disease and Related Health Problems—ICD-10), as well as the number of oral potentially malignant disorders (OPMDs), and non-neoplastic oral mucosal lesions and diseases of the soft tissue.

Statistical analysis

A retrospective cross-sectional observational study was carried out. Categorical data were analyzed using the chi-square tests, and for numerical values with a non-normal distribution, the Mann–Whitney test was performed. The data analyses were carried out in 2024. The relationship between the occurrence of oral lesions and major factors was assessed by univariate and multivariate logistic regression models. Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS 26.0 (Chicago, IL, USA), MS Excel (Microsoft, Redmond, WA, USA), Statistica 14.0.1.25 (TIBCO Software, Palo Alto, CA, USA), and PAST 1.86. [24]. P values lower than .05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

Socio-demographics

During the study period, 8266 people participated in the oral cancer screening study. Participants were divided into two groups: those from socio-economically challenged regions in converging municipalities (n = 4275; 51.7%) and those attending individually organized screening events across Hungary in other municipalities (n = 3991; 48.3%).

Demographic data indicate that women (5791) participated at a rate 2.3× higher than men (2473) across both groups. In the converging municipalities, women were overrepresented with 75.5% female and 24.5% male participants, while in other municipalities, 64.3% were women and 35.7% men (Table 1). The median age of the adult participants (over the age of 18) was 58 in converging municipalities and 53 in other municipalities.

The questions on education were answered by a total of 3168 (37.8%) people in both groups. Of these, 1419 (33.27%) were from the converging municipalities, and 1749 (43.8%) were from the other group. Lesions were significantly more common among those with lower education compared to those with secondary or higher education (36.4% vs. 25.0% and 16.2%; P < .001) [16].

Risk assessment and oral cancer screening examination metrics

Other surveyed factors included smoking and drinking habits, healthy eating habits, and oral hygiene routines, as detailed in Table 1. A review of dental visit patterns revealed a significant difference in the prevalence of oral mucosal lesions: 19.9% in individuals who visited the dentist regularly, 23.1% in those who visited the dentist when a complaint occurred, and 41.0% in those who said they had never visited the dentist (P < .001). Dental hygiene habits were also analyzed, showing that individuals who brushed their teeth more than once a day were less likely to have oral mucosal lesions than those who did not brush or only brushed occasionally (P < .001). Out of 8266 examinations, oral mucosal lesions in the soft tissues were detected in 1725 individuals (20.9%), with similar rates in men (21.1%) and women (20.8%).

Smoking, alcohol consumption, and age were also compared between those who screened negative or positive for oral mucosal lesions. Results show that 33.6% of screen-positive individuals currently smoke or formerly smoked, compared to 27% among those who screened negative (P < .001). Individuals with a smoking history were more likely to positively screen for oral mucosal lesions. When examining the alcohol consumption habits (regular and frequent consumption) between the screen-positive and screen-negative individuals in the study, data imply that there was not a significant difference in the oral mucosa detection between the two groups.

The median age of screen-negative individuals was 54, compared to 61 for those who screened positive, indicating that age plays a significant role in screening outcomes (P < .00001) in this study. Male and female proportions were similar across groups that screened negatively or positively, suggesting that sex did not significantly affect results (Table 2).

Combinations of the risk factors, such as smoking and alcohol use, were also analyzed. Data show that individuals in the group who did not smoke, but consumed alcohol, have a higher likelihood of developing oral mucosal lesions compared to the group featuring individuals did not drink and did not smoke (P < .00001). A statistically significant difference was also found between those who drank but did not smoke and those who both smoked and consumed alcohol (P = .0001). The results suggest that individuals who both smoke and drink have a greater risk of developing lesions than those who do neither. Smoking appears to have a significant role in the development of oral mucosal lesions, especially if combined with alcohol use, while alcohol alone does not seem to have a significant effect on the screening outcome in this study (data not shown).

The univariate logistic regression model identified age, smoking, and participation from a converging municipality as key risk factors for developing oral mucosal lesions, while gender and alcohol consumption showed no effect (Table 3). Establishing multivariate model, age, smoking, and participation from a converging municipality maintained their effects on the risk for oral lesions. The observed inefficacy of alcohol consumption on the risk should be interpreted with caution, due to self-reported data. Such a method is considered to provide fairly accurate estimates of actual drinking levels for light to moderate drinkers, however, the underreporting of alcohol consumption tends to rise with heavier drinking [25]. Note that the rate of alcohol consumption did not differ between individuals for whom oral lesions were detected vs. not detected (26.6% vs. 25.8%; P = .68). Rates were similar between those with and without lesions.

The National Centre for Public Health and Pharmacy tracked the screen-positive individuals who visited a specialist for diagnosis using the ICD codes in the National Health Insurance Fund Manager’s database. Of the 1725 individuals with oral mucosal lesions, only 66 people (3.8%)—13 men and 53 women—saw a specialist. The results were cross-checked with the data of the National Cancer Registry using the patient’s social security number. Among the 66 individuals who visited a dentist or care provider, 57.6% were over the age of 60 in converging municipalities and 54.5% in other municipalities, showing a similar age distribution.

Discussion

The WHO considers oral health of the population as integral to general health and to the overall quality of life [26]. Since Hungary has one of the highest oral cancer burdens, current prevention policies must be strengthened. As part of the “Hungarian Mobile Health Screening Program” program, the second largest number of oral screenings in recent decades was conducted in Hungary on mobile screening units. Preliminary results were published in Hungarian [16, 27]. This current study offers a more comprehensive analysis [16, 27]. Across municipalities, there was a strong female predominance (70%) among participants. Literature suggests that sex may be a positive or a negative predictor of participation in oral cancer screenings. The findings remain ambiguous [28].

Compared to previously published international studies, our observational did not focus exclusively on cancer, but any oral mucosal alterations. Since our study design extended the definition of screen positivity, the percentage of screen-positive individuals was significantly higher. The goal of this current study was not only to discover cancerous lesions, or precancerous lesions but also to detect any abnormalities in the soft tissue of the oral mucosa, and refer all mucosal and soft tissue changes, including benign and neoplastic-unrelated (such as inflammatory) lesions. Furthermore, the examination was carried out by dental hygienists and students, who did not have the mandate to set up a clinical or preliminary diagnosis of OPMDs and any abnormalities discovered were referred to a specialist. Out of the 8266 individuals screened, 1725 (20.9%) showed suspicious lesions or abnormalities and were referred for specialist evaluation. In contrast, the Trivandrum Oral Cancer Screening RCT study in Kerala, India reported 6.28% screen-positive rate, however the goal of the screening was to identify precancerous lesions such homogeneous leukoplakia, non-homogeneous leukoplakia, erythroplakia, oral submucous fibrosis, or oral cancer. Screen positivity was defined as the presence of referable lesions. The screening was performed similarly to the current study by healthcare workers [29].

A study of Bangladeshi individuals living in London using mobile units found a 5.7% referral rate [30]. In Taiwan, a national screening program for high-risk individuals screened 2 334 299 people between 2004 and 2009, reporting a 0.8% screen-positive rate (18 116 individuals) [31]. These studies focused only on identifying oral potentially malignant disorders (OPMD) and the examinations were carried out by dentists. Unfortunately, in the current study, the usage of the ICD10 coding system did not help provide further conclusions, since by definition only 6 patients belonged to the OPMD group, while 28 cases were defined as “Other and unspecified lesions of oral mucosa,” which shared relatively limited information. Combined, these two categories accounted for 0.41% of the 8266 individuals screened.

In this study, compliance with follow-up dental visits among screen-positive individuals was significantly lower than in other international screenings. Of the 1725 individuals referred, only 66 people (3.8%) attended confirmatory tests (13 men and 53 women) by 31 October 2023. In comparison, the Kerala RCT study reported a 59% follow-up rate (3298 referred of 5586) [32]. The London study involving the Bangladeshi community had a 75% compliance (55 of 75 referred) and the process was aided by follow-up from the reference center and as well as local advocates. The reason for non-attendance was also investigated by contacting patients by phone [30]. In the current study, a follow-up mechanism was not implemented to increase the participation of individuals who screened positive. A recent oral cancer screenings study in Portugal shows that follow-up was carried out among 168 individuals, where 161 were identified with premalignant lesions and 7 participants with suspicious neoplasia. The telephone follow-up reached 165 individuals, out of whom 48 stated that they had undergone biopsy [33].

Closer monitoring and follow-up are essential to help screen-positive individuals navigate the care pathway for future programs in Hungary. This support may be provided by local General Practitioner (GP) or designated staff through written or verbal communication. Given the reluctance of high-risk groups to visit dentists, the referral pathway may need future revision. Oral surgery departments of the local hospitals may be added as referral points alongside dental practices. The particularly low rate of compliance of screen-positives in Hungary also highlights the need for further research into reasons for non-attendance. Oral hygiene habits in Hungary are generally poor—only about half the population visits the dentist regularly, and many seek care only when in pain. Notably, 75% of patients with leucoplakia visit the dentist only in case of experiencing pain [34]. In this study, oral lesions were more common among people with poor oral hygiene, those who smoked, consumed alcohol, the elderly, and those with lower levels of education. The aforementioned parameters are well-known risk factors which corroborates to our results [35]. The only inconsistent finding concerns alcohol consumption; however, the large amount of missing data may have distorted our results. On the other hand, based on the current study design, it was shown that oral mucosa lesions were more common in people living in converging municipalities, which was a unique approach. This aligns with previous research showing higher oral cancer incidence and mortality in Hungary’s disadvantaged north-eastern regions [36, 37].

Data also indicated that respondents from the converging municipalities were more likely to visit a dentist only when experiencing complaints. They use toothbrushes and toothpaste less frequently than those in other areas—about 25% reported not using them at all, compared to just 5% in the other group. Improving oral hygiene through education and strengthened primary prevention will be a key strategy for the future.

The cost-effectiveness of the current screening study was not assessed. However, risk prediction models recommended for screening of oral cancer are considered cost-effective [38, 39]. Models for head and neck and oral cancer—such as those analyzed by Vokó et al. [40]—suggest that opportunistic screening of high-risk individuals would be cost-effective in Hungary. These risk prediction models may also be utilized for future screenings of high-risk groups in the future [39]. However, the current study highlighted that closer patient follow-up would be critical in effective prevention.

Although incidence and mortality of oral cancer have slightly decreased in recent years, Hungary’s age-standardized estimated incidence and mortality for both sexes remains among the highest in Europe. This highlights a need for substantial improvement in the oral health of the population, particularly in the prevention of dental caries and periodontal disease. Mobile screening units can be an effective tool in secondary prevention, especially in rural, socioeconomically challenging settings to screen high-risk groups. However, low compliance of screen-positive individuals to attend follow-up visits poses a threat to the effectiveness of screening programs, and must be addressed.

The preventive strategy may be enhanced by the design of a national oral health improvement plan that integrates oral health into other national health policies and promotes intersectoral collaborations with a strong focus on prevention among youth in primary and secondary school settings.

Limitations

In this study, compared to other oral cancer screening studies, any abnormalities in the oral mucosal soft tissues were recorded as screen-positive cases, and were referred to a specialist for diagnosis. A limitation of the study is the low compliance for further investigation, with only 3.8% of screen-positive individuals visiting a specialist to confirm the diagnosis. Additional limitations included lack of data on certain variables, such as the level of education and risk assessment of alcohol and tobacco consumption habits, as their collection was voluntary and time-consuming, and due to a lack of resources, the screening itself was prioritized over data collection on risk factors.
