Development and application of formation damage mitigation system for heavy oil reservoir
Authors: Xi Lu, Lin Meng, Haibo Wang, Changbo Zhai, Tong Zhou, Wenfang Song, Jianfang Sun
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractA compound chemical system was developed to address the formation damage in heavy oil reservoirs caused by asphaltene deposition and clay swelling. The dynamic process of asphaltene deposition was simulated through laboratory experiments and the effect of clay types and their mass fractions were investigated. The extent of formation damage from these mechanisms was quantified by measuring the reduction in permeability. Sodium dodecyl benzenesulfonate (SDBS) and n-pentanol were selected as the viscosity reducer and the additive, respectively, as their combination achieved the highest viscosity reduction rate and anti-coalescence rate. Different acids were tested based on the dissolution rates of three types of clay. The mixture of 8% HCl and 12% HBF4 was found to be the most effective. The formation damage mitigation system was formulated using SDBS, n-pentanol, HCl, and HBF4 at a mass ratio of 5:2:8:12. The sandpack experiments demonstrated a significant reduction in permeability due to the combined formation damage, followed by an effective restoration of permeability after the mitigation treatment. This compound system was successfully applied to 56 wells of the Shengli Oilfield, with over 90% of the wells achieving a significant increase in oil production rate by 35% and a notable reduction in injection pressure by 42%.Keywords: Heavy oil, Asphaltene deposition, Clay swelling, Formation damage, Mitigation system, Performance evaluation, Field testSubject terms: Energy, Chemical engineering

Introduction

Heavy oil reserves represent a significant portion of the world’s petroleum resources, accounting for approximately 70% of global crude oil reserves1. As global energy demand continues to rise, the production of heavy oil has become increasingly essential to meet these energy needs. With substantial reserves located in regions such as Canada, Venezuela, and China, heavy oil production plays a pivotal role in the broader dynamics of the petroleum industry1–3. However, the recovery of heavy oil presents unique challenges, one of the most prominent being the formation damage, which often occurs during drilling and recovery process and progressively reduces oil mobility and well performance over time. The formation damage in heavy oil reservoirs is more complex than that observed in conventional oil reservoirs, due to the combined effects of reservoir characteristics and the intricate composition of heavy oil3–5.

Heavy oil reservoirs are vulnerable to a wide range of formation damage mechanisms. Among these, the asphaltene deposition, clay swelling, and thermal recovery-related damage are the most critical factors contributing to formation damage in heavy oil reservoirs6–17.

Heavy oil is characterized by a significant proportion of high-molecular-weight hydrocarbons, such as asphaltenes and resins, which contribute to its elevated viscosity and propensity to form solid deposits. These solid particulates obstruct the pore spaces and flow pathways within the reservoir, leading to a reduction in both porosity and permeability. Consequently, flow resistance is increased, thereby diminishing the reservoir’s production capacity. Additionally, the heavy components in the oil exhibit oil-wetting properties, enabling them to adsorb onto the surface of the porous media. This adsorption alters the wettability of the reservoir rocks, inducing a shift from hydrophilic to oleophilic behavior, which further decreases the effective permeability to the oil phase, impeding flow ability and ultimately lowering oil recovery rates6–9.

Nearly all heavy oil formations contain varying amounts of clays, including common types such as montmorillonite, illite, and kaolinite. Formation damage due to clay swelling typically occurs when water is introduced into the formation during drilling or production, leading to a decrease in formation permeability. This damage is often challenging to detect and can significantly impair reservoir performance. Mitigation efforts are costly, particularly given the marginal economics of many heavy oil fields, making it crucial to prevent such damage whenever possible10–13.

During steam injection in heavy oil reservoirs, the application of high-temperature and high-pressure thermal fluids can induce intense water-rock interactions, leading to the loosening of rock cementation, detachment of particles, and subsequent pore throat blockage. These interactions not only alter the physical properties of the reservoir rocks but also disrupt flow dynamics, exacerbating the challenges associated with oil recovery. The detachment and migration of rock particles further intensify the blockage, resulting in reduced steam injection efficiency, lower production rates, and shortened production cycles14–17.

To mitigate formation damage in heavy oil reservoirs, various methods have been proposed to address blockages, with both physical and chemical approaches being the most widely investigated13,16,18–35. Physical methods employ mechanical techniques, such as hydraulic fracturing and electromagnetic waves, to eliminate blockages16,18–25. Hydraulic fracturing involves injecting high-pressure water into the formation, resulting in the creation of fractures that significantly increase permeability in the wellbore region. This process shifts the flow regime from radial to linear around the wellbore, enhancing the productivity index of reservoirs. However, hydraulic fracturing can potentially induce new skin damage in the gas condensate reservoir, as liquid droplets may accumulate on the fracture wings18–20. Ultrasonic (US) and microwave (MW) treatments have shown some effectiveness in laboratory experiments for mitigating skin damage, improving rheological properties, preventing asphaltene and paraffin precipitation, and aiding in demulsification. These treatments are typically performed by placing a resonator or antenna near the pay zone. However, due to the limited action area of these techniques, few studies have reported their successful field application for mitigating damage in condensate wells21–25.

Chemical methods involve the injection of specialized chemicals to dissolve or disperse blockages, thereby restoring the reservoir’s permeability. Common chemical agents include acids, alkalis, surfactants, and compound systems. These methods are often effective and fast-acting, enabling targeted blockage removal through precise control over the type and quantity of chemicals injected. While chemical methods are widely used and often effective, their success hinges on precise design to mitigate potential risks such as incompatibility with formation minerals or fluids, which could otherwise lead to secondary precipitation and shortened treatment longevity26–28.

Currently, most chemical methods focus on the drilling and fracturing processes, where drilling fluids and fracturing fluids are developed to remove filter cakes and enhance the fracturing effect. Bageri et al.29 used a 20% ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) solution to remove barite filter cake, with the effectiveness varying according to rock type and the presence of specific ions. Jaberi et al.30 combined diethylenetriamine pentaacetic acid (DTPA) and EDTA to improve performance. The carbonate core flooding tests demonstrated that this solution effectively inhibited barite and increased permeability in macropores. Mahmoud et al.31 tested four types of nanoparticles and found that Fe₂O₃ and Fe₃O₄ exhibited the greatest performance in minimizing formation damage, suggesting their potential as alternatives to conventional sodium-based bentonite fluids for reducing formation damage during drilling operations. However, for heavy oil reservoirs, formation damage occurs not only during the drilling and fracturing but throughout the entire development process. Few studies have focused on the targeted chemical systems to address this issue, which was primarily influenced by by reservoir characteristics and asphaltene deposition in heavy oil26–35.

Due to the complex mechanisms and long-lasting nature of formation damage in heavy oil reservoirs, conventional formation damage mitigation methods are often ineffective in addressing challenges related to reduced oil mobility and blockages in the near-wellbore region13,16,29,33–38. Therefore, this study aims to address the primary research questions: (1) how can formation damage be comprehensively evaluated under heavy oil reservoir conditions; (2) how can an effective system for mitigating this formation damage be constructed and optimized. To overcome these challenges, this study developed a compound chemical formation damage mitigation system, comprising a heavy oil viscosity reduction system and an acidizing clay dissolution system, which work synergistically to ensure efficient formation damage mitigation in the near-wellbore area and significant enhancement of heavy oil mobility. Furthermore, this cold chemical production method avoids formation damage typically associated with thermal recovery processes. The compound chemical system was successfully applied to 56 wells in the Shengli Oilfield, China, achieving a blockage removal success rate of over 90%, thereby effectively supporting field production.

Viscosity reduction rate and anti-coalescence rate measurement

The viscosity of heavy oil was obtained from the Shengli Oilfield, and the viscosity reduction rate was measured at 60 °C in accordance with the China Petroleum Industry standards SY/T 5767 − 2005, “Technical Specification for the Practice of Transporting Crude Oil Treated with Pour Point Depressants through Pipelines,” and SY/T 0520–2008, “Viscosity Determination of Crude Petroleum—Equilibrium Method by Rotational Viscometer.” To evaluate the anti-coalescence performance, the emulsified oil sample was allowed to rest for 1 h. The anti-coalescence rate was calculated as the ratio of the viscosity reduction rate after resting to the viscosity reduction rate before resting.

Clay dissolution rate measurement

The dissolution effects of clays treated with compound acid systems, which are introduced in Sect. Selection of clay dissolution system, at normal pressure and 60 °C were measured as follows: First, 5.0 g of the clay sample was weighed and placed into a glass beaker. Then, 20 mL of the compound acid solution was added, and the mixture was thoroughly stirred. The beaker was sealed and incubated in a water bath at 60 °C for 4 h. After dissolution, the mixture was filtered, the residue was dried, and the resulting material was weighed. The mass loss rate of the clay minerals was then calculated to assess the dissolution effects of acid solutions on the clay.

Formation damage by asphaltenes deposition measurement

The sandpack filled with quartz sand was first vacuumed, then saturated with stratum water, and subsequently saturated with a heavy oil sample from the Shengli Oilfield at reservoir temperature (60 °C). Permeability was measured after the sandpack had been subjected to 24 h of conditioning. At different temperatures of 60 °C and 90 °C, heavy oil was injected at a constant rate of 0.2 mL/min, and the injection volume along with the pressure differential was recorded. Permeability was measured once the pressure difference stabilized, and changes in permeability relative to the injection volume were also documented.

Formation damage by clay swelling measurement

Prior to the experiment, the stratum water was filtered through the vacuum filtration pump to remove impurities. The experimental procedure commenced with the mixing of quartz sand with kaolinite, montmorillonite, and illite, respectively at the following weight ratios: 0%, 1.5%, 2%, 3%, 4%, 5%, 7.5%, and 10%. Stratum water was injected at 0.2 mL/min simultaneously, ensuring consistent flow across all setups. Once the effluent flow rate stabilized, the displacement experiment was initiated. Throughout the process, flow rate and pressure changes were monitored, and permeability was calculated.

Formation damage mitigation measurement

Quartz sand was mixed with kaolinite, montmorillonite, and illite at a mass fraction of 7.5% for each clay type. The initial permeability was measured by injecting stratum water at a rate of 2 mL/min to minimize clay swelling. The sandpack was then saturated with heavy oil and conditioned for 24 h to allow full clay swelling and asphaltene deposition. Permeability was subsequently measured to determine the damaged permeability. Following this, 0.5 pore volumes (PV) of the formation damage mitigation system, with a mass fraction of 3%, were injected, and permeability was measured again to assess the mitigation permeability.

Results and discussion

Evaluation of formation damage

Asphaltenes deposition

As shown in Fig. 2, the process of asphaltene deposition from heavy oil exhibited distinct temperature-dependent behavior. At 60 °C, asphaltene deposition began after the injection of 4.60 PV of heavy oil and stabilized at 6.77 PV, resulting in a 38% reduction in permeability. At 90 °C, deposition initiated after 17.78 PV of heavy oil was injected and stabilized at 27.23 PV, with a 31% reduction in permeability. These results indicated that asphaltene deposition occurred earlier and led to a more significant permeability reduction at lower temperatures. In contrast, at higher temperatures, the deposition of heavy components occurred later, leading to a smaller decrease in permeability.

Effect of injected heavy oil volumes on the permeability at different temperatures.

During the initial stages of heavy oil injection, the deposition and aggregation of asphaltenes and other organic heavy components led to a significant reduction in the permeability of the sandpack. As the injection volume increased, some of the previously deposited blockages were displaced by the shear forces exerted by the injected fluid. Eventually, a dynamic equilibrium was reached, where the permeability stabilized as a balance was established between the deposition and displacement of the blockages. Additionally, temperature had a complex effect on asphaltene deposition. On one hand, the solubility of asphaltenes increased with rising temperature, resulting in a lower mass of asphaltene deposition. On the other hand, at elevated temperatures, the expansion of lighter components reduced the solubility of asphaltenes, thereby exacerbating their aggregation. Afshari et al.32 conducted filtration experiments using an Iranian heavy oil sample to quantify asphaltene deposition at different temperatures. Their results indicated a decrease in asphaltene deposition with increasing temperature, which was consistent with the findings of this study.

Clay swelling

The sensitivity of various clay minerals in water-bearing sandpack at different mass fractions was investigated. As shown in Fig. 3, the sensitivity of the clay minerals varied, with montmorillonite exhibiting the strongest sensitivity, followed by kaolinite and illite. The extent of permeability reduction increased with the rising mass fraction, although permeability remained relatively stable when the mass fraction exceeded 7.5%. The presence of montmorillonite caused a 70.7% decrease in permeability, reducing it from 1121.8 mD to 328.3 mD. In comparison, kaolinite and illite reduced permeability by 54.5% and 50.7%, respectively.

Effect of mass fraction of three types of clay on the permeability.

The expansion of clay minerals reduced the pore volume of the sand layer, leading to a decrease in permeability. As the clay mineral content increased, the permeability of the sand decreased accordingly. The observed differences in permeability reduction could be attributed primarily to the unique crystal structures of each clay mineral. Illite and montmorillonite often formed particle aggregates that adhered tightly to the sand grains, creating a continuous, dense clay “coat”. When water infiltrated this sand-clay system, each clay particle attracted water, forming a water film around it, which further reduced permeability33,34. However, due to differences in their crystal structures, montmorillonite formed a thicker water film compared to illite, resulting in a more significant reduction in permeability. Additionally, clay particles such as montmorillonite induced crystal expansion and coalescence in the formation water, increasing the volume of clay aggregates. This process led to the formation of larger aggregates with a higher yield value, contributing to a logarithmic decrease in permeability. These findings helped explain why the montmorillonite-sand system exhibited significantly higher water sensitivity compared to the illite-sand and kaolinite-sand systems39–42.

Construction of formation damage mitigation system

Selection of viscosity reducer system

Based on previous studies, seven types of viscosity reducers were evaluated for their viscosity reduction and anti-coalescence performance, including 5% sulfamic acid (SA), 5% polyethylene glycol (PEG), 100% oleic acid (OA), 2% sodium dodecyl benzenesulfonate (SDBS), 2% sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS), 2% sodium ligninsulfonate (SL), and 2% octaphenyl polyoxyethylene (OPE) [13,23,24,29]. The crude oil samples used in this study were sourced from the Shengli Oilfield, with an initial viscosity of 234 mPa·s at the reservoir temperature of 60 °C. Viscosity reducer solutions were added to the crude oil under simulated reservoir conditions (60 °C), and viscosity changes were monitored to calculate the viscosity reduction rate. The emulsified oil samples were subsequently stored for one hour, after which the viscosity was measured again to assess the anti-coalescence ability of each viscosity reducer.

As shown in Fig. 4, the viscosity reduction effects of the various reducers exhibited significant variability, with reduction rates ranging from 47.29% to 91.81%. Among the tested viscosity reducers, SA demonstrated the least effective viscosity reduction, while SDBS proved to be the most effective, achieving a maximum viscosity reduction rate of 91.81%. After one hour of conditioning, the emulsified oil samples exhibited varying degrees of coalescence. SDBS was selected as the primary viscosity reducer based on its superior performance in initial screening tests, where it achieved the highest viscosity reduction rate among seven candidate chemicals (SA, PEG, OA, SDBS, SDS, SL, OPE). Its strong anionic sulfonate group promotes effective adsorption at the oil-water interface, leading to excellent emulsification and dispersion of heavy oil components.

Viscosity reduction rate and anti-coalescence rate of different types of viscosity reducers.

Alcohols were known to have lower surface tension and could enhance the dissolution of strongly polar substances in crude oil, making them effective additives for the emulsification of heavy oil. Additionally, certain alcohols could significantly lower the freezing point of water, thereby preventing the solution from solidifying at low temperatures, which was particularly beneficial in cold environments13. To further improve the performance of the selected viscosity reducer, SDBS, experiments were conducted using 5% mass fraction solutions of various alcohols to identify the most effective additive for enhancing the viscosity reduction effect24–26. Optimization of SDBS mass fraction

The mass fraction of the viscosity reducer was a critical factor in field applications, as it directly influenced both the viscosity reduction performance and the overall project cost. In this section, the viscosity reduction effect of SDBS on heavy oil from the Shengli Oilfield was evaluated to determine the optimal mass fraction of SDBS. With the mass fraction of n-pentanol fixed at 5%, SDBS was added at mass fractions of 1%, 2%, 3%, 4%, and 5%. The results of the viscosity reduction experiments are shown in Fig. 6.

Effect of SDBS mass fraction on the viscosity reduction rate and anti-coalescence rate.

The experimental analysis demonstrated that the increasing mass fraction of SDBS resulted in an improved viscosity reduction rate, which exceeded 90%. However, when the mass fraction was above 2%, the viscosity reduction rate showed a diminishing return. Additionally, the anti-coalescence ability improved with higher mass fractions of SDBS, surpassing 90% when the mass fraction exceeded 2%. At 60 °C, the combination of 2% SDBS and 5% n-pentanol reduced the viscosity of the heavy oil from 234 mPa·s to 12.6 mPa·s, achieving a viscosity reduction rate of 94.62%. This result effectively met the goal of reducing the viscosity of ordinary heavy oil reservoir. Therefore, the mass fraction of SDBS was determined to be 2%.

Optimization of mass fraction for viscosity reduction system

Evaluation experiments were conducted to determine the optimal mass fraction of the compound viscosity reduction system, with mass fraction of 1%, 2%, 3%, 4%, 5%, and 6%. The corresponding viscosity reduction curves are shown in Fig. 7. Analysis of the experimental results revealed that as the mass fraction increased, the viscosity of the heavy oil sample decreased significantly. As the mass fraction increased from 1% to 3%, the viscosity reduction rate and anti-coalescence rate increased from 87.53% to 84.6% to 92.98% and 89.63%, respectively. Based on these findings, the optimal mass fraction of the viscosity reduction system is determined to be 3%.

Optimization of formation damage mitigation system

The formation damage mitigation system was formulated based on the following mass ratio: n-pentanol: SDBS: HCl: HBF4 = 5:2:8:12. This section presented tests on the viscosity reduction, anti-coalenscence and formation damage mitigation performance of the system to identify the optimal mass fraction for field application.

Viscosity reduction test

The viscosity reduction performance of the composite plugging system was evaluated at mass fractions of 1%, 2%, 3%, 4%, 5%, and 6% to identify the optimal concentration for various composite systems. The viscosity reduction and anti-coalescence curve for each mass fraction were presented in Fig. 9. Analysis of the experimental results revealed that increasing the mass fraction of the formation damage mitigation system significantly reduced the viscosity of the heavy oil samples. At a mass fraction of 1%, the viscosity reduction rate and anti-coalescence rate were 87.53% and 84.6%, respectively. However, as the mass fraction increased to 3%, both the viscosity reduction and anti-coalescence rates showed only marginal improvements. Based on these findings, the optimal mass fraction of the composite plugging system was determined to be 3%.

Effect of mass fraction of formation damage mitigation system on the viscosity reduction rate and anti-coalescence rate.

Formation damage mitigation test

Figure 10 illustrated the formation damage mitigation results for four replicate cases. Due to the combined effect of clay swelling caused by stratum water injection and asphaltene deposition from heavy oil, the average permeability was severely reduced by 97.5%, from 2100.7 mD to 53.3 mD. This significant permeability decline helped explain the production challenges in the Shengli Oilfield, such as low liquid rates and high injection pressures. After injecting 0.5 PV of the formation damage mitigation system, average permeability was effectively restored to 1356.8 mD, achieving a mitigation rate of 62.1%. Figure 11 shows a photograph of the sand sample after the mitigation treatment. The dark solid deposits and other organic blockages were markedly reduced, demonstrating the significant effectiveness of the compound mitigation system.

Field test

The formation damage mitigation system was applied to a total of 56 wells in Shengli Oilfield, including 32 injection wells and 24 production wells. The results indicated that 91% of the wells were effectively unplugged, resulting in a significant increase in injection volume and a notable reduction in injection pressure by 42% for the injection wells. Moreover, the production wells exhibited a marked increase in both liquid production rate by 37% and oil production rate by 35%. To further illustrate the system’s performance, one production well and one injection well were selected as representative cases for detailed analysis.

Injection well X1

Injection well X1 utilized a general pressure drive for water injection. This well consisted of 20 layers, with a total thickness of 27.6 m, porosity ranging from 7.6% to 14.3%, and permeability varying between 0.4 and 10.6 mD. Following the establishment of the design scheme, a deep etching process was conducted, involving the injection of 1520 m³ of treatment fluid. A large flow rate of 1.3 m³/min was maintained, while the pump pressure gradually decreased by 3.4 MPa, indicating the successful removal of organic blockages and effective enhancement of injectivity.

Production well X2

Production well X2 consisted of 6 layers with a total thickness of 8.4 m, porosity ranging from 20.9% to 26.5%, and permeability varying between 3.9 and 20.8 mD. Severe calcium carbonate scaling was observed in the surface pipelines. The proposed treatment scheme involved deep etching to remove the blockage, reduce injection pressure, and increase the injection volume. The treatment included the injection of 600 m³ of the formation damage mitigation system at a 3% mass fraction, creating a treatment radius of 9.88 m. The injection rate was gradually increased from 0.8 to 1.5 m³/min, while the injection pressure steadily decreased from 35 MPa to 29 MPa. The formation damage mitigation system facilitated the rapid dissolution of the blockage and ensured continuous unblocking. Following the treatment, the corresponding injection well resumed operation, with the daily injection volume increasing from 5 m³ to 40 m³ and the injection pressure decreasing by 6.0 MPa. The results demonstrated a 100% increase in the liquid production rate, rising to 30 t/d, and a 150% increase in the oil production rate, reaching 5 t/d.

Conclusions

In this study, the formation damage mechanisms of heavy oil reservoirs were analyzed and quantified. Sandpack tests were conducted to assess the effects of asphaltene deposition and clay swelling. The high asphaltene content of the heavy oil was identified as a primary driver of deposition-related formation damage. Results showed that the asphaltene deposition caused permeability decrease by more than 30% during heavy oil flow through the porous media, with high temperatures partially mitigating this permeability reduction. The swelling of three clay types—montmorillonite, illite, and kaolinite—caused significant permeability reduction, with a decrease of more than 50% when their mass fractions exceeded 7.5%.

A compound formation damage mitigation system was developed by combining a viscosity reduction system and a clay dissolution system. The viscosity reduction system, composed of SDBS and n-pentanol at optimal mass fractions of 2% and 5%, respectively, achieved the highest viscosity reduction (94.14%) and anti-coalescence rate (91.39%). Among the nine tested acid systems, an 8% HCl and 12% HBF4 mixture was selected due to its superior dissolution rate for all three types of clays. The compound formation damage mitigation system was formulated at a mass ratio of n-pentanol: SDBS: HCl: HBF4 at 5:2:8:12, with an optimal total mass fraction of 3%.

Four replicate sandpack flooding tests demonstrated that the combined effects of asphaltene deposition and clay swelling resulted in over 97% permeability reduction. After injecting 0.5 PV of the mitigation system, permeability was significantly restored, achieving a mitigation rate of more than 62%. This compound system was successfully applied in 56 wells at the Shengli Oilfield, with over 90% of the wells experiencing successful blockage removal, leading to a substantial increase in oil production rate and a marked reduction in injection pressure.

Future recommendations

While this study developed and validated a compound chemical system for mitigating formation damage in heavy oil reservoirs, several areas warrant further investigation to enhance the understanding and application of this technology. The following recommendations are proposed to address the identified knowledge gaps and to guide future research:

1) Long-term stability and geochemical impact: The long-term stability of the mitigation effect, particularly the potential for re-deposition of dissolved minerals or re-aggregation of asphaltenes, requires extensive evaluation. Future work should involve long-term core flooding experiments and geochemical modeling to predict the interactions between the chemical system and the reservoir minerals over extended periods. This is crucial to assess the risk of secondary precipitation and to ensure the sustainability of the treatment [19.29,33–35].

2) Integration with Enhanced Oil Recovery (EOR) Processes: The interaction of this mitigation system with subsequent EOR processes, such as steam flooding, chemical flooding, or microbial EOR, remains unexplored. Investigating the synergy between near-wellbore damage mitigation and conformance control or mobility control agents could lead to integrated strategies that not only restore injectivity/productivity but also significantly improve ultimate recovery factors36–38,40–42.

3) Mechanistic studies on asphaltene inhibition: The current system effectively reduces viscosity and dissolves existing deposits, but its role in inhibiting asphaltene deposition is not fully understood. Future research should focus on the inhibitory mechanisms at the molecular level, using techniques such as molecular dynamics simulation or atomic force microscopy, to develop even more effective preventive formulations15–19,30–35.

Addressing these gaps will not only solidify the scientific foundation of the proposed mitigation system but also expand its applicability, efficiency, and sustainability to recover heavy oil resources.
