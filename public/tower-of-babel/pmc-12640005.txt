Recent advances in electric-stimulus assisted microneedle patches for skin disease treatment
Authors: Xiao-Nan Tao, Lin Yao, Xiujun Cheng, Xiao-Wei Xiang, Jian Qiu, Hui Zhao, Ke-Fu Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractDue to the natural barrier of skin structure, traditional topical drug utilization for skin disease management has been severely constrained by low bioavailability. While current microneedle (MN)-based systems confront challenges related to therapeutic efficacy, penetration depth, and delivery precision, they offer considerable promise as minimally invasive platforms. To address these limitations, electric stimulus (ES) has been strategically integrated as a programmable external trigger within microneedle patches, enabling spatiotemporal control over drug release kinetics. This review systematically delineates recent advancements in ES-assisted microneedle systems for managing dermatological conditions, highlighting their dual capacity to enhance transdermal permeability while minimizing systemic toxicity through localized delivery, in alignment with emerging trends in functional integration and device miniaturization. This encompasses a comprehensive analysis of the regulatory role of ES, strategic selection of power supply modules, and the rational design of microneedles in conjunction with their corresponding drug-loading strategies. Guided by the Three I Principles (Integrated-Intelligent-Individualized), future developments should focus on creating closed-loop systems with embedded biosensors for real-time biomarker monitoring, implementing AI-driven adaptive dosing algorithms, and developing modular microneedle arrays. This paradigm shift towards patient-centered care will require cross-disciplinary convergence of flexible electronics, biocompatible energy harvesters, and precision medicine approaches to accommodate personalized treatment regimens.Graphical abstract Keywords: Skin disease treatment, Electric stimulus, Microneedle patch, Drug delivery

Introduction

The skin, the body’s largest organ, serves as a critical barrier against external antigenic threats. However, skin diseases arising from allergic or infectious causes remain a major global health burden, affecting an estimated 30–70% of the population worldwide. Current treatment strategies largely rely on topical drug administration [1]. Conventional therapeutic approaches for skin diseases primarily involve the topical administration of pharmacological agents [2–5]. However, due to the natural structure of skin, only 10%−20% of topically applied drugs can penetrate the skin, resulting in inefficient drug utilization [6–8]. As a result, higher drug doses are required to achieve therapeutic concentrations at the pathological sites, which frequently induces undesirable side effects and poses health risks [9]. Various techniques have been developed for enhancing the drug bioavailability and enhance transdermal drug bioavailability, physically or biochemically [10]. Among these, microneedle (MN)-based patches have been developed for treating skin diseases with the advantages of minimal invasiveness, painlessness, low risk of infection and self-administration [1, 11, 12]. Since the typical length of microneedles ranges from 10 μm to over 1000 μm, the barrier of the stratum corneum can be overcome in a mild manner, which enhances the drug transport beyond the characteristics of low molecular weight, high solubility and small sizes across the skin while maintaining the patient adherence [13].

However, given the growing demand for comprehensive skin disease management, conventional microneedles without specialized structural designs are often insufficient to meet the complex treatment requirements. These conventional microneedles primarily rely on passive delivery mechanisms, which may be less effective for targeting deeper skin layers or more challenging conditions. Furthermore, they typically lack the ability to actively modulate skin responses or address conditions that require enhanced therapeutic effects. This has led to an increased focus on the development of advanced microneedle patches. In recent years, stimuli-responsive microneedles are deemed promising intelligent microneedles, owing to their capability for triggerable drug release or variable tissue permeability. These stimuli encompass internal biochemical indicators within tissues, such as pH and reactive oxygen species (ROS), as well as external factors including light, ultrasound, magnetic field and electric field [10, 14]. Among them, electric stimulus (ES) has been acknowledged for its regulatory effects on molecular motion and biological responses in biomedical applications [15, 16]. For instance, iontophoresis and electroporation are well-established approaches that enhance drug distribution and cellular uptake within tissues [17, 18]. In recent years, the combination of intelligent materials has enabled electro-triggered releases to be achieved with electro-degradable films or electro-responsive material configuration changes for enhancing the controllability and sustainability [19, 20]. Benefiting from the progress in wearable and flexible bioelectronic devices, ES-assisted microneedle patches have exhibited appealing superiority in controlled and on-demand drug delivery, as bioelectronics facilitate compact system integration with both electrical functionality and biocompatibility [21].

In this review, we highlight the recent advancements in ES-assisted microneedle patches for skin disease treatment. The regulatory roles of ES in different systems have been recognized for targeted enhancement. Besides, we discuss the components of ES-assisted microneedle patch, including the power supply unit and microneedle types. On this basis, we further summarize the research progress of ES-assisted microneedle patches for treatment of several common skin diseases (Fig. 1), including skin infections [9, 22], chronic wounds [23, 24], psoriasis [6, 25], skin tumors [26, 27], atopic dermatitis [19] and abnormal scars [28, 29]. Finally, we make an outlook for the development of ES-assisted microneedle patches, focusing on the convergence of multifunctional microneedle modules to promote the integration of diagnosis, monitoring and treatment [30]. Advances in cloud data processing and artificial intelligence algorithms can render intelligent decision-making on drug release strategies (drug dose and administration timing) to achieve adaptive control, complementing active control based on smartphone communication [20]. Synergy with individualized precision medicine will further provide opportunities for revolution of patient-centered care [31].

Systematic composition of ES-assisted microneedle patch

Topical administration of creams, gels, lotions and other drugs often achieves limited therapeutic efficacy due to inadequate penetration through both the cutaneous and cellular barriers. However, with the involvement of ES-assisted microneedle patch, drug delivery can be facilitated by physical penetration of the microneedle and ES for specific intentions like improving drug distribution and cellular bioavailability or on-demand release of drug [15]. ES-assisted microneedle patches comprise two primary parts: the power supply unit for electric output and the microneedle patches for drug loading and release. In the following sections, the role of ES in the systems, commonly used power supply units and microneedle patch configurations are discussed separately.

Role of ES

Regulating molecular transport

Biological barriers at multiple hierarchical levels impose significant restrictions on molecular transport [32]. ES can facilitate molecular movement, promoting uniform distribution within tissues, helping molecules traverse cellular membranes, and enabling controlled, on-demand release. In this way, ES improves both the spatial and temporal precision of molecular delivery, enhancing the effectiveness of interventions in complex biological environments.

Iontophoresis

The implement of sustained electric current (typically with low-intensity of 0.5 mA/cm2 or less) can enhance molecular transport into the tissue, a process known as iontophoresis [33]. Iontophoresis is based on the “Nernst-Planck effect” that ionized molecules are oriented to move into the target tissue by electro-repulsion. Positive ions are attracted to the anode side while negative ions to the cathode side [10]. Charged drug molecules are driven across the stratum corneum through electrophoresis [34]. The transport of non-charged drug molecules is also benefited through the electroosmotic flow since an electrochemical gradient is generated by the electric field (Fig. 2A). As a minimally invasive strategy for augmenting drug delivery, the integration of iontophoresis with microneedles represents a significant advancement, enabling more efficient and precise drug administration into deeper tissue [33]. The continuous molecular movement ceases after the ES is removed which indicates the controllability of molecule transport by optimizing the electric parameters [35].

Electroporation

The cell membrane acts as the natural barrier for non-permeable or low-permeable drugs to enter the cells which hinder the utilization of drugs. Electric field with short duration and high strength (typically several hundred kV/cm) [36] can trigger the perforation of the cell membrane which paths way for effective intracellular entry of drugs (Fig. 2B). Highly precise dose control allows the electric field to reversibly disrupt the membrane without undesirable damage to cell viability [37]. The implement of electroporation has been confirmed effective in reducing the demand of drug, such as bleomycin and cisplatin [38]. Besides, electroporation also facilitates the transcellular transport of large protein and nucleic acids [37, 39]. Since electroporation often creates long-lived aqueous pathway which renders maintained molecular transport with the comprehensive effects of osmotic pressure, localized small voltages and concentration gradients, even after the withdrawal of external ES [35]. The transport efficacy depends on concentrations and molecular weights of drugs [17].

Electric-triggered release

Electric-triggered drug release strategies have enhanced the controllability of drug administration, showing promising potential for wearable drug delivery systems [10, 14, 40]. The development of the strategies mainly relies on two mechanisms: electro-degradable coatings and electro-responsive materials (Fig. 2C). Electro-degradable coatings have been used to realize the controlled release of drugs by modulate drug encapsulation. Joule heating effect is the common phenomena of electric current which can be harnessed to modulate drug encapsulation behavior as electrothermal has shown advantages in manufacturing wearable and low-cost drug delivery device. Thermal-sensitive coatings can serve the role of regulating drug release rate, which renders the active on-demand delivery [27]. Additionally, thin films based on biocompatible metals like Mg, Mo and Au, have the potential as a gate for drug reservoirs as well where electrically triggered anodic oxidation of Mg or Mo and crevice corrosion of Au coating provide feasible approaches for actively controlling the delivery of molecules into biofluids [20]. Electro-responsive materials refer to such kind of smart materials which possess the capacity of altering mechanical or physical properties when exposed to the ES [41]. Most electro-responsive drug delivery systems are developed with the utilization of intrinsically conducting polymers [42]. For instance, polypyrrole (PPy) has been utilized in ES-assisted drug delivery systems by transitions from oxidative and reductive states to control the drug encapsulation and release [19].

Regulating cellular behavior and functions

Electric stimulus has been reported to modulate a broad spectrum of cellular behaviors and functions, and thus being employed in bioelectronic medicine to treat conditions ranging from cardiac arrhythmias to chronic pain, wound healing, spinal cord injury, movement disorders and rheumatoid arthritis [43]. Here, therapeutic potential of ES in the ES-assisted microneedle patches are discussed with an emphasis of treating skin disorders. The electric field is recognized as a key signal in wound healing, with endogenous fields generated immediately following the disruption of transepithelial potentials caused by skin injury. Accordingly, ES can modulate the interplay between exogenous and endogenous electric fields, thereby influencing the wound repair process [44]. Besides, immunomodulatory effects of ES have been widely recognized, particularly in the treatment of inflammatory skin diseases. More recently, ES has also been reported to induce transcriptomic regulation involving angiogenesis and extracellular matrix remodeling, suggesting its potential in managing skin conditions such as scar prevention and management [45].

Directed cell migration

Cell migration is central to fundamental biological processes such as wound healing, immune responses, and cancer invasion, suggesting that its regulation may represent a promising therapeutic target in both wound repair and cancer treatment [46]. ES has emerged as an effective approach to stimulate cells (Fig. 2D), holding significant implications for biomedical applications [47]. Notably, diverse cell types, including fibroblasts, macrophages, osteoblasts, and endothelial progenitor cells, exhibit a broad electrotactic response under ES, manifested as directional migration toward either the cathode or the anode depending on the specific cell type [44]. For instance, microsecond pulsed electric fields have been reported to modulate fibroblast migratory behavior by regulating actin cytoskeleton reorganization and focal adhesion turnover, resulting in a 2.5-fold acceleration of skin wound healing [48].

Macrophage polarization

Macrophages are pivotal immune cells that play essential roles in host defense and wound healing. They can be broadly classified into M1 macrophages, which mediate pro-inflammatory responses, and M2 macrophages, which promote anti-inflammatory and tissue repair processes [49]. It is worth noted that ES exhibits a dual immunomodulatory effect on macrophage polarization, which holds promises for skin tumor treatment and regulating the wound healing process (Fig. 2E). It has been reported that electrical signals activate proinflammatory macrophages, inducing significant inhibition of tumor cell proliferation [50]. Besides, ES also promotes M2 polarization in primary human macrophages, attenuating inflammatory activation and fostering a microenvironment favorable for skin repair. These findings highlight ES as a promising therapeutic strategy for macrophage modulation across diverse injury and immune defense microenvironments [51].

Transcriptome regulation

ES has also been confirmed to exert broad regulatory effects on the cellular transcriptome [45], encompassing key biological processes such as extracellular matrix remodeling and angiogenesis. (Fig. 2F). Extracellular matrix remodeling represents a critical component of skin wound healing and plays an important role in scar prevention and management [5]. Ultrafast pulsed electric fields have been reported to induce collagen fiber alignment within the three-dimensional ECM, thereby providing contact guidance for morphological adaptation and accelerating cell migration, ultimately promoting wound repair [48]. In addition, ES has been shown to mitigate scar formation by inhibiting the TGF-β/Smad signaling pathway [52]. Pulsed electric fields induce ECM remodeling by activating matrix metalloproteinases and reducing collagen synthesis through the downregulation of procollagen Iα1 (pro-Coll1) and transglutaminase 2 (TG2), which may contribute to the attenuation of hypertrophic scars and keloids [45]. Regarding angiogenesis, ES has been reported to enhance the angiogenic potential of adipose-derived stem cells by upregulating VEGF expression. Moreover, in a co-culture system of fibroblasts and human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs), ES was shown to activate the NOS signaling pathway and increase FGF2 expression, which subsequently triggered the MAPK/ERK cascade and further promoted VEGF production [53].

Power supply units

With the growing demand for versatile and user-friendly wearable electronics, energy efficiency and sustainability have emerged as critical considerations in device design. Conventional wired power sources impose constraints on practicality and daily usability. The advent of novel wireless power transfer and energy harvesting strategies has led to diverse developments in power supply units for ES-assisted microneedle patches. Here, we review recent advances in power supply units for ES-assisted microneedle patches and examine their respective advantages and limitations.

Mechanical energy conversion systems

Nanogenerators have been recognized as sustainable mechanical energy conversion systems featuring high instantaneous output power, broad material compatibility, eco-friendly and low cost [54]. These devices function by converting other kinds of energy into electrical energy, showing great potential for power supply in the self-powered device development. Currently, triboelectric nanogenerator (TENG) [55] and piezoelectric nanogenerator (PENG) [56] are two key types which possess the capacity of converting mechanical energy into electrical energy. Besides, TENGs and PENGs have gained popularity in the self-powered device development [54, 57, 58].

TENG can operate in three different modes including contact separation, sliding and freestanding based on different configurations [55]. TENG composed of polyimide (PI) film as the flexible substrate layer, silver (Ag) film as the electrode layer, copper (Cu) as the conductor, polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE) film as the friction layer, and polished aluminum (Al) foil as friction layer was developed for the power supply for a multifunctional microneedle patch (Fig. 3A). The TENG converted human mechanical motion into electric energy with a short-circuit current of 25.21 nA and an open-circuit voltage of 9.64 V for a regular twisted of the wrist joint [59]. The fabricated flexible TENG (F-TENG) working at typical contact-and-separation mode was integrated in a wearable self-powered transdermal microneedle patch for deep-seated melanoma treatment (Fig. 3B). In a single operation cycle, the measured short-circuit current of F-TENG reached up to 54 µA, the open-circuit voltage was at 482 V and the short-circuit charge transfer was approximately 0.35 µC [26]. An exercise self-powered triboelectric-responsive microneedle array was devised for controllable release of optogenetically engineered extracellular vesicles (Fig. 3C). When the motion frequency varied from 0.5 to 2.5 Hz, the open-circuit voltage remained at about 255 V and the transferred charge at about 80 nC. Simultaneously, the short-circuit current increased apparently from 3 to 16 µA [60].

PENG can harvest biomechanical energy from compressive and bending motions as well as vibrations operating in two distinct modes [57]. PENG composed of Parylene-C and ultrathin polyethylene terephthalate (PET) as the encapsulation layer, poly(vinylidene fluoride) (PVDF) as the double piezoelectric layer, and silver (Ag) as the electrode layer was integrated into a self-powered photodynamic therapy system for cancer treatment (Fig. 3D). Copper tape is employed to fix the lead wire with electrode layers. The open-circuit voltage and short-circuit transferred charge for the double PVDF layers with parallel connection as a twinning structure was 200 V and 0.46 µC, respectively [61]. PENG converting biomechanical energy into electrical energy by joints bending or hand slapping was included in a self-powered controllable transdermal drug delivery system for the treatment of psoriasis-like skin disease (Fig. 3E). The PENG was composed of PET film, PVDF, silver and Kapton film, serving as the encapsulation layer, the piezoelectric layer, the electrode layer and the substrate layer, respectively. When using the linear motor to simulate human movement, the open-circuit voltage, short-circuit current and transferred charge of PENG reached 100 V, 2 µA and 300 nC [6]. PENG integrating the PI film, carbon black conductive layer, the PVDF-BT film with the copper wires connected to the carbon black as electric contacts was utilized as the power supply unit in a novel self-powered multifunctional microneedle patch which was designed for the regulation of the microenvironment within infected diabetic wounds (Fig. 3F). The piezoelectric output of PENG attached to the wrist was recorded. When the wrist joint was regularly twisted, the maximum values of the open-circuit voltage and short-circuit current were 11.94 V and 84.62 nA, respectively [62].

Both TENG and PENG can serve as effective and sustainable power units for the wearable, self-powered systems due to their superior capacity in harvesting and converting biomechanical energy into electrical energy. Such systems are particularly advantageous for the long-term management and treatment of chronic diseases with good biocompatibility. Nonetheless, their energy generation is intrinsically reliant on biomechanical motion, resulting in a strong coupling between electrical output and physical activity. This dependency imposes spatial constraints on their deployment, limiting their applicability to anatomical sites where sufficient mechanical energy can be consistently harvested. Furthermore, instantaneous energy harvesting and output may exhibit stability issues, which can be mitigated by incorporating capacitors or battery modules for temporary energy storage. Additional logic circuits may be required for the reshape and integration of the electric pulses for specific applications, which increases device complexity in certain contexts [26]. Hence, advanced power management system may help extend the applicability of the nanogenerators [63].

Environmental and biochemical energy harvesting systems

In addition to the common energy conversion systems and power supply units, various other forms of energy are harvested to power wearable biomedical devices, including specific types of bioenergy and energy collected from the surrounding environment.

Moist-electric generators (MEGs) were used in a self-powered, wearable transdermal iontophoretic patch which harvested ambient humidity for energy generation, thus obviating the need for complex power management modules and mechanical components (Fig. 4A). The ambient moisture-driven self-powered iontophoresis patch rendered the controlled transdermal drug delivery by iontophoresis-facilitated drug administration. A single MEG could provide a consistent voltage of 0.63 V for over 10 days under ambient conditions at 60% relative humidity (RH) and 25 °C, demonstrating sustainability and stability [64]. An efficient hydrogel-based MEG was integrated into the sustainable wearable devices. The assembled MEG was based on hydrophilicity and ion concentration gradients derived from the rational combination of polyvinyl alcohol (PVA), cellulose nanofibers (CNF), phytic acid (PA), lithium bromide (LiBr), and multi-walled carbon nanotubes (MWCNT) (Fig. 4B). The PPC/Li layer composed of PVA, CNF, PA, and LiBr served as a high ion concentration and high hydrophilicity layer, whereas the PMC layer composed of PVA and MWCNT acted as a low ion concentration and low hydrophilicity layer. The working mechanism of PPC/Li-PC MEG involved the coexistence of hydrophilicity gradient, ionic concentration gradient, and redox reaction. Single PPC/Li-PMC MEG unit produced an output voltage of 0.77 V at a low RH of 10% while at 80% RH, the PPC/Li-PMC MEG achieved a stable output voltage of 1.0 V with the power density of 26.5 µW cm− 2. Besides, an increased current (4.0 mA) and voltage (45.3 V) could be achieved by parallel and series integration of MEG banks, respectively [65].

Enzymatic biobatteries, which utilize enzymes as an electrocatalysts for anodic oxidation of biochemical fuels and cathodic reduction of atmospheric O2, provide a feasible power supply for the iontophoretic transdermal patch (Fig. 4C). The fructose/O2 biobattery system was composed of four pairs of anode and cathode electrodes which were connected in series to generate sufficient transdermal electroosmotic flow. Specifically, the fructose dehydrogenase (FDH)-modified flexible carbon fabric (CF) anodes and the bilirubin oxidase (BOD)-modified CF cathodes were arranged on cotton cloth containing 0.2 M D-fructose. The measured open-circuit voltage roughly reached 2.8 V and the maximum output current was 0.52 mA/cm2. The current measured on a pig skin with resistance of 10 kΩ was around 0.2 mA/cm2 which met the requirement of effective transdermal iontophoresis [66].

The innovative continuous energy harvesting systems offer valuable insights into efficient energy utilization and management, holding significant potential for applications in wearable biomedical devices with the advantage of self-charging, suitability for long-term operation and good biocompatibility. However, the practical implementation of these distinct energy supply devices may be limited in consideration of actual surrounding environments and biosafety, which can also compromise output stability [67].

Electronic power systems

Compared to self-powered systems, various battery-powered bioelectronic modules have been developed for different intentions of ES. Although the electronic module introduces greater system complexity compared to nanogenerators, its flexible topological configuration enables broader adaptability to diverse application scenarios—particularly those requiring high operational stability and versatile electric waveforms [68, disadvantages of various microneedles are demonstrated69]. For example, one implantable bioelectronics was developed to accelerate the healing of large skin wounds by triggering membrane electroporation and facilitating drug delivery (Fig. 5A). The integration of the booster circuit was able to provide programmable output voltage with different waveforms to meet various potential requirements. Besides, the pulse duration and pulse number could be optimally selected for the improved efficiency of electroporation. The capacity of the battery set was sufficient to output a 50-ms pulsed electric field steadily at 30 V, and last for 720 rounds of electrotransfection. The use of the rechargeable lithium-ion battery further rendered better reusability [70].

Besides, inductive coupling allows instantaneous energy transfer, with the receiver directly harvesting power from the transmitter, thereby overcoming the trade-off between volume and energy density for energy storage components. As a mature wireless power transmission technique, inductive coupling can render a relatively high power output for wearable devices [71]. To realize on-demand drug delivery with high spatiotemporal controllability, a skin-interfaced, electrically triggered, gated microneedle patch was devised as the drug delivery system. The spatiotemporal on-demand patch (SOP) utilized a thin gold layer coated on the surface of the microneedle to ensure the drug encapsulation and protection, which could be effectively disintegrated by small electrical triggers for 30 s to initiate the drug release. The wireless spatiotemporal on-demand patch consisted of an energy harvesting module for wireless power, a power amplification module, a System-on-Chip (SoC) module for remote control, and a microneedle array coupled with counter electrodes. A flexible printed circuit board provided interconnected traces between the drug-loaded microneedle patches and the near-field communication (NFC) module for the wireless control of triggering signals (Fig. 5B). Based on a receiver coil, a full-bridge rectifier and a regulator, direct current (DC) output stabilized at around 2.5 V with a standard deviation of ~ 0.1 V was produced to render precise corrosion control of the gold coatings for initiating electro-responsive drug release [20].

Since iontophoresis has been well acknowledged for facilitating the drug delivery, a wearable iontophoresis-driven microneedle patch based on the iontophoresis-driven devices which was powered by a lithium battery could be recharged via a micro-USB port (Fig. 5C). The iontophoresis-driven circuit included the charging module, the boost module and the constant current output module. The boost module adjusted the output voltage of the lithium battery to the desired high voltage for the constant current chip (LM334). The iontophoresis-driven device could stably output a constant current of 0.5 mA for 1 h with little fluctuation for vaccine delivery facilitated by iontophoresis [18]. With the acknowledgment of electric stimulation’s role in regulating biological responses, combined therapies integrating controlled drug delivery and electric stimulation have gained popularity. In this aspect, a wearable reconfigurable integrated smart device (ISD) was developed for the treatment of rheumatoid arthritis combining electro-responsive drug delivery and ES (Fig. 5D). The electronics are assembled on two printed circuit boards with a power management system to harvest energy from two solar cells, storing power in a Li-polymer battery for self-powering. The anti-inflammatory effect of electrical stimulus on macrophages was revealed by molecular mechanism investigation. The ISD could output short pulses of tunable frequency (2 ~ 100 Hz) with sine or square waveforms for subsequent application [68].

Overall, electronic modules for powering ES-assisted MN patches are generally classified into two categories: those based on energy storage components such as compact batteries and supercapacitors, and those employing mature wireless power transfer techniques such as inductive coupling. Energy storage components can provide stable, long-term power for wearable systems and have therefore been widely employed in ES-assisted MN patches. However, their limited energy capacity and the requirement for periodic recharging constrain overall performance, and a fundamental trade-off exists between device size and achievable energy density [72]. Inductive coupling enables instantaneous energy transfer, allowing the receiver to harvest power directly from the transmitter and providing relatively high output for wearable applications [71]. Nonetheless, inductive coupling is susceptible to misalignment between the transmitter and receiver coils, which can reduce energy transmission efficiency and cause losses. Furthermore, increasing the distance between coils may interrupt power transfer. Besides, the potential heating caused by inductive coupling can raise safety concerns. Wireless power transmission based on electromagnetic field also poses potential risks associated with electromagnetic radiation, necessitating strict adherence to the safety guidelines of the International Commission on Non-Ionizing Radiation Protection (ICNIRP) and the Federal Communications Commission (FCC), which vary across different frequency bands [73]. It is worth noting that the bioelectronic module is compatible with sample and record of the electrophysiological signals, which holds extended potential for real-time monitoring, precise diagnosis and on-demand treatment [74]. Therefore, wearable bioelectronics based on electronic modules are expected to have broader applications with integral and multiple functions in skin disease treatment.

Microneedle types and drug loading strategies

Microneedles can be categorized into different types according to their configurations. Once penetrating the skin, microneedles can facilitate drug delivery and painless sampling. To facilitate the integration with electrical stimuli, the conductive properties of microneedles are essential, typically realized employing conductive materials or surface metallization [9, 75]. Common microneedle arrays integrated into the ES-assisted microneedle patch mainly include solid microneedles, coated microneedles, hollow microneedles and hydrogel microneedles [15]. Besides, due to the heterogeneity in physical structures and distinct chemical characteristics of their constituent materials, drug encapsulation methods of the microneedles exhibit considerable variation (Table 1). Types of conductive microneedle patches for ES-assisted drug delivery

Solid microneedle

Solid microneedles made of metals, polymers, and silicon can be fabricated by precision techniques such as mechanical micromachining, laser cutting or etching [12]. In consideration of conductivity and mechanical performance, metal and metal alloys are commonly used in manufacture, which are widely employed for ES. Solid microneedles made of metal usually have ultra-high stiffness which ensures the effective skin penetration [80]. Solid microneedles primarily employ the ‘Poke and Patch’ mechanism to achieve drug delivery. By puncturing the skin, they create transient microchannels that increase permeability and facilitate the transport of therapeutics, including macromolecules and biological compounds hindered by the stratum corneum. Drug-loaded dressings are typically applied to these channels before or after microneedle insertion to enhance delivery efficiency [81]. This strategy is attractive for its operational simplicity and considerable translational potential. However, the rapid resealing of microchannels restricts drug absorption over extended periods, posing a limitation for sustained release. To overcome this, rolling solid microneedles have been developed with structural modifications that allow repeated puncturing to prolong channel availability [82]. However, the maintenance of open pores also raises safety concerns, as it may increase the risk of pathogen or toxin invasion, highlighting critical biosafety challenges [21].

Coated microneedle

Coated microneedles are further developed by applying coatings and decorative encapsulation to solid microneedles, utilizing techniques such as spray coating, dip coating, and piezoelectric ink-jet printing [78]. The coatings typically employ a water-soluble formulation, which allows for effective dissolution once exposed within the skin [17]. This ‘Coat and Poke’ strategy enables the co-delivery of multiple compounds with distinct physicochemical properties, broadening the therapeutic scope. Further, due to electrostatic attraction, negatively charged substances like DNA or viruses can also be loaded onto the positively charged microneedles to form a coating, underscoring their versatility as platforms for diverse drugs [78]. However, the limitations of coated microneedles are reflected in the trade-off between the drug loading capacity and the sharpness of microneedles, as well as concerns regarding the stability and integrity of drugs in the coatings due to formulation migration during production and storage [21]. In recent years, multi-layer coatings, assembled layer-by-layer, can impart multiple functions to the microneedles by stepwise release and further increase the drug loading capacity [83, 84]. Besides, the additional coating of electro-responsive layers may help improve the controlled release [27].

Hollow microneedle

Hollow microneedles are characterized by their distinctive structural design, which features hollow or porous configurations integrated within the microneedle body [85]. Hollow microneedles can be engineered from a variety of materials, including polymers, metals, and silicon. This distinctive design not only mitigates the discomfort associated with traditional injection methods but also enhances the precision and efficacy of drug administration by directly delivering drugs through the hollow lumen into the skin, which is often referred to as the “Poke and Flow” strategy [12]. This approach closely mimics conventional injection methods while retaining the advantages of minimally invasive, rapid delivery, thereby offering the potential to improve patient acceptance and facilitate self-administration. Further, the structure facilitates the encapsulation and subsequent release of pharmaceutical agents or other biomedical substances like proteins and cells, which renders the recognized application in clinical vaccine administration. Additionally, hollow microneedles exhibit good compatibility with extended drug reservoirs, thereby enabling sustained and controlled drug release [86]. Besides, hollow microneedles also demonstrate excellent performance in sampling of interstitial fluid (ISF) and glucose monitoring [21]. Fabrication techniques for hollow microneedles encompass advanced methodologies such as three-dimensional (3D) printing, drawing lithography and etching [11]. Nonetheless, the key concerns about hollow microneedles remain regarding the potential risk of clogging, poor biocompatibility and reduced mechanical strength due to hollow architectures, all of which may compromise safe and reliable skin penetration. Besides, the widespread adoption of hollow microneedles is currently constrained by high cost of their production and considerations for inadequate mechanical strength during skin penetration [78].

Hydrogel microneedle

Hydrogel microneedles are meticulously fabricated from crosslinked hydrogels such as Gelatin Methacrylate (GelMA) and hyaluronic acid methacrylate (HAMA). Hydrogels are recognized with the capacity of the interaction with extracellular matrix (ECM) and cells, which show inherent advantages in cell therapy and tissue engineering [24]. Commonly, this type of microneedles employs an internal drug loading manner, wherein the encapsulated pharmaceuticals are released through the process of interstitial fluid absorption and subsequent swelling [21]. Hydrogel microneedles generally employ “Poke and Swell” strategy during drug delivery, where gel materials dissolve and swell upon insertion, expanding within the skin to generate pores that promote drug diffusion [78]. The natural morphology of hydrogels endowed by fabrication techniques like micro-molding and 3D printing renders the absorbance of ISF, which indicates the potential in painless sampling and disease monitoring. Besides, hydrogel microneedles can be removed intact after application, leaving no residual material in the skin, which improves their safety profile [79]. However, concerns are raised with regard to the potential drug inactivation during the cross-linking process for fabrication of hydrogel microneedles [34]. Moreover, the commercial viability of hydrogel microneedles may be further compromised considering the inherent toxicity stemming from crosslinkers and the pronounced deficiencies in mechanical strength [12].

Application of ES-assisted microneedle patch for skin disease treatment

Skin injury management

Skin Anti-infection

Skin infections caused by bacteria, fungi and viruses pose significant therapeutic challenges due to limited drug penetration through the stratum corneum, resulting in low bioavailability and inadequate sustained release at infection sites. Conventional topical treatments are effective for superficial infections but fail against deep cutaneous infections and microbial biofilms. The dense extracellular matrix of biofilms restricts antimicrobial agent penetration while promoting microbial resistance through enzyme secretion and horizontal gene transfer. Furthermore, systemic antibiotic administration exacerbates resistance risks. These limitations highlight the urgent need for improved drug delivery strategies to enhance therapeutic efficacy while minimizing resistance development [22]. Antimicrobial microneedle patches have been designed for enhancing the utilization of the drugs with the combination of ES for further drug delivery. Conductive microneedles fabricated with carbon nanotubes (CNTs) and GelMA were used to deliver miconazole into the subcutaneous layer to treat fungal infection and induce protective immunity (Fig. 7A). ES of 0.5 mA for 5 min was applied to achieve the electrically controlled drug release. The electrically stimulated microneedle patches loaded with miconazole nitrate (M-MNs) outperformed the miconazole cream and M-MNs without ES in biofilm penetration capability and fungicidal efficacy. The anti-fungal and wound healing effects have been confirmed in ex-vivo and in-vivo models. Besides, the subcutaneous neuro-immune axis was stimulated to induce protective immunity by dermal dendritic cells and γδ-T cells [9].

Microenvironment of the chronic wounds matters especially under the condition of infection due to diabetes. The self-powered multifunctional antibacterial microneedle patch was designed with the intention of regulating the microenvironment of infected wound. GA@DFO@ZIF-8 nanoparticles were loaded in the microneedles and controlled to release by the acidic microenvironment and ES powered by PENG (Fig. 7B). The multifunctional microneedle patch loaded with GA@DFO@ZIF-8 nanoparticles and powered by PENG (GDZ@mMNP-PENG) exhibited excellent capabilities in the aspects of antibacterial (antibacterial rate exceeded 96%), antioxidation and angiogenesis, along with the synergistic regulation of the inflammatory environment in the infected wounds [62].

To render the conductivity of microneedle patches, conductive drugs also provide a feasible solution. By covalent bonding between Levofloxacin and carbon quantum dots, LevCDs were synthesized as conductive drugs which were further incorporated into PEGDA-enhanced classical PAAm-alginate composition to fabricate the conductive microneedle patch (LevMN). LevMN powered by TENG delivered both ES and drugs in a combinatorial manner (Fig. 7C), which showed decent efficacy in antimicrobial treatment of infected wounds, improving the drug-loading capacity, patient compliance, and biosafety compared to traditional methods [107].

With the growing demand for real-time theranostics, significant progress has been made in integrating diagnostic modules into ES-assisted microneedle patches. An integrated system based on TENG and steel microneedle patches, which were coated with soluble polyvinylpyrrolidone MN infused with antibiotics or sliver and carbon nanotubes, was developed for infected wound management (Fig. 7D(i)). The MN-TENG system was confirmed to be effective in inhibiting bacterial growth for both S. aureus (Fig. 7D(ii)) and E. coli (Fig. 7D(iii)) and thus expediting wound healing through sustained and augmented drug delivery in mechanical and electronical manners [58].

The implementation of ES-assisted microneedle patches enhances the transdermal permeability and utilization efficiency of conventional drugs for skin anti-infection therapy. Beyond improving drug delivery, the incorporation of ES fundamentally transforms the administration strategy for combating bacterial, fungal, and viral infections. Furthermore, the integration of novel nanomaterials into the microneedle matrix, in conjunction with ES, enables a functional upgrade of the system and further enhances its systemic antimicrobial performance [22].

Wound healing acceleration

Wound healing is a significant process for maintaining skin integrity after injury. Chronic wounds or wounds that take a long time to heal impose threats on the global public health with millions of people affected every year [5, 113]. The process of wound healing is complicated, involving multiple factors related to cell migration and inflammatory response [114]. Untreated wounds can result in evident integumentary necrosis, with their severity depending on the depth and extent of the damage. The common strategy for treating open wounds involves the use of dressings composed of various drug-embedded materials to enhance the wound healing process [70, 113]. However, the penetration and pharmacodynamic efficacy of drugs are constrained by their molecular weight and biostability in vivo, making the optimization of drug administration strategies a critical challenge. Combining microneedle patch and ES provides a feasible solution for improved wound healing [15, 23].

A microneedle-based self-powered transcutaneous ES system has been developed for the administration of epidermal growth factor (EGF), a small polypeptide, which is essential for regulating cellular behavior such as survival, growth, migration and differentiation (Fig. 8A). The microneedle-based self-powered transcutaneous ES system (mn-STESS) is powered by a sliding free-standing triboelectric nanogenerator (sf-TENG). Polylactic acid coated with gold (PLA-Au) and cross-linked gelatin–cross-linked hyaluronic acid (cGel-cHA) composite microneedle patches (CMNPs) are used for loading EGF and imposing ES (Fig. 8A(i)). Since glutathione may destabilize the EGF structure by breaking the disulfide bonds, resulting in the inactivation of EGF in vivo, ES inhibits the GSH-mediated reduction of EGF by modulating the mobility of both molecules, thereby preserving the stability of exogenous EGF (Fig. 8A(ii)). Hence, the mn-STESS can effectively improve the delivery of EGF to aid wound healing [75].

The microneedle-embedded closed-loop smart dressing (MelD) has been designed for enhancing the drug loading capacity and improving the drug penetration into the deeper tissue (Fig. 8B). The MelD integrates the impedance sensing module and hollow-microneedle-based drug reservoir for closed loop management of wound healing, coupling early diagnosis and timely therapy (Fig. 8B(i) and (ii)). MelD renders sufficient delivery of growth factor to the wound, promotes drug entry into deep tissue layers and induces faster wound healing in the treated mice.

Furthermore, other techniques have been investigated for intervening and accelerating the process of wound healing. Among these, ES has been acting as the regulator for promoting cell migration, proliferation and differentiation with DC stimulus [115] or pulsed electric field (PEF) stimulus [48], demonstrating its potential for effective wound treatment. Cells can react to different ES. Under DC electric stimulation with low amplitude, cells exhibit electrotaxis, thereby promoting cell migration towards the wound site [116]. The microsecond PEF with a relatively high intensity can activate the fibroblasts, trigger actin rearrangement and regulate the migrating modes of cells with reduced cell damage, which facilitates the healing of wound [48]. Besides, at the tissue level, the epithelial layer of intact skin exhibits a potential difference derived from the heterogeneous ion distribution of skin epithelial cells [117]. After the appearance of trauma, the endogenous electric field builds up for the short circuit of the normal epidermal transepithelial potential, which is closely related to the differences in ionic gradients at the wound site. Hence, regulating the interaction between external electric field and endogenous electric field of wound has been a hot topic for accelerating the wound healing process [118].

Overall, ES-assisted microneedle patches offer a promising strategy that synergistically combines electrical stimulation and drug delivery to accelerate wound healing. These systems enhance therapeutic performance by enabling controlled release and improving pharmacokinetics, thereby increasing delivery efficiency, reducing drug-related toxicity, and supporting comprehensive wound management [15, 75, 115].

Scar prevention and management

Scar formation represents a common pathological outcome of tissue repair processes across various organs, with the skin being the most frequently affected site due to its high susceptibility to injury. Various factors can induce improper wound healing including prolonged inflammation and genetic susceptibility. Usually, scars appear when the recovery of restoration of tissue integrity induces the formation of fibrotic tissue. Wound healing usually undergoes hemostasis, inflammation, proliferation and remodeling. Pathological scar formation commonly happens in the remodeling stage, of which hypertrophic scars and keloids are two typical pathological scarring. Hypertrophic scar usually exhibits the characteristics of thin collagen fibers arranged in nodules while keloids are characterized by an increased ratio of collagen deposition with increased fibroblast proliferation rates for invasive growth beyond the injuring sites [119]. Besides, transforming growth factor (TGF) family is differentially expressed for hypertrophic scars and keloids. The expression of TGF-β1 and TGF-β2 is higher in hypertrophic scars than keloids while the expression of TGF-β3 is higher in keloids. Differences also exist in the level of inflammatory infiltration, ratio of specific matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs), tissue inhibitors of metalloproteinases (TIMPs), thickness of the epidermis and rete ridges [120].

Current therapeutic strategies for treating scar mainly involve topical treatment, surgery, lasers, radiotherapy and glucocorticoid injection [120,121]. Topical treatments, such as anti-scar gels, scar creams, and flavonoids, exhibit limited therapeutic efficacy due to poor tissue penetration. While surgical and other physical interventions are beneficial for scar management, they each present specific limitation. Additionally, the injection of pharmacological agents, including glucocorticoids, 5-fluorouracil (5-FU), and interferons, carries potential risks of drug toxicity and adverse effects associated with high doses [121].

The management of scar involves scar prevention during wound healing and scar treatment after formation. ES assisted microneedle patch combined the dual effects for managing the scar. Microneedle patches loaded with glucose oxidase (GOx) and horseradish peroxidase (HRP), which are encapsulated in ZIF-8 nanoparticles in anode and cathode arrays respectively, take advantage of the local hyperglycemia in diabetic wounds to trigger enzymatic cascade reaction (Fig. 9A(i)), generating stable microcurrent to promote rapid but scar preventative healing (Fig. 9A(ii-iii)). The self-powered enzyme-linked microneedle patch also exhibits excellent anti-bacterial properties, high biocompatibility and efficient inflammatory inhibition [28]. Besides, microneedles have been confirmed to have therapeutic effects on hypertrophic scars through the signaling pathway of mechanical communication in a drug-free manner [122]. Further, PEF has also exhibited the potential of inducing extracellular matrix remodeling by activating MMPs and thus decreasing collagen production, which benefits the fading of scars [45]. Based on a battery-powered PEF device and surface needle electrode, PEF induced a reduction of 40%−50% in dorsal burn wounds of rats [29]. By means of partially irreversible electroporation, a reduction of 57.9% for scar area after burn injury can be observed [123]. Recently, a paper battery powered iontophoresis-driven microneedle patch (PBIMNP) was developed for the self-management of hypertrophic scars (Fig. 9B(i)). By employing the drug delivery strategy consisting of pressing and poking, phase transformation, diffusion and iontophoresis (Fig. 9B(ii)), the system effectively promoted the formation of evenly distributed and loosely arranged collagen fibers (Fig. 9B(iii)), underscoring its potential clinical significance [124].

Treatment of chronic inflammatory diseases

Treatment of psoriasis

Psoriasis is a common chronic inflammatory disease with a global prevalence of approximately 2–3%. The incidence of psoriasis has demonstrated an increasing trend, varying from 30.3 per 100,000 person-years to 321.0 per 100,000 person-years [125]. The clinical types of psoriasis can be classified into plaque psoriasis, guttate psoriasis, inverse psoriasis, pustular psoriasis, and erythrodermic psoriasis, each exhibiting different histopathological features. Treatment of psoriasis imposes a heavy burden on individuals and health systems worldwide due to its treatment resistance and tendency to relapse with no known cure at present stage [6, 126]. With the aim of reducing inflammation, eliminating lesions and preventing complications, conventional therapies for psoriasis include topical corticosteroids, vitamin D analogues, calcineurin inhibitors, keratolytics and targeted phototherapy [127]. ES combined with microneedle patches has provided feasible options for efficacious treatment, where sufficient drug delivery and effective electron injection impose synergistic effects on promoting specific immunomodulation and tissue reconstruction [11].

A wearable, battery-free, multi-component drug-loaded electronic microneedle patch (mD-eMN) integrating TENG and metal microneedles coated with multi-component chemical drugs (Fig. 10A(i)) has been developed for the treatment of inflammatory skin diseases (ISDs) with psoriasis as the representative. To form multiple-component drug coatings, tazarotene (TAZ) encapsulated in fat-soluble polymer polyvinylpyrrolidone (PVP) and betamethasone (BM) dissolved with water-soluble carbomer were assembled layer-by-layer as the outer and inner layers of the microneedle, respectively (Fig. 10A(ii)). Since TAZ possesses immunomodulatory effects by regulating gene transcription and palliates abnormal cell proliferation, BM can alleviate the stimulatory reaction caused by TAZ with the anti-allergic effect. The multi-component chemical drug layers rendered rapid drug release within 5 min, which promoted the drug penetration into cells with synergistic effects of ES from TENG. The proposed mD-eMN system was proven effective in alleviating the symptoms of psoriasis (Fig. 10A(iii)), realizing rapid remission of inflammation and cutaneous function reconstruction [83].

To deliver a controllable amount of drug to the target sites and satisfy the on-demand drug administration requirement of patients, a smart transdermal drug delivery system coupling a microneedle patch loaded with dexamethasone (Dex) and a PENG was designed (Fig. 10B(i) and (ii)). The self-powered controllable transdermal drug delivery system (sc-TDDS) could release Dex in response to the ES derived from mechanical deformation of PENG by virtue of the introduction of PPy film (Fig. 10B(iii)). PPy possesses the superior capability in drug loading and controlled release by transitions between the oxidative and reductive states (Fig. 10B(iv)), which is therefore regarded as an intelligent electro-responsive material. There was almost no drug release from the system when PENG stopped working, which indicated the effective control of drug delivery. The proposed sc-TDDS outperformed the traditional Dex solution coating in treating psoriasis-like skin disease with the inflammatory skin recovering to normal after 5 days [6].

The advances in microneedle patch empowered by ES usher in new prospects for the treatment of psoriasis with the functions including controlled drug release and precise control over penetration depth and dose administration. Future research initiatives are expected to concentrate on the development of genetic and immunological drug delivery systems, with the objective of fostering personalized and patient-friendly therapeutic modalities.

Treatment of atopic dermatitis

Atopic dermatitis (AD) is a common chronic inflammatory skin disease, affecting 15–30% of children and 2–10% of adults in developed countries [128]. The clinical features include recurrent, pruritic, localized eczematous rashes, often with seasonal fluctuations [2]. Prolonged inflammation and scratching can cause the skin to thicken, leading to persistent pruritus, which can severely affect daily activities and sleep and reduce quality of life. Therefore, pruritus serves as a fundamental element of the Hanifin-Rajka diagnostic criteria and is broadly utilized in epidemiological investigations, clinical trials, and clinical settings for the diagnosis of AD [129]. The primary objectives of AD therapy are to alleviate pruritus and establish long-term disease management. On the basis of current research into the pathogenesis of AD, the onset of AD is considered to be associated with an excessively activated and aggressive immune response to specific allergens or irritants encountered in the surrounding environment [3]. First-line approaches for the treatment of AD like topical corticosteroids and systemic immunosuppressants exhibited limitations associated with adverse effects and poor patient adherence [11, 128]. Therefore, improving the pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic properties of different drugs for efficacious treatment of AD assumes an essential role.

A novel drug delivery system based on electro-responsive microneedle patch was developed, improving the drug release performance and spatiotemporal drug concentration after the microneedle patch pierces into the skin. The controlled transdermal drug delivery system (c-TDDs) integrated two-electrode microneedle patch (t-EMNP) including one PLA-Pt-PPy microneedle array and one PLA-Pt microneedle array (Fig. 11 (i)). The t-EMNP was powered by external traditional battery (1.5 and 3.0 V) placed behind the patch with an electrical circuit through the skin tissue interstitial fluid in vivo. The treatment efficacy of c-TDDs containing Dex for AD therapy was verified on 2,4-dinitrochlorobenzene (DNCB)-induced AD-bearing mouse model on the naked dorsal skin with manifest symptoms (Fig. 11 (ii) and (iii)). The results showed that symptoms like skin edema, bleeding, erythema, abrasion, and erosion were gradually alleviated and the weight of mice returned to normal in the c-TDDS group (Fig. 11 (iv)). The skin H&E staining and ELISA results demonstrated reduced inflammatory cell infiltration and decreased concentration of proinflammatory mediators which indicated the significant therapeutic efficacy of the c-TDDs in AD therapy [19]. Besides, photosensitive dermatitis induced by excessive ultraviolet exposure shares pathological features with AD, including reduced dermal collagen fibers, tissue degeneration, and abnormal elastic fiber deposition. A recent study introduced a novel galvanic cell microneedle (GCMN) patch incorporating magnesium-based bipolar electrodes for the treatment of photosensitive dermatitis. Based on a galvanic cell mechanism, GCMN patches could generate microcurrents and release hydrogen gas and magnesium ions via redox reactions. The combined effects of hydrogen’s antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties, microcurrent-induced cell migration, and magnesium-mediated angiogenesis and macrophage M2 polarization synergistically contributed to wrinkle reversal and skin rejuvenation. These findings further underscored the applicability of ES-assisted microneedles in alleviating diverse dermatitis symptoms and highlighted their potential for aesthetic and dermatological applications [130].

Though intelligent ES-assisted microneedle patches have shown promising benefits in AD therapy, the physiological progress of AD exhibits acute or chronic characteristics, which brings about the demand of spatio-temporal regulation on precise drug delivery through in-time monitoring and voltage modulation [11]. Besides, the promising individualized medicine may further reduce the drug stimulation and render safer immunological regulation in the treatment of AD, circumventing adverse effects and restoring skin barrier function [128, 129].

Treatment of skin tumor

The prevalence of skin cancer has shown an increasing trend resulting from the accelerated population aging worldwide, with squamous cell carcinoma, basal cell carcinoma, and malignant melanoma as the primary forms seriously threatening humans [4, 131]. Common therapeutic interventions for superficial skin cancer involve surgical resection, radiotherapy, chemotherapy, phototherapy and immunotherapy. In the treatment of cutaneous tumors, topical administration has exhibited distinct advantages over traditional intravenous or intratumor injection and oral administration. Besides, local delivery of chemotherapeutic drugs by microneedle patches shows great potential in treating superficial skin tumors with reduced drug dose and minimal side effects [1]. The combination with flexible electronics has achieved great progress in the treatment of superficial skin tumors [18, 26].

A flexible bioelectronic microneedle patch (FBMP) for active on-demand controlled delivery was developed and integrated with the flexible printed circuit, the flexible substrate, the eutectic gallium indium (EGaIn) liquid metal heating film and the heat-responsive microneedle module (Fig. 12A (i)). By wireless communication, the output power of the flexible circuit board was modulated to control the heat production of the liquid metal heating film which further controlled the thermal response phase change of the material on the microneedle surface. The electric heat regulation ultimately adjusted the drug release rate depending on the material’s phase, and thus rendered the on-demand drug delivery. Sustained release delivery was realized in the anti-tumor treatment based on subcutaneous murine melanoma B16F10 tumor model. The result demonstrated that the FBMP-facilitated PRO-IFN-γ delivery provided outstanding suppression of the tumor volume compared to the other groups (Fig. 12A (ii)). Besides, FBMP combined delivery of IFN-γ and PRO induced anti-tumor immune responses with reduced expression of PD-L1 and increased intra-tumoral T-lymphocyte infiltration [27].

Since abundant antigen-presenting cells such as dendritic cells are broadly distributed in the skin, effective antigen presentation within the skin tissue holds great potential for the initiation of transcutaneous immunization for immunotherapy. However, antigen and chemokine proteins possess the characteristics of big molecular weight and fast degradation, which results in insufficient antigen presentation with the stratum corneum further impeding the penetration. To improve the bioavailability and cellular uptake of free antigens and chemokines, a simple and safe transcutaneous vaccination platform was constructed based on poly-glycidyl methacrylate-composed porous microneedle array with conductive properties. Outer membrane vesicles (OMVs) derived from Escherichia coli which were transformed by plasmid DNAs (pDNAs) encoding gp100 and CCL21 genes, were entrapped into the porous microneedle patch (Fig. 12B(i) and (ii)). ES acted the role of iontophoresis to stimulate the release of transgenic OMVs and promote the cellular uptake of proteins for enhanced bioavailability. The iontophoresis-driven microneedle arrays containing transgenic OMVs demonstrated excellent anti-tumor effects and significantly suppressed the tumor growth, increasing the survival rate, which indicated the advantages of OMVs as an immune vaccine carrier (Fig. 12B(iii)) [109]. Besides, iontophoresis can be utilized to effectively treat the deep-seated melanoma by enhancing transdermal drug delivery. A wearable, self-powered microneedle patch was developed by integrating a F-TENG with water-soluble microneedles. These microneedles were encapsulated with negatively charged, pH-responsive nanoparticles that were loaded with therapeutic drugs. With the synergistic assistance of microneedle insertion and iontophoresis regulation, therapeutic nanoparticles could be delivered to deep skin locations to induce superior therapeutic efficacy for deep-seated melanoma [26].

Recent advances in ES-assisted microneedle patches for skin tumor treatment mainly help improve the penetration and the utilization of anti-tumor drugs for reduced dose demand and enhanced immunological regulation [18]. Besides, endeavors involving the innovation of drug carriers and the modulation of ES to regulate the drug release and induce a synergistic anti-tumor effect may further revolutionize the application of ES-assisted microneedle patches in skin tumor therapy for precision and personalized medicine [16, 132].

Outlook and perspective

As an emerging biomedical technology, ES-assisted microneedle (MN) patches face several challenges that may hinder widespread clinical adoption. Table 2 provides representative preclinical studies related to MN patches combined with ES. Current clinical trials have predominantly centered on dermatological aesthetics and skin rejuvenation, encompassing anti-aging interventions, melasma alleviation, and the treatment of periorbital static wrinkles [133–135]. Beyond these indications, products such as ePatch have demonstrated favorable tolerability in humans and have been utilized for the delivery of nucleic acid-based vaccines, highlighting the prospective applicability of this platform in gene therapy and skin tumor management [88]. Moreover, nano-pulse stimulation with microneedles has been investigated for the treatment of sebaceous hyperplasia [136], while microneedle-mediated electroporation with corticosteroids or calcium has shown clinical efficacy in managing keloid scars [102, 137]. Nevertheless, the clinical use of ES-assisted MN patches in skin-related disorders remains confined to small-molecule drugs and a limited subset of hormone-based therapeutics [17]. Therefore, although their current clinical status is still at a preliminary stage, continuous research and translational efforts are progressively driving ES-assisted MN patches toward broader clinical implementation.

In addition, several critical concerns need to be addressed to facilitate the clinical translation of ES-assisted microneedle patches. Safety concerns are primarily associated with the stability and reliability of electrical stimulation under diverse and complex skin conditions. Moreover, systematic investigations are required to evaluate individual variability and to optimize electrical parameters, including pulse waveform, intensity, frequency, and duration. The clinical therapeutic efficacy of ES-assisted MN patches for diverse skin disorders, together with their cellular and tissue toxicology and potential biological effects arising from the synergistic interactions of electrical stimulation, microneedles, and drug delivery, warrants further systematic evaluation in both the short and long term. Besides, user compliance remains an important issue, as establishing standardized protocols for their application is still a formidable task [15]. The establishment of evidence-based guidelines will be critical for ensuring the safe and consistent application of ES-assisted MN patches in clinical practice.

Another critical concern pertains to device development since the development of ES-assisted microneedle patches is still at a preliminary stage. Manufacturing challenges primarily involve the selection of conductive microneedle matrices that not only provide adequate mechanical strength and biocompatibility but also enable structural tunability to achieve multifunctional drug delivery [138]. In addition, issues of manufacturability and scalability persist, as certain fabrication techniques are intrinsically constrained in their potential for large-scale production [30]. Addressing these challenges will depend heavily on advances in novel biomaterials and fabrication strategies. With the increasing maturity of microneedle manufacturing techniques and the continuous expansion of application scenarios, the development of ES-assisted microneedle patches will be more focused on a patient-centered care in the actual skin disease treatment for clinical practices [138]. Here, we proposed the Three I Principles: Integrated, Intelligent, Individualized, which provided an outlook on the application of ES-assisted microneedle patches in the treatment of skin diseases. Through spatial partitioning of diagnostic and therapeutic functionalities within microneedle modules, diverse features can be seamlessly integrated into a unified system. This modular approach paves the way for advanced closed-loop management, incorporating multiple diagnostic and therapeutic functions, on-demand intelligent treatment interventions, and personalized medicine for individualized skin disease management, as shown in Fig. 13 [13, 31].

Outlooks and perspectives of ES-assisted microneedle patch

Integrated multifunctional domains

With the growing demand for in time monitoring and diagnosis, a variety of microneedle-based sensors have been developed [69]. The concept of closed-loop management has been broadly adopted in diabetic management [80, 139] and wound management [100] combining real-time diagnosis and timely treatment. In this aspect, endeavors are needed to put into integrating different functional regions within the limited area of the microneedle patch, so that the entire system can possess diverse functionalities while maintaining a compact form [21, 74]. Since many biophysical or biochemical biomarkers provide insights into the condition of skin lesions, offering important references for the targeted treatment of skin diseases [30, 140]. The variations of the impedance assume an essential indicator during the treatment of wound [117]. The dynamic fluctuations within the tissue microenvironment, such as pH and the enrichment of specific biomolecules (enzyme, metabolite), which provide significant insights into the physiological conditions, play a pivotal role in dictating the optimal timing and dose for drug administration [141]. Besides, the localized drug concentration with the tissue matters as well to render lasting effective interventions without undesired side effects. The integrated ES-assisted microneedle patches with diverse diagnostic and therapeutic functions may serve as a robust data-driven bioelectronic platform, facilitating continuous monitoring of the disease progression and enabling subsequent tailored, on-demand therapeutic interventions [142].

Intelligent On-Demand drug delivery

For intelligent, on-demand treatment, biomarker data can be transmitted to the cloud for large-scale processing and comparative analysis. By leveraging medical artificial intelligence algorithms, the system enables integrated diagnostic interpretation, supporting intelligent clinical decision-making and the adaptive optimization of therapeutic paradigms [141]. Adaptive control of drug delivery can compensate for routine patient-managed dosing via smartphones, thereby reducing user dependence [31]. The integration of both active and passive drug administration modes facilitates the precise regulation of drug concentration, ultimately enhancing therapeutic efficacy. Furthermore, since ES acts essential roles in the entire system, accurate modulation of pulse waveforms, electrical dosage, and parameter optimization is of critical significance [15, 62]. These elements can synergistically modulate drug release kinetics, spatial distribution, and cellular uptake through controlled electrical stimulus [27].

Individualized precision medicine

As the drug loading manners differ for different configurations of microneedles, the ES-assisted microneedle patch possess the potentials toward individualized precision medicine in the scenario of gene therapy or cell therapy [143]. Apart from common therapeutic compound, pharmaceutical agents encapsulated within microneedles have been extended to acellular matrices as well as genetically modified cellular entities [30]. Acellular matrix-based microneedles comprise therapeutic molecules such as antibodies, nucleic acids, and extracellular vesicles, which can be obtained from artificial synthesis or cellular extraction [24, 144]. Conversely, the cellular matrix incorporates immune cells, including T lymphocytes, dendritic cells and macrophages, which are meticulously encapsulated within the microneedles using specific techniques to ensure precise and efficacious delivery [25, 145]. It is imperative to differentiate these biologically active components from conventional chemical compounds, given the risk of degradation and inactivation of antibodies and nucleic acids, as well as the reduced viability of the encapsulated cells [146]. Furthermore, the integration of these microneedles into clinical practice may necessitate isolation from ES or utilization of adjusted electric parameters within a safe threshold to preserve the integrity and functionality of the encapsulated agents [146]– [147]. Moreover, the strides made in the realm of precision medicine shed light on an individualized diagnosis targeting specific biomarkers, which help propel the progression of patient-centric healthcare paradigms [31].
