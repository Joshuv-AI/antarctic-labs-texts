Searching for Bloch wave packets with (almost) definite momentum direction
Arseni Goussev, Gregory V. Morozov

Abstract
The motion of a quantum particle in a one-dimensional periodic potential can be described in terms of Bloch wave packets. Like free-particle wave packets, they can propagate without attenuation. Here, we examine this similarity more closely by investigating whether Bloch wave packets can maintain a definite – or nearly definite – momentum direction, a property inherent to free-particle wave packets. This question is particularly relevant to the feasibility of using solid-state-based systems in the search for the first experimental realization of quantum backflow, a quantum interference effect in which a particle’s probability density flows in a direction opposite to its momentum.



I
Introduction

When a quantum particle moves through a periodic potential, its behavior differs fundamentally from that of a classical particle. According to the celebrated Bloch theory, a perfectly periodic potential becomes effectively transparent to a quantum matter wave of suitable energy, allowing it to propagate “without attenuation because of the coherent constructive interference of the scattered waves”
Ashcroft and Mermin 1976
. As Kittel states it, “matter waves can propagate freely in a periodic structure,” and “Bloch functions can be assembled into wave packets to represent electrons that propagate freely through the potential field of the ion cores”
Kittel 2004
. This free-particle-like motion of an electron through the periodic potential of a crystal is crucial for understanding the electrical properties of solids, such as the emergence of ballistic transport in mesoscopic systems
Holcomb 1999
;
Jalabert 2016
. The resemblance between quantum particle propagation in periodic potentials and in free space suggests that mesoscopic systems could be used to explore quantum backflow -- a classically impossible effect in which the probability density of a particle moves in a direction opposite to its momentum

provide a comprehensive list of more recent advancements in the field.

Quantum backflow is typically considered for a free particle on a line. The particle is prepared in a quantum state with a definite momentum direction, assumed to be positive for concreteness. Since free-space evolution conserves the momentum direction, any measurement of the particle’s momentum at a later time is guaranteed to yield a positive value, albeit of a random magnitude. Quantum backflow then manifests as the emergence of a negative probability current at certain points in space and time, meaning that despite having positive momentum, the particle can locally move in the negative direction.

Although quantum backflow was first recognized as a distinct phenomenon over three decades ago
Bracken and Melloy 1994
, it has yet to be experimentally demonstrated. Significant progress has been achieved in laboratory studies of its optical analog
Eliezer et al. 2020
;
Daniel et al. 2022
;
Ghosh et al. 2023
;
Zhang et al. 2025
, but no experiment has successfully observed quantum backflow with massive particles, such as electrons or atoms. (An experimental scheme utilizing Bose-Einstein condensates was proposed in Refs.
Palmero et al. 2013
;
Mardonov et al. 2014
, but to our knowledge, it has never been realized in a laboratory.) The challenge lies in the fact that quantum backflow is inherently weak – according to theory, less than
4\%
of the total probability can be transported against the particle’s momentum
Bracken and Melloy 1994
;
Eveson et al. 2005
;
Penz et al. 2006
– as well as in the exotic nature of the theoretical backflow-maximizing state, which is spatially unbounded and has infinite mean energy
Yearsley et al. 2012
. (Some of these difficulties can be mitigated by considering quantum backflow in a circular geometry
Goussev 2021
.) Identifying favorable physical systems is therefore crucial for making the first experimental observation of the effect possible, as well as for exploring potential technological applications.

A promising approach to realizing quantum backflow experimentally is to use electrically charged particles moving along an effectively one-dimensional channel, as proposed in Ref.
Bracken and Melloy 1994
. Since the electric current is proportional to the probability current, detecting a current (divided by charge) flowing opposite to the particle’s momentum would serve as experimental confirmation of quantum backflow. In this regard, an electronic wave packet propagating ballistically through a mesoscopic quantum wire presents an appealing experimental scenario. This raises a crucial question: does the dynamics of an electron in the periodic potential of a crystal lattice allow for time-dependent states with positive momentum, which are essential for accessing quantum backflow physics? In other words, do there exist normalizable superpositions of Bloch waves – henceforth referred to as Bloch wave packets – whose momentum maintains a definite direction at all times? If not, what is the highest possible probability that a momentum measurement performed on a Bloch wave packet yields a positive value? Moreover, does this probability remain constant, or does it change in time as the particle moves through the crystal? These questions are the focus of the present study.

While the properties of stationary Bloch waves in one-dimensional periodic potentials have been extensively studied
Cottey 1971
;
Eastham 1973
;
Morozov and Sprung 2011
;
Ruz-Cuen and Gutiérrez-Vega 2021
, time-dependent Bloch wave packets have received relatively little attention. Nonetheless, some important differences between the motion of Bloch wave packets and that of free particles have been investigated. Kroemer first observed that the propagation of a single-band Bloch wave packet with a narrow quasi-momentum distribution is not uniform but rather “similar to the motion of a caterpillar”: the wave packet undergoes periodic shape variations superimposed on an average velocity, equal to the group velocity
Kroemer 1975
. The origin of these shape variations was later elucidated by Churchill, who showed that a narrow-quasi-momentum wave packet can be expressed as a discrete superposition of “subpackets” with distinct but constant shapes, all propagating at the same group velocity while having different phase velocities
Churchill 1977a
. Additional insights and methods for analyzing the instantaneous shape, position, and velocity of various features of Bloch wave packet envelopes are discussed in Refs.
Churchill 1977b
;
Churchill and Holmstrom 1987
.

In this paper, we identify and explore another key difference between Bloch wave packets and free-particle wave packets: unlike the latter, the former cannot maintain a definite momentum direction. Specifically, we show that Bloch wave packets composed of Bloch waves from a single allowed energy band can never have a definite momentum direction, even instantaneously. In contrast, those involving multiple energy bands may attain a definite direction at certain times but cannot maintain it indefinitely. Then, considering the case of a cosine potential, we examine how its amplitude affects the maximum probability of the particle’s momentum being positive. Overall, our findings suggest that a quantum particle in a one-dimensional periodic potential – such as an electron in a ballistic quantum wire – is not an ideal candidate for experimentally investigating the quantum backflow effect in its conventional formulation. Instead, alternative formulations that relax the strict positive-momentum condition (see, e.g., Refs.
Miller et al. 2021
;
Barbier and Goussev 2021
) may be necessary to explore quantum backflow in mesoscopic systems.

The paper is organized as follows. Section
II
presents an intuitive argument suggesting that a one-dimensional quantum particle in a non-constant potential is unlikely to maintain a definite momentum direction. A more rigorous analysis follows in Sections
III
and
IV
. In particular, Section
III
introduces the concept of a Bloch wave packet and establishes the notation used throughout the paper. Section
IV
examines the probability that a particle in a periodic potential has positive momentum. We demonstrate that when a wave packet consists solely of Bloch waves from the same energy band, this probability remains constant but strictly less than one. However, if the wave packet contains contributions from different energy bands, the probability oscillates over time, with its time average still falling below one. Section
V
explores a specific case in which the periodic potential has a cosinusoidal form. We determine how the least upper bound on the probability of positive momentum depends on the potential amplitude and derive analytical approximations for this dependence in the weak- and strong-potential regimes. Finally, Section
VI
summarizes our findings and discusses their implications in the context of the quantum backflow problem.

II
Preliminary considerations

We begin by presenting some preliminary considerations to clarify what it means for the momentum of a one-dimensional particle to have a definite direction, which, for concreteness, we take to be positive. Using Dirac notation, we denote the particle’s state at time
t=0
by
|\Psi_{0}\rangle
, assuming it is normalized to unity,
\langle\Psi_{0}|\Psi_{0}\rangle=1
. We then introduce the projection operator onto the subspace of positive momenta,

\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{+}=\int_{0}^{\infty}dp\,|p\rangle\langle p|\,,

(1)

where
|p\rangle
represents the eigenstate of the momentum operator corresponding to the eigenvalue
p
. At
t=0
, the particle’s momentum is positive with certainty (and thus has a definite direction) if and only if

\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{+}|\Psi_{0}\rangle=|\Psi_{0}\rangle\,.

(2)

Clearly, any state of the form
|\Psi_{0}\rangle=\int_{0}^{\infty}dp\,\varphi(p)|p\rangle
, where
\varphi(p)
is a complex-valued function, satisfying
\int_{0}^{\infty}dp\,|\varphi(p)|^{2}=1
to ensure normalization, fulfills Eq. (
2
) and represents a positive-momentum particle. It is therefore always possible to construct a quantum state with positive momentum at a given instant.

In order for the particle’s momentum to remain positive (with certainty) throughout its time evolution,
|\Psi_{0}\rangle
has to satisfy

\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{+}\,\hat{\mathcal{U}}(t)|\Psi_{0}\rangle=\hat{\mathcal{U}}(t)|\Psi_{0}\rangle

(3)

for all
t
, where
\hat{\mathcal{U}}(t)
is the evolution operator. Differentiating both sides of the last equation
n
times with respect to
t
, applying the Schrödinger equation
\frac{d}{dt}\hat{\mathcal{U}}(t)=-\frac{i}{\hbar}\hat{\mathcal{H}}\hat{\mathcal{U}}(t)
, where
\hat{\mathcal{H}}
is the system’s Hamiltonian, and evaluating the result at
t=0
, we obtain the following set of conditions:

\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{+}\hat{\mathcal{H}}^{n}|\Psi_{0}\rangle=\hat{\mathcal{H}}^{n}|\Psi_{0}\rangle\,,\quad n\geq 1\,.

(4)

Here, the Hamiltonian
\hat{\mathcal{H}}
is assumed to be time-independent. Then, using Eq. (
2
), we replace
|\Psi_{0}\rangle
in the right-hand side of Eq. (
4
) by
\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{+}|\Psi_{0}\rangle
to obtain

[\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{+},\hat{\mathcal{H}}^{n}]|\Psi_{0}\rangle=0\,,\quad n\geq 1\,,

(5)

where
[\cdot,\cdot]
denotes the commutator.

Any quantum state
|\Psi_{0}\rangle
representing a particle with positive momentum at all times must satisfy Eqs. (
2
) and (
5
). For a free particle, the infinite set of equations (
5
) is trivially fulfilled by any state since the Hamiltonian
\hat{\mathcal{H}}
consists solely of the kinetic term, which commutes with the projection operator
\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{+}
. As a result, any free-particle state that has positive momentum at
t=0
will retain this property throughout its time evolution. However, for a particle in a periodic potential, the commutators
[\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{+},\hat{\mathcal{H}}^{n}]
are generally nonzero. This implies that for
|\Psi_{0}\rangle
to represent a state with positive momentum at all times, it must belong to the intersection of the null spaces of infinitely many operators:
(\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{+}-1)
,
[\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{+},\hat{\mathcal{H}}]
,
[\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{+},\hat{\mathcal{H}}^{2}]
,
[\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{+},\hat{\mathcal{H}}^{3}]
,
\ldots
. Since these operators are generally distinct, it is reasonable to expect that their null spaces have an empty intersection, implying that the desired positive-momentum state
|\Psi_{0}\rangle
does not exist.

This simple argument provides an intuitive basis for expecting the non-existence of states with a definite momentum direction in a general periodic potential, but it does not constitute a proof. More importantly, it offers no insight into how close the probability of finding the particle with positive momentum can be to one. In the following sections, we undertake a detailed analysis of these questions.

III
Bloch wave packets

To set the stage for our study, we first define the concept of a Bloch wave packet and establish the notation used throughout the paper.

We consider a quantum particle of mass
\mu
in a one-dimensional periodic potential
V(x)
with period
L
, i.e.
V(x+L)=V(x)
. Throughout the paper, we assume that the periodic potential has a finite – though possibly very large – number of nonzero Fourier coefficients. More concretely, we take

V(x)=\sum_{n=-N}^{N}V_{n}e^{inqx}\,,

(6)

where

q=\frac{2\pi}{L}

(7)

is the period of the corresponding reciprocal lattice. Here, the Fourier coefficients
V_{n}
satisfy
V_{-n}=V_{n}^{*}
to ensure that
V(x)
is a real-valued function, and
N
is such that
V_{-N}=V_{N}^{*}\not=0
. The form given by Eq. (
6
) is quite general and encompasses a wide range of physically meaningful periodic potentials.

The time-independent Schrödinger equation, that determines stationary states
F^{(j)}(x,\kappa)
and energies
E^{(j)}(\kappa)
of the particle, reads

\left(-\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2\mu}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x^{2}}+V(x)\right)F^{(j)}(x,\kappa)=E^{(j)}(\kappa)F^{(j)}(x,\kappa)\,.

(8)

According to the Bloch theorem
Ashcroft and Mermin 1976
;
Kittel 2004
;
Cottey 1971
, the stationary states – known as (Floquet-)Bloch waves – can be expressed as the product of a plane wave
e^{i\kappa x}
and an
L
-periodic function of
x
, i.e.

F^{(j)}(x,\kappa)=e^{i\kappa x}\sum_{n=-\infty}^{+\infty}f_{n}^{(j)}(\kappa)e^{inqx}\,.

(9)

Here,
f_{n}^{(j)}(\kappa)
are the Fourier expansion coefficients of the periodic part of the Bloch wave, while
\hbar\kappa
represents the quasi-momentum, which, without loss of generality, can be restricted to the first Brillouin zone,
-q/2<\kappa\leq q/2
. The superscript
j=0,1,2,\ldots
labels the allowed energy bands in order of increasing energy.

The substitution of Eq. (
9
) into Eq. (
8
) gives rise to the following eigenproblem:

\sum_{m=-\infty}^{+\infty}\left(\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2\mu}(\kappa+nq)^{2}\delta_{m,n}+V_{n-m}\right)f_{m}^{(j)}(\kappa)\\
=E^{(j)}(\kappa)f_{n}^{(j)}(\kappa)\,.

(10)

Solving this eigenproblem for every quasi-momentum
\hbar\kappa
on the interval
-q/2<\kappa\leq q/2
, one obtains the eigenvalues
E^{(0)}(\kappa)<E^{(1)}(\kappa)<\ldots

IV
Momentum positivity

We now turn to the question of momentum positivity for Bloch wave packets. Do there exist particle states in which a momentum measurement, performed at any instant, is guaranteed to yield a positive value? If such states existed – analogous to those in free space – they would correspond to particles with a well-defined momentum direction. However, as we show below, no Bloch wave packet can sustain a definite momentum direction throughout its propagation in periodic potentials with non-overlapping allowed energy bands.

We start by expanding the Bloch wave packet
\Psi
, given by Eq. (
13
), in terms of plane waves
\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{ikx}
, which are the eigenstates of the momentum operator
p=-i\hbar\frac{\partial}{\partial x}
,

\Psi(x,t)=\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}dk\,\varphi(k,t)\frac{e^{ikx}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\,.

(15)

Here,
\varphi(k,t)
plays the role of a time-dependent momentum wave function and is given by the inverse Fourier transform of
\Psi(x,t)
:

\varphi(k,t)=\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}dx\,\Psi(x,t)\frac{e^{-ikx}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\,.

(16)

Substituting Eq. (
13
) into the last expression and calculating the integral over
x
, we find (see Appendix
B
)

\varphi(k,t)=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\varphi^{(j)}(k,t)\,,

(17)

where

\varphi^{(j)}(k,t)=\sqrt{2\pi}\phi^{(j)}(k-nq)f_{n}^{(j)}(k-nq)\\
\times\left.e^{-iE^{(j)}(k-nq)t/\hbar}\right|_{n=[k/q]}

(18)

is the momentum wave function contribution from the
j^{\text{th}}
allowed energy band, and
[\cdot]
denotes rounding to the nearest integer.

If a momentum measurement is performed at time
t
on a Bloch wave packet in the state
\Psi(x,t)
, the outcome
\hbar k
is obtained with probability density
|\varphi(k,t)|^{2}
. Therefore, the probability that the particle’s momentum is positive at time
t
is given by

P_{+}(t)=\int_{0}^{\infty}dk\,|\varphi(k,t)|^{2}\,.

(19)

In what follows, we show that no Bloch wave packets satisfy
P_{+}(t)=1
for all
t
, implying that a particle in a periodic potential cannot have a definite momentum direction at all times. Our argument proceeds in two stages: first, for wave packets composed of Bloch waves from a single allowed energy band, and then for those occupying multiple bands.

IV.1
Single-band wave packets

We first consider wave packets that are superpositions of Bloch waves from the same allowed energy band. That is, we take

\phi^{(j)}(\kappa)=\phi(\kappa)\delta_{j,J}

(20)

for some fixed
J
. The function
\phi(\kappa)
is the quasi-momentum amplitude in the
J^{\text{th}}
energy band and satisfies the normalization condition [c.f. Eq. (
14
)]

\int_{-q/2}^{q/2}d\kappa\,|\phi(\kappa)|^{2}=1\,.

(21)

It follows from Eqs. (
17
) and (
18
) that

|\varphi(k,t)|^{2}=\left.2\pi\left|\phi(k-nq)f_{n}^{(J)}(k-nq)\right|^{2}\right|_{n=[k/q]}\,.

(22)

Then, substituting the last expression into Eq. (
19
) and carrying out straightforward manipulations (see Appendix
C
for details), we obtain

P_{+}=\int_{-q/2}^{q/2}d\kappa\,\Lambda(\kappa)|\phi(\kappa)|^{2}

(23)

with

\Lambda(\kappa)=2\pi\left|f_{0}^{(J)}(\kappa)\right|^{2}\Theta(\kappa)+2\pi\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\left|f_{n}^{(J)}(\kappa)\right|^{2}\,,

(24)

where
\Theta(\cdot)
is the Heaviside step function.

Let us make the following observations. First,
P_{+}
is independent of time, meaning that the probability of the particle’s momentum being positive remains constant as it moves through the periodic potential. This situation is fully analogous to the free-particle scenario. Second, by comparing Eq. (
24
) with Eq. (
11
), we observe that
0\leq\Lambda(\kappa)\leq 1
for all
\kappa
. Combined with the normalization condition (
21
), this implies that
0\leq P_{+}\leq 1
.

We now take this a step further and show that

\Lambda(\kappa)<1

(25)

for all
\kappa
. We prove this statement by contradiction. To this end, we assume that there exists an eigenvector
\{f_{n}^{(J)}(\kappa)\}_{n=-\infty}^{+\infty}
, satisfying Eq. (
10
) for
j=J
and some fixed
\kappa
, such that
\Lambda(\kappa)=1
, which, in view of Eq. (
24
), is equivalent to

\left|f_{0}^{(J)}(\kappa)\right|^{2}\Theta(\kappa)+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\left|f_{n}^{(J)}(\kappa)\right|^{2}=\frac{1}{2\pi}\,.

(26)

Additionally, the eigenvector has to satisfy the normalization condition (
11
). Conditions (
11
) and (
26
) can be simultaneously fulfilled only if

f_{n}^{(J)}(\kappa)=0\quad\text{for}\quad n\leq-1\,.

(27)

In view of Eq. (
27
), the eigenproblem (
10
) reduces to

\sum_{m=0}^{N-1}V_{n-m}f_{m}^{(J)}(\kappa)=0\quad\text{for}\quad-N\leq n\leq-1

(28)

and

\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\Bigg[\left(\frac{\hbar^{2}(\kappa+nq)^{2}}{2\mu}-E^{(J)}(\kappa)\right)\delta_{m,n}\\
+V_{n-m}\Bigg]f_{m}^{(J)}(\kappa)=0\quad\text{for}\quad n\geq 0\,.

(29)

It follows from Eq. (
28
) that

f_{n}^{(J)}(\kappa)=0\quad\text{for}\quad 0\leq n\leq N-1\,.

(30)

Subsequently, from Eqs. (
29
) and (
30
), we get

f_{n}^{(J)}(\kappa)=0\quad\text{for}\quad n\geq N\,.

(31)

(Details of the calculations leading to Eqs. (
30
) and (
31
) are presented in Appendix
D
.) Combining Eqs. (
27
), (
30
), and (
31
), we find that
f_{n}^{(J)}(\kappa)=0
for all
n
, violating both the normalization condition (
11
) and assumption (
26
). Hence, we arrive at a contradiction, proving that
\Lambda(\kappa)
cannot equal unity and thus establishing that
\Lambda(\kappa)<1
for all
\kappa
.

It then follows from Eqs. (
23
) and (
25
) that
P_{+}<\int_{-q/2}^{q/2}d\kappa\,|\phi(\kappa)|^{2}
. Subsequently, using the normalization condition, Eq. (
21
), we arrive at

P_{+}<1\,.

(32)

This result implies that a single-band Bloch wave packet propagating through a periodic potential with a finite number of Fourier coefficients cannot have a well-defined momentum direction even instantaneously.

IV.2
Multiple-band wave packets

We now consider normalized superpositions of Bloch waves from more than one allowed energy band. The substitution of Eqs. (
17
) and (
18
) into Eq. (
19
) yields

P_{+}(t)=\overline{P}+\widetilde{P}(t)\,,

(33)

with

\overline{P}=\sum_{j}\int_{0}^{\infty}dk\,\left|\varphi^{(j)}(k,t)\right|^{2}

(34)

and

\widetilde{P}(t)=\sum_{j}\sum_{j^{\prime}\not=j}\int_{0}^{\infty}dk\,\varphi^{(j)}(k,t)^{*}\varphi^{(j^{\prime})}(k,t)\,.

(35)

In the rest of this section, we do not explicitly state the summation limits to keep the notation uncluttered; however, they are clear, as both indices run from zero to infinity, with
j^{\prime}
skipping
j
.

The first term,
\overline{P}
, is a sum of (time-independent) single-band contributions of the type investigated in Sec.
IV.1
, each of which satisfies
\int_{0}^{\infty}dk\,\left|\varphi^{(j)}(k,t)\right|^{2}<\int_{-q/2}^{q/2}d\kappa\,\left|\phi^{(j)}(\kappa)\right|^{2}
. Therefore, taking into account the normalization condition (
14
), we conclude that
\overline{P}<1
.

The second term,
\widetilde{P}(t)
, is an interference term involving Bloch waves from different energy bands. Substituting Eq. (
18
) into Eq. (
35
) and following steps similar to those in Appendix
C
, we obtain

\widetilde{P}(t)=\sum_{j}\sum_{j^{\prime}\not=j}\int_{-q/2}^{q/2}d\kappa\,\Lambda^{(j,j^{\prime})}(\kappa)\phi^{(j)}(\kappa)^{*}\phi^{(j^{\prime})}(\kappa)\\
\times\exp\left\{\frac{i}{\hbar}\left[E^{(j)}(\kappa)-E^{(j^{\prime})}(\kappa)\right]t\right\}

(36)

with

\Lambda^{(j,j^{\prime})}(\kappa)=2\pi f_{0}^{(j)}(\kappa)^{*}f_{0}^{(j^{\prime})}(\kappa)\Theta(\kappa)\\
+2\pi\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}f_{n}^{(j)}(\kappa)^{*}f_{n}^{(j^{\prime})}(\kappa)\,.

(37)

Since
\Lambda^{(j,j^{\prime})}(\kappa)^{*}=\Lambda^{(j^{\prime},j)}(\kappa)
, the function
\widetilde{P}(t)
is real-valued. Assuming, as before, that there is no overlap between allowed energy bands, we observe that
\widetilde{P}(t)
is composed of sine and cosine functions of time with nonzero frequencies. Consequently, its time average is zero, implying that there are moments at which the function is non-positive:
\widetilde{P}(t)\leq 0
for some
t
.

Finally, from Eq. (
33
) and the fact that
\overline{P}<0
and
\widetilde{P}(t)\leq 0
for some
t
, we conclude that

P_{+}(t)<1\quad\text{for some }t\,.

(38)

This result implies that no multiple-band Bloch wave packet can maintain a definite momentum direction at all times. While the probability of positive momentum may reach unity at certain instants, it must inevitably decrease, remaining below one on average.

V
Cosine potential

To examine how large
P_{+}
can be depending on the details of the periodic potential, we analyze an example scenario where the potential is given by a cosine function,

V(x)=A\cos(qx)\,,

A>0

\Psi

J=0

\sum_{m=-\infty}^{+\infty}\left[(z+n)^{2}\delta_{m,n}+\alpha\delta_{|n-m|,1}\right]f_{m}^{(0)}(z)\\

VI
Discussion and conclusion

We examined the momentum of a one-dimensional quantum particle propagating through a periodic potential and investigated the probability
P_{+}
of it being positive. We demonstrated that
P_{+}
can never remain equal to one at all times, implying that the particle cannot propagate through the potential while maintaining a definite momentum direction. When the particle’s state is a superposition of Bloch waves from a single allowed energy band,
P_{+}
remains constant over time and is strictly less than one. However, if the state includes contributions from multiple energy bands,
P_{+}
oscillates in time (around an average value smaller than one) and can, in principle, reach one at certain instants.

Our analysis is based on three key assumptions. First, the periodic potential is assumed to have a finite (though possibly very large) number of nonzero Fourier components, as given in Eq. (
6
). Second, the allowed energy bands are assumed to be non-overlapping. Third, we consider only Bloch wave packets that do not include contributions from stationary states at the Brillouin zone boundary. We cannot rule out the possibility that relaxing some (or all) of these assumptions could allow for Bloch wave packets with a definite momentum direction.

In general, the maximal value of
P_{+}
depends on the specific form of the periodic potential and the energy bands accessible to the particle’s wave packet. We investigated this dependence for a wave packet constrained to the lowest energy band of a cosine-shaped potential. In this scenario, we computed the least upper bound on
P_{+}
as a function of the (dimensionless) potential amplitude
\alpha
and found that
\sup P_{+}
decreases monotonically with increasing
\alpha
, ultimately saturating at
1/2
in the limit of an infinitely strong potential. In the weak-amplitude regime, the decay is quadratic [Eq. (
46
)], whereas in the strong-amplitude regime, it follows an algebraic decay with an exponent of
-1/4
[Eq. (
45
)].

Our analysis of the cosine potential scenario also reveals that the wave packets maximizing
P_{+}
are those composed of Bloch waves with small positive quasi-momenta – indeed, the function
\Lambda(\kappa)
in Fig.
1
attains its supremum as
\kappa\to 0^{+}
. In this quasi-momentum region, the energy dispersion relation is approximately parabolic, resembling that of a free particle. This reinforces the well-known principle that, for a lowest-energy-band wave packet to propagate in a free-particle-like manner, it should be formed near the bottom of the band. However, it is crucial to emphasize that, regardless of how close a Bloch wave packet is to the bottom of the band, it will never possess a definite momentum direction – unlike a free-particle wave packet, which can.

The findings of this paper provide valuable guidance for future explorations of the quantum backflow effect in mesoscopic systems. In this regard, two main conclusions emerge from our study. First, since the maximal probability of the particle’s momentum being positive decreases as the amplitude of the periodic potential increases, systems with shallow potentials should be favored to enable wave packets with an
almost
definite momentum direction. Second, to investigate signatures of quantum backflow in solid-state systems, the theoretical framework of the phenomenon should be extended to accommodate quantum wave packets with negative momentum components.

Appendix A
Derivation of Eq. (
12
)

This section provides a derivation of the normalization condition for Bloch waves, given by Eq. (
12
). Starting from Eq. (
9
), we write

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}

\displaystyle dx\,F^{(j)}(x,\kappa)^{*}F^{(j^{\prime})}(x,\kappa^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}dx\,\left(e^{-i\kappa x}\sum_{m=-\infty}^{+\infty}f_{m}^{(j)}(\kappa)^{*}e^{-imqx}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\times\left(e^{i\kappa^{\prime}x}\sum_{n=-\infty}^{+\infty}f_{n}^{(j^{\prime})}(\kappa^{\prime})e^{inqx}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{m,n}f_{m}^{(j)}(\kappa)^{*}f_{n}^{(j^{\prime})}(\kappa^{\prime})\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}dx\,e^{i[\kappa^{\prime}-\kappa+(n-m)q]x}

\displaystyle=2\pi\sum_{m,n}f_{m}^{(j)}(\kappa)^{*}f_{n}^{(j^{\prime})}(\kappa^{\prime})\delta\big(\kappa-\kappa^{\prime}+(m-n)q\big)

\displaystyle=2\pi\sum_{n=-\infty}^{+\infty}\sum_{s=-\infty}^{+\infty}f_{n+s}^{(j)}(\kappa)^{*}f_{n}^{(j^{\prime})}(\kappa^{\prime})\delta(\kappa-\kappa^{\prime}+sq)\,.

Since both
\kappa
and
\kappa^{\prime}
are restricted to the first Brillouin zone,
\kappa,\kappa^{\prime}\in(-q/2,q/2]
, their separation is smaller than the reciprocal lattice period,
|\kappa-\kappa^{\prime}|<q
. Therefore, the delta-function
\delta(\kappa-\kappa^{\prime}+sq)
vanishes for all
s\not=0
, yielding

\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}dx\,F^{(j)}(x,\kappa)^{*}F^{(j^{\prime})}(x,\kappa^{\prime})\\
=2\pi\delta(\kappa-\kappa^{\prime})\sum_{n=-\infty}^{+\infty}f_{n}^{(j)}(\kappa)^{*}f_{n}^{(j^{\prime})}(\kappa)\,.

For the case when
j^{\prime}\not=j
, the eigenvectors
\{f_{n}^{(j)}(\kappa)\}_{n=-\infty}^{+\infty}
and
\{f_{n}^{(j^{\prime})}(\kappa)\}_{n=-\infty}^{+\infty}
correspond to different energy bands (and so to distinct eigenvalues,
E^{(j)}(\kappa)
and
E^{(j^{\prime})}(\kappa)
, respectively), which implies their orthogonality. Hence,

\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}dx\,F^{(j)}(x,\kappa)^{*}F^{(j^{\prime})}(x,\kappa^{\prime})\\
=2\pi\delta(\kappa-\kappa^{\prime})\delta_{j,j^{\prime}}\sum_{n=-\infty}^{+\infty}\left|f_{n}^{(j)}(\kappa)\right|^{2}\,.

Finally, making use of the normalization condition (
11
), we arrive at the desired result, given by Eq. (
12
).

Appendix E
Derivation of Eq. (
45
)

Here, we derive the large-
\alpha
asymptotic approximation for
\sup P_{+}
, as given by Eq. (
45
). We begin by noticing that the zero-quasi-momentum Bloch wave
F^{(0)}(x,0)
is an
L
-periodic function [see Eq. (
9
)], which, over the one-period interval
0<x<L
, can be approximated by ground state of the harmonic expansion of
V(x)
around
x=L/2
. Thus, using, in the vicinity of
x=L/2
, the quadratic expansion

\displaystyle V(x)

\displaystyle\simeq V(\tfrac{L}{2})+\frac{V^{\prime\prime}(\tfrac{L}{2})}{2}\left(x-\tfrac{L}{2}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=-A+\frac{\mu\omega^{2}}{2}\left(x-\tfrac{L}{2}\right)^{2}

with

\omega=\sqrt{\frac{Aq^{2}}{\mu}}=\frac{\hbar q^{2}}{\mu}\sqrt{\alpha}\,,

we write, for
0<x<L
, that

\displaystyle F^{(0)}(x,0)

\displaystyle\simeq C\exp\left[-\frac{\mu\omega}{2\hbar}\left(x-\frac{L}{2}\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=C\exp\left[-\frac{\sqrt{\alpha}}{2}q^{2}\left(x-\tfrac{L}{2}\right)^{2}\right]\,,

where
C>0
is a normalization constant. The latter is determined from the normalization condition

\int_{0}^{L}dx\,\left|F^{(0)}(x,0)\right|^{2}=\frac{1}{q}\,.

The validity of this normalization condition follows straightforwardly from Eqs. (
9
) and (
11
):

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{L}dx\,

\displaystyle\left|F^{(0)}(x,0)\right|^{2}=\int_{0}^{L}dx\,\sum_{m=-\infty}^{+\infty}f_{m}^{(0)}(0)^{*}e^{-imqx}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\times\sum_{n=-\infty}^{+\infty}f_{n}^{(0)}(0)e^{inqx}

\displaystyle=\sum_{m,n}f_{m}^{(0)}(0)^{*}f_{n}^{(0)}(0)\int_{0}^{L}dx\,e^{i(n-m)qx}

\displaystyle=\sum_{m,n}f_{m}^{(0)}(0)^{*}f_{n}^{(0)}(0)L\delta_{mn}

\displaystyle=L\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}\left|f_{n}^{(0)}(0)\right|^{2}=L\frac{1}{2\pi}=\frac{1}{q}\,.

Hence,

C^{2}\int_{0}^{L}dx\,\exp\left[-\sqrt{\alpha}q^{2}\left(x-\frac{L}{2}\right)^{2}\right]=\frac{1}{q}\,.

For large
\alpha
, we extend the integration interval to the entire line, thus obtaining
C^{2}\sqrt{\pi/(\sqrt{\alpha}q^{2})}\simeq 1/q
, or

C\simeq\frac{\alpha^{1/8}}{\pi^{1/4}}\,.

Therefore, we arrive at the following (normalized) large-
\alpha
approximation for the lowest-allowed-band zero-quasi-momentum Bloch wave on the interval
0<x<L
:

F^{(0)}(x,0)\simeq\frac{\alpha^{1/8}}{\pi^{1/4}}\exp\left[-\frac{\sqrt{\alpha}}{2}q^{2}\left(x-\frac{L}{2}\right)^{2}\right]\,.

We now calculate the
n=0
Fourier coefficient of
F^{(0)}(x,0)
[see Eq. (
9
)]:

\displaystyle f_{0}^{(0)}(0)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{L}\int_{0}^{L}dx\,F^{(0)}(x,0)

\displaystyle\simeq\frac{\alpha^{1/8}}{\pi^{1/4}}\frac{1}{L}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}dx\,\exp\left[-\frac{\sqrt{\alpha}}{2}q^{2}\left(x-\frac{L}{2}\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha^{1/8}}{\pi^{1/4}}\frac{1}{L}\sqrt{\frac{2\pi}{\sqrt{\alpha}q^{2}}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}\pi^{3/4}}\alpha^{-1/8}\,.

Finally, substituting this expression for
f_{0}^{(0)}(0)
into Eq. (
44
), we obtain the sought result, Eq. (
45
).

Appendix F
Derivation of Eq. (
46
)

Below we derive Eq. (
46
), approximating
\sup P_{+}
in the weak-amplitude limit. Our strategy is to find a small-
\alpha
perturbative expression for the Fourier coefficient
f_{0}^{(0)}(0)
and then use Eq. (
44
) to obtain
\sup P_{+}
.

For convenience, we carry out the calculation using scaled Fourier coefficients

g_{n}^{(j)}\equiv\sqrt{2\pi}f_{n}^{(j)}(0)\,,

in terms of which the normalization condition (
11
) reads
\sum_{n=-\infty}^{+\infty}\left|g_{n}^{(j)}\right|^{2}=1
. Since we assume throughout this paper that there is no overlap between different allowed energy bands, the vectors
g^{(j)}
and
g^{(j^{\prime})}
with
j^{\prime}\not=j
are mutually orthogonal. Combined with the normalization condition, this leads to the orthonormality condition

\sum_{n=-\infty}^{+\infty}\left[g_{n}^{(j)}\right]^{*}g_{n}^{(j^{\prime})}=\delta_{j,j^{\prime}}\,.

Written for vectors
g^{(j)}
, the eigenproblem (
10
) takes the form

(H+U)g^{(j)}=\varepsilon^{(j)}g^{(j)}\,,

where
H
is the unperturbed (diagonal) matrix with elements

H_{mn}=n^{2}\delta_{mn}\,,

U
is the perturbation matrix with elements

U_{mn}=\alpha\delta_{|m-n|,1}\,,

and
\varepsilon^{(j)}=2\mu E^{(j)}(0)/(\hbar q)^{2}
is the corresponding dimensionless eigenvalue. To the second order in the perturbation parameter
\alpha
, we write

g^{(j)}\simeq g^{(j)}|_{0}+g^{(j)}|_{1}+g^{(j)}|_{2}\,.

where the unperturbed eigenvector
g^{(j)}|_{0}
has elements
g^{(j)}_{n}|_{0}=\delta_{j,n}
. The first and second order corrections,
g^{(j)}|_{1}
and
g^{(j)}|_{2}
respectively, are given by
Landau and Lifshitz 1981

g^{(j)}|_{1}=\sum_{m\not=j}\frac{U_{mj}}{j^{2}-m^{2}}g^{(m)}|_{0}

and

g^{(j)}|_{2}=\sum_{m\not=j}\sum_{n\not=j}\frac{U_{mn}U_{nj}}{(j^{2}-m^{2})(j^{2}-n^{2})}g^{(m)}|_{0}-\sum_{m\not=j}\frac{U_{jj}U_{mj}}{(j^{2}-m^{2})^{2}}g^{(m)}|_{0}-\frac{g^{(j)}|_{0}}{2}\sum_{m\not=j}\frac{|U_{mj}|^{2}}{(j^{2}-m^{2})^{2}}\,.

Evaluating these expressions for
j=0
, we get

g_{0}^{(0)}|_{1}=\sum_{m\not=0}\frac{U_{m0}}{-m^{2}}g_{0}^{(m)}|_{0}=\sum_{m\not=0}\frac{\alpha\delta_{|m|,1}}{-m^{2}}\delta_{m0}=0

and

\displaystyle g_{0}^{(0)}|_{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{m\not=0}\sum_{n\not=0}\frac{U_{mn}U_{n0}}{(-m^{2})(-n^{2})}g_{0}^{(m)}|_{0}-\sum_{m\not=0}\frac{U_{00}U_{m0}}{(-m^{2})^{2}}g_{0}^{(m)}|_{0}-\frac{g_{0}^{(0)}|_{0}}{2}\sum_{m\not=0}\frac{|U_{m0}|^{2}}{(-m^{2})^{2}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{m\not=0}\sum_{n\not=0}\frac{\alpha\delta_{|m-n|,1}\alpha\delta_{|n|,1}}{(-m^{2})(-n^{2})}\delta_{m0}-\sum_{m\not=0}\frac{\alpha\delta_{0,1}\alpha\delta_{|m|,1}}{(-m^{2})^{2}}\delta_{m0}-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{m\not=0}\frac{\left(\alpha\delta_{|m|,1}\right)^{2}}{(-m^{2})^{2}}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{m\not=0}\frac{\left(\alpha\delta_{|m|,1}\right)^{2}}{(-m^{2})^{2}}=-\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\alpha^{2}}{(-1^{2})^{2}}+\frac{\alpha^{2}}{(-(-1)^{2})^{2}}\right)=-\alpha^{2}\,.

Therefore, up to the terms of order
\alpha^{2}
, we have
g_{0}^{(0)}\simeq 1-\alpha^{2}
, or, equivalently,

f_{0}^{(0)}(0)\simeq\frac{1-\alpha^{2}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\,.

Finally, substituting this expression into Eq. (
44
) and discarding terms of order higher than
\alpha^{2}
, we obtain the sought result.
