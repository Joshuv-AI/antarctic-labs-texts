Symptoms of acute stress, anxiety, and depression following Türkiye-Syria earthquake among Syrian population: national survey during the acute phase
Authors: Mhd Homam Safiah, Khaled Kalalib Al Ashabi, Mhd Obai Alchallah, Hani Alolabi, Sham Ataya, Youssef Latifeh
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground: On 6 February 2023, an earthquake with a 7.8 magnitude wreaked destruction in northern Syria to add a new emergency status in the country. This study aims to evaluate the mental health status of the Syrian population in the immediate phase of the earthquake.Methods: This nationwide cross-sectional study was conducted in Syria from 17 February to 6 April. Data were collected on socio-demographics, trauma exposure characteristics, and levels of acute stress, depression, and anxiety. The NSESSS Checklist for the APA was used to assess ASD symptoms, PHQ-9 for depression, and GAD-7 for anxiety. Linear mixed-effects models were employed to identify predictive factors while accounting for governorate as a random effect and time elapsed as a fixed factor.Results: The rate of probable ASD symptoms was significant, with 55.3% of participants exhibiting these symptoms, including 10.3% reporting severe-to-extreme levels of severity. Similarly, 71.6% of participants exhibited symptoms of depression and 59.1% showed symptoms of anxiety. Furthermore, 40.4% of participants displayed a co-occurrence of ASD along with anxiety and depression. Linear mixed effects models indicated that symptoms were predicted by female sex, displacement, poor economic status, and a positive mental health history time elapsed, physical trauma, belief in the ongoing risk of earthquakes, feelings of dizziness, recurrent night-time fear arousal, and a continued sensation of seismic activity.Conclusion: This study demonstrated the high rates of symptoms of ASD, anxiety, and depression across the Syrian population during the immediate aftermath of the earthquake. High portions of people may acquire co-occurring symptoms represented by different mental health problems. These findings highlight the need for immediate actions to enhance mental health support in Syria.

Introduction

A devastating earthquake with a magnitude of 7.7 struck the Turkish-Syrian area on February 6th, resulting in thousands of injuries and deaths. The earthquake’s epicenter was located approximately 60 kilometres north of Aleppo in northwestern Syria. This was followed by several powerful aftershocks, including a 7.5-magnitude quake that hit the same area just hours later. The earthquake affected large swaths of Syria, with the most severe impacts reported in the governorates of Idlib, Aleppo, Latakia, and Hama (Syrian Arab News Agency (SANA), 2023). The United Nations Office for the Coordination of Humanitarian Affairs (OCHA) stated that the collective death toll in Syria is close to 7259, and many thousands were displaced from their homes. In Syria, where the populace already faces one of the most complex humanitarian circumstances, this traumatic event further damaged already compromised vital supplies (Syria, 2023). Natural disasters have a massive impact on humans’ wellness and put all aspects of life in threatening situations. Mental health is a critical element to discuss on multiple levels, especially for individuals directly affected by such events. Stress disorders, depression, and anxiety are the most commonly studied mental illnesses after natural disasters (Makwana, 2019).

Acute Stress Disorder (ASD) is a short-term unpleasant mental health condition that occurs during the first month after experiencing a traumatic event. In contrast, stress reactions that continue for an extended period of more than four weeks meet the criteria for post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) (American Psychiatric A, 2013; Riggs & Foa, 2004). Pretty much like PTSD, ASD impairs patients’ mental health with multiple psychological symptoms such as flashbacks of the traumatic event, irritability or anger outbursts, nightmares or intrusive memories, avoidance of event reminders, difficulty remembering the accident, sleep disturbances, and difficulty concentrating. Despite their significant similarities, dissociative symptoms can serve as a distinguishing marker between ASD and PTSD. Specifically, one symptom from four symptom clusters – reexperiencing, avoidance, and arousal – is characteristic of ASD (Riggs & Foa, 2004). The frequency rate of ASD is highly changeable due to several factors, one of which is the type of the trauma, and the time screening onset after the event, accounting for 40% after two weeks of the event in some reports (Fanai & Khan, 2023). Acute stress can lead to various other psychological problems. Patients with ASD are more prone to develop PTSD, depression, and anxiety. In addition, the suicide attempt risk is 24 times higher in comparison with the absence of ASD (American Psychiatric A, 2013).

Depression and anxiety are one of the most common mental health issues linked with tragic events. Both have a higher prevalence among traumatic individuals and commonly co-occur with stress, by 48% to 55% for depression and nearly 50% for anxiety (Lee et al., 2020). Furthermore, depression and anxiety can be a long-term issue following tragedies, particularly for those who were more exposed to the event. Experiences related to the earthquake was associated with depression among survivors 37 years of the Tangshan earthquake (Gao et al., 2019). In addition, more than three-fourths exhibited symptoms of an anxiety disorder following the Pakistani Earthquake (Hashmi et al., 2011).

Numerous factors contribute to developing mental disturbance after a natural disaster. Besides the effect of traumatic events, contextual factors also play a role such as younger age, the female sex, or low economic status. Those factors are categorized under the term ‘Pre-disaster risk factors’. Peri-disaster risk factors, on the other hand, relate to the characteristics of the event itself including the type of disaster, being affected directly like being close to the event site and witnessing tragic events, or indirectly like following up on news on media, and the exposure severity. Moreover, increased exposure is a reliable and significant indicator of psychological morbidity. Life stressors along with a lack of social support are prominent post-disaster risk factors for developing mental distress (Goldmann & Galea, 2014).

The last earthquake struck a country where mental health difficulties have been well established due to cumulative crises in more than a decade. Furthermore, controlling mental stability and providing psychological first aid is challenging, especially in the affected areas (Albaba, 2023). This study aimed to assess the psychological impact of the earthquake in the immediate aftermath of the event, highlighting symptoms of acute stress syndrome (ASD), as well as anxiety and depressive symptoms. In addition, we aimed to provide a more comprehensive and rigorous examination of the predictors of ASD, depression, and anxiety symptoms in the Syrian population following the earthquake. This involved simultaneously assessing the influence of individual-level characteristics as well as contextual factors related to geographic location and temporal proximity to the traumatic event.

Methods

Study design, setting, and participants

A cross-sectional survey was conducted in all major cities in Syria (Damascus, Rural Damascus, Aleppo, Daraa, Deir ez-Zor, Hama, AlHasakah, Qamishli, Homs, Idlib, Latakia, Quneitra, ArRaqqah, As-Suwayda, Tartous) collected during the month right after the earthquake, from 17 February to 6 April. The sample size was calculated using Epi Info™ software. According to data from the United Nations (UN), the estimated Syrian population in 2023 is about 23 million (World Population Prospects). Based on this data, we conducted a statistical power analysis to calculate the required sample size. With a conservative assumption of 50% with disturbed mental health flowing the disaster, an alpha of 0.05, a power of 80%, and a desired margin of error of 1.5%, the desired sample size was found to be 4268.

A nationally representative sample of Syrian people was questioned using a structured self-administered questionnaire. The questionnaire was developed based on previously existing literature, and then underwent modifications to make it suitable for the Syrian community. Inclusion criteria to participate included that the person is: (1) 18 years old or above, (3) Syrian (or equivalent) and living in Syria at the time of the event, and (4) willing to complete the whole questionnaire. Illiterate people were asked through interviews with the research team. The participants were informed that their participation was voluntary, all of their responses were recorded anonymously, responses to all questions were not mandatory, and they were allowed to withdraw from participation at any time. Informed consent was obtained from every participant before participation. The questionnaire was piloted on 30 persons to ensure its clarity and adjustments were made based on their feedback, they were excluded from the final sample to prevent bias. Many residents lack internet connection, either because the required infrastructure has been destroyed or because of the country’s dire financial situation. Therefore, we distributed the questionnaire using two methods to ensure the proper diversity in the sample and to prevent bias. One through Google Form on social media platforms (Facebook, WhatsApp, and Telegram), and the other through personal distribution, in which the questionnaire was handed to participants, patients or their companions, and workers in public hospitals and accommodation centres. In the online distribution method, chain-referral sampling was used, and respondents were requested to forward the questionnaire to their friends, family, and co-workers. In the method of paper form distribution, convenience sampling techniques were used.

Study instruments

The questionnaire used in this study is displayed in Appendix 1. The questionnaire consisted of 43 questions divided into four sections: Socio-demographic characteristics: contained 10 questions about age, sex, governorate, residence, if displaced or not, marital status, economic status, educational level, type of property, and history of mental health problem. Exposure characteristics: 10 questions related to the event: whether one has felt the earthquake or not, whether injured or not, if close associate’s was injured or died [2 questions], whether transferred to a shelter house or not, witnessing the event, keen to follow the events, fear and believes about earthquake recurrence [3 questions]. Acute Stress Disorder (ASD) symptoms were assessed using the National Stressful Events Survey Acute Stress Disorder Short Scale (NSESSS) which is provided by the American Psychiatric Association (APA). NSESSS assesses the severity of symptoms of ASD in individuals aged 18 and older following a highly stressful event or experience, this measure meets the diagnosis criteria of DSM-V for ASD. The severity of the individual’s acute stress disorder is classified in terms of none (0), mild (1), moderate (2), severe (3), or extreme (4) based on the scoring outcome (Kilpatrick & Friedman, 2013). Anxiety symptoms were assessed using The Arabic Version of the Generalized Anxiety Disorder 7-item (GAD-7), which is a practical, self-rated scale, and well-designed tool to screen for different types of common anxiety disorders. Items on the GAD-7 were scored over a 4-point Likert scale: 0 = not at all, 1 = several days, 2 = more than half the days, and 3 = nearly every day, providing a 0–21 severity score range. a cut-off score of 0–4 indicates no to minimal anxiety symptoms (Löwe et al., 2008), Mohsen et al., 2022). Depressive symptoms were assessed using The Arabic Patient Health Questionnaire 9-item (PHQ-9), items on the PHQ-9 were scored over a 4-point Likert scale: 0 = not at all, 1 = several days, 2 = more than half the days, and 3 = nearly every day, providing a 0–27 severity score range. A cut-off score of 0–4 indicates no to minimal depressive symptoms (Mohsen et al., 2022).

Reliability analysis

To the best of our knowledge, no Arabic validation for the NSESSS scale is available. The NSESSS scale was translated to Arabic (the local Language) and re-translated into English to check consistency in the meaning of words and concepts. Cronbach’s alpha test was applied to determine the internal consistency of the questionnaire, the value of the Arabic questionnaire was 86.9% indicating good internal consistency (Ursachi et al., 2015). Both GAD-7 and PHQ-9 were previously validated in the Arabic language (Mohsen et al., 2022). Furthermore, based on the results of this study, Cronbach’s alpha values were 92.2% and 89.3% for GAD-7 and PHQ-9 respectively. The Cronbach’s alpha value for the entire Arabic questionnaire was 0.883, indicating a good level of internal consistency across all items.

Statistical analysis

Data from the hard copy questionnaires were entered manually by the investigators into the original Google Form online questionnaire that was used to collect online data, then data was extracted from Google Form directly to an Excel spreadsheet. After that, we encoded the raw data in the Excel sheet to make it compatible with the statistics software. We used Statistical Package for Social Sciences version 26.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, United States). The governorates of origin were divided into two categories based on whether or not it is afflicted, the cities afflicted are Aleppo, Latakia, Idlib, and Hama (Syrian Arab News Agency (SANA), 2023) (Figure 1). For the univariate analysis, chi-square tests, t-tests, and MANOVA tests were conducted, as appropriate. To examine the effect of the earthquake event on the three psychological scores while accounting for the nested structure of the data, linear mixed-effects models were employed. This approach allowed us to model both fixed effects, which represent the average effects of predictors on the outcome, and random effects, which capture the variability across different levels of grouping factors. The dependent variables in our analysis were the scores of three measures (NSESSS, PHQ-9, and GAD-7), which were measured on a continuous scale and analysed separately in different models. In each model, the governorate clusters were treated as random effects, and the time elapsed between the earthquake and symptom assessment was treated as a fixed effect. The inclusion of independent variables in the models was based on rational and theoretical reasoning, in addition to the results of the univariate analysis. Model fit was assessed using the log-likelihood value and the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC). Lower AIC values indicate better model fit. Statistical significance was determined using a threshold of P < 0.05. Figure 1.Map of Syria. Results

Participants characteristics

Out of the initial sample of 5,054 participants, 4,473 individuals met the inclusion criteria, resulting in a response rate of 88.5%. Among the included participants, 30.9% were males, and 69.1% were females. The median age of the study sample was 23 years, with a range of 18 to 86 years. The majority, 59.4%, were aged between 18 and 24 years. Additionally, 17.7% were between 25 and 30 years old, 13.9% were between 30 and 45 years old, and 9% were above 45 years old. Approximately half of the participants belonged to afflicted governorates, namely Aleppo, Latakia, Idlib, and Hama. Out of the total sample, 22.2% declared being displaced due to war. Regarding marital status, 72.7% of the participants were single. In terms of economic status, 40.5% declared a good economic status, 40% reported a moderate economic status, while 12.7% and 6.8% reported poor and excellent economic statuses, respectively. Education-wise, 80.9% of the participants held a university degree, 8.8% had a secondary education, and 10.3% had a basic education. Exposure characteristics

Exposure characteristics are presented in Table 1. Out of the total sample, 89.2% felt the earthquake. Physical trauma was reported by 15.1% of participants, while 21.8% had close associates who were injured. Additionally, 16.6% experienced the death of a close associate due to the event. Among the participants, 6.9% were transferred to a shelter house. Regarding involvement in the earthquake response, 8.4% stated that they were among the survivors, 3.4% were part of the rescue team, and 20.7% declared witnessing terrible events. Participants’ perceptions of the ongoing earthquake risk varied, with 10.6% believing that the risk still exists and 33.4% sharing the same belief. On the other hand, 38.6% expressed a neutral stance. In terms of other symptoms, 37.2% reported feeling dizzy, 42.1% experienced recurrent night-time fear arousal, and 57.7% reported a continued sensation of seismic activity. The mean of time elapsed between the earthquake and symptom assessment in days was 41 (±9).

Symptoms’ frequency of ASD, depression, and anxiety

The mean of the NSESSS scale was 8.5 (±6.5). Of 4474, 55.3% exhibited positive symptoms of ASD. The severity was as follows: 57.5% had mild symptoms, 32.2% had moderate symptoms, and 10.3% had severe to extreme symptoms. The frequency rate of depressive symptoms was 71.6%, while the rate of anxiety symptoms was 59.1%. The mean of the GAD-7 scale was 7.4 (±6.5). Regarding anxiety symptoms severity, 40.9% reported no symptoms, mild symptoms were reported by 28.3%, 16.7% had moderate symptoms, and 14.1% had severe symptoms. The mean of the PHQ-9 scale was 9.8 (±7.3). In terms of depression severity, none was reported by 28.4%, mild symptoms were reported by 29%, 18.9% had moderate symptoms, 13.3% had moderately severe symptoms, and 10.4% had severe symptoms. Symptoms of ASD, depression, and anxiety were more prevalent among participants belonging to the afflicted governorates (Figure 2). When examining the overlapping pattern between mental health symptoms, findings indicate that only 3.5% of individuals with ASD symptoms do not have concurrent depressive or anxiety symptoms. Additionally, 10.8% have only depressive symptoms, and 2.4% have only anxiety symptoms. The majority, 40.4%, experience both ASD symptoms and concurrent depressive and anxiety symptoms (Figure 3). Predictors of ASD, anxiety, and depressive symptoms

The first LME model examined ASD, GAD-7, and PHQ-9 as dependent factors, and independent factors were governorate variables as random effect and time elapsed as fixed factor. Among all the governorates, Idlib exhibited the strongest associations across all three scales, with significant positive associations observed for ASD (β = 3.32, 95% CI [1.58, 5.06], P < .001), GAD-7 (β = 2.75, 95% CI [.96, 4.54], P = .004), and PHQ-9 (β = 2.82, 95% CI [.91, 4.74], P = .005). Aleppo showed a significant positive association with ASD (β = 1.71, 95% CI [.44, 2.98], P = .01), while did not with GAD-7 (β = 0.37, 95% CI [-.99, 1.73], P = .56) or PHQ-9 (β = .57, 95% CI [−.79, 1.94], P = .38). Latakia also demonstrated a significant positive association with ASD (β = 1.84, 95% CI [.48, 3.19], P = .01) and a marginally significant association with PHQ-9 (β = 1.65, 95% CI [.19, 3.12], P = .02), but no significant association with GAD-7 (β = 1.21, 95% CI [−.21, 2.65], P = .09). Homs exhibited a significant positive association with GAD-7 (β = 2.64, 95% CI [.85, 4.43], P = .005), while Damascus showed a significant negative association with ASD (β = −1.82, 95% CI [−3.1, −.55], P = .008) and Damascus Countryside demonstrated a significant negative association with ASD (β = −1.5, 95% CI [−2.82, −.19], P = .027). Furthermore, As-Suwayda exhibited significant negative associations with all three scales: ASD (β = −2.66, 95% CI [−4.61, −.7], P = .009), GAD-7 (β = −2.98, 95% CI [−4.98, −.98], P = .005), and PHQ-9 (β = −3.29, 95% CI [−5.44, −1.13], P = .004). Lastly, time was included as a covariate and showed a significant negative association with all three scales: ASD (β = −0.03, 95% CI [−.05, −.01], P = .005), GAD-7 (β = −.05, 95% CI [−.7, −.02], P < .001), and PHQ-9 (β = −.06, 95% CI [−.08, −.03], P < .001). Discussion

This research shed light on the high frequency of mental health problems following a devastating earthquake in the context of Syria. ASD poses challenges in terms of screening due to the rapid onset of symptoms following the catastrophic event. Furthermore, individuals affected by the earthquake may delay seeking professional help until their symptoms meet the criteria for PTSD diagnosis.

High-frequency rates

The rate of probable ASD symptoms can vary significantly and is influenced by factors such as the nature and intensity of the event (Fanai & Khan, 2023). Other factors, including the study design, assessment instruments employed, and sample size, can also contribute to variations in the frequency rates (Worku et al., 2022). In the context of this large-scale study, over half of the participants showed symptoms that met the threshold criteria for ASD. Limited research has been conducted to assess probable ASD symptoms, specifically in the aftermath of natural disasters. The Hurricane Katrina study stated a higher rate of ASD symptoms (62%) (Mills et al., 2007). Other studies investigating ASD symptoms following natural disasters, such as the L’Aquila earthquake study (Casacchia et al., 2012), the Panzhihua-Huilia study (Wang et al., 2012), and the El Salvador Earthquakes study (Sattler et al., 2006), did not explicitly report the rate of these symptoms. Nonetheless, the frequency rate is substantially higher when compared to those in the context of human-made or violent events (Fullerton et al., 2004; Holbrook et al., 2005; Worku et al., 2022). It is worth noting that we used a validated scale presented by the American Psychiatric Association but without a prior firm validation in the Syrian context. Thus, the estimated frequency rate of probable ASD (55.3%) should be interpreted with caution. The high rate of positive ASD symptoms reported in this study may reflect the scale’s sensitivity in detecting acute stress reactions following a major disaster. The true prevalence of clinically significant ASD symptoms may be lower; however, this is unlikely given the previous report indicating a high rate of PTSD within the Syrian community (Aysazci-Cakar et al., 2022; Kakaje et al., 2021). Despite this limitation, the current findings provide valuable insights into the mental health impact of the earthquakes and highlight the need for comprehensive psychological support and interventions targeting acute stress reactions in the affected cities.

Depression and anxiety are commonly observed following exposure to various types of traumatic events. In the present study, it was found that over half of the participants experienced symptoms of anxiety, while 66% exhibited symptoms of depression in the immediate aftermath of the event. It is important to note that this study is not the first to assess the prevalence of depression and anxiety on a national level in Syria. Another study conducted during the COVID-19 pandemic in Syria, which employed the same screening measures, reported even higher prevalence rates. Specifically, this study found a prevalence of 83% for depressive symptoms and 69% for anxiety symptoms (Mohsen et al., 2021). These findings suggest that the rates of depression and anxiety may vary depending on the specific context and time period in which the assessment is conducted.

Determining whether there has been a decrease in psychological distress in the period between the COVID-19 pandemic and the event under study is challenging. The interpretation of these frequency rates may be complex due to several factors. Firstly, this study was conducted in a country that has experienced repeated traumatic events, which can contribute to ongoing psychological distress. Additionally, the community may still be facing difficulties that impact mental health. However, when evaluating the correlations with exposure characteristics, it becomes apparent that these rates are partially influenced by the recent event being studied. Specifically, individuals who were closer to the event site exhibited higher rates of depressive and anxiety symptoms compared to those residing in relatively distant governorates. This suggests that proximity to the traumatic event may contribute to increased psychological distress among individuals. This study’s rates of depressive and anxiety symptoms are higher than other studies such as the Northern Peru earthquake (Valladares-Garrido et al., 2022), the Jiuzhaigou earthquake in 2020 (Xi et al., 2020), and the September 19th, 2017 Earthquake in Mexico (Maya-Mondragón et al., 2019).

It is important to note that the significance of physical distance from the event was no longer observed in the final model when accounting for other factors. This suggests that the influence of distance from the event on psychological distress may be confounded or mediated by other variables included in the model. The presence of these additional factors may explain why distance alone did not retain its significance in predicting psychological distress in the final model.

Overlapping psychological reactions

Emerging research in psychiatry suggests that mental disorders fall into each other and there may be different aspects to mental disease; therefore, a person may be more susceptible to particular disorders than others based on how they achieve each aspect (Marshall, 2020). Given the research findings, regardless of ASD severity, only 5.3% of participants presented with ASD in the absence of detectable depression or anxiety symptoms. Conversely, over 40% of the sample exhibited ASD, anxiety, and depressive symptoms.

Predictors

The predisposing factors for disrupted mental health following a natural disaster can be categorized into three groups: pre-disaster, peri-disaster, and post-disaster factors. Younger age, female gender, and pre-existing mental health issues are primary pre-disaster risk factors (Goldmann & Galea, 2014). Women are considerably more susceptible to developing psychological disorders (Karsten et al., 2011; Riecher-Rössler, 2017), while after catastrophic events men are more prone to develop resilience (Bonanno & Gupta, 2009). The study’s findings point to the same effects concerning developing ASD, anxiety, and depressive symptoms after tragedy. The gender gap is related to biological bases and it is present regardless of age and population differences (Norris et al., 2002; Riecher-Rössler, 2017). In contrast, the effect of age on the risk of developing a mental disorder varies across societies. However, consistent with the search results, it is well known that young and middle-aged adults are more at risk of having psychological distress, especially during a crisis (Best et al., 2023; Jorm et al., 2005). A history of mental illness and experiencing stressful events before the traumatic event carries a huge probability of conceiving a mental disorder (Benjet et al., 2016). The 12-year-old ongoing conflict has rendered the area ripe for various mental illnesses (Alhaffar & Janos, 2021). The most recent earthquake occurred in a region with poor economic and humanitarian conditions, where difficulties permeate all facets of daily life. The earthquake is not the sole overwhelming crisis impacting Syria, which has also endured the prolonged effects of the COVID-19 pandemic (Latifeh & Mohsen, 2021). This suggests the chronic toll on mental health in the region. Syria’s mental health landscape can be characterized as notoriously complicated, given the recurrent traumatization over the past decade. The research findings further corroborate this concerning state of affairs.

Peri-disaster risk factors are those that take place around the event, such as the severity of trauma exposure, personal loss of beloved ones or material losses, being at the event site, witnessing the destruction and death, or seeing images on the media (Goldmann & Galea, 2014; Pfefferbaum et al., 2014; Pietrzak et al., 2013). Considering the findings of the study, in comparison to residents of other governorates, those in the impacted governorates were more inclined to experience psychological stress and ASD. In addition, being displaced, maybe once again, to an asylum house, where one directly witnessed tragic events, loss of loved ones, or getting injured was correlated with developing psychological symptoms. However, exposure to the media during the event and the development of mental disturbance were not well established in these research findings. Regarding post-disaster factors, people’s beliefs about earthquake recurrence were significantly correlated with psychological symptoms developments.

The current study found that the predictors for ASD, anxiety, and depression were not entirely consistent. While most factors were associated with all three mental health outcomes, other variables demonstrated more selective correlations. These findings suggest that some predictors may be more closely related to one psychological response than another. For example, the loss of close associates may be a more salient predictor of depressive symptoms and prolonged grief reactions. Similarly, the role of media consumption and engagement with earthquake-related information may play a distinct part in the development of ASD. Interestingly, participants who reported ‘keen from time to time’ news-following behaviour (as compared to those who followed the news ‘most of the time’ or ‘tried to avoid it’) showed a correlation with both ASD and depressive symptoms, this perhaps reflects a coping mechanism or an attempt to make sense of the tragedy. This cross-sectional study design cannot determine the unique etiological pathways and maintenance factors underlying these mental health conditions. Further research will be necessary to investigate this matter more thoroughly.

What do we already know?

Natural disasters primarily impact developing countries since they are far more likely to have subpar warning systems, shorter emergency responses, substandard disaster control systems, and inadequate acute aid programs, which was implied in the last tragedy. The mental health field in Syria is in shortage of necessary equipment, one of which is human resources, which makes its ability to contain current or futuristic crises inadequate. Nevertheless, stigma against the need for psychiatrists’ help, and lack of awareness of social and mental health support are among other obstacles to practical work in the field of mental health in Syria.

Strengths and limitations

This large-scale study investigates the psychological impact of a major natural disaster. Additionally, the timeframe in which the survey was conducted is highly representative of acute symptom screening. Symptoms of Acute Stress Disorder (ASD) were assessed using a tool provided by the American Psychiatric Association (APA), which demonstrated a reliable outcome in this research. However, one key limitation of the current study is that the assessment tool used has not been previously validated across the Syrian population. Secondly, the sample may not fairly represent the overall Syrian population in terms of gender distribution, as there is an overrepresentation of females in the study sample, although the mean age is fairly consistent with the general population. In addition, the use of chain-referral sampling through social media might introduce bias, as it may not be representative of the broader population. Thirdly, this research was conducted in a retraumatized country, thus interpreting the results compared with other regions should be done with caution. In addition, the result of a significant increase in morbidity could not be obtained. Moreover, a cross-sectional study design may not be able to deduce causality and connection, thus, additional longitudinal studies are needed to obtain more precise results. Finally, this study did not apply a follow-up approach, thus we could not interpret the course of this research findings.

Conclusion

The findings of this study shed significant light on the high rates of acute stress, depressive, and anxiety symptoms among Syrians in the immediate aftermath of a natural catastrophe, as well as the connections between these mental health problems and the risk factors that contribute to their development. Syrians experience high levels of psychological frustration, which was direr in the impacted areas. Female gender, younger adult age, and pre-existing mental health issues were key pre-disaster predisposing factors for the development of ASD, as well as anxiety and depression. This, together with peri-event factors related to the individual’s unique experience of the event, and post-event factors such as one’s own beliefs about the event and the physical symptoms following it, contributed to the overall psychological impact. Effective action must be implemented towards mental health in Syria to lessen the cumulative consequences on human health and to prevent further community threats such as increased suicide rates.
