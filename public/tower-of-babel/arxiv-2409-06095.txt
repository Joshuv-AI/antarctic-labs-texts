2.1.1.
The Cauchy problem without source

When the initial datum
\overline{u}
has just one jump at the point
x_{0}
from the left limit
\overline{u}(x_{0}-)
to the right limit
\overline{u}(x_{0}+)
, and if the source term is vanishing, then the Cauchy problem is called Riemann problem: the solution is given by a self-similar profile which is the discretization, with some threshold
\varepsilon_{\nu}
, of

•

the profile of the inverse of the derivative of the convex envelope
f_{*}
of
f
in
[\overline{u}(x_{0}-),\overline{u}(x_{0}+)]
when
\overline{u}(x_{0}-)
is less than
\overline{u}(x_{0}+)
: for
t>0
, properly defining such inverse,
u(t,x_{0}+h)=(\frac{d}{du}f_{*}^{\nu})^{-1}(h/t)
.

•

If instead
\overline{u}(x_{0}-)>\overline{u}(x_{0}+)
, then the same is done with the concave envelope of
f
in
[\overline{u}(x_{0}+),\overline{u}(x_{0}-)]
.

For convex fluxes, downwards jumps will be traveling, as they are, with the Rankine-Hugoniot speed
\frac{f(\overline{u}(x_{0}+))-f(\overline{u}(x_{0}-))}{\overline{u}(x_{0}+)-\overline{u}(x_{0}-)}
; for increasing jumps, from the discontinuity point there is the discretization of a continuous wave fan opening up.
In particular, the maximum principle holds, differently from the case of
1d
-systems.

In order to provide a piecewise constant approximation to the entropy solution to the Cauchy problem (
1.1
) more generally, the starting point is fixing a piecewise constant approximation
\overline{u}_{\nu}
of the initial datum
\overline{u}
, requiring also that
\hbox{\testo Tot.Var.}(\overline{u}_{\nu})\leq\hbox{\testo Tot.Var.}(\overline{u})
.
If
x_{1}\,,\,\dots\,,\,x_{k}
are the points where
\overline{u}_{\nu}
jumps, at each point
x_{i}
one solves the Riemann problem corresponding to the jump from
\overline{u}_{\nu}(x_{i}-)
to
\overline{u}_{\nu}(x_{i}+)
: this provides a local approximate solution just by patching together the solutions to the Riemann problem.
When the source is not present, this solution can be prolonged up to a first time
t_{1}>0
where two or more lines of discontinuity, coming from the solution to adjacent Riemann problems at time
t=0
, bump one into the other.
At this time one solves the newly arose Riemann problem, prolonging the solution to a later time
t_{2}>t_{1}
where a second set of wave-front interactions take place, and so on.

For a single equation, since the total variation is not increasing, there are simpler argument to conclude that the number of interaction is finite, producing in total a finite number of discontinuity fronts: the procedure works in defining a global in time approximation
u_{\nu}
.
We rather introduce key functionals that are essential to control the total variation and to provide key estimates for compactness in the case of systems, granting in turn to find the entropy solution as a limit solution of such approximations.
Such functionals are the total variation, that along the approximate solution of a single equation is non-increasing, and Glimm interaction functionals.

Definition 9
.

If

\hbox{\testo Tot.Var.}(v)\leq M

2.2.
Fine convergence of discrete approximation

In this section we restrict to the case of a balance law (
1.1
) with uniformly convex flux function
f
and with a smooth source
g
that satisfies Assumption
(G)
.

Apart from the fact that the approximation scheme converges to the entropy solution to the balance law, there are finer properties of the convergence: very roughly, big jumps in the approximation converge well to big jumps in the limit solution. Such finer properties are useful in order to study separately the evolution of the continuous and of the jump part of the spatial derivative of the entropy solution.

The first step toward that is to better understand what we mean by ‘big jumps in the approximation’.

4.
Sketch of main arguments

4.1.
\mathrm{SBV}
-regularity for uniformly convex balance laws

Among the many classical and powerful approximations for a scalar balance law in one space dimension, we refer to the operator splitting method summarized in §
2.1
.
Even if the approximation
u_{\nu}
is piecewise constant,
\nu\uparrow\infty
, we can distinguish a part

which

and called jump part; see Definitions

In particular,

The measure

in turn

-converges to the continuous part

of

Differentiating in

and jump balance measure

-regularity estimates.
When a source term is present, we simultaneously introduce a source measure

All such measures we introduce are bounded uniformly in

by the Glimm functional and by

In particular, in the limit, by compactness and up to a subsequence, we are able to define limit measures

,

In particular,

The most important role of the measures we introduce is providing balance estimates which lead to a two sided generalization of the fundamental Olëinink decay estimate:

-regularity comes then as a corollary.

can be present at most countably many times.

Proof.

Define the nonnegative Radon measure on the real line with the following values on intervals:

\mu([s,t])=\left\lvert\xi^{\mathrm{cont}}\right\rvert([s,t]\times\mathbb{R})+\left\lVert f^{\prime\prime}\right\rVert_{\infty}\mu^{\mathrm{source}}([s,t]\times\mathbb{R})+\hbox{\testo Tot.Var.}^{-}\left(\Upsilon(u_{\nu});(s,t]\right)\,.

For a Borel set
K
with
\mathcal{L}^{1}(K)=0
, taking the limit as
s\to t
, from (
4.1
) we get

0\leq[D_{x}^{\mathrm{cont}}u(t)]^{+}(K)\leq\mu(\{t\})\qquad\text{and}\qquad 0\leq[D_{x}^{\mathrm{cont}}u(t)]^{-}(K)\leq\mu(\{t\})\,.

In particular,
\left\lvert D_{x}u(t)\right\rvert(K)>0
is allowed only if
\mu
has an atom: this can happen at most at countably many instants.
Since the Cantor part of
D_{x}u(t)
is the part of the continuous measure
D^{\mathrm{cont}}_{x}u(t)
which is not absolutely continuous, thus it is concentrated on a
\sigma
-compact set
K
with
\mathcal{L}^{1}(K)=0
, we conclude that
D^{\mathrm{Cantor}}_{x}u(t)
can be present at most countably many times, as stated.
∎

4.2.
Balances on characteristic regions.

Before explaining where Olëinink type estimates (
4.1
) come from, we need an auxiliary tool: approximate balances on the flow of the union of finitely many intervals.
Let’s begin with simpler approximate balances on the
\nu
-flow of a closed interval
J(t)=[a(t),b(t)]
.

We recall the definition of generalized characteristics, introduced by Dafermos
[
12
]
.

Definition 23
.

A generalized characteristic curve of
u_{\nu}
is a Lipschitz continuous function
y_{\nu}:[0,T]\to\mathbb{R}
whose velocity
\dot{y}_{\nu}(t)
is between
f^{\prime}(u_{\nu}(t,y_{\nu}(t-))
and
f^{\prime}(u_{\nu}(t,y_{\nu}(t+))
at
\mathcal{L}^{1}
-a.e. time
t\in[0,T]
.

If
a_{\nu}(t)\leq b_{\nu}(t)
are generalized characteristics of
u_{\nu}
, setting
J_{\nu}(t)=[a_{\nu}(t)\,,b_{\nu}(t)]
for
0\leq s\leq t\leq T
we compute

\displaystyle\left[{{\mathfrak{\upsilon}}}^{\nu,\mathrm{cont}}(t)\right](J_{\nu}(t))-\left[{{\mathfrak{\upsilon}}}^{\nu,\mathrm{cont}}(s)\right](J_{\nu}(s))=\mu^{\nu,\mathrm{cont}}\left(A_{\nu}\right)+\Phi_{\nu}^{\mathrm{cont}}(A_{\nu})

where
A_{\nu}=\{(\tau,\xi)\ :\ s<\tau\leq t\,,\ \xi\in J_{\nu}(t)\}
is the region bounded by
a_{\nu}(t)
,
b_{\nu}(t)
and
\Phi_{\nu}^{\mathrm{cont}}(A_{\nu})
is the flux of continuous waves through the boundary of
A_{\nu}
.
More precisely,
\Phi_{\nu}^{\mathrm{cont}}
is, in principle, the sum of sizes of waves that are not
\beta_{\nu}
-approximate discontinuities of
u_{\nu}
, see Definition
12
, and that enter the region
A_{\nu}
in the time interval
(s,t]
, minus the sum of sizes of waves that are not
\beta_{\nu}
-approximate discontinuities of
u_{\nu}
and that exit the region
A_{\nu}
in the time interval
[s,t)
.

Since neither rarefaction waves nor shock waves can leave the region
A_{\nu}
from lateral boundaries, due to the fact that it is bounded by generalized characteristics and the flux is convex, with the irrelevant exception of rarefactions arising at update points along the boundary, then the only contribution to the lateral flux is the one of rarefactions and small jumps entering the region: except at most one time per boundary, when
a_{\nu}(t)
or
b_{\nu}(t)
might bump into a jump and later they follow this jump, this entering of waves is possible only if
a_{\nu}(t)
or
b_{\nu}(t)
are small jumps that interact with the wave that is entering.
Due to this analysis, and due to interaction estimates recalled in Remark (
10
),
\Phi_{\nu}^{\mathrm{cont}}(A_{\nu})
is estimated from above by the decrease of
\Upsilon
due to cancellations located on the lateral boundary of
A_{\nu}
.

In the same way as for the continuous part, we compute

\displaystyle\left[{{\mathfrak{\upsilon}}}^{\nu,\mathrm{jump}}(t)\right](J_{\nu}(t))-\left[{{\mathfrak{\upsilon}}}^{\nu,\mathrm{jump}}(s)\right](J_{\nu}(s))=\mu^{\nu,\mathrm{jump}}\left(A_{\nu}\right)+\Phi_{\nu}^{\mathrm{jump}}(A_{\nu})

where
\Phi_{\nu}^{\mathrm{jump}}
is, in principle,

•

the sum of sizes of waves that are
\beta_{\nu}
-approximate discontinuities of
u_{\nu}
, see Definition
12
, and that enter the region
A_{\nu}
in the time interval
(s,t]
, minus

•

the sum of sizes of waves that are
\beta_{\nu}
-approximate discontinuities of
u_{\nu}
and that exit the region
A_{\nu}
in the time interval
[s,t)
.

Since no
\beta_{\nu}
-approximate discontinuity can leave the region, and since
\beta_{\nu}
-approximate discontinuities entering the region bring a negative contribution, then
\Phi_{\nu}^{\mathrm{jump}}(A_{\nu})\leq 0
.

Extending the estimate when
J_{\nu}
is the evolution of the union of countably many closed intervals one obtains

(4.2)

\displaystyle\left[{{\mathfrak{\upsilon}}}^{\nu}(t)\right](J_{\nu}(t))-\left[{{\mathfrak{\upsilon}}}^{\nu}(s)\right](J_{\nu}(s))\leq\left\lvert\mu^{\nu}\right\rvert\left(A_{\nu}\right)+\hbox{\testo Tot.Var.}^{-}\left(\Upsilon(u_{\nu});(s,t]\right)\,,

(4.3)

\displaystyle\left[{{\mathfrak{\upsilon}}}^{\nu,\mathrm{cont}}(t)\right](J_{\nu}(t))-\left[{{\mathfrak{\upsilon}}}^{\nu,\mathrm{cont}}(s)\right](J_{\nu}(s))\leq\left\lvert\mu^{\nu,\mathrm{cont}}\right\rvert\left(A_{\nu}\right)+\hbox{\testo Tot.Var.}^{-}\left(\Upsilon(u_{\nu});(s,t]\right)\,,

(4.4)

\displaystyle\left[{{\mathfrak{\upsilon}}}^{\nu,\mathrm{jump}}(t)\right](J_{\nu}(t))-\left[{{\mathfrak{\upsilon}}}^{\nu,\mathrm{jump}}(s)\right](J_{\nu}(s))\leq\left\lvert\mu^{\nu,\mathrm{jump}}\right\rvert\left(A_{\nu}\right)\,.

Such balances are the final ingredient to get Olëinink type estimates (
4.1
).
We stress that looking at the evolution of
D_{x}f^{\prime}(u_{\nu})
instead of
D_{x}u_{\nu}
, as explained in Remark
20
, we would equally find

(4.5)

\displaystyle\left[\eta^{\nu,\mathrm{cont}}(t)\right](J_{\nu}(t))-\left[\eta^{\nu,\mathrm{cont}}(s)\right](J_{\nu}(s))\leq\left\lvert\xi^{\nu,\mathrm{cont}}\right\rvert\left(A_{\nu}\right)+\left\lVert f^{\prime\prime}\right\rVert_{\infty}\hbox{\testo Tot.Var.}^{-}\left(\Upsilon(u_{\nu});(s,t]\right)\,,

(4.6)

\displaystyle\left[\eta^{\nu,\mathrm{jump}}(t)\right](J_{\nu}(t))-\left[\eta^{\nu,\mathrm{jump}}(s)\right](J_{\nu}(s))\leq\left\lvert\xi^{\nu,\mathrm{jump}}\right\rvert\left(A_{\nu}\right)\,,

(4.7)

\displaystyle\left[\eta^{\nu}(t)\right](J_{\nu}(t))-\left[\eta^{\nu}(s)\right](J_{\nu}(s))\leq\left\lvert\xi^{\nu}\right\rvert\left(A_{\nu}\right)+\left\lVert f^{\prime\prime}\right\rVert_{\infty}\hbox{\testo Tot.Var.}^{-}\left(\Upsilon(u_{\nu});(s,t]\right)\,.

4.3.
Sketch of Olëinink type two-sided estimates.

Let’s see the origin of the Olëinink type estimates (
4.1
).

4.3.1.
Lower Olëinink type estimate (
4.1a
)

First focus on the single interval
B=J=J_{\nu}(0)
.
Simplify notations denoting
s=0
,
t=t^{*}
and consider generalized characteristics
a_{\nu}
,
b_{\nu}
as in the previous paragraphs.
Since we want to estimate the negative part of the measure, consider the initial condition
\left[\eta^{\nu,\mathrm{cont}}(0)\right](J)<0
.
Case 1: In case

-\frac{d}{dt}\mathcal{L}^{1}(J_{\nu}(r))=\dot{a}_{\nu}(r)-\dot{b}_{\nu}(r)>-\frac{1}{4}\left[\eta^{\nu,\mathrm{cont}}(0)\right](J)\geq 0\qquad\text{for a.e.~$0<r<t^{*}$ then}

just by integrating we get the desired inequality

(4.8)

\mathcal{L}^{1}(J_{\nu}(0))-\mathcal{L}^{1}(J_{\nu}(t^{*}))>-\frac{t^{*}}{4}\left[\eta^{\nu,\mathrm{cont}}(0)\right](J)\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad-\left[\eta^{\nu,\mathrm{cont}}(0)\right](J)<\frac{4}{t^{*}}\mathcal{L}^{1}(J)\,.

Case 2:
Suppose instead there is some time
\overline{r}\in(0,t^{*})
such that

(4.9)

-\frac{d}{dt}\mathcal{L}^{1}(J(\overline{r}))=\dot{a}_{\nu}(\overline{r})-\dot{b}_{\nu}(\overline{r})<-\frac{1}{4}\left[\eta^{\nu,\mathrm{cont}}(0)\right](J)\,,\qquad-\left[\eta^{\nu,\mathrm{cont}}(0)\right](J)\geq 0.

Define the boundary error
\mathfrak{d}(t)=\dot{a}_{\nu}(\overline{r})-\dot{b}_{\nu}(\overline{r})-f^{\prime}(u_{\nu}(\overline{r},a_{\nu}(\overline{r})-))+f^{\prime}(u_{\nu}(\overline{r},b_{\nu}(\overline{r})+))
. In particular, by how the discretization of rarefactions is defined and by the Rankine-Hugoniot condition,
\mathfrak{d}(t)=\hat{\rho}_{1}\left[D_{x}f^{\prime}(u_{\nu}(\overline{r}))\right](a_{\nu}(\overline{r}))+\hat{\rho}_{2}\left[D_{x}f^{\prime}(u_{\nu}(\overline{r}))\right](b_{\nu}(\overline{r}))
with
\hat{\rho}_{1},\hat{\rho}_{2}\in[0,1]
so that

\displaystyle-\frac{d}{dt}\mathcal{L}^{1}(J(\overline{r}))

\displaystyle=-(D_{x}f^{\prime}(u_{\nu}(\overline{r})))(J(\overline{r}))+\mathfrak{d}(\overline{r})

\displaystyle=-\left[D_{x}f^{\prime}(u_{\nu}(\overline{r}))\right]((a_{\nu}(\overline{r}),b_{\nu}(\overline{r})))+(\hat{\rho}_{1}-1)\left[D_{x}f^{\prime}(u_{\nu}(\overline{r}))\right](a_{\nu}(\overline{r}))+(\hat{\rho}_{2}-1)\left[D_{x}f^{\prime}(u_{\nu}(\overline{r}))\right](b_{\nu}(\overline{r}))

\displaystyle\geq-\left[\eta^{\nu,\mathrm{cont}}(\overline{r})\right](J_{\nu}(\overline{r}))-2\varepsilon_{\nu}

where in the last inequality we possibly neglected the positive contribution of
\beta
-approximate discontinuities and we estimate possible rarefactions at the boundary by
\varepsilon_{\nu}
.
Jointly with (
4.9
) then

\displaystyle-\left[\eta^{\nu,\mathrm{cont}}(\overline{r})\right](J_{\nu}(\overline{r}))

\displaystyle\leq-\frac{1}{4}\left[\eta^{\nu,\mathrm{cont}}(0)\right](J)+8\varepsilon_{\nu}\,.

A similar argument works if
J_{\nu}(t)
is the evolution of
k
intervals, with an error
8k\varepsilon_{\nu}

Applying (
4.5
) we arrive to

\displaystyle-\left[\eta^{\nu,\mathrm{cont}}(0)\right](J)-\left\lvert\xi^{\nu,\mathrm{cont}}\right\rvert\left(A_{\nu}\right)-{\left\lVert f^{\prime\prime}\right\rVert_{\infty}}\hbox{\testo Tot.Var.}^{-}\left(\Upsilon(u_{\nu});(s,t]\right)

\displaystyle\leq-\frac{1}{4}\left[\eta^{\nu,\mathrm{cont}}(0)\right](J)+8k\varepsilon_{\nu}\,.

Rearranging terms, we get

-\left[\eta^{\nu,\mathrm{cont}}(0)\right](J)\leq 2\left\lvert\xi^{\nu,\mathrm{cont}}\right\rvert\left(A_{\nu}\right)+2{\left\lVert f^{\prime\prime}\right\rVert_{\infty}}\hbox{\testo Tot.Var.}^{-}\left(\Upsilon(u_{\nu});(s,t]\right)+16k\varepsilon_{\nu}\,.

We are close to estimate (
4.1a
) we were looking for, as
A_{\nu}\subset[0,t^{*}]\times\mathbb{R}
and jointly with (
4.8
).
What remains, roughly, is first a careful limiting procedure paired with the approximation of
B

J
of finitely many intervals.
After that, which also allows to localize the limit estimate where
[D_{x}u^{\mathrm{cont}}]^{+}
and

f^{\prime}(z+h)-f^{\prime}(x)\geq\kappa h
allows to get the estimate for the derivative of
u
in place of the one for the derivative of
f^{\prime}(u)

4.4.
Proof of the Theorem
4

Point (
1
) is based on a separate analysis of various regions of the domain
[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}
.
One decomposes
[0,T]\times R
into

•

a jump set
J
of points
(t,x)
where

•

into

•

Since
J_{t}=J\cap\{t\}\times\mathbb{R}

[D_{x}^{\mathrm{Cantor}}f^{\prime}(u(t))](J_{t})=0
.
Moreover, by Vol’pert chain rule
D_{x}f^{\prime}(u)=f^{\prime\prime}(u)D_{x}u
, therefore the measure

vanishes on

because it is multiplied by
0
.
The idea to treat

is that, by the tame oscillation condition, one can construct a countable covering of
P
with open triangles
T_{k}
where
f^{\prime\prime}(u(T_{k}))
has a positive lower bound, or a negative upper bound, plus a remaining set
S_{0}\times\mathbb{R}
having at most countable projection on the
t

, Theorem
2
applies, so that
D_{x}u(t)\restriction_{T_{k}}
has a Cantor part at most for
t\in S_{k}
with
S_{k}
at most countable: for
t\notin S=\cup_{k=0}^{\infty}S_{k}
therefore

is a special function of bounded variation.
We do not work out all details of the covering, which has the same difficulty of the case of systems, see
[
6
, § 2]
,
[
3
, § 2.2]
.

Point (
2
) follows if one shows that there is no Cantor part of
D_{x}u
on the set
F
introduced above.
Indeed, consider any triangle
T_{k}
of the covering of
P
just mentioned: the proof of Point (
1
) shows that

is a special function of bounded variation, on that triangle, if

does not belong to the at most countable set

.
Moreover,
[D_{x}^{\mathrm{Cantor}}u(t)](J_{t})=0

is at most countable.
The remaining set to analyze of the partition is

The fact that the Cantor part of
D_{x}u

\mathcal{L}^{1}(u(F))=0
.
In order to understand this, consider any compact set
K\subseteq F\cap{\{t=\overline{t}\}}
of continuity points of

with
\mathcal{L}^{1}(K)=0
.
We identify
K

We now prove that
\left\lvert D_{x}v\right\rvert(K)=0

Given
\varepsilon>0

\mathcal{L}^{1}({v(K)})=0

Define the sequence of functions

Notice that by construction, by the choice of the intervals exploiting
\mathcal{L}^{1}(v(K))=0

\left\lvert v(x)-v_{\varepsilon}(x)\right\rvert\leq{\sum_{i=1}^{k}\mathcal{L}^{1}(v(I_{i}))<\varepsilon^{2}}

By the lower semicontinuity of the variation in the

Since

\left\lvert D_{x}v\right\rvert(\mathbb{R})=\left\lvert D_{x}v_{\varepsilon}\right\rvert(\mathbb{R})+\left\lvert D_{x}v\right\rvert\left(\cup_{i=1}^{k}v(I_{k})\right)\geq\left\lvert D_{x}v_{\varepsilon}\right\rvert(\mathbb{R})+\left\lvert D_{x}v\right\rvert(K)
