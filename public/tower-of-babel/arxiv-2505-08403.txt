ConDiSim: Conditional Diffusion Models for Simulation-Based Inference
Mayank Nautiyal, Andreas Hellander, Prashant Singh

Abstract
We present a conditional diffusion model - ConDiSim, for simulation-based inference of complex systems with intractable likelihoods. ConDiSim leverages denoising diffusion probabilistic models to approximate posterior distributions, consisting of a forward process that adds Gaussian noise to parameters, and a reverse process learning to denoise, conditioned on observed data. This approach effectively captures complex dependencies and multi-modalities within posteriors. ConDiSim is evaluated across ten benchmark problems and two real-world test problems, where it demonstrates effective posterior approximation accuracy while maintaining computational efficiency and stability in model training. ConDiSim offers a robust and extensible framework for simulation-based inference, particularly suitable for parameter inference workflows requiring fast inference methods.



1
Introduction

Statistical inference of model parameters from empirical observations is a fundamental challenge in scientific research, enabling researchers to derive meaningful insights from complex simulation models. These parameters govern the behavior of simulators that replicate real-world phenomena, providing a bridge between theoretical constructs and empirical observations
[
Lavin et al. 2021
]
. Calibrating these parameters to ensure that simulator outputs align with observed data constitutes an inverse problem, formally defined within the framework of simulation-based inference (SBI)
[
Cranmer et al. 2020
]
. Solving this inverse problem involves addressing uncertainties arising from model stochasticity and potential multi-valuedness, where different sets of parameter values can produce similar observations or similar parameters may lead to varied outputs. Additionally, parameter inference becomes increasingly complex when simulators operate as ‘black boxes’ with intractable likelihood functions, rendering traditional likelihood-based Bayesian methods impractical
[
Sisson et al. 2018
]
.

Recent advances in machine learning have introduced powerful generative models capable of approximating complex data distributions without explicit likelihood computations. Among these,
diffusion models
have emerged as a particularly promising class
[
Sohl-Dickstein et al. 2015
]
. Originally developed for image and signal processing, diffusion models iteratively denoise data starting from pure noise, effectively learning data distributions through a sequence of transformations. Compared to alternative generative frameworks like Variational Autoencoders (VAEs)
[
Kingma and Welling 2014
]
, Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs)
[
Goodfellow et al. 2014
]
, and flow-based models
[
Rezende and Mohamed 2015
]
, diffusion models offer significant advantages. They avoid issues like posterior collapse in VAEs and mode collapse in GANs, and are not constrained by invertible transformations as in flow-based models, enabling them to better model complex, high-dimensional, and multi-modal distributions. Diffusion models also demonstrate superior training stability and come with theoretical convergence guarantees due to their foundation in stochastic differential equations
[
Ho et al. 2020
]
. Their flexible architectures and sampling processes allow practitioners to balance sample quality and generation speed, enhancing their applicability in various contexts, including SBI.

Applying diffusion models to SBI leverages their ability to implicitly learn the conditional distribution of simulator parameters given observations, circumventing the need for explicit likelihood evaluations. This is particularly advantageous for inverse problems involving black-box simulators. Moreover, diffusion models naturally support
amortized inference
: a single, globally trained model approximates the posterior distribution across multiple observations, eliminating the need for separate optimization for each new observation
[
Kingma and Welling 2014
]
. This capability is especially valuable when rapid inference is required. However, integrating diffusion models into SBI presents challenges. Their iterative sampling process can be computationally intensive, and their performance is sensitive to hyperparameters like noise schedules and network architecture. Addressing these issues is crucial for enhancing the practical utility of diffusion-based SBI methods
[
Nichol and Dhariwal 2021
]
.

Contribution
: We present a conditional diffusion model (ConDiSim) for simulation-based inference that balances model expressiveness with training efficiency and simplicity. Experiments on benchmark tasks and real-world datasets show the effectiveness of the approach in recovering complex posterior distributions, while incurring minimal computational overhead. Further, simulation-based calibration checks validate the reliability and robustness of the proposed parameter inference approach.

The paper is organized as follows. Section
2
outlines the related work. In Section
3
, we formally define the SBI problem, followed by a detailed explanation of the ConDiSim model in Section
3.1
and Section
3.2
details our amortized inference strategy. A comprehensive comparison on benchmark, and real-world test problems is presented in Section
4
, with additional results provided in the Appendix. Section
5
discusses the results, while Section
6
summarizes the key findings and suggests future directions.

2
Related Work

Simulation-based inference (SBI) has advanced significantly in recent years, evolving from traditional likelihood-free methods to modern deep learning approaches. Classical techniques like Approximate Bayesian Computation (ABC)
[
Beaumont et al. 2002
]
and its sequential variants such as Sequential Monte Carlo-ABC (SMC-ABC)
[
Toni et al. 2009
]
, provide mathematical convergence guarantees but often struggle with high-dimensional problems
[
Sisson et al. 2018
]
. Although neural network-learned summary statistics
[
Jiang et al. 2017
,
Wrede et al. 2022
]
have contributed to mitigating this limitation, they introduce additional computational complexity.

Early advancements in neural network-based amortized inference for SBI were pioneered by Neural Posterior Estimation (NPE)
[
Papamakarios and Murray 2016
]
, which introduced conditional normalizing flows to model the posterior distribution, significantly improving accuracy and scalability over traditional methods. Sequential extensions like SNPE-A
[
Papamakarios and Murray 2018
]
, SNPE-B
[
Lueckmann et al. 2017
]
, and SNPE-C (also known as Automatic Posterior Transformation or APT)
[
Greenberg et al. 2019
]
enhanced efficiency by iteratively refining the model to focus on relevant parameter regions. However, these sequential methods require multiple training rounds and are constrained by the invertible transformations required in flow-based models.

Alternative sequential algorithms have also been proposed, such as Sequential Neural Ratio Estimation (SNRE)
[
Hermans et al. 2020
]
, which learns the likelihood-to-evidence ratio, and Sequential Neural Likelihood Estimation (SNLE)
[
Papamakarios et al. 2019
]
, which approximates the likelihood function directly. Generative Adversarial Training for SBI (GATSBI)
[
Ramesh et al. 2022
]
leverages adversarial networks to model complex distributions without explicit likelihood calculations. While effective for challenging inference problems, GATSBI faces issues common to adversarial training, such as instability and mode collapse
[
Arjovsky and Bottou 2017
,
Arjovsky et al. 2017
]
, which can limit robustness and complicate training, and it struggles in low-dimensional spaces as noted in
[
Ramesh et al. 2022
]
.

Recently, Simformer
[
Gloeckler et al. 2024
]
combined score-based diffusion models with transformer architectures to accurately learn complex, high-dimensional posteriors, offering high flexibility albeit with substantial computational overhead. In contrast, our proposed method, ConDiSim, utilizes denoising-based diffusion models with a simpler architecture, maintaining competitive performance while being computationally efficient. This makes it suitable for applications such as model exploration, that may involve numerous parameter inference problems with inference time being a key consideration
[
Wrede and Hellander 2019
]
.

In this study, we evaluate CondiSim against four distinct simulation-based inference (SBI) approaches: GATSBI
[
Ramesh et al. 2022
]
, APT
[
Greenberg et al. 2019
]
, NPE
[
Papamakarios and Murray 2016
]
, and Simformer
[
Gloeckler et al. 2024
]
. These methods represent a diverse spectrum of generative modeling techniques—namely, flow-based, adversarial, diffusion, one-shot, and sequential frameworks—adapted for SBI. This comprehensive selection facilitates an evaluation that emphasizes efficiency and scalability rather than focusing solely on the accuracy of posterior approximations. For further details on deep learning methodologies applied to SBI, readers are referred to
[
Zammit-Mangion et al. 2024
,
Radev et al. 2023a
]
.

3
Simulation-Based Inference

Simulation-based inference (SBI) aims to infer the posterior distribution
p(\bm{\theta}\mid\mathbf{y})
of model parameters
\bm{\theta}
, given observed data
\mathbf{y}
. From Bayes’ theorem:

p(\bm{\theta}\mid\mathbf{y})\propto p(\mathbf{y}\mid\bm{\theta})\,p(\bm{\theta}),

(1)

where
p(\mathbf{y}\mid\bm{\theta})
is the likelihood function and
p(\bm{\theta})
is the prior distribution. In many complex models, especially those involving stochastic simulators, the likelihood function
p(\mathbf{y}\mid\bm{\theta})
is intractable or computationally expensive to evaluate, rendering traditional inference methods like Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) algorithms
[
Andrieu et al. 2003
]
infeasible due to their reliance on explicit likelihood evaluations.

When the likelihood function is inaccessible, a popular strategy is to generate empirical samples using a stochastic simulator
\mathcal{M}(\bm{\theta},\bm{\xi})
that produces synthetic data
\mathbf{y}
given parameters
\bm{\theta}
and random variables
\bm{\xi}
representing system noise. Sampling
\bm{\theta}
from a prior distribution
p(\bm{\theta})
informed by domain knowledge or chosen to be noninformative and generating the corresponding
\mathbf{y}
through the simulator, we obtain samples from the joint distribution
p(\bm{\theta},\mathbf{y})
. These joint samples allow us to approximate the posterior distribution
p(\bm{\theta}\mid\mathbf{y})
using generative modeling techniques that learn the inverse mapping from observations
\mathbf{y}
to parameters
\bm{\theta}
without requiring explicit likelihood calculations.

3.1
ConDiSim: A Conditional Diffusion Framework for Simulation-Based Inference

We now introduce ConDiSim — a denoising conditional diffusion model
[
Ho et al. 2020
]
that systematically addresses the challenge of intractable likelihoods by leveraging samples from the joint distribution
p(\bm{\theta},\mathbf{y})
, enabling efficient and accurate posterior inference without the need for explicit likelihood evaluations. The model consists of a forward diffusion process that gradually adds Gaussian noise to the parameters
\bm{\theta}
, transforming them into a tractable noise distribution. The reverse process then denoises these parameters, conditioned on observed data
\mathbf{y}
, to approximate the posterior distribution
p(\bm{\theta}\mid\mathbf{y})
. Figure
1
provides a schematic overview of the ConDiSim model.

In the
forward diffusion process
, we construct a discrete-time Markov chain
\{\bm{\theta}_{t}\}_{t=0}^{T}
, initialized at
\bm{\theta}_{0}=\bm{\theta}
. At each step
t
, the state
\bm{\theta}_{t}
is derived from
\bm{\theta}_{t-1}
by perturbing it with Gaussian noise according to the transition kernel:

q(\bm{\theta}_{t}\mid\bm{\theta}_{t-1})=\mathcal{N}\big(\bm{\theta}_{t};\,\sqrt{1-\beta_{t}}\,\bm{\theta}_{t-1},\,\beta_{t}\mathbf{I}\big),

(2)

where
\beta_{t}\in(0,1)
determines the noise level at each step, and
\mathbf{I}
is the identity matrix. This step-wise transformation progressively corrupts
\bm{\theta}_{0}
, moving its mean towards zero and increasing its variance to unity, such that as
t\to T
, the state converges to
q(\bm{\theta}_{T})=\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I})
.
Alternatively,
\bm{\theta}_{t}
can be directly computed from
\bm{\theta}_{0}
using the marginal distribution:

q(\bm{\theta}_{t}\mid\bm{\theta}_{0})=\mathcal{N}\big(\bm{\theta}_{t};\,\sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_{t}}\,\bm{\theta}_{0},\,(1-\bar{\alpha}_{t})\mathbf{I}\big),

(3)

where
\bar{\alpha}_{t}=\prod_{s=1}^{t}(1-\beta_{s})
is the cumulative product of the noise scaling factors. This formulation allows efficient computation of
\bm{\theta}_{t}
at any step
t
, directly linking
\bm{\theta}_{t}
to
\bm{\theta}_{0}
. The joint forward distribution is given by:

q(\bm{\theta}_{1:T}\mid\bm{\theta}_{0})=q(\bm{\theta}_{T}\mid\bm{\theta}_{0})\prod_{t=2}^{T}q(\bm{\theta}_{t-1}\mid\bm{\theta}_{t},\bm{\theta}_{0}).

(4)

In the
reverse diffusion process
, latent states
\{\bm{\theta}_{t}\}_{t=T}^{0}
are denoised from
\bm{\theta}_{T}\sim\mathcal{N}(\bm{0},\bm{I})
to recover
\bm{\theta}_{0}
, with every step conditioned on observed data
\mathbf{y}

[
Saharia et al. 2021
]
. The joint reverse distribution is formulated as:

p(\bm{\theta}_{0:T}\mid\mathbf{y})=p(\bm{\theta}_{T})\,\prod_{t=1}^{T}p(\bm{\theta}_{t-1}\mid\bm{\theta}_{t},\mathbf{y}),

(5)

Training the reverse model involves maximizing the log
-
posterior of the latent variables given the observations:

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{ConDiSim}}=\max\log p(\bm{\theta}_{0}\mid\mathbf{y}),

(6)

\text{where,}\quad\log p(\bm{\theta}_{0}\mid\mathbf{y})=\log\int p(\bm{\theta}_{0:T}\mid\mathbf{y})\,d\bm{\theta}_{1:T}.

(7)

However, evaluating
\log p(\bm{\theta}_{0}\mid\mathbf{y})
directly is intractable, as it requires integrating over the full trajectory of intermediate latent states. To address this, we employ a variational approach by introducing the joint forward distribution
q(\bm{\theta}_{1:T}\mid\bm{\theta}_{0})
. Using Jensen’s inequality, the lower bound on the log-posterior is given by:

\log p(\bm{\theta}_{0}\mid\mathbf{y})\;\geq\;\mathbb{E}_{q(\bm{\theta}_{1:T}\mid\bm{\theta}_{0})}\!\biggl[\log\frac{p(\bm{\theta}_{0:T}\mid\mathbf{y})}{q(\bm{\theta}_{1:T}\mid\bm{\theta}_{0})}\biggr].

(8)

Applying Eqs. (
4
) and (
5
) to Eq. (
8
) leads to the following decomposition:

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{ConDiSim}}=\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{recon}}+\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{prior}}+\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{denoise}}

(9)

where, the reconstruction term,
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{recon}}=\mathbb{E}_{q}[\log p(\bm{\theta}_{0}\mid\bm{\theta}_{1},\mathbf{y})]
, evaluates how well the original parameters are recovered from the final denoised state. The prior term,
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{prior}}=-D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q(\bm{\theta}_{T}\mid\bm{\theta}_{0})\,\|\,p(\bm{\theta}_{T}))
, enforces consistency with the prior at the final noised step. The denoising term,
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{denoise}}=-\sum_{t=2}^{T}\mathbb{E}_{q}[D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q(\bm{\theta}_{t-1}\mid\bm{\theta}_{t},\bm{\theta}_{0})\,\|\,p(\bm{\theta}_{t-1}\mid\bm{\theta}_{t},\mathbf{y}))]
, aligns reverse transitions with the forward process. For sufficiently large
T
, the reconstruction and prior terms vanish, leaving the denoising KL as the dominant training objective:

\mathcal{L}=\sum_{t=2}^{T}\mathbb{E}_{q}\bigl[D_{\mathrm{KL}}\bigl(q(\bm{\theta}_{t-1}\mid\bm{\theta}_{t},\bm{\theta}_{0})\;\|\;p_{\phi}(\bm{\theta}_{t-1}\mid\bm{\theta}_{t},\mathbf{y})\bigr)\bigr].

(10)

The first term in the KL divergence,
q(\bm{\theta}_{t-1}\mid\bm{\theta}_{t},\bm{\theta}_{0})
, can be written using Bayes’ theorem as
q(\bm{\theta}_{t-1}\mid\bm{\theta}_{t},\bm{\theta}_{0})=\frac{q(\bm{\theta}_{t}\mid\bm{\theta}_{t-1},\bm{\theta}_{0})\,q(\bm{\theta}_{t-1}\mid\bm{\theta}_{0})}{q(\bm{\theta}_{t}\mid\bm{\theta}_{0})}
. From Eq. (
2
) and Eq. (
3
), each term is Gaussian, so the resulting distribution is also Gaussian:
q(\bm{\theta}_{t-1}\mid\bm{\theta}_{t},\bm{\theta}_{0})=\mathcal{N}(\bm{\theta}_{t-1};\bm{\mu}_{q},\bm{\Sigma}_{q})
, where:

\displaystyle\bm{\mu}_{q}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_{t-1}}(1-\alpha_{t})}{1-\bar{\alpha}_{t}}\,\bm{\theta}_{0}+\frac{\sqrt{\alpha_{t}}(1-\bar{\alpha}_{t-1})}{1-\bar{\alpha}_{t}}\,\bm{\theta}_{t},

(11)

\displaystyle\bm{\Sigma}_{q}

\displaystyle=\frac{(1-\bar{\alpha}_{t-1})\alpha_{t}}{1-\bar{\alpha}_{t}}\,\mathbf{I}.

To eliminate the explicit dependence on
\bm{\theta}_{0}
, we substitute
\bm{\theta}_{0}=(\bm{\theta}_{t}-\sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_{t}}\,\bm{\epsilon})/\sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_{t}}
(with
\bm{\epsilon}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I})
) into Eq. (
3
), thereby reparameterizing
\bm{\mu}_{q}
to yield a noise-parameterized expression for the mean:

\displaystyle\bm{\mu}_{q}(\bm{\theta}_{t},t)

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_{t}}}\Bigl(\bm{\theta}_{t}-\tfrac{\beta_{t}}{\sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_{t}}}\,\bm{\epsilon}\Bigr).

(12)

Assuming the learned reverse process preserves the covariance structure (
\bm{\Sigma}_{p}=\bm{\Sigma}_{q}
), the KL divergence in Eq. (
10
) reduces to the squared Euclidean distance between the means:

\mathcal{L}=\sum_{t=2}^{T}\mathbb{E}_{q(\bm{\theta}_{1:T}\mid\bm{\theta}_{0})}\Bigl[\big\lVert\bm{\mu}_{q}(\bm{\theta}_{t},\bm{\theta}_{0})\;-\;\bm{\mu}_{p}(\bm{\theta}_{t},\mathbf{y},t)\big\rVert^{2}\Bigr].

(13)

From Eq. (
12
), the mean of learned reverse process can be expressed as:

\displaystyle\bm{\mu}_{p}(\bm{\theta}_{t},\mathbf{y},t)

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_{t}}}\Bigl(\bm{\theta}_{t}-\tfrac{\beta_{t}}{\sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_{t}}}\,\bm{\epsilon}_{\phi}(\bm{\theta}_{t},\mathbf{y},t)\Bigr),

(14)

where,
\bm{\epsilon}_{\phi}(\bm{\theta}_{t},\mathbf{y},t)
is neural network that, given the noisy latent
\bm{\theta}_{t}
, conditioning
\mathbf{y}
, and time step
t
, predicts the noise. Substituting the mean parameterizations from Eqs.(
12
) and (
14
) into the loss of Eq.(
13
) and discarding all constant factors, yields the compact noise–prediction objective:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{ConDiSim}}=\mathbb{E}_{\,t,\,\bm{\epsilon},\,(\bm{\theta}_{0},\mathbf{y})\sim p(\theta,y)}\Bigl[\big\lVert\bm{\epsilon}-\bm{\epsilon}_{\phi}(\bm{\theta}_{t},\mathbf{y},t)\big\rVert^{2}\Bigr].

(15)

Figure 1:
The ConDiSim Model:
(a) The forward diffusion process incrementally adds Gaussian noise to the parameter vector (
\bm{\theta}
), generating a sequence of latent states (
\{\bm{\theta}_{t}\}
); (b) The reverse diffusion process reconstructs the posterior distribution (
p(\bm{\theta}\mid\mathbf{y})
) by leveraging the observed data (
\mathbf{y}
) for denoising.

3.2
Amortized Inference with ConDiSim

In our framework, we implement the reverse diffusion process as an instance of amortized inference. For a given observation
\mathbf{y_{o}}
, the reverse process is initialized by drawing
N
latent variables
\bm{\theta}_{T}^{(i)}
from the standard normal distribution
\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I})
for
i=1,\dots,N
. These latent samples are then conditioned on
\mathbf{y_{o}}
and iteratively refined through the learned reverse diffusion process. Specifically, at each reverse time step, the transition is characterized by,

\bm{\theta}_{t-1}^{(i)}\mid\bm{\theta}_{t}^{(i)},\bm{y_{o}}\sim\mathcal{N}\!\left(\bm{\mu}_{\phi}\!\left(\bm{\theta}_{t}^{(i)},\mathbf{y_{o}},t\right),\,\bm{\Sigma}_{t}\right),

where
\bm{\mu}_{\phi}
and
\bm{\Sigma}_{t}
represent the learned mean and covariance parameters that determine the probabilistic mapping between consecutive latent states in the reverse process. After iterating from
t=T
down to
t=0
, the process yields refined latent samples
\bm{\theta}_{0}^{(i)}
that approximate the target posterior distribution
p(\bm{\theta}\mid\mathbf{y_{o}})
. This method efficiently produces high-quality posterior samples suitable for applications such as parameter estimation and uncertainty quantification.

Benchmark Posterior Analysis

In this section, we analyze three representative benchmarks—
Two Moons
,
SLCP
, and
SLCP Distractors
—selected for their distinct challenges in posterior inference. The Two Moons benchmark poses the challenge of capturing a bi-modal posterior with a distinctive crescent geometry. As depicted in Figure
2(a)
, ConDiSim accurately recovers both modes and the complex posterior structure. The ECDF plots for
\theta_{1}
and
\theta_{2}
(Figure
2(b)
) demonstrate that the inferred distributions are well-calibrated, with the majority of rank statistics falling within the 90% confidence bands; a minor deviation for
\theta_{1}
suggests a slight degree of overconfidence.

The SLCP benchmark encompasses a range of parameter behaviors - including uniform, Gaussian, multi-modal, and skewed distributions. Its variant, SLCP Distractors, further tests the model’s robustness by incorporating uninformative noise dimensions, thereby simulating scenarios with heavily corrupted data. Figures
3(a)
and
3(b)
illustrate that ConDiSim is able to capture these complex posterior landscapes with high fidelity, and also effectively isolate the informative parameters from the distractors. The rank ECDF plots for both SLCP variants (Figures
14(a)
and
14(b)
in the Appendix) also remain centered within the confidence intervals, confirming that the model reliably captures parameter variability even under noisy conditions. Detailed descriptions of these benchmarks, are provided in Appendix
A
.

4.2
Application to Real-World Scenarios

We evaluate the performance of ConDiSim on two real-world simulation models. First, the
Hodgkin-Huxley (HH)
model
[
Gloeckler et al. 2024
]
which simulates neuronal action potentials by modeling the membrane voltage dynamics governed by sodium, potassium, and leak currents. Second, we consider the
Genetic Oscillator
model which is a high-dimensional gene regulatory network that exhibits oscillatory behavior, driven by a positive-negative feedback loop mimicking a circadian clock
[
Vilar et al. 2002
]
. The model encompasses 9 species undergoing 18 reactions, parameterized by 15 reaction rate constants. Although the full system involves multiple interacting components, we focus on the inference of only three species (C, A, R).
Detailed description is available in Appendix
A
.

Unlike the SBI benchmarking problems, the true posterior distributions for these real-world models are unknown. To assess ConDiSim, we use well motivated parameter combinations from literature to generate corresponding synthetic observations (e.g., parameter combination resulting in stable oscillations in case of the genetic oscillator). These observations serve as the observed data for inference, enabling us to evaluate the inferred posterior by comparing its concentration around the true parameter value. We also perform a posterior predictive check to validate the model. In this procedure, five posterior samples are randomly selected and used to simulate corresponding observations, which are then compared with the observed data. This check provides an additional measure of the consistency and reliability of the inferred posteriors.

For the HH model, Figure
4(a)
shows that ConDiSim effectively infers a posterior distribution concentrated around the true parameter values, while Figure
4(b)
confirms that the simulated voltage trajectories closely match the reference signals.
The parameter estimation task for the genetic oscillator is more challenging due to broad and varied parameter priors. Figure
5(a)
indicates that the peak of the posterior distribution is near the true parameter values. Figure
5(b)
illustrates both the true and generated trajectories for three selected species, obtained by randomly sampling three posterior sets and plotting the corresponding simulations. Initially, these trajectories align well with the true data, with slight deviations emerging over time due to the inherent stochastic nature of the model. Figure
7
(Appendix) depicts multiple trajectories simulated using the true parameter combination - which closely match the inferred results. In both cases, the trajectories are very close to each other during initial time steps, and develop small deviations owing to model stochasticity as time steps evolve. The plots highlight the system’s inherent variability, and also validating the inferred solution.

5.2
Scalability

ConDiSim’s scalability was extensively evaluated across diverse benchmark problems, spanning from low-dimensional (Two Moons, GMM, SIR; 2 parameters) to higher-dimensional scenarios (Gaussian Linear, Bernoulli GLM; 10 parameters, HH Model; 7 parameters, genetic oscillator; 15 parameters). From the C2ST metric values in Table
1
, and the posterior plots spanning from Figure
2(a)
to Figure
13
, it can be seen that ConDiSim maintained the posterior approximation accuracy with increasing problem dimensionality and complexity. Model training times in Table
2
also show that ConDiSim is computationally efficient to train and scales favorably with problem complexity, making it suitable for high-dimensional SBI tasks. Its ability to handle larger parameter spaces without significant loss of performance is a key advantage, as evidenced in the genetic oscillator test problem involving 15 parameters.

5.3
Comparative Evaluation of different Inference Methods

Tables
1
and
4
indicate that diffusion models (Simformer, ConDiSim) generally outperform flow-based (NPE, APT) and GAN-based (GATSBI) approaches in accuracy, owing to their ability to model complex posteriors. Simformer achieves more accurate posterior approximations in most test problems (also see Table
4
) owing to its complex architecture, however it also entails longer training times due to the involved complexity (Table
2
). In contrast, ConDiSim’s simplified conditioning mechanism and architecture reduces training complexity, and its iterative denoising process does not impede inference speed in our experiments. The known challenges of adversarial training affect GATSBI, while APT’s sequential nature increases its computational cost relative to NPE.

5.4
Robustness

Robustness to observational noise is a critical factor in the practical application of simulation-based inference (SBI) methods. To rigorously evaluate this aspect, we conducted experiments on two challenging tasks: the SLCP Distractors and the Bernoulli GLM Raw. The SLCP Distractors task introduces 92 non-informative variables to the observations, significantly increasing the inference complexity. In contrast, the Bernoulli GLM Raw task utilizes raw data instead of well-behaved summary statistics, presenting a more realistic scenario often encountered in real-world applications. The results for the SLCP Distractors and Bernoulli GLM RAW, illustrated in Table
1
and Figures
3(b)
,
12(b)
demonstrate ConDiSim’s robustness to both observational noise. Such resilience is important as it indicates ConDiSim’s capacity to effectively handle real-world data, which is often characterized by measurement errors and inherent uncertainties.

5.5
Reliability

To further validate the learned inference model, we employed Simulation-Based Calibration (SBC) as a rigorous diagnostic for assessing the quality of approximate posterior distributions. The empirical cumulative distribution function (ECDF) plots demonstrate ConDiSim’s ability to produce accurate and well-calibrated posteriors, with rank statistics closely aligning along the diagonal—indicative of unbiased and reliable inference. This alignment supports the statistical validity and robustness of ConDiSim’s inferences across diverse and challenging scenarios.

6
Conclusion

In this work, we introduced ConDiSim, a denoising conditional diffusion model for simulation-based inference. We demonstrated ConDiSim’s ability to effectively capture complex posterior distributions efficiently on 10 benchmark problems and 2 real-world test problems. ConDiSim performs comparably to the state-of-the-art while being substantially faster to train. As part of an ablation study, we also explored the significance of the variance schedule and the number of diffusion steps in influencing model performance. ConDiSim offers a flexible and efficient approach to simulation-based inference, with potential applications across various scientific and engineering domains.
Future work includes exploring adaptive variance schedules, integrating domain-specific knowledge into the model, and extending ConDiSim towards learning both the likelihood and posterior distributions.

Appendix A
SBI Problem Description

We present the general setup for simulation-based inference (SBI), which is employed across various benchmarking problems to estimate the posterior distribution
p(\bm{\theta}|\mathbf{y})
over parameters
\bm{\theta}
, given observed data
\mathbf{y}
. As outlined in
[
Lueckmann et al. 2021
]
, the framework for SBI problems typically includes the following components:

•

Prior Distribution:
A prior distribution
p(\bm{\theta})
that defines the initial beliefs about the parameter vector before any data is observed. This distribution serves as the starting point for the inference process.

•

Simulation Model:
A stochastic simulation model
\mathcal{M}
that allows empirical evaluation of the likelihood function
p(\mathbf{y}|\bm{\theta})
, illustrating how synthetic data
\mathbf{y}
is generated given specific parameter values
\bm{\theta}
. This model encapsulates the underlying mechanisms of the problem and provides samples from the joint distribution
p(\bm{\theta},\mathbf{y})
, enabling inference without direct likelihood computation.

•

Dimensionality:
The dimensionality of both the parameter vector
\bm{\theta}
and the observed data
\mathbf{y}
is crucial for parameter inference. It can vary greatly between problems, and directly impacts the complexity and computational cost of the inference task. High-dimensional spaces often present greater challenges in accurately estimating the posterior distribution.

A.1
Two Moons

This parameter inference task is designed to test the performance of inference methods under multi-modal conditions, featuring a posterior distribution with both global (bi-modal) and local (crescent-shaped) characteristics.

•

Prior:

\bm{\theta}\sim\mathcal{U}(-1,1)

•

Simulator:

\mathbf{y}|\bm{\theta}=r\begin{bmatrix}\cos(\alpha)\\
\sin(\alpha)\end{bmatrix}+0.25\begin{bmatrix}-|\theta_{1}+\theta_{2}|/\sqrt{2}\\
(-\theta_{1}+\theta_{2})/\sqrt{2}\end{bmatrix},

where
\alpha\sim\mathcal{U}(-\pi/2,\pi/2)
and
r\sim\mathcal{N}(0.1,0.01^{2})
.

•

Dimensionality:

\bm{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{2},\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}

A.2
Gaussian Mixture

This task challenges inference methods to estimate the shared mean of a mixture of Gaussian distributions with distinct covariances, thereby assessing their performance in handling multi-modal and overlapping data distributions.

•

Prior:

\bm{\theta}\sim\mathcal{U}(-10,10)

•

Simulator:

\mathbf{y}|\bm{\theta}=0.5\left(\mathcal{N}(\bm{\theta},\mathbf{I})+\mathcal{N}(\bm{\theta},0.01\mathbf{I})\right)

•

Dimensionality:

\bm{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{2},\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}

A.3
Gaussian Linear

This task involves estimating the mean vector
\bm{\theta}
of a multivariate Gaussian distribution with a fixed and known covariance matrix. Since both the prior and the likelihood are Gaussian, they form a conjugate pair, resulting in a Gaussian posterior distribution. This conjugacy makes the task an ideal benchmark for evaluating inference methods, as it allows for a clear assessment of their ability to accurately capture known posterior distributions.

•

Prior:

\bm{\theta}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},0.1\mathbf{I})

•

Simulator:
Generates data
\mathbf{y}
using
\mathbf{y}|\bm{\theta}\sim\mathcal{N}(\bm{\theta},0.1\mathbf{I})

•

Dimensionality:

\bm{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{10},\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{10}

A.4
Gaussian Linear Uniform

This task is a variant of the Gaussian Linear problem, where the mean parameter
\bm{\theta}
is assigned a uniform prior distribution
\bm{\theta}\sim\mathcal{U}(-1,1)
instead of a Gaussian prior. The introduction of a uniform prior imposes bounded support on the parameters, altering the posterior distribution from a Gaussian to a truncated Gaussian. This modification tests the inference method’s ability to handle non-Gaussian priors and to accurately capture the resulting posterior distributions.

A.5
SLCP

The SLCP task presents a more challenging inference scenario where, despite the simplicity of the likelihood function, the posterior distribution exhibits complex, non-linear dependencies among parameters. The parameter vector
\bm{\theta}
is five-dimensional, and the simulator generates two-dimensional data points by applying non-linear transformations to
\bm{\theta}
. This setup assesses the inference method’s capability to accurately capture intricate posterior structures arising from non-linear relationships between parameters and observations.

•

Prior:

\bm{\theta}\sim\mathcal{U}(-3,3)

•

Simulator:
Outputs data
\mathbf{y}|\bm{\theta}=(y_{1},\ldots,y_{4})
, where:

y_{i}\sim\mathcal{N}\left(\begin{bmatrix}\theta_{1}\\
\theta_{2}\end{bmatrix},\begin{bmatrix}\theta_{3}^{2}&\tanh(\theta_{5})\theta_{3}\theta_{4}\\
\tanh(\theta_{5})\theta_{3}\theta_{4}&\theta_{4}^{2}\end{bmatrix}\right)

•

Dimensionality:

\bm{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{5},\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{8}

A.6
SLCP with Distractors

An extension of the SLCP task that incorporates additional non-informative dimensions (distractors) into the observed data. This setup evaluates the robustness of inference methods when faced with high-dimensional data containing redundant information.

•

Prior:

\bm{\theta}\sim\mathcal{U}(-3,3)

•

Simulator:
Outputs data
y|\theta=(y_{1},\ldots,y_{100})
, where a permutation function
p(y)
reorders
y
with a fixed random permutation.

•

Dimensionality:

This task involves performing inference in a Generalized Linear Model (GLM) with Bernoulli-distributed observations and a 10-dimensional parameter vector

\beta
and the function f, promoting smoothness in the parameter estimates. This setup evaluates the inference method’s ability to handle discrete data and accurately capture the posterior distribution in models with latent smoothness constraints.

A.11
Hodgkin-Huxley Model

The Hodgkin-Huxley (HH) model describes the generation and propagation of action potentials in neurons by modeling the membrane voltage
V(t)
as influenced by ionic currents through voltage-gated sodium (
\text{Na}^{+}
), potassium (
\text{K}^{+}
), and leak channels. We adopt the formulation from
[
Gloeckler et al. 2024
]
. The model is given by the following set of stochastic differential equations:

\frac{dV}{dt}=\frac{I_{\text{inj}}(t)-g_{\text{Na}}m^{3}h(V-E_{\text{Na}})-g_{\text{K}}n^{4}(V-E_{\text{K}})-g_{\text{L}}(V-E_{\text{L}})}{C_{m}}+0.05\,dw_{t},

(18)

where
dw_{t}
represents a Gaussian white noise term that captures stochastic fluctuations in the membrane potential.

The gating variables
m(t),h(t),n(t)
follow first-order kinetics:

\displaystyle\frac{dm}{dt}

\displaystyle=\alpha_{m}(V)(1-m)-\beta_{m}(V)m,

(19)

\displaystyle\frac{dh}{dt}

\displaystyle=\alpha_{h}(V)(1-h)-\beta_{h}(V)h,

(20)

\displaystyle\frac{dn}{dt}

\displaystyle=\alpha_{n}(V)(1-n)-\beta_{n}(V)n,

(21)

The voltage-dependent rate functions
\alpha_{x}(V)
and
\beta_{x}(V)(x\in\{m,h,n\})
defined as follows:

\alpha_{m}(V)=0.32\times\frac{\text{efun}(-0.25(V-V_{0}-13.0))}{0.25},\quad\beta_{m}(V)=0.28\times\frac{\text{efun}(0.2(V-V_{0}-40.0))}{0.2},

\alpha_{h}(V)=0.128\times\exp\left(\frac{-(V-V_{0}-17.0)}{18.0}\right),\quad\beta_{h}(V)=\frac{4.0}{1+\exp\left(\frac{-(V-V_{0}-40.0)}{5.0}\right)},

\alpha_{n}(V)=0.032\times\frac{\text{efun}(-0.2(V-V_{0}-15.0))}{0.2},\quad\beta_{n}(V)=0.5\times\exp\left(\frac{-(V-V_{0}-10.0)}{40.0}\right).

Here,
\text{efun}(x)
is defined to ensure numerical stability when evaluating small arguments:

\text{efun}(x)=\begin{cases}1-\frac{x}{2}&\text{if }x<1\text{e-4},\\
\frac{x}{\exp(x)-1.0}&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

The metabolic cost
H(t)
, representing the energy consumption due to sodium channel activity, is modeled as:

\frac{dH}{dt}=g_{\text{Na}}m^{3}h(V-E_{\text{Na}}).

(22)

•

Parameters:
The parameter vector is
\bm{\theta}=(C_{m},g_{\text{Na}},g_{\text{K}},g_{\text{L}},E_{\text{Na}},E_{\text{K}},E_{\text{L}})
, where
C_{m}
is the membrane capacitance,
g_{\text{Na}},g_{\text{K}},g_{\text{L}}
are the conductances for sodium, potassium, and leak currents, and
E_{\text{Na}},E_{\text{K}},E_{\text{L}}
are the corresponding reversal potentials.

•

Prior:
The parameters
\bm{\theta}
are sampled from a uniform distribution:

\bm{\theta}\sim\mathcal{U}\left([1.0,60,10,0.1,40,-100,-90],[2.0,120,30,0.5,70,-60,-60]\right).

•

Simulator:
The HH model is simulated for 200 ms. An input current of
I_{\text{inj}}(t)=4\,\text{mA}
is applied between
50
ms and
150
ms. The voltage equation is integrated with added Gaussian white noise
(0.05\,dw_{t})
. To reduce dimensionality, the raw voltage trace is summarized into a set of statistics:

–

Spike count,

–

Mean and standard deviation of the resting potential,

–

Mean voltage during spiking,

–

Higher-order moments (normalized skewness and kurtosis) of the voltage response,

–

Total energy consumption due to sodium current.

•

Dimensionality:
The parameter space is
\bm{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{7}
, and the output summary statistics
\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{8}
.

•

Fixed Parameters:
The initial membrane voltage is
V_{0}=-65.0\,\text{mV}
, with gating variables initialized to their steady-state values at
V_{0}
. Simulations use a time resolution of
5000\,\text{steps}
over
200\,\text{ms}
simulation window.

In this section, we describe the hyperparameters and simulation environments employed across all methods. Training data for the SBI benchmark problems were generated using the sbibm
[
Lueckmann et al. 2021
]
, except for the LV and SIR models, which were generated with the BayesFlow framework
[
Radev et al. 2023b
]
. For the HH model, we adopt the implementation provided by
[
Gloeckler et al. 2024
]

[
Akesson et al. 2022
]
. In this model, the simulated data is high dimensional, with a shape of
(N\times 3\times 200)
representing three species observed over 200 timesteps. We compress this data using an autoencoder whose latent space is set to match the number of simulator parameters. For Simformer, we use hyperparameters published in their original works
[
Gloeckler et al. 2024
]
. For GATSBI, task-specific hyperparameters were taken from
[
Ramesh et al. 2022
]
and adjusted as needed based on each problem’s structure and computational demands.

For NPE, we follow
[
Lueckmann et al. 2021
]
, which evaluated NPE across ten benchmark problems. As reported in Appendix H of sbibm paper, Neural Spline Flows (NSF) generally outperform Masked Autoregressive Flows (MAF), originally recommended for NPE. Consequently, we use the NSF implementation from the
sbi
package
[
Tejero-Cantero et al. 2020
]
as the density estimator in all experiments. The implementation of Automatic Posterior Transformation (APT, or SNPE-C) is also taken from the
sbi
package; as a sequential method, the simulation budget per round is computed by dividing the total budget by the number of rounds. For time series problems, a summary network is recommended for NPE/APT
[
Zeghal et al. 2022
]
. Accordingly, we modify and optimize the standard
CNNEmbedding
from the
sbi
package to convert the data into summary statistics with dimensions matching the parameters (4 for the LV test problem and 2 for the SIR task).

For the
ConDiSim
model, we optimized the hyperparameters for each of the ten benchmark problems from the
sbibm
library, as detailed in Table

. Common hyperparameters were applied uniformly across all tasks to ensure training consistency and stability. We used the

optimizer with a learning rate of
0.001
and a weight decay of
0.0001
, along with a
ReduceLROnPlateau
scheduler to decrease the learning rate by a factor of
0.5
if validation loss did not improve over five consecutive epochs. The noise schedule followed a cosine/quadratic scheme, with
\beta_{\text{min}}
as
0.0001
and
\beta_{\text{max}}
as
0.02
, to regulate progressive noise addition during diffusion. Data standardization was conducted using

, and an

20

M=500
prior samples, simulate corresponding observations, and draw
L=250

[
Säilynoja et al. 2022

A uniform ECDF indicates well-calibrated posteriors, while deviations suggest miscalibration: curves above the diagonal indicate overconfidence, and those below indicate underconfidence. Using

[

Appendix F
SBI Metrics

F.1
Limitations of the C2ST Metric

The Classifier Two-Sample Test (C2ST) evaluates the similarity between a generated posterior
P
and the true posterior
Q
by training a classifier to differentiate their samples. A score near 0.5 indicates that the two distributions are similar, while higher values indicate discrepancies. Although widely used in SBI literature for its straightforward interpretability—providing a clear score between 0.5 and 1—the metric can be overly sensitive, as a single poorly estimated dimension may distort the results. Furthermore, its reliability depends significantly on the design and training of the classifier.

F.2
MMD

To overcome these limitations, we also consider the Maximum Mean Discrepancy (MMD), which measures the distance between the mean embeddings of
P
and
Q
in a reproducing kernel Hilbert space (RKHS). MMD is defined as,

\mathrm{MMD}^{2}(P,Q;\mathcal{H})=\left\|\mathbb{E}_{x\sim P}[\phi(x)]-\mathbb{E}_{y\sim Q}[\phi(y)]\right\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2},

(23)

where
\phi
is a feature mapping into the RKHS
\mathcal{H}
. A low MMD value indicates that the generated posterior closely approximates the true posterior. Unlike C2ST, MMD provides a robust, holistic measure of similarity between distributions without being overly influenced by discrepancies in a single dimension. A low MMD value indicates that the distributions
P
and
Q
are similar, while a larger value suggests significant differences.
