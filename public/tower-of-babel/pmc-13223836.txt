Screening of strategies for enhancing and preserving antibacterial efficacy: optimizing inactivation and desiccation of black soldier fly larvae against avian enteric pathogens
Authors: Jianwei Hao, Lixiu Li, Yuanyuan Yang, Chujun Li, Shuang Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Chicken manure is widely used for rearing black soldier fly larvae (BSFL). The effects of chicken manure-borne pathogens (Escherichia coli and Salmonella pullorum) and subsequent larval processing treatments on antimicrobial peptide expression in BSFL remain poorly clarified. Here, BSFL were exposed to single or combined inoculation of Escherichia coli (E. coli) and Salmonella pullorum (S. p). Compared with the control group, all pathogen-inoculated groups showed significant increases in larval weight and body length. Each group was further divided by stimulation type into three sub-treatments: unstimulated, heat-stimulated (Hs), and Microwave-stimulated (Ws). At 6 h after stimulation, the expression of defensin and cecropin, as well as antibacterial activity were determined. Under unstimulated conditions, 11 of 16 infection groups exhibited lower gene expression fold changes than uninfected control. No sample inhibited E. coli, while 7 of 16 suppressed S. p. Both heat and microwave stimulation markedly upregulated defensin and cecropin expression. All stimulated samples strongly inhibited S. p; most also showed antibacterial activity against E. coli (16 Ws, 14 Hs). Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis indicated that defensin ΔCt (sample Ct minus reference Ct) was negatively correlated with the inhibition diameter of E. coli (r = −0.4381), and could effectively discriminated antibacterial efficacy against both pathogens. A unified cutoff value was identified at 2.43. Larval pretreatment with 1 % starch-5 % sucrose-0.1 % Tween-80 before drying optimally retained anti-S. p activity, with a maximum inhibition diameter of 2.65 mm in the Sp2E4 — Ws treatment. This study provides theoretical and technical guidance for BSFL-antibacterial-protein production.

Introduction

Avian enteric pathogens, especially avian pathogenic Escherichia coli (E. coli) and Salmonella pullorum (S. p), pose a persistent, multifaceted threat to the global poultry industry and public health (Saini et al., 2026). These pathogens disrupt poultry intestinal health, reducing productivity and causing economic losses. As foodborne threats, they transmit to humans via poultry products and induce gastrointestinal and systemic diseases. (Sharma et al., 2025; Ruiz et al., 2025). Widespread restrictions on antibiotic use have been enforced in livestock farming, making novel natural antimicrobials imperative for sustainable animal production. Of these candidates, insect-derived antimicrobial peptides (AMPs) display broad-spectrum antibacterial activity, low risk of antibiotic resistance, and excellent biocompatibility. (Xia et al., 2021; Bucataru and Ciobanasu, 2024). As a typical insect resource, black soldier fly larvae (BSFL, Hermetia illucens L., Diptera: Stratiomyidae) possess great potential to inhibit avian enteric pathogens. (Xia et al., 2021; Szczepanik et al., 2024).

BSFL are saprophytic insects with a remarkable capacity for organic waste bioconversion. Poultry manure, a dominant substrate for BSFL bioprocessing, can be converted into high-value protein biomass by BSFL (Zhao et al., 2023; Liu et al., 2022). More importantly, BSFL endogenously produce multiple AMPs, among which defensins and cecropins predominate. These two core peptides exert strong inhibitory activity against avian pathogenic E. coli and S. p. (Luo et al., 2024). However, several critical scientific questions remain poorly addressed. Whether varying concentrations of E. coli and S. p derived from poultry manure affect the growth, AMP synthesis, and antibacterial capacity of BSFL remains unclear, especially under combined pathogen co-exposure. Clarifying this issue is essential, since dose-dependent data of single and combined pathogen exposure can guide the targeted production of nutritional and functional BSFL protein.

Furthermore, AMP expression in BSFL can be triggered by multiple stimuli, including bacterial injection, body stab wounding, and superficial thermal injury. Nakagawa et al. (2023) evaluated AMP synthesis responses of BSFL to these approaches and confirmed thermal injury as an efficient induction strategy. Compared with injection and stab wounding, thermal injury is safer and operationally simpler. The authors established a standardized thermal injury protocol: heating a pin on a gas stove for 3 s, followed by 1 s contact with the larval thorax to induce epidermal injury. While this method is operationally simple, several critical issues remain unresolved. It is unclear how to integrate it into large-scale BSFL production with defined quantitative temperature parameters, and whether standardized temperature control can sustain its AMP-inducing efficacy. Furthermore, the feasibility of extending the thermal injury mechanism to microwave inactivation also warrants further exploration.

Loss of antibacterial activity during desiccation is a major industrial bottleneck. Drying at high temperatures directly disrupts antimicrobial peptide conformation and induces degradation. Severe dehydration promotes peptide aggregation. Moreover, uneven temperature distribution caused by BSFL stacking further accelerates activity loss. Starch, sucrose, and Tween 80 provide potential solutions for the stability and retention of antimicrobial peptides, relying on their distinct physicochemical properties (Zhu et al., 2017; Chang et al., 2005; Li et al., 2024; Starciuc et al., 2020). It remains unclear whether immersion in different cryoprotectant solutions before drying can preserve BSFL AMPs.

To explore potential solutions for key bottlenecks in the development of BSFL antimicrobial preparations, this study establishes a integrated research framework with three core objectives. First, it characterizes the responses of BSFL growth and antimicrobial peptide gene expression to single and combined challenges of E. coli and S. p. under sterile culture. Second, a novel two-step inactivation strategy is established based on thermal injury mechanisms to replace routine one-step treatment, and microwave treatment is adopted to achieve comprehensive thermal damage effects. Third, a cost-effective protective formulation prior to low-temperature drying is explored to sustain the antibacterial activity of BSFL during dehydration. Collectively, these strategies establish an integrated technical system to enhance and preserve the antibacterial efficacy of BSFL against avian enteric pathogens, achieving full-process regulation from induction to industrial preparation.

Materials and methods

Bacterial preparation and insect rearing

Black soldier fly (BSF) eggs were purchased from Wuliang Biotechnical Co., Ltd. (Guangzhou, China). E. coli (Gram-negative, CVCC: 1569 strain) and S. p (Gram-negative, CVCC: 533) were separately inoculated into 10 mL of Luria-Bertani (LB) broth (1 % tryptone, 0.5 % yeast extract, 0.5 % NaCl) and incubated at 37°C for 24 h with constant shaking. A 1 mL aliquot of each bacterial culture was transferred to fresh LB broth and incubated at 37°C until the optical density (OD) at 600 nm reached 1.0, at which point the cultures were used for experimental treatments.

BSF eggs were hatched, and larvae were reared on Gainesville (GV) diet (50 % wheat bran, 30 % alfalfa meal, 20 % corn meal, dry mass ratio) supplemented with 2 mL water per gram of diet, until 4 days old. The 4-day-old larvae were subjected to sequential surface disinfection with 75 % ethanol, rinsed thoroughly with sterile normal saline, and air-dried prior to allocation into 16 experimental groups, with three biological replicates per group. All experimental procedures were conducted in a laminar flow cabinet, where larvae of each group were reared throughout the experiment; room temperature was maintained at 25°C via air conditioning to prevent exogenous microbial contamination. Each replicate consisted of 1 kg autoclaved GV feed inoculated with 0.5 g of the disinfected 4-day-old BSFL, and the experimental grouping and sub-treatment design are listed in Table 1. Each group included three biological replicates. Insect Unstimulated and Stimulation Treatments

At 14 days of age, larvae were separated and divided into three aliquots; one aliquot was maintained under sterile conditions without feed for 6 h as unstimulated (Us)(Table 1) larvae. The unstimulated larvae were killed by exposure to dry heat at 120°C for 1 min in a hot-air oven, as optimized for rapid inactivation with minimal protein degradation.

To determine the optimal duration of thermal and microwave exposure that minimizes larval mortality while inducing targeted injury, independent preliminary experiments were conducted to evaluate larval survival rates under different treatment durations (see Fig. S1). The results revealed that short-term treatment with high temperature (120°C, 5 s) and microwave radiation (4 W/g, 3 s) maintained high larval survival rates, thereby supporting the follow-up experiments.

High-temperature stimulation treatment

To induce thermal injury to the larval epidermis, the oven was preheated to 120°C. Sealed circular aluminum boxes (5 cm diameter) were placed in the oven until the box internal temperature stabilized at 120°C. One-third of the 14-day-old larvae from each group were individually transferred into the preheated boxes and subjected to thermal exposure for 5 s. Following treatment, larvae were immediately collected and incubated at 25 ± 1 °C in a sterile environment for 6 h for high-temperature stimulation (Hs)(Table 1). Larvae were inactivated by exposure to dry heat at 120°C for 1 min in a hot-air oven.

Microwave stimulation treatment

To induce internal thermal injury to larvae (targeting internal tissues rather than the superficial epidermis), microwave power was calibrated to 4 W/g based on the average body weight of the larvae. One-third of the 14-day-old larvae from each group were individually placed in sterile Petri dishes and exposed to the calibrated microwave radiation for 3 s. After microwave treatment, larvae were immediately transferred out of the Petri dishes and maintained under optimal rearing conditions (temperature: 25 ± 1°C) for 6 h as microwave treatment (Ws)(Table 1). Larvae were inactivated by exposure to dry heat at 120°C for 1 min in a hot-air oven.

Hemolymph and fat body collection

Hemolymph and fat body were collected from larvae in groups from the Hs, Ws, and Us. Briefly, larvae were surface-sterilized with 70 % ethanol and punctured between the eighth and ninth abdominal segments using microscissors or a sterile capillary. Hemolymph was collected into ice-cold tubes with l-ascorbic acid to prevent melanization, centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 10 min at 4°C, and the supernatant was stored at −80°C until use (Nakagawa et al., 2023; Scieuzo et al., 2023). White fat body was collected simultaneously. Each hemolymph sample (from 50 larvae) was used for antimicrobial activity assays, with three independent replicates per sub-treatment. Each fat body sample (from 50 larvae) was subjected to total RNA extraction and RT-qPCR analysis, with three independent replicates per sub-treatment.

Expression analysis of defensin and cecropin genes

RNA isolation and cDNA synthesis

Total RNA was extracted using the FastPure Complex Tissue/Cell Total RNA Isolation Kit (Vazyme, Nanjing, China) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, the fat bodies were ground to a fine powder in liquid nitrogen; 600 μL pre-mixed Buffer SRL was added to 30 mg tissue, and the lysate was vortexed until homogeneous. After centrifugation at 12,000 rpm (13,400 × g) for 3–5 min, the supernatant was mixed with 0.5 × volume of absolute ethanol, loaded onto FastPure RNA Columns VI, and centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 30 sec with the flow-through discarded. The column was washed with 700 μL Buffer RWA and 500 μL Buffer RWB, followed by centrifugation at 12,000 rpm for 30 sec and 2 min, respectively. RNA was eluted with 20 μL RNase-free ddH₂O into a new 1.5 mL RNase-free tube after 1 min incubation and centrifugation at 12,000 rpm for 1 min. Total RNA quality and concentration were assessed using a NanoOne-s spectrophotometer (Yooning, Hangzhou, China). For cDNA synthesis, 0.8–1 μg of total RNA was reverse-transcribed using the HiScript III 1st Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Vazyme, Nanjing, China) in a 20 μL reaction volume following the kit protocol. The resulting cDNA was used as templates for quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR).

Quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR)

Expression levels of the antimicrobial peptide genes defensin and cecropin were analyzed using a LightCycler Real-Time PCR System (Roche, Switzerland). Primers for defensin and cecropin were designed based on transcriptome-predicted AMP sequences (Candian et al., 2023):Defensin (forward: 5′-TCGTCCCATGGCAATACAAT-3′; reverse: 5′-TAGTGGAGCAGCATTATCGGG-3′),Cecropin (forward: 5′-GGTCAAAGCGAAGCTGGTT-3′; reverse: 5′-TGCCAGAACATTGGCTCCTT-3′).

Relative gene expression was calculated by the 2⁻ΔΔCt method and presented as fold change relative to the corresponding control group.

Effects of different stimulations on antibacterial activity of larval peptide fraction

AMP extraction from hemolymph

To separate putative AMPs from high-molecular-weight proteins in hemolymph samples, plasma was subjected to organic solvent precipitation with a methanol-aceticacid-water mixture (90:1:9, v/v) (Scieuzo et al., 2023). The sample was mixed with the solvent at a ratio of 1:9 (v/v) and centrifuged at 16,000 rcf for 45 min at 4°C. The supernatant (containing compounds with molecular weight < 30 kDa) was dried by nitrogen evaporation to remove organic solvents, then resuspended in sterile water to its original plasma volume. To eliminate co-extracted lipids, an equal volume of hexane (Merck Millipore, Burlington, MA, USA) was added. The mixture was vortexed and centrifuged at 16,000 rcf for 20 min at 4°C. The upper lipid phase was discarded, and the remaining aqueous extracts were stored at 4°C until further use.

Peptide quantification

Peptide concentrations in all samples were quantified with the total Protein Assay kit (Nanjingjiancheng, Nanjing, China). A calibration curve was generated using serially diluted bovine serum albumin standards. Absorbance was recorded at 595 nm with an infinite 200 pro spectrophotometer (Tecan, Switzerland).

Hemolymph extracts for inhibition zones assay

The in vitro antimicrobial activity of hemolymph extracts was evaluated via an inhibition zone assay on LB‑agar plates, with slight modifications to a previously described method (Scieuzo et al., 2023). Single colonies of E. coli and S. p were separately inoculated into 10 mL of LB broth and incubated overnight at 37°C under constant shaking. Bacterial cultures were uniformly spread on LB-Agar plates using a sterile cotton swab. After adsorption, 5 μL of each hemolymph peptide extract was dispensed onto the plates; 5 μL of sterile water served as the negative control. All tests were performed in triplicate, and plates were incubated overnight at 37°C to measure inhibition zone diameters.

Effects of different solutions on antibacterial protection activity

Four solutions were prepared at 1 L volume for subsequent treatments: 1 % starch, 5 % sucrose, 1 % starch + 5 % sucrose, and 1 % starch + 5 % sucrose + 0.1 % Tween 80. All reagents were of analytical grade, except Tween 80 which was food-grade. Based on the antimicrobial results of previous hemolymph assays, three samples with prominent antimicrobial activity were selected from −80°C preserved larvae of microwave‑stimulation groups and then subjected to soaking and drying. After thawing to room temperature, each sample was divided equally into five aliquots. Individual aliquots were wrapped with gauze mesh and soaked separately in deionized water, 1 % starch, 5 % sucrose, 1 % starch + 5 % sucrose, or 1 % starch + 5 % sucrose + 0.1 % Tween 80 for 45 min to prevent flotation. After soaking, all 15 aliquots from three samples were suspended in gauze bags to drain for 15 min, then oven-dried at 60°C until the moisture content decreased below 10 %. All soaking, draining, and drying conditions were consistent across experimental aliquots.

AMP extraction from dried larvae

Prior to AMP extraction, dried BSF larvae were ground into a fine powder using a mortar and pestle. The powder was mixed with an extraction solution (10 % acetic acid and 0.01 mol/L Na₂EDTA) at 1:10 (w/v). The mixture was centrifuged at 3500 rpm for 25 min, and the supernatant was collected and freeze‑dried at −50°C. The dried residue was stored at −20°C as the crude AMP extract. Each aliquot was subjected to AMP extraction in three independent biological replicates.

Larval extracts for inhibition zones assay

Protein concentration was quantified via the BCA assay as reported earlier. The inhibitory zone assay was carried out with slight modifications to a previously described method under optimized conditions: LB-agar plates, bacterial concentration of 10⁶ CFU/mL, and 12 h incubation. Bacterial suspensions were evenly spread onto plates with a sterile cotton swab. Four sterile Oxford cups (stainless steel cylinders: outer diameter 7.8 mm, inner diameter 6.0 mm, height 10 mm) were placed on the agar. A 100 μL aliquot of crude AMP extract (0.2 g/mL) from dried larvae was added into each cup. Plates were incubated at 30°C, and inhibition zones were measured after 12 h. Larval extracts of each biological replicate per aliquot were assayed in triplicate on three individual agar plates.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted in GraphPad Prism software (GraphPad Software Inc., San Diego, CA, USA). One-way ANOVA followed by the least significant difference (LSD) multiple comparison test was employed to detect significant differences among treatments. Spearman correlation analysis was conducted to assess the relationship between antimicrobial peptide gene expression and inhibition zone diameters. The Mann–Whitney U test was used for compare differences between -positive and -negative antimicrobial groups. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was adopted to assess the diagnostic ability of antimicrobial peptide genes in distinguishing between positive and negative antimicrobial outcomes. The Youden index was calculated to identify the optimal cutoff and corresponding diagnostic efficiency.

Correlation and ROC analyses of defensin/cecropin relative expression (ΔCt) for discriminating antibacterial inhibition zone positivity

Based on inhibition zone assays, all Us treatments showed no antibacterial activity against E. coli, along with Sp4E8 — Hs and Sp6E8 — Hs. Eight unstimulated groups also lacked inhibitory effects against S. p (Table 6). These inactive groups were defined as negative samples, and all remaining specimens were classified as positive samples. To further validate the diagnostic capability of defensin ΔCt for identifying with antibacterial-active samples, ROC curve analysis was conducted. Compared with defensin ΔCt, cecropin ΔCt may be less suitable for distinguishing between antibacterial-negative and -positive samples, regardless of E. coli or S. p. Although cecropin ΔCt differed significantly between E. coli groups (P = 0.0001) with an AUC of 0.7024, its Youden’s index was only 0.3296 (cutoff value = 6.375), reflecting poor diagnostic accuracy. For S. p., Cecropin ΔCt showed smaller intergroup differences (P = 0.0024), a lower AUC (0.6960), and an equally insufficient Youden’s index (Youden index = 0.3519; cutoff value = 6.185) (Fig. S2).

The different solutions on antibacterial activity protection

Three Ws samples (CK — Ws, Sp4 — Ws, Sp2E4 — Ws) were selected to assess the retention of antibacterial activity following treatment with different protectants and drying at 60°C to a moisture content of 10 %. After drying, inhibitory activity against E. coli was completely abolished, while activity against S. p was well maintained (Fig. 5). Sp2E4 — Ws treated with 1 % starch–5 % sucrose–0.1 % polysorbate 80 solution exhibited the largest inhibition zone (mean = 2.6 cm). The mean inhibition zone diameters for other solutions were 1.3 cm to 1.7 cm (Fig. 5). Discussion

Given feed and food safety concerns, most studies regarding E. coli and S. p. in BSFL centered on the larval capacity for pathogen inhibition and waste bioconversion. Genome-wide analysis revealed 2251 differentially expressed genes in larvae challenged with E. coli (Cho et al., 2024), which can reduce substrate E. coli but not eliminate persistence in pupae and adults (Van Looveren et al., 2024). Similarly, BSFL reduce Salmonella loads in chicken manure by 2–4 log units (Zhang et al., 2025). This study differs from existing reports in focus. We examined the effects of two avian pathogenic bacteria on BSFL under different infection conditions. In terms of growth performance, the control group exhibited inferior weight gain, average body length and maximum body weight relative to the challenged groups (Table 2, 3). This phenotype may be associated with disturbances in the gut microbiota. BSFL can bioconvert various organic wastes, and their gut microbiota contributes substantially to this process (Eke et al., 2023; Yu et al., 2023).

To avoid interference from environmental microbes and better characterize pathogenic impacts, feed was autoclaved and 4-day-old larvae were surface-sterilized using a modified gnotobiotic protocol (Yu et al., 2023), and all experiments were conducted under axenic conditions. This procedure may reduce gut microbial complexity and might compromise nutrient digestion and absorption in larvae. Traditionally, pathogenic bacteria are believed to suppress host growth via nutrient competition, toxin secretion, or immune activation (She et al., 2023). The observed growth-promoting effect of pathogens in this study implies that the BSFL gut microbiota may undergo beneficial functional remodeling upon specific microbial challenge. For example, commensal microbes including Enterococcus and Klebsiella are key drivers of waste bioconversion by BSFL (Eke et al., 2023). Pathogen exposure might stimulate the metabolic activity or abundance of these beneficial microbes, enhancing the degradation and uptake of complex nutrients and thus supporting larval growth (Salam et al., 2024).

In insect innate immunity, defensins and cecropins are pivotal AMPs (Szczepanik et al., 2024). Microbial exposure is well documented to markedly upregulate AMP expression in BSFL (Ho et al., 2021; Shah et al., 2024; Satori et al., 2025). In contrast, our data revealed an atypical expression pattern: fold changes of defensin and cecropin were lower in 11 out of 16 infection groups relative to the control, respectively. Both genes exhibited highly significant differences in relative transcript abundance among larval groups (both P < 0.0001) (Table 4). We propose that this observation does not indicate immune inactivation or suppression, but rather the complex nature of host–pathogen interactions governed by multiple intertwined physiological and molecular mechanisms. Insect immune responses follow strict temporal dynamics. AMP gene transcription typically peaks at 3 – 6 h post bacterial infection and rapidly declines to basal or sub-basal levels within 24 h (Prakash et al., 2022). Sampling at the immune resolution phase may capture a post-feedback repressed state rather than an absent immune response. Immune priming-mediated immune memory can further reshape AMPs temporal profiles upon pathogenic challenge (Prakash et al., 2022). As immune activation is energetically demanding, BSFL may undergo metabolic reprogramming under high-density bacterial exposure to prioritize essential physiological processes or alternative defense pathways, thereby restraining AMP transcription to conserve ATP (Ho et al., 2021). Intracellular pathogens such as Salmonella have evolved sophisticated immune-suppressive strategies. They secrete effector including SopE2 to antagonize the host NF-κB pathway and repress Toll/IMD signaling-dependent AMP transcription, resulting in unexpectedly low AMP expression levels (Chu et al., 2023). AMPs exhibit distinct tissue-specific expression patterns and are strongly induced in immune-active tissues including the fat body and midgut epithelium (Shah et al., 2024). Quantification of gene expression using fat body tissue in this study minimized potential bias arising from tissue-heterogeneous expression.

BSFL are advantageous in their ability to degrade and convert diverse organic wastes. However, variations in rearing substrates markedly modulates the expression of AMPs in BSFL (Candian et al., 2023). Combined with the inherent complexity of host-pathogen interactions, this limitation greatly hinders the scalable production of endogenous functional proteins in BSFL. Recent studies have adopted non-specific physical stimuli to induce AMP synthesis in BSFL (Nakagawa et al., 2023), among which thermal stress has been recognized as a potent inducer (Santori et al., 2025). Nevertheless, such approaches remain difficult to implement or standardize in industrial production. Herein, we screened feasible thermal intervention nodes in conventional BSFL industrial workflows and applied short-duration thermal or microwave stimuli at practical temperatures and parameters. We further compared AMP gene expression and inhibition zone diameters across different infection groups and stimulation regimens. Defensin expression exhibited highly significant differences among infection groups under unstimulated conditions (P < 0.0001), whereas these differences were abolished after thermal stimulation (Hs: P = 0.5183) and microwave stimulation (Ws: P = 0.1976). In contrast, cecropin expression remained significantly different regardless of stimulation. These results indicate that both stimulation modes exert a homogenizing regulatory effect on defensin transcription (Table 5). Within each infection group, both Hs and Ws significantly enhanced the transcription of defensin and cecropin relative to unstimulated controls (Fig. 1). Consistent with gene expression profiles, unstimulated samples showed no inhibitory activity against E. coli (Fig. 2) and only limited inhibition against S. p in partial groups (Fig. 3). By contrast, extracts from stimulated BSFL displayed strong antibacterial activity against both E. coli and S. p (Fig. 2, 3; Table 6). As invertebrates, insects rely primarily on a robust innate immune system to defend against pathogens. This system recognizes pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) via pattern recognition receptors (PRRs), thereby activating downstream signaling pathways and subsequently inducing AMP expression (Perveen et al., 2023). Non-specific stimuli such as heat may trigger a stress response that crosstalk with immune pathways, consequently elevating AMP expression. For instance, Nakagawa et al. (2023) demonstrated that thermal injury stimulates the expression of multiple AMPs without activating prophenoloxidase or the melanization cascade. In contrast, the mechanism underlying microwave stimulation remains unclear. We hypothesize that microwave-induced dielectric heating triggers lipid phase transitions in cell membranes and promotes intracellular Ca²⁺ influx, which might synergistically enhances the IMD pathway and further amplifies AMP production (CHO et al., 2024).

Concurrently, antibacterial assays against two pathogenic microbes revealed that S.p was consistently more susceptible to BSFL-derived antimicrobial peptides than E. coli, irrespective of stimulation. Under unstimulated conditions, partial BSFL samples exhibited inhibitory activity against S. p but no activity against E. coli. Following thermal or microwave stimulation, the mean diameter of inhibition zones against S. p was markedly larger (mean inhibition diameter range: 0.72-1.34 cm for S. p vs mean inhibition diameter range: 0.25-1.43 for E.coli). These observations may stem from intrinsic structural variations in the cell walls of the two pathogens. The length of the O-antigen-the polysaccharide chain extending outward from lipopolysaccharide (LPS) is variable in Salmonella, conferring a relatively higher outer membrane permeability (Santori, et al., 2025). Notably, HiDef3 possesses high binding affinity for the core oligosaccharide of Salmonella LPS, with a dissociation constant (Kd) of approximately 82 nM. Such strong affinity enables HiDef3 to anchor more efficiently to the Salmonella outer membrane, thereby executing potent antibacterial activity (Nakagawa et al., 2023). Correlation analysis of defensin / cecropin transcription and antibacterial phenotypes showed that defensin ΔCt values reliably discriminated positive from negative antibacterial outcomes against E. coli and S. p (Fig. 4), and displayed a significant linear correlation with the inhibition zone diameter for E. coli (r = −0.4381). By contrast, cecropin ΔCt values showed little correlation with antibacterial activity against either pathogen (Fig. S3). Furthermore, cecropin exhibited a high ΔCt cutoff value, accompanied by unfeasibly high Ct values in practical quantitative detection. The ΔCt value is widely used in relative quantification for diagnostic discrimination. For instance, Benke et al. (2023) employed ΔCt values of miR-24-miR-200b to identify malignant potential in pancreatic cystic lesions. Although absolute quantification is more accurate, it is less applicable for production monitoring due to the high diversity of defensin isoforms; thus, it was not considered in this study. Collectively, these findings-including robust discrimination between positive and negative inhibition samples, reliable diagnostic performance validated by ROC curve analysis, and high Youden's Index values (0.7222 for E. coli and 0.7778 for S. p.) at a unified optimal cutoff of 2.43 demonstrate that defensin ΔCt is a highly promising candidate detection indicator for assessing the antibacterial capacity of BSFL in industrial production.

In this study, Ws exhibited better stability and uniformity than Hs. In practice, Hs performance is easily compromised by larval aggregation and uneven heat distribution among individuals, resulting in poorer stimulation consistency compared with Ws. This discrepancy was also reflected in our experimental observations, including incomplete gene expression datasets and less consistent inhibition zone diameters under Hs. Accordingly, three Ws-treated samples were selected for protective agent soaking prior to drying. Relative to pure water, soaking in a solution containing 1 % starch, 5 % sucrose, and 0.1 % Tween 80 maximized the retention of antibacterial activity against S. p in crude larval protein extracts (Fig. 5). The mechanisms underlying the protein-stabilizing effects of starch, sucrose, and Tween 80 have been well characterized (Zhu et al., 2017; Chang et al., 2005; Li et al., 2024; Starciuc et al., 2020). Starch acts as an encapsulating matrix. It protects AMPs via physical barriers and intermolecular interactions, while also reducing BSFL adhesion (Zhu et al., 2017). Sucrose is a cost-effective cryoprotectant and vitrification matrix. Based on the water replacement hypothesis, sucrose stabilizes the structure of antimicrobial peptides and inhibits denaturation during drying and storage (Chang et al., 2005; Li et al., 2024). As a surfactant, Tween 80 protects antimicrobial peptides by inhibiting aggregation, improving solubility, and enhancing interfacial stability (Patel et al., 2023). For BSFL with a chitin-rich exoskeleton, food-grade 0.1 % Tween 80-a generally recognized safe concentration-serves as a key protective component, presumably by lowering solid-liquid interfacial tension. Furthermore, this soaking procedure is implemented post inactivation and before drying; its simple and low-cost formulation makes it highly feasible for large-scale industrial production.

Conclusion

This study targeted the process-oriented optimization for industrial production of BSFL. Given the extensive application of chicken manure in large-scale BSFL rearing, we evaluated larval immune responses to two prevalent avian enteric pathogens under multiple infection models. The two distinct stimulation treatments significantly enhanced the transcription of defensin and cecropin genes and their corresponding inhibition activity. For the first time, we validated the practical efficacy of microwave stimulation, and observed that Ws-treated samples exhibited relatively more stable antimicrobial activity compared with Hs. We further established a reliable detection framework using ROC analysis based on defensin expression and antibacterial inhibition assays, and verified the protective efficacy of food-grade Tween 80 during drying. These integrated improvements across inactivation and drying provide systematic and practical guidance for industrial-scale BSFL processing.

Nevertheless, several limitations of the present study should be noted. First, physical stimulation treatments were conducted under laboratory conditions, and their industrial feasibility requires further large-scale validation. Second, only defensin and cecropin were investigated, while other immune effectors and signaling pathways remain uncharacterized. Third, the precise molecular mechanism underlying microwave-induced AMP upregulation awaits experimental verification. In addition, the antibacterial spectrum was limited to E. coli and S. p, with in vivo efficacy and biosafety in agricultural applications remaining to be explored. Despite these limitations, this study offers practical, operationally feasible references and directions for the functionalized protein production of industrial BSFL, which may lay a preliminary foundation for subsequent validation and sustainable development.
