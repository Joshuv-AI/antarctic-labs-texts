Deep learning versus manual measurement of hallux valgus angle and intermetatarsal angle on Weight-Bearing X-rays in hallux valgus
Authors: Qiong Wang, Jun-Hu Wang, Dong-Dong Ji, Jing-Qi Liang, Xin-Quan Yang, Hong-Mou Zhao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
PurposeThis study aimed to develop two deep learning (DL)-based automated measurement systems (AMSs) for hallux valgus (HV) angles estimation, namely axis segmentation-based and landmark-based AMS, and to evaluate their reliability, accuracy, and efficiency relative to manual measurements.MethodsAMSs were developed using weight-bearing dorsoplantar radiographs (1,683 images from 1,232 patients with HV). An evaluation cohort (208 feet: 121 from patients with HV and 87 from controls) underwent manual and automated measurements of the hallux valgus angle (HVA) and intermetatarsal angle (IMA). Intraclass correlation coefficients (ICCs), Pearson correlation coefficients (r), mean absolute error (MAE), root mean square error (RMSE), Bland-Altman plots, and measured average processing time were calculated.ResultsThe axis segmentation-based AMS outperformed the landmark-based AMS in both groups, with stronger correlations for HVA (HV: r = 0.96 vs. 0.95; controls: r = 0.77 vs. 0.74) and IMA (HV: r = 0.83 vs. 0.78; controls: r = 0.57 vs. 0.45; all P < 0.0001). In both AMSs and groups, HVA measurements exhibited lower errors and stronger correlations than IMA measurements. Both models significantly distinguished patients with HV from controls (P < 0.0001). The average time required was 3.5 min for manual measurement versus 6.3 s (axis segmentation-based) and 6.5 s (landmark-based) for automated measurements.ConclusionOur findings indicated that DL-based AMSs provided reliable, reproducible, and accurate HVA and IMA measurements from HV radiographs, with the axis segmentation-based AMS outperforming the landmark-based AMS.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12891-025-09420-2.

Introduction

Given the low cost and widespread availability of radiography, precise measurement of the hallux valgus angle (HVA) and intermetatarsal angle (IMA) on weight-bearing radiographs remains the first-choice radiographic assessment for hallux valgus (HV) and surgery planning [1–3]. Accurate measurement is clinically critical, as it directly informs surgical decision-making, such as when IMA exceeding 15 degrees in moderate to severe hallux valgus necessitates a corrective osteotomy [4]. However, manual measurements are time- consuming and subject to intra- and inter-observer variability. With the widespread application of artificial intelligence in healthcare [5–9], particularly the application of deep learning (DL) in medical imaging [10, 11], automated measurement systems (AMS) for HVA and IMA estimation have been developed to enhance precision and efficiency in clinical settings.

DL-based AMS for HVA and IMA estimation can be broadly categorized into axis segmentation- and landmark-based AMS, which are both based on DL algorithms. Axis segmentation-based AMSs initially employ a bone segmentation network to delineate the axes of the first metatarsal, first proximal phalanx, and second metatarsal, followed by angle computation using linear regression algorithms. Xu et al. [12] experimentally demonstrated that the segmentation model achieves optimal performance when the bone segment width is set to four pixels, thereby facilitating the accurate estimation of HVA and IMA. Takeda et al. [13]applied a Gaussian filter to the thickened axis lines in order to smooth their representation, and thereby improve the measurement accuracy. Landmark-based AMSs employ a keypoint detection network to identify key skeletal landmarks of the first metatarsal, first proximal phalanx, and second metatarsal, followed by angle computation using geometric algorithms. Li et al. [14] proposed an early landmark-based deep learning algorithm for HV angle measurement. Ma et al. [15]. proposed a multistage approach combining AG-UNet and SE-DNN architectures, which enhanced prediction accuracy by integrating a self-attention mechanism.

Although these approaches have shown satisfactory performance, they have several limitations. First, the datasets and AMSs are not publicly available, which impedes objective validation and reproducibility. Second, studies have used different datasets, which hinders effective comparisons of the different methods. Third, validations have been limited to HV cohorts; accordingly, the lack of control group has impeded the assessment of the broader applicability.

In this cross-sectional study, the primary study objective was to develop two distinct DL-based AMSs, including an axis segmentation-based and landmark-based AMS, as described by Takeda et al. [13] and Li et al. [14], respectively. However, unlike previous studies, we implemented these architectures within a unified framework using the same dataset and evaluation protocol, and will make our code and model weights publicly available to facilitate replication and comparison. Subsequently, we aimed to evaluate whether their HVA and IMA values are correlated with measurements manually obtained by trained observers and assess the test-retest reliability. The secondary study objectives were to compare the accuracy of the two DL-based AMSs, assess whether they could distinguish between patients with HV and controls, and compare the time required for manual and automated measurements.

Materials and methods

This study was approved by our center. Since the dataset was collected retrospectively and all patient-related sensitive information was anonymized, the Ethics Committee waived the requirement for obtaining informed consent from patients.

Study cohorts and dataset preparation

Figure 1 illustrates the development and evaluation pipeline of the DL-based AMSs. We collected 2,000 weight-bearing dorsoplantar radiographs obtained in our center between January 2019 and December 2024. We included patients who met the symptomatic HV criteria (IMA ≥ 10 degrees; HVA ≥ 15 degrees) [16], as clinically and radiographically confirmed by a fellowship-trained foot and ankle surgeon. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) cortical bone alterations, including fractures, prior surgery, or internal fixation; (2) poor image quality (blurred or unannotated images); and (3) age < 18 years. The inclusion and exclusion criteria were determined based on previous studies [12–15]. Accordingly, we obtained 1,683 images from 1,232 patients. The dataset was randomly divided at the patient level into a training set (1,178 radiographs, 70%) and a validation set (505 radiographs, 30%) for AMS development and internal performance assessment, respectively. To ensure consistency with current AMSs [12–15], which typically rely solely on HV cases for model training, both the training and validation datasets included only HV subjects in this study.

The evaluation cohort was established using the same inclusion and exclusion criteria. This cohort included both patients with HV and control subjects to assess model generalizability across different anatomical distributions. A total of 208 feet from 167 adults were enrolled. The HV group (102 patients, 121 feet) was recruited from our institution not involved in model development to ensure validity. The control group (65 participants, 87 feet) consisted of healthy volunteers without forefoot pathologies who underwent weight-bearing dorsoplantar radiography. Age and sex matching was performed during control selection to minimize confounding and enhance group comparability.

All radiographs were acquired using a Siemens device. The original DICOM images were anonymized by assigning arbitrary identifiers and removing all sensitive metadata. Subsequently, they were converted to BMP format and finally to JPG format for model development. For data annotation, two trained observers (foot and ankle surgeons with 3 and 5 years of experience, respectively) independently measured HVA and IMA on weight-bearing dorsoplantar radiographs using the LabelMe [17] tool. Prior to formal annotation, both observers underwent a joint calibration session to review and align their understanding of the standard anatomical landmarks and measurement protocols for HVA and IMA. One observer performed the measurements twice with a 4-week interval to assess intra-observer agreement. To evaluate inter-observer agreement, the second observer independently annotated each image once, and the results were compared with those of the first observer. The final ground truth annotations were reviewed and confirmed by an experienced musculoskeletal radiologist.

DL-Based AMSs architecture and implementation

Both the axis segmentation-based and landmark-based AMSs employed the same backbone and front-end architecture, specifically the DeepLabV3+ [18] built on MobileNetV3 [19]. The distinction between the two models lies in their output heads that the segmentation-based AMS generates pixel-level semantic masks corresponding to anatomical axes, whereas the landmark-based AMS predicts discrete anatomical coordinates representing key points of interest. Statistical analysis

The normality of distribution of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test [20]. Categorical variables were compared using the chi-square [21] or Fisher’s exact test [22], while continuous variables were compared using Student’s t-test [23] or the Wilcoxon rank-sum test [24]. Intraclass correlation coefficients (ICCs) [25, 26] were calculated to assess the intra-, inter-, and intra-method reliability. The intra-method reliability of the DL-based AMSs was evaluated by applying each model twice to the evaluation dataset. Correlation was evaluated using Pearson’s correlation coefficients (r) [27, 28]. Mean absolute error (MAE) and root mean squared error (RMSE) [29–31] were used to assess the average deviation and variability between the predicted values and manual reference measurements. Bland-Altman plots [32] were generated to illustrate the mean difference and limits of agreement, which was defined as the mean ± 1.96 standard deviations (SD) between manual and DL-based AMSs. To enable direct comparison between the two DL-based AMSs, two-tailed paired t-tests were performed on the MAE values for each group.

Although specific clinical thresholds for acceptable error remain undefined, prior studies have reported that measurement variability of up to ± 5° for HVA and ± 3° for IMA is typical in manual assessments [12, 13, 33, 34]. Accordingly, this study adopted these thresholds (± 5° for HVA and ± 3° for IMA) to evaluate the clinical applicability of the AMSs.

To evaluate efficiency, we randomly selected 50 radiographs from the evaluation cohort to measure the time required for manual annotation and DL-based AMSs. The time required to measure each radiograph was recorded separately for manual annotation (by two trained observers) and for each DL-based AMS. A paired t-test was used to determine the statistical significance of the time differences between manual and automated measurements on the same set of images. The timing tests were conducted on a Windows 11 system equipped with a 12th Gen Intel® Core™ i5-12600KF CPU (3.7 GHz).

All statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism version 10 or Python 3.6.9.

In the evaluation cohort, the HV group included 102 patients (121 feet), with a mean age of 53.4 ± 12.8 years and 70.6% female. Measurement performance

Both manual and DL-based AMSs demonstrated significantly higher HVA and IMA values in the HV group compared to the control group. For Manual measurements, the mean HVA was 31.74 (95% CI, 30.11–33.38) in the HV group and 10.03 (95% CI, 9.22–10.80) in the control group (P < 0.001). The axis segmentation-based and landmark-based AMSs exhibited similar trends, with the mean HVA of 32.33 (95% CI, 30.72–33.91) and 32.80 (95% CI, 31.26–34.26) for HV group, respectively. While in the control group, the values were 11.56 (95% CI, 10.58–12.51) and 11.42 (95% CI, 10.46–12.42) (P < 0.001 for both).

Similarly, manual IMA measurements yielded a mean of 10.90 (95% CI, 10.34–11.48) in the HV group and 5.43 (95% CI, 5.05–5.85) in the control group (P < 0.001). The axis segmentation-based AMS reported mean IMA values of 12.44 (95% CI, 11.87–12.99) and 7.09 (95% CI, 6.52–7.63) for the HV and control groups, respectively. The landmark-based AMS produced comparable results, with a mean IMA of 12.75 (95% CI, 12.20–13.34) in the HV group and 7.05 (95% CI, 6.46–7.66) in the control group (P < 0.001 for both). These findings confirm that both DL-based models accurately captured anatomical differences between HV and non-HV feet.

As shown in Table 2, Pearson’s correlation coefficients demonstrated strong linear relationships between manual HVA measurements and those predicted by the axis segmentation-based AMS, with the coefficient being 0.96 (95% CI, 0.94–0.97) for the HV group and 0.77 (95% CI, 0.66–0.84) for the control group. The corresponding values for the landmark-based AMS were 0.95 (95% CI, 0.94–0.97) for the HV group and 0.74 (95% CI, 0.63–0.82) for the control group (all P < 0.0001). Similarly, moderate-to-strong correlations were observed between manual IMA measurements and predictions by the axis segmentation-based AMS, with r = 0.83 (95% CI, 0.76–0.88) for the HV group and 0.57 (95% CI, 0.41–0.70) for the control group; the corresponding values for the landmark-based AMS were 0.78 (95% CI, 0.69–0.84) for the HV group and 0.45 (95% CI, 0.26–0.60) for the control group (all P < 0.0001).

The MAE and RMSE further demonstrated the accuracy of the DL-based AMSs. For HVA measurements, the axis segmentation-based AMS achieved MAEs of 2.10° (95% CI, 1.81–2.42) and 2.52° (95% CI, 2.10–2.97) in the HV and control groups, respectively, with corresponding RMSEs of 2.65° (95% CI, 2.24–3.04) and 3.35° (95% CI, 2.69–4.00). The landmark-based AMS showed slightly higher MAEs of 2.50° (95% CI, 2.22–2.81) and 2.50° (95% CI, 2.03–2.99), and RMSEs of 2.97° (95% CI, 2.64–3.30) and 3.43° (95% CI, 2.74–4.14) for HV and control groups, respectively. For IMA, the axis segmentation-based AMS reported MAEs of 1.99° (95% CI, 1.74–2.24) and 2.02° (95% CI, 1.64–2.47) in the HV and control groups, with RMSEs of 2.43° (95% CI, 2.14–2.69) and 2.84° (95% CI, 2.26–3.45). The landmark-based AMS yielded MAEs of 2.24° (95% CI, 1.95–2.56) and 2.41° (95% CI, 2.03–2.81), and RMSEs of 2.84° (95% CI, 2.37–3.35) and 3.08° (95% CI, 2.57–3.59) for HV and control groups, respectively.

Crucially, direct statistical comparison using paired t-tests revealed that the axis segmentation-based AMS achieved lower MAE than the landmark-based AMS for HVA measurement in the HV group (mean difference: -0.40°, P = 0.003). An advantage for the axis segmentation-based AMS was also observed for IMA measurement in the HV group (P = 0.039). In contrast, no statistically significant differences in MAE were found between the two models in the control group for either HVA (P = 0.801) or IMA (P = 0.139).

For axis segmentation-based AMS, the mean differences exceeded ± 5° for HVA and ± 3° for IMA in the HV group at 5.79% (7/121) and 19.01% (23/121), respectively, and in the control group at 10.34% (9/87) and 17.24% (15/87), respectively. Manual HVA and IMA measurements demonstrated excellent intra-observer (ICC = 0.96 for HVA; ICC = 0.95 for IMA) and inter-observer reliability (ICC = 0.89; ICC = 0.87). Moreover, DL-based AMSs demonstrated complete output consistency for identical inputs, achieving an ICC of 1.00 in both groups, as expected for deterministic systems.

The average time required for manual measurement, axis segmentation-based AMS, and landmark-based AMS were 3.5 m, 6.3 s, and 6.5 s, respectively (P < 0.001 for both comparisons).

Discussion

In our study, both DL-based AMSs showed strong correlations with manual measurements and were able to differentiate patients with HV from controls. The axis segmentation-based AMS exhibited lower measurement errors than the landmark-based AMS. Compared with manual measurements, DL-based AMSs demonstrated significantly higher test-retest reliability, produced consistent outputs for identical inputs as expected from deterministic systems, and were faster. We have granted open access to the DL-based AMSs (https://drive.google.com/file/d/10gBJL1_srfabi-pX1ooDvvEE-f6gPeQ5/view?usp=sharing) in order to facilitate broader clinical application.

The DL-based AMSs leverages DL algorithms to enable fully automated calculation of HVA and IMA, eliminating the need for manual annotation and thereby minimizing user-related variability. In this study, both DL-based AMSs produced perfectly consistent outputs for repeated inputs (ICC = 1.00), as expected from deterministic computational systems. In contrast, manual measurements are inherently subject to intra- and inter-observer variability. Specifically, the intra- and inter-observer reliabilities for manual measurements were 0.96 and 0.89 for HVA, and 0.95 and 0.87 for IMA, respectively. These results highlight the advantage of DL-based AMSs in reproducibility.

As demonstrated in Results section, both axis segmentation-based and landmark-based AMSs successfully distinguished patients with HV from controls. However, the predictive accuracy, as reflected by correlation coefficients, was substantially lower in the control group. For HVA, the r value dropped from 0.961 to 0.765 (axis segmentation-based) and from 0.954 to 0.739 (landmark-based). For IMA, this decline was even more pronounced, with r values decreasing from 0.829 to 0.568 and from 0.776 to 0.450, respectively. Additionally, both MAE and RMSE were consistently higher in the control group across methods. This performance gap is likely attributable to the fact that the current AMSs [12–15] were trained exclusively on HV data, without exposure to anatomically normal feet during model development. Consequently, the models become specialized in recognizing deformities but perform poorly on the diverse anatomy of normal feet. This limitation can be addressed by including normal cases in the training data or employing domain adaptation to learn features that are accurate for all patients.

The axis segmentation-based AMS exhibited lower measurement errors than the landmark-based AMS for both HVA and IMA, as evidenced by various metrics including r, Bland-Altman plots, MAE, and RMSE, although the process of establishing the landmark-based AMS was more similar to the human annotation process. It applies a geometric algorithm to directly calculate the HVA and IMA based on 12 landmarks from the output of a deep learning model. Minor inaccuracies at individual landmarks may accumulate and shift the axis, leading to inaccurate angle measurements. This has been previously confirmed in the automated prediction of flatfoot angles using landmarks [35]. Contrastingly, the axis segmentation-based AMS models each axis as a line segment with a width of four pixels. Each line segment comprises hundreds of points, which allows the linear regression algorithm to automatically eliminate outlier points and provide greater fault tolerance [12].

Both DL-based AMSs demonstrated lower errors for the HVA than for the IMA. This could be attributed to the fact that HVA measurement involves identifying the proximal axes of the first metatarsal and first proximal phalanx. Clear bone edges and distinct grayscale contrast help improve the stability and accuracy of deep learning models [28, 36, 37]. Contrastingly, IMA measurement requires simultaneous detection of the axes of both the first and second metatarsals. However, the second metatarsal has a narrow articular surface and its contour is less distinct than that of the first proximal phalanx. Taken together, our findings suggest that HVA may be a more stable parameter for radiographic assessments using DL-based AMSs. The performance of the axis segmentation-based AMS in anatomically complex regions, as evidenced by IMA measurements, presents a promising solution for standardizing other challenging radiographic evaluations in the foot, notably the assessment of metatarsus adductus, a measurement plagued by substantial inter-observer variability.

This study has several limitations. First, the data used in this study were obtained from a single institution, the lack of multicenter validation across diverse geographic regions and ethnic populations may restrict the generalizability of the AMSs in broader clinical settings. Future studies involving multi-institutional and multi-ethnic datasets are warranted to assess the models’ robustness and applicability across different patient demographics. Second, although the training and validation datasets comprised only HV cases to align with current AMSs standards, the exclusion of control data during model development may limit the generalizability and robustness of the AMSs when applied to non-HV populations. Third, the evaluation cohort was relatively limited in size, particularly for the control group. This may affect the representativeness and statistical power of the validation.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study demonstrates that DL-based AMSs provide reliable and reproducible measurements of HVA and IMA in patients with HV. The axis segmentation-based AMS proved significantly more accurate than the landmark-based AMS, particularly for IMA measurements where anatomical complexity poses greater challenges. The public release of our models addresses a critical need for transparent, reproducible tools in this field. By reducing measurement time from minutes to seconds while maintaining accuracy within clinically acceptable bounds our systems offer a viable path toward standardizing radiographic assessment.
