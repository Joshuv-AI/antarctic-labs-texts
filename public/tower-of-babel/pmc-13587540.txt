Supragingival plaque microbiome features in allergic rhinitis and allergic asthma and their association with dental caries: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Qi Yu, Yang Zheng, Yachun Li, Shengjie Li, Wenzhi Wu, Lei Lan, Jiajie Mao, Kaiwen Fang, Zhuo Chen, Ya-dong Gao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAllergic rhinitis (AR) and allergic asthma (AS) are associated with increased risk of dental caries, potentially associated with alterations in the oral microbiome.ObjectiveTo characterize supragingival plaque microbiome features and their associations with caries severity in allergic adults.DesignThis cross-sectional study enrolled patients with cat dander-induced AR or AS, along with age- and sex-comparable normal controls (NC). Supragingival plaque was collected for 16S rRNA gene sequencing. Microbial diversity, ANCOM-BC2 with multiple-testing correction and pseudo-count sensitivity analysis and LEfSe exploratory analysis were performed. Factors of caries severity (DMFT/DMFS) were examined with a random forest model and multivariable negative binomial regression.ResultsA total of 89 participants (32 AR, 46 AS and 11 NC) were included. Allergic patients exhibited significantly higher α-diversity than NC. ANCOM-BC2 analysis identified 24 taxa with an omnibus FDR threshold; a family-resolved, genus-unclassified Prevotellaceae passed the pseudo-count sensitivity analysis. LEfSe analysis identified Treponema and Bacteroidota as AR-enriched taxa, and Actinobacteriota as AS-enriched taxa. Treponema was the top-ranked random forest feature. Among allergic patients, regular dental check-ups were associated with lower DMFT (IRR = 0.47, 95% CI: 0.30–0.75) and DMFS (IRR = 0.34, 95% CI: 0.17–0.69). Corynebacterium abundance was inversely associated with DMFT (IRR = 0.56, 95% CI: 0.31–0.98), and Actinomyces abundance was positively associated with DMFS (IRR = 2.96, 95% CI: 1.07–8.19).ConclusionsPlaque microbial communities differed across groups, and selected taxa were associated with caries severity among allergic adults. Future investigations are needed to evaluate temporality and mechanisms.Keywords: Oral microbiome, dental plaque, allergic rhinitis, allergic asthma, dental caries, 16S rRNA

Introduction

As the second-largest microbial habitat directly connected to the external environment, the oral cavity harbors the body's second-largest microbiome [1]. The oral microbiome plays an indispensable role in maintaining the homeostasis of the local oral environment, restoring the oral epithelial barrier and regulating the host immune system [2]. Alterations in oral microbial composition are central to oral diseases such as dental caries and mucosal diseases and have also been reported in association with several systemic conditions, including allergic and infectious diseases [3]. These observations suggest that oral microbial ecology may reflect interactions between oral and systemic health.

In recent years, the incidence of allergic diseases, represented by allergic rhinitis (AR) and allergic asthma (AS), has shown a rapidly rising trend [4,5]. Multiple clinical studies have indicated that patients with allergic diseases have a concurrently increased risk of dental caries, which may be closely related to factors such as changes in the oral physical environment caused by mouth breathing, as well as reduced saliva flow rate and pH levels [6]. The latest World Health Organization (WHO) oral health fact sheet (2025) reported that oral diseases affect nearly 3.7 billion people worldwide, and untreated dental caries remains the single most common health condition, with the global burden continuing to rise over the past three decades [7]; China's Fourth National Oral Health Epidemiological Survey revealed that the prevalence of caries in permanent teeth among 12 to 15-year-old children has reached as high as 38.5–44.4% and shows a marked upward trend [8]. The high degree of overlap in epidemiological trends between allergic diseases and dental caries, and the intersection of their shared risk factors, suggests that the mechanisms of association and comorbidity between the two require further investigation [9].

Previous studies have reported associations among allergic disease, oral microbial composition and oral-health outcomes; however, the direction, temporality and underlying mechanisms of these relationships remain uncertain [10,11]. Furthermore, relatively little is known about whether specific supragingival plaque microbial features are associated with dental caries severity among individuals with established AR or AS [12]. Therefore, this study recruited patients with AR and AS, as well as normal controls (NC), and used 16S rRNA sequencing to compare supragingival plaque microbial composition and diversity across the three groups. Random forest model was used to identify microbial features that discriminated among the groups. Among participants with AR or AS, multivariable negative binomial regression was used to examine associations of selected microbial features, demographic and clinical characteristics and oral hygiene behaviors with dental caries severity, as measured by the DMFT and DMFS indices.

Method

Study design and participants

This cross-sectional study was conducted by enrolling participants with AR and/or AS treated in the Department of Allergy at the First Affiliated Hospital, Zhejiang University School of Medicine, between March 2025 and March 2026.

Participants were classified as AR, AS or NC according to prespecified diagnostic criteria. The AR group comprised patients with cat dander-induced allergic rhinitis according to Allergic Rhinitis and its Impact on Asthma (ARIA) guidelines who did not meet the diagnostic criteria for asthma according to Global Initiative for Asthma (GINA) guidelines [13,14]. The AS group comprised patients with cat dander–induced allergic asthma according to GINA guidelines, with or without concomitant allergic rhinitis. NC were recruited as healthy volunteers at the same study center during the same period without clinical history or current symptoms of AR/AS and negative allergen testing. Given the exploratory nature of this study, control participants were recruited to achieve comparable demographic characteristics in terms of age and sex distribution.

Inclusion criteria included: (1) age ≥ 18 years; (2) positive skin prick test (SPT) and serum-specific IgE to cat dander extract, with clinical correlation between allergen exposure and symptoms for AR/AS; (3) willingness and ability to complete all study procedures. Exclusion criteria: (1) acute illnesses, severe chronic diseases or significant psychiatric disorders; (2) antibiotic or probiotic use within the past month; (3) long-term smoking or heavy alcohol consumption; (4) severe oral diseases affecting examination; (5) inability to complete clinical assessments; (6) any condition deemed unsuitable for participation by investigators.

This study was approved by the Clinical Research Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital, Zhejiang University School of Medicine (2024-IIT-1446-rapid) and the Stomatology Hospital, Zhejiang University School of Medicine (2026-IIT-37), and adhered to the ethical guidelines of the Declaration of Helsinki. Informed consent was obtained from each participant. The study was conducted and reported conforming the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guideline for observational studies.

Clinical data assessment and allergen tests

Demographic data, including age, sex, urban–rural distribution, medical insurance status and education level were collected from all eligible participants. Allergen-specific IgE (sIgE) was measured with a panel of common inhalant allergens: Dermatophagoides pteronyssinus, Dermatophagoides farinae, cat dander, dog dander, tree pollens, weed pollens and molds with the ImmunoCAP Phadia 1000 (Thermo Fisher, Uppsala, Sweden); values ≥ 0.35 KUA/L was considered positive. SPT was performed to confirm cat dander allergy using extract from the Allergen Products Research and Development Center (Peking Union Medical College's Hospital, Beijing, China), with histamine phosphate (10 mg/mL) and saline serving as positive and negative controls, respectively. A wheal diameter ≥ 3 mm was considered positive. Total IgE (tIgE), and peripheral blood eosinophil count were also assessed. Spirometry and bronchodilation test were performed using Jaeger MasterScreen system (Jaeger, Hochberg, Germany) according to the CTS/CAPA guideline [15]. Pre-bronchodilator FEV1, FEV1/FVC ratio were recorded. Fractional exhaled nitric oxide (FeNO) was measured using UBREATH BA-200 system (e-LinkCare, Zhejiang, China) with normal values of FeNO < 25 ppb.

Dental examination and dental plaque collection

Dental examination was performed for all participants by a single dentist between January 2026 to March 2026. An oral health questionnaire including oral hygiene behaviors, diet habitat, previous history of oral diseases or procedures was completed simultaneously (Supplementary Material S1). Dental caries were examined according to the World Health Organization (WHO) guidelines [16], the decayed, missing and filled teeth index for permanent dentition (DMFT), the decayed, missing and filled surfaces index for permanent dentition (DMFS), and the plaque index (PI) were recorded. Supragingival plaque samples were collected in the morning at least 2 h after the last meal or fasting. Participants were instructed not to brush teeth and rinsed their mouths with clean water prior to sampling. Plaque was obtained from the buccal, lingual and proximal surfaces of both molar and anterior teeth using sterile dental scaler and transferred onto a sterile swab, pooled into a 2 mL sterile centrifuge tube, transported to the laboratory and stored at −80 °C within 2 h.

16S rRNA amplicon sequencing

DNA extraction, quality assessment, PCR amplification, library preparation and sequencing were performed by OE Biotech (Shanghai, China). DNA concentration and integrity were assessed using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) and agarose gel electrophoresis, respectively. The extracted DNA was used as a template for PCR amplification of bacterial 16S rRNA genes with the barcoded primers and Takara Ex Taq (Takara). For bacterial diversity analysis, V3-V4 variable regions of 16S rRNA genes were amplified with universal primers 343F (5′-TACGGRAGGCAGCAG-3′) and 798 R (5′-AGGGTATCTAATCCT-3′) [17] for V3-V4 regions. Detailed thermal-cycling conditions are provided in the Supplementary Material S2. Purified amplicons were quantified using the Qubit dsDNA assay and sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform with 250-bp paired-end reads.

Paired-end reads were preprocessed using Cutadapt to remove adapter sequences. The trimmed reads were subsequently quality-filtered, denoised, merged and screened for chimeras using DADA2 [18] in QIIME 2 with default parameters [19]. The resulting amplicon sequence variants (ASVs) were generated and taxonomically annotated against the SILVA database (version 138) using the q2-feature-classifier. The sequencing depth was sufficient to capture the majority of microbial diversity, with detailed sequencing-depth and coverage metrics provided in Supplementary Material S2. Alpha diversity was assessed using Chao1, Shannon and observed species, beta diversity was evaluated using unweighted UniFrac, weighted UniFrac and binary Jaccard. Group differences in beta diversity were assessed using PERMANOVA with 999 permutations.

Differential abundance analysis was performed using ANCOM-BC2 on non-rarefied count data excluding taxa present in fewer than 10% of samples. Benjamini–Hochberg FDR correction was applied to omnibus tests; mixed directional FDR was used for pairwise comparisons. Findings were considered confirmatory if they met the corrected significance threshold and passed pseudo-count sensitivity analysis [20]. LEfSe was performed as an exploratory biomarker-discovery analysis with a nominal P < 0.05 and LDA score > 3.0 [21]. A random forest classification model was used to identify taxa that most strongly distinguished the three clinical groups. Variable importance was ranked by the mean decrease Gini index, and the top-ranked genera were selected for subsequent association analyzes with caries severity (Supplementary Materials S3). Sequencing data have been deposited in the NCBI Sequence Read Archive (BioProject PRJNA1475580).

Sample size and statistical analysis

This exploratory cross-sectional study did not perform a formal a priori sample size calculation, as it was primarily descriptive in nature with no hypothesis testing for specific effect sizes. A convenience sample of all eligible participants were recruited based on feasibility and accessibility during the study period. To assess the potential influence of group-size imbalance of small NC group, design-based minimum detectable effects were estimated and alpha-diversity findings were evaluated using balanced subsampling, NC leave-one-out and group-stratified bootstrap analyzes with details provided in the Supplementary Methods S4.

Statistical analyzes were performed using R software (version 4.3.2). Continuous variables are presented as means with standard deviations for normally distributed data or medians with interquartile ranges (IQR) for non-normally distributed data. Normally distributed continuous data were compared using the t-test or analysis of variance (ANOVA), non-normally distributed continuous data were analyzed using the Mann‒Whitney U test or Kruskal‒Wallis test. Categorical variables were expressed as absolute frequencies and percentages, with between-group comparisons conducted using either the χ2 test or Fisher's exact test. Associations between allergic status, supragingival plaque microbiome, oral health variables and caries outcomes characterized by DMFT/DMFS were evaluated using negative binomial regression model due to overdispersion of DMFT and DMFS [22]. Results were reported as incidence rate ratios (IRRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). A two-tailed P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Result

Clinical characteristics

Eighty-nine participants were included in this study; 64/89 (71.91%) were female, median age was 28.0 (IQR 25.0–30.5) ranged from 22 to 40, 78 allergic patients (32 AR, 46 AS) and 11 NC. There were no significant differences in age, sex, BMI, DMFT and DMFS among AR group, AS group and NC group. Patients with AS had higher blood eosinophil counts (0.38 [0.21–0.61] vs. 0.22 [0.15–0.42] × 109/L, P = 0.046) and FeNO levels (63.1 [38.2–89.8] vs. 40.3 [25.8–57.1] ppb, P = 0.005) than those with AR, whereas other clinical characteristics were comparable between the two groups (Table 1).

Microbiota composition and biodiversity in dental plaque

A total of 608, 527 and 271 specific bacterial genera were identified in the AR, AS and NC groups, respectively (Figure 1A). At the genus level, Prevotella, Capnocytophaga, Fusobacterium, Corynebacterium and Leptotrichia are the top five genera in AR patients. Capnocytophaga, Corynebacterium, Prevotella, Actinomyces and Fusobacterium are the top five genera in AS patients. The five most abundant genera in the healthy population were Prevotella, Corynebacterium, Capnocytophaga, Leptotrichia and Actinomyces (Figure 1B). Compared with the healthy group, the relative abundances of Prevotella and Leptotrichia were lower in the AR and AS groups, while the relative abundance of Capnocytophaga was higher.

The alpha diversity results showed that the Chao-1, Shannon and observed species indices were all significantly higher in patients with AR and AS than in NC (Figure 1C). However, no significant differences were observed in the Chao-1 and Shannon indices between patients with AR and those with AS (P > 0.05). Beta diversity was evaluated with PCoA and PERMANOVA (Adonis) analyzes based on unweighted unifrac, weighted unifrac and binary Jaccard distances. Among the three groups, there were significant differences in the beta diversity of plaque microbiota with R2 = 0.036, P = 0.006 for the unweighted unifrac distance algorithm; R2 = 0.056, P = 0.008 for the weighted unifrac distance algorithm; and R2 = 0.025, P = 0.037 for the binary Jaccard distance algorithm (Figure 1D).

Differential analysis of dental plaque microbiota

After construction of the genus/lowest-available-lineage feature set and exclusion of features present in fewer than 10% of samples, 254 non-overlapping taxonomic features were retained. In the omnibus analysis across the NC, AR and AS groups, 24 features met the Benjamini–Hochberg FDR threshold. Only a family-resolved, genus-unclassified Prevotellaceae passed the pseudo-count sensitivity analysis and was sensitivity-robust (W = 53.96, P = 3.78 × 10−12, q = 9.61 × 10−10; Figure 2A). This Prevotellaceae was detected in 3/11 (27.3%) NC, 20/32 (62.5%) AR and 26/46 (56.5%) AS samples. In the pairwise comparisons, five features in the AR-NC comparison, six in the AS-NC comparison and one in the AS–AR comparison met the mdFDR threshold, but none remained robust after pseudo-count sensitivity analysis (Figure 2B). A secondary analysis restricted to 202 explicitly genera identified 18 features meeting the omnibus Benjamini–Hochberg FDR threshold, but none passed the pseudo-count sensitivity analysis (Supplementary Figure S1).

Exploratory LEfSe identified that in the three-group comparison, Bacteroidota and Treponema were identified as AR-associated exploratory features (LDA = 4.594, P < 0.001 and LDA = 4.046, P = 0.002, respectively), whereas Actinobacteriota was identified as an AS-associated exploratory feature (LDA = 4.555, P = 0.027) (Figure 2C). Pairwise LEfSe analysis further identified Treponema and Campylobacter as exploratory AR-associated features relative to NC, whereas Leptotrichia was associated with NC relative to AS (Supplementary Figure S2). However, none was confirmed by a sensitivity-robust pairwise ANCOM-BC2 result. A complementary exploratory Kruskal–Wallis analysis identified nominal between-group differences in six phyla and ten genera, whereas none of the genus-level findings remained significant after Benjamini–Hochberg FDR correction (Supplementary Figure S3).

Factors of dental caries severity among allergic patients

Using a random forest model, we identified the microbial taxa that contributed most to distinguishing the three groups (AS, AR and NC) (Figure 3). Treponema received the highest variable-importance score (Mean Decrease Accuracy [MDA] = 8.19, Mean Decrease Gini [MDG] = 3.03), followed by Porphyromonas (MDA = 6.26, MDG = 2.43), Clade_Ia (MDA = 4.58, MDG = 2.29), Fusobacterium (MDA = 6.09, MDG = 2.25) and Neisseria (MDA = 3.47, MDG = 2.25).

Regular dental check-up was associated with lower DMFT (IRR = 0.47, 95% CI: 0.30–0.75, P < 0.001) and DMFS indices (IRR = 0.34, 95% CI: 0.17–0.69, P = 0.003). In microbial associations, Corynebacterium was nominally inversely associated with DMFT (IRR = 0.56, 95% CI: 0.31–0.98, P = 0.049), whereas Actinomyces was nominally positively associated with DMFS (IRR = 2.96, 95% CI: 1.07–8.19, P = 0.037). With respect to the PI, Fusobacterium (IRR = 1.77, 95% CI: 1.22–2.59, P = 0.003) and Lactobacillus (IRR = 1.40, 95% CI: 1.12–1.75, P = 0.003) were nominally associated with higher PI, while Corynebacterium (IRR = 0.79, 95% CI: 0.63–1.00, P = 0.048) and Clade_Ia (IRR = 0.73, 95% CI: 0.60–0.90, P = 0.003) were nominally associated with lower PI.

Discussion

This study characterizes supragingival plaque microbial community features across the NC, AR and AS groups and separately examines associations between selected microbial features and dental caries severity among participants with AR or AS. We demonstrate that participants with AR and AS exhibited higher plaque alpha diversity and modest differences in overall community structure compared with NC. ANCOM-BC2 identified one sensitivity-robust omnibus association involving Prevotellaceae, LEfSe suggested several potentially discriminatory taxa, including Bacteroidota, Treponema, Actinobacteriota, Campylobacter and Leptotrichia. In separate multivariable analyzes among allergic participants by random forest models and multivariable negative binomial regression revealed that Corynebacterium abundance was inversely associated DMFT, while Actinomyces was positively associated with DMFS. These findings suggest that allergic conditions may be associated with alterations in oral ecology and caries susceptibility.

Our findings revealed significant remodeling of the oral microbiome in patients with AR and AS compared to NC, with the allergic groups exhibiting higher α-diversity than the NC group. These findings align with a European cross-sectional study (ASMAPORT), which reported increased oral bacterial α-diversity in AS patients relative to normal controls, along with differential abundance of 10 core genera (including Actinomyces, Fusobacterium, Gemella, Haemophilus, Leptotrichia, Neisseria, Porphyromonas, Prevotella, Streptococcus and Veillonella) [23]. However, our observations contrast with several previous studies that reported reduced oral microbial diversity in allergic or respiratory disease populations. For instance, a large-scale American population-based study using oral rinse samples found significantly lower oral microbial α-diversity in individuals with chronic respiratory diseases [24]. Similarly, a longitudinal study on salivary microbiota in European children showed that allergy development was associated with reduced diversity, characterized by enrichment of Gemella haemolysans and depletion of Lactobacillus gasseri and L. crispatus [25]. Such discrepancies are likely attributable to differences in sampling sites and the distinct ecological properties of the oral niches examined. Supragingival dental plaque biofilms, as sampled in the present study, represent highly structured, site-specific communities that are more reflective of local physicochemical conditions on tooth and mucosal surfaces. In contrast, saliva and oral rinse samples capture a more transient and mixed microbial population heavily influenced by salivary flow, mechanical clearance and host immune factors (e.g. secretory IgA and lysozyme) [26]. Furthermore, patients with AR and AS frequently exhibit mouth breathing due to nasal obstruction, which can reduce salivary flow rate, alter oral pH and oxygenation levels and create new ecological niches within plaque biofilms. These microenvironmental changes may promote greater microbial diversity in adherent plaque communities compared to the more stable, anaerobic-dominated ecosystems observed in gut or salivary studies [27,28].

ANCOM-BC2 and exploratory LEfSe analyzes identified several taxa showing possible associations with allergic disease. The genus-unclassified Prevotellaceae was the only sensitivity-robust omnibus taxonomic finding. Although members of the Prevotellaceae (e.g. Prevotella spp) have been reported to be associated with asthma and type 2 airway inflammation, the family is taxonomically and functionally heterogeneous. In children with asthma, Yan et al. reported an increased oral abundance of Prevotella and showed that P. bivia-derived molecular-mimicry peptide could induce the expression airway inflammatory cytokines, including IL-4 and IL-5; while P. bivia could alter lipid metabolism in bronchial epithelial cells [29]. In adults with asthma, Prevotella was identified as a bacterium associated with eosinophilic asthma and atopic asthma [30,31]; meanwhile, its abundance was positively associated with lung-function decline [32]. Treponema was identified in the exploratory LEfSe analysis and also met the omnibus ANCOM-BC2 FDR threshold. Its recurrence across analyzes may justify future validation but does not establish it as an AR-specific biomarker. Instead, we speculate that this enrichment may be associated with AR-related mouth breathing, which may reduce salivary flow and buffering, thereby creating anaerobic niches that favor obligate anaerobes [23,33]. However, this mechanistic interpretation remains hypothetical, as we did not measure salivary parameters or plaque oxygen tension. Likewise, the enrichment of Actinobacteriota in the AS group should be regarded as a hypothesis-generating observation, although previous studies have shown that Actinobacteria modulate local immune responses, enhance eosinophilic inflammation and promote type 2 inflammation [34,35]. Nevertheless, exact differential taxa may vary according to cohort characteristics, oral sampling niche, taxonomic aggregation, sparsity and analytical method. Larger cohorts and oral site-specific metagenomics validation studies are required before these candidate taxa can be considered as disease-associated features.

We also further explored the effects of allergic clinical features, oral microbiome composition and oral health behaviors on caries severity (DMFT and DMFS) and plaque accumulation in patients with allergic diseases. Among the core taxa identified by random forest modeling, Corynebacterium showed an inverse association with both DMFT and plaque accumulation. Previous studies demonstrated that Corynebacterium matruchotii may serve as a hallmark of a ‘caries-free’ state [36]. In healthy supragingival plaque, Corynebacterium acts as a keystone species, forming the central ‘trunk’ of the characteristic ‘hedgehog’ biofilm architecture observed via fluorescence in situ hybridization. This highly organized structure helps maintain biofilm homeostasis and restricts the overgrowth of acidogenic and acid-tolerant bacteria [37]. As a commensal organism, Corynebacterium has been proposed to inhibit cariogenic pathogens through the secretion of extracellular vesicles and modulation of the local microenvironment. Its relative abundance is typically higher in individuals with low DMFT scores compared to those with high DMFT scores [38]. In contrast, Actinomyces was positively associated with DMFS, consistent with its established role in caries progression, particularly in enamel and root surface lesions, where certain Actinomyces species exhibit strong acidogenic and acid-tolerant properties [39].

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the relatively small NC group may have limited the precision and statistical power of comparisons involving NC, as design-based sensitivity estimates indicated adequate power only for relatively large standardized effects; smaller or moderate effects may have been missed. Balanced subsampling, NC leave-one-out analyzes and stratified bootstrap analyzes generally supported the direction of the alpha-diversity differences, the frequency of Holm-adjusted pairwise also supported the stability of pairwise findings between AR/AS versus NC comparisons. Taxon-level findings should also be interpreted cautiously, as no pairwise ANCOM-BC2 contrast passed the pseudo-count sensitivity analysis and LEfSe results remained exploratory. Second, the cross-sectional design precludes temporal, causal or mediation inference and reverse causation and residual confounding cannot be excluded. Third, allergy-related assessments and dental examinations were not performed concurrently. Although we selected the allergen test results closest in time to the dental examination to reduce temporal discordance, residual differences in timing may still have introduced a degree of bias and should be considered when interpreting the observed associations. Fourth, the inherent limitations of 16S rRNA sequencing and the absence of experimental or metagenomic validation preclude functional inference. Further prospective cohort studies with metagenomics validation are still warranted to clarify the explicit mechanisms among allergy, oral microbiome and caries.

Conclusion

This study identified higher supragingival plaque microbial α-diversity and microbial community differences in participants with AR and AS compared with NC. Among allergic participants, Corynebacterium and Actinomyces abundances were, respectively, inversely and positively associated with caries indices. Allergic conditions may be associated with alterations in oral ecology and caries; however, future longitudinal studies with metagenomics validation are still needed to evaluate temporality and potential mechanisms.
