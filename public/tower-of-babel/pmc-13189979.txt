Biofilm Formation in Indwelling Percutaneous Nephrostomy Catheters: Luminal Loss and Bacterial Colonization
Authors: Samuel G. Savidge, Bo Yu, Elahe Abbaspour, Ahmadreza Badali, Seyed Sina Zakavi, David Field, Peiman Habibollahi, Mohamed M. Ibrahim, Nariman Nezami
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Purpose: Biofilm formation on the surface of percutaneous nephrostomy and percutaneous nephroureteral catheters is presumed to result in luminal narrowing and predispose to infection. This presumption drives clinical practice in varying ways. However, no study thus far has quantifiably characterized biofilm development in percutaneous nephrostomy/percutaneous nephroureteral catheters.Material and Methods: In this prospective study, removed percutaneous nephrostomy and percutaneous nephroureteral catheters were collected from patients undergoing catheter exchange. Catheters were stained with crystal violet and analyzed to assess biofilm deposition on the internal and external catheter surfaces and to quantify internal biofilm thickness. Pre- and post-exchange urine samples were collected from the catheters and analyzed for bacteria and leukocytes per high-power field.Results: A total of 38 catheters were collected (28 percutaneous nephrostomy, 10 percutaneous nephroureteral). Biofilm was present in all catheters and significantly increased with time. Luminal diameter loss due to biofilm (mean ± standard deviation) was 67 ± 17 μm at 0-2 weeks (n = 3), 104 ± 12 μm at 2-4 weeks (n = 4), 149 ± 32 μm at 1-2 months (n = 10), 236 ± 73 μm at 2-3 months (n = 11), 249 ± 39 μm at 3-4 months (n = 7), and 349 ± 8 μm at >6 months (n = 3). Catheter exchange resulted in a reduction in mean urine leukocytes (p = 0.0004) and bacteria per high-power field (p = 0.0061), but not complete elimination.Conclusions: Biofilm forms on percutaneous nephrostomy/percutaneous nephroureteral catheters within a few days after placement and gradually progresses, occupying more than 200 μm of the luminal diameter by 2 months. Catheter exchange results in an immediate reduction in urine leukocytes and bacteriuria.

Introduction

Percutaneous nephrostomy (PCN) and percutaneous nephroureteral (PCNU) catheters are commonly placed in patients requiring urinary diversion or relief of urinary obstruction resulting from a variety of pathologies, including pyonephrosis, renal and ureteral calculi, malignancy, ureteral stricture, retroperitoneal fibrosis, and urinary fistula [1-3]. While catheter placement is short-term for some patients, many require prolonged catheterization, which increases the incidence of catheter occlusion as well as susceptibility to symptomatic urinary tract infection (UTI), pyelonephritis, and sepsis [4-8].

To prevent complications, chronically indwelling PCN and PCNU catheters are routinely exchanged every 8-12 weeks [9-11]. Despite optimal care and routine exchange, UTI is frequently associated with indwelling PCN and PCNU catheters. The incidence of UTI in patients with percutaneous urinary catheters has been reported to be as high as 20% within a 90-day period [12, 13]. Standard protocol for managing patients with PCN/PCNU catheters and UTI involves the administration of appropriate antibiotics, often concurrent with catheter exchange. Some studies have suggested that bacterial colonization of the catheter and biofilm formation precede infection, necessitating catheter removal to prevent chronic or recurrent infections [14, 15].

Prior studies have shown that biofilm formation contributes to antimicrobial resistance and heightens the risk of persistent infection [16-18]. Common pathogens responsible for catheter-associated upper urinary tract infections have been well-documented [19, 20]. However, analysis of biofilm development and bacterial seeding of indwelling PCN/PCNU catheters has not been conducted in human subjects. This study aims to characterize the progression of biofilm in PCN/PCNU catheters and evaluate the effect of catheter exchange on bacterial clearance.

Material and Methods

Study design and data collection

This prospective study was approved by the institutional review board and was compliant with the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act. The requirement for informed consent was waived. Patients aged 18 years and older undergoing PCN/PCNU catheter exchange, removal, or conversion for any reason were identified at a single medical center between December 2022 and October 2023.

Catheter care and collection

As part of the standard protocol, the catheters were flushed with 10 mL of normal saline twice daily. Indwelling PCN/PCNU catheters (polyurethane base catheters) were retrieved from patients undergoing catheter exchange, removal, or conversion. Standard over-the-wire catheter exchange procedures were performed using aseptic techniques and concurrent administration of prophylactic antibiotics [9, 21]. Both catheters were obtained in cases of bilateral exchange. Following removal, all catheters were manually and gently flushed with 10 mL of normal saline to clear residual urine from the lumen. Catheters were stored in a sterile bag and stained and analyzed within 48 hours after removal.

Biofilm analysis

Collected nephrostomy catheters were longitudinally split into equal halves. Three 2 cm portions (proximal, middle, and distal) were stained with crystal violet to detect biofilm covering the internal and external surfaces. Representative crystal violet staining samples of catheters' internal surfaces over distinct indwelling periods are depicted in Figure 1. Cross-sections were cut from the middle portion in an area where biofilm was present and imaged along a parallel axis with 1:1 macro magnification to enable measurement of internal biofilm thickness. Image analysis was performed using ImageJ software (Rasband, W.S., ImageJ, U.S. National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA) to quantify biofilm coverage of the internal and external catheter surfaces as well as internal biofilm thickness. Mean values were calculated from the three catheter portions. Luminal diameter loss due to biofilm was determined by doubling the internal biofilm thickness to account for biofilm on opposing surfaces. Image analysis was used to calculate the mean internal diameter of three 8.5 French catheters, three 10 French catheters, and three 12 French catheters. These values were used to calculate the percentage of catheter luminal diameter loss caused by biofilm.

Urinalysis

Urine specimens were collected in a sterile fashion from the indwelling nephrostomy catheter immediately prior to exchange and from the new catheter immediately following exchange. Microscopic urinalysis was performed on both pre- and post-exchange samples. Samples were analyzed for gross bacteria and urine leukocytes per high-power field (hpf) [22]. Bacterial load was reported as negative, trace, small, moderate, or large.

Demographic data

An electronic medical record review was performed to determine patient age, gender, type, and French size of PCN or PCNU catheter, catheter dwell time, and reason for exchange. Serum white blood cell (WBC) count on the day of exchange and during the 3 days following exchange was also obtained if available.

Statistical analysis

Data were collected and stored in a Microsoft Excel (Redmond, WA, USA) file and analyzed using GraphPad Prism v10.1.0 (GraphPad Software Inc., La Jolla, CA, USA). Patients were grouped by length of catheter dwell time: 0-2 weeks, 2-4 weeks, 1-2 months, 2-3 months, 3-4 months, and greater than 6 months. Univariate linear regression analysis was performed to correlate dwell time and biofilm characteristics. Multivariate regression analysis was used to determine the correlation between biofilm characteristics and catheter type (PCN vs PCNU), catheter size (French), or the presence of infection at the time of exchange while controlling for indwelling time.

A paired t-test was used to compare mean leukocytes per high-power field in pre-exchange and post-exchange urine samples. Since bacteria were reported as negative, trace, small, moderate, and large, numerical values of 0, 1, 2, 3, and 4 were respectively assigned during analysis. Pre- and post-exchange numerical means were calculated and compared using a Wilcoxon matched-pairs signed-rank test. Paired t-tests were used to compare post-exchange serum WBC counts with those on the day of exchange.

Unpaired t-tests were used to compare the biofilm characteristics of proximal and distal ends of PCNU catheters. Additionally, unpaired t-tests were used to compare biofilm characteristics of right and left catheters in patients with bilateral tubes. All data are presented as means with standard deviations. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered significant.

Study population

A total of 38 catheters were collected from 23 patients. Twenty-four pre-exchange and 23 post-exchange urine samples were collected. Serum WBC count was available for 19 patients on the day of exchange, four patients on post-exchange day 1, three patients on post-exchange day 2, and four patients on post-exchange day 3. Results

The mean biofilm characteristics analyzed at the selected indwelling periods are summarized in Table 2. Univariate linear regression analysis revealed that catheter indwelling time had a significant positive correlation with biofilm coverage of the catheter internal surface (β = 0.15, p = 0.0004), coverage of the catheter external surface (β = 0.074, p = 0.0062), and internal biofilm thickness (β = 0.27, p < 0.0001) (Figure 2). Analysis of variance revealed a significant difference between the indwelling periods for biofilm coverage of the catheter internal surface (p < 0.0001), biofilm coverage of the catheter external surface (p < 0.0180), and internal biofilm thickness (p < 0.0001).

Mean and standard deviation luminal diameter loss due to biofilm was 67 ± 17 μm at 0-2 weeks, 104 ± 12 μm at 2-4 weeks, 149 ± 32 μm at 1-2 months, 236 ± 73 μm at 2-3 months, 249 ± 39 μm at 3-4 months, and 349 ± 8 μm at >6 months. The mean and standard deviation of the internal diameter of an 8.5 French catheter were 1.763 ± 0.027 mm, 2.259 ± 0.027 mm for a 10 French catheter, and 2.744 ± 0.006 mm for a 12 French catheter. The percentage of luminal loss caused by biofilm ranged from 3.8% at 0-2 weeks to 20% after 6 months for an 8.5 French catheter, 3.0% to 15.4% for a 10.2 French catheter, and 2.4% to 12.7% for a 12 French catheter (Figure 3).

Multivariate linear regression analysis controlling for indwelling time demonstrated no significant correlation between catheter type (PCN vs PCNU) and internal biofilm coverage (p = 0.206), external biofilm coverage (p = 0.071), or internal biofilm thickness (p = 0.808). Additionally, multivariate linear regression analysis considering indwelling time as a confounding factor demonstrated that there was no significant correlation between catheter size and biofilm thickness (p = 0.666). Multivariate linear regression analysis controlling for indwelling time did not find a significant correlation between the presence of infection at the time of exchange and biofilm internal percentage coverage (p = 0.896), external coverage (p = 0.810), or internal thickness (p = 0.714). There was no significant difference in indwelling time between patients with and without infection (p = 0.450) after excluding an outlier with infection at 611 days.

There was no significant difference between the proximal and distal portions of PCNU catheters for internal biofilm coverage (p = 0.5174) or external biofilm coverage (p = 0.1045). Additionally, in patients with bilateral nephrostomy catheters, there was no significant difference between right and left catheters in biofilm internal percent coverage (p = 0.8026), external coverage (p = 0.7578), or internal thickness (p = 0.4587).

The mean and standard deviation of urine leukocyte count were 45 ± 14 WBC/hpf before exchange and 24 ± 22 WBC/hpf after exchange. There was a significant reduction in mean urine leukocytes per high-power field following catheter exchange (p = 0.0004). Bacteria were reported as negative, trace, small, moderate, and large. Numerical values of 0, 1, 2, 3, and 4 were respectively assigned to these categories during analysis. The mean pre-exchange bacteria value was 1.75 ± 1.45, and the mean post-exchange bacteria value was 0.83 ± 1.11, with a significant decrease in bacterial load following catheter exchange (p = 0.0061). There was no significant difference in serum WBC count compared with the day of catheter exchange on post-exchange day 1 (p = 0.8880), post-exchange day 2 (p = 0.7948), or post-exchange day 3 (p = 0.7267).

Discussion

Biofilm formation begins within days after PCN/PCNU catheter placement and increases significantly over time. By 2 months, over half of the catheter internal surface is covered with biofilm, and the mean luminal diameter loss due to biofilm exceeds 200 μm. Catheter exchange results in an immediate reduction in mean urine leukocytes and bacteria per high-power field but does not significantly change serum WBC count.

Biofilm coverage of the catheter external surface also increases over time, although there is considerable variability. There is greater biofilm coverage of the external surface compared with the internal surface for indwelling periods less than 2 months. It is unclear why biofilm develops more rapidly on the catheter external surface, but part of the explanation may be due to differences in surrounding flow and shear stress [23]. The flow surrounding the catheter external surface is more static compared with the internal surface, which is exposed to regular urine flow [23, 24]. As a result, the catheter external surface likely experiences less shear stress, a factor known to diminish biofilm proliferation [25].

There are several clinical implications of this study. Characterizing the progression of biofilm on PCN/PCNU catheters' internal surfaces provides additional data supporting the benefit of routine exchange. Most institutions recommend percutaneous nephrostomy catheter exchange every 2-3 months to prevent obstruction [9-11]. At 2-3 months, luminal diameter loss due to biofilm is approximately 236 μm - more than 10% of the internal diameter of a 10.2 French catheter. Based on our quantitative analysis, smaller-diameter catheters―particularly 8.5-Fr devices―demonstrated a higher percentage of luminal diameter loss over time compared with larger-bore catheters, despite similar absolute biofilm thickness across catheter sizes. Although multivariate analysis controlling for indwelling time did not show a statistically significant association between catheter size and biofilm thickness, the relative impact of biofilm on luminal patency was greater in smaller-diameter catheters, resulting in proportionally greater luminal compromise. These findings suggest that, in patients anticipated to require long-term indwelling nephrostomy or nephroureteral drainage, selection of a larger-bore catheter may reduce the relative risk of clinically significant obstruction.

Additionally, catheter biofilms are commonly inhabited by urease-producing species such as Proteus mirabilis, which hydrolyze urea to ammonia, increase urine pH, and allow struvite crystals to form [19, 26, 27]. Biofilm entraps these crystals within its matrix, and crystal aggregation leads to macroscopic encrustation and obstruction, particularly at longer indwelling times [28]. Total obstruction of an already contaminated urinary system allows infection to rapidly disseminate, potentially leading to sepsis [8, 12]. Thus, routine catheter exchange is encouraged to reset the progressive luminal narrowing and encrustation caused by bacterial biofilms. In addition, peri-procedural antibiotic administration at the time of catheter exchange remains appropriate, given the risk of bacterial dissemination from established biofilm during catheter manipulation, contrast injection, and catheter exchange, which can result in transient bacteremia or, in rare cases, progression to clinically significant infection or sepsis. Therefore, while routine treatment of asymptomatic bacteriuria is not recommended, targeted prophylactic antibiotics in the setting of catheter exchange represent a reasonable risk-mitigation strategy that balances infection prevention with principles of antimicrobial stewardship.

This study was not designed to recommend whether nephrostomy catheters should be exchanged in the setting of infection. However, it provides translational data demonstrating a potential benefit of exchange. This study shows that biofilm is ubiquitous in indwelling PCN/PCNU catheters. Prior studies have demonstrated how biofilm formation confers antimicrobial resistance and can perpetuate chronic infection [16-18]. In the setting of infection, catheter exchange eliminates the presence of biofilm and removes a potential source of antibiotic resistance. Furthermore, catheter exchange results in an immediate reduction in local bacteria and leukocytes, indicating improved source control. Nevertheless, bacteria are not completely eliminated by removing the existing catheter, and persistent bacteria likely contribute to the formation of new biofilm within days following exchange.

The final clinical implication of this study is that bacteria detected in urine samples from indwelling nephrostomy catheters should not serve as a sole indicator of infection. Biofilm was shown to be ubiquitous in percutaneous nephrostomy catheters, and bacteria are frequently detected in urine samples without overt infection. When assessing for a UTI in patients with indwelling nephrostomy catheters, providers must rely on clinical signs and symptoms of infection rather than urinalysis alone. This is comparable to the assessment of UTI in patients with indwelling urethral catheters, who rapidly develop asymptomatic bacteriuria after catheterization [29].

This study is limited by being a single-center analysis, and considerable bacterial diversity is known to exist among healthcare institutions [30, 31]. It is possible that biofilm composition and characteristics would vary at different medical centers. Regardless of composition, biofilm would likely be ubiquitous in indwelling nephrostomy catheters and gradually progress with time. This study is also limited by a relatively small sample of catheters, particularly at time points less than 1 month and greater than 4 months. The shortest catheter indwelling period in this study was 9 days, during which biofilm was already observed. Therefore, it was not possible to conclude exactly how quickly biofilm formation begins after catheter implantation, but clearly, it is present within days. At indwelling times greater than 4 months, biofilm coverage is extensive, and variations observable with larger sample sizes would likely not be significant. Finally, this study is limited by the possibility that over-the-wire exchange and traction on surrounding tissue during removal could have disrupted internal and external biofilm in the collected catheters. Manual flushing of the catheters, along with the absence of a standardized flushing protocol controlling pressure during debris removal, represents an additional limitation. Thus, the observed biofilm characteristics may underestimate biofilm characteristics in vivo.

Further research is necessary to explore the biological steps leading from biofilm growth and asymptomatic bacteriuria to symptomatic infection. All patients included in this study had detectable biofilm, yet very few had symptomatic infections. Exploring the processes that lead to overt infection could identify therapeutic targets. PCN catheters coated with a combination of minocycline-rifampin and chlorhexidine have been shown to inhibit biofilm formation for up to 3 weeks in vitro [32]. However, it is unclear if this would reduce rates of infection or encrustation/obstruction in vivo.

Conclusions

This first human study characterizing the progression of biofilm in indwelling nephrostomy catheters shows that biofilm is ubiquitous in PCN/PCNU catheters and gradually progresses over time, leading to significant luminal narrowing. There is a catheter size-dependent risk of obstruction, with smaller-diameter catheters associated with a higher risk of occlusion. Catheter exchange replaces the existing catheter with associated biofilm and reduces urinary leukocytes and bacteria, but does not completely eliminate bacteria. Further work is necessary to reduce rates of PCN/PCNU catheter obstruction and associated infection.
