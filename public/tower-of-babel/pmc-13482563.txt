Eubacterium rectale Mitigates Inflammatory Bowel Disease via Modulation of Glutamine Metabolism Through the GLS2 and NF-κB Pathway
Authors: Lu Han, Yiyi Jin, Wen Lin, Zide Liu, Chunyan Zeng, Youxiang Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractInflammatory Bowel Disease (IBD) is a chronic inflammatory intestinal disorder with complex etiology, closely associated with gut microbiota dysbiosis. This study demonstrates that Eubacterium rectale (ER), a beneficial commensal bacterium, alleviates dextran sulfate sodium (DSS)-induced colitis in mice, as evidenced by improved clinical symptoms, restored intestinal barrier integrity, and reduced pro-inflammatory cytokine levels. Mechanistic investigations revealed that ER specifically upregulates the expression of glutaminase 2 (GLS2), ameliorates DSS-induced disturbances in glutamine (Gln) metabolism within intestinal epithelial cells, and subsequently inhibits the NF-κB signaling pathway to exert anti-inflammatory effects. Further validation showed that GLS2 deficiency abolishes the anti-inflammatory effects of ER. Collectively, this work identifies a therapeutically relevant mechanism: ER mitigates colitis via the GLS2/NF-κB axis. These insights pave the way for developing ER-derived live biotherapeutic products for IBD.Subject terms: Diseases, Gastroenterology, Microbiology

Introduction

Inflammatory Bowel Disease (IBD), including ulcerative colitis and Crohn’s disease, is a chronic, relapsing inflammatory disorder of the gastrointestinal tract, whose global incidence is rising rapidly1,2. The pathogenesis of IBD involves multiple factors, including genetic susceptibility, immune dysregulation, environmental factors, and significant disruption of the gut microbiota, known as dysbiosis3,4. As a critical “microbial organ”, the gut microbiota is essential for maintaining intestinal homeostasis. Commensal bacteria preserve barrier integrity through multiple mechanisms, such as competitive exclusion of pathogens, stimulation of mucus production, and enhancement of epithelial tight junctions5–7. Moreover, microbial metabolites such as short-chain fatty acids, tryptophan derivatives, and secondary bile acids, serve as crucial signaling molecules that directly modulate host immune responses and suppress intestinal inflammation8,9.

Studies have shown that the gut microbiota can modulate host cell metabolism, which in turn influences the production of pro-inflammatory factors and immune responses3. 16S rRNA sequencing analysis revealed that the abundance of Eubacterium rectale (ER) is significantly lower in the intestines of dextran sulfate sodium (DSS)-induced colitis mice compared to normal controls, suggesting that ER may be a dominant commensal species under non-inflammatory conditions10,11. ER exhibits typical characteristics of a gut-resident bacterium and potential probiotic functions, such as its unique morphology and its ability to secrete antibacterial peptides that inhibit the growth of harmful pathogens. However, current research on ER remains limited and subject to controversy12–15. Therefore, whether ER exerts an anti-inflammatory effect in IBD and its precise mechanism are still unclear.

Our preliminary untargeted metabolomics analysis revealed that, compared with normal control mice, the KEGG pathways most significantly enriched in DSS-induced colitis mice were primarily centered on “Alanine, aspartate and glutamate metabolism”. Differential metabolite analysis showed significant alterations in glutamine (Gln), α-ketoglutarate (α-KG), glutamate (Glu), and aspartate (Asp). Although modest changes in aspartate and proline were observed, the overall data indicate that the metabolic disturbance is highly concentrated on the Glu node and its immediate upstream (Gln) and downstream (α-KG) metabolites, rather than a broad, non‑specific amino acid imbalance.

Glutamine metabolism represents the most direct source and conversion pathway for glutamate, particularly in intestinal epithelial cells and immune cells. Gln serves as a critical energy substrate and biosynthetic precursor for intestinal epithelial cells, playing a vital role in maintaining barrier integrity and cellular proliferation16–18. Under inflammatory conditions, the energy demand of epithelial cells surges, often accompanied by a disturbance of Gln metabolism, which may further compromise barrier function and exacerbate the inflammatory cascade. We observed that in the colon tissues of DSS-treated mice, the levels of Gln and its metabolite α-KG were significantly decreased, while the level of Glu was increased. Given this uncoupled disturbance in amino acid metabolism and the fact that Glu serves as a central hub of this metabolic axis, we therefore focused on Glu and its most closely associated Gln metabolic axis.

To investigate the link between Gln metabolic disturbance and ER deficiency, we employed a DSS-induced colitis mouse model and administered ER via gavage to evaluate its anti-inflammatory effects. Notably, the key rate-limiting enzyme affecting glutamine metabolism is glutaminase (GLS), which has two isoforms: GLS1 and GLS2. Previous reports suggest that GLS1 is often associated with pro-inflammatory and pro-carcinogenic mechanisms, whereas GLS2 may possess anti-inflammatory and barrier-protective functions19–21. Meanwhile, our preliminary experiments revealed that DSS treatment led to a decrease in GLS2 protein levels in colon tissues without significantly affecting GLS1, and that ER supplementation not only reversed the GLS2 protein level but also ameliorated the disturbances in the glutamine metabolic axis. Therefore, we hypothesize that ER exerts its anti-inflammatory effects by modulating the GLS2-mediated glutamine metabolic axis.

Previous studies have reported that dysregulated Gln metabolism can modulate the production of pro-inflammatory factors through the NF-κB axis22,23. To test this hypothesis, we will employ a DSS-induced colitis model combined with ER gavage and GLS2-knockdown mice to investigate whether the anti-inflammatory effect of ER is mediated via GLS2-regulated Gln metabolism and to examine its impact on the downstream NF-κB signaling pathway.

Results

Depletion of ER and Gln Metabolic Dysregulation in DSS-induced Colitis Mice

16S rRNA sequencing of fecal samples revealed significant differences in gut microbiota composition between DSS-induced colitis mice and normal control (NC) mice (Fig. 1a-c). Notably, ER was significantly enriched in the NC group (Fig. 1d). KEGG pathway enrichment analysis identified three significantly enriched pathways (FDR < 0.05): “Arginine biosynthesis”, “Glutathione metabolism”, and “Alanine, aspartate and glutamate metabolism”, with the latter exhibiting the highest impact (Fig. 1e, S-Fig. 1b, Table S5). All three pathways share Glu as a central metabolic node. Differential metabolite analysis revealed significant changes in metabolites including Gln, α-KG, Glu, and Asp (Fig. 1f): compared with the NC group, the DSS group showed decreased levels of Gln, Asp, and α-KG, along with increased Glu (Fig. 1g). In contrast, representative metabolites from the other two pathways, such as L-alanine, pyroglutamic acid, and carbamoyl phosphate, showed no statistically significant changes (S-Fig. 1c). Notably, this metabolic pattern—decreased Gln, increased Glu, but decreased α-KG—suggests an uncoupling between the conversion of Gln to Glu and the downstream metabolism of Glu to α-KG. Together, these results indicate that Gln metabolic disorder is an important feature of DSS-induced colitis. Given that DSS-induced colitis is also accompanied by reduced intestinal ER abundance, we next investigated whether ER supplementation could restore Gln metabolism and alleviate colitis.

ER Alleviates DSS-Induced Colitis and Restores Intestinal Barrier Integrity

A DSS-induced mouse model of colitis was established, with ER administered via oral gavage (Fig. 2a). DSS administration led to significant body weight loss, elevated DAI scores (Fig. 2b), and a marked reduction in colon length (Fig. 2c, d). Histopathological analysis of colon tissues revealed extensive mucosal damage, disorganized glandular architecture, and significant inflammatory cell infiltration in DSS-treated mice (Fig. 2e, f). ER intervention significantly ameliorated these inflammatory phenotypes, as evidenced by reduced DAI scores (Fig. 2b), restored colon length (Fig. 2c, d), preserved crypt architecture, and attenuated inflammatory infiltration (Fig. 2e, f). IF staining showed that ER effectively inhibited the DSS-induced depletion of the tight junction protein ZO-1 (Fig. 2g, h) and protected intestinal barrier integrity (Fig. 2i). Furthermore, ER treatment suppressed the DSS-induced increase in plasma levels of the pro-inflammatory cytokines IL-6 and TNF-α (Fig. 2j, k). Collectively, these findings indicate that ER significantly alleviates DSS-induced colonic inflammation.

ER Modulates the Gln Metabolic Pathway

To investigate whether ER exerts its anti‑inflammatory effects by regulating the Gln metabolic pathway, we analyzed typical key enzymes of this pathway in colon tissue samples. At the transcriptional level, ER intervention restored the expression of genes involved in Gln metabolism in DSS‑treated mice to levels close to those of the NC group, including aspartate aminotransferase 2 (GOT2), GLUD1, GPT2, and Gclc (S‑Fig. 1d‑g). ER treatment significantly restored the DSS‑reduced levels of glutamate dehydrogenase (GDH), glutaminase (GLS), and GOT, and reversed the DSS‑induced increase in glutamine synthetase (GS) level in colon tissues (S‑Fig. 1h‑k). In addition, ER treatment restored GSH level (S‑Fig. 1l). The above metabolomics results suggested a specific disturbance in the conversion of Gln to Glu and the subsequent metabolism of Glu to α‑KG. Glutaminase is the key rate‑limiting enzyme catalyzing the conversion of Gln to Glu, and it is highly expressed in intestinal epithelial cells, where it maintains intestinal homeostasis. To investigate the direct role of ER in modulating Gln metabolism, we examined the protein expression of GLS isoforms in colon tissues from different groups. The results showed that ER significantly reversed the DSS‑induced decrease in GLS2 protein expression, while having no significant effect on GLS1 protein levels (Fig. 3e, f). In addition, the Glu/Gln ratio derived from metabolomics data was significantly increased in DSS mice and normalized by ER treatment (Fig. 3d), providing metabolomic corroboration of enhanced GLS2‑mediated conversion. Taken together, these results show that ER treatment positively correlates with the restoration of GLS2 protein expression, and with the Glu/Gln ratio. Next, we performed GLS2 knockdown experiments to assess its functional contribution.

ER Suppresses NF-κB Activation via Glutamine Metabolism

We performed combined in vivo and in vitro experiments. The results showed that in the animal model, DSS treatment significantly increased p65 phosphorylation and IL-1β levels in colon tissue, while downregulating IκBα expression. In contrast, ER treatment effectively inhibited p65 phosphorylation, reduced IL-1β levels, and blocked IκBα degradation (Fig. 3g, h). At the cellular level, ER pretreatment significantly reversed LPS‑induced downregulation of GLS2 protein. The Gln metabolism activator GLN exhibited effects similar to those of ER, restoring GLS2 expression, reducing LPS‑induced p65 phosphorylation, and elevating IκBα levels (Fig. 4a, b). In rescue experiments, the GLS inhibitor (BPTES, BP) was used to block the Gln metabolic axis. The results showed that BP significantly attenuated the anti‑inflammatory effects of ER, as evidenced by persistently low GLS2 expression, elevated levels of p‑p65 and IL‑1β, and reduced IκBα levels (Fig. 4c, d). Regarding intestinal barrier function, both ER and GLN significantly upregulated ZO‑1 expression, and this effect of ER was completely abolished by co‑treatment with BP (Fig. 4e, f). In summary, these findings indicate that ER can regulate the NF‑κB signaling pathway by influencing the Gln metabolic axis, thereby alleviating the inflammatory response and restoring intestinal barrier integrity.

ER Exerts Anti-inflammatory Effects via the GLS2/NF‑κB pathway

We successfully constructed a GLS2-KD mouse model via tail vein injection of GLS2-specific shRNA lentivirus (Fig. 5a, b). Morphological and histopathological analyses indicated that GLS2-KD completely blocked the protective effects of ER. Relative to DSS-treated mice receiving ER intervention, the DSS + ER + GLS2-KD group exhibited significantly shorter colon length (Fig. 5c, d), along with more pronounced body weight loss and higher DAI scores (Fig. 5e). Regarding inflammatory markers, plasma levels of IL-6 and TNF-α were significantly higher in the DSS + ER + GLS2-KD group than in the DSS + ER group (Fig. 5f, g). H&E staining further revealed more severe mucosal damage in the colons of GLS2-KD mice (Fig. 5h). Additionally, exacerbated impairment of intestinal barrier function was observed in the GLS2‑KD group (Fig. 5j‑l).

IHC results showed that, compared with NC group, the DSS group displayed destroyed colonic glandular structure, accompanied by downregulated GLS2 expression and upregulated p65 expression. ER intervention restored these indicators and tissue morphology to near-normal levels. However, in GLS2-KD mice, ER failed to reverse the DSS-induced high expression of p65, and glandular structure showed no obvious repair (Fig. 6a, b). ER alleviates colitis via the GLS2-mediated glutamine metabolism pathway.

LPS challenge downregulates GLS2, which impairs Glu catabolism in the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle. This leads to Glu accumulation alongside decreased levels of Gln and α‑KG. The resulting metabolic disturbance promotes NF‑κB activation, pro‑inflammatory cytokine release, and loss of intestinal barrier integrity, thereby exacerbating colitis. In contrast, supplementation with ER upregulates GLS2, restores Glu flux into the TCA cycle, and elevates Gln and α‑KG levels. These changes collectively suppress NF‑κB signaling and preserve the intestinal barrier, ultimately mitigating inflammation. (Note: L‑Aspartate can be converted to Gln via transaminases such as ALT/AST).

Discussion

Dysbiosis of the gut microbiota is a key contributor to the pathogenesis and progression of IBD. Given their favorable safety profile and patient acceptability, microbiome-targeted interventions have emerged as promising therapeutic strategies for IBD management. In this study, 16S rRNA fecal gene sequencing was performed on DSS‑induced colitis mice and NC mice, and we found that the abundance of ER bacteria was significantly reduced in the DSS group. Studies have shown that Eubacterium species play a key role in maintaining intestinal homeostasis through multiple mechanisms, including energy provision, enhancement of the intestinal barrier, modulation of immune responses, and suppression of pathogens11,24,25. As a gut commensal within the Eubacterium genus possessing distinct phylogeographic population structure and functional adaptability, the role of ER bacteria in IBD remains unclear, with limited and somewhat conflicting reports available26. Therefore, determining whether ER can be used as a standalone probiotic supplement in IBD patients holds important clinical implications.

Meanwhile, we performed metabolomic profiling on the above DSS‑induced colitis mouse model. The results showed significantly decreased levels of Gln and α‑KG, markedly increased Glu levels, accompanied by alterations in Asp and proline metabolism. Combined with KEGG pathway enrichment analysis, the differentially enriched pathways all featured glutamate as a central metabolic node, and glutamate is precisely a key intermediate in the Gln metabolism pathway. Based on these findings, this study focuses on the regulatory interplay between Gln metabolism and ER bacteria. Gln, an essential amino acid that serves as a major energy source for intestinal epithelial cells and is critical for mucosal repair, is often depleted in IBD27. In the present study, DSS-induced IBD model mice also exhibited a pronounced decrease in Gln levels. Notably, GLS is the key rate‑limiting enzyme regulating the Gln metabolic axis, and subsequent functional validation experiments further confirmed that GLS2 plays an indispensable role in the ER‑mediated intestinal protective effects. However, whether the loss of ER directly contributes to dysregulated Gln metabolism, and whether ER can effectively ameliorate DSS-induced colitis, remained unexplored28–30.

Mechanistically, ER intervention was associated with upregulation of GLS2 protein expression, which correlated with the restoration of Gln metabolic flux at the Gln to Glu node, while not significantly affecting GLS1. This targeted modulation helped redirect Glu into the TCA cycle, elevating α‑KG and thereby restraining NF‑κB signaling. This suggests that ER’s modulation extends beyond a simple linear pathway and likely involves additional regulatory layers, such as adjustments in Gln transport, cellular compartmentalization, or contributions from immune cells, which collectively shape the Gln pool. Within the context of DSS-mediated suppression of Gln metabolic influx, ER-driven upregulation of GLS2 facilitated the entry of accumulated Glu into the TCA cycle. This led to increased α-KG levels and restoration of Gln homeostasis, thereby reducing the activation of the NF-κB signaling pathway. Subsequently, the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines (e.g., IL-1β) was blocked, ultimately protecting the intestinal barrier from damage.

To mechanistically dissect this pathway, we employed both gain-of-function and loss-of-function approaches. At the cellular level, a glutaminase activator mimicked ER’s ability to alleviate LPS-induced inflammation, whereas a GLS inhibitor abolished ER’s anti-inflammatory effects, confirming the central role of Gln metabolism. Mechanistically, ER-mediated upregulation of GLS2 protein, with enhanced Gln metabolic influx, correlated with suppressed NF-κB signaling—evidenced by reduced p65 phosphorylation and decreased IκBα degradation—ultimately leading to downregulated pro-inflammatory cytokine expression and preserved intestinal barrier integrity. This therapeutic axis was validated in vivo, where the beneficial effects of ER were abolished in GLS2-KD mice. These mice showed no improvement in key pathological features: DSS-induced intestinal barrier damage, disrupted glandular architecture, impaired Gln metabolic influx, and accumulation of pro-inflammatory factors. Crucially, under GLS2-deficient conditions, ER failed to suppress the sustained activation of the colonic NF-κB pathway. In summary, our findings position ER not merely as a passive marker within the host-microbiota ecosystem but as an active key player. In this role, host metabolic adaptations centered on GLS2 and shifts in microbial composition occur in parallel, jointly orchestrating the alleviation of colitis.

Notably, several questions and limitations in this study remain to be addressed. First, the upstream molecular mechanisms by which ER regulates GLS2 expression warrant further investigation. Second, the observed metabolic disruption represents an uncoupled disturbance that involves not only Gln metabolism but also broader interconnected amino acid pathways, such as those of Asp and proline. Future studies should explore these additional nodes to fully map the metabolic network underlying the protective effects of ER, rather than focusing solely on GLS2. Third, the long-term safety and efficacy of ER administration require further evaluation in future studies. Moreover, the amelioration of colitis by ER likely involves parallel and interdependent adjustments in host Gln metabolism (centered on GLS2) and the gut microbial ecosystem, rather than a simple unidirectional causal relationship. Future studies should aim to elucidate the full landscape of interconnected amino acid metabolism and identify the specific molecular mediators underlying the functional crosstalk between host metabolism and the gut microbiota.

In summary, this study demonstrates that ER exerts robust anti-inflammatory effects by promoting GLS2-mediated modulation of Gln metabolism, thereby inhibiting DSS-induced aberrant activation of the NF-κB signaling pathway. These findings not only suggest the potential of ER as a novel probiotic candidate for IBD treatment but also highlight the interplay between specific gut microbes, host metabolic pathways, and inflammatory signaling in IBD pathogenesis.

Methods

Cell Culture and Plasmids

The human colonic epithelial cell line NCM460 (obtained from the Cancer Research Institute of Central South University, China) was authenticated by short tandem repeat (STR) profiling and then cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) or Roswell Park Memorial Institute (RPMI)-1640 medium, each supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin. Passaging was performed using 0.25% trypsin-0.02% EDTA solution for dissociation. shRNA sequences targeting GLS2 (listed in Table S1) were synthesized by GenePharma (China). All point mutations and truncations were generated by site-directed mutagenesis PCR and verified by Sanger sequencing.

Bacterial Strain and Culture Conditions

The ER strain (JCM17463) was obtained from Mingzhou Biotechnology Co., Ltd. Bacteria were cultured in a modified DSMZ Medium 110. The basal medium contained the following components per liter: 30.0 g Trypticase Peptone, 10.0 g beef extract, 5.0 g yeast extract, 5.0 g K₂HPO₄, 0.5 g L-cysteine hydrochloride, 0.5 mg resazurin, 4.0 g glucose, 1.0 g cellobiose, 1.0 g maltose, and 1.0 g soluble starch (pH adjusted to 7.0 ± 0.2). The medium was sterilized by autoclaving at 121°C for 20 minutes. After autoclaving and upon cooling, it was aseptically supplemented with hemin (5 mg/L) and vitamin K1 (1 mg/L). Chopped meat particles were added to sustain an anaerobic environment.For bacterial expansion, the activated culture was inoculated into fresh CMC medium at a 2% (v/v) inoculum and incubated anaerobically at 37°C for 16-18 h. Bacterial growth was monitored by measuring the optical density at 600 nm (OD₆₀₀). Cultures were harvested in the late exponential phase when the OD₆₀₀ reached 1.0-1.2, corresponding to a bacterial concentration of approximately 10⁸-10⁹ colony-forming units per milliliter (CFU/mL).

For long-term storage, the cultured broth was centrifuged at 3000 r/min for 5 min at 37°C. After discarding the supernatant, the pellet was resuspended in 20% glycerol solution and stored at -80°C.

Animal Experimental Design

All animal procedures were performed in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations and were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Nanchang University (Approval No.: CDYFY-IACUC-202509GR017). A total of 40 male C57BL/6 mice (6 weeks old, 18-20 g) were supplied by Henan Skibios Biology Co., Ltd. Mice were housed under specific pathogen-free conditions with a 12-hour light/dark cycle, temperature maintained at 25 ± 2°C, and relative humidity of 50-60%. Standard rodent diet and water were provided ad libitum. Body weight, stool consistency, fecal occult blood, activity levels, and coat condition were monitored and recorded daily. The Disease Activity Index (DAI) was calculated based on these parameters. All mice were fasted for 12 hours and euthanized on 3 days after the cessation of DSS administration. At the experimental endpoint, prior to blood collection, mice were anesthetized via inhalation of isoflurane (induction concentration: 3-4%; maintenance concentration: 1.5-2%) to ensure unconsciousness and minimize suffering. Following blood collection, all mice were euthanized by cervical dislocation. This method was chosen in accordance with the AVMA Guidelines for the Euthanasia of Animals to ensure a rapid and humane death. Blood samples were collected via retro-orbital venous plexus puncture using a capillary tube. Colon tissue was harvested for length measurement, and segments were either fixed in formalin for histology or snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen for molecular analysis. Fresh fecal contents were collected and stored at -80°C for microbiota and metabolomic analyses.

For the generation of a GLS2-knockdown (GLS2-KD) mouse model, lentiviruses expressing GLS2-specific shRNA were delivered systemically via tail vein injection four weeks prior to the start of the above timeline (Week 2). Following injection, mice were randomly assigned to two groups: one injected with control lentivirus and the other with GLS2-KD lentivirus. The investigators performing the tail vein injections and all subsequent animal procedures were blinded to the group allocation until the completion of the data analysis.

The experimental timeline was as follows: after a one‑week acclimatization period (Week 1), gut microbiota was depleted during Week 2 via oral administration of a broad‑spectrum antibiotic cocktail (0.5 g/L ampicillin, 0.5 g/L neomycin, 0.5 g/L metronidazole, and 0.25 g/L vancomycin) in drinking water. During Week 3, ER pre‑intervention was performed: mice in the experimental group received 200 μL of ER bacterial suspension (10⁸ CFU/mL) by oral gavage every other day, while control groups received an equal volume of sterile saline on the same schedule. In Week 4, colitis was induced and ER intervention continued: mice in the DSS‑induced colitis group received 3% DSS in drinking water for 7 days; during this period, the ER and vehicle control groups continued daily oral gavage (same dose and concentration as before), whereas the normal control group received regular drinking water.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria were as follows: only mice that appeared healthy with no abnormalities after the one‑week acclimatization were included. Mice were excluded from analysis if they died before the end of the experiment, failed to develop colitis (e.g., no significant weight loss or DAI score increase) after DSS administration, or experienced gavage‑related complications.

Gut Microbiome Analysis

The V3-V4 hypervariable regions of the bacterial 16S rRNA gene were PCR-amplified. Amplicons were separated by 2% agarose gel electrophoresis, excised, and purified using Vazyme VAHTSTM DNA Clean Beads. Sequencing libraries were constructed with the TruSeq Nano DNA LT Library Prep Kit (Illumina, USA) and sequenced on an Illumina platform by Wuhan Servicebio Technology Co., Ltd. (China).

Bioinformatics analysis was performed using QIIME2 (version 2023.9) and R (version 4.3.1). To assess inter-group differences in microbial community structure (β-diversity), weighted and unweighted UniFrac distances were calculated, followed by visualization of the resulting distance matrices using principal coordinate analysis (PCoA). To evaluate within-sample (alpha) diversity, we employed the Chao1 index, the Shannon index and the ACE index. Linear discriminant analysis Effect Size (LEfSe) was used to identify differentially abundant taxa (LDA score > 2.0, p < 0.05).

RNA Extraction and Quantitative Real-Time PCR (qRT-PCR)

Total RNA was extracted using an RNA Extraction Kit (Yeasen Biotechnology, China). RNA purity and concentration were determined using a Nanodrop 3000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). cDNA was synthesized using the Eco M-MLV Mix Kit for qPCR with gDNA Clean (Yeasen). qRT-PCR was performed using SYBR® Green Real-Time Quantitative PCR Master Mix (Yeasen) on an ABI 7900HT Fast Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems, USA). GAPDH mRNA levels were used as internal controls. Primer sequences are listed in Table S2. Melting curve analysis was performed to confirm amplification specificity. Relative gene expression was calculated using the 2^(-ΔΔCt) method.

Immunoblotting (WB) Analysis

Cells or tissue samples were lysed in RIPA buffer containing 1% phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride (PMSF). Protein concentration was determined using a BCA assay kit (Abbott, China). Equal amounts of protein were separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred to PVDF membranes. Immunoreactive bands were detected using the Pierce ECL Kit or ECL Plus (Thermo Fisher, China) and visualized with a chemiluminescence imaging system. Primary antibodies used are listed in Table S3.

Immunohistochemical (IHC) Staining

Tissue samples were fixed in formaldehyde, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned at 3-5 μm thickness. Antigen retrieval was performed using heat-induced epitope retrieval or enzymatic digestion. Sections were blocked with BSA or normal serum to minimize nonspecific binding, then incubated overnight at 4 °C with a primary antibody targeting the protein of interest. After washing, a horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibody was applied. Signal was developed using 3,3’-diaminobenzidine (DAB) substrate, and sections were counterstained with hematoxylin before imaging.

Immunofluorescence (IF) Staining

Cells grown on coverslips were rinsed with PBS and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde. Tissue sections were prepared as described for IHC. Samples were permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 and blocked with serum. Primary antibody diluted in PBS containing BSA was applied and incubated overnight at 4 °C. After washing, samples were incubated with a fluorophore-conjugated secondary antibody at room temperature in the dark. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI. Coverslips were mounted with antifade medium and visualized under a fluorescence microscope.

Metabolite Analysis

The levels of relevant metabolites in colonic mucosal tissue were measured strictly in accordance with the instructions of the corresponding assay kits (Table S4). The colon tissue samples were thawed on ice, and approximately 50 mg of tissue was accurately weighed. An appropriate amount of pre-chilled PBS or the extraction buffer specified in the kit was added. The tissue was then fully homogenized using a tissue homogenizer under ice-bath conditions. The homogenate was subsequently centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 15 minutes at 4°C. The supernatant was carefully collected as the tissue metabolite extract for subsequent assays. Repeated freezing and thawing of the tissue extract should be avoided to preserve metabolite stability.

Fecal Metabolomics Analysis

Fecal samples were analyzed using ultra‑high performance liquid chromatography‑tandem mass spectrometry (UHPLC‑MS/MS). Metabolite identification was carried out by matching accurate mass and MS/MS spectra against public databases including HMDB, MassBank, LipidMaps, mzCloud, KEGG, and an in‑house standard library built by Bionovogene. Multivariate statistical analyses were performed using the R package ropls (v1.22.0). Principal component analysis (PCA), partial least‑squares discriminant analysis (PLS‑DA), and orthogonal PLS‑DA (OPLS‑DA) were applied to visualize group separation; model validation was performed by permutation tests (n = 100), and variable importance in projection (VIP) values were extracted from the OPLS‑DA model. Differentially abundant metabolites were identified using a threshold of VIP > 1.0 and Student’s t‑test p‑value < 0.05. The false discovery rate (FDR) was controlled using the Benjamini‑Hochberg procedure, and metabolites meeting these criteria were considered statistically significant. Pathway enrichment analysis was performed using KEGG pathways (https://www.kegg.jp/); enrichment was evaluated by a hypergeometric test followed by FDR correction (q < 0.05 considered significant). For visualization, a bubble plot was generated using –log10(raw P‑values) as the default output of the pathway impact analysis.

Measurement of intestinal permeability

Intestinal barrier integrity was assessed in vivo using fluorescein isothiocyanate-labeled dextran (FITC-dextran, 4 kDa; Sigma-Aldrich). At the end of the treatment period, mice were fasted for 4 h and then orally gavaged with FITC-dextran (600 mg/kg in PBS). After 4 h, blood was collected from anesthetized mice. Plasma was obtained by centrifugation (4 °C, 3,000 × g, 15 min), diluted 1:1 with PBS, and protected from light. Fluorescence intensity (ex/em: 485/528 nm) was measured using a fluorescence microplate reader. The plasma concentration of FITC-dextran was determined against a standard curve prepared in naïve mouse plasma.

Statistical Analysis

Data are expressed as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Group comparisons were performed using one-way ANOVA, followed by Dunnett’s post-hoc test for multiple comparisons. Comparisons between two groups were conducted using Student’s t-test. All analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 7 and SPSS 24.0 software. A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant (*p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, ****p < 0.0001.
