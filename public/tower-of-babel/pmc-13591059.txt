Asthma and Lung Function in School‐Aged Children After Severe RSV‐A and RSV‐B Bronchiolitis, Is There Any Difference?
Authors: María Arroyas Sánchez, Patricia Alonso López, Guadalupe Tena García, Cristina Calvo, María Iglesias‐Caballero, Vicente Mas, Inmaculada Casas, María‐Luz García‐García
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroductionHuman respiratory syncytial virus (HRSV) is a major cause of respiratory disease. It is classified into two antigenic subgroups. While several authors have studied the potential link between HRSV infection and later development of recurrent wheezing (RW) or asthma, no previous studies have assessed the role of HRSV subtypes. The aim of this study was to assess the medium‐term respiratory morbidity in children and adolescents with prior severe bronchiolitis due to HRSV‐A or HRSV‐B infection.MethodsFrom September 2023 to June 2025 an observational, prospective, cross‐sectional follow‐up study was conducted, including children and adolescents (5–18 years) with a history of hospital admission due to single HRSV infection. Patients underwent a structured questionnaire, measurement of exhaled nitric oxide, spirometry and skin prick testing for allergic sensitization.ResultsA total of 185 patients were recruited (129 HRSV‐A, 56 HRSV‐B). No differences in the variables regarding hospital admission for bronchiolitis were detected. The prevalence of current asthma in both groups was similar (HRSV‐A 17.2% vs. 25.5%, p = 0.190). Patients with prior HRSV‐B bronchiolitis reported more severe respiratory symptoms, particularly speech‐limiting wheezing (HRSV‐A 1.5% vs. HRSV‐B: 8.9%, p = 0.039, OR 2.62 (95% CI: 1.3–5.4)) and more frequent nocturnal dry cough not associated with infections (HRSV‐A: 7.7% vs. HRSV‐B 16.9%, p = 0.044, OR: 1.78, (95% CI: 1.78–2.9)). Multivariate analysis revealed HRSV‐B prior bronchiolitis to be an independent risk factor for medical diagnoses of asthma and nocturnal cough.ConclusionsSchoolchildren and adolescents with prior severe bronchiolitis due to HRSV‐B have poorer respiratory outcome compared to those with HRSV‐A, with more frequent intercrisis symptoms.Keywords: asthma, bronchiolitis, human respiratory syncytial virus, severe respiratory infections

Introduction

Respiratory infections are the first cause of consultation and hospitalization in children under 5 years of age with most of cases attributed to viral pathogens [1]. Human respiratory syncytial virus (HRSV) is a major cause of respiratory disease with a variety of pathology ranging from upper respiratory tract infection to more severe processes such as bronchiolitis and pneumonia [1, 2]. Nearly 70% of infants are infected with HRSV in the first year of life and, at the age of 2 years, almost all children (90%) have suffered a HRSV infection [2, 3]. Bronchiolitis, defined as the first episode of respiratory distress in children under 2 years of age [4], is the most common viral respiratory condition requiring hospitalization. Approximately 70% of bronchiolitis is caused by HRSV.

HRSV is an enveloped virus with a single‐stranded, negative‐sense RNA genome, that belongs to the Paramyxovidae family, genus Orthopneumovirus. It is classified into two antigenic subgroups, A and B, which exhibit genome‐wide divergence in nucleotide and amino acid sequence identity. Fusion protein (F) and glycoprotein (G) are the two major surface proteins [3]. Between the groups, the F protein has 89% amino acid sequence identity and has a high level of antigenic cross‐reactivity, while G protein differs between both subgroups [2, 3]. HRSV‐A and B can circulate simultaneously during seasonal epidemics and have been reported worldwide. Since the discovery of these subgroups, attempts have been made to clarify whether HRSV subgroups correlates with disease severity with inconclusive results [5, 6, 7, 8, 9].

The potential link between bronchiolitis in early childhood and the development of recurrent wheezing or long‐term asthma has been subject of study for years. There is growing evidence to support the association between early‐life HRSV lower respiratory tract infection (LRTI) and recurrent wheezing (RW) or asthma [2]. The mechanisms by which HRSV is related to the development of asthma and RW are not well established, but it seems to be linked to the confluence of timing of the infection [10], direct viral damage to the respiratory tract, environmental influences, and the host's microbioma and pre‐existing individual predisposition [2, 6, 11]. Although several authors have studied the association between HRSV infection and asthma development [12], to our knowledge, there are no previous studies that have assessed the role of the HRSV subtypes in the development of medium‐term wheezing and asthma.

This study aimed to address the gap by analyzing a cohort of school‐aged children who were hospitalized in the first 2 years of life with bronchiolitis caused by HRSV A or B. We assessed and compared their medium‐term respiratory outcomes, in terms of prevalence of asthma, lung function values and rate of allergic sensitization at school age.

Methods

We conducted an observational, prospective, cross‐sectional follow‐up study of a cohort of infants hospitalized for RSV bronchiolitis in the Severo Ochoa University Hospital, a public secondary‐care hospital in Madrid, Spain, between September 2023 and June 2025.

The study included children and adolescents with a history of hospital admission for acute viral bronchiolitis, defined by the criteria of McConnochie [4], between January 1, 2004 and December 31, 2017 due to a single HRVS infection. These patients were originally enrolled during hospitalization as part of an ongoing clinical‐virological study of severe pediatric respiratory infections, conducted in collaboration with the Influenza and Respiratory Viruses Laboratory at the National Centre of Microbiology (ISCIII) since 1995.

At the time of admission, a sample of nasopharyngeal aspirate was collected for viral identification and HRSV subgroup determination using molecular diagnostic techniques. During the hospital stay, a questionnaire was filled out by a physician providing information on epidemiological and clinical variables. Nasopharyngeal aspirate samples were screened for multiple viruses using molecular techniques; however, only those cases involving RSV monoinfection were included in the present study.

The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Severo Ochoa University Hospital in July 2023 (no specific ethical number available in ethical approval letter) and the study was conducted in accordance with the principles set forth in the Declaration of Helsinki.

Inclusion Criteria

Children and adolescents aged 5 to 18 years with a history of admission for bronchiolitis in the Pediatrics Department of the University Severo Ochoa Hospital, between January 1, 2004, to December 31, 2017, caused by a single HRSV‐A or HRSV‐B infection, and whose parents had previously consented to participate in the original etiological study.

Exclusion Criteria

Children and adolescents with serious illness that prevents the performance of respiratory function tests, as well as the refusal of parents or children to participate in the study.

Study Procedures

Eligible participants were contacted by telephone and were invited to take part in the follow‐up study. For those who agreed, an appointment was scheduled at the hospital. After obtaining informed consent, participants were enrolled. Informed consent was obtained from the parents for children under 12 years of age. For those over 12, informed consent was obtained from both the parents and the minors; the latter was adapted to a minor‐friendly language. Clinical data regarding hospital admission were collected from databases created at the time of admission, including age at admission, sex, personal history of preterm birth, the length of stay, presence and duration of fever, need for and duration of oxygen therapy and the need for admission to a pediatric intensive care unit (PICU), total white blood cell count and C‐reactive protein level if applicable.

At the time of the follow‐up visit, age was calculated, and weight and height measurements were taken to calculate the corresponding lung function z‐score values.

During the follow‐up visit, a structured questionnaire was used to gather information on breastfeeding, nursery school, wheezing episodes, related hospital admissions, use of bronchodilators and maintenance medication for asthma [inhaled corticosteroid, combination inhaled corticosteroid/long‐acting beta‐2 agonists (ICS/LABA), leukotriene antagonists], physician‐diagnosed conditions including atopic dermatitis, allergic rhinitis and food allergies. Additional data were collected on parental smoking habits and family history of allergy, eczema and asthma in first‐degree relatives (parents or siblings), previously diagnosed by a physician.

Patients were encouraged to complete the Spanish‐validated ISAAC questionnaire [13]. The ISAAC respiratory symptom questionnaire was completed by the patients themselves with their parents’ assistance in the case of older children (> 12 years old), and directly by the parents for the younger ones. This eight‐item questionnaire is widely used in epidemiological studies to assess asthma prevalence. It includes questions on lifetime and recent wheezing episodes (within the last past 12 months), frequency and impact on daily activities (sleep, exercise or speech) and the presence of nocturnal dry cough not associated with infections. Question 2‐ (‘Have you had wheezing or whistling in the chest in the last 12 months?’) is the one most associated with the presence of current asthma and was used in this study as the primary criterion for defining current asthma prevalence.

Following the interview, participants underwent measurements of exhaled nitric oxide, performance of spirometry and bronchodilator test and skin prick testing for allergic sensitization.

Fraction of Exhaled Nitric Oxide (FeNO)

FeNO was measured using the NIOX VERO handheld device. Values < 25 ppb were considered to be normal.

Lung Function Test

Forced spirometry was performed in accordance with the Spanish Society of Pulmonology and Thoracic Surgery (SEPAR). The parameters recorded were forced vital capacity (FVC), forced expiratory volume in 1 s (FEV1), FEV1/FVC ratio and mean expiratory flow between 25% and 75% of FVC (FEF25–75). Results were interpreted using the reference values provided by The Global Lung Function Initiative (GLI) (2023) available at GLI reference site (https://www.ers-education.org/guidelines/global-lung-function-initiative/about/). Spirometry was considered to be normal when FEV1 and FVC were ≥ 80%, FEV1/FVC > 90% and FEF25–75 ≥ 65% of their predicted values.

Post‐bronchodilator spirometry was conducted following the administration of 400 μg of inhaled salbutamol. The test was considered positive when an increase of FEV1 of ≥ 12% over the baseline was observed.

Allergic Sensitization

Allergic sensitization was evaluated by skin prick test to common aeroallergens, following the recommendations of the European Academy of Allergy and Clinical Immunology (EAACI) [14]. The following allergens were included: Dermatophagoides pteronyssinus, Dermatophagoides farinae, Alternaria alternata, Cladosporium, grass, cypress, olive and plane tree (Platanus) pollen, as well as dog, cat and horse dander. Standardized extracts (ABELLO) were used, along with positive (10 mg/mL histamine) and negative (glycerol saline vehicle solution) controls. A test was considered to be positive when the wheal diameter was equal to or greater than that of histamine and at least 3 mm larger than the negative control.

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables were described using mean and standard deviation. Categorical variables were described using absolute and relative frequencies. To compare qualitative variables, Chi‐squared test or Fisher's exact test was used if the expected frequency was ≤ 5 in at least one cell of the contingency tables. Odds ratio (OR) values and 95% confident intervals (CI) were also calculated. For quantitative variables Student's T test was applied for those with a normal distribution while the Mann–Whitney U test for non‐normally distributed data. A two‐sided value of p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Risk factors with a p‐value < 0.10 in the bivariant analysis were entered in a stepwise logistic regression analysis to estimate the independent association among RSV‐A, RSV‐B bronchiolitis and other factors with asthma prevalence.

All statistical analyzes were performed using SPSS software, version 25 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, USA).

Power/Sample Size

According to Sigurs et al. [15] 43% of former hospitalized HRSV bronchiolitis with current age of 13 years will present asthma in the past 12 months. We predefined an absolute difference of 15% as clinically meaningful, as such a magnitude would represent a substantial variation in long‐term respiratory morbidity and potentially relevant implications for risk stratification after severe bronchiolitis. Accepting an alpha risk of 0.05 and a beta risk of 0.2 in a two‐sided test, 99 subjects are necessary in the HRSV‐B group and 198 in the HRSV‐A group to find as statistically significant a proportion difference, expected to be of 0.15 in group 1 and 0.3 in group 2.

Results

A database of 470 patients admitted for bronchiolitis with a single RSV infection was available. Of these, 120 could not be contacted due to a change in phone number or because they had moved to a different city. Of the 350 patients informed about the study, 185 agreed to participate as of June 1st, 2025, with a median age 12.44 ± 3.6 years (5–18 years). Of these, 99 were boys (53.5%), and 86 (46.5%) were girls. Regarding the HRSV subgroup, 129 (69.7%) belonged to HRSV‐A infection, while 56 (30.3%) were infected with HRSV‐B. Of the patients who declined to provide informed consent, only one had deceased as a consequence of a bone neoplasm; none of them suffered from severe chronic lung disease.

Discussion

Multiple studies have linked the history of bronchiolitis and viral infection with the subsequent development of asthma or wheezing in childhood and adolescence [10, 11, 12, 16, 17, 18, 19]. To our knowledge, this is the first study to compare the medium‐term respiratory evolution in patients with a history of severe bronchiolitis with unique RSV infection requiring hospitalization, comparing the two main RSV antigenic groups A and B. According to our results, the frequency of asthma in the past 12 months was similar in both groups; however, patients with a history of HRSV‐B bronchiolitis exhibited greater respiratory morbidity. Specifically, they reported speech‐limiting respiratory symptoms 2 to 3 times more frequently and experienced nocturnal dry cough at twice the rate observed in the HRSV‐A group. Notably, bronchiolitis caused by HRSV‐B emerged as an independent risk factor for nocturnal dry cough and showed a trend toward a higher prevalence of physician‐diagnosed asthma at any time point.

It is important to highlight that intercritical symptoms, such as dry nocturnal cough, are a key criterion in the classification of asthma severity. Their presence alone is sufficient to categorize asthma as persistent, even in the absence of frequent exacerbations. These findings support the hypothesis that asthma may present greater severity in children and adolescents with a history of HRSV‐B bronchiolitis.

Previous studies have suggested that early‐life viral infections—such as bronchiolitis caused by rhinovirus, HRSV, or viral coinfections—may contribute to the development of severe asthma later in childhood, potentially through variations in the host inflammatory response during the acute phase of illness [18, 20, 21]. Recent studies have shown that humoral responses to RSV infection are predominantly strain‐specific, with minimal cross‐neutralization between A and B subgroups, particularly concerning the G protein. In contrast, the F protein elicits more broadly reactive antibodies across subtypes [22, 23]. Furthermore, differential expressions of cytokines such as TNF‐α, IFN‐γ, IL‐1α, and IL‐1β has been observed in infants infected with specific RSV‐A (e.g., ON1) and RSV‐B (e.g., BA9) genotypes, suggesting subtype‐dependent modulation of innate and inflammatory responses [24]. In addition, HRSV‐B may induce a weaker interferon‐mediated antiviral response compared to certain HRSV‐A strains, possibly through differential engagement of innate immune receptors such as TLRs, RIG‐I, and NOD2, leading to a more skewed Th2‐type inflammatory profile and prolonged airway inflammation [25]. Taken together, these immunological differences support the hypothesis that the infecting HRSV subtype may influence long‐term respiratory outcomes.

Interestingly, among infants infected with HRSV‑B—the subgroup in which greater asthma severity was observed in our study—the only significant baseline difference compared to HRSV‑A was a higher frequency of maternal atopy. Maternal atopy, encompassing conditions such as eczema, allergic rhinitis, and asthma, has been independently associated with an increased risk of allergic disease and wheezing disorders in offspring. Evidence suggests that maternal atopic status can influence fetal immune development through mechanisms such as epigenetic modification, skewing of neonatal T‐cell responses toward a Th2 profile [26]. These observations may suggest that interactions between maternal atopy, host immune predisposition, and viral subtype may influence the long‐term trajectory of respiratory health in affected infants.

A previous study by Sigurs et al. [15] which included long‐term follow‐up of patients with a history of HRSV bronchiolitis, reported a higher prevalence of atopic asthma at 18 years of age compared to general population. These individuals also exhibited elevated FENO levels and reduced lung function compared to healthy controls, findings consistent with airway remodeling likely induced by severe early‐life HRSV infection. In our study, we found no significant differences in lung function parameters, including FEV1, FVC, the FEV1/FVC ratio, and FEF25–75, between patients with a history of severe HRSV‐A and HRSV‐B bronchiolitis. The mean values of FEV1 and FEV1/FVC were within normal ranges for age in both groups. To our knowledge, this is the first study to specifically compare medium‐term lung function outcomes between the HRSV‐A and HRSV‐B subtypes. It is possible that any potential differences in lung function between the two groups are subtle and would require a larger sample size to achieve statistical significance.

Regarding clinical severity during the admission for bronchiolitis, no significant differences were found in our study between the two main HRSV antigenic groups. The clinical significance of HRSV subtyping, HRSV‐A versus HRSV‐B, in bronchiolitis severity remains a subject of ongoing debate, with evidence pointing in both directions. Several studies have reported that HRSV‐A is associated with more severe clinical outcomes compared to HRSV‐B, particularly some genotypes of HRSV‐A—such as ON1 and GA5—[27]. The proposed mechanisms include greater replicative capacity and enhanced induction of proinflammatory cytokines (e.g., IL‐6, IL‐8, TNF‐α), potentially exacerbating the host immune response and airway injury [25]. However, HRSV‐B has also been shown in some cohorts to induce a comparable or even more sustained inflammatory response, especially in genotypes such as BA9, though this may not always correlate with more severe clinical manifestations [24].

A recent meta‐analysis concluded that while HRSV‐A is more frequently detected and may be associated with increased viral load, the evidence supporting consistently greater disease severity is inconclusive and potentially confounded by other factors [28].

The principal strength of our study lies in the medium‐term follow‐up of a substantial cohort of patients whose clinical data were prospectively collected during hospitalization for severe bronchiolitis due to a single HRSV infection. This design allowed us to investigate the medium‐term clinical impact of the two major antigenic groups of HRSV—an area that, to our knowledge, has not been previously addressed in medium and long‐term studies.

However, our study also has certain limitations. The sample size was smaller than the initially calculated target, and the number of patients infected with HRSV‐B was notably lower than those with HRSV‐A, reflecting the distribution typically observed in clinical practice. The study was powered to detect an absolute difference of 15% in asthma prevalence between the RSV‐A and RSV‐B groups. Smaller differences might still be clinically relevant; however, the study was not powered to detect effects of this magnitude, and therefore a type II error cannot be excluded. In addition, the study population included a wide pediatric age range (5–18 years). Nonetheless, it is generally accepted that from the age of five, asthma symptoms can be considered well established, distinguishing them from the transient wheezing phenotype often observed in younger children. In fact, we did not find any significant difference between children < than 6 years or ≥ 6years of age regarding the prevalence of current asthma. It should also be acknowledged that the symptom survey was conducted at a single time point; therefore, some degree of recall bias is possible. Patients and their families—especially in the case of older children—may have difficulty accurately recalling details such as the age at the last wheezing episode or the precise number of recurrent wheezing episodes per year. Lastly, the potential for selection bias must be acknowledged given the substantial number of patients who were not enrolled; however, it is worth noting that the primary reasons for non‐participation were logistical, namely residing outside the city or a lack of time to attend the follow‐up visit.

In conclusion, our findings suggest that school‐aged children and adolescents with a history of hospitalization for HRSV‐B bronchiolitis experience poorer respiratory outcomes during childhood and adolescence, including more frequent intercurrent symptoms. Furthermore, HRSV‐B infection emerged as an independent risk factor for limited speech and nocturnal dry cough at school age and during adolescence. According to our results, since a history of HRSV‐ B bronchiolitis appears to be associated with a poorer prognosis, closer monitoring of this patient group—with better medical management of respiratory symptoms during the first years of life—could lead to reduced medium‐term morbidity.
