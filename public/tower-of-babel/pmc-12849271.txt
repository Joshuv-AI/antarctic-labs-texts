2D nanomaterial-triggered ferroptosis in cancer therapy
Authors: Yue Gao, Jiahao Xu, Yuanqiu Lai, Yaoxuan Fan, Yuhan Zhou, Ying Tu, Xiang Liu, Xueqian Xia, Gui-Na Wei, Jia-Wei Shen, Wei Duan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractCancer therapy faces significant challenges due to the resistance of traditional apoptosis-based treatments. Ferroptosis, an iron-dependent, lipid peroxidation-driven form of programmed cell death, has emerged as a novel therapeutic strategy owing to its unique metabolic regulation mechanisms. However, conventional small-molecule ferroptosis inducers suffer from poor targeting and rapid metabolism. The integration of nanotechnology provides innovative solutions for precise ferroptosis modulation. Among various nanomaterials, two-dimensional (2D) nanomaterials stand out due to their distinctive physicochemical properties and multifunctionality, making them widely studied in ferroptosis-mediated cancer therapy. In this review, we first outline the development, physiological mechanisms, detection markers, and common methodologies of ferroptosis, while highlighting the current challenges in ferroptosis-related cancer therapies. Second, we discuss the general principles of 2D materials in inducing ferroptosis, emphasizing how these platforms enhance ferroptosis efficacy. Third, we classify and elaborate on different types of 2D materials that induce ferroptosis in tumor cells. Finally, we present future perspectives in this field. We hope this review will facilitate the rational design of 2D materials for ferroptosis induction and pave the way for broader biomedical applications.Graphical Abstract Keywords: Two-dimensional nanomaterials, Ferroptosis, Reactive oxygen species, Targeted delivery, Synergistic tumor therapy

Introduction

Cancer remains a major global health threat, with prevention and control posing persistent challenges. While advances in medical technology and expanded early screening have contributed to a modest decline in overall mortality, the burden of cancer is still substantial. It is projected that approximately 618,120 people in the United States will die from cancer in 2025 [1]. The risk factors contributing to cancer are complex and diverse, encompassing both internal and external triggers such as long-term smoking [2], environmental pollutants [3, 4] and occupational exposures, poor dietary patterns [5], and insufficient physical activity [6]. These factors intertwine with intricate mechanisms of influence, further complicating prevention and control efforts [7]. Therefore, achieving transformative breakthroughs in cancer treatment continues to be an urgent priority for the global scientific and medical community. Traditional chemotherapeutic agents primarily induce tumor cell apoptosis, but clinical evidence shows that cancer cells can develop resistance through multiple mechanisms, leading to therapeutic failure [8, 9]. This challenge has driven scientists to explore alternative forms of regulated cell death, among which ferroptosis has emerged as a promising research frontier in cancer therapy due to its unique mechanistic features and close association with tumor metabolism [10, 11].

Ferroptosis, an iron-dependent form of programmed cell death, is characterized by dysregulated iron metabolism and abnormal accumulation of lipid peroxides. Distinct from traditional apoptotic pathways, ferroptosis disrupts cellular redox homeostasis, offering novel therapeutic targets for cancer treatment. This process has attracted significant attention in life sciences not only because it reveals a new form of cell death but also because it provides innovative strategies for treating various diseases [12, 13]. Studies demonstrate that many tumor cells exhibit heightened susceptibility to ferroptosis, making its modulation a viable approach to suppress tumor growth and metastasis [14]. Furthermore, ferroptosis mechanisms are closely linked to the pathogenesis of autoimmune disorders, neurodegenerative diseases, and other conditions [15–19], suggesting its broad therapeutic potential.

Most previous studies have focused on small-molecule compounds to induce ferroptosis for anticancer therapy. However, these agents suffer from inherent limitations such as poor water solubility, weak cellular targeting, and rapid metabolic clearance, hindering their long-term efficacy. To overcome these challenges, researchers have developed nanomaterial-based approaches for ferroptosis induction. With advances in ferroptosis research and nanotechnology, various nanomaterials have proven to be highly effective in triggering ferroptosis. For instance, iron-based nanomaterials can directly release Fe2+/Fe3+ ions and elevate intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels, offering a straightforward and potent means to induce tumor cell ferroptosis. However, as the understanding of ferroptosis deepens, single-component iron-based nanomaterials are no longer sufficient to meet the demand for precise regulation, necessitating the development of novel nanomaterial systems.

Since the successful isolation of single-layer graphene in 2004 by the University of Manchester, this prototypical 2D nanomaterial has sparked worldwide research interest. Other graphene-like materials, including covalent-organic framework (COF), black phosphorus (BP), transition metal dichalcogenides (TMDs), layered double hydroxides (LDHs), and emerging MXenes, have also demonstrated vast potential in electronics/optoelectronics, catalysis, energy storage/conversion, sensing, and environmental remediation. Recently, 2D nanomaterials have achieved groundbreaking progress in biomedical applications, particularly in regulating ferroptosis, where they exhibit unique advantages. The distinctive physicochemical properties of 2D nanomaterials make them ideal tools for multi-pathway ferroptosis induction in tumor cells. First, their intrinsic structural features—such as surface vacancies, defect sites, and enzyme-mimicking catalytic activities—enable direct modulation of key ferroptosis pathways without the need for drugs. Second, their atomic-scale thickness and ultrahigh surface area provide abundant active sites for efficient loading and targeted delivery of ferroptosis-inducing agents. Additionally, some 2D materials possess multifunctional capabilities (e.g., photothermal, ultrasound, or magnetic responsiveness), allowing spatiotemporally controlled therapy under external stimuli and enabling multimodal synergistic treatments.

Given the transformative progress in 2D nanomaterials for biomedical applications and their unique advantages in modulating cell death pathways, a comprehensive review of recent advances in this field is warranted. Such developments may unlock unprecedented opportunities for cancer intervention. As illustrated in Fig. 1 this review summarizes the latest research on 2D nanomaterials for ferroptosis-mediated cancer therapy, including detailed discussions of ferroptosis mechanisms, detection markers, and methodologies, as well as the design principles of 2D materials for ferroptosis induction (e.g., intrinsic material properties, drug delivery strategies, and exogenous stimuli). Furthermore, we present representative examples of ferroptosis-induced cancer therapy using various classes of 2D nanomaterials. Finally, we outline future prospects and challenges in this field. This review highlights that the integration of nanotechnology and molecular biology positions 2D nanomaterials as revolutionary tools for ferroptosis-related disease treatment, paving new avenues for precision medicine in cancer and other major diseases.

Ferroptosis

Basic introduction

Ferroptosis is a form of regulated cell death (RCD) formally named by Brent Stockwell’s team in 2012 [20], which is biochemically and morphologically distinct from other RCD pathways dependent on specific execution proteins such as caspases. The core mechanism of ferroptosis involves abnormal accumulation of iron-dependent lipid peroxides, primarily driven by Fe²⁺ and ROS. This process typically occurs through direct inhibition or indirect depletion of glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4) activity, weakening the cell’s antioxidant defenses, leading to uncontrolled lipid peroxidation and massive production of lipid ROS, ultimately resulting in cell death. Morphologically, ferroptosis is characterized by distinct features: mitochondria undergo significant changes, including increased membrane density, reduced volume, decreased or lost cristae structure, often accompanied by outer membrane rupture. The nucleus generally retains normal size and chromatin structure, without obvious nuclear condensation or DNA fragmentation. In fact, ferroptosis-like phenomena were observed before the term was officially coined. As early as 2003 [21], Dixon et al. discovered that the small molecule erastin could induce a unique form of cell death, characterized by the absence of typical apoptotic nuclear morphological changes such as chromatin condensation and DNA fragmentation, laying the foundation for the subsequent concept of ferroptosis. In 2007 [22], another inducer, Glutathione (GSH) peroxidase 4 inhibitor (RSL3), was identified to induce cell death by inhibiting GPX4. Further studies in 2008 confirmed that GPX4 deficiency leads to lipid peroxide accumulation, triggering a non-apoptotic cell death process [23].

As shown in Fig. 2, it was not until 2012 that Stockwell and colleagues formally introduced the term “ferroptosis”, marking the establishment of this research field [20]. Since then, the regulatory mechanisms of ferroptosis have rapidly expanded. In 2014, GPX4 was identified as a key regulatory factor [24]. In 2015, studies revealed that Tumor Protein 53 (p53) can regulate ferroptosis through atypical mechanisms, linking tumor suppression with oxidative stress response [25]. In 2016, the oxidation of polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs) mediated by lipoxygenases was identified as a critical step in ferroptosis [26]. Entering the era of precise regulation, in 2017, Long-chain acyl coenzyme A synthetase 4 (ACSL4) was found to play a crucial role in determining cellular sensitivity to ferroptosis [27]; in 2018, selenium was identified as essential for maintaining GPX4 enzymatic activity, highlighting the relationship between trace elements and antioxidant systems [28]. With further research, GPX4-independent anti-ferroptosis pathways have been gradually revealed. In 2019, the ferroptosis suppressor protein 1 (FSP1)-ubiquinol (CoQ10H2) system was established as a novel ferroptosis suppression axis [29, 30]. In 2020, the GTP Cyclohydrolase 1 (GCH1)/tetrahydrobiopterin (BH4) system was also found to offer protective effects [31, 32]. In 2021, new research identified the mitochondrial enzyme Dihydroorotic acid dehydrogenase (DHODH) as another independent suppressor of ferroptosis, further expanding the role of mitochondria in this process [32].

Timeline of ferroptosis

Mechanism of ferroptosis

Ferroptosis, an iron-dependent form of cell death driven by lipid peroxidation, plays a critical role in the pathogenesis of various diseases, including neurodegenerative disorders [33, 34], cancer [35, 36], atherosclerosis [37, 38], diabetes [39, 40], and acute kidney injury [41], Its execution involves the dysregulation of a multi-layered metabolic network: dyshomeostasis of iron metabolism leads to the accumulation of redox-active iron, which catalyzes oxidative damage via the Fenton reaction; reprogramming of lipid metabolism enriches cellular membranes with polyunsaturated fatty acids, providing substrates for peroxidation; and the collapse of redox homeostasis, particularly the dysfunction of the core System Xc⁻–GSH–GPX4 antioxidant axis [42, 43], results in the lethal accumulation of lipid peroxides. Moreover, emerging evidence highlights the notable immunogenicity of ferroptosis—the release of damage-associated molecular patterns during ferroptotic death can activate dendritic cells and promote CD8⁺ T cell-mediated antitumor immunity, establishing a bidirectional crosstalk with the immune microenvironment and offering novel synergistic strategies for cancer immunotherapy [60, 61]. As summarized in Fig. 3, key pathways—including iron uptake, lipid peroxidation, and both GPX4-dependent and independent protective mechanisms such as the FSP1-CoQ10 axis and the BH4 system —are comprehensively integrated [16, 29–31], providing a clear framework for understanding the molecular network of ferroptosis and its potential as a therapeutic target in human diseases.

Schematic illustration of the ferroptosis mechanism

Iron metabolism

Ferroptosis is a form of cell death characterized by iron-dependent lipid peroxidation, with disruption of intracellular iron homeostasis serving as its fundamental driving force. Cells acquire iron through transferrin-dependent endocytosis mediated by TfR1 [16]. Within endosomes, Fe³⁺ is reduced to Fe²⁺ and released into the cytoplasm, forming an active iron pool that participates in oxidative reactions. When iron uptake increases, iron efflux is impaired, or ferritin storage function is compromised, excessive accumulation of free Fe²⁺ provides ample substrate for the Fenton reaction to generate highly reactive free radicals, rapidly amplifying lipid oxidative stress. Concurrently, ferritinophagy releases stored iron, further amplifying this reaction and predisposing cells to iron overload and oxidative imbalance [44]. Iron dysregulation permeates the entire ferroptosis process; abnormalities in transport, storage, or degradation pathways may serve as pivotal triggers for lipid peroxidation outbreaks [45].

The central role of iron metabolism in ferroptosis lies in its regulation of redox balance. From transmembrane iron transport to subcellular distribution, and from molecular-level iron chelation to systemic iron circulation, disruption at any of these steps may serve as a key to triggering ferroptosis. This iron-dependent cell death mechanism not only reveals the deep reliance of life on iron, but also highlights the dual role of iron metabolic dysregulation in disease development.

Lipid metabolism

Lipid peroxidation is the direct execution event of ferroptosis, involving the oxidative modification of phospholipids rich in PUFAs in the cell membrane by free radicals or lipoxygenase-mediated processes, yielding cytotoxic lipid peroxides [46]. This process first relies on ACSL4 to activate PUFAs into PUFA-CoA, which is then selectively incorporated into the phospholipid bilayer by LPCAT3, rendering the cell membrane a highly oxidizable structural foundation. Subsequently, under the catalysis of free Fe²⁺ or LOXs, hydrogen atoms are abstracted from lipids to form lipid radicals, which undergo rapid chain reactions generating abundant LOOH [10, 16, 47, 48]. This ultimately disrupts membrane structural integrity and leads to mitochondrial dysfunction. Accumulation of LOOHs disrupts cellular membrane integrity. Shah et al. [49] showed that overexpression of LOX-5, LOX-12, and LOX-15 increases cellular susceptibility to ferroptosis. Furthermore, POR mediates redox reactions by transferring electrons from NADPH/NADH (reduced/oxidized nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate) to downstream proteins. During this process, POR transfers electrons to oxygen, producing hydrogen peroxide which promotes lipid peroxidation. In 2020, Zou et al. [50] reported that POR associated with cytochrome P450 elevates lipid peroxidation and ROS levels.

Redox metabolism

Disruption of redox homeostasis is the ultimate driver of ferroptosis, and cells attempt to suppress lipid peroxidation chain reactions through multiple antioxidant pathways. The GPX4-GSH axis constitutes the most central defense system, wherein System Xc⁻ is responsible for cysteinyl glutathione uptake [42]. This promotes GSH synthesis, enabling GPX4 to reduce LOOH to the non-toxic lipoic acid [51]. Disruption of redox homeostasis is the ultimate driver of ferroptosis, and cells attempt to suppress the lipid peroxidation chain reaction through multiple antioxidant pathways. The GPX4-GSH axis constitutes the central defense system, wherein System Xc⁻ facilitates cysteinyl-GSH uptake, promotes GSH synthesis, and enables GPX4 to reduce LOOH to the non-toxic lipid alcohol. When System Xc⁻ or GPX4 is inhibited, lipid peroxides accumulate rapidly without clearance, irreversibly initiating ferroptosis [52–55]. Beyond GPX4, cells activate alternative protective pathways like the FSP1-CoQ10H₂ and mitochondrial DHODH-CoQ10H₂ axes. These systems directly scavenge membrane lipid radicals by reducing CoQ10 to antioxidant ubiquinol, thereby delaying membrane rupture and the propagation of oxidative damage [29, 30]. NADPH provides critical reducing power for diverse antioxidant reactions, playing a central role in ferroptosis susceptibility by sustaining GSH and CoQ10 regeneration [24, 56]. The varying degrees of dependence on these antioxidant systems across cell types confer distinct cell-specific patterns of ferroptosis.

Immunogenicity

Beyond being a form of metabolic cell death, ferroptosis is notably immunogenic, endowing it with significant potential in antitumor immunity. During ferroptosis, lipid peroxidation and the disruption of plasma membrane integrity lead to the release of various damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) and oxidized metabolites, such as ATP, HMGB1, and oxidized phospholipids [57–59]. These molecules can be recognized by dendritic cells (DCs), macrophages, and other innate immune cells, promoting their maturation and antigen-presenting capacity, thereby activating a specific antitumor immune response mediated by CD8⁺ T cells [60, 61].

Ferroptosis not only acts directly on tumor cells but also influences the tumor immune landscape by modulating the immune status. Firstly, it enhances the function of immune-activating cells. The induction of ferroptosis can promote the polarization of tumor-associated macrophages towards the pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype while reducing the abundance of immunosuppressive M2-type macrophages. This shift alters the immune balance in the tumor microenvironment (TME), fostering a tumor-suppressive milieu [62, 63]. Secondly, ferroptosis suppresses immune evasion mechanisms. By altering intracellular iron and lipid metabolism in tumor cells, ferroptosis can undermine mechanisms associated with immune evasion, rendering the tumor cells more vulnerable to clearance by the immune system [64]. Finally, ferroptosis interferes with the function of immunosuppressive cells; for instance, it can modulate the activity of tumor-infiltrating regulatory T cells (Tregs) and myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs), thereby reducing their immunosuppressive effects [58].

A positive feedback loop exists between ferroptosis and T cell function in the interplay between immune and tumor cells. For example, IFN-γ released by CD8⁺ T cells can downregulate the expression of SLC7A11, a key subunit of system Xc⁻, in tumor cells. This suppression compromises the antioxidant defense of tumor cells and sensitizes them to ferroptosis, establishing a self-reinforcing “immunity-induced ferroptosis/ferroptosis-promoting immunity” cycle [65]. This characteristic underscores that ferroptosis is not merely a cell death pathway for tumor cells but also a critical node in tumor immunotherapy. Enhancing tumor cell susceptibility to ferroptosis can potentiate the efficacy of immunotherapies, while the immune activation triggered by ferroptosis offers novel strategic avenues for combination immunotherapies.

It is important to note that the immune effects of ferroptosis are context-dependent, varying with cell type and the specific microenvironment. Under certain conditions, excessive lipid peroxidation or its byproducts may impair immune cells, leading to T cell exhaustion or DC dysfunction. Therefore, combining ferroptosis inducers with immunotherapy requires a careful balance between the intensity of ferroptosis induction and the degree of immune activation. Overall, by releasing immunostimulatory signals and remodeling the immune microenvironment, ferroptosis plays a dual role in antitumor immunity. This property provides a theoretical foundation and extends the research significance for the subsequent discussion on two-dimensional nanomaterials-induced ferroptosis and its applications in cancer therapy.

Common biomarkers and detection techniques of ferroptosis

Ferroptosis is a distinct form of RCD characterized by unique morphological features that set it apart from other RCD pathways, mainly including apoptosis, necroptosis, autophagy, pyroptosis, cuproptosis, and PANoptosis [66] (Table 1). The process of ferroptosis is intricate, involving numerous metabolic intermediates and enzymes. While a definitive method for specifically detecting ferroptosis has yet to be established, multiple experimental approaches have been commonly employed in current research. In this section, we summarize these detection methods to support and guide future studies in the field.

Morphological features and hallmarks of cell death

Morphological detection is considered the most intuitive approach for identifying cell death. In ferroptotic cells, the plasma membrane, nucleus, and chromatin are usually relatively intact, while the hallmark features include mitochondrial shrinkage, cristae reduction, outer membrane rupture, and increased membrane density of the bilayer [67]. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) can reveal morphological changes in mitochondria, membrane rupture, and lipid droplet formation in ferroptotic cells [68]. As the major source of reactive ROS in mammalian cells, mitochondria contribute to ferroptosis when ROS levels rise. Mito-Tracker Green and MitoSox Red are used to label mitochondria and mitochondrial ROS, respectively [69], allowing visualization under confocal microscopy. Moreover, increased mitochondrial membrane permeability and decreased membrane potential are key structural changes during ferroptosis [70]. Thus, beyond morphological observation, changes in mitochondrial membrane potential and permeability can further confirm the occurrence of ferroptosis. Common methods for assessing membrane potential include tetramethylrhodamine methyl ester (TMRM) and the fluorescent probe JC-1, while membrane permeability can be detected using fluorescent dyes such as 3,3’-dihexyloxacarbocyanine iodide (DIOC6) [71] and MitoTracker Deep Red FM [72].

Profiling of genes, proteins, and transcription factors associated with ferroptosis

In ferroptosis research, multiple genes and proteins have been widely employed as key molecular markers for determining ferroptosis status, assessing cellular sensitivity, and analyzing metabolic alterations. Unlike metabolic components within the ferroptosis mechanism, these molecules emphasize their utility in experimental detection and signal interpretation. Iron metabolism-related factors represent one of the most commonly used detection targets for ferroptosis. Elevated TfR1 expression often indicates increased cellular iron uptake demand, making it a frequently employed surface molecular marker for ferroptosis detection via Western blot or flow cytometry [73]. Concurrently, NCOA4 upregulation reflects heightened ferritin autophagy activity, typically accompanied by decreased ferritin heavy and light chain levels—a pattern regarded as a hallmark signal of cellular iron pool mobilization [74]. Conversely, reduced levels of the iron export protein FPN can assess impaired cellular iron efflux. Given its close correlation with iron loading, FPN also holds significant diagnostic value in ferroptosis research [75].

Lipid metabolism-related genes also serve as key indicators for characterizing ferroptosis susceptibility in experiments. ACSL4, as a crucial enzyme promoting the synthesis of phospholipids rich in polyunsaturated fatty acids, exhibits elevated expression that is often regarded as the molecular basis for increased cellular vulnerability to lipid peroxidation attacks [76]. Consequently, it holds high reference value in qPCR and protein detection. Upregulation of genes such as LPCAT3 and LOXs is also frequently used to indicate enhanced lipid oxidation tendencies, which correlate positively with ferroptosis [49]. In contrast, ACSL3 and SCD1 are frequently monitored as indicators of ferroptosis suppression, with their elevated expression levels often associated with cellular tolerance or evasion of ferroptotic stimuli [77–79],. Concurrently, significant upregulation of PTGS2 frequently serves as a concomitant molecular hallmark of ferroptosis occurrence, aiding in experimental confirmation of whether cells undergo ferroptotic responses [43].

Analysis of factors related to the antioxidant system aids in assessing a cell’s capacity to resist ferroptosis. The System Xc⁻ components SLC7A11 and SLC3A2 are employed across multiple experimental systems to evaluate glutathione synthesis potential [80]; reduced expression of these genes often indicates difficulty in maintaining normal glutathione levels [81]. Changes in GPX4 are considered one of the most direct indicators for ferroptosis detection, with its reduction typically signifying a weakened antioxidant defense. Additionally, molecules such as FSP1 and GCH1 are employed to monitor GPX4-independent defense pathways [82]. Fluctuations in these genes often indicate that cells are attempting to mobilize alternative antioxidant mechanisms to counter lipid oxidation. Beyond genes and proteins, cellular metabolites like the GSH/GSSG ratio [81] and NADP⁺/NADPH ratio frequently serve as crucial parameters for quantifying ferroptosis susceptibility [81, 83]. These can be precisely analyzed using assay kits or chromatographic methods [29, 84].

Detection of various reaction products in ferroptosis

The abnormal accumulation of intracellular iron, particularly Fe²⁺ within the labile iron pool, represents both a hallmark and a critical driving force of ferroptosis. Elevated levels of intracellular free iron can result from enhanced iron uptake (e.g., via TfR1), decreased iron storage (e.g., autophagic degradation of ferritin, known as ferritinophagy), or impaired iron export (e.g., downregulation of ferroportin expression). Excess Fe²⁺ catalyzes the production of reactive ROS via the Fenton reaction, thereby directly triggering lipid peroxidation. This section outlines the commonly used methods and reagents for detecting intracellular iron ions. Currently available techniques for quantifying cellular iron include fluorescent probe assays (such as Calcein-AM), chemical staining (e.g., Prussian blue staining), and inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS). For the specific detection of Fe²⁺, fluorescent probes such as RhoNox-1, FerroOrange, and FRET iron probe 1 (FIP-1) are widely applied.

Ferroptosis is a peroxide-induced, non-apoptotic cell death process closely associated with intracellular iron and reactive ROS levels [46]. ROS detection methods are primarily categorized into three types: intracellular ROS, lipid ROS, and mitochondrial ROS. Intracellular ROS are typically measured using 2’,7’-dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (DCFH-DA), a probe sensitive to hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂), which emits green fluorescence upon increased ROS levels [85]. Another widely used method to detect cellular ROS is dihydroethidium (DHE), which can freely penetrate live cell membranes and is oxidized by intracellular ROS to form ethidium. This ethidium intercalates into chromosomal DNA, resulting in red fluorescence [86].

Lipid peroxide accumulation is considered a critical determinant in ferroptosis [87]. Specific detection of these lipid peroxidation events during ferroptosis can deepen our understanding of the regulatory mechanisms involved. Accumulated phospholipid peroxides decompose into various lipid peroxidation end products [88]. Commonly used detection methods for lipid peroxides and their metabolites include fluorescent probes, chemiluminescence, immunoassays, and high-resolution liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC-MS)-based oxidative lipidomics. The LC-MS-based oxidative lipidomics technique allows precise detection and identification of oxidized phospholipids and their metabolic products. C11-BODIPY581/591, a lipophilic live-cell dye, is widely used to detect lipid peroxidation levels and has recently been applied to indicate lipid peroxide accumulation in ferroptotic cells [89]. Under normal conditions, C11-BODIPY581/591 exhibits bright red fluorescence; oxidation of its polyunsaturated butadiene moiety causes a fluorescence emission shift from 590 nm to 510 nm, changing fluorescence color from red to green [90]. Spy-LHP is a fluorescent dye designed to detect LOOHs [91] Its two alkyl chains enhance binding affinity to lipid bilayers, enabling selective reaction with LOOHs to produce fluorescent oxidized Spy-LHP (excitation/emission at 524/535 nm). Due to these properties, Spy-LHP has been utilized in some studies to monitor lipid peroxidation during ferroptosis [46]. Another strategy for assessing lipid oxidation involves measuring its products [92] Two common lipid peroxidation products are 4-hydroxynonenal (4-HNE) and malondialdehyde (MDA). MDA reacts with thiobarbituric acid to form a chromogen, the concentration of which can be quantified spectrophotometrically; corresponding assay kits are widely used to evaluate lipid peroxidation [93, 94] Furthermore, the aldehyde group of 4-HNE reacts with 2,4-dinitrophenylhydrazine to quantify protein carbonylation in biological samples [95]. Some researchers employ thiobarbituric acid reactive substances (TBARS) assays to measure lipid peroxidation in cells, with absorbance measured at 532 nm using a microplate reader [96]. MDA detection kits are the most commonly used in lipid peroxidation research, applicable to both cells and tissues. Because MDA levels in cells are relatively low, a larger cell number is advised to obtain clearer and more reliable results.

Principles and unique advantages of ferroptosis induced by 2D nanomaterials

2D materials, owing to their distinctive physicochemical characteristics, offer a novel technological route for inducing ferroptosis in tumor cells. This chapter systematically reviews the mechanisms by which 2D nanomaterials induce ferroptosis. They synergistically facilitate ferroptosis through multiple pathways: primarily, the inherent physicochemical features—such as surface defect sites, enzyme-mimicking catalytic properties, and surface functionalization—can directly modulate ferroptosis-associated signaling pathways; furthermore, their high specific surface area allows for effective loading of diverse ferroptosis-inducing agents and enables precise release triggered by the tumor microenvironment. Importantly, certain 2D materials (e.g., black phosphorus, MoS2) also exhibit photothermal conversion abilities, which can be activated by external stimuli to further enhance the efficiency of ferroptosis induction.

Intrinsic properties of 2D materials

2D materials demonstrate complex, multilayered regulatory mechanisms in inducing ferroptosis in tumor cells, owing to their distinctive surface properties. First, atomic vacancy defects on the surface serve as highly efficient catalytic sites that promote Fenton reactions, generating sustained reactive ROS that directly drive lipid peroxidation. Second, certain 2D materials exhibit structural similarities to natural enzymes, enabling them to mimic the activities of key enzymes such as GPX4 and peroxidases, thereby specifically disrupting cellular antioxidant defense systems. Additionally, surface functionalization and optimization strategies—including the incorporation of targeting ligands or the development of stimuli-responsive release systems—can markedly improve tumor targeting and ferroptosis induction efficacy. Table 2 compiles recent examples where intrinsic properties of 2D materials have been leveraged to induce ferroptosis. These multidimensional controls, rooted in material inherent characteristics, not only overcome the limitations of traditional ferroptosis inducers but also open new technological pathways for precise cancer therapy.

Surface atomic vacancies

Surface structural defects of materials, particularly surface vacancies—point defects resulting from missing atoms—have been demonstrated to play a crucial role in modulating cellular ferroptosis. Rather than diminishing material performance, these defects, due to their high catalytic activity, act as pivotal sites that facilitate Fenton and Fenton-like reactions, efficiently producing highly oxidative hydroxyl radicals (•OH). Additionally, they may regulate the release and cycling of iron ions, thereby forming the molecular foundation of the material’s inherent ability to induce ferroptosis.

Based on this mechanistic understanding, 2D nanomaterials, represented by graphene, have emerged as highly promising platforms for modulating ferroptosis. Their appeal lies in a unique combination of physicochemical properties—including substantial specific surface area, abundant surface defect sites, and excellent biocompatibility—which together enable efficient interaction with biological systems. For instance, graphene-based derivatives have been shown to promote ferroptosis in cancer cells by interfering with central antioxidant pathways such as the system Xc⁻/GSH/GPX4 axis [97].

Beyond graphene, other classes of 2D materials such as TMDs and COFs have also demonstrated significant ferroptosis-inducing capabilities. TMDs can trigger ferroptosis through mechanisms like activating ferritinophagy and disrupting iron homeostasis [98]., whereas rationally designed COFs can integrate photoresponsive units and redox-active motifs to synergistically enhance lipid peroxidation and deplete intracellular antioxidants [99]. These examples illustrate the programmable nature and therapeutic versatility of 2D materials in targeting ferroptosis.

In summary, diverse 2D functional nanomaterials regulate ferroptosis through multiple, often material-specific mechanisms, with surface defects frequently serving as a common structural motif that links nanomaterial properties to biological outcomes. This establishes a strong materials-science foundation for developing precision nanotherapeutics aimed at inducing ferroptosis for cancer treatment.

Enzymatic activity

Enzymatic activity refers to the capability of enzymes to catalyze specific chemical reactions, usually evaluated by reaction rate. However, natural enzymes face challenges such as poor stability, susceptibility to inactivation, and difficulty in recovery, which limits their biomedical applications. To address these issues, researchers have developed nanozymes with high stability and biomimetic catalytic functions capable of mimicking critical enzymes like GPX4 and LOX, directly catalyzing the key ferroptosis reaction—lipid peroxidation. These nanozymes, integrated with smart nanocarriers, can accurately deliver iron ions or Fenton catalysts to the tumor microenvironment, improving therapeutic targeting. More importantly, nanozymes overcome the single-function limitations of natural enzymes by synergistically amplifying oxidative damage through mechanisms including GSH depletion and enhanced oxidative stress, inducing ferroptosis at multiple levels and offering new therapeutic strategies for cancer treatment.

For instance, certain piezoelectric 2D nanosheets can efficiently generate ROS under ultrasound stimulation, while their surface-rich unsaturated atomic sites confer dual enzyme-like activities, such as catalase and peroxidase. Transcriptomic and protein-level analyses indicate that such systems can activate key ferroptosis-related pathways—including GPX4, DHODH, ferritin, and long-chain acyl-CoA synthetase 4—through piezoelectric effects, thereby significantly enhancing tumor cell death [100].

Similarly, other biodegradable piezoelectric 2D nanomaterials exhibit tumor-microenvironment–responsive peroxidase-like activity, catalyzing the conversion of H₂O₂ into hydroxyl radicals in acidic conditions. Concurrently, their metal redox cycles deplete intracellular glutathione, inactivate GPX4, and amplify lipid peroxidation [101]. This combination of ROS generation and antioxidant suppression establishes a self-reinforcing oxidative mechanism that collectively drives ferroptosis.

Surface functionalization

Current studies show that 2D lamellar nanomaterials (e.g., graphene, TMDs) still face challenges such as unknown mechanism, poor targeting and off-target toxicity in inducing iron death. To enhance their biosafety and therapeutic efficacy, researchers have attempted to modify and functionally optimize their surfaces through various strategies. For example, access to targeting ligands (e.g., antibodies, peptides) can enhance the tumor selectivity and reduce the non-specific toxicity of the materials; polymer coating (e.g., polyethylene glycol PEG) or biomolecule modification (e.g., proteins) can improve the dispersion and stability; and loading of iron-based catalysts or modulation of the valence of material surfaces can enhance the lipid peroxidation reaction and further clarify the mechanism of the induction of iron death.

Furthermore, iron-based graphene oxide nanocomposites have been developed to enhance ferroptosis in hepatocellular carcinoma. These materials amplify tumor cell death primarily through substantial ROS release, effectively activating ferroptosis pathways. Similarly, magnetic graphene oxide composites, integrating GO with iron oxide nanoparticles, combine strong paramagnetism with versatile functionalization potential, providing a multimodal platform for therapeutic intervention [102].

Beyond graphene derivatives, the exploration of MXene materials has broadened the scope of ferroptosis-inducing nanotherapeutics. MXenes are a family of 2D transition metal carbides, nitrides [104], or carbonitrides, characterized by a unique layered structure [105], highly tunable surface chemistry, and versatile functionality conferred by selective etching processes. For example, biomimetic scaffold systems incorporating MXenes have been designed to release iron ions in the tumor microenvironment. This controlled release promotes glutathione depletion, downregulates GPX4, and activates lipid peroxidation, collectively inducing ferroptosis for effective cancer therapy, as demonstrated in osteosarcoma models. These advances illustrate how the potential of 2D nanomaterials to induce ferroptosis is continuously expanded through rational structural design and multifunctional integration [103].

Ferroptosis inducers

Ferroptosis inducers are compounds or nanomaterials that specifically trigger programmed ferroptosis. Their core mechanism involves disrupting redox balance and promoting lipid peroxidation accumulation. Key pathways include: (1) inhibiting GPX4 activity, thus weakening the GSH-dependent antioxidant system; (2) depleting GSH, which diminishes free radical scavenging capacity; (3) increasing intracellular free iron levels or introducing Fenton catalysts to generate hydroxyl radicals, thereby inducing a lipid oxidation chain reaction.

Building on these insights, multifunctional nanoplatforms have been engineered to precisely intervene in ferroptosis through synergistic mechanisms. For example, one class of nanoplatforms combines acidic and redox-responsive components to deplete glutathione, release Fenton-active metal ions, and simultaneously deliver inhibitors of antioxidant pathways such as GCH1/BH₄, thereby coordinately elevating lipid peroxidation and accelerating ferroptosis [106]. To enhance tumor specificity, other platforms utilize targeting ligands like folic acid to improve cellular uptake while integrating agents that suppress the mevalonate pathway, leading to reduced GPX4 expression and enhanced ferroptotic susceptibility [107].

Beyond targeting the canonical GSH/GPX4 axis, recent designs also focus on disrupting lipid metabolism and iron homeostasis. Nanosystems based on two-dimensional materials such as black phosphorus have been developed to co-deliver GPX4 inhibitors and modulate key ferroptosis markers including ferritin and long-chain acyl-CoA synthetase 4 [108]. Similarly, covalent organic framework-based platforms enable the simultaneous release of Fenton-like catalysts and GPX4 inhibitors. This dual strategy ensures persistent oxidative damage even in the presence of residual cellular antioxidants, ultimately triggering ferroptosis through comprehensive disruption of membranes, mitochondria, and lysosomes [109].

In summary, these advanced nanoplatforms exemplify a trend toward multi-pathway regulation, where synergistic targeting of antioxidant defense, lipid peroxidation, and iron metabolism collectively enhances ferroptosis induction. This integrated approach represents a promising direction for developing efficient, multi-targeted, and precise ferroptosis-based therapies.

Iron ion supplements

To effectively induce ferroptosis, iron ion supplements have emerged as a material strategy that exogenously provides active iron to elevate intracellular LIP levels. These supplements typically induce ferroptosis by enhancing the Fenton reaction, promoting ROS production, and synergistically inhibiting the antioxidant system.

Building upon these strategies, synergistic nanoplatforms have been designed by integrating ferroptosis inducers with other therapeutic modalities to enhance anticancer efficacy. For instance, iron-doped LDHs can serve as both a photothermal agent and a source of active Fe²⁺ ions [110]. In the tumor microenvironment, these ions drive the Fenton reaction to generate cytotoxic reactive oxygen species, thereby inducing ferroptosis. This approach can be further refined by co-delivering natural compounds such as epigallocatechin-3-gallate (EGCG), which promotes the Fe³⁺/Fe²⁺ redox cycle to amplify Fenton chemistry [111]. Such systems not only enhance hydroxyl radical production but also downregulate key antioxidant defenses like xCT and GPX4 while activating pro-apoptotic signals, resulting in a combined ferroptotic and apoptotic outcome.

Similarly, other acid-degradable LDH-based platforms have been utilized to deliver artemisinin derivatives like dihydroartemisinin (DHA) [112]. Upon degradation in the acidic tumor niche, the released iron ions synergize with DHA to intensify Fenton-mediated oxidative stress, co-activating apoptosis and ferroptosis pathways. These examples highlight a promising trend in nanotherapeutics: the rational combination of ferroptosis inducers with bioactive agents or stimuli-responsive materials to achieve multi-mechanistic anticancer activity through coordinated redox disruption and signaling modulation.

Protein and nucleic acid formulations

Protein and nucleic acid formulations, as precise regulators of key ferroptosis pathways, offer the dual benefits of inducing ferroptosis and activating anti-tumor immunity, thus becoming a major direction in ferroptosis therapy. Targeted intervention of ferroptosis regulatory molecules via efficient delivery systems can significantly improve therapeutic outcomes.

In addition, nanoplatforms have been engineered to co-deliver nucleic acid regulators and metal ions, enabling synergistic induction of ferroptosis and modulation of the tumor immune microenvironment. For instance, siRNA targeting immune checkpoints such as PD-L1 can be delivered to simultaneously block immunosuppressive signals and downregulate key ferroptosis defense proteins like GPX4. This dual action enhances T-cell infiltration and promotes ferroptotic cell death, demonstrating a mutually reinforcing interaction between ferroptosis and antitumor immunity [113].

Similarly, other designs focus on the co-delivery of ferroptosis-inducing metal ions (e.g., Fe²⁺ or Mn²⁺) and siRNA targeting antioxidant enzymes such as DHODH. In the acidic tumor microenvironment, the released ions catalyze ROS generation via Fenton-like reactions, while the siRNA suppresses cellular antioxidant capacity, collectively amplifying lipid peroxidation and accelerating ferroptosis [114].

Furthermore, nanocarriers integrating pro-inflammatory cytokines (e.g., interferon-γ) with redox-active metal ions have been developed. These systems not only deplete glutathione and promote hydroxyl radical production through metal-ion catalysis but also inhibit the expression of central ferroptosis regulators such as SLC7A11 via cytokine signaling, thereby synergistically activating the ferroptosis pathway and reshaping the immunogenic tumor microenvironment [65].

In summary, these advanced nanoplatforms illustrate a convergent strategy: precise co-delivery of biological regulators (proteins or nucleic acids) together with metal-ion catalysts enables simultaneous induction of ferroptosis and immunomodulation. This combined approach enhances therapeutic efficacy by targeting multiple vulnerability nodes in tumors, providing a robust foundation for the development of efficient and synergistic cancer therapies.

The combination therapy not only overcomes the limitations of single treatments but also significantly enhances treatment selectivity and efficacy through spatiotemporal control of stimuli, offering new possibilities for clinical translation. Near-infrared light (NIR)

Near-infrared light is an electromagnetic wave with wavelengths ranging from 700 to 1300 nm, lying between visible light and mid-infrared light. NIR has several unique advantages: its wavelength range enables penetration of biological tissues by several centimeters (e.g., 5–10 cm subcutaneously), making it ideal for non-invasive therapy and imaging. Especially in the 750–900 nm (NIR-I) and 1000–1300 nm (NIR-II) bands, absorption and scattering by biological tissues are minimized, offering both low phototoxicity and a high signal-to-noise ratio, ensuring safe and efficient in vivo applications [124]. Additionally, NIR can remotely activate photosensitizers or nanomaterials, enabling precise spatiotemporal control of drug release, catalytic reactions (such as Fenton reaction), and photothermal therapy.

Building on these strategies, photoresponsive nanoplatforms further enhance tumor treatment by synergizing photothermal effects with ferroptosis induction. For instance, biomimetic nanostructures utilize photothermal materials to generate localized hyperthermia under near-infrared irradiation, which not only directly ablates tumor cells but also promotes the degradation of co-loaded iron-based nanoparticles and accelerates Fe²⁺ release. This process significantly amplifies the Fenton reaction and the production of reactive oxygen species. Meanwhile, the photothermal effect itself can directly intensify membrane lipid peroxidation, collectively driving the onset of ferroptosis [115].

Moreover, such platforms are often designed as multifunctional integrated systems. The photothermal effect can alleviate hypoxia in the tumor microenvironment, thereby promoting the polarization of immunosuppressive macrophages toward the antitumor M1 phenotype and activating adaptive immune responses. By combining photothermal therapy with chemodynamic therapy, photodynamic therapy, and other mechanisms, these nanosystems achieve synergy among ferroptosis induction, immune microenvironment remodeling, and direct thermal ablation, demonstrating significant tumor suppression both in vitro and in vivo [116].

In summary, through the rational integration of photothermal conversion materials and ferroptosis-inducing components, along with coordinated modulation of the tumor immune microenvironment, such photoresponsive nanoplatforms represent a multi-mechanistic and synergistic treatment strategy, offering a novel and efficient approach for precise cancer therapy.

Ultrasound (US)

Ultrasound is a mechanical wave with a frequency exceeding the upper limit of human hearing (20 kHz) and is widely applied in clinical diagnostic imaging, transdermal drug delivery, thrombolysis, gene therapy, and local drug activation. In ferroptosis treatment, ultrasound significantly improves efficacy through three mechanisms: the activation of sonosensitizers to generate reactive ROS, promotion of iron ion delivery, and enhancement of cell membrane permeability.

For instance, certain ultrasound-responsive two-dimensional nanoplatforms can be designed to release metal ions and generate substantial ROS under sonication. These processes collectively amplify oxidative stress, induce endoplasmic reticulum stress, and trigger lipid peroxidation, thereby driving ferroptosis in tumor cells [125]. Moreover, the ROS burst and released ions can further activate immunogenic cell death pathways, such as the cGAS/STING signaling, and promote the release of damage-associated molecular patterns, which in turn enhance antitumor immunity. When combined with immune checkpoint inhibitors, such ultrasound-activated systems have demonstrated synergistic therapeutic effects in preclinical tumor models, along with improved biosafety and prolonged circulation achieved through biomimetic surface modification [119].

In summary, ultrasound, by enhancing ROS generation and activating ferroptosis-related pathways, in combination with the precise targeted release of nanomaterials, shows remarkable anti-tumor potential and represents a promising approach for ferroptosis-based combination therapy.

Magnetic field (MF)

The magnetic field is generated by magnetic materials (e.g., Fe₃O₄, cobalt ferrite) or external magnetic field devices, which can deeply penetrate biological tissues through magnetic and magneto-thermal effects to achieve precise drug release and multiple physico-chemical synergisms, and provide a highly specific and low-toxicity modulation platform for iron-dead tumor therapy.

For instance, certain two-dimensional magnetic nanomaterials exhibit not only superparamagnetic properties for enhanced tumor accumulation under external magnetic guidance but also high photothermal conversion efficiency [121]. This combination allows for imaging-guided therapy—where the material itself serves as a contrast agent for magnetic resonance imaging—along with spatially controlled photothermal and chemodynamic effects, enabling real-time monitoring and localized treatment.

In conclusion, the combination of magnetic fields and magnetic nanomaterials not only synergistically promotes tumor ferroptosis through magnetic heating and chemical reactions but also enables efficient treatment localization and imaging guidance, highlighting their vast clinical application potential.

Radiation (RT)

Ionizing radiation, which mainly includes high-energy rays such as X-rays, γ-rays and proton beams, has become an important means of treating advanced solid tumors by virtue of its precise localization ability and deep tissue penetration. Radiation not only directly damages DNA, but also stimulates the production of reactive ROS, which in turn synergistically activates new cell death pathways such as iron death, achieving efficient tumor killing and drug resistance reversal.

In this context, functionalized nanomaterials have been employed as radiosensitizers to amplify these effects. For example, certain nano-radiosensitizers can enhance radiation-induced DNA damage while concurrently promoting lipid peroxidation, thereby co-activating the ferroptosis pathway and improving tumor control [123]. Other strategies utilize two-dimensional nanosheets with enzyme-like activity, which under radiation catalyze the conversion of intracellular H₂O₂ into highly toxic hydroxyl radicals [122]. This process depletes antioxidant reserves like glutathione and elevates oxidative stress, leading to the simultaneous induction of apoptosis and ferroptosis.

In conclusion, ionizing radiation combined with functionalized nanomaterials synergistically enhances radiosensitization of ferroptosis through multiple mechanisms, showing significant clinical potential, particularly for overcoming radiotherapy resistance and treating advanced, refractory tumors.

Tumor ferroptosis induced by different 2D nanomaterials

Based on the above design principles, 2D nanomaterials have made significant progress in inducing iron death therapy in tumors, and representative research cases are presented below by material, mainly including graphene, layered double hydroxides, black phosphorus, transition metal sulfides, MXenes, 2D COF, and other 2D materials.

Graphene

Graphene is a 2D honeycomb crystal formed by a single layer of carbon atoms via sp² hybridization. Each carbon atom is covalently bonded to three neighboring atoms, and the non-hybridized p-orbital electrons form a delocalized π-bond network, imparting ultrathin properties (with a thickness of just 0.335 nm), high symmetry, and unique quantum characteristics. Furthermore, graphene also has exceptionally high mechanical strength and excellent elasticity [126], ltra-high electron mobility and outstanding electrical conductivity [127], as well as excepti uonal thermal conductivity [128]and optical transparency [129]. From a chemical perspective, graphene possesses a high specific surface area, with its edges and defect sites readily available for functional modification. These remarkable properties make it an ideal material for electronic devices, transparent flexible electrodes, and hold great promise for applications in catalysis, adsorption, and biomedicine.

However, strong van der Waals forces and π-π stacking interactions between graphene layers lead to poor solubility in solution, causing aggregation that significantly impacts its biocompatibility and processing performance [130, 131], thereby restricting its biomedical applications. To address these limitations, researchers have developed graphene derivatives. GO is a chemically modified form of graphene, with its surface and edges functionalized with a variety of oxygen-containing groups (e.g., hydroxyl, epoxy, carboxyl, and carbonyl) [132], transforming it from a hydrophobic conductor to a hydrophilic material, although its conductivity is significantly reduced. Reduced graphene oxide (rGO) is produced by thermally, chemically, or UV-reducing GO [133], partially restoring its conductivity while lowering oxygen content, surface charge, and hydrophilicity [134]. Compared with graphene, both GO and reduced rGO demonstrate superior performance in biomedical applications, primarily due to their hydrophilic functional groups, which aid in stable aqueous dispersion and prevent aggregation driven by van der Waals forces and hydrophobic interactions [135]. Moreover, the abundance of surface functional groups on GO enables its role as a multifunctional nanoplatform, facilitating the conjugation of various agents and broadening its use in targeted therapy, photodynamic therapy (PDT), PTT, and cancer diagnostics [136].

Shukla et al. [137] engineered a transferrin-immobilized graphene oxide nanocomposite (Tfn@GOAPTES) for cancer cell-targeted therapy (Fig. 4A). Infrared spectroscopy confirmed the successful grafting of amino propyl triethoxysilane(APTES) onto the GO surface through the formation of Si–O–C bonds, validating the construction of the composite. The nanomaterial maintained stable zeta potential and particle size over a 7-day period, indicating excellent physicochemical stability. In vitro, Prussian blue staining revealed that Tfn@GOAPTES was effectively internalized by HeLa cells, promoting upregulation of TfR expression, with staining intensity correlating positively with material concentration (Fig. 4B). Reactive ROS detection using the DCFH-DA assay (Fig. 4C) showed that Tfn@GOAPTES markedly enhanced ROS generation in HeLa cells. This oxidative stress led to DNA double-strand breaks (DSBs), as evidenced by increased γ-H2AX expression (Fig. 4D), indicating that the composite induces cancer cell death via a ROS-mediated mechanism. Western blot analysis further showed a significant decrease in acetylation of α-tubulin at lysine 40 (Ac-K40) in Tfn@GOAPTES-treated cells (Fig. 4E ⅰ), suggesting enhanced microtubule rigidity that may impair cellular motility. Additionally, expression of autophagy-related protein LC3a/b was upregulated (Fig. 4E ⅱ), implying elevated autophagic activity. Collectively, Tfn@GOAPTES promotes cancer cell death through ROS induction, reduced tubulin acetylation, and enhanced autophagy, demonstrating its potential as a promising new chemotherapeutic agent.

Layered double hydroxides

Layered double hydroxides are positively charged hydroxide layers formed by hexahydroxy complexes of transition metal ions, which electrostatically bind interlayer anions to form 2D layered nanomaterials analogous to hydrotalcite (Mg(OH)₂). LDHs can exist as nanoparticles composed of several layers or can be exfoliated into few-layer or even single-layer nanosheets [138]. Their general chemical formula is M1 − x²⁺ Mx³⁺(OH2) x⁺ [An⁻]x/n · mH2O, where M²⁺ and M³⁺ represent divalent and trivalent metal ions (e.g., Mg²⁺/Al³⁺), respectively; trivalent ions substitute divalent ions generating positive charge, with a molar ratio typically between 2 and 4. Interlayer anions (An⁻) compensate for this positive charge. By adjusting the types and ratios of metal cations as well as the intercalated anions, LDHs exhibit diverse physicochemical properties [139], including in-plane isomorphic substitution, tunable size and chemical composition, biodegradability, and pH sensitivity [140–144], making them promising drug carriers and imaging enhancers.

Liu et al. [65] developed ultra-thin manganese-doped layered double hydroxide nanosheets (uMn-LDHs) via a simple bottom-up approach. In this process, H₂O₂ served as an oxidizing agent to reduce interlayer stacking without the need for additional additives, followed by thermal treatment at 100 °C to promote exfoliation. Taking advantage of the high surface area and positive charge density of uMn-LDHs, the pro-inflammatory cytokine Interferon-gamma (IFNγ) was electrostatically adsorbed onto their surface, constructing a ferritin-based nano-immunotherapeutic system, IFNγ/uMn-LDHs (Fig. 5A).

Western blot analysis in 4T1 cells showed that both free IFNγ and IFNγ/uMn-LDHs significantly downregulated the expression of SLC7A11, confirming that IFNγ activity was preserved after nanoformulation. The accumulation of IFNγ at tumor sites inhibited de novo GSH synthesis resulting from SLC7A11 suppression, synergistically enhancing GSH depletion and lipid peroxidation in tumor cells (Fig. 5E). DAMPs released during ferroptosis activated DCs (Fig. 5F), leading to upregulation of CD80 and CD86. In vivo, treatment of the primary tumor in a bilateral tumor-bearing mouse model also significantly suppressed distant tumor growth, indicating effective activation of systemic antitumor immunity and prevention of recurrence. Collectively, this study presents a uMn-LDHs/IFNγ-based nano-immunotherapy platform that synergistically induces ferroptosis and immune activation, providing a promising strategy for the development of next-generation immunoregulatory anticancer agents.

Black phosphorus

Black phosphorus, an allotrope of elemental phosphorus, is produced by transforming white phosphorus under conditions of high temperature (∼200 °C) and high pressure (~ 12,000 atm). It possesses a distinctive 2D layered crystalline structure. Its monolayer counterpart, known as phosphorene, consists of puckered phosphorus atoms arranged in a zigzag pattern, connected by strong intralayer covalent P–P bonds, while adjacent layers are held together by van der Waals forces, forming a corrugated, honeycomb-like architecture [145]. This unique anisotropic structure imparts BP with exceptional electrical conductivity [146], intriguing topological characteristics, and outstanding optical and mechanical properties [147], distinguishing it from other 2D materials. Owing to its narrow and direct bandgap, BP holds great promise for use in free-space and fiber-based optical communication technologies.

Compared with other 2D materials such as MoS₂, graphene, and tungsten disulfide (WS₂), BP offers more facile synthesis along with superior biodegradability and minimal cytotoxicity [148]. Its distinctive puckered lattice structure enhances the surface-to-volume ratio [149] and endows BP with exceptional chemical sensing capabilities [150]. Coupled with high charge carrier mobility, a unique electronic configuration, and relatively large interlayer spacing, BP has emerged as a highly promising material for biomedical and electronic applications [151]. Furthermore, BP demonstrates excellent drug-loading efficiency and outstanding properties for PDT and PTT. In nanosheet or quantum dot form, as opposed to bulk materials, BP exhibits favorable renal clearance, superior biocompatibility, low toxicity, and a tunable bandgap suitable for diverse application scenarios.

Liu et al. [152]designed a novel ferroptosis-inducing nanostructure by constructing a PEGylated Cu⁺/Cu²⁺-doped black phosphorus@polypyrrole (BP@CPP) Z-scheme heterojunction. Copper ions were chelated onto the surface of BP via phosphate functional groups, followed by in situ polymerization of pyrrole to yield the composite (Fig. 6A). As a Z-type heterojunction, BP@CPP exhibits outstanding photocatalytic performance. Under laser irradiation, the heterostructure effectively promotes the separation of photogenerated electron–hole pairs, thereby enhancing both oxidative and reductive capabilities. Characterization techniques including X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS), solid-state UV–Vis–NIR diffuse reflectance spectroscopy, and photocurrent response analysis were employed to investigate its band structure and heterojunction behavior.Specifically, Fig. 6B reveals that the bandgaps (Eg) of BP and CP (polypyrrole) are 1.53 eV and 3.10 eV, respectively, as derived from curvature tangent analysis. The XPS spectra (Fig. 6C) indicate valence band maxima of 1.18 eV for BP and 2.94 eV for CP. Photocurrent response measurements over time (Fig. 6D) show that BP@CPP exhibits a rapid and reversible response to 808 nm laser irradiation, maintaining stable output over five cycles. These results confirm the material’s high electron–hole separation efficiency and strong photocatalytic activity. Collectively, these findings support a Z-scheme electron transfer pathway for BP@CPP (Fig. 6E). In vitro studies employed Thiol Tracker Violet fluorescence staining to assess intracellular GSH levels. Under 808 nm laser irradiation, a marked decrease in green fluorescence was observed in BP@CPP-treated cells, indicating effective GSH depletion and subsequent accumulation of reactive ROS. Concurrently, Western blot analysis revealed significant downregulation of GPX4 expression, leading to elevated levels of lipid peroxides (Fig. 6F and Fig. 6G), thereby enhancing ferroptotic cell death. In vivo therapeutic evaluations demonstrated that BP@CPP effectively suppressed tumor growth via ferroptosis induction while maintaining favorable biosafety profiles.

Transition metal dichalcogenides

Transition metal dichalcogenides are a family of layered compounds with the general formula MX₂, where M is a transition metal (e.g., Mo, W) and X is a chalcogen element (e.g., S, Se, Te). The monolayer structure of TMDs exhibits a characteristic sandwich-like configuration, in which a single metal atom layer is confined between two chalcogen layers. Intralayer atoms are bonded via strong covalent interactions, while adjacent layers are stacked through weak van der Waals forces. This distinctive layered configuration enables the fabrication of monolayer or few-layer nanosheets via approaches such as mechanical exfoliation and chemical vapor deposition (CVD). Owing to their inherent layered nature, TMDs possess a high specific surface area, tunable bandgap [153], appropriate electrical conductivity [154] and low cytotoxicity, making them highly attractive for biosensor applications. The enlarged surface area provides abundant molecular binding sites, whereas their electrical conductivity and bandgap characteristics are critical determinants of sensing performance. The biocompatibility and electrical behavior of TMDs are highly dependent on their crystalline phase and composition, including the type of transition metal and chalcogen, as well as atomic arrangement [155]. Furthermore, their bandgap is strongly influenced by nanosheet thickness and surface functionalization [156]. To date, prototypical TMDs including MoS₂, WS₂, molybdenum selenide (MoSe₂), tungsten selenide (WSe₂), and molybdenum telluride (MoTe₂) have received extensive research attention [157].

Duan et al. [158]innovatively designed an integrated microneedle (MN) system to synergistically activate ferroptosis pathways and enhance the combined anti-tumor effects of nanozyme catalysis, PTT, and traditional Chinese medicine (TCM). This system employed dissolvable MNs for transdermal delivery of a novel bimetallic plasmonic nanozyme, BPNzyme (gold-decorated MoS₂, Au@MoS₂), in combination with a TCM molecule, β-elemene (β-ELE). Specifically, 2D MoS₂ nanosheets were obtained via conventional liquid-phase exfoliation (LPE) from MoS₂ powder. Taking advantage of MoS₂’s intrinsic self-reducing property, Au nanoparticles (AuNPs) were subsequently in situ anchored onto the surface, forming the composite nanozyme Au@MoS₂ (Fig. 7A). Both AuNPs and MoS₂ have been extensively reported to exhibit POD-like activity, mimicking natural horseradish peroxidase (HRP) [159]. More importantly, MoS₂ contains unsaturated sulfur atoms that serve as active sites for efficient GSH oxidation [160]. Here, the dual enzyme-mimetic activity of BPNzyme Au@MoS₂ was validated through two classical colorimetric assays (Fig. 7B). The colorimetric conversion of the chromogenic substrates 3,3′,5,5′-tetramethylbenzidine (TMB) and 5,5′-dithiobis-(2-nitrobenzoic acid) (DTNB) confirmed the robust POD-like activity of BPNzyme Au@MoS₂ (Fig. 7C and Fig. 7D). Density functional theory (DFT) calculations (Fig. 7E) demonstrated that high-density AuNP doping significantly enhanced the NIR photothermal conversion efficiency and POD-like nanozymatic activity of MoS₂, thereby maximizing its potential for synergistic photothermal/catalytic therapy. To further investigate the underlying antitumor molecular mechanisms, transcriptomic analysis was conducted to explore differential gene expression in B16F10 cells treated with Au@MoS₂ + β-ELE under NIR irradiation. KEGG enrichment analysis (Fig. 7F) revealed that the Au@MoS₂ + β-ELE + NIR treatment group induced B16F10 cell death primarily via activation of ferroptosis-associated signaling pathways, including HIF-1, TNF, apoptosis, and glutamate metabolism. This dual-mode strategy, combining TCM and NIR-based photothermal therapy, markedly remodeled the TME and further promoted ferroptosis in tumor cells. By incorporating BPNzyme and β-ELE into biocompatible and water-soluble hyaluronic acid (HA) MNs for localized delivery to melanoma lesions, this system achieved highly efficient nanozyme/PTT/TCM combinatorial therapy with optimal tumor inhibition. This study offers valuable insights into the modulation of heterobimetallic nanozyme catalytic activity and its role in TME-mediated ferroptosis regulation. Beyond β-ELE, other anticancer agents or ferroptosis inducers may also serve as promising candidates for combination therapies with the BPNzyme-based MN platform in future investigations.

MXenes

MXenes are a class of 2D materials derived from the selective etching of transition metal carbides, nitrides, or carbonitrides [161]. They typically follow the general formula Mₙ₊₁XₙTₓ, where M represents an early transition metal (e.g., Ti, V, Mo), X denotes carbon and/or nitrogen atoms [104], and Tₓ refers to surface-terminating functional groups such as –OH, –O, or –F. Structurally, MXenes feature a graphene-like layered architecture composed of alternating transition metal and carbon/nitrogen layers. The etching process introduces various surface terminations, thereby endowing MXenes with tunable surface chemistries [105].Owing to their ultrathin lamellar structure and abundant hydrophilic terminal groups (e.g., –F, –OH, –O), MXenes exhibit excellent metallic conductivity and high ion adsorption capacity [162–165]. Furthermore, they possess a high negative zeta potential (exceeding − 40 mV), which ensures remarkable colloidal stability in aqueous solutions without the need for surfactants [162, 166]. Among them, Ti₃C₂Tₓ stands out as a highly promising drug delivery platform, thanks to its large specific surface area, rich surface functionalities, and 2D planar morphology that enables high drug-loading capacity.

Zhu et al. [122] developed a photothermal-responsive drug delivery system based on nTCTs, where the natural compound RSV was loaded onto the nanosheets to form a composite material termed nTCTRPs. This platform enabled synergistic radiosensitization therapy (Fig. 8A and Fig. 8B). Acting as a nanozyme, the nTCTs exhibited radiation-enhanced cascade enzyme-like activity, significantly catalyzing the decomposition of hydrogen peroxide into •OH under X-ray irradiation. Concurrently, they depleted intracellular GSH in tumor cells, as confirmed using DTNB as a GSH-specific probe. Upon X-ray or NIR laser irradiation, nTCTs led to greater GSH consumption, with apparent specific activities of 2.66 U mg⁻¹ and 2.07 U mg⁻¹, respectively, compared to 1.70 U mg⁻¹ without irradiation (Fig. 8C).

(A) Schematic representation of the preparation of nTCTRPs; (B) Illustration of the synergistic radiosensitizing effect of natural RSV and nTCT, and their inhibitory action on tumor metastasis; (C) Substrate-specific catalytic activity of TiO₂ nanoparticles and nTCTs under X-ray irradiation using GSH; (D–E) Catalytic activity of nTCTRPs, 808 nm NIR-irradiated nTCTRPs, and continuously irradiated nTCTRPs, using H₂O₂ (D) and GSH (E) as substrates; (F) Fluorescence imaging of 4T1 cells under different treatments: (a) intracellular ROS detected using DCFH-DA probe (scale: 50 μm); (b) GSH detected by ThiolTracker™ Violet (scale: 50 μm); (c) DNA damage visualized via γ-H2AX fluorescent labeling (scale: 10 μm); (d) mitochondrial membrane potential assessed by JC-1 staining (scale: 50 μm); Copyright 2023 Published by Elsevier Ltd

To achieve on-demand activation, stearic acid and lauric acid were co-loaded with nTCTs to form the nTCTRPs composite. Upon 808 nm NIR irradiation, the fatty acid matrix transitioned from solid to liquid, thereby re-exposing the previously blocked active sites and restoring the cascade enzyme activity enhanced by radiation (Fig. 8D and Fig. 8E).In vitro experiments demonstrated that 4T1 cells treated with nTCTRPs and X-ray alone exhibited similar colony-forming ability as radiation therapy alone. However, the combination of 808 nm NIR irradiation significantly suppressed colony formation, and the synergistic application of photothermal therapy and radiotherapy further enhanced the antitumor effect. Using fluorescence probes DCFH-DA and ThiolTracker™ Violet to detect intracellular ROS and GSH levels, the results indicated that combined photothermal and radiotherapy treatment markedly increased ROS generation while depleting GSH (Fig. 8F). The elevated ROS levels induced extensive DNA damage, evidenced by increased γ-H2AX foci. Additionally, mitochondrial lipid peroxidation led to mitochondrial dysfunction, cytochrome c release, and caspase-3 activation, ultimately triggering ferroptosis to eliminate cancer cells.

In summary, this study presents a temperature-controlled spatiotemporal drug release platform that synergizes the radiosensitization effect of natural RSV with Ti₃C₂Tₓ nanosheets. The multifunctional composite exhibits great potential as a radiosensitizer for enhanced radiotherapy and metastasis inhibition in cancer treatment.

2D covalent organic frameworks

2D covalent organic frameworks [167–170] are a class of polymers constructed from organic monomers linked via covalent bonds in two dimensions, which subsequently stack in an ordered manner through non-covalent interactions. COFs are defined by their permanent porosity and high crystallinity, typically characterized by nitrogen adsorption measurements and powder X-ray diffraction (PXRD). Unlike three-dimensional COFs (3D-COFs), whose crystallinity and structural growth are determined solely by covalent bond formation, the nature and magnitude of non-covalent interactions in 2D-COFs profoundly influence their crystallinity (or lack thereof in some cases) and functional properties.

Non-covalent interactions play a critical role in the formation of 2D-COFs and significantly affect the bulk properties of the resulting materials, including surface area, electrical conductivity [171–173], capacitance [174, 175], and photoluminescence [176–180]. Owing to their highly ordered porous structures, excellent chemical stability, and tunable functional groups, COFs possess broad application potential across diverse fields such as catalysis, sensing, electrochemistry, adsorption and separation, energy storage, and nanomedicine [181–185].Particularly in biomedical applications, COFs have garnered significant attention due to their large specific surface area and abundant porosity. The extensive pore network not only serves as an excellent carrier for hydrophobic drugs but also facilitates the outward diffusion of reactive ROS during photothermal therapy and diagnosis. Furthermore, imine-linked COFs exhibit acid-sensitive degradability, rendering them potentially biodegradable within the acidic tumor microenvironment. As highly crystalline materials, the ordered structure of COFs also markedly enhances their photothermal conversion efficiency [186]. Leveraging these unique advantages, the research on COFs in energy and biomedical fields has seen rapid growth in recent years [187–189].

Zhou et al. [109] developed an innovative COF-based therapeutic system, RSL3@COF-Fc, designed to enhance chemokinetic therapy (CDT) by disrupting the redox homeostasis of tumor cells. This system employs ferrocenecarboxaldehyde (FcCHO) as a Fenton-like catalyst, partially substituting COF monomers to form covalently modified ferrocene-containing COF-Fc nanoparticles, into which the small-molecule GPX4 covalent inhibitor RSL3 is integrated (Fig. 9A). Under the mildly acidic conditions of the tumor microenvironment, COF-Fc efficiently catalyzes H₂O₂ to generate highly reactive •OH (Fig. 9B). Following cellular internalization, the gradual release of RSL3 inhibits GPX4, a key enzyme of the tumor cell antioxidant system, thereby disrupting redox homeostasis, activating ferroptosis pathways, and leading to significant accumulation of lipid peroxides (Fig. 9C). Metabolomic analysis further confirmed the significant upregulation of up to 35 oxidized lipid metabolites (Fig. 9D), including inflammatory mediators such as iPF₂α-I and LTB4. Subcellular alterations including mitochondrial shrinkage, lysosomal deacidification, and plasma membrane rupture corroborated ferroptosis-mediated cellular damage.

In an HT-1080 tumor xenograft model, a single intratumoral injection of RSL3@COF-Fc markedly inhibited tumor growth, accompanied by reduced GPX4 activity, elevated MDA levels, and decreased GSH content (Fig. 9E–G). Moreover, treatment with the ferroptosis inhibitor Lip-1 effectively reversed these therapeutic effects, further validating the central role of ferroptosis in the mechanism of action. This nanomaterial not only offers advantages such as facile scalable synthesis, high selectivity, and favorable biosafety but also provides a novel strategy and paradigm for COF-based nanomedicine design.

Other materials

Bismuth

Graphene, TMDs and black phosphorus are widely studied 2D materials [190–192]. In recent years, beyond these classical 2D materials, many emerging layered materials such as MXenes and COFs have demonstrated unique potential in radiotherapy sensitization. Bismuth (Bi), the heaviest element in the pnictogen group (atomic number 83), is an ideal candidate for RT applications due to its high atomic number [193, 194]. Moreover, Bi exhibits low toxicity, and its compounds have been used as drugs for over two centuries [195]. Ultrathin 2D bismuth nanosheets (BNs) have shown excellent promise in cancer diagnosis and therapy, including photoacoustic/computed tomography (PA/CT) imaging, PTT, chemotherapy, immunotherapy, and radiotherapy [196–200]. However, to date, no reports have demonstrated the enhancement of ferroptosis-mediated radiotherapy by 2D bismuth materials.

Yin et al. [201] prepared a multi-enzyme mimetic nanozyme composite by in situ growth of MnOx nanoparticles on 2D BNs, forming a Bi-MnOx nanozyme (BMs) designed to synergistically enhance radiotherapy via dysregulation of iron metabolism. The BMs exhibited POD, GPX, and NADPH oxidase (NDH)-like activities. Further functionalization with BSA yielded well-dispersed and biocompatible BMs-MnOx@BSA nanozymes (termed BMBs) (Fig. 10A).

Under simulated tumor microenvironment conditions (pH 5.5), BMBs demonstrated remarkable triple enzyme activities: efficient consumption of H₂O₂ (POD-like activity with a Michaelis constant, Km = 315.9 mM), depletion of GSH from 10 mM to 1.5 mM within 12 h (GPX-like activity, Km = 112.6 mM), and 73.5% consumption of NADPH under neutral conditions (NDH-like activity, Km = 0.772 mM) (Fig. 10B–D). This multifaceted enzymatic activity synergistically depleted key antioxidants in the tumor microenvironment, inducing ferroptosis and enhancing antitumor efficacy.

Based on GSH and NADPH depletion induced by BMBs, it was hypothesized that BMBs suppress GPX4 and FSP1 activities, thereby promoting ferroptosis under IR. Using the lipid peroxidation probe C11-BODIPY, the strongest fluorescence signal was observed in the BMBs + IR group, indicating significantly enhanced lipid peroxidation (Fig. 10E). Western blot semi-quantitative analysis further confirmed that GPX4 and FSP1 protein levels were significantly downregulated in Lewis lung carcinoma (LLC) tumors treated with RT + BMBs compared to RT alone (Fig. 10F), demonstrating that BMBs promote ferroptosis by inactivating GPX4 and FSP1, thus enhancing radiotherapy efficacy.

In summary, the BMBs nanozyme leverages the high atomic number of Bi to enhance X-ray absorption, while MnOx mediated multi-enzyme mimetic activities deplete antioxidants, synergistically activating ferroptosis and significantly improving tumor radiotherapy outcomes. This approach holds great promise for clinical translation.

Iron phosphorus trisulfide

Iron phosphorus trisulfide (FePS₃) is a typical layered phosphorous trisulfide belonging to the MPX₃ family (M = Fe, Mn, Ni, Co; X = S, Se), attracting broad attention due to its unique physicochemical properties and applications in catalysis [202, 203], batteries [204, 205], sensors [206–208], and photodetectors [208, 209]. In biomedical fields, FePS₃ nanosheets exhibit excellent photothermal performance [210] and notable Fenton catalytic activity owing to their high specific surface area and abundant active sites, making them promising candidates for CDT of tumors [211]. As a single 2D nanoscale platform, FePS₃ integrates PTT, PDT, and Fenton catalysis, offering a versatile approach for synergistic tumor treatment.

Ye et al. [212] exploited these properties to develop a smart hydrogel system for osteosarcoma treatment and bone regeneration. The system consists of a Gelatin Methacryloyl (GelMA) hydrogel matrix uniformly embedding nano-hydroxyapatite (nHA) and FePS₃ composite particles, forming a GelMA-nHA/FePS₃ scaffold (Fig. 11A). The scaffold exhibited sufficient photothermal temperature for tumor ablation (Fig. 11B), and probe studies with TMB and DPBF confirmed enhanced •OH and ¹O₂ generation under NIR irradiation, indicating effective CDT, PDT, and PTT synergy (Fig. 11C-D).

In vitro antitumor assays showed that GelMA-nHA/FePS₃ significantly inhibited tumor cell proliferation under NIR light. ROS levels detected by DCFH-DA probe displayed markedly increased fluorescence in GelMA-FePS3 and GelMA-nHA/FePS3 groups upon irradiation (Fig. 11E), demonstrating that photothermal effects synergize with Fenton and PDT processes to generate abundant ROS. Moreover, both scaffold groups exhibited reduced GSH/GSSG ratios (Fig. 11F) and GPX activity (Fig. 11G), regardless of laser exposure, suggesting intracellular Fe3+ is reduced to Fe2+ by GSH, triggering GSH depletion and GPX4 inhibition, thereby activating ferroptosis.Beyond tumor suppression, the GelMA-nHA/FePS3 scaffold showed excellent biocompatibility and promoted proliferation and osteogenic differentiation of bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs). Under physiological conditions, the scaffold gradually degraded and sustainedly released Ca2+, PO₄3−, and Fe3+ ions (Fig. 11H–I), with Ca2+ and PO₄3− supporting bone mineralization, and Fe³⁺ facilitating osteogenesis and angiogenesis.

In summary, the GelMA-nHA/FePS₃ scaffold serves as a smart bifunctional platform combining antitumor therapy and bone repair, exhibiting favorable responsiveness, biosafety, and therapeutic efficacy. This work provides new design insights for tumor-associated bone repair systems and expands the biomedical applications of Fenton nanomedicine and phosphorus-based 2D materials.

Graphitic carbon nitride

g-C₃N₄, composed of earth-abundant carbon (C) and nitrogen (N), possesses a 2D layered structure analogous to graphene, where individual layers consist of triazine or heptazine units connected covalently and stacked via van der Waals interactions. This unique architecture endows g-C₃N₄ with excellent physicochemical properties, including visible-light-responsive catalytic activity, chemical and photostability, good biocompatibility and low toxicity, facile surface functionalization, and cost-effectiveness [213–219]. Consequently, g-C₃N₄ has attracted significant attention in biomedical applications, particularly for tumor PDT and intelligent drug delivery systems [220].

Wei et al. [221] designed and synthesized a rhenium(I)-coordinated g-C₃N₄-based photosensitizer (Re(I)-g-C₃N₄) (Fig. 12A), which selectively targets the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) and enables efficient photodynamic immunotherapy even under hypoxic conditions, with a notable capability to induce ferroptosis (Fig. 12B). Multiple assays demonstrated that upon laser irradiation, Re(I)-g-C₃N₄ generates abundant reactive ROS inside tumor cells, including •OH and •O₂⁻, maintaining high activity despite hypoxia (Fig. 12C). This leads to pronounced lipid peroxidation, evidenced by enhanced green fluorescence of the lipid peroxidation probe C11-BODIPY and confirmed by flow cytometry quantifying lipid peroxide accumulation.

Western blot analysis (Fig. 12E) showed significant downregulation of GPX4 in Re(I)-g-C₃N₄ treated cells, molecularly validating ferroptosis induction. Mechanistically, the study revealed a crosstalk between ferroptosis and pyroptosis: GPX4 inhibition by Re(I)-g-C₃N₄ promotes lipid peroxide buildup, triggering GSDMD-N cleavage and caspase-1 activation, thus initiating pyroptotic cell death (Fig. 12E). The use of inhibitors such as Ferrostatin-1 and Disulfiram notably rescued cell viability, further supporting this mechanism (Fig. 12D).

Importantly, Re(I)-g-C₃N₄ not only induces tumor cell death through ferroptosis and pyroptosis but also enhances ICD signals, including calreticulin (CRT) exposure, high-mobility group box 1 (HMGB1) release, heat shock protein 70 (HSP70) translocation, and ATP secretion, thereby activating dendritic cell maturation and T cell proliferation (Fig. 12F).

In summary, this study establishes a multimodal synergistic cell death platform that, for the first time, integrates ER-targeted PDT with ferroptosis induction and tumor immune activation. This approach overcomes the traditional oxygen dependency limitations of PDT and provides a promising new strategy for treating drug-resistant tumors, especially TNBC, as well as offering valuable guidance for the development of novel immuno-synergistic ferroptosis inducers.

Summary and outlook

Summary

In recent years, the application of 2D nanomaterials in ferroptosis-related research has grown rapidly. Compared to traditional small-molecule inducers and zero-dimensional/one-dimensional/three-dimensional nanoplatforms, two-dimensional materials exhibit a series of unique advantages in structure, surface chemistry, and biological behavior, enabling them to demonstrate higher efficiency and multifunctionality in ferroptosis regulation. Their primary advantages can be summarized as follows:

Structural superiority: enhanced catalytic activity and membrane interaction

2D nanomaterials exhibit a series of intrinsic advantages that distinguish them from traditional small-molecule ferroptosis inducers and conventional nanoplatforms. Their atomic-level thickness and ultrahigh specific surface area expose abundant metal centers, defect sites, and catalytic sites, thereby markedly enhancing Fenton/Fenton-like reactivity, promoting Fe²⁺/Fe³⁺ cycling, and driving robust lipid peroxidation. Meanwhile, the unique sheet-like architecture enables extensive interaction with cellular membranes, improving cellular uptake, inducing membrane perturbation that favors lipid peroxidation, and facilitating iron transport or release to provide a sustained iron pool.

Elemental and structural tunability for multipathway integration

Beyond structural benefits, 2D nanomaterials offer exceptional elemental tunability. Materials such as MXenes, TMDs, black phosphorus, and metal oxide nanosheets can integrate multiple ferroptosis-regulating pathways—including iron donation, ROS amplification, and GSH depletion—within a single platform, while also influencing ferroptosis-related gene expression. More importantly, benefiting from their tunable layered architectures, these 2D nanomaterials can be precisely engineered at the atomic scale through strategies such as phase engineering, defect engineering, heteroatom doping, and interlayer modulation [222]. These approaches not only optimize their electronic structures and catalytic activities but also enable the introduction of oxygen vacancies, adjustment of interlayer spacing, or induction of crystalline–amorphous transitions. Together, these features overcome the limitations of single-pathway small molecules and the complexity of multicomponent nanocarriers, offering broad opportunities for the rational design of high-performance biomedical functional materials [223].

Stimulus-responsive programmability and multimodal synergy

Moreover, the ease of constructing stimulus-responsive systems—responsive to light, heat, pH, magnetic fields, or H₂O₂—enables precise spatial and temporal control over ferroptosis induction. Such programmability significantly improves therapeutic selectivity compared with diffusible small molecules. Their rich surface chemistry also supports conjugation with targeting ligands and co-delivery of therapeutic payloads, facilitating multimodal antitumor strategies such as photothermal, photodynamic, and immunotherapy-enhanced ferroptosis [224]. The 2D geometry further provides high loading capacity and stable interlayer encapsulation.

Favorable biodegradability and optimizable biosafety profiles

Importantly, several 2D materials exhibit favorable biodegradability and safety profiles [225]. Black phosphorus, MXene and Mg-based nanosheets, for example, degrade into non-toxic ions, reducing long-term accumulation. Structural tuning—including thickness, layer number, and lateral dimension—can optimize tumor accumulation, accelerate metabolic clearance, and minimize off-target oxidative injury, offering advantages over persistent metallic nanoparticles.

Collectively, these features position 2D nanomaterials as uniquely powerful platforms for ferroptosis modulation [121]. Their synergistic combination of catalytic activity, membrane interaction, structural tunability, programmability, and biodegradability not only enhances the efficiency of ferroptosis induction but also enables the development of multifunctional, controllable, and clinically translatable ferroptosis-based therapeutic systems. As a result, 2D nanomaterials are emerging as a central focus in the advancement of ferroptosis research and cancer nanomedicine.

Challenges and future perspectives

Control of reaction kinetics and efficiency

Despite promising preclinical results, the clinical translation of 2D nanomaterial-induced ferroptosis faces several complex and interrelated challenges. A key issue is achieving precise spatiotemporal control within the intricate tumor microenvironment. The release kinetics of iron ions from 2D materials in vivo remain difficult to predict and are influenced by variable biological factors, often resulting in suboptimal or off-target catalytic activity. Moreover, the therapeutic potential of the Fenton reaction is limited by the tumor’s biochemical reality: low endogenous H₂O₂ levels and potent cellular antioxidant defenses. This gap between ideal in vitro conditions and the actual in vivo setting frequently leads to insufficient ROS generation to reliably initiate lipid peroxidation at therapeutic levels. Future strategies should therefore focus on engineering materials with “on-demand” or self-amplifying catalytic activity—for instance, through stimuli-responsive doping or the design of cascade nanozymatic systems that can generate and exploit H₂O₂ in situ.

Need for subcellular targeting precision

Another critical challenge lies in the limited ability to direct ferroptotic stimuli to specific subcellular compartments. Ferroptosis is spatially regulated, with distinct roles played by mitochondria, lysosomes, and the plasma membrane. However, most current 2D materials distribute randomly inside cells following endocytosis, failing to concentrate their catalytic or iron-delivery functions at these key sites. To fully exploit the pathway’s potential—and to minimize off-target organelle damage—future designs must evolve from general “cell-internalized” carriers to “organelle-addressable” systems. This could be achieved by conjugating organelle-specific targeting motifs, such as triphenylphosphonium for mitochondria or certain peptides for lysosomes.

Dual role in immune modulation

The immunomodulatory effects of ferroptosis add further complexity to its therapeutic application. On one hand, ferroptosis can stimulate antitumor immunity through the release of DAMPs. On the other, excessive lipid peroxidation and its byproducts may impair dendritic cell and T-cell function, potentially leading to immunosuppression. Therefore, simply maximizing ferroptosis may not yield optimal outcomes. Instead, 2D nanomaterial designs should incorporate features that promote productive immune activation—for example, by controlling DAMP release profiles or combining with immune checkpoint modulators. This would help shift ferroptosis from a predominantly cytotoxic event toward a coordinated immunotherapeutic strategy.

Toward predictive and mechanism-driven design

Addressing these challenges requires a shift from empirical optimization to predictive, mechanism-informed design. Integrating multi-omics approaches—such as single-cell transcriptomics to identify cell-type-specific ferroptosis vulnerabilities, spatial lipidomics to map peroxidation-sensitive membrane domains, and proteomics to study material–biological interfaces—can provide essential datasets. When combined with machine learning and computational modeling, these data can help build predictive frameworks that link material properties (e.g., defect density, surface charge, composition) to biological outcomes (e.g., iron release rate, GPX4 inhibition, immunogenicity). Such models would enable the in silico screening and rational design of next-generation 2D nanomaterials tailored for precise ferroptosis induction in specific cancers.

In conclusion, the successful clinical translation of 2D nanomaterials for ferroptosis therapy will require moving beyond proof-of-concept cytotoxicity studies. A coordinated, interdisciplinary effort is needed to develop materials that overcome limitations in reaction control and subcellular delivery, intelligently modulate immune responses, and are guided by predictive computational tools. By tackling these fundamental mechanistic and translational hurdles, 2D nanomaterials can evolve from powerful laboratory tools into reliable and precise clinical agents for cancer treatment.
