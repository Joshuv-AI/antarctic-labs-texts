Long-time behavior of resonant time-dependent perturbations of periodic transport equations on ℝ {\mathbb{R}}
Maria Teresa Rotolo

Abstract
We consider linear, time-dependent and skew-adjoint perturbations of periodic transport equations on the one-dimensional torus. We describe the long-time behavior of solutions for all non-degenerate perturbations in resonant regime, proving that either there exist solutions whose Sobolev norms explode exponentially fast, provoking energy transfer phenomena, or all solutions remain stable for arbitrarily long time scales. The proof combines pseudodifferential tools with dynamical systems results: we perform a resonant normal form procedure to reduce our analysis to the classical dynamics for the resonant equation. The main difficulty lies in the proof of the instability result, for which we explicitly construct an escape function associated to the dynamics. This is obtained by means of a positive commutator estimate on the operator associated with the escape function, exploiting microlocal analysis.



1
Introduction and main result

In this article we study long-time behavior of solutions for a class of linear and time-dependent transport equations on the one-dimensional torus. Two phenomena that typically occur both in linear and nonlinear PDEs are long-time stability of solutions or existence of unstable orbits, and a valid tool to depict such behaviors is the study of the Sobolev norms (
1.3
) of solutions. On the one hand, an unbounded growth in time of the positive Sobolev norms of some solutions is an indicator of instability, in the form of energy transfer phenomena; on the other, stable equations are characterized by uniform-in-time bounds for all Sobolev norms of all solutions, on long time scales.
The equations that we consider are small in size,
2\pi
-periodic in time perturbations of constant coefficient transport equations. Precisely,

\partial_{t}u=(m+{\varepsilon}V(t,x))\partial_{x}u+\frac{{\varepsilon}}{2}\partial_{x}V(t,x)u,\hskip 10.00002ptx\in{\mathbb{T}}:={\mathbb{R}}/2\pi{\mathbb{Z}},\ t\in{\mathbb{R}},

(1.1)

where
m\in{\mathbb{N}}^{+}
,
{\varepsilon}
is a positive parameter,
V\in C^{\infty}({\mathbb{T}}^{2};{\mathbb{R}})
. Observe immediately that the right-hand side of (
1.1
) is skew-adjoint over
L^{2}({\mathbb{T}})
, thus one has, for every solution of (
1.1
),

\left\|u(t)\right\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{T}})}=\left\|u(0)\right\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{T}})},\hskip 10.00002pt\forall t\in{\mathbb{R}}.

(1.2)

Moreover, defining the scale of Sobolev spaces
{\mathcal{H}}^{s}
,
s\in{\mathbb{R}}
on the torus as

{\mathcal{H}}^{s}({\mathbb{T}},{\mathbb{C}})={\mathcal{H}}^{s}:=\{u\in L^{2}({\mathbb{T}}):\left\|u\right\|_{s}^{2}:=\sum_{k\in{\mathbb{Z}}}\left\langle k\right\rangle^{2s}|u_{k}|^{2}<+\infty\},

(1.3)

(where we use the notation
\left\langle k\right\rangle:=(1+|k|^{2})^{\frac{1}{2}}
for the Japanese brackets and we denote by

the Fourier coefficients of the function

{\varepsilon}=0

\left\|u(t)\right\|_{s}=\left\|u(0)\right\|_{s},\hskip 10.00002pt\forall s\in{\mathbb{R}},t\in{\mathbb{R}}.

(1.4)

This property is anyway not stable under perturbation: as soon as

In the recent work
[
40

, the authors consider equation (
1.1
) on
{\mathbb{T}}^{n}

and prove that there exists an open set of time-dependent perturbations

that provoke unstable solutions (where, by unstable solutions, we intend solutions exhibiting unbounded growth in time of their positive Sobolev norms). Moreover, this set also turns out to be dense when

Roughly speaking we prove that, for such perturbations, only two possibilities occur: either all Sobolev norms of all solutions are uniformly bounded for long time scales, or instability phenomena arise.

1.1
Outline of the Proof

In this section we quickly describe the main tools needed to prove Theorem
1.5
.

Even though the strategies to prove the long-time stability and the instability result are quite different, the starting point is the same: for every smooth perturbation
V\in C^{\infty}({\mathbb{T}}^{2};{\mathbb{R}})
we are able to conjugate the initial equation (
1.1
) to a new transport equation, having as leading coefficient of the transport operator the resonant average
\left\langle V\right\rangle_{m}
of
V
(see Definition
1.1
). We refer to Lemma
3.2
for a precise statement of this result. An analogous procedure has already been used in
[
40
]
, but we remark here that, thanks to the one dimensional setting of the present work, we are able to treat both stability and instability starting with this initial conjugation, whilst in
[
40
]
the procedure is tailored to the instability result.

We now briefly describe the strategy to prove long-time stability: the core idea of our proof is that, when considering
V\in{\mathcal{V}}_{m,s}
(see (
1.6
)), the above-mentioned conjugation procedure results in a transport equation with never vanishing transport term, up to arbitrarily small-in-size remainders (precisely, it is possible to obtain remainders of order
{\varepsilon}^{N}
for every
N\in{\mathbb{N}}^{+}
). This enables us to reduce the equation to a constant-coefficient transport equation, again up to arbitrarily small-in-size non-constant remainders. Roughly speaking, the constant coefficient is obtained integrating the inverse of the resonant average (see Proposition
3.1
for the precise procedure). For this reason, we crucially need to have
\left\langle V\right\rangle_{m}\in{\mathcal{V}}_{m,s}
. Finally, we remark that, due to the presence of the remainder term, we can not hope to obtain global in time stability. Anyway, the time scale for which stability holds can be chosen arbitrarily long, up to reducing the size of the perturbation accordingly.

We now turn to the description of the proof of the instability result. In this case, we use a different approach: we use the information on the dynamics of the ODE
\dot{x}=\left\langle V\right\rangle_{m}(x)
to analyze the associated dynamics of the Hamiltonian vector field
{\mathcal{X}}_{h}
on
T^{*}{\mathbb{T}}\simeq{\mathbb{T}}\times{\mathbb{R}}
, with Hamiltonian
h(x,\xi):=\xi\left\langle V\right\rangle_{m}(x)
. As one can immediately see from the Hamilton equations (see (
4.2
)), the two dynamics are strictly related and, in particular, the key point of our proof is the construction of an
escape function
for the Hamiltonian dynamics. Roughly speaking, it is a function that increases along the flow of
{\mathcal{X}}_{h}
. Next we use pseudodifferential calculus and microlocal tools such as Gårding inequality to recover the exponential growth in (
1.10
) via a positive commutator estimate (see Section
4.2
).

Anyway, we anticipate here that the construction of the escape function can be far from trivial, and strongly depends on the topological setting of the Hamiltonian system, and thus in turn on the spacial domain of the initial PDE. In the present work we adapt to our context the constructions of
[
15
]
and
[
31
]
, and we show that, in this one dimensional periodic case, it is sufficient to consider smooth vector fields
having only (but at least one) hyperbolic critical points. We briefly mention that, in higher dimensional contexts, it is no more sufficient to ask only for the existence of critical elements of the vector fields, but a central role is played by the hyperbolicity assumptions, that are in such cases much more involved: we only cite here
[
19
]
, where the microlocal point of view to treat similar types of problems is introduced for Anosov vector fields and the recent book
[
33
]
and the references therein, for an introduction to the geometrical tools needed in higher dimension.

, for tori of any dimension. We report it here since we slightly modify the discussion to adapt it also to the proof of the stability result (see Section
3
).

First of all we remark that a linear, invertible transformation
u=A(t)u_{1}
, with
A(t)
periodic in time of period
2\pi
, transforms equation
\dot{u}=Hu
into
\dot{u}_{1}=A(t)_{*}Hu_{1}
, where the pushforward
A(t)_{*}H
is given by

A(t)_{*}H:=A(t)^{-1}[HA(t)-\partial_{t}A(t)].

(2.8)

We remark, to avoid any confusion in the notation, that
\partial_{t}A(t)
is the derivative in time of the linear transformation (and not the composition
\partial_{t}\circ A(t)
). Next, we consider a family of time-dependent diffeomorphisms of the torus of the form

\varphi_{k}(t):{\mathbb{T}}\to{\mathbb{T}},\hskip 10.00002ptx\mapsto x+{\varepsilon}^{k}\beta(t,x),\hskip 10.00002ptk\in{\mathbb{N}}^{+},\ {\varepsilon}>0,\ \beta\in C^{\infty}({\mathbb{T}}^{2};{\mathbb{R}}).

(2.9)

It has been proved (see for example
[
1
, Lemma B.4]
) that for
{\varepsilon}>0
small enough
\varphi_{k}(t)
is an invertible diffeomorphism and its inverse has the form

\varphi^{-1}_{k}(t):{\mathbb{T}}\to{\mathbb{T}},\hskip 10.00002pty\mapsto y+{\varepsilon}^{k}\tilde{\beta}(t,y),\hskip 10.00002ptk\in{\mathbb{N}}^{+},{\varepsilon}>0,

(2.10)

for some
\tilde{\beta}\in C^{\infty}({\mathbb{T}}^{2};{\mathbb{R}})
. We define the transformation associated with (
2.9
) as:

\tilde{\Phi}_{k}(t):u(x)\mapsto(1+{\varepsilon}^{k}\partial_{x}\beta)^{\frac{1}{2}}(u\circ\varphi_{k})(x),\hskip 10.00002pt\forall t\in{\mathbb{T}},k\in{\mathbb{N}}^{+},

(2.11)

with inverse given by

\hskip 10.00002pt\tilde{\Phi}_{k}(t)^{-1}:u(y)\mapsto(1+{\varepsilon}^{k}\partial_{x}\tilde{\beta})^{\frac{1}{2}}(u\circ\varphi_{k}^{-1})(y),\hskip 10.00002pt\forall t\in{\mathbb{T}},k\in{\mathbb{N}}^{+}.

(2.12)

We stress here that
\tilde{\Phi}_{k}(t)\equiv\tilde{\Phi}_{k,{\varepsilon},\beta}(t)
, i.e., the transformation depends both on
\beta
and
{\varepsilon}
, but we will always write as in (
2.11
), omitting such labels to simplify the notation.

Remark that, for every
k\in{\mathbb{N}}^{+}
, both
\tilde{\Phi}_{k}(t)
and
\tilde{\Phi}_{k}(t)^{-1}
are
2\pi
-periodic in time. We now show that, for every
k\in{\mathbb{N}}^{+}
, the operator
\tilde{\Phi}_{k}(t)
in (
2.11
) is unitary: substituting the explicit expressions of (
2.9
) and (
2.10
) in the identity

\varphi_{k}|_{x=y+{\varepsilon}^{k}\tilde{\beta}(t,y)}\circ\varphi_{k}^{-1}(y)=y\hskip 20.00003pt\forall t\in{\mathbb{R}},\ y\in{\mathbb{T}},\ k\in{\mathbb{N}}^{+},

(2.13)

and deriving (
2.13
) with respect to

one obtains that, for every
{\varepsilon}>0
and
\beta\in C^{\infty}({\mathbb{T}}^{2};{\mathbb{R}})
,

1+{\varepsilon}^{k}\partial_{y}\tilde{\beta}(t,y)=\frac{1}{1+{\varepsilon}^{k}\partial_{x}\beta(t,x)|_{x=y+{\varepsilon}^{k}\tilde{\beta}(t,y)}},\hskip 10.00002pt\forall y\in{\mathbb{T}},\ k\in{\mathbb{N}}^{+}.

(2.14)

This in turn gives, via a direct computation, unitarity of
\tilde{\Phi}_{k}(t)
, namely

\tilde{\Phi}_{k}(t)^{*}=\tilde{\Phi}_{k}(t)^{-1},\hskip 10.00002pt\forall t\in{\mathbb{T}},k\in{\mathbb{N}}^{+},

(2.15)

where
\tilde{\Phi}_{k}(t)^{*}
denotes the adjoint operator of
\tilde{\Phi}_{k}(t)
. We now turn to studying the action of
\tilde{\Phi}_{k}(t)
on transport operators.

Lemma 2.4
.

Take any
f
,
g\in C^{\infty}({\mathbb{T}}^{2};{\mathbb{R}})
,
n\in{\mathbb{N}}^{+}
and
{\varepsilon}>0
. Define

H_{n}(t):=\operatorname{Op^{w}}(i\xi(f(t,x)+{\varepsilon}^{n}g(t,x))).

(2.16)

For any
\beta\in C^{\infty}({\mathbb{T}}^{2};{\mathbb{R}})
there exists
{\varepsilon}_{\beta}>0
such that, for every
0<{\varepsilon}<{\varepsilon}_{\beta}
, the associated transformation
\tilde{\Phi}_{n}(t)
defined as in (
2.11
) satisfies

\tilde{\Phi}_{n}(t)^{-1}H_{n}(t)\tilde{\Phi}_{n}(t)=\operatorname{Op^{w}}(i\xi(f(t,x)+{\varepsilon}^{n}(g(t,x)+f(t,x)\partial_{x}\beta(t,x))+{\varepsilon}^{n+1}{\mathcal{R}}^{1}_{n,{\varepsilon}}(t,x)))

(2.17)

and

\tilde{\Phi}_{n}(t)^{-1}\partial_{t}\tilde{\Phi}_{n}(t)=\operatorname{Op^{w}}(i\xi({\varepsilon}^{n}\partial_{t}\beta(t,x)+{\varepsilon}^{n+1}{\mathcal{R}}^{2}_{n,{\varepsilon}}(t,x))),

(2.18)

where
{\mathcal{R}}^{1}_{n,{\varepsilon}}
,
{\mathcal{R}}^{2}_{n,{\varepsilon}}\in C^{\infty}({\mathbb{T}}^{2};{\mathbb{R}})
have all seminorms uniformly bounded in terms of
{\varepsilon}\in[0,{\varepsilon}_{\beta}]
.

Proof.

Given
\beta\in C^{\infty}({\mathbb{T}}^{2};{\mathbb{R}})
, let
\tilde{\Phi}_{n}(t)
be the associated transformation in (
2.11
), with
k\leadsto n
and recall the expression for its inverse in (
2.12
). Fix
{\varepsilon}_{\beta}>0
such that, for every
0<{\varepsilon}<{\varepsilon}_{\beta}
,
\tilde{\Phi}_{n}(t)
is a bounded and invertible transformation (recall previous discussion in (
2.9
) and (
2.10
) and see
[
1
, Lemma B.4]
). We first prove (
2.17
). To this aim, first remark that, since
H_{n}(t)
in (
2.16
) is skew-adjoint (see Lemma
2.1
), using (
2.15
) we have

[\tilde{\Phi}_{n}(t)^{-1}H_{n}(t)\tilde{\Phi}_{n}(t)]^{*}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{unitary}}}{{=}}-\tilde{\Phi}_{n}(t)^{-1}H_{n}(t)\tilde{\Phi}_{n}(t).

Next, using Lemma
2.1
, we can rewrite
H_{n}(t)
in (
2.16
) as

H_{n}(t)=(f(t,x)+{\varepsilon}^{n}g(t,x))\partial_{x}+\frac{1}{2}\partial_{x}(f(t,x)+{\varepsilon}^{n}g(t,x)).

(2.19)

Thus, using (
2.19
), (
2.11
), (
2.12
), (
2.14
) and performing a direct computation one obtains

\begin{split}\tilde{\Phi}_{n}(t)^{-1}H_{n}(t)\tilde{\Phi}_{n}(t)&=(f+{\varepsilon}^{n}g)(t,\varphi_{n}^{-1}(t)[x])(1+{\varepsilon}^{n}\partial_{x}\beta(t,\varphi^{-1}_{n}(t)[x]))\partial_{x}\\
&+\frac{1}{2}\partial_{x}\left((f+{\varepsilon}^{n}g)(t,\varphi_{n}^{-1}(t)[x])(1+{\varepsilon}^{n}\partial_{x}\beta(t,\varphi^{-1}_{n}(t)[x]))\right)\\
&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{comp.symbols}}}{{=}}\operatorname{Op^{w}}(i\xi((f+{\varepsilon}^{n}g)(t,\varphi_{n}^{-1}(t)[x])(1+{\varepsilon}^{n}\partial_{x}\beta(t,\varphi^{-1}_{n}(t)[x]))).\end{split}

(2.20)

Analogously, the expression for
\tilde{\Phi}_{n}(t)^{-1}\partial_{t}\tilde{\Phi}_{n}(t)
can be explicitly computed and is given by

\begin{split}\tilde{\Phi}_{n}(t)^{-1}\partial_{t}\tilde{\Phi}_{n}(t)&={\varepsilon}^{n}\partial_{t}\beta(t,\varphi^{-1}_{n}(t)[x])\partial_{x}+\frac{{\varepsilon}^{n}}{2}\partial_{x}\left(\partial_{t}\beta(t,\varphi^{-1}_{n}(t)[x])\right)\\
&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{comp.symbols}}}{{=}}\operatorname{Op^{w}}(i\xi\partial_{t}\beta(t,\varphi^{-1}_{n}(t)[x])).\end{split}

(2.21)

Finally, performing a Taylor expansion of both (
2.20
) and (
2.21

we obtain respectively (
2.17

2.18
) concluding the proof.
∎

We conclude this preliminary section with the following Lemma, which contains a computation that we will use repeatedly along the work.

Lemma 2.5
.

Let
m\in{\mathbb{N}}^{+}
and
V\in C^{\infty}({\mathbb{T}}^{2};{\mathbb{R}})
. There exists a smooth function
\beta_{m,V}\in C^{\infty}({\mathbb{T}}^{2};{\mathbb{R}})
such that

V(t,x)+m\partial_{x}\beta_{m,V}(t,x)-\partial_{t}\beta_{m,V}(t,x)=\left\langle V\right\rangle_{m}(x+mt)

(2.22)

(recall the definition (
1.5
) of resonant average of
V
with respect to
m
).

Proof.

Denote by
T(t,x):=V+m\partial_{x}\beta_{m,V}-\partial_{t}\beta_{m,V}
, with
\beta_{m,V}\in C^{\infty}({\mathbb{T}}^{2};{\mathbb{R}})
to be determined and write the Fourier expansion of
T
:

T(t,x)=\sum_{k,\ell\in{\mathbb{Z}}}(v_{k,\ell}+i(km-\ell)\beta_{{m,V}_{k,\ell}})e^{ikx+i\ell t},

(2.23)

where
V(t,x)=\sum_{k,\ell}v_{k,\ell}e^{i(kx+\ell t)}
and
\beta_{m,V}(t,x)=\sum_{k,\ell\in{\mathbb{Z}}}\beta_{{m,V}_{k,\ell}}e^{i(kx+\ell t)}
. Define

\beta_{{m,V}_{k,\ell}}:=\begin{cases}\frac{v_{k,\ell}}{i(\ell-km)}&\mbox{ if }\ell\neq km,\\
0&\mbox{ otherwise.}\end{cases}

(2.24)

The corresponding function
\beta_{m,V}(t,x):=\sum\beta_{{m,V}_{k,\ell}}e^{i(kx+\ell t)}
is a well defined smooth function over
{\mathbb{T}}^{2}
. This can be directly checked using that
V
is smooth and that, since
m\in{\mathbb{N}}^{+}
, the sequence
\{\ell-km\}_{\ell\neq km}
in (
2.24
) never accumulates to zero. Substituting the Fourier coefficients of
\beta_{m,V}
(see (
2.24
)), in the expression of
T(t,x)
in (
2.23
), one obtains (
2.22
), concluding the proof.
∎

(3.17)

) and (

).
We can now conclude as in the previous step: applying Lemma

\beta_{m,V}^{n}(t,x)\in C^{\infty}({\mathbb{T}}^{2};{\mathbb{R}})
such that

\hat{W}_{n}(t,x)+m\partial_{x}\beta_{m,V}^{n}(t,x)-\partial_{t}\beta_{m,V}^{n}(t,x)=\left\langle\hat{W}_{n}\right\rangle_{m}(x+mt),

(recall (

solves (

,
\tilde{Z}_{n+1}:=\tilde{Z}_{n}+{\varepsilon}^{n+1}\left\langle\hat{W}_{n}\right\rangle_{m}(x+mt)

∎

Proof of Proposition

.

Let now

[\Lambda u](t,x):=(1+\lambda^{\prime}(x))^{\frac{1}{2}}u(t,x+\lambda(x)),\hskip 10.00002pt\forall u\in C^{\infty}({\mathbb{T}}^{2})

and thus formally writing
\Lambda^{-1}

Denote by

\omega(t,x):={\varepsilon}\left\langle V\right\rangle_{m}(x)+{\varepsilon}^{2}Z_{N}(x)+{\varepsilon}^{N+1}\tilde{W}_{N}(t,x),

4
Instability

In this section we prove the second point of Theorem
1.5
. As anticipated, the core of the proof is the construction of an escape function for
h(x,\xi)=\xi\left\langle V\right\rangle_{m}(x)
, with
V\in{\mathcal{V}}_{m,u}
(see (
1.7
)). This is done in Section
4.1
, adapting the construction of
[
15
]
(see also
[
31
]
). Next, in section
4.2
we prove existence of unstable solutions performing first a normal form reduction as the one in Lemma
3.2
and then a positive commutator estimate in which we exploit the properties of the escape function.

4.1
Construction of the escape function

In this section we consider a smooth function

h(x,\xi):=\xi X(x),\hskip 10.00002pt(x,\xi)\in T^{*}{\mathbb{T}}\simeq{\mathbb{T}}\times{\mathbb{R}},\ X\in C^{\infty}({\mathbb{T}}),

(4.1)

we assume that that
X
is non degenerate and has at least one zero (see Definition
1.3
) and we construct an
escape function
for
h
. We denote by
{\mathcal{X}}_{h}
the Hamiltonian vector field of
h
on
T^{*}{\mathbb{T}}
, that in coordinates reads

\mathcal{X}_{h}(x,\xi)=(X(x),-\xi\partial_{x}X(x)),\hskip 10.00002pt\forall(x,\xi)\in T^{*}{\mathbb{T}},

(4.2)

and we denote by
\Phi^{t}_{{\mathcal{X}}_{h}}(x,\xi)
the flow of
{\mathcal{X}}_{h}
on
T^{*}{\mathbb{T}}
.
Finally, for every
f\in C^{\infty}(T^{*}{\mathbb{T}})
we write the Poisson brackets between
h
and
f
as

\{h,f\}:=\partial_{\xi}h\partial_{x}f-\partial_{x}h\partial_{\xi}f.

(4.3)

We now give the definition of escape function. For all this section we will use the notation
T^{*}{\mathbb{T}}\setminus\{0\}=\{(x,\xi)\in T^{*}{\mathbb{T}}:\xi\neq 0\}
.

Definition 4.1
.

We say that a smooth function
f:T^{*}{\mathbb{T}}\setminus\{0\}\to{\mathbb{R}}
is positively homogeneous of degree
\rho
if

f(x,\lambda\xi)=\lambda^{\rho}f(x,\xi),\hskip 10.00002pt\forall(x,\xi)\neq 0,\ \forall\ \lambda>0.

Definition 4.2

(Escape function)
.

Let
h:T^{*}{\mathbb{T}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
be as in (
4.1
). We say that a function
a\in C^{\infty}(T^{*}{\mathbb{T}}\setminus\{0\})
, positively homogeneous of degree one (see Definition
4.1
)
is an
escape function
for
h
if there exists some positive
\delta
such that

\{h,a\}\geq\delta|\xi|,\hskip 10.00002pt\forall(x,\xi)\in T^{*}{\mathbb{T}}\setminus\{0\}.

(4.4)

The main results of this section is the following proposition.

Proposition 4.3
.

Let
X\in C^{\infty}({\mathbb{T}})
and suppose that
X
has at least one zero and that all the zeroes are non degenerate (see Definition
1.3
). Then the function
h
defined in (
4.1
) admits an escape function in the sense of Definition
4.2
. Moreover, there exists an open subset
{\mathcal{W}}\subset{\mathbb{T}}
such that

a(x,\xi)\leq-\frac{|\xi|}{2},\hskip 10.00002pt\forall x\in{\mathcal{W}}.

(4.5)

Dynamical properties.

In order to prove Proposition
4.3
we need some dynamical properties of the Hamiltonian flow induced by
h(x,\xi)
in (
4.1
), that we list in this section. First of all, we recall (see, for example,
[
32
, Chapter 9]
) that a smooth vector field is
complete
if it generates a global flow, or equivalently if, for every initial datum, the associated flow exists for all in times. We have the following result, for whose proof we refer to
[
42
, Section 5.2]
.

Lemma 4.4
.

Let
h
as in (
4.1
). Then the Hamiltonian vector field
{\mathcal{X}}_{h}
(see (
4.2
)) is complete.

Remark 4.5
.

Denoting by
\Phi^{t}_{{\mathcal{X}}_{h}}(x,\xi)=(X^{t}(x,\xi),\Xi^{t}(x,\xi))
the components of the flow of
{\mathcal{X}}_{h}
in (
4.2
) with initial datum
(x,\xi)\in T^{*}{\mathbb{T}}
, the following homogeneity property holds:

(X^{t}(x,\lambda\xi),\Xi^{t}(x,\lambda\xi))=(X^{t}(x,\xi),\lambda\Xi^{t}(x,\xi)),\hskip 10.00002pt\forall\lambda\in{\mathbb{R}},\ \forall(x,\xi)\in T^{*}{\mathbb{T}},\ \forall t\geq 0.

(4.6)

We now identify global attractors and repellors for the flow. In particular, we show that the dynamics of
{\mathcal{X}}_{h}
on
T^{*}{\mathbb{T}}
is completely determined once the zeroes of
X
are known. To this aim let us first introduce the following terminology:

Definition 4.6

(Attractor-Repellor structure)
.

We say that the flow
\Phi^{t}_{{\mathcal{X}}_{h}}
on
T^{*}{\mathbb{T}}
has a
global attractor-repellor
structure if there exist
A
,
R
closed invariant subsets of
T^{*}{\mathbb{T}}
such that:

\begin{split}&\mbox{ for every }(x,\xi)\in T^{*}{\mathbb{T}}\setminus R,\quad\Phi^{t}_{{\mathcal{X}}_{h}}(x,\xi)\to A\quad\mbox{as}\quad t\to+\infty,\\
&\mbox{ for every }(x,\xi)\in T^{*}{\mathbb{T}}\setminus A\quad\Phi^{t}_{{\mathcal{X}}_{h}}(x,\xi)\to R\quad\mbox{as}\quad t\to-\infty.\\
\end{split}

(4.7)

We denote the basins of attraction of
A
and
R
respectively as
B(A):=T^{*}{\mathbb{T}}\setminus(R)
and
B(R):=T^{*}{\mathbb{T}}\setminus(A)
.

Lemma 4.7
.

Let
h(x,\xi)=\xi X(x)
and suppose that
X
has at least one zero, but does not have any degenerate zero (see Definition
1.3
). Denote by

K^{+}:=\{x\in{\mathbb{T}}:X(x)=0,\ X^{\prime}(x)<0\}\hskip 10.00002pt\mbox{ and}\hskip 10.00002ptK^{-}:=\{x\in{\mathbb{T}}:X(x)=0,\ X^{\prime}(x)>0\},

(4.8)

and by

\Gamma^{+}:=\{(x,\xi):x\in K^{+}\}\simeq K^{+}\times{\mathbb{R}}\hskip 10.00002pt\mbox{ and }\hskip 10.00002pt\Gamma^{-}:=\{(x,\xi):x\in K^{-}\}\simeq K^{-}\times{\mathbb{R}}.

(4.9)

Then the sets
\Gamma^{+}
,
\Gamma^{-}
and
{\mathcal{T}}:=\{\xi=0\}
are invariant for the flow of
{\mathcal{X}}_{h}
in (
4.2
). Moreover the flow
\Phi^{t}_{{\mathcal{X}}_{h}}
has a global attractor-repellor structure (see Definition
4.6
) with attractor
\Gamma^{+}
and repellor
\Gamma^{-}
. In particular, for all
(x_{0},\xi_{0})\in T^{*}{\mathbb{T}}\setminus(\Gamma^{+}\cup\Gamma^{-})
we have

\Phi^{t}_{{\mathcal{X}}_{h}}(x_{0},\xi_{0})\to\Gamma^{+},\ \mbox{ as }t\to+\infty,\hskip 10.00002pt\mbox{ and }\hskip 10.00002pt\Phi^{t}_{{\mathcal{X}}_{h}}(x_{0},\xi_{0})\to\Gamma^{-},\ \mbox{ as }t\to-\infty.

(4.10)

Proof.

First of all remark that, since
X
has no degenerate zeroes, then

X^{-1}(0)=K^{+}\cup K^{-}.

(4.11)

Moreover, using the explicit expression of
{\mathcal{X}}_{h}
in (
4.2
), one can check that
{\mathcal{T}}
,
\Gamma^{+}
and
\Gamma^{-}
are the only invariant sets for the flow. We now prove (
4.10
): let
(x_{0},\xi_{0})\in T^{*}{\mathbb{T}}\setminus(\Gamma^{+}\cup\Gamma^{-})
and recall that, from Lemma
4.4
, the flow
\Phi^{t}_{{\mathcal{X}}_{h}}(x_{0},\xi_{0})
exists for all
t\in{\mathbb{R}}
. Thus, in order to prove (
4.10
) we have to show that
x(t)\to K^{+}
as
t\to+\infty
and
x(t)\to K^{-}
as
t\to-\infty
(see (
4.8
) and (
4.9
)). This follows immediately from the fact that the dynamics of
x(t)
is determined by the one dimensional system
\dot{x}=X(x)
(see (
4.2
)) and from the definitions of
K^{+}
and
K^{-}
in (
4.8
), thus we omit the details of this last part of the proof.
∎

Remark 4.8
.

From the structure of the flow described in Lemma
4.7
one can check that any conical neighborhood
{\mathcal{U}}^{+}
of
\Gamma^{+}
is positively invariant for the flow (i.e.,
\Phi^{t}_{{\mathcal{X}}_{h}}({\mathcal{U}}^{+})\subset{\mathcal{U}}^{+}
, for all
t>0
) and analogously any conical neighborhood of
\Gamma^{-}
is invariant for the flow at negative times.

4.1.1
Construction of the escape function

In this section we use the structure of the flow of
{\mathcal{X}}_{h}
(see (
4.2
)) described in Lemma
4.7
and the sets defined therein ((
4.8
),(
4.9
)) to construct an escape function for
h(x,\xi)
in the sense of Definition
4.2
. We first construct a local escape function close to the zeroes of
X
and then we progressively extend it to the whole space.

Lemma 4.9
.

Let
h(x,\xi)=\xi X(x)
and suppose that
X\in C^{\infty}({\mathbb{T}})
has at least one zero and is non degenerate (see Definition
1.3
). There exist conical neighborhoods
{\mathcal{U}}^{\pm}
of
\Gamma^{\pm}
(see (
4.9
)),
\delta>0
and a smooth function

such that

\hskip 10.00002ptk(x,\xi)=\pm|\xi|\ \mbox{ on }\Gamma^{\pm},\hskip 10.00002pt\mbox{ and }\hskip 10.00002pt\{h,k\}\geq\delta|\xi|,\hskip 10.00002pt\forall(x,\xi)\in{\mathcal{U}}^{+}\cup{\mathcal{U}}^{-}.

(4.12)

Moreover the function
k
is positively homogeneous of degree one (see Definition
4.1
) on its domain.

Proof.

Denote by
\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\}
the zeroes of
X(x)
and, using non-degeneracy of
X
, let
\nu:=\min_{i}|X^{\prime}(x_{i})|>0
. Let
\tilde{{\mathcal{U}}}^{\pm}\subseteq{\mathbb{T}}
be neighborhoods of
K^{\pm}
in
{\mathbb{T}}
(see (
4.8
)) to be fixed later, and let
\tilde{k}\in C^{\infty}({\mathbb{T}})
be such that

\tilde{k}(x)=\begin{cases}1&\mbox{ if }x\in\tilde{{\mathcal{U}}}^{+}\\
-1&\mbox{ if }x\in\tilde{{\mathcal{U}}}^{-}.\end{cases}

(4.13)

Set
k(x,\xi):=|\xi|\tilde{k}(x)
. Clearly
k
is positively homogeneous of degree one and
k=\pm|\xi|
on
\Gamma^{\pm}
. Moreover, computing
\{h,k\}(x_{i})
for any

we also have

\{h,k\}(x_{i},\xi)=-|\xi|X^{\prime}(x_{i})\tilde{k}(x_{i})=|\xi||X^{\prime}(x_{i})|\geq|\xi|\nu,\hskip 10.00002pt\forall i=1,\ldots,n,

where for the last equality we have used (

) and the definition of
K^{\pm}
in (
4.8
).
Thus, up to choosing
\tilde{{\mathcal{U}}}^{\pm}
small enough, we have by continuity

\{h,k\}\geq\frac{\nu}{2}|\xi|\mbox{ on }{\mathcal{U}}^{+}\cup{\mathcal{U}}^{-}

(where
{\mathcal{U}}^{\pm}:=\tilde{{\mathcal{U}}}^{\pm}\times{\mathbb{R}}
), concluding the proof of (
4.12

.
∎

The next goal is to extend the local escape function

There exists a smooth function
{\mathtt{m}}:T^{*}{\mathbb{T}}\setminus\{0\}\to{\mathbb{R}}

{\mathtt{m}}(x,\xi)=\{h,k\}(x,\xi),\ \forall(x,\xi)\in{\mathcal{U}}^{\pm},\hskip 10.00002pt\mbox{ and }\hskip 10.00002pt{\mathtt{m}}(x,\xi)\geq\frac{\delta}{2}|\xi|,\hskip 10.00002pt\forall\ (x,\xi)\in T^{*}{\mathbb{T}},

(4.14)

where

,
\delta
and
{\mathcal{U}}^{\pm}
are as in Lemma
4.9

Proof.

Let
{\mathcal{U}}^{\pm}
be the conical neighborhoods of
\Gamma^{\pm}
given by Lemma
4.9
. Denote by
\tilde{{\mathcal{U}}}^{\pm}\subset{\mathbb{T}}
the neighborhoods of
K^{\pm}
obtained projecting

, i.e.

\tilde{{\mathcal{U}}}^{\pm}:=\{x\in{\mathbb{T}}:\ (x,\xi)\in{\mathcal{U}}^{\pm},\ \forall\xi\in{\mathbb{R}}\}.

Then it is possible to define
\tilde{{\mathtt{m}}}:{\mathbb{T}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
, a smooth function satisfying

\tilde{{\mathtt{m}}}(x)=\{h,k\}(x,1)\hskip 10.00002pt\forall\ x\in(\overline{{\mathcal{U}}^{+}}\cup\overline{{\mathcal{U}}^{-}})\hskip 10.00002pt\mbox{ and }\tilde{{\mathtt{m}}}\geq\frac{\delta}{2}\hskip 10.00002pt\forall\ x\in{\mathbb{T}}.

(4.15)

This can be done since, from (
4.12

in a smooth way outside
\overline{\tilde{{\mathcal{U}}}^{\pm}}

4.15

Finally, we define
{\mathtt{m}}:T^{*}{\mathbb{T}}\setminus\{0\}\to{\mathbb{R}}

∎

We can now extend the local escape functions to the sets

(see Definition
4.6
). In the next Lemma we write

Lemma 4.11
.

Let
z=(x,\xi)\in B(\Gamma^{\pm})
and define

\ell^{\pm}(z):=\lim_{t\to\pm\infty}\Big[k(\Phi_{{\mathcal{X}}_{h}}^{t}(z))-\int_{0}^{t}{\mathtt{m}}(\Phi_{{\mathcal{X}}_{h}}^{s}(z)){\rm d}s\Big],

where

Then

(i)

are well defined on

, smooth, positively homogeneous of degree one and
\ell^{\pm}=k
on
\Gamma^{\pm}
;

(ii)

\ell^{\pm}
are escape functions on
B(\Gamma^{\pm})
. In particular, let

be given by Lemma
4.9
, then

\{h,\ell^{\pm}\}\geq\frac{\delta}{2}|\xi|,\hskip 10.00002pt\forall z\in B(\Gamma^{\pm}).

(4.17)

Proof.

We prove the statement with +.

Let
z\in B(\Gamma^{+})
and let
t_{0}(z)\geq 0
be such that
\Phi_{{\mathcal{X}}_{h}}^{t_{0}(z)}(z)\in{\mathcal{U}}^{+}
, with
{\mathcal{U}}^{+}
the neighborhood of
\Gamma^{+}
defined in Lemma
4.9

of

, such that
\left\|u_{1_{0}}\right\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{T}})}=1
and

\left\langle\operatorname{Op^{w}}(-\tilde{a})u_{1_{0}},u_{1_{0}}\right\rangle_{L^{2}({\mathbb{T}})}-\frac{\beta_{{\varepsilon},W,\tilde{a}}}{\delta_{2}}>0,

(4.46)

where the constants
\delta_{2},\beta_{{\varepsilon},W,\tilde{a}}
are given by Lemma
4.13
.
This gives (
4.35
) and, as discussed above, concludes the proof of the second point of Theorem
1.5
.
To prove (
4.46
) we recall the property (
4.5
) of the escape function, and we define
\chi_{3}\in C_{c}^{\infty}({\mathbb{T}};[0,1])
, such that
\text{supp}(\chi_{3})\subset{\mathcal{W}}
, with
{\mathcal{W}}
given in (
4.5
). Next we define the family of functions

u_{\xi_{0}}(x):=\frac{\chi_{3}(x)}{\left\|\chi_{3}\right\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{T}})}}e^{i\xi_{0}x},\hskip 10.00002pt\forall x\in{\mathbb{T}}.

(4.47)

We claim that there exist constants
C_{\chi},C_{r}>0
such that

\left\langle\operatorname{Op^{w}}(-\tilde{a})u_{\xi_{0}},u_{\xi_{0}}\right\rangle_{L^{2}({\mathbb{T}})}\geq C_{\chi}|\xi_{0}|-C_{r}.

(4.48)

Thus choosing
\xi_{0}
big enough, (
4.48
) proves (
4.46
) with
u_{1_{0}}=u_{\xi_{0}}
. To prove (
4.48
) first remark that, using symbolic calculus results in Lemma
2.1
we have

\left\langle\operatorname{Op^{w}}(-\tilde{a})u_{\xi_{0}},u_{\xi_{0}}\right\rangle_{L^{2}({\mathbb{T}})}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{comp.symbols}}}{{=}}\frac{1}{\left\|\chi_{3}\right\|^{2}_{L^{2}({\mathbb{T}})}}\left\langle\operatorname{Op^{w}}(-\tilde{a}\chi_{3}^{2})e^{i\xi_{0}x},e^{i\xi_{0}x}\right\rangle_{L^{2}({\mathbb{T}})}+\left\langle\operatorname{Op^{w}}(r_{0})u_{\xi_{0}},u_{\xi_{0}}\right\rangle_{L^{2}({\mathbb{T}})},

(4.49)

where
r_{0}\in{\bf S}^{0}({\mathbb{T}})
results from the remainders in the pseudodifferential operations. Next, recalling the definition of
\tilde{a}
in (
4.29
) and using (
4.5
) we obtain

\begin{split}\left\langle\operatorname{Op^{w}}(-\tilde{a}\chi_{3}^{2})e^{i\xi_{0}x},e^{i\xi_{0}x}\right\rangle_{L^{2}({\mathbb{T}})}&\geq\left\langle\operatorname{Op^{w}}\left(\frac{|\xi|}{2}(1-\chi)\chi_{3}^{2}\right)e^{i\xi_{0}x},e^{i\xi_{0}x}\right\rangle_{L^{2}({\mathbb{T}})}-C_{g}\\
&=\left\langle\operatorname{Op^{w}}\left(\frac{|\xi|}{2}(1-\chi)\right)\chi_{3}e^{i\xi_{0}x},\chi_{3}e^{i\xi_{0}x}\right\rangle_{L^{2}({\mathbb{T}})}-C_{g}+\left\langle\operatorname{Op^{w}}(r_{1})e^{i\xi_{0}x},e^{i\xi_{0}x}\right\rangle_{L^{2}({\mathbb{T}})},\end{split}

(4.50)

where
C_{g}>0

2.1
and thus
r_{1}\in{\bf S}^{0}({\mathbb{T}})
results from the remainders in the pseudodifferential operations. Finally, using the definition of Weyl quantization and performing a direct computation one obtains

\left\langle\operatorname{Op^{w}}\left(\frac{|\xi|}{2}(1-\chi)\right)\chi_{3}e^{i\xi_{0}x},\chi_{3}e^{i\xi_{0}x}\right\rangle_{L^{2}({\mathbb{T}})}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{tilde.a}}}{{\geq}}\int_{|\xi|\geq 1}|\xi|\left(\int_{{\mathbb{T}}^{2}}\chi_{3}(y)\overline{\chi_{3}(x)}e^{i(\xi-\xi_{0})(x-y)}{\rm d}y{\rm d}x\right){\rm d}\xi.

(4.51)

Using the Fourier series of

\chi_{3}(x)=\sum_{k\in{\mathbb{Z}}}c_{k}e^{ikx}

\int_{{\mathbb{T}}^{2}}\chi_{3}(y)\overline{\chi_{3}(x)}e^{i(\xi-\xi_{0})(x-y)}{\rm d}y{\rm d}x=(2\pi)^{2}\sum_{k\in{\mathbb{Z}}}|c_{k}|^{2}\delta(\xi-\xi_{0}-k),

where

\begin{split}\left\langle\operatorname{Op^{w}}\left(\frac{|\xi|}{2}(1-\chi)\right)\chi_{3}e^{i\xi_{0}x},\chi_{3}e^{i\xi_{0}x}\right\rangle_{L^{2}({\mathbb{T}})}&\geq(2\pi)^{2}\sum_{k}|c_{k}|^{2}\int_{|\xi|\geq 1}|\xi|\delta(\xi-\xi_{0}-k){\rm d}\xi\\
&=(2\pi)^{2}\sum_{k}|c_{k}|^{2}|\xi_{0}+k|\\
&\geq(2\pi)^{2}|\xi_{0}|\left\|\chi_{3}\right\|_{L^{2}}^{2}-C_{0}\left\|\chi_{3}\right\|_{\frac{1}{2}}^{2}.\end{split}

(4.53)

5
Genericity and conclusion of the proof of Theorem
1.5

We now conclude proving Point 3 of Theorem
1.5
. To this aim we first define the set

{\mathcal{R}}:=\{f\in C^{\infty}({\mathbb{T}}):f(x)=0\implies f^{\prime}(x)\neq 0\}\subset C^{\infty}({\mathbb{T}}),

(5.1)

of smooth non degenerate functions on
{\mathbb{T}}
(see Definition
1.3
). We have the following property:

Lemma 5.1
.

The set
{\mathcal{R}}
is open and dense in
C^{\infty}({\mathbb{T}})
endowed with its Fréchet topology (i.e., the one induced by the family of seminorms
(\left\|\cdot\right\|_{C^{k}})_{k\geq 0}
).

Let us postpone the proof of this fact and first use it to conclude the proof. To this aim, consider the application

{\mathcal{F}}:C^{\infty}({\mathbb{T}}^{2})\to C^{\infty}({\mathbb{T}}),\hskip 10.00002pt{\mathcal{F}}(V(t,x))=\left\langle V\right\rangle_{m}(x).

(5.2)

One can verify that
{\mathcal{F}}
is linear and continuous. Moreover
{\mathcal{V}}_{m,s}\cup{\mathcal{V}}_{m,u}={\mathcal{F}}^{-1}({\mathcal{R}})
. This immediately implies that
{\mathcal{V}}_{m,s}\cup{\mathcal{V}}_{m,u}
is open. We now prove density of
{\mathcal{V}}_{m,s}\cup{\mathcal{V}}_{m,u}
. To this aim, take any
W\in C^{\infty}({\mathbb{T}}^{2})
, and consider its resonant average
\left\langle W\right\rangle_{m}(x)
(recall Definition
1.1
). From density of the set
{\mathcal{R}}
in (
5.1
), for every
{\varepsilon}>0
there exists
V_{0}\in{\mathcal{R}}
such that
{\mathtt{d}}_{C^{\infty}({\mathbb{T}})}(V_{0},\left\langle W\right\rangle_{m})<{\varepsilon}
. Thus, considering the function

W_{0}(t,x):=V_{0}(x+mt)+(W(t,x)-\left\langle W\right\rangle_{m}(x+mt))

(5.3)

one finds that
\left\langle W_{0}\right\rangle_{m}(x)=V_{0}(x)\in{\mathcal{R}}
by construction. Moreover,

{\mathtt{d}}_{C^{\infty}({\mathbb{T}}^{2})}(W_{0},W)={\mathtt{d}}_{C^{\infty}({\mathbb{T}})}(V_{0},\left\langle W\right\rangle_{m})<{\varepsilon},

concluding the proof.

We now sketch the proof Lemma
5.1
.

Proof of Lemma
5.1
.

We omit the proof of the fact that the set
{\mathcal{R}}
is open, since it is a direct application of Sard’s Lemma and of the properties of the Fréchet topology (which ensures a control both on the
C^{0}
and
C^{1}
norms). We now prove that
{\mathcal{R}}
is dense in
C^{\infty}({\mathbb{T}})
: take any function
g\in C^{\infty}({\mathbb{T}})\setminus{\mathcal{R}}
(this means that
g
has some degenerate zeroes). Using Sard’s Lemma, for any
{\varepsilon}>0
, there exists
{\varepsilon}_{0}\in[-{\varepsilon},{\varepsilon}]
,regular value for
g
. Thus defining
h(x):=g(x)-{\varepsilon}_{0}
, we obtain that
{\mathtt{d}}_{C^{\infty}({\mathbb{T}})}(h,g)={\varepsilon}_{0}<{\varepsilon}
. Moreover, the zeroes of
h(x)
are the points such that
g(x)={\varepsilon}_{0}
, thus
h^{\prime}(x)=g^{\prime}(x)\neq 0
, for all such
x\in{\mathbb{T}}
since
{\varepsilon}_{0}
is a regular value for
g
. Thus
h\in{\mathcal{R}}
concluding the proof.
∎
