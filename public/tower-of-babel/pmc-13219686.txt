Cost-Effectiveness of Alternative Immunization Strategies to Protect Infants Against Respiratory Syncytial Virus in Germany: A Decision Modeling Study
Authors: Caroline Lade, Erin Quinn, Lea J Bayer, Amy W Law, Christof von Eiff, Julia Schiffner-Rohe, Mark Atwood, Bennet Huebbe, Ahuva Averin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionGermany’s Standing Committee on Vaccination (STIKO) recommends the use of monoclonal antibody nirsevimab for infants in their first respiratory syncytial virus (RSV) season; however, incorporating RSVpreF maternal vaccination may protect more infants while lowering healthcare costs. We therefore evaluated the cost-effectiveness of a complementary immunization approach comprising maternal RSVpreF vaccination during pregnancy and nirsevimab for infants not yet protected to prevent lower respiratory tract disease due to RSV (RSV-LRTD) among infants in Germany.MethodsClinical and economic outcomes of RSV-LRTD and the expected impact of interventions were modeled from birth to age < 1 year. Model inputs were based on German data as available. Intervention strategies included no intervention, nirsevimab alone (N-all; uptake 70%), and the complementary immunization approach (MV + N). MV + N comprised seasonally administered maternal vaccination (MV; uptake 50%) with nirsevimab (N; uptake 70%) for unprotected infants (MV + N). Nirsevimab was administered seasonally with catch-up for infants born outside the RSV season. Analyses were conducted from the societal perspective and discounted 3% annually.ResultsWithout immunization, 70,041 cases of RSV-LRTD (hospital: 23,273, ambulatory: 46,768) and 22 deaths were projected; total RSV-attributable medical care costs were 118.6 million (M) €. With N-all, there were 39,247 cases (hospital 12,953; ambulatory 26,294) and 13 deaths; total associated costs were 311.5M € (intervention 237.2M €; medical care 66.0M €; indirect 8.4M €). Compared to N-all, MV + N prevented an additional 666 cases and 1 death, with a difference in total costs of − 17.8M € (intervention − 12.7M €; medical care − 4.7M €; indirect − 0.4M €). With 31 QALYs gained, MV + N was dominant versus N-All.ConclusionA complementary strategy with maternal RSVpreF vaccination and nirsevimab immunization for otherwise unprotected and preterm infants in Germany would provide a greater benefit compared to nirsevimab alone, protecting substantially more infants at lower total costs.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s40121-026-01344-1.Keywords: Respiratory syncytial virus, Maternal immunization, Cost-effectiveness analysis, Germany, Infants, Immunity, Vaccines, Passive immunization, Monoclonal antibodies

Key Summary Points

Why carry out this study? Respiratory syncytial virus (RSV) causes a substantial burden of lower respiratory tract disease and hospitalizations among infants in Germany, leading to significant healthcare costs.Both maternal vaccine RSVpreF and monoclonal antibody nirsevimab have been authorized for use in Germany, with nirsevimab currently recommended by Germany’s Standing Committee on Vaccination.This study evaluated the cost-effectiveness of a complementary immunization approach with maternal RSV vaccination or nirsevimab compared to nirsevimab alone for prevention of RSV in infants. What was learned from the study? Results suggest that the complementary strategy would protect more infants and reduce RSV-related healthcare costs, thus being a more cost-effective strategy than nirsevimab alone.These findings support considerations of a complementary approach to immunization in order to maximize infant protection and optimize resource use in Germany.

Introduction

Respiratory syncytial virus (RSV) in infants and young children imposes a substantial medical and economic burden on healthcare facilities in Germany and globally, especially when presenting as severe lower respiratory tract disease (LRTD) [1]. Nearly all children experience an episode of RSV within the first 2 years of life; young infants, infants born prematurely, and infants with a history of chronic medical conditions have been identified as having an increased risk of severe RSV-LRTD, with combinations of these risk factors believed to have a compounding effect [1]. Beyond the acute burden of RSV-LRTD, infection during early childhood may also lead to long-term sequalae, increasing the risk of recurrent wheezing and asthma [1].

Palivizumab, a multidose monoclonal antibody, has been authorized by the European Medicines Agency (EMA) to protect infants against RSV-LRTD since 1999 and has since been available for use in Germany; however, eligibility criteria were strict. Palivizumab is indicated by the EMA only for children born at ≤ 35 weeks of gestation who are aged < 6 months at the onset of RSV season and children aged < 2 years born with a heart disease or treated for bronchopulmonary dysplasia within last 6 months [2]. As a result of these eligibility criteria as well as high acquisition costs, the burden of RSV-LRTD in infants remains high in Germany [1].

Two more recently developed interventions are now available for prevention of RSV-LRTD in infants: single-dose monoclonal antibody (i.e., nirsevimab) and bivalent stabilized prefusion F subunit maternal vaccine (i.e., RSVpreF). Nirsevimab and RSVpreF vaccine have both been evaluated in phase 3 clinical trials and proven to be safe and efficacious [3, 4]. The EMA has authorized use of nirsevimab (for infants during their first RSV season and children aged < 24 months who remain vulnerable through their second RSV season) [5] and RSVpreF vaccine (for pregnant individuals at 24–36 weeks of gestation) to protect infants against RSV-LRTD via transplacental transfer of antibodies from mother to child [6]. Another single-dose monoclonal antibody, clesrovimab, has also received a positive opinion recommending approval from the EMA’s Committee for Medicinal Products for Human Use, and is currently awaiting approval [7]. In 2024, Germany’s Standing Committee on Vaccination (STIKO) issued a recommendation for the use of nirsevimab to protect all infants during their first RSV season [1]. Expanding the recommendation to include RSVpreF vaccine may result in a larger number of infants receiving RSV immunoprophylaxis.

A recently published epidemiologic study employed a dynamic transmission model to evaluate the impact of various immunization strategies in Germany, including nirsevimab for all infants and, separately, seasonal maternal vaccination; no strategy involving a complementary approach of maternal vaccine and nirsevimab was evaluated [8]. It was projected that immunization with nirsevimab would most significantly reduce the burden of RSV; however, substantially higher uptake was assumed for nirsevimab compared to maternal vaccination. This leaves open the question of whether a complementary immunization approach (i.e., administration of maternal vaccine with nirsevimab immunization for infants still vulnerable to RSV) could protect even more infants. Therefore, we evaluated the cost-effectiveness of a complementary immunization strategy for prevention of RSV-LRTD in infants in Germany.

Methods

Model Description

We employed a population-based Markov model to evaluate the clinical outcomes and economic costs associated with RSV-LRTD as well as the impact of various immunization strategies (i.e., comprising nirsevimab and/or RSVpreF vaccine) to protect infants against RSV-LRTD (Fig. 1). The model tallies RSV-LRTD-related outcomes among infants during their first year of life as well as the potential impact of premature RSV-LRTD-related death (including its impact on future/lifetime productivity). Infants may receive protection via maternal vaccination, receive protection via nirsevimab administration, or remain unprotected against RSV-LRTD; administration of interventions may be assumed to occur year-round or be limited to only select months leading up to or during the RSV season.

Model schematic depicting uptake of RSV interventions: complementary RSVpreF vaccine with nirsevimab. RSV-Amb, RSV treated in ambulatory settings; RSV-H, RSV requiring hospitalization. *Protected and unprotected infants may be subject to different probabilities of infection, depending on intervention effectiveness. †Indicates Markov node. ‡Model tallies RSV-related outcomes until age 1 year or death, whichever occurs first; dotted lines indicate that infants may be repeatedly exposed to RSV throughout this time. The model estimates clinical and economic outcomes with each intervention strategy on a monthly basis for the model population. Clinical outcomes include RSV-LRTD requiring hospitalization (RSV-H), RSV-LRTD treated in an ambulatory setting (RSV-Amb), and deaths associated with RSV-H. RSV-Amb encounters include all patients who attend a primary care consultation but do not require hospitalization. Infants who experience RSV-LRTD and their primary caregivers may also incur an infection-related utility decrement. Infants with protection from either RSVpreF vaccine or nirsevimab may be assumed to be at lower risk of RSV-LRTD infection; the protection conferred by RSVpreF vaccination may differ for RSV-H and RSV-Amb to reflect the tendency for higher vaccine effectiveness against more severe outcomes [9]. Economic outcomes include direct medical care costs, costs associated with intervention acquisition/administration, and indirect costs associated with productivity loss. Costs and benefits are discounted on a monthly basis. A comprehensive model description can be found in a recent publication evaluating the cost-effectiveness of maternal RSVpreF vaccine and nirsevimab immunization strategies among infants in the Netherlands [10].

Model Estimation

Model inputs were assumed to vary by infant age, care setting, and/or gestational age at birth, whenever appropriate. Data were extracted from Germany-specific sources, if available; for inputs for which German data were not available, data from comparable country settings were employed. Input values employed in base case analyses are summarized in Tables 1–3 and Fig. 2, with more detailed information available in Supplementary Tables S1–S5; input values employed in scenario analyses and probabilistic sensitivity analyses are summarized in Supplementary Tables S6–S7.

Population

The model population comprised 1 year’s worth of pregnant women (N = 766,635) and live-born infants (N = 778,262) in Germany, based on data from the German Federal Statistics Office (FSO) [11, 12]. Infants were categorized based on term status (i.e., defined by gestational age in weeks [wGA] at birth: full term, ≥ 37 wGA; late preterm, 32–36 wGA; early preterm, 28–31 wGA; and extreme preterm, ≤ 27 wGA) and calendar month of birth [13].

Disease Rates

Age-specific incidence rates of RSV requiring hospitalization were based on a retrospective health claims study (Table 1) [14]. Incidence of RSV treated in ambulatory settings was derived based on number of ambulatory visits from 2000 to 2008 in the UK [15], which largely resembled available estimates of one-season data from Germany [16]. Because some patients are expected to have more than one visit with their general practitioner or other specialist during the same episode of RSV, the number of visits from Cromer et al. was divided by 1.4, the average number of visits per RSV-Amb case [15, 17]. Incidence for infants aged < 6 months was based on UK data and allocated by single month of age based on US data; lacking data for ages 6 to < 12 months, the relative risk for infants aged 6 to < 12 months versus 0 to < 6 months in the USA was applied to existing UK data [15, 18]. Age-specific rates of RSV-H and RSV-Amb were allocated by term status (i.e., full term, late/early preterm, extreme preterm) based on relative risk and distributed across calendar months using data from the aforementioned German claims study [14]. RSV-Amb rates were further adjusted by the model to include only cases of RSV presenting as LRTD, assuming 65% presented as LRTD in infants aged < 6 months and 30% among those aged ≥ 6 months, based on US data [19].

Case Fatality and Mortality Rates

Case fatality rate (CFR) among hospitalized episodes of RSV-LRTD was estimated to be 0.06 per 100 for infants aged < 6 months and 0.16 per 100 for infants aged ≥ 6 months based on data from a recent German analysis [20]. CFR was assumed to be invariant by term status. There was assumed to be no RSV-related mortality among infants with RSV-Amb.

General infant mortality (i.e., unrelated to RSV-LRTD) for infants aged < 1 month was estimated based on 2023 data from the FSO and—lacking granular source data—allocated by single month of age using US data [21]. German estimates for relative risk of general infant mortality by term status—available for infants aged < 7 days—were scaled using US ratios (for infants aged < 1 month, 1 to < 6 months, 6 to < 12 months) to derive relative risks for the first year of life [21, 22].

Intervention Effectiveness

Effectiveness for RSVpreF vaccine and nirsevimab, respectively, was extracted from a published cost-effectiveness analysis of the two interventions in which Hodgson et al. applied a Bayesian framework to estimate effectiveness through 12 months based on published clinical trial data from MATISSE (RSVpreF vaccine) and MELODY (nirsevimab) (Fig. 2) [3, 23–25]. Note that Fig. 2 was adapted from Langedijk et al. [10] with permission from the authors. For RSVpreF vaccine, trial-based efficacy for severe RSV-positive medically attended lower respiratory tract illness (LRTI) was used as a proxy for vaccine effectiveness (VE) against RSV-H, and trial-based efficacy against RSV-positive medically attended LRTI was used as a proxy for VE against RSV-Amb. For nirsevimab, trial-based efficacy against RSV-related LRTI was applied for RSV-LRTD treated in both care settings [23]. For maternal vaccine, extreme and early preterm infants and all infants born < 2 weeks after maternal administration of RSVpreF vaccine were assumed to receive 0% effectiveness; all other infants were assumed to receive effectiveness as depicted in Fig. 2. There was assumed to be a 1-week delay in nirsevimab protection after administration, corresponding to assumptions employed by the US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) [26].

Direct Costs

Term-specific costs of RSV-H were based on long-term RSV-attributable mean excess costs reported by Lade et al. (Table 2) [14]. Estimated cost per RSV-Amb episode from Ehlken et al. was assumed to be invariant by age and term status [27]. All costs were inflated to reflect 2024 euros (€) [28].

Indirect Costs

Costs related to caregiver productivity loss were calculated using labor force participation data for mothers of children aged < 1 year (i.e., 9%, 45% of which working full time) [29], estimates of average daily wage (i.e., 171.28 €) [29], and number of work-loss days (i.e., RSV-H: 3.5 days, based on duration of illness and assuming a 5-day work week; RSV-Amb: assumed 2.0 days) [30]. Indirect costs related to lost future earnings from premature RSV-related mortality in infants were estimated based on workforce participation rates, discounted annual earnings, and life expectancy in Germany [29, 31, 32]. Specifically, each infant death associated with RSV was assigned an indirect cost for each year that the infant would have been expected to live otherwise (i.e., based on general population mortality data); the indirect cost assigned for each year was calculated as age-specific average annual earnings multiplied by age-specific workforce participation rate, with discounting applied.

Intervention Price

RSVpreF vaccine acquisition cost was assumed to be 213.61 € (plus 10.79 € in administration costs) [33, 34]. Nirsevimab acquisition was estimated to cost 416.50 € for infants receiving nirsevimab in the hospital shortly after birth and 453.83 € for infants receiving nirsevimab at their general practitioner’s office (plus 9.30 € in administration costs) [35, 36].

Utilities and Disutilities

Lacking published data on health-related quality of life for infants, general infant utility was assumed to be 1 [37]. Utility values for infants with RSV-LRTD were estimated to be 0.59 (RSV-H) and 0.84 (RSV-Amb) throughout the period of illness (i.e., 14 days for hospital and 7 days for Amb). Total quality-adjusted life year (QALY) loss was thus estimated to be 0.0157 and 0.0061 for infants treated in hospital and ambulatory settings, respectively (Table 3) [38]; QALY loss was assumed to be invariant by term status [39].

General population utility values were estimated based on age-specific German EQ-5D-5L scores and adjusted to appropriate age groups using FSO data [32, 40]. Lacking data on utility values among persons aged 1–17 years, linear interpolation was employed between assumed utility for infants and estimated utility for adults aged 18–49 years to estimate utility values for young people. Health-related quality of life loss among caregivers was not considered because of insufficient data.

Analyses

To provide context for the potential impact of immunization, the baseline clinical and economic burden of RSV among infants in Germany in the absence of immunization was evaluated. Immunization strategies considered in cost-effectiveness analyses included the currently recommended standard of care, nirsevimab for all infants (herein, “nirsevimab strategy”) and a complementary immunization approach comprising RSVpreF vaccine for pregnant women as well as nirsevimab for all infants who remained unprotected at birth or were born at < 32 wGA (herein, “complementary immunization strategy”). In the nirsevimab strategy, all infants were considered eligible for nirsevimab. In the complementary immunization strategy, RSVpreF vaccine was offered to pregnant women between 24 and 36 weeks of gestation during June through February (i.e., whose infant’s expected delivery date was during RSV season [October through March]); nirsevimab eligibility was limited to full term and late preterm infants who were not protected through maternal vaccination with RSVpreF vaccine and to all early/extreme preterm infants (i.e., regardless of RSVpreF vaccine use) [41]. Full term and late preterm infants were only considered protected by RSVpreF vaccine if their mothers were vaccinated ≥ 2 weeks before birth. Cost-effectiveness of the complementary immunization strategy versus the nirsevimab strategy was evaluated in terms of cost per QALY gained; costs and benefits were discounted at 3% annually, consistent with Institute for Quality and Efficiency in Health Care (Institut für Qualität und Wirtschaftlichkeit im Gesundheitswesen, IQWiG) recommendations [42, 43].

Baseline Clinical and Economic Analysis

In the analysis establishing baseline clinical and economic burden of RSV-LRTD in Germany, infants were assumed not to be immunized against RSV (either via maternal RSVpreF vaccine or nirsevimab).

Base Case Analysis

In the complementary immunization strategy, 50% of pregnant women—with eligibility based on expected delivery month—were assumed to receive RSVpreF vaccine according to observed maternal pertussis vaccination rates in 2022 [44]. The distribution of uptake by week of gestation was based on assumptions given what is currently known about vaccination timing [45]. In all strategies, nirsevimab uptake was assumed to be 70% among eligible infants given high acceptance of direct infant immunization in Germany [8].

Among infants eligible for nirsevimab, infants who were born outside of the RSV season (i.e., April through September) were assumed to receive nirsevimab in September (40%), October (40%), or November (20%) based on current recommendations and expectations [5]. Infants born during the RSV season (i.e., October through March) were assumed to receive nirsevimab within the first 3 months of life. For full term and late preterm infants, 80% of administration was assumed to occur before discharge from the birth hospitalization while 20% was assumed to occur at 1 month of age; for early and extreme preterm infants who typically require an extended stay in the hospital after birth, nirsevimab administration was assumed to be delayed until 1–3 months of age (i.e., early preterm: 50% at 1 month, 50% at 2 months; extreme preterm: 50% at 2 months, 50% at 3 months).

Sensitivity and Scenario Analyses

Deterministic sensitivity analyses (DSA) and scenario analyses were conducted to evaluate the impact of variation in key model parameters. DSA considered variations in the following inputs: incidence rates (± 20% of base case values), medical care costs (± 20% of base case values), intervention efficacy (± 20% of base case values), and discount rates (0% and, alternatively, 5%).

Scenario analyses considering alternative uptake rates for the complementary immunization strategy (40% and 60% for RSVpreF vaccine; 60% and 80% for nirsevimab) were also conducted to address uncertainty surrounding acceptance of interventions within a complementary immunization approach. Additional scenario analyses employed the following alternative assumptions: RSVpreF vaccine administered year-round (i.e., easier for administrative implementation and more robust to changes in RSV seasonal patterns), nirsevimab administration delayed to 3.2 months of age on average (i.e., reflective observed real-world uptake of palivizumab) [8], and nirsevimab efficacy assumptions based on the recently published dynamic transmission model by Krauer et al. [8].

Probabilistic Sensitivity Analyses

Probabilistic sensitivity analyses (PSA) were conducted to account for uncertainty around key model parameters by applying measures of variability. The analyses (n = 1000 replications) generated a scatterplot of the incremental cost per QALY.

Results

Baseline Clinical and Economic Analysis

There were 778,262 live born infants in the model population. Without use of interventions, there would be 23,273 RSV-H episodes, 46,768 RSV-Amb episodes, and 22 RSV-related deaths among infants each year; associated medical care costs for treatment for RSV-LRTD would be 118.6 million (M) €.

Base Case Analysis

In the nirsevimab strategy, intervention was administered to 542,584 infants. There were 12,953 RSV-H episodes and 26,294 RSV-Amb episodes (Table 4, Fig. 3). Total costs were 311.5M €, comprising 66.0M € in medical care costs, 237.2M € in nirsevimab acquisition and administration costs, and 8.4M € in indirect costs due to productivity loss (Fig. 4).

In the complementary immunization strategy, 596,115 infants were considered protected against RSV-LRTD (via RSVpreF vaccine 178,298; via nirsevimab 417,817), resulting in 980 fewer RSV-H episodes and 313 additional RSV-Amb episodes, and yielding 31 additional QALYs (vs. nirsevimab strategy). Among the 980 hospitalizations prevented with the complementary immunization strategy, 664 (68%) were attributable to infants aged < 2 months and 965 (98%) were attributable to infants aged < 6 months. Compared to nirsevimab alone, the complementary immunization strategy prevented 198 more ambulatory episodes among infants aged < 2 months, but 511 fewer episodes among infants aged 2 to < 12 months, resulting in a net increase of 313 RSV-Amb episodes. The complementary immunization strategy thus reduced overall costs by 17.8M € (medical care 4.7M €; interventions 12.7M €; indirect costs 0.4M €), and was found to be dominant (i.e., more effective and less costly) compared to the nirsevimab strategy.

Sensitivity and Scenario Analyses

Apart from the scenario analysis applying 80% nirsevimab uptake within the complementary immunization strategy, all DSA and scenario analyses found the complementary immunization strategy to be cost-saving compared to the nirsevimab strategy (Table 5, Fig. 5). In three analyses (i.e., year-round RSVpreF vaccine administration, effectiveness based on Krauer et al., 60% nirsevimab uptake within the complementary immunization strategy), the nirsevimab strategy yielded higher QALYs than the complementary immunization strategy, with differences in QALYs of 3, 1, and 27, respectively. However, the complementary immunization strategy yielded additional QALYs (vs. nirsevimab alone) in the 12 other analyses (range 25–91). Discussion

STIKO has recommended the use of nirsevimab as the only RSV prevention strategy to protect infants during their first RSV season [1]. Analyses presented herein evaluated if a complementary immunization strategy comprising maternal RSVpreF vaccine during pregnancy and nirsevimab administered during the first year of life to infants not protected by RSVpreF vaccine would further reduce the clinical and economic burden of RSV-LRTD among infants in Germany. Findings from these analyses suggest that the complementary approach is more effective and less costly than the current use of nirsevimab alone, preventing 980 (7.6%) more hospitalizations while reducing total costs by 17.8M € (5.7%). The complementary strategy remained dominant over nirsevimab alone in almost all sensitivity and scenario analyses, demonstrating its considerable benefits, though the differences in QALYs imply that clinical benefits of both strategies are similar in magnitude. Complementary immunization was most beneficial among the youngest, most vulnerable infants, yielding a 28% reduction in RSV-LRTD hospitalizations and a 15% reduction in RSV-LRTD ambulatory episodes among infants aged < 1 month, compared to nirsevimab alone. This is substantial given the first few months of life is when the risk of RSV-LRTD is highest and infants are most vulnerable to a severe course of infection and sequelae [14, 46].

Findings from this study demonstrate that a complementary immunization approach consistently delivers key advantages across varying real-world conditions and implementation scenarios. The benefits conferred by RSVpreF vaccine in terms of protection for the youngest infants are even more pronounced when nirsevimab administration is not assumed to occur immediately after discharge from the birth hospitalization or immediately following home birth. This was demonstrated in the scenario analysis employing timing of nirsevimab administration that is consistent with observed real-world timing of palivizumab administration (i.e., average age at administration 3.2 months) [8]. Under such delayed conditions, the complementary immunization strategy prevented in excess of six times more total cases of RSV-LRTD (vs. nirsevimab alone) than in the base case. Because maternal vaccination is, per indication, expected to occur mostly within the timeframe required for adequate transfer of antibodies, deviations in real-world versus optimal implementation are likely to be less impactful in the complementary strategy than with nirsevimab alone. Furthermore, because maternal RSVpreF vaccination accounts for only a fraction of the total intervention costs (i.e., 18% in the base case; 14–40% in scenarios/sensitivities) while contributing meaningfully to the medical care cost reduction via disease prevention, the complementary approach consistently reduces the economic burden of RSV compared to nirsevimab alone. These cost savings are especially significant given growing concerns in Germany over costs of healthcare [47]. Cost reductions are most prominent in the scenario analysis considering year-round RSVpreF vaccination within the complementary strategy, lowering costs considerably with similar numbers of clinical events (vs. nirsevimab alone). Year-round RSVpreF vaccination may also be more resistant to changes in seasonal trends, though this was not evaluated in the analyses presented herein [48]. Additionally, as the uptake of maternal RSVpreF vaccination increases, the cost savings with the complementary approach become greater. These findings highlight the value of a complementary immunization strategy in maximizing clinical benefit and achieving cost savings under various implementation scenarios.

In addition, a complementary immunization approach may also relieve some of the implementation burden on clinicians and hospital staff. With nirsevimab alone, physicians must immunize all eligible infants born outside of RSV season between September and November during a period of already heightened healthcare utilization due to seasonal illnesses. If the seasonal surge in demand leads to delayed or missed immunization, outcomes would be worse than projected by the optimal conditions as assumed in our model, especially if nirsevimab is the only immunization strategy available. In contrast, maternal RSVpreF vaccine has been approved by the EMA for administration between 24–36 wGA. A strategy leveraging routine prenatal care visits for maternal immunization has proven successful for pertussis vaccination, which has been recommended for pregnant women in Germany since 2020 [49]. By expanding immunization efforts across both prenatal and pediatric care settings, a complementary approach would alleviate strain on pediatric services, increase opportunities for timely protection, and may result in higher overall immunization coverage [50].

Although maternal RSVpreF vaccine is not yet recommended by STIKO, reimbursement agreements are in place for a substantial proportion of statutory health insurances. For approximately one-third of pregnant women, maternal RSVpreF vaccine is currently covered via a pre-payment reimbursement model, giving parents the choice and also minimizing financial obstacles. These agreements represent an important step forward in expanding preventive care and reflect growing recognition of the maternal vaccine’s value. However, access to RSVpreF vaccine remains limited as pregnant women insured by the remaining two-thirds of providers currently lack the opportunity. Additionally, recent surveyed data suggests hesitancy among German parents towards nirsevimab use in infants, with only 40.5% of surveyed parents agreeing to nirsevimab administration for their newborn during the 2024–2025 RSV season [51]. Offering opportunity and enabling parental choice for how to immunize their child may be key to increasing overall uptake and ensuring broader protection, particularly during the critical first few months of life.

The projected clinical impact of interventions in modeling exercises such as this one is contingent upon effectiveness assumptions. Krauer et al. recently published an analysis, in consultation with STIKO, modeling the clinical impact of RSVpreF vaccine and nirsevimab, respectively, versus the former standard of care (i.e., palivizumab for high-risk infants) for infants in Germany using a dynamic transmission model [8]. Although differences in model design and comparators preclude us from benchmarking our findings against those published by Krauer et al., we did conduct a scenario analysis employing the effectiveness estimates that were adopted in their model. Krauer et al. assumed 100% effectiveness of nirsevimab against RSV-H at the time of administration (with waning applied for months 1 to < 12 after administration) based on MELODY trial data which demonstrated 78.4% efficacy against RSV-H in preterm infants (29 to < 35 wGA) through 150 days after administration [3, 8]. While MELODY trial data demonstrated only 62.1% effectiveness of nirsevimab among infants born at ≥ 35 wGA, Krauer et al. did not appear to adjust effectiveness values to account for the distribution of infants born < 35 wGA and ≥ 35wGA [3, 8, 52]. Krauer et al. also assumed effectiveness of RSVpreF vaccine against RSV-H to be only 75% at birth, which is lower than the 81.1% efficacy against severe RSV-LRTI in the first 90 days of life as shown in the MATISSE trial [25]. We note that this differs greatly from effectiveness reported by the US CDC, which was comparable for the two interventions (RSVpreF, 70–79%; nirsevimab, 79–82%) [53]. Because effectiveness assumptions employed by Krauer et al. are more favorable for nirsevimab and less favorable for RSVpreF vaccine than our base case inputs, our scenario analysis employing effectiveness from Krauer et al. demonstrated lower clinical impact for the complementary strategy than for nirsevimab alone.

Our study is subject to several limitations relating to input parameters employed. Trial effectiveness endpoints for RSVpreF (e.g., efficacy against severe medically attended RSV-related LRTI and against medically attended LRTI) do not perfectly align with the clinical outcomes reported by our cost-effectiveness model (i.e., hospitalization, ambulatory visit). Similarly, effectiveness of nirsevimab was assumed to be invariant by setting of care due to limitations of available trial data. We also conservatively assumed that RSVpreF vaccination would not confer any protection to infants born at < 32 wGA. While vaccine trial results do not provide enough data to make conclusions on effectiveness among early and extreme preterm infants, recently published immunogenicity data suggests that the transplacental transfer of maternal antibodies may occur as early as 24 weeks of gestation (i.e., demonstrated by an overlap of confidence intervals for preterm versus full term infant titres) [25]. These data suggest that early preterm infants would be protected by RSVpreF vaccine if there were ≥ 14 days between vaccination and birth, in which case the impact of RSVpreF vaccine is somewhat underestimated in analyses conducted herein [25]. As the body of real-world effectiveness data grows, the accuracy of the assumptions employed in this analysis will become clearer.

Our analyses may also be subject to limitations related to the lack of appropriate Germany-specific data. Some model inputs were based on data from other high-income countries, assessed on a case-by-case basis. Most significantly, incidence rates for RSV-LRTD treated in the ambulatory setting were estimated using UK and US data [15, 18]. German data on the burden of RSV-Amb were not employed because there is only one season’s worth of published data [44]; however, we note that the available German incidence of RSV-Amb among infants aged < 1 year (i.e., 124.0 per 1000) is highly comparable to UK RSV-Amb rate employed herein (i.e., 130.2 per 1000) [15–18, 44]. Though proxy data may introduce some uncertainty, we employed conservative assumptions whenever possible in an attempt to replicate the German landscape as closely as possible and minimize potential impact. We also note that, while results herein are meant to be specific to Germany, a complementary immunization strategy like the one employed herein would be cost saving to the healthcare system of any country currently employing nirsevimab alone (i.e., assuming cost of nirsevimab is higher than that of RSVpreF globally).

Lastly, there are several benefits of RSVpreF vaccine and nirsevimab which are not evaluated by the model, resulting in likely underestimation the benefits of interventions. These include potential benefits of RSVpreF vaccine for pregnant women, prevention of long-term consequences of RSV-LRTD, and prevention of cases of RSV not requiring medical attention and/or manifesting as upper respiratory tract disease [54, 55]. Such simplifications in model design likely confer a conservative bias against RSVpreF vaccine and/or nirsevimab.

Conclusions

Immunization with nirsevimab alone and complementary strategy (i.e., RSVpreF vaccination among pregnant women complemented by nirsevimab administration among infants not yet protected) would yield similar overall clinical benefits against RSV-LRTD among infants; however, the complementary strategy would yield substantial cost savings, reducing costs by 17.8 million € compared to nirsevimab alone. The complementary immunization strategy would also increase overall coverage, protect the youngest infants, reduce the burden on clinicians, and expand choices for families. As RSV immunization policy continues to evolve, data suggests that adding RSVpreF to the current recommendation and thus adopting a complementary strategy would represent a transformational shift for the statutory healthcare system in Germany.
