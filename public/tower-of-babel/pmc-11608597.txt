Personalized Medicine in Histiocytic Disorders: Novel Targets in Patients Without MAPK Alterations
Authors: Katherine ER Smith, Aldo A Acosta-Medina, Surendra Dasari, Wasantha Ranatunga, Karen L Rech, Aishwarya Ravindran, Jason R Young, Patrick W McGarrah, Gordon J Ruan, Saurabh S Zanwar, Jenny J Li, Julio C Sartori-Valinotti, Jessica N Snider, Thomas E Witzig, Gaurav Goyal, Ronald S Go, Jithma P Abeykoon
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractPURPOSEBRAF and MEK inhibitors are standard treatments in histiocytic disorders, such as Erdheim-Chester disease (ECD). Some patients lack MAPK-pathway alterations, making these treatments less effective.METHODSWe describe three patients with histiocytic disorders who have novel non-MAPK pathway alterations. These alterations were studied through genomic and in silico analyses when applicable, then treated with off-label medications rationally selected on the basis of genomic alterations.RESULTSPatient 1 had rapidly progressive ECD involving the CNS. A CSF1R in-frame deletion (p.S560_P566del) was identified, and in silico modeling predicted a gain-of-function mutation. This alteration was targeted with pexidartinib, which led to a clinical complete response (CR) within 2 months, and a partial response (PR) on imaging after 3 months. After 15 months, the disease became resistant to pexidartinib and transformed to histiocytic sarcoma. Patient 2 has skin-only involvement of a xanthogranuloma disorder. A KIF5B-FGFR1 fusion was identified on RNA sequencing and targeted with pemigatinib. At 24 months of follow-up, she remains in a clinical PR. Patient 3 has ECD involving the bone marrow, gastrointestinal tract, and subcutaneous tissues. A MEF2C-FLT3 fusion was identified and targeted with sorafenib. He achieved a clinical CR and radiographic PR within 3 months, which has continued for 30 months.CONCLUSIONWe report three patients with histiocytic disorders harboring novel alterations who had sustained responses to off-label kinase inhibitors specific to their histiocytic disorder. Pathogenic variants outside of the MAPK pathway, including variants of unknown significant, may be targeted with readily available small molecules.

INTRODUCTION

Histiocytic malignancies are a heterogeneous group of rare myeloid-derived neoplasms classified into five categories: Langerhans-related (L group), which includes Erdheim-Chester disease (ECD); cutaneous and mucocutaneous non-langerhans cell histiocytosis (LCH; C group); malignant histiocytoses (M group); Rosai-Dorfman disease (R group); and hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis; and macrophage activation syndrome (H group).1,2 Here, we focus on ECD, of which there are approximately 1,500 cases documented.3 Clinical manifestations range from involvement of a single organ system with minimal symptoms to a multi-system, rapidly progressive disease. Diagnosis involves clinicopathologic and radiologic correlation in conjunction with molecular studies. Typical histopathologic features include abundant foamy (xanthomatous) histiocytes with associated Touton giant cells. By immunohistochemistry (IHC), these histiocytes are positive for macrophage markers (CD68, CD163, and factor XIIIa), and negative for Langerhans cell markers (CD1a and langerin).4 Many cases have activating MAPK pathway mutations (RAS/RAF/MEK), which have led to the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) approval of BRAF and MEK inhibitors (BRAFi and MEKi) as a standard of care.2,5

For patients with BRAFV600E-mutated disease, treatment with the BRAFi vemurafenib is indicated. Otherwise, if there are any other MAPK pathway mutations or no detectable mutations, then the MEKi cobimetinib is recommended.2 Treatment options independent of BRAF status can include cytarabine, cladribine, methotrexate, and others.2 Additionally, there are reports of actionable mutations, including ALK fusions, NTRK fusions, RET fusions, and CSF1R alterations.2,6-8

Our study describes three patients with histiocytic disorders who have novel non-MAPK pathway alterations that were targeted with an off-label drug and achieved sustained clinical benefit. These included a CSF1R in-frame deletion (p.S560_P566del) treated with pexidartinib, a CSF1 inhibitor; a KIF5B-FGFR1 fusion found on RNA sequencing treated with pemigatinib, a fibroblast growth factor receptor (FGFR) inhibitor; and a MEF2C- FLT3 fusion targeted with sorafenib, a multikinase inhibitor that has activity against FLT3. In silico modeling was used for the CSF1R alteration to confirm a gain-of-function alteration. After disease progression with pexidartinib, we reevaluated tumor genomics and describe how the disease evolved. Overall, we highlight the use of personalized treatment approaches rationally selected on the basis of molecular alterations in histiocytic disorders.

METHODS

Patients and Drug Approval

All three patients were treated at the Mayo Clinic and provided permission to be included in our research efforts. Pexidartinib was obtained under an emergency Investigational New Drug (IND) approval from the FDA, then later covered by the patient's insurance. Pemigatinib and sorafenib were approved through the patients' insurance companies.

DNA and RNA Sequencing

Targeted next-generation sequencing (NGS) was obtained through either the Tempus xT/xO assay, a 648-gene panel, or FoundationOne CDx, a 324-gene panel. Both NGS platforms are in vitro diagnostic tests that use targeted high-throughput hybridization-based capture technology and DNA isolated from formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tumor tissue specimens to detect genetic alterations.9-12 Tempus xT DNA library preparation is performed using Integrated DNA Technologies (IDT) unique dual-index adapters, followed by hybrid capture with custom-designed IDT xGen Lockdown probes. The library is sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 instrument.9 Whole-transcriptome RNA sequencing is used for gene rearrangement detection.10 FoundationOne CDx uses whole-genome shotgun library construction and hybridization-based capture of DNA extracted from FFPE tumor tissue before deep sequencing on the Illumina HiSeq 4000 for analysis.11,12 Full gene panels for both Tempus and FoundationOne can be found in the Data Supplement.

In Silico Modeling

The variant forms of CSF1R (CSF1R S560_P566 and CSF1R_F563_P566) were modeled by submitting the sequence to the I-Tasser server (I-TASSER13,16), including the missing regions in the wild-type (WT) structure (aa 555-561 and aa 687-747) and the N-terminus. The Coot software program14,17 was used to test the variant structures for torsion, planar peptide, transpeptide, and Ramachandran restraints, then made corrections by applying those restraints. The models were then simulated using AutoDock Vina 1.1.2 (Scripps Research) and tested using Coot software. All structures (WT with pexidartinib-bound PDB: 4r7h, CSF1R S560_P566, and CSF1R_F563_P566) were aligned and visualized, and figures were made using Chimera 1.14.15,18

IHC

The clones and commercial source used included BRAF V600E: VE1 clone, Abcam; p-ERK: D13.14.4E clone, Cell Signaling; and p-AKT: LP18, Leica. Testing was performed on paraffin-embedded whole-tissue sections using standard methods on Ventana Benchmark automated stainers (Ventana Medical Systems, Tucson, AZ).

Response Assessments

Response was assessed per the 2020 LCH consensus recommendations for determining surveillance modality and response assessment criteria, which are applicable to ECD as well.1,2 Radiographic response was assessed centrally by an expert radiologist. The patient with disease confined to skin was monitored with physical examinations, while the other two patients with multisystem disease were surveilled with imaging.

Statement on Personal Health Information

All patients discussed in this study consented to be included. The patient with photographs included (deidentified) agreed to sign a release form.

RESULTS

Patient 1: CSF1R In-Frame Deletion

The patient was diagnosed at age 27 years after she presented with headaches, vertigo, and numbness in the feet that later progressed to gait instability, numbness in the hands, diplopia, and hearing loss. Imaging revealed enhancing lesions in the CNS, both the brain and the spinal cord. After biopsy of a brain lesion, she was diagnosed with ECD primarily involving the CNS. Pathology showed atypical histiocytic proliferation, strong factor XIIIa expression, and negative BRAFV600E staining. NGS lacked MAPK-pathway pathogenic variants, so she was first treated with cobimetinib 60 mg daily on days 1-21 of a 28-day cycle.2 She required a dose reduction from 60 mg to 20 mg because of severe rash and fatigue, but the disease progressed rapidly. She was then treated with cytarabine (500 mg/m2 every 12 hours for six doses) and high-dose steroids, but her motor and sensory deficits continued to progress to the point that she was bedbound and feeding tube-dependent.

A variant of unknown significance (VUS), CSF1R in-frame deletion (p.S560_P566del), with a variant allele frequency (VAF) of 31.9%, was reported on her NGS from Tempus. Additionally, she had a PTPN11 alteration (VAF 22.8%) and several other VUS alterations (POT1, DICER1, and CSF1R p.S938_Y969del). The CSF1R p.S560_P566del occurred near the same region of the CSF1R protein as our previously published report of a young woman with CNS-predominant ECD, which was validated in vitro to be an activating mutation.6 In silico modeling showed this was a gain-of-function effect in CSF1R, which we hypothesized to be targetable with the CSF1R inhibitor, pexidartinib, a drug otherwise indicated for tenosynovial giant cell tumors (TGCTs; Figs 1A and 1B).6,16 Given CSF1R may activate both the MAPK and PI3K-AKT pathways in parallel, we performed IHC of phosphorylated ERK (p-ERK) and phosphorylated AKT (p-AKT) to evaluate for activation of these pathways. Tumor cells were negative for p-ERK and positive for p-AKT, suggesting activation of the PI3K-AKT pathway only, consistent with our previous in vitro findings that a similar alteration occurring at the same location of the CSF1R protein preferentially activates PI3K-AKT pathway (Fig 2).6 These results supported the hypothesis of targeting the CSF1R alteration with pexidartinib and an emergent FDA IND approval was submitted to obtain this medication.

After a few weeks of treatment with pexidartinib 400 mg twice a day, the patient's neurologic deficits significantly improved to the point that she was able to eat unassisted and walk with a rolling walker. After 2 months, she was in a clinical complete response (CR) with no significant residual neurologic deficits. Imaging at 3 months showed a partial response (PR; Fig 3).

Three months of pexidartinib: A near-complete response from baseline. Fourteen months of pexidartinib: Progression of disease (arrows). Fifteen months of pexidartinib: Anterior maximum-intensity projection from an F-18 fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography showing hypermetabolic thoracic lymph nodes (arrows). MRI, magnetic resonance imaging.

Fifteen months after therapy initiation, she developed progressive neurologic symptoms, including vision changes and paresthesias. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) demonstrated disease progression throughout the CNS (Fig 3). A subsequent F-18 fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography-computed tomography (PET-CT) scan identified new hypermetabolic thoracic lymphadenopathy (Fig 3). Biopsy of a brain lesion showed nuclear atypia with occasional small aggregates with histiocytes positive for or CD163, CD68, cyclin D1 (dim/partial, highlighting nuclear irregularity), OCT2 (dim), and PU.1, and negative for PDL1 and factor XIIIa. Focal histiocytic aggregates were positive for PD-L1, but this may represent non-neoplastic histiocytes. A thoracic lymph node was biopsied as well, which showed significant atypia, more so than the brain biopsy, consistent with a malignant histiocytosis or histiocytic sarcoma. IHC was strongly positive for PD-L1 (90%), CD163, cyclin D1 (partial), OCT2, factor XIIIa, p53 (strong), and ZBTB46 (rare), and negative for S100, CD1a, and langerin. Repeat NGS from Tempus on the lymph node biopsy found a new TP53 missense variant (VAF 19.2%), changes to the CSF1R alterations, and new VUS alterations (SLX4, POT1, MAGI2, IRS2, ELOC, and TERT). PTPN11 persisted. NGS was attempted on the brain biopsy, but there was insufficient tissue. However, both the brain and the lymph node biopsy were consistent with the pathologic diagnosis of histiocytic malignancy. At time of progression, both original CSF1R variants were lost and a new CSF1R in-frame deletion of c.1686_1697del p.FS63_P566del was identified, which in silico acts like CSF1R WT, in addition to new SLX4, MAGI2, IRS2, ELOC, and TERT alterations (Figs 1C and 1D). Several salvage therapies were attempted, but ultimately she passed away 41 months after diagnosis and 26 months after starting pexidartinib.

Patient 2: KIF5B-FGFR1 Fusion

Patient 2 was diagnosed at age 54 years. She presented 5 years before with diffuse flesh-colored cutaneous papules described as xanthelasma-like lesions. Treatments included isotretinoin, hydroxychloroquine, methotrexate, intravenous immunoglobulin, and thalidomide without improvement. Biopsy showed intermixed xanthomatized histiocytes positive for CD163, and negative for factor XIIIa and BRAFV600E. A PET-CT scan showed mild fludeoxyglucose (18F)-avidity in bilateral lower leg muscles, which was determined to be physiologic. Since the disease involves the skin only, her presentation may be considered ECD limited to the skin or a histiocytic xanthogranuloma disorder (Figs 4A and 4B).

Tumor transcriptomic analysis from Tempus identified a K15FB-FGFR1 fusion (Fig 4C). The only other genomic alteration was a VUS, RET c.2802-4G>T splice region variant. Since the K15FB-FGFR1 fusion is a translocation, there is no alteration of the protein, thus in silico modeling was not performed. Since she did not have a MAPK pathway mutation, a MEKi would not likely be effective. Thus, we hypothesized that this fusion would be activating as it involves the kinase domain, and pemigatinib could target the FGFR fusion. She started on pemigatinib 4.5 mg, taken on days 1-14 of a 21-day cycle. Within a few weeks of treatment, she had a clinical PR in the cutaneous lesions as demonstrated by fewer and smaller lesions. Her response continued to improve at 6 months, but she was still a PR (Fig 4B). Treatment has been tolerated well without significant adverse effects. Given her predominant skin involvement, routine PET-CT imaging was not conducted. At her last follow-up after 24 months of pemigatinib, she remained in a clinical PR.

Patient 3: MEF2C-FLT3 Fusion

Patient 3 was diagnosed at age 35 years. He presented 3 years earlier with symptoms of myalgias, subcutaneous lesions, lower-extremity edema, and anemia. A biopsy of a subcutaneous lesion found foamy histiocytes and was negative for BRAFV600E. A PET-CT scan showed involvement of bilateral femora and tibiae with subcutaneous soft tissue stranding, in addition to mild-to-moderate FDG-avidity of the subcutaneous face, upper arms, posterior neck, shoulder, chest, lower abdomen, pelvis, and distal legs (Fig 5A). Bone marrow biopsy demonstrated 5%-10% involvement of foamy histiocytes. He was diagnosed with ECD, biopsy-proven to involve the subcutaneous tissues and bone marrow. The disease progressed despite treatment with cladribine, hydroxyurea, methotrexate, tocilizumab, and cobimetinib.

NGS via FoundationOne found that a genomic MEF2-FLT3 fusion with the C-terminal of the chimeric protein contains a portion of exon 14 onward of FLT3 protein encompassing its kinase domain, which was hypothesized to be a disease driver and responsive to FLT3 inhibition (Fig 5B). Several VUS alterations were also found, including ALK, BLM, FLT4, KDM4C, MPL, RELN, and WHSCI. Sorafenib was chosen because of its multikinase inhibitor activity, including activity against FLT3. Within 2 months of starting treatment with sorafenib 200 mg daily, he had rapid resolution of subcutaneous edema leading to a weight loss of >15 kg because of the excess fluid, in addition to improvement in constitutional symptoms (fever and pain). Later on in his course, there was a decrease in the size of the subcutaneous nodules, hemoglobin increased from 10 to 14 g/dL (normal 13.2-16.6 g/dL), and PET-CT scan showed a PR in dermal, subdermal, and osseous disease (Fig 5A). His response has continued as of follow-up at 30 months with no clinical signs, symptoms, or laboratory indications of adverse effects. He is monitored every 3 months, and there are no signs of secondary myeloid malignancies, as indicated by normal laboratory evaluations, including the absence of abnormalities in the complete blood count and differential.

DISCUSSION

We present three patients with histiocytic disorders who lacked MAPK-pathway alterations and benefitted from targeting three alterations originally thought to be VUS's with the off-label use of three different kinase inhibitors: pexidartinib, pemigatinib, and sorafenib (Appendix Table A1). To our knowledge, this case series represents the second documented case of a patient with a CSF1R-altered CNS-predominant histiocytic neoplasm where pexidartinib led to a sustained clinical and radiographic response, and the first to report transformation of ECD to a more aggressive histiocytic sarcoma through selection of a pexidartinib-resistant subclone.6 To our knowledge, this is also the first documentation of using pemigatinib for an FGFR fusion and sorafenib for a FLT3 fusion in histiocytic disorders. Patients 1 and 3 were both refractory to MEK inhibition, which is a standard-of-care treatment option in ECD. Although patient 2 was not treated with a MEKi, her clinical team thought the benefit would be low because of lack of MAPK alteration. The antineoplastic activity demonstrated herein further supports the presence of alternative, poorly understood pathogenic mechanisms in ECD other than MAPK pathway alterations.6,8

CSF1R was recently identified as a gene associated with ECD pathogenesis. In 2022, Abeykoon et al6 reported an index case where a patient with CNS-predominant ECD, no MAPK pathway alterations, and a CSF1R in-frame deletion experienced a CR with pexidartinib. This patient had previously progressed on several lines of therapy, including cobimetinib. Clinically, she was significantly disabled because of neurologic symptoms from the CNS involvement. NGS revealed a CSF1R in-frame deletion with a VAF of 27.4%. In silico characterization showed that this led to a gain of function, which could respond to a CSF1R inhibitor, such as pexidartinib. Within 1 month of treatment, clinical benefit was noted, seen as improvements in hyporeflexia and paraparesis, resolution of diplopia, and ability to ambulate with assistance. After 3 months, she was in a radiographic CR on MRI and remained in a CR for 38 months without any dose-limiting toxicities before experiencing disease progression. This is very similar to patient 1 from our case series in that they are both young women with ECD primarily involving the CNS, were bedbound and debilitated due to disease, lacked MAPK pathway alterations, had a CSF1R in-frame deletion leading to a gain of function, and had a remarkable clinical response to pexidartinib. Our patient differs in that she progressed while on pexidartinib after 15 months with loss of the original CSF1R alterations while also gaining new CSF1R alterations in addition to a TP53 alteration, suggesting clonal evolution of the original ECD rather than development of a second malignant process. This conclusion is supported by the presence of same pathologic characteristics (CD163, cyclin D1, and OCT2, and factor XIIIa), PTPN11 E76K somatic variant in both biopsies, and CSF1R alterations.17 CSF1R alterations may be a driver in CNS-predominant ECD and patient 1 exemplifies the largely unknown escape mechanisms of malignant clones within histiocytic disorders. Now that two patients have been identified with a CSF1R in-frame deletion and in silico analyses show this results in a gain of function, CSF1R could be classified as a known alteration, rather than a VUS.

Pexidartinib was initially approved for TGCTs where tumors harbor a translocation between CSF1 (located at 1p13) and COL6A3 (on 2q35), resulting in CSF1 overexpression, which then recruits CSF1R-expressing cells, predominantly macrophages, to the tumor.18 Targeting CSF1R is effective in treating TGCTs as it inhibits the macrophage-/monocyte-driven inflammatory cascade, which is a hallmark of TGCTs. In a minority of cases, a CSF1 translocation is not identified, yet CSF1 is still expressed, suggesting there are additional pathways leading to CSF1 upregulation leading to the development of TGCTs.19 CSF1R is required for differentiation and migration of mature dendritic cell lineages regardless of mutation status and accelerates the generation of monocyte-derived macrophages both in vivo and in vitro.18,20 However, in the setting of isolated CSF1 stimulation without additional costimulatory signaling, macrophage development is polarized toward an M2-like immunosuppressive phenotype.21 Upregulation of CSF1/CSF1R in animal models results in a rapid increase in the precursor populations of tissue macrophages and the expansion of resident tissue macrophage numbers.21 We acknowledge that CSF1R-dependent expansion of histiocytic clones may play a role in disease pathogenesis and progression irrespective of the tumoral genomic landscape.

In addition to using genomic alterations to guide treatments, our study highlights the need to consider transcriptomic evaluation as well since the KIF5B-FGFR1 fusion in patient 2 was identified on RNA, rather than DNA, sequencing. Targetable alterations found in transcriptome analysis are associated with clinical benefit in other advanced cancers.22 Fusion between intracellular tyrosine kinase domains, including those of RET and ALK, and the dimerization domain of KIF5B, have been shown to constitutively activate proliferative signals in other solid neoplasms, and may be found in genomic or transcriptomic evaluations.22-25 Currently, the only data regarding FGFR fusions in histiocytic neoplasms are in multicentric reticulohistiocytosis, in which a KIF5B-FGFR1 fusion was also identified.23 Outside of histiocytic diseases, there is one other case of a KIF5B-FGFR1 fusion reported in a patient with acute B-lymphoblastic leukemia, and this was also identified on RNA sequencing.26

Although there is minimal literature regarding FLT3 alterations in ECD, emerging data suggest a clonal relationship between ECD and myeloid malignancies with 42.5% of patients developing clonal hematopoiesis and nearly 16% developing a myeloid malignancy.27-30 There is one report of a FLT3 mutation in a patient with ECD who subsequently developed AML treated with induction chemotherapy and midostaurin.30 The only other case of FLT3 in histiocytic neoplasms is a case of a histiocytic sarcoma.31 We reviewed the literature for other MEF2C-FLT3 fusions, but did not find another case in any disease.

Some limitations of our study are a small sample size, molecularly diverse disease, and inability to control all confounders. However, these variables are inherent to a retrospective case series of a rare disease. Although larger studies for these novel treatment approaches are needed, this may not be feasible, given the rarity of ECD, especially with non-MAPK pathway alterations. Despite these limitations, our findings highlight that a personalized approach involving both genomic and transcriptomic evaluations is an important option to rationally identify treatments for patients with rare diseases that would otherwise have very limited treatment options. All histiocytic malignancies are noncurable, and around 10%-15% of patients will not have alterations in the MAPK pathway.3 Additionally, 30%-40% of patients with MAPK pathway-altered histiocytic cancers will progress despite being on targeted therapy and treatment discontinuation rates are close to 50%-60%.32,33 These data suggest that almost all patients with histiocytic neoplasms will eventually need additional treatment options.

In conclusion, we report three patients with ECD who were successfully treated with off-label therapies targeted to a non-MAPK pathway VUS alterations found on commercially available NGS platforms. These included a CSF1R in-frame deletion (p.S560_P566del) treated with pexidartinib, a CSF1 inhibitor; a KIF5B-FGFR1 fusion treated with pemigatinib, a FGFR inhibitor; and a MEF2C-FLT3 fusion targeted with sorafenib, a multikinase inhibitor that has activity against FLT3. To our knowledge, this is the second study to document a CSF1R gain-of-function mutation treated with pexidartinib in a histiocytic disorder, and the first to describe subsequent genomic evolution because of pexidartinib resistance. We report the index cases for targeting KIF5B-FGFR1 and MEF2C-FLT3 fusions, one which was found on RNA sequencing. Overall, a personalized treatment approach using both genomic and transcriptomic evaluations can reveal novel treatment targets when standard-of-care options are limited in rare diseases.
