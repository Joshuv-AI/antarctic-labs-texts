Synthesis of enantioenriched atropisomers by biocatalytic deracemization
Authors: Casey B. Roos, S. Luke Schulert, Lara E. Zetzsche, Spencer E. McMinn, Angela E. Cheong, Eunjae Shim, Eugene E. Kwan, Alison R. H. Narayan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The synthesis of enantiopure materials is vital for pharmaceutical and agrochemical industries owing to the inherently chiral nature of biological systems and the fact that two enantiomers can have markedly different biochemical properties1. In particular, enantioselective preparation of atropisomers is of great interest owing to their privileged status as chiral ligands and pharmacophores2–4. Although chromatographic- or crystallization-based methods are commonly used to separate atropisomers, we urgently need more efficient and economical approaches to access enantioenriched atropisomers5,6. The use of stereoconvergent methods to access molecules with point chirality is well established but we have not tapped the potential of stereoconvergent catalytic methods to arrive at enantioenriched atropisomers. Here we report deracemization activity of a P450 enzyme and explore its ability to deliver a stereoconvergent route towards enantioenriched atropisomers. Using a curated set of P450 variants, we found that a wide variety of symmetric and non-symmetrically substituted 2,2′-binaphthol (BINOL) building blocks can be deracemized to high enantiomeric purity. This deracemization activity is mechanistically distinct from the activity of previously reported P450 enzymes, which operate through enantioselective bond formation to afford enantioenriched atropisomers. By contrast, the deracemization process reported here is proposed to proceed through bond rotation. As engineered variants have complementary selectivity profiles and substrate scope, this biocatalytic platform should be readily tunable for any desired substitution pattern. We anticipate that these results will inspire new stereoconvergent approaches to synthesizing conformationally stable atropisomers.

Main

The three-dimensional shape of a molecule dictates its physical properties, reactivity and binding interactions. Absolute configuration matters in biological systems: two enantiomers of a substance can interact differently with biological targets despite identical connectivity and relative stereochemistry1. Stereochemical differences can result in markedly different biological properties from one another. Access to stereochemically pure materials is therefore critical. Atropisomers—an important class of stereoisomers with an axis of chirality owing to restricted rotation around a single bond—are increasingly valuable scaffolds in drug development2,3 (Extended Data Fig. 1). This restricted rotation can improve compound activity by locking a drug into its most active conformation4.

One way to obtain enantioenriched material is to convert an achiral starting material into a chiral product through an enantioselective bond-forming process (Fig. 1a). Despite recent substantial advances in asymmetric catalysis5, there remain cases in which resolution or separation of a racemic product is preferable. For example, efforts to develop an enantioselective Suzuki–Miyaura step in later-generation syntheses of KRAS inhibitor 1 failed to provide an efficient route to a single atropisomer; chromatographic or salt resolution was used instead, limiting theoretical yield to 50% (ref. 6). Alternatively, stereoenrichment of a scalemic mixture—through resolution or a stereoconvergent process—can increase the yield of the desired enantiomer7,8.Fig. 1Stereodynamic reactions of atropisomers.a, Approaches to installing point chirality. b, Comparison of enantiomerization processes for point chiral and atropisomeric compounds. c, State of the art for BINOL deracemization and enzymatic cross-coupling; present work on biocatalytic BINOL deracemization.

Among the types of stereoconvergent process, deracemization reactions convert a racemic mixture of enantiomers into a singular enantiomer of the same substance9. However, practical implementation faces thermodynamic and kinetic challenges, including: (1) a need for a driving force to enrich one enantiomer and (2) an accessible pathway allowing enantiomers to interconvert through a sequence of mechanistically independent steps. The latter is relatively well established for a variety of point-chiral molecules, achieved by a stereoablative bond-breaking step followed by stereoselective bond formation to afford the enriched product8 (Fig. 1b). However, strategies to produce axially chiral atropisomers catalytically are less well explored, as interconversion relies on bond rotation—a conformational change—rather than the bond breaking and reforming sequence described above. Thus, the barrier to rotation dictates whether enantiomers can interconvert at a given temperature (Fig. 1b). For conformationally stable atropisomers such as BINOL, high temperatures are required to overcome the rotational barriers and achieve interconversion of enantiomers10. For certain scaffolds, external stimuli can reduce the barrier to enantiomerization, catalysing dynamic interconversion at much milder temperatures. Examples include light, changes in protonation, redox events or coordinating to metal complexes11. Although these effects have been studied for molecular motors and rotors12, their application to enantioselective, stereodynamic transformations and deracemization processes is limited. For instance, BINOL deracemization processes have been developed but lack a driving force for enantioenrichment, requiring the use of stoichiometric chiral reagents to maintain optical purity under thermodynamic control13,14 (Fig. 1c). Catalytic deracemization using substoichiometric chiral agent in the enantioselective steps have not been reported for atropisomers. (After publication of this work, we realized we had omitted a citation for a recent study15 which reported a biaryl deracemization process accomplished through a two enzyme sequence.)

Here we report a new catalytic, stereoconvergent route to atropisomers based on the newly discovered deracemization activity of a P450 enzyme. Enzymes offer advantages over many small-molecule catalysts because they can interface with the entire shape of the substrate to control both bond-forming events and conformational changes. In previous work, we reported the synthesis of atropoenriched biaryl compounds through intermolecular enantioselective biaryl bond formation, which is consistent with reactivity observed in nature16 (Fig. 1c). Although not known to occur naturally, we proposed that the enzyme active site could also template conformational changes to drive the enantioenrichment of atropisomers by promoting bond rotation rather than bond formation (Fig. 1c). This method offers a biocatalytic approach to atropisomer synthesis that relies on controlled bond rotation over bond formation.

Discovery and proof of principle

As BINOL derivatives are critical in asymmetric catalysis17, we initially aimed to develop a general biocatalytic oxidative coupling to make symmetric and non-symmetric BINOL-type compounds. Drawing on our previous work with fungal P450s to synthesize bicoumarins, we saw potential for the extension of this catalytic platform for the synthesis of atropisomeric BINOLs16. To broaden scope and practicality, we built a small library of reductase-fused bacterial P450s expressible in Escherichia coli and suitable for in vitro reactions16. From this library, we identified a wild-type P450, CYP158A2 (ref. 18) (A2), which produced non-racemic substituted BINOL products, albeit with low yields and poor stereocontrol.

From this starting point, we initiated a directed evolution campaign to improve the yield and enantioselectivity of the cross-coupling (Fig. 2a). In reactions assessing the performance of these A2 variants for atroposelective product formation, we observed that the enantiomeric ratio of the cross-coupled 2 depended on several reaction parameters, notably time. A time-course experiment revealed that the enantiomeric ratio of 2 increases from 61:39 er to 78:22 er over the first hour of the reaction (Fig. 2a). These surprising results indicate that the initial C–C bond formation in the coupling of 3 and 4 may not be solely responsible for the measured enantiomeric ratio of product 2 (ref. 19), implying that a more complicated mechanistic scenario could be in operation. We considered two processes that could account for the increasing enantiomeric ratio over the course of the reaction: kinetic resolution or deracemization. In a kinetic resolution, one enantiomer is consumed selectively, thus, enantioenrichment arises at the expense of yield7. By contrast, in a deracemization process, one enantiomer is converted to the other and can, in principle, reach 100% yield. To distinguish between these two mechanisms, we subjected BINOL (5) as rac-5, (R)-5 or (S)-5 to the reaction conditions and monitored the recovery and enantiomeric ratios of 5 over time (Fig. 2c). Starting from rac-5, the enantiomeric ratio increased from 50:50 er to 90:10 er, with high recovery of the BINOL starting material. Notably, the enantioenrichment of (R)-5 eroded to the same 90:10 ratio. Conducting the same experiment with (S)-5, we observed stereoinversion: increasing formation of (R)-5 until an 89:11 ratio favouring (R)-5 was reached. These results—substantial enantioenrichment combined with high recovery (91–95%)—support a deracemization over a kinetic resolution (see Supplementary Fig. 2). Mechanistic hypothesis

Deracemization is an endergonic process, as converting a two-state racemate to a single enantiomeric state incurs an entropic penalty9,20 (Supplementary Fig. 3). Therefore, a catalytic deracemization requires input of an external energy such as stoichiometric chemical reagent21, light22 or mechanical energy23 to drive the process and overcome the unfavourable free-energy change. Despite the high barrier to rotation around the biaryl bond in BINOL, there are several reports demonstrating that external stimuli—especially oxidizing metal complexes—can induce conformational lability at ambient temperature owing to pyramidalization at the atropisomeric axis11,19,24–27 (Fig. 2b). However, in contrast to our observations, racemization of BINOL derivatives is the common outcome19,26,27. We suggested that, in our system, oxidation of BINOL by the P450 biocatalyst generates a radical intermediate with reduced conformational stability. The chiral environment provided by the P450 enzyme could then be the key to atropoenrichment14,19,28.

To identify which components drive this biocatalytic deracemization, we tested the role of each element (Fig. 3a). Given the established oxidation chemistry of P450s, we proposed that BINOL (5) could be oxidized by a reactive Fe-oxo (or Fe-peroxo) haem species that could initiate the process through hydrogen atom transfer (HAT)29–31 (Fig. 3a). Formation of this reactive intermediate requires the reduction of haem-bound oxygen by an external electron source, commonly a redox partner or reductase32,33 (see Supplementary Fig. 4). Our biocatalytic system16 uses a fused RhFRed reductase domain, which obtains its reducing equivalents from NADPH that is supplied in situ through a cofactor recycling system. Specifically, glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PDH) converts NADP+ to NADPH using glucose-6-phosphate (G6P) as the terminal electron source34. Using the full cocktail of reaction components in our standard conditions with rac-5 (P450-fused reductase, NADPH recycling system, sodium ascorbate and oxygen), we obtained 5 in 93:7 er (Fig. 3b, entry 1). Omitting the A2-RhFRed variant (renamed BinDR1-RhFRed for BINOL deracemase-1) eliminated deracemization activity (Fig. 3b, entry 2); however, removing the fused reductase domain from BinDR1 did not have a negative impact (Fig. 3b, entry 3). Further elimination of the NADP+ cofactor and the NADPH recycling system did not have a notable impact on reactivity (Fig. 3b, entry 4). Although we initially considered that the reductase domain would be key to the oxygen activation mechanism32, these data suggested that generation of peroxide provided an alternative pathway. Inclusion of catalase, a peroxide scavenger, eliminated deracemization reactivity (see Supplementary Fig. 11). Together, these results identify a streamlined set of reaction components—P450, NaAsc and atmospheric oxygen—that bypass the traditional oxygen reduction pathway for P450s.

We then investigated the potential roles of oxygen or peroxide as the oxidant and NaAsc as the reductant powering deracemization. Under anaerobic conditions, P450 and NaAsc alone produced no change in the enantiomeric ratio of rac-5. However, addition of peroxide restored activity in the absence of oxygen (see Supplementary Fig. 5), suggesting that peroxide can serve as the active oxidant. Time-course analysis under ambient atmospheric conditions with BinDR1 and NaAsc gave 88% recovery and 84:16 er after 3 h (Fig. 3c, entry 1). Peroxide alone produced a rapid initial increase in enantiomeric ratio, followed by a plateau after 15 min of reaction time (Fig. 3c, entry 2). The high concentration of peroxide probably led to enzyme deactivation and substantial substrate loss, consistent with insufficient reductant present after the initial substrate oxidation35. Combining NaAsc and peroxide accelerated deracemization (Fig. 3c, entry 3), yet the high concentration of exogenous peroxide still reduced BINOL recovery compared with NaAsc alone. Thus, we focused our efforts on developing conditions using atmospheric oxygen and NaAsc to drive deracemization with the highest substrate recovery.

NaAsc concentration affects the rate and extent of deracemization (Fig. 3d). When incubating BinDR1 with BINOLs 5 and 6, increasing the concentration of NaAsc from 2 to 16 mM accelerated enrichment and increased the final enantiomeric ratio achieved. Beyond 16 mM, the maximum enantiomeric ratio (97:3 for substrate 6) did not improve; higher NaAsc only increased the rate of deracemization to this enantiomeric ratio. At 64 mM NaAsc, the reaction with 6 initially reached 97:3 er but eroded slightly at later time points (Fig. 3d). These results indicate that a concentration window exists in which increasing NaAsc increases the achievable enrichment. Once the maximum enantiomeric ratio is achieved, higher concentrations of NaAsc lead to faster reaction without increasing the final enantiomeric ratio (see Supplementary Fig. 10). These experiments define the minimal components for this chemical-driven deracemization: P450 biocatalyst, oxygen and NaAsc. We propose that oxygen serves as the terminal oxidant and NaAsc as the terminal reductant in this net-redox-neutral process. Reagent compatibility of the substrate, oxidant and reductant is critical (see Supplementary Fig. 6). In the absence of P450, oxidants such as oxygen and hydrogen peroxide react minimally with NaAsc36,37. This underscores an advantage of biocatalytic deracemization: the enzyme generates and localizes a reactive species, enabling redox-neutral deracemization in a single vessel despite the presence of both oxidant and reductant reagents11.

Having identified O2 and NaAsc as the terminal oxidant and reductant that drive the uphill deracemization, we considered how (S)-5 is net converted to (R)-5 through mechanistically independent steps for oxidation and reduction (see Supplementary Fig. 7). We propose a cyclic deracemization in which an oxidizing P450 species generates a stereolabile phenoxy radical intermediate (I-5)30,31 (Fig. 4b). Both literature reports and our calculations indicate that BINOL radical species have a substantially decreased barrier of rotation about the chiral axis14,19. The computed transition state energy for the neutral radical I-5 is 25.5 kcal mol−1, comparable with values reported for related radical cations28 and consistent with accessible interconversion at ambient temperatures (see Supplementary Fig. 12). Binding interactions between this intermediate and the enzyme active site could further reduce this barrier. Reduction of the radical intermediate (I-5) through HAT or proton-coupled electron transfer (PCET) would then regenerate closed-shell BINOL 5, restoring the high rotational barrier and rigidifying the chiral axis in the favoured conformation. Possible reductants include direct reduction with NaAsc38,39, self-exchange with the phenol of another substrate molecule or enzyme-mediated PCET with a redox-active residue38. The large magnitude of the rate constant for phenol self-exchange is ascribed to the formation of a pre-PCET hydrogen bonding complex, which may be constrained in the active site40. Productive closure of the catalytic cycle probably involves a second HAT step by Cpd II to regenerate the resting ferric haem41. Unproductive pathways could lead to rebound hydroxylation and overoxidation of the substrate or enzyme inactivation oxidation of a proximal residue42. For a cyclic deracemization process to produce enrichment, at least one of the two mechanistic steps must be stereoselective. Although we cannot exclude a reductive step occurring within the active site, there is no evidence that ascorbate, a chiral molecule, influences the enantioselectivity, as the same enantiomeric ratio is achieved in the absence of sodium ascorbate (compare Fig. 2c with Fig. 3b, entry 4). By contrast, the oxidation event is anticipated to occur in the chiral enzyme active site and provide the necessary selectivity to achieve deracemization. Substrate scope

We evaluated the synthetic utility of this P450-mediated deracemization across substituted BINOLS using four engineered variants (BinDR1 to BinDR4; Supplementary Fig. 8) chosen to provide complementary selectivity across a series of substituted BINOL derivatives (Fig. 5). Amino acid substitutions that distinguish BinDR1 to BinDR4 from the wild-type enzyme are described in Fig. 5b. Racemic substrates were subjected to each P450-RhFRed in the presence of NaAsc. These reactions were then analysed for enantiomeric ratio (Fig. 5c) and recovered yield (Fig. 5d).

Under the conditions described in Fig. 5a, BinDR1 enriched substrate 5 to 88:12 er with 84% recovery. This result was reproduced on the preparative scale (88:12 er, 79% isolated yield). We surveyed substituted BINOL—common catalysts or ligands43,44 or building blocks for enantioselective sensors and chiral materials45—to map yield and selectivity trends. Although most BINOL-based catalysts are C2-symmetric, non-symmetric BINOL derivatives are promising scaffolds, yet less available46. As this reaction was discovered during an engineering campaign targeting an enantioselective cross-coupling reaction to access unsymmetrical 3-substituted BINOLs, we first assessed selectivity with variation at the 3-position.

Variants BinDR1 and BinDR2 exhibited consistently high selectivity for substrates 6–9, (BinDR1, 94:6 to 98:2 er). However, 3-phenyl substitution (see 10) gave lower recovery and enantioselectivity across the panel (BinDR1, 77% yield, 8:92 er). Furthermore, the low aqueous solubility of these substrates required higher loading of DMSO co-solvent to observe deracemization (see Supplementary Fig. 9). BinDR3 showed a distinct selectivity profile: excellent selectivity for 3-halogenated substrates (6–8) but substantially diminished enantioenrichment for 3-methoxy and 3-phenyl derivatives (9 and 10). BinDR4 was the least selective variant for these monosubstituted BINOLs.

We then examined symmetric 3,3′-substituted BINOLs to investigate steric effects of positions commonly modified in catalyst development17. Generally, for 3,3′-dihalogenated BINOLs (11–13), enantioenrichment decreased with increasing halogen size, reversing the trend observed for monosubstituted BINOL derivatives (6–10). BinDR4 exhibited the greatest amount of enantioenrichment, although still modest, for substrates 11–13. For example, 12 was enriched to 43:57 er with BinDR1, whereas reactions with BinDR4 provided 12 in 28:72 er. Although reactions of 3-iodo-BINOL (8) and 3-methoxy-BINOL (9) afforded material in moderate to high enantioenrichment with each variant, reactions with substrates 13 and 14, which have an added substituent at the 3′-position, abolished enrichment for all four variants tested.

We then investigated C2-symmetric BINOLs (15–22), readily accessible racemically through oxidative coupling reactions47 but often difficult to synthesize from enantiopure commercially available parent BINOL, particularly with substituents at the 5,5′- and 7,7′-positions43. Variants BinDR1 to BinDR3 showed low selectivity for 5,5′- and 6,6′-substituted BINOLs (15–17; BinDR1: 58:42–66:34 er), whereas BinDR4 delivered markedly higher selectivity (92:8–95:5 er), illustrating complementary selectivity profiles of these variants. An exception was the 6,6′-difluoro substrate 18, for which BinDR1 and BinDR2 gave the highest selectivity (BinDR1: 91:9 er; BINDR2: 92:8 er). For 7,7′-substituted BINOLs (19–21), selectivity ranged from poor to moderate (66:34–89:11 er) but the 7,7′-difluoro 22 showed uniformly high enrichment across all variants (93:7–97:3 er). These outcomes indicate that enantioselectivity depends on both substrate substitution and enzyme variant and that the deracemization can be tailored to particular biaryls by choosing or engineering an appropriate P450 catalyst.

The four ‘generalist’ BinDR variants showed broad activity and selectivity but were not optimal for every substrate, as 12, 13, 20 and 21 were only poorly enriched by the four P450s tested. Noting that high enantioenrichment improved over rounds of evolution (Fig. 5e). We tested for variants better suited to these challenging substrates. An initial lysate screen of 92 variants against substates 12 and 21 provided candidates with improved enrichment versus BinDR1 to BinDR4 and purified enzyme assays confirmed further gains. For 7,7′-dimethoxy-BINOL (21), a new variant BinDR5 delivered 79:21 er—a 13% improvement over BinDR1. BinDR5 shares four mutations with BinDR1 (P45E, A83L, S181G and A245Q) but differs at four positions (BinDR1: R90Q, A290M; BinDR5: V74L, H183K). For 3,3′-dibromo-BINOL (12), BinDR6 improved enrichment from 28:72 er (BinDR4) to 18:82 er; this gain arises from reverting two mutations present in BinDR4 (S181G, H183K) while retaining the other substitutions. These findings demonstrate that the BinDR scaffold can be tuned to create specialist enzymes that markedly improve deracemization for targeted substrates.

Comparison of enzymes

We next examined whether deracemization activity is present in wild-type P450s known to catalyse oxidative coupling and whether the wild-type enzyme shows substrate-dependent preferences similar to engineered BinDR variants. Focusing on substrates 5 and 6, we ran time-course reactions with wild-type enzyme using (R)-, (S)- and racemic starting materials (Extended Data Fig. 2a). With enantioenriched starting materials, the wild-type enzyme caused low to moderate erosion of enantiopurity, indicating that it can facilitate enantiomerization. With racemic substrates, we detected modest but measurable enantioenrichment, most notably for 6 (59:41 er), showing that the wild-type enzyme has weak deracemization activity. These findings imply that the native enzymes already sample the relevant chemistry and that selecting variants based on enantioselectivity can substantially amplify this latent activity over the course of a directed evolution campaign.

We also tested whether deracemization is a general feature of coupling P450s by evaluating the fungal dimerase KtnC, which converts the coumarin monomer 23 to bicoumarin 24 in 95% yield and >99:1 er (refs. 16,48). Saccharomyces cerevisiae expressing wild-type KtnC was incubated with either monomer 23 or racemic 24 and product quantity and enantiomeric ratio were tracked over 2 days (Extended Data Fig. 2b). The dimerization of 23 gave 24 with constant enantiomeric ratio (>99:1) over the course of the reaction. Conversely, subjecting rac-bicoumarin 24 to the same conditions did not produce detectable enantioenrichment. These results indicate that the deracemization activity observed with CYP158A2 and its variants is not a general function for all P450s that mediate oxidative coupling.

We then asked whether bacterial P450s more closely related to CYP158A2 than the fungal P450 KtnC have deracemization activity. A panel of wild-type bacterial P450s was tested for their ability (1) to dimerize 4 to give 5 and (2) facilitate enantiomerization of (S)-5. Many of the enzymes profiled dimerized 4 to 5 with varying degrees of enrichment (Extended Data Fig. 2c, left). However, when incubated with (S)-5 to test for enantiomerization, only CYP158A2 and two other enzymes (boxed in Extended Data Fig. 2c, right) showed any enantiomerization to the (R)-enantiomer. These results indicate that deracemization is a distinct mechanism from enantioselective C–C bond formation: some P450s can give enantioenriched products by selective bond formation, yet only a subset can mediate the interconversion-based deracemization we describe.

To conclude, we discovered and developed a biocatalytic deracemization strategy for enantioenriching BINOL derivatives. This serendipitous finding offers a complementary, stereoconvergent route to atropisomers that relies on controlled bond rotation rather than bond formation, and it is effective across a range of BINOL scaffolds. Notably, the modest deracemization activity observed in the wild-type enzyme could be substantially improved by protein engineering, demonstrating that this latent function can be amplified and tuned for specific substrates. More broadly, the work establishes a proof of concept for enzymatic control of stereodynamic processes and highlights the promise of new biocatalytic approaches for control over stereochemistry beyond conventional asymmetric carbon centres.
