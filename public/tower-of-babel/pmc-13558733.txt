The emergence and diversification of the DUX gene family across placental mammals
Authors: Alberto Budillon, Richard J. L. F. Lemmers, Stephen J. Tapscott, Silvère M. van der Maarel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The DUX gene family encodes transcription factors with paired homeodomains. It has critical roles in embryogenesis and disease, including facioscapulohumeral muscular dystrophy (FSHD) and cancer. This study conducts a comparative analysis of the DUX gene family—DUXA, DUXB (including DUXBL), and DUXC (including DUX4 and Dux)—across placental mammals, highlighting their structural diversity within macrosatellite repeat contexts. Using long-read genomes, we explore gene distribution, array patterns, and phylogenetic relationships in various vertebrate species. Our analysis reveals that DUXA and DUXB are highly conserved, with intriguing variations such as intronless forms likely arising from ancestral retrotransposition events. While DUXBL is inconsistently retained across clades, its locus—which in non-placental mammals harbors the ancestral single-homeodomain sDUX gene—served as an evolutionary hub for diversification, giving rise to DUXA, DUXB and DUXC, as well as macrosatellite tandem array structures. Sequence conservation and syntenic analyses demonstrate array adaptability, exemplified by higher-order repeats in orangutans and disrupted patterns of concerted evolution in elephants. Furthermore, analysis of human pseudo-DUX4 arrays indicates their potential role in disease mechanisms, including as possible contributors to rare cases of FSHD, warranting further investigation. This study thus provides insights into DUX-family gene evolution, offering a foundation for future research into developmental roles and disease implications.

Introduction

Double homeobox (DUX) genes encode a group of unconventional transcription factors found exclusively in placental mammals. They are unconventional in having two closely positioned homeodomains (HD)1–3. These conserved 60-amino-acid HDs serve as ancient DNA-binding motifs critical for the regulation of developmental processes4,5. Among the DUX gene family, DUX4 has emerged as a pivotal player in both developmental biology and genetic (facioscapulohumeral muscular dystrophy - FSHD)6–8 and acquired (cancer) diseases9–11.

The DUX gene family comprises three major clades: DUXA, DUXB (including DUXBL), and DUXC, which includes DUX4 and Dux, respectively, representing the human and mouse copy of DUXC by protein similarity3. This gene family includes both intronless (DUX4 and Dux) and intron-containing homologues (DUXA, DUXB, Duxbl and DUXC)3. Orthologues of DUXA and DUXB have been found in diverse placental species including primates while prior studies identified Duxbl unique to rodents3. Yet some non-functional Duxbl orthologues have been detected in other species3. Some DUXC members (DUX4, Dux, DUXC) have previously been found in a tandem array organization where multiple copies of the gene are positioned at equidistant intervals. Since each gene copy is embedded in a single macrosatellite unit, the number of units corresponds to the number of gene copies. DUX4 (found in Primates and Afrotheria) and Dux (in Glires) are intronless genes, each characterized by two HDs and a CTD, and are often found in arrays. The phylogenetic chart illustrates the broad evolutionary relationships among major mammalian clades43.

Phylogenetic and syntenic analyses suggest that this gene family arose from a common ancestor: intron-containing sDUX (single-homeobox gene) which has been found in genomes of non-placental animals and may have created the double homeobox gene structure through a gene-duplication event3. While intron-containing members are encoded by single- or multi-copy genes, intronless DUX4 and Dux are retrogenes located within macrosatellite repeat arrays in humans and mice, respectively13. These retrogenes are in fact not intronless but the open reading frame is encoded by a single exon, characteristic of retrogenes, while having acquired extra non-coding exons.

Despite being retrogenes, both DUX4 and Dux play a role in zygotic genome activation during early embryogenesis, with human DUX4 being active at the four-cell stage and mouse Dux at the two-cell stage5,14,15. Shortly after this critical period, DUX4 and Dux are rapidly silenced and remain repressed in most tissues for the rest of an individual’s life. However, in FSHD patients, incomplete repression of DUX4 in skeletal muscles triggers complex pathological pathways, ultimately leading to muscle cell death7,8. In cancer, DUX4 is implicated in cancer stemness10,16, immune evasion and resistance to checkpoint blockade9,11. DUX4 can also be observed as a fusion gene in cancer, such as the CIC-DUX4 fusion in sarcomas17–19 and IGH-DUX4 fusion in acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL)20,21. In all these situations, DUX4 contributes to oncogenesis through dysregulated transcriptional programs.

The genetic hallmark in most FSHD patients is a contraction of the 3.3 kb D4Z4 macrosatellite repeat array located at subtelomeric chromosome 4q3522. Each unit of this array contains a copy of the DUX4 retrogene. In healthy individuals, this region contains 8 to >100 D4Z4 units, whereas in the most common dominantly inherited form of FSHD, it is reduced to 1–10 units (FSHD type 1; FSHD1)22. A D4Z4 repeat array contraction is accompanied by a partial relaxation of the local chromatin structure, leading to the de-repression of DUX4 and the sporadic presence of DUX4 in myonuclei23,24. De-repression of DUX4 in FSHD muscle can also be the result of pathogenic variants in D4Z4 chromatin modifiers, most often SMCHD1 (FSHD type 2; FSHD2)25.

Each copy of the DUX4 retrogene in the D4Z4 repeat is incomplete as it lacks a somatic polyadenylation signal (PAS)26. In FSHD pathogenesis, the DUX4 retrogene of the most distal repeat unit can make use of a polyadenylation signal (PAS) encoded by a 3‘ UTR exon in the region immediately distal to the D4Z4 array to facilitate stable DUX4 expression in skeletal muscle22,23,26. The 4q subtelomere has undergone several rearrangements and translocations during recent evolution, which has resulted in an almost identical D4Z4 repeat array on chromosome 10q and an alternative sequence immediately distal to the D4Z4 repeat array on chromosome 4 (haplotype 4qB). Based on a worldwide analysis of the D4Z4 repeat array, the chromosome 4 carrying the DUX4 gene in the most distal unit (haplotype 4qA) was identified as the ancestral location27. Chromosome 10, and 4qB chromosomes lack this somatic PAS explaining the absence of DUX4 expression upon D4Z4 contraction on these chromosomal backgrounds and making them non-permissive to FSHD22.

Other related D4Z4-like structures have been observed in humans. These are typically located in pericentromeric regions and have only been partially studied28. From a cDNA homologous to 3.3 kb dispersed repeated D4Z4-like elements, a 170 amino acid protein (DUX1) with two paired-type homeodomains has been characterized29 and DUX4-like transcripts from the pericentromeric region of chromosome 22 were detected in some cancer cell lines10, suggesting the presence of other transcribed genes embedded within dispersed D4Z4-like units.

Apart from humans, mice also present with a tandem array organization comprised of repeat units carrying the Dux retrogene16, and similar structures containing DUX4, Dux or DUXC sequences are also reported in other species30,31. For instance, DUX4 has been encountered in Afrotheria as well as in primates, and DUXC has been described in both Laurasiatheria and Xenarthra. In contrast to DUXA and DUXB, the DUXC homologues incorporate a well-conserved nearly 55 amino acid-long C-terminal transactivation domain (CTD)3 playing crucial roles in development, FSHD and cancer32.

Although great progress has been made in understanding the DUX gene family, we still know little about its dispersion in placental animals, its genetic structure and the tissue and developmental expression patterns of members of the DUX gene family. Notably, repeat array structures are challenging to study because of their repetitive nature, and in this case, their large size. Making use of a growing body of long-read next generation sequencing-based reference genomes of placental and non-placental animal species33–35 (Supplementary Fig. 1), in this study we revisited the evolution of the DUX gene family including members that are organized as macrosatellite repeats.

Our rationale is that studying the evolutionary diversification of DUX genes provides insight into which features of the gene family are deeply conserved and which arose more recently. This distinction is critical, as conserved elements are more likely to underpin essential functions, whereas lineage-specific innovations may contribute to help explain why, in humans, DUX4 has retained activity after its evolution and how it contributes to disease.

Results

For our study, we used the DUX family definitions based on a previous study3 (Fig. 1). We developed a bioinformatic pipeline (Supplementary Fig. 2) to analyze available mammalian long read reference genomes for the evolution of the DUX gene family based on protein similarities and repeat structure.

DUXA, DUXB and DUXBL

First, our efforts focused on homologues DUXA, DUXB and DUXBL, forming the group of DUX4 (with Dux and DUXC) paralogues, meaning that they have a shared common ancestry, but they all encode for different proteins. Our results match the wide distribution of DUXA and DUXB copies across diverse mammal groups already described in literature3 and expand on what little was reported about DUXBL, until now thought to be exclusive to the Glires (see below). As expected, no double homeodomain genes related to DUX family were found in non-placental animals3.

DUXA and DUXB were identified in almost all primates examined, except for DUXB in marmoset. In orangutan, marmoset, and loris, multiple copies of DUXA were found, with some of them having intronless ORFs (see below). In macaque, in addition to DUXA (chr19) and DUXB (chr20), the bioinformatic pipeline we used predicted a smaller copy of DUXBL (chr9) with two intact HDs in a region that likely marks an evolutionary breakpoint (Figs. 2–4). In other primates Duxbl has evolved into a pseudogene3 (Fig. 3). Outside primates, DUXA and DUXB are generally conserved but show strong lineage-specific variation, with frequent DUXA duplications and intronless copies (notably in sea lion and Xenarthra), consistent with recurrent retrotransposition (Figs. 2, 4). DUXBL persists mainly in rodents, while Afrotheria retain DUX genes only in elephants, although apparent absences may reflect technical limitations rather than true loss.

Most of the DUXA loci found are composed of 6 exons, while only a few of them are intronless. Nonetheless, intronless forms may either serve a functional role or, as retrogenes, provide evidence that DUXA is present and actively transcribed. For example, orangutan shows a 6 exon DUXA on chromosome 20 syntenic to human DUXA located on chromosome 19 (anchor gene being USP29) and an intronless copy on chromosome 17 with the same predicted size of 204 amino acids and a sequence identity and similarity of 98% and 99%, respectively. Moreover, the 5’ and 3’ UTRs are even more conserved with a 100% similarity sharing even the polyadenylation signal (PAS) – AATAAA – which is also present in human DUXA. Browsing the UCSC genome website, we retrieved RefSeq data and found the NM_001012729.2 transcript to be identical to the intron-containing copy in orangutan on chromosome 20, but, despite its similarity, we did not find evidence of active transcription from the intronless copy36.

Previously, DUXBL was thought to exist exclusively in Glires, where it plays a pivotal role in activating developmental genes37. However, while the Leidenroth study reported the presence of a DUXBL pseudogene in macaque (Macaca mulatta)3, our study did identify a potentially functional copy of DUXBL in Macaca nemestrina (Supplementary Fig. 3). Also, by leveraging syntenic information, we found evidence for functional copies of DUXBL in two additional mammalian groups: Xenarthra and Afrotheria. In all those species DUXBL is located adjacent to the anchor gene TMEM254, and in sloth an additional intronless form of DUXBL was found on chromosome 9. Interestingly, in Afrotheria, an inversion appears to span both TMEM254 and DUXBL, altering the canonical syntenic arrangement with ANXA11 and PLAC9 (Fig. 3). Thus, there seems to be no mutually exclusive relationship between DUXB and DUXBL as was suggested in a previous study3.

DUX4, DUXC and Dux Arrays

Previous studies have documented the widespread distribution of DUX4 and its orthologues, Dux and DUXC, across diverse animal taxa12. Building on this information, we not only aimed to corroborate these findings but also to extend current knowledge by providing novel insights into their genomic positioning and array architecture, enabled by long-read sequencing technologies. Some of the most relevant findings are shown below.

Among our selected samples, we were able to collect predicted DUX4, DUXC, and Dux sequences from long read sequence data of 30 different species for alignment. We next derived the sequence of the individual units in the repeat arrays in which most of these genes are embedded, and we calculated the average pairwise distance to better understand the degree of homogeneity between the units (see Methods section).

Using human DUX4 as reference, we confirmed the high conservation of the two DNA-binding domains and the CTD. Additionally, our data based on DUX4, DUXC and Dux sequences reveal conservation of a proline-rich amino acid sequence, including a PPPQPA motif at positions 331–336, and two smaller conserved motifs of only three amino acids each, located between positions 340 and 360 (Supplementary Fig. 4). dN/dS analysis, i.e. the ratio of non-synonymous to synonymous substitutions, revealed that most codons in DUX4 exhibit a dN/dS ratio greater than 1, suggesting a general trend of positive selection (Supplementary Fig. 5). However, only a subset of these codons showed statistically significant evidence of positive selection (ω > 1, p < 0.05), indicating strong support for adaptive changes at specific sites. Those codons match with amino acid 43, 69, 242 and 333 of human DUX4 protein. The first two are embedded in the HD1, while the others are located in less conserved regions (outside both the HD and CTD); remarkably, the proline in position 333 aligns with the previously identified PPPQPA conserved motif further supporting the presence of a new small conserved sequence suggested by the alignment. This finding, together with evidence of positive selection at sites within the homeodomains, is consistent with adaptive changes in DNA-binding specificity, while the conserved motif in the C-terminal region may indicate an additional regulatory role. Notably, some regions with a dN/dS ratio very low or exactly 0 in selection analysis corresponded to highly conserved domains (HDs and CTD), likely reflecting structural or functional constraints (purifying selection).

In addition to the previously reported DUXC identified in dolphins, cows, horses, dogs, bats, and armadillos, we found evidence of DUXC in hippopotamus, pig, dromedary, rhinoceros, tapir, pangolin, tamandua, and sloth, all belonging to the Laurasiatherian and Xenarthra clades (Figs. 2, 4). We identified a three-unit DUXC repeat array in dromedary on chromosome 17, and a single nearly identical copy on chromosome 33. In rhinoceros, different repeat arrays ranging from 7 to 14 copies were found on four different chromosomes. In hippopotamus, we identified 72 copies of DUXC, forming a single nearly 600 kb large repeat array on chromosome 8 (Fig. 5). Given that DUXC consists of 5 exons (Fig. 1), the average unit size (start codon to start codon) is larger compared to the intronless DUX4 or Dux repeat arrays (3.3 kb and 4.9 kb, respectively), ranging from 8.1 kb in hippopotamus to 10.5 kb in horse.

Interestingly, in pigs DUXC copies are not contained within a repeat array but located immediately adjacent to an array of variable unit numbers of 3.8 kb each, not encoding for a DUX gene (Fig. 5). All eight copies found are confined within a 3.5 Mb region on chromosome 10. Although these genes do not form a traditional cluster, their unique localization within a single region, without dispersing to other chromosomes, is noteworthy.

In Xenarthra, all identified DUXC copies are clustered within a region bordered by the anchor genes ANXA11 and SFTPD, which is the DUXBL region. For instance, in sloth, five distinct DUXC copies were identified adjacent to DUXBL, in varying orientations (Fig. 3). Additionally, numerous partial sequences highly similar to DUXC were manually detected within the same region. However, since these sequences are incomplete, they were excluded by our gene prediction pipeline. A comparable situation was noted in the armadillo and tamandua, where fewer DUXC copies were present, and the regions were considerably more extensive.

Among eight different Glires species examined, we identified the presence of Dux in half of them (jerboa, snow vole, mouse, rat) (Fig. 4). In snow vole and jerboa, we found multiple copies of Dux embedded in small arrays, even though in jerboa we could not conclusively characterize the array structure due to its localization in a scaffold region of the assembly. While analyzing the mouse arrays, we encountered two adjacent clusters of Dux arrays on chromosome 10 of three and eighteen repeat units, respectively. This finding contrasts with previous work16, where we identified three adjacent clusters (of 10, 18 and 3 repeat units, respectively) using PacBio long-read sequencing. To resolve this discrepancy, we expanded our analysis to two additional mouse assemblies, both recently released (C57BL_6J-T2T_v1 and CAST_EiJ-T2T_v1). The comparison (Supplementary Fig. 6) revealed that the Dux locus in mice exhibits considerable heterogeneity among different strains. For example, the long-read genome of C57BL matches our previous findings (10, 18, 3-unit clusters) accurately, while the CAST strain, native to southeastern Asian house mice, displays a markedly different and complex pattern (clusters of 9, 5, 2, 6, and 22 units). Subsequently, we also analyzed the Dux array by Southern blotting in eight different mouse strains and found the 10-18-3 array organization in seven strains and another combination (10 and 3 units) in one strain (Supplementary Fig. 6). This shows that the reference mouse genome sequence (mMusMus1.1 with adjacent arrays of 18 and 3 units) is not representative for all strains. In rats, multiple copies of Dux are embedded in a region of approximately 800 kb on chromosome 20, forming an atypical array structure on both the positive and negative strand (Figs. 4, 5).

Every animal selected belonging to Afrotheria showed an array pattern of DUX4. For instance, dugong and hyrax have a repeat array of 77 and 33 complete units, respectively (Fig. 4). Because of an earlier study including elephant DUX412, we decided to include both Asian and African elephant species in our analysis. Each repeat unit belonging to elephants has a length of ~5.8 kb. Interestingly, in addition to two different canonical arrays located in syntenic regions of both species (anchor genes NECTIN3 and SPECC1, respectively), we identified an Asian elephant-specific sub-telomeric array on chromosome 14 comprising of 78 repeats. Because of its low homogeneity score (see below) we decided to examine this locus in detail. Surprisingly, within this array, we identified a proximal subarray (units 1-44) encoding a 469 amino acid long intronless DUX4 ORF and a distal sub-array (units 46-78) hosting an intronless DUX4 ORF of 432 amino acids. Although being extremely similar (97%), those two predicted genes mainly differ because in the former a single nucleotide change – TGA (STOP) > TGG (Trp) – resulted in an ORF extension of 39 amino acids. These subarrays are separated by a 10 kb large Long Interspersed Nuclear Element-1 (LINE-1) retrotransposon belonging to the L1M family, L1MB4 (Fig. 5). Interestingly, even while the individual units are highly similar between both arrays, there is more homology within each subarray than between the subarrays.

Flying lemur, the sole representative of the Dermoptera order and closely related yet distinct from primates, has a DUX4 array of 80 complete units of ~4.3 kb each.

In primates and other related species, we found at least one DUX4 copy in each species except for lemur. Loris is showing 7 copies of DUX4 on two different chromosomes and macaque is showing two arrays of 16 and 36 units on chromosome 1 and chromosome 6, respectively, both of ~6.5 kb. A single array of 8 repeats was found in marmoset while the gibbon assembly hosts five regions where DUX4 is organized in a tandem repeat array structure with unit size of ~3.1 kb or ~4.5 kb, both encoding for a complete DUX4 ORF of 364 and 369 amino acids, respectively (Fig. 5).

In humans there is less heterogeneity within the DUX4 ORF than the entire D4Z4 unit suggesting conservation of the ORF26. By calculating the average pairwise distance we found that the human D4Z4 repeat array on chromosome 4 is less homogeneous than the chromosome 10 one, with scores of 0.171 and 0.120, respectively. Similarly low scores (below 0.4) were observed in primates, with the most homogeneous arrays found in gorilla and chimpanzee chromosome 2, scoring 0.037 and 0.045, respectively.

In contrast, orangutans exhibited more heterogeneous arrays, with scores ranging from 9.736 to 18.268. More detailed analysis showed that the units forming the arrays in the orangutan are either 3.3 kb, similar to units in other primates, or 3.5 kb, not encoding for a complete DUX4, and that they are often alternating (Figs. 4,5). Thus, we performed the homogeneity analysis separately on the different types of units (3.3 kb and 3.5 kb) revealing that both are equally homogeneous with scores ranging from 0.418 for the 3.3 kb repeat units to 0.182 for the 3.5 kb units, which is comparable with the uniform length unit array scores in other primate species. This suggests that the array homogenization takes into account the establishment of a higher-order repeat unit of ~6.8 kb made of the two distinct units combined38,39. This structure has been identified in every orangutan cluster except those on chromosomes 2 and 11 which are homogenous arrays of 3.3 kb units.

Our intra-cluster variability analysis revealed that, among all species studied, primates exhibit low pairwise distance scores, indicating that their arrays are likely to be highly homogeneous. Additionally, primates display significant variability in the number of repeat units (though our estimates serve as a proxy due to inter-individual heterogeneity) and have the shortest average unit lengths, probably due to the intronless appearance of the DUX4 gene. Across all species studied, no correlation appears to exist between the similarity score, the number of repeat units, and the average unit length, suggesting that these variables are independent (Supplementary Fig. 7).

Great Apes DUX4 and D4Z4 Comparison

Human DUX4 is embedded in the subtelomeric 3.3 kb D4Z4 macrosatellite repeat array on chromosome 4q35, which is duplicated to the 10q subtelomere. The number of D4Z4 repeat units forming this array on chromosome 4q35 is crucial in determining the FSHD status. In other animal species, multiple copies of DUX4, Dux and DUXC are also arrayed in macrosatellite repeat structures, but a direct comparison with D4Z4 is difficult due to substantial differences in length and composition of the repeat unit in which the DUX locus is embedded. We therefore studied the evolutionary relationship of the D4Z4 repeat array containing 4q subtelomere in the most related species to humans, namely the great apes.

All great apes investigated show complex array organizations of multiple DUX4 ORFs within what are now termed D4Z4-like arrays, each with a repeat unit length of approximately 3.3 kb. Human T2T encompasses 33 complete repeat units in both chromosome 4 and 10 arrays. Notably, the 4q35 locus hosts DUX4c, an incomplete inverted copy of DUX4, FRG1 and FRG2 together with FAT1 and SLC25A4 all proximal to the D4Z4 repeat array40,41. We cross referenced this ancestral DUX4 region in humans within great apes using the proximal human genes as anchor (Fig. 6). The gorilla chromosome 3 locus might have been rearranged over time as we could not find FRG2 even though the distance between the array and FRG1 is consistent compared to that in related species. The bonobo chromosome 3 array keeps all the characteristics of the human 4q35 locus, yet in terms of organization 61 repeat units are split into 5 sub-clusters having alternating strand orientations. Similarly, the orangutan chromosome 3 region matches with the human 4q35 region albeit its 39 repeat units’ array is heterogeneous (see above). All these DUX4 loci are sub-telomeric although with significant variable distance from the telomere. Surprisingly, the corresponding region in chimpanzee located on chromosome 3 lacks an array, although a single truncated DUX4 pseudogene was found. In the chimpanzee genome, we identified the sole non-4q35-syntenic DUX4 array, containing 80 repeat units, on chromosome 2. The bonobo genome includes an additional, single-array DUX4 cluster of 47 units on chromosome 2, while the gorilla assembly shows an extra array of 34 units on chromosome 2. The gene prediction tool we employed encountered challenges in detecting the inverted repeat DUX4c ORF near FRG2, often merging the two into a single prediction due to their close proximity. To address this issue, we manually examined the region in the UCSC browser and discovered significant variability across different apes. While the repeat unit is consistently inverted relative to the array, its presence is not uniform: being present or absent, and in some cases, incomplete as it lacks the CTD (Fig. 6). This variability underscores the evolutionary divergence and structural instability of this genomic region across different species, making it difficult for automated tools to generate accurate predictions.

DUX4 Homologues in the Human Genome

Finally, we sought to identify additional dispersed D4Z4 and DUX4 sequences within the human genome, given their potential relevance to FSHD. This is particularly important as not all individuals diagnosed with FSHD exhibit the canonical genetic and epigenetic alterations at the D4Z4 locus on chromosome 4q35.

Using our pipeline to retrieve DUX genes with two intact homeodomains, we examined every potential DUX4 homologue in the human T2T assembly. Among the predicted genes listed, most were located at the known D4Z4 array sites on chromosomes 4q35 and 10q26. Through a phylogenetic analysis, we identified DUXA and DUXB as the only genes that aggregate far away from the array clusters.

The most complete DUX4 ORF prediction we identified was a 389-amino-acid intronless DUX gene located on chromosome 21 (chr21:6177686-6178855) (marked with red box in Supplementary Fig. 8), also showing a partial sequence match to the CTD, with 78% identity and 81% similarity to DUX4. A single nucleotide substitution creates a stop codon that truncates the CTD and shortens the protein. However, the subsequent nucleotide sequence that could potentially translate to the remaining 35 amino acids of the CTD remains intact.

Following the same approach, we also found DUX1 on chromosome 21 (chr21:1,045,474-1,044,962) (marked with blue box in Supplementary Fig. 8). It is a 170 amino acids large DUX4 homologue reported to be expressed in skeletal muscle and in heart26. Also in the case of DUX1 the nucleotide sequence following its stop codon (after a frameshift) matches the DUX4 nucleotide sequence with 87% identity (1106/1275) underscoring the strong structural relationship between them. As expected, both HDs are very similar to those of DUX4 while the CTD is also very conserved, though not transcribed in DUX1.

All the other predicted DUX4 homologous ORF sequences were intronless, with lengths ranging from 170 to 389 amino acids, and they lack a complete CTD. They are all located on acrocentric chromosomes 13, 14, 15, 21, and 22 and are embedded in several pseudo-D4Z4 structures which are non-contiguous and partial D4Z4 units which also appear to be 0.6 kb shorter than the canonical D4Z4 (Supplementary Fig. 8). Those structures also host several other interrupted DUX4 ORFs which were discarded by our pipeline because of their partial nature.

Discussion

Performance of the Pipeline

We developed a bioinformatic pipeline (Supplementary Fig. 2) to analyze the available long read reference genomes for the evolution of the DUX gene family based on protein similarities and repeat structure. This study shows how the DUX family is dispersed over DUXA, DUXB (including DUXBL), and DUXC (including DUX4 and Dux) branches. Our efforts were mainly directed through the recognition of a recurrent genetic pattern and were based on the use of the machine learning tool AUGUSTUS trained with human, chicken and zebrafish RNA-seq aligned data42. Although the developers claim that their human model performs well across all mammalian species, we implemented our pipeline also with the only non-mammalian vertebrate models for which long read genomes are available, namely chicken and zebrafish. This was done with the expectation that a downstream filter – selecting only genes with two consecutive complete HDs – would be sufficient to refine the predictions.

Using our pipeline, we identified numerous DUXA and DUXB genes across various groups of placental mammals. Recognizing the limitation that our approach may not identify all DUX gene family members in all species analyzed, we also gathered orthologues from literature and the NCBI gene database, filling most of the gaps such as the Asian elephant DUXA (present in NCBI database) or canine DUXC30. To validate this integration, we performed a retrospective analysis using the genomic coordinates of NCBI-annotated DUX genes that were absent from the AUGUSTUS output. In all cases examined, these genes were already present among the initial BLAST hits at the expected genomic locations, confirming that BLAST successfully detected the relevant homeobox sequences (including DUX genes and sDUX ones), while AUGUSTUS failed to produce a complete gene model. These cases were therefore added manually. Representative examples are reported in the Supplementary Table 1. However, some gaps remain. Due to the proximity of species carrying these genes, we attribute their absence to the imperfect sensitivity of the current gene prediction tools or to incomplete or less accurate sequence information. For DUXC, DUXBL, and Dux, however, we relied solely on our own efforts, as the NCBI database only includes orthologues for human genes. DUX4, DUXC, and Dux are present in most placentals, with only a few species lacking a CTD-containing DUX gene. This absence could be due to limitations of the gene prediction tool we used or alignment errors in the corresponding assembly.

DUX Gene Detection

The ORFs of DUXA, DUXB, and DUXBL are encoded by multiple exons even though we discovered also some intronless copies like the DUXA intronless copy in orangutans or the DUXBL intronless copy in sloth. These copies have likely arisen by retrotransposition, a process which is thought to have played a crucial role in the evolution of DUX414,30,43. Thus, like DUXA retrotransposition presumably taken place recently in the orangutan genome, the intronless DUX4 and Dux genes have been produced by retrotransposition of an intron containing ancestor, apparently showing some degree of similarity with DUXC.

In contrast to an earlier study3 we found evidence for a functional copy of DUXBL in macaque. Our prediction pipeline recognized a double homeodomain structure clustering immediately adjacent to Glires Duxbl. Subsequent BLASTp analysis, matched DUXBL both with DUX1-like of Macaca nemestrina and DUXB-like of Piliocolobus tephrosceles with more than 95% identity. Interestingly, the macaque DUXBL sequence is shorter than DUXBL in other species (264 amino acids vs. ~350), as it completely lacks the C-terminal part of the protein (see below). Despite this, the gene appears potentially transcribable, as suggested by its intact promoter region and sequence characteristics. The same structure has already registered (LOC123575570) in our selected assembly even though no RefSeq mRNA has been mapped to the same sequence, probably because Duxbl is expressed briefly during embryonic development in mice37. Through syntenic information, we demonstrated the topological relation between our predicted structure and mouse Duxbl1 (anchor genes Anxa11, Plac9 and Tmem254), thus confirming our hypothesis. Our gene prediction pipeline did not detect intact DUXBL copies in other phylogenetically closely related species, and nor could we find it manually using syntenic information. Thus, the DUXBL family presence is not constant across animals, as it is absent in species placed evolutionary between macaques and mice in our dataset. While most great apes carry a DUXBL pseudogene3, our findings suggest that in macaques this gene may have regained its functional role. Interestingly, among Glires, an intact Duxbl gene was only found in species that also carried a Dux gene or Dux array, suggesting some degree of interdependency between them43.

DUX in Context: Comparison with Other Homeobox Families

Among the mammalian-specific homeobox families, DUX genes are structurally unique as the only double homeobox family3, whereas Rhox, ETCHbox, and Obox and others contain a single homeobox domain44. DUX genes are also distinctive in their genomic organization, often being embedded within macrosatellite repeat arrays, in contrast to the clustered but non-repetitive arrangements observed for other PAIRED (PRD)-like homeobox gene families45,46. The rapid evolutionary dynamics observed across all these families likely reflect a combination of factors: strong selective pressures on reproduction and early embryogenesis, the partially overlapping functions of paralogues within each family, and even cross-family compensation, as exemplified by the functional convergence of ETCHbox genes in primates46 and OBOX genes in rodents47 in regulating zygotic genome activation. Together with their localization within genomically unstable regions such as subtelomeres prone to duplication, recombination, and gene conversion48, these features have facilitated the strikingly fast diversification of these developmental regulators while preserving their essential roles in mammalian embryogenesis. Among those genes we noticed the consistent presence of CPHXL with DUX gene loci, either in the DUXBL locus in mouse and sloth (Fig. 3) and in the DUXB locus in human. Thus, CPHXL shows separate clustering with other homeobox genes (Fig. 7), suggesting that CPHXL is closely related with DUX-related genes. CPHXL is a non-clustered single-homeobox PRD gene whose function has been linked to early developmental transcriptional programs, with functional similarities to those regulated by DUX transcription factors49,50. Given its absence in the non-placental species analyzed in this study, and its close phylogenetic relationship with sDUX (Fig. 7), it is reasonable to hypothesize that CPHXL originated in placental mammals alongside the emergence of the DUX gene family, possibly from a duplication of an ancestral sDUX-like gene. Structural and Functional Innovation in DUX Genes

The Leidenroth study identified sDUX, a single-homeobox gene in non-placental animals, as the ancestral form of the double-homeobox DUX genes3. Despite having only a single homeodomain, sDUX binds DNA as a head-to-head dimer, similar to the tandem homeodomains of DUX443. Like DUX4, the activation domain of sDUX resides in its C-terminal region, as demonstrated by a mutant lacking the C-terminal 74 amino acids, which showed no toxicity and failed to induce target genes, confirming the necessity of its C terminus for cytotoxicity and transcriptional activation43. Protein sequence alignment showed that, in addition to the HD, sDUX shares a conserved terminal amino acid sequence with the other members of DUX family (Supplementary Fig. 9), with the exception of DUXA. Thus, this sequence is likely the precursor of the complete CTD observed in DUXC, Dux, and DUX4. This suggests that the CTD did not need to be acquired from other structures but instead evolved over time, undergoing sequence variation that led to its optimal configuration.

Unlike DUX4, however, DUXA and DUXB do not have a functional CTD domain and are non-toxic43, possibly due to their reduced ability to bind regions of closed chromatin. Interestingly, DUXA is one of the most potently upregulated targets of DUX4, suggesting a potential feedback-inhibitory mechanism43, akin to the role of mouse Duxbl against mouse Dux in regulatory pathways37.

Dog has an intron containing DUXC that produces both a single and double homeodomain isoform. The single HD isoform binds as a dimer similar to the DUX single HD30. This suggests that the single HD isoform might have substituted for the function of sDUX and added additional features with the double HD isoform. Pig is predicted to also have a single and double HD isoform based on gene structure and available RNA seq data31, maybe horse as well. Essentially, all characterized DUXC transcripts (which is only a few) have both a dominant single HD isoform and a less frequent double HD isoform, suggesting that the retrotransposition forced the double HD isoform to be expressed exclusively both in intronless DUX4 and Dux forms.

Like DUXBL, sDUX is linked to the ANXA11 anchor gene (Fig. 3). In placental mammals, likely a duplication event within the sDUX gene’s homeodomain, likely caused by mechanisms such as unequal crossing-over or misaligned homologous recombination, resulted in the creation of the ancestral double-homeobox DUX gene3. This duplication was followed by a fusion event, possibly involving a deletion that joined the two homeoboxes into a single functional reading frame. This ancestral double-homeobox gene then diversified through subsequent mutational events, domain divergence, and retrotransposition, giving rise to various members of the DUX family.

DUX Family Evolution.

The DUX ancestor arose from a duplication of the sDUX homeodomain in placental mammals and two subsequent gene duplication produced DUXA and DUXB. DUXBL is likely to be the direct descendent of the DUX ancestor, both because sequence similarity to sDUX and syntenic data. Consistent with Xenarthra data, DUXC likely derived from the same locus of DUXBL also gaining a CTD; then, both DUX4 and DUX resulted from retrotransposition events. DUXC, DUX4 and Dux organized forming arrays; and speciation, mutations, and array homogenization concurred in shaping the current extreme arrays variability.

DUXB and DUXBL are closely related and share similarities, including anchor genes like CPHXL, but they differ significantly in function: DUXB is a complete and expressed gene, while DUXBL is often a pseudogene3. Nevertheless, the CTD-containing genes are more closely related to DUXBL than to DUXA or DUXB (Fig. 2). Notably, the DUXBL locus is highly dynamic, showing variability across species (Fig. 3). This suggests that the DUXBL locus serves as the “primordial soup” for the DUX family, facilitating the emergence of DUXA, DUXB and DUXBL and, eventually, possibly also of CTD-containing genes like DUXC.

From DUXC, multiple retrotransposition events gave rise to intronless forms DUX4 and Dux3. Our analyses suggest that this event likely occurred four times, as evidenced by the presence of four distinct clusters of intronless DUXC orthologues—found in Primates, Glires, Afrotheria, and Dermoptera—which appear as separate branches in our phylogenetic tree (Fig. 2). It is interesting to note that while the characterized DUXC gene transcripts indicate the presence of both a single HD isoform and double HD isoform30, all identified retrotranspositions create the double HD retrogene. This could reflect the timing and tissue of isoform expression, or suggest that locking-in the double HD DUXC isoform had a selective advantage in lineage divergence that was lacking in the single HD isoform.

Both DUXC and its derivatives (DUX4 and Dux) are prone to tandem array formation, though the reasons for this remain unclear. Perhaps they may play a role in facilitating bursts of gene expression, as observed in 4 C cells, where multiple copies of DUX4 might be expressed simultaneously. This could provide a mechanism for robust and rapid activation of DUX4 during this specific developmental window. Following such bursts of expression, repeat-mediated silencing mechanisms could act as an efficient way to rapidly suppress the locus, preventing sustained or inappropriate activation. This dynamic interplay between activation and silencing might serve to tightly regulate the temporal expression of DUX4, balancing its functional roles while minimizing potential deleterious effects.

DUX-containing arrays are common in placental species and contribute to the complex evolutionary landscape of the DUX family. Over time, speciation and random mutations, coupled with array homogenization, have led to increased diversity and heterogeneity in the DUX gene family, making their evolutionary history both intricate and fascinating.

Arrays Evolution and Genomic Spread

Our extensive analysis of DUX-homologue-containing arrays across various placental species, combined with the complexity of pseudo-array structures found in humans, provides a foundation for developing also a comprehensive theory of array evolution.

While the evolution of DUX genes has unfolded over millions of years, the structural organization and patterns of these related arrays exhibit remarkable variation, even among closely related species. For instance, the human DUX sequences are nearly identical to their homologues in chimpanzees and bonobos, reflecting their conserved role and recent divergence in evolutionary terms. However, the genomic arrays in which these sequences are embedded often display strikingly different patterns between these species. These differences can include variations in the number of repeat units, sequence homogeneity, and array organization, suggesting that while the DUX4 genes themselves are conserved, their surrounding genomic context is subject to more dynamic evolutionary pressures. Moreover, this variability is not limited to interspecies comparisons. Within a single species, such as humans, the number of repeat units in D4Z4 arrays can vary significantly between individuals, leading to substantial inter-individual diversity.

In most species, these arrays (notably DUX4 arrays in Primates and Afrotheria) are located in sub-telomeric regions of chromosomes, making them prone to rearrangements. In humans, the chromosome 4q35 array is the most ancient and gave rise to the 10q26 array through a recent duplication event, which also involved FRG2, but not the inverted DUX4c genes27. Although potentially non-functional for the development of FSHD, the 10q26 array is remarkably homogeneous, even more so than the original 4q35 array, suggesting having a functional role deployed in cleavage stage embryos23. Also, among great apes, D4Z4 arrays lack complete conservation, and not all carry an inverted repeat encoding DUX4c. This variability raises doubts about the DUX4c ORF having an essential conserved function across primates.

In addition to the homogenization observed in the two D4Z4 arrays in humans, we also identified other potential forms of array evolution. In most orangutan D4Z4-like arrays, a 6.8 kb higher-order repeat unit has been established through the merging of two adjacent non-identical subunits via a known mechanism of array widening38. This results in a macro unit, unique within the primate clade, that still encodes a complete DUX4 open reading frame (ORF). Mice and rats, on the other hand, exemplify unusual array patterns where the units are not fully contiguous but instead are interspersed, sometimes oriented differently, and often arranged in smaller arrays as a consequence of in cis duplications. This pattern, also observed in other species such as bonobos, lacks a clear functional explanation. Additionally, the Asian elephant array on chromosome 14 presents a distinctive structure of two sub-arrays composed of similar-length units with different forms of the DUX4 ORF. These sub-arrays are separated by a wider unit that contains a LINE-family transposable element. While each sub-array is highly homogeneous, there is significantly less homogeneity between them. We speculate that this LINE-1 insertion drives the divergence of the two sub-arrays51, creating an intriguing evolutionary scenario that would be nearly impossible to replicate experimentally.

The presence of DUX4 orthologues across diverse species—whether intronless or intron-containing—underscores their evolutionary and functional relevance. Our data show that human DUX4 arrays, like those of other primates, are not outliers compared to other animals, but instead fall within a conserved range of homology together with other macrosatellites hosting DUXC, Dux, and DUX4 genes (Supplementary Fig. 7). Such conservation is likely maintained by selective pressures related to their essential role in embryogenesis, a function that extends across species.

Many pseudo-DUX4 arrays, characterized by an imperfect repeat array structure, have been identified on human acrocentric chromosomes, all located in non-sub-telomeric regions marked by beta-satellite repeats. Given their structural similarity and comparable unit lengths to the DUX4 arrays, it is likely these pseudo-arrays originated from both the chromosome 4q35 and 10q26 D4Z4 array. Their relocation to non-sub-telomeric regions may have disrupted their regulatory functions, leading to the accumulation of loss-of-function mutations. As a result, these arrays have become highly heterogeneous rather than undergoing the concerted evolution typical of functional arrays52.

Although DUX4 expression from pseudo-arrays is unlikely, it is not impossible, as demonstrated by the expression of DUX1 from a pseudo-array on chromosome 21 and the DUX-like transcript in some cancers10. DUX1 is described as an expressed gene with specific roles, particularly in DNA-binding and transcriptional activation and it is suggested to play a role similar to other paired-type homeodomain proteins, which are involved in developmental processes29. This indicates that DUX1, despite some sequence divergence and the loss of transcription from its CTD, remains functionally active and is involved in transcriptional regulation. Moreover, a functional IRES element was described just upstream of the CTD that could produce functional small protein when upstream stop codons in the DUX4 ORF occur23. Independent of its role in DUX4, this CTD was shown to have biological activity in regulating differentiation43 and innate immune signaling19. This might be relevant to some of the pericentromeric transcripts with premature stop codons or partial CTD transcripts.

Beyond their evolutionary significance, pseudo-D4Z4 structures may also have clinical relevance. While diagnosing FSHD has become more straightforward in recent years, a small subset of patients presents a clinical FSHD phenotype and detectable DUX4 expression in biopsies yet lacks both a significant contraction of D4Z4 repeats and mutations in methylation modifier genes associated with FSHD2. Thus, we propose that the investigation of these pseudo-D4Z4 arrays in ultra-rare, selected cases, should be considered, as they may reveal the underlying cause of DUX4 re-expression in elusive forms of FSHD.

Overall, this study also provides a foundation for further research into the complex nature of pseudo-array regions and their implications for development and in disease mechanisms. The interplay between array structure and the regulation of DUX gene expression—both in normal physiology and in disease states—offers an intriguing area for future research, particularly in understanding how genomic architecture influences evolutionary fitness and susceptibility to disorders like FSHD.

Methods

Database Collection

More than 50 species including 42 placental mammals were selected following an evolutionary distance-based criterion (Supplementary Fig. 1). Since literature suggested DUX4 homologues to be shared only within placental mammals groups, we selected (i) 10 different species of Primatomorpha (main sub-group being Primates): Homo sapiens, Pan troglodytes, Pan paniscus, Gorilla gorilla, Pongo pygmaeus, Symphalangus syndactylus, Macaca fascicularis, Callithrix jacchus, Nycticebus coucang, Lemur catta, Cynocephalus volans; (ii) 9 species from Non-primatomorpha (main group being Glires): Tupaia chinensis, Lepus oiostolus, Erethizon dorsatum, Sciurus carolinensis, Thomomys bottae, Jaculus jaculus, Chionomys nivalis, Mus musculus, Rattus norvegicus; (iii) 15 species from Laurasiateria: Bos taurus, Tursiops truncatus, Balaenoptera musculus, Hippopotamus amphibius kiboko, Sus scrofa, Camelus dromedarius, Diceros bicornis minor, Tapirus indicus, Equus caballus, Zalophus californianus, Felis catus, Canis lupus familiaris, Manis pentadactyla, Hipposideros larvatus, Sorex araneus; (iv) 3 species from Xenarthra: Dasypus novemcinctus, Tamandua tetradactyla, Choloepus didactylus, (v) 4 species from Afrotheria: Loxodonta africana, Elephas maximus, Heterohyrax brucei, Dugong dugon; (vi) 3 species from Marsupialia: Monodelphis domestica, Trichosurus vulpecula, Dromiciops gliroides, Notamacropus eugenii; (vii) 3 species from Monotremata: Ornithorhynchus anatinus; and (viii) 4 species from non-placental animals, from the other taxonomic classes of vertebrates: Tachyglossus aculeatus, Gallus gallus, Zootoca vivipara, Rana temporaria, Danio rerio. Assemblies were selected from the NCBI publicly available dataset (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/datasets/), prioritizing those meeting specific criteria, including chromosome-level resolution and sequencing by PacBio long-read technology. Among the selected assemblies, the average number of contigs was 831, with a contig N50 of 23.6 Mb. Most assemblies were submitted by the National Human Genome Research Institute (NHGRI), the Vertebrate Genome Project (VGP), or the Wellcome Sanger Institute (WSI), starting from March 2020. The complete list is available in Supplementary Data 7.

DUX Genes Identification

Each selected assembly was analyzed using tBLASTn (amino acid vs translated nucleotide database) available on the NCBI BLAST website (https://blast.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/Blast.cgi), but run locally. We used a 46-amino acid sequence from the second homeodomain of human DUX4 (GRRKRTAVTGSQTALLLRAFEKDRFPGIAAREELARETGLPESRIQ) as query, as this is the longest highly conserved sequence of DUX4, translated from a single exon3 (PARAMETERS: -evalues 5, -word_size 2). From the matched sequence coordinates, we extracted ~36,000 nucleotide sequences by adding 18,000 nucleotides upstream and downstream of each hit.

The AUGUSTUS gene prediction tool53 was then run locally to analyze possible amino acid sequences from the predicted gene sequences (PARAMETERS: --genemodel=complete –singlestrand=true –species=human –softmasking=False alternatives-from-sampling=true –sample=100). While the human model was deemed sufficient for most mammalian species, we also ran the analysis using the chicken and fish models, as they are the closest available models to mammals. This produced a list of potential alternative transcripts.

Finally, to manage all predicted gene forms, we used a custom Python program designed to detect specific patterns of highly conserved amino acids within the homeodomain (Supplementary Data 1). This allowed us to identify sequences with two adjacent complete in frame homeodomains, thereby filtering for complete DUX homologues and excluding pseudogenes, incomplete transcripts, and unrelated genes that may have been inadvertently collected due to proximity to matched sequences.

In addition to the extracted sequences, homologues of DUXA and DUXB were also retrieved from the NCBI database and relevant literature and subsequently incorporated into the collection.

Sequence Alignments and Phylogenetic Analysis

Predicted and collected DUX genes (all amino acid sequences with two complete and thus presumably functional homeodomains) (Supplementary Data 2) were aligned using MAFFT (multiple alignment using fast Fourier transform) v7.51154 with default parameters and trimmed using ClipKIT55 with a balanced trade-off strategy between trimming away noise and preserving phylogenetic signal (smart-gap). Phylogenetic analysis was then conducted using FastTree 2.1.11 Double Precision56 with default settings. The resulting tree was visualized using iTOL v657 using platypus sDUX sequence as the root, with main branches corresponding to DUX4 homolog clades pruned to show detailed branching. The gene names were assigned based on (i) the clustering of the genes in the phylogenetic tree relying on a set of known genes used as reference (human DUX4, DUXA, DUXB; dog30 DUXC and pig31 DUXC, mouse Dux and Duxbl); (ii) synteny analysis based on BLAST program with selected close genes (anchor); (iii) direct amino-acid sequence comparison of selected conserved sequences (HDs and CTD).

Highlighting the Arrays

For each species, we collected and listed the DUXC members (DUX4, DUXC and Dux), using their gene coordinates to manually identify potential arrays. Arrays were defined as patterns of at least three genes of identical amino acid length, separated by nearly identical distances between start codons. Custom sequences were imported into a Genome Browser to verify array-specific features, including high GC content and RepeatMasker features.

Defining the Repeat Units

After identifying the arrays, we divided them into single units using a custom Python tool (Supplementary Data 3). For each species, a 10-nucleotide sequence located 1 kb upstream of the start codon, presumed to include the upstream regulatory region of each DUXC homologue, was extracted. The most recurrent 10-nucleotide sequence per species was used as a virtual restriction enzyme to segment the arrays into individual units. For certain arrays requiring refined separation, the virtual enzyme was manually adjusted.

D4Z4 Study

Using MAFFT, FastTree, and iTOL (as described above in Sequence Alignments and Phylogenetic Analysis), we visualized the clustering of individual repeat units. To better understand intra-cluster variability, we calculated the distance matrix using the Kimura correction method with distmat (-nucmethod 2), obtaining the average pairwise distance via a custom Python script (Supplementary Data 4). This allowed us to calculate the homogeneity score of the arrays, defined as the average pairwise similarity between each unit and all other units within the same array (Supplementary Data 8).

Assessing Evolutionary Pressure in DUX4

After collecting the nucleotide sequences of DUX4 from great ape species, a codon-based alignment was performed using PRANK58 with default parameters. Selection pressure was then assessed using the dN/dS ratio, computed with HyPhy59 under the Fixed Effects Likelihood (FEL) method.

Southern Blotting

Spleens were collected from different mouse strains (listed in supplementary Fig. 6).

Spleens were mechanically dissociated through a 70 µm cell strainer in PBS. Cells were centrifuged at 450 × g for 5 min at 4 °C, and the pellet was resuspended in 3 mL erythrocyte lysis buffer (155 mM NH₄Cl, 10 mM KHCO₃, 1 mM EDTA, pH 8.0). After two additional wash steps, cells were resuspended in SE buffer (75 mM NaCl, 25 mM EDTA, pH 8.0) and adjusted to 1 × 10⁷ cells/mL. The suspension was mixed with 1% low-melting-point agarose (Fisher Scientific) and cast into a Perspex mold. Solidified plugs ( ~ 1 × 10⁶ cells each) were incubated in 10 mL lysis solution (SE buffer, 1% N-lauroylsarcosine, 6 mg pronase) at 37 °C for 48 h to obtain high-quality DNA plugs.

DNA, corresponding to approximately 600,000 cells, was digested with EcoRV and separated by pulsed-field gel electrophoresis (PFGE) on a 0.85% agarose gel over a 22-hour run at 8.5 V/cm. The separation was performed in two identical cycles, with the switch time linearly increasing from 1 second to 20 seconds. Following electrophoresis, the DNA was transferred to positively charged nylon membranes (Hybond-XL, Amersham) via Southern blotting and hybridized with a radioactively labeled mDUX probe. The mDUX probe was generated by PCR amplification from C57BL/6 genomic DNA using primers 5’-GCA AAT GGA CAC AAC ACC TG-3’ and 5’-ACT GGC CTG TAA AAC CTT GC-3’, covering 579 nt of a mDUX repeat unit. Hybridizing fragments were visualized by phosphor imaging on a Typhoon scanner (Amersham).

Statistics and Reproducibility

This study is primarily based on computational genomic and phylogenetic analyses performed using publicly available datasets and standardized pipelines, as described in the Methods. Default parameters were used unless otherwise specified, and analyses were applied consistently across all species. Custom Python scripts (provided as Supplementary Data) were used for sequence filtering and downstream analyses. No formal statistical hypothesis testing was performed. For repeat unit analyses, summary metrics (e.g., average pairwise distances) were calculated across all units within each array. Southern blot experiments were conducted using standard protocols as described, with no statistical analysis due to their qualitative nature.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our comparative analysis of DUX-family genes across diverse vertebrate species reveals a previously under-appreciated diversity in DUX gene structure and organization. We provide evidence of the presence of DUXBL in other clades and its possible implication in the origin of the other DUX genes. The identification of unique array patterns, such as disrupted homogenization in the Asian elephant and the higher-order repeats in orangutans, highlights the evolutionary complexity and adaptability of these sequences across the animal kingdom. In humans, the presence of pseudo-D4Z4 structures lacking a full DUX4 ORF on acrocentric chromosomes suggests an evolutionary divergence in array organization, reflecting different functional or regulatory dynamics. Overall, these insights deepen our understanding of the evolutionary trajectory and functional potential of DUX-family genes, emphasizing the power of high-quality genome assemblies in uncovering molecular diversity and evolutionary mechanisms.
