Structured bigel-based foams with tailored textural properties: a bigel-based strategy for cream replacement
Authors: Mengke Li, Mengle Li, Ying Qian, Xiangsong Chen, Jianglin Chen, Long Jin, Lili Cao, Min Pang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study developed bigels using beeswax/soybean oil-based oleogels and gelatin hydrogels, which were then converted into bigel-based foams via whipping and aeration as alternatives to natural cream. The effects of different oleogel ratios on the physicochemical properties, microstructure, mechanical properties, and thermal behavior of the bigel-based foams were investigated. The bigel-based foam containing 40% oleogel exhibited the best performance: its expansion ratio reached 33% (close to the 37% of commercial cream), and sensory analysis indicated it received the highest score, achieving the overall highest acceptability. Increasing the oleogel proportion led to a decrease in the hardness of the bigel-based foams, reaching a minimum at 70% oleogel content. Differential scanning calorimetry analysis showed that higher oleogel content reduced the melting and crystallization temperatures of the bigel-based foams. Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy confirmed that the formation mechanism of the bigels was based on physical interactions between the oil and water phases. Rheological analysis revealed that all bigel-based foams had a loss tangent (tan δ) less than 1, indicating a stable, solid-like gel network (G′ > G″) essential for structural retention. These results comprehensively demonstrate the structural and functional characteristics of the bigel-based foams, highlighting their potential for applications in the food sector.

Introduction

Cream is deeply favored by consumers due to its dense texture and excellent flavor. However, the limited production and high cost of natural cream based on milk have gradually made it unable to meet consumer demand. Plant-based cream, with its capacity to mimic the texture and mouthfeel of natural cream, presents advantages such as lower cost, improved whipping performance, and extended shelf life (Liu et al., 2021). Consequently, it has emerged as a widely accepted substitute for natural cream. In its production, hydrogenated palm oil is a commonly used ingredient (Qian et al., 2025). This ensures partial coalescence of the fat at the air bubble interface, thus increasing the rigidity of the interfacial film and providing the cream with good foam stability after aging and whipping processes (Liu et al., 2021). However, studies have well established the adverse health effects of saturated fatty acids (SFAs) and trans fatty acids (TFAs) found in traditional plant-based creams (Baum et al., 2012; Hooper et al., 2020; Mozaffarian et al., 2006). Excessive intake of these components is known to elevate low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) levels, thereby increasing the risk of various cardiovascular and cerebrovascular diseases (Lian et al., 2020). Therefore, developing cream alternatives to meet the market demand for healthier creams, reducing SFA and TFA intake while increasing unsaturated fatty acid and functional nutrient intake, has become a popular research target.

Recognized as a promising cream substitute, bigels demonstrate considerable potential for developing low-saturated-fat and low-energy fats (Cui et al., 2022; Nutter et al., 2023). By modifying their formulation and processing, these biphasic systems—formed by combining an oleogel and a hydrogel (Hu et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2025) and unified under high shear (Shakeel et al., 2021)—can mimic solid fat properties while reducing energy content (Tian et al., 2026; Yang et al., 2022). Individually, hydrogels and oleogels possess distinct characteristics and limitations. Hydrogels offer a high water content, low caloric density, and the ability to encapsulate hydrophilic nutrients, but they inherently lack the lubricity, plasticity, and rich mouthfeel of traditional solid fats. Conversely, oleogels can successfully mimic the rheological properties and texture of solid fats with a healthier lipid profile; however, when used alone, they often suffer from a greasy or waxy mouthfeel and still carry a relatively high caloric burden. Bigels overcome these individual limitations by synergistically integrating both phases into a unified network. This biphasic architecture not only provides tunable mechanical properties and good storage stability at ambient temperature (Jiang et al., 2024; Paul et al., 2018), but also achieves a superior balance: it reduces the overall caloric value compared to pure oleogels while delivering a better sensory profile and fat-like lubricity that pure hydrogels cannot provide.

Hydrogels, as polymeric materials with a three-dimensional network structure, have attracted considerable attention in fields such as food science, biomedicine, and tissue engineering due to their favorable physicochemical properties, biocompatibility, and tunable functionalities (Liu et al., 2024). Oleogels hold broad application prospects in the food and healthcare industries, as they are capable of structuring organic liquid phases into solid-like networks using minimal gelling agents. These materials are designed to replace solid fats containing trans and saturated fatty acids in daily diets, thereby reducing the risk of cardiovascular disease (Li, Jiang, et al., 2026;Li, Zhu, et al., 2026). The oleogel and hydrogel phases are structured by gelling agents, which form structural units through self-assembly or crystallization. The entanglement of these units forms a three-dimensional network that immobilizes liquid oil and water, leading to a viscoelastic bigel structure (Loza-Rodríguez et al., 2023). Consequently, a variety of bigel systems have been developed by manipulating the oleogelator, hydrogelator, and phase ratio, primarily for fat replacement. Reported formulations include xanthan gum hydrogel with lecithin/monoglyceride oleogel (Yi et al., 2025), monolaurin/gellan gum-based systems (Wang et al., 2024), edible bigels of guar gum, walnut oil, and rice bran wax (Shakouri et al., 2025), nanocellulose hydrogel with monoglyceride oleogel (Hou et al., 2024), and ethyl cellulose oleogel with konjac glucomannan hydrogel (Xue et al., 2024). While research on bigels as general structural fat replacers is extensive, their specific application as a functional substitute for natural cream remains a significant challenge (Guo et al., 2024; Lotfi Shirazi and Koocheki, 2025). Unlike simple fat substitution in solid foods, cream replacement requires the matrix to possess not only solid-like viscoelasticity but also exceptional aeration performance and long-term foam stability. The ability of a biphasic system to trap and stabilize air bubbles—thereby transitioning from a dense gel into a porous, ultra-light bigel-based foam—is critical for mimicking the delicate texture of commercial cream. To date, the rational design of food-grade bigel-based foams derived from bigels, particularly the mechanistic understanding of how the oleogel-to-hydrogel ratio dictates the stability of the resulting aerated structure, has been rarely explored. Therefore, this study meticulously fabricated a beeswax/soybean oil-based bigel system to advance research in this field.

The structural and functional properties of bigels are primarily governed by the intrinsic characteristics of the lipid and aqueous phases, the types of gelling agents employed, and the oleogel-to-hydrogel ratio. The choice of these components is critical, as they determine the network density, thermal stability, and sensory profile of the resulting system. In this study, soybean oil was selected as the liquid lipid phase due to its abundance of essential polyunsaturated fatty acids and vitamin E (Tian et al., 2023); however, its liquid state necessitates a robust structuring strategy to achieve the plasticity required for cream alternatives. To construct this framework, beeswax (BW) and glycerol monostearate (GMS) were utilized as synergistic oleogelators. BW provides a stable crystalline lattice that immobilizes the oil, while GMS acts as an interfacial stabilizer and crystal modifier, both of which are essential for maintaining the structural integrity of the oil droplets within the biphasic matrix. For the aqueous phase, gelatin (GEL) was chosen as the hydrogelator. Beyond its biocompatibility, GEL is valued for its exceptional foaming, emulsifying, and gelling capabilities, which are indispensable for stabilizing the air-water interface during the whipping process and ensuring the formation of a self-supporting bigel-based foam (Zhu et al., 2023). Collectively, the rational combination of these food-grade ingredients aims to create a stable, nutritious, and highly whippable bigel-based system. Prior research has extensively demonstrated that bigels can serve as effective fat replacers across various food matrices. For instance, a bigel formulated with a grass pea protein isolate (GPPI) hydrogel and a candelilla wax (CW)-stabilized sesame oil oleogel exhibited high hardness, superior viscoelasticity, and long-term stability, making it a viable substitute for structural fats (Lotfi Shirazi and Koocheki, 2025). However, despite their proven success as solid fat mimetics, the preparation of bigel-based foam by aerating bigels via whipping—specifically as a substitute for natural cream—remains underexplored and requires further investigation. In recent years, oleofoams—defined as aerated oleogels—have emerged as a promising class of structured lipid materials for fat reduction, leveraging oil crystals to stabilize air bubbles (Binks and Vishal, 2021). Building upon this, the integration of hydrogels into these systems to form bigels has led to the development of whippable bigel-based foams (Guo et al., 2024). These multiphasic foams offer superior versatility compared to pure oleofoams, as the synergistic interaction between the aqueous and oil networks can significantly enhance overrun and textural rigidity (Jiang et al., 2024; Lotfi Shirazi and Koocheki, 2025). Therefore, in this study, a biphasic system consisting of a beeswax/soybean oil-based oleogel and a gelatin hydrogel was meticulously fabricated. These components jointly form a bigel-based foam that possesses excellent mechanical performance and promising consumer palatability. Our aim is to evaluate the physicochemical properties of the bigel-based foam under different oleogel ratios using optical microscopy, overrun, Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy, rheological analysis, differential scanning calorimetry, texture profile analysis, colorimetry, and sensory analysis. This evaluation will assess their potential as natural cream substitutes in food applications.

Material and methods

Material

Glycerol monostearate (GMS; C18:0 and C16:0 > 95%) was purchased from Shanghai Yuanye Bio-Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Beeswax (BW) was obtained from Huasheng Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd. (Tianjin, China). Soybean oil was supplied by Yihai Kerry Foods Marketing Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Gelatin (GEL, Type A) was purchased from JELO Bio-Technology Co., Ltd. (Shandong, China). The commercially available cream (UHT whipping cream, featuring a dairy fat base with a 35.5% fat content) was purchased from Anchor (Shanghai, China). Among them, GMS, BW, and GEL are all food-grade ingredients.

Methods

Preparation of bigels

An oleogel was prepared by heating a mixture of 90 wt% soybean oil, 10 wt% BW, and 2.5 wt% GMS by total mass in a water bath, heating at 85 °C, and continuously stirring at 400 r/min for 30 min. The obtained solution was then left to remain at room temperature for at least 2 h, after which the oleogel was left to be stored at 4 °C for 24 h. A hydrogel was prepared from 5 wt% gelatin, deionized water. The mixture was swollen at room temperature for 20 min and then heated at 600 r/min for 30 min in a water bath at 50 °C. The obtained solution was then cooled to room temperature after which the hydrogel was placed for storage at 4 °C for 24 h. The previously stored gels were heated in an oven at 75 °C for at least 1 h to completely melt their initial crystalline and network structures, returning them to a liquid state. This step was crucial to ensure a uniform emulsification. The bigels were then prepared by mixing the oleogel and hydrogel in the weight ratios of 2:8, 3:7, 4:6, 5:5, 6:4, 7:3, 8:2, which were designated as 2O8W, 3O7W and the like. Immediately after mixing, the hot liquid mixtures were homogenized at a mixing rate of 8000 r/min for 120 s using a homogenizer (GJJ-0.03/60, Shanghai Taiyi Light Industrial Equipment Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China). To prevent the premature crystallization of beeswax, the temperature during homogenization was maintained at approximately 70–75 °C. Finally, the resulting emulsions were stored at 4 °C for 24 h to allow for the simultaneous co-gelation and formation of the biphasic bigel networks.

Preparation of bigel-based foams

The prepared bigels were removed from the 4 °C refrigerator and immediately whipped into foams using a KitchenAid mixer (5KSM3311, KitchenAid Co., St. Joseph, MI, USA) equipped with a wire beater. This aeration process was conducted in a room-temperature environment for 5 min at speed 4 (approximately 1000 r/min) to achieve optimal hardness and overrun. These specific whipping conditions were determined through preliminary screening experiments, using the maximum overrun and the formation of a stable, self-supporting foam structure as the primary evaluation criteria. The foamability of the resulting gel-based foams was assessed immediately, after which all systems were stored at 25 °C for later analysis.

Optical and polarized light microscopy

The microstructure of the bigel was analyzed using optical and polarized light microscopes (XPH-25 C, Puda Co., Ltd., China) equipped with digital cameras. All observations were performed at room temperature (approximately 25 °C) with the samples in their intrinsic semi-solid state. A small sample was placed on a glass slide via a capillary tube and gently covered with a coverslip. Observation commenced at a low magnification (10×), followed by examination at higher magnifications (20× or 50×) under both non-polarized and polarized light modes, respectively.

Determination of overrun

Unwhipped bigels were foamed using a rotary kitchen mixer (5KSM3311, KitchenAid Co., St. Joseph, MI, USA). Prior to whipping, the container was pre-cooled at 4 °C for 1 h to optimize the process. The sample was then whipped at 1000 r/min and 25 °C for 5 min. The overrun was subsequently calculated using the equation below, based on the mass difference between equal volumes of the unwhipped and whipped bigel:Overrun(%)=M1−M2M2×100%where M1 and M2 represent the masses of the unwhipped and whipped bigel, respectively, at identical volumes.

Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) measurement

Using an FTIR spectrometer (Thermo Nicolet, Nexus, Waltham, MA, USA), spectra were recorded at room temperature (25 °C). The scanning was conducted across 4000–650 cm−1 with a resolution of 4 cm−1, accumulating an average of 32 scans per spectrum (Li et al., 2024).

Differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) analysis

Thermal analysis was performed on a Mettler Toledo DSC823e differential scanning calorimeter (Columbus, USA). Following sample preparation (5–10 mg sealed in Al pans), a heating scan from 10 °C to 110 °C at 10 °C/min was executed, and the corresponding heat flow was monitored throughout the temperature ramp.

Rheological properties analysis

The rheological properties of the bigel-based foams were measured using a DHR-3 rheometer (TA, USA) fitted with a 40 mm aluminum parallel plate and a circulating water temperature control system. To determine the linear viscoelastic range (LVR), preliminary strain sweep tests were performed at a constant frequency of 1 Hz with strain ranging from 0.01% to 10%. Based on these results, a constant strain of 0.1% (well within the identified LVR plateau for all samples) was selected for the frequency sweep tests, which were conducted at a temperature of 25 °C, covering a frequency range from 0.1 to 10 Hz.

Texture profile analysis (TPA)

A TA-XT Plus texture analyzer (TA. XT Plus990000, UK) was employed to study the texture properties of the samples, following a reported method (Zhu et al., 2021). The instrument, equipped with a 50 kg load cell and a cylindrical P/0.5 probe, compressed samples placed in a 100 mL cylindrical glass beaker (sample height: 2.0 cm). The test protocol comprised a pre-test, test, and post-test speed of 2.0 mm/s each, with a trigger force of 0.049 N. The probe penetrated the sample to a depth of 1.2 cm (60%) for two consecutive compression cycles. Each sample was measured in triplicate. From the resulting force-time curves, the following texture parameters were determined: hardness (the peak force of the first compression cycle), cohesiveness (the ratio of the work area of the second compression to the first), springiness (the height difference at the start of the second compression), chewiness (Hardness × Cohesiveness × Springiness), and resilience (the ratio of the recovery work to the compression work during the first cycle).

Color analysis

Color values (L∗, a∗, b∗) were measured using a Minolta CR-400 colorimeter (Minolta Co., Ltd., Osaka, Japan). Prior to measurement, the instrument was calibrated with a standard white plate (L∗ = 91.89, a∗ = −0.22, b∗ = 4.49). Samples were placed on a white background and measured at room temperature, with three readings taken per sample for each experimental replicate.

Sensory analysis

Sensory evaluation was conducted by ten semi-trained panelists (5 male, 5 female) selected from the Department of Food Science and Engineering based on health, interest, and familiarity with whipped cream. Following the method of Zhao et al. (2013) with adaptations, they rated the samples on a 10-point scale for three attributes: Flavor and taste (10: no criticism; 9–7: delicate flavor; 6–4: lack of flavor; 3–1: other defects), Body and texture (10: no criticism; 9–7: gummy; 6–4: fluffy; 3–1: other defects), and Color and appearance (10: no criticism; 6–4: dull color; 3–1: unnatural color). Bigel-based foam samples and the commercial cream control were stored at 4 °C throughout sensory analysis to ensure freshness and integrity.

Statistical analysis

All tests were conducted with at least three independent replicates. The resulting data were subjected to analysis of variance (ANOVA) using IBM SPSS Statistics 23.0 (IBM, USA), with statistical significance defined at P < 0.05. Finally, statistical charts and illustrations were generated using Origin Pro 2022 (OriginLab, USA) and Adobe Illustrator 2025 (Adobe, USA).

Results and discussion

Microstructure

As illustrated in Fig. 1a, bigels were meticulously fabricated and then aerated via whipping to produce bigel-based foams. Macroscopic images of the bigels with varying oleogel contents (208W, 307W, 406W, 505W, 604W, 703W, and 802W) and the corresponding micrographs of the resulting bigel-based foams are presented in Fig. 1b, c and d, respectively. The micrographs include observations under both optical and polarized light microscopy. Upon alteration of the oil-to-water phase volume ratio, the formed network structure also changes, meaning the hydrogel phase or the oleogel phase becomes the dominant continuous phase, respectively. At the critical point of phase inversion, a bicontinuous configuration can be observed, where both phases exhibit a bicontinuous state. Notably, when the oleogel-to-hydrogel ratio exceed 6O4W, both phases form bicontinuous phases. With a further increase in the oleogel proportion, the system undergoes phase inversion, transforming into a W/O system where the oil phase becomes continuous. In this case, the droplets display more irregular shapes than those in the O/W system, which is likely attributed to the substantial squeezing force imposed by GMS crystals within the oleogel on the hydrogel droplets (Du et al., 2021). In summary, modulating the oil-to-water phase ratio induces a systematic phase inversion in the bigel, transitioning from an O/W type to a bicontinuous phase and eventually to a W/O type. These microstructural transitions demonstrate that the internal architecture of the bigel system can be precisely tailored by adjusting the phase proportions, which serves as a structural foundation for the subsequent development of aerated foams with specific textural requirements.

Overrun and appearance of bigel-based foams

The overrun and product appearance play a pivotal role in the practical application of bigel-based foams (Guo et al., 2024). Fig. 2 depicts the overrun and appearance of commercially available cream and several bigel-based foams at room temperature. Based on visual observation, all bigel-based foams were self-supporting semi-solids. With an increase in the oleogel ratio from 20% to 60%, the samples demonstrated enhanced shape definition and a more upright posture. Conversely, when the ratio exceeded 60%, a reduction in extruded height and a smoothing of surface texture were observed. This phenomenon is closely linked to the phase inversion occurring at these high oil-to-water ratios. Specifically, as the system transitions into a W/O configuration where the oleogel becomes the continuous phase, the structural dominance of the rigid gelatin hydrogel network is diminished. The reduction in gelatin content leads to a significant decrease in intermolecular hydrogen bonding, which weakens the mechanical strength of the foam's “backbone”. Consequently, the high-oleogel foams exhibit lower resistance to gravitational collapse compared to the hydrogel-dominated O/W systems, resulting in the observed loss of extruded height and definition.Fig. 2The decorated appearance (a) and overrun (b) of cream and bigel-based foam at room temperature. (2O8W–8O2W represent bigels with oleogel accounting for 20–80%, respectively.)

Previous preliminary experiments revealed that pure hydrogels and pure oleogels exhibited extremely low overrun values after whipping, which is consistent with the findings of Guo et al. (2024). In their study, bigels were prepared by blending gellan gum hydrogels with beeswax-based oleogels, and these bigels were subsequently whipped to obtain bigel-based foams. As the oleogel content increased from 20% to 40%, the overrun of the bigel increased markedly, reaching its maximum value (33.15 ± 2.84%) at an oleogel content of 40%. At this point, the overrun of the bigel is similar to that of cream (37.42 ± 1.56%). This indicates that introducing a specific amount of oil droplets can enhance the foaming capability of O/W emulsions (Guo et al., 2024). When the oleogel content reached 50%–60%, the overrun then dropped sharply. At approximately 60% oleogel content, the bigel formed a bicontinuous structure with a low expansion ratio (14.12 ± 1.48%). Additionally, when the oleogel content approached 70%, the bigel transformed into a W/O type, and the overrun rose again as the oleogel proportion increased. This indicates that incorporating a specific quantity of water droplets can improve the foaming performance of W/O emulsion foams. Such improvement may be attributed to non-covalent interactions, including hydrogen bonding, promoted by the presence of water droplets. These droplets are capable of forming hydrogen bonds with hydrophilic moieties on surfactant molecules or with other polar constituents in the emulsion. The resulting hydrogen bonding can reinforce the interfacial layer separating the water droplets from the oil phase, which contributes to greater emulsion stability (Binks and Vishal, 2021). A more robust interfacial film helps inhibit coalescence of water droplets and decreases water drainage from the foam, thus boosting foam stability. Prior studies also demonstrate that adding a controlled amount of aqueous phase to an oleogel enhances its overrun (Du and Meng, 2023). A limited number of water droplets may become immobilized between air bubbles, promoting both foam formation and stability (Guo et al., 2024).

FTIR of bigel-based foams

FTIR spectroscopy was employed to identify the functional groups and characterize the intermolecular interactions between the oleogel and hydrogel phases (Zampouni et al., 2023). Furthermore, this analysis aimed to determine whether the formation of the biphasic bigels involved the creation of new chemical bonds or was driven by physical interactions. The FTIR spectra and peak information of the bigels are shown in Fig. 3 and Table 1. The 2O8W–8O2W spectrum revealed a broad band between 3367.10 and 3314.72 cm−1, corresponding to O-H stretching vibrations associated with hydrogen bonding in the hydrogel (Lee et al., 2024). With increasing oleogel content, the intensity of the O-H stretching vibration showed a decreasing trend, suggesting a weakening of hydrogen bonding in the system. Notably, this characteristic band vanished in the 6O4W–8O2W samples, indicating that the oleogel phase reduced hydrogen bond formation and impeded biopolymer self-association (Zheng et al., 2020). This may be attributed to the water-in-oil (W/O) state, where the hydrogel becomes the dispersed phase, existing as droplets thoroughly surrounded by the continuous oil phase. This structural arrangement effectively "shield" the water droplets, making the internal molecular vibrations difficult to detect. Peaks at 2919.71–2915.34 cm−1 and 2852.05–2847.69 cm−1 were identified as C-H stretching vibrations of CH3 and CH2 groups derived from the saturated and unsaturated fatty acids in soybean oil (Wang et al., 2023). The 2O8W sample exhibited the lowest intensity at these peaks, which may be attributed to its relatively lower oleogel content. Absorption peaks were observed at 1739.02–1732.47 cm−1, where 604w-802w exhibited sharper and more intense peaks. This peak corresponds to the C=O stretching vibration of ester groups in lipids and fatty acids. Notably, a sharp and strong peak observed at 1640.81 cm−1 for 2O8W–3O7W is likely associated with O-H bending, possibly arising from intramolecular hydrogen bonds or residual water within the system (Chen et al., 2023). Additionally, the C-H bending vibration of CH3 groups appeared in the range of 1466.22–1459.67 cm−1 (Hong et al., 2022). The absorption band at 1175.96–1171.59 cm−1 corresponds to the C-O stretching vibration in the bigels. The peak at 724.20–717.65 cm−1, attributed to the C-H rocking vibration of CH2 groups, is less pronounced in 2O8W–3O7W. Furthermore, compared to the FTIR spectra of the bigel-based foams, natural cream typically exhibits distinct Amide I and Amide II bands (around 1650 and 1540 cm−1) (Kong and Yu, 2007) due to the presence of milk proteins, whereas bigel-based foams lack these specific protein signatures. DSC analysis of bigel-based foams

Thermodynamic parameters are vital for controlling thermal stability in the food industry (Li et al., 2024; Zhou et al., 2023). The DSC melting and crystallization profiles of bigel-based foams are shown in Fig. 4. All bigel-based foams exhibited a prominent dominant peak during melting (Fig. 4a) and showed a single broad peak at 55.58 ± 0.52 °C, 55.65 ± 0.38 °C, 55.44 ± 0.65 °C, 55.51 ± 0.67 °C, 55.58 ± 0.11 °C, 56.15 ± 0.29 °C, and 55.37 ± 0.43 °C, respectively, indicating their melting point. The results indicate that increasing the oleogel content in the bigel-based foams exhibiting only a slight overall decreasing trend. This may be attributed to the thermal behavior of the bigel system is predominantly governed by the crystalline network of the oleogelator. Since the composition within the oleogel phase remained constant across all ratios, the intrinsic melting and crystallization points of the lipid network did not undergo significant shifts, presenting only a marginal decreasing trend. Notably, a single peak was observed during melting, suggesting the occurrence of a polymorphic eutectic during the melting process (Ma et al., 2024).Fig. 4DSC melting (a) and crystallization (b) behavior of 2O8W–8O2W. Based on Fig. 4b, two exothermic crystallization peaks are observed in the bigel-based foams, indicating the presence of a polycrystalline structure. During exothermic crystallization, initial peaks were observed at 45.91 ± 0.72 °C, 47.88 ± 0.59 °C, 46.73 ± 0.25 °C, 47.12 ± 0.66 °C, 46.69 ± 0.31 °C, 46.60 ± 0.18 °C, and 46.63 ± 0.73 °C for all bigel-based foam samples, respectively, followed by a second crystallization peak around 35 °C. Notably, similar to the melting process, the crystallization temperature also shows a slight overall decreasing trend as the content of oleogel in the bigel-based foams increases. This could be attributed to a change in the continuous phase of the system with increasing oleogel content. Such alterations in the continuous phase affect molecular mobility and crystallization behavior within the system, leading to the reduction in crystallization temperature. Compared to bigel-based foams, Liu et al. (2021) reported that the melting temperature of natural cream generally falls within the much lower range of 35–45 °C, which often leads to rapid structural collapse at ambient conditions. Notably, the melting temperature of the optimized bigel-based foams significantly exceeded that of natural whipping cream, indicating that the robust beeswax/gelatin network provides superior thermal stability, acting as an advantageous alternative for room-temperature structural retention.

Rheological properties of bigel-based foams

The incorporation of air during the whipping process transforms the biphasic bigel into a complex triphasic colloidal foam. To evaluate the structural integrity and stability of this newly formed aerated matrix, dynamic oscillatory rheology was performed. The viscoelastic properties of the aerated systems, characterized by the storage modulus (G′) and loss modulus (G″), provide crucial insights into their suitability as cream substitutes (Wang et al., 2025). Among all samples, 6O4W displays the highest G′. This suggests that the network structure of the bigel-based foams have the strongest non-covalent "physical" crosslinking at this specific ratio. The G′, also known as the elastic modulus, corresponds to the energy stored and recovered during sinusoidal oscillatory deformation, thus reflecting the elastic character of the material (Wang et al., 2020). Fig. 5a shows that the G′ generally increases with rising frequency, indicating that the samples exhibit a stronger elastic response at higher frequencies. Differences in the G′ curves among bigel-based foams with different oil-to-water ratios suggest that the oil-to-water ratio may regulate the material's elastic energy storage capability.Fig. 5Frequency sweep of bigel-based foams with different ratios. The G″, referred to as the viscous modulus, corresponds to the energy dissipated or lost per cycle under sinusoidal deformation, which reflects the viscous behavior of the material (Wang et al., 2020). Fig. 5b shows that the G″ monotonically increases with frequency, indicating that viscous dissipation in the bigel-based foams intensifies as frequency rises, meaning their energy dissipation capability enhances. Curves for different oil-to-water ratios show similar trends, but exhibit distinct differences in the high-frequency region. The variations in the high-frequency region suggest that the phase composition may influence the internal friction and viscous dissipation within the foam matrix. Similar viscoelastic mechanisms have been proposed in other biphasic gel systems, where the interfaces between the hydrogel and oleogel domains act as dynamic sites for structural rearrangement and energy dissipation under stress (Geremias-Andrade et al., 2016). The degree of interpenetration between the two phases determines the frictional resistance of the network during high-frequency oscillations (Alves Barroso et al., 2022). Furthermore, for all bigel-based foams, the G′ is higher than the G″, indicating that the samples consistently exhibit a gel-like state (Keskin Uslu and Yılmaz, 2021).

The loss tangent (tan δ), defined as the ratio G″/G′, represents the tangent of the phase shift angle δ between the stress and strain vectors. This ratio represents the material's damping capacity. Fig. 5c shows that tan δ decreases with increasing frequency, indicating that the bigel-based foams are predominantly viscous at low frequencies and gradually become more elastic-dominated at high frequencies. Notably, tan δ values differ significantly between samples at low frequencies, whereas at high frequencies, the curves for the various samples converge, and tan δ tends towards similar values. This suggests that the influence of the oil-to-water ratio on the material's viscoelastic behavior weakens at high frequencies, potentially dominated by inherent structural characteristics. A higher loss tangent indicates a greater viscosity ratio, suggesting that the system exhibits more fluid-like behavior. The results show that tan δ is less than 1 for bigel-based foams across all oil-to-water ratios, confirming that the systems possess a predominantly elastic, stable network capable of supporting their aerated structure (Chen et al., 2022).

For edible cream substitutes, rheological properties are critical indicators of macroscopic performance during application. Specifically, a higher G′ and yield stress ensure that the whipped cream can firmly hold its 3D extruded shape against gravity without collapsing. Conversely, an appropriate level of viscous behavior under high shear is required to guarantee smooth extrudability from a piping bag and a melt-in-the-mouth sensation. Compared to bigel-based foams, natural cream typically exhibits solid-like viscoelastic behavior, a characteristic highly dependent on its fragile partially coalesced fat globule network. The optimized biphasic bigel-based foams demonstrate highly similar viscoelastic parameters within this functional range. This indicates that the designed biphasic bigel network successfully mimics the required mechanical rigidity of natural cream while avoiding the heavy reliance on saturated milk fats.

Texture characteristics of bigel-based foams

The texture parameters of bigel-based foams were evaluated via TPA, with results shown in Table 2 (Alkabaa et al., 2024). Hardness corresponds to the peak force measured in the initial compression cycle. The hardness of all bigel-based foams was relatively low, which may be attributed to aeration during the whipping process. It is noteworthy that as the oleogel ratio increased, the hardness of the bigel-based foams initially showed a decreasing trend, reaching a minimum at 70% oleogel content. This initial decrease is due to the reduction in hydrogel content, which decreases hydrogen bonding and weakens the primary structural network of the O/W systems. This is consistent with the findings of Zampouni et al. (2023), who developed bigels using a gelatin and κ-carrageenan hydrogel with GMS in an olive oil oleogel. Singh et al. (2014) also noted that the hardness of guar gum blocks with sesame oil depends on the proportion of oleogel. However, at an 80% oleogel ratio, the hardness increased significantly to 90.108 ± 4.954 N. This resurgence in hardness may be attributed to the system's transition to a W/O type, where the continuous oil phase becomes more dominant. The higher concentration of beeswax and GMS in the 802W formulation likely facilitates a more rigid and densely packed crystalline network, providing greater structural support than the more diluted 703W system. Furthermore, the cohesiveness of bigel-based foams across different ratios ranged between 0.771 ± 0.037 and 0.808 ± 0.07, showing no significant differences. This high level of cohesiveness suggests that the bigel-based foams retained strong internal structural integrity during mechanical deformation. Springiness, which characterizes the rubbery sensation of bigel-based foams perceived in the mouth, reflects their elastic recovery capacity. The springiness of all bigel-based foams was about 1, which indicates that they have high elasticity. Both chewiness and resilience for all bigel-based foams exhibited trends similar to the hardness values. The textural profile of a cream directly dictates its handling characteristics and sensory mouthfeel. In practical applications, an adequate hardness is essential for providing structural support and resisting deformation when stacked between cake layers. Meanwhile, moderate adhesiveness and cohesiveness are highly desired, as they contribute to the excellent spreadability of the cream, preventing structural fracture during coating and ensuring a smooth, uniform layer on baked goods. Previous studies have indicated that natural cream typically exhibits a relatively low firmness comparable to that of bigel-based foams, due to its highly shear-sensitive and delicate partially coalesced fat network (Peng et al., 2018). Despite this similarity in general firmness, bigel-based foams exhibit significantly enhanced structural rigidity by comparison. This superior mechanical strength and shape retention capability effectively compensate for the shortcomings of natural cream, providing a distinct advantage in applications demanding high structural stability.

Color analysis of bigel-based foams

The color parameters (L∗, a∗, b∗) of the bigel-based foams are presented in Table 3. In the CIELab color space, L∗ is the luminance or lightness component, which ranges from 0 (black) to 100 (white) (León et al., 2006). The chromatic components are represented by a∗ (ranging from green to red) and b∗ (ranging from blue to yellow), both typically ranging from −120 to 120. Specifically, a positive a∗ value corresponds to redness, whereas a negative value indicates greenness; similarly, a positive b∗ value represents yellowness, while a negative value represents blueness (Pang et al., 2024). Through dynamic monitoring of the lightness index (L∗ value), the study found a subtle correlation between the light-dark variations in the bigel-based foams system and the component ratios. As the oil phase proportion increased, the L∗ value exhibited a decreasing trend, indicating that the samples reflected progressively less light (Zampouni et al., 2023). This difference may be attributed to the visual observation that bigel-based foams with a lower oil phase ratio appear relatively more transparent than those with a higher oil phase ratio. The higher oil phase ratio in bigel-based foams leads to a more compact internal structure. Consequently, less light is transmitted through the material to be reflected by the white background, resulting in a lower L∗ value (lightness). This optical behavior contrasts with that of bigels, where light transmission through the translucent hydrogel and scattering at the oleogel phase creates a combination of transmitted, reflected, and scattered light. The dependence of lightness on microstructure is further supported by the qualitative relationship between the apparent oil droplet size and the reflected lightness. This aligns with the established principle that the L∗ value of dispersed systems is governed by composition, microstructure, and droplet size (McClements, 2002). Consistent with this, the 208W sample, which exhibited qualitatively larger apparent oil droplets in the micrographs (Fig. 1c), showed the highest lightness value. As shown in Table 3, the a∗ value for the 208W sample was significantly higher than those of the other bigel-based foams. For aerated systems like bigel-based foams, the presence of air bubbles is a critical factor influencing color. The high refractive index contrast between the air bubbles and the surrounding gel matrix promotes intense light scattering at the gas-liquid interfaces, which significantly enhances the lightness and whiteness of the foams. Therefore, the observed variations in L∗ values are the collective result of the oil-to-water phase ratio, droplet size distribution, and the degree of aeration achieved during the whipping process. Additionally, an increase in the oil phase not only decreased the a∗ value but also increased the b∗ value, signifying that the bigel-based foams showed more green and yellow tones. This observation is also consistent with the changes in color parameters reported by Zheng et al. (2020) for bigels formulated using corn oil oleogels.

Color is the first sensory attribute perceived by consumers and plays a decisive role in the commercial viability of cream substitutes. Natural cream is characterized by a bright, opaque white appearance. Therefore, achieving a high lightness value (L∗) and whiteness index in the bigel-based foams is of great significance, as it closely mimics the visual appeal of real dairy products, thereby avoiding negative psychological rejection and enhancing overall consumer acceptance.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates that a whipped bigel system, based on beeswax/soybean oil oleogel and gelatin hydrogel, can be formulated into a bigel-based foam with desirable gel-like properties and enhanced thermal stability, serving as a novel cream alternative. Microstructural, rheological, and thermal analyses confirm that the product's characteristics can be effectively modulated by the oil-to-water ratio, with optimal sensory quality achieved at a specific formulation. These bigel-based foams provide a nutritious, low-saturated-fat alternative to natural cream, successfully balancing excellent whipping performance with high levels of unsaturated fatty acids and vitamins. The formation of the system relies on physical interactions between phases, and its biphasic coexistence offers a unique platform for the co-encapsulation of both hydrophilic and hydrophobic bioactive compounds. This work provides a practical and highly accessible formulation strategy for developing healthier and more sustainable food colloidal materials using common food-grade ingredients. The translation of these biphasic bigels into functional aerated foams represents a viable approach for cream replacement, whose industrial application and functional development merit further exploration.
