Abstract

Canonical polyadic decomposition (CPD) consists of expressing a tensor (multidimensional array) as a sum of several rank-1 tensors, each of which is an outer/separable product of vectors.
The number of rank-1 tensors used in a CPD is called the rank of the CPD, and the minimum possible rank of a CPD for a given tensor is called the rank of the tensor.
CPD is at the core of fast matrix multiplication, a computational problem with widespread implications across several seemingly unrelated problems in computer science.
Much recent progress in this field has used randomized heuristic search to find new CPDs, often over a finite field.
However, if these techniques fail to find a CPD with low enough rank, they cannot prove that no such CPD exists.
Consequently, these methods fail to resolve certain long-standing questions, such as whether the tensor corresponding to
3\times 3
matrix multiplication has rank less than 23.

To make progress on these problems, we develop a novel algorithm that preserves exactness, i.e. they can provably verify whether or not a given tensor has a specified rank.
Compared to brute force, when searching for a rank-
R
CPD of a
n_{0}\times\dots\times n_{D-1}
-shaped tensor over a finite field
\mathbb{F}
, where
n_{0}\geq\dots\geq n_{D-1}
, our algorithm saves a multiplicative factor of roughly
|\mathbb{F}|^{R(n_{0}-1)+n_{0}(\sum_{d\geq 1}n_{d})}
.
Additionally, our algorithm runs in polynomial time.
We also find a novel algorithm to search border CPDs, a variant of CPDs that is also important in fast matrix multiplication.

Finally, we study the maximum rank problem and give new upper and lower bounds, both for families of tensor shapes and specific shapes.
Although our CPD search algorithms are still too slow to resolve the rank of
3\times 3
matrix multiplication, we are able to utilize them in this problem by adding extra search pruners that do not affect exactness or increase asymptotic running time.

Introduction

Given a tensor (multidimensional array)
T\in{\reflectbox{$\mathcal{R}$}}^{n_{0}\times\dots\times n_{D-1}}
over a ground ring

\mathcal{R}

, a
rank-
R
canonical polyadic decomposition (CPD)
of
T
is a list of matrices
A_{d}\in{\reflectbox{$\mathcal{R}$}}^{n_{d}\times R},\ 0\leq d<D
such that

T=\begin{bmatrix}\sum_{0\leq r<R}\prod_{0\leq d<D}(A_{d})_{i_{d},r}\end{bmatrix}_{i_{0},\dots,i_{D-1}},

i.e. the
(i_{0},\dots,i_{D-1})
entry of
T
is
\sum_{0\leq r<R}\prod_{0\leq d<D}(A_{d})_{i_{d},r}
.

We call the
A_{d}
“factor matrices” and abbreviate the right-hand side as
\left[\left[A_{0},\dots,A_{D-1}\right]\right]
. Note that this expression is also equal to
\sum_{r}\bigotimes_{d}(A_{d})_{:,r}
, where
\otimes
denotes the tensor product.

The notation we use for tensors is typical in computer science.
In algebraic geometry, tensors are typically notated as multilinear forms over formal variables.
Specifically, the above tensor
T
would be the
D
-linear form
\sum_{i_{0},\dots,i_{D-1}}T_{i_{0},\dots,i_{D-1}}(\alpha_{0,i_{0}}\cdots\alpha_{D-1,i_{D-1}})
for formal variables
\alpha_{d,i_{d}}
, and the CPD expression
\left[\left[A_{0},\dots,A_{D-1}\right]\right]
would be
\sum_{r}\left(\sum_{i_{0}}(A_{0})_{i_{0},r}\alpha_{0,i_{0}}\right)\cdots\left(\sum_{i_{D-1}}(A_{D-1})_{i_{D-1,r}}\alpha_{D-1,i_{D-1}}\right)
.

The rank of
T
, denoted
\operatorname{rk}_{\reflectbox{$\mathcal{R}$}}\left(T\right)
, is the smallest
R
such that there exists a rank-
R
CPD of
T
over the ring

\mathcal{R}

. Determining tensor rank is the central problem underlying fast matrix multiplication
[
8
]
. Formally, the action of multiplying a
m\times k
matrix with a
k\times n
matrix can be represented as a
mk\times kn\times nm
tensor commonly denoted
\left\langle m,k,n\right\rangle

2
2

2

Typically,
\left\langle m,k,n\right\rangle
represents the action of matrix multiplication followed by a transpose, which makes certain symmetries of the tensor easier to notate. This does not change the rank of this tensor, since applying a transpose to the output is equivalent to permuting the slices of
\left\langle m,k,n\right\rangle
along axis 2 (the
nm
-length axis).
; then a rank-
R
CPD of this tensor can be converted into a divide-and-conquer algorithm for multiplying two
N\times N
matrices in
O(N^{3\log_{mkn}R})
time. The quantity
3\log_{mkn}R
is known as the
running time exponent
of such a CPD. The famous Strassen algorithm
[
38
]
corresponds to a rank-7 CPD of
\left\langle 2,2,2\right\rangle
.
Furthermore, every fast matrix multiplication algorithm corresponds to a CPD of some
\left\langle m,k,n\right\rangle
, as long as the algorithm is restricted to arithmetic operations
[
8
]
.

The asymptotically fastest known algorithm for matrix multiplication
[
2
]
and its predecessors correspond to CPDs of very large
\left\langle m,k,n\right\rangle
tensors; this is a consequence of applying several algebraic techniques, each of which produces a
sequence
of CPDs whose running time exponents converge to some limit.
As a result, the constant factors of such algorithms render them impractical
[
8
]
, despite new developments to mitigate this issue
[
4
]
.
Furthermore, it is known that the optimal running time exponent must be a limit, i.e. it cannot be achieved by any single CPD
[
15
]
.

An alternative approach to fast matrix multiplication is to directly find low-rank CPDs of small
\left\langle m,k,n\right\rangle
tensors, which will yield suboptimal running time exponents but hopefully manageable constant factors. Much work has been done in this direction using computer search
[
36
,
16
,
20
,
18
,
23
,
24
,
6
,
17
]
, which we detail in Section
2
.
We are most interested in the latter approach to fast matrix multiplication, due to impractical constant factors in the former approach. However, we deviate from previous research in two ways:

1.

We restrict ourselves to
exact
algorithms for finding low-rank CPDs. Although there has been exciting progress with heuristic search methods in fast matrix multiplication
[
16
,
36
,
20
,
18
,
23
,
31
]
, these methods still have not resolved questions such as whether
\operatorname{rk}(\left\langle 3,3,3\right\rangle)<23
, which has been open since 1976
[
25
]
.

2.

We generalize to arbitrary tensors, not just those for matrix multiplication. Our reasons for doing so are: to find general search optimizations in tensor CPD that may have been overlooked; and to make our algorithms applicable to problems besides matrix multiplication that use CPD, such as the light bulb problem
[
5
]
and dynamic programming
[
3
]
.

To ensure an exact algorithm is possible in principle, we restrict the ground ring to be a finite field, so that the search space is finite. Doing so is not too strong of a handicap, as some previous work
[
18
,
23
,
24
]
has successfully lifted novel CPDs of matrix multiplication tensors from a finite field to the integers.

It is likely impossible to solve tensor rank in polynomial time, as tensor rank is NP-hard over the finite fields (for arbitrary tensors)
[
19
]
.
However, we are still interested in improving the runtime as much as we can.
Our main result, developed over the course of
[
40
,
41
]
, is:

Theorem 1
.

Given a concise tensor
T\in\mathbb{F}^{n_{0}\times\dots\times n_{D-1}}
, finding a rank-
R
CPD of
T
or determining that none exists can be done in
O^{*}\left(|\mathbb{F}|^{(R-n_{0})(\sum_{d\geq 1}n_{d})\ +\ \min\left(R,\ \sum_{d\geq 2}n_{d}\right)}\right)
time and
O^{*}(1)
space.

We explain our proof of Theorem
1
and the definition of “concise” in Section
3.1
.
We also detail several search pruning strategies that significantly speed up our algorithm in practice.

Compared to brute force, which would run in
O^{*}\left(|\mathbb{F}|^{R(\sum_{d}n_{d})}\right)
time, Theorem
1
decreases the exponent by
Rn_{0}+n_{0}(\sum_{d\geq 1}n_{d})-\min(R,\sum_{d\geq 2}n_{d})
; for large enough
D
and
n_{d}
, this simplifies to
R(n_{0}-1)+n_{0}(\sum_{d\geq 1}n_{d})
.

We also study
border
CPDs, which are essentially CPDs over the polynomial ring
\mathbb{F}[x]/(x^{H})
for some positive integer
H
, and are equally important as ordinary CPDs in terms of minimizing the asymptotic complexity of fast matrix multiplication (more detail in Section
3.2
). Over this ring we achieve the following:

Theorem 2
.

Given a concise tensor
T\in\left(\mathbb{F}[x]/(x^{H})\right)^{n_{0}\times\dots\times n_{D-1}}
, finding a rank-
R
CPD of
T
or determining that none exists can be done in
O^{*}\left(|\mathbb{F}|^{H\sum_{1\leq r\leq R}\sum_{d}\min(r,n_{d})}\right)
time and
O^{*}(1)
space.

Compared to brute force, which would take
O^{*}\left(|\mathbb{F}|^{HR\sum_{d}n_{d}}\right)
time, Theorem
2
decreases the exponent term by roughly
\frac{1}{2}H\sum_{d}n_{d}^{2}
.

Finally, in Section
4
we improve existing bounds on the maximum possible rank of a tensor with a given shape, some with the help of our algorithm for Theorem
1
.

Previous work

To our knowledge, there are no previously known algorithms that find CPDs of arbitrary tensors with guaranteed correctness, besides Gröbner bases, which solves general systems of polynomial equations.
For CPDs over
\mathbb{R}
, there are some practical numerical methods (e.g. Jennrich’s algorithm) that work for “nice” tensors but not all.

Much previous work in this field has focused on finding CPDs of matrix multiplication (MM) tensors. This work can be divided into two camps: the first uses algebraic methods to indirectly construct CPDs for (very large) MM tensors, in order to reduce the MM exponent
\omega
by any means necessary; the second directly searches for CPDs of small MM tensors.

A good overview of the algebraic camp for MM is given in
[
8
]
. For the search camp, we give a list of such approaches that we previously wrote in
[
41
]
:

•

By hand: Strassen’s algorithm was extended by Hopcroft and Kerr
[
21
]
to prove
\operatorname{rk}(\left\langle m,2,n\right\rangle)\leq\left\lceil\frac{3mn+\max\left\{m,n\right\}}{2}\right\rceil
.
Laderman
[
25
]
proved
\operatorname{rk}(\left\langle 3,3,3\right\rangle)\leq 23
, which today is still the best known upper bound for
\left\langle 3,3,3\right\rangle
. Many other upper bounds of small
\left\langle m,k,n\right\rangle
are listed on Sedoglavic’s table
[
35
]
.

However, the most successful manual approach has been Pan’s trilinear aggregation
[
33
,
34
]
, consisting of several upper bounds on
\operatorname{rk}(\left\langle n,n,n\right\rangle)
. The lowest running time exponent obtained by this approach is
\approx 2.7734
, from the rank bound
\operatorname{rk}(\left\langle 44,44,44\right\rangle)\leq 36\ 133
.

•

Numerical optimization: Smirnov
[
36
]
converts tensor CPD into an optimization problem whose objective is to minimize the sum-of-squares error between the target tensor
T
and a CPD
\left[\left[A,B,C\right]\right]
of fixed rank. The primary optimization technique is
alternating least squares
, where one cycles through each factor matrix
A,B,C
, and analytically minimizes the objective with respect to that matrix while fixing all other factor matrices. Together with clever regularization, along with post-processing (to convert a sufficiently accurate CPD into an exact CPD), Smirnov proved
\operatorname{rk}(\left\langle 3,3,6\right\rangle)\leq 40
over the rationals (running time exponent
\approx 2.7743
), nearly surpassing trilinear aggregation
[
34
]
.

•

Boolean SAT: several works, such as Courtois et. al.
[
16
]
and Heule et. al.
[
20
]
, have searched for CPDs over the finite field
\mathbb{F}_{2}
by formulating the problem as boolean SAT, specifically to try finding a rank-22 CPD of
\left\langle 3,3,3\right\rangle
. Although unsuccessful, thousands of new rank-23 CPDs were discovered (over
\mathbb{F}_{2}
) that are inequivalent to Laderman’s CPD.

•

Reinforcement learning: Fawzi et. al.
[
18
]
(AlphaTensor) formulates tensor CPD as a single-player game where one starts with a given tensor, a move consists of subtracting away a rank-1 tensor, and the goal is to end with all-zeros in as few moves as possible.
Using a neural network together with Monte-Carlo tree search, several new CPDs were found, including
\operatorname{rk}(\left\langle 3,4,5\right\rangle)\leq 47
over the integers (running time exponent
\approx 2.8211
) and
\operatorname{rk}(\left\langle 4,4,4\right\rangle)\leq 47
over
\mathbb{F}_{2}
(running time exponent
\approx 2.7773
); the latter result could not be lifted to the integers.

•

Flip graphs: Kauers and Moosbauer
[
23
,
24
]
begin with an arbitrary CPD
T=\sum_{r}a_{r}\otimes b_{r}\otimes c_{r}
, then (when possible) iteratively apply the following “flip” transformation to a randomly chosen pair of summands with matching factors in the first axis,

a\otimes b\otimes c+a\otimes b^{\prime}\otimes c^{\prime}

\rightarrow a\otimes(b+b^{\prime})\otimes c+a\otimes b^{\prime}\otimes(-c+c^{\prime}),

or an equivalent transformation with the tensor axes and the order of the summands permuted.
It is worth noting that this is essentially an elementary row operation in a matrix factorization.

After each iteration, a CPD is also “reduced” when possible (detected using some conditions involving linear span) so that its rank decreases by 1.

Kauers and Moosbauer first proved
\operatorname{rk}_{\mathbb{F}_{2}}(\left\langle 5,5,5\right\rangle)\leq 95
by initializing their search at the rank-96 CPD discovered by AlphaTensor
[
18
]
.
In a subsequent paper
[
24
]
, the authors proved
\operatorname{rk}(\left\langle 2,6,6\right\rangle)\leq 56
over the integers by lifting a CPD from
\mathbb{F}_{2}
, marking the first improvement on
\left\langle 2,6,6\right\rangle
since Hopcroft and Kerr
[
21
]
.

Further modifications of this technique, such as adaptive flip graphs
[
6
]
and flip graphs with symmetry
[
31
]
have given stronger bounds.

•

Constraint programming: closest to the spirit of our work, Deza et. al.
[
17
]
use the IBM ILOG CP Optimizer to exhaustively search for CPDs with elements in
\left\{-1,0,1\right\}
, with symmetry-breaking constraints added that do not affect correctness (e.g., forcing lexicographic order among the summands
a_{r}\otimes b_{r}\otimes c_{r}
). Although the authors do not find new upper bounds on tensor rank, they do recover the previously known bounds
\operatorname{rk}(\left\langle 2,2,2\right\rangle)\leq 7
and
\operatorname{rk}(\left\langle 2,2,3\right\rangle)\leq 11
.

A table of upper bounds on
\operatorname{rk}(\left\langle m,n,k\right\rangle)
for many (thousands of) small
\left\langle m,n,k\right\rangle
is given in
[
35
]
.
Despite this progress, the lowest known MM exponent that comes from a small tensor is still Pan’s latest trilinear aggregation result
[
34
]
from 1982, and its small improvement over Strassen’s algorithm is likely outweighed by increased constant factors.

T:=\begin{bmatrix}f(i_{0},\dots,i_{D-1})\end{bmatrix}_{i_{0},\dots,i_{D-1}}
means that the
(i_{0},\dots,i_{D-1})

T
is
f(i_{0},\dots,i_{D-1})

•

Tensor slices are denoted with NumPy notation, and indices are 0-indexed.

–

e.g., for a two-dimensional tensor
A
:
A_{0,:}
is the first (topmost) row of
A
; and
A_{:,:c}
denotes the submatrix containing all rows and the first (leftmost)
c

•

The tensor product of
A\in{\reflectbox{$\mathcal{R}$}}^{n_{0}\times\dots\times n_{D-1}}

B\in{\reflectbox{$\mathcal{R}$}}^{s_{0}\times\dots\times s_{E-1}}

The chain tensor product
A_{0}\otimes\dots\otimes A_{n-1}

\bigotimes_{0\leq i<n}A_{i}
.

•

The axis-
d

T\in{\reflectbox{$\mathcal{R}$}}^{n_{0}\times\dots\times n_{D-1}}
by
M\in{\reflectbox{$\mathcal{R}$}}^{n^{\prime}_{d}\times n_{d}}
is

M\times_{d}T:=\begin{bmatrix}\sum_{i_{d}}M_{i^{\prime}_{d},i_{d}}T_{{}_{i_{0},\dots,i_{D-1}}}\end{bmatrix}_{i_{0},\dots,i_{d-1},i^{\prime}_{d},i_{d+1},\dots,i_{D-1}}.

•

A rank-

\left[\left[A_{0},\dots,A_{D-1}\right]\right]:=\sum_{r}\bigotimes_{d}(A_{d})_{:,r}
.

•

\operatorname{rref}\left(M\right)
denotes the reduced row echelon form of matrix
M
.

•

\operatorname{GL}\left(n,\ \mathbb{F}\right)
denotes the set of invertible

\mathbb{F}
.

3
Algorithms

Here we describe our algorithms for both standard CPD and border CPD, which were cited in our previous works
[
41
]
and
[
40
]
, respectively. The key insight we use is to extract information about the input tensor
T
via axis-operations:

M\times_{d}T:=\begin{bmatrix}\sum_{i_{d}}M_{i^{\prime}_{d},i_{d}}T_{{}_{i_{0},\dots,i_{D-1}}}\end{bmatrix}_{i_{0},\dots,i_{d-1},i^{\prime}_{d},i_{d+1},\dots,i_{D-1}},

defined for a suitably sized matrix
M
(see notation in Section
2.1
).

The following relation between axis-operations and CPDs is the crux of our algorithm and is easy to check by hand:

M\times_{d}\left[\left[A_{0},\dots,A_{D-1}\right]\right]=\left[\left[A_{0},\dots,A_{d-1},MA_{d},A_{d+1},\dots,A_{D-1}\right]\right].

A consequence is that applying an axis contraction on a tensor cannot increase its rank.

3.1
CPD DFS

When
T
has elements in a field
\mathbb{F}
, we can reduce
T
in a manner similar to row reduction for matrices. More precisely, for each axis
d
:

•

unfold
T
into the matrix
T_{\left(d\right)}:=\left[\begin{array}[]{c}\vdots\\
\hline\cr\operatorname{vec}\left(T_{\cdots,:,i_{d},:,\cdots}\right)\\
\hline\cr\vdots\end{array}\right]_{i_{d}}
;

•

find some invertible matrix
Q
s.t.
QT_{\left(d\right)}=\operatorname{rref}\left(T_{\left(d\right)}\right)
;

•

and replace
T
with
Q\times_{d}T
.

Afterwards, remove any all-zeros slices from
T
; doing so does not change
\operatorname{rk}(T)
.

Since each such
Q
can be constructed in polynomial time using Gaussian elimination,
and any rank-
R
CPD of the original tensor
T
can be converted into a CPD of the new
T
(and vice versa) by applying axis-operations,
we can assume without loss of generality (WLOG) that the input tensor
T
with shape
n_{0}\times\dots\times n_{D-1}
satisfies
\operatorname{rk}(T_{(d)})=n_{d}
; such a tensor is called
concise
:

Definition 1
.

A tensor
T\in\mathbb{F}^{n_{0}\times\dots\times n_{D-1}}
is concise if for each axis
d
, the unfolding
T_{\left(d\right)}:=\left[\begin{array}[]{c}\vdots\\
\hline\cr\operatorname{vec}\left(T_{\cdots,:,i_{d},:,\cdots}\right)\\
\hline\cr\vdots\end{array}\right]_{i_{d}}
satisfies
\operatorname{rk}(T_{\left(d\right)})=n_{d}
, i.e.
T_{\left(d\right)}
has full row rank.

It is also clear that if a concise tensor
T
with rank
\leq R
must have each side length be
\leq R
:

Lemma 1
.

If
T\in\mathbb{F}^{n_{0}\times\dots\times n_{D-1}}
is concise and
\operatorname{rk}(T)\leq R
, then
\forall d:n_{d}\leq R
.

Proof.

Suppose
T=\left[\left[A_{0},\dots,A_{D-1}\right]\right]
is a rank-
R
CPD. Then unfolding along axis
d
yields the relation
T_{(d)}=A_{d}\left[\begin{array}[]{c}\vdots\\
\hline\cr\operatorname{vec}\left(\otimes_{d^{\prime}\neq d}(A_{d^{\prime}})_{:,r}\right)\\
\hline\cr\vdots\end{array}\right]_{r}
, implying
\operatorname{rk}(T_{(d)})\leq R
.
∎

An immediate consequence is that finding a rank-1 CPD is easy:

Lemma 2
.

Given an arbitrary tensor
T\in\mathbb{F}^{n_{0}\times\dots\times n_{D-1}}
, returning a rank-1 CPD of
T
or determining that no such CPD exists can be done in
O^{*}(1)
time.

Proof.

WLOG assume
T
is concise; then
\operatorname{rk}(T)\leq 1
if and only if
\forall d:\ n_{d}\neq 1
.
∎

Now we describe the main algorithm of Theorem
1
.
Suppose a concise tensor
T
has a rank-
R
CPD
\left[\left[A_{0},\dots,A_{D-1}\right]\right]
. By performing row reduction on
A_{0}
, there exists some
Q\in\operatorname{GL}\left(n_{0},\ \mathbb{F}\right)
such that
QA_{0}=\operatorname{rref}\left(A_{0}\right)
. Since
T
is concise,
\operatorname{rk}(A_{0})=n_{0}
, so
\operatorname{rref}\left(A_{0}\right)=\left[\begin{array}[]{c|c}I_{n_{0}}&C\end{array}\right]
for some
C
, up to simultaneous permutation of columns of all
A_{d}
.

Consider isolating the
I_{n_{0}}
portion as follows:

Q\times_{0}T=\left[\left[I_{n_{0}},(A_{1})_{:,:n_{0}},\dots,(A_{D-1})_{:,:n_{0}}\right]\right]+\left[\left[C,(A_{1})_{:,n_{0}:},\dots,(A_{D-1})_{:,n_{0}:}\right]\right]

\Leftrightarrow Q\times_{0}T-\left[\left[C,(A_{1})_{:,n_{0}:},\dots,(A_{D-1})_{:,n_{0}:}\right]\right]=\left[\left[I_{n_{0}},(A_{1})_{:,:n_{0}},\dots,(A_{D-1})_{:,:n_{0}}\right]\right]

\Leftrightarrow\widetilde{Q}\times_{0}\widetilde{T}=\left[\left[I_{n_{0}},(A_{1})_{:,:n_{0}},\dots,(A_{D-1})_{:,:n_{0}}\right]\right],

where
\widetilde{Q}=\begin{bmatrix}Q|C\end{bmatrix}
,
\widetilde{T}_{:n_{0},:,\cdots}=T
, and
\widetilde{T}_{r,:,\cdots}=-\bigotimes_{d\geq 1}(A_{d})_{:,r}
for
n_{0}\leq r<R
.

The right-hand side of the last equation is a tensor with shape
n_{0}\times\dots\times n_{D-1}
whose
i
-th slice along axis 0 is
\otimes_{d\geq 1}(A_{d})_{:,i}
, since the
i
-th row of
I_{n_{0}}
has a one at column
i
and zeros everywhere else.
Because each of these slices is a rank-1 tensor and uses a disjoint subset of unknowns, the last equation is equivalent to

\forall 0\leq i<n_{0}:\ \operatorname{rk}((\widetilde{Q}\times_{0}\widetilde{T})_{i,:,\cdots})\leq 1

\Leftrightarrow\forall 0\leq i<n_{0}:\ \operatorname{rk}(\widetilde{Q}_{i,:}\times_{0}\widetilde{T})\leq 1.

Suppose we knew
\widetilde{T}
and just had to solve for
\widetilde{Q}
; then the following conditions on
\widetilde{Q}
are necessary and sufficient:

•

each row of
\widetilde{Q}

3.1.1
Pruners

Algorithm
1
can be implemented in a recursive depth-first search fashion, by fixing each tuple of columns
((A_{d})_{:,r})_{d\geq 1}
for each
r
at a time, e.g.,
r=n_{0},\dots,R-1
.
By examining more closely what we fix these variables to, we can prune some subtrees of the search space and achieve significant practical speedups.

Suppose we have currently fixed
((A_{d})_{:,n_{0}:r})_{d\geq 1}
for some
n_{0}\leq r\leq R
: we can take the condition
\left(\forall i:\operatorname{rk}\left((\widetilde{Q}_{i,:}\times_{0}\widetilde{T})\leq 1\right)\right)
that our algorithm would check if all
((A_{d})_{:,n_{0}:})_{d\geq 1}
were fixed, isolate the fixed variables from the indeterminate variables as much as possible, then weaken the condition so that only the fixed variables remain.
The remaining expression is what we call a “pruning condition” or “pruner”.
We will call a pruner “practical” if its running time is
O^{*}(|\mathbb{F}|^{O(R+\sum_{d}n_{d})})
, i.e. the exponent is at most linear in the rank threshold
R
and shape lengths
n_{d}
of the tensor
T
.

We avoid applying pruners in base cases
r=R
, since Algorithm
1
already resolves each of those search states in
O^{*}(|\mathbb{F}|^{R})
time.

In this section, we list three pruners, all of which we use in our Java implementation of Algorithm
1
. All three pruners are practical if
D=3
, which is the case we are most interested in.

Lemma 3

(“Rref-pruning”)
.

Let
T\in\mathbb{F}^{n_{0}\times\dots\times n_{D-1}}
be a concise tensor, and suppose we have fixed thte submatrices
((A_{d})_{:,n_{0}:r})_{d\geq 1}
for some
n_{0}\leq r\leq R
. If there exists a rank-
R
CPD
T=\left[\left[A_{0},\dots,A_{D-1}\right]\right]
that is consistent with what we have fixed, then the set

S^{\prime}:=\left\{v\in\mathbb{F}^{1\times r}:\ \operatorname{rk}(v\times_{0}T^{\prime})\leq R-r+1\right\},

where
T^{\prime}
is the augmented tensor such that
T^{\prime}_{:n_{0},:,\cdots}=T
and
T^{\prime}_{r^{\prime},:,\cdots}=-\bigotimes_{d\geq 1}(A_{d})_{:,r^{\prime}}
for
n_{0}\leq r^{\prime}<r
,
contains
n_{0}
many vectors
v_{0},\dots,v_{n_{0}-1}
such that the matrix
\left[\begin{array}[]{c}\vdots\\
\hline\cr(v_{i})_{:,:n_{0}}\\
\hline\cr\vdots\end{array}\right]_{i}
has rank
n_{0}
.

Proof.

Let
\widetilde{T}
be the augmented tensor such that
\widetilde{T}_{:n_{0},:,\cdots}=T
and
\widetilde{T}_{r^{\prime},:,\cdots}=-\bigotimes_{d\geq 1}(A_{d})_{:,r^{\prime}}
for
n_{0}\leq r^{\prime}<R
.

Using similar reasoning as in Section
3.1
, a CPD exists if and only
S:=\left\{v\in\mathbb{F}^{1\times R}:\ \operatorname{rk}(v\times_{0}\widetilde{T})\leq 1\right\}
contains
n_{0}
many vectors
v_{0},\dots,v_{n_{0}-1}
such that the matrix
\left[\begin{array}[]{c}\vdots\\
\hline\cr(v_{i})_{:,:n_{0}}\\
\hline\cr\vdots\end{array}\right]_{i}
has rank
n_{0}
.

We want to separate the fixed portion
(A_{d})_{:,n_{0}:r}
of each factor matrix from the unfixed portion
(A_{d})_{:,r:}
. To do this, we rewrite
v\times_{0}\widetilde{T}
as
v_{:,:r}\times_{0}\widetilde{T}_{:r,:,\cdots}+v_{:,r:}\times_{0}\widetilde{T}_{r:,:,\cdots}
,
then note that
having
\operatorname{rk}(v\times_{0}\widetilde{T})\leq 1
implies
\operatorname{rk}(v_{:,:r}\times_{0}\widetilde{T}_{:r,:,\cdots})\leq R-r+1
.
Finally, since
T^{\prime}=\widetilde{T}_{:r,:,\cdots}
, we have
S\subseteq S^{\prime}
.

Since

, any satisfying subset of vectors
\left\{v_{i}\right\}_{i}\subseteq S
would yield a satisfying subset
\left\{(v_{i})_{:,:r}\right\}_{i}\in S^{\prime}
, since
\left((v_{i})_{:,:r}\right)_{:,:n_{0}}=(v_{i})_{:,:n_{0}}
.
∎

We call Lemma
3
“rref-pruning”, since it indirectly relies on the fact that the factor matrix

has a reduced row-echelon form. When

, rref-pruning can be implemented to run in

time and

space, since constructing

consists of evaluating the ranks of many matrices, and we can generate elements of

on the fly in a similar manner as in Algorithm

[

:

Theorem 3

(
[
28
]
)
.

If a concise tensor
T\in\mathbb{F}^{n_{0}\times\dots\times n_{D-1}}
has rank
\leq R
, then
s:=\sum_{v\in\mathbb{F}^{1\times n_{0}}}\operatorname{rk}(v\times_{0}T)
is at most
R(|\mathbb{F}|^{n_{0}}-|\mathbb{F}|^{n_{0}-1})
.

Proof.

Let
\left[\left[A_{0},\dots,A_{D-1}\right]\right]
be a rank-

CPD of
T
.
Then
v\times_{0}T=\left[\left[vA_{0},A_{1},\dots,A_{D-1}\right]\right]
,
so
\operatorname{rk}(v\times_{0}T)
is at most the number of nonzeros in

, which we denote as

We can upper-bound the sum

with

s\leq\sum_{v\in\mathbb{F}^{1\times n_{0}}}\#_{\neq 0}(vA_{0})

=\sum_{v\in\mathbb{F}^{1\times n_{0}},\ 0\leq r<R}1[v(A_{0})_{:,r}\neq 0]

=\sum_{0\leq r<R}|\mathbb{F}|^{n_{0}}-\left|\textrm{left\_nullspace}((A_{0})_{:,r})\right|;

since
\operatorname{rk}\left((A_{0})_{:,r}\right)\leq 1
, the rank-nullity theorem implies
\operatorname{dim}\left(\textrm{left\_nullspace}((A_{0})_{:,r})\right)\geq n_{0}-1
, so

s\leq\sum_{0\leq r<R}|\mathbb{F}|^{n_{0}}-|\mathbb{F}|^{n_{0}-1}=R(|\mathbb{F}|^{n_{0}}-|\mathbb{F}|^{n_{0}-1}).

∎

Lemma 4

(“Lask-pruning”)
.

Let
T\in\mathbb{F}^{n_{0}\times\dots\times n_{D-1}}
be a concise tensor, and suppose we have fixed
((A_{d})_{i,:})_{d\geq 1}
. If there exists a rank-
R
CPD
T=\left[\left[A_{0},\dots,A_{D-1}\right]\right]
that is consistent with what we have fixed, then

\sum_{v\in\mathbb{F}^{1\times n_{0}}}\min_{w\in\mathbb{F}^{1\times(r-n_{0})}}\operatorname{rk}\left(v\times_{0}T\ -\ \left[\left[w,(A_{1})_{:,n_{0}:r},\dots\right]\right]\right)\leq(R-(r-n_{0}))(|\mathbb{F}|^{n_{0}}-|\mathbb{F}|^{n_{0}-1}).

Proof.

There must exist some assignment of
(A_{0})_{:,n_{0}:r}
such that
\operatorname{rk}\left(T-\left[\left[\dots,(A_{d})_{:,n_{0}:r},\dots_{d}\right]\right]\right)\leq R-(r-n_{0})
. Applying Theorem
3
directly yields

s:=\min_{(A_{0})_{:,n_{0}:r}\in\mathbb{F}^{n_{0}\times(r-n_{0})}}\sum_{v\in\mathbb{F}^{1\times n_{0}}}\operatorname{rk}\left(v\times_{0}\left(T-\left[\left[\dots,(A_{d})_{:,n_{0}:r},\dots_{d}\right]\right]\right)\right)\leq(R-(r-n_{0}))(|\mathbb{F}|^{n_{0}}-|\mathbb{F}|^{n_{0}-1}).

Iterating over all possible
(A_{0})_{:,n_{0}:r}
would be too slow, so we look for a lower bound of the left-hand side that is easier to compute.
Expanding
v\times_{0}\left(T-\left[\left[\dots,(A_{d})_{:,n_{0}:r},\dots_{d}\right]\right]\right)
yields

v\times_{0}\left(T-\left[\left[\dots,(A_{d})_{:,n_{0}:r},\dots_{d}\right]\right]\right)=v\times_{0}T\ -\ \left[\left[v(A_{0})_{:,n_{0}:r},(A_{1})_{:,n_{0}:r},\dots\right]\right];

notice that
v(A_{0})_{:,n_{0}:r}
is a

vector, so it does not contain too many possible values. Consider replacing this with an arbitrary

vector that has complete freedom relative to
v
and
(A_{0})_{:,n_{0}:r}
; then

s\geq\sum_{v\in\mathbb{F}^{1\times n_{0}}}\min_{w\in\mathbb{F}^{1\times(r-n_{0})}}\operatorname{rk}\left(v\times_{0}T\ -\ \left[\left[w,(A_{1})_{:,n_{0}:r},\dots\right]\right]\right).

∎

We call Lemma

“Laskowski-pruning” or “lask-pruning” for short, named after the author of
[

time and
O^{*}(1)
space in the case

.

Both of the aforementioned pruners are “negative”, meaning they might prove whether a given search state is infeasible. In contrast, our third pruner is “positive”, meaning it might find a solution to a search state much faster than Algorithm

•

•

, rref-pruning proves

.

•

For

Other pruners are possible, some of which we list here for the case

; but we did not use them, either because they were too slow or because they did not seem to help in finding CPDs for some hand-picked tensors.

In all examples,

Lemma 5

(“

-th order rref-pruning”)
.

For any
1\leq k\leq n_{0}
, the set

\left\{v_{0}\wedge\dots\wedge v_{k-1}:\ v_{0},\dots,v_{k-1}\in\mathbb{F}^{1\times n_{0}},\ \operatorname{rk}\left(\left[\begin{array}[]{c}v_{0}\\
\hline\cr\vdots\\
\hline\cr v_{k-1}\end{array}\right]\times_{0}T\right)\leq R-n_{0}+k\right\}\subseteq\mathbb{F}^{\overbrace{n_{0}\times\dots\times n_{0}}^{k}}

spans all of
\mathbb{F}^{\overbrace{n_{0}\times\dots\times n_{0}}^{k}}
,
where
v_{0}\wedge\dots\wedge v_{k-1}:=\sum_{\sigma\in\operatorname{Sym}(k)}\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma)\bigotimes_{0\leq i<k}v_{\sigma(i)}
is (isomorphic to) the wedge product in exterior algebra.

Lemma 6

(“frequency-pruning”)
.

For any
1\leq k\leq n_{0}
,
\left|\left\{v\in\mathbb{F}^{1\times n_{0}}:\ \operatorname{rk}(v\times_{0}T)\leq R-n_{0}+k\right\}\right|\geq|\mathbb{F}|^{k}
.

3.2
Border CPD

We now present our search algorithm for border CPD, a looser form of CPD that is essential for most progress on fast matrix multiplication. Most of the text in this subsection is duplicated from
[
40
]
.

3.2.1
Motivation and Definitions

Intuitively, a border CPD is a parameterized CPD that approximates a target tensor
T
arbitrarily well. Such a CPD may have a strictly smaller rank than
\operatorname{rk}\left(T\right)
: a famous example is
T=\begin{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}0&1\\
1&0\end{bmatrix}&\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\
0&0\end{bmatrix}\end{bmatrix}
, which has rank 3 but is approximated by the rank-2 border CPD
\frac{1}{x}\left(\begin{bmatrix}1\\
x\end{bmatrix}^{\otimes 3}-\begin{bmatrix}1\\
0\end{bmatrix}^{\otimes 3}\right)
as
x\rightarrow 0
.

To formally define border CPDs, we multiply everything by a high enough power of
x
so that everything is a polynomial in
x
. Define the following for a tensor with elements in
\mathbb{F}

[
8
]
:

•

\underline{\operatorname{rk}}_{H}(T):=\operatorname{rk}(x^{H-1}T)
over the polynomial ring
\mathbb{F}[x]/(x^{H})
; we call
H
the
exponent threshold
.

•

\underline{\operatorname{rk}}\left(T\right):=\min_{H\geq 1}\underline{\operatorname{rk}}_{H}(T)
; this is the
border rank
of
T
.

Border rank is useful in fast MM because one can essentially pretend it is regular rank when bounding the running time exponent
\omega
: a border rank-
R
CPD of
\left\langle m,n,k\right\rangle
for any exponent threshold
H
can be converted into an
O(N^{(3\log_{mkn}R)+\varepsilon})
-time algorithm for
N\times N
matrix multiplication, for arbitrarily small
\varepsilon>0

[
8
]
.

3.2.2
Preliminaries

To make the border CPD search problem finite, we fix the exponent threshold
H
. We also generalize the problem to finding the CPD rank of an arbitrary tensor over the ring
\mathbb{F}[x]/(x^{H})
, i.e., tensor elements are not restricted to
\mathbb{F}
-multiples of
x^{H-1}
.

For brevity, we denote
\mathbb{F}[x]/(x^{H})
as
\underline{\mathbb{F}}_{H}
and call it the
border ring of
\mathbb{F}
with exponent threshold
H
.

We first construct a border analogue of row reduction exists on matrices, which allows us to assume tensor conciseness WLOG in border CPD.

Lemma 7
.

An element
\alpha\in\underline{\mathbb{F}}_{H}
has a multiplicative inverse if and only if it is not a multiple of
x
. Furthermore, such an inverse is unique and can be found in
O(H^{2})
time.

Proof.

We can express
\alpha
as
\sum_{0\leq h<H}\alpha_{h}x^{h}
, for
\alpha_{h}\in\mathbb{F}
.
Clearly, if
\alpha_{0}=0
then there is no
\beta\in\underline{\mathbb{F}}_{H}
such that
\beta\alpha=1
.
Otherwise, we can set
\beta=\sum_{0\leq h<H}\beta_{h}x^{h}
, where
\beta_{0}=\frac{1}{\alpha_{0}}
and
\forall h\geq 1:\beta_{h}=-\frac{1}{\alpha_{0}}\left(\sum_{h^{\prime}<h}\alpha_{h-h^{\prime}}\beta_{h^{\prime}}\right)
, so that
\beta\alpha=1
.
∎

Lemma 8
.

Given any
M\in\underline{\mathbb{F}}_{H}^{m\times n}
, there exists an invertible matrix
Q
and a permutation matrix
P
such that
QMP=\left[\begin{array}[]{c|c}U&V\end{array}\right]
, where

•

U
is square upper-triangular, and
V
is arbitrary

•

the diagonal of
U
contains elements that are nondecreasing powers of
x

(including 0, which is equal to
x^{H}
and considered a higher power of
x
than any
x^{p}
for
p<H
)

•

QMP
contains exactly
\operatorname{rk}(M)
many nonzero rows, which consist of the topmost rows

•

Q,P
can be found in polynomial time

Proof.

We sketch a procedure for reducing
M
by applying row operations and column permutations:

•

if
M=O
, stop;

•

otherwise, find some
(i_{0},j_{0})
such that
M_{i_{0},j_{0}}\not\in x\underline{\mathbb{F}}_{H}
;

•

if such
(i_{0},j_{0})
exists:

–

swap rows 0 and
i_{0}
, and columns 0 and
j_{0}
,
so that this element is moved to
(0,0)

–

multiply row 0 by an inverse of
M_{0,0}
, so that
M_{0,0}=1

–

add scalar multiples of row 0 to all other rows so that
M_{1:,j_{0}}=\overrightarrow{0}

–

recursively apply the procedure on
M_{1:,1:}

•

otherwise: we must have
M=xM^{\prime}
for some
M^{\prime}\in\underline{\mathbb{F}}_{H}^{m\times n}
;

apply the procedure on
M^{\prime}
, tracking the list of row and column operations we apply; then apply those operations on
M

The corresponding matrices
Q,P
can be constructed and updated as we apply each row and column operation. It is clear from the procedure that
QMP
has the structure

\begin{bmatrix}\begin{array}[]{ccccc|ccc}x^{p_{0}}&y_{0,1}&y_{0,2}&\cdots&y_{0,r-1}&y_{0,r}&\cdots&y_{0,n-1}\\
&x^{p_{1}}&y_{1,1}&\cdots&y_{1,r-1}&y_{1,r}&\cdots&y_{1,n-1}\\
&&x^{p_{2}}&\cdots&y_{2,r-1}&y_{2,r}&\cdots&y_{2,n-1}\\
&&&\ddots&\vdots&\vdots&&\vdots\\
&&&&x^{p_{r-1}}&y_{r-1,r}&\cdots&y_{r-1,n-1}\end{array}\\
\hline\cr O\end{bmatrix},

where
r
is the number of nonzero rows in
QMP
, each
p_{i}<H
is a nonnegative integer,
p_{0}\leq\dots\leq p_{r-1}
, and each
y_{i,j}
is an element in
\underline{\mathbb{F}}_{H}
.
Additionally, each
y_{i,j}
is a multiple of
x^{p_{i}}
, since at each step of the reduction process,
p_{i}
is the smallest number such that the current state of
M_{i:,:}
contained some element
\not\in x^{p_{i}+1}\underline{\mathbb{F}}_{H}
. As a consequence, for each
i
,
x^{p_{i}}
divides all elements in
(QMP)_{i:,:}
.

To prove
r
is indeed equal to
\operatorname{rk}(M)
, we will apply additional column operations on
QMP
(which are not necessarily column operations).
Because of hte aforementioned divisibility properties of
x^{p_{i}}
,
it is possible to first add scalar multiples of column 0 to all other columns such that all
y_{0,:}
are eliminated, then repeat for column 1, etc., so that we end up with the matrix

\left[\begin{array}[]{c|c}\begin{array}[]{ccc}x^{p_{0}}\\
&\ddots\\
&&x^{p_{r-1}}\end{array}&O\\
\hline\cr O&O\end{array}\right].

Clearly, the rank of this matrix is at most
r
(since there are
r
many nonzero terms) and at least
\operatorname{rk}(x^{H-1}I_{r})
(since we can discard all-zero rows and columns, then multiply on the left by a suitable diagonal matrix).

We present a proof from Austin Conner
[
14
]
that
\operatorname{rk}(x^{H-1}I_{r})=r
.
Suppose not: then there would exist matrices
U\in\underline{\mathbb{F}}_{H}^{r\times r^{\prime}},\ V\in\underline{\mathbb{F}}_{H}^{r^{\prime}\times r}
, for some
r^{\prime}<r
, such that
x^{H-1}I_{r}=UV
.
In the larger polynomial ring
\mathbb{F}[x]
, this condition is equivalent to there existing some
M\in\mathbb{F}[x]^{r\times r}
such that
x^{H-1}I_{r}+x^{H}M=UV
.
The left-hand side has a polynomial determinant with coefficient 1 at the
x^{r(H-1)}
term, but the right-hand side has determinant 0 because it is rank-deficient; contradiction.
∎

3.2.3
Border CPD algorithm

Lemma
8
gives a border analogue of row-echelon form for a matrix.
As a consequence, any tensor
T
can be transformed with invertible axis-operations to be concise, in polynomial time.
Using the same argument as in Section
3.1
, a concise tensor
T
with rank
\leq R
must have its length along each axis be
\leq R
.

Unfortunately, we cannot always obtain a border analogue of
reduced
row-echelon form (rref), i.e. force the matrix
U
in Lemma
8
to be diagonal, using only row operations and column permutations, because some terms on the diagonal of
U
may be non-invertible and thus unable to eliminate elements directly above them. Consider
M=\begin{bmatrix}1&1\\
&x\end{bmatrix}
; there is no scalar multiple of
M_{1,:}
that can be added to
M_{0,:}
such that
M_{0,1}
is eliminated.

It is important to emphasize that when performing axis-operations on a tensor, we are not allowed to use general column operations. Doing an axis-
d
operation on a tensor with CPD
\left[\left[A_{0},\dots,A_{D-1}\right]\right]
is equivalent to replacing the factor matrix
A_{d}
with
MA_{d}
(left multiplication), for some matrix
M
, which performs a row operation on
A_{d}
(ignoring invertibility).
However, replacing
A_{d}
with
A_{d}M
(right multiplication) changes the resulting tensor in a way that cannot be determined without knowing the individual factor matrices.
Column
permutations
are allowed though, because if
P
is a permutation matrix, then
\left[\left[A_{0}P,\dots,A_{D-1}P\right]\right]=\left[\left[A_{0},\dots,A_{D-1}\right]\right]
due to commutativity of addition.

The lack of border rref means that when we attempt to use the same algorithm as in CPD over a field, the axis-0 slices of
\widetilde{Q}\times_{0}\widetilde{T}
mixes together the outer products
\bigotimes_{d\geq 1}(A_{d})_{:,i}
for multiple
i
, so we cannot treat these slices independently.
Instead, our border CPD search algorithm relies on a simpler idea:

Proof of Theorem
2
.

If
\operatorname{rk}(T)\leq R
(and
R>0
), then there exist vectors
u_{d}\in\underline{\mathbb{F}}_{H}^{n_{d}},\ 0\leq d<D
, such that
\operatorname{rk}\left(T-\bigotimes_{d}u_{d}\right)\leq R-1
. We can thus determine whether
\operatorname{rk}(T)\leq R
by enumerating all possible tuples
(u_{d})_{d}
and recursively solving the corresponding subproblem (while transforming the tensor in each subproblem to be concise).
The case
R=0
is trivial.

To bound the running time of this algorithm, a subproblem with inputs
(T^{\prime}\in\mathbb{F}^{n^{\prime}_{0}\times\dots\times n^{\prime}_{D-1}},\ r)
will have at most
|\mathbb{F}|^{H\sum_{d}n^{\prime}_{d}}
children, all of the form
(\dots,\ r-1)
.
WLOG we can assume in each subproblem that
T^{\prime}
is concise and
\forall d:\ n^{\prime}_{d}\leq r
.
Thus, the total runtime of deciding whether
\operatorname{rk}(T)\leq R
is
O^{*}\left(|\mathbb{F}|^{H\sum_{1\leq r\leq R}\sum_{d}\min(r,n_{d})}\right)
,
where
n_{0}\times\dots\times n_{D-1}
denotes the shape of the original tensor
T
(after being transformed to be concise).
∎

3.3
Barriers

We note some limitations to our algorithms for ordinary and border CPD, and to our pruners. Unless otherwise stated, assume
T
is concise, has shape
n_{0}\times\dots\times n_{D-1}
, and satisfies
n_{0}\geq\dots\geq n_{D-1}
.

The simplest nontrivial rank threshold that our ordinary CPD algorithm can solve is
R=n_{0}
, since there are no variables from any
A_{d}
that have to be fixed. In this case, our algorithm runs in
O^{*}(|\mathbb{F}|^{n_{0}})
time and is equivalent to running both rref-pruning (Lemma
3
) and rref-heuristic.

In hopes of generalizing this result, we tried analyzing the rank threshold
R=n_{0}+1
using
k
-th order rref-pruning.
Unfortunately, the following tensor breaks this idea for
k=2
:

Example 5
.

Over the field
\mathbb{F}_{2}
, the tensor

T=\begin{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}1&1\\
&1\\
&&\phantom{0}\end{bmatrix}&\begin{bmatrix}\phantom{0}\\
&1&1\\
&&1\end{bmatrix}&\begin{bmatrix}1\\
&\phantom{0}\\
1&&1\end{bmatrix}\end{bmatrix}

satisfies both rref-pruning (Lemma
3
) and 2nd-order rref-pruning (Lemma
5
,
k=2
) for rank threshold
R=4
and no variables assigned (
r=n_{0}
), along all axes simultaneously.
In fact, for any two distinct indices
i
and
j
,
\operatorname{rk}\left(\begin{bmatrix}T_{i,:,:}&T_{j,:,:}\end{bmatrix}\right)\leq 3
, and the same property holds after rotating the axes of
T
.

However, running Algorithm
1
shows that
\operatorname{rk}_{\mathbb{F}_{2}}\left(T\right)=5
.

Remark 2
.

The tensor in Example
5
also passes lask-pruning with rank threshold
R=4
(no variables assigned) on all axes.

Remark 3
.

The axis-0 slices of the tensor in Example
5
are diagonal shifts of each other.
Explicitly,
T=\begin{bmatrix}A&PAP^{\intercal}&P^{2}A(P^{2})^{\intercal}\end{bmatrix}
, where
A=\begin{bmatrix}1&1\\
&1\\
&&\phantom{0}\end{bmatrix}
and
P=\begin{bmatrix}&&1\\
1\\
&1\end{bmatrix}
.

Example
5
was found by first running a computer search, then transforming the resulting tensor so that
k
-th order rref pruning is easier to visually check.

We are not sure if a counterexample exists for
k=3
or higher, although we suspect one does:

Problem 1
.

Does there exist a 3-dimensional tensor
T\in\mathbb{F}^{n_{0}\times n_{1}\times n_{2}}
such that:

•

T
passes
k
-th order rref-pruning (Lemma
5
) for all
1\leq k\leq 3
and rank threshold
R:=n_{0}+1
, along all axes;

•

but
\operatorname{rk}(T)>n_{0}+1
?

Another algorithmic barrier is that rref-pruning and lask-pruning are unable to prove superlinear lower bounds on tensor rank.
Specifically, when the input tensor
T
has shape
n_{0}\times n_{1}\times n_{2}
(with
n_{0}\geq n_{1}\geq n_{2}
) and no variables have been assigned (
r=n_{0}
):

•

rref-pruning cannot disprove the statement
\operatorname{rk}(T)\leq R
when
R\geq n_{1}+n_{0}
, because
\operatorname{rk}(v\times_{0}T^{\prime})
is guaranteed to have rank
\leq n_{1}
just by considering matrix shape, so
S^{\prime}
will contain all of
\mathbb{F}^{1\times n_{0}}

(refer to Lemma
3
for notation);

•

and lask-pruning cannot disprove
\operatorname{rk}(T)\leq R
when
R\geq\frac{|\mathbb{F}|^{n_{0}}\cdot n_{1}}{|\mathbb{F}|^{n_{0}}-|\mathbb{F}|^{n_{0}-1}}=\frac{|\mathbb{F}|}{|\mathbb{F}|-1}n_{1}
, since the sum
s
is at most
|\mathbb{F}|^{n_{0}}\cdot n_{1}

(refer to Lemma
4
for notation).

In fact, providing any algorithm that is capable of proving superlinear lower bounds on tensor rank for 3-dimensional tensors is an open problem (see Problem
2
). We suspect the other pruners we have listed are also unable to prove superlinear lower bounds (without becoming impractically slow, e.g., running
k
-th order rref-pruning for high
k
).

The situation for border CPD is much more difficult, as the extra multiplicative
H
term in the exponent of Theorem
2
renders our search algorithm prohibitively slow for most inputs beyond tensor shape
3\times 3\times 3
, rank threshold
R=3
, exponent threshold
H=2
, and ground field
\mathbb{F}=\mathbb{F}_{2}
(substituting this into the asymptotic running time expression yields
2^{36}
).
Additionally, the lack of reduced row-echelon form makes it difficult to devise efficient pruners similar to those for ordinary CPD search.

One potential area for improving our border CPD search, as mentioned in
[
40
]
, is to analyze the case when the rank threshold is close to
n_{0}
(in the extreme case,
R=n_{0}
). We suspect that most recursive function calls are terminated immediately because the input tensor is too long along some axis after being made concise (Algorithm
2
, line 3), so preventing the algorithm from making these calls in the first place may reduce asymptotic time complexity.

4
Maximum rank

In this section we study tensors more generally, beyond the context of fast matrix multiplication.
One problem of interest is finding the maximum possible rank that a tensor of a given shape
n_{0}\times\dots\times n_{D-1}
can have over a field
\mathbb{F}
, which we denote
\boldsymbol{R}_{\mathbb{F}}(n_{0},\dots,n_{D-1})
. We omit
\mathbb{F}
if it is clear from context.
Outside of this introduction, we only study 3-dimensional tensors (
D=3
).

By permuting axes, we immediately have that
\boldsymbol{R}(n_{0},\dots,n_{D-1})=\boldsymbol{R}(n_{\sigma_{0}},\dots,n_{\sigma_{D-1}})
for any permutation
\sigma\in\operatorname{Sym}(D)
.
Additionally, the 2-dimensional case is solved by Gaussian elimination:
\boldsymbol{R}(m,n)=\min(m,n)
.

By a simple counting argument, the following lower bound holds over any finite field
\mathbb{F}
; it also holds over infinite
\mathbb{F}
is we count degrees of freedom instead of number of tensors.

Theorem 4
.

\boldsymbol{R}(n_{0},\dots,n_{D-1})\geq\frac{\prod_{d}n_{d}}{\sum_{d}n_{d}}
.

In the other direction, we can construct a CPD of any tensor by choosing some axis
d
and constructing each 1D slice
(i_{0},\dots,i_{d-1},:,i_{d+1},\dots,i_{D-1})
at a time, for each tuple of coordinates
(i_{d^{\prime}})_{d^{\prime}\neq d}
. This construction proves the following upper bound:

Theorem 5
.

\boldsymbol{R}(n_{0},\dots,n_{D-1})\leq\min_{d}\prod_{d^{\prime}\neq d}n_{d^{\prime}}
.

As an immediate consequence, for 3-dimensional cube-shaped tensors we have
\frac{n^{2}}{3}\leq\boldsymbol{R}(n,n,n)\leq n^{2}
. In fact, over a finite field
\mathbb{F}
, a uniformly random tensor will have rank
\geq\frac{n^{2}}{3}
with high probability.
Unfortunately, we do not know an
explicit
(i.e. deterministic) tensor with rank nearly as high; the following is a major open problem:

Problem 2

(
[
9
]
)
.

Is there an explicit tensor
T\in\mathbb{F}^{n\times n\times n}
satisfying
\operatorname{rk}(T)\geq(3+\varepsilon)n
for any constant
\varepsilon>0
?

The strongest known lower bound on
\boldsymbol{R}(n,n,n)
from an explicit tensor is
3n-o(n)
(Example
9
, Remark
5
).

The main reason why most values
\boldsymbol{R}(n_{0},\dots,n_{D-1})
are difficult to find is because of a lack of symmetry:
the set of all
n_{0}\times\dots\times n_{D-1}
tensors has size
|\mathbb{F}|^{\prod_{d}n_{d}}
, or
\prod_{d}n_{d}
degrees of freedom (DoF);
but the group of all invertible axis-operations on the tensor shape,
\operatorname{GL}\left(n_{0},\ \mathbb{F}\right)\times\dots\times\operatorname{GL}\left(n_{D-1},\ \mathbb{F}\right)
, has size
\Theta(|\mathbb{F}|^{\sum_{d}n_{d}^{2}})
(for constant
D
), or only
\sum_{d}n_{d}^{2}
degrees of freedom
[
13
]
.
When
D=2
, the latter has more DoF than the former, so there exists a simple canonical form of matrices up to invertible axis-operations (obtainable by applying Gaussian elimination on the rows, and then on the columns); but for most shapes with
D\geq 3
, there is no such form.

This problem with degrees of freedom also explains why
\boldsymbol{R}(n,n,2)
has been completely solved (see Theorem
6
) but
\boldsymbol{R}(n,n,3)
is still not well-understood: the former has
2n^{2}
DoF for tensors, which is comparable to
2n^{2}+2^{2}
for invertible axis-operations; but the latter has
3n^{2}
versus
2n^{2}+3^{2}
.

4.1
Previous work

Theorem
4
is the strongest known lower bound on maximum rank when the tensor shape is roughly cubic. For unbalanced shapes, stronger results are known.

The shape
m\times n\times 2
is completely solved:

Theorem 6

(
[
37
]
)
.

For a finite field
\mathbb{F}
and integers
m\geq n\geq 2
, the following holds:

if
\mathbb{F}=\mathbb{F}_{2}
:
\boldsymbol{R}_{\mathbb{F}_{2}}(m,n,2)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}n+\left\lceil m/2\right\rceil&n\leq m\leq 2n-2\\
2n-1&m=2n-1\\
2n&m\geq 2n\end{array}\right\}
;

otherwise:

Theorem 8

(
[

\boldsymbol{R}(m,n,p)\leq\boldsymbol{R}(m-1,n-1,p)+p

Proof.

By permuting and scaling slices of a tensor

, WLOG we can assume

; then we can add scalar multiples of

to all other

so that for all

\begin{bmatrix}1\\
\lambda_{1}&1\\
\vdots&&\ddots\\
\lambda_{m-1}&&&1\end{bmatrix}\times_{0}

Now for each

\boldsymbol{R}(n,n,n)\leq\left\lceil\frac{3}{4}n^{2}\right\rceil

4.2
Substitution method

To find new results, as well as restate previous ones in a clean form, we will often want to prove lower bounds on the tensor rank of specific tensors. One powerful method to do so is the
substitution method
, first described by
[
32
]
in the context of polynomials and repurposed by
[
1
]
for tensors; we follow the latter source. Intuitively, if we remove a slice along an arbitrary axis from a tensor, there exists a way of adding scalar multiples of that slice to the remaining slices so that the tensor rank decreases by at least one.

Theorem 10

(
[
1
]
, Appendix B)
.

Let
T\in\mathbb{F}^{n_{0}\times\dots\times n_{D-1}}
be a tensor,
d
be an axis, and
0\leq i<n_{d}
be a coordinate such that the slice
T_{\cdots,:,i,:,\cdots}
along axis
d
is nonzero. Then there exist scalars
\lambda_{j}
for each
0\leq j<n_{d},\ j\neq i
such that the tensor
T^{\prime}
with axis-
d
slices
T_{\cdots,:,0,:,\cdots}+\lambda_{0}T_{\cdots,:,i,:,\cdots},\ \cdots,\ T_{\cdots,:,n_{d}-1,:,\cdots}+\lambda_{n_{d}-1}T_{\cdots,:,i,:,\cdots}
satisfies
\operatorname{rk}(T^{\prime})\leq\operatorname{rk}(T)-1
.

Proof.

By permuting axes and slices, it suffices to prove the case
d=0,\ i=0
; then
T^{\prime}=\begin{bmatrix}\lambda_{1}&1\\
\vdots&&\ddots\\
\lambda_{n_{0}-1}&&&1\end{bmatrix}\times_{0}T
.

Let
T=\left[\left[A_{0},\dots,A_{D-1}\right]\right]
be a rank-
\operatorname{rk}(T)
CPD. Because
T_{0,:,\cdots}\neq O
, there must exist some
r
s.t.
(A_{0})_{0,r}\neq 0
. Then setting
\lambda_{j}=-\frac{(A_{0})_{j,r}}{(A_{0})_{0,r}}
forces the
r
-th column of
\begin{bmatrix}\lambda_{1}&1\\
\vdots&&\ddots\\
\lambda_{n_{0}-1}&&&1\end{bmatrix}A_{0}
to be all-zeros.
Since
T^{\prime}=\left[\left[\begin{bmatrix}\lambda_{1}&1\\
\vdots&&\ddots\\
\lambda_{n_{0}-1}&&&1\end{bmatrix}A_{0},A_{1},\dots,A_{D-1}\right]\right]
, removing the
r
-th column of each factor matrix from this CPD does not change the resulting tensor, so
\operatorname{rk}(T^{\prime})\leq\operatorname{rk}(T)-1
.
∎

Corollary 2
.

Using the same
T
and
T^{\prime}
as in Theorem
10
, if we can find some
R
such that
\operatorname{rk}(T^{\prime})\geq R
for all choices of scalars
\lambda_{j}
, then
\operatorname{rk}(T)\geq R+1
.

When applying the substitution method to the slice
T_{\cdots,:,i,:,\cdots}
along some axis
d
, we call this action “subbing out
(\cdots,:,i,:,\cdots)
”.

Overall, the substitution method is the strongest method of proving tensor rank lower bounds without resorting to computational search
[
9
]
. Because using the substitution method to its full potential may require subbing out along every axis (see Example
9
), it is difficult to combine it with our CPD search algorithm (Theorem
1
).

Below we list some examples of the substitution method, including previously discovered results:

Example 6
.

The “W-state” tensor
T=\begin{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}1\\
&\phantom{0}\end{bmatrix}&\begin{bmatrix}&1\\
1\end{bmatrix}\end{bmatrix}
has rank 3 over any field.

Proof.

Subbing out
(:,1,:)
yields
\begin{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}1&\phantom{0}\end{bmatrix}&\begin{bmatrix}x&1\end{bmatrix}\end{bmatrix}
for some scalar
x
we cannot control.
Then, subbing out
(:,:,1)
yields
\begin{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}1\end{bmatrix}&\begin{bmatrix}y\end{bmatrix}\end{bmatrix}
for a (possibly different) scalar
y
. Finally, subbing out
(:,:,0)
yields
\begin{bmatrix}\end{bmatrix}
(an empty tensor), so we cannot continue.

Since an empty tensor trivially has rank 0, and we subbed out three slices from
T
, we have proven
\operatorname{rk}(T)\geq 3
.
On the other hand,
\operatorname{rk}(T)\leq 3
because there are only 3 nonzero elements in
T
.

∎

Example
6
is an explicit tensor of shape
n_{0}\times n_{1}\times n_{2}
with rank strictly higher than any
n_{d}
, which is not possible for matrices.

The next example combines the substitution method with axis-operations:

Example 7

(Thm,
[
11
]
)
.

The tensor
T=\left\langle 2,2,2\right\rangle
corresponding to matrix multiplication of two
2\times 2
matrices has rank 7 over any field.

Proof.

We can permute slices along several axes so that
T
becomes

\begin{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}1\\
&\phantom{0}\\
&&1\\
&&&\phantom{0}\end{bmatrix}&\begin{bmatrix}&1\\
\phantom{0}\\
&&&1\\
&&&\phantom{0}\end{bmatrix}&\begin{bmatrix}&&&\phantom{0}\\
1\\
&\phantom{0}\\
&&1\end{bmatrix}&\begin{bmatrix}\phantom{0}\\
&1\\
\phantom{0}\\
&&&1\end{bmatrix}\end{bmatrix}.

Subbing out
(:,3,:)
yields

\begin{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}1\\
&\phantom{0}\\
&&1&\phantom{0}\end{bmatrix}&\begin{bmatrix}&1\\
\phantom{0}\\
&&&1\end{bmatrix}&\begin{bmatrix}&&x&\phantom{0}\\
1&&y\\
&&z\end{bmatrix}&\begin{bmatrix}\phantom{0}&&&x\\
&1&&y\\
&&&z\end{bmatrix}\end{bmatrix}

for some scalars
x,y,z
we cannot control.

We notice that the sub-tensor
(:2,:,:)
has rank 4 (because its axis-2 slices are linearly independent). To prove
\operatorname{rk}(T)\geq 7
, we would like to sub out slices
(2,:,:)
and
(3,:,:)
, and then prove the resulting tensor has rank
\geq 4
. However, if we did that right now, we would destroy the 1s at
(0,2,2)
and
(1,2,3)
, and the resulting tensor would at best only have rank 2.

To avoid destroying terms in
(:2,:,:)
, we perform some invertible axis-operations (which do not affect tensor rank) to cancel some unwanted terms in
(2,:,:)
and
(3,:,:)
. First, we apply
\begin{bmatrix}1\\
&1\\
-z&&1\\
&-z&&1\end{bmatrix}\times_{0}
to get

\begin{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}1\\
&\phantom{0}\\
&&1&\phantom{0}\end{bmatrix}&\begin{bmatrix}&1\\
\phantom{0}\\
&&&1\end{bmatrix}&\begin{bmatrix}-z&&x&\phantom{0}\\
1&&y\\
&&\end{bmatrix}&\begin{bmatrix}\phantom{0}&-z&&x\\
&1&&y\\
&&&\end{bmatrix}\end{bmatrix};

then we apply
\begin{bmatrix}1&z\\
&1\\
&&1\end{bmatrix}\times_{1}
to get

\begin{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}1\\
&\phantom{0}\\
&&1&\phantom{0}\end{bmatrix}&\begin{bmatrix}&1\\
\phantom{0}\\
&&&1\end{bmatrix}&\begin{bmatrix}&&x+yz&\phantom{0}\\
1&&y\\
&&\end{bmatrix}&\begin{bmatrix}\phantom{0}&&&x+yz\\
&1&&y\\
&&&\end{bmatrix}\end{bmatrix}.

At this point, we replace all indeterminates with asterisks to simplify notation, as we will no longer have to examine their exact structure.

Now we sub out slices
(2,:,:)
and
(3,:,:)
to get

\begin{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}1\\
&\phantom{0}\\
&&1&\phantom{0}\end{bmatrix}+(*)\begin{bmatrix}&&*&\phantom{0}\\
*&&*\\
&&\end{bmatrix}+(*)\begin{bmatrix}\phantom{0}&&&*\\
&*&&*\\
&&&\end{bmatrix}&\phantom{0}&\begin{bmatrix}&1\\
\phantom{0}\\
&&&1\end{bmatrix}+(*)\begin{bmatrix}&&*&\phantom{0}\\
*&&*\\
&&\end{bmatrix}+(*)\begin{bmatrix}\phantom{0}&&&*\\
&*&&*\\
&&&\end{bmatrix}\end{bmatrix}

=\begin{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}1&&*&*\\
*&*&*&*\\
&&1&\phantom{0}\end{bmatrix}&\begin{bmatrix}&1&*&*\\
*&*&*&*\\
&&&1\end{bmatrix}\end{bmatrix}.

This tensor has rank at least 4, no matter what each * is replaced with,
since we can sub out
(:,:,0)
,
(:,:,1)
,
(:,:,2)
, and
(:,:,3)
in that order; each slice we sub out avoids destroying the 1s in later slices.

Since we sub out seven slices in total,

\operatorname{rk}(T)\geq 7
.
Because we did not perform any scalar divisions, this proof works over any field.

On the other hand,
\operatorname{rk}(T)\leq 7
over any field, because the CPD corresponding to Strassen’s algorithm only contains integers.
∎

4.3
New results

We first improve existing bounds on
\boldsymbol{R}(n,n,n)
and
\boldsymbol{R}(m,n,mn-k)
by tuning parameters more carefully.

Theorem 13
.

\boldsymbol{R}(n,n,n)\leq\frac{23}{32}n^{2}+O(n)
over any field.

Proof.

We combine the following previous results:

•

Theorem
8
:
\boldsymbol{R}(m,n,p)\leq\boldsymbol{R}(m-1,n-1,p)+p

•

Theorem
6
(weakened):
\boldsymbol{R}(m,n,2)\leq n+\frac{m}{2}+O(1)
for
m\geq n

Using the latter result,
\boldsymbol{R}(m,n,p)\leq\left\lceil\frac{p}{2}\right\rceil\left(n+\frac{m}{2}+O(1)\right)=\frac{p}{2}(n+\frac{m}{2})+O(n+m+p)
,
by splitting a
m\times n\times p
tensor along axis 2 into
m\times n\times 2
-shaped pieces (plus a smaller remaining piece).

Consider applying Theorem
8
to
\boldsymbol{R}(n,n,n)
some
0\leq k\leq n
many times, then invoking the aforementioned bound derived from Theorem
6
:

\begin{array}[]{lll}\boldsymbol{R}(n,n,n)&\leq\boldsymbol{R}(n-k,n-k,n)+kn\\
&=\boldsymbol{R}(n,n-k,n-k)+kn\\
&\leq\frac{n-k}{2}((n-k)+\frac{n}{2})+kn+O(n)&\textrm{if }n\leq 2(n-k);\end{array}

The optimal
k
is
\arg\min_{k}\frac{n-k}{2}((n-k)+\frac{n}{2})+kn+O(n)=\arg\min_{k}\frac{1}{4}(2k^{2}-kn)=\frac{n}{4}
, yielding
\boldsymbol{R}(n,n,n)\leq\frac{23}{32}n^{2}+O(n)
.
We check that the condition
n\leq 2(n-k)=2\cdot\frac{3}{4}n=\frac{3}{2}n
is satisfied.
∎

Theorem 14
.

For
k=o(\min(m,n)^{2})
,
\boldsymbol{R}(m,n,mn-k)\geq mn-3\sqrt{3k}+O(1)
over any field.

Proof.

Choose
1\leq r\leq m,\ 1\leq s\leq n
such that
rs\geq k
, and let
T^{\prime}
be a maximal-rank
r\times s\times(rs-k)
tensor. Then we can construct the
m\times n\times(mn-k)
tensor
T
such that

•

T_{:,:,:(rs-k)}=T^{\prime}
;

•

for
i>rs-k
, each
T_{:,:,i}
has a 1 at a distinct coordinate
(a,b)
not inside
\left\{0,\dots,r-1\right\}\times\left\{0,\dots,s-1\right\}
, and zeros everywhere else.

We have
\operatorname{rk}(T)\geq\operatorname{rk}(T^{\prime})+(mn-rs)
, since if we sub out each slice
(:,:,i)
at a time for
i>rs-k
, and then remove slices
(r:,:,:)
and
(:,s:,:)
, we are left with
T^{\prime}
.

Thus,
\boldsymbol{R}(m,n,mn-k)\geq\boldsymbol{R}(r,s,rs-k)+mn-rs
.
Using the counting lower bound (Theorem
4
) on
\boldsymbol{R}(r,s,rs-k)
yields

\boldsymbol{R}(m,n,mn-k)\geq\frac{rs(rs-k)}{rs+r+s-k}+mn-rs=\frac{-rs(r+s)}{rs+r+s-k}+mn.

To minimize
f(r,s):=\frac{rs(r+s)}{rs+r+s-k}
, we set its partial derivatives to 0.

\begin{array}[]{rrl}&\frac{\partial}{\partial r}\frac{r(r+s)}{rs+r+s-k}&=0\\
&&=\frac{2r+s}{rs+r+s-k}-\frac{r(r+s)(s+1)}{(rs+r+s-k)^{2}}\\
\Rightarrow&(2r+s)(rs+r+s-k)-r(r+s)(s+1)&=0\\
&&=r^{2}s+(r+s)^{2}-(2r+s)k;\\
\end{array}

by symmetry, having
\frac{\partial f}{\partial s}=0
implies
s^{2}r+(r+s)^{2}-(2s+r)k=0
.

Taking the sum and difference of these two equations yields

\begin{array}[]{rl}rs(r+s)+2(r+s)^{2}-3(r+s)k&=0\\
rs(r-s)-(r-s)k&=0\\
\end{array}

\Rightarrow\begin{array}[]{ccc}rs+2(r+s)-3k&=0&\textrm{(since }r+s\neq 0\textrm{)}\\
(r-s)(rs-k)&=0\\
\end{array}

If
rs-k=0
, then
f(r,s)=k
. On the other hand, if
r-s=0
, the first equation implies
r^{2}+4r-3k=0\Rightarrow r^{*}=s^{*}=-2+\sqrt{4+3k}
.

To simplify analysis, it suffices to plug in
r^{*}=s^{*}=\sqrt{3k}
instead;
then
f(\sqrt{3k},\sqrt{3k})=\frac{3k\cdot 2\sqrt{3k}}{2k+2\sqrt{3k}}=\frac{3k\cdot\sqrt{3k}}{k+\sqrt{3k}}=\frac{3k\cdot\sqrt{3k}}{k}\cdot\frac{k}{k+\sqrt{3k}}=3\sqrt{3k}\cdot\left(1-\frac{\sqrt{3k}}{k+\sqrt{3k}}\right)=3\sqrt{3k}\cdot\left(1-O\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{k}}\right)\right)=3\sqrt{3k}-O(1)
.

Thus,
\boldsymbol{R}(m,n,mn-k)\geq mn-3\sqrt{3k}+O(1)
.

To ensure the conditions
r^{*}\leq m,\ s^{*}\leq n
are met, it suffices to have
k=o(\min(m,n)^{2})
.
∎

\mathbb{F}_{2}
. To achieve this, we canonicalized the tensors
T
we searched up to invertible axis-operations along two of the three axes (axes 1 and 2):

•

force
T_{0,:,:}=\begin{bmatrix}I_{r}&O\\
O&O\end{bmatrix}
for some
r
(i.e. simultaneously row-reduced and column-reduced);

•

for
i=1,2,\dots
:

force
T_{i,:,:}
to be lexicographically minimized over all transformations
M\mapsto PMQ^{\intercal}
, for pairs of invertible matrices
(P,Q)
such that
P\times_{1}(Q\times_{2}T_{:i,:,:})=T_{:i,:,:}
;

Additionally, when possible, we force
\operatorname{rk}(T_{0,:,:})\geq r_{0}
for some manually chosen value
r_{0}
.
Specifically, if we already know

4.4
Barriers

Here we list some difficulties in improving lower and upper bounds on maximum rank.
Besides the still-high runtime of our CPD search algorithm, finding maximum rank over finite fields will likely require field-specific techniques.

First, the best known upper bound on maximum rank over the complex numbers (Theorem
9
), which is roughly half of the trivial upper bound, fails badly over finite fields, as it relies on the following lemma:

Lemma 9

(
[
7
]
)
.

Given two arbitrary square matrices
A,B\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
, there exist diagonal matrices
J,K\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
such that
A+J
and
B+K
are simultaneously diagonalizable.

Lemma
9
crucially relies on
\mathbb{C}
being infinite and algebraically closed. As a counterexample over a finite field
\mathbb{F}
, consider setting
B=\begin{bmatrix}0&1\\
&&\ddots\\
&&&1\\
&&&0\end{bmatrix}
;
then the geometric multiplicity of each eigenvalue of
B+K
is at most one, because
\operatorname{rk}(B+K-\lambda I)\geq n-1
for any
\lambda
and
K
.
Since any square matrix over a finite field
\mathbb{F}
trivially has
\leq|\mathbb{F}|
many eigenvalues,
B+K
is not diagonalizable if
n>|F|
.

Additionally, the rank of a specific tensor can be change across different fields, and usually the rank seems to increase over smaller fields:

Example 10
.

The tensor
T=\begin{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}1\\
&1\end{bmatrix}&\begin{bmatrix}&1\\
1\end{bmatrix}\end{bmatrix}
(Ex.
2
) satisfies

\operatorname{rk}_{\mathbb{F}}\left(T\right)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}3&\operatorname{char}\left(\mathbb{F}\right)=2\\
2&\textrm{else}\end{array}\right\}
.

Proof.

The upper bound for the case
\operatorname{char}\left(\mathbb{F}\right)=2
can proven with the CPD

T=\begin{bmatrix}1\\
\phantom{0}\end{bmatrix}\otimes\begin{bmatrix}1\\
1\end{bmatrix}\otimes\begin{bmatrix}1\\
1\end{bmatrix}+\begin{bmatrix}1\\
1\end{bmatrix}\otimes\begin{bmatrix}1\\
\phantom{0}\end{bmatrix}\otimes\begin{bmatrix}\phantom{0}\\
1\end{bmatrix}+\begin{bmatrix}1\\
1\end{bmatrix}\otimes\begin{bmatrix}\phantom{0}\\
1\end{bmatrix}\otimes\begin{bmatrix}1\\
\phantom{0}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}1&1\\
1&1\end{bmatrix}&O\end{bmatrix}+\begin{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}&1\\
\phantom{0}\end{bmatrix}&\begin{bmatrix}&1\\
\phantom{0}\end{bmatrix}\end{bmatrix}+\begin{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}&\phantom{0}\\
1\end{bmatrix}&\begin{bmatrix}&\phantom{0}\\

.

The case
\operatorname{char}\left(\mathbb{F}\right)\neq 2
and a lower bound for the case
\operatorname{char}\left(\mathbb{F}\right)=2
can be seen by running Algorithm

with rank threshold
R=2
, which we demonstrate symbolically.

If
\operatorname{rk}(T)=2
, there must exist two linearly independent solutions
\begin{bmatrix}a&b\end{bmatrix}
to the equation
\operatorname{rk}(\begin{bmatrix}a&b\end{bmatrix}\times_{0}T)=1
. Since
\begin{bmatrix}a&b\end{bmatrix}\times_{0}T=\begin{bmatrix}a&b\\
b&a\end{bmatrix}
is a
2\times 2
matrix, the equation is equivalent to forcing the determinant of this matrix to be 0, so
a^{2}-b^{2}=0=(a+b)(a-b)
.
If
\operatorname{char}\left(\mathbb{F}\right)\neq 2
, there exist solutions
\begin{bmatrix}a&b\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}1&1\end{bmatrix},\ \begin{bmatrix}1&-1\end{bmatrix}
. Otherwise, the polynomial equation degenerates to
(a+b)^{2}=0\Rightarrow a+b=0
, so the set of solutions is contained in a 1-dimensional subspace.
∎

.

.

Remark 10
.

The tensor in Example
11
is equal to
W\boxtimes W
, where
W=\begin{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}1\\
&\phantom{0}\end{bmatrix}&\begin{bmatrix}&1\\
1\end{bmatrix}\end{bmatrix}
is the “W-state” tensor from Example
6
,
and
\boxtimes
is the Kronecker product of tensors, which is defined similarly as is for matrices.

A potential problem with this phenomenon of tensor rank increasing over finite fields is that general lower bounds techniques, like the substitution method, may be too weak. Recall the indeterminate variables
\lambda_{i}
in Theorem
10
: to our knowledge, most mathematical proofs that use the substitution method try to avoid using these variables, or cancel them out when possible (e.g., the proof that
\operatorname{rk}(\left\langle 2,2,2\right\rangle)\geq 7
due to
[
11
]
that we rephrase in Example
7
). We call such proofs “symbolic”; in contrast, stronger lower bounds may require enumerating some or all assignments of the indeterminates
\lambda_{i}
and recursing, which we call “combinatorial”. Doing so may lead to a depth-first search problem with complexity comparable to our CPD search algorithm, with its own set of pruners that can speed up computation in practice.

5
Conclusion

We present algorithms for both ordinary CPD and border CPD search of an arbitrary tensor over a finite field, which run in exponential time much faster than naïve methods while only using polynomial space. For ordinary CPD, we also describe pruning conditions that can reduce the search space in practice for specific tensor inputs.

We also study the maximum possible rank of a tensor with a given shape over a specific field.
We rephrase existing results in a notation we believe is easier to read, and we improve some of these results by tuning parameters more carefully.
Finally, we use our aforementioned CPD search algorithm to exactly determine maximum rank of shapes
3\times 3\times 4
,
3\times 4\times 4
, and
3\times 3\times 5
over the field
\mathbb{F}_{2}
.

We suspect that the runtime of our ordinary CPD search is near the limit for algorithms relying on linear analysis (e.g. contractions of the input tensor along a single axis, linear systems of linear equations) and that further improvements must use nonlinear methods.
Even so, the best known algorithms for this task (e.g., Gröbner bases) still use linear algebra at a high level by treating a polynomial as a linear combination of monomials, so a lot of challenging problems remain.

For border CPD search, there may be more room for improvement. The main difficulty we face is that a reduced row-echelon form of a matrix does not always exist over a border ring, but a more careful analysis of this ring may get around this obstacle.
