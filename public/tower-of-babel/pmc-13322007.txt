Machine learning–based transcriptomic analysis of differentially expressed genes in the epithelium and stroma of keratoconus
Authors: Kaiyue Du, Rongmei Peng, Gege Xiao, Yi Qu, Liang Han, Jing Hong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis study was conducted to delineate layer-specific transcriptomic alterations in the keratoconus (KC) epithelium and stroma and to identify potential biomarkers using machine learning (ML)-based analysis.MethodsTwo publicly available microarray datasets were integrated for epithelium analysis. For stroma analysis, RNA sequencing data from 31 KC patients and four normal donors were combined with a public microarray dataset. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified separately, after which functional enrichment, gene set enrichment, and gene set variation analyses were performed. Four ML algorithms were then applied, and feature importance was ranked using SHapley Additive exPlanations. The top 50% of genes across all the models were intersected, and hub genes were identified on the basis of protein–protein interaction (PPI) networks. Background

Keratoconus (KC) is a progressive corneal ectatic disorder characterized by central or paracentral stromal thinning and anterior corneal protrusion, leading to progressive myopia, irregular astigmatism, and significant visual impairment [1]. KC is a major indication for corneal transplantation worldwide, with a prevalence of approximately one in 2,000 individuals and an even higher incidence among young adults [2].

KC affects multiple corneal layers, including the epithelium, Bowman’s layer, and stroma. Its histopathological features include epithelial thinning, breaks in Bowman’s layer, superficial iron deposition, and disruption of stromal collagen organization [3]. Transcriptomic studies have revealed widespread molecular alterations across the entire corneal thickness associated with KC, including responses to mechanical stress [4], dysregulation of immune and inflammatory pathways [5, 6], and both antiproliferative and proapoptotic phenotypes [7]. Notably, distinct molecular signatures have been reported in the epithelium and stroma, with epithelial changes characterized by impaired barrier function [8], abnormal apoptosis and differentiation [9], mitochondrial dysfunction, metabolic disturbances [10] and stromal alterations linked mainly to extracellular matrix (ECM) disorganization and aberrant mechanotransduction [9–11].

These findings highlight the multilayered and multifactorial nature of KC pathogenesis. However, significant gaps in knowledge remain. First, most prior studies have analysed either the entire cornea or individual layers and lack systematic interlayer comparisons or insights into potential cross-layer regulatory mechanisms. Second, although several dysregulated pathways have been identified, the key regulatory hubs and master transcription factors that coordinate these molecular disturbances remain unclear.

Machine learning (ML) approaches have shown considerable promise for analysing high-dimensional biological data [12, 13]. Compared with traditional statistical approaches, ML methods are better suited for modelling nonlinear relationships, handling noisy data, and integrating heterogeneous data types, thereby enabling a more comprehensive characterization of complex biological patterns and underlying mechanisms [14]. As such, ML approaches can complement conventional analytical frameworks and provide additional insights into transcriptomic architecture. Nevertheless, applications of ML in KC transcriptomics remain limited, particularly in terms of elucidating layer-specific molecular mechanisms and cross-layer regulatory hubs. To address this gap in research, we integrated publicly available transcriptome datasets with newly generated stromal RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) data and applied ML algorithms to separately analyse epithelial and stromal transcriptomic profiles in KC and control samples. Model outputs were further interpreted using SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) to quantify gene-level contributions to disease classification [15].

Methods

Study design and participants

This prospective study included patients who underwent deep anterior lamellar keratoplasty for KC at the Centre for Sight, Peking University Third Hospital in Beijing, China, between January 1, 2020, and February 1, 2025. Human donor corneas were obtained from the Peking University Third Hospital Eye Bank in Beijing, China, for use as controls. Written informed consent was obtained from all patients or their legal guardians (if the individual was under 18 years of age) and the next-of-kin of the control donors for research purposes. The study protocol was approved by the Medical Science Research Ethics Committee of Peking University Third Hospital (Protocol No. M2023859) and adhered strictly to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The inclusion criteria for the KC group were as follows: a diagnosis of primary KC without corneal hydrops or scarring; no history of ocular surgery; and no preoperative ocular complications, including corneal ulcers, keratitis, eye trauma, or cataracts. Patients with severe systemic diseases or genetic disorders were excluded. No additional complications or contraindications, including previous eye diseases, ocular surgery, chronic systemic autoimmune or inflammatory conditions, or transmissible diseases, were identified in any of the donor corneas. Slit-lamp examination confirmed the absence of infection, corneal neovascularization, abnormal corneal morphology, pterygium, synechiae, or signs of tumours or metastases in patients with KC [16].

Clinical data

All patients with KC underwent preoperative visual acuity examinations, slit-lamp examinations, fundoscopy, intraocular pressure measurements, autorefractions (Topcon RM 8800; Topcon Corporation, Tokyo, Japan), phoropters (Topcon CV-5000; Topcon Corporation, Tokyo, Japan), trial lenses, anterior segment optical coherence tomography (Visante, Zeiss, Oberkochen, Germany), and corneal tomography (Pentacam, Oculus, Washington, DC, USA). All patients exhibited contact lens intolerance and had not worn contact lenses for at least 6 months prior to the study.

RNA extraction

Following excision of the corneal button, the corneal stroma was immediately immersed in an RNA stabilization solution (RNAlater; Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) and stored at − 80 °C until RNA extraction. Human donor corneas were obtained and preserved in Optisol-GS storage medium (Bausch & Lomb, Irvine, CA, USA) at 4 °C within 24 h post-mortem. For both types of samples, RNA extraction was performed within 14 days of tissue preservation. Corneal stroma tissue (wet weight range: 15.58–42.37 mg; median: 32.95 mg) was transferred to liquid nitrogen and ground with a precooled mortar and pestle. Total RNA was extracted and purified using an RNAprep Pure Tissue Kit (DP431; TIANGEN, Beijing, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The quality and quantity of the purified RNA were assessed using an RNA Nano 6000 Assay Kit on a Bioanalyzer 2100 system (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA). The RNA pellets were resuspended in 20 µL of RNase-free H2O, and the total RNA samples were stored at − 80 °C until further analysis was conducted.

Total RNA library preparation and sequencing

Sequencing libraries were generated using a NEBNext Ultra™ RNA Library Prep Kit from Illumina (NEB, USA). Index-coded sample clustering was performed on a cBot Cluster Generation System using a TruSeq PE Cluster Kit v3-cBot-HS (Illumina) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. After cluster generation, the libraries were sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq platform, yielding 150 bp paired-end reads.

Dataset collection and preprocessing

Adapter sequences and low-quality reads were removed from the raw sequencing reads of our stromal RNA-seq dataset using Fastp (v0.23.1) [17]. The cleaned reads were subsequently aligned to the GRCh38 human reference genome with HISAT2 (v2.0.5) [18] and assembled in a reference-guided manner using StringTie (v1.3.3b) [19]. Gene-level read counts were quantified using featureCounts (v1.5.0-p3) [20].

For analysis, two transcriptome microarray datasets comparing KC and normal epithelium (GSE92935 [21] and GSE204791 [10]) and one dataset comparing KC and normal stroma (GSE204791) were obtained from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO, https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/) [22]. RNA-seq count matrices were processed using edgeR (v3.42.0) [23] for trimmed mean of M-value normalization and subsequently transformed using the voom function in limma (v3.54.0). For the microarray data, probe-to-gene mapping was performed using the platform annotation file (GPL570) obtained from GEO. Probe IDs were mapped to HUGO Gene Nomenclature Committee gene symbols, and when multiple probes mapped to the same gene, the median expression value across probes was used to represent gene-level expression. The microarray data were normalized using normalizeBetweenArrays in limma [24]. After the genes were filtered for shared genes, separate expression matrices were generated for the epithelium and stroma and subsequently merged within each tissue type. The epithelial dataset comprised GSE92935 and GSE204791, whereas the stromal dataset included GSE204791 and our cohort. Design matrices accounting for batch effects and disease status were then constructed for downstream analyses.

To validate the key genes, two RNA-seq datasets comprising epithelium and stroma (GSE77938 [25] and GSE151631 [26]) data were retrieved from GEO. A detailed description of all public datasets used in this study is presented in Supplementary Table 1. Raw counts for the target key genes were extracted from each dataset, and a small expression matrix containing only these genes was constructed. The raw counts were then converted to transcripts per million for visualization purposes.

Transcriptomic data analyses

Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed using all expressed genes after low-abundance features were filtered out. PCA plots were generated before and after batch adjustment using removeBatchEffect in limma, with the dataset specified as the batch variable.

Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between KC and normal samples were identified separately for the epithelium and stroma using the limma–voom pipeline. Voom-transformed expression values were fitted with linear models using lmFit in limma, including batch as a covariate, followed by empirical Bayes moderation with eBayes. Genes with Benjamini–Hochberg adjusted p < 0.05 and absolute fold change (|FC|) > 1.5 were considered significant.

The functional enrichment of Gene Ontology (GO) terms and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathways for epithelial and stromal DEGs was performed using clusterProfiler (v4.6.0) [27], with Benjamini–Hochberg adjusted p values < 0.05 considered to indicate statistical significance. Enrichment analysis was performed for Homo sapiens, and the gene universe was defined as all genes retained after expression filtering and successful mapping to Entrez identifiers. Differences in cell infiltration between KC sample and controls were assessed using the xCell algorithm (v1.1.0) [28].

Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) was performed using the gseGO and gseKEGG functions in clusterProfiler. For each contrast, genes were ranked using a continuous statistic derived from the differential expression analysis, generating a single ordered gene list for enrichment testing. The organism was specified as Homo sapiens, and the same filtered gene universe was used as the background. KEGG pathway annotations were obtained from the KEGG database (accessed July 15, 2025). Pathways with Benjamini–Hochberg adjusted p values < 0.05 were considered significantly enriched.

Additionally, gene set variation analysis (GSVA) was performed using the GSVA R package (v1.46.0) [29] to evaluate pathway-level differences, with p values < 0.05 considered significant. Gene sets were derived from the Molecular Signatures Database (v2025.2), specifically the Hallmark collection (category “H”), and retrieved using the msigdbr package (v25.1.1) for Homo sapiens. Gene identifiers were used directly as HUGO Gene Nomenclature Committee symbols without additional conversion, and gene sets were formatted as lists mapping pathway names to their corresponding gene symbols. Prior to GSVA, genes were filtered to retain those with expression values greater than zero in at least two samples.

The intersection of epithelial and stromal DEGs, along with genes identified by the ML models, was used to construct protein–protein interaction (PPI) networks in the STRING database [30] (minimum interaction score = 0.4) and visualized in Cytoscape (v3.10.0) [31], with the top 10 hub nodes highlighted. For hub gene validation in two independent public transcriptomic datasets, differential expression analysis was performed on the target gene expression matrices, and the KC and control groups were compared using the DESeq2 R package (v1.20.0) [32]. Genes with Benjamini–Hochberg adjusted p values < 0.05 were considered significantly differentially expressed. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis was then conducted to evaluate the discriminatory power of each gene in distinguishing KC samples from control samples.

Machine learning

The machine learning analyses were performed in R (v4.4.3; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). The epithelial and stromal datasets were analysed separately, with the DEGs used as input features and samples labelled as normal or KC. Data preprocessing included centring, scaling, and the removal of predictors with near-zero variance.

Four models were constructed for each tissue type: logistic regression (LR) using glmnet (v4.1–8) [33], random forest (RF) using randomForest (v4.7–1.1), support vector machine (SVM) with a radial kernel using e1071 (v1.7–14) combined with caret, and extreme gradient boosting (XGBoost) using xgboost (v1.7.6). Model performance was evaluated using nested resampling with tenfold outer cross-validation repeated ten times. Within each outer training fold, hyperparameter tuning was conducted using inner fivefold cross-validation. A fixed random seed (123) was applied to ensure reproducibility, and the tuning grids are provided in Supplementary Table 2. Performance was assessed using accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, F1 score, area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC), area under the precision–recall curve, and Brier score. The accuracy, sensitivity, and specificity are reported with 95% confidence intervals and were calculated using the Wilson method [34]. All the performance metrics were computed exclusively on the outer test folds and summarized across folds. SHAP values were computed using fastshap (v0.1.0) [35] with 200 Monte Carlo simulations and a fixed random seed (123) to quantify gene-level contributions. The intersection of the top 50% of the ranked features across all four algorithms was subsequently used for PPI analysis and hub gene identification.

To assess potential information leakage associated with DEG-based feature input, all the models were re-evaluated using leakage-free nested cross-validation. Within each outer fold, DEGs were reidentified using only the outer training samples (limma–voom, with batch included as a covariate), and these training-derived DEGs were used as input features for model fitting. In addition, a batch-only baseline glmnet classifier, using batch labels as predictors, was constructed to quantify the degree of batch–phenotype association.

Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qRT–PCR)

qRT–PCR validation of epithelial and stromal tissues was performed using eight KC corneas and four control corneas. In the KC group, samples were obtained from eight patients who underwent keratoplasty. Prior to corneal excision, the epithelium was separated, enabling independent analysis of the epithelial and stromal compartments from each cornea. In the control group, stromal tissue was derived from donor corneas, two of which were also included in the RNA-seq cohort. Epithelial tissue was obtained from four healthy individuals who underwent refractive surgery.

Complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized using the iScript™ gDNA Clear cDNA Synthesis Kit (Bio-Rad, USA). The primer sequences are listed in Supplementary Table 3. qRT–PCR was conducted using fivefold diluted cDNA and the SYBR Green Pro Taq HS qPCR Kit (AG11701; Accurate Biotechnology, China) in a 20 µL reaction volume containing 10 µL of 2× SYBR Green Pro Taq HS Premix, 0.4 µL each of forwards and reverse primers (10 µM), and RNase-free water. Amplification was performed on a Gentier 96R Real-Time PCR System (Tianlong, China) using the following thermal cycling conditions: initial denaturation at 95 °C for 30 s, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 5 s and 60 °C for 30 s. Melting curve analysis was conducted to confirm specificity. Relative gene expression was quantified using the 2−ΔΔCt method, with GAPDH used as the housekeeping gene. Group differences were evaluated using unpaired two-tailed Welch’s t tests in R (v4.4.3). For multiple-gene comparisons, p values were adjusted using the Benjamini–Hochberg method. A two-sided p < 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance.

Results

Stromal sample characteristics and sequencing quality

Our stromal dataset included 31 corneas from patients with KC (K1–K31) and four corneas from control donors (N1–N4), as summarized in Supplementary Table 4; no family history of KC was reported. Sequencing quality metrics, including total reads, base-calling accuracy, and genome mapping rates for all stromal samples, are provided in Supplementary Table 5. Gene expression levels were quantified using fragments per kilobase of transcript per million mapped reads values.

Layer-specific and shared differential expression

After the data were integrated and preprocessed, PCA was conducted to assess sample variability in the epithelial datasets (GSE92935 and GSE204791) and stromal datasets (GSE204791 and our cohort). PCA plots generated prior to batch effect correction (Fig. 1a and b) revealed that clustering was influenced by the dataset origin. After batch-effect adjustment (Fig. 1c and d), technical variation was effectively reduced. Subsequent PCA plots annotated by disease status (Fig. 1e and f) revealed a clearer separation between the patient and control groups in both tissues. Differential expression analysis of the epithelial dataset revealed 272 DEGs, including 136 upregulated and 136 downregulated genes (Padj < 0.05) (Fig. 1g). The stroma dataset yielded 5,124 DEGs, with 1,505 upregulated and 3,619 downregulated (Padj < 0.05) (Fig. 1h). The overlap between dysregulated transcripts in the epithelium and stroma is shown in Fig. 1i, highlighting 19 commonly upregulated and 53 commonly downregulated DEGs.

Molecular function analysis revealed significant downregulation of ECM structural constituents, protein binding, signal transduction, ion channels, chemokines, and transcriptional activity in the KC stromal samples (Padj < 0.05) (Fig. 2a). Cellular composition analysis

In the epithelium, immune, microenvironment, and stromal scores did not differ significantly between KC and control samples (Fig. 2e). In contrast, compared with control samples, stromal KC samples had significantly lower immune, microenvironment, and stromal scores (Padj < 0.05) (Fig. 2f). Consistent with these findings, the proportions of inferred cell types were comparable between groups in the epithelium (Fig. 2g), whereas fibroblast and natural killer T-cell signatures were significantly reduced in the KC stroma (Padj < 0.05) (Fig. 2h).

Pathway-level alterations across corneal layers

GSEA revealed significantly enriched GO terms and KEGG pathways in both the epithelium and stroma of KC corneas (Padj < 0.05). In the epithelium, the enriched GO terms included upregulated nucleolus and RNA processing, as well as downregulated response to stimulus (Fig. 3a). Moreover, KEGG analysis revealed several downregulated metabolic pathways (Fig. 3b). In the stroma, the top 20 GO terms were associated with the following: downregulation of humoral and inflammatory responses, response to external biotic stimuli, ECM organization, and cellular proliferation and migration (Fig. 3c). Consistently, KEGG analysis revealed the downregulation of immune-associated signalling pathways, including cytokine–cytokine receptor interactions, chemokine signalling, and JAK–STAT signalling. Other pathways were also affected, including downregulation of neuroactive ligand–receptor interactions, and upregulation of oxidative phosphorylation, nucleocytoplasmic transport, and protein processing in the endoplasmic reticulum (Fig. 3d).

Machine learning–based classification model

To identify the most valuable biomarkers, four ML algorithms (LR, RF, SVM, and XGBoost) were employed to construct classification models for the epithelium and stroma separately. The performance of the models are summarized in Table 1. Among the models, XGBoost achieved the highest AUROC for the epithelium (Fig. 5a and b), whereas LR performed best for the stroma (Fig. 5c and d). To enhance model interpretability, we calculated SHAP values to quantify the contributions of individual genes (Fig. 5e and f). In the epithelium, the top 50% of genes (136 genes) identified across the four models intersected, yielding 26 shared genes (Fig. 5g). In the stroma, the top 50% of genes (2,562 genes) across the four models intersected, resulting in 1,211 shared genes (Fig. 5h). The top 50% of genes in LR and XGBoost were nearly identical, with 2,548 genes overlapping.

Hub gene identification

To investigate the interactions among the 85 shared DEGs in the epithelium and stroma, we constructed a PPI network and identified 10 hub genes using CytoHubba (Fig. 5i). Among these genes, CXCL10, AKR1B10, AKR1C3, HSD17B2, TYR, and GATA3 were downregulated in both tissues, whereas UGT1A6, UGT1A8, FUS, and SNTA1 were upregulated in the epithelium but downregulated in the stroma.

During model interpretation, CytoHubba analysis of the top 26 overlapping SHAP-ranked epithelial genes revealed four hub genes, namely, CXCL10, TMPRSS2, SLC1A1, and SLC5A1, all of which exhibited reduced expression. In the stroma, an analysis of the 1,211 intersecting genes revealed 10 hub genes: IL6, IL1B, CXCL8, CXCL10, ICAM1, VCAM1, ITGAM, CCL2, CCL3, and MMP9. Each of these genes demonstrated decreased expression (Fig. 5j). CXCL10 was consistently identified as a hub gene in both epithelial and stromal compartments (Fig. 5k).

Validation of gene expression patterns using public transcriptomic datasets

Validation of CXCL10, AKR1B10, AKR1C3, HSD17B2, TYR, and GATA3, which were found to be downregulated in both tissues, was performed using the full-thickness corneal RNA-seq dataset GSE77938 and the epithelial–stromal RNA-seq dataset GSE151631. All six genes were significantly downregulated (Padj < 0.05) in at least one dataset. ROC analysis further demonstrated strong discriminative potential, with each gene achieving an AUROC of 0.847 or higher in at least one dataset (Fig. 6).

Discussion

In this study, publicly available KC transcriptomic datasets were integrated with newly generated RNA-seq data, encompassing samples from European, Indian, and Chinese populations. The combined dataset included corneal epithelial samples from 13 KC patients and 9 normal controls and stromal samples from 39 KC patients and 11 normal controls. By increasing the sample size and population diversity, the dataset enhances statistical robustness and generalizability. The corneal epithelium and stroma were analysed separately, enabling layer-specific comparisons of transcriptomic changes in KC. This approach facilitated the identification of both shared molecular mechanisms and compartment-specific alterations that may be overlooked in whole-cornea or single-dataset analyses.

The epithelium had 272 DEGs whose expression increased from 27-fold to 166-fold, whereas the stroma had 5,124 DEGs whose expression was upregulated by 124-fold and downregulated by more than 1,000-fold. This extensive stromal dysregulation highlights the central role of the stroma in KC pathogenesis, which is consistent with the findings of prior studies [10].

Among the DEGs, 53 genes were consistently downregulated in both layers, accounting for 39% of the downregulated epithelial DEGs, whereas the overlap of upregulated genes was minimal. Enrichment analysis linked these shared downregulated genes to retinoid, testosterone, and aldose metabolism. Retinoid metabolism is central to ocular surface health; it regulates epithelial proliferation, differentiation, wound healing, and ECM remodelling and has anti-inflammatory effects [36]. Previous studies have demonstrated reduced retinoic acid synthesis and increased retinoic acid degradation in KC stroma [10]; our findings extend retinoid dysregulation to the epithelium. Hub gene analysis of the shared DEGs further revealed consistent downregulation of the expression of AKR1B10, a key enzyme involved in retinoid homeostasis and oxidative stress defence [37], supporting the potential involvement of retinoid metabolism in KC.

Sex hormone pathways were also disrupted. Androgens generally stabilize the tear film and protect the ocular surface, whereas oestrogens promote epithelial inflammation, increase corneal thickness, and reduce stromal stiffness [38]. Clinical studies have reported altered androgen and oestrogen levels in KC, indicating complex hormonal involvement [39]. Consistently, testosterone metabolism was disrupted in both layers in this study. The expression of the hub genes AKR1C3, which synthesizes active testosterone and oestradiol [40], and HSD17B2, which inactivates them [41], decreased across layers. GSVA further revealed stromal-specific downregulation of oestrogen signalling, indicating a stronger role for hormonal regulation in stromal homeostasis.

Aldose reductase activity was consistently reduced in both layers. GSVA also revealed epithelium-specific decreases in xenobiotic metabolism, DNA repair, and the unfolded protein response, indicating reduced detoxification and repair capacity and increased vulnerability to oxidative stress [42]. CytoHubba analysis of intersecting DEGs revealed that UGT1A6 and UGT1A8 were upregulated in the epithelium but downregulated in the stroma. As UGT enzymes mediate the detoxification of drugs, toxins, hormones, and metabolic byproducts [43], their upregulation in epithelial cells may reflect a compensatory response to surface stress. However, this response appears insufficient, while the stroma shows broader metabolic alteration.

In addition to the epithelial detoxification response, enrichment analysis and GSEA revealed upregulation of RNA processing and nucleolar functions in the epithelium. In the stroma, oxidative phosphorylation, nucleocytoplasmic transport, and endoplasmic reticulum protein processing increased, indicating disrupted transcriptional control and increased protein synthesis. GSVA further revealed stromal-specific increases in peroxisome activity and protein secretion, suggesting adaptive responses to oxidative stress and ECM turnover. Although initially protective, these responses may become insufficient under chronic stress and contribute to keratocyte dysfunction and disease progression. GSVA further revealed the downregulation of JAK–STAT3 and IL2–STAT5 signalling in both the epithelium and stroma, indicating the downregulation of immune and inflammatory responses. The downregulation of the KRAS, epithelial–mesenchymal transition, and Wnt/β-catenin pathways suggested reduced cell proliferation, survival, plasticity, and tissue remodelling, whereas a diminished UV response indicated weakened stress resistance.

Previous studies have reported inconsistent findings. Some describe epithelial upregulation of DNA repair, JAK–STAT, and TGF-β pathways [8], with increased inflammation and oxidative phosphorylation in severe disease [9]. Proteomic analyses of advanced stroma also revealed immune activation [11]. In contrast, other transcriptomic studies of stromal and whole-corneal tissue have reported the downregulation of inflammatory and repair pathways [10], including those involving TGF-β, Hippo, and Wnt signalling [25].

Spatial transcriptomics highlights regional heterogeneity, with increased central stromal inflammation and reduced peripheral inflammation [44]. This pattern aligns with a “doughnut-shaped” epithelial response, where wound-response genes are upregulated centrally but downregulated peripherally [45]. Together, these findings suggest that the observed anti-inflammatory signature may reflect peripheral downregulation and a shift from active inflammation and repair towards a more quiescent state [46]. xCell analysis provides further context by estimating cell-type composition. The immune, microenvironment, and stromal scores of the epithelial samples were similar between the KC patients and controls, with no major differences in the inferred cell populations. In contrast, the stroma showed significantly reduced scores, along with decreased numbers of fibroblasts and natural killer T cells. These findings suggest that reduced immune and stromal cell representation, rather than transcriptional downregulation alone, contributes to the observed downregulation of immune pathways. Consistently, all 10 stromal hub genes identified (namely, IL6, IL1B, CXCL8, CXCL10, CCL2, CCL3, ICAM1, VCAM1, ITGAM, and MMP9) were immune related and downregulated, indicating pronounced alterations in stromal immune activity. CXCL10, a key chemokine involved in immune cell recruitment and activation [47], was identified as a hub gene in both layers. The consistent downregulation of CXCL10, together with GATA3, a regulator of innate and adaptive immunity [48], possibly reflects decreased immune cell infiltration and activation in the stromal microenvironment.

In addition to inflammation and stress responses, enrichment and GSEA revealed the downregulation of neural-related pathways, including synaptic structures, neuronal cell bodies, and neuroactive ligand–receptor interactions, in the stroma. FUS, a hub gene involved in neurodevelopment and DNA damage repair [49], was upregulated in the epithelium but downregulated in the stroma. These findings suggest layer-specific neurogenic and neuroinflammatory mechanisms, which are consistent with reports of epithelial nerve thickening [50] and stromal nerve loss with neurodegeneration [51]. SNTA1 also exhibited opposing expression, with upregulation in the epithelium and downregulation in the stroma. Previously highlighted in another KC stromal RNA-seq study [52], SNTA1 encodes a membrane-associated adaptor protein that organizes signalling complexes [53]. However, its specific role in the cornea remains unclear. TYR, which is implicated in anterior segment development [54], was consistently downregulated in both layers, although its relevance to KC is uncertain. Similarly, SLC1A1, a glutamate transporter expressed in neuronal and epithelial cells [55], was identified as a key epithelial feature and was downregulated in both layers. Its role in KC pathophysiology also requires further investigation.

Previous transcriptomic studies involving bulk [10] or single-cell [11] sequencing revealed limited epithelial changes, mainly mitochondrial dysfunction, metabolic alterations, and reduced proliferation. By integrating multiple datasets and analysing corneal layers separately, our multilevel analyses revealed that KC involves the downregulation of immune and inflammatory signalling, stress and redox responses, cellular proliferation, tissue repair, and neural function, along with dysregulated retinoid and steroid hormone metabolism across corneal layers. Although compensatory responses were observed, they were insufficient to counteract the overall pathological trend. Compared with the epithelium, the stroma exhibited more extensive and profound changes. Several genes, including CXCL10, AKR1B10, AKR1C3, HSD17B2, TYR, and GATA3, were consistently downregulated in both tissues, whereas UGT1A6, UGT1A8, FUS, and SNTA1 were upregulated in the epithelium but downregulated in the stroma. CXCL10 was repeatedly identified across multiple analyses and in both compartments.

To prioritize candidate genes, we integrated differential expression analysis with four ML approaches, enabling the identification of genes consistently ranked across models with distinct inductive biases [56]. LR served as an interpretable linear baseline model; however, its assumption of linear relationships may limit its ability to capture complex gene–gene interactions [57]. RF and XGBoost, which are both ensemble tree-based methods, can model nonlinear patterns and interaction effects. RF is relatively robust to noise and less prone to overfitting, although its variable importance measures may be biased [58]. XGBoost offers efficient optimization and effective handling of missing data but may overfit without appropriate hyperparameter tuning [57]. SVMs are well suited for high-dimensional transcriptomic data with limited sample sizes, although their performance depends on careful parameter selection [58]. While applying multiple algorithms enhances robustness and reduces model-specific bias, importantly, feature importance reflects statistical associations rather than causal biological roles [12]. Similarly, PPI prioritization reflects topological properties within PPI networks rather than mechanistic centrality. Therefore, the genes identified in this study should be interpreted as candidates for further investigation rather than as definitive drivers of KC.

Notably, DEG-based feature filtering was performed prior to model training to obtain a stable gene set for downstream analyses. Although this approach facilitates consistent gene prioritization, it may introduce optimistic bias. To address this issue, leakage-free nested cross-validation was performed, in which DEG selection was repeated within each training fold. The stromal models maintained similarly high performance, suggesting that the observed signal was not solely driven by feature-selection leakage. However, batch-only baseline evaluation revealed a moderate association between batch and phenotype in the stromal datasets. Therefore, the performance of the stromal models should be interpreted as evidence of strong compartment-specific transcriptomic separation within the analysed datasets rather than as universally generalizable diagnostic performance, underscoring the need for validation in independent cohorts.

These findings suggest several important directions for future research. First, functional studies are needed to validate the roles of prioritized genes, particularly those consistently identified across analytical approaches, such as CXCL10 and AKR1B10. Second, given the distinct yet interconnected alterations observed in the epithelium and stroma, spatial transcriptomics or single-cell approaches may help clarify the cellular origins and spatial organization of these changes. In particular, the integration of single-cell–resolution analyses is essential for distinguishing true transcriptional regulation from shifts in cellular composition, thereby improving the interpretation of bulk RNA-seq data. Importantly, the pathway downregulation observed in this study should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating rather than evidence of direct functional suppression. The observed changes in metabolic pathways require validation through complementary approaches, including targeted metabolomics, protein-level quantification, and enzyme activity assays, to determine whether the observed transcriptomic alterations are translated into functional effects. In addition, larger and more diverse prospective cohorts are needed to assess the robustness and generalizability of these findings and to evaluate the potential of candidate genes as biomarkers or therapeutic targets.

Limitations

The integration of RNA-seq with public microarray datasets may have introduced batch effects, despite normalization. In addition, differences in tissue preservation and storage conditions across KC and control samples represent a potential source of technical variability. The overall sample size was relatively small, and the ML models lacked an independent testing cohort. Therefore, larger, well-characterized cohorts and experimental studies are needed to validate biomarkers and clarify their mechanistic roles in KC.

Conclusions

This study provides a comprehensive, layer-resolved transcriptomic analysis of KC, identifying coordinated alterations in immune, metabolic, and repair-related pathways across the corneal epithelium and stroma. Although stromal changes were more pronounced, shared molecular signatures suggest common underlying processes. By integrating differential expression and ML–based prioritization, we identified candidate genes associated with KC. Further functional and multiomics studies are needed to elucidate the mechanistic role of these genes in KC pathogenesis.
