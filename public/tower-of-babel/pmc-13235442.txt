Migration of a cutter knife blade into the abdominal aorta following penetrating chest trauma: A case report
Authors: Masashi Kabasawa, Masao Hirano, Hiroki Ikeuchi, Mitsuru Aoki, Kozo Matsuo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
We report a rare case of intravascular migration of a cutter knife blade after penetrating chest trauma. A 40-year-old man presented with lower abdominal pain 3 months after an unreported occupational injury to the left anterior chest wall. Computed tomography scan revealed a plate-like foreign body embedded in the distal abdominal aorta and a localized hematoma adjacent to the cardiac apex. The blade was successfully removed during semi-emergent abdominal aortic replacement. This case highlights the potential for delayed migration of sharp intravascular foreign bodies and the importance of whole body imaging in patients with atypical symptoms after penetrating trauma.

Intravascular foreign bodies are rare but potentially serious conditions that may lead to vascular injury, embolization, or infection. Although various foreign bodies such as needles and catheter fragments have been reported, migration of a sharp object after penetrating chest trauma is extremely uncommon. We report a rare case of a cutter knife blade that migrated into the abdominal aorta.

Case report

A 40-year-old man had come to Japan as a migrant worker. While working with a cutter knife, he accidentally sustained a penetrating injury to the left anterior chest wall. Due to language barriers and financial constraints, he did not seek medical attention and removed the cutter knife himself. He remained asymptomatic thereafter.

Approximately 3 months after the injury, he developed lower abdominal pain and visited a nearby hospital. Conservative treatment with analgesics was initiated; however, his symptoms did not improve. He was subsequently referred to our hospital for further evaluation. Contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT) suggested the presence of an intravascular foreign body in the abdominal aorta, and the patient was referred to our cardiovascular surgery department.

Chest CT scan revealed a localized hematoma adjacent to the cardiac apex on the left anterior chest wall (Fig 1). The patient reported that he had accidentally stabbed his left anterior chest wall while using a cutter knife and removed it immediately after the injury. Although the exact mechanism of blade detachment remains unclear, it is possible that excessive force during withdrawal caused separation of the blade, leaving a fragment within the body.

The operation was performed via a midline laparotomy. The retroperitoneum was opened, and the abdominal aorta was exposed. The infrarenal abdominal aorta and both common iliac arteries were encircled with vessel loops. After systemic heparinization, the infrarenal abdominal aorta was cross-clamped, and a longitudinal aortotomy was made. Severe inflammatory thickening of the aortic wall was observed, extending from just distal to the origin of the inferior mesenteric artery to the aortic bifurcation (Fig 4, A). After controlling the lumbar arteries, inspection of the aortic lumen revealed a cutter knife blade measuring approximately 10 mm in width and 40 mm in length. The blade was embedded in the aortic wall, extending from the abdominal aorta distal to the inferior mesenteric artery to the origin of the right common iliac artery (Figs 4, B, C). Given the extent of intimal injury and inflammatory changes, abdominal aortic replacement was performed using a 16 × 11 mm bifurcated prosthetic graft (J-Graft, Japan Lifeline Co) (Fig 4, D). Written informed consent for publication of this case and accompanying images was obtained from the patient.

Discussion

Intravascular foreign bodies are most commonly associated with iatrogenic causes, such as catheter-related procedures.1 In contrast, traumatic intravascular foreign bodies are rare, and only a limited number of cases involving sharp objects, including ballpoint pens and fish bones, have been reported.2,3 Migration of a sharp metallic object within the arterial system, particularly with delayed symptom onset, is exceedingly uncommon.

In the present case, the patient did not seek medical attention at the time of injury, making it impossible to directly confirm the route of intravascular entry. However, the presence of a penetrating wound on the left anterior chest wall corresponding with the width of the cutter knife blade, together with a localized hematoma adjacent to the cardiac apex on CT scan, strongly suggests that the blade entered the left-sided cardiac system. Furthermore, there were no subsequent traumatic events or iatrogenic procedures that could explain the intravascular entry. Taken together, these findings indicate that migration from the cardiac apex into the systemic circulation represents the most plausible explanation.

Another notable feature of this case is the prolonged asymptomatic period of approximately 3 months before clinical presentation. Sharp intravascular foreign bodies are known to cause potentially fatal complications, including vascular perforation, thrombosis, embolization, and infection. Nevertheless, such foreign bodies may remain clinically silent for an extended period, leading to delayed diagnosis. This case underscores the importance of maintaining a high index of suspicion for retained or migrated sharp foreign bodies, even when initial symptoms after penetrating trauma are minimal.

Regarding surgical management, an endovascular approach was considered; however, it was deemed unsuitable in this case. The foreign body was firmly embedded in the aortic wall, and there was a significant risk of vascular injury or distal embolization during endovascular retrieval. Therefore, open surgical removal was considered the safest and most reliable option. Next, simple removal of the foreign body with primary repair of the aortic wall was considered. However, this approach was deemed inappropriate due to several concerns: significant intimal injury at the site of blade impaction, the risk of localized dissection or postoperative pseudoaneurysm formation, and the inability to completely rule out infection of the aortic wall caused by contamination of the cutter knife blade. In addition, marked inflammatory thickening of the aortic wall raised concerns about luminal narrowing after primary closure. Considering the patient's young age and the importance of long-term durability, abdominal aortic replacement with a prosthetic graft was selected as the most appropriate treatment strategy.

Although excision of the aortic wall for histological examination and culture was considered, it was not performed because there were no macroscopic findings suggestive of active infection. Therefore, prolonged antibiotic therapy was not administered. Instead, standard perioperative antibiotic prophylaxis was used, and careful postoperative monitoring, including laboratory data, was undertaken. No evidence of infection was observed postoperatively.

Although intravascular foreign bodies are rare, such cases may be under-reported, particularly in patients who do not seek immediate medical attention after trauma. This rare case provides important clinical lessons for both trauma and vascular surgeons, demonstrating that sharp foreign bodies can migrate intravascularly after penetrating chest trauma, remain asymptomatic for a prolonged period, and present with delayed, nonspecific symptoms. Whole body CT scans are essential for accurate diagnosis and for determining the optimal therapeutic approach in such cases.
