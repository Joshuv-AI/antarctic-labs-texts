The impact of different decibels of ambient noise on attention
Authors: Fangfang Liu, Fang Liu, Wen Wen, Yuhang Li, Yulin Duan, Shuang Wei, Peiyao Liu, Min Huang, Fan Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAmbient noise is often perceived as a distractor; however, some individuals may benefit from certain levels of ambient noise to enhance cognitive performance. Despite this, the optimal range of ambient noise for improving attention remains unclear. To disclose this relationship, thirty-eight healthy college students were recruited to perform a 6 ⋅ 6 Schulte grid test under varying ambient noise levels. All participants performed the tasks under three noise conditions presented in a fixed order of increasing intensity (20–40 dB, 40–60 dB, and 60–80 dB). Functional near-infrared spectroscopy (fNIRS) was employed to measure dynamic changes in hemoglobin levels, while facial expressions and physiological signs were also assessed. The findings demonstrated that 40–60 dB group exhibited strongest visual brain functional connectivity. Among the three groups, the 40–60 dB group exhibited the highest values for anger expression, surprise expression, and heart rate (HR) (all p < 0.0001). Additionally, this group showed significantly higher heart rate variability (SDNN) compared to the 60–80 dB group (p < 0.05). The region of interest (ROI) pars triangularis Broca’s area left~ pars triangularis Broca’s area right revealed a significant difference (P = 0.004). Furthermore, the pairwise post hoc tests revealed the significant differences between the 20–40 dB group and 60–80 dB group (P = 0.007), as well as between the 40–60 dB group and 60–80 dB group (P = 0.017). In conclusion, moderate noise (40–60 dB) appears to induce an optimal level of arousal. In contrast, high-intensity noise (60–80 dB) likely prompts functional reorganization between the bilateral triangular parts of Broca’s area to support cognitive control and interference suppression.The moderate intensity of noise may arouse the attention.Keywords: Attention, Ambient noise, fNIRS, Facial expressions, Life signsSubject terms: Neuroscience, Physiology

Introduction

Ambient noise, the pervasive and unstructured sound present in environments, is often considered detrimental to cognitive performance, particularly attention. Some studies indicated that exposure to high-intensity noise (e.g., > 85 dBA) reliably impairs functions such as reaction time and memory1,2. Even at lower levels, noise could be subjectively distracting and increase stress, potentially undermining task focus, as reported in occupational settings like emergency medicine3.

However, the relationship between noise and cognition may not be linear. The Yerkes-Dodson law posits an inverted U-shaped relationship between arousal and performance, suggesting that moderate levels of stimulation could be facilitative. Supporting this, some research indicates that intermediate noise levels might enhance certain cognitive domains, such as creativity3. This points to a potential dual role of ambient noise: harmful at extremes but possibly beneficial at moderate intensities. The neurophysiological mechanisms underlying this complex relationship, especially concerning attentional networks, remain unclear.

The critical pathway linking ambient noise to attention lies in the modulation of the dorsal attention network by the arousal system4,5. Theoretical models suggest that moderate noise induces an optimal state of arousal, which enhances neural synchronization within frontoparietal regions, manifesting as increased functional connectivity6. Conversely, high-intensity noise may trigger over-arousal or stress responses that disrupt the integration of attentional networks, leading to diminished connectivity efficiency7.

Attention, the process of selectively allocating neural resources, is often dichotomized into bottom-up and top-down systems. Top-down attention, crucial for planned behavior, is subserved by networks including the dorsal frontoparietal system8. When performing a visual task in a noisy environment, the brain must integrate competing auditory input with goal-directed visual processing, engaging a distributed network spanning frontal, parietal, and temporal regions9,10.

Building on the arousal-performance framework11 and the established neurobiology of attention12, this study specifically investigated the effects of ambient noise intensity on top-down attention and its underlying neural correlates. The Schulte grid task has been widely adopted in multiple studies as a standard paradigm for visual search and sustained attention, often combined with EEG/ERP to investigate attention mechanisms and the impact of environmental stimulation on top-down attention and cognitive performance13,14. While the task inherently involves visual scanning and processing speed, it requires subjects to locate numbers in sequence. This process continuously demands goal-directed visual search, working memory updating, and interference suppression. These are core cognitive functions that rely predominantly on top-down attention control. These executive components rely predominantly on top-down attentional control to coordinate cognitive resources, thereby making the task a valid probe for investigating the integrity of attentional networks under environmental noise.

The attention may be affect by many outside factors, including the noise. However, which range intensity of noise may affect the attention remains unclear, moreover the related functional connectivity dynamic changes and further operation ability in the brain remaining unknown. Therefore, We formulated the hypothesis that as ambient noise levels increase, brain functional connectivity become more complex when subjects perform attention tasks. To test this hypothesis, thirty-eight healthy participants (16 males and 22 females) were recruited. Participants were invited to complete three Schulte grid tasks15, which consisting of a 6 ⋅ 6 matrix. These tasks were carried out under three consecutive decibel levels of ambient noise conditions (20–40, 40–60, and 60–80 dB). Concurrently, to capture participants’ facial expressions high-resolution camera was positioned in front of them. To measure their brain activity during the tasks a functional near-infrared spectroscopy (fNIRS) device was worn by participants throughout the experiment. This design allows us to elucidate how different noise intensities modulate attentional neurophysiology and to identify a potential optimal range for cognitive engagement. The novelty of this work is to identify this critical noise intensity range, and the core contribution is to reveal the dynamic changes in functional connectivity and their impact on operational ability.

Methods

Ethics statements

This study was previously approved by Chengdu medical college ethics committee (2023NO.113). The experimental procedures employed in this study were non-invasive and non-contact, ensuring participant safety and comfort. Throughout the experiment, professional staff closely monitored the entire process to ensure a smooth and efficient execution.

Sample size calculation

An a priori power analysis was conducted using G*Power 3.1.9.7 (Faul et al., 2009). Given the within-subject design of the present study, the test family was set as F tests and the statistical test was repeated-measures ANOVA (within factors). Assuming a medium effect size of Cohen’s f = 0.25, a significance level of α = 0.05, a desired statistical power of 1 − β = 0.90, one group, three measurements, a correlation among repeated measures of 0.50, and a nonsphericity correction of 1, the required minimum sample size was estimated to be 36 participants. Considering potential data loss due to motion artifacts or poor signal quality in fNIRS recordings, 38 healthy participants were recruited for the present study, including 16 males and 22 females.

Participants

A total of 38 participants (16 males, 22 females) with a mean age of 20.0 ± 0.9 years were recruited for this study. Inclusive criteria consisted of subjects who agreed to have their physiological data recorded and had no history of mental or other psychological diseases or significant intellectual disabilities. All participants provided written informed consent.

Test procedure

At the beginning, participants were instructed to wear the headcap, which housed fNIRS opcode detectors and receivers. Participants completed three 6 × 6 Schulte squares of comparable difficulty, with completion times for each task provided in Supplementary Table 2. Additionally three different levels of ambient noise(20 ~ 40 dB, 40 ~ 60 dB, 60 ~ 80 dB) were played orderly during the tests, with a 10-second break separating each test, see Fig. 1. All test procedures were pre-programmed using E-Studio3.

General workflow.

Face expressions and life signs

The high solution camera (Logitech B525 web camera) was used to capture the full-face expression throughout the entire process. The Noldus 7.0 face reader software analyzed face expressions, including neutral, happy, sad, angry, surprised, scared, and disgusted. Additionally, life signs such as heart rate (HR) and Standard Deviation of Normal-to-Normal heartbeat (SDNN) were measured via the Mindray VS 900 device.

Data acquisition by fNIRS

All fNIRS signals were recorded by a multichannel fNIRS system (NirSmartII-3000 A, Danyang Huichuang Medical Equipment Co., Ltd., China) using two wavelengths (730 and 850 nm) at a sampling rate of 11 Hz. The stretchable head cap, which covered the whole brain area including frontal lobe, bilateral temporal lobe, parietal lobe and occipital lobe, featured 24 sources and 16 detectors (source-to-detector separation, 3 cm) arranged in 48 measurement channels, see Fig. 2. The spatial locations of the sources, detectors and anchor points (at Nz, Cz, Al, Ar and Iz, according to the standard international 10–20 system of electrode placement) were measured using an on-head electromagnetic 3D digitizer device (Patriot, Polhemus, USA). Supplementary Table 1 provides anatomical regions corresponding to each channel and their respective coverage percentages.

Schematic layout of the functional near-infrared spectroscopy (fNIRS) measurement channels.

Data preprocessing of fNIRS signals

The fNIRS signals were preprocessed using NirSpark V1.8.1 (Danyang Huichuang Medical Instrument Co., Ltd.). First, motion artefacts were detected (change in amplitude > 0.5 and/or change in standard deviation > 6 over a 1-second time period), followed by digital bandpass filtering of the raw optical density signals within the 0.01–0.2 Hz range. Next, relative attention curves of oxygenated hemoglobin (HbO), Hb and total Hb were obtained using the modified Beer-Lambert law16. The pathlength factor for each wavelength was set to 6, resulting in relative attention signals for HbO, Hb and total Hb. Finally, the channels were checked for integrity after preprocessing and excluded if any data were missing. After preprocessing, the integrity of each channels was checked, and any channel with missing data or poor quality was excluded. Specifically, a channel was excluded if the data loss exceeded 10% for that channel during the entire measurement session. Additionally, any channel with a signal standard deviation greater than a predefined threshold (e.g., amplitude change > 0.5 or a quality indicator below an acceptable threshold) was also excluded.

Functional connectivity

Pearson correlation coefficients were calculated for HbO in each of the channels on the time series. A 48 × 48 correlation matrix was constructed from the 48-channel data. To improve normality, the correlation coefficients were converted into Z-scores using Fisher r–z transformation. These transformed values were defined as functional connectivity strength between channels. The brain regions were categorized according to Brodmann’s system, with regions of interest including premotor cortex, inferior frontal gyrus, pars triangularis of Broca’s area, frontopolar area, primary somatosensory cortex, primary motor cortex, dorsolateral prefrontal cortex, supramarginal gyrus, visual language center and visual association cortex (see Supplementary Table 1).These regions were the core cortical networks involved in attentional resource allocation17,18, inhibitory control of external interference, visual spatial search19, and attention-motor coordination20, which was highly consistent with the core scientific question of this study.

Statistical analysis

Raw data were entered and organized using Microsoft Office 365, and all statistical analyses were performed using Stata 18.0. Prior to formal analysis, the data were screened for completeness, outliers, and recording errors. Normality of quantitative variables was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Normally distributed data are presented as mean±standard deviation (SD), whereas non-normally distributed data are presented as median (interquartile range, IQR). Mauchly’s test was used to assess sphericity, and the Greenhouse–Geisser correction was applied when necessary. Since the same participants were measured under three different noise-intensity conditions, one-way repeated-measures ANOVA was used to compare HbO dynamic changes, facial expressions, and vital signs for normally distributed data, followed by Bonferroni-corrected post hoc pairwise comparisons when appropriate. For non-normally distributed repeated-measures data, the Friedman test was used, followed by Wilcoxon signed-rank tests with Bonferroni correction for post hoc comparisons when appropriate. Categorical variables were analyzed using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. A two-sided p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Discussion

This study recruited 38 subjects to investigate the effects of three sequential intensity ranges decibel levels (20–40 dB, 40–60 dB, 60–80 dB) of ambient noise on human attention. Although many studies have confirmed the distracting and negative effects of noise22–25, Liu et al. proposed that noise can have positive effects in certain situations. Moreover, people’s perception of noise is gradually shifting from controlling noise to utilizing it26, which is consistent with our conclusion. Additionally, we found that moderate noise could enhance attentional physiology resonates with research demonstrating facilitative effects of intermediate noise levels on other cognitive domains. Such as Mehta R et al. suggests that moderate (70dB) and low (50dB) levels of ambient noise can improve creativity27. This consistency across studies supported the notion that a moderate increase in environmental stimulation might optimize certain cognitive states.

In this study, the analysis of visual brain functional connectivity indicated that subjects exhibited the strongest functional connectivity when ambient noise levels ranged from 40 to 60 dB. In contrast, the effects observed at the other two decibel ranges (20 to 40 dB and 60 to 80 dB) differed significantly from those noted at 40 to 60 dB. This phenomenon may be explained within the framework of the Yerkes-Dodson law, which posits an inverted U-shaped relationship between arousal and performance11. The moderate noise level (40–60 dB) likely induced an optimal state of arousal, as suggested by heightened autonomic arousal (elevated heart rate and SDNN) and distinct emotional responses (increased negative emotions like anger and surprise). This moderate arousal might have enhanced attentional engagement by narrowing the focus of cognitive resources onto the task at hand, a mechanism supported by theories linking emotional arousal to attention prioritization28. Consequently, we observed the most robust functional connectivity during this condition. One possible interpretation is that the stabilized neural activity, indicated by the reduced intra-group variability in HbO levels, reflects efficient and focused information processing This efficient processing could, in turn, be associated with the observed strengthening of functional connectivity between key neural pathways29,30 The results of a similar study conducted by Hiwa et al.31 were also worth noting. They found that participants who perceived white noise as pleasant exhibited enhanced functional connectivity in memory-related brain regions, which was accompanied by improved task performance. These results also indicate that the effects of white noise on brain activity vary significantly across different groups.

The results of the one-way ANOVA indicated that the functional connectivity strength between the left inferior prefrontal gyrus and the pars triangularis of the right Broca’s area differed significantly across the three noise conditions. However, corrected post hoc pairwise comparisons did not reveal statistically significant differences between the groups. Methodologically, this may reflect the limited statistical power of the present experimental design (such as a sample size of 38 participants and a 20 dB increment in noise levels) in detecting subtle, stepwise changes in this connectivity. Additionally, substantial inter-individual variability may have obscured the group-level mean differences.

From a neurofunctional standpoint, the the left inferior prefrontal gyrus primarily supports higher-order functions such as syntactic processing, verbal working memory, and cognitive control32,33. The triangular part of the right Broca’s area, as a homologous brain region to the left language area, is not a region that dominantly generates language in the traditional sense. It plays key roles not only in prosody perception, attention allocation, but also in the coordinated regulation of bilateral brain networks34,35. As proposed by Novick et al. (2005), the left inferior frontal gyrus is a crucial hub for domain-general cognitive control, specifically responsible for detecting and resolving incompatible stimulus representations and overriding highly regularized, automatic processes36.The Schulte Grid task used in this study focuses on visuospatial attention and working memory and does not involve overt language production. Therefore, the connectivity between these two regions may operate in a dynamic adjustment state across varying noise intensities to help maintain task performance, though its specific adjustment pattern was not clearly delineated in the present study.

Furthermore, the functional connectivity strength between the left and right triangular part of Broca’s area showed significant differences across the three noise-level conditions. Corrected post-hoc pairwise comparisons further confirmed that these differences specifically originated from the comparisons between the 60–80 dB group and the 20–40 dB group, as well as between the 60–80 dB group and the 40–60 dB group.

Under low to moderate noise environments (20–60 dB), the functional connectivity between the bilateral triangular parts of Broca’s area remained stable, suggesting that within this noise range, the brain exhibits strong resistance to noise interference and can maintain attention-related cognitive functions through intrinsic bilateral coordination mechanisms without significantly altering interregional connectivity strength. However, when noise intensity increased to 60–80 dB, the functional connectivity within this region changed significantly. A plausible explanation for this phenomenon is that 60–80 dB noise may consume substantial cognitive resources, interfering with the execution of visuospatial attention tasks, thereby compelling the brain to reconfigure the connectivity pattern between the bilateral triangular parts of Broca’s area to coordinate cognitive control and interference suppression functions (Fig. 8). This interpretation is supported by findings from several similar studies. Liang Peng et al. found a statistically significant interaction effect between electromagnetic radiation (430.1357 MHz, 10.75 W/m2) and low-frequency noise (0–200 Hz, 72.9 dB) when simultaneously exposing subjects to both for 30 min. We conclude that exposure to this complex environment can cause a statistically significant increase in the mental workload level of operators, and even alterations in their cognitive function37. Xu et al. also found changes in cognitive abilities and functional networks in rats after exposure to broadband intense noise in animal experiments38.

The noise affects brain network.

This study has several limitations. The sample consisted only of a small number of healthy young university students, which limits the generalizability of the findings to other age groups or populations with different cognitive profiles. Additionally, potential confounding factors such as medication history and lifestyle variables were not systematically controlled, even though they may influence neurovascular function. Furthermore, key acoustic characteristics of noise, including spectral and temporal properties, were not considered, although they may meaningfully modulate attentional load. Moreover, the three noise conditions were presented in a fixed order. This design introduces potential order effects, such as practice effects or fatigue, which may confound the observed neural and behavioral responses. Specifically, performance changes in the high-intensity condition could be partially attributed to time-on-task effects rather than noise intensity alone. Future studies should adopt a randomized or counterbalanced design to better isolate the specific effects of noise intensity.

Conclusion

Moderate noise (40–60 dB) appears to induce an optimal level of arousal, which may facilitate attentional engagement by enhancing the efficiency of cognitive resource allocation. In contrast, high-intensity noise (60–80 dB) is associated with altered functional connectivity between the bilateral triangular parts of Broca’s area to support cognitive control and interference suppression. Therefore, the present study suggests that moderate noise may act as an attention modulator in young healthy adults. This finding implies that calibrating the acoustic environment, rather than enforcing mandatory silence, could potentially benefit cognitive performance in settings populated by this demographic, such as university libraries and educational spaces. However, given the specific sample used, caution is warranted when generalizing these findings to other age groups or clinical populations.
