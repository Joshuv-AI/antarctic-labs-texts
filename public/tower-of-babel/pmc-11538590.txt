Catch the wind: Optimizing wind turbine power generation by addressing wind veer effects
Authors: Linyue Gao, Christopher Milliren, Teja Dasari, Alexander A Knoll, Jiarong Hong
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractWind direction variability with height, known as “wind veer,” results in power losses for wind turbines (WTs) that rely on single-point wind measurements at the turbine nacelles. To address this challenge, we introduce a yaw control strategy designed to optimize turbine alignment by adjusting the yaw angle based on specific wind veer conditions, thereby boosting power generation efficiency. This strategy integrates modest yaw offset angles into the existing turbine control systems via a yaw-bias-look-up table, which correlates the adjustments with wind speed, and wind veer data. We evaluated the effectiveness of this control strategy through extensive month-long field campaigns for an individual utility-scale WT and at a commercial wind farm. This included controlling one turbine using our strategy against nine others in the vicinity using standard controls with LiDAR-derived wind veer data and a separate 2.5 MW instrumented research turbine continuously managed using our method with wind profiles provided by meteorological towers. Results from these campaigns demonstrated notable energy gains, with potential net gains exceeding 10% during extreme veering conditions. Our economic analysis, factoring in various elements, suggests an annual net gain of up to approximately $700 K for a 100-MW wind farm, requiring minimal additional investment, with potential for even larger gains in offshore settings with the power of individual turbines exceeding 10 MW nowadays. Overall, our findings underscore the considerable opportunities to improve individual turbine performance under realistic atmospheric conditions through advanced, cost-effective control strategies.

Significance Statement

Wind veer, i.e. changes in wind direction with height, impacts wind turbine power generation. Existing control systems, relying on single-point measurements, fail to adjust for this variability, resulting in reduced efficiency. We introduce a novel yaw control strategy that precisely adjusts turbine alignment based on specific wind veer conditions, boosting energy production. This strategy, proven to exceed 10% energy gains in extreme conditions during field tests, requires no extra instrumentation, thus providing a cost-effective enhancement for both existing and new turbines, especially in offshore settings with larger units. Our findings pave the way for developing innovative, practical control strategies that optimize turbine power generation under other complex field conditions such as strong shear, low-level jets, stratification, and precipitation.

Introduction

In 2023, the global wind energy sector experienced substantial growth, with 117 GW of new wind power capacity added to power grids, reaching a milestone cumulative capacity of 1 TW (1). Despite this progress, the Global Wind Energy Council (2) underscores that this third-best year falls short of aligning with a net-zero trajectory or meeting the 1.5 °C global warming goal outlined in the Paris Agreement by 2100. To effectively address climate targets, it is imperative to quadruple the current annual rate of wind installations (3). In tandem with accelerating the deployment of new wind farms, enhancing the energy generation of existing facilities through innovative techniques is paramount.

As wind turbines grow in size to generate higher power output, their interaction with the atmospheric boundary layer (ABL) becomes increasingly intricate. A noteworthy element of this complexity is the variability in wind direction with height, commonly known as “wind veer (γ)” (4). Wind veer occurs due to changes in the balance of forces within ABL, where near-surface winds are angled toward low pressure as a result of the interplay between the pressure-gradient force and the combined effects of the Coriolis and friction forces. In the Northern Hemisphere (NH), the apparent Coriolis force acts 90° to the right of the wind, while in the Southern Hemisphere (SH), it acts to the left. As friction decreases with height, winds become more geostrophic, with the pressure-gradient force balancing only the Coriolis force, causing winds to flow perpendicular to both and rotate clockwise with height in the NH and counterclockwise in the SH, known as veering winds. Two additional mechanisms that can influence wind veer are the impact of horizontal temperature gradients (e.g. thermal winds over the North American Great Plains) on wind flows and the inertial oscillation in the ABL caused by changes in atmospheric stability (5). The inertial oscillation rotates eventually aligning with the mean wind overnight to form a jet structure, known as low-level jets, which occur globally (6, 7) and act as additional driver for wind veer (5, 8). These effects can also result in backing winds, which shift counterclockwise with height in the NH and clockwise in the SH. Wind veer exhibits significant diurnal cycles (9), closely tied to the variations in atmospheric stability (10). Sanchez Gomez and Lundquist (11) analyzed observations from a field campaign in Iowa and noted that wind veer becomes more pronounced at night due to the suppression of convective turbulence, boundary-layer decoupling, and a decrease in boundary layer height. Similar diurnal variations in wind veer were also observed in our previous study in Minnesota (12).

In the past 5 years, a growing number of studies have been conducted to investigate the impact of wind veer on wind turbine power production (12–14), and wind turbine wake effects (15–22). Our prior investigation (12) revealed that veering winds lead to a substantial decrease in power output (approximately 6.5% for a 2.5-MW turbine located in Minnesota) for clockwise rotating wind turbines in the NH, while backing winds result in a slight increase in power (around 1.6%). Gomez and Lundquist (20) found that significant wind veering with minimal speed shear can lead to up to 13% underperformance in wind turbines, based on their field campaign in Iowa. Theoretical analysis, utilizing the blade element velocity triangle, further substantiated these trends, indicating that wind turbines rotating in the opposite direction would exhibit contrasting effects—power losses with backing winds and power gains with veering winds. Similar conclusions on the impact of wind veer were drawn by Englberger et al. (23) through numerical simulations focusing on turbine wakes. Based on our findings, we recommend a systematic assessment of wind veer characteristics during the wind farm planning phase to optimize wind turbine energy production. This optimization involves selecting suitable types of wind turbines for specific target wind sites. Following the IEC 61400-50-1:2022 standard (24), on-site meteorological measurements conducted over a 1-year period are essential for wind resource assessment, with wind veer considered a key factor in this evaluation. Specifically, if veering winds in the NH significantly influence a site, counterclockwise rotating turbines are more likely to yield higher energy output. Conversely, clockwise rotating turbines are more advantageous for sites where backing winds prevail. However, due to the predetermined design, existing wind turbines are unlikely to change their rotor rotational direction, underscoring the necessity for alternative techniques or strategies to address this issue.

Most utility-scale wind turbines employ yaw control systems to optimize energy capture by aligning the rotor-swept area with the incoming wind direction. Typically, wind direction is measured by a cup-and-vane anemometer or sonic anemometer on the nacelle's roof (25). In conditions lacking wind veer, a single-point measurement of wind direction at the hub height suffices to determine turbine orientation. However, the presence of wind veer across the rotor introduces bias angles to each blade section, indicating that hub-height wind direction may not be the most suitable orientation for the turbine. To address this issue and maximize power generation, we propose a novel control modification strategy, termed “wind veer control strategy,” specifically tailored for existing utility-scale wind turbines operating under wind veer conditions. To enhance and implement this strategy, we conducted an extensive field campaign (namely “LSFC”) in 2021 at a large-scale wind farm exceeding 100 MW in capacity. Additionally, in 2022, we executed an advanced field campaign (namely “Eolos station field campaign [ESFC]”) focusing on the automated version of the “wind veer control strategy,” targeting a utility-scale well-instrumented 2.5 MW wind turbine. The potential economic advantages introduced by the suggested strategy were further deliberated, along with its impact on wind turbine structural loadings. This study presents practical solutions for wind turbines to leverage wind veer phenomena, contributing valuable insights to the ongoing development of wind turbine control systems.

Wind veer control strategy

We propose a “wind veer control strategy” to compensate energy losses resulting from severe wind veer (abs(γ)≥0.1∘/m). Figure 1A outlines the flowchart detailing the implementation of the wind veer control strategy. Initially, measurements of wind directions at heights corresponding to wind turbine hub height (θhub), blade top tip (θTT), and blade bottom tip (θBT) are required to characterize wind veer. The measurements can be performed using vertical profiling LiDAR (13, 26), nacelle LiDAR, or a well-equipped nearby meteorological tower. These techniques effectively characterize wind veer properties and avoid potential uncertainties in inflow measurements caused by drastic flow changes behind the rotor. In particular, the nacelle lidar has recently gained popularity for both onshore and offshore turbines, significantly lowering the barriers to implementing this strategy (27). When using vertical profiling/nacelle LiDAR or a meteorological tower, it is crucial to establish a specific wind direction range to mitigate the influence of turbine wake on the measurements, i.e. θhub∈[θ1,θ2]. Wind veer across turbine rotor span (γ=(θTT−θBT)/D, where D refers to turbine rotor diameter), lower rotor wind veer (γL=(θhub−θBT)/D/2), and upper rotor wind veer (γU=(θTT−θhub)/D/2) are then estimated. For the subsequent analysis, it is recommended to utilize 5- to 15-min averaged data (depending on the turbine model and its yaw control system) obtained from high-frequency (e.g. 1 Hz) raw measurements, with data availability threshold greater than 60%. This ensures robust statistical representation and prevents excessive high-frequency yawing that could have adverse effects on turbine structure loading. Upon detecting a severe wind veer capable of causing discernible power loss, an adjustment angle will be introduced into the existing turbine yaw system. This adjustment follows a predefined look-up table describing the offset value as a function of both the upper rotor wind veer and the wind speed at hub height, i.e. βo=f(γU,Uhub). In comparison to γ, γU is a more representative measure as it remains unaffected by tower shadow effects, making it the preferred choice for use in the look-up table. It is essential to note that this look-up table is contingent upon the type and model of wind turbine. The resulting power variation from the introduced yaw offset is suggested to be assessed by comparing it with the power production under no control and no wind veer conditions for future decision-making. The wind veer control strategy is recommended for two specific scenarios: clockwise-rotating wind turbines experiencing veering winds (Fig. 1B) and counterclockwise-rotating wind turbines facing backing winds (Fig. 1C) in the NH. It is important to emphasize that wind direction data obtained from LiDAR or meteorological towers must be input into the turbine control unit to facilitate seamless and automatic consecutive control. A specific example about how to implement the proposed strategy for a turbine with a diameter of 100 m can be found in the Supplementary material.

Eolos station and data processing in ESFC

Eolos Wind Research Field Station at the University of Minnesota (referred to as Eolos station hereafter) holds a 130-m meteorological tower (referred to as the met tower henceforth) located 170 m to the south (aligned with the prevailing wind direction of 180° according to long-term historical data) of the Eolos turbine (30). This met tower is equipped with four high-resolution sonic anemometers (Campbell Scientific, CSAT3) with a sampling rate of 20 Hz mounted at four representative elevations, i.e. rotor top tip (129 m), hub height (80 m), rotor bottom tip (30 m), and standard 10 m (Fig. S2A). Wind veer properties, i.e. γ, γU, and γL, are characterized based on those anemometer readings. Eolos turbine is a 2.5 MW, 3-bladed, horizontal-axis, variable-speed-variable-pitch-regulated clockwise-rotating wind turbine (Clipper Liberty C96, D = 96 m, H = 80 m). Eolos turbine employs different control strategies within three designated regions: region 1 (i.e. Uhub < 4 m/s, no power generation), region 2 (i.e. 6.9 m/s ≤Uhub≤ 9.2 m/s, generator torque-controlled with the maximum power coefficient of 0.472), and region 3 (i.e. Uhub > 11 m/s, full-span collective-pitch regulated to achieve stable rated power output). Region 1.5 and region 2.5 mask the transitional zones between region 2 and their respective neighboring regions (Fig. S2B). The Eolos turbine is more likely to operate within the range of regions 1.5–2.5 (>70%) as compared to region 3 (<5%) and region 1 (∼20%) (31).

The turbine tower sensor system consists of 20 single-axis strain gauges (Vishay MicroMeasurements, model CEA-06-125UN-350/P2) uniformly situated around the tower's foundation. Temperature effects are calibrated within the 20-Hz raw strain measurements to counteract temperature-induced impacts like thermal expansion and installation offsets (30). The resulting datasets are then utilized to characterize the normalized fore-aft bending moment (MFA*) and side-to-side bending moment (MSS*) of the turbine. Detailed information about the structural response calculation can be found in Ref. (30).

Results

Large-scale field campaign to explore yaw offset effects on turbine power

The large-scale field campaign (LSFC), aimed at exploring the influence of yaw offset on wind turbine power production in the presence of wind veer, was conducted in a large-scale (>100 MW) wind farm located in Midwest US from 05/10/2021 16:10:00 to 06/21/2021 00:00:00 local time. This farm is located on flat terrain, and in May and June, prevailing winds primarily originated from the Northwest. Ten 1.5-MW horizontal-axis variable-pitch-variable-speed clockwise-rotating wind turbines (hub height: H = 80 m, rotor diameter: D = 77 m) were selected for this campaign, including one test WT and Ref. WTs1-9, as shown in Fig. 2A. The test WT, in the wind farm's foremost row relative to the prevailing wind direction (Fig. S3A), boasts a commendable historical operational performance and is equipped with a service lift, facilitating convenient accessibility for installing a personality module to enable remote yaw offset adjustment. The Ref. WTs1-5 are situated in the first row of the wind farm while Ref. WTs6-9 are in the second row. A vertical Doppler profiling LiDAR system (WindCube; range: 40–287 m; spatial resolution: 13 m; ∼950 m away from the test WT) was deployed upstream of most wind turbines in the wind farm to characterize the inflow wind veer. Throughout the campaign, the LiDAR measurements accurately represent the inflow conditions for both test WT and Ref. WTs. Specifically, strong correlations have been observed between hub-height wind speeds (Uhub) measured by LiDAR and turbine nacelle anemometers (Fig. 2B), indicated by the Pearson correlation coefficients of r=0.91 for the test WT (Fig. S3B) and r = 0.82–0.91 for the reference WTs (Fig. S3C). The differences in wind speeds between LiDAR measurements and turbine nacelle anemometers for the test WT are negligible at −0.3%. For Ref. WTs1-9, these discrepancies, ranging from −8 to 0.3% (Fig. S4), are associated with wind farm layout and potential wake effects. These effects are accounted for in the data processing. In addition, the wind site is observed to encounter 68% veering winds and 32% backing winds during the field campaign (Fig. 2C). In addition, the wind veer is discovered to be correlated with the local wind shear (Fig. 2D), exhibiting a relationship of γ=23α−0.1, where α refers to wind shear exponent, a trend akin to observations made by Gomez and Lundquist (20) in central Iowa, US. Strong veering winds are usually correlated with strong shear flows (α>0.15), likely to bolster the efficacy of the proposed wind veer control strategy, primarily due to the greater wind speeds at the turbine upper rotors.

To evaluate how yaw offset impacts turbine power generation, the yaw control system of the test wind turbine was adjusted with incremental offset angles spanning from −2° to 3°, with intervals of 1°. Note that to ensure the safety of the turbine, no test cases involve an offset angle exceeding 3°. A positive βo corresponds to a counterclockwise offset, while conversely, a negative βo corresponds to a clockwise offset. Figure 2E depicts the modified power variation ratios relative to the maximum turbine capacity (Gcap) for both the Test WT and Ref. WTs1-9 under severe wind veer (abs(γ)≥0.1∘/m) and positive shear (α>0). When yaw offset is not applied, the Test WT and Ref. WTs1-9 demonstrate nearly identical Gcap ratios with only a 0.1% variation. The Test WT shows superior power generation compared to the Ref. WTs when positive yaw offsets are implemented, as shown in the histograms in Fig. 2E. The average power increases of the Test WT relative to the maximum capacity (PDCcap) are 0.5%, 0.9%, and 2.5%, respectively, for βo=1∘,2∘,3∘. Nevertheless, when negative yaw offsets of −1° and −2° are applied, the Test WT demonstrates power reductions compared to the Ref. WTs by 1.3 and 0.4%, respectively. These findings reinforce our proposition to utilize yaw offset as a means to offset power losses during wind veer conditions. The small yaw offsets prove effective in mitigating the adverse effects of wind veer, such as reduced relative velocity to the blade section or altered inflow angles.

ESFC to evaluate the automatic wind veer control strategy

To delve deeper into the strategy's performance in an automatic control scenario, an ESFC was undertaken from 2022 May 18 1:20:12 to 2022 July 31 22:00:15 local time at the University of Minnesota Eolos station in Rosemount, Minnesota, US. A Python script was developed and embedded to the turbine control system to automatically collect meteorological tower data, compute wind veer, and periodically adjust the turbine yaw offset (βo) at 5-min intervals following the flowchart in Fig. 1A and the look-up table (Fig. S5). At this wind site, wind conditions fall into three categories: no veer (abs(γ)<0.1∘/m) accounts for 17.24%, veering winds (γ≥0.1∘/m) are predominant at 82.16%, while backing winds (γ≤−0.1∘/m) are a minor contributor at 0.60%. During the test period, the yaw offsets were activated 66.7% of the time.

Throughout the ESFC, the proposed strategy positively impacts turbine power production. Specifically, the adoption of the control strategy results in substantial power gains when compared to the absence of the wind veer strategy under pronounced wind veer conditions (12). A power deviation coefficient (PDC) of 4.7% is noted when γU falls within the range of 0.20–0.25°/m (Fig. 3A). Such gains can surge to as high as 10.8%. Discussion

To obtain a thorough understanding of the potential economic benefits provided by the new strategy, we analyze the yearly economic gains resulting from mitigating the effects of wind veer (see Materials and methods). Given a 100-MW wind farm consisting of 50 2-MW wind turbines, the proposed wind veer control strategy can yield an annual economic gain ranging from $8,420 to $728,300 for an assumed $32/MWh cost of energy (34) (Fig. 4A), depending on the variability of local wind resources and turbine capacity factors (CF). The corresponding additional annual energy produced ranges from 263 MWh to 22,759 MWh, representing 0.35 to 1.75% of total annual energy production. For wind sites with abundant wind resources (i.e. WPD > class 3) and frequent severe wind veer, the proposed control strategy tends to yield greater benefits. For example, in class 3 areas with a φVV,γ>0.1 of 30%, the potential gain can be approximately $100,000, with a margin of ±$8,000.

Considering the long-term operation, it is crucial to assess the effects of implementing yaw offset on key turbine components and structural loadings to ensure the practical viability of the proposed strategy. It has been observed based on Eolos turbine data that small yaw offsets (βo<5∘) have minimal impact on the turbine bearing system, with a temperature increase of less than 2% compared to conditions with no offset (Fig. 4B). While the small yaw offsets may elevate both the turbine's fore-aft (Fig. 4C) and side-to-side bending moments (Fig. 4D) to some extent, such increments do not compromise turbine safety, as supported by the fatigue analysis of turbine tower welds under various yaw misalignments by Tao et al. (37). Nevertheless, it is important to note that large yaw offsets (βo>15∘) could potentially inflict considerable harm on the turbine structure and fatigue life, thus they are not recommended for long-term operation (38).

In summary, we introduce a novel control modification strategy, i.e. the wind veer control strategy, specifically designed to optimize power generation in turbines under wind veer conditions. This strategy holds significant potential for utility-scale wind turbine application, as evidenced by our long-term field campaigns conducted on both individual turbines and wind farms. Our approach stands out for its simplicity and cost-effectiveness, easily integrating into both new and existing turbines with only minor adjustments required in turbine controllers. This integration leverages commonly available equipment such as nearby meteorological towers or profiling/nacelle LiDAR (39), which are standard features in most wind farms. Notably, for offshore turbines that are already equipped with nacelle-mounted LiDAR (13), our strategy requires no additional instrumentation, simplifying deployment, and minimizing costs. The effectiveness of our strategy was highlighted by the potential for significant power gains. These gains could be additionally boosted by the following.

Expanding wind capacity and adapting our strategy to accommodate the unique challenges and opportunities presented by larger turbine sizes, especially as the industry moves towards offshore installations and turbines exceeding 10 MW (40). As wind turbine size increases, the influence of the Ekman layer becomes more pronounced, leading to greater Coriolis effect and consequently stronger wind veer effects. In such scenarios, our proposed wind veer control strategy would offer even greater benefits for wind turbines.

Future work to advance our proposed wind turbine control strategy from concept/prototype to practical implementation/commercialization will require systematic performance studies on the following aspects.

The proposed yaw control strategy can be easily implemented in utility-scale wind turbines. A communication module (i.e. wind veer feedback system, costing about $50–$200, if not accessible for the specific wind turbine), is needed to relay wind veer information measured by LiDAR or met tower as input for automatically adjusting the yaw offset in the turbine control system. The wind veer control algorithm should be embedded as an add-on into the yaw control system for long-term automatic operation, requiring minimal changes to the existing turbine yaw control system. Collaborating with wind turbine manufacturers or obtaining their permission for permanent updates to the yaw control system would facilitate commercialization.

The total implementation costs for our proposed wind veer control strategy are site dependent. For wind farms lacking a met tower or profiling/nacelle-mounted LiDARs, additional costs, including operational and maintenance expenses, should be considered. The number of LiDARs required to accurately characterize wind veer for all turbines in a wind farm depends on factors including the wind farm's geographic location, terrain type, layout (e.g. wake effects), and meteorological conditions. For a pilot project, it is recommended to deploy a single profiling LiDAR at a representative upstream location along the prevailing wind direction for a flat terrain wind farm, with subsequent relocation for a comprehensive study. A one-time investment of $150K–200 K for a single 200-m profiling LiDAR or $80K–130 K for a 100-m met tower is significantly lower than the potential gains of up to ∼$730 K/year from implementing our control strategy at 100-MW sites with severe wind veer issues. In a wind farm situated in complex terrain, the number of required LiDARs will significantly increase, leading to a trade-off between the economic benefits of the proposed strategy and the costs of LiDARs.

Offshore wind turbines, due to their large sizes, are prone to experiencing significant wind veer compared to onshore turbines. The proposed wind veer control strategy is expected to be suitable for fixed-bottom offshore turbines. However, for floating offshore turbines, the additional movement/motion associated with floating platforms and mooring systems complicates the interaction between ABL and the turbine. Further experimental or field investigations are required to adapt the current wind veer control strategy for practical application in floating offshore turbines.
