Prevalence and determinants of insecticide-treated bed-net utilization among under-five children in Ethiopia: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Amlaku Nigusie Yirsaw, Lakew Asmare, Eyob Getachew, Zeamanuel Anteneh Yigzaw, Tihtna Alemu, Gebeyehu Lakew
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMalaria continues to be a significant public health challenge in Ethiopia, especially among children under the age of five, who are more vulnerable to the disease. Insecticide-treated nets (ITNs) are widely recommended as an effective preventive measure. However, studies in Ethiopia have reported inconsistent findings regarding ITN utilization among under-five children. This study aimed to determine the pooled prevalence of ITN use and identify determinants among children under five in Ethiopia.MethodsA systematic review and meta-analysis was conducted by searching PubMed, CINAHL, Web of Science, Science Direct, SCOPUS, and Google Scholar for studies published from 2008 to 2024. Data were extracted into Excel and analysed using STATA 17. A random-effects meta-analysis model was applied to estimate the pooled prevalence and determinants. Statistical heterogeneity was assessed using the I2 statistic, and potential publication bias was evaluated using Egger’s test and visual inspection of forest plots.ResultsThe pooled prevalence of ITN utilization among under-five children in Ethiopia was 53% (95% CI 43%–63%, I2 = 99.46%, indicating substantial heterogeneity. Factors significantly associated with higher ITN utilization included having more than two sleeping spaces (OR = 4.596, 95% CI 2.075–7.117), good knowledge about ITNs (OR = 3.7, 95% CI 2.239–5.194), positive perception of ITNs (OR = 5.5, 95% CI 3.011–7.988), rural residence (OR = 3.211, 95% CI 1.464–4.958), low perceived barriers (OR = 2.21, 95% CI 1.90–2.52), monthly income above 1,000 birr (OR = 2.996, 95% CI 1.478–4.515), household head age over 25 years (OR = 2.75, 95% CI 2.279–3.217), and ownership of a radio (OR = 2.545, 95% CI 2.177–2.912).ConclusionThis systematic review and meta-analysis found that nearly half of Ethiopian children under five are not utilizing ITNs, leaving them vulnerable to preventable malaria infection. While factors such as caregiver knowledge, household income, rural residence, sleeping space availability, positive perceptions of ITNs, and media access were significantly associated with ITN use, the substantial heterogeneity among studies underscores the need for context-specific interventions. To improve ITN coverage and consistent use, targeted public health strategies should prioritize addressing socio-economic disparities, enhancing community education, and strengthening ITN distribution systems. These findings support the development of evidence-informed policies and behaviour change interventions, particularly in underserved rural and low-income communities.Keywords: Malaria, Insecticide-treated nets, Utilization, Under-five years old children, Ethiopia

Background

Malaria remains a leading public health threat in many tropical and subtropical countries, especially in sub-Saharan Africa (SSA), where socio-economic challenges exacerbate the disease burden. In Ethiopia, over 60% of the population is at risk of malaria, with 68% of the land conducive to transmission due to diverse microclimates shaped by mountainous terrain, seasonal rainfall, and fluctuating temperatures [1–5]. Children under five years old are particularly vulnerable, accounting for the majority of malaria-related morbidity and mortality globally and in Ethiopia [6–10]. According to the 2019 Ethiopian Demographic and Health Survey (EDHS), malaria caused 59 deaths per 1000 live births among this age group [11].

Insecticide-treated nets (ITNs) have been a cornerstone of malaria prevention for over two decades. They provide both a physical barrier and insecticidal protection, effectively reducing malaria transmission and parasitaemia when consistently used [12, 13]. Global and national distribution campaigns have aimed to achieve universal coverage, typically defined as one ITN for every two household members [2]. Between 2015 and 2017, approximately 552 million ITNs were distributed, with 83% allocated to SSA [2]. However, usage among children under five remains low, with only 54% coverage in SSA and 52.3% in Ethiopia, despite ownership rates of 66.8% of households [14, 15].

Numerous Ethiopian studies have identified barriers to ITN utilization, such as poor awareness, accessibility issues, and problems related to the physical condition, availability, and maintenance of nets [16–19]. However, these individual studies report wide variations in ITN usage among children under five, ranging from 9 to 82% [17, 20, 21]. This variation complicates efforts to design consistent and effective malaria prevention strategies across different regions.

Despite the availability of multiple studies on ITN utilization in Ethiopia, no prior meta-analysis has synthesized these findings to estimate a pooled prevalence or comprehensively assess the determinants. Such an evidence gap limits the ability of policymakers and programme planners to make informed, targeted interventions.

Therefore, this systematic review and meta-analysis aimed to estimate the pooled prevalence of ITN utilization among children under five in Ethiopia and identify key determinants. The findings are intended to support evidence-based strategies to increase ITN usage and reduce malaria-related morbidity and mortality among young children.

Methods

The study protocol

This systematic review and meta-analysis aimed to assess the pooled prevalence of ITN utilization and its determinants among children under five in Ethiopia. The study followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines [22], and included both published and unpublished quantitative studies reporting ITN use and related factors in this age group.

Inclusion criteria

Study setting

Studies conducted in any region of Ethiopia.

Study design and language

All original quantitative studies (cross-sectional and observational) published in English were considered eligible if they reported the prevalence of ITN utilization and its associated factors among children under five years old.

Publication status

Both published and unpublished (grey literature) studies were included.

Exclusion criteria

Studies were excluded if they were not available in full text, did not report the primary outcomes of interest, or were published in languages other than English.

Outcome measures and definition of terms

This systematic review and meta-analysis centered on two main outcomes. First, it aimed to estimate the pooled prevalence of ITN utilization among children under five in Ethiopia, defined as the proportion of children who slept under ITNs the night before the survey. The prevalence was calculated by aggregating the ITN utilization rates reported in the included studies. Second, the study sought to identify the pooled effects of determinants with ITN use among these children. ITNs are treated with insecticide to kill mosquitoes, serving as physical barriers to prevent bites. ITN utilization refers to the appropriate use of these nets by children under five during sleep.

Search strategies

Relevant published articles were gathered from databases including PubMed, Web of Science, Sciene Direct, SCOPUS, and Google Scholar. The review concentrated on primary cross-sectional studies published up to August 2024. The search strategy employed keywords such as "insecticide-treated net," "long-lasting insecticidal net," "mosquito net," "bed net," "utilization," "children under five," and "Ethiopia," using Boolean operators (AND, OR) to refine the search. Free keywords and MeSH terms were also included.

For example, the query used was ((“insecticidal” OR “insecticides”) AND “treated” AND (“bed net” OR “long-lasting insecticidal net” OR “mosquito net” OR “insecticide-treated net”)) AND “under-five age of children” AND “Ethiopia”).

Study selection

All retrieved references were imported into EndNote X9, where 476 duplicates were removed. The remaining 577 records underwent title and abstract screening by three independent reviewers (ANY, EG, GL). Of these, 52 articles were selected for full-text review, and finally 18 studies met all inclusion criteria and were included in the meta-analysis.

Of the initial 1053 studies identified, 476 were duplicates. Of the remaining 577, 525 were excluded after title and abstract screening due to irrelevance or wrong population and design. Some 52 full-text articles were assessed; 34 were excluded due to incomplete data, low quality scores, or not meeting the eligibility criteria. Ultimately, 18 studies were included in the final analysis (Fig. 1). The final 18 studies met all eligibility criteria, including sufficient data on ITN utilization and a minimum quality score of 7 on the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale.

PRISMA flow diagram showing the selection process of studies included in the systematic review and meta-analysis on ITN utilization among children under five in Ethiopia

Disagreements were resolved through consensus discussions between reviewers (ANY and GL), with a third reviewer (EG) consulted when needed. Inter-reviewer concordance was tracked qualitatively. As the focus was on achieving consensus rather than quantifying agreement, inter-reviewer reliability was handled narratively without computing statistical concordance metrics such as Cohen’s kappa.

Quality assessment

Methodological quality was evaluated using the Newcastle–Ottawa Quality Assessment Scale adapted for cross-sectional studies [23]. Five reviewers (ANY, EG, ZAY, LA, and GL) independently assessed each study. Only those scoring 7 or higher out of 10 were included in the meta-analysis.

Data extraction

Two reviewers (ANY and GL) independently extracted data from the full texts of the selected articles using a predefined Microsoft Excel 2017 template. They focused on categories such as author name, publication year, region, sampling methods, study design, sample size, response rate, and prevalence of ITN utilization. Discrepancies between the two reviewers were resolved by jointly re-extracting the data. Accuracy was further verified by additional reviewers through consensus discussions.

Statistical analysis

The extracted data were exported to STATA version 17.0 for synthesis and analysis. A random-effects meta-analysis model was applied to estimate the pooled prevalence of ITN utilization and the pooled effects of associated factors, accounting for expected heterogeneity across studies.

A forest plot was employed to assess heterogeneity among the included studies using the I-squared (I2) statistic. Heterogeneity was considered statistically significant at p ≤ 0.05. I2 values of 0%, 25%, 50%, and 75% were interpreted as indicating no, low, moderate, and high heterogeneity, respectively [24].

Subgroup analyses were used to explore the sources of heterogeneity across different Ethiopian regions and publication years. These analyses helped identify variations in ITN utilization patterns. Additionally, meta-regression was used to assess whether regional differences or study characteristics significantly explained the observed heterogeneity (I2 > 99%). Leave-one-out sensitivity analyses confirmed that no single study disproportionately influenced the pooled estimates, indicating robustness of the findings despite high heterogeneity.

A meta-regression analysis was performed to explore the impact of study-level covariates such as sample size, region and publication year on the pooled prevalence. However, none of these variables significantly accounted for the high between-study variability (p > 0.05), suggesting other unmeasured factors may explain the heterogeneity.

For the second outcome, factors most frequently associated with ITN utilization identified from the multivariate analyses of the primary studies were included in the analysis. A random-effects meta-analysis model was used to calculate the pooled odds ratios for the association between each factor and ITN utilization. The results were presented using forest plots, displaying the pooled prevalence and corresponding 95% confidence intervals.

Results

Selection of studies

A total of 1,053 records were identified through comprehensive database searches, including both published and unpublished studies. After removing 582 duplicate records, 471 titles and abstracts were screened for relevance. Of these, 381 were excluded based on title and abstract review. The remaining 70 full-text articles were assessed for eligibility, and 52 were excluded because they did not report the outcome of interest. Ultimately, 18 studies met the inclusion criteria and were incorporated into the final meta-analysis to estimate the prevalence of ITN utilization and its determinants among children under five in Ethiopia. The selection process is summarized in Fig. 1. This thorough process follows the PRISMA guideline and enhances the reliability of the included evidence.

Study characteristics

This systematic review and meta-analysis included 18 studies to estimate the overall prevalence of ITN utilization. Across these studies, a total of 14,282 participants were involved, with individual study sample sizes ranging from 398 to 2,375 participants, drawn from regions such as SNNPR and Harar, respectively [15, 19]. Most studies (88.9%) employed a cross-sectional design, while the remaining 11.1% were randomized controlled trials. Regarding regional distribution, five studies originated from SNNPR, five from Oromia, three from Gambella, two from Amhara, and one each from Harar, Tigray, and Afar (Table 1).

Pooled prevalence of ITN utilization among children under five in Ethiopia (meta‑analysis)

The overall pooled prevalence of ITN utilization among children under five in Ethiopia was 53% (95% CI 43%–63%). A high level of heterogeneity was observed across the included studies (I2 = 99.46%, p < 0.001) (Fig. 2), which justified the use of a random-effects model for estimating the pooled prevalence. Among the individual regional studies, the lowest non-adherence rate to ITN use was reported in the SNNPR region at 9%, while the highest non-adherence rate was seen in the Afar region at 82%.

Heterogeneity and publication bias

Publication bias was evaluated using funnel plot inspection, Egger’s regression test, and Begg’s rank correlation test. Begg’s test yielded a p-value of 0.8796, and Egger’s test produced a p-value of 0.3784, both indicating no significant statistical evidence of publication bias However, visual inspection of the funnel plot suggested some asymmetry (Fig. 3). To determine the cause of heterogeneity, meta-regression was performed using region and publication year as covariates. The analysis revealed that the study region (p < 0.001) and publication year (p < 0.001) were both statistically significant sources of heterogeneity, and an I2 statistic of 99.46% well above the conventional 50% threshold, supported the appropriateness of using a random-effects model (Table 2).

Subgroup analysis

To explore potential sources of heterogeneity, subgroup analyses were performed based on geographic regions and publication years, both of which revealed statistically significant differences between groups (p < 0.05). The pooled prevalence of ITN utilization among children under five varied notably across regions, with the highest prevalence observed in the Amhara region at 69% (95% CI 55%–84%) and the lowest in the SNNPR region at 49% (95% CI 37%–60%) (Fig. 4). Subgroup analysis by publication year also indicated an increase in ITN utilization. Specifically, studies published between 2008 and 2015 reported a pooled prevalence of 48% (95% CI 34%–62%), whereas studies published from 2016 to 2024 showed a higher pooled prevalence of 59% (95% CI 43%–75%) (Fig. 5).

Sensitivity analysis

A sensitivity analysis was performed to assess the influence of individual studies on the overall prevalence of ITN utilization among children under five in Ethiopia. This involved systematically excluding each study one at a time. The analysis revealed that no single study had a statistically significant impact on the pooled prevalence of ITN utilization (Fig. 6).

Determinants of ITN utilization among under-five children in Ethiopia

Before conducting the pooled analysis, 14 factors were identified as being associated with ITN utilization among under-five children in Ethiopia. These included: household family size < 5 members [13, 15], sleeping space > 2 [15, 16, 19], sharing sleeping place [15, 16, 19, 25], good knowledge about ITN [15, 16, 19, 20, 25–27], good knowledge about malaria [27], educational status of household head [28, 29], good perception of ITN [25, 30], rural residence [16, 29, 31], low perceived barrier [13, 16, 19], having > 2 ITNs [13, 25, 28, 32], monthly income > 1,000 birr [30, 32], male household head [30, 32], household head age > 25 [31–33], having a radio [31, 34] (Table 3).

The pooled effect of four studies showed that households with more than two sleeping spaces were significantly more likely to use ITNs, with 4.6 times higher odds compared to those with fewer than two sleeping spaces (OR = 4.596; 95% CI 2.075–7.117). Seven studies revealed that caretakers with good knowledge of ITNs were 3.7 times more likely to ensure ITN use for children under five compared to those with poor knowledge (OR = 3.7, 95% CI 2.239–5.194). The pooled effect of two studies indicated that ITN utilization was significantly higher among children under five whose caretakers had a positive perception of ITNs. These children had 5.5 times higher odds of using ITNs compared to those whose caretakers had a negative perception (OR = 5.5; 95% CI 3.011–7.988). Pooled multivariate analyses from four studies indicated that individuals residing in rural areas had over three-fold higher odds of using ITNs compared to urban residents (OR = 3.211; 95% CI 1.464–4.958).

A meta-analysis of three studies revealed a significant association between perceived barriers and ITN utilization. Caretakers with low perceived barriers were 2.21 times more likely to ensure ITN use by children under five compared to those with high perceived barriers (OR = 2.21; 95% CI 1.90–2.52).

The pooled effect of two studies identified a monthly income of over 1,000 Ethiopian birr as a significant predictor of ITN utilization. Caretakers earning more than 1,000 birr per month were approximately three times more likely to ensure ITN use for their children compared to those earning less (OR = 2.996; 95% CI 1.478–4.515).The pooled effect of two studies revealed that household heads older than 25 years are strongly associated with ITN utilization. Household heads over 25 were 2.75 times more likely to use ITNs compared to their younger counterparts (OR = 2.75, 95% CI 2.279–3.217).

A meta-analysis of two studies demonstrated a significant association between radio ownership and ITN utilization among children under five. Caretakers who owned a radio were 2.5 times more likely to use ITNs compared to those without a radio (OR = 2.545, 95% CI 2.177–2.912).

Discussion

This systematic review and meta-analysis aimed to determine the pooled prevalence of ITN utilization and its determinants among children under five years in Ethiopia. The findings revealed a very low pooled prevalence of ITN use (53%, 95% CI 43–63), highlighting an urgent need for strengthened national strategies to reduce malaria-related morbidity and mortality in this vulnerable group.

Compared to similar studies, our pooled prevalence is consistent with the 2015 national malaria indicator survey in Ethiopia (44%) [35], and rates reported in neighbouring East African countries, such as Kenya, and other African countries such as Ghana and Senegal, where ITN utilization ranges from 48 to 58% [36–38]. However, ITN use in Ethiopia remains lower than in some countries with more intensive malaria control programmes, such as Malawi (83%) [39]) and Burkina Faso (70%) [40]. These differences may reflect variation in programme coverage, resource allocation, and socio-economic factors affecting ITN accessibility and use.

Subgroup analysis revealed significant regional disparities within Ethiopia, with the highest ITN use observed in the Amhara region (69%) and the lowest in SNNPR (40%). These differences likely reflect practical factors such as variations in health promotion intensity, distribution efficiency, geographic accessibility, and local environmental conditions influencing mosquito densities. For instance, regions with more active community outreach and well-established distribution channels tend to achieve higher ITN coverage. Conversely, remote or underserved areas may face logistical challenges limiting ITN availability and uptake.

Over time, ITN utilization showed a modest increase, from 48% in studies before 2015 to 53% in more recent studies (2016–2024), suggesting gradual improvements in malaria prevention efforts, awareness and access to ITNs. Continued monitoring and targeted interventions are essential to sustain and accelerate this progress.

Regarding determinants with ITN use, the meta-analysis identified several significant determinants, including the number of sleeping spaces, caregiver knowledge and perception of ITNs, rural residence, perceived barriers, household income, age of household head, and ownership of a radio. While these findings align with previous Ethiopian studies, some effect estimates showed wide confidence intervals and high heterogeneity, indicating variability across study contexts and populations.

In this meta-analysis, caregivers with more than two sleeping spaces were found to be more likely to use ITNs [15, 16, 19], possibly reflecting larger household size and greater perceived need for protection. Caregivers with good knowledge of ITNs were 3.7 times more likely to utilize ITNs compared to those without such knowledge. The findings align with studies conducted in Ethiopia [15, 27, 41]. Such consistency emphasizes the importance of strengthening educational interventions to improve awareness around ITN use.

Caregivers’ perceptions of ITNs also emerged as a critical determinant. Those with a positive perception were 5.5 times more likely to use ITNs. This is supported by findings from Ethiopia [25, 32]. A positive perception may reflect trust in the efficacy of ITNs, motivating consistent use and potentially improving household-level malaria prevention. This study found that caregivers in rural areas were three times more likely to utilize ITNs than those in urban areas. This finding is consistent with previous Ethiopian studies [16, 29]. A possible reason is that rural communities may face a higher perceived risk due to environmental conditions favourable to mosquito breeding, such as proximity to stagnant water. Additionally, targeted health campaigns and mass distributions are often prioritized in rural settings, potentially increasing ITN access. Caregivers with low perceived barriers were 2.2 times more likely to use ITNs. This finding is supported by local studies [16, 13. Perceived barriers such as inconvenience, lack of knowledge, or difficulty accessing ITNs can inhibit utilization. Addressing these through tailored community engagement and support may reduce these obstacles. Caregivers with monthly incomes over 1,000 Ethiopian birr were approximately 2.99 times more likely to utilize ITNs compared to those with lower incomes, consistent with previous studies in Ethiopia [30, 33]. This association may be attributed to higher-income caregivers having greater access to resources, such as the ability to purchase or replace ITNs and maintain them effectively. They may also benefit from better access to health promotion services and information about malaria prevention.

Household heads older than 25 years were 2.75 times more likely to utilize ITNs. This result, consistent with previous studies [26, 33], may be due to greater maturity, life experience, and exposure to public health messaging among older adults. Caregivers who owned a radio were 2.5 times more likely to use ITNs, consistent with findings from Ethiopia [31, 34]. This may be because radio is an accessible and influential medium in rural areas and has been shown to effectively deliver malaria prevention messages.

Strengths and limitations of the study

The study conducted a comprehensive literature search and followed a rigorous selection process with well-defined inclusion and exclusion criteria. Established quality assessment tools and robust statistical techniques were applied, including the use of random-effects meta-analysis to account for heterogeneity between studies. Publication bias was also evaluated through statistical tests and visual inspection of funnel plots.

However, several limitations should be considered when interpreting the results. First, only studies published in English were included, which may have introduced language bias and led to the exclusion of relevant non-English studies. Second, the included studies primarily relied on self-reported ITN utilization, which may be subject to recall and social desirability bias, potentially overestimating actual usage rates and reducing the reliability of the measurement.

Third, the substantial heterogeneity across studies (I2 = 99.46%) indicates wide variability in outcomes, populations, and settings, which may limit the generalizability of the pooled estimates. Although subgroup and sensitivity analyses were conducted, residual heterogeneity remains a concern. Fourth, the majority of the included studies were cross-sectional in design, which restricts causal inference regarding the relationship between associated factors and ITN utilization. In addition, variation in study quality and measurement tools could influence the consistency of the pooled results.

Finally, although Egger’s and Begg’s tests did not indicate significant publication bias, the visual asymmetry of the funnel plot raises some concern. Moreover, potential confounding from unmeasured household or community-level factors such as access to health services, community malaria prevention campaigns, or cultural practices could not be controlled in this analysis and may influence the observed associations.

Conclusion

This systematic review and meta-analysis estimated that approximately 53% of children under five in Ethiopia utilize ITNs, with substantial regional and study-level variation. Key factors significantly associated with ITN utilization included caregiver knowledge, rural residence, household income, number of sleeping spaces, positive perceptions of ITNs, and access to information through radio ownership.

These findings highlight the need for locally tailored strategies to improve ITN use, particularly in underserved and low-adherence regions. Specific interventions should include expanding ITN distribution in rural and low-income communities, integrating ITN promotion into maternal and child health services, and implementing behaviour change communication initiatives targeting urban caregivers and households with limited access to media. Educational campaigns that address caregiver knowledge and perception of ITNs can also enhance usage.

Future research should explore cultural and contextual factors not fully captured in this review, such as local beliefs about malaria and ITNs, net maintenance and replacement practices, and seasonal variations in sleeping arrangements to inform more context-specific and sustainable malaria prevention strategies.
