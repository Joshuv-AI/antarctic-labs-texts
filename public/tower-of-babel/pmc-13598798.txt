Design of a Gelatin/Carboxymethyl Cellulose Hydrogel Containing Myrtus Communis Extract with Anti‐inflammatory Capabilities for Accelerated Wound Healing; in Vitro and in Vivo Study
Authors: Sepehr Zamani, Mahdi Naeiji, Hasan Maghsoodifar, Seyed Amir Hossein Sadeghi Douki, Nariman Rezaie Kolarijani, Morteza Alizadeh, Zohreh Arabpour, Zahra Kourdi, Ali R. Djalilian, Majid Salehi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundAchieving optimal skin integrity after an injury requires using a wound dressing with desirable hydrogel properties. This study aims to develop a hydrogel primarily made from gelatin and carboxymethyl cellulose (GEL/CMC).Methods & MaterialsWe incorporated Myrtus communis extract into the hydrogel to evaluate its healing effect on full‐thickness wounds. The plant extract acts as a regulator of inflammation, promoting proliferation and pro‐angiogenic factors, while the natural polymers support these processes. The characterization of Myrtus communis and the hydrogel involved various methods, including assessments of mechanical properties, morphology, hemocompatibility, cytotoxicity, and animal testing.ResultsThe hydrogels exhibited porous designs with interconnected pores, with a mean size of 119.73 ± 13.67. They also showed a swelling capability of up to 90% of their starting weight within 48 h. The hydrogels also demonstrated both biocompatibility and hemocompatibility characteristics. In the final phase, the assessment of wound healing in the animal model revealed that using the synthesized hydrogels greatly enhanced the healing process (closure of the wound area: 88.73 ± 3.95%).ConclusionIn summary, this scaffold has shown promise in promoting tissue re‐epithelialization and regeneration, which makes it a highly favorable option for improving the healing of skin wounds.

Introduction

The skin is the largest organ in the human body. It is an essential structure that protects the inner organs against thermal shock, microbial infection, ultraviolet (UV) rays, and mechanical injury [1]. The skin is extremely susceptible to injury because of its close connection with the outside environment [2]. Chemical, thermal, mechanical, or radiogenic factors can cause skin injuries, also known as wounds, that damage the skin's or organs' structural integrity [3]. Pressure ulcers (PUs), also known as decubitus ulcers or bedsores, develop from prolonged high pressure, primarily in areas of the body with bony prominences [4]. Skin injuries that happen as a result of pressure damage are painful, unpleasant, and costly to treat [5]. It happens as a result of a complete or partial restriction of blood flow to the soft tissue [6].

In the past years, several investigations have been assigned to the commercialization and progress of wound treatment products, such as foam, fibers, bandages, gauze, hydrogel, and colloidal dressings [7]. Hydrogel‐based medicinal materials are well‐known among current dressings. Hydrogels' advantages in wound treatment include reduced wound healing time, high adsorption capacity for exudate and wound fluids, wound moistening, mechanical wound protection, the ability to incorporate drugs into the hydrogel bandage, and ease of use by patients [8, 9, 10].

Biopolymer‐based materials for wound dressing have recently gotten special attention because of their non‐toxicity, biodegradability, biocompatibility, and affordability [11]. Gelatin, as a biopolymer, has attracted significant interest for its biological applications due to its excellent biocompatibility, degradability, and non‐toxicity [12]. Gelatin derived from collagen has ECM‐like physical and chemical characteristics, as well as a high wound exudate‐absorbing capacity [3]. Carboxymethylcellulose has been used for several purposes, such as tissue engineering, drug delivery, and wound dressing [13]. Furthermore, CMC wound dressings are known for their ability to absorb exudate, flexibility, autolytic debridement, and stimulation of angiogenesis. CMC is not toxic, it readily dissolves in water, and it is accessible in nature [14, 15].

Plants, mainly medicinal plants, play an essential and critical role in drug development, and they are very advantageous for healing many medical conditions in humans [16]. Myrtus communis, commonly referred to as myrtle, is a medicinal plant used to treat diseases such as inflammation [17], peptic ulcers, diarrhea [18], wound healing [19], and skin diseases [20]. Additionally, it has been demonstrated to have antioxidative, antidiabetic, neuroprotective, antibacterial, anti‐cancer, and hepatoprotective properties [21, 22, 23, 24, 25]. Recent studies have explored the wound‐healing effects of Myrtus communis extract, attributing these benefits primarily to its anti‐inflammatory, antimicrobial, and antioxidant activities. For instance, Haririan et al. synthesized a carboxymethyl chitosan‐gelatin film that was loaded with M. communis extract and mesoporous silica nanoparticles, which showed enhanced wound healing in BALB/c mice models [26]. Tayebeh Modiri‐Delshad and her colleagues examined the antibacterial properties of chitosan‐based nanofibrous mats incorporated with an extract from M. communis leaves. The aim was to evaluate their efficacy against pathogenic bacteria [27]. Furthermore, Fatemeh Ebrahimi et al.’s study revealed novel findings on the hemostatic impact of M. communis aqueous leaf extract, potentially influenced by its ability to promote platelet aggregation [28]. A review of the medicinal potential of M. communis highlighted its traditional use for various skin ailments and its prospective role in modern wound healing therapies [29].

Thus far, no research has been published explicitly addressing the wound‐healing efficiency of Myrtus communis‐based hydrogels generated from gelatin or CMC. While prior research has demonstrated the therapeutic potential of M. communis, these studies often lack an effective delivery mechanism that allows continuous absorption and sufficient moisture retention at the wound site. This study addresses the existing literature gap by introducing a biocompatible gel produced from carboxymethyl cellulose and gelatin, capable of stably encapsulating M. communis extract. The delivery system is expected to increase the therapeutic efficacy of the extract by establishing a moist healing environment, prolonging its retention at the site of application, and decreasing cytotoxicity. This study presents a novel and straightforward approach to expedite wound healing and tissue regeneration by encapsulating M. communis in a hydrogel matrix with advantageous physicochemical and biological characteristics.

Method & Materials

Materials

We sourced the gelatin for this study from porcine skin (G2500‐100G; Sigma‐Aldrich, Milan, Italy), carboxymethyl cellulose (CMC) from Sigma‐Aldrich, Steinheim am Albuch, Germany, thiazolyl blue tetrazolium bromide (MTT), and eosin from Sigma‐Aldrich. Gibco, USA, provided us with penicillin, streptomycin, Dulbecco's modified Eagle medium (DMEM), and fetal bovine serum (FBS). We obtained dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) from Bioidea, Iran. We obtained hematoxylin solutions from Santa Cruz, USA. For animal procedures, ketamine (100 mg/mL), xylazine (20 mg/mL), buprenorphine hydrochloride (0.3 mg/mL), and carprofen (50 mg/mL) were all obtained from Alfasan (Woerden, The Netherlands).

Plant Materials and Extraction

Myrtus communis leaves were purchased from a certified and well‑established herbal supplier, Giah Essence Phytopharm Company (Kesht va Sanat va Darusazi Giah Essence, Dr. Soleimani Co., Gorgan, Golestan Province, Iran). This company is a knowledge‑based phytopharmaceutical enterprise founded in 1989 with documented expertise in the cultivation, authentication, and processing of medicinal plants. Myrtus communis leaves were collected, washed several times, and subsequently dehydrated in an oven at a temperature of 40°C before utilization [30]. Then grind it into a fine powder using a blender. 100 g of powder was extracted with the percolation technique in 70% ethanol for 72 h at room temperature [31]. The samples were concentrated using a rotary evaporator [32].

Hydrogel Preparation

First, GEL/CMC (3% w/v) was dissolved in deionized water. The solution was then stirred using a 1:8 (v/v) volumetric ratio for 24 h at room temperature to ensure complete dissolution. Subsequently, the GEL/CMC hydrogel was thoroughly mixed with various concentrations of Myrtus communis (0%, 0.1%, 0.5%, 1%, 5%, and 10% w/w) [33].

Lyophilization of Herbal Extraction and Hydrogel

Initially, the Myrtus communis extraction and prepared hydrogel (Myrtus communis/GEL/CMC) were separately frozen for 24 h at −20°C, then the samples were frozen again for 24 h at −80°C. The samples were lyophilized using a lyophilizer (Telstar, Terrassa, Spain) at ‐54°C for 72 h to produce porous scaffolds [34].

Morphological Properties

The freeze‐dried samples with varying concentrations were cut to a diameter of 5 mm. The structure of GEL/CMC/Myrtus communis was then analyzed using a scanning electron microscope (SEM AIS2300, Seron Technology, South Korea) at an accelerating voltage of 20 kV. Before analysis, the samples were coated with a layer of gold for 250 s using a sputter coater (SC7620, Quorum Technologies, England) [35].

Fourier Transform Infrared Spectroscopy (FTIR)

FTIR spectroscopy was used to analyze the chemical structures of the porous scaffolds. The Bruker Aquinox 55 equipment, manufactured in Germany, was utilized to perform FTIR spectroscopy measurements within the range of 4000–400 cm− 1 [36].

Rheological Characterization

Viscosity measurements were performed for the GEL/CMC hydrogels containing different concentrations of Myrtus communis extract (0%, 0.1%, 0.5%, 1%, 5%, and 10% w/w) using a cone‐and‐plate viscometer equipped with spindle 7 (Lamy Rheology, France). The temperature was maintained at 25°C by connecting the apparatus to a water bath with controlled rotary settings. Viscosity was measured at various shear rates ranging from 1 to 100 s− 1 over a period of 60 s. The samples were subjected to a constant stress of 100 Pa, and the viscosity was calculated from the resulting flow curves. All measurements were performed in triplicate [37].

Swelling Studies

The investigation of the swelling properties of freeze‐dried hydrogels composed of GEL, CMC, and Myrtus communis was conducted in phosphate‐buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.4; Sigma‐Aldrich, USA) at ambient temperature. Initially, the hydrogel samples were dried in a vacuum oven at 50°C until they achieved a consistent weight. A specific quantity of the dry GEL/CMC/Myrtus communis hydrogel was then placed in PBS and stored at ambient temperature for 2 days. At each predetermined time point (2, 4, 6, 12, and 24 h), the swollen hydrogel samples were carefully removed from the PBS solution using sterile forceps. Each sample was immediately placed on a Whatman No. 1 filter paper (Whatman plc, Maidstone, UK) pre‐moistened with PBS to minimize evaporative losses. Excess surface water was removed by gently blotting the hydrogel surface with a second piece of dry filter paper, taking care to apply only light contact without compressing the hydrogel to avoid expulsion of internal fluid. The blotting procedure was repeated three times per sample, using fresh dry filter paper each time, until no visible surface moisture remained. The sample was then transferred immediately to a pre‐tared aluminum weighing pan, and its wet weight was recorded using an analytical balance (Mettler Toledo, Columbus, OH, USA; readability 0.1 mg). The entire blotting and weighing process was completed within 30 s to prevent surface dehydration. Following weighing, the samples were returned to fresh PBS to continue the swelling study. Assessments were performed, and the equilibrium swelling percentage was calculated using the appropriate equation. Equilibriummassswelling%=M1−M0M0×100

The M0 is the gel's weight when dried, and the M1 is its weight when expanded [38]. All swelling measurements were performed in triplicate for each group at each time point (n = 3 per group per time point).

Weight Loss Analysis

The hydrogel degradation rate was assessed based on the reduction in its mass in phosphate‐buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.4). To achieve this objective, the mass of each desiccated hydrogel disk was measured. At each specified time point (6, 12, 24, 48, and 72 h), three identical samples from each group were removed from the PBS using sterile forceps. Each sample was gently blotted on Whatman No. 1 filter paper following the same procedure described in Section 2.8 to remove excess surface PBS without compressing the hydrogel. The samples were then lyophilized to complete dryness, and the dry weight was recorded. The samples were thereafter measured according to the provided equation to determine the extent of degradation. Weightloss%=W0−W1W0×100

The W1 represents the mass of the gel when it is dried, and W0 represents the weight of the initial (before immersion) [39]. Three samples per group were analyzed at each time point (n = 3 per group per time point).

Release of Myrtus communis

The Myrtus communis release from the scaffold was evaluated using in vitro UV‑visible spectroscopy. The GEL/CMC hydrogel (1 mL) containing Myrtus communis extract was placed in phosphate‐buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.4; 5 mL) and maintained at 37°C. For consistency with swelling and degradation experiments, PBS was used as the release medium instead of simulated body fluid. During the designated time frame, about 1 mL of the liquid above the sediment was extracted, and an equal volume of fresh SBF solution was added. The supernatant was subjected to centrifugation, and the absorption level was evaluated after calculating and drawing the standard curve. The equation was used to measure the quantity of Myrtus communis released. The amount of Myrtus communis released in each sample was quantified by UV‐Vis spectrophotometry at 270 nm using a calibration curve constructed from serial dilutions of the extract. The concentration was converted to absolute mass (µg) based on the sample volume. Release experiments were conducted in triplicate for each concentration group (n = 3). Release%=amountofreleaseddruginsupernatantamountofloadeddrugintothehydrogel×100

To calculate the release rate (µg/h), the cumulative release data (in µg) were first obtained from the standard curve. Then, for each time interval (e.g., t1 to t2), the release rate was determined as the difference in cumulative release between the two time points divided by the duration of the interval: Releaserateμg/h=C2−C1t2−t1 where C2 and C1 are the cumulative amounts (µg) released at times t2 and t1, respectively. The calculated release rates were plotted at the midpoint of each interval. All calculations were performed for each hydrogel formulation in triplicate (n = 3) [40].

The UV‐Vis method was validated according to the International Conference on Harmonization (ICH) Q2(R1) guidelines [41]. The wavelength of maximum absorbance (λmax) was determined by scanning the extract over 200–500 nm in PBS (pH 7.4) [42]. The extract showed a characteristic absorption peak at 270 nm, which was used for all subsequent quantification. The calibration curve was constructed using serial dilutions of the extract, and linearity was assessed by the correlation coefficient (R2) [43]. The limit of detection (LOD) and limit of quantification (LOQ) were calculated using the formulas LOD = 3.3 σ/S and LOQ = 10 σ/S, where σ is the standard deviation of the response and S is the slope of the calibration curve [44]. Spiking recovery tests were performed to assess accuracy and matrix interference, with acceptable recovery defined as 80%–120% of the spiked amount [45].

Blood Compatibility

Sodium citrate anticoagulant with a concentration of 3.8% (w/v) prepared in deionized water. For this experiment, 2 mL of freshly collected blood was obtained from a Wistar rat. Then, 2 mL of the anti‐coagulated blood was diluted with 2.5 mL of normal saline. The diluted blood (0.2 mL) was added to 200 µL of the test samples. The mixture was kept at 37°C for 60 min and then centrifuged at 1500 rpm for 10 min. The supernatant was transferred to a 96‐well plate, where the absorbance was measured at approximately 545 nm using a Microplate Reader (BioTek Synergy 2 Multi‐Mode). The mean value of three measurements was calculated. Positive controls consisted of 0.2 mL diluted blood in 10 mL deionized water, while negative controls consisted of 0.2 mL diluted blood in 10 mL normal saline. The hemolysis degree was calculated by using the equation: Hemolysis%=Dt−DncDpc−Dnc where Dt is the absorbance of the sample, Dnc is the absorbance of the negative control, and Dpc is the absorbance of the positive control [46]. All hemolysis assays were performed in triplicate (n = 3 per group).

Evaluation of Blood Clotting Property

The composite hydrogel's coagulation property was assessed using the blood clotting index, as described in prior studies [47]. A decreased blood clotting index signifies enhanced coagulation efficacy [48]. The specimens were sliced into circular shapes and positioned within glass containers. Afterward, the beakers were submerged in a water bath, which was maintained at 37°C for one hour. Following that, we introduced 100 µL of anticoagulant‐containing blood into each sample. After 5 min, a quantity of 20 µL of a CaCl2 solution (0.2 M) was added. After five minutes, the samples were carefully supplemented with 25 mL of distilled water. The specimens were maintained at 37°C for five minutes. Ultimately, the absorbance of the sample was measured at a specific wavelength of 545 nm. The control group was comprised of 100 µL of blood combined with 25 mL of distilled water, without any sample. The blood clotting index (BCI) was determined by the following equation: Blood clotting index%=Asample/Acontrol×100%

A control represents the control group's absorbance level, while A sample refers to the absorbance value of each sample [49]. Measurements were carried out in triplicate (n = 3 per group).

PH Determination

The pH of each hydrogel groups (0%, 0.1%, 0.5%, 1%, 5%, and 10% w/w) were measured using the pH meter from Orion Research, Inc., USA. Before taking each measurement, the pH meter was calibrated by utilizing buffer solutions with pH values of 4.0, 7.0, and 9.0. The investigations were performed three times, and the mean values were computed (n = 3 per group) [50].

Inhibition of Protein Denaturation Assay

The literature was consulted to evaluate the potential of preventing albumin denaturation [51, 52]. A 5 mL reaction mixture was prepared by combining 0.2 mL of bovine serum albumin (BSA), 2.8 mL of PBS (pH 6.4), and 2 mL of Myrtus communis extract at concentrations of 5, 10, 15, 25, 50, and 100 mg/mL. The solution was placed in an incubator set at a temperature of 37°C for 15 min and then subjected to heating at a temperature of 70°C for 5 min. A control was established using distilled water of equivalent volume. Finally, the samples' absorbance was measured at a wavelength of 660 nm. The reference drug, acetylsalicylic acid (ASA), was utilized at concentrations of 5 mg/mL, 10 mg/mL, 15 mg/mL, 25 mg/mL, 50 mg/mL, and 100 mg/mL. It was subjected to the same treatment and evaluated at identical absorbance levels. The protein denaturation percentage inhibition was determined by employing the following calculation: Protein denaturation inhibition(%)=Acontrol--Atest/Acontrol×100%

Variable A control represents the control sample absorption, while variable A test represents the test sample absorption [53]. Each concentration was tested in triplicate (n = 3).

Cell Viability

The MTT assay, using 3‐(4, 5‐dimethylthiazol‐2‐yl)‐2, 5‐diphenyltetrazolium bromide obtained from GIBCO‐BRL in Eggenstein, Germany, was employed to accurately measure the activity of cells cultivated on the hydrogels. The 3T3 fibroblastic cell line was cultivated at a density of 1 × 104 cells on 150 µL hydrogels in Dulbecco's modified Eagle's medium: nutritional mixture F‐12 (DMEM/F12; Gibco, Grand Island, USA). The medium was enriched with 100 µg/mL of streptomycin (Sigma‐Aldrich, USA), 100 units/mL of penicillin (Sigma‐Aldrich, USA), and 10% (v/v) fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco, Grand Island, USA). The cells were cultivated in a high‐humidity, controlled environment supplemented with 5% carbon dioxide and a temperature of 37°C. The culture media were taken from the 96‐well plate at both time intervals (1 and 3 days after the cells were sown). Then, a volume of 0.2 mL of MTT solution at a concentration of 0.5 mg/mL was introduced to each well. The plate was placed in a light‐restricted environment and kept at a temperature of 37°C for 3–4 h. Subsequently, the solution was discarded, and DMSO (0.1 mL) was included in each well. The purple formazan crystals that were created were solubilized in DMSO. The absorbance was measured at a specific wavelength of around 570 nm using a microplate reader called Anthos 2020, manufactured by Biochrom in Berlin, Germany. The mean value of triplicate wells for each sample was calculated, and the entire experiment was repeated three times independently (n = 3 independent replicates per condition) [54].

In‐vivo Wound Healing Study

The wound‐healing ability of the hydrogels that were created was evaluated using a model that involved removing a full‐thickness section of skin. Eighteen male Wistar rats, aged 2 months and weighing between 200–220 g, were acquired from the Pasteur Institute in Tehran, Iran. The in‐vivo studies were authorized by the ethical council of Shahroud University of Medical Sciences and conducted in compliance with the university's norms (IR.SHMU.AEC.1401.006). This animal study was conducted in full compliance with the ARRIVE guidelines 2.0 (https://arriveguidelines.org). All essential elements of the ARRIVE checklist, including sample size justification, randomization, blinding, and statistical methods.

Anesthesia was initiated by injecting a combination of xylazine (7.5 mg/100 g body weight; Alfasan, Woerden, The Netherlands) and ketamine (0.8 mg/100 g body weight; Alfasan, Woerden, The Netherlands) via injection into the peritoneal cavity [55]. For post‑operative pain management, all animals received buprenorphine (0.05 mg/kg, subcutaneously, every 12 h for 48 h) and carprofen (5 mg/kg, subcutaneously, once daily for 3 days) following surgery. These analgesics were administered to minimize pain and inflammation in accordance with institutional guidelines and previous wound healing studies [56]. Subsequently, a full‐thickness lesion measuring 1.50 × 1.50 cm was surgically removed from the dorsal skin of rats, namely in the area surrounding the back of the neck, by a scalpel blade (Figure 1). Subsequently, the animals were randomly assigned into three groups using a computer‐generated random number sequence (www.random.org) performed by an independent researcher not involved in the treatments or outcome assessments. The sample size of six animals per group (n = 6) was determined based on preliminary pilot experiments (n = 3 per group) using similar hydrogel dressings in a full‑thickness excisional wound model in rats. Those pilot data revealed a mean difference in wound closure rate of approximately 25% between treatment and control groups after 14 days, with a pooled standard deviation of ∼8%, corresponding to an effect size (Cohen's d) of ∼2.5. A two‑sided t‑test with α = 0.05 and β = 0.20 (80% power) indicated a minimum requirement of 4–5 animals per group. To account for potential dropouts or technical failures, we conservatively selected n = 6 per group, which is also consistent with previous studies in this field [57, 58]. Humane endpoints: Animals were monitored twice daily (morning and evening) throughout the study period. Predefined humane endpoints included: (i) body weight loss exceeding 20% of initial weight within 48 h or progressive weight loss despite nutritional support; (ii) clinical signs of severe distress such as hunched posture, persistent piloerection, lethargy, reluctance to move, or vocalization upon gentle handling; (iii) wound complications including severe infection (purulent discharge, necrosis extending beyond wound margins, or systemic signs of illness); and (iv) a distress scoring system (0–3 for appearance, behavior, and wound appearance; maximum composite score 9). Any animal reaching a composite score ≥7 or a score of 3 on any single parameter was humanely euthanized. No animals met these endpoints during the 14‐day study period, and all survived until scheduled euthanasia [59].

Exclusion criteria and missing data: Predefined exclusion criteria were: (i) death before scheduled euthanasia, (ii) reaching any humane endpoint described above, (iii) wound infection requiring intervention, or (iv) technical failure during wound creation or dressing application. No animals met these criteria, and all 18 rats completed the 14‐day study. All wound images were captured and all skin samples were collected without any loss or technical failure. Consequently, there were no missing data points for any of the assessed parameters (wound closure, histology, body weight). No imputation methods were applied.

At the end of the 14‐day study period, animals were humanely euthanized by an intraperitoneal overdose of sodium pentobarbital (150 mg/kg) followed by cervical dislocation, in accordance with institutional guidelines. The wound tissue was then surgically removed and subsequently prepared for histological examination. The wound samples were promptly preserved using formalin (10% solution).

Histological Analysis

The excised skin samples from each group were gathered to assess the histological changes. The skin specimens were kept in formalin (10% solution), then treated and enclosed in paraffin. The skin specimens were cut at the wound surface into 4 µm slices. In histological analysis, skin sections underwent hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) and Masson's trichrome staining. The organ slices were exclusively stained with H&E [60]. Histopathological evaluation (re‐epithelialization, granulation tissue formation, collagen deposition, neovascularization, and skin appendage regeneration) was performed independently by two experienced histopathologists blinded to the group identities; any disagreements were resolved by consensus. Histological findings, including collagen deposition, re‐epithelialization, neovascularization, and granulation tissue formation, were further assessed using a semi‑quantitative scoring system (Poor +, Moderate ++, Good +++, Near‑normal ++++). The evaluation was performed by a blinded observer based on comparative microscopic examination of stained tissue sections, and the results were summarized in the corresponding histopathological scoring table [61].

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software (IBM SPSS Statistics, V.23, Armonk, NY, USA). For swelling ratio, weight loss, drug release, cell viability (over time), and wound closure data, which involve repeated measurements over time across multiple experimental groups, a two‐way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with time and treatment group as fixed factors was employed. The interaction term (time × group) was specifically evaluated. When a significant interaction was detected, Tukey's honest significant difference (HSD) post‐hoc test was used for multiple comparisons. Results

Morphology Evaluation

The scanning electron microscopy (SEM) image was used to study the microscopic morphology of the GEL/CMC/Myrtus communis hydrogel and assess its structure (Figure 2). The SEM picture showed that the synthesized hydrogel possesses a remarkably porous structure with interconnected pores. The average pore diameter has been determined by analyzing 20 randomly selected locations in each image using SEM. The analysis using ImageJ software (NIH, Bethesda, USA) revealed that the mean pore size for this scaffold was 87 ± 51 µm for GEL/CMC and 119.73 ± 13.67 µm for GEL/CMC/Myrtus communis (10%), respectively. In addition to these mean values, the overall pore size distribution across the scaffold ranged from 75 to 135 µm. The coexistence of an average pore size with a wider size range reflects the inherent heterogeneity of freeze‐dried hydrogels, which typically develop pores of varying dimensions. This heterogeneous yet interconnected porous network is considered beneficial for cellular adhesion and migration, as supported by previous studies [62].

This study has indicated that it could absorb extra fluid, and it would increase with adding Myrtus communis as an agent.

Weight Loss

The weight loss of GEL/CMC hydrogels containing varying concentrations of Myrtus communis (0%, 0.1%, 0.5%, 1%, 5%, and 10% w/w) was monitored in phosphate‐buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.4) at 37°C. The rate at which the wound dressing breaks down should be aligned with the rate at which the wound is healing. The slow rate of deterioration impedes the substitution of the wound dressing with the newly developed tissue. Conversely, the rapid rate at which the wound deteriorates may result in the wound site becoming vacant, thus stopping the process of wound healing [65]. The weight loss data for the produced hydrogels are presented in Figure 5. By adding Myrtus communis at the maximum concentration (10%), the degradability rate becomes 66.4%, which is probably due to its hydrophilic nature and may cause a gradual weakening of the scaffold's strength over time.

Release

Porosity and swelling are the two key hydrogel structural features that govern drug release [66]. The release of Myrtus communis from GEL/CMC hydrogels (0.1%, 0.5%, 1%, 5%, and 10% w/w) was quantified in phosphate‐buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.4) at 37°C. The release of Myrtus communis from the GEL/CMC hydrogel containing 0.5% Myrtus communis was measured at 48.21 ± 2.87 µg after the first 2 h, increased to 84.33 ± 4.01 µg after 6 h, and then exhibited a steady release of 322.11 ± 9.76 µg over a period of 3 days (Figure 6A). The findings show that Myrtus communis is gradually released from the GEL/CMC hydrogel at all concentrations, with the majority of the plant extract released during the first 48 h. The corresponding release rate profiles (µg/h) for all formulations are shown in Figure 6B. The initial release rate (0–2 h) was highest for the 10% group (305.62 µg/h) and lowest for the 0.1% group (4.94 µg/h). For all extract‐containing hydrogels, the release rate declined sharply after 6 h and reached a sustained phase (6 h–72 h) with rates ranging from 0.95 to 53.02 µg/h, confirming a controlled release profile suitable for wound healing applications. The UV‐Vis method validation results are summarized in Table 2. The wavelength of maximum absorbance (λmax) for the Myrtus communis extract in PBS (pH 7.4) was determined to be ∼270 nm. A strong linear relationship was observed between absorbance and concentration (R2 = 0.999) over the range of 5–150 µg/mL. The calculated LOD and LOQ were 1.2 µg/mL and 3.6 µg/mL, respectively. Spiking recovery tests yielded a mean recovery of 101.3% (range: 97.5–105.1%), confirming the accuracy of the method. No spectral interference from hydrogel degradation products was detected at 270 nm.

Hemocompatibility

Adaptation of the prepared wound dressing with blood cells, mainly red blood cells, is considered an essential requirement for the process of wound healing [67]. Wound dressing interacts with erythrocytes after implantation, determining subsequent reactions like inflammation [68]. To assess compatibility, GEL/CMC with and without Myrtus communis (0%, 0.1%, 0.5%, 1%, 5%, and 10% w/w) was tested with erythrocytes. The results from Figure 7 show that hemolysis induced by different prepared groups is notably lower than that of the positive control, which suggests that the prepared hydrogels are hemocompatible. However, an increase in the dosage of Myrtus communis leads to a rise in hemolysis.

Blood Clotting Index

Hemostasis is the first stage of wound healing to prevent blood loss before cell proliferation and tissue maturation can occur, enabling skin repair [69]. The blood clotting assay is an effective method to measure the antithrombotic properties of biomaterials. Generally, a higher BCI indicates a slower clotting rate and, therefore, improved blood compatibility [70]. The in vitro blood clotting ability of GEL/CMC hydrogel with Myrtus communis (0%, 0.1%, 0.5%, 1%, 5%, and 10% w/w) was evaluated. BCI measurements indicated that the synthesized hydrogels showed markedly reduced values in comparison to the control group (Figure 8).

Anti‐inflammatory

The process of protein denaturation was employed to evaluate the substance's anti‐inflammatory characteristics. This experiment aimed to assess the in vitro anti‐inflammatory effects of different doses of Myrtus communis, in contrast to acetylsalicylic acid, a commonly used standard anti‐inflammatory agent.

The analysis was carried out by measuring the degree of denaturation of BSA, or bovine serum albumin (Figure 9). All dosages tested demonstrated adequate inhibitory impact compared to acetylsalicylic acid. The highest and lowest levels of inhibition of BSA denaturation by Myrtus communis were seen at concentrations of 100 mg/mL and 5 mg/mL, resulting in inhibition percentages of 60.15 ± 3.27% and 26.33 ± 1.98%, respectively. The conventional drug acetylsalicylic acid exhibited inhibitions of 30.97 ± 0.89 % and 92.11 ± 5.16 % at concentrations of 5 and 100 mg/mL, respectively.

The study investigated the ability of various concentrations (5, 10, 15, 25, 50, and 100 mg/mL) of Myrtus communis to prevent the denaturation of proteins caused by heat, in comparison to acetylsalicylic acid (Aspirin), which was used as a reference medicine.

Cell Viability

MTT tests were performed at three separate time intervals (24, 48, and 72 h) to assess the viability of cells in GEL/CMC hydrogel with and without different concentrations of Myrtus communis (0%, 0.1%, 0.5%, 1%, 5%, and 10% w/w) (Figure 10). The findings indicated that, within 24 h of incubation, the cellular activity of all experimental groups was reduced compared to the positive control group (which did not involve the use of hydrogel). However, the data further showed that 72 h after cell implantation, the survival rate of the group treated with GEL/CMC/Myrtus communis was higher than that of the control group at concentrations up to 1%. This result indicates an absence of cytotoxicity, contributes to improved survival, and also demonstrates that cell proliferation depends on concentration. The MTT data showed that the cell survival rate of the group treated with 0.5% GEL/CMC/Myrtus communis was higher than in other groups at different periods. As a result, we determined that this concentration was optimal for the in vivo study.

Discussion

Recently, there has been significant progress in the field of skin tissue engineering, with a focus on creating innovative and efficient solutions that facilitate the regeneration of skin tissue. Moreover, hydrogel‐based wound dressings are widely regarded as suitable materials for wound closure, promoting angiogenesis, and achieving scar‐free healing, all of which are crucial for tissue regeneration [71]. Wound dressing materials based on carboxymethyl cellulose are highly appealing due to their advantageous physicochemical characteristics. CMC wound dressings are known for their flexibility, ability to absorb exudate, promotion of angiogenesis, and facilitation of autolytic debridement [14]. CMC‐based biomaterials, such as hydrogels, films, and nanocomposites, are extensively utilized in wound dressing and drug administration applications due to their non‐toxicity to humans, water solubility, and abundance in nature [72, 73]. Gelatin‐based hydrogels have garnered significant interest as bioactive matrices due to their biocompatibility, biodegradability, and many functional groups for chemical modification and structural resemblance to the natural extracellular matrix [74]. Myrtus communis, also referred to as common myrtle, is a flowering plant that is indigenous to the Mediterranean region. Historically, myrtle has been employed as a treatment for inflammation, bleeding, and skin‐related problems [17]. Therefore, in this study, we used two natural polymers, carboxymethyl cellulose and gelatin, to absorb exudate and provide conditions similar to the extracellular matrix. In addition, Myrtus communis was added to the polymer composite to promote healing at the wound site and create a suitable cover and dressing.

The SEM was employed to examine the pore structure and morphology of the hydrogel. The images obtained show that the hydrogels have a porous structure with interconnecting pores. The pore size of the scaffolds was calculated to be 119.73 ± 13.67 when combined with Myrtus communis. The size of the pores in scaffolds and their interconnected porous structure play a crucial role in influencing the capacity of cells to penetrate and the transfer of nutrients in tissue engineering applications [75]. Fibroblasts and keratinocytes, with a size ranging from 10 to 15 microns, can penetrate the innermost region of the hydrogel that has been formed [76].

The rheological behavior of hydrogels is a critical determinant of their performance as wound dressings [77]. Our findings demonstrated that all formulated GEL/CMC hydrogels exhibited shear‑thinning (pseudoplastic) behavior, which is characterized by a progressive decrease in viscosity with increasing shear rate. This phenomenon is primarily attributed to the temporary disentanglement and alignment of polymer chains along the direction of the applied flow, which reduces intermolecular resistance. Similar pseudoplastic behavior has been reported for CMC‑gelatin hydrogel systems in previous studies [37]. Furthermore, we observed a concentration‐dependent reduction in viscosity upon incorporation of the Myrtus communis extract. This observation is consistent with a previous study by Ramírez‐Brewer et al., who reported that the addition of natural extracts at specific concentrations modulated the rheological properties of polysaccharide‐based hydrogels [78]. In the context of our hydrogel system, this effect is likely mediated by the phytochemicals within the Myrtus communis extract, particularly polar phenolic compounds and polysaccharides. These components may disrupt the pre‐existing hydrogen bonding between the GEL and CMC polymer chains, leading to a less densely entangled network [79]. From an application standpoint, the maintenance of shear‐thinning properties is of great importance. This rheological profile ensures that the hydrogel retains sufficient viscosity to remain localized on the wound bed under static conditions, while facilitating application, spreadability, and conformability when shear forces are applied during dressing changes or patient movement [80].

As mentioned in previous research [17, 81, 82] the water uptake capability of hydrogel structures is attributed to the hydrophilic groups. Increased water absorption promotes the proliferation of cells and enhances the transfer of nutrients to the wound site [83]. Myrtus communis extract promotes water absorption due to the plant's abundance of hydrophilic phytochemicals, particularly polar phenolic and polysaccharide substances. The high number of hydroxyl (‐OH), carboxyl (‐COOH), and glycosidic groups in substances like gallic acid, quercetin, myristicin, and tannins, which exist in Myrtus communis, makes the GEL/CMC hydrogel network much more hydrophilic [84, 85]. The electrostatic repulsion between CMC carboxylate groups and hydrogen bonding with gelatin creates a three‐dimensional network inside the GEL/CMC system that can expand a lot [86, 87]. The Myrtus communis extract adds functional groups to the polymer matrix, which makes it more hydrophilic. These groups increase the number of places where water can bind and the number of hydrogen bonds that can form with water molecules. This increases the osmotic pressure and flow of water in the hydrogel. At higher concentrations, the extract may partially break up strongly crosslinked areas or create microstructural differences, which would lower the steric barrier and speed up water diffusion and swelling to equilibrium. The swelling behavior that changes over time becomes worse after 12 to 24 h, which means that the fluid stays in the body longer. This function helps dressings cope with exudate [49]. Our research shows that the highest level of water absorption takes place 24 h after incubation, leading to an increase in weight of almost 80% compared to the original dried weight. The phenomenon of swelling, when seen, shows beneficial properties for the delivery of medications and substances like Myrtus communis in the context of a wound region.

The degradation rate of hydrogel plays a crucial role in ensuring the timely and effective repair of damaged tissue [88]. The addition of Myrtus communis to the hydrogel increased weight loss. The maximum amount of biodegradation, measuring 63.25 ± 6.02%, was achieved in the CMC/CMCS/Myrtus communis 10% group after 72 h of incubation. This feature, as noted in the swelling test section, can be ascribed to the presence of hydrophilic chemicals, including phenols and polysaccharides, in the plant extract. These compounds enhance water infiltration into the hydrogel matrix and induce hydrolytic breakdown of ester/amide linkages in gelatin and glycosidic bonds in CMC, promoting chain scission and disintegration of polymer fragments [89, 90]. Furthermore, taking into account the temporal degradation pattern, the plant extract may partially diminish the interchain hydrogen bonds and ionic cross‐links within CMC/Gel, particularly at elevated concentrations (5%‐10%) [91, 92]. Prior research concerning biodegradable wound‐dressing hydrogels indicates favorable degradation rates between 40% and 80% over a period of 2 to 4 days [93, 94]. This ensures that the dressing retains adequate structural integrity during the initial stages of healing while progressively dissolving as tissue repair advances. Consequently, the degradation rate identified in our system corresponds closely with these documented ranges and is deemed suitable for applications in wound healing.

This release of the drug or other agents may result in cell interaction and nutrient transfer due to the standard degradation frequency [95]. The result of this study confirmed this concept and showed that the high biodegradability of the synthesized scaffold can lead to the slow but maximum release of the Myrtus communis contained in the hydrogel matrix. This property helps to reduce the time of dressing change to 48 h and reduces the waste of time, cost, and difficulties associated with daily dressing changes. Furthermore, the slow drug release prevents the accumulation of the drug at the wound site and possible complications.

Ahmadi F. et al. reported that the various material compatibilities influence the hemolysis rate; hemoglobin levels in the plasma are reflected by the amount of damage caused by erythrocytes [96]. According to established safety standards, a hemolysis rate below 5% is considered indicative of suitable hemocompatibility [97]. The hemolytic potential of a plant extract can vary significantly based on its specific composition and concentration. In our study, while all hydrogel formulations demonstrated hemolysis rates significantly lower than the positive control, a clear concentration‐dependent effect was observed. The hemolysis rate remained within this safe threshold (<5%) only at lower concentrations of Myrtus communis (up to 0.5% w/w). However, when the extract concentration reached 1% w/w and above, the hemolysis rate exceeded the 5% safety limit, confirming that hemolytic activity is directly proportional to the concentration of Myrtus communis in the hydrogel.

The blood clotting index was measured to determine the clotting effectiveness of the wound dressings. The hydrogel containing the medication exhibited superior clotting capabilities, fluid absorption capacity, platelet adhesion, and fluid balance on the wound bed compared to gauze. A lower value of the blood clotting index indicates a more efficient clotting effect of the dressing [98]. Ebrahimi et al. [28] studied the relative effects of Myrtus communis on the promotion of hemostasis, which could play a role in platelet aggregation and the subsequent initiation of primary hemostasis. This study presents empirical evidence supporting the medical application of Myrtus communis in managing bleeding; however, it does not endorse its usage as the sole agent for stopping bleeding. Our results also show that the contact hydrogels of CMC, gelatin, and Myrtus communis had a positive hemostatic effect and therefore could stop bleeding at the wound site.

The pH of the wound microenvironment can vary based on the wound type. For instance, acute wounds exhibit an average pH of 7.44, while chronic wounds fall within a pH range of 7.42 to 8.90 [99]. Based on the past studies [100] and the investigation carried out in this study, the alcoholic extract of Myrtus communis has acidic properties, which can lead to a balance of the pH and neutralization of the environment when used in the hydrogel structure, but it should be noted that adding a large amount of Myrtus communis leads to an increase in acidity (up to 1% w/w). Depending on the concentration, it can be used to heal wounds, especially chronic wounds.

The association between protein denaturation and the inflammatory process is extensively recognized. The main mechanism by which NSAIDs work is believed to be the prevention of protein denaturation [101]. Several studies show the negative effect of NSAIDs on wound healing due to reduced keratinization, epithelization, angiogenesis, and granulation, but it should be noted that inflammation can also prevent healing [102]. Therefore, a proper reduction of inflammation can help the body heal and enable it to return to its pre‐injury state [103]. Based on past studies [104, [105], both aqueous and ethanol extracts exhibit notable anti‐inflammatory properties during acute inflammation. This plant possesses a characteristic that stabilizes cell membranes, thereby restricting the release of mediators. It also decreases capillary permeability. Our results also showed that Myrtus communis extracts stopped albumin from breaking down at all tested concentrations. This shows that the extracts can lower the breakdown of inflammatory proteins. Although aspirin showed a higher inhibitory effect, the plant also prevented 60% of protein denaturation, which can prevent the complete quenching of inflammation so as not to hinder the healing process. The 0.5% Myrtus communis hydrogel demonstrates an enhanced ability to promote wound healing in living organisms, with approximately 89% of wounds closing within 14 days. This effectiveness can be attributed to its beneficial physicochemical properties and the sustained release of bioagents. The 0.5% concentration successfully created a strong anti‐inflammatory microenvironment, as shown by the protein denaturation assay, while likely preventing the complete inhibition of the inflammatory phase, which is crucial for initiating the healing process. This concentration produced the best release profile, allowing bioactive compounds to be delivered to the wound bed over a long period of time when they were most needed. It also reduced the chances of rapid depletion or local accumulation, which can cause irritation. The 0.5% formulation is preferable for clinical use due to its greater compatibility with blood cells compared to higher concentrations (5%–10%), which tend to cause increased hemolysis. Consequently, it poses a lower risk of damaging blood cells upon contact with wound exudate.

High doses of certain herbs utilized in traditional medicine might exhibit toxic characteristics and adversely affect tissue health. The MTT experiment carried out in this study demonstrated that the addition of Myrtus communis to the hydrogel did not have a noticeable effect on the viability of the fibroblast cells. In addition, the group treated with GEL/CMC/Myrtus communis (0.5%) exhibited the highest amount of cell proliferation in comparison to the other groups. Conversely, the groups treated with GEL/CMC/Myrtus communis (5% and 10%) had a drop in cell viability. The findings confirmed that the hydrogel, which contained Myrtus communis at concentrations of up to 0.5%, did not exhibit any adverse effects on the cells when administered at therapeutic levels. The concentration‐dependent cellular response observed in the MTT assay was instrumental in selecting the 0.5% Myrtus communis formulation for subsequent in vivo investigation. This concentration showed the perfect balance between bioactivity and biosafety. Lower concentrations (0.1%) had small effects, but the 0.5% extract loading caused the most fibroblast growth and survival over 72 h, which was much better than the plain GEL/CMC hydrogel. This result means that a concentration of 0.5% provides an adequate therapeutic dose of phytochemicals to boost metabolic activity and encourage tissue regeneration without harming cells. On the other hand, concentrations of 5% w/w or more caused a significant drop in cell viability, which could mean that this is the point at which cells become toxic. The 0.5% GEL/CMC/Myrtus communis hydrogel was found to be the best formulation because it had the most pro‐proliferative effects and was very biocompatible, which is important for a satisfactory wound dressing.

An ideal wound‐healing dressing shelters the wound tissue against bacterial infection, decreases inflammation, and stimulates cell proliferation to facilitate the regeneration of impaired tissue [106]. Ideally, it should also function as an antioxidant since free radicals are widely recognized as the primary cause of inflammation through the wound healing process [107]. Myrtus communis exerts the most significant impact on the process of wound healing. The administration of this plant led to a reduction in neutrophil levels, a rise in the count of fibroblasts, and an enhancement in the process of epithelialization. Research done by Sharififar et al. [108, 109] and Hosseinzadeh et al. [110] has demonstrated the potent anti‐inflammatory, antioxidative, and antibacterial characteristics of this plant. In particular situations, they can exert notable impacts on the process of wound healing.

Despite acknowledging the limitations of this study—including the absence of comprehensive rheological characterization (only viscosity and shear rate were measured; SAOS parameters G′ and G″ across frequencies were not evaluated), the lack of tensile and compressive properties in the wet state, the absence of adhesion/peel tests on ex vivo skin or standardized surfaces, the lack of elastic recovery and conformability tests, as well as the absence of molecular analysis, stability analysis, and evaluation of the dressing's efficacy in other wound models (e.g., burn wounds)—and the fact that CD31 staining for quantitative angiogenesis assessment was not performed due to high costs and because angiogenesis was not a primary endpoint, future studies will offer a more comprehensive understanding of the mechanism of action and efficacy of Myrtus communis for preclinical studies.

Furthermore, while we have validated the UV‐vis release data through spiking and interference tests, we did not perform HPLC or LC‐MS quantification of specific bioactive markers in the Myrtus communis extract due to budget and instrumentation constraints. The absence of detailed phytochemical fingerprinting is a limitation of the present study, and future investigations should address this by identifying and quantifying the key active compounds responsible for the observed wound‐healing and anti‐inflammatory effects.

Gelatin and carboxymethyl cellulose‑based wound dressing materials are very appealing because of their remarkable properties, such as tissue‑like traits, biocompatibility, biodegradability, and low cost. They are widely used in various applications within the pharmaceutical and biomedical fields. The present study provides a thorough analysis of the molecular pathways involved in the wound‑healing process in M. communis. The findings of this study provide evidence that M. communis has the potential to enhance wound healing by suppressing inflammation, reducing oxidative stress, promoting the formation of new blood vessels, and preventing infection in skin‑related conditions.

The in vitro and in vivo data together show that the 0.5% (w/w) Myrtus communis‐loaded GEL/CMC hydrogel is the best formulation in this study. Three key findings support the selection of this formulation as the best option: it demonstrated the highest rate of fibroblast proliferation in vitro, indicating its potential to significantly accelerate tissue regeneration. Second, it showed the best balance of anti‐inflammatory properties and hemocompatibility, which helped keep the wound environment stable without causing any problems. Overall, it sped up wound closure and improved tissue organization in a full‐thickness wound model more than the control and plain hydrogel groups. The 0.5% variant consistently demonstrated the most favorable therapeutic window among the concentrations evaluated, making it the most promising candidate for future translational development. This illustrates the importance of precise dosage optimization in herbal‐based therapies.

Conclusion

This study examined the collective impact of fabricated GEL/CMC/Myrtus communis hydrogels on skin wound healing in vitro and in vivo. Porosity and interconnected pores were established through characterization. The swelling property of the hydrogel demonstrated a capacity to absorb and maintain wound exudates. The results suggested that the characteristics of CMC/Gel were advantageous for the application of skin wound dressings. An in vitro cell growth study indicated that no cytotoxicity was observed at the recommended dose of the hydrogel. However, increasing the dose of the plant resulted in hemolysis. These findings indicate that GEL/CMC/Myrtus communis 0.5% (w/w) hydrogels hold significant promise for effective wound treatment, tissue regeneration, and anti‐inflammatory properties, warranting further comprehensive investigation.
