Quantum Walks and Exact RG

Abstract

The local physics of light scalar fields in de Sitter space is well described by classical random walks, as expressed through the framework of Stochastic Inflation. Recent work has clarified how this formalism arises from quantum field theory (QFT) and the renormalization group (RG), allowing for corrections to this formalism to be determined order by order. Yet, this description is incomplete. For example, the quantum dynamics of these fields are expected to become important when determining the tail of the probability distribution for the fluctuations. In this paper, we develop the understanding of fields in de Sitter as a quantum walk in order to bridge the gap between the classical and quantum description. We use the framework of exact RG to calculate the evolution equation for the reduced density matrix of the long wavelength fields. This master equation provides the direct map from light fields to models of quantum walks. We show how to reduce the master equation to Stochastic Inflation, and provide a new understanding of how the higher-order corrections arise. In the process, we demonstrate that divergences and secular growth in de Sitter, for both light and heavy fields, can be absorbed by (dynamical) renormalization.

The stochastic formalism is therefore limited to subsystems within de Sitter space

Despite this long history, the quantum description of light fields remains under-developed. For one, the stochastic evolution equation appears even in trivial free field theories, where the short and long wavelength models are uncorrelated. Although one recovers the expected evolution to leading order, the quantum mechanical nature of this evolution equation depends on this procedure. Tracing over the coins gives rise to different types of stochastic behavior, including both purely classical and uniquely quantum walks, depending on how the systems are coupled. Quantum Walks for Cosmology

The evolution of a scalar field in de Sitter has long been argued to (locally) resemble a random walk
[
5
,
6
]
. The resulting stochastic approach is classical in nature, leaving quantum mechanics responsible only for the microscopic origin of the noise. The universe, of course, remains quantum mechanical and ultimately the classical random walk must be replaced with a quantum generalization. In this section, we will present some simple quantum walks that will serve as useful intuition for de Sitter space. The models in the section are closely related to the literature on quantum Brownian motion
[
46
,
47
,
48
]
, however it is important that the quantum walks relevant to cosmology are not equivalent to these models and we will emphasize the points of departure.

2.1
Density Matrix of a Gaussian Walk

We will imagine our system is split into a massive field whose wavefunction in the position basis,
\Psi(X)
encodes the random fluctuations of a
N
harmonic oscillators in their ground states,

\psi({\vec{x}}_{i})=\frac{1}{(2\pi\sigma^{2})^{1/4}}\exp\left(-x_{i}^{2}/(4\sigma^{2})\right)\ .

(2.1)

In this regard, the location of heavy field,
X
, defines a pointer basis
[
51
]
for the measurement of the oscillators. We can implement a classical gaussian random walk using this quantum system as follows: for
N=0
, the initial state of the walker is definitely at the origin,
\Psi(X)=\delta(X)
. We then act on
|\Psi\rangle
by a shift proportional to
x_{i}
for each
i=1,..,N
:

|\Psi,N\rangle=e^{i\hat{P}\sum_{i}\hat{x}_{i}}|X=0\rangle\otimes\prod_{i}|\psi_{i}\rangle\ .

(2.2)

The reduced density matrix for the walker after coupling to
N
oscillators, is given by

\displaystyle{\rm Tr}_{\psi_{i}}\rho=\rho_{N}(X,X^{\prime})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\prod_{i}\int dx_{i}|\psi(x_{i})|^{2}\right)\left|X=\sum_{i}x_{i}\right\rangle\left\langle X^{\prime}=\sum_{i}x_{i}\right|

(2.3)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\delta(X-X^{\prime})\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^{2}N}}\exp(-X^{2}/(2\sigma^{2}N))\ .

(2.4)

The second line can be determined by solving Equation (
2.2
) in the momentum basis of the walker. This is mathematically equivalent to a common derivation of probability distribution for
N
-steps of a classical random walk using the Fourier transform. Indeed, this is a classical random walk for all practical purposes.

To gain intuition for our evolution equations, it is useful to derive the master equation for the evolution of the density matrix when increasing the number of harmonic oscillators from
N
to
N+1
,

\rho_{N+1}(X,X^{\prime})={\rm Tr}_{x_{N+1}}\left[e^{i\hat{P}\hat{x}_{N+1}}\rho_{N}\otimes|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|e^{-i\hat{P}\hat{x}_{N+1}}\right]\ .

(2.5)

We can use this expression to derive an equation for the time evolution in terms of
T=Ndt
in the continuum limit
N\to\infty
and
dt\to 0
while holding
T
and
\sigma^{2}N=\alpha T
finite.
Taking the trace in the
x_{N+1}
-position basis and treating
x_{N+1}={\cal O}(dt^{1/2})
we can expand both sides to
{\cal O}(dt)
to find

\frac{\partial}{\partial T}\rho=-\frac{\alpha}{2}\left(\hat{P}^{2}\rho+\rho\hat{P}^{2}-2\hat{P}\rho\hat{P}\right)\ .

(2.6)

In the
X
position basis,
\rho(X,X^{\prime},T)
this becomes the differential equation

\frac{\partial}{\partial T}\rho(X,X^{\prime},T)=\frac{\alpha}{2}\left(\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial X^{2}}+\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial X^{\prime}{}^{2}}+2\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial X\partial X^{\prime}}\right)\rho(X,X^{\prime},T)\ .

(2.7)

Naively, one might expect the diagonal elements,
X=X^{\prime}
, to obey the classical heat equation, but this is not the case. In fact, the density matrix is actually singular if we take
X=X^{\prime}
, which suggests that we need to be more careful to solving for the density matrix, even when we expect it to be diagonal.

The master equation for our walk is easier to solve in the momentum representation of the density matrix,
X\to P
and
X^{\prime}\to P^{\prime}
, so that our evolution is given by

\frac{\partial}{\partial T}\rho(P,P^{\prime},T)=-\frac{\alpha}{2}(P-P^{\prime})^{2}\rho(P,P^{\prime},T)\ .

(2.8)

We can directly solve this first order ODE to find

\rho(P,P^{\prime},T)=\rho(P,P^{\prime},0)\exp\left(-\frac{\alpha}{2}(P-P^{\prime})^{2}T\right)\ .

(2.9)

We can find the solution in the position basis by Fourier transform

\rho(X,X^{\prime},T)=\int\frac{dPdP^{\prime}}{(2\pi)^{2}}e^{iPX}e^{-iP^{\prime}X^{\prime}}\rho(P,P^{\prime},0)\exp\left(-\frac{\alpha}{2}(P-P^{\prime})^{2}T\right)\ .

(2.10)

If we impose that
\rho(P,P^{\prime},0)=1
, directly integrating reproduces Equation (
2.4
).

We can gain a lot of useful intuition for these walks rewriting our results in terms of Wigner distribution function
1
1

1

We can also define the Wigner distribution starting from momentum representation of the density matrix,
\rho(P,P^{\prime},T)
, by taking the inverse Fourier transform of
P-P^{\prime}\to x
, while the “momentum” of the Wigner representation becomes the other linear combination
P+P^{\prime}\to 2p
.
,

W(x,p)=\int d\Delta xe^{-ip\Delta x}\rho\left(X=x+\frac{\Delta x}{2},X^{\prime}=x-\frac{\Delta x}{2}\right)\ .

(2.11)

By direct calculation, our solution in Equation (
2.4
) takes a familiar form,

W(x,p,T)=\exp(-x^{2}/(2\alpha T))\ .

(2.12)

We can bring this even closer to the classical description by transforming the master equation for the density matrix, Equation (
2.7
), to an evolution equation for Wigner distribution. Specifically, if we change variabels to
X,X^{\prime}=x\pm\Delta x
and using the chain rule

\frac{\partial}{\partial x}=\frac{\partial}{\partial X}+\frac{\partial}{\partial X^{\prime}}\ .

(2.13)

This is the linear combination of derivatives in the evolution of the density matrix, so that

\frac{\partial}{\partial T}W(x,p,T)=\frac{\alpha}{2}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x^{2}}W(x,p,T)\ .

(2.14)

In this precise sense, we see that the Wigner distribution obeys the heat equation for a Gaussian quantum walk in the position basis. This is a strong hint that the Wigner distribution provides a more natural starting point for discussing the evolution of these kinds of quantum walks.

None of the conclusions in this section should be surprising, as we have simply translated a purely classical random walk into quantum language. However, in anticipating the possibility of a more fundamentally quantum system, it is useful to look at mathematically equivalent (and still effectively classical walks) in other representations. For example, suppose our walk was defined in momentum space so that one step of the walk shifts

|\Psi,N\rangle=e^{i\hat{X}\sum_{i}\hat{x}_{i}}|P=0\rangle\times\prod_{i}|\psi_{i}\rangle\ .

(2.15)

Following the above logic with
\hat{P}\to\hat{X}
, the density matrix obeys the master equation in the position basis

\frac{\partial}{\partial T}\rho(X,X^{\prime},T)=-\frac{\alpha}{2}(X-X^{\prime})^{2}\rho(P,P^{\prime},T)\ .

(2.16)

Of course, in terms of the Wigner distribution, using
X-X^{\prime}=2\Delta x
, this is just the heat equation

\frac{\partial}{\partial T}W(x,p,T)=\frac{\alpha}{2}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial p^{2}}W(x,p,T)\ .

(2.17)

In terms of Wigner distribution, both types of walks look purely classical. In contrast, recognizing a classical walk in terms of the density matrix directly requires picking the right variables from the outset.

2.2
Quantum Brownian Motion with a Potential

Now we would like to introduce additional forces on our quantum system. We start from the same Gaussian Walk for a quantum particle via
N
harmonic oscillators in a bath, but now we will additionally we immerse the walker,
X
, in a deterministic potential
V(X)
. As a result, we have the combined Hamiltonian of the walker and the harmonic oscillators as

H=\frac{P^{2}}{2M}+V(X)+P\sum_{i}x_{i}\ .

(2.18)

We can understand this Hamiltonian using the operator evolution equations, or the classical equations of motion,

\dot{X}=\frac{P}{M}+\textbf{}x_{i}\qquad\dot{P}=-V^{\prime}(X)\ .

(2.19)

This model is very similar to the Caldeira-Leggett model
[
46
]
but we notice that the noise directly in the position variable, rather than a random force that appears in the
\dot{P}
equation of motion. If
X\to x
and
P\to p
were purely classical statistical variables, we expect the classical phase space probability distribution
W_{c}(x,p,T)
to follow the Fokker-Planck equation

\frac{d}{dT}W_{c}(x,p,T)=\alpha^{2}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x^{2}}W_{c}(x,p,T)-\frac{p}{M}\frac{\partial}{\partial x}W_{c}(x,p,T)+V^{\prime}(x)\frac{\partial}{\partial p}W_{c}(x,p,T)\ .

(2.20)

Of course,
X
and
P
are operators and require a quantum mechanical equivalent of this evolution equation. After tracing over the harmonic oscillators,
x_{i}
, the evolution of the walker will be described by the evolution of the reduced density matrix,
\rho_{R}
. We can write this evolution using the Lindbladian formalism, so that

\dot{\rho}_{R}=i[H,\rho]+\sum_{\mu}\bigg(L_{\mu}\rho L_{\mu}^{\dagger}-\frac{1}{2}\{L_{\mu}^{\dagger}L_{\mu},\rho_{R}\}\bigg)\ ,\\

(2.21)

where
L_{\mu}
are the jump operators given by

L_{\mu}=iP{}_{i}\bra{\mu}\hat{x}\ket{0}_{i}\ .

(2.22)

The states
|\mu\rangle_{i}
and
|0\rangle_{i}
are states of the
i
th harmonic oscillator that we are tracing over at time
t
. The jump operator is non-zero only for the first excited state,

L_{\mu=1}=iP\alpha\qquad\alpha=\bra{1}\hat{x}\ket{0}=\sqrt{\frac{1}{2m\omega}}=\sigma\ ,

(2.23)

and the Lindbladian evolution is therefore

\displaystyle\dot{\rho}_{R}

\displaystyle=-i[H,\rho]-\alpha^{2}(p-p^{\prime})^{2}\rho

(2.24)

\displaystyle=-i\left(\frac{p^{2}}{2M}-\frac{p^{\prime 2}}{2M}\right)\rho-i(V(x)-V(x^{\prime}))\rho_{R}-\alpha^{2}(p-p^{\prime})^{2}\rho_{R}\ .

To compare to the classical probability distribution, we transform to the Wigner representation

\frac{\partial W}{\partial t}=\alpha^{2}\frac{\partial^{2}W}{\partial x^{2}}-i\left(V\left(x+\frac{i}{2}\partial_{p}\right)-V\left(x-\frac{i}{2}\partial_{p}\right)\right)W-\frac{p}{M}\frac{\partial W}{\partial x}\ .

(2.25)

Expanding around the extremum where
\partial_{p}
is small, we get that

V\left(x+\frac{i}{2}\partial_{p}\right)-V\left(x-\frac{i}{2}\partial_{p}\right)=i\frac{\partial}{\partial p}V^{\prime}(x)-\frac{i}{24}\frac{\partial^{3}}{\partial p^{3}}V^{\prime\prime\prime}(x)+\ldots

(2.26)

and thus we have

\frac{\partial W}{\partial t}=\alpha^{2}\frac{\partial^{2}W}{\partial x^{2}}-\frac{p}{M}\frac{\partial W}{\partial x}+V^{\prime}(x)\frac{\partial W}{\partial p}-\frac{V^{\prime\prime\prime}(x)}{24}\frac{\partial^{3}W}{\partial p^{3}}+\ldots

(2.27)

Comparing this to the classical phase space distribution (
2.20
), we see that we get higher order derivatives in
p
purely due to a quantum effect. These terms are suppressed by powers of
\hbar
.

2.3
A walk for stochastic inflation

Having introduced quantum walks with a potential, the final ingredient required for building intuition for cosmology is introduction of friction for the walker,
X
. This is straightforward in the Langevin description of a classical walk but is much more involved for a quantum walk. However, we have seen that quantum walks end up looking like classical walks in phase space when using the Wigner distribution. We will therefore consider a classical walk with friction that will reproduce key features of our quantum walk for the dS density matrix.

In the presence of friction, the general Langevin equation for a classical walker takes the form

\dot{x}=\frac{p}{m}+\xi_{1}\qquad\dot{p}=-\gamma p-V^{\prime}(x)+\xi_{2}\ ,

(2.28)

where
\gamma>0
controls the friction. For most of Newtonian physics applications, one considers Langevin equations with
\xi_{1}=0
. Such a system of equations describes random forces due to thermal baths, and the equilibrium probability distribution is the Maxwell-Boltzmann distribution

W_{\text{eq}}(x,p)\propto\exp\left(-\beta\left(\frac{p^{2}}{2m}+V(x)\right)\right)\ .

(2.29)

This is not the kind of walks that describes fields in de Sitter. Instead, we should consider a Langevin system where the noise is only in
x
rather than in
p
,
\xi_{1}\neq 0
and
\xi_{2}=0
. In other words, we want to study the following walk

\dot{x}=\frac{p}{m}+\sqrt{2D}\xi\qquad\dot{p}=-\gamma p-V^{\prime}(x)\ ,

(2.30)

which represents a particle in an external potential, with friction in
p
characterized by
\gamma
and kicks in
x
characterized by
D
. As we will see, even at
\hbar\to 0
, this equation reproduces key features that we will encounter in stochastic inflation. The phase space probability distribution,
W_{c}
, at leading order, evolves as

\frac{dW_{c}}{dt}=D\frac{d^{2}W_{c}}{dx^{2}}+\frac{d}{dp}(\gamma pW_{c}+V^{\prime}(x)W_{c})-\frac{p}{m}\frac{dW_{c}}{dx}\ .

(2.31)

In order to solve this system, we will consider the overdamped limit
\gamma\gg 1/t
, and will derive the solutions as a series in this limit. We assume that at leading order, the background value
x
is fixed while
p
fluctuates to come to equilibrium around a given value of
x
. The expectation value of
p
is described by

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\left\langle p\right\rangle_{x}

\displaystyle=-\gamma\left\langle p\right\rangle_{x}-V^{\prime}(x)\ .

(2.32)

The solution is

\left\langle p\right\rangle_{x}=e^{-\gamma t}\left\langle p(0)\right\rangle_{x}-\int_{0}^{t}ds\,e^{\gamma(s-t)}V^{\prime}(x)=e^{-\gamma t}\left\langle p(0)\right\rangle_{x}-\frac{V^{\prime}(x)}{\gamma}(1-e^{-\gamma t})\ ,

(2.33)

where we are treating
V(x)
as time independent at leading order. We see that in the overdamped limit,
\left\langle p\right\rangle_{x}\to-V^{\prime}(x)/\gamma
. Now, we can solve for the probability distribution of
x
. Integrating over
p
and substituting
\langle p\rangle_{x}
, we get the Fokker-Planck equation for
x

\frac{dP(x)}{dt}=D\frac{d^{2}P(x)}{dx^{2}}+\frac{d}{dx}\left(\frac{V^{\prime}(x)}{m\gamma}P(x)\right)\ ,

(2.34)

and hence the equilibrium distribution for
x
given by

P_{eq}(x)=Ae^{-V(x)/m\gamma D}\ .

(2.35)

We would also like to construct the equilibrium solution for the full phase space distribution, i.e., the equilibrium solution for (
2.31
). However, unlike the case for the Gibbs distribution, we do not have an analytic solution for generic
V(x)
. However, a closed form solution does exist for quadratic
V(x)
. Writing the potential as
V(x)=\frac{1}{2}kx^{2}
, we see that the equilibrium probability distribution is given by

W_{eq}=\alpha\exp(Ax^{2}+Bxp+Cp^{2})\ ,

(2.36)

where

A=-\frac{\gamma}{2D}\qquad B=-\frac{\gamma^{2}}{kD}\qquad C=-\frac{\gamma}{2mkD}-\frac{\gamma^{3}}{2k^{2}D}\ .

(2.37)

Explicitly, we have

W_{eq}\propto\exp\left(-\frac{\gamma}{2D}x^{2}-\frac{\gamma^{2}}{Dk}xp-\frac{\gamma}{2kD}\left(\frac{1}{m}+\frac{\gamma^{2}}{k}\right)p^{2}\right)\ .

(2.38)

On integrating out
p
, we get

P(x)=A\exp\left(-\frac{\frac{1}{2}k\gamma}{kD+D\gamma^{2}m}x^{2}\right)\ .

(2.39)

Writing this in the form
P\propto\exp(-V_{\rm eff}(x)/m\gamma D)
, we see that the effective potential is given by

V_{\rm eff}=\frac{1}{2}kx^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\frac{k^{2}x^{2}}{m\gamma^{2}}+\cdots

(2.40)

where the expansion is in
k/m\gamma^{2}\ll 1
. We see that even in the case of a Gaussian theory, the momentum will induce a non-trivial effect on the non-perturbative probability distribution of the position, and in particular on the tails. Finally, we note that our equilibrium solution is quite different from the thermal Gibbs distribution, which cleanly factorizes out as
\exp(-\beta(p^{2}/2m+V(x)))
. We will comment more on this in the next sections.

Corrections to the effective potential

Having provided the leading order solution for the evolution equation for
P(x)
, we want to understand the next order correction. We can do this in the Langevin picture

\dot{p}=-\gamma p-V^{\prime}(x)\qquad\dot{x}=\frac{p}{m}+\sqrt{2D}\xi(t)\ .

(2.41)

Formally, we can solve this via

\displaystyle p(t)

\displaystyle=-\int dse^{-\gamma(t-s)}V^{\prime}(x(s))

(2.42)

\displaystyle=-\int dse^{-\gamma(t-s)}V^{\prime}(x(t))-\int dse^{-\gamma(t-s)}(s-t)V^{\prime\prime}(x(t))\dot{x}(t)+\cdots

Using the Langevin equations to substitute
\dot{x}
in terms of
p
, we get

p=-\frac{V^{\prime}(x)}{\gamma}-\frac{V^{\prime}(x)V^{\prime\prime}(x)}{m\gamma^{3}}+\frac{V^{\prime\prime}(x)}{\gamma^{2}}\sqrt{2D}\xi(t)+\cdots

(2.43)

So, substituting this back into the equations for
x
, we get

\dot{x}=\sqrt{2D}\xi(t)\left(1+\frac{V^{\prime\prime}(x)}{m\gamma^{2}}\right)-\left(\frac{V^{\prime}(x)}{m\gamma}+\frac{V^{\prime}(x)V^{\prime\prime}(x)}{m^{2}\gamma^{3}}\right)\ .

(2.44)

Using the Stratonovich convention, we see that this translates into the Fokker-Planck equation

\frac{\partial P}{\partial t}=D\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\left(\left(1+\frac{V^{\prime\prime}}{m\gamma^{2}}\right)\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\left(1+\frac{V^{\prime\prime}}{m\gamma^{2}}\right)P(x)\right)+\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\left(\frac{V^{\prime}(x)}{\gamma}+\frac{V^{\prime}(x)V^{\prime\prime}(x)}{m\gamma^{3}}\right)P\ .

(2.45)

For the harmonic case, we get that

\frac{\partial P}{\partial t}=D\left(1+\frac{k}{m\gamma^{2}}\right)^{2}\frac{\partial^{2}P}{\partial x^{2}}+\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\left[\left(\frac{kx}{m\gamma}+\frac{k^{2}x}{m^{2}\gamma^{3}}\right)P\right]\ ,

(2.46)

For a Fokker-Planck equation of the form (
2.34
), the equilibrium solution is given by

P_{\rm eq}\propto\exp\left(-\frac{V_{\rm eff}}{m\gamma D}\right)\ .

(2.47)

Thus, for the harmonic case, since we have that our effective diffusion in the Fokker-Planck equation is
D^{\prime}=D\left(1+\frac{k}{m\gamma^{2}}\right)^{2}
, we get that
V_{\rm eff}
is given by

V_{\rm eff}=\frac{1}{2}kx^{2}\left(1+\frac{k}{m\gamma^{2}}\right)\left(1-2\frac{k}{m\gamma^{2}}\right)=\frac{1}{2}kx^{2}\left(1-\frac{k}{m\gamma^{2}}\right)\ ,

(2.48)

which matches the harmonic effective potential in (
2.40
). In section
4
, we will use a morally equivalent procedure to derive similar corrections to stochastic inflation.

Comments on the equilibrium distribution

While we have a stationary (time independent) solution, it is crucially different from the Gibbs distribution, which exhibits detailed balance. Detailed balance tells us that there are no probability currents, and that forward and backward transition rates are equally likely

\frac{dP_{i}}{dt}=\sum W(j\to i)P_{j}-\sum W(i\to j)P_{i}=0\qquad W(j\to i)P_{j}=W(i\to j)P_{i}\,\,\forall i,j

(2.49)

This is the statement of microscopic time reversibility. However, for our stationary solution, time reversibility and hence detailed balance is manifestly broken, and we have non-zero currents which tell us the preferential probability flow. Writing

\frac{dP}{dt}=-\nabla\cdot J=-(\partial_{x}J_{x}+\partial_{p}J_{p})\ ,

(2.50)

we get

J_{x}=\frac{p}{m}W-D\frac{\partial W}{\partial x}\qquad J_{p}=-\gamma pW-V^{\prime}(x)W\ .

(2.51)

Evaluating the currents for our equilibrium solution in equation (
2.38
), we get

J_{x}=\left(\gamma x+\frac{\gamma^{2}p}{k}+\frac{p}{m}\right)W_{eq}\qquad J_{p}=-\gamma\left(p+\frac{V^{\prime}(x)}{\gamma}\right)W_{eq}\ .

(2.52)

We can rewrite this, in our small parameter limit
k\ll m\gamma^{2}
, as

J_{x}=\frac{\gamma^{2}}{k}\left(p+\frac{V^{\prime}(x)}{\gamma}\right)W_{eq}\qquad J_{p}=-\gamma\left(p+\frac{V^{\prime}(x)}{\gamma}\right)W_{eq}\ .

(2.53)

We thus see that whenever we are off the momentum saddle point axis
p=-V^{\prime}(x)/\gamma
, we get a non-zero current. However, on integrating out
p
, we constrain ourselves to flow along this axis. Thus, the reduced one dimensional Fokker-Planck equation

\frac{dP}{dt}=D\frac{d^{2}P(x)}{dx^{2}}+\frac{d}{dx}\left(\frac{V^{\prime}(x)}{m\gamma}P(x)\right)

(2.54)

does satisfy detailed balance at equilibrium, as expected. Thus, stochastic inflation represented in the flat FRW slicing has an arrow of time given by non-zero currents when the observer has access to the full phase space distribution.

Exact RG and the Density Matrix

We would now like to take our intuition from quantum walks and apply it to the reduced density matrix of the long wavelength modes in de Sitter. Specifically, we will be constructing a density matrix

\rho_{R}[\varphi_{+}({\vec{x}}),\varphi_{-}({\vec{x}}),t]={\rm Tr}_{\Phi}\rho[\phi_{+},\phi_{-}]\qquad\phi_{\pm}=\varphi_{\pm}+\Phi

(3.1)

where
\varphi({\vec{x}},t)

\Phi({\vec{x}},t)
is the local field composed of the hard-modes with

We have intuition from RG flow and EFT that suggests that the evolution of the reduced density matrix can be understood as the exact RG for the density matrix
\rho[\phi_{+},\phi_{-}]
after integrating our a shell of modes with

where

(see also

3.1
Setup

We will start from the density matrix associated with the UV field
\phi

\rho[\phi_{+},\phi_{-}]

\Lambda_{0}

{\varphi}

\gamma\in(-\infty(1-i\epsilon),t_{0}]\cup[t_{0},-\infty(1+i\epsilon)
with the boundary condition
{\varphi}({\vec{x}},t_{\pm}\to t_{0})={\varphi}_{\pm}({\vec{x}})
where
t_{+}\in(-\infty(1-i\epsilon),t_{0}]
and
t_{-}\in[t_{0},-\infty(1+i\epsilon)]

\displaystyle\rho_{R}[{\varphi}_{+},{\varphi}_{-}]

\displaystyle=\int_{\gamma}D\varphi T\exp\left(iS_{0}+iS_{\rm int}+iS_{\partial}\right)

(3.2)

\displaystyle S_{0}[{\varphi}(\vec{x},t),\Lambda]

\displaystyle\equiv\int_{\gamma}dta^{3}(t)\int\frac{d^{3}k}{(2\pi)^{3}}\frac{1}{2}\nabla_{\mu}{\varphi}({\vec{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{364708}{458751}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle k$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{364708}{458751}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle k$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{417140}{458752}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle k$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{511514}{458751}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle k$}}}}}}},t)K^{-1}\left(\frac{k^{2}}{\Lambda^{2}}\right)\nabla^{\mu}{\varphi}({\vec{x}},t)

where
\Lambda
is the scale where we integrate out the short modes to define an EFT. The UV density matrix,
\rho[\phi_{+},\phi_{-}]
is defined in the same way, but with
\Lambda=\Lambda_{0}
. Our goal is to show that in integrating out a shell
[\Lambda,\Lambda+\Delta\Lambda]
, the form of the density matrix does not change.

We will define
\nabla_{\mu}
is the covariant derivative and
\nabla^{2}=\nabla_{\mu}\nabla^{\mu}
. To simplify notation, when acting on Fourier modes, it is implied that we also Fourier transformed the derivative, so that (for example)

\nabla_{\mu}\nabla^{\mu}f({\vec{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{364708}{458751}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle k$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{364708}{458751}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle k$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{417140}{458752}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle k$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{511514}{458751}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle k$}}}}}}},t)\equiv\left(\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial t^{2}}+3H\frac{\partial}{\partial t}+\frac{k^{2}}{a^{2}}\right)f({\vec{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{364708}{458751}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle k$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{364708}{458751}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle k$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{417140}{458752}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle k$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{511514}{458751}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle k$}}}}}}},t)\ .

(3.3)

The additional terms in the action
S_{I}
and
S_{\partial}
and the bulk interaction and boundary terms respectively. The reduced density matrix will then be traced over all modes with
k>aH
so we will have
\Lambda\geq aH(t)
.

For the density matrix, a boundary term is always required in order to have a well-defined action principle. At the quadratic level, this implies

S_{0,\partial}\supset-\int d^{3}xa^{3}(t)\frac{1}{2}\left({\varphi}_{+}K^{-1}\dot{\varphi}(t_{0,+})-{\varphi}_{-}K^{-1}\dot{\varphi}(t_{0,-})\right)\ ,

(3.4)

where
t_{0,\pm}
means we evaluate the derivative with respect to
t_{\pm}
and then take
t_{\pm}\to t_{0}
.

3.2
Integrating Out

Now we will proceed to integrate out a shell of momentum. Because we are performing the path integral over
t<t^{\prime}
, any change to the density matrix as we change
\Lambda
must arise from fields at time
t
. We will show that all such effects can be captured by the boundary action,
S_{\partial}
so that

\frac{\partial}{\partial s}\rho_{R}[\varphi_{+},\varphi_{-},t]=i\int_{\gamma}D\varphi\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial s}S_{0}+\frac{\partial}{\partial s}S_{I}+\frac{\partial}{\partial s}S_{\partial}\right)\exp\left(iS_{0}+iS_{\rm int}+iS_{\partial}\right)=0\,,

(3.5)

where
s\equiv\log\Lambda
. As in the case of exact RG, we are looking to define the change to the interaction and boundary terms that will make the vanishing of this expression manifest. At this stage, Equation (
3.5
) is a conjecture, as we must demonstrate that any dependence of the density matrix on
\Lambda
can be absorbed into
S_{\partial}
.

The key idea is to use our knowledge of the quadratic (bulk) action to simplify this expression. In order to simplify the notation, we will suppress the integral over Fourier modes and isolate the integration over time. Quantities that are integrated over same time variable we will write in the compact notation

f*g\equiv\int dtf(t)g(t)\qquad f*H*g\equiv\int dt\int dt^{\prime}f(t)H(t,t^{\prime})g(t^{\prime})\ .

(3.6)

We will keep track of time integration in this way in order to isolate the effect of the non-trivial boundary conditions imposed at
t=t_{0,\pm}
.

Quadratic Terms

Now we can use our knowledge of the free theory to write

\frac{\partial}{\partial s}S_{0}=\frac{1}{2}\frac{\delta}{\delta\varphi}S_{0}*G^{\prime}*\frac{\delta}{\delta\varphi}S_{0}+\delta S_{\partial,2}\ ,

(3.7)

where we defined the Green’s function
G(t,t^{\prime})
so that

G^{-1}=K^{-1}\nabla_{\mu}\nabla^{\mu}\qquad G^{-1}G=a^{-3}(t)\delta(t-t^{\prime})\delta({\vec{x}}-{\vec{x}}^{\prime})\qquad G^{\prime}=\frac{\partial}{\partial s}G=\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial s}K\right)G\ .

(3.8)

The boundary term
\delta S_{\partial,2}
is determined using integration by parts. Specifically, we can write

\frac{\partial}{\partial s}S_{0}=a^{3}(t)\frac{K^{\prime}}{K^{2}}{\varphi}\nabla^{2}{\varphi}=(K^{-1}{\varphi}\nabla^{2})*G^{\prime}*(K^{-1}\nabla^{2}{\varphi})\ ,

(3.9)

using the definition of the Green’s function. The correction to the quadratic boundary term comes from integrating Equation (
3.9
) by parts until it takes the form of Equation (
3.7
). Defining
\bar{S}_{0}=S_{0}+S_{0,\partial}
and using

\frac{\delta}{\delta{\varphi}}\bar{S}_{0}=K^{-1}a^{3}(t)\nabla^{2}{\varphi}\ ,

(3.10)

we can see that

\frac{\delta}{\delta{\varphi}}\bar{S}_{0}*G^{\prime}*\frac{\delta}{\delta{\varphi}}\bar{S}_{0}=K^{-1}{\varphi}\nabla^{2}*G^{\prime}*K^{-1}\nabla^{2}{\varphi}-K^{-1}(\nabla_{0}{\varphi}G^{\prime}-{\varphi}\nabla_{0}G^{\prime})|_{t_{0,-}}^{t_{0,+}}*\frac{\delta}{\delta{\varphi}}\bar{S}_{0}\ .

(3.11)

As written yet, this is not a boundary term because it still involves a time integral through the
\delta_{\varphi}\bar{S}_{0}
term. Fortunately we can integrate the second term by parts, and after simplification we find that only a boundary term remains of the form
\delta S_{\partial,0}=\int d^{3}xa^{6}(t_{0})K^{-2}\frac{1}{2}f({\varphi})
where

\displaystyle f

\displaystyle=({\varphi}_{-}-{\varphi}_{+})\left(\nabla_{t}G^{\prime}\left(t_{+}\right)\nabla_{t^{\prime}}{\varphi}\left(t_{-}\right)-\nabla_{t}G^{\prime}\left(t_{-}\right)\nabla_{t^{\prime}}{\varphi}\left(t_{+}\right)-\nabla_{t}\nabla_{t^{\prime}}G^{\prime}({\varphi}_{-}-{\varphi}_{+})\right)

(3.12)

\displaystyle-(\nabla_{t}{\varphi}(t_{-})-\nabla_{t}{\varphi}(t_{+}))\left(G^{\prime}(\nabla_{t}{\varphi}(t_{-})-\nabla_{t}{\varphi}(t_{+}))-\nabla_{t^{\prime}}G^{\prime}\left(t_{+}\right){\varphi}_{-}+\nabla_{t^{\prime}}G^{\prime}\left(t_{-}\right){\varphi}_{+}\right)

In writing this expression, we anticipated that

iG^{\prime}(t,t^{\prime},{\vec{p}})|_{t=t^{\prime}=t_{0}}\equiv\Delta_{0}(p)\qquad i\nabla_{t}\nabla_{t^{\prime}}G(t,t^{\prime},{\vec{p}})_{t=t^{\prime}=t_{0}}\equiv\Delta_{2}(p)

(3.13)

are independent of take
t_{\pm}\to t_{0}
. To determine the remaining terms, we note that
G(t,t^{\prime},{\vec{p}})
is the path ordered Green’s function along
\gamma
,

G^{\prime}(t,t^{\prime},{\vec{p}})=i\langle{\rm T}_{\gamma}{\varphi}({\vec{p}},t_{1}){\varphi}(-{\vec{p}},t_{2})\rangle^{\prime}=i\frac{dK}{ds}\frac{H^{2}}{2p^{3}}(1-ip\tau_{1})(1+ip\tau_{2})e^{ip(\tau_{1}-\tau_{2})}

(3.14)

where
t_{1}
(
t_{2}
) is the early (later) time along the contour
\gamma
and
\tau_{i}
is the conformal times associated with physical time
t_{i}
. As a result, we have, for the massless field

\displaystyle\Delta_{0}(p,t_{0})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle iG^{\prime}=iG^{\prime}(t_{0},t_{0},{\vec{p}})=-\frac{\partial K}{\partial s}\frac{H^{2}}{2p^{3}}(1+\tau_{0}^{2}p^{2})

(3.15)

\displaystyle\Delta_{2}(p,t_{0})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\partial_{t^{\prime}}iG^{\prime}(t_{0},t_{0},{\vec{p}})=-\frac{\partial K}{\partial s}\frac{H^{4}}{2p^{3}}\tau_{0}^{4}p^{4}\ .

(3.16)

The single derivatives depend on the order, and the order of limits that we take as
t,t^{\prime}\to t_{0}
matters, and we find

\displaystyle i\partial_{t}G^{\prime}(t,t_{0},{\vec{p}})|_{t=t_{\pm}}=i\partial_{t}^{\prime}G^{\prime}(t_{0},t^{\prime},{\vec{p}})|_{t^{\prime}=t_{\mp}}=-\frac{\partial K}{\partial s}\frac{H^{3}}{2p^{3}}(\tau_{0}^{2}p^{2})(1\pm i\tau_{0}p)\equiv\Delta_{R}\pm i\Delta_{I}\ .

(3.17)

where

\displaystyle\Delta_{R}

\displaystyle=-K^{\prime}\frac{H^{3}}{2p^{3}}(p^{2}\tau_{0}^{2})\qquad i\Delta_{I}=iK^{-1}K^{\prime}\frac{H^{3}}{2p^{3}}(\tau_{0}^{3}p^{3})=-i\frac{K^{\prime}}{2a^{3}}

(3.18)

The final result is that

\displaystyle if=

\displaystyle-\Delta_{2}({\varphi}_{-}-{\varphi}_{+})^{2}-\Delta_{0}(\nabla_{t}{\varphi}(t_{-})-\nabla_{t}{\varphi}(t_{+}))^{2}

(3.19)

\displaystyle+\Delta_{R}\{({\varphi}_{+}-{\varphi}_{-}),(\nabla_{t}{\varphi}(t_{-})-\nabla_{t}{\varphi}(t_{+}))\}

\displaystyle+i\Delta_{I}\bigg[({\varphi}_{-}-{\varphi}_{+})(\nabla_{t}{\varphi}_{-}+\nabla_{t}{\varphi}_{+})-(\nabla_{t}{\varphi}_{-}-\nabla_{t}{\varphi}_{+})({\varphi}_{+}+{\varphi}_{-})\bigg]\ .

These additional quadratic terms will play a significant role in stochastic inflation.

The corrections to the boundary action will be given two different interpretations but are equivalent. The first, in precise analogy with exact RG, is that we change the quadratic boundary action to absorb this term

\frac{\partial}{\partial s}S_{\partial}\supset-if\ .

(3.20)

This interpretation is most useful when discussing the resummation of logs in the sense of RG flow. Although this is a local boundary term, it is unfortunately not equivalent to unitary evolution. Specifically, we have mixed
{\varphi}_{+}-{\varphi}_{-}
terms that we associate with open quantum systems. The second, and more typical, interpretation of the boundary terms is to treat them terms as a change to the density matrix,

\frac{\partial}{\partial s}\rho_{R}\supset if\rho_{R}\ .

(3.21)

Here
if
is a local super-operator in the
{\varphi}_{\pm}
basis. This interpretation will be most useful for describing the density matrix during stochastic inflation.

Finally, the running of the required quadratic boundary terms of the action will also contribute a correction to the density matrix via,

\frac{\partial}{\partial s}S_{0,\partial}=a^{3}(t)\frac{K^{-2}}{2}\left(\frac{\partial K}{\partial s}\right)({\varphi}_{+}\nabla_{t}{\varphi}_{+}-{\varphi}_{-}\nabla_{t}{\varphi}_{-})\ .

(3.22)

Using the observation that

\Delta_{I}=\frac{H^{3}\tau_{0}^{3}}{2}\frac{\partial K}{\partial s}=-\frac{\partial K}{\partial s}\frac{1}{2a^{3}}\ ,

(3.23)

we can rewrite

i\frac{\partial}{\partial s}S_{0,\partial}=a^{6}(t)\Delta_{I}K^{-2}({\varphi}_{+}\nabla_{t}{\varphi}_{+}-{\varphi}_{-}\nabla_{t}{\varphi}_{-})\ .

(3.24)

Since
[{\varphi},\nabla_{t}{\varphi}]\neq 0
we need to prescribe a time ordering. The correct time ordering is the symmetrized one, which gives

i\frac{\partial}{\partial s}S_{0,\partial}=a^{6}(t)\Delta_{I}\frac{K^{-2}}{2}({\varphi}_{+}\nabla_{t}{\varphi}_{+}+\nabla_{t}{\varphi}_{+}{\varphi}_{+}-{\varphi}_{-}\nabla_{t}{\varphi}_{-}-\nabla_{t}{\varphi}_{-}{\varphi}_{-})\ .

(3.25)

Now we can combine all the terms to find

\displaystyle\bigg[\frac{1}{2}if+iK^{2}\frac{\partial}{\partial s}S_{0,\partial}\bigg]

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\Delta_{2}({\varphi}_{-}-{\varphi}_{+})^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\Delta_{0}(\nabla_{t}{\varphi}(t_{-})-\nabla_{t}{\varphi}(t_{+}))^{2}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\Delta_{R}\{({\varphi}_{+}-{\varphi}_{-}),(\nabla_{t}{\varphi}(t_{-})-\nabla_{t}{\varphi}(t_{+}))\}

\displaystyle-i\Delta_{I}(\nabla_{t}{\varphi}(t_{-})-\nabla_{t}{\varphi}(t_{+}))({\varphi}_{+}+{\varphi}_{-})\ .

Overall, we find that the evolution of the density matrix due to the quadratic terms takes the form

\frac{\partial}{\partial s}\rho_{R}\supset ia^{6}K^{-2}\frac{f}{2}\rho_{R}+\frac{\partial S_{0,\partial}}{\partial s}\ ,

(3.26)

where we have suppressed the implicit momentum integral.

Interaction Terms

After determining the quadratic boundary terms, we then proceed to use the Schwinger-Dyson (SD) equations to eliminate
S_{0}
. Specifically, at the operator level, the SD equations reflect our ability to integrate by parts so that

i\int_{\gamma}D\varphi\left({\cal O}\frac{\delta}{\delta{\varphi}}S_{0}\right)e^{i\left(S_{0}+S_{I}\right)}=-\int_{\gamma}D\varphi\left(i{\cal O}\frac{\delta}{\delta{\varphi}}S_{I}+\frac{\delta}{\delta{\varphi}}{\cal O}\right)e^{i\left(S_{0}+S_{I}\right)}\ ,

(3.27)

where
S_{I}=S_{\rm int}+S_{\partial}
. This equation holds for
\varphi(t\neq t_{0})
because we are integrating over all fields up to the boundary conditions at
t_{0}
. One should worry about the potential contributions at
t=t_{0}
; fortunately these are manifestly pure boundary terms and can be determined directly.

At this stage, there is no practical difference between the dS and the flat space in-in or density matrix calculations
[
40
,
32
]
, other than the precise form of
G
. Therefore, we apply the SD equations repeatedly, to write

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{\delta S_{0}}{\delta\varphi}\star G^{\prime}\star\frac{\delta S_{0}}{\delta\varphi}=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\int_{\gamma}dt\left(\frac{\delta^{2}S_{0}}{\delta\varphi\delta\varphi}G^{\prime}(t,t)\right)+\frac{1}{2}\frac{\delta S_{0}}{\delta\varphi}\star G^{\prime}\star\frac{\delta S_{I}}{\delta\varphi}

(3.28)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int_{\gamma}dt\left(\frac{\delta^{2}S_{I}}{\delta\varphi\delta\varphi}G^{\prime}(t,t)\right)-\frac{1}{2}\frac{\delta S_{I}}{\delta\varphi}\star G^{\prime}\star\frac{\delta S_{I}}{\delta\varphi}

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\int_{\gamma}dt\left(\frac{\delta^{2}S_{0}}{\delta\varphi\delta\varphi}G^{\prime}(t,t)\right)\ .

The term on the last line is a constant and just contributes to the overall normalization of
\rho
(which is fixed, in principle, by the trace condition,
{\rm Tr}\rho=1
). What remains are the two (well-known) contributions to the effective action from integrating out a shell of momentum, namely

\frac{\partial}{\partial s}S_{I}=-\frac{1}{2}\int_{\gamma}dt\left(\frac{\delta^{2}S_{I}}{\delta\varphi\delta\varphi}G^{\prime}(t,t)\right)+\frac{1}{2}\frac{\delta S_{I}}{\delta\varphi}\star G^{\prime}\star\frac{\delta S_{I}}{\delta\varphi}

(3.29)

The first term is purely a local function of
t
because there is a single integral and therefore contributes only to
S_{\rm int}
. The second term includes both local and boundary terms. Specifically, because
G^{\prime}(t,t)
is exponentially suppressed in
t-t^{\prime}
(or, as we will see,
t-t_{0}
or
t^{\prime}-t_{0}
), we can Taylor expand and integrate to generate local operators.

t

However, since the local action can only involve operators in one of the
\gamma_{+}=t_{+}\in(-\infty(1-i\epsilon),t_{0}]
or
\gamma_{-}=t_{-}\in[t_{0},-\infty(1+i\epsilon)]

S_{\rm int,\pm}=\int_{\gamma_{+}}dt_{\pm}{\cal L}\ ,

(3.30)

the correction to the bulk (local) action is

\frac{\partial}{\partial s}S_{\rm int,\pm}=-\frac{1}{2}\int_{\gamma}dt\left(\frac{\delta^{2}S_{\rm int,\pm}}{\delta\varphi\delta\varphi}G^{\prime}(t_{\pm},t_{\pm})\right)+\frac{1}{2}\frac{\delta S_{\rm int,\pm}}{\delta\varphi}\star G_{\pm,\pm}^{\prime}\star\frac{\delta S_{\rm int,\pm}}{\delta\varphi}\ .

(3.31)

Allowing for a boundary action
S_{\partial}({\varphi}_{+},{\varphi}_{-})
, we then find boundary terms

\frac{\partial}{\partial s}S_{\partial,{\rm int}}=\frac{\delta S_{\rm int,+}}{\delta\varphi}\star G_{+,-}^{\prime}\star\frac{\delta S_{\rm int,-}}{\delta\varphi}

(3.32)

It will generally be the case that
G_{+,-}
is exponentially suppressed in
t_{\pm}-t_{0}
and therefore

\frac{\partial}{\partial s}S_{\partial,{\rm int}}=\int d^{3}x\sum_{i,j}{\cal O}_{+,i}{\cal O}_{-,j}\ ,

(3.33)

where
{\cal O}_{\pm}
are local operators of
{\varphi}(t_{\pm})
defined at
t_{\pm}\to t_{0}
respectively that can be derived from the Taylor expansion of Equation (
3.32
) at
t_{+}=t_{-}=t_{0}
. We see this term necessarily mixes
{\varphi}(t_{\pm})
and therefore is not associated with conventional Hamiltonian evolution. However, because this is a pure boundary term, it can be interpreted as a redefinition of the operators at
t_{0}
.

Like the quadratic boundary terms another way to interpret mixed boundary terms is through the evolution of the density matrix. Specifically,
S_{\partial}
acts as a super-operator on density matrix and therefore can be incorporated through the master equation

\frac{\partial}{\partial s}\rho_{R}\supset\sum_{i,j}\int d^{3}x\,{\cal O}_{+,i}\rho_{R}{\cal O}_{-,j}\equiv\$_{\rm int}\rho_{R}\ ,

(3.34)

where
\$_{\rm int}
is the superoperator in the Kraus representation. In this representation, all corrections to the effective action in
S_{\rm int}
can be derived from any observable as they do not depend on
t_{0}
or the fact this is an in-in correlator. All terms specific to the in-in correlator at
t_{0}
contribute to the evolution of the density matrix.

While the presence of the additional boundary terms will be important for cosmological applications, such as stochastic inflation, it is important to note that they are needed in flat space when matching correlators
[
32
]
. The fact that they do not appear in matching S-matrix elements is a consequence of the fact that they can represent field redefinitions between the UV and IR descriptions.

Full Density Matrix RG

To summarize the exact RG calculation, integrating out a shell of momenta introduces a differential change to the reduced density matrix of the long wavelength modes,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\rho_{R}}{\partial s}

\displaystyle=\int\frac{d^{3}p}{(2\pi)^{3}}\,a^{6}(t)iK^{-2}\bigg[\frac{1}{2}f({\varphi})\rho_{R}+\frac{\partial S_{0,\partial}}{\partial s}\bigg]

\displaystyle=\int\frac{d^{3}p}{(2\pi)^{3}}\,a^{6}(t)K^{-2}\,\bigg[-\frac{1}{2}\Delta_{2}({\varphi}_{-}-{\varphi}_{+})^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\Delta_{0}(\nabla_{t}{\varphi}(t_{-})-\nabla_{t}{\varphi}(t_{+}))^{2}

(3.35)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\Delta_{R}\{({\varphi}_{+}-{\varphi}_{-}),(\nabla_{t}{\varphi}(t_{-})-\nabla_{t}{\varphi}(t_{+}))\}-i\Delta_{I}(\nabla_{t}{\varphi}(t_{-})-\nabla_{t}{\varphi}(t_{+}))({\varphi}_{+}+{\varphi}_{-})\bigg]\rho_{R}+\$_{\rm int}\rho_{R}\ ,

where
\$_{\rm int}
are higher order local terms defined in Equation (
3.34
). We can further simplify the quadratic terms in this equation by recognizing that

a^{3}(t)K^{-1}\nabla_{t}{\varphi}(t_{+})\rho_{R}=-i\frac{\delta}{\delta{\varphi}(t_{+})}\rho_{R}\qquad a^{3}(t)K^{-1}\nabla_{t}{\varphi}(t_{-})\rho_{R}=i\frac{\delta}{\delta{\varphi}(t_{-})}\rho_{R}\ ,

(3.36)

leading to

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\rho_{R}}{\partial s}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int\frac{d^{3}p}{(2\pi)^{3}}\,\,\bigg[\Delta_{0}\left(\frac{\delta}{\delta{\varphi}_{+}}+\frac{\delta}{\delta{\varphi}_{-}}\right)^{2}-a^{6}K^{-2}\Delta_{2}({\varphi}_{-}-{\varphi}_{+})^{2}

(3.37)

\displaystyle+i\frac{a^{3}}{K}\Delta_{R}\bigg\{({\varphi}_{+}-{\varphi}_{-}),\left(\frac{\delta}{\delta{\varphi}_{+}}+\frac{\delta}{\delta{\varphi}_{-}}\right)\bigg\}+2\frac{a^{3}}{K}\Delta_{I}\left(\frac{\delta}{\delta{\varphi}_{+}}+\frac{\delta}{\delta{\varphi}_{-}}\right)({\varphi}_{+}+{\varphi}_{-})\bigg]\rho_{R}+\$_{\rm int}\rho_{R}\ .

Here it will be important that this is a functional equation as we have suppressed the momentum,
\varphi=\varphi({\vec{p}})
.

At this point, it is instructive to compare this to the random walks that we have built intuition for in Section
2
. Schematically, the quadratic terms (i.e. dropping
\$_{\rm int}
) in our evolution equation are of the form

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\rho_{R}}{\partial s}

\displaystyle=\Delta_{0}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial x}+\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{\prime}}\right)^{2}\rho_{R}+\Delta_{2}(x-x^{\prime})^{2}\rho-i\Delta_{R}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial x}+\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{\prime}}\right)(x-x^{\prime})\rho_{R}

(3.38)

\displaystyle+\frac{\Delta_{I}}{2}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial x}+\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{\prime}}\right)(x+x^{\prime})\rho_{R}\ .

As we commented on earlier, it is easier to gain intuition for random walks in the Wigner phase space formalism. Transforming the above equation to the Wigner representation, we get

\displaystyle\frac{\partial W}{\partial t}=\Delta_{0}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x^{2}}W+\Delta_{2}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial p^{2}}W+\Delta_{R}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x\partial p}W+\Delta_{I}\frac{\partial}{\partial x}(xW)\ .

(3.39)

Looking at this equation, we can quickly identify what the different
\Delta
represent. The
\Delta_{0},\Delta_{2}
terms represent the diffusion in
x,p
, while the
\Delta_{R}
term tells us about the degree of correlation between the two sources of diffusion. The
\Delta_{I}
term, represents a quantum drift and arises from the fact the conjugate momentum to
\varphi
depends on
K
, and hence
\Lambda
. All in all, we see clearly how on integrating out a shell of momenta, we recreate the key structure of random walks. However, it’s essential for our discussion that Equation (
3.37
) is functional equation and not an ODE (i.e. it is a differential equation for
\varphi({\vec{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{364708}{458751}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle k$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{364708}{458751}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle k$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{417140}{458752}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle k$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{511514}{458751}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle k$}}}}}}})
). There remains much work to translate the RG equation into the partial differential equations that describe stochastic inflation.

3.3
Combining Time Evolution and RG

The actual problem of interest is to integrate in a shell of comoving momentum
p\in[\Lambda,\Lambda+\Delta\Lambda]
and evolve forward in time
a(t)\to a(t+\delta t)\approx a(t)(1+H\delta t)
so that the physical UV cutoff is unchanged

\Lambda_{\rm phys}=\frac{\Lambda}{a(t)}=\frac{\Lambda+\delta\Lambda}{a(t+\delta t)}\to\delta t\approx H^{-1}\frac{\delta\Lambda}{\Lambda}\ .

(3.40)

The nature of this transformation is illustrated in Figure
2
. Under time evolution the reduced density matrix evolves according to

\frac{d}{Hdt}\rho_{R}=\frac{\partial}{H\partial t}\rho_{R}+\frac{\partial}{\partial s}\rho_{R}\ ,

(3.41)

where
s=\log\Lambda
as before.
The first term is just the Hamiltonian evolution. Assuming our scalar field is a canonical field evolving on a potential, the Hamiltonian for the action (
3.2
) with a potential is

\hat{H}=\int\frac{d^{3}p}{(2\pi)^{3}}\bigg[K\left(\frac{p^{2}}{\Lambda^{2}}\right)\bigg[\frac{1}{2a^{3}}\hat{\Pi}^{2}\bigg]+K^{-1}\left(\frac{p^{2}}{\Lambda^{2}}\right)\bigg[\frac{a^{3}}{2}\frac{(\nabla{\varphi})^{2}}{a^{2}}\bigg]+a^{3}V({\varphi})\bigg]+\hat{H}_{\rm EFT}\ ,

(3.42)

where
\hat{H}_{\rm EFT}
includes any higher derivative terms. The Hamiltonian is responsible for time evolution is the usual way,

\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\rho_{R}=-i[\hat{H},\rho_{R}]\ .

(3.43)

Writing this in the
{\varphi}_{\pm}
basis, and dropping
H_{\rm EFT}
for now, we find

\displaystyle\dot{\rho}_{R}=\int\frac{d^{3}p}{(2\pi)^{3}}\bigg[\frac{iK}{2a^{3}}\left(\frac{\delta^{2}}{\delta{\varphi}_{+}^{2}}-\frac{\delta^{2}}{\delta{\varphi}_{-}^{2}}\right)-\frac{ia^{3}}{2K}\left(\frac{(\nabla{\varphi}_{+})^{2}}{a^{2}}-\frac{(\nabla{\varphi}_{-})^{2}}{a^{2}}\right)-ia^{3}(V({\varphi}_{+})-V({\varphi}_{-}))\bigg]\rho_{R}

(3.44)

Now, we can combine one step of RG and one step of time evolution to get one step of dynamical RG.

\boxed{\begin{aligned} \frac{d}{dt}\rho_{R}&=\frac{H}{2}\int\frac{d^{3}p}{(2\pi)^{3}}\,\,\bigg[\Delta_{0}\left(\frac{\delta}{\delta{\varphi}_{+}}+\frac{\delta}{\delta{\varphi}_{-}}\right)^{2}-\frac{a^{6}}{K^{2}}\Delta_{2}({\varphi}_{-}-{\varphi}_{+})^{2}\\
&+i\frac{a^{3}}{K}\Delta_{R}\bigg\{({\varphi}_{+}-{\varphi}_{-}),\left(\frac{\delta}{\delta{\varphi}_{+}}+\frac{\delta}{\delta{\varphi}_{-}}\right)\bigg\}+2\frac{a^{3}}{K}\Delta_{I}\left(\frac{\delta}{\delta{\varphi}_{+}}+\frac{\delta}{\delta{\varphi}_{-}}\right)({\varphi}_{+}+{\varphi}_{-})\bigg]\rho_{R}\\
&+\int\frac{d^{3}p}{(2\pi)^{3}}\bigg[\frac{iK}{2a^{3}}\left(\frac{\delta^{2}}{\delta{\varphi}_{+}^{2}}-\frac{\delta^{2}}{\delta{\varphi}_{-}^{2}}\right)\rho_{R}-\frac{ia^{3}}{2K}\left(\frac{(\nabla{\varphi}_{+})^{2}}{a^{2}}-\frac{(\nabla{\varphi}_{-})^{2}}{a^{2}}\right)\rho_{R}\\
&\qquad\qquad-ia^{3}(V({\varphi}_{+})-V({\varphi}_{-}))\rho_{R}\bigg]-i[H_{\rm EFT},\rho_{R}]+\$_{\rm int}\rho_{R}\ .\end{aligned}}

(3.45)

For completeness, we reintroduced
\hat{H}_{\rm EFT}
arising from the higher derivative contributions to
S_{\rm eff}
, and
\$_{\rm int}
is the interaction super-operator defined in Equation (
3.34
). In practice, these terms will not play a meaningful role in stochastic inflation at leading or subleading order. Therefore, we will drop
\hat{H}_{\rm EFT}
and
\$_{\rm int}
from our analysis. The remaining terms play the dominant role in the exact RG equation for the density matrix that we will analyze in the majority of the paper.

3.4
From Modes to Local Observables

One of the key issues in understanding the evolution of the density matrix is determining the operators that represent the long distance observations. To illustrate this issue and how it relates to the density matrix, let’s first assume that we are in a free theory so that
S_{\rm int}=0
. Due to the boundary terms, the density matrix still evolves according to

\frac{\partial}{\partial s}\rho_{R}\supset\int\frac{d^{3}p}{(2\pi)^{3}}\Delta_{0}(p)\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial{\varphi}_{+}({\vec{p}})}+\frac{\partial}{\partial{\varphi}_{-}({\vec{p}})}\right)\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial{\varphi}_{+}(-{\vec{p}})}+\frac{\partial}{\partial{\varphi}_{-}(-{\vec{p}})}\right)\rho_{R}[{\varphi}_{+},{\varphi}_{-}]\ ,

(3.46)

where
{\varphi}_{+}
and
{\varphi}_{-}
are the complete set of Fourier modes with
p,q<\Lambda(t)
. However, since
\Delta_{0}(p)
only has support over a range of momenta
p\in[\Lambda,\Lambda+\Delta\Lambda]
, the RHS is a diffusion term only for modes near the cutoff. Concretely, dropping the other terms, we would get a diffusion equation of the form

\frac{\partial}{H\partial t}\rho_{R}=\delta(p-\Lambda(t))\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial{\varphi}_{+}({\vec{p}})}+\frac{\partial}{\partial{\varphi}_{-}({\vec{p}})}\right)\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial{\varphi}_{+}(-{\vec{p}})}+\frac{\partial}{\partial{\varphi}_{-}(-{\vec{p}})}\right)\rho_{R}\ .

(3.47)

Assuming the initial density matrix is a product between the UV and the IR modes,
\rho_{0}=\rho_{0}(\varphi_{+},\varphi_{-})\otimes\rho_{0}(\Phi_{+},\Phi_{-})
, then after time
t>t_{0}
, we will have

\rho_{R}(\varphi_{+},\varphi_{-})={Tr}_{\Phi}\rho(\phi_{+}=\varphi_{+}+\Phi,\phi_{-}=\varphi_{-}+\Phi)=\rho_{0}(\varphi_{+},\varphi_{-})\ .

(3.48)

In other words, our diffusion equation does not couple modes and therefore cannot change the state of the long wavelength modes, including generating a mixed state from a pure state. This is true of all of the quadratic boundary terms. Of course, this is a trivial statement in terms of the wavefunction for a free theory,

\Psi[\phi,t]=Z\exp\left(i\int d^{3}k\frac{(aH)^{3}}{2H^{2}}\left(\frac{k^{2}}{(aH)^{2}}+i\frac{k^{3}}{(aH)^{3}}\right)\phi(-{\vec{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{364708}{458751}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle k$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{364708}{458751}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle k$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{417140}{458752}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle k$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{511514}{458751}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle k$}}}}}}})\phi({\vec{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{364708}{458751}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle k$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{364708}{458751}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle k$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{417140}{458752}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle k$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{511514}{458751}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle k$}}}}}}})\right)=\prod_{k}\psi[\phi({\vec{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{364708}{458751}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle k$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{364708}{458751}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle k$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{417140}{458752}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle k$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{511514}{458751}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle k$}}}}}}})]\ .

(3.49)

Here we are making the well-known observation that in a free translationally invariant theory, the Fourier modes are all independent and the wavefunction is a product state over these modes. As a result, tracing over any set of UV Fourier modes cannot create a mixed state for the remaining IR modes.

The description in terms of stochastic inflation and the resulting mixed state for the universe, is therefore not just the result of integrating out short distance physics but also limiting our measurements to a local region. A common approach is trace over the region outside of a sphere
[
58
]
, giving rise to non-trivial density matrix due to entanglement in space.

In contrast, we will focus on local measurements of the long-wavelength field,
\varphi({\vec{x}})
and its conjugate momentum
\pi({\vec{x}})
and trace over information that leaves
\varphi({\vec{x}})
(or
\pi({\vec{x}})
) fixed. Using translation invariance, we can choose this point to be
{\vec{x}}=0
and

\displaystyle\varphi({\vec{x}}=0)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\bar{\varphi}=\int\frac{d^{3}k}{(2\pi)^{3}}\varphi({\vec{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{364708}{458751}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle k$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{364708}{458751}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle k$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{417140}{458752}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle k$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{511514}{458751}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle k$}}}}}}})

(3.50)

\displaystyle\pi({\vec{x}}=0)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\bar{\pi}=\frac{1}{N_{k}}\int\frac{d^{3}k}{(2\pi)^{3}}K\left(\tfrac{k^{2}}{\Lambda^{2}}\right)\pi({\vec{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{364708}{458751}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle k$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{364708}{458751}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle k$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{417140}{458752}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle k$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{511514}{458751}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle k$}}}}}}})\ ,

(3.51)

where

is the number of modes, that we introduced so that

. We note that these are two local operators we can measure with a local detector. We should think of this in exact analogy with our quantum walk in Equation
2.3
; here
\prod_{\vec{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{417140}{458752}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle k$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{417140}{458752}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle k$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{364707}{458752}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle k$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{364707}{458752}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle k$}}}}}}}\psi[\phi({\vec{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{364708}{458751}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle k$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{364708}{458751}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle k$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{417140}{458752}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle k$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{511514}{458751}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle k$}}}}}}})]
is a product state of harmonic oscillators. It is only when we coupled

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}Z[J_{\pm},t]

While this equation still looks like a mess, it is a nice basis independent way of writing down the evolution of observables we measure in stochastic inflation. We will convert this equation to more well-known formulae in the next section.

The description of long modes in cosmology has long been understood to mirror a random walk. In particular, as modes cross the horizon, they become classical in the sense that for a given Fourier mode, the commutator vanishes as

[\dot{\varphi}({\vec{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{364708}{458751}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle k$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{364708}{458751}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle k$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{417140}{458752}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle k$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{511514}{458751}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle k$}}}}}}}),\varphi(\vec{k}^{\prime})]=iH^{2}(aH)^{-3}\delta({\vec{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{364708}{458751}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle k$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{364708}{458751}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle k$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{417140}{458752}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle k$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{511514}{458751}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle k$}}}}}}}+\vec{k}^{\prime})\ .

as a classical statistical variable when

4.1
Leading Order

The general strategy we will use in this section is to write the evolution equation in terms of the Wigner functional. As usual in the case of quantum random walks, the Wigner description most naturally makes contact with classical dynamical systems.

The advantage of defining the generating functional
Z[J]
in Equation (
3.52
) is that it is equivalent to many representations, including the Wigner functional. Specifically, as shown in Appendix
A
, we can write

\displaystyle W[\bar{\varphi},\bar{p}]

\displaystyle=\int d\Delta\varphi e^{-i\bar{p}\Delta\varphi}\int\frac{dk}{(2\pi)}e^{i\frac{k\Delta\varphi}{2}}e^{-ik\bar{\varphi}}Z\bigg[J_{+}=ik,J_{-}=i\Delta\varphi\bigg]\ .

(4.5)

Integration by parts will allow us to convert the evolution equation for
Z[J_{\pm}]
into an evolution equation for the Wigner distribution.

\displaystyle\frac{dZ[J_{\pm}]}{dt}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int\frac{d^{3}p}{(2\pi)^{3}}\bigg[\left(\frac{H^{3}}{2p^{3}}J_{+}^{2}-a^{2}(t_{0})H\frac{p}{2}\frac{(J_{-})^{2}}{N_{k}^{2}}-\frac{aH^{2}}{p}\frac{J_{-}J_{+}}{N_{k}}\right)\frac{\partial K}{\partial s}\bigg]Z[J_{\pm}]

(4.6)

\displaystyle+\frac{N_{k}}{a^{3}}J_{+}\left(-\frac{iJ_{+}}{2}+\frac{\partial}{\partial J_{-}}\right)Z[J_{\pm}]-\frac{J_{-}}{N_{k}}a^{3}\tilde{V}^{\prime}\left(-iJ_{+}\right)Z[J_{\pm}]

\displaystyle+\int\frac{d^{3}p}{(2\pi)^{3}}\left(K\frac{aH^{2}}{2p}\frac{J_{+}J_{-}}{N_{k}}-\frac{a^{2}Hp}{2}K\frac{J_{-}^{2}}{N_{k}^{2}}\right)Z[J_{\pm}]\ .

Since the Wigner distribution is closely related to the Fourier transform of
Z[J_{\pm}]
, the evolution equation from
Z[J_{\pm}]\to W[\bar{\varphi},\bar{p}]
can be determined using

J_{+}=ik\to-\frac{\partial}{\partial\bar{\varphi}}\qquad J_{-}=i\Delta\varphi\to-\frac{\partial}{\partial\bar{p}}\qquad\frac{\partial}{\partial J_{-}}-i\frac{J_{+}}{2}\to\bar{p}\ .

(4.7)

From here, we can immediately recognize some of the familiar from describing a quantum walk (where we are rescaling
\bar{p}\to\bar{p}/N_{k}
)

J_{+}^{2}\to\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\bar{\varphi}^{2}}\qquad J_{-}\tilde{V}^{\prime}(-iJ_{+})\to-\frac{1}{N_{k}}\frac{\partial}{\partial\bar{p}}V^{\prime}(\bar{\varphi})\qquad J_{+}\left(-\frac{iJ_{+}}{2}+\frac{\partial}{\partial J_{-}}\right)\to-N_{k}\frac{\partial}{\partial\bar{\varphi}}\bar{p}\ .

(4.8)

In contrast, the appearance of gradient terms, such as
a^{-2}(\nabla{\varphi})^{2}
, have typically not arisen in studies of stochastic inflation and corrections thereof. Intuitively, although one might imagine that gradients are small corrections, for the modes at the horizon (
p\sim aH
), this is not necessarily the case
2
2

2

Our analysis is most similar to the treatment of
[
10
]
, which similarly found additional gradient terms. However, they imposed the UV cutoff
\Lambda=\epsilon aH
such that these terms were suppressed by powers of
\epsilon
. This is sufficient to postpone the issue, but at the cost eliminating super-horizon modes with
\epsilon aH<p<aH
.
. Instead, as explained using SdSET, we should understand these terms as being purely scheme dependent and removable by a field redefinition. Concretely, in dimensional regularization, power-law divergences vanish, and therefore we would expect these terms to be absent as well.

The resolution here follows the same pattern as irrelevant operators in any RG. In the presence of a hard cutoff, one might generating mixing under RG between irrelevant and marginal operators, but these are removable by a change of basis. For illustration, suppose we have a scalar field theory in flat space with

S=\int d^{4}x\left(\frac{1}{2}\partial_{\mu}\phi\partial^{\mu}\phi-\frac{\lambda_{4}}{4!}\phi^{4}-\frac{\lambda_{6}}{6!}\frac{1}{\Lambda^{2}}\phi^{6}\right)\ .

(4.9)

With a hard cutoff
\epsilon\Lambda
, one a one-loop contribution to
\lambda_{4}

\beta
-function

\frac{\partial}{\partial\log\Lambda}\lambda_{4}\supset\frac{\lambda_{6}}{4\pi^{2}}\epsilon^{2}\ .

(4.10)

Here we have introduced
\epsilon
to show how it depends on the value of the hard cutoff, but ultimately we should take
\epsilon\to 1
as
\Lambda
is defined to be our RG scale. The key to eliminating this term is to realize that
\lambda_{6}
has a classical
\beta
-function so that

\frac{\partial}{\partial\log\Lambda}\lambda_{6}=2\lambda_{6}\to\frac{\partial}{\partial\log\Lambda}(\lambda_{4}-\frac{\lambda_{6}}{8\pi^{2}}\epsilon^{2})\equiv\frac{\partial}{\partial\log\Lambda}\tilde{\lambda}_{4}=0\ .

(4.11)

We see that by redefining the coupling as
\tilde{\lambda}_{4}
, we can eliminate the apparent contribution to RG from
\lambda_{6}
. Of course, when we regulate with dimensional regularization, the power-law divergence vanishes, and there is no need to redefine the coupling.

The evolution of the density matrix under dynamical RG is in precise analogy with the scalar field example. Using
N_{k}\approx(aH)^{3}
so that

4.3
NNLO and Beyond

When calculating the corrections to Stochastic Inflation from QFT, the first non-trivial correction arises at NNLO, which is

An essential difference between the approach in this paper and

Renomalization in dS

Although light fields are where the need for normalization is most acute, our understanding of QFT in dS requires RG for all masses and spins, just like flat space. The required matching between dS and flat space therefore strongly suggests that the RG resummation involves time-dependent logs in dS. on the same side of the Schwinger-Keldysh contour (i.e.
t,t^{\prime}\in(-\infty(1-i\epsilon),t_{0}]
or
t,t\in(-\infty(1+i\epsilon),t_{0}]
)
This ensures we can Taylor expand in
t-t^{\prime}
and integrate each term to find a finite result to all orders. In this sense, as far as the effective action is concerned, there is nothing about integrating out a shell of momentum in dS that is distinct from flat space.

The situation is similar from the contributions to the action from
t\in(-\infty(1-i\epsilon),t_{0}]
and
t^{\prime}\in(-\infty(1+i\epsilon),t_{0}]
(or vise versa). In these cases, the Green’s functions are again exponentially suppressed but now by

G(t=t_{+},t^{\prime}=t_{-})\propto e^{-\epsilon p|t_{+}-t_{0}|-\epsilon p|t_{-}-t_{0}|}\ .

(5.8)

These terms should therefore be expanded around
t_{\pm}=t_{0}
, giving rise to a local expansion in operators at the boundary. These terms are responsible for renormalizing the definition of local operators, or equivalently, the sources
J_{n}
.

The non-trivial changes that arise for in-in correlators in de Sitter space are the boundary terms at
t_{0}
and the fact that
\Lambda(t)
is combined with time-evolution in the full RG transformation. Yet, it is straightforward to understand the impact of these terms. Writing our generating functional as

\frac{d}{dt}Z[\{J_{n}\};t]={\rm Tr}_{\varphi}\left[\exp\left(\sum_{n}\int d^{3}xJ_{n}{\cal O}_{n}({\vec{x}})\right)\,\frac{d}{dt}\rho_{R}(\varphi,\varphi^{\prime})\right]\ .

(5.9)

Of course, this is identical to the derivation of the evolution in Equation (
3.54
), so that schematically we have

\frac{d}{dt}Z[J_{\pm},t]={\rm Tr}\left[\exp\left(\sum_{n}\int d^{3}xJ_{n}{\cal O}_{n}({\vec{x}})\right)\left(-i[H_{\rm eff},\rho]+\sum_{i}L_{i}\rho L^{\dagger}_{i}-\frac{1}{2}\{L_{i}^{\dagger}L_{i},\rho)\}\right)\right]\ .

(5.10)

Using the trace, we can exchange operators acting on
\rho
to acting on the local operator insertions. However, because these act locally on
{\cal O}_{n}({\vec{x}})
we can express time evolution as a local change to the operator (which would be the Heisenberg picture, in the absence of the
L_{i}
). Therefore, we can treat this apparent time evolution as the operator equation for

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}V[\{J_{n}\},t]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle i[H_{\rm eff},V[\{J_{n}\},t]]+\sum_{i}L^{\dagger}_{i}V[\{J_{n}\},t]L_{i}-\frac{1}{2}\{L_{i}^{\dagger}L_{i},V[\{J_{n}\},t]\}

(5.11)

\displaystyle V[\{J_{n}\},t]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\exp\left(\sum_{n}\int d^{3}xJ_{n}{\cal O}_{n}({\vec{x}})\right)\ .

(5.12)

If
{\cal O}_{n}
forms a complete basis of local operators, then the change to
V[\{J_{n}\},t]
is just a redefinition of
J_{n}
. This expression is purely local in time and therefore we can simply redefine
J_{n}\to J_{n}^{\prime}
to capture the time evolution of the system.

One might worry that the fact that
J_{n}({\vec{x}})
is a source that depends directly on position could introduce non-locality in space. This does not occur because the non-trivial evolution of the operators arises from equal time commutators, and therefore are local in space by causality. This is manifest in the UV Hamiltonian description. In terms of the Wilsonian RG, the evolution of the operators arises from contribution that couple the two branches of the Schwinger-Kelgysh contour (
t_{+}
and
t_{-}
) and are not equivalent to a local term in the action because of the path ordering of the exponential. Of course, the time ordering is only relevant for the terms that don’t commute, which are again local in space at equal time. The same features appear in flat space where several examples have been worked out in detail
[
32
]
.

The essential result here again follows from Equation (
3.5
). The sources can be added to the boundary action

S_{\partial,{\rm tot}}=S_{\partial}+\int d^{3}x\sum_{n}J_{n}{\cal O}_{n}({\vec{x}})\ .

(5.13)

In these variables, our partition function is simply

Z[\{J_{n}\};t]=\int_{\gamma}D\varphi T\exp\left(iS_{0}+iS_{\rm int}+iS_{\partial}\right)\ ,

(5.14)

and therefore obeys that same renormalization properties as the effective action. At this stage, the properties of the renormalization of de Sitter correlators and the flat space in-in correlators are identical, up to the definition of Green’s functions and the fact that the de Sitter RG transformation includes a step of time evolution.

It is, perhaps, worth noting that the analogous proof of “renormalizability” in AdS remains incomplete
[
82
]
. In that case, we have the benefit of AdS/CFT which implies that the dual description is a conventional QFT and therefore the bulk of AdS should inherit what is known renormalization. Yet, some of the peculiar aspects of dynamical RG in dS
[
81
]
have nearly identical avatars in AdS
[
83
,
84
]
. A proof in AdS would likely be instructive both for understanding holographic RG in AdS
[
85
]
, and as another connection between physics in AdS and dS.

6
Conclusions

Understanding the dynamics of light scalar fields on a fixed de Sitter background presents a surprising challenge. Perturbative calculations fail due to large time-dependent logs
[
4
,
86
]
. These logs have been understood and resummed using the ideas from classical random walks, but leave the quantum nature of the fields obscure. Moreover, while understanding light scalars should be a stepping stone to quantum cosmology, the stochastic formalism bears little resemblance to the Euclidean path integral or wavefunction of the universe
[
87
]
. Yet, both approaches yield the same equilibrium probability distribution
[
88
]
, which demands a deeper explanation. A comparison of the density matrices of the no-boundary proposal
[

,

with the stochastic formalism offers the opportunity to make a deeper connection between these descriptions.

Understanding the de Sitter entropy
[

]
, and cosmology as an open quantum system
[

motivates the need to describe the evolution of the density matrix from a stochastic perspective. In this paper, we developed the tools of exact RG to provide a non-perturbative evolution equation for the long-wavelength density matrix. While elements of the stochastic description arise from tracing out sub-horizon fluctuations, we also find that the description as a quantum walk only emerges when additionally tracing over information at separated points. This formalism clarifies and generalizes various aspects of stochastic inflation and should serve a powerful tool for exploring non-perturbative aspects of QFT in dS.
