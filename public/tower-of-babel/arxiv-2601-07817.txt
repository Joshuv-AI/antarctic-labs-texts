Introduction

This paper is concerned with positive integer solutions to the equation
u+v=w
in which
u,v
and
w

are square-full. We remind the reader that a positive integer
n
is said to be square-full if
p^{2}|n
for
every prime
p
dividing
n
. One may easily check that such an integer may be written uniquely as

x^{2}y^{3}
where
x
and
y
are positive integers, with
y
being square-free.

Apart from the intrinsic interest of square-full solutions to
u+v=w
, the equation arises in the study of
rational points on Campana orbifolds. For the divisor

\Delta=\tfrac{1}{2}[0]+\tfrac{1}{2}[1]+\tfrac{1}{2}[\infty]

the density of rational points of height at most
B
on the orbifold
(\mathbb{P}^{1},\Delta)
can be understood
via the counting function

n_{\mathrm{prim}}(B)=\#\{(u,v,w)\in\mathbb{N}_{\mathrm{prim}}^{3}:u+v=w;\,u,v,w\mbox{ square-full};\,w\leq B\},

where
\mathbb{N}_{\mathrm{prim}}^{3}
is the set of triples
(u,v,w)\in\mathbb{N}^{3}
with
\gcd(u,v,w)=1
. The question
of the behaviour of
n_{\mathrm{prim}}(B)
seems first to have been raised by Poonen
[
8
]
, who asks
whether
n_{\mathrm{prim}}(B)\sim B^{1/2}
, but the reader should note that Poonen is not using the notation

\sim
in precisely the same sense as is usual in analytic number theory.

The function
n_{\mathrm{prim}}(B)
was investigated further by Browning and Van Valckenborgh
[
2
]
,
who conjectured that
n_{\mathrm{prim}}(B)\sim c_{0}B^{1/2}
for a certain explicit constant
c_{0}>0
. They
viewed the equation
u+v=w
as
x_{1}^{2}y_{1}^{3}+x_{2}^{2}y_{2}^{3}=x_{3}^{2}y_{3}^{3}
, giving

n_{\mathrm{prim}}(B)=\sum_{y_{1},y_{2},y_{3}}\#\{(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}):x_{1}^{2}y_{1}^{3}+x_{2}^{2}y_{2}^{3}=x_{3}^{2}y_{3}^{3}\},

and noted that, for fixed values of
y_{1},y_{2},y_{3}
there are asymptotically
c(y_{1},y_{2},y_{3})B^{1/2}
solutions

(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})
, for appropriate constants
c(y_{1},y_{2},y_{3})
. This produces their conjecture, with

c_{0}=\sum_{y_{1},y_{2},y_{3}}c(y_{1},y_{2},y_{3}).

They showed further that

\liminf_{B\to\infty}B^{-1/2}n_{\mathrm{prim}}(B)\geq c_{0},

so that it is the question of upper bounds for
n_{\mathrm{prim}}(B)
which remains. Here Browning and Van
Valckenborgh showed that

n_{\mathrm{prim}}(B)\ll_{\varepsilon}B^{3/5+\varepsilon}

for any fixed
\varepsilon>0
. They discuss the possibility of improvements, and comment that obtaining an upper bound
of the expected order of magnitude is “much more challenging”. The question was investigated further by
Zhao
[
10
]
, who refined the estimate of Browning and Van Valckenborgh, showing that

n_{\mathrm{prim}}(B)\ll B^{3/5}(\log B)^{9}.

The goal of the present paper is to reduce the exponent
3/5
. It turns out that little extra effort is required to
include solutions in which
\gcd(u,v,w)>1
. Thus we have the following result.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
n(B)
be the number of triples
u,v,w\in\mathbb{N}
of square-full integers, such that
u+v=w
and

w\leq B
. Then

n(B)\ll_{\varepsilon}B^{3/5-3/1555+\varepsilon}

for any fixed
\varepsilon>0
.

The improvement, by
3/1555
, is disappointingly small. However it is hoped that the ideas in this
paper may inspire others to make more substantial progress now that an initial dent in the problem
has been made.

Theorem
1.1
will be an easy consequence of the following result.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
a_{1},a_{2},a_{3}
be non-zero square-free integers (of either sign) which are coprime in
pairs. Let
n(B;a_{1},a_{2},a_{3})
be the number of positive integer solutions

x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},y_{1},y_{2},y_{3}
of the equation

a_{1}x_{1}^{2}y_{1}^{3}+a_{2}x_{2}^{2}y_{2}^{3}+a_{3}x_{3}^{2}y_{3}^{3}=0

(1.1)

with
a_{1}y_{1},a_{2}y_{2}
and
a_{3}y_{3}
square-free, and for which

|a_{1}|x_{1}^{2}y_{1}^{3},|a_{2}|x_{2}^{2}y_{2}^{3},|a_{3}|x_{3}^{2}y_{3}^{3}\leq B

(1.2)

and

\gcd(x_{1}y_{1}\,,\,x_{2}y_{2}\,,\,x_{3}y_{3})=\gcd(a_{1}a_{2}a_{3}\,,\,x_{1}x_{2}x_{3}y_{1}y_{2}y_{3})=1.

(1.3)

Then
n(B;a_{1},a_{2},a_{3})\ll_{\varepsilon}B^{3/5-3/1555+\varepsilon}
, for any fixed
\varepsilon>0
.

To see how Theorem
1.1
follows from
Theorem
1.2
suppose that
u+v=w
with positive square-full integers
u,v,w
,
and let
h=\gcd(u,v,w)
. Then
h
must be square-full, and
uh^{-1}
must factor as
\prod p^{e}

with
e\geq 2
except possibly for primes
p|h
. Since every square-full integer may be
written as a product
x^{2}y^{3}
with
y
square-free, we conclude that
u=ha_{1}x_{1}^{2}y_{1}^{3}
for some
integer
a_{1}|h
coprime to
x_{1}y_{1}
, with
a_{1}y_{1}
square-free. We may argue similarly for
v

and
w
and conclude that

\displaystyle n(B)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}h\leq B\\
h\;\mathrm{square-full}\end{subarray}}\sum_{a_{1},a_{2},a_{3}|h}n(B/h;a_{1},a_{2},a_{3})

\displaystyle\ll_{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}h\leq B\\
h\;\mathrm{square-full}\end{subarray}}\left(\frac{B}{h}\right)^{3/5-3/1555+\varepsilon}\tau(h)^{3}

\displaystyle\ll_{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle B^{3/5-3/1555+\varepsilon},

as required for Theorem
1.1
. Here
a_{1},a_{2},a_{3}
are allowed to take both positive
and negative values, and we use the fact that if
h
runs over square-full
integers then
\sum_{h}h^{-\theta}<\infty
whenever
\theta>\tfrac{1}{2}
.

The main element in our strategy to prove Theorem
1.2
is to consider the bi-homgeneous
congruence

a_{1}x_{1}^{2}y_{1}^{3}+a_{2}x_{2}^{2}y_{2}^{3}\equiv 0\;(\text{mod}\;y_{3}^{3}).

After various manoeuvres we find that
(x_{1},x_{2},y_{1},y_{2})
lies on one of a small number of lines
in
\mathbb{P}^{3}(\mathbb{Q})
or, more precisely, on 2-dimensional lattices
\mathsf{M}\subset\mathbb{Z}^{4}
.
These produce equations of the shape

F(u,v)=-a_{3}y_{3}^{3}\square,

where
F
is a form of degree 5, and these are handled via the “square-sieve”. One needs to know
about the size of
\det(\mathsf{M})
for these lattices, which determines the range for the variables

u
and
v
above. Throughout these steps there are various special cases that arise which require
separate treatment.

One auxiliary result deserves mention here.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
\rho(B;a,b,c)
be the number of integer solutions
x,y,z
to the equation

ax^{3}+by^{3}+cz^{3}=0

(1.4)

with
\gcd(x,y,z)=1
and
\max(|x|,|y|,|z|)\leq B
. Then

\rho(B;a,b,c)\ll_{\varepsilon}B^{\varepsilon}

for any
\varepsilon>0
, uniformly for all non-zero integer
a,b,c
.

It is clear from the proof that one may replace
B^{\varepsilon}
by
B^{\varepsilon(B)}
with some explicit function

\varepsilon(B)
tending to zero, but we shall not use this fact.

For a general cubic form
C(x,y,z)
with at least one non-trivial integral zero the corresponding
counting function
\rho_{C}(B)
will be asymptotically
c_{B}(\log B)^{r/2}
, where
r
is the rank
of the Jacobian of
C
. It then follows that
\rho_{C}(B)\ll_{C,\varepsilon}B^{\varepsilon}
. It was shown by the author

[
6
, Theorem 3]
that one can remove the dependence on
C
under a certain
“Rank Hypothesis”. This states that if
r(E)
and
C_{E}
denote the rank and conductor of an
elliptic curve
E
, then
r(E)=o(\log C_{E})
. What is
special about our particular situation is that we can estimate the rank of the relevant elliptic curves
quite efficiently.

Auxiliary Results

We begin by proving Theorem
1.3
. As mentioned in the introduction, we will use a good bound
for the ranks of certain elliptic curves.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
M\in\mathbb{N}
be cube-free. Then if we write
r(M)
for the rank of the elliptic curve

E_{M}:Y^{2}=X^{3}-3M^{2}

we will have

r(M)\ll\ \omega(M)+1\ll\frac{\log 3M}{\log\log 3M}.

We first show how to deduce Theorem
1.3

2.1
.

Proof of Theorem
1.3
.

Clearly we may suppose that
\gcd(a,b,c)=1

is proportional to the vector product

(x_{1}^{3},y_{1}^{3},z_{1}^{3})\wedge(x_{2}^{3},y_{2}^{3},z_{2}^{3})=(y_{1}^{3}z_{2}^{3}-z_{1}^{3}y_{2}^{3}\,,\,z_{1}^{3}x_{2}^{3}-x_{1}^{3}z_{2}^{3}\,,\,x_{1}^{3}y_{2}^{3}-y_{1}^{3}x_{2}^{3}).

(2.1)

Since
\gcd(a,b,c)=1
we deduce that (
2.1
) is an integer multiple of

, whence

We now follow the argument given by the author
[
6

Suppose that

. Then we may take the Jacobian of the

Since

Our next auxiliary result is an application of the “square-sieve” (see the author
[
4
]
).

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
F(X,Y)\in\mathbb{Z}[X,Y]
be a non-zero form of odd degree
D\geq 3
, having no repeated factor.
Let
U,V\geq 1
and let
\mu_{0}(U,V)
be the number of pairs of integers

x\in[-U,U]
and
y\in[-V,V]
for which
F(x,y)
is a non-zero square. Then

\mu_{0}(U,V)\ll_{D}(UV)^{2/3}\log^{2}(2||F||)+U+V,

where
||F||
is the maximum modulus of the coefficients of
F
.

Proof.

Let
\Delta_{F}\not=0
be the discriminant of
F
, and let
P\geq 2

be a parameter to be decided later. Then if
F(x,y)
is a non-zero square, and
p
runs over primes, we have

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}P<p\leq 2P\\
p\nmid\Delta_{F}\end{subarray}}\left(\frac{F(x,y)}{p}\right)\geq\pi(2P)-\pi(P)-\omega(\Delta_{F}F(x,y)),

so that

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}P<p\leq 2P\\
p\nmid\Delta_{F}\end{subarray}}\left(\frac{F(x,y)}{p}\right)\gg\frac{P}{\log P}

provided that
P\gg_{D}\log(2||F||UV)
, as we henceforth assume. It follows that

\left(\frac{P}{\log P}\right)^{2}\mu_{0}(U,V)\ll\sum_{(x,y)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}}\exp\{-x^{2}/U^{2}-y^{2}/V^{2}\}\left\{\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}P<p\leq 2P\\
p\nmid\Delta_{F}\end{subarray}}\left(\frac{F(x,y)}{p}\right)\right\}^{2}.

If we expand the square on the right we will have to examine

S_{0}(q):=\sum_{(x,y)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}}\exp\{-x^{2}/U^{2}-y^{2}/V^{2}\}\left(\frac{F(x,y)}{q}\right),

with
q=p_{1}p_{2}
. When
p_{1}=p_{2}
this is

, and we conclude that

\mu_{0}(U,V)\ll UV(\log P)P^{-1}+(\log P)^{2}P^{-2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}P<p_{1}\not=p_{2}\leq 2P\\
p_{1},p_{2}\nmid\Delta_{F}\end{subarray}}|S_{0}(p_{1}p_{2})|.

(2.4)

To estimate
S_{0}(q)
we use Poisson summation to show that

\displaystyle S_{0}(q)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{(m,n)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}}\left(\frac{F(m,n)}{q}\right)\sum_{(r,s)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}}\exp\{-(m+qr)^{2}/U^{2}-(n+qs)^{2}/V^{2}\}

(2.5)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\pi UV}{q^{2}}\sum_{(u,v)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}}\exp\{-\pi^{2}u^{2}U^{2}q^{-2}-\pi^{2}v^{2}V^{2}q^{-2}\}\Sigma_{0}(u,v;q),

say, with

\Sigma_{0}(u,v;q)=\sum_{m,n\;(\text{mod}\;q)}\left(\frac{F(m,n)}{q}\right)e_{q}(mu+nv).

The usual multiplicativity argument shows that

\Sigma_{0}(u,v;p_{1}p_{2})=\left(\frac{p_{2}}{p_{1}}\right)^{D}\left(\frac{p_{1}}{p_{2}}\right)^{D}\Sigma_{0}(u,v;p_{1})\Sigma_{0}(u,v;p_{2})

(2.6)

when
p_{1}\not=p_{2}
, so that it is enough to investigate
\Sigma_{0}(u,v;q)
when
q
is prime,
equal to
p
, say.

It looks at first glance as if we will need the theory of two-dimensional exponential sums, but fortunately we can
simplify things by the following argument. If
t
is an integer coprime to
p
then
tm
and
tn
run over
all integers modulo
p
when

and

do. We therefore have

\displaystyle\Sigma_{0}(u,v;p)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{p-1}\sum_{t=1}^{p-1}\;\sum_{m,n\;(\text{mod}\;p)}\left(\frac{F(tm,tn)}{p}\right)e_{p}(t\{mu+nv\})

(2.7)

\displaystyle\hskip-56.9055pt=

\displaystyle\hskip-28.45274pt\frac{1}{p-1}\sum_{m,n\;(\text{mod}\;p)}\left(\frac{F(m,n)}{p}\right)\sum_{t=1}^{p-1}\left(\frac{t}{p}\right)^{D}e_{p}(t\{mu+nv\}).

Since

is odd we get

\Sigma_{0}(u,v;p)=\frac{\tau_{p}}{p-1}\sum_{m,n\;(\text{mod}\;p)}\left(\frac{F(m,n)}{p}\right)\left(\frac{mu+nv}{p}\right),

where
\tau_{p}=\sqrt{p}
or

is the Gauss sum. (Note that this remains true even when

.)
The form

cannot be a constant multiple of a square modulo

and

vanish

, since

, and

. Thus

\sum_{m,n\;(\text{mod}\;p)}\left(\frac{F(m,n)}{p}\right)\left(\frac{mu+nv}{p}\right)\ll_{D}p^{3/2}

for all

. We therefore have

,
whence the product formula (

We may of course reverse the roles of

Finally we observe that

\displaystyle\min\big(U+V\,,\,U^{1/2}V\,,\,V^{1/2}U\big)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\min\big(U\,,\,U^{1/2}V\big)+\min\big(V\,,\,V^{1/2}U\big)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle U^{1/3}\{U^{1/2}V\}^{2/3}+V^{1/3}\{V^{1/2}U\}^{2/3}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2(UV)^{2/3},

and the lemma follows.
∎

The forms

Lemma 2.5

Let

This follows immediately from Lemma

It remains to consider the situation in which

\displaystyle(\beta+\mu)^{2}M_{1}^{4}+g^{2}(\beta^{2}-\mu^{2})M_{1}^{3}M_{2}+g^{4}\mu^{2}M_{1}^{2}M_{2}^{2}

for

∎

Our next result describes solutions of quadratic congruences.

Lemma 2.6
.

Let
Q(u,v)\in\mathbb{Z}[u,v]
be a quadratic form, and let
r\in\mathbb{N}
. Then there are
lattices
\mathsf{\Lambda}_{1},\ldots,\mathsf{\Lambda}_{N}\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{2}
with
N\leq 2^{\Omega(r)}
, such that

Q(u,v)\equiv 0\;(\text{mod}\;r)
if and only if
(u,v)\in\cup_{n=1}^{N}\,\mathsf{\Lambda}_{n}
.

Proof.

We argue by induction on
\Omega(r)
, the case
r=1
being
trivial. When
r=p
is prime
the required lattices are
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
if
Q
vanishes modulo
p
, or

p\mathbb{Z}^{2}
if
Q
is
irreducible modulo
p
, or
\{(u,v):p|L(u,v)\}
and
\{(u,v):p|L^{\prime}(u,v)\}
when

Q
factors as
LL^{\prime}
modulo
p
. This suffices for the case
\Omega(r)=1
.

Now suppose the result holds
for
r
and consider divisibility by
pr
for some prime
p
. Suppose
that the lattices
corresponding to
r
are
<\mathbf{g}_{n},\mathbf{h}_{n}>
for
n\leq N\leq 2^{\Omega(r)}
,
so that
r|Q(u,v)
if and only if
(u,v)=x\mathbf{g}_{n}+y\mathbf{h}_{n}

for some
x,y\in\mathbb{Z}
and
some
n\leq N
. Then
Q(X\mathbf{g}_{n}+Y\mathbf{h}_{n})
must be
identically divisible by
r
,
and so equal to
rQ_{n}(X,Y)
, say. Thus
pr|Q(u,v)
if and only if
there is an
n
such that

(u,v)=x\mathbf{g}_{n}+y\mathbf{h}_{n}
with
p|Q_{n}(x,y)
. As we have
seen, this last condition
is equivalent to at most two lattice constraints, producing at most

2N\leq 2^{\Omega(pr)}

lattices corresponding to the congruence
Q(u,v)\equiv 0\;(\text{mod}\;pr)
. This completes
the induction step, and so suffices for the lemma.
∎

We will also use the following simple result on primitive integer
points in 2-dimensional lattices,
see the author
[
5
, Lemma 2]
, for example.

Lemma 2.7
.

Let
\mathsf{\Lambda}\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{2}
be a 2-dimensional lattice of determinant

\det(\mathsf{\Lambda})
, and let
E
be an
ellipse, centred on the origin, of area
A
. Then

\#\{(x,y)\in\mathsf{\Lambda}\cap A:\,\gcd(x,y)=1\}\ll\frac{A}{\det(\mathsf{\Lambda})}+1.

Our final auxiliary result is a version of a “Siegel’s Lemma”
sometimes thought of
as a “projective slicing” argument.
For a
k
-dimensional lattice
\mathsf{M}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
we write

\widehat{\mathsf{M}}=\{\mathbf{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}:\mathbf{u}^{T}\mathbf{m}\in\mathbb{Z},\;\forall\mathbf{m}\in\mathsf{M}\}.

Lemma 2.8
.

Let
\mathsf{M}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
be a
k
-dimensional lattice, where
k\geq 2
.
Then there is a set

H\subset\mathsf{M}
with
\#H\ll_{k}1+\det(\mathsf{M})^{1/(k-1)}

such that

||\mathbf{m}||_{\infty}\ll_{k}1+\det(\mathsf{M})^{1/(k-1)}

for all
\mathbf{m}\in H
, and so that every

\mathbf{u}\in\widehat{\mathsf{M}}
with
||\mathbf{u}||_{\infty}\leq 1
is
orthogonal to some non-zero element of
H
.

When
\mathsf{M}=\lambda\mathbb{Z}^{k}
for some
\lambda\geq 1
the proof is
straightforward, but for lattices
of general shapes more work is required.

Proof.

We begin by dealing with the easy case in which
\det(\mathsf{M})<k^{-k}
.
By Minkowski’s Theorem there is a shortest non-zero vector

\mathbf{m}_{1}\in\mathsf{M}
with

||\mathsf{m}_{1}\||_{\infty}\leq\det(\mathsf{M})^{1/k}<k^{-1}.

Then if
\mathbf{u}\in\widehat{\mathsf{M}}
with

||\mathbf{u}||_{\infty}\leq 1
we have

\mathsf{u}^{T}\mathsf{m}_{1}\in\mathbb{Z}
and
|\mathsf{u}^{T}\mathsf{m}_{1}|<1
, so that

\mathsf{u}^{T}\mathsf{m}_{1}=0
. We may therefore take

H=\{\mathbf{m}_{1}\}
in this case.

For the rest of the proof we may assume that
\det(\mathsf{M})\geq k^{-k}
.
Let
\mathcal{C}=[-1,1]^{k}
, and write

\mathcal{N}(t)=\#\left(t\mathcal{C}\cap\mathsf{M}\right)
.
We then claim that every

\mathbf{u}\in\widehat{\mathsf{M}}
with
||\mathbf{u}||_{\infty}\leq 1
is
orthogonal to some non-zero element of
H=2t\mathcal{C}\cap\mathsf{M}
, provided that
we have
\mathcal{N}(t)>1+2kt
. To prove this we note that each vector
\mathbf{m}
in

t\mathcal{C}\cap\mathsf{M}
has

\mathbf{u}^{T}\mathbf{m}\in\mathbb{Z}
, and
|\mathbf{u}^{T}\mathbf{m}|\leq kt
,
so that there are at most
1+2kt
possible values for

\mathbf{u}^{T}\mathbf{m}
. Thus if
\mathcal{N}(t)>1+2kt
there will be distinct
vectors
\mathbf{m}_{1}
and
\mathbf{m}_{2}
with

\mathbf{u}^{T}\mathbf{m}_{1}=\mathbf{u}^{T}\mathbf{m}_{2}
. It then follows that

\mathbf{u}^{T}(\mathbf{m}_{1}-\mathbf{m}_{2})=0
, with

0<||\mathbf{m}_{1}-\mathbf{m}_{2}||_{\infty}\leq 2t
. Since

\mathbf{m}_{1}-\mathbf{m}_{2}\in 2t\mathcal{C}\cap\mathsf{M}
the claim follows.

To investigate the size of
\mathcal{N}(t)
we use Lemma 1,
part (iii), of the author’s work
[
7
]
. This shows that there is a basis

\mathbf{b}^{(1)},\ldots,\mathbf{b}^{(k)}
of
\mathsf{M}
such that

c_{1}\prod_{i=1}^{k}(1+t/||\mathbf{b}^{(i)}||_{\infty})\leq\mathcal{N}(t)\leq c_{2}\prod_{i=1}^{k}(1+t/||\mathbf{b}^{(i)}||_{\infty})

(2.10)

and

\prod_{i=1}^{k}||\mathbf{b}^{(i)}||_{\infty}\leq c_{3}\det(\mathsf{M}),

(2.11)

with positive constants
c_{1},c_{2},c_{3}
depending only on
k
.
Since
k\geq 2
we may define
t_{0}
as the least positive number for which

c_{1}\prod_{i=1}^{k}(1+t_{0}/||\mathbf{b}^{(i)}||_{\infty})=1+c_{1}+2kt_{0}.

(2.12)

Then
\mathcal{N}(t_{0})>1+2kt_{0}
by (
2.10
), so that we may take

H=2t_{0}\mathcal{C}\cap\mathsf{M}
in the above. It follows from (
2.11
) and
(
2.12
) that

\frac{t_{0}^{k}}{\det(\mathsf{M})}\ll_{k}\frac{t_{0}^{k}}{\prod_{i=1}^{k}||\mathbf{b}^{(i)}||_{\infty}}\ll_{k}1+t_{0},

whence

t_{0}\ll_{k}\det(\mathsf{M})^{1/k}+\det(\mathsf{M})^{1/(k-1)}\ll_{k}\det(\mathsf{M})^{1/(k-1)},

since we are assuming that
\det(\mathsf{M})\geq k^{-k}
.
Thus to conclude the proof of the lemma it only remains to observe that

\#H=\mathcal{N}(2t_{0})\ll_{k}\prod_{i=1}^{k}(1+2t_{0}/||\mathbf{b}^{(i)}||_{\infty})\ll_{k}1+t_{0}

by (
2.10
) and (
2.12
), and that every
\mathbf{m}\in H
has

||\mathbf{m}||_{\infty}\leq 2t_{0}
.
∎

\mathsf{\Lambda}=\{(u_{1},u_{2})\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}:u_{2}\equiv\lambda u_{1}\;(\text{mod}\;y_{3}^{3})\}.

We proceed to count solutions
(x_{3},y_{1},y_{2})
of (
1.1
) for which
(y_{1},y_{2})
lies on some particular lattice

\mathsf{\Lambda}
. We write
D=
Diag
(Y_{1},Y_{2})
, so that
||D^{-1}(y_{1},y_{2})||_{\infty}\leq 1
. Moreover

D^{-1}(y_{1},y_{2})
lies in the lattice
D^{-1}\mathsf{\Lambda}
, which will have determinant

Y_{1}^{-1}Y_{2}^{-1}y_{3}^{3}
. We now choose a basis
\mathbf{g},\mathbf{h}
for
D^{-1}\mathsf{\Lambda}

with

||\mathbf{g}||_{\infty}=\lambda_{1}\;\;\;\mbox{and}\;\;\;||\mathbf{h}||_{\infty}=\lambda_{2},

where
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}

are the successive minima of
D^{-1}\mathsf{\Lambda}
for the
||\cdot||_{\infty}
norm. Then
any
(v_{1},v_{2})\in D^{-1}\mathsf{\Lambda}
may be written as
u\mathbf{g}+v\mathbf{h}
for integers
u

and
v
satisfying

u\ll\frac{||u\mathbf{g}+v\mathbf{h}||_{\infty}}{\lambda_{1}}\;\;\;\mbox{and}\;\;\;v\ll\frac{||u\mathbf{g}+v\mathbf{h}||_{\infty}}{\lambda_{2}}.

In particular,
D^{-1}(y_{1},y_{2})=u\mathbf{g}+v\mathbf{h}
with
|u|\leq U
and
|v|\leq V
, where
U\ll\lambda_{1}^{-1}

and
V\ll\lambda_{2}^{-1}
, so that

UV\ll(\lambda_{1}\lambda_{2})^{-1}\ll\det(D^{-1}\mathsf{\Lambda})^{-1}=Y_{1}Y_{2}y_{3}^{-3}.

We then have
(y_{1},y_{2})=uD\mathbf{g}+vD\mathbf{h}
, where
D\mathbf{g}
and
D\mathbf{h}
are in

\mathsf{\Lambda}
, and so are integer vectors. We therefore conclude that there are integral linear forms

L_{1}(u,v)
and
L_{2}(u,v)
such that
y_{1}=L_{1}(u,v)
and
y_{2}=L_{2}(u,v)
. Since
\mathbf{g}
and

\mathbf{h}
are linearly independent, so are
L_{1}
and
L_{2}
.

We now define

F(X,Y)=-a_{3}y_{3}\{a_{1}x_{1}^{2}L_{1}(X,Y)^{3}+a_{2}x_{2}^{2}L_{2}(X,Y)^{3}\},

so that
F(u,v)=(a_{3}y_{3}^{2}x_{3})^{2}
is a non-zero square whenever
y_{1}=L_{1}(u,v)
and
y_{2}=L_{2}(u,v)

for a solution of (
1.1
). Moreover,
F
cannot have a repeated factor since
X^{3}+Y^{3}
does not, and

L_{1}
and
L_{2}
are linearly independent. We may therefore apply Lemma
2.4
, which shows that
there are
O((UV)^{2/3}(\log B)^{2})
relevant pairs
y_{1},y_{2}
. Since the number of possible
\mathsf{\Lambda}

is
O_{\varepsilon}(B^{\varepsilon})
we conclude that there are

\ll_{\varepsilon}(UV)^{2/3}(\log B)^{2}B^{\varepsilon}\ll_{\varepsilon}(Y_{1}Y_{2})^{2/3}Y_{3}^{-2}B^{2\varepsilon}

triples
(x_{3},y_{1},y_{2})
, provided that
U,V\geq 1
. However if
U<1
, say, then
u=0
and so
y_{1}
and

y_{2}
cannot be coprime unless
v=\pm 1
. Thus there is at most one suitable set of positive integer
values
y_{1},y_{2},x_{3}
in this case, and similarly if
V<1
. The lemma then follows on re-defining
\varepsilon
.
∎

Lemmas
3.1
and
3.2
have the following corollary.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
i,j,k
be a permutation of the indices
1,2,3
, and let
\varepsilon>0
be given. Then

N_{0}(B)\ll_{\varepsilon}\{X_{i}X_{j}Y_{k}+B^{6/11}\}B^{\varepsilon}.

(3.6)

Proof.

It follows immediately from Lemma
3.2
that

N_{0}(B)\ll_{\varepsilon}\{X_{i}X_{j}Y_{k}+X_{i}X_{j}(Y_{i}Y_{j})^{2/3}Y_{k}^{-1}\}B^{\varepsilon}.

Thus either
N_{0}(B)\ll_{\varepsilon}X_{i}X_{j}Y_{k}B^{\varepsilon}
or
N_{0}(B)\ll_{\varepsilon}X_{i}X_{j}(Y_{i}Y_{j})^{2/3}Y_{k}^{-1}B^{\varepsilon}
.
In the latter case we see from Lemma
3.1
and (
3.2
) that either
N_{0}(B)\ll_{\varepsilon}B^{1/2+\varepsilon}
or

\displaystyle N_{0}(B)

\displaystyle\ll_{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle\min\{Y_{1}Y_{2}Y_{3}\,,\,X_{i}X_{j}(Y_{i}Y_{j})^{2/3}Y_{k}^{-1}\}B^{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\{Y_{1}Y_{2}Y_{3}\}^{5/11}\{X_{i}X_{j}(Y_{i}Y_{j})^{2/3}Y_{k}^{-1}\}^{6/11}B^{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(X_{i}^{2}Y_{i}^{3})^{3/11}(X_{j}^{2}Y_{j}^{3})^{3/11}Y_{k}^{-1/11}B^{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle\ll

\displaystyle B^{6/11+\varepsilon},

which suffices for the lemma.
∎

Finally we show that the only case preventing one getting an exponent less than
3/5
in Theorem

1.1
is that in which all the variables are around
B^{1/5}
.

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
\delta>0
. Then we have

N_{0}(B)\ll_{\varepsilon}B^{6/11+\varepsilon}+B^{3/5-\delta+\varepsilon}

for any fixed
\varepsilon>0
, except possibly when

X^{1/5-3\delta/2}\leq X_{k}\leq X^{1/5+3\delta/2}\;\;\;\mbox{and}\;\;\;X^{1/5-\delta}\leq Y_{k}\leq X^{1/5+\delta},\;\;(k=1,2,3).

Proof.

From Lemmas
3.1
and
3.3
we see that either
N_{0}(B)\ll_{\varepsilon}B^{6/11+\varepsilon}
or

\displaystyle N_{0}(B)

\displaystyle\ll_{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle\min\big(Y_{1}Y_{2}Y_{3}\,,\,X_{i}X_{j}Y_{k}\big)B^{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle(Y_{1}Y_{2}Y_{3})^{3/5}(X_{i}X_{j}Y_{k})^{2/5}B^{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle Y_{k}(X_{i}^{2}Y_{i}^{3})^{1/5}(X_{j}^{2}Y_{j}^{3})^{1/5}B^{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle\ll

\displaystyle Y_{k}B^{2/5+\varepsilon}

for any ordering
i,j,k
of the indices
1,2,3
, by (
3.2
). This proves the lemma when

Y_{k}\leq B^{1/5-\delta}
; and if
X_{k}\geq B^{1/5+3\delta/2}

we find from (
3.2
) that
Y_{k}\ll B^{1/5-\delta}
, so that the lemma follows in this case too.

Similarly, from Lemmas
3.1
and
3.3
we see that either
N_{0}(B)\ll_{\varepsilon}B^{6/11+\varepsilon}
or

\displaystyle N_{0}(B)

\displaystyle\ll_{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle\min\big(Y_{1}Y_{2}Y_{3}\,,\,X_{i}X_{j}Y_{k}\,,\,X_{i}X_{k}Y_{j}\big)B^{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle(Y_{1}Y_{2}Y_{3})^{1/5}(X_{i}X_{j}Y_{k})^{2/5}(X_{i}X_{k}Y_{j})^{2/5}B^{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle X_{i}^{2/3}(X_{i}^{2}Y_{i}^{3})^{1/15}(X_{j}^{2}Y_{j}^{3})^{1/5}(X_{k}^{2}Y_{k}^{3})^{1/5}B^{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle\ll

\displaystyle X_{j}^{2/3}B^{7/15+\varepsilon}.

This is sufficient for the lemma when
X_{j}\leq B^{1/5-3\delta/2}
; and if
Y_{j}\geq B^{1/5+\delta}

we find from (
3.2
) that
X_{j}\ll B^{1/5-3\delta/2}
, whence the lemma follows in this case too.
∎

In light of Lemma
3.4
it will suffice for Theorem
1.2
to handle the case in which

\begin{array}[]{c}X^{1/5-1/60}\leq X_{k}\leq X^{1/5+1/60},\\
\rule{0.0pt}{14.22636pt}X^{1/5-1/90}\leq Y_{k}\leq X^{1/5+1/90},\end{array}\;\;\;(k=1,2,3),

(3.7)

and we therefore assume these bounds for the rest of the paper.

4
Covering by Lines

We can now embark on the proof of Theorem
1.2
. In following the argument the reader may
find it helpful to think in particular about the situation in
which
|a_{1}|=|a_{2}|=|a_{3}|=1
and

B^{1/5}\ll X_{1}=X_{2}=X_{3}=Y_{1}=Y_{2}=Y_{3}\ll B^{1/5}.

Given Lemma
3.4
we shall refer to this as the “Critical Case”.

The key idea is to consider
the equation (
1.1
) as a congruence to modulus
y_{3}^{3}
.
Thus
y_{3}
will play a special role, and we emphasize this by using
the notation
y_{3}=q
, which is more
suggestive of the modulus of a congruence. We proceed to classify our solutions
further, according to the value of
q=y_{3}
. Moreover, since

x_{1},x_{2},y_{1}
and
y_{2}
are all coprime to
q
, we can
classify the solutions into smaller classes still, according to the value of

\kappa\;(\text{mod}\;q)
for which

x_{1}y_{1}+\kappa x_{2}y_{2}\equiv 0\;(\text{mod}\;q).

(4.1)

Throughout the rest of the paper, whenever we use the notations
q

and
\kappa
we shall tacitly
assume that
\tfrac{1}{2}Y_{3}<q\leq Y_{3}
with
q
square-free and coprime
to
a_{1}a_{2}
,
and that
\kappa
is taken modulo
q
, with
\gcd(q,\kappa)=1
.
We will write

S(q,\kappa)=S(q,\kappa;X_{1},X_{2},X_{3},Y_{1},Y_{2})

for the set of integral 4-tuples

(x_{1},x_{2},y_{1},y_{2})
satisfying (
4.1
) for given values of
q

and
\kappa
, such that

(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},y_{1},y_{2},q)
is in
S_{0}(B)
for some choice of
x_{3}
. As
a preliminary
observation we note that, since
q|a_{1}x_{1}^{2}y_{1}^{3}+a_{2}x_{2}^{2}y_{2}^{3}
and

a_{1},a_{2},x_{1},x_{2},y_{1}
and
y_{2}
are all coprime to
q

(
4.1
) implies that

a_{2}x_{1}\equiv\kappa^{3}a_{1}x_{2}\;(\text{mod}\;q)\;\;\;\mbox{and}\;\;\;\kappa^{2}a_{1}y_{1}+a_{2}y_{2}\equiv 0\;(\text{mod}\;q).

(4.2)

We begin the main argument with the following lemma.

Lemma 4.1
.

For every
(x_{1},x_{2},y_{1},y_{2})\in S(q,\kappa)
we have

\kappa^{2}a_{1}x_{2}y_{1}+a_{2}x_{2}y_{2}\equiv 0\;(\text{mod}\;q),

(4.3)

2a_{2}x_{1}y_{1}+\kappa^{3}a_{1}x_{2}y_{1}+3\kappa a_{2}x_{2}y_{2}\equiv 0\;(\text{mod}\;q^{2}),

(4.4)

and

5\kappa^{2}a_{1}a_{2}x_{1}y_{1}+a_{2}^{2}x_{1}y_{2}+\kappa^{5}a_{1}^{2}x_{2}y_{1}+5\kappa^{3}a_{1}a_{2}x_{2}y_{2}\equiv 0\;(\text{mod}\;q^{3}).

(4.5)

On the other hand, if
(x_{1},x_{2},y_{1},y_{2})
satisfies the congruences
(
4.2
), (
4.3
), (
4.4
) and (
4.5
), and

\gcd(y_{1},y_{2},q)=r
, then

18(a_{1}x_{1}^{2}y_{1}^{3}+a_{2}x_{2}^{2}y_{2}^{3})\equiv 0\;(\text{mod}\;q^{3}r^{2}).

(4.6)

Remark
.

Although
\kappa
was only defined modulo
q
, the
congruences (
4.4
) and (
4.5
)
none the less hold modulo
q^{2}
and
q^{3}
respectively, irrespective
of the choice of
\kappa
.

Proof.

We obtain (
4.3
) trivially by multiplying the second of the
congruences (
4.2
) by
x_{2}
.
For the other claims we begin by writing

u_{1}=a_{2}x_{1},\;u_{2}=\kappa^{3}a_{1}x_{2},\;v_{1}=\kappa^{2}a_{1}y_{1},\;v_{2}=a_{2}y_{2}

(4.7)

so that (
4.2
) produces
u_{1}\equiv u_{2}\;(\text{mod}\;q)
and

v_{1}\equiv-v_{2}\;(\text{mod}\;q)
.
Moreover

u_{1}^{2}v_{1}^{3}+u_{2}^{2}v_{2}^{3}=\kappa^{6}a_{1}^{2}a_{2}^{2}\left(a_{1}x_{1}^{2}y_{1}^{3}+a_{2}x_{2}^{2}y_{2}^{3}\right)\equiv 0\;(\text{mod}\;q^{3}).

(4.8)

We may now choose
\alpha
and
\beta
, both divisible by
q
, such
that

u_{1}\equiv u_{2}(1+\alpha)\;(\text{mod}\;q^{3})\;\;\;\mbox{and}\;\;\;v_{2}\equiv-v_{1}(1+\beta)\;(\text{mod}\;q^{3}).

The congruence (
4.8
) implies that

(1+\alpha)^{2}\equiv(1+\beta)^{3}\;(\text{mod}\;q^{3})
, so that

2\alpha+\alpha^{2}\equiv 3\beta+3\beta^{2}+\beta^{3}\equiv 3\beta+3\beta^{2}\;(\text{mod}\;q^{3}).

(4.9)

In particular we have

2\alpha\equiv 3\beta\;(\text{mod}\;q^{2}),

(4.10)

whence

2\alpha^{2}\equiv 3\alpha\beta\;(\text{mod}\;q^{3})
and

3\beta^{2}\equiv 2\alpha\beta\;(\text{mod}\;q^{3})
. It then follows from
(
4.9
) that

4\alpha+3\alpha\beta\equiv 4\alpha+2\alpha^{2}\equiv 6\beta+6\beta^{2}\equiv 6\beta+4\alpha\beta\;(\text{mod}\;q^{3}),

and finally that
4\alpha\equiv 6\beta+\alpha\beta\;(\text{mod}\;q^{3})
. We
multiply by
u_{2}v_{1}
, noting that
u_{2}\alpha\equiv u_{1}-u_{2}\;(\text{mod}\;q^{3})

and
v_{1}\beta\equiv-v_{1}-v_{2}\;(\text{mod}\;q^{3})
. This produces the congruence

4v_{1}(u_{1}-u_{2})\equiv 6u_{2}(-v_{1}-v_{2})+(u_{1}-u_{2})(-v_{1}-v_{2})\;(\text{mod}\;q^{3})
,
which simplifies to
5u_{1}v_{1}+u_{1}v_{2}+u_{2}v_{1}+5u_{2}v_{2}\equiv 0\;(\text{mod}\;q^{3})
.
Referring to (
4.7
) we now obtain (
4.5
).

Similarly, the congruence (
4.10
) yields

2v_{1}(u_{1}-u_{2})\equiv 3u_{2}(-v_{1}-v_{2})\;(\text{mod}\;q^{2})
, which simplifies to

2u_{1}v_{1}+u_{2}v_{1}+3u_{2}v_{2}\equiv 0\;(\text{mod}\;q^{2})
, giving us (
4.4
).

To handle the remaining claim in the lemma we retain the notation (
4.7
),
whence
r|v_{1}
in particular. Moreover, the relations (
4.2
),
(
4.4
) and (
4.5
) become

u_{1}\equiv u_{2}\;(\text{mod}\;q),\;\;\;v_{1}+v_{2}\equiv 0\;(\text{mod}\;q),

2u_{1}v_{1}+u_{2}v_{1}+3u_{2}v_{2}\equiv 0\;(\text{mod}\;q^{2}),

(4.11)

and

5u_{1}v_{1}+u_{1}v_{2}+u_{2}v_{1}+5u_{2}v_{2}\equiv 0\;(\text{mod}\;q^{3}),

(4.12)

while the claim that
q^{3}r^{2}|18(a_{1}x_{1}^{2}y_{1}^{3}+a_{2}x_{2}^{2}y_{2}^{3})
is equivalent to
the statement that
q^{3}r^{2}|18(u_{1}^{2}v_{1}^{3}+u_{2}^{2}v_{2}^{3})
.

Since
q|u_{1}-u_{2}
we may write

u_{1}=u_{2}+qu
for some integer
u
, and similarly we may take

v_{2}=qv-v_{1}
. Then (
4.11
)
becomes

A:=2uv_{1}+3vu_{2}\equiv 0\;(\text{mod}\;q),

(4.13)

while (
4.12
) reduces to

B:=4uv_{1}+6vu_{2}+quv\equiv 0\;(\text{mod}\;q^{2}).

(4.14)

Then, if

C:=18(u_{1}^{2}v_{1}^{3}+u_{2}^{2}v_{2}^{3})=18\{(u_{2}+qu)^{2}v_{1}^{3}+u_{2}^{2}(qv-v_{1})^{3}\},

we may calculate that

C=9qu_{2}v_{1}^{2}B+8q^{3}u^{2}vv_{1}^{2}-8q^{3}uvv_{1}A+21q^{2}uv_{1}^{2}A+2q^{3}vA^{2}-6q^{2}v_{1}A^{2}.

It then follows that
q^{3}r^{2}|C
, since
q|A
,
q^{2}|B
, and
r|v_{1}
.
∎

The rest of this section is devoted to the proof of the following lemma.

Lemma 4.2
.

There is a set
S_{0}(q,\kappa)\subseteq S(q,\kappa)
and a collection

\mathcal{L}(q,\kappa)
of
two-dimensional lattices
\mathsf{M}\subset\mathbb{Z}^{4}

with

\#S_{0}(q,\kappa)\ll_{\varepsilon}(Y_{1}Y_{2})^{-1/3}Y_{3}^{-2}B^{2/3+\varepsilon}

(4.15)

and

\#\mathcal{L}(q,\kappa)\ll_{\varepsilon}(Y_{1}Y_{2})^{-1/3}Y_{3}^{-2}B^{2/3+\varepsilon}

, such that

4.2
), (
4.3
),
(
4.4
), and (
4.5
) all hold for

\mathsf{M}

(x_{1},x_{2},y_{1},y_{2})\in\mathsf{M}

(x_{1},x_{2},y_{1},y_{2})

\mathsf{M}

(x_{1},x_{2})\not=(0,0)

(y_{1},y_{2})\not=(0,0)

Remark
.

In the Critical Case we will have
O(B^{2/5})
pairs
(q,\kappa)
and

\#S_{0}(q,\kappa)\ll_{\varepsilon}B^{2/15+\varepsilon},

.

Proof.

Let
\mathsf{\Lambda}\subseteq q^{-3}\mathbb{Z}^{4}
be the lattice generated by

where

denotes the inverse of
a_{2}
modulo
q^{3}
. Then

\det(\mathsf{\Lambda})=2q^{-6}
. Moreover if
\mathbf{g}
is any of the above
four generators the inner product
\mathbf{w}^{T}\mathbf{g}
will be an
integer for all
\mathbf{w}=(x_{1}y_{1},x_{1}y_{2},x_{2}y_{1},x_{2}y_{2})
with

(x_{1},x_{2},y_{1},y_{2})\in S(q,\kappa)
.
It follows that
\mathbf{w}^{T}\mathbf{g}\in\mathbb{Z}
for every

\mathbf{g}\in\mathsf{\Lambda}
, so that
\mathbf{w}\in\widehat{\mathsf{\Lambda}}
, in the
notation of Lemma
2.8
.
If

E=\mathrm{Diag}\left(X_{1}Y_{1},X_{1}Y_{2},X_{2}Y_{1},X_{2}Y_{2}\right)

we see that

E^{-1}\mathbf{w}=\left(\frac{x_{1}y_{1}}{X_{1}Y_{1}}\,,\,\frac{x_{1}y_{2}}{X_{1}Y_{2}}\,,\,\frac{x_{2}y_{1}}{X_{2}Y_{1}}\,,\,\frac{x_{2}y_{2}}{X_{2}Y_{2}}\right),

whence
||E^{-1}\mathbf{w}||_{\infty}\leq 1
. Moreover we will have

E^{-1}\mathbf{w}\in\widehat{E\mathsf{\Lambda}}
. Since

\det(E\mathsf{\Lambda})=2(X_{1}X_{2}Y_{1}Y_{2})^{2}q^{-6}
we may
now conclude from Lemma
2.8
that there is some set

H\subseteq E\mathsf{\Lambda}
with

\#H\ll 1+(X_{1}X_{2}Y_{1}Y_{2})^{2/3}q^{-2}\ll 1+(X_{1}X_{2}Y_{1}Y_{2})^{2/3}Y_{3}^{-2}

such that

(E^{-1}\mathbf{w})^{T}\mathbf{h}=0
for a suitable non-zero

\mathbf{h}\in H
. According to (
3.2
) we have

X_{1}X_{2}\ll(Y_{1}Y_{2})^{-3/2}B,

(4.16)

whence

\#H\ll 1+(Y_{1}Y_{2})^{-1/3}Y_{3}^{-2}B^{2/3}\ll(Y_{1}Y_{2})^{-1/3}Y_{3}^{-2}B^{2/3},

by (
3.7
). Since
(E^{-1}\mathbf{w})^{T}\mathbf{h}=0

\mathbf{w}^{T}\mathbf{g}=0

with

\mathbf{g}=E^{-1}\mathbf{h}\in E^{-1}H\subseteq\mathsf{\Lambda}\subseteq q^{-3}\mathbb{Z}^{4}.

E^{-1}H
has cardinality
O((Y_{1}Y_{2})^{-1/3}Y_{3}^{-2}B^{2/3})

conclude that there is a set of
O((Y_{1}Y_{2})^{-1/3}Y_{3}^{-2}B^{2/3})
non-zero
integer vectors
\mathbf{t}\in\mathbb{Z}^{4}
such that every quadruple

(x_{1},x_{2},y_{1},y_{2})

in
S(q,\kappa)
satisfies the equation

t_{1}x_{1}y_{1}+t_{2}x_{1}y_{2}+t_{3}x_{2}y_{1}+t_{4}x_{2}y_{2}=0

(4.17)

for some
\mathbf{t}
. According to Lemma
2.8
, we have

||\mathbf{h}||_{\infty}\ll(X_{1}X_{2}Y_{1}Y_{2})^{2/3}q^{-2}
for every
element of
H
, whence
also
||\mathbf{g}||_{\infty}\ll(X_{1}X_{2}Y_{1}Y_{2})^{2/3}q^{-2}
, since

X_{1},X_{2},Y_{1},Y_{2}
are
all at least 1. Hence
||\mathbf{t}||_{\infty}\ll(X_{1}X_{2}Y_{1}Y_{2})^{2/3}q\ll B^{4}
, say.

We have therefore to investigate solutions to the equation
(
4.17
). We begin by disposing

t_{1}t_{4}=t_{2}t_{3}
, which will produce the set

S_{0}(q,\kappa)
.
Suppose for example that

. Then

0=t_{1}(t_{1}x_{1}y_{1}+t_{2}x_{1}y_{2}+t_{3}x_{2}y_{1}+t_{4}x_{2}y_{2})=(t_{1}x_{1}+t_{3}x_{2})(t_{1}y_{1}+t_{2}y_{2}).

t_{1}x_{1}=-t_{3}x_{2}

t_{1}y_{1}=-t_{2}y_{2}
. Since we are assuming that

\gcd(x_{1},x_{2})=\gcd(y_{1},y_{2})=1
it follows that
\mathbf{t}

determines either the pair
(x_{1},x_{2})
or the pair

(y_{1},y_{2})
. When
t_{1}t_{4}=t_{2}t_{3}
but
t_{1}=0
, there will be some other

t_{i}
which is
non-vanishing, and a similar argument applies. When it is
(x_{1},x_{2})

that are determined by

q,\kappa
and
\mathbf{t}
we have

(y_{1}\overline{y_{2}})^{3}\equiv-\overline{a_{1}}a_{2}\overline{x_{1}}^{2}x_{2}^{2}\;(\text{mod}\;q^{3}),

where, as before,
\overline{n}\in[1,q^{3}]
denotes the inverse of
n

modulo
q^{3}
. Thus
we have
y_{1}\equiv\lambda y_{2}\;(\text{mod}\;q^{3})
where
\lambda
is one of the

O(3^{\omega(q)})=O_{\varepsilon}(B^{\varepsilon})
cube roots of

-\overline{a_{1}}a_{2}\overline{x_{1}}^{2}x_{2}^{2}

modulo
q^{3}
.
The constraint
y_{1}\equiv\lambda y_{2}\;(\text{mod}\;q^{3})
produces a two
dimensional lattice of
determinant
q^{3}
, so that there are
O(1+Y_{1}Y_{2}q^{-3})
coprime
solutions
(y_{1},y_{2})
in
the rectangle
0<y_{1}\leq Y_{1}
,
0<y_{2}\leq Y_{2}
, by Lemma
2.7
.
Since
\tfrac{1}{2}Y_{3}<q=y_{3}\leq Y_{3}
we see from (
3.7
) that there
are
O(1)
solutions
(y_{1},y_{2})
for each
\lambda
.
This therefore produces a contribution

\ll_{\varepsilon}(Y_{1}Y_{2})^{-1/3}Y_{3}^{-2}B^{2/3+\varepsilon}

to
S_{0}(q,\kappa)
from the case in which
t_{1}t_{4}=t_{2}t_{3}

and it is
(x_{1},x_{2})
that are determined by (
4.17
). There is a
similar argument when it
is the values of
y_{1},y_{2}
which are determined, giving an overall estimate

\ll_{\varepsilon}(Y_{1}Y_{2})^{-1/3}Y_{3}^{-2}B^{2/3+\varepsilon}

for
\#S_{0}(q,\kappa)
, corresponding to the case in which
t_{1}t_{4}=t_{2}t_{3}
.

Turning now to the case in which
\Delta:=t_{1}t_{4}-t_{2}t_{3}\not=0
we see
from (
4.17
) that

y_{1}(t_{1}x_{1}+t_{3}x_{2})=-y_{2}(t_{2}x_{1}+t_{4}x_{2}).

Since
y_{1}
and
y_{2}
are coprime we deduce that

hy_{1}=-t_{2}x_{1}-t_{4}x_{2}\;\;\;\mbox{and}\;\;\;hy_{2}=t_{1}x_{1}+t_{3}x_{2},

(4.18)

for some
h\in\mathbb{N}
. Then
h|\Delta x_{1}
and

h|\Delta x_{2}
, whence
h|\Delta
, since
x_{1}
and
x_{2}
are coprime.
Thus, given
\mathbf{t}
with
\Delta\not=0
, there are

O_{\varepsilon}(B^{\varepsilon})
possible values for

h
, each producing a line in
\mathbb{P}^{3}
, given by
(
4.18
). The corresponding integral points

(x_{1},x_{2},y_{1},y_{2})
then lie on a two-dimensional lattice,

<\mathbf{g},\mathbf{h}>
say, in
\mathbb{Z}^{4}
.

We proceed to show that the conditions (
4.2
), (
4.3
),
(
4.4
), and (
4.5
) restrict us to a
small number of sublattices of
<\mathbf{g},\mathbf{h}>
, which we
produce by a process of
successive refinements. It may happen that all the points

u\mathbf{g}+v\mathbf{h}
already satisfy
(
4.2
), but if not this condition will restrict
(u,v)
to lie on
a sublattice of
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
, so that

(x_{1},x_{2},y_{1},y_{2})
will run over a sublattice,

<\mathbf{g}^{\prime},\mathbf{h}^{\prime}>
say, of

<\mathbf{g},\mathbf{h}>
. The condition (
4.3
) is a trivial
consequence of (
4.2
). For (
4.4
)
we write

(x_{1},x_{2},y_{1},y_{2})=u\mathbf{g}^{\prime}+v\mathbf{h}^{\prime}=\left(x_{1}(u,v),x_{2}(u,v),y_{1}(u,v),y_{2}(u,v)\right),

say, so that (
4.4
) corresponds to a quadratic congruence

Q(u,v)\equiv 0\;(\text{mod}\;q^{2})
.
We now apply Lemma
2.6
with
r=q^{2}
. Since
q
is square-free,
each line (
4.18
) produces at most

2^{2\omega(q)}
two-dimensional lattices containing the relevant

(u,v)
, and on which (
4.2
),
(
4.3
) and (
4.4
) all hold. We can add the condition
(
4.5
) in the same way, producing a total
of at most
2^{2\omega(q)}\times 2^{3\omega(q)}\ll_{\varepsilon}B^{\varepsilon}

lattices, as required for the lemma.

Any lattice on which
a_{1}x_{1}^{2}y_{1}^{3}+a_{2}x_{2}^{2}y_{2}^{3}
vanishes
identically cannot contribute to

S(q,\kappa)
and may therefore be discarded. Moreover the points on
any lattice
\mathsf{M}
satisfy
equations of the type (
4.18
). Thus if
x_{1}=x_{2}=0
for a point on
such a lattice one sees that

y_{1}=y_{2}=0
, and similarly, if
y_{1}=y_{2}=0
we will have

x_{1}=x_{2}=0
. This concludes the proof
of the lemma.
∎

5
Awkward Cases

Before proceeding further we consider points corresponding to the exceptional lines arising from
Lemma
2.5
. Since

(a_{1}a_{2})^{2}\{a_{1}x_{1}^{2}y_{1}^{3}+a_{2}x_{2}^{2}y_{2}^{3}\}=(a_{2}x_{1})^{2}(a_{1}y_{1})^{3}+(a_{1}x_{2})^{2}(a_{2}y_{2})^{3}

it turns out that we will be interested (in Section
6
) in linear forms for which

L_{1}(u,v)=a_{2}x_{1}
,
L_{2}(u,v)=a_{1}x_{2}
,

M_{1}(u,v)=a_{1}y_{1}
and
M_{2}(u,v)=a_{2}y_{2}
.

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
S_{1}(B)
be the set of points
(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},y_{1},y_{2},y_{3})\in S_{0}(B)
for which there exist
non-zero rationals

and
\nu
such that

a_{2}x_{1}=\nu\{4a_{1}y_{1}-5g^{2}a_{2}y_{2}\},\;\;\;\mbox{and}\;\;\;a_{1}x_{2}=\nu g^{3}\{5a_{1}y_{1}-4g^{2}a_{2}y_{2}\},

and let
S_{1}^{*}(B)
be the set of points
(x_{1},x_{2},y_{1},y_{2})
for which there is a corresponding
pair
(x_{3},y_{3})
with
(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},y_{1},y_{2},y_{3})\in S_{1}(B)
. Then

\#S_{1}(B)=\#S_{1}^{*}(B)\ll B^{1/2}.

Remark
.

In fact one can easily prove a rather better bound, but this is more than enough for our purposes.

Proof.

We write
g=\alpha/\beta
with
\gcd(\alpha,\beta)=1
and
\nu/\beta^{5}=\gamma/\delta
with

\gcd(\gamma,\delta)=1
, so that

a_{2}x_{1}\delta=\gamma\beta^{3}\{4a_{1}\beta^{2}y_{1}-5a_{2}\alpha^{2}y_{2}\}

(5.1)

and

a_{1}x_{2}\delta=\gamma\alpha^{3}\{5a_{1}\beta^{2}y_{1}-4a_{2}\alpha^{2}y_{2}\}.

(5.2)

Thus
\gamma
divides both
a_{2}x_{1}\delta
and
a_{1}x_{2}\delta
. Since

\gamma
and
\delta
are
coprime we then see from the constraints (
1.3
) that
\gamma=\pm 1
, and indeed by changing the signs
of
\alpha
and
\beta
if necessary, we may assume that
\gamma=1
.

We next proceed to prove that
\delta|144
. We begin by eliminating

from (

) to deduce that
\delta|9(\alpha\beta)^{5}a_{1}y_{1}
. Similarly,
eliminating

yields
\delta|9(\alpha\beta)^{5}a_{2}y_{2}
,
and since the constraints (
1.3
) show that

\gcd(a_{1}y_{1},a_{2}y_{2})=1
we conclude that

\delta|9(\alpha\beta)^{5}
.

Suppose now that
p^{e}||\delta
. The prime
p
cannot divide both

\alpha
and
\beta
, and we may therefore
assume without loss of generality that
p\nmid\beta
, say. Let

. Then
p^{\min(e,2f)}|4a_{1}y_{1}
, by
(
5.1
). However the conditions of Theorem
1.2
ensure that

a_{1}y_{1}
is square-free, so that we must
have

\min(e,2f)\leq\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}1,&p\geq 3,\\
3,&p=2.\end{array}\right.

(5.3)

Suppose next that
p\geq 3
. If
f=0
then
p\nmid\alpha\beta
, and so

p^{e}|9
, since
\delta|9(\alpha\beta)^{5}
.
Thus if

either

and
e\leq 2
or
p>3
and
e=0
. On the
other hand, if
f\geq 1
the inequality
(
5.3
) yields

, whence
p^{2}|a_{1}x_{2}
by (
5.2
). Hence

p|x_{2}
since
a_{1}
is square-free.
Moreover, if
e=1
and
f\geq 1
the equation (
5.1
) shows that

p|a_{1}y_{1}
. This however is impossible
because
x_{2}
is coprime to
a_{1}y_{1}
in Theorem
1.2
. We
therefore conclude that
e=0
whenever

p\geq 3
and
f\geq 1
. Thus if
p
is odd, the exponent
e
can only
be positive when
p=3
, in which
case
e\leq 2
.

Finally, for
p=2
, if
e\geq 3f+2
the equation (
5.2
) would show
that
2^{2}|5a_{1}\beta^{2}y_{1}-4a_{2}\alpha^{2}y_{2}
,
whence
4|a_{1}y_{1}
. Since
a_{1}y_{1}
must be square-free we conclude that

, and the second part
of (
5.3
) then shows that

. We may now deduce that

\delta|144
as claimed.

To complete the proof of the lemma we now observe that
\alpha^{3}\ll|a_{1}|X_{2}
and
\beta^{3}\ll|a_{2}|X_{1}
, by
(
5.2
) and (
5.1
) respectively. Moreover these equations show that
the point
(a_{1}\beta^{2}y_{1},a_{2}\alpha^{2}y_{2})
lies in an
ellipse, centred on the origin, of area

O(|a_{1}a_{2}|X_{1}X_{2}|\alpha\beta|^{-3})
, and hence that
(y_{1},y_{2})
is
restricted to an ellipse, also centred on the origin, of area

. Such an ellipse
contains

\ll 1+X_{1}X_{2}|\alpha\beta|^{-5}

lattice points with

, by Lemma
2.7
.
Given

and
\delta
, the values of

and

determine

and

via

) and (

), and there is then at most one choice for

and

satisfying the conditions of

. Thus the total contribution to

is

\displaystyle\ll

\displaystyle\sum_{|\alpha|\ll(|a_{1}|X_{2})^{1/3}}\sum_{|\beta|\ll(|a_{2}|X_{1})^{1/3}}\{1+X_{1}X_{2}|\alpha\beta|^{-5}\}

\displaystyle(|a_{1}a_{2}|X_{1}X_{2})^{1/3}+X_{1}X_{2}

\displaystyle(B/X_{1}Y_{1}^{3})^{1/3}(B/X_{2}Y_{1}^{3})^{1/3}+X_{1}X_{2}

∎

Another awkward situation is that in which one of the lattices
\mathsf{M}
in

4.2
contains a short vector for which

.

Lemma 5.2
.

Define

D=\mathrm{Diag}(X_{1},X_{2},Y_{1},Y_{2}),

(5.4)

and let

be the set of pairs
(q,\kappa)
for which there is

and

,
with
(x_{1},x_{2})\not=(0,0)
,
(y_{1},y_{2})\not=(0,0)
, and

||D^{-1}(x_{1},x_{2},y_{1},y_{2})||_{\infty}\leq R
.
Then

\#Q(B;R)\ll_{\varepsilon}R(Y_{1}Y_{2})^{-3/4}Y_{3}B^{1/2+\varepsilon}

(5.5)

for any fixed
\varepsilon>0
.

Moreover

\#S(q,\kappa)\ll_{\varepsilon}Y_{1}Y_{2}Y_{3}^{-1}B^{\varepsilon}

(5.6)

for any pair
(q,\kappa)
and any fixed
\varepsilon>0
. Thus the number
of points

(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},y_{1},y_{2},q)\in S_{0}(B)

for which
the corresponding pair

lies in

is

\ll_{\varepsilon}R(Y_{1}Y_{2})^{1/4}B^{1/2+\varepsilon},

for any fixed
\varepsilon>0
.

Remark
.

The reader should notice that in the definition of

we do not impose the conditions
(
3.1
) or (
1.3
), or require

to be square-free.
In the Critical Case (
5.7
) is
O_{\varepsilon}(RB^{3/5+\varepsilon})
, giving a non-trivial bound as soon
as

is small.

Proof.

Since
(a_{2}x_{1})^{2}(a_{1}y_{1})^{3}=-(a_{1}x_{2})^{2}(a_{2}y_{2})^{3}
we deduce that

a_{2}x_{1}=ju_{1}^{3}
and

a_{1}x_{2}=ju_{2}^{3}
for suitable integers

and
u_{2}
, with
\gcd(u_{1},u_{2})=1
.
We then have

, and on setting

we find that

u_{1}^{3}\equiv\kappa^{3}u_{2}^{3}\;(\text{mod}\;q/h)
. It follows that if
u_{1},u_{2},h

and
q/h
are given, there
will only be
O_{\varepsilon}(B^{\varepsilon})
choices for
\kappa
modulo
q/h
, and
hence
O_{\varepsilon}(hB^{\varepsilon})

choices modulo
q
. Moreover there are
O(Y_{3}/h)
possibilities
for
q/h\leq Y_{3}/h
. Thus

and

determine
O_{\varepsilon}(Y_{3}B^{\varepsilon})

q
and
\kappa
.

Since
a_{1}
and
a_{2}
are square-free and coprime to
q
the relations

and

and

, showing that

and

. Thus, given

, there are
O(Rh^{-1}(X_{1}X_{2})^{1/2})

choices for

and

u_{2}
. Then, summing over

, we see that there are

available

, producing a total

O_{\varepsilon}(R(X_{1}X_{2})^{1/2}Y_{3}B^{2\varepsilon})
choices for
q
and

\kappa
. The estimate (
5.5
) then follows from (
4.16
) on re-defining
\varepsilon
.

We turn now to the proof of (
5.6
).
By Lemma
2.7
, the second of the congruences (

) has

relevant

, and since

For the final claim of the lemma one merely has to combine (

6

\mathsf{M}

Now that we have handled the exceptional cases covered by Lemmas
5.1
and
5.2
,
we are ready to count points
(x_{1},x_{2},y_{1},y_{2})\in S(q,\kappa)
which lie in
a particular lattice
\mathsf{M}
in Lemma
4.2
. This will
require us to investigate
\det(\mathsf{M})
, and in particular to understand
how often it might be small.

In fact it is convenient to work with

D^{-1}\mathsf{M}
rather than
\mathsf{M}
itself, where

D
is given by (
5.4
).
We choose a basis
\mathbf{g}
,
\mathbf{h}
for
\mathsf{M}
so that the
corresponding basis

D^{-1}\mathbf{g},D^{-1}\mathbf{h}
for
D^{-1}\mathsf{M}
is minimal, in the sense
that
||D^{-1}\mathbf{g}||_{\infty}
and
||D^{-1}\mathbf{h}||_{\infty}
are the successive

\lambda_{1}
and
\lambda_{2}
of
D^{-1}\mathsf{M}
, with

||D^{-1}\mathbf{g}||_{\infty}\leq||D^{-1}\mathbf{h}||_{\infty}

Moreover these basis vectors have the property that if

It turns out that the basis vectors

and

will

, so the reader may wish to focus their attention on

Lemma 6.1
.

If
\mathsf{M}
has a basis
\mathbf{g},\mathbf{h}
as above, then

\#\big(\left(S(q,\kappa)\cap\mathsf{M}\right)\setminus S_{1}^{*}(B)\big)\ll_{\varepsilon}B^{\varepsilon}\left\{||D^{-1}\mathbf{g}||_{\infty}^{-2/3}||D^{-1}\mathbf{h}||_{\infty}^{-2/3}+1\right\}.

(6.3)

Here

is the subset of

defined in Lemma

.

Proof.

We write the quadruple
(x_{1},x_{2},y_{1},y_{2})\in S(q,\kappa)\cap\mathsf{M}
as

u\mathbf{g}+v\mathbf{h}
. Thus we obtain
integral linear forms such that

x_{1}=x_{1}(u,v),\;x_{2}=x_{2}(u,v),\;y_{1}=y_{1}(u,v),\;\mbox{and}\;y_{2}=y_{2}(u,v).

Moreover the condition (
3.1
) shows that

||D^{-1}(x_{1},x_{2},y_{1},y_{2})||_{\infty}\leq 1
,
so that
||uD^{-1}\mathbf{g}+vD^{-1}\mathbf{h}||_{\infty}\leq 1
. We therefore have

|u|\leq U
and
|v|\leq V
with
U\ll 1/||D^{-1}\mathbf{g}||_{\infty}
and

V\ll 1/||D^{-1}\mathbf{h}||_{\infty}
.

Since
x_{1}
and
x_{2}
must be coprime we will have
\gcd(u,v)=1
, Thus if

V<1
for example, we see that
v=0
and

, giving us
O(1)
points in total.
We therefore suppose that
U,V\geq 1
in what follows. Moreover, if
x_{1}(u,v)

and
x_{2}(u,v)
are proportional one sees that they can give at most one pair of
positive coprime values of
x_{1}
and
x_{2}
, and then Lemma
3.2
shows that
there are
O_{\varepsilon}(B^{\varepsilon})
corresponding values of

and
y_{2}
, in light
of (
3.7

and

are proportional we get an overall contribution

in Lemma

.

We now write

L_{1}(u,v)=a_{2}x_{1}(u,v),\;L_{2}(u,v)=a_{1}x_{2}(u,v),

M_{1}(u,v)=-a_{1}a_{3}qy_{1}(u,v),\;\mbox{and}\;M_{2}(u,v)=-a_{2}a_{3}qy_{2}(u,v).

Then if we define
F(u,v)=L_{1}(u,v)^{2}M_{1}(u,v)^{3}+L_{2}(u,v)^{2}M_{2}(u,v)^{3}
we will have

\displaystyle F(u,v)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-a_{3}q^{3}(a_{1}a_{2}a_{3})^{2}\{a_{1}x_{1}(u,v)^{2}y_{1}(u,v)^{3}+a_{2}x_{2}(u,v)^{2}y_{2}(u,v)^{3}\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(a_{1}a_{2}a_{3}q^{3}x_{3})^{2}.

We are now ready to apply Lemma
2.5
. We wish to count the
number of
(u,v)

for which

is a non-zero square. We first claim that the
coefficients of
the forms
L_{1},L_{2},M_{1}
and
M_{2}
are bounded by powers of
B
. Since

\mathbf{g}

is a non-zero vector in
\mathsf{M}\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{4}
we have

||\mathbf{g}||_{\infty}\geq 1
.
Thus
||D^{-1}\mathbf{g}||_{\infty}
is bounded below by a negative power of

B
, and then (
6.1
) and
(
6.2
) show that
||D^{-1}\mathbf{g}||_{\infty}
and

||D^{-1}\mathbf{h}||_{\infty}
are bounded above
by a power of
B
. It then follows that the coefficients of
x_{1}(u,v),x_{2}(u,v),y_{1}(u,v)

and
y_{2}(u,v)
are bounded by powers of
B
, and hence so too are the
coefficients of
L_{1},L_{2},M_{1}
and
M_{2}
, and therefore also of
F
.

In order to apply Lemma
2.5
we need to know that neither of
the linear forms

L_{1}
or
M_{1}
is proportional to either
L_{2}
or
M_{2}
. We have already dealt with

x_{1}(u,v)
and
x_{2}(u,v)
, and with
y_{1}(u,v)
and
y_{2}(u,v)
, so we look at the
case in which
L_{1}
and
M_{2}
are proportional, both integer multiples of an integral
linear form
\ell(u,v)
, say. Since
\gcd(x_{1},y_{2})=1
we must have

\ell(u,v)=\pm 1
. We may parameterize the solutions of this linear equation
as integral linear functions
u=u_{1}t+u_{2}
,
v=v_{1}t+v_{2}
, where
t
is
an integer variable of size
|t|\ll U+V
. Then
F(u,v)
takes the shape

A_{1}X_{1}(t)^{2}+A_{2}Y_{2}(t)^{3}
with non-zero integer polynomials
X_{1}(t),Y_{2}(t)
of degree at
most 1. According to
Lemma
2.3
the number of values for
t
which produce a non-zero square is

\ll(U+V)^{1/2}\log B\ll(UV)^{2/3}\log B

(6.4)

unless
A_{1}X_{1}(t)^{2}+A_{2}Y_{2}(t)^{3}
is a constant multiple of a square. However this cannot
happen unless
X_{1}(t)
and
Y_{2}(t)
are both constant. It follows that we have at most
1 solution when
L_{1}
and
M_{2}
are proportional. Naturally, when
L_{2}
and
M_{1}

are proportional a similar argument applies.

In the statement of Lemma
6.1
we exclude points in
S_{1}^{*}(B)
, these
corresponding to points on the various exceptional
lines described in Lemmas
2.5
and
5.1
. Thus Lemma

2.5
gives us the bound

\ll(UV)^{2/3}(\log B)^{2}+1

for the number of remaining points, whether or not two of the forms

L_{1},L_{2},M_{1}
and
M_{2}
are
proportional. To complete the proof of the lemma we merely note that

UV\ll||D^{-1}\mathbf{g}||_{\infty}^{-1}||D^{-1}\mathbf{h}||_{\infty}^{-1}.

∎

We now need to estimate how frequently small generators

\mathbf{g},\mathbf{h}
arise.
As a preliminary observation, we note that, if

\mathbf{g}=(x_{1},x_{2},y_{1},y_{2})\in\mathsf{M}
,
then we cannot have

x_{1}=x_{2}=0
or
y_{1}=y_{2}=0
, by the final part of Lemma
4.2
. Thus
Lemma
5.2
handles all cases in which
a_{1}x_{1}^{2}y_{1}^{3}+a_{2}x_{2}^{2}y_{2}^{3}=0
.
Our next result handles the alternative case, for which
a_{1}x_{1}^{2}y_{1}^{3}+a_{2}x_{2}^{2}y_{2}^{3}\not=0
.

Lemma 6.2
.

Write
\mathbf{g}=(x_{1},x_{2},y_{1},y_{2})
. Then the number of triples

\left(\mathbf{g},q,\kappa\right)

with
a_{1}x_{1}^{2}y_{1}^{3}+a_{2}x_{2}^{2}y_{2}^{3}\not=0
, for which

\mathbf{g}
satisfies (
4.2
), (
4.3
), (
4.4
) and
(
4.5
), and such that

||D^{-1}\mathbf{g}||_{\infty}\leq R,

(6.5)

is
O_{\varepsilon}\left((Y_{1}Y_{2})^{-3/2}Y_{3}^{-3}R^{7}B^{2+\varepsilon}\right)
, for any fixed
\varepsilon>0
.

In particular, the number of pairs
(q,\kappa)
for which such a vector
\mathbf{g}

exists is also
O_{\varepsilon}\left((Y_{1}Y_{2})^{-3/2}Y_{3}^{-3}R^{7}B^{2+\varepsilon}\right)
, for any fixed
\varepsilon>0
.

Remark
.

Although the lemma gives a bound for the number of
relevant triples
(\mathbf{g},q,\kappa)
, it does not estimate the number of corresponding
lattices
\mathsf{M}
that might contain a suitable
\mathbf{g}
. This is because a given

\mathbf{g}
might lie in more than one lattice
\mathsf{M}
.

Proof.

We begin by classifying the triples
\left(\mathbf{g},q,\kappa\right)

according to the value of
r=\gcd(y_{1},y_{2},q)
.
Lemma
4.1
then shows that

18a_{1}x_{1}^{2}y_{1}^{3}+18a_{2}x_{2}^{2}y_{2}^{3}=fq^{3}r^{2}

for some integer
f
, which will be non-zero. We see from (
3.1
) and (
3.2
) that
the left hand side
has absolute value
O(R^{5}B)
and since
q=y_{3}>\tfrac{1}{2}Y_{3}
we find that

f\ll Y_{3}^{-3}R^{5}Br^{-2}.

The triple
(y_{1}/r,y_{2}/r,q/r)
is a coprime solution to the equation
(
1.4
) with coefficients

a=18a_{1}x_{1}^{2},\;\;\;b=18a_{2}x_{2}^{2},\;\;\;\mbox{and}\;\;\;c=-fr^{2}.

Moreover
a,b
and
c
are non-zero by our assumptions. Thus Theorem

1.3

shows that
x_{1},x_{2},r
and
f
determine
O_{\varepsilon}(B^{\varepsilon})
possibilities
for
y_{1},y_{2}
and
q
.
There are

\ll X_{1}X_{2}Y_{3}^{-3}R^{7}Br^{-2}\ll(Y_{1}Y_{2})^{-3/2}Y_{3}^{-3}R^{7}B^{2}r^{-2}

possible choices for
x_{1},x_{2}
and
f
, by (
4.16
), so that the total
number of possibilities for
\mathbf{g}
and
q
is

O_{\varepsilon}((Y_{1}Y_{2})^{-3/2}Y_{3}^{-3}R^{7}B^{2+\varepsilon}r^{-2})
,
for each
r
.

The second of the congruences
(
4.2
) shows that
qr^{-1}|\kappa^{2}a_{1}y_{1}r^{-1}+a_{2}y_{2}r^{-1}
,
which determines
O_{\varepsilon}(B^{\varepsilon})
values for
\kappa
modulo
qr^{-1}
, and hence

O_{\varepsilon}(rB^{\varepsilon})
values modulo
q
. We therefore have

O_{\varepsilon}((Y_{1}Y_{2})^{-3/2}Y_{3}^{-3}R^{7}B^{2+2\varepsilon}r^{-1})

choices for
\left(\mathbf{g},q,\kappa\right)
, for each value of

r
. The lemma then
follows on summing for
r\leq Y_{3}
and re-defining
\varepsilon
.
∎

As noted above, a generator
\mathbf{g}=(x_{1},x_{2},y_{1},y_{2})
cannot have

x_{1}=x_{2}=0
or
y_{1}=y_{2}=0
, by the final part of Lemma
4.2
.
If
a_{1}x_{1}^{2}y_{1}^{3}+a_{2}x_{2}^{2}y_{2}^{3}=0
the pair
(q,\kappa)
will therefore be in the set

Q(B;R)
defined in Lemma
5.2
. Thus Lemma
6.2
has the
following corollary.

Lemma 6.3
.

For each pair
q,\kappa
let
\nu_{1}(q,\kappa;R)
be the number of
non-zero vectors

\mathbf{g}
with
||D^{-1}\mathbf{g}||_{\infty}\leq R
that lie in some
lattice
\mathsf{M}
in
\mathcal{L}(q,\kappa)
. Then

\sum_{(q,\kappa)\not\in Q(B;R)}\nu_{1}(q,\kappa;R)\ll_{\varepsilon}(Y_{1}Y_{2})^{-3/2}Y_{3}^{-3}R^{7}B^{2+\varepsilon},

for any fixed
\varepsilon>0
.

For the second generator
\mathbf{h}
we do not need Lemma
5.2
.

Lemma 6.4
.

For each pair
q,\kappa
let
\nu_{2}(q,\kappa;S)
be the number of
non-zero vectors
\mathbf{h}
with
||D^{-1}\mathbf{h}||_{\infty}\leq S
that lie in some
lattice
\mathsf{M}
in
\mathcal{L}(q,\kappa)
, and with the further property
that there is a second vector
\mathbf{g}\in\mathsf{M}
, not proportional to

\mathbf{h}
, and such that
||D^{-1}\mathbf{g}||_{\infty}\leq||D^{-1}\mathbf{h}||_{\infty}
.

Then

\sum_{(q,\kappa)}\nu_{2}(q,\kappa;S)\ll_{\varepsilon}(Y_{1}Y_{2})^{-3/2}Y_{3}^{-3}S^{7}B^{2+\varepsilon},

for any fixed
\varepsilon>0
.

Proof.

For pairs
(q,\kappa)
for which
\mathbf{h}=(x_{1},x_{2},y_{1},y_{2})
with

a_{1}x_{1}^{2}y_{1}^{3}+a_{2}x_{2}^{2}y_{2}^{3}\not=0

we may apply Lemma
6.2
directly.
In general we consider vectors

\mathbf{h}^{\prime}=n\mathbf{g}+\mathbf{h}=(x_{1}(n),x_{2}(n),y_{1}(n),y_{2}(n)).

We then set
f(n)=a_{1}x_{1}(n)^{2}y_{1}(n)^{3}+a_{2}x_{2}(n)^{2}y_{2}(n)^{3}
. The
polynomial
f(X)
cannot
vanish identically, by the properties of
\mathsf{M}
established in
Lemma
4.2
.
Since
f
has degree 5 there is therefore some positive integer
n\leq 6
such that
f(n)\not=0
.
This produces a vector
\mathbf{h}^{\prime}\in\mathbf{M}
, with

||D^{-1}\mathbf{g}||_{\infty}\leq 7||D^{-1}\mathbf{h}||_{\infty}\leq 7S

to which Lemma
6.2
applies. The result then follows
on using this as a replacement for
\mathbf{h}
.
∎

We can now consider how many lattices
\mathsf{M}
have basis vectors
of a given size. We will
use the following result to control the first term on the right of (
6.3
).

Lemma 6.5
.

The number of lattices

\mathsf{M}\in\bigcup_{(q,\kappa)\not\in Q(B;R)}\mathcal{L}(q,\kappa)

which have basis vectors
\mathbf{g},\mathbf{h}
with

||D^{-1}\mathbf{g}||_{\infty}\leq||D^{-1}\mathbf{h}||_{\infty}
and

||D^{-1}\mathbf{g}||_{\infty}\leq R,\;\;\;||D^{-1}\mathbf{h}||_{\infty}\leq S,

is

\ll_{\varepsilon}(Y_{1}Y_{2})^{-5/3}Y_{3}^{-4}(RS)^{7/2}B^{7/3+\varepsilon}

for any fixed
\varepsilon>0
.

Remark
.

This bound will handle the first term on the right of (
6.3
), when

\mathbf{g}
and
\mathbf{h}
are not too big.

Proof.

Although it seems possible that a given vector
\mathbf{g}
may lie
in more than one lattice
\mathsf{M}
,
it is certainly the case that
\mathbf{g}
and
\mathbf{h}
together
do determine
\mathsf{M}
. Thus the number
of lattices in
\mathcal{L}(q,\kappa)
having basis vectors

\mathbf{g},\mathbf{h}
as in the lemma is at most

\nu_{1}(q,\kappa;R)\nu_{2}(q,\kappa;S)
. Alternatively, Lemma

4.2
shows that we may bound
this number by
\#\mathcal{L}(q,\kappa)\leq L
, with

L\ll_{\varepsilon}(Y_{1}Y_{2})^{-1/3}Y_{3}^{-2}B^{2/3+\varepsilon}.

Thus the number of lattices satisfying the conditions of the lemma is

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{(q,\kappa)\not\in Q(B;R)}\min\big(\nu_{1}(q,\kappa;R)\nu_{2}(q,\kappa;S)\,,\,L\big)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{(q,\kappa)\not\in Q(B;R)}\big(\nu_{1}(q,\kappa;R)\nu_{2}(q,\kappa;S))^{1/2}L^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle L^{1/2}\left(\sum_{(q,\kappa)\not\in Q(B;R)}\nu_{1}(q,\kappa;R)\right)^{1/2}\left(\sum_{(q,\kappa)\not\in Q(B;R)}\nu_{2}(q,\kappa;S)\right)^{1/2},

by Cauchy’s inequality. The result then follows from Lemmas
6.3

and
6.4
on re-defining
\varepsilon
.
∎

7
Combining all our Bounds

In this section we combine the various bounds we have obtained.
We remind the reader that, given the coefficients
a_{i}
in
(
1.1
), the values of
x_{1},x_{2},y_{1},y_{2}
determine at most one choice for
x_{3}

and
y_{3}
, whence it suffices to count points
(x_{1},x_{2},y_{1},y_{2})
. We will write

S_{0}(B)
as a union

S_{0}(B)=S_{1}(B)\cup S_{2}(B)\cup S_{3}(B)\cup S_{4}(B)\cup S_{5}(B)

and estimate
\#S_{i}(B)
separately for each index
i
. We already have

\#S_{1}(B)\ll B^{1/2}

(7.1)

from Lemma
5.1
,
accounting for points lying on the “bad” lines in Lemma
2.5
.
We define
S_{2}(B)
to be the subset of
S_{0}(B)
for which
(x_{1},x_{2},y_{1},y_{2})
lies
in the set
S_{0}(q,\kappa)
in Lemma
4.2
. Since there are
O(Y_{3}^{2})

possibilities for
(q,\kappa)
we see from (
4.15
) that

\#S_{2}(B)\ll_{\varepsilon}(Y_{1}Y_{2})^{-1/3}B^{2/3+\varepsilon},

(7.2)

for any fixed
\varepsilon>0
.

The remaining points of
S_{0}(B)
are covered by Lemma
6.1
.
We give ourselves a parameter
K\in(0,1]
to be chosen later, and take
S_{3}(B)

to be the set of points in
S_{0}(B)\setminus\left(S_{1}(B)\cup S_{2}(B)\right)

belonging to lattices
\mathsf{M}
for which the basis vectors satisfy

||D^{-1}\mathbf{g}||_{\infty}||D^{-1}\mathbf{h}||_{\infty}\geq K
.
Since Lemma
4.2

shows that the total number of lattices
\mathsf{M}
is

\ll_{\varepsilon}(Y_{1}Y_{2})^{-1/3}B^{2/3+\varepsilon},

we see that (
6.3
) yields

\#S_{3}(B)\ll_{\varepsilon}K^{-2/3}(Y_{1}Y_{2})^{-1/3}B^{2/3+2\varepsilon}.

(7.3)

It remains to handle lattices
\mathsf{M}
for which

||D^{-1}\mathbf{g}||_{\infty}||D^{-1}\mathbf{h}||_{\infty}\leq K
.
For these we do a dyadic subdivision for the ranges of

||D^{-1}\mathbf{g}||_{\infty}
and
||D^{-1}\mathbf{h}||_{\infty}
, writing

\tfrac{1}{2}R<||D^{-1}\mathbf{g}||_{\infty}\leq R\;\;\;\mbox{and}\;\;\;\tfrac{1}{2}S<||D^{-1}\mathbf{h}||_{\infty}\leq S,

(7.4)

with
RS\ll K
. Since
||\mathbf{g}||_{\infty}\leq||\mathbf{h}||_{\infty}
we will have

R\ll K^{1/2}
. Thus if we define
R_{0}=K^{1/2}\log B
we will have
R\leq R_{0}
as long
as
B
is suitably large. We now define
S_{4}(B)
to be the set of points

(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},y_{1},y_{2},q)\in S_{0}(B)
for which
the corresponding pair
(q,\kappa)
lies in
Q(B;R_{0})
, as described in
Lemma
5.2
. It then follows from (
5.7
) that

\#S_{4}(B)\ll_{\varepsilon}K^{1/2}(Y_{1}Y_{2})^{1/4}B^{1/2+2\varepsilon},

(7.5)

for any fixed
\varepsilon>0
.

Our final set
S_{5}(B)
consists of points in
S_{0}(B)
belonging to lattices

\mathsf{M}
whose generators lie in the ranges (
7.4
) with
RS\ll K
, and for which
the corresponding values
(q,\kappa)
do not lie in
Q(B;R_{0})
. For these we have

(q,\kappa)\not\in Q(B;R)

a fortiori
, so that the number of relevant lattices

\mathsf{M}
with generators in the ranges (
7.4
) is

\ll_{\varepsilon}(Y_{1}Y_{2})^{-5/3}Y_{3}^{-4}(RS)^{7/2}B^{7/3+\varepsilon}

by Lemma
6.5
. Moreover each such lattice provides

O_{\varepsilon}\left((RS)^{-2/3}B^{\varepsilon}\right)
points, by Lemma
6.1
, whence

\#S_{5}(B)\ll_{\varepsilon}(Y_{1}Y_{2})^{-5/3}Y_{3}^{-4}B^{7/3+2\varepsilon}\sum_{R,S}(RS)^{7/2-2/3},

the sum being over dyadic values of
R
and
S
with
RS\ll K
.
We therefore conclude that

\#S_{5}(B)\ll_{\varepsilon}(Y_{1}Y_{2})^{-5/3}Y_{3}^{-4}B^{7/3+2\varepsilon}K^{17/6}.

(7.6)

We may now combine (
7.1
), (
7.2
), (
7.3
), (
7.5
), and (
7.6
), and
replace
\varepsilon
by
\varepsilon/2
, to show that if
0<K\leq 1
then

N_{0}(B)=\#S_{0}(B)\ll_{\varepsilon}\left\{K^{-2/3}(Y_{1}Y_{2})^{-1/3}B^{2/3}+K^{1/2}(Y_{1}Y_{2})^{1/4}B^{1/2}\right.

\hskip 113.81102pt\left.\mbox{}+(Y_{1}Y_{2})^{-5/3}Y_{3}^{-4}B^{7/3}K^{17/6}\right\}B^{\varepsilon}

for any fixed
\varepsilon>0
, and any parameters in the ranges (
3.7
). We choose

K=(Y_{1}Y_{2})^{8/21}Y_{3}^{8/7}B^{-10/21}

so as to equate the first and third terms on the right, and note that
K\leq 1
by (
3.7
).
This choice gives us

N_{0}(B)\ll_{\varepsilon}\left\{(Y_{1}Y_{2})^{-37/63}Y_{3}^{-16/21}B^{62/63}+(Y_{1}Y_{2})^{37/84}Y_{3}^{4/7}B^{11/42}\right\}B^{\varepsilon}.

The second term on the left is smaller than the first, by (
3.7
).
We may therefore conclude that

N_{0}(B)\ll_{\varepsilon}(Y_{1}Y_{2})^{-37/63}Y_{3}^{-16/21}B^{62/63+\varepsilon}.

Although we said at the outset that we would work with congruences to modulus
y_{3}^{3}

we could just as well have used
y_{1}^{3}
or
y_{2}^{3}
, giving us analogous bounds with

Y_{1},Y_{2}
and
Y_{3}
permuted. Thus

\displaystyle N_{0}(B)^{3}

\displaystyle\ll_{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle(Y_{2}Y_{3})^{-37/63}Y_{1}^{-16/21}B^{62/63+\varepsilon}\times(Y_{1}Y_{3})^{-37/63}Y_{2}^{-16/21}B^{62/63+\varepsilon}

\displaystyle\hskip 56.9055pt\mbox{}\times(Y_{1}Y_{2})^{-37/63}Y_{3}^{-16/21}B^{62/63+\varepsilon}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(Y_{1}Y_{2}Y_{3})^{-122/63}B^{62/21+3\varepsilon},

so that
N_{0}(B)\ll_{\varepsilon}(Y_{1}Y_{2}Y_{3})^{-122/189}B^{62/63+\varepsilon}
.
We now see from Lemma
3.1
that either
N_{0}(B)\ll_{\varepsilon}B^{1/2+\varepsilon}
or

\displaystyle N_{0}(B)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle N_{0}(B)^{122/311}N_{0}(B)^{189/311}

\displaystyle\ll_{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle(Y_{1}Y_{2}Y_{3}B^{\varepsilon})^{122/311}\left\{(Y_{1}Y_{2}Y_{3})^{-122/189}B^{62/63+\varepsilon}\right\}^{189/311}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle B^{186/311+\varepsilon}.

) this suffices for Theorem

, since

.

is now that in which

,
