On the number of pairwise touching cylinders in ℝ d \mathbb{R}^{d}
Jozsef Solymosi, Joshua Zahl

Abstract
John E. Littlewood posted the question “Is it possible in 3-space for seven infinite circular cylinders of unit radius each to touch all the others? Seven is the number suggested by counting constants.” Bozóki, Lee, and Rónyai constructed a configuration of 7 mutually touching unit cylinders. The best-known upper bounds show that at most 10 unit cylinders in ℝ 3 \mathbb{R}^{3} can mutually touch. We consider this problem in higher dimensions, and obtain exponential (in d d ) upper bounds on the number of mutually touching cylinders in ℝ d \mathbb{R}^{d} . Our method is fairly flexible, and it makes use of the fact that cylinder touching can be expressed as a combination of polynomial equalities and non-equalities.



1
Introduction

In Problem 7 in his problem book
[
11
]
, John E. Littlewood posted the question
“Is it possible in 3-space for seven infinite circular cylinders of unit radius each to touch all the others? Seven is the number suggested by counting constants.”
(see also in
[
6
]
with some related questions). András Bezdek
[
2
]
proved that in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
the maximum number of pairwise touching cylinders (of arbitrary radii) is at most 24. In the case of cylinders of unit radius, this was subsequently improved to 18 by Koizumi
[
9
]
, and then to 10 by Dillon, Koizumi, and Luo
[
7
]
. In the other direction,
Bozóki, Lee, and Rónyai
[
5
]
constructed an arrangement of 7 mutually touching unit cylinders. For non-equal radii, there are numerical examples for 9 mutually touching cylinders in
[
8
]
.

In this paper, we consider the problem of bounding the number of pairwise touching cylinders (of unit radius or of arbitrary radius) in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. The parameter space of
n
-tuples of lines in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
has dimension
(2d-2)n
, while the requirement that the corresponding unit cylinders touch imposes
\binom{n}{2}
constraints. The image of a configuration of touching cylinders under a rigid transformation is also a configuration of touching cylinders. Thus by counting parameters, one might expect to find
n
touching unit cylinders in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
provided
(2d-2)n\geq\binom{n}{2}+\binom{d+1}{2}
. In
[
7
]
, Dillon, Koizumi, and Luo construct an arrangement of
2d-2
pairwise touching unit cylinders in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Prior to this work, it was not known if the maximum number of touching unit cylinders in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
for
d\geq 4
is finite. Our main results are the following:

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
C
be a set of mutually touching unit cylinders in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Then
|C|\leq 4d\cdot 7^{2d-3}
.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
C
be a set of mutually touching cylinders (with arbitrary radii) in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Then
|C|\leq 20(d+1)\cdot 7^{2d-2}
.

Theorem
1.1
is equivalent to the problem of bounding the maximum number of lines in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with pairwise unit distances. It sounds similar to the classical 2-distance set estimates for points by Larman, Rogers, and Seidel
[
10
]
and by Blukhuis
[
3
]
, but there are significant differences. There is no single polynomial vanishing on the parameters of two lines if and only if their distance is one. We have to restrict our approach to real spaces, where cylinder-touching can be expressed as a combination of polynomial equalities and non-equalities. As far as we know, this is a novel application of the polynomial method.

2
An algebraic expression for touching cylinders

In what follows, we will identify points in
\mathbb{R}^{2d-2}
with lines in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. For
x=(a,b)\in\mathbb{R}^{d-1}\times\mathbb{R}^{d-1}
, we define the line
L_{x}(t)=\{(t,at+b)\colon t\in\mathbb{R}\}
. Every line that is not parallel to the
\{x_{1}=0\}
plane can be expressed in this form. A pair of lines corresponding to the points
x=(a,b)
and
y=(c,d)
are parallel if and only if
a=c
.

Let
x=(a,b)
and
y=(c,d)
. We wish to compute the squared distance between the corresponding lines
L_{x}
and
L_{y}
. This will be a rational function of
x
and
y
. It can be expressed as the ratio of the Gram determinant of the displacement and directions to the Gram determinant of the directions.

Define

P(x,y)=\det\begin{pmatrix}|b-d|^{2}&(b-d)\cdot a&(b-d)\cdot c\\
a\cdot(b-d)&1+\|a\|^{2}&1+a\cdot c\\
c\cdot(b-d)&1+c\cdot a&1+\|c\|^{2}\end{pmatrix},\quad Q(x,y)=\det\begin{pmatrix}1+\|a\|^{2}&1+a\cdot c\\
1+c\cdot a&1+\|c\|^{2}\end{pmatrix}.

Then

(\operatorname{dist}(L_{x},L_{y}))^{2}=P(x,y)/Q(x,y).

(2.1)

For
x
fixed,
P(x,y)
and
Q(x,y)
are polynomials of degrees 4 and 2, respectively, in
y
. Similarly with the roles of
x
and
y
reversed. Observe that
Q(x,y)=0
if and only if
a=c
, i.e. the lines
L_{x}
and
L_{y}
are parallel.
For
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{2d-2}
, define

F(x,y)=P(x,y)-4Q(x,y).

(2.2)

Similarly, for
w=(x,r),\ v=(y,s)\in\mathbb{R}^{2d-2}\times\mathbb{R}
, define

G(w,v)=P(x,y)-(r+s)^{2}Q(x,y).

(2.3)

We record several observations. In what follows, we adopt the notation
w=(x,r)
and
v=(y,s)
.

•

If the unit cylinders with coaxial lines
L_{x}
and
L_{y}
touch, then
F(x,y)=0
. If the cylinder with coaxial line
L_{x}
and radius
r
touches the cylinder with coaxial line
y
and radius
s
, then
G(w,v)=0
.

•

Suppose the lines
L_{x}
and
L_{y}
are not parallel. If
F(x,y)=0
, then the unit cylinders with coaxial lines
L_{x}
and
L_{y}
touch. If
G(w,v)=0
and
r,s>0
, then the cylinder with coaxial line
L_{x}
and radius
r
touches the cylinder with coaxial line
L_{y}
and radius
s
.

•

For
x
(resp.
y
) fixed,
F(x,y)
is a polynomial in
y
(resp.
x
) of degree 4. For
w
(resp.
v
) fixed,
G(w,v)
is a polynomial in
v
(resp.
w
) of degree 4.

3
The restricted Zariski closure

Let
H(x,y)\colon\mathbb{R}^{k}\times\mathbb{R}^{k}\to\mathbb{R}
be a real polynomial. Define
H_{x}(y)=H(x,y)
and define
H^{y}(x)=H(x,y)
. Thus
Z(H_{x})=\{y\in\mathbb{R}^{k}\colon H(x,y)=0\}
and similarly for
Z(H^{y})
.

For
X\subset\mathbb{R}^{k}
and
H
as above, define

\overline{X}^{H}=\bigcap_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in\mathbb{R}^{k}\\
X\subset Z(H_{x})\end{subarray}}\!\!\!Z(H_{x})\ \ \cap\bigcap_{\begin{subarray}{c}y\in\mathbb{R}^{k}\\
X\subset Z(H^{x})\end{subarray}}\!\!\!Z(H^{y}),

(3.1)

where by convention we define
\overline{X}^{H}=\mathbb{R}^{k}
if there are no sets in the above intersection. Note that
\overline{X}
is closed (in both the Euclidean and Zariski topology) and that
X\subset\overline{X}\subset\overline{X}^{H}
, where
\overline{X}
denotes the Zariski closure of
X
. We call
\overline{X}^{H}
the (symmetric) restricted Zariski closure of
X
with respect to
H
.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
H(x,y)\colon\mathbb{R}^{k}\times\mathbb{R}^{k}\to\mathbb{R}
be a real polynomial, let
X\subset\mathbb{R}^{k}
be non-empty, and suppose
H(x,y)=0
for all
x,y\in X
. Then
H(x,y)=0
for all
x,y\in\overline{X}^{H}
.

Proof.

To streamline notation, define
X^{*}=\overline{X}^{H}
. Let
x\in X
. Since
H(x,y)=0
for all
y\in X
, we have
X\subset Z(H_{x})
, and hence
X^{*}\subset Z(H_{x})
. This means that for all
x\in X
and all
y^{*}\in X^{*}
, we have
H(x,y^{*})=0
. Fix
y^{*}\in X^{*}
. Then for all
x\in X
we have
H(x,y^{*})=0
, i.e.
X\subset Z(H^{y^{*}})
, and thus
X^{*}\subset Z(H^{y^{*}})
. Since
y^{*}\in X^{*}
was arbitrary, we conclude that
H(x^{*},y^{*})=0
for all
x^{*},y^{*}\in X^{*}
.
∎

Remark 3.1
.

For some applications, we might only know that
H(x,y)=0
for
distinct

x,y\in X
. In this case, we can apply Lemma
3.1
to the polynomial
\tilde{H}(x,y)=H(x,y)\|x-y\|^{2}
.

4
Milnor-Thom and its consequences

The Milnor-Thom theorem
[
12
,
13
]
bounds the sum of the Betti numbers of a real algebraic variety. The following formulation is Theorem 11.5.3 from
[
4
]
.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
Z\subset\mathbb{R}^{k}
be a real algebraic variety defined by polynomials of degree at most
D
. Then the sum of the Betti numbers of
Z
is at most
D(2D-1)^{k-1}
.

Note that Theorem
4.1
does not (a priori) require that
Z
be defined by a
finite
set of polynomials, though by Hilbert’s basis theorem we can always reduce to this case.
Recall that the zeroth Betti number
b_{0}(Z)
counts the number of Euclidean connected components of
Z
. We will record what happens when we apply Theorem
4.1
to the set
\bar{X}^{F}
defined in (
3.1
), when
F\colon\mathbb{R}^{2d-2}\times\mathbb{R}^{2d-2}
is the function from (
2.2
).

Corollary 4.2
.

Let
X\subset\mathbb{R}^{2d-2}
. Then
\overline{X}^{F}
is a union of at most
4\cdot 7^{2d-3}
Euclidean connected components.

A similar result holds for
Y\subset\mathbb{R}^{2d-1}
and
\overline{Y}^{G}
. However, even if (for example) all of the cylinders in
Y
have radius
\geq 1
, the set
\overline{Y}^{G}
may contain points corresponding to cylinders with zero, or negative, radii. In order to restrict attention to cylinders with radius at least one, we will intersect the set
\overline{Y}^{G}
with a half-space of the form
\{(x,r)\in\mathbb{R}^{2d-1}\times\mathbb{R}\colon r\geq 1\}
. The following result from
[
1
]
allows us to control the number of Euclidean connected components that results from this process.

Theorem 4.3
.

Let
Z\subset\mathbb{R}^{k}
be a real algebraic variety defined by polynomials of degree at most
D
. Let
P\colon\mathbb{R}^{k}\to\mathbb{R}
be a polynomial of degree at most
D
. Then the sum of the number of connected components of the three sets
Z\cap\{P<0\}
,
Z\cap\{P=0\}
and
Z\cap\{P>0\}
is at most
5D(2D-1)^{k-1}
.

Corollary 4.4
.

Let
Y\subset\mathbb{R}^{2d-1}
. Then
\overline{Y}^{G}\cap\{(x,r)\in\mathbb{R}^{2d-1}\times\mathbb{R}\colon r\geq 1\}
is a union of at most
20\cdot 7^{2d-2}
Euclidean connected components.

5
Bounding the number of pairwise touching cylinders

We are now ready to prove Theorems
1.1
and
1.2
.

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

Since at most
d
unit spheres in
\mathbb{R}^{d-1}
can mutually touch, at most
d
pairwise parallel unit cylinders in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
can mutually touch. Thus it suffices to prove that every set of mutually touching unit cylinders with distinct directions has cardinality at most
4\cdot 7^{2d-3}
.

Suppose to the contrary that there existed a set
C
of mutually touching unit cylinders with distinct directions that has cardinality
4\cdot 7^{2d-3}+1
. Applying a rotation if needed, we can suppose that none of these cylinders are parallel to the
\{x_{1}=0\}
plane. Let
X\subset\mathbb{R}^{2d-2}
denote the corresponding set of points, as described in Section
2
. Let
F
be as defined in (
2.2
). By Corollary
4.2
, there exists a Euclidean connected component
U\subset\bar{X}^{F}
that contains at least two points of
X
.

Let
\pi\colon\mathbb{R}^{2d-2}\to\mathbb{R}^{d-1}
be the projection to the first
d+1
coordinates (this corresponds to the direction of the line). We claim that if
x,y\in U
with
\pi(x)\neq\pi(y)
, then
\|x-y\|\geq 2
. This is because
x,y\in U\subset\overline{X}^{F}
implies (by Lemma
3.1
) that
F(x,y)=0
, while
\pi(x)\neq\pi(y)
implies that
L_{x}
and
L_{y}
are not parallel. This means that
\|x-y\|\geq\operatorname{dist}(L_{x},L_{y})=2
. In particular,
\pi(U)
is (at most) countable, since every
\geq 2
separated subset of
\mathbb{R}^{k}
is countable. On the other hand,
\pi(U)
is connected and contains at least two points, and thus is uncountable. This contradiction completes the proof.
∎

Proof of Theorem
1.2
.

The proof proceeds in a similar fashion to that of Theorem
1.1
. Since at most
d+1
circles in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
can mutually touch, it suffices to prove that every set of mutually touching cylinders with distinct directions has cardinality at most
20\cdot 7^{2d-2}
. Suppose for contradiction that there exists such a set of cylinders with cardinality
20\cdot 7^{2d-2}+1
. After rescaling we may suppose that each cylinder has radius
\geq 1
. Let
Y\subset\mathbb{R}^{2d-1}
be the corresponding set of points (the final coordinate denotes the radius of the cylinder). We wish to obtain a contradiction.

Arguing as above and using Corollary
4.4
in place of Corollary
4.2
, we obtain a Euclidean connected set
U\subset\bar{Y}^{G}\cap\{(x,r)\in\mathbb{R}^{2d-1}\times\mathbb{R}\colon r\geq 1\}
that contains at least two points of
Y
, and thus
\pi(U)
is uncountable. On the other hand, if
(x,r),\ (y,s)\in U
and
\pi(x,r)\neq\pi(y,s)
, then
\|x-y\|\geq\operatorname{dist}(L_{x},L_{y})\geq(r+s)\geq 2
. This means that
\pi(U)
must be (at most) countable. This is our desired contradiction.
∎
