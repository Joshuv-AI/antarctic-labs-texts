Biodegradable molybdenum intramedullary nails facilitate fracture repair through neutrophil-mediated immune response
Authors: Chen Zhu, Yuchen He, Yili Zhao, Yingyi Huang, Chen Zhang, Jiannan Zhou, Huibin Liang, Haotian Qin, Zhonghan Fang, Danyang Liu, Jingtao Dai, Ping Li, Junyu Qian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Molybdenum (Mo) has been considered as a promising biodegradable metal for fracture fixation owing to its favorable mechanical properties and controlled degradation behavior. Although existing studies have confirmed the osteoinductive potential of molybdenum-based materials, the biological mechanism through which molybdenum facilitates bone healing remains poorly elucidated. This study systematically evaluated the fracture repair performance of biodegradable Mo intramedullary nails in a rat femoral fracture model and further characterized the early peri-implant immune microenvironment using single-cell RNA sequencing in a day-3 femoral implantation model. Compared to the titanium (Ti)-based implant, the Mo-based implant significantly accelerated fracture healing, as evidenced by improved radiographic and micro-CT outcomes, enhanced matrix deposition, and mineralized bone formation, and increased expression of osteogenesis-related markers. Single-cell transcriptomic analysis revealed that the Mo-based metal induced a distinct immune landscape characterized by enrichment of neutrophils and macrophages and enhanced crosstalk between these two myeloid populations. Among the identified subsets, Cd177+ neutrophils and Spp1+ macrophages emerged as key potential cell populations associated with the pro-regenerative effect of Mo metal. Further ligand-receptor analysis, molecular docking, and histological validation suggested that these two subsets may interact through the Fn1-Syndecan-1 axis. Collectively, our findings indicate that the biodegradable Mo implant can promote fracture healing by remodeling the early osteoimmune microenvironment, and reveal a candidate neutrophil-macrophage intercellular communication mechanism identified in the implantation model that may underlie Mo-induced bone repair.

Introduction

Bone fractures remain one of the most common traumatic injuries worldwide and represent a major clinical burden, particularly in cases requiring surgical fixation [1,2]. For unstable or load-bearing fractures, osteosynthesis implants are indispensable for restoring alignment and providing mechanical support throughout the healing process [3]. Conventional bone implants, including titanium (Ti) alloys, stainless steel, and cobalt–chromium alloys, have been widely used in clinical practice owing to their excellent mechanical strength and corrosion resistance. However, these bioinert metals remain permanently in the body unless removed by secondary surgery, which increases the healthcare burden and the risk of additional complications [4].

To date, biodegradable metallic implants have emerged as promising alternatives for bone repair and regeneration, as they are expected to provide temporary stabilization and gradually disappear after fulfilling their function [5]. Although biodegradable metallic implants have demonstrated unique advantages and promising translational potential, their broader application in load-bearing fracture fixation remains constrained by intrinsic material limitations [6,7]. Magnesium (Mg)-based metals have already demonstrated clinical utility in selected orthopedic scenarios, yet their overly rapid degradation could cause mechanical failure [8,9]. Despite their favorable degradability and bioactivity, zinc (Zn)-based metals require precise regulation of degradation kinetics and Zn ion release to avert local cytotoxicity [10,11]. Among emerging candidates, molybdenum (Mo)-based metals have attracted increasing attention as potential biodegradable metals for orthopedic applications [[12], [13], [14], [15], [16]]. As an essential trace element, Mo ions participate in physiological metabolism mainly in the form of the molybdenum cofactor, a catalytic core for redox reactions, and are involved in the activity of several key enzymes, including xanthine oxidoreductase, aldehyde oxidase, and sulfite oxidase, respectively [17,18]. From a materials perspective, pure Mo exhibits exceptionally high mechanical strength, allowing the fabrication of thinner and smaller implants while still maintaining reliable support under complex loading conditions [12,[19], [20], [21]]. In addition, Mo-based metals have been reported to exhibit relatively uniform and controllable degradation behavior in vivo [13,22,23].

Notably, the mechanical reliability of Mo-based intramedullary nails (IMNs) has been directly demonstrated in vivo. A previous study demonstrated that Mo-based IMNs retained approximately 77% of their initial bending strength after 12 weeks of implantation, confirming sufficient mechanical integrity throughout the fracture healing period [13]. This mechanical stability ensures that Mo IMNs can provide sustained structural support comparable to conventional metallic implants during the critical phases of bone repair. Moreover, the released Mo ions at appropriate concentrations can promote the osteogenic differentiation of bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs), as evidenced by increased alkaline phosphatase activity, enhanced mineralization, and upregulated expression of osteogenic markers such as Runx2 and Bmp2 [13]. These results indicate that Mo-based metal could actively contribute to osteogenesis through its ionic degradation products, rather than functioning solely as a passive structural material. Importantly, the biosafety of Mo-based implants has been systematically evaluated in vivo. Histological examination of major organs revealed no pathological changes or tissue damage, and analysis of Mo concentrations in major organs showed no significant accumulation compared with control groups, with routine blood parameters remaining within normal ranges [13,22,23]. These characteristics make Mo particularly attractive for intramedullary fixation, where sufficient mechanical support, compact dimensions, and controlled degradation are all critically required. Although previous studies have demonstrated the biosafety and preliminary bone repair potential of Mo-based implants, the biological mechanisms by which they facilitate fracture healing remain incompletely understood.

Successful fracture healing depends not only on mechanical stabilization but also on a tightly coordinated early host response at the bone–implant interface. Following implantation, the initial immune reaction plays a critical role in shaping the local regenerative microenvironment [24,25]. Among early responding immune cells, neutrophils are rapidly recruited to the injury and implantation site and constitute one of the first cell populations interacting with the implanted material [26]. More importantly, neutrophils are now established as functionally heterogeneous cells governing diverse tissue repair processes: inflammation amplification, resolution, immune cell recruitment, and regenerative modulation [27]. Despite this growing recognition, studies on the host response to biodegradable metallic implants have focused predominantly on macrophages, whereas the early regulatory role of neutrophils in bone repair has rarely been reported [28]. Consequently, the osteoimmune basis underlying the favorable repair performance of Mo implants has yet to be clarified [29]. Conventional histological observations and bulk-level molecular analyses provide limited insight into the cellular heterogeneity and dynamic immune states at the early bone–implant interface. Single-cell RNA sequencing offers a powerful strategy for resolving these complex responses at high resolution and for identifying specific immune cell populations, functional states, and signaling programs associated with tissue regeneration [30]. This approach is increasingly recognized as a promising tool for developing next-generation biomaterials that actively modulate the local microenvironment to promote bone repair.

This study investigated the biological mechanisms underlying bone repair with a biodegradable Mo-based implant. The reparative capacity of the Mo-based implants was systematically evaluated in a fracture model over 12 weeks. Furthermore, single-cell transcriptomic profiling of the bone–implant interface was performed in a day-3 femoral medullary implantation model to analyze the local immune microenvironment, with a focus on neutrophil heterogeneity and associated signaling pathways. This work suggests that biodegradable Mo-based implants regulate the early host immune response, potentially establishing a pro-regenerative microenvironment conducive to downstream bone repair.

Materials and methods

Specimen preparation

For the in vitro studies, cylindrical disc specimens (10 mm in diameter and 1.5 mm in thickness) were machined from commercially purchased Ti-6Al-4V alloy (Grade 5, Foshan Angels Biotechnology Co., Ltd., China) and high-purity Mo (99.9 wt%, Qinghe Xiuhang Metal Materials Co., Ltd., China). For the in vivo studies, rod-shaped Mo specimens with different dimensions were prepared according to the experimental design. Specifically, cylindrical rods (φ 1 mm × 15 mm) were implanted into the rat medullary cavity and harvested at day 3 post-implantation for single-cell RNA sequencing analysis, and cylindrical IMNs (φ 1.5 mm × 25 mm) were used for fracture fixation in rats. Both Ti and Mo samples were mechanically polished with silicon carbide sandpaper from 400 to 2000 grit, followed by sequential ultrasonic cleaning in deionized water, ethanol, and acetone for 20 min each. After cleaning, specimens used for in vitro experiments were dried and sterilized by ultraviolet irradiation for 1 h before use, whereas specimens intended for animal implantation were sterilized with ethylene oxide prior to surgery.

Surface characteristics and corrosion tests

The surface morphology and elemental distribution of Mo were examined by scanning electron microscopy (SEM; JSM-IT500, JEOL, Japan) coupled with energy-dispersive spectroscopy (EDS; OXFORD X-Max 80, UK) at an accelerating voltage of 15 kV. The three-dimensional surface topography and roughness of Mo were evaluated using white light interferometry (Bruker Contour GT, Germany). The phase constitution and crystal structure of Mo were further analyzed by X-ray diffraction (XRD; X'Pert PRO, PANalytical, Almelo, the Netherlands) with Cu-Kα radiation in the 2θ range of 20°–90° at a scan rate of 8°/min.

The transient electrochemical corrosion behavior of the samples was evaluated using an electrochemical workstation (IM6, Zahner, Germany) with a conventional three-electrode setup, comprising a saturated calomel electrode as the reference electrode, a platinum plate (1.5 cm × 1.5 cm) as the counter electrode, and the test specimen as the working electrode with an exposed area of approximately 0.79 cm2. Prior to testing, each sample was connected to a copper wire and embedded in silicone rubber, leaving only the test surface exposed to the electrolyte. Electrochemical measurements were conducted in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) solution containing 8 g/L NaCl, 0.24 g/L KCl, 0.24 g/L KH2PO4, and 3.81 g/L Na2HPO4⋅12H2O, with the pH adjusted to approximately 7.4 using HCl or NaOH (Chengdu Kelong Chemical Co., Ltd., Chengdu, China). After immersion for 15 min to stabilize the open-circuit condition, potentiodynamic polarization (PDP) measurements were performed from −1.1 to 0.4 V at a scan rate of 1 mV/s. The self-corrosion potential (Ecorr) was directly obtained from the polarization curves, while the self-corrosion current density (icorr) was determined by Tafel extrapolation according to previous studies [19,31]. The corrosion rate (Vi, mm/y) was calculated from icorr according to the following equation: Vi = 5.09icorr [19]. Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) was subsequently carried out over a frequency range from 200 kHz to 0.01 Hz with a sinusoidal perturbation amplitude of 10 mV. The impedance data were fitted using ZSimpWin software (version 3.60, EChem Software, Michigan, MI, USA). At least four parallel specimens were tested for each group to ensure statistical reliability.

The in vitro immersion test was carried out in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.4) at 37 ± 0.5°C for up to 28 days. Prior to immersion, the samples were sealed with epoxy resin, leaving only the test surface exposed, and immersed in a solution at a volume-to-surface area ratio of 20 mL/cm2 [32]. The immersion medium was refreshed to maintain a relatively stable chemical environment. At predetermined time points (1, 3, 7, 14, 21, and 28 days), the surface corrosion morphology was observed and recorded (Sony Alpha 6400, Sony Corporation, Tokyo, Japan). Before each PBS refreshment, aliquots of the immersion medium were collected to determine the released Mo ion concentration. The concentration of released Mo ions was measured by inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS, NexION 300X, PerkinElmer, USA). The released Mo concentration was expressed as μg/mL, and at least three parallel samples were tested for each time point. In addition, samples retrieved after 14 and 28 days of immersion were further characterized by SEM and EDS to evaluate the surface microstructure and elemental distribution of the corrosion products. The phase composition of the corrosion products was analyzed by XRD with Cu-Kα radiation over a 2θ range of 20°–90° at days 7, 14, and 28. The chemical composition and valence states of the surface corrosion products were further examined by X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS; XSAM800, Kratos, UK) at days 7, 14, and 28. High-resolution spectra of Mo 3d, C 1s, O 1s, and P 2p were collected and deconvoluted to analyze the chemical states of the corrosion layer by XPS peak 4.1 software. The surface hydrophilicity of Mo before and after immersion was evaluated by static water contact angle measurement (DSA100, Krüss, Hamburg, Germany). Water droplets were gently deposited onto the sample surface, and the contact angle was recorded after stabilization. Measurements were performed on Mo samples before immersion and after 3, 7, 14, 21, and 28 days of immersion in PBS. The scanning Kelvin probe microscopy (SKPM, Dimension Icon, Bruker, Germany) was used to characterize the local surface potential distribution of the immersed Mo surface. SKPM measurements were performed on Mo samples after 28 days of immersion in PBS, and both two-dimensional and three-dimensional surface potential maps were recorded. Representative line profiles were extracted from the potential maps to evaluate the spatial variation in surface potential across the corrosion layer. To reveal the underlying corrosion morphology after immersion, the surface corrosion products were removed by pickling in a 200 mg/mL CrO3 solution at 80°C for 1 min, according to ASTM G1-03(2017) e1 [33].

In vivo animal study

Femur fracture model establishment and in vivo surgery

All animal procedures for the fracture implantation study were conducted in accordance with institutional ethical guidelines and were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Peking University Shenzhen Hospital (Approval No. 2024-795). Female Sprague-Dawley rats (8 weeks old, 250 g; Guangdong Medical Laboratory Animal Center) were anesthetized with isoflurane (Ante Animal Husbandry Technology Co., Ltd., Jinan, China), and a transverse osteotomy was created at the mid-shaft of the femur under sterile conditions. The fracture was stabilized by intramedullary implantation of the corresponding test rod. After surgery, the incision was closed in layers. To ensure animal welfare and minimize the impact of surgical stress, postoperative analgesia was provided using meloxicam. Rats received an intraperitoneal loading dose of meloxicam (2 mg/kg) immediately after surgery, followed by a maintenance dose of 1 mg/kg once daily for 48–72 h postoperatively, according to the recommended protocol for managing moderate-to-severe pain in rodent orthopedic procedures. For perioperative infection control, a single intraperitoneal injection of penicillin (20,000 IU per rat) was administered immediately before surgery, followed by once-daily intraperitoneal injections of penicillin (20,000 IU per rat) for three consecutive days after surgery. At 6 and 12 weeks postoperatively, the rats were re-anesthetized with isoflurane, and X-ray images of the implanted femur were obtained to evaluate fracture healing using a predefined scoring system (n = 6 per group). At the designated time points, the animals were euthanized, and the implanted femurs were harvested and fixed in 10% formalin. The femurs were scanned using a micro-computed tomography system (μCT 80, SCANCO Medical AG, Switzerland) at 70 kV, 114 μA, with an integration time of 250 ms and a resolution of 15.6 μm. Quantitative bone parameters, including bone mineral density (BMD), bone volume/total volume (BV/TV), trabecular number (Tb.N), trabecular thickness (Tb.Th), and trabecular separation (Tb.Sp), were analyzed in the region surrounding the fracture (n = 6 per group). After μCT analysis, the femurs were decalcified in 10% ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid for 2 months, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned into 5-μm slices for hematoxylin and eosin (H&E), Masson's trichrome, Goldner's trichrome, and immunohistochemical (IHC) staining. Immunohistochemical staining was performed for p-AKT, BMP-2, and COL I (n = 6 per group), and the stained sections were imaged using a high-resolution microscope (Olympus Co., Ltd.). For integrated optical density (IOD) quantification, three non-overlapping fields from the fracture callus region of each specimen were analyzed using ImageJ under consistent threshold settings. Areas with folding, tearing, or obvious staining artifacts were excluded. The mean IOD value from three fields was used as one biological replicate, and six specimens were analyzed per group.

In vivo degradation behavior of IMNs

Prior to decalcification, the IMNs retrieved at 6 and 12 weeks post-implantation were carefully extracted, rinsed three times with deionized water, and dried in a vacuum oven. The gross surface corrosion morphology of the retrieved IMNs was first recorded by optical microscopy. The surface microstructure and elemental composition were then further characterized by SEM and EDS, as described above.

Rat femoral medullary implantation model for single-cell RNA sequencing

Rat femoral medullary implantation model establishment

All animal procedures described in this section were conducted in strict accordance with institutional ethical guidelines and were approved by the local Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (Approval No. 202401012 and Approval No. GY2023-721). Eight-week-old Sprague-Dawley rats weighing 250 ± 50 g were randomly assigned to three groups: a control group (untreated bone defect), a Ti implant group, and a Mo implant group (n = 3 per group). The animals were anesthetized by intraperitoneal injection of Zoletil 50 (30 mg/kg) and xylazine hydrochloride (5 mg/kg). Under aseptic conditions, a 2-mm-diameter defect penetrating into the bone marrow cavity was created in the femoral condyle using a drill. In the Ti and Mo groups, cylindrical metal implants were inserted into the bone marrow cavity, whereas the control group received defect creation only without implantation. The surgical incisions were closed with 4-0 absorbable sutures. Postoperative pain management and infection prophylaxis were performed as described in Section 2.3.1. At postoperative day 3, the rats were euthanized by CO2 inhalation in accordance with the guidelines of the American Veterinary Medical Association. Regenerative tissues, including the femora and adjacent bone marrow tissues, were harvested for subsequent single-cell RNA sequencing analysis. The scRNA-seq libraries were constructed using the DNBelab C4 Series Single-Cell RNA-Seq platform.

scRNA-seq analysis

Following a standardized protocol, single-cell suspensions were prepared from regenerative tissues harvested on postoperative day 3. The experimental protocol was used, as previous studies reported [34]. In particular, the enzymatic digestion solution was prepared according to the tissue type and prewarmed in a 37°C water bath. Tissue samples were removed from 4°C storage, centrifuged, and the preservation medium was aspirated. The tissue was washed twice with DMEM, then rapidly minced into 2–3 mm fragments using a sterile scalpel. The minced tissue was transferred into a 15 mL conical tube containing the prewarmed digestion solution and incubated in a 37°C water bath. At 15–20 min intervals, the suspension was gently pipetted several times to facilitate mechanical dissociation. After 30 min, an aliquot was taken and stained with trypan blue to monitor the digestion status under a light microscope. Digestion was terminated when no obvious tissue clumps remained. The resulting cell suspension was filtered through a 40 μm cell strainer. Residual cells on the strainer were washed with 2–3 mL of ice-cold complete medium, and the flow-through was collected into a new centrifuge tube. The filtrate was centrifuged at 300 × g for 5 min at 4°C, and the supernatant was discarded. Based on the tissue type and the color of the cell pellet, an appropriate volume of room-temperature red blood cell lysis buffer was added to resuspend the pellet, followed by a 2–5 min incubation to lyse erythrocytes. An equal volume of ice-cold PBS containing 0.04% bovine serum albumin (BSA) was added to stop the lysis reaction. The suspension was centrifuged at 300 × g for 5 min at 4°C, and the supernatant was removed. Trypan blue staining was performed to assess cell viability and morphology. If necessary, a Dead Cell Removal Kit was used to eliminate dead cells and debris according to the manufacturer's instructions. The final cell pellet was washed once with PBS-BSA (0.04%), centrifuged at 300 × g for 5 min at 4°C, and resuspended in an appropriate volume of PBS-BSA (0.04%). Cell number and viability were determined using a hemocytometer.

Regarding data analysis, after quality control filtering (nFeature_RNA: 500–2500; nCount_RNA < 10,000; mitochondrial gene ratio < 10%), a total of 48,017 cells were retained for downstream analysis. The filtered cells were normalized, and variable features were identified and scaled using Seurat (v5.2.1). Principal component analysis (PCA) was then performed, and batch effects among samples were corrected using Harmony integration. Unsupervised clustering was conducted based on the Louvain algorithm (resolution = 0.5), and the cell populations were visualized by Uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) using dimensions 1-15, revealing 20 transcriptionally distinct clusters. Cell identities were annotated according to canonical marker genes and differentially expressed genes (DEGs). To explore functional heterogeneity, differential gene expression analysis was performed using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test (min.pct = 0.25), and cluster-specific marker genes were further identified. In addition, cell type proportion analysis was carried out to compare the relative abundance of different cell populations among the control, Ti implant, and Mo implant groups.

Histological staining

Femoral specimens were decalcified, dehydrated, paraffin-embedded, and sectioned as described above (Section 2.3.1). Paraffin sections (3 μm) were mounted on positively charged slides for histological, IHC, and immunofluorescence (IF) analyses. For H&E staining, the sections were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and stained following a standard protocol. After dehydration and clearing, the slides were coverslipped with neutral balsam and observed under a light microscope (DM2500, Leica). For IHC and IF staining, deparaffinized sections underwent antigen retrieval in citrate buffer. For IHC, endogenous peroxidase activity was blocked with hydrogen peroxide, followed by incubation with 3% bovine serum albumin (BSA) for 30 min. The sections were then incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies against Sdc1 (Bioss, bs-1039R), Elane (Bioss, bs-23549R), Cd68 (Servicebio, GB113109), and Fn1 (Servicebio, GB114491), followed by incubation with the appropriate secondary antibodies for 1 h at 25°C. For IF, the nuclei were counterstained with DAPI, and the sections were mounted in glycerol. Fluorescence images were captured using an upright microscope (DM2500, Leica).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism (version 10.0) and R Studio. For comparisons between two groups, unpaired, two-tailed Student's t-tests were employed. Comparisons involving more than two groups were analyzed by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). A p-value of less than 0.05 was defined as the threshold for statistical significance.

Results

Surface characteristics and corrosion tests

Fig. S1 shows the initial surface characteristics of the as-prepared Mo sample. The surface was relatively smooth with uniform polishing traces, and EDS mapping indicated that Mo was the dominant element with only minor C and O signals. White light interferometry revealed a homogeneous surface topography with a root mean square roughness of 132.2 nm, while XRD confirmed that the sample was mainly composed of metallic Mo with a weak MoO2 peak.

Fig. 1 presents the long-term electrochemical corrosion behavior and surface degradation evolution of Mo in PBS, as well as the corrosion morphology of the retrieved Mo IMNs after 6 and 12 weeks of implantation. Fig. 1A shows the PDP curves of Mo after different immersion periods in PBS. With increasing immersion time, the polarization curves shifted toward more negative potentials, accompanied by a clear increase in current density relative to the unimmersed state (Table S1). From 14 days onward, a pronounced anodic passivation plateau emerged, and the passivation region became progressively wider as immersion proceeded, suggesting the gradual establishment of an oxide-based surface layer [12,19]. At the later stages, slight current transients appeared in the high anodic polarization region, indicating continuous dynamic evolution of the surface film during prolonged oxidation and dissolution [35]. Fig. S2 shows that the corrosion rate of Mo increased after immersion in PBS relative to the unimmersed condition, while the overall corrosion rates remained within a range of approximately 13–40 μm/year. The Nyquist plots in Fig. 1B showed a gradual decrease in capacitive arc radius during immersion, which can be related to the continuous evolution of the surface corrosion products. This change also indicated a faster corrosion processes at later stages, in agreement with the polarization results. The bode-impedance plot in Fig. S3 shows a similar trend to the Nyquist spectra. Fig. 1C shows the corresponding Bode phase-angle plots. The phase response changed progressively with immersion time, particularly in the intermediate- and low-frequency regions, confirming the formation and subsequent evolution of the surface corrosion layer. The EIS spectra were fitted using the equivalent circuit of Rs(Qp(Rp(Qct(Rct)))), Rs represents the solution resistance; Qp and Rp correspond to the constant phase element and resistance of the surface corrosion-product/passive layer, respectively; and Qct and Rct represent the constant phase element and charge-transfer resistance at the interface. The fitted parameters are summarized in Table S1. Overall, both Rp and Rct decreased progressively with immersion time, indicating reduced protectiveness of the surface layer and facilitated interfacial charge transfer during prolonged immersion. Meanwhile, Qp and Rp also showed an overall downward trend, further suggesting continuous evolution of the corrosion-product layer and the interfacial electrochemical state. The macroscopic and microscopic observations revealed the progressive surface transformation and formation of corrosion products on Mo during immersion in PBS (Fig. 1D and E). The as-prepared Mo surface showed a metallic luster and a relatively smooth appearance. After 3 days of immersion, the surface became dark brown, and then gradually turned gray after 7–14 days. At 21 and 28 days, the surface appeared lighter and more homogeneous, indicating continuous surface transformation during immersion. Consistent with this visual change, the water contact angle decreased from approximately 65° before immersion to approximately 19° after 3 days of immersion and remained lower than that of the pristine Mo surface after 28 days (Fig. S4), indicating improved surface hydrophilicity after corrosion-product formation. SEM observation at 14 and 28 days revealed that the surface was covered by a continuous corrosion layer with a typical mud-crack-like morphology. EDS mapping demonstrated the relatively uniform distribution of Mo, O, P, Na, and C within the corrosion layer. Notably, the P signal was more pronounced at 28 days than at 14 days, indicating progressive incorporation of phosphate species from PBS into the surface products. The XRD patterns in Fig. 1F showed the characteristic peaks of the Mo substrate at all immersion time points. In addition, the main corrosion products were identified as MoO2 and MoO3. A peak assigned to Mo (OH)3PO4 was detected at 28 days, further confirming the participation of phosphate species in the corrosion products, which was consistent with the EDS results. XPS survey spectra (Fig. 1G) further confirmed the presence of O, C, Mo, and P on the surface. The atomic composition analysis (Table S2) showed slight changes in surface elemental contents during immersion, accompanied by a gradual increase in P content, suggesting the deposition of phosphate-containing corrosion products on the Mo surface. High-resolution Mo 3d spectra (Fig. 1H) showed the coexistence of metallic Mo and multiple oxidized Mo species, demonstrating progressive surface oxidation during immersion. The C 1s spectra (Fig. 1I) mainly contained C–C and C–O components, together with carbonate-related species, whereas the O 1s spectra (Fig. 1J) could be deconvoluted into contributions from H2O/O-C, Mo–O, and phosphate-related oxygen. The high-resolution P 2p spectra (Fig. S5) showed a characteristic phosphate-related peak, and the relative P content increased with prolonged immersion, indicating the gradual accumulation of phosphate-containing corrosion products on the surface. The released Mo concentration gradually increased during 28 days of immersion in PBS (Fig. S6), confirming sustained Mo dissolution. SKPM analysis showed a heterogeneous surface potential distribution after 28 days, with values ranging from 751.4 mV to 917.8 mV (Fig. S7). The representative line profile further confirmed local potential fluctuations across the corrosion layer. These results indicated that immersion in PBS induced continuous Mo ion release and the formation of a composite corrosion layer composed primarily of molybdenum oxides/hydroxides together with phosphate-containing species, accompanied by enhanced surface hydrophilicity and increased local surface-potential heterogeneity.

The in vivo results showed that Mo IMNs retained their gross structural integrity after implantation, while undergoing continuous surface degradation (Fig. 1K). At both 6 and 12 weeks, the nails remained macroscopically intact without obvious fracture or severe localized failure. However, SEM revealed the presence of a surface reaction layer with local cracks on the retrieved implants. EDS mapping detected Ca, P, O, C, and N in addition to Mo, suggesting the formation of a biologically derived interfacial layer containing inorganic and organic components under physiological conditions. Compared with the 6-week samples, the 12-week samples exhibited a reduced Mo signal and relatively enriched Ca- and C-containing deposits, indicating ongoing surface transformation and progressive accumulation of tissue- and mineral-related species in vivo. After removal of the corrosion products, the underlying surface showed a roughened morphology with network-like boundaries and local porous features (Fig. S8). These observations indicated that in vivo degradation had occurred beneath the corrosion layer, although part of the porous morphology can originate from the intrinsic sintered microstructure of the Mo substrate.

In vitro cellular responses to Mo ions and extracts

The Mo concentration in the 100% extract was approximately 3.0 μg/mL (Fig. S9A). MoCl5 at 0.1–0.5 mmol/L showed no obvious cytotoxicity toward BMSCs or RAW264.7 macrophages, whereas cell viability decreased markedly at concentrations of ≥1 mmol/L (Figs. S9B, C, J, K). The 100%, 50%, and 25% extracts maintained BMSC viability and normal cytoskeletal morphology (Fig. S9D–F). ALP activity and matrix mineralization generally increased with extract dilution, with the 25% extract showing the strongest osteogenic effect (Fig. S9G–I). Under LPS stimulation, all extract concentrations increased CD206 expression and decreased iNOS expression, suggesting that the Mo extract attenuated the pro-inflammatory macrophage phenotype and promoted anti-inflammatory polarization (Fig. S9L–N).

In vivo performance of femur fracture healing

Fig. 2 demonstrates that Mo IMNs promoted superior fracture repair compared with Ti in vivo. X-ray images showed more advanced callus bridging and less distinct fracture gaps in the Mo group at both 6 and 12 weeks (Fig. 2A). In parallel, fracture healing was further evaluated using the grading system for femoral shaft fracture healing (Table S3), which showed significantly higher scores in the Mo group than in the Ti group at both 6 and 12 weeks, indicating accelerated healing progression (Fig. 2B). Consistently, micro-CT reconstruction revealed better structural continuity at the fracture site in the Mo group (Fig. 2C). Quantitative analysis further confirmed that Mo significantly increased the values of BMD (Fig. S9), BV/TV, Tb.N, and Tb.Th, while decreasing Tb.Sp, relative to Ti at both time points (Fig. 2D–G). Histological results were in good agreement with the radiographic results. H&E staining showed more abundant newly formed bone (NB) and improved bridging of the fracture region in the Mo group at both 6 and 12 weeks (Fig. 2H). Masson's trichrome and Goldner's staining further revealed greater extracellular matrix deposition and more extensive mineralized bone formation in the Mo group, particularly at 12 weeks, indicating enhanced callus maturation and bone remodeling (Fig. 2I and J). Immunohistochemical staining demonstrated stronger BMP-2, Col I, and p-AKT signals in the Mo group than in the Ti group (Fig. 2K–M), and quantitative IOD analysis confirmed significantly higher expression of these markers at both time points (Fig. 2N–P). Analysis of cell composition revealed that both macrophages and neutrophils were enriched in the Mo group relative to the Ti group (Fig. 3F). Ligand–receptor interaction analysis was conducted to map the intercellular communication network across immune cell subsets. In the chord diagram, neutrophils (green) function as the sender population, delivering signals to other color-coded cell types; band width is proportional to interaction strength. The analysis revealed a stronger communication network between macrophages and neutrophils compared to other cell pairs, suggesting that myeloid cell interactions may represent a prominent feature of the early peri-implant immune microenvironment (Fig. 3G).

To validate the single-cell findings, IHC staining was performed on peri-implant bone sections collected at the early post-implantation stage. More abundant Elane neutrophils and Cd68+ macrophages were observed around Mo implants than around Ti implants, and quantitative analysis confirmed significantly increased positive-cell infiltration in the Mo group (Fig. 3H). Further GO analysis revealed distinct functional signatures for each neutrophil subset, with the top enriched terms highlighting their specialized roles (Fig. 4D). The Cd177+ subset was primarily associated with cellular motility and lipid metabolism, enriched for terms including positive regulation of cell adhesion, regulation of endocytosis, glycerolipid metabolic process, and small GTPase-mediated signal transduction. This functional profile points to specialization in migration, phagocytosis, and lipid synthesis, suggesting roles in chemotaxis toward inflammatory sites and tissue remodeling. The Il1b+ subset was enriched for antiviral immune responses, with top terms such as cytokine-mediated signaling pathway, leukocyte-mediated immunity, leukocyte migration, and defense response to virus, executing defense functions through cytokine signaling and leukocyte migration. The Defa11+ subset demonstrated involvement in cell cycle and DNA replication, characterized by terms including chromosome segregation, DNA replication, nuclear chromosome segregation, and sister chromatid segregation, representing a proliferative precursor state responsible for population expansion through cell division. The Prg2+ subset was linked to energy metabolism and translational preparedness, with top terms comprising ribosome biogenesis, aerobic respiration, protein-RNA complex organization, and cytoplasmic translation, exhibiting high metabolic demand and protein synthesis capacity to support effector functions. The Elane+ subset was characterized by ribosome biogenesis and RNA processing, enriched for terms including ribosome biogenesis, rRNA metabolic process, rRNA processing, and mRNA processing, focusing on post-transcriptional regulation to prepare components for protein synthesis.

To further characterize the functional state of Cd177+ neutrophils, we performed module scoring analysis using custom gene sets representing key functional programs: Inflammatory, NETs formation, Resolution, and Migration. As shown in Fig. S10, Cd177+ neutrophils in the Mo microenvironment exhibited lower module scores for Inflammatory and NETs pathways compared with the Ti group, while the Resolution pathway score was higher in the Mo group. The Migration pathway also showed a higher trend in the Mo group. These transcriptomic data indicated that Cd177+ neutrophils in the Mo group are transcriptionally skewed toward a non-inflammatory and pro-resolving phenotype.

Macrophage heterogeneity, identified through unsupervised clustering, revealed six distinct subpopulations: Lyz2+, Mki67+, Edil3+, Spp1+, Itgax+, and Cd163+. T-SNE analysis confirmed clear transcriptional and functional segregation among these subsets (Fig. 4E). Their relative abundance underwent significant shifts under Ti and Mo conditions (Fig. 4F). The Lyz2+ subset constituted the predominant population. The Mo group tended to have higher proportions of the Spp1+ and Cd163+ subsets compared with the other two groups (Fig. 4F). Finally, the Cd163+ subset, highlighted by Cd163, C1qa, and Mrc1.

Subsequent GO analysis revealed distinct functional signatures for each macrophage subset, with the top enriched terms highlighting their specialized roles (Fig. 4H). The Lyz2+ subset was primarily associated with inflammatory initiation and leukocyte recruitment, enriched for terms including cytoplasmic translation, positive regulation of the inflammatory response, leukocyte chemotaxis, and positive regulation of interleukin-1 production, driving early immune responses and chemotaxis during infection. The Mki67+ subset was closely linked to chromosome segregation and cellular proliferation, with top terms such as chromosome segregation, mitotic sister chromatid segregation, sister chromatid segregation, and DNA replication, representing an actively dividing state that supports population expansion through DNA replication. The Spp1+ subset was functionally enriched in antigen processing and presentation as well as cell guidance, with top terms comprising antigen processing and presentation, leukocyte migration, antigen processing and presentation of peptide antigen, and chemotaxis, specializing in bridging innate and adaptive immunity through antigen presentation and directed migration. Potential involvement of the Fn1-Sdc1 axis in neutrophil-macrophage communication associated with Mo implants

Analysis of neutrophil-macrophage crosstalk suggested that the strength of key ligand-receptor interactions varied with the implant material. Furthermore, these cellular interactions were differentially mediated by specific neutrophil subsets. A marked difference in these cellular interactions was observed under Ti and Mo conditions. Notably, the most pronounced intercellular communication occurred between the neutrophil subset Cd177+ and the macrophage subset Spp1+ in the presence of Mo (Fig. 5A). Analysis of the interaction intensity heatmap revealed that the crosstalk between Cd177+ neutrophils and Spp1+ macrophages were stronger in the Mo conditions (Fig. 5B), where red blocks indicate increased interaction strength in the Mo group compared with the Ti group. This interaction was transcriptionally characterized by elevated expression of Fn1 in Cd177+ neutrophils and concomitant upregulation of its cognate receptor Sdc1 in Spp1+ macrophages, as illustrated in the ligand-receptor matrix. The Cd177+–Spp1+ axis exhibited robust communication, with the highest interaction score detected in the Mo group, mediated by binding of Fn1 to Sdc1 (Fig. 5C). Molecular dynamics simulations were employed to validate this interaction and predict the Fn1-Sdc1 binding interface, which predicted that Fn1 embeds into the structural groove of Sdc1 (Fig. 5D). Multiplex immunofluorescence staining further revealed apparent spatial proximity between Fn1 and Sdc1 specifically around Mo implants (Fig. 5E). By contrast, no comparable interaction signal was detected in Ti implanted samples. Discussion

This study provides a more specific immune-centered explanation for the pro-regenerative effect of biodegradable metallic Mo during bone repair. Previous studies on Mo-based biomaterials have consistently demonstrated osteoinduction potential; yet, mechanistic interpretations have focused on the direct actions of Mo-derived cues on BMSCs, osteogenic cells, endothelial cells, or osteoclast precursors [13,[36], [37], [38]]. Much less is known about how Mo reorganizes the local immune niche immediately after implantation, particularly which innate immune cells respond first and how these early responders subsequently shape the downstream repair cascade.

By focusing on day 3 after implantation, our study shows that the response to Mo-based IMNs is not simply an increase in inflammatory cell infiltration, but rather a coordinated remodeling of the early peri-implant immune ecosystem centered on neutrophils and macrophages. More importantly, we identified Cd177+ neutrophils and Spp1+ macrophages as representative subsets within this process and further implicated the Fn1-Sdc1 axis as a potential communication route linking them. These findings suggest that the pro-osteogenic activity of Mo is not limited to direct stimulation of bone-forming cells, but importantly, also involves an upstream reshaping of the innate immune niche, which is then translated into a more favorable osteogenic microenvironment (Fig. 6) [39,40].Fig. 6Schematic illustration of the candidate mechanism by which the Mo-based intramedullary nail promotes fracture healing, as suggested by neutrophil–macrophage-mediated osteoimmune regulation identified in the day-3 implantation model.

The degradation products of Mo implants serve as the initial trigger for the observed immune cascade. As shown in Fig. 1, Mo undergoes gradual biodegradation, releasing soluble ions and particles into the peri-implant microenvironment—an active process that distinguishes the Mo surface from the relatively inert Ti surface. The direct regulation of macrophages by Mo degradation products has been partly demonstrated in our previous study. The degradation products from biodegradable Mo implants affected RAW264.7 macrophage morphology, Mo ion uptake, macrophage polarization markers, IL-6 secretion, and the expression of inflammation- and oxidation-related genes, including Tnf, Il6, Il1b, and Hmox1 [36]. These findings indicate that Mo degradation products can directly modulate macrophage-related inflammatory responses in vitro.

Consistently, other studies have suggested that the biological effects of Mo degradation products are concentration- and time-dependent, and that moderate Mo ion concentrations can regulate macrophage polarization through mitochondrial and metabolic reprogramming, thereby steering innate immune cells toward a more reparative state [37,41]. Building on these previous in vitro findings, the present study extends the mechanistic understanding of Mo-mediated immunomodulation to the early in vivo bone–implant interface. Our scRNA-seq and histological results suggest that Mo implantation not only influences macrophage-related responses but also reshapes neutrophil–macrophage communication, highlighting a coordinated myeloid-cell response during early osteoimmune regulation. However, the distinct immune landscapes between Mo and Ti implants should not be attributed solely to Mo degradation products. Mo and Ti also differ in their intrinsic surface physicochemical properties, including surface energy, roughness, and wettability, which may independently affect protein adsorption patterns, particularly the conformation of adsorbed fibrinogen and fibronectin, and thereby modulate subsequent cell adhesion and inflammatory-cell behavior [42,43]. Therefore, both Mo degradation products and material-dependent surface properties may contribute to the distinct early immune microenvironment observed around Mo implants.

The local immune response plays a central role in determining the outcome of bone repair. [[44], [45], [46]]. In our results, the identification of the Spp1+ macrophage subset provides a candidate cellular basis for Mo-mediated bone repair. Although macrophages are often broadly categorized as M1 or M2 [47], accumulating single-cell evidence indicates that macrophage states during tissue repair are substantially more diverse and functionally specialized than this binary framework suggests [[48], [49], [50]]. Spp1 encodes osteopontin, a matrix-associated molecule with well-established roles in bone remodeling, cell adhesion, and osteogenic regulation [51,52]. Notably, recent work has shown that osteoclast-derived extracellular vesicles enriched in cleaved Spp1 can promote MSC osteogenic differentiation and bone repair through TGFβ1/SMAD3 signaling [53]. In our dataset, Spp1+ macrophages exhibited a transcriptional profile suggestive of tissue remodeling and osteogenic modulation; consistent with this, a specialized Spp1+ macrophage subpopulation has been identified as a key driver of ECM remodeling in biomaterial-induced soft tissue regeneration [54]. Their enrichment in the Mo group correlates with accelerated fracture healing, suggesting this subset may contribute to shaping a pro-regenerative microenvironment.

Beyond macrophages, the early innate immune response to Mo implantation is also characterized by a prominent neutrophil component. The choice of day 3 for scRNA-seq analysis was based on well-established temporal dynamics of fracture healing, in which the transition from neutrophil-dominated to macrophage-dominated responses occurs around this time point [55]. At this stage, the provisional extracellular matrix—particularly fibronectin—is actively deposited by infiltrating neutrophils, providing structural support and signaling cues for incoming macrophages [56]. This interpretation is consistent with the emerging view that neutrophils function as early organizers of the repair niche rather than merely transient inflammatory bystanders [[57], [58], [59]]. Notably, not all cell types increased in the Mo group; however, neutrophils were significantly enriched compared with the Ti group, suggesting a specific response of this population to Mo implantation. Given that the early regulatory role of neutrophils in biodegradable metal-induced bone regeneration remains largely unexplored, we considered this subset to be of particular interest and worthy of focused investigation.

Neutrophils are the initial defense cells in the implant reaction, determining the following immune cascade. In our results, the enrichment of Cd177+ neutrophils and the strengthened Fn1-Sdc1 signaling suggest that neutrophils responding to Mo implantation may actively reshape the local ECM niche through Fn1 secretion. Although the precise biological function of Cd177 in neutrophils is not fully defined, this GPI-anchored protein has been implicated in neutrophil adhesion, transendothelial migration, and modulation of β2 integrin signaling [60]. Within this sequential regulatory process, the Fn1-Sdc1 axis may act as a key molecular bridge connecting neutrophil-mediated ECM remodeling to downstream macrophage functional reprogramming. Fn1 is not only a structural ECM component but also a regulator of cell adhesion, migration, and environmental sensing [61,62]. Sdc1, as a transmembrane heparan sulfate proteoglycan, participates in ECM organization and ligand retention while also directly modulating macrophage motility and inflammatory resolution [63]. Previous work has shown that Sdc1 deficiency impairs macrophage migration and promotes a more persistent inflammatory state [61]. Thus, the co-enrichment of Cd177+ neutrophils and Spp1+ macrophages, together with the strengthened Fn1-Sdc1 communication, supports a model in which Mo-shaped early immune cells establish a regenerative niche favorable for BMSCs recruitment and osteogenic commitment [[64], [65], [66]].

The transition from early osteoimmune remodeling to BMSCs/osteoblast activation is a critical step in the proposed cascade. Although direct causal evidence is lacking in our dataset, previous studies have shown that neutrophils recruit mesenchymal progenitors via the SDF-1/CXCR4 axis [59], while pro-reparative macrophages promote BMSCs osteogenic differentiation through paracrine signaling. In addition, Mo ions have been demonstrated to directly enhance BMSCs proliferation, ALP activity, and osteogenic marker expression, including Runx2 and Bmp2 [13]. Thus, while our data emphasize an immune-mediated mechanism, a direct contributory effect of Mo ions on osteogenic cells cannot be excluded. However, neutrophils are functionally plastic, and their net contribution to tissue repair is highly context-dependent [67]. In our scRNA-seq dataset, module scoring analysis revealed that Cd177+ neutrophils in the Mo microenvironment exhibited lower enrichment of inflammatory and NETs-associated programs, alongside higher scores for pro-resolving and migratory signatures (Fig. S10). This transcriptomic profile suggests a non-lytic, pro-homeostatic phenotype at day 3, which aligns with the absence of histological evidence of excessive tissue damage in Mo-treated tissues. These transcriptomic findings provide a basis for future functional studies aimed at defining the precise role of Cd177+ neutrophils in Mo-induced bone repair [67,68].

Several limitations should also be acknowledged. First, although day 3 was selected to capture the critical transition window of the early immune response, we analyzed only this single time point, which allowed us to capture the initial immunological response to Mo implantation but not the later dynamic stages of callus formation, remodeling, and continued material degradation. Second, Ti was used as the principal inert control, while other biodegradable metals such as Zn or Mg were not included for side-by-side comparison; therefore, our conclusions are currently best interpreted as defining the distinctive immunoregulatory features of Mo relative to an inert implant. Third, while our data suggest a potential Fn1-Sdc1 interaction, the immunofluorescence evidence is spatial and correlative. Further functional intervention studies are needed to establish causality. Fourth, although our data suggest a role for Mo degradation products—including both soluble ions and solid particles—in shaping the peri-implant immune microenvironment, the relative contribution of ionic versus particulate species and the potential cellular responses to particulate degradation products were not directly evaluated in this study. Fifth, while the functional healing outcome was assessed in a femoral shaft fracture model, our single-cell transcriptomic profiling was performed in a femoral implantation model at day 3. Thus, the mechanistic findings should be interpreted as candidate mechanisms derived from the implantation model.

Overall, the present study supports a more concrete working model in which early degradation/interface-derived cues from Mo first reshape neutrophil states, Cd177+ neutrophils contribute to provisional matrix reconstruction through Fn1, and this remodeled niche subsequently directs the recruitment and functional transition of Spp1+ macrophages through the Fn1-Sdc1 axis, thereby promoting inflammatory resolution, tissue remodeling, and osteogenic initiation. This framework extends the role of Mo from a simple direct osteogenic stimulator to an organizer of early immune-reparative programs during fracture healing [69].

Conclusion

This study identifies a candidate mechanism by which Mo promotes bone repair through the regulation of neutrophil-macrophage crosstalk in the early peri-implant microenvironment. Single-cell transcriptomic analysis in a day-3 femoral implantation model, combined with histological staining revealed that Mo implants are associated with the enrichment of neutrophils and macrophages in the peri-implant microenvironment, wherein Cd177+ neutrophils may interact with Spp1+ macrophages via the Fn1-Sdc1 ligand-receptor axis to potentially facilitate bone healing. These findings point to a potential immunomodulatory mechanism underlying Mo-mediated bone repair and provide critical insights for the future optimization and design of Mo-based biomaterials aimed at enhancing bone repair.
