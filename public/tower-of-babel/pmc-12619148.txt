Prevalence of Ureaplasma urealyticum, Chlamydia trachomatis, Neisseria gonorrhoeae and Herpes simplex virus in Hangzhou, China
Authors: Yongle Zhang, Runan Zhang, Li Mao, Xinping Shi, Fei Qin, Weifeng Yao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe aim of this study was to analyze the prevalence of common sexually transmitted infections, including Ureaplasma urealyticum (UU), Chlamydia trachomatis (CT), Neisseria gonorrhoeae (NG) and herpes simplex virus (HSV), in Hangzhou, China.MethodsAn analysis was conducted on 12,505 patients who visited the outpatient department of Tongde Hospital of Zhejiang Province from 2018 to 2024. Urinary and reproductive swabs were collected, and the deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA) contents of UU, CT, NG, and HSV 1/2 were detected via real-time polymerase chain reaction (PCR).ResultsAmong 12,505 outpatients, the age-adjusted prevalence was 30.93% for UU, 10.33% for CT, 11.51% for NG, and 10.64% for HSV. UU infection is prevalent among individuals aged 20 to 59, whereas CT and NG infections are most commonly observed in the 15 to 19 age group. UU and HSV infections primarily affected women, whereas CT and NG infections were more prevalent among men. The prevalence of HSV-2 infection was significantly positively correlated with age. Additionally, co-infection with UU and CT (16.61‰) was the most prevalent. UU was positively associated with CT, CT was positively associated with NG, while UU was negatively associated with NG. The age-adjusted prevalence of CT and NG, as well as the age-adjusted prevalence of UU in women, declined significantly in 2020 and then gradually increased thereafter.ConclusionsThis study furthers understanding of the epidemiological characteristics of UU, NG, CT, and HSV among outpatients in Hangzhou, providing a scientific basis for optimizing regional screening strategies and formulating effective prevention and control plans.Clinical trialNot applicable.Keywords: Sexually transmitted infections, Ureaplasma urealyticum, Chlamydia trachomatis, Neisseria gonorrhoeae, Herpes simplex virus, Prevalence

Introduction

Sexually transmitted infections (STIs) are infections caused by various pathogens transmitted through vaginal, anal, or oral sex. Over 30 different bacteria, viruses, and parasites can cause these infections, and some of these pathogens may also be transmitted from mother to child during pregnancy, childbirth, and breastfeeding. According to data from the World Health Organization (WHO), there were an estimated 374 million new cases of curable STIs globally in 2020 among individuals aged 15 to 49, including infections caused by chlamydia, gonorrhea, syphilis, and trichomoniasis [1]. Additionally, in 2016, more than 500 million people in this age group were infected with the genital herpes simplex virus (HSV) [2]. In China, the reported incidence rate of syphilis increased from 26.86 per 100,000 in 2010 to 37.60 per 100,000 in 2023 [3]. STIs continue to be a significant public health concern both globally and in China.

Ureaplasma urealyticum (UU) is a member of the Ureaplasma genus and is recognized as the smallest known free-living microorganism [4]. Due to its unique biological characteristics and potential for pathogenicity, UU has become one of the most extensively studied and controversial organisms in STI research [5]. Chlamydia trachomatis (CT), a Gram-negative bacterium measuring 0.2–1.4 µm in diameter, is classified as an obligate intracellular pathogen and is acknowledged as the most common curable sexually transmitted microorganism [6, 7]. Neisseria gonorrhoeae (NG) is the pathogenic agent of gonorrhea [8]. In recent years, the rise of antibiotic resistance among STIs, especially NG, has generated significant concern, as effective treatment options are increasingly limited [9]. HSV is classified into two types: HSV-1, which typically causes severe primary infections (including oral-facial and genital infections) with a relatively low recurrence rate, and HSV-2, which generally presents as a mild genital infection but has a higher recurrence rate [10].

UU, CT, and HSV are considered primary pathogens responsible for non-gonococcal urethritis (NGU). Between 1999 and 2000, the incidence rates of genital herpes and NGU in China both increased by more than 40%, with NGU cases exceeding those of gonorrhea by 2001, making it the most frequently reported disease among eight sexually transmitted infections [11]. These pathogens not only cause direct inflammation of the urogenital system but may also increase the risks of preterm birth, infertility, and neonatal encephalitis. Since the 1980s, symptomatic diagnostic methods have been commonly employed to identify STIs; however, UU, CT, NG, and HSV often present as asymptomatic or exhibit only non-specific symptoms in their early stages. This contributes significantly to the rising incidence of STIs. Despite advances in molecular detection technologies that have improved pathogen detection efficiency, their effectiveness in addressing the increasing incidence of STIs remains limited. Thus, there is an urgent need for medical and public health professionals to conduct in-depth studies on the epidemiological characteristics of UU, CT, NG, and HSV to facilitate early identification and intervention. Currently, reports on the prevalence of these pathogens, including both HSV-1 and HSV-2, in the Hangzhou area of China are still limited.

In this study, we retrospectively analyzed outpatients at Tongde Hospital of Zhejiang Province, focusing on the prevalence characteristics of four common STI pathogens (UU, CT, NG, and HSV) from 2018 to 2024. Our aim is to provide scientific evidence that will optimize regional screening strategies and develop effective prevention and control programs.

Patients and methods

Study populations

This study included 12,505 outpatients (7101 males, median age 31; 5404 females, median age 30) who were suspected by clinicians to be infected with STIs. These patients were treated at Tongde Hospital of Zhejiang Province between January 1, 2018, and December 31, 2024, and underwent real-time fluorescence polymerase chain reaction (PCR) testing for at least one of the following pathogens: UU, CT, NG, and/or HSV. The inclusion criteria for the study were as follows: (a) male or female residents of the Hangzhou area.; (b) males or females who underwent initial PCR testing for UU, CT, NG, and/or HSV between 2018 and 2024. The exclusion criteria included: (a) males or females aged less than 15 years; (b) individuals undergoing a second or subsequent round of nucleic acid screening for UU, CT, NG, and/or HSV; (c) individuals who had used antibiotics or antiviral medications within two weeks prior to sample collection.

Specimen collection and testing for UU, CT, NG and HSV

Sample collection was performed using commercially available swab collection kits (Kangwei Medical & Health Supplies Factory, Zhuji, China), adhering to gender-specific protocols. For males, a sterile swab was inserted into the urethra to collect urethral discharge. For females, a sterile swab was inserted into the cervical canal to collect cervical discharge. 2 mL of sterile saline was added to the collection tubes and mixed by shaking. The following real-time PCR kits were utilized according to the manufacturer’s instructions: UU real-time PCR kit, CT real-time PCR kit, NG real-time PCR kit, and HSV I & II real-time PCR kit (Liferiver Biotech). For processing, 1 mL of each sample was centrifuged at 13,000 rpm for 5 minutes at room temperature. The supernatant was discarded, and the precipitate was washed twice with sterile saline. After the second wash, the precipitate was resuspended in 50 µL of nucleic acid extract and heated at 100 °C for 10 minutes. Following another centrifugation at 13,000 rpm for 5 minutes, the supernatant was collected and used as the PCR template.

The reaction mixture was prepared according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Amplification was carried out on an ABI Prism Model 7500 fluorescence quantitative PCR instrument under the following conditions: 2 minutes at 37 °C, 2 minutes at 94 °C, followed by 40 cycles at 93 °C for 15 seconds and 60 °C for 60 seconds.

Prior to the evaluation of experimental results, quality control (QC) was conducted. The negative control required a cycle threshold (Ct) value of ≥ 40, while the positive control required a Ct value of ≤ 35; otherwise, the run was deemed invalid and was repeated. Following successful QC, test samples were interpreted according to the following criteria: positive if Ct ≤ 38; negative if Ct ≥ 40; and retesting was required if 38 < Ct < 40. The result of the retest was used for final interpretation.

Statistical analysis

To describe the burden characteristics of UU, CT, NG, and HSV among individuals older than 14 years in Hangzhou, a descriptive analysis was conducted. We calculated the age-adjusted prevalence rates of UU, CT, NG and HSV, as well as their age-adjusted prevalence rates across various age groups, genders, and years. First, we collected the number of cases and the total number of individuals in each age group for each pathogen. We also gathered the standard population distribution of Hangzhou (2020 updated census data of Hangzhou). Next, we calculated the prevalence rate for each age group within a specific pathogen group using the formula: prevalence rate = number of cases in an age group/total number of people in that age group. Subsequently, we calculated the age-adjusted prevalence rate for each specific pathogen using the formula: age-adjusted prevalence rate = ∑ (prevalence rate of an age group × standard population proportion). The complete formula is displayed in Fig. 1.

The age-adjusted prevalence calculation formula

When analyzing the epidemiological characteristics of co-infections among pathogens, we selected samples that were simultaneously tested for UU, CT, NG and HSV, and calculated the age-adjusted prevalence for each type of co-infection. Additionally, we assessed the associations between pathogens using the Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test. For example, to examine the association between UU and CT, we categorized cases into UU - CT -, UU + CT -, UU - CT +, and UU + CT +. Statistical analysis was performed using the Chi-square test, with a p-value of less than 0.05 considered indicative of a significant association between the two pathogens. We calculated the odds ratio (OR) between two pathogens to assess the strength of their association and relative risk. Due to the absence of co-infection cases of NG and HSV in our study, we were unable to compute the 95% confidence interval for the OR.

Given the limited number of cases for HSV-1 and HSV-2, we assessed their epidemiological characteristics by calculating their positive rates. The positive rate for HSV-1 was calculated as the number of HSV-1 positive samples divided by the total number of samples, employing the same method for HSV-2.

The positive rate was calculated by dividing the number of positive patients by the total number of patients tested. Confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated at the 95% confidence interval. Chi-square test or Fisher’s test was utilized to determine the statistical significance of differences in frequencies or distributions. Spearman’s rank correlation test was applied to verify trends. p-values of less than 0.05 or less were considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were performed using R 4.4.2, and figures were generated using the ggplot2 R package [12]. Figures were assembled using the patchwork R package [13].

The age adjusted prevalence and sex differences of UU, CT, NG, and HSV

A total of 12,505 outpatients were included. The age-adjusted prevalence rates of UU, CT, NG, and HSV were 30.93% (29.70%–32.16%), 10.33% (9.63%–11.03%), 11.51% (10.66%–12.36%), and 10.64% (9.64%–11.64%), respectively. These results indicate that the age-adjusted prevalence of UU was significantly higher than that of the other pathogens (Fig. 2).

Age-adjusted prevalence of common STIs and their differences by sex. Age-adjusted prevalence was 30.93% for UU, 10.33% for CT, 11.51% for NG, and 10.64% for HSV. UU and HSV infections primarily affected women, whereas CT and NG infections were more prevalent among men

Among the patients, there were 7,101 males and 5,404 females. The age-adjusted prevalence of UU and HSV was significantly lower in males compared to females (23.01% vs. 43.91%, p < 0.05; 7.87% vs. 15.20%, p < 0.05). Conversely, the age-adjusted prevalence of CT and NG was significantly higher in males than in females (12.08% vs. 7.53%, p < 0.05; 18.89% vs. 2.96%, p < 0.05) (Fig. 2).

Prevalence of UU, CT, NG and HSV from 2018-2024

To better understand the changes in the age-adjusted prevalence of UU, CT, NG, and HSV from 2018 to 2024, we analyzed data from male patients, with sample sizes as follows: 519, 763, 804, 1,045, 1,303, 1,168, and 1,499, and for female patients: 390, 634, 485, 555, 1,312, 925, and 1,103, respectively. The age-adjusted prevalence of UU peaked in 2022 at 39.00% (36.14% − 41.86%), while the lowest prevalence among female patients occurred in 2020 at 28.61% (18.20% − 39.02%). From 2018 to 2024, the age-adjusted prevalence of UU among females consistently remained higher than that among males (Fig. 3. a). The age-adjusted prevalence of CT reached its lowest point at 7.00% (5.32% − 8.68%) in 2020. With the exception of 2019, males exhibited a lower age-adjusted prevalence of CT compared to females (Fig. 3. b). The age-adjusted prevalence of NG reached its lowest levels in both 2020 and 2023, peaking in 2021. From 2018 to 2024, males consistently displayed a higher age-adjusted prevalence of NG than females (Fig. 3. c). Although changes in HSV prevalence were minimal, it is notable that in 2018, 2019, 2020, and 2021, the age-adjusted prevalence of HSV in females was significantly higher than in males: 30.10% vs 6.50% (p < 0.05), 53.07% vs 8.53% (p < 0.05), 50.63% vs 6.40% (p < 0.05), and 33.80% vs 7.48% (p < 0.05), respectively. This prevalence trend was followed by a decrease (Fig. 3. d).

Prevalence of UU, CT, NG and HSV in different age groups

To investigate the age differences in infections caused by UU, CT, NG, and HSV, we calculated the age-adjusted prevalence rates for each pathogen across different age groups. Participants were divided into seven age groups: 15–19 years, 20–29 years, 30–39 years, 40–49 years, 50–59 years, 60–69 years, and ≥70 years. The sample sizes for males and females were as follows: male group (n = 130; 2,736; 2,943; 913; 256; 92; 31) and female group (n = 128; 2,361; 1,978; 599; 246; 70; 22), corresponding to the defined age groups. The age-adjusted prevalence of UU was relatively high across all age groups from 15 to 59 years, with females exhibiting a higher age-adjusted prevalence than males (Fig. 4. a). The age-adjusted prevalence of CT exhibited a gradual decline with increasing age, except for an increase in the 50–59 age group. With the exception of the 15–19 age group, males consistently had a higher age-adjusted prevalence of CT compared to females across all other age groups (Fig. 4. b). The age-adjusted prevalence of NG was highest in the 15–19 age group, followed by fluctuations in subsequent age groups. The trend for males within each age group mirrored that of the overall population, with the lowest prevalence of 14.98‰ (14.31‰–15.65‰) observed in the 30–39 age group. Additionally, the age-adjusted prevalence of NG was significantly higher in males than in females (Fig. 4. c). The age-adjusted prevalence of HSV gradually increased with age and began to decline after 60 years. The peak prevalence for females in the 60–69 age group was 35.06‰ (29.91‰–40.21‰), while males peaked at 24.97‰ (22.14‰–27.80‰) in the 50–59 age group. Prior to age 70, the age-adjusted prevalence of HSV in females consistently exceeded that of males (Fig. 4. d).

Age-adjusted prevalence of common STIs across different age groups. a-d UU, CT, and NG infections were more common among young people aged 15–29 years. The age-adjusted prevalence of HSV demonstrated sex-specific disparities, peaking at 35.06‰ (29.91‰–40.21‰) among women aged 60–69 years and reaching 24.97‰ (22.14‰–27.80‰) in men aged 50–59 years

Co-infection differences in prevalence

The present study identified co-infections of UU + CT, UU + NG, UU + HSV, CT + NG, CT + HSV, UU + CT + NG and UU + CT + HSV. The most common co-infections were UU+CT, UU+NG and CT+NG, with age-adjusted prevalences of 16.61‰ (15.74‰–17.49‰), 9.90‰ (9.37‰–10.43‰) and 11.10‰ (10.51‰–11.70‰), respectively. The age-adjusted prevalence of UU+CT, UU+NG, CT+NG and UU+CT+NG was significantly higher in males than in females (12.12‰ vs. 9.93‰, p < 0.05; 7.60‰ vs. 2.5‰, p < 0.05; 12.49‰ vs. 1.18‰, p < 0.05; 2.54‰ vs. 0.39‰, p < 0.05) (Fig. 5). We found no cases of NG + HSV co-infection in our study. To understand the relationships among pathogens, we analyzed data from 5,387 patients (3,617 males and 1,770 females) who were simultaneously tested for all four pathogens. Chi-square tests revealed significant associations between UU and CT, UU and NG, as well as NG and CT (p < 0.05) (Fig. 6). To assess the strength of these associations, ORs were calculated, yielding 1.30 (1.08, 1.55) for UU and CT, 0.46 (0.37, 0.58) for UU and NG, and 1.78 (1.40, 2.27) for NG and CT. These results indicate a positive association between UU and CT, as well as between NG and CT, while a negative association was observed between UU and NG.

Prevalence of different types of HSV

Among the 6,471 tested patients, 435 were diagnosed with HSV infection, 19.31% with HSV-1 infection and 80.69% with HSV-2 infection. The positive rate of HSV-1 was significantly lower than that of HSV-2 (1.30% vs. 5.42%, p < 0.05). A marked sex-specific disparity was observed, with both HSV-1 and HSV-2 infections resulting in a significantly lower positive rate in males than in females (Fig. 7, a). Furthermore, Spearman’s rank correlation analysis revealed that the positive rate of HSV-2 increased with age (r = 0.88, p < 0.05) (Fig. 7, b). We found no cases of HSV-1 and HSV-2 co-infection in our study.

Discussion

By conducting an in-depth analysis of the epidemiological characteristics of UU, CT, NG, and HSV, the study aims to provide a scientific foundation for optimizing local screening strategies and developing effective prevention and control programs. Specifically, it focuses on infection rates, population distribution characteristics, prevalence trends, and co-infection patterns. This study revealed that the age-adjusted prevalence of UU was the highest among the tested pathogens. UU infection is prevalent among individuals aged 20 to 59, whereas CT and NG infections are most commonly observed in the 15 to 19 age group. Among these infections, UU and HSV infections were more prevalent in women, whereas CT and NG infections were more common observed in men. The positive rate of HSV-2 infection was significantly positively correlated with age. Co-infection was most frequently observed between UU and CT. Furthermore, men had significantly higher age-adjusted prevalence rates of co-infections with UU and NG, as well as CT and NG compared to women. UU was positively associated with CT, CT was positively associated with NG, UU was negatively associated with NG. The age-adjusted prevalence of CT and NG declined significantly in 2020 compared with 2019. These findings establish a critical evidence base for developing demographically tailored intervention protocols and optimizing population-specific STI surveillance frameworks.

Consistent with the findings of Y Y Liang et al., the age-adjusted prevalence of UU was significantly higher than that of CT, NG, and HSV [14]. Although UU is one of the potential causative agents of NGU, its pathogenicity needs to be fully assessed after ruling out other STIs [15]. In healthy women, UU colonizes the cervix and vagina and may cause asymptomatic infections in the upper genital tract; in men, UU also colonizes the urethra, and infection occurs only when the bacterial load exceeds the threshold that triggers NGU [16, 17]. This highlights the dual role of UU as both a potential sexually transmitted pathogen and a common commensal organism. The age-adjusted prevalence of UU and HSV is significantly higher in women than in men. This disparity may be attributed to the anatomical characteristics of the female urogenital tract and changes in the vaginal microbial homeostasis influenced by factors such as sexual frequency [18], hygienic practices [19], and hormonal cycles [20]. Furthermore, the prevalence of UU is elevated among female intrauterine device users [21]. The risk of genital herpes transmission from men to female partners is approximately 19%, whereas the risk from women to men is around 5% [22]. This disparity may stem from various factors, including the pooling of semen in the vagina, which increases female exposure, and the greater tissue trauma experienced during intercourse. In contrast, the age-adjusted prevalence of CT and NG, classical STIs, was significantly lower in women than in men, consistent with the findings of previous studies [23]. This difference stems in part from the fact that CT/NG infections in men are more symptomatic: NG in men tends to involve the urinary tract and is often marked by purulent discharge and pain within 2 to 6 days post-infection, whereas CT infections in men are also marked by purulent discharge [24]. Studies indicate that men are significantly more likely to exhibit symptoms of both NG and CT infections compared to women [25–27]. As a result, men are more likely to seek prompt treatment, contributing to gender disparities in the age-adjusted prevalence of NG and CT. Notably, despite the similar urogenital loads of NG in both sexes, higher NG loads in male urine samples were associated with genital symptoms but not in female vaginal swabs [28].

We found that the age-adjusted prevalence rates of CT and NG were highest among young people in the 15–29 years age group. This finding differs from those of previous studies conducted in Beijing, which may be attributed to differences in age grouping methods, such as combining the 10–14 and 15–19 age groups, affecting the comparability of the data [14]. Except for CT infections in the 15–19 age group, the age-adjusted prevalence rates of CT and NG infections are higher in men than in women across other age groups. In addition to the previously mentioned reason that symptoms of CT and NG infections tend to be more severe in men than in women, we should also consider another possibility: the ratio of sex workers to clients is typically one to many. This implies that the number of sex workers is relatively small, while the number of clients who have contact with them may be significantly larger. This asymmetrical pattern of sexual behavior could contribute to a relatively higher number of infected men. Notably, the age-adjusted prevalence of CT/NG in individuals aged 30–39 years was significantly lower, which may be associated with increased social and familial pressures experienced by individuals in this age group, reduced sexual activity, and higher levels of sex education. In contrast, the age-adjusted prevalence of CT/NG in individuals aged 50–59 years was higher. The presumed mechanisms include limited access to sex education and increased sexual activity following reduction of life stressors. In the Xihu District of Hangzhou, the average age of divorce is 40.1 years for men and 38 years for women [29]. This trend appears to correlate with an increased likelihood of engaging in unsafe sexual practices after the age of 40. Additionally, some high-risk individuals may have prolonged histories of risk exposure due to sustained patterns of sexual behavior. Importantly, the ongoing increase in NG drug resistance has led to higher rates of treatment failure and an elevated risk of sustained transmission within this age group [9]. In our study, UU was more prevalent in the 20–59 age group. Given that UU can colonize the urethra or genital tract, sexual contact increases the risk of transmission. Furthermore, the median age of first sexual intercourse among male adolescents in China is 20, while the median age for females is slightly later [30], which may help explain the differences in UU infection rates across different age groups. The spectrum of HSV infections indicates that the positive rate of HSV-2 is generally higher than that of HSV-1, which is consistent with the role of HSV-2 as the primary pathogen of genital herpes [31]. Roberts et al. speculated that unprotected oral sex may increase the likelihood of genital HSV-1 infection, particularly among individuals aged 16 to 21 [32]. The study also revealed that the HSV positive rate was higher among middle-aged and elderly individuals aged 50–69 years and that the HSV-2-positive rate was significantly positively correlated with age. This finding is consistent with the lifelong latent infection characteristics of HSV [33]. This highlights the need to focus on the prevention and control of the reactivation mechanisms of latent infections in this population.

Consistent with the results of most studies, co-infection with UU and CT is common [14, 34]. This study revealed a positive association between UU and CT infections, as well as between CT and NG infections, which aligns with the findings of Shasha Cai et al. [35]. However, a novel finding reveals a negative association between UU and NG infections. In our study, the age-adjusted prevalence of UU was higher in females, while the prevalence of NG was higher in males. This separation in pathogen-positive data may directly cause the statistical negative association between UU and NG. Additionally, women are more likely to self-treat with over-the-counter vaginal yeast medications and douches, which may conceal actual sexually transmitted infections. Studies have shown that females with NG infections have elevated levels of human lactoferrin (hLf) in reproductive tract secretions [36], and hLf may help enhance immune protection in the female reproductive tract [37]. This finding could contribute to the explanation of the negative association between UU and NG. Just as Lactobacillus maintains a healthy vaginal environment by inhibiting the overgrowth of other resident microbes [38], it remains unclear whether there is a microbial competition relationship between UU and NG, as relevant research is still lacking. Moreover, the study did not find cases of co-infection with HSV and NG. The reason for this phenomenon may be attributed to gender differences in pathogen infections. The primary type of HSV infection in the genitourinary system is HSV-2, which, in our study, was predominantly observed in females. As mentioned earlier, NG primarily infects males. This gender disparity can lead to incidental errors in detection. However, the association between pathogen infections still needs to be verified through in vivo or in vitro experiments, which will inform our future research.

In this study, the age-adjusted prevalence of CT and NG declined significantly in 2020 compared with 2019. The year 2020 was marked by the global outbreak of COVID-19. In response, China implemented a series of prevention and control measures to mitigate the spread of the pandemic. These policies may have impacted the epidemiology of STIs through two mechanisms: first, by altering sexual behavior patterns within the population; second, by reducing opportunities for STIs testing, thereby leading to a decrease in the number of reported cases [39]. Comparing 2018 with 2024, no significant changes were observed in the prevalence of UU, CT, or NG. This finding indicates that higher emphasis should be placed on the screening and treatment of UU, CT, and NG. Molecular diagnostic technology plays a crucial role in developing personalized intervention strategies in clinical practice by accurately identifying pathogens and their subtypes. This advancement significantly enhances the prevention and control of sexually transmitted diseases.

This study has several limitations. First, the sample was sourced from a single-center medical institution, and therefore, the findings may only reflect the epidemiological characteristics of specific STIs (UU, CT, NG, HSV) in Hangzhou. A multicenter study is needed to increase the sample size to improve the generalizability of these results. Second, some suspected cases of STIs did not undergo comprehensive pathogen testing. Given that most infections are asymptomatic in the early stages, this may lead to missed diagnoses and an underestimation of age-adjusted prevalence. Third, this study did not perform stratified analyses based on the demographic characteristics of the research subjects, including socioeconomic status, education level, marital status, and residential area. As a result, it is challenging to assess differences in risk factors across various populations. Finally, relying solely on pathogen nucleic acid test results to assess UU colonization and infection status has its limitations. Combining PCR results with clinical manifestations, along with the exclusion of other sexually transmitted pathogens, can provide a more accurate reflection of UU infection or colonization.

Conclusion

The analysis reveals that infections caused by UU, CT, NG, and HSV are associated with host factors such as age and sex. Additionally, significant epidemiological associations exist between these pathogens. Furthermore, the transmission of these pathogens may be influenced by public health policies. In summary, this study could deepen understanding of the epidemiological characteristics of UU, NG, CT, and HSV among outpatients in Hangzhou, providing a scientific basis for optimizing regional screening strategies and formulating effective prevention and control plans.
