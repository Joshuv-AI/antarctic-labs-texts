Machine learning-aided enhancement of white tea extraction efficiency using hybridized GMDH models in microwave-assisted extraction
Authors: Mostafa Khajeh, Mansour Ghaffari-Moghaddam, Jamshid Piri, Afsaneh Barkhordar, Turan Ozturk
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractWhite tea is valuable for having a high antioxidant content, which is considered to possess numerous beneficial effects on health. This study investigated the application of microwave-assisted extraction (MAE) for the extraction of total phenolic compounds from white tea. The experimental setup included four independent variables: microwave power (ranging from 100 to 300 W), extraction time (ranging from 10 to 40 min), temperature (ranging from 35 to 50 °C), and the ratio of food to solvent (ranging from 0.25 to 0.5 g/10 mL). The responses that were evaluated were IC50 (ppm) and total phenolic content (mg/g). The experimental design consisted of thirty runs conducted within the MAE system. The group method of data handling (GMDH) models were used to predict important efficiency measures (IC50 and total phenol content) in the extraction process. The models were assessed based on their ability to capture the relationships between input conditions and efficiency outputs. Three GMDH variants were compared: baseline GMDH, GMDH optimized with a genetic algorithm (GMDH-GA), and GMDH optimized with a harmony search algorithm (GMDH-HS). While all models achieved high predictive ability on a test set, GMDH-HS emerged as the superior performer. It achieved near-perfect agreement with observations (d-index > 0.998), minimal errors (NRMSE < 0.02), and effectively captured data variance (NSE > 0.99) for both outputs. Correlation diagrams and Taylor diagrams confirmed the superior performance of GMDH-HS in terms of linearity, correlation, and error minimization. This study demonstrates the effectiveness of hybridizing GMDH with a harmony search algorithm for complex modeling tasks, paving the way for improved efficiency and yield optimization in extraction processes.Keywords: White tea, Microwave assisted extraction, GMDH, Genetic algoritm, Harmony searchSubject terms: Drug discovery, Plant sciences

Introduction

Tea (Camellia sinensis) is a popular beverage that is enjoyed across the world and is known for its many associations with culture as well as its healthful properties1,2. Among its varieties, white tea is notable, having its origins in Fuding city and Zhenghe county, both located in Fujian province, China1. White tea’s attractiveness has grown because of its numerous health advantages, including antioxidative, anticarcinogenic, anti-inflammatory, and antibacterial effects, which have recently come into notice1,2. The production of white tea follows a simple method that involves only the freshly harvested tea leaves undergoing a process of withering and subsequent drying3. The withering step continues for a specific duration, typically up to 72 h, and is accompanied by a wide range of metabolic processes with various substances, including amino acids, polyphenols, carbohydrates, glycosides, and other constituents4. Based on the process of plucking, white tea can be divided into four categories: Silver Needle (Baihaoyinzhen) comprises only buds, White Peony (Baimudan) includes a bud with one or two leaves, Gongmei consists of a bud with more than two leaves, and Shoumei is derived from young shoots with more than two leaves5.

Tea polyphenols, making up approximately 10–40% of the dried weight of tea leaves, are an excellent example of plant polyphenols, which have been recognized for their important role in food preservation. Tea polyphenols, which include phenolic acids, anthocyanins, catechins, and a variety of flavonoids, represent a broad range of beneficial characteristics such as antioxidative, anti-inflammatory, anti-tumor, anti-aging, and radioprotective effects in this category6–8. Additionally, they contribute significantly to various health concerns such as helping to avoid long-lasting illnesses, protecting nerves, increasing good bacteria growth, and keeping the gut healthy and balanced9. Moreover, tea polyphenols have been shown to block the breakdown of fats and carbohydrates by inhibiting key digestive enzymes such as pancreatic lipase and amylases10.

Antioxidants are essential in avoiding diseases caused by oxidative stress as well as preserving the quality of food. They inhibit oxidative chain reactions in cells and tissues, acting as natural preservatives11. For a general definition, antioxidants are substances that delay or prevent oxidation through a variety of mechanisms, including removing oxygen (O2) and scavenging reactive oxygen and nitrogen species12. Assays such as DPPH (2,2′-diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl) are utilized for evaluating the potency of natural antioxidants such as flavonoids and phenolic acids in protecting cells from oxidative damage13. Antioxidants adsorb free radicals generated during biological oxidation, preventing cellular damage14. These substances are currently growing popularity due to their capacity to protect biological molecules and cell structures from free radical damage15.

Traditional methods for extraction typically involve a long-time process, use a lot of solvents and the potential risk of heat-induced degradation of sensitive compounds16,17. To address these limitations, microwave-assisted extraction (MAE) has been developed as a promising and environmentally friendly alternative17,18. MAE uses microwaves to directly heat the solvent and sample matrix, leading to rapid and uniform heating compared to traditional methods19,20. This synergistic effect of heat and mass transfer accelerates the extraction process, resulting in higher yields and reduced thermal degradation of target compounds17,19. Additionally, MAE requires less solvent and offers shorter extraction times compared to conventional techniques, making it a more cost-effective and sustainable approach18,21. However, MAE has limitations, including the limited use of non-polar solvents and the requirement for specialized apparatus19. Nevertheless, for suitable applications, MAE provides a valuable and efficient method for extracting various compounds from different matrices, making it a useful tool for sample preparation in the field of analytical chemistry to analyze inorganic and organic compounds in academic and industrial purposes16,22.

Artificial intelligence (AI) models have emerged as powerful tools for predicting and optimizing extraction techniques and phytochemical analysis. The Group Method of Data Handling (GMDH) algorithm, renowned for its self-organizing capabilities and ability to tackle complex, nonlinear relationships, has proven particularly effective in this domain. Numerous studies have demonstrated the application of GMDH and other AI models to extraction-related tasks. For instance, Xu et al. (2021) proposed a novel approach combining GMDH-type neural networks with graph kernel SVMs to improve multiple graph kernel learning(MGKL), demonstrating the potential of GMDH-based methods in enhancing machine learning algorithms for classification tasks23. Majid et al. (2022) utilized a hybrid GMDH-discrete differential evolution (GMDH-DDE) model to predict solution gas/oil ratio (Rs) from pressure/volume/temperature data in the oil and gas industry. Their study compared GMDH-DDE with standard GMDH and backpropagation neural networks (BPNN), demonstrating that the hybrid model outperformed the others in terms of prediction accuracy and computational efficiency. This showcases the potential of hybrid GMDH models in accurately predicting complex relationships in petroleum engineering applications24. Zhang et al. (2023) employed advanced machine learning techniques and nature-inspired methods to improve predictions of shale wettability, with implications for carbon capture utilization and storage (CCUS). Their study utilized multiple machine learning models, including multilayer perceptron (MLP) and radial basis function neural networks (RBFNN), optimized with various nature-inspired algorithms. The RBFNN model optimized with the multi-verse optimizer (RBFNN-MVO) achieved the highest prediction accuracy for contact angle, with an R² value exceeding 0.99. This research demonstrates the potential of hybrid AI models in accurately predicting complex geological properties crucial for CCUS projects25. Additionally, Jin et al. (2022) applied advanced optimization techniques to improve the extraction process of Yangyin Yiqi Huoxue prescription using natural deep eutectic solvents (NaDESs). Their study compared the effectiveness of response surface methodology (RSM) and genetic neural network (GNN) in optimizing both extraction yield and antioxidant activity. The GNN model outperformed RSM, providing more accurate predictions and better experimental results. Using betaine-lactic acid as the extractant, the GNN-optimized process achieved high extraction yields (3.12 mg/g) and antioxidant properties (86.27%), with minimal deviations from predicted values. This research demonstrates the potential of GNN in multi-objective optimization for complex extraction processes in traditional Chinese medicine26.

In numerous applications, maximizing desired compound yields hinges on extraction efficiency27,28. Mathematical modeling of the extraction process based on experimental data provides an avenue to optimize parameters for higher efficiency29. This work develops predictive models, using data from experimental extraction trials, for two critical efficiency metrics: IC50 and total phenol content. The Group Method of Data Handling (GMDH) model variants, which provide a comprehensive set, are used for this purpose. A multilayer polynomial neural network is constructed using GMDH as a modeling technique30,31. The core algorithm employs regression techniques across layers to find the optimal coefficient sets for reducing prediction error on the data. Prior research has enhanced GMDH model accuracy by integrating evolutionary computing algorithms for coefficient optimization, including genetic algorithms (GA)31,32 and harmony search (HS)33. This work investigates four GMDH variants: the base method, as well as versions that integrate GA, HS, or both. The goal of the modeling technique is to predict the two desired output metrics, IC50 and total phenolic content. In all cases, a lower IC50 and higher phenolic levels indicate more successful extraction. If these outputs could be reliably predicted given a set of input extraction parameters, process optimization might be feasible.

Materials and methods

Plant material

The white tea leaves were obtained from a local market in Zabol, Iran. After a thorough washing process to eliminate any surface impurities, the leaves were dried in a controlled environment to ensure consistent moisture removal. Finally, the dried leaves were ground into a fine powder using a laboratory grinder. This powder was then stored in closed plastic containers for further use in MAE.

Chemicals and reagents

This study employed commercially available analytical-grade chemicals and reagents without requiring any additional purification processes. The chemicals and reagents used in this study, including 2,2′-diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl (DPPH), methanol, Folin-Ciocalteu’s phenol reagent, gallic acid, and sodium bicarbonate, were obtained from Merck (Darmstadt, Germany).

Microwave assisted extraction (MAE)

The microwave assisted extraction (MAE) of white tea leaves was conducted using a Milestone ETHOS SEL laboratory equipment from Sorisole, Italy. The laboratory system was fitted with a sealed 10-vessel chamber. Four important factors were changed during the extraction process. The experimental factors included in the study were microwave power (100, 200, and 300 W), extraction time (10, 25, and 40 min), food-to-solvent ratio (0.25, 0.375, and 0.5 g/10 mL) and temperature (35, 42.5, and 50 °C). The extraction solvent selected was water. After the extraction, the samples were subjected to centrifugation at a speed of 7,000 rpm for a duration of 5 min. Afterwards, the samples were filtered using Whatman No. 1 filter paper. The extracted samples were carefully transferred to glass vials with a capacity of 10 mL and stored in a refrigerator. The samples were subsequently evaluated for its total phenolic content and its ability to scavenge DPPH free radicals.

Determination of total phenolic contents

The measurement of the total phenolic content of the extracts was performed using the Folin-Ciocalteu method as reported by Fadil et al. (2024)11. In summary, 100 µL of each extract was mixed with 500 µL of Folin-Ciocalteu reagent and allow to incubate for 5 min. Subsequently, 1.5 mL of a solution containing 20% (w/v) sodium carbonate was added, and after a period of 2 min, the volume was adjusted to 10 mL by adding water. The absorbance was recorded at 765 nm using a spectrophotometer after a 2-hour incubation at room temperature. The total phenolic content was calculated as milligrams of gallic acid equivalents per gram of dry extract based on a pre-determined calibration curve.

Assessing the scavenging activity against DPPH free radicals

To determine the ability of the extracts to scavenge free radicals, the DPPH assay was used, according to the procedure provided by Fadil et al. (2024)11. A 2 mL solution of DPPH with a concentration of 0.12 mM in methanol was combined with 0.3 mL of the extract. To achieve a complete reaction, it is necessary to rapidly mix the components and then incubate them at room temperature in the absence of light for a duration of fifty minutes. GMDH-GA (GMDH with genetic algorithm)

A genetic algorithm (GA) was developed to simultaneously optimize both the architectural design and polynomial coefficients of Group Method of Data Handling (GMDH) neural networks (Fig. 1)36. The goal was constructing accurate predictive models of the target variable y from the input variables x1 to xn.

GA implementation

The GA evolves a population of candidate GMDH networks over successive generations to improve model accuracy on training data. Each individual network is encoded as a chromosome consisting of:

Connectivity genes: These genes control the neuron connections across layers using random permutations of the inputs.

Coefficient genes: The polynomial parameters within each neuron are optimized by the GA. Polynomials take the standard GMDH form with cross terms.

The initial population contains NP chromosomes, representing unique random network configurations. The networks are evaluated on training data using root mean squared error (RMSE) between predicted outputs ŷ and true targets y.

GA operators

In each generation, the top NP/2 most accurate networks are selected to propagate their traits. New networks are generated from these using:

Crossover: Exchanges connectivity genes between parents to transfer beneficial structures.

Crossover: Exchanges connectivity genes between parents to transfer beneficial structures.

Mutation: Randomly alters connectivity to introduce variation.

Over NI generations, these operators drive the evolutionary optimization process, improving prediction accuracy. Finally, the network chromosome with the lowest RMSE is returned as the optimized model. In essence, the GA efficiently searches the combined high-dimensional search space of connectivity patterns and coefficients to minimize error and construct an accurate GMDH network.

GMDH-HS (GMDH with harmonic search)

An improved harmony search algorithm was integrated with the group method of data handling (GMDH) to simultaneously optimize neural network architectures and polynomial coefficients (Fig. 1)37.

Evaluation criteria for models

The Index of Agreement, also referred to as the Willmott Index or the d-index, measures how far observed and simulated data differ38. A high value indicates better agreement between the observed and simulated data. It ranges from 0 (no agreement) to 1 (perfect agreement).

n is the number of observations, Oi is the observed value, Pi is the simulated value, and Om is the mean of the observed values.

An NRSME (Normalized Root Mean Square Error) is a normalized version of the Root Mean Square Error (RMSE), which measures the squared difference between observed and simulated values39. By dividing the RMSE by the range of observed values, NRSME becomes dimensionless and can be compared across model outputs more easily. Lower values of NRSME indicate better model performance, with a value of 0 representing a perfect fit.

NSE (Nash-Sutcliffe Efficiency): A prevalent metric for gauging the predictive prowess of hydrological models, the Nash-Sutcliffe Efficiency (NSE), compares the residual variance (the difference between observed and simulated values) with the data variance (the difference between observed values and the mean)40. NSE ranges from -∞ to 1, with 1 indicating a perfect match between observed and simulated data. The NSE value of 0 indicates that the model’s predictions are as accurate as the data mean.

This metric represents the degree of agreement between observed and simulated data, indicating improved model performance with a value of 1 indicating perfect fit.

Figures 6 and 7 illustrate GMDH-HS remarkable efficacy by showing Taylor diagrams for IC50 and total phenolic content, respectively. In both diagrams, the GMDH-HS point is unusually close to the “Observed” reference, suggesting low errors, almost perfect correlation, and faithful replication of data variability. While the GMDH-GA model showed improved accuracy over the baseline GMDH, the GMDH-HS model displayed superior performance in terms of all assessment metrics and visualizations. Harmony search optimization improved GMDH’s ability to capture the intricate relationship between the two output efficiency measures and the four input extraction criteria by fine-tuning the neural network design and coefficients. The GMDH-HS model is superior for predicting IC50 and total phenol content of extractions based on new input variable values and validation on an unknown test set.

Computational efficiency

In addition to evaluating the predictive accuracy of the GMDH, GMDH-GA, and GMDH-HS models, it is crucial to consider their computational efficiency. This aspect is particularly important for practical applications, especially when dealing with large datasets or when real-time predictions are required. To assess the computational demands of each method, we measured the CPU time and peak memory usage during both the training and prediction phases. The experiments were conducted on a workstation with an Intel Core i7 CPU and 16GB of RAM. The results are summarized in Table 5.

The baseline GMDH model demonstrated the fastest training time and lowest memory usage, which is expected given its simpler architecture. However, this comes at the cost of reduced predictive accuracy as discussed earlier. Both GMDH-GA and GMDH-HS showed increased computational demands due to their optimization processes. GMDH-GA had the longest training time, likely due to the multiple generations required for the genetic algorithm to converge. GMDH-HS showed a slightly better balance between computational efficiency and predictive performance, with a training time about 17% shorter than GMDH-GA and lower peak memory usage. Interestingly, the prediction times for all three models were comparable, with only marginal differences. This suggests that once trained, the models have similar computational costs for making predictions, which is advantageous in real-time applications. While GMDH-HS demonstrates superior predictive performance, it does come with increased computational costs compared to the baseline GMDH. However, for many applications, the improved accuracy may justify the additional computational resources required. The choice between these models may depend on the specific requirements of the application, balancing the need for accuracy with available computational resources. It’s worth noting that for larger datasets or more complex problems, the differences in computational efficiency may become more pronounced. Future work could explore optimization techniques to further improve the efficiency of the GMDH-GA and GMDH-HS algorithms without sacrificing their predictive power. Given the specifications of the system used (Core i7 CPU with 16GB RAM), all models performed efficiently without approaching memory limits. This suggests that for similar extraction optimization tasks, these models can be comfortably run on standard desktop or laptop computers without requiring specialized high-performance hardware.

Conclusions

This study explored the process of extracting phenolic components from white tea using microwave-assisted extraction (MAE), examining how different GMDH neural network topologies predicted extraction process efficiency indicators. The experimental setup included four independent variables (power, extraction duration, temperature, and food-to-solvent ratio), with IC50 and total phenolic content as response variables. The GMDH-HS model, tuned using a harmony search method, outperformed the baseline GMDH and GMDH-GA (genetic algorithm) variations, remarkably capturing the intricate correlations between extraction variables and efficiency outputs. This achievement demonstrates the potential of hybridizing GMDH with cutting-edge optimization methods to tackle challenging modeling tasks. The computational efficiency of GMDH variants was evaluated on a standard desktop computer (Intel Core i7 CPU, 16GB RAM). While the baseline GMDH demonstrated the fastest training time (1.8s) and lowest memory usage (124 MB), GMDH-HS offered a balanced compromise between performance and resource utilization (12.7s, 196 MB). GMDH-GA, despite its high accuracy, required the most resources (15.3s, 230 MB). Importantly, all models exhibited similar prediction times (0.04–0.05 s), indicating their suitability for real-time applications. These findings suggest that even complex GMDH variants can be effectively deployed on standard hardware for extraction optimization tasks. Nevertheless, the present analysis has certain limitations. A comparatively modest dataset (25 data points for training and 5 for testing) was used in this investigation. Although the GMDH-HS model worked well, a larger and more varied dataset would improve its ability to generalize to new situations. This investigation also concentrated solely on GMDH variations. Exploring other machine learning models, such as support vector machines or deep neural networks, may offer additional insights and potentially improved prediction abilities.
