Assessing the initial impact of the Russian invasion on Ukrainian agriculture: Challenges, policy responses, and future prospects
Authors: Maryna Nehrey, Robert Finger
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
The war in Ukraine has caused significant losses to the Ukrainian agricultural sector and threatened global food security. This study aims to comprehensively analyse the impact of the Russian invasion on Ukrainian agriculture in its initial phase, examine the responses of Ukrainian agricultural policy and outline key elements for the post-war development of the Ukrainian agricultural sector. Using a systematic approach that includes thorough data collection and analysis from official sources, the Ukrainian press, relevant legislation and statistical data, our study focuses on the first five months of the conflict, from February 24, 2022 to July 24, 2022, to identify the main challenges during this period. The agricultural policy analysis shows that the Ukrainian government has adopted a reactive approach, including tax simplification, affordable credit, deregulation, financial support for the agricultural sector, reduced prices for inputs and resources to support farmers, and improvements in logistics. Critical factors identified for post-war development include repatriation and agricultural education, support for small and medium-sized farms, integration into global markets, emphasis on organic practices and sustainable development, and digitalization in agriculture. The integration of a systematic overview of the Ukrainian agricultural sector and an analysis of key elements of post-war development provides essential insights for policy-makers and researchers concerned with the impact of war on the agricultural sector and food security.

Introduction

The Russian invasion of Ukraine began on February 24, 2022. As a result of this invasion, many people were forced to leave their homes and countless people lost their lives. Many Ukrainians faced severe food shortages and limited access to medicines and essential goods. This led to a rapid deterioration in the country's economic situation. Gross domestic product (GDP), employment and the Ukrainian currency (hryvnia) fell, while inflation and the budget deficit rose. Other war-related problems that severely affected the country included agricultural and food production in Ukraine and agricultural trade with other countries. The effects of the war went beyond Ukraine's borders and had a negative impact on the rest of the world economy, leading to high fuel and food prices. Many European countries were overwhelmed by the large numbers of refugees crossing their borders. Food security in the Middle East and North Africa deteriorated due to the war. It is currently almost impossible to fully assess the impact and consequences of this war on the Ukrainian and global economies.

The Food and Agriculture Organisation of the United Nations, the World Bank and other international organizations have discussed the impact of the war on global agriculture and food security [[1], [2], [3]]. In addition, there are few previous studies on the impact of the Russian-Ukrainian war. The existing studies that examine the effects on Ukrainian agriculture focus on the country's role as an exporter of agricultural products [4,5]. Few studies have examined the situation of Ukrainian agriculture holistically and beyond exports [6,7]. Comprehensive studies on agriculture during the war are being conducted by scientists from the Kyiv School of Economics [8,9], experts from the Ukrainian Agribusiness Club [10] and the Ukrainian Ministry of Foreign Affairs [11].

However, there is still a lack of analysis of the state of the Ukrainian agricultural sector and the problems caused by the war. Moreover, the Ukrainian government's agricultural policy responses and adaptations to the challenges of the war have not yet been studied. Finally, there is a lack of knowledge about the prospects of the Ukrainian agricultural sector in the post-war period, e.g. concerning the possible further development of Ukrainian agriculture.

We aim to fill existing knowledge gaps by presenting a comprehensive body of evidence that highlights the multiple impacts of the Russian invasion in its initial phase on the Ukrainian agricultural sector and reveals its intricate interconnections. We focus in particular on the initial phase of the war, covering the first five months, to examine the emerging impacts. In particular, we examine the scope, magnitude and dynamics of the impact and consequences of the Russian invasion of Ukraine. At the same time, we examine the policy responses of the Ukrainian government to mitigate these effects. To achieve these objectives, we have adopted a multifaceted approach that includes (i) the systematic collection and analysis of information from official sources and the Ukrainian press, (ii) a comprehensive review of Ukrainian laws and regulations, and (iii) a careful analysis of relevant statistical data. Finally, our research extends to the identification of key elements that are crucial for the further development of the Ukrainian agricultural sector.

The following sections of this paper are structured as follows. First, an overview of Ukrainian agriculture in the pre-war period is given. This is followed by an in-depth analysis of the impact of the war on the Ukrainian agricultural sector and its implications for global agricultural markets and food security. The paper then examines the agricultural policies implemented during the first five months of the war. Finally, a comprehensive discussion of the main findings and their implications for policy and research is presented.

Methodology

Our aim is to provide an in-depth examination of the Ukrainian agricultural landscape, with a particular focus on the challenges posed by the Russian-Ukrainian war in its initial phase and the corresponding agricultural policy responses in Ukraine. Due to the dynamic nature of the situation, it is not sufficient to rely solely on academic literature or coherent information from government agencies. Therefore, we use and combine a wider range of approaches to address the issues at hand, including collecting news from official websites and the Ukrainian press, studying laws and regulations adopted by the Ukrainian government, and analyzing statistics from the State Statistics Service of Ukraine, FAO, etc. We also participated in 25 conferences and webinars where farmers, experts and government officials discussed various aspects of the problems faced by Ukraine's agricultural sector during the war (see Supplementary Material (S1) for details).

The time frame of our analysis is the first five months of the war, from February 24, 2022 to July 24, 2022. This timeframe allows for a nuanced understanding of the impacts and responses of the initial phase, which may differ from later phases of the war. Our analysis aims to provide in-depth insights into this particular period, which may serve as a basis for subsequent studies to address later phases of the war and the post-war period.

Our methodological approach consists of three main steps. First, we studied news from the official website of the Ministry of Agrarian Policy and Food of Ukraine (MAPF), the Ukrainian press, as well as a specialized agribusiness website and news from the Ukrainian Agribusiness Club Association. The official website of the MAPF (minagro.gov.ua) provides primary and reliable information on government policies and actions related to agriculture. The Ukrainian press, represented by outlets such as unian.ua and forbes.ua, offered a variety of views and opinions on the impact of the war on agriculture. In addition, the specialized platform latifundist.com and the Ukrainian Agribusiness Club Association (ucab.ua) provided in-depth insights tailored to the interests and challenges of the agricultural sector. The combination of these sources ensured a comprehensive coverage of information, including market trends, economic analysis, and consumer behaviour. Cross-validation of information was achieved using multiple sources. In cases of duplicate news, data from the Ministry of Agricultural Policy was preferred for consistency and authenticity. This approach, using a comprehensive and diverse dataset, facilitated a thorough understanding of the impact of the war on Ukrainian agriculture.

Second, we analyzed the agricultural policy of Ukraine, studying the legislative and regulatory acts adopted by the Verkhovna Rada of Ukraine [12], the Cabinet of Ministers of Ukraine [13], and the Ministry of Agrarian Policy and Food of Ukraine [14] for the period from February 24, 2022, to July 24, 2022 (end of the data collection period).

Third, we analyzed statistical data from the State Statistic Service of Ukraine, the Food and Agriculture Organization, and other official sources [2,[15], [16], [17], [18]].

The outcome of these three methodological steps provides a comprehensive overview of the dynamics of the Ukrainian agricultural sector in the period from February 24, 2022 to July 24, 2022. This overall analysis includes an elucidation of the sector's challenges, a differentiation of the responses emanating from both farmers and the government, and a critical assessment of its latent potential. Moreover, this panoramic overview serves not only as a baseline understanding but also as a cornerstone for deriving key conclusions relevant to the formulation of agricultural policy and the prospective growth trajectories essential for the continued development of Ukraine's agricultural sector.

Results

The pre-war state of Ukrainian agriculture

The agricultural sector was an important part of the Ukrainian economy, covering 70 % of the country's territory with 41.3 million hectares of agricultural land. In 2021, privately owned agricultural land in Ukraine amounted to over 31 million hectares, of which about 28 million hectares were privately owned by 7 million individuals. The remaining land was owned by the state and local authorities, which amounted to more than 10 million hectares [19]. The contribution of agriculture to Ukraine's GDP exceeded 10 % in 2021 and accounted for 41 % of the country's total exports. The structure of agricultural production in Ukraine in 2021 included 81.4 % crop production, with cereals and pulses accounting for 38.5 % and industrial crops for 28 %. In addition, animal production accounted for 18.6 %, including 10.1 % for livestock and 5.4 % for milk production [16]. Ukrainian agricultural holdings mainly specialized in the production of export-oriented crops, such as cereals, pulses, sugar beet, and sunflowers. On the other hand, small farms focused on the production of vegetables, fruits, and other agricultural products to meet the needs of the domestic market (Appendix 1).

The Ukrainian agricultural sector played an integral role in the global economy. In 2021, agricultural exports from Ukraine reached $27 billion. Ukraine was the world's largest exporter of sunflower oil, accounting for 53.28 % of all exports in 2021. Significant quantities of maize, wheat, barley and rapeseed were also exported (Appendix 3).

Ukraine's agricultural policy has been shaped by key laws and decisions. The Law on State Support of Agriculture [21] and the 2017 Concept of Farm Development [22] have set priorities such as rural job creation, farm support, credit conditions, and income growth through land leasing. Agricultural producers paid the single tax based on the value of land, providing implicit benefits of about UAH1 4.3 billion per year.

The level of state support for agricultural producers in Ukraine, as measured by the Producer Support Estimate (PSE), averaged 1.7 % of gross agricultural income in 2019–2021, which is much lower than in EU countries [23].2 The areas of state support for the agricultural sector in 2021 (i.e., pre-war) included i) cheaper loans for agro-industrial development, ii) support for the development of livestock and agricultural processing, and iii) compensation for the cost of Ukrainian-made agricultural machinery [24]. Reducing the cost of credit was essential to support agriculture, and this was achieved through programs such as “Financial Support to Agriculture through Concessional Loans” and “Affordable Loans 5-7-9%". The latter provided partial interest rate compensation and loan guarantees to overcome collateral and credit history constraints for non-productive expenses. However, smaller farms (<100 ha) were not eligible for this program [25].

Budget support to producers, mainly in the form of tax benefits and production support through short-term loans and fixed capital accumulation, accounted for less than 1 % of the gross income of agricultural enterprises but has contributed to positive overall support to producers in recent years, including in 2021. In total, in 2021, support was provided under the following programs: livestock development and agricultural processing (UAH 1.6 billion); farming development (UAH 120.8 million); compensation for the cost of machinery (UAH 991.4 million); compensation for the interest rate on loans (UAH 1.2 billion); development of horticulture, viticulture and hop growing (512 million UAH); compensation for lost crops (108 million UAH); development of niche crops (50 million UAH); development of potato growing (53 million UAH); development of irrigation (16.7 million UAH); compensation of the unified social tax for farmers and employees and their families (0.6 million UAH) [24].

In March 2021, the Cabinet of Ministers of Ukraine approved the National Economic Strategy for the period until 2030 [26]. One of the main directions of the strategic course of economic policy was the agro-industrial sector and the food industry. The main goals of agricultural development were balancing production and development of agricultural products processing, the market infrastructure improvement and product sales optimization on domestic and foreign markets, as well as the implementation of stimulating and advisory agricultural policy [27].

Before the war, Ukrainian agriculture faced various challenges that hindered its potential to develop. These included issues such as legal complexity, logistical complexity, and structural constraints [28]. Pre-war agricultural legislation lacked transparency in land regulation and was not aligned with EU standards [29,30]. An unreliable legal framework, high macroeconomic risk and a closed land market for foreign investors discouraged investment in the sector [31]. Logistical challenges included costly rail transport, poor railway infrastructure, limited road capacity, and deteriorating water transport [32]. Intensive land use by farmers led to soil degradation and reduced land productivity [28]. The market also suffered from unequal access for farms, with small farmers facing lower prices compared to agroholdings [33]. In addition, the sector lacked innovation, with limited funding for initiatives and key innovations mainly implemented by agroholdings [34].

Government policy on the development of Ukrainian agriculture has been in a constant state of flux, with a clear lack of a coherent and well-defined trajectory. There was a lack of consensus within the government on the development of the Ministry of Agrarian Policy and Food of Ukraine. Thus, on August 29, 2019, the functions of the ministry were transferred to the Ministry of Economic Development, Trade and Agriculture. On December 17, 2020, the MAPF was revived. Moreover, a lack of consistency and coherence with other ministries characterized the Ministry's policies. Frequent ministerial changes also had a negative impact on the Ministry's effectiveness [35].

The impact of the war on the agricultural sector: issues and challenges

The agricultural sector has been significantly affected and challenged by the war in Ukraine. During the first five months of the war, active hostilities took place over a large part of the territory of Ukraine, in the northern, eastern, and southern regions of the country. Almost all Ukrainian territory, except two regions (Zakarpattia and Chernivtsi), was subjected to rocket attacks. A map of the hostilities during the five months of the war is provided in Appendix 5. The analysis of news articles from official sources, the Ukrainian press and business platforms resulted in a compilation of 252 news items (see the Supplementary material (S2)). The news items were classified into two major categories: foreign affairs and domestic affairs. The main foreign policy topics were international aid, international meetings, and exports. In domestic policy, the main topics were identified as livestock, crop production, legislation, citizen appeals and war aid. Our analysis has revealed significant challenges that confronted Ukraine's agricultural sector, including the scarcity of resources for agricultural production, the destruction of storage facilities, territorial mining activities, the blockade of Black Sea ports, and disruptions in logistics chains. The blockade of Ukrainian ports led to a spike in world prices and stranded 13 ships carrying some 600,000 tonnes of grain. The limited availability of railway border crossings for the transit of agricultural products (4 with Poland, 3 with Romania, 2 with Slovakia, 2 with Hungary and 2 with Moldova) caused further difficulties due to the different track sizes between the European and Ukrainian railway systems [36]. As a result, logistics costs increased, with the cost of transporting a tonne of wheat to the port of Constanta (Romania) rising by $160 (from $28–33 to $190) after several months of war. Despite the signing by Russia and Ukraine, with the support of the United Nations and Turkey, of the “Initiative on safe transportation of grain and foodstuffs from Ukrainian sea ports”, sea exports did not increase significantly. Traders remained reluctant to buy Ukrainian grain due to the high transport risks [37]. International companies incurred significant losses on their pre-war purchases, which led traders to buy Ukrainian grain on the condition of payment after passage through the Corridor and the Bosphorus. The closure of seaports highlighted the need to diversify transport routes and develop land and river export channels.

The blockade of the ports has led to a lack of storage capacity in the elevators. Agricultural producers were unable to export most of their previous harvest [38]. In addition, some grain elevators (i.e. facilities for the storage or holding of grains) were located in temporarily occupied territories or were destroyed.

The total grain storage capacity of Ukrainian agriculture before the war was 75 million tonnes. During the first five months of the war, 10 % of storage facilities were damaged or destroyed, 14 % were in Russian-occupied territory, and about 30 % remained filled with 22 million tonnes of the previous year's harvest [39] (Fig. 3).Fig. 3Ukrainian grain storage capacity from 24 February to July 24, 2022.Fig. 3Source: Ministry of Agrarian Policy and Food, [39].

Ukrainian grain storage capacity from 24 February to July 24, 2022.

The shortfall in storage capacity has been estimated at 10–25 million tonnes for storing the 2022 harvest [40]. Farmers had three main options to solve the problem of lack of storage capacity. The first is to use temporary storage sleeves. Ukrainian farmers had the experience of storing maize and wheat in them. However, storing sunflower and rapeseed in them is more complicated. The second option is to build new elevators and expand old ones in western Ukraine and run a narrow rail line from Europe. The third option is to build elevators in Europe and a wide railway line from Ukraine.

Another problem is that the war has led to a shortage of skilled labour in the agricultural sector [41]. The mobilisation of men in the Ukrainian Armed Forces, their participation in territorial defence and the migration of men and women have become major challenges, also for agricultural labor. In March 2022, the government put almost all professions needed in the agricultural sector on the reserve list. 200,000 workers were reserved for six months so that they could continue to work in agriculture and not be mobilised. Agronomists, zootechnicians, veterinarians, traders, logisticians and financiers were professions that experienced a shortage in Ukraine's agricultural sector.

One of the main problems for Ukrainian agricultural producers was the rising cost of production, as high energy prices meant high fertiliser and fuel prices, and logistics were expensive. By 2022, Ukrainian farmers were selling their products at a price that barely covered the cost of production [42]. The inability to realize grain stocks created a critical financial gap between income and expenditure. Without additional external financing and assuming the same export rates, the additional financing needs of the agricultural sector for harvesting and planting of winter crops amounted to about $4–5 billion [38]. To cover these costs, it was necessary to attract funding through the credit programme, with the provision of government guarantees and certain interest rate compensation. The government had developed a mechanism for government loans to rehabilitate war-affected farms. However, this support was not sufficient to provide sufficient working capital.

The prospect of grain warehouses overflowing with grain, the impossibility of selling it and the consequent lack of funds to purchase resources had raised serious questions about the feasibility of sowing for the 2023 harvest. As a result, according to Potaeva's findings, millions of hectares of fields in the controlled territory of Ukraine were potentially left uncultivated for subsequent harvests in 2022 [43]. Thousands of farms faced bankruptcy and land rents remained unpaid. Almost half of the 7 million landowners suffered a significant loss of income, amounting to UAH 30–50 billion due to non-payment of rents. The impact extended to some 2.7 million people employed in the agricultural sector, including 520,000 workers, a significant proportion of whom faced unemployment. The income of the state and individual farmers also fell as a result of the theft of Ukrainian agricultural products [44].

In the first five months of the war, some 600,000 tonnes of stolen grain were recorded [45]. Thefts were reported in the Donbass, Luhansk, Kharkiv, Kherson and Zaporizhzhya regions.

Another problem was the contamination of agricultural land, which was estimated at around 25,000 square kilometres [46]. It was estimated that cleaning up this land would require about 10 million man-days, at a total cost of about €2 billion, assuming a daily cost of €200 per day [39]. This raised concerns about the systematic recording and analysis of contaminated sites [47] for which an internet portal, military.feodal.online, was set up. Through this portal, users could report detected ammunition, abandoned military weapons and areas contaminated with explosive ordnance by entering the data directly into a database.

In the face of the challenges of war, Ukrainian farmers showed a high degree of resilience [18]. They continued to produce agricultural products, even in the occupied territories and “wounded” fields. However, agricultural producers faced many problems. Many micro, small and medium-sized farms struggled in the first months of the war to generate income from their production from the year 2021 due to lost marketing possibilities. Restricted exports, a sharp decline in domestic prices for agricultural products and increased costs for material resources contributed to a significant decrease in the working capital of agricultural enterprises. As a result, most farms were uncertain about sowing winter crops [48] and increasingly in need of cash and access to affordable credit. According to the KSE AgroCentre, the war caused direct losses to the agricultural sector of $4.29 billion, and indirect losses to agriculture (due to reduced production, disrupted logistics and lower prices for export-oriented goods) are estimated at $23.3 billion [8,9]. These different estimates illustrate the complexity of assessing war losses and highlight the need for a detailed study of the impact of the war on Ukraine's agricultural sector.

The effects of war on Ukrainian exports and global food security

The global economy reacted swiftly to the situation in Ukraine, with higher oil, gas, and food prices, increased inflation and significant turbulence in commodity and financial markets. FAO's monthly real food price indices reached unprecedented levels, in particular 156.3 for the Food Price Index in March 2022, 246.4 for the Oil Price Index and 169.8 for the Cereal Price Index in May 2022 (Appendix 6).

The start of the Russian-Ukrainian war led to high food and fertilizer prices, but with effective support markets eventually stabilized [50]. Banse [4] estimates that the war caused global economic damage, resulting in a third asymmetric shock, the effects of which would have to be dealt with within 15 years. The first period of the war had a significant asymmetric impact on the economy. This was due to the direct [51,52] and indirect [53,54] effects, as well as the exponential growth of uncertainty, which negatively affected consumption, investment, GDP, and employment [55].

In addition, estimates for wheat exports in 2021/22 included reductions of 5.0 million tonnes for Ukraine and 3.5 million tonnes for the Russian Federation. These adjustments were attributed to port closures in the former and financial and freight challenges in the latter. At the same time, domestic consumption in Ukraine was around 20 million tonnes. By the end of July 2022, Ukraine had reached a capacity of about 3 million tonnes per month on roads, river ports and railways (5–6 million tonnes per month before the war).

The war in Ukraine has exacerbated global food security issues and posed a significant risk to the achievement of various sustainability goals [2,56,57]. A 50 % reduction in wheat exports from Ukraine and Russia could increase wheat prices by 15 %, leading to an 8 % reduction in wheat consumption and higher household food expenditures [58]. Disrupted exports from the Black Sea region and high prices further destabilized global food security. The Russian-Ukrainian war has caused severe food insecurity and malnutrition in Egypt, Yemen, Tunisia, and Lebanon, while African countries such as Congo and Tanzania were also at risk [59]. The impact of the war in Ukraine on food security in high-income European countries was negligible, but price increases affected food affordability, especially for low-income households [60]. However, global demand for cereals was eventually met as countries such as Australia, India, and the US increased exports to cover the deficit [61].

To reduce the impact of the war on food security, the FAO, the European Commission, and other organizations developed policy recommendations such as promoting open global trade in food and fertilizers, diversifying food suppliers and expanding social safety nets [1,62].

Adopting laws related to the agricultural sector.

In May, the Verkhovna Rada passed two laws aimed at streamlining bureaucratic procedures in the agricultural sector. In July, the law “Improving access to facilities for storage of grain and/or oilseeds” was also adopted. In particular, two laws were promulgated in March to change the Minister of Agricultural Policy and Food. This change in ministerial leadership subsequently led to personnel adjustments at lower levels of the ministry, including the positions of deputy ministers and department heads. As a result, it is reasonable to postulate that the change in ministerial leadership is likely to have had an impact on decision-making processes in the area of agricultural policy.

The Cabinet of Ministers of Ukraine has adopted a reactive approach to policy-making since the beginning of the war. It introduced many changes in the agricultural sector. In the early days of the war, the Cabinet of Ministers of Ukraine adopted a resolution to automatically register tax invoices that only reflect transactions with agricultural products and are made by a micro or small enterprise with the corresponding main type of activity. At the beginning of March 2022, the Cabinet of Ministers of Ukraine introduced a ban on the export of the following agricultural products: rye, oats, buckwheat, millet, sugar, salt, live cattle, meat and edible offal of cattle. Later, mineral and chemical fertilisers were added to this list. Licensing was also introduced for wheat, mixtures of wheat and rye, maize, meat and eggs of domestic fowl and sunflower oil. Also, additional seed certification procedures were cancelled for the period of martial law. In early April, the Cabinet of Ministers of Ukraine cancelled the zero quota and introduced a licensing system for the export of cattle meat.

In the area of financial support, the government significantly reduced the amount of support and cancelled the projects “Basic Loan for SMEs and Mid-Cap Companies” and “Basic Loan for the Agricultural Sector - Ukraine” and diverted part of the loan proceeds from the financing agreements in the amount of EUR 639 million to the general fund of the state budget.

To support the agricultural sector, the government took a number of measures: minimising bureaucratic procedures for the agricultural sector, allowing the use of agricultural machinery without registration, simplifying the import of seeds, introducing a zero rate of excise duty and reducing the amount of VAT on fuel. The government has also introduced special state programmes: low-cost loans, support for processing companies, the development of horticulture and the construction of greenhouses. The government also provided alternative logistics networks for exports by blocking ports. Tax exemptions have been introduced, providing for changes in the payment of land for state and municipal forms of ownership during martial law.

In early March, the Ukrainian Cabinet of Ministers adopted a decision to provide preferential loans to small and medium-sized agricultural producers with a turnover of no more than 20 million euros, which corresponds to an enterprise cultivating up to 10,000 ha. Loans were provided to agricultural producers for the purchase of fixed assets and to cover expenses related to the conduct of agricultural activities.

In response to the needs of the war, the Ministry drew up lists of companies to ensure the urgent needs of the functioning of the state under martial law conditions, of which 36 documents were recorded. This means that, in addition to its efforts to meet the military needs of the agricultural sector, the Ministry has continued to carry out regular activities aimed at protecting intellectual property. The government of Ukraine implemented various programs and measures to support the agricultural sector. Tax changes, VAT reductions, and the abolition of certain taxes have been aimed at providing financial support and incentives to businesses. Affordable credit and deregulation initiatives have made it somewhat easier to do business, and quick customs clearance has speeded up trade processes. However, the effectiveness of these programs has been suboptimal, resulting in limited availability to farmers. Traditional support programs in the agricultural sector have all but ceased, and allocated budget funds have been diverted to the reserve fund. Only a few programs, such as the 5-7-9 and portfolio credit programs, continue to operate. Efforts have been made to increase employment in agriculture through the construction of greenhouses and the development of horticulture. It should be noted that concessionary loans remained inaccessible to farmers with less than 100 ha of land, according to official data. In practice, lending institutions have set a higher threshold of 500 ha to qualify for a loan. As a result, a significant number of micro and small agricultural producers did not have access to affordable finance.

Foreign partners supported Ukrainian farmers on a piecemeal basis [63]. The European Union allocated funds to support small agricultural producers, providing a payment per hectare. Small producers cultivating up to 120 ha received a grant of UAH 3100 ($96) per hectare and UAH 10,300 ($318.5) per cow [64]. FAO had developed and updated its rapid response plan to assist Ukraine. It provided $115.4 million in assistance to nearly 1 million rural people [65]. Of these, 80,000 people received cash assistance to meet basic needs in the most affected regions of eastern and southern Ukraine [2]. The United States of America, through the United States Agency for International Development, launched the $100 million Agricultural Resilience Initiative for Ukraine (AGRI) in July 2022 [66]. The initiative aimed to support Ukrainian agricultural exports and mitigate the global food security crisis.

During the first five months of the war, Ukrainian farmers found themselves in a precarious situation, requiring assistance from government agencies and international organizations to ensure their livelihoods and the seamless continuity of essential services. Based on the previous analysis, a number of crucial directions have been identified that warrant prioritization in the context of short-term agricultural policy (see also [35]). Moreover, these directions require priority attention in future agricultural policy considerations. Firstly, the acceleration of the VAT refund process to improve cash flow within the sector. Second, the implementation of an expanded financing program specifically tailored to the needs of the agricultural sector. Particular attention should be paid to supporting small and medium-sized farmers, who often face greater financial constraints. Third, ensure a consistent supply of inputs, including fertilizers, pesticides, and machinery, at reduced prices to reduce production costs and increase farmer resilience. Facilitating the supply of quality seeds to farmers could increase agricultural productivity, leading to improved crop yields and overall sector performance. In addition, the reduction of tariffs for rail transport services could contribute to the reduction of the financial burden of the transport of agricultural products, thus improving the competitiveness of Ukrainian agricultural products in the market. Finally, the exploration of alternative logistics routes and transport options could be of crucial importance. This could not only mitigate supply chain disruptions but also enable the smooth movement of agricultural goods, contributing to the overall stability of the sector. By focusing on these strategic directions, the government and international organizations could provide vital support to Ukrainian farmers, safeguarding their livelihoods and sustaining the agricultural sector in the challenging circumstances of the war.

Discussion

The protracted nature of the Russian-Ukrainian war required a pragmatic approach to agricultural policy management in Ukraine. After five months of war, the primary objective for the agricultural sector was survival rather than the pursuit of a well-defined strategic agenda. Farmers have been forced to make tactical decisions to ensure the continued operation of their farms. These decisions have included securing essential inputs and feeds, procuring fuel and fertilizer, ensuring the welfare of livestock, implementing measures to ensure the safe storage of agricultural goods, and identifying viable markets for their products. The introduction of diversified agricultural production proved to be a mitigating factor against the negative effects of war and played a crucial role in ensuring food security in rural areas [67]. Agroholdings have faced similar challenges, including the destruction of assets, limited access to working capital and disrupted export channels. Despite these challenges, it is important to consider the prospects for Ukraine's post-war agricultural sector. Historically, the post-war period has been characterized by revival and growth in different contexts [68,69]. In the case of Ukraine, the agricultural sector is expected to play a key role in the country's economic development. Based on the comprehensive study of the pre-war and early-war agricultural sector and reflecting on the issues and policy responses identified above, we synthesize data from authoritative sources, including the Cabinet of Ministers of Ukraine [70], to formulate ideas for the way forward. From our perspective, the essential components for post-war agricultural development opportunities are as follows:1)Human capital plays a key role in the development of the agricultural sector. The outbreak of war led to significant population movements, with some 7.5 million refugees, 6.5 million internally displaced persons and nearly 2 million Ukrainians deported to Russia [71]. The agricultural sector faced a labor shortage as some farmers joined the Ukrainian armed forces. Attracting and recruiting new workers requires creating favorable conditions for their engagement in agriculture. Priority is being given to facilitating the return of post-war members of the Ukrainian Armed Forces (predominantly male) and the repatriation of emigrants (predominantly female) from abroad. These labor pools have unique experiences and a strong motivation to contribute to the reconstruction of the country. A fundamental aspect of agricultural development is the promotion of agricultural education and scientific endeavor. To achieve this, government support for agricultural professions is crucial, including budget allocations within higher education institutions for agricultural disciplines, as well as the establishment of a network of agricultural technical schools and colleges. This opportune moment allows for targeted skills development, especially in the context of future developments in the agricultural sector, e.g., concerning digitalization and new agricultural technologies [72,73]. In this respect, the use of digitalization and robotics, such as robots working on harvesters and tractors, represents a potential solution to the problem of labor shortages [[74], [75], [76]]. However, it also raises concerns about access to finance and the limits of automation in most agricultural jobs.2)In the post-war agricultural sector, one of the most promising avenues is the strengthening and development of small and medium-sized agricultural enterprises. These enterprises have significant potential to create new employment opportunities, promote competition in the domestic market and contribute to Ukraine's food security by facilitating market access for small farmers. Support for small farms is crucial for socioeconomic security and for supporting displaced people [67,77]. In addition, the development of small farmers has a positive impact on biodiversity conservation [78,79]. Supporting small farmers can revitalize and develop rural areas while building sustainable regional supply chains through small farmers plays a crucial role in facilitating comprehensive rehabilitation and growth.3)Ukraine's agricultural sector enjoyed improved access to EU markets amid the war. This development offers significant prospects for expanding trade opportunities and fostering deeper agricultural integration between Ukraine and the EU. In addition, strengthening climate-smart and sustainable production systems will be crucial to ensure the long-term productivity and resilience of the agricultural sector. The war in Ukraine has raised deep concerns about the extent of the damage, its impact and the country's prospects. Given the documented destruction and the protracted nature of the conflict, the consequences are likely to be severe and recovery will require significant time, financial and human resources. Beyond its impact on Ukraine, the war has had a global impact with far-reaching implications. Armed conflicts often exacerbate food insecurity, impacting areas beyond war zones [86]. In addition to the economic impact, there is a significant likelihood of environmental degradation, with potential impacts on neighbouring countries through interconnected ecosystems and rivers, and on more distant countries through disruptions to global food supply networks and loss of biodiversity [84,87]. In addition, the war could impede progress on decarbonization initiatives. Undeniably, the war poses challenges to Ukraine's pursuit of sustainable development goals. As a result, both the Ukrainian government and the international community need to prioritize addressing these pressing issues without delay.

Conclusion

This study analyses the impact of the Russian invasion on Ukrainian agriculture in its initial phase, i.e. the first five months from February 24, 2022 to July 24, 2022. We also analyzed the responses of Ukrainian agricultural policy and outlined key elements for the post-war development of the Ukrainian agricultural sector. We have combined the analysis of various sources, i.e. news from official websites and the Ukrainian press, the study of laws and regulations of the Ukrainian government, and the analysis of statistics from the State Statistics Service of Ukraine, FAO, etc. Our analysis shows that the war in Ukraine, in its early stages, has led to significant losses in the Ukrainian agricultural sector and poses a threat to global food security. Our analysis has identified several key challenges arising from the war, including: i) limited resources for agricultural production, ii) the blockade of Ukraine's Black Sea ports, iii) the destruction of 25 % of grain storage capacity, iv) the contamination of over 25,000 km2 of agricultural land, v) the theft of over 600,000 tonnes of Ukrainian grain. We show that in response to these challenges, the Ukrainian government has adopted a reactive approach to policymaking, supporting farmers through tax simplification, affordable credit and deregulation. The main policy adjustments in the first five months of the war were aimed at financing the agricultural sector, providing inputs and resources to farmers at reduced prices, and improving logistics. The international community played an important role in assisting Ukraine, with the European Union funding small farmers, the FAO developing a rapid response plan, and USAID supporting Ukrainian agricultural exports.

Our findings have policy implications. Supporting farmers requires a multipronged approach, including assistance in accessing resources, improving logistical efficiency, and expanding access to global markets. In terms of post-war development, key areas of focus include the repatriation and capacity building of displaced persons, the promotion of small and medium-sized farms, integration into global markets, a special emphasis on ecological and sustainable practices, and technological advances. Policies need to be designed to facilitate and support these efforts.

This study has limitations. First, it focuses on the initial phase of the war, particularly the first five months. This focus may not capture the wider impact of the war on Ukrainian agriculture and agricultural policy. Second, the data used in this study come from official channels, the Ukrainian press, relevant legislation and statistical data. While these sources provide valuable insights, they may not provide the full picture. The inclusion of farmers' perceptions and experiences during the war could add depth to the research. However, given the scope of this study, we have opted for a macro-analytical approach, which we believe can make an important contribution to the statistical analysis of the impact of the Russian invasion on the Ukrainian economy. Third, we have not attempted to compare our analysis of Ukraine with situations in other wars, so we cannot further generalise our findings.

Future research could address these gaps and also include the effects and policy adjustments in the later stages of the war to allow for comparison. In addition, a broader understanding of how agricultural policy can effectively address post-war challenges is crucial. In this sense, future research should address how the war-related changes in the economy and agricultural policy can reshape the vision for the recovery and development of Ukraine's agricultural sector in the future, especially in light of Ukraine's candidate status for EU accession, which holds the potential for transformative change in the agricultural sector.

The consequences of the war extend beyond Ukraine's borders, affecting not only the country's agricultural sector and economy but also posing a global risk. The international community must therefore act decisively to mitigate these risks and ensure the achievement of sustainable development goals, even after peace agreements are reached.
