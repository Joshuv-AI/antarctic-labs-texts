An update on schistosomicide and tumoricide in the future: arachidonic acid
Authors: Hatem Tallima, Rashika El Ridi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Blood-dwelling schistosomes and cancer severely impact human health, welfare, and survival, essentially because of their ability to evade host immune cells and humoral effectors. The host immune defenses interact with the surface membrane of the worm and tumor cell. Schistosomes use their sphingomyelin (SM)-rich outer lipid bilayer to form with the surrounding water molecules a tight hydrogen bond barrier in which they open, at will, pores that allow entry of nutrients but not lethal complement components and antibodies. In parasite-free humans, impaired expression of one or more cellular genes linked to lipid metabolism may allow the accumulation of SM at the plasma membrane. Drastic changes in surface membrane fluidity, permeability and integrity deprive the cell from its defense mechanisms and promote chaos in the signals it receives and emits, notably those controlling proliferation and locomotion. The hydrolysis of SM at the surface membrane of schistosomes and cancer cells to phosphocholine and the pro-apoptotic ceramide is feasible via the activation of membrane-associated neutral sphingomyelinase (nSMase). Arachidonic acid (ARA), a critical nutrient for all organisms and cells, is a powerful nSMase activator. Accordingly, ARA is the safest molecule for preventing schistosome and tumor cell development and growth. If readily accessible for human use in terms of availability and cost effectiveness, ARA could be considered a schistosomicide and tumoricide in the future.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12944-025-02823-z.

Methods-literature search and selection

The medical literature was searched for potentially relevant articles that address the topics of immune evasion, growth, survival and control of schistosomes and cancer cells. The purpose of this review is to summarize and synthesize existing research, including our own, with the aim of evaluating the contributions of sphingomyelin to the development, growth, and immune evasion of schistosomes and cancer cells, and the schistosomicidal and tumoricidal potential of the nutrient, conditionally essential fatty acid, and neutral sphingomyelinase-activator: arachidonic acid. A literature search was performed at PubMed/MEDLINE, Scopus, Google Scholar, ResearchGate, and Web of Science via the keywords “dietary arachidonic acid, arachidonic acid metabolites, schistosomes and cancer immune evasion, sphingomyelin properties, sphingomyelinases’ functions, inhibitors and activators” (January 1975 through September 2025}. The articles were selected and analyzed with the aim of delivering a balanced, comprehensive, and innovative perspective and overview.

Immune evasion of schistosomes

Schistosoma mansoni, Schistosoma hematobium and Schistosoma japonicum, digenean, dioecious, sexually dimorphic schistosomes, which exclusively live and sexually reproduce in the vasculature of vertebrates, hence the name blood flukes, cause schistosomiasis or bilharziasis, a severe debilitating disease. The infection impacts the health, quality of life, organ functions, and morbidity and mortality rates of 240 million people in poor rural communities of 80 tropical and subtropical countries [1, 2]. During the infective stage, the fresh water free-swimming cercaria exits the species-specific intermediate snail host and may lose infectivity within a few hours if it fails to permeate the epidermis of a suitable human or animal final host [2]. Upon epidermal stratum corneum penetration, the cercaria glycoprotein- and glycolipid-rich glycocalyx cover is shed, likely because of the increase in the surrounding salinity [3]. Skin linoleic acid (LA) appears to increase calcium influx into invading cercariae, leading to tail detachment [4, 5]. Skin cells produce free fatty acids, which are used by cercariae to facilitate invasion and development. Skin cells respond to injury by releasing type 1, type 2 and type 17 cytokines, which are known to counteract each other, and inflammatory eicosanoids [2, 6, 7]. These inflammatory products are antagonized by eicosanoids and mediators derived from the cercariae [2, 7–9]. The invading larvae have an active arachidonate cascade [9], while most skin cells lack the delta 6 and delta 5 desaturase enzymes necessary for LA conversion into arachidonic acid (ARA) [10, 11]. Additionally, the epidermis lacks blood and lymph capillaries, and accordingly, serum antibodies, which are able to impair the viability of tailless cercariae in vitro, have no relevant action in vivo [12, 13].

Glycocalyx-free, tailless larvae, now called schistosomula, take 48 to 72 h to access the different epidermal strata. Considerable metabolic changes end by replacing the conventional, trilaminate, one-lipid bilayer cover of the worm’s syncytial tegument with an uncommonly heptalaminated, two-lipid bilayer surface membrane [14–16]. The schistosomula are now ready to enter the dermis, locate a blood capillary, penetrate the bloodstream, and finally abode. This unusual seven-layered surface membrane is a prerequisite for all blood flukes to access and exclusively live in a medium containing > 2% of the total immune cells of the human body at any given time; it is a central player in leukocyte migration and circulation [17, 18] and a reservoir and travel route of antibodies, complement components, cytokines, chemokines, and other mediators [19, 20]. Accordingly, the molecules exposed on the apical lipid leaflet in the cartoon of Da’adara and Skelly [21] must be covered. Otherwise, copious amounts of antibodies of all specificities would bind to the strongly immunogenic determinants at the host‒parasite interface, relentlessly inducing antibody-dependent cell-mediated cytotoxicity (ADCC), which would lead to surrounding the worm with lymphoid cells and its eventual death. However, the leading author and colleagues have clearly documented the absence of any lymphoid cells around intravascular schistosomes in vivo [22]. With respect to ADCC-mediated death, all intravascular stages are known to be entirely resistant to ADCC, and schistosomes have survived in human blood capillaries for decades [23, 24]. Additionally, lung-stage, juvenile and adult worms are refractory to antibody-dependent complement (C)-mediated cytotoxicity as well as the C alternative and mannose-binding lectin activation pathways [25–28]. The generation and deposition of C3a and C5a anaphylatoxins fail to recruit immune cells around the worm [22]. Complement 3b and 5b opsonins may not mediate the phagocytosis of large organisms, and the discovery of pores induced by C5b-9 is not anticipated.

Blood lymphocytes and myeloid cells do not directly interact even with young schistosomula migrating to the lung and then to the liver and certainly not with adult worms in copula. This interaction is dependent on specific antibodies binding to molecules putatively exposed on the schistosome apical lipid leaflet but has been shown to have no effect on parasite well-being or survival [23–28]. Schistosome immune evasion is predominantly attributed to the acquisition of blood glycolipids, serum albumin, sialoglycoproteins, complement components, regulatory proteins, and immunoglobulins, i.e., soluble and amphipathic molecules, a phenomenon referred to as molecular mimicry [2, 29–32]. The host components must then make a tightly impervious coat around the whole length of the worm to prevent the approach of innocuous specific antibodies and complement factors. The host component-based shield interferes more or less effectively with nutrient uptake or the access of substrates needed for the worm-protective functions of apical membrane-located enzymes [21, 33, 34].

Schistosome immune evasion is also believed to be due to the ability of the esophageal gland to discern differences between orally ingested blood cells, filter out immune cells and eliminate them in the esophagus, thus preventing ADCC of the cells lining the digestive tract [35]. The major mechanism of schistosome immune escape involves the release of a plethora of mediators and molecules believed to be intended to modulate and prevent host immune effector generation or harm [30, 36–38]. The large number of schistosome-derived putative immune modulators implies that the immune system is a major schistosome enemy; therefore, it is important for the worm to devise strategies and produce molecules to counteract its threat. If that is entirely true, then schistosomes, like other worm pathogens, should strive better, stronger, and longer in immune-deficient or immune-suppressed hosts [39, 40]. The retarded development and poor reproduction of schistosomes in SCID (severe combined immunodeficiency), athymic, and T or B-cell-depleted mice indicate that healthy schistosomes are indifferent to host immune antibody and cell threats and, in contrast, exploit and depend on immune cells and products to secure a safe intravascular journey from the skin to the final venous space, persistence and longevity therein, and, above all, effective progeny transmission [41–51].

Ex vivo lung-stage S. mansoni and S. hematobium extracted from the lungs of mice and hamsters, respectively, under optimal media conditions, temperature, and CO2, appear elongated, motile, contractile and characteristic and undeniable as if within a plastic or nylon envelope [52]. Surface membrane immunofluorescence (IF) experiments indicate that such healthy and intact larvae do not bind the serum antibodies of mice immunized twice with irradiated cercariae. Lung-stage schistosomula do not feed on blood but do not allow IF binding of antibodies specific to the extracellular loop of the S. mansoni glucose transporter SGTP4, which must be localized in the apical lipid bilayer to ensure the import of glucose [21]. These findings indicate that molecules at the host‒parasite interface are not exposed to antibody or substrate access but are shielded, covered, and concealed (Table 1). The incubation of the lung-stage larvae in 90% corn oil for 6 h allowed access of the serum antibodies to the surface membrane antigens of the otherwise intact larvae. This experiment was the first to suggest that the barrier protecting worm molecules at the parasite‒host interface is essentially sensitive to the action of unsaturated fatty acids and that apical membrane antigens are confined to lipid-rich sites [52]. Methyl-beta-cyclodextrin-mediated extraction of surface membrane cholesterol allowed the exposure of S. mansoni but not S. hematobium larvae apical antigens to antibody binding via IF. These findings indicate that the outer lipid bilayer cholesterol plays a marginal role in the sequestration of surface membrane antigens [53]. Exposure of S. mansoni and S. hematobium larvae and adult worms to neutral sphingomyelinase (nSMase) inhibitors and activators suggested that sphingomyelin (SM) is the principal player in masking and concealing worm apical bilayer molecules and worm survival in vitro and in vivo [54, 55]. Treatment of lung-stage schistosomula with inhibitors and activators of SM biosynthesis and hydrolysis revealed that equilibrium in SM breakdown and biosynthesis allows nutrients, enzyme substrates, oligopeptides, and immune metabolites of < 600 kDa, but not larger molecules and especially antibodies, to access transporters and enzymes at the host-parasite interface [56]. all these small molecules are filtered at the apical lipid bilayer before accessing the inner lipid bilayer and the syncytial tegument. Every SM species was enriched in the surface membrane of male and female schistosomes [57]. The occurrence of the protein product of the genome-encoded nSMase gene was recorded in the tegument and gut lining of S. mansoni and S. hematobium [58–60]. Oxidative stress, notably reactive oxygen species (ROS) and nitric oxide (NO), elicits rapid and significant increases in nSMase levels and subsequent SM hydrolysis [60–62]. The impairment of SM-based barrier integrity likely explains the need for blood flukes to counteract the action of these harmful factors [36–38]. Notably, in a cross-sectional analysis that is part of a larger, longitudinal project conducted in three Brazilian villages, the serum content of eight SM species showed considerable differences among uninfected controls and individuals with low and high S. mansoni loads [63].

Sphingomyelin synthesis and breakdown

Sphingomyelin consists of a head group, phosphocholine, a backbone of aliphatic amino alcohols, and a fatty acid. The critical enzymes involved in SM synthesis are serine palmitoyl transferase and SM synthases (Fig. 1). Sphingomyelin synthase (SMS), which reversibly catalyzes the conversion of ceramide and phosphatidylcholine to SM and diacyl glycerol, is thought to be powerfully activated by 2-hydroxyoleic acid, a synthetic fatty acid and potent antitumor drug that induces sphingolipidosis-mediated cell cycle arrest and apoptosis [64]. However, 2-hydroxyoleic acid was recently found to be unable to activate SMS or lead to SM accumulation in cancer cells or mouse tissue homogenates. It inhibited rather than stimulated recombinant SMS1 and SMS2 activity but significantly reduced the cell phosphatidylcholine content, thus impacting the cell membrane structure and functions [65, 66]. Membrane-associated nSMase 2, encoded by SM phosphodiesterase gene 3 (SMPD3), is a pivotal enzyme in SM metabolism. It cleaves SM phosphodiester bonds to the bioactive lipid ceramide and phosphorylcholine. In mammals, nSMase 2 is composed of an N-terminal domain containing two hydrophobic segments (HS1 and HS2) that allow insertion into the inner leaflet of the plasma membrane, followed by a juxtamembrane region, which is a membrane-associated, lipid-activated linker, and a large C-terminal catalytic domain. The binding of lipids, notably phosphatidylserine and polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs), to the N-terminal domain and the linker acts as a conformational switch, which is critical for enzyme activation [62, 67–70]. Moderate nSMase2 activation may also be induced via the binding of phosphatidic acid to the N-terminal HS1 [71]. Notably, ARA is readily incorporated into phosphatidylserine and other anionic phospholipids, specifically at the sn-2 position of the glycerol backbone [72]. Arachidonic acid schistosomicidal impact

The incubation of lung-stage schistosomula with 10 (S. hematobium) or 20 (S. mansoni) µM ARA for 1/2 h led to the exposure of all test larvae to specific antibody binding. A slightly higher ARA concentration and exposure time led to larval death [54]. These findings (Table 1; Supplementary Fig. 1, Additional File 1) suggest that hosts harboring plasma ARA concentrations of approximately 50 µM may resist S. mansoni and S. hematobium infection. Arachidonic acid plasma concentrations ranging from 40 µM to 1 mM are readily detected in healthy young North Americans and Canadians [74, 75]. Higher ARA levels were recorded in the serum of healthy Singaporean men and women [76]. Information on ARA content in the systemic circulation of children and adults in African countries is scarce or unavailable [77, 78].

Female and male adult schistosomes are killed in vitro following exposure to 2.5 mM ARA for 3 h, resulting in disintegration of the double lipid bilayer and extensive damage to the syncytial tegument and underlying muscles [79]. Data on ARA content in portal circulation are not widely available, and it is unlikely that ARA levels will exceed 1.5 mM. Continuous exposure to even 1.0 mM ARA may eventually harm the worms and may explain the in vivo impact of ARA on schistosomicidal activity in mice and hamsters infected with S. mansoni and S. hematobium, respectively [54, 79], and in children with low and high S. mansoni infection intensities [80, 81].

Importantly, ARA impacts eggs obtained from the liver and small intestine of S. mansoni-infected mice. A substantial percentage of ex vivo eggs exposed to 30–500 µM ARA hatch before exposure to water and strong light, releasing dead miracidia [82]. Notably, exposure to ARA concentrations as low as 15 µM elicited a significant (P < 0.05) increase in the percentage of eggs that failed to hatch after exposure to water and light (Fig. 2). The resistance of albino Rattus norvegicus rats to S. mansoni infection and the semipermissiveness of CD-1 mice (Mus musculus) to S. hematobium were associated with consistently and significantly (P < 0.05) greater ARA contents in serum and lung and liver tissue than in fully susceptible hosts, indicating that ARA is an endoschistosomicide [84]. Natural S. mansoni infection in the water rat, Nectomys squamipes, was attributed to ARA-rich lipid stores in the liver [85]. Interestingly, the stunted growth of S. japonicum in SCID mice compared with normal mice was associated with the top enriched serum ARA metabolites before and after infection with S. japonicum [48]. The rodent Microtus fortis is perhaps the only mammal that is resistant to S. japonicum infection. Large numbers of leukocytes were found attached to S. japonicum and S. mansoni retrieved from M. fortis, indicating that damage to the shield exposes surface membrane antigens to host immune effectors. Invading S. japonicum die before day 22 in the liver, which is characterized by high ARA metabolite contents [86]. The endoschistosomicidal impact of ARA was additionally recorded in the snail intermediate host, whereby resistance of Biomphalaria alexandrina and Bulinus truncatus to infection with miracidia of S. mansoni and S. hematobium, respectively, was related to high LA and ARA contents in hemolymph and soft body tissue [87].

Importantly, the correlation between high ARA content and resistance to parasites is not restricted to schistosomes. Merino sheep resistance to infection with gastrointestinal nematodes was found to be associated with the overrepresentation of biological processes related to ARA metabolism and metabolites [88]. Arachidonic acid has been shown to have an antimalarial effect and has been suggested as a potential fighter against protozoan, nematode and tropical diseases [89–93] and against viruses, bacteria, and fungi [94].

Arachidonic acid mediates the effects of schistosomiasis drugs, infection, and vaccines

Micromolar amounts of Sclareol and a synthesized derivative were shown to kill larval, juvenile, and adult S. mansoni. The mechanism of action involves an increase in the abundance of worm endogenous free ARA and metabolites associated with blebbing, disruption and apoptosis of the outer membrane [95]. Additionally, in vitro PZQ worm treatment elicited alterations in ARA metabolism in adult Schistosoma mekongi [96] but not S. mansoni [95].

Considerable differences were recently reported in the ARA contents of the liver and small intestine of S. mansoni-infected mice. Naïve mice presented averages of 50 and 200 µg ARA/mg liver and small intestine protein extracts, respectively. While the ARA content in liver extracts started to increase at 12 weeks (wk) post infection (PI) to attain the highest levels at 20 wk PI, the ARA content in small intestine extracts sharply declined at 8 wk PI and remained at low levels until wk 20 PI [97]. These findings support a fundamental role for ARA in the attrition of S. mansoni eggs trapped in the liver of experimental mice, especially eggs that continue to pour after the periovular granulomas begin to decline in size and cellularity following maximum development at 16 wk PI. Eggs that reach the mucosa of the small intestine are spared ARA attack and are poised to exit the lumen and then to the exterior to continue their life cycle [97]. Arachidonic acid was also found to be predominant within the hepatic periovular granulomas of S. japonicum-infected hamsters [98].

Outbred CD-1 mice were immunized with the cysteine peptidase prototype, papain in an active or enzymatically inactive form, or S. mansoni gut cysteine peptidases in recombinant, peptide, or multiple antigen peptide constructs. Two immunizations of adjuvant-free or alum-emulsified immunogens consistently induced highly significant (P < 0.0005-< 0.0001) reductions in the challenge worm burden. Egg counts in the liver and small intestine remain unacceptably high, despite impaired vitality and viability. These findings were associated with a significant (P < 0.05) increase in the ARA content in the serum, lungs and liver at 7- and 17-days PI and until the end of the experiment at 7 wk PI. Arachidonic acid appears to contribute to the attrition of worms and worm eggs and to play a predominant role in promoting the surface membrane stoutness and reproductive functions of surviving worms [82, 99, 100].

Mechanisms of action

Intravascular schistosomes use surface-rich SM and aqueous environments to generate a strong, tight hydrogen bond belt that prevents the translocation of reactive oxygen and nitrogen species and small solutes. Complement components and antibody passage are prevented from being too large to pass through such structures [101, 102]. Schistosome larvae, juveniles, and male and female adults allow the entry of host-derived ARA (304.47 g/mol), which is necessary for normal worm physiology, life and reproduction, as these worms are unable to synthesize ARA de novo. Adult S. mansoni microsomal and cytosolic extracts display lipoxygenase but not cyclooxygenase activity; however, in addition to free ARA, large amounts of leukotrienes and prostaglandins have been detected in 2-propanol extracts of cercariae, worms and eggs [103]. The intake of high ARA concentrations activates worm membrane-associated nSMase, leading to SM hydrolysis, hydrogen bond barrier disintegration, and the accumulation of ceramides on the surface and in the cytosol [60]. Lipid peroxidation, but not the activation of caspase 3/7, is another major mechanism of the in vitro schistosomicidal impact of ARA [60]. Nevertheless, exogenous ARA supplementation has repeatedly been shown to increase the intracellular levels of glutathione (GSH) and other antioxidant defenses, thereby conferring resistance to, rather than eliciting, oxidative stress [61, 104]. Arachidonic acid affects the levels of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and nitric oxide (NO), which are able to penetrate the worm shield, revealing worm protective potential. In addition, low ROS content inhibits nSMase activity [60, 61], thus providing escape for the worm from the deleterious effect of high ARA concentrations. In support of these findings, incubation of adult S. mansoni with 10 mM ARA resulted in a decrease in ROS activity in the surface membrane and whole-body extracts from male worms [60]. Arachidonic acid treatment has opposite effects on female worms, notably eggs in the uterus, providing a critical nutrient source. However, exposure of S. mansoni ex vivo eggs retrieved from the liver or intestines of infected mice to ARA elicited deleterious effects in an ARA concentration- and incubation time-dependent manner [82]. In addition, in contrast to male S. mansoni, the exposure of female worms to 10 mM ARA elicited a significant (P < 0.001) increase in total ROS, hydrogen peroxide, and NO activity in the surface membrane and whole-body extracts [60].

In vivo, ARA content is directly correlated with ROS levels in S. mansoni-infected liver and small intestine tissue extracts, suggesting a role in the attrition of eggs trapped in the liver [97]. The association of the vaccine-induced protection with increase in ARA content in host serum and tissue extracts is likely due to the enhancement of humoral immunity to the immunogens [99, 100]. In support of these findings, ARA was recently shown to powerfully enhance humoral immunity to vaccines [105].

Arachidonic acid: the schistosomicide of the future

It is important to obtain precise information on the level of plasma ARA in children residing in rural areas of developing countries and its association with the absence or intensity of infection with S. mansoni and S. hematobium. Providing children with ARA-rich food or ARA supplements aimed at reaching circulating ARA levels of approximately 50 µM (15 mg/L) may lead to the death of most, if not all, migrating schistosomula before arrival in the liver. Arachidonic acid thus serves as prophylaxis against schistosomiasis and required nutrient for the growth, development, and welfare of poor children [59, 78]. Importantly, prophylaxis is the safest and most cost-effective way to prevent disease and interrupt pathogen transmission.

Arachidonic acid is able to treat human schistosomiasis mansoni (Supplementary Table 1, Additional File 2). In support of these findings, school-aged children with light, moderate and heavy S. mansoni infection received 10 mg ARA (ARASCO, Columbia, Maryland, USA)/kg per day for 15 days. A significant (P < 0.001) increase of 35% in ARA plasma levels was associated with cure rates similar to those achieved by a 40 mg/kg oral dose of praziquantel (PZQ) and contrary to PZQ, with no single side effect or adverse reaction [80, 81]. The study was commended for the entire lack of adverse reactions for a further 3-month follow-up period in all children and for the lack of reinfection with S. mansoni in cured children residing in communities with low endemicity. Monitoring reinfection in areas of high endemicity was considered superfluous because reinfection uniformly occurs in patients treated with PZQ. These findings are not expected to vary worldwide following treatment with ARA alone or in combination with PZQ. Additionally, cure rates achieved even in children with light infection and residing in low endemicity areas reached only 78% with ARA (83% with PZQ) [80]. Unlike PZQ, ARA often fails to reduce egg counts in uncured school-age children (Supplementary Table 1, Additional File 2) because ARA is a nutrient for the host and because the reproductive functions of the surviving worms necessitate a complete, not partial, cure [100]. Taken together, these data indicate that much higher ARA doses are needed to eradicate the infection. Current evidence suggests that increasing the intake of ARA (up to 1500 mg/day) has no adverse effects on human immune or physiological functions [106–110]. In contrast to PZQ, ARA treatment is beneficial for host health and can prevent reinfection in the short and long term upon intake of a moderate maintenance dose. Single-cell production of ARA from oleaginous organisms, notably Mortierella alpina [111, 112], will never be sufficient for even schistosomiasis chemotherapy with ARA [113]. The therapeutic benefits of ARA are fully understood only because of its long duration of chemical synthesis [114].

The greatest hope for ARA-mediated eradication of schistosomiasis afflictions and interruption of transmission resides in the sensitivity of miracidia invading and developing in the intermediate snail host to PUFAs, notably ARA [87]. Rearing and feeding intermediate snail hosts of S. mansoni, S. hematobium and Schistosoma japonicum with PUFA-rich algae before infection with schistosome miracidia may provide a proof of concept. Many algae are PUFA rich, among which one may select the most easily available and palatable for the snail [115, 116], notably the edible Spirulina platensis and Chlorella vulgaris, which present elevated levels of ARA [117, 118]. In support of these findings, feeding Biomphalaria glabrata snails on Spirulina tablets before infection with S. mansoni miracidia led to a 95% decrease in the recovery of cercariae, without any deleterious effects on snail viability or reproductive capacity [119]. Consistent and reproducible positive laboratory results should be expanded via extensive and mass cultivation of the most ecologically safe, and schistosomicidally potent algae wherever Oncomelania, Biomphalaria, and Bulinus snail beds and habitats are located near freshwater rivers, streams, lakes and ponds in China, Africa and South America.

Immune evasion of cancer cells

Throughout the human life span, which now readily extends to 100 years, tissue cells often undergo neoplastic changes. Cells in preneoplastic lesions are perhaps able to display tumor-associated peptides in association with HLA class I molecules, especially if intracellular bacteria or viruses are responsible for tumorigenesis. Cytotoxic T lymphocytes immediately eliminate the cells displaying the nonself signature. Cells unable to present peptides associated with tumorigenic changes on the surface membrane evade the cytotoxic action of activated CD8 + T cells and continue to proliferate. Dividing cells undergo metabolic and physiologic changes, leading to low or no expression of functional MHC class I molecules [120, 121]. Immune surveillance is now the advantage of natural killer (NK) cells. These large granular lymphocytes represent 10–20% of peripheral blood mononuclear cells; are located in the bone marrow, spleen, lymph nodes and blood; circulate through the blood, lymphatic system, organs and tissues; and patrol abnormal or transformed cells. They lyse MHC class I-negative cells essentially upon triggering of killer receptors following interaction with ligands on tumor cells. Sequestering or concealment of self-tumor cell ligands and the inability of otherwise bound NK cells to cancer cells allow immune evasion and cancer progression for clinical detection [122–125]. Dysregulation of the metabolism of cholesterol and/or SM leads to their accumulation at the plasma membrane, resulting in progressive hiding of the molecules responsible for cell-to-cell contact inhibition of proliferation and locomotion. Contact inhibition has been demonstrated to be a fundamental mechanism in embryonic and postnatal tissue growth and development as well as in wound healing and homeostasis, whereby cells stop dividing when they reach confluence owing to cell‒cell contact inhibition. The loss of contact inhibition is recognized as a hallmark of cancer cells, as they continue moving after contact with their neighbors, dividing, proliferating, and migrating over adjacent cells in disordered, multilayered masses [126–129]. Cholesterol has been reported to play a role in initiating and promoting tumorigenesis by affecting plasma membrane permeability [130–133]. However, SM is recognized as the most instrumental player in the changes in fluidity and permeability of the precancerous cell surface because of its propensity to establish intra- and intermolecular links [134, 135]. Curiously, relationships between sphingolipid metabolism and cell-to-cell contact, communication and trafficking have been discovered in plants [136].

Sphingomyelin and clinically detected tumors

Genetic and biochemical changes in SM metabolism leading to its accumulation at the cell surface may play a fundamental role in the initiation and progression of neoplastic processes by impacting the plasma membrane structure, organization, integrity, permeability and signaling functions. Tight junctions between the surface membrane SM, cholesterol, and surrounding water [137] may also transform the plasma membrane into a shield. This barrier allows entry of nutrients but prevents cell-to-cell contact inhibition, mediates immune evasion, and hides or over-exposes critical receptors, thus promoting chaos in the signals the cell emits and receives. This process is followed by progressively increasing changes in regulatory circuits that govern normal cell proliferation, disruption of diverse downstream pathway cascades, and alterations in the recruitment and activation of cytosolic enzymes and transcription factors. Additionally, an SM-based barrier around tumor cells protects blood and metastatic cancer cells from surrounding immune effectors [138–141]. In support of these findings, matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization (MALDI)-mass spectrometric imaging (MSI) of human liver tissue grown in immune-deficient mice revealed high SM content in the surface membrane of metastatic cells [138]. This has led to the targeting of sphingolipids, notably SM metabolism, as a therapeutic approach for hematologic malignancies [139, 142, 143]. Accordingly, efforts to elucidate the intricacies of the tumor cell surface membrane in order to prevent and treat neoplastic lesions are important [144].

Sphingolipids play a fundamental role in glioblastoma, the most common malignant brain tumor in adults with a poor diagnosis due to the limitations of existing therapies [141]. Glioblastoma cells in mice were recently reported to avidly grab serine from the environment and display a reduction in tumor growth when maintained on a serine-deficient diet [145]. Condensation of this amino acid with palmitoyl-CoA (coenzyme A) represents the first step in SM biosynthesis (Fig. 1). Replacement of serine by alanine prevents SM synthesis, as it leads to a product with a missing hydroxyl group at the first carbon atom and the synthesis of 1-deoxy-ceramide and 1-deoxy-dihydroceramide, which may not be degraded or converted to SM and are toxic to normal and tumor cells [146, 147]. In support of these findings, the restriction of serine from the culture medium of tumor cells in vitro promoted the accumulation of 1-deoxysphingolipids and their apoptosis (Fig. 1). Additionally, restriction of dietary serine/glycine potently induced the accumulation of 1-deoxysphingolipids in xenografts of human colon cancer cells and considerably decreased tumor growth [147].

Low serum concentrations of SM 42:2 and SM 42:3 correlated with laryngeal cancer progression, implying changes in the regulation of the lipid metabolic network and suggesting that SM 42-2 contributes to preventing MHC class I-like CD1a from binding to T cells [148]. Male esophageal carcinoma has been reported to be associated with genetic dysregulation of the chromosome Y TMSB4Y tumor suppressor gene, which encodes thymosin beta-4, a Y chromosome protein (44 amino acids) that stimulates SM biosynthesis [149]. Malabaricone-C-induced inhibition of SMS1 and SMS2 effectively suppressed male esophageal squamous cell proliferation and xenograft tumor growth [150]. The expression of SMS2 was upregulated in the ovarian cancer lines and in the ovarian cancer tissues of the majority of the 30 patients compared with the adjacent normal ovarian samples. Knockdown-mediated depletion of SMS2 suppressed the migration, growth and survival of ovarian cancer cells [151] and murine pancreatic tumors [152]. Importantly, SMS2 depletion not only altered lipid metabolism in human ovarian cancer cells but also elicited considerable oxidative stress and damage and mitochondrial dysfunction. Conversely, both SMS1 and SMS-2 play a cardinal role in SM de novo synthesis and control SM levels in the cytosol and membranes of cells, notably the cell surface membrane [153, 154]. High SMS2 expression is associated with breast cancer metastasis. Gain- and loss-of-function assays in vitro and in vivo revealed that the enzyme promotes cancer cell proliferation by suppressing ceramide-mediated apoptosis [155]. Equilibrium between surface membrane SM and ceramide was found to be instrumental for the apoptosis or survival of cancer cells. Specifically, downregulation of SM hydrolysis to ceramide due to hypermethylation of the SMPD3 gene, which encodes nSMase2, was observed in 60% of breast tumors. An increase in the plasma levels of SM was consistently recorded in patients with early-stage breast cancer and in surgery-derived breast cancer tissue compared with normal breast samples [156–160].

Lung adenocarcinoma and squamous cell carcinoma are subtypes of non-small cell lung cancer, but they differ in terms of cell type, location, and prognosis. Adenocarcinoma originates from mucus-making gland cells in the outer parts of the lung. Squamous cell carcinoma starts in flat cells lining the airways, typically in the central lung near the main bronchi. Dysregulation of sphingolipid metabolism and changes in the levels of SM species in paraffin-embedded lung cancer tissues are the most reliable indicators of diagnosis, prognosis, and recurrence prediction after surgery [161–163]. Additionally, tissues of both lung subtypes revealed an association of recurrence with an increase in the content of different isomers of phosphatidylcholine, which serve as donors of the phosphocholine headgroup for SM synthesis (Fig. 1) [164, 165]. These findings should be interpreted and extrapolated with caution, as a recent Mendelian randomization study revealed controversial, antagonistic, inverse and bidirectional links between plasma lipid species and the risk of cancer, including lung tumors [166].

Liquid lipidomic profiling was found to compete with clinically established methods for the diagnosis and monitoring of the progression of pancreatic cancer [167, 168] and cholangiocarcinoma [169] and to perform better in terms of sensitivity and specificity independent of the cancer stage. The reported changes consistently involve SM species, which are believed to impair the functions of the plasma membrane-located Kirsten-ras (KRAS) protein, which belongs to the RAS GTPase family, and relay signals governing cell proliferation, growth and differentiation from outside the cell to the nucleus [170, 171]. Notably, SM production is increased in adult T-cell leukemia cells as a consequence of increased expression of the RhoC protein, which is encoded by the Ras homolog family member (RhoC) gene. The RhoC protein is a GTPase that readily localizes to the plasma membrane cell‒cell junctions and the cleavage furrow, zones of high activity for processes such as cell movement and division [143]. Sphingomyelin (SM d18:0/16:1) and phosphatidylcholine (32:0; 31:0; 31:1) are highly expressed in experimental liver metastatic lesions [138] and appear to be responsible for human hepatocellular carcinoma initiation, progression, metastasis, and therapy, leading to the labeling of SM as a procancer [172, 173], a tumor-promoting lipid [143]. Increased production and content of SM species in tissues and plasma are also the most reliable indicators of colorectal [174, 175] and renal [176] tumorigenesis.

Concealing or overexposing receptors involved in cell growth and division may explain the uncontrolled proliferation of cancer cells and their subsequent death. Thus, when viable, nonpermeabilized bladder cancer cells were used, IF analysis of surface membrane-associated epithelial growth factor receptor (EGRF) expression revealed the absence of staining in 69 out of the 232 (30%) malignant samples, and 163 (70%) had grade 1–4 staining. A significantly higher staining rate was observed for squamous versus nonsquamous tumors [177]. Accordingly, this receptor is a valuable target for cancer treatment. Antibodies against EFGR were generated to prevent tumor (and normal?) cells, those that express the receptor, from receiving and translocating signals to the cell interior [177–179]. An anti-EGF vaccine was devised to kill cancer cells and prevent recurrence and metastasis [180]. Better than these ingenious approaches are those that target the core of the problem. It is necessary to determine the content of the surface membrane lipid species of normal, preneoplastic, cancerous, and metastatic cells of every tissue and the accessibility of antibodies and ligands to the molecules and receptors on viable, nonpermeabilized cells [144]. If SM is the instrumental culprit, then infusing the tumor mass with alanine will lead to the generation of toxic 1-deoxysphingolipids and halt SM synthesis [145–147]. Impairment of the expression or activity of SMS1 and SMS2 within the tumor mass and metastatic lesions impairs the proliferative capacity of tumors [149–155, 181, 182]. Herbal-derived compounds, notably withanolide D (460.7 Da), curcumin (368.4 Da) and protopanaxadiol (460.3 Da), have been reported to induce the apoptosis of human cancer cells via elevation of the ceramide pool, subsequent to the activation of nSMase2 and the inhibition of SMS activity [183–185]. Silencing the SMS2 gene suppressed tumor growth in a murine model of pancreatic carcinoma [152]. Sphingomyelin was determined to be the most abundant phospholipid in tissue samples from six patients with giant cell tumors of bone, associated with high expression of SMS 1 and 2 and low expression of nSMase2 in tumor tissues compared with nonneoplastic bone tissues [186]. Importantly, inhibition of the SMPD3 (nSMase2-encoding) gene resulted in increased migration, invasion and resistance to the EGFR inhibitor erlotinib in oral squamous carcinoma cells [187]. A high nSMase2 expression level appeared to be an independent predictor of better survival outcomes in hepatocellular carcinoma patients, and its overexpression significantly decreased cancer cell proliferation and migration in vitro and in nude mouse xenografts [188]. The enzyme encoding nSMase2 is often downregulated in human myeloid and lymphoid leukemia, and in metastatic melanoma, the overexpression of nSMase2 in mouse melanoma reduces tumor growth in syngeneic wild-type but not CD8-deficient mice, indicating that nSMase2 activity elicits surface membrane ceramide accumulation and immune molecule exposure [189]. These studies indicate that nSMase2 has advantages in cancer therapy [190], especially because it is the principal regulator of the plasma membrane ceramide content in cancer cells [181, 191] and is fully sensitive to PUFAs, notably ARA [67–71].

Arachidonic acid tumoricidal potential and mechanisms

Undurti Das, the pioneer in suggesting and documenting the tumoricidal impact of PUFAs, discovered that ARA selectively induces cancer apoptosis resulting from lipid peroxide-mediated activation of caspase 3 and caspase 9. Additionally, treatment of tumor cells with exogenous ARA increased its incorporation into the surface membrane, overcoming chemotherapeutic drug resistance by promoting drug uptake and reducing its efflux [192–196]. These results confirmed and expanded the documentation of apoptosis in brain, breast, lung, liver, and colon cancer upon exposure to free, unesterified ARA [197–204]. Moreover, ARA-mediated accumulation of lipid peroxides reportedly leads to hepatocellular carcinoma apoptosis via ferroptosis, an iron-dependent death driven by the peroxidation of PUFA-rich phospholipids in the cell membrane [205].

Arachidonic acid is renowned for its ability to activate and inhibit surface membrane ion channel functions [206]. Concomitant with the increased activity of enzymes involved in membrane lipid metabolism, notably, phosphatidylcholine, SM and cholesterol, the downregulation of water and Na+, K+, Ca2 + and chloride ion channels was recently reported to elicit hypoxia, ROS generation and tumorigenesis in lung tissues [207].

In addition to its impact on ion channels, free ARA interaction with cell membrane receptors directly modulates gene expression in the cell nucleus, leading to limited cell proliferation and survival. Indeed, breast, liver and colon cancer evasion of apoptosis is associated with high expression of enzymes that convert free ARA to its metabolites [201]. A powerful impact of unesterified ARA is on the activation of tumor plasma membrane-associated nSMase2, leading to a decrease in surface membrane SM and the exposure of otherwise concealed critical molecules, notably beta-2 microglobulin, the beta chain of HLA class I molecules [208] (Supplementary Fig. 1, Additional File 1). The activation of nSMase2 elicits the accumulation of ceramide at the cell membrane [181, 191, 208] and in the cytosol [208] of tumor cells. Ceramides act as bioactive lipids in key signaling pathways, mediating inflammation, oxidative stress, growth inhibition, metabolism, autophagy, and apoptosis [155, 157, 183–185, 208–210]. Arachidonic acid: a future tumoricide

The delay and fear of using exogenous ARA for preventing the initiation, progression and metastasis of cancer stem from the ability of its metabolites of the leukotriene and prostaglandin families to promote and enhance severe inflammation [203, 204]. However, ARA does not induce inflammation, as evidenced by the lack of adverse reactions following the ingestion of ARA-rich food daily. Inflammation begins in response to microbe invasion or tissue injury, which is followed by the capture of ARA from the cell membrane and the conversion of ARA to its inflammation-inducing biologically active products [203, 204, 208]. Many reports have indicated that dietary ARA does not induce or promote adverse responses to food allergens [109, 215], stimulates humoral immunity to vaccines [105], and enhances heart functions via the activation of nuclear factor-erythroid-related factor 2 (Nrf2), a major transcription factor that plays a crucial role in the cellular defense against oxidative and electrophilic stress [216]. Accordingly, controversies highlight the ability of ARA and its metabolites to promote the development, progression, and differentiation of tumors [203, 204, 217–220].

The intake of ARA is meant to prevent and control tumor growth (Table 1). Dietary ARA is absorbed and incorporated into tissue membranes, where it is used to maintain cell structure, function, and health in all tissues, particularly the brain. However, clinically detected tumor surgery, irradiation or chemotherapy elicit tissue injury, followed by the release of ARA from cell membranes and its oxidation by three main enzymatic pathways: cyclooxygenase (COX), lipoxygenase (LOX), and cytochrome P450 (CYP-450), generating mostly inflammatory metabolites. Prostaglandins (PGs), notably PGE2, promote cell proliferation, survival, and angiogenesis. Leukotrienes enhance inflammation-mediated vascular leakage, thus promoting tumor progression and metastasis [219, 221]. It has often been reported that these ARA metabolites are linked to poor prognosis, neglecting other clinical, physiological, and dietary factors [203, 204, 217–221]. Therapy can be initiated by directly infusing ARA inside the tumor lesion or mass before radio- or chemotherapy interventions. Das and colleagues pioneered safe and effective treatment of human gliomas via intratumoral injection of an ARA (C20:4n6) precursor, gamma linolenic acid (C18:3n6), at a rate of 1 mg/day over a period of 10 days [222, 223]. Like larval schistosomes, the apoptosis of cancer cells requires exposure to only μM ARA [192, 208]. Accordingly, ARA embolization into cancer masses can be implemented despite limitations in ARA availability in the market of developed and developing countries alike. Dietary ARA should be prescribed for patients whose tumors cannot be accessed or who have hematological malignancies. However, it will not be possible to prescribe the documented safe ARA dose of 1–1.5 g/day [105, 224–226] for any adult suspecting or detecting a cancer threat before (but not during or after) chemotherapy and radiotherapy [219, 225], unless the precious PUFA is readily available and at an affordable cost, i.e., after its long-awaited and sorely needed chemical synthesis [114].

Study strengths and limitations

Further justification and work are needed to consolidate the innovative findings reported in the article. Specifically, numerous laboratory tests should be implemented to document the schistosomicidal impact of PUFA-rich algae on development of schistosome larvae in the snail. Upon reaching positive and reproducible results, the approach must be examined for safety and efficacy in beds of schistosome-infected snails around local water rivers, streams, lakes and ponds. In this review, the role of cell surface membrane SM in tumor initiation, progression, and metastasis was emphasized. The proposals in this review are directly and indirectly supported by many published studies and findings. This review is, however, limited by the lack of experiments and trials dedicated to assessing the validity and efficacy of antitumor therapies based on reducing the level and distribution of SM in humans.

Conclusion

Arachidonic acid is a nutrient that is sorely in demand and needed for every resident of poor rural communities, especially children and pregnant women, and is lethal to schistosome miracidia in the snail intermediate host and to developing larvae, migrating schistosomula, adult worms and their eggs in the final human and animal host. It is possible to relieve humans from the awe, threat, and costs of cancer, surgery, radio- and chemotherapy if the precise reasons for tumor initiation, growth, and metastasis are discovered. Growing evidence points to changes in cell surface fluidity, permeability and defense functions due to mutations in genes related to lipids, notably SM metabolism. Excess SM synthesis may be prevented at the first and rate-limiting step (Fig. 1) by the replacement of serine with alanine and, finally, by SM hydrolysis via the activation of the membrane-associated nSMase via ARA. Accordingly, every effort should be made to render ARA available and cost-affordable for safe, effective, prophylactic and therapeutic control of schistosomiasis and cancer.

Poor residents of developing and developed countries often display low plasma ARA levels because they cannot afford the cost of ARA-rich food. Availability of cost-effective ARA supplement will allow children of poor rural communities to evade schistosomiasis via ARA prophylactic intake before and throughout the parasite transmission season. Before and upon clinical detection, cancer cells may be eradicated via daily intake of large ARA doses, thus avoiding or reducing the calamity of surgery and chemotherapy.
