Postpartum pain management for women undergoing cesarean sections using a multimodal pain control protocol: a retrospective study
Authors: Alana Siwicki, Rebecca M Adams, Kiana Cabasa, Saranya Madan, Holly Ouillette
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCesarean section is one of the most common major abdominal surgeries performed in the world. The American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists (ACOG) states that untreated maternal pain after Cesarean delivery is associated with greater opioid use, postpartum depression, and the development of chronic pain. Enhanced Recovery After Cesarean (ERAC) is a multidisciplinary approach to managing postpartum pain. It is proven to not only reduce postpartum pain scores, but also decrease opioid consumption, shorten hospital stays, limit costs, increase postoperative mobilization, and improve maternal-infant bonding. We aimed to determine if the ERAC protocol with multimodal pain control initiated in the year 2021 significantly improved postpartum pain for women who underwent Cesarean section at Henry Ford St. John Hospital in Detroit, MI. We additionally investigated whether the ERAC protocol with multimodal pain control significantly decreased opioid usage for these patients.MethodsWe obtained demographic data, medical history, pain scores, and opioid usage measured in morphine milliequivalents (MME) through chart review for 100 women who underwent Cesarean section in 2020 and 100 women who underwent Cesarean section in 2022. There was, however, a significant difference in MME usage between the groups on postoperative days 1 and 2, which remained significant in the multivariable analyses after controlling for demographic and medical history variables.ConclusionsAt Henry Ford St. John Hospital, the ERAC protocol with multimodal pain control implemented in 2021 resulted in a significant decrease in MME usage on postoperative days 1 and 2, even though there was not a significant decrease in maternal pain scores.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12884-025-07607-y.Keywords: Postpartum pain, Postoperative pain, Cesarean section, Enhanced Recovery After Cesarean, Enhanced Recovery After Surgery, Chronic pain, Opioid use

Background

Cesarean section is one of the most common major abdominal surgeries performed across the globe. In the United States alone, over one million Cesarean sections are performed annually, with 32.1% of deliveries in 2021 being by Cesarean section [1, 2]. Unlike other major abdominal surgeries, Cesarean sections are unique in that the patient not only has the task of recovering from major surgery, but also must tend to the demands of a newborn child in the postpartum period. These challenges underscore the need for sufficient pain control. A committee opinion published by the American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists (ACOG) states that untreated maternal pain is associated with greater opioid use, postpartum depression, and the development of chronic pain [3]. A 2007 study performed by Karlstrom et al. demonstrated that poor postoperative pain control can have a negative effect on breastfeeding and infant care [4]. In 2008, Eisenach et al. found an association between acute pain and the development of chronic pain and depression in the postpartum period [2]. They concluded that women with severe acute postpartum pain had a 2.5-fold increased risk of persistent pain and a 3.0-fold increased risk of postpartum depression compared to those with mild postpartum pain. These findings illustrate the importance of addressing pain in the immediate postpartum days [2].

Enhanced Recovery After Surgery (ERAS) is an evidence-based, multidisciplinary concept whose initial goal was to standardize perioperative pain management and accelerate the recovery period for colorectal surgery patients [5]. ERAS protocols include specific guidelines for patient care during the preoperative evaluation, immediate preoperative and intraoperative periods, and in the postoperative setting. These recommendations specifically focus on pain control and early mobilization. Because of its success, ERAS protocols are now being applied across several surgical specialties, including obstetrics. Enhanced Recovery After Cesarean (ERAC) is an approach to managing postpartum pain derived from the ERAS patient care pathway [5]. In a 2021 study performed by Patel et al., ERAS protocols for Cesarean sections not only significantly reduced postpartum pain scores, but also reduced opioid consumption in postpartum patients [6]. The use of ERAS protocols for Cesarean sections has also been associated with shorter hospital stays, lower costs, improved maternal-infant bonding, and increased postoperative mobilization [6, 7].

Our study was performed at Henry Ford St. John Hospital in Detroit, MI. This hospital serves a diverse patient population from both underserved Detroit communities and affluent surrounding neighborhoods. High risk obstetric cases are managed frequently at our hospital site, often with the help of maternal-fetal medicine (MFM) specialists. The primary goal of our study was to compare postpartum pain scores of women who underwent a Cesarean delivery at Henry Ford St. John Hospital before and after implementation of the ERAC protocol with multimodal pain control. Our secondary focus was to compare total opioid use measured in morphine milliequivalents (MME) between the pre-implementation and post-implementation groups. Variables assessed during this study included patient age, gravidity, parity, and the number of Cesarean sections undergone previously to potentially identify women who would benefit most from implementation of the ERAC protocol with multimodal pain control.

Methods

We conducted a retrospective chart review of patients who underwent a Cesarean section from January 1, 2020, through December 31, 2020, and January 1, 2022, through December 31, 2022, at Henry Ford St. John Hospital. The ERAC protocol with multimodal pain control was first implemented in July 2021. Therefore, these date ranges excluded the transitional period from the previous postpartum pain management methods to this new protocol. All patients who underwent a Cesarean delivery during these study periods were eligible for inclusion, except for those who were taking methadone or suboxone during their pregnancies. This included planned, unplanned but non-emergent, and emergent Cesarean deliveries utilizing any skin or uterine incisions. Patients who were taking methadone or suboxone during their pregnancies were excluded from our study due to the potential for sustained opioid use to alter their experience of pain and opioid requirements after Cesarean section. In 2020 and 2022, the first 100 women who delivered by Cesarean section and were not on methadone or suboxone were analyzed. Patient demographics, including age, race, and gender, medical history, gravidity, parity, type of anesthesia used during Cesarean section, total number of Cesarean deliveries, and presence of obstetric complications were reviewed. Our primary outcomes were maternal pain scores on postpartum days 1 and 2 compared between the pre- and post-ERAC protocol with multimodal pain control implementation groups. We did not evaluate pain scores after this time since most patients at Henry Ford St. John Hospital were discharged by postpartum day 2. An average pain score was calculated for each postoperative day by taking the mean of pain scores in a 24-hour period documented by labor and delivery nurses using an 11-point visual analog scale (VAS) at the time of medication administration. These scores reflected overall pain experienced by our patients after their Cesarean deliveries and were not specific to their incision or any other singular area. Our secondary outcomes were total opioid use measured in MME on postpartum days 1 and 2 compared between the pre- and post-ERAC protocol implementation groups.

To calculate a power analysis for our study, we used data reported by Peng et al. in 2021. Their study of patients undergoing anatomic lung resection used a VAS to compare pain scores on postoperative days 1 and 2 between the pre-ERAS and post-ERAS groups. They found a 15–20% decrease in pain scores using this scale on the matched days between the two groups [8]. Based on these data, using an 11-point VAS, we estimated the median and interquartile range (IQR) postpartum day 1 pain score to be 7.0 (IQR = 1.5) in the pre-ERAC implementation group and 6.0 (IQR = 1.5) in the post-ERAC implementation group. For 80% power and alpha = 0.05, with a pooled standard deviation of 1.0, at least 37 cases were needed per group. We included 200 total patients in our study.

Descriptive statistics were generated to characterize the subjects. Continuous variables were described as the mean with standard deviation or median with range or interquartile range. Categorical variables were described as frequency distributions. Univariable analysis of factors associated with the use of the ERAC protocol was assessed using Student’s t-test, chi-squared analysis, and Pearson’s correlation. The differences in postpartum pain scores between patients in the pre-ERAC group and those in the post-ERAC group were assessed using the Mann-Whitney U test. Multivariable analyses of postpartum pain scores and MME were performed using linear regression. Analyses were conducted with SPSS version 28.0, and a p-value less than 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance.

Results

The patient background characteristics were analyzed with a combination of Student’s t-test and chi-squared analysis as shown in Table 1. There were no statistically significant differences regarding age, race, or gravidity between the pre-ERAC implementation group in 2020 and the post-ERAC implementation group in 2022. However, in 2022, there were significant decreases in body mass index (BMI), parity, and number of Cesarean deliveries. In Table 2, the two groups were similar in terms of failed trials of labor after Cesarean, gestational age distribution, number of multiple gestations, and days admitted to the hospital with no statistically significant differences. For comorbidities, there were no statistically significant differences between the two groups apart from more diet controlled gestational diabetic patients in 2022 as seen in Table 3. The “other” comorbidities included non-diabetic endocrine diseases and respiratory, neurologic, hematologic, and psychiatric conditions.

The average pain scores were then analyzed by the Mann-Whitney U test, which provided a median pain score for each cohort on each postoperative day. When comparing pain scores on postoperative days 1 and 2 between the pre- and post-ERAC implementation groups, no significant differences were observed, as seen in Table 4.

Discussion

At Henry Ford St. John Hospital, the ERAC protocol with multimodal pain control resulted in a significant decrease in MME usage in the year after it was implemented, even though there was not a significant difference in maternal pain scores. Over the course of our study period, no other meaningful changes in care were made for our obstetric patients or their newborns. This further strengthens the results of our study. Our findings support limiting opioid use to the minimum dosage needed to control postoperative discomfort, as this does not appear to increase patients’ overall reported pain. In the year 2022, there was also a statistically significant difference in BMI, parity, and number of Cesarean sections. All three values were lower in the 2022 group. This raises the question of whether these factors independently favor more controlled postoperative pain, regardless of a multimodal pain control protocol.

Although our study did not reveal a meaningful difference in pain scores, the significant decrease in postpartum opioid usage has important implications. Limiting opioid consumption after delivery may reduce the potential for adverse maternal and neonatal effects in the postpartum period. Mothers can experience mild side effects from opioids such as nausea, vomiting, constipation, urinary retention, and drowsiness [9, 10]. More severe side effects may include impaired cognition, respiratory depression, as well as the potential for misuse, abuse, and addiction [9, 10]. Maternal use of opioids during breastfeeding can cause neonatal drowsiness, lethargy, poor sleep and feeding, and may progress to rare but severe central nervous system (CNS) depression [9]. Ensuring optimal health and wellbeing of both mothers and infants after delivery is essential, and our study supports minimizing the potential detrimental consequences of opioid use on these vulnerable groups.

After Cesarean delivery, ACOG recommends standard oral and parenteral medications such as acetaminophen, non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), or low-dose, low-potency, short-acting opioids with the duration of opioid use limited to the shortest reasonable course expected for treating acute pain [11]. A recent systematic review and meta-analysis of 23 studies on ERAC protocol outcomes found a statistically significant decrease in MME usage during hospital admissions in post-ERAC implementation groups [12]. Interestingly, this meta-analysis failed to reveal a significant difference in total MME prescribed at discharge [12]. Our Henry Ford St. John Hospital protocol states that if a narcotic prescription is needed at the time of discharge, a three-day supply should be given based on the prior 24 hours of use. Improving pain control while inpatient may therefore reduce the amount and duration of acute opioid use after discharge, minimizing the risks of both maternal and neonatal adverse events. Adoption of a similar practice for opioid discharge prescriptions at other hospital sites could further limit overall opioid usage during the postpartum period. Additional research on patients’ reported pain and prescribed opioid usage after discharge would bring greater clarity to how the inpatient ERAC protocol may affect recovery outside hospital walls.

Our study has a few limitations. The first is that our research was underpowered with regards to the difference in maternal pain scores between the two cohorts. Our power analysis indicated that 507 cases were needed per group, and only 100 cases per group were reviewed. Bias may have also contributed to our results as the patients reviewed were in order of the provided data set, rather than obtained randomly by the data collector. Additionally, this was a retrospective study performed at a single community hospital site in Detroit. Our results were consistent with a similar study at a single institution showing decreased opioid use in the postoperative period after Cesarean section in the patient group whose care involved the ERAC protocol [7]. However, future multi-site studies are necessary to further investigate the impact of the ERAC protocol on various patient populations with unique needs or comorbidities.

Another important consideration of our study is whether implementation of the ERAC protocol promoted more frequent pain score documentation by labor and delivery nurses during the postoperative period, adding additional tasks to an already long list of nursing responsibilities. This could potentially lead to inconsistent pain score documentation by overworked nursing staff, affecting the results of our study. At Henry Ford St. John Hospital, the labor and delivery nurses are required to document pain scores according to hospital protocol. This includes charting pain scores 20 minutes after administering intravenous (IV) pain medication and one hour after oral pain medication. This protocol was consistent throughout the pre- and post-ERAC time periods at our hospital, encouraging compliance amongst the nursing staff for pain score charting. Lack of consistent documentation did not prove to be a limitation at Henry Ford St. John Hospital, but this may be an important factor to consider at other hospital sites when introducing the ERAC protocol and similarly assessing its impact on overall pain.

Conclusions

At Henry Ford St. John Hospital, the ERAC protocol with multimodal pain control implemented in 2021 resulted in a significant decrease in MME usage on postoperative days 1 and 2, even though there was not a significant difference in maternal pain scores. Cesarean sections are performed daily worldwide, and proper pain control is of the utmost importance during recovery when the demands of parenting are thrust upon a patient healing from major abdominal surgery. However, the potential for maternal and neonatal side effects from opioid usage in addition to possible misuse and abuse necessitates that stronger pain control be prescribed conservatively yet adequately, according to evidence-based practices. Our study demonstrates that incorporating a standardized protocol like ERAC reduces opioid usage after delivery and therefore mitigates these potential consequences. Although performed at a single hospital site, these findings are informative for any Cesarean postoperative setting and provide an important foundation for future multi-site studies. Standardization of pain control after Cesarean section should be widely implemented to improve the way women heal and thrive after this common surgical procedure.
