The crucial role of m6A methylation modification in nasopharyngeal carcinoma
Authors: Lijie Jin, Jianxin Song
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractNasopharyngeal carcinoma (NPC) is an epithelial malignancy originating from the nasopharyngeal mucosa and represents one of the most prevalent malignant tumors globally. RNA modifications function as a key epigenetic regulatory mechanism, governing post-transcriptional gene regulation. Among these, N6-methyladenosine (m6A) is the most abundant internal modification in eukaryotic mRNA and plays a pivotal role in regulating RNA transcription, splicing, stability, and translation. Extensive research has demonstrated that m6A modification regulates the expression of numerous oncogenes and tumor suppressor genes and is closely associated with cancer initiation and progression. Notably, regulators of m6A methylation exhibit aberrant expression in NPC tissues, and their dysregulation is closely linked to tumor development and therapeutic resistance. This evidence suggests their potential as molecular targets for NPC diagnosis and treatment. This review examines the pivotal role of m6A in NPC, encompassing its influence on cell proliferation, migration, invasion, and resistance to radiotherapy and chemotherapy. Furthermore, it explores recent advances in the application of m6A for NPC diagnosis and molecularly targeted therapy, and summarises current techniques for detecting m6A modifications.Keywords: m6A, Nasopharyngeal carcinoma, Biological function, Drug resistance, Radiotherapy resistance

Introduction

Nasopharyngeal carcinoma (NPC) is the most prevalent head and neck cancer in southern China and Southeast Asia. In 2022, approximately 120,416 new cases of nasopharyngeal cancer were reported, along with 73,476 related deaths, with the majority of cases occurring in Southeast Asia and an observed increasing trend each year [1]. The nasopharyngeal location of this cancer complicates treatment due to challenges such as high rates of recurrence, metastasis, and resistance to radiotherapy and chemotherapy. Key characteristics of NPC include Epstein-Barr virus infection and extensive lymphocyte infiltration. Furthermore, researchers have conceptualized NPC as an ecological pathology, defining it as a multidimensional spatiotemporal ecosystem that exists in a pathological state of ‘ecological and evolutionary unity’. This innovative perspective offers a novel conceptual framework and paradigm for deciphering the complex etiological processes of NPC, with significant implications for its future prevention and therapeutic strategies [2]. Currently, the primary treatment for patients with NPC remains a comprehensive approach centered on radiotherapy, which has yielded positive outcomes in many instances. However, it is disheartening that over 50% of nasopharyngeal cancer patients are diagnosed at an advanced stage. Furthermore, NPC exhibits a significant propensity for distant metastasis [3]. Consequently, a thorough investigation into the pathogenesis and therapeutic targets of NPC has become a critical area of research.

Transcription, post-transcription, and post-translation represent three levels of modifications and changes in epigenetics, encompassing DNA methylation, histone modification, and chromatin remodeling [4, 5]. Post-transcriptional regulation involves RNA modification and non-coding RNA [6]. To date, more than 170 RNA modifications have been identified [7]. Among these, the most extensively studied RNA epigenetic modifications include m6A, 5-methylcytosine (m5C), N1-methyladenosine (m1A), 2’-O methylation (m6Am), pseudouridine (Ψ), and m7G [8–13]. These modifications regulate numerous critical cellular functions through their respective regulatory factors during development and disease. Although RNA modifications have been recognized since the 1990 s, our understanding of these modifications remains incomplete [14–16]. The discovery of RNA demethylases and the advent of methylated RNA co-immunoprecipitation sequencing technology have positioned RNA modification as a prominent area of research in both physiology and pathology [17].

N6-adenylate methylation (m6A) is a well-known post-transcriptional modification that was first discovered in 1974. It is regarded as the most prevalent internal modification in mammalian mRNA, as well as in small non-coding RNAs (ncRNA) and long non-coding RNAs (lncRNA) across eukaryotic species [16, 18]. This modification is particularly enriched in 3′ untranslated regions (3′ UTRs), near pause codons, within long exons, in intergenic regions, introns, and 5′ UTRs [19, 20]. The m6A methyltransferase is primarily composed of ‘writer’ proteins, such as methyltransferase-like protein 3 (METTL3), METTL5, METTL14, METTL16, RBM15, WTAP, VIRMA, and ZCCHC. Notably, METTL3 serves as the primary catalytic subunit within the METTL3/METTL14 complex that binds SAM and catalyzes methyl transfer, facilitating the conversion of adenosine to m6A [21–23]. Additionally, there are ‘eraser’ proteins with demethylation capabilities, including FTO and ALKBH5 [24, 25]. The unique recognition elements, known as ‘readers,’ include binding proteins such as YTHDC1/2 and YTHDF1/2/3 [26, 27] (Table 1).

m6A modification-related factors

m6A modification

To date, more than 170 distinct types of RNA chemical modifications have been identified, which serve to modify both coding and non-coding RNAs. As research into these modifications increases, their significant impact on human diseases has become evident [28, 29]. Among these, m6A is recognized as the most abundant and well-characterized internal modification in mRNA. It plays a crucial role in regulating the self-renewal of embryonic stem cells and cancer cells, thereby enhancing cellular survival following heat shock or DNA damage [30–32]. RNA modifications precisely regulate the biological functions of numerous molecules and contribute to the diversification of genetic information. A specific group of proteins has been identified that can deposit, remove, and recognize RNA modifications, thereby influencing RNA outcomes [33]. The regulatory mechanism of m6A methylation is a dynamic process, catalyzed by m6A methyltransferase ‘writers’ and reversed by ‘erasers.’ Ultimately, m6A ‘readers’ recognize and bind to the m6A methylation modification site, guiding the translation and degradation of downstream mRNA(Figure 1).

m6A “writers”

The m6A methylation modification is catalyzed by the methyltransferase complex (MTC), which transfers the methyl group from S-adenosylmethionine (SAM) to the 6th nitrogen atom of adenine [34]. The core component of the MTC is METTL3, while additional components include METTL14, METTL16, WTAP, VIRMA, RBM15, and ZC3H13 (Table 1) [35–37]. METTL3 possesses a methyltransferase domain (MTD) that binds and catalyzes the transfer of methyl groups from SAM to the adenine base of RNA, resulting in the production of S-adenosylhomocysteine (SAH) [38]. In glioblastoma (GBM), METTL3 plays an oncogenic role by regulating nonsense-mediated splicing factor mRNA decay and alternative splice isoform switching. Loss of METTL3 results in increased levels of BCL-XS isoforms and NCOR2α isoforms and inhibits GSC growth and self-renewal [39]. In gastric cancer, upregulation of METTL3 promotes the stability of ZMYM1, thereby promoting the EMT process in vitro and metastasis in vivo [40]. In addition, METTL3 can also participate in the regulation of target mRNAs in a modified manner, acting as a partial reader [41].METTL14 and METTL3 work synergistically to induce m6A modification [42]. The methyltransferase domain of METTL16 features a Rossmann-like fold characteristic of class I methyltransferases, with SAM serving as the methyl donor [43]. METTL14 regulates post-implantation embryonic development by promoting the transition of ectoderm from the initial to the primed state. Loss of METTL14 impairs the ability of embryonic stem cells [44]to initiate and further differentiate. In addition, the regulatory role of METTL14 in cancer stem cells has been revealed in [45].METTL16 binds a portion of mRNA and methylates long non-coding RNA (lncRNA) and U6 small nuclear RNA (U6 snRNA) [46]. The UACAGAGAA sequence is required for METTL16-mediated methylation, and the N-terminal module of METTL16 is required for RNA binding [47, 48].WTAP acts as a crucial cofactor within the MTC [49]. VIRMA plays a role in recruiting and directing the core components of the methyltransferase to specific regions of mRNA [50]. ZC3H13 facilitates the localization of the WTAP-RBM15 complex to the nucleus [51].

m6A “erasers”

m6A demethylation is capable of removing m6A methylation modifications and regulates m6A modifications in conjunction with m6A methyltransferases, thereby maintaining a dynamic balance of m6A methylation levels within the body [37]. This methylation modification of m6A has been demonstrated to be reversible and involves the participation of methyltransferases, demethyltransferases, and methylation-reading proteins. The identified m6A demethylases include FTO and ALKBH5, among others (Table 1) [52]. The FTO protein shares similarities with the ALKB protein family in its core domain; however, it possesses a unique long loop at the C-terminus that distinguishes it from the ALKB protein family. This unique domain enables the FTO protein to facilitate the demethylation of single-stranded DNA or RNA [53, 54]. Abnormal transcription levels of the FTO gene can lead to the onset of various diseases [55].FTO not only controls mRNA splicing by inhibiting SRSF2 binding at splice sites [56], but also regulates adipogenesis by increasing the relative expression of the pro-adipogenic RUNX1T1-S isoform [57]. FTO blocks YTHDF2-mediated mRNA degradation by reducing m6A levels of cyclin A2 and cyclin-dependent kinase 2, thereby promoting adipocyte cycle progression and adipogenesis [58].ALKBH5 is another significant demethylase capable of demethylating and modifying mRNA within the nucleus. It features an alanine-rich region and a distinctive coiled-coil structure at the N-terminus [59]. FTO and ALKBH5 are two independent demethylases, both localized in the nucleus. They belong to the α-ketoglutarate dioxygenase family and are dependent on Fe (II) and α-ketoglutarate for their activity, catalyzing the demethylation of m6A. The mechanism involves the oxidation of m6A to N6-hydroxymethylnonanoic acid (hm6A), followed by the conversion of hm6A into N6-formyladenosine (f6A), and ultimately, f6A is transformed into adenosine [59].

m6A“readers”

The m6A methyl recognition protein selectively recognizes and binds to the m6A methylation modification in target RNA, participating in various stages of downstream target RNA metabolism (Table 1) [29, 60, 61]. The IYT521-B homologous (YTH) family protein is the first m6A reader protein identified. m6A readers primarily influence the occurrence and development of cancer by regulating the metabolism of target RNA, which includes processes such as RNA splicing, export, translation, and degradation, ultimately leading to alterations in RNA biological functions. To date, a total of five YTH domain-containing proteins have been identified in the human genome, which can be categorized into three m6A-reading proteins: YTH m6A binding proteins 1 and 3 (YTHDF1 and YTHDF3), YTH domain 1 (YTHDC1), and YTH domain 2 (YTHDC2) [62]. The members of the YTH family exhibit distinct roles in mRNA degradation and translation, functioning through different pathways. For instance, YTHDF1 may recruit Argonaute 2 protein and miRNA via the YTH domain, facilitating their interaction to form P bodies—mRNA degradation centers found in both yeast and animal cells, also referred to as cytoplasmic bodies, Dcp bodies, or GW bodies—to degrade mRNA [63]. YTHDF1 promotes the translation of m6A-modified mRNA and recognizes m6A-modified target genes through multiple mechanisms. Consequently, YTHDF1 enhances RNA stability, thereby promoting gene expression. In contrast, YTHDF2 primarily relies on m6A modification to regulate signaling pathways in cancer cells. YTHDF2 facilitates the degradation of targeted mRNA transcripts by recruiting the CCR4-NOT deadenylase complex. Additionally, YTHDF2 can promote tumor cell proliferation by binding to tumor suppressors, triggering downstream cascade reactions, and exerting opposing effects through interactions with oncogenes [64]. Furthermore, YTHDF2 influences various aspects of RNA metabolism, including mRNA degradation and ribosomal pre-RNA processing. The YTHDF family proteins DF1-3 are the principal m6A-binding proteins in the cytoplasm and are believed to mediate the cellular effects of m6A. All three DF proteins contribute to mRNA destabilization and collectively mediate the degradation of m6A-containing mRNAs [65]. YTHDF3 collaborates with YTHDF1 to enhance protein synthesis and facilitate the decay of methylated mRNA by influencing YTHDF2. Thus, YTHDF1 and YTHDF3 play crucial roles in promoting the metabolism of m6A-modified mRNA in the cytoplasm [66]. YTHDC1 is the only m6A reader within the YTH protein family that is localized in the nucleus. This protein interacts with splicing factors that regulate RNA splicing. Its m6A-dependent functions include selective polyadenylation and nucleogenesis of m6A-modified mRNA, thereby controlling the maturation of nuclear mRNA. Recent studies have demonstrated a close relationship between chromatin-associated RNAs, non-coding RNAs, and regulatory RNAs, which can influence intracellular gene expression [67]. Research indicates that YTHDC1 plays a crucial role in cellular functions, including cancer cell proliferation, and may also enhance the efficacy of tumor immunotherapy [68]. YTHDC2 exerts its biological role by utilizing its unique RNA-binding domain to bind to target m6A RNA, thereby establishing a bridge between ribosomes, reducing the abundance of related mRNA, and improving the translation efficiency of this mRNA [69, 70].

Reader proteins include the HNRNP family as well as IGF2BP1, IGF2BP2, and IGF2BP3. The HnRNP family and IGF2BP1-3 jointly recognize m6A-modified RNA fragments [71]. HnRNPC functions as an m6A switch, influencing the abundance and alternative splicing of target RNA by modulating its binding activity. Additionally, HnRNPC promotes the conversion of nascent heteronuclear RNA into mature mRNA, stabilizes the mRNA structure, and regulates its translation process [72]. The RGG motif of HNRNPG directly binds to the phosphorylated carboxyl-terminal domain of RNA polymerase II (RNAPII). The interaction between the phosphorylated carboxyl-terminal domain and newly synthesized RNA facilitates the co-transcription of HnRNPG and RNAPII, leading to the alternative splicing of the newly formed RNA [73]. HnRNPA2B1 specifically recognizes and binds with higher affinity to RNA that contains the m6A modification, particularly those with the m6A co-sequence RGm6ACH. The presence of m6A enhances the binding ability of HnRNPA2B1 to specific sites, thereby augmenting its nuclear activity. Additionally, HnRNPA2B1 recruits the microprocessor complexes Drosha and DGCR8 to facilitate the processing of primary miRNA by binding m6A to primary miRNA transcripts [74]. In contrast, IGF2BP initially recognizes m6A-modified mRNA, increasing the stability of the mRNA target and thereby promoting its storage, translation, and overall gene expression output. Furthermore, IGF2BP may exert oncogenic effects on cancer cells by enhancing the stability of oncogenic target mRNA that is methylated [75, 76] (Fig. 1).

The m6A RNA methylation and its modification mechanisms involve a core methyltransferase complex composed of METTL3, METTL14, and WTAP, which catalyze the methylation of N6 adenosine in conjunction with various regulatory cofactors, including VIRMA, RBM15, ZC3H13, and METTL16. Importantly, the deposition of m6A is reversible and is regulated by the demethylases FTO and ALKBH5. Additionally, m6A modifications can be recognized by specific m6A binding proteins. For instance, YTHDC1 is involved in alternative splicing and RNA export, while YTHDF1, YTHDF2, and YTHDF3 modulate RNA translation and degradation. Furthermore, IGF2BP1, IGF2BP2, and IGF2BP3 enhance RNA stability, and hnRNPG/C play roles in regulating mRNA splicing

m6A modification in NPC

Studies have shown that the m6A writing protein is significantly altered in NPC. Studies of METTL3 expression in NPC have been inconsistent: some report increased levels [77–79], while others report decreased levels [80] or no change [81]. EBV infection can down-regulate METTL3 [82, 83]. Despite these differences, most studies agree that METTL3 promotes NPC malignancy [84, 85]. Compared with METTL3, METTL14 [81, 86, 87], WTAP [88], and VIRMA [89, 90] were consistently upregulated in NPC, and their high expression levels were associated with poor prognosis.m6A modification of 18S and 28S rRNA is mediated by the METTL5-TRMT112 complex and ZCCHC4, respectively. METTL5 and TRMT112 specifically methylate an adenine at position 1832 of 18S rRNA [91]. In NPC, this modification was significantly increased compared with normal controls. The expression of both METTL5 and TRMT112 is elevated in NPC, especially in tumors with distant metastases, and is associated with advanced disease stages [92]. The m6A modification status of 28 S rRNA or the expression of its author, ZCCHC4, in NPC remains unknown, which requires further investigation.

The m6A modification can be removed by two RNA demethylases, FTO and ALKBH5, which act as erasers for this modification. Some studies have shown that FTO mainly targets m6A. GEO database analysis showed that ALKBH5 was generally downregulated in NPC tissues compared to normal NPE [81, 92], whereas FTO expression remained largely unchanged. However, a recent study found a tendency for NPC mRNA to be hypomethylated compared to normal tissue. Immunohistochemical (IHC) analysis revealed upregulation of ALKBH5 and FTO, which are associated with advanced disease stage and worse prognosis [93]. Post-translational regulation of FTO may explain the different trends in eraser expression observed in different studies. These changes may also reflect the different pathological background of NPC, such as radioresistance. Thus, although there are currently limited studies on m6A erasers in NPC, these findings suggest promising directions for future research.

The current study lacks sufficient evidence for specific expression changes of YTH family proteins between different stages of NPC. Studies to date have been limited to gene expression analysis in public RNA-seq datasets, often showing inconsistent trends. For example, among the six GEO datasets (GSE64634, GSE12452, GSE13597, GSE34573, GSE53819, and GSE68799), YTHDF1 was upregulated only in GSE34573. YTHDF2 was upregulated in three datasets (GSE12452, GSE34573, and GSE53819), and YTHDF3 was upregulated in four datasets (GSE12452, GSE34573, GSE53819, and GSE68799) [80, 92]. However, different studies have also reported downregulation of YTHDF3 in NPC [94, 95] and a positive correlation with patient prognosis in datasets GSE68799 and GSE102349 [80, 94]. In addition, YTHDC1 was upregulated and downregulated in GSE34573 and GSE53819, respectively, while YTHDC2 was downregulated only in GSE68799 [80, 92]. TCGA database showed that YTHD1-3 and YTHDC1 were increased in NPC, while YTHDC2 showed no significant change. On the other hand, another study found that YTHDC2 was elevated in specimens from patients with higher radioresistance and was associated with a more advanced clinical stage and worse prognosis [96].

IGF2BP1, IGF2BP2, and IGF2BP3 are another group of m6A readers that recognize m6A modified RNA through their KH domains. These proteins are highly expressed in various cancer types and are involved in multiple molecular mechanisms. In most publicly available datasets, IGF2BP1 and IGF2BP2 did not show a significant increase in NPC compared to NPE [80, 92]. However, IGF2BP3 expression is significantly higher in NPC tissues than in normal tissues, especially in metastatic NPC [95]. High IGF2BP3 levels have been associated with poor overall survival (OS) and distant metastasis-free survival (DMFS) in NPC patients.In addition to being directly recognized by RNA binding proteins (RBPS), m6A also has the ability to “m6A switch”, altering local RNA structure to facilitate or hinder the binding of certain RBPS. The HNRNP family consists of RNA-binding proteins and is named hnRNPA1-U based on its molecular weight. Several studies of HnRNP proteins, including HnRNPC, HNRNPG, and HnRNPA2B1, have highlighted the critical role of the “m6A switch” mechanism in the regulation of their RNA splicing [73, 97, 98]. Several studies have suggested the involvement of HnRNP proteins in NPC. For example, cytoplasmic HnRNPK exhibits high expression in NPC compared to NPE and is associated with NPC progression and poor prognosis [99]. It is an upstream regulator of a variety of oncogenes, including TP, FLIP2, and MMP2 [100–102]. In the public GEO dataset, HnRNPC was upregulated in three of six datasets (GSE12452, GSE13597, and GSE34573), and HnRNPA2B1 was upregulated in two of six datasets (GSE12452 and GSE34573) [80, 92]. Some studies have reported that HnRNPC interacts with and reduces circITCH, and circITCH can absorb miR-224-3p, thereby promoting the occurrence of NPC [103]. HnRNPA2B1 is also a driver in NPC and acts as a functional target of miR-146b-5p and SOX2-OT [104]. The current study did not extensively elucidate the involvement of m6A in these processes and requires further clarification.

Frequently, numerous signaling pathways converge on the translation machinery to satisfy the heightened anabolic demands of cancer. Roles of m6A in NPC

The role of m6A modification in NPC proliferation, migration, and invasion

NPC is characterized by a high local infiltration rate and early distant metastasis, which are significant factors contributing to the low cure rate among patients. Consequently, m6A modification is closely associated with the invasion and metastasis of NPC. Numerous studies have demonstrated that METTL3 plays a role in the migration and invasion of tumor cells by promoting epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT). Yu et al. [78] reported that the METTL3 content in nasopharyngeal cancer tissue was significantly higher than that in para-cancerous tissue. Furthermore, patients with advanced nasopharyngeal cancer or lymph node metastasis exhibited elevated METTL3 expression levels. It was confirmed that the m6A modification of Snail mRNA promotes the EMT of nasopharyngeal cancer cells. Liu et al.found that METTL3 positively regulates the β-catenin/TCF signaling pathway in NPC cells, and the knockout of the METTL3 gene can inhibit the invasion and migration of these cells. Additionally, FAM225A, a newly identified long non-coding RNA (lncRNA), plays a crucial role in promoting tumor cell invasion, migration, and angiogenesis.Recent studies have demonstrated [105] that m6A modification enhances the stability of FAM225A, which in turn promotes the proliferation and invasion of NPC cells by activating the FAK/PI3K/Akt pathway. As research on m6A modification deepens, the potential impact of m6A on lncRNA has garnered significant attention. Emerging evidence indicates that the m6A writer METTL3 increases the stability of ncRNA by mediating its m6A modification [60]. Furthermore, IGF2BP3 is one of the most prevalent m6A readers, recognizing m6A modifications in both mRNA and ncRNA, thereby promoting their stability [106]. In the study conducted by Hu et al. [85], SUCLG2-AS1 was found to be upregulated in NPC tissue and associated with a poor prognosis in NPC patients. Mechanistically, METTL3 mediates the m6A modification of SUCLG2-AS1 in the nucleus, while IGF2BP3 recognizes and binds to SUCLG2-AS1 in the cytoplasm, facilitating its expression. Subsequently, SUCLG2-AS1 translocates back to the nucleus to exert its functions. The m6A modification of SUCLG2-AS1 enhances its RNA stability and promotes the invasion and migration of NPC cell lines, while also inhibiting apoptosis and radiosensitivity both in vivo and in vitro.Researchers such as Li et al. [107] have found that the expression of WTAP is significantly up-regulated in NPC, with increased WTAP expression correlating with poor prognosis. The upregulation of WTAP in NPCs is mediated through KAT3A-mediated H3K27 acetylation. Functionally, WTAP is essential for the growth and metastasis of NPC. Mechanistically, lncRNA DIAPH1-AS1 has been identified as the primary m6A target of WTAP. The m6A modification mediated by WTAP enhances the stability of DIAPH1-AS1 through an IGF2BP2-dependent pathway. Furthermore, DIAPH1-AS1 acts as a molecular linker, facilitating the formation of the MTDH-LASP1 complex, which upregulates LASP1 expression and ultimately promotes the growth and metastasis of NPC. Concurrently, research by Zheng et al. [89] demonstrated that VIRMA maintains the stability of E2F7 mRNA through an m6A-dependent mechanism. Consequently, increased E2F7 interacts with the CBFB-recruited RUNX1 to form a transcriptional complex that synergistically transactivates the expression of target genes ITGA2, ITGA5, and NTRK, thereby promoting the progression and metastasis of NPC.

In addition, Chen et al. [95] observed that the m6A “reader” IGF2BP3 was upregulated in NPC tissue, and that IGF2BP3 overexpression served as an independent predictor of poor prognosis in NPC patients. Ectopically expressed IGF2BP3 in NPC cells recognizes and binds to m6A-modified NOTCH3 mRNA, promoting its stability and inhibiting deadenylation-mediated mRNA decay. This process continuously activates the Notch signaling pathway, enhances tumor initiation, and ultimately promotes tumor metastasis.

m6A modification and apoptosis disorder of NPC cells

Disorders of the cell cycle and apoptosis in NPC cells are significant contributors to malignant cell proliferation. Apoptosis, defined as the spontaneous and orderly death of cells, is regulated by genes and primarily triggered by either the exogenous or endogenous pathways mediated by caspases. Multiple studies have demonstrated that low expression of METTL3 promotes tumor cell apoptosis by modulating the Bcl-2 signaling pathway, the SHH/GLI pathway, and the ZNF750-FGF14 pathway [108]. Zhang et al. [109] reported that m6A modification facilitates cell apoptosis through the ZNF750-FGF14 signaling pathway by maintaining low expression levels of zinc finger protein 750 (ZNF750) in NPC. Additionally, other studies have indicated that m6A modification is highly enriched in the oncogene ZFAS1, enhancing its RNA stability. Mechanistically, ZFAS1 upregulates the expression of ATG10 by competitively binding to miR-100-3p, regulates autophagy levels by inhibiting the PI3K/Akt signaling pathway, and promotes the progression of NPC. Furthermore, some studies have found that METTL3 inhibits apoptosis in NPC cells by increasing the expression of the EZH2 protein [110].

m6A modification and drug resistance in NPC

The development of drug resistance is a significant cause of mortality among cancer patients. Although individuals with NPC exhibit sensitivity to chemotherapy during the initial treatment phase, the subsequent emergence of acquired resistance often leads to treatment failure. Recent data suggest that members of the TRIM protein family are implicated in the onset, progression, and drug resistance associated with cancer [111]. Zhang et al. [112] reported that the expression level of the TRIM11 gene in drug-resistant NPC cells was markedly higher than that in non-drug-resistant counterparts, with m6A modification being highly enriched in TRIM11 within these drug-resistant cells, thereby enhancing the stability of its RNA. TRIM11 is crucial in promoting drug resistance in NPC by modulating β-catenin-ABCC9 signal transduction. Additionally, the research team led by Wang et al. [113] identified TEAD4 as a high-risk factor for NPC through an investigation of the interactions between transcription factors and their targets. TEAD4 facilitates NPC migration, invasion, and cisplatin resistance through its autopalmitoylation. Mechanistically, YTHDF2 recognizes WTAP-mediated m6A methylation of TEAD4, promoting its stability and resulting in an abnormal upregulation of TEAD4. The upregulated TEAD4 transcriptionally activates BZW2, thereby inducing the oncogenic AKT pathway, which further drives NPC progression. Notably, the transcriptional activity of TEAD4 operates independently of its canonical coactivator YAP/TAZ. Clinically, TEAD4 serves as an independent predictor of poor prognosis and cisplatin response in NPC. Chen et al. [114] have demonstrated that the METTL5/TRMT112 complex and the m6A modification (m6A1832) at the 1832 site of 18S rRNA are elevated in NPC, facilitating oncogenic transformation both in vivo and in vitro. Furthermore, the loss of METTL5’s catalytic activity results in the loss of its oncogenic function. Mechanistically, the m6A1832 modification of 18S rRNA enhances the assembly of 80S ribosomes by facilitating the interaction between RPL24 and 18S rRNA, thereby promoting the translation of mRNAs containing 5’ terminal oligopyrimidine (5’ TOP) motifs. Additional mechanistic analyses revealed that METTL5 activates the transcription of HSP90B1 by enhancing the translation of HSF4b. HSP90B1, in turn, binds to the oncogenic mutated p53 (mutp53) protein, inhibiting its ubiquitination-dependent degradation and consequently promoting the development of NPC and its resistance to chemotherapy.

m6A modification and radiotherapy resistance in NPC

NPC cells exhibit relative sensitivity to radiotherapy; however, the challenge of radiotherapy resistance remains a significant clinical concern, leading to poor prognoses for patients with nasopharyngeal cancer. The activation of the PI3K/Akt signaling pathway may contribute to the increased radiation resistance observed in tumor cells, with Akt serving as a pivotal molecule in PI3K-mediated radioresistance [115]. He et al. [96] reported that the m6A ‘reader’ YTHDC2 is highly expressed in radioresistant NPC cells, where it enhances the translation efficiency of IGF1R mRNA and subsequently increases radiotherapy resistance through the activation of the IGF1R/Akt/S6 signaling axis. YTHDC2 thus plays a regulatory role in the radiotherapy resistance of NPC and may represent a promising target for sensitizing these cancer cells to radiotherapy and for the treatment of recurrent NPC. Additionally, there are report [116] indicating that FTO is significantly upregulated in radioresistant NPC tissues and cells compared to their parental radiosensitive counterparts. FTO contributes to the radioresistance of NPC by inhibiting radiation-induced ferroptosis. Mechanistically, FTO functions as an m6A demethylase that promotes the expression of OTUB1 by removing the m6A modification from OTUB1 transcripts, thereby inhibiting radiation-induced ferroptosis and ultimately leading to radiotherapy resistance in NPC. In vivo experimental results demonstrate that the FTO inhibitor FB23-2 and the ferroptosis activator erastin modify tumor responsiveness to radiotherapy in NPC cell lines and patient-derived xenografts. This study indicates that FTO mitigates radiation-induced iron loss, thereby enhancing the radiotherapy resistance of NPC. Consequently, FTO may serve as a potential therapeutic target and a valuable prognostic biomarker. The research team led by Mi et al. [117] identified an upregulation of HOTAIRM1 levels in radioresistant NPC cell lines and tissues. A positive correlation exists between high HOTAIRM1 expression and increased NPC cell proliferation, reduced apoptosis, G2/M cell cycle arrest, and diminished cellular DNA damage following radiotherapy. HOTAIRM1 regulates the acetylation and stability of the FTO protein, and inhibiting FTO elevates the m6A methylation level of CD44 precursor transcripts in NPC cells. Additionally, silencing the m6A reader protein YTHDC1 results in increased CD44V expression. HOTAIRM1 enhances the resistance of NPC cells to ferroptosis and irradiation via the HOTAIRM1-FTO-YTHDC1-CD44 axis. Mechanistically, HOTAIRM1 interacts with FTO proteins and induces m6A demethylation of CD44 transcripts. The absence of the m6A modification in the CD44 transcript prevents recognition by YTHDC1, leading to a switch from CD44S to CD44V. The presence of CD44V inhibits irradiation-induced ferroptosis and contributes to the radioresistance observed in NPC.

m6A modification and other biological functions of NPC

In addition to the biological functions described above, several studies are investigating the impact of m6A modification on the progression of NPC. Tang et al. [118] demonstrated that KIF18B is highly expressed in NPC, with this elevated expression correlating with poor prognosis. These findings suggest that the oncogenic effects of KIF18B on NPC may be mediated through the activation of EMT and the promotion of immune evasion, indicating that the dysregulation of KIF18B may be influenced by m6A methylation. Regarding metabolic reprogramming, lipid metabolism reprogramming has emerged as a hallmark feature of cancer cells and plays a critical role in cancer progression. Lipids are essential components of cell membrane formation during rapid proliferation and serve as a significant energy source, facilitating growth and metastasis. The research conducted by Li et al. [81] elucidated the molecular mechanism through which METTL14 promotes lipid metabolism in NPC cells by upregulating ANKRD22. This process establishes an abnormal regulatory loop that sustains m6A modification in NPC cells. ANKRD22 influences cellular lipid metabolism, increases intracellular acetyl-CoA levels, enhances the expression of downstream H3K27AC genes, and ultimately promotes tumorigenesis. Researchers, including Zhang [82], have demonstrated that Epstein-Barr virus (EBV) infection can downregulate the m6A modification level of toll-like receptor 9 (TLR9), consequently inhibiting TLR9 expression. TLR9 possesses multiple m6A modification sites. The knockdown of the m6A ‘writer’ METTL3 results in reduced protein expression of TLR9 mRNA by destabilizing it. Mechanistically, Epstein-Barr nuclear antigen 1 facilitates the degradation of METTL3 protein via the K48-linked ubiquitin-proteasome pathway. Furthermore, the research team identified YTHDF1 as an m6A ‘reader’ of TLR9, which enhances TLR9 expression by promoting mRNA translation in an m6A-dependent manner. This indicates that EBV inhibits TLR9 translation by ‘hijacking’ the host m6A modification mechanism. The METTL3 inhibitor STM2457 is employed to suppress TLR9-induced B cell proliferation and immunoglobulin secretion, thereby antagonizing TLR9-induced immune responses and assisting the immune evasion of tumor cells. In clinical lymphoma samples, the expression levels of METTL3, YTHDF1, and TLR9 are highly correlated with immune cell infiltration. This study reveals a novel mechanism by which EBV utilizes the m6A modification system to regulate innate immune molecules, promoting immune evasion. These findings enhance our understanding of the critical role of m6A modification in the interactions between oncogenic viruses and their hosts, and they present a potential new therapeutic strategy for modulating cellular m6A modification, which could aid in the treatment of EBV-associated tumors, such as NPC (Fig. 2, Table 2).

Potential drugs and therapeutic strategies based on m6A modified proteins

Given that RNA modification, particularly m6A modification, plays a significant biological role in various types of cancer, the development of targeted drugs based on m6A modification has emerged as a highly promising strategy. While immunotherapy continues to be a hopeful approach for cancer treatment, its low responsiveness remains a challenge. In summary, we posit that m6A modification is crucial in tumor immunotherapy. The development of inhibitors and agonists of m6A regulators represents a promising therapeutic strategy to enhance immune responses, and their combination with immunotherapy may resensitize tumor cells to anticancer drugs. Although these inhibitors and agonists are not yet widely utilized in clinical practice, they have demonstrated potential in inhibiting tumor growth in animal models of cancer.

Among the m6A regulators, FTO emerges as the most promising target for inhibitor development. To date, more than ten FTO inhibitors have been identified, and their therapeutic effects have been validated in various models [55]. Rhein was the first FTO inhibitor discovered; it reversibly binds to the catalytic domain of FTO, effectively inhibiting FTO-catalyzed m6A demethylation with low cytotoxicity. Yan et al. demonstrated that when Rhein is combined with tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs), drug-resistant cells exhibit increased sensitivity to TKIs and a reduction in colony formation compared to monotherapy in leukemic cells [119]. Unfortunately, no clinical studies have reported on the effects of Rhein combined with immunotherapy in tumors. Recent studies have indicated that a combined strategy involving antigen-capturing nanomedicines and the FTO inhibitor FB23-2 shows significant potential to enhance immune checkpoint blockade (ICB)-based immunotherapy. This approach may help ICB prevent distant tumor growth and metastasis following primary tumor ablation therapy. FB23-2 is released from the nanomedicine, promoting antigen presentation and the maturation of tumor-infiltrating dendritic cells (TIDCs) by increasing m6A modification levels, thereby triggering robust anti-tumor immune responses [120]. Meclofenamic acid, a highly selective FTO inhibitor, has been loaded into the gamma-cyclodextrin cavity, and when combined with gold nanorod-based near-infrared radiation-II-mediated photothermal therapy, it enhances photothermal immunotherapy against prostate cancer (PCa). In vivo studies demonstrate that this synergistic treatment promotes the proliferation and infiltration of CD8 + T cells in the tumor microenvironment (TME) while inhibiting PD-L1 transcripts. As a result, mice receiving the combined therapy exhibited stronger tumor growth inhibition and significantly fewer lung metastatic nodules compared to control mice [121]. Recently, a study reported that FTO deletion inhibited tumor growth and blocked FTO-mediated immune evasion by enhancing CD8 + T cell infiltration within tumors. Moreover, a novel FTO inhibitor, Dac51, was found to enhance the effects of PD-L1 blockade therapy, leading to improved tumor control, slower growth, and longer overall survival by increasing T cell infiltration. This suggests effective synergistic strategies to enhance immune responses in melanoma and non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) [122]. Yang et al. consistently reported that FTO inhibition sensitizes the response to anti-PD-1 treatment in vivo by mediating the regulation of PD-1, CXCR4, and SOX10 in melanoma [123], highlighting the role of FTO inhibition in overcoming the resistance of melanoma cells to PD-1 blockade [124].

STM2457, an inhibitor developed targeting METTL3, has been formulated as a treatment for hematological malignancies. It has been demonstrated to impede the proliferation and colony formation of MOLM-13 cell lines, promote cell apoptosis, and show no adverse effects on normal hematopoietic function. In in vivo studies, STM2457 inhibits the proliferation of acute myeloid leukemia (AML) in patient-derived xenograft models and leukemia mouse models [125]. Cheng and colleagues developed two potent FTO inhibitors, FB23 and FB23-2, which can directly bind to FTO and selectively inhibit its m6A demethylase activity [126]. They subsequently developed two additional FTO inhibitors, CS1 and CS2, which exhibited strong anti-tumor effects across various cancers. In the context of leukemia, FTO inhibitors not only block the FTO/m6A/MYC/CEBPA signaling axis and inhibit the autologous renewal of cancer stem cells, but they also reduce the expression of the immune checkpoint LILRB4 and immune evasion, thereby enhancing T cell cytotoxicity [127]. Beyond the inhibitors discussed above, several other m6A-targeted agents including STC-15 and next-generation METTL3 modulators are currently in preclinical development for various solid tumors, suggesting that insights from NPC research may inform broader therapeutic strategies across multiple cancer types [128, 129].

In cancer treatment, an increasing number of studies have discovered and confirmed that m6A regulatory factors play a significant role in modulating the tumor immune microenvironment. For instance, YTHDF2, an m6A reader, sequesters m6A-circRNA and is crucial for suppressing innate immunity [130]. Han et al. demonstrated that YTHDF1 exerts anti-tumor effects through mRNA m6A methylation in dendritic cells (DCs). Research indicates that the anti-tumor response of antigen-specific CD1 + T cells is significantly enhanced in YTHDF1-deficient mice, leading to improved therapeutic efficacy of PD-L1 checkpoint blockade [131]. Cheng et al. discovered that hnRNPC inhibits prostate cancer (PCa) tumor immunity by enhancing the activation of Treg cells while suppressing effector CD8 T cells. Additionally, epigenetic alterations can contribute to resistance against chemotherapy and radiotherapy, thereby limiting their effectiveness [132]. Studies have shown that variations in the expression level of METTL3 can render PCa cells resistant to androgen receptor antagonists [133]. A recently identified circRNA, circ0008399, binds to WTAP, regulates the expression of target RNA through m6A modification, and diminishes cisplatin sensitivity in bladder cancer, indicating the potential therapeutic value of targeting this axis [134]. Chen et al. found that n6-methyladenosine-modified TRAF1 regulates apoptosis and angiogenesis in a METTL14-dependent manner, thereby promoting renal cancer resistance to sunitinib [135].

Currently, research on m6A modification-targeted therapies for NPC is limited. However, it is undeniable that investigations into potential drugs and treatment strategies associated with m6A modification are steadily increasing. Therefore, we can remain optimistic that research on nasopharyngeal cancer will thrive in the near future. It is imperative to note that cancer is increasingly viewed as an ecological and evolutionary disease. Its initiation and progression are not driven by genetic mutations alone but are profoundly shaped by the dynamic interactions between cancer cells and their surrounding microenvironment, which collectively form a complex “tumor ecosystem.” This ecosystem comprises intricate, multi-level interactions among heterogeneous cell populations, the stromal network, the immune-metabolic environment, the microbiome, and molecular signaling pathways. Within this system, cancer cell populations employ adaptive strategies—including communication, cooperation, and competition—to survive and thrive. Consequently, treatment resistance, recurrence, and distant metastasis can be understood as evolutionary outcomes of this adaptive process [2, 136]. This ecological framework implies that the role of m6A modification—whether oncogenic or tumor-suppressive—is not intrinsic but is contingent upon its specific context and ecological niche within the tumor. Therefore, therapeutic strategies targeting m6A modification must be designed with an appreciation for the highly individualized and dynamic nature of each cancer’s ecosystem.

m6A modification detection technology

Detection technologies related to m6A modification are instrumental in advancing research on tumor mechanisms and their clinical applications. Each technology possesses distinct advantages and limitations. Therefore, the development of optimal m6A modification detection technologies is crucial for identifying tumor diagnostic markers with significant clinical value.

m6A modification overall level detection technology

Commonly used methods for detecting the overall level of RNA m6A modification include high-performance liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (HPLC-MS/MS), dot blotting, and colorimetric assays. HPLC-MS/MS analyzes digested RNA to calculate the modification ratio of m6A to adenosine (A), providing accurate qualitative and quantitative results, but the procedure is complex and requires specialized equipment [137]. Dot blotting involves binding denatured RNA to a membrane, which is then probed with anti-m6A antibodies; while simple and widely used, it is generally only semi-quantitative [138]. In contrast, the colorimetric method, which operates on a principle similar to ELISA, allows for quantitative measurement by detecting colorimetric signals proportional to m6A levels using a standard curve. This approach is relatively easy to perform, offers high sensitivity, and is frequently employed in research using commercial kits such as the EpiQuik M6A RNA Methylation Quantification kit [139].

High-throughput sequencing technology

Currently, the mainstream high-throughput sequencing technologies for RNA m6A modification primarily include three types, each with distinct characteristics: MeRIP-Seq enriches m6A-containing RNA fragments using specific antibodies for sequencing, offering genome-wide mapping of enriched regions with relatively mature protocols, but is limited to a resolution of 100–200 nucleotide regions and cannot achieve single-base precision or absolute quantification [140]. In contrast, miCLIP-seq introduces UV cross-linking after antibody enrichment, which induces mutations or truncations during reverse transcription to enable mapping at single-base resolution; however, its complex procedure typically involves radioactive labeling, imposing higher experimental demands [141]. Alternatively, the MazF Enzyme-based Chip Method relies on the cleavage of unmethylated “ACA” sequences by the MazF enzyme to infer single-base modifications, providing single-base resolution as a strength, but its major limitation is the exclusive dependence on the ACA motif, leaving it unable to detect m6A sites in other sequence contexts and thus offering limited coverage [142]. In summary, these three technologies present a trade-off and complement each other in terms of resolution, throughput, experimental difficulty, and detection scope.

RNA m6A modification level detection technology for specific segments or sites of genes to be tested

Researchers have developed various technologies, including methylated RNA co-precipitation combined with qPCR (MeRIP-qPCR), SELECT, and MazF-qPCR, to quantitatively detect specific gene fragments or m6A levels containing m6A modification sites. MeRIP-qPCR is based on the principles of MeRIP-Seq, where RNA is enriched and subsequently detected using fluorescence quantitative PCR. This technology is capable of detecting RNA fragments approximately 150 bp in length and is currently the most widely used detection method in the field of RNA m6A modification research [143]. SELECT technology, developed by Jia Guifang’s research group at the School of Chemistry, Peking University in 2018, offers a rapid detection method for m6A. It leverages the characteristic that m6A can impede the extension and ligation efficiencies of specific enzymes during reverse transcription and gap repair, allowing for the detection of m6A in low-abundance RNA with single-base resolution. This technology is not only time-efficient but also exhibits higher detection sensitivity compared to MeRIP-qPCR [144]. MazF-qPCR, a new m6A detection technology that has emerged in the past two years, utilizes the principle of a single-base resolution chip. It allows for fluorescence quantitative PCR detection of RNA fragments digested by the MazF enzyme, specifically targeting those containing m6A sites. In employing these methods, it is essential to design primers for either a specific gene fragment or an RNA fragment containing the m6A site, followed by quantitative PCR analysis [145].

Conclusion and prospect

RNA modification serves as a crucial post-transcriptional regulator of gene expression, with its functional network intersecting multiple disciplines, including metabolism, epigenetics, chromatin remodeling, and the immune system. Significant advancements have been achieved in transcriptome research, identifying over 170 chemical modifications of both coding and non-coding RNAs; however, most studies have concentrated on the biological functions of one or a limited number of RNA modifications. This article outlines common RNA modifications currently under investigation, while acknowledging that numerous other types of RNA modifications await further exploration.

Compared to other RNA modifications, m6A modifications are the most abundant and well-characterized internal modifications in mRNA, which prompts the desire to fully understand their specific mode of action. Unlike DNA methylation and histone modifications, m6A RNA modification targets nearly all transcripts and broadly regulates their processing, stability, and translation levels [19, 146]. Collectively, both basic and clinical studies demonstrate that m6A modification can dynamically and reversibly regulate biological changes in cancer cells, offering new treatment ideas and directions for the management of nasopharyngeal cancer. This article reviews the role of m6A modification in proliferation, migration, and invasion in NPC, while summarizing some of the current potential clinical applications of m6A modification. Although research on m6A methylation modification in NPC is still in its early stages, elucidating the relationship between m6A methylation and the occurrence and development of NPC will be of great significance. This research may help in screening and exploring potential targets, conducting preclinical trials, and establishing new treatment strategies.

As research progressed, the significance of m6A modification became increasingly evident. However, it still faces certain limitations. Most current studies primarily focus on a limited number of regulatory factors, neglecting the importance of a comprehensive understanding of their synergistic effects, which necessitates further investigation. Additionally, while many studies examine the regulatory and oncogenic impacts of m6A methylation on downstream factors, there is a notable lack of research on upstream factors that may contribute to abnormal m6A methylation. In the context of NPC, most researchers have concentrated on the biological functions related to proliferation, migration, and invasion; conversely, there is a scarcity of studies addressing tumor angiogenesis, glucose and lipid metabolism, and the maintenance of cancer stem cells. Therefore, further research in these areas is warranted. Lastly, there is a dearth of large-scale multicenter clinical trials to validate the feasibility of these findings.In addition, epitranscriptome mechanisms play a crucial role in regulating various biological processes and cancer development. As a biomarker of tumor progression or prognosis, the overall level of RNA modification may not be reliable because RNA modification at different genes or loci can lead to different molecular outcomes, promoting or suppressing tumorigenesis. Moreover, different regulators of RNA modification have specific targets, and their expression can vary in different tumor samples. However, in specific cellular contexts, changes in RNA modifications at specific sites can produce definite oncogenic or tumor suppressive effects. These molecular alterations can be used as indicators of tumor progression and potential therapeutic targets. The overall upregulation of mRNA m6A modification in NPC suggests an important role in NPC development. In addition, m6A regulators, particularly the METTL3-METTL14 methyltransferase complex and the rRNA m6A writer METTL5, were consistently upregulated in multiple studies. Extensive changes in epitranscriptomics are highlighted and support the idea that RNA hypermethylation plays a key role in NPC pathogenesis. Nonetheless, these limitations do not undermine our confidence in this field, and we anticipate that relevant applied research will continue to emerge in the near future.
