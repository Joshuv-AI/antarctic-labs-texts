First-trimester pan-immune-inflammation value predicts preeclampsia in a dose-dependent linear pattern
Authors: Yi Zhu, Yanqiu Zhang, Wenshi Hu, Jun Cao, Bin Feng, Jieyu Jin, Sheng Zhang, Qingqin Tang, Longwei Qiao, Yuting Liang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPreeclampsia (PE), a multisystem hypertensive disorder complicating ~ 5% of pregnancies, remains a major global cause of maternal and perinatal morbidity and mortality. First-trimester screening methods are limited in accessibility and sensitivity, underscoring the need for affordable biomarkers. The pan-immune-inflammation value (PIV), derived from routine blood counts, shows potential as a predictor, but its independent predictive utility and dose–response association with PE are not yet fully defined.MethodsIn this retrospective cohort study, the association between first-trimester PIV and subsequent development of PE was evaluated. Multivariable logistic regression was used to assess PIV as an independent predictor, adjusting for confounders including maternal age, body mass index (BMI), parity, multifetal pregnancy and mode of conception (natural vs. in vitro fertilization [IVF, a method of assisted reproductive technology]). Dose–response patterns were explored using restricted cubic spline and two-piecewise linear regression models. Subgroup and interaction analyses were conducted to assess consistency across clinical strata. A dose-dependent increase in PE prevalence was observed across PIV quartiles, with the highest quartile associated with a 60.2% increased risk (OR, 1.602; 95% CI, 1.210–2.119; P < 0.001) compared to the lowest. Restricted cubic spline analysis suggested a nonlinear association in unadjusted models; however, an adequate linear fit was confirmed after adjustment (P for non-linearity > 0.05). These findings highlight the potential utility of PIV as an accessible, inflammation-based biomarker for early PE risk stratification. Introduction

Preeclampsia (PE) is a multisystem hypertensive disorder of pregnancy that affects approximately 5% of pregnancies and remains a leading cause of maternal and perinatal morbidity and mortality worldwide [1, 2]. Onset typically occurs after 20 weeks of gestation and is characterized by new-onset hypertension and proteinuria or other signs of end-organ dysfunction [3]. The condition may progress to eclampsia, HELLP syndrome, fetal growth restriction, preterm delivery, or even maternal and fetal death [4–6]. It has been estimated that PE contributes to approximately 46,000 maternal deaths and 500,000 fetal or neonatal deaths annually, particularly in low-resource settings [1]. These statistics underscore the urgent need to enhance early identification of women at risk.

However, accurate prediction of PE early in pregnancy remains a clinical challenge. First-trimester screening algorithms—typically incorporating maternal risk factors, mean arterial pressure, uterine artery Doppler, and angiogenic biomarkers such as placental growth factor (PlGF) or the sFlt-1/PlGF ratio—have improved the detection of preterm PE [7, 8], but a substantial proportion of cases continue to go undetected. Furthermore, several of these tools are constrained by limited availability or narrow windows of optimal use [1]. Given these limitations, attention has increasingly turned toward identifying novel and more accessible predictive markers. In this context, inflammation has been recognized as a fundamental mechanism in the pathogenesis of various diseases, including cardiovascular, renal, and autoimmune disorders [9–12]. Importantly, accumulating evidence has also highlighted the role of inflammation in the development and progression of PE, in which vascular inflammation and endothelial dysfunction are believed to play central roles in disease initiation [13, 14]. As a result, inflammation-related indices derived from routine blood tests have been proposed as cost-effective and widely available alternatives for early risk assessment. Hematologic markers such as the neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio (PLR), and systemic immune-inflammation index (SII) have been evaluated for their predictive utility in PE [15–17]. However, findings across studies have been inconsistent, underscoring the need for more reliable and integrated inflammatory biomarkers [18].

One such biomarker is the pan-immune-inflammation value (PIV), a composite index calculated as the product of neutrophil, platelet, and monocyte counts divided by lymphocyte count [19]. Because PE is associated with elevated neutrophil, platelet, and monocyte levels, PIV has been proposed as a candidate predictor of the condition [20–22]. Several recent cohort studies have reported that first-trimester PIV values are significantly higher among women who subsequently develop PE, supporting the concept that PIV may serve as a promising inflammation-based biomarker [18, 23, 24]. However, most existing studies have been limited by small sample sizes (typically fewer than 300 participants), cross-sectional or case-control designs, and reliance on univariate or ROC-based analyses. Few have investigated the continuous dose–response relationship between PIV and PE risk while controlling for potential confounders. It remains uncertain whether PE risk increases monotonically with higher PIV or if a specific threshold exists beyond which risk plateaus. In other domains of maternal health, dose–response modeling techniques, such as restricted cubic spline regression, have yielded useful insights. For example, one study identified a linear association between maternal soluble ST2 levels and PE risk [25]. To date, however, no study has rigorously examined the adjusted dose–response relationship between first-trimester PIV and the subsequent development of PE.

Accordingly, a single-center retrospective cohort study was conducted to investigate this association. The primary objective was to determine whether first-trimester PIV is independently associated with subsequent PE risk and whether the relationship follows a linear pattern or includes a threshold effect. The dose–response association was modeled using multivariable logistic regression incorporating restricted cubic spline and piecewise linear regression analyses. It was hypothesized that higher PIV values would be associated with incrementally increased PE risk in a linear fashion. By quantitatively characterizing the association between PIV and PE, this study aimed to enhance understanding of the role of inflammation in the pathogenesis of PE and to assess the potential utility of PIV as a biomarker for early risk prediction.

Materials and methods

Study design and population

The overall flow chart of this study is shown in Fig. 1. This retrospective cohort study included 11,894 pregnant individuals who underwent routine first-trimester prenatal screening before 14 weeks of gestation between 2015 and 2024, comprising 11,409 non-preeclampsia (non-PE) and 485 PE cases. Participants were excluded if key laboratory or obstetric data were missing (106 cases, 0.88% of total cohort); if there was a history of chronic hypertension; intrauterine fetal demise, termination of pregnancy, or spontaneous miscarriage; or if they had non-preeclamptic pregnancies complicated by intrauterine growth restriction or preterm birth. Only participants with complete data on the PIV and subsequent pregnancy outcomes were included in the final analysis.

Exposure measurement: pan-immune-inflammation value (PIV)

At the time of first-trimester screening (< 14 weeks of gestation), demographic and laboratory data were collected. The PIV was calculated from routine complete blood count parameters using the following formula:

PIV = (neutrophil count × platelet count × monocyte count)/lymphocyte count,

as previously described. To improve the clinical interpretability of the results, PIV was rescaled by dividing it by 100. This rescaling allowed the coefficients and odds ratios (OR) in the regression models to reflect the effect of a 100-unit increase in PIV, making the effect sizes easier to interpret in clinical practice. All hematologic indices were obtained from fasting peripheral blood samples. Participants were stratified into quartiles based on the distribution of PIV values.

Outcome assessment

The primary outcome was the development of PE, defined as new-onset hypertension and proteinuria after 20 weeks of gestation, as outlined in reference [26]. Hypertension was considered present if systolic blood pressure (SBP) was ≥ 140 mm Hg and diastolic blood pressure (DBP) was ≥ 90 mm Hg. Proteinuria was defined by meeting at least one of the following criteria: a urine dipstick result of ≥ 1 + on at least two occasions, a 24-hour urinary protein excretion ≥ 300 mg, or a spot urine protein-to-creatinine ratio ≥ 30 mg/mmol. In the absence of proteinuria, PE was diagnosed if new-onset hypertension was accompanied by evidence of maternal organ dysfunction, including but not limited to thrombocytopenia, impaired liver function, renal insufficiency, pulmonary edema, or new-onset cerebral or visual disturbances.

Obstetric outcomes were obtained from electronic medical records and independently confirmed by two obstetricians who were blinded to PIV status.

Covariates and confounders

Demographic and clinical variables, including maternal age, body mass index (BMI), parity, fetal plurality (singleton vs. multifetal pregnancy), mode of conception (natural vs. in vitro fertilization [IVF, a method of assisted reproductive technology]), were extracted from medical records and treated as potential confounders in subsequent analyses.

Statistical analyses

Continuous variables were expressed as means (standard deviations) or medians (interquartile ranges) and compared using the Student t test or Mann-Whitney U test, as appropriate. Categorical variables were compared using the χ² test. Multivariable logistic regression models were applied to estimate adjusted ORs and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for the association between first-trimester PIV and the risk of PE. Three models were constructed: Model 1: unadjusted. Model 2: adjusted for maternal age, BMI, and parity. Model 3: additionally adjusted for multifetal gestation and IVF conception. To examine potential dose-response relationships, PIV was analyzed both as a categorical variable (quartiles) and as a continuous variable using restricted cubic spline regression. Non-linearity was assessed by likelihood ratio tests comparing models with and without spline terms. Two-piecewise linear regression models were fitted to explore potential threshold effects, with the inflection point determined using a recursive algorithm. Model fit was evaluated via likelihood ratio testing. Subgroup analyses were performed to assess the consistency of associations across key clinical strata, including maternal age (< 35 vs. ≥ 35 years), BMI (< 30 vs. ≥ 30 kg/m²), parity (nulliparous vs. multiparous), IVF conception (yes vs. no), and multifetal gestation (yes vs. no). Interaction terms were incorporated into the models to assess effect modification, with statistical significance defined as P for interaction < 0.05. All analyses were conducted using R, and a two-tailed P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant [27].

Results

Association of first-trimester pan-immune-inflammation value with risk of PE

Before the 14th week of gestation (prior to the onset of PE), the PIV/100 was observed to be significantly elevated in the PE group compared with the non-PE group (Fig. 2). The median (first quartile, third quartile) for PIV/100 in the non-PE group was 3.2828 (2.2738, 4.7019), whereas in the PE group, it was 3.9904 (2.7865, 5.5901) (Supplementary Table 1). To further evaluate this association while controlling for potential confounding variables, a multivariate logistic regression analysis was performed. After adjustment for maternal age, BMI, parity, multifetal pregnancy and IVF conception, PIV/100 remained independently associated with PE (β = 0.073, SE = 0.017, Z = 4.378, P < 0.001; OR, 1.076; 95% CI, 1.041–1.112). These results indicate that even a marginal increase in PIV/100 is associated with a significantly increased risk of PE, independent of other maternal or obstetric factors (Fig. 3).

Other significant predictors of PE included IVF conception (OR, 2.235; 95% CI, 1.742–2.867), multifetal pregnancy (OR, 6.894; 95% CI, 4.726–10.058), advanced maternal age (OR, 1.049; 95% CI, 1.022–1.078), and elevated BMI (OR, 1.221; 95% CI, 1.189–1.254). In contrast, parity was associated with a decreased risk of PE (OR, 0.670; 95% CI, 0.538–0.835). Collectively, these findings highlight the potential of PIV to serve as an early biomarker for PE risk (Fig. 3).

Dose-response association between PIV quartiles and the risk of PE

The prevalence of PE was observed to increase progressively across higher quartiles of PIV/100. Among women in the lowest quartile (quartile 1), 2.69% developed PE, compared with 2.99%, 4.64%, and 5.99% in quartiles 2, 3, and 4, respectively (Fig. 4; Table 1). A significant association was detected between PIV/100 quartile and the occurrence of PE (χ² = 53.66; P < 0.001), indicating a dose-response relationship.

Pan-immune-inflammation value (PIV, divided by 100), analyzed as both a continuous and categorical variable, was significantly associated with the risk of preeclampsia across all models. When treated as a continuous variable, each unit increase in PIV/100 was independently associated with greater odds of preeclampsia in the unadjusted model (OR, 1.120; 95% CI, 1.087–1.154; P < 0.001), as well as in Model 2 adjusting for maternal age, BMI and parity (OR, 1.089; 95% CI, 1.056–1.124; P < 0.001) and in the fully adjusted Model 3 additionally controlling for multifetal pregnancy, and IVF conception (OR, 1.076; 95% CI, 1.041–1.112; P < 0.001).

To further evaluate this association while adjusting for potential confounders, a multivariate logistic regression analysis was performed. When PIV/100 was analyzed in quartiles, a clear dose–response relationship was observed. Compared with the lowest quartile (Q1), the odds of preeclampsia were significantly higher in Q3 and Q4 across all models. In the unadjusted model (Model 1), the ORs were 1.761 (95% CI, 1.331–2.330; P < 0.001) for Q3 and 2.303 (95% CI, 1.760–3.013; P < 0.001) for Q4. No significant difference was observed for Q2 in any model. A significant linear trend across quartiles was observed in all models (P for trend < 0.001), reinforcing the robustness and dose-dependent nature of the association (Fig. 5).

No threshold identified in the association between PIV and PE

RCS regression was used to assess the dose–response relationship between PIV/100 and PE. In unadjusted models, a non-linear pattern was suggested, with increased risk above the median PIV/100 level (Fig. 6A). However, after adjustment for maternal age, BMI, parity, multifetal pregnancy, and IVF conception, no evidence of non-linearity was detected, indicating that a linear model adequately described the data (P for non-linearity > 0.05) (Fig. 6B). Consistently, comparison of standard linear and two-piecewise linear regression models showed no significant improvement in fit for the piecewise model (likelihood ratio test P = 0.274) (Table 2). Together, these findings suggest that the association between PIV/100 and PE risk can be adequately modeled as linear, with no strong evidence for a threshold effect.

Subgroup and interaction analyses of the association between PIV and risk of PE

Subgroup analyses were performed to evaluate whether the association between PIV/100 and PE risk differed across clinically relevant strata, including advanced maternal age (age ≥ 35), obesity (BMI ≥ 30), parity, multifetal pregnancy, and IVF conception. Among patients without advanced maternal age, the association between PIV/100 and PE remained significant (P < 0.001), and the effect persisted in the advanced age subgroup (P = 0.002), with no statistically significant interaction detected (P for interaction = 0.286). Similarly, no significant interactions were found for obesity (P for interaction = 0.134), parity (P for interaction = 0.832), multifetal pregnancy (P for interaction = 0.123), or IVF conception (P for interaction = 0.644), indicating that the association between PIV/100 and PE risk was consistent across these subgroups (Table 3). These results suggest that the observed relationship between PIV/100 and PE is robust and not substantially modified by maternal age, BMI, parity, multifetal pregnancy conception, or conception method.

Discussion

In this study, it was observed that elevated PIV in early pregnancy (before 14 weeks’ gestation) was significantly associated with a higher risk of subsequent PE. This association remained statistically significant after adjusting for maternal age, BMI, parity, IVF conception, and multifetal pregnancy status. A clear dose–response gradient was evident: women in higher PIV quartiles experienced progressively greater risk of PE. Restricted cubic spline modeling suggested a non-linear exposure–response in unadjusted analysis, but an adequate linear fit after covariate adjustment; two-piecewise regression detected no significant inflection point, indicating that risk rises continuously across the PIV spectrum. Notably, the positive PIV–PE association was consistent across subgroups defined by age, BMI, parity, IVF status, and multifetal pregnancy (no significant interactions), underscoring the robustness of this finding.

Pathological inflammation is central to the development of PE [28]. The PIV index integrates neutrophil, monocyte, and platelet counts relative to lymphocytes, thereby capturing a systemic proinflammatory state. Neutrophils and monocytes, which mediate innate immune responses, are key effectors in PE [29, 30]: for example, placenta-derived IL-32β is markedly upregulated in PE and activates neutrophils to adhere to endothelium and upregulate adhesion molecules [31]. Correspondingly, PE is associated with expansion of pro-inflammatory monocyte subsets (non-classical and intermediate) [32]. Platelet activation is also heightened in PE; studies show that platelet activation markers (e.g. P-selectin expression and mean platelet volume) are significantly increased in preeclamptic pregnancies, reflecting a prothrombotic inflammatory milieu [33]. Collectively, a high PIV likely indicates a maternal milieu of innate immune activation and coagulation. This proinflammatory/procoagulant state can precipitate the placental ischemia, oxidative stress, and widespread endothelial injury that characterize PE.

Previous studies of first-trimester inflammatory indices have reported analogous findings. For example, Özkan et al. [23] reported that first-trimester PIV was higher in women who subsequently developed PE and Seyhanli et al. [18] found that first-trimester PIV significantly predicted later PE (identifying an optimal cutoff) [18]. Esercan et al. [24] likewise observed higher PIV in early-onset PE cases compared with controls. The present analysis extends these observations by quantifying a graded (dose-response) increase in risk across PIV quartiles and confirming that the association persists after multivariable adjustment. Importantly, the lack of a discrete threshold in piecewise analysis suggests that the risk of PE escalates steadily with higher PIV, a nuance not previously described. Moreover, by adjusting for multiple confounders (age, BMI, parity, IVF use, and plurality) and demonstrating consistency across key maternal subgroups, this study provides more rigorous evidence that elevated early-pregnancy PIV is independently associated with PE. These novel aspects (dose-response quantification, spline analysis, and subgroup validation) advance prior work by offering a more comprehensive characterization of the PIV-PE relationship.

From a clinical translation perspective, PIV, as a derivative of the routine complete blood count (CBC), offers significant advantages: it is extremely low-cost, readily available, and easily standardized for implementation in clinical settings. This contrasts with more specialized tools like PlGF testing or uterine artery Doppler ultrasonography, which are often costlier and less accessible, potentially making them more suitable for secondary screening or confirmatory testing in high-risk individuals. Thus, PIV has the potential to serve as a cost-effective first-line broad screening tool to identify pregnant individuals at potentially elevated risk for PE in the first trimester. These individuals could then be prioritized for referral to more specialized assessments (e.g., PlGF, Doppler), intensified prenatal monitoring, and initiation of preventive interventions (e.g., low-dose aspirin) where indicated.

However, our study has several limitations. First, its retrospective, single-center design may introduce selection bias, and the generalizability of the findings may be limited. Second, our findings lack external validation; the performance of PIV needs to be confirmed in prospective cohorts from diverse populations and healthcare centers to establish its broad applicability. Third, the sample sizes in certain subgroups (e.g., BMI ≥ 30, multifetal pregnancies) were small, potentially underpowering those analyses; larger studies focusing on these groups are needed in the future. Fourth, the lack of an established clinical cutoff for PIV limits its immediate clinical interpretability; future work is needed to define valid cut-off values. Finally, although we adjusted for several known confounders, the potential for unmeasured confounding exists. Factors such as underlying chronic inflammatory conditions or medication use could influence PIV levels and be associated with PE risk but were not captured in our data.

In this large cohort study, elevated PIV measured before 14 weeks of gestation was found to be independently associated with an increased risk of PE. This association persisted after adjustment for key maternal and obstetric confounders and was further supported by a clear dose-response gradient across PIV quartiles. Although a non-linear relationship was suggested in unadjusted analyses, adjusted models demonstrated a consistent linear association, with no statistically significant threshold identified. The strength and direction of this association were consistent across multiple clinically relevant subgroups, including maternal age, BMI, parity, mode of conception, and plurality, with no evidence of effect modification. These findings highlight the potential utility of first-trimester PIV as a biomarker for early risk stratification in PE. However, further prospective studies are crucial to validate these findings, establish reliable cut-offs, and fully evaluate its predictive performance (e.g., sensitivity, specificity).
