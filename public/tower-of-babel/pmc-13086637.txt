Racial and ethnic differences in informant reports of cognitive impairment
Authors: Sarah Newberry, Joey M. Saavedra, Phillip Cantu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractINTRODUCTIONThis study examined racial and ethnic differences in informant reports of cognitive decline in the United States.METHODSUsing data from the United States. Harmonized Cognitive Assessment Protocol (HCAP) (n = 2968), informant reports of cognitive impairment were measured with the Community Screening Instrument for Dementia (CSID). We applied ordinary least squares (OLS) regression for total CSID score and logistic regression for each CSID question to examine racial and ethnic differences controlling for directly assessed global cognitive functioning.RESULTSIn the adjusted OLS model for total CSID symptoms, Black respondents had significantly fewer informant‐reported symptoms (b = −1.27). Respondent female gender (b = 0.43), education (b = 0.14), global cognitive functioning (b = −2.66), informant age (b = −0.02), informant female gender (b = 0.77), and non‐family informant relationship (b = −0.91) were significantly associated with total CSID score. Black respondents were significantly less likely to have informants report eight symptoms.DISCUSSIONThere are racial and ethnic differences in informant reports of cognitive impairment when controlling for directly assessed cognitive functioning. Informant characteristics are crucial for interpreting informant reports of cognitive decline across racial and ethnic groups.

BACKGROUND

The clinical diagnosis of dementia relies on a combination of direct assessments, patient self‐reports, and informant reports provided by someone who knows the patient well, typically a family member or close friend. 1 , 2 These informants offer longitudinal, multicontext observations that help clinicians develop a more comprehensive understanding of an older adult's cognitive and functional status. Physicians often rely on informants to recognize and report signs of decline to support a formal diagnosis. 3 Population surveys also use informant assessments to estimate dementia prevalence in nationally representative samples, often with little consideration for informant characteristics. However, despite their value in diagnosing dementia, little is known about reporting differences among informants from diverse racial and ethnic backgrounds.

Over six million adults aged 65 years and older are living with dementia in the United States. 4 Survey estimates of population prevalence show that racial and ethnic minorities are disproportionately affected by dementia. Using data from the US‐based 2016 Harmonized Cognitive Assessment Protocol (HCAP), a substudy of the Health and Retirement Study (HRS), researchers found that non‐Hispanic Black older adults (hereafter, Black) have a higher prevalence of dementia, and Hispanic older adults have a higher prevalence of mild cognitive impairment, compared with non‐Hispanic White older adults (hereafter, White). 5 Despite the high prevalence of Alzheimer's disease among Black and Hispanic populations, individuals in these groups are more likely to be diagnosed at later stages of disease progression compared with their White counterparts. 6

A study using data from the Aging, Demographics, and Memory Study (ADAMS) examining Black and White older adults found that higher informant reports of decline, as reflected by higher Informant Questionnaire on Cognitive Decline in the Elderly (IQCODE) scores, were associated with greater odds of cognitive impairment, no dementia (CIND) in White, but not Black, older adults. Additionally, researchers using data from the HCAP and the Brain Attack Surveillance in Corpus Christi‐Cognitive (BASIC‐C; 2018 to 2020) found that, when controlling for older adults’ cognitive test scores from the HCAP, informant reports measured by the IQCODE were more strongly associated with actual cognitive decline in White older adults than in Hispanic/Latino older adults. 7 Together, these findings suggest inconsistent sensitivity of informant reports across racial and ethnic groups.

The prevalence and associated burdens of dementia are likely to increase as more Americans survive into older adulthood. Understanding the factors that influence how dementia is characterized is crucial for improving the accuracy of population‐level prevalence estimates. We therefore characterized the cross‐sectional associations between informant reports of cognition and respondent race and ethnicity, while accounting for directly assessed cognitive functioning, in a nationally representative sample of older adults. Our approach aims to clarify how race and ethnicity influence informant reports of cognitive impairment.

METHODS

Data

Data were drawn from the 2016 HRS‐HCAP, a random sample of nationally representative, non‐institutionalized HRS respondents aged 65 years and older. The HCAP includes direct cognitive assessments of respondents (i.e., older adults), as well as cognitive measures obtained from informant interviews. Informants were individuals nominated by respondents as people who had regular contact with them and could reliably report on their cognitive functioning. We limited our sample to respondents who had both a direct cognitive assessment and an informant interview in 2016. Of the 3496 respondents in the HCAP sample, 313 were excluded due to missing informant interviews. Of the remaining 3183 respondents, we excluded an additional 149 due to missing data on direct cognitive assessments. Finally, of the remaining 3034 respondents, we excluded 130 with incomplete data on education, gender, age, and/or informant relationships, resulting in a final analytical sample of 2904. The study protocol was approved by the University of Michigan Institutional Review Board, and all respondents and informants provided informed consent.

Community screening instrument for dementia

In the informant assessment, an adult capable of answering questions about the respondent's health and behavior – most frequently a spouse, adult child, or resident household caregiver – is asked to provide their perception of the respondent's cognitive functioning. We focused on items from the Community Screening Instrument for Dementia (CSID), 8 developed as a screening tool for dementia in cross‐cultural studies, which consists of both a direct assessment and an informant interview. Including an informant interview alongside the direct assessment improves the diagnostic accuracy of the CSID. 9 , 10 A short version of the informant interview can diagnose dementia with 94.7% sensitivity in Latin America. 11 While the CSID includes both direct and informant assessments, the HCAP protocol only includes the informant assessment.

The HCAP informant CSID included 21 informant questions. The questions consisted of three binary items and 18 multiple‐response items (no, sometimes, often, or equivalent levels). To facilitate the analysis and reduce ambiguity of “sometimes” responses, we dichotomized the multiple‐response items into binary variables (no, sometimes/often). Removing the level of impairment and focusing on item reports simplified the analysis. We also created a composite total CSID score based on the total number of items reported by informants to examine overall differences in informant reports. HCAP provides imputed data for missing variables derived from fully conditional specification (FCS) methods and using the SAS procedure “PROC MI,” details of which are explained elsewhere. 12 Briefly, this method imputes missing binary and categorical variables using logit and generalized logits models. In the present analyses, 9.2% of informant responses to individual questions was imputed. 13

Measurement of cognition

The HCAP direct assessment of cognitive functioning contains neuropsychological batteries to assess multiple cognitive domains, including orientation, memory, executive functioning, language, and visuospatial functioning. Prior research established a cross‐nationally harmonized cognition score for the HCAP. 14 The harmonized cognition score leverages common items in cognitive functioning obtained from several longitudinal studies to produce a standardized metric of cognition (Z‐score) with a mean of 0 and a standard deviation of 1, making the interpretation of cognition intuitive. The validity of this approach is explained in detail elsewhere. 14

Measurement of respondent characteristics and other covariates

The HCAP direct assessment of cognitive functioning also collected data on potential correlates of cognitive functioning. We included respondent race and ethnicity, age (years), gender (interviewer‐reported as male or female), education (years), and directly assessed cognitive functioning (harmonized cognition score). Information on race and ethnicity came from the HRS core interview survey, in which respondents were asked to report their race (White, Black, other) and ethnicity (Hispanic or non‐Hispanic). We combined race and ethnicity into three groups: White, Black, and Hispanic. Individuals whose race and ethnicity did not fit into these groups were excluded from analysis (n = 79). The informant interview included demographic and relationship questions. Demographic variables from the informant interview used in this study included informant age (years) and informant gender (interviewer‐reported as male or female). Informant relationship to the respondent was characterized as spouse, child, other family, or non‐family, along with co‐resident status (co‐resident lives with respondent or non–co‐resident). The co‐residence question in the informant HCAP includes options for “lives with the respondent”, “sees daily,” “several times a week,” “once a week,” “one to three times a month,” “less than once a month,” and “never.” Most spousal informants responded “sees daily” instead of “lives with respondent.” As a result, we coded spousal informants who reported “sees daily” as co‐resident informants.

Statistical methods

We assessed differences in respondent and informant characteristics by race using descriptive statistics. Chi‐squared (χ 2) tests and analysis of variance (ANOVA) were used to evaluate differences between racial and ethnic groups. We also examined the prevalence of each CSID question in the informant sample without adjusting for covariates. Ordinary least squares (OLS) regression was used to examine the total CSID score in three models: Model 1 examined the bivariate relationship between race and ethnicity and total CSID score; Model 2 examined racial and ethnic differences, controlling for respondent characteristics, including directly assessed cognitive functioning; and Model 3 examined racial and ethnic differences, controlling for both respondent and informant characteristics. Logistic regression was used to model the relationship between race and ethnicity and informant reports of cognitive decline for each of the 21 CSID questions separately, controlling for respondent and informant characteristics. Data were weighted to account for the HRS‐HCAP complex survey design, and all analyses were conducted using STATA 17 (StataCorp LLC, College Station, TX, USA).

DISCUSSION

This cross‐sectional study found that Black respondents had significantly lower CSID scores than White respondents, whereas scores among Hispanic respondents were comparable to those of White respondents when controlling for respondent and informant characteristics. Informant characteristics such as age, gender, and relationship to the respondent were significantly associated with respondents’ total CSID score. Furthermore, informants reported fewer cognitive symptoms for Black respondents relative to White respondents, while there were no significant differences between Hispanic and White respondents. These novel findings suggest incongruencies in how informants perceive and report cognitive impairment in Black respondents compared with the direct assessment of cognition.

In addition to racial and ethnic differences in the total number of symptoms reported by informants, we also found differences across many specific CSID questions. The bivariate relationship between symptoms reported by informants and race and ethnicity showed that Hispanic respondents had significantly higher informant reports for five of 21 questions. However, when controlling for informant and respondent correlates, we found Black respondents had significantly lower odds of informant reports for eight CSID symptoms.

In the descriptive results, we found important demographic differences in the composition of informants across racial and ethnic groups. Black respondents were more likely to have non‐family informants than White respondents, which may provide value for contextualizing results. Additionally, we found that Hispanic older adults were the most likely to have co‐resident informants. There is evidence that relationship characteristics between informants and respondents are related to the accuracy of informant reports of cognitive decline. A study using the National Alzheimer's Coordinating Center Uniform Data Set (NACC UDS) examined the association between informant reports of functioning, measured using the Functional Activities Questionnaire (FAQ) and the Neuropsychiatric Inventory Questionnaire (NPI‐Q), and changes in directly assessed cognitive functioning. The study found stronger associations for spousal and co‐resident informants compared with non‐spouse and non–co‐resident informants. 15 Black respondents were the least likely to have spousal and co‐resident informants, suggesting that their informants’ reports may be less associated with directly assessed cognitive functioning. Hispanics were the most likely to have co‐resident informants. Co‐residence and frequency of contact may fundamentally alter the way that informants report on symptoms of cognitive decline. On the one hand, informants who see respondents every day may be more attuned to changes in behaviors through frequent observation of changes in daily life. 16 Alternatively, informants who live with respondents may be less likely to report changes over time due to a normalization process to ignore or minimize changes in functional status. 17 Ultimately, our results show that there are racial and ethnic differences in the types of informants in the HCAP that impact the meaning of informant reports.

No prior research compared racial and ethnic differences in informant reports of cognitive decline using the CSID, but an extensive body of literature on other informant questionnaires offers meaningful points of comparison. Research using the FAQ found that Black informants were more likely to report better physical functioning in respondents with mild cognitive impairment. 18 While informant reports using the IQCODE were equally associated with dementia diagnosis for Black and White respondents, the IQCODE was not associated with mild cognitive impairment for Black respondents. 7 We found lower odds of reports of symptoms of cognitive decline for Black respondents in fully adjusted models. These results suggest that there may be systematic bias in informant assessments of cognitive decline for Black respondents, with informants assessing and reporting cognitive impairment differently than informants of White respondents.

While the Black–White racial difference in informant reports may be due to race‐specific perceptions and reports of cognitive decline, 19 , 20 there is also the possibility that significant differences in our study are due to bias in direct measures of cognitive impairment. The Telephone Interview for Cognitive Status, a direct measure of cognitive functioning, has been shown to have systematic racial bias that overestimates racial differences in cognition. 21 It is possible that similar biases in the HCAP overestimate cognitive impairment in the Black sample, and that racial differences in informant assessments provide a more accurate reflection of cognitive functioning.

Results for informants of Hispanic respondents were less consistent than those for Black and White respondents. In general, there was a higher prevalence of informant reports for more than half of the CSID items among Hispanic respondents, but only three items showed significant differences from White respondents in adjusted models. Prior research found a weaker association between informant IQCODE reports of cognitive decline and cognitive test performance for Hispanic respondents compared with White respondents. 22 Our results suggest compatibility between informant reports for Hispanic and White respondents. Although there is a greater overall prevalence of many CSID symptoms in the Hispanic sample, these differences appear to be explained by other correlates of cognitive functioning.

Current literature also indicates that informant characteristics beyond race and ethnicity, such as emotional state, relationship to the patient, and familiarity with the patient's daily functioning, may influence their reports on tools such as the IQCODE. 23 Similarly, we found that informants’ age, gender, and relationship to respondents were significantly associated with the total number of CSID items reported. Cross‐national research highlights the need for culturally sensitive considerations when interpreting data from the IQCODE and similar tools, 24 , 25 as there may be different approaches to or understandings of the questions or varying willingness to report observed decline. Our results on racial and ethnic differences in the CSID within the United States further underscore non‐cognitive sources of heterogeneity in informant measures of cognitive impairment that should be accounted for in population studies of cognitive functioning. Previous studies demonstrated the importance of considering informant characteristics such as gender, relationship to and time spent with the patient, socioeconomic burden, age, and level of education on recognition, as these factors may be potential mediators of reports of cognitive decline and/or mediators of the association between informant reports and objective neuropsychological performance. 26 , 27 Put another way, informant reports of cognitive decline are subjective measures of cognitive functioning, and while there are statistically significant differences in models of cognitive functioning, these results do not imply that some informants are “more” correct than others, rather that they respond in different and meaningful ways.

Our results highlight racial and ethnic differences in informant reports of cognitive decline but do not establish a causal direction for these differences. After accounting for differences in directly assessed cognitive functioning, Black respondents had significantly fewer informant‐reported symptoms of cognitive decline. Two competing hypotheses may explain these results. First, informants of Black respondents may report fewer symptoms than informants of White respondents due to factors at the informant level, such as stigma surrounding discussion of symptoms or lack of awareness of symptoms of cognitive decline. Few studies characterized racial and ethnic differences in dementia stigma; however, existing research has found low levels of such stigma among African Americans. 28 Lower reports of symptoms by informants of Black respondents may also reflect difficulty recognizing subtle symptoms, uncertainty about which symptoms are considered pathological, or problems finding reliable information, leading caregivers to rely on the Internet or advice from friends to decipher how to proceed. 29 Informal care and caregiving from informants may also play a role in racial differences in informant reports of cognitive impairment. Informal caregivers of Black and Hispanic older adults with dementia average 33.5 to 44.1 h of care a week respectively compared to 23.5 h for informal caregivers of White older adults with dementia. 30 The fewer symptoms reported may reflect the burden placed on these caregivers as they attempt to manage their caretaking responsibilities in addition to their own immediate families, jobs, and stressors. 31 Reduced capacity to notice gradual cognitive changes may be secondary to caregiver burnout, even among otherwise knowledgeable individuals. Common stressors among caregivers include isolation, lack of respite, economic strain, and emotional distress, each of which is capable of negatively impacting caregiver capacity. 29 , 32

Alternatively, the differences between the informant and direct assessments may reflect measurement issues with the direct assessment of cognitive functioning. The harmonized cognitive assessment may not accurately capture cognitive functioning across racial and ethnic groups, and informant reports may more accurately reflect true functional capacity. While both explanations are beyond the scope of this study, future research using longitudinal data from the HCAP will be able to more accurately assess the cause of racial and ethnic differences in informant reports.

Study strengths include the use of a large, population‐representative sample of older adults across the continuum of cognitive functioning. Unlike clinical samples, the HCAP sample provides a more accurate picture of community‐dwelling older adults and their informants. Additionally, we control for directly assessed cognitive functioning from the HCAP, allowing us to examine how informants report on cognitive decline net of differences in directly assessed cognitive functioning. However, several limitations should be considered. First, the cross‐sectional design limits our ability to make causal inferences. The ongoing HCAP study will enable more sophisticated analyses of these relationships using longitudinal data in the future. Second, we used HCAP informant data that had been imputed for missing values. Additional analysis using unimputed raw HCAP data yielded substantively similar results, though statistical power was reduced due to smaller sample size (Tables S1–S4). While the focus of our study is racial and ethnic differences in informant reports of cognitive decline, we are limited to examining the race/ethnicity of the respondent and not of the informants. The informant HCAP does not ask the race/ethnicity of informants, and our results are limited to conclusions about the race/ethnicity of respondents. While it is likely that most respondent–informant dyads are of the same race/ethnicity, we have no way of verifying this assumption with the HCAP informant data. Finally, the co‐residence question in the informant HCAP is imprecise. Very few spousal informants report living with respondents and instead responded that they see informants daily. In this study, we incorporated the “sees daily” spousal informants into the co‐resident group. While certainly there are spousal informants who do not live with respondents, we felt that we more accurately reflected the role of co‐residence with this coding decision. We conducted additional analyses with the co‐residence category as exclusively informants who responded “lives together” and results were consistent with the coding used in this analysis (additional analysis available upon request).

This study provides novel evidence of racial and ethnic differences in informant reports of cognitive symptoms among Black older adults. Specifically, informants for this group tend to underreport cognitive symptoms. Careful examination of the meaning of informant reports is necessary to improve comparability across racial and ethnic groups. Furthermore, ensuring that direct assessment tools accurately capture the cognitive status of racial minorities, alongside enhancing our understanding of how informant characteristics influence their reports, may help clinicians achieve earlier diagnoses in minority groups, ultimately leading to more effective treatment plans and better patient outcomes.
