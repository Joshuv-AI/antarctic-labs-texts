Gastric schwannoma: diagnostic challenges, pathological features, and surgical management : a narrative review
Authors: Guo Hou Loo, Suria Hayati Md Pauzi, Nik Ritza Kosai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT

Background

Gastric subepithelial tumours comprise a heterogeneous group of lesions arising from the mesenchymal components of the gastric wall, including the muscularis propria and submucosa [1, 2]. Among these, gastrointestinal stromal tumours (GISTs) are the most frequently encountered and clinically significant entity due to their malignant potential and implications for oncologic management [3]. Consequently, GIST is often presumed when a gastric mesenchymal tumour is identified. However, several benign neoplasms may closely mimic GIST in clinical presentation, endoscopic appearance, and radiologic characteristics, leading to diagnostic uncertainty and potential overtreatment [4, 5].

Gastric schwannoma is a rare benign tumour derived from Schwann cells of the peripheral nerve sheath within the gastric wall [6]. It accounts for a small proportion of gastric mesenchymal tumours and is most commonly discovered incidentally during endoscopy or cross-sectional imaging performed for unrelated indications [6, 7]. Despite its indolent biological behaviour and excellent prognosis following complete resection, gastric schwannoma frequently poses a diagnostic challenge because it shares substantial overlap with GIST in terms of tumour location, growth pattern, and enhancement characteristics on contrast-enhanced computed tomography [8–10]. Endoscopically, both entities typically appear as well-circumscribed submucosal masses with intact overlying mucosa, further limiting preoperative differentiation [9, 11].

Accurate distinction between gastric schwannoma and GIST is clinically important. While surgical excision with negative margins is curative for gastric schwannoma and adjuvant therapy is not required, GIST management may involve oncologic resection strategies, long-term surveillance, and consideration of targeted systemic therapy [3, 12]. Misclassification may therefore expose patients with benign disease to unnecessary anxiety, inappropriate perioperative planning, or overtreatment, whereas failure to recognise malignant potential in true GIST carries significant oncologic consequences [4, 12].

Although advances in imaging modalities, including endoscopic ultrasound and multiphase computed tomography, have improved characterisation of gastric subepithelial tumours, definitive diagnosis remains dependent on histopathological examination and immunohistochemical profiling [6, 9]. Gastric schwannomas are characterised by spindle cell morphology with Antoni A and Antoni B areas, frequent peritumoral lymphoid cuffing, and strong immunoreactivity for S-100 protein and SOX10, with absence of CD117 and DOG-1 expression [6, 13–15]. These diagnostic features are typically unavailable preoperatively due to the limitations and potential risks of biopsy, particularly when GIST is suspected [12, 16].

From a surgical oncology perspective, management strategies for gastric schwannoma have evolved with the adoption of minimally invasive techniques. Laparoscopic wedge resection remains the most commonly employed approach and is associated with excellent outcomes [7, 17]. In selected cases, particularly small intraluminal tumours, endoscopic resection techniques such as endoscopic submucosal dissection, endoscopic submucosal excavation, and endoscopic full-thickness resection have been increasingly reported [18–20]. However, patient selection, oncologic safety, and technical limitations remain areas of ongoing debate, and consensus guidelines specific to gastric schwannoma are lacking [17, 21].

This narrative review aims to synthesise current evidence on the epidemiology, diagnostic challenges, imaging characteristics, histopathological features, and surgical and endoscopic management of gastric schwannoma, with particular emphasis on issues relevant to contemporary surgical oncology practice.

Literature search strategy

This narrative review was conducted through a structured literature search of the PubMed, Scopus, and Web of Science databases. The search included studies published between January 1990 and December 2025 using combinations of the following keywords: “gastric schwannoma”, “gastric neurilemmoma”, “gastric subepithelial tumor”, “gastric mesenchymal tumor”, and “gastric spindle cell tumor”.

Eligible publications included clinical studies, case series, systematic reviews, and relevant case reports that described the epidemiology, imaging characteristics, pathological features, and management of gastric schwannoma. Articles not written in English, conference abstracts without full manuscripts, and studies lacking clinical or pathological confirmation were excluded.

Titles and abstracts were screened independently by the authors to identify relevant studies. Full-text articles were subsequently reviewed to extract data related to clinical presentation, diagnostic modalities, surgical and endoscopic management, and reported outcomes.

Because gastric schwannoma is a rare tumour and most available evidence consists of retrospective studies and case series, the review aimed to synthesise existing evidence rather than perform quantitative meta-analysis.

Epidemiology and biological behaviour

Gastric schwannoma is an uncommon mesenchymal tumour of the stomach, accounting for approximately 0.2–1.0% of all gastric neoplasms and 3–7% of gastric mesenchymal tumours, making it substantially less prevalent than GIST [6, 7, 22]. Due to its rarity, most epidemiological data are derived from retrospective institutional series and pooled analyses rather than large prospective studies [6, 22]. The epidemiological estimates reported in this review are derived from pooled international case series and therefore reflect global prevalence patterns rather than region-specific incidence.

The reported prevalence ranges reflect variation in study populations and case ascertainment methods. Estimates of 0.2–1.0% refer to the proportion of gastric schwannomas among all gastric tumours, whereas the higher range of 3–7% reflects their proportion among gastric mesenchymal tumours specifically. Across pooled clinicopathological series published to date, several hundred cases have been reported worldwide, although most studies remain limited to single-institution retrospective analyses.

The tumour most commonly presents in the fifth to sixth decades of life, with multiple series reporting a slight female predominance [6, 7, 23]. Gastric schwannomas are typically solitary lesions and most frequently arise in the gastric body, followed by the antrum and fundus [6, 22]. Unlike epithelial gastric malignancies, no association has been identified with Helicobacter pylori infection, hereditary cancer syndromes, or specific environmental risk factors [7, 24].

From a biological standpoint, gastric schwannoma demonstrates indolent growth and benign behaviour. Most patients are asymptomatic at diagnosis, with lesions often detected incidentally during endoscopy or imaging performed for unrelated clinical indications [7, 22]. When symptoms occur, they are usually non-specific and include abdominal discomfort, early satiety, or upper gastrointestinal bleeding, the latter typically resulting from mucosal compression and secondary ulceration rather than invasive growth [6, 23].

Importantly, gastric schwannoma exhibits a markedly different oncologic profile compared with GIST. Regional lymph node involvement, distant metastasis, and tumour-related mortality are exceedingly rare [6, 7, 25]. Long-term follow-up data from clinicopathological series consistently demonstrate excellent outcomes following complete surgical excision, with recurrence rates approaching zero, even in tumours exceeding 5 cm in size [6, 25, 26]. Malignant gastric schwannoma has been reported only exceptionally and is usually associated with atypical histological features such as increased mitotic activity, nuclear pleomorphism, or elevated proliferative indices [22, 25].

The benign clinical behaviour of gastric schwannoma correlates closely with its histological and molecular characteristics. Histologically, these tumours exhibit well-organised spindle cells with low mitotic activity and a characteristic peripheral lymphoid cuff, a feature rarely observed in GIST [6, 13]. Molecular analyses further support this distinction, as gastric schwannomas lack activating KIT and PDGFRA mutations that drive oncogenesis in GIST [15, 24].

Overall, gastric schwannoma should be regarded as a benign gastric mesenchymal tumour with an excellent prognosis following complete resection. Nevertheless, its epidemiological rarity and frequent overlap with more aggressive gastric mesenchymal tumours underscore the importance of accurate diagnosis to guide appropriate surgical management and to avoid unnecessary oncologic treatment or surveillance [7, 22, 26].

Diagnostic challenges and differential diagnosis

Gastric schwannoma presents a significant diagnostic challenge because its clinical, endoscopic, and radiologic features substantially overlap with those of gastrointestinal stromal tumours (GISTs), which are far more prevalent and carry malignant potential [27]. In routine clinical practice, this overlap often results in a presumptive diagnosis of GIST when a gastric mesenchymal tumour is identified, particularly for lesions arising from the muscularis propria with exophytic growth patterns [28]. Consequently, gastric schwannoma is frequently recognised only after definitive histopathological examination.

Other mesenchymal tumours that should be considered in the differential diagnosis include gastric leiomyoma, which arises from smooth muscle cells of the muscularis propria. Leiomyomas typically demonstrate positive staining for desmin and smooth muscle actin, in contrast to gastric schwannomas, which are negative for smooth muscle markers but strongly positive for S-100 protein [29].

Clinically, both gastric schwannoma and GIST are often asymptomatic and discovered incidentally during endoscopy or cross-sectional imaging performed for unrelated indications [22, 27]. When symptoms occur, they are non-specific and include epigastric discomfort, early satiety, or upper gastrointestinal bleeding, which may result from mucosal compression and ulceration rather than invasive tumour behaviour [23, 27]. Symptomatology alone therefore does not reliably distinguish between these entities.

Endoscopically, gastric schwannomas typically appear as well-circumscribed subepithelial lesions with smooth contours and intact overlying mucosa, features that closely resemble those of GIST and leiomyoma [9, 27]. Endoscopic ultrasound (EUS) may provide additional information regarding layer of origin and echotexture, as gastric schwannomas are commonly hypoechoic lesions arising from the muscularis propria with relatively homogeneous internal echoes and well-defined margins [10, 11]. However, these characteristics are not pathognomonic, and considerable overlap exists with low-risk GISTs, limiting the diagnostic accuracy of EUS alone [27, 30].

Contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT) plays a central role in the preoperative assessment of gastric subepithelial tumours, yet differentiation between gastric schwannoma and GIST remains challenging. Both lesions often present as well-defined, exophytic masses with preserved fat planes and absence of regional lymphadenopathy [8, 27]. Several studies have suggested that gastric schwannomas may demonstrate more homogeneous enhancement and a higher likelihood of progressive enhancement compared with GISTs, which more frequently exhibit heterogeneous enhancement due to necrosis or haemorrhage [8, 31]. Additionally, bidirectional growth across the gastric wall has been reported more commonly in gastric schwannomas, whereas GISTs demonstrate greater variability in growth patterns [31]. Despite these observations, imaging features alone are insufficient to establish a definitive diagnosis.

The role of preoperative biopsy in the evaluation of suspected gastric schwannoma remains controversial. Endoscopic ultrasound-guided fine-needle aspiration (EUS-FNA) can provide tissue for diagnosis; however, its diagnostic yield for subepithelial tumours is variable and may be limited by insufficient tissue sampling [32]. Furthermore, when GIST is suspected, preoperative biopsy is not routinely recommended in potentially resectable tumours due to concerns regarding tumour rupture and peritoneal dissemination, which are associated with poorer oncologic outcomes [12, 16]. As a result, many gastric schwannomas proceed directly to surgical resection without a preoperative tissue diagnosis.

Definitive differentiation between gastric schwannoma and other gastric mesenchymal tumours relies on histopathological and immunohistochemical analysis. Histologically, gastric schwannomas are characterised by spindle cells arranged in Antoni A and Antoni B patterns, with frequent peritumoral lymphoid cuffing, a feature that is uncommon in GIST [13, 33]. Immunohistochemically, gastric schwannomas demonstrate diffuse positivity for S-100 protein and SOX10, with absence of CD117, DOG-1, and CD34 expression, in contrast to GIST, which typically expresses CD117 and DOG-1 [13–15, 33]. These features are critical for establishing the correct diagnosis and guiding appropriate management.

Given these challenges, gastric schwannoma should remain an important differential diagnosis for gastric subepithelial tumours presumed to be GIST. Awareness of its distinctive pathological features and limitations of preoperative diagnostic modalities is essential to avoid overtreatment and to inform appropriate surgical planning in contemporary surgical oncology practice [22, 27, 31].

Imaging characteristics

Cross-sectional imaging and endoscopic ultrasound (EUS) play a central role in the initial evaluation of gastric subepithelial tumours. However, despite advances in imaging technology, reliable preoperative differentiation between gastric schwannoma and gastrointestinal stromal tumour (GIST) remains challenging due to overlapping radiologic and endoscopic features [27, 31].

Computed tomography

Contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT) is the most commonly utilised imaging modality for characterising gastric mesenchymal tumours. Gastric schwannomas typically appear as well-circumscribed, round or ovoid masses arising from the gastric wall, often demonstrating an exophytic or mixed growth pattern with preservation of surrounding fat planes [8, 27]. Regional lymphadenopathy and distant metastases are characteristically absent, reflecting the benign nature of the tumour [6, 25].

Several studies have attempted to identify CT features that may aid in differentiating gastric schwannoma from GIST. Gastric schwannomas typically demonstrate homogeneous attenuation on pre-contrast imaging and progressive, moderate enhancement during arterial and venous phases [8, 31]. In contrast, GISTs are more likely to display heterogeneous enhancement due to necrosis, haemorrhage, or cystic degeneration, particularly in larger lesions [31, 34]. The absence of intratumoral necrosis and the presence of uniform enhancement have therefore been proposed as supportive imaging features favouring gastric schwannoma [34]. Additionally, bidirectional growth across the gastric wall has been reported more commonly in gastric schwannomas, whereas GISTs exhibit greater variability in growth patterns, including predominantly exophytic growth [31].

Despite these observations, considerable overlap exists, and no single CT feature reliably distinguishes gastric schwannoma from GIST. Tumour size, location, and enhancement patterns should therefore be interpreted in conjunction with clinical and endoscopic findings rather than in isolation [27, 34].

Endoscopic ultrasound

Endoscopic ultrasound provides high-resolution assessment of the gastric wall layers and is particularly useful for determining the layer of origin of subepithelial lesions. Gastric schwannomas typically appear as hypoechoic, homogeneous lesions arising from the fourth layer (muscularis propria) with well-defined margins and minimal internal heterogeneity [10, 11]. In some cases, a peripheral hypoechoic halo corresponding to lymphoid cuffing may be observed. However, these findings overlap substantially with low-risk GIST, limiting diagnostic specificity [11, 33].

Comparative studies have shown that gastric schwannomas tend to demonstrate more uniform echotexture and clearer boundaries compared with GISTs, which may display heterogeneous echogenicity and irregular margins, particularly in higher-risk tumours [10, 30]. However, these differences are subtle, and significant overlap exists, limiting the diagnostic accuracy of EUS alone [27, 30].

EUS-guided fine-needle aspiration or biopsy may be considered to obtain tissue for diagnosis, but its role remains controversial. Diagnostic yield can be limited by inadequate sampling, particularly in firm, fibrous tumours such as schwannomas [32]. Moreover, when GIST is suspected and the tumour is considered resectable, routine preoperative biopsy is often avoided due to concerns regarding tumour rupture and peritoneal dissemination [12, 16]. As a result, imaging and EUS frequently guide surgical decision-making without definitive preoperative histological confirmation.

Recent advances in EUS-guided core biopsy needles (fine-needle biopsy, FNB) have improved tissue acquisition for subepithelial lesions, allowing better preservation of histological architecture and enabling immunohistochemical analysis [32, 35]. Studies have reported diagnostic yields exceeding 80% in experienced centres, although sampling limitations remain for fibrous tumours such as schwannomas.

Emerging imaging techniques

Recent studies have explored the role of advanced imaging techniques, including CT radiomics and quantitative texture analysis, in differentiating gastric schwannoma from GIST. Preliminary data suggest that radiomic features may improve diagnostic accuracy by capturing subtle differences in tumour heterogeneity and enhancement patterns [36, 37]. However, these approaches remain investigational and are not yet incorporated into routine clinical practice.

Overall, while CT and EUS provide valuable information regarding tumour size, location, growth pattern, and relationship to surrounding structures, imaging findings alone are insufficient to definitively distinguish gastric schwannoma from other gastric mesenchymal tumours. Definitive diagnosis continues to rely on histopathological and immunohistochemical evaluation following resection [27, 31].

Histopathology and immunohistochemistry

Histopathological examination remains the definitive method for diagnosing gastric schwannoma and for distinguishing it from other gastric mesenchymal tumours, particularly gastrointestinal stromal tumours (GISTs). Macroscopically, gastric schwannomas are typically well-circumscribed, encapsulated masses with a firm consistency and smooth external surface, reflecting their non-infiltrative growth pattern [6, 22]. Microscopically, gastric schwannomas are composed of spindle cells arranged in characteristic Antoni A and Antoni B patterns. Antoni A areas are hypercellular and consist of compact spindle cells with elongated nuclei arranged in fascicles, often demonstrating nuclear palisading and occasional Verocay bodies. Antoni B areas are hypocellular, with loosely arranged spindle cells set within a myxoid stroma [13, 33]. Mitotic activity is generally low, and significant cytological atypia is uncommon, correlating with the tumour’s benign biological behaviour [6, 25].

A distinctive and diagnostically useful feature of gastric schwannoma is the presence of a peripheral lymphoid cuff, which surrounds the tumour in a substantial proportion of cases. This lymphoid aggregation is thought to represent a host immune response and is rarely observed in GIST or other gastric mesenchymal tumours [13, 33]. Its identification on histological examination provides an important clue in differentiating gastric schwannoma from morphologically similar entities.

Immunohistochemistry plays a critical role in establishing the diagnosis. Gastric schwannomas demonstrate strong and diffuse immunoreactivity for S-100 protein, reflecting their Schwann cell origin [6, 13]. SOX10, a transcription factor expressed in neural crest–derived tumours, has also been shown to be a sensitive and specific marker for schwannian differentiation and is consistently positive in gastric schwannomas [14, 38]. In contrast, gastric schwannomas are characteristically negative for CD117 (KIT) and DOG-1, markers that are highly sensitive and specific for GIST [15, 33].

Additional immunohistochemical features further aid in differential diagnosis. Gastric schwannomas are typically negative for CD34 or show only focal positivity, whereas CD34 expression is frequently observed in GIST [15]. Smooth muscle markers such as desmin and smooth muscle actin are negative, helping to distinguish schwannomas from leiomyomas and leiomyosarcomas [13, 22]. Proliferative indices assessed by Ki-67 staining are generally low, usually below 5%, consistent with the tumour’s indolent behaviour [25, 26].

At the molecular level, gastric schwannomas lack the KIT and PDGFRA mutations that drive oncogenesis in GIST, further supporting their classification as a biologically distinct entity [15, 24]. This molecular distinction reinforces the clinical importance of accurate histopathological diagnosis, as it directly influences postoperative management, surveillance strategies, and the avoidance of unnecessary adjuvant therapy.

In summary, the diagnosis of gastric schwannoma relies on the integration of histomorphological features and immunohistochemical profiling. Recognition of Antoni A and B areas, peripheral lymphoid cuffing, and strong S-100 and SOX10 positivity with absence of CD117 and DOG-1 expression is essential for distinguishing gastric schwannoma from other gastric mesenchymal tumours and for guiding appropriate surgical oncology management [6, 13, 33].

Surgical management of gastric schwannoma

Surgical resection remains the cornerstone of treatment for gastric schwannoma and is considered curative in the vast majority of cases. Given the benign biological behaviour of the tumour, the primary goal of surgery is complete excision with negative margins while preserving gastric function and minimising operative morbidity [6, 22, 25].

The decision to proceed with surgical resection is typically based on tumour size, location, growth pattern, and the inability to reliably exclude malignancy preoperatively. In clinical practice, many gastric schwannomas are resected under the presumptive diagnosis of gastrointestinal stromal tumour (GIST), particularly when lesions arise from the muscularis propria or demonstrate exophytic growth on imaging [27, 31]. As a result, surgical intervention is frequently undertaken even in asymptomatic patients.

Laparoscopic wedge resection has emerged as the preferred surgical approach for most gastric schwannomas and is associated with excellent short- and long-term outcomes [17, 22, 26]. This technique allows en bloc removal of the tumour with its capsule intact, provides adequate oncologic clearance, and avoids unnecessary radical gastrectomy. Reported operative outcomes for laparoscopic resection of gastric schwannoma are highly favourable. Published series describe operative times ranging from 60 to 150 min, conversion rates below 5%, and postoperative morbidity rates between 0 and 10%, with most complications consisting of minor surgical site infections or delayed gastric emptying. Several series have demonstrated that laparoscopic resection is safe and feasible for tumours up to 5–6 cm in size, provided that their location permits preservation of gastric continuity and function [22, 25, 26].

Tumour location plays a critical role in determining the operative strategy. Lesions located along the greater curvature or anterior gastric wall are generally amenable to straightforward wedge resection. In contrast, tumours situated near the gastroesophageal junction, pylorus, or lesser curvature may pose technical challenges due to the risk of postoperative stenosis or functional impairment [25, 26]. In such cases, careful preoperative planning and intraoperative assessment are essential to balance complete resection with functional preservation.

Importantly, lymphadenectomy is not indicated in the surgical management of gastric schwannoma, as regional lymph node involvement is exceedingly rare [6, 25]. Similarly, there is no role for adjuvant chemotherapy or targeted therapy following complete resection, even in larger tumours, given the excellent prognosis and negligible recurrence rates reported in the literature [6, 7, 25].

Long-term outcomes following surgical resection are highly favourable. Multiple retrospective series have reported near-zero recurrence rates and excellent overall survival following R0 resection, regardless of tumour size [6, 25, 26]. Routine long-term oncologic surveillance is therefore generally unnecessary once a definitive diagnosis of gastric schwannoma has been established, although short-term follow-up may be performed to assess postoperative recovery and symptom resolution [22].

In summary, surgical resection with negative margins is the definitive treatment for gastric schwannoma. Laparoscopic wedge resection is the preferred approach in most cases, offering effective tumour removal with minimal morbidity and excellent long-term outcomes. Recognition of the benign nature of this tumour is essential to avoid unnecessary radical surgery and adjuvant therapy in contemporary surgical oncology practice [6, 25, 27].

Endoscopic management and minimally invasive alternatives

With advances in therapeutic endoscopy, endoscopic resection has emerged as a minimally invasive alternative to surgery for selected gastric subepithelial tumours, including gastric schwannoma. Endoscopic approaches aim to achieve complete tumour removal while avoiding surgical morbidity and preserving gastric anatomy, although careful patient selection is essential to ensure safety and efficacy [18, 19, 24].

Endoscopic techniques reported for gastric schwannoma include endoscopic submucosal dissection (ESD), endoscopic submucosal excavation (ESE), and endoscopic full-thickness resection (EFTR). These modalities are primarily applied to small tumours with intraluminal growth patterns arising from the muscularis propria or submucosa [18–20]. Several retrospective series have demonstrated high rates of complete (R0) resection using these techniques, with low rates of procedure-related complications when performed by experienced endoscopists [18, 24, 27].

Among these approaches, ESD and ESE are generally reserved for lesions measuring less than 2 cm with favourable intraluminal orientation and no evidence of extramural extension on imaging [18, 19]. EFTR allows resection of deeper lesions originating from the muscularis propria and has expanded the indications for endoscopic treatment; however, it is technically demanding and associated with a higher risk of perforation and postoperative complications [20, 24]. Closure of the gastric wall defect using endoscopic suturing or over-the-scope clipping is mandatory to prevent peritonitis and delayed leakage [20].

Comparative studies evaluating endoscopic resection versus surgical resection for gastric schwannoma suggest that endoscopic approaches may offer shorter hospital stay, faster recovery, and lower overall cost in carefully selected patients [25, 26]. However, surgical resection remains superior in terms of ensuring complete excision for larger tumours, lesions with extraluminal growth, or those located in anatomically complex regions such as the gastroesophageal junction or pylorus [25]. Importantly, incomplete resection following endoscopic treatment has been reported in 5–15% of cases, particularly for lesions originating from the muscularis propria or those demonstrating extraluminal extension. Residual tumours can typically be managed with repeat endoscopic therapy or surgical resection [25].

From an oncologic perspective, the benign nature of gastric schwannoma supports the use of minimally invasive techniques in selected cases. Nevertheless, preoperative diagnostic uncertainty remains a significant limitation. As gastric schwannoma is often indistinguishable from gastrointestinal stromal tumour preoperatively, endoscopic resection may inadvertently be applied to lesions with malignant potential, which could compromise oncologic principles if tumour rupture occurs [12, 16]. This concern underscores the need for cautious application of endoscopic techniques when a definitive preoperative diagnosis cannot be established.

In addition, endoscopic full-thickness resection and ESG-like suturing techniques involve full-thickness gastric wall manipulation, which may result in fibrosis and adhesion formation. These changes can complicate subsequent surgical intervention if conversion to operative resection becomes necessary, an important consideration in long-term management planning [24, 27].

Overall, endoscopic management represents a valuable minimally invasive option for carefully selected patients with small, intraluminal gastric schwannomas in centres with appropriate expertise. However, laparoscopic surgical resection remains the standard of care for larger tumours, extraluminal growth patterns, or cases with diagnostic uncertainty. A multidisciplinary approach integrating endoscopic, radiologic, and surgical expertise is essential to optimise outcomes and maintain oncologic safety [22, 25, 27].

Outcomes and prognosis

Gastric schwannoma is associated with an excellent prognosis, reflecting its benign biological behaviour and low malignant potential. Across published series, complete resection, whether achieved surgically or endoscopically, has been associated with near-zero tumour-related mortality and extremely low recurrence rates [6, 7, 25, 26]. These favourable outcomes contrast sharply with those of gastrointestinal stromal tumours (GISTs), reinforcing the importance of accurate diagnosis and appropriate management.

Long-term follow-up data from clinicopathological studies consistently demonstrate durable disease control following R0 resection. Even in tumours exceeding 5 cm in size, recurrence after complete excision is exceedingly rare [6, 25, 26]. Several large retrospective series have reported no local recurrence or distant metastasis over follow-up periods extending beyond five years, supporting the view that tumour size alone does not predict adverse outcome in gastric schwannoma [1, 6, 25].

Reports of malignant gastric schwannoma are exceptional and largely limited to isolated case reports or small series describing atypical histological features, such as marked nuclear pleomorphism, high mitotic activity, or elevated Ki-67 proliferative indices [22, 25]. In the absence of these features, the risk of aggressive behaviour remains negligible. Consequently, gastric schwannoma should be regarded as a benign entity once definitive histopathological confirmation has been established.

Given the excellent prognosis, there is no role for adjuvant chemotherapy, radiotherapy, or targeted therapy following complete resection of gastric schwannoma [6, 7, 25]. This distinguishes it fundamentally from GIST, for which adjuvant tyrosine kinase inhibitors may be indicated depending on risk stratification [12]. Avoidance of unnecessary adjuvant treatment is therefore a key benefit of correct diagnosis.

The optimal follow-up strategy for patients with gastric schwannoma has not been clearly defined due to the rarity of recurrence. After complete surgical resection and confirmation of benign pathology, most authors recommend clinical follow-up with endoscopy or cross-sectional imaging at 6–12 months to confirm absence of recurrence [22, 26]. In the absence of concerning features, routine long-term oncologic surveillance is generally unnecessary [6, 25].

In patients treated with endoscopic resection, closer short-term follow-up may be warranted to assess for residual disease or local recurrence, particularly in cases where resection margins are uncertain [25]. However, even in these settings, long-term outcomes remain favourable when complete excision is achieved.

Overall, the prognosis of gastric schwannoma is excellent following appropriate treatment. Recognition of its benign nature allows for tailored surgical or endoscopic management, avoidance of overtreatment, and rational follow-up strategies, all of which are central to contemporary surgical oncology practice [6, 7, 25].

Future directions and proposed diagnostic–management algorithm

Despite advances in imaging, endoscopy, and minimally invasive surgery, the preoperative diagnosis of gastric schwannoma remains challenging. Future progress will likely depend on improved integration of multimodal diagnostics, refinement of minimally invasive techniques, and clearer consensus on management pathways tailored to tumour biology rather than presumptive diagnoses [27, 31, 36].

Emerging imaging approaches, including CT radiomics and quantitative texture analysis, show promise in improving preoperative discrimination between gastric schwannoma and gastrointestinal stromal tumour (GIST) by capturing subtle differences in enhancement patterns and intratumoral heterogeneity that are not readily appreciable on conventional imaging [36, 37]. While preliminary studies suggest improved diagnostic performance, these tools require external validation and standardisation before routine clinical adoption. Similarly, advances in endoscopic ultrasound (EUS) technology and needle design may enhance tissue acquisition, potentially improving diagnostic yield while minimising procedural risk in selected cases [32].

From a therapeutic standpoint, continued evolution of endoscopic full-thickness resection (EFTR) and defect-closure techniques may expand the role of endoscopic management for carefully selected intraluminal tumours [20, 24]. However, given the persistent diagnostic uncertainty that often surrounds gastric subepithelial lesions, surgical resection remains the most reliable approach for achieving both definitive diagnosis and cure, particularly for larger tumours or those with extraluminal growth patterns [25, 27]. Long-term data comparing endoscopic and surgical outcomes remain limited, underscoring the need for multicentre registries and prospective studies focused specifically on gastric schwannoma.

Based on current evidence, a pragmatic diagnostic–management algorithm can be proposed. Patients with gastric subepithelial tumours identified on endoscopy should undergo contrast-enhanced CT to assess tumour size, growth pattern, and relationship to adjacent structures [8, 31]. EUS may be utilised to confirm the layer of origin and evaluate echotexture, particularly for smaller lesions [10, 11]. When imaging suggests a benign intraluminal lesion measuring less than 2 cm and endoscopic expertise is available, endoscopic resection may be considered after multidisciplinary discussion [18, 19, 25]. In contrast, tumours larger than 2 cm, those demonstrating extraluminal growth, or lesions located near anatomically complex regions such as the gastroesophageal junction or pylorus are best managed with laparoscopic surgical resection to ensure complete excision and minimise functional compromise [22, 25, 26].

Routine preoperative biopsy is not recommended for resectable lesions when GIST cannot be confidently excluded, due to the limited diagnostic yield and potential oncologic risks associated with tumour rupture [12, 16]. Definitive diagnosis should instead rely on postoperative histopathological and immunohistochemical evaluation, with confirmation of S-100 and SOX10 positivity and absence of CD117 and DOG-1 expression [13–15, 33]. Following complete resection and confirmation of benign gastric schwannoma, no adjuvant therapy is required, and follow-up can be individualised with limited surveillance [6, 25].

Future advances in imaging analytics and endoscopic technology may improve preoperative diagnostic accuracy and expand minimally invasive treatment options for gastric schwannoma. Until such tools are validated, management should prioritise complete resection with minimal morbidity through a multidisciplinary, patient-centred approach. Adoption of a structured diagnostic–management algorithm may help standardise care, reduce overtreatment, and optimise outcomes in contemporary surgical oncology practice [27, 31, 37].

Recent advances in digital health technologies, including the integration of Internet of Things (IoT) systems within medical practice, have begun to influence oncologic diagnostics and patient monitoring [39]. IoT-enabled platforms allow real-time integration of imaging, endoscopic data, and clinical parameters, potentially facilitating earlier detection and improved surveillance of gastrointestinal tumours. In the context of gastric subepithelial lesions, IoT-supported diagnostic networks and artificial intelligence-assisted imaging analysis may enhance the accuracy of tumour characterisation and support multidisciplinary decision-making.

Conclusion

Gastric schwannoma is a rare benign gastric mesenchymal tumour that continues to pose diagnostic and therapeutic challenges due to its close clinical and radiologic resemblance to gastrointestinal stromal tumour. Despite advances in imaging and endoscopic evaluation, reliable preoperative differentiation remains difficult, and definitive diagnosis is most often established only after histopathological and immunohistochemical analysis. Recognition of its characteristic pathological features, including spindle cell morphology, peripheral lymphoid cuffing, and strong S-100 and SOX10 expression with absence of CD117 and DOG-1, is critical for accurate diagnosis and appropriate management.

From a surgical oncology perspective, complete resection with negative margins is curative and associated with excellent long-term outcomes. Laparoscopic wedge resection remains the preferred treatment for most patients, offering definitive diagnosis and durable disease control with minimal morbidity. Endoscopic resection techniques may be considered in carefully selected cases, particularly for small intraluminal tumours in experienced centres, but should be applied cautiously given persistent diagnostic uncertainty and potential implications for subsequent surgical intervention.

Accurate distinction between gastric schwannoma and other gastric mesenchymal tumours is essential to avoid unnecessary radical surgery, adjuvant therapy, and prolonged surveillance. A multidisciplinary approach integrating radiologic, endoscopic, surgical, and pathological expertise remains central to optimal patient care. Future advances in imaging analytics and endoscopic technologies may further refine preoperative assessment, but until such tools are validated, management strategies should prioritise complete resection and functional preservation.

By consolidating current evidence on the epidemiology, diagnostic challenges, pathological features, and management of gastric schwannoma, this review aims to support informed clinical decision-making and promote tailored, oncologically sound treatment strategies in contemporary surgical oncology practice.
