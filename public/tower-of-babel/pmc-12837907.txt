Role of histone acetylation in the regulation of CD36 and ANGPTL1 in esophageal squamous cell carcinoma
Authors: Aadil Manzoor Baba, Ritis Kumar Shyanti, Mohammad Amin Hajam, Najma Nissa, Mansha Muzaffar, Tanzeela Bashir, Sauliheen Fida, Mudabir Abdullah, Rana P Singh, Nazir Ahmad Dar
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGiven the limited efficacy of available anti-angiogenic therapies in esophageal squamous cell carcinoma (ESCC), there is a need to explore new strategies to target this crucial tumour-promoting process. Emerging evidence suggests that epigenetic dysregulation, including histone deacetylation, may contribute to aberrant pro-angiogenic signalling. Therefore, we investigated whether histone deacetylase (HDAC) 3 and HDAC8-mediated hypoacetylation contribute to the silencing of anti-angiogenic genes angiopoietin-like 1 (ANGPTL1) and cluster of differentiation (CD36), thereby promoting angiogenesis in ESCC.MethodsESCC cell lines and clinical tissues were analysed for the expression of CD36, ANGPTL1, vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), HDAC3 and HDAC8 and for histone acetylation markers histone H3 lysine 9 acetylation (H3K9ac) and histone H4 lysine 16 acetylation (H4K16ac) using qRT-PCR and immunoblotting. Functional angiogenesis was assessed through in vitro human umbilical vein endothelial cell (HUVEC) tube formation, ex vivo rat aortic ring assays, and endothelial migration (wound healing/Trans well) following interventions with suberoylanilide hydroxamic acid (SAHA) treatment, HDAC3 siRNA knockdown, or CD36 overexpression.ResultsIn ESCC tissues and cell lines, expression of CD36 and ANGPTL1 was downregulated, while HDAC3 and HDAC8 were upregulated, correlating with reduced levels of H3K9ac and H4K16ac. The HDAC inhibitor SAHA partially restored histone acetylation, significantly upregulated CD36 and ANGPTL1, suppressed VEGF, and reduced angiogenesis in both in vitro and ex vivo models. Furthermore, knockdown of HDAC3 produced a significant anti-angiogenic effect, comparable to the effect of CD36 overexpression. Introduction

Esophageal cancer (EC) is a serious global health concern, ranking as the eleventh most common cancer overall and the sixth most common cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide [1]. Among the two primary histological subtypes of EC, adenocarcinoma and ESCC, the latter accounts for the majority of cases and constitutes more than 80–90% of cases in high-incidence areas [2]. The marked disparity between incidence and mortality reflects ESCC’s low prognosis, with five-year survival rates remaining below 5% in many low- and middle-income countries and ranging from 5 to 22% even in developed settings [3–6]. A critical factor underlying this poor outcome is the diagnosis at advanced stages, when curative surgery is no longer feasible and chemoradiotherapy achieves only modest benefit [7–10]. The aggressive progression of ESCC, characterised by rapid invasion and dissemination to adjacent structures, is closely associated with pathological angiogenesis [11, 12].

Angiogenesis in ESCC results from a tightly controlled interplay between pro-angiogenic and anti-angiogenic factors that regulate endothelial cell proliferation and new vessel formation [13]. However, during tumour development, this balance is often shifted by upregulation of angiogenic stimulators such as VEGF and downregulation of endogenous inhibitors, including thrombospondin-1. Previous studies have demonstrated that decreased CD36 expression disrupts thrombospondin-1–mediated inhibition of angiogenesis in various malignancies, thereby promoting neovascularisation [14]. Similarly, reduced ANGPTL1 levels have been shown to facilitate tumour angiogenesis, invasion, and metastasis in several cancers and are associated with poorer clinical outcomes [15, 16].

Emerging evidence indicates that epigenetic mechanisms, particularly histone modifications, are important to the regulation of angiogenic gene expression [17, 18]. HDACs mediate transcriptional repression by removing acetyl groups from histone tails, leading to chromatin condensation and silencing of anti-angiogenic genes [19, 20]. Class I HDACs have been directly implicated in tumour angiogenesis, in part through their ability to repress thrombospondin-1 and influence hypoxia-related pathways that promote VEGF expression [21, 22]. Notably, HDAC3 and, to some extent, HDAC8, have been reported to contribute to aberrant angiogenesis and tumour progression in certain malignancies [18, 23]. In this context, the pan-HDAC inhibitor SAHA has attracted considerable interest as a therapeutic strategy capable of restoring histone acetylation and reactivating silenced tumour-suppressive pathways [22, 23]. Studies have further demonstrated that HDAC inhibition can suppress pro-angiogenic factors such as VEGF, platelet-derived growth factor (PDGF), and angiopoietins, while upregulating anti-angiogenic mediators including thrombospondins and T-cell immunoglobulin and mucin domain-containing proteins (TIMPs) [21, 24]. These findings support the therapeutic potential of targeting HDACs to disrupt angiogenic signalling cascades in cancers.

Although epigenetic dysregulation of angiogenesis has been studied in other cancers, the histone modifications associated with CD36 and ANGPTL1 repression in ESCC remain largely unexplored. Notably, downregulation of these genes in other malignancies permits neovascularisation and tumour progression [14, 15]. Our prior transcriptomic analysis demonstrated downregulation of CD36 and ANGPTL1 in ESCC tissues compared with adjacent normal epithelium, accompanied by upregulation of HDAC3 and HDAC8. Based on these findings, we hypothesise that epigenetic repression mediated by these HDACs may underlie the suppression of anti-angiogenic genes in ESCC and contribute to its aggressive vascular phenotype. Elucidating these possible relationships may help in identifying potential treatment targets in ESCC.

Materials and methods

Microarray profiling

Paired ESCC and adjacent normal tissues (n = 4 pairs) were processed by Innovative Life Discoveries (Ref: BCH/MA/ILD/14). Total RNA was extracted using the RNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen), hybridised to Affymetrix Human PrimeView Arrays (49,395 probes) for 16 h at 45 °C, and scanned. Raw CEL files were normalised via the RMA algorithm, which includes background correction, quantile normalisation, geometric mean summarisation, and median centring (baseline transformation). Low-intensity probes (< 10th percentile) were excluded. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified using a moderated t-test with dual thresholds: (i) Primary screening, |fold change (FC)| ≥ 2.0 and p ≤ 0.05, and (ii) top DEG filter, |log₂FC| ≥ 6.0.

Cell Lines, culture conditions and reagents

HUVECs and endothelial culture medium (HiEndoXL) were obtained from HiMedia, while RPMI-1640 and DMEM were purchased from Gibco. Fetal bovine serum (FBS), penicillin-streptomycin, bovine serum albumin (BSA), trypsin-EDTA, and protease/phosphatase inhibitor cocktails were sourced from Hyclone. Western blotting reagents, including skimmed milk and protein ladder, were acquired from HiMedia and Amerco. Primary antibodies used in this study included HDAC3 (Cell Signaling Technology, #85057; 1:1000 dilution), KAT6A/PCAF (Cell Signaling Technology, #3378; 1:1000), CD36 (Abcam, #14347; 1:500), HDAC8 (Sigma-Aldrich, H9291), H3K9ac (Abclonal, #A7253; 1:1000), H4K16ac (Abclonal, #A2357; 1:1000), and ANGPTL1 (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, sc-365963; 1:500). Fluorescently labelled secondary antibodies were procured from LI-COR Sigma.

Matrigel and µ-Slide angiogenesis plates (ibidi) were purchased from Corning, while the HDAC inhibitor SAHA was obtained from Sigma-Aldrich (SML0061). ESCC cell lines (KYSE30, KYSE70, and KYSE410; Sigma-Aldrich Cat. Nos. 94072011, 94072012, and 94072023) and non-cancerous HEKN cells (NCC, Pune, India) were authenticated by short tandem repeat (STR) profiling and routinely screened for mycoplasma contamination using PCR-based kits.

Cell viability and cytotoxicity assay

KYSE30 and KYSE70 were seeded at a density of 6,000 cells per well in 96-well plates and allowed to attach overnight at 37 °C. The following day, cells were treated for 48 h with different doses of SAHA (1–5 µM; Sigma-Aldrich SML0061), with 0.1% DMSO serving as vehicle control. Cell viability was assessed using the MTT assay (HiMedia) according to the protocol used in the ATCC Cell Viability Assay Kit (Cat. No. 30–1010 K). MTT dye was prepared as a 5 mg/mL solution in PBS, added to each well, and the plates were incubated for 2 h at 37 °C until formazan crystals formed. The crystals were subsequently dissolved in DMSO and quantified by measuring absorbance at λ max 570 nm using a microplate reader.

IC50 values for SAHA were calculated after 48 h of treatment using nonlinear regression analysis in GraphPad Prism (v8.0). The working concentration used in all subsequent experiments was based on the determined IC50 values. All experiments were performed in three independent biological replicates, each with technical triplicates.

Transient transfections

KYSE30 and KYSE70 ESCC cells were maintained in RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin-streptomycin at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2. For siRNA transfection, cells at ~ 70% confluency were transfected with siRNA (final concentration 50 nM) using Lipofectamine 2000 (Invitrogen, USA) in Opti-MEM (Gibco), following the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, siRNA and Lipofectamine were diluted separately in Opti-MEM, incubated for 5 min, then combined and incubated for 20 min at room temperature before addition to cells in 1.5 mL fresh RPMI-1640 medium. For CD36 overexpression, cells were transfected with the pcDNA3.1-CD36 expression plasmid (Addgene). After 12–16 h, the medium was replaced with fresh complete medium. Cells were harvested at 48 and 72 h post-transfection, lysed in NP-40 buffer, and protein concentrations quantified by Bradford assay. Knockdown efficiency was confirmed by Western blotting with HDAC3-specific antibodies, and CD36 overexpression was verified by Western blotting for CD36.

Real-time quantitative polymerase chain reaction

Total RNA was isolated from KYSE30 and KYSE70 cells using TRIzol reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific), followed by cDNA synthesis with a High-Capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Gene expression was quantified using Power Up SYBR Green Master Mix (Applied Biosystems) on a QuantStudio real-time PCR system. Reactions were performed in triplicate with the following cycling conditions: 50 °C for 2 min, 95 °C for 10 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 15 s and 60 °C for 1 min. Melt curve analysis confirmed amplification specificity.

The various primer pairs used are listed below (5′→3′):

CD36: Fw-GCCAGTTGGAGACCTGCTTA, Rev-CAGCGTCCTGGGTTACATTT.

ANGPTL1: Fw-GATACCCTCGTGCCACAGAT, Rev-CATCTATCTCCCGCTTCTGC.

VEGF: Fw-AGGGCAGAATCATCACGAAGT, Rev-AGGGTCTCGATTGGATGGCA.

GAPDH (endogenous control): Fw - ATCCCATCACCATCTTCCAGGA, Rev-CAAATGAG CCCCAGCCTTCT.

Relative quantification was calculated using the 2 − ΔΔCt method, with GAPDH normalisation and fold changes expressed relative to control groups.

Western blotting

Cultured cells were either treated with the drug (dose- and time-dependent) or transfected with plasmid constructs. Forty-eight hours post-treatment/transfection, cells were washed with ice-cold PBS and lysed in NP-40 lysis buffer (150 mM NaCl, 1% NP-40, 50 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8.0) supplemented with protease inhibitor cocktail (PIC; Roche). Lysates were centrifuged at 15,000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C, and supernatants were collected for protein quantification using the Bradford assay (Bio-Rad), with absorbance measured at 595 nm on a microplate reader (BioTek). Equal protein amounts (20–50 µg) were resolved by SDS-PAGE under reducing conditions (5% β-mercaptoethanol) and transferred to nitrocellulose membranes (0.45 μm; Bio-Rad) using standard wet transfer. Membranes were blocked with 5% skimmed milk in TBST (Tris-buffered saline with 0.1% Tween-20) for 1 h at room temperature, then incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies (diluted in blocking buffer) under gentle agitation. After three 10-minute TBST washes, membranes were probed with species-matched IRDye-conjugated secondary antibodies (LI-COR Biosciences; 1:10,000) for 2 h at room temperature. Protein bands were visualised using an Odyssey CLx imaging system (LI-COR), and densitometric analysis was performed with Image Studio software.

Angiogenesis assays

To investigate SAHA’s anti-angiogenic attributes, 5,000 HUVECs were seeded per well on growth factor-reduced Matrigel-coated µ-slide angiogenesis plates and allowed to develop capillary-like networks. Two experimental protocols were used: (1) simultaneous treatment, where HUVECs were seeded with VEGF (50ng/ml) and SAHA (1.5–3 µM) and tube formation was monitored at 12 h intervals using phase-contrast microscopy (Nikon Eclipse Ti), and (2) post-seeding treatment, where HUVECs were allowed to attach for 6 h before SAHA administration, with tube networks quantified after 6–12 h of incubation. For conditioned media (CM) experiments, KYSE30 cells were either transfected with HDAC3-specific siRNA (si HDAC3) or CD36 plasmid; 48 h after CM were collected, filtered (0.22 μm), and mixed 1:1 with fresh Endo XL medium before application to HUVECs. Tube formation was assessed by counting the number of connected rudimentary tubes in three random fields per well using ImageJ software (v1.53), with experiments performed in biological triplicate [25, 26].

Rat aortic ring assay

Female Wistar rats (6–8 weeks old, 200–300 g; Hylasco Biotechnology Pvt. Ltd., Hyderabad) were anaesthetised via intraperitoneal injection of sodium pentobarbital (60 mg/kg body weight) until surgical anaesthesia was confirmed by absence of paw pinch reflex and decreased respiratory rate. Thoracic aortae were immediately removed under sterile conditions and submerged in ice-cold PBS containing 1% penicillin/streptomycin after humane euthanasia by exsanguination in compliance with CPCSEA guidelines. Aortae were cross-sectioned into 1 mm rings using micro-dissection scissors following the meticulous excision of perivascular adipose tissue with sterile surgical scalpels. Rings were embedded in growth factor-reduced Matrigel (Corning) within 24-well plates, allowed to polymerise for 15 min at 37 °C, and cultured in HiEndoXL medium (HiMedia) supplemented with 2% heat-inactivated FBS under standard conditions (37 °C, 5% CO₂). Experimental groups received either SAHA (1.5–3.5µM) or vehicle control Media only (0.1% DMSO), with medium changes every 48 h. Microvessel sprouting was quantified after 6 days using phase-contrast microscopy (Nikon Eclipse Ti), with angiogenic growth measured as total sprout length per ring using ImageJ software (v1.53) from three independent experiments [25].

Migration assays (Wound healing and trans well)

HUVECs were seeded on 6-well plates and grown in complete HiEndoXL medium to 90% confluence. After overnight incubation, monolayers were scratched with a sterile 10 µL pipette tip to create uniform wounds, and then replaced with CM from KYSE30 cells (either HDAC3 siRNA-transfected or control). To prepare CM, the supernatant was collected 48 h after transfection, filtered through 0.22 μm membranes, and mixed 1:1 with fresh HiEndoXL media. Wound closure was observed at 0, 12, and 24 h intervals using phase-contrast microscopy (Nikon Eclipse Ti), and migration distances were calculated using ImageJ software.

For trans well migration assays, 6 × 10⁵ HUVECs in serum-free medium were seeded into the upper chambers of 8 μm pore size Corning Trans well inserts pre-coated with 500 µL HUVEC growth medium. The lower chambers contained 10% FBS as a chemoattractant. After 24 h incubation with KYSE30-derived CM (prepared as above), non-migrated cells on the upper membrane surface were removed by cotton swab abrasion. Migrated cells on the lower surface were fixed with methanol: acetic acid (3:1 v/v) for 20 min, stained with 0.5% crystal violet for 30 min, and quantified by counting five random fields per insert at 100× magnification. All experiments included technical triplicates and were repeated in three biological replicates.

Statistical analysis

The statistical analysis was done using GraphPad Prism Software (version 8) and ImageJ. All experiments were performed in biological triplicate, and data are presented as mean ± SEM. The statistical differences between groups were assessed using unpaired Student’s t-tests or one-way ANOVA with Tukey’s post-hoc test. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

ESCC exhibits dysregulation of HDACs, angiogenic modulators, and histone acetylation

Our initial molecular characterisation revealed an epigenetic imbalance in ESCC models. Quantitative analysis revealed significantly reduced expression of CD36 and ANGPTL1 in KYSE cell lines compared to HEKN controls (p < 0.001, Fig. 1A), which correlated with elevated HDAC3 and HDAC8 expression. In clinical samples, similar findings were observed, compared to matched controls, CD36, ANGPTL1 and KAT6A were downregulated (p < 0.01), whereas VEGF (p < 0.001) and HDACs were upregulated in ESCC tissues (Fig. 1B). These expression patterns were consistent with the transcriptomic profiling, particularly with respect to HDACs, the acetyl transferase KAT6A, and antiangiogenic genes (Fig. 1C, Supplementary table S1).

B Paired Western blot analysis of protein expression in tumour and adjacent normal tissues from ESCC patients. Data in bar graphs represent mean ± SEM (n = 3 independent experiments). Statistical significance was determined using a two-tailed Student’s t-test (*p < 0.05, **p < 0.001, ***p < 0.0001). SAHA partially restored histone acetylation, leading to increased expression of CD36 and ANGPTL1

Pharmacological intervention with the HDAC inhibitor SAHA resulted in dose- and time-dependent epigenetic remodelling. Cell viability assays established optimal inhibitory concentrations IC50 = 3.48 ± 0.40 µM (KYSE30); 3.21 ± 0.40 µM (KYSE70), 48 h. (Fig. 2A). Treatment at the IC₅₀ for 48 h led to a transcriptional increase of the antiangiogenic genes CD36 and ANGPTL1 (p < 0.05), with concurrent downregulation of VEGF expression (p < 0.01) (Fig. 2B). Furthermore, at both IC₅₀ and IC₅₀/2 treatments, H3K9ac and H4K16ac levels increased significantly. This epigenetic effect was consistent across both KYSE30 (Fig. 2C) and KYSE70 (Fig. 2D) cell lines and correlated with a corresponding upregulation of CD36 and ANGPTL1 proteins. Time-course analysis revealed that treatment with IC₅₀/2 of SAHA induced a progressive accumulation of H3K9ac and H4K16ac in both KYSE30 and KYSE70 cells, with peak acetylation observed at 48 h (Figs. 2E-H). This epigenetic impact was temporally correlated with a concomitant increase in CD36 and ANGPTL1protein, which also peaked at 48 h (Figs. 2G-H). The synchronous nature of histone mark accumulation and target gene reactivation suggests a direct link between SAHA-induced acetylation and the transcriptional upregulation of these genes.

HDAC Inhibition and targeted epigenetic modulation disrupt angiogenic function

The functional impact of HDAC inhibition on angiogenesis was evaluated using complementary models. In HUVEC tube formation assays, SAHA treatment (1.5–3 µM) was associated with a visible suppression of network formation compared to VEGF-stimulated controls. This suggested an anti-angiogenic effect, which was characterised by an apparent reduction in network complexity, total tube length, and branch points after 12 h of treatment (Fig. 3A). This inhibitory activity was observed even in the presence of exogenous VEGF and at SAHA concentrations as low as 1.5 µM (Fig. 3B). The in vitro findings were corroborated in an ex vivo rat aortic ring assay, where treatment with SAHA or CM from KYSE cells either overexpressing CD36 or silenced for HDAC3 markedly suppressed microvessel sprouting as compared to VEGF stimulated controls (Fig. 3C).

To better define the role of specific HDAC3-mediated pathways in this process, we employed engineered cell models. Silencing of HDAC3 led to increased global H3K9ac and H4K16ac levels in both KYSE30 and KYSE70 cells. In KYSE30, this was accompanied by elevated expression of CD36 and ANGPTL1 proteins, whereas in KYSE70 cells, only CD36 expression was assessed and found to be increased (Figs. 4A, B). Similarly, direct overexpression of CD36 in KYSE30 cells led to a dose-dependent increase in its own expression and a concurrent decrease in VEGF expression (Fig. 4C).

To functionally test the consequence of these molecular changes, we analysed the angiogenic potential of CM from these genetically modified cells. CM from both CD36-overexpressing and HDAC3-silenced KYSE30 cells was associated with significantly reduced HUVEC tube formation, as quantified by a reduction in total tube length and branch points compared to CM from control cells (Figs. 4D, E).

Collectively, these data are consistent with a model in which HDAC3-mediated silencing of CD36 promotes a pro-angiogenic state. Pharmacological inhibition of HDACs with SAHA or direct targeting of this axis through HDAC3 knockdown or CD36 overexpression is sufficient to reverse this state and potently inhibit angiogenesis.

Epigenetic modulation impairs endothelial and tumour cell migration

Migration studies provided further evidence of vascular disruption mediated by the HDAC3/CD36/ANGPTL1 axis. In trans well assays, CM from CD36-overexpressing KYSE30 cells (CD36-CM) significantly reduced HUVEC migration compared to controls (p < 0.01, Fig. 5A). A similar anti-migratory effect was observed with media from HDAC3-silenced cells (siHDAC3-CM) (p < 0.05, Fig. 5B). Complementary wound healing experiments demonstrated significantly slower closure in HDAC3-silenced KYSE30 monolayers (p < 0.01, Fig. 5C), with persistent inhibition maintained throughout the 24 h observation period. The anti-migratory phenotype was confirmed in HUVECs, where CD36-CM and siHDAC3-CM also impaired wound closure (Fig. 5D). These consistent findings across multiple migration paradigms and cell types support the role of tumour cells. HDAC3 seems to regulate pro-angiogenic secretome factors, thereby impacting the initiation and progression of angiogenic processes in ESCC.

Discussion

Angiogenesis is a critical driver of ESCC progression and therapeutic resistance, yet the epigenetic pathways governing this process remain incompletely defined. In this study, we identified a regulatory axis in which upregulation of HDAC3—and potentially HDAC8—may contribute to angiogenesis. In this study, we functionally validated HDAC3, whereas the role of HDAC8 remains to be determined.

Building on our initial profiling, we validated this inverse relationship in clinical samples and cell lines (Figs. 1A, B). We further demonstrate that pharmacological inhibition with SAHA, or specific genetic knockdown of HDAC3, restores acetylation (Figs. 2 and 4A-B), reactivates these silenced genes, suppresses VEGF (Figs. 2B and 4C), and potently inhibits angiogenic phenotypes in vitro and ex vivo (Figs. 3, 4 and 5). This work extends previous reports of global HDAC overexpression in ESCC [27, 28] by defining the specific histone marks (H3K9ac/H4K16ac) and target genes (CD36/ANGPTL1) whose dysregulation underpins aberrant angiogenesis.

Functionally, the repression of CD36 and ANGPTL1 creates a permissive microenvironment for angiogenesis by disrupting thrombospondin-1–mediated signalling [14] and allowing unchecked VEGF pathway activation [12, 13]. These observations align with prior studies implicating HDAC-mediated repression of tumour suppressor genes in cancer progression [29, 30]. However, while HDAC overexpression has been associated with epithelial-mesenchymal transition and metastasis in colorectal and esophageal cancers [29, 31–35], our study is, to our best knowledge, the first to investigate H3K9/H4K16 hypoacetylation association with angiogenic switching in ESCC. These results, along with our previous work [36], suggest that HDAC-mediated repression of anti-angiogenic factors can also contribute to tumour progression in head and neck cancer.

Therapeutically, the pan-HDAC inhibitor SAHA partially reversed this epigenetic repression, was associated with reactivation of gene expression, and attenuated angiogenesis in vitro and ex vivo models. Notably, the HDAC3 knockdown was sufficient to elicit these effects (Figs. 4 and 5), suggesting a possible role for HDAC3 in this pathway and suggesting that isoform-selective HDAC3 inhibitors merit further exploration to determine whether they could achieve efficacy with fewer off-target effects [29]. HDAC3’s known interactions with HIF-1α [37] and regulators like FOXO1 [38] provide a plausible broader context for its role in fine-tuning the pro-angiogenic hypoxic response.

Limitations and future directions

While our study provides important insights, it also has certain limitations. First, we employed a pan-HDAC inhibitor (SAHA), which does not allow us to distinguish the individual contributions of HDAC3 and HDAC8; future work using isoform-selective inhibitors is warranted. Second, while we observed global changes in histone acetylation, ChIP-qPCR or ChIP-seq will be required to confirm promoter-specific histone modifications at the CD36 and ANGPTL1 loci. Third, although our in vitro and ex vivo data are robust, in vivo validation in ESCC models is needed to fully capture the complexity of the tumour microenvironment. Finally, larger patient cohorts will be necessary to determine whether HDAC3/8 expression and H3K9/H4K16 acetylation correlate with clinical outcomes, thereby strengthening the translational significance of this pathway.

Conclusion

Our findings support a role for HDAC3 in promoting angiogenesis via histone hypoacetylation-mediated silencing of CD36 and ANGPTL1. While HDAC8 was also upregulated, its functional role in this pathway remains to be clarified. Pharmacological inhibition with the pan-HDAC inhibitor SAHA partially restored acetylation, reactivated these genes, and reduced angiogenic function in vitro and ex vivo. These results highlight HDAC3 as a potential regulator of tumour angiogenesis in ESCC and suggest that isoform-selective HDAC inhibitors, particularly those targeting HDAC3, warrant further preclinical investigation.
