The effects of citrulline on horse milk components and fecal metabolism were analyzed based on LC-MS and 16s amplicon
Authors: Yumei Ma, Pengshun Liu, Xiaobin Li, Qingsong Hu, Jianwei Lin, Fan Yang, Kailun Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe lactation period in mares is critical, as it significantly influences foal health and subsequent reproductive outcomes. Developing a precise nutritional strategy for lactating mares is a primary research focus. L-citrulline, a bioactivenon-protein amino acid and precursor to arginine, has been shown to enhance mammary blood flow and reduce oxidative stress, yielding promising results in livestock lactation studies. However, research on its effects in Equus species remains limited. There is a notable gap in data regarding its impact on lactation performance blood biochemistry, and its role in the fecal microbiota and metabolism of mares. This study, therefore, seeks to explore the effects of L-citrulline supplementation on lactation performance, foal growth, fecal microbiota, and metabolomic profiles in grazing lactating mares, aiming to establish a foundation for dietary optimization and he advancement of the horse industry.ResultsIn this study, 32 lactating mares were selected and assigned to a control group (no amino acid supplementation) and experimental groups I, II, and III (15 g/d, 30 g/d, and 45 g/d of L-citrulline respectively) under identical grazing conditions, with 8 mares per group. The results demonstrated a significant increase in milk production for mares in group II (30 g/d) (P < 0.01). Additionally, milk fat percentage, foal weight and body size were higher in all experimental groups compared to the control group. Blood levels of lactate dehydrogenase and creatine kinase were extremely significantly lower in the experimental groups than in the control group. To further explore the effects of L-citrulline on fecal microbiota and metabolites,16 S rDNA sequencing and LC-MS/MS analysis were performed on the control group and the experimental group receiving 30 g/d (which showed the most pronounced lactation response). The microbiota level, analysis revealed Firmicutes as the dominant phylum, with significant differences in the species F082 and the genus Ruminococcaceae_bacterium_AE2021. Tax4Fun functional annotation showed the highest gene function abundance in digestive metabolism, genetic information processing and environmental information processing pathways. LC-MS/MS analysis identified differential metabolites primarily associated with biological systems and metabolism. In conclusion, L-citrulline supplementation in grazing lactating mares increased milk production, improved milk fat quality, and enhanced foal weight and body size.ConclusionL-citrulline supplementation enhances milk production in mares, improves milk fat, quality, reduces lactate dehydrogenase and creatine kinase levels in the blood, and promote the growth and development of lactating foals.Keywords: Lactating mare, L-citrulline, Lactation performance, Microbiota, Metabolism

Introduction

The formation of milk in lactating livestock is influenced by multiple factors, including genetic background, nutritional status, and environmental conditions [1]. Within a consistent genetic framework, nutrition is a key determinant of both milk yield and quality. Key nutrients involved in regulating milk synthesis include energy, protein (including amino acids), lipids (including fatty acids), vitamins, and minerals [2, 3]. Amino acids, in particular, play a critical role in milk production. As substrates, they contribute to the synthesis of milk proteins, while also servingas regulatory factors, in milk anabolism [4]. Lysine and threonine essential amino acids for Equus species, are identified as the primary andsecondary limiting amino acids in grazing animals. Jing et al. [5] demonstrated that supplementing lysine and threonine during lactation in Ilima mares significantly improved milk yield, milk fat percentage, and milk protein content. Targeted metabolomic analysis of plasma amino acids and free amino acids in milk revealed that lysine and threonine enhance nitrogen metabolism by increasing nitrogen cycling, thus improving milk yield and protein content. Additionally, lysine may promote mammary epithelial cell proliferation and milk protein synthesis through modulation of the STAT signaling pathway [6]. Nonessential amino acids also play a pivotal role as nitrogen sources, significantly contributing to protein metabolism and milk synthesis. Arginine supplementation has been shown to increase daily milk yield in dairy cows by 2.2 kg compared to control and alanine groups, along with enhanced milk fat content. Furthermore, the expression of genes related to milk fat synthesis in the mammary gland was upregulated, resulting in significant improvements in both milk fat content and overall milk yield [7]. Thus, mammalian milk synthesis requires not only substrate components such as amino acids, fatty acids, and glucose, but also non-substrate substances that regulate the overall synthesis process.

L-citrulline, a non-essential amino acid, derives its name from its initial extraction from watermelon juice. It is metabolized in the body and functions in concert with arginine and nitric oxide [8]. L-citrulline has diverse regulatory effects, including modulation of intestinal function, antioxidant and anti-inflammatory activities, and enhancement of lactation performance by increasing milk yield and improving milk composition [9-11]. However, it does not directly regulate milk synthesis; rather, it promotes mammary gland health through indirect mechanisms. Under grazing conditions, the nutritional intake of horses is limited by the quality of available. forage, which fluctuates with seasonal changes, ultimately influencing nutrient absorption and consequently the milk yield of lactating mares [12, 13]. In this study, Ili horses were selected as the experimental subjects. Under uniform feeding conditions, the effects of L-citrulline supplementation on lactation performance, production outcomes, fecal microbiota diversity, and metabolomics were analyzed. The objective was to explore differences in fecal microbiota and metabolites among lactating mares and to identify pathways through which L-citrulline may participate in regulating milk fat synthesis. This research aims to provide a theoretical basis and practical guidance for optimizing the nutritional management of lactating mares.

Materials and methods

Experimental design

The experiment was conducted in Coudre Grassland, Zhaosu County, Ili Kazakh Autonomous Prefecture, Xinjiang. A total of 32 healthy Yili lactating mares and their foals were selected as experimental subjects, with each mare and foal forming a dam-offspring pair. The mares, aged 7–9 years and, with 4–5 previous parities, had an average body weight of 415 ± 25.5 kg. They had foaled in May and were in the first 30 days of lactation. The foals approximately 2 months old, had an average body weight of 94.91 ± 14.3 kg, with an equal number of males and females. All mares and foals were dam-offspring pairs. The animals were randomly assigned to four groups, with eight mares per group (foals were grouped with): a control group (no amino acid supplementation), experimental group I (L-citrulline 15 g/d), experimental group II (L-citrulline 30 g/d), and experimental group III (L-citrulline 45 g/d). L-citrulline (≥ 99% purity on a dry matter basis) was purchased from Anhui Runtai Biotechnology Co., Ltd. The study included a 7-day pre-feeding period, followed by a 90-day formal experimental period.

Feeding and management

During the milking period, all mares underwent 8 h of daytime tied milking, with foals separated and not allowed to nurse. For the remaining 16 h, mares and foals were kept together, permitting the foals to nurse freely. Each day at 08:30, mares and foals were driven from the grazing pasture to the herders’ milking site. At 09:00, experimental mares received their respective L-citrulline supplements via a special feed bag. Simultaneously, mares were separated from their foals for milking. At 17:00, after the final milking session, mares and foals were reunited and returned to the pasture together. During the milking period, foals had unrestricted access to grazing and water. Measurement of growth performance of foals

Foals were weighed at 08:30 on days 0, 30, 60, and 90 of the experimental period. Body height, body length, chest circumference, and cannon circumference were measured using a measuring stick and tape measure. Total weight gain, average daily weight gain, total growth, and average daily growth were calculated based on these measurements.

Measurement of growth performance of foals

Throughout the 0–90-day experimental period, milk yield was recorded at each milking session (11:00, 13:00, 15:00, and 17:00 daily) using electronic scales.

On days 0, 30, 60, and 90, 25 mL of milk was collected at each milking session. The samples were thoroughly mixed and transferred into two 100 mL sampling tubes, which were then stored at -20 °C for subsequent analysis of milk composition.

Determination of blood biochemical indicators

Blood samples were collected from the jugular vein of mares and foals after an overnight fast on days 0, 30, 60, and 90 of the experiment, prior to morning feeding. The mare was restrained and separated from the foal, and the foal’s neck was disinfected with alcohol. A disposable blood collection needle was inserted at a 45° angle into the jugular vein, and 5 mL of blood was collected using a heparin sodium vacuum blood collection tube. The samples were then centrifuged at 3,500 rpm for 15 min. The supernatant was transferred to a centrifuge tube and stored at -20 °C for subsequent analysis.

Plasma indicators measured in this study included protein-related markers: total protein (TP), albumin (ALB), globulin (GLB), urea (UERA), and creatinine (CREA); sugar and lipid-related markers: glucose (Glu), triglycerides (TG), total cholesterol (TCH), total bilirubin (T-Bil), and direct bilirubin (D-BiL); and enzyme activity-related markers: alanine aminotransferase (ALT), lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), creatine kinase (CK), and alkaline phosphatase (ALP). All assays were performed according to with the methods provided with the respective kits, all of which were purchased from Beijing Huaying Biotechnology Research Institute. The analysis was conducted using an automated biochemical analyzer .

Determination of fecal pH and volatile fatty acids

Determination of pH

On the final day of the experiment, fresh feces were collected from each horse using disposable sterile gloves. The fecal samples were thoroughly mixed, and 10 g of feces were diluted in 10 mL of distilled water (a 1:1 ratio of feces to water) and stirred at room temperature for 3–5 min. The fecal pH was measured using a calibrated portable pH meter with an accuracy of 0.01 (FiveEasy22-Meter, Mettler-Toledo International Trading (Shanghai) Co., Ltd.).

Determination of volatile fatty acids

The volatile fatty acids (VFAs) in the fermentation medium were measured using a Shimadzu GC-2010 gas chromatograph equipped with a Stabilwax chromatographic, with 4-methylvalproic acid as the internal standard. Chromatographic conditions were as follows: the injection port temperature was set to 230 °C, the initial column oven temperature was 55 °C, and the temperature was increased at 13 °C/min to 200 °C, which was maintained for 0.5 min. The FID detector was set to 240 °C.

Sample processing and determination: Ten grams of fecal sample, were mixed with 10 mL of ultrapure water, vortexed oscillate for 2 min, filtered through four layers of gauze, and centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for 10 min. The supernatant was then frozen at 4 °C for 12 h. Afterward, the sample was centrifuged at 15,000 rpm for 15 min, and 1 mL of the filtrate was centrifuged again at 15,000 rpm for 5 min. 0.5 mL of the supernatant was transferred into a 1.5 mL centrifuge tube, to which 0.5 mL of 10%TCA and 0.1 mL of 40 mmoL 4-methylvaleric acid. were added. The mixture was vortexed, left to stand for 20 min, and then centrifuged at 20,000 ×g for 15 min. The supernatant (1.0 mL) was collected into a sample bottle, and 0.5 µL was used for machine analysis to obtain the VFA chromatogram. The internal standard method was applied to establish a regression equation with the VFA concentration ratio as the Y-axis and the peak-to-height ratio as the X-axis, allowing for the calculation of VFA content in the sample.

The total bacterial DNA extracted was amplified for the 16 S rDNA-V3-V4 region. The correct target band size was confirmed, and the total yield met the requirements for at least two library constructions, enabling subsequent library preparation. Sequencing was performed on the Illumina MiSeq platform. An average of 85,432 ± 12,456 high-quality reads were obtained per sample. The raw reads were subjected to quality filtering (removing reads with an average quality score below Q20 and discarding sequences shorter than 200 bp). Operational taxonomic units (OTUs) were clustered at a 97% similarity threshold using UPARSE software, and taxonomic classification was performed based on the Silva database.

PCR amplification was carried out with the following conditions: all PCR mixtures were added to 15 of µL Phusion High-Fidelity PCR Master Mix(New England Biolabs), 0.2 µM primers, and 10 ng genomic DNA templates. The reaction was denatured for 1 min at 98 ℃, followed by 30 cycles of 98 °C for 10 s,50 °C for 30 s, and 72 °C for 30 s. Finally, the reaction was maintained at 72 °C for 5 min.

Analysis of fecal metabolomics

Extraction of metabolites

Fecal metabolites were analyzed using an untargeted metabolomics approach. Fecal samples (100 mg) were ground with liquid nitrogen, and mixed with pre-cooled 80% methanol, followed by thorough vortex mixing. The samples were incubated on ice for 5 min and then centrifuged at 4 °C at 15,000 g for 20 min. The supernatant was diluted with LC-MS grade water to a final methanol concentration of 53%. The samples were then transferred to new Eppendorf tubes and centrifuged again at 4 °C at 15,000 g for 20 min. Finally, the supernatant was injected into the LC-MS/MS system for analysis.

UHPLC-MS/MS analysis

UHPLC-MS /MS analysis was conducted at Novogene (Beijing, China) using the Vanquish UHPLC system (Thermo Fisher). It was coupled with either the Orbitrap Q ExactiveTM HF mass spectrometer or the Orbitrap Q ExactiveTM HF-X mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher, Germany). The sample was injected onto a Hypersil Gold chromatographic column (100 × 2.1 mm, 1.9 μm), and a 12-minute linear gradient elution was performed at a flow rate of 0.2 mL/min. Eluents for the positive and negative polarity modes were: eluent A (aqueous solution containing 0.1% formic acid) and eluent B (methanol), respectively. The solvent gradient was as follows: 2% B for 1.5 min; 2–85% B for 3 min; 85–100% B for 10 min; 100-2% B for 10.1 min; 2% B for 12 min. The Q ExactiveTM HF mass spectrometer operated in both positive and negative polarity modes, with a spray voltage of 3.5 kV, a capillary temperature of 320 ° C, a sheath gas flow rate of 35 psi, an auxiliary gas flow rate of 10 L/min, an S-lens RF level of 60, and an auxiliary gas heater temperature of 350 ° C.

Statistical analysis

Experimental data were organized using Excel. The milk yield, milk composition, foal weight, and body measurements of lactating mares were analyzed with SPSS 27.0 software using two-way ANOVA with repeated measures over time, with treatment (control group, experimental group I, experimental group II, experimental group III) and time (days 0, 30, 60, and 90) as fixed factors, and their interaction was considered. Blood biochemical indices, fecal pH, and volatile fatty acid concentrations in lactating mares were also analyzed using SPSS 27.0, with post-hoc multiple comparisons conducted using Duncan’s method. Results are presented as mean ± standard deviation. Statistical significance was defined as P < 0.05, with P < 0.01 indicating extreme significance. Amplicon sequencing of the 16 S rDNA gene for microbial analysis and untargeted metabolomics were performed on the Nuohe Zhiyuan NuoHe Cloud platform. For downstream analysis, only operational taxonomic units (OTUs) or metabolites with a relative abundance exceeding 0.01% in more than 50% of samples within each group were included. Significant differences in metabolite levels were determined using the following criteria: VIP > 1, P < 0.05, and fold change (FC) ≥ 2 or ≤ 0.5. Correlation analyses were visualized with the Chiplot software package (https://www.chiplot.online/, Hangzhou Meizheng Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Hangzhou, China).

Effect of L-citrulline supplementation on bacterial diversity in feces of lactating mares

As shown in Fig. 2. the Venn diagram (Fig. 2A) based on OTUs reveals that the fecal microbiota of mares in both the control and experimental groups shareof 4,051 common OTUs, indicating substantial overlap between the groups.The control group harbored 3,568 unique OTUs, while the experimental group contained 3,482 unique OTUs. Further analysis of bacterial community structures using principal component analysis (PCA) and principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) is shown in Fig. 2B and C. In the PCA plot, the first principal component (PC1) explained 11.02% of the variation, while the second principal component (PC2) accounted for 8.94%, with overlapping clusters indicating high similarity between the two groups. The PCoA results also showed minimal differentiation between the groups, with PC1 contributing 26.13% and PC2 explaining 14.67% of the variance. Discussion

Amino acids, are essential building blocks of proteins and play a critical role in equine physiology, especially in lactating mares. Inadequate amino acid intake can impair milk production, subsequently hindering foal growth and development [17, 18]. The results of this study show that supplementing lactating mares with L-citrulline significantly boosts milk production. This aligns with findings from a study in Alpine goats, where daily supplementation of 20 g of L-citrulline increased milk yield [19]. One possible mechanism for this effect is that L-citrulline serves as a precursor to arginine. After absorption, L-citrulline is converted to arginine in the kidneys, which promoting mammary gland development, enhancing milk production, and improving milk quality [20, 21]. Additionally, L-citrulline may be converted into oxaloacetic acid, which enters the gluconeogenic pathway, and is ultimately transformed into phosphoenolpyruvate and lactose in the mammary glands, thereby increasing milk yield [22]. Milk yield, however, is not the sole determinant of lactation performance; milk quality is equally crucial. Important quality indicators include milk fat percentage, protein content, and lactose levels [23, 24]. In the present study, L-citrulline supplementation (15, 30, or 45 g/d) during the lactation period resulted in milk protein, fat, and lactose contents ranging from 1.70% to 1.74%, 1.17% to 1.37%, and 6.20% to 6.28%, respectively, across all experimental groups. These values are comparable to those observed in mares supplemented with lysine and threonine [25], indicating that amino acid supplementation can significantly influence the composition of mare’s milk. An et al. [26] also support these findings, showing that L-citrulline supplementation in Hu sheep ewes from day 60 of gestation increased milk fat content during the first 30 days postpartum, consistent with the results of this study. Thus, L-citrulline likely provides essential amino acids for mammary cells, participates in milk fat synthesis, and supports both fat synthesis and mammary tissue growth.

L-citrulline, a conditionally essential amino acid, cannot be synthesized in sufficient quantities by the body and must be obtained through dietary sources or supplementation. It serves as a key precursor in the biosynthesis of arginine [27]. Arginine, an essential amino acid, plays a vital role in providing nutritional support enhancing reproductive promoting, and promotes growth and development [28]. The findings of this study indicate that supplementing lactating mares with L-citrulline significantly increases milk production. Consequently, foals’ body weight and physical dimensions also improve in parallel with the enhanced milk yield. Thus, L-citrulline not only enhances lactation performance but also supports the growth and development of offspring. Previous studies have shown that L-citrulline supplementation in the diets of Hu sheep ewes and lambs significantly increased body weight of both groups [29, 26, 30]. This effect is likely due to the conversion of L-citrulline into arginine in the kidneys, where it participates in the urea cycle, generating urea and ornithine. This process may enhance milk quality, milk amino acid content, and promote foal development [31]. Additionally, unmetabolized L-citrulline may be transferred to the foal via breast milk, stimulating the secretion of growth hormone and influencing foal growth and development. Although plasma levels of L-citrulline and arginine were not measured in this study, future experiments will further explore the factors influencing lactation and growth performance based on metabolic profiling.

Animal health is influenced by multiple factors, with blood biochemical indicators serving as key parameters for assessing physiological conditions. In this study, protein metabolism-related indicators such as TP, ALB, GLB, UREA and CREA were analyzed to reflect the animal’s ability to absorb dietary crude protein, manage nitrogen metabolism, and deposit nutrients [32]. L-citrulline supplementation had no significant effect on these parameters. Regarding carbohydrate and energy metabolism, Glu and TG are important markers of sugar metabolism and energy conversion, both of which are essential for maintaining normal physiological function. The study found that L-citrulline supplementation did not significantly alter these indicators. Concerning of liver and kidney function as well as energy metabolism-related enzymes, results showed that ALT levels were higher in experimental groups I and III compared to the control group, while ALP levels in experimental group I were lower than those in the control group. The levels of ALP, LDH, and CK all decreased as the L-citrulline dosage increased. Li et al. [33] reported that L-citrulline supplementation in horses raised plasma ALB levels., while Zhang et al. [30] observed significant increases in plasma TP, ALB, and GLB concentrations in lambs. These which contrasts with the findings contrast with our study, where L-citrulline supplementation in lactating mares did not significantly affect plasma protein content. This discrepancy may be attributed to differences in physiological status, metabolic demands, and nutrient utilization efficiency among the experimental animals.

VFAs in equine feces are primarily produced through microbial fermentation in the cecum and colon, playing a critical role in maintaining intestinal health and providing energy to the host [34]. Intestinal pH, which affects the activity of digestive enzymes and the balance of gut microbiota, is also an important factor in gastrointestinal function [35]. In the present study, supplementation with L-citrulline (15, 30, or 45 g/d) led to numerical increases in acetic acid, propionic acid, and total VFAs in the feces of lactating mares compared to the control group. However, no statistically significant differences were found (P > 0.05). Notably, fecal pH consistently decreased with increasing L-citrulline dosage. This may be attributed to two factors: first, enhanced VFA production, which likely increases hydrogen ion (H+) concentration in the intestinal environment [36]; second, L-citrulline, acting as a nitrogen source, contributes to protein synthesis and modulates microbial metabolism in the gut. Specifically, it increased the abundance of Ruminococcaceae_bacterium_AE2021, a bacterium potentially involved in the production of short-chain fatty acids such as acetic and butyric acids, thus lowering intestinal pH. L-citrulline, a non-essential amino acid can be synthesized in intestinal epithelial cells via the glutamate-ornithine pathway and glutamine metabolism, using substrates like glutamine, glutamic acid, and proline [37, 38]. It may influence the intestinal microbiota composition and diversity by modulating nitrogen metabolism and the intestinal environment.Microbial analysis revealed that the core intestinal microbiota of mares shared 4,051 OTUs between the control and experimental groups. The control group contained 3,568 unique OTUs, with Chao1 = 2158.68, Pielou_e = 0.85, Shannon = 9.36, and Simpson = 0.99. The experimental group contained 3,482 unique OTUs, with Chao1 = 2138.04, Pielou_e = 0.86, Shannon = 9.46, and Simpson = 0.99. The high overlap between groups suggests that core ecosystem functions were preserved. However, five bacterial taxa exhibited differences at multiple classification levels (class, order, family, genus, species).At the genus level, Schwartzia is involved in amino acid metabolism and fatty acid synthesis, utilizing L-citrulline as a nitrogen source to enhance arginine metabolism. It may also support intestinal health by producing short-chain fatty acids. At the species level, Ruminococcaceae_bacterium_AE2021 is likely involved in cellulose degradation, with its abundance potentially modulated by L-citrulline induced changes in intestinal nitrogen metabolism. At the class level, Gracilibacteria may participate in the utilization of nitrogen sources derived from citrulline in the nitrogen cycle, thereby enhancing intestinal nitrogen compound metabolism. At the order level, Absconditabacteriales_SR1_is associated with the degradation of complex carbohydrates, and L-citrulline may facilitate short-chain fatty acid production by influencing nitrogen metabolism. At the family level, Ethanoligenenaceae, exhibiting ethanol-producing fermentation traits, may activate its fermentation pathway in response to nitrogen substrates or intermediates derived from L-citrulline, thereby regulating intestinal energy balance.Tax4Fun analysis revealed that gene abundance was highest in categories related to digestion and metabolism, genetic information processing, and environmental information processing. At the phylum level, Firmicutes and Bacteroidetes were dominant, though no significant differences were found. The Firmicutes to Bacteroidetes ratio may influence nitrogen source distribution in the gut: when Firmicutes dominate, their robust lipid metabolism and fermentation capabilities can increase amino acid breakdown and nitrogen accumulation; conversely, Bacteroidetes dominance may reduce excessive fat deposition and provide energy for intestinal epithelial cells to synthesize L-citrulline [39]. At the species level, the experimental group exhibited a higher abundance of Ruminococcaceae_bacterium_AE2021 which can produce short-chain fatty acids (e.g., butyrate) by degrading complex carbohydrates, potentially improving the efficiency of L-citrulline as a nitrogen source. Du et al. [40] reported that the presence of Ruminococcaceae_bacterium_AE2021 in the gut microbiota of fattening pigs supplemented with L-citrulline might enhance nutrient digestion and absorption.

The intestinal microbiota and its metabolic products play a pivotal role in maintaining host intestinal homeostasis. Certain metabolites have been identified for their functional properties, promoting growth, development, and overall health [41]. Metabolomics analysis identified 208 differential metabolites in both positive and negative ion modes, primarily consisting of lipids and organic acids. KEGG enrichment pathway analysis focused on two main areas: biological systems and metabolism. A total of 17 pathways were detected, including the pentose phosphate pathway, linoleic acid metabolism, and galactose metabolism. Notably, the pentose phosphate pathway is crucial in mammary cells, particularly during lactation, as it supports the proliferation of mammary epithelial cells and the expression of enzymes involved in milk fat synthesis through the oxidative and non-oxidative phases [42]. Linoleic acid, the primary polyunsaturated fatty acid (PUFA) in horse milk, and its metabolic products, such as arachidonic acid, contribute to the structural composition of triacylglycerols (TAG) in milk fat. These metabolites increase the PUFA content of milk fat, which can enhance foal growth performance [43]. Furthermore, amino acids in mammals are often derived from linoleic acid metabolism, where linoleic acid from dietary sources is desaturated to form gamma-linolenic acid, which is then further desaturated and extended to form amino acids, ultimately producing prostatic acids, and other metabolites [44]. Correlation analyses revealed significant positive relationships between various metabolites, milk production indicators, and specific intestinal bacteria. For instance, 5-valerolactone was correlated with milk yield and milk fat percentage, while 3,4-O-isopropylidene-shikimic acid was positively correlated with milk fat percentage. Cis-2-dodecenoic acid was positively associated with milk yield, and tributyrin and N, N-dicyclohexylurea were correlated with non-fat solids. The ratio of double-layer amine F to milk protein was positively linked to these indicators. Additionally, 7-hydroxy-6-methylheptyl-3,5-dienal and 3-hydroxysebacic acid showed positive correlations with Ruminococcaceae_ bacterium_ AE2021. These associations offer insights into the milk quality regulation mechanism and the interaction between intestinal flora and host metabolism, enriching the theoretical understanding of milk quality control, and confirming the role of metabolites regulating in milk yield and composition.

In conclusion, L-citrulline supplementation enhances metabolites involved in amino acid and fatty acid metabolism, thereby improving the associated metabolic pathways during lactation. On one hand, it promotes the utilization and metabolism of nutrients like protein and lipids in mares, on the other hand, it stimulates the growth and proliferation of mammary glands, ultimately increasing milk yield and improving milk quality.

Conclusion

L-citrulline can increase the high milk yield of mares, improve the milk fat quality, reduce the contents of lactate dehydrogenase and creatine kinase in blood, and promote the growth and development of nursing foals. The results of 16s sequencing showed that L-citrulline could change the microbial diversity and flora abundance in mares’ intestines (F082 and Ruminococcaceae_bacterium_AE2021), and Tax4Fun analysis showed that it had the highest functional abundance in digestion and metabolism, genetic information and environmental information processing. The LC-MS/MS results of fecal samples showed that the differential metabolites were mainly distributed in biological system and metabolism, and these differential metabolites mainly concentrated in two significant pathways: Pentose phosphate pathway and Linoleic acid metabolism. These results may provide new insights for L- citrulline in improving lactation performance of lactating mares and growth performance of lactating foals.
