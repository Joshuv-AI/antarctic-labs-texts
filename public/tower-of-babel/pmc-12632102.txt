Epigenetic orchestrator and drug enhancer: dual roles of butyrate in regulating post-translational modification and optimizing therapeutic delivery
Authors: Xiya Deng, Hongli Zhang, Wei-Dong Chen, Yan-Dong Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractButyrate, a four-carbon short-chain fatty acid produced by gut microbiota, has been widely recognized for its diverse physiological benefits. Functioning as a natural HDAC inhibitor, it critically modulates various protein post-translational modifications (PTMs). The endogenous transport systems mediating butyrate absorption provide a promising platform for developing butyrate-modified drugs with enhanced bioavailability and tissue targeting. Here, we offer a concise overview of butyrate’s production, transport, and metabolism, focusing on its role in regulating PTMs and the potential therapeutic applications of butyrate transporters in drug delivery. Building on current butyrate modification strategies, we also review its applications in drug formulation enhancement and synergistic therapeutic combinations. In summary, butyrate can function as a natural modulator of specific PTMs and as a drug modifier enhancing therapeutic delivery.Graphical Abstract Keywords: Butyrate, Gut microbiota, Protein post-translational modifications, Drug delivery, Drug modification, Combination therapy

Introduction

Short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) are metabolic end-products of gut microbial fermentation of indigestible dietary fibers in the human colon, with acetate, propionate, and butyrate constituting the major components at an approximate concentration ratio of 6.0:2.0:2.0 [1]. By stimulating G protein-coupled receptors (GPCRs) or inhibiting histone deacetylase (HDAC) activities, SCFAs exert multifaceted biological effects on host health, both locally in the gut and systemically, especially in terms of gastrointestinal homeostasis, cardiovascular homeostasis, metabolic health, cancer development, and immune balance, thereby establishing their critical role in host-microbiota crosstalk [2–4]. Among the three main SCFAs, propionate makes a significant contribution to gluconeogenesis, while acetate and butyrate act as key regulators of lipid metabolism in mammals [5], highlighting their functional differences. Compared with acetate and propionate, butyrate exhibits superior efficacy in improving lipid metabolism by activating the AMPK and PPAR signaling pathways [6]. Moreover, the reporter gene assay reveals that butyrate exhibits significantly more potent HDAC inhibition than propionate and acetate, with IC50 values of 1.13, 10, and >10 mM, respectively [7], further underscoring its far-reaching potential in cancer prevention and inflammation control. These unique features position butyrate as the most promising candidate for clinical applications.

As a saturated monobasic weak acid containing four carbon atoms, butyrate primarily exists as dissociated butyrate anions in physiological systems. Butyrate mediates its physiological effects both as a GPCR ligand to initiate rapid signal transduction and as an HDAC inhibitor to increase N-acetyl-lysine modifications from both histone and non-histone proteins, thereby epigenetically regulating gene expression through chromatin remodeling and transcriptional modulation [8–10]. HDACs serve as critical modulators of protein post-translational modifications (PTMs), which represent essential biochemical processes that dynamically regulate protein structure and function through covalent modifications following protein synthesis. This review focuses on butyrate’s involvement in PTM regulation, particularly elucidating how its metabolic intermediates influence PTMs on histone and non-histone proteins. We then explore the current knowledge on butyrate transporters and their potential applications in enhancing drug delivery systems. Finally, we summarize recent advances in butyrate structural modification and pharmaceutical development, emphasizing the promising application of butyrate-related combination therapy.

The production and metabolism of butyrate

Despite extensive research on butyrate’s production, absorption, and metabolism [8, 11, 12], we re-examine these processes from a metabolic perspective. Butyrate production in the human colon is predominantly mediated by Firmicutes microbiota, with key contributing genera including Faecalibacterium, Roseburia, Eubacterium, Anaerostipes, Butyrivibrio, and Coprococcus [13]. Among these, Eubacterium rectale/Roseburia spp. and Faecalibacterium prausnitzii are the two most abundant butyrate producers, typically constituting 5–10% of the detectable fecal bacteria in healthy adults [13]. Butyrate can be generated through four main metabolic pathways: pyruvate, glutarate, 4-aminobutyrate, and lysine, each serving as a distinct precursor substrate [14]. These pathways converge at the central metabolic intermediate crotonyl-CoA, with distinct synthesizing enzymes participating in each path: crotonase (Cro), 4-hydroxybutyryl dehydratase (4hbD), glutaconyl-CoA decarboxylase (Gcd), or 3-aminobutyryl-CoA ammonia lyase (Kal) (Fig. 1). At the critical metabolic node, the electron transferring flavoprotein/butyryl-CoA dehydrogenase (EtfAB/Bcd) complex catalyzes the reduction of crotonyl-CoA to butyryl-CoA, from which butyrate is generated subsequently through two distinct enzymatic routes. The first route involves the phosphotransbutyrylase (Ptb) and the butyrate kinase (Buk), while the butyryl-CoA: acetate CoA-transferase (BCoAT) catalyzes the secondary route [14]. Glutamate is metabolized through either the 4-aminobutyrate or the glutarate fermentation pathways to produce butyrate. In certain bacteria, such as Eubacterium hallii and Anaerostipes caccae, acetate and lactate can be converted into butyrate [15, 16]. Isotope labeling analysis revealed that 80% of the newly generated butyrate during oligofructose fermentation originates from the interconversion of extracellular acetate and lactate [17]. Utilizing ethanol and acetate as sole energy sources, Clostridium kluyveri produces butyrate through anaerobic fermentation [18].

During the transport process from the intestinal lumen to portal blood, the proportion of butyrate decreased significantly from 21% to 8%, with its concentration further declining sequentially from the portal vein (29 µmol/L) to the hepatic vein (12 µmol/L) and finally to the peripheral vein (4 µmol/L), demonstrating that butyrate uptake was most substantial in the colon, followed by the liver, and then further absorbed in peripheral tissues [1]. Colonocytes predominantly utilize butyrate as their primary energy substrate through mitochondrial β-oxidation [19, 20]. Following cellular uptake, butyrate is converted into butyryl-CoA in the cytosol, then enters the mitochondria to produce acetyl-CoA [21]. While acetyl-CoA participates in the tricarboxylic acid cycle to generate energy and support gluconeogenesis, it simultaneously functions as a biosynthetic precursor for cholesterol, palmitate, and other lipids [5]. The liver extensively metabolizes the absorbed fraction through three major pathways: gluconeogenesis, lipogenesis, and ketogenesis [5, 22]. The ketogenic pathway related to butyrate metabolism involves critical enzymatic steps: the acetyl-CoA acetyltransferase 1 (ACAT1)-mediated reversible condensation of two acetyl-CoA molecules into acetoacetyl-CoA [23] and the short-chain 3-hydroxyacyl-CoA dehydrogenase (SCHAD)-catalyzed generation of acetoacetyl-CoA [24, 25]. These reactions serve as crucial metabolic nodes bridging mitochondrial β-oxidation with ketogenesis. Along with providing substrates, sodium butyrate (NaBu) also stimulated 3-hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl-CoA synthase 2 (HMGCS2) to promote ketone synthesis [26]. The metabolic mechanisms of butyrate production and metabolism are crucial for comprehending its physiological roles and clinical applications.

Butyrate-inhibited HDACs and butyrate-modulated PTMs

Metabolic intermediates of butyrate and their linkages to protein post-translational modifications

The metabolic intermediates of butyrate are closely related to multiple protein post-translational modifications (Fig. 2). Short-chain acyl-coenzyme A synthetase (ACSS) typically catalyzes the essential activation of butyrate [50], generating butyryl-CoA as the direct, immediate donor for histone butyrylation-mediated epigenetic regulation. Although ACSS2 was initially believed to function as a cytoplasmic enzyme responsible for butyrate activation [51], recent evidence has shown that it lacks the catalytic capacity to generate butyryl-CoA or crotonyl-CoA directly from butyrate or crotonate in vivo [52]. Thus, the identity of ACSS catalyzing butyrate and crotonate activation requires further experimental validation. In the β-oxidation pathway, short-chain acyl-CoA dehydrogenase (SCAD, ACADS) catalyzes the oxidative conversion of butyryl-CoA to crotonyl-CoA [53, 54], which serves as the essential substrate for protein crotonylation. After β-oxidation, butyrate will eventually produce two molecules of acetyl-CoA [21], whose concentration directly affects the level of protein acetylation. In addition, as an HDAC inhibitor, butyrate maintains specific protein PTMs by blocking deacetylase activity.

Butyrate and its metabolic intermediates influence protein post-translational modifications. Following cellular uptake through its transporters (MCTs and SMCTs), butyrate contributes to cellular metabolism and modulates ubiquitination of non-histone proteins. Additionally, it exerts epigenetic effects in the cytoplasm and nucleus by inhibiting HDACs and providing acyl-donor substrates (butyryl-CoA, crotonyl-CoA, and acetyl-CoA). Protein post-translational modifications regulated by butyrate

Acetylation

Lysine acetylation (Kac), a critical post-translational modification, involves the covalent attachment of acetyl groups to lysine residues, dynamically controlling protein functions, cellular signaling, and epigenetic states. The contribution of butyrate to acetylation comes from two aspects: inhibiting HDACs and increasing carbon skeletons. Although HDACs are now recognized to regulate diverse PTMs, they were initially defined by their ability to remove acetyl groups from histone tails. HDAC inhibitors are structurally classified into four major categories: hydroxamate, short-chain fatty acids (carboxylate), benzamide, and cyclic peptide [55]. Current studies have demonstrated that butyrate upregulates acetylation levels through selective inhibition of HDAC1, 2, 3, 4, and 8 [27–39] (Table 1). ¹³C-tracing experiments in butyrate-treated Caco-2 cells demonstrated rapid ¹³C incorporation into histone acetylation marks within 30 min, directly confirming its role as an acetylation substrate provider [56]. Current evidence conclusively characterizes ACSS2 as the principal acetyl-CoA synthetase, and butyrate-induced upregulation of ACSS2 in hippocampal neurons significantly increased acetyl-CoA levels, subsequently enhancing histone H3K9 acetylation (H3K9ac) [57]. The acetylation-modulating capacity of butyrate positions it as a promising therapeutic candidate in treating cancer, inflammation, and neurological diseases (Table 1).

Butyrylation

In 2007, through proteomic analysis, lysine butyrylation (Kbu) was first identified and biochemically validated as a novel post-translational modification involving the incorporation of butyryl groups from butyryl-CoA into lysine residues [58]. The structural isomers, butyryl-CoA and isobutyryl-CoA, coexist intracellularly as dedicated donors for Kbu (lysine n-butyrylation) and lysine isobutyrylation, respectively [59]. The intestinal microbiota generates n-butyrate as the predominant form of butyrate, which is metabolized into butyryl-CoA and directly regulates cellular butyrylation. Quantitative metabolomic analysis revealed a dose-dependent increase in butyryl-CoA levels as the concentration of butyrate increased [40]. Butyrate (5 mM) significantly promoted histone 3-lysine 9-butyrylation (H3K9bu) [41], and butyryl-CoA treatment markedly upregulated the butyrylation level of histone H3 [60], confirming butyrate’s role as the direct acyl donor. Immunohistochemical analysis demonstrated that NaBu effectively restored the reduced expression of PanKbu, H3K9bu, and p300 in the renal tissues of diabetic kidney disease (DKD) mice, with the upregulated PanKbu predominantly localized in the nucleus [42]. Acyltransferases mediating butyrylation exhibited distinct substrate specificities: in vitro studies demonstrated that CBP/p300 (CREB-binding protein/E1A binding protein p300) directly butyrylates both histone H4 and non-histone protein p53 [58], whereas Gcn5 mutation significantly reduced histone H3 butyrylation [60]. The slight but significant increases in p300 activity induced by butyrate possibly correlated with the rapid formation of butyryl-CoA [43]. At the same time, in vitro identification of butyrylated p300/CBP established these acetyltransferases as mediators and targets of Kbu [58]. Notably, butyryl-CoA competitively binds to carnitine palmitoyltransferase 1 A (CPT1A), thereby alleviating the inhibitory effect of malonyl-CoA on CPT1A activity [51]. While preliminary data imply CPT1A butyrylation, conclusive validation requires in vitro modification assays and site-specific mutagenesis. These studies establish that butyrate positively regulates butyrylation by contributing to the substrate pool and enhancing acyltransferase activity.

Crotonylation

In 2011, lysine crotonylation (Kcr) was initially identified as a novel histone modification, using crotonyl-CoA as the donor substrate for crotonyltransferases to catalyze crotonyl group addition to histone lysine residues [61]. Subsequently, research on lysine crotonylation modification expanded to non-histone proteins in 2017 [62]. Current research has shown that butyrate is a Kcr promoter. First, butyrate undergoes metabolic conversion to crotonyl-CoA, providing the essential substrate for crotonylation [44, 61]. The crotonylation of H3K9 was significantly enhanced by NaBu but suppressed by ACADS knockout, revealing that ACADS regulates histone 3-lysine 9-crotonylation (H3K9cr) [45]. Secondly, as an HDAC inhibitor, butyrate can reduce the decrotonylation of histones [63]. The suppression of HDAC2 and HDAC3 induced by NaBu increased H4K8cr, reducing liver fibrosis in CCl₄-treated models [63]. Additional research supported this, showing that butyrate-mediated HDAC2 inhibition reduced hepatic fibrosis in biliary atresia [64]. NaBu upregulated Pan-Kcr expression in normal and pancreatic cancer cell lines [44]. Given the identification of 19,713 crotonylation sites across 7,637 proteins in hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) [65], follow-up studies could further investigate butyrate’s regulatory effects on these proteins’ crotonylation modifications.

Ubiquitination

Ubiquitination, a reversible post-translational modification, is characterized by the covalent attachment of ubiquitin molecules to the epsilon-amino group of protein lysine residues [66, 67]. Ubiquitin modifications exhibit remarkable functional diversity, depending on the types of formed linkages. Monoubiquitination typically modulates protein activity, localization, interaction partners, and endocytic trafficking [68], whereas K63-linked polyubiquitination mediates signal transduction pathways and K48-linked polyubiquitination targets substrates for proteasomal degradation [66, 67]. Accumulating evidence has established butyrate as a key epigenetic regulator of ubiquitin signaling networks, particularly influencing protein homeostasis in cellular antioxidant systems and ferroptosis pathways. In the nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 (Nrf2) antioxidant pathway, NaBu inhibited the ubiquitin-proteasome pathway, thereby enhancing Nrf2 stability, with an observed half-life extension of 80 min [46]. Subcellular tracking via immunofluorescence analysis showed that biotin-conjugated butyrate primarily accumulated in lysosomes in A549 and H1299 cell lines [47]. In the ferroptosis-related pathways, butyrate modulated iron homeostasis by increasing the recruitment of Fe2+ in lysosomes and significantly decreasing the expression level of SLC7A11 protein [47]. This reduction was prevented by MG132 (proteasome inhibitor) but not Lys05 (lysosome inhibitor), indicating that SLC7A11 protein is degraded via the ubiquitin-proteasome pathway rather than the lysosomal pathway [47]. NaBu could also downregulate the expression of ubiquitin-specific protease 5 (USP5), thereby enhancing ubiquitin-mediated degradation of the ferroptosis regulator GPX4 [48]. In addition, tumour necrosis factor receptor-associated factor 6 (TRAF6) is a RING domain-containing E3 ligase that regulates K63-polyubiquitination modification [69]. NaBu enhanced the interaction between TRAF6 and thioredoxin-interacting protein (TXNIP), affecting the polyubiquitination level of TXNIP [49]. Collectively, current evidence supports that butyrate can affect protein polyubiquitination levels.

Butyrate regulates these four PTMs through shared molecular mechanisms and distinct metabolic enzymes. In acetylation, butyrate enhances acetyl-CoA availability via ACSS2-mediated synthesis while simultaneously inhibiting histone deacetylases (HDAC1, 2, 3, 4, and 8), resulting in enhanced acetylation. For butyrylation, butyrate is activated by ACSSs to generate butyryl-CoA, which is then utilized by acyltransferases like p300/CBP and Gcn5 to modify target proteins. Concurrent inhibition of HDAC1/3 further promotes butyrylation. In crotonylation, butyrate is metabolized into crotonyl-CoA via β-oxidation, a process directly regulated by the enzyme ACADS. Inhibition of HDAC2/3 attenuates decrotonylation, thereby promoting the accumulation of crotonylated proteins. For ubiquitination, butyrate modulates polyubiquitination and protein stability primarily by interfering with the ubiquitin–proteasome system, including the regulation of E3 ligases and deubiquitinases.

Existing findings suggest that butyrate’s influence on PTMs likely extends beyond the four types mentioned above. For example, butyrate may indirectly regulate β-hydroxybutyrylation by enhancing hepatic ketogenesis, generating β-hydroxybutyrate as a critical substrate. In addition, CPT1A has been well-established as a succinyltransferase [70–72]. Butyrate upregulated CPT1A expression and activity [51, 73–75], potentially modulating cellular succinylation. Therefore, butyrate is a multi-PTM regulator (Fig. 2). The underlying mechanisms remain incompletely elucidated, including the potential effects of butyrate on other PTM types and its influence on PTM crosstalk networks.

Bridging butyrate and drug optimization

Due to its potent physiological effects, butyrate has garnered widespread attention for its practical applications, particularly in developing butyrate-related drugs and utilizing butyrate-producing probiotics. We are particularly interested in butyrate’s “indirect function” as a drug modifier to promote the absorption of other drugs before proceeding to summarize butyrate precursor modifications and its combination therapies with other medications.

Butyrate-modified drug delivery: enhancing bioavailability and targeted therapy via transporter exploitation

Monocarboxylate transporters in butyrate absorption and targeted drug delivery systems

In the intestinal lumen, butyrate mainly exists in an ionized form and necessitates specialized transmembrane transporters for cellular uptake [1, 76]. This process is mediated principally by proton-coupled monocarboxylate transporters (MCTs) and sodium-coupled monocarboxylate cotransporters (SMCTs). Among these transporters, MCT1 exhibits the broadest expression profile, with near-ubiquitous expression across major organ systems, including renal, gastrointestinal, neurological, and hepatic tissues [77]. The expression levels of MCT1, MCT4 (SLC16A4), SMCT1 (SLC5A8), and SMCT2 (SLC5A12) vary in different regions of the intestine [78–81], collectively facilitating the transport of butyrate from the intestinal lumen to the portal vein. Immunofluorescence analysis of Caco-2 cells revealed distinct subcellular localizations: MCT4 was exclusively localized to the basolateral plasma membrane, whereas MCT1 exhibited a more uniform distribution, indicating that MCT4 functions as a basolateral efflux transporter [78]. Unlike MCT1 and SMCT1, which exhibit affinity for three major SCFAs, SMCT2 exclusively transports butyrate [76]. It appears to function primarily in the absorption of dietary short-chain monocarboxylates rather than those produced by bacterial fermentation [80].

Due to its high expression in the intestine, MCT1 is regarded as a targeting strategy for enhancing oral absorption, with SCFAs acting as natural ligands for MCT1-targeted drug delivery [82]. Researchers have also proposed that butyrate-specific targeting strategies could exploit the elevated expression of MCT1 in hepatocellular carcinoma tissue [83]. Furthermore, butyrate itself upregulates the expression of MCT1 and MCT4 [78, 84], creating a positive feedback loop that enhances the cellular uptake of their substrates, including butyrate. Through this mechanism, butyrate led to increased uptake and basolateral transport of ferulic acid [78]. These findings provide a theoretical basis for developing drug delivery strategies targeting butyrate transport systems.

Modifying drugs with butyrate

Butyrate may be an attractive drug modifier when its tissue-specific transporter distribution and specific binding are strategically exploited. Butyrate-functionalized PEG nanoparticles (Bu-PEG NPs)

Butyrate-functionalized PEG nanoparticles (Bu-PEG NPs) were synthesized by modifying polyethylene glycol-modified nanoparticles (PEG-NPs) with butyrate-conjugated DSPE-PEG (DSPE-PEG-Bu) via a self-assembly method of nanoprecipitation [85]. This formulation strategy enhanced efficacy and reduced toxicity compared with co-administration of free butyrate and PEG-NPs [85]. Butyrate modification enhanced the cellular uptake and transepithelial transport of PEG-NPs partially through MCT1-mediated endocytosis and increased their accumulation in major organs such as the heart, liver, spleen, lungs, and kidneys [85].

Insulin (INS) and semaglutide (Sema) are first-line and commonly used medications for type 1 diabetes and type 2 diabetes, respectively. The encapsulation of insulin or semaglutide in Bu-PEG NPs formed insulin-loaded Bu-PEG NPs (BU-NP@INS) and semaglutide-loaded Bu-PEG NPs (BU-NP@Sema), significantly enhancing the oral bioavailability and hypoglycemic function of INS and Sema [85, 86]. Additionally, forskolin was shown to further improve the transport and absorption of Bu-PEG NPs by upregulating the expression of MCT-1 on the apical membrane and promoting its recruitment into caveolae, thereby enhancing the in vivo therapeutic effects of BU-NP@Sema [86].

Sorafenib has been established as a first-line oral systemic therapy in the treatment of hepatocellular carcinoma, demonstrating significant survival benefits in advanced-stage disease. Sorafenib-loaded nanoparticles with butyrate modification (BU-NP@Sor) utilize butyrate-modified polyethylene glycol nanoparticles (Bu-PEG NPs) to encapsulate sorafenib, thereby enhancing the oral administration of the drug. The functionalized ligands of butyrate enhanced the oral bioavailability, specificity, and nanoparticle concentration in the liver, resulting in improved therapeutic effects [83].

Resveratrol butyrate esters

Resveratrol (RE) is a natural polyphenolic compound with antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and anti-diabetic properties, as well as other pharmacological activities [91]. Although RE demonstrated strong absorption following oral administration in humans, its bioavailability remained limited [92]. Through the Steglich esterification reaction, resveratrol butyrate esters (RBE) were designed and synthesized to enhance the activity and stability of RE [87]. Compared with free RE, RBE demonstrated superior efficacy in inhibiting oleic acid-induced lipid accumulation and eliminating hydrogen peroxide [87, 88]. RBE supplementation in bisphenol A-exposed pregnant rats inhibited obesity development in female offspring [89] and alleviated liver injury in male offspring [90], partially mediated through modulating gut microbiota. Chromatographic separation and structural characterization of the RBE complex identified five ester derivatives, with 3,4’-di-O-butanoylresveratrol and 3-O-butanoylresveratrol demonstrating superior antioxidant activity relative to the parent mixture, confirming that the quantity and position of butyrate esterification sites correlate with antioxidant potency and cell survival rate [93].

Drug modification and combination therapy related to butyrate

Modifying butyrate

Continuous intravenous infusion of sodium or arginine butyrate caused a sudden increase in blood concentration, resulting in various acute toxicities, especially hypokalemia [94, 95]. The short half-life and rapid excretion of butyrate in the human body limit its therapeutic effect, thereby restricting its practical application [96, 97]. Furthermore, the unpleasant odor of butyrate compromises patient compliance with oral administration, presenting additional challenges to formulation. To overcome these shortcomings, the structural modification and pharmaceutical formulation optimization of butyrate have been explored (Fig. 3).

Structural modification

Butylated starch (HAMSB), a carbohydrate synthesized from high-amylose maize starch (HAMS) and butyric anhydride, serves as a colon-targeted delivery vehicle that enables the targeted and prolonged release of butyrate through gut microbiota-mediated enzymatic degradation. Unlike dietary SCFAs, which undergo rapid absorption in the foregut, HAMSB significantly raised the concentrations of SCFAs and butyrate in the cecal and distal colonic lumens of rats [98]. Compared to HAMS, HAMSB exhibited a reduced vulnerability to in vitro amylolysis after the cooking process [99], indicating increased stability and a higher delivery rate. HAMSB significantly elevated butyric acid and total SCFAs levels in the gut and portal vein by enhancing their intestinal biosynthesis [6, 99]. This effect was attributed to two colonic fermentation processes: the effective release of introduced acyl groups [6] and the microbial generation of additional SCFAs [6, 99]. Research has demonstrated that HAMSB reshapes gut microbial composition and attenuates inflammation in type 2 diabetic rat models, concomitant with significant improvements in metabolic parameters, including glycemic control and lipid homeostasis [100, 101].

As a derivative of NaBu, the N-(1-carbamoyl-2-phenyl-ethyl) butiramide (FBA) exhibits superior palatability and patient compliance. FBA demonstrated pharmacokinetic properties identical to those of butyrate, with the Ames and Micronucleus Tests showing no in vitro genotoxicity [102]. In HFD-induced rat models of insulin resistance and hepatic steatosis, FBA showed comparable therapeutic efficacy to NaBu, with significantly greater potency in reducing pro-inflammatory parameters, lipid accumulation, and HMGB1 transcription [103]. Notably, FBA demonstrated greater efficacy than NaBu in free fatty acid-induced proton leak and decreasing lipid density in obese insulin-resistant murine models [104]. Standardized preclinical testing will be necessary in the future to assess the safety of FBA.

As a neutral short-chain fatty acid triglyceride, tributyrin (TB) is an efficient butyrate prodrug with superior pharmacokinetic properties. Its chemical stability and rapid intestinal absorption overcome the inherent limitation of free butyrate, including short plasma half-life and failure to achieve therapeutic concentrations in target tissues. Prophylactic oral administration of TB (1 g/kg) could increase portal vein plasma butyrate and improve fatty acid oxidation, thereby alleviating liver injury in endotoxemic rats [105, 106]. In addition, TB treatment induced significant epigenetic remodeling in preneoplastic livers, including moderate hyperacetylation of histone H3K9 and H3K18 and robust hyperacetylation of H4K16 [107]. TB mitigated ethanol-induced liver steatosis and damage via HDAC1-CPT1A axis modulation [75], while showing potent chemoprevention in experimental rat hepatocarcinogenesis through multimodal mechanisms, including inhibiting angiogenesis, increasing p53 acetylation to promote apoptosis, and suppressing oncogenic pathways [107–110].

Butyrate-containing structured lipids (STLs), synthesized via enzymatic interesterification of TB and flaxseed oil, showed visible chemopreventive activity in experimental rat hepatocarcinogenesis [110]. As epigenetic modifiers, STLs can modulate the p53 signaling pathway, suppress oncogenic signaling pathways, reduce the activation of major hepatocarcinogenesis-related oncogenes, and inhibit the epithelial-mesenchymal transition [110–112]. TB and STLs showed similar antitumor activity; however, STLs demonstrated superior inhibition of oncogene expression [110]. Further research should evaluate whether TB or STLs demonstrate superior stability and therapeutic advantages.

Formulation modification

Liposomes are widely recognized as effective drug delivery platforms for enhancing pharmacokinetic characteristics, and their multiple surface modifications facilitate tissue-specific targeting [113]. Liposomal encapsulation of butyrate effectively reduced its cytotoxicity while enhancing therapeutic efficacy [114]. Both chitosan-coated and uncoated liposomes increased butyrate uptake and internalization in HepG2 cells, resulting in higher anti-inflammatory and anti-cancer activities than free butyrate [114]. Another strategy encapsulated NaBu into liposomes formulated with 1,2-dihexadecanoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphocholine (DPPC), 1,2-distearoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphoethanolamine-N-(methoxy (polyethylene glycol)−5000) (DSPE-PEG5k), and cholesterol [115]. This NaBu-liposome achieved a sustained release profile and enhanced tumor-targeted accumulation of NaBu following intravenous injection, relieving Fusobacterium nucleatum-induced chemoresistance in colorectal cancer [115].

Butyric acid-based nanoparticle (BNP) is an amphiphilic block copolymer synthesized from polyethylene glycol (PEG) and polyethylene butyrate, exhibiting improved palatability and reduced odor. Digestive enzymes slowly hydrolyze BNP in the gastrointestinal tract, resulting in continuous butyric acid release [116]. By activating PPARα (peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor α) and modulating CPT1A expression, BNP could reduce hepatic lipogenesis and fibrosis [117]. This modification, superior to free butyrate, enabled butyrate to persist in the liver at a supraphysiological level for up to 72 h, decreased adverse responses, and improved therapeutic efficacy [117]. This dosage modification may be extended to other SCFAs, potentially amplifying clinical benefits and reducing side effects.

Butyrate-related combination therapies

Combining butyrate with other drugs synergistically enhances therapeutic outcomes by improving therapeutic efficacy, reducing drug resistance, lowering the therapeutic threshold, and promoting drug absorption.

Sorafenib

Combination therapies targeting complementary molecular pathways represent a promising strategy to overcome resistance while potentiating therapeutic efficacy [118]. NaBu curtailed sorafenib resistance in HCC through epigenetic downregulation of resistance-associated microRNAs (miR-7641 and miR-199) [119]. It also mitigated sorafenib-induced aerobic glycolysis and enhanced sorafenib-mediated growth inhibition and apoptosis induction in HCC-LM3 cells [120]. To prolong plasma half-life and improve drug delivery, butyrate and sorafenib were co-encapsulated within antibody-functionalized mPEG-PLGA-PLL nanoparticles, which significantly reduced sorafenib-induced liver injury and suppressed hepatocellular carcinoma progression [121].

Bezafibrate

As a well-known pan-PPAR agonist, bezafibrate exhibits comprehensive therapeutic effects in lipid lowering and the management of non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) [122, 123]. PPARα controls the transcription of fibroblast growth factor 21 (FGF21), which is preferentially expressed in the liver and stimulates β-oxidation of fatty acids to generate ketones [124–126]. Mechanistically, butyrate activated the FGF21 gene promoter by inhibiting HDAC3, while bezafibrate enhanced PPARα-DNA binding activity [74]. Their synergistic activation could reduce the required dosage of PPARα agonists while maintaining therapeutic potency and lessening adverse effects [74].

1′-Acetoxychavicol acetate

1′-Acetoxychavicol acetate (ACA) is a naturally occurring component in Alpinia galanga and Alpinia conchigera, exhibiting multiple medicinal properties in the treatment of obesity, inflammation, and cancer [127]. Although ACA exhibited no noticeable impact on early rat hepatocarcinogenesis in vivo, it suppressed the growth of HepG2 and MH1C1 cells [128]. The combination of ACA and NaBu demonstrated a synergistic impact on HepG2 cell death, whereas in normal hepatocyte cells, neither alone nor in combination reduced cell numbers and increased ROS levels [129]. ACA and NaBu also synergistically upregulated phase II enzyme activities, partially via their shared ability to increase intranuclear acetyl-p53 and p-AMPK levels [130].

Genipin and acetaminophen

Excessive use of acetaminophen (APAP) and genipin (GP) is identified as a contributor to severe drug-induced liver injury [131, 132]. Nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 (Nrf2) is a master transcriptional regulator integrating diverse cellular processes, including antioxidant responses, NADPH biosynthesis, autophagy, and mitochondrial biogenesis [133]. Butyrate could reduce the hepatotoxicity of APAP and GP through activating Nrf2-mediated antioxidant signaling [134, 135]. By promoting autophagy of damaged mitochondria via the AMPK-ULK1-p62 axis, butyrate reversed APAP-induced ferroptosis, and its combination with N-acetylcysteine significantly enhanced the survival rate of APAP-poisoned mice [135].

Silybin

Silybin (SIL) is a naturally occurring plant compound with potent hepatoprotective properties [136]. Through the selective enrichment of SCFA-producing gut microbiota and the improvement of hepatic steatosis, SIL may contribute to the prevention and treatment of NAFLD [137]. In atherosclerosis (AS) mice, SIL promoted butyrate production by both selectively enriching butyrogenic gut microbiota and upregulating the butyryl-CoA: acetate CoA-transferase (BCoAT) pathway rather than the Ptb/Buk pathway [138]. The oral bioavailability of SIL was enhanced by both SIL-induced upregulation of MCT4 expression and butyrate-mediated stimulation of MCT1 in colon tissue [138]. The potential benefits of their combined treatment require further validation.

Clinical evaluation of butyrate-based therapies

Currently, while the majority of butyrate-based therapies remain at the preclinical stage, a limited number have progressed to clinical trials, demonstrating promising but variable therapeutic outcomes across different conditions.

In terms of colonic delivery, research has shown that cooked HAMSB serves as an effective carrier for the targeted delivery of butyrate to the colon. Specifically, in ileostomy volunteers, 73% to 76% of cooked esterified SCFAs were able to evade small intestinal digestion [139]. In healthy volunteers with low fecal butyrate concentrations, 79–90% of ingested HAMSB is released and absorbed in the gastrointestinal tract, with 57.2% of the release occurring in the colon [140]. Moderate consumption of butyrylated starch is safe and well-tolerated, with potential applications in colon health and related diseases. Still, it should be used as a functional food ingredient rather than a therapeutic agent.

In the treatment of Epstein-Barr virus-associated lymphoid malignancies, a phase 1/2 trial of 15 patients with refractory EBV(+) lymphoid malignancies showed that the maximum tolerated dose of arginine butyrate combined with ganciclovir was 1000 mg/(kg·d). Among these participants, 10 cases showed significant anti-tumor reactions, and 3 cases showed reversible somnolence or stupor at higher doses [141].

For inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), the efficacy of butyrate-based interventions is dependent on the administration route and interindividual variability. Clinical trials showed that sodium butyrate capsules administered as an adjuvant therapy showed no significant efficacy in newly diagnosed children and adolescents with IBD [142]. Similarly, sodium butyrate enemas (60 mL, 80 mM) failed to show superiority over placebo in improving clinical remission rates in distal ulcerative colitis (UC) [143]. However, local treatment with butyrate has demonstrated positive effects in some patients with refractory distal UC. In a study of 10 individuals unresponsive to conventional rectal therapies and oral aminosalicylates, nightly butyrate enemas (60 mL, 80 mM) resulted in a complete response in 40% of cases and an overall response in 60% of cases [143]. Another clinical trial further found that combined therapy with topical butyrate and 5-ASA was significantly more effective than 5-ASA monotherapy [144].

In the management of metabolic syndrome, the therapeutic potential of butyrate supplementation has attracted broad interest, with its efficacy varying across populations and disease subtypes. A randomized controlled trial in children revealed that six-month oral administration of sodium butyrate (20 mg/kg) significantly reduced the body mass index (BMI) [145]. However, two cases of transient and mild adverse effects, including nausea and headache, were reported during the initial phase of treatment [145]. Another study showed that active tablets containing calcium butyrate (500 mg/tablet), zinc gluconate (zinc 5 mg/tablet), and vitamin D3 (500 IU/tablet) improved liver steatosis and several NAFLD-related parameters [146]. Conversely, a separate intervention using either sodium butyrate (500 mg) or calcium butyrate (500 mg) did not exhibit a significant reduction in hepatic steatosis; however, changes in metabolic and inflammatory markers suggested potential benefits for specific subgroups of patients with metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) [147]. Additionally, probiotics enhance the glucose-lowering/hypoglycemic effects of metformin by facilitating butyrate production, reflected by reductions in fasting blood glucose, insulin resistance, and fibronectin levels [148]. In contrast, in patients with long-term type 1 diabetes, oral butyrate supplementation did not significantly affect the innate and adaptive immune responses in their peripheral blood [149].

In summary, butyrate-based therapies face considerable challenges, including heterogeneous treatment effects and limited long-term safety data. Although butyrate shows promise as a drug modifier, its efficacy in enhancing drug absorption beyond hepatic and intestinal tissues requires further investigation. Ongoing research into butyrate modification and combination therapies is expected to overcome these limitations and facilitate the clinical translation of butyrate-based treatments.

Conclusions

We present a systematic review of recent advancements in butyrate research, focusing on its functions in protein PTM modulation and pharmaceutical development. Butyrate functions as a broad-spectrum modulator of PTMs, regulating modifications (acetylation, crotonylation, and butyrylation) on histone and non-histone proteins, while influencing non-histone substrates’ ubiquitination. Regarding drug delivery systems, butyrate has emerged as an effective modifier to enhance drug solubility and transmembrane transport, thereby improving drug bioavailability and delivery efficiency. Structural modifications and advanced formulation strategies have shown great promise in overcoming the inherent limitations of butyrate. Furthermore, butyrate-related combination therapies show significant potential in mitigating drug resistance and hepatic toxicity, highlighting their clinical promise for treating complex diseases. In conclusion, a reappraisal is warranted to elucidate the role of butyrate as an HDAC inhibitor and its function in epigenetic control and PTM modulation. Advancing the clinical utilization of butyrate requires further optimization of its tissue specificity, pharmacokinetics, and therapeutic window. In the future, the clinical development of butyrate-based interventions might open up new possibilities for therapeutics, including metabolic syndrome, dyslipidemia, cancer, and cardiovascular diseases.
