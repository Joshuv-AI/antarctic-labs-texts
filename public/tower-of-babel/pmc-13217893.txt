Sepsis caused by Streptococcus canis: An underrecognized zoonotic infection in an elderly dog owner
Authors: Stephanie Hilton, Christopher Clark, Rebekah Morrow, Stephen DiGiuseppe
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractStreptococcus canis (S. canis) is an underrecognized zoonotic pathogen capable of causing invasive human infection. We report a case of sepsis with acute hypoxic respiratory failure due to S. canis bacteremia in a 73-year-old woman with multiple comorbidities, including breast cancer complicated by chronic lymphedema and cardiovascular disease. She presented with fever, progressive dyspnea, hypoxemia, leukocytosis, acute kidney injury, and rising cardiac troponin levels. Imaging demonstrated bilateral interstitial opacities consistent with pulmonary edema, and her clinical course required intensive care management. Two sets of blood cultures grew S. canis, a group G β-hemolytic streptococcus most commonly associated with dogs and cats. Species-level identification was achieved using polymerase chain reaction–based blood culture testing followed by automated biochemical confirmation. The patient was initially treated with broad-spectrum antibiotics and subsequently transitioned to intravenous penicillin G after susceptibility testing demonstrated β-lactam sensitivity. Further history revealed frequent exposure to a household dog without history of bites or scratches, supporting probable non-traumatic zoonotic transmission. The patient improved with targeted therapy and completed a 13-day course of intravenous antibiotics. This case demonstrates that S. canis can cause severe cardiopulmonary compromise in elderly patients and highlights the epidemiologic value of species-level identification in recognizing zoonotic transmission and prompting targeted assessment of animal exposure in patients with group G streptococcal sepsis.Keywords: Streptococcus canis, Zoonotic infection, Sepsis, Group G streptococcus, Bacteremia

Introduction

While most human streptococcal infections are caused by species adapted to human hosts, a subset of streptococci primarily colonize animals and may be transmitted to humans through zoonotic exposure [1], [2]. These organisms may be underrecognized in clinical practice because diagnostic workflows frequently emphasize Lancefield grouping rather than species-level identification, leading to potential misclassification and underestimation of animal-associated streptococcal infections [3], [4]. Among these zoonotic streptococci, Streptococcus canis (S. canis) is a β-hemolytic, Lancefield group G organism most commonly associated with dogs and cats but capable of causing invasive infection in humans [1], [5].

Reported human infections with S. canis include bacteremia, soft tissue infection, osteoarticular infection, and infective endocarditis [3], [4], [6], [7], [8], [9]. Advanced age and the presence of comorbid conditions appear to increase susceptibility to invasive disease, and reported cases frequently involve exposure to companion animals, particularly dogs, consistent with zoonotic transmission in older or immunocompromised hosts [1], [3], [7], [10]. Although zoonotic transmission is infrequently reported, asymptomatic colonization of domestic animals and close household contact, including exposure to animal saliva, may facilitate infection, particularly among individuals at increased risk for pet-associated disease [1], [3], [11].

Herein, we report a case of sepsis caused by S. canis in an elderly patient with multiple comorbidities, including a history of breast cancer and chronic lymphedema. This case highlights the underrecognition of S. canis as a human pathogen and underscores the importance of species-level identification and assessment of companion animal exposure when group G streptococci are isolated in at-risk patients.

Case presentation

A 73-year-old woman presented to the emergency department with a one-week history of fever, chills, cough, and nasal congestion, followed by progressive dyspnea and chest tightness over two days. Her medical history was notable for hypertension, dyslipidemia, hypothyroidism, breast cancer status post bilateral mastectomy complicated by chronic right upper-extremity lymphedema, atrial fibrillation, prior cardiac tumor resection, and grade II diastolic dysfunction. She reported allergies to cephalosporins and tetracyclines.

On arrival, she was febrile to 101.4 °F, tachycardic, and hypoxic with an oxygen saturation of 87% on room air. Arterial blood gas analysis demonstrated hypoxemia without hypercapnia, consistent with acute hypoxic respiratory failure. Physical exam was significant for scattered wheezing and rales in bilateral lungs as well as pitting edema in bilateral lower extremities. Laboratory evaluation on admission revealed leukocytosis (15.5 × 10⁹/L) with marked neutrophilic predominance (93%), mild normocytic anemia (hemoglobin 9.9 g/dL), and acute kidney injury (blood urea nitrogen 29 mg/dL and creatinine 1.19 mg/dL). Brain natriuretic peptide (BNP) was minimally elevated (182 pg/mL) and remaining laboratory values, including platelets and liver enzymes, were within normal limits at the time of presentation. During her assessment in the emergency department her initial cardiac troponin was measured at < 0.01 ng/mL rising to 0.04 ng/mL on repeat testing. Electrocardiography revealed ST-segment abnormalities unchanged from a prior study obtained two months earlier. Initial chest radiography demonstrated bilateral interstitial opacities without pleural effusion. Computed tomography angiography of the chest was performed to evaluate for pulmonary embolism and revealed no embolic or aortic pathology; however, interlobar septal thickening was noted, consistent with pulmonary edema (Fig. 1). Subsequent posterior–anterior upright chest radiography confirmed bilateral interstitial opacities, supporting the diagnosis of acute pulmonary edema (Fig. 2).

Two sets of blood cultures were obtained at admission, and empiric antimicrobial therapy with azithromycin was initiated for suspected community-acquired pneumonia with acute hypoxic respiratory failure. Supplemental oxygen therapy of 2 L nasal cannula and intravenous diuresis were administered for hypoxemia and pulmonary edema. Given the elevation in cardiac biomarkers and renal dysfunction, cardiology and nephrology consultations were obtained. Transthoracic echocardiography demonstrated mild mitral and tricuspid regurgitation with elevated right ventricular systolic pressure, consistent with previously documented pulmonary hypertension. Serial troponins peaked at 0.50 ng/mL before down trending. A repeat comprehensive metabolic panel showed an increase in blood urea nitrogen (47 mg/dL) and creatinine (2.02 mg/dL) suggesting acute kidney injury and diuretics were held per nephrology. Within 24 h of admission, preliminary blood cultures demonstrated gram-positive cocci, prompting the addition of intravenous vancomycin. The patient’s clinical course was complicated by worsening hypoxemia, rising leukocytosis, and subjectively reported new-onset blood-tinged sputum, which was not directly observed by healthcare providers. Repeat BNP measurements revealed elevation with a peak at 1093 pg/mL. There was concern that the patient was septic secondary to bacteremia with pneumonia, resulting in hypoxic respiratory failure with cardiac and renal dysfunction. The patient was transferred to the critical care unit for high-flow oxygen therapy at 40 L with 65% FiO2 and closer hemodynamic monitoring. Antimicrobial coverage was broadened with intravenous aztreonam.

On hospital day three, blood cultures from both sets grew S. canis. A streptococcal organism was initially detected using polymerase chain reaction (PCR) with the BIOFIRE® Blood Culture Identification 2 (BCID2) system (Biomérieux). Species-level identification of S. canis was subsequently confirmed using the VITEK® automated biochemical identification system (Biomérieux). Antimicrobial susceptibility testing performed using the VITEK® platform demonstrated susceptibility to β-lactam antibiotics and intermediate susceptibility to tetracycline. Infectious diseases consultation was obtained for antimicrobial guidance. Following organism identification, additional history revealed that the patient owned a dog and had frequent exposure to the animal’s saliva; specifically, the dog frequently licked her hands and occasionally her face. Although she denied any history of bites or scratches, the infectious disease team postulated that microabrasion caused by the compromised skin barrier secondary to chronic right upper extremity lymphedema may have been a nidus for infection. Given her reported cephalosporin allergy and concern for β-lactam cross-reactivity, a supervised penicillin challenge was performed, which she tolerated without adverse reaction. Antimicrobial therapy was subsequently narrowed to intravenous penicillin G.

Intravenous diuresis was resumed on day five of the hospitalization after improvement in blood urea nitrogen and creatinine levels. Additionally, repeat chest radiographs at this time showed improvement in interstitial opacities suggesting improving pulmonary edema. The patient’s supplemental oxygen needs were reduced to 25 L at 40% FiO2.

The patient required prolonged supplemental oxygen and remained in the critical care unit for nine days before weaning to 2 L of nasal cannula oxygen and transferring to a step-down unit. Serial blood cultures obtained after initiation of targeted therapy demonstrated no growth. Transesophageal echocardiography revealed no evidence of infective endocarditis. Cardiac catheterization showed no obstructive coronary disease, and the troponin elevation was attributed to demand ischemia in the setting of hypoxia and sepsis. After clinical stabilization, the patient was discharged to inpatient rehabilitation, where she completed a total of 13 days of intravenous penicillin G therapy with continued clinical improvement.

Discussion

Streptococcus canis (S. canis) is an opportunistic pathogen commonly found in companion animals, particularly domestic dogs and cats, where it can colonize the skin and mucosal surfaces of asymptomatic hosts [1], [5]. Although reported human infection is rare, colonized animals may serve as a reservoir for zoonotic transmission, particularly in the setting of close household contact [1], [3], [11]. In the present case, the patient owned a dog and reported frequent exposure to the animal’s saliva, supporting a plausible zoonotic source of infection.

Although bacteremia due to S. canis has been described in prior reports [3], [6], [9], many cases involve focal infections such as soft tissue infection, osteoarticular infection, or infective endocarditis [4], [8], [9]. Although sepsis without documented dog bite has been reported [7], this case demonstrates severe cardiopulmonary complications in an elderly patient with significant comorbidities, expanding the recognized clinical spectrum of S. canis infection. The absence of a clear portal of entry highlights the potential for non-traumatic transmission and underscores the importance of considering zoonotic pathogens in systemic infections of unclear origin [7], [11].

The patient’s respiratory failure was most consistent with cardiogenic pulmonary edema precipitated by sepsis. This interpretation is supported by a rising BNP, which increased from mildly elevated at presentation to markedly elevated during clinical deterioration, echocardiographic evidence of elevated right ventricular systolic pressure consistent with pulmonary hypertension, with preserved ejection fraction, and clinical improvement following resumption of intravenous diuresis. Although blood-tinged sputum was subjectively reported by the patient and her spouse, this was not directly observed during hospitalization and may have represented frothy or blood-tinged secretions associated with pulmonary edema rather than true hemoptysis. No sputum studies were obtained, and imaging did not demonstrate focal consolidation to suggest primary bacterial pneumonia. Together, these findings support a predominantly hydrostatic mechanism of pulmonary edema rather than a primary pulmonary infectious or non-cardiogenic inflammatory process. Worsening hypoxia following discontinuation of diuresis, with subsequent improvement after its resumption, further supports a hydrostatic mechanism.

Advanced age and underlying comorbid conditions appear to increase susceptibility to invasive β-hemolytic streptococcal infection, including infection caused by S. canis [3], [10]. Reported risk factors in published cases include malignancy and diabetes mellitus [3], [10]. The patient described here was 73 years old with a history of breast cancer and chronic upper-extremity lymphedema, factors that may have facilitated bacterial entry or impaired local immune defense. Notably, several published cases describe S. canis infection in the absence of animal bites or scratches, suggesting that non-traumatic exposure, such as contact with animal saliva and entry through microabrasions, may be sufficient for transmission [7], [8], [9], [11].

β-lactam antibiotics remain the preferred treatment for streptococcal infections, including those caused by S. canis, and clinical outcomes are generally favorable when appropriate therapy is administered [3], [9], [10]. In this case, antimicrobial therapy was narrowed to penicillin G following organism identification and clinical stabilization. For patients with confirmed severe β-lactam allergy, vancomycin is considered an effective alternative [10]. While S. canis typically remains susceptible to β-lactam agents, resistance to tetracyclines has been reported and appears to be the most frequently observed resistance pattern [1], [5]. In the present case, the isolate demonstrated intermediate susceptibility to tetracycline, consistent with previously described resistance trends. An important clinical lesson from this case is the value of obtaining a detailed exposure history when group G streptococci are identified [2], [7]. In this patient, inquiry regarding household pet exposure occurred only after species-level identification of S. canis. Given the widespread presence of companion animals in households, clinicians should routinely assess animal exposure as part of the infectious disease history, particularly in patients with immunocompromising conditions [11]. Recognition of zoonotic pathogens such as S. canis enables clinicians to provide targeted counseling aimed at minimizing high-risk behaviors and reducing the likelihood of recurrent infection [11].

Conclusion

Streptococcus canis should be considered in cases of group G streptococcal bacteremia presenting with systemic illness, particularly in older adults with comorbid conditions. Although antimicrobial management may not differ from other β-hemolytic streptococci, species-level identification provides important epidemiologic insight and facilitates recognition of zoonotic transmission. Clinicians should routinely assess animal exposure in patients with systemic streptococcal infection to guide appropriate counseling and reduce the risk of recurrent disease.
