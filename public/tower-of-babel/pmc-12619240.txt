Extended-spectrum β-lactamase and carbapenemase-producing bacteria urinary tract infections in adult HIV-positive outpatients at Embu County Referral Hospital, Kenya
Authors: Norah Kagwiria Kiongo, John M. Maingi, Kelvin Wangira Nyongesa, Abednego Moki Musyoki
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAntimicrobial resistance (AMR) poses a significant threat to public health, with the Global Action Plan advocating, among others, for strengthening AMR knowledge and evidence base through surveillance and research and optimizing the use of antimicrobial medicines in human and animal health. However, due to inadequate healthcare infrastructure in many resource-constrained countries, epidemiological data to inform infection prevention interventions and guide antimicrobial policy guidelines in people living with HIV is limited. Here, we assessed the prevalence, antimicrobial resistance, and associated factors of bacterial urinary tract infections among adult HIV-positive outpatients in our study setting.MethodsThis was a hospital-based cross-sectional study among adult HIV-positive outpatients at Embu County Referral Hospital, Kenya, from January to February 2023. We consecutively recruited 142 patients presenting with or without UTI symptoms and excluded those aged below 18. Demographic and clinical data were collected using a pretested questionnaire, and midstream-clean-catch urine samples were processed by standard microbiological methods.ResultsThe overall prevalence of UTI was 9.9% (95% confidence interval (CI): 4.96–14.76% (14/142), with 6.3% symptomatic [95% CI: 2.33–10.35% (9/142)], 3.5% asymptomatic [95% CI: 0.49–6.55% (5/142)], and 4.2% recurrent [95% CI: 0.92–7.53% (6/142)] UTIs. Klebsiella pneumoniae was predominant overall (57.1%, 8/14), and in asymptomatic (80%, 4/5) and recurrent (66.7%, 4/6) UTIs. All the isolates were multidrug-resistant, with 36% (5/14) as meropenem-resistant and 14.3% (2/14) of carbapenem-resistant K. pneumoniae were colistin-resistant. E. coli isolates were susceptible to meropenem, amikacin, ciprofloxacin, and levofloxacin. The lowest resistance in K. pneumoniae was in amikacin (12.5%) and colistin (25%). Notably, 79% (11/14) and 35.7% (4/14) of the isolates were extended-spectrum β-lactamase (ESBL) and carbapenemase producers, respectively. Those with recurrent UTI were at higher odds of having UTI [adjusted odds ratio: 7.37, 95% CI: 1.93—28.07 (p = 0.003)].ConclusionsOur findings highlight a high prevalence of bacterial urinary tract infections, including recurrent ones caused by ESBL-, carbapenemase-producing, and colistin-resistant bacteria among HIV-infected adults. A comprehensive One Health approach is urgently required to reduce the burden of AMR among HIV-infected people through promoting responsible antibiotic use via the existing regulatory frameworks and knowledge transfer, as well as AMR uninterrupted surveillance.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12866-025-03872-1.

Introduction

The World Health Organization (WHO) global estimates show that 39 million people were living with human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) in 2022, and there were 0.63 million HIV-related deaths, with the burden disproportionately higher in the African region [1]. In Kenya, approximately 1.4 million people are living with HIV, with about 18,000 HIV-associated deaths [2]. Despite the widespread use of antiretroviral therapy, HIV infection remains a significant cause of non-AIDS-defining infections, such as urinary tract infections (UTIs), in clinical practice [3, 4]. In HIV-positive people, UTIs are attributable to immune suppression [5], with those having < 200 cell/μl CD4 + counts [6, 7] most likely to acquire the infection. Other factors associated with UTI in HIV-infected persons are female gender [3], chronic diabetes, and history of hospitalization [8].

UTIs are associated with significant disease burden, antimicrobial resistance (AMR), reduced quality of life, and impacts clinical and economic burdens on healthcare systems [9, 10]. Multidrug-resistant (MDR) uropathogens are reportedly high in HIV-infected people, underscoring the need for prescriptions based on antibacterial susceptibility testing results [11]. Notably, HIV-positive people frequently utilize healthcare facilities, predisposing them to the risk of MDR bacterial infection [12–14]. Hospital environments harbor MDR organisms more often than the community setting [15] due to selective pressure from extensive use of antibiotics, inadequate hand hygiene and environmental contamination, among other factors [15].

The WHO classifies MDR strains resistant to third-generation cephalosporins (3GCs) and carbapenems as critical priority pathogens of public health importance to guide research and strategies to prevent and control antimicrobial resistance [16]. Resistance to these drugs is usually via the efflux pump, reduced permeability, altered transpeptidases, and inactivation by β-lactamases [17]. The β-lactamases, including extended-spectrum β-lactamases (ESBL) and carbapenemases, constitute the most common resistance mechanism in 3GCs and carbapenems. The location of these enzymes in plasmids and other mobile elements allows for frequent horizontal gene transfer and spread among pathogenic bacterial species [17].

AMR is fueled mainly by the misuse and overuse of antimicrobials in human medicine and food production [18], and it causes more deaths than malaria and HIV/AIDS combined [19]. Without appropriate actions by health policymakers globally, AMR will be killing 10 million people and causing 100 billion US dollars in economic loss annually by 2050 [20]. In Africa, AMR mortality is disproportionally higher than in other WHO regions [21], perhaps due to high infection burden [22], with suboptimal sanitation, hygiene and infection prevention measures, and inadequate clinical microbiology laboratories likely to play a pertinent role. The Global Action Plan on AMR adopted by the 68th World Health Assembly in May 2015 advocates, among others, for strengthening AMR knowledge and evidence base through surveillance and research and optimizing the use of antimicrobial medicines in human and animal health [18, 23]. However, due to inadequate healthcare infrastructure in many resource-constrained countries, there is limited epidemiological data to inform infection prevention interventions and guide antimicrobial policy guidelines in HIV-infected people. Here, we assessed the prevalence, antimicrobial resistance, and associated factors of bacterial urinary tract infections among adult (≥ 18 years) HIV-positive outpatients at Embu County Referral Hospital, Kenya.

Materials and methods

Study setting, design and population

We conducted this study at Embu County Referral Hospital (ECRH), a 618-bed capacity teaching and referral facility along the Nairobi -Meru highway in Embu town, Kenya. The hospital offers referral services to patients from Embu, Tharaka Nithi, Kirinyaga, Kitui and Machakos counties and provides teaching opportunities for Kenyatta University, Chuka University, Kenya Methodist Univesity, Kenya Medical Training College, and Kyeni Hospital’s Fisenza Nursing School. ECRH operates various clinics, including Pediatrics, Obstetrics/Gynecology, Psychiatry, Ear-Nose-Throat (ENT), Ophthalmology, and a Comprehensive Care Clinic (CCC) for people living with HIV. Embu County is among Kenya’s counties with high HIV burden, with a prevalence of 3.3% [24] when compared with the national HIV prevalence of 3.7% [25]. The CCC offers HIV counselling and testing, as well as patient care and management, and routine laboratory tests including viral load, blood chemistry and tuberculosis. Cryptococcal capsular polysaccharide antigen (CrAG) tests and CD4 counts are based on physician request.

This study was hospital-based and cross-sectional among HIV-positive persons seeking outpatient healthcare services at the ECRH CCC. We consecutively recruited 142 participants between January and February 2023, including patients presenting with or without UTI symptoms and excluding those aged below18 years. The sample size was determined using the Fisher et al. (1998) formula for maximum sample size at a 95% confidence interval [26] and a 10.3% UTI prevalence previously reported among HIV-infected persons in Ethiopia [27]. The study obtained informed consent from each patient and carried out the research project following the Declaration of Helsinki, observing participants’ well-being and ensuring the clinicians in charge of the patients got results timely on all critical findings. The protocol ethical clearance was obtained from the Kenyatta University Ethical Review Committee (PKU/2453/11584), whereas the National Commission for Science and Innovation (Ref No. Ref No: 933594) and ECRH management issued the research permit.

Data collection

This study collected the participant’s demographics using a structured pretested questionnaire (Supplementary file S1) through a face-to-face interview and clinical data from the hospital records. We instructed each participant to collect a midstream-clean-catch freshly voided morning urine sample (20–30 ml) into a sterile leak-proof wide-mouthed screw-capped container (Delta lab, Spain) after cleaning external genitalia with soap and water. The samples were transported to the ECRH Microbiology laboratory in a cool box and processed within one hour of collection [28].

Bacteriuria screening

We screened urine samples for bacteriuria using 10-parameter Mission Biochemical urine dipsticks (ACON lab incl., San Diego, USA) and microscopic examination of gram-stained urine preparation. Samples positive for nitrites and leukocytes in urine dipstick with microscopic evidence of bacteria were considered bacteriuria-positive and subjected to culture [28].

Bacterial isolation, identification and antimicrobial susceptibility testing

The bacteriuria-positive urine samples were further processed using the standard bacteriology methods [28], inoculating 10 µl of the sample on cysteine–lactose electrolyte deficient agar (CLED) (HI Media Laboratories LLC, India) using a sterile calibrated wire loop. The plates were incubated aerobically and overnight at 37 °C, and colonies were counted, with ≥ 105 CFU/mL of single or at most two bacteria considered a significant growth. We identified isolates and tested them for antimicrobial susceptibility per the 2023 Clinical Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) guideline [29] and using the Vitek 2® automated platform (bioMèrieux, Marcy l’. Etoile, France). The broth microdilution (BMD) method was used for colistin susceptibility testing. Escherichia coli (American-type culture collection [ATCC] 25,922) and Klebsiella pneumoniae (ATCC 1705) were used as negative and positive control organisms, respectively.

The study defined carbapenem resistance as resistance to meropenem (≥ 4 μg/ml) and third-generation cephalosporin resistance as resistance to either ceftazidime (≥ 16 μg/ml) or ceftriaxone (≥ 4 μg/ml) or cefotaxime (≥ 4 μg/ml) or cefpodoxime (≥ 8 μg/ml) [29].

Colistin susceptibility testing

We employed the broth microdilution (BMD) method to test for colistin susceptibility, following the CLSI 2023 guidelines. We labelled four tubes containing calcium magnesium Muller Hinton Broth as 0 μg/ml (control), 1 μg/ml, 2 μg/ml, and 4 μg/ml, corresponding to the colistin discs added. The tubes were gently vortexed for 30 min to allow the elusion of colistin from the disc. A 0.5 MacFarland standard equivalent suspension of each test organism was prepared, sequentially added 50 μl to the tubes labelled 0 μg/ml, 1 μg/ml, 2 μg/ml, and 4 μg/ml, and the original inoculum plated on blood agar (BA) using a ten [10] μl loop for purity check. The tubes and plates were incubated at 35 °C for 16–20 h. For the test to be valid, the colonies on the BA plate needed to be pure, with turbidity in the control tube (0 μg/ml). Following the CLSI 2023 guidelines, a minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) of ≤ 2 μg/ml was considered intermediate, while a MIC of ≥ 4 μg/ml was deemed resistant. The guideline does not provide colistin “susceptible” interpretation due to variability in testing methods and clinical outcomes. In this study, E. coli ATCC 25922 and Proteus mirabilis were control organisms.

Screening for carbapenemase production

We utilized the modified carbapenem inactivation method (mCIM) [29] to test the carbapenem-resistant isolates for carbapenemase production. The isolates were placed in 2 ml of Trypticase Soy Broth (TSB) (HiMedia, India) supplemented with meropenem (10 µg) and then left to incubate at 30–35 °C for 4 h. After that, we prepared an E. coli (ATCC 25922) strain suspension equivalent to 0.5 McFarland standard in sterile saline, plated it on Mueller–Hinton agar (MHA), and added a meropenem disk retrieved from the TSB culture of the test organism. Then, we incubated the mixture for 18–24 h at 35 °C. The study considered isolates with a 6–15 mm inhibition zone diameter as carbapenemase producers, whereas those with a zone ≥ 19 mm diameter were non-carbapenemase producers. We used E. coli (ATCC 25922) as the negative- and K. pneumoniae (ATCC 1705) as the positive-control organism [29, 30].

Screening for ESBL production

We tested the third-generation-resistant isolates for ESBL production using the Double Disk Synergy Test (DDST) [31], where a bacterial suspension equivalent to 0.5 McFarland standard was plated on Mueller–Hinton Agar (MHA) and allowed to air dry for 3 min. Subsequently, the plates with antibiotic disks containing cefotaxime (30 µg), ceftazidime (30 µg), and amoxicillin/clavulanic acid (20 µg/10 µg) on the agar at a 30 mm radius distance from each other were incubated overnight at 37 °C in ambient air. An inhibition zone around the cefotaxime and/or ceftazidime that expanded towards the β-lactam inhibitor indicated ESBL production. For quality control, we used K. pneumoniae ATCC 700603 and E. coli ATCC 25922 [31].

Data analysis

This study used Stata version 13 (STATA Corporation, College Station, TX, USA). The data were analyzed for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test, with categorical data captured in frequencies and relative frequencies and continuous data in mean, and presented in figures and tables. The authors also calculated 95% Confidence Intervals (CI) for proportions and considered isolates resistant to at least one antibiotic in three or more antibiotic classes as multidrug-resistant (MDR) [31]. We calculated multiple antibiotic resistance indices (MARI) as a/b, where a = the number of antibiotics the isolate was resistant to, and b = the total number of antibiotics tested [32]. Bivariate models were developed to investigate the association between UTIs and patients’ demographic and clinical characteristics, with results summarized in a table for clarity. Bivariate analysis was not performed for categorical variables with zero (0) cell entries to ensure model stability and better estimation using maximum likelihood estimation (MLE). Variables with p-values ≤ 0.2 from the bivariate analysis were subjected to multivariable analysis using binary logistic regression, removing non-significant interactions to maintain model simplicity and to ensure that variables with marginal significance were not excluded hastily from the analyses. Before performing multivariable analysis, we checked for multicollinearity using the variance inflation factor (VIF) to ensure that the estimates from bivariate models were not affected by inflated variances due to correlated predictors. In this study, all the VIF values were less than 5, indicating a lack of multicollinearity. The statistical significance level was p < 0.05 [95% Confidence Interval (95% CI)], with significant associations bolded in Table 4.

The overall prevalence of UTIs among HIV-positive people in our study setting was 9.9% (95% confidence interval (CI): 4.96- 14.76% (14/142)). Nine patients had symptomatic UTIs (6.3%, 95% CI: 2.33- 10.35% (9/142)), whereas five had asymptomatic UTIs (3.5%, 95% CI: 0.49- 6.55% (5/142)). Antimicrobial susceptibility profiles

Here, we did not present antimicrobial susceptibility profiles of Pseudomonas aeruginosa, for it was a single isolate. E. coli was 100% resistant to sulfamethoxazole-trimethoprim (SXT) and 40% to nitrofurantoin (NIT), whereas K. pneumoniae exhibited 62.5% SXT resistance and 75% resistance to NIT, Table 2. All the K. pneumoniae and E. coli isolates were resistant to extended-spectrum cephalosporins (3rd and 4th generation cephalosporins), with resistance ranging from 75–100%. Multidrug resistance among the study isolates

All the isolates in this study were MDR, with 36% (5/14) of them (1 Pseudomonas aeruginosa and 4 K. pneumoniae) as meropenem-resistant, Table 3. Two of the carbapenem-resistant K. pneumoniae (CRKP) were colistin non-susceptible. Discussion

In this study, the overall prevalence of UTIs among HIV-positive persons was 9.9%, lower than reported by other similar studies in Southern Ethiopia (10.3%) [27], Eastern Ethiopia (18%) [33], and Uganda (26%) [34]. The prevalence of asymptomatic UTIs in the current study (3.5%) was lower than documented among HIV-positive people by Tessema and others (5.4%) in Southern Ethiopia [27], Ngowi et al. (12.3%) in Northern Tanzania [35], Olowe and colleagues (21.1%) in Osogbo, south-western Nigeria [6], Banu and Jyothi (25.7%) [36] and Yadhav and Samreen (36.7%) in india [37]. In our study, symptomatic UTIs (6.3%) prevalence was higher than (4.9%) reported among adult people living with HIV at Haswassa University Comprehensive Specialized Hospital, Hawassa, Southern Esthiopia [27], but lower than (23.2%) documented among HIV-1 infected patients in Poland [3].

In the current study, the prevalence of recurrent UTIs, defined as having two or more acute UTIs within six months or three within a year, was 4.2%. While immunodeficiency alone typically does not lead to UTI recurrence, it has previously been linked to multidrug-resistant bacterial infections [38]. This association may account for the recurrent UTI cases observed in our study, where all the isolates were MDR. Further, recurrent UTIs are more common in sexually active women and those with conditions such as bladder stones, neoplasms, indwelling catheters, stents, foreign bodies, ureteral stenosis or strictures, and inflammatory, fibrotic, or neoplastic conditions affecting the urinary system [39]. For these patients, maximizing personal hygiene, avoiding spermicides, wiping correctly, increasing fluid intake and hydration, and vaginal estrogens, if appropriate, are recommended [39]. Generally, UTIs are among the most common conditions encountered in clinical practice [40]. Compared with HIV-negative persons, those living with HIV are at higher risk of UTI [27, 41, 42] caused by multidrug-resistant organisms due to suppression of their immunity by the virus and frequent exposure to hospital environment [27]. Therefore, evidence-based diagnosis is critical to guide UTI treatment for better clinical care of HIV-infected patients and mitigating the undesirable effects of antibiotic use and misuse.

Klebsiella pneumoniae was the predominant isolate causing UTIs overall and among those presenting with asymptomatic and recurrent UTIs but remained equally distributed with Escherichia coli in symptomatic UTIs. Our findings contradict other studies that reported Enterococci [43], E. coli [27, 33, 44], E. coli and Enterococcus faecalis [45], and Enterococcus spp. and Staphylococcus aureus [35] as the most frequent isolates in patients with HIV disease. The predominant isolate in patients without HIV disease is reportedly Escherichia coli [43]. Even though the spectrum of bacterial uropathogens may not differ between HIV-infected and non-infected persons [5], the epidemiology, especially that of multidrug-resistant ones, is subject to geographical variation, depending on organisms’ inherent or acquired characteristics, infection prevention and control (IPC) policies, sanitation, and antimicrobial stewardship programs in human and animal populations [46]. All K. pneumoniae were MDR and accounted for 80% of CP isolates in our study. K. pneumoniae are well-known for biofilm formation that decreases the host immune response and antimicrobial effectiveness [47]. Further, although we did not determine the virulence characteristics of the study isolates, hypervirulent and MDR pathotypes drive the global dissemination of K. pneumoniae [48, 49], with the prevalence expectedly higher among HIV patients due to the suppression of their immunity [5] and frequent exposure to healthcare facilities and antibiotic therapies [12, 27].

In the current study, E. coli and K. pneumoniae isolates were resistant to first-line drugs for UTIs, including nitrofurantoin (NIT) and sulfamethoxazole-trimethoprim (SXT) [39]. The resistance in E. coli was 100% in SXT and 40% in NIT, whereas K. pneumoniae exhibited 62.5% SXT resistance and 75% resistance to NIT. SXT is unrecommended when the local resistance rate exceeds 20%, while NIT is contraindicated in suspected pyelonephritis (due to poor tissue penetration) or in patients with less than 30 mL/min creatinine clearance [39]. The susceptibility of the other first-line options, fosfomycin and pivmecillinam, were not tested due to unavailability. The second-line options for UTIs are amoxicillin-clavulanate (AMC), cefdinir, cefadroxil, and cefpodoxime (CPD). We tested the isolates against AMC and CPD, with the isolates (E. coli and K. pneumoniae) displaying 100% resistance to CPD. AMC resistance was 60% in E. coli and 62.5% in K. pneumoniae. Together, these findings suggest a declining clinical relevance of first- and second-line drugs for the management of UTIs among HIV-infected patients in our study setting.

Without an appropriate second-line option, the recommended alternatives are fluoroquinolones, including levofloxacin (LXV), ciprofloxacin (CIP) or norfloxacin). In our study, K. pneumoniae isolates exhibited 62.5% to CIP and 37.5% to LVX, while E. coli were 100% susceptible to the two tested fluoroquinolones. However, these drugs are not safe to use during pregnancy and are on the WHO watch list of antibiotics to be opted out whenever possible to minimize the development of quinolone resistance [39, 50]. With no MDR infection, 3GCs such as ceftriaxone or piperacillin-tazobactam are prescribed [39]; however, in our study, all isolates were MDR. Carbapenems (imipenem, meropenem, ertapenem or doripenem) are the most common treatment option for MDR infections [39], but their clinical value is vanishing due to resistance having spread globally [51]. In the current study, 36% of MDR isolates (1 Pseudomonas aeruginosa and 4 Klebsiella pneumoniae) were meropenem-resistant.

Infections caused by carbapenem-resistant (CR) bacteria are increasingly difficult to treat due to limited therapeutic options and are frequently associated with increased morbidity, mortality, and healthcare costs [16]. A triple combination of polymyxin, carbapenem, and rifampin or tigecycline is used widely for infections caused by CR bacteria; however, this treatment strategy faces efficacy uncertainties arising from resistance [30]. Two carbapenem-resistant Klebsiella pneumonia (CRKP) in our study were colistin non-susceptible. The use of antibiotics in farming is among the drug resistance drivers in the modern world, and it is on the rise in Kenya. For instance, a recent study exploring antibiotic use practices and drivers of potential overuse by poultry farmers in Kiambu County, Kenya, found that 13% of the farmers used colistin in poultry feeds [52]. This practice has a far-reaching negative public health implication due to the emergence of colistin resistance, further reducing treatment options for CR bacterial infections.

The multiple antibiotic resistance indices (MARI) greater than 0.2 observed in our study, the highest (69–94%) among colistin-resistant CRKP, indicate that these isolates were from high antibiotic use or ‘high-risk’ source of contamination [53]. Apart from colistin, there are newer treatment options for CR infections, such as imipenem-cilastatin-sulbactam, cefiderocol, ceftazidime-avibactam, eravacycline, ceftolozane-tazobactam, plazomicin, and meropenem-vaborbactam. However, there are use limitations of these newer drugs due to inadequate high-quality clinical data, delayed approval for susceptibility testing methods, the complexity of antibacterial spectra, and high purchase costs [30, 54]. In our study, meropenem, amikacin, ciprofloxacin, and levofloxacin were the best choices for E. coli-associated UTIs but not those caused by K. pneumoniae. The lowest resistance in K. pneumoniae was observed in amikacin (12.5%) and colistin (25%).

In this study, the majority (79%) of isolates predominated by K. pneumoniae were ESBL producers, translating to a UTI prevalence of 7.7%. A higher UTI prevalence caused by ESBLs–producing E. coli was reported among HIV-infected persons by Nwokolo and others in Nigeria (6.9%) [55] and Olaru and colleagues in Zimbabwe (26.8%) [56]. Additionally, Olaru and others showed that ESBL-producing organisms were more common among participants with HIV than in participants without HIV infection. ESBLs are bacterial enzymes phenotypically characterized by the ability to hydrolyze expanded spectrum β-lactam antibiotics and inhibition by β-lactamase inhibitors, particularly clavulanate [31, 57]. In some bacterial species, these enzymes occur naturally, but in many bacteria, they are plasmid-borne, allowing their global widespread and, as such, substantial rise in carbapenems consumption and resistance [57]. ESBL-producing isolates present UTI treatment challenge in the current study setting, considering that some were carbapenemase producers and colistin-resistant. In our study, CP bacteria accounted for 3.5% of UTIs, dominated by K. pneumoniae but with no CP E. coli. Simeneh and others reported a lower prevalence (2.0%) of CP bacterial UTI among adult HIV-positive patients in Southern Ethiopia [58]. Also, distinct from the current study, these authors found carbapenemase production in both K. pneumoniae (9.4%)and E. coli (6.25%) [59]. CP bacterial strains are of significant public health concern because they are frequently multidrug-resistant, and the genes that encode the carbapenemase enzymes are often located on mobile genetic elements, allowing easy transfer between different bacteria and enhancing the global spread of resistance. The CP pathogens found in this study can cause a wide range of difficult-to-treat infections, including bloodstream infections, pneumonia, and urinary tract infections. CP bacteria infections' containment efforts are usually resource-intensive through stringent infection control measures, including surveillance, isolation of infected patients, and rigorous cleaning protocols, which can prove challenging to establish and maintain, especially in resource-constrained settings with inadequate healthcare infrastructure.

In our study, individuals with a history of recurrent UTIs were seven times more likely to have UTIs. Recurrent UTI in females has been linked with a history of a relative having UTI [60, 61], suggesting a genetic component for increased susceptibility to these infections. Other factors previously associated with UTI recurrence are recent 1-month intercourse frequency, 12-month spermicide use and new sex partner during the past year, age at first UTI episode (⩽15 years) and UTI history in the mother [61]. In the current study, however, no other factor was linked to UTI, which could be attributable to the low number of UTI-positive cases (14/142). Hence, multi-centred studies for an in-depth understanding of this crucial health issue are required in our setting and beyond.

This study has some limitations. As a single-centred study with a small sample size, the data obtained may not apply to other hospitals in our area. Therefore, we recommended a multicenter study to determine the epidemiology of UTIs among people living with HIV compared to the general patient population. Additionally, due to financial constraints, we did not analyze the molecular characteristics of the ESBL- and carbapenemase-producing bacteria, which could have provided insight into the molecular resistance mechanisms of these isolates. Nonetheless, our findings underscoring the high rate of MDR isolates that were ESBL and carbapenemase producers and colistin-resistant strains, especially in a setting with limited healthcare infrastructure, are significant.

Conclusions

Our findings highlight a high prevalence of bacterial urinary tract infections, including recurrent ones caused by multidrug-resistant isolates that were ESBL-, carbapenemase-producing, and colistin-resistant among HIV-infected adults with a history of recurrent UTI. A comprehensive One Health approach is essential to reduce the burden of AMR among people living with HIV through promoting responsible antibiotic use via the existing regulatory frameworks and knowledge transfer, as well as AMR uninterrupted surveillance. A multicenter study with a larger population and extended investigation period could bring more robustness in understanding the epidemiology of UTIs in the current study setting.
