Comparison of viral respiratory pathogens circulation among Iranian pilgrims during two large religious mass gatherings: the Hajj and Arbaeen walk 2025
Authors: Akram Sadat Ahmadi, Sevrin Zadheidar, Tasnim Jamalvandi, Fatemeh Saadatmand, Nazanin Zahra Shafiei-Jandaghi, Ghobad Moradi, Mahshid Nasehi, Jila Yavarian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe Hajj and Arbaeen walk pilgrimages are significant religious mass gatherings (MGs) among Muslims. Respiratory tract infections (RTIs) remain a major concern during such events. This study aimed to evaluate the prevalence of respiratory viral infections among Iranian pilgrims during the 2025 Hajj and Arbaeen walk.MethodsThroat swabs were collected from 110 symptomatic Iranian pilgrims returning from these MGs. Molecular techniques were used to detect respiratory viruses.ResultsA total of 60 Hajj pilgrims and 50 Arbaeen walk pilgrims, with an age range 8 to 82 years for the combined groups, participated. Rhinovirus was the most common virus detected among symptomatic pilgrims in both MGs. Notably, the rate of SARS-CoV-2 infection was 1.7% in Hajj pilgrims and 32% in Arbaeen pilgrims. Co-infections of rhinovirus and SARS-CoV-2 were identified in 8% of Arbaeen pilgrims. Additionally, coronavirus OC43/HKU1, influenza A (H3N2), and influenza B virus were detected at low frequencies. No cases of MERS-CoV were found among the 2025 Hajj pilgrims.ConclusionThese findings provide preliminary data on the circulation of respiratory viruses during religious MGs in 2025. Continued surveillance is essential to reduce respiratory infection risks during such mass gatherings.Keywords: Hajj, Pilgrim, Respiratory viruses, Rhinovirus, Arbaeen, COVID-19

Introduction

According to the World Health Organization’s definition, mass gatherings (MGs) are events where large numbers of people assemble in a specific location for a designated purpose during a defined timeframe. Among Muslims, the annual Hajj pilgrimage stands out as the largest Islamic ritual, drawing approximately 3 million pilgrims from over 180 countries to Mecca and Madinah, Saudi Arabia, each year [1–3]. Another significant MG is the Arbaeen walk in Iraq, where an estimated 17–21 million pilgrims walk from Najaf to the holy place of Karbala, south of Baghdad [1, 2]. In the Arbaeen ritual, Shia Muslims gather at the end of the 40 days following Ashura, for the celebration of the martyrdom of the third Shia Muslim Imam, Hossein ibn Ali’s [3].

Religious MGs present a significant risk to global health, as the high-density crowds and extended close contact during the Hajj and Arbaeen walk rituals facilitate the transmission of respiratory pathogens among pilgrims [2, 4–6]. The conditions at MGs elevate the risk of accelerating infectious diseases, including respiratory infections, due to their contagious nature, crowded environments, and the weakened immune systems of fatigued pilgrims [7]. The evidence indicates that over one-third of pilgrims experience respiratory symptoms during the Hajj season, with viral respiratory tract infections (RTIs) occurring more frequently. Since most pilgrims are elderly, those with chronic illnesses may face an increased risk of infection from viral respiratory agents [8]. In a study among 300 pilgrims of the Arbaeen 2023, flu-like illnesses in 53% of pilgrims were reported [9]. The common viral respiratory pathogens for RTIs include influenza A and B viruses, adenovirus, coronavirus, rhinovirus, respiratory syncytial virus (RSV), and human parainfluenza viruses (PIVs) [4]. Given the significant public health risk posed by viral respiratory infections during Hajj and Arbaeen, this epidemiological study aimed to identify the circulation of various viral respiratory infections among pilgrims returning to Iran from Saudi Arabia and Iraq during Hajj and Arbaeen 2025, respectively.

Methods

To screen and investigate the prevalence of viral respiratory infections, throat swabs were collected from symptomatic Iranian pilgrims returning from Saudi Arabia and Iraq to Iran, respectively. These samples were submitted to the National Influenza Center (NIC) at the Virology Department, School of Public Health, Tehran University of Medical Sciences, Iran, during the Hajj season and Arbaeen walk in 2025.

Detection of respiratory viral infections

The nucleic acid extraction from throat swabs was performed using the Zybio PRT DNA & RNA Extraction Kit (Magnetic Bead Method, China) according to the manufacturer’s recommendations. We used the ARVI Screen Real-TM Sacace Biotechnologies Kit (Italy) to identify human B, C, E adenoviruses, bocavirus, rhinovirus, RSV, human metapneumovirus (HMPV), HPIV 1–4, coronaviruses 229E/NL63, and coronaviruses OC43/HKU1. Additionally, detection of influenza viruses A and B, and severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) was carried out using in-house standardized qualitative real-time PCR with CDC-recommended primers and probes. Positive and negative (no template) controls were included in all runs. Additionally, testing for Middle East respiratory syndrome coronavirus (MERS-CoV) was conducted exclusively for the Hajj pilgrims.

Statistical analysis

Demographic data, including age, gender, and infection type, were recorded for all participants. Due to the limited sample size and the descriptive nature of the study, only descriptive statistics were applied. Categorical variables are presented as frequencies and percentages. All statistical analysis were performed using SPSS version 22.

Results

In this study, 60 Hajj pilgrims participated, including 36 males (60%) and 24 females (40%), with a mean age of 58 ± 11.70 years and an age range of 28 to 82 years. More than 80% of the participants were older than 50 years. Overall, 30% of Hajj pilgrims tested positive for at least one respiratory virus. Rhinovirus was detected in 23.3% (14/60), coronavirus OC43/HKU1 in 3.3% (2/60), influenza A (H3N2) in 1.7% (1/60), and SARS-CoV-2 in 1.7% (1/60). No Hajj pilgrims tested positive for MERS-CoV, and no co-infections were detected.

Among the 50 Arbaeen walk pilgrims, 37 were males (74%), and 13 were females (26%), with a mean age of 35.18 ± 16.47 years and an age range of 8 to 68 years, and 22% of pilgrims were over 50 years old. Overall, 78% of Arbaeen pilgrims tested positive for at least one respiratory virus. Rhinovirus was detected in 36% (18/50), SARS-CoV-2 in 32% (16/50), coronavirus 229E/NL63 in 6% (3/50), coronavirus OC43/HKU1 in 2% (1/50), and influenza B virus in 2% (1/50). Four cases (8%) showed co-infection with rhinovirus and SARS-CoV-2. In both recent MGs, the number of male participants exceeded that of females. Discussion

Religious MGs pose a serious risk for spreading respiratory viruses among pilgrims. As a result, this often leads to more hospitalizations after they return home [10]. In 2025, about 86,700 Iranian pilgrims traveled to Saudi Arabia for Hajj, and over 21 million Iranians participated in the Arbaeen walk that same year [11]. In this study, we evaluated the frequency of viral respiratory pathogens among symptomatic Iranian pilgrims during the Hajj and Arbaeen walk in 2025.

Our results showed a high prevalence of rhinovirus in both groups of pilgrims during the 2025 religious MGs. Our findings are in agreement with previous studies, which mainly identified rhinoviruses as highly prevalent among Hajj pilgrims [12–14]. Alsayed et al. examined 185 patients during the 2019 Hajj in Saudi Arabia and noted that rhinovirus was the most commonly detected virus (42.6%), followed by influenza A (H1N1) (8%) [15]. Research from 2017 on Iranian pilgrims reported a high prevalence of rhinovirus (20.19%), including genotypes A21, B91, and HRV-C [6]. However, during the 2022 Hajj season, concurrent with the COVID-19 pandemic, rhinovirus frequency dropped to 3.9% [16]. In contrast, the analysis revealed a higher frequency of rhinovirus during the 2025 Hajj and Arbaeen walk. The decrease in rhinovirus and other respiratory infections observed during the COVID-19 pandemic, compared to the post-pandemic period, appears to be attributable to the effective implementation of non-pharmacological interventions (NPIs). The higher frequency of rhinovirus observed in this study might be influenced by factors such as overcrowding and hygiene practices; however, as these were not directly measured, this interpretation remains speculative and requires confirmation in future studies.

Seasonality is an important factor in respiratory virus transmission. Hajj and Arbaeen shift across seasons due to the lunar calendar, which can affect circulation of infection, types and transmission [1]. In 2025, both events occurred in summer with temperatures up to 40 °C, a period when influenza viruses typically decline and we observed this decrease. In contrast, Yavarian et al. (2013–2016) reported 12.7% influenza prevalence among pilgrims, with influenza A (H3N2) as the predominant strain [3]. Additionally, Iranian Ministry of Health data from 2019 reported 8,333 respiratory illness cases and 106 deaths among returning pilgrims from Iraq between October and December [17, 18], this disparity may be related to seasonal changes and the circulation patterns of seasonal influenza viruses.

Despite low influenza rates in 2025, SARS-CoV-2 infection rose sharply from 1.7% (one case) during Hajj to 32% (16 cases) during Arbaeen. Lami et al. reported cough (25.6%) and nasal discharge (22.6%) as common symptoms among pilgrims [19], highlighting the need for hand hygiene facilities to reduce respiratory infection risks. The observed difference in SARS-CoV-2 prevalence between the two events may reflect differences in population density, healthcare infrastructure, or preventive measures. However, due to the lack of data on these factors and the differences in sampling procedures, no direct comparison can be made between Hajj and Arbaeen regarding the risk of infection. It should be noted that the low prevalence of COVID-19 in Hajj (1.7%) might be related to the fact that vaccination was strongly recommended for Hajj pilgrims, but not for Arbaeen pilgrims. Unfortunately, we did not have access to vaccination data for the pilgrims.

In a systematic review, Goumballa et al. [20] reported SARS-CoV-2 infection rates of 1–15% during Hajj/Umrah (2021–2022) and 3% during 2021 Arbaeen pilgrimage. They noted that transmission is influenced by pilgrim density, viral spread in home/host countries, circulated variants, preventive measures, and immunity. Returning pilgrims from Iraq pose a transmission risk to others, and new variants may add further challenges.

We detected no MERS-CoV cases among Hajj pilgrims in 2025. This finding is consistent with previous reports from Mercier et al. [12], Alfelali et al., and Al-Tawfiq et al., who also found no MERS-CoV infections among pilgrims between 2013 and 2019. However, Yavarian et al. (2014) reported five MERS-CoV cases in Iran, though no further cases were detected among returning pilgrims in subsequent studies [14, 21, 22].

Endemic coronavirus prevalence was low in both groups: 6% for 229E/NL63 and 2% for OC43/HKU1 in Arbaeen pilgrims, and 3.3% for OC43/HKU1 in Hajj pilgrims. These findings align with Barasheed et al. (2% OC43/229E in 2013 Hajj pilgrims) [23]. However, higher frequencies have been reported by Hashem et al. (NL63: 15.5%, 229E: 9.2%) [24], Mercier et al. (229E: 12%, OC43: 8.4%) [12], and Hoang et al., who noted increased 229E/OC43 circulation but no SARS-CoV-2 [10]. Rucinski et al. (2020) reported high circulation of endemic coronaviruses (OC43, HKU1, 229E, and NL63) during winter and early spring [25]. This seasonal pattern may explain the low prevalence observed in our study, as both events occurred during the warm months of the year.

Several limitations should be acknowledged in this study. The sample size was relatively small, which limits the generalizability of our findings. In addition, we lacked access to detailed clinical data and vaccination history of the pilgrims. Additionally, the diagnostic kit used in this study could not differentiate between coronavirus OC43 and HKU1, nor between coronavirus 229E and NL63. Therefore, these viruses were reported as coronavirus OC43/HKU1 and coronavirus 229E/NL63.

Conclusions

Our findings indicate that rhinovirus was the most common virus among pilgrims during the 2025 Hajj and Arbaeen gatherings, while SARS-CoV-2 was more prevalent among Arbaeen pilgrims. These results highlight the importance of continuous monitoring and surveillance of returning pilgrims to their home countries. Despite the small sample size, this study provides preliminary data that may help plan future public health strategies to reduce respiratory infection risks during mass religious MGs.
