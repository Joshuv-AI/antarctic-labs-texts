Some generic properties of processes
Benjamin Weiss

Abstract
. For a given ergodic measure preserving transformation T of a standard measure space ( X , ℬ , μ ) (X,\mathcal{B},\mu) each finite labelled partition defines an ergodic stationary process. There is a complete metric on the space of partitions which is separable. Various generic properties of these processes will be given. For example: 1. The generic partition defines a process that is not Rosenblatt mixing. 2. If T is a K-automorphism that is not Bernoulli then the generic partition is also K but not Bernoulli. Extensions to the relative setting and to actions of amenable groups will also be discussed.



1.
Introduction

The study of generic properties of measure preserving transformations goes back to the early days of ergodic theory, and the first results are clearly
exposed in the chapter
on Category in the classic book of Paul Halmos
[
H
]
. More recent results can be found, for example in
[
GTW2
]
,
[
AGTW
]
and
[
Sc
]
. In this note I shall
take up the study of generic properties of the class of finite valued processes that are defined by one fixed ergodic measure preserving system
(X,\mathcal{B},\mu,T)
. Such processes are defined
by labelled partitions of the measure space.

With the metric on sets defined by the measure of the symmetric difference between two sets, one defines a metric
on the space of labelled partitions with the same number of elements with respect to which the space becomes a Polish space. The metric that this induces on the processes
defined by these partitions is the well-known d-bar metric. My study of these generic properties was motivated by the open problem: Does Rosenblatt mixing imply Bernoulli (for the definition see
the next section). Instead of an explicit construction I thought that perhaps this can be true generically. Instead I found that the opposite is the case, namely for any ergodic system
the generic process is not Rosenblatt mixing. This shows that the converse implication fails generically as explained in the next section.

In general, any process defined by a partition defines a factor system. The standard mixing properties descend to factors. On the other hand a system can fail
to have a mixing property while it might have many factors that do. For example any K-system has many Bernoulli factors even if the system itself is not Bernoulli.
However, we will see in Section 3 that the generic partition of a non Bernoulli system defines a non Bernoulli process.

Continuing this study revealed that
a similar result holds for strong, mild and weak mixing as detailed in
\lx@sectionsign 4
. In
\lx@sectionsign 5
these results will be extended to the
to the relative setting, and in
\lx@sectionsign 6
to
actions of countable amenable groups.

Perhaps a more basic property of a finite partition is that of being a generator.
The results in section 4 show that for a zero entropy ergodic transformation the subsets
A
such that the partition
\{A,X\setminus A\}
is a generator, is generic. In positive entropy
the continuity of the entropy shows that a similar statement cannot hold. In
\lx@sectionsign 7
I will give two kinds of extensions of this result to positive entropy.
In the first, one fixes an arbitrary factor
\mathcal{B}_{0}
of full entropy and then a generic partition together with
\mathcal{B}_{0}
generates. In the second, one restricts to the partitions
that have full entropy, which is a closed set, and in this closed set, the generators form a generic set. These results are analogous to the fact that for positive entropy systems the generic extension
is isomorphic to the to the base, see
[
AGTW
]
.
As a general reference for the standard facts that I will make use of see
[
R
]
and
[
Sh
]
.

2.
Rosenblatt mixing

A stationary stochastic process
\{X_{n}\}
satisfies the Rosenblatt mixing condition if the dependence coefficient

\alpha(n)
defined by

\alpha(n)=\textrm{sup}\{|\mathbb{P}(A\cap B)-\mathbb{P}(A)\mathbb{P}(B)|:A\in\mathcal{F}\{X_{i}:i<0\};\hskip 5.69046ptB\in\mathcal{F}\{X_{i}:i\geq n\}

tends to zero as
n
tends to infinity.

Here
\mathcal{F}\{X_{i}:i\leq 0\}
denotes the
\sigma
-algebra generated by the random variables
\{X_{i}:i<0\}
and similarly for

\mathcal{F}\{X_{i}:i\geq n\}
.

Such processes are also called
\alpha
-mixing. Nathaniel Martin showed in
[
M
]
that if the decay is rapid enough then the process is isomorphic to a Bernoulli shift.
Somewhat earlier, Meir Smorodinsky in
[
Sm
]
exhibited an example of a process that was isomorphic to a Bernoulli shift but was not Rosenblatt mixing.
His proof relied on an unpublished result of Harry Kesten. In
[
B
]
Bradley gave a detailed proof of Kesten’s result. In this section we will
show that non-Rosenblatt mixing processes are in fact generic in any ergodic system, in particular in Bernoulli shifts. Since factors of Bernoulli shifts are also
Bernoulli this gives a plethora of examples of non-Rosenblatt mixing transformations that are Bernoulli.

Let
(X,\mathcal{B},\mu,T)
be an aperiodic ergodic process. For simplicity we will work in the space of two valued processes but the argument will work just as well for
finite valued processes. Any function
f:X\rightarrow\{0,1\}
defines a stationary process by setting
\{X_{n}(x)=f(T^{n}x)\}
. With the

L_{1}
-metric these functions form a complete separable metric space
Z
. We shall show that the set of functions that define non-Rosenblatt mixing processes
contains a dense
G_{\delta}
subset of this space.

It will be convenient to identify elements
f\in Z
with the partition

\mathcal{P}=\{P_{0},P_{1}\}

they define where
P_{i}=f^{-1}(i)
. We will use the notation

\mathcal{P}_{k}^{l}=\bigvee_{i=k}^{l}T^{-i}\mathcal{P}
. Define now the subsets:

U_{n}=\{f\in Z:\exists\hskip 5.69046ptk\in\mathbb{N},\hskip 5.69046pt\textrm{and sets}\hskip 5.69046ptC\in\mathcal{P}_{-k}^{0},\hskip 5.69046ptD\in\mathcal{P}_{n}^{n+k}

\textrm{such that}\hskip 28.45274pt|\mu(C\cap D)-\mu(C)\mu(D)|>\frac{1}{10}\}.

These sets are clearly open and if
f\in\bigcap U_{n}
then clearly the process that
f
defines is not Rosenblatt mixing. It thus remains to show, and this is of course the more difficult
part of the proof, that each
U_{n}
is dense.
For this we will use a version of the Rokhlin lemma which is perhaps not so widely known
and so we will sketch a proof in the ergodic case.

Lemma 1
.

If
(X,\mathcal{B},\mu,T)
is aperiodic and ergodic then for any
N
one can find a measurable set
B
such that
the sets
\{T^{i}B:0\leq i<N\}
are disjoint while
\mu(\bigcup_{i=0}^{N}T^{i}B)=1
.

Proof:

By the aperiodicity we can find a set
A
of positive measure so that
the sets
\{T^{i}A:0\leq i<N^{2}\}
are pairwise disjoint. Let
r_{A}(x)
be the time of the first return of a point
x\in A
to the set
A
. By assumption

r_{A}(x)\geq N^{2}
and we partition
A
into
A_{k}=\{x\in A;r_{A}(x)=k\}
. Each column above an
A_{k}
can be partitioned into disjoint consecutive blocks of size

N
and
N+1
since the height is at least
N^{2}
. Indeed writing the height
H\geq N^{2}
as
H=qN+r
with
r<N
and
q\geq N-1
we can use
r
blocks of size
N+1
and then
continue with blocks of size
N
until we exhaust the column of height
H
. By ergodicity these columns exhaust the measure of the space and we define
B
to be the first level
in each of these blocks. Clearly
B
will have the desired properties.
\Box

This decomposition of the space is called a
(B,N)
-
Kakutani-Rohlin
tower, or briefly a

(B,N)
-KR tower.

We return now to the proof that each
U_{n}
is dense.
Suppose that we are given a function
f\in Z
with a corresponding partition
\mathcal{P}
, a
U_{n}
and
\epsilon>0
. Choose

N>n
and also such that
\frac{1}{N}<\frac{\epsilon}{10}
. let
B
be the base of an
(B,3N^{2})
-tower. We modify
\mathcal{P}
to
\mathcal{Q}
in two steps.

Step 1
. Put in
Q_{0}
all points in the first
N
levels of the tower, i.e.
\cup_{i=0}^{N-1}T^{i}B\subset Q_{0}
, then put
T^{N}B
in
Q_{1}
and the next
N
levels again in
Q_{0}
,

\cup_{i=N+1}^{2N}T^{i}B\subset Q_{0}
.

Step 2
. All levels of height
k\frac{N}{2}
with
k>4
are put in
Q_{1}
.

On all the other levels we put
x\in Q_{i}\hskip 5.69046pt\textrm{if and only if}\hskip 5.69046ptx\in P_{i}
.

Clearly the resulting partition
\mathcal{Q}
differs from
\mathcal{P}
by at most
\epsilon
. It remains to show that
\mathcal{Q}\in U_{n}
. The purpose of the modification that we made was to ensure that
the base
B
is now measurable with respect to
\bigvee_{i=a}^{b}T^{-i}\mathcal{Q}
for all
a<b
such that
b-a>3N^{2}+2N
. This is because the string
s=0^{N}10^{N}
occurs
along the
\mathcal{Q}
-name of
\mu
-a.e. point uniquely with gaps either
3N^{2}
or
3N^{2}+1
apart. To see that indeed
\mathcal{Q}\in U_{n}
in the definition of
U_{n}
take
k=N^{2}
and define the sets

C
and
D
as follows:

C=\{x:T^{i}(x)\in B\hskip 5.69046pt\textrm{for some}\hskip 5.69046pti\in[-N^{2},-2N)\}

and

D=\{x:T^{i}(x)\in B\hskip 5.69046pt\textrm{for some}\hskip 5.69046pti\in[n+2N+N^{2}]\}.

Each of these sets has measure slightly less than
\frac{1}{3}
while since
n<N
their intersection is empty. It follows that
\mathcal{Q}\in U_{n}
and this concludes the proof of the following
theorem:

Theorem 1
.

In any ergodic system
(X,\mathcal{B},\mu,T)
for a generic set of two valued functions the process that they define is not Rosenblatt mixing.

A similar result is valid for finite valued processes with a similar proof.

3.
K-processes that are not Bernoulli

Suppose now that
(X,\mathcal{B},\mu,T)
is K-system. By Sinai’s theorem there are many partitions that define Bernoulli shifts. Recall that we call a process
Bernoulli
if it is
isomorphic to an independent process. Don Ornstein showed that all factors of a Bernoulli process are Bernoulli. The fact that a d-bar limit of Bernoulli processes is also Bernoulli
implies that the collection of Bernoulli processes in any system is closed in the space of partitions. If
(X,\mathcal{B},\mu,T)
is not isomorphic to a Bernoulli system then we shall show that
those partitions that define a non-Bernoulli process are generic. To prove this, what we have to show is that the non-Bernoulli partitions are dense since we already know that
the set of partitions generating non-Bernoulli factors is open. We will need the weak-Pinsker
property that was established by Tim Austin
[
A
]
a few years ago.
The weak-Pinsker property asserts that if
(X,\mathcal{B},\mu,T)
is a positive entropy system then for any
\epsilon>0
there are
independent factors
(X,\mathcal{B}_{i},\mu,T)
for
i=0,1
such that:
(i)
the entropy of
(X,\mathcal{B}_{0},\mu,T)
is less than
\epsilon
; (ii)
(X,\mathcal{B}_{1},\mu,T)
is Bernoulli; and (iii)
\mathcal{B}=\mathcal{B}_{0}\bigvee\mathcal{B}_{1}
.

It follows that if
(X,\mathcal{B},\mu,T)
is non-Bernoulli then
(X,\mathcal{B}_{0},\mu,T)
is necessarily non-Bernoulli.

Theorem 2
.

If the ergodic system
(X,\mathcal{B},\mu,T)
has positive entropy and is not Bernoulli then the non-Bernoulli partitions form a dense open set
in the space of partitions.

Proof:

We are given a partition
\mathcal{P}
of
X
,

\epsilon>0
and our goal is to find a partition
\mathcal{P}^{\prime}
that is within
\epsilon
of
\mathcal{P}
and is not Bernoulli. We apply Austin’s theorem to
find a non-Bernoulli factor
B_{0}
with entropy
h(X,\mathcal{B}_{0},\mu,T)=h<\frac{\epsilon}{10}
.

By a result of Manfred Denker
[
D
]
there is a finite generating partition

\mathcal{Q}
for
\mathcal{B}_{0}
such that the subshift defined by
\mathcal{Q}
is strictly ergodic and hence has
topological entropy equal to
h
. This means that if we denote by
\mathcal{C}\subset\bigvee_{i=0}^{n-1}T^{-i}\mathcal{Q}
those atoms that have positive
measure then for
n
sufficiently large
|\mathcal{C}|<2^{\frac{\epsilon}{5}n}
.

Fix now some
N
such that
\frac{1}{N}<\frac{\epsilon}{10}
and an
n
so that
\frac{N}{n}<\frac{\epsilon}{20}
.
Let now
B_{0},B_{1}
be the bases of an
\{n,n+1\}
-KR tower.
Partition the base
B_{0}
by
\bigvee_{i=0}^{n-1}T^{-i}\mathcal{Q}
and
B_{1}
by
\bigvee_{i=0}^{n}T^{-i}\mathcal{Q}
. If
A\subset B_{0}
is an atom of this partition
we modify
\mathcal{P}
on the levels
\{T^{i}A:0\leq i\leq n-1\}
as follows. The first
N
levels are put in
P_{0}^{\prime}
, the next level is put in
P_{1}^{\prime}
, the next
N
levels are put in
P_{0}^{\prime}
and then
every
\frac{N}{2}
-th level is put in
P_{1}^{\prime}
. Now let
\pi:\mathcal{C}\rightarrow\{0,1\}^{\frac{\epsilon}{5}n}
be a one to one map and use this map to relabel
the next
\frac{\epsilon}{5}n
free levels (i.e. levels that haven’t been modified in the first part of the argument) to encode the
\mathcal{Q}-n
-name of the atom
A
.
For all other levels
T^{i}A
we set
P_{j}^{\prime}=P_{j}
.
The columns above the base
B_{1}
are
treated similarly except that the
N
is replaced by
2N
so that we can distinguish between
B_{0}
and
B_{1}
.

Clearly
\mathcal{P}^{\prime}
differs from
\mathcal{P}
by less than
\delta
and given
\mathcal{P}^{\prime}
-name of a point one can reconstruct uniquely the
\mathcal{Q}
-name so that the process defined by
\mathcal{P}^{\prime}
is not
Bernoulli.
\Box

4.
Mixing properties

Recall the basic mixing properties of an ergodic system
(X,\mathcal{B},\mu,T)
: weak mixing (WM), mild mixing (MM), strong mixing (SM)and Kolmogorov processes (or, equivalently
completely positive entropy (CPE) systems). It is straightforward to see that each of these properties
descends to factors. It follows that if a system has one of these properties then all partitions of the space define processes that have the same property. What we shall show is, that if a system
fails to have one of these properties then for an open dense set of partitions the process defined by the partition fails to have that property.

The first thing to observe is that each of these mixing properties is closed in the d-bar metric. For K-processes this is exactly (
[
R
]
Exercise 7.5).
In our situation, where all the processes are coming from partitions of a fixed system this is fairly easy to see and here is a sketch of the proof.

We are given a sequence of finite partitions
\{\mathcal{P}_{n}\}
that converge to
\mathcal{P}
such that each one defines a K-process. By lumping atoms, which preserves the K-property
we may assume that the number of atoms of the
\mathcal{P}_{n}
is the same as that of
\mathcal{P}
. We now use the Rokhlin metric on partitions given by
\rho(\mathcal{Q},\mathcal{R})=H(\mathcal{Q}|\mathcal{R})+H(\mathcal{R}|\mathcal{Q})
.
If
C
is any set in the
\sigma
-algebra generated by
\mathcal{P}
our task is to show that the entropy of the partition
\mathcal{C}=\{C,X\setminus C\}
is positive. Now for a small enough

\epsilon
we find a
C_{0}\subset\bigvee_{i=-N}^{+N}T^{i}\mathcal{P}
such that,
setting
\mathcal{C}_{0}=\{C_{0},X\setminus C_{0}\}

\rho(\mathcal{C},\mathcal{C}_{0})<\epsilon
. Since the
\mathcal{P}_{n}
converge to
\mathcal{P}
if
n
is sufficiently large

\rho(\bigvee_{i=-N}^{+N}T^{i}\mathcal{P}_{n},\bigvee_{i=-N}^{+N}T^{i}\mathcal{P})<\epsilon
.

Letting
C_{n}
be the subset of
\bigvee_{i=-N}^{+N}T^{i}\mathcal{P}_{n}
that corresponds to
C_{0}
it follows that, setting
\mathcal{C}_{n}=\{C_{n},X\setminus C_{n}\}
we have

\rho(\mathcal{C}_{n},\mathcal{C}_{0})<\epsilon
.

Now the fact that
\mathcal{P}_{n}
defines a K-process implies that the process entropy
h(T^{k},\mathcal{C}_{n})
tends to
H(\mathcal{C}_{n})
as
k
tends to infinity.
Since process entropy is a Lipschitz function in the Rokhlin metric if
\epsilon
is sufficiently small this implies that process entropy of

\mathcal{C}
is positive.

In
[
Sh
]

it is shown that both ergodicity (Theorem I.9.15) and mixing (Theorem I.9.17) are closed in the d-bar metric. That weak mixing is closed follows from the characterization
of weak mixing as the ergodicity of the product system. We have not found a reference in the literature to the fact that mild mixing is closed in d-bar. This is not so obvious from the usual
definition of mild mixing as the absence of non-trivial rigid factor. However, there is an equivalent definition using the notion of IP*-convergence which can be found in (
[
F
]
Proposition 9.22).
With this definition it is quite straightforward to extend the proof of the closure of mixing in
[
Sh
]
to mild mixing. Jean-Paul Thouvenot pointed out that yet another proof can be based on the
characterization of mild-mixing as being disjoint from all rigid transformations. This characterization can be found in (
[
G1
]
Corollary 8.16).

The next observation is that the failure of any one of these properties is witnessed by the existence of some special factor that has zero entropy. For weak mixing this is the Kronecker factor.
For mild mixing this is a rigid factor which necessarily has zero entropy. For non-K processes this is the Pinsker factor. These are all well known. For non-mixing this is a
result of François Parreau
[
P
]
. He showed that if a system is not mixing then it has a factor which is disjoint from all mixing transformations. As such it must have zero entropy.
An exposition of Parreau’s theorem was given by Eli Glasner (see
[
G2
]
).

Our result will now follow from the next two propositions.

Proposition 1
.

If
\mathcal{Q}
is any fixed partition then the set of partitions

\mathcal{P}
such that
\mathcal{Q}\subset\bigvee_{-\infty<i<+\infty}T^{i}\mathcal{P}
is a
G_{\delta}
.

Proof:

Define for fixed
k,n
the set:

U(k,n)=\{\mathcal{P}:\hskip 5.69046pt\exists\mathcal{Q}_{0}\subset\bigvee_{i=-n}^{n}T^{i}\mathcal{P}\hskip 5.69046pt\textrm{such that}\hskip 5.69046ptd(\mathcal{Q}_{0},\mathcal{Q})<\frac{1}{k}\}.

These sets are clearly open and if
\mathcal{P}\in\bigcap_{1<k<\infty}\bigcup_{n}U(k,n)
then
\mathcal{P}
generates a factor containing
\mathcal{Q}
.
\Box

Proposition 2
.

Let
(X,\mathcal{B},\mu,T)
be an ergodic system and
\mathcal{P}=\{P_{0},P_{1},...P_{a}\}
be a partition of
X
. Suppose that
\mathcal{B}_{0}
is a factor sub-
\sigma
-algebra. If the factor has zero entropy then
there exists a finite generating partition
\mathcal{Q}
for the factor
\mathcal{B}_{0}
such that
for any
0<\epsilon
, there is a partition
\mathcal{P}^{\prime}
satisfying:

(1)

d(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{P}^{\prime})<\epsilon
, and

(2)

\mathcal{Q}\subset\bigvee_{-\infty}^{\infty}T^{-i}\mathcal{P}^{\prime}
.

Proof:

The proof is essentially the same as the proof of Theorem
2
. The partition
\mathcal{Q}
in the assertion is the one we get from Denker’s result.

\Box

The following theorem summarizes what we have done.

Theorem 3
.

Let
(X,\mathcal{B},\mu,T)
be an ergodic system.

(1)

If the system is not weakly mixing then the set of partitions
\mathcal{P}
such that the process they define is not weakly mixing is
both dense and open. In addition, the set of partitions that generate a factor containing the Kronecker factor is a dense
G_{\delta}
.

(2)

If the system is not mildly mixing then the set of partitions
\mathcal{P}
such that the process they define is not mildly mixing is
both dense and open. In addition, for any rigid factor
\mathcal{B}_{0}
the set of partitions that generate a factor containing
\mathcal{B}_{0}
is a dense
G_{\delta}
.

(3)

If the system is not mixing then the set of partitions
\mathcal{P}
such that the process they define is not mixing is
both dense and open. In addition, for any Parreau factor
\mathcal{B}_{0}
the set of partitions that generate a factor containing
\mathcal{B}_{0}
is a dense
G_{\delta}
.

(4)

If the system is not Kolmogorov then the set of partitions
\mathcal{P}
such that the process they define is not Kolmogorov is
both dense and open. In addition, the set of partitions that generate a factor containing the Pinsker algebra is a dense
G_{\delta}
.

Proof:

The fact that the various non-mixing partitions form open sets was discussed above. Proposition
2
shows that they are dense. For the second part we use in addition
Proposition
1
.

\Box

Remark 1
.

It follows from the theorem that for a zero entropy system for a generic collection of subsets
A
the partition
\{A,X\setminus A\}
is a generator.
For positive entropy this of course cannot be true. However if
(X,\mathcal{B},\mu,T)
has finite positive entropy
h
and we fix any full entropy factor defined by an invariant
\sigma
-algebra
\mathcal{B}_{0}
then a generic partition
\mathcal{P}
together with
\mathcal{B}_{0}
generates
\mathcal{B}
. (see the next section).

5.
The relative theory

When the system
(X,\mathcal{B},\mu,T)
has a factor
(Y,\mathcal{C},\nu,S)
, i.e there is a measurable mapping
\pi:X\rightarrow Y
such that
\pi\circ\mu=\nu
and
\pi T=S\pi
there
are well studied notions of relative weak-mixing (RWM), relative Kolmogorov (RK) and relative Bernouli (RB). It will be convenient to identify the factor
with
\pi^{-1}\mathcal{C}
which is a
T
-invariant sub-
\sigma
-algebra of
B
, and henceforth
\mathcal{C}
will be used to denote this. For each of these properties the analogue of
our previous results would be that if the extension
(X,\mathcal{B},\mu,T)
fails to have that property relative to the factor
(Y,\mathcal{C},\nu,S)
then the process defined by a generic partition
\mathcal{P}

of
X
also fails to have that property relative to
(Y,\mathcal{C},\nu,S)
. What we mean by that is that system formed by joining
\bigvee_{-\infty<i<+\infty}T^{i}\mathcal{P}
to
\mathcal{C}

the resulting extension of
(Y,\mathcal{C},\nu,S)
fails to have that property relative to
(Y,\mathcal{C},\nu,S)
.

As before, the first thing to check would be that the partitions that define processes that do satisfy one of these relative properties form a closed subset
in the space of partitions. For relative weak mixing this follows easily from the definition RWM as the ergodicity of the relative product of
(X,\mathcal{B},\mu,T)
with itself over the factor
(Y,\mathcal{C},\nu,S)
.
For relative Bernoulli this follows from Prop. 7 in Jean-Paul’s foundational work on the relative Bernoulli theory
[
T
]
.

For relative K a brief proof can be given based on the following characterization of relative K which can be found in
[
GTW1
]
.
An extension
(X,\mathcal{B},\mu,T)
of
(Y,\mathcal{C},\nu,S)
is relatively K if and only if it is relatively disjoint (over
(Y,\mathcal{C},\nu,S)
) from every extension that has
relative entropy equal to zero.

What is needed in order to prove that the relevant open set is dense is a relative version of Proposition
2
. However, since we are not aware of a relative analogue
of the result of Denker that was used in the proof of that proposition we will have to modify
the proof.

Proposition 3
.

Let
(X,\mathcal{B},\mu,T)
be an ergodic system which is an extension of
(Y,\mathcal{C},\nu,S)
by the factor map
\pi
, and let
\mathcal{P}=\{P_{0},P_{1},...P_{a}\}
be a partition of
X
.
Suppose that
\mathcal{Q}
is a partition of
X
such that the process defined by
\mathcal{Q}
has zero entropy relative to
\pi^{-1}(\mathcal{C})
. Then
for any
0<\epsilon
and
0<\delta
, there are partitions
\mathcal{P}^{\prime}
and
\mathcal{Q}_{0}
satisfying:

(1)

d(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{P}^{\prime})<\epsilon
,

(2)

d(\mathcal{Q}_{0},\mathcal{Q})<\delta
, and

(3)

\mathcal{Q}_{0}\subset\pi^{-1}(\mathcal{C})\bigvee_{-\infty}^{\infty}T^{-i}\mathcal{P}^{\prime}
.

Proof:

The proof is similar to the proof of Proposition
2
except that instead of the usual Shannon-McMillan theorem we need to use the relative
Shannon-McMillan theorem (see
[
KW
]
for an appropriate version) and carry out the construction on each fiber
\pi^{-1}(y)
. In addition since we don’t have any uniformity for the partition

\mathcal{Q}
we have another error since the fact that
\mathcal{Q}
has relative zero entropy only means that on typical fibers we can cover only most of the fiber by an
exponentially small number of atoms from
\bigvee_{i=0}^{n-1}T^{-i}\mathcal{Q}
.

We leave this routine modification to the reader.
\Box

Remark 2
.

A slightly more careful argument will show that the assumption of relative zero entropy for
\mathcal{Q}
can be replaced by the assumption that its
relative entropy
h
satisfies
h<\epsilon
.

We can now state the main result of this section.

Theorem 4
.

Let
(X,\mathcal{B},\mu,T)
be an ergodic system with a factor defined by
\mathcal{C}\subset\mathcal{B}
.

(i)
If the system is not weakly mixing relative to
\mathcal{C}
then the set of partitions
\mathcal{P}
such that the process they define is not weakly mixing
relative to
\mathcal{C}
is
both dense and open.

(ii)
If the system is not Kolmogorov relative to
\mathcal{C}
then the set of partitions
\mathcal{P}
such that the process they define is not Kolmogorov
relative to
\mathcal{C}
is
both dense and open.

(iii)
If the system is not Bernoulli relative to
\mathcal{C}
then the set of partitions
\mathcal{P}
such that the process they define is not Bernoulli
relative to
\mathcal{C}
is both dense and open.

Proof:

(i)
If the extension is not relatively weakly mixing there is a non-trivial intermediate factor which is a distal extension of
(Y,\mathcal{C},\nu,S)
. This distal extension has
zero entropy relative to
(Y,\mathcal{C},\nu,S)
. By the relative finite generator theorem (see
[
KW
]
and
[
DP
]
) there is a finite partition
\mathcal{Q}
that generates this distal extension
over
\mathcal{C}
and has zero entropy relative to
\mathcal{C}
. By the openness of the set of partitions that generate non RWM extensions, there
is some positive
\delta
such that if
\mathcal{Q}_{0}
is within
\delta
of
\mathcal{Q}
then
\mathcal{Q}_{0}
also generates a non RWM extension. Given an arbitrary partition
\mathcal{P}

and an arbitrary
\epsilon
we can apply Proposition
3
to find a
\mathcal{P}^{\prime}
within
\epsilon
of
\mathcal{P}
and a
\mathcal{Q}_{0}
within
\delta
of
\mathcal{Q}
such that
the extension generated by
\mathcal{P}^{\prime}
contains
\mathcal{Q}_{0}
and is therefore non RWM. This shows that the set is also dense as required.

(ii)
This proof is the same as case (i) because if the extension is not Kolmogorov there is a non-trivial intermediate factor with zero entropy.

(iii)
In this case we must begin with the partition
\mathcal{P}
and a positive
\epsilon
. By the relative weak-Pinsker property ( see
[
A
]
) there is an
intermediate extension
\mathcal{C^{\prime}}
with relative entropy
h<\epsilon
such that
(X,\mathcal{B},\mu,T)
is relatively Bernoulli over
\mathcal{C^{\prime}}
. We can now apply
Remark
2
and conclude the proof as in case (i).
\Box

6.
Amenable groups

There is a well developed theory of entropy and Bernoulli systems for arbitrary countable amenable groups
G
(see
[
OW
]
)and Theorem
2
makes good sense for any such group. In this section we shall extend
Theorem
2
to this setting. For the first part, namely that the partitions that define a Bernoulli process form a closed set, we use the fact that the Bernoulli processes are characterized by
being Finitely Determined, just as in the case of
\mathbb{Z}
. It follows from (
[
OW
]
III. Theorem 4) that these processes are closed under d-bar limits and thus the non-Bernoulli
partitions form an open set.

In the proof of the theorem for
\mathbb{Z}
we used strictly ergodic partitions. While there is an extension of this result to
amenable groups by Alain Rosenthal
[
Ro
]
we prefer to give a proof which does not rely on this. For clarity we will first describe this alternative proof for
\mathbb{Z}
.

Once again we start with a partition
\mathcal{P}=\{P_{0},P_{1},...P_{a}\}
and an
\epsilon>0
.
Next by the weak Pinsker property we can find some non Bernoulli factor that has entropy equal to
h<\frac{\epsilon}{2}
. By Krieger’s generator theorem
this factor has a finite generating partition
\mathcal{Q}
, and this
\mathcal{Q}
is of course non Bernoulli.
Since the non Bernoulli partitions are open there is some
\delta>0
such that any partition that is within
\delta
of
\mathcal{Q}

is also non Bernoulli. We will now show how to modify
\mathcal{P}
by less than
\epsilon
to obtain a
\mathcal{P}^{\prime}
in such a way that the process generated by
\mathcal{P}^{\prime}
will have as a factor
a partition
\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}
with
d(\mathcal{Q},\mathcal{Q}^{\prime})<\delta
. This will show that
\mathcal{P}^{\prime}
is non Bernoulli and
d(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{P}^{\prime})<\epsilon
which concludes the proof.

By the Shannon-McMillan theorem if
n
is sufficiently large there is a collection
\mathcal{G}
of atoms of
\bigvee_{i=0}^{n-1}T^{-i}\mathcal{Q}
such that:
(i)
\mu(\cup\mathcal{G})>1-\frac{\delta}{10}
; and (ii)
|\mathcal{G}|<2^{\frac{\epsilon}{2}n}
. Choose now some
N
such that
\frac{1}{N}<\frac{\epsilon}{10}
and
demand also that
n
is large enough so that
\frac{N}{n}<\frac{\epsilon}{10}
. Apply now the strong Rokhlin lemma to find a set
B
that is independent
of
\cup\mathcal{G}
and such that:(i) the sets
\{T^{i}B\}_{i=0}^{n-1}
are disjoint; and (ii)
\mu(\cup_{i=0}^{n-1}T^{-i}B)>1-\frac{\delta}{10}
.

In order to be able to identify the base
B
we first change
\mathcal{P}
on the first
2N+1
levels of the tower above
B
by putting all these levels, except for level
N+1
into the new
P^{\prime}_{0}
. Then
every
\frac{N}{2}
-th level is put into the new
P^{\prime}_{1}
. In addition all points not in
\cup_{i=0}^{n-1}T^{-i}B)
are also placed in

P^{\prime}_{1}
. Finally we consider the partition of the tower induced by the partition of the base according to the atoms of
\bigvee_{i=0}^{n-1}T^{-i}\mathcal{Q}
. To the atoms that are
in
\mathcal{G}
we can assign distinct elements
w\in\{0,1\}^{\frac{\epsilon}{2}n}
and use these elements to place those levels beyond
2N
that weren’t already placed in
P^{\prime}_{1}

into
P^{\prime}_{0}
or
P^{\prime}_{1}
according to the labels in
w
. For the atoms that are not in
\cup\mathcal{G}
we reserve a single element
w
that is not used in the correspondence with
\mathcal{G}
.

For all other points in the tower
P^{\prime}_{0}=P_{0}
and
P^{\prime}_{1}=P_{1}
. It is clear that
d(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{P}^{\prime})<\epsilon
and the process defined by
\mathcal{P}^{\prime}
encodes a partition
\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}
that is within

\delta
of
\mathcal{Q}
so that
\mathcal{P}^{\prime}
is non Bernoulli.

The argument above establishes the following proposition;

Proposition 4
.

Let
(X,\mathcal{B},\mu,T)
be an ergodic system and
\mathcal{P}
,
\mathcal{Q}
a pair of partitions. If the entropy
h
of the process defined by
\mathcal{Q}
satisfies

h<\epsilon
then for any
\delta>0
there are
\mathcal{P}^{\prime}
,
\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}
such that:

(1)

d(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{P}^{\prime})<\epsilon
,

(2)

\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}\subset\bigvee_{-\infty}^{\infty}T^{-i}\mathcal{P}^{\prime}
, and

(3)

d(\mathcal{Q},\mathcal{Q}^{\prime})<\delta
.

As we have seen the proof of Theorem
2
is quite straightforward assuming the above proposition. Now using the theory developed in
[
OW
]
it is a routine matter to extend
the proposition to arbitrary countable amenable groups. There are two basic ingredients that were used. The Shannon-McMillan theorem and the strong Rokhlin lemma. The Shannon-McMillan
theorem is valid for an arbitrary sequence of Følner sets and requires no special adaptation. For the strong Rokhlin lemma there is a version using many Følner sets
(see Theorem 6 on pp. 60-1 in
[
OW
]
). We won’t go into much detail here, with the exception of what needs to be done to encode the bases of the Rokhlin towers for the
Følner sets.

If we don’t insist on limiting the number of sets in our partitions then this encoding can be easily done using extra symbols. However,
if we want to restrict the number of elements of the partition, as was done in the proof in the proof of Theorem
2
(where in the passage from
\mathcal{P}
to
\mathcal{P}^{\prime}
no extra symbol
was used) a more elaborate argument is required.
For
\mathbb{Z}
we used two things, a block marked
0^{N}10^{N}
, and then the symbol
1
at a low density arithmetic progression so that the large
0
-block would be
uniquely placed. For a general group
G
the arithmetic progression is replaced by a
K
-separated and
K^{2}
-covering set for large finite symmetric
K\subset G

that contains the identity.

In more detail, we say that a subset
C\subset G
is
K-separated
if the sets
\{Kc:c\in C\}
are disjoint. If
K
is symmetric any maximal K-separated
set
C
is a
K^{2}
-covering
, which means that
K^{2}C=G
. The
K
will be chosen so that
\frac{1}{|K|}
is sufficiently small. Then we will let
L
be a larger
symmetric set so that
L
will contain in addition to
K^{2}
another disjoint translate of
K^{2}
. Then if the Følner set
F
is sufficiently invariant and
\frac{|K|}{|F|}

is sufficiently small we can mark the base of an
F
tower by placing all levels corresponding to
g\in L
in
\mathcal{P}_{0}^{\prime}

with the exception of the base itself which is put into
\mathcal{P}_{1}^{\prime}
.
Then on the rest of tower we take a maximal K-separated set and place the levels corresponding to this set in
\mathcal{P}_{1}^{\prime}
.
In addition all points not in the Rokhlin towers are placed in
\mathcal{P}_{1}^{\prime}
.

After marking the base of the tower the rest of the proof proceeds exactly as in the case of
\mathbb{Z}
.
Here is the proposition whose proof we have just sketched:

Proposition 5
.

Let
(X,\mathcal{B},\mu,T_{g})
be an ergodic system where
G
is a countable amenable group, and
\mathcal{P}
,
\mathcal{Q}
a pair of partitions. If the entropy
h
of the process defined by
\mathcal{Q}

satisfies

h<\epsilon
then for any
\delta>0
there are
\mathcal{P}^{\prime}
,
\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}
such that:

(1)

d(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{P}^{\prime})<\epsilon
,

(2)

\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}\subset\bigvee_{-\infty}^{\infty}T^{-i}\mathcal{P}^{\prime}
, and

(3)

d(\mathcal{Q},\mathcal{Q}^{\prime})<\delta
.

With this we can prove:

Theorem 5
.

If
G
is a countable amenable group and the ergodic system
(X,\mathcal{B},\mu,T_{g})
has positive entropy and is not Bernoulli then the non-Bernoulli partitions form a dense open set
in the space of partitions.

Proof:

We first note that, just as is the case for
\mathbb{Z}
, for any countable amenable group
G
, the Bernoulli
processes are closed in the d-bar metric. This implies that the non-Bernoulli partitions form an open set, and our task is to show that this set is dense. Given a partition
\mathcal{P}
and an
\epsilon

we first apply the weak Pinsker property for amenable groups (see
[
A
]
) to find a non Bernoulli factor with entropy
h<\epsilon
. We now need to use the extension of
Krieger’s finite generator theorem to arbitrary countable amenable groups which can be found for example in
[
DP
]
. This will give a generating partition
\mathcal{Q}
for this non Bernoulli factor. Since
the non Bernoulli partitions are open there is some
\delta>0
so that any partition within
\delta
of
\mathcal{Q}
is also non Bernoulli. Now we can apply Proposition
5
and conclude the proof.
\Box

7.
Generators

To begin with I will formulate Remark
1
as formal theorems.

Theorem 6
.

If
(X,\mathcal{B},\mu,T)
is ergodic and has zero entropy then for a generic set of subsets
A\subset X
the partition
\{A,X\setminus A\}
is a generator.

This follows immediately from Propositions
1
and
2
in section 4.

Theorem 7
.

If the ergodic system
(X,\mathcal{B},\mu,T)
has positive entropy and
\mathcal{B}_{0}
is an invariant sub-sigma algebra that has full entropy
then for a generic set of subsets
A\subset X
the partition
\mathcal{P}=\{A,X\setminus A\}
satisfies
\mathcal{B}=\mathcal{B}_{0}\bigvee(\bigvee_{-\infty}^{\infty}T^{-i}\mathcal{P})
,
i.e.
\mathcal{P}
is a relative generator.

This theorem follows easily from Proposition
3
in section 5.

If we are looking for finite partitions in positive entropy systems that generate the full
sigma-algebra by themselves then the value of the entropy gives a lower bound on the number of sets in the partition.
That this is the only restriction was first
shown by Krieger in
[
K
]
. He showed that if the system
(X,\mathcal{B},\mu,T)
has positive entropy
h<
log
a
then
there exists partition a
\mathcal{P}
of
X
with
a
elements that generate the sigma-algebra
\mathcal{B}
. As always, it is understood
that this is modulo
\mu
-null sets. In
[
BKS
]
a new proof of this theorem was given by showing that in a certain space of joinings a generic element
will define a generating partition with
a
elements. However this space of joinings takes us out of the space of partitions of
X
with
a
elements.

By the continuity of the entropy the space of partitions whose entropy is equal to
h
is closed. I mean by this, the entropy of the
process defined by the partition equals
h
. The next theorem claims that in this space the generators form a generic set. This requires a new proof since to prove the density
of generators in this set we have to perturb an arbitrary partition with full entropy to get a generator without increasing the number of elements of the partition.

Theorem 8
.

Let
(X,\mathcal{B},\mu,T)
be an ergodic system with entropy equal to
h<
log
a
, and denote by
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{P}}_{a}
the space of partitions into
a
elements
that define processes with entropy equal to
h
. For a dense
G_{\delta}
subset of
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{P}}_{a}
the partitions are generators.

Proof:
By the theorem of M. Denker in
[
D
]
there exists a partition
\mathcal{Q}
into
a
sets that is a generator and furthermore,
the subshift defined by
\mathcal{Q}
is strictly ergodic and hence has
topological entropy equal to
h
. The claim that the set of generating partitions in
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{P}}_{a}
is a
G_{\delta}
is proved using the same
proof as that of Proposition
1
in section 4. We repeat the definition of the relevant open sets:

U(k,n)=\{\mathcal{P}\in\boldsymbol{\mathcal{P}}_{a}:\hskip 5.69046pt\exists\mathcal{Q}_{0}\subset\bigvee_{i=-n}^{n}T^{i}\mathcal{P}\hskip 5.69046pt\textrm{such that}\hskip 5.69046ptd(\ Q_{0},\mathcal{Q})<\frac{1}{k}\}.

Each of these sets are clearly open and a partition is a generator if and only if it is contained in
\bigcap_{k=1}^{\infty}\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}U(k,n)
. Now comes the more difficult
part of the proof. We must show that for every fixed
k
the open set
V_{k}=\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}U(k,n)
is dense in
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{P}}_{a}
. We are given an arbitrary element
\mathcal{P}\in\boldsymbol{\mathcal{P}}_{a}

and some
\epsilon>0
. Our task is to construct
\hat{\mathcal{P}}
, an
\epsilon
-perturbation of
\mathcal{P}
, that is a member of
V_{k}
. In particular it has to have full entropy. To accomplish this we will show that
the invariant sigma-algebra that it generates already contains
\mathcal{Q}
.

Fix a small
\delta>0
which will be specified later. As a first step we replace
\mathcal{P}
by a partition
\mathcal{P}^{\prime}
that satisfies:

(1)

d(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{P}^{\prime})<\delta
, and

(2)

the subshift defined by
\mathcal{P}^{\prime}
is strictly ergodic.

For this fact see
[
W
]
or
[
D
]
. In the latter paper while this fact is not stated explicitly it does follow from the detailed proof given there.
The entropy of
\mathcal{Q}
relative to
\mathcal{P}^{\prime}
tends to zero as
\delta
tends to zero. By the relative Shannon-McMillan theorem for any sufficiently large
N

we can find a
(B,N)
-KR tower such that the following holds.

We first divide the tower into pure columns with respect to the partition
\mathcal{P}^{\prime}
.
For all but an
\frac{\epsilon}{3}
fraction of these columns when a column is further
purified by the partition
\mathcal{Q}
the number of these sub-columns does not exceed
e^{\frac{\epsilon}{100}\times N}
.

In the ensuing discussion, in order not to complicate the notation I have treated the tower
as though all columns have height
N
. Of course everything that follows should be done separately for the two bases and their columns of heights
N
and
N+1
.

Returning to the
\mathcal{P}^{\prime}
-pure columns which have a very small exponential number of
\mathcal{Q}
refinements,
we will change the partition
\mathcal{P}^{\prime}
on the first
\frac{\epsilon}{3}\times N
levels in order to include a coding of the

\mathcal{Q}-N
-name of the column. It is here that we make use of the fact that the partition
\mathcal{P}^{\prime}
is strictly ergodic so that its topological entropy is strictly less than log a,
while we have a symbols at our disposal.

The encoding is done as follows. For
N
sufficiently large the set of possible
\mathcal{P}^{\prime}-(\frac{\epsilon}{3}\times N)
names of points is at most

e^{(h+\frac{\epsilon}{100})\times(\frac{\epsilon}{3}\times N)}.

Since
\mathcal{Q}
is also strictly ergodic, for
N
sufficiently large, the same estimate holds for the number of
\mathcal{Q}
names of length
(\frac{\epsilon}{3}\times N)
.
With each
\mathcal{P}^{\prime}-N
name we have a number ranging from
1
to at most
e^{\frac{\epsilon}{100}\times N}
which together with the
\mathcal{P}^{\prime}-N
name determines
the entire
\mathcal{Q}-N
name of that column. We then take a subset
C
of
\{1,2,...a\}^{\frac{\epsilon}{3}\times N}
that is disjoint from
all
\mathcal{P}^{\prime}-\frac{\epsilon}{3}\times N
and
\mathcal{Q}-\frac{\epsilon}{3}\times N
names and has the appropriate cardinality
to rename the first
\frac{\epsilon}{3}\times N
levels in such a way as to recover the entire partition
Q
.
This is done by first determining the
\mathcal{P}^{\prime}-\frac{\epsilon}{3}\times N
name on these levels.
This completes the entire
\mathcal{P}^{\prime}-N
name and then additional number determines the
\mathcal{Q}-N
name of the entire column.

For the columns where we have no control on the further refinement by
\mathcal{Q}
, since they form a small set we are free to change
\mathcal{P}^{\prime}
on entire columns.
We simply change the partition
\mathcal{P}^{\prime}
to
\mathcal{Q}
. In order to ensure that we also can recover the base
B
we take another subset
D
of

\{1,2,...a\}^{\frac{\epsilon}{3}\times N}

that is disjoint from
C
and all
\mathcal{P}^{\prime}
and
\mathcal{Q}
names and has the appropriate cardinality to relabel the first
{\frac{\epsilon}{3}\times N}
levels
so that they correspond uniquely to the
\mathcal{Q}
name but are recognizable. On all other levels the
\mathcal{P}^{\prime}
name is simply changed ot the
\mathcal{Q}
name. Finally the
\delta

is chosen so that the assertions made above will be satisfied for
N
sufficiently large.
This completes the proof of the theorem.
\Box
