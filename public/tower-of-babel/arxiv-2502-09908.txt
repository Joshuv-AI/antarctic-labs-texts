Rigorous lower bound of the dynamical critical exponent of the Ising model
Rintaro Masaoka, Tomohiro Soejima, Haruki Watanabe

Abstract
We study the kinetic Ising model under Glauber dynamics and establish an upper bound on the spectral gap for finite systems. This bound implies the critical exponent inequality z ≥ 2 z\geq 2 , thereby rigorously improving the previously known estimate z ≥ 2 − η z\geq 2-\eta . Our proof relies on the mapping from stochastic processes to frustration-free quantum systems and leverages the Simon–Lieb and Gosset–Huang inequalities.



I
Introduction

The Ising model is a paradigmatic model of phase transition. As the namesake model for the Ising universality class, its critical exponents in particular have been studied extensively with numerical methods
[
1
,
2
]
.
Rigorous results, including the exact solution in two dimensions, have also been explored
[
3
,
4
]
.
An important version of the Ising model is its

variant, which captures how spins equilibrate when there are no conservation laws.
The kinetic Ising model, which implements the Glauber dynamics of the Ising model, belongs to this universality class.

When the Ising model is near or right at criticality, the relaxation time
\tau
is believed to undergo critical slowing down.
The degree of the critical slowing down can be captured by the dynamical critical exponent.
Near the critical point in an infinite system,

is expected to behave as

\tau\simeq\xi^{z},

(1)

where
\xi
is the correlation length, which also is expected to grow as
\xi\simeq|T-T_{c}|^{-\nu}
. For the kinetic Ising model, Halperin
[
5
,
6
]
showed
z\nu\geq\gamma
, where
\gamma
is the critical exponent of the susceptibility i.e.
\chi\simeq|T-T_{c}|^{-\gamma}

We review the discussion in Ref.
[
5
]
in Appendix
A
.
.
Using (unproven) Fisher’s identity
\gamma=(2-\eta)\nu
, this bound is converted to

\displaystyle z\geq 2-\eta,

(2)

where
\eta
is the anomalous dimension characterizing the decay of correlation functions at the critical point, i.e.,
\langle\sigma_{i}\sigma_{j}\rangle\simeq|i-j|^{-d+2-\eta}
as
|i-j|\to\infty
. The values of

are known exactly in
d=2
, and
d\geq 4
, and very accurately in
d=3

\eta=1/4
for
d=2
,
\eta=0.0362978(20)
for
d=3

[
2
]
, and
\eta=0
for
d\geq 4

[
4
]
. Numerical results performed with models in the stochastic Ising model give

[

]

Equivalently, right at the critical point in a finite system, the system size

\tau\simeq L^{z}.

(3)

The best rigorous bound so far, based on this definition, for the two-dimensional kinetic Ising model is
z\geq 7/4

11

2

In this work, we present a rigorous improvement of this bound for the kinetic Ising model in any dimension

The key step is our recent work, which proved

II
Definitions

II.1
Ising model in equilibrium

In this work, we consider the standard ferromagnetic Ising model with the nearest neighbor interaction on the
d
-dimensional hypercubic lattice
\Lambda_{L}=\big\{-\frac{L-1}{2},-\frac{L-3}{2},\cdots,\frac{L-1}{2}\big\}^{d}\subset\mathbb{Z}^{d}
,
where
L
is odd. We denote the
a
-th component of
i
by
i^{(a)}
(
a=1,2,\cdots,d
).
For each site
i\in\Lambda_{L}
, we associate a spin variable
\sigma_{i}=\pm 1
. We denote by
\bm{\sigma}=(\sigma_{i})_{i\in\Lambda_{L}}
a
spin configuration, and by
\mathcal{S}_{L}
the set of all spin configurations.

The energy of a spin configuration
\bm{\sigma}\in\mathcal{S}_{L}
is given by

\displaystyle E(\bm{\sigma})=-\sum_{(i,j)}\sigma_{i}\sigma_{j}=-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i\in\Lambda_{L}}\sigma_{i}\sum_{j\in B_{i}}\sigma_{j},

(5)

where the summation in the first expression is over nearest neighbor pairs, and
B_{i}
in the second expression is the set of sites adjacent to the site
i
. We use periodic boundary conditions to regard
\Lambda_{L}
as a torus.
For any function
O
of spin configurations, we denote its expectation value in the equilibrium state at inverse temperature
\beta>0
as

\displaystyle\langle O\rangle\coloneqq\frac{1}{Z}\sum_{\bm{\sigma}\in\mathcal{S}_{L}}O(\bm{\sigma})w(\bm{\sigma}),

(6)

where

\displaystyle w(\bm{\sigma})\coloneqq e^{-\beta E(\bm{\sigma})}>0

(7)

is the Boltzmann weight and

\displaystyle Z\coloneqq\sum_{\bm{\sigma}\in\mathcal{S}_{L}}w(\bm{\sigma})

(8)

is the partition function. We note that the spin flipping symmetry
w(\bm{\sigma})=w(-\bm{\sigma})
implies
\langle\sigma_{i}\rangle=0
. Also, the first Griffiths inequality
[
18
]
states

\displaystyle 0\leq\langle\sigma_{i}\sigma_{j}\rangle\leq 1.

(9)

It is well known that the model undergoes a ferromagnetic phase transition if
d\geq 2

[
19
,
20
]
. It has been proved that

\displaystyle\limsup_{L\to\infty}\sum_{i\in\Lambda_{L}}\langle\sigma_{o}\sigma_{i}\rangle_{L}\begin{cases}<\infty&\text{if $\beta<\beta_{c}$};\\
=\infty&\text{if $\beta\geq\beta_{c}$},\end{cases}

(10)

where
o\in\Lambda_{L}
is the origin
(0,0,\cdots,0)
and
\beta_{c}<\infty
is the critical inverse temperature.

II.2
Kinetic Ising model

Now we introduce the kinetic Ising model, also called the stochastic Ising model, which implements the Glauber dynamics.
Let
p(t,\bm{\sigma})\geq 0
be the probability for the spin configuration
\bm{\sigma}
to be realized at time
t
. We consider a continuous-time Markov process described by the master equation
2
2

2

See, for example, Ref.
[
37
]
for a general introduction to stochastic processes.

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}p(t,\bm{\sigma})=\sum_{\bm{\sigma}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}_{L}}W_{\bm{\sigma},\bm{\sigma}^{\prime}}p(t,\bm{\sigma}^{\prime}).

(11)

Let
P_{i}(\bm{\sigma})\geq 0
be the rate at
which the spin at
i
is flipped. Let us denote by
\tau_{i}(\bm{\sigma})
the spin configuration obtained by flipping the spin at site
i
in
\bm{\sigma}\in\mathcal{S}_{L}
. Then the transition rate matrix
W
is given by
\sum_{i\in\Lambda_{L}}W_{i}
, where

\displaystyle(W_{i})_{\bm{\sigma},\bm{\sigma}^{\prime}}=\begin{cases}P_{i}(\bm{\sigma}^{\prime})&(\bm{\sigma}=\tau_{i}(\bm{\sigma}^{\prime}))\\
-P_{i}(\bm{\sigma}^{\prime})&(\bm{\sigma}=\bm{\sigma}^{\prime})\\
0&(\text{otherwise})\end{cases},

(12)

which satisfies

\displaystyle\sum_{\bm{\sigma}\in\mathcal{S}_{L}}(W_{i})_{\bm{\sigma},\bm{\sigma}^{\prime}}=0.

(13)

This relation is necessary for the conservation of probability
\sum_{\bm{\sigma}\in\mathcal{S}_{L}}p(t,\bm{\sigma})=1
.

For concreteness, in this work we assume the heat-bath algorithm
[
22
]

\displaystyle P_{i}(\bm{\sigma})\coloneqq\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{2}\sigma_{i}\tanh\Big(\beta\sum_{j\in B_{i}}\sigma_{j}\Big)>0,

(14)

which satisfies the detailed balance condition

\displaystyle(W_{i})_{\bm{\sigma},\bm{\sigma}^{\prime}}w(\bm{\sigma}^{\prime})=(W_{i})_{\bm{\sigma}^{\prime},\bm{\sigma}}w(\bm{\sigma})

(15)

for any
\bm{\sigma},\bm{\sigma}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}_{L}
. Other choices of
P_{i}(\bm{\sigma})
can be treated in the same way with minor modifications as discussed in Appendix
C
. The Boltzmann weight
w(\bm{\sigma})>0
is an eigenvector of
W
with the eigenvalue
0
:

\displaystyle\sum_{\bm{\sigma}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}_{L}}(W_{i})_{\bm{\sigma},\bm{\sigma}^{\prime}}w(\bm{\sigma}^{\prime})=\Big(\sum_{\bm{\sigma}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}_{L}}(W_{i})_{\bm{\sigma}^{\prime},\bm{\sigma}}\Big)w(\bm{\sigma})=0,

(16)

As we will see in Sec.
II.3
,
w(\bm{\sigma})/Z
is the unique stationary distribution of our Markov process.

II.3
Real symmetric matrices

In order to relate the Markov process to a quantum mechanical problem, we use the standard similarity transformation to define a matrix
H_{i}
for each
i\in\Lambda_{L}
by

\displaystyle(H_{i})_{\bm{\sigma},\bm{\sigma}^{\prime}}\coloneqq-\sqrt{\frac{w(\bm{\sigma}^{\prime})}{w(\bm{\sigma})}}(W_{i})_{\bm{\sigma},\bm{\sigma}^{\prime}}.

(17)

The matrix will be used in (
26
) to define the (generalized) Rokhsar-Kivelson Hamiltonian
[
23
,
24
,
12
,
25
]
.
Note that the detailed balance condition (
15
) implies
(H_{i})_{\bm{\sigma},\bm{\sigma}^{\prime}}=(H_{i})_{\bm{\sigma}^{\prime},\bm{\sigma}}
and hence
H_{i}
is a real symmetric
matrix. Since
H_{i}
and
-W_{i}
are related to each other by the similarity transformation in (
17
), their eigenvalues are common.
Also, the non-positive nature of off-diagonal components of
H_{i}
, combined with Eq. (
16
), implies that
H_{i}
is positive semi-definite
3
3

3

Let
v
be an eigenvector of
H_{i}
with the lowest eigenvalue
\mu_{i}
and
u
be a vector whose components are given by
u_{\bm{\sigma}}=|v_{\bm{\sigma}}|
. Since off-diagonal components of
H_{i}
are nonpositive,
\mu_{i}=\sum_{\bm{\sigma},\bm{\sigma}^{\prime}}v_{\bm{\sigma}}(H_{i})_{\bm{\sigma},\bm{\sigma}^{\prime}}v_{\bm{\sigma}^{\prime}}\geq\sum_{\bm{\sigma},\bm{\sigma}^{\prime}}u_{\bm{\sigma}}(H_{i})_{\bm{\sigma},\bm{\sigma}^{\prime}}u_{\bm{\sigma}^{\prime}}
. Then the variational principle implies that
u_{\bm{\sigma}}
is also an eigenvector of
H_{i}
with the eigenvalue
\mu_{i}
. On the other hand,
w_{\bm{\sigma}}=\sqrt{w(\bm{\sigma})}>0
is an eigenvector of
H_{i}
with the eigenvalue
0
and
\sum_{\bm{\sigma}\in\mathcal{S}_{L}}w_{\bm{\sigma}}u_{\bm{\sigma}}=\sum_{\bm{\sigma}\in\mathcal{S}_{L}}w_{\bm{\sigma}}|v_{\bm{\sigma}}|\neq 0
. Therefore,
\mu_{i}=0
.
.
We also find from (
16
) that the normalized vector
\bm{\Phi}_{0}
defined by

\displaystyle(\bm{\Phi}_{0})_{\bm{\sigma}}\coloneqq\sqrt{\frac{w(\bm{\sigma})}{Z}}

(18)

is an eigenvector of
H_{i}
with eigenvalue
0
. It should be noted that the vector
\bm{\Phi}_{0}
does not depend on
i
. As long as
0\leq\beta<\infty
, the Perron–Frobenius theorem
[
27
]
implies that the eigenvalue 0 of
H=\sum_{i\in\Lambda_{L}}H_{i}
is nondegenerate. Therefore, we have
\lim_{t\to\infty}(e^{-Ht})_{\bm{\sigma},\bm{\sigma}^{\prime}}=\sqrt{w(\bm{\sigma})w(\bm{\sigma}^{\prime})}/Z
.

Let us denote by
\epsilon_{L}
the smallest eigenvalue of
-W
other than
0
. The quantity
\epsilon_{L}
, which is called the spectral gap in the mathematical literature, essentially determines the decay property of
autocorrelation functions. For example, the equilibrium autocorrelation function of an operator
O
is defined by

\displaystyle\langle Oe^{Wt}O\rangle=\frac{1}{Z}\sum_{\bm{\sigma},\bm{\sigma}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}_{L}}O(\bm{\sigma})(e^{Wt})_{\bm{\sigma},\bm{\sigma}^{\prime}}O(\bm{\sigma}^{\prime})w(\bm{\sigma}^{\prime}).

(19)

For any
\bm{\sigma}^{\prime}
,
\lim_{t\to\infty}(e^{Wt})_{\bm{\sigma},\bm{\sigma}^{\prime}}=w(\bm{\sigma})/Z
, which implies

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to\infty}\langle Oe^{Wt}O\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{\bm{\sigma}\in\mathcal{S}_{L}}O(\bm{\sigma})\frac{w(\bm{\sigma})}{Z}\sum_{\bm{\sigma}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}_{L}}O(\bm{\sigma}^{\prime})\frac{w(\bm{\sigma}^{\prime})}{Z}

\displaystyle=\langle O\rangle^{2}.

(20)

Then the autocorrelation function behaves as

\displaystyle\big|\langle Oe^{Wt}O\rangle-\langle O\rangle^{2}\big|\simeq Ce^{-\epsilon_{L}t}.

(21)

Suppose that the relaxation time
\tau=1/\epsilon_{L}
grows with
L
as
L^{z}
. This scaling defines the dynamical critical exponent
z
.

II.4
Theorem

Our main theorem is the following upper bound for the spectral gap
\epsilon_{L}
.

Theorem

Let
d\geq 2
. Then there exist constants
C
and
L_{0}
that depend only on
d
such that

\displaystyle 0<\epsilon_{L}\leq C\Big(\frac{\log L}{L}\Big)^{2}

(22)

for any
\beta\geq\beta_{c}
and
L\geq L_{0}
.

Comparing the bound (
22
) at
\beta=\beta_{c}
with the expected finite size scaling behavior
\epsilon_{L}\simeq L^{-z}
, we see that the theorem implies
z\geq 2
(assuming the existence of the exponent
z
). We also note that the bound (
22
) is most meaningful for
\beta=\beta_{c}
since it is expected that
\epsilon_{L}
decays much faster as
L
grows in the low-temperature phase with
\beta>\beta_{c}
. See Ref.
[
28
]
for known rigorous results for sufficiently large
\beta
.

III
Proof

We examine the behavior of the quantity

\displaystyle c(L)\coloneqq 2d\sum_{j\in\partial\Lambda_{L}}\langle\sigma_{o}\sigma_{j}\rangle

(23)

as a function of
L
, where
\partial\Lambda_{L}
is the set of lattice sites in which at least one of its components satisfies
|i^{(a)}|=\frac{L-1}{2}
. Note that
\partial\Lambda_{L}
does not represent the boundary since we are imposing periodic boundary conditions.

We shall prove a lower bound (
24
) and an upper bound (
34
) for the same quantity
c(L)
. The combination of the two bounds leads to the desired upper bound for the spectral gap.

III.1
Lower bound

A lower bound of
c(L)
can be derived based on the Simon-Lieb inequality
[
14
,
15
,
16
]
. If the correlation function
\langle\sigma_{i}\sigma_{j}\rangle_{L^{\prime}}
does not decay exponentially, then

\displaystyle c(L)\geq 1

(24)

for any
L=1,3,5,\cdots

[
14
,
15
,
16
]
. For readers’ convenience, we review the proof in the Appendix
B
.

III.2
Upper bound

In order to make use of results from quantum information, we shall identify the matrices discussed in the previous section with quantum mechanical operators. For each
i\in\Lambda_{L}
, let the local Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}_{i}
be the two-dimensional Hilbert space with the basis
\{|+1\rangle_{i},|-1\rangle_{i}\}
.
We consider a quantum system with the Hilbert space
\otimes_{i\in\Lambda_{L}}\mathcal{H}_{i}
and denote its basis states as

\displaystyle|\bm{\sigma}\rangle=\otimes_{i\in\Lambda_{L}}|\sigma_{i}\rangle_{i},

(25)

where
\bm{\sigma}\in\mathcal{S}_{L}
.
Given
(H_{i})_{\bm{\sigma},\bm{\sigma}^{\prime}}
in (
17
), we define the Rokhsar-Kivelson Hamiltonian
\hat{H}=\sum_{i\in\Lambda_{L}}\hat{H}_{i}
by

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{i}\coloneqq\sum_{\bm{\sigma},\bm{\sigma}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}_{L}}(H_{i})_{\bm{\sigma},\bm{\sigma}^{\prime}}|\bm{\sigma}\rangle\langle\bm{\sigma}^{\prime}|.

(26)

As detailed in the previous section, the spectrum of this Hamiltonian coincides with that of the transition rate matrix.

For the heat-bath algorithm (
14
), we find

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{i}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\cosh(\beta\sum_{j\in B_{i}}\hat{\sigma}_{j}^{z})}\Big(e^{-\beta\hat{\sigma}_{i}^{z}{}\sum_{j\in B_{i}}\hat{\sigma}_{j}^{z}}-\hat{\sigma}_{i}^{x}\Big),

(27)

which includes up to
(2d+1)
-spin interactions. Here
\hat{\sigma}_{i}^{x}
and
\hat{\sigma}_{i}^{z}
are operators defined by

\displaystyle\hat{\sigma}_{i}^{x}=\sum_{\bm{\sigma}\in\mathcal{S}_{L}}|\tau_{i}(\bm{\sigma})\rangle\langle\bm{\sigma}|,\quad\hat{\sigma}_{i}^{z}=\sum_{\bm{\sigma}\in\mathcal{S}_{L}}\sigma_{i}|\bm{\sigma}\rangle\langle\bm{\sigma}|,

(28)

which can be represented by Pauli matrices

\displaystyle\sigma^{x}=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix},\quad\sigma^{z}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&-1\end{pmatrix}

(29)

on
\mathcal{H}_{i}
.
It is easily checked that
[\hat{H}_{i},\hat{H}_{j}]=0
unless
|i-j|=1
.
As one can easily show,
\hat{H}_{i}^{2}=\hat{H}_{i}
, implying that
\hat{H}_{i}
is a projector. Furthermore,

\displaystyle|\Phi_{0}\rangle\coloneqq\sum_{\bm{\sigma}\in\mathcal{S}_{L}}(\bm{\Phi}_{0})_{\bm{\sigma}}|\bm{\sigma}\rangle

(30)

is a simultaneous ground state of all
\hat{H}_{i}
’s with the eigenvalue
0
:

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{i}|\Phi_{0}\rangle=-\sum_{\bm{\sigma}\in\mathcal{S}_{L}}|\bm{\sigma}\rangle(H_{i}\bm{\Phi}_{0})_{\bm{\sigma}}=0.

(31)

In general,
\hat{H}=\sum_{i}\hat{H}_{i}
is said to be frustration-free if a ground state
|\Phi\rangle
of
\hat{H}
is a simultaneous ground state of all
\hat{H}_{i}
’s.
(
31
) implies that
\hat{H}
is frustration-free.

For a general frustration-free Hamiltonian
\hat{H}=\sum_{i}\hat{H}_{i}
with
\hat{H}_{i}^{2}=\hat{H}_{i}
and for arbitrary operators
\hat{O}
and
\hat{O}^{\prime}

17

4

, (ii)

\displaystyle\frac{\langle\Phi_{0}|\hat{O}(1-\hat{G})\hat{O}^{\prime}|\Phi_{0}\rangle}{\|\hat{O}^{\dagger}|\Phi_{0}\rangle\|\|\hat{O}^{\prime}|\Phi_{0}\rangle\|}

\displaystyle\leq 2e^{2}\exp\Big(-\frac{D(\hat{O},\hat{O}^{\prime})-1}{c-1}\sqrt{\frac{\epsilon_{L}}{g^{2}+\epsilon_{L}}}\Big),

where
\hat{G}
is the projector onto the ground states,

\hat{H}

III.3
Proof of (
22
)

Inequalities (

\displaystyle\frac{\epsilon_{L}}{\epsilon_{L}+(2d)^{2}}\leq f(L)^{2},

\displaystyle f(L)\coloneqq\frac{2}{L-3}\ln(8e^{2}d^{2}L^{d-1}).

When

\displaystyle 0<\epsilon_{L}\leq(2d)^{2}\frac{f(L)^{2}}{1-f(L)^{2}}.

We find that

IV
Discussions

Our results are applicable not only to the critical point but also to the entire ordered phase.
In the ordered phase,
\epsilon_{L}
measures the finite-size gap of quasi-degenerate ground states, which is typically exponentially small.
A conjecture in Ref.
[
31
]
suggests that excitations are gapless throughout the ordered phase.
Furthermore, our methods naturally extend to broader classes of Markov chains; see Ref.
[
13
]
for a comprehensive discussion.

Before closing, we note that Refs.
[
32
,
33
,
34
,
35
]
establish upper bounds on the spectral gap for frustration-free Hamiltonians under
open
boundary conditions without boundary terms.
6
6

6

Under the open boundary condition without boundary terms, the Hamiltonian decomposes into a direct sum of sectors, each defined by fixing the boundary spins in every possible configuration. This situation differs from the familiar plus/minus boundaries, where all boundary spins are fixed to
+1
or
-1
, and from free boundaries, where the boundary spins remain completely unconstrained.

The bound
\epsilon_{L}\leq C/L^{2}
derived in the first three works is not directly applicable to our model (
27
), as it assumes strictly nearest-neighbor interactions.
The result in Ref.
[
35
]
implies a similar bound, but there are important differences: it considers anisotropic shapes in addition to open boundary conditions and focuses on the asymptotic behavior in the large
L
limit, rather than providing a bound for each finite system.
Hence, the conclusion is not directly comparable to ours.

Appendix A
Halperin’s bound

Here we review the argument for the previous best bound
z\geq 2-\eta
obtained by Halperin
[
5
]
in terms of the Rokhsar-Kivelson Hamiltonian.

Let us consider the autocorrelation function of the total magnetization
\hat{M}\coloneqq\sum_{i\in\Lambda_{L}}\hat{s}_{i}^{z}
.

\displaystyle C(t)\coloneqq\langle\Phi_{0}|\hat{M}e^{-\hat{H}t}(1-\hat{G})\hat{M}|\Phi_{0}\rangle,

(39)

where
\hat{G}=|\Phi_{0}\rangle\langle\Phi_{0}|
is the projector onto the ground state of
\hat{H}=\sum_{i\in\Lambda_{L}}\hat{H}_{i}
in Eq. (
27
). Let
|\Phi_{n}\rangle
(
n=1,2,\cdots
) be excited states and
E_{n}
be their eigenenergies. It follows that

\displaystyle C(t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n}|\langle\Phi_{n}|\hat{M}|\Phi_{0}\rangle|^{2}e^{-E_{n}t}.

(40)

We define the characteristic time
\bar{\tau}
by

\displaystyle\bar{\tau}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\frac{\int_{0}^{\infty}dtC(t)}{C(0)}=\frac{\langle\Phi_{0}|\hat{M}\frac{1-\hat{G}}{\hat{H}}\hat{M}|\Phi_{0}\rangle}{\langle\Phi_{0}|\hat{M}(1-\hat{G})\hat{M}|\Phi_{0}\rangle}=\frac{\sum_{n}\frac{|\langle\Phi_{n}|\hat{M}|\Phi_{0}\rangle|^{2}}{E_{n}}}{\sum_{n}|\langle\Phi_{n}|\hat{M}|\Phi_{0}\rangle|^{2}}\leq\frac{1}{\epsilon}=\tau.

(41)

To derive a lower bound of this quantity, let us define the uniform susceptibility (
T
is the temperature)

\displaystyle\chi\coloneqq\frac{1}{TL^{d}}\langle\Phi_{0}|\hat{M}(1-\hat{G})\hat{M}|\Phi_{0}\rangle

(42)

and a quantity

\displaystyle R

\displaystyle\coloneqq\frac{1}{L^{d}}\langle\Phi_{0}|\hat{M}\hat{H}(1-\hat{G})\hat{M}|\Phi_{0}\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2L^{d}}\langle\Phi_{0}|[\hat{M},[\hat{H},\hat{M}]]|\Phi_{0}\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{L^{d}}\sum_{i}\langle\Phi_{0}|\frac{1}{\cosh(\beta\sum_{j\in B_{i}}\hat{\sigma}_{j}^{z})}\hat{\sigma}_{i}^{x}|\Phi_{0}\rangle.

(43)

The Schwartz inequality

\displaystyle\langle\Phi_{0}|\hat{M}\frac{1-\hat{G}}{\hat{H}}\hat{M}|\Phi_{0}\rangle\langle\Phi_{0}|\hat{M}\hat{H}(1-\hat{G})\hat{M}|\Phi_{0}\rangle\geq\langle\Phi_{0}|\hat{M}(1-\hat{G})\hat{M}|\Phi_{0}\rangle^{2}

(44)

leads to

\displaystyle\tau\geq\bar{\tau}\geq\frac{T}{R}\chi.

(45)

Now, we take the
L\to\infty
limit. The susceptibility behaves as
\chi\simeq|T-T_{c}|^{-\gamma}
, while
R
does not show singularity around the critical point
T=T_{c}
.
Hence, if we assume
\bar{\tau}\simeq|T-T_{c}|^{-z\nu}
, one gets

\displaystyle z\nu\geq\gamma.

(46)

Here we review Simon and Lieb’s results

B.1

B.2
Proof

Let us define an integer

(50)

represents the integer part of a real number

, the Simon-Lieb inequality

,
15
,
16

\displaystyle\langle\sigma_{o}\sigma_{i}\rangle_{L^{\prime}}\leq\sum_{j\in\partial\Lambda_{L}}\sum_{k_{1}\in B_{j}\cap\partial\Lambda_{L+2}}\langle\sigma_{o}\sigma_{j}\rangle_{L}^{\text{free}}\langle\sigma_{k_{1}}\sigma_{i}\rangle_{L^{\prime}},

where

Plugging

(53)

Repeating this process

\displaystyle\langle\sigma_{o}\sigma_{i}\rangle_{L^{\prime}}\leq c(L)^{n_{i}}\max_{k_{1},k_{2},\cdots,k_{n_{i}}\in\partial\Lambda_{L+2}}\langle\sigma_{o}\sigma_{i-\sum_{j=1}^{n_{i}}k_{j}}\rangle_{L^{\prime}}\leq c(L)^{n_{i}}\leq Ce^{-\|i\|_{\infty}/\xi},

where we used

Appendix C
Generalization

Our discussion presented in the main text can be readily extended to other algorithms in which
P_{i}(\bm{\sigma})
(i) satisfies the detailed balance condition (
15
), (ii) depends only on spins in a finite distance from the site
i
, (iii) gives the transition matrix

with ergodicity.

To see this, note that the corresponding Rokhsar-Kivelson Hamiltonian

\displaystyle\hat{H}^{\text{(RK)}}=\sum_{i\in\Lambda_{L}}\hat{H}_{i}^{\text{(RK)}},

(55)

defined by (
12
) and (
17
), is frustration free and its unique ground state is given by
|\Phi_{0}\rangle
in (
30
).

Let
|i,n\rangle
(
n=1,2,\cdots
) be the eigenvectors of
\hat{H}_{i}^{\text{(RK)}}
with nonzero eigenvalues.
Then
\hat{P}_{i}^{\text{(RK)}}\coloneqq\sum_{n}|i,n\rangle\langle i,n|
is the projector version of
\hat{H}_{i}^{\text{(RK)}}
. Let
\epsilon_{L}^{\text{(RK)}}
and
\tilde{\epsilon}_{L}^{\text{(RK)}}
be the second smallest eigenvalue of
\hat{H}^{\text{(RK)}}
and
\sum_{i\in\Lambda_{L}}\hat{P}_{i}^{\text{(RK)}}
, respectively. Then we have

\displaystyle\hat{H}^{\text{(RK)}}\leq\max_{i\in\Lambda_{L}}\|\hat{H}_{i}^{\text{(RK)}}\|\sum_{i\in\Lambda_{L}}\hat{P}_{i}^{\text{(RK)}},

(56)

which implies

\displaystyle\epsilon_{L}^{\text{(RK)}}\leq\max_{i\in\Lambda_{L}}\|\hat{H}_{i}^{\text{(RK)}}\|\tilde{\epsilon}_{L}^{\text{(RK)}}.

(57)

Therefore, our discussion for projector Hamiltonians is sufficient.

For example, in the Metropolis algorithm, the flipping rate

is given by

\displaystyle P_{i}(\bm{\sigma})\coloneqq\min(1,e^{-2\beta\sigma_{i}\sum_{j\in B_{i}}\sigma_{j}})

(58)

and the corresponding Rokhsar-Kivelson Hamiltonian is

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{i}^{\text{(RK)}}

\displaystyle=\min(e^{\beta\sum_{j\in B_{i}}\hat{\sigma}_{j}^{z}},e^{-\beta\sum_{j\in B_{i}}\hat{\sigma}_{j}^{z}})\Big(e^{-\beta\hat{\sigma}_{i}^{z}{}\sum_{j\in B_{i}}\hat{\sigma}_{j}^{z}}-\hat{\sigma}_{i}^{x}\Big).

(59)

The largest eigenvalue of
\hat{H}_{i}^{\text{(RK)}}
gives
\|\hat{H}_{i}^{\text{(RK)}}\|=2
.

Another simple choice will be

\displaystyle P_{i}(\bm{\sigma})\coloneqq e^{-\beta\sigma_{i}\sum_{j\in B_{i}}\sigma_{j}},

(60)

which corresponds to

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{i}^{\text{(RK)}}

\displaystyle=e^{-\beta\hat{\sigma}_{i}^{z}{}\sum_{j\in B_{i}}\hat{\sigma}_{j}^{z}}-\hat{\sigma}_{i}^{x}

(61)

with
\|\hat{H}_{i}^{\text{(RK)}}\|=2\cosh(2d\beta)
.

Appendix E
Detectability Lemma

The proof of the Gosset–Huang inequality is based on the detectability lemma
[
38
,
39
]
.

E.1
Statement

Suppose that the Hamiltonian

\displaystyle\hat{H}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\hat{H}_{i}

(62)

is a sum of projectors
\hat{H}_{i}^{2}=\hat{H}_{i}
and is frustration free, i.e., all
\hat{H}_{i}
’s can be simultaneously minimized. We assume that the lowest eigenvalue of
\hat{H}
is 0.

Let
\hat{G}
be the projector onto the space of all the ground states of
\hat{H}
. Note that
\hat{H}_{i}\hat{G}=0
for any
i
. Let
\epsilon
be the smallest eigenvalue of
\hat{H}
other than
0
. Let us write
\hat{P}_{i}\coloneqq 1-\hat{H}_{i}
and define

\displaystyle\hat{P}\coloneqq\hat{P}_{\sigma(1)}\hat{P}_{\sigma(2)}\cdots\hat{P}_{\sigma(N)},

(63)

where
\sigma
is an arbitrary permutation of
1,2,\cdots,N
. Furthermore, we consider the interaction graph
(V,E)
associated with
\hat{H}_{i}
and
V=\{1,2,\cdots,N\}
and define the maximal degree
g
(see Sec.
D
). Then,

\displaystyle\|\hat{P}-\hat{G}\|\leq\sqrt{\frac{g^{2}}{g^{2}+\epsilon}}.

(64)

E.2
Proof

Take a state
|\psi\rangle
and we perform the following operations to the quantity
\|\hat{H}_{i}\hat{P}_{\sigma(j)}\cdots\hat{P}_{\sigma(N)}|\psi\rangle\|
.
If
\hat{H}_{i}
commutes with
\hat{H}_{\sigma(j)}
,

\displaystyle\|\hat{H}_{i}\hat{P}_{\sigma(j)}\cdots\hat{P}_{\sigma(N)}|\psi\rangle\|

\displaystyle=\|\hat{P}_{\sigma(j)}\hat{H}_{i}\hat{P}_{\sigma(j+1)}\cdots\hat{P}_{\sigma(N)}|\psi\rangle\|

\displaystyle\leq\|\hat{H}_{i}\hat{P}_{\sigma(j+1)}\cdots\hat{P}_{\sigma(N)}|\psi\rangle\|.

(65)

Otherwise,

\displaystyle\|\hat{H}_{i}\hat{P}_{\sigma(j)}\cdots\hat{P}_{\sigma(N)}|\psi\rangle\|

\displaystyle=\|\hat{H}_{i}(1-\hat{H}_{\sigma(j)})\hat{P}_{\sigma(j+1)}\cdots\hat{P}_{\sigma(N)}|\psi\rangle\|

\displaystyle\leq\|\hat{H}_{i}\hat{P}_{\sigma(j+1)}\cdots\hat{P}_{\sigma(N)}|\psi\rangle\|+\|\hat{H}_{i}\hat{H}_{\sigma(j)}\hat{P}_{\sigma(j+1)}\cdots\hat{P}_{\sigma(N)}|\psi\rangle\|

\displaystyle\leq\|\hat{H}_{i}\hat{P}_{\sigma(j+1)}\cdots\hat{P}_{\sigma(N)}|\psi\rangle\|+\|\hat{H}_{\sigma(j)}\hat{P}_{\sigma(j+1)}\cdots\hat{P}_{\sigma(N)}|\psi\rangle\|.

(66)

Note that the first term in the last expression is the original quantity but
j
is shifted to
j+1
.
If we start from
\|\hat{H}_{i}\hat{P}|\psi\rangle\|
and repeat this procedure,
\hat{H}_{i}
never reaches
|\psi\rangle
, because
\hat{H}_{i}\hat{P}_{i}=0
. At the end, we obtain the following terms:

\displaystyle\|\hat{H}_{i}\hat{P}|\psi\rangle\|\leq\sum_{\sigma(l)\in V_{i}}\|\hat{H}_{\sigma(l)}\hat{P}_{\sigma(l+1)}\cdots\hat{P}_{\sigma(N)}|\psi\rangle\|.

(67)

Since the square of an average is less than or equal to the average of the squares,

\displaystyle\|\hat{H}_{i}\hat{P}|\psi\rangle\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq|V_{i}|^{2}\Big(\frac{1}{|V_{i}|}\sum_{\sigma(l)\in V_{i}}\|\hat{H}_{\sigma(l)}\hat{P}_{\sigma(l+1)}\cdots\hat{P}_{\sigma(N)}|\psi\rangle\|\Big)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq|V_{i}|^{2}\Big(\frac{1}{|V_{i}|}\sum_{\sigma(l)\in V_{i}}\|\hat{H}_{\sigma(l)}\hat{P}_{\sigma(l+1)}\cdots\hat{P}_{\sigma(N)}|\psi\rangle\|^{2}\Big)

\displaystyle\leq g\sum_{\sigma(l)\in V_{i}}\|\hat{H}_{\sigma(l)}\hat{P}_{\sigma(l+1)}\cdots\hat{P}_{\sigma(N)}|\psi\rangle\|^{2}.

(68)

Therefore,

\displaystyle\langle\psi|\hat{P}^{\dagger}\hat{H}\hat{P}|\psi\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\langle\psi|\hat{P}^{\dagger}\hat{H}_{i}\hat{P}|\psi\rangle=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\langle\psi|\hat{P}^{\dagger}\hat{H}_{i}^{2}\hat{P}|\psi\rangle=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\|\hat{H}_{i}\hat{P}|\psi\rangle\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq g\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{\sigma(l)\in V_{i}}\|\hat{H}_{\sigma(l)}\hat{P}_{\sigma(l+1)}\cdots\hat{P}_{\sigma(N)}|\psi\rangle\|^{2}.

(69)

Exchanging the order of the summations, we find

\displaystyle\langle\psi|\hat{P}^{\dagger}\hat{H}\hat{P}|\psi\rangle

\displaystyle\leq g^{2}\sum_{l=1}^{N}\|\hat{H}_{\sigma(l)}\hat{P}_{\sigma(l+1)}\cdots\hat{P}_{\sigma(N)}|\psi\rangle\|^{2}

\displaystyle=g^{2}\sum_{l=1}^{N}\|(1-\hat{P}_{\sigma(l)})\hat{P}_{\sigma(l+1)}\cdots\hat{P}_{\sigma(N)}|\psi\rangle\|^{2}

\displaystyle=g^{2}\sum_{l=1}^{N}\big(\|\hat{P}_{\sigma(l+1)}\cdots\hat{P}_{\sigma(N)}|\psi\rangle\|^{2}-\|\hat{P}_{\sigma(l)}\cdots\hat{P}_{\sigma(N)}|\psi\rangle\|^{2}\big)

\displaystyle=g^{2}\big(\||\psi\rangle\|^{2}-\|\hat{P}|\psi\rangle\|^{2}\big).

(70)

Applying this result to
|\psi^{\perp}\rangle\coloneqq(1-\hat{G})|\psi\rangle
and using the definition of
\epsilon
,

\displaystyle\epsilon\|\hat{P}|\psi^{\perp}\rangle\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\langle\psi^{\perp}|\hat{P}^{\dagger}\hat{H}\hat{P}|\psi^{\perp}\rangle\leq g^{2}(\||\psi^{\perp}\rangle\|^{2}-\|\hat{P}|\psi^{\perp}\rangle\|^{2}),

(71)

which can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\|\hat{P}|\psi^{\perp}\rangle\|\leq\sqrt{\frac{g^{2}}{g^{2}+\epsilon}}\||\psi^{\perp}\rangle\|.

(72)

Therefore, using
\hat{P}\hat{G}=\hat{G}
,

\displaystyle\|(\hat{P}-\hat{G})|\psi\rangle\|=\|\hat{P}(1-\hat{G})|\psi\rangle\|=\|\hat{P}|\psi^{\perp}\rangle\|\leq\sqrt{\frac{g^{2}}{g^{2}+\epsilon}}\||\psi^{\perp}\rangle\|\leq\sqrt{\frac{g^{2}}{g^{2}+\epsilon}}\||\psi\rangle\|

(73)

for any
|\psi\rangle
, which implies (
64
).
