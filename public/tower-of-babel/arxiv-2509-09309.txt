Proactive AI Adoption can be Threatening: When Help Backfires
Dana Harari, Ofra Amir

Abstract
Artificial intelligence (AI) assistants are increasingly embedded in workplace tools, raising the question of how initiative-taking shapes adoption. Prior work highlights trust and expectation mismatches as barriers, but the underlying psychological mechanisms remain unclear. Drawing on self-affirmation and social exchange theories, we theorize that unsolicited help elicits self-threat, reducing willingness to accept assistance, likelihood of future use, and performance expectancy. We report two vignette-based experiments (Study 1: N = 761 N=761 ; Study 2: N = 571 N=571 , preregistered). Study 1 compared anticipatory and reactive help provided by an AI vs. a human, while Study 2 distinguished between offering (suggesting help) and providing (acting automatically). In Study 1, AI help was more threatening than human help. Across both studies, anticipatory help increased perceived threat and reduced adoption outcomes. Our findings identify self-threat as a mechanism explaining why proactive AI features may backfire and suggest design implications for AI initiative.

Generative AI; Mixed-initiative interfaces; AI in the workplace

Proactive AI Adoption can be Threatening: When Help Backfires

1
Introduction

Artificial intelligence (AI) assistants such as ChatGPT, GitHub Copilot, Microsoft 365 Copilot, and Anthropic are rapidly becoming part of everyday work practices. Field and experimental studies show that such tools can substantially increase productivity across domains: ChatGPT support improved professional writing speed and quality
(
Noy and Zhang 2023
)
, GitHub Copilot accelerated coding performance
(
Peng et al. 2023
)
, and generative AI increased customer support efficiency
(
Brynjolfsson et al. 2023
)
and consulting output quality
(
Dell’Acqua et al. 2023
)
. Industry reports echo these findings, highlighting how workplace AI assistants are being integrated at scale
(
Butler et al. 2023
)
.

These tools promise to boost productivity by drafting text, writing code, generating insights, and even conducting real-time meeting summarization with little effort from the user. Their growing presence in workplace technologies means that employees increasingly encounter AI not as an optional tool but as an embedded collaborator. Yet realizing the benefits of these systems depends not only on technical capability but also on whether employees are willing to adopt and rely on them in their daily work.

A central question concerns
initiative
: should assistants wait for explicit requests, or intervene proactively? For example, GitHub Copilot can generate code snippets in real time without prompting, Gmail’s Smart Compose fills in sentences as users type, and Microsoft 365 Copilot suggests edits or follow-up actions during meetings. Such anticipatory behaviors are already widespread, yet their effects on adoption remain unclear. While prior work has documented that mismatches in proactivity can reduce trust and acceptance
(
Liao et al. 2016
;
Meurisch et al. 2020
)
, the underlying
psychological mechanisms
are less well understood. This, in turn, raises the need to examine why people reject proactive AI assistance even when it could be useful.

To address this gap, we draw on organizational psychology and management research on helping. In managerial research regarding human–human collaboration, help can be
reactive
(requested by the recipient) or
anticipatory
(unsolicited, either suggested or delivered)
(
Nadler and Jeffrey 1986
;
Harari et al. 2022
)
. While reactive help is consistent with reciprocity norms of social exchange theory
(
Cropanzano and Mitchell 2005
;
Emerson 1976
)
, anticipatory help can threaten recipients’ independence and competence and is more likely to threaten reciprocity norms. According to self-affirmation theory, this effect emerges because unsolicited help undermines people’s positive self-view, producing defensive reactions
(
Steele 1988
;
Sherman and Cohen 2006
)
. We theorize that these dynamics also apply to human–AI interaction: anticipatory AI help may be perceived as more threatening than reactive help, even when the actual assistance provided is identical. Furthermore, in human-AI interaction it is more salient that anticipatory help can take different forms:
offering
(the system suggests help but awaits user acceptance) and
providing
(the system delivers help automatically).

We investigate these dynamics through two vignette-based experiments with large samples. Study 1 used a 2 (help type: anticipatory vs. reactive)
\times
2 (help source: AI vs. human) design to test whether anticipatory help is more threatening than reactive help (H1), whether threat mediates adoption outcomes (H2), and whether threat responses differ by help source (H3). Study 2, preregistered, extended this design by comparing reactive help to two forms of anticipatory help - offering and providing (H4). Across both studies, we focus on
self-threat
, i.e., help recipients’ experience of potential harm to their positive sense of self
(
Steele 1988
)
, as a mechanism linking AI initiative to user adoption outcomes.

Results from Study 1 suggest that reactive AI help was rated as more threatening than reactive human help, suggesting that reciprocity norms buffer human but not AI helpers. Results from both studies show anticipatory help elicited significantly more self-threat than reactive help, which in turn reduced adoption outcomes of AI (reduced willingness to accept assistance, reduced likelihood of future use, and reduced performance expectancy) of AI tools. In Study 2, both
offering
and
providing
anticipatory AI help heightened self-threat relative to reactive help, and the two forms did not significantly differ. Together, these results identify self-threat as a mechanism through which proactive AI features may backfire by lowering adoption of AI tools at work.

This paper makes three contributions:

1.

We advance accounts of mixed-initiative interaction by showing that identity-related costs, not just trust or expectation mismatches, explain rejection of proactive systems.

2.

We isolate the effect of initiative-taking on self-threat by integrating psychological theories with HCI research and provide evidence from two large-scale vignette experiments

3.

We derive design principles for AI initiative.

2
Related Work

Our work builds on three bodies of literature: HCI research on system initiative, organizational and psychological research on help-seeking, and empirical studies of AI adoption. Together, these literatures motivate our focus on
self-threat
as a mechanism explaining when and why proactive AI help undermines user adoption.

2.1
System Initiative in HCI

HCI has long examined how initiative is negotiated between users and systems. Horvitz’s classic work on mixed-initiative interfaces emphasized that system actions should be calibrated to confidence, user goals, and the cost of errors
(
Horvitz 1999
)
. Amershi et al.’s
(
Amershi et al. 2019
)
guidelines codified these insights into widely adopted principles for human–AI interaction, such as timing system actions appropriately and clarifying why the system acted. While these frameworks address
when
initiative may be appropriate, they leave psychological consequences under-specified.

Prior studies on proactivity found that proactive features are often perceived as interruptive or intrusive.

Liao et al. 2016
conducted a field study of AI in the workplace and found that proactive assistance carries a risk of interruption and is often not utilized. A survey on expectations from proactive AI systems
Meurisch et al. 2020
found that users expect such systems to balance initiative with control, transparency, and context-awareness, and that personality traits and socio-demographic characteristics affect user expectations for proactivity. A study of a stress management system found that proactive nudges were only well-received when they were carefully timed and personalized
(
Kocielnik et al. 2018
)
. Reports from industry mirror these findings: proactive Copilot features can accelerate productivity but also overwhelm workers when assistance is unsolicited
(
Butler et al. 2023
;
Block et al. 2024
)
. These studies demonstrate that initiative-taking is consequential, but they do not identify the psychological mechanism through which it affects user adoption. We address this gap by examining
self-threat
as one such mechanism.

2.2
Barriers to AI Adoption

Even when AI systems deliver measurable performance gains, adoption is not guaranteed. Large-scale field studies show productivity improvements across domains—writing
(
Noy and Zhang 2023
)
, customer support
(
Brynjolfsson et al. 2023
)
, programming
(
Peng et al. 2023
)
, and consulting
(
Dell’Acqua et al. 2023
)
. Yet, employees often remain reluctant to rely on AI tools. Research highlights barriers such as algorithm aversion
(
Dietvorst et al. 2015
)
, trust and transparency concerns
(
Chiu et al. 2021
;
Glikson and Woolley 2020
)
, and social consequences of adoption
(
Tang et al. 2023
;
Man Tang et al. 2022
;
Berg et al. 2023
)
. Explanations and transparency features can build trust
(
Ehsan et al. 2021
)
, but they may not resolve deeper feelings of threat. This suggests that adoption is shaped not only by accuracy and usability, but also by how assistance interacts with basic psychological needs for autonomy and competence.

2.3
Psychological Perspectives on Help-Seeking

Psychological research on helping offers perspective on why initiative taking may backfire. Previous research distinguishes between
reactive help
(requested by the recipient) and
anticipatory help
(unsolicited: offered or provided without an explicit request)
(
Nadler and Jeffrey 1986
;
Nadler 1991
)
. Although reactive help is often welcomed, anticipatory help can feel intrusive and threatening. Harari et al.
(
Harari et al. 2022
)
show that anticipatory help, especially from higher-status helpers, increases recipient self-threat and reduces evaluations of the helper and willingness to accept help. Relatedly, Lee et al.
(
Lee et al. 2019
)
show that anticipatory help is less likely to relate to gratitude and positive work outcomes.

The integration of two theoretical perspectives explain these effects.
Social exchange theory
suggests that solicited help is more likely to align with reciprocity norms, whereas unsolicited help disrupts them, leading recipients to devalue the helper
(
Cropanzano and Mitchell 2005
;
Emerson 1976
)
.
Self-affirmation theory
highlights another psychological mechanism: unsolicited help challenges recipient’s positive self-view by implying incompetence, and can therefore trigger threat
(
Steele 1988
;
Sherman and Cohen 2006
)
. This threat can trigger defensive reactions, including rejecting help or lowering the evaluations of the helper. Related work on feedback interventions similarly shows that unsolicited or negative feedback often undermines motivation and other positive outcomes
(
Ilgen and Davis 2000
;
Kluger and DeNisi 1996
)
. Importantly, these dynamics vary with context: the threat is strongest when help in given for identity-relevant tasks
(
Harari et al. 2022
)
.

2.4
Extending to Human–AI Interaction

We theorize that these dynamics extend to human–AI interaction. Anticipatory AI help—whether suggesting assistance (
offering
) or taking action automatically (
providing
)—may threaten users’ sense of competence and autonomy, reducing willingness to adopt AI tools. Reactive AI help, by contrast, affirms user control and is less likely to trigger threat.

Importantly, AI helpers may differ from human helpers. Reciprocity norms that often buffer human-to-human assistance may not apply to AI systems, meaning that even reactive AI help could feel more threatening than reactive human help. This builds on the Computers Assess Social Actors (CASA) paradigm, which shows that people apply social rules—such as reciprocity—to computers, but often in domain-dependent or mismatchable ways
(
Nass et al. 1994
;
Nass and Moon 2000
)
. Recent HCI studies suggest that human–AI interactions draw on familiar social processes while also introducing distinct ones: for example, trust formation can resemble human–human dynamics yet diverges due to opacity and lack of reciprocity
(
Glikson and Woolley 2020
)
, and mismatches between user expectations and system initiative can undermine trust in unique ways
(
Meurisch et al. 2020
)
.

Moreover, emerging work that has relied on the managerial research about helping has begun to explore how proactive vs. reactive AI behavior shapes perceptions of competence and system satisfaction. In particular, recent Information Systems research highlights similar dynamics. For example, Diebel et al.
(
Diebel et al. 2024
)
show that proactive AI support can reduce users’ competence-based self-esteem, particularly when the AI demonstrates high levels of knowledge. Their findings reinforce our argument that unsolicited initiative can undermine psychological needs for competence and autonomy, even when the underlying assistance is accurate or useful. Building on this, our work situates self-threat as the mechanism explaining such effects and extends them into the HCI domain, where adoption decisions are shaped not only by accuracy and usability but also by how proactive behaviors are experienced in everyday interactions with AI systems.
In HCI and CSCW literature, proactive initiative raises important questions about agency distribution and negotiation, particularly in creative or co-creative contexts
(
Wu et al. 2025
;
Chen et al. 2025
)
. Together, these lines of work motivate our empirical focus on
self-threat
as the mechanism linking initiative-taking (anticipatory vs. reactive) to adoption outcomes.

3
Hypotheses

Building on the literature reviewed above, we develop four hypotheses about how different forms of AI help influence perceptions of threat and subsequent adoption outcomes. Our goal is to examine the psychological effects of AI help and its different levels of initiative and to test whether self-threat mediates the effects of AI assistance and its various levels of initiative on the outcomes of AI adoption at work (willingness to accept help, future use of AI, performance expectancy of AI).

H1: Anticipatory help will be perceived as more threatening than reactive help.

Because anticipatory help is unsolicited, it is more likely to challenge recipients’ sense of competence and independence, producing greater threat compared to reactive (solicited) help.

H2: Perceived threat will mediate the relationship between help type and adoption outcomes.

Specifically, proactive (vs. reactive) help will indirectly reduce (a) willingness to accept help, (b) likelihood of future use, and (c) performance expectancy of AI tools, via increased threat.

H3: The source of help will moderate threat responses.

We expect that AI help will be more threatening than human help, particularly in reactive conditions where reciprocity norms usually buffer against threat.

H4: The form of anticipatory AI help will further shape threat perceptions.

We distinguish between
offering
(suggesting help but awaiting acceptance) and
providing
(delivering help automatically). Both are expected to increase threat relative to reactive help, but offering may attenuate threat by preserving minimal user agency.

Together, these hypotheses specify a framework in which
self-threat serves as the key mechanism
linking initiative-taking to adoption outcomes, while the source and type of help shape the degree of threat experienced by help recipients’ as summarized in Figure
1
.

4.1
Study 1: Help Type and Source

Design.

Study 1 employed a 2 (help type: anticipatory vs. reactive)
\times
2 (source: AI vs. human) between-subjects design. This allowed us to test whether anticipatory help increases perceived self-threat compared to reactive help (H1), whether self-threat mediates adoption outcomes (H2), and whether the effects differ by source: AI vs. human help (H3).

Participants.

We recruited 801 full-time working adults in the United States via Prolific Academic. After excluding participants who failed attention checks (
n=40
), the final sample was 761 participants (62% male,
M_{age}=40.7
,
SD=11.1
) from a broad range of occupations and industries.

Procedure.

Participants were asked to imagine themselves as part of a consulting team preparing a client presentation. Depending on condition, they either requested help (reactive) or received unsolicited help (anticipatory), and the helper was described as either a colleague (human) or ChatGPT (AI).

For example, in the
AI anticipatory providing
condition, participants read:

As you are working, a message from ChatGPT pops up: “Hey, let me help you with this. Just upload the data, and I’ll work on some ideas for the next project meeting.”

In the
AI reactive
condition:

You open ChatGPT and type, “Hey, can you help me with this? If I upload the data, maybe you can help me work on some ideas for the next project meeting?” ChatGPT responds: “Of course, I would be happy to help.”

In the human-helper conditions, the helper was framed as a colleague named Taylor who sent the message via Microsoft Teams, with otherwise parallel wording. Full vignette texts are provided in Appendix
C
.

Measures.

After reading the vignette, participants completed the following measures:

•

Self-threat
: 5 items adapted from prior work
(
Burris 2012
;
Harari et al. 2022
)
(e.g., “This helper makes me question my competence”),
\alpha=.92
.

Adoption outcomes.

•

Willingness to accept help
: 4 items
(
Harari et al. 2022
)
(e.g., “I would allow this helper to assist me”),
\alpha=.98
.

•

Future use
: a single-item measure of likelihood of continued use of the technology
(
Harari et al. 2022
)
.

•

Performance expectancy
: 4 items from UTAUT
(
Venkatesh et al. 2016
)
(e.g., “Using this helper would increase my productivity”),
\alpha=.95
.

Together, willingness, future use, and performance expectancy served as
adoption outcomes
. Reliability estimates were high, and manipulation checks confirmed the effectiveness of both help type and source manipulations (Appendix
A
).

Data Analysis.

To test H1 and H3, we conducted a two-way ANOVA predicting perceived self-threat from help type (reactive vs. anticipatory), source (AI vs. human), and their interaction. To test H2, we conducted mediation analyses using path models in which self-threat predicted willingness to accept help, future use, and performance expectancy. Indirect effects were estimated with bias-corrected bootstrapping (5,000 samples). All multi-item scales showed high reliability, and manipulation checks confirmed that participants perceived both manipulations as intended.

6
Discussion

Taken together, our research contributes to HCI’s longstanding concerns about system initiative. In particular, by adding psychological and managerial theoretical perspectives to HCI research, our findings show that initiative-taking in AI systems can have psychological effects that can shape adoption, beyond the concerns regarding usability or accuracy. Across two large vignette experiments, anticipatory (unsolicited) help consistently elicited self-threat, which in turn reduced adoption outcomes: willingness to accept assistance, likelihood of future use, and performance expectancy of AI tools. In this section, we interpret these results in light of psychological and HCI theory, highlight their implications for mixed-initiative design, reflect on methodological tradeoffs, and identify directions for future research.

6.1
Anticipatory help carries psychological costs

Across both studies, unsolicited assistance was more threatening than reactive help, and this threat mediated worse adoption outcomes: lower willingness to accept, less future use of AI tools, and lower performance expectancy of AI tools. These findings align with classic accounts of help-seeking that highlight the independence and competence costs of unsolicited help
Nadler and Jeffrey 1986
;
Nadler 1991
, and with organizational research documenting anticipatory help as threatening
Harari et al. 2022
. Our results extend prior work on proactivity mismatches
Liao et al. 2016
;
Meurisch et al. 2020
by clarifying an underlying mechanism: anticipatory help challenges users’ sense of competence and autonomy, producing
self-threat
.

Whereas earlier work framed rejection of proactive systems largely in terms of mismatched expectations or reduced trust, our results highlight that barriers can also arise from undermining the user’s positive self-view, making even accurate and well-timed help more likely to fail. Related literature on feedback similarly show that responses become less constructive when they shift attention toward the self
Kluger and DeNisi 1996
;
Ilgen and Davis 2000
.
Our findings are consistent with this pattern: unsolicited assistance may have shifted interpretation from being supportive to signaling incompetence, therefore challenging positive self-views and being threatening. Threat, in turn, predicted lower willingness to accept help, lower performance expectancy, and lower likelihood of future use of AI tools.

6.2
Reactive AI help is not immune from threat

Even when participants requested help, AI was experienced as more threatening than a human helper. This suggests that the social norm of reciprocity in human–human helping interactions
Nadler 1991
may not extend to AI in the same way. The result challenges the view that giving users control is sufficient to foster acceptance; the source of the help matters even when initiative is user-driven. This nuance complements evidence on algorithm aversion
Dietvorst et al. 2015
and work on social responses to computers
Nass et al. 1994
;
Nass and Moon 2000
, underscoring that people do not apply the same social rules to AI or do so a way that is uneven or mismatch-prone.

6.3
Offering vs. providing did not reduce threat

Contrary to a common design intuition, anticipatory
offering
(suggesting help and awaiting acceptance) did not reliably reduce threat compared to anticipatory
providing
(acting automatically). In our vignette-based setting, preserving minimal agency did not mitigate threat. This null result suggests that unsolicited help itself may carry sufficient psychological weight to overshadow smaller differences in framing.

Importantly, the null effect still has design implications. It suggests that simply offering instead of providing may not be enough to eliminate threat, particularly in short-term or low-stakes tasks. For example, a coding copilot that auto-completes functions (providing) may not be experienced as less threatening if it merely switches to a confirmation prompt (offering) before inserting the completion; similarly, a coding assistant surfacing edits as suggestions may still be interpreted as unsolicited critique of one’s programming. In both cases, our findings suggest that the
unsolicited
nature of the help, more than the micro-form of delivery, drives users’ threat.

6.4
Implications for mixed-initiative design

Classic HCI research on mixed-initiative interaction emphasized calibrating initiative to confidence, error costs, and user context
Horvitz 1999
. Amershi et al.’s
(
Amershi et al. 2019
)
guidelines distilled these principles into actionable rules such as “time services based on context” and “make clear why the system did what it did.” Our results extend these guidelines by introducing
psychological readiness
: designers must consider not only when the system is confident enough to act, but also when users are receptive to unsolicited help. We highlight three grounded implications.

Account for psychological costs alongside accuracy and error.
Anticipatory help increased self-threat even in low-stakes scenarios. This suggests that initiative calibration should include psychological costs, not only epistemic ones. In practice, systems that act proactively when confidence is high may still be poorly received if the help makes users feel less competent or independent.

Help source matters even under user initiation.
Reactive AI help elicited more threat than reactive human help. Designers should not assume that giving users control guarantees positive reception. The identity of the source matters: AI may require additional care to avoid signaling incompetence, even when the user explicitly requests help.

Minimal agency may not suffice.
In our studies, anticipatory
offering
(asking before helping) did not reduce threat compared to anticipatory
providing
(acting automatically). Simply inserting a confirmation step may be insufficient when assistance is unsolicited. Designers may need to consider richer ways of preserving user agency, such as tailoring the timing or form of proactive help rather than relying on a single acceptance prompt.

Beyond these implications, our results also motivate several directions for design exploration:

Progressive disclosure of proactivity.
Rather than introducing full proactivity immediately, systems can scale initiative gradually, allowing users to acclimate. For instance, Gmail’s Smart Compose began with short word completions and only later moved to full sentences. A similar staged approach could help reduce perceived threat in other domains, such as coding copilots that first provide completions on request before shifting toward anticipatory suggestions.

Collaborative framing.
How proactive help is worded matters. Even neutrally presented anticipatory help produced threat in our studies, highlighting the importance of framing. A system might affirm competence by positioning its contribution as collaborative rather than corrective. For example, instead of “I created a draft for you,” a writing assistant might say “Here are some ideas to build on your draft.” Such framing can affirm user agency.

Reciprocity cues.
Human helpers often buffer threat through reciprocity norms, but AI does not benefit from these norms in the same way. Designers may experiment with ways of simulating reciprocity, such as thanking users for prior input, explicitly acknowledging their role, or framing suggestions as augmentations rather than replacements. These interventions can be empirically tested to assess whether they reduce perceived threat and improve adoption.

Positive affirmation framing.
Our research highlights the challenges to people’s positive self views that is inherent in proactive helping interactions. Therefore, providing positive affirmations to users can mitigate the potential harm to their positive sense of self. As such, a system might provide positive affirmations to user’s performance prior to providing help with the areas in which required assistance is identified (e.g., your writing is well-done and coherent - good job! There are small changes you can make that will strengthen your writing even further.”). Such framing can provide positive self affirmations and hence decrease the extent to which proactive helping from AI is threatening for users, which in turn will increase future adoption and use of AI tools.

In short, our results extend existing mixed-initiative guidelines by introducing the design implications of
psychological processes
. Initiative-taking should be calibrated not only to accuracy or error costs, but also to users’ sense of competence and independence. Moreover, insights from research on human help may suggest design ideas to mitigate threat.

6.5
Limitations

Our vignette-based design isolated initiative-taking as a psychological variable with strong internal validity
Aguinis and Bradley 2014
, but at the cost of ecological validity. Real-world interfaces include richer cues (timing, interface design, accumulated history) that may moderate threat responses. Task domain also matters: presentation preparation may not reflect all identity-relevant settings, and our samples focused on U.S.-based full-time workers; cultural differences in help and feedback norms may yield different threat profiles
Morrison et al. 2004
;
Lee 1997
. Finally, our outcomes capture intentions rather than revealed behavior; linking threat to actual usage is an important next step.

6.6
Future research

Future work should evaluate these dynamics in interactive systems and over repeated interactions, test whether richer forms of agency or transparency reduce threat, and examine domain and cultural moderators. Personalization is a promising direction: the same user may prefer anticipatory help in a domain that is less identity-central (e.g., cooking) while preferring reactive help in a domain that is more central to their sense of self and competence (e.g., writing).
Team- and organization-level consequences are also open questions, as proactive AI may shift help-seeking norms and collaborative workflows. Cross-cultural studies are likewise critical, as unsolicited help may be interpreted differently in collectivistic vs. individualistic cultures.

Taken together, these avenues underscore the importance of treating initiative not only as a technical capability but also as a psychological design variable. By integrating psychological theories of help-seeking with HCI design, we provide both rigorous empirical evidence and actionable insights for building AI systems that balance proactivity with user agency.

7
Conclusion

Across two vignette-based experiments, we found that anticipatory AI help consistently heightened self-threat, which in turn reduced willingness to accept help, likelihood of future use, and performance expectancy. We also showed that even reactive AI help was more threatening than human help, highlighting limits of reciprocity norms in human–AI contexts. By identifying self-threat as a psychological mechanism underlying adoption barriers, this work extends HCI research on mixed-initiative systems with a theoretically grounded account of why proactive features often backfire. Initiative-taking should therefore be understood not only as a technical or design decision, but also in light of the psychological processes that it triggers and can shape user adoption of AI in organizations.

Appendix C
Appendix C: Full Vignette Texts

Below we reproduce the full vignette text for each experimental condition.

Study 1 Examples

AI Reactive:

You open ChatGPT and type, “Hey, can you help me with this? If I upload the data, maybe you can help me work on some ideas for the next project meeting?” ChatGPT responds: “Of course, I would be happy to help.”

AI Anticipatory:

As you are working, a message from ChatGPT pops up: “Hey, let me help you with this. Just upload the data, and I’ll work on some ideas for the next project meeting.”

Human Reactive:

You open Microsoft Teams and message Taylor, a colleague: “Hey, can you help me with this? If I upload the data, maybe you can help me work on some ideas for the next project meeting?” Taylor responds: “Of course, I would be happy to help.”

Human Anticipatory:

As you are working, a message from Taylor (via Microsoft Teams) pops up: “Hey, let me help you with this. Just upload the data, and I’ll work on some ideas for the next project meeting.”

Study 2 Examples

Reactive:

You open PPointAI and type, “Hey, can you help me with this? If I upload the data, maybe you can help me work on some ideas for the next project meeting?” PPointAI responds: “Of course, I would be happy to help.”

Anticipatory Offering:

As you are working, a message from PPointAI pops up: “Hey, let me help you with this. Just upload the data, and I’ll work on some ideas for the next project meeting. Would you like me to proceed?”

Anticipatory Providing:

As you are working, a message from PPointAI pops up: “Hey, I took the liberty of helping you with this. Using your data, I generated some ideas and I’m attaching a draft presentation for the next project meeting.”
