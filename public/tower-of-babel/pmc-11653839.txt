Qualitative research on the flipped classroom cognition of undergraduate nursing students
Authors: Ying Wang, Gai-Jing Wang, Li-Jing Yan, Jing Gao, Cong Fu, Hai-Mei Ren
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo assess undergraduate nursing students' understanding of the flipped classroom model, facilitate its integration into nursing education, and support its implementation and reform.MethodsThe in-depth interview method of phenomenological research was used to interview 10 undergraduate nursing students during their course on 15 January 2024 in the classroom. The Colaizzi analysis method was employed for the data analysis.ResultsStudents' cognition of the flipped classroom model highlights three main aspects: limitations of traditional education, improved understanding of the flipped classroom despite its infrequent use in higher education, and its potential benefits, including increased learning interest, better learning outcomes, enhanced language skills, improved nursing research abilities, and stronger emotional connections between teachers and students. Challenges include increased workload, low self-discipline in self-directed learning, classroom time inefficiencies, and difficulty replacing traditional teacher-led instruction. The cognition of the flipped classroom among undergraduate nursing students can be summarised into three aspects: the restraints posed by traditional education models; the improvement in students’ understanding of the flipped classroom model – although its usage in college education is still rare; and the model’s potential to enhance learning interest, improve learning outcomes, foster language skills, enhance nursing research capabilities, and increase emotional interaction between teachers and students. However, several challenges persist, such as increased learning burdens, low student self-discipline in self-directed learning, substantial time wastage in class, and the difficulty of substituting traditional teacher-led instructions.ConclusionThe ongoing updates in educational models suggest that the flipped classroom model is conducive to improving learning outcomes and promoting the effective integration of theory and practice. However, further enhancements to this model are needed as it continues to be applied.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-024-06426-7.

Introduction

The development of Internet technology has promoted constant innovation in educational methods. The new wave of nursing education reform has made it imperative for nurse educators to design instructional approaches that meet both the needs of the current healthcare system and the learning needs of students [1]. The emergence of the flipped classroom education model provides a new impetus for reforming our educational approach. Unlike the traditional model, where teachers lecture in class and students complete exercises afterwards [2], the flipped classroom reverses this process. In a flipped classroom, students are presented with lesson material before class, allowing class time to be used for deeper exploration through interactive activities [3]. This realignment of in-class and out-of-class time shifts the decision of learning from the teacher to the student. The flipped classroom is student-centred [4], with relevant topics discussed in class and the teacher acting as a knowledge facilitator [5]. This approach allows teachers to focus on solving the most difficult problems [6], better allocate class time, enhance problem-solving skills for both teachers and students [7] and improve academic performance. However, compared with traditional classrooms, students often struggle to adapt quickly to this paradigm shift, and educators may not be adequately prepared, resulting in less effective classes than anticipated [8].

The concept of the flipped classroom, which originated in the United States, has been implemented and adopted by high schools and universities worldwide. Initially appearing in secondary schools, it was later introduced to universities [9]. There are some experiences in the research of flipped classrooms in China, primarily focused on middle schools [10], and it has been more widely used in college English teaching [11]. However, this teaching model is rarely used in undergraduate nursing education courses. The field of nursing is continuously developing in China, making the education of undergraduate nursing students particularly important. The traditional education model has some shortcomings, such as weak student autonomy, a disconnect between theory and practice, and poor student ability [12]. To address this gap, our study focuses on applying the flipped classroom approach to nursing classes.

This study aims to use the phenomenology research method in qualitative research to understand the degree of understanding of the flipped classroom among undergraduate nursing students and to promote its application in undergraduate nursing teaching. By promoting nursing students’ problem-solving skills and improving their comprehensive quality, this study seeks to provide a basis for implementing and reforming teaching modes in nursing education. This study addresses the following research questions:What is the level of understanding and acceptance of the flipped classroom model among Chinese nursing students?How does the flipped classroom teaching method compare with traditional teaching methods in terms of advantages and challenges?What are the actual effects and outcomes of implementing the flipped classroom teaching method in nursing education?

Participants and methods

This study employed phenomenological research methodology as described by Colaizzi (1978), which focuses on understanding lived experiences through detailed description and systematic analysis [13]. This approach was chosen because it allows researchers to explore the essence of participants' experiences with the flipped classroom while bracketing preconceptions. The phenomenological method enables deep insight into how nursing students perceive and experience this pedagogical approach in their learning journey.

Participants of the study

This study was conducted at the School of Nursing, Hebei University. The participating students were third-year undergraduate nursing students enrolled in the Bachelor of Nursing program. The flipped classroom intervention was implemented in core nursing courses including Medical-Surgical Nursing and Fundamentals of Nursing. Before class, students engaged with instructor-prepared materials including video lectures, reading materials, and self-assessment quizzes through the university's learning management system. During class time, students participated in case discussions, simulation exercises, and problem-solving activities facilitated by experienced nursing educators. The course assessment included both formative (ongoing classroom participation and group work) and summative components (final examinations).The qualitative research method was used, employing random sampling to select participants from a list of undergraduate nursing students, ensuring that each student had an equal chance of being included in the study. The sample was selected during the students’ school day on 15 January 2024 in the classroom.

The inclusion criteria included the following: (1) undergraduate nursing students who are interns in the affiliated hospital of Hebei University; (2) those who have experienced the flipped classroom education model in their undergraduate nursing education; (3) students who can express their ideas clearly; and (4) voluntary participation in the study.

The exclusion criteria included the following: (1) students with speech impairments that hinder normal communication; (2) individuals with a history of mental illness; and (3) those who have experienced bereavement, divorce, or other substantial personal events affecting their emotional state in the last month.

This study obtained informed consent from all research participants and was approved by the ethics committee. All participants signed an informed consent form.

The sample size of the interviews was determined by saturation, continuing until no new topics emerged. During the interviews, sessions were recorded with the interviewees’ consent and were structured according to the interview outlines to ensure comprehensive coverage of all topics. Information saturation was reached after interviewing 10 undergraduate nursing students (Table 1). Research methods

Interview outline

The interview outline was developed based on comprehensive literature review of flipped classroom implementation in Chinese nursing education context. We particularly drew from the systematic review by Xu et al. (2019) [14], which analyzed 22 Chinese studies and identified key aspects of flipped classroom effectiveness in nursing education, including practical ability development, communication skills enhancement, and student engagement. Recent empirical research by Li et al. (2020) further informed our understanding of specific implementation strategies in the Chinese nursing education context [14, 15]. The core contents of the interview outline include the following: (1) In your undergraduate nursing education, which courses incorporate the flipped classroom model, and how many hours are dedicated to this? (2) How would you describe the flipped classroom as a teaching model? How does it differ from the teaching models you have experienced in the past? (3) Compared with traditional teaching methods, what advantages and challenges do you think the flipped classroom approach presents? (4) Do you believe it is necessary to implement the flipped classroom model in undergraduate nursing education in China? Why or why not?

Data collection methods

This study employs semi-structured interviews and observations to gather data. Each interviewee is referred to as a case and is numbered sequentially from A to J. Before conducting the interviews, the researchers received thorough training, which included interview and communication skills, as well as effective listening and questioning techniques to avoid leading questions. Each subject was interviewed by a team of two researchers. Before the interview, the purpose and significance of the study were explained to the participants, confidentiality of the interview contents was ensured, and informed consent was obtained and signed. The researchers and participants agreed on the interview time and place in advance to ensure a quiet, comfortable, and uninterrupted environment. During the interview, the interviewer listened attentively, observed the emotional changes of the interviewee, and recorded the entire interview process. Interview notes and memos were also prepared. Each interview lasted between 30 and 40 min and was recorded in full. To ensure research rigor, several strategies were employed. The research team consisted of three nursing educators with extensive teaching experience (5–15 years) and two qualitative research specialists. All team members maintained reflexive journals throughout the study to document their assumptions, beliefs, and potential biases. Regular team meetings were held to discuss and challenge these perspectives. The iterative process involved preliminary analysis after each interview, allowing for refinement of subsequent interviews and ensuring theoretical saturation. Member checking was conducted with participants to verify the accuracy of interview transcripts and interpretations. An audit trail was maintained documenting all methodological decisions and analytical processes.

Documentation arrangement

Within 24 h of the interview, the recordings were transcribed into text by two researchers. After the initial sorting, the original materials were stored separately for future reference.

Data analysis

The Colaizzi analysis method was used to analyse the data, combining NVIVO 11.0 software with manual analysis. This process was divided into three stages: preparation, coding, and analysis. Following the three-level coding steps, the original data were carefully read to extract meaningful keywords or sentences. Recurring viewpoints were coded, and 15 nodes were created to classify these encoded viewpoints. Excerpts that were consistent and meaningful to the undergraduate nursing interns’ understanding of the flipped classroom were summarised and refined. These nodes were then conceptualised and categorised, forming themes and theme groups, which led to the development of the result coding system for analysis and complete description. Finally, to ensure the authenticity and accuracy of the topic abstraction, the results were returned to the interviewees for verification of the content’s authenticity [16]. For detailed analysis results, see Appendix 1 on the last page.

Control bias

To effectively manage and mitigate bias within the interview process, it is crucial to adhere to the following essential steps:

(1) Before the interview, ensure thorough personnel training for the researcher, covering interview and communication skills, and avoid using provocative questions. (2) During the interview, techniques were employed to create a relaxed atmosphere, helping to alleviate the nervousness of the research participants and enabling them to express their feelings and thoughts fully. (3) Use a two-person interview approach to observe and record the facial expressions and body language of the research participants. (4) Have two individuals organise and analyse the data to prevent any ambiguity in the analysis results. (5) Return the results to the interviewees to further verify the authenticity of the information gathered.

Results

A Comparison of traditional education models and flipped classroom education models

Traditional education models constrict students

Traditional education often involves a lecture-based approach where students passively receive information. Respondent A noted that in traditional settings, students often become distracted, leading to missed content. Respondent D echoed this, stating that the lack of interactive discussion limits deeper understanding. Respondent H added that traditional models may create "examination machines," hindering overall student development. These insights highlight the constraints of traditional methods in fostering comprehensive learning and personal growth.

Increased awareness of the flipped classroom model

In contrast, the flipped classroom model emphasizes pre-class preparation and interactive in-class activities. Respondent G appreciated the model for its open communication between students and teachers. Respondent D noted that it shifts the focus to student-led learning, with class time dedicated to discussing and applying knowledge. Respondent I described it as a model where pre-class preparation allows for in-depth discussion and problem-solving during class.

The Application of flipped classrooms in college education is less common

The flipped classroom teaching model is used relatively often in primary and secondary schools but is less common in university education, especially in undergraduate nursing education [17]. Respondent B noted, ‘It was used in infectious disease nursing and obstetric nursing, but more often in some courses such as case analysis, about four hours’. Respondent E commented, ‘It has been used in nursing education and aged care, about five lessons’.

Positive themes

Increased learning interest

The flipped classroom advocates returning the classroom to the students, where the learning is student-led, with teachers guiding and assisting. This approach requires students to take the initiative to access materials and communicate with both teachers and peers when they encounter problems. The application of the flipped classroom teaching model has substantially enhanced students’ interest in active learning [18]. Respondent A stated, ‘With the application of the flipped classroom, students become the masters of the classroom, making them more active, engaged, and focused’. Respondent F added, ‘The flipped classroom is a new classroom mode. It differs from the traditional model, in which students just listen to the teacher. It allows students to seek answers independently and learn actively. This improves learning efficiency and increases interest, making the classroom more lively and content-rich than the traditional one’.

Enhanced learning effect

The flipped classroom teaching model improves students’ autonomy. Through group discussions, students present their findings, enhancing their understanding and forming their own knowledge systems, leading to deeper memory retention and better learning outcomes [19]. Respondent B noted, ‘The flipped classroom model allows us to internalise our professional knowledge, discuss and understand it, and then express it according to our own understanding, thus enhancing our learning effect’. Respondent E commented, ‘The traditional model is usually passive learning, but the flipped classroom shifts from passive to active learning, helping me to deepen my understanding and mastery of knowledge while training my ability to learn independently’. Respondent H shared, ‘By flipping the classroom model of learning, my professional knowledge has been transformed into a structured knowledge system in my brain. My memory is very deep, and I am not afraid of forgetting it quickly’.

Exercise your language skills

The role reversal between students and teachers allows students to give lectures from the podium, thereby enhancing their language expression abilities and improving their psychological resilience. Respondent C mentioned, ‘This way, students can understand the knowledge points more clearly, speak more in the lectures, better organise their thoughts, improve their courage, and train their understanding and language organising abilities’. Respondent G added, ‘The change in teacher–student roles provides students with a good opportunity to exercise themselves. I feel my eloquence has improved by constantly giving lectures on the podium’.

Improving nursing research abilities

The flexible teaching model of the flipped classroom increases students’ interest in learning and enhances their exploration and acquisition of new knowledge and fields. Continuous study and communication with teachers can improve students’ nursing research abilities. Respondent J shared, ‘In this way, I found many problems I had never noticed before. These problems gave me a lot of inspiration and motivated me to think and study’.

Increasing emotional communication

The flipped classroom model requires group discussions after class, in which students report their discussion results. The key points of certain problems are discussed and solved collaboratively by teachers and students. This free discussion and learning method increases emotional communication between students and teachers. Respondent C said, ‘I feel that I have a closer relationship with my classmates and teachers by flipping the classroom and using the Internet platform to communicate with them after class’. Respondent J remarked, ‘I like this free learning style. Teachers and students work together to solve problems. Instead of a serious relationship between teachers and students, we interact more like friends’.

Negative themes

Increased learning load

Medical students generally have a heavy academic burden due to numerous theoretical courses and experiments. The flipped classroom model, which requires students to learn and discuss material in advance, further increases their workload. Respondent J remarked, ‘This model requires me to consult materials after class, which obviously increases my study time after class and reduces my discretionary time’.

Low self-discipline in autonomous learning

The effectiveness of self-study in the flipped classroom model can be limited for students with low self-discipline. This approach is more suitable for students with strong self-discipline. Respondent D stated, ‘I need to prepare myself for my study and manage myself. I feel I have some difficulties with self-discipline. I usually need a teacher to keep an eye on my learning’. Similarly, Respondent I admitted, ‘I feel that my self-control is not high enough. Usually, some things in my life will be delayed. I will be lazy and do not want to prepare for the study’.

Classroom time inefficiencies

Students’ knowledge systems are often incomplete, and their abilities vary. When encountering difficulties, students may struggle to explain concepts clearly, necessitating repeated explanations from teachers, which wastes a lot of time. Interviewee C commented, ‘Some students do not understand difficult knowledge thoroughly, cannot explain difficult content clearly, and cannot extend the knowledge points well. Therefore, teachers need to explain the difficult knowledge repeatedly’. Interviewee E added, ‘I feel that the students’ explanations in the class take up a lot of time. There is a lot of learning content, and the progress of lectures is slow. The teaching plan cannot be completed on time, which limits the knowledge we have learned’. Respondent F noted, ‘Some difficult problems are challenging for students to overcome, and they easily go wrong and waste time’.

The indispensable role of teachers

The flipped classroom teaching model requires teachers to apply it dialectically and rationally, as not all courses are suitable, and teachers should avoid simple imitation [20]. While the flipped classroom shifts some responsibility to the students, the teacher’s role in explaining complex concepts and addressing doubts remains irreplaceable.

Respondent B stated, ‘Students cannot replace teachers in explaining certain problems. In the course of teaching, teachers not only explain textbook knowledge but also expand on knowledge points, enriching students’ learning’. Respondent G added, ‘There is a difference between students’ teaching and teachers’ teaching. Not all students can master and explain knowledge effectively, which substantially impacts the quality of courses’ (Table 2). Discussion

The present study explored the perceptions of undergraduate nursing students regarding the flipped classroom model and identified several key themes. Our findings indicate that while the flipped classroom model has numerous benefits, including increased engagement, enhanced learning outcomes, improved language skills, and better emotional communication, it also presents challenges such as increased workload, issues with self-discipline, and inefficiencies in classroom time.

The results of the interview show that the flipped classroom teaching model is fundamentally different from the traditional teaching mode. This view is confirmed by a comparative study conducted by Nina Jiang and others [21]. In the traditional teaching model, the teacher solely delivers knowledge in the classroom while the students are in a passive state of listening and speaking. This severely limits student development. However, the flipped classroom teaching model reverses this dynamic, transforming students from passive recipients to active learners. In this model, teachers assist students and foster the enhancement of their overall abilities. This shift in classroom dynamics also benefits students in terms of knowledge acquisition [22]. Interest is the best teacher, as Carlos [23] pointed out. The traditional dull classroom environment is not conducive to students’ absorption and expansion of knowledge. The interview results show that open-learning thinking and independent learning can help students grasp knowledge more effectively. These results align with those of Wu Jia et al. [24].

The flipped classroom teaching model has greatly improved students’ language expression skills and communication skills and enhanced their awareness of scientific research. The research conducted by Li et al. [25] shows that the flipped classroom teaching model helps students to express themselves in the classroom. In the traditional teaching model, the teacher constantly imparts knowledge in class, and students have little opportunity to express their ideas. The flipped classroom teaching model encourages self-study after class, and students are required to present their understanding of the knowledge in class, thus enhancing their language expression and problem-solving abilities. In the process of clinical practice, this approach also benefits the improvement of students’ communication skills with others [26]. These aspects are also evident in a study conducted by Lei et al. [27]. Moreover, this type of open learning approach enables students to study and explore new knowledge constantly, thereby enhancing their awareness and abilities in scientific research.

The interview results demonstrate that the flipped classroom teaching model has bridged the gap between students and teachers. A survey conducted by Tsao et al. [28] supports this finding. The flipped classroom teaching model reverses the traditional teacher–student relationship, with students becoming the main participants in the classroom. After class, the teacher assigns tasks for self-study, and students discuss and exchange ideas in groups. During class, students and teachers interact to solve key and challenging problems. Students are encouraged to freely express their opinions, and teachers provide feedback to facilitate the learning and progress of both teachers and students [29]. This free-learning approach brings students and teachers closer, allowing them to discuss problems as friends.

Zhou [30] points out that teachers can help students improve their learning methods by guiding them at different stages. First, the teacher can identify the key and difficult points of the curriculum according to the syllabus so that students can study with a specific goal in mind. This will help students avoid wasting too much time on certain problems and improve overall class efficiency. Second, after assigning tasks, the leader of each study group should report the progress of self-study to the teacher. The flipped classroom method provides students with strategies for reflecting, summarising, and analysing problems [31]. Additionally, students should be encouraged to report and discuss any problems they encounter during the self-learning process on the interactive platform. This can help students solve common problems through after-class discussions and prevent wasting a substantial amount of time during class.

In the process of education, teachers should adhere to the principle of fairness [32]. For students who struggle with learning, assign a student with good grades to help supervise their learning. This helps ensure that the learning level of the class is roughly the same and prevents polarisation. In the classroom, teachers can give more attention to students who struggle and encourage them to express their ideas and answer questions. This approach also allows for self-improvement and supervision. Teachers should strive to consider the needs of every student in the teaching process, promoting fairness in education.

Traditional education is the model students have been accustomed to. The flipped classroom model, as a new teaching method, transforms traditional classroom lectures into opportunities for interactive learning [33]. However, this change may initially make students feel uncomfortable. Students who are unable to keep up with the pace of the class should be encouraged to adjust their learning plans for the next class and integrate classroom knowledge with clinical practice.

Designing and delivering a course well is not an easy task, and both teachers and students need to commit a substantial amount of time to effectively promote student engagement [34]. To avoid wasting time and fully utilise the teacher’s guiding role, teachers should have a detailed plan for the lesson, including pre-lesson preparation, interactive formats, and post-lesson assessment, before flipping a course. During class, the teacher should not only teach knowledge but also introduce methods and skills for self-learning and improving learning efficiency without wasting time. Additionally, teachers should observe the reactions and participation of each student to ensure that no student is left behind. At the end of the course, the teacher should reflect on how to engage every student in class discussions. Furthermore, teachers should ask students about their feelings regarding the learning atmosphere and their level of understanding of the material covered in class so that teachers can adjust their teaching methods accordingly [16].

Limitations

This study is limited by its small sample size of 10 students from a single course, which may hinder the generalisability of its conclusions due to the homogeneity of the course and class. Future research could address this limitation by conducting qualitative studies or expanding the sample size to include students from multiple flipped courses.

Conclusion

With the rise of Internet technology and new educational methods, the flipped classroom model has become prominent. This model significantly differs from traditional teaching, offering enhanced learning outcomes and overall educational quality. However, it also presents challenges such as increased student workload and the need for self-discipline. Teachers must apply this model thoughtfully and adapt it to specific course needs. Future research should focus on refining the model to address its challenges and enhance its effectiveness in nursing education.
