Realising all countable groups as quasi-isometry groups
Paula Heim, Joseph MacManus, and Lawk Mineh

Abstract
. Given any countable group G G , we construct uncountably many quasi-isometry classes of proper geodesic metric spaces with quasi-isometry group isomorphic to G G . Moreover, if the group G G is a hyperbolic group, the spaces we construct are hyperbolic metric spaces. We make use of a rigidity phenomenon for quasi-isometries exhibited by many symmetric spaces, called strong quasi-isometric rigidity. Our method involves the construction of new examples of strongly quasi-isometrically rigid spaces, arising as graphs of strongly quasi-isometrically rigid rank-one symmetric spaces.



Introduction

Quasi-isometries are among the principal morphisms in the study of large-scale structure of metric spaces, and their use is fundamental in many areas of geometry and geometric group theory.
Just as one considers the group of isometries of a metric space as a natural group of automorphisms, one can consider the
quasi-isometry group
associated to a metric space.
This is the group of equivalence classes of self-quasi-isometries of a metric space, taken up to finite distance in the supremum metric.
The primary goal of this article is to address the question of which groups may be realised as quasi-isometry groups.

The quasi-isometry groups of many familiar metric spaces are quite wild, and their general theory is poorly understood.
For instance, we have an understanding of
\QI(\mathbb{R})
: it is infinite-dimensional
[
20
]
, left-orderable and virtually a direct product
[
36
]
, centreless
[
9
]
, and contains – for example – continuum-rank free groups and Thompson’s group
F

[
30
]
.
Beyond this, little is known; see
[
37
]
for a survey on
\QI(\mathbb{R})
and
[
27
,
6
]
for some results on
\QI(\mathbb{R}^{n})
for
n>1
.
The quasi-isometry groups of various solvable groups have also been computed
[
15
,
14
,
34
]
.
Of course, quasi-isometries of spaces induce isomorphisms on quasi-isometry groups.

Most unbounded geodesic spaces seem to have tremendously large and complicated quasi-isometry groups.
On the other hand, there are somewhat pathological metric spaces with trivial quasi-isometry groups, such as the set of factorials
\{n!:n\in\mathbb{N}\}
equipped with the induced metric as a subset of
\mathbb{R}
, though this space is not even coarsely connected.
To the best of our knowledge there were previously no known examples of any metric spaces with non-trivial finite quasi-isometry groups.
We show that the maximum diversity is in fact achieved among countable groups.

Theorem A
.

Let
G
be a countable group. Then there exists uncountably many quasi-isometry classes of proper geodesic metric spaces
X
with

G\cong\QI(X).

Moreover, if
G
is a hyperbolic group, then we may take every such
X
to be hyperbolic.

The above result fits into a long history of so-called
representation problems
: given a class of objects
\mathcal{C}
, can one determine which groups
G
can be realised as
\operatorname{Aut}(X)
for an object
X\in\mathcal{C}
?
An early instance of this question was posed by König
[
26
]
, who asked whether any group can be realised as the automorphism group of a graph.
König’s question was settled for finite groups by Frucht in 1939
[
17
]
, with an elegant and simple construction, now appearing in many standard texts on graph theory.
Frucht’s result was later extended to all groups independently by de Groot
[
10
]
and Sabidussi
[
29
]
, and will play a large role in the proof of Theorem
A
.

We give a brief and non-exhaustive account of similar representation problems from geometry.
Every finite group can be realised as the isometry group of a closed hyperbolic manifold in every dimension: see Greenberg for dimension 2
[
19
]
, Kojima for dimension 3
[
25
]
, and Belolipetsky–Lubotzky for higher dimensions
[
4
]
.
In the dimension 2 case, this was extended to all countable groups independently by Winkelmann
[
33
]
and Allcock
[
1
]
.
Remarkably, there also exists a single surface (of infinite type), such that varying the Riemannian metric can produce any countable group as the corresponding isometry group
[
2
]
.
Frigerio and Martelli have also shown that any countable group arises as the mapping class group of a hyperbolic
3
-manifold
[
16
]
.

Our strategy will be to exploit a rigidity phenomenon for quasi-isometries in order to reduce to a setting where we can apply a variant of Frucht’s theorem.
We say that a metric space
X
is
strongly quasi-isometrically rigid
if the natural map
\Isom(X)\to\QI(X)
is a surjection.
That is to say, every quasi-isometry of
X
is close to an isometry
X
.
Strongly quasi-isometrically rigid spaces form one of the few classes of metric spaces for which the quasi-isometry group admits a clear description, provided one understands the isometry group.
Note that essentially all spaces that are known to be strongly quasi-isometrically rigid satisfy a slightly stronger, uniform property: see Definition
1.3
.

Strong quasi-isometric rigidity seems to be an intrinsic property of most symmetric spaces.
Notably, Pansu showed that the octonionic plane
\mathbb{O}\mathbf{H}^{2}
and the quaternionic hyperbolic spaces
\mathbb{H}\mathbf{H}^{n}
are strongly quasi-isometrically rigid, as long as
n\geq 2

[
28
]
.
Kleiner and Leeb further showed that irreducible higher-rank symmetric spaces of noncompact type and thick irreducible Euclidean buildings with cocompact affine Weyl group are also strongly quasi-isometrically rigid
[
24
]
, and Bourdon–Pajot and Xie extended this to some hyperbolic buildings
[
7
,
35
]
.
When
n\geq 3
, the Cayley graphs of maximal non-arithmetic non-uniform lattices in
n
-dimensional rank one symmetric spaces and universal covers of hyperbolic
n
-manifolds with non-empty totally geodesic boundary also enjoy this property
[
31
]
.
We also mention a beautiful, but somewhat exceptional, example of a strongly quasi-isometrically rigid space due to Kleiner and Kapovich, that is the Cayley graph of a carefully constructed hyperbolic group
[
22
]
.

As an essential step in the proof of the main theorem above, we construct new examples of strongly quasi-isometrically rigid spaces.
These arise from the following theorem, which states that well-behaved graphs of uniformly strongly quasi-isometrically rigid spaces are themselves quasi-isometrically rigid.
We can view this as a sort of combination theorem for strong quasi-isometric rigidity.

Theorem B
.

Let
\mathbf{X}
be a graph of spaces. Suppose that
\mathbf{X}
is uniformly hyperbolic, link bottlenecked, link rigid, locally congruent, and has unbounded edge spaces. If the vertex spaces of
\mathbf{X}
are uniformly strongly quasi-isometrically rigid, then so is the realisation of
\mathbf{X}
.

We direct the reader to Section
4
for a precise definition of graph of spaces in this context, and to Sections
5
and
6
for definitions of the terms appearing in the theorem statement.
We give a brief overview of this list of adjectives here.
A graph of spaces is said to be
uniformly hyperbolic
if the vertex spaces are all hyperbolic with the same hyperbolicity constant, and the edge spaces in some sense diverge uniformly quickly from one another in each vertex space.

Link bottlenecked
essentially means that the edge spaces do not coarsely separate the vertex spaces, and
link rigid
means that any isometry of a vertex space that coarsely fixes its incident edge spaces is trivial.
Finally,
local congruence
means that, for any two vertices in the same orbit of an automorphism of the underlying graph, the corresponding vertex spaces and their configuration of incident edge spaces are identical.
The properties of being uniformly hyperbolic and link bottlenecked together imply that quasi-isometries of the graph of spaces coarsely respect the graph structure, so that we may use strong quasi-isometric rigidity of the vertex spaces to promote quasi-isometries to piecewise isometries of the whole space.
Link rigidity and local congruence then ensure that the isometry group is both rich enough and well-behaved enough that we can coherently glue these piecewise isometries together into a global isometry.

This paper is structured as follows.
Section
1
contains a collection of preliminary material.
In Section
2
, we discuss a strengthening of the aforementioned theorem of Frucht.
In Section
3
, we construct a sets of points in the boundary of a visibility manifold with a certain rigidity property. The next three sections are devoted to graphs of metric spaces: Section
4
contains definitions and basic properties, Section
5
introduces uniformly hyperbolic graphs of spaces and explores their properties, and in Section
6
we obtain control over the quasi-isometry groups of graphs of spaces to obtain Theorem
B
.
Section
7
includes an explicit construction of a graph of spaces to which Theorem
B
applies, and the proof of Theorem
A
.
Finally, Section
8
is dedicated to understanding geodesics in uniformly hyperbolic graphs of spaces, which is essential for the hyperbolicity claim of Theorem
A
.

1.
Preliminaries

We begin by standardising our notation and terminology.

1.1.
Metric notions

Let
(X,\dist_{X})
be a metric space.
Write
\Isom(X)
for the group of isometries
f\colon X\to X
.
Given
r\geq 0
,
x\in X
, we write
B_{X}(x;r)
for the closed ball of radius
r
centred at
x
.
Given
A\subset X
, we define
B_{X}(A;r)
as the union of all balls
B_{X}(x;r)
with
x\in A
.
If
A,B\subset X
, we write
\dist_{X}(A,B)
to mean the infimal distance between
A
and
B
, i.e.

\dist_{X}(A,B)=\inf_{a\in A,\ b\in B}\dist_{X}(a,b).

If
A=\{x\}
is a singleton, we abuse notation and write
\dist_{X}(x,B):=\dist_{X}(\{x\},B)
.
We denote by
\dHaus_{X}(A,B)
the
Hausdorff distance
in
X
between
A
and
B
. That is,

\dHaus_{X}(A,B)=\inf\{r>0:\text{$A\subset B_{X}(B;r)$ and $B\subset B_{X}(A;r)$}\}.

For any set
Y
, denote the supremum metric on functions
f,g\colon Y\to X
as

\dist_{\infty}(f,g)=\sup_{y\in Y}\dist_{X}(f(y),g(y)),

noting that this metric can take infinite values in general.

Definition 1.1

(Quasi-isometry)
.

Let
\lambda\geq 1
and
c\geq 0
. A map
\varphi\colon X\to Y
between metric spaces is called a
(\lambda,c)
-quasi-isometric embedding
if

\frac{1}{\lambda}\dist_{X}(x,y)-c\leq\dist_{Y}(\varphi(x),\varphi(y))\leq\lambda\dist_{X}(x,y)+c

for all
x,y\in X
.
If there is
K\geq 0
such that for all
y\in Y
there exists
x\in X
such that
\dist_{Y}(y,\varphi(x))\leq K
, we say that
\varphi
is a
K
-coarsely surjective
.

A
(\lambda,c)
-quasi-isometry
is a
(\lambda,c)
-quasi-isometry embedding that is
c
-coarsely surjective.
A map
\varphi\colon X\to Y
is simply called a
quasi-isometry
if it is a
(\lambda,c)
-quasi-isometry for some
\lambda\geq 1
and
c\geq 0
.
If
\varphi\colon X\to Y
,
\psi:Y\to X
are quasi-isometries, we call
\psi
a
quasi-inverse
to
\varphi
if there is some
Q\geq 0
with
\dist_{\infty}(\psi\circ\varphi,\id_{X})\leq Q
and
\dist_{\infty}(\varphi\circ\psi,\id_{X})\leq Q
.

It is a standard fact that for every
(\lambda,c)
-quasi-isometry
\varphi\colon X\to Y
, there exists another
(\lambda,c^{\prime})
-quasi-isometry
\psi\colon Y\to X
with
\dist_{\infty}(\psi\circ\varphi,\id_{X})\leq Q
, where
Q
and
c^{\prime}
depend only on
\lambda
and
c
. We call any such function a
quasi-inverse
to
\varphi
.

Definition 1.2

(Quasi-geodesics)
.

Let
I\subseteq\mathbb{R}
be a closed interval, bounded or unbounded, and
X
an arbitrary metric space.
A
(\lambda,c)
-quasi-geodesic
in
X
is a
(\lambda,c)
-quasi-isometric embedding
\varphi\colon I\to X
.
Similarly to quasi-isometries, we sometimes omit mention of the particular constants.
We may often abuse notation and identify a quasi-geodesic with its image in
X
.
Further, we will call such
\varphi
a
(\lambda,c)
-quasi-geodesic segment, ray
or
line
when
I
is a bounded interval, a half-bounded interval, and the entire real line respectively.

As a special case, an
geodesic
in
X
will for us be an isometric embedding
I\to X
(i.e. a
(1,0)
-quasi-geodesic).
Given two points
x,y\in X
, we will often denote by
[x,y]
a choice of geodesic in
X
with endpoints
x
and
y
.

We define the equivalence relation on quasi-isometries
\varphi,\psi
by setting
\varphi\sim\psi
if and only if
\dist_{\infty}(\varphi,\psi)
is finite.
Given metric space
X
and
Y
, let

\QI(X,Y)={\mathchoice{\raisebox{3.75pt}{$\displaystyle{\{\text{quasi-isometries $\varphi\colon X\to Y$}\}}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-1.83437pt}{$\displaystyle{\sim}$}}{\raisebox{3.75pt}{$\textstyle{\{\text{quasi-isometries $\varphi\colon X\to Y$}\}}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-1.83437pt}{$\textstyle{\sim}$}}{\raisebox{3.75pt}{$\scriptstyle{\{\text{quasi-isometries $\varphi\colon X\to Y$}\}}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-1.34427pt}{$\scriptstyle{\sim}$}}{\raisebox{3.75pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle{\{\text{quasi-isometries $\varphi\colon X\to Y$}\}}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-0.99101pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle{\sim}$}}}.

It is straightforward to check that composition of maps gives rise to a well-defined group operation on this set.
We write
\QI(X)
as shorthand for
\QI(X,X)
, and we call this the
quasi-isometry group of
X
.
Of course, every isometry of
X
is a quasi-isometry, so there is a map from the isometry group of
X
to its quasi-isometry group.
In general, this map need neither be injective nor surjective.
The property of surjectivity is of particular interest, as it means that the quasi-isometries of
X
behave in a very rigid way.

Definition 1.3

(Strongly QI-rigid space)
.

We say that a metric space
X
is
strongly QI-rigid
if the natural map
\Isom(X)\to\QI(X)
is a surjection.

We say that
X
is
uniformly strongly QI-rigid
if for all
\lambda\geq 1,c\geq 0
, there is
\mu\geq 0
such that for any
(\lambda,c)
-quasi-isometry
\varphi\colon X\to X
, there is an isometry
f\in\Isom(X)
with
\dist_{\infty}(\varphi,f)\leq\mu
.

We recall the notion of coarse connectedness for a metric space.

Definition 1.4

(Coarse connectedness)
.

Let
(X,\dist)
be a metric space,
\kappa>0
a constant, and
x,y\in X
.
A
\kappa
-path
between
x
and
y
is a sequence of points
(x_{0},\dots,x_{n})
in
X
for which
x_{0}=x,x_{n}=y,
and
\dist(x_{i-1},x_{i})\leq\kappa
for every
i=1,\dots,n
.
We say that
X
is
\kappa
-coarsely connected
if there is a
\kappa
-path between any two points of
X
.

The following lemma is a well-known observation about coarse separation of subsets (cf.
[
5
, Lemma 2.3]
).
Important for us here is the uniformity of the constants involved: we include a proof to account for these details.

Lemma 1.5
.

For every
\lambda\geq 1
and
c\geq 0
there is a constant
\kappa_{0}=\kappa_{0}(\lambda,c)\geq 1
such that for any
\kappa\geq\kappa_{0}

there are
\rho=\rho(\lambda,c,\kappa)
with
\underset{\kappa\to\infty}{\lim}\rho(\lambda,c,\kappa)=\infty
and
L=L(\kappa)\geq\rho

such that the following is true.

Let
\varphi\colon X\to X^{\prime}
be a
(\lambda,c)
-quasi-isometry of metric spaces.
Suppose that
Y\subseteq X
is
\lambda
-coarsely connected,
a,b\in Y
, and
Z\subseteq X
are such that
a
and
b
lie in different
\kappa
-coarsely connected components of
Y-B_{X}(Z;\kappa)
and

\dist_{X}(a,Z)>\lambda L+\lambda c\quad\mathrm{and}\quad\dist_{X}(b,Z)>\lambda L+\lambda c.

Then
\varphi(a)
and
\varphi(b)
lie in different
\rho
-coarsely connected components of the set
\varphi(Y)-B_{X^{\prime}}(\varphi(Z);L)
.

Proof.

Let
\psi\colon X^{\prime}\to X
be a quasi-inverse of
\varphi
, so that there is
Q\geq 0
depending only on the quasi-isometry constants of
\varphi
with
\dist_{\infty}(\psi\circ\varphi,\id_{X})\leq Q
.
We may suppose that
\psi
is also a
(\lambda,c)
-quasi-isometry.
Write
\rho_{0}=\lambda^{2}+c
, and note that
\varphi(Y)
is
\rho_{0}
-coarsely connected.
We further write
\rho^{\prime}_{0}=\lambda\rho_{0}+c
.
Define
\kappa_{0}=\rho^{\prime}_{0}+Q
, let
\kappa\geq\kappa_{0}
and set
\rho=\frac{1}{\lambda}(\kappa-c-Q)
,
\rho^{\prime}=\lambda\rho+c
and
L=\lambda\kappa+c+Q
.
It is clear that
\rho
diverges as
\kappa
tends to infinity.

Let
p=(x_{0},\dots,x_{n})
be a
\rho
-path in
\varphi(Y)
whose endpoints are
\varphi(a)
and
\varphi(b)
, which exists by
\rho_{0}
-connectedness of
Y
.
Then
\psi(p)=(\psi(x_{0}),\dots,\psi(x_{n}))
is a
\rho^{\prime}
-path whose endpoints are
\psi(\varphi(a))
and
\psi(\varphi(b))
.
Since
\psi
is quasi-inverse to
\varphi
, we obtain a
\kappa
-path
(a,\psi(x_{0}),\dots,\psi(x_{n}),b)
from
a
and
b
in
Y
.
By assumption,
a
and
b
lie in different
\kappa
-coarse components of
Y-B_{X}(Z;\kappa)
, so this path contains a point
z\in Y
with
\dist_{X}(z,Z)\leq\kappa
.
The assumptions imply that
a
and
b
are at a distance of greater than
\kappa
from
Z
, so we must have
z=\psi(x_{i})
for some
i=0,\dots,n
.
But then by the fact that
\varphi
is a
(\lambda,c)
-quasi-isometry and the choice of
L
,

\dist_{X^{\prime}}(x_{i},\varphi(Z))\leq\lambda\kappa+c+Q=L.

This means that any
\rho
-path in
Y
from
\varphi(a)
to
\varphi(b)
passes
L
-close to
\varphi(Z)
.
Since
a
and
b
are a distance greater than
\lambda L+\lambda c
from
\varphi(Z)
, the points
\varphi(a)
and
\varphi(b)
are a distance greater than
L
from
\varphi(Z)
.
Therefore
\varphi(a)
and
\varphi(b)
lie in distinct
\rho
-coarsely connected components of
Y-B_{X^{\prime}}(\varphi(Z);L)
as required.
∎

1.2.
Hyperbolic metric spaces

We here recall the definition of a hyperbolic metric space, along with some useful facts.

Definition 1.6

(Gromov product)
.

Let
(X,\dist)
be a metric space, and
x,y,z\in X
be points.
The
Gromov product
of
x
and
y
with respect to
z
is

\langle x,y\rangle_{z}=\frac{1}{2}\Big(\dist(x,z)+\dist(y,z)-\dist(x,y)\Big).

If
A,B\subseteq X
are subspaces, we write

\langle A,B\rangle_{z}=\sup\{\langle a,b\rangle_{z}\,|\,a\in A,b\in B\}\in[0,\infty]

Definition 1.7

(Thin triangles)
.

Let
\Delta
be a geodesic triangle with vertices
x,y
, and
z
in a metric space
X
, and let
\delta\geq 0
.
Call
T_{\Delta}
the tripod with leg lengths
\langle x,y\rangle_{z},\langle x,z\rangle_{y},
and
\langle y,z\rangle_{z}
.
There is a unique map
\varphi\colon\Delta\to T_{\Delta}
such that
x,y
, and
z
map to the extremal vertices of
T_{\Delta}
and
\varphi
restricts to an isometry on each side of
\Delta
.
We say
\Delta
is
\delta
-thin
if
\operatorname{diam}\varphi^{-1}(\{t\})\leq\delta
for all
t\in T_{\Delta}
.

Definition 1.8

(Hyperbolic metric space)
.

Let
X
be a geodesic metric space.
If there is
\delta\geq 0
such that every geodesic triangle in
X
is
\delta
-thin, we say that
X
is a
\delta
-hyperbolic metric space
.

Remark 1.9
.

It is well-known that
\delta
-hyperbolicity of a space
X
implies the following four point condition: for all
x,y,z,w\in X

\min\{\langle x,y\rangle_{w},\langle y,z\rangle_{w}\}\leq\langle x,z\rangle_{w}+2\delta.

Remark 1.10
.

It is well known that the real hyperbolic plane
\mathbb{R}\mathbf{H}^{2}
is
\delta
-hyperbolic with
\delta=\log(3)
.
Any
\operatorname{CAT}(-1)
space is therefore also
\log(3)
-hyperbolic.

The following lemma is essentially a restating of the thin triangles property in a form that is convenient for our later use.

Lemma 1.11
.

Let
(X,\dist)
be a
\delta
-hyperbolic metric space,
x,y,z\in X
.
Any geodesic
[y,z]
contains a point
t
with
\dist_{X}(t,x)\leq\langle y,z\rangle_{x}+\delta
.

Proof.

Let
\varphi\colon\Delta\to T_{\Delta}
be the canonical map from the triangle
\Delta
with vertices
x,y,z
to the associated tripod
T_{\Delta}
.
Call
\ast\in T_{\Delta}
the middle point of the tripod.
Take
s\in[x,y]
and
t\in[y,z]
to be the unique points of
[x,y]
and
[y,z]
in the preimage
\varphi^{-1}(\{\ast\})
respectively.
By definition,
\dist(x,s)=\langle y,z\rangle_{x}
and, since
\Delta
is
\delta
-thin,
\dist(s,t)\leq\delta
.
The lemma follows now by applying the triangle inequality.
∎

We will use the following statement about closest point projections.

Lemma 1.12
.

Let
(X,\dist)
be a
\delta
-hyperbolic metric space,
Y\subseteq X
a subspace,
\pi\colon X\to Y
a closest point projection.
For any
x\in X
and
y\in Y
we have

\langle x,y\rangle_{\pi(x)}\leq\delta

and

\dHaus_{X}([x,y],[x,\pi(x)]\cup[\pi(x),y])\leq 2\delta.

Proof.

By hyperbolicity of
X
there exist
a\in[x,\pi(x)]
and
b\in[\pi(x),y]
with
\dist(a,b)\leq\delta
and
\dist(a,\pi(x))=\dist(b,\pi(x))=\langle x,y\rangle_{\pi(x)}
.
By the fact that
\pi
is a closest point projection and
a
lies on a geodesic between
x
and
\pi(x)
, we have

\langle x,y\rangle_{\pi(x)}=\dist(b,\pi(x))=\dist(a,\pi(x))\leq\dist(a,b)\leq\delta.

By the thinness of triangles, we also have that
\dHaus_{X}([x,y],[x,a]\cup[b,y])\leq\delta
, from which it follows that
\dHaus_{X}([x,y],[x,\pi(x)]\cup[\pi(x),y])\leq 2\delta
.
∎

A hyperbolic metric space admits a natural bordification
\partial X
called the
Gromov boundary
.
Among proper spaces, this coincides with the
geodesic boundary
, defined as equivalence classes of geodesic rays considered up to bounded Hausdorff distance; see
[
12
, Chapter 11]
for details.
The Gromov product on a proper hyperbolic metric space extends naturally to its Gromov boundary.
Given a (bi-)infinite geodesic
\gamma\colon I\to X
in a proper hyperbolic space
X
, its
endpoints
are the points each of its subrays represents in
\partial X
.

A core feature of hyperbolic metric spaces is the stability of quasi-geodesics.
This is encapsulated in the following, commonly known as the Morse Lemma.

Lemma 1.13

(
[
12
, Theorem 11.72 and Lemma 11.105]
)
.

Let
(X,\dist)
be a proper
\delta
-hyperbolic metric space,
\lambda\geq 1
and
c\geq 0
.
There is
M=M(\lambda,c,\delta)\geq 0
such that for any
(\lambda,c)
-quasi-geodesic
\varphi\colon I\to X
, any geodesic
\gamma\colon J\to X
with the same endpoints as
\varphi
satisfies

\dHaus_{X}(\varphi(I),\gamma(J))\leq M.

Here, endpoints can be points in
X
or
\partial X

2.
Frucht’s theorem

In this section we will discuss Frucht’s theorem.
We will need a particular variant for countable groups which produces uncountably many regular graphs of arbitrary degree, and the main goal of this section is to prove this.
This can essentially be pieced together from proofs already present in the literature, but we include our own for the sake of self-containment.
We first introduce some terminology.

Definition 2.1

(Vertex-free and edge-free graphs)
.

A graph
\Gamma
is said to be
vertex-free
if the stabiliser of every vertex in
\Aut(\Gamma)
is trivial. Similarly,
\Gamma
is said to be
edge-free
if the setwise stabiliser of every pair

of adjacent vertices is trivial.

We will mostly be interested in vertex-free graphs, but it is possible for us to ensure our constructed graphs are also edge-free.
A vertex-free graph
\Gamma
is edge-free if and only if
\Aut(\Gamma)
acts
without edge inversions
.
That is, no isometry of
\Gamma
transposes the two endpoints of any edge.

Definition 2.2

(Labelled graphs)

Let

Given

of

A

Let

be a set. An

of

is a partial orientation of
\Gamma
together with an assignment of an element of

to every edge.
We call

A graph with an

.

We define the

, denoted
\Aut_{\ell}(\Gamma)
, as the subgroup of
\Aut(\Gamma)
which preserves the labelling of
\Gamma
.

Cayley graphs are the prototypical examples of labelled graphs.

Definition 2.3

(Cayley graph)
.

Let
G
be a group, and

a generating set.
Assume further that if

Remark 2.4
.

The group of labelled automorphisms of a Cayley graph is exactly the original group.
This is straightforward to see by the action of the group on its Cayley graph by (left) translation.

The following is the central construction of the section.
It associates to each 3-regular labelled graph another 3-regular graph, whose automorphism group is the same as the group of label-preserving automorphisms of the original graph.

3.
Boundary sets with trivial stabiliser

In this section, we construct a finite set in the visual boundary
\partial M
of certain non-positively curved manifolds
M
with trivial pointwise stabiliser in
\Isom(M)
.
These sets will determine the configuration of edge spaces in our graph of groups construction in Section
7
.
A general setting this construction makes sense in is the class of visibility manifolds.

Definition 3.1
.

A complete, simply connected and non-positively curved Riemannian manifold
M
of dimension at least two is called a
visibility manifold
if for every two distinct points
x,y\in\partial M
there exists a bi-infinite geodesic
\gamma
with
\gamma(\infty)=x
and
\gamma(-\infty)=y
.

Note that every simply connected symmetric space of non-compact type is a visibility manifold
[
13
]
.

Roughly speaking, the idea in constructing our set of boundary points is to pick the vertices of an ideal polytope that is large enough to not be contained in any proper geodesically complete and totally geodesic submanifold.
As such, there will be no non-trivial isometries stabilising its vertices.
For this purpose, let us introduce the following terminology.

Definition 3.2
.

Let
M
be a visibility manifold, and let
Z\subseteq\partial M
be a set of cardinality at least two. We define
hull
of
Z
, denoted
\hull(Z)
, to be the minimal geodesically complete and totally geodesic submanifold of
M
whose limit set contains
Z
.

Note that
\hull(Z)
does not generally coincide with the convex hull of
Z
, but is rather the minimal totally geodesic submanifold containing its convex hull.
We see that an isometry stabilising a set
\Omega
with the property that its hull spans the entire visibility manifold must be the identity.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
M
be a visibility manifold.
If
\Omega\subseteq\partial M
is a subset with
\hull(\Omega)=M
, then
\Omega
has trivial pointwise stabiliser in
\Isom(M)
.

Proof.

First we observe that the condition
\hull(\Omega)=M
implies that
\Omega
has at least three points, for otherwise
\hull{\Omega}
is a geodesic with endpoints the two points of
\Omega
.

Suppose
f
is an isometry of
M
whose induced map
\partial f
on
\partial M
fixes
\Omega
pointwise.
By well-known facts about isometries of visibility manifolds (see e.g.
[
3
]
),
\partial f
fixes 0, 1, 2, or infinitely many points of
\partial M
.
As
\partial f
fixes
\Omega
and
\left\lvert\Omega\right\rvert\geq 3
, it must be that
\partial f
fixes infinitely many points of
\partial M
.
Thus
f
is an elliptic isometry.

By
[
3
, Lemma 6.3]
, the fixed point set of
f
in
M
is a complete totally geodesic submanifold containing all geodesics between pairs of points in
\Omega
.
By the lemma hypothesis, this must be
M
. It follows that
f
is the identity map.
∎

There are finite subsets in the boundary of a visibility manifold satisfying the condition of the above lemma, whose cardinality depends only on the dimension of the space.

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
M
be a visibility manifold or

\Omega\subset\partial M

|\Omega|\leq\dim M+1
such that
\hull(\Omega)=M
.

Proof.

We proceed by induction on the dimension.
By definition, a visibility manifold has dimension at least
2
, so the base case is that
M=\mathbb{R}
.
In this case,
\partial M
only has two points, and the geodesic spanning both of them is the entire line.

Now let
n>1
, and suppose the statement holds for visibility manifolds of dimension at most
n-1
.
Let
N\subset M
be a geodesically complete and totally geodesic proper submanifold of maximal dimension.
Such a submanifold always exists since every bi-infinite geodesic is a complete totally geodesic submanifold.
By geodesic completeness we have
\dim N<\dim M
.

We claim that
N
is also a visibility manifold, so that the induction hypothesis applies.
Let
x,y\in\partial N
and choose
o\in N
and geodesic rays
\gamma_{x},\gamma_{y}
based at
o
with
\gamma_{x}(\infty)=x,\gamma_{y}(\infty)=y
.
Consider the sequence of geodesic segments
\gamma_{t}=[\gamma_{x}(t),\gamma_{y}(t)]
, and note that
\gamma_{t}\subset N
for every
t
as
N
is totally geodesic.
By completeness, the geodesic
\gamma
that
(\gamma_{t})_{t}
subconverges to is contained in
N
.
Necessarily, we have that
\gamma(\infty)=x
and
\gamma(-\infty)=y
.
It follows that
N
is a visibility manifold.

Now let
\Omega^{\prime}\subset\partial N
be a set of boundary points with
\hull(\Omega^{\prime})=N
with
\left\lvert\Omega^{\prime}\right\rvert\leq\dim N+1
.
Take any point
\omega\in\partial M-\partial N
, which exists since
\dim N<\dim M
, and set
\Omega=\Omega^{\prime}\cup\{\omega\}
.
It follows that
\left\lvert\Omega\right\rvert\leq\dim N+2\leq\dim M+1
.
By definition,
\hull(\Omega)
is a totally geodesic submanifold and contains
N\subseteq\hull(\Omega^{\prime})
.
Since
\omega\notin\partial N
and
\hull(\Omega)
is geodesically complete,
\dim\hull(\Omega)>\dim N
.
As
N
was a proper submanifold with these properties of maximal dimension, we must have that
\hull(\Omega)=M
.
∎

Proposition 3.5
.

Let
M
be a visibility manifold.
Then there exists a finite set
\Omega\subset\partial M
with
|\Omega|=n+1
that has trivial pointwise stabiliser in
\Isom(M)
.

Proof.

Lemmas
3.4
and
3.3
imply the existence of a set of boundary points
\Omega
with trivial pointwise stabiliser in
\Isom(M)
. If
\Omega
has cardinality less than
\dim M+1
, we may add rays in our favourite directions, which does not change the properties of
\Omega
.
∎

Remark 3.6
.

Given a particular visibility manifold, it may be possible to choose such a set
\Omega
with strictly less than
\dim M+1
elements.
This can be done by analysing its totally geodesic submanifolds in more detail.
However, for our purposes, this non-sharp statement suffices.

4.
Graphs of metric spaces

In this section, we will define our notion of a graph of metric spaces, which is an adaptation of a construction already appearing in Scott–Wall
[
32
, p. 155]
.

Let
\Gamma
be a graph. In this paper, every graph will be undirected and simplicial. That is,
\Gamma
will have no loops and no double-edges.
Denote by
V(\Gamma)
its vertex set, and
E(\Gamma)
its set of edges.
Given any graph
\Gamma
, we will always fix an orientation on its edge set, so that each edge
e\in E(\Gamma)
has a fixed initial vertex
\iota(e)
and terminal vertex
\tau(e)
.
As an abuse of notation, we will write
\bar{e}
to denote the edge
e\in E(\Gamma)
with reverse orientation, so that
\iota(\bar{e})=\tau(e)
and
\tau(\bar{e})=\iota(e)
.
For a vertex
v\in V(\Gamma)
we denote by
\In(v)
the set of edges incident to the vertex
v
, that is, either
\iota(e)=v
or
\tau(e)=v
.

Definition 4.1

(Graph of spaces)
.

A
graph of metric spaces

\mathbf{X}=(\Gamma,X_{-},Y_{-},\alpha_{-})
consists of the following data:

(1)

a connected, simplicial graph
\Gamma
;

(2)

for every
v\in V(\Gamma)
, a geodesic metric space
X_{v}
;

(3)

for every
e\in E(\Gamma)
, a length metric space
Y_{e}
;

(4)

for every
e\in E(\Gamma)
, a pair of isometric embeddings
\alpha_{e}\colon Y_{e}\to X_{\tau(e)}
and
\alpha_{\bar{e}}\colon Y_{e}\to X_{\iota(e)}
.

For brevity, we will often refer to a graph of metric spaces as simply a ‘graph of spaces’ throughout; the presence of additional metric structure is always implicit.
Following Scott–Wall
[
32
]
, we construct a realisation of a graph of metric spaces.

Definition 4.2

(Realisation of a graph of metric spaces)
.

Let
\mathbf{X}=(\Gamma,X_{-},Y_{-},\alpha_{-})
be a graph of metric spaces.
Let
A
denote the disjoint union

A=\bigsqcup_{v\in V(\Gamma)}X_{v}\sqcup\bigsqcup_{e\in E(\Gamma)}Y_{e}\times[0,1].

Let
\sim
be the equivalence relation on
A
generated by relations of the form

(x,0)\sim\alpha_{e}(x),\ (x,1)\sim\alpha_{\bar{e}}(x),

where
e\in E(\Gamma)
,
x\in Y_{e}
.
The
realisation
of
\mathbf{X}
is then the quotient space
\left\lvert\mathbf{X}\right\rvert=A/\sim
.

We will refer to the image of each
X_{v}
in
|\mathbf{X}|
as a
vertex space
, and images of the cylinders
Y_{e}\times[0,1]
as
edge cylinders
.
Edge cylinders will always be equipped with the
\ell_{2}
-metric corresponding to the product.
We will often write
Z_{e}=Y_{e}\times[0,1]
to denote an edge cylinder, and we call the subspace
Y_{e}\times(0,1)
the
interior of
Z_{e}
.
A subset of
\mathbf{X}
is called a
piece of
\mathbf{X}
if it is equal to either
X_{v}
for some
v\in V(\Gamma)
or
Z_{e}
for some
e\in E(\Gamma)
.

Note that, as
\Gamma
is simplicial, the projection map
A\to\left\lvert\mathbf{X}\right\rvert
described above restricts to a topological embedding on pieces of
\mathbf{X}
.
Hence we may view these as genuine subspaces of
\left\lvert\mathbf{X}\right\rvert
.

We now describe how to metrise the realisation of a graph of metric spaces.
This construction is roughly based on
[
8
, § I.7]
.

Definition 4.3

(String)
.

Let
\mathbf{X}=(\Gamma,X_{-},Y_{-},\alpha_{-})
be a graph of metric spaces, and let
x,y\in\left\lvert\mathbf{X}\right\rvert
.
A
string
in
\mathbf{X}
between
x
and
y
is a finite sequence
S=(x_{0},\ldots,x_{n})
,
x_{0}=x
,
x_{n}=y
, together with a choice of piece
P_{i}\subset|\mathbf{X}|
containing both
x_{i-1}
and
x_{i}
for each
i=1,\ldots,n
.
The
length
of
S
, denoted
\ell(S)
, is defined as

\ell(S)=\sum_{i=i}^{m}\dist_{P_{i}}(x_{i-1},x_{i}).

Definition 4.4

(Intrinsic metric)
.

Let
\mathbf{X}
be a graph of metric spaces.
We call the function
\dist_{\mathbf{X}}\colon\left\lvert\mathbf{X}\right\rvert\times\left\lvert\mathbf{X}\right\rvert\to\mathbb{R}
defined by

\dist_{\mathbf{X}}(x,y)=\inf\{\ell(S):\text{$S$ is a string with endpoints $x$ and $y$}\}.

the
intrinsic metric on
\left\lvert\mathbf{X}\right\rvert
.

Remark 4.5
.

Suppose that
\mathbf{X}=(\Gamma,X_{-},Y_{-},\alpha_{-})
is a graph of spaces and
\Gamma^{\prime}\subseteq\Gamma
is a subgraph.
Writing
\mathbf{X}^{\prime}=(\Gamma^{\prime},X_{-},Y_{-},\alpha_{-})
, there is a canonical map of realisations
\left\lvert\mathbf{X}^{\prime}\right\rvert\to\left\lvert\mathbf{X}\right\rvert
, which is a topological embedding.
Moreover, any string in
\mathbf{X}^{\prime}
maps to a string of the same length in
\mathbf{X}
under this embedding, so that
\dist_{\mathbf{X}}\leq\dist_{\mathbf{X}^{\prime}}
on the image of
\left\lvert\mathbf{X}^{\prime}\right\rvert
in
\left\lvert\mathbf{X}\right\rvert
.

The function
\dist_{\mathbf{X}}
defined above is easily seen to be a pseudo-metric, at least: lengths of strings are always non-negative and the triangle inequality follows by concatenating strings.
We will see shortly that it is in fact a metric.

Definition 4.6

(Reduced string)
.

Let
\mathbf{X}
be a graph of metric spaces.
A string
S
in
\mathbf{X}
is called
reduced
if the pieces of
S
alternate between vertex spaces and edge cylinders, and any consecutive triple of pieces are pairwise distinct.

Note that it is not necessary for consecutive terms in a reduced string to be distinct.
It is straightforward to see that the distance between two points can be realised as the infimum of lengths of reduced strings.

Lemma 4.7
.

Let
\mathbf{X}
be a graph of metric spaces.
For any
x,y\in\left\lvert\mathbf{X}\right\rvert
, we have

\dist_{\mathbf{X}}(x,y)=\inf\{\ell(S):S\textnormal{ is a {reduced} string between }x\textnormal{ and }y\}.

Proof.

For each string
S
, there is a reduced string
S^{\prime}
with the same endpoints, obtained by deleting entries of
S
and then, if needed, adding redundant entries.
Moreover, each edge space is isometrically embedded in its adjacent vertex spaces, so
\ell(S^{\prime})\leq\ell(S)
by applying the triangle inequality in each piece.
∎

On small scales, the intrinsic metric on the realisation of a graph of spaces resembles the metric on individual pieces.

Lemma 4.8
.

Let
\mathbf{X}
be a graph of metric spaces and let
P
be a piece of
\mathbf{X}
.
If
x,y\in P
are such that
\dist_{\mathbf{X}}(x,y)<1
, then
\dist_{\mathbf{X}}(x,y)=\dist_{P}(x,y)
.

Proof.

Let
\varepsilon>0
and let
S
be a reduced string from
x
to
y
with
\ell(S)<\dist_{\mathbf{X}}(x,y)+\varepsilon
.
Since
\dist_{\mathbf{X}}(x,y)<1
, we may assume without loss of generality that
\ell(S)<1
.
Recall that if
Z=Y\times[0,1]
is an edge cylinder,
\dist_{Z}((a,0),(b,1))\geq 1
for any
a,b\in Y
.
Hence the string
S
does not contain two points on opposite sides of an edge cylinder.
In particular, each of the points of
S
belong to a single vertex space
X
and its adjacent edge cylinders.

Since the pieces of
S
alternate between edge cylinders and vertex spaces, every other piece of
S
must be
X
.
As
S
is reduced and the first and last pieces of
S
are both
P
, this implies that
S
has exactly one piece.
This means that
S=(x,y)
, and so
\ell(S)=\dist_{P}(x,y)
.
Hence
\dist_{P}(x,y)<\dist_{\mathbf{X}}(x,y)=\epsilon
as required.
∎

Lemma 4.9
.

Let
\mathbf{X}
be a graph of metric spaces.
The intrinsic metric
\dist_{\mathbf{X}}
is a metric.

Proof.

To verify that
\dist_{\mathbf{X}}
is a metric, we need only check that it is positive definite.
Let
x
and
y
be points of
\left\lvert\mathbf{X}\right\rvert
and suppose that
\dist_{\mathbf{X}}(x,y)=0
.
By Lemma
4.7
,
\dist_{\mathbf{X}}(x,y)
is the infimum of lengths of reduced strings between
x
and
y
.

Suppose
x
is contained in the interior of an edge cylinder
Z\cong Y\times(0,1)
, so that
x=(a,r)
for some
a\in Y
and
r\in(0,1)
.
Let
\varepsilon=\max\{r,1-r\}
and let
S=(x_{0},\dots,x_{n})
be a reduced string between
x_{0}=x
and
x_{n}=y
with
\ell(S)<\varepsilon
.
By the choice of
\varepsilon
, it must be that
x_{i}\in Z
for all
i=1,\dots,n
.
Lemma
4.8
then implies
\dist_{Z}(x,y)=\dist_{\mathbf{X}}(x,y)=0
.
As
\dist_{Z}
is a metric, we must have
x=y
,
Similarly, if
y
is in the interior of an edge cylinder, we also have
x=y
.

Consider now the remaining case, that
x
and
y
are both contained in vertex spaces.
Now let
S=(x_{0},\dots,x_{n})
be a reduced string between
x_{0}=x
and
x_{n}=y
with
\ell(S)<1
.
It must be that
x
and
y
belong to some vertex space
X
, since the edge cylinders have width
1
.
Again Lemma
4.8
implies
\dist_{X}(x,y)=\dist_{\mathbf{X}}(x,y)=0
, so
x=y
as required.
∎

We describe a general construction that will be useful in describing paths in a graph of spaces.

Definition 4.10

(Path and string maps)
.

Let
\mathbf{X}=(\Gamma,X_{-},Y_{-}.\alpha_{-})
be a graph of spaces.
Denote by
\mathcal{S}_{\mathbf{X}}
the set of strings in
X
and
\mathcal{P}_{\mathbf{X}}
the set of continuous paths in
\left\lvert\mathbf{X}\right\rvert
whose image meets the interior of finitely many pieces of
\mathbf{X}
.
We define the map
\operatorname{Str}_{\mathbf{X}}\colon\mathcal{P}_{\mathbf{X}}\to\mathcal{S}_{\mathbf{X}}
as follows.
Given a continuous path
p\colon I\to\left\lvert\mathbf{X}\right\rvert
in
\mathcal{P}_{\mathbf{X}}
, where
I
is a closed interval, let
t_{0},\dots,t_{n}
be the partition of
I
that is minimal with respect to the property that
p|_{(t_{i-1},t_{i})}
is contained in the interior of exactly one piece
P_{i}
of
\mathbf{X}
for each
i=1,\dots,n
.
Then we define the string

\operatorname{Str}_{\mathbf{X}}(p)=(p(t_{0}),\dots,p(t_{n})).

with the given piece decomposition
P_{1},\dots,P_{n}
.

For each piece
P
of
\mathbf{X}
and each pair of points
x,y\in P
, fix a choice of geodesic
[x,y]
.
We define the map
\operatorname{Path}_{\mathbf{X}}\colon\mathcal{S_{\mathbf{X}}\to\mathcal{P}_{\mathbf{X}}}
as follows.
Suppose that
S=(x_{0},\dots,x_{n})
is a string in
\mathbf{X}
and, for each
i=1,\dots,n
, let
P_{i}
denote the choice of piece of
\mathbf{X}
containing
x_{i-1}
and
x_{i}
.
Then define
\operatorname{Path}_{\mathbf{X}}(S)\colon[0,\ell(S)]\to\left\lvert\mathbf{X}\right\rvert
as the path that is the concatenation of the
P_{i}
-geodesics
[x_{i-1},x_{i}]
over
i=1,\dots,n
.

Remark 4.11
.

If there is
\delta\geq 0
such that all vertex spaces of
\mathbf{X}
are
\delta
-hyperbolic, then any two geodesics in a piece with the same endpoints will be a Hausdorff distance of
\delta+1
from one another.
In such a situation,
\operatorname{Path}_{\mathbf{X}}(S)
is independent of the choice of geodesics made in the definition of
\operatorname{Path}_{\mathbf{X}}
given a string
S
, up to a Hausdorff distance of
\delta+1
.
Adding
\delta+1
to constants in the relevant statements, therefore, we may make any choice of geodesics that is convenient to us in each given situation.
We will use this fact without reference in the remainder of the paper.

Under some basic assumptions on the underlying graph and vertex and edge spaces, this intrinsic metric makes the realisation into a geodesic space in an effective manner.
We will invoke these assumptions fairly often in the remainder of the paper, so we collect them here.

Convention 4.12
.

Given a graph of spaces
\mathbf{X}=(\Gamma,X_{-},Y_{-},\alpha_{-})
, we will assume that the graph
\Gamma
is locally finite, and
X_{v}
and
Y_{e}
are complete and locally compact for each
v\in V(\Gamma)
and
e\in E(\Gamma)
.

Proposition 4.13
.

Let
\mathbf{X}=(\Gamma,X_{-},Y_{-},\alpha_{-})
be a graph of metric spaces.
Under Convention
4.12
,
\left\lvert\mathbf{X}\right\rvert
is a proper geodesic space.

Moreover, for any geodesic
p
in
\left\lvert\mathbf{X}\right\rvert
, there is a reduced string
S_{p}
whose points lie on
p
, and
p
is a concatenation of geodesics between the terms of
S_{p}
, each of which lie in the interior of a single piece of
\mathbf{X}
.

Proof.

Recall that the Hopf–Rinow theorem tells us that being complete and locally compact implies being proper and geodesic, among the class of length metric spaces
[
8
, Proposition I.3.7]
.
Hence we need only verify the former properties.
Let
x\in\left\lvert\mathbf{X}\right\rvert
be a point.
As
\Gamma
is locally finite,
B=B_{\mathbf{X}}(x;1)
intersects at most finitely many pieces of
\mathbf{X}
.
Thus the closure
\mkern 3.0mu\overline{\mkern-3.0muB\mkern-1.0mu}\mkern 1.0mu
is the union of finitely many closed and bounded subsets in pieces of
\mathbf{X}
.
Again by the Hopf–Rinow theorem each piece of
\mathbf{X}
is proper, so each of these finitely many subsets are compact.
Hence
\mkern 3.0mu\overline{\mkern-3.0muB\mkern-1.0mu}\mkern 1.0mu
is compact, and so
x
has a compact neighbourhood.

To see that
\left\lvert\mathbf{X}\right\rvert
is complete, let
(a_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a Cauchy sequence in
\left\lvert\mathbf{X}\right\rvert
.
For each
n\in\mathbb{N}
, let
P_{n}
be a piece of
\mathbf{X}
containing
a_{n}
.
There is
N\in\mathbb{N}
such that for
n,m\geq N
, we have
\dist_{\mathbf{X}}(a_{n},a_{m})<1
.
This implies that for any
n\geq N
, the terms
a_{n}
and
a_{N}
lie in adjacent pieces.
As
\Gamma
is locally finite, there are only finitely many pieces adjacent to
P_{N}
, so we may pass to a subsequence
(a_{n_{i}})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
whose terms eventually lie in the same piece
P
.
By Lemma
4.8
, this subsequence is also Cauchy in
P
and, hence converges in
P
, as
P
is complete.
It follows that
\left\lvert\mathbf{X}\right\rvert
is complete, since any Cauchy sequence with a convergent subsequence converges.

For the latter claim, suppose that
p\colon I\to\left\lvert\mathbf{X}\right\rvert
is a geodesic, where
I=[a,b]
is a closed interval.
Since
p
is continuous, its image
p(I)
is a compact subset of
\left\lvert\mathbf{X}\right\rvert
.
As
\left\lvert\mathbf{X}\right\rvert
is a complete metric space,
p(I)
is totally bounded.
In particular, there is a finite cover of
p(I)
by subsets whose diameter is at most
1/2
, say.
Now since
\Gamma
is locally finite, each of these subsets meets at most finitely many pieces of
\mathbf{X}
.
Therefore
p(I)
meets the interior of at most finitely many pieces of
\mathbf{X}
.
Thus
S_{p}=\operatorname{Str}_{\mathbf{X}}(p)
is defined, and we may take it to be reduced as
p
is geodesic and the edge spaces of
\mathbf{X}
are isometrically embedded in their adjacent vertex spaces.
Moreover, each subpath of
p
is also geodesic, so that its subpaths between the finitely many terms of
S
are geodesics lying in pieces of
\mathbf{X}
, as required.
∎

5.
Uniformly hyperbolic graphs of spaces

5.1.
Definition and basic properties

We are particularly interested in graphs of hyperbolic metric spaces that are assembled in a uniformly regular way. We will call these
uniformly hyperbolic
graphs of spaces.
In this section we will see that in such graphs of spaces, geodesics are well-behaved.
As a consequence, these spaces satisfy auspicious metric properties such as quasiconvexity of the vertex spaces.

Definition 5.1
.

(Uniformly hyperbolic graph of spaces)
Let
\mathbf{X}=(\Gamma,X_{-},Y_{-},\alpha_{-})
be a graph of metric spaces,
\delta\geq 0
, and
C\geq 0
.
We say that
\mathbf{X}
is
(\delta,C)
-uniformly hyperbolic
if:

(1)

X_{v}
is
\delta
-hyperbolic for every
v\in V(\Gamma)
;

(2)

for any edge
e\in E(\Gamma)
, there is
y_{e}\in Y_{e}
such that for any distinct edge
e^{\prime}\in E(\Gamma)
we have

(a)

\langle\alpha_{e}(Y_{e}),\alpha_{e^{\prime}}(Y_{e^{\prime}})\rangle_{\alpha_{e}(y_{e})}\leq C
if
\tau(e^{\prime})=\tau(e)
;

(b)

\langle\alpha_{\bar{e}}(Y_{e}),\alpha_{e^{\prime}}(Y_{e^{\prime}})\rangle_{\alpha_{\bar{e}}(y_{e})}\leq C
if
\tau(e^{\prime})=\iota(e)
.

Moreover, for any
e,e^{\prime}\in E(\Gamma)
with
\tau(e)=\tau(e^{\prime})
, we have
\alpha_{e}(y_{e})=\alpha_{e^{\prime}}(y_{e^{\prime}})
.

The last condition implies that for any given vertex space
X_{v}
there is a unique point
y_{v}=\alpha_{e}(y_{e})
, which does not depend on the choice of incident edge
e
, satisfying condition (2). We will call this point the
basepoint
of
X_{v}
.

The second condition should be seen as a sort of acylindricity condition. It essentially states that the images of distinct edge spaces diverge from one another uniformly quickly in the vertex spaces.

Remark 5.2
.

Let
\mathbf{X}=(\Gamma,X_{-},Y_{-},\alpha_{-})
be a
(\delta,C)
-uniformly hyperbolic graph of spaces.
Since each edge space isometrically embeds into its adjacent vertex spaces, each edge space is also
\delta
-hyperbolic, so long as it is a geodesic space.

Moreover, by hyperbolicity, any two geodesics joining the same two points of
X_{v}
are
\delta
-Hausdorff close in
X_{v}
.
Since each
\alpha_{e}
is an isometric embedding, this implies that
\alpha_{e}(Z)
is
\delta
-quasiconvex in
X_{\tau(e)}
for each connected component
Z\subseteq Y_{e}
.

The realisation of a uniformly hyperbolic graph of spaces contains a naturally embedded copy of its underlying graph
\Gamma
, as the union of subsets
\{y_{e}\}\times[0,1]
ranging over
e\in E(\Gamma)
, where the points
y_{e}
are given by uniform hyperbolicity.
It is immediate from Lemma
4.8
that this embedding is a local isometry.
We see that this copy of
\Gamma
is in fact globally isometrically embedded.

Lemma 5.3
.

Let
\mathbf{X}=(\Gamma,X_{-},Y_{-},\alpha_{-})
be a
(\delta,C)
-uniformly hyperbolic graph of spaces, and
consider the subspace

\left\lvert\mathbf{Y}\right\rvert=\underset{e\in E(\Gamma)}{\bigcup}\{y_{e}\}\times[0,1]\subset\left\lvert\mathbf{X}\right\rvert,

where
y_{e}
are the points given by uniform hyperbolicity.
Then
|\mathbf{Y}|
equipped with the subspace metric
\dist_{\mathbf{Y}}
induced from
|\mathbf{X}|
is a geodesic and convex subspace of
|\mathbf{X}|
and isometric to the geometric realisation
|\Gamma|
of
\Gamma
.

Proof.

Define a map

\displaystyle\Phi\colon V(\Gamma)

\displaystyle\to\{(y_{e},1):\,e\in E(\Gamma)\},

\displaystyle v

\displaystyle\mapsto(y_{e},1),\text{ where }e\text{ is such that }v=\tau(e).

Note that
\Phi(v)
exists for every vertex
v\in V(\Gamma)
because
\Gamma
is connected.
The definition of uniform hyperbolicity implies that
\Phi(v)
is well defined, for whenever we have two edges
e
and
e^{\prime}
with
\tau(e)=\tau(e^{\prime})
then
\alpha_{e}(y_{e})=\alpha_{e^{\prime}}(y_{e^{\prime}})
in
X_{\tau(e)}
.
In other words,
(y_{e},1)=(y_{e^{\prime}},1)
in
|\mathbf{X}|
.

This can be extended to a map
\Psi\colon|\Gamma|\to|\mathbf{Y}|
.
Let
e\in E(\Gamma)
be an edge with
\iota(e)=u
and
\tau(e)=v
.
We map a point
t\in[0,1]
in the interior of the realisation of edge
e
to
(y_{e},t)\in Z_{e}
.
The map
\Psi
is a continuous bijection.
We will now show that
\Psi
is an isometry.

Let
\varepsilon>0
, let
x,y\in|\mathbf{Y}|
and let
p\subset|\mathbf{X}|
be a path from
x
to
y
such that
\ell(p)\leq d_{\mathbf{X}}(x,y)+\varepsilon
.
As auxiliary terminology, we will say that
p

traverses an edge cylinder

Z_{e}
whenever
p\cap Z_{e}
is connected and
p\cap X_{i(e)}\neq\emptyset,p\cap X_{\tau(e)}\neq\emptyset
.
Of course,

\ell(p)\geq\#\{e\in E(\Gamma):p\text{ traverses }Z_{e}\},

so that when
x,y\in\Psi(V(\Gamma))
we can already conclude that

d_{\mathbf{Y}}(x,y)=d_{\mathbf{X}}(x,y)\geq d_{\Gamma}(\Psi^{-1}(x),\Psi^{-1}(y))-\varepsilon.

In the general case one needs to add the distance to the first and last vertex, which by construction of
\Psi
is at least as large as the corresponding distance in the graph. The same inequality of distances follows.
As
\varepsilon>0
was arbitrary and
\Psi
is a bijection, we have
\dist_{\mathbf{Y}}(\Psi(u),\Psi(v))\geq\dist_{\Gamma}(u,v)
for all
u,v\in|\Gamma|
.

On the other hand, for every pair of points
\Psi(u),\Psi(v)
in
|\mathbf{Y}|
, let
p
be the geodesic in
|\Gamma|
from
u
to
v
.
Then
\Psi(p)
is a path from
\Psi(u)
to
\Psi(v)
whose length is equal to
\ell_{\Gamma}(p)
, so that
d_{\mathbf{Y}}(\Psi(u),\Psi(v))\leq d_{\Gamma}(u,v)
.
Thus
\Psi
is an isometry between
\left\lvert\Gamma\right\rvert
and
\mathbf{Y}
.
As
\left\lvert\Gamma\right\rvert
is geodesic, so
|\mathbf{Y}|
is geodesic.

It remains to show that
|\mathbf{Y}|
is convex. We define a projection

\displaystyle\Pi\colon|\mathbf{X}|

\displaystyle\to|\mathbf{Y}|

\displaystyle x

\displaystyle\mapsto\begin{cases}\alpha_{e}(y_{e})&\text{ if }x\in X_{\tau(e)},\\
(y_{e},t)&\text{ if }x=(z,t)\in Z_{e}.\end{cases}

Now
\Pi
is continuous and length non-increasing on pieces, so that
\ell(p)\geq\ell(\Pi(p))
for any rectifiable path
p
in
\mathbf{X}
.
Further, whenever
p
intersects a piece
P
in a segment
q
such that
q\cap|\mathbf{Y}|\neq\emptyset
and
q\cap(|\mathbf{X}|-|\mathbf{Y}|)\neq\emptyset
, then
\ell_{P}(q)>\ell_{P}(\Pi(q))
, so the above length inequality is strict.
We already know that any two points
x,y\in|\mathbf{Y}|
can be joined by a geodesic contained entirely in
|\mathbf{Y}|
, so every other geodesic also has to be contained entirely in
|\mathbf{Y}|
.
∎

5.2.
Geodesics in uniformly hyperbolic graphs of spaces

This subsection is devoted to understanding geodesics in uniformly hyperbolic graphs of spaces.
We will show that geodesics in a uniformly hyperbolic graph of spaces trace out quasi-geodesics in the underlying graph.
We begin by analysing strings whose lengths are close to realising the distances between points.
It turns out that such strings cannot travel too deep into any vertex piece, besides the start and end terms.

Lemma 5.4
.

Let
\mathbf{X}=(\Gamma,X_{-},Y_{-},\alpha_{-})
be a
(\delta,C)
-uniformly hyperbolic graph of spaces, and let
x,y\in\left\lvert\mathbf{X}\right\rvert
.
There is
D=D(\delta,C)\geq 0
such that for any
\varepsilon>0
, the following holds.

Let
S=(x_{0},\dots,x_{m})
be a reduced string in
\mathbf{X}
with
\ell(S)\leq\dist_{\mathbf{X}}(x_{0},x_{m})+\varepsilon
.
For each
i=1,\dots,m
, write
P_{i}
for the piece of
\mathbf{X}
containing
x_{i-1}
and
x_{i}
and suppose that
P_{1}
and
P_{m}
are vertex spaces of
\mathbf{X}
.
If
P_{i}=X_{v}
is a vertex space then the following is true:

•

if
2\leq i\leq m-3
, then
x_{i}\in B_{X_{v}}(y_{v};D+\varepsilon)
; and

•

if
4\leq i\leq m-1
, then
x_{i-1}\in B_{X_{v}}(y_{v};D+\varepsilon)
.

where
y_{v}
is the basepoint of
X_{v}
given by the definition of uniform hyperbolicity.

Proof.

Define
D=4C+4\delta+1
.
We will show the first inclusion in the statement, for the latter will follow from an exactly symmetrical argument.

Let
2\leq i\leq m-3
and suppose
P_{i}
corresponds to a vertex space
X_{v}
as in the statement.
Since
S
is a reduced string,
P_{i-1}
,
P_{i}
, and
P_{i+1}
are distinct pieces of
\mathbf{X}
.
Thus,
P_{i-1}
and
P_{i+1}
correspond to distinct edge cylinders
Z_{e}
and
Z_{e^{\prime}}
incident on
X_{v}
.
As
i\leq m-3
and
S
is reduced,
P_{i+2}
is a vertex space
X_{v^{\prime}}
distinct from
X_{v}
.
We will first consider the former case.

Let
y_{v}\in X_{v}
be the basepoint of
X_{v}
given by uniform hyperbolicity.
Lemma
1.11
gives us a point
s
on the
X_{v}
-geodesic
[x_{i},x_{i+1}]
such that

(2)

\dist_{X}(s,y_{v})\leq C+\delta.

Moreover, observe that
i+2\leq m-1
, and so
P_{i+3}
must be an edge cylinder adjacent to
P_{i+2}
.
Applying the same argument as above to
P_{i+2}
, there is a point
t
on the
X_{v^{\prime}}
-geodesic
[x_{i+2},x_{i+3}]
such that

(3)

\dist_{X}(t,y_{v^{\prime}})\leq C+\delta,

similarly writing
y_{v^{\prime}}
for the basepoint of
X_{v}
.
Define the string

S^{\prime}=(x_{0},\dots x_{i},s,y_{v},y_{v^{\prime}},t,x_{i+3},\dots,x_{m}).

Now
S^{\prime}
by definition has length

(4)

\begin{split}\ell(S^{\prime})=\ell(S)&-\dist_{P_{i}}(s,x_{i+1})-\dist_{P_{i+1}}(x_{i+1},x_{i+2})-\dist_{P_{i+2}}(x_{i+2},t)\\
&+\dist_{P_{i}}(s,y_{v})+\dist_{P_{i+1}}(y_{v},y_{v^{\prime}})+\dist_{P_{i+2}}(y_{v^{\prime}},t).\end{split}

The triangle inequality combined with (
2
) implies that

\dist_{P_{i}}(s,y_{v})-\dist_{P_{i}}(s,x_{i+1})\leq 2C+2\delta-\dist_{P_{i}}(x_{i+1},y_{v})

and similarly (
3
) gives that

\dist_{P_{i+2}}(t,y_{v^{\prime}})-\dist_{P_{i+2}}(t,x_{i+2})\leq 2C+2\delta-\dist_{P_{i+2}}(x_{i+2},y_{v^{\prime}}).

As
\dist_{P_{i+1}}(y_{v},y_{v^{\prime}})=1
by definition, combining the above two equations with (
4
) and the lemma hypothesis yields

\ell(S^{\prime})\leq\dist_{\mathbf{X}}(x_{0},x_{m})-\dist_{P_{i}}(x_{i+1},y_{v})-\dist_{P_{i+2}}(x_{i+2},y_{v^{\prime}})+\varepsilon+4C+4\delta+1.

Finally, we have
\ell(S^{\prime})\geq\dist_{\mathbf{X}}(x_{0},x_{m})
by definition of the metric, so we obtain the inequality
\dist_{P_{i}}(x_{i+1},y_{v})\leq D+\varepsilon
.
∎

Reduced strings close to realising the distance between two points track a path through the underlying graph that is close to being a geodesic.

Proposition 5.5
.

Let
\mathbf{X}=(\Gamma,X_{-},Y_{-},\alpha_{-})
be a
(\delta,C)
-uniformly hyperbolic graph of spaces.
There is a constant
K=K(\delta,C)\geq 0
such that the following is true.

Let
u,v\in V(\Gamma)
and
x\in X_{u},y\in X_{v}
, and let
\varepsilon>0
.
Suppose that
S
is a reduced string between
x
and
y
with
\ell(S)\leq\dist_{\mathbf{X}}(x,y)+\varepsilon
.
Write
v_{1},\dots,v_{n}\in V(\Gamma)
for the sequence of vertices for which a term in
S
is contained in
X_{v_{i}}
, in the order they appear.
Then

n\leq\dist_{\Gamma}(u,v)+K+5\varepsilon.

In particular, the path in
\Gamma
with vertices
v_{1},\dots,v_{n}
is a
(1,K+5\varepsilon)
-quasi-geodesic between
u
and
v
in
\Gamma
.

Proof.

Let
S=(x_{0},\dots,x_{m})
be a reduced string between
x=x_{0}
and
y=x_{n}
as in the statement, with
\dist_{\mathbf{X}}(x,y)\leq\ell(S)+\varepsilon
.
For each
i=1,\dots,m
, denote by
P_{i}
the piece of
\mathbf{X}
containing
x_{i-1}
and
x_{i}
.
As
S
is reduced, the pieces
P_{i}
alternate between vertex spaces and edge cylinders.
Since
P_{1}
and
P_{m}
are both vertex spaces, this means that
m
must be odd.
We thus have that
m=2n-1
and
P_{2i-1}
is the vertex space
X_{v_{i}}
for each
i=1,\dots,n
.
Without loss of generality we may take
n\geq 3
, for otherwise setting
K=2
gives us the statement trivially.

Let
y_{v_{1}},\dots,y_{v_{n}}
be the basepoints of vertex spaces
X_{v_{1}},\dots,X_{v_{n}}
, and
D
the constant given by Lemma
5.4
applied to
S
.
We will use these points to obtain our estimate for
n
.
Take
s\in P_{3}
to be a point on a
P_{3}
-geodesic
[x_{2},x_{3}]
with

(5)

\dist_{P_{3}}(s,y_{v_{2}})\leq C+\delta,

which exists by Lemma
1.11
.
Likewise, there is a point
t\in P_{m-2}
on
P_{m-2}
-geodesic
[x_{m-3},x_{m-2}]
with

(6)

\dist_{P_{m-2}}(t,y_{v_{n-1}})\leq C+\delta.

By the definition of uniform hyperbolicity, we have
\dist_{P_{2k}}(y_{v_{k}},y_{v_{k+1}})=1
for each
k=1,\dots,n-1
.

Recall that every edge space of
\mathbf{X}
is isometrically embedded in its adjacent vertex spaces, so that

\dist_{P_{2k}}(a,b)=\dist_{P_{2k-1}}(a,b)=\dist_{P_{2k+1}}(a,b)\quad\textnormal{ for all }k=1,\dots,n-1

whenever
a,b\in P_{2k}=Y\times[0,1]
are elements with the same second component, where
Y
is the edge space corresponding to the cylinder
P_{2k}
.
We will use this fact freely below without further reference.

Observe that
\dist_{P_{1}}(x_{1},y_{v_{1}})\leq\dist_{P_{2}}(x_{1},x_{2})+\dist_{P_{2}}(x_{2},y_{v_{2}})+\dist_{P_{2}}(y_{v_{1}},y_{v_{2}})
by the triangle inequality.
Moreover,
\dist_{P_{2}}(x_{2},y_{v_{2}})\leq\dist_{P_{3}}(x_{2},s)+\dist_{P_{3}}(s,y_{v_{2}})
.
Similar estimates hold for
\dist_{P_{m}}(x_{m-1},y_{v_{n}})
and
\dist_{P_{m-2}}(x_{m-2},y_{v_{n-1}})
.
Combining these with (
5
) and (
6
), we obtain

(7)

\begin{split}\dist_{P_{1}}(x_{1},&y_{v_{1}})\leq\dist_{P_{2}}(x_{1},x_{2})+\dist_{P_{3}}(x_{2},s)+C+\delta+1\quad\textnormal{and}\\
\dist_{P_{m}}(x_{m-1},y_{v_{n}}&)\leq\dist_{P_{m-1}}(x_{m-1},x_{m-2})+\dist_{P_{m-2}}(x_{m-2},t)+C+\delta+1.\end{split}

By the definition of uniform hyperbolicity, we have that
\dist_{\mathbf{X}}(y_{v_{1}},y_{v_{n}})\leq\dist_{\Gamma}(u,v)
.
Now by the triangle inequality,

\begin{split}\dist_{\mathbf{X}}(x_{0},x_{m})\leq&\dist_{\mathbf{X}}(x_{0},x_{1})+\dist_{\mathbf{X}}(x_{0},y_{v_{1}})\\
&+\dist_{\mathbf{X}}(y_{v_{1}},y_{v_{n}})+\dist_{\mathbf{X}}(y_{v_{n}},x_{m-1})+\dist_{\mathbf{X}}(x_{m-1},x_{m}).\end{split}

Together with the pair of inequalities in (
7
), this gives us that

\begin{split}\dist_{\mathbf{X}}(x_{0},x_{m})\leq&\dist_{P_{1}}(x_{0},x_{1})+\dist_{P_{2}}(x_{1},x_{2})+\dist_{P_{3}}(x_{2},s)+\dist_{\Gamma}(u,v)\\
&+\dist_{P_{m-1}}(x_{m-1},x_{m-2})+\dist_{P_{m-2}}(x_{m-2},t)+2C+2\delta+1.\end{split}

By (
5
) and Lemma
5.4
, we can also see that
\dist_{P_{3}}(x_{2},s)\leq\dist_{P_{3}}(x_{2},x_{3})+C+\delta+D+\varepsilon
.
Again, a symmetrical estimate holds for
\dist_{P_{m-2}}(x_{m-2},t)
.
Therefore, we can deduce from the previous inequality that

(8)

\dist_{\mathbf{X}}(x_{0},x_{m})\leq\ell(S)-\sum_{i=4}^{m-3}\dist_{P_{i}}(x_{i-1},x_{i})+\dist_{\Gamma}(u,v)+4C+4\delta+2D+2\varepsilon+2.

On the other hand,
\ell(S)\leq\dist_{\mathbf{X}}(x_{0},x_{m})+\varepsilon
by assumption, so that (
8
) yields

(9)

\sum_{i=4}^{m-3}\dist_{P_{i}}(x_{i-1},x_{i})\leq\dist_{\Gamma}(u,v)+4C+4\delta+2D+3\varepsilon+2.

Repeated applications of the reverse triangle inequality to the left hand side give us a lower bound on the left hand side sum:

\sum_{i=2}^{n-2}\dist_{P_{2i}}({y_{v_{i}},y_{v_{i+1}}})-\dist_{P_{3}}(x_{3},y_{v_{2}})-\dist_{P_{m-2}}(x_{m-3},y_{v_{n-1}})\leq\sum_{i=4}^{m-3}\dist_{P_{i}}(x_{i-1},x_{i}).

Combined with (
9
) and Lemma
5.4
, we have

\sum_{i=2}^{n-2}\dist_{P_{2i}}(y_{v_{i}},y_{v_{i+1}})\leq\dist_{\Gamma}(u,v)+4C+4\delta+4D+5\varepsilon+2.

By definition, the sum on the left is simply equal to
n-3
.
Setting
K=4C+4\delta+4D+5
hence gives us the required inequality.
∎

5.3.
Local metric properties

We will now explore the consequences of the machinery developed above.
Firstly, we see that the vertex spaces in a uniformly hyperbolic graph of spaces are quasi-isometrically embedded.

Proposition 5.6
.

Let
\mathbf{X}=(\Gamma,X_{-},Y_{-},\alpha_{-})
be a
(\delta,C)
-uniformly hyperbolic graph of spaces satisfying Convention
4.12
.
There is a constant
k=k(\delta,C)\geq 0
such that the inclusion
X_{v}\hookrightarrow\left\lvert\mathbf{X}\right\rvert
is a
(1,k)
-quasi-isometric embedding for all
v\in V(\Gamma)
.

Proof.

Let
v\in V(\Gamma)
and
x,x^{\prime}\in X_{v}
.
By definition
\dist_{\mathbf{X}}(x,x^{\prime})\leq\dist_{X_{v}}(x,x^{\prime})
.
Suppose then that
\dist_{\mathbf{X}}(x,x^{\prime})<\dist_{X_{v}}(x,x^{\prime})
.
Since
\left\lvert\mathbf{X}\right\rvert
is a geodesic space by Proposition
4.13
, this means that there is a geodesic
p
in
\left\lvert\mathbf{X}\right\rvert
between
x
and
x^{\prime}
which is not contained in
X_{v}
.
It follows as in (
7
) from the proof of Proposition
5.5
that

\dist_{X_{v}}(x,y_{v})+\dist_{X_{v}}(x^{\prime},y_{v})\leq\ell(p)+2\delta+2C+2,

where
y_{v}
denotes the basepoint of
X_{v}
.
By the triangle inequality, this implies

\dist_{X_{v}}(x,x^{\prime})-2\delta-2C-2\leq\ell(p)=\dist_{\mathbf{X}}(x,x^{\prime})

so that setting
k=2\delta+2C+2
completes the proof.
∎

Another consequence is that the vertex spaces in a uniformly hyperbolic graph of spaces are uniformly quasiconvex.
This is, of course, an application of Proposition
5.5
to the case that the quasi-geodesic in the underlying graph is a loop.

Proposition 5.7
.

Let
\mathbf{X}=(\Gamma,X_{-},Y_{-},\alpha_{-})
be a
(\delta,C)
-uniformly hyperbolic graph of spaces satisfying Convention
4.12
.
Then there is a constant
\sigma=\sigma(\delta,C)\geq 0
such that each vertex space of
\mathbf{X}
is
\sigma
-quasiconvex in
\left\lvert\mathbf{X}\right\rvert
.

Proof.

Let
D=D(\delta,C)\geq 0
be the constant of Lemma
5.4
, and
K=K(\delta,C)\geq 0
the constant of Proposition
5.5
.
We will show that each vertex space of
\mathbf{X}
is
\sigma
-quasiconvex with
\sigma=K+D+1
.

By Proposition
4.13
,
\left\lvert\mathbf{X}\right\rvert
is a geodesic space.
Let
v\in V(\Gamma)
and let
p\colon I\to\left\lvert\mathbf{X}\right\rvert
be a geodesic with endpoints
x,y\in X_{v}
.
Proposition
4.13
tells us that these is a reduced string
S_{p}=(x_{0},\dots,x_{m})
whose terms lie on
p
, and
p
is a concatenation of geodesics
[x_{i-1},x_{i}]
in the interiors of pieces joining points of
S_{p}
.
Let
v_{1},\dots,v_{n}\in V(\Gamma)
be the sequence of vertices for which the points of
S_{p}
are contained in
X_{v_{i}}
.
As
S_{p}
is reduced, we have
m=2n-1
.
Of course,
v_{1}=v_{n}=v
, so by Proposition
5.5
, we have
n\leq K
.

Write
P_{1},\dots,P_{m}
for the pieces of
S_{p}
, so
X_{v_{i}}=P_{2i-1}
for
i=1,\dots,n
.
Let
y_{3},\dots,y_{m-2}
be the points obtained from Lemma
5.4
.
Write
Z_{e_{i}}=Y_{e_{i}}\times[0,1]
for the edge cylinder such that
\iota(e_{i})=v_{i}
and
\tau(e_{i})=v_{i+1}
for
i=1,\dots,n-1
.
We write
y_{1}=\alpha_{\bar{e_{1}}}(y_{e_{1}})
, where
y_{e_{1}}\in Y_{e_{1}}
is provided by uniform hyperbolicity.

Now let
i=1,\dots,m
and let
z
be a point on the geodesic subsegment
[x_{i-1},x_{i}]
of
p
.
If
i=1
or
i=m
then
z\in X_{v}
trivially, so suppose otherwise.
If
i
is odd, then
z
is contained in a
\delta
-neighbourhood of
[x_{i-1},y_{i}]\cup[x_{i},y_{i}]
by uniform hyperbolicity of
\mathbf{X}
.
Moreover, by Lemma
5.4
,
x_{i}
is at distance of at most
D
from
y_{i}
if
i\leq m-3
, and similarly
x_{i-1}
from
y_{i}
if
i\geq 4
.
Hence, in these cases,
z
is contained in a
\sigma
-neighbourhood of
X_{v}
.
As the edge cylinders are equipped with the
\ell^{2}
-metric and have width
1
, this inclusion also holds when
i
is even and in the same range.
Finally, the cases that
i=3
and
i=m-2
are dealt with by noting that
[x_{2},y_{3}]
and
[x_{m-2},y_{m-2}]
are contained in a
1
-neighbourhood of
X_{v}
, since they are contained in edge pieces
P_{2}
and
P_{m-1}
which are adjacent to
P_{1}=P_{m}=X_{v}
.
This proves that
X_{v}
is
\sigma
-quasiconvex, as required.
∎

Remark 5.8
.

With only superficial modifications to the proof, the above also yields that the subspace of
\left\lvert\mathbf{X}\right\rvert
consisting of a vertex space
X_{v}
and its adjacent edge cylinder – that is,
B_{\mathbf{X}}(X_{v};1)
– is also
\sigma
-quasiconvex.
Increasing the value of
\sigma
by 1, the same proof shows that
X_{u}\cup Z_{e}\cup X_{v}
is
\sigma
-quasiconvex when
X_{u}
and
X_{v}
are adjacent vertex spaces joined by edge cylinder
Z_{e}
.

We are also able to analyse the intersections of neighbourhoods of pairs of pieces.
We consider the cases that both the pieces in question are components of an edge space, or are both vertex spaces.

Lemma 5.9
.

Let
\mathbf{X}
be a
(\delta,C)
-uniformly hyperbolic graph of spaces.
For any
r\geq 0
, there is
R=R(r,\delta,C)\geq 0
such that for any distinct edge pieces
Z_{e}
and
Z_{e^{\prime}}
of
\mathbf{X}
, the set

B_{\mathbf{X}}(Z_{e};r)\cap B_{\mathbf{X}}(Z_{e^{\prime}};r)

has diameter at most
R
.

Proof.

Let us first observe that the lemma reduces to the case that
Z
and
Z^{\prime}
are both adjacent to the same vertex space
X
.
Indeed, any path joining a point in
\left\lvert X\right\rvert
to a point in an edge cylinder must pass through a vertex space adjacent to this edge cylinder.
It follows that
B_{\mathbf{X}}(Z;r)\cap B_{\mathbf{X}}(Z^{\prime};r)\subseteq B_{\mathbf{X}}(Z;r)\cap B_{\mathbf{X}}(Z^{\prime\prime};r)
, where
Z^{\prime\prime}
shares an adjacent vertex space with
Z
, since this observation applies to a path of length
r
joining a point in the former intersection to
Z
.
Hence without loss of generality suppose that
Z
and
Z^{\prime}
are such.

Let
x
and
y
be points in the given intersection.
Then there are points
x_{Z},y_{Z}\in Z\cap X
and
x_{Z^{\prime}},y_{Z^{\prime}}\in Z^{\prime}\cap X
at most
r
from
x
and
y
respectively.
We can deduce

(10)

\dist_{\mathbf{X}}(x,y)\leq\dist_{\mathbf{X}}(x_{Z},y_{Z})+2r

by the triangle inequality.

Now uniform hyperbolicity gives us that there is
z\in Z\cap Z^{\prime}
with
\langle y_{Z},y_{Z^{\prime}}\rangle_{z}\leq C
.
By assumption, we have
\dist_{\mathbf{X}}(x_{Z},x_{Z^{\prime}})\leq 2r
and
\dist_{\mathbf{X}}(y_{Z},y_{Z^{\prime}})\leq 2r
.
Applying Proposition
5.6
, there is
k=k(\delta,C)\geq 0
such that the above implies
\dist_{X}(x_{Z},x_{Z^{\prime}})\leq 2r+k
and
\dist_{X}(y_{Z},y_{Z^{\prime}})\leq 2\lambda r
.
Combining these two inequalities implies

\dist_{X}(x_{Z},z)+\dist_{X}(x_{Z^{\prime}},z)\leq 2C+2r+k.

An identical equation holds for
y_{Z}
and
y_{Z^{\prime}}
in place of
x_{Z}
and
x_{Z^{\prime}}
respectively.
Hence, all four points
x_{Z},x_{Z^{\prime}},y_{Z},
and
y_{Z^{\prime}}
lie in
B_{X}(z;2C+2r+k)
.
In particular,

(11)

\dist_{X}(x_{Z},y_{Z})\leq 4C+4\lambda r.

Observing that
\dist_{\mathbf{X}}(x_{Z},y_{Z})\leq\dist_{X}(x_{Z},y_{Z})
, equations (
10
) and (
11
) give us

\dist_{\mathbf{X}}(x,y)\leq 4C+4\lambda r+2r,

so that setting
R=4C+4\lambda r+2r
completes the lemma.
∎

Lemma 5.10
.

Let
\mathbf{X}=(\Gamma,X_{-},Y_{-},\alpha_{-})
be a
(\delta,C)
-uniformly hyperbolic graph of spaces.
For any
r\geq 0
, there is
R=R(r,\delta,C)\geq 0
such that for any distinct vertex pieces
X_{u}
and
X_{v}
of
\mathbf{X}
, then either

(1)

\dist_{\Gamma}(u,v)=1
and any connected component of
B_{\mathbf{X}}(X_{u};r)\cap B_{\mathbf{X}}(X_{v};r)
has diameter at most
R
or is contained in
B_{\mathbf{X}}(Z_{e};R)
, where
e\in E(\Gamma)
has endpoints
u
and
v
and
Z_{e}\subseteq Y_{e}
is an edge cylinder; or

(2)

\dist_{\Gamma}(u,v)>1
and
B_{\mathbf{X}}(X_{u};r)\cap B_{\mathbf{X}}(X_{v};r)
has diameter at most
R
.

Proof.

Let
z\in B_{\mathbf{X}}(X_{u};r)\cap B_{\mathbf{X}}(X_{v};r)
.
Observe that there are paths
p_{u}
and
p_{v}
of length at most
r
joining
z
to a point of
X_{u}
and a point of
X_{v}
respectively.
For the first statement, if either
p_{u}
or
p_{v}
intersect
Z_{e}
, then
z\in B_{\mathbf{X}}(Z_{e};r)
and we are done.
Suppose otherwise, that then either
p_{u}
or
p_{v}
(or both) must pass through two distinct edge cylinders
Z
and
Z^{\prime}
.
But then
z
belongs to a path component of
B_{\mathbf{X}}(Z;r)\cap B_{\mathbf{X}}(Z^{\prime};r)
, which has diameter at most
R=R(r,\delta,C)
by Lemma
5.9
.

For the latter case, as
\dist_{\Gamma}(u,v)>1
, the paths
p_{u}
and
p_{v}
must pass through at least two distinct edge cylinders
Z
and
Z^{\prime}
.
It follows that
z\in B_{\mathbf{X}}(Z;r)\cap B_{\mathbf{X}}(Z^{\prime};r)
, whence again applying Lemma
5.9
bounds the diameter by
R
.
∎

6.
Strong QI-rigidity for graph of spaces

In this section we describe a series of additional technical properties pertaining to graphs of spaces, which will provide strong control over their quasi-isometry groups.
The purpose of these properties is to guarantee strong QI-rigidity for the realisation of a graph of spaces; the results are summarised in the statement of Theorem
6.17
.

6.1.
Link bottlenecked pairs

The first condition we describe allows us to control what happens to vertex spaces under a quasi-isometry of graphs of spaces
\mathbf{X}
and
\mathbf{Y}
.
Roughly speaking, the condition states that the edge spaces of
\mathbf{Y}
do not coarsely separate vertex spaces of
\mathbf{X}
into arbitrarily deep components.
Before we define it formally, it will be helpful introduce the following notation.

Definition 6.1

(Link of a vertex space)
.

Let
\mathbf{X}=(\Gamma,X_{-},Y_{-},\alpha_{-})
be a graph of spaces. Let
v\in V(\Gamma)
. Then the
\mathbf{X}
-link
of
v
, denoted
\mathrm{lk}_{\mathbf{X}}(v)
, is defined as the space

\mathrm{lk}_{\mathbf{X}}(v):=\bigcup_{e\in\mathrm{In(v)}}\alpha_{e}(Y_{e}),

equipped with the subspace metric induced by the metric
\dist_{X_{v}}
on
X_{v}
.

Definition 6.2

(Link bottlenecked pairs)
.

Let

\mathbf{X}=(\Gamma,X_{-},Y_{-},\alpha_{-}),\ \ \mathbf{Y}=(\Delta,X^{\prime}_{-},Y^{\prime}_{-},\beta_{-})

be graphs of spaces. We say that the ordered pair
(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Y})
is
link bottlenecked
if:

(LB)

for all
\lambda\geq 1
and
c\geq 0
, there are
K\geq 0
and
\rho\geq 0

such that for all
v\in V(\Gamma)
and all subsets
Z\subset X_{v}
, if there exists
u\in V(\Delta)
such that
Z
admits a
(\lambda,c)
-quasi-isometric embedding into
\lk_{\mathbf{Y}}(u)
, then there exists a unique
\rho
-coarse component
U\subset X_{v}-Z
with
U\not\subset B_{X_{v}}(Z;K)
.

If the pair
(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{X})
is link bottlenecked, then we simply that say
\mathbf{X}
is link bottlenecked
.
If both
\mathbf{X}
and
\mathbf{Y}
are link bottlenecked, and both pairs
(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Y})
and
(\mathbf{Y},\mathbf{X})
are link bottlenecked, we call
(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Y})
a
totally link bottlenecked pair
.

Example 6.3
.

We describe some basic examples.

(1)

Consider a graph of spaces
\mathbf{X}
where every vertex space is isometric to the Euclidean plane
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, and every
\mathbf{X}
-link is uniformly bounded in diameter. It is easy to verify that
\mathbf{X}
satisfies (
LB
).

(2)

Given any graph
\Gamma
, one can form a trivial graph of spaces
\mathbf{X}
where each vertex and edge space is just a single point, and so trivially
\left\lvert\mathbf{X}\right\rvert
is isometric to
\Gamma
. This is generally
not
link bottlenecked.

Remark 6.4
.

The condition (
LB
) depends only on the geometry of the vertex spaces and their links.
Thus, if
\mathbf{X}
,
\mathbf{Y}
are graphs of spaces such that all vertex spaces of
\mathbf{X}
and
\mathbf{Y}
are pairwise isometric, and all links across both
\mathbf{X}
and
\mathbf{Y}
are pairwise isometric, then the pair
(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Y})
is totally link bottlenecked if and only if
\mathbf{X}
and
\mathbf{Y}
are link bottlenecked.

We record some basic and useful consequences of being link bottlenecked.

Lemma 6.5
.

Let
\mathbf{X}=(\Gamma,X_{-},Y_{-},\alpha_{-})
be a link bottlenecked graph of spaces, where
\Gamma
is not a single point. Then each vertex space
X_{v}
is one-ended.

Proof.

If
X_{v}\subset\left\lvert\mathbf{X}\right\rvert
were bounded then it is trivially quasi-isometric to a subset of some link, which contradicts (
LB
).
Thus, no vertex space is bounded.
Let
\rho
be the constant given by the definition (
LB
) for the coarseness of components.
Suppose then that
X_{v}
is unbounded and has more than one end.
Then there exists a bounded subset
Z\subset X_{v}
such that
X_{v}-Z
contains two
\rho
-coarse
components containing points arbitrarily far from
Z
.
This also contradicts (
LB
).
∎

Lemma 6.6
.

Let
\mathbf{X}=(\Gamma,X_{-},Y_{-},\alpha_{-})
be a link bottlenecked graph of spaces. Let
v\in V(\Gamma)
. Then

\dHaus_{\mathbf{X}}(X_{v},\lk_{\mathbf{X}}(v))=\infty
.

Moreover, if
u,v\in V(\Gamma)
are distinct vertices then

\dHaus_{\mathbf{X}}(X_{v},X_{u})=\infty
.

Proof.

If these were not the case then we would immediately contradict (
LB
).
∎

We now give a technical, more quantitative version of Lemma
6.6
, which states that (
LB
) implies that given any point in a vertex space, there is always a uniformly nearby point which is far away from the link.

Lemma 6.7
.

Let
\mathbf{X}=(\Gamma,X_{-},Y_{-},\alpha_{-})
be a link bottlenecked graph of spaces.
Then for all
D>0
, there exists
r=r(D,\mathbf{X})>0
such that for all
v\in V(\Gamma)
and all
x\in X_{v}
there exists
y\in X_{v}
such that

(1)

\dist_{X_{v}}(x,y)\leq r
,

(2)

\dist_{X_{v}}(y,\lk_{\mathbf{X}}(v))>D
.

Proof.

Suppose this were not the case. Then this means that there exists
D>0
such that for all
r>0
, there exists
v\in V(\Gamma)
and
x\in X_{v}
such that for all
y\in B_{X_{v}}(x;r)
, we have that
\dist_{X_{v}}(y,\lk_{\mathbf{X}}(v))\leq D
.
Let
\rho=\rho(1,2D)
be the constant given by the definition (
LB
).
For
r>\rho
, consider the set

S_{r}=\{z\in X_{v}:\dist_{X_{v}}(x,z)\in[r-\rho,r]\}.

By our assumption, any closest point projection of
S_{r}
to
\lk_{\mathbf{X}}(v)
is a
(1,2D)
-quasi-isometric embedding into
\lk_{\mathbf{X}}(v)
.
However,
x
lies in a bounded
\rho
-coarse component of
X_{v}-S_{r}
.
Since
X_{v}
is unbounded by Lemma
6.5
, this contradicts (
LB
) if
r
is chosen to be sufficiently large with respect to
K(1,2D)
.
∎

The key consequence of property (
LB
) is that the images of vertex spaces under quasi-isometries of link bottlenecked graphs of spaces are uniformly close to vertex spaces in the codomain.

Lemma 6.8
.

Let
\mathbf{X}=(\Gamma,X_{-},Y_{-},\alpha_{-})
,
\mathbf{Y}=(\Delta,X^{\prime}_{-},Y^{\prime}_{-},\beta_{-})
be uniformly hyperbolic graphs of spaces.
Suppose further that the pair
(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Y})
is totally link bottlenecked. Then for all
\lambda\geq 1
,
c\geq 0
, there exists
C=C(\lambda,c,\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Y})\geq 0
such that for all
(\lambda,c)
-quasi-isometries
\varphi\colon\left\lvert\mathbf{X}\right\rvert\to\left\lvert\mathbf{Y}\right\rvert
and all
v\in V(\Gamma)
, there exists a unique
u\in V(\Delta)
such that

\dHaus_{\mathbf{Y}}(X^{\prime}_{u},\varphi(X_{v}))\leq C.

In particular, every quasi-isometry
\varphi\colon\left\lvert\mathbf{X}\right\rvert\to\left\lvert\mathbf{Y}\right\rvert
induces a map
\Phi(\varphi)\colon V(\Gamma)\to V(\Delta)
on the vertices of the underlying graphs of
\mathbf{X}
and
\mathbf{Y}
.

Proof.

Let
\psi\colon\mathbf{Y}\to\mathbf{X}
be a quasi-inverse to
\varphi
, so
\dist_{\infty}(\id_{\mathbf{X}},\psi\varphi),\dist_{\infty}(\id_{\mathbf{Y}},\varphi\psi)\leq Q
where
Q
depends only on
\lambda
and
c
.
After possibly increasing
c
, we may assume
\psi
is also a
(\lambda,c)
-quasi-isometry.
Since
\mathbf{X}
and
\mathbf{Y}
are uniformly hyperbolic, Proposition
5.7
gives us a constant
k\geq 0
such that for all
v\in V(\Gamma)
,
v^{\prime}\in V(\Delta)
, the inclusions
X_{v}\hookrightarrow\left\lvert\mathbf{X}\right\rvert
and
X^{\prime}_{v^{\prime}}\hookrightarrow\left\lvert\mathbf{Y}\right\rvert
are
(1,k)
-quasi-isometric embeddings.

We first show that
\varphi(X_{v})
lands arbitrarily deep inside vertex spaces of
\mathbf{Y}
.

Claim
.

For each
a>0
, there is
u\in V(\Delta)
such that

\varphi(X_{v})\cap\left(X_{u}^{\prime}-B_{X_{u}^{\prime}}(\lk_{\mathbf{Y}}(u);a)\right)\neq\emptyset.

Proof of claim.

For any
x\in X_{v}
, there is
x^{\prime}\in\left\lvert\mathbf{Y}\right\rvert
in a vertex space, say
x^{\prime}\in X^{\prime}_{u}
, such that
\dist_{\mathbf{Y}}(x^{\prime},\varphi(x))\leq 1
(projecting to one side of an edge cylinder if necessary).
Let
r=r(a+c+k,\mathbf{Y})
be as in Lemma
6.7
so that, given
x^{\prime}\in X^{\prime}_{u}
, there is
y^{\prime}\in X_{u}^{\prime}
with

\dist_{X_{u}^{\prime}}(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\leq r\quad\textnormal{ and }\quad\dist_{X_{u}^{\prime}}(y^{\prime},\lk_{\mathbf{Y}}(u))>a+c+k.

Moreover, any
y^{\prime}\in\left\lvert\mathbf{Y}\right\rvert
is a distance at most
c
from the image of
\varphi
, as
\varphi
is
c
-coarsely surjective.
Thus by the triangle inequality and the fact that the inclusion
X^{\prime}_{u}\hookrightarrow\left\lvert\mathbf{Y}\right\rvert
is a
(1,k)
-quasi-isometric embedding, for any
x\in X_{v}
there is
y\in\left\lvert\mathbf{Y}\right\rvert
with

(12)

\dist_{X_{u}^{\prime}}(y,\lk_{\mathbf{Y}}(u))>a\quad\textnormal{ and }\dist_{\mathbf{Y}}(y,\varphi(x))\leq 1+r+c.

We show that for
x\in X_{v}
far enough from
\lk_{\mathbf{X}}(v)
, the
y
obtained as above lies in
\varphi(X_{v})
.
To this end, take
R=\lambda(1+r+2c)+k
.
By Lemma
6.6
, there is
x\in X_{v}
such that

\dist_{X_{v}}(x,\lk_{\mathbf{X}}(v))>R.

Let
z\in\varphi^{-1}(y)
.
We verify that
\dist_{\mathbf{X}}(x,z)<\dist_{\mathbf{X}}(x,\lk_{\mathbf{X}}(v))
, which implies that
z\in X_{v}
.
Using that
\varphi
is a quasi-isometry and (
12
),

\dist_{\mathbf{X}}(x,z)\leq\lambda\left(\dist_{\mathbf{Y}}(\varphi(x),y)+c\right)\leq\lambda(1+r+2c)=R-k.

To conclude, again note that since
X_{v}\hookrightarrow\left\lvert\mathbf{X}\right\rvert
is a
(1,k)
-quasi-isometric embedding, the above equation implies

\dist_{\mathbf{X}}(x,z)\leq R-k<\dist_{X_{v}}(x,\lk_{\mathbf{X}}(v))-k\leq\dist_{\mathbf{X}}(x,\lk_{\mathbf{X}}(v))

as required.
∎

Next, we use (
LB
) to show that
\varphi(X_{v})
cannot contain points too far away from
X_{u}^{\prime}
.
This is one of the two needed inclusions to prove the lemma.

Claim
.

There exists
A=A(\lambda,c,\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Y})\geq 0
and
u\in V(\Delta)
such that

\varphi(X_{v})\subset B_{\mathbf{Y}}(X^{\prime}_{u};A).

Moreover, the vertex
u
is unique.

Proof of claim.

Suppose otherwise, so that for any
A\geq 0
and
u\in V(\Delta)
, there is
y\in X_{v}
with
\dist_{\mathbf{Y}}(\varphi(y),X^{\prime}_{u})>A
.
By the first claim, there is
u\in V(\Delta)
such that
\varphi(X_{v})\cap\left(X_{u}^{\prime}-B_{X_{u}^{\prime}}(\lk_{\mathbf{Y}}(u);A)\right)\neq\emptyset
.
That is, there is
x\in X_{v}
such that
\varphi(x)\in X_{u}^{\prime}
and
\dist_{\mathbf{Y}}(\varphi(x),\lk_{\mathbf{Y}}(u))>A
.
By definition, we have that
\lk_{\mathbf{Y}}(u)
separates
\varphi(x)
from
\varphi(y)
.
In particular, for any
\kappa\geq 0
we know that
\varphi(x),\varphi(y)
are in different
\kappa
-coarse components of
\left\lvert\mathbf{Y}\right\rvert-B_{\mathbf{X}}(\lk_{\mathbf{Y}}(u),\kappa)
.
Now applying Lemma
1.5
,
we obtain constants
\rho=\rho(\lambda,c,\kappa)

and
L>0
, depending only on
\kappa,\lambda
and
c
, such that the set

B=B_{\mathbf{X}}(\psi(\lk_{\mathbf{Y}}(u));L)

separates
\psi\varphi(x)
from
\psi\varphi(y)
in
\left\lvert\mathbf{X}\right\rvert
, so long as
A\geq\lambda L+\lambda c
.
Setting
Z=B\cap X_{v}
, then, we have that
x
and
y
lie in distinct
(\rho+2Q)
-coarse components of
X_{v}-Z
, when equipped with the subspace metric induced from
\left\lvert\mathbf{X}\right\rvert
, as
\dist_{\infty}(\psi\varphi,\id_{\mathbf{X}})\leq Q
.

Recall that all vertex spaces in
\mathbf{X}
and
\mathbf{Y}
are
(1,k)
-quasi-isometrically embedded in
\left\lvert\mathbf{X}\right\rvert
and
\left\lvert\mathbf{Y}\right\rvert
.
By Lemma
1.5
, we know that there exists
\rho^{\prime}=\rho^{\prime}(1,\rho+2Q+k)
such that
x,y
lie in different
\rho^{\prime}
-coarse components of
X_{v}-Z
, when equipped with the metric of
X_{v}
. Note that
\rho^{\prime}
diverges as
\kappa
tends to
\infty
.

Moreover,
Z
as a subspace of
(X_{v},\dist_{X_{v}})
, admits a

(\lambda,c+\lambda k)
-quasi-isometric embedding into
\lk_{\mathbf{Y}}(u)
.
Let
K=K(\lambda,c+\lambda k)
be as in the definition of property (
LB
).
Now if
A\geq\lambda(K+L)+c
, we have

\displaystyle\dist_{X_{v}}(x,Z)

\displaystyle\geq\dist_{\mathbf{X}}(x,B)

\displaystyle\geq\dist_{\mathbf{X}}(x,\psi(\lk_{\mathbf{Y}}(u)))-L

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{\lambda}(\dist_{\mathbf{Y}}(\varphi(x),\lk_{\mathbf{Y}}(u))-c)-L

\displaystyle>\frac{1}{\lambda}(A-c)-L\geq K

Identical reasoning also shows that
\dist_{X_{v}}(y,Z)>K
.

Observe that we can do this for any
\kappa\geq 0
, so that there is no
\rho^{\prime}
such that at most one of the
\rho^{\prime}
-coarse components of
X_{v}-Z
is at bounded distance from
Z
.
This contradicts property (
LB
), and thus the first statement in the claim is shown.
That the vertex is unique follows immediately from Lemma
6.6
.
∎

Finally, we conclude the proof of the lemma by symmetry.
Let
A^{\prime}=A(\lambda,c,\mathbf{Y},\mathbf{X})
and
v^{\prime}\in V(\Gamma)

be as in second claim applied to
\psi\colon\left\lvert\mathbf{Y}\right\rvert\to\left\lvert\mathbf{X}\right\rvert
, so that

\psi(X_{u}^{\prime})\subset B_{\mathbf{X}}(X_{v^{\prime}};A^{\prime}).

We first argue that
v^{\prime}=v
.
Indeed, it follows that
\psi\varphi(X_{v})
is contained in a bounded neighbourhood of
X_{v^{\prime}}
.
Since
\psi
is quasi-inverse to
\varphi
, this implies
X_{v}
is contained in a bounded neighbourhood of
X_{v^{\prime}}
.
Hence
v=v^{\prime}
by Lemma
6.6
.

Now, as
\varphi
is a
(\lambda,c)
-quasi-isometry,
\varphi\psi(X^{\prime}_{u})\subseteq B_{\mathbf{Y}}(\varphi(X_{v});\lambda A^{\prime}+c)
.
Since
\dist\varphi\psi,\id_{\mathbf{Y}})\leq Q
, we have
\dHaus_{\mathbf{Y}}(\varphi\psi(X^{\prime}_{u}),X^{\prime}_{u})\leq Q
.
Combining these shows that
\varphi|_{X_{v}}
is
A^{\prime\prime}
-coarsely surjective onto
X^{\prime}_{u}
, where
A^{\prime\prime}=\lambda A^{\prime}+c+Q
.
Setting
C=\max\{A,A^{\prime},A^{\prime\prime}\}
concludes the proof.
∎

Remark 6.9
.

Uniform hyperbolicity of
\mathbf{X}
is essential in the above, as it provides a bound on the distortion of the vertex spaces in
\left\lvert\mathbf{X}\right\rvert
.
In principle, one could forgo this assumption, formulating property (
LB
) in terms of the global metric
\dist_{\mathbf{X}}
as opposed to the local metrics
\dist_{X_{v}}
.
However, such a condition would be unwieldy – potentially very difficult – to verify in practice.

As a consequence of the above, quasi-isometries of a link bottlenecked graph of spaces also coarsely preserve the edge cylinders.

Lemma 6.10
.

Let
\mathbf{X}=(\Gamma,X_{-},Y_{-},\alpha_{-})
be a uniformly hyperbolic, link bottlenecked graph of spaces.
Let
\varphi\colon\left\lvert\mathbf{X}\right\rvert\to\left\lvert\mathbf{X}\right\rvert
be a
(\lambda,c)
-quasi-isometry such that
\Phi(\varphi)=\id_{\Gamma}
.
There is
T=T(\lambda,c,\mathbf{X})\geq 0
such that for any
e\in E(\Gamma)
,

\dHaus_{\mathbf{X}}(\varphi(Z_{e}),Z_{e})\leq T,

where
Z_{e}\subseteq\left\lvert\mathbf{X}\right\rvert
is the edge cylinder corresponding to
e
.

Proof.

Let
\varphi\colon\left\lvert\mathbf{X}\right\rvert\to\left\lvert\mathbf{X}\right\rvert
be a
(\lambda,c)
-quasi-isometry such that
\Phi(\varphi)=\id_{\Gamma}
.
Let
Z_{e}
be an edge cylinder connecting vertex spaces
X_{u}
and
X_{v}
.
Note that, by assumption,
u=\Phi(\varphi)(u)
and
v=\Phi(\varphi)(v)
.
We have that

Z_{e}=B_{\mathbf{X}}(X_{u};1)\cap B_{\mathbf{X}}(X_{v};1).

And so, since
\varphi
is a
(\lambda,c)
-quasi-isometry,

\varphi(Z_{e})\subseteq B_{\mathbf{X}}(\varphi(X_{u});\lambda+c)\cap B_{\mathbf{X}}(\varphi(X_{u});\lambda+c).

Let
C=C(\lambda,c,\mathbf{X},\mathbf{X})
be as in Lemma
6.8
.
The lemma tells us that
\varphi(X_{w})
is a Hausdorff distance of at most
C
from
X_{w}
for all
w\in V(\Gamma)
, as
w=\Phi(\varphi)(w)
.
Therefore,

\varphi(Z_{e})\subseteq B_{\mathbf{X}}(X_{u};\lambda+c+C)\cap B_{\mathbf{X}}(X_{v};\lambda+c+C).

Now, by Lemma
5.10
, the set on the right-hand side is contained in
B_{\mathbf{X}}(Z_{e};K)
, for some
K>0
depending only on
\lambda
,
c
, and the uniform hyperbolicity constants of
\mathbf{X}
.
Thus the above inclusion yields

\varphi(Z_{e})\subseteq B_{\mathbf{X}}(Z_{e};K).

Applying the same argument to a quasi-inverse
\psi
of
\varphi
shows that
\dHaus_{\mathbf{X}}(\varphi(Z_{e}),Z_{e})
is bounded by an constant
T\geq 0
depending only on
\lambda
,
c
, and
\mathbf{X}
.
∎

We can say more about the function
\Phi
from Lemma
6.8
.

Lemma 6.11
.

Let
\mathbf{X}
,
\mathbf{Y}
be a totally link bottlenecked pair of uniformly hyperbolic graphs of spaces.
For any quasi-isometry
\varphi\colon\left\lvert\mathbf{X}\right\rvert\to\left\lvert\mathbf{Y}\right\rvert
, the map
\Phi(\varphi)\colon V(\Gamma)\to V(\Delta)
is a bijection.
Moreover, if
\mathbf{X}
and
\mathbf{Y}
have unbounded edge spaces, then
\Phi(\varphi)
is a graph isomorphism and
\Phi
is a group homomorphism
\QI(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Y})\to\Isom(\Gamma,\Delta)
.

Proof.

We first show that
\Phi(\varphi)
is surjective.
If not,
\varphi
cannot be coarsely surjective by Lemma
6.6
.
Next, note that
\Phi(\varphi)
must also be injective, as otherwise two vertex spaces would lie at finite Hausdorff distance, which again contradicts Lemma
6.6
.

Now, suppose
\mathbf{X}
has unbounded edge spaces.
Then by Lemma
5.10
we have that the intersection of any tubular neighbourhoods of any two distinct vertex spaces
X_{u}
and
X_{v}
is infinite if and only if
u
and
v
are adjacent in
\Gamma
.
This implies that
\Phi(\varphi)(v)
and
\Phi(\varphi)(u)
are adjacent if and only if
u
and
v
are adjacent.

The above shows that
\Phi\colon\QI(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Y})\to\Isom(\Gamma,\Delta)
is a well-defined function.
It only remains to check that it is a homomorphism.
Let
\varphi,\varphi^{\prime}\colon\left\lvert\mathbf{X}\right\rvert\to\left\lvert\mathbf{X}\right\rvert
be quasi-isometries.
Applying Lemma
6.8
, we see that
\varphi\varphi^{\prime}(X_{v})
is Hausdorff close to the vertex space
X_{\Phi(\varphi\varphi^{\prime}(v))}
on one hand.
However, the same lemma implies that
\varphi^{\prime}(X_{v})
is close to
X_{\Phi(\varphi\varphi^{\prime})(v)}
.
On the other hand, then, we have
\varphi\varphi^{\prime}(X_{v})
is close to
X_{\Phi(\varphi)\Phi(\varphi^{\prime})(v)}
.
Therefore Lemma
6.6
gives that
\Phi(\varphi\varphi^{\prime})(v)=\Phi(\varphi)\Phi(\varphi^{\prime})(v)
for all
v\in V(\Gamma)
, as required.
∎

Corollary 6.12
.

Let
\mathbf{X}=(\Gamma,X_{-},Y_{-},\alpha_{-})
,
\mathbf{Y}=(\Delta,X^{\prime}_{-},Y^{\prime}_{-},\beta_{-})
be uniformly hyperbolic graphs of spaces with unbounded edge spaces, such that the pair
(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Y})
is totally link bottlenecked.
If
\mathbf{X}
and
\mathbf{Y}
are quasi-isometric then
\Gamma
and
\Delta
are isometric.

Let
\iota\colon\Isom(\mathbf{X})\to\QI(\mathbf{X})
denote the natural map, and let us write

\Psi=\Phi\circ\iota,

where
\Phi
is the map of Lemma
6.8
.
We have the following commutative diagram:

(
\dagger
)

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\Isom(\mathbf{X})}}

6.2.
Local congruence

Our second condition provides a criterion for isometries of the underlying graph to lift to isometries of the whole graph of spaces.

Definition 6.13

(Locally congruent)
.

Let
\mathbf{X}=(\Gamma,X_{-},Y_{-},\alpha_{-})
be a graph of spaces. We say that
\mathbf{X}
is
locally congruent
if the following holds:

(LC)

for all
f\in\Isom(\Gamma)
,

, there exists an isometry
\tilde{f}_{v}:X_{v}\to X_{f(v)}
such that for all

\tilde{f}_{v}(\alpha_{e}(Y_{e}))=\alpha_{f(e)}(Y_{f(e)})
.

Intuitively, a graph of spaces is locally congruent if, given two vertices of the underlying graph in the same automorphic orbit, the corresponding vertex spaces and their links look identical.

Proposition 6.14
.

Let
\mathbf{X}=(\Gamma,X_{-},Y_{-},\alpha_{-})
be a locally congruent graph of spaces. Then there exists an injective function

is uniformly hyperbolic, link bottlenecked, and has unbounded edge spaces, then

.
In particular,
\Psi
and
\Phi
are surjective.

Proof.

Let

.
We construct an isometry

as follows.
Given

, we define
\tilde{f}
on

via

\chi(f)|_{X_{v}}=i_{v}\circ\tilde{f}_{v}
,
where
\tilde{f}_{v}
is the isometry instantiated by (

is the natural inclusion.
Now (

) implies that there is an isometry

for any edge

.
We use this to extend our definition of

to the edge cylinders

\chi(f)(y,t)=({\alpha_{f(e)}}^{-1}\tilde{f}_{v}\alpha_{e}(y),t)

6.3.
Local rigidity

The third and final property we discuss provides control over the kernel of the homomorphism
\Phi\colon\QI(\mathbf{X})\to\Isom(\Gamma)

Definition 6.15

(Link rigidity)
.

Let
\mathbf{X}=(\Gamma,X_{-},Y_{-},\alpha_{-})

\mathbf{X}
is
link rigid
if the following assertion holds:

(LR)

for all

\hskip-42.67912pt\dHaus_{X_{v}}(\alpha_{e}(Y_{e}),\psi(\alpha_{e}(Y_{e})))<\infty,

8.
Hyperbolicity of uniformly hyperbolic graphs of spaces

In this section, we will prove that the metric realisation of a uniformly hyperbolic graph of spaces is hyperbolic if and only if the underlying graph is.
We will prove hyperbolicity by verifying the thin triangles condition explicitly. To do this, we need a good understanding of geodesics in uniformly hyperbolic graphs of spaces.

Recall that Proposition
5.5
tells us that geodesics in the graph of spaces project onto quasigeodesics of the graph.
Building on this, we implement a series of local moves that yield a set of standardised paths which are Hausdorff close to geodesics.
This reduces thinness of geodesic triangles to thinness of triangles whose sides are given by such standardised paths, a much simpler condition to verify.

\dHaus_{\mathbf{X}}(\operatorname{Path}_{\mathbf{X}}(S),\operatorname{Path}_{\mathbf{X}}(S^{\prime}))\leq 2\delta+1
.
∎

Lemma 8.5
.

Let
\mathbf{X}=(\Gamma,X_{-},Y_{-},\alpha_{-})
be a
(\delta,C)
-uniformly hyperbolic graph of spaces satisfying the assumptions of Convention
4.12
.
Suppose that
p
is a geodesic and let
S_{p}=(x_{0},\dots,x_{m})
be its associated reduced string, with pieces
P_{1},\dots,P_{m}
.

Suppose that
P_{i}=Z_{e}
is an edge cylinder with adjacent vertex spaces
P_{i-1}=X_{u}
and
P_{i+1}=X_{v}
for some

.
Let
x_{i-1}^{\prime}=\pi_{u,e}(x_{i-2}),x_{i}^{\prime}=\pi_{v,e}(x_{i+1})
, and write

.
Then we have

\dHaus_{\mathbf{X}}(p,\operatorname{Path}_{\mathbf{X}}(S^{\prime}))\leq 12\delta+4.

Proof.

For simplicity, we suppose

, so that
S_{p}=(x_{0},x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})
,
x_{1}^{\prime}=\pi_{u,e}(x_{0})
, and
x_{2}^{\prime}=\pi_{v,e}(x_{3})
.
Since the difference between
S_{p}
and
S^{\prime}
is at most two consecutive terms, this assumptions is without loss of generality.

Lemma
8.3
shows that
\dHaus_{\mathbf{X}}(p,\operatorname{Path}_{\mathbf{X}}(x_{0},x_{1},\overline{x_{2}},x_{3}))\leq 4\delta+1
.
By Lemma
8.4
,

\dHaus_{\mathbf{X}}(\operatorname{Path}_{\mathbf{X}}(x_{0},x_{1},\overline{x_{2}},x_{3}),\operatorname{Path}_{\mathbf{X}}(x_{0},x_{1}^{\prime},\overline{x_{2}},x_{3}))\leq 2\delta+1.

Again by Lemma
8.3
, we have

\dHaus_{\mathbf{X}}(\operatorname{Path}_{\mathbf{X}}(x_{0},x_{1}^{\prime},\overline{x_{2}},x_{3}),\operatorname{Path}_{\mathbf{X}}(x_{0},x_{1}^{\prime},\overline{x_{1}^{\prime}},x_{3}))\leq 4\delta+1.

Finally, from Lemma
8.4
we obtain

\displaystyle\dHaus_{\mathbf{X}}(\operatorname{Path}_{\mathbf{X}}

\displaystyle(x_{0},x_{1}^{\prime},\overline{x_{1}^{\prime}},x_{3}),\operatorname{Path}_{\mathbf{X}}(x_{0},x_{1}^{\prime},x_{2}^{\prime},x_{3}))\leq 2\delta+1.

Combining the four inequalities above completes the proof.
∎

We can show that whenever two different geodesic reduced strings start at the same point in a vertex space but travel through different edge cylinders initially, then at least one of them passes close to the base point in that vertex space.

Lemma 8.6
.

Let
\mathbf{X}=(\Gamma,X_{-},Y_{-},\alpha_{-})
be a
(\delta,C)
-uniformly hyperbolic graph of spaces satisfying the assumptions of Convention
4.12
.

Let
p
be a geodesic in
\left\lvert\mathbf{X}\right\rvert
, and write
S_{p}=(x_{0},\dots,x_{m})
for the reduced string associated to
p
, with pieces
P_{i}
.
If
m\geq 2
and
P_{1}=X_{v}
is a vertex space, then

\dHaus_{X_{v}}([x_{0},x_{1}]\cup[x_{1},y_{v}],[x_{0},y_{v}])\leq 14\delta+4,

where

is the basepoint of
X_{v}
.

Proof.

Let
x_{1}^{\prime}=\pi_{v,e}(x_{0})
and write

.

8.5
tells us that
\operatorname{Path}_{\mathbf{X}}(S^{\prime})
a Hausdorff distance at most

.
Moreover, by Lemma
1.12
, we have
\dHaus_{X_{v}}([x_{0},y_{v}],[x_{0},x_{1}^{\prime}]\cup[x_{1}^{\prime},y_{v}])\leq 2\delta
.

\dHaus_{X_{v}}([x_{0},y_{v}],[x_{0},x_{1}]\cup[x_{1},y_{v}])\leq 14\delta+4,

∎

We can use Lemma
8.6
to show that geodesics that pass through at least two edge cylinders fellow-travel a path through basepoints.

Proposition 8.7
.

Let
\mathbf{X}=(\Gamma,X_{-},Y_{-},\alpha_{-})
be a
(\delta,C)
-uniformly hyperbolic graph of spaces with the assumptions of Convention
4.12
.
There is a constant
F=F(\delta,C)\geq 0
such that the following is true.

Let
p\colon I\to\left\lvert\mathbf{X}\right\rvert
be a geodesic with endpoints
x
and
z
.
Write
v_{1},\dots,v_{n}\in V(\Gamma)
for the sequence of vertices for which the image of
p
meets vertex spaces
X_{v_{1}},\dots,X_{v_{n}}
.
For each
i=1,\dots,n
, let
y_{v_{i}}
be the basepoint of
X_{v_{i}}
given by uniform hyperbolicity.
If
p
intersects at least two edge cylinders, then

\dHaus_{\mathbf{X}}(p,\operatorname{Path}_{\mathbf{X}}(S))\leq F,

where
S=(x,y_{v_{1}},\dots,y_{v_{n}},z)
.

Proof.

Let
S_{p}=(x_{0},\dots,x_{m})
be the reduced string associated to
p
, where
x_{0}=x
and
x_{m}=y
.
If the first piece of this string is a vertex space, define
x_{0}^{\prime}=x_{0}
.
Otherwise
x_{0}=(z,t)
lies in an edge cylinder
Z_{e}
, and
x_{1}
lies in a vertex space
X_{v}
with
e
incident on
v
.
Let
u\neq v
denote the other vertex upon which
e
is incident; without loss of generality suppose that
(z,0)\in X_{u}
(otherwise,
(z,1)\in X_{u}
).
We write
x_{0}^{\prime}=(z,0)
and, similarly define
x_{m}^{\prime}
if the final piece of
S_{p}
is an edge cylinder.
Of course,
\dist_{\mathbf{X}}(x_{0},x_{0}^{\prime})\leq 1
and
\dist_{\mathbf{X}}(x_{m},x_{m}^{\prime})\leq 1
.
Now the string
S=(x_{0}^{\prime},x_{0}^{\prime},x_{1},\dots,x_{m-1},x_{m}^{\prime},x_{m}^{\prime})
is reduced and its first and last pieces are vertex spaces.
We write
p^{\prime}=\operatorname{Path}_{\mathbf{X}}(S)
so that

(13)

\ell(S)\leq\dist_{\mathbf{X}}(x_{0}^{\prime},x_{m}^{\prime})+4,

and moreover,
\dHaus_{\mathbf{X}}(p,p^{\prime})\leq 1
.

For convenience, we relabel
S=(x_{0},\dots,x_{m})
.
Since the first and last pieces of
S
are vertex spaces,
m=2n-1
is odd.
For each
i=1,\dots,n
, let
v_{i}\in V(\Gamma)
be such that
x_{2i-2}
and
x_{2i-1}
are contained in
X_{v_{i}}
.
By uniform hyperbolicity and Lemma
1.11
, there are points
t
and
t^{\prime}
on geodesics
[x_{2},x_{3}]\subseteq X_{v_{2}}
and
[x_{m-3},x_{m-2}]\subseteq X_{v_{n-1}}
such that

\dist_{X_{v_{2}}}(t,y_{v_{2}})\leq C+\delta\quad\textnormal{ and }\quad\dist_{X_{v_{n-1}}}(t^{\prime},y_{v_{n-1}})\leq C+\delta

Further, applying Lemma
5.4
with (
13
), we have that
\dist_{X_{v_{i}}}(x_{2i-1},y_{v_{i}})\leq D+4
for
2\leq i\leq n-3
and
\dist_{X_{v_{i}}}(x_{2i-2},y_{v_{i}})\leq D+4
for
4\leq u\leq n-1
.
Thinness of triangles implies that
p^{\prime}
remains
(D+\delta)
-close to
\operatorname{Path}_{\mathbf{X}}(y_{v_{2}},\cdots,y_{v_{n-1}})
on the subpath between
t
and
t
(since
D\geq C+\delta
, in any case).
By hyperbolicity of
X_{v_{2}}
we have

\dHaus_{X_{v_{2}}}([\overline{x_{2}},t],[\overline{x_{2}},y_{v_{2}}])\leq D+\delta+4

whereas the geometry of the edge cylinder guarantees

\dHaus_{Z_{e_{1}}}([x_{1},y_{v_{1}}]\cup[y_{v_{1}},y_{v_{2}}],[x_{1},\overline{x_{2}}]\cup{[\overline{x_{2}},y_{v_{2}}]})=1.

A symmetrical argument shows that analogous inequalities hold involving the final terms of the string.
Thus applying Lemma
8.6
allows us to conclude that

\dHaus_{\mathbf{X}}(\operatorname{Path}_{\mathbf{X}}(S),p^{\prime})\leq D+15\delta+9.

As
\dHaus_{\mathbf{X}}(p,p^{\prime})\leq 1
, setting
F=D+15\delta+10
thus completes the proof.
∎

The next lemma shows that geodesics whose endpoints lie in edge cylinders are close to geodesics with endpoints in vertex spaces.
Thus, we will only have to check thinness of triangles with vertices in vertex spaces.
We introduce a notation for projections which makes the statement of the following lemma much easier.

Definition 8.8

(Projection to vertex space)
.

Let
\mathbf{X}=(\Gamma,X_{-},Y_{-},\alpha_{-})
be a graph of spaces.
If
v\in V(\Gamma)
is a vertex and
e\in E(\Gamma)
is such that
\tau(e)=v
, we define the
projection from the edge cylinder

Z_{e}=Y_{e}\times[0,1]
to
X_{v}
as

\displaystyle\pi_{e,v}\colon Z_{e}\to X_{v_{i}},(z,t)\mapsto\alpha_{e}(z).

For
x
in the interior of an edge cylinder
Z_{e}
, we write
v(z)=\{\pi_{e,u}(x),\pi_{e,v}(x)\}
and for a point
x
in a vertex space, we write
v(x)=\{x\}
.

Lemma 8.9
.

Let
\mathbf{X}=(\Gamma,X_{-},Y_{-},\alpha_{-})
be a
(\delta,C)
-uniformly hyperbolic graph of spaces, and let us assume that the assumptions of Convention
4.12
are satisfied.
Suppose that
\Gamma
is
\delta_{\Gamma}
-hyperbolic for some
\delta_{\Gamma}\geq 0

There is a constant
\Theta=\Theta(\delta,C,\delta_{\Gamma})\geq 0
such that the following is true.

Let
x,x^{\prime}\in\left\lvert\mathbf{X}\right\rvert
and let
p
be a geodesic between
x
and
x^{\prime}
.
If at least one of
x
or
x^{\prime}
lie in an edge cylinder, then for any geodesic
q
between a point of
v(x)
and
v(x^{\prime})
, we have

\dHaus_{\mathbf{X}}(p,q)\leq\Theta.

Proof.

Suppose that there is
v\in V(\Gamma)
such that
x
and
x^{\prime}
both lie in
X_{v}
or its adjacent edge cylinders.
Let
q
be a geodesic as in the lemma statement.
Then
q
has endpoints in
B_{\mathbf{X}}(X_{v};1)
, which is
\sigma
-quasiconvex following Remark
5.8
, where
\sigma
depends only on
\delta
and
C
.
Therefore
q
is contained in
B=B_{\mathbf{X}}(X_{v};\sigma+1)
.

As
X_{u}
is
\delta
-hyperbolic, the set
B
is
\delta^{\prime}
-hyperbolic where
\delta^{\prime}=\delta+2\sigma+2
.
Now applying the Morse Lemma to the
(1,2)
-quasi-geodesic in
B
that follows
p
and shares endpoints with
q
yields the required statement.

It remains to consider the cases that
x
and
x^{\prime}
are neither in adjacent pieces of
\mathbf{X}
, nor share any adjacent pieces.
It must be that both
p
and
q
intersect at least two edge cylinders in this case.
We write
z\in v(x)
and
z^{\prime}\in v(z^{\prime})
for the endpoints of
q
.
Let
X_{u_{1}},\dots,X_{u_{n}}
be the sequence of vertex spaces intersected by
p
, and
X_{v_{1}},\dots,X_{v_{m}}
those by
q
.
We also write
p^{\prime}=\operatorname{Path}_{\mathbf{X}}(x,y_{u_{1}},\dots,y_{u_{n}},x^{\prime})
and
q^{\prime}=\operatorname{Path}_{\mathbf{X}}(z,y_{v_{1}},\dots,y_{v_{m}},z^{\prime})
.

By Proposition
8.7
, there is
F=F(\delta,C)
such that

(14)

\dHaus_{\mathbf{X}}(p,p^{\prime})\leq F\quad\textnormal{and}\quad\dHaus_{\mathbf{X}}(q,q^{\prime})\leq F.

Moreover, by Proposition
5.5
,
u_{1}\dots u_{n}
and
v_{1}\dots v_{m}
are
(1,K)
-quasi-geodesics in
\Gamma
, where
K=K(\delta,C)\geq 0
.
By assumption,
\Gamma
is
\delta_{\Gamma}
-hyperbolic.
Hence we have that
\dHaus_{\Gamma}(u_{1}\dots u_{n},v_{1}\dots v_{m})\leq 2M
, where
M=M(1,K,\delta_{\Gamma})\geq 0
is the constant of the Morse lemma.
As
\Gamma
is isometric to
\left\lvert\mathbf{Y}\right\rvert
by Lemma
5.3
and the edge cylinders have unit width,
\dHaus_{\mathbf{X}}(p^{\prime},q^{\prime})\leq 2M+1
.
Combining this with (
14
) and setting
\Theta=2F+2M+1
gives the lemma statement.
∎

A final useful observation is that whenever two geodesics starting at a point in a vertex space
X_{v}
and enter different edge cylinders, then at least one of them must pass close to the basepoint of
X_{v}
.

Lemma 8.10
.

Let
\mathbf{X}=(\Gamma,X_{-},Y_{-},\alpha_{-})
be a
(\delta,C)
-uniformly hyperbolic graph of spaces satisfying the assumptions of Convention
4.12
.
Let
p,q
be geodesics in
\left\lvert\mathbf{X}\right\rvert
with the same initial point
x_{0}\in X_{v}
.
Let
S_{p}=(x_{0},x_{1},\dots,x_{m})
and
S_{q}=(x_{0},x_{1}^{\prime},\dots,x_{n}^{\prime})
be the reduced strings associated to
p
and
q
.
Suppose that
x_{1}
and
x_{1}^{\prime}
are contained in distinct edge cylinders
Z_{e}
and
Z_{e^{\prime}}
.
Then there is a point
a\in X_{v}
lying on
p
or
q
with

\min\{\dist_{X_{v}}(a,y_{v}),\dist_{X_{v}}(b,y_{v})\}\leq C+5\delta,

where
y_{v}\in X_{v}
is the basepoint of
X_{v}
.

Proof.

We write
z=\pi_{v,e}(x_{0})
and
z^{\prime}=\pi_{v,e^{\prime}}(x_{0})
.
Lemma
1.12
give us that

(15)

\langle x_{0},y_{v}\rangle_{z}\leq\delta\quad\textnormal{ and }\quad\langle x_{0},y_{v}\rangle_{z^{\prime}}\leq\delta.

By uniform hyperbolicity we know that
\langle z,z^{\prime}\rangle_{y_{v}}\leq C
.
The four-point condition of Remark
1.9
thus gives

\min\{\langle x_{0},z\rangle_{y_{v}},\langle x_{0},z^{\prime}\rangle_{y_{v}}\}\leq C+2\delta.

Suppose the minimum is attained with the first inner product.
Then (
15
) gives that

\dist_{X_{v}}(y_{v},z)=\langle x_{0},y_{v}\rangle_{z}+\langle x_{0},z\rangle_{y_{v}}\leq C+3\delta.

Moreover, Lemma
1.12
tells us that
[x_{0},x_{1}]
is a Hausdorff distance of at most
2\delta
from
[x_{0},z]\cup[z,x_{1}]
.
Hence there is a point
a\in[x_{0},x_{1}]
such that
\dist_{X_{v}}(z,a)\leq 2\delta
.
Combining with the above inequality, we have
\dist_{X_{v}}(y_{v},a)\leq C+5\delta
as required.
Similarly, if the minimum were attained with the second inner product, we would have
a\in[x_{0},x_{1}^{\prime}]
with the same property.
∎

We are now ready to prove the main result of this section.

Theorem 8.11
.

Let
\mathbf{X}=(\Gamma,X_{-},Y_{-},\alpha_{-})
be a
(\delta,C)
-uniformly hyperbolic graph of spaces, and suppose that
\mathbf{X}
satisfies the assumptions of Convention
4.12
. Then
|\mathbf{X}|
is a hyperbolic metric space if and only if its underlying graph
\Gamma
is. More precisely:

(1)

If
\Gamma
is
\delta_{\Gamma}
-hyperbolic, then there exists
\delta^{\prime}=\delta^{\prime}(\delta,C,\delta_{\Gamma})\geq 0

\left\lvert\mathbf{X}\right\rvert
is
\delta^{\prime}
-hyperbolic.

(2)

If
\left\lvert\mathbf{X}\right\rvert
is
\delta^{\prime}
-hyperbolic, then so is
\Gamma
.

Proof.

The second statement is a direct consequence of the fact that there exists a convex subspace of
|\mathbf{X}|
isometric to
\Gamma
, as in Lemma
5.3
.
The remainder of the proof is dedicated to a proof of the first statement.

Throughout this proof, let
D=D(\delta,C)
be the constant from Lemma
5.4
,
\sigma=\sigma(\delta,C)
the quasiconvexity constant of Proposition
5.7
, and

the constant of Proposition
5.5
.
Let

be the constant of Proposition
8.7
.
Recall that
\left\lvert\mathbf{Y}\right\rvert
is an isometrically embedded copy of
\Gamma
in
\left\lvert\mathbf{X}\right\rvert
containing the basepoints of
\mathbf{X}
, as in Lemma
5.3
.

Assume that
\Gamma
is
\delta_{\Gamma}
-hyperbolic and let
\Delta
be a geodesic triangle in
|\mathbf{X}|
with vertices
x,y,z
.
Let
\gamma_{a}
be the side of
\Delta
with endpoints
x
and
z
,
\gamma_{b}
with
x
and
y
, and
\gamma_{c}
with
y
and
z
.

We distinguish cases depending on the number of edge cylinders traversed by sides of
\Delta
: write
a,b,
and
c
for the numbers of edge cylinders intersected by the three sides
\gamma_{a},\gamma_{b},\gamma_{c}
of
\Delta
, respectively.
By Lemma
8.9
, there is
\Theta=\Theta(\delta,C,\delta_{\Gamma})\geq 0
such that the sides of
\Delta
are a Hausdorff distance of at most
\Theta
from geodesics with endpoints in vertex spaces.
It thus suffices to consider the case when the vertices of
\Delta
are in vertex spaces, say

and
z\in X_{w}
.
In each case, we will replace

with a triangle

Finally, we will write
\Delta_{\Gamma}
to denote the triangle in

, and
\gamma_{c}

(1,K)
-quasi-geodesic triangle in

As

is

-hyperbolic,

is

-thin, where

The case

:

It must be that

for some

.
Since

is
\delta
-hyperbolic,

is
\delta
-thin.

The case

:

Assume that

and

The case

The case

:

Necessarily, the vertices

and

\max\{\dist_{X_{u}}(p_{x},y_{u}),\dist_{X_{v}}(p_{y},y_{v}),\dist_{X_{w}}(p_{z},y_{w})\}\leq C+5\delta.

There are two cases, after possibly relabelling: either

and

, or otherwise
p_{x}\in\gamma_{a},p_{y}\in\gamma_{b},p_{z}\in\gamma_{c}
.
In any case, write
\mathbf{X}_{1},\mathbf{X}_{2},
and
\mathbf{X}_{3}
for the subgraphs of spaces with underlying graphs on
\{u,w\},\{u,v\},
and
\{v,w\}
respectively.
As in Remark
4.5
, we identify each
\left\lvert\mathbf{X}_{i}\right\rvert
with its natural image in
\mathbf{X}
.
By the previous cases, each
\left\lvert\mathbf{X}_{i}\right\rvert
is
\delta_{0}
-hyperbolic, where
\delta_{0}=33\delta+10
.

Let us treat the first case.
We consider the geodesic polygons
P_{1}\subseteq\left\lvert\mathbf{X}_{1}\right\rvert
with vertices
\{p_{x},y_{u},y_{w},p_{z}\}
,
P_{2}\subseteq\left\lvert\mathbf{X}_{2}\right\rvert
with vertices
\{x,p_{x},y_{u},y_{v},p_{y}\}
, and
P_{3}\subseteq\left\lvert\mathbf{X}_{3}\right\rvert
with vertices
\{y,z,p_{z},y_{w},y_{v},p_{y}\}
.
For any
i=1,2,3
, the space
\left\lvert\mathbf{X}_{i}\right\rvert
is
\delta_{0}
-hyperbolic, so each side of
P_{i}
is contained in a
4\delta_{0}
neighbourhood of the others, as the polygons have at most
6
sides.
The sides of
P_{i}
are either segments of distinct sides of
\Delta
, or are otherwise at most
2C+10\delta+1
from two sides of
\Delta
by (
16
).
Moreover,
\Delta
is covered by the sides of
P_{1},P_{2},
and
P_{3}
.
Hence
\Delta
is
\delta^{\prime}
-thin, where
\delta^{\prime}
is the maximum of
3C+15\delta+2
and
4\delta_{0}+2C+10\delta+1
.

In the other case, we similarly divide
\Delta
using the pentagons
P_{1}\subseteq\left\lvert\mathbf{X}_{1}\right\rvert
with vertices
\{p_{x},y_{u},y_{w},p_{z},z\}
,
P_{2}\subseteq\left\lvert\mathbf{X}_{2}\right\rvert
with vertices
\{x,p_{x},y_{u},y_{v},p_{y}\}
, and
P_{3}\subseteq\left\lvert\mathbf{X}_{3}\right\rvert
with vertices
\{y,p_{y},y_{v},y_{w},p_{z}\}
.
Each of these is contained in
\left\lvert\mathbf{X}_{i}\right\rvert
for some
i=1,2,3
and is therefore
3\delta_{0}
-thin.
By the same considerations as above,
\Delta
is
\delta^{\prime}
-thin.

The case
a\geq 2,b,c=1
:

First, suppose that
\gamma_{b},\gamma_{c}
meet the same edge cylinder, so that
\gamma_{a}
is a geodesic with both endpoints in a single vertex space, say
X_{v}
.
In this case,
\gamma_{a}\subseteq B_{\mathbf{X}}(X_{v};\sigma)
by Proposition
5.7
.
This effectively reduces this case to that of
a=0,b,c=1
as above.
Hence we may suppose that
\gamma_{b}
and
\gamma_{c}
do not intersect the same edge cylinder.
Necessarily
v
is adjacent to both
u
and
w
.

Let
S_{a}
be the string
(x,y_{1},\dots,y_{n},z)
, where
y_{1},\dots,y_{n}
are the basepoints of the vertex spaces
\gamma_{a}
meets.
By Proposition
5.5
, the path
\gamma=\operatorname{Path}_{\mathbf{X}}(y_{1},\dots,y_{n})
is
(1,K)
-quasi-geodesic and Proposition
8.7
gives
\dHaus_{\mathbf{X}}(\gamma_{a},\operatorname{Path}_{\mathbf{X}}(S_{a}))\leq F
.
Since
\dist_{\Gamma}(u,w)\leq 2
, we have
\ell(\gamma)\leq K+2
.
Writing
\gamma^{\prime}_{a}=\operatorname{Path}_{\mathbf{X}}(x,y_{u},y_{v},y)
, then, we have
\dHaus_{\mathbf{X}}(\gamma_{a},\gamma^{\prime}_{a})\leq F+K+2
.
Let
\Delta^{\prime}
be the triangle with sides
\gamma_{a}^{\prime},\gamma_{b}
, and
\gamma_{c}
.

By Lemma
8.10
, there is a point
p\in X_{v}
on either
\gamma_{b}
or
\gamma_{c}
with
\dist_{X_{v}}(p,y_{v})\leq C+5\delta
; suppose it is the former without loss of generality.
Let
\mathbf{X}_{1}
and
\mathbf{X}_{2}
be the subgraphs of spaces on vertices
\{u,v\}
and
\{v,w\}
respectively, and identify each
\left\lvert\mathbf{X}_{i}\right\rvert
with its natural image in
\left\lvert\mathbf{X}\right\rvert
as in Remark
4.5
.
Consider the geodesic rectangle
P_{1}\subseteq\left\lvert\mathbf{X}_{1}\right\rvert
with vertices
\{x,y_{u},y_{v},p\}
and the geodesic pentagon
P_{2}\subseteq\left\lvert\mathbf{X}_{2}\right\rvert
with vertices
\{y,z,y_{w},y_{v},p\}
.
As in the case
a,b,c=1
, each side is contained in a
3\delta_{0}
-neighbourhood of the others, and the sides either belong to distinct sides of
\Delta^{\prime}
or a distance of at most
C+5\delta+1
from two sides of
\Delta^{\prime}
.
It follows that
\Delta^{\prime}
is
\delta^{\prime}
-thin, where
\delta^{\prime}=3\delta_{0}+C+5\delta+1
, and hence that
\Delta
is
(\delta^{\prime}+F+K+2)
-thin.

The case
a,b\geq 2,c=1
:

By Proposition
8.7
the sides
\gamma_{a}
and
\gamma_{b}
are
F
-close to paths of strings
S_{a}=(y,y_{v},y_{1},\ldots,y_{m},y_{u},x)
and
S_{b}=(z,y_{w},y_{1}^{\prime},\ldots,y_{n}^{\prime},y_{u},x)
respectively.
By Proposition
5.5
, the segments of these paths in
\left\lvert\mathbf{Y}\right\rvert
form
(1,K)
-quasi-geodesics.
Write
S^{\prime}_{b}=(z,y_{w},y_{v},y_{1},\dots,y_{m},y_{u},x)
and observe that
\operatorname{Path}_{\mathbf{X}}(S_{b})
and
\operatorname{Path}_{\mathbf{X}}(S^{\prime}_{b})
are a Hausdorff distance of at most
M^{\prime}=M(1,K+1,\delta_{\Gamma})
apart, since they differ only in the middle segments, and
\left\lvert\mathbf{Y}\right\rvert
is
\delta_{\Gamma}
-hyperbolic.
We will write
\gamma^{\prime}_{a}=\operatorname{Path}_{\mathbf{X}}(S_{a})
and
\gamma^{\prime}_{b}=\operatorname{Path}_{\mathbf{X}}(S^{\prime}_{b})
.

Let
e\in E(\Gamma)
be the edge joining
v
and
w
, and write
\Delta^{\prime}
for the triangle with sides
\gamma^{\prime}_{a},\gamma^{\prime}_{b}
, and
\gamma_{c}
.
Outside of
H=X_{v}\cup Z_{e}\cup X_{w}
, the sides of the triangle
\Delta^{\prime}
coincide.
The intersection of
\Delta^{\prime}
with
H
is a triangle whose sides consist of two geodesics and one
(1,1)
-quasigeodesic.
As we showed
H
is
(33\delta+10)
-hyperbolic in the case
a,b=1,c=0
, this triangle is
(33\delta+M^{\prime\prime}+10)
-thin, where
M^{\prime\prime}=M(1,1,33\delta+10)\geq 0
is obtained by the Morse lemma.
Hence
\Delta
is
(33\delta+M^{\prime\prime}+M+2F+10)
-thin.

The case
a,b,c\geq 2
:

By Proposition
8.7
the sides of
\Delta
are
F
-close to the sides of
\Delta_{\Gamma}
, concatenated with
[x,y_{u}],[y,y_{v}],
and
[z,y_{w}]
as appropriate.
This latter triangle is
(\delta_{\Gamma}+2M)
-thin, as
\Delta_{\Gamma}
is
(\delta_{\Gamma}+2M)
-thin and its sides coincide away from
\Delta_{\Gamma}
.
Hence
\Delta
is
(\delta_{\Gamma}+2M+2F)
-thin.
∎

Remark 8.12
.

Some cases in the proof of the above theorem may be bypassed if one applies the ‘guessing geodesics’ criterion for hyperbolicity (see, for example,
[
21
, Lemma 3.5]
).
However, checking the coherence condition holds among the chosen paths is tantamount to checking many of the same things as is done above.
With this in mind, we present the potentially longer but more direct proof above.
