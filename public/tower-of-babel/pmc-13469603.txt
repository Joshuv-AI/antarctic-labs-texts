Clinical determinants of retinal age gap estimated from fundus photographs in glaucoma patients
Authors: Kengo Utsunomiya, Zhenyang Zhu, Xiaoyang Mao, Kenji Kashiwagi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRetinal age gap (RAG), defined as the difference between artificial intelligence-predicted retinal age and chronological age derived from fundus photographs, has been proposed as a potential biomarker of biological aging; however, the influence of ocular factors on RAG remains unclear. This retrospective observational study investigated clinical determinants of RAG in 283 eyes of adults who underwent 45-degree color fundus photography at the University of Yamanashi Hospital between February 2010 and April 2025. Retinal age was estimated using a deep learning-based algorithm developed from the Japan Ocular Imaging Registry. Associations between RAG and ocular and clinical variables were evaluated using linear mixed-effects models to account for within-subject correlations. The mean chronological age was 65.5 ± 11.7 years, the mean predicted retinal age was 59.9 ± 17.0 years, and the mean RAG was − 5.7 ± 19.1 years. Lens status was strongly associated with RAG, with phakic eyes showing greater RAG values than pseudophakic eyes. Worse visual acuity and more hyperopic refractive error were also associated with greater RAG values. Overall glaucoma severity was not significantly associated with RAG, and mean deviation was not associated with RAG when analyzed as a continuous variable. In phakic eyes, glaucoma eyes showed greater RAG values than control eyes. These findings indicate that fundus-based retinal age estimation is influenced by ocular structural and optical factors, particularly lens status, and should be interpreted with consideration of ocular conditions.Keywords: RAG, Artificial intelligence, Lens status, GlaucomaSubject terms: Biomarkers, Diseases, Medical research

Introduction

Recent advances in artificial intelligence (AI) have enabled accurate prediction of chronological age from retinal fundus photographs1,2. Several studies have demonstrated that the RAG (RAG)—defined as the difference between AI-predicted retinal age and chronological age—is associated with systemic conditions such as cardiovascular disease, diabetes, and mortality risk3–6. These findings suggest that RAG may serve as a potential biomarker of biological aging. However, retinal age estimation is derived from structural features extracted from retinal images and may therefore be influenced not only by systemic aging but also by ocular factors. In particular, age-related ocular structural changes7 and ocular diseases may alter retinal morphology and image characteristics, potentially affecting AI-based retinal age estimation.

Recent studies have suggested that deep learning models can detect ocular structural characteristics from fundus photographs, including features associated with refractive status and axial length. Furthermore, AI interpretation analyses have indicated that retinal vascular patterns, optic disc morphology, and macular structural features may contribute to age prediction from retinal images. These findings suggest that AI-based retinal age estimation may partly reflect ocular structural alterations, including retinal nerve fiber layer thinning, myopic changes, and other age-related retinal abnormalities. Nevertheless, the influence of ocular diseases on RAG has not been sufficiently investigated.

Therefore, in the present study, we evaluated the association between glaucoma severity and RAG to investigate whether glaucoma-related structural changes influence AI-based retinal age estimation.

Aging is associated with various structural changes in the eye, including alterations at the vitreoretinal interface, narrowing of retinal vessels, thickening of vascular walls, and enlargement of the optic nerve head cup8–10. Several studies have also demonstrated age-related changes in retinal microstructure. For example, Trinh et al. reported that the thickness of multiple retinal layers decreases with age, particularly within the inner retinal layers11. Similarly, Wei et al. demonstrated age-related narrowing of retinal vessels, reduced retinal blood flow, and thinning of the inner retina10. Demirkaya et al. also reported progressive thinning of the retinal nerve fiber layer and ganglion cell layer with increasing age12.

Glaucoma is characterized by progressive and irreversible optic neuropathy and is one of the leading causes of blindness worldwide13. Structural changes associated with glaucoma, such as enlargement of the optic nerve head cup and narrowing of retinal vessels, resemble age-related retinal changes8–10. Because these structural alterations may influence retinal age estimation, ocular diseases such as glaucoma may potentially affect RAG values derived from fundus photographs.

Most AI models for retinal age prediction have been developed using datasets derived primarily from non-Japanese populations. The Japan Ocular Imaging Registry (JOIR) is a nationwide ophthalmic database established by the Japanese Ophthalmological Society that collects large-scale ophthalmic clinical data, including retinal images and diagnostic information14. Using an age-prediction algorithm developed from fundus photographs registered in JOIR, the present study investigated ocular factors associated with RAG.

In particular, we focused on the impact of glaucoma and its severity on RAG. Because glaucomatous structural alterations may influence retinal age estimation, RAG may potentially reflect retinal changes associated with glaucomatous neurodegeneration. Therefore, in this study, we evaluated the association between glaucoma, its severity, and RAG derived from fundus photographs.

Subjects and methods

Study design

This was a retrospective observational study conducted at the University of Yamanashi Hospital.

Ethics statement

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the University of Yamanashi School of Medicine (Approval Code: 2635). For this study informed consent has been waived by the Ethics Committee of the University of Yamanashi School of Medicine due to the anonymity and retrospective nature of the study. The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Participants

Adult patients attending the glaucoma clinic (specialized glaucoma outpatient service) in the Department of Ophthalmology at the University of Yamanashi Hospital who underwent 45-degree color fundus photography between February 2010 and April 2025 were included in this study.

In some patients, both eyes were included in the analysis when eligible images were available. Patients with glaucoma were eligible for inclusion. Individuals with mild anterior segment conditions, such as mild cataract or dry eye, were also included. Patients who had undergone cataract surgery at least six months before imaging without operative complications were also eligible.

The following cases were excluded: eyes with fundus diseases other than glaucoma, such as epiretinal membrane, age-related macular degeneration, and diabetic retinopathy, as well as images judged unsuitable for evaluation by two ophthalmologists, including images that were blurred, out of focus, or partially captured.

Control subjects were defined as eyes that were judged to be free of glaucoma based on fundus photographs by two board-certified ophthalmologists and had intraocular pressure within the normal range.

Classification of glaucoma severity

Glaucoma diagnosis was based on optic nerve head changes and corresponding visual field defects. Glaucoma severity was classified according to the Hodapp-Parrish-Anderson classification based on the mean deviation (MD) value obtained from standard automated perimetry (Humphrey Field Analyzer, 24–2 program). Mild glaucoma was defined as an MD better than − 6 dB, moderate glaucoma as an MD between − 6 dB and − 12 dB, and severe glaucoma as an MD worse than − 12 dB. In addition, to address the potential loss of information associated with categorization of a continuous variable, supplementary analyses were performed treating MD as a continuous variable.

Clinical evaluation

For all participants, best-corrected visual acuity, spherical equivalent refractive error, lens status, and detailed fundus examinations were recorded. Systemic conditions including hypertension and diabetes mellitus were also evaluated.

Age prediction algorithm

Detailed information on the age prediction algorithm is available at the following website: http://www.joir.jp/data/en_index.html.

Briefly, retinal fundus images and health examination data were collected from a single health screening facility. The training dataset consisted of 12,734 retinal images from 12,734 individuals (7375 right eyes and 5359 left eyes) who had no retinal abnormalities and were considered “healthy.” Left-eye images were horizontally flipped and used for training as if they were right-eye images.

The criteria for defining healthy participants were as follows: no significant medical history; a cardio-ankle vascular index within the mean value ± 2 standard deviations for each age group; systolic blood pressure < 140 mmHg and diastolic blood pressure < 90 mmHg.

The mean absolute error between the actual chronological age and the age predicted by the AI model was 2.39 years.

Statistical analysis

The primary outcome of this study was the RAG (years). A positive RAG indicated that the AI-predicted retinal age was older than the chronological age, whereas a negative RAG indicated that the AI-predicted retinal age was younger than the chronological age.

Based on clinical relevance and previous studies, the following variables were examined as explanatory factors: presence and severity of glaucoma (classified into control, mild, moderate, and severe according to the mean deviation (MD) value of the Humphrey Field Analyzer), continuous MD value, sex, laterality (right or left eye), lens status (phakic or pseudophakic), HT, DM, logMAR visual acuity, and spherical equivalent refractive error.

Glaucoma severity was classified as control, mild, moderate, or severe according to the mean deviation (MD) value obtained using the Humphrey Field Analyzer. Continuous MD value was also evaluated in additional analyses among eyes with glaucoma. Because RAG was calculated by subtracting chronological age from the AI-predicted retinal age, chronological age was not included as an explanatory variable in the primary regression models. This approach was adopted to avoid mathematical coupling, overadjustment, and potential regression-to-the-mean bias.

Although axial length is generally considered a more direct indicator of myopic ocular changes than refractive error, refractive error was used as a surrogate marker of myopic ocular characteristics because axial length measurements were not consistently available for all eyes in this retrospective study.

Because of the retrospective study design, the presence or absence of systemic diseases such as hypertension and diabetes mellitus was determined based on information available in the electronic medical records, including internal medicine notes, prescription records, and patient questionnaires.

To account for within-subject correlation due to the inclusion of both eyes from some participants, the primary analysis was conducted using a linear mixed-effects model (LMM), with subject ID included as a random effect. The above ocular and clinical variables were included as fixed effects, whereas chronological age was excluded from the primary models for the reasons described above.

All statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.5.1. Mixed-effects models were fitted using the lme4 and lmerTest packages. Estimated marginal means (EMMs) were calculated using the emmeans package, and model diagnostics were performed using the performance package.

Random effects were specified with eye nested within patient ID (1|id/side). The above variables were included as fixed effects. Age was centered when necessary, and an interaction between lens status and age was evaluated. Significance of fixed effects was assessed using Type III sums of squares with degrees of freedom estimated by the Satterthwaite or Kenward–Roger approximation.

For categorical variables, group comparisons were conducted using estimated marginal means (EMMs), with multiple comparisons adjusted using the Benjamini–Hochberg method. The inclusion of interaction terms was evaluated using likelihood ratio tests. Effect sizes were presented as regression coefficients (β) with 95% confidence intervals.

Model assumptions were assessed using diagnostic plots evaluating normality of residuals, homoscedasticity, and influential observations.

As sensitivity analyses, the outcome variable was replaced with absolute error and log(abs_error + 0.5), and similar models were applied. When necessary, the random-effects structure was simplified to (1|id). These analyses were performed to examine the robustness of the effects of lens status and age.

For supplementary descriptive analyses, comparisons between two groups were performed using the t-test or Mann–Whitney U test, and comparisons among multiple groups were conducted using one-way ANOVA or the Kruskal–Wallis test. Correlations were assessed using Pearson or Spearman correlation coefficients. A two-sided p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

ChatGPT (GPT-4/5; OpenAI) were used in a limited capacity to assist with code template generation, interpretation of error messages, and formatting of outputs using packages such as gtsummary, emmeans, and ggeffects. These tools were not used for data collection, interpretation of results, or determination of study conclusions. All code and outputs were verified by the authors, and final decisions were made by the researchers.

Lens status and prediction error

Because lens status showed a strong association with prediction error, additional analyses were performed separately for phakic and IOL eyes. In phakic eyes (n = 194), older age was significantly associated with a smaller prediction error (β = − 0.65, p = 4.6 × 10⁻10).

To examine differences in the age effect between lens groups, age was centered and a lens × age interaction term was introduced into the primary model. The interaction was significant (F = 8.53, p = 0.00377). At the mean age, the difference between groups was phakia − IOL = 12.1 years (95% CI 7.59–16.7, p < 0.0001).

The age-related decrease in predicted age was steeper in IOL eyes than in phakic eyes.

The estimated slope was − 1.121 years per year in IOL eyes (95% CI − 1.469 to − 0.772), compared with − 0.564 years per year in phakic eyes (95% CI − 0.758 to − 0.370). The difference in slopes was − 0.557 years/year (p = 0.0042) (Fig. 3). In addition, logMAR (β = + 12.73, p = 0.0032) and SERE (β = + 0.60, p = 0.030) were also significantly associated with prediction error, whereas hypertension showed a negative association (p = 0.047).

Discussion

Key findings

Previous studies investigating factors associated with the RAG have been limited3,5,15,16. In the present study, lens status emerged as the factor most strongly influencing RAG, with a difference exceeding 14 years between phakic and pseudophakic eyes. Age, best-corrected visual acuity, and spherical equivalent refractive error were also independently associated with RAG. Furthermore, in phakic eyes, glaucoma eyes showed higher RAG values than control eyes; however, RAG was not significantly associated with MD when glaucoma severity was analyzed as a continuous variable. These findings suggest that RAG may reflect ocular structural or optical factors associated with glaucoma rather than the degree of visual field loss itself.

Performance of the retinal age prediction algorithm

The mean prediction error of the present algorithm was approximately − 5.7 ± 19.1 years, which is comparable to or slightly lower than that reported in previous retinal age prediction studies1,15–17. Several factors may explain this difference. First, the algorithm was originally developed using images from individuals without glaucoma, whereas the present study included patients with glaucoma.

In addition, although pseudophakic eyes were not explicitly excluded in the original training dataset, the influence of lens status on retinal age prediction performance was not specifically evaluated. It has been reported that refractive error and axial length may influence fundus parameters7. Therefore, differences in ocular parameters between the training and evaluation datasets may have influenced model performance in the present study.

Second, the deep learning architecture differs from that used in previous studies such as that of Poplin et al.1 Third, racial or ethnic differences in retinal characteristics may influence model performance. In addition, unlike the study by Kamei et al.16, the present analysis did not include fine-tuning of the algorithm for the present dataset. Differences in demographic and clinical characteristics among study populations may also have contributed to the observed variation in prediction accuracy.

Importantly, the algorithm consistently underestimated age in the present cohort, suggesting a systematic bias likely related to differences between the training and evaluation datasets.

Ophthalmic factors influencing RAG

Retinal age prediction models are typically trained using images from systemically healthy individuals, as was the case for the algorithm used in this study. However, several clinical and lifestyle factors—including blood pressure, BMI, HbA1c, and alcohol consumption—have been reported to significantly influence RAG. Therefore, interpretation of RAG values requires adjustment for these factors as well as for age, sex, and ethnicity to reduce bias18–21.

In addition to systemic factors, ocular parameters are also known to influence retinal structure. Previous OCT-based studies have demonstrated that retinal layer thickness is affected by factors such as age, sex, refractive error, and axial length12,22,23. Accordingly, ophthalmic variables should also be considered when interpreting RAG.

In the present study, lens status was the strongest determinant of RAG. Previous reports using the same algorithm did not specifically evaluate lens status15–17. Tanito et al. reported that pseudophakia was associated with RAG, although the magnitude of the effect was smaller than that observed in the present study17. Our results demonstrated a substantial difference between phakic and pseudophakic eyes, with relatively higher prediction accuracy in pseudophakic eyes.

One possible explanation is that lens opacity or age-related changes in lens transparency may alter the optical characteristics of fundus images.

However, additional analyses adjusting for best-corrected visual acuity (logMAR), which may partially reflect media opacity, demonstrated similar associations between glaucoma and RAG in phakic eyes. These findings suggest that the observed associations were not solely explained by reduced image quality associated with lens opacity.

Because deep learning models rely on subtle image features, such optical variations may influence model outputs. These findings suggest that retinal age prediction algorithms may be sensitive to optical factors related to the crystalline lens and image quality. Therefore, lens status should be carefully considered when interpreting RAG in clinical or epidemiological studies.

Although phakic eyes demonstrated significant differences in RAG among glaucoma severity groups, additional analyses treating MD as a continuous variable did not demonstrate significant associations between MD and RAG, even after stratification by lens status. These findings suggest that RAG may not directly reflect the degree of visual field loss itself and may instead capture other biological or structural changes associated with glaucoma.

RAG and glaucoma

The present study demonstrated significantly greater RAG values in phakic glaucoma eyes compared with control eyes. However, consistent severity-dependent associations were not observed across all glaucoma stages. Glaucoma is a leading cause of irreversible blindness worldwide, and a large proportion of affected individuals remain undiagnosed because early disease stages are often asymptomatic24–26. In addition, many patients develop normal-tension glaucoma despite having intraocular pressure within the normal range24.

Current diagnostic indicators for glaucoma include visual field testing, retinal nerve fiber layer thickness measurements, and optic disc evaluation. However, these methods have limitations, including measurement variability and dependence on specialized equipment27–29. In this context, retinal age estimation may partially reflect structural retinal alterations associated with glaucoma and could potentially provide complementary information in disease assessment.

In addition to glaucoma, previous studies have reported associations between RAG and other ocular conditions. For example, diabetic retinopathy progression has been associated with increased RAG3. Komatsu et al. also reported that the presence of drusen influences RAG values16. These findings suggest that RAG may capture structural or vascular changes in the retina associated with various ocular diseases.

Limitations

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, the retinal age prediction algorithm used in this study was trained on fundus images from individuals without ocular diseases, including glaucoma, and was not fine-tuned for the present dataset. This may have affected model performance in glaucoma eyes.

Second, although the total sample size was 283 eyes and within-patient correlations were accounted for using mixed-effects models, statistical power may have been limited in some subgroup analyses. Because both eyes and multiple time points were included, singularity warnings occurred in some random-effects structures, requiring simplification of the statistical model.

In addition, because lens status and glaucoma severity were both associated with the RAG, disentangling their independent effects may have been challenging despite subgroup analyses. Furthermore, normal control eyes did not undergo standard automated perimetry; therefore, correlations between the RAG and mean deviation could only be evaluated within glaucoma eyes.

Because cataract severity was not quantitatively graded using systems such as the Lens Opacities Classification System (LOCS), the influence of media opacity could not be fully evaluated. In phakic eyes, increased RAG values may partially reflect cataract-related image degradation rather than glaucoma-specific retinal changes. Although best-corrected visual acuity was available and may partially reflect media opacity, detailed cataract grading and glaucoma surgical history were not consistently available in this retrospective study.

Third, as this was an observational study, residual confounding cannot be completely excluded.

Another important limitation is the absence of an established gold standard for biological age in the present study. Although the influence of media opacity or lens status on retinal age estimation may be clinically interpretable, it remains difficult to determine whether the association between glaucoma severity and the RAG reflects confounding by ocular structural changes or true acceleration of biological aging. Independent aging biomarkers, such as PhenoAge or other systemic biological age measures, were not available in this study. Therefore, RAG should be interpreted as an output of the fundus image-based prediction model rather than direct evidence of accelerated biological aging. Accordingly, terms implying age overestimation or accelerated aging should be interpreted with caution.

Because this was also a cross-sectional study, generational differences in ocular characteristics may have influenced the results. In particular, the prevalence and severity of myopia have increased substantially in younger generations, especially in Asian populations. Therefore, the retinal age prediction algorithm may have partially captured cohort-related myopic retinal characteristics rather than purely age-related retinal changes. This possibility should be considered when interpreting RAG findings in cross-sectional datasets.

Finally, the retinal age prediction model used in this study was trained primarily on healthy adults with a mean age of approximately 42 years, whereas the present cohort consisted mainly of older glaucoma patients with a mean age of approximately 65 years. This difference in age distribution represents a distributional shift that may have contributed to systematic underestimation of retinal age in older individuals.

In conclusion, the present study demonstrates that RAG is influenced not only by systemic factors but also by ocular factors, particularly lens status. Therefore, ophthalmic variables should be considered when interpreting RAG values. In phakic eyes, glaucoma eyes demonstrated greater RAG values than control eyes, although the influence of glaucoma severity and media opacity could not be fully separated.

Further studies are required to clarify the relationship between RAG and ocular diseases and to explore the potential clinical applications of retinal age estimation.
