Effect of diet mixing strategies on growth performance and carcass characteristics of feedlot cattle
Authors: Naomi L Waldon, Lyda G Garcia, Doug Clevenger, Alejandro E Relling
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractFeeding cattle a total mixed ration (TMR) instead of offering dietary ingredients separately (instead of individually) is generally recommended. Feeding TMR enhances production by reducing feed sorting, decreasing manual labor, and preventing digestive diseases. However, producing TMR requires a specialized mixer that can be expensive and necessitates trained personnel and regular maintenance. Furthermore, some roughages need prior processing before adding to the mixer. Consequently, providing TMR is a methodology hard to adopt by small-sized farm operations. The goal of this study was to evaluate growth performance and carcass characteristics of feedlot cattle offered a TMR or non-mixed ration (NMR; hay and concentrate were provided separately). Angus-Simmental cattle (324.8 ± 29.6 kg) were fed using either a TMR strategy (TMRS; n = 29) or a non-mixed ration strategy (NMRS; concentrate and hay were fed separately; n = 30). Steers were housed individually and fed once a day using individual feed bunks. Irrespective of the treatment, feed offered was subjected to a slicked bunk management. For the NMRS treatment, each bunk was bisected by a wooden plunk bolted to it, for hay and concentrate to be offered in one half each. The ratio hay-to-concentrate (7-to-93) offered was the same between treatment groups. Steers were weighed every 28 d while feed offer and refusals were recorded daily. Post slaughter, yield grade, hot carcass weight, back fat, ribeye area, kidney-pelvic-heart fat, and quality grades (marbling, skeletal maturity, lean maturity) were recorded at the slaughterhouse. Data were analyzed considering the fixed effect of treatment. No differences (P ≥ 0.15) were observed for growth performance or carcass characteristics between treatments. These results likely stem from bunk management practices, allowing adjustments in the amount of feed offered based on the amount remaining in the bunk each day, thus maintaining similar feedstuff ratios across treatments. However, the feed management approaches used in this study might not align with animal group housing, conventionally used in feedlot operations, due to bunk competition and diet selection.

Introduction

Historically, cattle rations consisted of forage, concentrates, and supplements offered individually, which is known as component feeding (Ishler 2023). Hence, this strategy refers to a non-mixed ration where animals are allowed to select from all the dietary ingredients offered. In contrast, McCoy et al (1966) indicated that a better way to ensure that cattle consumed a nutritionally balanced diet with an optimal proportion of feed ingredients was by premixing feedstuffs before feeding, which was defined as “complete ration” or total-mixed ration (TMR). Feeding TMR in large-size farm operations has many advantages. Broadly, it limits sorting behaviors, reduces risks of digestive and metabolic disorders, increases milk and meat production, allows for feeding nonprotein nitrogen sources, increases feed palatability, and reduces manual labor costs (Ishler 2023; Schingoethe 2017). On the other hand, feeding TMR requires mixers and additional equipment as well as special facilities, which increase farm investments, which might be a constraint for small-sized farm operations to adopt a TMR feeding system (Ishler 2023; Schingoethe 2017). Currently in U.S. dairy farms, about 90% of large herds (>500 cows/herd) are fed a TMR, while less than 20% of small herds (30 to 99 cows/herd) are fed a TMR (Schingoethe 2017; USDA NAHMS 2014).

A common bunk management strategy is feeding cattle ad libitum. The principle of this regimen is that cattle are continuously provided with feed in an attempt to maximize dry matter intake (DMI; Schwartzkopf-Genswein et al 2003). Ad libitum feeding, however, can lead to lower average daily gains (ADG) and, therefore, decreased gain-to-feed ratios (G: F) as compared to different restricted feeding regimens (Galyean and Hales 2023; Pritchard and Bruns 2003). Ad libitum feeding is discouraged because it allows feed sorting, DMI fluctuations (Ishler 2023; Schingoethe 2017), and feed is often wasted (Schutz et al 2011). Changes in daily DMI can lead to a lower rumen pH and an increased chance of acidosis (Galyean et al 1992). On the other hand, restricting DMI can reduce digestive disorders by limiting feed overconsumption (Schwartzkopf-Genswein et al 2003); but, also, it might limit animal growth. However, there are other feed bunk managements that can improve animal performance, such as slick bunk management. The slick bunk management has been proposed as an alternative to deal with the drawbacks of either ad libitum or restricted DMI strategies (Pritchard 1993). At present, slick bunk management is widely used in the feedlot industry to facilitate ease of bunk management (Smock et al 2021). Specifically, this strategy involves observing the feed bunk at a set time during the day before feeding, qualitatively assessing refusals, and assigning a score to the bunk to allow for increased, decreased, or maintained feed offerings. The score is typically a scale of 0 to 4 (Pritchard and Bruns 2003), where 0 is no feed remaining in the bunk (as if “licked clean”) and 4 is feed is virtually untouched (Pritchard 1993). Typically, if bunks are scored 0 for 2 consecutive days, feed offerings are increased. If bunk scores are greater than 0.5 for 2 consecutive days, feed offerings are decreased. This feeding strategy showed to improve growth and efficiency in feedlot cattle.

Results from Atwood et al (2001), Moya et al (2011, 2014), and Iraira et al (2015) suggested that growth performance and feed efficiency of ad libitum TMR-fed cattle may be similar to or even better than that of ad libitum component-fed animals. However, we are not aware of the existence of studies that compare TMR versus non-mixed rations feeding strategies under slick bunk management. It is hypothesized that growth performance and carcass characteristics would remain unaffected between finishing steers subjected to TMR or component feeding systems when slick bunk management strategies are imposed due to similar consumption of dietary ingredients. Therefore, our objective was to evaluate different feed mixing practices using slick bunk management.

Materials and methods

Animals and treatments

All animal care, handling, and procedures were approved by the Ohio State University Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (2022A00000078). Fifty-nine Angus-Simmental steers were sourced from two Ohio farms and housed at the Wooster Beef Farm in individual pens (2.6 × 1.5 m), with individual feed bunks in each pen. Upon arrival, steers were weighed and fed a growing diet (60% corn silage and 40% concentrate) fed as TMR for 3 mo. After the growing phase, steers were adapted to a finishing diet (Table 1; NASEM 2016) fed as a TMR. Once transitioned to the final diet, steers were weighed (324.8 ± 29.6 kg; without feed or water withheld) and blocked by body weight (BW) into four blocks. Within each block, animals were randomly assigned to one of two treatment groups: (i) total mixed ration management strategy (TMRS, n = 29), where hay and concentrates (grain plus supplements) were mixed prior to feeding time, or (ii) non-mixed ration management strategy (NMRS, n = 30), where the same hay and concentrates were offered separated to steers. For the NMRS treatment, the grain plus supplements were mixed in a mixer wagon. For the NMRS treatment, each bunk was bisected by a wooden plunk bolted to it, for hay and concentrate to be offered in one half each. Regardless of treatment, slick bunk management practices were used to manage the daily feed offered. Feed bank reading and scoring were done every morning, two hours before feeding time. For the bunk scoring, we used a binary system, where a score of “0” was the same as the 0 previously described (Pritchard and Bruns 2003). A score of “1” indicated traces or larger quantities of feed remained in the bunk during assessment, similar to a 0.5 to 4 score described previously (Pritchard and Bruns 2003). For there to be an assignment of a bunk score of 0 for steers in NMRS, both sides of the bunk needed to be empty. When there were more than traces of feed in either bunk, a “1” score was assigned. Once the feed bunks were scored, if there was feed remaining, the feed bunks were emptied, and the refusal was weighted to estimate DMI. The amount of feed offered depended on the previous two readings. If there was no feed in two consecutive days, the feed offered was increased by 5%, and if there was feed remaining in the bunck for two conscutive days the feed offered was decreased by 5%. The ratio of forage to concentrate fed to steers in both treatments was the same. Feed dry matter content was determined weekly to recalculate the composition of diets that were subsequently fed. Feed samples were collected, dried, and frozen every 2 wk for nutrient analysis. Steers were weighed every 28 d, until d 126 to 154, depending on when each block reached the targeted BW (550 kg). Body weight was collected 1 h before the feeding time, with no withholding of feed or water from the previous day. Body weight was used to capture growth measurements such as initial, monthly, and final BW and ADG, measured as the difference in BW from two consecutive weight days divided by the time interval. Feed offered and refusals were measured daily to determine DMI. Both were used to determine G: F. Diet nutrients analysis were conducted as described previously (Pittaluga et al 2021).

Carcass measurements

Due to capacity at the slaughterhouse facility, steers were slaughtered during a 5-wk period, where 12 steers were slaughtered each week, except in the first week when 11 steers were slaughtered. On each slaughter day, there was an equal representation of animals per treatment, except for the first period. The selection of the order in which the steers were sent to slaughter was done based on the initial blocking body weight of the steers. During each slaughter week, steers were weighed the day before and the day of the slaughter and then transported for 26.29 km to a local slaughterhouse. Beef carcasses were first ribbed between the 12th and 13th ribs, allowing a 20-min exposure to oxygen for the development of a cherry red color. Beef carcasses were evaluated for yield grade (hot carcass weight [HCW], back fat [BF], ribeye area [REA], kidney-pelvic-heart fat [KPH], and quality grade factors [marbling, skeletal maturity, and lean maturity]) 48 h postmortem. Factors were determined by trained personnel utilizing a USDA PYG ruler, ribeye dot grid, and USDA marbling cards as reference devices. Due to the meat processing facility removing the KPH at the time of slaughter, an industry average of 2.5% was used for the yield grade calculation. Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed as a complete block design using PROC MIXED (SAS 9.4). Steer was considered the experimental unit. The model tested for performance and carcass traits considered the fixed effect of treatment and the random effect of block. For final BW, initial BW was added as a covariate. We considered treatment differences when P ≤ 0.05 and marginal differences when there was a P value ≤ 0.1 and >0.05. Data were presented as least square means and the standard error of the mean.

Results and discussion

We did not observe differences (P ≥ 0.15, Table 2) in either growth performance or carcass traits of finishing steers fed a 7%-hay and 93%-concentrate diet under slicked bunk management independent of the feeding strategy used, ie, TMR vs. NMR. These results indicate there is no effect of treatments on steers independent of the feeding strategy, likely because of the feed management techniques used. Previous studies (Atwood et al 2001; Cooke et al 2004; Horcada et al 2016; Lee et al 2021; Moya et al 2011, 2014) compared component-feeding strategy against TMR one, allowing the animals to freely access to most of the separated dietary components. Moya et al (2011, 2014) found that, under an ad libitum regimen, TMR-fed cattle had greater growth rate and gain-to-feed compared with cattle fed under a free-choice strategy. The contrast with results of the present study might be explained by differences in bunk management between studies. Ad libitum access to feedstuffs might have allowed for differences in dietary nutrient intake among treatments in Moya et al (2011, 2014) study, but not in the present one, where feed offer was managed using slick bunk strategy. In this regard, Pittaluga et al (2021) and Lee et al (2021) observed that performance differences between TMRS- and NMRS-finished steers may be related to non-roughage NDF dietary content and dietary energy concentration, respectively. Contrarily to the aforementioned studies (Lee et al 2021; Moya et al 2011, 2014; Pittaluga et al 2021), in the current work, both treatment groups were fed the same diet because of the imposed slick bunk management, which assured similar nutrient intakes between groups.

In conclusion, ensuring that cattle consume the formulated diet, whether through providing a TMR or feeding the hay separated from the concentrate, did not affect growth performance or carcass characteristics under slick bunk management. A similar approach has to be investigated in group-housed animals; competition for feed ingredients among animals would be expected.
