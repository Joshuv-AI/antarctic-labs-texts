Eigenstructure of the linearized electrical impedance tomography problem under radial perturbations
Markus Hirvensalo

Abstract
. We analyze the Fréchet derivative F F , that maps a perturbation in conductivity to the linearized change in boundary measurements governed by the conductivity equation. The domain is taken to be the unit ball B ⊂ ℝ d B\subset\mathbb{R}^{d} with d ≥ 2 d\geq 2 , and we choose perturbations η \eta from the Hilbert space L 2 ​ ( B ) L^{2}(B) . Under the condition that the perturbations are rotationally symmetric, we show that the eigenfunctions of the linear approximation F ​ η F\eta correspond to the spherical harmonics. Furthermore, we establish an explicit formula for the associated eigenvalues and show that for perturbations from any bounded subset, the decay of these eigenvalues is uniform with respect to the degree of the spherical harmonics. The established structure of F ​ η F\eta enables us to show that the Fréchet derivative F F can be approximated by finite-rank operators when restricted to rotationally symmetric perturbations. Both the extension to L 2 ​ ( B ) L^{2}(B) perturbations and the approximability by finite-rank operators are favorable properties for further analysis of F F in numerical algorithms.



1.
Introduction

We study the classical conductivity equation, which describes how an electric potential is induced within a conductive medium by an input current density applied on its surface.
Our analysis is carried out in a simplified setting, where the domain is the Euclidean unit ball
B\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
in
d\geq 2
spatial dimensions, with boundary
{\partial B}
(the unit sphere).

The conductivity of the medium is described by a real-valued
1
1

1

Unless explicitly indicated, the function spaces considered in this work have
\mathbb{C}
as the multiplier field.
coefficient
\gamma\in L^{\infty}(B;\mathbb{R})
, which we assume to be strictly positive in the sense that
\mathop{\textup{ess\,inf}}\gamma>0
.
Given a surface current density

f\in L^{2}_{\diamond}({\partial B}):=\{g\in L^{2}({\partial B})\mid\langle g,1\rangle_{L^{2}({\partial B})}=0\},

the conductivity equation states that the corresponding electric potential
u
satisfies

-\mathrm{div}(\gamma\nabla u)=0\text{ in }B,\qquad\gamma\,\frac{\partial u}{\partial r}=f\text{ on }{\partial B},

(1.1)

where
r
is the radial coordinate.

The problem (
1.1
) admits a unique weak solution
u^{\gamma}_{f}\in H^{1}_{\diamond}(B)
by virtue of the Lax–Milgram lemma.
The Sobolev space for the solutions is defined as

H_{\diamond}^{1}(B):=\{w\in H^{1}(B)\mid w|_{{\partial B}}\in L^{2}_{\diamond}({\partial B})\}.

We may define the Neumann-to-Dirichlet (ND) map:

\Lambda(\gamma):f\to u^{\gamma}_{f}|_{{\partial B}},

which assigns each admissible boundary current
f
to the resulting potential observed on the boundary
{\partial B}
.
The ND map is a compact and self-adjoint operator on
L^{2}_{\diamond}({\partial B})
.

It is well known that the nonlinear forward map
\gamma\mapsto\Lambda(\gamma)
is Fréchet differentiable with respect to a complex-valued perturbations
\eta
.
We denote by
F\eta:=D\Lambda(1;\eta)
the Fréchet derivative of
\Lambda
at
\gamma=1
in the direction of
\eta
.
If
u_{f}
and
u_{g}
are harmonic functions in
B
with
f
and
g
as their Neumann boundary values, respectively, then
F\in\mathscr{L}(L^{\infty}(B),\mathscr{L}(L^{2}_{\diamond}({\partial B})))
is characterized by

\langle(F\eta)f,g\rangle_{L^{2}({\partial B})}=-\int_{B}\eta\nabla u_{f}\cdot\overline{\nabla u_{g}}\,\mathrm{d}x,

(1.2)

for all
\eta\in L^{\infty}(B)
and
f,g\in L^{2}_{\diamond}({\partial B})
.

The operator
F
arises in certain inverse boundary value problems, such as electrical impedance tomography (EIT).
A practical application is the single-step linearization approximation, where we assume
\Lambda(1)
is known or simulated and aim to recover a perturbation
\eta
from information on
\Lambda(1+\eta)
.
This approach has shown practical success (e.g.,
[
1
]
).
For a more comprehensive overview of EIT, we refer the reader to the review articles
[
2
,
3
,
4
]
.

While standard theory guarantees the continuity of
F:L^{\infty}(B)\to\mathscr{L}(L^{2}_{\diamond}({\partial B}))
, it offers little insight into its approximation by finite-rank operators.
According to
[
5
, Appendix A]
, the mapping
F
continuously extends to perturbations on
L^{d}(B)
, but its approximability by finite-rank operators and compactness remain unstudied.
In this work, we show that the Fréchet derivative
F
considered in a stronger topology, more specifically as a mapping
F:L^{2}(B)\to\mathscr{L}(L^{2}_{\diamond}({\partial B}))
is approximable by finite-rank operators, whenever the perturbations
\eta\in L^{2}(B)
are rotationally symmetric.

To the best of our knowledge, the extension to
L^{2}(B)
perturbations has only been shown in two spatial dimension (see the article by Garde and Hyvönen
[
6
]
), here we establish the extension in any spatial dimension
d\geq 2
for any
\eta
in an infinite dimensional subspace of
L^{2}(B)
.
We believe that this is the first result proving that the Fréchet derivative is compact and approximable by finite-rank operators with respect to a topology stronger (or equally strong) than the standard
\mathscr{L}(L^{\infty}(B),\mathscr{L}(L^{2}_{\diamond}({\partial B})))
topology.
The extension to
L^{2}(B)
perturbations and the approximability by finite-rank operators are desired properties from the standpoint of numerical implementations.

This paper is organized as follows.
Section
2
presents the main results, including Theorem
2.1
, which characterizes the eigenstructure of the linear approximation
F\eta
for
\eta
belonging to an infinite-dimensional subspace of
L^{2}(B)
, and Corollary
2.2
, which establishes the compactness of
F
when restricted to the same subspace.
Section
3
introduces two auxiliary results, Lemma
3.1
and Lemma
3.2
, which serve as key tools in the proof of the main theorem.
The proof of Theorem
2.1
is then given in Section
4
.

2.
The main results

The unit ball geometry
B
is highly symmetric, and thus it is natural to adopt polar coordinates.
For any
x\in B\setminus\{0\}
, we write

x=r\theta,\quad\text{where}\quad r=\lVert x\rVert,\quad\theta=\frac{x}{\lVert x\rVert}.

(2.1)

Here,
r\in(0,1)
denotes the radial distance from the origin and
\theta\in{\partial B}
represents the angular component.
Further, we denote the Euclidean norm by
\lVert x\rVert
.

In polar coordinates, the function space
L^{2}(B)
can be decomposed via separation of variables into a weighted radial space
L^{2}_{r^{d-1}}((0,1))
and an angular (surface) space
L^{2}({\partial B})
.
Here,
L^{2}_{r^{d-1}}((0,1))
is the space of square-integrable functions on the interval
(0,1)
with respect to the weight
r^{d-1}
, and
L^{2}({\partial B})
is the standard
L^{2}
space on the unit sphere.

A natural orthonormal basis for the radial space is given by certain Jacobi polynomials denoted as
P_{k}(r)
, where
k
indicates the degree of the polynomial.
For the angular component
L^{2}({\partial B})
, the spherical harmonics
f_{\ell,m}(\theta)
, indexed by degree
\ell
and order
m
, provide an orthonormal basis.
Further details regarding these basis functions are provided in Section
3
.

Before stating the main result of this work, we define the space of
radial perturbations
as

\mathcal{R}:=\left\{\eta\in L^{2}(B)\mid\eta(r,\theta)=\eta(r,\theta^{\prime})\text{ for almost every }r\in(0,1)\text{ and }\theta,\theta^{\prime}\in{\partial B}\right\}.

(2.2)

In essence,
\mathcal{R}
consists of functions in
L^{2}(B)
that are independent of the angular coordinate
\theta
.
That is, for each fixed radius
r\in(0,1)
, the function
\eta(r,\,\cdot\,)
is constant almost everywhere on the sphere.
In what follows, we abuse the notation by denoting with
F
the restriction of
F
to
\mathcal{R}
.

Since any
\eta\in\mathcal{R}
is essentially a function depending only on the variable
r
, it can be expanded solely in the Jacobi polynomials

\eta(r)=\sum^{\infty}_{k=0}a_{k}(\eta)\,P_{k}(r),\quad a_{k}(\eta):=\int^{1}_{0}\eta(r)\,P_{k}(r)\,r^{d-1}\mathrm{d}r.

(2.3)

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
\eta\in\mathcal{R}
be expanded as in (
2.3
) and let
f_{\ell,m}
denote the spherical harmonics on the sphere
{\partial B}
with degree
\ell\geq 1
and order
m
.
The operator
F\eta:L^{2}_{\diamond}({\partial B})\to L^{2}_{\diamond}({\partial B})
expanded in the spherical harmonic basis is diagonal and has an eigenstructure of the form

F\eta=\sum_{\ell,m}\lambda_{\ell}(\eta)\,\langle\,\cdot\,,f_{\ell,m}\rangle_{L^{2}({\partial B})}f_{\ell,m},

(2.4)

where

\lambda_{\ell}(\eta)=\sum^{2\ell-2}_{k=0}(-1)^{k+1}\,a_{k}(\eta)\,\frac{\sqrt{2k+d}}{\ell}\frac{(2\ell-2+d)!\,(2\ell-2)!}{(2\ell-2+d+k)!\,(2\ell-2-k)!}.

The eigenvalues
\lambda_{\ell}(\eta)
are independent of the index
m
and are bounded from above as

\lvert\lambda_{\ell}(\eta)\rvert\leq C_{d}\,\|\eta\|_{L^{2}(B)}\,\ell^{-\frac{1}{2}},

(2.5)

where the constant
C_{d}
depends only on the dimension
d\geq 2
.

Interestingly, Theorem
2.1
establishes that the linear approximation
F\eta
is compact for every
\eta\in\mathcal{R}
, which is not clear based on the compactness of the original ND map since
\mathcal{R}
is not a subspace of
L^{\infty}(B)
.
Moreover, if we consider perturbations
\eta\in\mathcal{R}
with a fixed norm upper bound
\|\eta\|_{L^{2}(B)}\leq\omega
, where
\omega>0
is some fixed constant, then the eigenvalues
\lambda_{\ell}(\eta)
decay uniformly in
\ell
.
This suggests that the Fréchet derivative
F
, viewed as an element of
\mathscr{L}\big(\mathcal{R},\mathscr{L}(L^{2}_{\diamond}({\partial B}))\big)
is compact.

A key ingredient in this argument is a general result from Banach space theory: given Banach spaces
X
and
Y
, a linear and bounded operator
A\in\mathscr{L}(X,Y)
is compact if it can be approximated in the operator norm by a sequence of finite-rank operators
A_{n}
.
This follows from the fact that the space of compact linear operators
\mathscr{K}(X,Y)\subset\mathscr{L}(X,Y)
is closed in the operator topology, and that every finite-rank operator is compact.

These observations yield the following compactness result for the Fréchet derivative.

Corollary 2.2
.

The Fréchet derivative
F\in\mathscr{L}\left(\mathcal{R},\mathscr{L}(L^{2}_{\diamond}({\partial B}))\right)
can be approximated by finite-rank operators in the operator topology.

Proof.

Let
\eta\in\mathcal{R}
and denote

F\eta=\sum^{\infty}_{\ell=1}\lambda_{\ell}(\eta)\Psi_{\ell},\quad\Psi_{\ell}:=\sum_{m\in\mathcal{I_{\ell}}}\langle\,\cdot\,,f_{\ell,m}\rangle_{L^{2}({\partial B})}f_{\ell,m}.

For each
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
, the orthogonal projection operator
\Psi_{\ell}\in\mathscr{L}(L^{2}_{\diamond}({\partial B}))
and the linear functional
\lambda_{\ell}:\mathcal{R}\to\mathbb{C}
are fixed.
Thus, a rank
N\in\mathbb{N}
approximation of
F
is obtained by truncating its eigendecomposition,

F_{N}\eta:=\sum_{\ell=1}^{N}\lambda_{\ell}(\eta)\Psi_{\ell}.

Both
F\eta
and
F_{N}\eta
are diagonal (normal) operators in the considered basis, thereby the singular values of the difference operator are given by the absolute value of the diagonal entries

\sigma^{N}_{\ell}(\eta):=\lvert\langle\left((F-F_{N})\eta\right)f_{\ell,m},f_{\ell,m}\rangle_{L^{2}({\partial B})}\rvert=\begin{cases}\lvert\lambda_{\ell}(\eta)\rvert,&\text{if}\;N<\ell,\\
0,&\text{if}\;N\geq\ell.\end{cases}

For a compact and normal operator
A:X\to X
on a separable Hilbert space
X
, the operator norm can be given

\|A\|_{\rm op}=\max_{i\in I}\,\sigma_{i},

where
\{\sigma_{i}\}_{i\in I}
is the set of singular values for
A
.
We use this result for
A=(F-F_{N})\eta
in the following.
By taking the limit with respect to the truncation index
N\to\infty
, Theorem
2.1
implies

\lim_{N\to\infty}\|F-F_{N}\|_{\mathrm{op}}=\lim_{N\to\infty}\,\max_{\ell,m}\bigg(\sup_{\eta\in\mathcal{R},\;\|\eta\|_{L^{2}(B)}=1}\sigma^{N}_{\ell}(\eta)\bigg)\leq C_{d}\,\lim_{N\to\infty}\max_{\ell>N}\,\ell^{-\frac{1}{2}}=0.

∎

In an earlier study
[
7
, Section 6.1]
, the radial singular functions of truncated
F
were computed numerically.
At the time, these functions were not explicitly interpreted as approximations for singular functions of
F
itself.
However, Corollary
2.2
suggests that repeating the numerical experiment within the framework and assumptions of the present work would yield approximations of the radial singular vectors of
F
.

3.
On the basis elements

In this section, some basic properties of the basis elements
P_{k}(r)
and
f_{\ell,m}(\theta)
are discussed.
In particular, we prove two intermediate results Lemma
3.1
and Lemma
3.2
that are the main tools in the proof of Theorem
2.1
.

3.1.
Spherical harmonics

We start by recalling some facts about spherical harmonics; for additional insights on the topic, consult for example
[
8
, Chapter 5]
and
[
9
]
.

Let
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{d}_{0}
be a
d
-tuple and use a multi-index notion
x^{\alpha}=\prod_{i=1}^{d}x_{i}^{\alpha_{i}}
and
\lvert\alpha\rvert=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\alpha_{i}
.
Then, we may define the space of
harmonic homogeneous
polynomials of degree
\ell\in N_{0}
as

\mathcal{P}_{\ell}=\Bigl\{p:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{C}\;\Big|\;p(x)=\sum_{|\alpha|=\ell}c_{\alpha}x^{\alpha},\;c_{\alpha}\in\mathbb{C},\;\Delta p=0\Bigr\}.

The space of spherical harmonics of degree
\ell
is defined as the restriction of the harmonic homogeneous polynomials of degree
\ell
onto the unit sphere:

\mathcal{H}_{\ell}=\{p|_{{\partial B}}\mid p\in\mathcal{P}_{\ell}\}.

We have

\dim\mathcal{H}_{\ell}=\dim\mathcal{P}_{\ell}=\binom{\ell+d-1}{d-1}-\binom{\ell+d-3}{d-1},

(3.1)

with the convention
\binom{m}{k}=0
for nonnegative integers
m<k
.

The
\mathcal{H}_{\ell}
-spaces are the eigenspaces for the Laplace–Beltrami operator
\Delta_{{\partial B}}
with

\Delta_{{\partial B}}f=-\ell(\ell+d-2)f,\quad f\in\mathcal{H}_{\ell}.

(3.2)

Hence, there are the following orthogonal decompositions:

L^{2}({\partial B})=\bigoplus_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\mathcal{H}_{\ell}\quad\text{and}\quad L^{2}_{\diamond}({\partial B})=\bigoplus_{\ell=1}^{\infty}\mathcal{H}_{\ell}.

Let
\{f_{\ell,m}\}_{m\in\mathcal{I}_{\ell}}
be an orthonormal basis for
\mathcal{H}_{\ell}
; in particular, the index set
\mathcal{I}_{\ell}
has the cardinality
\dim\mathcal{H}_{\ell}
.
Thereby,
\{f_{\ell,m}\}_{\ell\in\mathbb{N}_{0},m\in\mathcal{I_{\ell}}}
is an orthonormal basis for
L^{2}({\partial B})
and similarly
\{f_{\ell,m}\}_{\ell\in\mathbb{N},m\in\mathcal{I_{\ell}}}
is an orthonormal basis for
L^{2}_{\diamond}({\partial B})
.
Further, we can show that the orthogonality of the spherical harmonics extends to their gradients, which can be deduced from the following Lemma.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
f_{\ell}\in\mathcal{H}_{\ell}
and
f_{\ell^{\prime}}\in\mathcal{H}_{\ell^{\prime}}
, with arbitrary degrees
\ell,\ell^{\prime}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, then

\int_{{\partial B}}\nabla_{{\partial B}}f_{\ell}\cdot\overline{\nabla_{{\partial B}}f_{\ell^{\prime}}}\,\mathrm{d}S=\ell(\ell+d-2)\int_{{\partial B}}f_{\ell}\,\overline{f_{\ell^{\prime}}}\,\mathrm{d}S.

Proof.

In the following, let
\nabla
denote the gradient in
B
and
\nabla_{\partial B}
denote the surface gradient.
Let us artificially extend
f_{\ell}
and
f_{\ell^{\prime}}
to
B\setminus\{0\}
as constant in the radial dimension.
In polar coordinates we have

\big(\nabla f_{\ell}\cdot\overline{\nabla f_{\ell^{\prime}}}\big)(r,\theta)=r^{-2}\big(\nabla_{\partial B}f_{\ell}\cdot\overline{\nabla_{\partial B}f_{\ell^{\prime}}}\big)(1,\theta),

(3.3)

and from (
3.2
) it follows that

\big(\Delta f_{\ell}\big)(r,\theta)=r^{-2}\big(\Delta_{\partial B}f_{\ell}\big)(1,\theta)=r^{-2}\ell(\ell+d-2)f_{\ell}(\theta).

(3.4)

Define
B_{\varepsilon}
to be an origin centered ball with radius
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
and consider the integral

\int_{B\setminus B_{\varepsilon}}\nabla f_{\ell}\cdot\overline{\nabla f_{\ell^{\prime}}}\,\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\partial B}\overline{f_{\ell^{\prime}}}\,\frac{\partial f_{\ell}}{\partial r}\,\mathrm{d}S-\int_{{\partial B}_{\varepsilon}}\overline{f_{\ell^{\prime}}}\,\frac{\partial f_{\ell}}{\partial r}\,\mathrm{d}S-\int_{B\setminus B_{\varepsilon}}\overline{f_{\ell^{\prime}}}\,\Delta f_{\ell}\,\mathrm{d}x.

For a radially constant function
f_{\ell}
, the normal derivatives vanish and thus the surface integrals are zero.
From (
3.3
) and (
3.4
) we see that the radial contributions cancel from both sides and the claim follows.
∎

3.2.
Jacobi polynomials

We define the following
shifted
and
normalized
Jacobi polynomials

P_{k}(r)=\sqrt{2k+d}\,\sum^{k}_{q=0}(-1)^{q}\binom{k}{q}\binom{k+q+d-1}{k}\,r^{q},\quad k\in\mathbb{N}_{0},\quad r\in(0,1).

(3.5)

The definition of (
3.5
) is in accordance with
[
10
, §18.5(iii) Eq. 18.5.7]
, after using a change of variables
x\mapsto 1-2r
, fixing the parameters
\alpha=d-1
and
\beta=0
, and normalizing with respect to the
L^{2}_{r^{d-1}((0,1))}
inner product.
With these modification, we have orthonormality with respect to the order index
k
,

\langle P_{k}(r),P_{k^{\prime}}(r)\rangle_{L^{2}_{r^{d-1}}((0,1))}=\int^{1}_{0}P_{k}(r)\,P_{k^{\prime}}(r)\,r^{d-1}\mathrm{d}r=\delta_{k,k^{\prime}}.

(3.6)

We denote the Kronecker delta by
\delta_{k,k^{\prime}}
in equation (
3.6
).
Correspondingly, the recurrence identity
[
10
, §18.9(i) Eq. 18.9.2_1]
takes the form

rP_{k}(r)=-\sqrt{\frac{2k+d}{2k+d-2}}\frac{k(k+d-1)}{(2k+d-1)(2k+d)}P_{k-1}(r)\\
+\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{(d-1)^{2}}{(2k+d-1)(2k+d+1)}+1\right)P_{k}(r)-\sqrt{\frac{2k+d}{2k+d+2}}\frac{(k+1)(k+d)}{(2k+d)(2k+d+1)}P_{k+1}(r),

(3.7)

with the convention
P_{-1}(r)=0
.

From the orthonormality condition (
3.6
), we see that the set of polynomials (
3.5
) are linearly independent in
L^{2}_{r^{d-1}}((0,1))
.
We can use the Weierstrass approximation theorem to show that monomials of the type
r^{k}
with
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
are dense in
L^{2}_{r^{d-1}}((0,1))
.
Density of (
3.5
) in
L^{2}_{r^{d-1}}((0,1))
is inherited from these monomials, since for a fixed
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, we have

\mathop{\textup{span}}\{r^{k}\mid 0\leq k\leq n\}=\mathop{\textup{span}}\{P_{k}(r)\mid 0\leq k\leq n\},

which can be directly observed from the following lemma.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, then

r^{k}=\sum^{k}_{q=0}\chi_{k,q}\,P_{q}(r),\quad\text{where}\quad\chi_{k,q}=(-1)^{q}\,\frac{\sqrt{2q+d}\,(k+d-1)!\,k!}{(k+d+q)!\,(k-q)!}.

(3.8)

Proof.

We may use induction to prove the result.
The base case
k=0
, is easily verified

r^{0}=\sum^{0}_{q=0}\chi_{0,0}\,P_{0}(r)=1.

We initialize the induction step by assuming that (
3.8
) holds for some fixed
k\geq 0
.
Further, let us write the recurrence identity (
3.7
) in a condensed form

rP_{k}(r)=A_{k}P_{k-1}(r)+B_{k}P_{k}(r)+C_{k}P_{k+1}(r),

where the labels
A_{k},B_{k}\text{ and }C_{k}
correspond to the three coefficients in (
3.7
), respectively.
Finally, a direct calculation reveals that

\displaystyle r^{k+1}

\displaystyle=r\,\Big(\sum^{k}_{q=0}\chi_{k,q}\,P_{q}(r)\Big)

\displaystyle=(\chi_{k,1}A_{1}+\chi_{k,0}B_{0})P_{0}(r)+\sum^{k-1}_{q=1}(\chi_{k,q+1}A_{q+1}+\chi_{k,q}B_{q}+\chi_{k,q-1}C_{q-1})P_{q}(r)

\displaystyle\quad+(\chi_{k,k}B_{k}+\chi_{k,k-1}C_{k-1})P_{k}(r)+\chi_{k,k}C_{k}P_{k+1}(r)

\displaystyle=\chi_{k+1,0}\,P_{0}(r)+\sum^{k-1}_{q=1}\chi_{k+1,q}\,P_{q}(r)+\chi_{k+1,k}\,P_{k}(r)+\chi_{k+1,k+1}\,P_{k+1}(r).

After collecting the terms into a single sum, the proof is completed via a straightforward induction argument.
∎

4.
Proof of Theorem
2.1

We begin the proof by assuming that
\eta\in\mathcal{R}
is fixed and define the following series expansion

\eta=\sum^{\infty}_{k=0}a_{k}(\eta)\,P_{k},\quad a_{k}(\eta):=\int^{1}_{0}\eta(r)\,P_{k}(r)\,r^{d-1}\mathrm{d}r,

(4.1)

where
P_{k}
are the Jacobi polynomials given by (
3.5
).

Consider the problem (
1.1
) with
\gamma=1
, and choose a spherical harmonic function
f_{\ell,m}\in\mathcal{H}_{\ell}
as the Neumann boundary value.
Then, the corresponding harmonic solution is

u_{\ell,m}(r,\theta)=\ell^{-1}r^{\ell}f_{\ell,m}(\theta),\text{ whenever }\ell>0.

(4.2)

We focus on using the basis elements of the zero-mean space
L^{2}_{\diamond}({\partial B})
, and therefore assume
\ell\geq 1
.

Inspired by the characterization in (
1.2
), we proceed to calculate

\langle(F\eta)f_{\ell,m},f_{\ell^{\prime},m^{\prime}}\rangle_{L^{2}({\partial B})}=-\int_{B}\eta\nabla u_{\ell,m}\cdot\overline{\nabla u_{\ell^{\prime},m^{\prime}}}\,\mathrm{d}x,

(4.3)

where

\big(\nabla u_{\ell,m}\cdot\overline{\nabla u_{\ell^{\prime},m^{\prime}}}\big)(r,\theta)=r^{\ell+\ell^{\prime}-2}\big(f_{\ell,m}(\theta)\overline{f_{\ell^{\prime},m^{\prime}}(\theta)}+(\ell\,\ell^{\prime})^{-1}\big(\nabla_{{\partial B}}f_{\ell,m}\cdot\overline{\nabla_{{\partial B}}f_{\ell^{\prime},m^{\prime}}}\big)(1,\theta)\big).

(4.4)

Combining Lemma
3.1
with (
4.3
) yields

\langle(F\eta)f_{\ell,m},f_{\ell^{\prime},m^{\prime}}\rangle_{L^{2}({\partial B})}=-\frac{2\ell+d-2}{\ell}\left(\int^{1}_{0}\eta(r)\,r^{2\ell-2}\,r^{d-1}\,\mathrm{d}r\right)\delta_{\ell,\ell^{\prime}}\delta_{m,m^{\prime}}.

(4.5)

We observe that, in order for the inner product to be potentially non-zero, the indices of the spherical harmonics must coincide.

Expanding
\eta
as in (
4.1
), applying Lemma
3.2
to rewrite the monomial
r^{2\ell-2}
in terms of Jacobi polynomials, and then using their orthonormality, we obtain

\displaystyle\langle(F\eta)f_{\ell,m},f_{\ell,m}\rangle_{L^{2}({\partial B})}

\displaystyle=-\frac{2\ell+d-2}{\ell}\sum^{2\ell-2}_{k=0}a_{k}(\eta)\,\chi_{2\ell-2,k}

\displaystyle=\sum^{2\ell-2}_{k=0}(-1)^{k+1}a_{k}(\eta)\frac{\sqrt{2k+d}}{\ell}\frac{(2\ell-2+d)!\,(2\ell-2)!}{(2\ell-2+d+k)!\,(2\ell-2-k)!}

\displaystyle=\lambda_{\ell}(\eta).

It is already clear that the operator
F\eta
(defined by (
4.5
) for
\eta\in\mathcal{R}
) expressed in the spherical harmonic basis acts as a diagonal operator

(F\eta):f_{\ell,m}\mapsto\lambda_{\ell}(\eta)\,f_{\ell,m}.

(4.6)

However, it remains unclear whether the operator
F\eta
is an element of
\mathscr{L}(L^{2}_{\diamond}({\partial B}))
.
To address this, we proceed by analyzing the behavior of the eigenvalues
\lambda_{\ell}(\eta)
.

Let us keep
d\geq 2
fixed, then we may use the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality to show that

\displaystyle\lvert\lambda_{\ell}(\eta)\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\sum^{2\ell-2}_{k=0}\lvert a_{k}(\eta)\rvert^{2}}\,\sqrt{\sum^{2\ell-2}_{k=0}\frac{2k+d}{\ell^{2}}\left(\frac{(2\ell-2+d)!\,(2\ell-2)!}{(2\ell-2+d+k)!\,(2\ell-2-k)!}\right)^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\lvert{\partial B}\rvert^{-\frac{1}{2}}\|\eta\|_{L^{2}(B)}\,\sqrt{\sum^{2\ell-2}_{k=0}\frac{2k+d}{\ell^{2}}\frac{(2\ell-2+d)!\,(2\ell-2)!}{(2\ell-2+d+k)!\,(2\ell-2-k)!}}.

In the above, we denote the surface area of the unit sphere with
\lvert{\partial B}\rvert
. Also, the fraction of factorials is at most
1
, and in order to simplify the analysis we omit squaring it.

Next, we show that the sum

\sum^{2\ell-2}_{k=0}\frac{2k+d}{\ell^{2}}\frac{(2\ell-2+d)!\,(2\ell-2)!}{(2\ell-2+d+k)!\,(2\ell-2-k)!}

(4.7)

behaves as
\mathcal{O}(\ell^{-1})
when
\ell\to\infty
.
Since we are unable to derive a closed-form expression for (
4.7
), and its convergence to zero is not immediately evident in its current form, we resort to an integral test to study the behavior as a function of
\ell
.
Before proceeding to the integral test, let us construct an upper bound for the fraction of factorials in (
4.7
), which is undoubtedly the most tedious part of the summand.
To achieve this, we employ a technique developed in an earlier work
[
11
]
.

Let us denote
L=2\ell-1
and define a dummy function

\rho(x):=\frac{\Gamma(L+d)\,\Gamma(L)}{\Gamma(L+d+x)\,\Gamma(L-x)},\quad 0\leq x\leq L-1,

(4.8)

which is the continuous version of the fraction of factorials in (
4.7
) with the index of summation
k
replaced by a continuous variable
x
.
Also, we adopt the usual definition of the gamma function, which means that
\Gamma(n)=(n-1)!
.

We may differentiate (
4.8
) with respect to the variable
x
.
After using the product rule and the identity

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}z}\frac{1}{\Gamma(z)}=-\frac{\psi(z)}{\Gamma(z)},

where
\psi(z)
is the digamma function, we get

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x}\rho(x)=\rho(x)\big(\psi(L-x)-\psi(L+d+x)\big).

(4.9)

The digamma function is strictly monotonically increasing on the positive real axis, and thus the derivative (
4.9
) is negative for all
0\leq x\leq L-1
.

Our strategy is as follows:
we replace the difference of digamma functions in (
4.9
) with a less negative expression, and then resort to Grönwall’s inequality in order to construct an upper bound for
\rho
.
The hope is that the constructed upper bound simplifies the analysis of (
4.7
).

The derivative of the digamma function is the trigamma function
\psi_{1}(z)
which is a strictly convex and strictly decreasing function on the positive real axis.
According to
[
12
, Lemma 1]
, we have a lower bound

\psi_{1}(z)\geq z^{-1},\qquad z>0.

(4.10)

We may employ the fundamental theorem of calculus, Jensen’s inequality and (
4.10
) to get an estimate for the difference of digamma functions in (
4.9
):

\displaystyle-\left(\psi(L+d+x)-\psi(L-x)\right)

\displaystyle=-(2x+d)\bigg(\frac{1}{2x+d}\int^{L+d+x}_{L-x}\psi_{1}(z)\,\mathrm{d}z\bigg)

\displaystyle\leq-(2x+d)\,\psi_{1}\bigg(\frac{1}{2x+d}\int^{L+d+x}_{L-x}z\,\mathrm{d}z\bigg)

\displaystyle\leq-\frac{2x+d}{L+\frac{d}{2}}.

Since
\rho(x)>0
for all
0\leq x\leq L-1
, we have

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x}\rho(x)\leq\rho(x)\bigg(-\frac{2x+d}{L+\frac{d}{2}}\bigg),

and from the differential form of Grönwall’s inequality, a simple upper bound is acquired,

\rho(x)\leq\rho(0)\exp\bigg(-\int_{0}^{x}\frac{2z+d}{L+\frac{d}{2}}\mathrm{d}z\bigg)={\rm e}^{-\frac{2x(x+d)}{2L+d}},\qquad 0\leq x\leq L-1.

(4.11)

Collecting the various estimates together and returning to the discrete setting, we obtain

\lvert\lambda_{\ell}(\eta)\rvert\leq\lvert{\partial B}\rvert^{-\frac{1}{2}}\|\eta\|_{L^{2}(B)}\,\sqrt{\sum^{2\ell-2}_{k=0}\frac{2k+d}{\ell^{2}}\,\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{2k(k+d)}{4\ell-2+d}}}.

(4.12)

The summand in (
4.12
) is increasing as a function of
k
on the interval
(0,k^{*})
and decreasing on the interval
(k^{*},2\ell-2)
with respect to a unique critical point

k^{*}=\tfrac{1}{2}\big(\sqrt{4\ell-2+d}-d\big).

Therefore, in the integral test the maximal value needs to be taken into account, and we may estimate

\displaystyle\sum^{2\ell-2}_{k=0}\frac{2k+d}{\ell^{2}}\frac{(2\ell-2+d)!\,(2\ell-2)!}{(2\ell-2+d+k)!\,(2\ell-2-k)!}

\displaystyle\leq\sum^{2\ell-2}_{k=0}\frac{2k+d}{\ell^{2}}\,\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{2k(k+d)}{4\ell-2+d}}

\displaystyle\leq\int^{2\ell-2}_{0}\frac{2x+d}{\ell^{2}}\,\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{2x(x+d)}{4\ell-2+d}}\,\mathrm{d}x+\frac{2k^{*}+d}{\ell^{2}}\,\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{2k^{*}(k^{*}+d)}{4\ell-2+d}}

\displaystyle\leq\int^{2\ell-2}_{0}\frac{2x+d}{\ell^{2}}\,\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{2x(x+d)}{4\ell-2+d}}\,\mathrm{d}x+\frac{\sqrt{4\ell-2+d}}{\ell^{2}}\,\mathrm{e}^{d}.

For the integral, we first perform a change of variables
x\mapsto\sqrt{\ell}\,z
, then expand the domain of integration, and finally eliminate the
\ell
dependence from the fraction and the exponent:

\displaystyle\int^{2\ell-2}_{0}\frac{2x+d}{\ell^{2}}\,\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{2x(x+d)}{4\ell-2+d}}\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\int^{2\sqrt{\ell}-\frac{2}{\sqrt{\ell}}}_{0}\frac{2\sqrt{\ell}z+d}{\ell^{\frac{3}{2}}}\,\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{2\sqrt{\ell}z(\sqrt{\ell}z+d)}{4\ell-2+d}}\,\mathrm{d}z

\displaystyle\leq\int^{\infty}_{0}\frac{2\sqrt{\ell}z+d}{\ell^{\frac{3}{2}}}\,\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{2\ell z^{2}}{4\ell-2+d}}\,\mathrm{d}z

\displaystyle\leq\ell^{-1}\int^{\infty}_{0}(2z+d)\,\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{2z^{2}}{d+4}}\,\mathrm{d}z=\ell^{-1}\,\left(\frac{d(\sqrt{2\pi}\sqrt{d+4}+2)}{4}+2\right).

Combining the estimate for the maximal value and for the integral leads to the following conservative upper bound

\displaystyle\sum^{2\ell-2}_{k=0}\frac{2k+d}{\ell^{2}}\frac{(2\ell-2+d)!\,(2\ell-2)!}{(2\ell-2+d+k)!\,(2\ell-2-k)!}

\displaystyle\leq\ell^{-1}\left(\frac{d(\sqrt{2\pi}\sqrt{d+4}+2)}{4}+2\right)+\frac{\sqrt{4\ell-2+d}}{\ell^{2}}\,\mathrm{e}^{d}

\displaystyle\leq 4\,d^{2}\,\mathrm{e}^{d}\ell^{-1}.

Finally, we may substitute this estimate into (
4.12
) and apply the well-known formula

\lvert{\partial B}\rvert=\frac{2\pi^{\frac{d}{2}}}{\Gamma\big(\frac{d}{2}\big)}

to obtain the desired bound for the eigenvalues,

\lvert\lambda_{\ell}(\eta)\rvert\leq C_{d}\,\|\eta\|_{L^{2}(B)}\,\ell^{-\frac{1}{2}},\quad C_{d}=d\,\big(\tfrac{\mathrm{e}^{2}}{\pi}\big)^{\frac{d}{4}}\,\sqrt{2\,\Gamma\big(\tfrac{d}{2}\big)}.

(4.13)

Although the constant
C_{d}
may be suboptimal, we prefer it for its simplicity.

The final step in this proof is to formulate the eigenvalue decomposition of
F\eta
as an operator in
\mathscr{L}(L^{2}_{\diamond}({\partial B}))
.
To be quite precise, we have only established that the action of
F\eta
on individual spherical harmonics
f_{\ell,m}
is well-defined.

Let
g\in L^{2}_{\diamond}({\partial B})
be arbitrary and let us consider a truncated version of it,

g_{N}:=\sum^{N}_{\ell=1}\sum_{m\in\mathcal{I_{\ell}}}c_{\ell,m}f_{\ell,m},\quad c_{\ell,m}=\langle g,f_{\ell,m}\rangle_{L^{2}({\partial B})}.

Here, the cardinality of the index set
\mathcal{I_{\ell}}
is given by (
3.1
).

The mapping
(F\eta)g_{N}
is still well-defined as it is a
finite
linear combination of elements
(F\eta)f_{\ell,m}
.
Also, we have
(F\eta)g_{N}\in L^{2}_{\diamond}({\partial B})
, and thus we may expand it in the spherical harmonic basis, which results in

(F\eta)g_{N}=\sum_{\ell^{\prime},m^{\prime}}\Big\langle(F\eta)\Big(\sum^{N}_{\ell=1}\sum_{m\in\mathcal{I_{\ell}}}c_{\ell,m}f_{\ell,m}\Big),f_{\ell^{\prime},m^{\prime}}\Big\rangle_{L^{2}({\partial B})}f_{\ell^{\prime},m^{\prime}}.

(4.14)

From the orthogonality revealed by (
4.5
), we see that the inner product in (
4.14
) is potentially non-zero only when the indices of the basis functions match.
Therefore,

(F\eta)g_{N}=\sum^{N}_{\ell=1}\sum_{m\in\mathcal{I_{\ell}}}\langle(F\eta)c_{\ell,m}f_{\ell,m},f_{\ell,m}\rangle_{L^{2}({\partial B})}f_{\ell,m}=\sum^{N}_{\ell=1}\sum_{m\in\mathcal{I_{\ell}}}\lambda_{\ell}(\eta)\langle g,f_{\ell,m}\rangle_{L^{2}({\partial B})}f_{\ell,m}.

(4.15)

As the truncation index
N\to\infty
, the right-hand side of (
4.15
) converges in
L^{2}_{\diamond}({\partial B})
, due to the fact that the eigenvalues
\lambda_{\ell}(\eta)
accelerate the convergence of a sum that is already convergent.
Hence, the continuous extension of
F\eta
is given by the limit

(F\eta)g=\sum_{\ell,m}\lambda_{\ell}(\eta)\langle g,f_{\ell,m}\rangle_{L^{2}({\partial B})}f_{\ell,m},\quad g\in L^{2}_{\diamond}({\partial B}).

The proof is now complete.
∎
