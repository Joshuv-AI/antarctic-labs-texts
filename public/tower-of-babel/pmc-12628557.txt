Contributions of risk factors, additional yield and number needed to screen to prevalence of diabetes in tuberculosis-exposed households
Authors: Wan Qin, Zhi Zhang, Hao Chen, Yinxing Liu, Virasakdi Chongsuvivatwong, Min Guo, Shengqiong Guo, Tao Long, Weizheng Ou
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
IntroductionDiabetes mellitus (DM) and tuberculosis (TB) frequently coexist, with 25–33% of TB patients having DM, underscoring the need for integrated screening. Evaluating additional yield (newly detected cases) and number needed to screen (NNS) can optimize early diagnosis, but risk-specific contributions remain unclear. This study assesses these metrics through household contact tracing to identify high-risk subgroups and guide targeted DM/TB control strategies.ObjectiveThe precise contribution of different risk factors and additional yield of subgroups linked to DM and the NNS to identify a new case in TB case households remain elusive. We aimed to document the contribution magnitudes of potential risk factors, additional yields of subgroups, and NNS for detecting a DM case in TB index cases and their household contacts.MethodsThis cross-sectional study enrolled 801 confirmed TB patients and 972 household contacts from 11 randomly selected counties in Guizhou, China, between September 2019 and August 2020. Participants underwent face-to-face interviews assessing sociodemographic, behavioral, and clinical factors, followed by screenings for hypertension, diabetes, and other non-communicable diseases. The quantitative variables, such as age and monthly income, are segmented from continuous exposure variables into new categorical variables, which have important implications for future analysis. A Student’s t-test or variance analysis test was applied to compare groups as appropriately summarized using the mean and standard deviation. The 95% confidence interval (95%CI) was used for every relevant factor contributing to screening for DM where appropriate. The Odd Ratios were the values of the positive subgroup divided by the negative subgroup of the related factors contributing to the DM and non-DM cases (DM vs. non-DM) among the TB patients and their household contacts.ResultsAmong 801 TB index cases and 972 household contacts, DM detection rates were 8.7% and 3.2%, respectively, with additional yields of 20.0% (NNS = 53) and 25.8% (NNS = 119). Multivariate analysis identified advanced age, abnormal BMI, DM family history, and comorbidities (e.g., hypertension) as significant risk factors (P < 0.05). Subgroup analyses revealed higher yields and lower NNS in older individuals (OR = 2.1, 95%CI:1.4–3.2), those with DM family history (OR = 1.8, 95% CI:1.2–2.7), and TB patients with sputum-smear positivity (OR = 1.5, 95% CI:1.1–2.0).ConclusionIntegrated DM screening in TB-affected households is clinically valuable, with higher additional yields among TB index cases than contacts. Critical risk factors, such as age, BMI, DM family history, and comorbidities like hypertension, significantly influenced detection rates and screening efficiency.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-24628-6.

Introduction

The three-level prevention strategy-encompassing primary, secondary, and tertiary interventions-has been a cornerstone in the control of chronic and infectious diseases, including diabetes mellitus (DM) and tuberculosis (TB) [1, 2]. These two conditions exhibit a complex bidirectional relationship, with shared risk factors such as poor nutrition, immunosuppression, and socioeconomic disparities. Epidemiological studies suggest that DM not only increases the risk of TB but also complicates its treatment, with approximately 25–33% of TB patients concurrently diagnosed with DM [3, 4]. Given this overlap, integrated prevention strategies targeting common modifiable risk factors could yield dual benefits, reducing the incidence and severity of both diseases. Primary prevention, particularly through early risk factor modification, remains the most cost-effective approach and has gained widespread consensus among public health experts.

Delayed diagnosis of DM is a critical public health challenge, often leading to irreversible complications such as neuropathy, retinopathy, and cardiovascular disease. Similarly, undetected TB cases contribute to ongoing transmission and poorer treatment outcomes. Reciprocal screening for DM and TB-along with their associated determinants-could mitigate these adverse effects, offering substantial societal and economic returns. Both diseases impose a heavy burden on healthcare systems, particularly in low- and middle-income countries, where resource constraints exacerbate diagnostic and management challenges [5]. Implementing systematic screening programs in high-risk populations could thus serve as a pragmatic strategy for early detection and intervention.

A key metric in evaluating screening efficacy is the additional yield, defined as the proportion of newly detected cases relative to the total patient population (including both previously diagnosed and newly identified individuals). This differs from percent yield, which specifically measures previously undiagnosed cases detected through screening efforts [6]. A higher additional yield implies greater efficiency in case-finding, reducing the number needed to screen (NNS)-the average number of individuals who must be screened to identify one new case. NNS is mathematically derived as the inverse of undiagnosed disease prevalence within a screened population at a given time [7]. A low NNS indicates that screening is highly efficient, justifying its implementation in targeted groups such as TB patients and their close contacts.

Understanding the contributions of different risk factors to additional yield and NNS is essential for evidence-based policymaking. Health authorities require such data to prioritize high-risk populations, allocate limited resources effectively, and determine the feasibility of large-scale screening programs [8]. For instance, should screening focus on elderly TB patients, those with poor glycemic control, or household contacts with prolonged exposure? Despite the recognized importance of these metrics, empirical data on their magnitude and variability across subgroups remain scarce, hindering the development of optimized screening protocols.

Comparative studies assessing DM risk between TB patients and the general population face methodological challenges, including selection bias and confounding by healthcare access. However, household contact tracing-a well-established component of TB control-provides a unique opportunity to estimate additional yield and NNS in a structured manner. TB index patients and their household contacts represent a population with shared environmental and possibly genetic risk factors, offering insights that may generalize to broader communities. By identifying pivotal risk factors and tailoring interventions accordingly, policymakers can enhance the cost-effectiveness of integrated DM and TB control programs.

To address these knowledge gaps, we conducted a large-scale, household contact tracing-based study with two primary objectives: estimate additional yield and NNS for DM screening among TB patients and their household contacts, and exploratory analysis of risk factor contributions-examining whether factors with significant odds ratios (ORs) correlate with higher additional yields and lower NNS, thereby guiding future screening prioritization. Our findings aim to inform public health strategies, ensuring that limited resources are directed toward populations where screening will have the greatest impact.

Materials and methods

Study design

We conducted a population-based cross-sectional study in Guizhou Province, China (September 2019-August 2020). Using a multistage sampling approach, we randomly selected 11 of 88 counties/districts, stratified by TB incidence rates and urbanization level to ensure population representativeness. All participants underwent: (1) standardized face-to-face interviews collecting sociodemographic characteristics, health behaviors, and clinical history using a validated questionnaire. and (2) comprehensive non-communicable diseases (NCDs) screening including blood pressure measurements (hypertension: systolic ≥ 140 mmHg or diastolic ≥ 90 mmHg on two readings) and diabetes testing (fasting plasma glucose ≥ 7.0 mmol/L or random plasma glucose ≥ 11.1 mmol/L). Quality assurance measures included interviewer training, instrument calibration, and duplicate data entry.

The formula resulted in sample size is 1518. With the consideration of 10% of non-response, ultimately, 1669 people were recruited for DM screening. Household contacts were determined via power analysis (α = 0.05, β = 0.2) to detect ≥ 15% differences in DM prevalence vs. index cases. Generalizability applies primarily to TB-DM comorbidity in high-burden regions (index cases) and secondarily to NCD transmission risks in TB-affected households contacts, supported by probability-proportional-to-size sampling (PPS) across urban/rural strata and demographic matching with provincial health data.

At the same time, we followed China contact investigation guidelines anti-TB by systematically recruiting 1–3 members for whom the inspecting expenditure covered by free policy per index case (mean = 1.21), prioritizing: (1) cohabiting spouses (mandatory inclusion), (2) relat.ives sharing living spaces ≥ 5 years (52.1%), and (3) daily contacts ≥ 4 h (17.2%), excluding pregnant women and minors < 15 years per ethical protocols.

Finally, the study enrolled 801 microbiologically or clinically confirmed TB patients (via sputum smear microscopy, chest X-ray, or clinical symptoms) from the NTEPs and their 972 household contacts, meeting the minimum sample requirement (n = 720) calculated using the provincial TB incidence (78/100,000), 95% confidence level, and 5% margin of error. To ensure the size of 1.21 household contacts that is available to access, combining the anti-TB strategy in China, we drew one to three and up to three family contacts per index case.

Related definitions

Index TB case: At least two positive sputum smears, or one positive sputum with a chest X-ray and a smear positive after two weeks of antibiotic administration, or one sputum sample cultured containing bacilli, or cartridge-based tests such as Gene X-pert® (X-pert) positive [10].

Household contact: living in the same house as a TB index patient for 6 h per week [11]. A diagnosis of TB is followed from 3 months before diagnosis to 14 days after initiation of anti-tuberculosis treatment.

Diabetes mellitus (DM): Fasting plasma glucose (FPG) ≥ 126 mg/dl or random plasma glucose (RPG) ≥ 200 mg/dl or a previous diagnosis of DM. Prediabetes refers to FPG at least 110 but below 126 mg/dl [12] according to the parameters set by the American Diabetes Association (2016).

Limit of salt intake: more than six g/day/adult according to the Dietary Guidelines for Chinese Residents (2016) [13].

Limit of oil intake: According to the Dietary Guidelines for Chinese Residents (2016), more than 30 g/day/adult [13].

Regular serving of vegetables and fruits: Serve vegetables and fruits ≥ 3 times per week.

Regular serving meat: Serving meat ≥ 3 times per week.

Cigarette smoking: Smoking in the past 12 months, including daily and non-daily smoking.

Consuming alcohol: consuming alcohol in the past 12 months, including daily and non-daily alcohol consumption.

The informed consent to participate was obtained from the parents or legal guardians of any participant under the age of 16.

Study procedure and data collections

In our study, alcohol consumption was categorized using WHO criteria (1 unit = 8 g ethanol), classifying participants as non-drinkers (< 1 unit/week), moderate (8–14/21 units/week for women/men), or heavy drinkers (> 14/21 units/week). Smoking status was defined as never-smokers (< 100 lifetime cigarettes), former smokers (quit ≥ 6 months), or current smokers (≥ 1 cigarette/day for ≥ 1 year), with pack-years calculated. Anthropometric measurements followed WHO STEPS protocols: height and weight were measured to calculate BMI (kg/m²), while waist and hip circumferences determined waist-to-hip ratio. Blood pressure was measured using validated Omron devices after 5 min rest, with hypertension defined as average systolic BP ≥ 140 mmHg or diastolic BP ≥ 90 mmHg across three readings. All measurements were performed by trained staff (inter-observer consistency coefficient [ICC] > 0.90 for inter-observer reliability) following standardized protocols to ensure data quality.

Meanwhile, we conducted following the procedures as below. Initially, newly diagnosed TB index cases aged 15 and above reported to the National TB program from the study site and currently receiving treatment for 0–6 months were searched continuously. Those undergoing TB re-treatment, pregnant women, people with mental disorders, or those living alone were excluded.

The informed consent was obtained from the patient during the monthly visits to the hospital for medication of the cases and a home visit of whom would be booked as well.

During home visits, one to three and up to three family contacts, ranging over all ages, were investigated. When there were more than three available contacts in a family, the Kish method would be applied to choose respondents. Although we surveyed all household contacts for TB testing, only subjects 15 years and older were included in the DM related analysis in this report because participants younger than 15 years were less likely to develop DM than adults.

Later, a face-to-face interview with the questionnaire was conducted at home, followed by DM screening at a local hospital. The study population was screened according to the standard test of fasting plasma glucose (FPG) [12]. Systolic blood pressure (SBP)/diastolic blood pressure (DBP) and other anthropometric indicators were measured. The newly identified patients from the study were transferred to the corresponding hospitals for further treatment when sputum conversion was presented. After diagnosis, the participants with a positive result of DM and/or HTN were sent to the corresponding medical institutes for professional medical care and other related service.

Statistical analysis

Data obtained from the questionnaire survey and medical record review were input into EpiData 3.1 (http://www.epidata.dk/) and imported into R 3.6.4 (https://cran.r-project.org/). The quantitative variables, such as age and monthly income, are segmented from continuous exposure variables into new categorical variables, which have important implications for future analysis. A Student’s t-test or variance analysis test was applied to compare groups for age, FPG, RPG, SBP, and DBP as appropriately summarized using the mean and standard deviation. The 95% confidence interval (95%CI) was used for every relevant factor contributing to screening for DM where appropriate.

The additional yield of DM from screening was calculated as the newly diagnosed DM number divided by the sum of the previously known number plus the newly diagnosed DM [7]. The NNS was computed as one divided by the prevalence of newly diagnosed DM after excluding self-reported previously known cases of DM [14]. The same algorithm was applied to that of the household contacts group.

The Odd Ratios (ORs) were the values of the positive subgroup divided by the negative subgroup of the related factors contributing to the DM and non-DM cases (DM vs. non-DM) among the TB patients and their household contacts.

The univariate analysis was applied to each subject factor relevant to DM screening. Three types of variables were involved, namely socio-demographic (gender, age, ethnicity, occupation, monthly income), behavioral and awareness (smoking, drinking alcohol, doing exercise, staying up late, meat taking frequency, knowledge of edible oil and salt intake limits), and clinical characteristics (family history of DM and HTN and other NCDs, a subject diagnosed with other NCDs).

The primary outcome (DM diagnosis) was binary-coded (1 = confirmed case, 0 = non-case) based on ADA diagnostic criteria. Multivariable logistic regression models were constructed through a three-stage process: (1) preliminary bivariate screening of all potential covariates (retention threshold P < 0.20), (2) backward stepwise selection with Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) optimization, and (3) confirmation of linearity assumptions via fractional polynomial analysis. Model fit was assessed using Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness-of-fit tests (all P > 0.15) and area under ROC curve (> 0.75 for all final models). Subgroup analyses by age/sex/urban-rural residence were pre-specified, with interaction terms tested via likelihood ratio tests (α = 0.10 for effect modification). Variance inflation factors (< 3.0) and residual diagnostics confirmed absence of multi-collinearity and influential outliers. Sensitivity analyses included alternative coding schemes and multiple imputation for missing data (< 5% of variables).

Six people who did not respond and did not participate in the disease screening test were excluded from further analysis in the subsequent follow-up process. Then, 1016 adult family contacts were visited. Similarly, 39 did not respond, so they were not screened for DM. The incomplete questionnaire excluded two of the TB index cases and their five family contacts. In the end, 801 (45.2%) TB index and 972 (54.8%) family contacts were included in the study. Risk factors of prevalence of DM using multivariate analysis

A logistic regression model was employed, taking the prevalence of DM as the outcome variable and the significant factors of the univariate analysis as the independent variables. It shows that advanced age, abnormal BMI, presence of a DM family history, previous diagnosis of other NCDs, and sputum-smear positivity were the associated factors in the index cases. For the household contacts, the advanced age, DM family history and being previously diagnosed as HTN.

Subgroup contributions of sociodemographic characteristics

Figures 3 and 4 display the additional yield and NNS to find a new case for DM according to sociodemographic characteristics.

The advanced age, Han ethnicity, occupation, marital status, and BMI were the significant factors in the presence of DM in the index TB cases, whereas advanced age and marital status were the positive contributors to DM with significant odds ratios (ORs) in the household contacts through the univariate analysis (P < 0.05).

Regarding the occupation subgroup the additional yields and NNSs between the subgroups are similar in the TB index cases. The additional yields and NNS of age, ethnicity, marital status, and BMI subgroups varied. The NNSs of ethnicity and BMI in the household contacts are homogeneous. However, the estimates of additional yield are distinguishable (presenting significant differences of 95% CIs) in the subgroups (Figs. 3 and 4).

Discussion

In the overall investigated participants, the common risk factors for the prevalence of DM were advanced age, the presence of DM family history, and being previously diagnosed as HTN or other NCDs. The global additional yield of screening was approximately one-fifth to a fourth of the additional value over the estimated diagnosed cases. And the number needed to find a new case of DM in the index cases was half of that in the household contacts.

In this study, we further confirmed the risk factors for DM preliminarily found in the two previous studies in households with TB cases and yields. We also explored how the risk factors influence the additional yields and NNSs. It appears that the trend that the positive subgroups of the significant variables seemed to have lower NNSs than the negative ones. However, this trend is less evident in the additional yield values manifest in TB index cases and their families. It might be because there were too few newly detected cases found in this single screening activity or for other reasons worthy of further exploration.

Advanced age was reported to be a significant factor for DM, which may be related to the compromised immune ability in the older respondents, making them more predisposed to developing DM [14–17]. Unlike the result in a Bangladesh study [16], the family history of DM in the current research manifested a positive association corresponding to DM status. It might be because of the genetic homogeneity of the same families reacting through similar immunologic substances. Increasing data based on genetic research has emerged to demonstrate the hypothesis [18]. Two studies in China found that some genes were positively linked with the increasing risk of DM, impaired glucose homeostasis [19], and FPG levels in Chinese individuals [20].

In the present study, the participants of the index cases with advanced age had a higher additional yield percentage and a lower NNS value. The same picture was demonstrated in the elderly group among household contacts, except that the additional yield estimate was lower than the younger ones. The early detection of DM is of value in preventing complications. The additional yield from DM screening reflects its worth in families with a PTB member. The higher the additional yield is, the more significant the screening would be. The value of the additional yield in the present study is much lower than that of Nigeria (21.8% vs. 59.0%) [21]. Equally, the lower the NNS is, the more pivotal the screening would be. Likewise, the NNS in the current study was much higher than that in those countries in South India (77 vs. 12) [21]. It might be linked to the implementation status of the primary public health service in different countries.

There are still some broader intervals and higher values with 95% CI of the yields in advanced age, other ethnicities, and the presence of a family history of DM in the household contacts than in the index cases. However, the overall trends of the two populations are similar. In addition, the patient group would usually have a higher additional yield and a lower value of NNS compared to the contact group. It might be because the patients had more chances to go to the local clinics to get medical care for DM diagnosis than the contacts. Meanwhile, the index cases would absorb more attention from their families daily than the contacts.

Limitations

There are some limitations to this study. First, since this study-oriented is a cross-sectional study, it is difficult to determine the exact temporal correlation between DM and PTB. Second, asymptomatic PTB patients may be ignored because of the screening priority decided by a symptoms-and signs-based approach. Finally, prudence should be observed when the results of this study are generalized because of the nature of the single-time screening for DM without repeating data of the same respondent to support the spot results, which might have caused some deviation in the interested associations in this study.

Conclusion

The medical priorities should be paid to screening activities for DM and TB embodied as the first and second levels of the disease prophylaxis strategy instead of the third-class for treatment phase in households with TB cases to bring substantial socioeconomic benefits. Besides, the household contacts conferred as the substitute for the community population in the present study should be stuck on for more medical care.
