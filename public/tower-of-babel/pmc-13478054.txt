A comparative evaluation of chemical processes in anion exchange-based 62Zn/62Cu generator systems
Authors: Ferris L Bissen, Jose R Blanco, Elizabeth M Heidarisafa, Mojdeh Mahmoudian, Carolina A Ferreira, Suzanne E Lapi, Katharina A Domnanich
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe interest in 62Cu as a tracer for positron emission tomography (PET) has motivated several designs of a 62Zn/62Cu radionuclide generator, the most common of which is based on anion exchange resin and a mixed HCl/NaCl eluent. Since commercial 62Zn/62Cu generators are currently not available, the continued investigation of 62Cu-based radiopharmaceuticals relies on the implementation of in-house generator systems. This work presents a systematic evaluation of several key parameters relevant to anion exchange-based 62Zn/62Cu generators, including loading methodology, eluent composition, column dimensions, and 62Cu activity quantification.Results62Zn and the long-lived tracer 65Zn were produced via proton irradiation of Cu targets and used to evaluate 62Zn/62Cu generator performance. A simplified target processing method was developed for generator manufacturing, representing a practical approach for handling irradiated targets given the 9.2 h half-life of 62Zn. Two generator loading approaches were compared, batch loading and fixed-bed loading. Fixed-bed loading yielded significantly lower zinc breakthrough than batch loading, with 1.1(2) × 10–3% compared to 20.6(1)% zinc eluted in the first 20 mL, while maintaining similar loading times. The commonly used 0.2 M HCl/1.8 M NaCl eluent was compared with 2 M HCl to assess the influence of eluent composition on 62Cu elution efficiency and zinc breakthrough. The HCl eluent gave comparable 62Cu elution efficiency, achieving an 84(4)% yield in a 1 mL elution, and lower zinc breakthrough. Resin mass was also evaluated, with 200 mg columns providing similar 62Cu elution efficiencies to 150 mg columns while decreasing zinc breakthrough. Additionally, an improved methodology for 62Cu activity quantification was developed to support reliable generator performance assessment. The produced 62Cu was used to radiolabel diacetyl bis(N4-methylthiosemicarbazone) (ATSM) at high yield in both eluents and used in a proof-of-principal PET imaging study of a tumor-bearing mouse.ConclusionsThis study provides a comparison of loading method, eluent composition, and column configuration in anion exchange-based 62Zn/62Cu generator systems. The results highlight how these parameters influence zinc breakthrough, 62Cu elution efficiency, and downstream radiolabeling. This study outlines practical and transferable operating considerations that support the implementation of in-house 62Zn/62Cu generator systems, contributing towards a broader availability of 62Cu for future studies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s41181-026-00461-z.Keywords: Copper-62, Zinc-62, Generator, PET, Chromatography, Radiopharmaceutical

Background

The radioisotope copper-62 (62Cu, t1/2 = 9.67 m, Iβ+avg = 97.8%, Eβ+avg = 1315.5 keV) has been identified as a promising imaging agent for positron emission tomography (PET) imaging (Kręcisz et al. 2025; Singh et al. 2025). The high positron branching ratio of 62Cu produces an intense PET signal which, along with its short half-life, achieves PET imaging with favorable dosimetry (Green et al. 1990; Wallhaus et al. 1998). The successful use of 62Cu in PET imaging has been demonstrated for a variety of applications, including perfusion studies (Green et al. 1990; Wallhaus et al. 2001; Wong et al. 2008) and imaging of hypoxia (Takahashi et al. 2001; Wong et al. 2008). The monitoring of tissue hypoxia is especially important within oncology, as hypoxic tumors are often more aggressive and treatment-resistant than tumors that do not display hypoxia (Dehdashti et al. 2003; Wong et al. 2008). Numerous studies have investigated the use of the radiopharmaceutical copper-diacetyl-bis(N4-methylthiosemicarbazone) (Cu-ATSM) as a marker of hypoxia via PET imaging with several copper isotopes, including Cu (t1/2 = 23.7 m), 61Cu (t1/2 = 3.34 h), 62Cu, and 64Cu (t1/2 = 12.7 h) (Fujibayashi et al. 1999; Laforest et al. 2005; Lewis et al. 2008; Takahashi et al. 2001; Wong et al. 2008). Several therapeutic isotopes of copper, 64Cu and 67Cu (t1/2 = 61.8 h), have additionally been identified, enabling the use of 62Cu and other copper PET isotopes as part of a theragnostic pair (Kręcisz et al. 2025).

Of the positron-emitting copper isotopes, 62Cu is unique in its ability to be generated via the decay of a longer-lived parent, zinc-62 (62Zn, t1/2 = 9.19 h) (Kręcisz et al. 2025). This allows the setup of a 62Zn/62Cu radionuclide generator, which takes advantage of 62Zn’s longer half-life to produce a steady supply of 62Cu activity over a span of several days (Kręcisz et al. 2025). With the exception of 64Cu, the short half-lives of the other copper PET isotopes, viz. 60Cu and 61Cu, significantly limit their use in imaging facilities without in-house cyclotrons (Green et al. 1990). This challenge is effectively addressed through the 62Zn/62Cu radionuclide generator, which enables the on-site production of 62Cu and thereby expands access to 62Cu PET imaging.

One of the first 62Zn/62Cu generator designs, proposed by Robinson et al., remains among the most widely used within the literature (Bormans et al. 1992; Haynes et al. 2000; Ng et al. 2014; Robinson et al. 1980). This method is based on an anion exchange column and an eluent with high (approximately 2 M) chloride concentration to selectively retain 62Zn onto the resin as the negatively charged ZnCl42− and ZnCl3− complexes (Horne et al. 1957). The 62Cu, however, exists as the neutral CuCl2 species under the same conditions and is thus readily eluted due to its low affinity for the resin (Kraus and Moore 1953). While some iterations of this generator have used hydrochloric acid (HCl) eluents to achieve the desired chloride concentration (Green et al. 1990; Mathias et al. 1991), the more commonly used eluent encompasses a high concentration of sodium chloride (NaCl) in a low molarity HCl solution (Bormans et al. 1992; Haynes et al. 2000; Robinson et al. 1980). This mixed eluent, often comprised of 0.2 M HCl and 1.8 M NaCl, achieves the 2 M chloride anion concentration that is ideal for strongly retaining the 62Zn on the generator column while efficiently eluting the 62Cu (Korkisch 1988; Kraus and Moore 1953). Furthermore, this solution has the advantage of reduced eluate acidity, thereby decreasing the volume of pH-adjusting buffer solution required in downstream radiolabeling and contributing towards improved radiolabeling efficiency (Haynes et al. 2000). A commercial micro-generator for the 62Zn/62Cu system based on a small anion exchange column and mixed HCl/NaCl eluent was developed and distributed by Proportional Technologies, Inc. (Dash and Chakravarty 2019; Lacy et al. 2006). However, this generator was discontinued, and no 62Zn/62Cu generators are currently commercially available. Continued investigation of 62Cu-based PET imaging therefore relies on in-house generator systems. In this context, systematic studies that compare key operating parameters are needed to establish transferable procedures and to understand how these parameters affect generator performance.

This work presents a systematic evaluation of chemical and operational parameters relevant to anion exchange-based 62Zn/62Cu radionuclide generators. The study includes a target processing method which eliminates the need for matrix conversion, providing a facile means for generator production in approximately one hour. We additionally compared different generator loading approaches, evaluated the commonly used 0.2 M HCl/1.8 M NaCl eluent versus a 2 M HCl eluent, and assessed the influence of column dimensions on elution behavior. Generator performance was evaluated across three critical metrics: zinc breakthrough, 62Cu elution efficiency, and suitability of the eluate for 62Cu radiolabeling with the ATSM ligand. In addition, we developed an improved methodology for accurate 62Cu quantification, addressing challenges associated with reliable activity measurements for this radionuclide. Finally, 62Cu-labeled ATSM tracer was used in a proof-of-principle PET imaging experiment in a tumor-bearing mouse, demonstrating the applicability of the in-house 62Zn/62Cu generator system for preclinical applications. Collectively, these investigations provide a systematic comparison of generator parameters and practical guidance for implementing in-house 62Zn/62Cu generator systems.

Methods

Chemicals

Natural copper foils used for cyclotron production of 62Zn were obtained from Goodfellow (0.025 mm, 98.8% purity) and ESPI Metals (0.101 mm, 5N purity). Preliminary experiments for method development used 65Zn, which was obtained as a byproduct from 62Zn production, and 64Cu, which was purchased from the University of Alabama at Birmingham (UAB) Cyclotron Facility in a 0.1 M HCl solution.

For radiochemical separation and generator setup, the following chemicals were used: hydrochloric acid (HCl, Suprapur, 30%, Sigma-Aldrich or OmniTrace, 34–37%, Sigma-Aldrich), sodium chloride (NaCl, Suprapur, 99.99%, Sigma-Aldrich), and hydrogen peroxide (H2O2, 30%, ACS grade, Fisher Chemical). The required dilutions were prepared with water of ultrapure quality with a conductivity of 18.2 MΩ∙cm, obtained from the Thermo Scientific Barnstead GenPure xCAD Plus System. The columns were made with varying quantities of AG 1-X8 anion exchange resin (200–400 mesh) in its chloride form (Bio-Rad) in ISOLUTE 1 mL reservoirs (6 mm ID) with 20 µm polyethylene frits (Biotage).

The following chemicals were used for the synthesis of [62Cu]Cu-ATSM: sodium acetate (NaOAc, 99.99% metals basis, Alfa Aesar), diacetyl bis(N4-methylthiosemicarbazone) (H2ATSM, ≥ 95%, ABX, Germany), and dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO, 99.99995% metals basis, Sigma-Aldrich). Sep-Pak Light C18 cartridges (Waters) were used for [62Cu]Cu-ATSM purification and reconstitution into an ethanol matrix (HPLC grade, Sigma-Aldrich).

Instruments

Nuclide identification and quantification was achieved using a broad energy high purity germanium gamma spectrometer (BE2020, Canberra). The energy and efficiency calibration was done using a sealed 152Eu source (39.89 kBq at reference date 1 April 2020). The ISOCS/LabSOCS program (Mirion Technologies) was employed to simulate the geometry and composition of the measured samples. The dead time was kept under 5% for all measurements. The collected gamma spectra were analyzed using Genie 4.0 software. To compute the 62Cu activity from measured gamma spectra, specially-designed tube holders (depicted in Fig. 5A) were used to stop all emitted positrons within a discrete volume and a correction factor was applied to account for subsequent attenuation of the annihilation photons, as described in the supplementary material (Table S1). Activities of 62Cu elutions were additionally quantified with a CRC-55tR dose calibrator (Capintec) that was cross-calibrated with the aforementioned gamma spectrometer. To determine the radiolabeling yield, radiographs of thin layer chromatography (TLC) plates were recorded on an Amersham Typhoon Biomolecular Imager (GE Healthcare) and analyzed using ImageQuant TL software (GE Healthcare).

A Nylon tube holder with 1.7 cm thick walls for gamma spectroscopy measurements of 62Cu samples. B Schematic of positron annihilation within tube holder walls to generate annihilation photons

For all gamma spectroscopy measurements performed as single replicates, the reported error is based on the statistical counting errors associated with the activity quantification. For datasets obtained from averaging independent trials, mean values are reported and corresponding uncertainty was determined by propagating the instrumental uncertainties from each individual measurement to obtain an overall uncertainty for the mean. Radiolabeling yields were determined via the manual integration of TLC radiographs, which was performed two times for each sample. The yield is reported as the mean of the replicate integrations, and the corresponding uncertainty is given as the standard deviation of those replicates.

65Zn tracer experiments for method development

Distribution Coefficient Study: Zinc adsorption to the anion exchange resin was quantified at varying chloride concentrations via a batch-mode distribution coefficient (Kd, defined in Eq. 6.1 within the supplementary material) study. Approximately 0.2 MBq (5.5 µCi) of 65Zn in 2.5 mL HCl (1–2.5 M) and 0.2 M HCl/1.8 M NaCl was contacted with 50 mg anion exchange resin and shaken at 300 rpm for 30 min at room temperature in an Innova 42 shaking incubator (New Brunswick). After shaking, a 1 mL aliquot of solution was removed and the 65Zn activity was quantified via gamma spectroscopy.

Evaluation of 62Zn/62Cu Generator Loading: To assess the effect of anion exchange resin mass on generator performance, the degree of 65Zn breakthrough was determined using both batch load and fixed-bed load methods. In the batch load method, a solution of 65Zn in 2 M HCl was added to a syringe containing 200 mg of anion exchange resin and shaken under the same parameters as described for the Kd study. The syringe contents were passed through a fritted column by hand, allowing the resin to be collected in the column and the solution to pass through. Once the load solution was completely passed through the column, a frit was placed on top of the resin bed. The fixed-bed load method was performed by slurry packing an aqueous solution containing 200 mg of anion exchange resin into a fritted column and placing a frit on top of the resin bed, then passing the 65Zn load solution through the column via syringe pump at a flow rate of 1 mL/min. Both methods used 2.2–3.7 MBq (60–100 µCi) of 65Zn in an approx. 13 mL load solution and eluted the columns with 20 mL of 2 M HCl or 0.2 M HCl/1.8 M NaCl. Zinc breakthrough in the load flow-through and eluted solutions was quantified via gamma spectroscopy.

Target irradiation

Copper targets with thicknesses of 25–100 µm were irradiated with a TR-24 cyclotron (Advanced Cyclotron Systems, Inc.) at the UAB Cyclotron Facility using 24 MeV protons at a beam current of 16–65 µA. To increase produced activities, irradiations were performed using a 100 µm thick target, positioned at a 30 degree angle for an effective target thickness of 200 µm. The foils were irradiated for 30–180 min, after which they were left in the target station for approx. 2 h to allow for the partial decay of the short-lived 63Zn. Following the cool-down period, the foils were shipped to Michigan State University (MSU, East Lansing, MI) for chemical processing.

Radiochemical isolation of 62Zn and generator setup

The dissolution of the irradiated copper foil was performed in a beaker containing 5 mL of 2 M HCl heated to 125 °C. Hydrogen peroxide was added in 0.5 mL intervals until the foil completely dissolved, typically 1.5 mL total. The solution was diluted to 30 mL with 2 M HCl and allowed to cool to room temperature. A small sample (approx. 20 µL) was withdrawn to confirm the absence of hydrogen peroxide in solution using Quantofix peroxide test strips (Macherey–Nagel).

Isolation of 62Zn from bulk copper was achieved by passing the obtained solution through an anion exchange column (200–400 mg resin, 6 mm ID), which was slurry packed in water and preconditioned with 5 mL of 2 M HCl. After loading, the column was washed with 10 mL of 2 M HCl to remove bulk copper and other possible contaminants. The 62Zn was eluted in 10 mL of 0.001 M HCl and acidified to 2 M via addition of concentrated HCl. The acidified 62Zn solution was loaded onto a generator column, which contained 150–200 mg anion exchange resin (6 mm ID) that was preconditioned with 5 mL of 2 M HCl. The generator was washed immediately after loading with 3 mL of a 2 M HCl or 0.2 M HCl/1.8 M NaCl eluent. In the following waiting period of 30–60 min, the radioactive daughter 62Cu was generated and subsequently eluted with the aforementioned eluents. Multiple elution cycles were performed with each generator column and the obtained 62Cu activity was measured. All elutions of the purification columns and generators were done at a flow rate of 1 mL/min.

Synthesis of [62Cu]Cu-ATSM

The synthesis procedure of [62Cu]Cu-ATSM was developed using commercially-available 64Cu, taking advantage of its longer half-life.

A 2–5 M NaOAc solution was added to approximately 350 µL 62Cu in HCl or HCl/NaCl to adjust the pH to 4–5. The H2ATSM dissolved in DMSO (0.04 or 0.4 mM solution, 0.5–80 nmol added) was then added to the reaction vial, inverted several times, and incubated at room temperature for approximately 30 s.

Radiolabeling yields were assessed via thin layer chromatography using aluminum-backed silica TLC plates (Sorbtec) as the stationary phase and absolute methanol (HPLC grade, Sigma-Aldrich) as the mobile phase. Free 62Cu remained at the origin while 62Cu-labeled ATSM traveled with the mobile phase with a retention factor (Rf) of approximately 0.6. The developed TLC plates were phosphor imaged, and the resulting radiographs were analyzed to determine radiochemical yield.

PET/MR imaging with [62Cu]Cu-ATSM

Pre-clinical PET imaging of a mouse required the solution matrix to be changed to absolute ethanol, which was achieved using a C18 cartridge. The C18 column was preconditioned with 5 mL of ethanol and 10 mL of water. The labeled solution was diluted at a 1:1 ratio with water and passed through the column. To remove any unlabeled 62Cu, the C18 column was washed with 5 mL of water followed by 5 mL of air. The [62Cu]Cu-ATSM was eluted in approx. 250 µL ethanol, which was then evaporated and reconstituted in a 0.9% saline solution for injection. Approximately 3.7 MBq (100 µCi) of reconstituted [62Cu]Cu-ATSM solution was injected intravenously into a BXPC-3 tumor-bearing mouse. Simultaneous PET and magnetic resonance (MR) imaging was then performed using a Bruker BioSpec 70/30 preclinical MRI. Dynamic PET data were acquired immediately after intravenous injection of [62Cu]Cu-ATSM and reconstructed using a Maximum-Likelihood Expectation–Maximization (MLEM) with 18 iterations, followed by a Gaussian post-reconstruction filter of 0.5 mm full-width at half-maximum (FWHM), scatter correction and random coincidence correction. ​The 35‑min dynamic acquisition was binned into 21 frames (12 × 10 s, 3 × 60 s, 6 × 300 s, and all time-activity curves and image-derived ratios were calculated from these framed reconstructions. MRI was acquired using a 72 mm volume coil using a FISP sequence in FID mode with 500 µm resolution, TR/TE 5.7/2.9 ms, and a scan time of 4 min.

Results

Production and radiochemical isolation of 62Zn

The production of 62Zn for this work used irradiation of natCu targets of varying thickness with 24 MeV protons. Targets with effective thicknesses ranging from 25 to 200 µm resulted in exiting proton energies of 23.6–21.3 MeV. The radionuclides zinc-65 (65Zn, t1/2 = 244 d) and copper-64 (64Cu, t1/2 = 12.7 h) were detected as side products in the irradiated target foil. The short-lived zinc-63 isotope (63Zn, t1/2 = 38.4 min) was also predicted to be produced in significant quantity (Shahid et al. 2015), however, it was not detectable at the time of quantification, which occurred at least 24 h after the end of irradiation. Cross sections for 62Zn and side product production via natCu irradiation at proton energies relevant to this study are provided in the supplementary material (Fig. S1). No other radionuclides were detected in the irradiated foils or any subsequent solutions. The irradiation yields of all detected nuclides and their comparison to theoretically predicted yields are outlined in Table 1. The calculation of predicted yields is described in the supplementary material. The experimental yields from irradiations 3 and 4 deviated noticeably from their predicted values for 62Zn and 64Cu. One possible contributing factor may be the tilted target geometry used in these irradiations, which differed from the configuration employed for irradiations 1 and 2. While the increased effective thickness was accounted for in the yield predictions, additional factors may contribute to the observed discrepancies and are currently being investigated.

Dissolution of the irradiated copper targets was achieved using mildly-heated 2 M HCl, with 30% H2O2 added to promote oxidation of copper metal to its + 2 oxidation state (Cantrell 2015). Using this method, irradiated targets with masses up to approx. 70 mg were completely dissolved in less than five minutes, and the resulting solution was confirmed to contain no detectable hydrogen peroxide after the target dissolution. This dissolution method was confirmed to be reproducible in studies with non-irradiated copper foils for masses up to 500 mg. Preliminary studies also showed the need to further dilute the dissolved foil solution to a copper concentration of approx. 2800 ppm before loading onto the purification column, as solutions with higher copper concentrations displayed increased zinc breakthrough.

The separation of the produced 62Zn from the bulk target material was achieved using a column containing as little as 200 mg AG1-X8 (200–400 mesh) resin. Complete elution of the bulk copper, which was quantified via detection of the co-eluted 64Cu’s 1346 keV gamma line, was achieved in a 10 mL 2 M HCl elution, resulting in less than 0.001% loss of the 62Zn from the column. Using this small column size allowed for 98(5)% recovery of the 62Zn in 10 mL of 0.001 M HCl, which was then acidified to 2 M HCl before loading onto the generator column.

Generator setup and zinc breakthrough behavior

The performance of the two generator loading methods tested, batch loading and fixed-bed loading, was determined in tracer studies with 65Zn. The comparison of the breakthrough of 65Zn in the load flow-through and subsequent elution with the HCl/NaCl mixture is shown in Fig. 1. The batch load method resulted in significant breakthrough during the loading step and consistent breakthrough in following elutions, resulting in a total loss of 20.6(1)% of 65Zn within a 20 mL elution. Conversely, the fixed-bed loaded column yielded 1.1(2) × 10–3% 65Zn breakthrough within 20 mL of eluent, which was only present in the last eluted fraction. In addition to the significant decrease in breakthrough, fixed-bed loading achieves generator production in a similar timeframe to the batch loading procedure and requires less handling of potentially high activity load solutions.

The low breakthroughs observed using 200 mg columns loaded with the fixed-bed method prompted investigation into a smaller column using 150 mg of resin. However, as shown in Fig. 2, reducing resin mass from 200 to 150 mg resulted in an increase in zinc loss from the column. While the 200 mg column yielded 18 mL of eluate with no detectable 65Zn activity, the 150 mg column exhibited 65Zn loss after the first eluted 2 mL fraction. Breakthrough from the 150 mg column steadily increased over the 20 mL HCl/NaCl elution, resulting in a total desorption of 0.056(2)% of the loaded 65Zn.

The effect of eluent chloride concentration was determined in a batch-mode Kd study using 1–2.5 M HCl solutions and a 0.2 M HCl/1.8 M NaCl mixture. The results of this study are outlined in Fig. 3A, which shows that zinc adsorption to the anion exchange resin increases with increasing HCl concentration up to 2 M, where it reaches a maximum. The Kd of zinc is closely comparable between the 2 M HCl (1800 ± 100 mL/g) and 0.2 M HCl/1.8 M NaCl (1700 ± 100 mL/g) solutions, indicating that both may be suitable eluents for 62Cu while retaining 62Zn on the generator column.

Despite the comparable Kdvalues for zinc in 0.2 M HCl/1.8 M NaCl and 2 M HCl (Fig. 3A), columns eluted using these two solutions yielded different levels of 65Zn breakthrough, as shown in Fig. 3B. The HCl/NaCl eluent resulted in the desorption of 0.056(2)% of 65Zn within the first 20 mL elution of a 150 mg column, with detectable activities present after the first eluted 2 mL fraction. For the same column dimensions, however, the HCl eluent showed just 8(9) × 10–3% 65Zn loss in the first 20 mL, yielding 8 mL of eluate with no detectable 65Zn activity.

The insights gained from the generator performance tests were applied in establishing a set of four 62Zn/62Cu generators, as outlined in Table 2. The generators facilitated systematic testing of the two studied eluents (HCl/NaCl and HCl) and resin masses (150 mg and 200 mg) with respect to 62Zn breakthrough as well as 62Cu elution efficiency, which was not possible in the preliminary tests. Of these four generators, only the 150 mg column eluted with the HCl/NaCl eluent (generator 1 in Table 2) resulted in a detectable 62Zn breakthrough of 0.092(6)% within 36 mL cumulative elution volume (Fig. 4). All other generators yielded no detectable 62Zn activity in any eluted fractions up to approx. 30 mL total elution volume.

62Cu quantification

A technique for the accurate quantification of 62Cu via gamma spectroscopy was developed as part of this work. The decay of 62Cu is associated with very low intensity gamma emissions, with the most intense gamma ray, at an energy of 1172.97 keV, having an intensity of just 0.349% (Singh et al. 2025). This necessitates that the quantification of 62Cu activity via gamma spectroscopy uses the 511 keV signal produced from the annihilation of emitted positrons. Positrons dissipate their kinetic energy via collisions with surrounding particles before annihilating with an electron. Therefore, in samples containing particularly high-energy positron emitters, such as 62Cu (Eβ+avg = 1315.5 keV), the annihilation photons produced from positron decay may originate from outside the sample volume. Given that the detected activity is a function of the distance from the detector, the absence of a well-defined emission point introduces a significant measurement error. To address this effect, all 62Cu samples in this work were measured in specially-designed tube holders made with nylon (Fig. 5A). The increased density of nylon compared to air ensures that emitted positrons are fully stopped within a defined volume. However, the continuous positron energy spectrum results in positron annihilation occurring at varying positions within the tube holder. As a result, the produced annihilation photons traverse different path lengths through the material of the holder, leading to varying degrees of attenuation (Fig. 5B). Therefore, an attenuation correction factor was theoretically determined based on positron stopping power and photon attenuation data for the nylon material used, as described in detail in the supplementary material.

The accuracy of the quantification method was validated using a 68Ga sample, which has a high average positron energy of 836.0 keV as well as a direct gamma emission (Eγ = 1077.34 keV, Iγ = 3.22%) suitable for direct activity quantification by gamma spectroscopy. The 68Ga activity derived from the 511 keV annihilation peak using the nylon holder and applied correction factor was found to be consistent within experimental uncertainty with the activity obtained from the 1077 keV gamma-line (Fig. S2). Once validated, the method was used to quantify all 62Cu sample activities in this study. Comparative gamma spectroscopy measurements performed with and without the nylon holders showed a significant effect on the calculated activity. Without these holders, gamma spectroscopic quantification overestimated 62Cu activity by a factor of 1.2 ± 0.1 (n = 3).

Comparison of 62Cu elution conditions

The effect of the different generator eluents and resin masses on 62Cu elution efficiency was characterized. The theoretical 62Cu activity present on the generator column at the time of elution was estimated from the loaded 62Zn activity and applying the Bateman equation. The total elution volume was kept under 2 mL to prevent significant 62Cu regeneration during the elution step. Both HCl and HCl/NaCl eluents showed comparable performance at elution volumes above 0.6 mL. At smaller volumes, the HCl/NaCl eluent resulted in slightly higher elution efficiencies (see Fig. 6 and Fig. S3). Similarly, increasing the generator resin mass only affected 62Cu elution efficiency in elution volumes below 1 mL. While there is an approximately 10% decrease in elution efficiency at 0.25 mL for the 200 mg column compared to the 150 mg column, this difference in performance is offset at increased elution volume.

The isotopic purity of several eluted samples from each generator (n ≥ 3) was assessed via gamma spectroscopy at longer count times (1 h or longer), allowing the 62Cu to fully decay. These counts showed no detectable presence of radionuclidic contaminants, besides the 62Zn observed in a single generator as discussed above.

Radiolabeling of 62Cu and PET imaging

The 62Cu obtained from these generators was used to radiolabel the ATSM ligand. Labeling tests were performed using 62Cu eluted from generators of varying activity (5.5–185 MBq or 0.15–5 mCi) to characterize the dependence of labeling yield on 62Cu activity. These results, as well as tests done with 64Cu solutions, showed that the yield of [62,64Cu]Cu-ATSM is dependent on the radioactivity concentration of the solution (Fig. 7). At activity concentrations lower than 44 MBq/mL (1.2 mCi/mL), the radiolabeling yield steadily decreases with decreasing activity concentration.

Radiolabeling of 62Cu with ATSM was performed in both the 0.2 M HCl/1.8 M NaCl and 2 M HCl matrices. Both solutions were adjusted to a pH of 4–5 with NaOAc before addition of the H2ATSM ligand. Due to its increased acidity, the 2 M HCl solution required a higher addition of NaOAc solution to reach the desired pH. The increase in volume was partially compensated by using higher molarity NaOAc buffer, however, the resulting activity concentration of the 62Cu solution was still reduced. The labeling solutions in 2 M HCl ultimately required an approximately 4 times higher buffer volume for pH adjustment, leading to a corresponding decrease in the activity concentration of the solution. Despite this, the labeling tests between the two eluents, done at two different apparent molar activities (AMA), showed no significant difference in 62Cu labeling yield, as outlined in Table 3.

Discussion

Production and radiochemical isolation of 62Zn

The production of 62Zn via proton irradiation of natCu foil occurs via the 63Cu(p,2n) and 65Cu(p,4n) reaction pathways, with the p,2n reaction dominating at proton energies lower than 31 MeV (Shahid et al. 2015). At the proton energies used in this study (Ep = 24 → 21.4 MeV), the peak cross section for 62Zn production, which occurs at 23.5 MeV, was fully captured (Hermanne et al. 2018; Shahid et al. 2015), facilitating production of GBq amounts of 62Zn within a several-hour irradiation. The long-lived isotope 65Zn was formed as a minor byproduct of irradiation, contributing to less than 0.1% of the 62Zn activity at EOB. Over the course of several days, however, its relative presence can rise to about 15% during the generator’s lifetime. Despite this increase, the very low breakthrough observed in these generators ensured that negligible amounts of 65Zn were carried into the eluate.

The dissolution of copper foils using 2 M HCl and hydrogen peroxide was achieved in less than five minutes and avoided the lengthy conversion of a nitric acid to chloride matrix used in much of the established literature (Bormans et al. 1992; Haynes et al. 2000; Robinson et al. 1980; Zweit et al. 1992). Since matrix conversion procedures often involve multiple precipitation or evaporation steps, they introduce additional handling and opportunities for activity loss during processing of the relatively short-lived 62Zn. In contrast, the dissolution method used in this work enables a comparatively simple and quick target processing approach prior to generator loading. The low volumes required for target dissolution additionally facilitated expeditious debulking of the 62Zn from the target material. Using a column containing 200 mg AG 1-X8 anion exchange resin (8 mm × 6 mm), 70 mg of copper target material was fully removed in less than 10 mL of a 2 M HCl wash. Using this comparatively simple processing approach, the complete generator setup could be accomplished in approximately one hour.

Generator setup and zinc breakthrough behavior

While the fixed-bed method for loading of the parent isotope is perhaps more widely used within generator literature, the batch load method has seen use in several generator designs for a variety of applications, including reducing resin degradation from radiolysis and improving adsorption behavior with high loading masses (McDevitt et al. 1999; Tanase et al. 1997). The batch approach may also shorten loading times, as packing columns with preloaded resin removes the need to introduce a load solution at low flow rates. Generators loaded using the batch and fixed-bed methods in this study, however, showed significantly different levels of performance, with the batch method yielding zinc breakthrough several orders of magnitude higher than the fixed-bed method. The 7.60(9)% zinc loss observed in the flow-through of the batch loading experiment is largely in agreement with the predicted breakthrough of 6.67%, calculated from the measured zinc Kd at 2 M chloride concentration (1800 ml/g), as outlined in the supplementary material (Eq. 6.1–6.3). This indicates that while zinc exhibits a high affinity for the anion exchange resin, a single equilibrium contact in the batch loading method does not result in fully quantitative uptake under these conditions. Subsequent eluted fractions displayed a consistent level of zinc breakthrough, which is likely caused by the uniform distribution of zinc throughout the resin bed, creating a static equilibrium and limiting re-adsorption of zinc as it travels through the column (Roesch 2012). Conversely, for generators that were loaded using the fixed-bed method, low levels of zinc were only observed after approximately 20 mL of eluent had passed through the column. This behavior can be explained by the establishment of dynamic column conditions, in which zinc is initially adsorbed on top of the resin bed and gradually migrates downward through repeated desorption and re-adsorption steps (Roesch 2012).

The batch-mode Kd study done in this work (Fig. 3A) confirmed previous results that reported an increase in zinc affinity for similar anion exchange resins with increasing concentration of HCl, reaching a maximum at 2 M HCl (Korkisch 1988; Saito 1984). Additionally, the Kd of zinc was shown to be very similar between 2 M HCl and the commonly used 0.2 M HCl/1.8 M NaCl, suggesting that the chloride anion concentration plays a significant role in determining zinc adsorption behavior. Column studies using these two eluents (Fig. 3B), however, revealed differences in zinc adsorption behavior that were not observed in the batch-mode Kd study. Despite the comparable zinc Kd values between the two tested solutions, columns eluted with 0.2 M HCl/1.8 M NaCl showed nearly 7 times higher zinc breakthrough within 20 mL of eluate than columns eluted with 2 M HCl. These observations suggest that the secondary cation influences zinc adsorption in this system. The phenomenon of the secondary cation, sometimes referred to as the non-exchange electrolyte, affecting transition metal ion-exchange behavior—including the zinc chloride/anion exchange system—has been documented by several researchers (Chu and Diamond 1959; Horne 1957; Marcus and Maydan 1963). Importantly, when using the batch method, the Kd of zinc on anion exchange resin is nearly identical for 2 M HCl and 2 M NaCl (Horne 1957); however, with the column method, zinc’s adsorption affinity differs between HCl and NaCl solutions of the same electrolyte activity (Marcus and Maydan 1963). The mechanism of the secondary cation effect remains a point of discussion within the literature (Du Preez 2000). However, a related study suggests that the mechanical action of the batch method provides increased contact between the hydrophobic sorbent material and aqueous extracting media, compensating for the difference in zinc adsorption (Jackson et al. 1984). At the electrolyte concentrations used in this study, the mechanical agitation introduced by the batch method can be attributed as the key factor for overcoming the difference in zinc adsorption behavior between the two eluents.

Zinc breakthrough was evaluated for different generator configurations and elution conditions, with the highest value observed for the 150 mg generator eluted with the HCl/NaCl mixed eluent, corresponding to a maximum 62Zn breakthrough of 8.6(5) × 10–3% within 1 mL. Conversely, generators of the same size eluted with 2 M HCl yielded no detectable 62Zn breakthrough even at cumulative elution volumes up to 30 mL. Increasing the resin mass from 150 to 200 mg further reduced zinc breakthrough, facilitating higher eluate volumes to be collected without detectable 62Zn. Accordingly, the use of 2 M HCl eluent or increased resin mass enabled the collection of eluent volumes without detectable 62Zn breakthrough. The maximum breakthrough levels observed in this work are comparable to a similar generator design reporting 0.01% breakthrough for the same delivered volume (Bormans et al. 1992). These results are also comparable to those of a previously available commercial generator, which exhibited a 1 × 10–3% breakthrough per 0.25 mL dose (Ng et al. 2014), placing the breakthrough levels observed here within the range of previously reported 62Zn/62Cu generator systems.

62Cu quantification

This work establishes a gamma-spectroscopic approach for the quantification of 62Cu activity based on the 511 keV positron annihilation photons. The use of thick-walled nylon sample holders was shown to correct an approximately 20% overestimation of 62Cu activity arising from the annihilation of high-energy positrons outside the defined sample volume. Similar effects have been reported in point source geometries, where deviations of 30–37% between activities derived from annihilation radiation and direct gamma emissions were observed (Lépy et al. 2010). For low energy positrons emitters, like 18F or 11C, annihilation is expected to occur predominantly within the solution or container volume (Lépy et al. 2010). However, this assumption does not hold for high-energy positron emitters such as 62Cu, where positron range effects become significant. Therefore, appropriate correction is required when quantifying 62Cu activity via gamma spectroscopy. This is particularly relevant for the determination of 62Cu elution efficiencies from 62Zn/62Cu generators, which depend on the 62Zn activity at the time of elution, as 62Zn can be accurately quantified via gamma spectroscopy of its characteristic gamma emissions. However, without the applied correction, 62Cu activities derived from annihilation photons may lead to overestimation of elution efficiencies.

Comparison of 62Cu elution conditions

The elution of 62Cu from the studied generators was achieved in a small volume, recovering 84(4)% of the 62Cu activity in approximately 1 mL regardless of column size and eluent. The 62Cu elution efficiency was very comparable between the HCl/NaCl and HCl eluents, with observable differences only at elution volumes smaller than 0.6 mL. The slight difference in 62Cu elution behavior at low volumes is likely due to the aforementioned secondary cation effect. Additionally, resin mass was shown to have only a slight influence on the 62Cu elution efficiency at low elution volumes and no significant effect at elution volumes greater than 0.8 mL. The 62Cu elution efficiencies achieved in this work are comparable to similar generator designs previously reported (Bormans et al. 1992; Haynes et al. 2000). Long-time gamma spectroscopy measurements further confirmed that the eluted 62Cu was free of radionuclidic impurities, including 64Cu. In combination with the efficient radiolabeling of ATSM discussed below, these results further suggest minimal contribution of stable copper from the irradiation target, as elevated levels of non-radioactive copper would be expected to reduce labeling efficiency.

Radiolabeling of 62Cu and PET imaging

The 62Cu radiolabeling tests of the ATSM ligand evaluated radiolabeling performance over a range of 62Cu activity concentrations, including low-activity conditions. The results (Fig. 7) suggest that 62Cu labeling of ATSM is dependent on the activity concentration of the labeling solution, with effective chelation (> 90% RCY) at an AMA of 1 MBq/nmol requiring activity concentrations greater than 25 MBq/mL (0.68 mCi/mL). While reduced labeling performance at lower activity concentrations is expected, such low-activity labeling conditions have not been extensively described for the [62Cu]Cu-ATSM system in literature (Fukumura et al. 2006; Wong et al. 2008; Zhang et al. 2013), and the present results provide insight into the practical activity concentration range required for efficient labeling. The efficient radiolabeling observed at higher activity concentrations additionally supports the suitability of the generator-produced 62Cu for subsequent radiopharmaceutical applications.

The choice of generator eluent, 2 M HCl or 0.2 M HCl/1.8 M NaCl, was shown to have no significant effect on 62Cu labeling yields at the investigated apparent molar activities (Table 3). While high labeling yields were achieved for 62Cu eluted using 2 M HCl, its higher acidity compared to the HCl/NaCl solution necessitates the addition of greater amounts of buffer to reach a desired pH for radiolabeling. For pre-clinical applications, radiolabeled solutions from both eluents require a matrix exchange due to their high osmolarity, which can be achieved using a C18 cartridge, as previously reported (Green et al. 1990; Mathias et al. 1991). In clinical studies, generator-produced 62Cu eluted in 0.2 M HCl/1.8 M NaCl has been successfully radiolabeled and administered directly after appropriate dilution (Robinson et al. 1980; Wallhaus et al. 1998). Conversely, the higher volume of the pH-adjusted 2 M HCl solution of radiolabeled 62Cu may limit its suitability for direct application. However, both approaches remain dependent on formulation and handling requirements in the respective application context.

PET imaging with 62Cu-labeled ATSM produced from this generator system was demonstrated to successfully image a hypoxic tumor in a mouse bearing a BXPC-3 xenograft (Fig. 8). The peak tumor uptake reached 11.1%ID/mL at 115 s (Fig. 8C), consistent with previously reported dynamic PET studies demonstrating rapid and robust accumulation of Cu-ATSM tracers in hypoxic tumors (Nie et al. 2020; Zhang et al. 2013). While the tumor-to-muscle ratio exhibited a transient early peak (143.5 at 75 s), its subsequent decline was largely driven by the low and stable muscle activity rather than loss of tracer from the tumor, as absolute tumor uptake remained stable at approximately 10–11%ID/mL (Fig. 8C and D).​ Therefore, absolute tumor uptake and tumor-to-blood ratio may provide a more robust representation of biologically relevant tracer retention during this early dynamic imaging window (Holland et al. 2009; McCall et al. 2012; Wong et al. 2008). Analysis of PET data showed the highest non-target 62Cu uptake in the liver (Fig. 8B), a well-established characteristic of Cu-ATSM tracers, often attributed to hepatic copper metabolism and clearance pathways (Andreozzi et al. 2017; Colombié et al. 2015; Pyun et al. 2023). The liver is frequently reported as the principal dose-limiting organ for ATSM compounds, with significant physiological uptake exceeding other non-target tissues (Colombié et al. 2015; Laforest et al. 2005). Overall, these findings demonstrate that [62Cu]Cu-ATSM prepared from the generator-produced 62Cu enabled PET visualization of hypoxic tumors in the BXPC-3 xenograft model, supporting the suitability of the produced 62Cu for preclinical PET imaging applications.

Conclusion

This work presents a comparative evaluation of key parameters influencing the setup and operation of an anion exchange-based 62Zn/62Cu generator, providing a systematic assessment relevant for implementing in-house generator systems. A streamlined procedure for the rapid processing of irradiated copper targets was developed, enabling efficient generator fabrication. The loading strategy was shown to impact generator performance, with fixed-bed loading exhibiting significantly reduced zinc breakthrough compared to the batch method. Generator configuration and elution conditions, including resin mass and eluent composition, affected 62Zn breakthrough while having only minor impact on 62Cu elution efficiency at relevant volumes. Both 2 M HCl and 0.2 M HCl/1.8 M NaCl eluents provided comparable 62Cu elution yield, with differences primarily in zinc breakthrough. A gamma-spectroscopy based method for 62Cu quantification was established and validated to account for positron range and annihilation effects, enabling more accurate 62Cu activity determination. Radiolabeling of the ATSM ligand with 62Cu was evaluated over a range of activity concentrations and found to be independent of the eluent matrix under the investigated conditions. Finally, the synthesized [62Cu]Cu-ATSM was used for proof-of-principle PET imaging of a hypoxic tumor-bearing mouse, supporting the applicability of the in-house generator-produced 62Cu for preclinical applications. Overall, the systematic comparison of generator parameters and quantification methodology presented here provides a framework for reliable in-house production of 62Cu.
