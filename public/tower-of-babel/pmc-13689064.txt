Multidimensional manifestations of artificial intelligence literacy among clinical nurses: a qualitative descriptive study
Authors: Jinping Gu, Lin He, Ling Gu, Zhulai Zhan, Haoyue Liu, Jingqiong Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundArtificial intelligence (AI) is increasingly integrated into nursing practice, but its safe, critical, and responsible use depends on nurses’ AI literacy. Existing studies have mainly used quantitative approaches to assess overall levels of AI literacy, providing limited insight into how it is manifested in clinical practice. This study aimed to explore the multidimensional manifestations of AI literacy among clinical nurses from a qualitative perspective.MethodsThis qualitative descriptive study was conducted in a tertiary Grade A general hospital in southwest China. Clinical nurses were recruited using maximum variation purposive sampling, considering age, years of experience, educational background, role, and department type. Data were collected from 26 February through March 2026 using semi-structured individual interviews and analysed using framework-based content analysis guided by four core AI literacy domains and an additional attitudinal domain. Reporting followed the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ).ResultsTwenty-three clinical nurses participated. Participants’ accounts suggested that AI literacy in clinical nursing was not merely a technical skill, but a practice-based capability shaped by real-task use, professional judgement, and institutional context. Nurses often approached AI through a “tool-first” logic: perceived usefulness in information retrieval, documentation support, patient education, and learning preceded systematic understanding of AI mechanisms. In practice, AI was mainly used as a human-led auxiliary resource; nurses valued its efficiency but emphasised the need to revise, verify, and contextualise AI-generated outputs. A prominent finding was ethical misalignment: some nurses had limited awareness of privacy and governance-related risks, yet strongly regarded themselves as responsible for the consequences of AI-assisted care. Attitudes towards AI were generally open but ambivalent, with expectations of workload relief and professional support coexisting with concerns about dependence, replacement, and disruption of clinician–patient trust.ConclusionsAI literacy among clinical nurses is multidimensional, context-embedded, and heterogeneous. It should be understood not simply as the ability to operate AI tools, but as a broader capability involving judgement, responsibility, and professional role negotiation. Future efforts should move beyond technical training alone to develop tailored, practice-oriented support strategies that emphasise safe and appropriate use, thereby facilitating effective AI integration into nursing practice.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12912-026-05128-3.Keywords: Artificial intelligence, Artificial intelligence literacy, Clinical nurses, Qualitative research, Nursing practice

Introduction

Artificial intelligence (AI) has been increasingly applied across multiple areas of healthcare delivery and has shown broad potential in nursing-related contexts, including risk prediction, clinical documentation support, patient education, information retrieval, decision support, and workflow optimisation [1–4]. As technologies such as machine learning, natural language processing, and large language models continue to evolve, the scope of AI applications in nursing practice is expanding accordingly. For example, machine learning algorithms can be used to identify risks of adverse events such as pressure injuries [5], while natural language processing and AI-powered documentation systems can support the processing of nursing notes and the improvement of clinical documentation efficiency and quality [6, 7]. These developments suggest that AI is gradually shifting from a stand-alone technical tool to a new practice resource that is reshaping how nursing work is performed.

However, the adoption of AI in healthcare settings does not necessarily mean that it can be used safely or effectively. On the one hand, generative AI may produce factual errors and “hallucinations,” and the quality of its outputs depends heavily on the information provided by users and the data on which it is trained [8]. On the other hand, issues such as algorithmic bias, limited explainability, data privacy, and accountability mean that AI must be evaluated cautiously in high-risk clinical decision-making and practice [9]. At present, the rapid deployment and uptake of AI tools have clearly outpaced the development of systematic educational strategies, potentially leading to misuse, overreliance, erroneous conclusions, and other inappropriate applications, while further amplifying existing health inequities [10]. The World Health Organization has proposed that the use of AI in health should be guided by principles such as protecting autonomy, promoting well-being and safety, ensuring transparency and explainability, upholding responsibility and accountability, and advancing equity and inclusiveness [11]. Against this backdrop, AI literacy has emerged as a key concept linking technology to safe practice. It extends beyond the ability to use AI-based tools and also involves recognising and understanding AI, appreciating its capabilities and limitations, critically evaluating its outputs, and using it responsibly within ethical and privacy frameworks [12, 13]. Conceptual models of AI literacy further support this multidimensional understanding. Long and Magerko conceptualised AI literacy as a set of competencies that enable individuals to understand, evaluate, communicate with, and critically use AI technologies [14]. Similarly, Ng et al. proposed that AI literacy involves knowing and understanding AI, using and applying AI, evaluating and creating with AI, and considering ethical issues [15]. These models indicate that AI literacy should not be reduced to technical operation, but should be understood as a broader capability involving knowledge, use, evaluation, and ethical judgement.

In nursing practice, nurses’ AI literacy influences whether AI can be used not only as a convenient tool, but also as a safe, critical, and responsible resource embedded in nursing work [6]. Before AI literacy is manifested in practice, nurses’ baseline perceptions and readiness for AI integration provide an important starting point. Readiness in this context is not limited to willingness to adopt new technologies, but also involves nurses’ knowledge of AI, confidence in using AI-related technologies, perceived ability to adapt to AI-supported workflows, and awareness of related ethical and privacy issues. Although nurses may generally recognise AI’s potential to improve efficiency, enhance patient care quality, and alleviate workload and burnout, their AI-related knowledge may remain limited, and concerns about patient privacy and ethical challenges may continue to hinder AI integration in healthcare settings [16]. These findings suggest that nurses may be open to AI and perceive its potential value before they have developed sufficient knowledge, confidence, or critical judgement to use it safely in clinical contexts. In nursing education, there have been calls to systematically incorporate generative AI literacy into curricula and continuing education, emphasising that its core components include critical evaluation of AI-generated content, recognition of ethical implications, and responsible use, rather than operational skills alone [6]. In parallel, guidelines, consensus statements, and standards for AI use are emerging across the healthcare sector. A systematic review found inconsistencies in their methodological and reporting quality, suggesting that frontline practitioners need transferable literacy skills to support contextual judgement and risk management [13]. Qualitative research from nurses’ perspectives has also shown that, although nurses generally recognise the potential of AI to improve efficiency and support decision-making, they also highlight risks related to ethics, accountability, and insufficient training [17], further underscoring the urgency of strengthening nurses’ AI literacy. Existing quantitative studies indicate that clinical nurses’ AI literacy is generally at a moderate level and is influenced by factors such as age, training experience, use experience, information technology competence, and attitudes towards AI [18, 19]. Together, these findings suggest a transition from perceived readiness to actual literacy manifestations: nurses may begin to encounter, accept, and use AI before they have fully developed the judgement, verification skills, ethical awareness, and contextual understanding needed for responsible use. AI literacy therefore does not develop automatically with tool diffusion; rather, systematic training and context-sensitive support remain essential to promote its safe, appropriate, and effective use [20]. Moreover, substantial differences may exist among nurses in their opportunities to engage with AI, their use experience, level of understanding, confidence, and perception of risk, indicating that training strategies should not rely on a single uniform approach but instead be grounded in a fuller recognition of the heterogeneity of nurses’ AI literacy [21].

Although existing quantitative studies on nurses’ AI literacy have been able to describe its overall level and associated factors, they are often less able to capture how nurses differ in their understanding, use, judgement, and ethical responses to AI, or to explain the contextual factors and practice logics underlying these differences. AI literacy in nursing practice is also likely to be shaped by cultural and institutional contexts. Nurses’ opportunities to engage with AI, their trust in AI-generated information, their understanding of responsibility, and their expectations of professional roles may vary across healthcare systems, organisational resources, regulatory environments, and culturally embedded norms regarding clinician–patient relationships and professional accountability [11]. This is particularly relevant in clinical settings where AI tools are being introduced rapidly but formal guidance, training, and responsibility boundaries may still be developing.

This study was delimited to registered clinical nurses working in a tertiary general hospital in southwest China who had either used AI tools or had at least a basic understanding of AI applications and could discuss them in relation to clinical nursing contexts. The aim was not to measure AI literacy levels or develop a generalisable theory, but to provide a practice-based qualitative description of how AI literacy is manifested across cognition, use, critical thinking, and ethics, together with an additional attitudinal domain, and how these manifestations vary among clinical nurses.

Methods

Aims

The aim of this study was to explore how clinical nurses manifest AI literacy in practice and how these manifestations vary across cognition, use, critical thinking, and ethics, together with an additional attitudinal domain.

Study design

This study adopted a qualitative descriptive design. Qualitative descriptive research seeks to stay close to participants’ lived experiences and provides a straightforward account of the phenomenon with a relatively low level of interpretation, making it well suited to research questions that aim to understand how a phenomenon is manifested and differentiated in practice contexts [22]. As this study sought to describe variations in the manifestation of AI literacy in actual work settings from the perspective of clinical nurses, data were collected through semi-structured individual interviews and analysed using framework-based content analysis. The study process and reporting of findings followed the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) to enhance transparency and traceability [23].

Participants and data collection

Clinical nurses were recruited from a tertiary Grade A general hospital in southwest China using maximum variation purposive sampling. During sampling, factors including age, years of experience, educational background, professional title, role, and department type were taken into account to include nurses with diverse backgrounds and clinical experience, thereby enabling a richer description of differences in AI literacy among clinical nurses.

The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) currently employed registered nurses holding a valid nursing license; (2) at least 3 months of clinical nursing experience; (3) prior use of AI tools, or, if not regular users, at least a basic understanding of relevant AI applications and the ability to discuss them in relation to clinical contexts; and (4) good verbal communication skills and willingness to participate. The exclusion criteria were: (1) nurses in internship training or those who had not yet obtained a nursing license; and (2) those primarily engaged in administrative work and away from clinical practice for more than 6 months. Inclusion criterion (3) was specified to ensure that participants could provide information-rich accounts of AI-related experiences or perceptions in practice.

The interview guide was developed on the basis of the four dimensions underpinning the AI literacy scale proposed by Wang et al.—cognition, use, critical thinking, and ethics [12]. These four dimensions were treated as the core AI literacy domains in this study, as they reflect nurses’ understanding of AI, actual use, evaluation of AI-generated outputs, and ethical awareness in AI-supported practice. This operationalisation was also consistent with broader AI literacy frameworks, which conceptualise AI literacy as involving understanding, application, evaluation, and ethical judgement rather than technical operation alone [14, 15]. In addition, an attitudinal domain was incorporated to capture nurses’ overall openness, expectations, concerns, perceived replacement risk, and views on professional role change in relation to AI. This domain was not intended to replace or redefine the original AI literacy framework, nor was it treated as a literacy competency equivalent to the four core domains. Rather, it provided a contextual lens for understanding how AI literacy is manifested and negotiated in clinical nursing practice. Unlike cognition, which focused on understanding AI, or ethics, which focused on risk awareness and responsibility, the attitudinal domain focused on nurses’ psychological orientation towards AI and their positioning of AI in relation to work, responsibilities, and professional identity. The inclusion of attitude was also consistent with the qualitative descriptive aim of the study, which sought to explore not only what nurses knew or did with AI, but also how they positioned AI in relation to their work, responsibilities, and professional identity. The interview guide was reviewed and refined through research team discussions before formal data collection, and minor adjustments were made to the wording of several questions and probing strategies based on pilot interviews. Data were collected between 26 February and March 2026, after ethical approval had been obtained on 25 February 2026. Interviews were conducted one-to-one by the first author in a quiet and private setting. With participants’ consent, all interviews were audio-recorded and lasted 25–52 min, with a median duration of 31 min. Interviews were transcribed verbatim within 24 h and de-identified during transcription. Field observations and reflexive memos were also documented to capture the interview context and support subsequent analysis. Data collection and preliminary analysis proceeded concurrently. The final number of participants was determined by maximum variation sampling and data saturation rather than by a pre-specified sample size. Recruitment aimed to include nurses with variation in age, years of experience, educational background, professional title, role, and department type. After the 20th interview, no new major categories or subcategories were identified. Three additional interviews were then conducted to confirm category sufficiency and to ensure that the data sufficiently addressed the research questions. Data saturation was considered to have been achieved when additional interviews repeated existing category meanings and generated no new major categories or subcategories. In total, 23 face-to-face interviews were completed.

Data analysis

The data were analysed by the co-first author, who had a nursing background, had received training in qualitative research, and had relevant background knowledge of artificial intelligence. Reflexive notes were maintained throughout the study to attend to the potential influence of the researcher’s background on data interpretation. Interview data were analysed using framework-based content analysis, with data management and coding conducted in NVivo 11, following an inductive analytical process guided by a deductive framework [24].

First, the researcher repeatedly read the interview transcripts to become familiar with the data. The four core AI literacy domains—cognition, use, critical thinking, and ethics—together with the additional attitudinal domain were used as the initial analytical framework and top-level categories, and corresponding nodes were created in NVivo 11. Second, meaning units relevant to the research question were identified from the transcripts as the unit of analysis, condensed as necessary while preserving their original meaning, and assigned initial codes. During coding, data were first assigned to the most relevant dimension; when necessary, the same meaning unit could be double-coded across dimensions to preserve the complexity of the original context. Most meaning units were assigned to one primary analytical domain. Double-coding was used selectively rather than routinely, mainly when a participant’s account clearly crossed the boundaries of two domains. Such instances occurred most often between use and critical thinking when participants described using AI while also checking its outputs, between critical thinking and ethics when verification was linked to patient safety or responsibility, and between ethics and attitude when concerns about privacy, accountability, or replacement reflected broader orientations towards AI. In these cases, the primary domain was determined according to the main analytical focus of the meaning unit, while the secondary code was retained to preserve contextual complexity. Interpretive disagreements mainly concerned the most appropriate domain assignment or the boundary between adjacent subcategories. These disagreements were resolved by returning to the original transcript, comparing the meaning unit with the category definitions, reviewing similar coded examples, and reaching consensus through research team discussion. Third, open coding and inductive categorisation were undertaken within each dimension, and conceptually similar codes were grouped into subcategories. The codebook and category definitions were continuously refined through ongoing discussions within the research team. Content that could not be accommodated within the existing framework was initially coded under an “emergent themes” node and subsequently discussed to determine whether new subcategories should be created or whether the original framework required refinement. Finally, the research team repeatedly compared and reviewed the category structure, the boundaries between subcategories, and their meanings to produce the final analytical results, with representative quotations used to illustrate the main characteristics of each subcategory. An illustrative example of the coding pathway for the cognition domain is provided in Supplementary File 3.

Rigour and trustworthiness

This study adhered to the principles of rigour for qualitative content analysis proposed by Graneheim and Lundman [25] and was guided by Lincoln and Guba’s criteria of credibility, transferability, dependability, and confirmability [26]. To enhance credibility, the research team continuously compared data across participants during data collection and analysis and made interpretive judgements by drawing on interview transcripts, field observations, and analytical memos. At the initial stage, three researchers independently conducted trial coding on selected interview transcripts and developed a preliminary codebook through comparison and discussion. Subsequently, two researchers undertook the main coding of the interview data, and disagreements were resolved using the procedures described in the Data analysis section. Representative quotations were included in the findings to improve the transparency of both the analytical process and the interpretation of the results.

To support transferability, the study provides detailed descriptions of the research context, participant characteristics, sampling strategy, data collection process, and analytical procedures, enabling readers to assess the applicability of the findings to similar settings. To ensure dependability, records of design modifications, coding iterations, and category development were retained throughout the study, forming a relatively complete audit trail. To strengthen confirmability, reflexive notes were maintained throughout data collection and analysis. The research team consisted of nursing researchers and clinical nurses with experience in qualitative research and varying degrees of familiarity with AI tools. The co-first author, who led the data analysis, had a nursing background and a research interest in AI-related issues in nursing. The team recognised that their professional background and interest in this topic might sensitise them to the potential usefulness of AI in nursing, while their nursing training might also lead them to pay particular attention to patient safety, verification, responsibility, and ethical risk. In particular, the researchers reflected on how their nursing backgrounds might influence the interpretation of the ethics and responsibility categories, including a possible tendency to emphasise nurses’ responsibility for checking AI-generated information before applying it to patient care. During interviews, the interviewer used open-ended questions and avoided presenting AI as either beneficial or harmful. During analysis, interpretations, especially those related to ethics and responsibility, were repeatedly checked against the original transcripts and discussed within the research team to ensure that they remained grounded in participants’ accounts rather than in the researchers’ professional assumptions. In addition, preliminary findings were returned to five participants for member checking. A brief summary of the categories, subcategories, and key interpretations was discussed with them face to face, and they were invited to comment on whether the findings reflected their experiences and whether any interpretation appeared unclear or inconsistent. The participants generally agreed that the findings were consistent with their experiences, and no major disagreement with the categories, subcategories, or main interpretations was raised.

Ethical considerations

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Ethics Committee of Xinqiao Hospital, Army Medical University (approval number: 2026-No.067 − 01; approval date: 25 February 2026). Before data collection began, all participants were provided with full study information, including the study purpose, data collection procedures, data storage and management, anonymisation, and confidentiality measures, and written informed consent was obtained from each participant. Participants were informed that their participation was entirely voluntary and that they could withdraw from the study at any time without giving a reason and without any impact on their work or related rights and interests. All interviews were audio-recorded and de-identified during transcription. The research data were used for research purposes only, stored in encrypted form by designated members of the research team, and made accessible only on a restricted basis. In the published paper, quotations are presented using participant codes only to ensure that neither the participants nor their institutions can be identified. Consent for publication was obtained from all participants for the use of anonymised quotations and de-identified data in publications.

Findings

A total of 23 clinical nurses were included, ranging in age from 22 to 46 years (median: 32 years) and with 1 to 25 years of work experience (median: 8 years). Most participants were female (n = 18), held a bachelor’s degree (n = 17), and worked as frontline staff nurses (n = 19). Participants were drawn from a range of clinical settings, including medical, surgical, specialty, and intensive care units, ensuring diversity in professional background and experience. Category 1: Cognition

Sources shaping nurses’ understanding of AI

Nurses’ understanding of AI was shaped by a combination of external information sources and individual exploration. Participants reported that formal learning opportunities, such as hospital training sessions, in-house lectures, and academic conferences, helped introduce them to AI-related knowledge; however, these channels were often limited in scope and mainly served to raise initial awareness. In contrast, informal sources—including online recommendations, media coverage, short-video platforms, and information shared by others—were more commonly involved in shaping their understanding and were characterised by greater immediacy and fragmentation. In addition, exchanges with colleagues, word-of-mouth communication, and cross-disciplinary influences from family members further broadened nurses’ exposure to AI. On this basis, some participants also engaged in active searching, experimentation, and self-directed exploration driven by personal interest or work-related needs, through which their understanding gradually developed from a vague impression into a clearer one.

The hospital had provided some training. We were added to a group specifically for that training. Last year, when they organised learning related to nursing and DeepSeek, we were included in it. P20

I do not really understand concepts like large language models. I learned a lot of it through conversations with my husband. He is quite interested in computers, so when these topics came up in our casual chats, I would ask more questions and gradually developed some understanding. P1

Perceived usefulness outweighed understanding of technical principles

Participants generally showed an initial recognition of the practical value of AI. They were able to perceive its usefulness in healthcare and nursing work and had formed relatively intuitive understandings of where it could be applied. Some nurses noted that AI already had a certain foundation of application in areas such as medication and disease information retrieval, presentation preparation, patient education, nursing rounds, ultrasound diagnosis, robotic surgery, and integration into hospital information systems. Its advantages were seen particularly in improving convenience, integrating information, and reducing human workload. At the same time, some participants observed that the implementation of AI in hospitals tended to be specialised and that its level of development varied across hospitals and regions. Further uptake was perceived to remain constrained by practical conditions such as technical infrastructure, financial investment, and resource support.

However, compared with their intuitive sense that AI was “useful” and “convenient,” participants’ understanding of how it works, relevant technical terminology, and differences between AI technologies was generally limited. Many nurses said they had “heard of” terms such as large language models, algorithms, and neural networks, but did not really understand what they meant. Others attempted to explain AI in experiential terms, describing it as “big data capture,” “database integration,” or “like searching in a browser and then organising the answer.” Yet such understandings largely remained at the level of analogy and intuitive impression. Some participants understood AI as a process of collecting, filtering, and organising existing data, while others found it difficult to determine which applications should genuinely be considered AI. This suggests that their understanding of AI’s capabilities, boundaries, and technical types remained somewhat blurred.

Overall, clinical nurses’ understanding of AI appeared to have moved from complete unfamiliarity to some degree of exposure and preliminary recognition of its value. However, this understanding was grounded more in user experience, observations of practical applications, and external informational influences than in systematic and in-depth technical knowledge. In other words, participants had a relatively clear sense of AI’s practical value and functional usefulness, but a more limited understanding of its underlying principles, specialised terminology, and boundaries of application.

Its role? For me at the moment, it is mainly useful for looking up medications and diseases, especially conditions I am less familiar with, including those from other specialties. It can also help with tasks such as preparing teaching materials. P9

My understanding of how AI works is that it draws on big data by capturing a large body of existing knowledge, bringing it together for synthesis and analysis, and then extracting the information we want. P13

Differences in understanding shaped subsequent use and judgement

Differences in participants’ understanding of AI did not remain at the level of abstract views, but further shaped their training needs, ways of positioning AI in practice, and attitudes towards risk. Some nurses, based on their prior exposure to and use of AI, gradually came to realise that relying on AI alone could not truly solve problems and might even constrain their thinking. As a result, their understanding of AI shifted over time—from initially seeing it as a tool that could be directly relied upon to viewing it instead as an auxiliary resource that required active human guidance and mobilisation. Other participants reported that they judged whether an AI system was “useful” based on their user experience, such as whether it could understand prompts and help organise content, while also noting that clear and consistent criteria for such judgement were still lacking.

These differences in understanding also shaped participants’ expectations regarding both the content and format of training. Some nurses felt that AI had not yet been given sufficient attention in current clinical training and that proposals to add relevant courses might even be dismissed as too basic to warrant serious consideration. Others expressed a desire to learn from higher-level academic conferences in order to access broader and more advanced experience from different regions and to better understand the logic and ways of thinking involved in AI use. Still others placed greater emphasis on practical forms of training, arguing that purely online learning could easily remain superficial, whereas face-to-face training, live demonstrations, hands-on practice, and ready-to-use prompt templates were more helpful for understanding and mastering AI tools.

Differences in how deeply nurses understood AI and what aspects they focused on also appeared to influence how they judged the boundaries of misuse and the seriousness of related risks. Some nurses adopted a relatively permissive view of AI misuse, believing that there was little need for excessive concern as long as it did not involve illegality or threaten safety or survival. In their view, some of the more serious risks fell within the domain of national regulation and legal governance rather than being issues that required primary attention in routine clinical use.

…When I first started using AI, I felt that I could rely on it completely and tended to follow its line of thinking. Later, I realised that I was being constrained by it, because I could only think along the path it suggested. Category 2: Use

Motivations for using AI

Participants commonly reported that their use of AI was primarily driven by practical needs arising in their work. Convenience was one of the most frequently mentioned motivations. Nurses explained that when they encountered problems in their work and were temporarily unable to find answers, they would often turn to AI first because it offered a quick and convenient way to obtain the information they needed. Some participants also emphasised that one major advantage of AI was that it allowed them to seek help without “disturbing other people”; even when dealing with basic, trivial, or repeatedly confirmed questions, they could ask AI at any time without worrying about bothering colleagues or supervisors.

Problem-solving was another important motivation for using AI. Participants noted that when uncertainties arose in clinical work, when they were unsure about specific content, or when they needed to supplement information or organise their thoughts, they often used AI for information searching and preliminary organisation. In these situations, AI was more often regarded as an immediate source of informational support that could quickly provide leads and help them think further or carry out subsequent verification. Some nurses also noted that AI could add new information to their existing line of thinking, broaden their perspective, and compensate for gaps in their knowledge and judgement. In this sense, AI was not only a tool for “finding answers,” but also a resource for supporting thinking and filling individual knowledge gaps.

In addition, nurses’ motivations for using AI varied according to the nature of the task. Some participants considered AI particularly useful for supportive tasks such as writing, teaching preparation, and presentation development. A few also mentioned that their initial engagement with AI had been prompted by novelty or recommendations from people around them; in other words, they began experimenting with AI out of curiosity or by following broader trends as AI tools became more widely discussed and circulated.

When I run into a problem, I think of AI. I might ask it what a disease is and how it is usually treated. The main reason is that it is convenient. P18

When I feel that my own view is not comprehensive enough, I may look it up and see how other people think, then synthesise those perspectives, because I feel my own limitations are quite substantial. P5

Experiences of using AI

Participants’ experiences of using AI were generally characterised by a sense of duality. On the one hand, AI was widely experienced as a convenient and efficient tool that could rapidly integrate information, generate relatively comprehensive answers, and reduce workload in tasks such as information gathering, framework development, and idea generation. For some nurses, this sense of speed and completeness constituted the most immediate impression of using AI.

On the other hand, this positive experience was clearly conditional. Participants repeatedly emphasised that AI was better suited to information collection, preliminary organisation, and logical structuring than to directly replacing professional judgement. Particularly in situations involving patient safety, content accuracy, or strong clinical contextuality, nurses were more inclined to treat AI as an auxiliary tool, with final decisions still relying on human checking, filtering, and revision. This pattern of human-led, AI-assisted collaboration was one of the most prominent features in participants’ accounts of their user experience. Participants were also clearly aware of AI’s limitations. Although its outputs were generated quickly, they were often perceived as superficial in rigorous, complex, or highly individualised nursing situations, making it difficult for them to fit specific cases or meet high-precision demands. When AI responses failed to match expectations, participants also described a clear sense of frustration.

These limitations further led some nurses to recognise that a good user experience did not depend solely on the tool itself, but also on whether the user possessed the necessary knowledge base, prompting skills, and independent thinking ability. Participants generally believed that AI could function effectively as an aid only when users already had a certain level of professional knowledge; otherwise, its outputs were difficult to interpret and use appropriately. A few participants also noted that AI did not always produce one-directional gains in efficiency. In some clinical application scenarios, although it could improve accuracy, it could also generate additional workload because of device lag, extra operational steps, or patients’ subsequent questioning after using AI themselves.

For example, as I just mentioned, the system itself may have problems. It may even end up increasing our workload. I cannot think of anything else for the moment, but I feel that even if it provides many answers, especially when precision is required or patient safety is involved, human verification is still essential. I think you need to think for yourself first before relying on AI, rather than turning to it from the very beginning. If you start by using AI without thinking anything through yourself, you may get part of what you need, but I do not think it will ever be comprehensive enough, because it lacks your own thinking. Cross-context use and variations in depth of engagement

Participants reported that the contexts in which AI was used had gradually expanded from single-task applications to multiple work- and life-related situations, although work-related purposes still predominated overall. The most common uses were concentrated in searching for patient education materials, document editing, presentation preparation, information retrieval, academic reading, preparation for nursing rounds, and searching for disease-related knowledge. For some nurses, AI had become a support tool they would turn to whenever they encountered a problem in their daily work, mainly serving functions such as information retrieval, content integration, structure generation, and idea support.

In clinically related contexts, the role of AI was more often one of indirect support rather than direct substitution for nursing practice. Participants commonly used it to help organise nursing problems during rounds, supplement postoperative rehabilitation and patient education content, and quickly retrieve information on unfamiliar diseases or treatments. A small number of participants also mentioned guiding patients to use AI to obtain information on postoperative rehabilitation, nutritional support, exercise training, or stoma care, suggesting that AI use had extended from “nurses using it themselves” to “supporting patient learning.”

At the same time, the data showed marked differences in depth of use across participants. Some nurses had become relatively proficient in the basic functions of different tools and were able to select appropriate platforms according to the task, using them to generate outlines, analyse literature, check wording, or support learning. Others, however, still used AI at a relatively superficial level, mainly as a reference tool for obtaining preliminary information, broadening their knowledge, or stimulating ideas, with less exploration of its more complex functions. This variation in depth of use appeared to be closely related to how necessary AI was perceived to be in one’s work. For some participants, AI had been incorporated quite frequently into their daily work and learning processes; for others, it remained an auxiliary tool used only when needed and was not seen as indispensable, leaving little motivation to continue learning or to develop a deeper mastery of its operation.

Sometimes I use my personal DeepSeek account to look up materials related to documentation or patient education, because its editing functions are much more intelligent than doing it myself. It can also help with things like formatting and smart layout. P1

I think that, in healthcare and nursing, AI is most useful for patients’ postoperative rehabilitation guidance. Patients can be guided to use AI so that they can access information on rehabilitation and nutritional support more quickly and conveniently. In our current clinical practice, especially in areas such as breast function exercises and stoma care, we often guide patients to learn more about this kind of information. P10

Barriers to AI use and adaptive strategies

Participants commonly noted that using AI was not simply a matter of “knowing how to ask,” but instead involved a certain threshold of competence. The most prominent barriers were unclear questioning, difficulty in using prompts to express needs precisely, and limited understanding of relevant terminology and functional logic. When a question was not framed accurately enough, AI responses were likely to deviate from expectations, making it difficult for users to obtain the information they actually needed. Some participants therefore attributed unsatisfactory use to “not knowing how to ask” or “not knowing how to ask better.” Beyond the tool itself, participants also linked these barriers to insufficient personal knowledge and cognitive foundations. In their view, using AI more effectively first required clarity about one’s own goals—knowing what one wanted to ask and what one hoped to obtain—and the usefulness of AI depended to a large extent on the user’s professional background and ability to articulate their needs.

At the organisational level, participants repeatedly pointed to insufficient training support. Although some nurses had attended AI-related training provided by their hospitals, they generally felt that the content was overly technical and terminology-driven, with limited connection to actual clinical use scenarios, making it difficult to translate into everyday practical competence. Rather than training that simply explained principles, they expressed a stronger preference for hands-on, practice-oriented learning that was closely aligned with nursing work. In response to these barriers, participants gradually developed a range of adaptive strategies. Common approaches included breaking complex needs down into keywords or outline-style prompts, progressively refining questions by adding information, adjusting wording, and repeatedly following up in order to move closer to the desired answer. Some participants also deliberately assigned roles or scenarios to obtain responses that better matched their needs. This suggests that nurses’ use of AI was not a one-step process of “asking a question and receiving an answer,” but rather one of continuous trial, revision, and optimisation through which use gradually became more effective.

Age, learning habits, and acceptance of digital technology also appeared to influence the extent to which AI was mastered. Compared with younger nurses, some more senior participants were more likely to remain at a relatively superficial stage of use and to view AI as a general retrieval tool rather than an intelligent system with which more interactive and optimised use was possible.

Sometimes I am not sure how to formulate prompts properly, and when the response does not meet my expectations, I wonder whether the problem lies in how I phrased the prompt and how I could adjust it to reach the intended outcome. Also, when I ask many questions in a day, it becomes difficult to scroll back and find earlier answers. P4

I feel that level of understanding is too specialised and would be very difficult to achieve. So, from my perspective as a user, I would just extract some keywords from my ideas and use them to formulate a question. P2

Category 3: Critical thinking

Critical thinking within cautious acceptance

When using AI, participants generally did not accept its outputs at face value, but instead demonstrated a degree of cautious judgement. Most nurses noted that AI-generated content could only serve as a reference. Especially in relation to key steps, important data, or highly specialised issues, they believed that personal judgement still needed to be applied, and that the information should only be used after verification through checking, comparison, or returning to original sources.

Participants not only considered the conclusions provided by AI, but also examined whether the sources were reliable, whether citations were authentic, whether the reasoning process was sound, and whether the information might be taken out of context, incomplete, or beyond the scope of their own professional judgement. For some nurses, AI was useful for providing a relatively comprehensive overall framework, but its content still often required further human scrutiny in terms of detail, professional rigour, and completeness. Participants were also highly alert to the possibility of AI errors and “hallucinations.” Some nurses explicitly pointed out that AI could generate fabricated references, incorrect data, or misleading answers. Once they found the content suspicious, they would turn to guidelines, journal articles, professional books, or professional consultation. Some also cross-checked outputs across different large language models or compared them against their own prior clinical experience to judge whether the information was trustworthy.

However, this critical stance was not always stable. The data suggested that some participants were also aware that when AI responses aligned closely with their expectations and were presented in a sufficiently complete manner, users might accept erroneous content without adequate discernment and thus be misled. For this reason, many nurses repeatedly emphasised that AI should not be fully trusted in long-term use. Its value lay more in providing direction than in replacing independent human judgement.

Then I look through its whole search process to see how it analysed the information. After that, I check whether the analysis is correct before using it. It is not something I would consider entirely correct. P1

I have questioned it. Sometimes I feel that what it says does not match what I have encountered or observed, so I use different AI apps to cross-check whether the information is accurate. I also consider whether what it tells me is actually suitable in my own real-life context. P7

Factors shaping critical thinking

The development of critical thinking appeared to be shaped jointly by the professional demands of nursing work, awareness of AI’s limitations, and personal experience of use. Nurses generally regarded nursing practice as requiring a high degree of rigour, particularly when AI-generated outputs related to patient education, nursing decision-making, or the communication of professional information. In such situations, the accuracy of the content and the reliability of its sources were considered to require careful verification. This professional orientation inclined participants to maintain a cautious stance towards AI rather than to accept its answers directly.

Some participants indicated that critical judgement arose from an awareness of their own uncertainty. Because they could not fully confirm the truthfulness, accuracy, or applicability of AI-generated information, they tended to retain a degree of scepticism and reduce the risk of misjudgement by consulting multiple sources and using cross-verification. Previous experiences of encountering incorrect or inaccurate AI outputs were also an important source of critical thinking. Some participants reported having found AI-generated answers to be wrong, superficial, or outdated in actual use. These negative experiences weakened their default trust in AI and made them more likely to consult multiple sources and repeatedly verify information before adopting it in later use.

Participants also recognised that the value of AI outputs depended not only on the tool itself, but also on whether the user possessed the ability to evaluate them appropriately. Particularly in healthcare and nursing contexts, even when presented with the same input, nurses still needed to draw on their professional knowledge to judge which information was suitable to share with patients, which points required emphasis, and which content might be incorrect or inapplicable.

It is not that I do not trust it; I just need to check whether its sources are really what I am looking for and whether they are correct, because this is something that requires a high degree of rigour. P23

It is because of my own lack of understanding, as I cannot tell whether what it gives me is actually correct or not. P16

Assessing boundaries of applicability and potential risks

Participants generally questioned whether AI could truly meet the individualised needs of nursing practice. They believed that although AI could rapidly identify information and provide standardised suggestions, its outputs often resembled “general answers” generated from common patterns of data and were therefore insufficiently responsive to patients’ specific conditions, individual differences, and real-life circumstances. Especially in relation to personalised care planning, judgement in specialist contexts, and tailored patient guidance, AI was seen as more suitable for an auxiliary role than for replacing the targeted judgements made by nurses on the basis of clinical experience.

Some participants further pointed out that whether AI-generated content could be adopted also depended on whether it was grounded in authoritative and standardised sources. Compared with broad or generic responses, nurses placed greater value on whether the information aligned with professional standards, expert consensus, or other recognised forms of evidence, so as to avoid misleading patients or compromising judgement because of poorly founded information sources. In their view, the value of AI lay not only in its ability to provide an answer, but also in whether that answer was supported by accepted standards and could be taken up in professional practice.

It may be affected by individual differences, because the data it provides may be based on groups, whereas what you are actually dealing with is an individual, and that is not the same. This kind of individual variation may create potential risks. People differ from one another, so there still needs to be a subjective judgement based on each patient’s specific situation. P20

Everything should follow established standards, such as group standards or expert consensus, so that the information patients retrieve is basically standardised. P10

Category 4: Ethics

Limited awareness of privacy risks and boundaries of responsible use

Participants’ responses to privacy protection were neither uniformly highly vigilant nor uniformly inattentive. Rather, they reflected a combination of limited risk awareness, pragmatically oriented judgement, reliance on institutional trust, and growing expectations for governance. Some nurses showed relatively low sensitivity to privacy risks in AI use, believing that most of what they entered consisted of general work-related content rather than important secrets; therefore, even if a risk of disclosure existed, it would not necessarily constitute a serious problem. Others openly acknowledged that they had not seriously reflected on AI-related ethical issues and understood data privacy mainly in terms of whether directly identifiable information was provided, assuming that it was relatively safe as long as names or identity details were not entered. This limited vigilance appeared closely related to their broader perception of today’s digital environment. Some participants felt that, in the era of big data, personal information was already highly transparent or even “already leaked,” and that AI merely intensified this sense. As a result, some nurses displayed a degree of tacit acceptance or resigned adaptation to privacy leakage and tended to judge the seriousness of ethical risk according to whether it caused tangible harm to personal interests or involved property or safety concerns.

However, participants did not entirely overlook privacy issues. Some nurses had already recognised that they themselves could become a conduit for privacy breaches during AI use, particularly when patient conditions, laboratory data, or other sensitive information were entered into a system. Even when names, identity card numbers, or other directly identifiable details were not included, they believed that patient privacy boundaries could still be implicated. A small number of participants also reported having encountered information leakage in actual use, which further intensified their practical concerns about data security. At the same time, the data showed that some nurses placed considerable trust in institutional mechanisms for privacy protection. They expressed greater confidence in local deployment, internal hospital networks, and the security mechanisms of hospitals and the state, believing that port isolation, firewalls, and localised deployment could reduce risks to some extent. Yet this trust was not unconditional. While recognising hospital- and state-level safeguards, some participants still worried about post-breach accountability and the possibility that AI might in future be misused, manipulated, or applied beyond appropriate regulatory boundaries.

Some participants had already begun to consider AI ethics from a governance perspective. In their view, as AI continues to develop, privacy protection, ethical norms, laws and regulations, and dedicated regulatory mechanisms all need to be strengthened in parallel. In healthcare settings, they believed that concern should extend beyond whether data might be leaked to include patients’ wishes, the boundaries of information disclosure, and whether AI-generated analyses were appropriate to communicate directly to patients. These findings suggest that nurses’ ethical understanding was no longer confined to the question of whether privacy might be breached, but had begun to extend to how AI should be regulated and used responsibly.

In this respect, I feel relatively reassured, because the hospital has certainly put firewall protections in place. One reason why our hospital has so many separate systems is that they are not fully interconnected, precisely because of security considerations. So, since this version of DeepSeek is locally deployed, I believe those issues must already have been taken into account. P2

There is still a risk of leakage, but people generally do not seem to care that much. They feel that, in this broader environment, privacy has already been compromised anyway—you can tell from the number of nuisance calls people receive every day. P9

Nurses as the primary bearers of responsibility

Within the ethical dimension, participants showed a relatively consistent view regarding responsibility for the consequences of AI use: nurses remained the primary responsible party. Although a small number of participants felt that the extent of responsibility should vary according to the specific circumstances, severity of harm, and consequences of the event, most nurses believed overall that once AI-related information was used in nursing practice and ultimately led to a problem, responsibility should still lie mainly with the nurse. This attribution of responsibility was grounded first in how participants understood the nature of AI as a tool. They generally defined AI as an auxiliary tool that could provide reference information but could not replace nurses in making clinical judgements. Precisely because AI was not regarded as the “true decision-maker,” nor as an independently accountable subject, responsibility was not seen as shifting away from nurses simply because AI had been introduced.

Participants repeatedly emphasised that, in the use of AI, nurses were not only executors but also reviewers and supervisors. In their view, nursing work itself requires a high degree of rigour, and nurses are expected to assess and verify the correctness, applicability, and safety of AI-generated information before adopting it. Therefore, even if an error originated from AI output, responsibility remained unavoidable if the nurse had used that information without adequate verification. Although participants differed in how they estimated the proportion of responsibility—with some suggesting 60%, others 70%–80%, and some even full responsibility—these differences reflected variation in the degree of responsibility rather than in who the responsible party was.

As the human actor remains the primary subject, responsibility is unavoidable. The information provided by AI is meant to assist us, not to decide for us what to do, so in clinical work we still need to verify the information it provides. Because we are the ones who ultimately carry out the action, responsibility still lies with us; as the primary subject remains human, that responsibility cannot be avoided. P13

I do not think AI can bear responsibility; in the end, it is still the person who has to take responsibility, because if you use it, you should verify it yourself. P14

Category 5: Attitude

Expectations of empowerment alongside concerns about dependence

Participants generally held positive views towards AI, but this positivity did not amount to unreserved acceptance; rather, it reflected a relatively complex attitudinal structure shaped by expectations, dependence, and concern. Most nurses first acknowledged AI’s potential to reduce workload and empower nursing practice, believing that it could improve efficiency in areas such as patient education, information integration, knowledge acquisition, nursing documentation, workflow reminders, and the handling of repetitive tasks. In doing so, AI was seen as helping to reduce workload and, to some extent, allowing nurses to devote more time and energy to patient care. For some participants, AI was not only a time-saving tool, but also a source of support for personal growth, knowledge expansion, and optimisation of work practices.

Some participants also expected that, as technology develops, AI might not only improve efficiency but also create new possibilities for patient follow-up, emotion recognition, and support for humanistic care. This positive attitude was also reflected in a willingness to learn. Some nurses explicitly stated that they were willing to invest additional time in learning AI-related skills in exchange for greater efficiency in the future. Others felt that, although the introduction of AI had brought new learning pressures and adaptation challenges, this was a necessary transition for nurses, and that only by adapting proactively could they better realise their professional value in future practice.

At the same time, participants’ attitudes were not simply optimistic. Many nurses also expressed concern about overreliance on AI, noting that while it was convenient, it might gradually lead people to stop thinking, reduce their willingness to explore independently, and even foster a habit of “letting AI generate everything first.” They worried that if users adopted AI outputs directly without sufficient judgement and reflection, this might not only weaken individual thinking ability but also reduce their sense of responsibility and contribute to technological misuse.

During rounds, I already have to check the patient’s tubes once, then come back and document it in the nursing record, and then hand it over again at shift change. Yes, it is repetitive work, and I hope AI can be applied to these kinds of tasks. P9

That is possible. Some people may feel that they finally no longer need to think because something can give them the answers, and once that happens, they may gradually stop thinking altogether, which would be a step backwards. P20

Concerns about replacement alongside viewing AI as supportive

Although some nurses acknowledged that, with technological development, AI might replace certain simple, repetitive, and mechanical aspects of nursing work, most did not believe that it could fully replace nurses. They generally pointed out that AI still had clear limitations in dealing with complex clinical problems, carrying out procedural tasks, and improving actual work efficiency. Its capabilities were seen as especially difficult to compare with those of humans in nursing situations characterised by substantial individual variation, rapidly changing conditions, and the need for immediate judgement. This view that AI was “not fully replaceable” was grounded largely in participants’ understanding of the nature of nursing work. In their view, nursing involves not only the application of knowledge and the execution of procedures, but also substantial practical skills, clinical assessment, real-time judgement, and communication and interaction with patients. AI was therefore seen as better suited to an auxiliary role than to becoming a genuine substitute. For most nurses, AI could assist with documentation, analysis, education, and some process-related tasks, but final execution, verification, and responsibility still rested with human professionals.

Participants were not entirely free of replacement-related anxiety. Some nurses were genuinely concerned that, as AI takes on more tasks, the demand for nursing positions might decline, thereby affecting their sense of job security. At the same time, this concern did not simply translate into rejection. Rather, it led them to position themselves more as operators, users, and managers of technology, and to view learning and adaptation as ways of achieving role transformation. They hoped that AI would take over more tedious, mechanical, and repetitive work, thereby reducing nurses’ burden and enabling them to return more fully to patients and take on work of greater professional and caring value. Some participants even believed that this shift might promote the nursing profession towards greater refinement, specialisation, and quality.

Nevertheless, the humanistic care, emotional support, communication, and companionship involved in nursing are difficult for AI to truly replace. Participants generally believed that patients are not only confronted with disease and procedures, but are also people who need to be understood, comforted, and responded to. Because AI lacks “human warmth,” this was seen as a fundamental reason why the role of nurses remains irreplaceable.

I am not worried about that. I do not think the relationship with AI is one of replacement; rather, I see it as something mutually enhancing. I think it can provide us with support, but it will not replace us, because in clinical work we are caring for patients, and patients are human beings. Machines are not human, and although AI is ‘intelligent’ and may be fed by big data, I think it still cannot really provide human warmth. P18

In other areas, such as treatment and nursing care, I do not really hope that AI will replace us. What I would prefer is for it to take over some of the more tedious and complicated tasks we deal with every day, so that nurses can truly return to patients and focus on the work of healthcare professionals. I would rather see AI support us in improving work efficiency. P6

Perceived potential to improve clinician-patient relationships alongside relational concerns

The impact of AI on clinician–patient relationships was seen as dual in nature. On the one hand, some nurses expressed hope that AI could help improve these relationships by enhancing information transparency, supporting intelligent consultation, providing discharge guidance, and offering supplementary explanations. In their view, such functions could help alleviate tensions arising from insufficient communication, inadequate patient education, and information asymmetry, thereby promoting mutual understanding between clinicians and patients to some extent.

On the other hand, more participants simultaneously expressed clear concerns. They pointed out that, after using AI, patients might regard its outputs as more “standard” or “authoritative” than the explanations provided by healthcare professionals and might therefore question the interpretations of clinicians or nurses. When AI-generated outputs were inconsistent with clinical judgement, patients’ trust in healthcare professionals could decline and even give rise to new clinician–patient conflicts. These concerns were also linked to patients’ limited ability to critically appraise AI outputs and to the ambiguity of responsibility attribution. Participants noted that patients might not fully understand the conditions of applicability and limitations of AI-generated information; once misled, any resulting conflict would ultimately fall back on healthcare professionals. At the same time, if errors occurred after AI had been involved in judgement or nursing support, unclear boundaries of responsibility could further intensify the risk of disputes.

I think conflicts between healthcare professionals and patients often arise from a lack of information sharing. Patients may not know what we are doing and may not understand many aspects of their care. If things could be made more transparent and more of their questions could be addressed, I think that would help reduce such conflicts. P17

In terms of clinician–patient relationships, I think there may be some negative effects. Many patients also use DeepSeek or other AI tools to look things up, and they may find that the answers differ from what doctors tell them. What a doctor says is shaped by individual judgement, so different people may explain things differently and patients may also understand them differently. By contrast, AI tends to provide what appears to be a standardised answer. Once patients see that, it may raise issues of trust in clinician–patient relationships. P20

Discussion

This study revealed the multidimensional and heterogeneous manifestations of AI literacy among clinical nurses. Unlike previous quantitative studies that have mainly examined overall AI literacy levels or associated factors [27–29], the present study suggests that AI literacy in clinical nursing is not simply a matter of skill level, but a practice-based capability shaped by use experience, risk judgement, responsibility awareness, and professional role understanding. The findings show that nurses’ relationship with AI is not one of straightforward acceptance or rejection, but an ongoing process of human–AI interaction, negotiation, and adjustment in clinical practice.

This interpretation is also supported by evidence from different geographical and practice contexts. A recent systematic review of nursing students and practising nurses showed that AI adoption in nursing is often characterised by guarded optimism, moderate literacy and readiness, and persistent infrastructural, ethical, educational, and governance-related barriers [30]. Similar patterns have been reported among nurses in Turkey and Iran, where nurses generally recognised the potential value of AI but still showed limited knowledge, uneven readiness, and concerns about privacy or ethical issues [16, 31]. Qualitative evidence from registered nurses in Saudi Arabia further indicates that AI adoption is influenced by organisational support, training opportunities, technical barriers, ethical concerns, and perceived threats to professional autonomy [32]. Evidence from nursing students in Saudi Arabia also suggests that receptiveness to AI can emerge before clinical employment, but readiness remains moderate and requires structured educational preparation [33]. Compared with these studies, the present study provides a hospital-based qualitative account from clinical nurses in China and further shows how AI literacy is enacted in everyday nursing work through utility-based use, cautious verification, responsibility attribution, and professional role negotiation. These comparisons suggest that AI literacy should not be understood as a uniform or context-free competency, but as a context-sensitive capability shaped by clinical setting, organisational resources, educational exposure, cultural expectations of professional responsibility, and the maturity of AI governance. The tertiary Grade A hospital context of this study may have further shaped these manifestations. Compared with many lower-level or primary healthcare settings, tertiary hospitals in China are more likely to have stronger specialist resources, digital information systems, opportunities for professional training, and exposure to emerging technologies. This may partly explain why many participants had at least some awareness of AI and why their accounts frequently referred to hospital-led training, local deployment of AI tools, hospital information systems, documentation, patient education, and information retrieval. At the same time, this context may also have shaped participants’ discussions of responsibility and governance, as their concerns were often expressed in relation to institutional safeguards, local deployment, and hospital-level regulation. Therefore, the findings should be interpreted within the context of a relatively resource-rich tertiary hospital. Nurses in primary care, community hospitals, rural settings, or institutions with limited digital infrastructure may have different opportunities, barriers, and AI literacy needs.

This study found that clinical nurses’ understanding of AI was grounded more in perceived practical value and actual user experience than in a systematic understanding of underlying technical principles. Most participants were able to recognise the practical usefulness of AI in information retrieval, patient education, presentation preparation, knowledge integration, and idea generation, but had a relatively limited understanding of algorithms, models, technical terminology, and the boundaries of AI capability. This finding is consistent with previous research suggesting that, although nursing populations generally hold positive attitudes towards AI and show considerable interest in it, the depth of their understanding, extent of use experience, and level of preparedness remain uneven [34]. Notably, the present study further points to a “tool-first” pattern in the development of AI literacy. In this pattern, nurses’ initial engagement with AI appeared to begin less from formal conceptual learning and more from concrete work or learning tasks. Perceived usefulness served as the main entry point: nurses first judged AI according to whether it could help them solve immediate problems, save time, organise information, or support routine work. Through repeated use, however, they also encountered limitations such as superficial responses, inaccurate or unverifiable outputs, insufficient individualisation, and dependence on the quality of prompts. These experiences appeared to prompt further reflection on AI’s boundaries, the need for verification, and the importance of maintaining human judgement. Therefore, the development of AI literacy among clinical nurses may not fully follow a linear path of learning theory first and applying it later; rather, it may be a practice-based process in which functional use, recognition of limitations, and reflective judgement gradually interact. Accordingly, future training should move beyond the transmission of technical concepts alone and instead be organised around authentic nursing tasks, with more stratified, hands-on, and reflective learning pathways to help nurses progress from merely using AI tools to understanding, managing, and critically judging them [35].

This study also found that clinical nurses generally tended to adopt a stance of cautious acceptance rather than unquestioning trust in their use of AI. Previous research has noted that, although the implementation of AI in nursing practice holds potential for improving efficiency, supporting decision-making, and optimising workflows, it is also accompanied by concerns related to data quality, algorithmic bias, privacy risks, limited explainability, and difficulties in building trust. Its value in practice therefore depends on active professional appraisal, clinical interpretation, and responsible use [36]. The present study further suggests that nurses’ critical stance towards AI is not best understood as a stable individual trait that exists independently of context. Rather, it more closely resembles a form of “situated vigilance” that is continually activated through the combined influence of high-risk clinical situations, professional responsibility, and prior experiences of error. In other words, nurses do not simply possess or lack critical thinking about AI by nature; instead, they develop a practice logic of questioning, verifying, and filtering AI outputs in response to the professional obligation to safeguard patient safety. This finding indicates that fostering critical thinking as part of AI literacy should not be reduced to abstract cognitive training alone, but should instead be integrated with clinical case analysis, error recognition, output verification, and risk response training, so as to help nurses develop more stable and transferable patterns of prudent judgement in real nursing contexts [37, 38].

At the ethical level, this study identified a noteworthy finding: some nurses showed relatively limited awareness of AI-related privacy risks, yet displayed a strong degree of consistency in attributing responsibility for the consequences of AI use, generally believing that ultimate responsibility still rests primarily with nurses. Previous research has shown that the implementation of AI in nursing and broader healthcare settings involves not only technical performance, but also issues of privacy protection, data security, algorithmic bias, transparency, accountability, and wider ethical, legal, and social concerns [36]. Existing studies have also proposed that understanding ethical and social implications should form an important component of future AI training in nursing [39]. Building on this, the present study further identified a phenomenon that may be described as a form of “ethical misalignment,” whereby nurses were not always highly sensitive at the level of risk awareness, yet strongly concentrated responsibility attribution on themselves.

This ethical misalignment may be further understood from three aspects. First, participants generally positioned AI as an auxiliary tool rather than as an independent decision-maker or accountable actor. In their view, AI could provide reference information, but once such information was selected, interpreted, communicated, or applied in nursing care, responsibility returned to the nurse who used it. Second, nursing practice is grounded in professional norms of patient safety, verification, and accountability. Nurses are trained to check, confirm, and take responsibility for information before applying it to patient care; therefore, even when they did not fully understand the technical mechanisms, data flows, or privacy risks behind AI systems, they still tended to regard themselves as the final checkpoint. Third, AI-assisted nursing practice is embedded in a wider sociotechnical system involving users, AI tools, hospital information infrastructures, data governance arrangements, and regulatory mechanisms. When organisational policies, approved-use boundaries, data input rules, and responsibility allocation remain insufficiently explicit, accountability may become distributed at the system level, while the perceived burden of responsibility remains concentrated on frontline nurses. In this sense, nurses’ internalisation of responsibility may reflect not only individual professional caution, but also a potential responsibility gap in AI-supported nursing practice [40]. This combination of limited risk awareness and heavy responsibility burden suggests that nurses may occupy an asymmetrical position in their use of AI: they may not fully understand all the risks involved, yet have already assumed that they are expected to bear the main consequences. This finding suggests that future training and institutional development should not stop at emphasising individual prudence alone, but should also establish clearer organisational support, data governance norms, responsibility boundaries, and institutional protection mechanisms [41]. Otherwise, AI may appear to improve nursing efficiency on the surface while, at a deeper level, further intensifying nurses’ responsibility pressure and ethical burden.

This study further found that clinical nurses’ attitudes towards AI were open but ambivalent. Participants recognised AI’s potential to reduce workload, support learning, and optimise workflows, yet they were concerned that overreliance might weaken independent thinking, dilute responsibility, or increase the risk of misinformation and misuse. This finding is broadly consistent with previous research showing that nurses and related nursing groups often hold positive or cautiously optimistic attitudes towards AI, while also expressing concerns about risks, misuse, and insufficient capability [41]. Although some participants acknowledged that AI might replace repetitive or mechanical tasks, most did not believe that it could replace the communication, comfort, companionship, and humanistic care central to nursing. This echoes previous research suggesting that AI is unlikely to substitute for the emotional labour, empathy, trust-building, and interpersonal connection involved in nursing care [42, 43]. An important contribution of the present study is that it does not reduce nurses’ attitudes towards AI to a simple positive–negative dichotomy. Rather, it shows that nurses are reinterpreting their professional value in relation to AI: they hope to use AI to reduce tedious and repetitive work while preserving clinical judgement, responsibility, and humanistic care as core elements of nursing. From this perspective, AI literacy can be understood as a bridging capability between traditional humanistic care and the evolving role of nurses as “technology managers.” AI literacy extends beyond technical operation and includes understanding AI, applying it appropriately, evaluating its outputs, and recognising its ethical implications [15]. This broader literacy helps nurses recognise which aspects of care require empathy, compassion, trust-building, relational presence, contextual judgement, and direct human response [36]. It also enables them to take on technology-related responsibilities, including selecting appropriate AI tools, formulating effective prompts, verifying AI-generated information, identifying risks, and deciding how AI outputs should or should not be incorporated into nursing practice. This is consistent with emerging evidence that AI integration may require nurses to supervise AI outputs, maintain human oversight, and ensure that AI-supported processes remain aligned with patient care needs [44]. In this sense, AI literacy does not move nurses away from humanistic care, but helps them negotiate a professional role in which they remain responsible for care quality while also supervising, interpreting, and managing AI-supported processes.

Participants’ concern that AI may lack “human warmth” further reflects the tension between AI-assisted efficiency and the preservation of therapeutic interaction. AI may reduce communication-related workload by supporting patient education, discharge guidance, information explanation, and structured assessment. Evidence from AI-assisted assessment of therapeutic communication skills suggests that AI can provide consistent and highly reliable evaluation compared with human raters, particularly in structured educational or simulation contexts [45]. However, therapeutic interaction in clinical nursing is not limited to information transmission or evaluation; it also involves empathy, emotional responsiveness, trust-building, contextual judgement, and sensitivity to patients’ changing needs. Therefore, although AI may support communication-related education and assessment, its use in clinical practice should be positioned as an aid to, rather than a substitute for, nurses’ relational and humanistic work. This distinction helps explain why participants expected AI to improve efficiency while still expressing concern that it might weaken trust or introduce new tensions in clinician–patient relationships.

Strengths, limitations and implications for practice and research

A strength of this study is that it addresses the emerging and practically important issue of AI literacy among clinical nurses. By examining cognition, use, critical thinking, ethics, and attitude, it systematically captures the multidimensional manifestations of AI literacy in practice and the variation within them. This addresses a gap in previous research, which has relied largely on quantitative measurement and has been less able to reveal how AI literacy is enacted in concrete practice. Using a qualitative descriptive design in combination with framework-based content analysis, the study further illuminates clinical nurses’ experiences, judgements, and concerns in real nursing contexts on the basis of existing AI literacy dimensions. In addition, the use of maximum variation sampling to include nurses with different ages, years of experience, roles, and departmental backgrounds helped enhance the richness of the data.

This study also has several limitations. First, it was a single-centre study, and all participants were recruited from a tertiary Grade A general hospital in southwest China. The findings therefore primarily reflect the clinical experiences and organisational context of this type of institution, and their applicability to other regions, hospital levels, and specialty settings requires further examination. Second, most participants had already had some exposure to AI or at least a basic awareness of it. As a result, the findings may reflect the perspectives of those with prior exposure more strongly and may be less able to capture the barriers and needs of nurses with no prior contact with AI. Third, the use of framework analysis guided by four core AI literacy domains and an additional attitudinal domain helped strengthen the focus and structure of the analysis, but it may also have constrained the emergence of entirely inductive themes to some extent. Finally, as the study was based on one-time interviews conducted within a limited period, it mainly reflects clinical nurses’ understandings and experiences at a specific stage and was less able to capture how AI literacy may change dynamically over time.

These findings may inform nursing education, clinical implementation, and organisational governance related to AI use. First, AI literacy training could be tailored according to nurses’ prior exposure to AI, frequency of use, and self-assessed proficiency. Nurses with little or no experience may benefit from basic orientation to AI concepts, common clinical applications, and safe-use boundaries, whereas nurses who use AI more frequently may require further support in prompt formulation, output verification, privacy protection, and assessment of clinical applicability. Second, scenario-based training may be useful, as participants in this study described AI use in practical situations such as patient education, discharge guidance, nursing documentation, clinical information retrieval, and preparation for nursing rounds. Training activities could therefore incorporate examples from these nursing scenarios and guide nurses to compare AI-generated outputs with guidelines, institutional protocols, and professional judgement. Third, at the organisational level, the findings suggest a need for clearer guidance on AI use in nursing practice, including which tools may be used, what types of information should not be entered into AI systems, how patient information should be de-identified, and how AI-generated information should be checked before use. Finally, AI integration in nursing should be considered in relation to the humanistic nature of care. Participants expected AI to reduce repetitive and information-related workload, but also emphasised that clinical judgement, patient communication, emotional support, and individualised care should remain central to nursing practice. Future research could evaluate the feasibility and effectiveness of scenario-based AI literacy training and examine how organisational policies influence nurses’ safe and responsible use of AI in different clinical settings.

Conclusion

This study suggests that AI literacy among clinical nurses is not a single, static technical skill, but a broader capability embedded in nursing practice that encompasses understanding, use, verification, responsibility, and role negotiation. Clinical nurses’ understanding of AI often begins with whether it is perceived as a useful tool and gradually develops, through actual use, into an awareness of its limitations, risks, and boundaries of applicability. Overall, its use was characterised by a pattern of human-led, AI-assisted practice, and by a logic of cautious acceptance rather than unquestioning trust, driven by concerns for patient safety and professional responsibility. Notably, some nurses still showed limited awareness of AI-related privacy and governance risks, while strongly internalising responsibility for its consequences. Future efforts should strengthen individual-level capability training while simultaneously improving organisational support, data governance, and clarity around responsibility boundaries in order to promote the safe, appropriate, and responsible use of AI in nursing practice.
