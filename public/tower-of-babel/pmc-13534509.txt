Highly efficient long-range conduction through a biosynthetic nickel-organic framework
Authors: Filip J R Meysman, Bent Smets, Silvia Hidalgo-Martinez, Nathalie Claes, Bob C Schroeder, Jeanine S Geelhoed, Yun Liu, Jiji Alingapoyil Choyikutty, Tamazouzt Chennit, Thijs Bodson, Alberto Collauto, Maxie M Roessler, Dmitry Karpov, Sylvain Bohic, Matteo Aramini, Shusaku Hayama, Maxwell Wetherington, Martijn A Zwijnenburg, Galina Pankratova, Isabel Pintelon, Jean-Pierre Timmermans, Gert Nuyts, Karolien De Wael, Sara Bals, Jo Verbeeck, Han Remaut, Henricus T S Boschker
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBiobased electronics aims for disruptive innovation in sustainable electronics but is obstructed by the low intrinsic conductivity of biomaterials. Recently, fibres were discovered within the cell envelope of multi-cellular cable bacteria, which display an exceptional conductivity for a biomaterial. Yet, the molecular structure and electron transport mechanism remain unresolved, thus precluding a detailed structure-function understanding and the development of biomimetic analogues. Here, we demonstrate that each fibre embeds an extended nickel-organic framework, which consists of a bundle of intertwined nanoribbons, each built from stacked repeat units in which multiple nickel centres are linked by organic dithiolene ligands. This metal-organic supramolecular architecture provides extensive conjugation and electron delocalization, thus enabling exceptional conductance over macroscale distances. This suggests a novel design principle for bio-based electronic materials and opens possibilities for biosynthesis of metal-organic frameworks.Subject terms: Biopolymers in vivo, Metalloproteins, Bacterial structural biology

Introduction

Electron transfer is fundamental to life, as metabolism relies on redox reactions in which electrons are exchanged. Therefore, evolution has produced conductive protein structures that are known to facilitate electron transport from the nanometre scale, as seen in the membrane complexes involved in photosynthesis and respiration1–3, up to the micrometre scale, as encountered in the multi-heme cytochrome appendages of metal reducing bacteria4,5. This has inspired the field of biobased electronics, which aims to combine the benefits of biomaterials with electronic functionality, while reducing reliance on finite resources and minimizing environmental footprints6,7. Yet, this effort is hampered by a critical bottleneck, as the conductivity of biomaterials is simply not high enough to be competitive with the metal-based conductive inks or conductive organic polymers used in printed electronics.

The idea that biomaterials cannot attain high conductivity has, however, been recently challenged by the discovery of so-called cable bacteria8–10. These long, multicellular bacteria stand out in the prokaryotic world for their capability to channel electrical currents over distances spanning several centimetres, which equips them with a competitive advantage for survival in the seafloor11–14. To enable long-range conduction, their cell envelope contains a network of 20–70 protein fibres that run in parallel along the entire centimetre-long filament15. Electrical characterization has shown that these fibres exhibit remarkable electrical properties for a bio-material16–19, including an electrical conductivity up to 500 S/cm18, which rivals that of the electronic inks used in printed electronics. The combination of high conductivity with functionality not encountered in traditional electronic materials (flexibility, self-assembly, biodegradability) renders them a potential promising material for bio-based electronics20.

Presently, the molecular structure of the conductive fibres remains unresolved, thus precluding rational design and synthetic in vitro production of these fibres. More fundamentally, it remains enigmatic how a protein structure can attain such an extraordinary conductivity. The reigning dogma is that all conductive protein systems display a similar architecture, which was developed early on in the evolution of life1,2. Multi-step electron transfer through proteins requires the inclusion of Fe-containing cofactors, such as Fe-S clusters or hemes, which act as relay centres for electrons to pass through the otherwise insulating protein matrix2. The associated electron transport is conventionally described as an incoherent, stepwise and thermally activated “hopping” process, in which electrons tunnel between cofactors, losing their phase coherence before moving to the next3,6. Recent evidence suggests that conduction in cable bacteria does not conform to this Fe-based hopping architecture. Raman spectroscopy reveals that the periplasmic fibres do not contain of Fe-based cofactors, but a sulphur-ligated Ni complex21–23. Likewise, model analysis indicates that conduction is orders of magnitude too fast for non-adiabatic hopping24.

Here, we demonstrate that conduction in cable bacteria occurs within a supramolecular structure that radically deviates from the conventional architecture of conductive proteins. Our data show that an extended, nickel-organic framework runs through the centre of the conductive fibres, thus forming a uninterrupted conductive conduit that displays a surprising long-range order for a biological material. Metal-organic frameworks are not known to be biologically produced but are attracting considerable interest within organic electronics and material science25. Our results reveal that already long ago, microbial evolution has explored metal-organic configurations to achieve flexible structures with high conductivity. As such, the fibres produced by cable bacteria may provide a promising design principle for novel bio-based electronic materials.

RESULTS

Fibres contain an intertwined bundle of Ni-rich nanoribbons

To document the substructure of their internal fibre network, cable bacterium filaments were individually isolated from a clonal enrichment culture. Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM) shows that the outer surface of the filaments displays parallel ridges (width 134 ± 3 nm; n = 248), while local constrictions indicate internal cell-cell interfaces (Fig. 1a, b). Transmission Electron Microscopy (TEM) of resin-embedded cross-sections (Fig. 1c) reveals that each ridge harbours a periplasmic fibre (diameter 49 ± 5 nm; NF = 68 fibres per filament), which appears as an unstained patch (Fig. 1d), thus suggesting a dense structure resistant to penetration of the applied stain.

Sequential extraction of native filaments removes the lipid membranes and cytoplasm, thus producing a so-called fibre skeleton15,21(Fig. S1a–h). The conductive fibers remain aligned in a regular parallel pattern in the fibre skeleton (Fig. S1f), thus suggesting there is a connective layer between fibers. The fibre skeletons remain equally conductive as native filaments16,19, thus demonstrating that the fibre network remains structurally intact and that the extraction does not notably affect the electrical properties of the fibres. Nano-X-Ray Fluorescence (nXRF) imaging of fibre skeletons reveals a distinct pattern of parallel lines in the Ni area density (ρANi) map (Fig. 1e; Fig. S1i–p), thus confirming the presence of Ni in the fibers21–23. The same Ni enrichment pattern is seen in native filaments (Fig. S1i,k), thus indicating that the fibres account for most of the Ni in the cell. Lines are equidistant and remain continuous across the interfaces between cells (in Fig. 1e, the Ni lines run continuously over three consecutive cell-cell junctions). The regular spacing of the ρANi peaks (147 ± 9 nm; n = 182) matches the geometry of the ridges as seen in SEM (Fig. 1b), and the full width at half maximum of the ρANi peaks (52 ± 15 nm; n = 128; Fig. S2b) concurs with the fibre diameter as seen in TEM (Fig. 1c, d).

The cell-cell interfaces are not enriched in Ni, but show an elevated Cu signal that traces the shape of the so-called “cartwheel” structure (Fig. 1f), which consists of a set of rod-like “spokes” that extend inwards from underneath each ridge to converge in a central node15,23. This cartwheel is also conductive, thus providing an electric interconnection between fibres and imposing redundancy on the electrical network26. Superposition of the Cu and Ni maps shows that the terminal ends of the Cu-rich spokes are connecting to the Ni-rich fibres (Fig. S2a). In native filaments, sulphur (S) is homogeneously distributed across the filament (Fig. S1k), while in the fibre skeletons, it shows a strong enrichment in both fibres and cartwheels (Fig. S1l). When fibre skeletons were sonicated, loosened fibres and cartwheel fragments were produced, which confirm that S colocalizes with Ni in the fibres and with Cu in the cartwheels (Fig. 1g–o). While individual fibres show trace levels of Cu, their estimated Ni/Cu ratio is 8.1, thus confirming strong Ni enrichment in the fibres. In the cartwheel fragments, Ni remains undetectable, and the Cu concentration is ~5.5 times higher compared to the fibre material, thus signifying Cu enrichment (calculation details in SI). Native filaments show a homogeneous distribution of Fe across the cell (Fig. S1o), likely originating from cytochromes distributed over cytoplasm and periplasm, while fibre skeletons show an overall low abundance of Fe with only some localized hotspots, likely originating from poly-P inclusions (Fig. S1p). There is no marked enrichment of iron (Fe) in the periplasmic fibres nor the cartwheel, thus indicating that neither hemes nor Fe-S clusters are present16,21.

Together, these data suggest that the conductive network in cable bacteria incorporates two distinct metal complexes: a Ni/S-entity in the periplasmic fibres and a Cu/S-entity in the cartwheel spokes. Cable bacteria contain ~18 × 106 Ni atoms per cell (75 per nm of fibre x 68 fibers x 3500 nm length of cell; Table S1), which greatly exceeds the typical Ni content of aerobic bacteria (11 µM Ni in E. coli; 1 µm3 cell volume; 0.006 × 106 Ni per cell27) as well as that of anaerobic methanogens producing high levels of Ni-dependent enzymes (142 µM Ni; 4 µm3 cell volume; 0.36 × 106 Ni per cell28). While genomic analysis reveals that cable bacteria may express several Ni-containing enzymes, such as [Ni‑Fe]‑hydrogenase, carbon monoxide hydrogenase and acetyl‑CoA‑synthase, along with some of their accompanying accessory proteins and nickel‑binding chaperones29, our results indicate that the Ni/S-entity in the fibres accounts for >95% of the cellular Ni (assuming a cytoplasmic Ni concentration of ~75 µM). Therefore, when applying spectroscopic techniques to native cable bacterium filaments (e.g. EPR, XAS – see below), the signal of the Ni/S-entity will be dominant.

High-angle annular dark-field imaging (HAADF) scanning transmission electron microscopy (STEM) shows that each fibre harbours a set of electron-dense “nanoribbons” that follow a wavy trajectory along the longitudinal axis of the fibres (Fig. 2a). These nanoribbons were consistently observed across samples. Energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDX) confirms that the ribbons are strongly enriched in Ni and S (Figs. 2b–g and S3), and so they harbour the Ni/S complex. Multiple ribbons converge and diverge within the centre of each fibre, thus forming an intertwined channel network that flows in the direction of the fibres (Fig. 2a). The number of the nanoribbons is highly similar between fibres (11 ± 2 nanoribbons per fibre; n = 12) as is their shape (nanoribbon diameter 1.40 ± 0.16 nm; n = 10); Fig. S2j–l). Nanoribbons are confined within a central zone (~30 nm wide) of the fibres (Fig. 2a–h), thus concurring with previous Scanning Tunnelling Microscopy data, which suggested that the fibres contain a conductive core21. Detailed analysis of nXRF and EDX maps enables quantification of Ni and S concentrations in the fibres and nanoribbons (see SI for model details), which matches previous estimates of the S/Ni ratio in the fibers23 (SI). When accounting for the signal from S-containing amino acids in the surrounding protein (Figs. 2i and S2–3), the molar S/Ni ratio of the nanoribbons can be constrained to range between 3.0 and 4.6 (Tables S1–S2).

The Ni/S complex is a conjugated nickel bis(dithiolene)

Previously, we observed that the Ni/S-entity in the nanoribbons displays a characteristic Raman fingerprint16,21,22. This spectrum is characterized by two intensive vibrational modes at ~367 cm−1 and ~490 cm−1 that stand out due to their remarkable intense Raman signal across a broad spectral range (405–1064 nm), suggesting strong resonance (Fig. S4a). This Raman signature is present in different morphotypes of cable bacteria retrieved from various freshwater and marine environments21, implying that it is present across the entire cable bacteria clade. This Raman fingerprint is not detected in any other microorganism and is markedly different from the spectra of known Ni-containing enzymes (see discussion in SI). Accordingly, the Ni/S complex does not seem to have a known biological analog, and so we expanded the search for potential structures towards abiotic sulfur-coordinated nickel complexes22. Assignment of vibrational modes, supported by stable isotope labeling, suggests that the structure of the Ni-cofactor shares a close resemblance to that of nickel bis(1,2-dithiolene) complexes22. These organic coordination compounds consist of a central nickel, coordinated by two bidentate 1,2-dithiolene ligands, thus forming a planar Ni(S2C2R2)2 complex with a D2h symmetry and a delocalized electronic structure30 (Fig. S5). The R-substituents on the dithiolene ligands define the structure and properties of the complex31, but remain hitherto unresolved for the Ni/S-entity in cable bacteria22.

To determine the R-substituents, we compared the Raman spectra of fibre skeletons to a set of synthetic Ni-bis(dithiolene) references, including six monomeric (one Ni centre) and two polymeric (multiple Ni centres) compounds (structures in Table S3; Raman acquisition parameters in Table S4 and Fig. S4). The Ni/S complex cannot feature nitrile groups or aromatic rings as R-substituents, as their characteristic Raman bands are not seen in the fibre skeleton spectra32. Moreover, the strong resonance observed with red and near-infrared excitation wavelengths (Figs. 3a and S4a) suggests that the complex is extensively conjugated. As such, the complex is likely not monomeric33, but oligomeric, with multiple Ni centres interconnected by dithiolene ligands to form a larger conjugated complex. We refer to this entity as the NiBiD (NiBisDithiolene) complex.

Overall, the Raman spectra of NiBiD show the closest resemblance to those of the neutral coordination polymers poly-nickel(ethene tetrathiolate) (pNi(ett)) and poly-nickel(TetraThiaFulvalene tetrathiolate) (pNi(TTFtt)), which only feature a limited number of bonds (Ni-S, C-S, C=C). There is a close correspondence between the low-frequency peak positions near 366 cm−1 (Ni-S) and 494 cm−1 (C-S plus ring deformation), and thiocarbonyl radical (C=S.) peaks within the 1150–1200 cm−1 region (Fig. 3a; annotation in Table S5). Likewise, the peak intensities show a similar dependence on the excitation wavelength (Table S6). This indicates that the NiBiD complex must share a high degree of structural homology with pNi(ett) and pNi(TTFtt).

This is supported by X-ray Absorption Spectroscopy (XAS) applied to native filaments, which shows that the Ni K-edge of NiBiD closely resembles that of the Ni-bis(dithiolene) complexes (Figs. 3c and S6g). A detailed comparison of the NiBiD spectrum to that of inorganic reference compounds, Ni bis-dithiolene reference compounds, and the Ni pincer containing LarA enzyme is provided in the SI. Consistent with a square planar coordination, NiBiD shows a pre-edge peak at 8333 eV, corresponding to the dipole-forbidden 1s → 3d transition34, and a prominent rising-edge shoulder at 8338 eV, which arises from a dipole-allowed 1 s→4pz transition35,36. NiBiD structurally differs from the Ni pincer in LarA37, as the spectrum of the latter shows a more pronounced pre-edge feature, a lower rising-edge shoulder, a more intense white line at 8350 eV, and also markedly differs within the EXAFS region > 8350 eV (Fig. S6h). Among the Ni-bis(dithiolene) compounds investigated, the XAS spectrum of NiBiD is most similar to that of the polymeric forms pNi(ett) and pNi(TTFtt), both in the XANES (Fig. 3c) and EXAFS (Fig. 3d) regions. This confirms the high level of structural homology between NiBiD and these polymeric Ni-bis(dithiolene) compounds as also suggested by the Raman spectra (Fig. 3a).

The coordination environment of the Ni centre

The presence of stable thiocarbonyl radicals suggests a charge-neutral complex where dithiolene ligands are stabilized by ligand-centred radicals38. This hypothesis is supported by Electron Paramagnetic Resonance (EPR) spectroscopy of native cable bacterium filaments, which show a signal emerging at g ~2 that is suitably simulated by an isolated S = ½ spin system (Figs. 3b and S6a). The average g value, gav = (2·g⊥+g∥)/3 = 2.014, is consistent with an unpaired electron primarily residing on an organic ligand rather than a transition metal, as also observed previously for pNi(ett) and pNi(TTFtt)39. The significant shift from the g-value of the free electron (ge = 2.0023) supports the localisation of the radical on a heavier element like sulphur, characterized by a larger spin-orbit coupling than seen in lighter elements such as carbon, oxygen, or nitrogen40. The low-field ‘shoulder’ can be accounted for by a small axial anisotropy of the electron Zeeman interaction (Fig. 3b). The signal increases with decreasing temperature (Fig. S6e) as expected for a centre with a paramagnetic ground state.

Spin concentrations were significantly less than one spin per Ni centre (0.12 ± 0.03, n = 3; Fig. S6d and Table S7), aligning with spin values previously observed for poly-metal-(ett) structures41. To assess the origin of these spins, we performed DFT calculations for Ni(ett)-trimers with different terminal end-cappings (structures I-VI in Fig. 3e). The predicted electronic ground state of partially capped structures (structures I and II; Fig. 3e) are closed-shell singlets. When Ni(ett)-trimers are fully capped with thiol groups or S–S bonded moieties (e.g., as crosslinked to cysteine residues or other Ni(ett)-trimers; structures III and IV in Fig. 3e), these display an open-shell singlet ground state (Table S8). Congruent with the EPR data, the open-shell character originates from two spins that are predominantly localized on the sulphur and carbon atoms of the two terminal dithiolene ligands, while there is negligible spin density on the nickel (frontiers orbitals for model structure IV in Fig. 3f). Conjointly, the spin data and DFT calculations support an oligomeric NiBiD complex with multiple Ni centres, where spins only reside on the terminal ligands, thus explaining the low spin value per Ni centre.

Axially aligned NiBiD complexes form 1D nanoribbons

Combined, the Raman, EPR and XAS data support the hypothesis that NiBiD comprises an oligomeric form of either Nin(ett)n+1 or Nin(TTFtt)n+1, where n denotes the number of Ni centres. The molar S/Ni ratio of 3.0–4.6 as determined by nXRF and STEM-HAADF-EDX imposes a strong constraint on the molecular configuration and is only consistent with an ett-like (S/Ni ~4) and not a TTFtt-like (S/Ni ~8) structure. The Nin(ett)n+1 structure also explains the fragmentation patterns previously obtained by ToF-SIMS analysis of fibre skeletons, which produced two specific organosulphur fragments (C2S2− and C2S2H−) tightly associated with [NiS]n- fragments21. Still, the oligomer length (n number) of the Nin(ett)n+1 complexes is only weakly constrained by our current data. A monomeric form is likely not sufficiently conjugated to display resonance Raman scattering in the near-infrared (Fig. 3a), and hence n > 1. Likewise, the molar S/Ni ratio (3.0–4.6) and spin concentration (0.12 spins per Ni centre) are only compatible with longer oligomeric lengths n = 5–20 (Fig. 3g,h).

The relative orientation and packing of NiBiD units within the nanoribbons is more tightly constrained. The mean distance between Ni centres in Nin(ett)n+1 is 0.603 nm (as derived from DFT models of Ni3(ett)4; Fig. 3e), and so the estimated Ni concentration in a fibre (75 Ni centres per nanometre; Table S2) implies that ~45 NiBiD units must be present in parallel within a fibre. Distributing these among ~11 nanoribbons per fibre (Fig. 2a), suggests that ~4 NiBiD units must be aligned in parallel to form a single nanoribbon (Fig. 5). A brick wall stacking of 4 layers at ~0.35 nm distance (inter-plane distance for stacked dimers of synthetic Ni-bis(dithiolenes)42,43) provides a nanoribbon height of ~1.4 nm, which agrees well with the nanoribbon dimensions seen in STEM-HAADF (Fig. S2l). However, further investigation is needed to more precisely constrain the number of layers and the stacking configuration.

The linear alignment of NiBiD units is supported by polarized light microscopy of fibre skeletons, which show strong birefringence, thus suggesting that components are preferentially oriented in parallel to the electron current (Fig. 4a–d). This polarized behaviour is consistent with previous Raman results, which show that the characteristic NiBiD modes display strong anisotropic scattering22. Angle-resolved Raman spectroscopy (−10 to 220°) confirms that the Ni-S stretching mode (367 cm−1) and the ring deformation mode (494 cm−1) only produce intense Raman signals upon excitation with light polarized in parallel (0°, 180°) to the conductive fibres (Fig. 4e). Polarized Raman spectroscopy additionally reveals that all NiBiD modes are highly polarized (depolarization ratio ρ < 0.25; Table S9), with signals almost completely disappearing when moving towards perpendicular polarization (90°) (Fig. 4f–h). Such polarization effects are characteristic for planar molecules that are aligned along their long axis44. As the conductive fibres run in parallel to the longitudinal axis of the cable bacterium filaments, this indicates that the NiBiD units are aligned with their long axis in parallel with the fibres.

DISCUSSION

The conductive supramolecular structure in cable bacteria differs in fundamental ways from the canonical hopping architecture that is thought to be universal for conductive proteins in biology. Foremost, the electron transport mechanism is Ni-based rather than Fe-based. This is remarkable as Ni is known to be included in only nine enzymes, and in each of these, the Ni-site serves a catalytic and not an electron relay function45. Sufficient nickel availability is likely not an issue. Cable bacteria live in aquatic sediments where porewaters attain elevated nickel concentrations (>100 nM)46, and genome analysis reveals a strongly expanded repertoire of nickel import and export enzymes compared to their closest phylogenetic relatives29. A second distinct feature is the size of the cofactor. Rather than including a series of small, spatially segregated metal cofactors, cable bacteria incorporate a continuous electrical conduit that extends along the entire length of the centimetre-long fibres (Fig. 2a). To this end, planar nickel-organic NiBiD complexes are stacked into a coordination network that provides thin, long nanoribbons. Multiple nanoribbons are then intertwined to form a one-dimensional conductive conduit that runs within the centre of a fibre (Fig. 5). This multi-level, supramolecular structure bears a striking analogy to the braided copper wires used in the power cords of household appliances, and the purpose and advantages may be similar. Cable bacteria are motile and make sharp turns47, all while the fibre network must remain fully functional and conductive. An entangled wire allows the conductor to be highly flexible and malleable, but strong at the same time.

Combining Raman, EPR, and XAS data with DFT calculations, our data suggest that the NiBiD complex, which forms the repeat unit of the coordination network, most likely possesses a Nin(ett)n+1 configuration. This oligomeric Ni-bis(dithiolene) structure has no direct homology with other known enzyme cofactors. Molybdenum- and tungsten-proteins feature the molybdopterin cofactor, which has two adjacent thiolates coordinating the metal centre48,49, while lactate racemase (LarA) contains the Ni-pincer cofactor, in which Ni is coordinated by two sulphurs, one carbon, and one imidazole (histidine)37. However, these cofactors do not feature the square planar -S2NiS2- geometry of NiBiD, nor do they display the extensive conjugation induced by repetitive nickel coordination.

Effectively, the nanoribbon bundles (Fig. 5) can be regarded as a novel, unconventional, realization of a 1D metal-organic framework, in which metal centres are connected by organic linker molecules to provide repetitive, supramolecular nanoribbons extending in the same direction, while voids remain in between the nanoribbons. A metal-organic framework configuration has not been previously seen in biology, but explains the unusual electric properties that are documented for cable bacteria fibres16–19. Our data indicate a high structural homology between the nanoribbon material and Ni-bis(dithiolene) polymers38, which show extended conjugation and electron delocalization. The Raman spectra of NiBiD (Fig. 3a) show strong resonance under excitation with red (785 nm) and near-infrared (1064 nm) lasers, which can only come from chromophores with extended conjugation. Furthermore, nickel is a d8 electron transition metal, and its dsp2 hybridization in NiBiD results in a square-planar configuration. This planarity likely favours an ordered assembly of the NiBiD units into a nanoribbon (Fig. 5), thus enabling π-π-stacking between NiBiD units that provides additional conjugation and electron delocalization.

Delocalization over multiple cofactors has been recently advanced as a way to increase charge transport in polymerized cytochromes, as it increases the electron coupling between cofactors50. The nanoribbon structure proposed here (Fig. 5) exhibits extensive conjugation and overlapping π-systems, thus promoting electron delocalization along the oligomers and between stacked oligomers, and thereby facilitating charge transport24. Moreover, to achieve high conductivity in a biological wire, the electronic conduit should be isolated from the solvent and be as rigid as possible, both acting to lower the reorganization energy51. The ett-ligands consist of π-conjugated fragments without rotatable single bonds, making the planar NiBiD oligomers highly rigid. Stacking will confer additional stiffness, while the protein wrapping may induce isolation from the periplasmic solvent. This model explains previous results from detailed electrochemical characterization of cable bacteria, showing that the fibre conductance exhibits a markedly low reorganization energy ~160 meV17–19. Furthermore, temperature-dependent characterization reveals that conductance of the fibre network remains high at temperatures <20 K, signifying vibronic coupling19. In-plane vibrational modes of the NiBiD oligomers (also providing Raman peaks in Fig. 3a) are candidates to supply this electron-phonon coupling.

More generally, our findings uncover a mechanism through which electrical currents can be efficiently guided through an organic structure with a low metal content. The previously reported experimental values for the conductivity of the cable bacteria fibres (5–500 S cm−1) 16–19 were calculated using the whole fibre cross-section (diameter 25 nm). If we restrict the conductance solely to the nanoribbon network, then the estimated conductivity of a single nanoribbon (diameter 1.35 nm and 11 nanoribbons per bundle, as deduced above) amounts to 150–15.000 S cm−1. These conductivities rank among the highest reported for any metal-organic structure52. The regular assembly of NiBiD units into a long, one-dimensional transport channel (nanoribbon) appears key to enable fast electron transport53. This configuration bears a striking similarity with synthetic 1D coordination polymers, like pNi(ett)38 and pNi(TTFtt)54, which also can attain a high conductivity for a carbon-based material (up to 1200 S cm−1). Pressed pellets of pNi(TTFtt) display glassy-metallic behavior with low reorganization energy54, which has been attributed to π-stacking between planar molecules as well as lateral S–S interactions between sheets, thus generating electronic overlap that is notably robust to disorder by structural perturbations. Most likely, similar effects are responsible for the high conductivity of the nanoribbons. Accordingly, cable bacteria appear to have evolved a highly efficient form of organic conductivity, which could bring forward novel designs for sustainable electronic materials.

Methods

Cable bacteria cultivation

Cable bacterium filaments were produced by enrichment culturing and clonal culturing. The natural sediment used for culturing was collected from an intertidal creek within Rattekaai salt marsh (51.4391°N, 4.1697°E; The Netherlands). Enrichment culturing of cable bacteria was performed as described previously21,29. Briefly, natural sediment was first sieved to remove debris, plant material, and fauna (1.4 mm stainless steel mesh). Homogenized sediment was subsequently packed into transparent PVC core liners (inner diameter 36 mm, height 100 mm) with a rubber stopper at the bottom. Sediment cores were submerged in plastic containers containing artificial seawater (salinity 30, Instant Ocean) that was continuously aerated and incubated at a constant temperature (20 °C) in the dark. After 4–6 weeks, a dense population of thick (~4 µm diameter) cable bacteria developed in the sediment cores of enrichment culture. The large filament diameter indicates that filaments belonged to Candidatus Electrothrix gigas55.

Because one starts from a natural sediment, enrichment cultures can contain multiple strains of cable bacteria. Therefore, we created a so-called clonal culture that contained only a single strain of cable bacteria. This clonal culture (strain JX3-16) was attained from the same Rattekaai sediment source via transfer of individual filaments to autoclaved sediment cores as described in detail in ref. 29. To confirm clonality (i.e., the presence of only a single cable bacterium strain), we performed V3‑V4 16S rRNA amplicon sequencing29. The clonal culture was maintained in the lab by regularly transferring a small amount of the clonal enrichment culture as inoculum to freshly autoclaved sediment cores. The JX3-16 strain belongs to the species Candidatus Electrothrix gigas55.

Individual cable bacteria filaments were harvested from the sediment cores derived from the cultures. Here to, a small portion of sediment was deposited on a glass microscopy cover slip. Custom-made glass hooks were used to isolate cable bacterium filaments from the sediment and handle them under a stereomicroscope. Filaments were washed six times by transferring them between droplets (∼20 µl) of Milli-Q water (mQ), thus removing attached particles and salts, until clean filaments were obtained (further referred to as “native filaments”).

During the initial phase of the study, cable bacterium filaments were retrieved from enrichment cultures with Ca. E. gigas and used for EPR and nXRF analysis. Subsequently, when the JX3-16 clonal culture became available, filaments were solely retrieved from this clonal culture for further analysis (Raman, XAS, HAADF-STEM-EDX). To verify consistency, we compared the morphology of the filaments retrieved from the enrichment and clonal cultures via TEM cross-sections at various time points. The thick filaments initially retrieved from enrichment cultures displayed a similar size and morphology, and also had the similar number of ridge compartments as the JX3-16 strain, thus indicating that all filaments investigated belonged to the same species, i.e., Candidatus Electrothrix gigas55.

Fibre skeleton extraction

Through sequential extraction, the conductive fibre network can be isolated from the cell envelope of individual filaments,15,16,21,22. This extraction procedure removes the membranes and cytoplasm, thus producing a so-called “fibre skeleton” (Fig. S1e). The extraction procedure was performed as described previously21. Briefly, under a binocular microscope, an individual native filament was deposited for 10 min in a droplet (∼20 µl) of 1% (w/w) sodium dodecyl sulphate (SDS) to break down the cell membranes and release the cell contents. After 10 min, the filament was rinsed 6 times by sequential transfer to fresh mQ droplets to remove cell remains and wash off excess SDS. Next, the filament was submerged for 10 min in a droplet (∼20 µl) of 1 mM ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA), adjusted to pH 8, to remove residual SDS. The filament finally underwent a final 6–10 washes in fresh mQ droplets, thus providing a fibre skeleton that was used in subsequent analysis.

The quality of fibre skeleton extraction was monitored with Raman microscopy, TEM and AFM imaging. Fibre skeletons attain a flattened, cylindrical shape (Fig. S1), which retains the conductive fibres, embedded in parallel on top of a basal carbohydrate-rich sheath21. EDTA is known to be a performant chelator of metals, but previous work has shown that the EDTA extraction at 1 mM does not affect the Raman signal intensity of the NiBiD complex, thus suggesting that the structure of the Ni/S entity remains unaltered21,22. This was confirmed here. The SDS-EDTA extraction procedure reduces the biomass of native filaments by removing cytoplasmic and periplasmic material. Extracted fibre skeletons displayed the characteristic Raman fingerprint of the NiBiD complex (Fig. S4a), but did not show any marked signal of cytochromes, which are removed during the SDS-EDTA extraction16. TEM imaging (Fig. S1f) and AFM imaging (Fig. S1g,h) confirmed that biomass of the fibre skeletons was substantially reduced compared to native filaments, and that the height (Fig. S1h) fell within the 250-350 nm range previously measured for fibre skeletons15,21. The cartwheel structure in the cell-cell interfaces remains largely intact upon extraction, thus producing elevated regions within the AFM images of fibre skeletons (Fig. S1h). Finally, electrical characterization with patterned micro-electrodes (procedure as in ref. 9) showed that fibre skeletons produced from the JX3-16 clonal culture remained highly conductive after extraction (providing fibre conductivities in the 5–100 S cm−1 range).

Fibre skeleton fractionation

Fibre skeletons were fragmented into subcomponents through probe sonication, thus producing individual fibres and pieces of the cartwheel (further referred to as “fractionated fibre skeletons”; Fig. 1g–o). To this end, fibre skeletons were transferred to 200 µL of mQ in an Eppendorf 1.5 mL vial. The sonicator (Q125 probe sonicator, Qsonica) operated at 20% amplitude for 10 min in a cyclic fashion (30 s on alternated by 30 s off). The vial was cooled with ice while sonicating.

Atomic force microscopy

Native filaments and fibre skeletons were transferred onto a 50 nm gold coated silicon wafer (Platypus Technologies) in a drop of mQ water and subsequently air-dried. The wafer was securely fixed with double sided carbon tape to a 12 mm diameter stainless steel metal disc. AFM images were acquired in tapping mode with a XE-100 scanning probe microscope (Park Systems) equipped with an aluminium SPM probe with a tip radius <10 nm (AppNano ACTA-200), resonant frequency of 200–400 kHz, and a nominal spring constant of 13–77 N/m. Topographic and amplitude data were recorded and processed with the Gwyddion software56.

Polarized-light microscopy

Polarized-light microscopy was performed on native cable bacterium filaments retrieved from a natural sediment incubation of Rattekaai sediment, containing Ca. E. gigas filaments but also thinner filaments from other species of cable bacteria (Fig. 4a–d). Images were collected with an Olympus BX50 microscope with the polarizer (at 90°) and analyser (at 0°) crossed. Images were captured with a Canon 6D full-frame digital camera. Adobe Camera Raw was used to set image contrast and brightness with curves and to edit colour balance. Both thick and thinner filaments gave the same birefringence response (Fig. 4a–d).

Scanning electron microscopy

SEM images were acquired of native cable bacterium filaments using a FEI Quanta 250 FEG environmental scanning electron microscope (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Before imaging, filaments were coated with an Au layer via sputtering (Leica EM ACE600). Images were collected in Secondary Electron (SE) mode using an accelerating voltage of 20 kV and a working distance of 10 mm.

Transmission electron microscopy

Native filaments and fibre skeletons were pipetted onto a 200 mesh Formvar-coated copper grid (Micro to Nano) and allowed to air-dry on the grids. The samples were stained with 2% uranyl acetate for 1 min. After air-drying, the grids were examined with two TEM instruments: a FEI Tecnai G2 Spirit BioTWIN operating at 120 kV, or a JEOL 1400 TEM equipped with LaB6 filament and TVIPS F416 CCD camera, also operating at 120 kV.

To arrive at resin embedded cross-sections, native filaments and fibre skeletons were incubated for 15 min in freshly prepared fixative (4% paraformaldehyde, Sigma-Aldrich) in 1× phosphate-buffer saline (PBS; 137 mM NaCl, 2.7 mM KCl, 10 mM Na2HPO4, 1.8 mM KH2PO4, pH 7.4) at room temperature. Fixative removal involved two 3-min washes in 1× PBS, followed by two 3-minute washes in 0.1× PBS and a final rinse in mQ water. Samples were then embedded in a thin layer of 2% low melting point agarose (Fisher Bioreagents). After solidifying, agarose-embedded specimens were reshaped into small rectangles and subjected to secondary fixation in 2% paraformaldehyde in 0.1 M phosphate buffer (pH 7.4), washed with 0.01 M phosphate buffer (pH 7.4), and dehydrated using a graded ethanol series (50%, 70%, 90%, 95%, and three changes of 100% ethanol for 20 min each). Infiltration of Unicryl resin in the agarose blocks was accomplished by incubation in 30% resin in ethanol for 1 h, followed by at least three changes with fresh 100% resin. Subsequently, samples were submerged in fresh Unicryl resin and cured under UV light for 60 h at 4 °C followed by 72 h at room temperature. The resulting Unicryl blocks were sectioned using an ultramicrotome equipped with a diamond knife, yielding 50 nm thick sections. Thin sections were then transferred onto Au TEM grids (Electron Microscopy Sciences). Next, sections were stained with 2% uranyl acetate and lead citrate for 1 min, washed, and dried before being examined with transmission electron microscopy (TEM). Thin sections were imaged with a FEI Tecnai G2 Spirit BioTWIN operating at 120 kV.

Synchrotron nano-X-ray Fluorescence elemental mapping

Native cable bacterium filaments, intact fibre skeletons, and fractioned fibre skeleton samples were subjected to nXRF imaging. Samples were deposited on silicon nitride membranes (Silson). A droplet of ultrapure water (Optima™ LC/MS Grade, Thermo Fisher Scientific) was deposited over the sample and blotted with paper to leave a thin layer of liquid. Next, the sample was plunge frozen in liquid ethane at 93 K (Leica EM GP), transferred to a bath of liquid nitrogen (Leica EM VCM), and inserted in a vacuum cryo-transfer shuttle system (Leica EM VCT500). During transfer, the shuttle was kept at a temperature between 120 and 113 K. The cryo-transfer system was then connected to the experimental chamber of the ID16A Nano-Imaging Beamline (European Synchrotron Radiation Facility, Grenoble, France) to introduce the sample on the sample stage, which is kept in cryogenic conditions (100 K) to reduce the radiation damage on the sample during X-ray fluorescence mapping. A light microscope focused on the sample was used to locate the sample on the membrane and to define the scanning region. The X-ray beam was operated at a constant energy of 17 keV and focused to a spot size of 35 nm. The nXRF maps were obtained by scanning the beam over the fibre skeletons in 10–25 nm steps and recording the X-ray fluorescence signal emitted from each sample pixel with two silicon drift detectors (16-element Ardesia and 7-element Vortex ME-7) positioned perpendicular to the X-ray beam57. The elemental areal mass concentration was calculated using the Fundamental Parameters (FP) approach implemented in PyMca software package58 and a reference standard material containing elements of certified concentration (RF7-200-S2371, AXO Dresden) with uniform mass depositions in the range of ng/mm² (1–3 atomic layers).

STEM-HAADF-EDX imaging

Scanning Transmission Electron Microscopy (STEM) imaging was performed on fractioned fibre skeleton samples in High-Angle Annular Dark-Field (HAADF) mode, combined with Energy Dispersive X-Ray (EDX) mapping, on a Tecnai Osiris (Thermo Fisher Scientific), equipped with a high brightness X-Feg electron, an analytical pole piece (6 mm pole piece gap), a 4-quadrant Super X detector, and a Gatan US1000XP CCD camera. The microscope was operated at 200 kV with a beam current of ~150–200 pA. Image analysis was performed in the Quantax software, with additional post-processing in Fiji/ImageJ59 and R.

Reference compounds

Six monomeric (single Ni centre) nickel bis(1,2-dithiolene) complexes were obtained as powders from TCI Europe n.v. Ni(II)(mnt)2, Ni(III)(mnt)2, Ni(III)(dmit)2, Ni(III)(dttdt)2, Ni(II)(dtb)2 and Ni(II)(dmdtb)2 - catalogue numbers and structures provided in Table S3. Two polymeric nickel bis(1,2-dithiolene) complexes, Poly-nickel(ethene tetrathiolate) (pNi(ett)) and Poly-nickel(TetraThiaFulvalene tetrathiolate) (pNi(TTFtt)), were synthesized as powders according to the protocol described in ref. 38. The chemical formulas and associated molecular structures are listed in Table S3. All Ni-bis(dithiolene) compounds were stored under dark and anaerobic conditions (N2 glovebox) until further use. The Ni-pincer containing enzyme LarA was synthesized by the procedure described in ref. 37.

Multi-wavelength Raman microscopy

Using multi-wavelength Raman microscopy, fibre skeletons were compared to a set of reference compounds, which included six monomeric Ni-bis(dithiolene) compounds and two polymeric Ni-bis(dithiolene) compounds, pNi(ett) and p(TTFtt) (Table S3). Fibre skeletons and powders of Ni-bis(dithiolene) reference compounds were deposited on Si wafers coated with 50 nm gold or small pieces of aluminium foil attached to a microscopy slide. Raman spectra were collected using a Renishaw inVia™ Qontor® microscope equipped with four Renishaw solid state lasers (405 nm, 532 nm, 785 nm, 1064 nm). We used a 20x (NA 0.50) microscopy objective and confocal aperture of 65 µm to focus incident laser light on the samples and capture Raman scattered photons. Two electrically cooled (−70 °C) detectors were used to detect Raman scattered photons in the visible range (Renishaw Centrus Charge Coupled Device) and near-infrared range (Andor Indium Gallium Arsenide photodiode). Spectrum acquisition parameters, including grating, acquisition time, and laser power, were optimized to ensure optimal signal response and spectral resolution (±1 cm−1), and are summarized in Table S4. Raman spectra were processed using the LabSpec6 software (version 6.6.1.11; Horiba). Processing was limited to cosmic ray removal and baseline correction. Baselines were removed by fitting a polynomial to the acquired spectrum. Finally, processed spectra were averaged to improve the signal-to-noise ratio.

Angle-resolved Raman microscopy

For angle-resolved Raman spectroscopy, fibre skeletons were deposited onto a Si wafer coated with 50 nm gold (Platypus Technologies). Spectra were acquired using a Horiba LabRam HR Evolution confocal Raman microscope equipped with a 633 nm HeNe laser (ThorLabs HRP-350-EC). An analyser was placed in front of the spectrometer such that parallel polarized Raman scattered light was recorded as a function of the incident angle. The optical components of the Raman system included a 100x objective (0.90 NA), a confocal hole aperture of 50 μm, and 600 gr/mm grating. A back-illuminated deep-depleted Si array (1024 × 512 pixels) detector, calibrated using the Raman response of a single crystal Si sample, was used to detect Raman scattered light. Spectra were collected by focusing the laser spot, delivering approximately 12 mW of laser power during 1 s, on the middle of the fibre skeletons. An automated ½ waveplate in the common path controlled the polarization of the excitation laser, which was rotated from −10° to 220° with respect to the direction of the periplasmic fibres in the filaments. The silicon in the Si wafer has a diamond cubic structure with a Si(100) crystallographic plane. The intensity of the Raman signal is determined by the alignment of the laser’s polarization vector with the Raman tensor of the Si(100) crystal, providing intensity maxima when the light polarization is aligned with the crystal directions. Because of the fourfold symmetry of the Si (100) crystal lattice, these directions are 90° apart, providing a Raman peak at 521 cm−¹ for 0°, 90°, 180°, and 270°. This “four-leaf clover” pattern of Si was used as a reference (Fig. 4e).

Polarized Raman microscopy

Polarized Raman microscopy was done using the 785 nm excitation laser of the Renishaw inVia™ Qontor® microscope. A λ/2 waveplate was put in the optical path to change the incident polarization between horizontal (X) and vertical (Y) polarization. An analyser kit, consisting of a λ/2 waveplate and a polarizer, was used to examine the polarization of the Raman scattered light. This setup enabled us to test 4 polarization conformations: Z(X,X)Z̄, Z(X,Y)Z̄, Z(Y,X)Z̄, and Z(Y,Y)Z̄, where Z denotes the direction of the incident laser beam and Z̄ the direction of the scattered light being collected. The symbols in parentheses specify the polarization direction of the incident laser and scattered light, respectively. Spectra were collected for fibre skeletons that were oriented horizontally (X) on the Si wafer (Fig. 4). The wafer was oriented with the crystal axes parallel to the polarizations of the incident laser light. This way, the 520 cm−1 Si-mode functioned as a well-characterized, polarized reference mode. The excitation laser delivered 5 mW of laser power during 30 s to the sample. Per conformation, 10 spectra were recorded at different spots in the fibre skeleton. After processing, spectra were averaged to obtain the final spectra. The depolarization ratio (ρ) of the NiBiD complex modes was calculated (Table S9) as the ratio of the intensity of perpendicular versus the parallel component of the Raman signal (Z(X,Y)Z̄/Z(X,X)Z̄).

X-band Electron Paramagnetic Resonance (EPR) spectroscopy

Native cable bacterium filaments were individually retrieved from the enrichment incubation, cleaned, and gathered in a dense clump (>1000 filaments) in droplet of mQ water on a microscope slide. This clump was then transferred to a Suprasil EPR tube (100 mm length, 1.1 mm inner diameter (ID), 1.6 mm outer diameter (OD); Wilmad Labglass WG-222T-RB) filled with mQ water. The EPR tube was inserted in a 15 mL conical centrifuge tube together with filler material to avoid breaking the tube during centrifugation, and the tube assembly was centrifuged for 5 min at 2500 g. As a result, the filament clump migrated to the bottom of the EPR tube and formed a small pellet. The supernatant was removed from the EPR tube using a syringe with a long needle until ±1 mm of water remained, and subsequently, the EPR tube was flash frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at 77 K until further use. Three replicate EPR tubes with sample were prepared according to this procedure.

EPR measurements were performed on a Bruker EMX X-band continuous wave (CW) EPR spectrometer equipped with a Bruker ER 4122SHQE super-high-sensitivity resonator and a closed-circuit Helium cryostat (Cryogenic Ltd) controlled by a Model 350 temperature controller (Lake Shore Cryotronics, Inc.) and a Cernox sensor (Lake Shore Cryotronics, Inc.). The magnetic field was calibrated using 2,2-diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl (DPPH) as a standard (g = 2.0036)60. Immediately before measurement, the Suprasil EPR tube containing the sample was loaded inside a clear fused quartz (CFQ) tube (2 mm ID, 3 mm OD, Wilmad Labglass 705-SQ-250M). The latter was evacuated to a final pressure of approximately 1 mbar using a 3 mm NMR tip-off manifold (Wilmad Labglass) to prevent condensation of liquid oxygen inside the EPR tube during the low-temperature measurements. This whole procedure was performed under liquid nitrogen to prevent the biological sample from thawing. The assembly was loaded in the pre-cooled cryostat so that the pellet containing the cable bacteria is positioned at the centre of the EPR resonator. For each of the three replicates CW-EPR spectra were recorded at 12 K using microwave powers of 20.2 mW, 12.8 mW, 8.0 mW 3.2 mW and 1.0 mW (unless indicated otherwise), a field modulation of 20 G at 100 kHz, a magnetic field sweep rate of 13.3 G/s, a resolution of 1.02 points/G, a conversion time of 73.44 ms and a time constant of 20.48 ms. The microwave frequency was ≈9.37 GHz. Background spectra from an empty CFQ quartz tube (2 mm ID, 3 mm OD) were recorded under the same experimental conditions. Simulations to extract the magnetic parameters were performed using EasySpin (version 5.2.35)61.

X-ray Absorption Spectroscopy (XAS) at the Ni K-edge

Square pieces (5 ×5 mm) of polytetrafluorethylene (PTFE) were glued onto a 3D-printed sample holder. Dense clumps of native cable bacterium filaments were deposited onto each square, air dried and fixed by polyimide tape (Kapton). Reference compounds included nickel(II)oxide (NiO; TCI Europe N1265), nickel(IV)oxide (NiO2; Sigma-Aldrich 72262-5 G), six monomeric Ni-bis(dithiolene) compounds (same as for Raman spectroscopy; Table S3) and two polymeric Ni-bis(dithiolene) compounds (pNi(ett) and p(TTFtt); Table S3). All reference compounds were in powder form and diluted with cellulose binder for pellet pressing. Dilution for optimal X-ray absorption to minimize self-absorption was calculated using the XAFSmass software65. Calculated amounts were weighed on an electronic precision balance, and cellulose was added to a final weight of 60 mg. Powders and binder were mixed by grinding with a pestle and mortar for 15 min. Next, the mixtures were pressed into 1 mm thick pellets with a diameter of 8 mm using a pressure of 5 tons. Finally, the pellets were inserted in a metal sample holder and held in place with polyimide tape.

Ni K-edge XAS spectra were recorded at the I20 Scanning Beamline of the Diamond Light Source synchrotron66, which operates at a ring energy of 3 GeV and current of ~200 mA. The I20 beamline uses a Si (111) four-bounce monochromator with an energy resolution of 1.5 × 10−4 (ΔE/E), resulting in an energy resolution of 1.25 eV at the Ni K-edge (8333 eV), and mirror focusing that provides a beam spot size of 250 × 400 μm (height × width). All spectra were recorded in fluorescence mode due to the dilute nature of the samples, and under cryogenic conditions (9 K, pulsed-tube top-loading He cryostat) as to limit beam damage. A 64-element monolithic Ge detector with Xspress4 modular digital pulse processing system measured fluorescence photons from the sample perpendicular to the X-ray beam. The incident beam energy was calibrated to 8333 eV using a Ni foil. Reference spectra were measured over a range from 8200 to 8900 eV in steps of 0.2 eV using an acquisition time of 1 s. Cable bacteria samples were examined in a range from 8200 to 8900 eV with steps of 5 eV in the pre-edge region and 0.2 eV in the edge region, and acquisition time of 3 s per step. Multiple replicate scans were acquired per sample, and the effect of radiation damage was carefully monitored. The acquired XAS spectra were normalized and merged using “Athena” module of the Demeter XAS processing software (version 0.9.26)67.

Image analysis and processing of SEM, TEM and nXRF

Image analysis was performed in Fiji/ImageJ (version 1.54 g)59. To derive the ridge compartment width from SEM images of native filaments, line transects were drawn perpendicular to the filament, which revealed consecutive intensity maxima corresponding to ridge crests. The ridge compartment width was measured as the distance between two ridge maxima, and the mean and standard deviation were calculated for a number of n replicates. TEM cross-section images were analysed in ImageJ to determine the fibre diameter within individual ridge compartments. To this end, the fibre core that appeared less stained (Fig. 1d) was delineated via the Freehand Line tool and the surface area A was calculated. The fibre diameter was calculated as d=2A/π. The mean and standard deviation are reported for a number of n replicates.

The elemental areal mass density maps resulting from nXRF mapping were processed within ImageJ and R software. In the elemental mappings of fibre skeletons, we determined (1) the mean ridge width from the Ni map (i.e., the distance between two ridge maxima), and (2) the Ni and S areal mass density of the fibre skeletons, which then provides the S/Ni ratio. In the mappings of fragmented fibre skeletons, we determined (1) the Ni content of individual fibres and (2) the S/Ni ratio of individual fibres. Error propagation was implemented via standard expressions. Mean and standard deviations are reported.

Density Functional Theory (DFT) simulations

DFT calculations were performed to predict the electronic ground state of four different structural models of Ni3(ett)4 oligomers with different end-capping groups (Fig. 3e). Trimers (n = 3) were chosen as representative for Nin(ett)n oligomers to keep the computational cost at bay. DFT calculations were performed using the Turbomole code68 implementing different density functionals: PBE69, OPBE70 and B3LYP71,72, in combination with Grimme’s D3 dispersion correction73,74 including Becke-Johnson dampening, and either the def2-SVP or def2-TZVP basis-set75. The geometries of the oligomers were initially optimized as closed-shell singlets before stability analysis76 was used to check the stability of the closed-shell Kohn-Sham (KS) wavefunction with respect to open-shell instabilities. For those cases where the closed-shell singlet KS wavefunction was found to be unstable, the geometries of the oligomers were additionally optimized for a set of possible open-shell spin states (open-shell singlet, triplet, quintet).

Additionally, time-dependent DFT (TD-DFT) simulations of the Ni K-edge were performed for pNi(ett) oligomers with three Ni centres (Nin(ett)n+1 with n = 3; end capping as in structure III of Fig. 3e) using the ORCA DFT package77. The geometries of the simulated molecular structures were optimized using the B3LYP density functional71 in combination with the dispersion correction of Grimme’s method73,74 until convergence was achieved within 10−8 a.u. The Ahlrichs’ def2-TZVPP basis-set was chosen for the simulations after comparison of the ground-state variational energy against both the Pople’s style and Dunning’s correlation consistent basis sets75. The ZORA approximation was used to account for the spin-orbit coupling, while the RIJCOSX approximation was included to speed up the calculations. The generalized-gradient PBE functional, hybrid B3LYP, PBE0 functionals, and long-range separated hybrid functional ωB97X-D3 functional78 were employed in the TD-DFT calculations within the Tamm-Dancoff approximation formalism for the simulation of XANES spectra. The simulated XANES spectra show the results obtained with the ωB97X-D3 functional, which proved to be most accurate in predicting the energies of the excited states and the relative intensities of XAS features. All simulated spectra have been convoluted with a Gaussian broadening, representing the Ni 1s core-hole lifetime of 1.5 eV.

Statistics and reproducibility

To document the structure of the periplasmic cell envelope, TEM and SEM imaging was conducted on native filaments from the E. gigas JX3−16 clonal strain. Imaging was applied to filaments retrieved from different clonal cultures (i.e., cultures grown independently after transfer) and on multiple filaments within the same clonal culture. Filaments always displayed a similar geometry (cell diameter) and morphology (number of ridge compartments) as illustrated by the representative examples in Figs. 1b–d and S1b–d, S1f–g.

Nano-X-ray Fluorescence elemental mapping was conducted in two separate sessions at beamline ID16A of the ESRF synchrotron facility (Grenoble, France). In the first session, samples from native filaments and fibre skeletons were imaged. In total, 12 two-dimensional μXRF scans were performed, thus providing ROIs of filaments containing multiple consecutive cells (as exemplified by the images in Figs. 1e–f and S1i–p, S2a, b). In the second session, fibre skeletons and fractionated fibre skeletons were sampled, and 11 two-dimensional μXRF scans were collected (the images in Fig. 1g–o provide representative examples). The elemental maps on both nanoXRF sessions were highly consistent.

STEM-HAADF imaging was conducted in four separate sessions, which different individually prepared samples of fractionated fiber skeletons deposited on the TEM grids (TEM grid codes A6, A10, C10, C9). Nanoribbon bundles were systematically present on all grids prepared (Figs. 2a and 2b–g are images obtained from different TEM grids; Figs. S2j–k and 3 images are taken from the same grid as Fig. 2b–g).

Multi-wavelength Raman microscopy data were repeatedly (>5 times) collected in different sessions to native filaments and fibre skeleton samples extracted from different clonal cultures of E. gigas JX3−16. Raman spectra (as depicted in Figs. 3a and S4a) were highly comparable between sessions. Polarized-light microscopy was performed one time on native filaments retrieved from a natural sediment incubation of Rattekaai sediment, containing Ca. E. gigas filaments, but also thinner filaments from other species of cable bacteria (Fig. 4a–d). EPR spectroscopy was conducted on native filaments retrieved from a single culture. Three biological replicates were prepared (as described above and in Table S7).

X-ray Absorption Spectroscopy (XAS) was conducted in three separate sessions at the I20 Scanning beamline of the Diamond Light Source synchrotron (UK). Within each session, XAS spectra were collected for intact filaments retrieved from a different clonal cultures of E. gigas JX3-16 (Figs. 3c, d and S6h–i). The spectra collected on these three occasions were highly consistent and similar.
