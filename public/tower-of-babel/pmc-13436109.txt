Sepsis of urinary vs non-urinary tract origin in nonagenarians and centenarians admitted to Australian and New Zealand intensive care units: a propensity-matched multicentre cohort analysis
Authors: Je Min Suh, Laurence Weinberg, Kaden Tran, Nattaya Raykateeraroj, David Pilcher, Dong-Kyu Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesTo compare clinical outcomes between urinary and non-urinary tract sepsis in nonagenarians and centenarians admitted to intensive care units (ICUs).DesignRetrospective propensity score-matched multicentre cohort study.SettingAdult ICUs participating in the Australian and New Zealand Intensive Care Society Adult Patient Database across Australia and New Zealand (2010–2023).ParticipantsWe included all ICU admissions for sepsis in patients aged 90 years or older, classified as urinary or non-urinary tract sepsis using Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation III-J diagnostic codes. After 1:1 nearest neighbour propensity score matching, 1130 patients (565 per group) were included.Primary and secondary outcome measuresThe primary outcome was all-cause mortality within 180 days and beyond 180 days. Secondary outcomes included ICU mortality, hospital mortality, ICU length of stay and hospital length of stay.ResultsOf 1669 patients, 573 had urinary tract sepsis and 1096 had non-urinary tract sepsis. After matching, 1130 patients were included (565 per group). In the matched cohort, non-urinary tract sepsis was associated with higher mortality within 180 days than urinary tract sepsis (HR 1.67, 95% CI 1.33 to 2.09, p<0.001), whereas mortality beyond 180 days did not differ (HR 1.03, 95% CI 0.80 to 1.34, p=0.816). ICU and hospital length of stay were similar between groups.ConclusionsAmong nonagenarian and centenarian ICU patients with sepsis, infection source was an important determinant of early but not late mortality, with non-urinary tract sepsis conferring a higher risk than urinary tract sepsis. Incorporating infection source and simple physiological markers alongside illness severity scores may improve prognostication and support shared decision-making in this very old population.

Introduction

Sepsis poses a major global challenge at the crossroads of population ageing and infection management. Although advances in diagnostics and treatment have decreased sepsis-related mortality on a case-by-case basis,1 sepsis incidence continues to rise by 1%–8% annually.2 3 This trend is particularly consequential for nonagenarians and centenarians. The global nonagenarian population is expanding rapidly, increasing from an estimated 16.3 million in 2015 to a projected 30.9 million by 2030.4 With ageing, rising susceptibility to infection and its complications results in disproportionately elevated morbidity and mortality,5 6 owing to the combined effects of altered physiology, immunosenescence and multimorbidity.6

Infections triggering a maladaptive host response can tip the balance from defence to life-threatening organ injury, the hallmark of sepsis.7 Infection origin might critically influence the trajectory of sepsis. Urinary tract infections (UTIs) are among the most frequent sources of sepsis in older people.8 However, non-urinary sources, such as pulmonary, intra-abdominal and bloodstream infections, typically provoke more severe inflammatory responses.9 Although vulnerability to infections and sepsis increases with age, evidence in nonagenarians and centenarians remains sparse.8 10–12 Given their reduced physiological reserve, greater burden of frailty and distinct critical care decision-making considerations, evidence from younger older adults may not be directly applicable to nonagenarians and centenarians.

Findings from broader adult cohorts have indicated that UTI-related sepsis is associated with more favourable prognosis than sepsis of non-urinary origin.13 Whether infection source determines health trajectories in patients of very advanced age, whose immune and physiological state vastly differ from those of younger patients, remains unclear.

A critical evidence gap pertains to whether infection source influences outcomes in this age group and how such knowledge can be applied in practice. Determining whether prognosis might differ between sepsis with UTI-associated vs non-urinary sources is essential for accurate prognostication, guiding treatment decisions and optimising intensive care resource use. This study was aimed at addressing these gaps by investigating how mortality, complications and length of stay differ between sepsis of urinary tract origin vs non-urinary tract origin among nonagenarians and centenarians admitted to intensive care units (ICUs).

Methods

Data sources, study population and variables

The study followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines. We used de-identified patient-level data from the Australian and New Zealand Intensive Care Society (ANZICS) Adult Patient Database, which includes demographics, clinical characteristics, interventions and outcomes. This database captures approximately 98% of ICUs in Australia and 68% in New Zealand, with data collected and validated by the ANZICS Centre for Outcome and Resource Evaluation.

We included all nonagenarians and centenarians admitted to a participating ICU between 2010 and 2023 with a primary diagnosis of sepsis, defined using the Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation (APACHE) III-J system.14 The source of sepsis was classified using these standardised diagnostic codes based on the treating clinical team’s diagnosis and was not independently confirmed by microbiological culture. Demographic variables collected were age, sex, admission type and source, discharge destination, and ICU and hospital admission dates. Biochemical markers were recorded as peak and nadir values within the first 24 hours of ICU admission. Total urine output for the first 24 hours was calculated; if urine collection was incomplete or the ICU stay was shorter than 24 hours, volumes were extrapolated accordingly. Clinical information included frailty, assessed with the modified Clinical Frailty Scale, and illness severity, assessed with the APACHE III score. Comorbidities recorded were immunosuppression, malignancy, and chronic respiratory, cardiovascular, hepatic and renal conditions.

For transparency, we note that a previous Scientific Reports publication that was recently accepted analysed patients with septic shock only, based on a different APACHE III-J diagnostic code.15 The present manuscript represents an expanded analysis that includes all sepsis cases. We intend to ensure full compliance with journal and Committee on Publication Ethics (COPE) standards and to avoid any perception of redundant publication.

Study outcomes

The primary outcome was time-dependent all-cause mortality following ICU admission, assessed using time-split Cox proportional hazards regression to evaluate differences in mortality between patients with urinary tract and non-urinary tract sepsis. Secondary outcomes included ICU mortality, hospital mortality, ICU and hospital lengths of stay, and differences in physiological parameters and biomarker profiles within the first 24 hours of ICU admission between the two groups.

Statistical analyses

All statistical analyses were performed in R version 4.4.3 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). To decrease confounding and achieve balanced baseline characteristics between patients with urinary tract vs non-urinary tract sepsis sources, we conducted nearest-neighbour 1:1, calliper 0.1, no replacement propensity score matching with the MatchIt package. Propensity scores were generated via logistic regression models including age, sex, APACHE III score, Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) score, admission source, admission type, chronic respiratory disease, chronic cardiovascular disease, chronic renal disease, immunosuppression, malignancy (metastatic disease, lymphoma and leukaemia), hepatic failure, cirrhosis, immune disease and immunotherapy. Covariate balance was assessed with standardised mean differences and illustrated with Love plots with shows standadised mean difference (SMD) post-match of less than 0.1 for all variables. Continuous variables are reported as medians with IQRs, and were compared with the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. Categorical variables were compared with χ² or Fisher’s exact tests, as appropriate. The proportional hazards assumption was evaluated with Schoenfeld residuals and found to be violated. Accordingly, time-dependent Cox proportional hazards models were used to evaluate early (≤180 days) and late (>180 days) mortality separately, stratified by follow-up period, and met the proportional hazards assumption. ICU and hospital length of stay were log-transformed, analysed with linear regression and then back-transformed to obtain geometric mean ratios (GMRs) for interpretability.

The discriminative performance of key clinical scores and admission biomarkers for ICU and hospital mortality was further assessed with the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC), and 95% CIs were obtained through 2000 bootstrap replicates. Missing data were minimal across the variables included in the analyses. Therefore, complete case analysis was performed, resulting in the exclusion of only a very small number of observations, and no imputation was undertaken. All tests were two-sided, and p values <0.05 were considered statistically significant. No adjustments were made for multiple comparisons in secondary or exploratory outcomes.

Results

Baseline characteristics

The study included 1669 nonagenarians and centenarians with ICU admission for sepsis (baseline characteristics before and after propensity score matching in table 1, online supplemental table 1, table 2, and online supplemental table 2 respectively). In the unmatched cohort (table 1 and online supplemental table 1), patients with sepsis of non-urinary tract origin (n=1096), rather than urinary tract origin (n=573), had significantly higher partial pressure of arterial carbon dioxide (PaCO2) (35 vs 33 mm Hg, p=0.003) and bilirubin (14 vs 12 µmol/L; p<0.001). Patients with urinary tract sepsis had higher maximum and minimum creatinine (139 vs 128 µmol/L, p<0.001; 126 vs 118 µmol/L, p<0.001), maximum and minimum white blood cell counts (16.6 vs 14.1×10⁹/L, p<0.001; 13.6 vs 11.7×10/L, p<0.001), and urine output (1380 vs 1200 mL/day, p<0.001), and lower minimum bicarbonate (20 vs 21 mmol/L, p=0.049), than those with non-urinary tract sepsis. Age; APACHE III, SOFA and Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) scores; and survival days did not significantly differ between groups.

After 1:1 propensity score matching (figure 1), 1130 patients were included (565 in each group). No significant differences were observed in age (91.9 vs 92.0 years, p=0.735) (table 2 and online supplemental table 2), or APACHE III (70 vs 70, p=0.468), SOFA (4 vs 4; p=0.550), or GCS (15 vs 15, p=0.629) scores at ICU admission between groups. Several statistically significant laboratory and physiological differences remained between groups. The patients with urinary tract sepsis had higher maximum creatinine (138 vs 121 µmol/L, p<0.001), minimum creatinine (126 vs 114 µmol/L, p=0.002), maximum white blood cell counts (16.6 vs 14.6×10⁹/L; p<0.001), minimum white blood cell counts (13.6 vs 11.9×10⁹/L, p=0.004) and urine output (1385 vs 1211 mL/day; p=0.007), whereas the patients with non-urinary tract sepsis had higher bilirubin (13 vs 12.5 µmol/L; p=0.008), and maximum and minimum bicarbonate (23 vs 22 mmol/L, p<0.001; 21 vs 20 mmol/L, p=0.034).

Analysis of categorical variables after matching showed similar distributions of sex (female: 43.7% vs 42.3%; p=0.631), hospital source of admission, ICU source of admission and readmission between groups. The urinary tract sepsis group had lower ICU mortality (5.7% vs 9.6%, p=0.015) and hospital mortality (12.6% vs 20.6%, p<0.001) than the non-urinary tract sepsis group.

Mortality analyses

In the unmatched cohort (table 3), patients with sepsis of non-urinary tract origin had a 63.8% higher hazard of death within 180 days than those with urinary tract sepsis (table 3; HR 1.638, 95% CI 1.341 to 2.001, p<0.001). Beyond 180 days, no statistically significant difference in mortality risk was observed between groups (HR 0.945, 95% CI 0.754 to 1.184, p=0.622).

In the matched cohort, the excess early mortality risk persisted. Patients with non-urinary tract sepsis had a 66.7% higher hazard of death within 180 days than those with urinary tract sepsis (HR 1.667, 95% CI 1.328 to 2.094, p<0.001). Beyond 180 days, the mortality risk between groups converged and no longer significantly differed (HR 1.032, 95% CI 0.795 to 1.339, p=0.816).

Associations between baseline variables and mortality were also evaluated. In both unmatched and matched analyses, the APACHE III score was strongly associated with mortality, with each 1-point increase corresponding to a 2.7% increase in the hazard of death (unmatched HR 1.027, 95% CI 1.022 to 1.032, p<0.001; matched HR 1.027, 95% CI 1.021 to 1.033, p<0.001). Greater age was also associated with mortality, with each additional year corresponding to a 4.5% increase in the hazard of death in the unmatched cohort and a 5.1% increase in the matched cohort (unmatched HR 1.045, 95% CI 1.013 to 1.078, p=0.005; matched HR 1.051, 95% CI 1.008 to 1.095, p=0.019). In contrast, SOFA score (unmatched HR 0.996, 95% CI 0.963 to 1.031, p=0.840; matched HR 1.003, 95% CI 0.959 to 1.048, p=0.911), sex (unmatched HR 1.065, 95% CI 0.927 to 1.222, p=0.375; matched HR 1.042, 95% CI 0.876 to 1.239, p=0.639) and elective admission (unmatched HR 1.399, 95% CI 0.847 to 2.310, p=0.190; matched HR 1.421, 95% CI 0.824 to 2.451, p=0.207) were not significantly associated with mortality in either cohort.

Sensitivity analyses using multivariable Cox regression in the propensity score-matched cohort demonstrated that the association between non-urinary tract sepsis and higher early mortality remained significant after adjustment for age, sex, APACHE III score, SOFA score, elective admission status and treatment limitation status (adjusted HR 1.611, 95% CI 1.278 to 2.032, p<0.001; online supplemental table 3). No significant association was observed beyond 180 days (adjusted HR 1.018, 95% CI 0.781 to 1.327, p=0.895).

Length of stay

The associations between covariates and ICU and hospital length of stay were assessed (table 4). In the unmatched cohort, higher SOFA score (GMR 1.039; 95% CI 1.017 to 1.062, p<0.001) and APACHE III score (GMR 1.004; 95% CI 1.001 to 1.006, p=0.016) were significantly associated with longer ICU stay, whereas no variables were associated with hospital stay. In the matched cohort, both SOFA score (GMR 1.036; 95% CI 1.009 to 1.063, p=0.010) and APACHE III score (GMR 1.005; 95% CI 1.002 to 1.009, p=0.006) remained significantly associated with prolonged ICU stay. No covariates were significantly associated with hospital length of stay in the matched analysis.

Discriminatory performance of clinical prognostic factors

The top five univariate prognostic factors of ICU and hospital mortality were ranked by AUROC and stratified by infection source (table 5). Among patients with sepsis of non-urinary tract origin, the APACHE III score demonstrated the highest discriminatory performance for ICU mortality (AUROC 0.767; 95% CI 0.715 to 0.819) and was followed by urine output (0.714; 95% CI 0.654 to 0.775), SOFA score (0.709; 95% CI 0.656 to 0.761), systolic blood pressure (0.709; 95% CI 0.650 to 0.767) and lactate (0.693; 95% CI 0.604 to 0.783). In this group, the APACHE III score (AUROC 0.726; 95% CI 0.687 to 0.765) and urine output (0.671; 95% CI 0.626 to 0.715) were also the strongest prognostic factors of hospital mortality, whereas urea (0.652; 95% CI 0.610 to 0.694), SOFA score (0.651; 95% CI 0.610 to 0.691) and lactate (0.640; 95% CI 0.573 to 0.706) had more modest prognostic accuracy.

Discussion

Key findings

This binational retrospective cohort study of critically ill nonagenarians and centenarians with sepsis demonstrates that infection source significantly influences short-term mortality outcomes. Patients with non-urinary tract sepsis experienced substantially higher mortality during the initial 6 months compared with those presenting with urinary tract sepsis, a difference that persisted despite rigorous propensity score matching. Notably, this mortality disparity was confined to the acute and subacute phases of illness, with survival curves converging beyond 6 months, suggesting that infection source primarily affects early rather than long-term prognosis in this exceptionally elderly population. Despite successful matching for age and established severity scores including APACHE III, SOFA and Glasgow Coma Scale, distinct physiological and biochemical phenotypes emerged between groups, with urinary tract sepsis patients demonstrating higher creatinine levels, white blood cell counts and urine output, while non-urinary tract sepsis patients exhibited elevated bilirubin and bicarbonate levels.

Our multivariable analyses identified APACHE III score and age as the sole independent predictors of mortality, while SOFA score, sex and elective admission status failed to demonstrate prognostic significance in this cohort. The relationship between severity scores and resource utilisation proved complex, with both APACHE III and SOFA scores correlating with prolonged intensive care unit length of stay, yet neither influenced overall hospital duration. Discriminatory analyses consistently established APACHE III score as the superior mortality prognostic factor across both infection groups and timepoints, with urine output emerging as a complementary prognostic marker. The robust performance of APACHE III scoring irrespective of infection source underscores its utility as a primary risk stratification tool for elderly patients with sepsis, while the prognostic value of simple physiological parameters such as urine output emphasises the continued relevance of basic clinical monitoring in guiding therapeutic decisions for this vulnerable population.

Relationship of the findings to the literature

Our findings align with and extend existing evidence highlighting infection source as an important determinant of mortality in sepsis, particularly among older critically ill populations. Prior systematic reviews have reported substantial variability in sepsis outcomes by infection site.16 17 Large multicentre observational studies have consistently indicated lower mortality in patients with UTIs rather than respiratory or intra-abdominal infections, even after adjustment for illness severity.13 18 19 Those observations closely parallel our results, thereby reinforcing that infection source is an independent prognostic indicator.

Mechanistic data further support these findings. Host-response profiling studies have demonstrated that infection site shapes inflammatory, immune and coagulation responses, thus influencing both organ dysfunction and outcomes. These findings are represented by variations in biomarker expression and immune cascade activation by infection site, thus indicating the biological plausibility of our observation of distinct survival trajectories.20

Our study addresses a notable gap by evaluating patients 90 years or older and using propensity score matching to rigorously assess the influence of infection source on mortality. Propensity score matching minimised confounding and enabled rigorous isolation of the prognostic effect of infection source. Non-urinary sepsis was associated with a 67% higher hazard of mortality within 180 days than urinary sepsis, despite comparable baseline illness severity.

Finally, our identification of APACHE III score and urine output as the strongest individual prognostic factors of mortality (AUROC >0.75) supports prior work demonstrating the prognostic value of simple physiological measures in sepsis.21 22 These findings reinforce ongoing calls to integrate pragmatic bedside variables into risk stratification models, alongside established severity scores, to improve prognostication in sepsis.

Clinical implications

Our findings have potential clinical implications. The observation that non-urinary tract sepsis was associated with substantially greater short-term mortality than urinary tract sepsis, despite similar baseline illness severity between groups, suggests that conventional severity scores alone may not fully capture prognostic differences in this population. Infection source may be considered alongside established clinical and physiological factors during early risk stratification and goals-of-care discussions for patients of very advanced age. The strong discriminatory value of simple physiological indices, particularly urine output, highlights the potential for pragmatic bedside measures to complement established severity scores. Future studies should evaluate whether integrating infection source and key physiological predictors into prognostic models improves risk stratification and shared decision-making in frail older populations.

Directions for future research

Future work should elucidate the mechanisms underlying the disproportionate early mortality risk associated with non-urinary sepsis in older adults. Prospective studies incorporating pathogen-specific data, antimicrobial resistance, immune profiling and adequacy of source control will be essential to clarify causal pathways. Validation of infection source as a prognostic marker in diverse healthcare systems, including resource-limited settings, would strengthen the generalisability of the findings. Furthermore, prognostic models integrating infection source with dynamic physiological markers should be prospectively tested to determine whether they increase predictive accuracy and clinical utility. Implementation research should also assess whether source-informed triage and treatment strategies might translate into improved outcomes and more efficient ICU utilisation.

Strengths and limitations

This study has several important strengths. To our knowledge, it is the first study examining the prognostic influence of infection source in sepsis among nonagenarian and centenarian ICU patients. The large sample size, drawn from a comprehensive binational intensive care registry, provided strong statistical power and supports generalisability within this age group. The use of 1:1 propensity score matching decreased confounding and ensured balance between groups. The application of time-split Cox models addressed violations of the proportional hazards assumption, thus clarifying the distinction between early and late mortality. Finally, AUROC-based analyses of physiologic predictors reinforced the practical value of simple bedside measures such as urine output and arterial pH.

Several limitations should also be acknowledged. The retrospective study design precluded causal inference, and residual confounding cannot be excluded despite matching. In particular, unmeasured factors such as the severity of frailty, treatment limitation orders and clinical decision-making thresholds may have influenced both management and outcomes. The study was confined to ICUs in Australia and New Zealand, both high-resource settings, thus potentially limiting the applicability of the findings to health systems with different sepsis epidemiology and ICU capacity. Microbiological data, including pathogen type and resistance profiles, were unavailable, and information on adequacy of source control and pre-ICU treatment delays was not captured. The source of infection was identified using APACHE III-J diagnostic coding rather than microbiological confirmation, which may introduce misclassification bias. Although APACHE III and SOFA scores were well balanced after matching, small residual differences in individual renal parameters remained, and residual confounding cannot be completely excluded. Moreover, although log-transformed linear regression is a commonly used approach for analysing skewed length-of-stay data, death may act as a competing event for discharge. Consequently, a competing-risk framework may provide additional insight and should be considered in future studies. Finally, although the use of a large binational multicentre registry enhances generalisability, ICU site identifiers were not available in the analysis dataset, and we were therefore unable to explicitly account for clustering at the ICU level. Residual between-centre variation may therefore remain.

Conclusion

Among critically ill patients aged 90 years or older with sepsis, non-urinary tract infection was associated with a 67% higher risk of early mortality than urinary tract infection, although this difference attenuated beyond 6 months. The combination of routine physiological parameters, particularly urine output, with APACHE III scoring demonstrated excellent prognostic discrimination. These findings support further evaluation of prognostic models tailored to the oldest ICU populations that incorporate infection source and readily available bedside measures to improve risk stratification and inform shared decision-making.
