Explainable and domain-adaptive prediction models for refrigerant charging in air conditioning systems within industrial processes
Authors: Junwon Lee, Joonho Chang, Jinsoo Kim, Minsuk Kwak, Heechul Jung
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAccurate prediction of refrigerant deficiency in consumer air conditioning (AC) systems is critical for optimizing energy efficiency and operational stability. However, existing data-driven models often suffer from significant performance degradation due to domain shift across different AC types and a lack of explanatory transparency. To address these challenges, we propose AC-RPX (AC-Refrigerant Prediction eXplainable AI), a unified framework that integrates a Domain Encoder augmented with domain-specific tokens and a Large Language Model (LLM) adapted via Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA). The Domain Encoder aligns sensor distributions across six consumer AC types to enable domain-robust refrigerant level prediction. Simultaneously, the LoRA-tuned LLM, trained on 280,000 expert-aligned sensor–reasoning pairs, generates case-specific chain-of-thought (CoT) explanations that explicitly link abnormal sensor patterns to the predicted refrigerant charge state. Validation on six AC sensor datasets demonstrates that AC-RPX achieves state-of-the-art accuracy and F1 scores, significantly outperforming conventional deep learning and domain adaptation baselines. Moreover, by providing intuitive natural language explanations, AC-RPX improves predictive performance over conventional rule-based heuristic methods while providing clearer diagnostic explanations on real-world consumer data. These results establish AC-RPX as a practical solution for automated refrigerant monitoring and decision support in real-world maintenance services.Keywords: Air Conditioning, Refrigerant Charging, Domain Adaptation, Explainable AI, Large Language ModelSubject terms: Engineering, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

In air conditioning (AC) systems, refrigerant plays a pivotal role in the cooling cycle by transferring heat from a low-temperature reservoir to a high-temperature reservoir through phase changes within the heat exchanger. Maintaining an optimal refrigerant charge is essential for ensuring energy efficiency and operational stability. Insufficient refrigerant not only degrades cooling performance but also lead to severe failures, including compressor damage and oil carbonization, when the system continues operating under deficit conditions. Conventionally, refrigerant diagnosis has relied on heuristic approaches based on thermodynamics, typically requiring experienced service engineers. Such manual inspection processes are labor-intensive and economically inefficient, incurring substantial time and service costs. Moreover, accurate refrigerant charge estimation itself is inherently challenging: sensor measurements are strongly influenced by ambient temperature, user behavior, and control logic, while fault states occur far less frequently than normal operation, resulting in sparse and imbalanced supervision even in large-scale deployments.

Recent advances in Artificial Intelligence (AI) have achieved remarkable success in domains such as computer vision1,2, natural language processing3,4, and medical diagnosis5,6, motivating their application to refrigerant charge prediction in AC systems. Although these approaches demonstrate promising performance under controlled conditions, widespread adoption faces two significant challenges. The first challenge is domain shift, which significantly limits generalization. Existing models perform well only when the test data distribution aligns with the training data. As illustrated in Fig. 1a, data-driven models achieve high accuracy within familiar domains. However, as shown in Fig. 1b, applying a model trained on one AC type to unseen AC units often results in substantial performance degradation. While collecting new data for every AC model could theoretically resolve this problem, it is operationally impractical in real-world consumer markets where new models are frequently released. This limitation necessitates Domain Adaptation (DA) methodologies, which mitigate distribution discrepancies and adapt learned knowledge from source domains to unseen target domains, thereby enabling robust prediction across diverse AC types.

The figure illustrates three challenges in traditional refrigerant charge level predictions: (a) the capability of AI-based models to predict refrigerant quantity at an expert level, (b) the domain shift problem complicating predictions when sensor data from different air conditioner types are input, and (c) the ’black box’ nature of existing AI models, which lack explainability and do not provide expert-level rationale for their predictions.

The second challenge lies in the lack of interpretability inherent to deep learning models. Despite achieving high accuracy, many existing models operate as ‘black boxes’, and fail to provide clear explanations for their predictions, as depicted in Fig. 1c. In industrial settings, field engineers often hesitate to trust opaque predictions–especially when safety or maintenance costs are at stake–without verifiable evidence, such as identifiable sensor anomalies. To bridge this gap, eXplainable Artificial Intelligence (XAI) has emerged as a critical research direction. XAI techniques can be broadly categorized into intrinsic interpretability approaches, where models are transparent by design, and post-hoc explainability, which interpret trained models after inference has been performed. While previous studies primarily relied on post-hoc methods, there is a growing demand for models that can provide intuitive, natural language reasoning to fully support human decision-making. In particular, practical AC maintenance requires explanations that clearly state which sensor variables and operating conditions support each prediction, rather than only providing numerical importance scores or saliency maps.

To overcome these challenges, we propose a unified framework named AC-Refrigerant Prediction eXplainable AI (AC-RPX). This novel architecture integrates a Domain Encoder augmented with domain-specific tokens and a Large Language Model (LLM) optimized via Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA). The Domain Encoder aligns sensor distributions across different AC units to enable robust prediction, while the LoRA-tuned LLM provides chain-of-thought (CoT) explanations that clearly connect abnormal sensor patterns to the predicted state. In practice, AC-RPX first encodes multivariate sensor sequences and AC-type identifiers into a domain-aware representation. This representation is then projected into the token embedding space of the LLM, allowing the model to jointly produce discrete refrigerant level predictions and free-form textual explanations from shared latent features. As illustrated in Fig. 2, AC-RPX not only predicts the refrigerant level accurately but also articulates key influencing factors in natural language, thereby improving user trust and interpretability. To ensure practical utility, AC-RPX was trained on 280,000 expert-aligned sensor-explanation pairs collected from real-world consumer AC systems, thereby grounding its outputs in empirical field engineering knowledge. We validate AC-RPX on six consumer AC datasets and a field study involving 90 real-world units. Experimental results demonstrate that AC-RPX achieves state-of-the-art accuracy and F1 scores across DA benchmarks and surpasses rule-based heuristic methods in both predictive performance and explanatory clarity on real consumer data. These findings indicate that AC-RPX can provide a practical foundation for automated refrigerant monitoring and decision support in consumer AC maintenance services. Related work

Rule-based Heuristic methods

Traditionally, system engineers have diagnosed refrigerant charge levels in air conditioning (AC) systems using rule-based heuristic method7, as summarized in Table 1. This approach involves a sequential review of ’Pre-Condition Failure’ and ’Failure’ conditions. If a ’Pre-Condition Failure’ factor is identified, the diagnostic process proceeds to evaluate the ’Failure’ conditions in the subsequent step. A persistent ’Failure’ state for 10 minutes is then utilized as a definitive indicator of insufficient refrigerant. As detailed in Table 1, ’Pre-Condition Failure’ is triggered when four variables fall within predefined operational ranges. Among these, the ’operation mode’ is required to be set to ’Cooling’ for the application of the diagnostic rule. Similarly, the ’Failure’ state is defined based on whether another set of four variables satisfies specific criteria. Despite its widespread use, this heuristic approach is inherently inefficient and prone to accuracy limitations, as it struggles to account for complex, simultaneous interactions between multiple operational variables in real-world environments.

Domain adaptation (DA)

Deep learning models typically achieve high predictive performance when the feature space of test data aligns with that of training data. However, when models are evaluated on data outside the training distribution, a phenomenon known as domain shift occurs. This leads to a degradation in inferential performance due to the discrepancy between the source and target data distributions. To alleviate this challenge, Domain Adaptation (DA) methodologies have been developed to transfer knowledge from a source domain to a target domain, thereby minimizing distributional discrepancies.

Previous research in this field of DA has explored several approaches. For instance, Multiple Source Domain Adaptation (MSDA)8 integrates knowledge from multiple source domains (e.g., data from various machines) to diagnose defects in a new target domain. Another approach, Contrastive Adversarial Domain Adaptation (CADA)9, combines contrastive learning with adversarial DA to minimize inter-domain differences by pulling similar cross-domain samples closer in the latent space while pushing dissimilar ones apart. To tackle data scarcity, Hierarchical Alignment10 employs synthetic data to compensate for the lack of labeled real-world environmental data. Furthermore, unsupervised learning frameworks11 have been introduced to extract features without target labels, employing Maximum Mean Discrepancy (MMD) to minimize feature-level distance, alongside compressed models that eliminate redundant convolutional channels to reduce computational overhead. Most recently, the Knowledge Mining based Semantic-aware Network (KMSA-Net)12 was proposed to capture discriminative features across domains, maintaining cross-domain consistency, and imposes semantic constraints during the knowledge transfer process. While these methods effectively address distribution alignment, they often lack the interpretability required for industrial diagnostics–a gap our proposed AC-RPX framework aims to fill by integrating domain-aware encoding with natural language explanations.

Explainable AI (XAI)

Recent Artificial Intelligence (AI) systems deliver accurate predictions, yet their internal decision-making processes often remain opaque, leading to their perception as ’black boxes’. This lack of transparency can impede user trust and complicate the identification or rectification of erroneous outputs, a challenge formally recognized as the ’lack of explainability’. To address this, eXplainable AI (XAI) research aims to make model operations transparent, thereby fostering user confidence and enabling more reliable human-AI collaboration. XAI methodologies are generally categorized into intrinsic interpretability and post-hoc explainability.

Among intrinsic approaches, fuzzy logic13 serves as an effective methodology for modeling uncertainty in complex systems. It has been utilized to develop diagnostic frameworks for robot spot welding defects based on sensor data14, leveraging its ability to manage decision-making uncertainty for precise fault identification. Similarly, random forest15, an ensemble of decision trees, has been applied to the analysis and predictive maintenance of event logs in ship electric propulsion systems16. This approach enables the assessment of variable importance and identification of crucial information for monitoring system status. For more complex architectures, post-hoc methods are more prevalent. Grad-CAM17 visualizes the decision-making process of Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) by highlighting salient regions within the input data. It has been extensively used to validate the reliability and explainability of a power quality disturbance classifier18, to diagnose defects in linear motion guides19, and identify fault-related patterns in sensor data images20. Furthermore, SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP)21 provides the numerical contribution of each input feature to a specific prediction. This approach has been applied to defect diagnosis in industrial processes22 and real-time polyp diagnosis23 to interpret complex decision-making processes. However, while these methods provide visual saliency maps or numerical importance scores, they often fall short of providing the intuitive, natural language reasoning required by field engineers. Our proposed AC-RPX framework addresses this limitation by generating CoT explanations that translate abstract sensor patterns into human-readable rationales.

LLM and LMM

Recent advancements in Large Language Models (LLMs) and Large Multimodal Model (LMMs) have led to significant performance improvements by integrating diverse data modalities, including text24, images25, and audio26. LLMs have become instrumental in automating text-based tasks and emulating human-like cognitive processes. Sophisticated prompting techniques, such as chain-of-thought (CoT)27 and Plan-and-Solve28, have further enhanced complex problem-solving and reasoning capabilities. Consequently, LLMs are being increasingly deployed in specialized domains requiring expert-level knowledge, such as law29, finance30, and healthcare31. In industrial settings, LLMs such as GPT-4 have been utilized to optimize Heating, Ventilation, and Air Conditioning (HVAC) systems32. These models have demonstrated the capacity to develop high-performance controllers using significantly fewer samples than traditional reinforcement learning methods33. Building upon these advancements, specialized LMMs have emerged to handle the complex, multimodal data inherent in industrial environments. For instance, AnomalyGPT34 utilized large vision-language models to detect and localize industrial anomalies through interactive dialogue. Furthermore, FaultGPT35 integrates vibration signals with LLMs to automate the generation of comprehensive fault diagnosis reports, while Time-LLM36 aligns multivariate time-series sensor data with the linguistic space of LLMs for robust predictive maintenance. These advancements in LLM and LMM architectures enable the integrated analysis of multimodal data by bridging numerical sensor sequences with natural language reasoning, thereby offering transformative solutions for complex problem-solving.

Materials and methods

Materials

Data collection and annotation

In this study, we integrated sensor data from various indoor and outdoor units to construct a comprehensive dataset for predicting refrigerant charge levels in air conditioning (AC) systems. To ensure a robust representation of diverse operating conditions, we acquired a large-scale dataset comprising 500,000 samples for each of six AC types (denoted as A to F), resulting in a total of three million raw sensor samples. As illustrated in Fig. 3, each indoor unit was uniquely paired with a corresponding outdoor unit to maintain operational coherence.

Integration of sensor data from various indoor and outdoor units to construct a comprehensive dataset for refrigerant charge level prediction.

The data collection period varied by unit type. Data for types A, B, and C were collected over an 11-month period from September 17, 2021, to July 1, 2022. For types D, E, and F, the collection spanned a 3-month period from March 17 to May 17, 2023, resulting in six distinct datasets that cover various seasonal and environmental conditions. The experimental protocol was strictly controlled, beginning with an initial refrigerant charge set at 60% capacity, followed by incremental 10% additions on a weekly basis until reaching 100%. This cycle was reset to the 60% baseline every five weeks. The operational pattern of the AC unit was set to cycle ON and OFF every 10 minutes within a three-hour interval. Data were meticulously annotated based on refrigerant charge levels, delineated at 60%, 70%, 80%, 90%, and 100% for each AC type. Although these units represent diverse commercial hardware configurations, the specific model names and trademarks were anonymized as arbitrary identifiers (A to F) in compliance with confidentiality agreements.

We analyzed an initial dataset of 126 variables and performed a variable selection process to identify features pertinent to refrigerant charge level prediction in AC systems. We first excluded 59 constant-value variables (e.g., 0, 1, 255, NaN) and 12 attribute variables (e.g., LED brightness, serial numbers, human detection mode, and operational hours) deemed irrelevant to predictions. To distill the remaining 55 variables down to the most influential features, we employed the SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) methodology. Figure 4 presents a representative magnified SHAP visualization for class 0 (60% refrigerant level), which clearly shows the variable names and their corresponding importance scores. The complete SHAP visualizations for the remaining refrigerant levels are provided as n Figs. S1–S4 online. Following consistent analysis across all AC types, 17 common variables were selected, as summarized in Table 2.

The raw sensor data were originally collected as continuous time-series over a five-week cycle ranging from 60% to 100% refrigerant levels. Sequential structures were not directly utilized for model training and evaluation. Instead, our team reconstructed the dataset using a state-based sampling strategy. Data were grouped by discrete refrigerant levels including 60%, 70%, 80%, 90%, and 100%, and an equal number of samples were randomly drawn from each group across the entire temporal range. This procedure intentionally breaks temporal continuity and reduces the likelihood that temporally adjacent or highly correlated samples are simultaneously included in both training and test sets. Each sample corresponds to a stabilized operating state after sufficient system runtime, which allows the observations to be treated as approximately independent. The risk of temporal data leakage is reduced through this design. The reconstructed dataset was subsequently split into training, validation, and test sets with a ratio of 80%, 10%, and 10%, respectively while preserving identical class distributions across all splits.

Refrigerant charge is inherently a continuous physical quantity. In this study, it is treated as a discrete classification problem. In practical maintenance scenarios, service engineers typically assess system conditions using discrete levels (e.g., HIGH, MID, and LOW) rather than estimating exact values. The intervals used in this study (60% to 100%) are designed to reflect these real-world diagnostic criteria. Furthermore, since the data were collected at fixed 10% increments under steady-state conditions, they are naturally organized into well-defined discrete levels. In addition, sensor measurements are affected by environmental conditions and operational noise, under which a classification approach provides more stable and robust predictions than regression.

Building upon the 17 identified sensor variables, we constructed an explanatory dataset to support expert-aligned diagnostic explanation generation. We sampled 50,000 instances for each AC type (totaling 300,000) from the large-scale pool to generate diagnostic rationales regarding refrigerant charge levels. Given the impracticality of manually drafting explanations for such a large volume of data, we utilized open-source Large Language Models (LLMs), specifically Mixtral-8x7B37 and Llama2-70B38, to automate the rationale generation process. Comparative experiments demonstrated that the Mixtral-8x7B model provided superior logical consistency and more accurate explanations, with an error rate of approximately 10%, compared to the 20% error rate observed with Llama2-70B.

Consequently, Mixtral-8x7B was selected for the final data generation. Figure 5 shows the comparison of explanatory data generation results between the two models. Then, we developed an optimized template for data generation, which was combined with the sensor data to construct the input, and performed zero-shot generation locally. As shown in Fig. 6a, expert judgment criteria were used as prompts to enable the LLM to articulate the reasoning behind the predicted refrigerant levels. Following the automated generation, we conducted an evaluation process as depicted in Fig. 6b. Human experts manually reviewed the outputs to correct sensor value errors and eliminate logically flawed inferences. This rigorous pipeline resulted in a final dataset of 280,000 high-quality explanatory pairs that aligned with expert opinions.

To ensure the reliability of the generated explanatory data, the outputs from Mixtral-8x7B were not directly used as final labels. Instead, they were treated as preliminary drafts and subsequently refined through a human validation process conducted by domain experts. During this stage, the generated rationales were reviewed for consistency with sensor measurements, and physically implausible or logically inconsistent explanations were removed. This human-in-the-loop curation process allows that the final dataset reflects domain knowledge rather than relying solely on model-generated outputs. Although this procedure does not completely eliminate all potential biases, it mitigates the impact of model-induced artifacts and improves the overall reliability of the training data used for the LoRA-tuned LLM.

Methods

We introduce AC-RPX, a unified framework that integrates domain-aware feature extraction with large-scale language understanding to address domain shift and provide interpretability. As illustrated in Fig. 8, AC-RPX consists of three synergistic components. First, a Domain Encoder as shown in Fig. 7 infused with domain-specific tokens (D tokens) extracts domain-invariant features from raw sensor data, allowing the model to generalize across diverse AC types. Second, a Sensor-to-Text Projection Layer serves as a cross-modal bridge by transforming the numerical features into semantic token embeddings compatible with an LLM. Last, our approach employs Mistral-8x7B39, fine-tuned using LoRA40, to leverage its extensive prior knowledge in interpreting sensor patterns. This integrated design enables a structured and logically grounded inference process (SQ → A) generating diagnostic outputs and expert-aligned natural language explanations (A) from sensor data (S) and associated questions (Q).

SWA limits the range of tokens to be processed at each layer by performing calculations only on tokens within a specified window size. Experiments

Prior to describing our experiments, we curated a large-scale dataset derived from three million air conditioning (AC) sensor records. Recently, data synthesis leveraging Large Language Models (LLMs) has gained significant traction. For instance, Alpaca43 utilized GPT-3 to process a ’Self-Instruct’ pipeline, generating 52,000 instructional examples to train the LLaMA-70B38 model. Similarly, Vicuna44 employed conversational datasets obtained from user interactions with ChatGPT.

In this study, we utilized an LLM to generate an explanatory dataset for refrigerant charge levels. Due to the proprietary nature of the corporate sensor data, the use of closed-source models such as ChatGPT or Gemini was unsuitable for maintaining data security. Consequently, we opted for the open-source Mixtral-8x7B37 to generate explanatory rationales. This approach enabled us to construct a curated dataset of 280,000 high-quality sensor–reasoning pairs, grounded in expert judgment criteria and refined through error feedback. Further technical details on the data generation pipeline are documented in the Materials section.

To address this, we evaluated several DA baselines, including DAN47, DANN48, CDAN49, DCAN50, CADA51, SDAT52, and DAT7, which improved accuracy by aligning source and target feature distributions. However, these methods were originally developed for image-based domains and primarily focus on achieving global distribution alignment. As a result, they are less effective at capturing complex physical relationships and specific sensor logic inherent in refrigerant cycles. In contrast, our proposed DA-TE utilizes the TE as a feature extractor, enhanced by the inclusion of a D Token to achieve superior accuracy. Rather than forcing all data into a singular global distribution, these tokens function as machine-specific identifiers that allow the model to dynamically adjust feature importance based on the unique characteristics of each AC type. This design enables the encoder to learn representations that are both physically meaningful and robust across domains.

The further performance improvement achieved by AC-RPX is attributed to the integration of an LLM. While conventional numerical models rely solely on statistical correlations, the LLM incorporates diagnostic patterns learned from expert-aligned explanations, including physically meaningful relationships such as the association between discharge temperature and refrigerant mass.

This knowledge-aligned explanation generation is particularly effective for borderline cases where purely data-driven models often struggle to make consistent decisions. Furthermore, AC-RPX demonstrates enhanced predictive stability. The lower standard deviation observed for AC-RPX compared to DA-TE suggests that the LLM contributes to more reliable and stable performance across diverse domain configurations. Collectively, these results indicate that the synergistic integration of domain-aware feature extraction and expert-aligned diagnostic explanation generation enables AC-RPX to robustly bridge distribution gaps across diverse AC systems.

Comparison with existing XAI methods

To evaluate the reliability and human-readability of the explanations provided by AC-RPX, we conducted comparative experiments against other XAI models. Specifically, we applied Random Forest, SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP), and Counterfactual Explanations to the Domain Encoder to serve as baselines.

Feature-based methods, such as Random Forest and SHAP, interpret model decisions by assigning importance scores to individual input variables. While Random Forest assigns importance scores based on node impurity, a metric that primarily evaluates variables in isolation and often overlooks complex inter-variable dependencies, SHAP considers inter-variable interactions, providing more consistent and reliable explanations at both global and local levels. As shown in Fig. 9, both Random Forest and SHAP predict refrigerant charge levels based on the importance of variables within the input sensor data. However, these approaches are inherently limited to identifying ’which’ variables are statistically significant, failing to provide a causal ’why’ or a logical rationale that a user can intuitively follow. Counterfactual Explanations53 describe model predictions by generating hypothetical inputs similar to the given input but with different predictive results. This method helps users understand which input adjustments would shift the model’s output toward a desired state. As shown in Fig. 10, when the generated sensor data closely aligns with the desired outcomes, refrigerant charge levels can be predicted. However, because these outputs are restricted to raw numerical values, they do not elucidate the underlying physical behavior of the AC system or provide the technical justification required for maintenance actions.

To overcome these limitations, AC-RPX was specifically designed to provide logically coherent and easily understandable explanations that incorporate the causal impact of various operating variables. Unlike conventional approaches, AC-RPX transforms complex model reasoning into a structured and intuitive natural language explanation. As demonstrated in Fig. 10, AC-RPX integrates raw sensor data with a structured reasoning process to provide interpretable diagnostic outputs, significantly enhancing model transparency. By providing evidence-based reasoning, it ensures its findings are both reliable and directly applicable to real-world industrial maintenance tasks.

Discussion

Accurate prediction of refrigerant charge levels in AC systems is critical for preventing efficiency degradation and component failure. However, the application of deep learning models in this domain has been hindered by two primary challenges: the domain shift problem arising from diverse AC types and the ’black-box’ nature of neural networks, which limits interpretability. In this study, we propose AC-RPX to address these limitations simultaneously by combining a DA-TE with an LLM. Our experimental results demonstrate that the domain shift problem, where performance degrades due to discrepancies between training and testing data distributions, can be effectively mitigated through the use of D tokens. As shown in Table 4, conventional methods such as ResNet-50 and the standard TE exhibited significantly lower accuracy (averaging 0.467 and 0.522, respectively) when evaluated on unseen domains. In contrast, our proposed DA-TE achieved a substantial improvement with an average accuracy of 0.764, and AC-RPX further optimized this to 0.773, establishing a new state-of-the-art performance. This suggests that the integration of D tokens enables the model to capture discriminative features across different AC units, maintaining cross-domain consistency without the need for extensive retraining on new datasets. Furthermore, the integration of Mixtral-8x7B with LoRA fine-tuning enables the generation of logically coherent explanations, addressing the lack of interpretability in existing models. While traditional XAI methods, such as SHAP or counterfactual analysis, provide numerical feature importance or hypothetical scenarios, they often fail to offer intuitive diagnostic explanations required for practical field applications. As illustrated in Fig. 6, AC-RPX surpasses these approaches by generating natural language rationales grounded in a curated dataset of 280,000 expert-aligned sensor–reasoning pairs, thereby producing diagnostic insights that align with expert-refined diagnostic reference explanations.

As shown in Table 12, field tests conducted on 90 real-world consumer AC units further validated this capability, where AC-RPX successfully identified ’HIGH’ risk cases that heuristic rule-based methods failed to detect (e.g., ID 9, 19, 23). This indicates that our data-driven approach provides improved diagnostic accuracy and clarity compared with conventional rule-based heuristic methods. Despite these promising results, there are some limitations to consider. The current model utilizes a specific subset of 17 sensor variables selected via SHAP analysis from an initial pool of 126. While these features proved effective for the AC types examined, future applications to broader systems may require a re-evaluation of feature importance. Additionally, although LoRA significantly reduces the number of trainable parameters to just 0.60% (approximately 60 million parameters), the computational cost of running an LLM remains higher than that of lightweight numeric models.

For future work, the proposed approach can be extended from static feature selection based on SHAP to a dynamic feature recalibration process within the Domain Encoder. We plan to incorporate attention-based gating mechanisms54,55 to automatically prioritize relevant sensor inputs according to real-time operational contexts. These methods allow for a more precise assessment of feature dependencies at the instance level and enable an adaptive diagnostic process tailored to varying load conditions. Given the diversity of industrial HVAC systems, such as Variable Refrigerant Flow (VRF) units and chillers, we aim to incorporate meta-learning56,57 for rapid adaptation to unseen domains with minimal data samples. Furthermore, to enable deployment on resource-constrained embedded hardware, we will optimize the LLM through model compression techniques such as Quantization-Aware Training (QAT)58,59 and knowledge distillation. These approaches are expected to significantly reduce computational latency and memory usage, establishing AC-RPX as a viable and efficient tool for real-time maintenance forecasting in diverse industrial settings.

Conclusion

This paper introduced AC-RPX, a novel approach that integrates domain adaptation with XAI to accurately predict and interpret refrigerant charge levels in AC systems. By incorporating D tokens into a DA-TE and leveraging a LoRA-tuned LLM for expert-aligned diagnostic explanation generation, AC-RPX effectively mitigates the domain shift problem while providing transparent, human-readable results. Our extensive experiments confirmed that the proposed model achieves state-of-the-art performance in domain adaptation and generates explanations with improved clarity compared to existing XAI methods. The practical utility of AC-RPX was validated through real-world experiments, where it outperformed the conventional rule-based heuristic method used as a field diagnostic baseline. By utilizing a curated dataset of 280,000 expert-aligned sensor–reasoning pairs, AC-RPX generates natural language rationales with improved clarity and logical coherence compared with conventional XAI outputs based on feature importance or counterfactual values. These findings suggest that AC-RPX can serve as a reliable tool for commercial applications, enabling efficient maintenance and reducing service costs by decreasing reliance on immediate on-site human intervention. By offering a solution that is both accurate across diverse AC configurations and explainable for end-users, this research contributes to the advancement of intelligent HVAC management systems. Moving forward, we plan to extend this framework to a broader spectrum of industrial cooling systems and optimize it for edge-device deployment through model compression techniques, ensuring real-time efficiency and broader industrial applicability.
