Novel method for displaying the contact index in fixed-loop pulsed field ablation catheters
Authors: Kazuki Ito, Yoshihisa Naruse, Makoto Sano, Yuichiro Maekawa
Published: 2026

Key Teaching Points •Evaluating circumferential electrode–tissue contact remains challenging during pulsed field ablation with fixed-loop catheters. Conventional imaging, such as fluoroscopy and intracardiac echocardiography, confirms macroscopic positioning but cannot guarantee individual electrode-level apposition.•A novel jumper-cable modification overcomes native system constraints by duplicating and rerouting electrode signals on the 3-dimensional mapping platform. This approach enables complete real-time visualization of contact index across all 9 electrodes of a fixed-loop pulsed field ablation catheter.•Impedance-based contact index feedback provides objective, electrode-specific confirmation of tissue contact before pulse delivery. Guiding ablation overlapping based on this visualization demonstrates an excellent spatial correlation with the final isolation line, may help standardize lesion placement.

Introduction

Adequate electrode–tissue contact is considered important for effective pulsed field ablation (PFA) in patients with atrial fibrillation (AF) using a fixed-loop PFA catheter (PulseSelect PFA Loop catheter, Medtronic, Minneapolis, MN). The contact index (CI) in EnSite X Software (Abbott, Abbott Park, IL) helps visualize tissue contact through impedance elevation.1 However, CI was originally designed for a spline-based PFA catheter (FARAWAVE, Boston Scientific, Marlborough, MA). When applied to a fixed-loop PFA catheter, only odd-numbered electrodes are displayed, making comprehensive assessment of all 9 electrodes difficult.

Case report

We modified the cable connection to allow CI visualization of all 9 electrodes. EnSite X standard configuration enables the display of odd-numbered unipolar signals from the fixed-loop PFA catheter as a flower-type catheter on the 3-dimensional (3D) map. Figure 1B demonstrates this technique during pulmonary vein isolation (PVI) for persistent AF. The fixed-loop PFA catheter is depicted in a flower-shaped configuration. CI-positive electrodes are shown in blue, with contact defined as a >12% impedance increase from baseline. Macroscopic apposition at the left superior pulmonary vein (LSPV) ostium was confirmed by fluoroscopic catheter deformation and absence of a visible gap on intracardiac echocardiography (ICE). The lower right panel of Figure 1B shows a sharp local pulmonary vein potential recorded by the fixed-loop PFA catheter at the LSPV ostium with a positive CI, consistent with a near-field electrogram. CI enabled real-time confirmation of electrode–tissue contact before pulse delivery (Supplemental Video). Although the patient had a history of persistent AF, the duration of the arrhythmia was relatively short, and the entire ablation procedure was performed during sinus rhythm without requiring direct-current cardioversion. For both the PV ostium and antrum, the number of PFA applications was guided by the 3D anatomical map and real-time CI feedback. Although a single application can theoretically create a continuous lesion under ideal contact conditions, positive contact (CI > 12%) at all 9 electrodes is not always achieved because of anatomical constraints and the inherent structural gap between electrodes 9 and 1. Therefore, the catheter was repeatedly rotated and repositioned using real-time CI feedback. During each application, 5-mm spherical tags were manually placed on the 3D map at the precise 3D locations of the electrodes that demonstrated a positive CI. However, during the procedure, local electrograms were frequently eliminated after the initial PFA applications, even at sites with incomplete catheter–tissue contact. Therefore, the identification of potential conduction gaps and the decision to deliver additional applications were based primarily on the spatial distribution of these 5-mm CI-positive tags rather than residual electrical activity. Additional PFA applications were sequentially delivered and overlapped until any anatomical gaps between the 5-mm tags were reduced to less than 5 mm, thereby ensuring a circumferential, gap-free lesion regardless of the early disappearance of local electrograms.

Figure 2A shows the voltage map acquired during sinus rhythm before ablation. Both the pre- and post-ablation voltage maps were created using the HD Grid mapping catheter (Abbott, Abbott Park, IL). The numbers of applications delivered in the LSPV, left inferior PV, right superior PV, and right inferior PV were 11 (3 at ostium and 8 at antrum), 9 (2 at ostium and 7 at antrum), 10 (2 at ostium and 8 at antrum), and 9 (2 at ostium and 7 at antrum), respectively. The proportion of CI-positive electrodes was 77% at the ostium and 37% at the antrum, respectively. Figure 2B illustrates the voltage map after PVI, highlighting ablation tags with positive CI. Importantly, the distribution of these ablation tags representing positive CI demonstrated a spatial correlation with the PVI line, supporting the feasibility of the 9-electrode visualization method. Discussion

Reliable assessment of electrode–tissue contact is important for effective PFA lesion formation. Several strategies have been reported to assess catheter–tissue contact during ablation using a fixed-loop PFA catheter. Fluoroscopy and ICE provide macroscopic assessment of catheter position and tissue apposition but cannot confirm contact at every electrode. Impedance-based metrics, such as CI, provide objective contact information through local impedance changes.1,2 Because standard EnSite X displays only the odd-numbered electrodes of a fixed-loop catheter, comprehensive assessment remains limited. Our signal-duplication method visualizes all 9 electrodes and enables electrode-level assessment before pulse delivery. In clinical practice, fixed-loop and variable-loop circular PFA catheters (VARIPULSE, Biosense Webster) may be favored in older patients or those at high bleeding-risk because of their smaller sheath profiles3 and lower reported hemolysis than spline-based PFA catheters,4 particularly when potassium loading is a concern in patients undergoing dialysis. In Japan, only fixed-loop and spline-based PFA catheters are approved for persistent AF; thus, a fixed-loop PFA catheter may be selected for older patients, patients at high bleeding risk, or patients undergoing dialysis who have persistent AF. Furthermore, a recent small study reported fewer silent cerebral lesions with the fixed-loop PFA catheter than with the variable-loop circular catheter, although this finding remains preliminary.5 When a fixed-loop PFA catheter is selected for these clinical reasons, our technique provides comprehensive electrode-level contact information during PFA.

This approach provides electrode-level contact feedback, allowing catheter adjustment before ablation, particularly at complex sites such as the ridge or carina. Once configured, it requires no additional hardware or changes in energy settings and complements fluoroscopic and ICE guidance. Whether it improves lesion durability requires further study.

The signal-duplication concept may be adaptable to other mapping systems. We explored a similar approach using the CARTO 3 system (Version 8.1, Biosense Webster), suggesting that similar signal-duplication strategies may be feasible across different mapping environments. However, compared with dedicated integrated platforms such as the OPAL HDx™ Mapping System (Boston Scientific), our non-native approach has several limitations. Integrated platforms use native, catheter-specific contact algorithms, whereas our method relies on a ≥12% CI threshold originally developed for a spline-based catheter and not formally validated for fixed-loop geometry. Additionally, manual jumper-cable routing increases workflow complexity and the risk of connection errors or instability. Furthermore, although the fixed-loop PFA catheter is displayed, the unnecessary flower-shaped graphical interface of the spline-based PFA catheter remains visible and cannot be removed.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. Impedance behavior may differ between native and previously ablated myocardium, and CI reliability in such settings is uncertain. Additionally, CI was not formally validated against lesion formation or durability. Although acute bidirectional block was achieved in all pulmonary veins, the impact of CI-guided positioning on long-term outcomes remains unknown and requires prospective evaluation.

Conclusion

This comprehensive CI visualization shifts the operator’s focus from subjective catheter manipulation to objective electrode-level tissue contact assessment and may improve procedural efficiency.
