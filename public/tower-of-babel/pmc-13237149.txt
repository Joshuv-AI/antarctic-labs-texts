Strength and resilient performance of expansive subgrades stabilized with apricot kernel shell ash
Authors: Muhammed TANYILDIZI
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractExpansive subgrades pose significant challenges for road pavements due to their poor engineering properties. To address this issue using a sustainable waste-derived material, this study, for the first time, investigated the stabilization of expansive subgrades of different origins with apricot kernel shell ash (AKSA). Two expansive soils, a high plasticity clay (CH) and a low plasticity clay (CL), were treated with 0–15% AKSA by dry mass. A comprehensive program comprising compaction, unconfined compressive strength (UCS), direct shear, fall cone, California bearing ratio (CBR), resilient modulus (Mr), and scanning electron microscopy (SEM) was conducted, alongside Mr prediction using artificial neural networks (ANN) and finite element analyses in Plaxis 2D. AKSA reduced the maximum dry density and increased optimum moisture content, while mechanical performance improved up to an optimum AKSA content (≈ 6% for CH and ≈ 9% for CL). Peak UCS gains at 28 days reached ~ 107% (CH) and ~ 98% (CL); CBR increased by ~ 47% (CH, 6% AKSA) and ~ 71% (CL, 12% AKSA). Mr rose by ~ 1.47× (CH, 6% AKSA) and ~ 1.50× (CL, 9% AKSA). SEM revealed denser, less porous microstructures with evidence of cementitious gel formation. The ANN outperformed multiple regression in predicting Mr (R²_test ≈ 0.96). Plaxis 2D simulations indicated reduced deformations and shear strains for optimally stabilized subgrades. Furthermore, compared to cement stabilization, the AKSA-based specimen can minimize carbon emissions by approximately 40 and 43 tons for 1000 m³ of CH and CL soils, respectively, when replacing 3% cement, highlighting its significant environmental and practical advantages. These results demonstrate that AKSA is a promising, low-carbon stabilizer for expansive subgrades and supports circular use of agricultural waste. Introduction

Expansive subgrades (shrink-swell soils) present persistent challenges to lightly-loaded civil infrastructure, particularly roadways1,2. These soils, which are typically identified by their high clay content, exhibit considerable volume changes and poor load-bearing capacity due to fluctuations in moisture content3–6. In the presence of moisture, expansive subgrade soils are prone to swell, while in dry conditions, they tend to shrink. This issue can result in substantial geotechnical issues, including cracking, uplifting, slope failure and differential settlement7–11. The instability of expansive subgrades threatens pavement durability and safety12–14. Accordingly, appropriate stabilization is essential to mitigate risk and extend service life15–18. Several techniques have been adopted to enhance the engineering properties of expansive subgrades to cope with the aforementioned issues19–21. Conventional methods cover mechanical treatment through compaction, in which soil grains are compressed to improve the density and to decrease porosity22. Chemical stabilization, including the use of cement and lime, is highly effective in enhancing the strength performance and reducing swell/shrink properties of expansive soils23–25. Although chemical treatments (e.g., lime, cement) are effective, they incur economic and environmental cost, particularly CO2 emissions18,26–28. For this reason, alternative stabilizers such as fly ash, slag and silica fume have been thoroughly explored as more sustainable approaches. These sustainable stabilizers enhance soil properties through pozzolanic reactions, filler effects and result lower carbon footprint18,29–33. In recent years, studies have focused on the use of agricultural waste materials as novel stabilizers for minimizing environmental effects and triggering sustainability. While promising results have been declared, limited research has focused on using apricot kernel shell ash (AKSA), especially considering expansive subgrades from different origins and their behavior under mechanical, resilient, microstructural and numerical analyses. Among alternative binders, AKSA is a promising candidate due to its calcium-rich chemistry and alkaline nature. Apricot kernel shell is a type of agricultural waste obtained during the processing of apricots, especially in regions with a significant agricultural industry. The global production of fresh apricots is anticipated to be approximately 3.500.000 tons per annum, with Turkey contributing approximately 700.000 tons. Turkey is the leading producer of apricots, accounting for approximately 20% of global production. The predominant source of apricot kernels is the by-products of the juice, jam, cake, dried fruit and processing plant industries, as well as domestic waste. It is predicted that the annual average yield of apricot kernels is 650.000 tons. These by-products mainly serve as biofuel in bakeries34. The ultimate product resulting from the process of combustion of apricot kernel shell is ash, which is characterized by its high calcium content. Calcium-based stabilizers improve the performance of expansive subgrades through pozzolanic reactions, cation exchange and flocculation35. AKSA’s stabilization potential arises from pozzolanic and cation exchange reactions: soil aluminosilicates react with calcium from AKSA to form cementitious C–S–H/C–A–H gels that bind particles and improve bearing capacity. Additionally, the alkaline nature of AKSA contributes to the neutralization of acidic soil conditions, thus increasing its stability36. Recent studies have focused on the use of apricot kernel shells as an aggregate replacement material in cementitious mixtures for the production of sustainable composites37–40. However, a survey of the published literature indicates an absence of studies that have investigated the usability of AKSA in stabilizing expansive subgrades. In light of the substantial volume of waste, there is a need to devise sustainable methodologies that utilize AKSA to stabilize expansive subgrade soils.

This study is, to the author’s knowledge, the first to evaluate AKSA for stabilizing expansive subgrade soils. AKSA was obtained from the bakeries using apricot kernel shells as biofuel. To this end, the mechanical, microstructural and resilient modulus (Mr) behavior of two different classified (high plasticity clay (CH) and low plasticity clay (CL)) expansive subgrades of different origins. Comprehensive laboratory experiments including compaction, unconfined compressive strength (UCS), direct shear test, fall cone test and California bearing ratio (CBR) test were conducted on expansive subgrades with /out AKSA. The findings of CBR tests of AKSA stabilized expansive subgrades were compared with soils treated with different ratios of cement and lime to analyze their applicability as a sustainable subgrade for road pavements. Resilient modulus (Mr) testing simulated traffic loading to assess stiffness improvements due to AKSA. In addition, microstructural analyses were performed through the scanning electron microscope (SEM) technique to offer a foundational understanding of the stabilization process. Moreover, artificial neural network (ANN) and multiple regression (MR) models were constructed to estimate the Mr parameters of subgrade soils, including AKSA. Furthermore, finite element analysis of specimens was made with Plaxis 2D. The results indicate that AKSA can substantially enhance mechanical performance while supporting lower carbon, circular practices in road construction. In this sense, the current study will likely fill the significant research gap related to the use of AKSA in stabilizing expansive subgrades. It is therefore anticipated that the present study will address the significant research deficit concerning the use of AKSA in stabilizing expansive subgrades.

Research significance

Stabilizing expansive subgrades is an important issue for road construction, especially in regions where these types of soils are common41. This study adopted AKSA as a sustainable stabilizer to enhance the engineering properties of expansive subgrades. Here, AKSA was obtained from bakeries where apricot kernel shell was used as biofuel for a sustainable energy system42. Combustion of apricot kernel shells yields ash that is often discarded. The generation of AKSA can reach significant amounts and cause environmental pollution if not managed appropriately43. The ash is rich in calcium which is beneficial in the expansive subgrade soil stabilization process through cation exchange and pozzolanic reactions, improving the strength performance35. Given its chemistry and performance, AKSA shows potential as a stabilizer for road subgrades. In addition, the use of AKSA not only helps to address the challenges of expansive subgrade soils, but also promotes sustainable development by minimizing the need for traditional stabilizers, which often have high environmental costs, and encourages the re-use of agricultural waste. As research into the usage of AKSA in expansive subgrade soil stabilization continues to develop, it is likely to become an important component of environmentally sound and cost-effective transportation engineering practices in the future.

Materials

In the study, two different expansive road subgrades classified as high plasticity clay (CH) and low plasticity clay (CL) were adopted to be stabilized with AKSA. The soils were obtained from two distinct road cuts. The expansive subgrade soils were obtained from a depth of approximately 1–1.5 m after stripping off organic matter. The comparatively finer grains of AKSA can significantly fill the pores and voids among the soil particles, resulting in the formation of a more compact structure. Additionally, the finer grain size ensures a larger specific surface area, improving the pozzolanic reactions between soil and AKSA particles. The combination of different particle sizes triggers a more stable matrix, thereby increasing the engineering performance of the specimens. Method

Comprehensive laboratory analyses were performed on pure soil and soil samples, including 3%, 6%, 9%, 12%, and 15% AKSA by dry weight, to analyze the efficiency of AKSA on the strength, resilient and microstructural properties of expansive subgrade soils. Soil and AKSA were air dried (7 days, ambient) and then oven dried at 105 °C to constant mass. The maximum dry density (MDD) and optimum moisture content (OMC) of soil samples were obtained through modified proctor test according to the ASTM D155751. UCS tests were conducted after curing for 1, 7, and 28 days following the ASTM D216653. To prevent moisture loss, the specimens were wrapped in nylon bags after being prepared at corresponding MDD and OMC values and kept in a place where the laboratory conditions. The soaked California bearing ratio (CBR) values were found following the ASTM D188350. Shear strength parameters were determined using direct shear tests per ASTM D652854. The soil samples were sheared under normal stresses of ~ 109, ~218, and ~ 327 kPa. The samples were sheared immediately after the application of normal stress without allowing time for consolidation or drainage for simulating unconsolidated–undrained conditions. Undrained shear strength (Su) was estimated using the fall cone test per BS EN ISO 17,892 655, with a 30°/0.785 N cone. SEM analyses were conducted on soils with the highest CBR values. Mr tests were conducted following the AASHTO T307 9956 with 15 load sequences, specified in Table 3. Artificial neural network (ANN) and multiple regression (MR) models were constructed to predict the Mr values of AKSA stabilized expansive subgrade soils. The finite element analysis of soils was conducted through the Plaxis 2D (Version 8.2 Professional, Update Pack 4, Build 811, Plaxis B.V.; https://www.plaxis.com).

Results and discussion

Compaction parameters

Figure 3a,b present the relationships between dry unit weight and moisture content of CH (a) and CL (b) specimens with various AKSA content, respectively. The water content at the corresponding maximum dry density (MDD) is known as optimum moisture content (OMC). The changes in the MDD and optimum moisture content OMC at different AKSA contents are also demonstrated in Fig. 4a,b, respectively. With increasing AKSA content, MDD decreased and OMC increased for both CH and CL. The MDD of CH decreased from 16.59 to 14.77 kN/m3, while OMC increased from 21.69 to 23.78% with a 15% AKSA ratio. For soil CL, the MDD value reduced from 17.49 to 15.08 kN/m3 and OMC increased from 15.31 to 20.11% with a 15% AKSA ratio.

Dry unit weight and moisture content relationship for CH (a) and CL (b) mixtures.

Variations in the MDD and OMC values of CH (a) and CL (b) mixtures with different AKSA content.

The reduction of MDD of soil mixtures with AKSA ratio can be attributable to the lower specific gravity and higher water affinity of AKSA relative to the soils. This is a common phenomenon encountered during the stabilization process when the additive has a lower specific gravity than the soil57,58. The increase in OMC of soil samples with AKSA can be due to the higher water attraction of AKSA compared to soil particles. The increase in OMC with AKSA content may be due to the higher water affinity of AKSA to lubricate the soil particles for achieving the corresponding MDD compared to soil particles59.

Unconfined compressive strength (UCS)

The axial stress-strain graphs of CH and CL soils containing AKSA at ratios that achieved maximum strength performance at different curing durations are given in Fig. 5. The variations in the UCS ​​of the CH and CL soils with different AKSA contents and curing durations are given in Fig. 6a,b, respectively. It was deduced from the figures that the UCS values of soils were affected by the AKSA ratio and curing durations. The UCS values of pure CH and CL soils were 152.01 and 287.62 kPa for a 1-day curing, respectively. For CH, UCS increased up to 6% AKSA (1-day curing) and declined thereafter; with 28-day curing, the optimum shifted toward 9%. The improvement in UCS of CH was 34.94% with a 6% AKSA ratio. For soil CL, the UCS value increased gradually with up to 9% AKSA content and then started to decrease with further addition of AKSA for a 1-day curing period. The improvement in UCS of CL soil with 9% AKSA content for 1-day curing was 18.26%. There was a progressive increase with 7 and 28 days curing in the UCS of CH and CL soils with AKSA content. For CH soil, approximately 51.67% and 107% increases were observed with 9% AKSA content after 7 and 28 days of curing, respectively. For CL soil, approximately 49% and 92% increases were observed with 9% AKSA content at the end of the 7 and 28 days of curing, respectively. For CL soil, approximately a 51% increase with 9% AKSA content at the end of 7 days of curing and a 98% increase with 12% AKSA content with 28 days of curing were observed. The improvement in the UCS value of soil specimens with a certain AKSA content can be stated by the enhancement in the interconnectivity between the soil and additive particles at a relevant rate, thus binding the grains strongly to each other. The reduction in the UCS values after a certain point can be due to the disintegration of the structure, resulting in a decrease in the strength performance60. In addition, the enhancement of the UCS values with curing periods can be due to the pozzolanic reactions between silica and alumina in soil and calcium in AKSA, resulting in the formation of the cementitious compounds (calcium silicate hydrates (CSH) and calcium aluminate hydrates (CAH)) thus improving the long-term shear strength, stability and bearing capacity performance of the soil mixtures61,62.

Figure 7a,b demonstrate the change in energy absorption capacity (EAC) obtained from the stress-deformation graph during the UCS test for CH and CL soils with varying AKSA content, respectively. EAC, i.e., toughness, increased with AKSA addition for each soil type. Increasing EAC means that the soil specimen reaches the capacity to undergo more deformation until the moment of collapse under load and a more ductile material is obtained. The maximum EAC value recorded for CL soil was 27.83 kJ/m³, with 12% AKSA content, following the 28-day curing period.

Direct shear test

Figure 8a,b present the variations in the cohesion (c) and internal friction angles (Φº) values of CH and CL specimens, respectively, obtained from the direct shear test. AKSA increased apparent cohesion while reducing the friction angle, consistent with finer matrix/filler effects and flocculation. The cohesion value of CH increased steadily from 29.87 to 45.69 kPa, while the internal friction angle was reduced from 33.54 ˚ to 19.14 ˚with 15% AKSA content. The cohesion value of CL increased consistently from 36.33 to 51.33 kPa with 15% AKSA content, while the internal friction angle value decreased from 40.05 to 16.52 ˚ with 15% AKSA content. The improvement in the cohesion values of soils with AKSA content can be due to the filler effect of the additive with relatively lower grain size, resulting in the increase of compactness and firmness of the soil composite. In the manuscript (see Fig. 1), the particle size distribution of AKSA is shown to be significantly finer than that of the native expansive soil. This granulometric difference is considered a key contributor to the observed filler effect, which enhances the cohesion and stability of the stabilized soil. Additionally, in the manuscript, SEM images of the stabilized specimens (see Figs. 27 and 28) clearly show that the AKSA-stabilized samples exhibit a more compact structure compared to the untreated soils. This observation supports the filler effect hypothesis, where the finer particles of AKSA fill the voids between soil particles, resulting in a more cohesive and stable structure. Furthermore, flocculation and agglomeration that occurred in the stabilization process may be responsible for the increase in the cohesion of the soil matrix65. This interpretation is further supported by the SEM images presented in the study (see Figs. 27 and 28), which reveal more aggregated and interconnected particle structures in the AKSA-stabilized samples compared to the untreated soil. The denser and more clustered arrangement observed under SEM indicates the presence of flocculated microstructures, which likely contributed to the improved interparticle bonding and the resulting increase in cohesion. The decrease in the internal friction angle of soils with AKSA content can be due to the increase in the fineness of the soil matrix with the additive66.

California bearing ratio (CBR)

Figure 10a,b present the load-penetration graphs determined from the CBR test of CH (a) and CL (b) samples, respectively. The variations in CBR values ​​of CH (a) and CL (b) specimens with different AKSA contents are given in Fig. 11a,b, respectively. The CBR value of CH soil increased with up to 6% AKSA content and started to reduce from that point. The maximum CBR value for soil CL was obtained with 12% AKSA content. The increase in CBR value of CH was approximately 47% with 6% AKSA addition, while this rate was 71% for CL soil with the addition of 12% AKSA. It can be deduced that at a certain AKP content, the soil sample becomes more stable, the compactness between the soil and AKP particles increases, and as a result, an increase in strength is achieved68. In addition, pozzolanic reactions occurred between silica in the soil and calcium in AKSA in the aqueous medium, resulting in the formation of cementitious compounds, thus improving the relative strength performance of soil mixtures69. This macroscopic improvement is supported by the microstructural analysis, which demonstrated a denser and more compact soil structure in the SEM images (see Figs. 27 and 28). The formation of cementitious gels detected in the SEM images correlates with the improved soil stiffness and strength, explaining the observed increase in CBR values. Therefore, the combination of enhanced macro-properties, such as the CBR, and the improved microstructure demonstrates the effectiveness of AKSA in soil stabilization.

Figure 16a–g show the variations in the Mr of CL soil and CL soil with 3%, 6%, 9%, 12% and 15% AKSA content, respectively, under varying deviator stresses. The Mr values for CL soil with 9% AKSA content changed between 122.2 and 156.8 MPa, which was about 1.50 times that of pure CL soil. Upon examining Figs. 13, 14, 15, 16, 17 and 18, it is seen that the soil specimens demonstrated stress softening behavior with deviator stress. This phenomenon is generally common for clayey soil since the easy affection of loose clay particles by deviator stress73–75. In addition, a rise in confining pressure resulted in an improvement in the Mr values, which can be attributed to the enhancement of inter-particle interlocking and internal friction among particles, resulting in less strain potentials75–78. The rise in the Mr with a certain amount of AKSA can be due to the good packing of the soil mixture at that corresponding value of the additive, increasing the stiffness properties75.

Prediction of resilient modulus (Mr) with ANN

An artificial neural network (ANN) model was adopted using the measured test values for the prediction of Mr of AKSA stabilized expansive subgrades due to their effectiveness in capturing complex, non-linear relationships among influencing factors such as material properties, moisture content, and stabilization conditions. The result of the ANN model was compared with the multiple regression (MR) model. A total of 180 data sets were used to create ANN models, 144 of which were used in the training set and 36 in the test set. These 36 testing data points are distinct from the 144 training data sets, ensuring that the model’s performance is assessed on data that was not used during the training phase, thus preventing overfitting and improving the generalizability of the model. Table 7 presents the variables in the multiple regression models. The architectures of developed ANN models are presented in Fig. 19. A feed forward ANN with one input layer, one hidden layer (8 neurons, tan sigmoid), and one output layer (linear) was trained using Levenberg–Marquardt with early stopping. Input layers include AKSA (%), LL, PI, OMC, CBR, MDD, σc and σd. The neural network toolbox within MATLAB (Version R2016a, 9.0.0.341360, The MathWorks, Inc.; https://www.mathworks.com), a computer-aided software, was employed for training/testing of ANN model. Through a trial-and-error process, the optimal number of neurons in the hidden layer was determined to be 8, which provided a balance between model accuracy and generalization. The performance of the developed models was evaluated statistical parameters such as the coefficient of determination (R2), the mean absolute error (MAE), root mean square error (RMSE) and mean absolute percentage error (MAPE).

Finite element analyses using plaxis 2D

Plaxis 2D program, a widely used finite element analysis software in geotechnical engineering problems, was adopted to model the subgrade with a typical road platform. The Mohr-Coulomb model was used as the constitutive model due to its simplicity and proven effectiveness in modeling soil behavior, particularly in applications such as bearing capacity, deformation evaluation, stability analysis and embankment design84. Model parameters such as cohesion, internal friction angle, unit weights and Mr were derived from laboratory tests performed in the current study. The model was developed based on the axial symmetry and plane unit deformation. The slope applied in highway road embankments was adopted as 1(vertical):4(horizontal). The developed road platform consisted of a 45 cm high subbase over a 200 cm high subgrade. The pavement layer was a hot-mix asphalt with a 10 cm thickness. For the simulation of the traffic loads, a 600 kPa wheel load over a 15 cm diameter was applied to the asphalt layer. The bottom boundary was fixed in both vertical and horizontal directions, while the lateral boundaries were fixed horizontally but free vertically. Mesh sensitivity analysis was conducted, and a finer mesh was used around the loading area to capture stress concentration effects. Additionally, ten different points were selected inside the subgrade layer to monitor the deformation changes for soil specimens as shown in Fig. 23. The coordinates of nodes are A(0:2.00), B(0:1.33), C(0:0.33), D(0.58:2.00), E(0.55:1.24), F(0.53:0.34), G(1.20:1.49), H(1.54:1.03), I(1.72:0.49) and J(2.33:0.17). The characteristics of the asphalt layer and subbase were determined based on the literature85,86. Design Mr was taken as the average across load sequences per the Türkiye Highways Resilient Modulus Test87. The road platform layer properties are presented in Table 9. The results of the Plaxis 2D analysis regarding the horizontal displacement, total displacement, total strain and shear strain are tabulated in Table 10. Figures 24 and 25 demonstrate the deformed shapes and shear strains under the traffic loading of the selected specimens that show the maximum and minimum deformation for each specimen, respectively. The deformations of the selected nodes within the subgrade are given in Table 11 and the change in the deformation for points is illustrated graphically in Figs. 26a,b for CH and CL soils, respectively. From the deformed shapes of the selected samples, it is understood that the deformations become more intense under the traffic loads. It is seen that the shear strains are higher in the regions close to the load. The deformations and shear strains of subgrades were restricted through stabilization with AKSA. While the highest deformations and shear strains were observed in CH soil, the lowest deformations and shear strains were seen in soil CL stabilized with 9% AKSA content. For soil CH and CL, the deformations and shear strains were minimized with 6% and 9% AKSA content, respectively. A more rigid and stable road platform occurred after stabilization with AKSA, resulting in fewer deformations. Examination of Fig. 26 revealed that displacement values progressively decreased with increasing distance from the point of applied load and toward the deeper layers of the subgrade soil.

Microstructural analyses

A piece of soil sample was obtained from the CH soil with 6% AKSA and the CL soil with 9% AKSA, from which specimens yielded the highest CBR value, for use in microstructural analyses. SEM analysis was conducted to assess the effectiveness of AKSA on the microstructural changes in expansive subgrade soils and to make comparisons of the surfaces of the pure soils and AKSA-stabilized soils. Figure 27 demonstrates the SEM of CH and CH soil stabilized with 6% AKSA. In SEM images of pure CH soil, it can be observed voids, micro-cavities, large pores with dispersed structures and flaky clay-shaped particles. After the stabilization of CH soil with 6% AKSA, more compact, dense and pore-minimized microstructure formations were observed. The average porosity of pure CH soil determined using image-based analysis decreased from about 16% to 6% with 6% AKSA content, indicating an overall reduction of about 63%. The SEM analyses of pure CL soil and CL soil stabilized with a 9% AKSA content are presented in Fig. 28. The microstructure of pure CL soil is loosely packed with dispersed and porous structures. Also, voids, micro-cavities and flaky clay shape particles can be detected in the microstructure. After 9% AKSA addition, a denser and well-packed compact structure formation with minimized pores was observed. The average porosity of pure CL soil, as obtained by image-based analysis, was reduced from about 10% to 3% with 9% AKSA content, resulting in an overall reduction of about 70%. Furthermore, the formation of cementitious gels with the pozzolanic reactions between the silica in the soil and the calcium in the AKSA can be observed in stabilized SEM images of CH and CL soils, resulting in a compact and denser structure in comparison to the untreated soil composite.

Conclusions

This study presented here investigated the usability of apricot kernel shell ash (AKSA) on the stabilization of different classified expansive road subgrades of different origins. The results of the compaction, UCS, direct shear, fall cone, CBR, resilient modulus (Mr), ANN to predict Mr, finite element modeling and microstructural analyses of unstabilized soils and soils stabilized with 3%, 6%, 9%, 12%, and 15% AKSA contents were evaluated. The main findings could be drawn from the results of the study:

The Maximum Dry Density (MDD) decreased, while the Optimum Moisture Content (OMC) increased with increasing AKSA content. For example, with 15% AKSA, MDD decreased from 16.59 to 14.77 kN/m³ for CH soil, and from 17.49 to 15.08 kN/m³ for CL soil, while OMC increased from 21.69% to 23.78% (CH) and from 15.31% to 18.58% (CL).

The Maximum Dry Density (MDD) decreased, while the Optimum Moisture Content (OMC) increased with increasing AKSA content. For example, with 15% AKSA, MDD decreased from 16.59 to 14.77 kN/m³ for CH soil, and from 17.49 to 15.08 kN/m³ for CL soil, while OMC increased from 21.69% to 23.78% (CH) and from 15.31% to 18.58% (CL).

Unconfined Compressive Strength (UCS) and Energy Absorption Capacity (EAC) values showed marked improvement with both AKSA content and curing period. The UCS of CH soil increased by 92% (with 9% AKSA, 28-day curing), and CL soil by 98% (with 12% AKSA). Correspondingly, EAC values reached up to 13.27 kJ/m³ (CH) and 27.83 kJ/m³ (CL) with 12% AKSA content under the same curing conditions.

While apricot kernel shell ash (AKSA) was utilized in the current study due to the its local availability, the overarching aim is to demonstrate the potential of similar biomass waste materials in transportation geotechnical applications. The AKSA used was sourced from bakeries that already employed apricot shells as a biofuel, thus requiring no additional energy ash for production. This circular use of waste aligns with sustainable practices and provides a replicable model for regions with other types of agricultural residues, such as sugarcane bagasse, whose global production is estimated at over 700 million tons annually, generating a considerable amount of ash upon combustion. Another notable agricultural waste is rice husk, with global production considered to surpass 150 million tons88. Similarly, the global annual coconut production surpasses 60 million tons, of which coconut husk production is estimated at 7 million tons89. The methodology presented can therefore be adopted to different local contexts, especially in regions with plentiful agricultural wastes, supporting the broader sustainable approaches and low-carbon soil treatment.

Study limitations and future research directions

While this study provides promising results, several limitations and future directions should be acknowledged:

The experimental program in the current study was performed under controlled laboratory conditions. This may not fully indicate the site conditions. Future research should investigate large-scale site conditions to corroborate the performance of AKSA-stabilized specimens under real conditions.

The experimental program in the current study was performed under controlled laboratory conditions. This may not fully indicate the site conditions. Future research should investigate large-scale site conditions to corroborate the performance of AKSA-stabilized specimens under real conditions.

The chemical composition of AKSA may vary depending on factors such as raw material origin and combustion conditions, which can affect the stabilization performance. Therefore, future research should investigate the influence of variations in the chemical properties of AKSA on the behavior of soils.

The chemical composition of AKSA may vary depending on factors such as raw material origin and combustion conditions, which can affect the stabilization performance. Therefore, future research should investigate the influence of variations in the chemical properties of AKSA on the behavior of soils.

This study investigated short-term curing durations (up to 28 days), and the long-term performance were not assessed. Future studies should investigate the performance of the AKSA-stabilized specimens over long-term curing.

This study investigated short-term curing durations (up to 28 days), and the long-term performance were not assessed. Future studies should investigate the performance of the AKSA-stabilized specimens over long-term curing.

The ANN model was constructed utilized a specific dataset. Its applicability to varying soil types may need further validation. Future attempts should expand the ANN dataset and make a comparison with different prediction methods.

The ANN model was constructed utilized a specific dataset. Its applicability to varying soil types may need further validation. Future attempts should expand the ANN dataset and make a comparison with different prediction methods.

The current study investigated mainly the effect of AKSA on the mechanical and stiffness behavior of expansive subgrades. Further studies should focus on the physical and durability properties, such as swelling/shrinking, wetting-drying and environmental leaching of soil samples stabilized with AKSA.

The current study investigated mainly the effect of AKSA on the mechanical and stiffness behavior of expansive subgrades.
