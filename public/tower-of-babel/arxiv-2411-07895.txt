Homological stability for symplectic groups via algebraic arc complexes
Ismael Sierra, Nathalie Wahl

Abstract
. We use algebraic arc complexes to prove a homological stability result for symplectic groups with slope 2 3 \frac{2}{3} for rings with finite unitary stable rank. Symplectic groups are here interpreted as the automorphism groups of formed spaces with boundary , which are algebraic analogues of surfaces with boundary, that we also study in the present paper. Our stabilization map is a rank one stabilization in the category of formed spaces with boundary, going through both odd and even symplectic groups.



1.
Introduction

Let
R
be a commutative ring. The symplectic group
\operatorname{Sp}_{2n}(R)
is the automorphism group of the hyperbolic space
\HH^{\oplus n}
, where
\HH=(R^{2},\lambda_{\HH})
is
R^{2}
equipped with the non-degenerate alternating form
\lambda_{\HH}=\begin{pmatrix}\ 0&1\\
-1&0\end{pmatrix}
.
A pair
(M,\lambda)
, with
M
an
R
-module and
\lambda
an alternating form, is called a formed space, and one has traditionally studied the stability properties of symplectic groups as coming from stabilizing by direct sum with copies of
\HH
in the category of formed spaces.

In the present paper, we improve the best known stability range for the homology of symplectic groups in the case of rings
R
with finite unitary stable rank
usr(R)
(see Definition
2.21
), by working instead in the new category
\mathcal{F}_{\partial}
of
formed spaces with boundary

(M,\lambda,\partial)
. Here
\partial:M\to R
is the additional data of a linear map, which we think of as a boundary. We then replace the rank two stabilization map
\oplus\HH
by a rank one stabilization
\#X
with the object
X=(R,0,\id)
of
\mathcal{F}_{\partial}
, where
\#
is a monoidal structure on
\mathcal{F}_{\partial}
, with the property that both
X^{\#2g}
and
X^{\#2g+1}
are closely related to
\HH^{g}
. More specifically, there is an identification
\operatorname{Sp}_{2n}(R)\cong\operatorname{Aut}(X^{\#2n+1})
,
while the intermediate
odd symplectic group

\operatorname{Sp}_{2n-1}(R):=\operatorname{Aut}(X^{\#2n})
identifies with a parabolic subgroup in
\operatorname{Sp}_{2n}(R)
. These particular odd symplectic groups have appeared elsewhere before e.g.; in
[
5
]
or in
[
16
,
17
]
in the context of homological stability, but maybe defined in a more ad-hoc manner.

Our main result is the following:

Theorem A
.

Let
R
be a commutative ring with
usr(R)<\infty
and set
c=0
if
R
is a PID, and
c=2usr(R)+2
otherwise. Then the map

H_{i}(\operatorname{Sp}_{n}(R);\mathbb{Z})\to H_{i}(\operatorname{Sp}_{n+1}(R);\mathbb{Z})

is an epimorphism for
i\leq\frac{n-c}{3}
, and a monomorphism for
i\leq\frac{n-c-3}{3}
(
n
odd) and for all
i
(
n
is even).
In particular, restricting to even symplectic groups we get that

H_{i}(\operatorname{Sp}_{2g}(R);\mathbb{Z})\to H_{i}(\operatorname{Sp}_{2g+2}(R);\mathbb{Z})

is an epimorphism for
i\leq\frac{2g-c}{3}
and an isomorphism for
i\leq\frac{2g-c-2}{3}
.

The above slope
2/3
stability result improves the earlier results of Charney
[
3
, Cor 4.5]
and Mirzaii-van der Kallen
[
13
, Thm 8.2]
, who gave a stability result for even symplectic group with a slope
1/2
for respectively Dedekind domains and rings with finite unitary stable rank. The range given here is however not optimal for finite fields, where stability is known to hold with the better slope
1
instead by Sprehn and the second author
[
20
]
(for fields other than
\mathbb{F}_{2}
), and for local rings with infinite residue field, where a slope
2
result was recently proved by Schlichting
[
17
]
.
Rationally in the case
R=\mathbb{Z}
, the stability slope is known to be exactly
1
by
[
21
]
.

Finite degree coefficients

As is often the case for homological stability results, stability also holds for finite degree coefficient systems. A sequence of compatible
\operatorname{Sp}_{n}(R)
–representations
M_{n}
is here called a finite degree coefficient system if the maps
M_{n}\to M_{n+1}
are injective with trivial iterated cokernels, and a certain braid condition holds, see Definitions
5.3
and
5.5
.

Theorem B
.

Let
R
be a commutative ring with
usr(R)<\infty
and set
c=0
if
R
is a PID, and
c=2usr(R)+2
otherwise. Let
M_{n}
be a coefficient system of degree
r
. Then the map

H_{i}(\operatorname{Sp}_{n}(R);M_{n})\to H_{i}(\operatorname{Sp}_{n+1}(R);M_{n+1})

is an epimorphism for
i\leq\frac{n-c-3r+1}{3}
and an isomorphism for
i\leq\frac{n-c-3r-2}{3}
.

Stability results for even symplectic groups with finite degree coefficient systems appear in
[
15
, Thm 5.15-16]
and
[
4
, Thm 3.25]
, with a stability slope
1/2
(corresponding to
1/4
in terms of the rank
n
). One should though note that, while examples tend to fit in both our framework and the framework of
[
15
,
4
]
, the definition of finite degree coefficients in those papers actually differs from ours. See Remark
5.7
for more details.

Abelian coefficients

Theorem
5.2
states that stability also holds with
abelian coefficients

M
, that is those systems of coefficients coming from an action of
H_{1}(\operatorname{Sp}_{n}(R);\mathbb{Z})\cong H_{1}(\operatorname{Sp}_{\infty}(R);\mathbb{Z})
on a fixed module
M
, with essentially the same bounds as in Theorem
A
, improving again on earlier results of
[
15
,
4
]
. However this is only relevant when
H_{1}(\operatorname{Sp}_{n}(R);\mathbb{Z})
is non-zero. In particular it is not relevant for
R=\mathbb{Z}
by
[
1
, Lem A1]
.

Orthogonal and unitary groups

It is natural to ask whether Theorems
A
and
B
also hold for orthogonal or unitary groups, that are often treated simultaneously to symplectic groups. Indeed, these groups also identify with the automorphism group of a version of
\HH^{\oplus n}
, in the context of formed spaces with a different flavour of
form parameters
, with alternating forms replaced by symmetric or Hermitian forms, see e.g.
[
19
]
.
Our proof here however does not directly adapt to these other groups. In fact, a naive adaptation of the framework presented here to symmetric or Hermitian forms, stabilizing with the analogue of
X
in such contexts, relates instead to a different family of groups, unrelated to stabilizing with hyperbolic summands. (See Remark
2.19
for more details.)

Algebraic arc complexes and the proof of Theorems
A
and
B

To prove Theorems
A
and
B
, we use the general stabiltity machine of
[
15
,
9
]
: we show that the stabilization maps considered come from the action of an
E_{2}
-module (the braided monoidal subcategory of
\mathcal{F}_{\partial}
generated by
X
) on an
E_{1}
-algebra (the monoidal category
\mathcal{F}_{\partial}
). In fact the results hold more generally for the stabilization maps

\operatorname{Aut}_{\mathcal{F}_{\partial}}(A\#X^{\#n})\longrightarrow\operatorname{Aut}_{\mathcal{F}_{\partial}}(A\#X^{\#n+1})

for
A=(M,\lambda,\partial)
any formed space with boundary. (See Theorems
5.1
and
5.8
.)

Stability follows if one can show that a certain
destabilization complex
is highly connected. Guided by the case of mapping class groups of surfaces, we identify this destabilization complex with an algebraic version of the disordered arc complex of
[
6
]
. An
arc
in a formed space with boundary
(M,\lambda,\partial)
is defined here as an element
a\in M
such that
\partial(a)=1
. Building on
[
18
, Sec 6]
, we then define what it means for an arc to be
non-separating
and
disordered
in this algebraic context, see Definitions
3.4
and
3.14
.

As already noted in
[
18
]
, the connectivity of the non-separating arc complex can be deduced from that of a certain poset of unimodular vectors, see Proposition
3.10
and Theorem
3.7
that generalize earlier results of
[
13
,
4
,
18
]
. We then deduce the connectivity of the disordered arc complex (Theorem
3.16
) mimicking the geometric argument of
[
6
]
. The proof uses the interplay between the
arc genus
, that is the number of
X
–summands that can be split off, and the
hyperbolic genus
, that is the number of
\HH
-summands one can split off. It requires most importantly having a good grasp on how the arc genus behaves when “cutting along a simplex of arcs”. The key properties of the ring used are the finite unitary stable rank, that allows cancellation of
\HH
–summands, and the fact that, for rings with finite Bass stable rank and
M
with large enough hyperbolic genus, any
l:M\to R
unimodular restricts to a unimodular map on some hyperbolic summand, see Proposition
2.23
. The stability slope
2/3
comes from the fact that the arc genus behaves just like the geometric arc genus when cutting arcs, under our assumption on the rings, see Corollary
2.27
. It was surprising to us that this geometrically inspired argument works for rather general rings; there are for example no restriction on the characteristic. All the way through, our results have slight improvements for PIDs because of their special proporties recorded in Lemma
2.24
.

The identification between the disordered arc complex and the destabilization complex requires a weak cancellation property for formed spaces with boundary. We give in the paper two cancellation results, with very different proofs: one valid for general rings with finite unitary stable rank, see Theorem
4.1
, and a second result that works for PIDs and is deduced from a classification of formed spaces with boundary in that particular case, see Theorems
B.3
and
B.1
.

Organization of the paper

In Section
2
we define the category of formed spaces with boundaries, and study its properties. We define and relate the arc and hyperbolic genera. Section
3
studies algebraic arc complexes and proves the high connectivity of the disordered arc complex. Section
4
gives the equivalence (up a skeleton) between the disordered arc complex and the complex of destabilization. The results of Sections
3
and
4
are then combined in Section
5
, to prove the stability theorems.

2.
Formed spaces with boundary

In this section, we define a braided monoidal category
\mathcal{F}_{\partial}
of
formed spaces with boundary
, that is an algebraic version of the category of bidecorated surfaces of
[
6
]
.
A
bidecorated surface
is an (oriented) surface
S
equipped with two marked (oriented) intervals in its boundary. It has a monoidal structure induced by gluing intervals in pairs. The category of
formed spaces with boundary
defined here will be the algebraic shadow of the category of bidecorated surfaces, with objects behaving like the first homology of the surface relative to the two intervals, with an appropriately defined intersection form, and a “boundary map” corresponding to the boundary map in the long exact sequence of homology groups. We will also construct a monoidal structure on
\mathcal{F}_{\partial}
that corresponds to the one of bidecorated surfaces.

We start by recalling what formed spaces are, in the context relevant to symplectic groups, that is where the forms are alternating forms.

Let
R
be a commutative ring.

Definition 2.1
.

A
formed space
is a pair
(M,\lambda)
with
M
a finitely generated free
R
–module and
\lambda:M\otimes M\to R
an alternating form on
M
, i.e.
\lambda
is a
R
-bilinear map such that
\lambda(v,v)=0
for all
v\in M
. We denote by
\mathcal{F}
the category of formed spaces with morphisms the module maps that preserve the forms.

The category
\mathcal{F}
is symmetric monoidal, with monoidal structure
\oplus
induced by taking the direct sum of modules and orthogonal direct sum of forms.

The
hyperbolic form
is the formed space
\HH=(R^{2},\lambda_{\HH})
for
\lambda_{\HH}
given in the standard basis by the matrix

\lambda_{\HH}=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
-1&0\end{pmatrix}.

The
2g
-dimensional hyperbolic formed space
\HH^{\oplus g}
is isomorphic to the formed space
(H_{1}(S_{g}),\lambda)
with
S_{g}
a closed surface of genus
g
and
\lambda
the intersection pairing on
S_{g}
, and the symplectic group
\operatorname{Sp}_{2g}(\mathbb{Z})
identifies with its automorphism group:

\operatorname{Sp}_{2g}(R)\cong\operatorname{Aut}_{\mathcal{F}}(\HH^{\oplus g}),

see e.g. Example 6.1(i) in
[
13
]
.

We will work with the following enhanced version of the category
\mathcal{F}
:

Definition 2.2
.

The category
\mathcal{F}_{\partial}
of
formed spaces with boundary
has objects triples

(M,\lambda,\partial)
where
(M,\lambda)
is a formed space and
\partial:M\to R
a linear map.
Morphisms in
\mathcal{F}_{\partial}
are structure-preserving module maps.

The subcategory of formed spaces
(M,\lambda,\partial)
with
\partial=0
is isomorphic to the category
\mathcal{F}
defined above.
The case of particular importance to us is actually the opposite case, namely the triples
(M,\lambda,\partial)
where
\partial
is surjective instead.

We define a monoidal structure on the category
\mathcal{F}_{\partial}
of such objects by setting

(M_{1},\lambda_{1},\partial_{1})\#(M_{2},\lambda_{2},\partial_{2})=(M_{1}\oplus M_{2},\lambda_{1}\#\lambda_{2},\partial_{1}+\partial_{2})

where

\lambda_{1}\#\lambda_{2}=\begin{pmatrix}\lambda_{1}&\partial_{1}^{T}\partial_{2}\\
-\partial_{2}^{T}\partial_{1}&\lambda_{2}\end{pmatrix}.

The monoidal unit is
0:=(0,0,0)
.
On morphisms the monoidal structure is given by direct sum.
One checks that this monoidal structure is strictly associative.

Remark 2.3
.

While the above definition of the monoidal structure was guided by the geometric gluing of surfaces, it is also a natural choice if one thinks of it purely algebraically. Indeed,
an object in
\mathcal{F}_{\partial}
is a triple
(M,\lambda,\partial)
with
\lambda\in\Lambda^{2}M^{\vee}
and
\partial\in M^{\vee}
.
There are natural isomorphisms
(M_{1}\oplus M_{2})^{\vee}\cong M_{1}^{\vee}\oplus M_{2}^{\vee}
and
\Lambda^{2}(M_{1}\oplus M_{2})^{\vee}\cong\Lambda^{2}M_{1}^{\vee}\oplus\Lambda^{2}M_{2}^{\vee}\oplus M_{1}^{\vee}\otimes M_{2}^{\vee}
, making the choices

\partial_{1}\#\partial_{2}=\partial_{1}\oplus\partial_{2}
and
\lambda_{1}\#\lambda_{2}=\lambda_{1}\,\oplus\,\lambda_{2}\,\oplus\,\partial_{1}\otimes\partial_{2}
with respect to the above canonical decomposition, “most obvious” choices. These correspond to the choice given in the above definition.

Note that the functor
\mathcal{F}\to\mathcal{F}_{\partial}
taking
(M,\lambda)
to
(M,\lambda,0)
is a monoidal functor. On the other hand, the forgetful functor
\mathcal{F}_{\partial}\to\mathcal{F}
is not monoidal. We will see below that the monoidal structure of
\mathcal{F}_{\partial}
is chosen so that there is a monoidal functor from the category
\mathcal{M}_{2}
of bidecorated surfaces mentioned above, to
\mathcal{F}_{\partial}
.

Our main example of a formed space with boundary (and also the minimal such object with
\partial
surjective) is

X=(R,0,\id).

It is an example than can be associated to the disc in the category of bidecorated surfaces, as we will explain in the following section.

2.1.
Formed spaces with boundary from bidecorated surfaces

Recall from
[
6
]
the category
\mathcal{M}_{2}
of bidecorated surfaces. It has objects triples
(S,I_{0},I_{1})
, where
S
is an oriented surface and
I_{0},I_{1}
are two disjoint compatibly oriented intervals marked in its boundary, and morphisms are mapping classes fixing the marked intervals.

To a bidecorated surface
S=(S,I_{0},I_{1})
, we associate a new surface

S^{+}=S\cup H
obtained from
S
by attaching a handle
H=I\times I
to
S
along the identification of
\partial I\times I
with
I_{0}\sqcup I_{1}\hookrightarrow\partial S
. (See Figure
1
.)
Since
(S,I_{0},I_{1})
is oriented datum then
S^{+}
is naturally oriented, so there is a natural intersection pairing on
H_{1}(S^{+};R)
induced, under the Poincaré duality isomorphism
H_{1}(S^{+};R)\cong H^{1}(S^{+},\partial S^{+};R)
, by the (relative) cup product
H^{1}(S^{+},\partial S^{+};R)\otimes H^{1}(S^{+},\partial S^{+};R)\xrightarrow{\cup}H^{2}(S^{+},\partial S^{+};R)\cong H_{0}(S^{+};R)=R
.

Figure 1.
Two bidecorated surfaces
(S,I_{0},I_{1})
and their associated surface
S^{+}=S\cup H

The long exact sequence in homology
H_{*}(-)=H_{*}(-;R)
for the pair
(S^{+},H)

0=H_{1}(H)\longrightarrow H_{1}(S^{+})\longrightarrow H_{1}(S^{+},H)\cong H_{1}(S,I_{0}\sqcup I_{1})\xrightarrow{0}H_{0}(H)\xrightarrow{\cong}H_{0}(S^{+})

shows that there is an isomorphism
H_{1}(S^{+})\cong H_{1}(S,I_{0}\sqcup I_{1})
.
The inverse map

M=H_{1}(S,I_{0}\sqcup I_{1})\longrightarrow H_{1}(S^{+})

is given explicitly by representing classes in
M
by collections of arcs and circles, and closing any arc component in
H_{1}(S,I_{0}\sqcup I_{1})
using the core
I\times\{\frac{1}{2}\}
of the handle.

Now we define a functor

\operatorname{F}:\mathcal{M}_{2}\longrightarrow\mathcal{F}_{\partial}

by

\operatorname{F}(S,I_{0},I_{1}):=(H_{1}(S,I_{0}\sqcup I_{1}),\lambda,\partial)

where
\partial:H_{1}(S,I_{0}\sqcup I_{1})\to\tilde{H}_{0}(I_{0}\sqcup I_{1})\cong R\langle b_{1}-b_{0}\rangle
, for
b_{0},b_{1}
the midpoints of
I_{0},I_{1}
, is the boundary map in the long exact sequence for reduced homology, and
\lambda
is the intersection pairing on
H_{1}(S^{+})
, identified with
H_{1}(S,I_{0}\sqcup I_{1})
by the above isomorphism.
On morphisms, the functor
F
takes a mapping class to its induced map on relative homology.

Remark 2.4

(Curved forms)
.

The above construction of the surface
S^{+}
may seem a little ad hoc.
It is possible to instead work directly with
(H_{1}(S,I_{0}\cup I_{1}),\partial)
, replacing the alternating form
\lambda
with a
curved form

\lambda^{\prime}
, satisfying the condition
\lambda^{\prime}(x,y)+\lambda^{\prime}(y,x)=2\partial(x)\partial(y)
.
This “curved” symmetry equation arises indeed geometrically when defining an intersection product on
H_{1}(S,I_{0}\cup I_{1})
:
one can define an asymmetric intersection product
\lambda^{\prime}(a,b)
on classes
a,b
with potentially non-trivial boundary, by choosing a representative of
a
with boundary in
(0,\frac{1}{2})
in
I_{0}
and
I_{1}
and for
b
with boundary in
(\frac{1}{2},1)
instead.
One can check that the resulting form is not alternating on classes that have non-trivial boundary, and instead satisfies the above equation.

There is an equivalence of categories between
the category of alternating forms with boundary
(M,\lambda,\partial)
and that of curved forms with boundary
(M,\lambda^{\prime},\partial)
, where the correspondence is given by setting
\lambda^{\prime}(x,y)=\lambda(x,y)+\partial(x)\partial(y)
.
This form
\lambda^{\prime}
will play a role a few places in the paper, for example to define what it means for a formed space with boundary to be non-degnerate, see Section
2.3
.

Note that adding a handle to form the surface
S^{+}
from the tuple
(S,I_{0},I_{1})
, interpreted as the association
(H_{1}(S,I_{0}\sqcup I_{1}),\lambda,\partial)\mapsto(H_{1}(S^{+}),\lambda)
, becomes the forgetful functor
\mathcal{F}_{\partial}\to\mathcal{F}
.
Note also that the formed space
(H_{1}(S,I_{0}\sqcup I_{1}),\lambda,\partial)
contains
(H_{1}(S),\lambda|_{H_{1}(S)},0)
as a subspace, where
H_{1}(S)
identifies with
\ker(\partial)
, and where
\lambda|_{H_{1}(S)}
is the standard intersection pairing of
H_{1}(S)
. This type of subspace will play an important role below. (See also Remark
2.8
.)

Example 2.5
.

Consider the disc
D=(D^{2},I_{0},I_{1})
in the category
\mathcal{M}_{2}
. We have

F(D)=\big(H_{1}(D^{2},I_{0}\sqcup I_{1}),\lambda,\partial\big)=(R,0,\id)=X.

Explicitly,

H_{1}(D^{2},I_{0},I_{1})\cong R
generated by an arc
\rho
from
b_{0}
to
b_{1}
in the disc. The surface
(D^{2})^{+}
is a cylinder and the generator if
H_{1}((D^{2})^{+})
is represented by the middle circle in the cylinder.

Recall from
[
6
, Sec 3.1]
that the monoidal structure of
\mathcal{M}_{2}
is defined for
S_{1}=(S_{1},I^{1}_{0},I^{1}_{1})
and
S_{2}=(S_{2},I_{0}^{2},I_{1}^{2})
by

S_{1}\mathbin{\text{{\#}}}S_{2}=(S_{1}\cup S_{2}/\sim\,,\,I_{0}\,,\,I_{1})

where, for
j=0,1
, the equivalence relation identifies the second half of
I_{j}^{1}
with the first half of
I_{j}^{2}
, and the interval
I_{j}
is the union of the first half of
I_{j}^{1}
and the second half of
I_{j}^{2}
.

A particulary interesting example for us is the surfaces obtained as iterated sums of discs in this category: by
[
6
, Lem 3.1]
, there is an isomorphism

D^{\mathbin{\text{{\#}}}2g+i}\cong(S_{g,i},I_{0},I_{1}),\ \ i\in\{1,2\}

2.2.
Braided monoidal structure

We will formulate our main stability result as stability of the automorphism groups of objects of
\mathcal{F}_{\partial}
with respect to the stabilization map summing with the object
X
. To apply the framework of
[
15
]
, or its generalization by Krannich in
[
9
]
, one needs an action of a braided monoidal category on the category
\mathcal{F}_{\partial}
. We will define this action by giving a braided monoidal structure on the subcategory of
\mathcal{F}_{\partial}
generated by
X
. This structure will be constructed using the geometric model, namely the category
\mathcal{M}_{2}
.

In
[
6
, Sec 3.2]
, it is shown that the monoidal category
\mathcal{M}_{2}
is not braided, but that none-the-less the disc-object
D
is a Yang-Baxter element in
\mathcal{M}_{2}
, that is, it comes equipped with a morphism

T:D\mathbin{\text{{\#}}}D\to D\mathbin{\text{{\#}}}D
satisfying the braid relation:

(T\mathbin{\text{{\#}}}\id_{D})(\id_{D}\mathbin{\text{{\#}}}T)(T\mathbin{\text{{\#}}}\id_{D})=(\id_{D}\mathbin{\text{{\#}}}T)(T\mathbin{\text{{\#}}}\id_{D})(\id_{D}\mathbin{\text{{\#}}}T).

The underlying surface of
D\mathbin{\text{{\#}}}D
is a cylinder, and
T
is the Dehn twist along the middle circle of the cylinder. It turns out that, just as in
[
6
]
, the inverse Dehn twist
T^{-1}
is actually most convenient for our purpose. Given that
\operatorname{F}:\mathcal{M}_{2}\to\mathcal{F}_{\partial}
is a monoidal functor, it follows that the pair
(X,\beta)
, for
\beta=\operatorname{F}(T^{-1})
, is a Yang-Baxter element in
\mathcal{F}_{\partial}
.
What we will show here is that, unlike
D
, the object
X
satisfies the stronger condition that the full monoidal subcategory of
\mathcal{F}_{\partial}
generated by it is braided, i.e.
\beta
is also natural in morphisms of
\mathcal{F}_{\partial}
.
(We prove the naturality property of
\beta
only for completness. It is not as such necessary for our main results.)

Proposition 2.9
.

The twist
\beta:X^{\#2}\to X^{\#2}
defines a braiding on the full monoidal subcategory
\mathcal{F}_{\partial,X}
of
\mathcal{F}_{\partial}
generated by
X
.
Moreover, the resulting block braid

\beta_{n,m}:X^{\#n}\#X^{\#m}\xrightarrow{\cong}X^{\#m}\#X^{\#n}

is explicitly given in matrix form, with respect to the standard basis of
\mathbb{Z}^{n+m}
by the matrix

\beta_{n,m}=\begin{pmatrix}A_{m,n}&I_{m}\\
(-1)^{m}I_{n}&0\end{pmatrix}\ \ \textrm{with}\ \ (A_{m,n})_{ij}=(-1)^{i+1}2

Proof.

The hexagon identity follows from the Yang-Baxter equation, as a direct consequence of the Yang-Baxter equation for
T^{-1}

[
6
, Sec 3.2]
. The matrix description of
\beta_{n,m}
can be read out from the effect of block braids on the arcs denoted
\rho_{i}
in
[
6
]
, whose homology classes correspond to the standard generators of
\mathbb{Z}^{n+m}
under the functor
\operatorname{F}
: The computation of the braiding on these arcs is done in the proof of Proposition 5.7 in
[
6
]
, and the matrix description for
\beta_{n,m}
can be read directly from this computation.

Left is to check naturality of the braiding, i.e. that for any
\phi\in\operatorname{Aut}(X^{\#n})\subset GL_{n}(\mathbb{Z})
and any
\psi\in\operatorname{Aut}(X^{\#m})\subset GL_{m}(\mathbb{Z})
we have
\beta_{n,m}\circ(\phi\oplus\psi)=(\psi\oplus\phi)\circ\beta_{n,m}
.
This holds if
A_{m,n}\phi=A_{n,m}=\psi A_{m,n}
, which we check now.

Explicitly, if
\phi
and
\psi
are given in matrix form by matrices
B
and
C
, then

A_{n,m}\phi=A_{n,m}B=\begin{pmatrix}2\sum_{j}b_{j1}&\dots&2\sum_{j}b_{jn}\\
-2\sum_{j}b_{j1}&\dots&-2\sum_{j}b_{jn}\\
\vdots&&\vdots\\
(-1)^{m+1}2\sum_{j}b_{j1}&\dots&(-1)^{m+1}2\sum_{j}b_{jn}\end{pmatrix}

and

\psi A_{m,n}=CA_{n,m}=\begin{pmatrix}2\sum_{i}(-1)^{i+1}c_{1i}&2\sum_{i}(-1)^{i+1}c_{1i}&\dots&2\sum_{i}(-1)^{i+1}c_{1i}\\
2\sum_{i}(-1)^{i+1}c_{2i}&2\sum_{i}(-1)^{i+1}c_{2i}&\dots&2\sum_{i}(-1)^{i+1}c_{2i}\\
\vdots&\vdots&&\vdots\\
2\sum_{i}(-1)^{i+1}c_{mi}&2\sum_{i}(-1)^{i+1}c_{mi}&\dots&2\sum_{i}(-1)^{i+1}c_{mi}\\
\end{pmatrix}

The map
\partial
is represented by the matrix
\partial=\begin{pmatrix}1&1&\dots&1\end{pmatrix}

with respect to the standard basis, hence the equation
\partial B=\partial
gives that each column in
B
must sum to 1, giving the identity
A_{m,n}B=A_{m,n}
.

To check that
CA_{m,n}=A_{m,n}
we will use both the invariance of
\partial
and
\lambda
: The fact that
\partial C=\partial
gives that
C^{T}(\partial^{T}\partial)C=\partial^{T}\partial
, and
since
C^{T}\lambda C=\lambda
, we have that
C^{T}\lambda^{\prime}C=\lambda^{\prime}
where
\lambda^{\prime}=\lambda+\partial^{T}\partial
is the “curved” form of Remark
2.4
. In matrix form, we have

\lambda^{\prime}=\begin{pmatrix}1&2&2&\dots&2\\
0&1&2&\dots&2\\
\vdots&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&\dots&0&1&2\\
0&&\dots&0&1\end{pmatrix}\ \ \textrm{with}\ \ (\lambda^{\prime})^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}1&-2&\ 2&\dots&(-1)^{m-1}2\\
0&1&-2&\dots&\\
\vdots&\ddots&\ddots&\ \ \ddots&\vdots\\
0&\dots&0&1&\ -2\\
0&&\dots&0&\ \ 1\end{pmatrix}

from which we can compute that

(\lambda^{\prime})^{-1}\partial^{T}=\begin{pmatrix}(-1)^{m-1}\\
\vdots\\
-1\\
1\end{pmatrix}=(-1)^{m-1}\begin{pmatrix}1\\
-1\\
\vdots\\
(-1)^{m-1}\end{pmatrix}.

Now putting together that
C^{T}=\lambda^{\prime}C^{-1}(\lambda^{\prime})^{-1}
and
C^{T}\partial^{T}=\partial^{T}
, we get that
C((\lambda^{\prime})^{-1}\partial^{T})=(\lambda^{\prime})^{-1}\partial^{T}
, from which
the identity
CA_{m,n}=A_{m,n}
follows.
∎

2.3.
The hyperbolic genus and the arc genus I

Recall from above the hyperbolic formed space
\HH=(R^{2},\lambda_{\HH})
in
\mathcal{F}
.
Let
\HH^{\oplus g}=\HH\oplus\dots\oplus\HH
its
g

The
hyperbolic genus
(called
Witt index
in
[
4
]
) of a formed space
M=(M,\lambda)\in\mathcal{F}
is

g_{\HH}(M,\lambda):=\max\{g\in\mathbb{N}\ |\ M\cong\HH^{\oplus g}\oplus M^{\prime}\ \textrm{in}\ \mathcal{F}\}.

Remark 2.12
.

We could likewise define the hyperbolic genus of a formed space with boundary
(M,\lambda,\partial)\in\mathcal{F}_{\partial}
using the same formula, replacing
\HH
by the object
(\HH,0)
of
\mathcal{F}_{\partial}
, setting the boundary map to be trivial. Note that
(\HH,0)^{\#g}=(\HH^{\oplus g},0)
since the boundary map is trivial. In fact, the
(\HH,0)
–genus of
(M,\lambda,\partial)
in
\mathcal{F}_{\partial}
is really the same thing as the hyperbolic genus of
(\ker\partial,\lambda|_{\ker\partial})
. It will appear in this latter form in our computations below.

For the rest of the paper the most relevant quantity will be the
arc genus
(or
X
–genus) of a formed space
(M,\lambda,\partial)
, defined as

g_{X}(M,\lambda,\partial):=\max\{g\in\mathbb{N}\ |\ M\cong M^{\prime}\#X^{\#g}\ \textrm{in}\ \mathcal{F}_{\partial}\}.

This is the quantity that will rule the relevant stability bounds.

The above definitions of hyperbolic and arc genus are based on how many copies of
\HH
or
X
one can split off
(M,\lambda)
or
(M,\lambda,\partial)
.
We will see below, in Lemma
2.14
, that the splitting condition “comes for free” for such objects. The result will use that the formed spaces
X^{\#n}
are non-degenerate, in a sense that we define now.

Recall that a formed space
(M,\lambda)
is
non-degenerate
if the form
\lambda:M\otimes M\to R
is a non-degenerate pairing.
The hyperbolic space
\HH^{\oplus g}
is for example non-degenerate for every
g
.
For formed spaces with boundary, we generalize this definition as follows:
Recall that the curved form associated to a formed space
(M,\lambda,\partial)
is the form
\lambda^{\prime}
defined by
\lambda^{\prime}(x,y)=\lambda(x,y)+\partial(x)\partial(y)
.

Definition 2.13
.

A formed space with boundary
(M,\lambda,\partial)
is
non-degenerate
if the curved form
\lambda^{\prime}:M\otimes M\to R
is a non-degenerate pairing, i.e. if the map
M\to M^{\vee}
,
m\mapsto\lambda^{\prime}(m,-)
is an isomorphism.

The formed spaces with boundary
X^{\#n}
are non-degenerate for any
n\geq 1
, as can be checked by inspecting their curved forms, whose matrix is given in the proof of Proposition
2.9
above.

Lemma 2.14
.

(i)

Any morphism
\HH^{\oplus g}\xrightarrow{f}(M,\lambda)
in
\mathcal{F}
gives an orthogonal splitting

(M,\lambda)\cong\HH^{\oplus g}\oplus(\operatorname{Im}(f)^{\perp},\lambda|_{\operatorname{Im}(f)^{\perp}}).

(ii)

Any morphism
X^{\#n}\xrightarrow{f}(M,\lambda,\partial)
in
\mathcal{F}_{\partial}
gives a splitting

(M,\lambda,\partial)\cong(M\setminus\operatorname{Im}(f),\lambda|_{M\setminus\operatorname{Im}(f)},\partial|_{M\setminus\operatorname{Im}(f)})\#X^{\#n}

where for
N\subset M
we let
M\setminus N:=\{m\in M:\;\lambda^{\prime}(n,m)=0\;\forall n\in N\}
.

Proof.

Part (i) follows from the fact that
\HH^{\oplus g}
is non-degenerate, see e.g.
[
4
, Chap 3]
. The same proof applies to Part (ii), as we detail now.

Part (ii): Since
\lambda^{\prime}
is non-degenerate on
X^{\#n}
then
f
has to be injective and we get a splitting
M=(M\setminus\operatorname{Im}(f))\oplus\operatorname{Im}(f)
as

Observe that
\partial=\partial|_{M\setminus\operatorname{Im}(f)}\oplus\partial|_{\operatorname{Im}(f)}
splits with respect to the above decomposition.
By definition, if
x\in M\setminus\operatorname{Im}(f)
and
y\in\operatorname{Im}(f)
then
\lambda^{\prime}(y,x)=0
and hence
\lambda(y,x)=-\partial y\partial x
so that
\lambda
also agrees with the sum

\lambda|_{M\setminus\operatorname{Im}(f)}\#\lambda_{\operatorname{Im}(f)}
on cross-terms.
Thus, we get

(M,\lambda,\partial)=(M\setminus\operatorname{Im}(f),\lambda|_{M\setminus\operatorname{Im}(f)},\partial|_{M\setminus\operatorname{Im}(f)})\#(\operatorname{Im}(f),\lambda|_{\operatorname{Im}(f)},\partial|_{\operatorname{Im}(f)}).

Since

, as required.
∎

Lemma 2.15

By the definition of

Remark 2.19

(Other form parameters)
.

The orthogonal group
O_{n,n}(R)
and the unitary group
U_{n,n}(R)
can both be defined, just like the symplectic group
\operatorname{Sp}_{2n}(R)
, as the automorphism group of the hyperbolic space
\HH^{n}
, just changing the
form parameters
, working with quadratic or Hermitian forms instead of alternating forms, see e.g.
[
13
]
. The category
\mathcal{F}_{\partial}
can be defined in these other contexts, and the object

exists just as well. Adapting accordingly the definition of the monoidal structure in
\mathcal{F}_{\partial}
, we can also ensure that
X\#X\cong(\HH,\partial)
. However, it will no longer hold that

has a large hyperbolic genus as

increases, and hence the sequence of groups
\operatorname{Aut}(X^{\#n})
will not be related to the classical groups
O_{n,n}(R)
and
U_{n,n}(R)
in those cases.
For example, in the case of symmetric forms one can check that over
\mathbb{Z}
the form associated to

has signature
(1,n-1)
and hence its hyperbolic genus is
1
for any
n\geq 2
.

Remark 2.20

(Direct sum monoidal structure)
.

The category
\mathcal{F}_{\partial}
has a monoidal structure
\#
as discussed in Section
2
, but it also has a more obvious monoidal structure
\oplus
given by direct sum
(M_{1},\lambda_{1},\partial_{1})\oplus(M_{2},\lambda_{2},\partial_{2})=(M_{1}\oplus M_{2},\lambda_{1}\oplus\lambda_{2},\partial_{1}+\partial_{2})
.
This second monoidal structure is symmetric but not relevant for our purposes since
\lambda_{X^{\oplus n}}=0
for all

so
\{X^{\oplus n}\}_{n\geq 0}
is unrelated to symplectic groups.
However, by Proposition
2.16

X^{\#2g}\oplus X^{\#2h}\cong X^{\#2(g+h)}
so the full subcategory
\mathcal{C}
of
\mathcal{F}_{\partial}
with objects formed spaces with boundary which are isomorphic to some

and

.
It turns out that they are equivalent as monoidal structures but not as braided monoidal ones (hence in particular the face maps of the corresponding destabilization complexes are a priori different). More precisely,

(i)

is strong monoidal.
In fact, we can give an explicit example of strong monoidality

for the functor

as follows: given
\underline{M_{i}}=(M_{i},\lambda_{i},\partial_{i})\in\mathcal{F}_{\partial}
for

we let

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces L_{\underline{M_{1}},\underline{M_{2}}}:F(\underline{M_{1}})\#F(\underline{M_{2}})}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces F(\underline{M_{1}}\oplus\underline{M_{2}})}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces(x,y)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces(x-(\partial_{2}y)\cdot m_{1},y),}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\simeq}

where

is uniquely characterized by

(ii)

There is no non-zero braided strong monoidal functor

Part (i) can be checked explicitly using Proposition
2.9
and Remark

and using that

, and part (ii) follows from the fact that the braidings of Proposition

never square to the identity on non-zero modules.

2.4.
Properties of the base ring

In this section we will address the question of which (commutative) base rings
R
satisfy the main results of this paper. These conditions will be some of the so called “stability conditions”.

Recall that a vector
m\in M
is called
unimodular
if it generates a split
R
-summand in
M
.
Equivalently,
m
is unimodular if there exits
\phi\in M^{\vee}
such that
\phi(m)=1
.
More generally, a sequence
(m_{1},\dots,m_{n})
of vectors in
M
is called
unimodular
if the canonical map
R\langle m_{1}\rangle\oplus\cdots\oplus R\langle m_{n}\rangle\to M
is a split injection.
Equivalently,
(m_{1},\dots,m_{n})
is unimodular if there exist
\phi_{1},\dots,\phi_{n}\in M^{\vee}
such that
\phi_{i}(m_{j})=\delta_{i,j}
.

Definition 2.21
.

[
13
, Sec 5 and Def 6.3]

(1)

A ring
R
satisfies the
stable range condition

(S_{n})
if for every unimodular vector
(r_{0},\dots,r_{n})
in
R^{n+1}
there are
t_{0},\dots,t_{n-1}\in R
such that the vector
(r_{0}+t_{0}r_{n},\dots,r_{n-1}+t_{n-1}r_{n})\in R^{n}
is unimodular.
If
n
is the smallest such number we say that
R
has
stable rank

sr(R)=n
, and it has
sr(R)=\infty
if no such
n
exists.

(2)

The
unitary stable rank
usr(R)
is the smallest number
n\geq sr(R)
and such that the subgroup
ESp_{2n+2}(R)
of elementary matrices acts transitively on the set of unimodular vectors in
R^{2n+2}
.

The group
ESp_{2n+2}
is defined in
[
13
, Sec 6]
. We will not use the explicit description of this group, but rather the transitivity and cancellation properties derived from the unitary stable rank condition in
[
13
,
4
]
.

By definition,
sr(R)\leq usr(R)
. The following are some examples of rings with bounds on the unitary stable ranks.

Example 2.22
.

[
13
, Ex 6.5]

(i)

A commutative Noetherian ring
R
of finite Krull dimension
d
satisfies
usr(R)\leq d+1
.
In particular,
if
R
is a Dedekind domain then
usr(R)\leq 2
and for a field
k
, the polynomial ring

K=k[t_{1},\dots,t_{n}]
satisfies
usr(K)\leq n+1
.

(ii)

More generally, any
R
-algebra
A
that is finitely generated as an
R
-module satisfies
usr(A)\leq d+1

for
R
again a commutative Noetherian ring of finite Krull dimension
d
.

(iii)

Recall that a ring
R
is called
semi-local
if
R/J(R)
is a left Artinian ring, for
J(R)
the Jacobson
radical of
R
. A semi-local ring satisfies
usr(R)=1
.

The following result may be seen as somewhat surprising, as it gives a good behavior of hyperbolic summands under a genus condition ruled by the stable rank
sr(R)
, and not the unitary stable rank
usr(R)
.
In the following section, we will combine this result with finiteness of the unitary stable rank to deduce some important properties of the hyperbolic genus that will be used for the rest of the paper.

Proposition 2.23
.

Let
R
be a (commutative) ring with
sr(R)<\infty
. Let
(M,\lambda)\in\mathcal{F}
with
g_{\HH}(M,\lambda)\geq\frac{sr(R)}{2}
and let
l\in M^{\vee}
be unimodular.
Then there is a hyperbolic summand
\HH\cong(H,\lambda|_{H})\subset(M,\lambda)
such that
l|_{H}
is unimodular in
H^{\vee}
.

Proof.

We will proceed in two steps, the first one is a special case and the second one is to deduce the general case from it. Write
g:=g_{\HH}(M,\lambda)
.

Step 1.
Suppose first that
(M,\lambda)=\HH^{\oplus g}\oplus(R,0)
.
Forgetting the form, we can identify
M=R^{2g+1}
as an
R
-module. Denote
x_{1},\dots,x_{2g+1}
the standard basis vectors of
R^{2g+1}
, where the first
2g
vectors span
\HH^{\oplus g}
and
x_{2g+1}
spans the summand
(R,0)
.

Since
l
is unimodular then
(l(x_{1}),\dots,l(x_{2g}),l(x_{2g+1}))\in R^{2g+1}
is a unimodular vector.
By assumption
2g\geq sr(R)
, so by definition of the stable rank there exist
t_{1},\dots,t_{2g}\in R
such that
(l(x_{1})+t_{1}l(x_{2g+1}),\dots,l(x_{2g})+t_{2g}l(x_{2g+1}))\in R^{2g}
is unimodular.
Thus, there exist
a_{1},\dots,a_{2g}\in R
such that
\sum_{i=1}^{2g}{a_{i}(l(x_{i})+t_{i}l(x_{2g+1}))}=1
. Note that the vector
(a_{1},\dots,a_{2g})\in R^{2g}
is necessarily itself also unimodular.

Now consider
u=(u_{0},u_{1})\in R^{2g}\oplus R=M
, where
u_{0}=(\sum_{i=1}^{2g}{a_{i}x_{i}})
and
u_{1}=(\sum_{i=1}^{2g}a_{i}t_{i})x_{2g+1}
.
By construction
l(u)=1
,
and
u_{0}
is unimodular. Let
v\in\HH^{\oplus g}
be such that
\lambda(u_{0},v)=1
.
Now we also have
\lambda(u,v)=1
since
x_{2g+1}
is orthogonal to
\HH^{\oplus g}
.
Then
H=\langle u,v\rangle\subset M
is a hyperbolic summand by Lemma
2.14
(i), and it has the desired properties.

Step 2.
Now consider a general formed space
(M,\lambda)
. Up to isomorphism,

(M,\lambda)=\HH^{\oplus g}\oplus(N,\lambda_{N})
.
Since
l
is unimodular, then there is some
x\in M
such that
l(x)=1
. Write
x=x_{0}+x_{1}
with
x_{0}\in\HH^{\oplus g}
and
x_{1}\in N
.
Since
N
is orthogonal to
\HH^{\oplus g}
and any vector is isotropic, we get a (not necessarily injective) morphism in
\mathcal{F}

\phi=\id\oplus x_{1}:\HH^{\oplus g}\oplus(R,0)\longrightarrow\HH^{\oplus g}\oplus(N,\lambda|_{N})=(M,\lambda).

Note that the pull-back map
l^{\prime}:=l\circ\phi
is unimodular since
l^{\prime}((x_{0},1))=l(x_{0}+x_{1})=1
by construction.
Thus, by Step 1 there is a hyperbolic
H\subset\HH^{\oplus g}\oplus(R,0)
such that
l^{\prime}|_{H}
is unimodular in
H^{\vee}
.
Hence, by Lemma
2.14
(i),
\phi(H)\subset(M,\lambda)
is a hyperbolic summand and by construction
l|_{\phi(H)}
is unimodular in
\phi(H)^{\vee}
, as required.
∎

Since PIDs have finite unitary stable rank, all of the above applies to them. However, we will in the following treat the case of PIDs separately, because PIDs have special properties that allow to get slightly better results than for general rings.
We summarize these properties in the following lemma:

Lemma 2.24
.

Let
R
be a PID and
M
a finitely generated free module. Then

(i)

If
m\in M
is non-zero then
m=r\cdot m^{\prime}
where
r\in R
and
m^{\prime}\in M
is unimodular. Moreover,
GL(M)
acts transitively on the set of unimodular vectors in
M
.

(ii)

If
\lambda\in\Lambda^{2}M^{\vee}
is an alternating form on
M
then
\lambda
is isomorphic to a canonical form

\bigoplus_{i=1}^{k}d_{i}\!\HH\oplus(R^{l},0)

such that
d_{1}|d_{2}|\cdots|d_{k}
are non-zero and unique up to multiplying by units of
R
, and
d\HH
means
\Big(R^{2},\begin{pmatrix}0&d\\
-d&0\end{pmatrix}\Big)
.

(iii)

If
(M,\lambda)\in\mathcal{F}
then
g_{\HH}(M,\lambda)
is half the number of units in the Smith normal form of a matrix representing
\lambda
.

Proof.

To prove (i), we can assume
M=R^{n}
for some
n
. Pick standard basis
e_{1},\dots,e_{n}
and let
m=r_{1}e_{1}+\cdots+r_{n}e_{n}
,
r_{i}\in R
.
Then let
r\in R
be such that there is an equality of ideals
(r_{1},\dots,r_{n})=(r)
in
R
. We must have
r\neq 0
since
m\neq 0
and we can write
r_{i}=rr_{i}^{\prime}
for unique
r_{i}^{\prime}\in R
.
Then let
m^{\prime}=r_{1}^{\prime}e_{1}+\cdots+r_{n}^{\prime}e_{n}
.
By construction
r\in(r_{1},\dots,r_{n})
so there are
a_{1},\dots,a_{n}\in R
such that
r=a_{1}r_{1}+\cdots a_{n}r_{n}
, so
1=a_{1}r_{1}^{\prime}+\cdots a_{n}r_{n}^{\prime}
.
Thus,
m^{\prime}
is unimodular and
m=rm^{\prime}
, proving the statement.

The moreover part follows by Smith normal form since we can assume without loss of generality that
M=R^{n}
.

The proof of (ii) can be found in
[
14
, Theorem IV.I]
. It is stated for skew-symmetric forms in characteristic not 2, but the same proof works also for alternating forms in characteristic 2.

Part (iii) follows immediately from (ii) as any two matrices representing
\lambda
will have the same Smith normal form, and the Smith Normal Form of the canonical form defined above is given by
diag(d_{1},d_{1},\dots,d_{k},d_{k},0,\dots,0)
.
∎

2.5.
The hyperbolic genus and the arc genus II

Our goal now is to relate the hyperbolic and arc genera and then get bounds on how much the arc genus can decrease when taking kernels of linear functionals. We will later define the operation of “cutting arcs” in a given module, see Section
3.3
, and these results will imply bounds on how the arc genus decreases when cutting arcs. Given a formed space with boundary
(M,\lambda,\partial)
, we will control its arc genus using the hyperbolic genus of
(M,\lambda)
and that of the kernel of
\partial
.
To begin with, we summarize some properties of hyperbolic forms that will be used for the rest of the paper.

Proposition 2.25
.

Let
R
be a ring
and
(M,\lambda)
a formed space of genus
g=g_{\HH}(M,\lambda)
.

Suppose that
g\geq usr(R)+1
or
R
is a PID.

(i)

If
f:\HH\rightarrow(M,\lambda)
is a morphism in
\mathcal{F}
,
then
g_{\HH}(\operatorname{Im}(f)^{\perp},\lambda|_{\operatorname{Im}(f)^{\perp}})=g-1
.

(ii)

The group
\operatorname{Aut}(\HH^{\oplus g})\cong\operatorname{Sp}_{2g}(R)
acts transitively on the set of unimodular vectors of
R^{2g}
.

(iii)

If
l\in M^{\vee}
is unimodular,
then there is a hyperbolic basis
(e_{1},f_{1},\dots,e_{g},f_{g})
in
M
such that
l(e_{1})=1
,
l(e_{i})=0
for
2\leq i\leq g
, and
l(f_{i})=0
for all
i
.
Moreover, if
R
is a PID, then for any
l\in\HH^{\vee}
, there is a hyperbolic basis
e,f
such that
l(f)=0
.

(iv)

If
l\in M^{\vee}
is unimodular,
then
g_{\HH}(\ker l,\lambda|_{\ker l})\geq g-1
.
Moreover, if
R
is a PID then the unimodularity condition can be dropped.

Proof.

Part (i)
: Let us first consider the case that
R
is not a PID. By Lemma
2.14
(i) we have
(M,\lambda)\cong\HH\,\oplus\,(\operatorname{Im}(f)^{\perp},\lambda|_{\operatorname{Im}(f)^{\perp}})
. Hence
g_{\HH}(M,\lambda)=g\geq 1+g_{\HH}(\operatorname{Im}(f)^{\perp},\lambda|_{\operatorname{Im}(f)^{\perp}})
.
For the reversed inequality, we have that
(M,\lambda)\cong\HH^{\oplus g}\oplus(N,\lambda_{N})=\HH\oplus(\HH^{g-1}\oplus(N,\lambda_{N}))
. Given that
g\geq usr(R)+1
,
cancellation (
[
4
, Cor 3.14]
)
gives that
(\operatorname{Im}(f)^{\perp},\lambda|_{\operatorname{Im}(f)^{\perp}})\cong\HH^{g-1}\oplus(N,\lambda_{N})
, proving the result.

When
R
is a PID, use again Lemma
2.14
(i) to write
(M,\lambda)\cong\HH\oplus(\operatorname{Im}(f)^{\perp},\lambda|_{\operatorname{Im}(f)^{\perp}})
. The number of units in the Smith normal form of a matrix representing
\lambda
is thus the number of units in the Smith normal form of a matrix representing
\lambda|_{\operatorname{Im}(f)^{\perp}}
plus
2
.
This gives the result by Lemma
2.24
(iii).

Part (ii)
: This follows directly from the definition of
usr(R)
under the assumption
g\geq usr(R)+1
. When
R
is a PID, we need to check that the result also holds for small values of
g
. Let
x\in\HH^{\oplus g}
be a unimodular vector.
Since
\lambda
is unimodular, there must exist
y\in\HH^{\oplus g}
such that
\lambda(x,y)=1
. Thus,
\langle x,y\rangle
is a hyperbolic summand in
\HH^{\oplus g}
, and it follows from Lemma
2.24
(ii) that the orthogonal complement
\langle x,y\rangle^{\perp}
is isomorphic to
\HH^{\oplus g-1}
. This reduces the computation to genus 1, where we have that
\operatorname{Sp}_{2}(R)=\operatorname{SL}_{2}(R)
(since
R
is commutative), from which transitivity follows by transitivity of the
GL_{2}(R)
-action, which can be shown by writing any unitary vector in coordinates as a matrix and using that its
Smith normal form is
(1,0)
.

Part (iii)
:
Assume first that
g=g_{\HH}(M,\lambda)\geq{usr(R)+1}
. Then
g\geq sr(R)+1\geq\frac{sr(R)}{2}
, so we can find a hyperbolic summand
\HH\subset(M,\lambda)
such that
l|_{\HH}
is unimodular by Proposition
2.23
, and write
(M,\lambda)=\HH\oplus(\tilde{M},\tilde{\lambda})
by Lemma
2.14
(i).
Now use part(i) to deduce that
g_{\HH}(\tilde{M},\tilde{\lambda})=g-1
and thus we can write
(M,\lambda)=\HH^{\oplus g}\oplus(N,\lambda_{N})
such that
l|_{\HH^{\oplus g}}
is surjective.

Since
\lambda|_{\HH^{\oplus g}}
is non-degenerate then there is
v\in\HH^{\oplus g}
unimodular such that
l|_{\HH^{\oplus g}}=\lambda(v,-)|_{\HH^{\oplus g}}
. Let
(e_{1},f_{1},\dots,e_{g},f_{g})
be a hyperbolic basis of
\HH^{\oplus g}
.
By part (ii) we can apply an automorphism of
\HH^{\oplus g}
to assume without loss of generality that
v=-f_{1}
, which gives the result.

Now suppose that
R
is a PID and consider first the case where
l\in\HH^{\vee}
is any linear map.
Pick a hyperbolic basis
e,f
of
\HH
and let
d\in R
be the generator of the ideal
(l(e),l(f))
, i.e.
(d)=\operatorname{Im}(l)\subset R
.
If
d=0
then we are done. Otherwise, let
A,C\in R
be such that
d=Al(e)+Cl(f)
.
Now consider the ideal
(A,C)\subset R
. We claim that
(A,C)=R
: let
r\in R
be a generator for
(A,C)
then
A=rA^{\prime}
,
C=rC^{\prime}
for some
A^{\prime},C^{\prime}\in R
, so
d=rd^{\prime}
where
d^{\prime}=A^{\prime}l(e)+C^{\prime}l(f)\in(l(e),l(f))=(d)
, so
d^{\prime}=ud
for some
u\in R
.
Thus,
d=rd^{\prime}=rud
, so
ru=1
so
r
is a unit, as required.
Therefore, there are
B,D\in R
such that
AD-BC=1
. Hence there is a matrix
\begin{pmatrix}A&B\\
C&D\end{pmatrix}\in SL_{2}(R)=\operatorname{Sp}_{2}(R)
, sending the hyperbolic basis
e,f
to a new hyperbolic basis
e^{\prime},f^{\prime}
such that
l(e^{\prime})=d
and
d|l(f^{\prime})
.
Applying the symplectic transformation
f^{\prime}\mapsto f^{\prime}-(l(f^{\prime})/d)e^{\prime}
gives the last part of the statement.

Assume finally that
l\in M^{\vee}
is unimodular with
R
a PID, and let
(e^{\prime},f^{\prime})
be a basis of a hyperbolic summand in
M
with
l(f^{\prime})=0
as just obtained. Consider
M^{\prime}=<e^{\prime},f^{\prime}>^{\perp}\subset M
. Since
l
is unimodular, there is some
x\in M^{\prime}
such that
l(te^{\prime}+x)=1
for some
t\in R
.
Let
f^{\prime\prime}=te^{\prime}+x+f^{\prime}
. Then
l(f^{\prime\prime})=1
and
\lambda(e^{\prime},f^{\prime\prime})=1

is a new hyperbolic summand of

is surjective. Now we can proceed as in the non-PID case, using the results already proved in part (i) and (ii).

Part (iv)
:
In the non-PID case, this folows from part (iii) by considering
\langle e_{2},f_{2},\dots,e_{g},f_{g}\rangle
.
In the case that

is a PID,
write
(M,\lambda)=\HH^{\oplus g}\oplus(N,\lambda_{N})
.
Consider the restriction of

to

\lambda_{\HH^{g}}(v,-)
for some

. If

use Lemma

for some

and

unimodular.
Since

.
This follows from part (ii) since we can assume

∎

Recall the formed space
X=(R,0,\id)
.
Note that

for the simple reason that

has no “arc” vector with boundary 1. More generally, if we want to have

then we need

to be surjective, which in our context is the same as unimodular, provided that

.
In general, we have the following result relating

to

Proposition 2.26
.

Let
R
be a ring and
(M,\lambda,\partial)
a formed space with boundary over
R
.

(a)

g_{X}(M,\lambda,\partial)\leq 1+g_{\HH}(M,\lambda)+g_{\HH}(\ker\partial,\lambda|_{\ker\partial})
.

In particular, if
g_{X}(M,\lambda,\partial)\geq 2n
then
g_{\HH}(M,\lambda,\partial)\geq n
.

(b)

If
{usr(R)}<\infty
and one of the following three conditions holds:

(i)

\partial
is unimodular and
R
is a PID.

(ii)

\partial
is unimodular and
g_{\HH}(M,\lambda)\geq{usr(R)+1}
.

(iii)

g_{X}(M,\lambda,\partial)\geq 2usr(R)+2
.

then equality holds:

g_{X}(M,\lambda,\partial)=1+g_{\HH}(M,\lambda)+g_{\HH}(\ker\partial,\lambda|_{\ker\partial}).

Proof.

We first show that
g_{X}(M,\lambda,\partial)\leq 1+g_{\HH}(M,\lambda)+g_{\HH}(\ker\partial,\lambda|_{\ker\partial})
always holds.

Pick a morphism
X^{\#n}\xrightarrow{f}(M,\lambda,\partial)
with
n=g_{X}(M,\lambda,\partial)
.
If

, Proposition
2.16
gives that the underlying form on

is
\HH^{\oplus g}
, and we can pick a hyperbolic basis in
\HH^{\oplus g}
such that
\partial=\lambda(e_{1},-)
so
\ker\partial\supset 0\oplus\HH^{g-1}
in this basis. Using
f
, we then get that
g_{\HH}(M,\lambda)\geq g
and
g_{\HH}(\ker\partial,\lambda|_{\ker\partial})\geq g-1
.
If

, Proposition
2.16
gives that

restricts to a morphism of formed spaces
(\HH^{\oplus g},0)\rightarrow(M,\lambda,\partial)
.
Thus,
g_{\HH}(M,\lambda)\geq g_{\HH}(\ker\partial,\lambda|_{\ker\partial})\geq g
and the inequality also follows. Finally, from the inequality, it follows that
g_{\HH}(M,\lambda)\leq n-1
implies that
g_{X}(M,\lambda,\partial)\leq 2(n-1)+1=2n-1
, giving the second part of (a).

Now we will show the reverse inequality
g_{X}(M,\lambda,\partial)\geq 1+g_{\HH}(M,\lambda)+g_{\HH}(\ker\partial,\lambda|_{\ker\partial})
whenever
\partial
is unimodular and
g_{\HH}(M,\lambda)\geq{usr(R)+1}
or

is a PID, that is (b) assumption (i) or (ii). (Note that when

and the inequality does not hold.)

By Proposition
2.25
(iv),
g_{\HH}(\ker\partial,\lambda|_{\ker\partial})\geq g_{\HH}(M,\lambda)-1
. On the other hand,
g_{\HH}(\ker\partial,\lambda|_{\ker\partial})\leq g_{\HH}(M,\lambda)
.
Hence there are two cases: when

and

have the same hyperbolic genus, and when they differ by one.

If

then pick a morphism of forms

.
By Lemma

(i), there is a splitting

.
By construction

and by assumption
\partial
is surjective, so

must be surjective too.
Pick

with

, then

is a morphism, and the source is isomorphic to

by Proposition

2.14
(ii).

If
g_{\HH}(\ker\partial,\lambda|_{\ker\partial})=g_{\HH}(M,\lambda)-1
: let

. By Proposition
2.25
(iii) with

there is a morphism

such that
\partial(\phi(e_{1}))=1
and
\partial(\phi(f_{1}))=\partial(\phi(e_{2}))=\dots=\partial(\phi(f_{g}))=0
.
In particular,

gives a morphism

and by construction

is surjective, hence unimodular.
By Proposition
2.16
and Proposition

, giving the required result by Lemma
2.14
(ii). This proves (b) under assumptions (i) or (ii).

Finally, if we assume that
g_{X}(M,\lambda,\partial)\geq 2usr(R)+2
then

is automatically surjective
and hence unimodular; and
g_{\HH}(M,\lambda)\geq{usr(R)+1}
by (a), which finishes the proof by the previous part.
∎

The following corollary will give us in the next section an estimate of the arc-genus of forms obtained after “cutting arcs”.

If

Proof.

to both

.

Note first that the unimodularity condition implies that

are unimodular. If

and

In the non-PID case, we check that both modules have large enough hyperbolic genus, checking assumption (b)(ii) instead.
Given that

, we have that

1+2g_{\HH}(M,\lambda)\geq 1+g_{\HH}(M,\lambda)+g_{\HH}(\ker\partial,\lambda|_{\ker\partial})\geq g_{X}(M,\lambda,\partial)\geq 2{usr(R)}+4,

where we used Proposition
2.26

This is only possible if

Since

2.25

Therefore, we can apply Proposition

and

\displaystyle g_{X}(M,\lambda,\partial)

\displaystyle=1+g_{\HH}(M,\lambda)+g_{\HH}(\ker\partial,\lambda|_{\ker\partial})

where we applied Proposition

3.
Arc complexes

In this section, we define algebraic analogues of arc complexes and study their connectivity. Our goal is to define an algebraic version of the disordered arc complex of
[
6
]
and show that it is highly connected. The proof of connectivity will
be parallel to the proof in the geometric case, using a complex of “non-separating arcs” along the way. We will see that algebraic arc complexes are closely related to posets of unimodular vectors, classically used to study linear groups. And in Section
4
, we will show that the algebraic disordered arc complex identifies with the destabilization complex for stabilization by
X
, in the sense of
[
15
]
.

We will work with three types of simplicial objects: simplicial complexes, semi-simplicial sets and nerves of posets. We start with a short section detailing how connectivity results can be passed between such types of objects in a specific situation. This will allow us to combine existing results proved in the context of posets, proof techniques developed in the context of simplicial complexes, while also using vertex ordering properties of semi-simplicial sets.

3.1.
Simplicial complexes, semi-simplicial sets and posets

Recall that a simplicial complex
\mathcal{S}
has a set of vertices, and
p
-simplices are given as unordered collections of
p+1
distinct vertices. On the other hand, in a semi-simplicial set
X=X_{\bullet}
, there is a set
X_{p}
of
p
-simplices for every
p
, and face maps
d_{i}:X_{p}\to X_{p-1}
for each
i=0,\dots,p
. Applying face maps repeatedly associates to any
p
-simplex
\sigma\in X_{p}
an ordered collection of
p+1
not necessarily distinct vertices in
X_{0}
. And this ordered collection of vertices need not determine
\sigma
.

Any simplicial complex
\mathcal{S}
can be given a semi-simplicial set structure by picking a total ordering of its set of vertices, defining the face map
d_{i}:\mathcal{S}_{p}\to\mathcal{S}_{p-1}
to be the map forgetting the
i
th vertex.
To
\mathcal{S}
, we can also associate the semi-simplicial set
\mathcal{S}^{ord}
, with the same vertices as
\mathcal{S}
and with a
p
-simplex for each ordered sequence
(a_{0},\dots,a_{p})
of vertices of
p
-simplices
\{a_{0},\dots,a_{p}\}
of
\mathcal{S}
. So
\mathcal{S}^{ord}
has
(p+1)!
simplices for every
p
-simplex of
\mathcal{S}
.

To
\mathcal{S}
, we can likewise associate two posets: the poset
s\mathcal{S}
of simplices of
\mathcal{S}
, oredered by inclusion, and
the poset
P(\mathcal{S})
of sequences
(a_{0},\dots,a_{p})
of vertices of
\mathcal{S}
with the property that
\{a_{0},\dots,a_{p}\}
is a
p
-simplex of
\mathcal{S}
, ordered by refinement. That is, for every
p
-simplex of
\mathcal{S}
, there is one element in the poset
s\mathcal{S}
and
(p+1)!
elements in the poset
P(\mathcal{S})
.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
\mathcal{S}
be a simplicial complex, with its associated semi-simplicial set
S^{ord}
and poset
P(\mathcal{S})
as above. Then
P(\mathcal{S})\cong s\mathcal{S}^{ord}
is isomorphic to the poset of simplices of
S^{ord}
. Moreover, if
\sigma=\{a_{0},\dots,a_{p}\}
is a
p
-simplex of
\mathcal{S}
, and
P(\mathcal{S})_{a_{0},\dots,a_{p}}
denotes the subposet of sequences in
P(\mathcal{S})
that end with
a_{0},\dots,a_{p}
, then

P(\mathcal{S})_{a_{0},\dots,a_{p}}\cong s\Big(\big(\link_{\mathcal{S}}(\sigma)\big)^{ord}\Big)

is isomorphic to the poset of simplices of the order complex of the link of
\sigma=\{a_{0},\dots,a_{p}\}
in
\mathcal{S}
.

Proof.

The statement follow directly from the definitions.
∎

The geometric realization of a simplicial complex or semi-simplicial set has a copy of the standard
p
-simplex
\Delta^{p}
for each
p
-simplex, glued along face inclusion, and the geometric realization of a poset has a
p
-simplex
\Delta^{p}
for each length
p
chain
a_{0}<\dots<a_{p}
in the poset. When we talk about connectivity properties of such objects, we will always mean the connectivity properties of their geometric realization.
Recall that the geometric realization of the poset of simplices
s\mathcal{S}
identifies with the barycentric subdivision of
\mathcal{S}
. In particular,
\mathcal{S}
and
s\mathcal{S}
have the same connectivity.

A simplicial complex
\mathcal{S}
is called
weakly Cohen-Macaulay
(or
wCM
for short)
of dimension
n
if it is
(n-1)
-connected, and the link of any
p
-simplex
\sigma
in
\mathcal{S}
is at least
(n-p-2)
-connected.
Proposition 2.14 of
[
15
]
states that when
\mathcal{S}
is wCM of dimension
n
, then
\mathcal{S}^{ord}
is necessarily at least
(n-1)
-connected. Building on this result, the following proposition shows that, under the wCM property, the connectivity properties of
\mathcal{S}
,
\mathcal{S}^{ord}
and
P(\mathcal{S})
are closely related:

Proposition 3.2
.

Let
\mathcal{S}
,
\mathcal{S}^{ord}
and
P(\mathcal{S})
be as above. Then the following are equivalent:

(i)

\mathcal{S}
is wCM of dimension
n
;

(ii)

\mathcal{S}^{ord}
is
(n-1)
-connected and
\big(\link_{\mathcal{S}}(\{a_{0},\dots,a_{p}\})\big)^{ord}
is
(n-p-2)
-connected for every
p
-simplex
\{a_{0},\dots,a_{p}\}
of
\mathcal{S}
;

(iii)

P(\mathcal{S})
is
(n-1)
-connected and
P(\mathcal{S})_{a_{0},\dots,a_{p}}
is
(n-p-2)
-connected for every
p
-simplex
(a_{0},\dots,a_{p})
of
\mathcal{S}^{ord}
.

Proof.

First note that (ii) and (iii) are equivalent since we have
P(\mathcal{S})\cong s\mathcal{S}^{ord}
and
P(\mathcal{S})_{a_{0},\dots,a_{p}}\cong s\link_{\mathcal{S}}(\{a_{0},\dots,a_{p}\})^{ord}
by the lemma, and since the realization of poset of simplices of a simplicial object identifies with the barycentric subdivision of the realization, and hence are homeomorphic.

We will show that (i) and (ii) are equivalent. Assuming (i),
[
15
, Prop 2.14]
gives that
\mathcal{S}^{ord}
is
(n-1)
-connected. Now note that, if
\sigma,\tau
are simplices in
\mathcal{S}
with
\tau\in\link_{\mathcal{S}}(\sigma)
, then
\link_{\link(\sigma)}(\tau)\cong\link_{\mathcal{S}}(\tau*\sigma)
. It follows that if
\mathcal{S}
is wCM of dimension
n
, then the link of any
p
-simplex
\sigma
in
\mathcal{S}
is wCM of dimension
n-p-1
. Indeed, if
\tau
is a
q
-simplex, then
\tau*\sigma
is a
(p+q+1)
-simplex, and the assumption on
\mathcal{S}
implies that
\link_{\link(\sigma)}(\tau)
is
(n-(p+q+1)-2)=((n-p-1)-q-2)
-connected, as needed. So we can also apply
[
15
, Prop 2.14]
to
\link_{\mathcal{S}}(\{a_{0},\dots,a_{p}\})
to deduce that
\link_{\mathcal{S}}(\{a_{0},\dots,a_{p}\})^{ord}
is
(n-p-2)
-connected, showing that (i) implies (ii).

Assume now that (ii) holds. Forgetting the orderings induces a map on realizations
|\mathcal{S}^{ord}|\to|\mathcal{S}|
, and picking a total ordering of the vertices of
\mathcal{S}
defines a section of that map. It follows that
\mathcal{S}
is at least as highly connected as
\mathcal{S}^{ord}
. Likewise,
\link_{\mathcal{S}}(\sigma)
is at least as highly connected as
\link_{\mathcal{S}}(\sigma)^{ord}
. Hence (ii) implies that
\mathcal{S}
is wCM of dimension
n
.
∎

Finally, we give an adaptation of the simplicial approximation result for simplicial complexes, to semi-simplicial sets of the form
\mathcal{S}^{ord}
:

Proposition 3.3
.

Let
S
be a simplicial complex and
Y\subset\mathcal{S}^{ord}
a sub-semi-simplicial set of the order complex of
S
.
Let
K
,
L
be finite simplicial complexes,
with
L
a subcomplex of
K
. Let
f:|K|\to|\mathcal{S}^{ord}|
be a continuous map such that the restriction
f|_{L}

is a semi-simplicial map from
L
to
Y
for a semi-simplicial set structure on
L
. Then there exists a relative subdivision
(K_{r},L)
of
(K,L)
, a semi-simplicial set structure on
K_{r}
extending that of
L
and
a semi-simplicial map
g:K_{r}\to\mathcal{S}^{ord}
such that
g|_{L}=f|_{L}
and
g
is homotopic to
f
keeping
L
fixed.

Proof.

Consider
(\hat{f},f):(|K|,|L|)\to(|\mathcal{S}|^{ord},|Y|)\cong(|s\mathcal{S}^{ord}|,|sY|)
, with
s\mathcal{S}^{ord}
and
sY
denoting the poset of simplices, or equivalently barycentric subdivisions, of
\mathcal{S}^{ord}
and
Y
. The barycentric subdivision of any simplicial object has the property that any
p
-simplices is determined by its
p+1
(distinct) vertices. Hence, forgetting the ordering of the vertices, it can be considered as a simplicial complex, and we can use PL approximation of
[
23
]
for simplicial complexes. This gives a subdivision
K_{r}
of
K
(relative to
L
) and a simplicial map

\hat{f}^{\prime}:K_{r}\longrightarrow s\mathcal{S}^{ord}

restricting to
f
on

and such that
(\hat{f}^{\prime},f)\simeq(\hat{f},f)
.

Consider the forgetful map
u:s\mathcal{S}^{ord}\to\mathcal{S}^{ord}
that takes a vertex of
s\mathcal{S}^{ord}
, that is a simplex
(a_{0},\dots,a_{p})
of
\mathcal{S}^{ord}
, to the last vertex
a_{p}
. This defines a simplicial map since a simplex of
s\mathcal{S}^{ord}
is a chain of simplices of
\mathcal{S}^{ord}
, and the last vertices of such a chain necessarily define together a simplex of
\mathcal{S}
(of possibly smaller dimension). Moreover, using linear interpolation in the simplices, one can check that, on realizations and after identifying

, this map is homotopic to the identity.

Finally we can define a semi-simplicial set structure on
K_{r}
extending that of

as follows: Let
\sigma=\{v_{0},\dots,v_{q}\}
be a

-simplex of
K_{r}
. Because any

-simplex of
\mathcal{S}^{ord}
has

distinct vertices, the image of
\sigma
under the composed map

g:K_{r}\xrightarrow{\hat{f}^{\prime}}s\mathcal{S}^{ord}\xrightarrow{\ u\ }\mathcal{S}^{ord}

is determined by the collection of images
\{g(v_{0}),\dots,g(v_{q})\}
, that forms a
p
-simplex of
\mathcal{S}
for some

, together with the map
\theta_{\sigma}:\{v_{0},\dots,v_{q}\}\to\{0,\dots,p\}
that records where the vertices are mapped. We need to pick, for each such simplex
\sigma
, an ordering of its vertices that makes

order preserving. We start by picking, arbitrarily extending that of

, for each vertex
v
of
\mathcal{S}^{ord}
, an ordering of the vertices of the subcomplex of
K_{r}
of simplices mapped to
v
. Now for a general simplex
\sigma
with
g(\sigma)=(v_{0},\dots,v_{p})
, we can write
\sigma=\sigma_{v_{0}}*\dots*\sigma_{v_{p}}
where
\sigma_{v_{i}}
is the face of
\sigma
mapped to
v_{i}
. We order the vertices of
\sigma
using on each
\sigma_{v_{i}}
the ordering already chosen in the previous step, extending it to the total ordering induced by the ordering of the vertices
v_{i}
.
∎

3.2.
Complexes of non-separating arcs

Let
(M,\lambda,\partial)
be a formed space with boundary.
Recall that a collection
v_{1},\dots,v_{n}\in M
is unimodular if it generates a split
R^{n}
–summand.
In what follows, we will write
\lambda(a,b)=a\bullet b
for
\lambda
the form in the formed space
a
and
b
are considered in.

Definition 3.4
.

Let
M=(M,\lambda,\partial)
be a formed space with boundary.

(i)

An
arc
in
M
is an element
a\in M
such that
\partial(a)=1
.

(ii)

An arc
a
is
non-separating
if
\{a\bullet-,\partial\}
is unimodular in
M^{\vee}=\operatorname{Hom}(M,R)
.

Note that an arc is automatically unimodular in
M
since we can split
M=\ker\partial\oplus R\langle a\rangle
as
R
-modules.
On the other hand,
a\bullet-
is not necessarily unimodular in
M^{\vee}
; in fact it can be the zero map, see e.g. the arc
x-y+z
in
X^{\#3}
in the proof of Proposition
2.16
.
If
\partial
is assumed surjective, it is by definition unimodular in
M^{\vee}
, so the non-separating condition is equivalent to requiring that
a\bullet-
is unimodular in
(\ker\partial)^{\vee}
.

If
a
is a geometric arc in a bidecorated surface
(S,I_{0},I_{1})
going from
I_{0}
to
I_{1}
, then its homology class
[a]
defines an arc in the above sense in the associated formed space with boundary
(M,\lambda,\partial)
described in Section
2.1
. And if
a
is a non-separating arc, then that precisely means that there exists a curve
c
in
S
“connecting both sides of
a
”, i.e. an element
c
with
\partial c=0
and
a\bullet c=1
. So a geometric non-separating arc in a bidecorated surface will likewise define an algebraic non-separating arc.

Definition 3.5
.

(i)

The
algebraic arc complex

\mathcal{A}^{alg}(M,\partial)
is the simplicial complex whose vertices are arcs in
M
, and where a collection of arcs
\{a_{0},\dots,a_{p}\}
defines a
p
–simplex if the vectors together form a unimodular sequence.

(ii)

The
algebraic non-separating arc complex

\mathcal{B}(M,\lambda,\partial)
has vertices non-separating arcs, and
a collection of arcs
\{a_{0},\dots,a_{p}\}
forms a
p
-simplex if
\{a_{0}\bullet-,\dots,a_{p}\bullet-,\partial\}
is unimodular in
M^{\vee}
, i.e. the arcs are jointly non-separating.

As indicated above, the last condition has a geometric meaning when the arcs come from (pairwise disjoint) geometric arcs in a surface. It actually precisely corresponds to being non-separating by a Mayer-Vietoris argument.
It can equivalently be stated as:
\{a_{0}\bullet-,\dots,a_{p}\bullet-\}
is unimodular in
(\ker\partial)^{\vee}
, provided
\partial
is itself surjective.
Observe also that the condition in (ii) implies that of (i) so there is an inclusion
\mathcal{B}(M,\lambda,\partial)\subset\mathcal{A}^{alg}(M,\partial)
.

Our first goal in this section is to prove a connectivity result for
\mathcal{B}(M,\lambda,\partial)
. It will folllow from a result about certain posets of unimodular sequences, as studied in particular by van der Kallen, Maazen, Charney, see e.g.
[
22
,
11
,
2
]
. We will not actually need the connectivity of
\mathcal{A}^{alg}(M,\lambda)
, but it will be a direct consequence of these other results. We will work with the following posets:

Definition 3.6
.

Let
\mathbb{U}(M)
denote the poset of ordered non-empty unimodular sequences in
M
. For
N\leq M
a submodule and
m\in M
an element, we define
\mathbb{U}(M,m+N)
to be the subposet of
\mathbb{U}(M)
of unimodular sequences in
M
of elements of the form
m+n
with
n\in N
.

Our first connectivity results will be formulated in terms of the stable rank
sr(R)
of Definition
2.21
and following relative rank

r(M,N):=\max\{\rk(L):L\subset N\text{ is a direct summand of }M\}.

Theorem 3.7
.

Let
M
be a finitely generated free module.
The poset
\mathbb{U}(M,m+N)
is
(r(M,N)-sr(R)-1)
–connected, and for
v_{1},\dots,v_{k}\in\mathbb{U}(M,m+N)
, the subposet
\mathbb{U}(M,m+N)_{v_{1},\dots,v_{k}}
of sequences ending in
(v_{1},\dots,v_{k})
is
(r(M,N)-sr(R)-k-1)
–connected.

This result that can be found in
[
18
, Thm 6.5]
in the case
R=\mathbb{Z}
, and is based on earlier results of
[
22
, 2.6]
,
[
4
, Thm 2.4]
. A complete proof is given in Appendix
A
.

Let
\mathcal{U}(M)
and
\mathcal{U}(M,m+N)
denote the corresponding simplicial complexes of unimodular vectors: vertices of
\mathcal{U}(M)
are unimodular vectors, and a collection of unimodular vectors forms a
p
-simplex in
\mathcal{U}(M)
if they together form a unimodular sequence. And
\mathcal{U}(M,m+N)
is the full subcomplex of
\mathcal{U}(M)
on unimodular vectors of the form
m+n
for
n\in N
.

Theorem
3.7
has the following consequence:

Corollary 3.8
.

Both
\mathcal{U}(M,m+N)^{ord}
and
\mathcal{U}(M,m+N)
are
(r(M,N)-sr(R)-1)
–connected. Moreover
\mathcal{U}(M,m+N)
is wCM of dimension
r(M,N)-sr(R)
.

Proof.

The result thus follows from combining Theorem
3.7
with Proposition
3.2
since the poset
\mathbb{U}(M,m+N)
identifies with the poset of sequences
P(\mathcal{U}(M,m+N))
.
∎

Proposition 3.9
.

Let
M
be a finitely generated free module of rank
\rk(M)
and
\partial:M\to R
a linear map. If
\partial
is surjective, then
the algebraic arc complex
\mathcal{A}^{alg}(M,\partial)
is
(\rk(M)-sr(R)-2)
–connected.

Proof.

Since
\partial
is surjective, we have that
\mathcal{A}^{alg}(M,\partial)
is non-empty, proving the result for
\rk(M)-sr(R)-2\leq-1
.
Hence we can assume that
\rk(M)\geq sr(R)+2
.

The complex of
\mathcal{A}^{alg}(M,\partial)
identifies with
\mathcal{U}(M,a+\ker\partial)
for
a\in M
an arc. Since
\partial
is surjective then
\ker\partial\subset M
is a summand, so we have that
\mathbf{r}(M,\ker\partial)=\rk(\ker\partial)
.
Moreover,
R^{\rk(M)}\cong M\cong R\oplus\ker\partial
as
R
-modules, since
M
is finitely generated free. Then cancellation,
[
4
, Proposition 2.7]
, gives that
\ker\partial\cong R^{\rk(M)-1}
since
\rk(M)\geq sr(R)+1
.
The result then follows from Theorem
3.7
.
∎

The connectivity of the complex of geometric non-separating arcs is usually deduced from that of the complex of all arcs. In the algebraic case, while there is a map
\mathcal{B}(M,\lambda,\partial)\to\mathcal{A}^{alg}(M,\lambda)
,
it is more convenient to deduce the connectivity of
\mathcal{B}
from that of an arc complex closer to it and whose connectivity is also determined by Theorem
3.7
. We describe this complex now.

Let
\lambda^{\vee}(\ker\partial)
be the submodule of
(\ker\partial)^{\vee}
of maps that can be written as
c\bullet-
for
c\in\ker\partial
. Fix some arc
m
.
There is a map

\mathcal{B}(M,\lambda,\partial)\to\mathcal{U}((\ker\partial)^{\vee},m\bullet-+\lambda^{\vee}(\ker\partial))

associating to
a
the map
a\bullet-:\ker\partial\to R
.

For
M=(M,\lambda,\partial)
, we write

\mathbf{r}(M,\lambda,\partial):=r\big((\ker\partial)^{\vee},\lambda^{\vee}(\ker\partial)\big).

Proposition 3.10
.

Let
(M,\lambda,\partial)\in\mathcal{F}_{\partial}
. Then

\mathcal{B}(M,\lambda,\partial)
and
\mathcal{B}(M,\lambda,\partial)^{ord}
are
(\mathbf{r}(M,\lambda,\partial)-sr(R)-1)
–connected.

Recall from
[
8
]
that a simplicial complex
\mathcal{X}
is a
complete join complex
over another simplical complex
\mathcal{S}
if there is a surjective map
\pi:\mathcal{X}\to\ \mathcal{S}
and vertices
x_{0},\dots,x_{p}
of
\mathcal{X}
form a
p
-simplex if and only if the projections
\pi(x_{0}),\dots,\pi(x_{p})
form a
p
-simplex in
\mathcal{S}
. The proof will use that the simplicial complex
\mathcal{B}
can be seen as a join complex over the simplicial complex of unimodular sequences just described.

Proof.

The forgetful map
\mathcal{B}(M,\lambda,\partial)\to\mathcal{U}((\ker\partial)^{\vee},m\bullet-+\lambda^{\vee}(\ker\partial))
taking an arc
a
to the map
a\bullet-:\ker\partial\to R
exhibits
\mathcal{B}(M,\lambda,\partial)
as a complete join complex over
\mathcal{U}((\ker\partial)^{\vee},m\bullet-+\lambda^{\vee}(\ker\partial))
.
Indeed, a vertex of
\mathcal{B}(M,\lambda,\partial)
is a vertex
\phi
of
\mathcal{U}((\ker\partial)^{\vee},m\bullet-+\lambda^{\vee}(\ker\partial))
together with the choice of an arc
a\in M
such that
\phi=a\bullet-
, and we can write
a=m+c
for some
c\in\ker\partial
. Moreover, the condition for arcs
a_{0},\dots,a_{p}
to form a
p
-simplex of
\mathcal{B}(M,\lambda,\partial)
is determined by whether their images form a
p
–simplex in
\mathcal{U}((\ker\partial)^{\vee},m\bullet-+\lambda^{\vee}(\ker\partial))
.

Now
\mathcal{U}((\ker\partial)^{\vee},m\bullet-+\lambda^{\vee}(\ker\partial))
is wCM of dimension
r((\ker\partial)^{\vee},\lambda^{\vee}(\ker\partial))-sr(R)
. It then follows from
[
8
, Prop 3.5]
that
\mathcal{B}(M,\lambda,\partial)
is also wCM of dimension
r((\ker\partial)^{\vee},\lambda^{\vee}(\ker\partial))-sr(R)
, and in particular
(r((\ker\partial)^{\vee},\lambda^{\vee}(\ker\partial))-sr(R)-1)
–connected. Finally by
[
15
, Prop 2.14]
, the ordered complex
\mathcal{B}(M,\lambda,\partial)^{ord}
has the same connectivity.
∎

The following result will allow us to rewrite the above connectivity bound in terms of the arc genus.

Proposition 3.11
.

Let
(M,\lambda,\partial)
be a formed space with boundary such that
g_{X}(M,\lambda,\partial)\geq 2usr(R)+2
, or
R
is a PID. Then
\mathbf{r}(M,\lambda,\partial)\geq g_{X}(M,\lambda,\partial)-2
.

Proof.

The result is trivial if
g_{X}(M,\lambda,\partial)=0
, so we can assume without loss of generality that
g_{X}(M,\lambda,\partial)\geq 1
, i.e. that
\partial
is unimodular.
By Proposition
2.26
(b)((i),(iii)) we have

g_{X}(M,\lambda,\partial)=g_{\HH}(M,\lambda)+g_{\HH}(\ker\partial,\lambda|_{\ker\partial})+1.

By Proposition
2.26
(a), the inequality
g_{X}(M,\lambda,\partial)\geq 2usr(R)+2
implies
g_{\HH}(M,\lambda)\geq usr(R)+1
, and hence by Proposition
2.25
(iv) we have

2g_{\HH}(\ker\partial,\lambda|_{\ker\partial})\geq g_{\HH}(M,\lambda)+g_{\HH}(\ker\partial,\lambda|_{\ker\partial})-1.

Pick a hyperbolic subspace
L\subset\ker\partial
such that
(L,\lambda|_{L})\cong\HH^{g_{\HH}(\ker\partial,\lambda)}
.
Then,
\lambda^{\vee}(L)
is a direct summand of
(\ker\partial)^{\vee}
because of the orthogonal decomposition
\ker\partial=L\oplus L^{\perp}
(see Lemma
2.14
(i)).
Thus
\mathbf{r}(M,\lambda,\partial)=r\big((\ker\partial)^{\vee},\lambda^{\vee}(\ker\partial)\big)\geq\rk(\lambda^{\vee}(L))=\rk(L)=2g_{\HH}(\ker\partial,\lambda)
, which proves the result since the above computations give that
2g_{\HH}(\ker\partial,\lambda)\geq g_{X}(M,\lambda,\partial)-2
.
∎

The above results assemble to the following result:

Corollary 3.12
.

Let
(M,\lambda,\partial)
be a formed space with boundary with
g_{X}(M,\lambda,\partial)\geq 2usr(R)+2
, or such that
R
is a PID,
then
\mathcal{B}(M,\lambda,\partial)
and
\mathcal{B}(M,\lambda,\partial)^{ord}
are
(g_{X}(M,\lambda,\partial)-sr(R)-3)
–connected.

3.3.
Cutting arcs

Following the geometric argument in
[
6
]
, we need to understand the effect of “cutting a simplex of arcs” in
\mathcal{B}(M,\lambda,\partial)
.

Recall from Lemma
2.14
that for
N\subset M
, we denote

M\backslash N=\{m\in M\ |\ \lambda^{\prime}(n,m)=0\ \forall n\in N\}
, where
\lambda^{\prime}(n,m)=\lambda(n,m)+\partial n\partial m
.
Given a
p
-simplex
\sigma=\{a_{0},\cdots,a_{p}\}
, we will write
M\backslash\sigma=M\backslash N
for
N=\langle a_{0},\dots,a_{p}\rangle
the subspace generated by the arcs. Given that
\partial(a_{i})=1
for each
i
, we can rewrite this subspace as

M\backslash\sigma:=\bigcap_{i=0}^{p}{\ker(\partial+a_{i}\bullet-)}\subset M.

We call
(M\backslash\sigma,\lambda|_{M\backslash\sigma},\partial|_{M\backslash\sigma})
the
cut formed space
.

When
a_{0},\dots,a_{p}
are homology classes of geometric arcs in a bidecorated surface,
the cut formed space
M\backslash\sigma
identifies with the relative first homology group associated to the surface obtained by cutting the corresponding collection of (geometric) arcs, for an appropriate choice of marked intervals
I_{0},I_{1}
in the cut surface.

The main result we will need about cut formed spaces is the effect cutting arcs has on the rank and arc-genus.

Lemma 3.13
.

Let
\sigma=\{a_{0},\dots,a_{p}\}
be a
p
-simplex in
\mathcal{B}(M,\lambda,\partial)
.

(i)

If either
\rk(M)\geq sr(R)+p+1
or
R
is a PID then

\rk(M\backslash\sigma)=\rk(M)-(p+1).

(ii)

If either
g_{X}(M,\lambda,\partial)\geq 2usr(R)+2p+4
or
R
is a PID then

g_{X}(M\backslash\sigma,\lambda|_{M\backslash\sigma},\partial|_{M\backslash\sigma})\geq g_{X}(M,\lambda,\partial)-(2p+1).

Proof.

By definition
\{a_{0}\bullet-,\cdots,a_{p}\bullet-,\partial\}
is unimodular in
M^{\vee}
so
\{\partial+a_{0}\bullet-,\cdots,\partial+a_{p}\bullet-\}
is also unimodular, which implies that
\rk(M\backslash\sigma)=\rk(M)-(p+1)
which applies under the current assumption by
[
4
, Prop 2.7]

, proving (i) when
R
is not a PID.
For
R
a PID we use the same argument noting that
M\setminus\sigma
is a summand of the free finitely generated
R
-module
M
with quotient isomorphic to
R^{p+1}
, so it has to be free of rank
\rk(M)-p-1
by the classification theorem of finitely generated modules over PIDs.

We will show that
g_{X}(M\backslash\sigma,\lambda|_{M\backslash\sigma},\partial|_{M\backslash\sigma})\geq g_{X}(M,\lambda,\partial)-(2p+1)
by induction on
p
.
Let us begin by assuming the case
p=0
and finish the induction, and then we will study the case
p=0
in detail.

Let
\tau=\{a_{0},\dots,a_{p-1}\}
, then
\tau
is a
(p-1)
-simplex in
\mathcal{B}(M,\lambda,\partial)
so by induction we have
g_{X}(M\backslash\tau,\lambda|_{M\backslash\tau},\partial|_{M\backslash\tau})\geq g_{X}(M,\lambda,\partial)-(2p-1)
. We want to apply Corollary
2.27
to
M\backslash\tau
with
\partial=\partial|_{M\backslash\tau}
and
l=(\partial+a_{p}\bullet-)|_{M\backslash\tau}
.
Since
\{a_{0}\bullet-,\cdots,a_{p}\bullet-,\partial\}
is unimodular in
M^{\vee}
then so is
\{\partial+a_{0}\bullet-,\cdots,\partial+a_{p-1}\bullet-,\partial+a_{p}\bullet-,\partial\}
.
Therefore
\{(\partial+a_{p}\bullet-)|_{M\backslash\tau},\partial|_{M\backslash\tau}\}
is unimodular in
(M\backslash\tau)^{\vee}
. Moreover, either
R
is a PID, or we have

g_{X}(M\backslash\tau,\lambda|_{M\backslash\tau},\partial|_{M\backslash\tau})\geq g_{X}(M,\lambda,\partial)-(2(p-1)+1)\geq 2usr(R)+2p+4-2p+1=2usr(R)+5,

using induction for the first inequality and the assumption for the second one.
Hence we can apply Corollary
2.27
, and it gives the required result to finish the induction step.

Now let us consider the case
p=0
, so that
\sigma=\{a\}
.
Here we cannot apply the above induction step since that would only show that
g_{X}(M\backslash\sigma,\lambda|_{M\backslash\sigma},\partial|_{M\backslash\sigma})\geq g_{X}(M,\lambda,\partial)-2
instead of the required improved bound
g_{X}(M\backslash\sigma,\lambda|_{M\backslash\sigma},\partial|_{M\backslash\sigma})\geq g_{X}(M,\lambda,\partial)-1
.
To simplify the notation let us denote
\tilde{M}=M\backslash\sigma=\ker(\partial+a\bullet-)
,
\tilde{\lambda}=\lambda|_{M\backslash\sigma}
and
\tilde{\partial}=\partial|_{M\backslash\sigma}
for the rest of this proof.

By definition,
a
defines a map
X\to(M,\lambda,\partial)
so by Lemma
2.14
(ii) we have an isomorphism
(M,\lambda,\partial)\cong(\tilde{M},\tilde{\lambda},\tilde{\partial})\#X
.

Since
\{a\bullet-,\partial\}
is unimodular in
M^{\vee}
, then
\tilde{\partial}
is unimodular in
{\tilde{M}}^{\vee}
.
Without loss of generality
\rk(M)\geq 2
(otherwise the result we want to show is trivial), and hence
\rk(\tilde{M})\geq 1
by the first part of this lemma, since the assumption of (ii) is stronger than that of (i) because
2usr(R)+2\geq sr(R)+1
.

By Proposition
2.26
(a) and our assumption on
g_{X}(M,\lambda,\partial)
, we have that
g_{\HH}(M,\lambda,\partial)\geq usr(R)+2
, and (b)(ii) of the proposition gives that

g_{X}(M,\lambda,\partial)=1+g_{\HH}(M,\lambda)+g_{\HH}(\ker\partial,\lambda|_{\ker\partial}).

Applying now Proposition
2.25
(iv) with
l=\partial+a\bullet-
, we also get that
g_{\HH}(\tilde{M},\tilde{\lambda})\geq usr(R)+1
, so we can also apply Proposition
2.26
(b)(ii) to
\tilde{M}
, which gives

g_{X}(\tilde{M},\tilde{\lambda},\tilde{\partial})=1+g_{\HH}(\tilde{M},\tilde{\lambda})+g_{\HH}(\ker\tilde{\partial},\tilde{\lambda}|_{\ker\tilde{\partial}}).

We are left to compare the terms in the formulas for
g_{X}(M,\lambda,\partial)
and
g_{X}(\tilde{M},\tilde{\lambda},\tilde{\partial})
.
We first show that there is an isomorphism of formed spaces

(3.1)

f:(\ker\partial,\lambda|_{\ker\partial})\ \xrightarrow{\ \cong\ }\ (\tilde{M},\tilde{\lambda})

so that they both have the same
\HH
-genus. (This is an algebraic version of the geometric observation in Remark
2.8
.)
Indeed, the map
f
can be defined by
f(u)=u-(a\bullet u)a
, with inverse
g:\tilde{M}\rightarrow\ker\partial
defined by
g(v)=v-\partial(v)a
, where one checks that both maps preserve the forms.

Secondly, the unimodularity of
\{a\bullet-,\partial\}
in
M^{\vee}
implies that there is
x\in M
such that
\partial x=0
and
a\bullet x=1
.
We then claim that the forms
(\ker\tilde{\partial},\tilde{\lambda}|_{\ker\tilde{\partial}})
and
(\langle a,x\rangle^{\perp},\lambda|_{\langle a,x\rangle^{\perp}})
are isomorphic, so they have the same
\HH
-genus.
Indeed, the maps
f^{\prime}:\ker\tilde{\partial}\rightarrow\langle a,x\rangle^{\perp}
,
f^{\prime}(u)=u+(x\bullet u)a
, and
g^{\prime}:\langle a,x\rangle^{\perp}\rightarrow\ker\tilde{\partial}
,
g^{\prime}(v)=v-(\partial v)a
, give a pair of inverse isomorphisms preserving the forms.

Since
\langle a,x\rangle\subset M
is isomorphic to
\HH
then Proposition
2.25
(i) implies that
g_{\HH}(\langle a,x\rangle^{\perp},\lambda|_{\langle a,x\rangle^{\perp}})=g_{\HH}(M,\lambda)-1
, which finishes the proof.
∎

3.4.
Disordered arc complexes

Geometric arcs in a surface with the same endpoints
b_{0},b_{1}
in the boundary and with disjoint interiors, are naturally ordered at
b_{0}
and
b_{1}
. This leads to two natural subcomplexes of the complex of all (non-separating) collections of such arcs: the one where the orderings of the arcs at
b_{0}
and
b_{1}
agree, and the one where the ordering is reversed. The latter is the first one that was considered in the literature, and it goes under the name the
ordered arc complex
. The first one is the one whose algebraic version will be relevant to us, and, to distinguish it from its “opposite” version, it goes under the name
disordered arc complex
. (See
[
6
, Sec 2]
). We define now its algebraic analogue:

Definition 3.14
.

The
algebraic disordered arc complex

\mathcal{D}(M,\lambda,\partial)
is the simplicial subcomplex of
\mathcal{B}(M,\lambda,\partial)
, with the same vertices and where
\{a_{0},\dots,a_{p}\}
forms a
p
-simplex in
\mathcal{D}(M,\lambda,\partial)
if it is a
p
-simplex of
\mathcal{B}(M,\lambda,\partial)
and its vertices can be ordered in such a way that
a_{i}\bullet a_{j}=1
for all
i<j
.

More generally, for
\sigma=\langle a_{0},\dots,a_{p}\rangle
a simplex of
\mathcal{B}(M,\lambda,\partial)
, we define
\mathcal{D}_{<\sigma}(M,\lambda,\partial)
to be the simplicial subcomplex of
\link_{\mathcal{B}(M,\lambda,\partial)}(\sigma)
on the vertices
b
such that
b\bullet a_{i}=1
for all
i=0,\dots,p
, with
b_{0},\dots,b_{q}
forming a
p
-simplex if they can be ordered in such a way that
b_{i}\bullet b_{j}=1
for all
i<j
.

Note that the vertices of a simplex of
\mathcal{D}(M,\lambda,\partial)
are canonically ordered if
char(R)\neq 2
, so the complex is actually naturally a subcomplex of
\mathcal{B}(M,\lambda,\partial)^{ord}
in that case. Also, a vertex
b
in the link of a simplex
\sigma
in
\mathcal{D}(M,\lambda,\partial)
will have a position with respect to
\sigma
in that case; as apparent in the above definition, we will be particularly interested in vertices “below
\sigma
”, i.e. such that
b\bullet a_{i}=1
for all vertices
a_{i}
of
\sigma
. These different positions of vertices in the link do not exist in characteristic 2, making the characteristic 2 case actually simpler, as we will see in the proof of the theorem below.

Lemma 3.15
.

Let
\sigma
be a simplex of
\mathcal{B}(M,\lambda,\partial)
.
Then
\mathcal{D}_{<\sigma}(M,\lambda,\partial)\cong\mathcal{D}(M\backslash\sigma,\lambda|_{M\backslash\sigma},\partial|_{M\backslash\sigma})
.

Proof.

We will check both inclusions. Let us write
\sigma=(a_{0},\dots,a_{p})
.

Let
\tau=(b_{0},\dots,b_{q})
be a
q
-simplex in
\mathcal{D}_{<\sigma}(M,\lambda,\partial)
, then
(b_{0},\dots,b_{q},a_{0},\dots,a_{p})
is a
(p+q+1)
-simplex in
\mathcal{B}(M,\lambda,\partial)
. By definition,
b_{i}\bullet a_{j}=1=\partial(b_{i})
for all
i,j
so
b_{i}\in M\backslash\sigma
is an arc in the cut formed space for all
i
.
Unimodularity of
\{\partial,b_{0}\bullet-,\dots,b_{q}\bullet-,a_{0}\bullet-,\dots,a_{p}\bullet-\}
in
M^{\vee}
implies unimodularity of
\{\partial|_{M\backslash\sigma},b_{0}\bullet-,\dots,b_{q}\bullet-\}
in
(M\backslash\sigma)^{\vee}
.
Thus,
\{b_{0},\dots,b_{q}\}
is a
q
-simplex in
\mathcal{B}(M\backslash\sigma,\lambda|_{M\backslash\sigma},\partial|_{M\backslash\sigma})
.
Finally, the condition
b_{i}\bullet b_{j}=1
for
i<j
shows that
\tau
is a
q
-simplex in
\mathcal{D}(M\backslash\sigma,\lambda|_{M\backslash\sigma},\partial|_{M\backslash\sigma})
.

Conversely, if
\tau=(b_{0},\dots,b_{q})
is a
q
-simplex in
\mathcal{D}(M\backslash\sigma,\lambda|_{M\backslash\sigma},\partial|_{M\backslash\sigma})
then
\partial(b_{i})=1
for all
i
, so each
b_{i}\in M
is an arc too, and
\{\partial|_{M\backslash\sigma},b_{0}\bullet-,\cdots,b_{q}\bullet-\}
is unimodular in
(M\backslash\sigma)^{\vee}
, which implies unimodularity of
\{\partial,b_{0}\bullet-,\cdots,b_{q}\bullet-,a_{0}\bullet-,\cdots,a_{p}\bullet-\}
in
M^{\vee}
since
\{\partial,a_{0}\bullet-,\cdots,a_{p}\bullet-\}
is unimodular in
M^{\vee}
by definition.
Finally, condition
b_{i}\in M\backslash\sigma
says
b_{i}\bullet a_{j}=\partial(b_{i})=1
for all
i,j
, Thus,
\tau
is
q
-simplex in
\mathcal{D}_{<\sigma}(M,\lambda,\partial)
, as required.
∎

Theorem 3.16
.

Let
(M,\lambda,\partial)\in\mathcal{F}_{\partial}
. The complex
\mathcal{D}(M,\lambda,\partial)
is

\frac{g_{X}(M,\lambda,\partial)-2usr(R)-6}{3}
-connected. If
R
is a PID, the connectivity bound can be improved to
\frac{g_{X}(M,\lambda,\partial)-5}{3}
-connected.

The idea of the proof is to deduce the connectivity of the disordered arc complex
\mathcal{D}(M,\lambda,\partial)
from that of the simplicial complex
\mathcal{B}(M,\lambda,\partial)
. When the characteristic of
R
is not 2, we will actually work with the associated semi-simplicial set
\mathcal{B}(M,\lambda,\partial)^{ord}
. We will use a “bad simplex argument”, appropriately adapted to order complexes of simplicial complexes in the characteristic not 2 case; see Remark
3.17
for general comments about the generalization from simplicial complexes to such semi-simplicial sets, in the language of the general framework given in
[
7
, Sec 2.1]
.

Proof.

Let
A(R)=5
if
R
is a PID and
A(R)=2usr(R)+6
otherwise. So we want to show that
\mathcal{D}(M,\lambda,\partial)
is

\frac{g_{X}(M,\lambda,\partial)-A(R)}{3}
-connected.
Note that
A(R)\geq sr(R)+3
, since
sr(R)=2
when
R
is a PID, and since
2usr(R)+6>sr(R)+3
in the general case.

When
g_{X}(M,\lambda,\partial)\leq A(R)-4
the result is vacuously true.
When
g_{X}(M,\lambda,\partial)\in\{A(R)-3,A(R)-2,A(R)-1\}
we just need to show that
\mathcal{D}(M,\lambda,\partial)\neq\emptyset
, which is the case since
A(R)\geq 5
(in both cases) so we can pick a split
X^{\#2}
summand in
M
by Lemma
2.14
(ii) and the generator of the first
X
–summand will be a non-separating arc in
M
, and hence also a vertex of
\mathcal{D}(M,\lambda,\partial)
.

Thus we have a commutative diagram

\textstyle{S^{k}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{f}
\textstyle{\mathcal{D}(M,\lambda,\partial)\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{D^{k+1}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}

.

Remark 3.17

(Bad simplex argument for semi-simplicial sets)
.

We describe here how the proof to Theorem
3.16
, in the characteristic not 2 case, can be interpreted as a quite direct adaptation to semi-simplicial sets of the bad simplex argument, as formalized in
[
7
, Sec 2.1]
. For brevity, we will write
\mathcal{B}(M)=\mathcal{B}(M,\lambda,\partial)
and
\mathcal{D}(M)=\mathcal{D}(M,\lambda,\partial)
.

The heart of the proof of the theorem is to modify a map
D^{k+1}\to\mathcal{B}(M)^{ord}
, from a PL triangulation of the disc to the ordered complex of
\mathcal{B}(M)
, so that its image in the end lands in the subcomplex
\mathcal{D}(M)
of disordered arcs. To achieve this, we defined the notion of a “bad” simplex: a simplex
(a_{0},\dots,a_{p})
is bad if there is a
j>0
such that
a_{0}\bullet a_{j}\neq 1
.
Our definition of badness uses the ordering of the vertices, and hence does not make sense in
\mathcal{B}(M)
. This is the reason we work with
B(M)^{ord}
rather than
\mathcal{B}(M)
, even though
\mathcal{D}(M)
can also be considered as a subcomplex of the simplicial complex
\mathcal{B}(M)
.

Our badness condition has the property that

(1)

Any simplex of
\mathcal{B}(M)^{ord}\setminus\mathcal{D}(M)
has a bad face;

(2)

If two faces of a simplex are bad, their join is also bad.

In
[
7
]
, for a simplex
\sigma
of the larger complex,
\mathcal{B}^{ord}(M)
in our case, they call a simplex
\tau
in its link
good for
\sigma
if any face of
\tau*\sigma
that is bad is a face of
\sigma
. In our case, we see that the simplices that are good for
\sigma
will necessarily be part of the “down-link”
\link_{<\sigma}
, the simplices
\tau
in the link such that
\tau*\sigma
is a simplex, with the vertices of
\tau
placed before those of
\sigma
in the ordering. In fact the subcomplex
G_{\sigma}
of simplices that are good for
\sigma
identifies with the complex
\mathcal{D}_{<\sigma}(M)
, that we showed in Lemma
3.15
to be isomorphic to
\mathcal{D}(M\setminus\sigma)
.

Following
[
7
, Prop 2.1]
, keeping in addition track of the ordering of the vertices, we can apply the bad simplex argument to show that
\mathcal{D}(M)
is
n
-connected, deforming any disc of dimension
k+1\leq n+1
, if we can show that
G_{\sigma}
is
(n-p)
–connected for each bad
p
-simplex
\sigma
. Now this is done by induction, treating the first case by hand, and using our genus estimate for
M\setminus\sigma
. In our case,
n=\frac{g(M)-c}{3}
, where
c
is a constant, and Lemma
3.13
says that
g(M\setminus\sigma)\geq g(M)-(2p+1)
. The needed connectivity of
G_{\sigma}
follows from the fact that
\frac{g(M)-(2p+1)-c}{3}\geq\frac{g(M)-3p-c}{3}=n-p
for any
p\geq 1
, which is enough since there are no bad 0-simplices.

4.
Cancellation properties and destabilization complex

In order to get the final homological stability results we need to compare the disordered arc complex to the canonically defined destabilization complex in
[
15
]
. This comparison will use a cancellation property in the category
\mathcal{F}_{\partial}
, that we start by describing.

4.1.
Cancellation

In this section we prove a general cancellation result based on the dual unitary stable rank, with a proof using the connectivity of the disordered arc complex
\mathcal{D}
. In Appendix
B
, we prove a stronger cancellation result in the case when the base ring
R
is a PID, using instead a classification result for formed spaces with boundary that is only valid for PIDs, see Theorems
B.1
and
B.3
.

Theorem 4.1

(General cancellation)
.

Let
(M_{i},\lambda_{i},\partial_{i})\in\mathcal{F}_{\partial}
with
\partial_{i}
surjective for
i=1,2
and such that

(M_{1},\lambda_{1},\partial_{1})\#X\cong(M_{2},\lambda_{2},\partial_{2})\#X.

If
g_{X}(M_{1},\lambda_{1},\partial_{1})\geq 2usr(R)+5
then
(M_{1},\lambda_{1},\partial_{1})\cong(M_{2},\lambda_{2},\partial_{2}).

Proof.

Let
(M,\lambda,\partial)=(M_{1},\lambda_{1},\partial_{1})\#X
and let
a_{1},a_{2}
denote the canonical generators of the
X
-summands under the identification

(M,\lambda,\partial)=(M_{1},\lambda_{1},\partial_{1})\#X\cong(M_{2},\lambda_{2},\partial_{2})\#X,

where we have fixed an isomorphism as in the statement. For
i=1,2
we have that
(M_{i},\lambda_{i},\partial_{i})=(M\setminus a_{i},\lambda|_{M\setminus a_{i}},\partial|_{M\setminus a_{i}})
by Lemma
2.14
(ii), and our assumption on
\partial_{i}

is equivalent to saying that the arcs
a_{1},a_{2}
are non-separating.
The above cancellation property is thus equivalent to the fact that
\operatorname{Aut}_{\mathcal{F}_{\partial}}(M,\lambda,\partial)
acts transitively on the set of non-separating arcs in
(M,\lambda,\partial)
.

We will now show that transitivity of the action follows from the connectivity of the complex
\mathcal{D}(M,\lambda,\partial)
, which holds by Theorem
3.16
since
g_{X}(M,\lambda,\partial)\geq 2(usr(R)+1)+4
by our assumption.

Observe that the set of vertices of
\mathcal{D}(M,\lambda,\partial)
is precisely the set of non-separating arcs. Thus it suffices to show that if
a_{1},a_{2}
are connected by a sequences of edges in
\mathcal{D}(M,\lambda,\partial)
, then there is an automorphism in
\operatorname{Aut}_{\mathcal{F}_{\partial}}(M,\lambda,\partial)
taking
a_{1}
to
a_{2}
.
Without loss of generality assume that
(a_{1},a_{2})
is a
1
-simplex in
\mathcal{D}(M,\lambda,\partial)
. Then it defines a morphism
(a_{1},a_{2}):X^{\#2}\to(M,\lambda,\partial)
, and by Lemma
2.14
(i)
we have a splitting
(M,\lambda,\partial)=(M^{\prime},\lambda^{\prime},\partial^{\prime})\#X^{\#2}
where
M^{\prime}=M\setminus(a_{1},a_{2})
denotes the cut form.
Now the braiding
\beta_{1,1}^{-1}
on the last two coordinates defines such a morphism, finishing the proof.
∎

In the language of
[
15
]
, the above theorem says that the category
\mathcal{F}_{\partial}
satisfies
local cancellation
, see Definition 1.9 in that paper.

Remark 4.2
.

One could define the
cancellation stable rank

csr(A,R)
of an
R
-module
A
to be the smallest integer
k
with the property that for any
N\in\mathcal{F}_{\partial}

N\#X^{\#p+1}\cong A\#X^{\#n}\ \ \textrm{with}\ \ g_{X}(N)>0\ \ \textrm{and}\ \ 0\leq p\leq n-k\ \ \Longrightarrow\ \ N\cong A\#X^{\#n-p-1}.

In this language, Theorem
4.1
says that
csr(A,R)\leq 2usr(R)+6
for any
R
-module
A
. And Theorem
B.1
, proved in the appendix, improves the above bound to
csr(A,R)=2
when
R
is a PID.

Note that the connectivity of the destabilization complex is always related to a local cancellation property, see
[
15
, Lem 2.3]
. But here we do not know yet that the complex of disordered arc is (essentially) the destabilization complex in the sense of
[
15
]
, and in fact we will use the cancellation result to show the equivalence between the two complexes.

4.2.
The destabilization complex

The monoidal category
\mathcal{F}_{\partial}
of formed spaces with boundary introduced in Section
2
has a subcategory
\mathcal{F}_{\partial,X}
, generated by the object
X=(D^{2},0,\id)
, that is braided monoidal by Proposition
2.9
. The monoidal structure of the full category
\mathcal{F}_{\partial}
then makes the classifying space
B\mathcal{F}_{\partial}
into an

E_{1}
-module over the
E_{2}
-algebra
B\mathcal{F}_{\partial,X}
in the language of
[
9
]
, see in particular Section 7 of that paper that gives this particular generalization of the set-up of
[
15
]
needed here.

Given a choice of an object
A
of
\mathcal{F}_{\partial}
, there is a
destabilization complex

W_{n}(A,X)
associated to stabilization by
X
, whose connectivity, under mild conditions, rules homological stability for adding copies of
X
. We describe the space (semi-simplicial set)
W_{n}(A,X)
below, and relate it to the disordered arc complex
\mathcal{D}(A\#X^{\#n})
. Before doing so, we will modify the category
\mathcal{F}_{\partial}
slightly, as in
[
15
, Rem 1.1]
, to force a stronger cancellation property than the one given by Theorems
B.1
and
4.1
, as the stronger cancellation property is needed to be able to deduce homological stability from the connectivity of
W_{n}(A,X)
. This will have the effect of modifying
W_{n}(A,X)
so that it is the correct complex of destabilization.

Define
\mathcal{F}_{\partial}^{c}(A,X)
to be the category with one object
N_{k}:=A\#X^{\#k}
for each
k\geq 0
, and with the automorphisms groups
\operatorname{Aut}_{\mathcal{F}_{\partial}}(N_{k})
as only morphisms. Now
B\mathcal{F}_{\partial}^{c}(A,X)
is again an
E_{1}
-module over the
E_{2}
–algebra
B\mathcal{F}_{\partial,X}
via the sum
\#
, and it satisfies local cancellation by construction: For all
0\leq p\leq n-1
,

N_{k}\#X^{\#p+1}\cong A\#X^{\#n}\Longrightarrow\ \ N_{k}\cong A\#X^{\#n-p-1},\ \textrm{and in particular }k=n-p-1.

We are now ready to define the destabilization complex.

Definition 4.3
.

[
9
, Def 7.5]

Let
A
be a formed space with boundary, and let
n\geq 1
. The destabilization complex
W_{n}(A,X)
is a semi-simplicial set with set of
p
–simplices

W_{n}(A,X)_{p}=\{(N_{k},f)\ |\ N_{k}=A\#X^{\#k}\textrm{ and }f:N_{k}\#X^{\#p+1}\xrightarrow{\cong}A\#X^{\#n}\ \textrm{in}\ \mathcal{F}_{\partial}^{c}(A,X)\}/_{\sim}

where
k=n-p-1
is necessary for the isomorphism to exist as we just saw, and
(N_{k},f)\sim(N_{k},f^{\prime})
if
f^{\prime}=f\circ(g\#\id)
for some
g\in\operatorname{Aut}_{\mathcal{F}_{\partial}}(N_{k})
. (The data of the
complement

N_{k}
is actually superfluous her, since
N_{k}=N_{n-p-1}
is the only possibility.)

The
i
th face map is obtained by adding a copy of
X
to the complement
N_{k}
and precomposing
f
with an appropriate braiding to get a new pair
d_{i}(N_{k},f)=(N_{k}\#X,d_{i}f)=(N_{k+1},d_{i}f)
with

d_{i}f:N_{k}\#X\#X^{\#p}\xrightarrow{\id\#\beta_{X^{\#i},X}^{-1}\#\id}N_{k}\#X^{\#i}\#X\#X^{\#p-i}\xrightarrow{\ f\ }A\#X^{\#n}.

There is a simplicial action of
\operatorname{Aut}(A\#X^{\#n})
on
W_{n}(A,X)
by postcomposition.

Note that the action of
\operatorname{Aut}(A\#X^{\#n})
on
W_{n}(A,X)
is transitive on the set of
p
-simplices for all
p
by construction. (This is in fact equivalent to the local cancellation property enforced on the category, see
[
15
, Thm 1.10(a)]
). Hence we have an identification

W_{n}(A,X)_{p}\cong\operatorname{Aut}(A\#X^{\#n})/\operatorname{Aut}(A\#X^{\#n-p-1}).

4.3.
Disordered arcs as a destabilization complex

Suppose
(M,\lambda,\partial)\in\mathcal{F}_{\partial}
has genus
g_{X}(M,\lambda,\partial)=n
, so that we can write
M=A\#X^{\#n}
for some
A\in\mathcal{F}_{\partial}
.
We will now relate the disordered algebraic arc complex
\mathcal{D}(M,\lambda,\partial)
and the destabilization complex
W_{n}(A,X)
just defined.

Recall that the vertices of simplices of
\mathcal{D}(M,\lambda,\partial)
are canonically ordered when
\operatorname{char}(R)\neq 2
. Hence
\mathcal{D}(M,\lambda,\partial)
can be considered as a semi-simplicial set in that case, the
i
th face a simplex
\sigma=(a_{0},\dots,a_{p})
being the simplex
d_{i}\sigma
obtained from
\sigma
by removing its
(i+1)
st vertex
a_{i}
. In the characteristic 2 case,
\mathcal{D}(M,\lambda,\partial)
cannot be identified with a semi-simplicial set, and we will instead compare
\mathcal{D}(M,\lambda,\partial)
to the simplicial complex
S_{n}(A,X)
associated to
W_{n}(A,X)
. The complex
S_{n}(A,X)
has the same vertices as
W_{n}(A,X)
, and vertices
v_{0},\dots,v_{p}
form a simplex in
S_{n}(A,X)
if there exists a
p
-simplex of
W_{n}(A,X)
having
v_{0},\dots,v_{p}
as its vertices. By
[
15
, Thm 2.10]
, under good conditions, the simplicial complex
S_{n}(A,X)
is highly connected if and only if the semi-simplicial set
W_{n}(A,X)
is highly connected, which implies that it is most often equivalent to work with the one or the other for the purpose of homological stability. (In this case, we will indeed be in the situation where
W_{n}(A,X)
has
(p+1)!
simplices for each
p
-simplex of
S_{n}(A,X)
, having one for each ordering of the vertices, and we could equivalently compare
\mathcal{D}^{ord}(M,\lambda,\partial)
with
W_{n}(A,X)
.)

Proposition 4.4
.

Let
(M,\lambda,\partial)\!\cong\!A\#X^{\#n}
be a formed space with boundary with
g_{X}(M,\lambda,\partial)\!\geq\!n
.

(i)

If
\sigma\in\mathcal{D}(M,\lambda,\partial)
is a
p

M\cong M\backslash\sigma\,\#\,X^{\#p+1}
.

(ii)

p
–simplices of
\mathcal{D}(M,\lambda,\partial)
are in one-to-one correspondence with maps
f:X^{\#p+1}\to(M,\lambda,\partial)
such that
\partial
is still surjective on the cut form
M\backslash\operatorname{Im}(f)
.

(iii)

If
\operatorname{char}(R)\neq 2
, then

sk_{\leq n-2usr(R)-6}\mathcal{D}(M,\lambda,\partial)\cong sk_{\leq n-2usr(R)-6}(W_{n}(A,X)).

.

(iv)

If
\operatorname{char}(R)=2
, then

sk_{\leq n-2usr(R)-6}\mathcal{D}(M,\lambda,\partial)\cong sk_{\leq n-2usr(R)-6}(S_{n}(A,X)).

When
R

(n-2)

We could have changed the definition of
\mathcal{D}

Proof.

Without loss of generality, we can assume that
M=A\#X^{\#n}
.

Part(i):
A
p

Appendix A
Connectivity of the complexes of unimodular sequences

In this section we will prove Theorem
3.7
, which gives a bound on the connectivity of the poset of unimodular sequences constrained to an affine subspace.
The main ingredient of the proof is a generalisation of a result of van der Kallen in
[
22
]
, combined with further generalisations by Friedrich in
[
4
]
and the first author in
[
18
]
.

As in
[
22
,
4
]
,
for a set

we let
\mathcal{O}(X)
denote the poset of finite non-empty sequences of elements of
X
, ordered by refinement.

We say that
F\subset\mathcal{O}(X)
satisfies the
chain condition
if it is closed under taking subsequences.
For
(v_{1},\cdots,v_{n})\in F
we let

denote the poset of sequences
(w_{1},\cdots,w_{m})\in\mathcal{O}(X)
such that
(w_{1},\cdots,w_{m},v_{1},\cdots,v_{n})\in F
.

If
M
is an

-module, the poset
\mathbb{U}(M)
identifies with the subposet of sequences in
\mathcal{O}(M)
which are unimodular.
And for

a submodule and

an element, we have

\mathbb{U}(M,l+N)=\mathcal{O}(l+N)\cap\mathbb{U}(M)

is the subposet of
\mathbb{U}(M)
of unimodular sequences in

of elements of the form

with

.

The proof of Theorem
3.7
uses the following lemma from
[

]
, which we state here for convenience.

Lemma A.1
.

[
22
, Lem 2.13]

Suppose

satisfies the chain condition.
Let

be a subset.

(1)

If
O(X)\cap F
is
d
-connected, and
\mathcal{O}(X)\cap F_{(v_{1},\cdots,v_{m})}
is
(d-m)
-connected for all sequences
(v_{1},\cdots,v_{m})\in F\setminus\mathcal{O}(X)

then

is
d
-connected.

(2)

If
\mathcal{O}(X)\cap F_{(v_{1},\cdots,v_{m})}

(d+1-m)
-connected for all sequences
(v_{1},\cdots,v_{m})\in F\setminus\mathcal{O}(X)

with

is
(d+1)
-connected.

It will be convenient for the sake of the proof of the theorem to work in

, so we always have additional unimodular vectors at hand, and we will consider posets of the form

an affine subspace of

The proof follows closely
[

]

]

Recall that

\mathbf{r}(M,N):=\max\{\rk(L):L\subset N\text{ is a direct summand of }M\}.

.

Let

denote the first standard generator in

. We will prove the result by induction on

(b)

Without loss of generality we can assume that

Appendix B
Cancellation and classification of formed spaces with boundary For PIDs

The goal of this appendix is to improve the bounds of the cancellation Theorem
4.1
when the ring
R
is a PID.
The proof uses a classification of the objects of
\mathcal{F}_{\partial}
that holds when
R
is a PID. We start by stating the cancellation theorem, and then give the classification result.

Theorem B.1

(Cancellation for PIDs)
.

Let
R
be a PID and
(M_{i},\lambda_{i},\partial_{i})\in\mathcal{F}_{\partial}
with

\partial_{i}
surjective for
i=1,2
and such that

(M_{1},\lambda_{1},\partial_{1})\#X\cong(M_{2},\lambda_{2},\partial_{2})\#X.

Then
(M_{1},\lambda_{1},\partial_{1})\cong(M_{2},\lambda_{2},\partial_{2})
.

Note that the assumption that both
\partial_{i}
are surjective, and hence both
(M_{i},\lambda_{i},\partial_{i})
have positive arc genus, is needed. Indeed,
Proposition
2.16
gives a decomposition
X^{\#2g+1}\cong(\HH^{g},0)\#X
but
X^{\#2g}\ncong(\HH^{g},0)
.

The classification result used in the proof of the above theorem will be given in terms of the following invariant:

Definition B.2
.

Let
R
be a PID and let
(M,\lambda,\partial)
be a formed space with boundary with
M\neq 0
. Its
form data
is the tuple

\mathbb{D}(M,\lambda,\partial)=(n,l,d_{1},\dots,d_{k},\delta_{1},\dots,\delta_{k+1})

where

(i)

n=\rk(M)
is the rank of
M
;

(ii)

(l,d_{1},\dots,d_{k})
is the Smith normal form of
\lambda
, representing the form

diag(d_{1},d_{1},\dots,d_{k},d_{k},0,\dots,0)

with
l=n-2k\geq 0
zeroes;

(iii)

for
1\leq i\leq k
,

(\delta_{i})=\partial(M_{i})
, where
M_{i}=\{m\in M:\forall m^{\prime}\in M\;d_{i}|\lambda(m,m^{\prime})\}
.

(iv)

(\delta_{k+1})=\partial(\operatorname{Rad}(M,\lambda))
, where
\operatorname{Rad}(M,\lambda)=\{m\in M:\forall m^{\prime}\in M\;\lambda(m,m^{\prime})=0\}
;

As already used in Lemma
2.24
, the fact that the Smith normal form of
\lambda
has the above form follows from
[
14
, Theorem IV.I]
, where
d_{1}|d_{2}|\dots|d_{k}
are uniquely determined up to scaling them by units in
R
.
The above form data adds to the Smith normal form the data of the ideals of
R
given by applying
\partial
to the subspaces
M_{i}
determined by
\lambda
.

The
\delta_{i}
’s are likewise well-defined up to scaling by a unit, and
we have
\delta_{i-1}|\delta_{i}|\frac{d_{i}}{d_{{i-1}}}\delta_{i-1}
for
i\geq 2
because
\frac{d_{i}}{d_{{i-1}}}M_{i-1}\subset M_{i}\subset M_{i-1}
. Setting
M_{k+1}=\operatorname{Rad}(M,\lambda)
, we see that the relation still hold for
i=k+1
.

We say that two tuples
(n,l,d_{1},\dots,d_{k},\delta_{1},\dots,\delta_{k+1})
and
(n^{\prime},l^{\prime},d_{1}^{\prime},\dots,d_{k}^{\prime},\delta_{1}^{\prime},\dots,\delta_{k+1}^{\prime})
are
equivalent
if
n=n^{\prime}
,
l=l^{\prime}
and there are units

such that

and
\delta_{i}=v_{i}\delta_{i}^{\prime}
.
The following result gives a classification of all the objects in
\mathcal{F}_{\partial}
:

(Classification of formed spaces with boundary over a PID)
.

If
R
is a PID then the isomorphism class of a formed space
(M,\lambda,\partial)\in\mathcal{F}_{\partial}
with
M\neq 0
is determined by its equivalence class of form data
\mathbb{D}(M,\lambda,\partial)

Moreover, any form data
(n,l,d_{1},\dots,d_{k},\delta_{1},\dots,\delta_{k+1})
satisfying the relations

d_{1}|d_{2}|\dots|d_{k}
and
\delta_{i-1}|\delta_{i}|\frac{d_{i}}{d_{{i-1}}}\delta_{i-1}
for

(n,l,d_{1},\dots,d_{k},\delta_{1},\dots,\delta_{k+1})
is a tuple satisfying the above relations. We start by constructing a

realizing it:

. Writing the standard basis of

\zeta\in\operatorname{Aut}(d_{i}\!\HH\oplus d_{j}\!\HH)
defined by

\displaystyle\zeta(e_{i})=e_{i},\ \ \zeta(f_{i})=f_{i}+e_{j},\ \ \zeta(e_{j})=e_{j},\ \ \zeta(f_{j})=f_{j}+\frac{d_{j}}{d_{i}}e_{i},

which gives a new basis with the property that now

\partial\zeta(f_{i})=\partial e_{j}

and
\partial\zeta(f_{j})=\frac{d_{j}}{d_{i}}\partial e_{i}
.
Applying step 2 to the
i
th and
j
th summand then gives a new basis
(e_{i}^{\prime},f_{i}^{\prime},e_{j}^{\prime},f_{j}^{\prime})
such that
\partial e_{i}^{\prime}
generates the ideal
(\partial e_{i},\partial e_{j})
and
\partial e_{j}^{\prime}
generates the ideal
(\partial e_{j},\frac{d_{j}}{d_{i}}\partial e_{i})
, while
\partial f_{i}^{\prime}=0
,
\partial f_{j}^{\prime}=0
. This gives the desired congruences since
\partial e_{i}^{\prime}
then divides both
\partial e_{j}
and
\frac{d_{j}}{d_{i}}\partial e_{i}
, so that
\partial e_{i}^{\prime}|\partial e_{j}^{\prime}
and
\partial e_{j}^{\prime}
divides
\frac{d_{j}}{d_{i}}\partial e_{i}
and
\frac{d_{j}}{d_{i}}\partial e_{j}
, and hence also
\frac{d_{j}}{d_{i}}\partial e_{i}^{\prime}
.

Observe that this special transformation changes the spans of both
e_{i},f_{i}
and
e_{j},f_{j}
but, as it is an automorphism of
(M,\lambda)
, the submodules
M_{1},\dots,M_{k},\operatorname{Rad}(M,\lambda)
are unchanged.

Step 5
: This step inductively uses step 4 to ensure that
\partial e_{m}=\delta_{m}
for each
m=1,\dots,k
.

Assume that for a fixed
1\leq m\leq k
we already have that
\partial e_{j}=\delta_{j}
for
m+1\leq j\leq k
. Then we will modify
e_{m},f_{m}
so that
\partial e_{m}=\delta_{m}
(and
\partial f_{m}=0
).
Here the start of the induction, the case
m=k
, where the assumption holds trivially.

We make the induction step in two parts.

(i)

This step applies an automorphism of
d_{m}\!\HH\oplus d_{m+1}\!\HH
to ensure that
\partial e_{m}|\delta_{m+1}|\dots|\delta_{k+1}
.

If
m=k
, this holds by step 3 that already ensured that
\partial e_{k}|\delta_{k+1}
, so there is nothing to do.
If
m<k
, apply the step 4 transformation with
i=m<m+1=j
.
This changes
(e_{m},f_{m},e_{m+1},f_{m+1})\mapsto(e_{m}^{\prime},f_{m}^{\prime},e_{m+1}^{\prime},f_{m+1}^{\prime})
.
We firstly claim that we sill have
\partial e_{m+1}^{\prime}=\delta_{m+1}
, up to a unit: this is because
\partial e_{m+1}^{\prime}
generates the ideal
(\partial e_{m+1},\frac{d_{m+1}}{d_{m}}\partial e_{m})
and
\partial e_{m+1}=\delta_{m+1}
by induction hypothesis and
\frac{d_{m+1}}{d_{m}}e_{m}\in M_{m+1}
by definition so
\delta_{m+1}|\frac{d_{m+1}}{d_{m}}\partial e_{m}
.
Secondly,
\partial e_{m}^{\prime}
generates the ideal
(\partial e_{m},\delta_{m+1})
so in particular
\partial e_{m}^{\prime}|\delta_{m+1}
. Since
\delta_{m+1}|\dots|\delta_{k+1}
by construction then we are done.

(ii)

This step applies an automorphism of
d_{1}\!\HH\oplus\dots\oplus d_{m}\!\HH
to ensure that

\partial e_{m}|\frac{d_{m}}{d_{i}}\partial e_{i}
for each
1\leq i<m
.

Apply Step 4 inductively for each pair
(i,j)
with
1\leq i<m=j
. This replaces
(e_{i},f_{i},e_{m},f_{m})
with
(e_{i}^{\prime},f_{i}^{\prime},e_{m}^{\prime},f_{m}^{\prime})
satisfying in particular that
\partial e_{m}^{\prime}|\frac{d_{m}}{d_{i}}\partial e_{i}^{\prime}
. Note that the modified
\partial e_{m}^{\prime}
generates the ideal
(\partial e_{m},\frac{d_{m}}{d_{i}}\partial e_{i})
. In particular it divides
\partial e_{m}
and so the congruences obtained in part (i) still hold, and likewise for the congruences
\partial e_{m}|\frac{d_{m}}{d_{i}^{\prime}}\partial e_{i^{\prime}}
for
i^{\prime}<i
, for the same reason.

We are left to check that the above procedure does indeed force the equality
\partial e_{m}=\delta_{m}
, finishing the induction step.
By definition
\delta_{m}
generates the ideal
\partial(M_{m})
and
M_{m}
has a basis given by

\Big\{\frac{d_{m}}{d_{1}}e_{1},\frac{d_{m}}{d_{1}}f_{1},\frac{d_{m}}{d_{2}}e_{2},\frac{d_{m}}{d_{2}}f_{2},\dots,e_{m},f_{m},e_{m+1},f_{m+1},\dots,e_{k},f_{k},g_{1},\dots,g_{l}\Big\}.

By the above steps we have that
\partial e_{m}|\frac{d_{m}}{d_{i}}\partial e_{i}
for
1\leq i\leq m-1
and
\partial e_{m}|\partial e_{m+1}|\cdots|\partial g_{1}=\dots=\partial g_{l}
, giving (up to a unit) the equality
\partial e_{m}=\delta_{m}
. This finishes the proof.

∎

Proof of Theorem
B.1
.

By the surjectivity assumption on
\partial_{i}
,
we have
(M_{i},\lambda_{i},\partial_{i})\cong(M^{\prime}_{i},\lambda^{\prime}_{i},\partial^{\prime}_{i})\#X
.
Adding
\#X^{\#2}
to each side and using that
X^{\#3}\cong X\#\HH
(Proposition
2.16
), we get isomorphisms

(M_{1},\lambda_{1},\partial_{1})\#\HH\cong(M_{1},\lambda_{1},\partial_{1})\#X^{\#2}\cong(M_{2},\lambda_{2},\partial_{2})\#X^{\#2}\cong(M_{2},\lambda_{2},\partial_{2})\#\HH.

Thus, it suffices to study of cancellation by
\HH
instead.
Now, the effect of
-\#\HH
on the form data invariants is as follows:
let

\mathbb{D}(M,\lambda,\partial)=(n,l,d_{1},\dots,d_{k},\delta_{1},\delta_{2},\dots,\delta_{k+1})

be the original form data. Then,

\mathbb{D}((M,\lambda,\partial)\#\HH)=(n+2,l,d^{\prime}_{1},d_{2}^{\prime},\dots,d_{k+1}^{\prime},\delta_{1}^{\prime},\delta_{2}^{\prime},\dots,\delta_{k+2}^{\prime})

where
(d^{\prime}_{1},\delta^{\prime}_{1})=(1,\delta_{1})
and
(d_{i}^{\prime},\delta^{\prime}_{i})=(d_{i-1},\delta_{i-1})
for
2\leq i\leq k+1
, because
\partial
vanishes on
\HH
.
In particular, the form data invariants of
(M,\lambda,\partial)
are determined by those of
(M,\lambda,\partial)\#\HH
, which implies cancellation.
∎
