On the quantum chromatic gap
Lorenzo Ciardo

Abstract
The largest known gap between quantum and classical chromatic number of graphs, obtained via quantum protocols for colouring Hadamard graphs based on the Deutsch–Jozsa algorithm and the quantum Fourier transform, is exponential. We put forth a quantum pseudo-telepathy version of Khot’s d d -to- 1 1 Games Conjecture and prove that, conditional to its validity, the gap is unbounded: There exist graphs whose quantum chromatic number is 3 and whose classical chromatic number is arbitrarily large. Furthermore, we show that the existence of a certain form of pseudo-telepathic XOR games would imply the conjecture and, thus, the unboundedness of the quantum chromatic gap. As two technical steps of our proof that might be of independent interest, we establish a quantum adjunction theorem for Pultr functors between categories of relational structures, and we prove that the Dinur–Khot–Kindler–Minzer–Safra reduction, recently used for proving the 2 2 -to- 2 2 Games Theorem, is quantum complete.



1
Introduction

Take a graph
\mathbf{G}
and
n
distinct colours. Try to assign a colour to each of the vertices of
\mathbf{G}
in a way that no edge is monochromatic. What is the minimum
n
required for completing the task? The answer to this question is a classic graph-theoretic parameter, studied from the very beginning of the history of graph theory: the chromatic number of
\mathbf{G}
(in symbols,
\chi(\mathbf{G})
).

Consider now a one-round game involving two cooperating, non-communicating, computationally unbounded players (Alice and Bob) and a verifier. The goal of Alice and Bob is to convince the verifier that
\mathbf{G}
can be coloured using
n
colours. The verifier questions the players by selecting two vertices
v_{A}
and
v_{B}
of
\mathbf{G}
at random and sending
v_{A}
to Alice and
v_{B}
to Bob. Alice replies with a colour
c_{A}
, and Bob replies with a colour
c_{B}
. The game is won if the answers are consistent with each other (i.e.,
c_{A}=c_{B}
when
v_{A}=v_{B}
) and compatible with a proper colouring of
\mathbf{G}
(i.e.,
c_{A}\neq c_{B}
when
v_{A}
and
v_{B}
are adjacent). What is the minimum
n
that allows Alice and Bob to win the colouring game, no matter which questions the verifier asks? A moment of thought reveals that the answer is again
\chi(\mathbf{G})
: If
\mathbf{G}
is
n
-colourable (i.e., if
n\geq\chi(\mathbf{G})
), a winning strategy for Alice and Bob can be obtained by choosing a colouring before the game start, and always replying according to that colouring. If
\mathbf{G}
is not
n
-colourable (i.e., if
n<\chi(\mathbf{G})
), any strategy can be broken by some pair of questions chosen by the verifier.

Quantum pseudo-telepathy

Describing the chromatic number in the language of multiplayer games / multiprover interactive proofs has the advantage of turning the original, purely combinatorial question into a question about information—in particular, about the limits imposed by the physical model we choose for describing information transfer. At a high level, the reason why Alice and Bob cannot find a perfect strategy using fewer colours than
\chi(\mathbf{G})
is that the lack of communication in the game sets strong restrictions on the possible correlations between the players’ answers. To make these restrictions explicit, imagine to build an experimental setup for the colouring game.
Before the game starts, Alice and Bob are free to communicate, which means that they have complete access to a shared amount of information, call it
\mathcal{I}
.
When the game starts, to enforce no communication, we make sure that the distance separating them is big enough (and the questions and replies are sent simultaneously in at least one reference frame). This means that now Alice has only access to one part
\mathcal{I}_{A}
of
\mathcal{I}
, and Bob has only access to the other part
\mathcal{I}_{B}
.
Upon receiving question
v_{A}
, Alice aims to read her answer
c_{A}
from
\mathcal{I}_{A}
; physically, this can be expressed by Alice
measuring
the state of
\mathcal{I}_{A}
via some measurement depending on
v_{A}
. Bob does the same: He measures
\mathcal{I}_{B}
according to the question
v_{B}
, and sends the measurement outcome
c_{B}
to the verifier.
This setup precisely captures the intended rules of the colouring game in a theory of physics that is
realistic
(the outcome of a measurement is completely determined by the description of the measurement and the physical properties of the system being measured) and
local
(Alice’s choice of measurement does not impact Bob’s outcome, and vice versa).

Yet, if we were to actually implement the experiment, we might be surprised at finding that, for some choices of
\mathbf{G}
, Alice and Bob are able to convince the verifier that
\mathbf{G}
can be coloured with
strictly fewer
colours than
\chi(\mathbf{G})
, thus effectively violating the restrictions imposed by local realism. Indeed, by

(i)
encoding the shared information
\mathcal{I}
in a bipartite quantum system,
(ii)
preparing the system in a suitably entangled state, and
(iii)
performing on each of the two parts of the system carefully chosen positive-operator valued (or, without loss of generality, projection valued) measurements, the players’ answers can exhibit non-classical correlations that allow them to win with certainty the classically unwinnable game. This phenomenon is at the heart of Bell’s Theorem
[
Bel64
]
on the incompatibility of quantum theory with local hidden-variable theories, and it became known as
quantum pseudo-telepathy

[
BBT05
,
BCT99
]
. In fact, it is an example of a so-called
proof of Bell’s Theorem without inequalities
(other famous such examples being the three-player GHZ game
[
GHZ89
,
GHSZ90
]
and the two-player Mermin “magic-square” game
[
Mer90
,
Mer93
,
Per90
]
), in that the best quantum strategy not only outperforms all classical strategies, but it also allows the players to win with certainty—unlike other examples of violations of Bell’s inequality such as the CHSH game
[
CHSH69
]
.

Correspondences

Let
\chiq(\mathbf{G})
be the minimum number
n
of colours required for two entangled players to win the colouring game on
\mathbf{G}
with certainty; in
[
CMN+07
]
, this parameter was named the
quantum chromatic number
of
\mathbf{G}
. Since quantum strategies are at least as powerful as classical strategies, it is clear that the inequality
\chiq(\mathbf{G})\leq\chi(\mathbf{G})
holds for each
\mathbf{G}
.
The problem of understanding the maximum possible gap between quantum and classical chromatic number of graphs was already considered in
[
BCW98
,
BCT99
,
DW01
,
BCWDW01
]
.
The first example of a graph for which the inequality is strict was exhibited in
[
BCT99
]
, with the description of a winning quantum strategy for colouring the vertices of certain Hadamard graphs based on the Deutsch–Jozsa
protocol
[
DJ92
]
using fewer colours than the classical chromatic number, see also
[
BCW98
]
. The result was later extended to all Hadamard graphs in
[
AHKS06
]
, via a different protocol based on the quantum Fourier transform
[
Cop02
,
MZ04
]
. In particular, in combination with the known asymptotic behaviour of the classical chromatic number of Hadamard graphs
[
FR87
]
, those results imply the existence of a class of graphs exhibiting an
exponential
gap between quantum and classical chromatic number; i.e.,
\chi=\Omega(2^{\chi_{q}})
.
Another line of work focussed on producing smaller and smaller examples of graphs for which
\chi_{\operatorname{q}}<\chi
. The number of vertices of the smallest explicit instance of a graph whose quantum and classical chromatic numbers differ was progressively decreased from 32768
[
GTW03
]
, to 1609
[
AHKS06
]
, to 18
[
CMN+07
]
, to 14
[
MR16a
]
(see also related work in
[
SS11
,
EW19
,
Lal23
,
GS24
,
AJ25
]
).
The perfect quantum strategies for those graphs are mostly based on real
or complex orthogonal representations of graphs, with the notable exception of
[
MR16a
]
.
Despite extensive work on this problem, it is currently open whether the
\chi_{\operatorname{q}}
vs.
\chi
gap can be larger than exponential.

Let us now set aside the question on the quantum chromatic gap for a moment, and turn to a problem from classical complexity theory. Take a graph
\mathbf{G}
and
n
distinct colours. What is the computational complexity of finding an explicit
n
-colouring of
\mathbf{G}
, assuming that one exists? Unless
n\leq 2
, in which case a simple polynomial-time algorithm works,
n
-colouring
is a classic NP-hard computational problem, which already appeared
on Karp’s original list
[
Kar72
]
. It is then natural to consider the following approximate version of it: Assuming that
\mathbf{G}
is
n
-colourable, find an explicit
n^{\prime}
-colouring, for some
n^{\prime}>n
. A variant of this problem was already considered in
[
GJ76
]
, where NP-hardness was conjectured to hold for every
3\leq n\leq n^{\prime}
. Despite decades of attempts, the complexity of the
approximate graph colouring
problem (
AGC
) is still wide open in general. For example, the case

n=3
,
n^{\prime}=4
was only proved to be NP-hard in
[
KLS93
]
, see also
[
GK04
]
for a simpler proof. More generally,
[
KLS00
]
showed hardness of the case

n^{\prime}=n+2\lfloor n/3\rfloor-1
. This was improved to
n^{\prime}=2n-2
in
[
BG16
]
, and recently to
n^{\prime}=2n-1
in
[
BBKO21
]
. In particular, this last result implies
hardness of the case
n=3
,
n^{\prime}=5
—while even the complexity of the case
n=3
,
n^{\prime}=6
is
still open. The asymptotic state-of-the-art hardness is
exponential
: Building on
[
Kho01
,
Hua13
]
, NP-hardness of the case
n^{\prime}={n\choose\lfloor n/2\rfloor}-1
was established in
[
KOWŽ23
]
.

CSP approximation

Graph colouring is an example of
constraint satisfaction problem
(CSP)—i.e., the problem of assigning one of a given set of labels (for example, colours) to each of a given set of variables (for example, vertices of a graph) in a way that a list of constraint predicates (for example, “adjacent vertices should be assigned distinct colours”) is satisfied. Other typical examples of CSPs are Boolean satisfiability problems, graph and hypergraph homomorphism problems, and linear equations. The complexity landscape of the
exact
version of CSPs has been explored, at this level of generality, since the foundational work
[
FV98
]
(while specific fragments have been investigated since the dawn of complexity theory, for example
[
Kar72
,
Sch78
,
HN90
]
). In particular, a line of work initiated in
[
Jea98
,
JCG97
]
investigated the complexity of CSPs in relation to
polymorphisms
—invariance properties of the algebraic structures encoding the CSP constraints. This approach eventually culminated with a proof of the CSP Dichotomy Theorem
[
Bul17
,
Zhu17
,
Zhu20
]
, establishing a P vs. NP-hard dichotomy for the whole class of finite-domain CSPs.

In the
approximation
regime, the complexity of CSPs is far less understood than in the exact regime. Many of the known approximation hardness results were derived from the
Probabilistically Checkable Proofs (PCP)
Theorem
[
ALM+98
,
AS98
,
FGL+96
,
Din07
]
.
In particular, Håstad derived from the PCP Theorem a celebrated result on the
hardness of approximating linear equations

[
Hås01
]
: For every constant
\epsilon>0
, it is NP-hard to find an assignment for a system of Boolean equations having, say, three variables per equation (i.e., an instance of
3XOR
) satisfying
\frac{1}{2}+\epsilon
fraction of the equations, even when the system is promised to satisfy
1-\epsilon
fraction of the equations. This inapproximability gap is optimal: On the completeness side, increasing
1-\epsilon
to
1
(i.e., requiring perfect completeness) would make the problem tractable in polynomial time via Gaussian elimination; on the soundness side, assigning a random value to each variable satisfies half of the equations in expectation and, thus, gives a
\frac{1}{2}
-approximation algorithm.

While the PCP Theorem provided optimal inapproximability thresholds for various well-studied problems (in the context of CSPs and beyond), including

Set Cover

[
Fei98
]
,
3SAT

[
Hås01
]
,
Clique

[
Hås96
]
, and the aforementioned
3XOR
, for many other fundamental computational problems the gap between the best known tractability factor and the
best known hardness factor could not be decreased to zero. This was the main motivation behind Khot’s introduction of the

Unique Games Conjecture (UGC)

[
Kho02
]
, postulating that a special type of
label cover
CSPs having
1
-to-
1
(i.e., bijective) constraints are hard to approximate. By exhibiting suitable reductions from
Unique Games
, the precise threshold of approximation hardness for a variety of flagship computational problems in theoretical computer science was found conditional to the truth of the UGC, primary examples being

Max-Cut

[
KKMO07
]
,

Vertex Cover

[
KR08
]
, and
Max Acyclic Subgraph

[
GMR08
]
.
The inherent imperfect
completeness of the UGC makes it unsuitable as a basis for conditional hardness results concerning
AGC
. On the other hand, the
d
-to-
1
version of the conjecture—also introduced in
[
Kho02
]
—has been extensively investigated as a source of hardness for
AGC
. In particular,
[
DMR09
]
showed hardness of
\mathcal{O}(1)
-colouring a
4
-colourable graph under the
2
-to-
1
Conjecture.
In addition, hardness in the
3
vs.
\mathcal{O}(1)
regime and in a
4
vs. superconstant regime was obtained in
[
DMR09
]
and
[
DS10
]
, respectively, conditional to certain non-standard variants of the conjecture.
Later,
[
GS20
]
proved hardness of
3
vs.
\mathcal{O}(1)
colouring under the
d
-to-
1
Conjecture for any fixed
d
.

Recently, a substantial step towards the proof of the UGC was done in the sequence of works
[
KMS17
,
KMS18
,
DKK+18a
,
DKK+18b
]
. Using Håstad’s inapproximability of
3XOR
as the
source of hardness
, the
2
-to-
2
Conjecture (with imperfect completeness) was proved, which immediately yields “half” of the UGC: Given a
(\frac{1}{2}-\epsilon)
-satisfiable instance of
Unique Games
, it is NP-hard to find an
assignment satisfying an
\epsilon
-fraction of the constraints. The result is obtained via an advanced analysis of the expansion properties of Grassman graphs arising from a reduction from
3XOR
.
While the imperfect completeness of the result does not allow deducing from it hardness of
AGC
via
[
DMR09
,
GS20
]
, it yields hardness for a variant of
AGC
where one aims to “almost colour” (i.e., to find a proper colouring for a large subgraph of the instance graph) almost 3-colourable graphs
[
HMS23
]
.

Contributions

We prove that
the gap between quantum and classical chromatic number of graphs is unbounded, conditional to a hypothesis on the pseudo-telepathy of
d
-to-
1
label cover and
3XOR
games
. Our result is obtained by translating certain machinery developed in the context of CSP approximation hardness into the setting of quantum pseudo-telepathy.
For the first part of our result, we consider known pseudo-telepathic non-local games encoding
d
-to-
1
CSPs, and we develop a framework for using them as a
source of pseudo-telepathy
for different games—in particular, for the colouring game. To that end, we prove a quantum version of adjunction for functors between relational structures known as
Pultr functors

[
Pul70
]
. Via this adjunction, we obtain soundness and completeness of a large class of reductions used in the context of CSP approximation—including all gadget reductions as well as reductions that are provably not encoded by gadgets.
In this way, we derive an optimal
3
vs.
\mathcal{O}(1)
quantum chromatic gap conditional to a
strengthened
version of pseudo-telepathy for
d
-to-
1
games, involving measurement scenarios that are
locally compatible
, meaning that the measurements corresponding to bounded-size subinstances of the CSP can be performed simultaneously.
The second part of the result gives a possible avenue for proving strong pseudo-telepathy of
2
-to-
1
games (and, thus, unbounded quantum chromatic gap) by amplifying the pseudo-telepathy of
3XOR
games
[
Mer90
,
Mer93
,
Per90
]
. To do that, we prove that the reduction constructed in
[
KMS17
,
KMS18
,
DKK+18a
,
DKK+18b
]
to establish the
2
-to-
2
Games Theorem is quantum complete. While in the classical approximation-hardness setting the result holds with
imperfect
completeness (as the exact version of
3XOR
, the source of hardness, is solvable in polynomial time), in the quantum pseudo-telepathy setting
perfect
completeness is preserved.

Related work

It was shown in
[
Cia24
]
that the occurrence of pseudo-telepathy in non-local games is governed by the polymorphisms of the corresponding CSPs, thereby connecting pseudo-telepathy to
exact
CSP solvability. The current work extends the connection to the
approximation
regime.
Several recent works have made use of classical CSP reductions to investigate
the computational complexity of the quantum version of classes of CSPs, see e.g.
[
CMS24
,
CM24
,
MS25
]
.
In particular, a reduction from
3
SAT
to
3
-colouring preserving quantum homomorphisms was considered in
[
FILG11
,
Ji13
]
. This was recently generalised in
[
Har24
]
, which described a reduction from arbitrary non-local games to the quantum
3
-colouring game.
These reductions, based on so-called commutativity gadgets, are not sound in the gap regime: Starting from a highly unsatisfiable instance, they do not guarantee high classical chromatic number of the output graph, and thus cannot be used to produce graphs exhibiting a large
\chiq
vs.
\chi
gap.
It was proved in
[
AKS19
]
that gadget reductions for Boolean CSPs preserve the existence of perfect unitary operator assignments. The result was recently extended to the non-Boolean case in
[
BŽ24
]
, where it is mentioned that it does not apply to quantum CSP assignments, as considered in
[
MR16b
,
Cia24
]
(as well as
[
ABdSZ17
]
and the current work), since the latter are defined in terms of idempotent operators (projectors).
It was recently proved in
[
BCKT25
]
that the ratio between classical and quantum chromatic number of a graph is upper bounded by a function that depends only on the dimension of the Hilbert space describing the shared quantum system used in the quantum strategy.
Non-local games exhibiting a large separation between classical and quantum value (often called
strong violations of Bell’s inequality
in the literature) have been investigated in quantum physics, due to their applicability to demonstrate the presence of quantum entanglement, see e.g.
[
WW01
]
.
The existence of infinite families of games whose classical value approaches
0
despite their quantum value being
1
follows from
[
Bel64
,
Raz95
]
. Instances of unique (i.e.,
1
-to-
1
) games with quantum value arbitrarily close to
1
and classical value arbitrarily close
to
0
can be obtained from
[
KRT10
]
in combination with the SDP integrality gaps from
[
KV15
]
. A weaker violation can be similarly obtained for
d
-to-
d
games via
[
KRT10
, Theorem 4.8]
, see also
[
DSV15
]
.

Overview of results and techniques

In this section, we discuss the main results of this paper and highlight the ideas needed for their proof.
Most of the concepts appearing in this section are described here at the intuitive level.
The formal definitions, as well as the complete proofs of the results, are given in the subsequent sections.

2.1
The source of pseudo-telepathy

Traditionally, several hardness of approximation results have been established via the proof composition paradigm introduced by
Arora–Safra
[
AS98
]
. At a high level, this consists in composing an
“outer verifier”—based on a prototypical NP-hard computational problem such as
Gap-3SAT
or
Gap-3XOR
, whose existence is derived from the PCP Theorem—with an “inner verifier”—often based on modifications of the long code,
allowing to transfer approximation hardness through a sequence of transformations of the initial computational problem.
Our first step is to build a suitable source from which we can transfer pseudo-telepathy up to the colouring game. In analogy to the approximation setting, we choose a particular type of constraint satisfaction problem known as
d
-to-
1
label cover as the source of pseudo-telepathy.

Classical setting

An instance
\Phi
of constraint satisfaction problem (CSP) consists of a set of variables, a set of local constraints on tuples of variables, and a set of weights over the constraints. The task is to assign to each variable a value from some fixed alphabet, with the goal of maximising the total weight of satisfied constraints.
The maximum weight of simultaneously satisfiable constraints is the classical value
\operatorname{sat}(\Phi)
of
\Phi
. We say that
\Phi
is a
d
-to-
1
label cover instance for some
d\in\mathbb{N}
if the constraints
(i)
are binary,
(ii)
form a bipartite graph, and
(iii)
are
d
-to-
1
, in the sense that, given a constraint on variables
(x,y)
, for each labelling of
x
there is a unique admitted labelling of
y
and for each labelling of
y
there are
d
admitted labellings of
x
.

Checking whether a
d
-to-
1
instance
\Phi
admits a perfect classical assignment (i.e., checking whether
\operatorname{sat}(\Phi)=1
) is easily seen to be NP-hard unless
d=1
(in which case, the instance is known as a unique game).
Khot famously conjectured that the soundness can be reduced arbitrarily while preserving NP-hardness.

Conjecture 1

(
d
-to-
1
Conjecture,
[
Kho02
]
)
.

Fix
d\geq 2
. For each
\epsilon>0
there exists an integer
n
such that, for a
d
-to-
1
instance
\Phi
on alphabet size
n
, it is NP-hard to decide whether
\operatorname{sat}(\Phi)=1
or
\operatorname{sat}(\Phi)<\epsilon
.

As mentioned in the Introduction, the
d
-to-
1
Conjecture—as well as the Unique Games Conjecture, corresponding to the case
d=1
with imperfect completeness—have been used in the last two decades as the source of several optimal inapproximability results for CSPs.

Quantum setting

We now introduce quantum assignments in the picture.
Just like the colouring game discussed in the Introduction, any CSP instance
\Phi
can be expressed as a two-player one-round game involving non-communicating, cooperating, computationally unbounded players and a verifier. The existence of a perfect classical strategy for the game corresponds to
\Phi
being perfectly satisfiable. However, when the players are allowed to share a bipartite quantum system as part of the strategy and base their replies on measurements of their parts of the system,
the answers can exhibit stronger-than-classical correlations that might give rise to perfect strategies even when
\operatorname{sat}(\Phi)<1
—a phenomenon known as pseudo-telepathy.

In particular, the non-local game corresponding to
d
-to-
1
instances is pseudo-telepathic; in fact, the same holds for the game associated with any NP-hard CSP, as was shown in
[
Cia24
]
.
In analogy with Khot’s Conjecture
1
, we conjecture the existence of
d
-to-
1
games that can be won with certainty by quantum players, but for which the best winning probability for classical players is arbitrarily close to
0
.
Furthermore, we conjecture that the quantum strategy can be taken to be
locally compatible
, in a sense that we now discuss.

As formally described in Section
3
, a quantum strategy corresponds to a collection of measurements indexed by the variables of the instance
\Phi
. Imagine, following
[
HMPS17
, §.7]
, that each variable is a laboratory where the players conduct experiments upon receiving their questions from the verifier; the outcomes of the experiments are the answers sent to the verifier. In quantum mechanics, commuting observables can be simultaneously measured. Hence, if two laboratories are sufficiently close in the metric associated with the instance (say, their distance in the Gaifman graph of
\Phi
is at most
k
), we may require that a joint experiment could be conducted in the two laboratories—and, thus, that the corresponding measurements commute. This gives rise to a notion of
k
-compatible
quantum strategies,
1
1

1

for CSPs (which capture the bounded-width or local-consistency algorithm in the classical setting), as recently introduced in
[
Kar25
]
.
whose projectors are simultaneously diagonalisable on subinstances of
\Phi
of diameter
k
. In particular, for
k=0
, this notion does not enforce any commutativity requirement, as is commonly done in the context of binary games, see for example
[
MR16b
,
KRT10
]
. The case
k=1
corresponds to enforcing commutativity for the measurements associated with variables lying in a common constraint, which is typically required for games having non-binary constraints
[
ABdSZ17
]
. The case
k=2
has appeared in
[
MS25
]
, where commutativity between measurements associated with paths of length
2
was required to derive completeness of a reduction between the quantum versions of
Unique Games
and
MaxCut
(such strategies were called
weak quantum assignments
therein, see
[
MS25
, Definition 22]
). At the other extreme, when
k
is the diameter of the Gaifman graph associated with
\Phi
, the requirement corresponds to simultaneous diagonalisability of all measurements and, thus, it implies a collapse to classical strategies.
We propose the following pseudo-telepathy version of Conjecture
1
.

Conjecture 2
.

Fix
d\geq 2
. For each
\epsilon>0
and each
k\in\mathbb{N}
there exists a
d
-to-
1
instance
\Phi
(on some alphabet size
n
) such that

\Phi
admits a perfect
k
-compatible quantum assignment but
\operatorname{sat}(\Phi)<\epsilon
.

2.2
Quantum adjunction of Pultr functors

Having fixed the outer verifier, the next step is to see how to transfer pseudo-telepathy up to the colouring game described in the Introduction. To that end, we make use of categorical operators known as
Pultr functors
between categories of relational structures
[
Pul70
]
. These are pairs of transformations
(\Lambda,\Gamma)
between relational structures on possibly different signatures, parameterised by a fixed
Pultr template
(see the formal definitions in Section
4
). The functor
\Lambda
(known as the
left
Pultr functor) acts on a structure
\mathbf{X}
by replacing its vertices with copies of the structures in the Pultr template, and then identifying some of the vertices. The functor
\Gamma
(the
central
Pultr functor) acts on a structure
\mathbf{Y}
by replacing its vertices with
homomorphisms
between the structures in the Pultr template and
\mathbf{Y}
.

Example 3
.

To illustrate the type of combinatorial operations that can be expressed as Pultr functors at the intuitive level, we now describe two examples of pairs
(\Lambda,\Gamma)
of Pultr functors, both defined on the category of directed graphs (i.e., relational structures having a unique, binary relation).
Fix a digraph
\mathbf{G}
. The first pair
(\Lambda^{\prime},\Gamma^{\prime})
of Pultr functors is defined as follows: For given digraphs
\mathbf{X}
and
\mathbf{Y}
,
\Lambda^{\prime}\mathbf{X}
is the direct product
\mathbf{X}\times\mathbf{G}
, while
\Gamma^{\prime}\mathbf{Y}
is the exponential digraph
\mathbf{Y}^{\mathbf{G}}
(for the definitions, we refer the reader to
[
HN04
, §2.4]
).
The second pair
(\Lambda^{\prime\prime},\Gamma^{\prime\prime})
of Pultr functors is defined as follows: For given digraphs
\mathbf{X}
and
\mathbf{Y}
,
\Lambda^{\prime\prime}\mathbf{X}
is the digraph obtained by replacing each vertex
x
of
\mathbf{X}
with a directed edge
(x^{(1)},x^{(2)})
, and then identifying
x^{(2)}
with
y^{(1)}
for each edge
(x,y)
of
\mathbf{X}
;
\Gamma^{\prime\prime}\mathbf{Y}
is the digraph whose vertex set is the edge set of
\mathbf{Y}
, and whose edge set contains all pairs of edges of the form
((x,y),(y,z))
.
It is not hard to check that both pairs of functors satisfy the relation
\Lambda\mathbf{X}\to\mathbf{Y}

\Leftrightarrow

\mathbf{X}\to\Gamma\mathbf{Y}
for each pair
\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Y}
of digraphs. As we shall see, this holds in general for every pair of Pultr functors.

The benefit of considering such an abstract type of transformations is that they are general enough to capture many classes of reductions used in the theory of CSPs. In particular,
gadget reductions
can be viewed as left Pultr functors for specific Pultr templates.
Crucially, various CSP reductions that are
not
gadget reductions are also captured via Pultr functors, a primary example being the
line-digraph
reduction—a standard graph-theoretic operation
[
HN60
]
corresponding to the functor
\Gamma^{\prime\prime}
in Example
3
.
The reason why Pultr functors yield reductions between classical CSPs is that they satisfying a (thin) adjunction property.
In the next statement,
\rho
and
\tau
are the relational signatures associated with the Pultr template, while the notation
\mathbf{A}\to\mathbf{B}
denotes the existence of a
homomorphism
between
\mathbf{A}
and
\mathbf{B}
(which, in this case, need to have the same signature).

Theorem 4

(
[
Pul70
]
)
.

Let
\mathfrak{T}
be a
(\rho,\tau)
-Pultr template. For any
\tau
-structure
\mathbf{X}
and any
\rho
-structure
\mathbf{Y}
it holds that

\Lambda\mathbf{X}\to\mathbf{Y}
if and only if
\mathbf{X}\to\Gamma\mathbf{Y}
.

In order to use Pultr functors for transferring pseudo-telepathy across non-local games, we need a quantum version of the adjunction theorem stated above.
First of all, let us introduce some notation. Any two structures
\mathbf{A}
and
\mathbf{B}
on the same signature can be viewed as a CSP instance
\Phi
where the variables are the vertices of
\mathbf{A}
, the labels in the alphabet are the vertices of
\mathbf{B}
, the constraints correspond to tuples in the relations of
\mathbf{A}
, and the admitted assignments to the constraints are expressed by tuples in the relations of
\mathbf{B}
.
2
2

2

Since, in this context, we only deal with perfect strategies, the constraints can be weighted according to any positive probability distribution.
In this way,
\operatorname{sat}(\Phi)=1
precisely when
\mathbf{A}\to\mathbf{B}
. We indicate the existence of a perfect
k
-compatible quantum strategy for such CSP instance by the notation
\mathbf{A}\leadsto^{k}\mathbf{B}
. The next result shows that one direction of Theorem
4
can be quantised, provided that the Pultr template is connected (in a technical sense defined in Section
4
).

Theorem 5
.

Let
\mathfrak{T}
be a connected
(\rho,\tau)
-Pultr template, let
\mathbf{X}
and
\mathbf{Y}
be a
\tau
-structure and a
\rho
-structure, respectively, let
k\in\mathbb{N}
, let
k^{\prime}=(k+1)\cdot\operatorname{diam}(\mathfrak{T})
, and suppose that
\Lambda\mathbf{X}\leadsto^{k^{\prime}}\mathbf{Y}
. Then
\mathbf{X}\leadsto^{k}\Gamma\mathbf{Y}
.

The next result gives the quantum version of the other direction of Theorem
4
, provided that the Pultr template satisfies a technical property that we call faithfulness.

Theorem 6
.

Let
\mathfrak{T}
be a faithful
(\rho,\tau)
-Pultr template, let
\mathbf{X}
and
\mathbf{Y}
be a
\tau
-structure and a
\rho
-structure, respectively, let
k\in\mathbb{N}
, and suppose that
\mathbf{X}\leadsto^{k}\Gamma\mathbf{Y}
. Then
\Lambda\mathbf{X}\leadsto^{k}\mathbf{Y}
.

We point out that the two directions of Pultr adjunction behave differently with respect to local compatibility of the quantum strategies. Indeed, while the implication
\mathbf{X}\leadsto\Gamma\mathbf{Y}

\Rightarrow

\Lambda\mathbf{X}\leadsto\mathbf{Y}
preserves the level of compatibility, the converse implication decreases it by a multiplicative factor depending on the structure of the Pultr template (in particular, its diameter, see formal definitions in Section
4
). This “compatibility decay” appears to be intrinsic in the way quantum strategies for the
\Lambda\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Y}
game are translated into quantum strategies for the
\mathbf{X},\Gamma\mathbf{Y}
game (see the proof of Theorem
5
), and it is the reason why arbitrarily high compatibility for
d
-to-
1
instances is required in Conjecture
2
.
More in general, many classic reductions from hardness of approximation theory involve “increasing the scope of constraints by capturing relations within chains of adjacent variables”—which is the intuitive description of the action of a central Pultr functor
\Gamma
. Examples of this are the reduction from
Unique Games
to
MaxCut
of
[
KKMO07
]
used in
[
MS25
]
; the reduction from weighted

-to-
1
games to unweighted

games from
[

and Appendix

of the current paper; and the line-digraph reduction mentioned above.
Hence, while the current work focusses specifically on the colouring game, some level of compatibility of the quantum strategies for
d
-to-
1
instances appears to be necessary in order to transfer pseudo-telepathy to other types of CSPs using general reductions from CSP approximation.

2.4
The quantum chromatic gap via
3
XOR games

How to construct strongly pseudo-telepathic
d
-to-
1
games required for proving unbounded quantum chromatic gap?
A natural starting point is to look at non-local games encoding Boolean linear equations. Indeed, many of the well-known examples of violations of Bell’s inequality involve cooperating entangled players proving that certain classically unsatisfiable systems of linear equations admit a solution—e.g., the Greenberger–Horne–Zeilinger (GHZ) game
[
GHZ89
,
GHSZ90
]
and the Mermin–Peres magic square game
[
Mer90
,
Mer93
,
Per90
]
. Such games have a natural
many-to-
1
structure, in that, for every partial assignment to some equation, there exists
precisely one consistent assignment to each of the variables appearing in it. As a consequence, a first approach could be to consider a parallel repetition of the non-local game associated with Boolean linear equations as in
[
CSUU08
]
, in order to decrease the classical value while preserving the existence of a perfect quantum assignment. The reason why this approach does not work is that the resulting game is a
d
-to-
1
instance for increasingly large

(in particular,
d
would be a function of the soundness parameter
\epsilon
in Conjecture
2
). However, transferring pseudo-telepathy to the colouring game in order to obtain an unbounded chromatic gap requires

to be fixed.

For this reason, we consider a suitably modified repetition of
3XOR
games (encoding Boolean linear equations having three variables per equation), where the players’ answers are required to be consistent across the iterations of the game.
By assuming pseudo-telepathy of such games (with any classical soundness threshold strictly smaller than
1
), we derive a
4
vs.
\mathcal{O}(1)
gap between quantum and classical chromatic numbers.
In the statement below,
\mathcal{G}(S,n)
indicates the

-fold repetition of the game corresponding to a regular system

of equations (see Section
6
for the formal definitions).

Theorem 8
.

Suppose that there exists some

such that, for each

, there exists a regular instance

of

admits a perfect

4

Note that the assumption of Theorem

The result is proved by showing that pseudo-telepathic instances for the

,

to prove hardness of approximating

2

The reduction we use to prove Theorem

Some care is only required when manipulating

3
Classical and quantum CSPs

In this preliminary section, we formally describe classical and quantum assignments for constraint satisfaction problems. We adopt the language of

predicate satisfiability
, which yields the most natural description of specific types of CSPs that shall appear frequently (in particular,
d
-to-
1
and
d
-to-
d
). Two alternative descriptions will sometimes be preferable:
the language of
non-local games
, providing a physical intuition of classical, quantum, and locally compatible quantum assignments as winning strategies for two-player cooperative games; and the language of
homomorphisms between relational structures
, allowing for a more direct connection with categorical constructs such as the Pultr adjunction, as we shall see in Section
4
.
The three, equivalent descriptions are given in Sections
3.1
,
3.2
, and
3.3
, respectively.

3.1
Predicate satisfiability

A CSP instance consists of a tuple
\Phi=(X,E,A,\pi,\phi)
where

•

X
is a finite set (whose elements are called
variables
);

•

E
is a multiset of
constraint
tuples
\mathbf{x}
of variables, each having a finite length called
arity
and denoted by
\ar(\mathbf{x})
;

•

A
is a finite set (called the
alphabet
or
label set
);

•

\pi
is a positive probability distribution over
E
;

•

\phi
is a collection of predicates
\phi_{\mathbf{x}}:A^{\ar(\mathbf{x})}\to\{0,1\}
for each
\mathbf{x}\in E
.
3
3

3

We shall often identify the function
\phi_{\mathbf{x}}
with the set
\{\mathbf{a}\in A^{\ar(\mathbf{x})}\mbox{ s.t. }\phi_{\mathbf{x}}(\mathbf{a})=1\}
.

A
classical assignment
for a CSP instance
\Phi
is a function
f:X\to A
.
The value of
f
on a constraint
\mathbf{x}
is
\phi_{\mathbf{x}}(f(\mathbf{x}))
, where
f(\mathbf{x})
is the entrywise application of
f
to the entries of
\mathbf{x}
.
The total value of
f
is the weighted fraction of satisfied constraints; i.e., the quantity

\displaystyle\operatorname{sat}_{f}(\Phi)=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}\sim\pi}\phi_{\mathbf{x}}(f(\mathbf{x})).

If
\operatorname{sat}_{f}(\Phi)=1
, we say that
f
is a
perfect
classical assignment.
The
classical value
of
\Phi
, denoted by
\operatorname{sat}(\Phi)
, is the maximum of
\operatorname{sat}_{f}(\Phi)
over all classical assignments
f:X\to A
.

Given a finite set
S
and a finite-dimensional Hilbert space
\mathbb{H}
, we let
\PVM_{\mathbb{H}}(S)
be the set of projection-valued measurements over
\mathbb{H}
whose outcomes are indexed by
S
. I.e.,
\PVM_{\mathbb{H}}(S)
contains all sets
\{Q_{s}:s\in S\}

where each
Q_{s}
is a projector onto
\mathbb{H}
and, in addition,
\sum_{s\in S}Q_{s}
is the identity projector
\id_{\mathbb{H}}
.
A
quantum assignment
over
\mathbb{H}
for a CSP instance
\Phi
consists of a function
\mathfrak{Q}:X\to\PVM_{\mathbb{H}}(A)
. For notational convenience, given a variable
x\in X
and a label
a\in A
, we shall denote by
Q_{x,a}
the
a
-th projector in the measurement
\mathfrak{Q}(x)
. The value of
\mathfrak{Q}
on a constraint
\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},\dots,x_{r})\in E
of arity
r
is the quantity

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\dim(\mathbb{H})}\sum_{\mathbf{a}\in A^{r}}\phi_{\mathbf{x}}(\mathbf{a})\,\tr(Q_{x_{1},a_{1}}\cdot Q_{x_{2},a_{2}}\cdot\ldots\cdot Q_{x_{r},a_{r}}).

The total value of
\mathfrak{Q}
is then the quantity

\displaystyle\operatorname{qsat}_{\mathfrak{Q}}(\Phi)=\frac{1}{\dim(\mathbb{H})}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}\sim\pi}\sum_{\mathbf{a}\in A^{\ar(\mathbf{x})}}\phi_{\mathbf{x}}(\mathbf{a})\,\tr(Q_{x_{1},a_{1}}\cdot Q_{x_{2},a_{2}}\cdot\ldots\cdot Q_{x_{\ar(\mathbf{x})},a_{\ar(\mathbf{x})}}).

(1)

We say that
\mathfrak{Q}
is a
perfect
quantum assignment if
\operatorname{qsat}_{\mathfrak{Q}}(\Phi)=1
.

If all constraints of
\Phi
have arity
1
or
2
, the quantity in (
1
) is always real, and the assignment
\mathfrak{Q}
is sometimes called a
quantum noncommutative strategy
(see, for example,
[
MS25
, § 3.1]
).
In the general case, in order to enforce that the traces of products of projectors in the expression above be real, one further requires that projectors corresponding to variables appearing in a common constraint should commute. We call these quantum assignments
1
-compatible
.
More in general, as discussed in Section
2.1
, we consider quantum assignments enforcing commutativity between projectors corresponding to variables that are close in the metric over
X
induced by the constraints in
E
.
Enforcing this type of
local compatibility
for quantum assignments of a CSP instance is needed in order to
achieve quantum completeness of Pultr reductions, which is the focus of Section
4
.

We now formally define locally compatible quantum assignments.
Given two variables
x,x^{\prime}\in X
, we let
\operatorname{dist}_{\Phi}(x,x^{\prime})
denote the distance between
x
and
x^{\prime}
in the
Gaifman graph
of
\Phi
—i.e., the undirected graph having vertex set
X
, where two vertices are adjacent if and only if they appear in a common constraint. For two operators
Q
and
Q^{\prime}
, let
[Q,Q^{\prime}]=QQ^{\prime}-Q^{\prime}Q
be their commutator. We say that a quantum assignment
\mathfrak{Q}
is
k
-compatible
for some
k\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
if
[Q_{x,a},Q_{x^{\prime},a^{\prime}}]=O
for each pair
x,x^{\prime}\in X
such that
\operatorname{dist}_{\Phi}(x,x^{\prime})\leq k
and each pair
a,a^{\prime}\in A
. Clearly, the
k
-compatibility requirement becomes progressively stronger as
k
increases.
In the extreme case, if
\delta
is the diameter of the Gaifman graph of
\Phi
, a
\delta
-compatible quantum assignment requires that the projectors in
all
PVMs of the assignment should commute with each other and, thus, be simultaneously diagonalisable. Hence,
\delta
-compatible quantum assignments collapse to classical assignments.
Note that
projector commutativity is not transitive, so locally compatible quantum assignments do not automatically collapse to globally compatible, and thus classical, assignments.

The total value of a
k
-compatible quantum strategy is clearly real if
k\geq 1
, regardless of the arity of its constraints. Hence, the
k
-compatible quantum value of
\Phi
, denoted by
\operatorname{qsat}_{k}(\Phi)
, can be defined as the supremum of
\operatorname{qsat}_{\mathfrak{Q}}(\Phi)
over all
k
-compatible quantum assignments
\mathfrak{Q}
.
The discussion above shows that the following chain of inequalities holds:

\displaystyle\operatorname{qsat}_{1}(\Phi)\geq\operatorname{qsat}_{2}(\Phi)\geq\dots\geq\operatorname{qsat}_{\delta}(\Phi)=\operatorname{qsat}_{\delta+1}(\Phi)=\dots=\operatorname{sat}(\Phi).

In addition, in the unary or binary case, we have that
\operatorname{qsat}_{0}(\Phi)\geq\operatorname{qsat}_{1}(\Phi)
. Hence, the notion described above allows to smoothly decrease the value of a CSP from the quantum noncommutative to the classical one, by considering PVM assignments that “look classical” over progressively larger subinstances of
\Phi
.

We now define a particular type of CSP instances that shall frequently appear in this work.
A
label-cover
instance is a CSP instance
\Phi=(X,E,A,\pi,\phi)
all of whose constraints are binary. Notice that, in this case,
(X,E)
is a digraph (possibly with repeated edges). We say that
\Phi
is
bipartite
if

E\subseteq X_{1}\times X_{2}
for some decomposition
X=X_{1}\sqcup X_{2}
.
Following
[
DS14
,
DSV15
]
,
we say that a bipartite label-cover instance
\Phi
is
projective
if there is a decomposition
A=A_{1}\sqcup A_{2}
such that, for each
\mathbf{x}\in E
, it holds that
(i)

\phi_{\mathbf{x}}\subseteq A_{1}\times A_{2}
, and
(ii)
for each
a_{1}\in A_{1}
there exists precisely one value
a_{2}\in A_{2}
for which
\phi_{\mathbf{x}}(a_{1},a_{2})=1
.
4
4

4

Sometimes, in the literature on both classical and quantum CSPs/non-local games, the expression “label-cover instances” indicates what we call here “projective label-cover instances”.

Clearly, if
f:X\to A
is an optimal classical assignment for
\Phi
, there is an optimal classical assignment
f^{\prime}
such that
f^{\prime}(X_{1})\subseteq A_{1}
and
f^{\prime}(X_{2})\subseteq A_{2}
. Hence, we can assume without loss of generality that optimal classical assignments satisfy the latter condition. (We shall see that the same holds for optimal quantum assignments, see Proposition
40
.)
We say that a projective label-cover instance is
d

1
for some

if for each
\mathbf{x}\in E

a_{2}\in A_{2}
there exist precisely
d

a_{1}\in A_{1}
for which
\phi_{\mathbf{x}}(a_{1},a_{2})=1
.
Observe that, in this case,
|A_{1}|=d\cdot|A_{2}|
.

3.2
Non-local games

The following two-player cooperative game can be associated with a CSP instance
\Phi=(X,E,A,\pi,\phi)
(see for example
[
ABdSZ17
]
):

•

The verifier sends Alice a constraint
\mathbf{x}\in E
sampled according to the probability distribution
\pi
, and Alice responds with a tuple
\mathbf{a}\in A^{\ar(\mathbf{x})}
;

•

the verifier sends Bob a variable
x\in X
sampled uniformly at random, and Bob responds with a label
a\in A
.

Alice and Bob win the game if, whenever
x=x_{i}
for some
i\in[\ar(\mathbf{x})]
, it holds that
a=a_{i}
.
It is straightforward to see that a perfect classical (deterministic or randomised) strategy for Alice and Bob exists precisely when
\operatorname{sat}(\Phi)=1
. A
quantum
strategy
\mathfrak{E}
consists of a finite-dimensional Hilbert space
\mathbb{H}
, a state
\psi\in\mathbb{H}\otimes\mathbb{H}
, and two collections of measurements—one for Alice and one for Bob—indexed by the verifier’s questions and having as possible outcomes the admitted players’ answers.
More in detail, there is a measurement
\{E_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{a}}\}_{\mathbf{a}\in A^{\ar(\mathbf{x})}}\in\PVM_{\mathbb{H}}(A^{\ar(\mathbf{x})})
for each
\mathbf{x}\in E
, and a measurement
\{{E^{\prime}_{x,a}}\}_{a\in A}\in\PVM_{\mathbb{H}}(A)
for each
x\in X
. Upon receiving the verifier’s questions, Alice and Bob select the corresponding PVMs and perform the corresponding measurement on their part of the state
\psi
; then, their answers to the verifier are the obtained outcomes.
Hence, in order for the strategy to be perfect, the following conditions must hold:

•

\psi^{*}({E_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{a}}}\otimes{E^{\prime}_{x,a}})\psi=0
whenever
x
appears in
\mathbf{x}
with index
i
and
a\neq a_{i}
;

•

\psi^{*}({E_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{a}}}\otimes\id_{\mathbb{H}})\psi=0
whenever
\phi_{\mathbf{x}}(\mathbf{a})=0
.

The next result shows the connection between perfect quantum strategies and perfect quantum assignments.

Theorem 9

(
[
ABdSZ17
]
)
.

Let
\Phi
be a
\CSP
instance. Then

\Phi
has a perfect
1
-compatible quantum assignment if and only if the non-local game associated with
\Phi
admits a perfect quantum strategy.

We can easily modify the non-local game above to capture locally compatible quantum assignments. To that end, we add “fake” constraints to
\Phi
that have no effect on classical strategies, but enforce commutativity of the projectors corresponding to the selected variables. Formally, we consider the CSP instance
\Phi^{(k)}=(X,E^{\prime},A,\pi^{\prime},\phi^{\prime})
where
E^{\prime}
is obtained from
E
by adding a binary constraint
(x,x^{\prime})
for each two variables
x,x^{\prime}
such that
\operatorname{dist}_{\Phi}(x,x^{\prime})\leq k
. The corresponding set of admitted labels is
\phi_{(x,x^{\prime})}=A^{2}
; i.e., no restriction is posed on the value of the constraint. Finally,
\pi^{\prime}
assigns probability, say,
\frac{\pi(\mathbf{x})}{2}
for each
\mathbf{x}\in E
, and probability
\frac{1}{2\alpha}
for each new binary constraint
(x,x^{\prime})
, where
\alpha
is the number of the new binary constraints. The following fact is immediate from Theorem
9
.

Corollary 10
.

Let
\Phi
be a CSP instance, and take
k\in\mathbb{N}
. Then

\Phi
has a perfect
k
-compatible quantum assignment if and only if the non-local game associated with
\Phi^{(k)}
admits a perfect quantum strategy.

3.3
Homomorphism problems

We end this preliminary section by giving an equivalent description of CSPs based on homomorphisms between relational structures.
A
signature

\rho
is a finite set of relation symbols
R
, each with its
arity

\ar(R)\in\mathbb{N}
.
A
relational structure

\mathbf{S}
with signature
\rho
(in short, a
\rho
-structure) consists of a finite set
S
and a relation
R({\mathbf{S}})\subseteq S^{\ar(R)}
for each symbol

. A

and
\mathbf{T}
on a common signature

of
\mathbf{S}
to the domain

of
\mathbf{T}
that preserves all relations; i.e.,
f(\mathbf{s})\in R({\mathbf{T}})
for each symbol
R\in\rho
and each tuple
\mathbf{s}\in R({\mathbf{S}})
(where

is the entrywise application of
f
the the entries of
\mathbf{s}
). We denote the existence of a homomorphism from
\mathbf{S}
to
\mathbf{T}
by the notation
\mathbf{S}\to\mathbf{T}
.

Given a CSP instance
\Phi=(X,E,A,\pi,\phi)
, we can associate with
\Phi
two relational structures
\mathbf{X}
,
\mathbf{A}
over a common signature
\rho
as follows.
The domains of
\mathbf{X}
and of
\mathbf{A}
are the variable set
X
and the label set
A
, respectively. The signature
\rho
contains an
r
-ary symbol for each distinct map
\phi_{\mathbf{x}}:A^{r}\to\{0,1\}
;
the interpretation
R(\mathbf{X})
of
R
in
\mathbf{X}
contains all tuples
\mathbf{x}\in E
such that

corresponds to the symbol
R
, while
the interpretation of
R
in
\mathbf{A}
is the set
R(\mathbf{A})=\{\mathbf{a}\in A^{r}:\phi_{\mathbf{x}}(\mathbf{a})=1\}

Conversely, given two structures
\mathbf{X}
and
\mathbf{A}
on a common signature
\rho
, we can associate with them a CSP instance
\Phi=(X,E,A,\pi,\phi)
such that
X
and

are the domains of
\mathbf{X}
and
\mathbf{A}
, respectively;
E=\bigcup_{R\in\rho}R(\mathbf{X})

and
\mathbf{x}\in R(\mathbf{X})

is the Boolean map defined by
\mathbf{a}\mapsto 1
for each
\mathbf{a}\in R(\mathbf{A})
and
\mathbf{a}\mapsto 0
for each

is any positive distribution over

. Note that, as far as

(classical or quantum) assignments are concerned, the particular choice of

is irrelevant.
Let
\mathbf{X}
and
\mathbf{A}
be the
\rho
-structures corresponding to a CSP instance

. Clearly, it holds that
\operatorname{sat}(\Phi)=1
if and only if
\mathbf{X}\to\mathbf{A}
. For

admits a perfect
k
-compatible quantum assignment. (If

and

have only unary and binary relations, we also use the notation

When

is the CSP instance corresponding to two structures

and

, we denote

by

.

Example 11
.

Let
\mathbf{G}
be an undirected graph, and view it as the relational structure having a single, binary relation containing, for each undirected edge

of

and
(g^{\prime},g)

Recall that the

of

[

The next proposition easily follows from a result proved in

Proposition 12
.

Let
\mathbf{X}
and
\mathbf{Y}
be
\rho
-structures and let

. Then
\mathbf{X}\leadsto^{k}\mathbf{Y}

\mathbf{x}\in R(\mathbf{X})

,

If

. Suppose that

Remark 13
.

We point out that Proposition

also holds for

if

and

are graphs, as proved in

[

,
MR16b
]
.

As a consequence,
we shall encode a perfect
k
-compatible quantum assignment for
\mathbf{X}
and

as a collection

of projectors onto some Hilbert space

.
We will often make use of the following result.

Proposition 14
.

Let
\mathbf{X},\mathbf{X}^{\prime},\mathbf{Y},\mathbf{Y}^{\prime}
be
\rho
-structures such that
\mathbf{X}\to\mathbf{X}^{\prime}\leadsto^{k}\mathbf{Y}^{\prime}\to\mathbf{Y}
for some
k\in\mathbb{N}
. Then
\mathbf{X}\leadsto^{k}\mathbf{Y}
.

Proof.

Let
f:\mathbf{X}\to\mathbf{X}^{\prime}
and
g:\mathbf{Y}^{\prime}\to\mathbf{Y}
be homomorphisms, and let
\mathfrak{Q}
be a perfect
k
-compatible quantum assignment for
\mathbf{X}^{\prime},\mathbf{Y}^{\prime}
over some Hilbert space
\mathbb{H}
. Consider, for each
x\in X
and
y\in Y
, the linear application

\displaystyle W_{x,y}=\sum_{y^{\prime}\in g^{-1}(y)}Q_{f(x),y^{\prime}}.

(2)

We claim that the set
\mathfrak{W}=\{W_{x,y}\mbox{ s.t. }x\in X,y\in Y\}
yields a perfect
k
-compatible quantum assignment for
\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Y}
(over the same Hilbert space
\mathbb{H}
).
First of all, the summands in (
2
) are mutually orthogonal projectors and, thus, the linear applications in
\mathfrak{W}
are projectors. The fact
\sum_{y\in Y}W_{x,y}=\id_{\mathbb{H}}
follows from the analogous property of
\mathfrak{Q}
. Hence,
\mathfrak{W}
is a quantum assignment.
Take now an
r
-ary symbol
R\in\rho
and two tuples
\mathbf{x}\in R(\mathbf{X})
and
\mathbf{y}\in Y^{r}\setminus R(\mathbf{Y})
. Suppose, for the sake of contradiction, that

\displaystyle O\neq\prod_{i\in[r]}W_{x_{i},y_{i}}=\prod_{i\in[r]}\,\sum_{y^{\prime}\in g^{-1}(y_{i})}Q_{f(x_{i}),y^{\prime}}.

We deduce that there exist elements
y^{\prime}_{i}\in g^{-1}(y_{i})
for each
i\in[r]
such that

\displaystyle O\neq\prod_{i\in[r]}Q_{f(x_{i}),y^{\prime}_{i}}.

(3)

Consider now the tuple
\mathbf{y}^{\prime}=(y^{\prime}_{1},\dots,y^{\prime}_{r})
. Since
f
is a homomorphism and
\mathbf{x}\in R(\mathbf{X})
, it holds that
f(\mathbf{x})\in R(\mathbf{X}^{\prime})
. Since
\mathfrak{Q}
is a perfect quantum assignment, we deduce from (
3
) that
\mathbf{y}^{\prime}\in R(\mathbf{Y}^{\prime})
. Since
g
is a homomorphism, we conclude that
\mathbf{y}=g(\mathbf{y}^{\prime})\in R(\mathbf{Y})
, a contradiction. This means that the assignment
\mathfrak{W}
is perfect.
Finally, to show that
\mathfrak{W}
is
k
-compatible, observe that
\operatorname{dist}_{\mathbf{X}^{\prime}}(f(x),f(\tilde{x}))\leq\operatorname{dist}_{\mathbf{X}}(x,\tilde{x})
for each
x,\tilde{x}\in X
since
f
is a homomorphism. Hence, the
k
-compatibility of
\mathfrak{Q}
implies that

\displaystyle\Big[W_{x,y},W_{\tilde{x},\tilde{y}}\Big]=\Big[\sum_{y^{\prime}\in g^{-1}(y)}Q_{f(x),y^{\prime}},\sum_{\tilde{y}^{\prime}\in g^{-1}(\tilde{y})}Q_{f(\tilde{x}),\tilde{y}^{\prime}}\Big]=\sum_{y^{\prime}\in g^{-1}(y)}\,\sum_{\tilde{y}^{\prime}\in g^{-1}(\tilde{y})}\Big[Q_{f(x),y^{\prime}},Q_{f(\tilde{x}),\tilde{y}^{\prime}}\Big]=O

for each
x,\tilde{x}\in X
with
\operatorname{dist}_{\mathbf{X}}(x,\tilde{x})\leq k
and each
y,\tilde{y}\in Y
.
Therefore, the assignment
\mathfrak{W}
witnesses that
\mathbf{X}\leadsto^{k}\mathbf{Y}

∎

4
Quantum adjunction of Pultr functors

The goal of this section is to prove a quantum version of Theorem
4

Definition 16
.

Let
\rho,\tau

(\rho,\tau)
-

consists of the following data:

\rho

;

•

\rho

.

Let

be a

-Pultr template. The

-Pultr template. The

\displaystyle W_{x,h}=\prod_{a\in A}Q_{a^{(x)},h(a)}.

\displaystyle\prod_{i\in[r]}Q_{a_{i}^{(x)},h(a_{i})}=O.

Let

is the central Pultr functor corresponding to a connected Pultr template

∎

5.4
Putting all together

Combining the results of Subsections

,
5.2
, and

Theorem

(Theorem
7
restated)
.

Suppose that Conjecture

holds for some

. Then, for any

, there exists a graph with quantum chromatic number

and classical chromatic number larger than

.

Proof.

Take an integer
d\geq 2
such that Conjecture
2
holds for

.
Recall the functions
\alpha
and
\beta
defined in Section
5.3
.
For

, we denote by
\alpha^{(i)}
(resp.
\beta^{(i)}
) the function obtained by iterating
\alpha
(resp.
\beta
)
i
-many times.
Observe that
\beta(n)>n
whenever
n\geq 4
. Hence, there exists some
j\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\beta^{(j)}(4)\geq 2d
. Let
i=j+2
, and define
k^{\prime}=3\cdot 2^{i}-2
.
Assume, without loss of generality, that
c\geq 2
.
Define
\epsilon=\frac{1}{\alpha^{(i)}(c)}
, and choose
\epsilon^{\prime}>0
,

witnessing the truth of Theorem
26
applied to
\epsilon
. Using Proposition
24
, find a

d
instance
\Phi
such that

\Phi
admits a perfect

-compatible quantum assignment
but
\operatorname{isat}_{t}(\Phi)<\epsilon^{\prime}
.
Consider the digraph
\delta^{(i)}\eta\Phi
, where
\delta^{(i)}
denotes the
i
-fold application of the line-digraph operator
\delta
of Section
5.3
and
\eta
is the reduction from Section
5.2
. Let also
\mathbf{G}
be the undirected graph obtained by replacing each directed edge of
\delta^{(i)}\eta\Phi
by an undirected edge. We claim that
\mathbf{G}
has quantum chromatic number

and classical chromatic number larger than

, which would conclude the proof.

(Classical) soundness
. Theorem

implies that
\eta\Phi
has no independent sets of relative size

is larger than

ensures that
\eta\Phi\leadsto^{k^{\prime}}\mathbf{K}_{\beta^{(j)}(4)}

\displaystyle\delta^{(i)}\eta\Phi\leadsto^{1}\delta^{(i)}\mathbf{K}_{\beta^{(j)}(4)}.

(19)

where the last homomorphism comes from the fact that

Remark 32
.

We point out that the proof of Theorem
7
can be easily modified to enforce local compatibility of the perfect quantum assignment witnessing that the graph is quantum
3
-colourable. More precisely, conditional to Conjecture

, for each

having arbitrarily large classical chromatic number and such that

This fact, together with an upper bound on the quantum chromatic gap in terms of the number of shared qubits in the quantum strategy recently proved in

, has the interesting consequence that the Hilbert spaces necessary for the validity of Conjecture

cannot have bounded dimension.
More precisely, it was shown in
[

, Theorem 29]
that, for each graph
\mathbf{G}
and each

, if

on a Hilbert space of dimension

, then
\mathbf{G}\to\mathbf{K}_{n\cdot\alpha_{p}}
with
\alpha_{p}=(2+\mathcal{o}(1))^{p-1}
.
Now, all reductions from
d
-to-

games to

vs.
\mathcal{O}(1)
colouring described in Section

preserve the Hilbert space

witnessing perfect quantum assignments, as discussed in Remarks

and

. It follows that, in Conjecture

, the dimension of the Hilbert spaces witnessing the presence of a perfect quantum assignment for the classically

.

6
The quantum chromatic gap via
3
XOR games

The Mermin–Peres “magic square” construction gives a well-known example of a pseudo-telepathic system of Boolean equations. The classical value of the system is strictly smaller than
1
; however, by sharing a pair of entangled Bell states and measuring it via PVMs arising from combinations of Pauli matrices, the corresponding non-local game can be won with probability
1
by two cooperating non-communicating players
[
Mer90
,
Mer93
,
Per90
]
.
The Mermin–Peres system is an instance of
3
XOR, i.e., the CSP expressing systems of Boolean equations involving three variables each. Furthermore, the Mermin–Peres system is
2
-regular
, in that every variable appears in at most (in fact, precisely)
2
equations, and two distinct equations share at most one variable.
Systems of linear equations can be naturally interpreted as instances of “many-to-
1
” games: For every partial assignment to some equation, there exists precisely one consistent assignment to each of the variables appearing in the equation.
Hence, a naive approach to proving Conjecture
2
—and, thus, establishing Theorem
7
unconditionally—could be to consider a parallel-repetition version of the Mermin–Peres magic square game. In that way, the classical value of the game is decreased while preserving the existence of a perfect quantum assignment (which is obtained by simply taking the tensor product of PVMs corresponding to one iteration of the game).

Unfortunately, it is not hard to see that this approach cannot work: Increasing the number of repetitions of the game generates label-cover instances that are
d
-to-
1
for increasingly large
d
. On the other end, in order to prove Theorem
7
by transferring pseudo-telepathy from the instances in Conjecture
2
, one would need to reduce the soundness parameter
\epsilon
to zero while leaving
d

fixed
.
Therefore, we consider a stronger version of repetition for
3
XOR games. It shall be convenient to present it in the form of a non-local game. Let
S
be a system of linear equations over the Boolean field
\mathbb{F}_{2}
, and fix an integer
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Alice receives from the verifier a list
\mathbf{u}=(u_{1},\dots,u_{n})
of equations from
S
, and replies with an assignment
\vartheta:\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}\to\mathbb{F}_{2}
, where
\tilde{\mathbf{u}}
is the set of all variables appearing in the equations from
\mathbf{u}
. Similarly, Bob receives from the verifier a list
\mathbf{w}=(w_{1},\dots,w_{n})
of equations from
S
and replies with an assignment
\vartheta^{\prime}:\widetilde{\mathbf{w}}\to\mathbb{F}_{2}
. Alice and Bob win the game if
\vartheta
respects the equations in
\mathbf{u}
,
\vartheta^{\prime}
respects the equations in
\mathbf{w}
, and the replies are consistent, in the sense that

\displaystyle\vartheta|_{\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}\cap\widetilde{\mathbf{w}}}=\vartheta^{\prime}|_{\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}\cap\widetilde{\mathbf{w}}}.

(21)

We denote this game by
\mathcal{G}(S,n)
.
Note that the last requirement would not be enforced in a standard parallel repetition of
3
XOR, where Alice’s and Bob’s replies need not be consistent across different iterations of the game.

Let
s(\mathbf{u})
denote the set of assignments
\vartheta:\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}\to\mathbb{F}_{2}
such that
\vartheta
satisfies all equations in
\mathbf{u}
.
Using Proposition
12
, we have that a perfect quantum strategy
\mathfrak{Q}
for the
\mathcal{G}(S,n)
game is given by projectors
Q_{\mathbf{u},\vartheta}
onto a Hilbert space
\mathbb{H}
for each
n
-tuple
\mathbf{u}
of equations and each
\vartheta\in s(\mathbf{u})
, such that

•

\sum_{\vartheta\in s(\mathbf{u})}Q_{\mathbf{u},\vartheta}=\id_{\mathbb{H}}
for each
\mathbf{u}
, and

•

Q_{\mathbf{u},\vartheta}\cdot Q_{\mathbf{w},\vartheta^{\prime}}=O
whenever
\vartheta|_{\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}\cap\widetilde{\mathbf{w}}}\neq\vartheta^{\prime}|_{\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}\cap\widetilde{\mathbf{w}}}
.

We shall further require that the strategy is
1
-compatible; i.e.,

•

[Q_{\mathbf{u},\vartheta},Q_{\mathbf{w},\vartheta^{\prime}}]=O
whenever
\tilde{\mathbf{u}}\cap\tilde{\mathbf{w}}\neq\emptyset
.

As usual, we let
\operatorname{sat}(S)
denote the maximum fraction of equations simultaneously satisfiable by a classical assignment.
Moreover, as a generalisation of the regularity property of the Mermin–Peres construction, a system of linear equations is said to be
regular
for some
p\in\mathbb{N}
(which we consider as a fixed constant) if
(i)
every variable appears in at most
p
equations, and
(ii)
two distinct equations share at most one variable. The main result of this section is the following.

Theorem

(Theorem
8
restated)
.

Suppose that there exists some
0<s^{*}<1
such that, for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
, there exists a regular instance
S
of
3
XOR for which
\operatorname{sat}(S)<s^{*}
but
\mathcal{G}(S,n)
admits a perfect
1
-compatible quantum assignment. Then, for each
c\in\mathbb{N}
, there exists a graph with quantum chromatic number at most
4
and classical chromatic number larger than
c
.

6.1
Description of the reduction

We now outline the reduction used in
[
KMS17
,
KMS18
,
DKK+18a
,
DKK+18b
]
to establish the
2
-to-
2
Games Theorem with imperfect completeness. We shall mostly follow the presentation in
[
Min22
, ch. 3]
.

Let
S
be a regular instance of
3
XOR.
The final reduction
\rho

is the composition of two reductions
\rho_{1}
and
\rho_{2}
:
The first takes
S
as input and returns a label-cover instance
\rho_{1}S
all of whose constraints are either
1
-to-
1
or
2
-to-
2

\rho_{1}S
as input and returns a
2
-to-
2
instance

Fix positive integers

and

For any equation

, we let its

be the number

such that

is the equation

.
Consider the set
\mathcal{U}
containing all

Let

be the set of variables appearing in the system

.
We shall consider the set
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{X}
, which we view as a

. Given a set

\mathbb{F}_{2}^{X^{\prime}}
is canonically identified with a subspace of

(where the identification simply consists in padding each vector

For

be the one-hot encoding of

Also, for each

.
Denote the space

. For a space

if

for which

is the right-hand side of

. Fix a tuple

denote the set of variables appearing in the equations of

Consider the set

containing all vector subspaces

.
The next property of

Lemma 33

(
[

]
)
.

Choose

and

. Then any linear function on

that respects
\mathbf{u}
admits a unique linear extension to
L\oplus H_{\mathbf{u}}\oplus H_{\mathbf{w}}
that respects
\mathbf{w}
.

We let
\rho_{1}S
be the unweighted label-cover instance
(V,E,A,\phi)
defined as follows.
The variable set
V
is the set

\{L\oplus H_{\mathbf{u}}:\mathbf{u}\in\mathcal{U},L\in\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{u}}\}
.
The alphabet
A
is the set
[2^{\ell}]
.
Since each of the

-many linear functions

can be uniquely extended to a linear function on

that respects
\mathbf{u}
, the elements of

\mathbf{u}
.
Given a pair

of variables, we let

if and only if

(in which case the constraint is

\dim(L\oplus H_{\mathbf{u}}\oplus H_{\mathbf{w}})=\dim(L^{\prime}\oplus H_{\mathbf{u}}\oplus H_{\mathbf{w}})=\dim(L\oplus L^{\prime}\oplus H_{\mathbf{u}}\oplus H_{\mathbf{w}})-1

-to-

of admitted labellings is defined as follows. Given a linear function

(i.e., an element of the alphabet),

be its unique linear extension to

\mathbf{w}
, as per Lemma

6.2
Quantum completeness of
\rho

The reduction
\rho
is easily seen to preserve perfect
classical
completeness, in the sense that
\rho S
admits a perfect classical assignment whenever
S
does. In fact,
\rho
also preserves imperfect classical completeness, in that almost satisfiable
3
XOR instances are mapped to almost satisfiable
2
-to-
2
instances. This is crucial for the purpose of
[
KMS17
,
KMS18
,
DKK+18a
,
DKK+18b
]
, as Håstad’s NP-hardness of approximating
3
XOR games
[
Hås01
]
only holds in the imperfect completeness regime (otherwise, the problem trivially becomes tractable). Our goal is now to show that
\rho
preserves
quantum
completeness, and can thus be used to transfer pseudo-telepathy from
3
XOR to
2
-to-
2
games. Since
3
XOR games are pseudo-telepathic despite being tractable in the exact version, we prove perfect quantum completeness.

Theorem 34
.

Let
S
be a regular
3
XOR instance. If
\mathcal{G}(S,n)
admits a perfect
1
-compatible quantum assignment, the same holds for the
2
-to-
2
instance
\rho S
.

Proof.

Let
\mathfrak{Q}=\{Q_{\mathbf{u},\vartheta}\}
be a perfect
1
-compatible quantum assignment for
S
over a Hilbert space
\mathbb{H}
. Let
X
be the set of variables appearing in
S
.
Given a function
\xi:X^{\prime}\to\mathbb{F}_{2}
for some
X^{\prime}\subseteq X
, we denote by
\widehat{\xi}:\mathbb{F}_{2}^{X^{\prime}}\to\mathbb{F}_{2}
the corresponding linear function defined by
\widehat{\xi}(v_{x})=\xi(x)
for each
x\in X^{\prime}
and extended by linearity. (Note that the set
\{v_{x}\}_{x\in X^{\prime}}
forms a basis for
{\mathbb{F}_{2}}^{X^{\prime}}
since
v_{x}
is the one-hot encoding of
x
.)
Recall that
\mathcal{U}
denotes the set of all legitimate
n
-tuples of equations of
S
.

We first prove quantum completeness for the first part
\rho_{1}
of the reduction
\rho
.
Take a variable
L\oplus H_{\mathbf{u}}
of
\rho_{1}S
;
i.e.,
\mathbf{u}\in\mathcal{U}
and
L\in\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{u}}
. Take also a label
\psi\in A
, which corresponds to a linear map
\psi:L\oplus H_{\mathbf{u}}\to\mathbb{F}_{2}
that respects
\mathbf{u}
. Consider the linear application

\displaystyle W_{L\oplus H_{\mathbf{u}},\psi}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\vartheta\in s(\mathbf{u})\\
\widehat{\vartheta}|_{L\oplus H_{\mathbf{u}}}=\psi\end{subarray}}Q_{\mathbf{u},\vartheta}.

(22)

We claim that the set
\mathfrak{W}=\{W_{L\oplus H_{\mathbf{u}},\psi}\}
is a
1
-compatible perfect quantum assignment for
\rho_{1}S
over
\mathbb{H}
.

First of all, since each family
\{Q_{\mathbf{u},\vartheta}\mbox{ s.t. }\vartheta\in s(\mathbf{u})\}
is a PVM, the linear applications in (
22
) are projectors. Take now two different labels
\psi\neq\psi^{\prime}:L\oplus H_{\mathbf{u}}\to\mathbb{F}_{2}
. If
\vartheta,\vartheta^{\prime}\in s(\mathbf{u})
are such that
\widehat{\vartheta}|_{L\oplus H_{\mathbf{u}}}=\psi
and
\widehat{\vartheta}^{\prime}|_{L\oplus H_{\mathbf{u}}}=\psi^{\prime}
, it must hold that
\vartheta\neq\vartheta^{\prime}
, which means that
Q_{\mathbf{u},\vartheta}\cdot Q_{\mathbf{u},\vartheta^{\prime}}=O
. It follows that
W_{L\oplus H_{\mathbf{u}},\psi}\cdot W_{L\oplus H_{\mathbf{u}},\psi^{\prime}}=O
; i.e., the projectors in
\mathfrak{W}
corresponding to the same variable are mutually orthogonal. Furthermore, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{\psi\in A}W_{L\oplus H_{\mathbf{u}},\psi}=\sum_{\psi\in A}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\vartheta\in s(\mathbf{u})\\
\widehat{\vartheta}|_{L\oplus H_{\mathbf{u}}}=\psi\end{subarray}}Q_{\mathbf{u},\vartheta}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\vartheta:\tilde{\mathbf{u}}\to\mathbb{F}_{2}\\
\widehat{\vartheta}\mbox{ respects }\mathbf{u}\end{subarray}}Q_{\mathbf{u},\vartheta}=\sum_{\vartheta\in s(\mathbf{u})}Q_{\mathbf{u},\vartheta}=\id_{\mathbb{H}}.

This shows that each set
\{W_{L\oplus H_{\mathbf{u}},\psi}\mbox{ s.t. }\psi\in A\}
is a PVM and, thus,
\mathfrak{W}
is a quantum assignment.

Consider now a pair
e=(L\oplus H_{\mathbf{u}},L^{\prime}\oplus H_{\mathbf{w}})\in E

(where
E
is the edge set of
\rho_{1}S
), and take a pair
(\psi,\psi^{\prime})
of labels, with
\psi:L\oplus H_{\mathbf{u}}\to\mathbb{F}_{2}
and
\psi^{\prime}:L^{\prime}\oplus H_{\mathbf{w}}\to\mathbb{F}_{2}
. Recall the definition of the linear maps
\psi^{(\mathbf{w})}:L\oplus H_{\mathbf{u}}\oplus H_{\mathbf{w}}\to\mathbb{F}_{2}
and
\psi^{\prime(\mathbf{u})}:L^{\prime}\oplus H_{\mathbf{u}}\oplus H_{\mathbf{w}}\to\mathbb{F}_{2}
given in Section
6.1
. Suppose that
(\psi,\psi^{\prime})\not\in\phi_{e}
, which means that

\displaystyle\psi^{(\mathbf{w})}|_{(L\oplus H_{\mathbf{u}}\oplus H_{\mathbf{w}})\cap(L^{\prime}\oplus H_{\mathbf{u}}\oplus H_{\mathbf{w}})}\neq\psi^{\prime(\mathbf{u})}|_{(L\oplus H_{\mathbf{u}}\oplus H_{\mathbf{w}})\cap(L^{\prime}\oplus H_{\mathbf{u}}\oplus H_{\mathbf{w}})}.

Consider two linear functions

\widehat{\vartheta}|_{L\oplus H_{\mathbf{u}}}=\psi
and
\widehat{\vartheta^{\prime}}|_{L^{\prime}\oplus H_{\mathbf{w}}}=\psi^{\prime}
. Suppose, for the sake of contradiction, that

Since the quantum assignment

is perfect, it follows that

. Hence, there exists a well defined linear map

extending both

\mathbf{u}
and
\mathbf{w}

and

agree on the intersection of their domains, which contradicts (

We deduce that

\displaystyle W_{L\oplus H_{\mathbf{u}},\psi}\cdot W_{L^{\prime}\oplus H_{\mathbf{w}},\psi^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\Big(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\vartheta\in s(\mathbf{u})\\
\widehat{\vartheta}|_{L\oplus H_{\mathbf{u}}}=\psi\end{subarray}}Q_{\mathbf{u},\vartheta}\Big)\Big(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\vartheta^{\prime}\in s(\mathbf{w})\\
\widehat{\vartheta}^{\prime}|_{L^{\prime}\oplus H_{\mathbf{w}}}=\psi^{\prime}\end{subarray}}Q_{\mathbf{w},\vartheta^{\prime}}\Big)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\vartheta\in s(\mathbf{u})\\
\widehat{\vartheta}|_{L\oplus H_{\mathbf{u}}}=\psi\end{subarray}}\;\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\vartheta^{\prime}\in s(\mathbf{w})\\
\widehat{\vartheta}^{\prime}|_{L^{\prime}\oplus H_{\mathbf{w}}}=\psi^{\prime}\end{subarray}}Q_{\mathbf{u},\vartheta}\cdot Q_{\mathbf{w},\vartheta^{\prime}}=O,

as needed. We conclude that the quantum assignment

\psi^{\prime}:L^{\prime}\oplus H_{\mathbf{w}}\to\mathbb{F}_{2}

Suppose, for the sake of contradiction, that
\tilde{\mathbf{u}}\cap\tilde{\mathbf{w}}=\emptyset
. It follows that
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{\tilde{\mathbf{u}}}\cap\mathbb{F}_{2}^{\tilde{\mathbf{w}}}=\{\mathbf{0}\}

and

We now need to extend quantum completeness to the second part

.
We have

\displaystyle\operatorname{qsat}_{\mathfrak{W}}(\Phi)-\operatorname{qsat}_{\mathfrak{Q}}(\Phi)=\frac{1}{\dim(\mathbb{H})}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}\sim\pi}\sum_{\mathbf{a}\in\phi_{\mathbf{x}}}\tr(M_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{a}}).

(24)

We claim that
\tr(M_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{a}})\geq 0
for each
\mathbf{x}\in E
and each
\mathbf{a}\in\phi_{\mathbf{x}}
. Suppose for concreteness that
i=1
and
j=2
, so
\tilde{x}\in X_{1}
,
\tilde{a}\in A_{2}
, and
\hat{a}\in A_{1}
(the other case is analogous).
If
x_{1}\neq\tilde{x}
or
a_{1}\not\in\{\tilde{a},\hat{a}\}
, then
M_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{a}}=O
and the claim is true, so we can assume that
x_{1}=\tilde{x}
and
a_{1}\in\{\tilde{a},\hat{a}\}
.
Since
\mathbf{a}\in\phi_{\mathbf{x}}\subseteq A_{1}\times A_{2}
, it must hold that
a_{1}\in A_{1}
, so
a_{1}\neq\tilde{a}
. The only remaining case is when
x_{1}=\tilde{x}
and
a_{1}=\hat{a}
. We obtain

\displaystyle M_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{a}}=W_{\tilde{x},\hat{a}}W_{x_{2},a_{2}}-Q_{\tilde{x},\hat{a}}Q_{x_{2},a_{2}}=(Q_{\tilde{x},\tilde{a}}+Q_{\tilde{x},\hat{a}})Q_{x_{2},a_{2}}-Q_{\tilde{x},\hat{a}}Q_{x_{2},a_{2}}=Q_{\tilde{x},\tilde{a}}Q_{x_{2},a_{2}}.

Since the trace of the product of two positive semidefinite matrices is nonnegative, it follows that
\tr(M_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{a}})\geq 0
, as required. As a consequence, (
24
) implies that
\operatorname{qsat}_{\mathfrak{W}}(\Phi)\geq\operatorname{qsat}_{\mathfrak{Q}}(\Phi)
.

Observe now that
|S_{\mathfrak{W}}|=|S_{\mathfrak{Q}}|-1
. Hence, after repeating the process for a finite number of times, we end up with a
k
-compatible quantum assignment satisfying the conditions of the proposition.
∎

Lemma 41
.

There is an (efficient) procedure that, given as input an unweighted left-regular
d
-to-
1
instance
\Phi
, outputs an
unweighted
d
-to-
d
instance
\Phi^{\prime}
satisfying the following:

1.

If
\Phi
admits a perfect
2k
-compatible quantum assignment then
\Phi^{\prime}
admits a perfect
k
-compatible quantum assignment;

2.

for any
\epsilon>0
and any
t\in\mathbb{N}
, if
\operatorname{isat}_{t}(\Phi^{\prime})\geq\epsilon
, then
\operatorname{sat}(\Phi)\geq\frac{\epsilon}{t^{2}}
.

Proof.

Let
\Phi=(X_{1}\sqcup X_{2},E,A,\phi)
be the given
d
-to-
1
instance.
Recall from the definition of
d
-to-
1
games in Section
3.1
that the alphabet
A
can be decomposed as
A=A_{1}\sqcup A_{2}
in a way that
\phi_{\mathbf{x}}\subseteq A_{1}\times A_{2}
for each
\mathbf{x}\in E
.
Following
[
DMR09
]
, we consider the unweighted label-cover instance
\Phi^{\prime}=(X_{1},E^{\prime},A_{1},\phi^{\prime})
defined as follows. For each pair
(x,y)
,
(x^{\prime},y)
of edges in
E
sharing a variable in
X_{2}
, we add the edge
(x,x^{\prime})
in
E^{\prime}
. The corresponding set of admitted labellings is

\displaystyle\phi^{\prime}_{(x,x^{\prime})}=\{(a,a^{\prime})\in A_{1}^{2}\mbox{ s.t. }\exists b\in A_{2}:\,(a,b)\in\phi_{(x,y)},\,(a^{\prime},b)\in\phi_{(x^{\prime},y)}\}.

It is easy to check that all constraints of
\Phi^{\prime}
are
d
-to-
d
, and it was shown in
[
DMR09
, Lemma A.9]
that condition
2.
holds.

We now show that condition
1.
holds, too.
Take a perfect
2k
-compatible quantum assignment
\mathfrak{Q}=\{Q_{x,a}:\,x\in X_{1}\sqcup X_{2},a\in A\}
for
\Phi
over some Hilbert space
\mathbb{H}
.
Using Proposition
40
, we can assume without loss of generality that
Q_{x,a}=O
whenever
x\in X_{i}
and
a\in A_{j}
for
i\neq j\in[2]
.
We define
W_{x,a}=Q_{x,a}
for each
x\in X_{1}
and each
a\in A_{1}
. Note that

\displaystyle\sum_{a\in A_{1}}W_{x,a}=\sum_{a\in A_{1}}Q_{x,a}=\sum_{a\in A}Q_{x,a}=\id_{\mathbb{H}},

where the second equality comes from the second condition in Proposition
40
.
Hence, the set
\mathfrak{W}=\{W_{x,a}\mbox{ s.t. }x\in X_{1},a\in A_{1}\}
is a quantum assignment for
\Phi^{\prime}
.

Consider now an edge
(x,x^{\prime})\in E^{\prime}
, and let
y
be the corresponding vertex of
X_{2}
such that
(x,y)
and
(x^{\prime},y)
are both in
E
. Take
a pair
(a,a^{\prime})\in A_{1}^{2}\setminus\phi^{\prime}_{(x,x^{\prime})}
, which means that, for each
b\in A_{2}
, either
(a,b)\not\in\phi_{(x,y)}
or
(a^{\prime},b)\not\in\phi_{(x^{\prime},y)}
.
Since the quantum assignment
\mathfrak{Q}
is perfect, for each
b\in A_{2}
it holds that

\displaystyle Q_{x,a}\cdot Q_{y,b}\neq O\quad\Rightarrow\quad(a,b)\in\phi_{(x,y)}\quad\Rightarrow\quad(a^{\prime},b)\not\in\phi_{(x^{\prime},y)}\quad\Rightarrow\quad Q_{x^{\prime},a^{\prime}}\cdot Q_{y,b}=O,

which means that

\displaystyle Q_{x,a}\cdot Q_{y,b}\cdot Q_{x^{\prime},a^{\prime}}\cdot Q_{y,b}=O.

Since that the vertices
x^{\prime}
and
y
are adjacent in
\Phi
, the corresponding projectors of
\mathfrak{Q}
commute, so we obtain

\displaystyle Q_{x,a}\cdot Q_{x^{\prime},a^{\prime}}\cdot Q_{y,b}=O

for each
b\in A_{2}
. Taking the sum over
A_{2}
(and using again the second condition of Proposition
40
), we find

\displaystyle O

\displaystyle=\sum_{b\in A_{2}}Q_{x,a}\cdot Q_{x^{\prime},a^{\prime}}\cdot Q_{y,b}=Q_{x,a}\cdot Q_{x^{\prime},a^{\prime}}\cdot\sum_{b\in A_{2}}Q_{y,b}=Q_{x,a}\cdot Q_{x^{\prime},a^{\prime}}\cdot\id_{\mathbb{H}}

\displaystyle=Q_{x,a}\cdot Q_{x^{\prime},a^{\prime}}=W_{x,a}\cdot W_{x^{\prime},a^{\prime}},

whence we deduce that the quantum assignment
\mathfrak{W}
is perfect.

Finally, it is clear from the definition of
\Phi^{\prime}
that
\operatorname{dist}_{\Phi^{\prime}}(x,x^{\prime})=\frac{1}{2}\cdot\operatorname{dist}_{\Phi}(x,x^{\prime})
for each pair of vertices
x,x^{\prime}\in X_{1}
. Hence, the
k
-compatibility of
\mathfrak{W}
follows directly from the
2k
-compatibility of
\mathfrak{Q}
, thus concluding the proof.
∎

Proof of Proposition
24
.

Fix

as in the statement of the proposition, and consider the parameters
\delta=\frac{\epsilon}{3t^{2}}
,
\epsilon^{\prime}=\frac{\epsilon}{3d\lceil 1/\delta\rceil^{2}t^{2}}
,
\epsilon^{\prime\prime}=\frac{\epsilon^{\prime}}{2}
, and
\epsilon^{\prime\prime\prime}=\frac{\delta\epsilon^{\prime\prime}}{2}

Using Conjecture
2
, let
\Phi
be a

-to-

\Phi

2k
-compatible quantum assignment
but

We now consecutively apply to

38
,
39
, and
41
.
Note that the transformation in Lemma
38
does not change the constraints in the input label-cover instance. Hence, it maps
d
-to-
1
instances to
d
-to-
1
instances. Let
\Phi^{\prime}
be the result of applying Lemma

to
\Phi
with parameter

; let
\Phi^{\prime\prime}
be the result of applying Lemma

to
\Phi^{\prime}

and

to
\Phi^{\prime\prime}
. We claim that the resulting

-to-

\Phi^{\prime\prime\prime}
