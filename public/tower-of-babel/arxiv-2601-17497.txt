On the Impossibility of Simulation Security for Quantum Functional Encryption
Mohammed Barhoush, Arthur Mehta, Anne Müller, Louis Salvail

Abstract
Functional encryption is a powerful cryptographic primitive that enables fine-grained access to encrypted data and underlies numerous applications. Although the ideal security notion for 𝖥𝖤 \mathsf{FE} —simulation security—has been shown to be impossible in the classical setting, those impossibility results rely on inherently classical arguments. This leaves open the question of whether simulation-secure functional encryption can be achieved in the quantum regime. In this work, we rule out this possibility by showing that the classical impossibility results largely extend to the quantum world. In particular, when the adversary can issue an unbounded number of challenge messages, we prove an unconditional impossibility, matching the classical barrier. In the case where the adversary may obtain many functional keys, classical arguments only yield impossibility under the assumption of pseudorandom functions; we strengthen this by proving impossibility under the potentially weaker assumption of pseudorandom quantum states. In the same setting, we also establish an alternative impossibility based on public-key encryption. Since public-key encryption is not known to imply pseudorandom quantum states, this provides independent evidence of the barrier. As part of our proofs, we show a novel incompressibility property for pseudorandom states, which may be of independent interest.



1
Introduction

Traditional encryption offers an all-or-nothing guarantee: a ciphertext can either be fully decrypted or not at all. While this suffices for basic confidentiality, it falls short in modern applications—such as cloud services, data outsourcing, or fine-grained access control—where different parties should be able to access only specific aspects of the data. Functional encryption (FE) was proposed as a solution to this limitation, first informally introduced by Sahai and Waters
[
24
]
and later developed into a formal framework by Boneh, Sahai and Waters
[
4
]
.

In an
\mathsf{FE}
scheme, a master secret key
mk
enables the generation of functional keys
{\mathsf{\vphantom{p}fk}}_{F}
associated with functions
F
from a given family. Possession of
{\mathsf{\vphantom{p}fk}}_{F}
allows a user to compute
F(m)
from an encryption of
m
, but nothing beyond this prescribed output. The precise interpretation of “nothing beyond” has been the subject of extensive study
[
4
,
1
]
, and plays a central role in shaping both the feasibility and the strength of
\mathsf{FE}
constructions.

Two central paradigms have emerged for formalizing security in this context: simulation-based security (
SIM
–security) and the weaker but more permissive indistinguishability-based security (
IND
–security). Each of these can be combined with additional dimensions: adaptive versus non-adaptive queries to the functional-key derivation oracle, single versus multiple challenge ciphertexts, and single versus multiple functional key queries. Together, these refinements yield 16 distinct security notions, denoted by
q\text{-}x\text{-}y\text{-}z
, where
q\in\{1,\textsf{M}\}
specifies a single or many ciphertext queries,
x\in\{1,\textsf{M}\}
a single or multiple functional key queries,
y\in\{\textsf{NA},\textsf{AD}\}
the adaptivity, and
z\in\{\textsf{IND},\textsf{SIM}\}
the underlying framework.

M

Among these variants,
SIM
–security is the stronger and more desirable goal, since
IND
–security becomes vacuous for certain classes of functionalities
[

. Unfortunately, a series of impossibility results
[

SIM
–secure (classical)

\textsf{M}\text{-}1\text{-}\textsf{AD}

SIM

1\text{-}\textsf{M}\text{-}\textsf{NA}

In essence, this means that security breaks down once the adversary is allowed either numerous non-adaptive functional key queries or, alternatively, multiple ciphertext queries together with a single adaptive functional key query.

Crucially, these impossibility results apply only in the
classical
setting. Their proofs rely on inherently classical arguments that do not directly carry over to the quantum world. This raises a central question:

Do the classical impossibilities of
SIM
–secure
\mathsf{FE}
generalize to the quantum regime?

The history of quantum cryptography offers strong evidence that classical impossibility does not always imply quantum impossibility. A range of primitives—such as unclonable encryption, certified deletion, unforgeable money—are provably impossible classically, yet attainable quantumly. A particularly notable example is the recent result showing that
quantum
public keys enable public key encryption from one-way functions
[
3
]
, in contrast to the classical setting where a separation is known
[
12
]
. These examples suggest that quantum resources can overcome barriers that appear absolute in the classical world. By the same analogy, it is natural to ask whether quantum public keys, ciphertexts, or functional keys might provide the extra layer of security needed to realize simulation-based notions of functional encryption.

Given the foundational role of
\mathsf{FE}
in cryptography, determining whether quantum resources can overcome these classical barriers is a central question for the theory of quantum cryptography.

1.2
Related Works

Several earlier works explored the feasibility of
\mathsf{QFE}
schemes in different settings. Hiroka et al.
[
10
]
construct certified everlasting functional encryption by enhancing
\mathsf{FE}
for classical circuits with quantum ciphertexts. A receiver holding a quantum ciphertext can later produce a certificate proving that the ciphertext has been irreversibly destroyed, along with any embedded information such as the plaintext.
Beyond deletion,
[
15
]
introduce
\mathsf{FE}
with secure key leasing from any secret-key
\mathsf{FE}
. Under the assumption of sub-exponentially secure indistinguishability obfuscation and sub-exponential LWE they construct single-decryptor
\mathsf{FE}
secure against bounded collisions: the adversary can only obtain one functional key and the keys are copy-protected in the sense that the adversary cannot create a second usable copy. The work of Çakan and Goyal
[
7
]
improves upon this result by building
\mathsf{FE}
with copy-protected secret keys that withstand unbounded collisions from similar assumptions as
[
15
]
. This result does not contradict our impossibility result regarding many key-queries since they achieve
IND
–security and we rule out that such a construction could achieve
SIM
–security.

Barhoush and Salvail
[
2
]
explore
\mathsf{QFE}
in the bounded-quantum-storage-model, where adversaries have limited quantum memory, and provide
SIM
–secure construction. Note that this model differs from the plain model and the impossibility results presented in this work do not apply to this model.

Mehta and Müller
[
18
]
initiated a broader formal investigation of
\mathsf{QFE}
. They create definitions for simulation and indistinguishability based notions of
\mathsf{QFE}
and provide a construction for single-query, single-message adaptive
SIM
–secure
\mathsf{QFE}
. They show that, equivalently to the classical case, the simulation-based notion is stronger and implies the indistinguishability-based notion. Their construction is not succinct.
Our work builds on this emerging line of research by focusing on simulation-based security.

1.3
Impossibilities of Quantum Functional Encryption for Classical Functionalities

Our results complement the classical literature of impossibility results for
\mathsf{FE}

[
1
,
4
,
22
]
by showing several impossibilities for
\mathsf{QFE}
. An open question remains whether quantum capabilities such as quantum computation and quantum ciphertexts could help circumvent the impossibilities if only classical functionality is required. For our first impossibility result, the adversary only makes use of classical messages and the identity functionality, which is a classical function. Therefore, the impossibility of many-message single-query adaptive
SIM
–secure
\mathsf{QFE}
for classical functionalities but with quantum capabilities immediately follows. In the second impossibility result, we rely on the compressibility of
\mathsf{PRS}
, an inherently quantum primitive. This could be replaced by a
\mathsf{PRF}
and a similar argument regarding the incompressibility of classical information as in
Lemma

4
. Therefore, the impossibility of succinct single-message, single-query non-adaptive
SIM
–secure
\mathsf{FE}
for classical circuits in a quantum world also follows. The reason we opted to use
\mathsf{PRS}
instead of
\mathsf{PRF}
is to get an impossibility result from a weaker assumption. The third result assumes the existence of a
\mathsf{PKE}
scheme with classical keys. If another condition of classical ciphertexts is assumed then the required functionality is also completely classical and an impossibility result for single-message many-query
\mathsf{QFE}
for classical functionalities follows. As a
\mathsf{PKE}
scheme with quantum ciphertexts can be built from weaker assumptions as a
\mathsf{PKE}
with classical ciphertexts, we opted to allow for quantum ciphertexts in our proof.

1.4
Future Directions

While this work establishes several impossibility results for simulation-secure
QFE
, several intriguing avenues remain for future exploration:

•

Our impossibility results target
QFE
for general circuit classes. However, restricted classes may still permit simulation security. For instance, O’Neill
[
22
]
constructed non-adaptive
SIM
–secure
\mathsf{FE}
for a class known as “preimage sampleable” circuits. Future research should investigate which specific subclasses of quantum circuits bypass our impossibility barriers.

•

Moreover, the robustness of our results currently hinges on specific cryptographic assumptions. Strengthening these findings by weakening or entirely removing these assumptions would provide a more fundamental understanding of the boundaries of
\mathsf{QFE}
.

•

Finally, our results leave open the specific regime where the adversary receives a single ciphertext and a single non-adaptive functional key query (prior to the challenge), followed by multiple adaptive queries. Given that De Caro et al.
[
9
]
demonstrated the feasibility of this setting in the classical model, a primary direction for future work is to determine if their construction (or a quantum-resistant variant) maintains security in the quantum realm.

2
Technical Overview

We describe our impossibility results more concretely. Throughout this paper we work with secret-key
\mathsf{QFE}
in the simulation-based security setting. We allow the ciphertexts and functional keys to be quantum while the master secret-key is classical. The impossibilities for secret-key
\mathsf{QFE}
immediately imply the equivalent impossibilities for public key
\mathsf{QFE}
, even with quantum public keys.

2.1
Impossibility of
SIM
–security with many ciphertext queries

Our first results demonstrates the impossibility of
SIM
–secure
\mathsf{QFE}
in the setting where the adversary receives many ciphertext queries and a single adaptive functional key query. This is a quantum version of the classical impossibility result given in
[
4
]
. More formally, we show the following:

Theorem 2.1
(informal)

There does not exist a
\textsf{M}\text{-}1\text{-}\textsf{AD}

SIM
–secure
\mathsf{QFE}
scheme.

The proof proceeds via the following straightforward attack strategy. The adversary begins by requesting the encryption of
n
random messages
(m_{i})_{i\in[n]}
, where
n
is some sufficiently large polynomial in the security parameter. After receiving the corresponding ciphertexts from the challenger, the adversary then requests the
functional key for the identity function
I
. This key enables recovery of all messages directly from the ciphertexts. Thus, the adversary only makes a
single
adaptive functional-key query, and receives
many
challenge ciphertexts.

Assuming the reader’s familiarity with the
SIM
–security game (
Definition

7
), we now describe why this interaction is hard to simulate. Observe that since the
functional key is queried
after
the challenge messages, the simulator must
first produce
n
ciphertexts without any knowledge of the underlying messages.
Suppose the simulator outputs ciphertexts
(\mathsf{ct}_{i})_{i\in[n]}
. When the adversary
subsequently requests the functional key for
I
, the simulator receives the
messages
(m_{i})_{i\in[n]}
and must construct a state
\mathsf{fk}_{I}
such that

\textsf{Dec}(\mathsf{fk}_{I},\mathsf{ct}_{i})=m_{i}\quad\text{for all }i\in[n].

However, two fundamental issues arise:

1.

The size of
\mathsf{fk}_{I}
is bounded by a fixed polynomial. Hence, as long as
n
is chosen large enough,
\mathsf{fk}_{I}
is of much smaller size.

2.

The ciphertexts
(\mathsf{ct}_{i})_{i\in[n]}
were
generated independently of the messages
(m_{i})_{i\in[n]}
.

Together, these imply that
\mathsf{fk}_{I}
cannot contain enough information to
describe a mapping from each ciphertext to its corresponding message.
Classically, this impossibility is straightforward. In the quantum setting,
however, one must contend with the fact that both ciphertexts and functional
keys may be quantum states, which in principle can encode much more information
than classical strings.

To address this issue, we found that it is useful to reinterpret the simulator’s task as an
information
compression problem
. Upon receiving the
n
messages, the simulator would need
to compress them into a quantum state
\mathsf{\vphantom{p}fk}_{I}
of size strictly less than
n
qubits, in
such a way that this compressed state, when combined with independently chosen
ciphertexts, could still be used to perfectly recover all
n
messages.

We show that such a compression procedure is impossible, even in the quantum
setting, by using a related result from
[
20
]
. This establishes the impossibility of
\textsf{M}\text{-}1\text{-}\textsf{AD}

SIM
–secure
\mathsf{QFE}
.

Interestingly, we obtain an impossibility result for another variant of
\mathsf{QFE}
using our incompressibility result along with
[
1
]
. Recall that Agrawal et al.
[
1
]
showed that in the classical setting
1\text{-}\textsf{M}\text{-}\textsf{NA}

SIM
–secure
\mathsf{FE}
is impossible assuming the existence of
\mathsf{PRF}
2
2

2

Their result is actually unconditional, given that
\mathsf{FE}
implies
\mathsf{PRF}
in the classical setting.
. This result only applies to classical schemes as it relies on a classical incompressibility result. With our quantum incompressibility result, we can adapt their result to the quantum setting with minimal modifications obtaining impossibility of
1\text{-}\textsf{M}\text{-}\textsf{NA}

SIM
–secure
\mathsf{QFE}
, assuming the existence of
\mathsf{PRF}
s.

2.2
Impossibility of
SIM
–security for succinct schemes

In the second result, we show the impossibility of succinct
SIM
–secure
\mathsf{QFE}
in the simplest setting where only one message and one non-adaptive function query are allowed. The high-level idea is inspired by the classical impossibility result of
[
1
]
:

1.

Show that there exists a class of incompressible circuits.

2.

Show that a succinct
\mathsf{QFE}
scheme for such a class of circuits implies a compression and decompression algorithm for this class of circuits.

We show that a
\mathsf{PRS}
generator is an incompressible circuit, therefore, we obtain the following theorem.

Theorem 2.2
(informal)

Assuming the existence of
\mathsf{PRS}
, there does not exist a succinct
1\text{-}1\text{-}\textsf{NA}

SIM
–secure secure
\mathsf{QFE}
scheme.

Recall that the security of a
\mathsf{PRS}
is defined with respect to Haar random states, which is the uniform distribution over pure quantum states of a given dimension. A
\mathsf{PRS}
is secure if an adversary cannot distinguish whether he is given a polynomial amount of copies of a Haar random state or a
\mathsf{PRS}
. We use this relationship to work with Haar random states instead of
\mathsf{PRS}
in the first step of the proof. In particular, we show that there do not exist CPTP maps
\mathsf{Comp},\mathsf{Decomp}
such that a Haar random state can undergo the compression
\mathsf{Comp}
and then be recovered by the decompression
\mathsf{Decomp}
with high fidelity.

Lemma 1
(informal)

Let
m<n
. There do not exist CPTP channels
\mathsf{Comp}:D(\mathcal{H}^{n})\rightarrow D(\mathcal{H}^{m}),\mathsf{Decomp}:D(\mathcal{H}^{m})\rightarrow D(\mathcal{H}^{n})
such that

\Pr\left[F(|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|,\mathsf{Decomp}\circ\mathsf{Comp}(|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|))\geq 1-\mathsf{negl}\left(n\right)\right]\geq\frac{1}{\mathsf{poly}\left(n\right)}

where the probability is taken over choosing a Haar random state.

This Lemma can be seen as a quantum analog of the classical fact that uniformly random strings cannot be compressed. Note that in this Theorem the compression does not depend on the number of qubits by which we compress the state. Even a compression of just a single qubit makes it nearly impossible to recover the state. This is in contrast to the classical setting where removing a single bit from a random string leaves an adversary with a high chance of recovering the string by guessing the missing bit.

The proof of the incompressibility of Haar random states proceeds as follows. First, we compute the expected fidelity
\mathsf{F}_{\mathsf{avg}}
of the channel
\Phi=\mathsf{Decomp}\circ\mathsf{Comp}
applied to a Haar random state by relating it to the entanglement fidelity
\mathsf{F}_{\mathsf{E}}
. This allows us to analyze the effect of the map
\Phi
on one half of the maximally entangled state instead of a Haar random state. Formally the expected fidelity and the entanglement fidelity are related as
\mathsf{F}_{\mathsf{avg}}=\frac{2^{n}\mathsf{F}_{\mathsf{E}}+1}{2^{n}+1}
. A key step in the proof is the decomposition of the CPTP maps
\mathsf{Comp}
and
\mathsf{Decomp}
in their Kraus representations
\{A_{i}\}
and
\{B_{i}\}
. Due to the required dimension of the maps we know that
A_{i}
outputs states in a
2^{m}
-dimensional space and
B_{i}
, acting on this space, also has outputs contained in a
2^{m}
-dimensional subspace. We conclude that the combined map
\Phi
has rank at most
2^{m}
which we can use to show that the entanglement fidelity is
\mathsf{F}_{\mathsf{E}}=\frac{2^{m}}{2^{n}}
. Therefore the average fidelity is
\mathsf{F}_{\mathsf{avg}}(\Phi)\leq\frac{1}{2^{n-m}}+O(\frac{1}{2^{n}})
. Finally, we can apply Levy’s Lemma, which states that if a function
f
does not change too abruptly (a Lipschitz function), then its value on a Haar random state
f(|\psi\rangle)
does not deviate much from the expected value of the function. The probability of a deviation by
\epsilon
is exponentially small in
\epsilon^{2}
and the dimension
2^{n}
. For a successful compression and decompression algorithm we expect the fidelity to be close to 1 but we have just computed that the expected fidelity
\mathsf{F}_{\mathsf{avg}}(\Phi)
is much lower, i.e. even for only a single qubit of compression
m=n-1
we get
\mathsf{F}_{\mathsf{avg}}(\Phi)\leq\frac{1}{2}+O(\frac{1}{2^{n}})
. Due to Levy’s Lemma we see that the fidelity on a random state cannot deviate much and we conclude that
\mathsf{Enc},\mathsf{Dec}
with the required properties cannot exist.

To finish the first step in our proof, we need to define a class of incompressible circuits. From the previous Lemma, it easily follows that
\mathsf{PRS}
are incompressible. If there was a compression and decompression algorithm for pseudo-random states, then we could distinguish
\mathsf{PRS}
from Haar random states by applying the algorithms and checking if we got the correct state back. Note that to check if we get the correct state then we need at least one additional copy of the
\mathsf{PRS}
or Haar random state to apply a Swap test. Then the adversary can guess not with certainty but with high enough probability if he was given a
\mathsf{PRS}
or a truly random state.

What remains is the second step in the proof: Showing that a
\mathsf{QFE}
scheme implies a compression and decompression algorithm for
\mathsf{PRS}
. Assuming the reader’s familiarity with the
SIM
–security game (
Definition

6
) we give a brief description of the procedures
\mathsf{Comp}
and
\mathsf{Decomp}
based on the simulator of the security game. In the non-adaptive security game the adversary first requests a function secret key for the circuit
\mathsf{PRS}.\mathsf{Gen}(\cdot):\{0,1\}^{\lambda}\rightarrow D(\mathcal{H}^{s})
which is the generator for a pseudorandom state. Then the adversary samples a random key
k\in\{0,1\}^{\lambda}
and requests an encryption of this key. The simulator is now given the output of the circuit applied to the key
\mathsf{PRS}.\mathsf{Gen}(k)=|\psi_{k}\rangle
and needs to produce a ciphertext of size
t
. Now, if
s>t
then this is exactly the task of compressing
|\psi_{k}\rangle
with knowledge of the state and the circuit but not the key
k
, which we showed to be impossible. Decompression of the state is the same as applying the decryption algorithm of the
\mathsf{QFE}
scheme given the ciphertext and the function secret key.

2.3
Impossibility of
SIM
–security with many functional-key queries

Our final result demonstrates the impossibility of
SIM
–secure
\mathsf{QFE}
in the setting where the adversary receives one ciphertext query and many non-adaptive functional key queries, assuming
\mathsf{PKE}
. More formally, we show the following:

Theorem 2.3
(informal)

Assuming the existence of
\mathsf{PKE}
with classical public keys, there does not exist a
1\text{-}\textsf{M}\text{-}\textsf{NA}

SIM
–secure
\mathsf{QFE}
scheme.

High-level intuition.

In our reduction, we generate functional keys that compute
public key encryptions
for a
MK-CPA
secure
\mathsf{PKE}
scheme under many independent public keys. The correctness of a
\mathsf{QFE}
scheme ensures that a ciphertext of a message
M
(under the
\mathsf{QFE}
scheme) can be used along with the functional keys to obtain encryptions of
M
instead under the
\mathsf{PKE}
scheme with the various public keys. We move from a case where all the ciphertexts encrypt a
single
message
M
(under different public keys) to a hybrid where each ciphertext encrypts a
distinct
message. Indistinguishability of the
\mathsf{PKE}
scheme then forces the
\mathsf{QFE}
simulator to produce one short
\mathsf{QFE}
ciphertext that, when evaluated via the functional keys, yields many valid
\mathsf{PKE}
ciphertexts that yield all distinct messages. This turns the simulator into an
information compressor
: from a single object it must enable recovery of
n
independent encrypted messages. This violates basic information-theoretic limits, yielding a contradiction.

From
IND-CPA
to
IND-MK-CPA
.

We first formalize a convenient strengthening of
\mathsf{PKE}
security, which we call
IND-MK-CPA
: roughly, security must hold even when the adversary simultaneously receives challenge ciphertexts under
many
independently generated public keys. A standard hybrid argument shows that ordinary
IND-CPA
implies
IND-MK-CPA
.

Attack against
IND-MK-CPA
.

Assume for contradiction that there exists a
1\text{-}\textsf{M}\text{-}\textsf{NA}

SIM
–secure
\mathsf{QFE}
scheme given by the algorithms
(\mathsf{Setup},

\mathsf{KeyGen},

\mathsf{Enc},\mathsf{Dec})
(see

3.1
Notation

Let
\mathcal{H}^{d}
be a
d-
dimensional Hilbert space. Let
D(\mathcal{H})
denote the set of density operators on

. A quantum transformation is a completely positive trace preserving (CPTP) map which maps a density operator to a density operator. The trace distance for two density matrices is defined as
\mathsf{T}(\rho,\sigma):=\frac{1}{2}\|\rho-\sigma\|_{1}=\frac{1}{2}\mathsf{Tr}[\sqrt{(\rho-\sigma)^{{\dagger}}(\rho-\sigma)}]
, where
\mathsf{Tr}
denotes the trace operator.
Throughout this paper we use the following notation for quantum states:
\mathsf{\vphantom{p}sk},\mathsf{ct},\mathsf{m}
, especially when a definition allows for either classical or quantum keys, messages, etc. For specifically classical elements we use
sk,m,ct
. For explicitly quantum states we also use
\rho,\sigma
or the bra-ket notation
|\phi\rangle
.

Lemma 2

(Gentle Measurement
[
26
]
)

Let
\rho
be quantum state and let
\{\Pi,\mathbb{I}-\Pi\}
be a projective measurement such that
\mathsf{Tr}(\Pi\rho)\geq 1-\delta
. Then the post measurement state post-selected on obtaining the first outcome is
\rho^{\prime}=\frac{\Pi\rho\Pi}{\mathsf{Tr}(\Pi\rho)}
. It holds that
\mathsf{T}(\rho^{\prime},\rho)\leq 2\sqrt{\delta}
.

Fact 1
(Trace distance vs. Euclidean distance for pure states)

Let
|\psi\rangle,|\phi\rangle
be unit vectors in
\mathbb{C}^{d}
.

{\lvert\psi\rangle}{\langle\psi\rvert}=|\phi\rangle\langle\phi|
. Then

\|{\lvert\phi\rangle}{\langle\phi\rvert}-{\lvert\psi\rangle}{\langle\psi\rvert}\|_{1}\leq 2\|{\lvert\psi\rangle}-{\lvert\phi\rangle}\|_{2}.

Proof

The trace distance between two pure states admits the closed form

\tfrac{1}{2}\|{\lvert\phi\rangle}{\langle\phi\rvert}-{\lvert\psi\rangle}{\langle\psi\rvert}\|_{1}=\sqrt{\,1-|\langle\psi|\phi\rangle|^{2}\,}.

On the other hand, the Euclidean distance satisfies

\|{\lvert\psi\rangle}-{\lvert\phi\rangle}\|_{2}^{2}=2\big(1-\mathrm{Re}\,\langle\psi|\phi\rangle\big)\;\geq\;2\big(1-|\langle\psi|\phi\rangle|\big).

For
c=|\langle\psi|\phi\rangle|\in[0,1]
we have

1-c^{2}=(1-c)(1+c)\leq 2(1-c)
.
Combining these relations yields

\sqrt{\,1-|\langle\psi|\phi\rangle|^{2}\,}\;\leq\;\||\psi\rangle-|\phi\rangle\|_{2},

and multiplying both sides by 2 gives the claim. ∎

(\tilde{\mathsf{ct}}_{i})_{i\in[n]}\leftarrow\mathsf{Sim}_{1}(1^{\lambda},mk,1^{n})
for
n
messages without knowing any evaluations. Next,
\mathcal{A}_{2}
requests the functional key for the identity function. Therefore,
\mathsf{Sim}_{2}
receives the messages
m\coloneqq(m_{1},\ldots,m_{n})
and outputs a
p
-qubit state
\tilde{\textsf{fk}}_{I}\leftarrow\mathsf{Sim}_{2}(1^{\lambda},mk,I,m,1^{n})
.

Set
\tilde{\mathsf{ct}}\coloneqq\otimes_{i\in[n]}\tilde{\mathsf{ct}}_{i}
. Consider the operation of
\mathcal{A}_{2}
: it takes
\tilde{\mathsf{\vphantom{p}fk}}_{I}
and
\tilde{\mathsf{ct}}
as input, computes
\tilde{m}_{1}\leftarrow\textsf{Dec}(\tilde{\textsf{fk}}_{I},\tilde{\mathsf{ct}}_{1})
, inverts the operation to retrieve a state close to
\tilde{\mathsf{\vphantom{p}fk}}_{I}
, and repeats this process for each
i\in[n]
to obtain
\tilde{m}\coloneqq(\tilde{m}_{1},\ldots,\tilde{m}_{n})
.

For the simulation to be indistinguishable, we require that the outcome of this operation satisfies
\tilde{m}=m
with at least
1-\mathsf{negl}\left(\lambda\right)
probability.

On the other hand, since
(m_{i})_{i\in[n]}
are sampled at random, there are
2^{n}
possible values for these messages. In essence, this means that the
\mathsf{Sim}
and
\mathcal{A}
give a pair of non-uniform compression circuits (with advice
\tilde{\mathsf{ct}}
and
mk
) as follows.

The compression circuit
\mathsf{Comp}(m;mk)
, with advice
mk
, computes
\tilde{\mathsf{\vphantom{p}fk}}_{I}\leftarrow\mathsf{Sim}_{2}(1^{\lambda},mk,I,m,1^{n})
and outputs the result
\tilde{\mathsf{\vphantom{p}fk}}_{I}
. Meanwhile,
\mathsf{Decomp}(\tilde{\mathsf{\vphantom{p}fk}}_{I};\tilde{\mathsf{ct}})
, with advice
\tilde{\mathsf{ct}}
, decrypts the ciphertexts
\tilde{\mathsf{ct}}
using
\tilde{\mathsf{\vphantom{p}fk}}_{I}
: it computes
\tilde{m}_{1}\leftarrow\textsf{Dec}(\tilde{\textsf{fk}}_{I},\tilde{\mathsf{ct}}_{1})
, inverts the operation to retrieve a state close to
\tilde{\mathsf{\vphantom{p}fk}}_{I}
, and repeats this process for each
i\in[n]
to obtain
\tilde{m}\coloneqq(\tilde{m}_{1},\ldots,\tilde{m}_{n})
.

We already deduced that
\Pr[\tilde{m}=m]\geq 1-\mathsf{negl}\left(\lambda\right)
and given that
n>2p
, this means that
(\mathsf{Comp},\mathsf{Decomp})
are
\left(n,\frac{n}{2}+1\right)
-compression circuits. But this contradicts
Lemma

4
, so
\textsf{M}\text{-}1\text{-}\textsf{AD}

SIM
–secure
\mathsf{QFE}
schemes do not exist.
∎

Interestingly, we obtain another impossibility result for
\mathsf{QFE}
from
Lemma

4
and
[

]
. Recall that Agrawal et al.
[
1
]
showed that in the classical setting
1\text{-}\textsf{M}\text{-}\textsf{NA}

SIM
–secure
\mathsf{FE}
is impossible assuming the existence of
\mathsf{PRF}
. Notably this result only applies to classical schemes as it relies on an incompressibility result for classical information. With
Lemma

4
, we have an incompressibility result for quantum information that can be used to adapt their result to the quantum setting with minimal modifications to the proof, giving the following theorem.

Theorem 4.2

There does not exist a
1\text{-}\textsf{M}\text{-}\textsf{NA}

SIM
–secure
\mathsf{QFE}
scheme assuming the existence of
\mathsf{PRF}
s.

5
Impossibility of Simulation-Security for Succinct Schemes

In this section, we show the impossibility of succinct
1\text{-}1\text{-}\textsf{NA}

SIM
–secure
\mathsf{QFE}
. The proof proceeds by showing that there is a class of incompressible circuits and that a succinct
\mathsf{QFE}
scheme would constitute a compression scheme for this class of circuits. The incompressible circuit class is the generator circuit for pseudorandom states. To show their incompressibility, we first show that Haar random states, which are indistinguishable from
\mathsf{PRS}
for a bounded adversary, are incompressible.

For the proof, we will need the following Lemma, which can for example be found in
[
25
,
23
]
.

Lemma 5
(Levy’s Lemma )

Given a Lipschitz function
f:\mathbb{S}^{r}\to\mathbb{R}
defined on the

r
-dimensional hypersphere
\mathbb{S}^{r}
, and a point

\psi\in\mathbb{S}^{r}
chosen uniformly at random,

\Pr\big[|f(\psi)-\mathbb{E}f|\geq\epsilon\big]\leq 2\exp\!\left(\frac{-2C(r+1)\epsilon^{2}}{\eta^{2}}\right),

where
\eta
is the Lipschitz constant of
f
, and
C
is a positive constant (which can be taken to be
C=(18\pi^{3})^{-1}
).

Pure states
{\lvert\psi\rangle}
sampled Haar randomly from a space of dimension
d
are in correspondence with points
\psi\in\mathbb{S}^{2d-1}
sampled uniformly at random.

Lemma 6
(Incompressibility of Haar Random States)

Let
n,m\in\mathbb{N}
such that
m<n
and let
d=2^{n},k=2^{m}
. There do not exist CPTP channels
\mathsf{Comp}:D(\mathcal{H}^{n})\rightarrow D(\mathcal{H}^{m}),\mathsf{Decomp}:D(\mathcal{H}^{m})\rightarrow D(\mathcal{H}^{n})
such that

\Pr\left[F(|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|,\mathsf{Decomp}\circ\mathsf{Comp}(|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|))\geq 1-\mathsf{negl}\left(n\right)\right]\geq\frac{1}{\mathsf{poly}\left(n\right)}

where the probability is taken over choosing a Haar random state.

Proof

Let
\Phi=\textsf{D}\circ\textsf{C}
, then the average fidelity of a state to itself after passing through this channel is

\mathsf{F}_{\mathsf{avg}}(\Phi)=\int d\psi\langle\psi|\Phi(|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|)|\psi\rangle

where the integral is over the Haar measure on
\mathbb{C}^{d}
.

The entanglement fidelity is defined as

\mathsf{F}_{\mathsf{E}}(\Phi)=\langle\phi^{+}|(\mathcal{I}\otimes\Phi)(|\phi^{+}\rangle\langle\phi^{+}|)|\phi^{+}\rangle

where
\phi^{+}
is the maximally entangled state in
d
dimensions
|\phi^{+}\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\sum_{i=1}^{d}|i\rangle\otimes|i\rangle
.

The following relationship between the average fidelity and the entanglement fidelity is described by Horodecki et.al.
[
11
]
and Nielsen
[
21
]
:

\mathsf{F}_{\mathsf{avg}}(\Phi)=\frac{d\;\mathsf{F}_{\mathsf{E}}(\Phi)+1}{d+1}\kern 5.0pt.

Let
\{A_{i}\}
be a Kraus representation of
\mathsf{Comp}
and let
\{B_{j}\}
be a Kraus representation of
\mathsf{Decomp}
. Let
\{C_{\alpha}\}
be the corresponding Kraus representation of
\Phi
, given by
C_{i,j}=B_{j}A_{i}
.

\displaystyle\mathsf{F}_{\mathsf{E}}(\Phi)

\displaystyle=\langle\phi^{+}|\sum_{\alpha}(\mathcal{I}\otimes C_{\alpha})(|\phi^{+}\rangle\langle\phi^{+}|)(\mathcal{I}\otimes C_{\alpha}^{\dagger})|\phi^{+}\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{\alpha}\langle\phi^{+}|(\mathcal{I}\otimes C_{\alpha})(|\phi^{+}\rangle\langle\phi^{+}|)(\mathcal{I}\otimes C_{\alpha}^{\dagger})|\phi^{+}\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{\alpha}\left|\langle\phi^{+}|(\mathcal{I}\otimes C_{\alpha})|\phi^{+}\rangle\right|^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\alpha}\left|\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\sum_{j=1}^{d}\langle j|\otimes\langle j|\right)(\mathcal{I}\otimes C_{\alpha})\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\sum_{i=1}^{d}|i\rangle\otimes|i\rangle\right)\right|^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\alpha}\left|\frac{1}{d}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{d}\langle j|\otimes\langle j|\right)\left(\sum_{i=1}^{d}|i\rangle\otimes C_{\alpha}|i\rangle\right)\right|^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\alpha}\left|\frac{1}{d}\sum_{j=1}^{d}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\underbrace{\langle j|i\rangle}_{0\text{ if }i\neq j}\otimes\langle j|C_{\alpha}|i\rangle\right|^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\alpha}\left|\frac{1}{d}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\langle i|C_{\alpha}|i\rangle\right|^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{d^{2}}\sum_{\alpha}\left|\mathsf{Tr}(C_{\alpha})\right|^{2}\kern 5.0pt.

Due to the compression requirement,
A_{i}
outputs states in a
k
-dimensional space, and since
B_{j}
acts on a
k
-dimensional space, the range of
B_{j}
is contained in a
k
-dimensional subspace of
\mathbb{C}^{d}
. Therefore, the operator
C_{ij}=B_{j}A_{i}
which is a Kraus operator for the composed channel
\Phi
has rank
(C_{ij})\leq k
.

Using the inequality
|\mathsf{Tr}(X)|^{2}\leq\text{rank}(X)\mathsf{Tr}(X^{\dagger}X)
and the fact that
\sum_{ij}C^{\dagger}_{ij}C_{ij}=\mathcal{I}_{d}
we get

\displaystyle\mathsf{F}_{\mathsf{E}}(\Phi)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{d^{2}}\sum_{i,j}\left|\mathsf{Tr}(C_{ij})\right|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{d^{2}}\sum_{i,j}k\mathsf{Tr}(C_{ij}^{\dagger}C_{ij})

\displaystyle=\frac{k}{d^{2}}\mathsf{Tr}(\mathcal{I}_{d})

\displaystyle=\frac{k}{d}\kern 5.0pt.

Therefore we get that the average fidelity of
\Phi
is

\mathsf{F}_{\mathsf{avg}}(\Phi)\leq\frac{d\frac{k}{d}+1}{d+1}=\frac{k+1}{d+1}\kern 5.0pt.

Plugging in
d=2^{n},k=2^{m}
we get

\displaystyle\mathsf{F}_{\mathsf{avg}}(\Phi)

\displaystyle=\frac{2^{m}+1}{2^{n}+1}

\displaystyle=\frac{2^{m}}{2^{n}}+\frac{1}{2^{n}+1}-\frac{\frac{2^{m}}{2^{n}}}{2^{n}+1}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2^{n-m}}+O\left(\frac{1}{2^{n}}\right)\kern 5.0pt.

In particular, if we decide to compress by only one qubit, which corresponds to setting
m=n-1
, we get

\mathsf{F}_{\mathsf{avg}}(\Phi)\leq\frac{1}{2}+O\left(\frac{1}{2^{n}}\right).

For the final step define the function
f:\mathbb{S}^{2d-1}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
as
f(\psi)={\langle\psi\rvert}\Phi({\lvert\psi\rangle}{\langle\psi\rvert}){\lvert\psi\rangle}
. We then see that
\mathbb{E}f=\mathsf{F}_{avg}
. Now, we show that
f
is 4-Lipschitz.

\displaystyle|f(\phi)-f(\psi)|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle|

\displaystyle\mathsf{Tr}[\Phi({\lvert\psi\rangle}{\langle\psi\rvert}){\lvert\psi\rangle}{\langle\psi\rvert}]-\mathsf{Tr}[\Phi({\lvert\phi\rangle}{\langle\phi\rvert}){\lvert\phi\rangle}{\langle\phi\rvert}]|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle|

\displaystyle\mathsf{Tr}\left[\Phi({\lvert\psi\rangle}{\langle\psi\rvert}){\lvert\psi\rangle}{\langle\psi\rvert}\right]-\mathsf{Tr}\left[\Phi({\lvert\phi\rangle}{\langle\phi\rvert}){\lvert\psi\rangle}{\langle\psi\rvert}\right]

\displaystyle+\mathsf{Tr}\left[\Phi({\lvert\phi\rangle}{\langle\phi\rvert}){\lvert\psi\rangle}{\langle\psi\rvert}\right]-\mathsf{Tr}\left[\Phi({\lvert\phi\rangle}{\langle\phi\rvert}){\lvert\phi\rangle}{\langle\phi\rvert}\right]|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle|

\displaystyle\mathsf{Tr}[(\Phi({\lvert\psi\rangle}{\langle\psi\rvert})-\Phi({\lvert\phi\rangle}{\langle\phi\rvert})){\lvert\psi\rangle}{\langle\psi\rvert}]+\mathsf{Tr}[\Phi({\lvert\phi\rangle}{\langle\phi\rvert})({\lvert\phi\rangle}{\langle\phi\rvert}-{\lvert\psi\rangle}{\langle\psi\rvert})]|\kern 5.0pt.

We can compute the first term by applying the Hölder inequality
\mathsf{Tr}(AB)\leq\lVert A\rVert_{1}\lVert B\rVert_{\infty}
.
We then get

\displaystyle\mathsf{Tr}[(\Phi({\lvert\psi\rangle}{\langle\psi\rvert})-\Phi({\lvert\phi\rangle}{\langle\phi\rvert})){\lvert\psi\rangle}{\langle\psi\rvert}]

\displaystyle=\mathsf{Tr}[(\Phi({\lvert\psi\rangle}{\langle\psi\rvert}-{\lvert\phi\rangle}{\langle\phi\rvert})){\lvert\psi\rangle}{\langle\psi\rvert}]

\displaystyle\leq\lVert\Phi({\lvert\psi\rangle}{\langle\psi\rvert}-{\lvert\phi\rangle}{\langle\phi\rvert})\rVert_{1}\lVert{\lvert\psi\rangle}{\langle\psi\rvert}\rVert_{\infty}\kern 5.0pt.

For pure states it holds that
|||\psi\rangle\langle\psi|\rVert_{\infty}=1
and since CPTP maps are contractive we have
\lVert\Phi({\lvert\psi\rangle}{\langle\psi\rvert}-{\lvert\phi\rangle}{\langle\phi\rvert})\rVert_{1}\leq\lVert{\lvert\psi\rangle}{\langle\psi\rvert}-{\lvert\phi\rangle}{\langle\phi\rvert}\rVert_{1}
.

We can compute the second term by making use of trace cyclicity and also applying the Hölder inequality:

\mathsf{Tr}[\Phi({\lvert\phi\rangle}{\langle\phi\rvert})({\lvert\phi\rangle}{\langle\phi\rvert}-{\lvert\psi\rangle}{\langle\psi\rvert})]\leq\lVert{\lvert\phi\rangle}{\langle\phi\rvert}-{\lvert\psi\rangle}{\langle\psi\rvert}\rVert_{1}\lVert\Phi({\lvert\phi\rangle}{\langle\phi\rvert})\rVert_{\infty}\kern 5.0pt.

For any density operator such as
\Phi({\lvert\phi\rangle}{\langle\phi\rvert})
it holds that
||\Phi({\lvert\phi\rangle}{\langle\phi\rvert})\rVert_{\infty}\leq 1
.
Therefore, using
1
,

\displaystyle|f(\phi)-f(\psi)|

\displaystyle\leq 2\lVert{\lvert\phi\rangle}{\langle\phi\rvert}-{\lvert\psi\rangle}{\langle\psi\rvert}\rVert_{1}

\displaystyle\leq 4\lVert{\lvert\phi\rangle}-{\lvert\psi\rangle}\rVert_{2}\kern 5.0pt,

which shows that
f
is 4-Lipschwitz.

Now, we can apply Levy’s Lemma

\Pr\big[|f(\psi)-\mathbb{E}f|\geq\epsilon\big]\leq 2\exp\!\left(\frac{-4Cd\epsilon^{2}}{4^{2}}\right)\kern 5.0pt,

where
d=2^{n}
and
C
is a constant as specified in
Lemma

5
.

Finally, in the Lemma statement we require the fidelity to be negligibly close to 1 which corresponds to
\epsilon\leq 1-\mathsf{negl}\left(n\right)-\frac{1}{2^{n-m}}-O\left(\frac{1}{2^{n}}\right)=1-\frac{1}{2^{n-m}}-\mathsf{negl}\left(n\right)
which makes the right hand term of the above equation negligible.

In particular, even for a single bit of compression
m=n-1,\epsilon=1-\frac{1}{2}-negl(n)
the success probability is negligible.

\displaystyle\Pr\big[|f(\psi)-\mathbb{E}f|\geq\epsilon\big]

\displaystyle\leq 2\exp\!\left(\frac{-C2^{n}(\frac{1}{2}-\mathsf{negl}\left(n\right))^{2}}{4}\right)

\displaystyle\leq 2\exp\left(-\Theta(2^{n})\right)\kern 5.0pt.

We conclude that there cannot exist CPTP maps
\mathsf{Comp}:D(\mathcal{H}^{n})\rightarrow D(\mathcal{H}^{m})
and
\mathsf{Decomp}:D(\mathcal{H}^{m})\rightarrow D(\mathcal{H}^{n})
, for any
m<n
, that achieve inverse polynomial success probability since they contradict the fact that any CPTP map achieves fidelity close to 1 only with negligible probability. ∎

Now we can relate the incompressibility of Haar random states to pseudorandom states.

Corollary 2
(Incompressibility of Pseudorandom States)

Let
\lambda\in\mathbb{N}
and let
n=\mathsf{poly}\left(\lambda\right)
. There do not exist QPT algorithms
\mathsf{Comp}:D(\mathcal{H}^{n})\rightarrow D(\mathcal{H}^{n-1}),\mathsf{Decomp}:D(\mathcal{H}^{n-1})\rightarrow D(\mathcal{H}^{n})
such that

\Pr_{k\leftarrow\{0,1\}^{p(\lambda)}}\left[T(|\psi_{k}\rangle\langle\psi_{k}|,\mathsf{Decomp}\circ\mathsf{Comp}(|\psi_{k}\rangle\langle\psi_{k}|))\leq\mathsf{negl}\left(\lambda\right)\right]\geq\frac{1}{\mathsf{poly}\left(\lambda\right)}

where
\mathsf{Gen}(1^{\lambda},k)=|\psi_{k}\rangle
.

Proof

Assume towards contradiction that there exist algorithms
\mathsf{Comp},\mathsf{Decomp}
that can compress and decompress pseudorandom states and achieve fidelity
F\geq 1-\mathsf{negl}\left(\lambda\right)
. These algorithms can then be used to break the pseudo-randomness property of
\mathsf{PRS}
as follows.
The adversary receives multiple copies of the
\mathsf{PRS}
state or Haar random state in the
\mathsf{PRS}
security game. Then the adversary against the pseudorandomness of
\mathsf{PRS}
can distinguish
\mathsf{PRS}
from Haar random states by applying
\mathsf{Comp}
and
\mathsf{Decomp}
and checking if the result is close to the input by applying a Swap test.
The Swap test accepts with probability
\frac{1}{2}(1+{\langle\psi\rvert}\Phi({\lvert\psi\rangle}{\langle\psi\rvert}){\lvert\psi\rangle})
where
|\psi\rangle
is the original state and
\Phi({\lvert\psi\rangle}{\langle\psi\rvert})
is the state after the compression and decompression algorithm was applied. Per assumption the fidelity is
F\geq 1-\mathsf{negl}\left(\lambda\right)
therefore the Swap test accepts with probability
\Pr[\mathsf{SWAP}\text{ accepts}|\psi\text{ is PRS}]=\frac{1}{2}(1+1-\mathsf{negl}\left(\lambda\right))
conditioned on the state being
\mathsf{PRS}
. On Haar random states, by
Lemma

6
, we have
\Pr[\mathsf{SWAP}\text{ accepts}|\psi\text{ is Haar}]=\frac{1}{2}(1+1-\frac{1}{\mathsf{poly}\left(n\right)})
.

Overall, the probability that the adversary in the
\mathsf{PRS}
security game wins is

\displaystyle\Pr[\mathcal{A}\text{ wins}]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\Pr[\mathsf{SWAP}\text{ accepts}|\psi\text{ is PRS}]+\frac{1}{2}\Pr[\mathsf{SWAP}\text{ rejects}|\psi\text{ is Haar}]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\Pr[\mathsf{SWAP}\text{ accepts}|\psi\text{ is PRS}]+\frac{1}{2}(1-\Pr[\mathsf{SWAP}\text{ accepts}|\psi\text{ is Haar}])

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\frac{1}{2}(1+1-\mathsf{negl}\left(\lambda\right))+\frac{1}{2}(1-\frac{1}{2}(1+1-\frac{1}{\mathsf{poly}\left(n\right)}))

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}-\frac{\mathsf{negl}\left(\lambda\right)}{4}+\frac{\frac{1}{\mathsf{poly}\left(n\right)}}{4}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{\mathsf{poly}\left(\lambda\right)}\kern 5.0pt.

Therefore, the adversary has non-negligible advantage in distinguishing
\mathsf{PRS}
from Haar random states and we conclude that there cannot exist compression and decompression algorithms that recover a
\mathsf{PRS}
with fidelity
1-\mathsf{negl}\left(\lambda\right)
with high probability.

We can relate the success probability in the fidelity to the success probability in the trace distance via the Fuchs-van de Graaf inequalities.

1-\sqrt{F(\rho,\sigma)}\leq T(\rho,\sigma)\kern 5.0pt.

Let
\rho=|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|
and
\sigma=\mathsf{Decomp}\circ\mathsf{Comp}(|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|)
, then

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\mathsf{poly}\left(n\right)}

\displaystyle\leq\Pr\left[F(\rho,\sigma))\geq 1-\mathsf{negl}\left(n\right)\right]

\displaystyle\leq\Pr\left[(1-\mathsf{Tr}(\rho,\sigma))^{2}\geq 1-\mathsf{negl}\left(n\right)\right]

\displaystyle\leq\Pr\left[1-\mathsf{Tr}(\rho,\sigma)\geq 1-\mathsf{negl}\left(n\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\Pr\left[\mathsf{Tr}(\rho,\sigma)\leq\mathsf{negl}\left(n\right)\right]\kern 5.0pt.

Therefore, if we have a compression and decompression algorithm for pseudorandom states that achieves a certain success probability in recovering the state with a fidelity of
1-\mathsf{negl}\left(\lambda\right)
, then these algorithms also achieve a negligible trace distance with the same success probability. ∎

Now we can show the main theorem of this section, the impossibility of succinct single-message, single-query
SIM
–secure
\mathsf{QFE}
.

Theorem 5.1

Assuming the existence of
\mathsf{PRS}
, there does not exist a succinct
1\text{-}1\text{-}\textsf{NA}

SIM
–secure
\mathsf{QFE}
scheme,
\mathsf{QFE}=(\mathsf{Setup},\mathsf{KeyGen},\mathsf{Enc},\mathsf{Dec})
,
where the messages, keys and ciphertexts may be quantum.

Proof

Assume
\mathsf{PRS}
exists. Assume towards contradiction that there also exists a succinct
1\text{-}1\text{-}\textsf{NA}

SIM
–secure
\mathsf{QFE}
scheme. Let
\lambda\in\mathbb{N}
be the security parameter and
\mathcal{M}
be the message space of the scheme with messages of size
\ell_{M}=\mathsf{poly}\left(\lambda\right)
. Let
\ell_{CT}=\mathsf{poly}\left(\lambda,\ell_{M}\right)
be the ciphertext size of the scheme. Such a scheme implies a compression and decompression function.

First fix a master secret key
mk
sampled from the
\mathsf{QFE}.\mathsf{Setup}
and
\mathsf{\vphantom{p}fk}_{C}\leftarrow\mathsf{QFE}.\mathsf{KeyGen}(mk,C)
, where
C=\mathsf{PRS}.\mathsf{Gen}:\{0,1\}^{\lambda}\rightarrow\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{C}^{s})
, for some
s=\mathsf{poly}\left(\lambda\right),s>\ell_{CT}
. For a given state
\rho
define
\mathsf{Comp}(\rho)
as follows:
The simulator
\mathsf{Sim}
obtains the master secret key mk, a description of the circuit
\mathsf{PRS}.\mathsf{Gen}
and the state
\rho\in\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{C}^{s})
and creates the ciphertext
\mathsf{ct}
. The ciphertext
\mathsf{ct}
is the output of the compression. More formally,

\mathsf{Comp}(\rho)=\mathsf{Sim}(1^{\lambda},mk,C,\rho)\kern 5.0pt.

The decompression
\mathsf{Decomp}
proceeds as follows. Given an input
\mathsf{ct}

\mathsf{Decomp}
runs the decryption algorithm of the
\mathsf{QFE}
scheme on
\mathsf{ct}
and the functional secret key
\mathsf{\vphantom{p}fk}_{C}
to obtain

\mathsf{Decomp}(\mathsf{ct})=\mathsf{QFE}.\mathsf{Dec}(\mathsf{\vphantom{p}fk}_{C},\mathsf{ct})\kern 5.0pt.

In the case that
\rho=\mathsf{PRS}.\mathsf{Gen}(k)
we see that
\mathsf{Comp}\circ\mathsf{Decomp}(\rho)
and
\rho
must be computationally indistinguishable. This follows based on the correctness of the
\mathsf{QFE}
scheme and the indistinguishability of the ideal and real world. To see that
\mathsf{Comp}\circ\mathsf{Decomp}(\rho)
acts as a compression procedure for the
\mathsf{PRS}
we must further show that these states are close in trace distance. To see this simply note that an efficient distinguisher in the SIM security game can obtain both a copy of both
\rho
and
\mathsf{Comp}\circ\mathsf{Decomp}(\rho)
at the same time, since the adversary chooses
k
and creates one copy of
\rho=\mathsf{PRS}.\mathsf{Gen}(k)
for himself and sends
k
to the simulator to obtain
\mathsf{ct}=\mathsf{Comp}\circ\mathsf{Decomp}(\rho)
. Now the adversary can apply the SWAP test to
\rho
and
\mathsf{ct}
which rejects with non-negligible probability if the trace distance is non-negligible. This means that in this situation computational indistinguishability must further imply that these states are close in trace distance. However, this contradicts the incompressibility of
\mathsf{PRS}
which we showed in
Corollary

2
. Therefore
1\text{-}1\text{-}\textsf{NA}

SIM
–secure
\mathsf{QFE}
cannot exist.

∎

Remark 1

In the above proof we use the fact that in
Definition

6
in the ideal world
\mathsf{QFE}.\mathsf{Setup}
and
\mathsf{QFE}.\mathsf{KeyGen}
are run honestly by the experiment and not controlled by the simulator. If we allow the simulator to control
\mathsf{KeyGen}
he can send an entangled state and later teleport the
\mathsf{PRS}
into the key, thereby circumventing the size restriction on the ciphertext. If the functional keys are classical, as in
[
18
]
, this problem does not arise and the alternative security definition is also excluded.

6
Impossibility of Simulation-Security under Many Functional-Key Queries

In this section, we establish the impossibility of
1\text{-}\mathsf{M}\text{-}\mathsf{NA}

SIM
–secure
\mathsf{QFE}
under the assumption that
\mathsf{PKE}
exists. We refer the reader to the technical overview (
Section

2
) for an intuitive description of the proof to aid readability.

Note that the earlier impossibility result (
Theorem

5.1
) relied on
\mathsf{PRS}
and applied only to succinct schemes. By contrast, our new impossibility extends to the broader class of non-succinct schemes, though it requires a different assumption—namely,
\mathsf{PKE}
. Importantly,
\mathsf{PKE}
and
\mathsf{PRS}
are not directly comparable
[
8
,
19
]
: while a separation between
\mathsf{PKE}
and
\mathsf{PRS}
is known
[
16
]
, there is neither an implication nor a separation in the reverse direction. Thus, the two results represent independent approaches to establishing barriers for
\mathsf{QFE}
.

Theorem 6.1

Assuming the existence of public-key encryption with classical keys, there does not exist a (secret-key)
1\text{-}\textsf{M}\text{-}\textsf{NA}

SIM
–secure
\mathsf{QFE}
scheme.

Proof

Assume for contradiction that there exists a (secret-key)
1\text{-}\textsf{M}\text{-}\textsf{NA}

SIM
–secure
\mathsf{QFE}
scheme
\Pi_{\mathsf{QFE}}=(\mathsf{Setup},\mathsf{KeyGen},\mathsf{Enc},\mathsf{Dec})
.

Also, assume that there exists a
CPA
–secure
\mathsf{PKE}
scheme. This implies the existence of a
MK-CPA
–secure
\mathsf{PKE}
scheme
\Pi_{\mathsf{PKE}}=(\mathsf{PKE.Gen},\mathsf{PKE.Enc},\mathsf{PKE.Dec})
by
Lemma

7
.

Since
\mathsf{Enc}
is a QPT algorithm, there exists a polynomial
q
such that the size of the output of
\mathsf{Enc}
is bounded by
q(\lambda)

Remark 2

Lemma 7

If a
\mathsf{PKE}
scheme is
CPA
–secure then it is
MK-CPA
–secure.

Proof

Let
\Pi=(\mathsf{KeyGen},\mathsf{Enc},\mathsf{Dec})
be a
\mathsf{PKE}
scheme. Assume the scheme is
CPA
–secure but not
MK-CPA
secure for the sake of obtaining a contradiction. Then, there exists a QPT adversary
\mathcal{A}
such that

\displaystyle\Pr[\mathsf{PKE}^{\textnormal{IND-MK-CPA}}_{\Pi,\mathcal{A}}({\lambda})=1]>\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{\mathsf{poly}\left(\lambda\right)}

for infinitely many
\lambda
.
This implies that in the security experiment
\mathsf{PKE}^{\textnormal{IND-MK-CPA}}_{\Pi,\mathcal{A}}({\lambda})
,

\begin{split}\left|\Pr[\mathcal{A}(\right.\hskip-2.84544pt&\left.\{\mathsf{ct}_{i}\}_{i\in[n]})=1:\mathsf{ct}_{i}\leftarrow\mathsf{Enc}(pk_{i},m_{0,i})\,\,\forall i\in[n]]\right.\\
&\quad\left.-\Pr[\mathcal{A}(\{\mathsf{ct}_{i}\}_{i\in[n]})=1:\mathsf{ct}_{i}\leftarrow\mathsf{Enc}(pk_{i},m_{1,i})\ \forall i\in[n]]\right|>\delta\end{split}

(2)

for some function
\delta=\delta(\lambda)\in\frac{1}{\mathsf{poly}\left(\lambda\right)}
and infinitely many
\lambda
.

Define
\mathbf{H}_{j}
for
j\in[n]
to be a hybrid of the security experiment
\mathsf{PKE}^{\textnormal{IND-MK-CPA}}_{\Pi,\mathcal{A}}({\lambda})
, but where the challenge ciphertexts are generated instead in the following way. If

, then
\mathsf{ct}_{i}\leftarrow\mathsf{Enc}(pk_{i},m_{0,i})
and if
i<j
, then
\mathsf{ct}_{i}\leftarrow\mathsf{Enc}(pk_{i},m_{1,i})
. Let the output of the hybrid be the output of
\mathcal{A}
when run on these ciphertexts.

Let

\displaystyle\epsilon_{j}\coloneqq\left|\Pr[\mathbf{H}_{j}=1]-\Pr[\mathbf{H}_{j-1}=1]\right|.

(3)

Set

. We have

\displaystyle\delta

\displaystyle<\left|\Pr[\mathbf{H}_{n}=1]-\Pr[\mathbf{H}_{1}=1]\right|

(4)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left|\Pr[\mathbf{H}_{j}=1]-\Pr[\mathbf{H}_{j-1}=1]\right|

(5)

\displaystyle\leq n\cdot\epsilon_{j^{*}}.

(6)

Therefore,
\epsilon_{j^{*}}>\frac{\delta}{n}
. We can now design a QPT algorithm
\mathcal{D}
that breaks

–security as follows.

\mathcal{D}

.
