Prevalence and Maintenance of Clinical Remission in Patients with Severe Uncontrolled Asthma Undergoing Biologic Therapies
Authors: Elena Dorado-Fonseca, Julia García de Pedro, Zichen Ji, Beatriz Recio-Moreno, Laura de Miguel-Rodríguez, María Jesús Buendía-García, Javier de Miguel-Díez
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractClinical remission is an emerging treatment goal in severe asthma, although real-world data on its long-term maintenance remain limited. We retrospectively evaluated 50 patients who initiated first-line biologic therapy with omalizumab, mepolizumab, or reslizumab between 2015 and 2019. Clinical remission at 12 months was defined as no moderate or severe exacerbations, an asthma control test (ACT) score of ≥20, post-bronchodilator forced expiratory volume in 1 second of ≥80% predicted, and no use of maintenance oral corticosteroid. Of these patients, 13 (26.0%) met the criteria for remission. They had significantly higher unadjusted baseline forced vital capacities (FVC; 103.6% vs. 85.7%, p = 0.002) and ACT scores (17.5 vs. 11.0, p < 0.001), and 6 (46.2%) maintained remission over a median follow-up of 63.0 months. Although remission was achieved in approximately one-quarter of patients, it was maintained in fewer than half. Our baseline FVC and ACT findings require confirmation in larger studies.Keywords: Severe asthma, Clinical remission, Biologic therapy

Biologic therapies have improved the management of severe asthma by reducing exacerbations and systemic corticosteroid exposure and improving symptom control and lung function.1, 2 Consequently, clinical remission has emerged as a potential treatment goal. However, its definitions and reported frequencies remain heterogeneous, particularly in retrospective real-world cohorts.3 The Spanish Consensus on Remission in Asthma (REMAS) defines clinical remission during treatment as sustained disease control, no exacerbations or need for systemic corticosteroids, preserved lung function, and a negative bronchodilator response for at least 12 months.4

In this study, we assessed the proportion of patients who met the REMAS definition of clinical remission 1 year after initiating first-line biologic therapy, explored their baseline characteristics, and evaluated the long-term maintenance of clinical remission. This study was intended as an exploratory description rather than an analysis of independent predictors or comparative effectiveness.

This single-center retrospective observational study included patients with severe uncontrolled asthma who initiated first-line biologic therapy with omalizumab, mepolizumab, or reslizumab at Gregorio Marañon General University Hospital between 2015 and 2019. Those previously treated with monoclonal antibodies or lost to follow-up during the first year of treatment were excluded.

The following data were obtained from patients’ electronic medical records at baseline and at 12 months: symptoms, smoking status, asthma control test (ACT) score, moderate and severe exacerbations, maintenance oral corticosteroid (OCS) treatment, spirometry results, serum eosinophil and immunoglobulin E (IgE) levels, and fractional exhaled nitric oxide (FeNO). Study-defined remission at 12 months was defined as no moderate or severe exacerbations, an ACT score of ≥20, post-bronchodilator forced expiratory volume in 1 second (FEV1) of ≥80% predicted, and no use of maintenance OCS at 12 months. We evaluate “study-defined remission” because a negative bronchodilator response, required by the REMAS, was not systematically available.

Patients who met the inclusion criteria were followed through 2025. Continuous variables were compared between remission and non-remission groups using Student's t-test or the Mann–Whitney U test, and categorical variables using the chi-square or Fisher's exact test. Given the small size of the cohort and remission group (n = 13), no multivariable model was fitted. All comparisons are unadjusted and exploratory, and drug-specific maintenance data are descriptive.

This study was approved by the Research Ethics Committee of Gregorio Marañon General University Hospital (approval no.: 02/2026; approval date: January 19, 2026). The requirement for informed consent was waived due to the study's use of retrospective anonymized data that was analyzed in aggregate, in accordance with national regulations.

This study included 50 patients with a mean age of 52.5 ± 13.0 years, of whom 13 (26.0%) were male, and 15 (30.0%) were active smokers. At baseline, the mean FEV1 was 75.3 ± 19.9% predicted, the mean FVC was 90.3 ± 17.2% predicted, and the median ACT score was 13.5 (interquartile range [IQR]: 10.0–18.0). Eighteen patients (36.0%) received maintenance OCS before starting first-line biologic therapy. Biologic therapy was omalizumab in 23 patients (46.0%), mepolizumab in 16 (32.0%), and reslizumab in 11 (22.0%).

Thirteen patients (26.0%) met the study-defined remission criteria at 12 months: 5/16 patients (31.3%) on mepolizumab, 5/11 (45.5%) on reslizumab, and 3/23 (13.0%) receiving omalizumab. Baseline clinical and functional characteristics are compared between the remission and non-remission groups in Table 1. At baseline, FVC (103.6% vs. 85.7% predicted, p = 0.002) and ACT scores (17.5 vs. 11.0, p < 0.001) were significantly higher in the remission group than in the non-remission group, whereas FEV1 was numerically but not significantly higher (84.1% vs. 72.2%, p = 0.065). No other examined variables differed significantly between groups.

In this real-world cohort, approximately one-quarter of patients met the study definition of clinical remission after 1 year of biologic therapy. This proportion is broadly consistent with, though slightly below, pooled estimates indicating that approximately 30–38% of biologic-treated patients attain clinical remission depending on the definition applied.5 Almost half of those who met the study definition for clinical remission remained in remission over prolonged follow-up, supporting the view that remission is a dynamic rather than invariably sustained state.6, 7 However, this estimate applies only to the small subgroup who achieved clinical remission at 12 months and should not be extrapolated to the entire cohort.

At baseline, ACT scores were higher in the remission group, consistent with previous cohorts in which better initial symptom control was associated with subsequent remission.5, 8 However, this finding does not establish the ACT score as an independent predictor of remission. Similarly, FVC was higher in the remission group at baseline, though this finding requires particular caution. Although FEV1 showed a near-significant difference, the analysis was unadjusted, and FVC may be influenced by obesity, air trapping, smoking, and spirometric technique.9 Without lung-volume measurements or adjustment for confounding factors, our FVC finding is hypothesis-generating and should not be assigned a specific pathophysiological meaning.

Our findings cannot be compared across biologic agents because they target different phenotypes, are administered via different routes, and have very small subgroup sizes. Since detailed data on phenotype or endotype, adherence, treatment interruptions, discontinuation, and switching were not consistently available, clinical remission cannot be attributed to a specific agent, as our analysis cannot rule out differences in phenotype, exposure, or baseline severity. The EXACTO (exacerbations, ACT, systemic corticosteroids, and obstruction-FEV1) scale was not prespecified and could not be applied reliably.

However, our study had several limitations that should be acknowledged, including its retrospective single-center design, small cohort, lack of multivariable adjustment, and incomplete availability of asthma duration, body mass index, nasal polyposis, atopy, and psychiatric comorbidity. In addition, its definition of clinical remission did not include a negative bronchodilator test and therefore remains less stringent than the REMAS definition. Nevertheless, its long observation period describes maintenance of clinical remission at 12 months, an outcome less often reported than attainment at a single time point.

In conclusion, clinical remission was observed in approximately one-quarter of patients and was maintained in fewer than half of these patients during long-term follow-up. Although baseline FVC and ACT scores were higher among patients who achieved clinical remission, these unadjusted exploratory findings require confirmation in larger, phenotype-characterized cohorts using current consensus criteria.
