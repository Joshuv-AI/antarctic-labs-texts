Recurrent Nephrolithiasis and Beyond: The Long Diagnostic Odyssey of a Case of CLDN16 Mutation
Authors: Abdullah Al Noman Bhuiyan, Nazia Akter, Tahniyah Haq, Md. Fariduddin, Shahjada Selim
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTFamilial Hypomagnesemia with Hypercalciuria and Nephrocalcinosis (FHHNC) is a rare tubulopathy resulting from mutations in the CLDN16 and CLDN19 genes. The affected individuals commonly present with polyuria, polydipsia, excessive urinary magnesium (Mg) and calcium (Ca) wasting, nephrocalcinosis, nephrolithiasis, recurrent urinary tract infections, and renal dysfunction. We herein report a 22‐year‐old male born to consanguineous parents presenting with recurrent nephrolithiasis since early childhood. He gave a previous history of muscle cramps, tetany, and seizures on multiple occasions with reported hypocalcemia and hypomagnesemia. He had no ocular symptoms or any history of salt wasting. His family history is noncontributory. Laboratory parameters demonstrated persistent hypomagnesemia, hypercalciuria, secondary hyperparathyroidism, and mild renal impairment. Ultrasound and plain X‐ray kidney–ureter–bladder (KUB) region illustrated bilateral nephrolithiasis and nephrocalcinosis. Genetic study identified a homozygous missense mutation in CLDN16 which correlates with the patient's clinical features. Mild renal dysfunction indicates that this patient may retain partial CLDN16 function. Treatment focused on high fluid intake with dietary changes along with potassium citrate and magnesium supplementation. FHHNC may go unnoticed during the evaluation of recurrent renal stones. Hence, this case report highlights the need for careful assessment of patients presenting with recurrent nephrolithiasis manifesting from an early age. Early distinction of the disease from other inherited tubulopathies may decelerate the progression of end‐stage renal disease (ESRD) in FHHNC patients.

Introduction

Familial Hypomagnesemia with Hypercalciuria and Nephrocalcinosis (FHHNC) is a rare renal tubulopathy with autosomal recessive inheritance caused by a loss‐of‐function mutation in the CLDN16 or CLDN19 genes [1]. These genes encode the tight‐junction proteins claudin‐16 and claudin‐19, respectively, that are expressed in the thick ascending limb of the loop of Henle (TAL) and are essential in modulating the paracellular transport of Mg and Ca [2]. Patients typically present with renal Mg wasting with persistent hypomagnesemia, hypercalciuria, nephrocalcinosis, nephrolithiasis, and varying degrees of renal impairment [3]. FHHNC patients possess a substantial risk of developing progressive renal failure, and around one‐third of the patients reach ESRD already during adolescence [4, 5]. Patients with CLDN16 and CLDN19 mutations exhibit comparable renal phenotypes; however, claudin‐19 proteins are expressed in the nervous system and retina in addition to TAL which might be attributed to extra‐renal manifestations of CLDN19 patients [6]. We, hereby, present a case of FHHNC type 1 in a young male with recurrent renal stones.

Case History/Examination

A 22‐year‐old male, 2nd issue of consanguineous parents, was referred to us for evaluation of recurrent renal stones with persistent hypomagnesemia, marked hypercalciuria, and recurrent hypocalcemia. His medical history revealed a longstanding issue with renal stones starting at age 3 years and 8 months. Since then, he has had multiple stone removal procedures including percutaneous nephrolithotomy 2 times on both sides, open nephrolithotomy 1 time on the right side, cystolithotomy 1 time, and extracorporeal shock wave lithotripsy 9 times. He also has a history of recurrent urinary tract infections, occasional muscle cramps, tetany, and seizures with documented hypomagnesemia and hypocalcemia requiring hospital admission on multiple occasions and was treated with intravenous and oral supplementations in outside clinics. However, the diagnostic workup was inconclusive. He has no family history of similar types of illness and his two siblings are otherwise healthy.

Physical examination was unremarkable except for scar marks on the right loin and suprapubic region from stone removal procedures; height–167 cm, weight–61 kg, body mass index–21.8 kg/m2. Ophthalmological and hearing assessments revealed no abnormalities.

Methods

Biochemical and Imaging Tests

We performed laboratory evaluation at presentation (Table 1), and it showed low serum Ca and Mg, raised serum creatinine, normal serum sodium and potassium, and raised intact parathyroid hormone (PTH). Serum inorganic phosphate and uric acid were normal; vitamin D was 18.1 ng/mL (20–50 ng/mL). On routine urine microscopic examination, there was ++ proteinuria, pus cells 20–30/HPF, RBC 10–12/HPF, and urinary pH was 6.5. Afterwards, 24 h urinary Ca and Mg were measured, and it revealed excessive urinary Ca and Mg wasting. Ultrasound kidney–ureter‐bladder (KUB) showed bilateral early renal parenchymal disease, bilateral nephrocalcinosis and multiple renal calculi (Figure 1). Plain X‐ray KUB also demonstrated bilateral multiple renal calculi (Figure 2).

Genetic Testing Results

Based on biochemical and imaging findings, a provisional diagnosis of FHHNC was made. Our differentials were autosomal dominant hypocalcemia, Dent disease, Bartter syndrome and Gitelman syndrome. Thereby we opted for a genetic study for confirmation. A peripheral blood sample was collected from the patient. Next‐generation whole exome sequencing (WES) was done. The DNA extracted from the patient's blood sample was enriched for the targeted regions using a protocol based on hybridization and then used for preparing the exome library. The genomic DNA was utilized to perform targeted gene capture of the GRCh38 genome assembly to sequence the protein coding genes. The libraries were sequenced to have a mean coverage of > 90× on the Illumina sequencing platform. The CLDN16 gene of the patient exhibits a homozygous variation (OMIM: 248250) of c.437G>A (p.Arg146His) in exon 4. According to the American College of Medical Genetics classification the variant was classified as likely pathogenic [7] In silico prediction tools predict the identified variant to be damaging by DEOGEN2, LIST‐S2, MutPred, BLOSUM, MVP, PrimateAI, PROVEAN, SIFT, DANN, EIGEN, FATHMM, LRT, M‐CAP, MutationTaster and Mutation Assessor. Hence, he was diagnosed with FHHNC type 1 due to the CLDN16 mutation with autosomal recessive inheritance. This variant has been reported 4 times as pathogenic/likely pathogenic in the ClinVar database. The p.Arg146His variant in CLDN16 was seen in 6/1613938 alleles in the Genome Aggregation Database (gnomAD v4.1.0).

Treatment

We treated the patient with dietary modifications including high fluid intake, low salt and sugar, and normal Ca in the diet. Oral Mg supplementation was provided in the form of magnesium oxide along with potassium citrate. Vitamin D in the form of cholecalciferol was also added. Genetic counseling was done.

Family Screening

Family screening was advised to his parents and 2 siblings with serum Mg, serum Ca, 24 h urinary Mg and Ca (Table 2) as well as ultrasound KUB region; however, all the reports were unremarkable. Unfortunately, we could not perform genetic study of family members due to financial constraints.

Outcome and Follow‐Up

At 2 months follow‐up of the patient (Table 3), serum Ca became normal, serum Mg was significantly increased, serum creatinine was 1.63 mg/dL (0.6–1.3 mg/dL) and vitamin D was 28.1 ng/mL. As serum vitamin D was sufficient oral cholecalciferol supplementation was stopped; after 1 year, serum creatinine was 1.84 mg/dL (eGFR‐ 52 mL/min/1.73m2) and intact PTH was 173 pg/mL (11–67 pg/mL). During this period of follow‐up, there was no recurrence of symptoms; serum calcium was within the reference range; however, serum magnesium levels remained below the normal range. The patient was adherent to the treatment provided with no adverse events. The full biochemical follow‐up of the patient is summarized in Table 3. Discussion

With a prevalence of fewer than 1 in 1,000,000 individuals, FHHNC is a rare genetic disorder, and only around 200 cases have been identified globally [8, 9]. Michelis et al. first described FHHNC in 1972 (Michelis‐Castrillo syndrome) [10]. Rodríguez‐Soriano et al. were the first to associate FHHNC with defects in Mg and Ca reabsorption in the TAL [11], the primary site of the nephron responsible for the paracellular transport of these cations [12, 13]. Following the analysis of 12 families with FHHNC, Simon et al. identified a gene, PCLN1 (now described as CLDN16), linked to the disorder [14]. Afterward, Konrad et al. studied a cohort of FHHNC patients presenting with severe ocular defects without mutations in CLDN16, leading to the identification of CLDN19 as a second gene implicated in the disease [6].

Usual presenting features are polyuria, polydipsia, recurrent urinary tract infections, and nephrolithiasis [1, 3]. Additionally, tetany, seizures, rickets, gouty arthritis, arterial hypertension, and chondrocalcinosis have been reported previously [9]. Ocular abnormalities like severe myopia, nystagmus, chorioretinitis, and macular colobomata have been described in CLDN19 disease [1]. Our patient presented to us with a history of recurrent renal stones and urinary tract infections with no ocular abnormalities. He also had a history of occasional tetany and seizures.

Hypocalcemia is not commonly observed in these patients, even with considerable urinary Ca excretion, as it is mitigated by increased intestinal calcium absorption and enhanced calcium mobilization from bones [15]. However, in our case, there is a history of several episodes of documented symptomatic hypocalcemia during the course of the illness. This might be attributed to severe renal calcium wasting exceeding PTH's ability to maintain normocalcemia. Concurrent vitamin D deficiency could also have contributed, as serum calcium levels normalized following its correction. Unlike our patient, hyperuricemia has been reported in the majority of previously identified cases [3, 16]. ABG analysis of our patient ruled out acidosis; although, some patients have incomplete distal renal tubular acidosis [17]. Urinary acidification defects along with hypercalciuria and hypomagnesaemia lead to nephrocalcinosis and nephrolithiasis in FHHNC [1]. Although serum Ca level was normalized and vitamin D was corrected upon follow‐up, our patient, who is in the early stage of renal failure, showed a persistent rise in PTH levels. Several studies have documented elevated serum PTH levels in the initial stages of the disease, irrespective of any decline in glomerular filtration rate [3, 5]. PTH stimulation, occurring even before chronic kidney disease (CKD) manifests, is attributed to renal Ca wasting with the propensity to hypocalcemia [3]. As there is no defect in the tubular transport of potassium and chloride, hypokalemia is absent in this context, unlike Bartter syndrome and Gitelman syndrome [3]. FHHNC differs from most other tubulopathies as it carries a high risk of progressive renal failure, with nearly one‐third of patients developing ESRD in their adolescence [1]. Our patient was at CKD stage 3A (eGFR‐ 52 mL/min/1.73m2) at 23 years and had a slower progression of renal failure than most of the previously documented cases.

We performed whole exome sequencing to confirm the diagnosis, and it revealed a homozygous guanine to adenine substitution at position 437 of the open reading frame in exon 4 of the CLDN16 gene. This single nucleotide change resulted in a single amino acid substitution from arginine to histidine at position 146 of claudin 16 protein which was consistent with FHHNC type 1. This variant has not been reported previously in the literature. Studies have demonstrated that individuals carrying a partial loss of function variant (PLOF) on at least one allele exhibit a slower decline in renal function, with mean annual GFR reductions of 7.3 (5.0–9.6) mL/min/1.73 m2 in complete loss of function (CLOF) compared to 2.9 (1.6–4.9) mL/min/1.73 m2 in PLOF. The mean age at symptom onset occurred considerably earlier in CLOF mutations (2.2 years, 95% CI 1.1–3.3) compared to PLOF mutations (5.6 years, 95% CI 3.6–7.0) [18]. Even though the nature of the p.Arg146His mutation was not characterized by functional analysis, the age of symptom onset and relatively preserved kidney function in our patient suggest that this mutation is consistent with partial loss of function, which may result in residual claudin‐16 activity. Similarly, a Chinese case with homozygous Leu116Val (conserved motif) had delayed onset and mild phenotype, attributed to partial residual function [19].

As of now, no established guidelines exist for the management of patients with FHHNC. Thus, the therapeutic approach relies primarily on supportive measures. The patient was treated essentially with high fluid intake, low salt, and a normal Ca diet along with Mg supplementation and potassium citrate. Owing to the risk of volume depletion and a further reduction in serum Mg levels we didn't advocate thiazides in our case. Though it was widely used in many of the previously reported cases, some authors stated that thiazides didn't effectively reduce urinary Ca excretion [3, 5].

Currently, there is limited published data on FHHNC from South Asia, with only a few genetically confirmed cases reported from India. The limitation is partly due to unawareness of the condition and lack of accessibility of genetic testing on a broad scale. One such report detailed two siblings, one of whom had a nonsense mutation in the CLDN16 gene (c.620G>A) and exhibited recurrent nephrolithiasis, which mirrored the clinical presentation of our case [15]. A pair of siblings with a novel missense mutation in exon 4 of CLDN16 (c.392G>A) were reported in another case, where the older one presented with recurrent gross hematuria and the younger one with hypomagnesemia and nephrocalcinosis, underscoring the region's phenotypic overlap and genotypic variability [20]. To better understand the spectrum of FHHNC and direct early intervention, our case contributes to this small pool of data, highlighting the necessity of greater awareness, genetic screening, and regional documentation. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first reported case of FHHNC from Bangladesh.

Conclusion

FHHNC, though a rare disease, can be overlooked in patients presenting with more typical issues like recurrent nephrolithiasis. Clinicians should have a high index of suspicion for rare tubulopathies, particularly in patients with unexplained early‐onset recurrent renal stones. Early differentiation of FHHNC from other inherited tubulopathies by genetic testing is crucial to avoid unnecessary investigations and interventions as well as to delay the onset of ESRD, which is the major cause of mortality in these patients. However, mild renal dysfunction in this case may indicate a partial loss‐of‐function mutation, resulting in residual claudin‐16 activity and a slower disease progression.
