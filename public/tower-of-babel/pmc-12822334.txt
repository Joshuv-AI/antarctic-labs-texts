Neuropathic pain in knee osteoarthritis: functional burden beyond cartilage thickness—a cross-sectional study
Authors: P. Shadan Javad, Ravi Gaur, Nitesh Manohar Gonnade, T. K. Abins, Nagma Sheenam, M. K. Jamshiya Ashraf
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveKnee osteoarthritis (OA) is a common degenerative condition leading to pain and disability. While structural changes such as cartilage loss are central to OA pathology, neuropathic pain (NP) may also contribute to symptom severity and outcomes. This study cross-sectional study aimed to evaluate the association between femoral cartilage thickness and NP in patients with knee OA, and to assess its impact on pain, function, and quality of life.ResultsSixty patients with knee OA were evaluated and classified into NP and non-NP groups using the painDETECT questionnaire (30 per group). Compared with non-NP patients, those with NP reported significantly higher pain scores (VAS: 4.70 vs. 3.37, p < 0.001), worse functional impairment (WOMAC: 71.0 vs. 55.9, p = 0.002), and lower quality of life across several SF-36 domains, particularly Role Emotional (p = 0.004) and Bodily Pain (p < 0.001). Femoral cartilage thickness at the medial condyle, intercondylar area, and lateral condyle did not differ significantly between groups. Trends toward higher obesity, more physically demanding occupations, and greater Kellgren–Lawrence severity were observed among NP patients. These findings suggest that NP adversely influences pain and function in knee OA independent of cartilage thickness, underscoring the importance of early identification and targeted management.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13104-025-07589-2.

Introduction

Locomotion is a fundamental aspect of human mobility, serving as the cornerstone for many activities of daily living (ADLs) such as walking, sitting, and standing. Efficient locomotion promotes energy conservation, independence, and overall quality of life [1]. Impairments in locomotion, especially in older adults and those with chronic conditions, can significantly affect physical and mental well-being and increase fall risk [2, 3]. The knee joint, as a central load-bearing and shock-absorbing structure, plays a key role in gait and coordination [4]. However, degenerative conditions like osteoarthritis (OA) compromise joint integrity, resulting in abnormal movement patterns, pain, and reduced mobility [5, 6].

Knee OA is one of the most prevalent musculoskeletal disorders, particularly among middle-aged and elderly individuals, leading to progressive cartilage degradation and joint dysfunction [7, 8] (Fig. 1). As cartilage erodes, joint space narrowing, osteophyte formation, and inflammation contribute to worsening symptoms and functional limitations [8, 9]. Notably, the disease can also trigger neuropathic pain due to joint-related nerve damage or sensitization, which complicates management and is associated with poorer functional outcomes [10, 11]. With the prevalence of OA in India ranging from 22% to 39%, particularly among aging women, it presents a significant public health burden [12, 13].

OA progression involves a complex cascade of structural and inflammatory changes, with early involvement of the medial compartment and gradual deterioration of joint biomechanics [14–16]. The hallmark symptom is pain—often described as aching or deep and worsened by weight-bearing activities—accompanied by stiffness, reduced range of motion, swelling, and instability [17, 18]. In recent years, neuropathic pain, characterized by burning, electric shock-like sensations, and altered sensory responses, has been increasingly recognized in OA patients [19, 20]. Prevalence estimates of neuropathic pain in OA range from 5.4% to 52%, emphasizing the need for comprehensive pain evaluation [21].

Neuropathic pain adds significantly to disease burden, affecting not just physical functioning but also emotional well-being, social participation, and sleep quality [22, 23]. Early diagnosis of OA is critical, as subtle initial symptoms may progress to significant joint damage and disability if untreated [24, 25]. Radiography remains the standard for diagnosing and grading OA severity using the Kellgren-Lawrence scale, although it may not detect early cartilage changes [26, 27].

Ultrasonography (USG) offers a non-invasive, radiation-free, and accessible alternative for evaluating both soft tissue structures and cartilage thickness in OA knees. USG can detect early cartilage thinning, synovial inflammation, and osteophytes, providing valuable information for disease staging and treatment monitoring [28, 29]. However, existing literature offers limited and inconsistent evidence regarding whether neuropathic pain features correlate with measurable structural degeneration such as femoral cartilage thinning. This uncertainty represents a key knowledge gap, given that neuropathic pain may arise through mechanisms independent of cartilage loss, including peripheral or central sensitization. Understanding whether neuropathic pain occurs independently of ultrasonographic cartilage morphology is crucial for refining pain phenotyping and guiding mechanism-based rehabilitation and treatment strategies. Accordingly, the present study examines the association between neuropathic pain features and femoral cartilage thickness in symptomatic knee OA patients.

Methods

Study design and participants

This cross-sectional study was conducted at a single tertiary-care academic center in the Department of Physical Medicine and Rehabilitation at All India Institute of Medical Sciences (AIIMS), Jodhpur, over a period of 18 months following approval from the Institutional Review Board and Ethics Committee. Participants aged over 40 years with chronic knee pain (≥ 3 months) and radiographically confirmed knee osteoarthritis (Kellgren–Lawrence grade 2–3) were consecutively recruited from the outpatient clinic. To reduce confounding influences on pain perception and functional assessment, patients with prior knee trauma or surgery, peripheral neuropathies, radiculopathy, inflammatory arthritis, or severe systemic illnesses were excluded.

The sample size was initially computed using OpenEpi (version 3), based on the study by Nilgün et al., to be 12 participants per group (total 24) for 80% power, 5% significance level, and 95% confidence interval. To increase statistical power and enhance the robustness of the findings, a higher sample size of 30 participants per group (total 60) was recruited. Participants were enrolled through consecutive sampling, wherein eligible patients attending the Physical Medicine and Rehabilitation outpatient department during the study period were included until the required sample size was achieved. As this was an exploratory study, p-values were interpreted descriptively and were not adjusted for multiple comparisons.

Clinical assessments

Participants were categorized into two groups based on the Pain DETECT questionnaire score, using a cut-off of 13. Those scoring above 12 were classified as the ‘Neuropathic Pain group’, and those scoring 12 or below formed the ‘Non-Neuropathic Pain group’, with 30 participants in each group [20].

Demographic data, comorbidities, and symptom duration were recorded. Pain intensity was assessed using a 10-cm Visual Analogue Scale (VAS), where participants rated their current knee pain from 0 (“no pain”) to 10 (“worst possible pain”) [30, 31]. The presence of neuropathic pain was evaluated using the Pain DETECT questionnaire [32, 33]. Functional status was assessed using the Western Ontario and McMaster Universities Osteoarthritis Index (WOMAC), a validated 24-item instrument comprising three subscales: pain (5 items), stiffness (2 items), and physical function (17 items). Each item was scored using a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 0 (no symptoms or difficulty) to 4 (extreme symptoms or difficulty), with higher scores reflecting more severe pain, greater stiffness, or increased functional limitations. Participants were instructed to rate their experiences over the previous 48 h, providing a comprehensive self-assessment of their symptom severity and functional ability. The scores for each subscale were summed to yield domain-specific scores, and a total WOMAC score was calculated by aggregating the scores from all 24 items [34]. Health-related quality of life was assessed using the SF-36 questionnaire, which evaluates eight domains: Physical Functioning (limitations of activities), Role-Physical (physical health-related work limitations), Bodily Pain, General Health Perception, Vitality (energy levels), Social Functioning, Role-Emotional (emotional health-related role limitations), and Mental Health (emotional well-being) [36]. Each domain was scored on a scale from 0 to 100, with higher scores indicating better health status or functioning in that area. Participants responded to questions reflecting their experiences over the past four weeks, allowing for a self-reported measure of their perceived health and quality of life.

All assessment tools (VAS, WOMAC, and SF-36) were administered in a standardized outpatient setting by trained study personnel to ensure uniformity and objectivity. The questionnaire details are provided in the supplementary annexures [35], and femoral cartilage thickness was measured bilaterally at three sites using ultrasound.

Ultrasound assessment of femoral cartilage

Femoral cartilage thickness was assessed using a Venue Go R3 ultrasound machine with a high-frequency linear probe (3.5–12.6 MHz). Participants were positioned supine with the knee in full flexion to optimize visualization of the femoral trochlea and condyles. Adequate gel application and minimal probe pressure were ensured to enhance image clarity and avoid cartilage compression [36].

Measurements were performed bilaterally at three standardized sites: medial femoral condyle, intercondylar notch, and lateral femoral condyle. With the probe placed in the axial plane on the suprapatellar region, three readings were taken at each site and averaged to obtain final thickness values in millimeters. Cartilage was identified on the ultrasound images as a hypoechoic (dark) band located between the highly echogenic (bright) line of the subchondral bone and the overlying joint capsule. All ultrasound assessments were performed by a single experienced physiatrist with over five years of musculoskeletal ultrasonography experience. Each measurement was repeated thrice, and the mean value was used for analysis to ensure intra-observer reliability. Inter-observer reliability was not assessed, as all evaluations were performed by the same examiner. Reported normative values are approximately 1.95 mm (medial), 2.17 mm (lateral), and 3.17 mm (intercondylar), serving as key reference points in clinical and research settings [37] (Fig. 2).

All analyses were performed using SPSS version 26. Continuous variables were checked for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test. As all continuous variables were non-normally distributed, comparisons between the Neuropathic Pain (NP) group (N = 30) and the Non-NP group (N = 30) were performed using the Mann–Whitney U test, and results are reported with sample size (N), U statistics where required, and p-values. Categorical variables were compared using the Chi-square test with degrees of freedom (df) and p-values. Correlations between age and cartilage thickness were assessed using the Spearman correlation coefficient (ρ). A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

A total of 60 participants diagnosed with knee osteoarthritis (OA) were recruited for the study, consisting of 24 males (40%) and 36 females (60%). The mean age was 51.6 ± 7.12 years, with the majority being middle-aged (40–60 years). In terms of occupation, 58.3% were engaged in moderate physical activity, 38.3% in heavy work, and only 3.3% reported sedentary work. A high prevalence of obesity was observed, with 88.3% of participants having a BMI ≥ 25 kg/m². (Table 1).

The two groups demonstrated comparable baseline characteristics, with no significant differences in BMI (29.3 ± 3.31 vs. 28.5 ± 3.11 kg/m²; p = 0.372) or gender distribution (χ²=0.278, p = 0.598), indicating that these variables were unlikely to confound the comparisons between neuropathic and non-neuropathic pain groups.

A total of 60 patients were categorized based on KL grade and presence of neuropathic pain (NP). Among patients with NP (N = 30), 66.7% (n = 20) were KL grade III and 33.3% were KL grade II, whereas among patients without NP (N = 30), 56.7% (n = 17) were KL grade II and 43.3% were KL grade III. A Chi-square test comparing KL grade distribution between the NP group (N = 30) and Non-NP group (N = 30) showed a non-significant trend toward higher KL grade III in the NP group (χ² = 3.30, df = 1, p = 0.069). These findings substantiate the statement regarding the observed but non-significant trend in radiological severity among participants with NP.

Statistical analysis was performed using the Mann-Whitney U test due to the non-parametric distribution of the data. No significant differences were found in femoral cartilage thickness between the NP group (N = 30) and Non-NP group (N = 30) across all measured sites. Consistent with the p-values, effect size analysis showed negligible differences in femoral cartilage thickness between the NP group (N = 30) and Non-NP group (N = 30) across the medial, lateral, and intercondylar regions (rrb = 0.024–0.073) indicating minimal structural variation (Table 2). To further examine factors associated with cartilage morphology, correlations between age and femoral cartilage measurements were analyzed using Spearman’s correlation (N = 60). A weak but statistically significant negative correlation was observed between age and medial femoral cartilage thickness (ρ = −0.303, p = 0.019), while no significant associations were detected for the lateral (ρ = −0.188, p = 0.150) or intercondylar (ρ = −0.227, p = 0.081) regions. These findings indicate that although age-related thinning exists, it is subtle and predominantly localized to the medial compartment in this cohort.

Discussion

Knee osteoarthritis (OA) is a progressive degenerative disease of the joint cartilage, primarily affecting weight-bearing joints such as the knees, and remains a major cause of pain and disability worldwide. In the present study involving 60 symptomatic knee OA patients, a female predominance (70%) was observed, with a mean age of 51.6 ± 7.12 years, consistent with previous literature indicating higher OA prevalence in women and middle-aged to older adults. Most participants were engaged in moderate to heavy physical activity, and a significant proportion (88.3%) were either overweight or obese, reinforcing the established association between increased BMI and OA due to both mechanical overload and inflammatory mechanisms. Chandra Prakash Pal et al. similarly reported a higher prevalence among obese and female patients; however, their cohort primarily included older and unemployed individuals, while the present study noted a growing burden among middle-aged, working individuals, suggesting a shift in the demographic profile of symptomatic OA [38].

Although not statistically significant, radiological severity (Kellgren-Lawrence grading) showed a trend toward greater involvement in the neuropathic pain (NP) group, with KL grade III more frequent among those with NP, while KL grade II was more common in the non-NP group. These findings are comparable to those of Demir et al., who found a significant association between NP features and higher KL grades, proposing that more advanced OA may predispose patients to neuropathic mechanisms due to increased joint degeneration and altered afferent input [39].

Femoral cartilage thickness (FCT), measured via ultrasound, did not differ significantly between the NP and non-NP groups across all evaluated sites. This suggests that cartilage degeneration alone may not explain the development of neuropathic pain in OA and highlights the possible role of central sensitization, altered peripheral and central pain processing, and low-grade inflammation not detectable by ultrasound alone. Nilgün Mesci et al. reported similar findings, emphasizing that FCT may not directly correlate with subjective pain characteristics in OA patients with neuropathic features [40]. The analysis of age-related changes revealed only a weak negative correlation between age and medial cartilage thickness, with no significant associations in the lateral or intercondylar regions. This mild relationship suggests that within the studied age range (≥ 40 years), age-related cartilage thinning is subtle and localized rather than generalized. This pattern suggests that age alone is not a major determinant of femoral cartilage morphology in early to moderate OA.

Patients with NP demonstrated significantly higher VAS scores, indicating more intense pain perception. This observation aligns with the findings of J.D. Power et al., who identified neuropathic-like pain as a contributor to amplified pain responses in OA [41]. Furthermore, WOMAC scores were significantly elevated in the NP group, reflecting greater functional limitation. These results are in agreement with Shereen R. Kamel et al., who suggested that higher levels of β-nerve growth factor may mediate both pain and functional impairment in this subgroup [42].

Regarding quality of life, significant differences were observed in SF-36 domains such as bodily pain, general health, role-emotional, mental health, and social functioning, all of which were markedly lower in the NP group. These findings are consistent with those of Ayhan Aşkın et al., who reported similar reductions in these domains, underscoring the substantial psychosocial burden associated with neuropathic pain in OA [22, 43]. Although differences in vitality and physical functioning did not reach statistical significance, the overall impact of NP on mental and emotional health remains evident.

In this study, the direct comparison between individuals with and without neuropathic pain provided valuable insights into the distinct clinical and functional impact of neuropathic pain in knee osteoarthritis. The use of validated and widely accepted assessment tools—including VAS, WOMAC, SF-36, and Kellgren-Lawrence grading—strengthened the methodological rigor and enhanced the reliability of the findings. The multifactorial approach, incorporating clinical, radiological, and quality-of-life parameters, enabled a holistic evaluation of disease burden. From a clinical standpoint, these findings underscore the importance of early identification of neuropathic pain features in patients with knee OA. Conventional analgesic strategies often target nociceptive mechanisms and may provide limited benefit in individuals with central sensitization or neuropathic components. Incorporating screening tools such as the painDETECT questionnaire into routine assessment can help clinicians recognize NP phenotypes early. Once identified, these patients may benefit from a multimodal, mechanism-based management approach, combining conventional therapy with centrally acting agents (e.g., duloxetine, pregabalin, amitriptyline) and individualized rehabilitation protocols focusing on desensitization, neuromuscular re-education, and graded activity. Cognitive-behavioral therapy and patient education further support pain coping and functional recovery. Overall, the present study highlights that neuropathic pain represents a distinct and underrecognized clinical phenotype within knee OA—one that is associated with greater symptom burden and poorer quality of life, independent of measurable cartilage loss. Recognizing this phenotype early and adopting individualized, multimodal strategies may improve patient outcomes, optimize rehabilitation, and enhance quality of life.

Conclusion

This study demonstrated that neuropathic pain features in individuals with knee osteoarthritis were not associated with femoral cartilage thickness, indicating that neuropathic pain may arise independently of structural joint degeneration. Despite comparable ultrasonographic cartilage measurements between groups, patients with neuropathic pain experienced significantly greater pain intensity, higher functional impairment, and markedly poorer quality of life, particularly in domains related to pain perception, emotional well-being, and social functioning.

These findings suggest that neuropathic pain represents a distinct pain phenotype in osteoarthritis, influencing symptom severity even in the absence of advanced structural deterioration. Although functional disability in this study was assessed using validated patient-reported scales, future studies integrating objective performance-based measures may further strengthen clinical interpretation. Clinically, routine screening for neuropathic pain features in patients with knee osteoarthritis is recommended, as early identification may enable personalized management strategies—including neuromodulatory pharmacotherapy and targeted rehabilitation approaches—to optimize pain control and improve functional outcomes.

Limitations

While the study offers valuable insights through a comprehensive clinical evaluation, its cross-sectional design limits causal interpretation. The use of ultrasound provided practical assessment of cartilage thickness, though advanced imaging or biomarkers could have offered deeper structural and neurophysiological insights. Functional disability was assessed using validated patient-reported outcome measures; however, objective assessments were not included due to the extensive clinical evaluation already undertaken. Future studies incorporating these objective measures may provide additional clarity. Additionally, the overlap between nociceptive and neuropathic pain poses challenges in precise classification, highlighting the need for future longitudinal and mechanistic research.
