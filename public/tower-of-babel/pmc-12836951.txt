Research progress on the mechanisms of cGAS-STING signaling in immune cell infiltration associated with vascular remodeling
Authors: Xiaoyu Zhang, Liming Cheng, Xuejuan Ma, Kaiyang Ma, Jiahui Xie, Wenting Gui, Jiaxiang Chen, Kai Liu, Runwei Ma
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundVascular remodeling is a hallmark of various cardiovascular diseases and is increasingly recognized as an immune-mediated process. Recent evidence has identified the cyclic GMP-AMP synthase (cGAS)-stimulator of interferon genes (STING) signaling pathway as a critical mediator linking cytosolic DNA sensing to innate immune activation. Beyond its classical role in host defense against pathogens, the cGAS-STING pathway plays a pivotal role in regulating the recruitment, activation, and functional polarization of diverse immune cell populations, including macrophages, neutrophils, T cells, B cells, dendritic cells, and eosinophils, thereby contributing to vascular inflammation and pathological remodeling.Main textThis review summarizes recent advances in understanding how cGAS-STING signaling governs immune cell infiltration and intercellular communication that underlie vascular remodeling. We also explore the direct effects of cGAS-STING activation on vascular endothelial and smooth muscle cells, and its crosstalk with other key signaling pathways such as Toll-like receptors (TLRs), Hippo-YAP, and bone morphogenetic proteins (BMPs). In addition, we discuss current and emerging therapeutic strategies targeting this pathway, including small-molecule inhibitors and combination therapies utilizing nano-delivery systems.ConclusionsBy highlighting the role of immune cell infiltration as a novel therapeutic axis, this review provides new perspectives for the modulation of vascular remodeling and the treatment of cardiovascular diseases. These insights may inform future research on pathway-specific interventions and the development of personalized therapeutic approaches.

Introduction

Vascular remodeling is a hallmark pathological feature of various cardiovascular diseases, including pulmonary arterial hypertension (PAH), atherosclerosis (AS), and aortic dissection (AD). It is fundamentally characterized by structural alterations of the vascular wall, involving dysfunction of vascular cells, imbalance in extracellular matrix metabolism, and abnormal infiltration of immune cells [1–3]. Innate immunity, serving as the first line of defence against pathogens and cellular damage, plays a pivotal regulatory role in vascular remodeling [5–9]. Among the key pathways, the cGAS-STING signaling axis is central to cytosolic DNA sensing. It detects exogenous pathogenic DNA or abnormally released self-DNA, such as nuclear or mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) [8–11], and functions as a crucial molecular hub linking DNA damage to sterile vascular inflammation [12–14].

Recent studies have revealed that beyond its classical role in anti-infective immunity, the cGAS-STING pathway actively contributes to the pathogenesis of vascular remodeling by regulating the recruitment and activation of various immune cells, including macrophages, T cells, B cells, and neutrophils. Moreover, it has been implicated in rare genetic disorders such as Gaucher disease and Fabry disease, where it mediates vascular remodeling processes [15–17]. Therefore, elucidating the molecular mechanisms by which this pathway induces immune cell infiltration may provide novel theoretical insights and therapeutic targets for the prevention and treatment of cardiovascular diseases.

It is noteworthy that current research has focused predominantly on the roles of macrophages and neutrophils, given their direct involvement in the initiation of vascular inflammation and extracellular matrix degradation. In contrast, evidence regarding B cells, dendritic cells, and eosinophils remains relatively limited. Accordingly, this review allocates discussion proportionally to the strength of existing evidence, emphasizing well-established mechanisms while objectively presenting unresolved scientific questions.

In addition, while mouse models serve as the primary experimental system for investigating the mechanisms by which the cGAS-STING pathway regulates vascular remodeling, there are significant differences between mice and humans in the distribution and composition of immune cell subsets associated with vascular pathology [7]. Furthermore, the pathological induction of vascular remodeling in mice is relatively simplistic (e.g., high-fat diet + specific gene knockout for AS, Su5416 + hypoxia for PAH, and β-aminopropionitrile for AD, etc.). In contrast, human vascular diseases are mostly caused by the combined effects of multiple risk factors such as aging, diabetes, hypertension, smoking, and genetics. Such multifactorial stimuli may alter the activation patterns of the cGAS-STING pathway and the output of its downstream signals. Therefore, caution is required when interpreting animal experimental data, and it also highlights the necessity of validating relevant findings in human samples.

In this review, we systematically summarise the molecular mechanisms of the cGAS-STING pathway, with a particular focus on its role in mediating immune cell infiltration during vascular remodeling. Additionally, we discuss potential therapeutic strategies targeting this pathway, aiming to stimulate further research interest in its function and to provide a reference for future investigations in this field.

Molecular regulatory mechanisms of the cGAS-STING pathway

The canonical cGAS-STING pathway

The canonical cGAS-STING pathway centres on cytosolic DNA sensing and type I interferon (IFN) responses. cGAS acts as a cytosolic sensor of double-stranded DNA (dsDNA) via a zinc finger domain, recognising exogenous pathogenic DNA (e.g., from viruses or intracellular bacteria) or self-DNA released aberrantly (e.g., nuclear DNA from micronuclear rupture or mtDNA from mitochondrial injury) [18, 19]. This recognition induces conformational changes in cGAS, leading to its dimerisation [20, 21]. Activated cGAS catalyses the synthesis of cyclic GMP-AMP (cGAMP) using ATP and GTP as substrates, forming a unique 2′,5′ and 3′,5′ mixed phosphodiester bond structure (c[G(2′,5′)pA(3′,5′)p]), which distinguishes it from bacterial cyclic dinucleotides and ensures signaling specificity [22–24]. As a second messenger, cGAMP binds to the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) transmembrane protein STING, inducing a conformational shift from an open to a closed state [25]. This results in V-shaped STING dimers that further assemble into tetramers or higher-order oligomers [26, 27]. Activated STING recruits TANK-binding kinase 1 (TBK1), which subsequently phosphorylates the transcription factor IRF3, promoting the expression of type I IFNs (e.g., IFN-β). Concurrently, STING activates the nuclear factor-κB (NF-κB) signaling pathway, enhancing the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6, thereby initiating robust immune responses [28, 29] (Fig. 1, black arrows represents the canonical pathway).Fig. 1Molecular regulatory mechanisms of the cGAS-STING pathway. Non-canonical cGAS-STING signaling pathways

Translational regulation

Following cGAMP binding, STING can directly activate PKR-like ER kinase (PERK) on the ER membrane, leading to phosphorylation of eIF2α and modulation of translation programs related to inflammation and cell survival. Notably, this occurs upstream of TBK1–IRF3 activation and is independent of the unfolded protein response [30–32]. Studies have shown that 18β-glycyrrhetinic acid (18β-GA) may ameliorate PAH by inhibiting the PERK/eIF2α/NF-κB pathway [33]. Additionally, PERK plays a key role in regulating STING trafficking and downstream signal transduction [34–36] (Fig. 1, red arrows represents the canonical pathway “a”).

Regulation of canonical and non-canonical autophagy

The cGAS-STING pathway is closely intertwined with cellular autophagy. As a central regulator linking innate immunity and cellular metabolism, STING participates in immune responses via non-canonical autophagy while modulating non-immune functions through canonical autophagy, and both mechanisms play essential roles in vascular remodeling [37, 38].

STING can directly bind to autophagy-related protein LC3 through its LC3-interacting regions (LIRs), thereby initiating non-canonical autophagy. This process is dependent on the PI3P effector WIPI2 and the ATG12–ATG5–ATG16L1 complex [39], and it mediates a negative feedback loop by facilitating STING degradation to modulate immune activation [40–42] (Fig. 1, blue arrows represents the canonical pathway “b”). This mechanism is distinct from classical autophagy, which requires Beclin-1 and the ULK1 complex. Furthermore, STING activation can induce LC3B lipidation on perinuclear single-membrane vesicles in a V-ATPase-ATG16L1–WD40 domain-dependent manner, independent of upstream classical autophagy signals [43, 44].

Canonical autophagy, which relies on autophagy-related genes (ATGs), involves the formation of autophagosomes, the ATG5–ATG8 conjugation cascade, and autolysosome fusion, thereby maintaining cellular homeostasis. STING regulates autophagosome-lysosome fusion by interacting with the SNARE protein STX17 and promotes the clearance of accumulated substrates [38]. Additionally, by acting as a proton channel, STING activates the V-ATPase-ATG5–ATG8 axis, driving TFEB dephosphorylation and nuclear translocation, which upregulates autophagy and lysosome-related gene expression. This mechanism is especially critical in lysosomal storage diseases (LSDs) [45, 46]. Interestingly, non-canonical and canonical autophagy pathways serve distinct roles in LSDs. STING-driven non-canonical autophagy, such as that triggered by DNA or cGAMP, functions independently of classical ATG machinery and primarily mediates innate immune responses. For instance, in Gaucher disease (GD)-associated pulmonary hypertension, the accumulation of Gaucher cells in the lungs may trigger inflammation through this pathway [15]. In Fabry disease (FD), glycosphingolipid accumulation may initiate rapid STING-mediated immune responses, exacerbating vascular endothelial dysfunction [17]. In contrast, STING-induced canonical autophagy is essential for metabolic homeostasis and substrate clearance in LSDs. Together, these pathways contribute to the complex pathological phenotypes observed in such diseases.

Moreover, cGAS has been implicated in the initiation of classical autophagy. cGAS-dsDNA polymers can undergo liquid-liquid phase separation (LLPS) to form condensates that act as nucleation sites for autophagy [47]. This may also occur via cGAS-Beclin-1 interactions, LLPS of cGAS-DNA complexes, or STING-induced ER stress and mTOR signaling [48–50].

In summary, the cGAS-STING pathway modulates autophagy through both canonical and non-canonical routes. However, the precise molecular mechanisms of STING-induced autophagy remain incompletely understood, and further studies are needed to identify associated proteins and signaling cascades.

Regulation of cell death

STING activation initiates a cascade of signaling events that induce anti-proliferative responses such as cellular senescence and ultimately lead to cell death. In the absence of apoptosis, STING can trigger RIPK3-dependent necroptosis via type I IFN and TNF signaling pathways [51]. For instance, in models of AD, STING activation promotes vascular wall destruction through the necroptosis of smooth muscle cells. Concurrently, STING enhances the expression of matrix metalloproteinases (e.g., MMP9) in macrophages via the NF-κB pathway, promoting extracellular matrix degradation and vascular remodeling.

Cellular and molecular mechanisms of cGAMP as an immunotransmitter

Beyond its role as a second messenger, cGAMP can function as an “immunotransmitter,” mediating intercellular immune activation [52]. Its extracellular transmission and regulation rely on specific molecular mechanisms.

Transmembrane transport

In human monocytes and macrophages, the solute carrier protein SLC46A2 mediates the uptake of extracellular cGAMP. As the primary cGAMP importer in primary human monocytes, SLC46A2 enables host immune cells to sense tumour-derived cGAMP, thereby amplifying anti-tumour immune responses [53].

Extracellular degradation

Extracellular cGAMP is regulated by ENPP1 and ENPP3. ENPP1 is an ectoenzyme that hydrolyses the 2′,5′ bond of cGAMP, while ENPP3 compensates in ENPP1-deficient settings. Both enzymes serve as negative regulators by limiting extracellular cGAMP-STING signaling and dampening immune responses [54–56].

Intercellular communication

In the tumour microenvironment, tumour cells can release cGAMP via extracellular vesicles (e.g., R-EVs), which are then transferred to host immune cells. This activates the STING pathway and induces IFN-β production, thereby promoting the infiltration of immune cells [57].

Studies above have revealed that the aberrant activation of the cGAS-STING pathway under pathological conditions can directly regulate endothelial dysfunction and inflammatory responses of vascular ECs, as well as the proliferation, phenotypic switching, apoptosis, and senescence of VSMCs. It also crosstalks with signaling pathways such as TLRs and Hippo-YAP to collectively participate in vascular remodeling. Furthermore, the pathway can activate innate immunity and regulate immune cell functions by inducing inflammatory mediators such as type I interferons (IFN-I). Subsequently, through the interactions between regulated immune cells and vascular cells (e.g., immune cells secrete inflammatory factors to stimulate abnormal proliferation of vascular cells, and vascular cells release damage signals to recruit immune cells), it exacerbates perivascular inflammation and vascular remodeling. These findings provide a multi-dimensional immunological and molecular targeting strategy for the treatment of vascular-related diseases.

Mechanisms by which the cGAS-STING pathway mediates immune cell infiltration leading to vascular remodeling

Regulation of macrophages

Macrophages are the predominant infiltrating immune cells involved in vascular remodeling. Their phenotypic polarisation (pro-inflammatory M1 vs. anti-inflammatory M2) directly shapes the vascular inflammatory microenvironment. The cGAS-STING pathway regulates macrophage function through multiple mechanisms.

Recruitment and activation of the cGAS-STING pathway

Injured vascular cells, including smooth muscle cells and endothelial cells, can release cytosolic DNA that activates the cGAS-STING pathway. This activation triggers the secretion of chemokines such as CCL2 and CCL3, which recruit circulating monocytes that subsequently differentiate into macrophages [58]. In an AD model, mtDNA released from smooth muscle cells activated the cGAS-STING-IRF3 signaling cascade in macrophages, leading to increased MMPs expression, extracellular matrix degradation, and progressive vascular remodeling [9, 59].

Similarly, in a hypoxia-induced pulmonary arterial hypertension (PAH) model, upregulation of the CCL2/CCR2 axis was associated with sustained accumulation of perivascular and alveolar macrophages [60]. In ApoE -/- mice fed a high-fat diet, macrophages within atherosclerotic plaques displayed marked DNA damage and elevated cGAMP levels [61]. These findings established macrophages as the major effector cells in AS, where mtDNA damage activates the cGAS-STING pathway to promote the production of proinflammatory cytokines, thereby amplifying chronic vascular inflammation and driving disease progression [61].

Adding to this evidence, a recent study by Huangfu et al. Identified TDP43 as a novel upstream activator of the cGAS-STING–NF-κB axis in macrophages. Mechanistically, TDP43 facilitates the release of mtDNA and upregulates CD36 expression through the β-catenin/PPAR-γ complex, thereby enhancing lipid uptake, foam cell formation, and inflammatory cytokine production. Notably, macrophage-specific deletion of TDP43 in ApoE -/- mice markedly attenuated vascular inflammation and atherosclerotic plaque development, underscoring its key role in linking mtDNA-induced cGAS-STING activation to macrophage-driven vascular remodeling [62]. Furthermore, genetic ablation of STING itself alleviated atherosclerotic lesions and reduced macrophage infiltration in ApoE -/- mice on a high-fat diet [63], collectively reinforcing the central importance of the cGAS-STING signaling axis in macrophage-mediated vascular inflammation and remodeling.

Phenotypic polarization

A study on chronic thromboembolic pulmonary hypertension (CTEPH) revealed that macrophages with a pro-inflammatory phenotype play a significant role in pulmonary vascular remodeling [64]. Interestingly, eosinophil-derived IL-4 and cationic proteins such as mEar1 can inhibit NF-κB signaling, reversing M1 macrophage polarisation, suggesting a crosstalk between the cGAS-STING pathway and other immune signaling cascades [65].

Regulation of neutrophils

In addition to macrophages, neutrophils play a crucial role in vascular remodeling, particularly through the formation of neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs). The cGAS-STING pathway is a key regulator in this process.

NETs formation

In PAH models, cytosolic DNA activates the cGAS-STING pathway in neutrophils, inducing NET release. These NETs activate the TLR4-NF-κB signaling pathway, promoting angiogenesis in endothelial cells (ECs) and proliferation in smooth muscle cells [66, 67]. Moreover, DNA within NETs can further activate the cGAS-STING pathway, creating a positive feedback loop, DNA-NETs-cGAS-STING, that amplifies vascular inflammation [68].

Effects on vascular remodeling

NETs can induce ECs to release matrix metalloproteinase 9 (MMP-9) and heparin-binding EGF-like growth factor (HB-EGF), thereby enhancing vascular permeability and cell motility, and promoting vascular remodeling. NETs also stimulate pulmonary arterial smooth muscle cell proliferation, contributing to pathological remodeling [66]. Additionally, multi-omics analyses in patients with AD have shown that macrophage-derived CXCL3, via the CXCL3/CXCR2 axis, promotes NET formation in neutrophils, accelerating disease progression [68].

In summary, activation of the cGAS-STING pathway promotes the release of inflammatory cytokines and chemokines that drive neutrophil recruitment and accumulation. The activated neutrophils rapidly infiltrate sites of vascular injury and, through the release of NETs, cytokines, and proteolytic enzymes, directly or indirectly contribute to the pathological remodeling of the vascular wall [69].

Regulation of T cells

T cells contribute to vascular remodeling primarily through adaptive immune responses. The cGAS-STING pathway regulates T cell function by maintaining the balance of T cell subsets. The non-canonical NF-κB pathway modulates Th1/Th2 balance by inhibiting type I IFN expression. Excessive activation of the cGAS-STING pathway may disrupt this balance, leading to increased infiltration of pro-inflammatory T cell subsets, such as Th17, and exacerbating vascular inflammation [70]. For example, in PAH models, Treg cell deficiency enhances cGAS-STING activation, intensifying T cell-mediated vascular inflammation [71].

In another study by Zhao et al. [72], a cGAS knock-in (KI) mouse model capable of inducing systemic autoimmunity was employed. Inducible intracellular expression of cGAS promoted the upregulation of chemokine receptor 5 (CCR5) on CD8+ T cells and stimulated vascular endothelial cells to produce its ligand, chemokine (C-C motif) ligand 5 (CCL5). This process facilitated the recruitment of peripheral CD8+ T cells to the lungs, where these cells subsequently secreted chemokine (C-X-C motif) ligand 13 (CXCL13) and interferon-γ (IFN-γ). Notably, IFN-γ induced endothelial cell death, contributing to pulmonary vascular remodeling.

Regulation of B cells

Although the direct mechanism of action remains unclear, type I interferons (IFN-I) induced by the activation of the cGAS-STING (cyclic GMP-AMP synthase-stimulator of interferon genes) pathway can enhance B cell activation and antibody secretion, thereby contributing to autoimmune-mediated vascular remodeling [73]. Notably, the cGAS-STING pathway exerts an indirect regulatory effect on B cell function. In systemic sclerosis-associated pulmonary arterial hypertension (SSc-PAH), B cells display distinct developmental abnormalities, including altered VDJ recombination frequency, increased fixation of somatic hypermutations, expansion of IgD+ B cell subsets, and a higher proportion of antibody-secreting cells [74]. While the direct regulatory mechanisms have not been fully elucidated, IFN-I induced by cGAS-STING pathway activation may further promote B cell activation and antibody secretion, which in turn contributes to autoimmune-mediated vascular injury. Furthermore, B cell depletion therapy has been shown to temporarily reverse immunological abnormalities in both SSc-PAH and idiopathic pulmonary arterial hypertension. These findings collectively suggest that targeting the interaction between B cells and the cGAS-STING pathway could hold therapeutic potential for these conditions [75, 76].

Regulation of DCs

As professional antigen-presenting cells, DCs play a critical role in bridging innate and adaptive immunity, and their function is modulated by the cGAS-STING pathway [77].

Maturation and activation

STING activation promotes DC maturation through a type I interferon–dependent pathway [78], leading to the upregulation of CD80/CD86 and enhancement of antigen-presenting capacity [79]. In PAH, mature DCs accumulate around pulmonary vessels [80]. Additionally, lung monocyte-derived DCs can further promote vascular smooth muscle cell proliferation and suppress endothelial gene expression, thereby exacerbating vascular remodeling [81].

Immune modulation

STING-dependent DNA-sensing pathways in DCs activate the non-canonical NF-κB signaling pathway, influencing T-cell polarisation. Under hypoxic conditions, activation of conventional DC2 (cDC2) subsets has been observed, and their depletion alleviates pulmonary hypertension and vascular remodeling [82].

Although direct evidence linking cGAS–STING activation in DCs to vascular remodeling remains lacking, STING-regulated DCs can create a proinflammatory microenvironment that facilitates vascular remodeling in PAH.

Regulation of eosinophils

Eosinophils contribute to vascular remodeling by releasing cytokines and cationic proteins, with emerging evidence suggesting possible crosstalk with innate immune DNA-sensing pathways such as cGAS-STING.

Protective role

In an abdominal aortic aneurysm model, eosinophil-derived IL-4 and cationic proteins (e.g., mEar1) suppressed NF-κB signaling, promoted macrophage polarisation toward the M2 phenotype, and reduced the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines. This suppressed vascular remodeling and aneurysm progression. Similar findings were observed in studies using human cardiac cells, where eosinophil cationic protein (ECP), eosinophil-derived neurotoxin (EDN), and recombinant murine mEar1 inhibited cardiomyocyte death and hypertrophy and attenuated TGF-β signaling in cardiac fibroblasts [83].

Pro-remodeling role

Conversely, eosinophils can also promote vascular remodeling. In AS models, eosinophil-derived cationic proteins (e.g., ECP, EDN) bind to bone MBP receptors (BMPR-1A/1B) on vascular smooth muscle cells(VSMCs), activating the Smad1/5/8-Runx2 pathway and promoting smooth muscle calcification and disease progression [84]. Eosinophils also interact with platelets to promote atherogenesis and vascular remodeling, and stabilise thrombi through the formation of eosinophil extracellular traps [85]. Additionally, eosinophil-derived IL-4 mediates neutrophil recruitment and degranulation, participating in inflammatory responses. Collectively, the precise role of eosinophils in vascular remodeling remains to be fully elucidated.

Potential crosstalk with the cGAS-STING pathway

Recent evidence indicates that eosinophils release extracellular traps composed of DNA and cationic granule proteins. These DNA-rich structures can serve as endogenous ligands for cGAS, potentially triggering STING activation in neighbouring endothelial cells or macrophages. Moreover, cytotoxic eosinophil proteins such as ECP and MBP can injure vascular or epithelial cells, leading to mtDNA leakage and subsequent cGAS-STING activation. Conversely, type 2 cytokines (IL-4, IL-13) released by eosinophils may suppress STING expression, implying a complex bidirectional regulation. Although direct mechanistic evidence remains limited, these findings suggest that eosinophil-derived DNA and cationic proteins may indirectly modulate cGAS-STING-mediated inflammatory signaling, thereby influencing immune-cell infiltration and vascular remodeling. Future studies should clarify whether inhibiting eosinophil release of extracellular traps or manipulating cGAS-STING activity alters eosinophil-driven vascular pathology.

Direct regulation of vascular cells by the cGAS-STING pathway and its interactions with other signaling pathways

Effects on vascular ECs

Vascular ECs serve as a barrier in the vessel wall, and their dysfunction represents a critical initiating event in vascular remodeling. The cGAS-STING pathway directly modulates endothelial function. In diabetic models, palmitic acid-induced mitochondrial damage triggers the release of mtDNA and activates the cGAS-STING-IRF3 axis, leading to the dysregulation of the Hippo-YAP pathway [86]. This disruption impairs endothelial angiogenic function and promotes vascular remodeling [87]. Moreover, in pulmonary hypertension (PH), Bertero et al. [88] demonstrated that YAP can be activated by extracellular matrix stiffness, facilitating collagen deposition and exacerbating vascular remodeling, highlighting YAP’s crucial role in cardiovascular pathologies.

Endothelial dysfunction

In AS models, IQGAP1 promotes mitochondrial damage and mtDNA release, thereby activating the cGAS-STING pathway and inducing pyroptosis in ECs through the NOD-like receptor pyrin domain-containing 3 (NLRP3) inflammasome, worsening endothelial dysfunction [89].

Inflammatory regulation by ECs

Studies have shown that cigarette smoke extract (CSE) induces cytoplasmic DNA accumulation in ECs, triggering cGAS-STING activation. This may contribute to the pathogenesis of AS. Furthermore, elevated levels of circulating mitochondrial cell-free DNA (mt-cfDNA) have been proposed as a potential biomarker for AS [90].

Effects on VSMCs

Similar to ECs, VSMCs play a pivotal role in vascular remodeling through aberrant proliferation, phenotypic switching, and apoptosis, all of which are modulated by the cGAS-STING pathway.

Proliferation and phenotypic switching

In PAH models, DNA damage-induced cytosolic DNA activates the cGAS-STING-NF-κB axis, promoting PASMC proliferation and a phenotypic switch from contractile to synthetic type. β-sitosterol inhibits this pathway, reduces PASMC proliferation, and alleviates pulmonary vascular remodeling [10]. In atherosclerotic plaques, oxidative stress in VSMCs causes mtDNA damage, which activates the cGAS-STING-IFN-I pathway. IFN-I promotes VSMC senescence and phenotypic transformation via autocrine and paracrine mechanisms, reducing fibrous cap-forming VSMCs and thinning the cap, thereby increasing plaque vulnerability [90]. Similarly, in aortic aneurysm and dissection (AAD) tissues, DNA damage-induced phenotypic switching of VSMCs is observed. In Sting-/- mice, stress-induced phenotypic transformation toward an inflammatory state is suppressed [91].

Apoptosis and senescence

In AAD models, nuclear and mtDNA damage in VSMCs activates STING signaling, inducing apoptosis and necrosis, which compromises vascular wall integrity [9]. Cellular senescence is intricately linked with cGAS-STING activity [18, 92–94, 97], involving the P53/P21Cip1 and P16INK4a/Rb-mediated growth arrest and functional impairment [96]. Cytosolic exposure of nuclear dsDNA in senescent cells activates the cGAS-STING pathway, further accelerating ageing and cell death [97]. Various pathological stimuli, including oxidative stress, radiation, oncogene inactivation, tumour suppressor dysfunction, and drug toxicity, can activate cGAS-STING, suggesting its central role in driving cellular senescence [98]. Telomere attrition is another key mechanism of vascular senescence [99–101]. In vascular injury models, telomere damage induces micronuclear DNA release and activates cGAS-STING, promoting VSMC senescence and immune infiltration [102].

Crosstalk with other signaling pathways

The cGAS-STING pathway interacts with multiple other signaling cascades to cooperatively regulate vascular remodeling.

cGAS-STING and TLRs

In the pathogenesis of AS, innate immune receptors such as TLRs play a pivotal role [103]. TLRs mediate the recruitment of macrophages and leukocytes and are critically involved in both the initiation and progression of atherosclerotic lesions [104]. TLRs detect exogenous DNA/RNA and damage-associated endogenous nucleic acids, thereby linking sterile inflammation to vascular injury. In AS, activation of the cGAS-STING pathway further amplifies this response by enhancing NF-κB signaling, which augments the production of proinflammatory cytokines [105]. Interestingly, MyD88 also limits TLR3-mediated IKKε (but not TBK1)-dependent IRF3 activation, thereby preventing excessive immune activation [106]. cGAS inhibitors

Several small-molecule inhibitors have been developed, including RU0.521, G150, and PF-06928215 [108–110]. RU0.521 occupies the cGAS catalytic pocket, suppressing enzymatic activity and reducing endothelial pyroptosis and foam cell formation in AS [111]. Han et al. [112] demonstrated that intracerebral infusion of RU0.521 in hypertensive mice reduced microglial activation, neuroinflammation, and sympathetic excitation, ultimately lowering blood pressure and ameliorating vascular remodeling.

PF-06928215 is a substrate-competitive inhibitor that blocks ATP/GTP binding to cGAS by interacting with Lys362 and Lys350, thereby preventing cGAMP production [110]. Gong et al. showed that in high-fat diet models, PF-06928215 and Astin C negated palmitic acid-induced cardiomyocyte dysfunction by suppressing cGAS-STING activation [113].

Antimalarials such as hydroxychloroquine (HCQ), quinacrine (QC), and compound 6-amino-2-methoxy-9-chloroacridine (X6) can inhibit cGAS-dsDNA binding and exhibit anti-inflammatory and anti-atherosclerotic effects [114–117]. X6 and HCQ reduced cGAMP levels and fibrosis in Trex1-/- mice, while HCQ treatment lowered serum TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-8, thereby improving metabolic risk factors [114, 115, 117]. However, these inhibitors remain in preclinical stages, and their clinical utility requires further validation.

Sting inhibitors

β-Sitosterol inhibits STING oligomerisation and reduces type I IFN release, thereby attenuating PASMC proliferation and remodeling in PH [10].

C-176, a nitrofuran derivative, covalently modifies STING at Cys91, preventing palmitoylation and subsequent activation [118]. It has been shown to suppress macrophage activation and vascular inflammation in AS models [61, 119]. In vitro, C-176 blocks palmitic acid-induced endothelial pyroptosis and type I IFN transcription [120]. Aspirin has also been shown to alleviate STING-mediated coagulopathy in COVID-19 [121].

ENPP1/3 modulators

ENPP1 degrades extracellular cGAMP and represents a novel target in cancer and autoimmune vascular diseases. Enhancing ENPP1 or ENPP3 activity suppresses STING activation and may alleviate vascular inflammation [55, 122].

NLRP3 inhibitors

Inhibition of the NLRP3 inflammasome reduces the production of IL-1β and IL-18, thereby alleviating pulmonary inflammation and offering a potential anti-remodeling strategy in chronic inflammatory conditions [123, 124]. Moreover, a direct mechanistic link exists between the NLRP3 inflammasome and the cGAS-STING pathway: Apelin-13 suppresses NLRP3 activation and decreases the generation of ROS [125]; since ROS is a key trigger of cytosolic DNA release (e.g., mtDNA), which serves as the ligand for cGAS-STING activation, Apelin-13 can directly inhibit cGAS-STING activation through this route. The selective inhibitor MCC950 blocks activation of the NLRP3/ASC/Caspase-1/GSDMD-N axis and reduces macrophage pyroptosis [126], a process that releases cytosolic DNA. Consequently, MCC950 limits the availability of DNA substrates for cGAS-STING activation and has also been shown to downregulate cGAS-STING target gene expression in ApoE−/− mice [127]. In addition, miR-223 suppresses NLRP3 activation by targeting RHOB and concurrently inhibits the TLR4/NF-κB pathway [128]; since NF-κB participates in the transcriptional regulation of both cGAS and STING, miR-223 thereby exerts a direct inhibitory effect on the cGAS-STING pathway.

Although NLRP3 inhibitors do not directly target AS, they have demonstrated clinical benefits in heart failure (e.g., dapansutrile Phase I trial [129]) and coronary artery disease (e.g., colchicine treatment [130]). However, a meta-analysis of 32 randomized controlled trials revealed that while these therapies reduce ischemic events, they do not improve mortality [131], which may be attributed to the stage-specific roles of inflammation in AS.

Hippo-YAP pathway activation

Given its interplay with cGAS-STING [85], YAP activation may mitigate vascular ageing. Sladitschek-Martens et al. [132] reported that YAP activation suppressed cGAS-STING signaling and preserved aortic integrity, delaying senescence.

Combination therapeutic strategies

Combination of cGAS-STING agonists with immune checkpoint blockers

Nano-delivery technology for targeted regulation of the cGAS-STING pathway. While activating the cGAS-STING pathway, MnTe₂ nanosheets, when combined with PD-L1 inhibitors, can enhance dendritic cell maturation and T-cell infiltration, significantly improving the effect of anti-tumour vascular remodeling [70].

Combination with metabolic regulators

A combination of cGAS-STING and metabolic regulators, such as Calcitonin gene-related peptide (CGRP), can reduce mtDNA release by protecting mitochondria. When combined with cGAS-STING inhibitors, it can synergistically alleviate PAH [133]. Another interesting study revealed that in ischemic/hypoxic injury, lactic acid upregulates Arg1 through H3K18la, which affects the remodeling of mitochondrial cristae. Meanwhile, hypoxia-induced VDAC1 lactylation leads to MPTP opening and the release of mtDNA from VSMCs. The released mtDNA activates the cGAS-STING pathway, triggering PANoptosis of VSMCs, while the VSMC-targeted nanomaterial NP-siArg1 can improve vascular dysfunction [134].

These findings demonstrate the current progress of therapeutic modulation of the cGAS-STING pathway. However, translating these strategies into clinical application remains challenging and warrants further investigation (see Future Perspectives).

Future perspectives

Although remarkable progress has been made in elucidating the role of the cGAS-STING pathway in immune cell infiltration and vascular remodeling, several critical challenges and unanswered questions remain to be addressed before this pathway can be effectively translated into clinical therapy.

Challenges in translational application

The core challenge in translational application lies in the fact that most current studies rely on single-factor animal models, which can only partially replicate the complex pathophysiological conditions of cardiovascular diseases, such as metabolic disorders, inflammation, and hypoxia. These simplified models fail to fully reflect the multifactorial pathological environment of human vascular diseases, where metabolic abnormalities, inflammatory responses, and hemodynamic stress act in concert. Consequently, the differential activation patterns of the cGAS-STING pathway under such multifactorial conditions remain poorly defined. Moreover, there is currently a lack of clinically validated biomarkers that accurately reflect the activity of this pathway in patient populations, which significantly hampers the translation of preclinical findings into clinical applications. In addition, long-term inhibition of innate immune signaling may disrupt essential homeostatic functions, highlighting the need to carefully evaluate the safety, dosage control, and potential off-target effects of pathway inhibitors [135, 136].

Insufficient research on cell-specific roles and non-canonical mechanisms

Current evidence mainly focuses on macrophages and neutrophils, while studies on the roles of B cells, dendritic cells, and eosinophils remain limited. Meanwhile, the specific mechanisms of non-canonical signaling pathways require further validation. In-depth analysis of the unique characteristics of these immune cells and their interactions with endothelial and smooth muscle cells, including the crosstalk between canonical and non-canonical pathways [137], is crucial for achieving precise therapeutic interventions.

Need for integrated analysis of pathway interactions

The cGAS-STING axis does not act in isolation but interacts with other regulatory pathways, including TLRs, Hippo-YAP, and BMP signaling. However, the dynamic balance among these pathways and their temporal activation sequences during vascular injury are poorly characterized. Future studies combining proteomics, phospho-proteomics, and computational network modeling could identify key regulatory nodes and guide the design of combinatorial therapeutic strategies.

Emerging research and technological directions

To overcome current limitations, future research should integrate single-cell RNA sequencing, spatial transcriptomics, and advanced imaging to map the spatial–temporal dynamics of cGAS-STING activation within vascular tissues. Furthermore, the development of bioresponsive and intelligent nanocarriers, such as pH- or ROS-sensitive systems, may enhance targeted delivery and minimize systemic toxicity [138]. However, issues including manufacturing cost, stability, and long-term safety remain major barriers to clinical translation [139, 140]. Artificial intelligence-driven drug design and organ-on-a-chip models may accelerate preclinical screening and improve predictability [141].

Clinical outlook

Ultimately, precise modulation of the cGAS-STING pathway requires the convergence of basic mechanistic research, innovative technologies, and translational studies. Future clinical strategies should focus on personalized therapeutic approaches, biomarker-guided patient selection, and combination therapies that balance immune activation and suppression. Such integrative efforts will pave the way for the safe and effective targeting of the cGAS-STING pathway in cardiovascular diseases.

Conclusion

The cGAS-STING pathway regulates immune cell infiltration through both canonical and non-canonical mechanisms and serves as a core regulatory hub in vascular remodeling. Its inhibitors and combination therapies have shown therapeutic potential. However, limitations such as cell specificity, associations with non-canonical pathways, model translation, and pathway crosstalk still need to be addressed. In the future, approaches including single-cell technologies, multi-aetiology models, systems biology, and nanomedicine will enable precise modulation of the pathway, providing new strategies for immune-targeted therapy of cardiovascular diseases.
