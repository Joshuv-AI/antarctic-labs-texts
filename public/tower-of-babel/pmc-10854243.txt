Introduction

The incidence of pediatric maxillofacial fractures is <15% among all facial fractures. Over which, their occurrence is even rare, about 0.6–1.4% in children below the age of 5 years.1 Incidence is supported by various etiological factors like unsupervised physical activities, sports injuries, falls, and road traffic accidents.2 The most common fractures seen in children are mandibular fractures, and boys show more predilection than girls in all age groups.3 A study by Posnick et al. reported the incidence of mandibular fractures to be 39% of all facial fractures, and the most common site involved was the condylar region (55%) followed by parasymphysis (27%), body (9%), and angle (8%).4 Anatomic features of the face and mandible can be a reason attributed to the high incidence of facial fractures. At birth, the ratio of cranium to face is 8:1, which acts as a protective shield to the face as the face is in a retruded position to the cranium. With increasing age, this ratio keeps on decreasing as the face and mandible grow in a forward and downward direction and become more prone to fractures.5 Children have high osteogenic potential and rapid healing rate; therefore, fibrous union can occur within 3–4 days. This indicates the need to reduce the fracture as early as possible.6 Also, the masticatory stresses help in good bone remodeling even if there is no perfect reduction; thus, minimal manipulation is required for the reduction of fractures.6 The ultimate goal of the treatment is to achieve a bony union with satisfactory occlusion, restoring the normal form and function with minimal negative effects on the growth of the child.

The present case report emphasized the positive outcomes of the treatment done by lesser showcased mode of noninvasive treatment using orthodontic archwires and elastic traction for maxillomandibular fixation (MMF) in a young child.

Case Description

A 5-year-old boy reported to the Department of Pedodontics, Postgraduate Institute of Dental Sciences, Rohtak, Haryana, India, with a history of falling from a bike on the same day. The patient was awake with normal vitals. Clinical examination revealed a bruise over the chin, anterior open bite, deranged occlusion, step deformity on the left side mandible, and deviation to the right side, with limitation during mouth opening (Fig. 1). A slight swelling was present over the right side of the face (Fig. 2). An orthopantomogram (OPG) was advised for the radiological examination which revealed a right condylar fracture and left parasymphysis fracture (Fig. 3). The child was young with primary dentition, so open reduction and internal fixation was not considered for the treatment as it could traumatize the developing tooth buds. Moreover, mouth opening was limited, so a cap splint could not be fabricated. Considering all the factors, immobilization using orthodontic archwires was planned. Under local anesthesia, banding was done on all the second primary molars, and orthodontic brackets were bonded under isolation. A 018 nickel–titanium (NiTi) archwire was placed in the upper arch, and a 014 NiTi archwire in the lower arch as the step was present in the parasymphyseal region and secured with elastic modules. Red elastics were used for traction in order to reduce the step deformity, and MMF was done for 1 week (Fig. 4). The patient was kept on a liquid diet for this period. After 1 week, MMF was removed, and guiding elastics were placed in order to guide and stabilize the occlusion, which was advised to be changed daily by the parents. (Fig. 5). Various mouth exercises were taught to the patient so that the risk of ankylosis could be reduced, and porridge with a liquid diet was advised to the patient. The patient was recalled after 2 weeks, and an OPG of the fractured site was taken to check the proper reduction of fractured segments at the parasymphysis area (Fig. 6). Debonding was done, and bands were removed. The patient was advised to have a semisolid diet for 1 month and was kept on follow-up at 1, 3, and 6 months. Mouth opening, occlusion, and deviation were evaluated at every follow-up visit. OPG was taken at 6 months, which revealed complete healing of the fractured sites (parasymphysis as well as condylar area), and clinically fully satisfactory occlusion was present with good mouth opening having no deviation (Figs 789).

Discussion

The occurrence of fractures in pediatric patients is very traumatic to both the child and his parents. Proper management depends on timely diagnosis and appropriate intervention. Radiological examinations, including OPG and cone-beam computed tomography, should be done in order to diagnose the type and extent of fracture, along with clinical examination.7

The management protocols in children differ from those in adults due to the presence of developing tooth buds, small jaw size, and active growth potential. Depending upon the type of fracture, the time elapsed since fracture, the type of dentition present, and the stage of growth and development, treatment options can be conservative, noninvasive methods like closed reduction, immobilization, or fixation to open reduction and internal fixation.8 Sometimes open reduction and internal fixation leads to destruction of the periosteal architecture of bone which ultimately exerts unpredicted negative effects on the growth of child. Moreover, permanent tooth buds lying in the mandible are also at risk of damage due to open reduction, and healing by scarring is also a disadvantage.9 Therefore, closed reduction is favored over open reduction when the fracture is undisplaced and satisfactory occlusion is present.

Irrespective of all the above-mentioned issues and problems, the goal of the treatment remains to simulate the bone architecture to a preinjury state with conservative methods. In the literature, various treatment options for closed reduction are mentioned, like bridle wiring, eyelet wiring, and circummandibular wiring. Wiring by different techniques is widely used, but the limitation remains the cooperation of the patient and the risk of damaging the developing tooth buds. Cap splint fabrication is popularly used, but it is time-consuming and requires an impression of the arches for the fabrication of the splint, which demands good mouth opening. Laster et al. reported in a study about NiTi staples, which are relatively noninvasive and pain-free, can be easily inserted/removed, and do not get osseointegrated. One of the disadvantages is their superficial location, which increases the risk of inhibition of bone formation at the fracture site.11

Few case reports showing the use of orthodontic brackets and elastics for fixation have been reported in the literature. Magennis used modified brackets for use in IMF.12 Aizenbud used a modified orthodontic splint in his study where second primary molars were banded; stainless steel wire was soldered on the buccal and lingual aspect, splint was made over it.13 The use of orthodontic elastics was mentioned in one of his literature for creating compressive horizontal force in order to reduce the fractured area.

The present case report describes the use of orthodontic brackets and elastics in a new manner. Archwires were used in this case to completely stabilize all the teeth and produce effects similar to arch bars in the adult dentition. All second molars were banded, brackets were bonded, and NiTi archwires were secured in bracket slots using elastic modules. Over it, high-strength elastics were placed for IMF for 1 week. IMF for longer periods of time increases the risk of ankylosis in condylar fractures; therefore, it is restricted to 1 week, followed by active jaw movements.14 Follow-ups were done at 1 and 3 weeks and at 1, 3, and 6 months. Mouth opening and deviation were evaluated at each visit. A radiographic examination was done at the 6-month follow-up. The use of high-strength elastics not only provides traction but also secures the archwires in place and restricts their movements. No unwanted movements occur by the use of NiTi archwires in 1 week, and if they occur, their effects are not permanent and can resolve to normal after their removal. This method has not been used yet. Advantages of this method include ease of application, noninvasive, minimal manipulation required, and sufficient reduction can be achieved in 1 week. A disadvantage can be diet restriction for the period of IMF. The use of orthodontic archwires for fixation is something new that needs to be promoted, and more studies should be done in this context to evaluate the possible outcomes of the treatment approach.

Conclusion

Pediatric mandibular fractures should be managed conservatively wherever possible in order to prevent damage to developing tooth buds and unrestricted activity of the growth centers. The treatment approach reported in the article is new and can be used as an alternative to conventional methods.
