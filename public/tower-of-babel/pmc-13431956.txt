Educational Needs Analysis for Postoperative Early Ambulation Among Surgical‐Ward Nurses and Patients: Using Borich Needs Assessment and Locus for Focus Models
Authors: Minji Park, Younhee Jeong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAims and ObjectivesTo identify and prioritize educational needs for early ambulation after abdominal surgery from the perspectives of surgical‐ward nurses and postoperative patients.BackgroundEarly ambulation is a core component of enhanced recovery after abdominal surgery, yet educational gaps in nurses' practice and patients' participation remain underexplored.DesignA descriptive cross‐sectional study.MethodsThe study was conducted in a 2700‐bed tertiary hospital in Seoul, Republic of Korea from May to August 2022. Nurses (n = 100) and postoperative patients (n = 111) after abdominal surgery completed validated self‐report questionnaires assessing demographic and clinical characteristics and the perceived importance and performance of 15 early ambulation‐related activities using 5‐point Likert scales. Educational priorities were evaluated using the Borich Needs Assessment Model and the Locus for Focus Model.ResultsBoth nurses and patients rated early ambulation as important, but notable gaps between perceived importance and actual performance were identified. Pre‐ambulation preparation activities, including muscle‐strength assessment and simple in‐bed exercises, consistently showed the greatest discrepancies and were ranked as the highest‐priority unmet educational needs across both Borich and Locus for Focus analyses.ConclusionsEarly ambulation after abdominal surgery is highly valued but inconsistently implemented by nurses and patients, revealing substantial unmet educational needs. Focusing education on pre‐ambulation preparation, particularly muscle‐strength assessment and in‐bed exercises, may enhance engagement in early ambulation.Relevance to Clinical PracticeThe findings provide an evidence‐based rationale for developing targeted educational programmes for surgical‐ward nurses and postoperative patients that emphasize structured pre‐ambulation preparation. Integrating these priorities into postoperative care may strengthen early ambulation practices and support improved recovery outcomes.Reporting MethodSTROBE guidelines.Patient or Public ContributionPostoperative patients and surgical‐ward nurses participated in the study by completing the questionnaires used for data collection. No further patient or public involvement occurred in the design or analysis of this study.

Introduction

Early ambulation is an essential nursing intervention for enhancing postoperative outcomes and is considered one of the most effective strategies for reducing hospital length of stay (Olson et al. 2021; Tazreean et al. 2022). Beyond expediting discharge, timely mobilization lessens the risk of common postoperative complications and accelerates functional recovery (Kanejima et al. 2020). In contrast, prolonged bed rest is a major contributor to delirium, venous thromboembolism, paralytic ileus, and pressure ulcers (Chen et al. 2020; Engel et al. 2022). Reflecting this evidence, Enhanced Recovery After Surgery (ERAS) guidelines strongly recommend early ambulation (Gustafsson et al. 2019). However, compliance remains suboptimal because ambulation is often perceived as one of the most complex nursing interventions to implement and evaluate in routine practice.

Background

Although clinicians recognize the value of early ambulation, several barriers remain, including nursing workload, staffing shortages, conflicting clinical priorities, uncontrolled patient pain, post‐operative drainage, the absence of standardized protocols, and inadequate education for both patients and staff (Edfeldt et al. 2024; Singam 2024; Tang et al. 2021; Tazreean et al. 2022; Xavier et al. 2024). Among these factors, education is a pivotal determinant of the motivation of nurses and patients to engage in early ambulation (Kim et al. 2019; Svensson‐Raskh et al. 2020). A systematic review by Tazreean et al. (2022) concluded that preoperative education is invaluable for overcoming traditional bed rest beliefs and enhancing patient motivation for early ambulation.

Designing effective, context‐specific educational and non‐educational interventions requires a formal needs analysis to identify gaps between current practice and desired standards (Graham et al. 2025). Because nurses facilitate ambulation and patients perform it, the perspectives of both groups must be considered (Brown et al. 2007). Recent studies have indeed demonstrated perceptual discrepancies between patients and health‐care professionals that may hinder implementation (Herzog et al. 2023).

Educational needs assessments provide a structured foundation for developing consistent, effective training programmes (Graham et al. 2025). Methods include the Borich Needs Assessment Model and the Locus for Focus Model, which are especially useful for prioritizing educational content by quantifying importance–performance gaps (Borich 1980). However, few studies have applied both models concurrently to explore educational needs related to early ambulation.

Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate the perceived importance and current performance of early ambulation among surgical‐ward nurses and postoperative patients after abdominal surgery, and to identify their educational priorities.

Methods

Design

A descriptive cross‐sectional design was employed to identify the educational needs associated with early ambulation after abdominal surgery from the perspectives of surgical‐ward nurses and postoperative patients.

Participants

This study was conducted in seven general surgical wards of a 2700‐bed tertiary hospital (Asan Medical center) in Seoul, Republic of Korea (May–August 2022). Nurses were eligible if they had at least 6 months of surgical‐ward experience; nurses with less than 6 months' experience or those temporarily seconded (floating) from other departments were excluded. Patients were eligible if they had undergone open abdominal surgery and had no contraindications to early ambulation; patients requiring prolonged bed rest or those unable to communicate were excluded. To reduce selection bias, we applied explicit inclusion and exclusion criteria for both nurses and patients, recruited participants from all seven general surgical wards, and consecutively invited eligible nurses and patients present during the data collection period who met the criteria and agreed to participate to complete the survey. Sample size was calculated with G*Power 3.1 using α = 0.05, power = 0.80, and a medium effect size (f 2 = 0.15), yielding a minimum of 85 participants per group. Anticipating an attrition rate of 20% among nurses and 30% among patients, 107 nurses and 121 patients were recruited (In et al. 2020; Iqhrammullah et al. 2024). After removal of incomplete questionnaires, data from 100 nurses and 111 patients were analysed.

Instruments

We used structured self‐report questionnaires to assess general characteristics as well as the perceived importance and performance of early ambulation. All items were adapted with permission from the original developer (Kim and Choi 2021) and validated by an expert panel (item‐level content validity index > 0.80 for every item).

General Characteristics

For nurses, the questionnaire collected information on sex, age, educational level, years of clinical experience, job position, prior education related to early ambulation, and current ward assignment. For patients, the questionnaire included items on sex, age, educational level, BMI classification, presence of a primary caregiver, diagnosis, comorbidities, admitting department, frequency of hospitalization, length of stay, and ward.

Perceived Importance of Early Ambulation

Importance was measured with a 5‐point Likert instrument (1 = not important at all to 5 = very important) developed by Kim and Choi (2021). Cronbach's α was 0.87 for nurses and 0.84 for patients.

Performance of Early Ambulation

Performance was assessed with a parallel 5‐point Likert scale (1 = never performed to 5 = always performed) developed by Kim and Choi (2021). Cronbach's α was 0.85 for nurses and 0.82 for patients.

Data Collection

All participants received the same written and verbal instructions, and questionnaires were completed anonymously without the presence of managerial staff to reduce social desirability bias. In this study, the term “postoperative” for patient participants did not refer to a fixed postoperative day but to the period after patients had undergone at least one episode of assisted ambulation and were clinically stable. Eligible patients were identified on the ward after their first ambulation, and recruitment and questionnaire completion took place at the same time. This approach ensured that all participating patients had already experienced early ambulation and were sufficiently alert to provide informed consent and complete the survey, thereby reducing the potential influence of immediate postoperative narcotic analgesia.

We collected key demographic and clinical variables for both nurses and patients and examined differences in perceived importance and performance across these characteristics using t‐tests, ANOVA, and non‐parametric tests where appropriate. This allowed us to explore potential confounding by factors such as age, education level, work setting, and prior education on early ambulation.

Ethical Considerations

This study received approval from the Asan Medical center's Institutional Review Board (IRB No. 2022‐0460) and the institution's nursing department. Data were collected by convenience sampling between 4 May and 23 August 2022. Participants received full information about the study purpose, procedures, voluntary participation, and data confidentiality prior to involvement. Written informed consent was obtained prior to participating in the study.

Data Analysis

Data were analysed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 27.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) with significance set at p < 0.05. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize participants' general characteristics. Independent t‐tests and one‐way ANOVA were used to examine differences in importance and performance across demographic variables. When normality assumptions were violated, Mann–Whitney U and Kruskal–Wallis tests were applied. Post hoc comparisons were conducted using Tukey's HSD test. Educational needs were analysed using paired t‐test and prioritized using the Borich Needs Assessment Model and the Locus for Focus Model. Detailed procedures for calculating Borich needs scores and applying the Locus for Focus Model have been described in a previous methodological paper, and the same analytic approach was adopted in the present study (Lee and Jeong 2022).

Differences in Perceived Importance and Performance of Early Ambulation

Significant differences in perceived importance and performance were observed according to nurses' characteristics. A one‐way ANOVA revealed a significant difference in the perceived importance of early ambulation across age groups (F = 3.93, p = 0.023). Tukey's HSD post hoc analysis indicated that nurses aged 40 years and older rated early ambulation as more important than those under 30 years. Male nurses reported significantly higher performance scores than female nurses (t = 2.69, p = 0.008). Nurses who had received prior education on early ambulation demonstrated higher performance scores than those without such training (t = 3.13, p = 0.002). In addition, nurses working in general wards reported higher importance scores than those working in comprehensive wards (t = 2.00, p = 0.048).

Educational Needs Analysis of Nurses

Importance–performance analysis and the Borich needs assessment, together with the Locus for Focus model for early ambulation among nurses and patients, are presented in Tables 3 and 4. Among nurses, a paired t‐test revealed statistically significant differences between the perceived importance and performance of most items related to early ambulation (Table 3). In particular, Items 4 (Ankle exercise in bed), 5 (Knee‐to‐chest), 6 (Raise head), 7 (Hips lifting in bed), 8 (Bedside ergometer), and 9 (Upper extremity exercise) demonstrated large effect sizes (Cohen's d > 0.85), indicating substantial gaps in actual performance. Cohen's d is typically interpreted using the following thresholds: values around 0.2 are considered a small effect, values around 0.5 a medium effect, and values around 0.8 a large effect (Cohen 1992). Based on the Borich needs assessment, the top five priority items, corresponding to the top 30%, were identified as follows: “Knee‐to‐chest (Item 5),” “Ankle exercise in bed (Item 4),” “Hips lifting in bed (Item 7),” “Raise head (Item 6),” and “Upper extremity exercise (Item 9).” Although these items, characterized by low importance and high discrepancy, their high Borich scores indicate substantial weighted gaps between importance and performance. Therefore, they should be considered priority areas for educational intervention. According to the Locus for Focus analysis (Figure 2), “Check muscle strength and range of motion (Item 1)” was located in Quadrant I (HH), while Items 4 through 9 were situated in Quadrant II (LH), indicating hidden educational needs—areas where performance is lacking despite lower perceived importance. Finally, a cross‐analysis of the top‐ranked items in the Borich assessment and the priority quadrants (HH, LH) of the Locus for Focus model revealed that Items 4, 5, 6, 7, and 9 were identified as high‐priority educational needs in both methods. These overlapping items should be considered core components in the development of targeted educational programmes for nursing staff. Furthermore, Item 1 (Check muscle strength and range of motion) also warrants attention. Although it ranked seventh in the Borich assessment, its Borich coefficient exceeded 2.00 and it was the only item located in Quadrant I (high importance–high discrepancy).

Educational Needs Analysis of Patients

Among patients, a paired t‐test revealed statistically significant differences between the perceived importance and performance of most items related to early ambulation (Table 4). In particular, Items 1 (Check muscle strength and range of motion), 5 (Knee‐to‐chest), 6 (Raise head), 8 (Bedside ergometer), and 9 (Upper extremity exercise) showed large effect sizes (Cohen's d ≥ 0.85), indicating substantial gaps between perceived importance and actual performance. According to the Borich needs assessment, the top 30% priority items were identified as: “Knee‐to‐chest (Item 5),” “Check muscle strength and range of motion (Item 1),” “Upper extremity exercise (Item 9),” “Bedside ergometer (Item 8),” and “Ankle exercise in bed (Item 4).” Although these items were characterized by low perceived importance and high discrepancy, their high Borich scores indicate substantial weighted gaps between importance and performance. Therefore, they should be considered priority areas for educational intervention. In the Locus for Focus model (Figure 3), Items 1 and 4 were positioned in Quadrant I (HH), while Items 5, 6, 7, 8, and 9 were located in Quadrant II (LH), indicating either core or hidden educational needs. Finally, a cross‐analysis between the Borich top‐ranked items and the Locus for Focus priority quadrants (HH and LH) revealed that Items 1, 4, 5, 8, and 9 were consistently identified as high‐priority educational needs by both methods. These overlapping items should be considered key targets in the development of early ambulation education programmes for patients.

Discussion

This study examined the perceived importance and performance of early ambulation after abdominal surgery among nurses and postoperative patients and identified priority educational needs using the Borich Needs Assessment and Locus for Focus models. The findings revealed a consistent gap between perceived importance and actual performance, indicating that early ambulation is widely recognized as important but not always implemented effectively in clinical practice. Notably, both nurses and patients demonstrated high educational needs in the pre‐ambulation preparation stage, particularly regarding the assessment of muscle strength and joint range of motion and the performance of simple in‐bed exercises. These findings suggest that early ambulation should be understood not as a single activity but as a structured process that begins with adequate preparation and patient education.

Nurses' and Patients' Perceptions of the Importance and Performance of Early Ambulation

This study identified statistically significant differences in nurses' perceptions of the importance and performance of early ambulation based on general characteristics. Nurses aged 40 years or older and those working in general wards perceived early ambulation as more important than nurses under 30 years or those working in integrated nursing care service wards. Regarding performance, male nurses and those with prior education related to early ambulation showed significantly higher scores, indicating that both gender and educational experience may influence clinical practice. These findings suggest that clinical experience and targeted education regarding early ambulation positively affect nurses' perceptions and practice of this intervention. This is consistent with Benner's Novice to Expert Theory (Benner 1984), which proposes that nurses progress through stages of increasing clinical competence as they gain experience, leading to enhanced confidence and more effective performance of nursing interventions. Recent studies also highlight the link between professional experience, education, and enhanced confidence in performing early ambulation (Kim et al. 2019). However, the current nursing workforce in Korea is relatively inexperienced. As of 2020, 24.2% of hospital nurses had between one and 3 years of experience, and over half (51.2%) had less than 5 years of experience, indicating a shortage of experienced nurses in clinical settings (Hospital Nurses Association 2021). This lack of experience is linked to increased missed nursing care, especially in direct patient care such as early ambulation (Bae 2024). Compared to nurses in general wards, those in integrated nursing care service wards reported lower perceived importance of early ambulation, likely because the role of patient ambulation is often delegated to supporting staff in these settings. This may lead nurses to perceive it as outside their primary responsibility, potentially contributing to missed nursing care (Kwon and Kang 2021; Moradi et al. 2024). These results highlight the importance of institutional support for continuous education and opportunities to accumulate clinical experience among nurses, as well as promoting awareness of their professional nursing roles.

For patients, there were no statistically significant differences in perceived importance or performance of early ambulation according to general characteristics such as sex, age, education level, BMI, or type of ward. These results indicate that sociodemographic factors may play a limited role in patients' early ambulation. Instead, individual motivation, health beliefs, emotional responses, and support from healthcare providers appear to be more influential (Higgins et al. 2021; Xavier et al. 2024; Xu et al. 2025). For example, patients frequently retain a historical belief that prolonged bed rest after surgery is essential for recovery, resulting in lower motivation to ambulate early (Svensson‐Raskh et al. 2020). A lack of clear explanation, motivation, and emotional support from healthcare providers can hinder patient engagement, while targeted educational interventions are essential for dispelling misconceptions and enhancing participation (Lim et al. 2020). Pain was consistently identified as the most significant barrier (Park et al. 2023; Tang et al. 2021), underscoring the critical role of effective pain management in improving patient mobility (Rivas et al. 2022). In addition to pain management, innovative approaches such as delivering education via animations that illustrate the harm of prolonged bed rest (Jones et al. 2019) and using wearable activity trackers (Twomey et al. 2020) have recently been implemented. These approaches have shown potential to increase patients' understanding, motivation, and engagement in early ambulation. These findings suggest that patient education should incorporate not only information but also emotional support and technological strategies to promote engagement in early ambulation.

Educational Needs for Early Ambulation

The needs analysis showed that both nurses and patients reported lower performance compared with perceived importance, confirming unmet educational needs. A cross‐analysis using the Borich needs assessment and the Locus for Focus model identified priority items as 4, 5, 6, 7, and 9 for nurses and 1, 4, 5, 8, and 9 for patients. Notably, Items 4 (Ankle exercises in bed), 5 (Knee‐to‐chest), and 9 (Upper extremity exercise) overlapped in both groups. These items focus on pre‐ambulation preparation conducted in bed, emphasizing that early ambulation is not just about walking but is a structured clinical process where preparatory actions play a crucial role. Prior studies support the importance of this preparation. Schram et al. (2019) and Simsek Yaban et al. (2023) emphasized that educating patients on in‐bed exercises improves compliance, highlighting the necessity of repeated preoperative instruction. However, current findings suggest that these preparatory steps are often overlooked (Kim and Choi 2021). For patients, “Assessing my muscle strength and joint range of motion before surgery” was identified as the top educational need among patients, pointing to the importance of self‐awareness and physical readiness. Because pain, anxiety, or fear of falling are major barriers to patients' ambulation (Lim et al. 2020), education should help patients understand their capabilities in advance. Nurses play a critical role in facilitating this by assessing physical function and guiding patients to prepare appropriately using supportive tools and explanations (Tazreean et al. 2022). This can enhance patients' confidence and encourage participation in early ambulation (Svensson‐Raskh et al. 2020).

These findings call for the development of structured, stage‐based educational programmes that target both nurses and patients, integrating pain management, repeated preoperative instruction, and emotional support. Such interventions are expected to enhance patient participation, prevent missed nursing care, and ultimately improve postoperative recovery outcomes.

Organizational Context and Transferability of Findings

The organizational context of Korean surgical wards and integrated nursing care service wards also needs to be considered when interpreting the transferability of these findings. In integrated nursing care service wards, for example, the responsibility for patient ambulation is often delegated to healthcare assistants or support staff rather than being viewed as a core nursing task (Kwon and Kang 2021; Moradi et al. 2024). As suggested by previous work, such delegation can function as a double‐edged sword, potentially improving efficiency but also blurring professional boundaries and increasing the risk of missed nursing care (Moradi et al. 2024). Similar patterns of delegation and role diffusion have been reported in other health systems, but the extent and nature of task‐shifting between registered nurses and support staff vary considerably across countries and institutions. Consequently, while the identification of pre‐ambulation preparation and simple in‐bed exercises as high‐priority educational needs is likely to be relevant internationally, the degree to which these results can be directly transferred to other settings may depend on local staffing models, nurse‐to‐patient ratios and the scope of practice of support workers. Future cross‐national studies are warranted to examine how different organizational structures and delegation practices influence nurses' and patients' engagement in early ambulation and to tailor educational strategies accordingly.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the data were collected from nurses and postoperative patients in a single tertiary hospital, which may limit the generalisability of the findings to other clinical settings. Second, the study relied on self‐reported perceptions of importance and performance, which may be subject to response bias. Third, the cross‐sectional design precludes causal interpretation of the observed relationships. Despite these limitations, this study provides a comprehensive analysis of educational priorities for early ambulation by integrating the Borich Needs Assessment and Locus for Focus models. This combined approach offers a systematic method for identifying priority areas for nursing education and may serve as a useful framework for future studies examining clinical education needs in perioperative care.

Strengths and Implications

Despite these limitations, this study has several strengths and important implications for clinical nursing practice. It simultaneously examined the perspectives of both surgical‐ward nurses and postoperative abdominal‐surgery patients, providing a comprehensive view of early ambulation that reflects the realities of those who facilitate and those who perform mobilization. By combining importance–performance analysis with the Borich Needs Assessment and Locus for Focus models, the study offers a rigorous and transparent approach for prioritizing educational needs that can be readily adapted in other clinical settings. The identification of specific high‐priority areas—particularly muscle‐strength assessment and simple in‐bed preparatory exercises—moves beyond generic recommendations to provide actionable targets for nurse and patient education programmes. In the context of enhanced recovery after surgery pathways, these findings contribute practical, evidence‐informed guidance that can help reduce missed nursing care and support safer, more consistent implementation of early ambulation worldwide.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study identified key educational priorities related to early ambulation among surgical‐ward nurses and postoperative patients. Both groups recognized the importance of early ambulation but demonstrated gaps between perceived importance and actual performance. Pre‐ambulation preparation, particularly assessing muscle strength and joint range of motion and performing simple in‐bed exercises, emerged as the highest‐priority educational need. These findings suggest that targeted education focusing on structured pre‐ambulation preparation may strengthen the implementation of early ambulation in postoperative care. Developing and integrating such educational strategies into clinical practice may help support safer and more consistent postoperative mobilization and contribute to improved recovery outcomes.
