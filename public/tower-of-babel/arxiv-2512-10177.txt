Bell coloring graphs: realizability and reconstruction
Shamil Asgarli, Sara Krehbiel, Simon MacLean

Abstract
. Given a graph G G , the Bell k k -coloring graph ℬ k ​ ( G ) \mathcal{B}_{k}(G) has vertices given by partitions of V ⁡ ( G ) V(G) into k k independent sets (allowing empty parts), with two partitions adjacent if they differ only in the placement of a single vertex. We first give a structural classification of cliques in Bell coloring graphs. We then show that all trees and all cycles arise as Bell coloring graphs, while K 4 − e K_{4}-e is not a Bell coloring graph and, more generally, K n − e K_{n}-e is not an induced subgraph of any Bell coloring graph whenever n ≥ 6 n\geq 6 . Preliminaries

2.1.
Notation and conventions

Let
\mathbb{N}=\{1,2,3,\dots\}
denote the set of positive integers. For sets
X
and
Y
, we use the notation
X\setminus Y=\{x\in X\ |\ x\notin Y\}
for the set difference and
X\cup Y
for set union; in the special case when
Y=\{v\}
is a singleton, we also denote
X\setminus\{v\}
by
X-v
and
X\cup\{v\}
by
X+v
. Given a graph
G
, we let
\overline{G}
denote its complement. We write
G\sqcup H
for the disjoint union of graphs
G
and
H
.

Definition 2.1
.

A
stable
k
-partition
of a graph
G
is a multiset
P=\{V_{1},\dots,V_{k}\}
of independent sets that partition
V(G)
, where we allow some of the parts
V_{i}
to be empty.

Let
\mathcal{P}_{k}(G)
denote the set of all stable
k
-partitions of
G
. For
v\in V(G)
, we write

P-v\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\{V_{i}-v:1\leq i\leq k\},

viewed as a multiset of
k
subsets.

A Bell
k
-coloring graph
\mathcal{B}_{k}(G)
is the graph whose vertex set is
\mathcal{P}_{k}(G)
. Two vertices
P_{1},P_{2}\in\mathcal{P}_{k}(G)
are adjacent if and only if
P_{1}-v=P_{2}-v
for some
v\in V(G)
.

Throughout, partitions
P,Q,R\in\mathcal{P}_{k}(G)
are treated as
unordered multisets
of parts. We write
P\sim Q
to denote adjacency in
\mathcal{B}_{k}(G)
. To specify the vertex responsible for the adjacency, we use the following notation:

P\sim_{v}Q\iff P\sim Q\text{ and }P-v=Q-v.

We say that a vertex
v\in V(G)
is
responsible
for an edge
PQ
in
\mathcal{B}_{k}(G)
if
P\sim_{v}Q
. Equivalently, the edge
PQ
is
realized
by
v
. The condition
P-v=Q-v
implies that
P
and
Q
agree on the structure of the partition when restricted to
V(G)\setminus\{v\}
.

We record the following useful fact, which we use several times in the paper: if
P_{1}\sim_{v}P_{2}
and
P_{2}\sim_{v}P_{3}
, then
P_{1}
and
P_{3}
agree on
V(G)\setminus\{v\}
, so
P_{1}\sim_{v}P_{3}
.

2.3.
Multiple edges

As we see in Figure
3
, an interesting structural feature of Bell coloring graphs is that two partitions might be adjacent via more than one vertex simultaneously. The following lemma characterizes this phenomenon.

Lemma 2.5

(Characterization of doubly realized edges)
.

Let
P,Q\in\mathcal{P}_{k}(G)
be distinct partitions. If
P\sim_{v}Q
and
P\sim_{w}Q
for distinct vertices
v,w\in V(G)
, then
v
and
w
are nonadjacent in
G
. Furthermore,
P
and
Q
decompose as:

\displaystyle P=\{\{v,w\},\emptyset\}\cup R,\quad\text{ and }\quad Q=\{\{v\},\{w\}\}\cup R

for some multiset
R
consisting of
k-2
independent sets.

Proof.

Since
P\sim_{v}Q
, the partitions coincide when
v
is removed. Thus, there exist sets
V_{1},V_{2}\subseteq V(G)\setminus\{v\}
and a collection of parts
R^{\prime}
such that:

\displaystyle P

\displaystyle=\{V_{1}\cup\{v\},V_{2}\}\cup R^{\prime},

\displaystyle Q

\displaystyle=\{V_{1},V_{2}\cup\{v\}\}\cup R^{\prime}.

Since
P\neq Q
, we must have
V_{1}\neq V_{2}
. Now consider the condition
P\sim_{w}Q
. If
w
were in some part
C\in R^{\prime}
, then
P-w=Q-w
would imply
\{V_{1}\cup\{v\},V_{2}\}=\{V_{1},V_{2}\cup\{v\}\}
, forcing
V_{1}=V_{2}
, a contradiction. Thus
w\in V_{1}
or
w\in V_{2}
. Without loss of generality, assume
w\in V_{1}
.

Write
V_{1}=V_{1}^{\prime}\cup\{w\}
. Then:

\displaystyle P-w

\displaystyle=\{V_{1}^{\prime}\cup\{v\},V_{2}\}\cup R^{\prime},

\displaystyle Q-w

\displaystyle=\{V_{1}^{\prime},V_{2}\cup\{v\}\}\cup R^{\prime}.

Equality forces the multisets to be the same:
\{V_{1}^{\prime}\cup\{v\},V_{2}\}=\{V_{1}^{\prime},V_{2}\cup\{v\}\}
. This implies
V_{1}^{\prime}=V_{2}
. For the parts of
P
to be disjoint, we must have
(V_{1}^{\prime}\cup\{w,v\})\cap V_{2}=\emptyset
. Since
V_{1}^{\prime}=V_{2}
, this requires
V_{1}^{\prime}=\emptyset
, so
V_{2}=\emptyset
. Thus
P=\{\{v,w\},\emptyset\}\cup R^{\prime}
and
Q=\{\{v\},\{w\}\}\cup R^{\prime}
. Finally, since
\{v,w\}
is a part in
P
,
v
and
w
must be nonadjacent in
G
.
∎

We encountered this double–edge phenomenon in the smallest case

\mathcal{B}_{2}(\overline{K}_{2})\cong K_{2}
: there are two partitions

P=\{\{u,v\},\emptyset\}
and
Q=\{\{u\},\{v\}\}
, and both
u
and
v

are responsible for the adjacency
P\sim Q
. Thus, the unique edge of

\mathcal{B}_{2}(\overline{K}_{2})
acts like a doubly realized edge, corresponding exactly to the
special case of Lemma
2.5
with
R=\emptyset
.

Remark 2.6
.

Let
P,Q\in\mathcal{P}_{k}(G)
be distinct partitions. Then
PQ
is a doubly realized edge with distinct
v,w\in V(G)
such that
P\sim_{v}Q
and
P\sim_{w}Q
if and only if there is a collection
R
of
k-2
parts such that, up to relabeling of parts,

P=\{\{v,w\},\emptyset\}\cup R\quad\text{and}\quad Q=\{\{v\},\{w\}\}\cup R.

In particular, at most two vertices of
G
can be responsible for a single edge
PQ
in
\mathcal{B}_{k}(G)
. Indeed, if three distinct vertices
v,w,x
were all responsible for
PQ
, then applying Lemma
2.5
to the pairs
(v,w)
and
(v,x)
would force two different decompositions of
P
of the above form, contradicting the uniqueness of the part containing
v
. Equivalently, for every edge
PQ
in
\mathcal{B}_{k}(G)
, the set

\{u\in V(G):P\sim_{u}Q\}
has size at most
2
.

The relationship between colorings that give rise to cliques in a

for every edge

of the clique. In Subsection

, we use our classification result to prove that certain nearly complete graphs cannot appear as Bell coloring graphs or as induced subgraphs of Bell coloring graphs.

Lemma 3.7
.

Let

. Let

We now state and prove the general classification theorem.

Theorem 3.8
.

For any clique
\mathcal{K}=\{P_{1},\dots,P_{m}\}
with

3.8
.

Let
\mathcal{K}=\{P_{1},\dots,P_{m}\}
be a clique in

. Let

, we have
|V_{ij}|\in\{1,2\}
. We proceed by a case analysis on

Case

(vertex).

is an

-clique.

Case

(edge).

The unique edge in

is either singly realized or doubly realized (see Lemma

is an

Case

(triangle).

Apply Lemma

to

Case

(tetrahedron).
If all faces are

,

Otherwise,

contains a

. By Lemma

. Any fourth vertex

is determined entirely by the restrictions of its vertices to

The graph of stable partitions on three vertices is isomorphic to

).

3.2.
Forbidden induced subgraphs

As a first application of the clique classification, we identify a small graph that cannot occur as a Bell coloring graph.

.

The diamond graph

Suppose

4.
Matching graphs as a source of Bell coloring graphs

In this section, we introduce the matching reconfiguration graph and establish its connection to Bell coloring graphs. As a consequence, we obtain Theorem
4.9
, stating that all trees and cycle graphs are realizable as Bell coloring graphs.

Recall that a
matching
is a set of edges that are pairwise vertex-disjoint. For a matching
M\subseteq E(G)
, we let
G\langle M\rangle
denote the spanning subgraph of
G
with edge set precisely
M
. In other words,
G\langle M\rangle
is obtained from
G
by removing all edges in
E[G]\setminus M
, while preserving the vertex set of
G
.

Definition 4.1
.

Given a graph
G
and
k\in\mathbb{N}
, the
matching reconfiguration graph

\mathcal{M}_{k}(G)
is the graph whose vertex set consists of all matchings in
G
of size at least
k
. Two matchings
M_{1},M_{2}
are adjacent if there exists
v\in V(G)
such that
G\langle M_{1}\rangle-v=G\langle M_{2}\rangle-v
.

We write
M_{1}\sim_{v}M_{2}
to indicate adjacency via
v
, which implies that
M_{1}
and
M_{2}
differ only by edges incident to
v
. We write
M_{1}\sim M_{2}
if
M_{1}\sim_{v}M_{2}
for some
v\in V(G)
.

We show that the matching graph

has a Bell coloring graph interpretation. Figure

4.2
establishes the general result.

Proposition 4.2
.

If
G
is a triangle-free graph of order

, then
\mathcal{M}_{k}(G)\cong\mathcal{B}_{n-k}(\overline{G})
.

Proof.

We construct a graph isomorphism between
\mathcal{M}_{k}(G)
and
\mathcal{B}_{n-k}(\overline{G})
. Given a matching
M\subseteq E(G)
, we define a stable partition
\Phi(M)
of

. Suppose

contains edges
u_{1}v_{1},\dots,u_{\ell}v_{\ell}
and isolated vertices
w_{1},\dots,w_{n-2\ell}
where
\ell\geq k
. Let

\Phi(M)=\{\{u_{1},v_{1}\},\dots,\{u_{\ell},v_{\ell}\},\{w_{1}\},\dots,\{w_{n-2\ell}\}\}.

For each
1\leq i\leq\ell
, the set
\{u_{i},v_{i}\}
is indeed independent in
\overline{G}
. The map
\Phi
is injective because a stable partition of the above form uniquely determines the underlying matching. Moreover,
\Phi
is surjective because
\overline{G}
contains no independent set of size three (equivalently,
G
is triangle-free), so every stable
(n-k)
-partition of the indicated form comes from a matching.

Finally, note that
M_{1}
and
M_{2}
are adjacent matchings if
G\langle M_{1}\rangle-v=G\langle M_{2}\rangle-v
. This is equivalent to
\Phi(M_{1})-v=\Phi(M_{2})-v
. We deduce that
\Phi
is a graph isomorphism.
∎

The isomorphism in Proposition
4.2
enables us to realize all cycles and trees as Bell coloring graphs. We focus on the case where
n=|V(G)|=2k+1
. More formally, if
n=2k+1
then we call
\mathcal{M}_{k}(G)
the
near-perfect matching graph
of
G
. The following lemma gives three equivalent conditions for two near-perfect matchings to be adjacent in a matching graph, which helps us reason about matching graphs that are cycles. In particular, it shows that near-perfect matchings are adjacent if and only if the unmatched vertices
u
and
v
have a common neighbor
w
such that the symmetric difference of the matching is
\{uw,uv\}
.

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
M_{1},M_{2}
be two near-perfect matchings of
G
with
v
unmatched in
M_{1}
and
u
unmatched in
M_{2}
. The following are equivalent:

(1)

G\langle M_{1}\rangle-w=G\langle M_{2}\rangle-w
for some
w\in V(G)
.

(2)

M_{1}=(M_{2}\setminus\{vw\})\cup\{uw\}
for some
w\in V(G)
.

(3)

uw\in M_{1}
,
vw\in M_{2}
, and
G\langle M_{1}\rangle-u-v-w=G\langle M_{2}\rangle-u-v-w
for some
w\in V(G)
.

Proof.

The desired equivalence follows from three separate implications.

(
1
)
\implies
(
3
). Suppose
G\langle M_{1}\rangle-w=G\langle M_{2}\rangle-w
. Then certainly
G\langle M_{1}\rangle-u-v-w=G\langle M_{2}\rangle-u-v-w
. This implies that any edges in
M_{1}
, but not in
M_{2}
, must be between the vertices
u,v,w
. Since only one such edge can be present in any valid matching, there must be
k-1
edges present in
G\langle M_{1}\rangle-u-v-w
. Since
G\langle M_{1}\rangle-u-v-w
contains only
2k-2
vertices, there must not be any edges in
M_{1}
or
M_{2}
with one endpoint in
\{u,v,w\}
and another in
G\setminus\{u,v,w\}
. Therefore, both
M_{1}
and
M_{2}
consist of
k-1
common edges with both endpoints in
G\setminus\{u,v,w\}
, and one edge each in the set
\{uv,uw,vw\}
. If
uv\in M_{1}
, then
G\langle M_{1}\rangle-w
has
k
edges, which would fix
M_{1}=M_{2}
, a contradiction. Therefore, by the distinctness of
M_{1}
and
M_{2}
, we must have that
uw\in M_{1}
and
vw\in M_{2}
, up to relabeling.

(
3
)
\implies
(
2
). Suppose that
uw\in M_{1}
,
vw\in M_{2}
, and
G\langle M_{1}\rangle-u-v-w=G\langle M_{2}\rangle-u-v-w
. This means that
M_{1}=E(G\langle M_{1}\rangle-u-v-w)\cup\{uw\}
and
M_{2}=E(G\langle M_{2}\rangle-u-v-w)\cup\{vw\}
. Therefore,
(M_{2}\setminus\{vw\})\cup\{uw\}=E(G\langle M_{2}\rangle-u-v-w)\cup\{uw\}=M_{1}
.

(
2
)
\implies
(
1
). Suppose
M_{1}=(M_{2}\setminus\{vw\})\cup\{uw\}
, then
M_{1}\setminus\{uw\}=M_{2}\setminus\{vw\}
. This implies that
G\langle M_{1}\rangle-w=G\langle M_{1}\setminus\{uw\}\rangle-w=G\langle M_{2}\setminus\{vw\}\rangle-w=G\langle M_{2}\rangle-w
, as desired.
∎

We are now ready to prove that every odd cycle is a Bell coloring graph. Figure

Lemma 4.4

We conclude that

∎

We can build many near-perfect matching graphs with the help of Propositions

and

to show that any tree is a near-perfect matching graph of another tree.

Corollary 4.8

Let

be the tree of order

\mathcal{B}_{n}(\overline{T_{s}})\cong T
.

Proof.

We first argue that the near-perfect matching graph of any subdivided tree is the tree itself. As a base case, observe that
\mathcal{M}_{1}(P_{3})=P_{2}
, with the ends of the
P_{2}
corresponding to the near-perfect matching with the leaves of
P_{3}
unmatched.

Let
T
be a tree of order
n
. Because any tree can be described as a leaf attached to a smaller tree, let
T^{\prime}
be the tree of order
n-1
and let

be such that

for either

. Assume inductively that

is such that the near-perfect matching graph of

is

and all vertices in the bipartition of

containing its leaves are uniquely unmatched vertices in

. Then applying Proposition
4.7
, we conclude that the near-perfect matching graph of

is

.

Finally, Proposition
4.2
gives that

.
∎

Proposition
4.7
can be applied in other ways to yield additional families of graphs realizable as Bell coloring graphs. For example, note that although the graphs yielding even cycles do not have uniquely unmatched vertices, odd cycles do. Therefore, any graph that can be constructed by iteratively joining odd cycles is a Bell coloring graph. We leave further exploration of families of graphs that can be constructed similarly for future work and instead summarize our results of this section as follows.

Theorem 4.9
.

All trees and all cycle graphs are realizable as Bell coloring graphs.

Example 5.5

Given an integer

This yields a bijection between

, so

We now translate adjacency. Let

be two such partitions, with corresponding strings

and

(i) Moving

for

If

(ii) Moving

of

via

In a tree with at least three vertices, two adjacent vertices cannot both be leaves, so at most one of

and

exists, and in any case

. Thus, there is no partition

that is adjacent to both

via
v
and
Z_{v}
via

, a contradiction.
∎

This lemma is particularly powerful when combined with our ability to identify from

the unique vertex

whose corresponding partition has an empty part. We can identify this vertex because Lemma
5.3
says that it has degree

5.2
ensures it is the unique vertex of maximum degree. With

by a vertex of

and then apply Lemma
5.7
to identify exactly those pairs of vertices that are adjacent. This adjacency matrix completely describes the tree, and the corollary below follows.

Corollary 5.8
.

Let
T_{1}
and
T_{2}
be two trees of order
n
with bipartitions
Z_{1}=\{X_{1},Y_{1},\emptyset\}
and
Z_{2}=\{X_{2},Y_{2},\emptyset\}
with
\lvert X_{1}\rvert,\lvert X_{2}\rvert,\lvert Y_{1}\rvert,\lvert Y_{2}\rvert\geq 3
. Then
\mathcal{B}_{3}(T_{1})\cong\mathcal{B}_{3}(T_{2})
if and only if
T_{1}\cong T_{2}
.

We now combine the classification of tree types with the specific reconstruction results for each case to prove the main result on Bell
3
-colorings of trees.

Theorem 5.9
.

For trees

and
T_{2}
, we have
\mathcal{B}_{3}(T_{1})\cong\mathcal{B}_{3}(T_{2})
if and only if
T_{1}\cong T_{2}
.

Proof.

One of the implications is immediate. For the converse, assume

. Since the graphs have

vertices,
T_{1}
and
T_{2}
must have the same order. By Lemma
5.4
,
T_{1}
and
T_{2}
must belong to the same class of trees (generic, double broom, or star) based on the degree sequence of the Bell coloring graph.

If they are stars, they are trivially isomorphic as they share the same order. If they are double brooms, Lemma
5.6
shows that nonisomorphic double brooms yield Bell coloring graphs with different edge counts, forcing
T_{1}\cong T_{2}
. If they are generic, Lemma
5.7
allows the unique reconstruction of the adjacency matrix of the tree from the common neighbors of the vertices adjacent to the unique bipartition vertex
Z
.
∎
