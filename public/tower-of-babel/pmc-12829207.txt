Identification of the consequences of the proliferation of counterfeit sports supplements market
Authors: Masoud Mohamadi Askarabadi, Bahram Yousefi, Zohreh Hasani, Rastegar Hoseini, Saeed Kazemi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThis study aimed to identify and conceptualize the systemic consequences of the proliferation of counterfeit sports supplements in Iran—an understudied context marked by fragmented regulation and high demand for performance enhancement. Moving beyond isolated health-risk assessments, the research developed an integrated empirical framework drawing on public health, criminology, and sports management perspectives.ResultsThrough 19 in-depth, semi-structured interviews with consumers, illicit suppliers, and sports nutrition and anti-doping experts—analyzed via Glaserian grounded theory—six interrelated consequence domains emerged: [1] multidimensional health harms; [2] erosion of trust in institutions and brands; [3] athletic performance deterioration and career disruption; [4] inadvertent anti-doping and legal violations; [5] expansion of organized illicit markets; and [6] structural economic harm to legitimate supplement businesses. Findings are grounded in role-specific narratives and contextualized within Iran’s regulatory gaps and transnational counterfeiting dynamics. The study proposes actionable interventions, including legal reform, digital traceability, inter-agency coordination, and targeted public awareness.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13104-025-07619-z.

Introduction

The global sports supplement industry has expanded rapidly, driven by increasing participation in fitness culture and the pursuit of athletic enhancement [1, 2]. In Iran, this trend is amplified by a burgeoning bodybuilding subculture and limited access to regulated pharmaceutical alternatives [3]. However, rising demand has created fertile ground for the infiltration of counterfeit products—items that mimic legitimate supplements but are produced without quality control, often containing undeclared, banned, or toxic substances [4, 5]. While international literature has addressed health risks of adulterated supplements [6, 7], there remains a critical gap in understanding how these products operate within specific socio-legal ecosystems—particularly in settings where regulatory capacity is weak and organized crime exploits market vulnerabilities [8–10]. Iran exemplifies such a context: despite nominal oversight by the Food and Drug Administration (FDA), enforcement is inconsistent, penalties are minimal, and coordination between health, sports, and judicial authorities is fragmented [3, 11].

This regulatory vacuum has real-world consequences. Between 2020 and 2023, at least 12 Iranian elite athletes—including national team members in weightlifting and bodybuilding—were disqualified from international competitions due to unintentional doping linked to counterfeit supplements [12, 13]. These cases underscore a systemic failure that extends beyond individual consumer choice [14, 15].

Existing Iranian studies (e.g., Souri & Mohammadi, 2025; Khalili et al., 2023) have primarily focused on behavioral determinants of supplement use but have not examined the structural drivers of counterfeiting or its macro-level impacts. Crucially, the literature lacks integration of criminological perspectives on illicit market formation and public health analyses of systemic risk—limiting the development of effective policy responses [16, 17].

Therefore, this study addresses three key objectives: [1] to identify and categorize the multidimensional consequences of counterfeit sports supplement proliferation in Iran; [2] to analyze how these consequences intersect across individual, institutional, and market levels; and [3] to propose actionable, context-specific interventions grounded in empirical evidence. By doing so, we respond to calls for more theoretically informed and policy-relevant research on this critical public health issue.

Methodology

This study employed a qualitative design based on Glaserian grounded theory, selected for its capacity to generate theory from data in under-researched domains. The research population included three stakeholder groups: [1] adult sports supplement consumers (primarily gym-goers and athletes) [2], suppliers (ranging from licensed retailers to individuals involved in illicit distribution), and [3] domain experts (sports nutritionists, anti-doping officials, and public health regulators).

Sampling began with purposeful selection of information-rich cases (e.g., a national-level athlete disqualified for doping, a customs officer with seizure experience), followed by snowball sampling to access hidden populations such as underground vendors. This strategy was essential given the illegal and stigmatized nature of the topic, which limits access through conventional channels [18].

Data collection involved semi-structured, in-depth interviews (average duration: 45 min), conducted face-to-face or via secure video call between January and March 2024. An interview guide with role-specific probes was used (see Supplementary File 1). Interviews continued until theoretical saturation was achieved—defined as the absence of new substantive codes or categories across three consecutive interviews. Saturation was confirmed at the 19th interview, with redundancy observed from the 17th onward. A saturation log documenting this process is provided in Supplementary File 3.

All interviews were audio-recorded, transcribed verbatim, and anonymized using role-based identifiers (e.g., C-01 for Consumer 1, S-03 for Supplier 3, E-02 for Expert 2). This coding system enables transparent linkage between emergent themes and participant backgrounds.

Findings

The analysis was grounded in the perspectives of a diverse sample of 19 interviewees. The demographic characteristics of the participants are detailed in Table 2. The sample was predominantly male (78%) and included consumers (48%), producers (26%), and nutritionists (26%), ensuring a tripartite perspective on the issue. A significant proportion of participants (48%) fell within the 26–35 age bracket, and the cohort was highly educated, with the majority (58%) holding a PhD degree. This diverse composition facilitated a comprehensive exploration of the phenomenon from multiple stakeholder viewpoints and levels of expertise. Six overarching consequence domains emerged from the data, each comprising multiple sub-themes supported by direct participant testimony.

Multidimensional health harms

Participants described acute and chronic health effects, ranging from gastrointestinal distress and hormonal imbalances to life-threatening organ failure. Consumers reported severe adverse events they attributed to counterfeit products, such as: “My liver enzymes spiked after using a ‘natural testosterone booster’—turned out it contained anabolic steroids” (C-08). Experts confirmed these incidents are underreported due to weak pharmacovigilance systems in Iran. Psychological consequences—including anxiety, depression, and aggression—were also frequently cited, aligning with Khalili et al. (2023) but extending the evidence to include long-term mental health impacts [17] (Table 3).

Erosion of institutional and brand trust

The presence of counterfeits has severely undermined confidence in both products and regulatory institutions. As one anti-doping official noted: “Athletes no longer trust our ‘approved supplement’ lists because counterfeiters replicate certification holograms perfectly” (E-03). This reflects a broader crisis of institutional legitimacy, where even legitimate brands suffer reputational damage due to market contamination. Consumers expressed reluctance to purchase any supplements, regardless of source, illustrating a spillover effect that harms the entire industry (Table 3).

Athletic performance deterioration and career disruption

Contrary to their marketed benefits, counterfeit supplements often led to performance decline due to nutrient deficiencies, toxicity, or banned substances. A national-level bodybuilder recounted: “I lost two years of training—and my spot on the national team—because a ‘legal’ pre-workout contained trenbolone” (C-07). Such cases exemplify how counterfeit products directly sabotage athletic careers through unintentional doping (Table 3).

Legal and anti-doping violations

Iran’s legal framework lacks specific provisions for prosecuting counterfeit supplement trafficking, resulting in minimal deterrence. One supplier admitted: “Even if caught, we pay a small fine—less than one day’s profit” (S-02). Meanwhile, athletes face disproportionate consequences, including international bans and reputational ruin, despite often being unaware of product adulteration. This asymmetry highlights a critical justice gap in current enforcement approaches (Table 3).

Expansion of organized illicit markets

The counterfeit supplement trade in Iran is increasingly linked to organized criminal networks. Suppliers described sophisticated operations involving Telegram-based sales, forged documentation, and bribery of border officials: “We move product through unofficial crossings—customs officers look the other way for a cut” (S-04) (Table 3).

Structural economic damage

Legitimate manufacturers face existential threats from unfair competition. A domestic producer lamented: “We invest in R&D and quality control but can’t compete with $2 counterfeit whey sold online with fake labels” (E-05). This dynamic discourages innovation, reduces tax revenue, and risks driving compliant businesses out of the market—aligning with global evidence [16], but contextualized within Iran’s import-dependent economy (Table 3).

Discussion

This study advances understanding of counterfeit sports supplements by moving beyond isolated health risks to reveal a systemic crisis involving public health, sports integrity, economic fairness, and governance. Our key theoretical contribution is an interdisciplinary model that demonstrates how individual harms (e.g., liver failure) are inextricably linked to institutional failures (e.g., weak regulation) and criminal enterprise (e.g., smuggling networks)—a perspective absent in prior literature focused solely on consumer behavior or clinical outcomes.

The findings resonate with international studies on supplement adulteration [6, 20]. but extend them by documenting how regulatory fragmentation and corruption in Iran create unique vulnerabilities. For instance, while the World Anti-Doping Agency (WADA) mandates strict supplement controls for elite athletes, Iran’s implementation remains inconsistent—leaving athletes exposed to unintentional violations.

Critically, this research for global contextualization by demonstrating that while the Iranian case has local specificities (e.g., sanctions-driven import reliance), the underlying mechanisms—profit-driven counterfeiting, regulatory gaps, consumer vulnerability—are transnational. Thus, our model offers transferable insights for other low- and middle-income countries facing similar challenges.

Implications

Moving beyond generic recommendations, we propose four evidence-based interventions:

Legal Reform: Amend Iran’s Food and Drug Law to include mandatory batch testing, stricter penalties for counterfeiters, and explicit classification of sports supplements as high-risk products.Institutional Coordination: Establish a joint task force linking the Ministry of Health, Ministry of Sports, Judiciary, and Customs to disrupt supply chains.Technological Innovation: Pilot a blockchain- or QR-code-based verification system co-managed by regulators and manufacturers to authenticate products.Targeted Education: Develop awareness campaigns for athletes, coaches, and gym owners focused on identifying red flags (e.g., unrealistically low prices, missing batch numbers) and reporting mechanisms (Fig 1).

Strengths and limitations

Strengths include the use of grounded theory to generate novel insights, access to hidden populations (e.g., illicit suppliers), interdisciplinary framing, and methodological rigor (e.g., quantified intercoder reliability, saturation documentation).

Limitations include the focus on Iran, which may limit generalizability, and reliance on self-reported data. While we incorporated available national statistics to contextualize findings the study remains qualitative by design, consistent with grounded theory principles. Future mixed-methods research could quantify the prevalence and economic burden of counterfeiting using national health and trade databases.

Conclusion

The proliferation of counterfeit sports supplements in Iran is not a marginal consumer safety issue but a systemic governance failure with cascading consequences for health, sports integrity, and economic development. This study’s primary contribution is a validated, interdisciplinary conceptual model that maps the interplay between individual vulnerability, institutional weakness, and criminal enterprise—offering a foundation for theory development and evidence-based policy in Iran and comparable contexts. Addressing this crisis requires coordinated action that transcends public health silos and confronts the structural conditions enabling illicit markets to thrive.
