Ultra-high density perovskite nanowire array memristor-based multi-layer perceptron
Authors: Swapnadeep Poddar, Biswadeep Khan, Shivam Kumar, Jinghao Li, Yucheng Ding, Zhesi Chen, Kaichen Wang, Chenxi Jin, Jinchen Wei, Zhirong Peng, On-mongkol Jaesiri, Mansun Chan, Julie L. Semmelhack, Zhiyong Fan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Passive memristor crossbars present a high-density and low-power platform for neuromorphic computing. Although perovskite memristors present advantages such as re-configurability between electrochemical metallization and valence change mechanisms compared to traditional oxides, they face issues with uniformity, variability, and filamentary instability, which impede their large-scale integration. This work demonstrates that these limitations are overcome by a three-dimensional perovskite nanowire array architecture, where a precisely engineered ITO barrier is instrumental in maintaining controlled, analog conductance modulation. The resulting memristors exhibit 139 non-overlapping Ohmic conduction states within an optimal analog range (10–100 µS), long retention ( > 10⁶ s), endurance exceeding 4 × 10⁵ cycles, an asymmetric non-linearity factor of 0.3, and minimal variability (device-to-device <5%, cycle-to-cycle <1.5%). The memristors are further integrated into a 64 × 64 crossbar array and utilized in developing a fully integrated multi-layer perceptron for zebrafish head and jaw movement analysis, thereby establishing a scalable pathway for robust perovskite-based neuromorphic hardware.

Introduction

The escalating computational demands of artificial intelligence are intensifying the search for post-von Neumann architectures, with neuromorphic computing being recognized as a leading candidate. At the heart of this paradigm are memristor crossbar arrays, in which vector-matrix multiplication (VMM), the fundamental operation of neural networks, is executed in-memory through the leveraging of Ohm’s law for multiplication and Kirchhoff’s law for summation1–3. While easier control is offered by one-transistor-one-resistor (1T1R) cells, their density is limited by the size of the transistor. In contrast, the ultimate integration density and energy efficiency are promised by passive (0T1R) crossbar arrays through the elimination of the transistor, but immense demands are placed on the memristor’s performance, requiring high uniformity, linear conductance modulation, and minimal sneak-path currents2–7. Consequently, the demonstration of complex, multi-layer perceptrons (MLPs) using any memristor technology in a passive crossbar configuration remains a significant challenge as reliable, analog-grade devices across a large array are necessitated and reports on such physical MLPs have been limited3,8–11.

Halide perovskites are recently attracting attention as memristive materials due to their excellent ionic transport properties, facile synthesis, and re-configurability between electrochemical metallization (ECM) and valence change (VCM) mechanisms12–14. However, pervasive issues of poor operational lifetime, high variability, and unreliable switching are caused by perovskites’ notorious instability and the stochastic nature of switching in thin films15. These challenges have largely confined perovskite memristor research to reports on single devices or small arrays, with system-level demonstrations often relying on simulations rather than physical hardware16–20. Bridging the gap between a single promising device and a functional, large-scale computing system is thus regarded as the foremost struggle in the field.

In this work, these barriers are overcome through the demonstration of a high-density, reliable perovskite nanowire memristor array that enables an MLP for processing complex, real-world data. First, a porous alumina membrane (PAM) is employed to template a robust, vertically aligned array of MAPbBr₃ (methyl ammonium lead bromide) nanowires (NWs), which provides encapsulation and ensures exceptional device-to-device uniformity. Second, a quasi-ECM switching mechanism is introduced by engineering a 15 nm thin ITO layer as a semi-permeable barrier between the Ag electrode and the perovskite NWs. This barrier acts as a kinetic metering valve, with the injection of Ag⁺ ions being rate-limited to enable controlled, radial filament growth, as validated by in-depth density functional theory (DFT) and COMSOL simulations, time-of-flight secondary ion mass spectroscopy (ToF-SIMS) and cross-sectional transmission electron microscopy based energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (TEM-EDS). Highly controllable analog switching is obtained, characterized by a high dynamic range (200), 139 linear and ohmic conduction states within the optimal analog computing range of 10 µS and 100 µS, optimum asymmetric-non linearity factor of 0.3, minimal cycle-to-cycle (<1.5%) and device-to-device (<5%) variability, prolonged state retention (>10⁶ s), and robust endurance exceeding 4 × 10⁵ cycles.

This performance enables the creation of a 64 × 64 passive crossbar array (4,096 memristors), which is successfully deployed in several demanding scenarios, including image memorization, off-chip classification task via programming of 4095 weights and subsequent readout, and it forms the core of a physically wired three-layer perceptron (composed of 16 × 16, 8 × 8, and 4 × 6 arrays). On-chip inference for the simultaneous regression of head angle and classification of jaw movement in zebrafish larvae is performed by this MLP, processing complex biological kinematics. By conclusively demonstrating a scalable platform that integrates high-density memory, robust analog computing, hardware-level MLP integration and complex data processing, this work elevates halide perovskite memristors from a promising material system to a competitive technology for reliable neuromorphic hardware.

The NW array achieves an ultra-high density of approximately 1010 cm−2 with a filling ratio approaching 100%, as confirmed by top-view scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) mapping (Supplementary Fig. 1a–f). Cross-sectional SEM and scanning transmission electron microscopy (STEM) images (Supplementary Fig. 2a, b) further validate this complete pore filling, while STEM-EDS mapping (Supplementary Fig. 3a–h) demonstrates uniform elemental distribution and stoichiometry throughout the NWs. The resulting NWs measure ~40 nm in diameter and 1–1.5 μm in length.

The PAM scaffold provides crucial stabilization for the halide perovskite, enabling reliable memristive operation21,22. Unlike thin-film perovskite memristors that suffer from material degradation and unstable conductance states with poor endurance, retention, and temporal jitter23–25, the alumina matrix offers multiple protective functions: it acts as a barrier against moisture-induced surface diffusion, facilitates heat dissipation via its enhanced thermal conductivity, and provides electrical isolation between adjacent NWs to prevent lateral cross-talk26,27. This vertical NW architecture, achieved without lithography, avoids the assembly challenges of horizontal NW memristors28,29 while incorporating inherent encapsulation often missing in other vertical NW designs30.

Solution-based NW growth within the PAM offers additional advantages over chemical vapor deposition methods, including cost-effectiveness, low-temperature processing, and improved device-to-device uniformity22,23. High-resolution TEM imaging (Supplementary Fig. 4a–d) reveals the polycrystalline nature of the solution-processed perovskite NWs, with visible (200), (211), (110), and (210) planes exhibiting interplanar spacings of 2.95 Å, 2.4 Å, 4.03 Å, and 2.7 Å respectively, consistent with X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns (Supplementary Fig. 5). Notably, XRD patterns collected from four distinct regions of the same PAM sample show virtually identical characteristics, demonstrating uniform crystallographic phase, orientation, and quality across the entire array. Further evidence of consistent optoelectronic properties comes from photoluminescence (PL) characterization, which shows a peak emission at 532 nm and spatially uniform PL intensity across the PAM area under UV excitation (Supplementary Fig. 6), confirming homogeneous pore filling and material quality.

Device characterization and operational mechanism

The development of a reliable, analog-grade memristor is recognized as the cornerstone for building functional neuromorphic systems. 1T-1R arrays present a distinct advantage in terms of gate-modulated precise conductance tuning within the memristors, a feature absent in passive 0T-1R arrays9,31. Consequently, material and device engineering are essential to bestow passive crossbar memristors with linear, gradual and symmetric weight update capabilities along with the ability to host multiple states with low temporal jitter within a high dynamic range,which are prerequisites for large-scale AI accelerators32. In Fig. 2a, the current-voltage (I–V) characteristics of the MAPbBr₃ NW-based memristor are presented, where gradual conductance modulation is showcased as paramount for synaptic emulation. At a read voltage of 0.2 V, an ON-OFF ratio of ∼70 is achieved. Two key metrics for the device’s capability are defined: the full physical dynamic range and the optimal analog computing range. An extensive full dynamic range of 200, from 5 µS to 1000 µS, is exhibited by the device, demonstrating its innate capacity for a wide span of conductance values. Within this range, 327 distinct, non-overlapping states are experimentally mapped (Supplementary Fig. 7), a testament to its fine-grained tunability. However, for robust and energy-efficient neuromorphic applications, strategic operation within an optimal analog computing range of 10 µS to 100 µS is employed. This selection is guided by an engineering trade-off; at conductance below 10 µS, the impact of read noise and temporal jitter is more pronounced, while above 100 µS, power consumption is significantly increased. The potentiation-depression characteristics within this optimal range are demonstrated in Fig. 2b with the pulsing scheme of 2.5 V (write) /0.05 V (read) /-2.5 V (erase)/ 0.05 V (read). The optimized window boasts 139 highly stable, linearly separable states that are demonstrated (Supplementary Fig. 8), which are considered ideal for precise neural network weighting. To demonstrate the granularity and retention capability of these states, Fig. 2c displays 10 distinct, non-overlapping, representative states within a narrow conductance range of 10 µS to 20 µS, while Fig. 2d shows 10 representative states across a broader range from 10 µS to 100 µS. The conductance states exhibit temporal stability over 106 s, with a read voltage of 50 mV and a temporal jitter (2σ/mean) of less than 2%. Akin to the tuning of the conductance values by change in pulse number, the conductance is also tuned by varying the pulse height and pulse width, as shown in Supplementary Fig. 10a, b, respectively. The potentiation-depression characteristics (Fig. 2b) possess high linearity and symmetry. This is quantified using the Asymmetric Non-Linearity (ANL) factor, a holistic metric defined as ANL = (Gp(N/2) − Gᴅ(N/2)) / (Gmax − Gmin) where Gp(N/2) is the median conductance value during the potentiation half-cycle, Gᴅ(N/2) is the median conductance value during the depression half-cycle, Gmax and Gmin are the maximum and minimum conductance values in the cycle. Unlike separate non-linearity factors, the ANL is valued for simultaneously capturing both the linearity of individual updates and the critical symmetry between them, which is essential for stable network training via backpropagation. A low ANL of 0.3 is achieved by the MAPbBr3 NW memristor (Supplementary Fig. 10c), a value competitive with state-of-the-art memristors and indicative of balanced, predictable weight updates (Supplementary Table 1). Furthermore, all programmed states are characterized by perfect Ohmic conduction (Supplementary Fig. 11), ensuring accurate multiply-accumulate operations. Remarkable reliability in conductance tuning is also shown by the devices, with minimal cycle-to-cycle variability (<1.5%) as shown in Supplementary Fig. 12a, b, and device-to-device variability <5% as shown in Supplementary Fig. 13, which allows for accurate programming of weights across devices. The minimum energy consumption per synaptic event is estimated to be ∼7 nJ (programming) and ∼5 pJ (reading), at par with the state-of-the-art memristors utilized in analog computing (Supplementary Table 2, Supplementary Table 3). In this context, it should also be mentioned that the 200 µs programming and reading pulse width is further validated by electrochemical impedance spectroscopy and current-time response, where the device’s RC time constant is confirmed to be significantly shorter than the pulse width (Supplementary Figs. 14, 15).

This enhanced performance is rooted in a quasi-ECM mechanism where the limitations of both traditional ECM and VCM cells are strategically circumvented. Traditional ECM cells (with a pure Ag electrode) offer Ohmic conduction states and demonstrate potential for accommodating multiple conduction states owing to the distinctly high difference between the low resistance state (LRS) and high resistance state (HRS) but are plagued by abrupt, uncontrollable filament growth due to rapid Ag⁺ injection, leading to impeded analog tuning (Supplementary Fig. 16a)4,33. Conversely, typical VCM devices offer gradual switching and more controllable weight modulation (Supplementary Fig. 16b) but are often compromised by suppressed conductance change per unit pulse, limiting the dynamic range, possesses more stochasticity, enhancing the temporal jitter in the states4,34,35. In our design, the benefits of both are ingeniously merged as an Ag source is retained for high dynamic range to allow multiple conduction states, but a precisely engineered 15 nm ITO layer is interposed as a semi-permeable barrier to create a quasi-ECM cell which facilitates gradual conductance modulation opposed to pure ECM devices.

To evaluate the contribution of the ITO to the overall conduction mechanism, current–voltage (I–V) characterization is performed on a control Ag/ITO (15 nm)/Al device. The DC I–V characteristics exhibit perfectly linear, Ohmic behavior with no observable hysteresis or resistive switching (Supplementary Fig. 17a), confirming that the ITO layer functions as an electronically transparent yet ionically resistive interface. Its negligible series resistance prevents a significant voltage drop across the barrier, thereby ensuring effective voltage control across the perovskite NWs.

A detailed examination of the SET sweep for the full device on a log–log scale reveals the electronic signature of this ionically driven metallization process (Supplementary Fig. 17b)36–39. The progression of slopes (0.3 → 1 → 10 → 2 → 1) maps the kinetically controlled, electrochemical evolution of a silver filament within the perovskite nanowire: (1) at the onset of the positive voltage sweep, thermally generated free carriers in the polycrystalline perovskite nanowire govern the minimal current. During this stage, the applied voltage remains insufficient to drive substantial Ag⁺ injection through the ITO barrier. (2) As the voltage increases, injected charge carriers begin to dominate. At this point, the metering function of the ITO barrier becomes active, allowing a controlled flux of Ag⁺ ions to be injected into the perovskite. Upon reduction, these ions occupy favorable sites within the nanowire. (3) The near-vertical current jump marks the point where the ionic and electronic processes become fully coupled: the density of reduced Ag atoms (metallic nucleation sites) reaches a critical percolation threshold, creating the first continuous metallic cluster. The ITO barrier ensures this transition is sharp yet controlled, preventing the stochastic behavior as often seen in pure ECM devices. (4) Once a percolation path is established, electron transport proceeds through a growing, spatially confined metallic filament network. The continued supply of Ag⁺ from the ITO enables the filament cross-section to increase according to trap-free space-charge-limited conduction (SCLC)—like kinetics, which underlies the analog, gradual modulation of conductance. (5) The subsequent reverse voltage sweep exhibits perfect Ohmic behavior, confirming the formation of a stable, continuous, and thick metallic Ag filament that fully bridges the electrodes. The term quasi-ECM precisely describes this engineered kinetic pathway, in which the ITO barrier imposes a rate-limited, electrochemically gated metallization sequence. This mechanism replaces stochastic ion drift with a predictable, controlled process, thereby enabling analog switching characteristics.

To further validate the stability of the quasi-ECM switching, consecutive DC I–V sweeps are performed. As presented in Supplementary Fig. 18, 100 consecutive cycles exhibit exceptional cycle-to-cycle reproducibility, directly confirming the inherent stability of the mechanism and highlighting the synergistic roles of the ITO barrier and the nanowire architecture. The ITO layer acts as a kinetic stabilizer, ensuring the consistency of the rate-limited Ag⁺ injection pathway and preventing the stochastic filament overgrowth typical of pure ECM cells. Simultaneously, the porous alumina membrane (PAM) scaffold provides nanoscale confinement and electrical isolation for each perovskite nanowire, localizing filament formation to individual channels.

The critical role of the ITO thickness is established through a systematic study (5 nm to 100 nm, Supplementary Fig. 19). A thickness of 15 nm is identified as the optimum, striking a perfect balance where it is thick enough to form a continuous layer that rate-limits Ag⁺ ion flux, yet thin enough to allow for a controlled, gradual injection and consequent well-controlled radial growth of the filament. Thinner layers (5 nm) are found to act as a sieve, leading to abrupt, ECM-like switching, while thicker layers (50–100 nm) severely impede ion flow, suppressing conductance range and promoting stochastic, VCM-like behavior. Specifically, when the 100 nm based VCM type design is compared to the 15 nm based quasi-ECM architecture, the dynamic range is halved, temporal jitter is enhanced by ∼4 times, the number of states is less by ∼3.5 times, and the ANL is doubled. These findings unequivocally underscore the superiority of the quasi-ECM design over the conventional VCM design utilized in perovskite memristors for neuromorphic applications40,41. The uniqueness of ITO as a mixed ionic-electronic conductor is further proven through control experiments with insulating oxides (HfO₂, Al₂O₃) and metals (Cr, Ti), all of which fail to replicate the quasi-ECM behavior, yielding either blocked ion transport or poor VCM performance (Supplementary Fig. 20, Supplementary Note 1). The physical origin of this metering effect is elucidated by Density Functional Theory (DFT) calculations, where a high migration barrier for Ag in ITO (2.29 eV) is revealed, compared to the perovskite lattice (0.85 eV), with Ag ions navigating through pathways in the oxygen sub-lattice of the amorphous ITO (Supplementary Fig. 21, Supplementary Note 2)22. This ionic traffic-control is vividly captured in time-resolved COMSOL simulations (Supplementary Note 3), where the evolution of Ag⁺ concentration from the HRS to the LRS is traced (Supplementary Fig. 22a–f). A controlled, progressive ion injection in the Ag/ITO device is demonstrated by the simulations, in stark contrast to the sharp, abrupt invasion observed in the control device without the barrier (Supplementary Fig. 22g–l). This visual evidence directly explains the gradual switching observed in the I–V curves. Furthermore, Supplementary Fig. 23a and Supplementary Fig. 23b illustrate that the change in electrolyte potential for devices in the low resistance state with the 15 nm ITO barrier is significantly smaller compared to those where the NWs are in direct contact with the active Ag electrode, indicating a more controlled conductance change upon electrical stimulation. In addition, as demonstrated in Supplementary Fig. 24, time-dependent simulations of the pulse-based operation reveal a cumulative, step-wise Ag⁺ injection into the perovskite NW with successive voltage pulses. This progressive filament growth is directly linked to the analog switching capability inherent to the quasi-ECM device.

This mechanistic model is unequivocally validated by a suite of direct compositional analyses. SEM based EDS line scans are shown in Supplementary Fig. 25a–c. When switched to LRS, the devices with an ITO barrier show lower intensities of Ag in the NWs compared to the device with pristine Ag as the top contact. Furthermore, TEM EDS mappings presented in Supplementary Fig. 26a–g exhibit a mean Ag atomic concentration of 1.11% for the device (in LRS) with ITO barrier, whereas the device (in LRS) without the barrier shows a mean Ag concentration of 2.53%. Additionally, time-of-flight secondary mass ion spectroscopy (ToF-SIMS) depth mapping across the length of the NWs for these two device types, presented in Supplementary Fig. 27, corroborates the reduced amount of Ag injection in devices with the ITO barrier. Also, the most unambiguous visual proof of metered Ag penetration through the ITO into the perovskite NWs is provided by cross-sectional TEM-EDS mapping (Supplementary Figs. 28, 29), where a significantly higher Ag concentration is observed within the NWs in the LRS compared to the HRS. These multi-faceted evidences irrefutably link the controlled physical movement of Ag to the enhanced electrical characteristics.

Following the evaluation of single-device performance and exploration of the working mechanism, a crossbar array incorporating MAPbBr3 NW memristors is constructed. The NWs are grown in a freestanding PAM, sandwiched between crisscrossing Ag/ITO and Al electrodes in a 64 × 64 configuration, as depicted in Fig. 3a (refer to the “Methods” section for additional details on device fabrication). The electrode line width is 100 µm, with an average spacing of 24.5 µm between lines, as shown in Fig. 3b, c. For optimal neuromorphic processing, low variation (2σ/mean) is essential across devices for metrics, including threshold writing/erasing voltage and ON/OFF ratio. Figure 3d displays the writing voltage map for the 4096 NW memristors, showing a variation of 15.55%, while Fig. 3e presents the erasing voltage map with a variation of 25.8% across all devices. The ON/OFF ratio, measured at a read voltage of 0.2 V, demonstrates a variation of 15.12% across all memristors, as illustrated in Fig. 3f. No degradation in the pattern is observed over a timeframe of 106 s, highlighting the robust retention trait of the crossbar array. Strike detection analysis in head-fixed larval zebrafish

Zebrafish are a widely used model organism in neurobiological research due to their transparent embryos and rapid development43. Studying the dynamic and complex motor patterns exhibited by larval zebrafish during behaviors such as hunting can provide insights into neural circuit functions, particularly how sensory input is integrated with motor output in vertebrates. Unlike the static images of the MNIST dataset, zebrafish head and jaw movements are real-world biological data that change over time and are influenced by multiple external factors. This makes them an excellent testbed for technologies intended for real-world biological, experimental and ecological monitoring applications.

Although most visual systems are capable of estimating object distances44, the neural mechanisms underlying this ability remain largely unexplored. Humans utilize depth perception to navigate environments and perform daily tasks, while animals employ it in various visual behaviors, such as hunting. Larval zebrafish exhibit a range of distance-dependent visual behaviors during hunting; at medium distances, convergence of the eyes and tail movements is employed to orient towards prey, but when the prey is within striking distance, a strike is executed. This strike involves the generation of a suction force to draw the prey into their mouths, facilitated by an upward movement of the head and a rapid downward movement of the jaw. Although the initial phases of prey capture have been extensively studied in head-fixed larvae45,46, strikes in such conditions have been rarely documented. To investigate distance estimation, a head-fixed strike assay is developed, capable of evoking strikes quantified by head elevation to understand the underlying neural circuits. The experimental setup for obtaining the zebra-fish strike dataset is detailed in Supplementary Note 5, Supplementary Figs. 31–33.

Quantification of these strikes necessitates the development of an algorithm. In a previously reported work, an algorithm was developed that measures the total upward movement of the head using a dense optical flow algorithm47–49, achieving approximately 92% accuracy in detecting strikes based on upward head movement. However, the algorithm also incorrectly classified some non-strike movements, like struggles and gulping, as strikes. This misclassification occurred because the algorithm was solely based on upward head movement, whereas true strike kinematics in free-swimming zebrafish involve both head elevation and jaw depression, the latter being critical for generating suction force. In the head-fixed assay, genuine strikes corresponded with jaw depression, a factor the algorithm failed to consider. Moreover, the algorithm only identified the frame of head elevation without capturing other vital kinematic data, such as the timing of head descent, the coordination of jaw depression with head elevation, and their temporal differences. These limitations highlight the necessity for advanced deep neural networks to include these additional parameters, thereby improving its accuracy and effectiveness for studying zebrafish behavior as well as identify the underlying neural circuit.

Off-chip zebrafish strike detection with memristor array

A deep neural network with five layers is developed to accurately determine the head and jaw movements for detecting strikes, as shown in Fig. 4a. The neural network is composed of two 3 × 3 convolutional layers dedicated to feature extraction, followed by three linear layers that perform classification tasks. Supplementary Fig. 34 illustrates the process-flow of classification, which encompasses the following steps. Initially, the training data undergoes preprocessing, where the image of a fish is extracted from a video captured by the data acquisition process described Supplementary Note 5, and the fish head is cropped from the original image and compressed to a 32 × 32 pixel format to conform to the neural network’s input size. Following this, the 4095 weights of the last hidden layer are transferred to the 64 × 64 MAPbBr3 NW memristor array as conductance values, as depicted in Fig.4b, Supplementary Fig. 35, via the half-biasing voltage scheme. The programming is done via an open-loop write technique (discussed in depth in Supplementary Note 4), which results in a write disturb window of 0.6 µS and 3 µS for the highest and lowest programmed values of conductance in the array. After this, the conductance values of the memristors are read out using the ground read technique, where a read voltage of 0.1 V is applied row-by-row to the entire array. The deviation between the ideal (obtained from software) conductance values and the ones read from the array is plotted as a read error map in Supplementary Fig. 36a. The ground-read scheme is employed for its high read margin and effective suppression of sneak-path currents, which renders the array functional for multiply-accumulate operations50. This configuration leaves line resistance as the dominant, yet manageable, error source, inducing a maximum conductance error of ~9% (Supplementary Fig. 36a). Crucially, the combined impact of this error and minor write disturbs (outlier points in the read error map) is negligible at the system level as the classification accuracy remains high even with simulated conductance deviations significantly larger than those experimentally observed (Supplementary Fig. 36b). Without the ground-read scheme, the sneaky path currents dominate the readout rendering the array dysfunctional (Supplementary Fig. 36c). A classification accuracy of 94% is achieved, as depicted in Supplementary Fig. 37. The nine different classes of head and jaw movements for detecting strikes using tuned weights of the physical memristor array are presented in Supplementary Fig. 38 and Supplementary Movie 3. The classification result in Fig. 4c clearly indicates that the individual nine classes are effectively recognized, with the classified type of head/jaw movement having the highest neuronal output with respect to the other types. Multi-layer perceptron for on-chip inference

To investigate the detection of strikes in zebrafish, head angle is identified as one of the reliable kinematic markers. Additionally, jaw depression, which facilitates mouth opening during strikes, serves as a mechanical precursor to feeding or attacking actions. Studying jaw depression and head angle can offer insights into the coordination between motor and sensory systems in the brain. Both head angle and jaw depression offer precise temporal and spatial markers for aligning strike behavior with neural data. By analyzing how these behaviors correlate with neural activation, researchers can map the specific neural circuits governing strike behavior. This understanding is essential for elucidating the neural circuitry involved in distance estimation, as it involves identifying specific neurons in various brain regions linked to these events and their interactions51. From the device perspective, the deployment of the memristor-based MLP for zebrafish kinematics provides a critical validation of its analog computing prowess, moving beyond simpler benchmarks. Unlike the high-contrast input images, which are often binarized, the accurate classification of zebrafish movements depends on nuanced grayscale information, demanding true analog processing from input to computation. This complex task directly validates the essential device attributes that enable such fidelity: multiple non-overlapping conductance states within a high dynamic range and Ohmic linearity for precise analog multiplication. Consequently, this demonstration of on-chip inference proves the system’s suitability for processing the intricate, real-world data streams found in biological sensing.

Based on the above premise and intention to exhibit a low-power consuming perovskite nanowire memristor based MLP, a deep neural network is designed such that it effectively helps to elucidate the strike pattern of the fish by estimating the head angle of the fish via regression (1 output) and opening/closing of the mouth by classification method (2 outputs), as illustrated in Fig. 5a. The neural network comprises two sections. The first section performs feature extraction with four convolutional layers equipped with ReLU activation function and four pooling layers. The second section comprises three linear layers to perform the regression and classification tasks (see Supplementary Note 6 for details). As shown in Fig. 5b and Fig. 5c, three MAPbBr3 NW-based memristor arrays of dimensions 16 × 16, 8 × 8 and 4 × 6 are sequentially utilized as an MLP for emulating the three linear layers of the above-mentioned DNN. For seamless relay of data across the layers and production of final regression and classification outputs, a complementary circuitry is used. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 39, the data acquisition board integrates 16 channels of DAC (Digital-to-Analog Converter), 8 channels of differential signal preprocessing circuits, and supports memristor arrays of up to 16 × 16. Due to the existence of differential pairs, the effective configuration can be considered as 16 × 8. The 16 DAC channels are controlled sequentially using the SPI (Serial Peripheral Interface) bus and a multiplexer, enabling the voltage values to be written in sequence to drive the memristor array. The data lines of the memristors are arranged in differential pairs and connected to the signal preprocessing circuits. The current signals are first converted into voltage using a Trans-Impedance Amplifier (TIA) and then further amplified to an appropriate range by a second-stage amplifier. The amplified signals are routed to a 16-bit ADC (Analog-to-Digital Converter) through an 8-channel multiplexer, allowing precise data acquisition. Supplementary Fig. 40 details the connection of the control board, which integrates a power supply module and a Microcontroller Unit (MCU), capable of managing up to three data acquisition boards. The MCU facilitates the sequential writing of data to the DACs and the reading of data from the ADCs on the data acquisition boards. Communication with the host computer is achieved via a UART (Universal Asynchronous Receiver-Transmitter) serial port, enabling seamless data transfer and system control. The working of the interlayer data relay is schematically illustrated in Supplementary Fig. 41. The operational mode functions through a sequential and iterative process. Initially, the raw input data is written to the digital-to-analog converters (DACs) on the first data acquisition board. Subsequently, the first-order data is extracted via the analog-to-digital converter (ADC) and undergoes basic data processing. Following this, the processed data is transferred to the DACs on the second data acquisition board, where second-order data is acquired through the ADC. This entire procedure is then repeated iteratively across the boards until the desired output values are achieved. Furthermore, a comprehensive system-level analysis is conducted to evaluate the technological standing and scalability of the perovskite nanowire memristor platform as detailed in Supplementary Tables 4, 5, Supplementary Note 8. The intrinsic analog core demonstrates exceptional efficiency, consuming only ~34 nJ (≈0.1 nJMAC-1) to perform all 344 multiply-accumulate operations of the implemented three-layer perceptron and overall core latency of 600 µs. This places the perovskite memristor core competitively within the landscape of memristor-based emerging analog in-memory computing technologies (Supplementary Table 6). The general-purpose nature of MAPbBr3 NW-based MLP architecture is also validated through a canonical MNIST classification task, where a hardware-based three-layer perceptron achieves an inference accuracy of 94% (Supplementary Note 9, Supplementary Fig. 45), confirming the system’s versatility and competitive performance on a standard machine learning benchmark.

Discussion

In this work, a high-density, reliable memristor technology based on a vertically aligned MAPbBr₃ nanowire array within a porous alumina membrane is demonstrated. This architecture is key to achieving exceptional device-to-device uniformity and stability. A quasi-ECM switching mechanism is introduced, where a precisely engineered ITO layer acts as a semi-permeable barrier to meter silver ion injection, which steers the controlled radial filament growth. This mechanism is comprehensively validated through density functional theory, COMSOL simulations, and direct compositional analysis, and is shown to be responsible for improved analog switching characteristics. These include a high dynamic range, multiple linear, ohmic and stable conductance states, minimal cycle-to-cycle and device-to-device variability, long retention and robust endurance. This performance enables the creation of a 64 × 64 perovskite-based passive crossbar array, the largest reported to date, with exceptionally low variation in switching parameters. The array’s reliability is confirmed through demanding tasks such as prolonged image memorization and successful weight tuning for off-chip classification. The core achievement of this work is the physical construction of an MLP using these perovskite memristor arrays. This hardware-based MLP is successfully deployed for complex, real-world information processing, performing simultaneous regression of head angle and classification of jaw movement in zebrafish larvae during strikes. Thus, a scalable platform that integrates high-density memristors, robust analog computing, and hardware-level MLP integration is conclusively demonstrated. This work elevates halide perovskite memristors from a promising material system to a competitive technology for reliable neuromorphic hardware.

Methods

Materials

Unless otherwise indicated, all chemicals were obtained from Sigma-Aldrich and utilized as received, without undergoing any additional purification. Specifically, the chemicals used to form the perovskite nanowires include anhydrous methylammonium bromide (≥99%), lead bromide (99.999% trace metals basis), and dimethyl sulfoxide (≥99.9%).

Device fabrication

Freestanding PAM fabrication: To fabricate the porous alumina membrane (PAM), 1.5 cm × 2 cm aluminum chips undergo electro-polishing to eliminate surface roughness, utilizing a solution of 25% HClO4 and 75 vol. % absolute ethanol (CH3CH2OH). A highly ordered PAM is achieved through a two-step anodization process employing 0.2 M oxalic acid at 40 V. The initial anodization layer is subsequently removed using a mixture of phosphoric acid (6 wt.% H3PO4 and 1.8 wt.% CrO3) at 98 °C, leaving behind a natural hexagonal ordering that enhances the uniformity of the subsequent anodization. The dimensions of the PAM channels, including length and diameter, are controllable through the applied voltage and the duration of the anodization. For this process, anodization at 40 V for 30 min yields a PAM template approximately 1−1.5 μm in thickness with pore diameters around 40 nm. The PAM is then barrier thinned with 0.2 M oxalic acid solution by a voltage ramping down process to ensure electronic tunneling between the perovskite NWs and the bottom electrode through the ∼ 4 nm barrier layer26. A layer of crystal bond (509, Ted Pella, Inc.), an adhesive with a flow point of 120 °C, is initially applied to cover the PAM/aluminum chip. The entire assembly is then immersed vertically in a saturated HgCl2 solution for approximately 1 h to etch away the aluminum substrate. Following this, the sample is transferred to acetone to remove the crystal bond. After about 30 min, the freestanding PAM is collected using a clean silicon chip and immersed in fresh acetone for an additional 20 min to eliminate any residual crystal bond. Ultimately, the PAM is baked at 80 °C for 2 min, allowing it to naturally detach from the silicon substrate.

Growth of MAPbBr3 NWs and electrode deposition: On the barrier-layer side of the blank PAM, 100 µm wide aluminum lines with 100 nm thickness are then sputtered at 200 W and 0.3 Pa. The PAM with Al lines is placed on a Si substrate with the Al lines facing the Si. The MAPbBr3 nanowire array is fabricated by solvent engineering on the as-produced freestanding PAM template, which is supported on a fresh silicon substrate. The precursor solution is prepared by dissolving CH3NH3Br and PbBr2 in dimethyl sulfoxide (1.1 g/mL) at a 1:1 molar ratio. Prior to spin coating, the plasma-cleaned PAM chip is rendered highly hydrophilic to ensure excellent wettability by the perovskite precursor solution. Upon dispensing, a critical static incubation period of 1-–2 min is employed. During this interval, pore filling is dominated not by centrifugal force, but by capillary action, which fills the precursor solution uniformly into the high-aspect-ratio pores from the bottom up. This passive filling process is inherently more uniform for nanostructures than a dynamic spinning process alone, as the formation of air bubbles and pressure gradients that lead to incomplete filling is avoided. Subsequently, the spin-coating is performed at 3000–4000 RPM to remove the excess solution from the PAM top surface, preventing the formation of a continuous capping film, and to define the meniscus for initial confinement of the perovskite within each pore. Annealing at 100 °C for 10 min drives the controlled crystallization of the MAPbBr₃ within the nano-confinement of the pores, where the anodized alumina walls act as a scaffold guiding crystal growth and promoting highly oriented nanowires. The spin speed is empirically optimized to a narrow window; lower speeds are found to result in a residual surface film that necessitates an additional low-voltage Ar⁺ ion-milling step, while higher speeds can cause pore de-wetting. Then, Indium tin oxide (ITO) lines, measuring 15 nm in thickness, are deposited via a radio frequency sputtering process at approximately 0.3 Pa and low power of 50 W to ensure no damage to the perovskite NWs. Subsequently, Ag lines, 85 nm thick, are aligned precisely with the ITO lines and deposited through thermal evaporation, and wire-bonding is carried out on the Ag-ITO lines. An ultra-thin layer of UV-epoxy (NOA81) is applied to the top surface of the chip, followed by a 10-min curing period under UV illumination. The epoxy/Ag-ITO/MAPbBr3 NWs in PAM/Al-based chip are detached from the silicon substrate and flipped over. Wire-bonding is carried out on the Al lines. The crisscrossing Ag-ITO and Al lines effectively form memristors in the array, with each cell having a dimension 100 µm × 100 µm.

Material characterization

Scanning Electron Microscope (SEM) images are acquired using a JEOL 7100 F SEM at an acceleration voltage of 15 kV and an FEI Helios G4 UX SEM at 5 kV. Transmission Electron Microscopy (TEM) is performed with a JEM ARM200F. The cross-sectional lamella for SEM/TEM analysis is fabricated using a dual-beam FIB/FESEM system (FEI Helios G4 UX), which integrates a Ga+ ion beam for milling and a high-resolution electron column for imaging. Particularly for the TEM-EDS analyses corresponding to Supplementary Fig. 26, the NWs were extruded from the PAM by a pulverization and sonication method21. Specifically, the NWs are grown in a freestanding PAM and then subjected to intense pulverization in a mortar-pestle. Next, the powder is mixed with Toluene and made to undergo ultra-sonication for ten minutes. The clear solution at the top contains the extruded NWs and is dropped on the TEM grid, followed by heating the grid at 60 °C for 5 minutes to evaporate the solvent. Photoluminescence (PL) is measured using an Edinburgh FS5, while material absorption is evaluated with a Lambda 1050+ spectrophotometer from PerkinElmer. X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis is conducted using a Bruker D8 X-ray diffractometer. Secondary Mass Ion Spectroscopy is executed with a ToF SIMS V (ION-TOF GmbH).

Electrical characterization

Single device measurement: The generation of electrical pulses with varying amplitude, width, and interval, along with the subsequent recording of output current for the single device, is conducted using the arbitrary waveform generator module of the Keithley 4225 PMU and the Keithley 4200 SCS systems. The recording of temporally stable conductance levels, as well as current-voltage (I–V) characterization, is performed using a Keithley 2450 source meter in conjunction with custom LABVIEW programs.

Measurement for the 64 × 64 array: The memristor arrays were interfaced via a 64 × 64 probe card, which was connected to a comprehensive peripheral control and readout system. For the selective programming of conductance states, a 1/2 voltage biasing scheme is implemented to mitigate sneak-path currents. In this scheme, selected rows are biased at +V/2 and selected columns at –V/2, applying the full operating voltage across the target cell while biasing all unselected lines to ground to minimize parasitic voltage drops across non-target devices.

For readout operations, a ground-read scheme is employed. In this configuration, the input voltages are applied to the selected rows, while all columns are terminated with a bank of transimpedance amplifiers (TIAs). The array measurement technique and related biasing scheme details are further elaborated in Supplementary Fig. 30, Supplementary Note 4.

Multi-layer perceptron implementation

Digital input signals are converted into analog voltages utilizing HGC5615 Digital-to-Analog Converters (DACs). These analog voltage values are subsequently directed to memristors, each possessing distinct synaptic weights or conductance values within the passive crossbar array. The current flowing through each memristor is directly proportional to the product of the input voltage and the memristor’s conductance, thereby effectively executing analog multiplication. The memristor array performs the multiply-accumulate (MAC) operation through vector-matrix multiplication across the entire layer, with the resultant currents summing at each output node. Operational amplifiers (op-amps) OPA4376 are employed to derive differential outputs from adjacent memristor bit-lines and to condition the output current, ensuring that it attains suitable levels for subsequent processing. Multiplexers (MUX) ADG1606 are utilized to route the pertinent signals to the following layer, while the ADC7683 is responsible for converting the conditioned analog signals into digital form, which are then relayed to the ESP32-WROOM-32D multicontroller unit (MCU). The activation function is applied in the digital domain within the microcontroller (ESP32-WROOM-32D MCU) after ADC conversion. This design choice offers maximum flexibility for algorithm development and proof-of-concept demonstration. In a fully integrated, optimized system, this function could be implemented with low-power analog circuits (e.g., current-mode comparators) to minimize latency and energy overhead. The MCU mainly orchestrates the sequential writing of data to the DACs and the reading of data from the ADCs situated on the data acquisition boards. These DACs reconvert the digital outputs into analog voltages, which are then applied to the memristors of the subsequent layer. This procedure is reiterated for each successive layer within the neural network, thereby facilitating complex multilayer processing. Communication with the host computer is accomplished via a UART (Universal Asynchronous Receiver-Transmitter) serial port, enabling seamless data transfer and system control.

Acquisition of zebrafish dataset

Zebrafish studies are approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of the Hong Kong University of Science and Technology, Protocol no.: AEP-2022-0107. The behavior of zebrafish larvae is recorded using established protocols47,48. The behavioral assay involves the maintenance of zebrafish larvae in Danieau’s solution, with the larvae being fed paramecia (Paramecium multimicronucleatum, sourced from Carolina Biological Supply Company) on days 5 and 7 post-fertilization. Prior to experiments, larvae that are 6, 7, or 8 days post-fertilization (dpf) are embedded in 2% low-melt agarose on Rinzl Plastic Coverslips, with their eyes and tails freed for movement. Experiments are conducted in a 22 °C darkroom using a rectangular acrylic arena (60 × 80 × 30 mm) with a Teflon fabric screen and infrared illumination (850 nm, MicroBrite High Intensity Spotlight from Advanced Illumination). High-speed cameras (200 fps) recorded tail and eye movements: one BFS-U3-04S2M-CS USB3.1 FLIR Blackfly S Monochrome Camera with a VZM 450i Right Angle Zoom Imaging lens on the left and a Photon Focus MV1-D1312E-160-CL-12 with a Sigma 105 mm lens beneath. Visual stimuli, designed with Python and PsychoPy, include a 4 mm UV dot sweeping horizontally (200°/s, −70° to 70°) on a black background, presented in randomized order during 10-s sessions with 1-min intervals.
