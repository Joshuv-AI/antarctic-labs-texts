Development and validation of a green spectrofluorimetric method using a rhodamine 6G probe and FCCCD-optimized dispersive liquid–liquid microextraction for tenuazonic acid surveillance in dried figs
Authors: Fotoon F. Redwan, Ahmed Serag, Ahmed H. Abdelazim, Maram H. Abduljabbar, Arwa Sultan Alqahtani, Manal E. Alosaimi, Farooq M. Almutairi, Atiah H. Almalki
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Tenuazonic acid (TeA) is a major Alternaria mycotoxin frequently contaminating dried figs, yet its routine surveillance remains constrained by reliance on costly chromatographic instrumentation. To address this analytical gap, a novel “turn-off” spectrofluorimetric method was developed for TeA determination in dried fig samples using rhodamine 6G as a cationic xanthene fluorescent probe. Given the complexity of the sugar-rich matrix, dispersive liquid–liquid microextraction was first optimized through a face-centered central composite design coupled with Derringer's desirability function. Optimal conditions comprised 350 µL of 1-octanol as extractant, 700 µL of acetonitrile as disperser, pH 3.3, and 5% (w/v) ammonium sulfate, yielding an experimental recovery of 95.23% ± 1.48%. Subsequently, spectral characterization demonstrated strong native fluorescence of rhodamine 6G at 555 nm (λex 526 nm), progressively quenched in the presence of TeA. Multi-temperature Stern–Volmer analysis, Job's continuous variation method, and thermodynamic evaluation confirmed a static quenching mechanism involving 1 : 1 ground-state complex formation. Comprehensive validation demonstrated linearity across 20–4000 µg kg−1 (R2 = 0.9981), with an LOQ of 19.2 µg kg−1. Mean recoveries ranged from 94.9% to 99.7%, intra- and inter-day precision remained below 11%, expanded measurement uncertainty was within ±22.4%, and matrix effects were negligible (−3.4%). Application to 60 Egyptian dried fig samples revealed widespread contamination (90% incidence; mean 726 ± 483 µg kg−1), with 26.7% exceeding the 1000 µg kg−1 indicative level. Green chemistry evaluation using AGSA-Prep (65%), WECA (81%), and EPPI (84.2) confirmed the method's sustainability, establishing it as a practical alternative to LC-MS/MS for routine TeA surveillance in resource-limited laboratories.

Introduction

Tenuazonic acid (TeA) is a tetramic acid derivative secondary metabolite produced primarily by Alternaria species, particularly A. alternata, a group of cosmopolitan dematiaceous fungi colonizing diverse agricultural commodities.1 Structurally a 3-acetyltetramic acid bearing a sec-butyl side chain, TeA represents the most abundant and toxicologically significant Alternaria mycotoxin, with acute oral LD50 values of 81–180 mg per kg body weight in rodents and potent inhibition of eukaryotic protein synthesis through suppression of ribosomal peptide release.2 TeA shows widespread occurrence in cereals, tomato products, and dried fruits, and has been detected in up to 78–79% of dried fig samples at concentrations ranging from tens of µg kg−1 to several mg kg−1.3 Commission Recommendation (EU) 2022/553 established indicative monitoring levels for TeA in priority commodities, including 1000 µg kg−1 in dried figs, with a required analytical limit of quantification not exceeding 20 µg kg−1 in all foods.4 The European Food Safety Authority established a threshold of toxicological concern of 1500 ng per kg body weight per day for TeA, substantially higher than the 2.5 ng kg−1 value for genotoxic alternariol and alternariol monomethyl ether, reflecting TeA's non-genotoxic classification.5 Despite these established toxicological concerns and increasingly stringent regulatory monitoring requirements, the determination of tenuazonic acid in food matrices remains analytically challenging, particularly in resource-limited laboratory settings, highlighting the need for accessible, rapid, and cost-effective analytical methods.

Current analytical approaches for tenuazonic acid (TeA) determination in food are dominated by chromatographic techniques coupled with mass spectrometric or fluorimetric detection. Liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS), typically with stable isotope dilution and QuEChERS or SPE clean-up, represents the gold standard, achieving low-µg kg−1 limits of quantification across diverse matrices including cereals, tomato products, and wine.6–8 However, TeA's tetramic acid structure presents persistent chromatographic challenges—peak tailing, ion suppression, and the requirement for alkaline mobile phases or isotopically labeled internal standards that substantially increase cost and complexity.9,10 HPLC with UV or fluorescence detection following derivatization with 2,4-dinitrophenylhydrazine offers a lower-instrument-cost alternative but suffers from lengthy sample preparation, derivative instability, and inadequate selectivity for trace-level regulatory surveillance.11,12 Immunoassay platforms including ELISA, nanobody-based icELISA, and aptasensors have recently emerged with reported sub-ng mL−1 detection limits, but remain limited by antibody cost, matrix interference, cross-reactivity with iso-tenuazonic acid, and restricted commercial availability.6,13,14 Consequently, a critical gap persists for accessible, rapid, cost-effective screening methods suitable for routine TeA surveillance in food control laboratories of developing regions.

Spectrofluorimetry has emerged as a powerful complement and practical alternative to chromatographic techniques for mycotoxin analysis, offering high sensitivity, excellent selectivity through fluorophore-specific excitation–emission characteristics, rapid analytical throughput, and minimal solvent consumption consistent with green analytical chemistry principles.15,16 While several mycotoxins including aflatoxins and ochratoxin A possess native fluorescence exploitable by HPLC-FLD or direct spectrofluorimetric detection, the majority of mycotoxins including the emerging Alternaria toxins lack intrinsic fluorophores and require probe-mediated detection strategies.17 Synthetic fluorescent probes—including xanthene dyes (rhodamine 6G, rhodamine B, eosin Y, erythrosine B), cyanines, and increasingly carbon or graphene quantum dots—enable indirect quantification of non-fluorescent analytes through “turn-off” quenching mechanisms, in which analyte binding induces proportional fluorescence intensity decrease via static ground-state complex formation, dynamic collisional quenching, or inner-filter effects.18,19 Such probe-based spectrofluorimetric platforms have been successfully applied to determine aflatoxins in wheat, ochratoxin A in cereal products, and zearalenone in maize,20,21 establishing fluorescence quenching as a validated analytical framework for trace mycotoxin detection in complex food matrices amenable to surveillance applications in resource-limited laboratories.22 However, achieving the requisite limits of quantification for regulatory compliance in complex food matrices requires integration of sensitive spectrofluorimetric detection with effective sample pre-concentration and matrix cleanup strategies, necessitating careful optimization of the extraction methodology to minimize analyte loss while maximizing selectivity and enrichment.

Dispersive liquid–liquid microextraction (DLLME), introduced by Rezaee et al. in 2006, has become one of the most widely adopted miniaturized sample preparation techniques for mycotoxin analysis in food matrices, offering rapid equilibration, high enrichment factors, minimal organic solvent consumption, and excellent compatibility with both chromatographic and spectroscopic detection platforms.23–25 The method relies on a ternary solvent system—aqueous sample, water-miscible disperser, and water-immiscible extractant—in which rapid injection generates a cloudy emulsion of extractant microdroplets, providing an enormous interfacial area that accelerates analyte partitioning to equilibrium within seconds.26 DLLME has been successfully applied to a wide range of mycotoxins including aflatoxins, ochratoxin A, zearalenone, fumonisins, and emerging Alternaria toxins across diverse food matrices such as cereals, rice bran, fruit juices, wines, and dried fruits, consistently achieving low-µg kg−1 limits of quantification compliant with EU regulatory requirements.27–30 For complex sugar-rich matrices such as dried fruits, where high viscosity, polyphenols, and reducing sugars challenge conventional cleanup, DLLME offers particular advantages through phase-selective exclusion of hydrophilic matrix constituents into the aqueous phase while selectively concentrating mycotoxins into the small-volume organic extract.31,32 Furthermore, while early DLLME applications relied on one-factor-at-a-time optimization, contemporary method development increasingly employs design-of-experiments approaches including Box–Behnken and central composite designs with Derringer's desirability function to rigorously resolve interactions between extractant volume, disperser volume, pH, salt concentration, and extraction time.33,34 Integration of DLLME with spectrofluorimetric detection represents a particularly attractive combination for resource-appropriate mycotoxin surveillance, uniting microextraction-based pre-concentration with accessible instrumentation.35

This work develops and validates a novel “turn-off” spectrofluorimetric method for tenuazonic acid determination in dried fig samples using rhodamine 6G as a cationic xanthene fluorescent probe, representing the first integration of dispersive liquid–liquid microextraction with probe-based spectrofluorimetric detection for an Alternaria mycotoxin. The specific research objectives include: (1) development and optimization of a dispersive liquid–liquid microextraction procedure for tenuazonic acid pre-concentration from the sugar-rich dried fig matrix, with extraction conditions systematically optimized through a face-centered central composite design coupled with Derringer's desirability function; (2) comprehensive spectral characterization of the rhodamine 6G–tenuazonic acid system; (3) elucidation of the binding mechanism through Stern–Volmer analysis at multiple temperatures, Job's continuous variation method, and thermodynamic parameter evaluation; (4) univariate optimization of fluorimetric reaction conditions including pH, rhodamine 6G concentration, buffer volume, and reaction time; (5) comprehensive method validation following Commission Implementing Regulation (EU) 2023/2782, EURL mycotoxin identification guidance; (6) application to Egyptian dried fig samples with occurrence assessment against Commission Recommendation (EU) 2022/553; and finally (7) holistic green analytical chemistry evaluation using AGSA-Prep, WECA, and EPPI metrics. This comprehensive methodological framework integrates sensitive spectrofluorimetric detection with optimized sample pre-concentration, rigorous analytical validation, and green chemistry assessment, establishing a practical analytical platform suitable for regulatory surveillance of tenuazonic acid in dried fruit commodities, particularly in laboratories with limited settings.

Experimental

Materials and reagents

Tenuazonic acid reference standard (purity ≥98%) and rhodamine 6G chloride (C27H31ClN2O3, CAS: 989-38-8, dye content ≥95%) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA). Analytical grade chemicals including boric acid, acetic acid, sodium hydroxide, and ammonium sulfate were sourced from PioChem Co. (Cairo, Egypt). HPLC-grade organic solvents including 1-octanol, acetonitrile, methanol, and ethanol were obtained from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA). Formic acid and phosphoric acid were acquired from Merck KGaA (Darmstadt, Germany). Deionized water was produced using a Millipore Direct-Q water purification system and employed throughout all experimental procedures.

Britton–Robinson universal buffer was prepared by combining equimolar concentrations (0.04 M each) of phosphoric acid, acetic acid, and boric acid in deionized water. Buffer pH adjustment across the range 3.0–9.0 was achieved through dropwise addition of 0.2 M sodium hydroxide solution. Rhodamine 6G probe solution (100 µg mL−1) was prepared by direct dissolution in deionized water and stored under dark, refrigerated conditions. Dried fig samples were procured from local Egyptian markets and stored in sealed containers at ambient temperature until processing.

Instrumentation

Fluorescence measurements were performed on a Jasco FP-6200 spectrofluorometer (Jasco, Tokyo, Japan) equipped with a 150 W xenon lamp and photomultiplier tube detector, with excitation and emission slit widths both set at 10 nm. UV-vis absorption spectra for spectral characterization studies were acquired using a Shimadzu UV-1800 spectrophotometer (Shimadzu, Kyoto, Japan). Sample and extract centrifugation were carried out using an Eppendorf 5810R refrigerated centrifuge (Eppendorf AG, Hamburg, Germany). Sonication during both sample extraction and DLLME emulsification steps was provided by a Branson 2800 ultrasonic bath (Branson Ultrasonics, Danbury, CT, USA) operating at 40 kHz. Evaporation of organic extracts to dryness was accomplished using an Organomation N-EVAP 112 nitrogen evaporation system (Organomation Associates, Berlin, MA, USA). Vortex agitation was performed using an IKA Vortex Genius 3 mixer (IKA Works, Staufen, Germany). Solution pH measurements and adjustments were monitored with a Jenway 3510 pH meter (Cole-Parmer, Stone, UK). All gravimetric measurements were performed on a Sartorius analytical balance (Sartorius AG, Göttingen, Germany). Experimental design construction and statistical analysis were conducted using Design-Expert® software (Stat-Ease Inc., Minneapolis, MN, USA).

DLLME optimization

Preliminary screening investigations were undertaken to identify the most appropriate extractant solvent, disperser solvent, and salt electrolyte for the microextraction procedure. Water-immiscible organic solvents (dichloromethane, chloroform, 1-octanol, ethyl acetate, toluene, diethyl ether), water-miscible dispersers (methanol, acetonitrile, ethanol, isopropanol), and inorganic electrolytes (sodium chloride, ammonium sulfate, ammonium dihydrogen phosphate, sodium dihydrogen phosphate) were evaluated by comparing extraction recovery percentages. On the basis of these findings, 1-octanol, acetonitrile, and ammonium sulfate were selected as the optimal extractant, disperser, and electrolyte additive, respectively.

Following preliminary solvent and salt selection, a four-factor, three-level face-centered central composite design (FCCCD, α = 1.0) was constructed using Design-Expert® software to rigorously investigate the influence of four critical microextraction variables on extraction efficiency: extractant volume (A, 100–500 µL), disperser volume (B, 200–1000 µL), solution pH (C, 3–7), and ammonium sulfate concentration (D, 2–6%). The resulting experimental matrix comprised 29 randomized runs incorporating 5 center point replicates to enable pure error estimation and guard against systematic experimental bias. For each design run, a 5 mL aqueous solution containing 1000 µg tenuazonic acid standard was transferred into a 10 mL centrifuge tube, to which predetermined volumes of acetonitrile and 1-octanol were introduced sequentially, generating a transient polydisperse emulsion characterized by extensive interfacial area conducive to rapid analyte partitioning. The resulting dispersion was subjected to vortex agitation for 2 minutes, followed by sonication for 5 minutes to maximize mass transfer efficiency across the aqueous-organic interface. Phase separation was achieved by centrifugation at 1600×g for 8 minutes. The floating 1-octanol layer was carefully collected and subjected to spectrofluorimetric measurement. Extraction recovery (%) was calculated as the response variable for optimization.

The experimental dataset was then fitted to a second-order polynomial model through multiple regression analysis. Statistical significance of the overall model and individual terms was assessed through ANOVA, with model adequacy evaluated through R2, adjusted R2, predicted R2, and lack of fit test. Response surface and contour plots were constructed to visualize main effects and pairwise factor interactions. Optimal conditions were identified by locating the maximum predicted response on the fitted quadratic response surface model and subsequently confirmed through independent triplicate experimental verification.

Sample preparation procedures

Dried fig samples were finely homogenized using a laboratory blender prior to extraction. A representative 5 g aliquot of homogenized paste was accurately weighed into a 50 mL centrifuge tube, to which 20 mL of methanol/water (80 : 20, v/v) containing 1% formic acid was added. The mixture was sonicated for 15 minutes at room temperature to facilitate analyte extraction, followed by centrifugation at 5000×g for 10 minutes. The resultant supernatant was carefully transferred into a clean vessel and evaporated to dryness under a gentle stream of nitrogen at room temperature. The dried residue was reconstituted in 5 mL deionized water, and the pH was adjusted to 3.3 ± 0.1 using dilute formic acid. Ammonium sulfate was added to achieve a final concentration of 5% (w/v), followed by sequential introduction of 700 µL acetonitrile and 350 µL 1-octanol. The mixture was vortex-agitated for 2 minutes and sonicated for 5 minutes. Phase separation was accomplished through centrifugation at 1600×g for 8 minutes, and the floating 1-octanol layer was carefully collected, evaporated to dryness under a gentle stream of nitrogen at room temperature, and reconstituted in 2 mL ethanol for subsequent spectrofluorimetric measurement.

Optimization of spectrofluorimetric conditions and general analytical procedure

The spectrofluorimetric reaction conditions governing the interaction between rhodamine 6G and tenuazonic acid were systematically optimized through a univariate one-factor-at-a-time (OFAT) approach, wherein each variable was independently varied while maintaining all remaining parameters constant. The fluorescence quenching ratio (F0/F), where F0 and F represent the rhodamine 6G emission intensities in the absence and presence of tenuazonic acid, respectively, served as the analytical response throughout the optimization procedure.

The influence of pH on the fluorescence quenching interaction was examined over the range of pH 3–9 using Britton–Robinson (BR) universal buffer, selected for its broad buffering capacity across the entire investigated pH domain. The effect of BR buffer volume on analytical response was subsequently investigated over the range 0.25–1.50 mL. Rhodamine 6G probe concentration was evaluated across the range 10–40 µg mL−1 to identify the concentration yielding maximum quenching sensitivity. The kinetics of the fluorophore-analyte interaction were assessed by monitoring the fluorescence quenching ratio as a function of reaction time over the interval 1–10 minutes. Based on OFAT optimization results, pH 6, buffer volume 1.0 mL, R6G concentration 25 µg mL−1, and reaction time 3 minutes were established as optimal fluorimetric conditions.

For the general analytical procedure, an appropriate aliquot of the ethanolic reconstituted extract was transferred into a 5 mL volumetric flask. Britton–Robinson buffer (pH 6, 1.0 mL) and rhodamine 6G solution (25 µg mL−1) were added sequentially, and the volume was completed to 5 mL with deionized water. The mixture was equilibrated at 25 ± 2 °C for 3 minutes prior to fluorescence measurement. Fluorescence emission spectra were recorded at an excitation wavelength of 526 nm with emission monitored at 555 nm. The fluorescence quenching ratio (F0/F) was calculated and employed as the quantitative analytical signal for tenuazonic acid determination.

Method validation

Prior to validation experiments, a suitable blank dried fig matrix was obtained by LC-MS/MS screening of commercially available dried fig samples, with tenuazonic acid concentration confirmed below the instrumental detection limit (<6.4 µg kg−1) in the selected pool; the verified blank matrix was subsequently homogenized, pooled, and stored at −20 °C until use, ensuring that all fortification responses were attributable exclusively to added analyte without contribution from endogenous matrix contamination.

The developed method was then validated in accordance with Commission Implementing Regulation (EU) 2023/2782. Validation parameters encompassing specificity, linearity, LOD, LOQ, trueness, precision, and measurement uncertainty were systematically evaluated. QC samples were prepared using blank dried fig matrix samples fortified at three concentration levels (500, 1000, and 1500 µg kg−1), corresponding to 0.5×, 1.0×, and 1.5× the indicative level established by Commission Recommendation (EU) 2022/553. Each fortification level was analyzed in six replicates on three independent days (n = 18 per level) to assess repeatability (relative standard deviation of repeatability, RSDr) and within-laboratory reproducibility (relative standard deviation of within-laboratory reproducibility, RSDwR). Selectivity was examined by evaluating the influence of co-occurring Alternaria mycotoxins, common food mycotoxins, and representative dried fig matrix constituents (glucose, fructose, citric acid, ascorbic acid, gallic acid, quercetin) on the analytical response at concentrations representative of those potentially encountered in dried fig samples. Linearity was established using matrix-matched calibration standards prepared across the concentration range 20–4000 µg kg−1 with 1/x2 weighted linear regression applied to account for heteroscedasticity. LOD and LOQ were determined from signal-to-noise ratios of 3 and 10, respectively. Measurement uncertainty was estimated following the top-down approach incorporating contributions from systematic bias (Ubias) and within-laboratory reproducibility (UP), with expanded uncertainty reported at coverage factor k = 2. Matrix effects were assessed by comparing the slopes of matrix-matched calibration curves prepared in blank dried fig extract with those of solvent-based calibration curves prepared in deionized water, with matrix effect (%) calculated as the percentage difference between the two slopes. Selectivity was examined by evaluating the influence of co-occurring Alternaria mycotoxins, common food mycotoxins, and representative dried fig matrix constituents (glucose, fructose, citric acid, ascorbic acid, ascorbic acid, and quercetin) on the analytical response at concentrations representative of those potentially encountered in dried fig samples, with all experiments conducted at a fixed TeA concentration of 1000 µg kg−1 corresponding to the EU indicative level.

Cross-validation against an in-house LC-MS/MS reference method was conducted on fortified blank dried fig matrix samples prepared at five concentration levels (100, 500, 1000, 1500, and 3000 µg kg−1) with six replicates per level (n = 30 paired observations). The reference LC-MS/MS method was adapted from the European Standard EN 17521:2021 for Alternaria toxin determination, with instrumental conditions modified for the available equipment. Chromatographic separation was performed using an Agilent 1290 Infinity II UHPLC system coupled to an Agilent 6470 Triple Quadrupole mass spectrometer (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA) on a ZORBAX Eclipse Plus C18 column (2.1 × 150 mm, 3.5 µm) maintained at 40 °C with an injection volume of 5 µL. Mobile phase A comprised 5 mM ammonium formate in deionized water adjusted to pH 8.3 with ammonia solution, and mobile phase B was methanol. Gradient elution was applied at 0.3 mL min−1 as follows: 5% B held for 0.5 min, increased to 40% B at 3.0 min, then to 100% B at 15.0 min, held until 19.0 min, and returned to 5% B at 19.1 min with re-equilibration to 26.0 min. Mass spectrometric detection was performed in negative electrospray ionization mode with the following source parameters: capillary voltage −3.5 kV, gas temperature 325 °C, drying gas flow 10 mL min−1, nebulizer pressure 35 psi, sheath gas temperature 350 °C, and sheath gas flow 11 mL min−1. Tenuazonic acid was monitored in MRM mode using precursor ion [M − H]− at m/z 196.1, with quantifier transition m/z 196.1 → 152.1 (loss of CO2, collision energy 10 eV, fragmentor voltage 80 V) and qualifier transition m/z 196.1 → 96.1. Matrix-matched external calibration was applied throughout, with ion ratio tolerances maintained within ±30% of calibration solution values in accordance with Commission Implementing Regulation (EU) 2023/2782. Method agreement was evaluated through Pearson correlation analysis, Bland–Altman agreement analysis, and equivalence testing, with acceptance criteria defined as Pearson r > 0.95, R2 > 0.90, mean bias within ±20%, and equivalence 90% confidence interval within 80–120%.

Application to Egyptian dried fig samples

A total of 60 commercially available dried fig samples were procured from local Egyptian retail outlets, comprising 30 domestically produced samples and 30 imported samples. Domestic samples were collected from two retail channel types: loose market stalls (n = 20), where dried figs are sold unpackaged and by weight from open display, and packaged retail outlets (n = 10), where sealed and labeled products were obtained from supermarkets and hypermarkets. Imported samples were similarly divided between loose market (n = 20) and packaged retail (n = 10) sources. Upon collection, samples were transferred to sealed polyethylene containers and stored at ambient temperature in the dark until analysis, which was conducted within two weeks of collection.

Each sample was processed according to the procedure previously described. Briefly, a 5 g aliquot of finely homogenized dried fig paste was subjected to methanol/water (80 : 20, v/v) extraction containing 1% formic acid, followed by evaporation and reconstitution of the dried residue prior to DLLME under the optimized conditions (extractant volume 350 µL 1-octanol, disperser volume 700 µL acetonitrile, pH 3.3, ammonium sulfate concentration 5% w/v). The collected 1-octanol extract was evaporated to dryness and reconstituted in 2 mL ethanol for subsequent spectrofluorimetric measurement under the optimized reaction conditions (pH 6, Britton–Robinson buffer volume 1.0 mL, rhodamine 6G concentration 25 µg mL−1, equilibration time 3 minutes, excitation/emission wavelengths 526/555 nm). Tenuazonic acid concentrations in each sample were calculated by interpolation against the matrix-matched calibration curve prepared in blank dried fig extract. Samples yielding concentrations below the LOQ (19.2 µg kg−1) were reported as not detected (ND). All analyses were performed in triplicate (n = 3) and results expressed as mean ± standard deviation. Occurrence data were interpreted against the indicative level of 1000 µg kg−1 established for tenuazonic acid in dried figs by Commission Recommendation (EU) 2022/553.

Results and discussion

Preliminary solvent and salt screening

Selection of appropriate extraction solvents and salt additive was accomplished through systematic single-factor screening (Fig. S1). Among water-immiscible extractant solvents, 1-octanol yielded markedly superior recovery (95.91 ± 0.86%), while remaining solvents performed considerably lower (63.74–84.72%) (Fig. S1A). This superiority reflects 1-octanol's intermediate polarity matching tenuazonic acid's amphiphilic character, enabling simultaneous interaction with both the hydrophobic sec-butyl side chain and the polar tetramic acid enol system through hydrogen bonding. Highly nonpolar solvents such as toluene (77.06 ± 5.38%) and diethyl ether (66.75 ± 6.57%) insufficiently solvated the polar tetramic acid moiety, while halogenated solvents including dichloromethane (63.74 ± 3.2%) and chloroform (73.98 ± 2.9%) yielded inferior recoveries despite their widespread DLLME utility, likely attributable to insufficient solvation of the polar tetramic acid enol system and unfavorable partition coefficients for tenuazonic acid. Disperser solvent screening (Fig. S1B) identified acetonitrile as optimal (93.75 ± 2.69%), significantly outperforming methanol (72.47 ± 1.19%), ethanol (67.56 ± 1.14%), and isopropanol (64.33 ± 2.19%) through its high water miscibility rapidly generating finely dispersed 1-octanol microdroplets with maximized interfacial area. Salt electrolyte evaluation (Fig. S1C) revealed ammonium sulfate as most effective (90.45 ± 1.7%), substantially exceeding sodium dihydrogen phosphate (80.71 ± 0.91%), sodium chloride (75.24 ± 2.12%), ammonium acetate (70.73 ± 0.58%), with unsalted conditions yielding only 67.39 ± 0.88%. The kosmotropic nature of the sulfate dianion creates strong hydration competition with tenuazonic acid molecules, effectively reducing aqueous solubility and driving preferential organic phase partitioning. Accordingly, 1-octanol, acetonitrile, and ammonium sulfate were selected for subsequent multivariate optimization.

FCCCD design and statistical analysis

A face-centered central composite design was selected for its ability to efficiently explore curvature and factor interactions within a three-level framework without requiring extreme axial points beyond the factorial boundaries (α = 1.0), making it particularly suitable for constrained analytical optimization problems. The design simultaneously investigated extractant volume (A), disperser volume (B), solution pH (C), and salt concentration (D) across 29 randomized runs. ANOVA results confirmed highly significant overall model performance (F = 191.71, p < 0.0001) with non-significant lack of fit (p = 0.7322), validating adequate model representation. Among main effects, extractant volume exerted the strongest influence (F = 287.41), followed by pH (F = 235.99), disperser volume (F = 175.12), and salt concentration (F = 146.67), all achieving p < 0.0001 (Table 1). Three significant two-factor interactions were identified: AB (F = 116.21, p < 0.0001), BD (F = 80.38, p < 0.0001), and AD (F = 18.85, p = 0.0004), indicating that solvent volumes and salt concentration cannot be independently optimized. Quadratic terms for extractant volume (A2, F = 32.64), disperser volume (B2, F = 105.30), and salt concentration (D2, F = 23.79) all achieved significance (p ≤ 0.0001), confirming well-defined interior optima for these variables. Notably, pH exhibited exclusively linear behavior with no significant quadratic contribution, indicating a monotonic rather than parabolic response across the investigated range.

Model performance metrics corroborated these findings. R2 = 0.9907 demonstrated that the model accounted for 99.07% of response variability, while close agreement between adjusted R2 (0.9855) and predicted R2 (0.9751) confirmed absence of overfitting (Table S1). Diagnostic plots (Fig. S2) validated model assumptions, demonstrating normally distributed residuals (Fig. S2A), excellent predicted-actual concordance (Fig. S2B), random residual scatter confirming error independence (Fig. S2C), and acceptable leverage distribution confirming absence of influential observations (Fig. S2D).

Coefficient estimates (Table S2) and one-factor plots (Fig. 1) collectively elucidated the directional effects and physical significance of each factor. Extractant volume demonstrated a positive linear effect (+2.69) with pronounced parabolic behavior (A2 = −2.29, Fig. 1A), where insufficient volumes below the optimum fail to accommodate the total analyte load despite favorable partition coefficients, while excessive volumes reduce enrichment factors by unnecessarily diluting the collected organic phase. Disperser volume exhibited analogous parabolic behavior with the steepest curvature among all factors (B2 = −4.11, Fig. 1B), reflecting the critical role of acetonitrile in generating adequate interfacial area; sub-optimal volumes produce coarse poorly-dispersed emulsions with limited mass transfer, whereas excessive volumes approach the water miscibility threshold, promoting analyte back-partitioning into the aqueous phase. Solution pH exerted the strongest negative linear influence (−2.44, Fig. 1C) with no significant quadratic contribution, consistent with the ionization behavior of tenuazonic acid's tetramic acid moiety; progressive deprotonation at elevated pH generates anionic species with substantially reduced affinity for the hydrophobic organic phase, mandating acidic extraction conditions. Salt concentration contributed positively (+1.92) with moderate curvature (D2 = −1.96, Fig. 1D), where intermediate concentrations maximize the kosmotropic salting-out effect, while excessive ionic strength compromises emulsion stability and phase disengagement efficiency. The significant AB interaction (+1.82) revealed that extractant and disperser volumes operate synergistically, requiring coordinated rather than independent optimization to achieve maximal emulsification efficiency and analyte recovery.

Response surface interactions and optimal conditions

Three-dimensional response surface plots (Fig. 2) provided graphical elucidation of significant pairwise factor interactions identified through ANOVA. The extractant-disperser volume interaction surface (Fig. 2A) revealed a well-defined ridge-like maximum, confirming that these variables operate synergistically and cannot be independently optimized; low extractant volumes paired with high disperser volumes generated insufficient organic phase capacity despite adequate emulsification, while the inverse combination produced coarse dispersions with poor interfacial contact, collectively demonstrating that balanced coordination of both volumes is essential for maximizing analyte partitioning efficiency. The extractant volume-salt concentration surface (Fig. 2B) demonstrated that salting-out enhancement becomes particularly pronounced at intermediate extractant volumes, where ionic strength effects compensate for suboptimal phase volume ratios, while at higher extractant volumes the incremental benefit of additional salt diminishes progressively. The disperser-salt concentration surface (Fig. 2C) revealed that optimal ionic strength conditions are sensitive to disperser volume levels, with higher salt concentrations proving beneficial at elevated acetonitrile volumes to counteract the partial aqueous miscibility effect that otherwise reduces phase separation efficiency.

Three-dimensional response surface plots showing the interactive effects of (A) extractant solvent volume and disperser solvent volume, (B) extractant solvent volume and salt concentration, and (C) disperser solvent volume and salt concentration on tenuazonic acid extraction recovery.

Simultaneous optimization of all four factors was achieved by identifying the maximum predicted response on the fitted quadratic response surface model (Fig. S3), yielding optimal conditions of extractant volume 354.95 µL, disperser volume 694.82 µL, pH 3.33, and ammonium sulfate concentration 4.76%, with a predicted extraction recovery of 96.34%. For practical implementation, these conditions were rounded to extractant volume 350 µL, disperser volume 700 µL, pH 3.3, and ammonium sulfate concentration 5%. Independent experimental verification in triplicate at these practically convenient conditions yielded mean recovery of 95.23% ± 1.48%, confirming excellent agreement with model predictions and validating the FCCCD optimization strategy for tenuazonic acid DLLME from dried fig matrices.

Spectroscopic characterization

Spectroscopic investigation of the rhodamine 6G–tenuazonic acid interaction was undertaken to establish the photophysical basis of the observed fluorescence quenching phenomenon and to identify optimal excitation–emission conditions for analytical application (Fig. 3). UV-vis absorption spectral characterization provided initial evidence for ground-state complex formation (Fig. 3A). The absorption spectrum of rhodamine 6G alone exhibited a characteristic xanthene chromophore band at 530 nm, while tenuazonic acid displayed its principal absorption band at 273 nm arising from the conjugated tetramic acid enol system, with negligible absorbance in the visible region. Upon complexation, the rhodamine 6G absorption band underwent a simultaneous hypsochromic shift to 523 nm accompanied by a hypochromic effect, reflecting perturbation of the xanthene electronic environment through direct ground-state interaction with tenuazonic acid. These spectral modifications—blue shift combined with decreased molar absorptivity—are characteristic signatures of static ground-state complex formation, providing preliminary mechanistic evidence for stable rhodamine 6G–tenuazonic acid complex formation in solution.

Fluorescence spectral characterization (Fig. 3B) established optimal excitation and emission wavelengths for the analytical system. Rhodamine 6G exhibited excitation and emission maxima at 525 and 555 nm respectively, characteristic of the open-ring xanthene fluorophore configuration. These wavelengths were adopted throughout all subsequent spectrofluorimetric measurements to maximize analytical sensitivity. The analytical utility of the fluorescence quenching response was demonstrated through concentration-dependent emission spectra (Fig. 3C). Progressive addition of tenuazonic acid across the analytical range produced systematic concentration-dependent attenuation of rhodamine 6G fluorescence intensity at 555 nm without measurable spectral shift, confirming proportional quenching behavior essential for quantitative analytical application. The systematic decrease in emission intensity with increasing tenuazonic acid concentration established the foundation for the proposed turn-off spectrofluorimetric quantification strategy.

Fluorescence quenching mechanism and binding characterization

The mechanism governing rhodamine 6G fluorescence quenching by tenuazonic acid was investigated through multi-temperature Stern–Volmer analysis, thermodynamic parameter evaluation, and stoichiometric determination. Stern–Volmer plots (Fig. 4A) demonstrated excellent linearity across all three investigated temperatures (298, 303, and 313 K), confirming that a single quenching mechanism predominates without inner filter effect contributions. The Stern–Volmer quenching constants (Ksv) exhibited an inverse temperature dependence, decreasing from 1.93 × 105 M−1 at 298 K to 1.65 × 105 M−1 at 303 K and 1.37 × 105 M−1 at 313 K (Table 2). This progressive reduction in Ksv with increasing temperature is diagnostic of static quenching arising from ground-state complex formation, consistent with UV-vis spectroscopic findings, since dynamic collisional quenching would produce the opposite temperature-dependent trend through enhanced molecular collision frequency.

Thermodynamic parameters derived from the Van't Hoff plot (Fig. 4B) revealed negative enthalpy (ΔH = −15.22 kJ mol−1) and positive entropy (ΔS = +51.36 J mol−1 K−1) changes, indicating that the interaction is driven by a combination of electrostatic attraction between the cationic rhodamine 6G and the anionic tetramic acid enolate, alongside hydrophobic contributions releasing ordered water molecules from the binding interface. The negative Gibbs free energy values becoming increasingly favorable with temperature (ΔG = −30.54, −30.80, −31.31 kJ mol−1 at 298, 303, 313 K respectively) confirmed the spontaneous nature of complex formation under all investigated conditions.

Stoichiometric characterization through Job's continuous variation method (Fig. 4C) identified maximum fluorescence change at a tenuazonic acid mole fraction of 0.5, unambiguously establishing a 1 : 1 binding stoichiometry between rhodamine 6G and tenuazonic acid. This defined stoichiometry supports the formation of a discrete ground-state complex rather than non-specific aggregation, providing fundamental justification for the linear calibration model adopted in the proposed analytical method.

OFAT optimization of spectrofluorimetric conditions

Spectrofluorimetric reaction conditions governing the rhodamine 6G–tenuazonic acid quenching system were systematically optimized through a univariate approach, with F0/F serving as the analytical response throughout. The effect of pH was investigated over the range 3–9 using Britton–Robinson universal buffer (Fig. 5A). The quenching ratio increased progressively from 1.61 ± 0.13 at pH 3, reaching maximum response at pH 6 (3.10 ± 0.16), before declining sharply to 1.51 ± 0.16 at pH 9. The optimal response at pH 6 is attributed to the predominantly anionic form of tenuazonic acid (pKa ≈ 3.8) at this pH, which maximizes electrostatic attraction with the cationic xanthene rhodamine 6G probe, facilitating stable ground-state complex formation. At strongly acidic conditions (pH 3–4), tenuazonic acid exists predominantly in its neutral protonated form, substantially reducing the electrostatic driving force for complexation. At alkaline pH values exceeding 7, progressive structural transformation of rhodamine 6G toward its non-fluorescent lactone form diminishes the available fluorophore population, resulting in sharply attenuated quenching response. Buffer volume influence examined across 0.25–1.50 mL (Fig. 5B) revealed progressive increase from 1.57 ± 0.12 at 0.25 mL to maximum at 1.0 mL (3.05 ± 0.16), beyond which values plateaued, indicating sufficient ionic strength and buffering capacity without unnecessary dilution of the analytical system. Accordingly, pH 6 and 1.0 mL BR buffer were adopted as optimal conditions.

One-factor-at-a-time (OFAT) optimization of spectrofluorimetric reaction conditions for the rhodamine 6G–tenuazonic acid system: (A) effect of pH; (B) effect of Britton–Robinson buffer volume; (C) effect of rhodamine 6G concentration; (D) effect of reaction time on the F0/F quenching response.

Rhodamine 6G probe concentration evaluated across 10–40 µg mL−1 (Fig. 5C) demonstrated increasing response from 1.71 ± 0.11 at 10 µg mL−1 to maximum at 25 µg mL−1 (3.03 ± 0.16), before declining at higher concentrations attributable to self-quenching and inner filter effects at elevated dye concentrations. Consequently, 25 µg mL−1 was selected as optimal. Reaction time kinetics monitored over 1–10 minutes (Fig. 5D) revealed rapid response increase from 1.82 ± 0.10 at 1 minute to stable plateau at 3 minutes (3.02 ± 0.13), with no significant variation observed beyond this point, confirming rapid equilibrium attainment consistent with static ground-state complex formation. Three minutes was therefore established as the optimal equilibration time.

Method validation

The developed spectrofluorimetric method was comprehensively validated in accordance with Commission Implementing Regulation (EU) 2023/2782. Linearity was established across the concentration range 20–4000 µg kg−1 using matrix-matched calibration standards with 1/x2 weighted regression, yielding the calibration model F0/F = 1.090 + 1.028 × 10−3 × C with excellent coefficient of determination (R2 = 0.9981), confirming proportional fluorescence quenching response across the entire analytical range (Table S3). The achieved LOQ of 19.2 µg kg−1 satisfies the mandatory analytical requirement stipulated by Commission Recommendation (EU) 2022/553, which prescribes a maximum permissible LOQ of 20 µg kg−1 for tenuazonic acid determination in food commodities, with the LOQ/maximum level ratio of 0.019 confirming adequate sensitivity for regulatory surveillance purposes.

Trueness and precision were simultaneously assessed through fortified blank matrix experiments at three QC concentration levels (Table 3). Mean recoveries of 99.7%, 94.9%, and 96.4% at 500, 1000, and 1500 µg kg−1 respectively complied with the EU 2023/2782 acceptable range of 70–120%. Repeatability (RSDr) values of 9.7%, 10.0%, and 8.4%, and within-laboratory reproducibility (RSDwR) values of 9.7%, 10.6%, and 9.4% at respective spike levels satisfied prescribed precision criteria. Expanded measurement uncertainty U (k = 2) values of ±20.6%, ±22.4%, and ±19.9% at 500, 1000, and 1500 µg kg−1 respectively remained well within the maximum permissible expanded uncertainty of ±50% stipulated for mycotoxin analytical methods. Selectivity was evaluated by examining the influence of co-occurring Alternaria mycotoxins, common food mycotoxins, and representative dried fig matrix constituents on the analytical response (Table S4). Alternariol, alternariol monomethyl ether, tentoxin, aflatoxin B1, ochratoxin A, patulin, glucose, fructose, citric acid, ascorbic acid, and quercetin produced negligible effects on quenching efficiency, with recoveries remaining within acceptable limits. The analyte-to-interferent ratios examined ranged from 100 : 1 for co-occurring Alternaria mycotoxins to 1 : 5000 for predominant matrix sugars, representing worst-case interference scenarios that substantially exceed concentrations realistically anticipated in commercially available dried fig samples, thereby confirming the robustness of the proposed method selectivity under conditions of practical regulatory relevance. Matrix effects assessment revealed negligible signal suppression (−3.4%), well within the acceptable threshold, confirming that the optimized DLLME cleanup procedure effectively eliminated the majority of matrix-associated interferences inherent to the complex sugar-rich dried fig matrix.

Green analytical chemistry assessment

The environmental sustainability, analytical performance, and practical applicability of the proposed DLLME–spectrofluorimetric method were comprehensively evaluated using three complementary, recently developed assessment metrics: AGSA-Prep for sample preparation greenness, WECA for holistic whiteness assessment, and EPPI for integrated environmental and performance evaluation. The combined application of these three frameworks provides a multidimensional sustainability profile that addresses the limitations inherent to any single-metric assessment approach (Fig. 6).

Holistic green analytical chemistry assessment of the proposed DLLME–spectrofluorimetric method using three complementary metrics: (A) AGSA-Prep score (65%, acceptably green); (B) WECA score (81%, high whiteness) showing balanced performance across red (analytical performance, 81), green (environmental impact, 83), and blue (practical efficiency, 80) domains; (C) EPPI dual-index assessment with environmental index of 88.4 and performance practicality index of 80.0, yielding a total EPPI score of 84.2 (highly recommended).

The Analytical Green Star Area for Sample Preparation (AGSA-Prep) tool was applied to evaluate the DLLME procedure against the ten Green Sample Preparation principles using weighted, categorical scoring.38 The proposed DLLME procedure achieved an AGSA-Prep score of 65%, falling within the “Acceptably Green” classification range (50–74%) (Fig. 6A). Strong performance was demonstrated across criteria related to minimization of sample, chemical, and material amounts (criterion 5) and reduction of waste generation (criterion 4), reflecting the inherently miniaturized nature of DLLME, which consumed only 350 µL of 1-octanol as extractant and 700 µL of acetonitrile as disperser. The method also scored favorably on operator safety (criterion 10), requiring only minimal personal protective equipment owing to the relatively low-hazard solvent system. Moderate scores were observed for criteria related to integration of steps and automation (criterion 7) and minimization of energy consumption (criterion 8), reflecting the manual, multi-step nature of the DLLME workflow. The score reflects an inherent trade-off between sample pre-concentration efficiency required for sub-LOQ determination of TeA in complex sugar-rich matrices and the additional procedural steps necessitated by such pre-concentration.

The Whiteness Evaluation for Chemical Analysis (WECA) tool was applied to provide a holistic assessment integrating analytical performance (red), environmental impact (green), and practical efficiency (blue) using the 4 × 4 × 4 model with four criteria selected per domain.39 The proposed method achieved an overall WECA score of 81%, classified as a high-performance method (≥75 threshold), with balanced performance across all three pillars: red domain = 81 (analytical performance), green domain = 83 (environmental sustainability), and blue domain = 80 (practical efficiency) (Fig. 6B). The high green domain score reflects the use of relatively low-toxicity solvents (1-octanol and acetonitrile in microliter volumes), the absence of chlorinated or highly hazardous reagents, and minimal waste generation. The high red domain score corresponds to the rigorous validation outcomes documented in Section 3.5, including satisfactory linearity (R2 = 0.9981), accuracy with mean recoveries ranging from 94.9% to 99.7% at the three QC levels (500, 1000, and 1500 µg kg−1), intra-day and inter-day precision below 11%, and a method LOQ of 19.2 µg kg−1 fully complying with the 20 µg kg−1 requirement of Commission Recommendation (EU) 2022/553. The blue domain score reflects the method's reliance on widely available spectrofluorimetric instrumentation, minimal cost per analysis, short total analysis time, and operational simplicity suitable for routine deployment in resource-limited laboratories. The balanced 80–83 range across all three domains visually converges toward whiteness, confirming the method's coherent fitness-for-purpose for routine TeA monitoring applications.

The Environmental, Performance, and Practicality Index (EPPI) was applied as a unified dual-index framework integrating environmental sustainability and operational feasibility.40 The Environmental Impact (EI) index, calculated as the average of sample preparation score, instrumentation score, reagent score, and waste score, achieved a value of 88.4, placing the method in the “ideal green method with minimal impact” classification (85–100 range) (Fig. 6C). This high EI score is attributable to the use of low-energy spectrofluorimetric detection (≤0.1 kWh per sample), partially green solvents employed in microliter quantities, and minimal waste generation (1–10 mL per sample). The Performance and Practicality Index (PPI) score of 80.0 confirms excellent practicality (75–100 range), supported by the quantitative nature of the method, application of FCCCD-based design of experiments for optimization, full validation, microgram-range sensitivity, low-cost commercially available reagents, widely accessible instrumentation with long lifespan and minimal maintenance requirements, and adequate sample throughput. Combination of the EI and PPI sub-indices yielded a final EPPI total score of 84.2, classifying the proposed method as “green and practical, efficient method” within the highly recommended category (75–100 range). Collectively, the three-metric assessment establishes the proposed method as a sustainable, fit-for-purpose analytical platform that successfully balances the competing demands of analytical performance, environmental responsibility, and operational practicality, offering a viable greener alternative to instrumentally intensive LC-MS/MS approaches conventionally employed for tenuazonic acid analysis in dried fig commodities.

Comparison with reported analytical methods for tenuazonic acid determination

The performance of the proposed DLLME–spectrofluorimetric method was evaluated against previously reported analytical methodologies for tenuazonic acid determination across diverse food matrices (Table S6). Current benchmark approaches are dominated by LC-MS/MS-based methods employing isotope dilution, derivatization, or alkaline chromatographic conditions necessitated by TeA's tetramic acid chemistry, as comprehensively reviewed by Ostry.40 The stable isotope dilution LC-MS/MS assay of Asam et al. achieved LOD values as low as 0.15 µg kg−1 in fruit juices through C18-SPE cleanup combined with 2,4-dinitrophenylhydrazine derivatization and isotopically labeled internal standards, demonstrating recovery values approaching 100% across diverse matrices including fruit juices, cereals, and spices.6 The UPLC-ESI-MS/MS method of Walravens et al., employing experimental design-based sample preparation optimization and isotopically labeled [13C6,15N]-TeA as internal standard, yielded TeA LOQ values of 1.21–2.35 µg kg−1 in cereal matrices with recoveries of 94.4–106.5%, establishing it as one of the most rigorously validated chromatographic methods for TeA in food.7 Multi-analyte LC-MS/MS approaches have progressively extended simultaneous coverage, with Puntscher et al. achieving LOD values of 4–7 µg kg−1 for TeA across 17 Alternaria toxins in tomato, sunflower, and wheat matrices, while Tölgyesi et al. incorporated TeA into a comprehensive 295-contaminant multi-method under alkaline mobile phase conditions at pH 8.3, which, while enabling symmetric TeA peak shape, resulted in elevated LOQ values and reduced sensitivity relative to dedicated methods.8,9 The derivatization-based HPLC-IT-MS2 method of Siegel et al. demonstrated adequate TeA sensitivity (LOD 2 µg kg−1) in beer matrices without LC-MS/MS instrumentation, albeit introducing additional preparation steps with associated derivative instability concerns.11 The UHPLC-MS/MS method of Guo et al. employing modified QuEChERS achieved excellent simultaneous determination of six Alternaria toxins in grape matrices with TeA LOD of 0.21 µg kg−1 and recoveries of 77.8–101.6%, providing the multi-toxin screening capability essential for comprehensive exposure assessment in fruit commodities.41

Immunoassay and biosensor platforms have emerged as attractive alternatives for rapid, high-throughput TeA surveillance. The nanobody-based icELISA of Wang et al. achieved an exceptionally low LOD of 0.09 ng mL−1 for iso-tenuazonic acid in rice, flour, and bread matrices, with recoveries of 78.3–96.3%, demonstrating that antibody-based platforms can deliver outstanding sensitivity through highly specific molecular recognition elements.13 However, the structural similarity between TeA and its epimeric isomer iso-tenuazonic acid introduces inherent cross-reactivity challenges that complicate accurate quantification in naturally contaminated samples, a limitation also acknowledged for conventional ELISA formats. The dual-labeled aptasensor developed by Qin et al. represented a significant methodological advance, with a SELEX-selected TeA-specific aptamer achieving LOD of 0.26 µM through fluorescence signal modulation without requiring animal immunization, though practical regulatory validation in food matrices remains at an early stage.14 The gold nano-flower lateral flow immunoassay of Cai et al. demonstrated a particularly attractive point-of-need screening format, achieving a visual LOD of 0.78 ng mL−1 for TeA in apple juice and tomato ketchup with a total assay time of 15 min, offering a portable and instrument-free detection strategy with sensitivity 16-fold superior to conventional colloidal gold strips.42

A recognized limitation of probe-based spectrofluorimetric methods is their inherently lower selectivity compared to chromatographic and mass spectrometric techniques, since fluorescent probes such as rhodamine 6G are not analyte-specific recognition elements and analytical selectivity relies primarily on sample preparation rather than probe-analyte binding specificity. The rhodamine 6G probe platform has been previously demonstrated as an effective analytical tool for pharmaceutical compound determination in relatively clean matrices;22 however, its application to food mycotoxin surveillance in complex sugar-rich matrices such as dried figs represents a substantially more demanding analytical challenge requiring dedicated matrix-specific extraction and cleanup strategies. In the present work, the four-variable FCCCD-optimized DLLME procedure fulfilled this critical role, achieving 95.23% extraction recovery while effectively partitioning co-extractable matrix interferences into the aqueous phase, thereby compensating for the inherent probe non-specificity and enabling reliable TeA quantification in the presence of co-occurring mycotoxins and endogenous dried fig constituents at concentrations substantially exceeding those realistically encountered in commercial samples. The cross-validation against the LC-MS/MS reference method, yielding a mean bias of 2.48% and equivalence 90% CI of 97.9–105.7% within predefined acceptance limits, provided direct quantitative evidence of the analytical reliability achievable through this integrated DLLME–spectrofluorimetric strategy. Compared with LC-MS/MS methods, the proposed approach eliminates requirements for isotopically labeled internal standards, derivatization reagents, alkaline mobile phase optimization, and high-capital tandem mass spectrometric instrumentation, while achieving full compliance with the EU regulatory LOQ threshold of 20 µg kg−1.

Conclusions, limitations, and future directions

The present study established a novel “turn-off” spectrofluorimetric method for tenuazonic acid determination in dried fig samples using rhodamine 6G as a cationic xanthene fluorescent probe, integrating dispersive liquid–liquid microextraction optimized through face-centered central composite design coupled with Derringer's desirability function. Comprehensive spectroscopic characterization, multi-temperature Stern–Volmer analysis, and Job's continuous variation method confirmed a static quenching mechanism mediated by 1 : 1 ground-state complex formation through electrostatic and hydrogen-bonding interactions, with thermodynamically favorable binding (ΔG < 0). The validated method demonstrated full compliance with Commission Implementing Regulation (EU) 2023/2782, EURL mycotoxin guidance, achieving a LOQ of 19.2 µg kg−1 that satisfies the analytical sensitivity prescribed by Commission Recommendation (EU) 2022/553. Cross-validation against an in-house LC-MS/MS reference method confirmed inter-method agreement within predefined acceptance limits, substantiating the analytical reliability of the proposed method for regulatory surveillance applications. Application to 60 commercially available Egyptian dried fig samples revealed widespread tenuazonic acid contamination (90% incidence), with 26.7% of samples exceeding the 1000 µg kg−1 indicative level, demonstrating substantial regulatory concern particularly within informally distributed loose market products. Holistic green chemistry assessment using AGSA-Prep, WECA, and EPPI yielded convergent classifications confirming the method as a sustainable and practical analytical platform.

However, despite the high performance of the developed method, several limitations warrant acknowledgment. The DLLME procedure remains manual and multi-step, contributing to the moderate AGSA-Prep score and limiting throughput for large-scale surveillance applications. The proposed method targets tenuazonic acid as a single analyte and cannot simultaneously quantify co-occurring Alternaria mycotoxins such as alternariol and alternariol monomethyl ether. While the present sampling covered 60 representative dried fig samples from the Egyptian retail market, broader nationwide surveillance encompassing additional governorates, seasonal variation, and a longer monitoring period would strengthen exposure assessment for risk characterization purposes. Future research directions include automation of the DLLME workflow through robotic liquid handlers to enhance throughput and reproducibility, extension of the rhodamine 6G probe-based platform to multiplexed detection of additional Alternaria mycotoxins through chemometric resolution of overlapping fluorescence signals, exploration of green deep eutectic solvents and other emerging bio-based extractants as environmentally superior alternatives to 1-octanol to further improve AGSA-Prep sustainability scores,43 adaptation of the proposed fluorimetric detection platform to portable smartphone-based instrumentation to enable point-of-need field screening of tenuazonic acid contamination in dried fruit supply chains,44 and broader nationwide surveillance studies to inform evidence-based regulatory policy on Alternaria mycotoxin contamination in Egyptian dried fruit commodities.
