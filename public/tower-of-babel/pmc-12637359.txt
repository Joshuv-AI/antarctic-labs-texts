Low Block Hybrid Rhinoplasty as a New Alternative for Mixed-Race Noses
Authors: Ronny Azabache, Jaime Robles, Carlos Segura
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background:Rhinoplasty is among the most popular plastic surgical procedures worldwide. In Latin America, performing rhinoplasty on mixed-race noses is challenging. Therefore, developing new approaches and techniques to improve surgical outcomes is important. This study describes the low block technique and evaluates its efficacy in preserving the dorsum and shaping the nasal tip.Methods:Forty-six patients underwent surgery using the low block technique in a private center in Trujillo, Peru, between August 2023 and January 2024. Nasal dorsum height and tip projection were measured using a projectometer before surgery, immediately after, and at 1, 3, and 6 months postoperatively. Surgical indications were classified according to the authors’ system (types I, II, and III) based on tissue morphology and cartilage quality.Results:The technique was performed on 42 women and 4 men aged 15–43 years (mean, 25 y). According to the approach, 16 patients (34.8%) were type I, 6 (13%) type IIa, 9 (19.6%) type IIb, and 15 (32.6%) type III. Mean operative time was 198 minutes. Pre- and postoperative measurements varied significantly (P < 0.05) and remained stable for 6 months. No adverse events occurred.Conclusions:The low block technique is effective in preserving and shaping the nasal tip and dorsum, as demonstrated by stable postoperative measurements during 6 months of follow-up. The technique proved safe, with no complications reported.

Takeaways

Question: Is the low block technique effective in maintaining the preservation and structuring of the nasal tip and dorsum?

Findings: The variation of measurements using a projectometer in the preoperative and postoperative periods shows a significant variation, which is maintained over time (first month, third month, and sixth month, P < 0.05).

Meaning: The low block technique is effective in maintaining the preservation and structuring of the nasal tip and dorsum, which is demonstrated in the measurements carried out during the 6 months after surgery. It is also a safe technique, with no complications evident at the end of this follow-up time.

INTRODUCTION

Rhinoplasty is among the most popular surgical procedures worldwide because it has functional, aesthetic, and psychosocial implications for patients.1 Given their widely varying appearances, mixed-race noses are characterized by diverse shapes and tissue types, thick skin of varying subtypes, and thin and insufficient cartilage for making grafts. In this regard, surgeons should consider different surgical approaches to address these characteristics.2

Hybrid rhinoplasty combines dorsal preservation and nasal tip shaping with grafts to maximize long-term aesthetic and functional outcomes, especially focusing on nose structures, in patients with mixed-race noses.3

Dorsal preservation rhinoplasty produces good overall outcomes by maintaining the dorsal aesthetic lines and improving functional outcomes. Notably, impaction rhinoplasty, which is performed on humps larger than 2 mm using the push down, let down, spare roof A, or spare roof B techniques, combined with high, medium, or low strips in the middle third of the nose, offers good outcomes.4

In this study, we describe a new technique known as the low block technique that was developed in an attempt to mitigate the challenges associated with one of the most frequently performed techniques during the last 10 years. It focuses on nasal tip shaping and prioritizing quadrangular cartilage sparing by preserving the dorsum, avoiding the use of grafts to reconstruct the keystone area (K area), and using them mainly for strong and symmetrical nasal tip shaping, starting from the nasal spine.3

Furthermore, we assessed the efficacy of the low block technique as an effective and reproducible management alternative in mixed-race noses for ensuring dorsal hump reduction and nasal tip stability, projection, and rotation by measuring nasal parameters using a nasal projectometer for a period of 6 months after the procedure.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

We included 46 patients who underwent surgery using the low block technique in a private center in Trujillo, Peru, between August 2023 and January 2024. All interventions were performed by the lead author (R.A.).

The inclusion criteria were patients aged 18–50 years who underwent primary rhinoplasties and whose nasal characteristics met the mixed-race nose criteria proposed by Ortiz-Monasterio and Olmedo2 (thick skin and weak or insufficient cartilage). We also included patients with a dorsal hump greater than 2 mm, deviated septum, nasal tip ptosis, and nasal tip hypoprojection. Patients with poorly controlled comorbidities, a Goldman cardiac risk index greater than class II, irregular osseocartilaginous dorsum, and dorsal aesthetic lines exceeding 12 mm in width, as well as cases of dorsal augmentation rhinoplasties, were excluded.

Using a projectometer, nasal parameters were measured before surgery in the immediate postoperative period, as well as 1, 3, and 6 months postoperatively during follow-up. The clinical information and anatomical measurements were recorded on a Microsoft Excel version 16.86 database using a Microsoft 365 license. All information was coded using random and coded numbers. The statistical analyses were performed using Statistical Package for the Social Sciences version 29.0. Simple frequencies were used for demographic data, and nonparametric Friedman tests were used for measurements and follow-up data. Statistical significance was set at a P value of less than 0.05.

All patients provided informed consent for the use of their audiovisual material and for the use of their clinical information. This study was performed in accordance with the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Surgical Technique

Anesthesia

Under sedation, nasal infiltration was performed using a solution comprising 0.9% sodium chloride (15 mL), 2% lidocaine (20 mL), 1 mg of adrenaline (half ampoule), and 1 g of tranexamic acid (half ampoule) in the nerve blocking points and peripheral to the nasal pyramid and nasal base, and then extended to the septum to Cottle areas 1 and 2. No infiltration was performed on the surface of the entire length of the nasal dorsum.

Approach

The approach differed depending on the type of nasal shape and the objectives of the surgery, and was grouped into 3 categories: type I, types IIa and IIb, and type III (Table 1).

Indications for Low Block Approach Types

The type I approach was indicated for noses with thin skin, sufficient septal cartilage, irregular dorsal aesthetic lines, and an S-shaped nasal bone. An open technique with a columellar skin incision and wide nasal flap dissection to perform osteotomies and dorsal hump surgery under direct vision was used in this group.5

Types IIa and IIb were indicated for noses with thick or thin skin, insufficient or thin septal cartilage, irregular dorsal aesthetic lines, and an S-shaped nasal bone. Type IIa comprised an open transcolumellar approach with Pitanguy ligament preservation, whereas type IIb comprised a closed approach with wide or limited dorsal dissection, always with Pitanguy ligament preservation.

Type III was indicated for noses with thick or thin skin, insufficient or thin septal cartilage, adequate dorsal aesthetic lines, and a V-shaped nasal bone. The procedure involved a closed approach with very limited dissection of the skin and soft tissues. The lateral walls of the nose were detached without detaching the entire nasal dorsum or the Pitanguy ligament (Fig. 1). DISCUSSION

When implementing new techniques in regular rhinoplasties, we observed that most cases require more cartilage grafts to obtain high-quality outcomes. However, over time (>5 years), we observed some obvious imperfections in our patients over the long term (mainly irregularities in the dorsum). Therefore, we explored preservation techniques to provide more consistent results. However, preservation maneuvers such as the use of a “strut” are insufficient in most of our patients with mixed-race noses. This is why we proposed hybrid rhinoplasty4 as the ideal technique and, based on our experience, recommended the use of the low block technique. Many techniques combine dorsal preservation with nasal tip shaping. However, in the low block technique used in this study, we were able to combine 3 of the most versatile techniques, namely, the spare roof B method6 for dorsal preservation, simplified preservation of quick rhinoplasty (SPQR),7,8 and DCSR for nasal tip shaping. This adds the benefit of the use of fewer grafts, which helps us to avoid the use of costal cartilage in most cases.

We described a novel technique developed as a result of a search toward improving the shortcomings of the majority of techniques we have used during the last 10 years, focusing on nasal tip shaping. The low block technique prioritizes quadrangular cartilage sparing by preserving the dorsum, thereby avoiding grafts to reconstruct the K area and instead using these grafts for a strong and symmetrical nasal tip shaping, starting from the nasal spine itself. The use of a technique that fully preserves the articulation of the triangular cartilages with the K area and the cartilaginous septum itself demonstrated safety in terms of function and shape, as observed in the SPQR dorsal preservation associated with a low strip.7–10 The name “low block” follows the same philosophy of a low strip technique with anteroinferior rotation of the septal flap. In the low block technique, instead of removing a low strip, we removed most of the quadrangular cartilage en bloc, leaving only a 10-mm septal remnant natively articulated to the triangular cartilages.11 This low block is complete and intact; therefore, grafts can be created. By harvesting most of the quadrangular cartilage, we were forced to perform a caudal septum replacement, which, based on our experience, was performed with 2 graft sheets, wrapping the septal remnant to generate greater stability.12

Resection of a large portion of the quadrangular cartilage allowed us to replace the caudal region with a double graft. That is, instead of leaving an “L”-shaped framework, as in most techniques, we changed to another equally stable format. This implies that there should be no instability of the septal support, much less of the internal nasal valves, because the latter undergo no surgical alteration. We have been performing the low block technique in most cases since August 2023, and we have not observed any significant complications regarding the stability of the nasal dorsum or tip. Regarding functionality, we have observed that patients have better long-term (>12 mo postoperatively) evolution than with other dorsal structuring techniques.

In contemporary rhinoplasty, particularly within techniques such as the low block approach, surgical objectives do not involve the creation of extensively resected empty spaces. Instead, the focus is on a meticulous restructuring of the nasal architecture. The resulting configuration of the nasal tip after a low block procedure is directly comparable to that achieved with the SPQR method, pioneered by Dr. Valerio Finocchi, albeit with the distinction of an extended caudal region. This methodological similarity underscores that, rather than leaving significant resected voids, the procedure inherently promotes the continuous reorganization and adaptation of the treated tissues. Furthermore, most structuring techniques leave an “L”-shaped framework to maintain nasal stability and functional support. In our technique, we leave a similar framework without opening the dorsal surface of the nose across its entire length. This provides great stability and, importantly, does not alter the angles of the internal nasal valves; it may even open them to some extent; consequently, respiratory function is maintained without alterations.

Regarding hump reduction, we performed surface surgery on humps smaller than 2 mm and impaction surgery on humps larger than 2 mm, using the spare roof B technique6 in almost all patients.

The major advantage is the conservation of cartilage grafts, which avoids reconstruction of the internal nasal valves during the double septo-caudal replacement, because we preserve the nasal valves and reinforce the nasal tip structures.

Our follow-up results showed that nasal tip projection and stability were similar 6 months after surgery, thereby demonstrating the efficacy of the technique in maintaining these aesthetic results over time (Figs. 5, 6). No complications occurred during this follow-up period.

One limitation of our study is the short follow-up time (6 months); therefore, studies including low block techniques with longer follow-up times are required. This would allow for better technique evaluation and the ability to analyze its evolution over time. In our experience, we have observed that patients who underwent this technique had favorable results. Other limitations of the study is the lack of an assessment of respiratory function, which is an important factor that can complement the aesthetic results obtained. Studies with this type of assessment are needed in future research.

The current study establishes foundational observations; however, its defined evaluation window is recognized as insufficient for a robust objective assessment of both final aesthetic outcomes and prolonged postoperative functionality. To expand on these findings, a systematic functional assessment will be undertaken at 12 months postoperatively, using validated questionnaires including the Nasal Obstruction Symptom Evaluation scale and the visual analog scale. This extended follow-up period will serve as a key objective for forthcoming research.

The low block technique is a versatile dorsal preservation and nasal tip shaping technique, providing cephalocaudal firmness from the radix to the nasal spine. This confers great advantages for the correction of all types of nasal septum deviations and nostril symmetrization by centralizing everything to the nasal axis.3

CONCLUSIONS

The low block technique is effective in dorsal preservation and nasal tip shaping, as demonstrated through measurements performed 6 months after surgery. The technique is also safe, with no complications observed at the end of the follow-up period.
