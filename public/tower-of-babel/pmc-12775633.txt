Resection of bilateral dumbbell C2 ganglioneuroma
Authors: Nicholas Dietz, Aashka Sheth, Thomas Altstadt
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Ganglioneuroma is a rare pathology of neural crest lineage that typically presents outside the spine, with common symptoms including weakness, gait disorder, and back pain. This condition is frequently associated with neurofibromatosis 1 and typically presents in younger female patients with a mean age of 40 years. We report a video resection of bilateral C2 “dumbbell” ganglioneuroma in an older male patient with symptomatic cervical myelopathy. The patient is an 83-year-old male with several months of progressive symptoms of cervical myelopathy including upper extremity weakness and bladder incontinence. His imaging was remarkable for bilateral C2 nerve sheath lesions with canal stenosis and significant cord compression bilaterally, resulting in cord edema and myelomalacia changes. To our knowledge, a case of bilateral cervical ganglioneuroma with myelopathic symptoms in a patient aged 80 years has not been previously reported in the literature. Older patients with myelopathic symptoms must be managed urgently to prevent irreversible spinal cord injury. Our patient was successfully managed with complete surgical excision of the tumors, and resolution of symptoms was seen at follow-up, representing a successful treatment paradigm for similar cases.

What is conventional and what is novel/modified?

• C2 ganglioneuroma has been previously reported, predominantly in younger females. Conventionally, unilateral ganglioneuromas are managed via total surgical excision if possible.

• Total surgical resection resulted in symptom resolution with no postoperative complications seen in our patient. Introduction

Ganglioneuroma is a rare benign tumor derived from neural crest lineage (1) with only 10% found in the spine, typically presenting in younger female patients with a mean age of 40 years (2,3). Most common symptoms include weakness of extremities, gait disorder, and lower back pain with radiculopathy (1). Total surgical excision is recommended to reduce the recurrence risk (4). If total excision is not possible due to size or location, partial resection is an acceptable management option. In cases of partial resection, further chemotherapy or radiation is usually not necessary (4). Most ganglioneuroma presentations are solitary and unilateral, but may rarely occur bilaterally, resulting in spinal cord compression (5). We present an unusual case of bilateral C2 ganglioneuroma in a male octogenarian with severe cervical myelopathy. Given the evidence of cervical myelopathy and high risk of future progression to spinal cord injury, the patient was managed operatively via tumor excision with the intent of full symptom resolution. We present this article in accordance with the SUPER reporting checklist (available at https://jss.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jss-25-147/rc).

Preoperative preparation and requirements

This procedure was performed in a surgical suite at an academic hospital. The surgical team consisted of an attending neurosurgeon, a resident neurosurgeon, and a surgical technician. Special instruments used included Gardner-Wells tongs, Jackson table, Bovie electrosurgical cautery, Kerrison punches, and Kerrison curettes.

The patient is an 83-year-old male who presented with 6 months of progressive intermittent neck pain and bilateral upper extremity numbness in the hands. He endorses difficulty buttoning his shirt and tying his shoes, bladder urgency, and gait instability. He takes hydrocodone for pain as needed and warfarin for history of atrial fibrillation. On physical exam, biceps strength is 4+/5 bilaterally, triceps strength is 4+/5 on left, and grip strength is 4+/5 bilaterally. Intrinsic hand strength is 4/5 bilaterally. He has full strength in his lower extremities. Bilateral Hoffman’s reflexes are present. Gait is wide-based, and the patient is unable to perform tandem steps. Preoperative axial magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) demonstrates two distinct non-enhancing lesions at C1–C2 level on both sides of the spinal cord, with significant mass effect (Figure 1). Both lesions appeared intradural, involving the nerve roots.

Prior to the operation, a Foley catheter was placed and the patient was given prophylactic antibiotics. Intravenous and general endotracheal anesthesia were given, and electrodes were placed for neurophysiologic monitoring. Baseline motor and somatosensory evoked potentials were obtained. The posterior occipital cervical region was prepped and draped in usual sterile fashion.

All procedures performed in this study were in accordance with the ethical standards of the institutional and/or national research committee(s) and with the Helsinki Declaration and its subsequent amendments. Written informed consent was obtained from the patient for publication of this article, accompanying images, and video. A copy of the written consent is available for review by the editorial office of this journal.

Step-by-step description

Intraoperatively, a midline occipital cervical incision were planned, and local marcaine and epinephrine was injected (Video 1). The incision was created and widened with Bovie electrocautery. The occiput, C1, C2, and C3 spinous processes and laminae were dissected, and bipolar cautery and scissors were used to widen the dissection. Next, the sulcus arteriosus and vertebral arteries were identified bilaterally to ensure that they would remain intact during the dissection. The tumors between C2 and C3 on either side were identified. The C1 lamina was undermined and excised with Kerrison punches and curettes and the superior portion of the C2 spinous processes lamina was removed and the bilateral nerve sheath tumors were identified. The operative microscope was brought in, and the tumor capsule was incised on the left side, using an 11-blade and microsection techniques. Bleeding from the epidural veins was controlled with bipolar cautery and FloSeal. Approximately 4 cm of tumor was excised and the attachment of the tumor to the nerve root was identified, coagulated, and divided. A section of the tumor was sent to pathology. At this point, motor and somatosensory potentials demonstrated significant improvement. The intussuscepted dural sleeve was then identified after being released with tumor removal. This was excised distally with bipolar cautery and scissors. Hemostasis was obtained with bipolar cautery and FloSeal. This process was then repeated on the right side.

Case presentation and surgical resection of bilateral C2 “dumbbell” ganglioneuroma.

After tumor removal was complete bilaterally, the wounds were irrigated with vancomycin solution and drains were placed. The wounds were then closed with Vicryl and nylon. Somatosensory evoked potentials and motor evoked potentials were monitored throughout the procedure and demonstrated significant improvement from baseline after the operation was completed. Operative time was 3.5 hours. There were no intraoperative complications and blood loss was estimated to be 150 mL.

Postoperative considerations and tasks

After the operation, a Jackson-Pratt drain was placed and drain output was monitored. Histopathologic examination of the tumor showed mature ganglion cells in a neuromatous stroma, confirming the diagnosis of ganglioneuroma (Figure 2). Large, scattered polyhedral ganglion cells with abundant cytoplasm, large nuclei, and fibrillary stroma were seen. The patient was placed on deep venous thrombosis prophylaxis, appropriate pain regimen, and referred to physical/occupational therapy. The patient was monitored for postoperative complications. Complications depend on the location of the tumor, but common complications reported in the literature include Horner’s syndrome, chylothorax and pneumothorax (5,6). Postoperative imaging showed resolution of spinal cord stenosis and no residual tumor was seen (Figure 3). At one-month follow-up in the clinic, the patient had improvement in fine motor skills, gait, and urinary hesitancy. Hoffman’s sign was negative bilaterally, demonstrating that the operation was successful with symptom resolution.

Tips and pearls

As ganglioneuromas usually present unilaterally, this case highlights how bilateral resection may be technically pursued. Additionally, few cases exist that discuss surgical excision of bilateral ganglioneuroma in the cervical region. This surgical report can guide management for similar cases and increase awareness of this rare pathology. Although preoperative diagnosis is challenging, MRI can serve as a helpful diagnostic tool, as ganglioneuromas have distinct features: defined boundaries, encapsulation, and heterogeneous contrast enhancement (7). “Dumbbell” refers to the hourglass shape of the tumor as it invades into the intervertebral foramina, becoming both intradural and extradural (8). This dumbbell sign can be seen in our preoperative images (Figure 1). Additionally, it is important to note common physical exam findings present in patients with ganglioneuroma. Ganglioneuromas frequently result in myelopathy and present with upper motor neuron signs on physical examination. Therefore, positive Hoffman’s sign, Babinski reflex, or clonus can provide diagnostic aid.

Discussion

This case of bilateral cervical ganglioneuroma in an older male represents a unique presentation of ganglioneuroma, as ganglioneuroma typically presents unilaterally in younger females. Ganglioneuromas commonly present in the posterior mediastinum, adrenal gland, retroperitoneal space, and neck (7). They generally arise from ganglion cells of the sympathetic chain but have also been reported to form from sympathetic nerves and peripheral nerves. There is also an association with ganglioneuroma formation and neurofibromatosis (NF) type 1 (9).

Multiple cases have been reported with bilateral dumbbell cervical ganglioneuroma in NF1 patients under the age of 50 years (9-11). After a thorough review of the current literature through PubMed, we determined that there were 9 previous reports of bilateral symmetric cervical ganglioneuroma, highlighting the rarity of this presentation (8-10,12-16). Seven out of these previous 9 reports were in patients with NF1, demonstrating this close association (8,10-15). The average age of these previous cases was 37 years, consistent with the typical age of presentation for unilateral ganglioneuroma. However, our patient was 83 years old with no symptoms or previous diagnosis of NF1, differing from the other reporting presentations.

The clinical presentation of ganglioneuroma varies, as most cases can be completely asymptomatic and found incidentally (12). However, ganglioneuroma involving the spine may result in compression and patients may present with symptoms like those seen in this case. Most bilateral ganglioneuroma cases presented with symptoms of progressive myelopathy and muscle weakness. Previous reported cases of patients with bilateral cervical ganglioneuroma had symptoms of upper motor neuron damage, with positive Hoffman’s sign, Babinski, and clonus. When spinal cord compression is present, patients should be managed via surgical decompression promptly as in cases of degenerative cervical myelopathy (11).

Surgical resection is currently the most effective treatment option for ganglioneuroma, and symptomatic improvement is seen postoperatively (11,12). The goal of resection should be to decompress the spinal cord and care should be taken to prevent spinal cord injury. Wang et al. report a case of bilateral cervical ganglioneuroma in a 34-year-old male that was successfully managed by posterior spinal cord decompression and tumor resection (7). As it is challenging to make a definitive diagnosis preoperatively, histopathologic examination of the specimen postoperatively allows for confirmation of ganglioneuroma (11,12). If total surgical resection is not achieved, patients should be further monitored for recurrent symptoms and surveillance imaging (11).

Conclusions

Ganglioneuroma may rarely present bilaterally in the cervical region and cause cervical myelopathy in older patients. This case is unique due to the tumor location and characteristics, as well as the patient’s demographics. To our knowledge, a case of bilateral cervical ganglioneuroma with myelopathic symptoms in the octogenarian population has not been previously reported in the literature. Gross total resection was achieved with resolution of myelopathic symptoms at follow up. Cervical myelopathy in older patients is a progressive pathology that can cause spinal cord injury, prompting surgical excision and neural tissue decompression.
