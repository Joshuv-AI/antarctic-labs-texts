Association of nutritional indices with early clinical remission in patients with moderate-to-severe ulcerative colitis treated with vedolizumab
Authors: Hao Zhang, Yueyuan Wang, Jun Wu, Hongying Wang, Congcong Min, Pin Meng, Hua Liu, Tao Mao, Yonghong Xu, Lixin Jin, Baihui Wang, Xiaonan Han, Ailing Liu, Xueli Ding
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe relationship between nutritional indices and treatment response in ulcerative colitis (UC) remains poorly characterized.ObjectivesTo assess the discriminatory ability of Geriatric Nutritional Risk Index (GNRI), Malnutrition Universal Screening Tool (MUST), and Prognostic Nutritional Index (PNI) in evaluating the clinical efficacy of vedolizumab (VDZ) in patients with moderate-to-severe UC.DesignA retrospective multicenter study.MethodsThis study enrolled moderate-to-severe UC patients receiving VDZ at three centers in northern China from 2021 to 2025. The relationship between baseline nutritional indices and clinical remission, alongside conventional biomarkers, was analyzed. Discriminatory performance was evaluated using receiver operating characteristic curves and area under the curve (AUC) analysis.ResultsA total of 184 patients were included. At week 14, multivariable logistic regression identified GNRI, PNI and MUST as factors significantly associated with clinical remission. GNRI showed the strongest correlation with clinical remission (r=0.340, p<0.001), followed by HGB (r=0.316, p<0.001), PNI (r=0.308, p<0.001), PLR (r=-0.271, p<0.001), MUST (r=-0.216, p=0.003), and NLR (r=-0.163, p=0.027). GNRI had the highest AUC (0.697), followed by HGB (0.683), PNI (0.678), Alb (0.660), PLR (0.657) and MUST (0.614). GNRI exhibited significantly greater discriminatory ability for 14-week clinical remission than Alb (p=0.018) and MUST (p=0.041). No significant differences in diagnostic accuracy were found between GNRI, HGB, PNI, PLR, and NLR (p>0.05). At week 30 and 54, multivariate analysis showed that only baseline HGB remained independently associated with remission.ConclusionsPatients with lower nutritional risk—as reflected by higher GNRI and PNI scores, alongside lower MUST scores-were significantly more likely to achieve clinical remission at week 14.Keywords: vedolizumab, clinical remission, ulcerative colitis, Geriatric Nutritional Risk Index, Malnutrition Universal Screening Tool, Prognostic Nutritional Index

Introduction

Ulcerative colitis (UC) is a chronic inflammatory disorder confined to the colon and rectum. Although its precise etiology remains unknown, UC is thought to result from a combination of genetic factors, immune dysfunction, and gut microbiota imbalance. Clinically, it manifests with diarrhea, abdominal pain, and mucopurulent bloody stool. The characteristic relapsing-remitting natural course markedly compromises the health-related quality of life of affected patients. Notably, UC incidence has been rising in recent years. 1 Malnutrition is prevalent among UC patients, with protein-calorie malnutrition affecting 22.2% of individuals with UC. 2 This is primarily due to metabolic disruptions, restrictive diets, and inadequate nutritional intake. 3

Vedolizumab (VDZ), a humanized antibody, inhibits T cell migration to the gut by targeting the α4β7 integrin and preventing its binding to intestinal endothelial receptors. This mechanism inhibits lymphocyte migration to inflamed intestinal tissue, thereby reducing inflammation. 4 VDZ has proven effective in inducing and maintaining remission, as well as promoting mucosal healing in moderate-to-severe UC. 5 The American Gastroenterological Association (AGA) recommends VDZ for patients with moderate-to-severe UC. 6 Meta-analyses report clinical remission rates of approximately 40% during induction and 45% during maintenance, with mucosal healing observed in 45% of patients. 7 However, a significant portion of patients experience suboptimal responses.

As precision medicine plays an increasingly central role in UC management, identifying reliable predictors of therapeutic response is crucial for optimizing treatment strategies. Established predictors of VDZ efficacy encompass both inflammatory and nutritional biomarkers, including C-reactive protein (CRP), serum albumin (Alb), the CRP-to-albumin ratio, hemoglobin (HGB), and fecal calprotectin, as well as clinical parameters such as drug trough levels, prior biologic exposure, and concomitant corticosteroid use.8–14 Few studies, however, have explored the prognostic value of nutritional indices in predicting treatment response. Tools such as the Geriatric Nutritional Risk Index (GNRI), Malnutrition Universal Screening Tool (MUST), and Prognostic Nutritional Index (PNI), which assess serum Alb, lymphocyte count, and body weight, are commonly used to evaluate nutritional status.15–17 GNRI integrates serum Alb and body weight to assess protein and energy reserves; PNI combines Alb levels with lymphocyte count to link nutritional status with immune function; and MUST provides a rapid screening tool for nutritional risk. These indices reflect the malnutrition-inflammation complex in chronic inflammatory diseases like UC, potentially serving as predictors of therapeutic response.15–17 Sobolewska-Włodarczyk et al. reported that NRI, MUST, and Controlling Nutritional Status (CONUT) score may serve as predictors of clinical remission at week 14 in patients with UC receiving VDZ therapy. 15 However, their study was carried out on a small cohort of UC patients (n=32). Additionally, no studies have explored the discriminatory value of nutritional indices for VDZ efficacy at weeks 30 and 54. Building on our prior investigation into VDZ efficacy, 18 the present study was designed to evaluate GNRI, MUST and PNI as factors associated with VDZ treatment response in patients with moderate-to-severe UC at weeks 14, 30, and 54, and to benchmark their discriminatory performance against conventional laboratory biomarkers. The findings could inform clinical decision-making and enhance individualized treatment approaches.

Methods

Study design and participants

This retrospective, multicenter study consecutively enrolled patients diagnosed with UC who received VDZ treatment at three medical institutions in northern China between March 2021 and March 2025: the Department of Gastroenterology, Affiliated Hospital of Qingdao University; Qilu Hospital of Shandong University; and Rizhao People’s Hospital. Eligibility criteria included: (i) a confirmed UC diagnosis based on clinical, endoscopic, radiologic, and histologic evaluations 19 ; (ii) age over 18 at first infusion, and (iii) completion of three or more infusions with one or more follow-up visits; (iv) moderate-to-severe disease (total Mayo score≥6). Exclusion criteria were: (i) severe cardiovascular or cerebrovascular disease, cirrhosis, or malignancy; (ii) major surgery or trauma within the previous three months; or (iii) perioperative status.

The treatment regimen consisted of 300 mg VDZ dose as standard induction treatment at weeks 0, 2, and 6, then 300 mg dose every 8 weeks for maintenance. In cases of loss of response (LOR), dosing was adjusted to either every 4 weeks (Q4W) or every 6 weeks (Q6W).

The primary endpoint was clinical remission at week 14, defined as partial Mayo score (PMS) ≤2, with no individual subscore>1. Secondary endpoints included clinical remission at weeks 30 and 54, and endoscopic remission, defined as a Mayo endoscopic subscore of ≤1 at 30 ± 8 weeks.

The reporting of this study conforms to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) statement. 20 The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Date collection

Patient characteristics, including demographics, disease extent and duration, body mass index (BMI), and baseline total Mayo score and Mayo endoscopy subscore (MES), were recorded. Previous use of corticosteroids and biologics was also noted. Lab values—erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR), platelet count (PLT), lymphocyte and neutrophil counts, white blood cell count (WBC), HGB, Alb, and CRP were documented. The CRP/Alb ratios, neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), and platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio (PLR) were calculated from the respective absolute values of neutrophils, PLT, CRP, lymphocytes, and Alb.

Clinical and endoscopic assessments were conducted at weeks 14, 30, and 54. Disease activity was evaluated at each time point. Data were manually extracted from electronic medical records (EMRs) by two trained reviewers, with verification for accuracy. Discrepancies were resolved through consensus and cross-checked against source records.

The GNRI was computed using the formula: GNRI=(14.89×serum albumin [g/dL]) + (41.7×[actual weight/ideal weight]), with ideal weight based on height and a reference BMI of 22 kg/m2. If actual weight exceeded ideal weight, the weight ratio was set to 1.17,21

The MUST was applied to assess nutritional risk across three factors: BMI (scored as 0 for >20 kg/m2, 1 for 18.5–20 kg/m2, and 2 for <18.5 kg/m2), involuntary weight loss during the preceding 3-6 months (scored as 0 for <5%, 1 for 5–10%, and 2 for >10%), and the presence of acute disease with no or minimal nutritional intake for more than five days (scored as 2 if applicable). 22

The PNI was calculated using the formula: PNI=serum albumin (g/L) + 5×total lymphocyte count (109/L). 23

Definitions

UC was classified using the Montreal system into proctitis (E1), left-sided colitis (E2), and extensive colitis (E3). Disease severity was determined by the total Mayo score, with moderate activity defined as a score of 6–10 and severe activity as 11–12. The total Mayo score incorporated the physician’s global assessment, MES, rectal bleeding, and stool frequency. 24 Clinical status was monitored with the PMS, excluding the MES, while endoscopic activity was assessed separately using the MES.

Treatment response to VDZ was evaluated at weeks 14, 30, and 54 using the PMS. Clinical remission was defined as a PMS≤2, with no individual subscore>1. Endoscopic remission was defined as an MES≤1, and was evaluated at weeks 30±8.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed utilizing SPSS 24.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Categorical variables are presented as counts or percentages. Normally distributed variables are shown as mean±standard deviations (SD), and skewed data as median (IQR). Group comparisons used Mann–Whitney U test or t-test for continuous variables, and Chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests for categoricalones. Spearman’s rank correlation was used for assessment of associations with clinical remission. The discriminatory performance of GNRI, PNI, and selected laboratory biomarkers including HGB, PLR, NLR and Alb was assessed using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis. AUCs were calculated and compared pairwise using DeLong’s method. All ROC analyses were performed with MedCalc software. Multivariate logistic regression determined the independent discriminatory ability of nutritional indicators. Variables with p values<0.05 in univariate analysis were included in the multivariable model. Multicollinearity was assessed using variance inflation factors (VIF), with a threshold of VIF<5 deemed acceptable. Statistical significance was set at p<0.05.

Results

Baseline patient characteristics

A total of 184 patients with moderate-to-severe UC were enrolled (Figure 1). At week 14, 99 patients achieved clinical remission, while 85 did not. The remission group had significantly higher baseline GNRI and PNI scores (p<0.001), lower MUST scores (p=0.003), and higher BMI, HGB, and Alb levels (p≤0.001) compared to the non-remission group. In contrast, the remission group had significantly lower PLT, ESR, NLR, and PLR (p<0.05). The remission group also had lower rates of prior steroid and anti-TNF-α use (p=0.008). There were no significant differences in age at VDZ initiation, sex, disease duration or extent, total Mayo score, MES, or baseline values for WBC, CRP, and CRP/Alb ratio (p>0.05) (Table 1).

During follow-up, VDZ was discontinued in 20 patients for reasons including loss of response (n=2), lack of clinical response (n=12), non-remission (n=1), adverse events (n=4), and latent tuberculosis (n=1). Additionally, 10 patients were lost to follow-up, and 15 did not complete the 30-week follow-up. Thus, 139 patients were included in the week 30 analysis, with 90 in remission and 49 in non-remission. At week 30, the remission group showed higher baseline GNRI and PNI scores (p=0.005 and p=0.008, respectively), while MUST scores were similar between groups (p=0.681). HGB and Alb levels remained elevated in the remission group, while total Mayo score, MES, ESR, and the CRP/Alb ratio were lower (p<0.05). A significantly lower rate of prior steroid use was observed in the remission group relative to the non-remission group (p=0.008). There were no significant differences in BMI, sex, disease duration or extent, prior anti-TNF-α use, or baseline WBC, PLT, NLR, and PLR levels (p=0.05) (Supplemental Table 2).

By week 54, VDZ was discontinued in 18 patients due to loss of response (n=14), adverse events (n=3), or switching to adalimumab for spondylitis (n=1). Additionally, 18 patients did not complete follow-up, and 11 were lost to follow-up. Consequently, 92 patients remained: 65 in remission and 27 in non-remission. At week 54, the remission group exhibited a higher baseline PNI score (p=0.043). However, there were no significant differences in baseline GNRI or MUST scores between the groups. Only baseline HGB was significantly higher in the remission group (p<0.05). No significant differences were found in other variables, including sex, age, BMI, disease duration or extent, total Mayo score, MES, prior therapies, or laboratory indices (WBC, PLT, Alb, CRP, ESR, CRP/Alb, NLR, PLR) (p>0.05) (Supplemental Table 3).

Association of nutritional indicators and other biological markers with clinical remission

At week 14, multivariable logistic regression identified GNRI, PNI and MUST as independent factors associated with clinical remission. Additionally, the remission group had lower PLR and NLR ratio, along with higher HGB level, compared to the non-remission group (p<0.05). The remission group also had a higher baseline BMI at the initiation of VDZ therapy (p=0.004) (Supplemental Table 4). Baseline GNRI, PNI, and HGB levels showed a positive association with clinical remission (p<0.001), whereas MUST, PLR and NLR demonstrated a negative association (p<0.05). GNRI exhibited the strongest correlation (r=0.340), followed by HGB (r=0.319), PNI (r=0.308), PLR (r=-0.271), MUST (r=-0.216), and PLR (r=-0.163) (Table 2). By week 30, multivariate analysis indicated no significant differences between the groups in terms of GNRI, PNI and MUST. Only baseline HGB was identified as an independent factor associated with clinical remission (Supplemental Table 5) and remained significantly positively correlated with clinical remission (r=0.379, p<0.01). At week 54, multivariate analysis identified baseline HGB as the sole independent factor associated with clinical remission (odds ratio = 1.041, 95% CI: 1.013–1.069; p=0.004), with a significant positive correlation (r = 0.357, p<0.001).

Association of nutritional indicators and other biological markers with endoscopic remission

At baseline, both GNRI and PNI were significantly and inversely correlated with the MES (p < 0.001), whereas no significant correlation was observed for MUST (r = 0.094, p = 0.210). Among the three indices, PNI demonstrated the strongest inverse correlation with MES (r = -0.209), followed closely by GNRI (r = -0.206).

At weeks 30±8, a total of 88 patients completed the endoscopic assessment. Of these, 43 achieved endoscopic remission, while 51 did not. Compared with the non-remission group, the remission group demonstrated significantly higher baseline GNRI and PNI scores, as well as greater Alb and HGB levels, alongside lower ESR and PLR values. No significant difference was observed in MUST scores (Table 4).

Discussion

Nutritional status exerts a profound impact on clinical outcomes in UC exacerbations, when inflammatory responses drive markedly increased energy expenditure. Previous studies have demonstrated that malnutrition is independently associated with higher disease severity in UC patients. 23 Emerging evidence indicates that poor nutritional status may attenuate the therapeutic efficacy of biologic agents. 15 Malnutrition-induced immune dysfunction may impair lymphocyte trafficking capacity, 25 thereby potentially attenuating the inhibitory effect of VDZ on α4β7 integrin-mediated gut homing. Furthermore, albumin functions as a carrier protein for numerous drugs, and hypoalbuminaemia has been shown to increase VDZ clearance. 26 These mechanisms collectively suggest that nutritional status may have a particularly pronounced influence on VDZ efficacy relative to other biologic agents. The present study demonstrates that the nutritional indices are reliably associated with clinical remission in patients with moderate-to-severe UC receiving VDZ therapy at week 14.

The GNRI, developed by Bouillanne et al. in 2005 from the original Nutritional Risk Index, was initially designed to assess nutritional risk in older adults. 27 However, it is now applied to broader populations, including younger individuals. The PNI, derived from serum Alb and lymphocyte counts, was originally developed to evaluate nutritional and immune status in patients undergoing gastrointestinal surgery or with gastrointestinal malignancies.28,29 The MUST, developed by the British Association for Parenteral and Enteral Nutrition (BAPEN) in 2003, evaluates malnutrition risk based on BMI, recent unintentional weight loss, and the impact of acute illness. Due to its simplicity and reliability, MUST is commonly employed to evaluate nutritional status in outpatient populations with IBD. 30 Chen et al. found that lower GNRI and PNI scores were associated with greater disease severity in IBD patients. 22 Sobolewska-Włodarczyk et al. previously demonstrated that lower baseline MUST scores were associated with higher rates of clinical remission at week 14 in VDZ-treated patients. The present findings are in close agreement with this prior evidence, providing independent corroboration in a distinct patient cohort. In our study, multivariate logistic regression analysis demonstrated that GNRI, PNI and MUST were independent factors associated with clinical remission at week 14 in UC patients treated with VDZ. However, none of them was significantly associated with clinical remission at weeks 30 or 54, nor endoscopic remission at week 30±8. Collectively, these findings suggest that nutritional indices may be more informative as factors associated with early clinical remission than with long-term outcomes. The time-dependent attenuation of predictive value implies that nutritional status primarily modulates the early phase of treatment response, while sustained outcomes are more likely governed by factors such as disease phenotype and therapeutic drug monitoring.31,32

Yildirim O et al. have reported that nutritional indices such as PNI and CONUT can predict endoscopic disease activity in UC, 33 and Zhang M et al. demonstrated a significant inverse correlation between PNI and the MES in this patient population. 34 The present findings are consistent with these observations: both PNI and GNRI were significantly and inversely correlated with baseline MES, whereas MUST was not. Nevertheless, none of the nutritional indices was independently associated with endoscopic remission at week 30 ± 8. This dissociation may be partially explained by the fact that Alb and lymphocyte counts — the constituent components of PNI and GNRI — reflect the systemic, inflammation-driven nutritional depletion, whereas the MES captures local intestinal mucosal inflammation. 35 The two are linked through a shared inflammatory pathway but are not equivalent. Beyond nutritional status, endoscopic remission following VDZ is determined by a multitude of factors,14,36 and future studies incorporating a more comprehensive multivariable framework are warranted.

Among the indices examined, GNRI exhibited the strongest association with clinical remission at week 14 and achieved the highest overall discriminatory ability. Its superiority over Alb alone was statistically significant, affirming the added value of incorporating body weight into a composite nutritional screening tool. Nevertheless, its discriminatory capacity remained modest, and its discriminatory performance was not significantly superior to that of simpler laboratory markers, including HGB, PNI, PLR and NLR. These findings suggest that GNRI should not be employed as a standalone predictive factor; rather, its optimal utility lies within a broader, multiparametric clinical assessment framework.

HGB, a constituent component of the Truelove-Witts severity criteria, is a well-established indicator of UC disease activity. 19 Baseline HGB emerged as a consistent independent factor associated with clinical remission at both weeks 30 and 54, underscoring its sustained prognostic value throughout the treatment course. Anemia in UC is commonly caused by systemic inflammation, impaired erythropoiesis, shortened red blood cell lifespan, and ongoing gastrointestinal bleeding.37–39

Baseline PLR was additionally identified as a significant factor associated with endoscopic remission at 30±8 weeks. The result is similar to that of Bertani L et al., which has demonstrated that PLR may predict endoscopic remission following anti-TNF-α therapy. 40 Baseline PLR, reflecting inflammatory-immune imbalance, is closely associated with severe intestinal mucosal inflammation. NLR remained an independent risk factor in multivariate analysis, but had an AUC<0.6 and significant discriminatory ability only for 14-week clinical remission. This indicates that inflammatory markers alone have limited discriminatory power for therapeutic response.

The associations of additional biomarkers, including PLT, ESR, serum Alb and the CRP/Alb ratio, with VDZ efficacy were also examined. In this study, PLT and ESR were significantly associated with clinical remission at week 14, while ESR, Alb, and the CRP/Alb ratio correlated with remission at week 30. However, none of these biomarkers was independently associated with clinical remission. The levels of these indicators are affected by multiple factors, limiting their use as standalone indicators of nutritional status or VDZ efficacy. For instance, PLT levels may rise in prothrombotic states. 41 And CRP, an acute-phase reactant, is often influenced by infection, malignancy, or other inflammatory conditions.42,43 Alb levels are also affected by inflammation, as well as hepatic or renal dysfunction. Although the CRP/Alb ratio provides a more integrated measure of systemic inflammation than either marker alone, 44 it lacks disease specificity.

N Plevris et al. reported that patients who were naïve to anti-TNF therapy achieved a higher clinical remission rate at week 12. 45 Similar to previous research, our study also found that prior anti-TNF exposure was associated with reduced VDZ efficacy at week 14. Although prior steroid use was associated with a lower likelihood of achieving clinical remission at weeks 14 and 30, it did not serve as an independent risk factor in the multivariate analysis.

Our study has several potential limitations. First, the retrospective design inherently limits causal inference and is susceptible to unmeasured confounding. Second, the number of patients with available data at week 54 was relatively small, raising the possibility of attrition bias; patients who remained in follow-up to that time point may disproportionately represent those with greater treatment adherence or earlier clinical response, potentially inflating estimates of long-term efficacy. Third, endoscopic assessments were not performed at uniform time points, owing to variability in individual patient willingness to undergo colonoscopy; accordingly, endoscopic outcomes were analyzed within a window of 30 ± 8 weeks rather than at a fixed interval. Finally, the constituent components of GNRI, PNI, and MUST — including serum Alb, body weight, and lymphocyte count — are susceptible to the confounding effects of systemic inflammation and active disease severity. Although multivariable models were adjusted for baseline total Mayo score and key inflammatory markers, residual confounding cannot be entirely excluded. Consequently, these indices may partly reflect inflammatory burden rather than nutritional status per se. Future studies incorporating objective measures of body composition, such as bioelectrical impedance analysis, would be valuable for isolating the independent contribution of malnutrition to treatment response. Notwithstanding these limitations, the present findings offer novel evidence for the discriminatory value of nutritional biomarkers in stratifying VDZ response. Larger prospective multicenter studies are warranted to validate these observations and establish their generalizability across broader UC populations.

Conclusion

To the best of our knowledge, this is the largest multicenter cohort study with the longest follow-up duration to demonstrate that three nutritional indices — GNRI, PNI, and MUST — may serve as factors associated with clinical remission at week 14 in patients with moderate-to-severe UC receiving VDZ therapy. Patients with lower nutritional risk — as reflected by higher GNRI and PNI scores, alongside lower MUST scores — were significantly more likely to achieve favorable early therapeutic outcomes. Notably, baseline GNRI demonstrated the greatest discriminatory capacity for remission at week 14. These findings support prospective investigation into whether pre-treatment optimization of nutritional status can improve outcomes in patients receiving VDZ.
