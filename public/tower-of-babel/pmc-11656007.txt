Realization of high-end PET devices that assist conventional PET devices in improving image quality via diffusion modeling
Authors: Qiyang Zhang, Chao Zhou, Xu Zhang, Wei Fan, Hairong Zheng, Dong Liang, Zhanli Hu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeThis study aimed to implement high-end positron emission tomography (PET) equipment to assist conventional PET equipment in improving image quality via a distribution learning-based diffusion model.MethodsA diffusion model was first trained on a dataset of high-quality (HQ) images acquired by a high-end PET device (uEXPLORER scanner), and the quality of the conventional PET images was later improved on the basis of this trained model built on null-space constraints. Data from 180 patients were used in this study. Among them, 137 patients who underwent total-body PET/computed tomography scans via a uEXPLORER scanner at the Sun Yat-sen University Cancer Center were retrospectively enrolled. The datasets of 50 of these patients were used to train the diffusion model. The remaining 87 cases and 43 PET images acquired from The Cancer Imaging Archive were used to quantitatively and qualitatively evaluate the proposed method. The nonlocal means (NLM) method, UNet and a generative adversarial network (GAN) were used as reference methods.ResultsThe incorporation of HQ imaging priors derived from high-end devices into the diffusion model through network training can enable the sharing of information between scanners, thereby pushing the limits of conventional scanners and improving their imaging quality. The quantitative results showed that the diffusion model based on null-space constraints produced better and more stable results than those of the methods based on NLM, UNet and the GAN and is well suited for cross-center and cross-device imaging.ConclusionA diffusion model based on null-space constraints is a flexible framework that can effectively utilize the prior information provided by high-end scanners to improve the image quality of conventional scanners in cross-center and cross-device scenarios.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40658-024-00706-3.Keywords: PET/CT imaging, Diffusion model, Null space, Cross-center, Cross-device

Introduction

As a core imaging device in the field of nuclear medicine, positron emission tomography/computed tomography (PET/CT) has irreplaceable roles in the diagnosis of tumors and neurological diseases and in drug development [1, 2]; however, owing to the variety of physical degradation factors, the resolution and quality of images obtained from conventional PET devices need to be further improved. New diagnostic theories and new therapeutic programs also need to be supported by high-quality (HQ) PET images [3]. In addition, the need for lower-dose or faster PET imaging methods to reduce the radiation exposure levels faced by patients or increase hospital throughput has resulted in less valid information being obtained during the scanning process, further limiting the ability to obtain HQ PET images from a limited number of counts and making it increasingly difficult for conventional or older equipment to cope with the rigors of imaging [4]. Therefore, advanced technology that can re-enable these conventional devices to push their limits and satisfy these demanding imaging requirements is urgently needed.

Approaches for improving the quality of PET images include traditional iterative techniques, constrained iterative techniques, and artificial intelligence (AI)-based techniques, such as edge-guided methods [5], dictionary learning [6], and deep generalization methods [7]. Among these techniques, AI-based techniques outperform other state-of-the-art methods in terms of both preserving structural information and quantitatively analyzing standardized uptake values (SUVs) and have achieved significant results when performing low-count PET image denoising tasks [8–12] and modal conversion tasks [13–15].

However, all of the above methods are based on the utilized device itself; using technical means in all aspects of a device to limit the extraction of information for improving image quality cannot overcome the attribute limitations of the device, and an image quality ceiling is present [16]. Recently, equipment manufacturers released high-end PET/CT equipment with an axial size of 1.94 m, whose sensitivity is more than 40 times greater than that of conventional axial field-of-view equipment, and compared with conventional PET technology, it can obtain ultra-HQ images under the same dosage and scanning time [17–20]; therefore, if HQ imaging information can be extracted from such high-end equipment to assist conventional equipment in achieving improved image quality, it will provide new application value to conventional equipment. With the rapid development of AI technology, image generation technology based on diffusion modeling has surpassed state-of-the-art generative adversarial networks (GANs) in several aspects [21, 22], including image generation [23–25] and superresolution [26, 27].

Diffusion models [23, 25], including denoising diffusion probabilistic models (DDPMs) and models based on score functions, form a class of distribution learning-based models that attempt to map normal distributions to specific data distributions on the basis of iterative refinement. In the forward pass, noise is gradually injected into the given image. During the backward pass, data samples are generated by removing noise from the image after multiple refinement steps. Specifically, prior information can be added to the model for conditional image generation [28–31]. Diffusion models have provided encouraging benefits in medical imaging tasks such as magnetic resonance (MR) image reconstruction [32, 33] and PET image denoising [30].

Diffusion models can achieve conditional imaging with zero sample training because of their excellent generalization properties [31, 34]. Therefore, it is possible to train a model on a high-end PET device with the help of diffusion modeling, and later, this model can be used to assist a low-end PET device in improving its image quality. The study of the above methods has high clinical value and can revitalize old PET equipment and reduce financial pressure on medical institutions to purchase new equipment. Therefore, we propose the use of a denoising diffusion null-space model (DDNM)[29], a derivative of the DDPM model, for research on cross-device-assisted imaging. The nonlocal means (NLMs) method [35], UNet method [36] and GAN method [37] were used as reference methods. In this study, a total of 180 cases of data derived from different centers and devices were used to evaluate the proposed method. The experimental results yielded strong performance in both quantitative and qualitative assessments, demonstrating the feasibility of the diffusion model in improving the quality of low-end PET images using high-end PET images.

Materials and methods

Datasets

Two datasets were used to conduct experiments to evaluate the performance of the proposed diffusion model in terms of PET image quality enhancement and to further explore the feasibility of enhancing the quality of conventional PET images with the aid of HQ images derived from long-axis PET equipment. Patient characteristics

One of the datasets (Dataset 1) was obtained from the Sun Yat-sen University Cancer Center and included a total of 137 patients. The inclusion criterion was age (between 15 and 90 years), and the exclusion criterion was waiting time after 18F-FDG injection (> 75 min). All the data were collected by a total-body PET/CT uEXPLORER (United Imaging Healthcare, Shanghai, China) scanner with a mean injection dose of 0.11 mCi/kg. Approximately 5 min after 18F-FDG injection, we collected raw data for approximately 5 min. The institutional ethics committee approved this study, and all the subjects provided written informed consent before participating in this study.

Another part of the data (Dataset 2) was obtained from The Cancer Imaging Archive (TCIA)[38–40]. A total of 43 cases were selected, with the following inclusion criteria: age (between 15 and 90 years) and reconstruction via the OSEM algorithm; the exclusion criterion involved images that did not include the abdominal or thoracic cavities.

For Dataset 1, 50 patient datasets were used for training, and 87 datasets were used for testing and validation (see T1 in the supplementary file for details). The normal-dose PET images of 50 patients were used to train the standard DDPM, and the trained model was directly plugged into the null space-constrained diffusion model imaging method proposed in this study. The noisy low-dose PET images of the remaining 87 patients were used to test this imaging method. The low-dose dataset was generated by extracting a portion of events from the normal-dose list-mode dataset, and the PET images reconstructed with 1% of the extracted event data were used as the low-dose data in this experiment. All the PET images were reconstructed via TOF-OSEM with the following parameters: PSF modeling, 3 iterations, 20 subsets, a matrix size of 256 × 256, a slice thickness of 2.89 mm, a voxel size of 2.34 × 2.34 × 2.89 mm3, Gaussian postfiltering (3 mm), and all the necessary correction methods, including scattering and attenuation corrections. Dataset 2, containing data from 43 patients, was only used to test the proposed method and was not used to train DDPM to verify its effect and performance in cross-center and cross-device scenarios. All PET images in the experiments were converted to SUV units, and the size of each image was resampled to a matrix of 128 × 128 pixels so that the deep learning model used in this study was runnable on our computational workstation. Implementation details

The neural network Uθ used to predict the noise at each time step was a UNet structure containing attention and residual blocks, as described in [25]. In addition, to avoid axial artifacts in the coronal and sagittal views, we provide several neighboring axial slices for this UNet as network inputs. The input of the network included multislice data with a size of H × W × S, where H and W denote the height (128) and width (128) dimensions, respectively, and S denotes the depth of successive adjacent multislice data, which prevented axial artifacts in the coronal and sagittal sections. where E denotes the expectation, the number of time steps T was set to 1000, t was chosen uniformly from 1 to T, and βt was set to grow linearly from β1= 0.0001 to βT= 0.02, as in [25].

The diffusion network was trained for 400 epochs over approximately 4 days with a batch size of 15, and the initial learning rate was 1 × 10−4. In the experiments, to speed up the sampling process, the DDIM sampling method is adopted, which employs 100 steps for sampling. The network was constructed via the PyTorch deep learning framework on an Ubuntu 16.04 system with an RTX4090 graphics processing unit (GPU) and was optimized via the adaptive moment estimation (Adam) optimizer with an annealing strategy to accelerate the convergence process.

Reference methods

In the experiments, the NLM method [35], UNet [36], and a conditional GAN [37] were used as reference methods. For the UNet and GAN methods, the training dataset and the number of training parameters were the same as those of the neural network Uθ based on the DDPM method. The search window, patch size and smooth kernel for the NLM method were 11 × 11, 2 × 2 and 1.5, respectively. The three-channel inputs for UNet and the GAN were also the same as those of the DDPM model used in this study. UNet and the GAN were trained equally for 400 epochs under the condition of ensuring that no overfitting occurred.

Imaging analysis

To evaluate the performance of the proposed methods, we conducted an objective quantitative analysis and a subjective qualitative analysis on Dataset 1. For Dataset 2, only a subjective qualitative analysis was used because there was no ground truth.

The objective image quality assessment uses two quantitative metrics: the peak signal-to-noise ratio (PSNR) and the structural similarity index measure (SSIM). In addition, we performed an SUV analysis on the regions of interest (ROIs). All the ROIs were placed on HQ ground-truth images and then transferred to other processed LQ images to ensure that the ROIs were in the same location and had the same size in all the images.

The subjective qualitative PET image quality assessment was independently scored on a 5-point Likert scale by two nuclear radiologists (a senior radiologist with more than 9 years of experience and a radiologist with more than 5 years of experience). The images were anonymized and presented to the experts as random-order volume data. The scores included three different categories: (1) the amount of image noise, (2) the clarity of the main suspected malignant lesion, and (3) the clarity of the organ anatomy. The experts rated the quality of each image on a five-point scale: 1, uninterpretable; 2, poor; 3, adequate; 4, good; and 5, excellent. The average rating produced across all the readers for each image was calculated and then compared. To compare the different methods, we perform Wilcoxon signed rank tests for the PSNR, SSIM, and reading scores. p < 0.05 indicated a statistically significant difference.

Clinical readings

In this study, two experts with sufficient clinical experience subjectively scored the PET images, both real images and those generated by different methods, of a total of 130 patients from the test set in Dataset 1 and Dataset 2, as shown in Table 2. For the PET images generated in Dataset 1 via the method proposed in this paper, the mean subjective quantitative scores of the two nuclear medicine experts were (1) 3.5 in terms of image noise, (2) 4.2 in terms of lesion clarity, and (3) 3.2 in terms of organ anatomy clarity. For Dataset 2, the mean subjective quantitative scores were (1) 3.6 in terms of image noise, (2) 4.2 in terms of lesion clarity, and (3) 3.6 in terms of organ anatomy clarity. Statistically significant differences were observed between the proposed method and the other methods with respect to all three subjective evaluation aspects on both datasets (p < 0.05).

Discussion

This study shows that image quality improvement can be achieved with the aid of a diffusion model, enabling high-end devices to assist conventional devices. Null-space constraints can effectively suppress image noise and restore tissue structure details. Compared with other methods, the proposed method performs well across various modalities and centers and can effectively suppress the outliers and artifacts caused by the insufficient generalization that occurs in supervised learning methods.

Dataset 1, acquired on a uEXPLORER device with an axial distance of 1.94 m and used for training, can cover the whole body in one scan with a 40-fold increase in sensitivity compared with that of conventional PET scanning devices. Therefore, HQ PET images of the whole body can be simultaneously acquired without interruption; thus, a diffusion model trained on this dataset can learn excellent prior information. Zero-shot PET imaging across different centers and devices can be achieved via the method proposed in this paper.

Because Dataset 2 was obtained from the TCIA and no original list-mode data existed, no ground truth was available as a reference image for quantitative analysis purposes, and only a qualitative analysis (in terms of visualization) was performed in this study. The images were visually anonymized and subjectively assessed by professional nuclear medicine physicians and experts. The UNet and GAN networks trained on Dataset 1 showed deficiencies in generalizability when addressing unseen Dataset 2, for which UNet incorrectly degraded the organizational structure (Markers ② and ④ in Fig. 6), and the GAN incorrectly generated high-uptake regions (Markers ① and ③ in Fig. 6). This suggests that the adoption of UNet and GAN with supervised learning, similar to the approach used in this paper, is prone to producing fake lesions when working with unfamiliar data. Therefore, they are currently not suitable for cross-center, cross-device imaging.

In terms of the subjective evaluation of the proposed method, the average score for Dataset 2 obtained from the TCIA was higher than that of Dataset 1 in all evaluation aspects because the quality of the TCIA dataset is better than that of the LQ images that we simulated in Dataset 1, in which we only extracted 1% of the list-mode data for the reconstruction; thus, the image noise level was very high. The TCIA data come from an actual clinic, so although their quality is slightly worse, they can satisfy the basic requirements of diagnosis.

For the comparison methods in this work, UNet and the conditional GAN, we did not make any special design for the loss function or convolution module but made only a small modification; that is, we adjusted the input channel to be the same as that of the adopted methods. The UNet and conditional GAN are both data-driven networks that require pairs of image data, and in all the experiments in this paper, the results of these two methods perform smoothly because the amount of data used in training is not sufficient and the proportion of header data is small, which leads to poor generalization ability of the model and insufficient detail recovery.

Diffusion model-based posterior sampling methods have produced state-of-the-art imaging results in image enhancement tasks, and many such sampling methods have been proposed. Among them, the DPS method is a typical method that is capable of addressing the Poisson noise model, which has great potential for handling PET imaging scenarios[31]. In the supplementary file, we present a preliminary comparison test conducted on the DPS method (see Ext. Results in the supplementary file for details), and the results show that the PET images obtained via DPS sampling have comparable performance to that of the method proposed in this paper.

This study has several limitations. First, few commercial scanners with high-sensitivity PET capabilities are available for ultralong axial fields of view, which is inconvenient for large-scale model training. The cross-center and cross-device aspects of the work performed in this paper still have much research potential, and the support of device manufacturers is needed to obtain raw list-mode data for conducting more in-depth studies and to validate the method on more conventional devices.

Conclusion

In this study, we incorporated a null space-based diffusion model with PET imaging techniques and trained a diffusion model on an HQ PET image set to improve the quality of LQ PET images. The experimental results show that the proposed method outperforms the other tested methods in terms of quantitative analysis, visual quality comparison and subjective evaluation. It also achieves cross-center and cross-device imaging. The use of the proposed method can significantly reduce equipment costs, re-enable old and routine equipment to improve their imaging quality, and benefit more patients. In our future work, we aim to collect more HQ images, train even better models, and apply the method in clinical practice for cross-center applications to assist radiologists in diagnosing patients.
