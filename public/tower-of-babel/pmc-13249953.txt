Bundibugyo virus—perspectives for the current outbreak
Authors: Thomas Hoenen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
In May 2026 an outbreak of Bundibugyo virus, a close relative of Ebola virus, was detected in the Democratic Republic of the Congo and Uganda. Rapidly increasing case numbers together with the regional circumstances and current lack of licensed countermeasures available against this virus make this outbreak of great concern for public health in the affected region. A quick and decisive response will be required to control this outbreak.

Bundibugyo virus

In May 2026, the World Health Organization (WHO) was alerted to an outbreak of Bundibugyo virus (BDBV) centered in the Ituri province in the north eastern region of the Democratic Republic of the Congo (DRC) (Fig. 1)1. Cases have also been reported in the nearby North Kivu and South Kivu provinces, and several imported cases with limited local transmission in Uganda. On May 17th 2026 the WHO declared this outbreak a public health emergency of international concern, based on the already high suspected case numbers, the likelihood that the outbreak was even larger than recognized at the time, and a significant risk for further local and regional spread due to the high population density in the region, the involvement of urban areas, and significant population movement within the affected area2,3.Fig. 1Provinces in DRC affected by the BDBV outbreak.Provinces with confirmed BDBV cases are highlighted in orange. BDBV belongs to the same genus Orthoebolavirus as Ebola virus (EBOV), although they are classified into different species4. Together with the orthomarburgviruses, the cuevaviruses, which are the only filoviruses found in Europe, and the dianloviruses, as well as several genera so far found exclusively in reptiles and fish, orthoebolaviruses constitute the family of filoviruses, which are named after the distinct filamentous shape of their virus particles. From a molecular point of view BDBV and EBOV are very similar, and it is likely that much of our knowledge about the biology of EBOV and other orthoebolaviruses can also be directly applied to BDBV.

In nature, orthoebolaviruses most likely use bats as natural reservoir (reviewed in ref. 5), with rare spillover events into humans occurring either directly or via intermediate hosts such as non-human primates. Importantly, while human infection with many orthoebolaviruses causes a severe hemorrhagic fever called Ebola disease (reviewed in ref. 6), apathogenic orthoebolaviruses such as Reston virus, and likely Bombali virus, also exist7,8. The mechanisms responsible for these differences in pathogenic potential remain poorly understood, but appear to reflect underlying differences in the efficiency of basic viral processes9. After an incubation time, which in the case of BDBV averages 6 to 7 days (but in some cases can last up to 21 days)10, infection with pathogenic filoviruses initially produces nonspecific symptoms such as high fever, headaches, and fatigue. The disease later progresses to include gastrointestinal symptoms such as vomiting and diarrhea with very high-volume fluid losses. Hemorrhagic symptoms can also occur, although they do not manifest in all patients, and these include hematemesis, bloody stool, petechiae, and bleeding from venipuncture sites. Human-to-human transmission of pathogenic orthoebolaviruses requires close contact with ill or deceased patients or their body fluids (reviewed in ref. 11). Survivors often show prolonged sequelae, including arthralgia, ocular complications such as eye pain, blurry vision, and photophobia, as well as neurological complications12. For EBOV, it has been shown that the virus can persist in immunoprivileged sites such as the testes for several months13, thereby facilitating sexual transmission from recovered patients, which then represents a potential source of disease recurrence within communities without acute cases14.

However, despite many similarities, there are also differences between EBOV and BDBV. In contrast to EBOV, which has caused 25 documented outbreaks with 33,884 cases to date, there have so far only been two previously recognized BDBV outbreaks totaling 169 cases15. Further, based on the available data, BDBV likely exhibits a lower virulence than EBOV, although the comparison of case fatality rates in different outbreaks is complicated by differences in the extent of diagnostics, case numbers, and the improved standard of care achieved during the last decade. The impact of these factors was highly evident during the West African EBOV epidemic, which was by far the largest EBOV outbreak to date with 28,646 cases, and during which unprecedented diagnostic efforts were undertaken, resulting in the identification of mild cases that likely would have remained undetected in early outbreaks. This resulted in a case fatality rate (CFR) of 40%, whereas other EBOV outbreaks prior to 2014 had shown a much higher average CFR of 75%. In contrast, the average CFR for BDBV outbreaks has so far been reported to be 33%15.

Available countermeasure candidates

The large number of cases during the West African epidemic from 2013 to 2016 and the subsequent epidemic in DRC from 2018 to 2020 allowed clinical trials for the evaluation of several vaccines and therapeutics against Ebola disease caused by EBOV. This led to the licensure of several medical countermeasures, including a vector vaccine based on a recombinant vesicular stomatitis virus (VSV) expressing the EBOV glycoprotein GP (Ervebo)16, which has since been used for targeted vaccination of high-risk populations (e. g. healthcare workers), as well as for ring vaccination of contacts of confirmed or suspected cases during outbreaks17. As such, it represents an invaluable tool for outbreak control by interrupting transmission chains. Further, two monoclonal antibody therapies directed against EBOV GP, i.e., Inmazeb (previously REGNEB3) and Ebanga (Ansuvimab, previously mAb114), have also been licensed, after being shown to significantly lower mortality among infected patients18,19.

Unfortunately, these countermeasures are specific to EBOV, and there are currently no equivalent therapeutics or vaccines available for BDBV. However, there are several countermeasure candidates in development that have shown promise against BDBV in preclinical trials. In particular, Remdesivir (GS-5734), a nucleoside analog initially developed against EBOV20, has shown activity against a wide range of other filoviruses21,22, and also exhibits a high degree of inhibitory activity against BDBV viral RNA synthesis23. Given that Remdesivir has also shown efficacy against several filoviruses in non-human primates20,22, which represent the most difficult to protect animal model of Ebola disease, as well as previous experience using Remdesivir in clinical trials to treat EBOV infection19, it is highly likely that this drug will also show clinical benefit against Ebola disease caused by BDBV. Importantly, while Remdesivir has to be administered intravenously, an oral prodrug of Remdesivir, Obeldesivir, has recently been developed and has also shown promise in preclinical trials against several orthoebolaviruses, as well as other more distantly related filoviruses24–26.

Based on clinical experience from recent EBOV outbreaks18,19, monoclonal antibodies are also highly promising therapeutic candidates. Although no such monoclonal antibodies are currently licensed for the treatment of BDBV, MPB 134 has shown protection against this virus in both ferrets and non-human primates27. This antibody cocktail consists of two monoclonal antibodies with broadly neutralizing activities that were isolated from a survivor of the West African EBOV outbreak, one of which was then further specificity-matured to increase the breadth of reactivity of the cocktail28. Notably, combining Remdesivir and monoclonal antibodies has been shown to increase treatment efficacy against several filoviruses in nonhuman primate models, even under conditions where the therapy is initiated late29,30, making this combination therapy approach the most promising treatment option currently available.

With respect to vaccine development, a vector vaccine based on a VSV backbone and encoding the BDBV glycoprotein has shown full protection in non-human primates if given pre-exposure31, and 83% protection as a post-exposure vaccine26. However, currently no clinical-grade material is available, and as such it remains unclear if this vaccine candidate could be deployed within a time-frame that will be relevant to the control of the current outbreak. While the use of the existing Ervebo vaccine has been discussed as a potential substitute, the non-human primate data for efficacy of this vaccine against BDBV are mixed, with some studies suggesting partial protection32, whereas other studies showed no protection using Ervebo alone31. As such, its use in the current outbreak has to be viewed critically, as a failed vaccine trial could severely erode trust within the affected communities.

Perspective for the 2026 outbreak

At the time of writing, we are still at a very early point in this outbreak, and there is a great deal of uncertainty regarding its real extent, both with respect to the case numbers and its geographical distribution. Nonetheless, based on the available information, it is clear that this outbreak poses a significant risk within the area, i.e. DRC, Uganda, and other neighboring countries, although it also has to be stressed that the risk for countries outside this region remains low. Concerns regarding this outbreak are due on the one hand to the rapidly rising case numbers, but also on factors related to the problematic security situation in the region, which greatly impacts outbreak control measures. Further, the high population density, presence of large urban centers, and high mobility of the local population are all cause for concern regarding the potential for future spread. In particular, the fact that affected provinces in DRC (i.e. Ituri, North Kivu, and South Kivu) share land borders with South Sudan, Uganda, Rwanda, and Burundi presents a high risk for international spread of the outbreak.

In order to limit the impact of the current outbreak it is now essential to quickly mount a robust outbreak response using classical control measures to curb transmission chains. This involves strengthening community awareness and participation by providing information regarding Ebola disease caused by BDBV, and in particular measures for infection prevention and the identification of probable cases. Further, the establishment and strengthening of fast and reliable diagnostics capabilities, efficient tracing and monitoring of contacts, as well as the isolation of patients in dedicated treatment units will be paramount. It is equally essential that these treatment units are properly equipped and have well-trained staff to avoid cases among health care workers and the disastrous impacts this can have on the ability to sustain an effective outbreak response. At the same time, specific treatment of patients has to be facilitated, and indeed, the clinical trial protocols for Remdesivir and MBP134 are already being finalized and will likely be initiated in the very near future33. Further initiatives are also considering Obeldesivir, which does not require intravenous application, and thus represents an attractive option as a post-exposure prophylaxis for interrupting community spread, at least until a vaccine can be deployed.

Previous EBOV outbreaks, and in particular the West African EBOV epidemic, have demonstrated the devastating consequences that Ebola disease can have on affected countries. However, the experiences during recent outbreaks have also demonstrated the power of classical control measures combined with direct preventative and/or therapeutic approaches to limit the scope of these events. A quick and decisive response is now needed to ensure rapid control of this most recent BDBV outbreak, whereby every available assistance should be extended to the affected countries.
