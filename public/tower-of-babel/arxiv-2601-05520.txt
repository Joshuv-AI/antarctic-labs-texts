CHisAgent: A Multi-Agent Framework for Event Taxonomy Construction in Ancient Chinese Cultural Systems
Xuemei Tang, Chengxi Yan, Jianghang Gu, Chu-Ren Huang

Abstract
Despite strong performance on many tasks, large language models (LLMs) show limited ability in historical and cultural reasoning, particularly in non-English contexts such as Chinese history. Taxonomic structures offer an effective mechanism to organize historical knowledge and improve understanding. However, manual taxonomy construction is costly and difficult to scale. Therefore, we propose CHisAgent , a multi-agent LLM framework for historical taxonomy construction in ancient Chinese contexts. CHisAgent decomposes taxonomy construction into three role-specialized stages: a bottom-up Inducer that derives an initial hierarchy from raw historical corpora, a top-down Expander that introduces missing intermediate concepts using LLM world knowledge, and an evidence-guided Enricher that integrates external structured historical resources to ensure faithfulness. Using the Twenty-Four Histories , we construct a large-scale, domain-aware event taxonomy covering politics, military, diplomacy, and social life in ancient China. Extensive reference-free and reference-based evaluations demonstrate improved structural coherence and coverage, while further analysis shows that the resulting taxonomy supports cross-cultural alignment 1 1 1 https://anonymous.4open.science/r/ACL-BC1E/ .

Introduction

Recently, LLMs have achieved strong performance in real-world understanding tasks such as emotion understanding
2
and scene planning
21
. However, several works suggest that these models exhibit limited capacity for historical and cultural reasoning, particularly in non-English cultural contexts
11
;
25
;
22
;
7
;
4
.
Moreover, even when provided with explicit historical texts, models often fail to capture deeper cultural structures beyond surface-level patterns
22
.

These limitations indicate that purely text-based modeling is insufficient for historical and cultural understanding. Explicit taxonomic organization provides an effective way to structure historical knowledge and support LLMs in capturing diverse cultural contexts.
Previous works have explored taxonomies in specific historical domains to enhance models’ understanding capabilities, such as Italian public administration
18
and traditional Chinese time systems
26
. However, these efforts remain limited in scale and cultural scope due to the labor-intensive nature of manual taxonomy construction
3
.
Recent studies demonstrate that LLMs can effectively support automated taxonomy induction and expansion
5
;
14
;
20
;
8
.
These approaches typically decompose the complex taxonomy construction pipeline into multiple subtasks or stages, with LLMs employed to perform different roles across the process.

Chinese historical records span roughly three thousand years and provide one of the most comprehensive records of human activities in a general geographic area, focused on a society with shared cultural identification. Yet there is no existing taxonomy of Chinese history and culture that provides both broad coverage and strong generalization.
In this work, we construct a large-scale taxonomy of Chinese history and culture based on the
Twenty-four Histories
. These are the orthodox, i.e. formal and reliable, histories of China, compiled between 91 BCE and 1736 CE, covering the period from approximately 400 BCE to 1644 CE, the end of the Ming dynasty. The
Twenty-four Histories
collectively span roughly two thousand years, both in terms of the historical period they describe and the timeframe over which they were compiled. Since events and roles constitute fundamental units for representing social processes in historical narratives
9
;
16
, we adopt an event-centered taxonomy as a natural and interpretable organizational framework.

Building on this foundation, we propose a multi-agent LLM framework for historical event taxonomy construction, in which LLMs are assigned distinct roles across three stages.
The framework first induces an initial taxonomy from raw historical corpora in a bottom-up manner, then expands it through top-down generalization using LLM world knowledge, and finally refines it by incorporating corpus-derived evidence with external structured historical taxonomies to ensure faithfulness.

Our main contributions are summarized as follows:

•

We propose
CHisAgent
, the multi-agent LLM framework specifically designed for large-scale historical event taxonomy construction, which decomposes the task into induction, expansion, and completion stages with explicitly role-specialized agents.

•

We construct the largest domain-aware event taxonomy for ancient Chinese history and culture, derived from the
Twenty-Four Histories
, covering core aspects of political, military, diplomatic, and social life.

•

We conduct extensive evaluations from both reference-free and reference-based aspects, and further validate the taxonomy’s cross-cultural alignment capability.

Related Work

Taxonomy construction is traditionally labor-intensive and costly, motivating extensive exploration into automated methods
6
;
15
.
Recent LLMs have been widely used to construct and expand taxonomies. For example,
29
proposed an in-context learning framework (Chain-of-Layer) for inducing taxonomies from a given set of entities, highlighting the potential of LLMs for automated taxonomy construction.

19
reformulated entity set expansion, taxonomy expansion, and seed guided taxonomy construction as sibling and parent identification tasks, and trained instruction tuned LLMs to solve them. Similarly,
30
proposed CodeTaxo, which encodes existing taxonomies as executable Python programs and uses code based prompting to guide LLMs in taxonomy expansion. In addition, LLM driven approaches have been applied to taxonomy construction in specific domains, such as engineering, social media and research fields
(
1
;
14
;
31
)
.

Building on these single model approaches, in recent works, LLMs are assigned specialized roles and may collaborate with other agents or human experts to construct and refine taxonomies. For instance,
17
proposed CLIMB, which abstracts clustering results into canonical occupational nodes and iteratively refines the hierarchy through a generator and evaluator interaction.
13
introduced TaxoAdapt, a multidimensional adaptive framework that uses LLMs to dynamically adjust the width and depth of scientific literature taxonomies according to topical distributions.

Despite recent progress in LLM-based taxonomy construction, organizing historical knowledge remains challenging because of cultural specificity, temporal complexity, and domain conventions. For Chinese historical taxonomy,

26
introduced including a traditional Chinese time ontology for historical dating systems.

27
developed an event taxonomy for event extraction.

23
constructed a relation taxonomy to support structured historical information extraction. However, existing efforts are mostly limited to specific domains or task-oriented settings. In this work, we try to leverage LLMs to construct a large-scale and systematic taxonomy of ancient Chinese history and culture.

3
Methodology

Our framework consists of three LLM agent modules: the
Inducer
, the
Expander
, and the
Enricher
.
As shown in Figure
1
, each agent is designed to operate at a different stage of the taxonomy construction process, adopting either bottom-up or top-down strategies.
The
Inducer
performs bottom-up induction, deriving an initial event hierarchy directly from the corpus.
The
Expander
addresses structural incompleteness caused by inducer bias, acting as a domain expert to introduce missing intermediate concepts and improve hierarchical coherence.
The
Enricher
ensures historical faithfulness by incorporating both corpus-derived evidence and external structured knowledge.

3.1
Inducer

The Inducer is designed as a collaborative agent group for bottom-up taxonomy induction.
We first construct a corpus by sampling eight representative chapters from each book of
The Twenty-Four Histories
, which serves as the foundational material. The detailed statistics of the dataset are provided in Appendix
A
.

Extractor
agents are then employed to extract event instances, event types and their corresponding trigger words from the historical documents.
This process yields an event set

E=\{e_{1},\dots,e_{i},\dots,e_{M}\}
.
We use multiple extractors to improve coverage and mitigate extractor-specific biases arising from the linguistic and narrative characteristics of classical Chinese texts.
The extracted event types are subsequently assigned to top-level domains by a
classifier
agent, where
d
represents a specific domain, and
d\in D
.
By integrating domain descriptions with event samples, the classifier assigns each event type
e_{i}\in E
a domain label.
Event types belonging to the same domain
d
are aggregated:

\vskip-2.84526ptE_{d}=\{e_{1},\dots,e_{i},\dots,e_{m}\}.\vskip-2.84526pt

(1)

Detailed statistics of event types for each domain are provided in Appendix
B
.
These domains are motivated by empirical observations and expert knowledge.
Preliminary experiments with baseline models show that induced event categories naturally cluster into these thematic areas, and expert review is applied to adjust domain boundaries and ensure conceptual coherence. Therefore, we organize the extracted events into eight predefined high-level domains as follows.

•

Politics
: Encompasses events related to state governance, power structures, policy formulation and implementation, as well as judicial and legal affairs.

•

Military
: Encompasses events related to warfare, military operations, strategic planning, and army organization and management.

•

Diplomacy
: Includes inter-state political and cultural interactions conducted through official channels, such as envoy missions, tribute relations, and peace negotiations.

•

Society
: Covers social activities, cultural practices, customs, and collective events involving groups, organizations, or institutions beyond the individual level.

•

Ritual
: Refers to ceremonial and ritual activities conducted by ancient states or royal families.

•

Economy–Livelihood
: Covers events related to economic production, resource distribution, daily subsistence, and state finance.

•

Nature
: Includes natural phenomena, related events, and their impacts on human society.

•

Individual
: Covers events related to individual life trajectories, identity attributes, personal behaviors, and social relationships.

Inducing the Initial Taxonomy.

After classification, taxonomy induction within each domain follows a bottom-up strategy realized by two specialized LLM agent roles.
This role separation is designed to reduce abstraction-induced information loss, which often arises when a single LLM is required to summarize large and heterogeneous event sets.

Each
Generator LLM
merges semantically related event types to form higher-level nodes.
After each generation round, a
Merger LLM
performs deduplication and concept unification to refine the generators’ outputs.
The refined results are then fed back to each Generator to produce the next level of abstraction.
This iterative process continues until the number of high-level event categories within a domain falls below ten.
In practice, this stopping criterion is typically reached after two or three merging rounds, resulting in a initial taxonomy.

Each node in the taxonomy is denoted as
n_{d,l,i}
, where
d
indicates the domain,
l
the hierarchy level, and
i
the node index within that level.
The taxonomy for domain
d
is represented as

\vskip-2.84526pt\mathcal{T}_{d}=(\mathcal{N}_{d},\mathcal{H}_{d}),\vskip-2.84526pt

(2)

where

\mathcal{N}_{d}=\{(n_{d,l,i},\textit{definition})\}

and

\mathcal{H}_{d}=\{(n_{d,l,i},n_{d,l+1,j})\}
denotes the parent-child hierarchical relations.
The complete taxonomy is defined as

\vskip-2.84526pt\mathcal{T}=\{\mathcal{T}_{d}\mid d\in D\}.\vskip-2.84526pt

(3)

3.2
Expander

The Expander is a domain expert agent performs layer-aware top-down expansion, inserting underrepresented nodes to ensure structural coherence and completeness within each domain.
The expansion process consists of initial deduplication, layer-wise hierarchical expansion, and a final global deduplication pass.

Deduplication of Nodes.

To ensure semantic distinctness, we perform both within-domain and cross-domain deduplication.
For any node pair
(n_{i},n_{j})
within the same domain or across different domains, we compute their semantic similarity

s_{ij}=\text{Sim}(n_{i},n_{j})
.
If
s_{ij}>h
, the nodes are considered redundant, and a
judger
LLM selects a representative node to resolve the conflict.

Layer-wise Hierarchical Expansion.

After deduplication, we perform layer-wise hierarchical expansion from top to bottom. The
Expander
is then used to assess semantic coverage and identify conceptually missing categories that are underrepresented.
For a target layer
l
, the Expander is provided with the current layer nodes and definitions
\{n_{d,l,i},\textit{definition}\}
, their child nodes and definitions
\{n_{d,l+1,j},\textit{definition}\}
, and domain descriptions
d
.

The expansion operator performs three constrained operations:
(1) identifying missing sibling categories at layer
l
,
(2) generating intermediate nodes between layer
l
and
l+1
when necessary, and
(3) reassigning child nodes to the expanded layer while enforcing parent–child semantic consistency.
Formally, the expanded taxonomy is obtained by:

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{d}=\text{Expander}(

\displaystyle\{n_{d,l,i},\textit{definition}\},

(4)

\displaystyle\{n_{d,l+1,j},\textit{definition}\},

\displaystyle d).

Finally, a global deduplication step is applied to remove redundant nodes introduced during the expansion process.

3.3
Enricher

The LLM-based
Enricher
aims to improve the completeness and coverage of the expanded taxonomy while preserving faithfulness to historical evidence.
It adopts a two-step design to recover important event nodes that may be omitted or pruned during the
Inducer
and
Expander
stages.
Specifically, the Enricher refines the taxonomy by integrating corpus-derived evidence and external structured knowledge.

Candidate Event Collection.

We first construct a comprehensive candidate event set
E_{c}
from three complementary sources.
(1)
High-frequency events
extracted from the initial event set
E
: event types whose frequencies exceed a threshold (
f>5
) are selected as frequent candidates, denoted as
E_{\text{freq}}
.
(2)
Topic-based events
derived from the original corpus using BERTopic
10
, yielding a set of latent topics
Topics=\{t_{1},\dots,t_{k}\}
that capture thematic patterns potentially corresponding to missing mid- or high-level event categories
24
. The detailed topics information is provided in Appendix
6
.
(3)
Ontology-derived relations
collected from existing historical knowledge bases, including the Chinese Biographical Database (CBDB) and the historical relation ontology proposed by
23
, resulting in a relation set
R=\{r_{1},\dots,r_{n}\}
.

An LLM-based
Event Conceptualizer
then converts the topic set
Topics
and relation set
R
into explicit event types, producing
E_{\text{Topics}}
and
E_{\text{R}}
, respectively.
The union of all candidates forms the initial event set:

\vskip-5.69054ptE_{c}=E_{\text{freq}}\cup E_{\text{Topics}}\cup E_{\text{R}}.\vskip-5.69054pt

(5)

To ensure conceptual uniqueness, we compute pairwise semantic similarity among candidate event types and remove redundant entries.
The resulting set
E_{c}
constitutes a deduplicated pool of candidate events for subsequent enrichment.

Positioning Candidate Events.

For each candidate event
e\in E_{c}
, the Enricher first predicts its most likely domain
d
based on the event type and domain description.
Conditioned on the current domain taxonomy
\mathcal{T}_{d}
, it then checks whether the corresponding node
e
is semantically redundant with existing nodes using similarity-based comparison.
If no duplication is detected, the Enricher determines an appropriate insertion position for
e
within
\mathcal{T}_{d}
, yielding the enriched domain taxonomy

\mathcal{T}_{d}=\mathcal{T}_{d}\cup\{e\}
.
The Enricher serves to bridge the gap between the induced taxonomy and the underlying historical texts, improving corpus alignment and representational fidelity.

4
Experiments

4.1
Experimental Setup

Based on the observed task-specific strengths of different LLMs in our baseline experiments, we assign them to specialized roles across stages.
In the extraction and induction stage, DeepSeek-V3, Qwen3, and GPT-4o act as extractor and generator agents, with GPT-4o additionally serving as the domain classifier. GPT-5 is assigned the merger role.
In the expansion stage, GPT-5 functions as the judger for resolving semantic duplicates, and also serves as the expander for generating higher-level taxonomy nodes.
In the enrichment stage, GPT-4o is used as the event conceptualizer, while GPT-5 acts as the Enricher to integrate candidate events into the taxonomy.

We use the following model versions in our experiments:
GPT-4o (
gpt-4o-2024-11-20
),
DeepSeek-V3 (
DeepSeek-V3-0324
),
Qwen3 (
qwen-plus-2025-07-28
),
and GPT-5 (
gpt-5-2025-08-07
).
For all models except GPT-5, the temperature is set to 0.

For all semantic similarity computations, taxonomy nodes and their definitions are encoded using
text-embedding-3-small
, and cosine similarity is applied for comparison. Semantic duplication is identified using a similarity threshold of 0.6, above which node pairs are considered redundant; this threshold is determined through manual evaluation.

4.2
Baselines

We design four baseline settings to compare different taxonomy construction strategies.

LLM + Domains.

The model is given only the predefined domains and instructed to construct the taxonomy in a top–down manner, generating increasingly fine-grained event types from high-level categories. We conduct experiments under this setting using GPT-4o, DeepSeek-V3, and Qwen3.

LLM + Event Corpus.

The LLM is provided with all fine-grained event types extracted by Extractors and constructs the taxonomy in a bottom–up manner by iteratively clustering and abstracting similar events. GPT-4o, DeepSeek-V3, and Qwen3 are tested under this configuration.

LLM + Event Corpus + Domains.

In this setting, the model is provided with both pre-defined domains and fine-grained event types in each domain, and directly induces mid-level categories by integrating domain knowledge with event semantics.
We evaluate GPT-4o, DeepSeek-V3, and Qwen3 under this setting.
In addition, we reproduce
Chain-of-Layer

29
and
TaxoAdapt

13
as representative baselines in this category.
Details of the reproduction process are provided in Appendix
D
.

Human-crafted Taxonomy.

We include the CHED taxonomy
27
as a human-crafted baseline. Curated from the
Twenty-Four Histories
for event extraction, it covers high-level event types and serves as a coarse-grained human reference.

4.3
Evaluation Metrics

We evaluate the constructed taxonomies using two types of metrics reference-free and reference-based, covering both structural and semantic aspects. The detailed calculation procedures for all metrics are provided in Appendix
E
.

Reference-free Metrics.

•

Path Granularity

13
. This metric assesses whether the node (
n_{d,l,i}
) is semantically more specific than its parent. The score (0 or 1) is assigned by GPT-4o.

•

CSC

28
.
A structural consistency metric that evaluates whether the distances between nodes in a taxonomy align with their semantic distances.
Higher CSC scores indicate stronger alignment between hierarchical structure and semantic relations.

•

Coverage Rate
. This metric measures the taxonomy’s coverage of historical events in the
Twenty-Four Histories
. The events are extracted from chapters of the
Twenty-Four Histories
that do not overlap with the data used to construct the taxonomy.

Reference-Based Metrics.

These metrics compare the constructed taxonomy with the human-curated taxonomy of CHED
27
.

•

Node Recall
.
Measures the proportion of nodes in the human taxonomy that are captured by the automatically generated taxonomy, based on semantic similarity above a defined threshold.

•

Novelty

12
. Quantifies the creativity of the constructed taxonomy. A node is considered novel if no semantically corresponding node exists in the human taxonomy. Higher scores indicate higher creativity.

•

Significance

12
. Measures how much finer-grained the constructed taxonomy is compared to the human taxonomy. Higher values indicate greater granularity. A negative score indicates that the constructed taxonomy is more coarse-grained than the human taxonomy.

Ablation Study

Table

presents an ablation study that evaluates the contributions of the three agent modules: Inducer, Expander, and Enricher.
Using only the Inducer results in the lowest Node Recall and Coverage Rate, indicating that the initial stage alone is insufficient to construct a structurally complete taxonomy.

4.7
Human Evaluation

Following
31
, we conduct a human evaluation to assess the quality of taxonomies generated by different methods (GPT-4o (Corpus + Domain), CoL, TaxoAdapt, and Ours).
The evaluation focuses on semantic clarity, structural coherence, and semantic distinction, and considers five complementary perspectives as follows.

Node Clarity.

For each taxonomy, we randomly sample 50 nodes and ask evaluators to judge whether the node’s semantics are clear and whether the node can appropriately represent an event type in ancient Chinese cultural system. Scores range from 0 to 5.

Hierarchical Coherence.

For each taxonomy, we randomly sample 50 nodes together with their full paths, and evaluators assess whether the hierarchical structure from the root to the leaf is logically coherent. Scores range from 0 to 5.

In-domain Orthogonality.

For each taxonomy, we randomly sample 50 nodes and retrieve their top 5 most similar nodes within the same domain. Evaluators then assess whether the semantics of the sampled node overlap with those similar nodes. If no semantic overlap exists, the node receives a score of 5; if there is overlap with one node, the score is 4; and so on.

Cross-domain Orthogonality.

The procedure is similar to in-domain orthogonality, except that the top 5 similar nodes are retrieved from other domains. Evaluators judge whether cross-domain semantic overlap exists.

Completeness.

Evaluators judge whether each complete taxonomy sufficiently covers the conceptual space of Chinese historical events. Scores range from 0 to 5.

We employ three independent evaluators with expertise in Chinese history. Evaluation guidelines are provided in Appendix
G
. The inter-annotator agreement across the five evaluation dimensions is 0.38, and final scores are computed by averaging the ratings from all evaluators.
Human evaluation results are presented in Figure
3
. TaxAdapt achieves the highest scores in Clarity and Completeness but performs worst in Cross-domain Orthogonality, indicating semantic overlap across domains despite extensive node generation. GPT-4o (Corpus + Domain) attains the highest In-domain and Cross-domain Orthogonality, reflecting strong semantic separation among categories.
In contrast, our method achieves consistently strong performance across all metrics, balancing completeness, semantic clarity, and structural orthogonality in a manner that aligns well with human judgment.

Case Study.
We conduct a fine-grained analysis of diplomatic events using tributary interactions as a representative case.
While tribute-based diplomacy appears across East Asian polities, our taxonomy reveals systematic differences in how comparable interactions are organized from distinct narrative perspectives.
Specifically, as shown in Appedndix
H
, tribute events are mapped to separate nodes for incoming tribute (
來貢
) and outgoing tribute (
進貢
), explicitly encoding receiver-centered and actor-centered viewpoints. All extracted tribute-related event samples are manually verified.
We observe that events from Chinese and Japanese corpora predominantly align with the incoming tribute (
來貢
) perspective, Vietnamese records more frequently align with outgoing tribute (
進貢
), and Korean records exhibit a mixed pattern.
These findings demonstrate that perspective-aware taxonomy nodes are crucial for cross-cultural comparison, enabling structured alignment of semantically similar practices while preserving culturally grounded differences in historical narration.

Conclusion

In this study, we propose a multi-agent framework for constructing a taxonomy of the ancient Chinese cultural system. The taxonomy construction process is decomposed into three stages: the Inducer, the Expander, and the Enricher, each fulfilled by different LLM agents. We evaluate the generated taxonomies from multiple perspectives and further demonstrate their applicability in aligning historical corpora across East Asian cultures.

Limitations

This work has several limitations.

First, although we employ multiple LLMs to reduce the bias introduced by any single model, LLM-based taxonomy construction inevitably inherits biases from model pretraining. In particular, modern language models may project contemporary conceptual structures onto historical contexts or introduce hallucinated abstractions when expanding sparse historical evidence.

Second, the performance of current LLMs on Classical Chinese remains limited. Classical Chinese texts are highly concise, context-dependent, and often ambiguous, posing challenges for accurate semantic interpretation. As a result, the generated taxonomy may still contain errors or omissions, and human intervention is occasionally required to ensure correctness.

Third, while the
Twenty-Four Histories
constitute a highly authoritative and representative corpus of ancient Chinese historiography, they primarily reflect official and elite perspectives. Certain aspects of ancient Chinese culture, such as folk practices, regional traditions, and informal social dynamics, are underrepresented, limiting the completeness of the resulting taxonomy.

Fourth, our event-centered taxonomy design, while effective for modeling historical narratives and social processes, may not fully capture cultural phenomena that evolve gradually or are not naturally event-driven, such as philosophical traditions or long-term ideological shifts.

Finally, although we employ both automatic metrics and human evaluation, these evaluation protocols have inherent limitations. Structural metrics cannot fully capture cultural appropriateness, and human evaluation is constrained in scale and may be influenced by annotators’ backgrounds.

Ethics Statement

The human evaluations in this study were conducted by internal members of the research team (excluding the authors). All participants are research assistants and graduate students with expertise in the relevant domain. Participation was entirely voluntary. All participants were fully informed of the study’s objectives and the nature of the tasks. No personal, sensitive, or identifiable information was collected during the evaluation. Accordingly, this study adheres to ethical standards and poses no risk to participants.

Appendix D
Baselines

Chain-of-Layer

29
.
This method constructs a taxonomy in a top-down manner by iteratively selecting entities to form hierarchical layers, given a set of entities extracted from existing taxonomies.
Following this procedure, we first collect entity sets from taxonomies generated by DeepSeek-V3 (Corpus + Domain), Qwen3 (Corpus + Domain), and GPT-4o (Corpus + Domain).
Next, we use GPT-4o to generate mid-level entities based on the collected entity sets, forming an expanded pool of candidate entities.
Finally, GPT-4o is employed to iteratively select entities from this pool and construct the taxonomy layer by layer.

TaxoAdapt

13
.
This method constructs a taxonomy in a top-down manner, adaptively expanding its width and depth based on the distribution of event types associated with each node.
Following the original setting, we use predefined domains and an event-type pool obtained from the Classifier.
GPT-4o is first employed to generate pseudo labels that serve as candidate taxonomy nodes.
Event types are then assigned to these pseudo labels via classification.
If more than 50 event types remain unassigned, we expand the taxonomy width by introducing additional sibling nodes.
Conversely, if a node is associated with more than 20 event types, we further expand the taxonomy depth by generating child nodes under this node.

Appendix E
Metrics

Path Granularity.
We use GPT-4o to score each parent–child pair, assigning 1 if the child is more specific than the parent and 0 otherwise. Finally, we compute the proportion of pairs that satisfy this condition.

CSC
. CSC is a structure-level metric that quantifies the alignment between the taxonomy’s hierarchical structure and the semantic similarity of concepts.
Specifically, it measures the extent to which concept pairs that are farther apart in the taxonomy are also semantically less similar, while closer nodes exhibit higher semantic similarity.

W_{n_{a},n_{b}}=\frac{2\cdot\text{lca}(p(n_{a}),p(n_{b}))}{|p(n_{a})|+|p(n_{b})|}

(6)

where
p(n_{a})
denote the path from the root concept to a target concept
n_{a}
. Furthermore, let
\text{lca}(p(n_{a}),p(n_{b}))
denotes the depth of the least common ancestor of the paths
p(n_{a})
and
p(n_{b})
.

\text{CSC}:=\tau\left(W_{n_{a}n_{b}},S_{n_{a}n_{b}}\right)

(7)

where
\tau
denotes Kendall rank correlation, and
S_{n_{a}n_{b}}
represents the semantic similarity between concept representation. We encode each node using
text-embedding-3-small
and compute cosine similarity to obtain
S
.

Coverage Rate.
This metric evaluates how well the generated taxonomy can organize knowledge from the
Twenty-Four Histories
.
We sample two chapters from each book (excluding those used during experiments), and use Qwen3, DeepSeek-V3, and GPT-4o to extract event samples (text + event type). After deduplication,
we compute the cosine similarity between each event type and every node in the taxonomy. If the similarity is above 0.6, we consider that event type covered. The final coverage rate is the proportion of event types that are covered. All nodes are encoded using
text-embedding-3-small
.

Node Recall.
This metric measures how many nodes in the human taxonomy (
\mathcal{T}
) are recalled by the generated taxonomy (
\mathcal{C}
). We encode each node in both taxonomies using
text-embedding-3-small
and compute cosine similarity between node pairs. If the similarity exceeds 0.6, we consider the human node recalled.

Novelty.
Novelty quantifies the proportion of completely new classes or categories in a taxonomy that are not present in any previous taxonomies. For a generated taxonomy (
\mathcal{C}
) and a human taxonomy (
\mathcal{T}
), it is defined as:

\text{Novelty}(\mathcal{C},\mathcal{T})=\frac{\sum_{c\in\mathcal{C}}\text{new}(\mathcal{C},\mathcal{T},c)}{|\mathcal{C}|}\in[0,1]

(8)

Here, (
\text{new}(\mathcal{C},\mathcal{T},c)=1
) if class (
c
) is entirely new compared to taxonomy (
\mathcal{T}
), and 0 otherwise. A higher value indicates that the taxonomy introduces more novel information.

To determine whether a class
c
is new relative to the human taxonomy, we use the same cosine-similarity procedure as in Node Recall. If a node in the generated taxonomy cannot find any human node with similarity greater than 0.6, we consider it a novel node.

Significance.
We measure the significance of a taxonomy by its ability to provide fine-grained categorization of events.
To compare a generated taxonomy (
\mathcal{C}
) with a human-curated taxonomy (
\mathcal{T}
), we adopt classification delta, defined as:

\text{class.\ delta}(\mathcal{C},\mathcal{T},R)=\frac{|\sim_{\mathcal{C}}|-|\sim_{\mathcal{T}}|}{|R|}

(9)

where (
R
) is the set of event types extracted by LLMs during the Coverage Rate evaluation and successfully matched by both taxonomies.

Here,
(
|\sim_{\mathcal{C}}|
) denotes the number of distinct taxonomy nodes in (
\mathcal{C}
) that events in (
\mathcal{R}
) are assigned to,
(
|\sim_{\mathcal{T}}|
) denotes the corresponding number of distinct nodes in the human taxonomy (
\mathcal{T}
).

A positive classification delta indicates that events in (
R
) are distributed across more nodes in (
\mathcal{C}
) than in (
\mathcal{T}
), implying that (
\mathcal{C}
) provides a more fine-grained categorization.
A delta of zero indicates that (
\mathcal{C}
) matches the granularity of the human taxonomy, while a negative value suggests that (
\mathcal{C}
) is coarser and assigns events to fewer categories.
