Physicochemical Characterization of Choline Amino Acid Protic Ionic Liquids as Sustainable Lubricant Candidates
Authors: Davis Kipkania Kiboi, Brendan Mahoney, Esmond Lau, Erik C. Dillon, Michael G. Coleman, Filippo Mangolini, Patricia Iglesias
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Choline amino acid protic ionic liquids (PILs) have emerged as promising candidates for sustainable lubrication due to their tunable structures, low toxicity, and strong interfacial activity. In this study, a series of ten choline amino acid PILs, incorporating aliphatic, branched, multifunctional, and aromatic amino acid anions, were synthesized and systematically characterized to establish structure–property relationships relevant to lubrication applications. The physicochemical behavior of the PILs was investigated through viscosity measurements, ionic conductivity analyses, thermal characterization, wettability assessment, miscibility evaluation with polar and nonpolar base oils, and corrosion studies on copper surfaces. Their lubricating performance when used as boundary lubricants was assessed. The results reveal a strong dependence of bulk and interfacial properties on the anion structure. PILs containing compact aliphatic anions exhibited low viscosities and high ionic conductivities, reflecting weak intermolecular interactions and high ion mobility. In contrast, PILs with branched, aromatic, or multifunctional anions displayed higher viscosities and reduced conductivities because of enhanced hydrogen bonding, steric effects, and supramolecular structuring. Notably, the aspartic acid-containing PIL showed significantly elevated viscosity compared with other small-anion systems, highlighting the dominant influence of multiple carboxylic acid functionalities. Thermal analysis indicated generally high stability for moderate-temperature lubrication applications across the series, with aromatic and multifunctional systems exhibiting increased glass transition temperatures and decomposition resistance. Wettability and miscibility studies demonstrated that the interfacial behavior is governed by both side-chain chemistry and substrate or solvent polarity, with clear differences observed between steel and aluminum surfaces and between ester- and PAO-based oils. Corrosivity evaluations showed that PILs with small, highly mobile anions promote greater surface reactivity on copper, whereas bulkier or multifunctional anions provide improved surface stability through reduced ion mobility and steric shielding. Tribological evaluation through reciprocating sliding friction and wear tests revealed trends consistent with the observed structure–property relationships, supporting the relevance of the measured physicochemical properties to lubrication performance. This work establishes a comprehensive framework linking molecular structure to physicochemical and interfacial behavior in choline amino acid PILs. The findings provide insight into the design of environmentally compatible ionic liquids with tailored properties and potential for lubrication and related applications.

Introduction

Understanding how the molecular structure of liquids governs their behavior at solid interfaces remains a central challenge in materials and interfacial science. In lubrication systems operating under boundary conditions, where fluid films are confined to only a few molecular layers, even subtle variations in liquid chemistry can lead to pronounced differences in surface affinity and tribochemical film formation. Under such conditions, bulk hydrodynamic effects become secondary, and the physicochemical properties of the lubricant, including molecular polarity, hydrogen bonding capability, and interfacial reactivity, play a decisive role in controlling friction, wear, and surface stability. ,

On the scale of real engineering systems, these molecular-level processes directly translate into macroscopic energy losses and material degradation. Friction and wear remain major contributors to energy dissipation, reduced component lifetimes, and increased maintenance costs across a wide range of applications. It is estimated that a significant fraction of global energy consumption is lost to frictional processes, , underscoring the need for advanced lubricants that can improve efficiency while meeting increasingly stringent environmental and sustainability requirements.

The need for environmentally friendly lubricants as replacements for more traditional, petroleum-based lubricants is ever-present: it has been estimated that 50% of all lubricants worldwide end up in the environment through leaks, spills, improper disposal, and equipment failure. Conventional lubricant formulations often rely on additive packages containing sulfur, phosphorus, or metal-based compounds that, while effective in reducing friction and wear, raise significant concerns related to toxicity, volatility, and poor biodegradability. , Many of these additives can decompose under high temperatures or severe contact conditions, leading to the formation of environmentally persistent byproducts and corrosive species that compromise both component durability and ecological safety. In addition, the volatility and thermal instability of certain conventional additives limit their effectiveness in extreme operating environments, necessitating frequent replenishment and an increase in maintenance demands. These limitations, together with growing regulatory pressure to reduce hazardous chemical usage, have intensified interest in alternative lubrication chemistries that can deliver comparable or superior performance while offering improved environmental compatibility and long-term sustainability.

In this context, ionic liquids (ILs) have emerged as a promising class of lubricants and lubricant additives. ILs are salts that remain liquid below 100 °C. − First studied as lubricants in 2001, ILs are now the subject of hundreds of tribological publications each year. − ,− They exhibit several properties that make them attractive candidates for use as lubricants and additives, including low vapor pressure, high thermal stability, low flammability, and the capacity to form protective tribolayers that reduce friction and wear. ,,− Structurally, ILs are typically composed of sterically bulky and asymmetric ions that weaken the strong electrostatic interactions characteristic of conventional salts, while their propensity to form hydrogen bonds further reduces their melting points compared to more traditional ionic compounds. , The vast variety of available cations and anions gives rise to over a million possible ILs, each exhibiting distinct physicochemical and tribological behaviors. , ILs are generally classified into two main types: protic and aprotic. Protic ILs (PILs) are generally easier to synthesize, formed via a simple acid–base reaction followed by drying to remove excess water. , In contrast, aprotic ILs require more complex and expensive synthesis routes and often contain halogens like fluorine and chlorine, which can react with water to form highly corrosive and toxic acids. , ILs can be more costly to synthesize than traditional lubricants, but this can be overcome by using ILs as additives to base oils, performing similarly to neat ILs but at a much lower cost; ,, using ILs as additives can eliminate the need for other lubricant additives, including antiwear, friction-reducing, and high-pressure additives. ,

Choline-amino acid-based PILs are particularly appealing from a sustainability perspective. Choline is a naturally occurring, low-toxicity cation, while amino acids provide a diverse set of anions with tunable polarity, functionality, and steric structure. , Together, they form a versatile class of bioderived ILs that have shown promise in fields ranging from biomass processing to tribological applications. More recently, choline-amino acid PILs have attracted attention as potential lubricants and lubricant additives, − demonstrating effective friction reduction and surface protection under boundary lubrication regimes. However, while tribological performance has been reported for selected systems, ,, a systematic understanding of how the molecular structure of amino acid anions governs the physicochemical properties of choline-based PILs, and how these properties, in turn, influence lubrication behavior, remains limited. Properties such as viscosity, ionic conductivity, thermal stability, glass-transition temperature, wettability, and corrosivity play a critical role in determining lubricant performance, film-formation kinetics, and compatibility with different metal surfaces. ,, Without a comprehensive structure–property framework, the rational design and selection of PILs for lubrication applications will remain challenging.

In this work, we present a detailed physicochemical characterization of a series of choline amino acid PILs with systematically varied amino acid anions, encompassing compact aliphatic, branched aliphatic, and aromatic structures. The study integrates rheological measurements, ionic conductivity analysis, thermal characterization, wettability evaluation, and corrosion experiments. By correlating molecular structure with bulk properties and interfacial behavior, this work establishes clear structure–property-performance relationships that expound the mechanisms governing lubrication, corrosion, and film stability in choline amino acid PILs. The characterizations presented here allow us to determine the use cases of each liquid. Additionally, these characterization methods provide a way of comparing different ILs and their properties independently, which will help design next-gen high-performance lubricants and lubricant additives. Accordingly, this work focuses primarily on establishing the physicochemical and interfacial framework governing choline-amino acid PIL behavior, while tribological performance is evaluated under a limited set of operating conditions to provide an initial connection between molecular properties and lubrication response.

The insights provided in this research contribute to a fundamental understanding of bioderived PILs as functional molecular fluids and offer practical guidance for designing sustainable, high-performance lubricants tailored to specific materials and operating conditions.

Materials and Methods

Synthesis

The choline amino acid PILs investigated in this study were synthesized through an acid–base neutralization approach as reported in our previous work. Briefly, choline hydroxide and the corresponding amino acid were combined in equimolar amounts and reacted at 60 °C for 16 h. Water removal was facilitated using a Dean–Stark apparatus, and reaction completion was determined by the absence of further water collection. The resulting product was subsequently subjected to reduced pressure and vacuum drying using a Kugelrohr distillation apparatus to afford the corresponding protic ionic liquid (PIL). The water content of the synthesized PILs was determined by thermogravimetric analysis and is reported in Table , ranging from 0.18 to 2.36 wt %. All physicochemical measurements were performed using the dried PILs as synthesized, and samples were stored in sealed containers prior to characterization to minimize moisture uptake. Full details regarding the synthesis procedure and materials used are provided in ref. Table shows the anion and cation structures of the materials used in the synthesis process. The synthesized choline amino acid PILs can be broadly classified based on the size and functionality of the amino acid side chains. PILs containing short, compact aliphatic substituents (e.g., glycine and alanine) represent the simplest structures with minimal steric hindrance. A distinct category is formed by multifunctional polar anions, such as aspartic acid, which possess an additional carboxyl group and exhibit significantly different hydrogen bonding and coordination behavior compared to simple aliphatic systems. PILs with medium-sized or branched aliphatic side chains (e.g., leucine, isoleucine, and lysine) introduce increased steric bulk and hydrophobic character, while those containing bulky cyclic or aromatic moieties (e.g., proline, phenylalanine, histidine, and tyrosine) exhibit more rigid structures with enhanced intermolecular interactions. This classification reflects not only differences in molecular size but also variations in functionality that strongly influence the physicochemical and interfacial behavior of the PILs.

All compounds exhibited the characteristic resonances associated with the choline cation, including the N­(CH3)3 group and hydroxylethyl methylene carbons, together with signals corresponding to the respective amino acid side chains. The observed chemical shifts are consistent with the proposed structures and, when considered alongside the FTIR evidence of carboxylate formation, support successful proton transfer and formation of the target choline amino acid PILs. Complete 1H and 13C NMR spectra and detailed peak assignments are provided in the Supporting Information document. Figure presents the FTIR spectra of the synthesized PILs. A key molecular feature of the choline amino acid PILs is the presence of the carboxylate (COO–) functionality and the absence of the carboxylic acid group present in the parent amino acid. This transformation is evidenced by the disappearance of the broad carboxylic acid O–H stretching band (3300–2500 cm–1) and the strong carboxylic acid carbonyl (CO) stretching vibration typically observed near 1700–1725 cm–1. Formation of the protic ionic liquids is further confirmed by the appearance of characteristic asymmetric and symmetric carboxylate stretching bands at approximately 1550–1650 cm–1 and 1400–1450 cm–1, respectively. Together, these spectral changes are consistent with proton transfer from the amino acid to choline hydroxide, resulting in deprotonation of the amino acid and formation of the corresponding choline amino acid PIL as indicated in Figure . These bands indicate delocalized bonding within the carboxylate group, rather than distinct single and double bonds. In addition to confirming carboxylate formation, the FTIR spectra show subtle structure-dependent differences among the PILs. PILs with small aliphatic anions ([CHO]­[GLY], [CHO]­[ALA]) exhibit comparatively simpler spectra with less intense C–H stretching bands in the 2800–3000 cm–1 region, whereas PILs containing branched or longer aliphatic side chains ([CHO]­[LEU], [CHO]­[ILEU], [CHO]­[LYS]) show stronger C–H absorptions due to their greater hydrocarbon content. The spectra of PILs with aromatic, heterocyclic, or hydroxyl-containing side chains ([CHO]­[PHE], [CHO]­[HIS], [CHO]­[TYR]) appear more complex in the fingerprint region, reflecting differences in side-chain functionality and intermolecular interactions. Variations in the intensity and exact position of the carboxylate-related bands further suggest differences in ion pairing and hydrogen-bonding strength across the series. Furthermore, the separation between the asymmetric and symmetric carboxylate stretching bands is consistent with the presence of ionic carboxylate species, providing additional evidence for deprotonation of the amino acid and formation of the corresponding protic ionic liquid.

Dynamic Viscosity

The dynamic viscosities of the synthesized choline amino acid PILs were determined using a Discovery HR-2 Hybrid Rheometer (TA Instruments) equipped with a cone–plate geometry. Measurements were performed under a temperature ramp from 25 to 100 °C, with the samples equilibrated at each temperature before data collection. Viscosity values were obtained at a constant shear stress of 20 Pa. Because [CHO]­[TYR] was solid at room temperature, its viscosity measurements were initiated at 40 °C, where it first became fully molten and rheologically stable. In addition to the temperature-dependent viscosity measurements, flow curves (viscosity vs shear rate) were obtained at 50 °C for all PILs to evaluate their shear-dependent behavior and confirm whether they exhibit Newtonian or non-Newtonian flow characteristics.

Ionic Conductivity

Ionic conductivity was assessed with an Orion Star A212 Conductivity Benchtop Meter (Thermo Scientific). Each ionic liquid was tested at four different temperatures: room temperature, 40 °C, 70 °C, and 100 °C. Samples were held at the target temperature until steady readings were achieved. For accuracy, three separate measurements were taken for each condition, and the mean values are presented.

Thermal Analysis

Thermal stability of the choline amino acid PILs was first evaluated using a Q500 thermogravimetric analyzer (TA Instruments). Approximately 10 mg of sample was placed in a platinum pan and heated from 10 to 600 °C at a constant heating rate of 10 °C/min under a nitrogen atmosphere. The onset decomposition temperature and water content were determined from the weight-loss profile to assess the thermal resistance of the ILs.

Thermal behavior of the lubricants was also investigated using a TA Instruments Q2500 differential scanning calorimeter (DSC) with T-zero aluminum pans and lids. The DSC analysis was carried out by subjecting each sample to multiple heating–cooling cycles. Initially, the samples were cooled to −80 °C and kept at that temperature for 5 min. They were then heated to 100 °C at a rate of 10 °C/min and held isothermally for 5 min. Afterward, the temperature was lowered again to −80 °C at the same cooling rate. This cycle was performed three consecutive times to confirm repeatability and to identify the glass transition and other thermal transitions.

Solubility

The synthesized PILs were evaluated for miscibility with two commercially relevant base oils, ExxonMobil ESTEREX NP343 (EST), a low-viscosity synthetic ester base oil (API Group V) with polar ester functionalities and high additive solvency, and ExxonMobil SPECTRASYN 4 (PAO), a polyalphaolefin base oil (API Group IV) that is chemically nonpolar and representative of hydrocarbon-based lubricants. Each PIL was blended at 1 wt %, a typical additive concentration, to assess compatibility in base oils of contrasting polarity. Miscibility was evaluated qualitatively by visual inspection for clarity, phase separation, or sedimentation, with photographs taken immediately after mixing and after 1 h, 24 h, 7 days, and 30 days to monitor both short- and long-term stability. While a quantitative analysis would require other analytical approaches (e.g., optical transmittance, particle/droplet size measurements, centrifugation stability), the approach used herein enables an assessment of how base oil polarity and PIL molecular structure influence additive dispersion and formulation compatibility.

Wettability

Wettability experiments were carried out for both the neat PILs and their mixtures using polished 6061 aluminum disks (Ra ≈ 0.035 μm, measured over 9 mm2) and AISI 52100 steel disks (Ra ≈ 0.03 μm, measured over 9 mm2). A Ramé-Hart standard goniometer (Model 250-U1) was employed to measure static contact angles. For each test, a droplet of lubricant was deposited on the surface, and the contact angle was recorded after 5 min to ensure equilibrium. A total of 300 measurements were collected per trial, with three trials performed for each lubricant. Between successive trials, the substrates were cleaned with isopropyl alcohol.

Corrosion Testing

Before testing, copper disks were cleaned in a 70% ethanol solution for 5 min using an ultrasonic bath, followed by air-drying. Three drops of the respective PILs were deposited on the disk surfaces, and the samples were left under static exposure for 15 and 30 days. Upon completion of the tests, the disks were rinsed with 70% ethanol without ultrasonic agitation to avoid alteration of the corroded surfaces. Corrosion evaluation was carried out by visual inspection, mass change measurements, and surface characterization using surface roughness analysis and Raman spectroscopy.

Tribological Evaluation

Friction and wear testing were carried out in the boundary lubrication regime using a custom ball-on-flat reciprocating tribometer. Tests were run under pure sliding conditions at a room temperature of around 23 °C and a relative humidity between 40% and 70%. For the experiments, 1.5-mm-diameter 52100 steel balls and 52100 steel disks were employed. Before testing, the disks were mechanically polished to an average surface roughness of Ra ≈ 0.03 μm, measured over 9 mm2. All tests were run with a stroke length of 3.0 mm, a frequency of 5 Hz, a normal load of 3 N, a duration of 60 min, a sliding speed of 0.03 m/s, and a sliding distance of 108 m. The mean Hertzian contact pressure between the ball and disk was 1.00 GPa. All PILs were tested at room temperature. Three tests were performed for each PIL for each set of conditions, and the results were averaged to confirm repeatability.

After the friction tests, the wear-track width was measured at 30 evenly distributed locations along the track to obtain an average value. The reported values represent the average of three independent measurements, with error bars corresponding to one standard deviation. Boundary lubrication conditions were selected because they are the most demanding regime for lubricant performance, where direct surface interactions dominate and the influence of the molecular structure, adsorption behavior, and tribofilm formation can be most clearly evaluated. It is important to note that tribological testing was limited to [CHO]­[GLY], [CHO]­[ALA], [CHO]­[LYS], [CHO]­[LEU], [CHO]­[ILEU], [CHO]­[PRO], and [CHO]­[PHE], which span the compact, branched aliphatic, and cyclic/aromatic structural groups. The more viscous PILs, [CHO]­[ASP], [CHO]­[HIS], and [CHO]­[TYR], were not included because their substantially higher viscosities are expected to shift lubrication conditions away from the predominantly boundary regime established for the selected test parameters.

Results and Discussion

Viscosity

The temperature dependence of viscosity for the synthesized PILs (Figure ) reveals a clear correlation between molecular structure and viscosity. Table 1S shows the values of dynamic viscosity at various temperatures. Across all samples, viscosity decreases exponentially with temperature, consistent with Arrhenius-type behavior typical of hydrogen-bonded PILs. , However, the magnitude of the viscosity and its rate of decline vary significantly among structural classes.

Variation of the dynamic viscosity of the choline-amino acid PILs with temperature. The dynamic viscosity of [CHO]­[TYR] could not be evaluated under the same conditions as it remained solid at room temperature.

PILs with short or compact amino acid anions, like glycine and alanine, exhibited the lowest viscosities over the entire temperature range. Their small, less sterically hindered structures promote weaker cation–anion interactions and minimal entanglement, resulting in enhanced ion mobility and reduced cohesive forces. − Consequently, [CHO]­[ALA] and [CHO]­[GLY] demonstrated the lowest viscosities at both 25 and 100 °C (0.81 Pa·s to 0.02 Pa·s and 1.11 Pa·s to 0.03 Pa·s, respectively), consistent with reports that small, highly dissociated PILs flow more readily due to reduced structural ordering.

By contrast, PILs with medium-sized or branched aliphatic side chains on their anions, such as leucine, isoleucine, and lysine, display intermediate viscosities at measured temperatures, consistent with the trend that larger, and more hydrophobic anions increase liquid viscosity by restricting ionic mobility. , Within this group, [CHO]­[ILEU] shows the highest viscosity; this behavior can be rationalized by the β-branched sec-butyl side chain of isoleucine, whose compact and asymmetric geometry likely promotes tighter local packing and stronger hydrophobic clustering than the γ-branched isobutyl group in leucine. , Although lysine offers multiple hydrogen-bonding sites, its longer and more flexible aliphatic chain may reduce packing efficiency relative to the [CHO]­[ILEU], so that chain flexibility and hydrogen-bonding patterns together result in a lower viscosity than for the isoleucine-based PIL.

A distinct behavior is observed for [CHO]­[ASP], which cannot be grouped with the compact aliphatic PILs, despite its relatively small backbone. Unlike [CHO]­[GLY] and [CHO]­[ALA], [CHO]­[ASP] contains an additional carboxyl group, which introduces a second strongly polar and hydrogen-bonding site. This results in significantly higher viscosity across all temperatures, exceeding not only the compact PILs but also the branched aliphatic systems. The elevated viscosity can be attributed to the formation of an extensive hydrogen-bonding network and stronger electrostatic interactions, which increase cohesive energy density and restrict ionic mobility. The presence of two carboxylate functionalities also enhances ion pairing and possible intermolecular bridging, leading to a more structured liquid and a higher resistance to flow.

The PILs containing cyclic or aromatic amino acid anions (proline, phenylalanine, histidine, and tyrosine) showed the highest viscosities, in line with the expectation that rigid rings, π-electron systems, and multiple donor/acceptor sites promote strong supramolecular structuring and extensive hydrogen bonding within the liquid. Aromatic amino acid anions can additionally engage in π-stacking and enhanced dispersion interactions, further reducing ionic mobility. Within this group, [CHO]­[HIS] and [CHO]­[TYR] displayed extremely high viscosities. [CHO]­[TYR] remained solid at room temperature and only became molten above 40 °C, consistent with the strong cohesive interactions typically reported for this IL, which often fails to form room-temperature liquids because of rigid, highly cohesive packing. Once molten, [CHO]­[TYR] remained substantially more viscous than the other PILs across the studied temperature range, reflecting the combined influence of aromaticity and multiple hydrogen-bonding sites on liquid structuring.

Complementary flow curve measurements at 50 °C (Figure ) showed that all PILs, irrespective of structural class, exhibited nearly constant viscosity across the tested shear rate range (1–1000 s–1), indicating Newtonian behavior under these conditions. This suggests that the differences in viscosity arise from fundamental molecular interactions rather than shear-dependent structuring, in agreement with rheological characteristics commonly reported for choline amino acid-based ILs. ,

Variation of the dynamic viscosity of the choline-amino acid PILs with shear rate. The tests were conducted at 50 °C.

The dynamic viscosity trends follow an unambiguous structural hierarchy: compact, then branched aliphatic, then bulky aromatic/cyclic, in increasing order, reflecting the progressive increase in steric hindrance, hydrogen-bonding density, and supramolecular organization within the PIL matrix. Multifunctional anions, such as [CHO]­[ASP], deviate from this trend and display significantly higher viscosity due to strong hydrogen bonding and enhanced ionic networking. These findings reinforce the strong structure–property relationships governing ion mobility and flow behavior in PILs.

At room temperature, the conductivities spanned over 2 orders of magnitude, from as low as 0.46 μS·cm–1 for [CHO]­[HIS] to as high as 141.7 μS·cm–1 for [CHO]­[GLY]. These large differences can be rationalized by considering the structural features of the amino acid anions. Short-chain and compact anions such as [ALA] – and [GLY] – lead to markedly higher conductivities across all temperatures due to their reduced steric hindrance and weaker hydrogen bonding, which facilitate ion dissociation and charge transport. Conversely, anions with bulky aromatic moieties (PHE]−, [TYR]−, [HIS]−) or additional functional groups capable of extensive hydrogen bonding ([ASP]−) exhibited substantially lower conductivities, reflecting restricted ionic mobility and higher degrees of ion association.

As the temperature increased, the gap between high- and low-conductivity PILs immensely increased. At 100 °C, [CHO]­[GLY] and [CHO]­[ALA] reached values above 2000 μS·cm–1, approaching those of classical room-temperature PILs reported in the literature. , In contrast, [CHO]­[LYS] remained below 5 μS·cm–1 even at 100 °C, underscoring the impact of the long, basic side chain in lysine, which promotes strong ion pairing and suppresses charge transport. , The slight variation observed at the highest temperature is within the experimental uncertainty and does not alter the overall conductivity trend. Similarly, [CHO]­[ASP] and [CHO]­[HIS] showed only modest increases in conductivity with heating, remaining an order of magnitude lower than the small-anion PILs, in line with previous observations that multiple hydrogen-bonding sites or aromatic stacking reduce ion dissociation efficiency.

Interestingly, the other PILs with aromatic rings ([CHO]­[PHE], [CHO]­[TYR]) displayed intermediate behavior. Although their conductivities were low at 25 °C, they rose sharply with temperature, with [CHO]­[TYR] surpassing 380 μS·cm–1 at 100 °C. Similar temperature-activated conductivity trends have been reported in experimental studies on choline-amino acid PILs containing aromatic anions, where restricted ionic mobility at low temperatures, attributed to π–π stacking and strong hydrogen-bonding networks, is progressively relieved upon heating, leading to enhanced charge transport. , Such trends have also been observed in other amino acid ILs with aromatic residues, ,,

The thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) results reveal a clear correlation between the thermal stability of the choline amino acid PILs and the chemical nature of their anionic counterparts. In contrast, [CHO]­[LYS] showed the lowest thermal stability with the lowest onset temperature (173 °C), reflecting the chemical lability of its flexible, highly basic side chains with a reactive primary amine, which facilitates early deamination or decarboxylation under heating. Similarly, [CHO]­[PRO] displayed a comparatively low onset temperature (179 °C), likely due to the conformational constraints of its cyclic structure, which can promote intramolecular ring-opening pathways.

Putting things together, these data indicate that thermal stability in choline amino acid PILs is governed principally by the presence and type of polar functional groups (which can increase T onset by strengthening ion pairing), side-chain bulk/rigidity (which impedes facile fragmentation , ), and aromatic/heteroaromatic motifs (which both stabilize by resonance and introduce distinct decomposition pathways, ,, ). This structure-dependent decomposition behavior has direct implications for selecting PILs for high-temperature lubrication applications or processing. Branched aliphatic and certain multifunctional anions combine high Tonset with simpler decomposition chemistry, whereas aromatic anions, while thermally resilient initially, may yield complex volatile products upon prolonged heating. , The measured onset decomposition temperatures (173–221 °C) indicate thermal stability suitable for many moderate-temperature lubrication applications, including hydraulic systems, industrial gearboxes, rolling-element bearings, electric motor bearings, and circulating lubrication systems, where bulk lubricant temperatures typically remain well below the onset of thermal degradation.

Differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) graphs in Figure predominantly exhibited glass transition temperatures (T g ) for the PILs, with no observable melting or crystallization events within the studied temperature range, consistent with previous reports. The only exception was [CHO]­[TYR], which showed a distinct melting transition, reflecting its solid-state nature at room temperature. T g values ranged from −71 °C for [CHO]­[GLY] to −35 °C for [CHO]­[TYR], indicating substantial variation in molecular mobility and cohesive energy density across the series. The low T g values observed for simple or small aliphatic anions such as [CHO]­[GLY] (−71 °C) and [CHO]­[ALA] (−68 °C) reflect enhanced segmental flexibility and weak intermolecular interactions, which facilitate ion rearrangement upon cooling. This trend has been reported in detailed DSC studies of choline amino acid PILs, which show low glass transition temperatures, indicating segmental flexibility and weak ion pairing. In contrast, PILs derived from bulkier aliphatic residues such as [CHO]­[LEU] (−60 °C) and [CHO]­[ILEU] (−63 °C) exhibit slightly higher T g values, attributable to steric hindrance and tighter ionic packing induced by branched alkyl side chains. , This restricted mobility increases the energy barrier for conformational rearrangements at the glass transition. Similarly, [CHO]­[LYS] (−58 °C), containing a polar amino side chain, shows an elevated T g due to additional Coulombic and hydrogen-bonding interactions involving the terminal amine, which increase cohesive network rigidity.

Solubility

The miscibility behavior of the choline amino acid PILs in polar (EST) and nonpolar (PAO) base oils provides important insight into their compatibility and potential as lubricant additives. Tables 2S and 3S in the Supporting Information document show visual images of the mixtures of PILs with nonpolar base oil (PAO) and polar base oil (EST), respectively, taken immediately after mixing, 1 h, 24 h, 7 days, and 30 days after mixing. In general, eight of the ten PILs demonstrated excellent solubility and long-term stability in both base oils, maintaining homogeneity for up to one month without any visible phase separation or sedimentation. This strong miscibility can be attributed to the balance between the hydrophilic choline cation and the amphiphilic nature of the amino acid anions, , which enables favorable interactions across oils of varying polarity. However, [CHO]­[PRO] and [CHO]­[PHE] showed poor miscibility in the polar ester base oil (EST), remaining phase-separated from the onset while they were miscible in the nonpolar oil, PAO. This behavior can be attributed to differences in side chain polarity among the amino acid anions. Proline possesses a cyclic, secondary amine side chain (pyrrolidine ring) that limits hydrogen bonding and reduces overall polarity, whereas phenylalanine has a bulky aromatic (benzyl) side chain that is strongly hydrophobic and reduces polarity. The lack of sufficient polar functional groups in both anions weakens their interaction with the polar ester base oil, resulting in poor miscibility. Furthermore, the successful dispersion of [CHO]­[PRO] and [CHO]­[PHE] in PAO suggests that complete polarity matching is not the sole factor governing miscibility. In the nonpolar PAO environment, the hydrophobic pyrrolidine and benzyl side chains can interact favorably with the hydrocarbon matrix through dispersion forces, whereas the more polar ester base oil relies to a greater extent on dipolar and hydrogen-bonding interactions for solvation. The inability of these PILs to participate effectively in such interactions likely reduces their thermodynamic stability in the ester phase, leading to an immediate phase separation.

The results, therefore, indicate that while most choline amino acid PILs are versatile additives compatible with both polar and nonpolar environments, structural features that enhance hydrophobicity or disrupt hydrogen-bonding interactions can significantly limit miscibility, particularly in polar lubricants. It should be noted that the present miscibility assessment is based on visual observation of phase stability and therefore provides a qualitative measure of additive compatibility. More detailed evaluation using quantitative techniques such as turbidity analysis, optical transmittance measurements, or centrifugation testing would provide additional insight into long-term formulation stability and will be considered in future studies.

Wettability

Table shows contact angles that reveal pronounced structure-dependent wetting behavior of the choline amino-acid PILs on steel and aluminum substrates at room temperature. On steel, the most wetting liquids (lowest contact angles) are those with flexible or small side chains: [CHO]­[LYS] (33°), [CHO]­[ILEU] (33°), [CHO]­[LEU] (34°), and [CHO]­[GLY] (37°). This indicates relatively strong adhesive interactions between the liquid and the iron oxide surface. In contrast, anions bearing multiple polar or aromatic functionalities, such as [CHO]­[ASP] and [CHO]­[HIS], give much larger contact angles on steel (72°), suggesting weaker macroscopic wetting despite their intrinsic polarity. On aluminum, the pattern shifts. [CHO]­[ALA] wets best (26°), while [CHO]­[LYS], [CHO]­[PRO], and [CHO]­[HIS] show comparatively poor wetting (about 65–67°). These differences reflect the interplay of anion side-chain polarity and hydrogen-bonding ability and specific Lewis acid/base interactions with the native oxide (Al2O3 vs Fe-oxide). For example, [CHO]­[LYS] shows excellent wetting on steel but poor wetting on aluminum, consistent with stronger amine–Fe coordination or surface complexation on iron oxides than on the more strongly Lewis acidic aluminum oxide, where protonation state and steric effects may limit binding.

These macroscopic contact-angle trends are also consistent with side-chain chemistry. Branched aliphatic anions in [CHO]­[LEU] and [CHO]­[ILEU] present moderate wettability on both metals, likely because their single carboxylate head anchors to the oxide, while the hydrophobic tail promotes spreading through van der Waals packing. Aromatic anions like [CHO]­[PHE] produce intermediate contact angles (58° on steel, 46° on Al), reflecting competing effects of carboxylate anchoring and hydrophobic aromatic packing. The anomalously high contact angles for [CHO]­[ASP] and [CHO]­[HIS] on steel suggest that strong, localized adsorption (e.g., formation of a dense, possibly less-wetting chelate layer) or incomplete dissociation/protonation at the interface may reduce macroscopic spreading; , a reminder that a low contact angle does not always correlate simply with anion polarity.

The observed wetting behavior is therefore governed by a combination of oxide surface chemistry and amino acid side-chain functionality, including polarity, hydrogen-bonding capability, steric effects, and potential surface complexation. While contact angle measurements provide useful insight into metal–liquid interactions, complementary surface energy analysis using multiple probe liquids would enable a more quantitative assessment of the underlying interfacial mechanisms and warrants future investigation.

Corrosion

Among all samples, [CHO]­[GLY] and [CHO]­[ALA] exhibited the most pronounced increases in roughness at both exposure times, which further increased from 15 to 30 days. The magnitude of the roughness change suggests localized surface modification consistent with corrosion or surface etching. In contrast, PILs containing longer aliphatic chains ([CHO]­[LEU] and [CHO]­[ILEU]), multifunctional anions ([CHO]­[ASP]), or aromatic/cyclic groups ([CHO]­[PRO], [CHO]­[PHE], [CHO]­[HIS], and [CHO]­[TYR]) showed comparatively minor roughness variations, indicating limited surface degradation.

The progressive roughness increase for [CHO]­[GLY] and [CHO]­[ALA] suggests that smaller, highly polar anions promote stronger interactions with the copper oxide surface, potentially facilitating localized dissolution–reprecipitation processes or surface complexation. The absence of significant mass loss indicates that the corrosion is superficial, likely involving oxide-layer modification rather than bulk copper removal. It is also noteworthy that [CHO]­[GLY] and [CHO]­[ALA] contained the highest residual water contents among the investigated PILs. Because water can facilitate ion transport and promote electrochemical oxidation processes at copper surfaces, the enhanced roughness and oxide formation observed for these PILs may not arise solely from the intrinsic characteristics of the amino acid anions. Instead, the corrosion behavior likely reflects a combined effect of high ionic mobility, strong surface interaction, and elevated residual moisture content. Further studies employing controlled water concentrations would be required to decouple these contributions quantitatively.

The stronger oxide signatures and increased intensity of COO–-related bands for [CHO]­[GLY] and [CHO]­[ALA] suggest coordination between the carboxylate group and surface copper sites, potentially destabilizing the native oxide layer and promoting further oxidation. This interpretation is consistent with their high ionic conductivity and low viscosity, which may enhance ion mobility and facilitate interfacial reactions.

In contrast, PILs with branched aliphatic side chains ([CHO]­[LEU], [CHO]­[ILEU]) and bulky/aromatic systems ([CHO]­[PHE], [CHO]­[HIS], [CHO]­[TYR], [CHO]­[PRO]) exhibit weaker oxide signatures and smaller spectral deviations from the control surface. These anions likely provide steric shielding or form more stable interfacial films that limit direct copper–anion interactions. Particularly for aromatic systems, π–π interactions and hydrogen bonding may promote the formation of a passivating surface layer that suppresses further oxidation.

In addition to the surface roughness and Raman analyses, the copper surfaces exposed to the PILs were also visually inspected. Tables 4S and 5S show images of copper surfaces exposed to the PILs and the sample masses before and after the corrosion test after 15 days of corrosion test. Discoloration of the surfaces was observed after the corrosion test on some samples, which disappeared once the samples were cleaned with an ultrasonic bath, as shown in Tables 4S and 5S. No significant changes in sample mass were observed, indicating that any corrosion processes were limited to surface modification rather than bulk material loss.

Corrosion behavior correlates strongly with anion structure. Small, highly mobile, and strongly coordinating anions (glycine and alanine) exhibited enhanced surface reactivity, resulting in increased surface roughness, oxide formation, and evidence of localized corrosion. These relatively simple structures, with small, highly polar side chains, promote strong ionic dissociation and proton activity in the liquid phase. This enhances electrochemical reactions at the copper interface, favoring oxidative pathways and the formation of Cu­(I)/Cu­(II) oxides. , In contrast, larger, hydrophobic, multifunctional, or aromatic anions reduce surface accessibility and limit interfacial chemical attack. These findings demonstrate that corrosion tendency in choline-amino acid PILs is governed not simply by polarity but by the combined effects of ion mobility, coordination strength, steric hindrance, and interfacial film formation.

Tribological Validation of Physicochemical Trends

To establish a link between the measured physicochemical properties and lubrication performance, tribological tests were conducted under steel–steel contact conditions. Friction measurements were limited to seven PILs ([CHO]­[GLY], [CHO]­[ALA], [CHO]­[LYS], [CHO]­[LEU], [CHO]­[ILEU], [CHO]­[PRO], and [CHO]­[PHE]) that were expected to operate predominantly in the boundary lubrication regime under the selected test conditions. The more viscous PILs, [CHO]­[ASP], [CHO]­[HIS], and [CHO]­[TYR], were not included in the friction comparison because their substantially higher viscosities are expected to promote mixed or hydrodynamic lubrication. The average coefficients of friction (COF) and wear volumes are presented in Figure .

Average friction coefficient (a) and wear volume (b) of the ten PILs on steel disks. The error bars represent standard deviations.

As shown in Figure a, all tested PILs exhibited relatively low and comparable COFs apart from [CHO]­[PRO] and [CHO]­[PHE], which exhibited slightly higher COFs. However, generally, the differences in COFs were modest suggesting that all seven PILs were capable of forming adsorbed boundary films that effectively reduced direct metal-to-metal contact. Under these conditions, the amino acid side-chain structure appears to have only a limited influence on the friction behavior.

A clearer structural effect was observed in the wear results (Figure b). The compact amino-acid PILs, [CHO]­[GLY] and [CHO]­[ALA], which exhibited the lowest viscosities and the highest ionic conductivities, produced the highest wear volumes. In contrast, the branched aliphatic PILs, particularly [CHO]­[LEU] and [CHO]­[ILEU], provided substantially better wear protection, with [CHO]­[ILEU] exhibiting the lowest wear volume among the tested PILs. This improvement is consistent with the viscosity trends discussed previously, where the larger and more hydrophobic branched side chains promoted stronger intermolecular interactions and increased resistance to film breakdown. The cyclic and aromatic PILs, [CHO]­[PRO] and [CHO]­[PHE], also exhibited relatively low wear volumes despite their slightly higher COFs, indicating that their more structured liquid networks remained effective in protecting the contact surfaces.

The wear behavior closely follows the physicochemical trends established throughout this study. PILs with low viscosity and high ionic mobility generally exhibited poorer wear resistance, whereas PILs with greater viscosity and stronger intermolecular interactions provided enhanced surface protection. These results support the structure–property relationships identified from the viscosity, conductivity, and thermal analyses and demonstrate that molecular architecture plays a key role in determining lubrication performance.

It should be emphasized that the tribological experiments reported here were conducted under a single set of operating conditions involving room-temperature reciprocating sliding and, therefore, do not constitute a comprehensive assessment of lubrication performance. Some of the PILs investigated in this work exhibited substantially different viscosities and were expected to operate under different lubrication regimes. Consequently, a rigorous comparison of lubrication performance would require systematic investigations across a broader range of temperatures, loads, sliding speeds, and slide-to-roll ratios, together with detailed analyses of lubrication regime transitions and tribofilm formation. Such an evaluation is beyond the scope of the present stud, but it represents an important direction for future work aimed at fully understanding the lubrication mechanisms of choline amino acid protic ionic liquids.

Implications for Tribological Application

The lubrication potential of the investigated choline-amino acid PILs can be rationalized through their physicochemical characteristics. Under boundary lubrication conditions, lubricant performance is governed primarily by molecular adsorption and tribochemical film formation rather than bulk hydrodynamics. , PILs exhibiting moderate viscosity and sufficient ionic mobility are expected to balance film persistence with rapid surface replenishment. In this context, small and compact anions such as [CHO]­[GLY] and [CHO]­[ALA], characterized by low viscosity and high ionic conductivity, provide enhanced ion transport and strong surface interactions through accessible carboxylate groups. However, their high mobility and strong coordination ability may also increase surface reactivity, as reflected in their greater corrosion tendency on copper and higher wear. Although [CHO]­[GLY] and [CHO]­[ALA] exhibited higher wear volumes as neat lubricants, their low viscosity and high ionic mobility suggest that they may be better suited for use as lubricant additives, where their surface activity can be exploited without compromising the flow properties of the base oil.

In contrast, PILs containing branched, bulky, or aromatic side chains ([CHO]­[LEU], [CHO]­[ILEU], [CHO]­[PHE], [CHO]­[HIS], [CHO]­[TYR], [CHO]­[PRO]) exhibit higher viscosities and lower ionic conductivities, indicating more structured hydrogen-bonding networks and reduced ion mobility. These features can promote the formation of more stable interfacial films and reduce aggressive metal–anion interactions. Additionally, higher thermal stability (as observed for [CHO]­[ASP] and aromatic systems) supports lubricant integrity at elevated temperatures, while wettability measurements confirm that effective spreading depends strongly on the interplay between side-chain polarity and substrate oxide chemistry. The data demonstrate that lubrication-relevant performance in choline-amino acid PILs is not governed by a single parameter but by a coordinated balance between ion mobility, structural rigidity, interfacial adsorption strength, and chemical stability, all of which are directly dictated by amino acid side-chain structure. Influence of Chemical Structures on the Physicochemical and Corrosion Behaviors of Choline Amino Acid PILs

Compact and highly polar anions, such as those of glycine and alanine, produced low-viscosity, high-conductivity liquids with strong surface affinity but increased surface reactivity toward copper. In contrast, PILs containing branched aliphatic, bulky, or aromatic side chains exhibited higher viscosities and lower ionic conductivities, reflecting stronger supramolecular interactions and reduced ion mobility. These structural features generally correlated with improved surface stability and reduced corrosion tendency. Multifunctional anions, such as the aspartic acid anion, demonstrated adequate thermal stability for moderate-temperature lubrication applications, whereas aromatic systems contributed to increased glass transition temperatures and structural rigidity.

Importantly, corrosion behavior was shown to depend not solely on polarity but on the combined effects of ion mobility, coordination strength, steric shielding, and interfacial adsorption. The absence of significant mass loss across samples indicates that degradation processes were limited to surface-level modifications rather than bulk material dissolution. Wettability results further demonstrated that metal–liquid interactions are strongly substrate-dependent, influenced by oxide chemistry and side-chain functionality.

This work demonstrates that choline–amino acid PILs can be rationally tuned through side-chain selection to balance mobility, thermal stability, interfacial affinity, and chemical compatibility. The comprehensive structure–property correlations established herein provide a molecular-level framework for the design of sustainable ionic liquid-based lubricants and lubricant additives. While the present study establishes clear structure–property relationships and provides preliminary evidence of lubrication potential, further investigations under representative boundary lubrication conditions, including detailed friction, wear, and tribofilm analyses, are required to fully validate the performance of these PILs as sustainable lubricant systems.
