Detecting Black Hole Microstates
Vijay Balasubramanian

Abstract
We demonstrate that the Euclidean two-point function of an appropriately chosen probe operator can detect the microstate of an asymptotically AdS black hole. This detection, which requires a tuned, state-dependent choice of probe, is the result of a new gravitational saddle, which dominates over the usual saddles. The gravitational result can be explicitly reproduced in the dual boundary CFT if we assume the eigenstate thermalization hypothesis. We also discuss a binary search protocol to detect the black hole microstate from a candidate list.

Introduction

Classically, there is no measurement at asymptotic infinity that detects the microstates that explain the Bekenstein-Hawking entropy of back holes.
Indeed, this is why black hole spacetimes have classical horizons.
Quantum mechanically we might hope that there would be some signal at infinity about the microstate, but any detection of such a signal must require non-perturbatively precise measurements or exponentially difficult computations
[
1
,
2
,
3
,
4
,
5
]
.

In this work, we examine these issues in an explicit construction via the Euclidean path integral of black hole microstates, namely the shell microstates
[
6
,
7
,
8
]
.
These microstates have coarse-grained descriptions as shells of dust propagating in the black hole interior, and are created in the dual field theory by the insertion of a heavy operator.
The results in
[
9
,
10
]
show that these states span the complete Hilbert space of states of one- and two-boundary quantum gravity.
Naively, they are overcomplete but non-perturbative effects in the gravitational path integral reduce the dimension of the space to precisely what we expect from the Bekenstein-Hawking formula.
These results have been extended to include quantum corrections to the entropy
[
11
]
and out-of-equilibrium settings
[
12
]
.
Thus the naive effective field theory (EFT) Hilbert space of the gravitational theory has enormously many null states, as expected, for example, from the toy model in
[
13
]
.
One consequence within the AdS/CFT correspondence is that the encoding of the bulk EFT of the black hole into the dual field theory will be non-isometric
[
14
]
.
Thus, reconstruction or detection of the microstate must be state-dependent
[
15
]
.
We will see this explicitly in our setting.

Specifically, we identify Euclidean computables that are sensitive to the shell microstates.
If we have a microstate
\ket{\Psi_{\mathcal{O}}}
corresponding to the shell operator
\mathcal{O}
, then the single-sided Euclidean two-point function
\left\langle\Psi_{\mathcal{O}}\right|\mathbb{1}_{L}\otimes\psi(\tau_{2})\psi^{\dagger}(\tau_{1})_{R}\ket{\Psi_{\mathcal{O}}}
is sensitive to the choice of the probe operator
\psi

For suitably chosen Euclidean times
\tau_{1},\tau_{2}
, this two-point function has a huge spike if and only if the probe operator
\psi
matches the shell operator
\mathcal{O}
, precisely as anticipated in
[
16
]
.
The key ingredient is a new gravitational saddle – dubbed the
annihilation
saddle – wherein two probe operators in the Euclidean past and future “annihilate” the corresponding shell operators.
This saddle is only present when the shell and probe operators are not orthogonal.
For appropriately tuned probe insertion times, the annihilation saddle dominates over the usual
propagation
saddle in which the shell and probe operators do not interact. The sharp peak in the Euclidean two-point function signals whether a black hole is in a given shell microstate
\ket{\Psi_{\mathcal{O}}}
or not. Given a finite list of candidate shell operators
\{\mathcal{O}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{O}_{N}\}
, we can then use a binary search to detect which operator corresponds to the black hole microstate.

In Section
2
, we use the gravitational path integral to compute the Euclidean two-point function and demonstrate the presence of the annihilation saddle for a suitable probe operator. We demonstrate this explicitly in two settings – the limit of large shell mass in arbitrary dimensions and for any mass in the
(2+1)
-dimensional case. We also discuss how to use a binary search protocol to detect the black hole microstate from a given set of candidates. In Section
3
, we reach the same conclusions from the boundary perspective by assuming the Eigenstate Thermalization Hypothesis (ETH).
In Section
4
, we show that our results to robust to statistical variance arising from ensemble averaging.
We conclude by discussing some directions for the future in Section
5
.

Gravity Calculation

We consider states in a theory of gravity with two asymptotic AdS boundaries with topology

S^{d-1}\times\mathbb{R}
, where
\mathbb{R}
represents time. Quantum gravity with
such boundary conditions is equivalent to the physics of two copies of a Conformal Field Theory (CFT), labeled
\mathrm{CFT}_{\mathrm{L}}
and
\mathrm{CFT}_{\mathrm{R}}
, living at the left/right boundaries and with Hamiltonians
H_{\mathrm{L}}=H_{\mathrm{R}}=H

H_{L}\ket{a,b}=E_{a}\ket{a,b},\quad\quad H_{R}\ket{a,b}=E_{b}\ket{a,b}.

(2.1)

Following
[
6

7

\beta_{\mathrm{R}}

\beta_{\mathrm{L}}

\ket{\Psi_{\mathcal{O}}}=\ket{\rho_{\tilde{\beta}_{\mathrm{L}}/2}\mathcal{O}\rho_{\tilde{\beta}_{\mathrm{R}}/2}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{Z_{1}}}\sum_{a,b}e^{-\frac{1}{2}(\tilde{\beta}_{\mathrm{L}}E_{a}+\tilde{\beta}_{\mathrm{R}}E_{b})}\mathcal{O}_{ab}\ket{a,b}\,,

(2.2)

where the normalization is

Z_{1}=\Tr\left(\mathcal{O}^{\dagger}e^{-\tilde{\beta}_{\mathrm{L}}H}\mathcal{O}e^{-\tilde{\beta}_{\mathrm{R}}H}\right)\,.

(2.3)

These fixed temperature states (
2.2
) are black hole microstates within the canonical ensemble. The authors of
[
9
,
10
]
show that these states form a basis, and also analyze the microcanonical and single-sided microstates. See

18

for discussion of the existence and stability of these and related states.
Note that the Euclidean times
\tilde{\beta}_{\mathrm{R},\mathrm{L}}

of the black holes.

Let us describe the geometry associated with this state.
We prepare the state in Euclidean signature and continue to Lorentzian signature at the

On the Euclidean boundary, we insert the operator

2.1
Detecting microstates

The states constructed above have bulk duals that contribute to the semi-classical black hole Hilbert space and thus are valid microstates. Indeed, they form a complete basis, as shown in
[
9
,
10
,
22
]
. A natural question to ask is whether or not these microstates can be detected by asymptotic correlation functions. The reason to think this might be possibe is that, while the classical geometry outisde the horizon contains no trace of the shell behind it, the quantum state does
[
6
]
, so that there is a form of ‘‘quantum hair’’. In this section, we address this question by computing the single-sided Euclidean two-point function
2
2

2

A related computation that is not sensitive to the black hole microstate is the thermal two-point function

\frac{1}{\Tr e^{-\tilde{\beta}_{\mathrm{R}}H}}\Tr\left(e^{-\tilde{\beta}_{\mathrm{R}}H}\psi(\tau_{2})\psi^{\dagger}(\tau_{1})\right)
.

\left\langle\Psi_{\mathcal{O}}\right|\mathbb{1}_{L}\otimes\psi(\tau_{2})\psi^{\dagger}(\tau_{1})_{R}\ket{\Psi_{\mathcal{O}}}=\frac{1}{Z_{1}}\Tr\left(\mathcal{O}^{\dagger}e^{-(\tilde{\beta}_{\mathrm{R}}/2)H}\psi(\tau_{2})\psi^{\dagger}(\tau_{1})e^{-(\tilde{\beta}_{\mathrm{R}}/2)H}\mathcal{O}e^{-\tilde{\beta}_{\mathrm{L}}H}\right)\,,

(2.17)

and showing how this quantity depends on the relationship between the interior shell operator
\mathcal{O}
and the probe operator
\psi
.

Consider an arbitrary probe of the form

\psi=w_{\mathcal{O}}\mathcal{O}+\sum_{i}w_{i}\phi_{i}\,,

(2.18)

where
w_{*}\in[0,1]
represent weights for the operators and
\{\mathcal{O},\phi_{i}\}
is some orthonormal basis of operators. (We have absorbed phases in coefficients into the definitions of the operators.)
One way to construct such a basis is to begin with a complete set of shell states, and to then use a Gram-Schmidt procedure to obtain an orthogonal set of states. We can insist that
{\cal O}
is a specific shell operator if we want, but many of the
\phi_{i}

However, the details of these
\phi_{i}
operators are not important to us. All we require is that an orthonormal basis exists with
\mathcal{O}
being a basis element, so any operator
\psi
can be decomposed into a component along
\mathcal{O}
and a component in its orthogonal complement.

We can assume that the probe operator is normalized, so the weights satisfy
w_{\mathcal{O}}^{2}+\sum_{i}w_{i}^{2}=1
.
It follows that the numerator of the two-point function in (
2.17
) can be expressed as

\mathcal{T}_{1}=w_{\mathcal{O}}^{2}\Tr\left(\mathcal{O}^{\dagger}e^{-\tilde{\beta}_{\mathrm{R}}H/2}\mathcal{O}(\tau_{2})\mathcal{O}^{\dagger}(\tau_{1})e^{-\tilde{\beta}_{\mathrm{R}}H/2}\mathcal{O}e^{-\tilde{\beta}_{\mathrm{L}}H}\right)\\
+\sum_{i}w_{i}^{2}\Tr\left(\mathcal{O}^{\dagger}e^{-\tilde{\beta}_{\mathrm{R}}H/2}\phi_{i}(\tau_{2})\phi_{i}^{\dagger}(\tau_{1})e^{-\tilde{\beta}_{\mathrm{R}}H/2}\mathcal{O}e^{-\tilde{\beta}_{\mathrm{L}}H}\right)\,.

(2.19)

The annihilation saddle allows us to detect the state, since it exists if and only if the probe operator matches the interior shell operator.

2.2
Large mass limit

We first demonstrate that one can detect a particular black hole microstate explicitly in the limit of large shell rest mass,
m\rightarrow\infty
.
This is the limit in which the shell mass significantly exceeds the asymptotic mass of the black hole.
Crucially, in this limit, the shell action (
2.12
) no longer depends on the inverse temperatures of the Euclidean geometries
[
6
]
,

I_{\mathrm{s}}(\beta_{R},\beta_{L})\approx I_{\mathrm{s}}(m)=2m\log R_{*}\,.

(2.25)

where
R_{*}\sim Gm
.
Moreover, the Euclidean time elapsed by the shell trajectory vanishes,
\Delta\tau(\beta_{R};\beta_{L})\approx 0
.
Therefore, using (
2.9
), it follows that
\tilde{\beta}_{\mathrm{L}}\approx\beta_{\mathrm{L}}
and
\tilde{\beta}_{\mathrm{R}}\approx\beta_{\mathrm{R}}
respectively.

We can now evaluate the saddle point contributions to the two-point function.
Each contribution can be expressed as a product of partition functions for the disks and shells involved in each diagram. This simplification arises because the shell action
I_{\mathrm{s}}
is independent of the disk parameters.
Therefore, the normalization factor simplifies to

Z_{1}\approx Z(\tilde{\beta}_{\mathrm{L}})Z(\tilde{\beta}_{\mathrm{R}})e^{-I_{\mathrm{s}}(m)}\,,

(2.26)

where we have omitted subleading contributions, including the one-loop corrections.
For the annihilation and propagation saddles, the saddle point equations are

\displaystyle\hat{\beta}_{\mathrm{T}}=\tilde{\beta}_{\mathrm{R}}/2-\tau_{2}\,,\qquad\hat{\beta}_{\mathrm{M}}=\tilde{\beta}_{\mathrm{L}}+\tau_{2}-\tau_{1}\,,\qquad\hat{\beta}_{\mathrm{B}}=\tilde{\beta}_{\mathrm{R}}/2+\tau_{1}\,.

(2.27)

\displaystyle\hat{\beta}_{\mathrm{C}}=\tilde{\beta}_{\mathrm{R}}-(\tau_{2}-\tau_{1})\,,\qquad\qquad\hat{\beta}_{\mathrm{R}}=\tau_{2}-\tau_{1}\,,\qquad\qquad\hat{\beta}_{\mathrm{L}}=\tilde{\beta}_{\mathrm{L}}\,.

(2.28)

Thus, these saddle point contributions simplify to

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}^{P}_{1}

\displaystyle\approx Z(\hat{\beta}_{\mathrm{L}})Z(\hat{\beta}_{\mathrm{C}})Z(\hat{\beta}_{\mathrm{R}})e^{-2I_{\mathrm{s}}(m)}\,,

(2.29)

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}^{A}_{1}

\displaystyle\approx Z(\hat{\beta}_{\mathrm{T}})Z(\hat{\beta}_{\mathrm{M}})Z(\hat{\beta}_{\mathrm{B}})e^{-2I_{\mathrm{s}}(m)}\,.

(2.30)

The explicit answers for
(d+1)
-dimensions are

\displaystyle\begin{split}\log\mathcal{T}^{A}_{1}\approx\frac{V_{\Omega}}{8G}\Biggl[&\left(\frac{2\pi}{\frac{\tilde{\beta}_{\mathrm{R}}}{2}-\tau_{2}}\right)^{d-1}-\left(\frac{2\pi}{\frac{\tilde{\beta}_{\mathrm{R}}}{2}-\tau_{2}}\right)^{d-3}+\left(\frac{2\pi}{\tilde{\beta}_{\mathrm{L}}+\tau_{2}-\tau_{1}}\right)^{d-1}-\left(\frac{2\pi}{\tilde{\beta}_{\mathrm{L}}+\tau_{2}-\tau_{1}}\right)^{d-3}\\

where the first term on the right side comes from the disk actions and the second term comes from the shell actions.

\mathcal{T}_{1}^{A}

This can be achieved by tuning the Euclidean insertion times
\tau_{1}
and

for the probe operators.
Note that the annihilation saddle contribution
\mathcal{T}_{1}^{A}
has a divergence at
\tau_{2}=-\tau_{1}=\frac{\tilde{\beta}_{\mathrm{R}}}{2}

\mathcal{T}_{1}^{P}

close to this divergence.
Apart from the Hawking temperature, no specific knowledge about the shell operator is needed for this tuning.
As mentioned, this tuning does not work in the Lorentzian correlators – the divergence in question can only be achieved in the Euclidean correlator.

More concretely, the explicit answers in the simplest case of
(2+1)
-dimensions are

\displaystyle\log\mathcal{T}^{P}_{1}

\displaystyle\approx\frac{\pi^{2}}{4G}\left(\frac{1}{\tilde{\beta}_{\mathrm{R}}-(\tau_{2}-\tau_{1})}+\frac{1}{\tau_{2}-\tau_{1}}+\frac{1}{\tilde{\beta}_{\mathrm{L}}}\right)+\frac{1}{32G}(\tilde{\beta}_{\mathrm{L}}+\tilde{\beta}_{\mathrm{R}})+4m\log Gm\,,

(2.33)

\displaystyle\log\mathcal{T}^{A}_{1}

\displaystyle\approx\frac{\pi^{2}}{4G}\left(\frac{1}{\tilde{\beta}_{\mathrm{R}}/2-\tau_{2}}+\frac{1}{\tilde{\beta}_{\mathrm{R}}/2+\tau_{1}}+\frac{1}{\tilde{\beta}_{\mathrm{L}}+\tau_{2}-\tau_{1}}\right)+\frac{1}{32G}(\tilde{\beta}_{\mathrm{L}}+\tilde{\beta}_{\mathrm{R}})+4m\log Gm\,.

(2.34)

The switchover of dominance between these saddles occurs when the difference,

\log\mathcal{T}^{P}_{1}-\log\mathcal{T}^{A}_{1}\approx\frac{\pi^{2}}{4G}\bigg[\left(\frac{1}{\tilde{\beta}_{\mathrm{R}}-(\tau_{2}-\tau_{1})}+\frac{1}{\tau_{2}-\tau_{1}}+\frac{1}{\tilde{\beta}_{\mathrm{L}}}\right)\\
-\left(\frac{1}{\tilde{\beta}_{\mathrm{R}}/2-\tau_{2}}+\frac{1}{\tilde{\beta}_{\mathrm{R}}/2+\tau_{1}}+\frac{1}{\tilde{\beta}_{\mathrm{L}}+\tau_{2}-\tau_{1}}\right)\bigg]\,,

(2.35)

vanishes. When this difference is positive, the propagation saddle dominates; otherwise, the annihilation saddle dominates.
Clearly, rescaling all parameters

,
\tilde{\beta}_{\mathrm{L}}
,
\tau_{1}
,
\tau_{2}
by the same factor does not affect the sign of this difference.
Thus, we can set
\tilde{\beta}_{\mathrm{R}}=1
and then study the effects of varying the other parameters.

2.3
(2+1)-dimensional case for generic mass

We would like to understand if our general results in the large mass limit extend to generic interior shell operators that are sufficiently heavy to backreact on the geometry.
First, we need to solve for the saddle point configuration at arbitrary shell mass.
Thus, we have to compute the inverse temperatures of the Euclidean disks, i.e.
\hat{\beta}
’s, by solving the saddle point equations in (
2.22
) and (
2.23
).
This cannot be done analytically because the shell propagation time
\Delta\tau
depends implicitly on the inverse temperatures.
Hence, we resort to numerical analysis, which is difficult in arbitrary spacetime dimensions because the implicit
\hat{\beta}
-dependence is more complicated.
Consequently, we work in
(2+1)

[

\Delta\tau(\beta_{\mathrm{R}};\beta_{\mathrm{L}})=\frac{\beta_{\mathrm{R}}}{\pi}\arcsin\left(\frac{r_{\mathrm{R}}}{R_{*}}\right).

(2.36)

\psi^{\dagger}(\tau_{2})

2.4
Detecting the microstate from a candidate list

Next, we demonstrate how an observer might detect a particular fixed temperature black hole microstate given a finite list of such microstates where only one matches the target of the search.
First, consider the simplest case in which we are given a list of
N
such shell states with corresponding operators
\mathcal{O}_{i}
for
i\in\{1,\dots,N\}
– we are told that exactly one of them corresponds to the black hole microstate and that there are no overlaps between any of these
N
states.
The other states are not required to be shell states in this first instance.
In this scenario, one can perform a simple binary search to detect the microstate.

We choose a probe operator given by a uniform linear combination of the first
\lfloor N/2\rfloor
states

\psi_{1}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\lfloor N/2\rfloor}}\sum_{i=1}^{\lfloor N/2\rfloor}\mathcal{O}_{i}.

(2.37)

We input this probe operator into our Euclidean two-point function (
2.17
) with appropriately chosen Euclidean preparation times.
If we detect a signal from the annihilation saddle, indicating that one of the candidates matches the interior shell operator, then we repeat this search protocol on these first
\lfloor\frac{N}{2}\rfloor
operators.
Otherwise, we take the second set of operators and do the same.
Thus, we will find the operator that matches the interior shell operator in
O(\log_{2}N)
steps.
Note that at each step of this protocol, we need to compute the Euclidean two-point function in the shell microstate being detected.

Now, let us relax the constraint that other operators have no overlap.
It was shown in
[
6
]
that two arbitrary shell states have an exponentially small overlap.
These overlaps will mean that we also get an exponentially small annihilation saddle contribution from each of these operators.
Since these overlaps are exponentially suppressed, the binary search protocol in
O(\log_{2}N)
steps will still be successful as long as
N
is parametrically smaller than
e^{S_{\mathrm{BH}}}
.

3
Boundary detection via eigenstate thermalization hypothesis

In this section, we study black hole microstate detection from the perspective of the dual boundary theory.
Having found the annihilation and propagation saddle in the bulk AdS theory, a natural next question is if we can find the corresponding saddles in the dual CFT.
The current understanding is that the semiclassical gravitational path integral computes some kind of coarse-grained average over an underlying ensemble, although precisely what this ensemble consists of is not yet settled

[
24
,
25
,
26
,
27
,
13
,
28
]
. In the AdS/CFT correspondence, there is evidence that the coarse-graining in question occurs, at least for relatively simple probe operators, because CFT states dual to heavy AdS configurations should satisfy the Eigenstate Thermalization Hypothesis (ETH). ETH provides a coarse-grained statistical description that takes a universal form
[
29
,
6
,
30
,
31
,
32
,
33
,
34
]
: the expectation values of operators in the dual CFT are given by a diagonal matrix comprised of the microcanonical expectation values plus corrections that are exponentially suppressed.
This idea has been studied in great detail in JT gravity
[
35
,
36
,
37
]
.
The ETH ansatz states that matrix elements of any operator in the energy basis takes the form
[
38
,
39
,
40
]

3
3

3

See also
[
41
]
for a another approach.

\bra{a}\psi\ket{b}=\overline{\psi}(E_{a})\delta_{ab}+e^{-f^{\psi}(E_{a},E_{b})/2}R^{\psi}_{ab},

(3.1)

where
\overline{\psi}(E_{a})
is the average value of the operator in the microcanonical energy band and
f^{\psi}(E_{a},E_{b})
characterizes the variance.
Both of these functions are expected to be smooth and depend on the details of the operator and the system.

R_{ab}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
is a complex Gaussian random matrix that gives a statistical interpretation to the corrections.
This ETH ansatz has been extensively used to study the ensemble averages that are computed by the gravitational path integral
[
6
,
7
,
42
,
43
,
44
,
45
]
.

Recall that the fixed temperature black hole microstate is given by

\ket{\Psi}=\ket{\rho_{\tilde{\beta}_{\mathrm{L}}/2}\mathcal{O}\rho_{\tilde{\beta}_{\mathrm{R}}/2}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{Z_{1}}}\sum_{a,b}e^{-\frac{1}{2}(\tilde{\beta}_{\mathrm{L}}E_{a}+\tilde{\beta}_{\mathrm{R}}E_{b})}\mathcal{O}_{ab}\ket{a,b}\,.

(3.2)

Using (
3.1
) for the operator
\mathcal{O}
, this state can be written as

\ket{\Psi}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{Z_{1}}}\sum_{a,b}e^{-\frac{1}{2}(\tilde{\beta}_{\mathrm{L}}E_{a}+\tilde{\beta}_{\mathrm{R}}E_{b}-f^{\mathcal{O}}(E_{a},E_{b}))}R_{ab}\ket{a,b},

(3.3)

where the normalization is given by

Z_{1}=\text{tr}\left(\mathcal{O}^{\dagger}e^{-\tilde{\beta}_{\mathrm{L}}H}\mathcal{O}e^{-\tilde{\beta}_{\mathrm{R}}H}\right)\approx\sum_{a,b}e^{-\tilde{\beta}_{\mathrm{L}}E_{a}-\tilde{\beta}_{\mathrm{R}}E_{b}-f^{\mathcal{O}}(E_{a},E_{b})}.

(3.4)

Here we used the average value
\overline{\mathcal{O}}(E)=0
which is required for consistency with the gravitational calculations
[
29
]
.
Having prepared our states in this way, we can construct the density matrix representation for our pure state as

\rho=\ket{\Psi}\bra{\Psi},

(3.5)

which enables us to define the reduced density matrices on the left and right boundary CFTs by tracing out the complementary subsystem as

\displaystyle\rho_{R}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{Z_{1}}e^{-\frac{\tilde{\beta}_{\mathrm{R}}}{2}H}\mathcal{O}e^{-\tilde{\beta}_{\mathrm{L}}H}\mathcal{O}^{\dagger}e^{-\frac{\tilde{\beta}_{\mathrm{R}}}{2}H}.

(3.6)

We are now ready to compute the two-point functions for the boundary CFTs.
We will insert the probe operators on the right, so the relevant two-point function is

\begin{split}\langle\psi(\tau_{1})\psi^{\dagger}(\tau_{2})\rangle_{\rho_{R}}&=\text{tr}(\psi(\tau_{1})\psi^{\dagger}(\tau_{2})\rho_{R})\\
=\frac{1}{Z_{1}}\text{tr}&\left(e^{(\tau_{1}-\tilde{\beta}_{\mathrm{R}}/2)H}\psi e^{(\tau_{2}-\tau_{1})H}\psi^{\dagger}e^{-(\tau_{2}+\tilde{\beta}_{\mathrm{R}}/2)H}\mathcal{O}e^{-\tilde{\beta}_{\mathrm{L}}H}\mathcal{O}^{\dagger}\right)\,,\end{split}

(3.7)

Similar to the gravity computation, we define the numerator as

\mathcal{T}_{1}=\text{tr}\left(e^{(\tau_{1}-\tilde{\beta}_{\mathrm{R}}/2)H}\psi e^{(\tau_{2}-\tau_{1})H}\psi^{\dagger}e^{-(\tau_{2}+\tilde{\beta}_{\mathrm{R}}/2)H}\mathcal{O}e^{-\tilde{\beta}_{\mathrm{L}}H}\mathcal{O}^{\dagger}\right).

(3.8)

We can evaluate these terms by taking the continuum limit under which the energy difference between adjacent states is taken to zero, so the sum over energy eigenstates in the trace gets replaced by an integral
\sum_{a}\to\int\mathrm{d}E_{a}e^{S(E_{a})}
.
In this limit, the normalization in (
3.4
) becomes

Z_{1}=\int\mathrm{d}E_{a}\mathrm{d}E_{b}\,e^{S(E_{a})+S(E_{b})}e^{-(\tilde{\beta}_{\mathrm{R}}E_{a}+\tilde{\beta}_{\mathrm{L}}E_{b})-f^{\mathcal{O}}(E_{a},E_{b})}\,.

(3.9)

To evaluate these integrals, we use saddle point analysis.
The saddle point equations are

S^{\prime}(E_{a})=\tilde{\beta}_{\mathrm{R}}+\partial_{E_{a}}f^{\mathcal{O}}(E_{a},E_{b})\,,\qquad S^{\prime}(E_{b})=\tilde{\beta}_{\mathrm{L}}+\partial_{E_{b}}f^{\mathcal{O}}(E_{a},E_{b})\,.

(3.10)

Note that these equations match with the gravitational saddle point equations in (
2.9
) if we identify
f^{\mathcal{O}}(E_{a},E_{b})
appropriately.
Explicitly, we have

f^{\mathcal{O}}(E_{a},E_{b})=\frac{\Delta\tau(\beta(E_{a});\beta(E_{b}))}{\beta(E_{a})}S(E_{a})+\frac{\Delta\tau(\beta(E_{b});\beta(E_{a}))}{\beta(E_{b})}S(E_{b})+I_{\mathrm{s}}(\beta(E_{a}),\beta(E_{b})).

(3.11)

where the functional forms for
\Delta\tau
and
I_{\mathrm{s}}
are given in (
2.8
) and (
2.12
) respectively.
The conformal dimension of a
\text{CFT}_{d}
boundary operator is related to the shell mass in the bulk geometry via

\Delta=\frac{d}{2}+\sqrt{m^{2}+\frac{d^{2}}{4}}.

(3.12)

The large mass limit on the gravity side corresponds to the limit of large conformal dimension, and
\Delta\approx m
in this limit. The gravitational results can be recovered from these expressions by choosing
f^{\mathcal{O}}
to match the corresponding quantities computed on the bulk side. In the limit of large conformal dimension, this expression simplifies to

f^{\mathcal{O}}(E_{a},E_{b})\approx I_{\mathrm{s}}(m)=2\Delta_{\mathcal{O}}\log R_{*}\,,

(3.13)

because the shell propagation times vanish, and the shell action becomes independent of the inverse temperatures as in (
2.25
).
In the remainder of this section, we will be agnostic to the particular functional form of the
f^{\mathcal{O}}
-function.

After choosing the appropriate
f^{\mathcal{O}}
-function, the saddle point equations in (
3.10
) have the simultaneous solutions
E_{a}=M_{R}
and
E_{b}=M_{L}
,
corresponding to the masses of the left and right black holes, respectively. Also, we can identify the inverse temperatures of these black holes as

\beta_{\mathrm{L}}=S^{\prime}(M_{\mathrm{L}})\,,\quad\quad\beta_{\mathrm{R}}=S^{\prime}(M_{\mathrm{R}})\,.

(3.14)

This concludes the analysis for the normalization
Z_{1}
.

3.1
Light Operators

We first consider light probe operators.
Recall that from the bulk perspective, these light probes do not backreact on the geometry, so the gravitational saddle is unchanged. Thus, these probes should not allow us to detect the microstate. In this subsection, we confirm this by performing an ETH calculation in the dual CFT.

Employing the ETH ansatz in (
3.1
),

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}_{ab}

\displaystyle=\bra{a}\mathcal{O}\ket{b}\approx e^{-f^{\mathcal{O}}(E_{a},E_{b})/2}R^{\mathcal{O}}_{ab}\,,

(3.15)

\displaystyle\psi_{ab}

\displaystyle=\bra{a}\psi\ket{b}\approx\overline{\psi}(E_{a})\delta_{ab}+e^{-f^{\psi}(E_{a},E_{b})/2}R^{\psi}_{ab}\,,

where for the operator corresponding to the black hole shell microstate
\mathcal{O}
, we assume
\overline{\mathcal{O}}(E_{a})=0
.
This can be achieved by redefining the operator with the average value subtracted out.
Note that this
\overline{\mathcal{O}}(E_{a})=0
condition is imperative if we want the ETH results to match the gravity results
[
29
,
6
]
.
It is also possible to choose
\overline{\psi}(E_{a})=0
, but we will keep it arbitrary.

Since the operators
\psi
and
\mathcal{O}
are uncorrelated, we can assume that the random matrices
R^{\psi}
and
R^{\mathcal{O}}
are independent.
Under this assumption, the numerator term becomes

\mathcal{T}^{\psi}_{1}=\int\mathrm{d}E_{a}\mathrm{d}E_{d}e^{S(E_{a})+S(E_{d})}e^{-(\tilde{\beta}_{\mathrm{R}}E_{a}+\tilde{\beta}_{\mathrm{L}}E_{d})-f^{\mathcal{O}}(E_{a},E_{d})}\left|\overline{\psi}(E_{a})\right|^{2}\\
+\int\mathrm{d}E_{a}\mathrm{d}E_{b}\mathrm{d}E_{d}e^{S(E_{a})+S(E_{b})+S(E_{d})}e^{-(\tilde{\beta}_{\mathrm{R}}+\tau_{1}-\tau_{2})E_{a}-(\tau_{2}-\tau_{1})E_{b}-\tilde{\beta}_{\mathrm{L}}E_{d}-f^{\psi}(E_{a},E_{b})-f^{\mathcal{O}}(E_{a},E_{d})}.

(3.16)

We now take the limit in which the probe operator is light, which means that its conformal dimension is
\Delta_{\psi}=d+\epsilon
with
\epsilon
small.
In this limit, we can use saddle-point analysis to obtain

\mathcal{T}^{\psi}_{1}\approx Z_{1}\times\left(\left|\overline{\psi}(M_{R})\right|^{2}+\int\mathrm{d}E_{b}\,e^{S(E_{b})-(\tau_{2}-\tau_{1})(M_{R}-E_{b})-f^{\psi}(M_{R},E_{b})}\right)\,.

(3.17)

This is because the light operator must not affect the saddle-point configuration.
Thus, the Euclidean correlator evaluates to

\langle\psi(\tau_{1})\psi^{\dagger}(\tau_{2})\rangle_{\rho_{R}}=\left|\overline{\psi}(M_{R})\right|^{2}+\int\mathrm{d}E_{b}\,e^{S(E_{b})-(\tau_{2}-\tau_{1})(M_{R}-E_{b})-f^{\psi}(M_{R},E_{b})}\,.

(3.18)

We can compare this result to the thermal two point function

\langle\psi(\tau_{1})\psi^{\dagger}(\tau_{2})\rangle_{\text{th}}=\frac{\mathcal{T}_{0}}{Z_{0}}=\frac{1}{Z_{0}}\text{tr}\left(e^{(\tau_{1}-\tau_{2}-\tilde{\beta}_{\mathrm{R}})H}\psi e^{(\tau_{2}-\tau_{1})H}\psi^{\dagger}\right),

(3.19)

where the normalization is

Z_{0}=\text{tr}\left(e^{-\tilde{\beta}_{\mathrm{R}}H}\right)\,.

(3.20)

Using a similar analysis as above, we obtain that for a light probe operator
\psi

\mathcal{T}_{0}=\int\mathrm{d}E_{a}e^{S(E_{a})}e^{-\beta_{\mathrm{R}}E_{a}}\left|\overline{\psi}(E_{a})\right|^{2}+\int\mathrm{d}E_{a}\mathrm{d}E_{b}e^{S(E_{a})+S(E_{b})}e^{-(\tilde{\beta}_{\mathrm{R}}+\tau_{1}-\tau_{2})E_{a}-(\tau_{2}-\tau_{1})E_{b}-f^{\psi}(E_{a},E_{b})}\,,

(3.21)

and

Z_{0}=\int\mathrm{d}E_{a}\,e^{S(E_{a})-\beta_{\mathrm{R}}E_{a}}\,.

(3.22)

Taking the probe operator to be light, it follows that

\mathcal{T}_{0}\approx Z_{0}\times\left(\left|\overline{\psi}(M_{R})\right|^{2}+\int\mathrm{d}E_{b}\,e^{S(E_{b})-(\tau_{2}-\tau_{1})(M_{R}-E_{b})-f^{\psi}(M_{R},E_{b})}\right)\,,

(3.23)

so the thermal two-point function is

\langle\psi(\tau_{1})\psi^{\dagger}(\tau_{2})\rangle_{\text{th}}=\left|\overline{\psi}(M_{R})\right|^{2}+\int\mathrm{d}E_{b}\,e^{S(E_{b})-(\tau_{2}-\tau_{1})(M_{R}-E_{b})-f^{\psi}(M_{R},E_{b})}\,.

(3.24)

This matches the result in (
3.18
).
Evidently, it is impossible to distinguish between the black hole microstate and the thermal state by using the Euclidean two-point function for a light probe.

3.2
Heavy Operators

In this subsection, we consider the case of probe operators with large conformal dimension corresponding to massive shells that significantly backreact on the dual bulk geometry.
Thus, they should have a non-trivial impact on the saddle point, unlike the previous case of light probe operators.

First, we consider a heavy probe operator
\psi=\phi
that is orthogonal to
\mathcal{O}
, the interior shell operator that prepares the black hole microstate (
3.2
).
In this case, the ETH ansatz takes the following form

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}_{ab}

\displaystyle=\bra{a}\mathcal{O}\ket{b}\approx e^{-f^{\mathcal{O}}(E_{a},E_{b})/2}R^{\mathcal{O}}_{ab}\,,

(3.25)

\displaystyle\phi_{ab}

\displaystyle=\bra{a}\phi\ket{b}\approx e^{-f^{\phi}(E_{a},E_{b})/2}R^{\phi}_{ab}\,,

where we assume
\overline{\mathcal{O}}(E_{a})=\overline{\phi_{i}}(E_{a})=0
as explained earlier.
Note that the orthogonality condition above concretely means that the random matrices
R^{\phi}
and
R^{\mathcal{O}}
are completely independent.

The numerator of the two-point function for this heavy orthogonal probe is

\mathcal{T}^{\phi}_{1}=\int\mathrm{d}E_{a}\mathrm{d}E_{b}\mathrm{d}E_{d}\,e^{S(E_{a})+S(E_{b})+S(E_{d})}e^{-(\tilde{\beta}_{\mathrm{R}}E_{a}+\tilde{\beta}_{\mathrm{L}}E_{d})+(\tau_{2}-\tau_{1})(E_{a}-E_{b})-f^{\mathcal{O}}(E_{a},E_{d})}e^{-f^{\phi}(E_{a},E_{b})}\,.

(3.26)

As earlier, we can evaluate this using a saddle point analysis.
The saddle point equations are

\begin{split}S^{\prime}(E_{a})&=\tilde{\beta}_{\mathrm{R}}+\tau_{1}-\tau_{2}+\partial_{E_{a}}f^{\mathcal{O}}(E_{a},E_{d})+\partial_{E_{a}}f^{\phi}(E_{a},E_{b})\,,\\
S^{\prime}(E_{b})&=\tau_{2}-\tau_{1}+\partial_{E_{b}}f^{\phi}(E_{a},E_{b})\,,\\
S^{\prime}(E_{d})&=\tilde{\beta}_{\mathrm{L}}+\partial_{E_{d}}f^{\mathcal{O}}(E_{a},E_{d})\,.\end{split}

(3.27)

These equations correspond to the propagation saddle in the gravitational analysis.
Indeed, we exactly recover the gravitational saddle point equations in (
2.22
) after choosing
f^{\phi}
and
f^{\mathcal{O}}
in (
3.11
).

Next, we take the probe operator to be the interior shell operator
\psi=\mathcal{O}
.
Again, we compute the numerator of the two-point function, which now has two copies each of
R^{\mathcal{O}}
and
\left(R^{\mathcal{O}}\right)^{\dagger}
.
Since we can contract these in two different ways, the numerator is given by a sum of two terms

\begin{split}\mathcal{T}_{1}^{\mathcal{O}}=&\,\int\mathrm{d}E_{a}\mathrm{d}E_{b}\mathrm{d}E_{d}\,e^{S(E_{a})+S(E_{b})+S(E_{d})}e^{-(\tilde{\beta}_{\mathrm{R}}E_{a}+\tilde{\beta}_{\mathrm{L}}E_{d})+(\tau_{2}-\tau_{1})(E_{a}-E_{b})-f^{\mathcal{O}}(E_{a},E_{d})}e^{-f^{\mathcal{O}}(E_{a},E_{b})}\\
&+\int\mathrm{d}E_{a}\mathrm{d}E_{b}\mathrm{d}E_{c}\,e^{S(E_{a})+S(E_{b})+S(E_{c})}e^{-\tilde{\beta}_{\mathrm{R}}E_{a}/2+\tilde{\beta}_{\mathrm{L}}E_{b}+\tilde{\beta}_{\mathrm{R}}E_{c}/2+\tau_{1}(E_{b}-E_{a})+\tau_{2}(E_{c}-E_{b})}\\
&\hskip 289.07999pt\times e^{-f^{\mathcal{O}}(E_{b},E_{c})-f^{\mathcal{O}}(E_{a},E_{b})}\,.\end{split}

(3.28)

The first term corresponds to the propagation saddle because the saddle point equations are identical to (
3.27
).
The saddle point equations for the second term are

\begin{split}S^{\prime}(E_{a})&=\frac{1}{2}\tilde{\beta}_{\mathrm{R}}+\tau_{1}+\partial_{E_{a}}f^{\mathcal{O}}(E_{a},E_{b})\,,\\
S^{\prime}(E_{b})&=\tilde{\beta}_{\mathrm{L}}+\tau_{2}-\tau_{1}+\partial_{E_{b}}f^{\mathcal{O}}(E_{b},E_{c})+\partial_{E_{b}}f^{\mathcal{O}}(E_{a},E_{b})\,,\\
S^{\prime}(E_{c})&=\frac{1}{2}\tilde{\beta}_{\mathrm{R}}-\tau_{2}+\partial_{E_{c}}f^{\mathcal{O}}(E_{b},E_{c})\,.\end{split}

(3.29)

These equations correspond to the propagation saddle in the gravitational analysis.
As earlier, we recover the gravitational saddle point equations in (

) after choosing

appropriately.

Lastly, we can decompose an arbitrary probe as a linear combination of orthonormal operators

Substituting the ETH ansatz in (

so the numerator term evaluates to

&\hskip 289.07999pt\times e^{-f^{\mathcal{O}}(E_{b},E_{c})-f^{\mathcal{O}}(E_{a},E_{b})}\\
&+\sum_{i}w_{i}^{2}\int\mathrm{d}E_{a}\mathrm{d}E_{b}\mathrm{d}E_{d}\,e^{S(E_{a})+S(E_{b})+S(E_{d})}e^{-(\tilde{\beta}_{\mathrm{R}}E_{a}+\tilde{\beta}_{\mathrm{L}}E_{d})+(\tau_{2}-\tau_{1})(E_{a}-E_{b})-f^{\mathcal{O}}(E_{a},E_{d})}e^{-f^{\phi_{i}}(E_{a},E_{b})}\,.\end{split}

(3.32)

Indeed, the first and third terms correspond to the propagation saddle, which exists for all operators in the decomposition in (
3.30
).
The second term corresponds to the annihilation saddle and only exists for the operator
\mathcal{O}
.
This is the term that leads to detectability.
From these results, we can conclude that the ETH answers perfectly match the gravitational path integral calculations.

5
Discussion

The results presented in this paper demonstrate that Euclidean correlation functions computed at asymptotic infinity can detect the microstate of a black hole in any dimension through nonperturbative gravitational effects.
Our analysis was restricted to Euclidean quantities that are not directly detected by asymptotic Lorentzian observers.
Indeed, the single-sided Lorentzian two-point function does not have an exponentially large annihilation saddle contribution because we can no longer tune the insertion times to be close to a divergence. That said, previous work has argued that complex states of gravity, including black hole microstates, could be identified in Lorentzian signature by sufficiently precise asymptotic measurements of quantities like the mass
[
1
]

or multipole moments
[
46
]
of the spacetime. Likewise see
[
23
]
for a recent analysis of Lorentzian quantities that can identify the interior state of a black hole.

In this paper we only dealt with the two-sided eternal black hole in the canonical ensemble for AdS spacetimes. It would be interesting to extend our analysis to asymptoticaly flat space, to single-sided black holes, and to fixed energy states rather than the fixed temperature ones that we considered here. We have also not addressed what happens if the spacetime is in a superposition of states, one of which we seek to detect. In fact, because any sufficiently large set of shell states provides a basis
[
6
,
9
]
, we could regard any given microstate as a superposition of a great many others. In that case, the amplitude for any superposition component will be exponentially small in the entropy. However, we could take a black hole to be in, for example, an equal superposition of two shell states and ask how to interpret a measurement of the state from the point of view of a bulk observer. Operationally this would presumably require a description in terms of a collapse of the wavefunction behind the horizon
[
47
]
.
