EventWeave: A Dynamic Framework for Capturing Core and Supporting Events in Dialogue Systems
Zhengyi Zhao, Shubo Zhang, Yiming Du, Bin Liang, Baojun Wang, Zhongyang Li, Binyang Li, Kam-Fai Wong

Abstract
Large language models have improved dialogue systems, but often process conversational turns in isolation, overlooking the event structures that guide natural interactions. Hence we introduce EventWeave , a framework that explicitly models relationships between conversational events to generate more contextually appropriate dialogue responses. EventWeave constructs a dynamic event graph that distinguishes between core events (main goals) and supporting events (interconnected details), employing a multi-head attention mechanism to selectively determine which events are most relevant to the current turn. Unlike summarization or standard graph-based approaches, our method captures three distinct relationship types between events, allowing for more nuanced context modeling. Experiments on three dialogue datasets demonstrate that EventWeave produces more natural and contextually appropriate responses while requiring less computational overhead than models processing the entire dialogue history. Ablation studies confirm improvements stem from better event relationship modeling rather than increased information density. Our approach effectively balances comprehensive context understanding with generating concise responses, maintaining strong performance across various dialogue lengths through targeted optimization techniques.

Current approaches to dialogue coherence include summarization-based memory
Araujo et al. (2023)
;
Zhong et al. (2022)
and graph-based methods
Yang et al. (2020)
;
Ong et al. (2024)
. While summarization methods implicitly capture events, they often condense information uniformly without explicitly modeling the relationships between events or their varying importance to the current dialogue turn
Kirstein et al. (2025)
. Similarly, graph-based approaches merge conversation segments into high-level nodes but may not effectively distinguish between core and supporting events
Walker et al. (2022)
. Both approaches face challenges in balancing comprehensive context understanding with generating concise, natural responses that don’t overwhelm users with unnecessary details.

To address these challenges, we introduce
EventWeave
, a framework that explicitly models event relationships to improve dialogue response generation by constructing a dynamic event graph that identifies and organizes conversational events based on their semantic relationships. Our key insight is that effective dialogue responses don’t require surfacing all available information—instead, they need to access the right information at the right time. Unlike systems that simply recall all details, EventWeave distinguishes between three types of event relationships to determine which information is most relevant to the current conversation turn, employing a multi-head attention mechanism that calculates relevance scores between the current query embedding and each event node representation. This selective attention mechanism dynamically selects the most appropriate events based on both semantic similarity and relationship structure within the graph, enabling the model to generate concise, natural responses that maintain conversational flow while addressing the user’s current needs without overwhelming them with exhaustive recaps of the conversation history.

We evaluate EventWeave on three benchmark datasets: Conversation Chronicle (CC)

, Multi-Session Conversations (MSC)

, and LoCoMo
Maharana et al. (2024)
. Our evaluation metrics focus not just on information recall but on response naturalness, relevance, and conversational appropriateness. Importantly, our ablation studies confirm that the improvements come from better event relationship modeling rather than simply including more information in responses. To address scalability concerns with longer dialogues, we additionally conduct a comprehensive analysis showing how EventWeave performs across varying dialogue lengths and graph sizes, demonstrating that EventWeave maintains strong performance up to approximately 35 turns, after which a pruning mechanism effectively manages computational overhead while preserving critical event relationships.

Our main contributions can be summarized as follows:

•

We introduce a framework for dialogue response generation that explicitly models relationships between events, enabling nuanced understanding of conversation context.

•

We develop a dynamic event graph construction method that organizes events into semantic clusters with three relationship types, helping distinguish between information that should be surfaced in responses and information that should remain in the background.

•

We demonstrate that our approach generates more natural and contextually appropriate responses while requiring less computational overhead compared to models that process the entire dialogue history.

•

We provide a detailed scalability analysis showing how our approach can be optimized for dialogues of varying lengths, with specific optimization techniques for long-term conversations.

;

3
Hierarchical Event Graph Construction

To effectively capture both core events and supporting events in dialogues, we propose a dynamic event graph construction method that incrementally organizes dialogue information as the conversation unfolds, shows in Figure
2
.

3.1
Initial Graph Construction

Event-Aware Dialogue Detection.

The first step processes each incoming turn
t_{i}=(s_{1}^{i},s_{2}^{i})
to identify whether it contains meaningful event information, where
s_{1}
and
s_{2}
represent sentences in this turn. Each turn
t_{i}
is encoded as a contextual embedding vector
\mathbf{v}_{t_{i}}
using a BERT-based encoder fine-tuned on DailyDialog and OpenDialKG datasets. To distinguish event-bearing turns from conversational fillers, we employ a binary classifier
f_{\text{event}}(\mathbf{v}_{t_{i}})
:

f_{\text{event}}(\mathbf{v}_{t_{i}})=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }t_{i}\text{ contains an event}\\
0&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

(1)

This classifier, implemented as a simple feedforward network with ReLU activation atop the encoder. For turns containing multiple events (identified via semantic segmentation based on discourse markers and topic shifts), we further process them to obtain sub-event embeddings
\{\mathbf{v}_{t_{i,j}}\}
, where
\mathbf{v}_{t_{i,j}}
represents embedding for sub-event
j
within turn
i
. Sub-events are detected using a combination of constituency parsing and discourse marker identification, allowing us to capture granular event information.

Event Cluster Formation.

Dialogue events rarely exist in isolation, rather, they form thematic groups that evolve throughout conversations. Without clustering, our experiments showed a 27% decrease in response relevance scores due to fragmented context representation. We therefore group related events into clusters
c
based on semantic similarity. For any two event embeddings
\mathbf{v}_{t_{i,j}}
and
\mathbf{v}_{t_{k,l}}
, we apply clustering with a similarity threshold
\delta
:

c=\left\{\mathbf{v}_{t_{i,j}}:\text{cos}(\mathbf{v}_{t_{i,j}},\mathbf{v}_{t_{k,l}})>\delta\right\}

(2)

Each resulting cluster forms an
Event Node

e_{j}=(c,\mathbf{v}_{e_{j}})
in the graph, where
\mathbf{v}_{e_{j}}
is the weighted average of all event embeddings within the cluster:

\mathbf{v}_{e_{j}}=\frac{1}{|c|}\sum_{\mathbf{v}_{t_{i,j}}\in c}\mathbf{v}_{t_{i,j}}

(3)

Relationship Types and Edge Formation.

We incorporate three relationship types, each serving a distinct purpose:

•

Sequential Edges:
Connect nodes
e_{i}
and
e_{i+1}
in temporal order, preserving conversation flow and ensuring chronological context

•

Temporal Edges:
Link events with explicit time references (e.g., “yesterday,” “next week”), enabling temporal reasoning across non-adjacent turns

•

Reasoning Links:
Connect thematically related nodes regardless of sequential position, enabling cross-reference of related topics

Reasoning links are particularly important and are established when the similarity between nodes
e_{i}
and
e_{j}
exceeds threshold
\theta
:

r_{ij}=\begin{cases}1&\text{if cos}(\mathbf{v}_{e_{i}},\mathbf{v}_{e_{j}})>\theta\\
0&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

(4)

While similarity-based linking may seem simplistic, our approach enhances this measure through context-aware embedding adjustments. Specifically, we compute similarity using contextualized embeddings that incorporate surrounding dialogue turns:

\text{cos}(\mathbf{v}_{e_{i}}^{ctx},\mathbf{v}_{e_{j}}^{ctx})=\text{cos}(f_{ctx}(\mathbf{v}_{e_{i}},C_{i}),f_{ctx}(\mathbf{v}_{e_{j}},C_{j}))

(5)

where
f_{ctx}
is a context integration function and
C_{i}
,
C_{j}
are the surrounding context windows. This approach outperformed more complex semantic role labeling methods in our validation tests while maintaining computational efficiency.

The final event graph
G_{t}=(E,R)
captures both the sequential flow and thematic coherence of the dialogue.

To support dynamic representation of long-term dialogue interactions, each event graph
G_{t}
is progressively incorporated into a hierarchical structure
\mathcal{G}
. Each completed segment
G_{t}
is represented by a summary node
s_{t}
in this hierarchical structure.

Each summary node
s_{t}
aggregates event embeddings
\mathbf{v}_{e_{j}}
from
G_{t}
using attention-weighted summation:

\mathbf{v}_{s_{t}}=\sum_{j}\alpha_{j}\mathbf{v}_{e_{j}}

(6)

The relevance weights
\alpha_{j}
are computed using a self-attention mechanism over event nodes, where:

\alpha_{j}=\frac{\exp(w^{T}\mathbf{v}_{e_{j}})}{\sum_{k}\exp(w^{T}\mathbf{v}_{e_{k}})}

(7)

with
w
being a learnable parameter vector trained on dialogue summarization data.

3.2
Interlayer Connection Linking

When constructing each higher layer
L_{k+1}
, we create interlayer connections that reflect event updates across layers. Rather than linking all node pairs, we selectively connect nodes based on semantic changes between layers.

For event
e_{j}
appearing in layers
L_{k}
and
L_{k+1}
with embeddings
e_{j}^{(k)}
and
e_{j}^{(k+1)}
, we detect significant updates when:

\text{cos}(e_{j}^{(k)},e_{j}^{(k+1)})<\beta

(8)

Importantly, this similarity-based approach is enhanced with contextual understanding through: (1) Preprocessing that extracts key content updates via entity tracking. (2) Incorporating dialogue acts and speaker information into similarity computation. (3) Using semantic role features to weight similarity scores. These enhancements help overcome the limitations of pure cosine similarity, allowing us to identify meaningful context changes even when surface similarity might be high.

For each significantly updated node, we establish interlayer connections weighted by normalized similarity:

w_{jk}=\frac{\text{cos}(e_{j}^{(k)},e_{j}^{(k+1)})}{\sum_{i}\text{cos}(e_{i}^{(k)},e_{j}^{(k+1)})}

(9)

3.3
Adaptive Node Preservation Strategy

We introduce an innovative adaptive node preservation strategy that dynamically determines which nodes to retain based on both contextual importance and predicted future utility. Unlike our initial approach which relied solely on time-based obsolescence, our enhanced method employs a reinforcement learning framework to optimize node retention decisions. Specifically, for each node
e_{i}^{(k)}
, we compute a utility score
U(e_{i}^{(k)})
as:

U(e_{i}^{(k)})=\lambda_{1}I(e_{i}^{(k)})+\lambda_{2}F(e_{i}^{(k)})-\lambda_{3}C(e_{i}^{(k)})

(10)

where
I(e_{i}^{(k)})
represents information uniqueness measured via embedding distance to other nodes,
F(e_{i}^{(k)})
estimates future reference probability with a trained LSTM predictor, and
C(e_{i}^{(k)})
is the computational cost of maintaining the node. The weights
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\lambda_{3}
are learned through policy gradient methods, optimizing for dialogue coherence.

Before deleting any node, we ensure connectivity preservation using our enhanced path-finding mechanism:

\displaystyle P_{\text{conn}}(e_{i}^{(k)},e_{j}^{(k+1)})=

(11)

\displaystyle\arg\min_{P}\sum_{(e_{m},e_{n})\in P}\frac{1}{\text{cos}(e_{m},e_{n})+\epsilon}\cdot\Omega(e_{m},e_{n})

where
\Omega(e_{m},e_{n})
is a learned compatibility function that incorporates semantic role alignment and dialogue coherence metrics between nodes.

4
Response Generation via Graph Retrieval

4.1
Multi-Perspective Query Mechanism

To address how relevant events are retrieved from graph during responsing, we introduce a multi-perspective query mechanism to dynamic event graph from three complementary angles:

Recency-based retrieval:
Prioritizes recent events using a temporal decay function:

\text{score}_{\text{recency}}(e_{i})=\exp(-\gamma(t_{\text{current}}-t_{e_{i}}))

(12)

where
\gamma
controls decay rate and
t_{e_{i}}
is the timestamp of event
e_{i}
.

Relevance-based retrieval:
Measures semantic similarity between the current dialogue context
\mathbf{c}_{\text{current}}
and event nodes:

\text{score}_{\text{relevance}}(e_{i})=\text{cos}(\mathbf{c}_{\text{current}},\mathbf{v}_{e_{i}})

(13)

Graph-structural retrieval:
Utilizes graph topology to identify structurally important nodes via a modified PageRank algorithm, where transition probabilities incorporate edge semantics:

\text{PR}(e_{i})=\alpha\sum_{j\in\text{In}(e_{i})}\frac{\text{PR}(e_{j})}{|\text{Out}(e_{j})|}\cdot S(e_{j},e_{i})+\frac{1-\alpha}{|E|}

(14)

where
S(e_{j},e_{i})
represents the semantic strength of the edge between nodes
e_{j}
and
e_{i}
.

The final retrieval score combines these perspectives:

\displaystyle\text{score}_{\text{final}}(e_{i})=

(15)

\displaystyle\omega_{1}\text{score}_{\text{recency}}(e_{i})+\omega_{2}\text{score}_{\text{relevance}}(e_{i})+\omega_{3}\text{PR}(e_{i})

The weights
\omega_{1},\omega_{2},\omega_{3}
are dynamically adjusted based on dialogue state using a lightweight MLP that considers features such as query type, conversation phase, and user engagement signals.

4.2
Integration with Response Generation

The top-k retrieved events
\{e_{i_{1}},e_{i_{2}},...,e_{i_{k}}\}
are used to condition the response generation model. Specifically, we augment the decoder’s input with a graph-context vector
\mathbf{g}_{\text{ctx}}
:

\mathbf{g}_{\text{ctx}}=\sum_{j=1}^{k}\frac{\exp(\varepsilon\cdot\text{score}_{\text{final}}(e_{i_{j}}))}{\sum_{l=1}^{k}\exp(\varepsilon\cdot\text{score}_{\text{final}}(e_{i_{l}}))}\cdot\mathbf{v}_{e_{i_{j}}}

(16)

where
\varepsilon
is a temperature parameter controlling attention distribution sharpness.

The decoder then generates responses conditioned on both the current turn and the retrieved graph context:

P(r_{t}|c_{t},G)=\text{Decoder}(c_{t},\mathbf{g}_{\text{ctx}})

(17)

5.3
Results on Event Correlation

Beyond general response quality metrics, we next examined how effectively each model detects and leverages events in multi-session dialogues. We evaluate the outputs according to four aspects:

•

Core Event Awareness (CEA):
Measures how well a response pinpoints the main purpose or topic in the dialogue.

•

Supporting Event Coverage (SEC):
Checks if a model captures the correct and relevant supporting events that influence the core event.

•

Event Coherence (EC):
Assesses how naturally a model combines core and supporting events in a single response, rather than listing fragments.

•

Temporal Update Accuracy (TUA):
Evaluates whether a model recognizes when events are updated or replaced within the conversation.

The detailed calculation of each metric is provided in Appendix
A.3
.

Table

(last four columns) shows the event-based evaluation results, providing a deeper understanding of how each method handles event-centric dialogue reasoning. Experimental results show that
EventWeave
substantially outperforms all baselines across all event-related metrics. These results validate our hypothesis that explicitly modeling the hierarchical and dynamic nature of events in conversations leads to more accurate and coherent responses. The LoCoMo dataset presents a particularly challenging test case for event tracking due to its extended dialogue sessions with complex interleaving topics. Other memory mechanisms struggle with identifying the relationships between events and tracking updates over time, two areas where our approach excels.

Figure

demonstrates that
EventWeave
achieves better performance while maintaining greater memory efficiency. Unlike methods that store raw dialogue turns or extensive summaries, our event-focused approach captures essential information in a compact representation, reducing token overhead by 30-40% compared to LifeLongMem while achieving better results. This efficiency is especially important for longer dialogues like those in LoCoMo, where context management becomes increasingly challenging. 5.4
Ablation Study

To better understand the contribution of different components in our approach, we conducted an ablation study by removing key elements of
EventWeave
in Table
2
. The results show that (1) removing the hierarchical structure (treating all events equally) results in a significant drop in performance, confirming the importance of distinguishing between core and supporting events. (2) Dynamic updates provide substantial benefits, especially for temporal update accuracy, highlighting the importance of actively revising event relationships as conversations evolve. (3) Event ranking has a smaller but still significant impact, showing that prioritizing relevant events for response generation helps focus the model’s attention on the most important context. These findings suggest that each component of our approach contributes meaningfully to the overall performance, with the hierarchical structure and dynamic updates being particularly crucial for accurate event modeling in multi-session dialogues. Further ablation study can be found in Appendix
B.4
.

5.6
Case Study

To illustrate how EventWeave operates in practice, we present a detailed case study from the MSC dataset. Figure
4
shows a conversation where a user discusses their missing cat, with explicit annotations of core and supporting events. Further study cases are in Appendix
E
.

EventWeave’s reasoning process for generating the system’s response at Turn 5 proceeds as follows: (1)
Event Identification
: The model identifies the core event trajectory (pet missing -> pet returned) and the supporting event (cat likes tuna). (2)
Graph Traversal
: When generating the response for Turn 5, EventWeave: (i) Retrieves the most recent core event: “Pet returned” from Turn 4 (following the sequential edge). (ii) Links this to the relevant supporting event: “Tuna preference” from Turn 3 (via a reasoning link, where the cosine similarity between embeddings exceeds the threshold
\theta
). (3)
Response Generation
: The model integrates both events to generate a response that acknowledges the cat’s return (core event) while incorporating the contextually relevant detail about tuna (supporting event). In our ablation studies, we observed that without tracking supporting events, the system would generate a generic response like “Glad she’s back!” without the personalized suggestion about tuna. Conversely, without proper tracking of core events, the system might generate an irrelevant response focused only on the tuna preference, such as “Try tuna-flavored cat food to attract her,” failing to acknowledge that the cat had already returned.

6
Conclusion

In this paper, we introduced
EventWeave
, a novel framework for enhancing dialogue systems through dynamic event graph construction and hierarchical maintenance. By explicitly identifying and updating both core and supporting events,
EventWeave
enables more nuanced understanding and context-aware responses across multi-turn and multi-session dialogues. Experimental results on two benchmark datasets demonstrate that
EventWeave
not only surpasses state-of-the-art models on standard response quality metrics but also excels in event-centric evaluations, highlighting its capacity to capture and integrate supporting details.

Limitations

While our proposed framework demonstrates promising results in enhancing dialogue systems through dynamic event graph construction and hierarchical maintenance, we acknowledge certain computational considerations common to graph-based approaches. As with similar methods in this domain, the event graph’s expansion introduces computational demands that scale with the complexity of dialogue scenarios. The hierarchical structure, while beneficial for contextual understanding, naturally requires additional computational resources for maintenance compared to simpler models. These considerations, which are inherent to most graph-based approaches rather than specific to our method, represent interesting avenues for optimization research. Our implementation has shown effective performance in experimental settings, and future work could explore further efficiency improvements through graph pruning techniques, distributed computing approaches, or adaptive hierarchy management strategies. Such optimizations would make graph-based dialogue systems increasingly practical for deployment across diverse application scenarios and computational environments.

Ethical Considerations

Our experimental methodology prioritizes ethical research practices through several key measures. All experiments utilize publicly available datasets that have been ethically sourced and comply with intellectual property regulations and privacy standards. By deliberately avoiding fine-tuning models on domain-specific data, our approach inherently minimizes the potential introduction of unintended biases that often accompany specialized training protocols. These methodological choices reflect our commitment to research integrity and responsible AI development, supporting broader principles of fairness, methodological transparency, and computational sustainability within the scientific community.

LoCoMo.

Maharana et al. (2024)
focuses on extended dialogue interactions with significantly longer conversation sessions. The dataset is specifically designed to challenge memory mechanisms by incorporating natural topic shifts, references to earlier dialogue content, and information that must be tracked across long temporal spans. This makes LoCoMo particularly valuable for evaluating a system’s ability to maintain coherence and contextual awareness in extended conversations where traditional context window approaches are insufficient. LoCoMo represents real-world scenarios where conversations develop over extended periods with substantial content between related references.

A.3
Metric Computing

We describe the evaluation metrics used to assess the performance of EventWeave. Each metric is designed to capture specific aspects of how well the model tracks, integrates, and responds to core and supporting events over the course of a conversation.

Core Event Awareness (CEA)

measures the model’s ability to recall the correct core event. It is calculated as the ratio of correctly recalled core events to the total number of correct events.

\text{CEA}=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbbm{1}(\hat{e}_{i}=e_{i})}{\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbbm{1}(e_{i})}

where
N
is the total number of dialogue turns.
\hat{e}_{i}
is the predicted core event in turn
i
, and
e_{i}
is the true core event.
\mathbbm{1}(\cdot)
is an indicator function that returns 1 if the prediction is correct, otherwise 0.

Supporting Event Coverage (SEC)

measures the model’s ability to capture relevant supporting events. It is calculated as the product of the ratio of recalled correct supporting events to the total correct events, and the ratio of recalled supporting events to the total recalled events.

\text{SEC}=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{M}\mathbbm{1}(\hat{s}_{i}=s_{i})}{\sum_{i=1}^{M}\mathbbm{1}(s_{i})}\times\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{M}\mathbbm{1}(\hat{s}_{i})}{\sum_{i=1}^{M}\mathbbm{1}(\hat{s}_{i}\neq\emptyset)}

where
M
is the total number of supporting events.
\hat{s}_{i}
is the predicted supporting event, and
s_{i}
is the true supporting event.

Event Coherence (EC)

quantifies the smoothness of the model’s response, considering both the recalled supporting events and the GPT-4 score. It is calculated as the product of the ratio of recalled events to total recalled events and the GPT-4 score.

\text{ES}=f_{g}(\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{M}\mathbbm{1}(\hat{s}_{i})}{\sum_{i=1}^{M}\mathbbm{1}(\hat{s}_{i}\neq\emptyset)}\times\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbbm{1}(\hat{e}_{i}=e_{i})}{N})

where
M
and
N
are the number of supporting and core events, respectively.
\hat{s}_{i}
represents the predicted supporting event, and
\hat{e}_{i}
represents the predicted core event.
f_{g}(\cdot)
is the fluency or coherence score assigned by GPT-4.

Temporal Update Accuracy (TUA)

evaluates whether the model correctly identifies updated or outdated events. It is computed as the weighted sum of correctly recalled updated events and outdated events, normalized by the number of correct events.

\text{TUA}=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(\lambda_{1}\cdot\mathbbm{1}(\hat{u}_{i}=u_{i})+\lambda_{2}\cdot\mathbbm{1}(\hat{o}_{i}=o_{i})\right)}{\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbbm{1}(e_{i})}

where
N
is the total number of events.
\hat{u}_{i}
is the predicted updated event,
u_{i}
is the actual updated event,
\hat{o}_{i}
is the predicted outdated event, and
o_{i}
is the actual outdated event.

A.4
Implementation Details

For fair comparison, all methods use the same context window size (4k tokens) and the same backbone LLMs. Event graphs in our approach are constructed incrementally as conversations progress, with a maximum of 30 nodes per graph. For each test case, we use all previous sessions to build the event graph, while keeping the final session for testing. For each turn in the test session, the model is provided with the current query and must generate an appropriate response utilizing information from past sessions. We use a maximum of 5 previous sessions as context.

B.5
Impacts of Graph Expansion

A critical consideration for graph-based approaches in dialogue systems is their scalability as conversations grow longer. As event graphs expand with additional dialogue turns, both storage requirements and computational complexity increase, potentially limiting practical application. This section analyzes EventWeave’s computational efficiency and scalability characteristics compared to baseline methods.

B.5.1
Storage Efficiency

EventWeave achieves relative storage efficiency through its event representation approach. Rather than storing large chunks of summarized dialogue history, our framework maintains compact event triplets that capture essential semantic information. Our analysis shows that a typical 10-turn dialogue requires only 15-20 event nodes in EventWeave, compared to the more substantial storage requirements of full-text summarization methods. This compact representation helps mitigate memory concerns as conversations grow longer.

B.5.2
Computational Considerations

The selective attention mechanism in EventWeave provides computational advantages that partially offset the costs of graph maintenance. By calculating relevance scores between the current query and each event node, our model focuses computational resources on the most relevant portions of the dialogue history. This approach means that inference computation scales more with the number of relevant events than with the total graph size.

B.5.3
Scalability Analysis

To quantify EventWeave’s efficiency at scale, we conducted additional experiments on extended dialogues. We expanded our test dialogues to 35+ turns and measured three key metrics: memory file size (KB), inference time (seconds), and response quality (evaluated by GPT-4 on a 10-point scale). As shown in Table
14
, EventWeave without pruning demonstrates slightly higher resource usage than baseline methods, with a memory footprint approximately 12% larger than LifeLongMem and 50% larger than MemWalker.

However, with our pruning technique applied, EventWeave’s resource requirements become comparable to LifeLongMem while maintaining substantially better performance. EventWeave+Pruning achieves a 3% reduction in memory usage and an 11% reduction in inference time compared to standard EventWeave, while preserving response quality scores significantly above the baselines.

The pruning mechanism operates by periodically removing event nodes with low global relevance scores while preserving the structural integrity of core event relationships. This approach enables EventWeave to maintain strong performance even as dialogues extend to dozens of turns, making it practical for deployment in real-world conversational systems.

For multi-speaker scenarios with potentially overlapping events, EventWeave’s relationship modeling provides additional advantages. By explicitly capturing speaker-specific event relationships, our framework can distinguish between similar events initiated by different participants, enhancing response appropriateness in group conversations. This capability becomes increasingly important as the number of speakers and potential event overlaps increase.

Our analysis demonstrates that while graph expansion does introduce computational overhead, EventWeave’s efficient event representation and selective attention mechanism, combined with strategic pruning, create a scalable approach that balances comprehensive context modeling with practical resource constraints.

B.5.4
Efficiency Justification for Embedding Models

While modern LLM-based embedding models (e.g., Qwen3-Embedding) undeniably offer superior semantic resolution, they introduce a strict trade-off with inference latency. In practice, these advanced models often exhibit a 5
\times
to 10
\times
slowdown during embedding generation compared to legacy bidirectional models. Because EventWeave is designed for dynamic dialogue environments, it fundamentally requires
real-time graph updates
and high-frequency retrieval operations at every conversational turn.

To maintain the system’s scalability, we deliberately prioritized low-latency interactions in our design choices. Our empirical observations demonstrate that BERT provides sufficient semantic distinctiveness for accurate Event Graph construction and node clustering. By utilizing a lighter-weight embedding model, we successfully circumvent the prohibitive computational costs associated with LLM-based embeddings. This architectural decision ensures that the structural advantages of EventWeave do not become an operational bottleneck, keeping the overall overhead strictly manageable even during rapid, multi-turn interactions.

We use a separate LLM to generate questions for the event-based evaluation. There are four metrics for the evaluation: Core Event Awareness (CEA), Supporting Event Coverage (SEC), Event Coherence (EC), and Temporal Update Accuracy (TUA). And we use different question-generation prompts for each metric. The prompts are shown in Figure

# QUESTION GENERATION Prompts

For Core Event Awareness

You are given a dialogue context in which there is a main topic or core event. Based on this context, generate a question that asks the model to identify the core event or the main topic of the conversation. The question should encourage the model to focus on identifying the key event or subject that the conversation revolves around. For example, the question might be: “What is the main topic of the conversation?” or “What is the core event discussed in the conversation?”

For Supporting Event Coverage

You are given a dialogue context that includes both a core event and supporting events. Generate a question that asks the model to identify the relevant supporting events that influence or augment the core event. The question should focus on asking for additional details that provide context or elaboration on the main event. For example, the question might be: “What supporting details or events are relevant to the main event?” or “What additional events provide context to the core event in this conversation?”

For Event Coherence

You are given a dialogue context that includes both a core event and supporting events. Generate a question that asks the model to combine these events into a coherent, fluent response. The goal is to evaluate how well the model can integrate both the core and supporting events into a single, smooth response. For example, the question might be: “How can you integrate the main event and supporting details into a single, coherent response?” or “Can you provide a smooth and natural response that combines the core and supporting events?”

For Temporal Update Accuracy

You are given a dialogue context in which there has been an update or change to an event. Generate a question that asks the model to identify any updated or replaced events in the conversation. The question should focus on detecting temporal changes, such as whether any events have been updated, replaced, or made outdated during the conversation. For example, the question might be: “Has any event been updated or replaced in the conversation?” or “Which events have changed or become outdated in this conversation?”

# Guidelines for Human Evaluation

Overview.

Each annotator will be presented with a short user query or a multi-turn dialogue, followed by a response generated by one of several models. We ask you to rate each response on the four dimensions described below. Each dimension should be assigned a numerical score from 1 (very poor) to 10 (excellent). You do not need to compare responses across models directly; instead, evaluate each response on its own merit with respect to the conversation.

1. Coherence:
Measures how logically consistent and contextually relevant the response is in relation to the conversation history. A coherent response should follow smoothly from the user’s query or previous dialogue turns, without contradictions or abrupt topic changes.

Example.

Poor (Score 2):

User: “Where did Marie Curie do most of her work?”
Response: “You can buy apples on sale at the corner store.” (Off-topic, unrelated, incoherent)

Strong (Score 9):

User: “Where did Marie Curie do most of her work?”
Response: “She conducted the majority of her research at the University of Paris, and later at her own Radium Institute in France.” (Fully aligns with the user’s question, consistent and relevant)

2. Correctness:
Measures the factual accuracy and reliability of the response. A correct response should adhere to established or verifiable facts when they are presented in the conversation or are widely known.

Example.

Poor (Score 3):

User: “Who painted the Mona Lisa?”

Response: “It was painted by Vincent van Gogh in 1889.” (Factually incorrect—Mona Lisa was painted by Leonardo da Vinci.)

Strong (Score 10):

User: “Who painted the Mona Lisa?”

Response: “It was painted by Leonardo da Vinci in the early 16th century.” (Facts are accurate and relevant)

3. Style:
Refers to the language clarity, fluency, and appropriateness of tone. A good style conveys the message in a clear, succinct, and context-appropriate manner.

Example.

Poor (Score 2):

User: “What’s your favorite book?”

Response: “Book good me is me read.” (Unclear grammar, lacks readability)

Strong (Score 9):

User: “What’s your favorite book?”

Response: “I really enjoy reading ‘To Kill a Mockingbird’ for its thoughtful perspective on justice and morality.” (Clear, fluent, properly toned)

4. Helpfulness:
Measures how effectively the response addresses the user’s needs or queries. A helpful response may offer additional clarification, relevant examples, or suggestions for next steps.
Example.

Poor (Score 2):

User: “Could you recommend a good fitness routine for beginners?”

Response: “Sure, have fun.” (Provides no actionable suggestions or relevant details)

Strong (Score 10):

User: “Could you recommend a good fitness routine for beginners?”

Response: “A balanced program could include three days of light cardio, such as walking or jogging for 20–30 minutes, and two days of bodyweight exercises like push-ups and squats. Starting slowly and gradually increasing intensity can help you avoid injury.” (Directly addresses the user’s request with clear suggestions)
