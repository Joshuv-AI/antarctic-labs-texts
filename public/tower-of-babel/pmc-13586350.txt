Coffee and Parkinson’s disease: associations by CYP1A2 genotype and sex in UK Biobank
Authors: Jieping Wang, Huanyu Wang, Kang wan, Ruwen Wang, Shanshan Guo, Fuyi Ma, Yang Xiang, Hao Ma, Geng Zong, Xiao Zhi, Xiaoli Tian, Guodong Zhou, Ru Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCoffee consumption has been associated with Parkinson’s disease (PD), but findings remain inconsistent. We examined whether this association differs by CYP1A2 genotype and sex in 435,551 UK Biobank participants free of PD at baseline. During a median follow-up of 15.7 years, 3319 incident PD cases were identified. In overall analyses, coffee intake was not associated with PD risk. However, coffee intake significantly interacted with CYP1A2 genotype. Among AA carriers, coffee intake below 5 cups/day was associated with lower PD risk (HR 0.83, 95% CI 0.74–0.93), and spline analyses indicated a nonlinear association with the lowest risk at around 3 cups/day. In contrast, high coffee intake (≥5 cups/day) was associated with higher PD risk in AC carriers (HR 1.43, 95% CI 1.11–1.85) and CC carriers (HR 1.84, 95% CI 1.08–3.11). Sex-stratified analyses showed that the inverse association in AA carriers was more evident in women, whereas increased risk at high intake among AC/CC carriers was mainly observed in men. These findings support heterogeneity in the association between coffee intake and PD risk according to CYP1A2 genotype. Subject terms: Diseases, Neurology, Neuroscience, Risk factors

Introduction

Parkinson’s disease (PD) is a progressive neurodegenerative disorder defined by dopaminergic neuron loss in the substantia nigra and Lewy body accumulation1. Clinically, PD manifests with motor and non-motor symptoms that impair quality of life2, and its global prevalence is projected to more than double by 2040, underscoring a growing public health burden3. Although the exact etiology of PD remains elusive, it is well established that over 90% of cases result from a complex interplay between genetic susceptibility and environmental exposures4. Pesticides, organic solvents, and heavy metals have been linked to higher risk, whereas lifestyle factors such as caffeine consumption and cigarette smoking have been associated with lower risk in epidemiological studies5,6. Moreover, sex differences are well documented. Men have approximately twice the incidence of PD compared with women7, whereas women show higher mortality and faster progression8, likely reflecting hormonal modulation and sex-specific metabolism of neuroactive compounds that may also influence responses to environmental and dietary exposures9.

Epidemiological studies have consistently demonstrated that coffee consumption is associated with a lower incidence of PD10. This protective effect is primarily attributed to caffeine rather than other coffee constituents, as decaffeinated coffee has not shown comparable benefits11. Several biologically plausible pathways have been proposed to underlie caffeine’s neuroprotective effects, including antagonism of adenosine A2A receptors, which modulate dopaminergic signaling and reduce neuroinflammation12, suppression of oxidative stress and apoptosis13, inhibition of alpha-synuclein aggregation and enhancement of autophagic clearance14, and modulation of the gut–brain axis15. However, evidence also suggests that caffeine’s effects may follow a nonlinear dose–response, with moderate intake conferring the greatest benefit10. Collectively, these findings suggest that coffee consumption, likely in part through caffeine-related mechanisms, may be associated with lower PD risk, although individual differences in metabolism and hormonal status may influence this association.

Despite these consistent observations, not all individuals benefit equally from caffeine consumption, suggesting that interindividual genetic and metabolic variability plays a critical role16. CYP1A2 is the primary enzyme for caffeine metabolism, and its rs762551 (A > C) variant alters inducibility, defining AA as fast and AC/CC as slow metabolizers, leading to longer systemic caffeine exposure in AC/CC carriers17–19. However, findings on gene–caffeine interactions have been inconsistent. Taken together, interaction findings are not fully consistent. Some reports suggest stronger inverse associations among CYP1A2 slow metabolizers20, whereas others find no rs762551 interaction6. These considerations underscore the need to test whether CYP1A2 genotype and sex-related hormonal factors jointly modify the association between coffee intake and PD risk in large, well-characterized cohorts such as the UK Biobank.

To address these gaps, we analyzed data from the UK Biobank, a large-scale prospective cohort with comprehensive lifestyle information, genome-wide genotypes, and long-term outcomes21, to evaluate how CYP1A2-mediated caffeine metabolism and sex modify the nonlinear association between coffee intake and PD risk. We aim to (1) clarify the role of CYP1A2 genetic polymorphisms in the relationship between coffee consumption and PD incidence, and (2) investigate whether sex and CYP1A2 genotype jointly modify this association. By integrating lifestyle, genetic, and demographic factors in a large, well-characterized cohort, this study seeks to uncover the sources of heterogeneity in caffeine’s effect on PD risk and provide population-based evidence to inform precision prevention strategies.

Results

Associations of coffee intake and genotype with parkinson’s disease

During follow-up, 3,319 participants developed PD. Multivariable adjusted Cox models (Supplementary Table 1) indicated no significant associations of coffee consumption or CYP1A2 genotype with PD risk. These findings were further supported by Kaplan-Meier survival curves, which showed no significant differences in PD incidence across levels of coffee intake (log-rank p = 0.19; Fig. 2) or across CYP1A2 genotypes (log-rank p = 0.31; Fig. 3), with curves largely overlapping. The numbers of PD cases in each genotype–coffee subgroup are presented in Supplementary Table 2. We therefore further examined the association after stratification by CYP1A2 genotype.

Interaction between coffee consumption and CYP1A2 genotype

In multivariable Cox models, a significant interaction between coffee intake and CYP1A2 genotype was observed (likelihood ratio test, p = 0.01, Supplementary Table 3), indicating that the association between coffee consumption and PD risk differed across genotype groups. In contrast, no significant interaction was detected between coffee intake and sex (p = 0.20), and the three-way interaction among coffee intake, genotype, and sex was not statistically significant (p = 0.34). Stratified analysis revealed in Table 2 (detailed information presented in Supplementary Table 4) that individuals with the AA genotype had a significantly lower risk of PD with low coffee intake (HR = 0.83, 95% CI: 0.74–0.93, p = 0.002), but no significant association was observed with high intake (HR = 0.92, 95% CI: 0.74–1.14, p = 0.44). In contrast, the AC genotype showed a significant increase in PD risk with high coffee intake (HR = 1.43, 95% CI: 1.11–1.85, p = 0.006), and a similar trend was observed for the CC genotype (HR = 1.84, 95% CI: 1.08–3.11, p = 0.02).

Stratified analyses by sex and CYP1A2 genotype

Stratified analyses by sex and CYP1A2 genotype showed heterogeneous associations(Fig. 4, details are provided in the Supplementary Table 5). Among women with the AA genotype, consuming <5 cups/day of coffee was associated with reduced PD risk (HR = 0.73, 95% CI: 0.61–0.88, p = 0.001), whereas no associations were found in men with the same genotype. In contrast, both AC and CC men exhibited elevated risk at high consumption. For example, consuming ≥5 cups/day showed increased risk in the AC genotype (HR = 1.53, 95% CI: 1.12–2.09, P = 0.007) and CC genotype (HR = 2.37, 95% CI: 1.26–4.44, p = 0.007).

Sensitivity analyses

Six sensitivity analyses were conducted to evaluate the robustness of the primary findings.

First, excluding participants with follow-up shorter than 2, 3, or 5 years yielded risk estimates for coffee intake across CYP1A2 genotypes that closely paralleled those from the main analysis, with no indication of newly emergent associations (Supplementary Table 6–1).

Second, using finer exposure classifications of coffee intake (0, 1–2, 3–4, and ≥5 cups/day) yielded similar effect directions and patterns across genotype groups (Supplementary Table 6–2).

Third, additional adjustment for cumulative smoking exposure (pack-years) did not materially change the results (Supplementary Table 6–3).

Fourth, alternative genotype classifications produced stable risk patterns. When AC and CC carriers were combined, coffee intake <5 cups/day showed no association with Parkinson’s disease, whereas intake ≥5 cups/day was associated with an elevated risk (HR 1.48, 95% CI 1.18–1.86). Analyses conducted separately for AC and CC carriers generated estimates in the same direction, demonstrating that the observed pattern was robust to different grouping strategies (Supplementary Table 6–4).

Finally, sensitivity analyses incorporating polygenic scores for caffeine metabolism were performed. The CMSG2 and CMSG4 scores were derived from variants previously associated with inter-individual variability in caffeine metabolism (CYP1A2 and AHR for CMSG2; with POR and CYP2A6 additionally included for CMSG4), with higher values indicating genetically predicted faster metabolism. Across all polygenic score strata, associations between coffee consumption and Parkinson’s disease risk remained broadly consistent with the primary findings, and no evidence of dose–response relationships or systematic effect modification by genetically predicted caffeine metabolism was observed (Supplementary Table 6–5). Although isolated associations reached nominal significance (e.g., wCMSG4 > 2–3: ≥5 cups/day, HR (95% CI): 1.33(1.02–1.74), these findings were not reproduced across adjacent strata or in the unweighted score (Supplementary Table 6–6).

Discussion

In this large population-based cohort, overall coffee consumption was not associated with incident Parkinson’s disease in unstratified analyses. After accounting for CYP1A2 genotype, clear heterogeneity emerged: among CYP1A2 AA carriers, considered fast metabolizers, low-to-moderate intake was associated with a lower risk and showed a nonlinear dose-response with an apparent nadir around two cups per day. In contrast, among AC/CC carriers, higher intake, for example, 5 or more cups per day, was associated with increased risk, and lower intakes showed little evidence of benefit. These genotype-specific patterns were directionally consistent across adjustment sets and sensitivity checks, and formal tests supported both interaction and nonlinearity, indicating that population-averaged analyses may mask meaningful metabolism-dependent subgroup effects.

Our findings extend previous research by clarifying inconsistencies in the literature on coffee and Parkinson’s disease. A recent systematic review22 highlighted substantial heterogeneity in studies examining caffeine–gene interactions, particularly those involving CYP1A2 and ADORA2A variants. Multiple investigations have suggested that these genes may modify the caffeine–PD association, albeit with inconsistent directions. A consortium analysis found stronger inverse associations among CYP1A2 slow metabolizers20, whereas other work detected no rs762551 interaction but identified ADORA2A effects6. Such discrepancies likely arise from differences in exposure assessment, PD ascertainment, and co-exposures that influence CYP1A2 activity, including smoking, diet, and medication use. This may also help explain why our results differed from those of Zhao et al. Their study suggested an inverse association between caffeinated coffee and PD risk, whereas no such association was observed for decaffeinated coffee. In contrast, the UK Biobank coffee variable used in our study included decaffeinated coffee, which may have attenuated any caffeine-related association in the overall analysis11. Another study found no genetic modification in the coffee–mortality association, likely because the broad outcome and linear modeling obscured genotype-specific effects, which may explain the difference from our findings23. In contrast, by modeling CYP1A2-mediated caffeine metabolism and nonlinear dose–response patterns, our analysis revealed a consistent pattern: fast metabolizers (AA) benefited from low-to-moderate intake, whereas slow metabolizers (AC/CC) showed potential harm at higher levels. This protective effect was more evident in women, consistent with evidence that estrogen modulates CYP1A2 activity and caffeine clearance24,25. By adding genotype-specific nonlinear curves, our study integrated these strands into a coherent framework in which metabolic phenotype and sex shape the effective exposure window for caffeine.

The metabolism-dependent associations observed in this study align with established pharmacokinetic mechanisms of caffeine. Fast metabolizers maintain moderate caffeine levels that antagonize adenosine A₂A receptors, enhancing dopaminergic transmission and reducing neuroinflammation12. In contrast, slow metabolizers experience prolonged exposure that may lead to receptor desensitization, oxidative stress, and mitochondrial dysfunction, particularly at high doses13. However, direct mechanistic evidence for caffeine-induced neurotoxicity at high habitual intake in humans remains limited, and our data cannot separate caffeine-specific effects from correlated lifestyle factors. Sex-related hormonal regulation further contributes to this variability. Women with the CYP1A2 AA genotype showed lower PD risk at low-to-moderate intake, whereas men with AC or CC genotypes exhibited higher risk at the highest intake. Estrogen modulates CYP1A2 activity and adenosine signaling, and the direction and magnitude of caffeine’s effects may vary with hormonal context26,27. Joint modeling of sex and CYP1A2 genotype reconciles prior heterogeneity and helps identify subgroups likely to benefit from modest intake and those at risk with excessive consumption. Polygenic caffeine-metabolism scores (CMSG2 and CMSG4) further support this mechanism. Derived from genome-wide association studies including CYP1A2, AHR, POR, and CYP2A628,29, these scores showed no additional heterogeneity across PRS strata, suggesting that PD risk variation mainly reflects CYP1A2 function rather than broader genomic influences. In addition, because the coffee variable used in this study included decaffeinated coffee, the observed associations should not be interpreted as direct evidence of caffeine-specific effects.

In summary, population-averaged analyses showed no association between coffee intake and incident Parkinson’s disease, but stratified models revealed clear heterogeneity by CYP1A2 genotype and sex. These findings integrate mixed results from prior cohorts into a coherent, genotype- and sex-aware exposure framework and suggest that the intake range most consistent with lower risk is context-dependent. Although residual confounding, exposure misclassification, and sparse data in extreme genotype–dose strata warrant caution, the overall pattern remained directionally consistent across model specifications and sensitivity analyses. Taken together, our results provide testable hypotheses for precision prevention and a framework for designing genotype-informed epidemiologic and interventional studies.

Despite its strengths, including a large sample size and long-term follow-up, this study has several limitations. Coffee consumption was self-reported only at baseline and was assumed to remain stable during follow-up, which may not reflect changes in individual intake habits over time and may have led to exposure misclassification. Such non-differential misclassification is likely to bias the associations toward the null. To partially address potential reverse causation, we conducted sensitivity analyses excluding the first 2, 3, and 5 years of follow-up, and the results remained materially unchanged. Also, the UK Biobank coffee variable included decaffeinated coffee and therefore did not allow us to isolate caffeine-specific exposure. Second, the generalizability of our findings may be limited because the UK Biobank cohort consists predominantly of individuals of White European ancestry. Parkinson’s disease cases were ascertained from hospital and registry records, and some cases—particularly those in early or undiagnosed stages—may have been missed, introducing potential outcome misclassification. In addition, the number of PD cases was limited within certain genotype–exposure strata, which may have reduced the precision of risk estimates, especially among CC homozygotes. Finally, as an observational study, our results do not establish causality and should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating for future mechanistic and interventional research.

In summary, in this large prospective cohort study, the association between coffee consumption and Parkinson disease risk was heterogeneous and significantly modified by CYP1A2 genotype, while sex-stratified analyses suggested additional subgroup differences. Population-averaged analyses masked important effect modifications: women with the fast-metabolizer AA genotype demonstrated reduced PD risk at low-to-moderate coffee intake, whereas men with slow-metabolizer genotypes (AC/CC) exhibited elevated risk at high consumption levels. These findings underscore the complex interplay between genetics, sex, and coffee exposure in PD pathogenesis, which cannot be adequately characterized by aggregate estimates. Our results emphasize the necessity of integrating both genetic and sex-based stratification into epidemiological models to elucidate context-dependent risk–benefit patterns, thereby supporting personalized prevention strategies and guiding future mechanistic research.

Methods

Study population

The UK Biobank is a large, prospective cohort study that enrolled more than 500,000 participants aged 37 to 73 years between 2006 and 2010. Recruitment and data collection were conducted through 22 assessment centers across England, Scotland, and Wales30. At baseline, participants completed touchscreen questionnaires capturing a wide range of health-related information, underwent standardized physical examinations, and provided biological samples including blood, urine, and saliva. Detailed descriptions of the study design and data collection procedures are available on the UK Biobank website (https://biobank.ndph.ox.ac.uk/showcase/). The study received ethical approval from the Northwest Multi-Centre Research Ethics Committee, and all participants provided written informed consent (21/NW/0157)21. This research was conducted under UK Biobank application number 96989 and complied with the Declaration of Helsinki

Assessment of coffee consumption

Coffee intake was assessed at baseline using UK Biobank field ID 1498 (Supplementary Table 7), derived from a touchscreen questionnaire asking: “How many cups of coffee do you drink each DAY (The variable includes both caffeinated and decaffeinated coffee)?”. This variable was recorded as an integer number of cups per day and therefore did not allow fractional responses such as half cups. According to UK Biobank coding, special values included “Less than one”, “Do not know”, and “Prefer not to answer”. These responses were treated as non-quantifiable and excluded from the main analysis. The cut-off of ≥5 cups/day was selected based on the distribution of coffee intake in the UK Biobank cohort and consistency with previous literature, where moderate to high consumption is typically defined within the range of 2–5 cups/day. To further evaluate the robustness of this categorization, sensitivity analyses were conducted using finer exposure classifications (0, 1–2, 3–4, and ≥5 cups/day, see Supplementary Table 6–2)31.

Genotyping

Genotyping in the UK Biobank was performed using two highly similar genome-wide arrays: the UK BiLEVE Axiom array (~50,000 participants) and the UK Biobank Axiom array (~450,000 participants). Centralized quality control procedures were applied, followed by imputation to the Haplotype Reference Consortium (HRC v1.1) and UK10K reference panels. Individuals exhibiting excessive heterozygosity, high missingness, sex mismatch, or identified as relatives based on kinship coefficients were excluded.

The primary genetic exposure of interest was the CYP1A2 rs762551 polymorphism, a functional variant known to influence interindividual variability in caffeine metabolism. This SNP was directly genotyped with a 99.8% call rate, exhibited a minor allele frequency of 0.27, and conformed to Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium (P = 0.75). Based on their genotypes, participants were classified into three categories representing distinct metabolic phenotypes: AA (homozygous major allele, fast metabolizers), AC (heterozygotes, intermediate metabolizers), and CC (homozygous minor allele, slow metabolizers)32,33.

Beyond rs762551, two polygenic scores reflecting genetically predicted caffeine metabolic capacity were also constructed28. These scores—CMSG2 and CMSG4—were derived from single-nucleotide polymorphisms previously identified as strong determinants of circulating metabolite levels. CMSG2 comprised two variants with the largest effect sizes in prior metabolite GWAS: rs2472297 (near CYP1A2) and rs6968554 (near AHR). CMSG4 additionally included rs17685 (in POR) and rs56113850 (near CYP2A6), both encoding enzymes involved in hepatic oxidation pathways relevant to caffeine clearance28,34.

Unweighted scores were computed as the sum of caffeine-increasing alleles. Weighted scores (wCMSG2 and wCMSG4) were generated by multiplying allele counts by SNP-specific β coefficients (β = z/√ [p (1–p)], where z is the SNP z-score for the paraxanthine: caffeine ratio and p the minor allele frequency). Weighted scores were subsequently scaled such that CMSG2/wCMSG2 ranged from 0 to 4 and CMSG4/wCMSG4 (0 to 8), with higher scores indicating genetically predicted faster caffeine metabolism. All SNPs included in the scores have previously been linked to habitual coffee intake in the UK Biobank cohort.

Outcome

The primary study outcome was the incidence of PD. PD cases were identified from hospital inpatient and outpatient records using the International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision (ICD-10), codes G20 (Parkinson’s disease) and G21 (secondary parkinsonism). The date of the first recorded diagnosis was used as the event date. Participants who self-reported a PD diagnosis prior to 2006 were excluded from the analysis. Follow-up for disease outcomes was available until December 2024, based on the latest update of the UK Biobank health records.

Covariates

Covariates were derived from baseline assessments in the UK Biobank, and the corresponding field IDs are provided in Supplementary Table 7. Demographic variables included age and Townsend deprivation index at recruitment, both modeled as continuous variables. Ethnicity was self-reported at baseline and recategorized into three groups: White (including British, Irish, and any other White background), Black (including African, any other Black background, Black or Black British, and White and Black African), and Other (all remaining categories).

Lifestyle factors were coded as follows: sleep duration was categorized as ≥7 hours or <7 hours per night; alcohol consumption status was classified as never, current or former, and prefer not to answer. Drinking frequency was dichotomized into “more than three times per week” and “three times per week or less.” Smoking status, tea intake, and educational attainment were categorized according to prespecified rules. Education was classified into five groups: college degree, other professional qualification, high school graduate, none of the above, and middle school graduate.

Clinical history variables included diabetes, cancer, heart disease, stroke, and hypertension. Cancer history was based on self-reported physician diagnosis and coded as yes/no, with uncertain or missing responses coded separately. Heart disease was defined as reporting any cardiovascular diagnosis; those reporting only “none” were coded as 0, and “prefer not to answer” responses were coded as missing. Stroke, hypertension, and diabetes status were defined analogously.

Statistical analysis

Baseline characteristics were summarized as means with standard deviations (SD) for continuous variables and as counts with percentages for categorical variables.

Kaplan–Meier survival curves were constructed to illustrate the cumulative incidence of PD across coffee intake categories and CYP1A2 genotypes. Group differences were evaluated using the log-rank test.

The primary association between coffee consumption and PD risk was evaluated using Cox proportional hazards regression models, with hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) estimated. Analyses were stratified by CYP1A2 genotype (AA, AC, CC) to assess potential effect modification by genetic background. To eliminate confounding, three progressively adjusted models were fitted, incorporating sociodemographic characteristics and chronic medical conditions.

To evaluate effect modification, likelihood ratio tests were used to compare nested Cox proportional hazards models with and without interaction terms. Interaction terms for coffee intake × CYP1A2 genotype, coffee intake × sex, and coffee intake × genotype × sex were included in the models, while retaining all lower-order terms and the same set of covariates.

Coffee consumption was modeled both categorically (0, <5 and ≥5 cups/day) and continuously. Restricted cubic spline models with five knots were applied to assess potential non-linear dose–response associations, with zero cup/day set as the reference. Wald χ² statistics were used to test for linearity and non-linearity.

Sensitivity analyses were performed to assess the robustness of the primary findings. First, to reduce potential bias from prodromal Parkinson’s disease or reverse causation, the analyses were repeated after sequentially excluding participants with follow-up durations of <2, <3, and <5 years.

Second, alternative categorizations of coffee intake were evaluated by modeling coffee consumption using finer exposure categories (0, 1–2, 3–4, and ≥5 cups/day) to assess whether the observed associations were sensitive to the choice of cut-off values.

Third, additional models were fitted with further adjustment for cumulative smoking exposure using pack-years of smoking to examine whether more detailed control for smoking altered the results.

Fourth, alternative genotype coding strategies were evaluated. Models were refitted after combining AC and CC carriers into a single group, and additional models were estimated separately within AC-only and CC-only strata to test whether the association between coffee intake and Parkinson’s disease was sensitive to different categorizations of CYP1A2 rs762551 genotypes.

Finally, analyses incorporating polygenic scores for caffeine metabolism were conducted. Two previously established caffeine-metabolism genetic scores (CMSG2 and CMSG4), based on variants in CYP1A2, AHR, POR, and CYP2A6, were constructed according to published algorithms. Participants were grouped into predefined score strata, and within each stratum, Cox proportional hazards models were refitted using the same covariate adjustment as in the primary analysis to examine potential effect modification by genetically predicted caffeine metabolism.

All sensitivity analyses used identical model specifications and covariate sets as the main analysis.
